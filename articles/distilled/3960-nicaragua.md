# Nicaragua

## Overview
Nicaragua is the largest country in Central America by area, known as the "land of lakes and volcanoes" for its fertile volcanic soil and major freshwater lakes. Its history is marked by indigenous cultures, Spanish colonization, a long hereditary dictatorship, a revolutionary period, and recent democratic backsliding under President Daniel Ortega, who has been in power since 2007.

## Timeline
- **c. 12,000 BCE** — Paleo-Indians first inhabit the region.
- **1502** — Christopher Columbus reaches the Mosquito Coast on his fourth voyage.
- **1524** — Spanish conquistador Francisco Hernández de Córdoba founds the cities of Granada and León.
- **1821** — Nicaragua gains independence from Spain as part of the Act of Independence of Central America.
- **1838** — Nicaragua becomes a fully independent republic after the dissolution of the Federal Republic of Central America.
- **1912–1933** — United States Marines occupy Nicaragua.
- **1936–1979** — The Somoza family rules Nicaragua through a hereditary dictatorship.
- **1979** — The Sandinista National Liberation Front (FSLN) overthrows the Somoza dynasty.
- **1981–1990** — The Contra War, a conflict between the Sandinista government and U.S.-backed Contra rebels.
- **1990** — Violeta Chamorro defeats Daniel Ortega in a general election, ending Sandinista rule.
- **2006** — Daniel Ortega returns to the presidency.
- **2018** — Large-scale anti-government protests are met with a violent crackdown.
- **2021** — Ortega wins a disputed election, leading to widespread condemnation and an authoritarian turn.

## Body

### Etymology and Pre-Columbian History
The name "Nicaragua" derives from the Nahuatl term *Nicānāhuac*, meaning "here surrounded by water," referring to the country's large lakes and rivers. Before Spanish arrival, the region was inhabited by diverse indigenous groups. The Pacific coast was settled by Mesoamerican-related peoples like the Chorotega and Nicarao, who migrated from present-day Mexico. The central and Caribbean regions were home to Macro-Chibchan language groups, including the Miskito and Matagalpas.

### Spanish Colonial Era and Independence
Spanish colonization began in earnest in the 1520s, with permanent settlements founded in 1524. The Spanish established a colonial society based on agriculture and trade, leading to the creation of a mestizo (mixed European and indigenous) majority. Nicaragua gained independence in 1821, initially joining the Mexican Empire and then the Federal Republic of Central America before becoming a sovereign republic in 1838. The 19th century was marked by political rivalry between the Liberal city of León and the Conservative city of Granada, and by foreign intervention, including a brief takeover by American filibuster William Walker in 1856.

### U.S. Intervention and the Somoza Dynasty
The United States occupied Nicaragua from 1912 to 1933, establishing the National Guard as a loyal military force. After the Marines withdrew, National Guard director Anastasio Somoza García assassinated rebel leader Augusto César Sandino and seized power in 1937, beginning a 43-year family dictatorship. The Somoza regime was marked by corruption and repression, enriching the family while neglecting the population, a failure highlighted by the mismanagement of relief after the devastating 1972 Managua earthquake.

### Revolution, Contra War, and Post-War Politics
The Sandinista National Liberation Front (FSLN), inspired by Sandino, overthrew the Somoza regime in 1979. The new government faced a U.S.-funded insurgency by "Contra" rebels, leading to the Contra War (1981–1990), which killed 30,000 people. The war ended with a 1990 election won by Violeta Chamorro. Daniel Ortega and the FSLN returned to power in 2006. Since then, Nicaragua has experienced significant democratic backsliding, with Ortega winning disputed elections in 2011, 2016, and 2021. A 2025 constitutional reform established a "revolutionary socialist state" and expanded presidential powers, which critics describe as institutionalizing a dictatorship.

### Geography and Economy
Nicaragua has three distinct regions: the fertile Pacific lowlands, the central highlands, and the sparsely populated Caribbean lowlands. It is the second-poorest country in the Americas by GDP per capita. The economy is primarily agricultural, with coffee, beef, and gold as key exports. Tourism, centered on colonial cities and natural attractions, is the second-largest industry. The country is a member of the Bolivarian Alliance (ALBA) and has long considered building a transoceanic canal.

### Demographics and Culture
Nicaragua's population is predominantly mestizo (69%), with significant white (17%), black (9%), and indigenous (5%) minorities. Spanish is the official language, but English and indigenous languages are spoken on the Caribbean coast. The culture blends Spanish and indigenous influences, with a rich tradition in poetry, exemplified by Rubén Darío. Baseball is the most popular sport.

## Terms
- **Mestizo**: A person of combined European and indigenous American ancestry.
- **FSLN (Sandinistas)**: The Sandinista National Liberation Front, a left-wing political party that led the 1979 revolution and currently governs Nicaragua.
- **Contras**: A U.S.-backed right-wing rebel group that fought the Sandinista government during the 1980s.
- **Guardia Nacional**: The national guard and police force created during the U.S. occupation and used by the Somoza dynasty to maintain power.
- **Filibuster**: An adventurer who engages in unauthorized military expeditions into foreign territories, as exemplified by William Walker in 1856.
- **Nicaraguan Sign Language**: A language that spontaneously emerged among deaf children in the 1970s and 1980s, of great interest to linguists.
- **La Purísima**: A major week-long religious festival in early December honoring the Immaculate Conception, central to Nicaraguan popular religion.
- **Gallo pinto**: Nicaragua's national dish, consisting of fried rice and beans.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The nature of Nicaragua's current political system is heavily debated, with the government describing it as a revolutionary state while international observers and critics label it an authoritarian dictatorship or electoral autocracy.
- The environmental and economic viability of a proposed interoceanic canal across Nicaragua remains a contentious topic, with scientists raising concerns about its potential impact.
- The historical accuracy of the country's etymology is settled, with the theory linking the name to the chief "Nicarao" debunked by the discovery of his real name, Macuilmiquiztli.
