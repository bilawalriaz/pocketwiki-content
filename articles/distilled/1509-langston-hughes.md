# Langston Hughes

## Overview
Langston Hughes (1901–1967) was a central figure of the Harlem Renaissance, an intellectual and cultural revival of African American art in the 1920s. He pioneered **jazz poetry**, adapting the syncopated rhythms of blues and jazz to verse, and insisted on portraying working-class Black life without shame or elitism. His 1926 manifesto, "The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain," argued that Black artists should embrace their racial identity as a source of creative strength rather than imitate white standards. Beyond literature, Hughes used his weekly column in *The Chicago Defender* (1942–1962) to advocate for civil rights, and his leftist sympathies in the 1930s linked the struggle against Jim Crow to global anti-colonial and anti-fascist movements.

## Timeline
- **Feb 1, 1901** — Born in Joplin, Missouri; raised primarily in Lawrence, Kansas, by maternal grandmother Mary Patterson Langston.
- **1921** — Publishes signature poem "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" in *The Crisis*; enrolls at Columbia University.
- **1922** — Drops out of Columbia due to racial prejudice and disinterest; immerses himself in Harlem.
- **1923–24** — Works as crewman on S.S. *Malone*, traveling to West Africa and Europe; stays in Paris and England.
- **1925** — Wins first prize in *Opportunity* magazine contest; meets patron Charlotte Osgood Mason.
- **1926** — Publishes first collection, *The Weary Blues*; publishes manifesto "The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain" in *The Nation*.
- **1927** — Tours the U.S. South with Zora Neale Hurston, documenting folklore; meets Bessie Smith.
- **1929** — Graduates with B.A. from Lincoln University (historically Black university in Pennsylvania).
- **1930** — First novel, *Not Without Laughter*, wins Harmon Gold Medal.
- **1932** — Travels to Soviet Union for abandoned film project; tours Central Asia, China, and Japan.
- **1935** — Receives Guggenheim Fellowship; co-writes screenplay for *Way Down South*.
- **1937** — Covers Spanish Civil War as correspondent; broadcasts from Madrid; writes "Roar, China!"
- **1942–62** — Writes weekly opinion column for *The Chicago Defender*, creating character Jesse B. Semple ("Simple").
- **1953** — Testifies before Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations (McCarthy committee); denies Communist Party membership.
- **May 22, 1967** — Dies in New York City at age 66 from complications of prostate cancer surgery.

## Body

### Ancestry and Formative Years
Hughes was born into a distinguished, politically active mixed-race lineage. His paternal great-grandmothers were enslaved African women; his paternal great-grandfathers were white slave owners in Kentucky, one identified as Sam Clay (a relative of statesman Henry Clay) and the other as Silas Cushenberry (claimed to be Jewish). His maternal grandmother, Mary Patterson, was of African American, French, English, and Native American descent; she attended Oberlin College and married Lewis Sheridan Leary, who died in John Brown’s 1859 raid on Harpers Ferry. She later married Charles Henry Langston, an abolitionist and brother of John Mercer Langston, a prominent Reconstruction-era politician.

Hughes’s parents separated shortly after his birth; his father fled U.S. racism for Cuba and Mexico. Hughes was raised largely by his grandmother in Lawrence, Kansas, where her stories of Black resistance instilled a sense of racial duty. He described a lonely childhood alleviated by reading. After her death, he lived with family friends and later with his mother and stepfather in Cleveland, Ohio. At Central High School, he wrote for the newspaper, edited the yearbook, and composed his first jazz poem, "When Sue Wears Red." A teacher introduced him to Carl Sandburg, cementing his poetic vocation.

### Education and Early Travels
Hughes enrolled at Columbia University in 1921 under a compromise with his father, who funded engineering studies but opposed a writing career. Hughes maintained a B+ average but faced campus segregation—denied a dorm room initially—and hostility from white peers. He preferred Harlem’s cultural life and the Rand School’s socialist lectures, dropping out in 1922.

In 1923, he shipped out on the S.S. *Malone* to West Africa and Europe. In Paris, he had a romance with Anne Marie Coussey, a Gold Coast elite. In England, he joined a Black expatriate community. Returning to the U.S. in 1924, he worked for historian Carter G. Woodson but quit to busboy at the Wardman Park Hotel, where poet Vachel Lindsay "discovered" him. With patronage from Mrs. Joel Spingarn, Hughes entered Lincoln University in 1926, graduating in 1929. During the 1930s, he resided briefly in Westfield, New Jersey, sponsored by Charlotte Osgood Mason.

### Harlem Renaissance and Artistic Philosophy
Hughes’s 1921 poem "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" became his signature work. His first book, *The Weary Blues* (1926), established him as a leader of the Harlem Renaissance alongside Zora Neale Hurston, Wallace Thurman, Claude McKay, Countee Cullen, Richard Bruce Nugent, and Aaron Douglas. They briefly collaborated on *Fire!!*, a magazine for younger Black artists.

Hughes rejected the Black middle class’s assimilationist aesthetics. In "The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain" (1926), he declared: "We younger Negro artists who create now intend to express our individual dark-skinned selves without fear or shame." He centered "low-life"—the authentic lives of the working class—celebrating blues, jazz, and vernacular. His 1927 Southern tour with Hurston, funded by Mason, profoundly shifted his view of the South from stereotype to deep empathy; encounters with Bessie Smith, chain-gang escapee Ed Pinkney, and folk culture fed *Not Without Laughter* (1930), *Mule Bone* (with Hurston), and *The Book of Negro Folklore*.

Hughes framed racial consciousness as a global project, influencing Francophone writers of the **Négritude movement** (Léopold Sédar Senghor, Aimé Césaire, Léon Damas). He insisted folk and jazz rhythms were the proper basis for a poetry of racial pride.

### Prose, Theater, and the "Simple" Columns
Hughes’s first novel, *Not Without Laughter* (1930), won the Harmon Gold Medal. His short story collection *The Ways of White Folks* (1934) offered pessimistic, sardonic vignettes of interracial interactions. He co-founded the New York Suitcase Theater (1931) and the Golden Stair Press (1931) with Prentiss Taylor, publishing *The Scottsboro Limited* (1932) on the infamous Alabama case. A 1932 Soviet film project on Black life failed, but Hughes toured the USSR, Central Asia, China, and Japan, meeting Arthur Koestler in Turkmenistan.

In 1937, he covered the Spanish Civil War for the *Baltimore Afro-American*, broadcasting from Madrid and writing "Roar, China!" linking Chinese resistance to the global color line. He supported the Scottsboro Boys defense, signed a 1938 statement backing Stalin’s purges, and joined the American Peace Mobilization (1940). During WWII, he endorsed the **Double V campaign**—victory over fascism abroad and Jim Crow at home.

From 1942 to 1962, his *Chicago Defender* column featured Jesse B. Semple ("Simple"), an everyday Harlem man commenting on race, politics, and daily life. The column became his most widely read work. He founded the Skyloft Players (1941) to nurture Black playwrights and taught occasionally at Atlanta University (1947) and University of Chicago Laboratory Schools (1949). With Arna Bontemps, he co-edited *The Poetry of the Negro* (1949), a landmark anthology.

### Political Views and McCarthyism
Hughes’s 1930s poetry engaged Communist-aligned themes of class struggle ("A New Song"). He participated in Communist-affiliated groups (John Reed Clubs, League of Struggle for Negro Rights) but remained a sympathizer, refusing Party discipline. He initially opposed U.S. entry into WWII due to Jim Crow, then supported the Double V campaign. He traveled to Spain (1937) and the USSR (1932–33), experiences that broadened his internationalist framework.

In 1953, Senator Joseph McCarthy’s subcommittee summoned Hughes. He denied Communist Party membership, stating his politics were "non-theoretical, non-sectarian, and largely emotional." Following testimony, he distanced himself from the Left, excluded radical 1930s verse from *Selected Poems* (1959), and shifted toward lyric subjects. He was criticized by former allies on the Left unaware of his secret pre-hearing interrogation.

### Sexuality
Scholars debate Hughes’s sexuality. Some argue he was homosexual or bisexual, coding desire in poems and in the story "Blessed Assurance" (on a father’s anger at his son’s effeminacy). Biographer Arnold Rampersad concludes Hughes was likely asexual and passive, though he exhibited a preference for African American men. Hughes never publicly identified as gay; biographers suggest he remained closeted to protect his livelihood and standing with Black churches. The 1989 film *Looking for Langston* by Isaac Julien reclaims him as a Black gay icon.

### Death and Legacy
Hughes died May 22, 1967, in New York City. His ashes are interred beneath an African cosmogram titled *Rivers* in the Schomburg Center for Research in Black Culture, Harlem. His Harlem home gained landmark status (1981) and is a museum. Honors include the Spingarn Medal (1960), Anisfield-Wolf Award (1954), Guggenheim Fellowship (1935), and a U.S. postage stamp (2002). His work remains widely taught; *Ask Your Mama: 12 Moods for Jazz* (1960) premiered at Carnegie Hall in 2009. The Langston Hughes Project tours globally. In 2016, *The New York Times* printed "I, Too" in response to Charlotte protests, testament to his enduring relevance.

## Terms
- **Harlem Renaissance**: 1920s–30s cultural explosion of African American literature, music, and art centered in Harlem, New York.
- **Jazz poetry**: Verse that incorporates the syncopated rhythms, improvisational feel, and vernacular of jazz and blues music.
- **The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain**: Hughes’s 1926 manifesto in *The Nation* urging Black artists to embrace racial identity rather than mimic white aesthetics.
- **Négritude**: Francophone literary and ideological movement (1930s–40s) affirming Black identity and culture against French colonialism; influenced by Hughes.
- **Double V campaign**: WWII-era slogan demanding victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism (Jim Crow) at home.
- **Jesse B. Semple ("Simple")**: Fictional everyman in Hughes’s *Chicago Defender* column (1942–62) who voiced Black working-class perspectives.
- **Scottsboro Boys**: Nine Black teenagers falsely accused of rape in Alabama (1931); case became an international cause célèbre; Hughes wrote *The Scottsboro Limited* (1932).
- **McCarthyism**: Early 1950s anti-Communist investigations led by Senator Joseph McCarthy; Hughes testified in 1953.
- **African cosmogram (Rivers)**: Symbolic floor design at the Schomburg Center covering Hughes’s ashes, drawn from his poem "The Negro Speaks of Rivers."
- **Fire!!**: Short-lived 1926 magazine by younger Harlem Renaissance artists (Hughes, Hurston, Thurman, et al.) challenging respectability politics.

## Debates and open questions
- **Sexual orientation**: Whether Hughes was gay, bisexual, asexual, or closeted remains unresolved; evidence includes coded poems, "Blessed Assurance," unpublished letters, and conflicting biographer interpretations (Rampersad vs. Aldrich vs. Julien).
- **Political commitment**: Historians debate the depth of Hughes’s Communist allegiance—sympathizer vs. fellow traveler—and whether his 1953 testimony represented genuine ideological shift or pragmatic self-preservation.
- **Literary reputation vs. Black Power**: In the 1960s, younger writers (e.g., James Baldwin) criticized Hughes as outdated or insufficiently radical; Hughes countered that they were too angry. The tension between integrationist pride and separatist militancy frames assessments of his late work (*Panther and the Lash*).
- **Patronage influence**: Charlotte Osgood Mason’s financial support (1927–30) came with ideological strings; their falling out raises questions about artistic autonomy vs. white patronage in the Renaissance.
