# Mexican–American War

## Overview
The Mexican–American War (1846–1848) was a conflict between the United States and Mexico, triggered by the U.S. annexation of Texas and a territorial dispute over the Rio Grande border. The U.S. victory resulted in Mexico ceding over half its territory, including present-day California, Nevada, Utah, and parts of Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico, and Wyoming. The war intensified the U.S. debate over slavery, contributed to the sectional tensions leading to the American Civil War, and profoundly shaped the national identities and political trajectories of both nations.

## Timeline
- **Dec 29, 1845** — Texas is annexed by the United States as the 28th state.
- **Nov 1845** — President Polk sends diplomat John Slidell to Mexico to purchase disputed territories.
- **Apr 25, 1846** — Mexican forces attack U.S. troops in the Thornton Affair, the first armed clash.
- **May 13, 1846** — U.S. Congress declares war on Mexico.
- **Jul 7, 1846** — Mexico officially declares war on the United States.
- **Aug 18, 1846** — U.S. General Kearny captures Santa Fe, claiming New Mexico Territory.
- **Sep 14, 1847** — U.S. forces under General Scott capture Mexico City.
- **Jan 13, 1848** — Treaty of Cahuenga ends armed resistance in California.
- **Feb 2, 1848** — Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo is signed, ending the war.
- **Mar 10, 1848** — U.S. Senate ratifies the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo.

## Body

### Background and Causes
Mexico gained independence from Spain in 1821 but was politically unstable and financially weakened. The U.S., driven by expansionist ideology ("Manifest Destiny") and economic interests, sought to purchase northern Mexican territories. The 1845 U.S. annexation of the Republic of Texas, which Mexico still claimed, was the immediate catalyst. A dispute over the Texas-Mexico border (the Rio Grande vs. the Nueces River) escalated when President Polk sent troops into the contested area. After Mexican forces attacked the U.S. patrol in the Thornton Affair, Polk used the incident to secure a declaration of war.

### Military Campaigns
The war was fought across multiple theaters. In the north, U.S. General Zachary Taylor won key battles at Palo Alto, Resaca de la Palma, and Monterrey. In the west, U.S. forces under Stephen Kearny and John C. Frémont, aided by the Bear Flag Revolt, quickly secured California and New Mexico. The decisive campaign was General Winfield Scott's amphibious landing at Veracruz and subsequent march inland, capturing Mexico City in September 1847 after battles at Cerro Gordo, Contreras, Churubusco, and Chapultepec. The U.S. military benefited from superior training, technology (like the Colt revolver), and logistics, while the Mexican army suffered from internal political divisions, poor equipment, and high desertion rates.

### Peace and Territorial Cession
Despite military defeat, Mexico's government was fragmented and initially refused to negotiate. U.S. diplomat Nicholas Trist, though dismissed by President Polk, successfully concluded the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo. Mexico ceded the "Mexican Cession" (about 529,000 square miles) to the U.S. and recognized the Rio Grande as the border. In return, the U.S. paid Mexico $15 million and assumed $3.25 million in Mexican debts to U.S. citizens. The treaty was ratified by both governments in 1848.

### Aftermath and Legacy
The war had profound consequences. For the U.S., the vast territorial acquisition re-ignited fierce debates over the expansion of slavery, exemplified by the Wilmot Proviso, which failed in the Senate but deepened sectional divides. Many future Civil War leaders, including Ulysses S. Grant and Robert E. Lee, gained combat experience in Mexico. For Mexico, the loss of territory, life, and national prestige led to a period of introspection and further political turmoil. The war is remembered in Mexico as a foundational trauma and in the U.S. as a controversial exercise in expansionism that "poisoned" the nation by hastening the Civil War.

## Terms
- **Manifest Destiny**: The 19th-century belief that the United States was destined to expand across the North American continent.
- **Treaty of Velasco**: The 1836 agreements signed by a captured Santa Anna after the Texas Revolution, which Texas claimed recognized its independence but Mexico never ratified.
- **Wilmot Proviso**: An 1846 proposal (passed by the House but not the Senate) to ban slavery in any territory acquired from Mexico.
- **Mexican Cession**: The name for the territory Mexico ceded to the U.S. in the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, excluding the previously annexed Texas.
- **Guerrilla Warfare**: Irregular warfare conducted by small, mobile groups (like Mexican forces after 1847) against a conventional army.
- **Soldaderas**: Mexican women who accompanied soldiers, providing support like cooking and nursing, and sometimes fighting.
- **San Patricios (Saint Patrick's Battalion)**: A unit of several hundred soldiers, mostly Irish and German immigrants, who deserted the U.S. Army to fight for Mexico.
- **Niños Héroes ("Boy Heroes")**: The legendary Mexican military cadets who died defending Chapultepec Castle, becoming national martyrs.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Was the war a deliberate act of U.S. aggression?** Historians debate whether Polk intentionally provoked Mexico to seize territory, as suggested by Ulysses S. Grant's memoirs, or was responding to a genuine casus belli.
- **How decisive was the war in causing the U.S. Civil War?** While the war intensified the slavery debate, some scholars argue other factors were more critical to the outbreak of civil conflict in 1861.
- **What was the role of racial ideology?** The war was fueled by American notions of racial superiority and "civilizing" missions, which influenced both the conflict and the subsequent treatment of Mexicans and Native Americans in the ceded territories.
- **How did Mexican internal divisions affect the outcome?** The constant political instability and factional fighting in Mexico severely hampered its ability to mount a unified defense against the U.S. invasion.
