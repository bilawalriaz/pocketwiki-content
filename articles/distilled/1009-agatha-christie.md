# Agatha Christie

## Overview
Agatha Christie (1890–1976) was an English author of 66 detective novels and 14 short-story collections, best known for creating Hercule Poirot, Miss Marple, and Tommy and Tuppence. Writing during the Golden Age of Detective Fiction, she became the world’s best-selling novelist, with over two billion copies sold. Her works, including *The Mousetrap*—the longest-running play in history—and *And Then There Were None*, one of the top-selling books ever, established her as the “Queen of Crime.”

## Timeline
- **1890**: Born Agatha Mary Clarissa Miller in Torquay, Devon  
- **1914**: Married Archibald Christie; served as a Voluntary Aid Detachment nurse during WWI  
- **1920**: *The Mysterious Affair at Styles* published, introducing Hercule Poirot  
- **1926**: Disappeared for 11 days amid marital crisis; found in Harrogate  
- **1928**: Divorced Archibald Christie; began relationship with Max Mallowan  
- **1930**: Married Max Mallowan; introduced Miss Marple in *The Murder at the Vicarage*  
- **1952**: *The Mousetrap* premiered in London’s West End  
- **1971**: Appointed Dame Commander of the Order of the British Empire (DBE)  
- **1976**: Died at Winterbrook House, aged 85  

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into a wealthy upper-middle-class family, Christie was largely home-schooled due to her mother’s preference. Her father’s death in 1901 marked the end of her childhood. She developed a passion for reading early, influenced by authors like Dickens and Carroll. Her mother’s death in 1926 triggered a mental health crisis culminating in her famous disappearance.

### Literary Career and Major Works
Christie began writing detective fiction during WWI, inspired by Belgian refugees and her work as a hospital dispenser. *The Mysterious Affair at Styles* (1920) launched her career, introducing Poirot. She created iconic detectives including Miss Marple (1927) and Tommy and Tuppence (1922). Her plays, especially *The Mousetrap*, achieved unprecedented success. She also wrote six novels under the pseudonym Mary Westmacott.

### Personal Life and Marriages
Her first marriage to Archibald Christie ended in divorce after his affair. Her 1926 disappearance sparked global media attention. She married archaeologist Max Mallowan in 1930, accompanying him on excavations in the Middle East, which influenced novels like *Murder on the Orient Express*. Their marriage lasted until her death.

### War Service and Pharmacology
During both world wars, Christie worked in hospital pharmacies, gaining expertise in poisons used in many of her novels. This knowledge informed her meticulous use of real toxins—such as arsenic and thallium—in her plots.

### Archaeology and Travel
Her travels with Mallowan across the Middle East enriched her fiction with authentic settings. She participated in digs at Ur and Nimrud, documenting finds and even funding expeditions. These experiences shaped novels like *Death on the Nile* and *Murder in Mesopotamia*.

### Legacy and Estate
Christie was the first recipient of the Mystery Writers of America’s Grand Master Award (1955). Her estate, managed through Agatha Christie Limited, maintains strict control over adaptations. Her works continue to be adapted globally, and her legacy includes numerous awards and cultural tributes.

## Terms
- **Golden Age of Detective Fiction**: Period (c. 1920s–1940s) marked by intricate puzzle-like mysteries and amateur sleuths  
- **Whodunit**: Mystery genre focusing on identifying the perpetrator  
- **Red Herring**: Misleading clue diverting attention from the truth  
- **Closed Circle**: Narrative structure limiting suspects to a confined group  
- **Fugue State**: Psychological condition involving temporary amnesia or altered consciousness  
- **Poirot**: Belgian detective known for his “little grey cells” and meticulous methods  
- **Marple**: Elderly spinster detective using village wisdom to solve crimes  
- **Mary Westmacott**: Christie’s pseudonym for romantic novels  
- **UNESCO Index Translationum**: Database tracking translated works globally  
- **Thallium Poisoning**: Historical method of murder depicted in Christie’s novels  

## Debates and Open Questions
Christie’s 1926 disappearance remains debated. Diagnoses ranged from fugue state to nervous breakdown. Some speculate it was a calculated act to embarrass her husband, while others view it as genuine psychological distress. Her portrayal of ethnic minorities in early works has also sparked controversy, leading to recent editorial changes in reissued editions to remove offensive language.
