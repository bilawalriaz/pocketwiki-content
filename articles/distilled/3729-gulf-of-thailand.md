# Gulf of Thailand

## Overview
The Gulf of Thailand is a large, shallow sea inlet of the South China Sea, bordered by Thailand, Cambodia, Vietnam, and Malaysia. Historically known as the Gulf of Siam, it is a vital region for fisheries, tourism, and shipping, but faces significant environmental challenges including pollution, coral bleaching, and coastal erosion. Its geography and history are central to understanding the ecology and geopolitics of Southeast Asia.

## Timeline
- **c. 26,000-20,000 years ago** — During the Last Glacial Maximum, low sea levels meant the gulf was a large freshwater paleolake within the Sundaland subcontinent.
- **16th century** — Portuguese exploration removed the "Dragon Tail" peninsula from European maps, clarifying the gulf's true shape.
- **1939** — The country of Siam officially changed its name to Thailand, leading to the gradual adoption of "Gulf of Thailand" as the official name.
- **1953** — The International Hydrographic Organization (IHO) defined the gulf's official delimitation in its publication *Limits of Oceans and Seas*.
- **2010** — Severe coral bleaching occurred at most reef sites in Thailand's waters.
- **2015** — Coastal water monitoring found no Thai coastal waters in "excellent" condition, with most rated as "fair."
- **2017** — A 10 km-long patch of plastic refuse was found floating off Chumphon Province.
- **2022** — A pipeline leak caused an oil spill of 20-50 tonnes in the gulf, affecting Rayong province.

## Body

### Geography and Delimitation
The Gulf of Thailand is a shallow body of water, with a mean depth of 58 metres and a maximum depth of only 85 metres. This shallowness slows water exchange, and strong river inflows reduce its salinity. The International Hydrographic Organization (IHO) officially defines its southern boundary as a line from Cape Cà Mau in Vietnam to the Kelantan River estuary in Malaysia. Approximately 70% of its coastline belongs to Thailand.

### Historical Geology
During the Last Glacial Maximum (26,000-20,000 years ago), global sea levels were about 125 metres lower. The area of the gulf was a large freshwater paleolake within the Sundaland subcontinent, fed by a major paleo-river system including the ancestor of the Chao Phraya River. This landscape existed until sea levels rose around 15,000 years ago.

### Environment and Ecology
The gulf's ecosystem is under stress. Coral reefs cover 75,590 rai, but only 5% are considered fertile. Severe coral bleaching events occurred in 2010 and were detected in new areas in 2016, linked to prolonged high water temperatures. Coastal water quality is generally "fair" to "poor," with the Inner Gulf being particularly degraded. Plastic pollution is a major issue, with Thailand identified as one of the world's worst polluters; an estimated 300 sea animals die annually from ingesting plastic. Oil spills, such as those in 2013 and 2022, also threaten the marine environment.

### Fisheries and Tourism
The gulf is crucial for Thailand's fishing industry, accounting for 41% of its total marine catch. Its warm waters and coral reefs also make it a major tourist destination, with popular islands like Ko Samui and Ko Pha Ngan. Whale watching for endemic and endangered species, such as Eden's whales and Irrawaddy dolphins, is a growing activity.

### Territorial Disputes
The gulf is subject to overlapping territorial claims. Malaysia and Thailand have agreed to jointly develop disputed areas, including the islands of Ko Kra and Ko Losin. A long-standing dispute between Cambodia and Vietnam concerns the island of Phú Quốc (Koh Tral in Khmer) and a claimed 48,000 km² of continental shelf area.

## Terms
- **Paleolake**: A lake that existed in the past but has since dried up or been inundated by rising sea levels.
- **Sundaland**: A subcontinent that connected mainland Southeast Asia with the islands of western Indonesia during periods of lower sea levels.
- **Salinity**: The concentration of dissolved salts in water.
- **Coral bleaching**: A phenomenon where corals expel symbiotic algae, often due to stress like high water temperatures, turning white and potentially dying.
- **EEZ (Exclusive Economic Zone)**: A maritime zone where a state has special rights regarding the exploration and use of marine resources.
- **Continental shelf**: The extended perimeter of each continent and associated coastal plain, which may be rich in natural resources.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise causes and long-term impacts of the severe coastal erosion affecting 670 km of Thailand's gulf coastline are ongoing concerns, with mangrove clearing for shrimp farms cited as a contributing factor.
- The effectiveness of current measures to combat plastic pollution and improve coastal water quality is debated, given the documented deterioration in water quality between 2014 and 2015.
- The resolution of complex territorial disputes, particularly between Cambodia and Vietnam over maritime boundaries and resources, remains an open geopolitical question.
