# Thales of Miletus

## Overview

Thales of Miletus (c. 626/623 – c. 548/545 BC) was a pre-Socratic Greek philosopher from Miletus in Ionia, Asia Minor, and one of the Seven Sages of Ancient Greece. Beginning in eighteenth-century historiography, he came to be regarded as the first philosopher in the Greek tradition, breaking from mythological explanations of the world and instead using natural philosophy—aiming to explain natural phenomena via rational hypotheses referencing natural processes themselves (*logos* rather than *mythos*). He is thus also called the first to have engaged in mathematics, science, and deductive reasoning. His most famous thesis was that all of nature is based on a single ultimate substance, which he theorized to be water, and he thought the Earth floated on water.

## Timeline

- **c. 626/623 BC** — Thales born in Miletus, a mercantile city at the mouth of the Maeander River (near modern Didim, Turkey).
- **c. 585 BC** — According to Herodotus, Thales predicted a solar eclipse that occurred during a battle between the Lydians and Medes; the eclipse of 28 May 585 BC reached totality in Anatolia.
- **c. 582 BC** — The Seven Sages were created in the archonship of Damasius at Athens; Thales was the first sage.
- **c. 548/545 BC** — Thales died at age 78 during the 58th Olympiad, reportedly of heat stroke and thirst while watching the games.

## Body

### Life and ancestry

The main source on Thales's life is the doxographer Diogenes Laërtius, writing in the third century AD—some eight centuries after Thales's death—whose sources often contained "unreliable or even fabricated information." His dates are roughly established by datable events: assuming an *acme* (floruit) at age 40, the chronicle of Apollodorus of Athens (2nd century BC) placed his birth about 625 BC. Herodotus described Thales as "a Phoenician by remote descent," and Diogenes reports that Thales belonged to the Thelidae, Phoenicians descended from Cadmus and Agenor. However, Friedrich Nietzsche and others interpret this as meaning only that his ancestors were seafaring Cadmeians from Boeotia; it is also possible he was of mixed ancestry (his father had a Carian name, his mother a Greek one). Diogenes also references an alternative account that he was "a genuine Milesian and of a distinguished family." Reports conflict on whether he married and fathered a son (Cybisthus or Cybisthon) or adopted his nephew of the same name; Plutarch says he remained single, answering Solon that he disliked worrying about children, but later adopted his nephew.

### Travels and sagacity

It is said Thales engaged in trade and visited Egypt or Babylonia, but there is no strong evidence for such visits, and the issue is disputed; later writers commonly attributed such travels to philosophers to explain the origin of their mathematical knowledge. Egypt is considered possible since Miletus had a permanent colony there (Naucratis). Regarding Babylon, historians debate Babylonian influence on Greek mathematics: some cite the sexagesimal (base 60) system, while others note it was unknown in Greece until around Hipparchus (c. 190–120 BC). Herodotus wrote the Greeks learnt the *gnomon* (a shadow-casting instrument) from the Babylonians, but this too is disputed.

As one of the Seven Sages, Thales was associated with the Delphic maxim *gnothi seauton* ("know thyself"), arguably the most famous of all such maxims. According to Diogenes, Thales advised the Milesians not to engage in a *symmachia* ("fighting together") with the Lydians; when Croesus was defeated by Cyrus the Great, Miletus was spared because it had taken no action. Thales also counselled the Ionian cities to establish a single seat of government at Teos, "the centre of Ionia," but only Miletus received favorable terms from Cyrus.

### Water as the arche

Thales's most famous idea—that the *arche* (originating principle) of nature was water—comes down through Aristotle's *Metaphysics*. Aristotle reported the theory and then offered his own conjectures for why Thales held it: that the nutriment of everything is moist, that heat itself is generated from moisture, and that the seeds of everything have a moist nature. Aristotle's statement that Thales held water as the principle is generally accepted as genuine, though his reasons are his own. The 1870 *Dictionary of Greek and Roman Biography and Mythology* notes Thales may have followed Orphic cosmogonies but, unlike them, sought to establish the truth of the assertion; no written work by Thales survives. Most agree Thales's stamp on thought is the unity of substance—not merely that all is water, but the deeper claim that all is one. Nietzsche wrote that Greek philosophy begins with the proposition that water is the primal origin, "in language devoid of image or fable," containing embryonically the thought "all things are one."

### Mathematics

Thales was known for introducing geometry to Greece and has been described as the West's "first mathematician," credited with the oldest definition of number: a "collection of units," following the Egyptian view. Evidence comes from Proclus (a thousand years later, believed to have had Eudemus's lost *History of Geometry*), who wrote that Thales first visited Egypt, brought mathematics to Greece, and "himself discovered many propositions." Proclus attributes to Thales the discoveries that a circle is bisected by its diameter, that base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal, and that vertical angles are equal. Two fundamental theorems are called Thales's theorem: one about a triangle inscribed in a circle with the circle's diameter as one side; the other (also called the intercept theorem) about an angle intercepted by two parallel lines forming similar triangles. Modern scholars are skeptical that anyone in Thales's time produced proofs to the standard of later Greek mathematics; attribution of formal proofs is now thought to be speculative rationalization by later authors. Stories say Thales measured pyramid heights by their shadows when his own shadow equaled his height (pleasing the pharaoh Amasis) and measured distances of ships at sea, indicating familiarity with the intercept theorem and the Egyptian *seked* (ratio of run to rise of a slope, i.e., cotangent).

### Astronomy and cosmology

Thales was a noted astronomer, credited with describing the position of Ursa Minor (useful for navigation), calculating the duration of the year and the timings of equinoxes and solstices, and the position of the Pleiades. Plutarch indicates an extant work, the *Astronomy*, in verse was attributed to him; other attributed works (*On the Solstice*, *On the Equinox*, *Nautical Star-guide*) were disputed even in antiquity, and none survive. He thought the Earth was a flat disk or mound floating in water, perhaps inspired by silt deposits; he thought stars were balls of earth on fire and seems to have correctly gathered that the moon reflects the Sun's light. Rather than supernatural whims, he explained earthquakes by theorizing the Earth floats on water and is rocked by waves. According to Seneca, he explained the Nile's flooding as due to the river being beaten back by the etesian wind. A story from Aristotle recounts how Thales reserved all the olive presses in Miletus after predicting a good harvest, renting them at high prices—the first historically known creation of futures or options—to prove philosophy could be useful. Regarding the eclipse prediction, Herodotus wrote that in the sixth year of the war between the Lydians and Medes, day turned into night as foretold by Thales, halting the battle; however, based on Herodotus's own list of Median kings, Cyaxares died 10 years before the eclipse, and some historians (D. R. Dicks, F. Martini, J. L. E. Dreyer, O. Neugebauer) reject the story's historicity. Plato, Diogenes, and Hippolytus relay that Thales, intent on watching the stars, fell into a well, mocked by a Thracian servant girl.

### Engineering, divinity, and influence

Herodotus reports (while doubting it himself) that Thales got Croesus's army across the unbridged river Halys by digging a diversion upstream to reduce the flow. According to Aristotle, Thales thought "all things are full of gods"—lodestones had souls because iron is attracted to them (magnetism), and amber for its static electricity; if something moved, it was alive, and if alive, it must have a soul. Cicero reports Thales held that "God is that Mind which shaped and created all things from water." Nicholas Molinari has recently argued Thales was influenced by the archaic water deity Acheloios, worshipped in Miletus, noting *hydor* meant specifically "fresh water" and that rivers were seen as the "sinews of Acheloios."

Thales profoundly influenced later Greek thinkers, though the extent is debated due to scarce sources. He was the first of the Milesian school, followed by Anaximander (who held all was made of *apeiron*, "the unlimited") and then Anaximenes (who held everything was air). According to the Suda, Thales was the "teacher and kinsman" of Anaximander. Thales is also considered an influence on Pythagoras, who reportedly visited him as a young man and was advised to travel to Egypt. Thales was also considered the teacher of the astronomer Mandrolytus of Priene.

## Terms

- **Arche**: The originating principle or first substance of nature; for Thales, water.
- **Pre-Socratic**: Greek philosophers before Socrates, focused on natural explanation rather than mythology.
- **Seven Sages**: Semi-legendary wise statesmen and founding figures of Ancient Greece; Thales was one of the canonical four with Solon, Pittacus, and Bias.
- **Gnomon**: A shadow-casting instrument used for timekeeping, which Herodotus said the Greeks learnt from the Babylonians.
- **Intercept theorem**: Also called Thales's theorem; about an angle intercepted by two parallel lines forming similar triangles.
- **Seked (seqed)**: The Egyptian ratio of run to rise of a slope (cotangent).
- **Apeiron**: "The unlimited"; Anaximander's proposed originating principle, contrasting with Thales's water.
- **Hylozoism**: The view that matter is alive; Thales's reasoning that if something moves, it has a soul.
- **Symmachia**: A "fighting together" or alliance; Thales advised Miletus against one with the Lydians.

## Debates and open questions

- Whether Thales actually visited Egypt or Babylonia is disputed; such travels were a commonplace attribution to philosophers by later writers.
- Whether Babylonian mathematics influenced Greek mathematics is controversial; some note the sexagesimal system was unknown in Greece until around Hipparchus's time.
- The historicity of the eclipse prediction is rejected by several historians (Dicks, Martini, Dreyer, Neugebauer), partly because Cyaxares reportedly died 10 years before the eclipse; Thomas Worthen proposes the battle was a "night engagement" disrupted by a predicted lunar eclipse.
- Whether Thales produced formal mathematical proofs is doubted; modern scholars see such attributions as speculative rationalization by later authors.
- The extent of Thales's influence on later philosophers and mathematicians is debated due to the scarcity and discrepancies of sources.
- Thales's ancestry is uncertain: Phoenician, Greek, or mixed, with conflicting ancient accounts.
