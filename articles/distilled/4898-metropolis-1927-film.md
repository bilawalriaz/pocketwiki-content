# Metropolis (1927 film)

## Overview
*Metropolis* is a 1927 German expressionist science-fiction silent film directed by Fritz Lang. It is a pioneering work of the genre, renowned for its ambitious scale, complex special effects, and enduring influence on cinema and popular culture. The film presents a futuristic class-divided dystopia and explores themes of industrialization, social conflict, and the need for mediation between opposing forces.

## Timeline
- **1925** — Thea von Harbou's novel *Metropolis* is published.
- **May 22, 1925** — Principal photography for the film begins.
- **October 30, 1926** — Filming is completed after 17 months.
- **January 10, 1927** — World premiere at the UFA-Palast am Zoo in Berlin.
- **March 1927** — U.S. premiere of a heavily edited version.
- **August 1927** — A shorter, politically altered version is released in German cinemas.
- **1984** — Giorgio Moroder releases a restored, re-scored version with a pop soundtrack.
- **February 15, 2001** — A new, comprehensive restoration premieres at the Berlin Film Festival.
- **July 1, 2008** — A near-complete print of Lang's original cut is discovered in Argentina.
- **February 12, 2010** — "The Complete Metropolis" restoration premieres in Berlin and Frankfurt.
- **January 1, 2023** — The film's U.S. copyright expires, entering the public domain.

## Body
### Production and Style
*Metropolis* was produced by Erich Pommer for UFA, with a screenplay by Thea von Harbou and Fritz Lang. Filming was a grueling 17-month process that exceeded its budget, costing over five million Reichsmarks. The film's visual design, by Otto Hunte, Erich Kettelhut, and Karl Vollbrecht, is eclectic, drawing from Art Deco, Bauhaus, Cubism, Futurism, and Gothic influences. Lang cited his first view of New York City's skyscrapers as a primary inspiration. The production pioneered special effects, most notably the "Schüfftan process," which used mirrors to composite actors into miniature sets. The iconic robot, the Maschinenmensch, was created by sculptor Walter Schulze-Mittendorff using a plaster cast of actress Brigitte Helm.

### Plot and Themes
Set in a dystopian future, the film depicts a vast social divide between the wealthy elite living in skyscrapers and the oppressed workers laboring underground. The story follows Freder, the son of city master Joh Fredersen, and Maria, a saintly figure to the workers. After Freder witnesses the harsh conditions, he seeks to bridge the class gap. The inventor Rotwang, motivated by a past love for Freder's deceased mother, Hel, creates a robot duplicate of Maria to sow discord. The film's central message, delivered in its final intertitle, is: "The Mediator Between the Head and the Hands Must Be the Heart." The narrative draws on biblical sources, such as the Tower of Babel, and reflects post-WWI Weimar-era concerns about industrialization, class conflict, and political ideologies.

### Reception and Legacy
Upon release, *Metropolis* received mixed reviews. Critics praised its visual spectacle and technical achievements but often found its story and political message simplistic. H.G. Wells called it "quite the silliest film." The film was substantially cut after its premiere, and for decades, only truncated versions were available. It has since undergone multiple restorations, culminating in the 2010 "Complete Metropolis." Today, it is widely regarded as one of the greatest and most influential films ever made, ranking 67th in *Sight and Sound*'s 2022 critics' poll. In 2001, it became the first film inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World International Register. Its imagery has profoundly influenced subsequent art, music, and film, from David Bowie's *Diamond Dogs* to the design of C-3PO in *Star Wars*.

## Terms
- **Expressionism**: An artistic movement emphasizing subjective emotion and experience over objective reality, often using distorted, exaggerated visuals to convey inner states.
- **Schüfftan process**: A pioneering special effects technique using mirrors to create the illusion that actors are occupying miniature sets.
- **Maschinenmensch**: The German term for the "machine-human" or robot created by Rotwang in the film.
- **Weimar period**: The era of German history from 1919 to 1933, corresponding with the Weimar Republic, known for its vibrant but politically unstable cultural flourishing.
- **Art Deco**: A style of visual arts, architecture, and design from the 1920s and 30s characterized by bold geometry and lavish ornamentation.
- **Bauhaus**: A German art school operational from 1919 to 1933 that combined crafts and fine arts, influencing modernist architecture and design.
- **UFA (Universum Film A.G.)**: Germany's largest film studio during the silent and early sound eras.
- **Parufamet**: A distribution company formed by Paramount Pictures and Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer to loan money to UFA and distribute its films.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Political Message**: The film's core message has been interpreted as both a call for social reconciliation and a simplistic, even naïve, political statement. Its alleged communist message was criticized at the time, and director Fritz Lang later disowned the film's thesis as a "fairy tale."
- **Original Vision vs. Subsequent Versions**: The original premiere cut of the film is lost. All versions seen until 2010 were based on heavily edited cuts. The 2010 restoration, while the most complete, still lacks about five minutes of the original footage, leaving some narrative elements reconstructed.
- **Authorial Intent**: The collaborative nature of the film between Lang and von Harbou, who later joined the Nazi Party while Lang fled Germany, complicates interpretations of its political and social intent.
