# Rabbi Akiva

## Overview
Akiva ben Joseph (c. 50 – 28 September 135 CE) was a foundational *tanna* (Mishnaic sage) who transformed Judaism from a Temple-centered religion into a portable, text-based tradition capable of surviving exile. Rising from illiterate shepherd to "Chief of the Sages," he systematized the Oral Law into the structural core of the Mishnah, pioneered a hermeneutic that derived infinite legal detail from every textual particle, and fixed the Hebrew biblical canon—championing the Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes. He supported the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–136 CE) as messianic, defied Hadrian’s ban on Torah study, and was executed by the Romans via flaying, dying while reciting the *Shema*. His intellectual architecture—balancing divine foreknowledge with human free will, and strict justice with mercy—defined rabbinic Judaism’s trajectory.

## Timeline
- **c. 50 CE** — Born in Lod to humble, possibly convert parentage.
- **c. 90 CE** — Begins Torah study at age ~40 under Eliezer ben Hurcanus at Lod academy; later studies under Joshua ben Hananiah and Nahum Ish Gamzu.
- **c. 95–96 CE** — Travels to Rome; visits Nehardea before 110 CE.
- **c. 100–130 CE** — Establishes academy at Beneberak; teaches thousands (traditions cite 12,000–48,000 disciples), including Meir, Judah bar Ilai, Simeon bar Yochai, Jose ben Halafta, Eleazar ben Shammua, Nehemiah.
- **c. 110–130 CE** — Serves on Sanhedrin; decisive in canonizing Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes; guides Aquila’s Greek translation; systematizes Mishnah, Tosefta, and halakhic Midrash (Mekhilta, Sifra, Sifre).
- **132–135 CE** — Backs Bar Kokhba as Messiah; defies Hadrian’s edicts against Torah teaching.
- **c. 132–135 CE** — Imprisoned; executed by Turnus Rufus in Caesarea via combing (flaying with iron combs); dies reciting *Shema*.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Akiva was born c. 50 CE in Lod to a poor family, possibly descended from converts. Until age 40 he was an illiterate shepherd for Ben Kalba Sabuaʿ, a wealthy Jerusalemite. According to the Talmud, Ben Kalba Sabuaʿ’s daughter (later named Rachel) married Akiva on condition he study Torah. Her father disowned them, plunging the couple into poverty. With her encouragement, Akiva spent twelve years at the academy of Eliezer ben Hurcanus in Lod—his only teacher he ever called "rabbi"—followed by a second twelve-year stint, returning with 24,000 disciples. His father-in-law eventually relented, granting him half his wealth. Parallel traditions note study under Joshua ben Hananiah (who ordained him) and Nahum Ish Gamzu, and a collegial relationship with Gamaliel II and Rabbi Tarfon.

### Scholarly Systematization: Mishnah, Midrash, and Canon
The pre-Akiva Halakha was an unsystematized mass of traditional practice and logical deductions, threatened by Sadducean challenges and, post-70 CE, by Hellenistic assimilation. Akiva imposed order through a threefold division: (1) **Mishnah**—codified law arranged topically; (2) **Tosefta**—concise logical arguments for the Mishnah; (3) **Halakhic Midrash**—scriptural exegesis deriving law from textual minutiae. His disciple Rabbi Meir’s Mishnah became the backbone of Judah ha-Nasi’s canonical Mishnah; the major extant halakhic Midrashim (Mekhilta of R. Shimon, Sifra, Sifre Zutta, Sifre Deuteronomy) originate from his school. Johanan bar Nappaha (199–279 CE) later attested that all these works "took Akiva for a model."

On the Sanhedrin, Akiva secured the canonicity of the **Song of Songs** (defending it as "the Holy of Holies") and **Ecclesiastes**, while rejecting the Apocrypha (e.g., Ben Sira) from public liturgical reading—though he privately used them. He supervised his disciple Aquila’s literal Greek translation (the "rabbinical Bible") to anchor a unified text for Diaspora Jews against Christian appropriation of the Septuagint.

### Hermeneutics: The Theology of the Text
Akiva’s revolutionary principle: **Torah language has no superfluity**—not a word, syllable, letter, or crown (*tagin*) is mere form; each carries infinite legislative meaning. Unlike the Pharisees’ earlier deduction from logical inference, Akiva derived law from orthographic peculiarities (e.g., the *et* particle, doubled infinitives). This reconciled Scripture’s immutability with Judaism’s need for endless development.

**Illustrations:**
- **Ritual purity:** He abolished the folk ban on menstruating women by re-reading Leviticus 15:19–24 as regulating only Temple-related purity, not social ostracism.
- **Hebrew slave:** Deuteronomy 21:10–14, read literally, permits a soldier to marry a captive woman; Akiva ruled the "sale" of a minor daughter (Exodus 21:7) creates no marriage title but obligates the buyer to raise her and marry her at majority—every Jew is a "prince."
- **Samaritans:** He treated them as full converts, permitting intermarriage and eating their bread, despite his general strictness on lineage.
- **Agriculture in the Land:** Love for the Holy Land drove leniencies in *Kil'ayim* (mixed seeds) to ease farming burdens.

### Religious Philosophy
**God and Man:** Akiva read Genesis 1:27 ("in an image") as *after* a primordial archetype (*Adam Kadmon*), not *in* God’s image—preserving absolute divine transcendence. He lowered angels (manna is their food) to elevate God’s incomparability. Man’s creation *be-tzelem* makes murder a crime against the divine archetype. The supreme principle: "Love your neighbor as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18)—yet Judaism is not "simply morality" (a charge he attributed to Christians); ritual law remains binding.

**Theodicy and Justice:** God blends *middat ha-din* (attribute of justice) and *middat ha-rachamim* (attribute of mercy). The world is judged "according to the preponderance of works" (Mishnah Avot 3:18). The pious suffer now for few sins to gain pure reward later; the wicked prosper now for scant good to face pure punishment later. In civil law, "No mercy in justice"—strict procedure exemplifies divine justice.

**Free Will and Foreknowledge:** "All is foreseen, yet freedom of choice is given" (Avot 3:18). Human responsibility is real despite divine omniscience.

### Bar Kokhba Revolt and Martyrdom
Akiva hailed Simon bar Kokhba as the Messiah ("This is the King Messiah"), the sole evidence of active revolt participation. Modern speculation that his 24,000 students died fighting originates with Nachman Krochmal (c. 1830) and lacks early sources. A *baraita* attributes his execution to violating Hadrian’s religious edicts (banning Torah study/teaching), not political rebellion—his imprisonment preceded the revolt’s suppression (c. 132–135 CE). Turnus Rufus (Quintus Tineius Rufus) sentenced him in Caesarea. He was flayed alive with iron combs (*combing*). Three death narratives converge: he defied the teaching ban; Rufus ordered execution; he was flayed; his last words were the *Shema* ("Hear O Israel… One"), expiring on *Echad* ("One"). A heavenly voice proclaimed, "Blessed are you, Rabbi Akiva, that your life expired with *Echad*."

### Legends and Legacy
- **Moses at Sinai (Menachot 29b):** Moses sees God affixing crowns to Torah letters for Akiva’s future derivations; visits Akiva’s academy, cannot follow the discourse, but is comforted when Akiva cites "A law to Moses at Sinai." Shown Akiva’s martyrdom, Moses protests; God silences him.
- **Turnus Rufus debates:** "Man’s work > God’s work" (raw material vs. art); circumcision perfects man.
- **Stone at the well:** Water dripping on stone inspired his late start: persistent Torah penetrates the heart.
- **Wealth miracles:** Loans guaranteed by "God and the sea" repaid via treasure washed ashore; overnight hardship (lost donkey, rooster, candle) saved him from bandits.
- **Rescuing a soul from Gehenna:** He educated a dead tax-collector’s son; the son’s *Barchu* freed the father.

## Terms
- ****Tanna** (pl. *Tannaim*)** — A rabbinic sage of the Mishnaic period (c. 10–220 CE) whose teachings form the Mishnah.
- ****Mishnah**** — The first written codification of Jewish Oral Law, edited c. 200 CE by Judah ha-Nasi; structured in six orders.
- ****Tosefta**** — A companion compilation to the Mishnah, preserving alternative traditions and legal arguments.
- ****Midrash Halakha**** — Exegetical derivation of legal rulings from biblical verses; Akiva’s school produced the major extant collections (Mekhilta, Sifra, Sifre).
- ****Sanhedrin**** — The supreme Jewish legislative and judicial council (71 members) in the Second Temple and early rabbinic periods.
- ****Semikhah**** — Rabbinic ordination authorizing a sage to adjudicate legal cases and transmit tradition.
- ****Pardes**** — Mystical "orchard" of esoteric Torah interpretation; Akiva alone of four sages entered and emerged unharmed.
- ****Shema**** — The central Jewish declaration of monotheism (Deut. 6:4–9); Akiva died reciting it.
- ****Bar Kokhba Revolt**** — Jewish uprising against Rome (132–136 CE) led by Simon bar Kokhba; Akiva endorsed him as Messiah.
- ****Aquila**** — Akiva’s disciple; produced a literal Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible (c. 130 CE) for rabbinic use.

## Debates and Open Questions
1. **Role in Bar Kokhba Revolt:** Sources only confirm Akiva’s messianic endorsement of Bar Kokhba; the extent of his political/military involvement (vs. purely religious support) is historically undetermined. The *baraita* citing religious, not political, charges for his execution complicates the revolt-participation thesis.
2. **Size of Discipleship:** Traditions vary wildly (12,000, 24,000, 48,000); the plague killing 24,000 students is aggadic, not historical.
3. **Aquila’s Translation:** Whether Akiva directly supervised Aquila’s Greek version is "traditionally debated" per the source.
4. **Canonical Stance on Song of Songs:** Grätz’s view that Akiva opposed its canonicity was rebutted by I.H. Weiss; the source affirms Akiva *defended* it.
5. **Historicity of Biographical Legends:** The well-stone epiphany, Rachel’s 24-year wait, Elijah’s visit, and the wealth miracles are later aggadic embellishments; the historical core (late start, poverty, father-in-law’s reconciliation) is inseparable from legend.
6. **Date of Death:** Source places execution c. 132 CE (before revolt’s end) based on imprisonment duration; traditional date 28 September 135 CE may be symbolic.
