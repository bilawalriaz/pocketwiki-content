# National Museum of China

## Overview
The National Museum of China is the world's largest museum by building area, located on Tiananmen Square in Beijing. It houses the world's richest collection of Chinese cultural relics, serving as a key institution for presenting Chinese history and the political narrative of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP).

## Timeline
- **1912** — Founding of the Preliminary Office of the National History Museum.
- **1949** — Founding of the Beijing National History Museum.
- **1950** — Founding of the Office of the National Museum of the Revolution.
- **1959** — The museum building is completed as one of the "Ten Great Buildings" for the PRC's 10th anniversary.
- **2003** — The Museum of the Chinese Revolution and the National Museum of Chinese History merge to form the current legal entity.
- **2011** — The museum reopens after a four-year renovation, tripling its exhibition space.
- **2012** — Xi Jinping articulates the "Chinese Dream" concept at the museum.
- **2020** — Attendance drops 78% to 1.6 million due to the COVID-19 pandemic.
- **2021** — The museum is ranked the world's second most-visited art museum, after the Louvre.

## Body

### History and Architecture
The museum's legal entity was formed in 2003 by merging two institutions that had shared the same building since 1959: the Museum of the Chinese Revolution (northern wing) and the National Museum of Chinese History (southern wing). The building itself was completed in 1959 as one of the "Ten Great Buildings" celebrating the 10th anniversary of the People's Republic of China, complementing the Great Hall of the People. It sits on 6.5 hectares with a frontal length of 313 meters. A major renovation, designed by the German firm Gerkan, Marg and Partners, was completed in 2011, creating 28 new exhibition halls and bringing the total floor space to nearly 200,000 square meters.

### Collections and Exhibitions
The museum's permanent collection of over 1.4 million items spans Chinese history from the Yuanmou Man (c. 1.7 million years ago) to the end of the Qing dynasty. Key artifacts include the Houmuwu Ding (the world's heaviest ancient bronze at 832.84 kg), a Shang dynasty bronze zun with four sheep heads, and Han dynasty jade burial suits. Its permanent exhibition, "The Road to Rejuvenation," presents modern Chinese history since the First Opium War, emphasizing the CCP's role. The museum also displays politically significant items, such as the haycutter used to execute revolutionary martyr Liu Hulan and a Stetson hat worn by Deng Xiaoping during his 1979 U.S. visit.

### Public Role and Controversies
The museum is a level-1 public welfare institution funded by the Ministry of Culture and Tourism. Its central location has made its facade a site for national countdown clocks, such as for the 1997 Hong Kong handover and the 2008 Beijing Olympics. The museum has faced criticism for a 2011 Louis Vuitton exhibition, which some argued was commercialism inappropriate for a state museum. Critics also allege its modern historiography focuses on CCP triumphs while minimizing politically sensitive periods like the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution.

## Terms
- **Ten Great Buildings**: A group of landmark structures in Beijing completed in 1959 for the 10th anniversary of the People's Republic of China.
- **Houmuwu Ding**: The heaviest piece of ancient bronzeware in the world, from the Shang dynasty, weighing 832.84 kg.
- **Sancai**: A Tang dynasty tri-colored glazed ceramic technique.
- **Chinese Dream**: A political concept articulated by Xi Jinping in 2012, defined as the "great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation."
- **Century of Humiliation**: A term used in Chinese historiography for the period of foreign domination and internal weakness from the mid-19th to mid-20th century.
- **Level-1 public welfare institution**: A classification for state-funded entities in China, indicating full government financial support.

## Debates and Open Questions
The museum's historiography is debated, with critics alleging it minimizes politically sensitive subjects such as the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution while emphasizing CCP achievements. The appropriateness of commercial exhibitions, like the 2011 Louis Vuitton display, in a state-level public museum is also contested.
