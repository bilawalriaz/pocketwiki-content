# Rock music

## Overview  
Rock music is a genre of popular music that originated in the United States in the late 1940s as "rock and roll," drawing from African-American genres like blues, rhythm and blues, and gospel, alongside country and folk. Centered on the electric guitar, bass, drums, and vocals, it evolved into a diverse global phenomenon, becoming the dominant popular music genre from the 1960s to the 2010s. Rock has been deeply intertwined with cultural and social movements, serving as a vehicle for youth rebellion, political activism, and the formation of distinct subcultures.

## Timeline  
- **Late 1940s–early 1950s** — Rock and roll emerges in the U.S., blending blues, R&B, gospel, and country
- **1954** — Bill Haley’s “Rock Around the Clock” tops charts; Elvis Presley debuts
- **1964** — The Beatles appear on *The Ed Sullivan Show*, catalyzing the British Invasion
- **Mid-1960s** — Album-oriented rock rises; psychedelic and progressive styles develop
- **Late 1960s** — Punk rock reacts against perceived excesses of mainstream rock
- **1970s** — Hard rock and heavy metal solidify; disco and new wave emerge
- **1980s** — Alternative rock and indie scenes grow underground
- **1991** — Nirvana’s *Nevermind* brings grunge to the mainstream
- **1990s** — Britpop, post-grunge, and pop-punk gain prominence
- **2000s** — Garage rock and post-punk revivals occur; digital technology influences production
- **2010s** — Rock loses mainstream dominance to hip-hop and EDM

## Body  

### Origins and Early Development  
Rock and roll originated in the U.S. in the late 1940s and early 1950s, rooted in African-American musical traditions such as rhythm and blues, gospel, and blues, fused with elements of country and western. Artists like Chuck Berry, Little Richard, and Elvis Presley helped define the sound, characterized by strong backbeats, electric guitars, and verse-chorus structures. The genre quickly gained popularity among white audiences, though its Black origins were often downplayed or unacknowledged.

### The British Invasion and Global Expansion  
By the early 1960s, British bands like the Beatles, the Rolling Stones, and the Who reinterpreted American rock and roll, adding their own energy and style. The Beatles' appearance on *The Ed Sullivan Show* in 1964 marked a turning point, launching the British Invasion and establishing rock as a global force. This period also saw the rise of surf rock, folk rock, and garage rock, each contributing to the genre's expanding sonic palette.

### Diversification and Subgenre Formation  
The late 1960s and 1970s witnessed an explosion of subgenres. Psychedelic rock, influenced by LSD culture, gave way to progressive rock, which emphasized artistic complexity and conceptual themes. Simultaneously, hard rock and heavy metal emerged, prioritizing volume and intensity. Punk rock arose as a raw, rebellious counterpoint to progressive excess, while new wave and post-punk introduced more experimental and art-influenced approaches.

### Mainstream Dominance and Cultural Impact  
From the 1960s through the 2000s, rock remained the preeminent popular music genre in much of the Western world. It became a powerful medium for social commentary, protest, and youth identity, influencing fashion, politics, and lifestyle. Movements like the 1960s counterculture, 1970s punk, and 1990s grunge each used rock as a platform for challenging societal norms.

### Decline and Contemporary Trends  
Since the 2010s, rock has ceded its mainstream dominance to hip-hop and electronic dance music. However, it continues to thrive in niche markets and through revivals of classic styles. Modern rock incorporates elements from hip-hop, EDM, and world music, reflecting ongoing evolution despite declining chart presence.

## Terms  
- **Rock and roll**: The foundational genre of rock music, emerging in the late 1940s, blending blues, R&B, and country.  
- **British Invasion**: The mid-1960s phenomenon of British rock bands gaining massive popularity in the U.S. and globally.  
- **Psychedelic rock**: A subgenre inspired by LSD culture, featuring distorted sounds, studio effects, and experimental structures.  
- **Progressive rock**: A complex, artistic subgenre emphasizing instrumental skill, conceptual themes, and extended compositions.  
- **Punk rock**: A raw, rebellious subgenre characterized by short songs, simple structures, and anti-establishment themes.  
- **Grunge**: A 1990s subgenre from Seattle, blending punk and metal with introspective lyrics and heavy distortion.  
- **Alternative rock**: A broad term for rock music outside the mainstream, gaining prominence in the 1990s.  
- **Heavy metal**: A loud, aggressive subgenre emphasizing powerful riffs, virtuosic solos, and dark themes.  
- **New wave**: A diverse 1970s–80s movement blending punk energy with pop sensibilities and synthesizers.  
- **Indie rock**: Rock music produced independently of major labels, often emphasizing artistic integrity over commercial appeal.

## Debates and Open Questions  
The definition and boundaries of rock music remain contested. Scholars debate whether rock can be rigidly defined given its eclecticism and constant borrowing from other genres. The extent of Black influence on rock, particularly in its early years, is acknowledged but often underrecognized in mainstream narratives. The role of rock as a vehicle for authenticity versus commercialization continues to be debated, especially as the genre loses mainstream relevance in the streaming era. Additionally, discussions persist about gender representation in rock, with critiques of male dominance and calls for greater inclusion of women and non-binary artists.
