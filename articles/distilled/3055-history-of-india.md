# History of India

## Overview
The history of India spans from the arrival of early hominins over two million years ago to the modern nation-state. It is a story of continuous cultural development, marked by the rise and fall of major empires, the birth of world religions, and significant technological and artistic achievements. This long trajectory was profoundly shaped by internal dynamics and external interactions, culminating in the colonial period and the struggle for independence, which led to the partition of the subcontinent in 1947.

## Timeline
- **c. 73,000–55,000 years ago** — Anatomically modern humans first arrive on the Indian subcontinent.
- **c. 7000 BCE** — Sedentariness and agriculture begin at Mehrgarh in Baluchistan.
- **c. 2500–1900 BCE** — The Indus Valley Civilisation flourishes.
- **c. 1500–500 BCE** — The Vedic Period; composition of the Vedas and development of the varna system.
- **c. 600–200 BCE** — Second urbanisation; rise of Mahajanapadas, Buddhism, and Jainism.
- **322–185 BCE** — The Maurya Empire, under Ashoka, unifies most of the subcontinent.
- **4th–6th centuries CE** — The Gupta Empire presides over a "Golden Age" of cultural and intellectual flourishing.
- **1206–1526** — The Delhi Sultanate rules much of northern India.
- **1526–1857** — The Mughal Empire becomes a major global economic and political power.
- **1757–1947** — British rule, first via the East India Company and then the Crown (British Raj).
- **1947** — Partition of British India into the independent dominions of India and Pakistan.

## Body

### Prehistory and Early Civilisations
Hominin expansion reached the subcontinent approximately two million years ago. Anatomically modern humans are estimated to have arrived between 73,000 and 55,000 years ago, with the earliest known human fossils dating to 30,000 years ago. Settled agricultural life began around 7000 BCE at Mehrgarh, evolving into the Indus Valley Civilisation, one of the three early cradles of civilisation in the Old World. This urban culture, centred in modern Pakistan and north-western India, flourished between 2500 and 1900 BCE before declining, possibly due to drought, with populations scattering.

### The Vedic Period and Second Urbanisation
Around 1800–1500 BCE, Indo-Aryan tribes migrated into the north-west from Central Asia. The subsequent Vedic Period (c. 1500–500 BCE) is defined by the composition of the Vedas and the early varna social hierarchy. Society was initially pastoral and tribal, expanding into the Indo-Gangetic Plain. By c. 600 BCE, a "second urbanisation" began in the Ganges plain, leading to the consolidation of smaller realms (janapadas) into great states (mahajanapadas). This era saw the rise of new ascetic movements, including Jainism and Buddhism, which challenged Vedic orthodoxy and were later synthesised into the broader Hindu tradition.

### Empires and Classical Ages
The Maurya Empire (322–185 BCE), founded by Chandragupta Maurya, was the first to unify most of the subcontinent. Its most famous ruler, Ashoka, embraced Buddhism after the violent Kalinga War and promoted non-violence. Following the Maurya collapse, the subcontinent fragmented. The Gupta Empire (4th–6th centuries CE) later reunified northern India, ushering in a "Golden Age" of Hindu religious and intellectual resurgence. This period saw the spread of Indian culture across Asia, forming "Greater India."

### Medieval Periods
After the Gupta decline, regional powers rose, including the Chola dynasty in the south, which built a maritime empire. The early medieval period (c. 650–1200) was marked by the Tripartite struggle for Kannauj and the flourishing of Hindu temple architecture. The late medieval period (c. 1200–1526) was dominated by the Delhi Sultanate in the north and the Hindu Vijayanagara Empire in the south. The Bhakti movement, a devotional trend in Hinduism, and the founding of Sikhism also occurred during this time.

### Early Modern Period and Colonial Rule
The Mughal Empire (1526–1857) conquered most of the subcontinent, becoming the world's largest economy and manufacturing power. Its decline in the 18th century allowed regional powers like the Marathas and Sikhs to rise. European traders, beginning with the Portuguese in 1498, gradually established political control. The British East India Company gained dominance after the Battle of Plassey (1757) and was replaced by direct British Crown rule (the British Raj) after the Indian Rebellion of 1857.

### Independence and Partition
The Indian independence movement, led by figures like Mahatma Gandhi, gained momentum after World War I. Following World War II, the British government decided to transfer power. Amid rising Hindu-Muslim tensions, the British Indian Empire was partitioned in August 1947 into the independent dominions of India and Pakistan, causing massive migration and communal violence.

## Terms
- **Varna system:** The early Hindu social hierarchy, traditionally divided into four classes (Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, Shudras).
- **Janapadas:** The realms, republics, and kingdoms of ancient India, particularly during the Iron Age.
- **Mahajanapadas:** The sixteen great kingdoms and oligarchic republics that existed in northern India from c. 600 to 300 BCE.
- **Śramaṇa movements:** Ascetic movements that challenged Vedic ritualism, giving rise to religions like Buddhism and Jainism.
- **Subsidiary alliance:** A treaty system used by the British East India Company to control Indian princely states indirectly.
- **Doctrine of Lapse:** A British policy of annexing any princely state where the ruler died without a natural heir.
- **Bhakti movement:** A medieval Hindu devotional trend emphasising personal devotion to a god, which later influenced Sikhism.
- **Partition of India:** The 1947 division of British India into the independent nations of India and Pakistan.

## Debates and open questions
- The precise timing and routes of the initial migration of anatomically modern humans into the subcontinent remain debated, with estimates ranging from 75,000 to 35,000 years ago.
- The causes of the decline of the Indus Valley Civilisation are not definitively known, with drought and river shifts being prominent theories.
- Historians debate the nature and scale of the Indo-Aryan migrations into the subcontinent around 1500 BCE.
- The economic impact of British colonial rule is a subject of ongoing scholarly debate, with nationalist schools arguing it impoverished India.
