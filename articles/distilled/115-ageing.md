# Ageing

## Overview

Ageing is the process of becoming older, referring mainly to humans, other animals, and fungi, while some organisms like bacteria and perennial plants are potentially biologically immortal. In humans, ageing encompasses physical, psychological, and social changes, and is the greatest known risk factor for most human diseases. Of the roughly 150,000 people who die daily worldwide, about two-thirds die from age-related causes. Current theories divide ageing into the damage concept (accumulation of damage like DNA oxidation) and the programmed ageing concept (internal processes like DNA methylation), distinct from programmed cell death (apoptosis).

## Timeline

- **At least 3.7 billion years ago** — Early single-celled organisms appear, reproducing by fission and thought potentially immortal
- **~1 billion years ago** — Eukaryotic sexual reproduction emerges with fungal/animal kingdoms, making individual organisms disposable
- **320 million years ago** — Seed-producing plants evolve, further entrenching sexual reproduction and mortality
- **1961** — Leonard Hayflick discovers the Hayflick limit: normal human cells die after ~50 divisions in culture
- **1987** — Rowe and Kahn characterize successful ageing as freedom from disease, high functioning, and social engagement
- **1997** — Jeanne Calment dies at 122, the oldest reliably recorded human
- **2008** — Inactivation of two genes converts annual plant Arabidopsis thaliana into a potentially immortal perennial
- **2021** — Vijg review states DNA damage is "the single most important driver" of ageing
- **2025** — Cell study finds ageing accelerates midlife, with steepest changes between ages 45–55

## Body

### Ageing versus immortality

Early life forms were single-celled organisms multiplying by fission, thought not to age under favourable conditions. However, evidence of ageing leading to death has been reported in the bacterium Escherichia coli, Caulobacter crescintus, and yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Ageing became more evident with eukaryotic sexual reproduction (~1 billion years ago) and seed plants (320 million years ago), as organisms could pass on genetic material and become disposable. Even within mortal species, cancer cells (like HeLa) and germ cells retain immortality potential; artificial cloning can rejuvenate adult cells to embryonic status.

### Signs and symptoms

Age-related changes vary across bodily systems and individuals, termed "mosaic ageing." Teenagers lose ability to hear high-frequency sounds above 20 kHz; wrinkles develop mainly from photoageing; female fertility falls rapidly after 35; presbyopia (lens hardening from decreasing alpha-crystallin) affects most by age 45–50. By 55, hair greys and pattern hair loss affects 30–50% of males. Menopause typically occurs between 44 and 58. Osteoarthritis incidence rises to 53% in the 60–64 cohort. Nearly half of those over 75 have hearing loss (presbycusis); mammals have genetically lost the ability to regenerate cochlear sensory cells that fish and birds retain. By 80, over half of Americans have cataracts. Frailty affects 25% of those over 85. Atherosclerosis, an ageing disease, leads to cardiovascular disease—the most common global cause of death. Dementia affects ~3% of those 65–74, 19% of 75–84, and nearly half over 85. Semantic memory and general knowledge typically increase or remain steady, while other memory types decline. After 20, total length of brain myelinated axons reduces 10% per decade. Macular degeneration affects nearly 12% of those over 80. "Proximal ageing" (recent factors) is distinguished from "distal ageing" (traced to early life causes).

### Biological basis

Research on ageing uses model organisms like the nematode C. elegans (2–3 week lifespan) for genetic manipulation. Factors influencing ageing fall into programmed and error-related categories. Programmed factors follow a biological timetable regulating maintenance and repair systems. The rate of ageing varies across species and is largely genetically based; inactivation of two genes converted annual Arabidopsis thaliana into a perennial. Notable long-lived species include bristlecone pine (5062–5067 years), hard clam (508 years), Greenland shark (400 years), and Antarctic sponges (15,000 years). A 2025 study found ageing accelerates around age 50, with steepest changes at 45–55, particularly in blood vessels; protein GAS6 triggered early vascular deterioration in mice, while the adrenal gland shows changes as early as 30.

### Evolution of ageing

Lifespan is selected for in evolution. Traits benefiting early survival are selected even if they cause earlier death—called antagonistic pleiotropy (a gene enabling reproduction young but costing lifespan) or disposable soma (organism diverting resources from maintenance to reproduction). Lifespan-regulating mechanisms likely evolved with first multicellular organisms over a billion years ago, though yeast models suggest ageing has earlier biological roots.

### Damage-related factors

DNA damage is thought the common basis of cancer and ageing; intrinsic causes of DNA damage may be the most important ageing causes. Genetic damage, mutations, and epimutations can cause abnormal gene expression, stopping cell division or inducing apoptosis. A meta-analysis of 36 studies (4,676 participants) showed association between age and DNA damage. Genetic instability: dogs lose ~3.3% of heart muscle DNA annually versus ~0.6% in humans, close to the 6:1 ratio of maximum longevities. Waste accumulation like lipofuscin interferes with metabolism; autophagy induction improves lifespan in yeast, worms, flies, rodents, and primates. Other theories include wear-and-tear, accumulation of errors, heterochromatin loss, and cross-linkage. Studies of mtDNA mutator mice show somatic mtDNA mutations cause ageing phenotypes but cast doubt on the assumption they increase reactive oxygen species. Caloric restriction reduces 8-OH-dG DNA damage in ageing rats and mice, associating reduced oxidative damage with slower ageing.

### Research

The Mediterranean diet lowers risk of heart disease and early death, mainly through vegetables, fish, fruits, nuts, and monounsaturated fatty acids. As of 2021, insufficient clinical evidence exists that calorie restriction affects ageing. Moderate to high physical exercise lowers mortality; most benefits achieved around 3500 MET minutes per week. Resistance training improves grip strength, gait speed, and muscle mass in sarcopenia and reduces fall risk. Exercise may mitigate neuromuscular junction degradation. A meta-analysis showed loneliness carries higher mortality risk than smoking.

### Society and culture

Age is measured in whole years since birth, with legal ages for voting, drinking, consent, and retirement. "Social ageing" (cultural expectations) is distinguished from "biological ageing" (physical state). Ageism cost the US $63 billion in one year. Population ageing—increasing number and proportion of older people—has three causes: migration, longer life expectancy, and decreased birth rate. Over 11% of the world's population is 60+, projected to reach ~22% by 2050. Life expectancy at birth exceeds 80 in 33 countries. Ageing is fastest in developing countries. Income security is a major concern; pensions benefit entire families in crises. Health care expenditures will grow relative to the economy. Sociology views ageing as maturity, decline, life-cycle event, generation, or survival. A debate exists on whether longevity pursuit is cost-effective; bioethicist Ezekiel Emanuel argues life is not worth living after 75, while neurosurgeon Miguel Faria contests this. Population ageing explains only 0.2 percentage points of the 4.3% annual growth in medical spending since 1970. Self-perception of ageing matters: positive self-perceptions correlate with better health and reduced mortality; the "paradox of ageing" is that perceived health improves with age when objective health is controlled. Ageing suits (GERT, R70i, AGNES) simulate elderly impairments to build empathy in designers and caregivers.

### Healthy and successful ageing

The World Health Organization's healthy ageing framework defines health as functional ability from interactions of intrinsic capacity (physical and mental abilities: cognition, locomotion, vitality/nutrition, psychological, sensory) and environments. A study found four intrinsic capacity profiles among older adults; over half remained stable, and those with deterioration had higher risk of frailty, disability, and dementia. Successful ageing, popularized in the 1980s, traditionally emphasized absence of disabilities; Rowe and Kahn (1987) defined it as freedom from disease, high functioning, and social engagement. Social engagement is most highly rated across cultures, though definitions vary. Education is considered important for longevity, better health, and sharper cognition; while solving unfamiliar problems may decline, applying accumulated knowledge can remain stable or improve.

## Terms

- **Apoptosis**: Programmed cell death, distinct from programmed ageing
- **Antagonistic pleiotropy**: A gene enabling reproduction at young age but costing lifespan in old age
- **Disposable soma**: Organism diverting limited resources from maintenance to reproduction
- **Hayflick limit**: Normal human cells die after about 50 divisions in culture
- **Presbycusis**: Age-related hearing loss inhibiting spoken communication
- **Sarcopenia**: Loss of muscle mass and strength with age
- **Photoageing**: Wrinkle development from sun exposure
- **Intrinsic capacity**: Physical and mental abilities drawn upon during ageing
- **Mosaic ageing**: Heterogeneity of age-related changes across bodily systems and individuals
- **Antagonistic pleiotropy**: Gene with double function—enabling early reproduction but costing life expectancy

## Debates and open questions

Whether longevity pursuit and postponement of senescence are cost-effective health care goals remains debated: Ezekiel Emanuel argues human life is not worth living after 75 and longevity should not be a policy goal, while Miguel Faria contends life can be worthwhile in old age with healthy lifestyles and intellectual activity. The classic idea that sexual organisms become disposable after passing on genetic material has been perturbed by evidence that E. coli may split into distinguishable daughter cells, opening theoretical "age classes" among bacteria. Whether mitochondrial mutations increase reactive oxygen species is questioned by mtDNA mutator mouse studies. The relative contributions of programmed versus damage-related ageing mechanisms remain unresolved.
