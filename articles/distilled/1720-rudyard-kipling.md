# Rudyard Kipling

## Overview
Joseph Rudyard Kipling (1865–1936) was an English journalist, novelist, poet, and short-story writer born in British India, a setting that defined much of his literary output. He achieved immense popularity in the late Victorian and Edwardian eras for works such as *The Jungle Book* (1894), *Kim* (1901), *Just So Stories* (1902), and poems including "If—" (1910) and "The White Man's Burden" (1899). In 1907, he became the first English-language recipient and the youngest-ever winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature. Kipling’s reputation has remained deeply contested: celebrated for his narrative innovation and vivid depiction of imperial life, he is simultaneously criticized for jingoistic imperialism and racial attitudes. As the age of empire receded, critics came to view him as an incomparable, if controversial, interpreter of how empire was experienced.

## Body

### Childhood and Education (1865–1882)
Kipling was born in Bombay where his father, John Lockwood Kipling, was Principal of the Sir J. J. School of Art. His mother, Alice MacDonald, was one of four notable sisters; their connections linked Kipling to the Pre-Raphaelite circle (Edward Burne-Jones, Edward Poynter) and to his first cousin, future Prime Minister Stanley Baldwin. At age five, Kipling and his sister Trix were sent to England to board with Captain and Mrs. Holloway at Lorne Lodge, Southsea—a period Kipling later described as the "House of Desolation," marked by calculated cruelty and religious bullying. He credited this trauma with forcing him to invent lies for self-preservation, which he considered the foundation of his literary effort. Relief came only during Christmas visits to his aunt Georgiana Burne-Jones. In 1877, his mother retrieved them; Kipling then attended the United Services College at Westward Ho! (1878–1882), a school preparing boys for the army. Though initially miserable, he formed firm friendships and found material for *Stalky & Co.* (1899). Denied an Oxford scholarship due to academic and financial limitations, his father secured him a journalistic post in India.

### Early Career in India (1882–1889)
Returning to India at sixteen, Kipling worked for the *Civil and Military Gazette* in Lahore (1883–1887) and *The Pioneer* in Allahabad (1887–1889). The Gazette, which he called his "mistress," demanded relentless output, but editor Kay Robinson eventually granted him creative freedom for short stories. Annual leave in Simla, the summer capital, provided the social milieu for many early tales. Between November 1886 and June 1887, 39 stories appeared in the Gazette, collected as *Plain Tales from the Hills* (1888). In 1888 alone, he published six short-story volumes (41 stories total) plus travel sketches later collected in *From Sea to Sea*. A dispute with *The Pioneer* led to his discharge; he sold his story rights for £250 and sailed for London via East Asia and America, meeting Mark Twain in Elmira, New York—a meeting Twain summarized: "Between us, we cover all knowledge; he covers all that can be known and I cover the rest."

### London, Marriage, and American Years (1889–1896)
In London, Kipling quickly gained literary acclaim, publishing *The Light That Failed* (1891) and collaborating with Wolcott Balestier on *The Naulahka*. After Balestier’s sudden death in 1891, Kipling proposed by telegram to Balestier’s sister, Caroline "Carrie" Balestier; they married in January 1892. The couple honeymooned in the US and Japan, but a bank failure in Yokohama forced them back to Vermont. They rented "Bliss Cottage" near Brattleboro, where daughter Josephine was born (Dec 1892) and *The Jungle Books* were conceived. They later built "Naulakha," a secluded house named for the Mughal pavilion in Lahore Fort. In four productive years, Kipling produced the *Jungle Books*, *Captains Courageous*, *The Day's Work*, *The Seven Seas*, and *Barrack-Room Ballads* (including "Mandalay" and "Gunga Din"). Life in New England included visits from his father and Arthur Conan Doyle (who taught him golf), and vivid autumn foliage Kipling described in lyrical letters. However, the 1895 Venezuela boundary crisis inflamed Anglo-American tensions, and Kipling felt targeted by anti-British press sentiment. A violent threat from Carrie’s bankrupt, alcoholic brother Beatty Balestier in May 1896 destroyed Kipling’s privacy; the family left the US permanently in July 1896.

### Imperial Voice and South Africa (1896–1914)
Settling first in Torquay, then Rottingdean, and finally Bateman's in Burwash (1902), Kipling became a prominent imperial commentator. Poems "Recessional" (1897) and "The White Man's Burden" (1899) sparked fierce debate: viewed by some as anthems of duty-bound empire, by others as racist propaganda, and by some as ironic warnings of imperial hubris. *Stalky & Co.* (1899) offered a cynical schoolboy view of patriotism. From 1898, the Kiplings wintered at Cecil Rhodes’s estate in South Africa, befriending Rhodes, Milner, and Jameson. Kipling supported the British cause in the Second Boer War (1899–1902), serving briefly as a correspondent for *The Friend* in Bloemfontein. In Sussex, he engaged in naval debates (publishing *A Fleet in Being*, 1898) and wrote *Kim* (1901)—later defended against Edward Said’s *Orientalism* critique for its sympathetic portrayal of Tibetan Buddhism—and *Just So Stories* (1902), written after Josephine’s death from pneumonia. He attacked Kaiser Wilhelm II in poems like "The Rowers" (1902), coining "Hun" as an anti-German slur, and advocated an Anglo-French alliance. He also wrote early speculative fiction ("With the Night Mail," 1905; "As Easy As A.B.C.," 1912), pioneering the "indirect exposition" technique later central to hard science fiction.

### First World War and Personal Tragedy (1914–1918)
Kipling enthusiastically supported the war effort, writing propaganda and signing the 1914 "Authors' Declaration" condemning Germany's invasion of Belgium. He framed the war as civilization versus barbarism, especially after the "Rape of Belgium" and the *Lusitania* sinking. Privately, he bitterly criticized British military incompetence, blaming pre-war politicians for ignoring Boer War lessons. He pressured his son John, rejected twice for poor eyesight, into the Irish Guards via his friend Lord Roberts. John was killed at the Battle of Loos (Sept 1915), aged 18. Kipling’s grief produced the epitaph "If any question why we died / Tell them, because our fathers lied"—interpreted variously as guilt over his son’s commission or anger at national unpreparedness. The poem "My Boy Jack" (1916) is often linked to John, though it likely refers to a naval casualty (Jack Cornwell) or a generic "Jack Tar." Kipling joined the Imperial War Graves Commission, selecting the inscriptions "Their Name Liveth For Evermore" (Ecclesiasticus 44:14) for Stones of Remembrance and "Known unto God" for unidentified graves, plus "The Glorious Dead" for the Cenotaph. He wrote a two-volume history of the Irish Guards (1923).

### Post-War Politics and Decline (1918–1936)
Post-war, Kipling opposed Bolshevism ("one sixth of the world had passed bodily out of civilization"), co-founded the anti-communist Liberty League (1920), and distrusted the League of Nations, favoring an Anglo-French-American alliance. He supported the French Occupation of the Ruhr (1923), arguing Versailles revision would guarantee German domination. He opposed the 1924 Labour government as "Bolshevism without bullets," admired Mussolini early on but later called him a "deranged and dangerous egomaniac," and warned early against Hitler ("The Hitlerites are out for blood," 1933). Despite Soviet ideological attacks, his works remained popular in Russia until banned under the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact (1939). Kipling created the "Ritual of the Calling of an Engineer" (1922) for Canadian graduates (iron ring ceremony) and served as Lord Rector of St Andrews (1922–25). He scripted the first Royal Christmas Message (1932). His output slowed in the 1930s; he died of a perforated duodenal ulcer on 18 January 1936, aged 70.

## Terms
- **Anglo-Indian**: 19th-century term for people of British origin living in India; Kipling’s parents identified as such, shaping his complex identity.
- **Ayah**: Indian nanny or nursemaid; Kipling’s childhood ayah told him stories in the vernacular he "thought and dreamed in."
- **Barrack-Room Ballads**: Collection of poems (1892) written in Cockney dialect, voicing ordinary British soldiers; includes "Mandalay," "Gunga Din," "Danny Deever."
- **Indirect exposition**: Narrative technique of revealing world-building details through context rather than explanation; Kipling developed it in India and used it in *The Jungle Book* and later sci-fi stories, influencing Robert Heinlein.
- **The White Man's Burden**: 1899 poem urging the US to take up imperial duties in the Philippines; became a catchphrase for racist imperialism, though Kipling’s text includes warnings of futility and ingratitude.
- **If—**: 1910 poem from *Rewards and Fairies*; a stoic exhortation to self-mastery, voted UK’s favourite poem in a 1995 BBC poll.
- **Imperial War Graves Commission (IWGC)**: Body (now CWGC) responsible for British war cemeteries; Kipling joined after son’s death, chose key inscriptions.
- **Naulakha / Naulakha Pavilion**: Mughal marble pavilion in Lahore Fort (spelled *Naulakha*); inspired title of Kipling’s novel *The Naulahka* (misspelled) and his Vermont house *Naulakha* (correct spelling).
- **Stalky & Co.**: 1899 collection of school stories based on United Services College; protagonists display cynical, anti-authoritarian "know-it-all" outlook.
- **Swastika**: Ancient Indian sun symbol (Sanskrit *svastika*, "well-being") used on early Kipling book covers; Kipling ordered its removal once co-opted by Nazis.

## Debates and open questions
- **Imperialism and racism**: Critics remain divided whether Kipling’s empire writing endorses racial hierarchy, critiques it through irony, or documents it ambivalently. Douglas Kerr notes his place in literary history is "far from settled."
- **"The White Man's Burden" intent**: Is the poem a sincere imperial summons, a satirical warning, or a complex mix? The phrase "burden" and lines like "the blame of those ye better / The hate of those ye guard" fuel all readings.
- **John Kipling’s death and "My Boy Jack"**: The poem’s protagonist (Jack) is variously identified as John Kipling, Jack Cornwell (VC at Jutland), or a generic sailor; Kipling’s family called their dog Jack, and John was always "John," complicating the autobiographical reading.
- **Guilt over son’s commission**: Kipling used influence to place medically unfit John in the Irish Guards. The epitaph "because our fathers lied" is read either as personal guilt or as indictment of the political generation that failed to prepare the army.
- **Kipling and the Jallianwala Bagh massacre**: Indian nationalists long alleged Kipling called General Dyer "the man who saved India" and organized a benefit fund. Historians (Kim Wagner, Derek Sayer, Subhash Chopra) now attribute the phrase and fund to *The Morning Post*; Kipling donated £10 with the note "He did his duty, as he saw it."
- **Orientalism in *Kim***: Edward Said cited *Kim* as Orientalist; scholars (Janice Leoshko, David Scott) counter that its sympathetic Buddhism and narrative structure reflect a Buddhist worldview, challenging Said’s thesis.
- **Speculative fiction legacy**: Kipling’s Aerial Board of Control stories (1905, 1912) are claimed as early "hard SF" using indirect exposition; their influence on Heinlein and the genre is acknowledged but the extent of direct lineage is debated.
