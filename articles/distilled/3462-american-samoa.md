# American Samoa

## Overview
American Samoa is a self-governing, unincorporated, and unorganized territory of the United States in the South Pacific. It is unique among U.S. territories for its preservation of indigenous Samoan culture and governance, including communal land ownership and a traditional chiefly system, which has led to its residents being U.S. nationals rather than citizens at birth.

## Timeline
- **1722** — First European contact by Dutch explorer Jacob Roggeveen.
- **1889** — U.S. and German warships confront each other in Apia harbor; a typhoon destroys both fleets.
- **1899** — Tripartite Convention partitions the Samoan Islands; the eastern islands become U.S. territory.
- **1900** — U.S. formally annexes Tutuila via Deed of Cession.
- **1904** — U.S. annexes the Manuʻa Islands via Deed of Cession.
- **1911** — Territory officially renamed "American Samoa."
- **1925** — Swains Island is annexed by the U.S.
- **1949** — An attempt to incorporate American Samoa via an Organic Act is defeated by Samoan chiefs.
- **1967** — Constitution takes effect, establishing a local self-governing republican system.
- **2009** — An 8.1 magnitude earthquake and tsunami strike, killing over 170 people in the region.
- **2021** — American Samoan legislature unanimously passes a resolution against birthright citizenship.

## Body

### History and Political Status
Inhabited by Polynesians for millennia, the islands were first contacted by Europeans in the 18th century. International rivalries led to the 1899 Tripartite Convention, which gave the eastern islands to the United States. The U.S. Navy administered the territory, expanding the naval station at Pago Pago. In 1949, Samoan chiefs successfully opposed a U.S. plan for incorporation, fearing it would undermine their culture and land system. Instead, they created their own legislature, the Fono. A constitution took effect in 1967, establishing a self-governing system with executive, legislative, and judicial branches. However, the territory remains "unorganized" under U.S. law, and its residents are U.S. nationals, not citizens, a status many Samoans actively support to protect indigenous governance and communal land ownership.

### Government and Law
American Samoa's government is a representative democracy with a governor as head of government. The legislative body, the Fono, has a House of Representatives and a Senate, with senators elected by the traditional matai (chiefs). The U.S. federal government, via the Department of the Interior, retains oversight, including the power to approve constitutional amendments. A unique feature is the territory's own immigration system. Land ownership is racially restricted; over 90% is communal land owned by extended families (ʻaiga) and managed by matai, with transfers limited to those with at least 50% Samoan ancestry.

### Economy and Society
The economy is heavily dependent on federal funding and the tuna canning industry, with StarKist being the major employer. Tourism is underdeveloped due to geographic isolation. The population is predominantly ethnic Samoan, with Samoan and English as official languages. The culture is deeply rooted in the faʻa Sāmoa (the Samoan way), with the ʻaiga (extended family) and matai system at its core. American Samoa has an exceptionally high rate of military enlistment in the U.S. armed forces.

## Terms
- **Matai**: A chief or head of an extended family (ʻaiga), who is the custodian of family property and represents the family in village councils.
- **ʻAiga**: The extended family unit, the basic socio-political organization of Samoan society, which holds communal land.
- **Fono**: The territorial legislature of American Samoa, consisting of a House of Representatives and a Senate.
- **Faʻa Sāmoa**: "The Samoan way," referring to the language, customs, and protocols that govern Samoan culture and social structure.
- **Unincorporated Territory**: A U.S. territory not considered part of any U.S. state, to which only select parts of the Constitution apply.
- **U.S. National**: A person born in a U.S. territory like American Samoa who owes allegiance to the U.S. and can reside and work there, but is not a U.S. citizen at birth and lacks full voting rights.
- **Deed of Cession**: The legal documents (1900 for Tutuila, 1904 for Manuʻa) by which Samoan leaders ceded sovereignty to the United States.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Citizenship Status**: A major ongoing debate is whether residents should be granted U.S. birthright citizenship. The territorial government and many Samoans oppose it, fearing it would lead to federal court challenges against their unique political structure, land ownership laws, and cultural practices. Legal challenges (e.g., *Tuaua v. United States*) have upheld the current status.
- **Political Integration**: There is periodic discussion about moving toward greater autonomy or independence, though the response from the populace is mixed.
- **UN Listing**: American Samoa remains on the United Nations list of "non-self-governing territories," a designation the territorial government disputes, considering itself self-governing.
