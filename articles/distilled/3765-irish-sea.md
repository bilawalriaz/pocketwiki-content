# Irish Sea

## Overview
The Irish Sea is a 46,007 km² body of water separating the islands of Ireland and Great Britain. It is a vital corridor for trade, transport, and energy, with over 12 million passengers and 17 million tonnes of goods crossing annually. Its ecology, economy, and history are shaped by its geography and human activity.

## Timeline
- **c. 10,000 BC** — As glaciers retreated, a freshwater lake reconnected to the sea, forming the modern Irish Sea.
- **Roman Era** — The sea was known as *Mare Hibernicus*.
- **Late 12th C.** — Norman invasion of Ireland occurred in stages from Wales.
- **1529** — Tudor invasion of Ireland crossed the sea.
- **1952** — Low-level radioactive waste discharge began from Sellafield.
- **1988** — Dumping of sewage sludge into Liverpool Bay became illegal.
- **1995** — Hamilton North gas field began production in Liverpool Bay.
- **2005** — Arklow Bank Wind Park, with world's first >3 MW offshore turbines, became operational.
- **2020-2022** — Several historic shipwrecks, including HMS Mercury and SS Mesaba, were discovered.

## Body

### Topography and Formation
The Irish Sea connects to the North Atlantic via the North Channel and St George's Channel. It features a deep western channel (80–275 m) and shallower eastern bays (<50 m). Its current form emerged after the last glacial period, when a freshwater lake reconnected to the ocean. The International Hydrographic Organization defines its precise northern and southern limits.

### Shipping and Transport
Lacking a fixed link, the sea is the primary route for trade between Ireland and Great Britain. Major ports include Liverpool (32 million tonnes of cargo), Holyhead, and those in Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland. Ferry services connect Wales, Scotland, and England to Ireland, with the world's largest car ferry, the *Ulysses*, operating on the Dublin–Holyhead route.

### Hydrocarbon Exploration
Several sedimentary basins beneath the sea contain oil and gas. The East Irish Sea Basin is mature, with estimated reserves of 210 billion m³ of gas and 176 million barrels of oil. The Liverpool Bay Development integrates five fields, including the Douglas and Hamilton fields. Other areas like the Caernarfon Bay and Cardigan Bay basins have proven petroleum systems but remain less explored, with the Dalkey Island prospect near Dublin holding significant potential.

### Environment and Wildlife
The sea's estuaries are internationally important for migratory birds. The seabed supports diverse invertebrate communities, with key species including horse mussels, scallops, and the Dublin Bay prawn. Plankton blooms in spring form the base of the food web, supporting fish, seabirds, and marine mammals like harbour porpoises, bottlenose dolphins, and leatherback turtles.

### Radioactivity
The Irish Sea has been contaminated by discharges from the Sellafield reprocessing plant since 1952. Discharges peaked in the 1970s and have since declined significantly. Soluble elements like caesium are flushed out via the North Channel, while less soluble plutonium binds to seabed sediments, particularly in the eastern Irish Sea. The main human exposure pathway is seafood consumption, though resulting doses are low compared to natural background radiation.

### Wind Power
The sea hosts major offshore wind farms. The Arklow Bank Wind Park was a pioneering project. The Walney Extension, west of Walney Island, was the world's largest offshore wind farm as of 2018. Other significant sites include North Hoyle and Burbo Bank.

## Terms
- **Graben:** A depressed block of the Earth's crust bordered by parallel faults.
- **Syn-rift sediments:** Sedimentary layers deposited during the active stretching of the Earth's crust.
- **Unconformity:** A break in the geological record where rock layers are missing.
- **Terabecquerel (TBq):** A unit of radioactivity, measuring one trillion nuclear decays per second.
- **Plankton:** Microscopic organisms drifting in water, forming the base of the marine food web.
- **Turbidity:** The cloudiness or haziness of a fluid caused by suspended particles.
- **CCGT:** Combined Cycle Gas Turbine, a power plant using both gas and steam turbines.

## Debates and Open Questions
The greatest exploration risk in basins like Caernarfon Bay is the timing of gas generation versus trap formation. The environmental impact and long-term fate of radioactive sediments from Sellafield remain subjects of study. Proposals for a fixed sea link (tunnel or bridge) have been discussed since 1895 but face immense technical and financial challenges.
