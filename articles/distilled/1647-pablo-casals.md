# Pablo Casals

## Overview
Pau Casals i Defilló (1876–1973), known professionally as Pablo Casals, was a Catalan cellist, composer, and conductor widely regarded as the preeminent cellist of the 20th century. He single-handedly rescued J.S. Bach’s Six Cello Suites from obscurity, transforming them from pedagogical exercises into the cornerstone of the cello repertoire through decades of study, performance, and landmark recordings. His international career was defined by a moral stance as much as his artistry: following the victory of Francisco Franco’s Nationalist forces in the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939), Casals went into self-imposed exile, refusing to perform in any country recognizing the dictatorship. From exile in Prades, France, and later Ceiba, Puerto Rico, he founded major festivals and institutions, including the Puerto Rico Symphony Orchestra and Conservatory of Music, using his platform to advocate for peace, democracy, and Catalan identity until his death at age 96.

## Body

### Childhood and Musical Awakening
Casals was born into a musical family; his father, Carles Casals i Ribes, was a parish organist and choirmaster who gave him rigorous early training in piano, violin, organ, and theory. A child prodigy, Casals played violin publicly at age six. His path to the cello began at age 11 when he heard the instrument played by a traveling ensemble; his father built him a crude cello from a gourd. In 1888, his mother took him to Barcelona to enroll at the Escola Municipal de Música. The pivotal moment came in 1890 when, browsing a harbor music shop, he discovered a crumpled edition of Bach’s Six Cello Suites (published 1825 but largely neglected). He practiced them daily for 13 years before performing them publicly, eventually creating his own performing edition and recording them, establishing the works as the "preeminent work for the cello."

### Studies and Early Professional Recognition
In 1893, composer Isaac Albéniz heard Casals in a café trio and secured him a royal stipend from Queen Regent María Cristina to study composition at the Madrid Royal Conservatory with Víctor Mirecki. He played informal palace concerts and joined the Quartet Society. After a brief, impoverished stint in Paris (1895) playing in the Folies Marigny orchestra, he returned to Barcelona in 1896 to join the faculty of the Escola Municipal and serve as principal cellist at the Liceu opera house. In 1897, he debuted as soloist with the Madrid Symphony Orchestra and received the Order of Carlos III.

### International Career and the Cortot-Thibaud Trio
Casals’s international breakthrough came in 1899 with performances at London’s Crystal Palace and for Queen Victoria. He toured Spain, the Netherlands, the U.S. (1901/02), and South America (1903). Highlights included a 1904 White House concert for Theodore Roosevelt and a Carnegie Hall debut playing Richard Strauss’s *Don Quixote* conducted by the composer. In 1906, he formed a legendary piano trio with pianist Alfred Cortot and violinist Jacques Thibaud; they concertized until 1933 and recorded extensively (1926–1929). Casals also pursued conducting, founding the Pau Casals Orchestra in Barcelona in 1919, which he led until the Spanish Civil War halted its activities in 1936. As a composer, his notable works include *La Sardana* (1926) for cello ensemble and the oratorio *El Pessebre* (premiered 1960).

### Exile and the Prades Festivals
An ardent supporter of the Spanish Republic, Casals vowed never to return to Spain nor perform in countries recognizing Franco’s government after the Nationalist victory in 1939. He settled in Prades, France (historically Catalan), enduring Francoist press attacks and a fine of one million pesetas. He performed sporadically in unoccupied France and Switzerland (1939–1942). In 1950, he broke his silence to conduct and play at the Prades Festival, organized for the Bach bicentenary, on condition that proceeds funded a refugee hospital in Perpignan. The festival became an annual institution, producing landmark live recordings with artists like Rudolf Serkin, Isaac Stern, and Mieczysław Horszowski.

### Puerto Rico: Building Institutions
Casals visited Puerto Rico (his mother’s birthplace) extensively in 1955, moving there permanently in 1956 to Ceiba. He inaugurated the annual Casals Festival (1956), founded the Puerto Rico Symphony Orchestra (1958), and established the Conservatory of Music of Puerto Rico (1959), profoundly shaping the island’s classical music infrastructure. He continued teaching global master classes (Gstaad, Marlboro, Berkeley) into his 90s.

### Late Honors and Diplomatic Exception
In 1961, Casals made a notable exception to his boycott, performing at the White House for President Kennedy, whom he admired. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1963. His final major public act was conducting his *Hymn to the United Nations* at the UN in 1971, where Secretary-General U Thant awarded him the U.N. Peace Medal. In acceptance, Casals delivered his "I Am a Catalan" speech, asserting Catalonia’s historical parliamentary democracy. His final conducting engagement was with the Jerusalem Youth Orchestra in 1973, shortly before his death.

### Personal Life and Instruments
Casals married three times: Susan Metcalfe (1914, divorced 1957), Francesca Vidal i Puig (1955, died same year), and Marta Montañez y Martinez (1957, 60 years his junior). For most of his career he played a cello labeled "Carlo Tononi 1733," acquired in 1913; after 50 years it was identified as the work of Venetian luthier Matteo Goffriller (c. 1700). He also played a 1710 Goffriller and a 1730 Tononi.

## Terms
- **Cello Suites (Bach)**: Six suites for unaccompanied cello (BWV 1007–1012) composed c. 1717–1723, published 1825. Casals’s advocacy made them central to the cello canon.
- **Pau Casals Orchestra**: Founded 1919 in Barcelona; disbanded 1936 due to Civil War.
- **Cortot-Thibaud-Casals Trio**: Celebrated piano trio (1906–1933) with Alfred Cortot (piano) and Jacques Thibaud (violin).
- **Prades Festival**: Annual festival founded 1950 in Prades, France, commemorating Bach’s death bicentenary; Casals conducted/performed, proceeds to charity.
- **Goffriller Cello**: Instruments by Venetian luthier Matteo Goffriller (c. 1700, 1710); Casals’s primary concert instruments, misattributed to Tononi for decades.
- **Spanish Civil War (1936–1939)**: Conflict between Republican government and Nationalist forces under Franco; Casals supported Republic, prompting exile.
- **Francoist Spain**: Dictatorship of Francisco Franco (1939–1975); Casals boycotted nations recognizing it.
- **El Pessebre**: Casals’s oratorio (The Manger), premiered 1960 in Acapulco; performed at UN 1963.
- **Hymn to the United Nations**: Casals’s 1971 composition for UN; conducted at premiere, awarded U.N. Peace Medal.
- **Conservatory of Music of Puerto Rico**: Founded 1959 by Casals; major music education institution.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authenticity of Bach interpretation**: Casals’s performing edition of the Cello Suites involved significant editorial choices (bowings, dynamics, phrasing) reflecting late-Romantic taste. Scholars debate how closely his approach aligns with Baroque performance practice versus creating a new, valid modern tradition.
- **Political boycott consistency**: Casals refused to play in countries recognizing Franco, yet accepted Kennedy’s 1961 White House invitation (U.S. recognized Franco since 1950). Defenders cite personal admiration for Kennedy and the symbolic value of the gesture; critics note the inconsistency.
- **Cello attribution**: The "Tononi 1733" cello played for 50 years was re-attributed to Goffriller c. 1700. The exact timeline of this discovery and its impact on Casals’s sound concept remain discussed in organology.
- **Legacy of *El Pessebre***: While *La Sardana* and the Bach recordings are canonical, the oratorio *El Pessebre* (text by Joan Alavedra) has not entered the standard repertoire; its musical merit versus its symbolic/political function is debated.
