# Margaret Mead

## Overview
Margaret Mead (1901–1978) was the most visible American cultural anthropologist of the mid-twentieth century, translating ethnographic insights into public discourse on gender, sexuality, education, and national character. Trained by Franz Boas and Ruth Benedict at Columbia University, she pioneered participant-observation fieldwork in Samoa, Manus, the Omaha Reservation, New Guinea, and Bali, arguing that personality and social roles—especially gender and adolescence—are culturally constructed rather than biologically fixed. Her 1928 bestseller *Coming of Age in Samoa* framed adolescence as a cultural variable, influencing the 1960s sexual revolution, while *Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies* (1935) presaged the sex–gender distinction central to feminist theory. With Gregory Bateson, she created a massive photographic and film archive in Bali, founding visual anthropology. During World War II she applied anthropology to morale, food habits, and "culture at a distance" studies of enemy nations. Her later career spanned museum curation, university teaching, cybernetics conferences, and UN environmental forums. Posthumous critiques—most famously Derek Freeman’s challenge to her Samoan data—sparked enduring debates over fieldwork ethics, cultural relativism, and the politics of representation.

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Mead was the first of five children in an academic Quaker-adjacent family; her father taught finance at Wharton, her mother studied Italian immigrants sociologically. Frequent moves led to grandmother-directed early schooling. A brief year at DePauw University (1919) preceded transfer to Barnard, where she encountered Boas’s cultural relativism and Benedict’s configurationism. Boas steered her toward Samoa to test whether adolescent "storm and stress" was biological or cultural—a question framing her career.

### Fieldwork Methodology and Innovations
Mead’s core method was long-term participant observation focused on childhood, adolescence, sexuality, and kinship, producing "multivocal" accounts of women, men, girls, and boys. In Bali (1936–38) with Gregory Bateson, she pioneered systematic still and motion photography (25,000 photos, 20,000 feet of film), creating one of anthropology’s first visual archives and earning the label "mother of visual anthropology." Wartime constraints prompted "culture at a distance": analyzing films, literature, and interviews with émigrés to profile national characters (e.g., Russia, Japan) without field access. She later championed longitudinal restudies, returning to Manus (1953), Bali, and Samoa to document change.

### Samoa: *Coming of Age in Samoa* (1928) and the Freeman Controversy
On Taʻū, Mead found Samoan adolescence relatively untroubled: children ignored until ~15–16, casual premarital sexuality (including homosexual experimentation), marriage as socioeconomic alliance, and minimal sexual neuroses. She contrasted this with American "constrained, awkward" adolescence, arguing culture—not biology—shaped the experience. Derek Freeman (1983, 1998) countered that Mead was misled by informants, ignored missionary records and police data showing sexual violence and delinquency, and misrepresented Samoa as sexually liberated. While many anthropologists rejected Freeman’s harshest claims, the controversy damaged Mead’s public reputation and became a flashpoint for debates on cultural relativism, nature vs. nurture, and ethnographic authority.

### Manus: Longitudinal Study of Modernization
Mead’s 1928–29 work in Peri village produced *Growing Up in New Guinea* (1930) and a kinship monograph (1934). Her 1953 return captured dramatic transformation: Catholic conversion (~1930), Japanese then massive U.S. military occupation (1M+ troops), and the Paliau Movement—a millenarian "New Way" (Nufela Fasin) led by Paliau Moloat that rejected tradition, innovated Christianity, created village assemblies, and advocated Papuan independence. Mead interpreted the movement’s rational modernization ("democratic government, schools, clinic, universal suffrage") as genuine social change, counterpointed by "The Noise," a cargo-cult ecstatic frenzy promising effortless Western goods. Her somatotyping project (nude photography of villagers using Sheldon’s endomorph/mesomorph/ectomorph typology) later drew ethical criticism for lacking villager consent, though Mead defended it as "United Nations business" useful for development.

### Omaha: *The Changing Culture of an Indian Tribe* (1932)
With Reo Fortune among the Omaha (pseudonymized as "The Antlers"), Mead posed as a wife while covertly recording women’s lives, justifying deception as necessary for access and anonymizing subjects as ethical compensation. She documented cultural collapse under the Dawes Act’s land allotment: "Since 1920, conditions have been getting steadily worse… the land… is irretrievably lost and nothing remains in its place," labeling the Omaha "a broken culture."

### New Guinea: *Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies* (1935)
In the Sepik basin, Mead profiled three groups with divergent gender temperaments: Arapesh (both sexes "feminine"—passive, nurturing), Mundugumor (both "masculine"—aggressive, competitive), Tchambuli (sex-reversed: women dominant/managerial, men decorative/emotional). She concluded that traits Westerners deem "naturally" masculine or feminine are culturally assigned, presaging the sex–gender distinction. Each characterization drew later scholarly challenge: Fortune on Arapesh, Nancy McDowell on Mundugumor, Deborah Gewertz and Frederick Errington on Tchambuli.

### Bali: Visual Anthropology and Child Development
Seeking a cultural "base" beneath Hindu, Chinese, and Javanese layers, Mead and Bateson chose Bajoeng Gede (simple ritual, few literate elites) and studied Brahman and casteless painters in Batoean. Their collaborative photography—Mead noting, Bateson shooting—yielded seven films (e.g., *Trance and Dance in Bali*, *Karba’s First Years*) and two landmark photo-analyses: *Balinese Character* (1942, with Bateson) and *Growth and Culture* (1951, with Frances Cooke Macgregor), establishing photography as analytic tool, not just illustration.

### Applied Work: Race, National Character, and Policy
Mead early critiqued racial IQ testing (1926), identifying three flaws: invalid equation of test scores with "racial admixture," confounding of social status/environment with biology, and language barriers—arguments paralleling Stephen Jay Gould’s later *The Mismeasure of Man*. She persuaded the American Jewish Committee to study shtetl life via immigrant interviews, a project later linked to the "Jewish mother" stereotype. At RAND (1948–50) she analyzed Russian attitudes toward authority. As an Episcopalian, she helped draft the 1979 Book of Common Prayer. In 1948 she controversially endorsed deploying Iban (Dyak) mercenaries in Malaya, arguing their cultural headhunting made them no less moral than conscripts taught killing was wrong.

### Personal Life and Sexuality
Mead married three men: Luther Cressman (1923–28, "student marriage"), Reo Fortune (1928–35, marked by his "puritanical jealousy" and physical violence), Gregory Bateson (1936–50, "the husband she loved most," father of Mary Catherine). She had significant romantic/sexual relationships with women, notably Ruth Benedict (implied sexual by daughter and biographer Lois Banner) and Rhoda Métraux (cohabiting 1955–78, confirmed romantic by 2006 letters). Mead privately espoused free-love ideals (influenced by Havelock Ellis, Edward Carpenter), describing herself as a "mixed type" attracted to both sexes, yet never publicly identified as lesbian or bisexual. In a 1974 talk she hypothesized an ideal lifespan trajectory: youthful homosexuality, midlife heterosexuality, late-life homosexuality. Her pediatrician Benjamin Spock incorporated her demand-feeding observations into his child-rearing advice.

### Institutional Career and Public Intellectual Role
Curator at AMNH (1946–69), adjunct at Columbia (1954–78), founder of Fordham Lincoln Center anthropology department (1968–70), Distinguished Professor at URI (1970–). President of Society for Applied Anthropology (1950), AAA (1960), AAAS (1975). Key participant in Macy cybernetics conferences; her 1968 address catalyzed second-order cybernetics. Co-founded Glyphs Inc. (1960s) for universal graphic symbols. Wrote monthly Redbook column with Métraux (1960s), appeared in *Vogue*, *Mademoiselle*, *NYT*, on record albums, and at UN Habitat I (1976). Mentored Jean Houston, Gail Sheehy, Timothy Asch, Susan Scrimshaw, among others.

### Legacy and Honors
Annual Margaret Mead Film Festival at AMNH (since 1976, longest-running U.S. documentary showcase). National Women’s Hall of Fame (1976). Presidential Medal of Freedom (1979). Margaret Mead Award (jointly SAAp/AAA) for public anthropology communication. Schools named for her in Illinois, Washington, New York. Depicted in *Hair* (1967), Supersisters cards (1979), USPS Celebrate the Century stamp (1998), novel *Euphoria* (2014). Biographies by Howard (1984), Bateson (1984), King (2019), Breen (2023), Blum (2024), Shankman (2009, 2021), Lutkehaus (2008), Coffman (2024).

## Terms
- **Cultural relativism**: The principle that a culture’s beliefs and practices should be understood within its own context, not judged by external standards; central to Boasian anthropology and Mead’s work.
- **Participant observation**: Core ethnographic method involving extended residence in a community, participating in daily life while systematically recording data.
- **Culture at a distance**: Wartime methodology analyzing films, literature, and émigré interviews to infer national character without field access; developed by Mead, Benedict, Métraux.
- **Sex–gender distinction**: Analytical separation of biological sex from culturally shaped gender roles/temperaments; presaged by Mead’s *Sex and Temperament* (1935).
- **Visual anthropology**: Subfield using photography/film as primary research tools, not just illustration; founded by Mead and Bateson’s Bali archive.
- **Longitudinal restudy**: Returning to a field site decades later to document social change; Mead pioneered this in Manus, Bali, Samoa.
- **Somatotyping**: Classification of body types (endomorph, mesomorph, ectomorph) per Sheldon/Tanner; Mead applied it in Manus (1953–68), later criticized for consent issues.
- **National character studies**: Research profiling culturally patterned personality traits of entire societies (e.g., U.S., Russia, Japan); Mead led WWII-era efforts.
- **Paliau Movement (Nufela Fasin)**: Millenarian modernization movement in Manus (1940s–50s) led by Paliau Moloat, blending Christian innovation, village democracy, and independence advocacy.
- **Mead–Freeman controversy**: Posthumous debate (from 1983) over the accuracy of Mead’s Samoan fieldwork, implicating ethics, methodology, and cultural relativism.

## Debates and open questions
- **Samoan data validity**: Freeman’s claim that Mead was hoaxed by informants vs. defenders’ argument that Freeman misread cultural context and relied on later, missionized Samoa. Unresolved: whether Mead’s core contrast (Samoan vs. U.S. adolescence) holds.
- **Gender temperament generalizability**: Subsequent Sepik researchers (Fortune, McDowell, Gewertz/Errington) challenge Mead’s characterizations of Arapesh, Mundugumor, Tchambuli as oversimplified or inverted. Open: how much reflects Mead’s theoretical lens vs. local complexity.
- **Ethics of covert research**: Mead’s Omaha deception (posing as wife) and Manus somatotyping (nude photos without villager consent) raise enduring questions about informed consent, power dynamics, and retrospective judgment of mid-century practices.
- **Public anthropology trade-offs**: Mead’s media celebrity amplified anthropology’s reach but invited reductionism and political attacks (conservative backlash against cultural relativism/sexual revolution). Debate continues on the scholar’s role as public intellectual.
- **Nature vs. nurture framing**: Mead’s stark culture-over-biology stance (especially in *Coming of Age*) is now seen as overcorrected; modern biosocial approaches seek integration. Open: how to weigh her historical impact against empirical revisions.
