# Indo-Gangetic Plain

## Overview
The Indo-Gangetic Plain is a vast, fertile alluvial plain in South Asia, formed by the deposits of the Indus, Ganges, and Brahmaputra rivers. It is one of the world's most densely populated and intensely farmed regions, home to nearly one-seventh of the global population. Its rich soil and water resources have supported major civilizations and empires throughout history.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BCE** — The region was home to the Indus Valley Civilisation.
- **c. 1500–600 BCE** — During the Vedic period, the region was referred to as "Aryavarta".
- **4th–2nd century BCE** — The Maurya Empire, with its center in the plain, unified most of the Indian subcontinent.
- **1st century CE** — The Kushan Empire expanded into the northwest of the subcontinent.
- **4th–7th century CE** — The Gupta period, noted for its arts and science, flourished.
- **1191 CE** — Rajput king Prithviraj Chauhan defeated Shihabuddin Ghori in the First Battle of Tarain.
- **12th century CE** — Shihabuddin's victory in the Second Battle of Tarain led to the rise of the Delhi Sultanate.
- **1526 CE** — Babur established the Mughal Empire, which ruled for nearly three centuries.
- **1757 & 1764** — The English East India Company's victories at Plassey and Buxar consolidated its power in the lower Gangetic plain.
- **1947** — The region was divided between India and Pakistan upon independence from British rule.

## Body

### Formation and Geology
The plain is the world's largest expanse of uninterrupted alluvium, formed by continuous silt deposition from the Indus, Ganges, and Brahmaputra rivers into a depression between the Himalayas and the Deccan Plateau. The origin of this depression is debated. Theories include a pre-existing furrow (Wadia), a large syncline (Suess), or a deep rift valley (Burrard), though the rift theory is rejected by geologists like Hayden and Oldham due to lack of evidence. Recent research suggests the trough formed from folded Tethys Sea sediments due to the northward drift of the Indian plate and the subsequent rise of the Himalayas.

### Geography and Sub-divisions
The plain stretches from the Himalayas to the northern edge of the Deccan Plateau and is divided by the Delhi Ridge into the Indus drainage basin (west) and the Ganga-Brahmaputra basin (east). It is characterized by four distinct longitudinal belts: the porous, rocky **Bhabar** at the Himalayan foothills; the wet, silt-rich **Terai**; the fertile, older alluvium of the **Bangar**; and the low-lying, new silt deposits of the **Khadir**. The plain is further sub-divided into regional units including the Sindh Plains, Rajasthan Plain (containing the Thar Desert), Punjab-Haryana Plain, the extensive Ganga Plain, the Brahmaputra Valley, and the world's largest river delta, the Ganges-Brahmaputra Delta.

### Climate, Agriculture, and Population
Rainfall varies significantly, from over 130 cm annually in the north to less than 25 cm in the arid west. The wetter east and north support rice cultivation, while the drier west grows wheat, barley, and millet. The region's fertile soil and abundant water make it intensely farmed and one of the most densely populated areas on Earth. The Ganges-Brahmaputra Delta alone has over 130 million inhabitants.

## Terms
- **Alluvium**: Soil, clay, or other material carried and deposited by flowing water.
- **Syncline**: A fold in rock strata with younger layers closer to the center of the structure.
- **Rift Valley**: A lowland region formed by the sinking of the Earth's crust between two faults.
- **Tethys Sea**: A prehistoric sea that existed between the ancient continents of Gondwana and Laurasia.
- **Bhabar**: The first, porous belt of the Indo-Gangetic Plain at the Himalayan foothills, where streams often disappear underground.
- **Terai**: The wet, marshy belt south of the Bhabar, characterized by thick vegetation.
- **Bangar**: The belt of older, fertile alluvium forming the main floodplain.
- **Khadir**: The belt of new, low-lying silt deposits subject to seasonal flooding.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary scholarly debate concerns the geological origin of the depression that the plains fill. The main competing theories are the "furrow" hypothesis, the "syncline" hypothesis, and the now largely rejected "rift valley" hypothesis. The current consensus leans towards a trough formed by tectonic activity related to the Himalayan orogeny.
