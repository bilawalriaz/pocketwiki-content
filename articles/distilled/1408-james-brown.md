# James Brown

## Overview
James Joseph Brown (1933–2006) was an American singer, songwriter, dancer, and bandleader widely recognized as the central progenitor of funk music and a pivotal figure in 20th-century popular music. Rising from extreme poverty and a juvenile detention sentence, Brown revolutionized rhythm and blues in the mid-1960s by stripping harmony down to a single chord ("The One") and emphasizing interlocking, syncopated rhythms played by horns, guitar, bass, and drums. This innovation birthed funk, directly influencing the development of hip-hop—making him the world's most sampled recording artist—and shaping genres from disco to house. A relentless touring performer known as "The Hardest-Working Man in Show Business," Brown maintained strict discipline over his large revue, scored 17 Billboard R&B No. 1 singles, and leveraged his fame for education advocacy and complex political engagement, though his legacy is shadowed by documented domestic violence, drug abuse, and legal troubles.

## Body

### Early Life and Musical Origins
Born in a wooden shack in Barnwell, South Carolina, to teenage parents, Brown moved to Augusta, Georgia, at age four or five. His mother left after an abusive marriage; Brown lived in an aunt's brothel and later a shared house. He worked shining shoes and dancing for troops at Camp Gordon, where he first heard Howlin' Wolf. Inspired by Louis Jordan's "Caldonia," he learned piano, guitar, and harmonica. At 16, a robbery conviction sent him to a Toccoa juvenile detention center. There, he formed a gospel quartet and earned the nickname "Music Box." A baseball game led to meeting Bobby Byrd, whose family helped secure Brown's early parole in 1952 on a work sponsorship with S. C. Lawson. Brown joined Byrd's sister's gospel group, the Ever-Ready Gospel Singers, before joining Byrd's evolving R&B group in 1954.

### The Famous Flames and Early Hits (1954–1961)
Byrd's group, the Gospel Starlighters, transitioned through names (Avons, Toccoa Band, Flames) before becoming the Famous Flames. Influenced by groups like the Midnighters and the Dominoes, they auditioned for Little Richard's manager, Clint Brantley, in 1955. Richard wrote "Please, Please, Please" on a napkin; Brown turned it into a song. Signed to King Records' Federal subsidiary, the 1956 re-release sold over a million copies. After follow-ups failed, Brown hired Ben Bart (Universal Attractions) as manager in 1957; Bart renamed the act "James Brown and The Famous Flames," causing the original Flames to quit. In 1958, "Try Me" hit No. 1 R&B. Brown recruited his first backing band (led by J.C. Davis) and a new Flames lineup (Byrd, Stallworth, Bennett, Terry). They debuted at the Apollo in 1959. By 1960, Brown managed distinct entities: the vocal group (Famous Flames) and the instrumental band (James Brown Band/Orchestra). The band's instrumental "(Do the) Mashed Potatoes" (billed as Nat Kendrick & the Swans) hit top ten R&B, moving Brown's contract to the parent King label for better support.

### Mr. Dynamite: Live at the Apollo and Crossover (1962–1966)
Brown's cover of "Night Train" (1962) hit top five R&B. Defying King president Syd Nathan's skepticism, Brown financed a live Apollo recording in October 1962. *Live at the Apollo* (June 1963) became a landmark, hitting No. 2 on the pop album chart and staying 14 months. He launched Try Me Records (signing Tammi Terrell) and scored a top-20 pop hit with "Prisoner of Love." In 1964, a production deal with Smash Records (Fair Deal) prompted a King injunction halting releases. Before the injunction, "Out of Sight" signaled a rhythmic shift. His *T.A.M.I. Show* performance—featuring gospel vocals, choreography, and explosive dancing—famously upstaged the Rolling Stones. Back at King in 1965, "Papa's Got a Brand New Bag" won a Grammy and cracked the pop top ten. "I Got You (I Feel Good)" and "It's a Man's Man's Man's World" followed, cementing his crossover dominance.

### Soul Brother No. 1: The Invention of Funk (1967–1970)
By 1967, Brown's sound coalesced into funk. "Cold Sweat" (1967) is often cited as the first true funk record: it hit No. 1 R&B, featured a drum break, and reduced harmony to a single chord. Tracks like "Give It Up or Turnit a Loose" (1968), "Licking Stick" (1968), and "Funky Drummer" (1969) developed intricate, interlocking riffs—strutting bass, syncopated drums, percussive guitar. Brown's vocals shifted to rhythmic declamation (a precursor to rapping). Guitarist Jimmy Nolen's ostinatos on "Ain't It Funky" and "Give It Up or Turnit a Loose" stripped New Orleans funk to rhythmic essence, treating guitar like an African idiophone; Alexander Stewart notes this feel passed from New Orleans through Brown to 1970s popular music and hip-hop sampling. The band, led by trumpeter Lewis Hamlin and saxophonist/keyboardist Alfred "Pee Wee" Ellis, included Fred Wesley, drummers Jabo Starks and Clyde Stubblefield, Maceo Parker, and Bootsy Collins (later). Brown bought radio stations (WRDW Augusta, WJBE Knoxville, WEBB Baltimore), expanded his empire, and recorded with outside orchestras (Dee Felice Trio, Louie Bellson) for adult contemporary appeal, while the Dapps backed funk tracks like "I Can't Stand Myself." He earned the nickname "Soul Brother No. 1" after losing a "King of Soul" contest to Solomon Burke. His influence spread to Sly Stone, Funkadelic, Michael Jackson, and countless others.

### Godfather of Soul: The J.B.'s and Political Turbulence (1970–1974)
In March 1970, Brown's 1960s band quit over money (the Famous Flames had disbanded in 1968 for the same reason). Bobby Byrd stayed. Brown recruited the Pacemakers—bassist Bootsy Collins, guitarist Phelps "Catfish" Collins—augmented by Fred Wesley and others, forming the J.B.'s. Their first session yielded "Get Up (I Feel Like Being a) Sex Machine," built on "The One" (heavy downbeat emphasis). This lineup dissolved after a 1971 European tour due to money disputes and Bootsy's LSD use; a new J.B.'s formed around Wesley, St. Clair Pinckney, and John Starks. Brown signed with Polydor (via Starday-King), launching the People label for sidemen (Fred Wesley, Bobby Byrd, Lyn Collins, Hank Ballard). His public endorsement of Nixon in 1972 triggered a Black-organization boycott, hurting sales; he failed to hit No. 1 R&B in 1973. The IRS claimed $4.5M in back taxes (previously $2M). He scored the film *Black Caesar* (1973) and returned to No. 1 R&B in 1974 with "The Payback," "My Thang," and "Papa Don't Take No Mess" (his final R&B chart-topper).

### Later Career: Decline, Resurgence, and Final Years (1975–2006)
Disco's slicker sound eclipsed Brown's raw funk; post-1976, only "Bodyheat" (1976) and "It's Too Funky in Here" (1979) reached the R&B Top 15. He left Polydor in 1981, renaming his band the Soul Generals. Residency shows at LA's Reseda Country Club (1982) revived his profile. Film roles followed: *The Blues Brothers* (1980), *Doctor Detroit* (1983), *Rocky IV* (1985), *Miami Vice* (1987). He teamed with Afrika Bambaataa on "Unity" (1984) and signed with Scotti Brothers for *Gravity* (1986), yielding the Top 10 pop hit "Living in America" (Grammy winner, 1987). *I'm Real* (1988, with Full Force) spawned final R&B Top 10 hits "I'm Real" and "Static." The "Funky Drummer" break became hip-hop's "national anthem" (Kurtis Blow). After prison (1988–91), Brown released *Love Over-Due* (1991), starred in a pay-per-view special, and saw his catalog reissued (*Star Time* box set). Final studio albums *I'm Back* (1998) and *The Next Step* (2002) had minimal chart impact. He toured relentlessly ("Seven Decades of Funk World Tour"), performing at Super Bowl XXXI (1997), Live 8 (2005), and the BBC Electric Proms (Oct 2006). His last US show was August 20, 2006, in San Francisco; he died December 25, 2006.

### Artistry: Vocal Style, Concert Production, and Band Leadership
**Vocal Style:** Brown shouted with gospel force; his "rhythmic grunts and expressive shrieks" (Encyclopedia of African-American Culture and History) harked back to ring shouts and field cries, reimporting rhythmic complexity lost to pop pressures.
**Concert Style:** Shows were extravagant, 330+ one-nighters yearly. An MC delivered a litany of nicknames and hits (captured on *Live at the Apollo*). Brown danced vigorously (Mashed Potato, splits, slides); the Flames/Revue performed choreography. Performers wore tuxedos/cummerbunds; Brown wore processed hair and capes. A signature routine during "Please, Please, Please": Brown feigned exhaustion, dropped to knees; MC Danny Ray draped a cape and escorted him off; Brown returned for an encore. Inspired by wrestler Gorgeous George and Little Richard.
**Band Leadership:** Brown demanded military discipline—uniforms (bought by Brown), punctuality, precision. He fined members for unshined shoes, missed notes, or dancing out of sync, signaling fines via hand signals while dancing with his back to the audience. He could sabotage opening acts by cutting sets or sitting in on drums. Musicians (Maceo Parker, Fred Wesley) described a high-pressure, high-standard environment blending R&B drive with jazz precision.

### Social Activism: Education, Civil Rights, and Politics
**Education:** Influenced by dropping out in seventh grade for "insufficient clothes," Brown released "Don't Be a Drop-Out" (1966), donating royalties to prevention programs. He met LBJ at the White House. He spoke in schools lifelong; his will directed estate funds to the I Feel Good, Inc. Trust for disadvantaged children and scholarships. Final single: "Killing Is Out, School Is In." He gave toys/turkeys to children in Augusta and Atlanta.
**Civil Rights & Self-Reliance:** Initially cautious about alienating crossover audiences, Brown recorded "America Is My Home" (1968), a pro-patriotism rap. After MLK's assassination (April 4, 1968), Brown televised a free Boston Garden concert (April 5), credited with preventing riots. Mayor Kevin White broadcast it on WGBH; Brown demanded $60k "gate fees," eventually receiving $15k from "The Vault" group. Pressured by activists (H. Rap Brown) and inner-city violence, Brown wrote "Say It Loud – I'm Black and I'm Proud" (1968), composed with Pee Wee Ellis. It became an anthem but cost him crossover audience; he later regretted defining by race ("teach race is to teach separatism"), though acknowledged it taught pride. Follow-ups: "World," "I Don't Want Nobody to Give Me Nothing" (self-reliance), "Get Up, Get into It, Get Involved," "Talkin' Loud and Sayin' Nothing" (1970). He toured Africa (1971), named freeman of Lagos. James Brown Enterprises provided Black jobs. Later commentary: "King Heroin," "Public Enemy" (drug addiction).
**Politics:** Endorsed Humphrey (1968), labeled "Uncle Tom." Switched to Nixon after 1969 inaugural ball; 1972 endorsement sparked boycotts ("Nixon's Clown"). Bailed on 1973 Youth Concert. Later reversed on Nixon ("You Can Have Watergate"), then "Funky President" (1974) after Ford took over. Supported Carter (1977 inaugural), Reagan (1984 reelection). Claimed neither party; cited JFK and Strom Thurmond as heroes (1999). 2003 fundraiser for National Republican Senatorial Committee. Mentored Rev. Al Sharpton (tour manager 1973–80); met Reagan with Sharpton (1981) for MLK Day.

### Personal Life: Marriages, Children, and Substance Abuse
**Relationships & Abuse:** Involved with Tammi Terrell (17) in 1962; Bobby Bennett witnessed severe beatings. Terrell fled. Bennett also alleged Brown kicked a pregnant girlfriend down stairs.
**Marriages:** (1) Velma Warren (1953–1969), one son; remained friends. (2) Deidre "Deedee" Jenkins (1970–1981), two daughters; separated 1974 after alleged years of abuse. (3) Adrienne Lois Rodriguez (1984–1996), contentious; domestic abuse complaints; Rodriguez filed for divorce 1988, died 1996.
**Tomi Rae Hynie:** Hired as backing singer; wedding ceremony Dec 23, 2002. Validity disputed: Hynie was married to Javed Ahmed (annulled April 2004). Brown sought annulment. 2015 lower court ruled Hynie legal widow (Ahmed's marriage bigamous); 2020 SC Supreme Court ruled she was not legally married, no estate rights. Family settled estate battle 2021.
**Children:** Acknowledged 11 (5 sons, 6 daughters), 8 grandchildren, 4 great-grandchildren. Eldest son Teddy died 1973 (car crash). DNA tests (2007) indicated ≥3 extra-marital children (LaRhonda Pettit, Michael Deon Brown). James Brown Jr. (b. 2001) confirmed biological son.
**Drug Abuse:** Strict drug-free policy for entourage (fired Famous Flames for alcohol; fired Bootsy/Catfish Collins for LSD in 1971). Mid-1970s: aide Bob Patton alleged shared PCP-laced joint. Mid-1980s: Vicki Anderson confirmed regular PCP ("angel dust") use pre-1982. Rodriguez used PCP with Brown. Resulted in violent outbursts, domestic violence arrests. 1988: four criminal charges in 12 months (driving, PCP, guns). CNN *Sonya Live* interview (April 1988) showed erratic behavior. Former mistress alleged PCP and cocaine (in Kool cigarettes), off-label sildenafil, paranoia (trees as surveillance). 1998: week in rehab for prescription drugs; arrested week after for handgun/cannabis. 2006: cocaine traces in urine at hospital admission.

### Legal Issues
**Early/Assault:** 16: theft conviction (3 years juvenile). 1963: Macon club shootout with Joe Tex (shotguns used, 7 shot per Johnny Jenkins); Brown on parole, Clint Brantley "made it disappear" with cash; never charged. 1978: arrested for failing to turn in radio station records after bankruptcy.
**1988 Spree:** April 3 (assault), May (drugs/weapons), Sept 24 (high-speed chase I-20 GA/SC border). Convicted: unlicensed pistol, assaulting officer, drug/driving offenses. Sentenced 6 years. Served 2.5; paroled Feb 27, 1991. FBI file (2007) claims police shot at car, harassed/assaulted him; authorities found no merit.
**Civil Suits (1990s):** Mary Simons (1998): alleged 3-day captivity, sexual demands, gunfire; dismissed. Lisa Rushton (background singer): alleged sexual demands, pay cuts, groping; withdrew. Lisa Agbalaya: alleged harassment (bull testicles comment, zebra underwear, oil massage); jury cleared harassment, liable for wrongful termination.
**Domestic Violence:** Four arrests 1987–95 vs. Adrienne Rodriguez (iron pipe, shooting at car). Charges dropped after her 1996 death. Jan 2004: arrested for pushing Tomi Rae Hynie; pleaded no contest, forfeited $1,087 bond.
**Rape Accusation:** Jacque Hollander (2005 suit, alleged 1988 rape in SC van at shotgun point). Dismissed 2002 (statute of limitations); DNA/polygraph not considered.

### Death, Memorials, and Estate Battle
Brown lived in Beech Island, SC (Type 2 diabetes, prostate cancer treated 2004). Dec 23, 2006: dentist noted ill health; sent to Emory Crawford Long Hospital. Canceled concerts but expected release for NYE shows
