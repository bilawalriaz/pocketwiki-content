# Babylonia

## Overview
Babylonia was an ancient Akkadian-speaking state and cultural region in central-southern Mesopotamia (modern Iraq), centered on the city of Babylon. It was a major power in the ancient Near East, rivaling Assyria and Elam, and is renowned for its contributions to law, astronomy, mathematics, and literature. Its history spans from its emergence as a minor Amorite-ruled state c. 1894 BC to its absorption into the Persian Achaemenid Empire in 539 BC.

## Timeline
- **c. 1894 BC** — Amorite chieftain Sumu-abum founds the First Babylonian Dynasty, establishing Babylon as a small state.
- **c. 1792–1750 BC** — Hammurabi conquers southern Mesopotamia, creates a short-lived empire, and compiles his famous law code.
- **1595 BC** — Hittite king Mursili I sacks Babylon, ending the First Dynasty and enabling the Kassite takeover.
- **1595–1155 BC** — Kassite dynasty rules Babylonia for 576 years, the longest dynasty in its history.
- **1155–1026 BC** — Native Akkadian-speaking Dynasty of Isin rules, including Nebuchadnezzar I who recovers the statue of Marduk from Elam.
- **911–619 BC** — Babylonia is repeatedly dominated by the Neo-Assyrian Empire.
- **626–539 BC** — Neo-Babylonian (Chaldean) Empire founded by Nabopolassar; reaches zenith under Nebuchadnezzar II.
- **539 BC** — Cyrus the Great of Persia conquers Babylon, incorporating Babylonia into the Achaemenid Empire.

## Body

### Origins and the First Dynasty
Babylonia emerged c. 1894 BC as an Amorite-ruled state in a region previously dominated by Sumerian and Akkadian city-states. It remained minor until the reign of Hammurabi (c. 1792–1750 BC), who conquered southern Mesopotamia, expelled the Elamites, and defeated the Old Assyrian Empire. Hammurabi's reign established Babylon as a major political and religious center, transferring the supreme deity status from Enlil of Nippur to Marduk of Babylon. His law code, the Code of Hammurabi, is a foundational legal document. After his death, the empire rapidly disintegrated, losing the south to the Sealand Dynasty and northern territories to a resurgent Assyria.

### Kassite and Isin Dynasties
The Hittite sack of Babylon in 1595 BC led to the establishment of the Kassite dynasty, which ruled for 576 years. The Kassites, a non-Semitic people from the Zagros Mountains, renamed the country Karduniaš. Their rule was generally weak, with Babylonia frequently under Assyrian and Elamite domination. The dynasty ended when Babylon was sacked by the Elamites in 1155 BC. Following this, the native Dynasty of Isin (1155–1026 BC) briefly restored Babylonian power, notably under Nebuchadnezzar I, but the region descended into chaos due to famine and Aramean migrations.

### Assyrian Domination and the Neo-Babylonian Empire
From 911 BC, Babylonia was repeatedly conquered by the Neo-Assyrian Empire. A brief period of Chaldean rule was followed by direct Assyrian control. Babylonian revolts, culminating in a major rebellion under the Assyrian prince Shamash-shum-ukin, were brutally suppressed. Assyrian internal civil wars in the 7th century BC allowed a Chaldean leader, Nabopolassar, to found the Neo-Babylonian Empire in 626 BC. Allied with the Medes, he destroyed Assyria. His son, Nebuchadnezzar II (605–562 BC), expanded the empire, crushing revolts in the Levant (including Judah) and defeating Egypt. The empire declined after his death, and in 539 BC, Cyrus the Great of Persia conquered Babylon without a major battle, ending native Mesopotamian rule.

### Culture and Legacy
Babylonian culture was a continuation of earlier Sumero-Akkadian traditions. The Babylonians made profound advances in astronomy, developing mathematical models to predict celestial events, and in mathematics, using a sexagesimal (base-60) system that gave us the 60-minute hour and 360-degree circle. Their medical texts, like the Diagnostic Handbook, introduced concepts of diagnosis and prognosis. Literature included the Epic of Gilgamesh. Babylon's legacy in Abrahamic religions is as a symbol of decadent power, referenced in the Bible as both a historical entity and an allegorical "Whore of Babylon."

## Terms
- **Akkadian**: An East Semitic language that became the lingua franca of Mesopotamia, used for official and literary purposes long after it ceased to be a spoken vernacular.
- **Amorites**: A Northwest Semitic-speaking people who migrated into Mesopotamia and founded the First Dynasty of Babylon.
- **Kassites**: A people of uncertain linguistic affiliation who ruled Babylonia for over 500 years after the Hittite sack.
- **Chaldeans**: A Northwest Semitic-speaking tribal group that settled in southeastern Babylonia and later founded the Neo-Babylonian Empire.
- **Marduk**: The patron god of Babylon, elevated to the head of the Mesopotamian pantheon by Hammurabi.
- **Code of Hammurabi**: One of the earliest and most complete written legal codes, proclaimed by the Babylonian king Hammurabi.
- **Sexagesimal**: A numeral system with a base of 60, developed by the Babylonians and still used in measuring time and angles.
- **Cuneiform**: The wedge-shaped writing system used in ancient Mesopotamia, written on clay tablets.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise chronology of the early second millennium BC is debated, with the date of the Hittite sack of Babylon (1595 BC in the middle chronology) varying by over 200 years across different chronological systems.
- The ethnic and linguistic affiliation of the Kassites remains unclear due to a scarcity of texts in their language.
- The extent to which Babylonian philosophy influenced early Greek philosophy, such as the works of Thales and Plato, is a subject of scholarly discussion.
