# Ibn Arabi

## Overview
Ibn 'Arabī (1165–1240) was an influential Andalusian-born Sunni Muslim scholar, Sufi mystic, and philosopher. He is a foundational figure in Islamic mysticism, best known for systematizing the doctrine of *wahdat al-wujūd* (unity of existence), which became a dominant intellectual framework across the Muslim world. His prolific writings, especially *The Meccan Illuminations* and *The Bezels of Wisdom*, synthesized philosophy, theology, and spiritual practice, shaping Sufi thought for centuries.

## Timeline
- **1165** — Born in Murcia, al-Andalus (present-day Spain).
- **c. 1180** — Experiences a first mystical vision (*fanā*).
- **1193** — Leaves Andalusia for the first time, travels to Tunis.
- **1200** — Leaves al-Andalus permanently after a vision instructs him to journey east.
- **1202** — Arrives in Mecca for the Hajj; begins writing *Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*.
- **1204–1205** — Travels through Syria, Palestine, and Iraq; meets disciples of ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlānī in Baghdad.
- **1206** — Visits Jerusalem, Mecca, and Egypt.
- **1223** — Settles in Damascus, his final home.
- **1229** — Composes *Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam* in Damascus.
- **1231** — Completes the first draft of *Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*.
- **1238** — Finalizes the second, more widely circulated draft of *Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*.
- **1240** — Dies in Damascus and is buried on Qasiyun Hill.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Ibn 'Arabī was born into a mixed Arab-Berber family in Murcia. His father served in the army of the local ruler, and after Murcia fell to the Almohad Caliphate in 1172, the family relocated to Seville. He received a traditional education in Islamic sciences and military training. As a teenager, he had a transformative mystical vision, which led him to adopt Sufism (*Tasawwuf*). He studied under numerous prominent scholars of his time, including the philosopher Ibn Rushd (Averroes), and later listed nearly seventy teachers who influenced his spiritual development.

### Travels and Major Works
After his parents' deaths, Ibn 'Arabī began extensive travels across the Islamic world. He left al-Andalus for the last time in 1200, following a vision to journey east. He performed the Hajj in 1202 and lived in Mecca for three years, where he began his magnum opus, *Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya* (The Meccan Illuminations). He subsequently traveled through Syria, Iraq, Anatolia, and Egypt, meeting scholars and mystics. In 1223, he settled in Damascus, where he completed *Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya* and composed *Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam* (The Bezels of Wisdom), a summary of his teachings on the role of prophets in divine revelation.

### Core Teachings
Ibn 'Arabī is credited with explicitly formulating the doctrine of *wahdat al-wujūd* (unity of existence), a monist philosophy holding that all things are manifestations of a single, absolute reality, which he called "the Absolute" (*al-wujūd al-muṭlaq*). He distinguished between three types of knowledge: rational knowledge (fallible), experiential knowledge (*dhawq*), and mystical knowledge granted to prophets. He developed the concept of *al-Insān al-Kāmil* (the Perfect Human), using the metaphor of a mirror to explain that humans reflect the divine essence. He regarded the Prophet Muhammad as the primary exemplar of this perfect human, the clearest proof of God.

### Legacy and Reception
Ibn 'Arabī's teachings spread rapidly after his death, influencing Sufi orders and popular poetry across the Muslim world through translations into Persian, Turkish, and Urdu. His legacy is complex; he is revered by many as *Shaykh al-Akbar* (The Greatest Shaykh) and a *wali* (saint), but his monist views also sparked significant controversy. His work was praised by some contemporaries as orthodox Ash'ari theology, while others, like the scholar Ibn ʿAbd al-Salam, reportedly condemned him. This debate over his orthodoxy and the interpretation of his writings has continued for centuries.

## Terms
- **Wahdat al-wujūd**: The doctrine of "unity of existence," a monist philosophy asserting that all reality is a manifestation of a single, absolute Being.
- **Sufism (Tasawwuf)**: The mystical dimension of Islam, focused on the inner, spiritual path to God.
- **Al-Insān al-Kāmil**: The "Perfect Human," a concept describing the ideal human being who fully embodies divine attributes and serves as a mirror reflecting God.
- **Fanā**: A state of mystical annihilation or ego-death, where the individual self is absorbed into the divine reality.
- **Dhawq**: "Taste" or experiential knowledge, a form of direct, intuitive understanding that cannot be attained through reason alone.
- **Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya**: "The Meccan Illuminations," Ibn 'Arabī's magnum opus, a vast encyclopedic work on mystical philosophy, Sufi practice, and visions.
- **Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam**: "The Bezels of Wisdom," a key work summarizing his teachings on the spiritual wisdom of the prophets.
- **Wali**: A Muslim saint or friend of God, recognized for their high spiritual rank.
- **Madhhab**: A school of Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh).

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Orthodoxy vs. Heresy**: A central debate concerns whether Ibn 'Arabī's teachings, particularly *wahdat al-wujūd*, are compatible with Sunni orthodoxy. Scholars and jurists have been divided since his death, with some declaring him a heretic and others a supreme saint.
- **Jurisprudential Allegiance**: There is scholarly debate over which school of Islamic law (*madhhab*) he followed. While he stated he did not blindly follow any one school, some argue he adhered to the Zahiri or Hanbali schools, while others contend he was independent.
- **Authenticity of Works**: Of the 850 works attributed to him, only about 700 are considered authentic, and the attribution of some major works, like *Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam*, has been questioned by critics who label them forgeries.
- **The Ibn ʿAbd al-Salam Controversy**: Historical accounts conflict on whether the respected scholar Ibn ʿAbd al-Salam secretly admired Ibn 'Arabī or publicly condemned him as "a master of evil," highlighting the deep divisions his work caused among contemporaries.
