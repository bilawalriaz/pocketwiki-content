# Nanjing Massacre

## Overview
The Nanjing Massacre (December 1937–March 1938) was a mass atrocity committed by the Imperial Japanese Army after capturing the Chinese capital of Nanjing during the Second Sino-Japanese War. It involved the widespread murder of civilians and prisoners of war, mass rape, and destruction, and is considered one of the worst wartime atrocities in history. The event remains a major point of contention in Sino-Japanese relations due to ongoing historical revisionism and denial in Japan.

## Timeline
- **Jul 7, 1937** — Second Sino-Japanese War begins after the Marco Polo Bridge Incident.
- **Aug 1937** — Japanese invasion of Shanghai begins.
- **Nov 1937** — Japanese forces capture Shanghai and advance toward Nanjing.
- **Dec 1, 1937** — Chinese government relocates from Nanjing.
- **Dec 5, 1937** — Prince Yasuhiko Asaka arrives at the front and issues an order to "kill all captives."
- **Dec 12, 1937** — Chinese General Tang Shengzhi orders a retreat from Nanjing.
- **Dec 13, 1937** — Japanese troops enter Nanjing; the massacre begins.
- **Dec 18, 1937** — General Iwane Matsui expresses dismay at the atrocities.
- **Late Jan 1938** — Japanese force refugees from the Safety Zone to return home.
- **Mar 28, 1938** — Japanese establish a collaborationist government in Nanjing; major atrocities subside.
- **Nov 12, 1948** — General Matsui and others are sentenced to death for war crimes.

## Body

### Military Context and Advance
The Second Sino-Japanese War escalated after the Battle of Shanghai (Aug–Nov 1937). Despite initial Japanese plans to avoid expanding the conflict, General Iwane Matsui insisted on capturing Nanjing, believing it would force China's surrender. As Japanese forces advanced 170 miles from Shanghai to Nanjing, they committed widespread atrocities in a terror campaign, including massacres of villages and killing contests. The Japanese military had contingency plans to use poison gas and incendiary bombs if the battle for Nanjing became too intense.

### The Massacre
After the fall of Nanjing on December 13, 1937, Japanese troops engaged in organized and spontaneous violence for weeks. "Mopping-up operations" targeted Chinese men of military age, leading to mass executions of prisoners of war and civilians falsely identified as soldiers. Notable massacres occurred at Mufushan and Straw String Gorge. Rape was systematic, with estimates ranging from 20,000 to 80,000 cases; victims were often killed afterward. Looting and arson destroyed about one-third of the city. While a Nanking Safety Zone, led by German businessman John Rabe, saved an estimated 200,000 lives, Japanese troops still entered it to commit crimes.

### Causes and Responsibility
The violence stemmed from multiple factors: ultranationalist and racist ideologies that dehumanized Chinese people, a brutal military hierarchy that socialized soldiers to cruelty, a breakdown in discipline, and a desire for revenge after the costly Battle of Shanghai. Prince Asaka's order to "kill all captives" provided official sanction. General Matsui, the overall commander, later expressed regret but took no effective action to stop the atrocities during the event.

### Aftermath and Legacy
Post-war tribunals, including the International Military Tribunal for the Far East (IMTFE), convicted General Matsui and others of war crimes; Matsui was executed. Prince Asaka was granted immunity as a member of the imperial family. The death toll is debated: the IMTFE estimated over 200,000 killed, while modern scholarly estimates range from 100,000 to 200,000. The massacre is a central issue in Sino-Japanese relations, with Japanese nationalists and some officials denying or minimizing it, while China commemorates it as a national tragedy.

## Terms
- **Second Sino-Japanese War**: The full-scale war between China and Japan that began in 1937 and merged into World War II.
- **International Military Tribunal for the Far East (IMTFE)**: The post-war Allied tribunal that tried Japanese war criminals, including those responsible for the Nanjing Massacre.
- **Nanking Safety Zone**: A demilitarized area in Nanjing established by Western expatriates to shelter civilians during the massacre.
- **"Kill all captives" order**: An order issued by Prince Asaka that sanctioned the execution of prisoners of war.
- **Mopping-up operations**: A euphemism used by the Japanese military for the systematic search and execution of former Chinese soldiers and suspected combatants.
- **Class-A war criminals**: Individuals charged with crimes against peace, such as planning and waging aggressive war, as defined by the IMTFE.
- **Yasukuni Shrine**: A Japanese shrine that honors war dead, including convicted war criminals, whose visits by politicians cause diplomatic tension.
- **Patriotic Education Campaign**: A Chinese government initiative from the 1990s that emphasized national humiliation, including the Nanjing Massacre, to foster patriotism.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Death Toll**: Estimates vary widely. The IMTFE cited over 200,000; Chinese government figures often cite 300,000; many modern historians support a range of 100,000–200,000. The 300,000 figure is seen by some as a symbolic representation of suffering rather than a literal count.
- **Duration and Scope**: Traditional historiography defines the massacre as six weeks within Nanjing city. Some scholars argue it began earlier (mid-November 1937) and lasted until late March 1938, encompassing surrounding counties.
- **Historicity of the Killing Contest**: The reported sword contest between officers Mukai and Noda is disputed. A 2005 Tokyo court ruling acknowledged the race occurred, but some scholars argue the story was fabricated for propaganda.
- **Genocide Classification**: Scholars debate whether the massacre constitutes genocide. Some argue the systematic killing of civilians and cultural groups meets the definition, while others contend it was a war crime without a centrally directed genocidal policy.
- **Japanese Denial and Textbooks**: Significant controversy exists in Japan over how the massacre is taught. Revisionist textbooks minimize the event, while mainstream texts include references. Political visits to Yasukuni Shrine exacerbate tensions.
