# Boxer Rebellion

## Overview
The Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901) was a violent anti-foreign and anti-Christian uprising in North China led by the Society of Righteous and Harmonious Fists, known as "Boxers." It was a reaction against foreign imperialist encroachment and the spread of Christianity, exacerbated by natural disasters. The Qing court's ambivalent support for the Boxers led to a military intervention by the Eight-Nation Alliance, resulting in a crushing defeat for China, a punitive peace treaty, and the further weakening of the Qing dynasty.

## Timeline
- **1897** — Juye Incident: German missionaries killed, leading to German seizure of Jiaozhou Bay.
- **1898** — "Scramble for Concessions" by foreign powers; Boxers first use the slogan "Support the Qing, destroy the foreigners."
- **1899** — Boxer movement spreads across Shandong; first major clash with Qing troops at Battle of Senluo Temple.
- **June 1900** — Boxers converge on Beijing; siege of the Legation Quarter begins.
- **June 21** — Empress Dowager Cixi issues imperial decree, a de facto declaration of war on foreign powers.
- **June–July** — Seymour Expedition to relieve legations is defeated and besieged.
- **July 14** — Eight-Nation Alliance captures Tianjin.
- **August 14** — Alliance relieves the 55-day Siege of the International Legations in Beijing.
- **September 7, 1901** — Boxer Protocol signed, imposing heavy indemnity and concessions on China.

## Body

### Origins and Causes
The Boxer movement arose in Shandong province, a region with a history of social unrest and martial societies. Its members, practicing Chinese martial arts, claimed supernatural invulnerability. The movement was fueled by resentment against foreign spheres of influence, Christian missionaries who protected their converts in legal disputes, and the destruction of Chinese sites for foreign infrastructure like railroads. Natural disasters, including drought and Yellow River floods in 1898–99, were blamed on foreign and Christian influence, driving desperate villagers to join the Boxers.

### Escalation and the Siege of Beijing
In early 1900, the Boxer movement spread rapidly north toward Beijing. Empress Dowager Cixi, initially hesitant, shifted to supporting the Boxers as a tool against foreign powers. In June, Boxers besieged the Beijing Legation Quarter, where foreign diplomats and Chinese Christians took refuge. The Eight-Nation Alliance intervened, storming the Dagu Forts on June 17. Cixi issued a de facto declaration of war on June 21. The ensuing 55-day siege was marked by fierce fighting, with Chinese forces employing artillery and mining, while defenders held out with limited resources.

### The Eight-Nation Alliance and Aftermath
The Alliance, led by British Lieutenant-General Alfred Gaselee, brought a relief force of 20,000 troops to Beijing, relieving the legations on August 14. The Qing court fled to Xi'an. The occupation was marked by widespread looting, summary executions, and atrocities by Allied troops, particularly German, Russian, and Japanese forces. The Boxer Protocol of 1901 imposed a massive indemnity of 450 million taels of silver, the execution of pro-Boxer officials, and the stationing of foreign troops in Beijing. The rebellion's failure further eroded Qing authority, contributing to the Late Qing reforms and ultimately the Xinhai Revolution of 1911.

## Terms
- **Boxers**: Members of the Society of Righteous and Harmonious Fists, a martial arts group that led the anti-foreign uprising.
- **Eight-Nation Alliance**: A coalition of American, Austro-Hungarian, British, French, German, Italian, Japanese, and Russian troops that intervened in China.
- **Legation Quarter**: The diplomatic district in Beijing where foreign representatives were besieged.
- **Boxer Protocol**: The 1901 peace treaty that ended the rebellion, imposing heavy reparations and concessions on China.
- **Yihetuan Movement**: The Chinese name for the Boxer movement, meaning "Society of Righteous and Harmonious Fists."
- **Indemnity**: The 450 million tael silver reparation China was forced to pay to the eight invading nations.
- **Mutual Defense Pact of the Southeastern Provinces**: An agreement by southern governors to remain neutral and not recognize the imperial court's declaration of war.

## Debates and Open Questions
The historical interpretation of the Boxers is contested. Scholars debate whether they were anti-imperialist patriots or irrational, violent reactionaries. The term "Boxer Rebellion" is itself a misnomer, as the Boxers did not rebel against the Qing dynasty but sought to "Support the Qing, destroy the foreigners." The extent of Qing court support, the role of individual officials like Ronglu who may have secretly protected foreigners, and the long-term impact on Chinese nationalism and revolution remain active areas of study.
