# Addiction

## Overview

Addiction is a neuropsychological disorder characterized by a persistent and intense urge to use a drug or engage in a behavior that produces immediate psychological reward, despite substantial harm and negative consequences. Repeated use produces long-lasting changes in brain networks involved in reward, executive function, stress reactivity, and mood, which underlie both the intense drive to use and the reduced capacity to control that urge. It is understood as a brain disorder arising from a complex mix of psychosocial and neurobiological factors, though some researchers argue this framing is incomplete and that addiction is better understood as learned behavior shaped by choice and social context. Roughly half the risk is genetic, and addiction is managed as a chronic, relapsing condition combining medication (where effective) with psychological and social support.

## Timeline

- **Late 18th century** — Benjamin Rush and Thomas Trotter independently consolidate the idea that compulsive drinking is a disease rather than a vice
- **1877** — Eduard Levinstein publishes *Die Morphiumsucht*, the first monograph treating morphine addiction as a disorder in its own right
- **1950s** — Dwight Heath conducts fieldwork with the Camba of Bolivia, producing the cultural model of heavy drinking
- **1960s–70s** — Ethnographic work, including "Taking care of business," documents New York street heroin users' social worlds
- **Early 1980s** — Critical medical anthropology is applied to drug use, shifting focus to political and economic structures
- **2013** — DSM-5 introduces a new diagnostic category for behavioral addictions; gambling disorder is the only condition included
- **2018** — Addiction Theory Network launched to challenge the brain disease model
- **2019** — Alcohol use responsible for an estimated 2.6 million deaths worldwide (4.7% of all deaths); psychoactive drug use for 0.6 million
- **2021** — About 163 million people worldwide living with a substance use disorder
- **2023** — Drug use one of only three risk factors whose age-standardized attributable DALY rate rose between 2010 and 2023
- **2025** — Estimated 69,973 drug overdose deaths in the US, down almost 14% from 81,313 in 2024

## Body

### Signs, symptoms, and complications

Impaired control is the core feature: using more or for longer than intended, unsuccessful attempts to cut down, and much time spent obtaining, using, or recovering. Social impairment follows, with failure to meet obligations and abandonment of former activities. Risky use (continuing despite known harm) and, for many substances, tolerance and withdrawal complete the picture. The most immediate danger is overdose, which for opioids and other sedatives acts by suppressing breathing. Injecting drug use carries risk of blood-borne infection including HIV and hepatitis C. Psychiatric conditions frequently co-occur, each worsening the outlook for the other, so both should be treated simultaneously. People with substance use disorders die by suicide roughly five times more often than the general population.

### Types

Addiction takes substance and non-substance forms. Substance use disorders involve psychoactive drugs; the DSM-5 discourages "drug addiction" due to its "uncertain definition and its potentially negative connotation," preferring "substance use disorder." The DSM-5-TR recognizes only gambling disorder as a behavioral addiction and lists internet gaming disorder as a condition for further study; the ICD-11 additionally classifies gaming disorder as a disorder due to addictive behaviors. Whether food can be addictive is disputed: neither manual recognizes a food addiction, and researchers disagree on whether it is best framed as addiction to specific foods, disordered eating, or neither. Proponents argue ultra-processed foods high in refined carbohydrate and fat meet conventional criteria for an addictive substance; critics respond that no single constituent has been isolated as the addictive agent.

### Causes

Genetic and environmental risk factors each account for roughly half of an individual's risk. Twin and adoption studies estimate heritability of alcohol use disorder at about 50%, with comparable figures across substances. This risk is polygenic—the combined small effects of many common variants. Environmental factors include adverse childhood experiences (showing a graded dose–response association with later harm), chronic stress, family and peer influences, socioeconomic constraint, and substance availability. Stress activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, raising cortisol and corticotropin-releasing hormone, which alters reward processing and increases the motivational pull of substances. Adolescence is the period of greatest risk: the prefrontal cortex, supporting planning and inhibitory control, matures more slowly than reward circuitry and is not fully mature until the mid-twenties. People with comorbid mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety, ADHD, or PTSD are more likely to develop substance use disorders.

### Mechanisms

Addiction is described as a recurring three-stage cycle. During binge/intoxication, dopaminergic signaling in the basal ganglia gives the drug and associated cues a powerful pull on attention and behavior. During withdrawal/negative affect, brain stress systems in the extended amygdala become overactive and ordinary pleasures become muted, so use is driven by relief rather than reward. During preoccupation/anticipation, reduced prefrontal cortex control leaves craving poorly restrained. Dopamine signaling in the nucleus accumbens—the same pathway responding to natural rewards—is central; dopamine release is more closely tied to "wanting" than "liking." Nearly all addictive drugs increase dopamine release along the mesolimbic pathway from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens, though by different routes. Chronic drug exposure produces lasting glutamate-mediated changes along prefrontal cortex projections. Repeated exposure causes the protein ΔFosB to accumulate in reward-related neurons, increasing drug self-administration and blunting sensitivity to adverse consequences. Epigenetic regulation—histone modification, DNA methylation, and microRNAs—changes gene expression without altering DNA sequence; in rodents, some drug-induced epigenetic marks can be passed to offspring, though this inheritance is not established in humans.

### Theories and models

The brain disease model posits that exposure to an addictive drug is the most significant environmental risk factor. Critics, including Marc Lewis and the Addiction Theory Network (launched 2018), argue the brain changes are better understood as a form of learning, with addiction explained by choice, learning, and social context. Psychological theories include psychoanalytic accounts (addiction as defense against hopelessness), conditioning models (environmental cues acquire motivational significance and trigger craving), and personality theories. Social learning theory (Albert Bandura) frames drug use as arising from reciprocal relationships between personal factors, environment, and behavior. Travis Hirschi's social control theory holds that adolescents with stronger attachments to family, religious, academic, and social institutions are less likely to engage in drug use. Evolutionary perspectives suggest vulnerabilities reflect evolutionary mismatch: purified drugs and direct routes of administration give a "false signal of a fitness benefit" that hijacks reward mechanisms.

### Diagnosis

The DSM-5 uses "substance use disorder" across a spectrum, eliminating the terms abuse and dependence, and grades severity as mild, moderate, or severe; severe substance use disorder is synonymous with drug addiction. Physical dependence occurs when the body attains homeostasis incorporating the substance; withdrawal from alcohol or benzodiazepines can be life-threatening. The ICD-11 distinguishes three diagnoses: Episode of Harmful Psychoactive Substance Use, Harmful Pattern of Psychoactive Substance Use, and Substance Dependence. Screening instruments include the WHO's ASSIST, the TAPS tool, the CRAFFT for adolescents, and the DAST.

### Prevention and management

Prevention is organized in three tiers: universal (whole population), selective (groups at raised risk), and indicated (individuals with early signs). Interventions with strongest evidence include family skills programs, school-based personal and social skills education, and policies limiting availability and raising price. Information-only or fear-based programs are not effective and may be counterproductive. Management treats addiction as chronic and relapsing. For opioid use disorder, methadone or buprenorphine maintenance retains people in treatment and suppresses illicit use; death risk is around six times higher in the four weeks after treatment stops. For alcohol use disorder, naltrexone and acamprosate are first-line; for tobacco, varenicline, cytisine, and nicotine e-cigarettes are among the most effective aids. No medication is approved for stimulant use disorder; contingency management has the strongest evidence there. The transtheoretical model describes change through precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, and maintenance stages, with relapse treated as expected. Harm reduction includes needle and syringe programs, naloxone distribution, drug checking, and supervised consumption facilities; a 2023 review found only nine countries implementing all five.

### Prognosis and epidemiology

An estimated 35–54% of people with a substance use disorder achieve remission (no longer meeting diagnostic criteria for at least six months), but typically only after a mean follow-up of around 17 years, with roughly 7–9% remitting in any given year. About 163 million people worldwide had a substance use disorder in 2021 (111 million alcohol, 53 million drug use disorders). Age-standardized prevalence fell about 17% between 1990 and 2021, peaking in young adults aged 25–29 and higher among males. Drug use was one of only three risk factors whose age-standardized attributable DALY rate rose between 2010 and 2023.

### History, society, and culture

The word addiction derives from Latin "addico," meaning "giving over," with both positive connotations (devotion) and negative ones (enslavement to a creditor in Roman law). Nineteenth-century temperance movements narrowed the definition to drug-related disease only. Substance use disorders are among the more heavily stigmatized health conditions, with negative attitudes documented among clinicians. Addiction imposes substantial costs from healthcare, lost productivity, accidents, suicides, homicides, and incarceration. Critical medical anthropology emphasizes self-medication of distress from social inequality, the social production of suffering, and the political economy of drug markets. Anthropological models include the cultural model (from Heath's Camba fieldwork, arguing drinking problems are culturally determined) and the subcultural model (reframing substance use as a social phenomenon).

### Research directions

Vaccines conjugating drug molecules to carrier proteins to generate antibodies have been tested against nicotine, cocaine, opioids, and fentanyl since the early 2000s, but none is licensed; a Cochrane review found no evidence nicotine vaccines improve long-term cessation. GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide have attracted interest because they act on the brain's reward system; large observational studies associate their use with lower rates of alcohol- and opioid-related harm, but randomized trials have not yet confirmed consistent benefit, and specialists describe them as promising but unproven.

## Terms

- **Substance use disorder**: A long-term, relapsing condition of continued substance seeking and use despite harm; the DSM-5 term replacing "abuse" and "dependence."
- **Tolerance**: The body adapting to a substance so larger amounts are needed for the same effect.
- **Withdrawal**: Physical and psychological symptoms appearing when a substance the body has adapted to is reduced or stopped.
- **Mesolimbic pathway**: The brain's reward pathway; dopaminergic neurons project from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens.
- **ΔFosB**: A protein that accumulates in reward-related neurons after repeated drug exposure; used in preclinical research as a marker of addiction.
- **Epigenetic regulation**: Processes changing gene expression without altering DNA sequence, including histone modification, DNA methylation, and microRNAs.
- **Harm reduction**: Measures reducing damage from continued substance use without requiring abstinence, such as needle programs and naloxone distribution.
- **Contingency management**: A behavioral therapy providing tangible rewards for verified abstinence.
- **Transtheoretical model**: A stages-of-change model (precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance) used to gauge readiness to change.
- **Evolutionary mismatch**: The idea that evolutionarily novel substances act on reward systems shaped under different ancestral conditions.

## Debates and open questions

Whether the brain disease model is complete is actively contested: critics argue addiction is better explained by choice, learning, and social context. Whether food can be addictive is disputed, with neither diagnostic manual recognizing it. Whether craving is central to addiction and whether addictive behavior is accurately described as compulsive remain debated among competing accounts (incentive-sensitization, opponent-process, habit, and impaired prefrontal control theories). Whether drug-induced epigenetic marks are inherited in humans and affect behavior has not been established. Whether GLP-1 receptor agonists are effective treatments for substance use disorders remains unconfirmed by randomized trials.
