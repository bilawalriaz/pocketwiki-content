# Nelson Mandela

## Overview

Nelson Mandela (1918–2013) was a South African anti-apartheid revolutionary, politician, and philanthropist who served as the country's first black president (1994–1999). Born into the Thembu royal family, he became a central figure in the African National Congress (ANC), transitioning from non-violent protest to armed resistance before leading negotiations to end apartheid. His presidency emphasized racial reconciliation, institutional reform, and the establishment of a multiracial democracy, earning him global recognition as a symbol of moral leadership and social justice.

## Body

### Early Life and Education

Mandela was born Rolihlahla Mandela in Mvezo, a village in the Transkei region, to a family of the Thembu royal lineage. His father, Gadla Henry Mphakanyiswa, was a local chief and councillor. Raised initially under the guardianship of Chief Jongintaba Dalindyebo at Mqhekezweni, Mandela received early education at Methodist schools, where he was baptized and given the name "Nelson." He underwent the ulwaluko circumcision ritual at age 16, marking his transition to manhood.

His formal education included attendance at Clarkebury Methodist High School and Healdtown Methodist College, followed by studies at the University of Fort Hare. However, he left Fort Hare without completing his degree due to suspension over a student protest. In 1941, he moved to Johannesburg, where he worked various jobs and enrolled in a University of South Africa law degree via correspondence.

### Rise in the ANC and Early Activism

Returning to Johannesburg, Mandela became involved with the ANC through Walter Sisulu, who helped him secure a position at the law firm Witkin, Sidelsky and Eidelman. He joined the ANC in 1943 and co-founded its Youth League in 1944 alongside Oliver Tambo and other activists. Influenced by Anton Lembede’s Africanist ideology, Mandela advocated for black self-determination and mass mobilization.

The 1948 election victory of the National Party, which implemented apartheid policies, radicalized Mandela and the ANC. He played a key role in the 1952 Defiance Campaign, a non-violent resistance movement against discriminatory laws. His leadership during this period elevated his profile within the ANC, leading to his election as president of the Transvaal branch in 1952.

### Shift to Armed Resistance

As apartheid intensified, Mandela concluded that non-violent protest was insufficient. Influenced by Marxist literature and the success of global liberation movements, he embraced the idea of armed struggle. In 1961, he co-founded Umkhonto we Sizwe (MK), the ANC’s militant wing, alongside Joe Slovo and Govan Mbeki. MK conducted sabotage campaigns targeting government infrastructure, aiming to minimize civilian casualties while maximizing political impact.

Mandela’s involvement in MK led to his arrest in 1962. During the subsequent Rivonia Trial (1963–1964), he delivered his famous "I Am Prepared to Die" speech, affirming his commitment to ending apartheid. Convicted of sabotage and conspiracy, he was sentenced to life imprisonment.

### Imprisonment and International Recognition

Mandela spent 27 years in prison, primarily on Robben Island, later transferred to Pollsmoor and Victor Verster prisons. Despite harsh conditions, he maintained his political resolve, engaging in clandestine negotiations and continuing his legal studies. His imprisonment transformed him into an international symbol of resistance, with the "Free Mandela" campaign gaining momentum globally.

International pressure mounted on the South African government, particularly after Mandela’s 70th birthday in 1988. President F.W. de Klerk, recognizing the unsustainable nature of apartheid, initiated secret talks with Mandela. In 1990, Mandela was released unconditionally, marking the beginning of the end of apartheid.

### Presidency and Reconciliation

Following his release, Mandela led the ANC in negotiations with the National Party to dismantle apartheid and establish a multiracial democracy. The 1994 elections, won decisively by the ANC, made him South Africa’s first black president. His government focused on reconciliation, establishing the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) to address past human rights abuses.

Mandela’s presidency also prioritized social reforms, including expanding access to healthcare, education, and housing. However, his administration faced challenges such as economic inequality, crime, and the emerging HIV/AIDS crisis. Despite these issues, Mandela’s emphasis on unity and forgiveness helped prevent a potential civil war.

### Post-Presidency and Legacy

After stepping down in 1999, Mandela continued advocating for social justice through the Nelson Mandela Foundation. He remained active in global causes, including HIV/AIDS awareness and conflict resolution. His later years were marked by declining health, and he passed away on December 5, 2013, at age 95.

Mandela’s legacy is complex—he is celebrated as a moral leader and icon of democracy, yet criticized for his government’s handling of HIV/AIDS and economic inequality. His life story continues to inspire movements for justice and reconciliation worldwide.

## Terms

- **Apartheid**: A system of institutionalized racial segregation and discrimination enforced by the National Party government in South Africa from 1948 to 1994.
- **African National Congress (ANC)**: A political party founded in 1912, central to the anti-apartheid struggle and South Africa’s first democratically elected government.
- **Umkhonto we Sizwe (MK)**: The armed wing of the ANC, established in 1961 to conduct sabotage against the apartheid state.
- **Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC)**: Established in 1995 under Archbishop Desmond Tutu to investigate human rights violations during apartheid, offering amnesty in exchange for truth-telling.
- **Freedom Charter**: A document adopted by the ANC in 1955 outlining a vision for a non-racial, democratic South Africa, including land redistribution and nationalization of key industries.
- **Robben Island**: A prison island off the coast of Cape Town where Mandela was held from 1964 to 1982.
- **Non-Aligned Movement**: A group of states not formally aligned with any major power bloc, of which Mandela served as Secretary-General from 1998 to 1999.
- **Ulwaluko**: A traditional Xhosa coming-of-age ritual involving circumcision, symbolizing the transition from boyhood to manhood.
- **CODESA**: The Convention for a Democratic South Africa, a series of negotiations (1991–1993) between the ANC and the National Party to end apartheid.
- **Rainbow Nation**: A term popularized by Mandela to describe post-apartheid South Africa’s diverse, multicultural society.

## Debates and Open Questions

Mandela’s legacy remains contested. Critics argue that his government failed to adequately address economic inequality and the HIV/AIDS pandemic, with some accusing him of being too conciliatory toward former apartheid supporters. Debates persist over his relationship with the South African Communist Party (SACP), with evidence suggesting he was a member, though he publicly denied this. Additionally, questions remain about his personal relationships, including allegations of fathering children outside his marriages. Scholars continue to analyze whether his approach to reconciliation was sufficient or if it allowed systemic injustices to persist.
