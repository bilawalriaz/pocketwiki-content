# George Gershwin

## Overview
George Gershwin (1898–1937) was an American composer and pianist who fused jazz, popular song, and classical forms, creating a distinctly American musical voice. His works—including *Rhapsody in Blue*, *An American in Paris*, *Porgy and Bess*, and countless Broadway hits—bridged Tin Pan Alley and concert halls, influencing both popular and classical music. His early death at 38 from a brain tumor cut short a career that redefined American music.

## Body

### Ancestry and Early Life
Gershwin was born to Jewish immigrant parents from the Russian Empire. His father, Moishe Gershwine (later Morris Gershwin), arrived in New York in 1890 to avoid conscription, and his mother, Roza (née Bruskina), was born in Saint Petersburg. The family settled in Brooklyn’s East New York neighborhood, where George was exposed to Yiddish theater and tenement life. At age 10, he was captivated by a friend’s violin recital, prompting his parents to buy a piano—though it was George, not his brother Ira, who became obsessed with it.

### Tin Pan Alley and Early Career (1913–1923)
At 15, Gershwin left school to work as a song plugger for Jerome H. Remick and Company, earning $15 a week. His first published song, “When You Want ’Em, You Can’t Get ’Em,” earned him 50 cents. He recorded extensively for Aeolian and Duo-Art, often under pseudonyms like Fred Murtha. His first recording was his own “Swanee” with the Fred Van Eps Trio in 1919. His breakthrough came in 1919 with “Swanee,” popularized by Al Jolson. During this period, he also collaborated with lyricists like Buddy DeSylva and composed the experimental one-act opera *Blue Monday* (1922), a precursor to *Porgy and Bess*.

### Classical Breakthrough and European Influence (1924–1928)
In 1924, Gershwin composed *Rhapsody in Blue* for Paul Whiteman’s jazz orchestra, blending jazz and classical idioms in a revolutionary way. The same year, he collaborated with Ira on *Lady, Be Good!*, which included “Fascinating Rhythm.” In the following year he wrote his Piano Concerto in F. In the mid-1920s, he traveled to Paris, applying to study with Nadia Boulanger and Maurice Ravel—both declined, fearing formal training would dilute his jazz style. There, he composed *An American in Paris*, a tone poem reflecting his impressions of the city.

### Mature Works and Hollywood (1929–1937)
Back in New York, Gershwin and Ira created *Girl Crazy* (1930), introducing “Embraceable You” and “I Got Rhythm.” *Of Thee I Sing* (1931) made history as the first musical to win the Pulitzer Prize for Drama, although as composer, he did not participate in reception of the award. In 1934, he spent time on South Carolina’s Folly Island, inspiring *Porgy and Bess* (1935), now regarded as a landmark American opera despite its initial commercial failure. After its premiere, Gershwin moved to Hollywood, scoring films like *Shall We Dance* (1937) and *A Damsel in Distress* (1937), producing enduring standards such as “They Can’t Take That Away from Me” and “A Foggy Day.” In 1937, Gershwin received his sole Academy Award nomination for Best Original Song at the 1937 Oscars for “They Can’t Take That Away from Me,” a posthumous nomination.

### Illness and Death
In early 1937, Gershwin experienced severe headaches and olfactory hallucinations. During a February concert in San Francisco, he suffered coordination problems and blackouts. Diagnosed too late, he collapsed on July 9 and was found to have a large brain tumor (likely glioblastoma). Despite emergency efforts, including summoning neurosurgeon Walter Dandy from Maryland, Gershwin died on July 11, 1937, at age 38. He was buried in Hastings-on-Hudson, New York. Gershwin died intestate, and his estate passed to his mother.

## Terms
- **Tin Pan Alley**: The popular music industry centered in New York City, known for its sheet music publishers and songwriters.
- **Song plugger**: A musician hired to promote and perform songs to increase sheet music sales.
- **Tone poem**: A piece of orchestral music that tells a story or evokes a scene, often in one movement.
- **Jazz standard**: A song that has become part of the repertoire of jazz musicians, widely performed and recorded.
- **Porgy and Bess**: An opera by Gershwin based on DuBose Heyward’s novel *Porgy*, notable for integrating jazz and classical elements.
- **Piano roll**: A perforated paper roll used in player pianos to reproduce music automatically.
- **Glioblastoma**: A malignant brain tumor, the likely cause of Gershwin’s death.
- **Schillinger System**: A method of musical composition based on mathematical principles, taught to Gershwin by Joseph Schillinger (1932–1936).

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Schillinger’s influence**: Joseph Schillinger taught Gershwin composition (1932–1936) and claimed significant credit for *Porgy and Bess* after its posthumous success. Ira Gershwin completely denied that his brother had any such assistance for this work, while others, including friend Vernon Duke, offered a more nuanced view.
- **Classification of *Porgy and Bess***: Critics struggled to categorize the work—as opera, musical, or something else entirely. Theater historian Robert Kimball noted it “crossed the barriers,” eliciting responses from both music and drama critics.
- **Ravel’s reaction**: Accounts vary on whether Ravel or Igor Stravinsky made the famous remark about giving Gershwin lessons, though Stravinsky confirmed hearing the story from Ravel.
