# Pythagoras

## Overview
Pythagoras of Samos (c. 570–c. 495 BC) was an ancient Ionian Greek philosopher and the eponymous founder of Pythagoreanism, a religious and philosophical movement. He is a pivotal figure in Western thought, influencing Plato, Aristotle, and later scientific and esoteric traditions. His life is shrouded in legend, and while he is credited with numerous mathematical and scientific discoveries, modern scholars debate the extent of his personal contributions versus those of his followers.

## Timeline
- **c. 570 BC** — Born on the island of Samos.
- **c. 530 BC** — Leaves Samos and founds a school in Croton, southern Italy.
- **c. 510 BC** — Croton defeats Sybaris; Pythagoreans later face persecution.
- **c. 495 BC** — Dies, possibly in Metapontum, during anti-Pythagorean unrest.
- **1st century BC** — Revival of Pythagorean teachings among Middle Platonists.
- **1543** — Copernicus cites Pythagorean influence for his heliocentric model.
- **1567** — Arthur Golding's translation of Ovid popularizes Pythagoras's vegetarian speech in English.

## Body

### Life and Sources
No authentic writings by Pythagoras survive. The earliest sources are brief and often satirical. The major biographies, from Diogenes Laërtius, Porphyry, and Iamblichus, are filled with myths and legends. Herodotus and Isocrates agree he was born on Samos to Mnesarchus. Ancient sources record his reputed travels to study with Egyptian priests, Persian Magi, and others, though these stories are likely later inventions to explain his unconventional beliefs. He is said to have studied under Greek thinkers like Pherecydes of Syros and possibly Thales or Anaximander.

Around 530 BC, Pythagoras moved to Croton, where he founded a school resembling a monastery. Initiates lived a communal, ascetic lifestyle, bound by a vow of secrecy. The community had two groups: the *mathematikoi* (learners) and the *akousmatikoi* (listeners). Pythagoras was reportedly progressive in his attitudes towards women. His death is uncertain; accounts place him at various locations during a persecution by democratic forces in Croton, with some legends claiming he died of starvation or was killed after refusing to run through a bean field.

### Teachings and Attributed Discoveries
The core doctrine securely attributed to Pythagoras is **metempsychosis**, the transmigration of souls, where every soul is immortal and enters a new body after death. He may have also taught **musica universalis**, the harmony of the spheres, where planets move according to mathematical ratios, producing an inaudible symphony.

Pythagoras is most famous for the **Pythagorean theorem** (a² + b² = c²), but historians debate his personal discovery of it, as the principle was known to Babylonians and Indians earlier. Other attributed discoveries include the five regular solids, the sphericity of the Earth, and the identification of the morning and evening stars as Venus. Modern scholars like Walter Burkert argue that the number philosophy and mathematical theories were innovations of later Pythagoreans like Philolaus, not Pythagoras himself.

### Later Influence
Pythagoras's teachings profoundly influenced Plato, whose dialogues like *Timaeus* exhibit Pythagorean ideas. A revival occurred in the 1st century BC with Neopythagoreanism. In the Middle Ages, he was revered as a founder of mathematics and music. His influence extended to modern science: Copernicus cited Pythagoreans for his heliocentric model, and Johannes Kepler, a self-proclaimed Pythagorean, was inspired by the harmony of the spheres. Pythagorean symbolism also shaped early modern esotericism, including Freemasonry and Rosicrucianism. His fictionalized vegetarian speech in Ovid's *Metamorphoses* influenced the modern vegetarian movement.

## Terms
- **Metempsychosis**: The doctrine of the transmigration of souls, where the soul is immortal and enters a new body after death.
- **Musica universalis**: The "harmony of the spheres," a doctrine that planets move according to mathematical ratios, producing a cosmic symphony.
- **Pythagorean theorem**: A geometric principle stating that in a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides (a² + b² = c²).
- **Mathematikoi**: The "learners" within Pythagoreanism, associated with a more rationalist and scientific approach.
- **Akousmatikoi**: The "listeners" within Pythagoreanism, associated with mysticism, numerology, and religious teachings.
- **Tetractys**: A triangular figure of four rows of dots summing to ten, a symbol of utmost mystical importance to Pythagoreans.
- **Symbola**: The secret teachings or sayings of Pythagoras, which initiates were sworn not to reveal.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historical Pythagoras vs. Legend**: Scholars debate what Pythagoras himself actually taught versus what was attributed to him by later followers. His mathematical discoveries are particularly contested.
- **Nature of the School**: The exact organization and beliefs of the Pythagorean community, including the distinction between *mathematikoi* and *akousmatikoi*, are unclear.
- **Cause of Death**: Accounts of Pythagoras's death are contradictory, ranging from suicide to starvation to being killed in a fire.
- **Dietary Rules**: Whether Pythagoras enforced strict vegetarianism is debated, with ancient sources providing conflicting reports.
