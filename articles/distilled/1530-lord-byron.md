# Lord Byron

## Overview
George Gordon Byron, 6th Baron Byron (1788–1824), was a leading British poet of the Romantic movement whose life and work defined the archetype of the "Byronic hero"—a flawed, passionate, rebellious exile. Born with a deformed foot into an aristocratic but indebted family, he achieved instant fame with *Childe Harold's Pilgrimage* (1812) and produced his masterpiece, the satirical epic *Don Juan*. His tumultuous personal life—marked by scandalous affairs, a disastrous marriage, rumors of incest with his half-sister Augusta Leigh, and bisexual relationships—forced him into permanent exile in 1816. He spent his final years in Italy and Greece, where he devoted his fortune and energy to the Greek War of Independence against the Ottoman Empire. He died of fever in Missolonghi at age 36, becoming a Greek national hero and a symbol of liberal nationalism across Europe.

- **1814–1815** — Publishes *Hebrew Melodies* (incl. "She Walks in Beauty"); marries Annabella Milbanke (Jan 1815); daughter Ada born (Dec 1815).
- **Jan–Apr 1816** — Separation from wife; scandal over debts, incest rumors, and sodomy allegations forces exile from England.
- **Summer 1816** — Stays at Villa Diodati, Switzerland with Percy and Mary Shelley; ghost-story contest inspires *Frankenstein* and *The Vampyre*; writes Canto III of *Childe Harold*.
- **1816–1823** — Lives in Italy (Venice, Ravenna, Pisa, Genoa); writes *Manfred*, *Cain*, *The Deformed Transformed*, and Cantos I–XII of *Don Juan*; studies Armenian; sells Newstead Abbey.
- **1823–1824** — Travels to Greece to support War of Independence; funds "Byron Brigade" and refits fleet; joins Alexandros Mavrokordatos at Missolonghi.
- **19 Apr 1824** — Dies of fever in Missolonghi, aged 36, after therapeutic bleeding.
- **1824** — Body returned to England; refused burial at Westminster Abbey; interred at Hucknall, Nottinghamshire.

## Body

### Early Life and Inheritance
Byron was born on Holles Street, London, the only child of Captain John "Jack" Byron and Catherine Gordon, heiress of the Gight estate in Aberdeenshire. His father, a fortune hunter, squandered Catherine’s £23,500 estate within two years and died in France in 1791. Byron was born with a deformity of the right foot (variously diagnosed as club foot, infantile paralysis, or dysplasia), causing a limp that inflicted lifelong psychological misery. In 1798, the death of his "wicked" great-uncle made the 10-year-old the 6th Baron Byron, granting him the dilapidated Newstead Abbey. His mother, described as volatile and financially ruined, leased the Abbey and raised him in Aberdeen and Nottinghamshire, where his sensitivity about his lameness and his mother’s erratic behavior shaped a character prone to "silent rages" and violent attachments.

### Education and Early Writing
Byron attended Aberdeen Grammar School, then Dr. Glennie’s school in Dulwich, and Harrow (1801–1805). At Harrow, he was an undistinguished student but played in the first Eton v. Harrow match. He experienced his first "adult sexual feelings" for Mary Chaworth and formed passionate, possibly homoerotic, friendships with boys like John FitzGibbon and John Thomas Claridge. At Trinity College, Cambridge (1805–1808), he befriended John Cam Hobhouse (who introduced him to Whig politics) and John Edleston, a younger chorister. Byron called his love for Edleston "a violent, though pure love and passion," writing the *Thyrza* elegies after Edleston’s death. His first poems, *Fugitive Pieces* (1806), were suppressed for amorous content; *Hours of Idleness* (1807) drew a savage review in the *Edinburgh Review* (by Henry Peter Brougham), prompting his first major satire, *English Bards and Scotch Reviewers* (1809).

### The Grand Turn and Celebrity
Forced to avoid Europe due to the Napoleonic Wars, Byron toured the Mediterranean (1809–1811) with Hobhouse. He visited Portugal, Spain, Albania (meeting Ali Pasha), and Greece, where his "poetical feelings begin and end." He swam the Hellespont in 1810, a feat commemorated in *Don Juan*. Returning to England, the publication of *Childe Harold* Cantos I–II (1812) made him the "most brilliant star" of Regency London. He produced the "Oriental Tales" (*The Giaour*, *The Bride of Abydos*, *The Corsair*, *Lara*) and *Hebrew Melodies* (1814–15). He entered a volatile affair with Lady Caroline Lamb (who called him "mad, bad, and dangerous to know") and renewed acquaintance with his half-sister Augusta Leigh, sparking incest rumors (Augusta’s daughter Medora, b. 1814, was suspected to be his).

### Marriage, Scandal, and Exile
To escape debt and gossip, Byron married the intellectual heiress Annabella Milbanke in January 1815. Their daughter, Ada (later Lovelace, a computing pioneer), was born in December. The marriage collapsed immediately due to Byron’s obsession with Augusta, affairs with actresses, and erratic behavior. Annabella left him in January 1816, securing a legal separation by March. The ensuing scandal—fueled by rumors of incest, sodomy, and marital violence—combined with crushing debts forced Byron to leave England in April 1816, never to return.

### Switzerland and the Shelleys
Byron settled at the Villa Diodati on Lake Geneva with his physician John Polidori. He befriended Percy Bysshe Shelley and Mary Godwin (later Shelley), and reunited with Claire Clairmont, Mary’s stepsister, who bore his illegitimate daughter Allegra (1817–1822). Trapped indoors by incessant rain in June 1816, the group read ghost stories, prompting Mary Shelley to write *Frankenstein* and Polidori *The Vampyre* (based on a Byron fragment). Byron wrote Canto III of *Childe Harold* and the poem *The Prisoner of Chillon*.

### Italian Years: Venice, Ravenna, Pisa, Genoa (1816–1823)
This was Byron’s most productive period. In Venice, he had affairs with Marianna Segati and Margherita Cogni, studied Armenian at the Mechitarist monastery on San Lazzaro degli Armeni, and co-authored Armenian-English grammars and a dictionary. He sold Newstead Abbey (1818) and wrote *Manfred*, *Cain*, *The Deformed Transformed*, and the first five cantos of *Don Juan* (1818–1820). In Ravenna (1819–1821), he lived for Teresa Guiccioli, a young married countess, writing the *Ravenna Diary* and continuing *Don Juan*. Shelley visited, describing Byron’s menagerie of monkeys, eagles, and peacocks roaming the house. In Pisa (1821–1822), he finished Cantos 6–12 of *Don Juan* and co-founded the journal *The Liberal* with Leigh Hunt and Shelley. He witnessed Shelley’s cremation after the poet drowned in July 1822. His final Italian residence was Genoa, where he lived with Teresa and the Blessingtons.

### Greek War of Independence
Bored in Genoa, Byron accepted overtures from Greek independence leaders in 1823. He chartered the *Hercules*, spending £4,000 to refit the Greek fleet, and arrived at Missolonghi in January 1824. He allied with politician Alexandros Mavrokordatos but was thwarted by factional infighting. He paid £6,000 to the Souliotes (irregular troops) who blackmailed him for back-pay, eventually dismissing them. He planned an attack on the Ottoman fortress at Navpaktos, but the Albanian garrison’s mutiny was crushed by their commander. Byron formed a "Byron Brigade" of 231 men, sold his Rochdale estate for £11,250, and attempted to unite rival Greek leaders (Mavrokordatos and Odysseas Androutsos). He lamented that Greeks spent more time feuding than fighting Ottomans.

### Death and Post-Mortem
In February 1824, Byron fell ill before a planned attack on Lepanto. Bloodletting by his doctors weakened him; a subsequent cold developed into a fatal fever. He died in Missolonghi on 19 April 1824. His physician, Julius van Millingen, could not save him. The Greeks mourned him as a hero (the poet Dionysios Solomos wrote *To the Death of Lord Byron*; a suburb of Athens was named Vyronas). His heart was reportedly buried in Missolonghi; his body was returned to England but refused by Westminster Abbey for "questionable morality." He was buried at Hucknall, Nottinghamshire, near Newstead. A statue by Bertel Thorvaldsen was rejected by British institutions for a decade before placement at Trinity College, Cambridge; a memorial was finally placed in Westminster Abbey in 1969.

### Personal Life: Sexuality and Children
Byron’s sexuality remains debated. He described intense early attachments to female cousins (Mary Duff, Margaret Parker) and disclosed childhood sexual abuse by a maid, May Gray, and advances by Lord Grey de Ruthyn. Biographers (Marchand, Crompton, MacCarthy) variously characterize him as bisexual, with a preference for adolescent males, or possessing a "complex sentimental and sexual life" defying simple labels. At Harrow and Cambridge, he had romantic relationships with younger boys (Edleston, Claridge, FitzGibbon). In Greece, he formed attachments to Nicolo Giraud (bequeathing £7,000) and Loukas Chalandritsanos (his page at death). He fathered at least three children: Ada Lovelace (with Annabella); Allegra (with Claire Clairmont, died age 5); and possibly Elizabeth Medora Leigh (with half-sister Augusta). He also acknowledged a son by a maid, Lucy Monk (b. 1809), and considered adopting a Turkish girl, Hato, in Greece.

### Political Career
Byron took his seat in the House of Lords in 1809. Aligned with the Holland House Whigs, he defended the Luddites (Nottinghamshire frame-breakers) in a famous 1812 speech mocking automation’s "benefits" and demanding "Twelve Butchers for a Jury and a Jeffries for a Judge." He spoke for Catholic Emancipation in 1812. His politics informed poems like *Song for the Luddites* (1816), *The Age of Bronze*, and satires attacking opponents: *Wellington: The Best of the Cut-Throats* and *The Intellectual Eunuch Castlereagh*.

### Major Works and Themes
*   **Childe Harold's Pilgrimage (1812–1818):** Four-canto Spenserian stanza poem tracing a world-weary aristocrat’s travels; established the Byronic hero and made Byron famous.
*   **Oriental Tales (1813–1816):** *The Giaour*, *The Bride of Abydos*, *The Corsair*, *Lara*; verse narratives set in the Ottoman East, exploring guilt, revenge, and exoticism.
*   **Hebrew Melodies (1814–1815):** Lyrics set to music by Isaac Nathan, including "She Walks in Beauty" and "The Destruction of Sennacherib."
*   **Manfred (1817):** A dramatic poem about a guilt-ridden nobleman defying spirits; a key Byronic hero text.
*   **Don Juan (1819–1824):** His magnum opus, 17 cantos in ottava rima. A satirical epic reversing the legend: Juan is passive, seduced by women. It attacks hypocrisy, cant, and poets like Wordsworth ("Turdsworth") and Southey. Published anonymously at first; publisher John Murray eventually refused to continue due to outrage.
*   **The Curse of Minerva / Childe Harold Canto II:** Denounces Lord Elgin’s removal of the Parthenon Marbles.
*   **Irish Avatar (1821):** Satirical pamphlet criticizing Irish unionism and George IV’s visit.

### Legacy and the Byronic Hero
Byron’s wife coined "Byromania" for the public frenzy surrounding him. He curated his image as a "man of action" rather than a scholar, anticipating the modern celebrity. The **Byronic hero**—talented, passionate, rebellious, aristocratic yet scornful of rank, haunted by a secret past, self-destructive, and exiled—pervades his work and influenced 19th-century literature (Brontë sisters, Pushkin, Lermontov) and philosophy (Nietzsche’s *Übermensch*). His reputation remains higher in continental Europe than in Britain. He inspired over forty operas and composers including Beethoven, Schubert, Berlioz, Liszt, and Schoenberg. The Byron Society re-founded in 1971 reflects enduring global fascination. In Greece, he is revered as *megalos kai kalos* ("great and good"), the only foreigner honored with a national day (19 April, Day of Philhellenism).

## Terms
- ****Byronic hero**** — A literary archetype derived from Byron’s protagonists: a gifted, proud, passionate, and cynical aristocrat who rebels against society, is haunted by a guilty past, and is ultimately self-destructive.
- ****Ottava rima**** — An eight-line stanza form (abababcc) of Italian origin, used by Byron in *Don Juan* for its capacity for conversational digression and satirical bathos.
- ****Hellenism / Philhellenism**** — The admiration of Greek culture; in Byron’s era, the political movement supporting Greek independence from the Ottoman Empire, which Byron joined materially and militarily.
- ****Luddites**** — Early 19th-century English textile workers who destroyed machinery threatening their livelihoods; Byron defended them in Parliament against the death penalty.
- ****Hebrew Melodies**** — A collection of lyrics (1814–15) written to be set to Jewish synagogue melodies by composer Isaac Nathan; includes "She Walks in Beauty."
- ****Mechitarist Order**** — An Armenian Catholic monastic congregation founded in 1701; their Venice monastery (San Lazzaro) was where Byron studied Armenian language and history.
- ****Souliotes**** — Orthodox Christian Albanian warrior clans from the Souli region; they fought for Greek independence but extorted Byron for pay at Missolonghi.
- ****Frame-breakers**** — Synonym for Luddites; workers who smashed automated looms (frames). Byron’s 1812 speech opposed the Frame-Breaking Act which made it a capital offense.
- ****Newstead Abbey**** — The ancestral Byron seat in Nottinghamshire, inherited in 1798; dilapidated, leased out, and eventually sold by Byron in 1818 to pay debts.
- ****Club foot / Talipes**** — Congenital deformity of the foot; Byron’s right foot deformity (cause debated: club foot, polio, dysplasia) caused a limp and profound psychological distress.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Nature of the Augusta Leigh relationship:** Biographers dispute whether Byron’s intense bond with his half-sister Augusta was incestuous (André Maurois argued it was proven) or a passionate but non-sexual "romantic friendship" filling an emotional void.
*   **Sexual orientation:** Scholars debate whether Byron was homosexual, bisexual, or "pansexual." Evidence includes his "pure" love for Edleston, Harrow/Cambridge liaisons with boys, coded language ("Plen. and opt. Cs") in letters to Hobhouse, relationships with women (Caroline Lamb, Teresa Guiccioli), and the bequest to Nicolo Giraud. Fiona MacCarthy posits a preference for adolescent males; Emily Bernhard Jackson argues the evidence is too "nebulous, contradictory and scanty" for a definitive label.
*   **Cause of foot deformity:** Medical retrospective diagnoses vary: congenital talipes equinovarus (club foot), infantile paralysis (poliomyelitis), or dysplasia (failure of bone formation). Contemporary treatments were painful and ineffective.
*   **Authorship of "Epitaph to a Dog":** Long attributed to Byron, a draft letter by Hobhouse (1830) claims Hobhouse wrote the 26-line poem; Byron used it for Boatswain’s monument instead of his own epitaph.
*   **Vaccine scepticism:** *English Bards* mocks the cowpox vaccine alongside quackery (tractors, galvanism), yet Byron privately had his protégé Robert Rushton inoculated, suggesting public satire vs. private pragmatism.
*   **Impact on Greek Independence:** Historian David Brewer argues Byron failed militarily and politically to unite Greek factions, succeeding only in humanitarian aid. Conversely, his death galvanized international support (Philhellenism), arguably altering the war’s diplomatic trajectory.
*   **Suppression of Memoirs:** One month after Byron’s death, Moore, Hobhouse, and publisher John Murray burned his *Memoirs* to protect reputations. The Murray archive subsequently suppressed details of his bisexuality until the mid-20th century, distorting biographical scholarship for over a century.
