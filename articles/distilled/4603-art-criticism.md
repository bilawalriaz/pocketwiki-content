# Art criticism

## Overview
Art criticism is the practice of discussing, interpreting, and evaluating visual art, often within the context of aesthetics (the theory of beauty). It seeks a rational basis for art appreciation but is influenced by socio-political circumstances. The field is divided between historical criticism (art history) and contemporary criticism of living artists, and its judgments are often revised over time.

## Timeline
- **1719** — Jonathan Richardson publishes *An Essay on the Whole Art of Criticism*, introducing the term "art criticism" and an objective ranking system.
- **1718** — Jean-Baptiste Dubos publishes *Réflexions critiques*, an early influential work on aesthetic theory.
- **1746** — Étienne La Font de Saint-Yenne writes about the Paris Salon, linking art to its socioeconomic context.
- **1765** — Denis Diderot's "The Salon of 1765" becomes a pioneering attempt to describe art in words.
- **1843** — John Ruskin publishes *Modern Painters*, a landmark of detailed, scientific art criticism.
- **1845** — Charles Baudelaire's *Salon of 1845* marks a bold, impassioned critical voice.
- **1877** — John Ruskin's attack on James McNeill Whistler's *Nocturne in Black and Gold* leads to a famous libel lawsuit.
- **1910** — Roger Fry organizes a "Post-Impressionist" exhibition, championing modernist art and formalism.
- **1914** — Clive Bell publishes *Art*, arguing for "significant form" as the essential quality in art.
- **1929** — Herbert Read publishes an influential essay on modern art and later helps organize the 1936 London International Surrealist Exhibition.
- **1940s-50s** — Clement Greenberg and Harold Rosenberg become leading critics, championing Abstract Expressionism in New York.
- **1970s** — Feminist art criticism emerges, examining representations of women and art by women.

## Body

### Definition and Methodology
Art criticism is the discussion and interpretation of art and its value, though its definition is fluid and sometimes overlaps with art history. Its methodology includes a descriptive aspect—translating a work into words—and an evaluative aspect, which depends on the critic's experience and theoretical approach. This can range from recording immediate impressions to a systematic analysis using technical knowledge, aesthetic theory, and sociocultural context.

### Historical Development
Critiques of art likely began with art itself, with early examples in texts by Plato and Vitruvius. Art criticism as a modern genre formed in the 18th century, spurred by public art exhibitions in Paris and London. Key early figures include Richardson, Dubos, and Diderot. The 19th century saw criticism become a more common vocation, marked by debates between Neoclassicists and Romantics, and by influential critics like Ruskin and Baudelaire. The late 19th and early 20th centuries saw a shift toward abstraction, formalized by critics like Roger Fry and Clive Bell, who argued for the primacy of "significant form" over subject matter.

### 20th Century and Modernism
After 1945, New York became a center for art and criticism. Critics like Clement Greenberg and Harold Rosenberg were instrumental in promoting Abstract Expressionism, with Greenberg championing painters like Jackson Pollock on formalist grounds. This period highlighted the critic's role in shaping an artist's reputation, sometimes at the expense of others. Feminist art criticism emerged in the 1970s as a distinct field.

### Contemporary Practice
Today, art critics work in print, online, on television, and in museums. The rise of the internet and social media has created new platforms for art blogs and online criticism. The International Association of Art Critics, affiliated with UNESCO, represents the profession globally.

## Terms
- **Aesthetics**: The branch of philosophy dealing with the nature of beauty and artistic taste.
- **Formalism**: An approach to art criticism that focuses on the formal qualities of a work (line, color, form) rather than its narrative or social content.
- **Significant Form**: A term coined by Clive Bell to describe the combination of lines and colors that provokes an aesthetic emotion, which he argued is the essential quality in art.
- **Abstract Expressionism**: A post-WWII art movement characterized by gestural brush-strokes or mark-making, and the impression of spontaneity.
- **Action Painting**: A style of painting in which paint is spontaneously dribbled, splashed or smeared onto the canvas, as championed by critic Harold Rosenberg.
- **Feminist Art Criticism**: A critical approach that examines the representation of women in art and the work of women artists, emerging from the feminist movement.
- **Salon**: A public exhibition of art, originally held in Paris, which became a key venue for art display and criticism in the 18th and 19th centuries.
