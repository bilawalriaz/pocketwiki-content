# Curitiba

## Overview
Curitiba is the capital and largest city of Paraná state in Southern Brazil, serving as a major cultural, political, and economic hub. It is internationally recognized for its innovative urban planning, particularly its Bus Rapid Transit system and green spaces, which have made it a model for sustainable city development.

## Timeline
- **1693** — Portuguese settlement founded as Vila da Nossa Senhora da Luz dos Pinhais.
- **1721** — Settlement renamed Curitiba.
- **1853** — Paraná becomes an independent province, with Curitiba as its capital.
- **1850s–1950s** — Major growth from logging and agriculture (Araucaria, mate, coffee).
- **1850s onward** — Waves of European immigration (Germans, Italians, Poles, Ukrainians).
- **1885** — Paranaguá–Curitiba railroad opens.
- **1912** — Federal University of Paraná established.
- **1940s–1950s** — First city plan drafted by Alfred Agache.
- **1965** — Curitiba Master Plan adopted, led by Jaime Lerner.
- **1974** — World's second Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) system, Rede Integrada de Transporte, opens.
- **2010** — Awarded Global Sustainable City Award.
- **2014** — Hosted FIFA World Cup matches.

## Body

### Etymology and Founding
The name "Curitiba" likely derives from Tupi words meaning "many araucaria seeds" or "large amount of pine trees," referencing the region's native Paraná pines. The Portuguese founded a settlement in 1693, initially naming it Vila da Nossa Senhora da Luz dos Pinhais. The name was changed to Curitiba in 1721, with the current spelling officially adopted in 1919.

### Historical Development
Curitiba's early economy was based on subsistence agriculture and mineral extraction. Its first major expansion came in the 19th century as a cattle-trading post between Rio Grande do Sul and São Paulo. Growth accelerated after 1850 with logging and agricultural cycles (first Araucaria wood, then yerba mate and coffee, later wheat, corn, and soybeans). Large-scale European immigration from the 1850s onward, particularly from Poland, Italy, Germany, and Ukraine, profoundly shaped the city's demographics and culture.

### Urban Planning and Governance
Curitiba is a global exemplar of intentional urban planning. After an initial, costly plan by Alfred Agache in the 1940s, the city adopted the transformative Curitiba Master Plan in 1965. Led by architect Jaime Lerner, it prioritized linear transit corridors, downtown pedestrianization, and flood control through parks instead of canals. The resulting Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) system, Rede Integrada de Transporte, opened in 1974 and is a cornerstone of the city's infrastructure. The city government is decentralized into nine regional administrations.

### Geography and Climate
The city sits on the First Plateau of Paraná at an average altitude of 934.6 m (3,066 ft). Its topography features smooth, rounded hills. The climate is temperate oceanic (Köppen: Cfb), with mild, damp winters (average July minimum 9 °C / 48 °F) and warm summers (average January high 25 °C / 77 °F). Snowfall is rare but has been recorded. The area is part of the Ombrophilous Mixed Forest (Araucaria moist forests), a subtype of the Atlantic Forest.

### Demographics and Economy
Curitiba's population is predominantly of European descent, reflecting its immigration history. The 2022 census recorded 1,773,718 residents, with 74.4% identifying as White. The economy is based on industry and services, with major automotive manufacturing (Brazil's second-largest car producer) and a strong technology sector. It is considered one of Brazil's best cities for investment and has a high Human Development Index (0.856).

### Culture and Education
The city's cultural life is enriched by its immigrant heritage, seen in neighborhoods like Santa Felicidade (Italian) and institutions like the Polish Immigrants Memorial. It hosts numerous museums, including the Oscar Niemeyer Museum, and festivals such as the Curitiba Theater Festival and Japanese Matsuri. Curitiba has the highest literacy rate among Brazilian capitals and is home to the Federal University of Paraná, founded in 1912.

## Terms
- **Bus Rapid Transit (BRT):** A high-capacity bus-based transit system that functions like a surface-level metro, using dedicated lanes and pre-board fare collection for efficiency.
- **Curitiba Master Plan:** The 1965 urban planning framework that guided the city's modern development, emphasizing transit corridors, zoning, and green space.
- **Rede Integrada de Transporte (RIT):** Curitiba's integrated public transport network, centered on its BRT system.
- **Araucaria angustifolia:** The Paraná pine, a native conifer historically vital to the region's economy and ecology.
- **Yerba mate:** A traditional caffeinated beverage from the leaves of the *Ilex paraguariensis* plant, a major historical export for Paraná.
- **Trinary Road System:** A road design featuring two one-way streets flanking a central two-lane road with an exclusive bus lane.
- **Urban Acupuncture:** A planning philosophy, advocated by Jaime Lerner, using small-scale, targeted interventions to catalyze positive urban change.
- **Human Development Index (HDI):** A composite statistic of life expectancy, education, and per capita income used to rank regions by development.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Favela Growth:** Despite its planning reputation, Curitiba faces challenges with irregular settlements. Favela population growth (12.4% between 2000-2010) outpaced the city's overall growth (10.3%).
- **Transportation Future:** While the BRT is celebrated, the city has the most cars per capita in Brazil and has long planned a metro system, with tenders announced in 2014 for a 17.6 km line.
- **Sustainability vs. Reality:** The city's "green" image is periodically scrutinized against issues like air pollution from high car ownership and the management of its river basins, which are prone to flooding.
