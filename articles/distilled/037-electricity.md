# Electricity

## Overview

Electricity is the set of physical phenomena associated with the presence and motion of matter possessing an electric charge. It is intimately related to magnetism, both being part of the unified phenomenon of electromagnetism, as described by Maxwell's equations. Electricity is fundamental to modern technology, powering everything from lighting and transport to communications and computation, and its study and application drove the Second Industrial Revolution.

## Timeline

- **c. 2750 BCE** — Ancient Egyptian texts describe electric fish as "protectors" of other fish.
- **c. 600 BCE** — Thales of Miletus observes static electricity from rubbed amber.
- **1600** — William Gilbert publishes *De Magnete*, distinguishing lodestone magnetism from static electricity; coins the term *electricus*.
- **1752** — Benjamin Franklin's kite experiment shows lightning is electrical.
- **1800** — Alessandro Volta invents the voltaic pile (battery), providing a reliable electrical energy source.
- **1819–1820** — Ørsted and Ampère recognize electromagnetism, the unity of electric and magnetic phenomena.
- **1821** — Michael Faraday invents the electric motor.
- **1831** — Faraday discovers electromagnetic induction and builds the first electrical generator.
- **1861–1862** — James Clerk Maxwell links electricity, magnetism, and light in "On Physical Lines of Force."
- **1887** — Heinrich Hertz discovers the photoelectric effect.
- **1947** — John Bardeen and Walter Brattain invent the first working transistor at Bell Labs.

## Body

### History

Long before theoretical knowledge existed, people knew of shocks from electric fish; ancient Egyptian texts from 2750 BCE described them, and later Greek, Roman, and Arab writers like Pliny the Elder attested to their numbing effect, sometimes directing patients with gout or headache to touch them. Ancient Mediterranean cultures also knew that rubbed amber attracted light objects. Thales of Miletus (c. 600 BCE) believed friction rendered amber magnetic—incorrectly, though later science proved a link between magnetism and electricity.

Electricity remained a curiosity until 1600, when William Gilbert's *De Magnete* distinguished the lodestone effect from static electricity, coining *electricus* (from Greek *elektron*, "amber"), which gave rise to the English words "electric" and "electricity" (first in print in Thomas Browne's 1646 *Pseudodoxia Epidemica*). Work continued in the 17th–18th centuries with Otto von Guericke, Robert Boyle, Stephen Gray, and C. F. du Fay. In 1752, Benjamin Franklin's kite experiment showed lightning was electrical; he also explained the Leyden jar (a charge-storing device) using positive and negative charges. In 1791, Luigi Galvani demonstrated that electricity was the medium by which neurons signal muscles. Volta's battery of 1800 provided a reliable source of electrical energy. Ørsted and Ampère recognized electromagnetism in 1819–1820; Faraday invented the electric motor in 1821; Georg Ohm mathematically analyzed circuits in 1827; and Maxwell definitively linked electricity, magnetism, and light in 1861–1862. The late 19th century saw electricity turned into an essential tool by figures including Edison, Tesla, Westinghouse, and Swan. In 1887, Hertz discovered the photoelectric effect; Einstein explained it in 1905 via quantized light energy, leading to the quantum revolution and his 1921 Nobel Prize. The first solid-state device, the "cat's-whisker detector," appeared in the 1900s; the first working transistor followed in 1947.

### Electric charge

By modern convention, electrons carry negative charge and protons positive; Franklin had earlier defined positive charge as that acquired by a rubbed glass rod. A proton carries exactly 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, defined as the elementary charge; no object can have a smaller charge, and all charges are multiples of it. Charge exists in matter and antimatter (antiparticles bear equal and opposite charge). Charges exert forces on each other: like charges repel, opposite charges attract, as deduced by Charles-Augustin de Coulomb in the late 18th century. The force magnitude follows Coulomb's law, an inverse-square relation; the electromagnetic force is second only to the strong interaction in strength but operates over all distances, and is 10⁴² times stronger than gravity between two electrons. Charge is a conserved quantity: net charge in an isolated system remains constant. "Static electricity" refers to a net charge imbalance, usually from rubbing dissimilar materials. Charge can be measured with instruments like the gold-leaf electroscope.

### Electric current and field

Electric current is the movement of electric charge, measured in amperes. By convention, positive current flows from the most positive to the most negative part of a circuit (conventional current), opposite to electron flow. Current flows through conductors but not insulators. Conduction can involve electrons in metals or ions in liquids/plasmas (electrolysis). While particles drift slowly, the driving electric field propagates near light speed. Current produces observable effects: electrolysis (discovered 1800 by Nicholson and Carlisle), resistive heating (studied by Joule in 1840), and magnetism (Ørsted's 1820 discovery). Current is described as direct current (DC, unidirectional) or alternating current (AC, reversing direction, usually as a sine wave); AC is affected by inductance and capacitance.

The electric field concept was introduced by Faraday: a charged body creates a field in surrounding space exerting force on other charges. Like gravity, it extends to infinity with an inverse-square relationship, but can attract or repel. Field strength is force per unit charge on a stationary "test charge"; it is a vector field. Electrostatics studies fields from stationary charges, visualized by imaginary field lines that originate at positive charges, terminate at negative charges, enter conductors at right angles, and never cross. A hollow conductor carries all charge on its outer surface, with zero field inside—the principle of the Faraday cage. Electrostatics matters in high-voltage design: beyond a medium's field limit, electrical breakdown occurs (air arcs at ~30 kV/cm over small gaps). Lightning results when charge separation in clouds raises the field beyond what air can withstand; a large lightning cloud may reach 100 MV and 250 kWh. Sharp points concentrate fields, exploited in lightning conductors.

### Electric potential, electromagnets, and circuits

Electric potential is the energy required to bring a unit test charge from infinity to a point, measured in volts (one volt = one joule per coulomb). More useful is potential difference between two points; the field is conservative, so path is irrelevant. The Earth serves as a common reference point ("ground"). Potential is a scalar, analogous to height; equipotential lines cross field lines at right angles and lie parallel to conductor surfaces. The electric field is the local gradient of potential, expressed in volts per metre.

Ørsted's 1821 discovery that a magnetic field surrounds a current-carrying wire revealed a direct electricity–magnetism link, with the force acting at right angles to the wire. Ampère found that parallel wires with currents in the same direction attract, opposite directions repel—the basis for the international definition of the ampere. This led to Faraday's 1821 electric motor and his 1831 discovery of electromagnetic induction: a potential difference is induced proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux (Faraday's law). He built the first generator, though his disc was inefficient.

An electric circuit is a closed path for charge flow, containing components like resistors, capacitors, switches, and transformers. The resistor resists current, dissipating energy as heat; Ohm's law states current is proportional to potential difference. The capacitor (a development of the Leyden jar) stores charge in an electric field between conducting plates separated by a dielectric; it blocks steady current. The inductor (usually a coil) stores energy in a magnetic field and opposes rapidly changing currents; its unit, the henry, is named after Joseph Henry.

### Electric power, electronics, and electromagnetic waves

Electric power is the rate of energy transfer, measured in watts (joules per second), given by P = IV (power = current × voltage). Electricity is sold by the kilowatt hour (3.6 MJ). Unlike fossil fuels, electricity is a low-entropy energy form convertible to motion or other forms with high efficiency.

Electronics deals with circuits using active components (vacuum tubes, transistors, diodes, integrated circuits). The nonlinear behaviour of active components enables digital switching, used in information processing and telecommunications. Most modern devices use semiconductor components; a modern integrated circuit may contain billions of transistors in a few square centimetres.

Faraday's and Ampère's work showed time-varying magnetic fields create electric fields and vice versa—these variations are electromagnetic waves. Maxwell's 1864 equations unified electric field, magnetic field, charge, and current, proving such waves travel at light speed in vacuum and that light itself is electromagnetic radiation.

### Generation, transmission, and applications

Electricity production began with Thales' amber experiments (triboelectric effect), but viable sources came only with the voltaic pile. Modern power is generated by electro-mechanical generators driven by steam (from fossil fuels or nuclear heat) or directly by wind or water; the steam turbine (invented by Charles Parsons, 1884) remains in use. Solar panels use the photovoltaic effect. Demand grows rapidly with modernization—the US showed 12% annual growth in the early 20th century, a rate now seen in India and China. Environmental concerns, especially climate change, have driven a shift toward renewables like wind and solar.

The late-19th-century transformer enabled efficient high-voltage, low-current transmission, allowing centralized power stations. Storage is difficult, so supply must match demand; variable renewables make this harder, increasing the role of storage (batteries, chemical, thermal, mechanical). Applications include lighting (practical incandescent bulbs from the 1870s), heating, motors (including electric vehicles), and telecommunications (transcontinental telegraphs by the 1860s enabled communication in minutes across the globe).

### Electricity and the natural world

A voltage applied to the human body causes current through tissues; perception threshold is about 0.1–1 mA at mains frequency. Higher currents cause muscle contraction, heart fibrillation, and burns. Electricity can be used as torture or for judicial execution (electrocution, now rare in US states). In nature, electricity appears as lightning; many macroscopic interactions (touch, friction, chemical bonding) are due to atomic-scale electric fields. The Earth's magnetic field comes from circulating currents in the core. Piezoelectricity (discovered 1880 by Pierre and Jacques Curie) generates voltage when crystals like quartz are pressed, and is reciprocal. Some organisms use electroreception (sharks) or generate voltages (electric fish like the electric eel, using modified muscle cells called electrocytes). All animals transmit information via action potentials—voltage pulses along cell membranes—which also coordinate activities in some plants.

## Terms

- **Electromagnetism**: The unified phenomenon of electricity and magnetism, described by Maxwell's equations.
- **Coulomb's law**: The inverse-square relation giving the force between electric charges.
- **Volt**: Unit of electric potential; one volt is the potential requiring one joule to bring one coulomb from infinity.
- **Conventional current**: Current defined as flowing from positive to negative, opposite to electron flow.
- **Electromagnetic induction**: The generation of a potential difference by a changing magnetic flux (Faraday's law).
- **Capacitor**: A device storing charge in an electric field between conducting plates separated by a dielectric.
- **Inductor**: A coil storing energy in a magnetic field, opposing rapid current changes.
- **Semiconductor**: A crystalline material (e.g., germanium) engineered to switch and amplify current, the basis of transistors.
- **Photoelectric effect**: Emission of electrons when light (in quantized packets) strikes a material; explained by Einstein in 1905.
- **Action potential**: A voltage pulse along a cell membrane used for communication between neurons and muscles.

## Debates and open questions

The source notes one debated anecdote: the claim that William Ewart Gladstone asked Faraday why electricity was valuable and Faraday replied, "One day sir, you may tax it." According to Snopes.com, this anecdote should be considered apocryphal, as it appears in no accounts by Faraday or his contemporaries and only surfaced well after his death.
