# Albrecht Dürer

## Overview
Albrecht Dürer (1471–1528) was a German painter, printmaker, and theorist whose work defined the Northern Renaissance. Based in Nuremberg, he revolutionized woodcut and engraving techniques, elevating printmaking to a major art form and distributing his reputation across Europe. His two journeys to Italy (1494–95, 1505–07) allowed him to synthesize Italian Renaissance principles—linear perspective, classical proportion, and the nude—with Northern Gothic detail and empirical naturalism. He was in contact with major Italian artists of his time, including Raphael and Giovanni Bellini. Patronized by Emperor Maximilian I from 1512, Dürer produced monumental propaganda prints, pioneering astronomical charts, and theoretical treatises on measurement, human proportion, and fortification written in German vernacular rather than Latin. His intense self-portraits and engagement with Reformation ideas cemented his status as the first Northern artist to achieve international celebrity during his lifetime.

## Timeline
- **21 May 1471** — Born in Nuremberg, son of goldsmith Albrecht Dürer the Elder.
- **1486** — Apprenticed to Michael Wolgemut, leading Nuremberg artist and woodcut producer.
- **1490–1494** — *Wanderjahre*: travels to Colmar, Basel, Strasbourg; exposed to Martin Schongauer's circle and Nikolaus Gerhaert's sculpture.
- **7 July 1494** — Marries Agnes Frey in Nuremberg.
- **1494–1495** — First journey to Italy; studies in Venice, influenced by Bellini, Mantegna, and Pollaiuolo; probably visits Padua and Mantua.
- **1498** — Publishes *Apocalypse* woodcut series (16 designs), establishing European fame.
- **1502** — Father, Albrecht Dürer the Elder, dies.
- **1504** — Engraves *Adam and Eve*, signed with full name, demonstrating mastery of burin and human proportion.
- **1505–1507** — Second Italian journey; paints *Feast of the Rosary* for San Bartolomeo, Venice; adopts blue paper for drawings.
- **1509** — Purchases house in Nuremberg from the heirs of astronomer Bernhard Walther.
- **1512** — Enters service of Emperor Maximilian I; begins *Triumphal Arch* (192 blocks).
- **1513–1514** — Creates "Master Engravings": *Knight, Death and the Devil*, *St. Jerome in His Study*, *Melencolia I*.
- **1514** — Mother, Barbara Holper, dies.
- **1515** — Produces first printed western star charts (planispheres) with Johannes Stabius and Konrad Heinfogel; woodcut of *Rhinoceros*.
- **1520–1521** — Journey to Netherlands to secure pension from Charles V; keeps detailed diary of print sales and meetings.
- **1525** — Publishes *Four Books on Measurement*, first German-language mathematics book for adults.
- **1527** — Publishes *Book on Fortification*.
- **1528** — Dies in Nuremberg, 6 April; *Four Books on Human Proportion* published posthumously.

## Body

### Early Formation and Wanderjahre
Dürer trained initially in his father’s goldsmith workshop, learning the burin technique essential for engraving. At fifteen, he apprenticed with Michael Wolgemut, whose workshop produced the woodcuts for the *Nuremberg Chronicle* (1493). Nuremberg’s status as a publishing hub with Italian trade links provided early exposure to Southern art. During his *Wanderjahre* (1490–94), Dürer sought Martin Schongauer in Colmar but arrived after his death; he worked with Schongauer’s brothers and encountered the sculpture of Nikolaus Gerhaert in Strasbourg. His 1493 painted self-portrait (Louvre), likely sent to his fiancée Agnes Frey, marks the beginning of a lifelong practice of self-dramatization.

### Italian Synthesis and the Print Revolution
Dürer’s first Italian trip (1494–95) was transformative. In Venice, he studied Giovanni Bellini (whom he called the best artist there), Antonio del Pollaiuolo’s anatomical studies, and Andrea Mantegna’s engravings, copying the latter directly. He probably also visited Padua and Mantua on this trip. Returning to Nuremberg in 1495, he opened a workshop—marriage being a guild requirement—and integrated Italian perspective and proportion into Northern Gothic foundations. His *Apocalypse* (1498), *Great Passion*, and *Life of the Virgin* woodcuts were larger, more complex, and better composed than previous German prints. Though he likely did not cut blocks himself, his goldsmith training gave him precise control over the medium. Simultaneously, he mastered engraving: *Nemesis* (1502), *Saint Eustace* (c. 1501), and *Adam and Eve* (1504) expanded tonal range and textural subtlety. Watercolours like *Young Hare* (1502) and *Great Piece of Turf* (1503) shifted from topographical recording to atmospheric capture.

### Imperial Patronage and Masterworks
From 1512, Maximilian I became Dürer’s principal patron. Dürer designed the *Triumphal Arch* (192 blocks), *Triumphal Procession*, and marginalia for the Emperor’s prayer book, collaborating with scholars Johannes Stabius, Willibald Pirckheimer, and architect Jörg Kölderer. Maximilian’s cash shortages contrasted with his high regard for artists—famously holding a ladder for Dürer when a noble refused. During 1507–1520, Dürer produced major paintings (*Adam and Eve* 1507, *Assumption of the Virgin* 1509, *Adoration of the Trinity* 1511) and published collected woodcut editions (1511). Complaining that painting paid poorly relative to time, he ceased painting 1513–16, focusing on the three "Master Engravings" (1513–14). *Melencolia I* (1514), dense with geometric tools and a magic square, epitomizes his fusion of art, mathematics, and Neoplatonic philosophy. He also produced the first printed celestial maps (1515) and the *Rhinoceros* woodcut (1515), based on a description, which remained a scientific reference for centuries.

### Theoretical Works: Mathematics in the Vernacular
Dürer wrote treatises in German using craftsmen’s terminology (e.g., *Schneckenlinie* for spiral) to bypass Latin, expanding German technical prose. *Four Books on Measurement* (1525) covered linear geometry (helices, conchoids), planar polygons (favouring Ptolemy over Euclid), applications to architecture and typography (geometric construction of Latin and Gothic alphabets), and polyhedra (Platonic and Archimedean solids plus original forms). *Four Books on Human Proportion* (1528, posthumous) synthesized Vitruvius, empirical measurement of "two to three hundred living persons," and an Albertian system from Francesco di Giorgio. The appended aesthetic essay rejected Leon Battista Alberti’s objective beauty for a relativist view based on variety, evolving from spontaneous inspiration to "selective inward synthesis"—the artist’s imagination built on accumulated visual experience. *Book on Fortification* (1527) applied geometry to military architecture, translated into Latin by 1535.

### Reformation Engagement and Final Years
Dürer’s diary (1520) expresses desire to portray Martin Luther, and he received Luther’s *Babylonian Captivity* from Cornelius Grapheus. Pirckheimer’s 1530 letter notes their early sympathy for Luther but later disillusionment. Dürer may have influenced Nuremberg’s 1525 mandate for Lutheran services, though his *Last Supper* woodcut (1523) and delayed *St. Philip* engraving (1523/26) suggest complex, evolving views on religious imagery. After returning from the Netherlands (1521) with a chronic illness, Dürer produced few paintings—*Four Apostles* (1526), given to Nuremberg—and focused on theoretical works and portrait engravings (Erasmus, Melanchthon, Pirckheimer). He died in 1528, leaving a substantial estate (6,874 florins) and a house (purchased in 1509 from the heirs of the astronomer Bernhard Walther) that remains a Nuremberg landmark.

## Terms
- **Burin**: A steel cutting tool with a sharp beveled point, used for engraving copper plates; Dürer learned its use in goldsmithing.
- **Woodcut**: Relief printing technique where the design is cut into a wood block; Dürer raised its complexity and tonal range.
- **Engraving**: Intaglio technique where lines are incised into a metal plate with a burin; Dürer’s preferred later medium for tonal subtlety.
- **Linear perspective**: Mathematical system for depicting three-dimensional space on a flat surface; Dürer studied it in Italy and codified it in German.
- **Human proportion**: Canonical ratios governing body measurements; Dürer’s treatises combined Vitruvius, empirical study, and Albertian systems.
- **Planisphere**: A flat map of the celestial sphere; Dürer, Stabius, and Heinfogel produced the first printed northern and southern hemispheres (1515).
- **Chiaroscuro modelling**: Use of light and shadow to create volume; Dürer developed this in post-Venetian woodcuts via a mid-tone block.
- **Melencolia I**: 1514 engraving personifying melancholy as a winged genius surrounded by geometric tools; emblematic of the artist-intellectual.
- **Selective inward synthesis**: Dürer’s mature aesthetic theory: beautiful images arise from the artist’s imagination recombining stored visual experience.
- **Triumphal Arch**: Monumental 192-block woodcut (1515–17) glorifying Maximilian I; designed by Dürer, cut by Hieronymus Andreae.

## Debates and open questions
- **Marriage and sexuality**: Dürer’s unhappy marriage to Agnes Frey (no children, hostile contemporary accounts) and homoerotic themes in *The Men’s Bath* fuel debate over his sexual orientation.
- **Reformation allegiance**: Dürer expressed sympathy for Luther and may have aided Nuremberg’s 1525 Lutheran mandate, but never formally left the Catholic Church; his late works (*Last Supper*, delayed *St. Philip*) show ambiguous Protestant leanings.
- **Viennese murals**: A 2020 discovery of handwriting at St. Stephen’s Cathedral, Vienna, and a 19th-century legend (depicted by August Siegert) suggest Dürer painted murals there c. 1505, possibly for Maximilian before documented service (1512).
- **Workshop attribution**: The extent of Dürer’s hand in block-cutting versus design, and the authorship of workshop productions like the *Berlin Picture Book* (fencing manual), remain uncertain.
- **Aesthetic theory**: Scholars debate whether Dürer’s "selective inward synthesis" represents a coherent epistemology or an evolving, unresolved struggle to define beauty without classical authority.
