# Jewish history

## Overview
The history of the Jews traces the development of the Jewish people and religion from their origins among the Israelites of the ancient Levant to the present day. It is a narrative of ancient kingdoms, exile, diaspora, cultural flourishing, persecution, and the eventual establishment of a modern state, shaping a distinct religious and ethnic identity across millennia.

## Timeline
- **c. 1200 BCE** — Earliest mention of "Israelites" on the Merneptah Stele.
- **c. 930 BCE** — Kingdom splits into Israel (north) and Judah (south).
- **720 BCE** — Kingdom of Israel falls to the Neo-Assyrian Empire.
- **586 BCE** — Kingdom of Judah falls to the Neo-Babylonian Empire; Temple destroyed; Babylonian Exile begins.
- **516 BCE** — Second Temple completed under Persian rule.
- **167–160 BCE** — Maccabean Revolt leads to independent Hasmonean kingdom.
- **70 CE** — Romans destroy the Second Temple after the Great Revolt.
- **135 CE** — Bar Kokhba revolt crushed; Jews banned from Jerusalem; province renamed Syria Palaestina.
- **1492** — Expulsion of Jews from Spain by Catholic Monarchs.
- **1897** — First Zionist Congress officially founds the Zionist movement.
- **1933–1945** — The Holocaust: Nazi genocide of approximately six million Jews.
- **1948** — Declaration of the State of Israel.

## Body

### Ancient Origins and Kingdoms
Jews originated from the Israelites of the Iron Age kingdoms of Israel and Judah in the Levant. The earliest archaeological mention of Israel is on the Merneptah Stele (c. 1213–1203 BCE). While Jewish tradition traces lineage to the patriarch Jacob (renamed Israel), modern scholarship indicates the Israelites emerged from Canaanite peoples, developing a distinct monotheistic religion centered on Yahweh. The northern Kingdom of Israel fell to Assyria (c. 720 BCE), and the southern Kingdom of Judah fell to Babylon (586 BCE), with the destruction of the First Temple and the exile of the elite to Babylon marking the start of the Jewish diaspora.

### Second Temple Period and Diaspora
Under Persian rule, exiles returned and rebuilt the Temple (completed 516 BCE). After Hellenistic and Hasmonean periods, Rome conquered Judea (63 BCE). The destruction of the Second Temple by Rome in 70 CE, following the Great Revolt, was a cataclysm that shifted Judaism from Temple-based sacrifice to Rabbinic Judaism centered on prayer and Torah study. The failed Bar Kokhba revolt (132–136 CE) led to further dispersion and the renaming of the province to Syria Palaestina. Diaspora communities flourished in Babylonia, where the Babylonian Talmud was compiled, and across the Roman Empire.

### Medieval Period: Islamic and Christian Lands
Under early Islamic rule (from 638 CE), Jews often experienced a "golden age," particularly in Moorish Spain (c. 711–1031), contributing to science and philosophy. In Christian Europe, Jews faced periodic persecution, massacres during the Crusades, and expulsions from England (1290), France (1396), and Spain (1492). The Spanish expulsion forced a massive migration to the Ottoman Empire, North Africa, and other regions, creating the Sephardic diaspora. Meanwhile, Ashkenazi Jews developed distinct communities in Central and Eastern Europe.

### Early Modern and Modern Periods
The 18th-century Haskalah (Jewish Enlightenment) promoted integration and secular learning, while Hasidic Judaism emphasized mysticism and piety. Jewish emancipation spread in 19th-century Europe, but so did new racial antisemitism. Pogroms in Eastern Europe spurred mass migration to the United States and renewed interest in Zionism—the movement for a Jewish homeland in Palestine. The British Balfour Declaration (1917) supported a "National Home" there.

### The Holocaust and the State of Israel
The Nazi rise to power (1933) led to the Holocaust, the systematic murder of six million Jews. Post-war, Jewish immigration to British Mandate Palestine intensified. Following the UN Partition Plan (1947) and the end of the British Mandate, David Ben-Gurion declared the establishment of the State of Israel on May 14, 1948. It was immediately invaded by neighboring Arab states. Israel has since developed into a parliamentary democracy, absorbing waves of Jewish immigrants (Aliyah) from Europe, the Middle East, and elsewhere.

## Terms
- **Diaspora**: The dispersion of Jews beyond the Land of Israel.
- **Hasmonean dynasty**: An independent Jewish kingdom (165–63 BCE) established after the Maccabean Revolt.
- **Rabbinic Judaism**: The form of Judaism that emerged after the Temple's destruction, based on the Oral Torah, prayer, and study.
- **Talmud**: Central texts of Rabbinic Judaism, comprising the Mishnah (oral law) and Gemara (rabbinic analysis).
- **Ashkenazi**: Jews whose ancestors settled in Central and Eastern Europe.
- **Sephardi**: Jews whose ancestors settled in the Iberian Peninsula and later the Ottoman Empire.
- **Mizrahi**: Jews whose ancestors lived in the Middle East and North Africa.
- **Zionism**: The modern political movement for the re-establishment of a Jewish homeland in Palestine.
- **Haskalah**: The Jewish Enlightenment movement of the 18th–19th centuries, promoting secular education and integration.
- **Aliyah**: Immigration of Jews to the Land of Israel.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The historicity of the United Monarchy of David and Solomon is debated among scholars.
- The causes and scale of the transition from Canaanite to Israelite culture in the Iron Age are subjects of ongoing archaeological and historical research.
- The precise nature and extent of Jewish autonomy under various rulers (e.g., in the Ottoman Empire) is analyzed through different historical lenses.
