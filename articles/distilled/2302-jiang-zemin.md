# Jiang Zemin

## Overview
Jiang Zemin was the paramount leader of China from 1989 to 2002, serving as General Secretary of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), President, and Chairman of the Central Military Commission. His tenure was marked by the consolidation of CCP rule after the 1989 Tiananmen Square protests, accelerated economic reforms, and China's integration into the global economy. He is considered the core leader of the third generation of Chinese leadership.

## Timeline
- **17 August 1926** — Born in Yangzhou, Jiangsu.
- **1947** — Graduated from National Chiao Tung University with a degree in electrical engineering; joined the CCP in college.
- **20 May 1989** — Appointed CCP General Secretary as a compromise candidate after the ousting of Zhao Ziyang.
- **9 November 1989** — Appointed Chairman of the Central Military Commission, succeeding Deng Xiaoping.
- **1992** — Coined the term "socialist market economy" at the 14th CCP National Congress, accelerating reforms.
- **27 March 1993** — Became President of the People's Republic of China.
- **1997** — Oversaw the handover of Hong Kong from the United Kingdom.
- **1999** — Oversaw the handover of Macau from Portugal; initiated a crackdown on Falun Gong.
- **2001** — China joined the World Trade Organization (WTO); signed the Treaty of Good-Neighborliness with Russia and co-founded the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation.
- **25 February 2000** — Introduced the "Three Represents" theory, later written into the CCP constitution.
- **November 2002** — Stepped down as CCP General Secretary, succeeded by Hu Jintao.
- **19 September 2004** — Relinquished his final post as Chairman of the Central Military Commission.
- **30 November 2022** — Died in Shanghai at age 96.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Jiang Zemin was an unlikely leader, rising through the Shanghai party apparatus. He became mayor in 1985 and party secretary in 1987. His handling of the 1989 Shanghai pro-democracy protests—balancing public dialogue with support for the central government's martial law declaration—caught the attention of paramount leader Deng Xiaoping. Following the Tiananmen Square massacre, Jiang was selected as a compromise candidate to replace the ousted Zhao Ziyang as CCP General Secretary in May 1989. He initially held a small power base and was seen as a transitional figure.

### Leadership and Domestic Policy
Jiang consolidated power by the early 1990s, becoming the "core leader." After Deng Xiaoping's 1992 southern tour urged faster reforms, Jiang fully embraced the "socialist market economy" model. His administration oversaw the breaking of the "iron rice bowl" (guaranteed state employment), privatized many state-owned enterprises, and initiated major infrastructure projects like the Three Gorges Dam. He promoted a "revitalize the country through science and education" strategy, launching Projects 211 and 985 to improve higher education. Domestically, he launched patriotic education campaigns, established internet controls (the "Great Firewall"), and in 1999, created the extralegal 610 Office to crack down on Falun Gong.

### Economic and Foreign Policy
Jiang's tenure saw sustained high GDP growth and China's entry into the WTO in 2001, which spurred further economic integration. He launched the "Going Global" policy to expand Chinese investment abroad. Foreign policy was largely pragmatic, following Deng's "hide your strength, bide your time" doctrine. Relations with Russia deepened, culminating in the 2001 Treaty of Good-Neighborliness. Tensions with the United States were frequent, marked by the 1996 Taiwan Strait Crisis, the 1999 Belgrade embassy bombing, and the 2001 Hainan island incident. Jiang oversaw the peaceful returns of Hong Kong (1997) and Macau (1999).

### Three Represents and Retirement
In 2000, Jiang introduced the "Three Represents" theory, which broadened the CCP's base to include private entrepreneurs and was written into the party constitution in 2002. He began transferring power to Hu Jintao in 2002 but retained the powerful Central Military Commission chairmanship until 2004, a period of dual leadership. His political faction, the "Shanghai clique," continued to exert influence. Jiang died in 2022 after a long retirement, during which he made occasional public appearances.

## Terms
- **Paramount Leader**: The most powerful political figure in the People's Republic of China, a position not defined by a single office but by holding key party, state, and military posts.
- **Core Leader**: A title denoting the central figure of a generation of leadership, conferring significant authority and influence within the CCP.
- **Socialist Market Economy**: The official economic system of China, combining state-owned enterprises and central planning with market mechanisms and private enterprise.
- **Iron Rice Bowl**: A term for the guaranteed lifetime employment and benefits provided by state-owned enterprises, which Jiang's reforms sought to dismantle.
- **Three Represents**: Jiang's political theory stating the CCP should represent advanced productive forces, advanced culture, and the fundamental interests of the majority of the Chinese people, justifying the inclusion of capitalists in the party.
- **Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO)**: A political, economic, and security alliance founded in 2001 by China, Russia, and four Central Asian states.
- **610 Office**: An extralegal security agency established in 1999 to coordinate the persecution of Falun Gong.
- **Going Global**: A national strategy launched in 1999 to encourage Chinese companies to invest and expand internationally.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Legacy of Tiananmen**: Jiang's rise was a direct result of the 1989 crackdown. His legacy is debated as one of stability and growth versus one that entrenched authoritarian rule and avoided political reform.
- **Three Represents**: Critics, including party hardliners, denounced it as a betrayal of communist ideology that legitimized capitalist interests within the party for political expediency.
- **Power Transition**: His retention of the military chairmanship after 2002 created an unusual period of dual leadership with Hu Jintao, seen by some as an attempt to maintain influence "from behind the curtain."
- **Corruption and Cronyism**: His tenure is associated with the rise of powerful interest groups and corruption, exemplified by the later downfall of his military allies, Xu Caihou and Guo Boxiong.
- **Self-Deification**: Historian Yang Jisheng argued Jiang's push to enshrine the "Three Represents" in the constitution was an attempt at "self-deification," placing himself alongside Mao and Deng.
