# Lake Maracaibo

## Overview
Lake Maracaibo is a large, ancient body of water in northwestern Venezuela, often classified as a brackish estuary or semi-enclosed bay due to its connection to the Gulf of Venezuela. It is one of the oldest lakes on Earth, formed 36 million years ago, and is a vital economic region for Venezuela, being the country's primary oil-producing area and a major fishing and agricultural hub. The lake is also renowned for hosting the world's most frequent lightning, the Catatumbo lightning.

## Body

### Geology and Hydrology
Lake Maracaibo is located in a faulted basin between the Perija and Merida mountain ranges. Formed 36 million years ago, it is one of Earth's oldest lakes. Its current shape was established in the Pliocene epoch. The lake is vase-shaped, covering 13,512 km², with a maximum depth of 35 meters. It is fed by numerous rivers, most significantly the Catatumbo River, which supplies 57% of the inflow. The water is brackish, with salinity higher in the north due to tidal influence from the Gulf of Venezuela and lower in the south due to freshwater input. A narrow strait connects it to the Gulf.

### Climate and Lightning
The lake area has an average annual temperature of 28°C. Precipitation is higher in the south (1,400 mm annually) than the north. The region is famous for the Catatumbo lightning, a meteorological phenomenon occurring over the southern lake. This near-continuous, almost silent lightning averages 233 strikes per km² per year and occurs on about 297 nights annually, peaking in September with up to 280 strikes per hour. It is so frequent it historically illuminated nighttime navigation.

### Human History and Industry
The lake was originally home to the Añú people, who called it Coquivacoa. European contact began in 1499. The lake is Venezuela's "oil lake," with large-scale exploitation starting in 1922. Oil fields are concentrated in the northeast and northwest. The city of Maracaibo, on the northwest coast, is a major oil port. The lake supports over 20,000 fishermen and is a key agricultural area for crops like bananas and coffee. The General Rafael Urdaneta Bridge, completed in 1962, connects the lake's shores. The lake district's population grew from about 300,000 in 1936 to over 3.62 million in 2007.

### Ecology and Contamination
The lake is biologically diverse, with highly oxygenated waters supporting algae and fish. It is home to about 145 fish species, including several endemics, and endangered mammals like the West Indian manatee. However, it faces severe environmental problems. Eutrophication from agricultural sewage, domestic wastewater, and oil pollution causes blooms of duckweed and algae. Aging oil infrastructure has led to numerous spills. Dredging the lake mouth in the 1930s-50s increased salinity by up to 1,000% in the north. A major duckweed bloom in 2004, triggered by heavy rains, covered 18% of the lake and cost millions to clean.

## Terms
- **Brackish water**: Water that has more salinity than fresh water, but not as much as seawater, often due to mixing.
- **Eutrophication**: Excessive richness of nutrients in a body of water, frequently due to runoff from the land, which causes a dense growth of plant life and death of animal life from lack of oxygen.
- **Catatumbo lightning**: A natural phenomenon occurring over the mouth of the Catatumbo River where it empties into Lake Maracaibo, characterized by a high frequency of lightning.
- **Endemic species**: A species that is native to and found only in a specific, limited geographic area.
- **Dredging**: The process of removing sediments and debris from the bottom of lakes, rivers, harbors, and other water bodies.
- **Tertiary sandstone**: A sedimentary rock layer from the Tertiary period (66 million to 2.6 million years ago), often a reservoir for oil and gas.
- **Cretaceous limestone**: A sedimentary rock layer from the Cretaceous period (145 to 66 million years ago), another common hydrocarbon reservoir.

## Debates and open questions
The source notes a classification debate: while commonly called a lake, its hydrological characteristics may better classify it as an estuary or semi-enclosed bay. The environmental impact of the extensive oil infrastructure and ongoing pollution remains a critical and unresolved issue for the lake's ecosystem.
