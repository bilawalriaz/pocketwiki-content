# Shandong

## Overview
Shandong is a coastal province in East China, pivotal to Chinese civilization as a cultural and religious center for Taoism, Buddhism, and Confucianism. Its strategic location at ancient trade routes has made it an economic powerhouse, and it is now China's second-most populous province and third-largest provincial economy.

## Timeline
- **c. 6500–1500 BC** — Succession of Neolithic cultures (Houli, Beixin, Dawenkou, Longshan, Yueshi) flourishes in the region.
- **567 BC** — The state of Qi annexes the Dongyi state of Lai, beginning the sinicization of eastern Shandong.
- **221 BC** — The Qin dynasty conquers Qi, founding the first centralized Chinese state.
- **1168 AD** — The Jin dynasty establishes the Shandong East and West Circuits, the first use of the name "Shandong."
- **1376 AD** — The Ming dynasty moves Shandong's capital from Qingzhou to Jinan.
- **1898 AD** — Germany leases Jiaozhou Bay and Qingdao; Britain leases Weihaiwei.
- **1919 AD** — The Shandong Problem (transfer of German holdings to Japan) sparks the May Fourth Movement.
- **1922 AD** — Shandong reverts to Chinese control after U.S. mediation.
- **1949 AD** — Communist forces take full control of Shandong Province.
- **1996 AD** — Shandong becomes the first province in China to achieve full household electrification.
- **2008 AD** — Qingdao hosts the sailing competitions for the Summer Olympic Games.
- **2021 AD** — Shandong's GDP reaches CN¥8.3 trillion (US$1.3 trillion), equivalent to Mexico's economy. |

## Body

### Etymology and Historical Identity
The name "Shandong" (山东) means "east of the mountain," referring to its location east of the Taihang Mountains. A common nickname is "Qilu" (齐鲁), from the Spring and Autumn period states of Qi and Lu. While Qi was a major political power, Lu became culturally renowned as the home of Confucius, and its cultural legacy is reflected in the province's official abbreviation, Lǔ (鲁).

### Ancient and Imperial History
Shandong was home to a succession of Neolithic cultures. During the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, it was dominated by the states of Qi and Lu. After unification under the Qin dynasty, the region was administered through various commanderies and circuits. It was a site of significant Buddhist activity, as evidenced by the 412 AD landing of the monk Faxian and the 1996 discovery of buried Buddhist statues in Qingzhou. The name "Shandong" was formally adopted during the Jin dynasty (1168). The modern province was established by the Ming dynasty, with Jinan as its capital since 1376.

### Late Qing and Modern Era
Shandong was heavily affected by Western influence after the Opium Wars. Germany leased Qingdao in 1898, and Britain leased Weihaiwei. The province was a center of the Boxer Rebellion. After World War I, the transfer of German holdings to Japan (the Shandong Problem) triggered the May Fourth Movement. Shandong reverted to Chinese control in 1922. During the Second Sino-Japanese War and subsequent civil war, the province saw significant conflict, with Communist forces gaining full control by 1949.

### People's Republic Era
Under Communist rule, Shandong suffered during the Great Chinese Famine (1959–62). The Cultural Revolution began in the province with the 1966 "Qingdao Incident." Since the Reform and Opening Up (post-1978), Shandong has experienced rapid economic growth. It became the first province to achieve full household electrification (1996) and the top province in industrial strength (2004). Major events include hosting Olympic sailing (2008) and the establishment of the China (Shandong) Pilot Free Trade Zone (2019).

### Geography and Climate
Shandong is on the eastern edge of the North China Plain and includes the Shandong Peninsula. Its highest peak is Jade Emperor Peak (1,545 m) in the Mount Tai range. The Yellow River flows through its western areas and enters the sea on its northern coast. The climate is temperate, humid continental (Köppen Dwa), with hot, rainy summers and cold, dry winters.

### Economy
Shandong has China's third-largest provincial economy (GDP of CN¥8.3 trillion in 2021). It is a leading agricultural producer (especially wheat, cotton, and garlic) and the largest agricultural exporter in China. The province is rich in mineral resources, including gold, sulfur, and gypsum. Key industries include petroleum (Shengli Oil Field), manufacturing, and a major wine industry centered in Yantai. The service sector contributed 49.5% of GDP by 2018.

### Demographics and Culture
With over 101.5 million people (2020 census), Shandong is China's second-most populous province. The population is predominantly Han (99.11%), with the Hui being the largest ethnic minority. The province is the birthplace of Confucius and a historic center for Taoism and Buddhism. Shandong cuisine (Lu cuisine) is one of China's eight great culinary traditions. The province has a strong educational system, hosting 153 higher education institutions.

## Terms
- **Sinicization:** The process of cultural assimilation to Han Chinese culture.
- **Dongyi:** Ancient peoples considered "barbarians" by the inhabitants of the Central Plain, who inhabited eastern Shandong.
- **Spring and Autumn period:** A period in Chinese history (771–476 BC) of fragmentation and the rise of regional states.
- **Commandery (jun):** A historical administrative division in China, similar to a prefecture.
- **Circuit (lu):** A political division used during the Tang and Song dynasties.
- **Boxer Rebellion:** An anti-foreign, anti-colonial, and anti-Christian uprising in China (1899–1901).
- **May Fourth Movement:** A cultural and political movement (1919) sparked by the Shandong Problem, protesting the Treaty of Versailles.
- **Reform and Opening Up:** Economic and political reforms launched in 1978 that liberalized China's economy.
- **Double First-Class Construction:** A Chinese government initiative to develop world-class universities and disciplines.
