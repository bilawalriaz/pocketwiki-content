# Geographic information system

## Overview
A Geographic Information System (GIS) is an integrated system of computer hardware, software, and data used to store, manage, analyze, and visualize geographic information. It uses location as a key index variable to relate disparate datasets, enabling spatial analysis and decision-making across numerous fields like urban planning, environmental science, and business. GIS is foundational to location intelligence and has evolved from specialized government tools to widespread commercial and public applications.

## Timeline
- **1832** — Charles Picquet creates a cholera mortality map of Paris, an early use of spatial analysis.
- **1854** — John Snow uses spatial analysis to pinpoint the source of a London cholera outbreak.
- **1963** — Roger Tomlinson develops the Canada Geographic Information System (CGIS), the first true operational GIS.
- **1964** — Howard Fisher forms the Harvard Laboratory for Computer Graphics and Spatial Analysis, developing influential software like SYMAP.
- **1969** — Tom Waugh develops the GIMMS software, a pioneering vector-based GIS.
- **1986** — MIDAS, the first desktop GIS product, is released.
- **1990s** — Commercial GIS vendors (Esri, Intergraph, etc.) emerge; public domain systems (GRASS GIS) are developed.
- **Late 1990s** — GIS begins moving to servers and the Internet, enabling distributed and web-based GIS.
- **21st Century** — Major trends include integration with cloud computing, mobile technology, and open-source software.

## Body

### History and Development
While digital GIS began in the mid-1960s, its conceptual foundations in spatial analysis date to the 19th century with epidemiological mapping. The first true operational GIS, the Canada Geographic Information System (CGIS), was developed in 1963 by Roger Tomlinson for land resource analysis. Concurrently, academic labs like Harvard's LCGSA developed influential software (e.g., SYMAP, ODYSSEY) that shaped future commercial systems. By the 1980s, commercial vendors like Esri and Intergraph emerged, and the 1990s saw the rise of desktop and Internet-based GIS, moving the technology from research departments into broader business and public use.

### GIS Software and Data Management
GIS software refers to general-purpose applications (e.g., Esri's ArcGIS, QGIS) used across many systems, distinct from a singular, custom GIS installation. The core of any GIS is a spatial database containing geographic data, which models phenomena as either discrete objects (e.g., roads) or continuous fields (e.g., rainfall). Data is commonly stored in raster (grid cells) or vector (points, lines, polygons) formats. Data acquisition involves primary capture (e.g., GPS, remote sensing), secondary capture (e.g., digitizing paper maps), and data transfer from external sources.

### Spatial Analysis and Cartography
GIS spatial analysis includes geoprocessing operations like overlay, proximity analysis, and network analysis. Terrain analysis derives information such as slope and aspect from elevation data. Geostatistics uses interpolation to predict values at unmeasured locations. Cartography, the design of maps, is a primary output function, now often delivered via web mapping services. These tools enable complex modeling, from hydrological simulations to multi-criteria decision analysis.

### Applications and Societal Implications
GIS is used for scientific research and resource management across human and natural domains, including archaeology, disaster response, public health, and urban planning. Its implementation can be project-based or enterprise-wide. The technology has significant societal implications, influencing education, local government operations, and public participation. It also raises issues of privacy, data accuracy, and potential misuse, as seen in its critical role during the COVID-19 pandemic for real-time monitoring.

## Terms
- **Geographic Information System (GIS):** An integrated system of hardware, software, and data for capturing, storing, analyzing, and displaying geographically referenced information.
- **Spatial Database:** A database optimized for storing and querying data that represents objects defined in a geometric space.
- **Raster Data:** A data model that represents geographic space as a grid of equally sized cells (pixels), each containing a value.
- **Vector Data:** A data model that represents geographic features as discrete points, lines, and polygons with precise locations.
- **Geoprocessing:** A GIS operation that manipulates spatial data, such as overlay, buffer, or clip, to create new information.
- **Geocoding:** The process of converting street addresses or other location descriptions into geographic coordinates (latitude/longitude).
- **Remote Sensing:** The acquisition of information about an object or phenomenon without making physical contact, typically via satellite or aircraft sensors.
- **Open Geospatial Consortium (OGC):** An international standards body that develops specifications for geospatial and location-based services to ensure interoperability.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes ongoing scholarly scrutiny of the social and political implications of GIS, including its potential for misuse to distort reality. It also highlights debates around data quality, accuracy, and the propagation of uncertainty through analysis. The rise of web mapping has sparked discussion about the reliability of crowdsourced geographic data and the erosion of cartographic standards in public-facing maps.
