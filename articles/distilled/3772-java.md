# Java

## Overview
Java is the world's most populous island and the economic, political, and cultural core of Indonesia. Despite covering only 7% of Indonesia's land area, it is home to approximately 56% of the nation's population. Its history is defined by powerful Hindu-Buddhist empires, the spread of Islam, Dutch colonial rule, and the center of the Indonesian independence struggle.

## Timeline
- **c. 1.3 million years ago** — Fossilized remains of *Homo erectus* ("Java Man") are found along the Bengawan Solo River.
- **5th–7th centuries** — The Taruma kingdom exists in western Java.
- **Early 8th century** — The Mataram kingdom is founded in central Java, patronizing Hindu and Buddhist institutions.
- **Late 8th–mid-9th century** — Monumental temple complexes Borobudur and Prambanan are constructed.
- **Late 920s** — The center of power shifts from central to eastern Java.
- **1293–c. 1520s** — The Majapahit empire, claiming suzerainty over much of the archipelago, dominates eastern Java.
- **Early 16th century** — Islam becomes the dominant religion; the first Muslim kingdom, Demak, achieves hegemony.
- **1613–1646** — Sultan Agung of Mataram establishes military and cultural dominance in central and eastern Java.
- **1596** — First Dutch contact with Indonesia; colonial influence expands over the 18th century.
- **1808** — Construction of the Great Post Road begins under the French-proxy Daendels administration.
- **1811–1816** — Java is briefly a British possession under Sir Stamford Raffles before returning to the Dutch.
- **1949** — Indonesian independence, centered in Java, is internationally recognized.

## Body

### Geography and Environment
Java is a volcanic island formed by the subduction of the Australian Plate under the Sunda Plate. It is the 13th largest island globally, with an east-west spine of volcanic mountains, including 35 active volcanoes. The highest is Mount Semeru (3,676 m), and the most active is Mount Merapi (2,930 m). The fertile volcanic soils support intensive wet-rice cultivation. The natural environment ranges from tropical rainforest in the wetter west to dry savanna in the east. This biodiversity is under severe pressure from human population density, leading to the extinction of endemic species like the Javan tiger and elephant.

### Early History and Hindu-Buddhist Period
Java has been inhabited by *Homo erectus* for over a million years. The arrival of modern humans led to the development of wet-field rice cultivation and small kingdoms. The Hindu-Buddhist "Classic" period (5th–15th centuries) saw the rise of powerful states. The Mataram kingdom in central Java built major temples like Borobudur (Buddhist) and Prambanan (Hindu). Power later shifted east to kingdoms like Kediri, Singhasari, and finally Majapahit, which at its height claimed control over much of the Indonesian archipelago.

### Spread of Islam and Colonial Rule
Islam spread via north-coastal port cities in the 16th century, with Demak becoming the first hegemonic Muslim power. The inland Mataram Sultanate later became dominant, synthesizing Islam with Javanese culture. European contact began in 1522, but the Dutch East India Company (VOC) gradually established control through intervention in local wars, whittling down the Mataram kingdom. The Dutch colonial period saw Java become a major producer of rice and later commercial crops like coffee, tea, and sugar under the Cultivation System. Infrastructure like the Great Post Road and railways was built to support the colonial economy.

### Independence and Modern Era
Indonesian nationalism emerged in the early 20th century, with Java as the center of the independence struggle post-WWII. Today, Java is divided into four provinces and two special regions (Jakarta and Yogyakarta). It is Indonesia's economic powerhouse, contributing about 60% of the nation's GDP. The population is dense and predominantly urbanized, with the western third being the most crowded. The island is ethnically dominated by the Javanese and Sundanese, with significant Madurese and Betawi minorities.

## Terms
*   **Subduction:** A geological process where one tectonic plate sinks beneath another, which formed Java's volcanic mountains.
*   **Wet-rice cultivation:** A method of farming rice in flooded paddies, requiring sophisticated water management and village cooperation.
*   **Hindu-Buddhist period:** The era (5th–15th centuries) when Javanese kingdoms were influenced by and patronized Hinduism and Buddhism.
*   **Majapahit:** The last and largest Hindu-Buddhist empire in Java (1293–c. 1520s), which claimed suzerainty over much of the archipelago.
*   **Sultanate:** An Islamic state led by a sultan, such as the coastal kingdoms that rose after Majapahit.
*   **Cultivation System (Cultuurstelsel):** A Dutch colonial policy forcing Javanese farmers to devote part of their land to cash crops for export.
*   **Transmigration program:** An Indonesian government policy (1970s–1998) to resettle people from densely populated Java to less populated outer islands.
*   **Kejawen:** The dominant cultural tradition of the Javanese heartland, blending pre-Islamic, Hindu, and Islamic elements.
*   **Abangan and Santri:** Terms describing a spectrum of Islamic practice in Java, from syncretic (abangan) to more orthodox (santri).
*   **Pesantren:** Traditional Islamic boarding schools in Java, led by a *kyai* (religious teacher).

## Debates and open questions
*   The exact origin of the name "Java" is unclear, with theories linking it to the *jawa* (foxtail millet) plant, the Malay word *jauh* ("beyond"), or other roots.
*   The extent of Majapahit's direct control versus its claimed suzerainty over the Indonesian archipelago is debated.
*   The impact of the Dutch Cultivation System is debated; some sources link it to famines and epidemics in the 1840s, while others attribute later population growth to colonial infrastructure that eliminated famine.
*   The population growth in economically depressed Central Java during 2010–2020, which more than doubled the previous decade's rate, is indicative of unresolved issues like migration or the after-effects of volcanic eruptions.
