# Nostradamus

## Overview
Michel de Nostredame (1503–1566), known as Nostradamus, was a French apothecary, physician, and astrologer who gained lasting fame for *Les Prophéties* (1555), a collection of 942 poetic quatrains allegedly predicting future events. Born into a family of Jewish converts to Catholicism in Provence, he studied medicine but was expelled from the University of Montpellier for practicing the "manual trade" of apothecary. After losing his first family to the plague, he achieved success treating outbreaks and publishing annual almanacs, attracting the patronage of Catherine de' Medici. Modern scholarship demonstrates that his prophecies relied heavily on paraphrasing classical historians, medieval chroniclers, and earlier prophecy collections (notably the *Mirabilis Liber*) rather than supernatural insight. Academics reject his prophetic abilities, arguing that perceived "hits" result from vague language, retrospective interpretation (postdiction), and deliberate mistranslations. His work was exploited for propaganda by both Nazi Germany and the Allies during World War II.

## Body

### Family Background and Education
Nostradamus’s paternal family were originally Jewish grain and money dealers in Avignon. His grandfather Cresquas converted to Catholicism c. 1459–60, adopting the surname "Nostredame" (Our Lady). Michel was born in 1503 in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence to notary Jaume de Nostredame and Reynière, one of at least nine children. At 14, he entered the University of Avignon to study the *trivium* (grammar, rhetoric, logic), but the university closed after just over a year due to a plague outbreak. He then spent eight years (1521–1529) traveling and researching herbal remedies as an apothecary. In 1529, he entered the University of Montpellier seeking a medical doctorate but was expelled almost immediately by procurator Guillaume Rondelet; university statutes forbade the "manual trade" of apothecary, and he was accused of slandering doctors. The expulsion document (BIU Montpellier, Register S 2 folio 87) survives.

### Medical Career and Plague Work
After expulsion, Nostradamus continued as an apothecary, gaining fame for a "rose pill" purportedly protecting against plague. In 1531, scholar Jules-César Scaliger invited him to Agen, where he married and had two children, all of whom died in the 1534 plague. He traveled France and possibly Italy before returning in 1545 to assist prominent physician Louis Serre during a major Marseille outbreak. He subsequently managed outbreaks in Salon-de-Provence and Aix-en-Provence. In 1547, he settled permanently in Salon-de-Provence, marrying Anne Ponsarde, a rich widow who bore him six children. Between 1556 and 1567, the couple acquired a 1/13 share in Adam de Craponne’s canal project (Canal de Craponne) to irrigate the region from the Durance river.

### Turn to Occultism and the Almanacs
Following a visit to Italy, Nostradamus shifted from medicine toward the "occult." In 1550, he published his first almanac, Latinising his name to Nostradamus. Its success prompted annual publications (titled *Almanachs*, *Prognostications*, or *Presages*), collectively containing at least 6,338 prophecies and eleven calendars starting 1 January. These almanacs attracted wealthy patrons seeking horoscopes and "psychic" advice, though Nostradamus typically required clients to supply birth charts; when he calculated them himself using published tables, he frequently erred by failing to adjust for client location or birth time. Catherine de' Medici became his foremost supporter after reading his 1555 almanacs, summoning him to Paris to draw horoscopes for her children. By his death, she had appointed him Counselor and Physician-in-Ordinary to King Charles IX.

### Composition and Sources of *Les Prophéties*
Nostradamus began writing a book of 1,000 French quatrains, published in three installments as *Les Prophéties* (1555, 1557, 1558). The publisher of the third installment refused to start mid-"Century" (book of 100 verses), causing the last 58 quatrains of Century VII to be lost. To obscure meaning and avoid religious opposition, he used "Virgilianised" syntax, word games, and a mix of Greek, Italian, Latin, and Provençal. The 1568 posthumous omnibus edition contains 942 quatrains (one unrhymed) in nine Centuries of 100 and one of 42. Printing practices (typesetting from dictation) meant no two editions were identical; no proof exists that any edition's spelling or punctuation reflects Nostradamus’s originals.

Nostradamus claimed to use *judicial astrology* (assessing the "quality" of events like births or coronations via horoscopes), but professional astrologers like Laurens Videl criticized his incompetence and reliance on *comparative horoscopy* (comparing future planetary configurations to past events). Research shows he heavily paraphrased sources: classical historians (Livy, Suetonius, Plutarch), medieval chroniclers (Villehardouin, Froissart), and astrological references from Richard Roussat’s *Livre de l'estat et mutations des temps* (1549–50). A major source was the *Mirabilis Liber* (1522), a collection of prophecies by Pseudo-Methodius, the Tiburtine Sibyl, Joachim of Fiore, and Savonarola; Nostradamus rephrased these in French, possibly via *bibliomancy* (randomly opening books for inspiration). He also drew from Petrus Crinitus’s *De honesta disciplina* (1504) and Iamblichus’s *De Mysteriis Aegyptiorum*. Despite claiming in 1555 to have burned his occult library, its contents are unknown. Nostradamus explicitly rejected the title "prophet" multiple times, stating he attributed to himself neither the name nor the role. His method likely involved only contemplation, meditation, and incubation; legends of flame or water gazing stem from a literal reading of his first two verses, which merely liken his efforts to the Delphic oracle.

### Other Works
Nostradamus wrote at least two medical texts: a paraphrase of Galen’s *Protreptic* and the *Traité des fardemens*, a "medical cookbook" describing plague treatments (including bloodletting, which apparently failed) and cosmetic preparations. A manuscript, *Orus Apollo*, in the Lyon municipal library (holding 2,000+ Nostradamus documents), is a purported translation of a Greek work on Egyptian hieroglyphs based on ignorant Latin versions (true decipherment awaited Champollion in the 19th century). Only the *Prophecies* remained popular post-mortem, with over 200 editions and 2,000+ commentaries, sustained by their vagueness and lack of dating, allowing selective retrospective application to dramatic events.

### Interpretations and Popular Claims
The quatrains focus on disasters (plagues, wars, earthquakes, invasions) and an impending Muslim/Antichrist invasion of Europe, reflecting contemporary Ottoman threats and the *Mirabilis Liber*. No two interpreters agree on specific predictions. Popular authors credit him with the Great Fire of London, French Revolution, Napoleon, Hitler, World Wars, Hiroshima/Nagasaki, Apollo 11, Challenger, Diana’s death, and 9/11—a "movable feast" where each new book claims prediction of the latest major event. Key popularizers include Henry C. Roberts (1947), Edgar Leoni (1961), Erika Cheetham (1973 onward), Jean-Charles de Fontbrune (1980), and John Hogue (1987 onward). These works invented extensive apocryphal biographies: descent from the tribe of Issachar; education by physician grandfathers; successful Montpellier doctorate and faculty lectures; heliocentrism; travels to the Habsburg Netherlands; scrying with mirror or water; predicting Henry II’s death via quatrain I.35 (first claimed in 1614, 55 years post-event); bequeathing a "lost book" of prophetic paintings; burial standing up; and a medallion predicting his exhumation date (legend recorded by Samuel Pepys in 1667). In 2000, Li Hongzhi claimed quatrain X.72 (1999) predicted the Falun Gong persecution.

### World War II Propaganda
Both sides exploited Nostradamus. Nazi Minister Josef Goebbels, alerted by his wife Magda to a 1921 book by C. L. Loog predicting a Polish crisis and English defeat in 1939, hired Swiss astrologer Karl Ernst Krafft (who allegedly predicted the November 1939 Hitler assassination attempt) to produce propaganda. Goebbels established an expert committee (Jan 1940) to "trash out" verses for use in France and neutral countries; German planes airdropped Krafft’s booklets with spurious verses over Belgium and France in May 1940. The Allies countered with pamphlets and four MGM morale films. Goebbels viewed Nostradamus purely as a "black propaganda tool," noting "The Americans and English fall easily for that type of thing." He personally authored fake verses (e.g., "people under the sign of the crooked cross... will triumph... for a thousand years") and allegedly forged parchment folios with spurious Latin/Gothic passages ("giant fish," "birds of iron"), which vanished from libraries after use.

### Scholarly Rebuttal
From the 1980s, French academics (Chomarat, Benazra, Halbronn, Lemesurier, Gruber, Wilson) used newly discovered private correspondence (1983), original 1555/1557 editions, and archival material to debunk claims. No popular biographical legend (Issachar descent, successful doctorate, scrying, Henry II prophecy, standing burial, medallion) has contemporary documentary evidence; they derive from unsourced rumors by later commentators (Jaubert 1656, Guynaud 1693, Bareste 1840), modern misunderstandings of 16th-century French, or pure invention. Skeptics (e.g., James Randi) attribute his reputation to "retroactive clairvoyance" (postdiction): fitting vague words to past/imminent events. No quatrain predicted a specific event *before* it occurred. Even dated quatrains (e.g., III.77: "in 1727... king of Persia captured by those of Egypt") are interpreted retrospectively (here, as an Ottoman-Persia treaty). Quatrain X.72 ("1999") describes no identifiable event; Nostradamus’s preface stated prophecies extend to 3797 (possibly derived from Roussat’s end-of-world calculation: 3797–1555=2242). Scholars also note almost all English translations are poor: ignorant of 16th-century French, tendentious, sometimes intentionally altered, and based on late editions (Garencières 1672 or 1568 omnibus), not the 1555 original. Hogue only admitted earlier biographical errors in 2003. Recent scholars strongly criticize internet-era attempts to extract hidden meanings via anagrams, numerical codes, or graphs.

## Terms
- ****Apothecary**** — A historical medical professional who prepared and sold medicines (herbal remedies, compounds); considered a "manual trade" banned for university medical students.
- ****Judicial Astrology**** — The astrological practice of assessing the "quality" or potential of specific events (births, weddings, coronations) via horoscopic charts to predict outcomes.
- ****Comparative Horoscopy**** — A technique comparing future planetary configurations to those accompanying known past events to forecast future occurrences; criticized as invalid by contemporaries.
- ****Quatrain**** — A stanza of four lines, usually rhyming; the structural unit of *Les Prophéties* (942 total in the 1568 edition).
- ****Century**** — In Nostradamus’s work, a book of 100 quatrains (except Century VII, incomplete at 42 in the 1568 edition).
- ****Mirabilis Liber**** — A 1522 collection of prophecies (Pseudo-Methodius, Tiburtine Sibyl, Joachim of Fiore, Savonarola, etc.) that served as a major source for Nostradamus’s quatrains.
- ****Bibliomancy**** — Divination by randomly opening a book (often sacred or historical) and interpreting the passage; suggested as Nostradamus’s method for selecting source material.
- ****Postdiction (Retroactive Clairvoyance)**** — The practice of interpreting vague predictions *after* an event has occurred to claim they foresaw it; the primary mechanism for Nostradamus’s perceived accuracy.
- ****Black Propaganda**** — False information or material (forged documents, spurious verses) disseminated to appear as if from a friendly or neutral source, used to deceive the enemy; Goebbels’s use of Nostradamus.
- ****Edema**** — Swelling caused by excess fluid trapped in body tissues; the complication of chronic gout that caused Nostradamus’s death in 1566.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authenticity of the 1558 third installment**: The third edition (adding 300 quatrains) reportedly printed in 1558 survives only as part of the 1568 omnibus; scholars debate whether the 1558 edition ever existed independently or if the 1568 version includes posthumous additions by secretary Jean de Chavigny.
- **Extent of Chavigny’s editorial role**: Chavigny edited the 1568 edition and is suspected of altering quatrains (e.g., Presage 141/152) to match Nostradamus’s death circumstances; the degree of his interpolation vs. preservation of authorial intent is unresolved.
- **Nature of the "rose pill" and plague treatments**: Nostradamus’s specific remedies (rose pill, bloodletting, *Traité des fardemens* recipes) are documented but their actual efficacy and ingredients remain historically obscure; the source notes bloodletting "apparently worked" not at all.
- **Lost quatrains of Century VII**: The final 58 quatrains of the seventh Century were omitted due to a publisher’s refusal to start mid-book; their content is permanently lost unless a manuscript surfaces.
- **Nostradamus’s true astrological competence**: While he claimed judicial astrology, contemporaries (Laurens Videl) and modern analysts cite frequent calculation errors (failure to adjust for latitude/time) and fundamental misunderstandings of astrological theory.
- **Intent: Prophecy vs. Literary Pastiche**: Scholars debate whether Nostradamus believed in his predictive ability or consciously constructed a literary pastiche of historical precedent for patronage/safety; his repeated explicit rejection of the title "prophet" supports the latter.
- **Origin of the 3797 end-date**: The preface’s claim that prophecies extend to 3797 (2242 years after 1555) likely derives from source Richard Roussat’s end-of-world calculation, but the specific transmission path is not definitively proven.
