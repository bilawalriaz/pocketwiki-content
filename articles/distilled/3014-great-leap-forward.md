# Great Leap Forward

## Overview
The Great Leap Forward (1958–1962) was a campaign by Chinese Communist Party Chairman Mao Zedong to rapidly industrialize China through mass mobilization and agricultural collectivization. It aimed to transform China from an agrarian society into a modern industrial power. The campaign is widely considered a catastrophic failure, causing the Great Chinese Famine, which resulted in an estimated 15 to 55 million deaths.

## Timeline
- **1958** — Mao launches the Great Leap Forward at a Nanjing meeting.
- **April 1958** — First experimental people's commune established in Henan.
- **August 1958** — Politburo decides to establish people's communes nationwide and doubles steel targets.
- **July–Aug 1959** — Lushan Conference; Marshal Peng Dehuai criticizes the Leap and is purged.
- **1959–1961** — The Great Chinese Famine occurs, with widespread starvation.
- **1960** — China continues to be a net grain exporter despite the famine.
- **1961** — The Ninth Plenum marks a shift toward economic "adjustment."
- **Jan–Feb 1962** — Seven Thousand Cadres Conference; Mao makes a self-criticism and cedes day-to-day leadership.
- **1963** — Mao initiates the Socialist Education Movement to re-consolidate power.
- **1966** — Mao launches the Cultural Revolution, partly to counter opposition from the Leap's failure.
- **1975** — Banqiao Dam, built during the Leap, collapses, causing a major flood.

## Body

### Background and Ideology
The Great Leap Forward emerged from Mao's belief that China could defy conventional development through mass mobilization, bypassing the Soviet model. By the late 1950s, increased Sino-Soviet tensions made China more reliant on self-reliance. The campaign aimed to "walk on two legs," developing heavy and light industry, urban and rural areas simultaneously, to address rural-urban imbalances.

### Agricultural Collectivization and People's Communes
From 1949 to 1958, the CCP gradually collectivized agriculture, moving from mutual aid teams to cooperatives. By 1958, private ownership was abolished, and all households were forced into state-operated people's communes. These communes centralized farming, labor, and social life, abolishing private plots and introducing communal kitchens. The system disrupted traditional farming, decreased efficiency, and, combined with inflated production reports, led to excessive state grain procurement that left peasants with insufficient food.

### Industrialization and Backyard Furnaces
A core pillar was rapid industrialization, particularly steel production. The government promoted "backyard steel furnaces," mobilizing millions of peasants to produce crude iron and steel. While the 1958 steel target was nominally met, much output was unusable. This diversion of agricultural labor to industrial projects directly contributed to the failure to harvest crops and the ensuing famine.

### The Great Chinese Famine
The famine resulted from disastrous policies, adverse weather (a 1959 drought and floods), and a breakdown in reporting. Local officials massively inflated grain yield figures, leading the state to set impossibly high procurement quotas. Despite the famine, China continued to export grain to maintain international prestige. The worst-hit provinces were those that most vigorously implemented Mao's reforms. The death toll is estimated between 15 and 55 million, with commonly cited figures around 30-36 million.

### Political Aftermath and Mao's Response
The disaster led to internal party criticism, most notably from Marshal Peng Dehuai at the 1959 Lushan Conference. Mao responded by purging Peng and launching the Anti-Rightist Deviation Struggle. After the famine, Mao acknowledged some mistakes at the 1962 Seven Thousand Cadres Conference and ceded day-to-day leadership to pragmatists like Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping. However, Mao maintained that the "three red flags" could not be challenged. He later launched the Socialist Education Movement and the Cultural Revolution to re-consolidate power.

## Terms
*   **People's Communes:** Large-scale collective units that merged agricultural cooperatives, abolished private plots, and organized all aspects of rural life.
*   **Backyard Steel Furnaces:** Primitive, small-scale furnaces promoted to rapidly increase steel production through mass peasant labor, often resulting in low-quality metal.
*   **Great Chinese Famine:** The devastating famine from 1959 to 1961 caused primarily by the policies of the Great Leap Forward.
*   **Anti-Rightist Campaign:** A political purge initiated in 1957 to silence critics, which intensified after the Lushan Conference.
*   **Lushan Conference (1959):** A pivotal CCP meeting where Marshal Peng Dehuai criticized the Great Leap Forward, leading to his purge.
*   **Seven Thousand Cadres Conference (1962):** A large party conference where the failures of the Great Leap were discussed, and day-to-day leadership shifted to moderates.
*   **Hukou:** A household registration system introduced in 1956 that restricted internal migration, keeping peasants tied to their rural communes.
*   **General Line for Socialist Construction:** The official CCP policy slogan adopted in 1958: "Go all out, aim high, and build socialism with greater, faster, better, and more economical results."

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Death Toll:** Estimates vary widely from 15 to 55 million. Scholars use different methods and adjust for data errors, leading to significant disagreement. The most commonly cited range is 15-45 million.
*   **Causes and Responsibility:** While policies are universally seen as the primary cause, the relative contribution of weather is debated. Some historians argue Mao's policies and totalitarianism bear direct responsibility, while others emphasize structural governance challenges.
*   **Intentionality:** There is debate over whether the famine constituted "terror-starvation" or manslaughter due to negligent policies, versus being an unintended catastrophic failure. The 1981 CCP Resolution blamed "errors of the Great Leap Forward" and natural calamities.
