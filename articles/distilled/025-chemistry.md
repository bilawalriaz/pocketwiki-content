# Chemistry

## Overview

Chemistry is the scientific study of the properties and behavior of matter—its composition, structure, properties, and the changes it undergoes during reactions with other substances. It occupies an intermediate position between physics and biology and is sometimes called the central science because it provides a foundation for understanding both basic and applied scientific disciplines, from pharmacology and geology to forensics and ecology. Chemistry has existed under various names since ancient times, evolving from alchemy into a modern experimental science, and now encompasses numerous specialized subdisciplines with major economic applications in the chemical industry.

## Body

### Etymology and Definition

The word chemistry comes from a modification during the Renaissance of the word alchemy, which referred to earlier practices encompassing elements of chemistry, metallurgy, philosophy, astrology, astronomy, mysticism, and medicine. Alchemy is often associated with the quest to turn lead or other base metals into gold. The modern word alchemy derives from the Arabic *al-kīmīā*, which may have Egyptian origins (from the ancient name Kemet) or may derive from the Greek *χημεία* meaning "cast together."

The definition of chemistry has changed over time. In 1661, Robert Boyle meant the subject of the material principles of mixed bodies. In 1663, Christopher Glaser described it as a scientific art of dissolving bodies and reuniting their components. Georg Ernst Stahl (1730) defined it as the art of resolving mixed bodies into their principles and composing them from those principles. By 1837, Jean-Baptiste Dumas referred to the science of molecular forces; by 1947 it meant the science of substances—their structure, properties, and reactions—a characterization accepted by Linus Pauling. In 1998, Raymond Chang broadened it to the study of matter and the changes it undergoes.

### Modern Principles: Matter, Atoms, and Elements

Matter is defined as anything that has rest mass and volume and is made up of particles. The atom is the basic unit of chemistry, consisting of a dense nucleus (positively charged protons and uncharged neutrons) surrounded by an electron cloud of negatively charged electrons. The mass of a nucleon is approximately 1,836 times that of an electron, yet the radius of an atom is about 10,000 times that of its nucleus. The atom is the smallest entity that retains the chemical properties of an element.

A chemical element is a pure substance composed of a single type of atom, characterized by its atomic number (Z)—the number of protons in its nuclei. Atoms of the same element may have different mass numbers (protons plus neutrons); these are called isotopes. The periodic table orders elements by atomic number in groups (columns) and periods (rows), revealing periodic trends.

### Compounds, Molecules, and Mixtures

A compound is a pure chemical substance composed of more than one element, with properties bearing little similarity to those of its elements. Nomenclature is set by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC); the Chemical Abstracts Service (CAS) assigns each substance a unique registry number.

A molecule is the smallest indivisible portion of a pure substance with its unique set of chemical properties—typically atoms bound by covalent bonds, electrically neutral with all valence electrons paired. Molecules exist as neutral units, unlike ions; charged versions are called molecular or polyatomic ions. Some molecules contain unpaired electrons, creating radicals, most of which are reactive (though nitric oxide, NO, is stable). Noble gases consist of lone atoms, while most solid substances on Earth—ionic compounds and network solids—lack discrete molecules and are described in terms of formula units or unit cells. A chemical substance has a definite composition and set of properties; a mixture is a collection of substances (e.g., air, alloys). The mole is the unit of amount of substance, defined to contain exactly 6.02214076×10²³ particles (the Avogadro constant).

### Phase and Bonding

A phase is a set of states of a chemical system with similar bulk structural properties over a range of conditions. Familiar phases are solids, liquids, and gases; many substances exhibit multiple solid phases (e.g., three phases of solid iron). Less familiar phases include plasmas, Bose–Einstein condensates, and fermionic condensates. When the distinction between phases is continuous, matter is in a supercritical state; a triple point is where three states meet under invariant conditions.

Atoms bonded together in molecules or crystals are held by chemical bonds: covalent, ionic, hydrogen, or Van der Waals forces. An ionic bond forms when a metal loses electrons to become a cation and a non-metal gains them to become an anion, held together by electrostatic attraction (e.g., sodium and chlorine forming NaCl). A covalent bond involves sharing valence electrons to achieve a noble gas configuration—eight electrons (octet rule) or two (duet rule, for hydrogen and lithium). For complex compounds like metal complexes, molecular orbital theory is generally used instead of valence bond theory.

### Energy and Reactions

Energy is an attribute of a substance resulting from its atomic, molecular, or aggregate structure; chemical transformations are invariably accompanied by energy changes. Reactions are exergonic if the final state is lower in energy than the initial state (endergonic if the reverse), and exothermic if they release heat (endothermic if they absorb it). Reactions require surmounting an activation energy barrier; the rate's exponential dependence on temperature is described by the Arrhenius equation. A reaction is feasible only if the change in Gibbs free energy is negative (ΔG ≤ 0); at ΔG = 0, the reaction is at equilibrium. Quantum mechanics requires quantization of energy; atoms or molecules in higher energy states are "excited" and often more reactive. Characteristic energy levels allow identification of substances via spectroscopy, including analysis of remote objects like stars.

A chemical reaction is a transformation of substances into different substances, symbolically depicted by a chemical equation in which the number of atoms on both sides is equal (if unequal, it is a nuclear reaction or radioactive decay). The sequence of steps in a reaction is its mechanism, proposed to explain kinetics and product mix; empirical rules like the Woodward–Hoffmann rules aid in proposing mechanisms. IUPAC defines a chemical reaction as "a process that results in the interconversion of chemical species."

### Ions, Acids, Redox, and Equilibrium

An ion is an atom or molecule that has lost or gained electrons—a cation if positively charged, an anion if negatively charged. Cations and anions can form crystalline lattices of neutral salts (e.g., NaCl). Plasma is gaseous matter that has been completely ionized, usually through high temperature.

Acids and bases are classified by several theories: Arrhenius (acids produce hydronium ions in water; bases produce hydroxide ions), Brønsted–Lowry (acids donate hydrogen ions; bases receive them), and Lewis (acids accept electron pairs; bases donate them). Acid strength is measured by pH (negative logarithmic scale of hydronium ion concentration) or by the acid dissociation constant (Ka).

Redox (reduction-oxidation) reactions involve changes in oxidation state—gaining electrons (reduction) or losing them (oxidation). Oxidizing agents remove electrons; reducing agents donate them. Oxidation and reduction properly refer to changes in oxidation number, not necessarily actual electron transfer.

Equilibrium arises when multiple states of chemical composition are possible; even with unchanging composition, systems at equilibrium are usually dynamic, with molecules continuing to react.

### History

Early civilizations (Egyptians, Babylonians, Indians) amassed practical knowledge of metallurgy, pottery, and dyes without systematic theory. A basic chemical hypothesis emerged in Classical Greece with Aristotle's theory of four elements (fire, air, earth, water). Greek atomism (c. 440 BC) was purely philosophical, with little concern for empirical observation. The idea of conservation of mass appears in Empedocles and Epicurus ("Nothing comes from nothing").

Alchemy first proliferated in the Hellenistic world, mingling magic and occultism with the goals of transmuting elements into gold and finding the elixir of eternal life. It continued in the Byzantine period (Zosimos of Panopolis) and the Arab world, where works attributed to Jabir ibn Hayyan introduced systematic classification of substances. Later Muslim philosophers like al-Bīrūnī and Avicenna disputed alchemical theories. In 16th-century Europe, Georg Agricola's *De re metallica* (1556) described complex mining and smelting processes, removing mysticism and earning him the title "father of metallurgy."

Under the Scientific Revolution, Robert Boyle, Robert Hooke, and John Mayow reshaped alchemy into a scientific discipline. Boyle formulated Boyle's law and rejected the four elements. Subsequent discoveries included carbon dioxide (Black, 1754), hydrogen (Cavendish), and oxygen (Priestley and Scheele). The phlogiston theory was overturned by Lavoisier, who established conservation of mass and a new nomenclature. Dalton proposed the modern atomic theory; Berzelius and Davy developed electrochemical theory (Davy discovered nine new elements using electric current). The periodic table was developed in the 1860s by Mendeleev and others; noble gases were discovered by Ramsay and Rayleigh. Wöhler's synthesis of urea advanced organic chemistry; Frankland (valence, 1852) and Gibbs and Arrhenius (thermodynamics, 1870s) made crucial contributions. At the turn of the 20th century, Thomson discovered the electron (1897), Becquerel and the Curies investigated radioactivity, and Rutherford discovered the atomic nucleus and proton. Bohr, Moseley, and Hahn (who discovered nuclear fission) improved on atomic structure; Pauling and Lewis developed the electronic theory of chemical bonds.

### Practice and Subdisciplines

Pure chemistry studies fundamental principles; applied chemistry uses that knowledge to solve real-world problems. Major subdisciplines include: analytical chemistry (analysis of material samples), biochemistry (chemicals and reactions in living organisms), inorganic chemistry (metals and minerals), materials chemistry (solid-state components), organic chemistry (carbon-based compounds, organized by functional groups), physical chemistry (energetics and dynamics of chemical systems), and theoretical chemistry (fundamental theoretical reasoning, including quantum and computational chemistry). Other subdivisions include electrochemistry, photochemistry, polymer chemistry, radiochemistry, and many more. Interdisciplinary fields include agrochemistry, astrochemistry, chemical engineering, environmental chemistry, geochemistry, green chemistry, medicinal chemistry, nanotechnology, and pharmacology. The chemical industry is a major economic activity: the global top 50 chemical producers in 2013 had sales of US$980.5 billion with a profit margin of 10.3%.

## Terms

- **Atom**: The basic unit of chemistry; a dense nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by an electron cloud.
- **Element**: A pure substance composed of a single type of atom, characterized by its atomic number (Z).
- **Isotope**: Atoms of the same element with different mass numbers (different numbers of neutrons).
- **Compound**: A pure substance composed of more than one element, with properties unlike its constituent elements.
- **Molecule**: The smallest neutral unit of a substance, typically atoms bound by covalent bonds with paired valence electrons.
- **Mole**: The unit of amount of substance, containing exactly 6.02214076×10²³ particles (Avogadro constant).
- **Phase**: A set of states of a chemical system with similar bulk structural properties over a range of conditions.
- **Ion**: A charged atom or molecule that has lost (cation) or gained (anion) electrons.
- **Redox**: Reduction-oxidation reactions in which atoms change oxidation state by gaining or losing electrons.
- **Activation energy**: The energy barrier that reactants must surmount for a chemical reaction to occur.

## Debates and open questions

The source notes that the definition of chemistry has changed repeatedly over time, from Boyle's "material principles of mixed bodies" (1661) to Chang's "study of matter and the changes it undergoes" (1998), indicating ongoing evolution of the field's scope. The etymology of the word chemistry is debated: *al-kīmīā* may derive from the Ancient Greek *χημία* (from Kemet, the ancient Egyptian name) or from *χημεία* meaning "cast together." Additionally, the distinction between organic and inorganic chemistry is noted as "not absolute," with much overlap, particularly in organometallic chemistry—an area of active interdisciplinary negotiation.
