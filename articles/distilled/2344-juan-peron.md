# Juan Perón

## Overview
Juan Domingo Perón was an Argentine military officer and politician who served as president three times (1946–1955, 1973–1974). A dominant and polarizing figure in 20th-century Argentine history, his policies and political movement, Peronism, reshaped the country's social, economic, and political landscape. His legacy remains central to Argentine politics.

## Timeline
- **8 October 1895** — Born in Lobos, Buenos Aires Province.
- **1911** — Enters the National Military College.
- **1930** — Supports the coup against President Hipólito Yrigoyen.
- **1939–1941** — Serves as military attaché in Europe, studying fascist regimes.
- **1943** — Participates in the GOU military coup; becomes Minister of Labour.
- **17 October 1945** — Released from arrest after mass worker demonstrations.
- **24 February 1946** — Wins the presidential election.
- **26 July 1952** — His wife, Eva Perón, dies.
- **16 June 1955** — Navy jets bomb Plaza de Mayo in a failed coup attempt.
- **16 September 1955** — Overthrown in a coup; begins 18-year exile.
- **25 May 1973** — Peronist Héctor Cámpora elected president, paving the way for Perón's return.
- **12 October 1973** — Inaugurated for his third presidential term.
- **1 July 1974** — Dies in office; succeeded by his wife, Isabel Perón.

## Body

### Rise to Power and First Term (1946–1952)
Perón rose to prominence as Minister of Labour in the 1943 military government, championing labor rights and forming alliances with unions. His popularity surged after mass demonstrations secured his release in October 1945. Elected in 1946, his administration focused on "social justice and economic independence." Key policies included nationalizing railways and the Central Bank, creating the state grain trading entity IAPI, and implementing a five-year industrialization plan. His government expanded social security, built over half a million homes, and, with Eva Perón's influence, granted women the right to vote. However, his rule also involved press censorship, persecution of dissidents, and the firing of over 2,000 university professors.

### Foreign Policy, Economic Challenges, and Downfall
Perón pursued a "Third Way" foreign policy to avoid Cold War alignments, restoring relations with the Soviet Union. Relations with the United States were strained, particularly after the U.S. excluded Argentine exports from the Marshall Plan. The economy boomed initially but faced crises after 1948 due to falling commodity prices, high imports, and severe drought. His second term (1952–1955) was marked by economic austerity, conflict with the Catholic Church over secular policies, and growing opposition. A coup attempt in June 1955, involving naval planes bombing Plaza de Mayo, led to his overthrow in September 1955.

### Exile and Ideological Evolution
During his 18-year exile, Peronism was outlawed. Perón lived in several countries before settling in Spain. He maintained political influence, instructing supporters to vote for specific candidates. In exile, he cultivated ties with both the far left (like the Montoneros) and the far right, and his rhetoric incorporated more revolutionary and socialist themes. He met with figures like Che Guevara and Salvador Allende, describing Peronism as a form of "indigenous Latin American socialism."

### Third Term and Death (1973–1974)
Perón returned to Argentina in 1973 amid the Ezeiza massacre, where left-wing supporters were attacked. He was elected president for a third time in September 1973. His short final term was dominated by violent conflict between left- and right-wing Peronist factions. His government implemented a "social pact" to curb inflation and boost wages, but also saw the rise of the right-wing José López Rega and the formation of the Triple A death squad. Perón publicly broke with the Montoneros in 1974. He died on 1 July 1974, succeeded by his vice president, Isabel Perón.

### The Peronism and Fascism Debate
The relationship between Peronism and fascism is a major scholarly debate. While Perón admired aspects of Mussolini's Italy and implemented authoritarian measures, most historians argue Peronism is not fascist. Key distinctions include its base in the working class (not the middle class), its lack of expansionist militarism, and its role in expanding democratic participation (e.g., women's suffrage) rather than destroying it. Scholars like Federico Finchelstein see it as a synthesis of nationalism and non-Marxist Christian socialism, while others note its roots in Argentine syndicalism and Catholic social teaching.

### Nazi Refugees and Jewish Community
Perón's government provided refuge to Nazi war criminals after WWII, including Adolf Eichmann and Josef Mengele, often facilitated by ratlines involving Swiss and Vatican officials. Perón saw this as a way to acquire German scientific and technical expertise. Simultaneously, Argentina accepted more Jewish immigrants than any other Latin American country. Perón appointed Jewish advisors, founded the Jewish wing of the Peronist Party, and maintained good relations with Israel, indicating pragmatic rather than ideologically anti-Semitic actions.

## Terms
- **Peronism**: The political movement based on the ideas and policies of Juan Perón, emphasizing social justice, economic independence, and political sovereignty for the working class.
- **Justicialism**: The official ideology of Peronism, described by Perón as a "third position" between capitalism and communism, focused on social justice and balanced societal development.
- **CGT (General Confederation of Labour)**: Argentina's largest trade union federation, a central pillar of Peronist political power.
- **Evita**: The popular name for Eva Perón, Juan Perón's second wife, a powerful political figure and symbol of social justice for the poor.
- **Third Way**: Perón's foreign policy doctrine of non-alignment during the Cold War, seeking relations with both the U.S. and the Soviet bloc.
- **IAPI (Institute for the Promotion of Trade)**: A state entity created to control and profit from Argentina's grain exports, funding social and industrial policies.
- **Triple A (Argentine Anticommunist Alliance)**: A right-wing death squad active during Perón's third term, believed to have committed hundreds of extrajudicial killings.
- **Montoneros**: A left-wing Catholic Peronist guerrilla group that initially supported Perón but later clashed with his government and the right-wing faction.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Was Peronism fascist?** The academic consensus is that while Perón borrowed some tactics and admired certain aspects of European fascism, the core of Peronism—its working-class base, social policies, and role in democratizing Argentine politics—makes it distinct from fascism.
- **What was Perón's true ideological stance?** Scholars debate whether he was a nationalist socialist, a pragmatic populist, a Christian democrat, or a unique synthesis. His own descriptions evolved from "national socialism" to "justicialism."
- **What was the extent of state involvement in harboring Nazis?** Documents show organized ratlines and high-level protection for war criminals, motivated by a desire for German technology and personnel, though the full scope remains researched.
- **How responsible was Perón for the violence of his third term?** His inability to control escalating conflict between left- and right-wing Peronists, and his empowerment of figures like López Rega, are seen as key factors leading to the "dirty war" that followed his death.
