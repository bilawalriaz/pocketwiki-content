# Game show

## Overview
A game show is a broadcast entertainment genre where contestants compete in a game, typically involving trivia or puzzles, to win prizes like cash or trips. The format is defined by objective task completion within a single episode, distinguishing it from reality competition shows that rely on voting or judging. Game shows have been a persistent feature of television since the 1930s, reflecting changes in technology, regulation, and audience tastes.

## Timeline
- **1938** — First TV game show (*Spelling Bee*) and first radio game show (*Information Please*) broadcast.
- **1941** — First commercially licensed TV game shows (*Truth or Consequences*, *CBS Television Quiz*) air as experimental broadcasts.
- **1950s** — Game shows become a daytime TV fixture; high-stakes primetime shows like *Twenty-One* rise in popularity.
- **1959** — 1950s quiz show scandals expose rigged shows, leading to cancellations and a decline in primetime games.
- **1964** — *Jeopardy!* debuts, becoming a long-running daytime staple.
- **1972** — *The New Price Is Right* debuts, marking CBS's return to daytime game shows.
- **1975** — *Wheel of Fortune* debuts on NBC.
- **1983-84** — *Wheel of Fortune* and a modernized *Jeopardy!* move to syndication, becoming highly successful.
- **1998** — *Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?* premieres in the UK, sparking a global high-stakes game show craze.
- **1999** — *Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?* debuts in the US, reviving the genre's primetime popularity.
- **2004** — Ken Jennings begins his record 74-game winning streak on *Jeopardy!*.
- **2010s** — A boom in prime-time revivals of classic game shows begins (e.g., *Celebrity Family Feud*, *The $100,000 Pyramid*). |

## Body

### Definition and Criteria
A traditional game show is a contest where prizes are won within a single episode through objective task completion, not fan or contestant votes. This excludes reality competition shows like *American Idol* or *Survivor*, which involve judging or voting. A notable exception is *Weakest Link*, which features voting but also trivia and single-episode prize wins. Streaming shows on major platforms (e.g., Netflix, HBO Max) qualify, while content on social media does not. A modern hybrid, the "reality game show" (e.g., *American Ninja Warrior*, *The Amazing Race*), combines task-based competition with reality elements but avoids voting systems.

### History and Evolution
Game shows originated on radio and television in the late 1930s. They became a daytime TV staple in the 1950s, but the 1959 quiz show scandals, which revealed rigged high-stakes games, crippled the primetime format. Daytime, lower-stakes shows survived, and panel shows (e.g., *What's My Line?*) offered an alternative. The 1970s saw a renaissance with hits like *The Price Is Right* and *Wheel of Fortune*, aided by the 1971 Prime Time Access Rule, which created slots for syndicated game shows. In the 1980s and 1990s, network daytime game shows declined, but syndicated giants *Wheel of Fortune* and *Jeopardy!* thrived. The 1999 US debut of *Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?* revived primetime interest, leading to a short-lived "million-dollar game show craze." Since the 2000s, the genre has seen syndicated stability, record-breaking runs (e.g., Ken Jennings on *Jeopardy!*), and a resurgence of prime-time revivals.

### International Format and Prizes
Game show formats are often franchised internationally. The US tends to cast stronger contestants than the UK or Australia, and historically restricted contestants from appearing on multiple shows. Prize funding often involves product placement and "promotional consideration." Networks may purchase prize indemnity insurance for large jackpots and often distribute major winnings as annuities. From the 1960s to the 1990s, US networks capped winnings to avoid scandal repeats, while UK regulations were even stricter until the 1990s, limiting prize values and banning games of chance. The lifting of these restrictions fueled the late-1990s high-stakes boom.

### The Bonus Round
A bonus round is a final, higher-stakes segment for the main game's winner, with tougher gameplay. It became a standard feature after the 1960s, pioneered by shows like *Password* (Lightning Round, 1961). Producer Mark Goodson argued that a show needed "something more" than the main game, making the endgame a required element. The audience-match segment of *Match Game*'s "Super Match" endgame inspired the format for *Family Feud*.

## Terms
- **Game Show:** A broadcast contest where contestants win prizes by completing objective tasks, typically in a single episode.
- **Reality Competition Show:** A series where contestants compete over a season, often eliminated by judging or voting (e.g., *Survivor*).
- **Panel Show:** A format where celebrities interview guests or answer questions, popular in the UK.
- **Prime Time Access Rule:** A 1971 FCC regulation barring networks from the 7-8 p.m. slot, creating opportunities for syndicated programming.
- **Syndication:** The licensing of a TV program to multiple stations, often for daily airings outside network control.
- **Prize Indemnity Insurance:** Insurance purchased by a network to cover the cost of rare, large prize payouts.
- **Promotional Consideration:** A subsidy from an advertiser in exchange for their product being awarded as a prize.
- **Bonus Round:** A final, high-stakes segment of a game show for the winner of the main game.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise boundary between a "game show" and a "reality competition" is debated, with shows like *Weakest Link* and hybrid "reality game shows" (e.g., *The Amazing Race*) blurring the lines.
- The impact of the 1950s quiz show scandals on long-term regulation and public trust remains a key historical discussion point.
- The sustainability of prime-time game show revivals versus the stability of syndicated daytime formats is an ongoing industry question.
