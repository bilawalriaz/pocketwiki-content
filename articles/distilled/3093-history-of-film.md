# History of film

## Overview
The history of film chronicles the artistic and cultural development of a visual art form that uses film technologies, beginning in the late 19th century. It evolved from a novelty into a global mass entertainment industry, profoundly influencing and being influenced by other media, technologies, and societal changes. Its development is marked by key technical innovations, the establishment of cinematic language, and the rise and fall of various national film industries and genres.

## Timeline
- **1895** — Lumière brothers hold the first commercial film screening in Paris.
- **1902** — Georges Méliès releases *A Trip to the Moon*, a pioneering science fiction film.
- **1903** — Edwin S. Porter's *The Great Train Robbery* advances narrative editing.
- **1915** — D. W. Griffith's *The Birth of a Nation* becomes a major, controversial success.
- **1920s** — German Expressionism flourishes; Hollywood studio system solidifies.
- **1927** — *The Jazz Singer* introduces synchronized dialogue, launching the sound era.
- **1930s** — Technicolor becomes commercially viable; "Golden Age of Hollywood" begins.
- **1950s** — Television competition spurs widescreen formats; Asian cinema enters a "Golden Age."
- **1960s** — New Hollywood movement begins with films like *Bonnie and Clyde*.
- **1970s** — Blockbuster era begins with *Jaws* and *Star Wars*.
- **1995** — *Toy Story* becomes the first feature-length computer-animated film.
- **2000s** — Digital cinema and 3D film experience a major resurgence.

## Body

### Precursors and Early Development
Film's origins trace to earlier arts like storytelling, theatre, and visual traditions such as magic lantern shows and phantasmagoria. Scientific study of optical illusions led to devices like the stroboscopic disk (1833). Chronophotography, pioneered by Eadweard Muybridge in 1878, captured motion sequences for scientific study. Inventors like the Lumière brothers, Thomas Edison, and others developed devices to capture and project moving images. The Lumière brothers' Cinématographe, first commercially screened in 1895, is generally regarded as the breakthrough for projected cinematographic motion pictures.

### The Novelty Era and Early Narrative
Early films were short, silent, and often actualities (documentary scenes). Filmmakers quickly experimented with narrative and special effects. In France, Georges Méliès used stop-trick and other effects to create fantasy narratives like *A Trip to the Moon* (1902). In England, the "Brighton School" filmmakers (George Albert Smith, James Williamson) pioneered continuity editing, close-ups, and cross-cutting. In the U.S., Edwin S. Porter's *The Great Train Robbery* (1903) used multiple locations and parallel action. The first permanent movie theaters, nickelodeons, opened in 1905, creating mass demand for story films.

### International Growth and the Silent Era
By the 1910s, film industries grew worldwide. Italy became known for historical epics, Denmark for melodramas, and France remained the global market leader until World War I. The war disrupted European production, allowing the U.S. industry, centered in Hollywood, to expand. Techniques like artificial lighting, cross-cutting, and intertitles became standard. D. W. Griffith's *The Birth of a Nation* (1915) demonstrated the feature film's dramatic potential. The 1920s saw the rise of the Hollywood studio system and the star system, while German Expressionist films used stylized sets to explore psychological themes.

### The Sound Era and Global Cinema
The release of *The Jazz Singer* (1927) with synchronized dialogue ended the silent era. The transition to sound was swift but costly, consolidating the power of major studios. Sound enhanced genres like musicals and gangster films. The 1930s-40s are often called Hollywood's "Golden Age." Meanwhile, a "Golden Age" of Asian cinema emerged in the 1950s, with acclaimed works from Japan (Akira Kurosawa), India (Satyajit Ray), and others. Post-WWII, Italian Neorealism and the French New Wave (1960s) challenged Hollywood conventions.

### New Hollywood, Blockbusters, and Digital Revolution
The decline of the studio system in the 1960s-70s allowed a new generation of directors (Scorsese, Coppola, Spielberg) to create more personal, often violent films. The blockbuster model, pioneered by *Jaws* (1975) and *Star Wars* (1977), focused on high-concept, effects-driven spectacles. The 1980s saw the rise of home video (VCR). The 1990s brought independent cinema success and the first computer-animated feature, *Toy Story* (1995). The 2000s-2010s were dominated by franchise filmmaking, 3D technology, and the shift to digital production and streaming distribution, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic.

## Terms
- **Actuality**: An early form of documentary film showing a real event or scene.
- **Continuity editing**: A system of editing that creates a smooth, coherent sense of time and space for the viewer.
- **Cross-cutting**: Editing between two or more scenes happening simultaneously to build tension or show parallel action.
- **Kinetoscope**: An early peep-hole viewer for motion pictures developed by Thomas Edison's company.
- **Cinématographe**: The camera/projector combination developed by the Lumière brothers.
- **Nickelodeon**: An early type of small, inexpensive movie theater.
- **Studio system**: The method of film production and distribution dominated by a few major studios in Hollywood's Golden Age.
- **Blockbuster**: A high-budget film intended for mass appeal, often with heavy marketing and special effects.
- **Auteur theory**: The idea that a film's director is its primary author, expressing a personal creative vision.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact "advent of film as an artistic medium" is not clearly defined, with debates over whether it began with the first screenings, the first narrative experiments, or later developments.
- The impact of new media (TV, internet, streaming) on theatrical film distribution and the traditional "moviegoing experience" remains a subject of ongoing industry and scholarly discussion.
- The relative importance of technological innovation versus artistic and narrative innovation in driving the medium's evolution is continually reassessed.
