# Kabir

## Overview
Kabir (fl. 15th century) was an Indian devotional mystic poet and saint whose verses bridge Hinduism, Sikhism, and Sufism. A disciple of the Vaishnava saint Ramananda, Kabir rejected the rituals and authority of both organized Hinduism and Islam, advocating a direct, nirguna (formless) devotion to a divine presence residing within every being. His poetry, composed orally in vernacular dialects, criticizes religious hypocrisy and the ego ("I") as barriers to truth. Persecuted by both communities during his life, he became a unifying figure after death, claimed by Hindus and Muslims alike. His legacy endures through the Kabir Panth (Path of Kabir), a Sant Mat sect with millions of followers, and his verses form the largest non-Sikh contribution to the Guru Granth Sahib.

## Timeline
- ****1398–1448** or **1440–1518**** — Scholarly date ranges for Kabir’s life; traditional birth date often given as 1398 (Vikram Samvat 1455).
- ****c. 15th century**** — Kabir becomes a disciple of Swami Ramananda in Varanasi, absorbing Vaishnava monist (Advaita) teachings.
- ****15th–17th century**** — Kabir composes poems orally (bāņīs, dohe, sākhīs) in Sadhukkadi/Panchmel Khichri; transmission viva voce across generations.
- ****1604**** — Kabir’s verses incorporated into the Adi Granth (Guru Granth Sahib), constituting the largest non-Sikh contribution.
- ****17th century**** — Kabir Bijak first compiled and written down; oral transmission leads to interpolation and multiple recensions.
- ****17th–18th century**** — Kabir Panth ("Path of Kabir") formally organizes as a Sant Mat sect in north and central India.
- ****1915**** — Rabindranath Tagore publishes *Songs of Kabir*, popularizing him in the West; authenticity of specific poems debated.
- ****1901 / Present**** — Kabir Panthis counted at 843,171 in 1901 census; estimated ~9.6 million today, spread across India and diaspora.

## Body

### Early Life and Background
Kabir’s birth and death dates remain uncertain; historians propose either 1398–1448 or 1440–1518. Traditional accounts place his birth in Varanasi (Banaras) in 1398. Legends describe his origin miraculously: followers believe he descended from Satloka (the true realm) as light onto a lotus in Lahartara Pond, witnessed by rishi Ashtanand. Historical narratives state a Muslim weaver couple, Niru and Nima, found him as an infant at Lahartara Lake and raised him. The family resided in Kabir Chaura, Varanasi, where the Kabīr maṭha and Nīrūṭīlā (housing Niru and Nima’s graves) now stand. Kabir became a disciple of Swami Ramananda, founder of the Ramanandi Sampradaya, who taught devotional Vaishnavism infused with monist Advaita philosophy—God resides inside every person. The Persian text *Dabestan-e Mazaheb* (early 17th century) identifies Kabir as a "Bairagi" (Vaishnava yogi) and disciple of "Gang" (Ramanand).

### Poetry: Language, Form, and Transmission
Kabir composed in **Sadhukkadi** (also called **Panchmel Khichri**—"five-grain porridge"), a lingua franca blending Khadi boli, Braj, Bhojpuri, Marwari, and Awadhi; some poems are in pure Bhojpuri. He named his oral utterances **bāņīs**, comprising songs and couplets called **dohe**, **śalokā** (Sanskrit: ślokā), or **sākhī** (Sanskrit: sākṣī, "witness"—implying the poem testifies to Truth). Major collections include *Kabir Bijak*, *Kabir Parachai*, *Sakhi Granth*, *Adi Granth* (Sikh scripture), and *Kabir Granthawali* (Rajasthan). Except for the *Adi Granth*, these texts exist in significantly different versions; *Kabir Bijak* alone has two major recensions. Poems were transmitted orally through the 17th century; the *Bijak* was first written down then. Scholars (notably Charlotte Vaudeville) note this process bred interpolation, corruption, and creative fabrication of new couplets attributed to Kabir out of reverence, not deceit. John Stratton Hawley observes distinct "personalities": Western (Rajasthani) collections portray a devotional Kabir; Eastern (Punjabi) collections depict a householder swayed by love’s intensity. Tagore’s 1915 *Songs of Kabir* remains influential, though V.C. Mishra argued only six of its hundred poems are authentic, suggesting Tagore projected early 20th-century theology onto Kabir.

### Philosophy: Nirguna Bhakti and Rejection of Orthodoxy
Kabir’s philosophy centers on **nirguna** (formless/attributeless) devotion. Linda Hess emphasizes he did not synthesize Hinduism and Islam but declared independence from both, attacking their "follies" and hypocrisy while adopting their terminology. He questioned the need for holy books, rituals (e.g., praying to avatars like Buddha), and external worship, urging seekers to look within: "truth" belongs to the righteous who see all creation as divine and remain passively detached. To know truth, one must drop the "I" (ego). Charlotte Vaudeville links Kabir’s Absolute to the Upanishadic **Brahman-Atman** and **Advaita Vedanta** (non-dualism), denying distinction between soul and God. Vaudeville notes a tension: if God is within, external *bhakti* (devotion) seems unnecessary; she suggests *saguna prema-bhakti* (tender devotion to a formed God) may be a provisional path toward realizing nirguna Brahman. David Lorenzen and Adrián Muñoz trace this nirguna Brahman to Adi Shankara’s Advaita, with differences.

### Influence of Islam
The extent of Islamic influence is contested. Lorenzen notes many scholars argue Kabir rejected Islam, drawing almost exclusively from Hindu tradition (vocabulary, concepts); contemporary Kabir Panth sadhus concur. Some scholars detect Sufi influence in sexual imagery (inverting the Sufi God-woman/devotee-man trope into Lord-husband/devotee-bride). Others argue Bhakti and Sufi traditions co-developed through mutual interaction. Ronald McGregor states Kabir "left Islam," yet he criticized Muslim cow-slaughter in terms Hindus used.

### Persecution and Social Impact
Kabir’s couplets claim he faced persecution from Hindus and Muslims (Sultan, Brahmin, Qazi). He welcomed slander as bringing him closer to God. Legends portray him as an underdog victorious in trials before rulers and clerics, appealing to the poor and oppressed. David Lorenzen reads these legends as protest against social discrimination and economic exploitation, voicing the powerless. However, many scholars doubt their historicity, citing lack of corroborating evidence and implausible scenarios (e.g., a Muslim Sultan taking orders from Hindu Brahmins or Kabir’s mother).

### Legacy: Kabir Panth and Sikh Scripture
Kabir’s literary legacy was promoted by disciples Bhāgodās and Dharamdas. Kshitimohan Sen collected songs from mendicants; Tagore translated them (1915); Arvind Krishna Mehrotra later produced translations praised for capturing Kabir’s "ferocity and improvisational energy." The **Kabir Panth** (a Sant Mat sect) formed in the 17th–18th centuries, recognizing Kabir as founder. Panthis (~9.6 million today) are vegetarians who abstain from alcohol; two temples in Varanasi (one Hindu-run, one Muslim-run) sing his songs daily with similar *aarti* and *prasad* rituals.

#### Kabir, Guru Nanak, and the Guru Granth Sahib
Kabir’s verses constitute the largest non-Sikh contribution to the *Adi Granth* (Guru Granth Sahib). Scholars debate his influence on Guru Nanak (founder of Sikhism, 15th century). Hew McLeod places Nanak in the Sant tradition alongside Kabir, arguing Nanak reproduced their fundamental doctrines. Surjit Singh Gandhi and J.S. Grewal disagree, stressing fundamental differences in thought, practice, and theology, and criticizing McLeod for ignoring practices and differences while highlighting conceptual similarities.

### Views on Women
Kabir’s depiction of women draws criticism. Nikky-Guninder Kaur Singh and Wendy Doniger label it contemptuous and misogynistic; Schomer cites metaphors like *kali nagini* (black cobra), *kunda naraka ka* (pit of hell), *juthani jagata ki* (refuse of the world), viewing women as obstacles to men’s spiritual progress. Conversely, Dass interprets the *Rag Asa* section of the *Adi Granth* as Kabir urging a woman to stop veiling—a critique of social custom. Dass argues Kabir’s "woman" can be read allegorically: the soul (bride) yearning for Rama (Lord-husband), not a literal human female.

## Terms
- ****Nirguna**** — "Without qualities/formless"; the conception of the Absolute (Brahman) as devoid of attributes, central to Kabir’s theology.
- ****Sadhukkadi / Panchmel Khichri**** — The hybrid vernacular language of Kabir’s poetry ("five-grain porridge"), mixing Khadi boli, Braj, Bhojpuri, Marwari, and Awadhi.
- ****Bāņī**** — "Utterance"; Kabir’s term for his orally composed poems of wisdom.
- ****Dohe / Śalokā / Sākhī**** — Forms of Kabir’s verses: couplets (dohe), stanzas (śalokā), and "witness" verses (sākhī) testifying to Truth.
- ****Sant Mat**** — "Path of the Sants"; a devotional tradition emphasizing inner realization, nirguna bhakti, and a living guru; Kabir Panth is a major sect.
- ****Kabir Panth**** — "Path of Kabir"; the religious community (sect) founded post-Kabir (17th–18th c.) recognizing him as founder; members are Kabir Panthis.
- ****Advaita Vedanta**** — Non-dualist school (systematized by Adi Shankara) teaching the identity of Atman (soul) and Brahman (ultimate reality); philosophical backdrop for Kabir’s nirguna.
- ****Bhakti Movement**** — Medieval Indian devotional movement emphasizing personal love for God over ritual; Kabir is a key nirguna exponent.
- ****Adi Granth / Guru Granth Sahib**** — The central scripture of Sikhism, compiled 1604; contains Kabir’s verses as the largest non-Sikh contribution.
- ****Sākhī**** — Literally "witness"; a verse form implying the poem is evidence of the Truth.

## Debates and Open Questions
1.  **Dates and Biography**: No consensus on birth/death (1398–1448 vs. 1440–1518); miraculous birth legends vs. historical weaver-family account.
2.  **Textual Authenticity**: Oral transmission (15th–17th c.) created multiple recensions, interpolations, and fabricated verses; no definitive critical edition exists for most texts (except *Adi Granth*).
3.  **Religious Synthesis vs. Rejection**: Did Kabir synthesize Hinduism and Islam, or radically reject both while using their vocabulary? (Hess vs. synthesis views).
4.  **Islamic/Sufi Influence**: Extent of borrowing is controversial—rejection of Islam (Lorenzen, Panth sadhus) vs. Sufi imagery/co-development theories.
5.  **Persecution Legends**: Historicity doubted due to lack of evidence and narrative implausibilities; viewed as hagiographic protest literature vs. historical record.
6.  **Influence on Guru Nanak**: McLeod (Sant tradition continuity, doctrinal similarity) vs. Gandhi/Grewal (fundamental theological/practical differences, flawed methodology).
7.  **Gender Views**: Literal misogyny (Singh, Doniger, Schomer) vs. allegorical soul-symbolism (Dass) vs. social critique of veiling/customs.
