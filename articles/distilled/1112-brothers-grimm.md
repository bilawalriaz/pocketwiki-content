# Brothers Grimm

## Overview

Jacob (1785–1863) and Wilhelm (1786–1859) Grimm were German academics, linguists, and cultural researchers whose collection and scholarly treatment of European folktales established the foundations of modern folkloristics and significantly influenced the study of the German language. Born in Hanau and raised in Steinau, they were educated in Kassel and at the University of Marburg, where they developed a lasting interest in medieval literature and German cultural history. Their first collection of folk tales, *Children's and Household Tales* (*Kinder- und Hausmärchen*), was published in 1812 and grew from 86 stories to over 200 through successive editions. Their work popularized tales such as "Cinderella," "Hansel and Gretel," "Rapunzel," and "Snow White," and their methodological approach to documenting oral traditions set lasting standards for folklore studies.

## Early Lives and Education

Jacob Ludwig Karl Grimm and Wilhelm Carl Grimm were born in Hanau in the Landgraviate of Hesse-Kassel, within the Holy Roman Empire, to Philipp Wilhelm Grimm, a jurist, and Dorothea Grimm (née Zimmer), daughter of a Kassel city councilman. They were the second- and third-eldest surviving siblings in a family of nine children, three of whom died in infancy. In 1791, the family moved to Steinau during Philipp's employment there as a district magistrate (Amtmann). The children were educated at home by private tutors, receiving strict instruction as Lutherans, which instilled in both a lifelong religious faith.

In 1796, Philipp Grimm died of pneumonia, causing great poverty for the large family. Dorothea was forced to relinquish the brothers' servants and large house, depending on financial support from her father and sister, who was then the first lady-in-waiting at the court of William I, Elector of Hesse. Jacob was the eldest living son, forced at age 11 to assume adult responsibilities (shared with Wilhelm) for the next two years. The two brothers then followed the advice of their grandfather, who continually exhorted them to be industrious. They left Steinau in 1798 to attend the Friedrichsgymnasium in Kassel, arranged and paid for by their aunt. At Marburg University, they became aware that students of lower social status were not treated equally and had to request dispensation to study law. Their poverty kept them from student activities, but their outsider status worked in their favor, and they pursued their studies with extra vigor.

## Scholarly Development and Methodology

At Marburg, the brothers were inspired by their law professor Friedrich von Savigny, who awakened in them an interest in history and philology. Through Savigny and his circle of German romantics such as Clemens Brentano and Ludwig Achim von Arnim, they were introduced to the ideas of Johann Gottfried Herder, who advocated that German literature should revert to simpler forms defined as *Volkspoesie* (natural poetry) as opposed to *Kunstpoesie* (artistic poetry). The brothers dedicated themselves enthusiastically to studying medieval German literature, viewing culture as tied to language and regarding the purest cultural expression in the grammar of a language.

They moved away from Brentano's practice of changing original oral styles to more literary forms, believing that the style of the people (*Volk*) reflected natural and divinely inspired poetry. Their methodology for collecting and recording folk stories became the basis for studies in folklore. Between 1812 and 1857, their collection of *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* went through many editions, growing from 86 stories to more than 200.

## Major Works and Career

In 1807, at Brentano's request, the brothers began gathering tales in an organized fashion. By 1810 they had produced a manuscript collection of several dozen tales, written after inviting storytellers to their homes and transcribing what they heard. These tales were heavily modified in transcription, many having roots in previously written sources. The first volume of *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* was published in 1812 with 86 folk tales, and a second volume with 70 additional tales was published late in 1814 (dated 1815 on the title page); together the two volumes and their 156 tales are considered the first of the Large editions.

In 1812 they also published *Die beiden ältesten deutschen Gedichte aus dem achten Jahrhundert* (The Two Oldest German Poems of the Eighth Century), containing the Song of Hildebrand and Hadubrand and the Wessobrunn Prayer.

Between 1816 and 1818, the brothers published *Deutsche Sagen* (German Legends), consisting of 585 German legends organized according to region and historical legends about real people or events. Unlike the fairy tale collection, *Deutsche Sagen* sold poorly but became a vital source for folklorists and critics.

In 1838, the brothers began work on a definitive dictionary, the *Deutsches Wörterbuch* (German Dictionary), an ambitious historical dictionary that remained uncompleted at their deaths. The first volume was not published until 1854, and the work continued until 1960, resulting in 32 volumes.

## Political Engagement and Later Years

In 1837, the brothers lost their university posts after joining the Göttingen Seven in protest against King Ernest Augustus of Hanover's dissolution of the parliament and demands for oaths of allegiance. For refusing to sign the oath, the seven professors were dismissed and three were deported from Hanover—including Jacob, who went to Kassel. He was later joined there by Wilhelm, Dortchen, and their four children. Without income and in extreme financial difficulty, they began the lifelong project of the German Dictionary.

In 1840, Savigny and Bettina von Arnim successfully appealed to Frederick William IV of Prussia on behalf of the brothers, who were offered posts at the University of Berlin. After the revolutions of 1848, both brothers were elected to the civil parliament, with Jacob becoming a prominent member of the National Assembly at Mainz. However, their political activities were short-lived as their hopes for a unified Germany dwindled.

Wilhelm died of an infection in Berlin on 16 December 1859, and Jacob, deeply upset by his death, became increasingly reclusive. He continued working on the dictionary until his own death on 20 September 1863.

## Reception and Legacy

*Kinder- und Hausmärchen* was published 17 times between 1812 and 1864, with seven Large editions and ten Small editions. It was not an immediate bestseller but its popularity increased with each new edition. By the 1870s, the tales had gained great popularity and were added to the teaching curriculum in Prussia. The work has maintained status as one of the most popular books in Germany, second only to the Bible.

The Grimms' tales have been translated into more than 160 languages, with 120 different editions available in the US alone. During the Third Reich, the Nazi regime promoted the tales as essential reading for every household, while in occupied Germany the book was temporarily banned. In the 20th century, educators debated the value of teaching stories containing violence and brutality, leading to sanitized versions of many tales.

The publication of Bruno Bettelheim's 1976 *The Uses of Enchantment* brought renewed interest in the stories as children's literature, emphasizing their therapeutic value. The Grimms' work continues to influence research in folklore and the history of the German language, with their methodological approach establishing lasting standards for the study of oral traditions.

## Terms

- **Volkspoesie**: Natural poetry of the people, as opposed to artificial art poetry (*Kunstpoesie*)
- **Hochdeutsch**: Standard High German, the standardized form of the German language
- **Friedrichsgymnasium**: Secondary school in Kassel where the brothers studied
- **Göttingen Seven**: Group of seven liberal professors dismissed from the University of Göttingen in 1837 for political protest
- **Deutsches Wörterbuch**: The brothers' definitive German dictionary, begun in 1838
- **Kinder- und Hausmärchen**: The brothers' collection of children's and household tales, first published in 1812
- **Philology**: The study of language in written historical sources, combining literary criticism and linguistics
- **Romantic nationalism**: 19th-century movement emphasizing national identity through folklore and cultural traditions
- **Ölenberg manuscript**: Earliest extant version of the Grimms' collection, discovered in 1920
- **Spinnstube**: Spinning room where women gathered to spin thread and tell stories

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars debate the extent to which the Grimms' collection represents authentic German folklore versus literary construction. Maria Tatar argues that the brothers "appropriated as uniquely German stories, such as 'Little Red Riding Hood,' that had existed in many versions and regions throughout Europe." Some critics contend that the tales were insufficiently German, influencing the brothers' editorial choices and language use.

Feminist critics, including Emma Tennant, argue that the tales reflect patriarchal values, relegating female characters to virtues of patience or physical beauty while emphasizing male courage and chivalry. The psychological interpretation of the tales, particularly Jungian analyses linking the prevalence of female villains to the brothers' personal losses, remains debated among scholars.

The question of whether the Grimms were collectors or co-authors of their tales continues to generate scholarly discussion. While some argue that Wilhelm's extensive rewriting and addition of religious and moral elements transformed the original stories, others maintain that the brothers preserved essential cultural truths through their editorial process. The ongoing debate over the appropriateness of the original violent content for children's education reflects broader questions about the role of folklore in modern society.
