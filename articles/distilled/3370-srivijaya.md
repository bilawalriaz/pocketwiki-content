# Srivijaya

## Overview
Srivijaya was a powerful Malay thalassocracy (a state whose power is based on the sea) based in Sumatra, modern-day Indonesia, that dominated much of Maritime Southeast Asia from the 7th to 11th centuries. It was a major center for the spread of Buddhism and a crucial entrepôt (a port where goods are collected and redistributed) on the maritime trade routes between China and India. Its control of the Strait of Malacca made it a wealthy and influential empire, whose legacy includes the spread of the Old Malay language as a regional lingua franca.

## Timeline
- **682** — Kedukan Bukit inscription, the earliest known mention of "Srivijaya," is dated.
- **671** — Chinese monk Yijing visits Srivijaya for six months, describing it as a Buddhist center.
- **c. 680s** — Srivijaya integrates the Melayu Kingdom (Jambi), its first major regional conquest.
- **8th-9th c.** — The Sailendra dynasty of Java rules the Srivijayan mandala; Borobudur is built.
- **860** — Nalanda inscription records Srivijayan Maharaja Balaputra's dedication at Nalanda University.
- **990** — King Dharmawangsa of Java launches a naval invasion against Srivijaya.
- **1006** — Srivijaya repels the Javanese invasion and later helps destabilize the Mataram Kingdom.
- **1025** — Rajendra Chola I of the Chola Empire raids Srivijayan ports, severely weakening its hegemony.
- **1178** — Last recorded Srivijayan mission to China; the term "Sanfoqi" thereafter refers to Jambi.
- **1918** — French historian George Cœdès formally postulates the existence of the forgotten empire.

## Body

### Formation and Early Growth
The earliest reference to Srivijaya dates to the 7th century. The Kedukan Bukit inscription (682) describes its founding by Dapunta Hyang Sri Jayanasa, who led a large force from Minanga Tamwan to consolidate power in Southeast Sumatra. The empire was organized into three zones: the estuarine capital at Palembang, the Musi River basin hinterland, and rival estuarine areas. Through conquest and alliances, Srivijaya absorbed the gold-rich Melayu Kingdom and controlled trade on the Strait of Malacca. Its political system was a mandala, a network of semi-autonomous port cities and vassal states that swore allegiance to the central Srivijayan maharaja (king).

### Golden Age and Regional Dominance
From the 8th to 10th centuries, Srivijaya reached its peak. The Sailendra dynasty, based in Java, became intertwined with the Srivijayan lineage, and at times ruled the entire mandala from Java. Srivijaya's main foreign interest was securing lucrative trade agreements with China, which lasted from the Tang to the Song dynasty. It also had religious and trade links with the Pala Empire of Bengal and the Islamic Caliphate. The empire's wealth, noted by the 10th-century Arab writer Ibn Rustah, came from its role as a regional trade hub, collecting and exporting commodities like camphor, spices, gold, and tin.

### Decline and Fall
Srivijaya's decline was triggered by foreign raids and internal rivalries. In 1025, Rajendra Chola I of the Chola Empire launched a devastating naval raid, capturing the Srivijayan king and plundering its ports. This attack shattered Srivijaya's hegemony and enabled the rise of regional kingdoms like Kedah and Jambi. The center of power shifted from Palembang to Jambi (Malayu). Although the Chinese term "Sanfoqi" was used for centuries afterward, it referred to the successor Malayu Dharmasraya kingdom. Srivijaya was later subjugated by the Javanese kingdoms of Singhasari and Majapahit.

### Economy and Society
Srivijaya's economy was a prestige goods-based system reliant on controlling maritime trade. It used a "riverine system model," dominating river-mouth centers to control the flow of goods from the hinterland. The empire traded a vast array of commodities, including spices, aromatics, precious metals, and Chinese ceramics. Its society was complex and stratified, as evidenced by the many official titles listed in the Telaga Batu inscription. While Buddhism, particularly Vajrayana, was the dominant religion and a key cultural force, Hinduism also coexisted. The use of Old Malay in inscriptions, rather than Sanskrit, helped spread the language as a regional lingua franca.

## Terms
- **Thalassocracy**: A state whose power is primarily derived from its naval and maritime dominance.
- **Mandala**: A political model common in classical Southeast Asia, where a central polity exerts influence over a network of semi-autonomous vassal states through ritual, alliances, and coercion, rather than fixed borders.
- **Kadatuan**: A local principality or polity within the Srivijayan mandala, ruled by a datu (chieftain).
- **Entrepôt**: A central port where goods are imported, stored, and re-exported, facilitating trade between different regions.
- **Siddhayatra**: A sacred journey or pilgrimage, as described in early Srivijayan inscriptions.
- **Vajrayana**: A form of Buddhism that emphasizes rituals and esoteric practices, which flourished in Srivijaya.
- **Old Malay**: The ancestor of the modern Malay language, used in Srivijayan inscriptions and as a trade lingua franca.
- **Sanfoqi/Sanfotsi**: The name used in Chinese historical records to refer to Srivijaya and its successor states.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Location of the Capital**: The traditional capital is Palembang, but archaeological evidence there is scarce. Some scholars argue for Muaro Jambi (Jambi) due to its richer temple remains, while others suggest locations in Java or the Malay Peninsula.
- **Nature of the State**: The exact political structure is debated. Was it a centralized empire, a loose confederation of port cities, or a "thalassocratic mandala" defined by its center rather than fixed borders?
- **Reasons for the Chola Attack**: The causes of the 1025 Chola raid are uncertain. Theories include Srivijaya obstructing Chola trade, a desire for prestige, or a pre-emptive strike related to regional alliances.
- **Archaeological Visibility**: The lack of monumental stone architecture in Palembang, unlike in Java, has led to debates about the nature of its urban settlement, which may have been based on floating houses and impermanent structures.
