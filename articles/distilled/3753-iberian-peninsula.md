# Iberian Peninsula

## Overview
The Iberian Peninsula is a large landmass in southwestern Europe, separated from the rest of the continent by the Pyrenees. It is the second-largest European peninsula by area and is home to the modern nations of Spain and Portugal, as well as Andorra, Gibraltar, and a small part of France. Its history is defined by successive waves of settlement, conquest, and cultural synthesis, making it a key region for understanding European and Mediterranean development.

## Timeline
- **c. 500 BCE** — Greek geographer Hecataeus of Miletus first uses the term "Iberia."
- **226 BCE** — Ebro Treaty between Rome and Carthage uses the River Ebro (Ibēros) as a boundary.
- **218 BCE** — Roman troops begin the conquest of the peninsula during the Second Punic War.
- **711 CE** — Muslim army from North Africa conquers the Visigothic Kingdom, beginning the era of Al-Andalus.
- **756 CE** — Abd al-Rahman I establishes the independent Emirate of Córdoba.
- **1085 CE** — Alfonso VI of León-Castile conquers Toledo, a major event in the Christian Reconquista.
- **1492 CE** — The Catholic Monarchs conquer Granada, ending Muslim rule; Jews are expelled from Spain.
- **1580-1640** — The Iberian Union politically unites the crowns of Spain and Portugal.
- **1609-1614** — Expulsion of the Moriscos (Muslims who had converted to Christianity) from Spain.
- **1823** — French geographer Jean-Baptiste Bory de Saint-Vincent coins the modern term "Iberian Peninsula."

## Body

### Etymology and Names
The name "Iberia" is anciently linked to the River Ebro (Ibēros in Greek). The Greek term Ἰβηρία (Ibēríā) was used by geographers like Strabo to describe the peninsula. The Romans used *Hispania* and *Hiberia* as synonyms, though *Hispania* became the standard Latin name. The modern phrase "Iberian Peninsula" was coined in 1823; prior terms included "Spanish Peninsula" or "Pyrenaean Peninsula."

### Prehistory and Proto-history
Human habitation dates back at least 1.2 million years. Neanderthals entered around 200,000 BP, followed by anatomically modern humans about 40,000 years ago. The Neolithic saw the spread of megalithic cultures and the Cardium culture from the east Mediterranean. The Chalcolithic (c. 3000 BCE) saw the rise of complex cultures and the Beaker culture. The Bronze Age began c. 2100 BCE, with cultures like El Argar showing signs of early state societies. By the Iron Age (8th century BCE), the peninsula was home to Celtic and Pre-Celtic peoples. Phoenicians began coastal exploration in the 12th century BCE, founding colonies like Gadir (c. 1100 BCE), followed by Greeks and Carthaginians.

### Roman Rule and Romanization
Rome conquered the peninsula starting in 218 BCE, dividing it into provinces like Hispania Ulterior and Citerior. The process of Romanization introduced Latin, which evolved into the modern Iberian Romance languages (except Basque). Hispania became a major source of minerals, agricultural products, and manufactured goods for the empire. The Romans also introduced urbanization and infrastructure.

### Medieval Period: Al-Andalus and the Christian Kingdoms
In 711, a Muslim army conquered the Visigothic Kingdom, establishing Al-Andalus. The region became a center of culture and learning, especially under the Caliphate of Córdoba. In the north, Christian kingdoms like León, Castile, and Aragon gradually expanded southward in the process known as the Reconquista. This period saw complex alliances and conflicts between Christian and Muslim polities. The fall of the Caliphate of Córdoba in the 11th century led to the taifa kingdoms, which were later challenged by North African dynasties like the Almoravids and Almohads.

### Early Modern Period and Imperial Expansion
The completion of the Reconquista with the fall of Granada in 1492 coincided with the start of Spanish and Portuguese overseas empires. The Iberian Union (1580–1640) united the two crowns under the Spanish Habsburgs. This era saw the expulsion of Jews and Moriscos, significant demographic shifts, and the influx of American bullion, which fueled European commerce but also contributed to later economic challenges.

### Modern Era
The 17th century was marked by economic recession and the end of the Iberian Union with the Portuguese Restoration War (1640–1668). The 19th century brought liberal revolutions and the start of modern state formation. The 20th century saw significant urban development and population shifts after World War II.

## Terms
- **Al-Andalus**: The Arabic name for the parts of the Iberian Peninsula under Muslim rule from 711 to 1492.
- **Reconquista**: The centuries-long process by which Christian kingdoms expanded southward, reclaiming territory from Muslim rule.
- **Romanization**: The spread of Roman culture, language (Latin), and institutions across the peninsula.
- **Paleohispanic scripts**: Ancient writing systems developed in the Iberian Peninsula before the Roman conquest, derived from the Phoenician alphabet.
- **Meseta Central**: A vast, elevated plateau covering much of the interior of the peninsula.
- **Iberian gauge**: A non-standard railway track gauge of 1,668 mm used traditionally in Spain and Portugal.
- **Moriscos**: Muslims in Christian-controlled Iberia who had converted to Christianity, often under duress.
- **Taifas**: The independent Muslim principalities that emerged after the collapse of the Caliphate of Córdoba.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact origin and meaning of the name "Iber" remain unknown, as the ancient Iberian language is undeciphered.
- The nature of the relationship between the Phoenician colony of Gadir and Carthage is debated, ranging from Carthaginian hegemony to greater Phoenician autonomy.
- The concept of the "Reconquista" is noted by some scholars as a product of later nationalist claims, potentially oversimplifying the complex centuries of interaction between Muslim and Christian kingdoms.
