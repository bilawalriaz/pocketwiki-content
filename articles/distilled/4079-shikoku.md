# Shikoku

## Overview
Shikoku is the smallest of Japan's four main islands, located south of Honshu and northeast of Kyushu. It is divided into four prefectures and is historically significant as the home of the 88-temple Buddhist pilgrimage and traditional culture, though it has faced population decline in recent decades.

## Timeline
- **c. 14 million years ago** — Major volcanic caldera active in the Mount Ishizuchi area during the Miocene.
- **Heian period (794–1185)** — Buddhist priest Kūkai establishes the 88-temple pilgrimage route.
- **Edo period (1603–1868)** — Sanuki udon becomes a dietary staple in Kagawa Prefecture due to wheat production.
- **1953** — Shikoku region's population reaches its peak.
- **1988** — Great Seto Bridge network opens, connecting central Shikoku to Honshu by road.
- **1998** — Kobe-Awaji-Naruto Expressway opens, linking eastern Shikoku to the Kansai area.
- **2014** — Tokushima Vortis football team competes in Japan's top division (J1).
- **2021** — Tokushima Vortis has a second spell in the J1 division.
- **2025** — Total population of Shikoku is 3,486,739.

## Body

### Geography and Climate
Shikoku covers about 18,800 square kilometres and is the 50th largest island in the world. A central mountain range divides it into a narrow, populated northern subregion facing the Seto Inland Sea and a larger, mountainous, sparsely populated southern area facing the Pacific Ocean. The highest point is Mount Ishizuchi (1,982 m). The major river is the Yoshino River (196 km long), famous for white-water rafting. Unlike Japan's other main islands, Shikoku has no active volcanoes. The climate supports intensive agriculture, including rice, wheat, and a wide variety of citrus fruits.

### Population and Economy
Shikoku had a population of 3,486,739 in 2025, making it the least populated of Japan's main islands. The largest city is Matsuyama (507,137). The region's population peaked in 1953 and has experienced negative growth since 1990. Industry includes ore processing (notably from the Besshi copper mine), pulp and paper, and agriculture. The warm southern climate allows for two annual rice crops and out-of-season vegetable farming.

### Culture and Traditions
Shikoku's historical isolation helped preserve traditional Japanese characteristics. It is most famous for the 88-temple pilgrimage (Shikoku Henro) established by the monk Kūkai. Pilgrims wear white jackets with the phrase "dōgyō ninin" ("two traveling together"). Major festivals include the Awa Odori dance in Tokushima and the Yosakoi festival in Kōchi. The island's cuisine features Sanuki udon, seared bonito, and abundant citrus fruits like yuzu and mikan. Natural farmer Masanobu Fukuoka developed his methods here.

### Transportation
Shikoku is connected to Honshu by three expressways: the Kobe-Awaji-Naruto (east), Nishiseto (west), and Seto-Chūō (central). The Great Seto Bridge network, completed in 1988, was expected to boost economic development, which has not fully materialized. The Shikoku Railway Company (JR Shikoku) operates several lines, including the Seto Ōhashi Line across the bridge. The island has four regional airports with domestic flights and some international service to Seoul. Ferries also provide links to Honshu, Kyushu, and surrounding islands.

## Terms
- **Shikoku Henro**: The 88-temple Buddhist pilgrimage circuit on the island, established by Kūkai.
- **Dōgyō ninin**: Phrase meaning "two traveling together," worn by pilgrims to signify Kūkai's companionship.
- **Sanuki udon**: A regional noodle dish from Kagawa Prefecture (formerly Sanuki Province), known for its firm texture.
- **Seto Inland Sea**: The body of water separating Shikoku from Honshu to the north.
- **Alluvial plain**: Flat, fertile land formed by river sediment, used for intensive agriculture like rice paddies.
- **Caldera**: A large volcanic crater formed by a major eruption and collapse, like the prehistoric one near Mount Ishizuchi.
- **Truck farming**: Commercial gardening of vegetables, often for distant markets, facilitated by Shikoku's mild southern climate.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The expected economic development from the completion of the Honshu-Shikoku bridges has not fully materialized, raising questions about the long-term impact of improved connectivity on the region's economy.
- The causes and potential solutions for Shikoku's sustained population decline since 1990 are ongoing concerns for regional planning.
