# Cuban Missile Crisis

## Overview
The Cuban Missile Crisis was a 13-day confrontation in October 1962 between the United States and the Soviet Union, triggered by the discovery of Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba. It is widely considered the closest the Cold War came to escalating into full-scale nuclear war, ending with a secret agreement that averted conflict but had profound political consequences for both superpowers.

## Timeline
- **1959-1961** — US deploys nuclear missiles in the UK, Italy, and Turkey, within range of Moscow.
- **April 1961** — US-backed Bay of Pigs invasion of Cuba fails.
- **July 1962** — Soviet and Cuban leaders agree to secretly place nuclear missiles in Cuba.
- **14 October 1962** — US U-2 spy plane photographs Soviet missile construction sites in Cuba.
- **22 October 1962** — President Kennedy announces a naval "quarantine" of Cuba in a televised address.
- **24 October 1962** — Quarantine goes into effect; some Soviet ships turn back.
- **27 October 1962** — "Black Saturday": A US U-2 is shot down over Cuba; a Soviet submarine nearly launches a nuclear torpedo.
- **28 October 1962** — Khrushchev announces the dismantling of Soviet missiles in Cuba, resolving the crisis.
- **November 1962** — US blockade ends after Soviet missiles and bombers are withdrawn.
- **April 1963** — US secretly removes Jupiter missiles from Turkey and Italy.

## Body

### Background and Causes
The crisis emerged from escalating Cold War tensions. The US had deployed nuclear missiles in Europe targeting the USSR and, after the failed 1961 Bay of Pigs invasion, sponsored a campaign of sabotage and terrorism against Cuba. The Soviet Union, concerned about a potential US invasion of Cuba and a Cuban drift toward China, agreed with Fidel Castro to secretly deploy nuclear missiles on the island. This would deter a US invasion and help redress the strategic nuclear imbalance, as the US held a significant advantage in ICBMs and warheads.

### Discovery and Confrontation
On 14 October 1962, a US U-2 flight confirmed the presence of medium-range ballistic missile sites. President Kennedy convened the Executive Committee of the National Security Council (EXCOMM) to debate responses. After rejecting an air strike and invasion as too escalatory, Kennedy chose a naval "quarantine" to prevent further missiles from reaching Cuba. Announced on 22 October, the quarantine was a limited military action designed to avoid the formal act of war a blockade would imply. The US raised its military alert level to DEFCON 2, the highest ever.

### Secret Negotiations and Resolution
The crisis peaked on "Black Saturday," 27 October, when a US U-2 was shot down and a Soviet submarine, depth-charged by US ships, nearly launched a nuclear torpedo. That day, Khrushchev sent two letters to Kennedy. The second publicly proposed trading the Cuban missiles for the removal of US Jupiter missiles from Turkey. The US, while publicly ignoring the second letter, secretly agreed to the trade. Robert Kennedy informed Soviet Ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin that the Jupiter missiles would be removed "within a short time" if the deal remained secret. On 28 October, Khrushchev announced the dismantling of the Cuban missiles. The US publicly pledged not to invade Cuba, and the secret removal of the Jupiter missiles was completed by April 1963.

### Aftermath and Consequences
The crisis ended with a perception of Soviet retreat, embarrassing Khrushchev and contributing to his ouster in 1964. For the US, it was a political victory, though General Curtis LeMay called it "the greatest defeat in our history." The near-catastrophe led to the establishment of the Moscow–Washington hotline for direct communication and the Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty. The crisis also influenced future US foreign policy, with Kennedy viewing Vietnam as the next place to "draw a line in the sand" against communism.

## Terms
- **Quarantine**: The naval blockade imposed by the US, termed a "quarantine" to avoid the legal implication of a formal blockade, which is an act of war.
- **EXCOMM**: The Executive Committee of the National Security Council, the group of senior advisers convened by Kennedy to manage the crisis.
- **DEFCON**: Defense Readiness Condition, a measure of US military alertness; DEFCON 2 is the second-highest level, just below nuclear war.
- **Operation Anadyr**: The codename for the secret Soviet plan to deploy nuclear missiles and troops to Cuba.
- **Maskirovka**: A Russian military doctrine of deception and denial, used by the Soviets to conceal the missile deployment.
- **Jupiter missiles**: US intermediate-range ballistic missiles deployed in Italy and Turkey, which became a key bargaining chip in the crisis resolution.
- **Hotline**: The direct communication link established between Washington and Moscow after the crisis to prevent future misunderstandings.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Strategic Impact**: Whether the 40 Soviet missiles in Cuba would have significantly altered the nuclear balance is debated. US Defense Secretary McNamara argued they made little difference given the existing US superiority.
- **Khrushchev's Motives**: Historians debate whether Khrushchev's primary goal was to defend Cuba, gain leverage on Berlin, or correct the strategic nuclear imbalance.
- **Castro's Role**: The extent to which Castro was a willing partner versus a pressured junior ally in accepting the missiles remains a point of analysis.
- **Near-Misses**: Revelations about the submarine B-59 close call and the presence of 100+ tactical nuclear warheads in Cuba (unknown to the US at the time) have led to reassessments that the risk of nuclear war was even higher than participants realized.
