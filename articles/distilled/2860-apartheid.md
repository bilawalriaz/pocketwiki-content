# Apartheid

## Overview
Apartheid was a system of institutionalized racial segregation and discrimination in South Africa and South West Africa (now Namibia) from 1948 to 1994. It was characterized by an authoritarian political culture based on *baasskap* (white domination), which ensured the minority white population dominated politically, socially, and economically. The system classified the population into a rigid racial hierarchy and enforced separation in nearly all aspects of life, leaving a legacy of profound inequality that persists today.

## Timeline
- **1948** — Herenigde Nasionale Party (HNP) wins election on apartheid platform; D.F. Malan becomes Prime Minister.
- **1949-1950** — First apartheid laws passed: Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act, Immorality Act, Population Registration Act.
- **1950** — Group Areas Act passed, enforcing residential segregation.
- **1955** — Freedom Charter adopted by Congress Alliance, envisioning a non-racial South Africa.
- **1960** — Sharpeville massacre; ANC and PAC banned.
- **1961** — South Africa becomes a republic and leaves the Commonwealth.
- **1964** — Nelson Mandela and others sentenced to life imprisonment in the Rivonia Trial.
- **1976** — Soweto uprising against Afrikaans-medium instruction.
- **1983** — Tricameral Constitution introduced, granting limited representation to Coloureds and Indians but excluding Blacks.
- **1985-1990** — States of Emergency declared to quell internal resistance.
- **1990** — F.W. de Klerk unbans ANC, PAC, and SACP; Nelson Mandela is released.
- **1991** — Apartheid legislation formally repealed.
- **1994** — First non-racial democratic elections held; Nelson Mandela elected President.

## Body

### Precursors and Ideology
Apartheid's roots lay in centuries of colonial racial discrimination, including Dutch and British pass laws, land dispossession, and labour controls. The ideology was formalized by the National Party after its 1948 election victory, which promised to ensure continued white domination. It was justified through theories of "separate development," where different racial groups were deemed distinct nations requiring separate political and economic structures. The system was divided into "petty apartheid" (segregation of public facilities) and "grand apartheid" (large-scale territorial separation and removals).

### Legislation and the Homeland System
The apartheid government enacted a comprehensive legal framework to enforce segregation. Key laws included the Population Registration Act (1950), which classified all South Africans into racial groups, and the Group Areas Act (1950), which dictated where people could live. A central pillar was the homeland (or Bantustan) system, which aimed to strip Black South Africans of their citizenship by assigning them to ten nominally independent territories. Between 1960 and 1983, 3.5 million Black people were forcibly removed to these homelands. Four homelands—Transkei, Bophuthatswana, Venda, and Ciskei—were declared independent, though recognized only by South Africa.

### Society and Resistance
Apartheid permeated every facet of society, from education and healthcare to sport and social interaction. The Bantu Education Act (1953) created a separate, inferior education system for Black students. Resistance grew from the 1950s onward, including the ANC's Defiance Campaign and the formation of its armed wing, Umkhonto we Sizwe, in 1961. The 1976 Soweto uprising and the rise of the Black Consciousness Movement further galvanized opposition. The government responded with brutal crackdowns, states of emergency, and the detention and torture of activists.

### International Relations and the End of Apartheid
Apartheid drew widespread international condemnation, leading to South Africa's withdrawal from the Commonwealth in 1961 and its increasing isolation through sports boycotts and economic sanctions. The Cold War context saw the apartheid regime frame itself as an anti-communist bastion, receiving some support from Western powers like the US and UK. By the late 1980s, a combination of internal resistance, economic pressure, and regional instability forced the National Party to negotiate. President F.W. de Klerk initiated reforms, unbanning liberation movements and releasing political prisoners. Negotiations between the government and the ANC culminated in the 1994 elections, which marked the official end of apartheid.

## Terms
- **Apartheid**: Afrikaans for "separateness"; the official policy of racial segregation and white minority rule in South Africa (1948-1994).
- **Baasskap**: Afrikaans for "boss-ship"; the ideology of white supremacy and domination that underpinned apartheid.
- **Bantustans (Homelands)**: Ten territories designated for Black ethnic groups under apartheid, intended to be independent nation-states to strip Black South Africans of their citizenship.
- **Group Areas Act (1950)**: Law that enforced residential segregation by assigning specific urban areas to different racial groups.
- **Population Registration Act (1950)**: Law that classified every South African into a racial group (White, Black, Coloured, Indian).
- **Tricameral Parliament**: A 1983 constitutional reform that created separate houses of parliament for White, Coloured, and Indian citizens, but excluded the Black majority.
- **Umkhonto we Sizwe (MK)**: The armed wing of the ANC, formed in 1961 to carry out sabotage against government and military targets.
- **Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC)**: A post-apartheid restorative justice body established to investigate human rights violations committed during apartheid.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The economic legacy of apartheid remains deeply contested, with ongoing debates about the effectiveness of post-1994 policies in redressing historical inequalities in land, wealth, and opportunity.
- Scholars debate the relative importance of internal resistance versus international pressure (sanctions, boycotts) in forcing the apartheid regime to the negotiating table.
- The role of the Cold War is analyzed, with questions about how Western anti-communist support prolonged the regime versus how Soviet support for liberation movements influenced the conflict.
- The effectiveness and moral implications of the TRC's amnesty-for-truth model continue to be discussed in the context of transitional justice globally.
