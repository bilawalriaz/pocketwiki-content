# Fugue

## Overview
A fugue is a complex contrapuntal compositional technique in classical music, built on a main theme (subject) that is introduced and then imitated across multiple independent voices. It represents one of the most developed forms of imitative counterpoint, serving as a fundamental demonstration of a composer's mastery of harmony and tonality, particularly during the Baroque era.

## Timeline
- **c. 1330** — The term "fuga" is first used as a theoretical term by Jacobus of Liege.
- **16th century** — The English term "fugue" originates; fugal technique begins to appear in instrumental and vocal works.
- **1550s** — Fugue is considered a technique of composition, not just for improvisation.
- **1725** — Johann Joseph Fux publishes *Gradus Ad Parnassum*, a highly influential treatise on counterpoint and fugue.
- **c. 1722–1742** — J.S. Bach composes *The Well-Tempered Clavier*, a landmark collection of preludes and fugues.
- **1772** — Joseph Haydn's "Sun" Quartets (Op. 20) include three fugal finales.
- **c. 1782** — Baron van Swieten introduces Mozart to the fugues of Bach and Handel, influencing his later work.
- **1826** — Beethoven completes the *Große Fuge* (Op. 133), a monumental and dissonant fugue for string quartet.
- **1936** — Béla Bartók's *Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta* opens with a celebrated slow fugue.
- **1951** — Dmitri Shostakovich begins composing his *24 Preludes and Fugues*, Op. 87.

## Body

### Structure and Technique
A fugue typically begins with an **exposition**, where the subject is stated alone in one voice in the tonic key. A second voice then enters with the **answer** (the subject transposed, usually to the dominant), while the first voice continues with a **countersubject** (new accompanying material) or free counterpoint. This process continues until all voices have entered. The exposition is followed by **episodes**—modulatory passages that develop material from the subject—and **middle entries**, where the subject returns in various related keys. The piece concludes with a final entry of the subject in the tonic key, often followed by a coda. Key techniques include **stretto** (overlapping entries of the subject) and various alterations of the subject, such as inversion or augmentation.

### Historical Development
The term "fuga" was used broadly in the Middle Ages for any imitative counterpoint. By the Renaissance, it specifically denoted imitative works. The fugue became a central compositional technique in the Baroque era, perfected by composers like J.S. Bach, whose works (e.g., *The Well-Tempered Clavier*, *The Art of Fugue*) remain the definitive models. In the Classical era, while no longer the central form, composers like Haydn and Mozart studied and incorporated fugues, often inspired by Bach and Handel. Beethoven used fugues extensively in his late works, expanding their expressive potential. The technique saw a resurgence in the 20th century, with composers like Bartók, Shostakovich, and Ligeti adapting it to modern harmonic languages.

### Types and Forms
Fugues are categorized by their structure and number of subjects. A **simple fugue** has one subject. A **double** or **triple fugue** has two or three subjects, which may be introduced simultaneously or in separate expositions. A **counter-fugue** prominently features an inverted (upside-down) version of the subject. A **permutation fugue** combines fugue and canon, with voices stating themes in a fixed, invertible pattern. A **fughetta** is a short, less formal fugue. A **mirror fugue** consists of two versions, one being the exact intervallic inversion of the other.

### Aesthetic and Theoretical Debate
A central debate is whether the fugue is a strict musical **form** or a flexible compositional **texture**. Musicologist Erwin Ratz argued it has a core formal structure based on harmonic plan (tonic emphasis in exposition/coda, modulation in episodes). Others, noting its adaptability, view it more as a technique. The fugue is often associated with intellectual rigor and profundity, a perception rooted in its complexity and its historical use for teaching counterpoint. Arnold Schoenberg described it as a composition with "maximum self-sufficiency of content," where all material derives from a single theme.

## Terms
- **Subject**: The main musical theme upon which a fugue is built.
- **Answer**: The subject transposed (usually to the dominant) as it enters in a second voice.
- **Countersubject**: A recurring accompanying theme that plays against the subject or answer, written in invertible counterpoint.
- **Exposition**: The opening section of a fugue where each voice states the subject or answer for the first time.
- **Episode**: A modulatory passage between subject entries, often developing material from the exposition.
- **Stretto**: A technique where entries of the subject overlap in different voices before the previous entry is complete.
- **Invertible counterpoint**: A contrapuntal technique where two or more melodic lines are written so they can be swapped in pitch (e.g., the higher part becomes the lower) without creating harmonic errors.
- **Tonic**: The home key of a musical piece or section.
- **Dominant**: The key a fifth above the tonic, a primary tonal area for modulation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Form vs. Texture**: Is the fugue a defined musical form with a prescribed harmonic structure, or is it a flexible compositional technique or texture? Scholars like Erwin Ratz argue for a core "Urform," while others emphasize its adaptability.
- **Perception and Aesthetics**: Why is the fugue historically associated with intellectual depth and seriousness? Is this due to its inherent complexity, its roots in pedagogy, or a long-standing cultural perception of counterpoint as profound?
- **Modern Adaptation**: How do 20th- and 21st-century composers reconcile the fugue's traditional tonal foundations with atonal, serial, or other modern harmonic systems? Works like Ligeti's *Requiem* push the technique into new textural and perceptual realms.
