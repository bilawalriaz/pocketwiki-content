# Cuban Revolution

## Overview
The Cuban Revolution (1953–1959) was a military and political movement that overthrew the dictatorship of Fulgencio Batista. It transformed Cuba from a U.S.-influenced republic into a socialist state allied with the Soviet Union, profoundly altering Cold War geopolitics and serving as both an inspiration for revolutionary movements and a catalyst for authoritarian crackdowns across Latin America.

## Timeline
- **10 March 1952** — Fulgencio Batista seizes power in a military coup, overthrowing President Carlos Prío Socarrás.
- **26 July 1953** — Fidel Castro leads a failed armed attack on the Moncada Barracks, marking the revolution's symbolic start.
- **15 May 1955** — Batista releases political prisoners, including the Castro brothers, who go into exile.
- **2 December 1956** — The yacht *Granma* lands in Cuba, carrying Fidel Castro, Che Guevara, and other revolutionaries.
- **17 January 1957** — Rebels win their first major victory at the Battle of La Plata in the Sierra Maestra.
- **13 March 1957** — The Revolutionary Directorate storms Havana's Presidential Palace in a failed attempt to assassinate Batista.
- **30 July 1957** — Revolutionary organizer Frank País is killed, sparking a massive general strike in Santiago.
- **14 March 1958** — The United States imposes an arms embargo on the Batista government.
- **31 December 1958** — Rebel forces capture Santa Clara; Batista flees Cuba the next day.
- **1 January 1959** — The revolution triumphs as Castro's forces take Santiago de Cuba.
- **October 1965** — The 26th of July Movement reforms into the Communist Party of Cuba.

## Body

### Background and Causes
In the 1950s, Cuba's economy was heavily dependent on sugar exports and U.S. investment, leading to high unemployment and stark inequalities. While national averages for life expectancy and GDP were high for Latin America, they masked severe rural poverty, with limited access to healthcare and education. Havana concentrated most infrastructure and wealth. The Batista regime (1952–1959) was marked by corruption, ties to organized crime, and repression, eroding public support. A history of political corruption and instability since independence in 1902 had created widespread disillusionment.

### The Armed Struggle (1953–1958)
After legal challenges to Batista's coup failed, Fidel Castro founded the 26th of July Movement (M-26-7). The failed Moncada attack in 1953 led to Castro's famous trial speech, "History Will Absolve Me." After amnesty and exile in Mexico, the movement reorganized. The 1956 *Granma* landing nearly failed, but survivors, including Castro, Che Guevara, and Raúl Castro, regrouped in the Sierra Maestra mountains. They waged a guerrilla war, supported by urban networks and other opposition groups like the Revolutionary Directorate. Key battles, such as La Plata (1957) and Santa Clara (1958), combined with a U.S. arms embargo and waning Batista support, led to the dictator's flight on 1 January 1959.

### Aftermath and Consolidation of Power
A provisional government under liberal lawyer Manuel Urrutia Lleó was initially established. However, Fidel Castro, as Prime Minister from February 1959, quickly consolidated power. He delayed promised elections, nationalized U.S. and Cuban properties, and aligned Cuba with the Soviet Union. This led to a complete rupture with the United States, which imposed a trade embargo and supported the failed Bay of Pigs invasion in 1961. The 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis cemented the Cold War standoff. Domestically, the revolution led to mass executions of Batista supporters, the suppression of dissent, and a wave of emigration, particularly among the upper and middle classes.

### International Impact and Legacy
The revolution made Cuba a key Soviet ally in the Western Hemisphere, leading to decades of economic dependence and military cooperation, including Cuban interventions in Africa (notably Angola). It inspired leftist movements across Latin America but also prompted conservative regimes to crack down on dissent. The U.S. embargo, tightened over decades, remains a central point of contention. The revolution's legacy includes the establishment of a one-party communist state, significant social gains in education and healthcare, and ongoing political and economic challenges following the collapse of the Soviet Union.

## Terms
- **26th of July Movement (M-26-7):** The primary revolutionary organization led by Fidel Castro, named after the date of the 1953 Moncada attack.
- **Sierra Maestra:** The mountain range in southeastern Cuba where Castro's guerrilla forces were based.
- **Operation Verano:** Batista's failed 1958 summer offensive to crush the rebels in the Sierra Maestra.
- **Popular Socialist Party:** Cuba's original communist party, which initially had little influence but later allied with Castro.
- **Revolutionary Directorate (DR):** A student-led opposition group that attempted to overthrow Batista independently, notably through the 1957 Presidential Palace attack.
- **Nationalization:** The revolutionary government's seizure of foreign (especially U.S.) and private Cuban-owned industries and properties.
- **Special Period:** The severe economic crisis in Cuba following the collapse of the Soviet Union and the end of Soviet subsidies in 1991.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Castro's Ideology:** Historians debate whether Fidel Castro was a committed communist from the start or evolved into one. Evidence includes his early anti-communist rhetoric, the movement's broad coalition, and his later Marxist-Leninist alignment.
- **Racial Dynamics:** The revolution's relationship with race is complex. While Batista's regime included many Afro-Cubans, some opposition rhetoric used racist stereotypes. Scholars debate the extent of mass black support for Batista versus the white predominance in the revolutionary movement.
- **U.S. Role:** The extent of U.S. involvement is debated, from its support of Batista to alleged early, covert funding of the 26th of July Movement and the planning of the Bay of Pigs invasion.
- **Soviet Alliance:** Scholars question whether the close Soviet-Cuban alliance was mutually beneficial or if the USSR primarily used Cuba as a strategic pawn in the Cold War, with Cuba bearing significant costs.
