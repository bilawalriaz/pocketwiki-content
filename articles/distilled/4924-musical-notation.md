# Musical notation

## Overview
Musical notation is any system used to visually represent music, allowing it to be recorded, transmitted, and interpreted. Its development is crucial for preserving musical traditions and enabling complex compositions, evolving from ancient mnemonic symbols to the precise, standardized systems used globally today.

## Timeline
- **c. 1400 BCE** — Earliest known musical notation on a cuneiform tablet from Nippur, Babylonia.
- **6th–4th century BCE** — Ancient Greek musical notation in use; few complete compositions survive.
- **6th century CE** — Byzantine neumes begin to develop for liturgical chant.
- **c. 850 CE** — Earliest surviving European neumatic notation for Gregorian chant.
- **13th century** — Franco of Cologne introduces mensural notation, allowing individual note rhythms.
- **c. 991–1033** — Guido d'Arezzo develops the modern staff and solmization (ut, re, mi...).
- **1252** — Safi al-Din al-Urmawi develops geometric rhythmic notation in the Near East.
- **15th–17th centuries** — Modern staff notation solidifies with regular bar lines and key/time signatures.
- **1931** — Cornelis Pot introduces Klavarskribo, an alternative chromatic staff notation.
- **1960s onward** — Graphic notation emerges, using non-traditional symbols for performance.
- **1980s onward** — Computer-based scorewriter software revolutionizes notation creation and editing.

## Body

### Ancient and Medieval Origins
The earliest notations were fragmentary and mnemonic. A cuneiform tablet from c. 1400 BCE in Babylonia provides the oldest known instructions for performing music. Ancient Greek notation (6th century BCE–4th century CE) used letter symbols for pitches but indicated rhythm only rudimentarily. In medieval Europe, monks developed neumes (symbols showing melodic contour) to standardize Gregorian chant, but these did not specify exact pitches or rhythms. The system evolved through the work of theorists like Franco of Cologne, who introduced mensural notation in the 13th century to indicate note durations.

### Development of Modern Western Staff Notation
The modern system was pioneered by Guido d'Arezzo (c. 991–after 1033), who established the five-line staff and solmization syllables (ut, re, mi...). Over the following centuries, notation became more precise. The use of regular bar lines to denote measures became common by the late 17th century. The system uses a staff of five lines where vertical position indicates pitch, modified by clefs (e.g., treble, bass), key signatures (sharps/flats), and accidentals. Note shapes and stems indicate duration. Time signatures (e.g., 4/4) define the rhythmic grouping of beats per measure.

### Notation in Global Traditions
Many cultures developed independent systems. Byzantine notation uses "differential" neumes indicating pitch steps rather than absolute notes, tied to a system of eight modes (echoi). In Korea, the Jeongganbo system (15th century) was the first East Asian notation to represent rhythm, pitch, and time. Indian classical music uses sargam, a solfege system (Sa, Re, Ga...), with notations like Swaralipi. Russian Znamenny Chant uses "hook and banner" signs that record melodic transitions and expressive qualities rather than discrete notes. Chinese Gongche notation used characters for scale degrees.

### Alternative and Modern Systems
Beyond the standard staff, numerous systems exist. Tablature (for instruments like lute and guitar) indicates finger positions on strings. Cipher notations, like the Chinese Jianpu or Javanese kepatihan, use numbers for scale degrees. Graphic notation, pioneered in the mid-20th century by composers like John Cage, uses abstract symbols to convey performance ideas. Computer technology has enabled digital scorewriters and new formats like ABC notation and MusicXML.

## Terms
- **Staff (Stave):** The set of five horizontal lines on which musical notes are written.
- **Clef:** A symbol at the start of a staff that assigns specific pitches to its lines and spaces (e.g., treble clef).
- **Key Signature:** A set of sharps or flats at the beginning of a staff indicating the key of the piece.
- **Time Signature:** A pair of numbers indicating the number of beats per measure and the note value that gets one beat.
- **Neume:** A basic element of early Western and Byzantine notation, a symbol representing a short musical phrase or melodic contour.
- **Mensural Notation:** A system where the shape of a note indicates its rhythmic duration.
- **Solfège:** A system of assigning syllables (do, re, mi...) to the notes of a musical scale.
- **Tablature:** A form of musical notation indicating instrument fingering rather than pitches.
- **Cipher Notation:** A system using numbers to represent scale degrees.
- **Graphic Notation:** The use of non-traditional symbols and text to convey musical performance instructions.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The interpretation of ancient notations, such as the Babylonian tablets and Greek fragments, remains controversial and incomplete.
- The exact formation and use of the medieval rhythmic modes described in *De Mensurabili Musica* are not fully clear.
- Scholars debate the precise meanings of "great signs" in Byzantine and Znamenny notation, which likely indicated embellishments and microtonal inflections.
- The "notational centricity" of Western musicology is critiqued for potentially biasing analysis towards notated traditions and overlooking oral practices.
