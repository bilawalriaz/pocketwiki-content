# Baffin Island

## Overview
Baffin Island, located in Nunavut, Canada, is the largest island in Canada and the fifth-largest in the world. It is a remote Arctic landmass with a sparse population, a harsh climate, and a rich history of Inuit habitation and early European exploration, making it a key site for studying Arctic ecology, archaeology, and climate change.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Earliest known inhabitants, the pre-Dorset people, settle the island.
- **c. 1000 CE** — Norse explorers, possibly led by Leif Erikson, sight the island, believed to be "Helluland."
- **1576** — English explorer Martin Frobisher makes landfall, naming it "Queen Elizabeth's Foreland."
- **1616** — William Baffin charts Baffin Bay; the island later receives his name.
- **1821** — Edward Parry officially bestows the name "Baffin Island."
- **1300 CE+** — Thule people, ancestors of the Inuit, completely replace the Dorset culture.
- **1987** — The community of Frobisher Bay votes to restore its Inuktitut name, Iqaluit.
- **2008** — Archaeologist Patricia Sutherland reports finds suggesting pre-Viking European contact.
- **2012** — Research announces Viking-era whetstones found on the island.
- **2015** — The Mary River iron ore mine begins operations.
- **2021** — Canadian census records the island's population as 13,039.

## Body

### Name and Geography
The island's Inuktitut name is *Qikiqtaaluk*, meaning "very big island." Its present English name honors explorer William Baffin. Geographically, it is separated from mainland Quebec by Hudson Strait and from Greenland by Davis Strait and Baffin Bay. The Baffin Mountains, part of the Arctic Cordillera, run along its northeastern coast, featuring peaks like Mount Odin (at least 2,143 m) and Mount Thor, noted for its extreme vertical drop. The two largest lakes are Nettilling Lake and Amadjuak Lake.

### History and Archaeology
Baffin Island has been inhabited for over 3,000 years by successive Paleo-Eskimo cultures: pre-Dorset, Dorset, and finally the Thule (ancestors of the Inuit). Norse sagas describe a land called "Helluland," which is thought to be Baffin Island. A major scholarly debate centers on archaeological finds reported by Patricia Sutherland, including yarn and artifacts that could indicate European contact before the Vikings. However, subsequent research, such as a 2018 study, argues that spun yarn technology was developed indigenously by Dorset peoples at least 2,000 years ago, predating any European arrival.

### Demographics and Economy
The population is predominantly Indigenous (74.06% as of 2016), with Inuit comprising 72.17% of the total. The capital, Iqaluit, has a higher non-Indigenous population. The economy includes mining, exemplified by the Mary River iron ore mine, which began shipping ore in 2015.

### Wildlife and Climate
The island hosts diverse Arctic wildlife. Year-round residents include barren-ground caribou, polar bears, Arctic foxes, and the Baffin Island wolf. Summer visitors include migratory birds, harp seals, walruses, beluga whales, and bowhead whales. The climate is extremely cold with long winters and foggy summers. Most of the island lies north of the Arctic Circle, experiencing polar night and midnight sun. The Barnes Ice Cap has been retreating since at least the 1960s.

## Terms
*   **Qikiqtaaluk**: The Inuktitut name for Baffin Island, meaning "very big island."
*   **Thule**: The ancestors of the Inuit who replaced the Dorset culture on the island after 1300 CE.
*   **Dorset**: A Paleo-Eskimo culture that inhabited the island before being replaced by the Thule.
*   **Helluland**: The name given to the island in Norse sagas, believed by many scholars to be Baffin Island.
*   **Arctic Cordillera**: A mountain system in the eastern Arctic, of which the Baffin Mountains are a part.
*   **Tundra climate (ET)**: A climate classification for regions with at least one month above 0°C but no month above 10°C, characterized by permafrost.
*   **Isostatic rebound**: The rise of land masses after the removal of the weight of ice sheets, a process studied on Baffin Island.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary scholarly debate concerns the nature and extent of pre-Viking European contact on Baffin Island. Archaeologist Patricia Sutherland's findings suggest possible Norse trading posts or settlements around 1000 CE. However, critics, including researchers like Michele Hayeur Smith and William W. Fitzhugh, argue that key evidence like spun yarn was an indigenous technology developed long before European arrival, and that the published evidence for sustained contact is insufficient. The origin of certain artifacts remains controversial and unresolved.
