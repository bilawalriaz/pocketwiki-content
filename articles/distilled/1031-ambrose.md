# Ambrose

## Overview
Ambrose of Milan (c. 339 – 4 April 397) was a Roman governor who became Bishop of Milan by popular acclamation in 374, serving until his death. As one of the four Great Latin Church Fathers, he defined the relationship between church and state in the West, asserting episcopal authority over doctrine and morals while opposing Arianism and state sponsorship of pagan cults. His theological writings, hymnody, and mentorship of Augustine of Hippo shaped Latin Christianity, while his confrontations with emperors Gratian, Valentinian II, and Theodosius I established a precedent for ecclesiastical independence from imperial power.

## Timeline
- **c. 339** — Born in Augusta Treverorum (Trier) to a Roman Christian family of Greek descent.
- **c. 372** — Appointed governor of Aemilia-Liguria with headquarters in Milan.
- **374** — Elected Bishop of Milan by popular acclamation; baptized, ordained, and consecrated within a week.
- **378–380** — Writes *De Fide* for Gratian; Council of Aquileia (381) deposes Arian bishops Palladius and Secundianus.
- **385–386** — Confronts Empress Justina and Valentinian II over Arian demands for Milanese basilicas; refuses imperial orders.
- **384** — Leads successful embassy to Magnus Maximus in Gaul.
- **390** — After Massacre of Thessalonica, compels Theodosius I to perform public penance before restoring communion.
- **393–394** — Retires to Bologna and Florence during Arbogast and Eugenius's usurpation; returns after Battle of the Frigidus.
- **395** — Delivers eulogy for Theodosius I at Milan.
- **4 April 397** — Dies at Milan; succeeded by Simplician.

## Body

### Early Life and Unexpected Election
Ambrose was born c. 339 in Trier, likely the son of a praetorian prefect of Gaul or a member of his administration. After his father’s death, the family moved to Rome, where Ambrose studied literature, law, and rhetoric. He entered public service, becoming governor of Aemilia-Liguria c. 372. In 374, the Arian bishop Auxentius died, sparking a succession crisis. Ambrose attended the election to keep order; the crowd acclaimed him bishop. Though a Nicene Christian, he was unbaptized and theologically untrained. He fled, but Emperor Gratian endorsed the choice. Within a week, Ambrose was baptized, ordained, and consecrated—the first high Roman official to accept the episcopate in the West. He adopted an ascetic lifestyle, gave his wealth to the poor, and delegated diocesan administration to his brother Satyrus.

### The Struggle Against Arianism
Arianism, teaching that the Son was created by and subordinate to the Father, divided the empire. Ambrose opposed it theologically and politically. In 381, he presided over a Western council at Aquileia that deposed Arian bishops Palladius and Secundianus. He resisted Arian influence at the courts of Valentinian II (who adhered to Arianism) and Theodosius I (who professed Nicene orthodoxy but ruled over many Arian clergy). Ambrose viewed ecclesiastical unity as essential to imperial stability, engaging in a decades-long struggle that mirrored the intensity of later Reformation conflicts.

### Imperial Relations: Gratian, Valentinian II, and Theodosius I
**Gratian (r. 367–383):** Modern scholarship disputes the traditional view of Ambrose as Gratian’s dominant spiritual guide. Their first recorded meeting occurred in 379. Gratian requested a statement of faith; Ambrose produced *De Fide* (two volumes), a polemic against Arianism. By 382, Ambrose influenced Gratian to divert state subsidies from pagan cults and oppose restoring the Altar of Victory. However, Gratian’s policies likely reflected his own piety and the political shock of the Battle of Adrianople (378) as much as Ambrose’s counsel.

**Valentinian II (r. 375–392):** Ambrose clashed repeatedly with Valentinian’s mother, the Arian Empress Justina. In 385–386, she demanded two Milanese churches for Arian use. Ambrose refused, invoking the Roman legal principle that a temple belongs to its god, not the state. He barricaded himself and his congregation in the basilica; imperial soldiers sent to seize it instead declared loyalty to him. Valentinian backed down. Ambrose later undertook a successful embassy to the usurper Magnus Maximus (384) but failed in a second. After Maximus invaded Italy (386–387), Theodosius restored Valentinian, who died under suspicious circumstances in 392. Ambrose’s funeral oration is the sole contemporary Western account, hinting at suicide without naming it.

**Theodosius I (r. 379–395):** Theodosius issued the Edict of Thessalonica (380) establishing Nicene Christianity as the sole legitimate faith. He and Ambrose likely first met in 388 after Theodosius defeated Maximus. Following the Massacre of Thessalonica (390), Ambrose wrote Theodosius demanding public penance, withholding communion until compliance. Theodosius submitted, attending church without imperial robes until Christmas. Later historians (notably Theodoret) mythologized this as Ambrose dominating the emperor, but modern scholars reject the "pious fiction" of a cathedral-door confrontation. Theodosius’s anti-pagan laws (391) were limited in scope and driven by state interests, not episcopal pressure. Their relationship was a negotiation between two institutional leaders, not a personal friendship.

### Later Years and Death
In 393, the usurper Eugenius and his general Arbogast courted Ambrose’s support; he withdrew to Bologna and Florence, returning only after Theodosius’s victory at the Frigidus (394). He eulogized Theodosius in 395 and died on 4 April 397. His body remains in the Basilica of Sant’Ambrogio in Milan.

### Character and Pastoral Ministry
Ambrose combined Roman aristocratic governance with Christian asceticism. He preached frequently, administered the Eucharist daily, cared personally for the poor, widows, orphans, and clergy, and respected local liturgical customs (advising Augustine: "When in Rome, do as the Romans do"). He asserted episcopal authority over doctrine and morals—"The emperor is in the church, not over the church"—yet acknowledged the limits of his power when imperial agents seized a political suspect from his church in 396. His writings were advocacy for Nicene Christianity, not personal reflection. Scholars debate whether his politics served personal power or genuine spirituality; his hymnody and mystical exegesis suggest deep piety.

### Attitudes Toward Jews and Pagans
**Jews:** In 388, Theodosius ordered a bishop to rebuild a synagogue destroyed by Christians at Callinicum. Ambrose protested, arguing compliance betrayed the faith and refusal created a martyr; Theodosius rescinded the order. Ambrose also pressed Theodosius to drop the case entirely, after which McLynn argues he was excluded from imperial counsels. Yet Ambrose extensively cited the Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria as a "faithful interpreter of Scripture," treating him almost as a Church Father, and used other Jewish sources positively. His negative rhetoric often grouped all non-Nicenes together for polemical effect.

**Pagans:** Ambrose opposed state sponsorship of pagan cults. He blocked a senatorial delegation appealing Gratian’s removal of the Altar of Victory (382) and defeated Symmachus’s appeal to Valentinian II for its restoration, arguing the emperor was a "soldier of God" bound to oppose idolatry. He later irritated Theodosius by intercepting a pagan delegation (389). His writings reflect the era’s triumphalism, citing pagan authors like Varro to expose "repulsive" practices.

### Theology and Exegesis
Ambrose is a Latin Doctor of the Church. His juridical training shaped a practical, utilitarian theology (*De officiis ministrorum* is a guide for clergy, not speculative treatise). He balanced sacramental mystery against ritualism, drawing on Stoic/Ciceronian ethics and Greek fathers (Basil, Origen, Philo). His exegesis followed the Alexandrian allegorical method. Key works include *De Fide* (anti-Arian), *De Spiritu Sancto*, *De officiis*, and the *Exameron* (on the six days of creation).

**Eschatology:** Scholars debate whether Ambrose was a universalist (*apokatastasis*). Passages in his commentary on 1 Corinthians 15 suggest all things will eventually be subject to Christ through obedience, leading to God being "all in all." Others read this as rhetorical or conditional on repentance, aligning with mainstream eternal judgment for unbelievers.

**Wealth and Poverty:** In *De Officiis*, Ambrose argues justice requires the rich to repay resources God gave equally but they usurped. Giving to the poor is not generosity but restitution; avarice breaks the mutual interdependence binding society. He preached against elite greed in the late 380s, framing solidarity with the poor as justice, not charity.

**Mariology:** Ambrose emphasized Mary’s virginity and role as *Theotokos* (Mother of God). She conceived by the Holy Spirit, not man; she is the temple, not the deity within. He viewed celibacy as superior to marriage, with Mary as the model of virginity.

### Augustine of Hippo
Ambrose’s preaching catalyzed Augustine’s conversion. Augustine admired his rhetoric but found him too busy for personal counsel; Simplician (Ambrose’s mentor) guided Augustine into Christian Neoplatonism. Ambrose baptized Augustine in 387. A famous passage in Augustine’s *Confessions* describes Ambrose reading silently—a rare practice in antiquity, where texts were typically recited aloud—marking a shift toward interiorized reading.

### Music and Hymnody
Ambrose is credited with inventing Christian Latin hymnody. Four hymns are universally attributed to him by Augustine: *Aeterne rerum conditor*, *Deus creator omnium*, *Iam surgit hora tertia*, and *Veni redemptor gentium*. Written in iambic tetrameter (eight four-line stanzas), their dignified simplicity became the model for Western hymnody. He did not compose Ambrosian chant (antiphonal psalmody), though it bears his name. The *Te Deum* is no longer attributed to him.

### Writings
Ambrose’s corpus (all in Latin) includes:
- **Exegetical:** *Exameron*, *De paradiso*, *De Abraham*, *Expositio evangelii secundum Lucam*, commentaries on Psalms and Isaiah.
- **Moral/Ascetical:** *De officiis ministrorum*, *De virginibus*, *De viduis*, *De virginitate*.
- **Dogmatic:** *De Fide*, *De Spiritu Sancto*, *De incarnationis dominicae sacramento*, *De sacramentis* (authorship debated), *De mysteriis*, *De paenitentia*.
- **Sermons:** *De excessu fratris*, *De obitu Valentiniani*, *De obitu Theodosii*, *Contra Auxentium*.
- **Letters:** 91 extant.
Standard editions include the Maurist (Paris, 1686–90), Migne’s *Patrologia Latina* (vols. 14–17), CSEL, and the *Fathers of the Church* English translations.

## Terms
- ****Arianism**** — A 4th-century Christological doctrine, taught by the priest Arius, holding that God the Father created the Son, making the Son a subordinate, non-eternal being of a different "essence" (*ousia*) from the Father.
- ****Nicene Christianity / Orthodoxy**** — The doctrine affirmed at the Council of Nicaea (325) that the Son is *homoousios* (of the same substance) as the Father—co-eternal and consubstantial.
- ****Theotokos**** — Greek for "God-bearer" or "Mother of God"; a title for Mary affirming that the one she bore is fully divine.
- ****Ambrosian Hymnody**** — The body of Latin hymns in iambic tetrameter (four-line stanzas) attributed to or modeled on Ambrose; the foundation of Western congregational singing.
- ****Apokatastasis**** — The doctrine of universal restoration, holding that all rational beings will eventually be reconciled to God; debated in Ambrose’s eschatology.
- ****Altar of Victory**** — A gold altar in the Roman Senate house bearing a statue of Victoria; its removal (382) and proposed restoration became a flashpoint in church-state relations.
- ****Exameron**** — A hexaemeral (six-day) commentary on the Genesis creation account; Ambrose’s *Exameron* (386–390) follows the Alexandrian allegorical tradition.
- ****De Officiis Ministrorum**** — Ambrose’s seminal ethical treatise (377–391) on clerical duties, adapting Ciceronian Stoicism to Christian pastoral practice; a foundational text of Western moral theology.
- ****Massacre of Thessalonica (390)**** — Theodosius I’s brutal suppression of a riot in Thessalonica, killing thousands; Ambrose withheld communion until the emperor performed public penance.
- ****Silent Reading**** — The practice of reading without vocalization; Ambrose’s habit, noted by Augustine, marks a cultural shift from oral/aural to interiorized textual engagement.

## Debates and open questions
- **Extent of episcopal influence on emperors:** Traditional narratives cast Ambrose as the "conscience" dominating Gratian, Valentinian II, and Theodosius. Modern scholarship (McLynn, Cameron, Liebeschuetz) argues his influence was negotiated, limited, and often overstated by later hagiographers like Theodoret.
- **Universalism vs. eternal punishment:** Passages in *De Fide* and the commentary on 1 Corinthians 15 suggest possible *apokatastasis*; others affirm standard judgment. No consensus exists.
- **Authorship of *De sacramentis*:** Attributed to Ambrose in manuscripts but stylistically distinct from *De mysteriis*; some scholars assign it to a later compiler or even Augustine (erroneously).
- **Attitude toward Jews:** The Callinicum affair (388) shows Ambrose pressuring Theodosius to excuse a bishop from rebuilding a synagogue, using rhetoric modern scholars call bigoted. Yet he revered Philo of Alexandria as a "faithful interpreter" and used Jewish sources extensively. Whether his anti-Jewish rhetoric was theological polemic or personal hostility remains debated.
- **Silent reading as innovation:** Augustine’s anecdote is often cited as evidence of a cognitive shift. Scholars debate whether silent reading was truly rare or merely unremarked in antiquity.
- **Composership of Ambrosian chant:** The repertory is named for Ambrose but no melodies are securely his; the chant tradition likely developed later in Milan’s liturgy.
