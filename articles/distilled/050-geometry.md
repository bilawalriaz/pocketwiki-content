# Geometry

## Overview

Geometry is a branch of mathematics concerned with properties of space such as distance, shape, size, and relative position of figures. Along with arithmetic, it is one of the oldest branches of mathematics; a practitioner is called a geometer. Originally developed to model the physical world, geometry has applications in almost all sciences, art, architecture, and graphics. Until the 19th century, geometry was almost exclusively Euclidean, but discoveries such as non-Euclidean geometries and Gauss's Theorema Egregium dramatically enlarged its scope, splitting the field into many subfields and changing the meaning of "space" from the physical world's three dimensions to any mathematical structure on which geometry is defined.

## Body

### Ancient geometry

The earliest recorded geometry comes from ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt in the 2nd millennium BC, where empirically discovered principles about lengths, angles, areas, and volumes met practical needs in surveying, construction, astronomy, and crafts. The earliest known texts are the Egyptian Rhind Papyrus (2000–1800 BC) and Moscow Papyrus (c. 1890 BC), and Babylonian clay tablets such as Plimpton 322 (1900 BC). Later Babylonian clay tablets (350–50 BC) show astronomers using trapezoid procedures for computing Jupiter's position, anticipating the Oxford Calculators' mean speed theorem by 14 centuries. The ancient Nubians also established a system of geometry including early sun clocks.

In the 7th century BC, Thales of Miletus used geometry to calculate pyramid heights and ship distances, and is credited with the first use of deductive reasoning in geometry. Pythagoras's school produced the first proof of the Pythagorean theorem. Eudoxus (408–c. 355 BC) developed the method of exhaustion—calculating areas and volumes of curvilinear figures by successive approximation—and a theory of ratios avoiding the problem of incommensurable magnitudes. Around 300 BC, Euclid's *Elements* introduced mathematical rigor through the axiomatic method (definition, axiom, theorem, proof), arranging known results into a single coherent logical framework. Archimedes (c. 287–212 BC) used the method of exhaustion to calculate the area under a parabola via infinite series, gave accurate approximations of pi, studied the spiral bearing his name, and obtained formulas for volumes of surfaces of revolution.

### Geometry in India and the Islamic world

Indian mathematicians made important contributions. The Shatapatha Brahmana (3rd century BC) contains ritual geometric construction rules similar to the Sulba Sutras, which contain the earliest extant verbal expression of the Pythagorean theorem and lists of Pythagorean triples. The Bakhshali manuscript includes geometric problems and employs a decimal place value system with a dot for zero. Aryabhata's Aryabhatiya (499) computes areas and volumes. Brahmagupta's Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628) stated his famous theorem on diagonals of a cyclic quadrilateral, a formula for its area (generalizing Heron's formula), and a complete description of rational triangles.

In medieval Islam, Al-Mahani (b. 853) conceived reducing geometrical problems like duplicating the cube to algebra. Thābit ibn Qurra (836–901) applied arithmetic operations to ratios of geometrical quantities, contributing to analytic geometry. Omar Khayyam (1048–1131) found geometric solutions to cubic equations. Theorems of Ibn al-Haytham, Omar Khayyam, and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi on quadrilaterals continued research on the parallel postulate, a line of inquiry that by the 19th century led to hyperbolic geometry.

### Early modern and 19th-century transformations

In the early 17th century, two developments changed geometry. René Descartes (1596–1650) and Pierre de Fermat (1601–1665) created analytic geometry—geometry with coordinates and equations—a necessary precursor to calculus and quantitative physics. Girard Desargues (1591–1661) systematically studied projective geometry, which examines properties of shapes unchanged under projections and sections, especially as related to artistic perspective.

Two 19th-century developments fundamentally changed the field. First, Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky, János Bolyai, and Carl Friedrich Gauss discovered non-Euclidean geometries—geometries without the parallel postulate that develop without contradiction. Second, Felix Klein's Erlangen programme formulated symmetry as the central consideration, generalizing Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries. Carl Friedrich Gauss's Theorema Egregium ("remarkable theorem") asserted that Gaussian curvature of a surface is independent of any specific embedding in Euclidean space, implying surfaces can be studied intrinsically as stand-alone spaces—expanded into the theory of manifolds and Riemannian geometry. Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866) introduced the Riemann surface, and Henri Poincaré founded algebraic topology and the geometric theory of dynamical systems. Non-Euclidean geometry underlies general relativity.

### Main concepts

Euclid's axiomatic approach—deducing properties from self-evident postulates—came to be known as axiomatic or synthetic geometry. In the 20th century, David Hilbert (1862–1943) employed axiomatic reasoning to provide a modern foundation. Points are fundamental objects, defined in modern mathematics as elements of an axiomatically defined set called space; however, Whitehead's point-free geometry (1919–1920) dispenses with points entirely. Lines, planes, curves (1-dimensional objects), surfaces (2-dimensional objects), and solids (3-dimensional objects bounded by closed surfaces) are generalized by manifolds—spaces where every point has a neighborhood homeomorphic (topology) or diffeomorphic (differential geometry) to Euclidean space. Angles are formed by two rays sharing a common endpoint; their study forms the basis of trigonometry. Length, area, and volume describe extent in one, two, and three dimensions; the concept of length generalizes to metrics (e.g., Euclidean, hyperbolic, Lorentz metrics of special relativity). Congruence (same size and shape) and similarity (same shape) describe relationships between figures. Classical geometers studied compass-and-straightedge constructions, though some problems proved impossible by these means alone. Dimension extended from 1–3 to higher dimensions (configuration spaces), infinite dimensions (Hilbert spaces), and positive real numbers (fractal geometry). Symmetry, expressed via transformation groups, determines what geometry is per Klein's Erlangen programme; projective geometry also exhibits a point-plane duality principle.

### Contemporary geometry

Euclidean geometry models the physical world and is used in mechanics, astronomy, crystallography, engineering, architecture, and navigation; it remains in most nations' mandatory curricula. Differential geometry uses calculus and linear algebra, and is crucial to general relativity's postulate that the universe is curved; it can be intrinsic (smooth manifolds with Riemannian metrics) or extrinsic (objects in ambient flat Euclidean space). Non-Euclidean geometry arises by replacing the parallel postulate (yielding hyperbolic and elliptic geometry) or admitting isotropic quadratic forms (yielding kinematic geometries). Topology concerns properties of continuous mappings—connectedness and compactness—and is technically a transformation geometry with homeomorphisms; subfields include geometric, differential, algebraic, and general topology. Algebraic geometry studies algebraic sets (common zeros of multivariate polynomials); Hilbert's Nullstellensatz (c. 1900) established its correspondence with commutative algebra, and Grothendieck's scheme theory (late 1950s–mid-1970s) allowed topological methods in purely algebraic contexts, enabling Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. Complex geometry studies structures modelled on the complex plane, beginning with Riemann surfaces and developed by the Italian school and Jean-Pierre Serre's sheaf theory; Calabi–Yau manifolds appear in string theory. Discrete geometry concerns relative positions of simple objects (sphere packings, triangulations). Computational geometry deals with algorithms for manipulating geometric objects (travelling salesman problem, hidden-line removal). Geometric group theory studies group actions on geometric objects, notably isometric actions on metric spaces. Convex geometry investigates convex shapes, dating to Archimedes's first precise definition of convexity.

### Applications

Geometry's applications are extensive. In art, perspective gave rise to projective geometry; artists from Michelangelo to modern comic book artists have used proportion concepts, and the golden ratio has a controversial role. Tessellations appear in Islamic art and M. C. Escher's work, which also used hyperbolic geometry. In architecture, geometry lies at the core of design, including projective geometry for forced perspective and conic sections for domes. In physics, astronomy has long been a source of geometric problems; Riemannian and pseudo-Riemannian geometry are used in general relativity, and string theory uses several geometry variants. In biology and medicine, geometric morphometrics combines landmark-based geometry with multivariate statistics for analyzing shape; fractal geometry models self-similar structures like vascular networks and the bronchial tree; geometric principles underlie biomechanics and medical imaging (CT, MRI). Geometry also influenced calculus—coordinates allowed plane curves to be represented as functions and equations, key to infinitesimal calculus—and number theory, from the Pythagoreans' discovery of incommensurable lengths to scheme theory in Wiles's proof.

## Terms

- **Axiomatic method**: Euclid's approach of deducing theorems from self-evident postulates; also called synthetic geometry.
- **Method of exhaustion**: Eudoxus's technique for calculating areas and volumes of curvilinear figures by successive approximation.
- **Non-Euclidean geometry**: Geometries developed without the parallel postulate, notably hyperbolic and elliptic geometry.
- **Theorema Egregium**: Gauss's theorem that Gaussian curvature is independent of embedding in Euclidean space, enabling intrinsic study of surfaces.
- **Manifold**: A space where every point has a neighborhood homeomorphic (topology) or diffeomorphic (differential geometry) to Euclidean space.
- **Analytic geometry**: Geometry using coordinates and equations, created by Descartes and Fermat.
- **Projective geometry**: Study of properties unchanged under projections and sections, related to artistic perspective.
- **Erlangen programme**: Klein's 1872 proclamation that symmetry, via transformation groups, determines what geometry is.
- **Scheme theory**: Grothendieck's foundational framework allowing topological and cohomological methods in algebraic geometry.
- **Geodesic**: Generalization of a line to curved spaces in differential geometry.
