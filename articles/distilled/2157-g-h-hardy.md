# G. H. Hardy

## Overview
G. H. Hardy (1877–1947) was a preeminent English mathematician who championed pure mathematics and brought rigorous, analytical methods to British mathematics. He is celebrated for his foundational work in number theory and analysis, his legendary mentorship of Srinivasa Ramanujan, and his influential essay *A Mathematician's Apology*, which offers a profound defense of mathematics as a creative art.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Hardy was born into an educationally inclined family and showed an early aptitude for mathematics, writing numbers up to millions at age two. After schooling at Cranleigh and Winchester College, he entered Trinity College, Cambridge in 1896. His mathematical outlook was profoundly shaped by studying Camille Jordan's *Cours d'analyse*, which introduced him to the rigorous continental European tradition. He placed fourth in the Mathematics Tripos in 1898 and later sought to abolish the system, viewing it as an end in itself.

### Mentorship of Ramanujan and Collaboration with Littlewood
In 1913, Hardy received a letter from the self-taught Indian genius Srinivasa Ramanujan. Recognizing his extraordinary talent, Hardy invited him to Cambridge, initiating a celebrated collaboration Hardy called "the one romantic incident in my life." Hardy considered his discovery of Ramanujan his greatest contribution to mathematics. From 1911, Hardy also collaborated extensively with John Edensor Littlewood. Their partnership, one of the most famous in mathematical history, produced major advances in analytic number theory, including the Hardy–Littlewood circle method and conjectures about prime numbers.

### Mathematical Philosophy and Work
Hardy was a staunch advocate for pure mathematics, which he defined as mathematics independent of the physical world. In his 1940 essay *A Mathematician's Apology*, he argued that the value of mathematics lies in its aesthetic beauty and permanence, not its utility. He famously stated he had never done anything "useful." Despite this, his work found applications in physics (e.g., the Hardy–Ramanujan asymptotic formula for partitions) and genetics. He formulated the Hardy–Weinberg principle in 1908, a foundational concept in population genetics, though he considered the mathematics "very simple" and had no interest in its biological implications.

### Personal Life and Character
Hardy was intensely shy, eccentric, and socially awkward, often covering mirrors in hotel rooms. He was a lifelong bachelor, cared for in his final years by his sister. An atheist from age 20, he was also a passionate cricket fan, using the sport as a metaphor for mathematical excellence. Politically, he was involved with pacifist and liberal groups during the World Wars. His final years were marked by depression and a loss of creative power following a heart attack in 1939.

## Terms
- **Pure Mathematics:** Mathematics studied for its own sake, independent of the physical world or practical applications.
- **Analytic Number Theory:** A branch of number theory that uses methods from mathematical analysis to solve problems about integers.
- **Hardy–Weinberg Principle:** A principle stating that allele and genotype frequencies in a population will remain constant from generation to generation in the absence of other evolutionary influences.
- **Hardy–Littlewood Circle Method:** A technique in analytic number theory for estimating the number of solutions to equations involving integers.
- **Mathematical Tripos:** The intensive mathematics examination at the University of Cambridge.
- **Asymptotic Formula:** A mathematical expression that describes the limiting behavior of a function as a variable approaches a particular value (often infinity).
- **Waring's Problem:** A problem in number theory asking whether every positive integer can be expressed as a sum of a fixed number of powers of integers.
