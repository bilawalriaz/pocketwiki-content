# Circle

## Overview
A circle is the set of all points in a plane at a fixed distance (the radius) from a given point (the centre). It is one of the most fundamental shapes in geometry, underpinning the wheel, gears, and much of modern machinery, while also inspiring developments in geometry, astronomy, and calculus. Its mathematical properties—such as the constant ratio π between circumference and diameter—have been studied since antiquity.

## Timeline
- **Prehistory** — Natural and constructed circles appear (stone circles, petroglyphs, Nebra sky disc)
- **c. 1700 BCE** — Rhind Papyrus gives area method yielding π ≈ 256/81
- **c. 300 BCE** — Euclid's *Elements* Book 3 systematically treats circle properties
- **c. 300 BCE** — Archimedes proves area formula and approximates π
- **1882 CE** — Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem proves π transcendental; squaring the circle impossible

## Body

### Definition and Basic Elements
A circle is the boundary of a disc (the region it encloses). Key elements include the centre (equidistant point), radius (centre-to-boundary segment), diameter (longest chord, twice the radius), circumference (boundary length), and chords, arcs, tangents, and secants. All circles are similar; ratios of circumference to diameter (π) and area to radius squared (π) are universal constants.

### Historical Development
Prehistoric cultures created circular artifacts and monuments. Ancient Egyptians approximated π in the Rhind Papyrus. Euclid axiomatized circle geometry in *Elements*. Archimedes derived the area formula and bounded π. Medieval scholars viewed circles as divine symbols. The 19th-century proof of π’s transcendence resolved the classical problem of squaring the circle.

### Analytic Geometry
In Cartesian coordinates, a circle with centre (a, b) and radius r satisfies (x−a)² + (y−b)² = r². Parametric forms use sine and cosine: x = a + r cos t, y = b + r sin t. Polar equations take the form r² − 2r r₀ cos(θ−φ) + r₀² = a². In the complex plane, |z − c| = r defines a circle.

### Key Theorems and Properties
- **Isoperimetric inequality**: The circle encloses maximum area for a given perimeter.
- **Symmetry**: Infinite reflection and rotational symmetry; symmetry group O(2,ℝ).
- **Inscribed angle theorem**: An inscribed angle is half the central angle subtending the same arc; angles subtending a diameter are right angles (Thales’ theorem).
- **Chord properties**: Equal chords are equidistant from the centre; perpendicular bisector of a chord passes through the centre.
- **Tangent properties**: Tangent is perpendicular to the radius at contact; two tangents from an external point are equal.
- **Power of a point**: For intersecting chords, products of segment lengths are equal.

### Special Circles and Constructions
- **Unit circle**: Centre at origin, radius 1; foundational in trigonometry.
- **Incircle and circumcircle**: Every triangle has a unique inscribed circle (tangent to sides) and circumscribed circle (through vertices).
- **Circle of Apollonius**: Locus of points with constant distance ratio to two fixed points.
- **Compass/straightedge constructions**: Circles constructed from centre and point, diameter, or three non-collinear points.

### Generalizations
- **p-norms**: In taxicab geometry (p=1), "circles" are squares; in Chebyshev distance (p=∞), also squares aligned to axes.
- **Topological circle**: Any space homeomorphic to the standard circle; oriented circles carry clockwise/counterclockwise signs.
- **Superellipses and Cassini ovals**: Circles as special cases of broader curve families.
- **Curves of constant width**: Circle is the simplest; width independent of orientation.

## Terms
- **Annulus**: Region between two concentric circles.
- **Arc**: Connected part of a circle’s circumference.
- **Chord**: Line segment with both endpoints on the circle.
- **Circumference**: Perimeter of a circle; C = 2πr = πd.
- **Disc**: Region bounded by a circle (interior plus boundary).
- **Radian**: Angle subtending an arc equal in length to the radius; full circle = 2π radians.
- **Sector**: Region bounded by two radii and an arc.
- **Segment**: Region between a chord and an arc.
- **Tangent**: Line touching the circle at exactly one point.
- **Sagitta**: Perpendicular from chord midpoint to arc; used to compute radius.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Squaring the circle**: Proven impossible due to π’s transcendence, yet persists among pseudomath enthusiasts.
- **Generalised circles**: Whether a locus is a true circle or a line depends on context (e.g., Apollonius condition with midpoint yields a line).
- **Higher-dimensional analogs**: Extension of circle properties to spheres and hyperspheres remains active in topology and geometry.
