# Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres

## Overview
Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres (1780–1867) was a French Neoclassical painter who viewed himself as the guardian of academic orthodoxy against the rising Romantic movement led by Eugène Delacroix. Though he aspired to the highest genre—history painting—his portraits and drawings are now considered his supreme achievement. Ingres developed a distinctive style early, characterized by flawless contour, expressive distortions of anatomy for sinuous line, and a rejection of visible brushwork in favor of local color. His work was initially condemned as "Gothic" and archaic, but his 1824 *Vow of Louis XIII* brought official acclaim. Paradoxically, his deliberate archaisms and spatial flattening made him a crucial precursor to modernism, directly influencing Picasso, Matisse, and later abstract artists.

## Body

### Early Formation and the David Studio
Born to a versatile artist father in Montauban, Ingres received early training in drawing and music. The French Revolution ended his formal schooling in 1791, a lifelong insecurity. In Toulouse, painter Guillaume-Joseph Roques instilled a veneration for Raphael that became decisive. In 1797, Ingres entered Jacques-Louis David’s Paris studio, absorbing the master’s shift from Roman severity toward Greek "purity, virtue, and simplicity." He won the Prix de Rome in 1801 with *The Ambassadors of Agamemnon*, a work already mixing Davidian muscularity with a graceful, bas-relief linearity. Before departing for Rome in 1806, he studied the Louvre’s newly seized masterpieces—Raphael, Bronzino, Pontormo, Jan van Eyck—fusing Renaissance clarity, Mannerist elegance, and Flemish precision into a personal style critics called "eclectic" and "plundering the past."

### The Roman Years: Isolation and Refinement (1806–1824)
Ingres arrived in Rome as the 1806 Salon savaged his work; David himself condemned him. Humiliated, he vowed never to exhibit at the Salon again, breaking his engagement to Julie Forestier. At the Villa Medici, he pursued a "revolution" in art from a private studio. His academy submissions—nude bathers (*Baigneuse*, *La Grande Bagneuse*) and *Oedipus and the Sphinx*—were rejected for insufficient idealization. He survived through portrait drawings of exceptional precision, producing an estimated five hundred, though he resented being seen as a mere "man who draws little portraits." Commissions from the French administration (General Miollis, the Murat court) provided history subjects (*Romulus' Victory*, *Virgil Reading the Aeneid*, *La Grande Odalisque*), but the 1815 collapse of Napoleon’s empire left him stranded. In Florence from 1820, his fortunes turned: *Roger Freeing Angelica* entered the Musée du Luxembourg, and the childhood friend Monsieur de Pastoret secured the commission for *The Vow of Louis XIII*.

### Official Triumph and the Neoclassical/Romantic Schism (1824–1834)
*The Vow of Louis XIII* (1824), a Raphaelesque image of church-state unity, perfectly suited the Restoration government. Ingres was lionized, decorated, and elected to the Institute. The 1827 Salon staged the defining clash: his *Apotheosis of Homer* (classical harmony, hierarchy) versus Delacroix’s *Death of Sardanapalus* (tumultuous color, violence). Ingres called Delacroix "the apostle of ugliness" with "dangerous tendencies." He welcomed the 1830 July Revolution as a conservative pacifist. His 1833 portrait of *Louis-François Bertin*—seated, massive, hyper-real in fabric yet idealized in face—became an icon of bourgeois power ("The Buddha of the Bourgeoisie"). But the 1834 *Martyrdom of Saint Symphorian*, labored over ten years, was attacked by both camps for historical inaccuracy, color, and a feminized saint. Ingres quit the Salon and accepted the directorship of the French Academy in Rome.

### Director in Rome and Final Paris Decades (1835–1867)
In Rome (1835–41), Ingres reformed the Academy, championed music (hosting Liszt, Paganini, Gounod), and studied early Christian and Renaissance art. He sent few works to Paris, notably *Odalisque and Slave* (1839) and *Antiochus and Stratonice* (1840), the latter earning royal favor and a recall to Paris. Back in France, he taught at the École des Beaux-Arts, warning students against Rubens. The 1848 Revolution barely disrupted him; he finished the long-gestating *Venus Anadyomene*. After his wife’s 1849 death, he remarried in 1852 (Delphine Ramel), sparking a late creative surge: *The Source* (1856), *Joan of Arc* (1854), *Apotheosis of Napoleon I* (1853, destroyed 1871), and major portraits (*Madame Moitessier*, *Princesse de Broglie*). He reworked earlier themes endlessly—Virgin variants, *Oedipus*, *Paolo and Francesca*. The *Turkish Bath* (tondo, 1862–63), synthesizing the *Petite Baigneuse* (1828) and Orientalist nudes, became a modernist touchstone. He died in 1867, bequeathing his studio contents (4,000+ drawings, violin) to Montauban.

### Style: Drawing as "Probity of Art"
Ingres’s style crystallized early and barely evolved. "Drawing is the probity of art," he declared; it constituted "seven eighths of painting." He sought a sinuous arabesque, deliberately distorting anatomy (extra vertebrae in *La Grande Odalisque*) and rejecting the "dreadful science" of correct structure. He abhorred visible brushwork and dramatic chiaroscuro, using local colors faintly modeled in halftones. This produced an "oil-and-water sensation" (Rosenblum) when hyper-real textures (fabrics, jewelry) abutted idealized, smoothed faces. He excelled in single- or two-figure compositions (*Oedipus*, *The Spring*, *Odalisque*) where "perfect physical well-being" replaced drama; multi-figure histories (*Apotheosis*, *Saint Symphorian*) suffered from static, airless crowds. His literary sources were narrow (Homer, Virgil, Dante, artists' lives), and he revisited a handful of themes across decades, often reworking paintings for years (*Venus Anadyomene* begun 1807, finished 1848).

### Portraits and Drawings
Though he considered portraiture a distraction, it is his modern glory. Baudelaire called him "the sole man in France who truly makes portraits"—"biography dramatized." He painted fabrics with microscopic precision to lend credibility to idealized faces. Female portraits range from sensuous (*Madame de Senonnes*) to Junoesque (*Madame Moitessier*) to chilly (*Princesse de Broglie*). His ~450 extant portrait drawings, executed in sharply pointed graphite on wove paper in four-hour sittings, are celebrated for capturing personality without cruelty. He demanded students master drawing first: "a thing well drawn is always a thing well painted." Preparatory drawings for histories involved hundreds of nude studies (often female models for male figures) and drapery studies, sometimes using a translucent veil to suppress detail and emphasize arabesque.

### The Ingres–Delacroix Rivalry
They embodied the century’s aesthetic divide: Neoclassicism (Winckelmann’s "noble simplicity and calm grandeur") vs. Romanticism (motion, color, emotion). The rivalry flared at the 1824, 1827, and 1855 Salons. Critics (Goncourt, Baudelaire, Delacroix himself) increasingly dismissed Ingres as "miserly," "incomplete intelligence," lacking "energetic temperament." Yet they shook hands amicably on the Institute steps late in life. Modern scholars often view both as expressions of the Romantic era’s dialectic.

### Pupils and Modernist Legacy
His star pupil, Théodore Chassériau, defected to Delacroix’s colorism, prompting Ingres to disown him. Other students (Flandrin, Lehmann, Amaury-Duval) had modest careers. But Ingres’s posthumous influence exploded. Degas (via Lamothe), then Picasso and Matisse, claimed him. The 1905 Salon d'Automne retrospective revealed *The Turkish Bath*’s flattened space and "deck of cards" figures, directly feeding *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* (1907) and Matisse’s *La Danse* (1909). Barnett Newman called him a progenitor of Abstract Expressionism: "He looked at the canvas more often than at the model." Surrealists prized his visionary distortions; Cubists, his plastic autonomy.

### Violon d'Ingres
A lifelong amateur violinist (second violin in Toulouse orchestra, chamber music in Rome with Paganini, Liszt, Gounod), Ingres gave French the phrase *violon d'Ingres*—a passionate avocation beside one’s profession. Man Ray famously photographed Kiki de Montparnasse as the *Valpinçon Bather* with f-holes painted on her back.

## Terms
- **Neoclassicism**: Art movement (late 18th–early 19th c.) reviving classical antiquity’s "noble simplicity and calm grandeur" (Winckelmann), emphasizing line, order, and idealized form over color and emotion.
- **Prix de Rome**: Premier French art scholarship (1663–1968); winner received 3–5 years at the French Academy in Rome (Villa Medici). Ingres won in 1801.
- **History painting**: Highest genre in the Academic hierarchy (est. Louis XIV), depicting heroic religious, historical, or mythological narratives with moral purpose. Ingres aspired to it above portraiture.
- **Salon**: Official annual/biennial exhibition of the Académie des Beaux-Arts in Paris; the primary venue for critical reception and state patronage. Ingres’s career turned on its verdicts.
- **Arabesque**: In Ingres’s usage, a sinuous, flowing linear rhythm that organizes the figure, often at the expense of anatomical accuracy.
- **Odalisque**: A female slave or concubine in a harem; a staple of Orientalist painting. Ingres painted multiple versions (*Grande Odalisque* 1814, *Odalisque and Slave* 1839, *Turkish Bath* 1862).
- **Grisaille**: Monochrome painting in shades of gray, used for underpainting or to simulate sculpture. Ingres showed *Apotheosis of Homer* in grisaille at the 1827 Salon.
- **Troubador style**: Early 19th-c. French genre of small, anecdotal paintings set in the Middle Ages/Renaissance (e.g., *Aretino and Tintoretto*, *Death of Leonardo*).
- **Violon d'Ingres**: French idiom for a hobby or second skill pursued with passion; derived from Ingres’s serious amateur violin playing.
- **École des Beaux-Arts**: France’s premier state art school; Ingres studied there (1799) and later taught (1841–51).

## Debates and open questions
- **Neoclassicist or Romantic?** Ingres self-identified as a Neoclassic defender of tradition, yet his expressive distortions, archaic flattening, and fetishistic detail align him with Romantic subjectivity. Scholars (e.g., Rosenblum, Cohn) argue he embodies the Romantic spirit he opposed.
- **History painting vs. portraiture:** Ingres insisted history painting was his vocation, but posterity elevates his portraits. Was his portraiture a commercial compromise or a parallel mastery?
- **Anatomy vs. arabesque:** Critics (Kératy, Landon) attacked his anatomical "errors" (elongated backs, boneless flesh). Ingres defended them as expressive necessity. The tension remains central to assessing his modernity.
- **Color deficiency?** The Institute (1838) and Baudelaire noted his students’ "dull, opaque" color. Ingres called color "handmaiden" to drawing. Was this a pedagogical failure or a coherent aesthetic choice?
- **Chassériau’s defection:** Ingres’s disowning of his most gifted pupil over stylistic drift (toward Delacroix) raises questions about his rigidity as a teacher versus his own eclectic borrowing.
- **Modernist appropriation:** Picasso, Matisse, and Newman claimed Ingres as a forefather of abstraction and flattening. Did Ingres anticipate modernism, or did modernists project their concerns onto his "primitive" passages?
