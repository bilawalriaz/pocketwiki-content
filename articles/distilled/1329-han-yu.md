# Han Yu

## Overview
Han Yu (768–824) was a Tang dynasty essayist, poet, and official whose advocacy for a revived Confucian orthodoxy and a plain "ancient prose" style (guwen) reshaped Chinese literature and laid the intellectual groundwork for Neo-Confucianism. He is ranked first among the "Eight Great Prose Masters of the Tang and Song." His career was defined by a volatile tension between his insistence on strong central authority and his repeated exiles for criticizing imperial policy, most famously his condemnation of Buddhist relic worship as a barbarian cult unsuited to Chinese civilization. Though his poetry was considered minor, his prose established the standard for Chinese expository writing until the modern era.

## Timeline
- **768** — Born in Heyang (modern Mengzhou, Henan) to a noble family.
- **774–777** — Family moves to Chang'an; banished to the south after patron Yuan Zai falls from power.
- **792** — Passes the *jinshi* imperial examination on his fourth attempt.
- **796–799** — Serves on staff of military governor of Bianzhou.
- **802** — Gains first central government post; soon exiled for political missteps (factional opposition, criticism of palace servants, or tax memorial).
- **807–819** — Holds series of posts in Luoyang and Chang'an; advocates central control over separatist northeastern provinces.
- **819** — Presents *Memorial on Bone-relics of the Buddha* to Emperor Xianzong; sentenced to death, commuted to exile in Chaozhou.
- **820** — Recalled to capital by Emperor Muzong; serves in War Office.
- **821** — Persuades rebellious commander to submit; promoted to high rank.
- **824** — Dies in Chang'an (Dec 25); buried in ancestral cemetery at Heyang (Apr 21, 825).

## Body

### Biography and Career
Han Yu was orphaned at age two and raised by his elder brother, Han Hui. After the family’s patron, Yuan Zai, was disgraced, they were exiled to the deep south, where Han Hui died in 781. Han Yu passed the rigorous *jinshi* exam in 792 but struggled to secure a capital post, serving instead under regional military governors. His career pattern—promotion followed by exile—reflected his refusal to compromise on political principle. He supported the throne’s efforts to crush separatist military governors (*fanzhen*) in the northeast, viewing strong central authority as essential to Confucian order. His most dangerous confrontation came in 819 when he denounced the Emperor’s veneration of a Buddha finger-bone relic. Emperor Xianzong ordered his execution; friends intervened to reduce the penalty to exile in distant Chaozhou. A formal apology and the Emperor’s death soon brought his recall. Under Emperor Muzong, he distinguished himself by negotiating the surrender of a rebellious commander, earning high office, including Rector of the Imperial University, before his death at fifty-six.

### Thought: Confucian Revival and the Critique of Buddhism and Taoism
Han Yu positioned himself as a defender of the Confucian *Way* (Dao) against the "barbarian" religion of Buddhism and the indigenous but socially corrosive Taoism. In his *Memorial on Bone-relics of the Buddha*, he argued that Buddhism originated with foreign peoples (*yidi*) who lacked the sovereign-subject and father-son bonds that define Chinese civilization. He demanded the relic be destroyed by "water and fire," citing Confucius’s dictum to "respect ghosts and spirits but keep them at a distance." In *The Origin of Dao* (*Yuandao*), he attacked the monasticism of both Buddhism and Taoism as economically unproductive and socially disruptive, contrasting them with Confucianism’s integration of private morality and public welfare. He adopted Mencius’s emphasis on political action as the expression of spirituality. Crucially, Han Yu formulated the concept of the **succession of the Way** (*daotong*)—a lineage of sages transmitting orthodoxy—and the role of the **teacher** (*shi*) as one who "transmits the Way, imparts knowledge, and resolves doubts" (*Shishuo*). Paradoxically, these structural concepts borrowed from the Buddhist ideas of dharma transmission and religious mentorship, even as he attacked the religion itself. He also distinguished Taoism as a native "harmful accretion" versus Buddhism as a foreign import.

### Literary Reform: The Classical Prose Movement (*Guwen*)
Han Yu led a revolt against *pianwen* (parallel prose), the dominant ornate, rhymed, and rigidly antithetical style of the Six Dynasties and early Tang. He championed *guwen* ("ancient writing"), modeled on the pre-Han and Han masters, valuing clarity, concision, utility, and logical argument over ornamentation. His *guwen* was not mere archaism but a new style built on ancient ideals, capable of "daring experimentation" and discursive breadth. With Liu Zongyuan, he spearheaded the Classical Prose Movement. His essays—polemics like the *Buddha Memorial* and *Yuandao*, satirical pieces like *Goodbye to Penury*, and the ritualistic *Text for the Crocodiles* (banishing reptiles from Chaozhou)—demonstrated the style’s power for argument, satire, and statecraft. Ming scholar Mao Kun later ranked him first among the Eight Great Prose Masters. His style became the orthodox model for Chinese prose through the Song dynasty (via Ouyang Xiu and Su Shi) until the 20th-century literary revolution.

### Poetry
Han Yu wrote prolifically in verse, often playful and covering mundane subjects—most famously *Losing Teeth*, where he calculates his remaining lifespan from the rate of tooth loss. Herbert Giles noted he transmuted the commonplace into wit. However, critical consensus, both traditional and modern, holds his poetry cannot match the "highest order" achieved by his prose or by Tang poetic giants like Du Fu or Li Bai.

### Significance and Legacy
Han Yu’s prose "reversed the literary decline of eight dynasties" (Su Shi). His *guwen* style, popularized by Song writers, remained the standard for nearly a millennium. Intellectually, his *daotong* and emphasis on the teacher provided the scaffolding for Song Neo-Confucians Cheng Yi and Zhu Xi, who revered him despite criticizing him as more a stylist than a moralist. Modern scholars acknowledge his literary centrality but often express discomfort with the "violence of his Confucian passions"—his intolerance toward Buddhism and his authoritarian political instincts. Memorials to him include the Han River in Chaozhou, Han Yu Temple, and the extinct crocodilian *Hanyusuchus* (named 2022), referencing his *Text for the Crocodiles*. Jorge Luis Borges later cited him as a literary precursor to Kafka.

## Terms
- ****Guwen** (古文)** — "Ancient writing"; a plain, logical, utilitarian prose style modeled on pre-Han and Han dynasty models, advocated by Han Yu to replace ornate *pianwen*.
- ****Pianwen** (駢文)** — "Parallel prose"; the dominant literary style of the Six Dynasties and early Tang, characterized by rigid parallelism, rich ornamentation, and rhyme.
- ****Daotong** (道統)** — "Succession of the Way"; Han Yu’s concept of an unbroken lineage of sages (Yao, Shun, Yu, Tang, Wen, Wu, Confucius, Mencius) transmitting Confucian orthodoxy.
- ****Shi** (師)** — "Teacher"; defined by Han Yu in *Discourse on Teachers* as one who "transmits the Way, imparts knowledge, and resolves doubts," regardless of age or status.
- ****Jinshi** (進士)** — "Presented scholar"; the highest degree in the imperial examination system, required for high office. Han Yu passed in 792 after four attempts.
- ****Fanzhen** (藩鎮)** — Regional military governorships that became semi-independent separatist regimes in the mid-to-late Tang; Han Yu advocated central suppression of them.
- ****Yidi** (夷狄)** — "Barbarians"; Han Yu’s term for non-Chinese peoples, applied to the Buddha to delegitimize Buddhism as a foreign cult.
- ****Yuandao** (原道)** — "The Origin of Dao"; Han Yu’s seminal essay attacking Buddhism and Taoism and defining Confucianism as the sole system linking self-cultivation to social order.
- ****Neo-Confucianism**** — A Song dynasty revival and reinterpretation of Confucian thought; Han Yu’s *daotong* and political spirituality are seen as its Tang dynasty precursors.
- ****Hanyusuchus**** — An extinct genus of gavialid crocodilian named in 2022 after Han Yu, referencing his *Text for the Crocodiles* written during his Chaozhou exile.

## Debates and open questions
- **Philosopher or stylist?** Song Confucians (Cheng Yi, Zhu Xi) revered Han Yu as a forerunner but criticized him for prioritizing literary form (*wen*) over moral substance (*dao*). Modern scholarship debates whether his *daotong* constitutes a genuine philosophical system or a rhetorical device for literary reform.
- **Indebtedness to Buddhism:** Scholars note the structural irony that Han Yu’s *daotong* (lineage transmission) and *shi* (teacher/mentor) concepts closely mirror Buddhist *dharma* transmission and guru-disciple relationships, which he virulently denounced.
- **Assessment of intolerance:** His *Memorial on Bone-relics* is cited as a landmark of religious intolerance. Historians debate whether this reflects genuine Confucian universalism, political calculation (attacking the Emperor’s favorites), or xenophobia.
- **Poetry vs. Prose:** The vast disparity between his canonical prose status and his minor poetry status raises questions about genre hierarchies in Chinese literary criticism and whether his verse is undervalued due to the shadow of his prose.
