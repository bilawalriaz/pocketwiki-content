# Nicolaus Copernicus

## Overview
Nicolaus Copernicus (19 February 1473 – 24 May 1543) was a Renaissance polymath whose heliocentric model—placing the Sun rather than Earth at the center of the universe—was published in *De revolutionibus orbium coelestium* (1543). This work catalyzed the Copernican Revolution and laid foundational groundwork for the Scientific Revolution. Though Aristarchus of Samos proposed a similar model c. 150 BCE, Copernicus likely developed his independently.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Copernicus was born on 19 February 1473 in the city of Toruń (Thorn), in the province of Royal Prussia, in the Crown of the Kingdom of Poland, to German-speaking parents. His father was a merchant from Kraków and his mother was the daughter of a wealthy Toruń merchant. His father died when he was ten; his maternal uncle, Lucas Watzenrode the Younger, Bishop of Warmia, oversaw his education. Copernicus studied at the University of Kraków from the fall of 1491, presumably until the summer or fall of 1495, where he encountered mathematical astronomy and critical analyses of Aristotelian and Ptolemaic systems. He later studied canon law at Bologna (1496–1501), where he met astronomer Domenico Maria Novara and observed an occultation of Aldebaran (1497). In Padua (1501–1503), he studied medicine and Greek. On 31 May 1503, having passed the obligatory examinations, he was granted the degree of Doctor of Canon Law at Ferrara.

### Administrative and Economic Work
Returning to Warmia in 1503, Copernicus served as secretary and physician to his uncle until 1512. He held various administrative roles, including magister pistoriae (manager of chapter economic enterprises) and chancellor. During the Polish–Teutonic War (1519–1521), he administered Olsztyn Castle and represented Polish interests in peace negotiations. He also advised on monetary reform, formulating an early version of Gresham's law ("bad money drives out good") in *Monetae cudendae ratio* (1526) and a quantity theory of money in 1517.

### Development of the Heliocentric Theory
He had formulated his theory by 1510. He wrote out a short overview of his new heavenly arrangement [known as the Commentariolus], also probably in 1510 [but no later than May 1514], and sent it off to at least one correspondent beyond Varmia. Around 1514, he circulated the *Commentariolus*, a brief manuscript outlining seven assumptions: no single center of celestial motion; Earth is not the universe’s center; all planets orbit the Sun; Earth’s motion explains apparent celestial movements; Earth rotates daily; Earth’s orbital motion causes solar phenomena; planetary retrograde motion results from Earth’s movement. He spent decades refining observations and calculations, completing *De revolutionibus* by 1532 but delaying publication due to fear of criticism.

### Publication and Reception
In 1539, Georg Rheticus, a Wittenberg mathematician, visited Copernicus and became his pupil. Under Rheticus’s urging, Copernicus published *De revolutionibus orbium coelestium* in 1543, just before his death. The book was structured in six books covering cosmology, spherical astronomy, solar and lunar motions, and planetary movements. Andreas Osiander added an unauthorized preface suggesting the model was a mathematical tool rather than physical reality. Initial reception was mixed; Protestant reformers like Melanchthon criticized it, while some Catholic scholars showed interest. The Catholic Church placed it on the Index of Forbidden Books in 1616, though it remained in academic curricula.

### Death and Legacy
Copernicus died at age 70 on 24 May 1543 in Frombork. He was buried in Frombork Cathedral. In August 2005, after scanning beneath the cathedral floor, archaeologists discovered what they believed to be Copernicus's remains. The discovery was announced only after further research, on 3 November 2008. On 22 May 2010, Copernicus was given a second funeral in a Mass led by Józef Kowalczyk, the former papal nuncio to Poland and newly named Primate of Poland.

## Terms
- **Heliocentrism**: Cosmological model placing the Sun at the center of the universe, with Earth and other planets orbiting around it.
- **Geocentrism**: Traditional model placing Earth at the center of the universe, as described by Ptolemy.
- **Epicycle**: Small circle whose center moves along the circumference of a larger circle (deferent), used to explain planetary motion.
- **Equant**: Point offset from the center of a planet’s orbit used by Ptolemy to account for non-uniform motion.
- **Tusi Couple**: Geometric construction where a small circle rolls inside a larger circle twice its diameter, producing linear motion from circular motion.
- **Urdi Lemma**: Technique for converting an eccentric orbit into a combination of epicycle and deferent.
- **Quantity Theory of Money**: Economic theory stating that money supply directly affects price levels.
- **Gresham’s Law**: Principle that "bad money drives out good" when currencies circulate together.
- **Commentariolus**: Copernicus’s unpublished manuscript outlining his heliocentric assumptions.
- **De revolutionibus orbium coelestium**: Copernicus’s 1543 treatise presenting the heliocentric model.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Independence vs. Influence**: While Aristarchus proposed heliocentrism c. 150 BCE, scholars debate whether Copernicus knew of this earlier work. Some argue Islamic astronomers like Ibn al-Shatir influenced his methods, though no direct textual link has been proven.
- **Nationality**: Copernicus was born in Royal Prussia under Polish rule to German-speaking parents. Modern scholars reject applying 19th-century national identities, describing him as a "Prussian" with mixed cultural heritage.
- **Religious Opposition**: Early critics included Protestant theologians like Melanchthon and Catholic figures like Bellarmine. The Church banned *De revolutionibus* in 1616, lifting restrictions only in 1835.
- **Publication Delay**: Copernicus delayed publishing for decades, possibly fearing backlash. His motivation remains debated—was it purely scientific caution or deeper theological concern?
- **Osiander’s Preface**: The unauthorized preface by Andreas Osiander, framing the model as purely mathematical, sparked long-standing debate about Copernicus’s intent—did he believe in physical heliocentrism or merely mathematical utility?
