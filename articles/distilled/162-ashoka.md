# Ashoka

## Overview

Ashoka (c. 304–232 BCE), also known as Ashoka the Great, was Emperor of Magadha from c. 268 BCE until his death, and the third ruler from the Mauryan dynasty. His empire covered much of the Indian subcontinent, from present-day Afghanistan to present-day Bangladesh, with its capital at Pataliputra. He is best known for his brutal conquest of Kalinga (c. 260 BCE), which reportedly caused him profound remorse and led him to embrace Buddhism and propagate "dhamma" (righteous conduct). His rock and pillar edicts are the earliest self-representations of imperial power in the Indian subcontinent, and he is credited with playing a major role in the spread of Buddhism across ancient Asia. The State Emblem of the modern Republic of India is an adaptation of the Lion Capital of Ashoka, and his wheel, the Ashoka Chakra, is adopted at the centre of the National Flag of India.

## Timeline

- **c. 304 BCE** — Ashoka born (exact date uncertain)
- **c. 268 BCE** — Ascends the throne after a disputed succession
- **c. 260 BCE** — Conquers Kalinga in his 8th regnal year; expresses remorse in Edict 13
- **c. 256–255 BCE** — Declares himself an upasaka (lay Buddhist) in Minor Rock Edict 1 (13th regnal year)
- **c. 260–259 BCE** — Makes pilgrimage to Bodhi Tree; begins propagating dhamma (8th–9th regnal years)
- **c. 257 BCE** — Starts inscribing edicts; appoints dhamma-mahamatta officers (12th–13th regnal years)
- **c. 251–252 BCE** — Third Buddhist council held (17th regnal year, per Sri Lankan tradition)
- **c. 250 BCE** — Buddhist missions sent to border areas (per Sri Lankan tradition)
- **c. 249 BCE** — Visits Lumbini, Buddha's birthplace (21st regnal year)
- **c. 232 BCE** — Dies in his 37th regnal year

## Body

### Sources of information

Information about Ashoka comes from his own inscriptions, other inscriptions, and ancient literature—especially Buddhist texts, which often contradict each other. His inscriptions focus mainly on dhamma and provide little information about the Maurya state or society; scholar John S. Strong suggests they are best viewed as propaganda presenting a favourable image of the king. The Buddhist legends about Ashoka exist in many languages and derive from two primary traditions: the North Indian tradition (Sanskrit texts like the Divyavadana and Ashokavadana, plus Chinese sources) and the Sri Lankan tradition (Pali texts like the Dipavamsa and Mahavamsa). These traditions differ significantly—for example, the Sri Lankan tradition emphasizes Ashoka's role in convening the Third Buddhist council and sending missionaries, while the North Indian tradition omits these events. Other sources include the Puranas, the Arthashastra, and Megasthenes' Indica, though these provide limited or debated information.

### Names, date, and ancestry

The name "A-shoka" literally means "without sorrow"; according to legend, his mother named him this because his birth removed her sorrows. He was also known as Priyadarsi ("he who regards amiably"), possibly a regnal name, and Devanampiya ("Beloved of the Gods"). The identification of Devanampiya with Ashoka is confirmed by the Maski and Gujarra inscriptions. Ashoka lived in the 3rd century BCE, as his inscriptions mention contemporary rulers whose dates are known—Antiochus II Theos, Ptolemy II Philadelphus, Antigonus II Gonatas, Magas of Cyrene, and Alexander. His father was Bindusara and his grandfather Chandragupta, the empire's founder, according to the Puranas and Mahavamsa. Legends name his mother variously as Subhadrangi, Dharma, or Janapada-kalyani.

### As a prince and ascension

Legends describe Ashoka's early life apocryphally. The Ashokavadana states his father disliked his rough skin, yet sent him to suppress a rebellion in Takshashila and (per Sri Lankan tradition) appointed him viceroy of Ujjain. At Ujjain, he met Devi, who bore his children Mahinda and Sanghamitta. His ascension was disputed: the Ashokavadana describes him being crowned by the gods after his father fell ill, while the Mahavamsa states he killed ninety-nine of his half-brothers (figures likely exaggerated to portray him as evil before his conversion). The date of his ascension depends on the debated date of Buddha's death; scholars propose 268–265 BCE.

### Kalinga war and conversion

Edict 13 of the Major Rock Edicts states that in his 8th regnal year, Ashoka conquered Kalinga: 100,000 men and animals were killed, 150,000 carried away captive, and "many times that number perished." The edict expresses profound remorse and says he devoted himself to the practice and propagation of dharma. The Sri Lankan tradition, however, claims he converted to Buddhism in his 4th regnal year—before the war—and makes no mention of Kalinga. Some scholars (like Eggermont) accept this earlier conversion; critics argue a Buddhist would not have waged such a war. Epigraphic evidence suggests his conversion was gradual: in his 13th regnal year, he states he had been an upasaka for over two and a half years but only recently drawn closer to the sangha. Different legends attribute his conversion to various monks—Nigrodha (Sri Lankan tradition), Samudra (Divyavadana), or a 7-year-old boy (A-yu-wang-chuan).

### Reign after Buddhist influence

Both Mahavamsa and Ashokavadana state Ashoka built 84,000 stupas or viharas. The pillars of Ashoka—monolithic columns with his edicts, averaging 12–15 m tall and weighing up to 50 tons—survive at twenty sites, exhibiting the characteristic Mauryan polish. His edicts describe welfare activities: medical facilities for humans and animals, planting medicinal herbs, digging wells, and planting trees along roads. By his 12th regnal year, he ordered officers to tour their jurisdictions every five years; by the next year, he created the post of dhamma-mahamatta. The Sri Lankan tradition describes the Third Buddhist council (17th regnal year), where Moggaliputta Tissa compiled the Kathavatthu, and nine missions sent to spread Buddhism, including to Sri Lanka. The North Indian tradition omits these events, leading to doubts about their historicity. However, scholars like Richard Gombrich argue Ashoka's inscriptions prove his interest in maintaining the sangha's unity.

### Religion and philosophy

Ashoka's Minor Rock Edict 1 confirms he was a Buddhist—he calls himself an upasaka and a sakya. Yet his edicts emphasize tolerance of all sects: he dedicated caves to non-Buddhist ascetics (the Ajivikas), stated that both Brahmins and shramanas deserved respect, and told people "not to denigrate other sects." There is no evidence Buddhism was a state religion. His "dhamma" meant "a moral polity of active social concern, religious tolerance, ecological awareness, the observance of common ethical precepts, and the renunciation of war"—including abolition of the death penalty, restrictions on animal killing, and medical facilities. The Kandahar inscriptions translate "Dharma" as eusebeia (Greek) and qsyt (Aramaic), suggesting it meant something more generic than Buddhism. His animal welfare edicts banned killing of specific species and nursing mothers, and abolished imperial hunting; some environmental historians see him as an early embodiment of environmental ethos.

### Foreign relations and death

Ashoka sent emissaries to Hellenistic kingdoms; Edict 13 mentions a "dhamma victory" reaching the Greek king Antiochos and four other rulers. Greeks in his dominion were described as following his dhamma instructions, and some Greek monks (like Dharmaraksita) were active in spreading Buddhism. His palace at Patna may have been modelled on the Achaemenid palace of Persepolis. His last dated inscription is from his 26th regnal year. According to the Ashokavadana, on his deathbed his only possession was half a myrobalan fruit, which he offered to the sangha; legend says his body burned for seven days during cremation. He died around 232 BCE.

### Modern scholarship

Ashoka was nearly forgotten until the 19th century, when James Prinsep deciphered the Brahmi script. In 1837, George Turnour discovered a Sri Lankan manuscript (Dipavamsa) associating Piyadasi with Ashoka, later confirmed by the Maski inscription. Scholars debate whether he was primarily a Buddhist monarch or a statesman propagating social ethics; his edicts support all major religions but also strongly indicate Buddhist affiliation. Christopher Beckwith's alternative theory—that Ashoka and Piyadasi were different kings—is rejected by mainstream scholarship as "an outlier." Some historians argue his pacifism undermined the empire's military backbone; others, like Romila Thapar, call this "grossly exaggerated."

## Terms

- **Dhamma/Dharma**: Righteous conduct or moral law; Ashoka's central theme, meaning "a moral polity of active social concern, religious tolerance, ecological awareness... and the renunciation of war"
- **Upasaka**: A lay follower of Buddhism
- **Sangha**: The Buddhist monastic community
- **Stupa**: A Buddhist commemorative monument, often containing relics
- **Vihara**: A Buddhist monastery
- **Brahmi script**: The ancient Indian script used in Ashoka's edicts, deciphered by James Prinsep in the 19th century
- **Chakravartin**: A universal ruler
- **Ajivikas**: A non-Buddhist ascetic sect; Ashoka donated caves to them
- **Nirgrantha**: A term for Jaina monks; also called Jnatiputra's followers
- **Mauryan polish**: The characteristic shiny finish on Ashokan stone pillars and capitals

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether Ashoka converted to Buddhism before or after the Kalinga war; the Sri Lankan tradition places his conversion earlier, but critics argue a Buddhist would not have waged such violence. The historicity of the Third Buddhist council and the Buddhist missions is questioned because the North Indian tradition omits them, though some scholars find corroborating archaeological evidence. The exact date of his ascension depends on the debated date of Buddha's death. Whether his "dhamma" was essentially Buddhist or a more generic moral-political ideology remains contested. Christopher Beckwith's theory that Ashoka and Piyadasi were different kings is rejected by mainstream scholarship. The legends of his post-conversion violence against Jains and Ajivikas are likely sectarian fabrications, as they appear neither in his inscriptions nor in Jain texts.
