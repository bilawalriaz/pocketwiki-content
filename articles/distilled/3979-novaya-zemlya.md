# Novaya Zemlya

## Overview
Novaya Zemlya is a remote Arctic archipelago in northern Russia, separating the Barents and Kara Seas. It is historically significant as a site of early exploration, a strategic military zone during World War II and the Cold War, and the USSR's primary nuclear test site, hosting the largest nuclear weapon ever detonated.

- **1870** — First permanent settlement established at Malye Karmakuly.
- **1942** — Convoy PQ 17 scatters near Novaya Zemlya; Operation Wunderland attacks in the Kara Sea.
- **1954** — Designated as the USSR's nuclear weapons testing venue.
- **1961** — Tsar Bomba, the most powerful nuclear weapon ever, is detonated in an air burst.
- **1990** — Last nuclear test explosion at the site.
- **2019** — State of emergency declared due to mass polar bear incursions into settlements.

## Body

### Exploration and Settlement
Known to Russians since the 11th century, Novaya Zemlya was explored by Western Europeans in the 16th century during searches for the Northern Sea Route. Dutch explorer Willem Barentsz reached it in 1594 and wintered there in 1596. The western coast was mapped by Fyodor Litke in the 1820s and systematically surveyed in the 1830s. The first permanent settlement was founded in 1870. The indigenous population consisted of Nenets families resettled in the 1870s, who lived by fishing, hunting, and herding until the civilian population was relocated to the mainland in 1957 before nuclear testing began.

### World War II
During WWII, the archipelago was a strategic point along the Allied Arctic convoy route to the USSR. In 1942, the scattered merchant ships of Convoy PQ 17 sought safety in the Matochkin Strait. Later that year, the German Navy's Operation Wunderland sent warships into the Kara Sea to attack Soviet shipping. In 1943, a German U-boat sank a Soviet research ship near the islands, and a secret German seaplane base was briefly established for surveillance of Allied convoys.

### Nuclear Testing
In 1954, Novaya Zemlya became the USSR's main nuclear test site, with three designated zones. Atmospheric tests included the 30 October 1961 air burst of the Tsar Bomba. After the 1963 Limited Test Ban Treaty, testing moved underground. The largest underground test in 1973 triggered a magnitude 6.97 earthquake and a massive avalanche. Over its history, the site hosted 224 nuclear detonations with a total yield of 265 megatons. The last full-scale test was in 1990, but subcritical underwater experiments have continued since 1998. Recent satellite imagery suggests possible preparation for resumed testing.

### Geography and Environment
The archipelago is a mountainous extension of the Ural Mountains, consisting of two main islands: glaciated Severny (Northern) and tundra-covered Yuzhny (Southern), separated by the Matochkin Strait. It has a severe maritime tundra climate with long winters. The ecology supports polar bears, seals, and various birds and plants. Climate change has reduced sea ice, forcing polar bears inland more frequently, as seen in a 2019 emergency.

## Terms
- **Archipelago:** A group of islands.
- **Matochkin Strait:** The narrow body of water separating the two main islands of Novaya Zemlya.
- **Tsar Bomba:** The most powerful nuclear weapon ever detonated, tested by the USSR on 30 October 1961.
- **Subcritical nuclear test:** A nuclear experiment that does not produce a self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction.
- **Isostatic rebound:** The rise of land masses after the removal of the weight of ice sheets.
- **Tundra:** A biome characterized by low temperatures, short growing seasons, and permafrost.
- **Glasnost:** A policy of increased openness and transparency in Soviet institutions.

## Debates and open questions
The source notes uncertainty about the exact location of a 1942 German attack described in a prisoner's account, questioning whether it occurred on Dikson Island or Novaya Zemlya. The discovery of Norway lemmings on the islands is recent and may indicate a new subspecies, suggesting ongoing ecological change. The purpose of new tunneling and construction activity observed in 2023 is debated, with indications it could signal preparation for resumed nuclear testing.
