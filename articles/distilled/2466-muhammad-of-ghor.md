# Muhammad of Ghor

## Overview
Muhammad of Ghor (c. 1144–1206) was a Ghurid ruler who, with his brother, forged a vast empire across Afghanistan, Persia, and northern India. His conquests east of the Indus River laid the foundation for Islamic rule in South Asia, culminating in the Delhi Sultanate, which endured for centuries. His reign illustrates the dynamics of medieval Central Asian empires, the role of slave commanders, and the lasting political impact of military expansion.

## Timeline
- **c. 1144** — Born in the Ghur region (modern Afghanistan).
- **1163** — His brother Ghiyath al-Din becomes Ghurid ruler; Muhammad is given charge of southern domains.
- **1173** — Installed as sovereign in Ghazni, launching eastward expansion.
- **1175** — Crosses the Indus, captures Multan and Uch.
- **1178** — Defeated by Rajput coalition at Mount Abu, forcing a change in invasion routes.
- **1186** — Conquers the last Ghaznavid strongholds, securing Punjab.
- **1191** — Defeated by Prithviraj Chauhan at the First Battle of Tarain.
- **1192** — Decisively defeats Prithviraj at the Second Battle of Tarain, executing him.
- **1194** — Defeats Rajput king Jayachandra at Chandawar, expanding into the Gangetic Plain.
- **1203** — Becomes sole Sultan after his brother's death.
- **1204** — Suffers a major defeat at Andkhud against the Khwarazmians and Qara Khitai.
- **1206** — Assassinated at Damyak, Punjab.

## Body

### Early Life and Rise to Power
Muhammad was born into the Ghurid dynasty. His early years were marked by hardship and imprisonment under his uncle. After his release, he and his brother Ghiyath al-Din established a dyarchy (joint rule). Ghiyath governed the western empire from Firozkoh, while Muhammad, based in Ghazni, focused on eastward expansion into India. This partnership allowed the Ghurids to become a major power in the eastern Islamic world.

### Conquests in India
Muhammad's campaigns began by targeting the Carmathians in Multan and the Ghaznavids in Punjab. After a setback in Gujarat in 1178, he shifted to northern routes. His most significant victories were against the Rajput Confederacy. After a defeat at the First Battle of Tarain (1191), he returned with a larger army and won a decisive victory at the Second Battle of Tarain (1192), executing the Rajput leader Prithviraj Chauhan. This victory opened the Gangetic Plain to Ghurid control. He further expanded east, defeating the Gahadavala king at Chandawar (1194) and annexing Varanasi and Kanauj. He delegated much of the ongoing administration and raiding in India to his elite slave commanders, notably Qutb ud-Din Aibak.

### Central Asian Struggles and Sole Rule
Muhammad's empire faced a powerful rival in the Khwarazmian Empire to the west. After his brother's death in 1203, Muhammad became the supreme ruler but soon suffered a devastating defeat at Andkhud (1204) against a Khwarazmian-Qara Khitai alliance. This loss weakened Ghurid control in Khurasan. Following this, a major revolt by the Hindu Khokhar tribe in the Punjab forced his final military campaign to crush the rebellion.

### Assassination and Succession
On 15 March 1206, while returning from the Khokhar campaign, Muhammad was assassinated at Damyak by Ismāīlī emissaries, possibly in collusion with the Khokhars. His death triggered a succession struggle. His slave commanders, who controlled his Indian territories, became independent rulers. Qutb ud-Din Aibak established the Delhi Sultanate, while the Ghurid heartland was eventually annexed by the Khwarazmians by 1215.

## Terms
- **Dyarchy**: A system of joint rule, here between Muhammad and his brother Ghiyath al-Din.
- **Ghurid Dynasty**: A Muslim dynasty from the Ghor region of Afghanistan that ruled parts of Iran, Afghanistan, and northern India in the 12th-13th centuries.
- **Rajput Confederacy**: An alliance of Hindu warrior clans in northern India, which opposed Muhammad's invasions.
- **Delhi Sultanate**: The Islamic kingdom established in India by Muhammad's slave commander Qutb ud-Din Aibak after Muhammad's death.
- **Iqṭāʿ**: A land grant given to military officers in lieu of salary, a common practice in Islamic empires.
- **Khuṭbah**: The Friday sermon in mosques, where the ruler's name was proclaimed, signifying sovereignty.
- **Manumission**: The act of freeing a slave; Muhammad's successors formally freed his key slave commanders.
- **Qara Khitai**: A Mongol and Chinese dynasty that ruled in Central Asia, allied with the Khwarazmians against the Ghurids.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Motives for Assassination**: Sources differ on whether the assassins were primarily Khokhars avenging their defeat, Ismāīlīs fearing his expansion, or acting in concert.
- **Scale of Armies**: Chroniclers' accounts of army sizes (e.g., 120,000-130,000 for Muhammad at Tarain) are considered likely exaggerations.
- **Legacy in India**: While his conquests were transformative, the short-lived nature of the Ghurid empire itself means his direct rule was brief; the lasting impact came through the successor states founded by his slaves.
