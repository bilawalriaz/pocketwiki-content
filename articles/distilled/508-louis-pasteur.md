# Louis Pasteur

## Overview
Louis Pasteur (1822–1895) was a French chemist and microbiologist whose experimental work established the germ theory of disease, disproved spontaneous generation, and founded modern bacteriology and immunology. He discovered molecular chirality, invented pasteurization to prevent beverage spoilage, and developed the first laboratory-attenuated vaccines for anthrax and rabies, establishing the principle that artificially weakened pathogens could induce immunity. His methods revolutionized public health, surgery, and the silk industry, though historical analysis of his private notebooks later revealed instances of scientific rivalry, secrecy, and methodological deviations from his public accounts.

## Timeline
- **27 Dec 1822** — Born in Dole, Jura, France.
- **1842** — Failed first entrance test for École Normale Supérieure (ENS); low ranking.
- **1843** — Entered ENS in Paris after passing entrance test with high ranking.
- **1848** — Discovered molecular chirality via tartrate crystals; appointed professor of physics at Dijon Lycée, then chemistry at University of Strasbourg.
- **1854** — Became Dean of Faculty of Sciences at University of Lille; began fermentation research.
- **1857–1858** — Published memoirs proving fermentation is caused by living microorganisms (yeast), not chemical decomposition.
- **1861** — Observed the "Pasteur effect": yeast ferments less sugar in presence of air.
- **1862** — Won Alhumbert Prize (2,500 francs) for experiments disproving spontaneous generation using swan-neck flasks.
- **1865** — Patented pasteurization (heating wine 60–100 °C) to prevent "diseases" of wine.
- **1865–1870** — Investigated silkworm diseases (pébrine and flacherie) in Alès; developed microscopic egg-selection method to control pébrine.
- **1868** — Suffered severe stroke causing left-side paralysis.
- **1877–1880** — Developed chicken cholera vaccine concept (attenuation by air exposure); introduced term "attenuation."
- **1881** — Public anthrax vaccine trial at Pouilly-le-Fort (5 May); used potassium dichromate-killed vaccine despite claiming "live" method.
- **6 Jul 1885** — First human rabies vaccination (Joseph Meister) using Roux's dried nerve-tissue method.
- **1887** — Pasteur Institute founded in Paris; inaugurated 14 Nov 1888.
- **28 Sep 1895** — Died near Paris; state funeral; remains interred in Pasteur Institute vault.

## Body

### Molecular Asymmetry and Crystallography
At ENS and Strasbourg, Pasteur investigated tartrates—salts of tartaric acid. He found that natural tartaric acid rotated polarized light, while synthetically produced acid (racemic mixture) did not, despite identical composition. Examining crystals, he observed two asymmetric forms: right-handed (dextrorotatory) and left-handed (levorotatory). He manually separated them, demonstrating that optical activity derives from molecular asymmetry (chirality). This was the first demonstration of molecular chirality and the first explanation of isomerism, founding stereochemistry and influencing structural and medicinal chemistry.

### Fermentation, Germ Theory, and Pasteurization
At Lille (1854), Pasteur studied fermentation at the request of a beetroot alcohol manufacturer. Confirming Theodor Schwann’s work that yeast is alive, he proved in 1857–1858 that fermentation is a biological process caused by specific microorganisms (yeast for alcohol, "lactic yeast" for souring), refuting the chemical decomposition theory of Jöns Jacob Berzelius and Justus von Liebig. He showed contamination by different microbes caused spoilage in wine, beer, and milk. This led to **pasteurization** (patented 1865): heating liquids to 60–100 °C to kill microbes. He proposed that microbes infecting animals cause disease, directly supporting the **germ theory of disease** and inspiring Joseph Lister’s antiseptic surgery.

### Disproving Spontaneous Generation
Pasteur’s fermentation work implied microbes come from pre-existing microbes, not spontaneous generation. The French Academy of Sciences offered the Alhumbert Prize to settle the debate with Félix Archimède Pouchet, who claimed air caused spontaneous generation. Pasteur’s 1860s experiments—boiled broth in sealed flasks, flasks open to dusty air, high-altitude flasks (less dust, less growth), and **swan-neck flasks** (curved necks trapped dust; growth only if liquid touched contaminated neck)—proved microbes originate from airborne particles (germs), not pure air or the liquid itself. He won the prize in 1862, declaring the doctrine mortally wounded.

### Silkworm Diseases (Pébrine and Flacherie)
From 1865–1870 in Alès, Pasteur addressed two diseases devastating French sericulture. **Pébrine**: caused by corpuscles (later identified as microsporidia) visible in silkworms. Initially misidentifying corpuscles as a symptom, he concluded in 1870 they were the cause and the disease was hereditary. He developed a practical control: crush moths after egg-laying, examine pulp microscopically, destroy eggs if corpuscles found. This saved the Cévennes silk industry. **Flacherie**: Pasteur attributed it to bacteria (now thought viral); hygiene prevented accidental spread, selecting healthy moths prevented hereditary spread. Despite success against pébrine, flacherie persisted, limiting full recovery of French sericulture.

### Immunology and Vaccination
Pasteur extended Edward Jenner’s smallpox principle (using a related mild virus) by **artificially attenuating** pathogens in the lab, coining the generic term "vaccines" in Jenner's honor.

*   **Chicken Cholera (1877–1880)**: Using *Pasteurella multocida* from Henry Toussaint, assistants Émile Roux and Charles Chamberland found old cultures lost virulence. Pasteur attributed attenuation to oxygen exposure. The vaccine did not consistently confer immunity, but established the attenuation concept.
*   **Anthrax (1881)**: Robert Koch had identified *Bacillus anthracis* (1876). Toussaint developed a heat-killed vaccine (1880); Pasteur dismissed killed vaccines, seeking live attenuation. His lab found growing bacilli at 42 °C prevented sporulation. At the public Pouilly-le-Fort trial (May 1881), 25 vaccinated sheep/goats survived virulent challenge; 25 controls died. Pasteur claimed a "live vaccine," but notebooks reveal he used Chamberland’s potassium dichromate-killed method, similar to Toussaint’s. A bitter priority dispute with Koch followed.
*   **Swine Erysipelas (1882–1883)**: Assistant Louis Thuillier identified the bacillus; passage through pigeons increased virulence, passage through rabbits attenuated it, yielding a vaccine.
*   **Rabies (1885)**: Roux developed a vaccine by drying infected rabbit spinal cord (desiccating virus). After 50 dog tests (notebooks show 11), Pasteur treated 9-year-old Joseph Meister (6 July 1885) over 11 days with progressively fresher (less attenuated) virus. Meister survived. Pasteur, not a licensed physician, risked prosecution but was defended by attending physicians. He treated 350 people in 1886 with one failure, founding modern rabies prophylaxis.

### Controversies and Historical Reassessment
Pasteur ordered his family to never reveal his laboratory notebooks. They remained sealed until donated to the French national library in 1964 (restricted until 1971, catalogued 1985). Historian Gerald L. Geison’s 1995 analysis revealed:
*   **Fermentation**: Priority dispute with Antoine Béchamp (who proposed incorrect "microzyme" theory); Pasteur’s 1858 publication followed Béchamp’s 1858 paper.
*   **Anthrax Vaccine**: Pasteur publicly claimed a live attenuated vaccine at Pouilly-le-Fort but used a killed vaccine (potassium dichromate), misleading the Academy and rival Toussaint.
*   **Experimental Ethics**: Meister vaccination lacked medical license, pre-clinical rigor (few dogs tested), and secrecy. Assistant Roux (a physician) refused to participate. Physicians Grancher and Vulpian supervised; Grancher administered injections.
*   **Character**: Debré (1998) described Pasteur as genius but unfair, combative, arrogant, and dogmatic.

### Awards, Honours, and Legacy
Major awards: Pharmaceutical Society (1,500 francs, 1853), Rumford Medal (Royal Society, 1856), Copley Medal (1874), Montyon Prize (1859), Jecker Prize (1861), Alhumbert Prize (1862), Leeuwenhoek Medal (1895). Foreign Member Royal Society (1869), Académie française (1881), Grand Cross Legion of Honour (1881). The **Pasteur Institute** (founded 1887, inaugurated 1888) became a global network (32 institutes in 29 countries by 1891+), pioneering microbiology education (Roux’s 1889 *Cours de Microbie Technique*). Streets, hospitals, universities, and statues worldwide bear his name. UNESCO/Institut Pasteur Medal awarded biennially since 1995.

### Personal Life
Married Marie Laurent (daughter of Strasbourg rector) in 1849; she assisted him scientifically. Five children; three died of typhoid (Jeanne, 9; Camille, 2; Cécile, 12). Survivors: Jean Baptiste (soldier, Franco-Prussian War) and Marie Louise. Pasteur suffered strokes in 1868 and 1894. He retained a spiritual, non-dogmatic Catholic faith, praying while working, but opposed mixing science with religion. Died 28 September 1895; state funeral; reinterred in Pasteur Institute vault with Byzantine mosaics depicting his work.

## Terms
- ****Molecular chirality**** — Geometric property where a molecule is non-superimposable on its mirror image (like hands), causing optical activity (rotation of polarized light).
- ****Racemic mixture**** — A 1:1 mixture of two enantiomers (mirror-image isomers) that is optically inactive because rotations cancel out.
- ****Germ theory of disease**** — The principle that many diseases are caused by specific microorganisms (bacteria, viruses, fungi) invading the body, rather than by miasma or spontaneous generation.
- ****Pasteurization**** — Heat treatment (60–100 °C) of liquids (milk, wine, beer) to kill pathogenic and spoilage microbes without boiling, extending shelf life.
- ****Spontaneous generation**** — The obsolete doctrine that living organisms can arise from non-living matter (e.g., microbes appearing in sterile broth without contamination).
- ****Swan-neck flask**** — Flask with a long, curved neck allowing air exchange but trapping dust/microbes on the walls, used by Pasteur to disprove spontaneous generation.
- ****Attenuation**** — Laboratory process of reducing a pathogen's virulence (ability to cause disease) while keeping it alive (or intact) to stimulate protective immunity.
- ****Pébrine**** — Silkworm disease caused by microsporidian parasites (Nosema bombycis), identified by black spots ("corpuscles"); hereditary and contagious.
- ****Flacherie**** — Silkworm disease ("dead-flat") causing digestive collapse; Pasteur attributed to bacteria, now thought viral; spread by hygiene failures or heredity.
- ****Rabies vaccine (Roux method)**** — First human rabies vaccine (1885): virus grown in rabbits, attenuated by drying (desiccating) infected spinal cord tissue over increasing time intervals.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Priority in fermentation**: Whether Pasteur or Antoine Béchamp deserves primary credit for establishing the microbial nature of fermentation; Béchamp published earlier in 1858 but proposed the incorrect "microzyme" theory.
*   **Anthrax vaccine methodology**: Pasteur’s public claim of a "live attenuated vaccine" at Pouilly-le-Fort (1881) contradicted his notebooks showing use of a potassium dichromate-killed vaccine (Chamberland/Toussaint method), raising questions about scientific integrity and credit assignment.
*   **Rabies vaccine ethics and efficacy**: The Meister trial lacked a medical license, rigorous animal pre-trials (notebooks: 11 dogs vs. claimed 50), and informed consent; the vaccine’s exact mechanism (killed vs. live attenuated) and batch consistency remained uncertain.
*   **Notebook secrecy**: Pasteur’s lifetime suppression of laboratory notebooks prevented contemporary verification; their late release (1971/1985) enabled historical critique but leaves some methodological details unresolved.
*   **Koch–Pasteur rivalry**: Nationalistic and personal tensions (France vs. Germany) fueled disputes over anthrax vaccine priority, culture purity, and statistical interpretation, complicating the historical record of early bacteriology.
