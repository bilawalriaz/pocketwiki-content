# Ibn Khaldun

## Overview
Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406) was a North African Arab scholar whose *Muqaddimah* (Introduction) established a systematic, causal philosophy of history and society. Writing amid the fragmented dynastic politics of the Maghreb and Mamluk Egypt, he argued that civilizations follow a predictable cycle driven by **ʿaṣabiyyah** (group solidarity). This cohesion enables nomadic groups to conquer sedentary states, but the resulting luxury and bureaucracy erode the solidarity that brought them to power, inviting conquest by a new cohesive group. He treated history as a science requiring critical verification of causes rather than mere transmission of reports, pioneering concepts in sociology, economics (labor theory of value, taxation cycles), and demography centuries before their European formulation.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Ibn Khaldun was born in Tunis in 1332 to a family that had fled Seville after the 1248 Reconquista. His brother, Yahya ibn Khaldun, was also a historian who wrote a book on the Abdalwadid dynasty and was assassinated by a rival for being the official historiographer of the court. The Black Death of 1348–1349 killed his parents and teachers, a turning point that redirected him from scholarly retreat to political engagement. He received a classical Islamic education—memorizing the Qur’an, mastering Arabic linguistics, Mālikī jurisprudence (*fiqh*), *hadith*, and *sharia*—and earned *ijāzah* (certification) in these fields. The philosopher Al-Abili introduced him to logic, mathematics, and the works of Averroes, Avicenna, al-Razi, and al-Tusi, grounding his later scientific approach to history. Al-Sakhāwī reports that he memorized the Qur’an and studied qirāʾāt (including the seven readings), along with Arabic and Mālikī jurisprudence in Tunis before his later travels and state service.

### Political Career in the Maghreb and Al-Andalus
Khaldun’s career epitomized the volatile politics of 14th-century North Africa. At 20, he entered the Tunisian chancellery but fled to Fez after the 1352 invasion by Abū Ziad of Constantine. Under the Marinids, he rose to write royal proclamations but was imprisoned (1357–1358) for intriguing against Sultan Abū Inan. He subsequently served—and conspired against—a succession of rulers in Fez, Tlemcen, and Béjaïa, leveraging his rapport with Berber tribes. Al-Sakhāwī summarizes his career as including travel through major western Islamic centers such as Fez, Granada, and Tlemcen, alongside repeated shifts between political roles. In 1364, Sultan Muhammad V of Granada sent him to negotiate a peace treaty with Pedro the Cruel of Castile; Khaldun declined Pedro’s offer to stay and reclaim his family’s Spanish estates. Rivalry with Granada’s vizier Ibn al-Khaṭīb forced his return to Africa, where he served as prime minister for the Hafsid ruler of Béjaïa and later allied with the Sultan of Tlemcen. Exhausted by constant allegiance-switching, he sought refuge with the Awlad ʿArif tribe at Ibn Salama (1375–1379), where he drafted the *Muqaddimah*. He returned to Tunis in 1378 to finish his history but fled to Egypt in 1382 under pretext of the Hajj after the ruler questioned his loyalty.

### Later Life in Mamluk Egypt
In Cairo, Khaldun found the stability lacking in the Maghreb. Sultan Barquq appointed him professor at the Qamhiyyah Madrasah and Grand Qadi of the Mālikī school (1384), a post he held six times but never for long. Al-Sakhāwī similarly states that Ibn Khaldūn taught at al-Azhar and was appointed to the Mālikī judgeship in Cairo more than once. He attempted judicial reforms but resigned within a year amid resistance. The 1384 shipwreck that killed his family deepened his withdrawal from politics. After a pilgrimage to Mecca (1388), he focused on teaching and writing. In 1400–1401, he accompanied Sultan Faraj’s campaign against Timur’s siege of Damascus. When Faraj abandoned the army, Khaldun remained for seven weeks, lowered by ropes to negotiate with Timur. He produced detailed reports on the Maghreb for Timur and on the Tatars and Timur’s character for the Marinid rulers in Fez. His final years involved teaching, judging, and completing his autobiography; he was alleged to have joined an underground party, Rijal Hawa Rijal, whose reform-oriented ideals attracted the attention of local political authorities. The elderly Ibn Khaldun was placed under arrest. He died in 1406.

### Major Works: *Kitāb al-ʿIbar* and the *Muqaddimah*
*Kitāb al-ʿIbar* ("Book of Lessons") is a seven-book universal history. Book 1, the *Muqaddimah*, is a standalone theoretical framework analyzing the rise and fall of dynasties. Books 2–5 cover world history; Books 6–7 focus on Berber and Maghrebi history, synthesizing sources like Ibn Abi Zar’s *Rawḍ al-Qirṭās* without citations—a methodological break from classical *isnād*-based historiography.

**Core Concept: *ʿAṣabiyyah* (Group Solidarity).** This cohesion arises spontaneously in kinship-based tribes and can be amplified by religious ideology. It enables groups to conquer sedentary civilizations. However, conquest brings luxury, sedentary habits, and bureaucracy, which dilute *ʿaṣabiyyah*. The dynasty weakens until a new group with stronger solidarity overthrows it—a cyclical pattern Khaldun considered a universal law of civilization.

**Economics and Labor.** Khaldun articulated a labor theory of value: cooperative division of labor (e.g., smith, carpenter, farmers) produces surplus far exceeding the workers’ needs. He analyzed taxation cycles: low assessments yield high revenue early in a dynasty; high assessments yield low revenue later due to economic discouragement—a precursor to the Laffer curve. He advocated intrinsic-value currency (gold *dinar*, silver *dirham*) with strict weight/purity standards (1 *dinar* = 1 *mithqal* ≈ 4.25g; 7 *dinars* = 10 *dirhams*).

**Sociology and Epistemology.** He divided knowledge into religious sciences (Qur’anic) and non-religious (intellectual: logic, geometry; auxiliary: language, poetry). He viewed the state as a necessary injustice—force restraining injustice, yet force itself is injustice. Civilization begins with basic needs, expands through productivity, peaks in sedentary crafts/arts, then decays. Educational tradition is essential to transmit achievements across generations. Mastery of Arabic language, not land or lineage, defined membership in Arab society. He identified *fikr* (human faculty of thought) as the driver of social cooperation and division of labor.

**Religious Thought.** Khaldun saw the human spirit as an immortal mediator between God and the sensory world. Prophets are souls retaining full communicative ability with God. He validated Sufi detachment *if* pursued sincerely, warning that desire for superiority leads to demonic, not divine, inspiration. True vision of the future requires a pure, pious spirit; otherwise, it is devilish illusion.

### Minor Works
- *Lubābu l-Muhassal* (age 19): Commentary on Fakhr al-Din al-Razi’s theology, supervised by Al-Abili.
- *Shifāʾu s-Sāʾil* (c. 1373): Treatise on Sufism, written in Fez.
- *ʿAllaqa li-s-Sulṭān*: Work on logic composed at the court of Muhammad V of Granada.
- *Al-Taʿrīf bi Ibn Khaldūn* (Autobiography): Detailed, document-rich account of his life and travels.

## Terms
- ****ʿAṣabiyyah**** — Group solidarity or social cohesion arising spontaneously in tribes/kinship groups; intensified by religion; the engine of dynastic rise and, through its erosion by luxury and bureaucracy, of dynastic fall.
- ****Muqaddimah**** — "Introduction" (Prolegomena); Book 1 of *Kitāb al-ʿIbar*, a theoretical philosophy of history and society analyzing the cyclical dynamics of civilization.
- ****Kitāb al-ʿIbar**** — "Book of Lessons"; seven-volume universal history: Book 1 theory, Books 2–5 world history, Books 6–7 Berber/Maghreb history.
- ****ʿUmrān**** — Civilization / settled life; the phenomenon Khaldun treats as the subject of his science, encompassing sedentary culture, crafts, politics, and urbanization.
- ****Fikr**** — The human faculty of thought/reason; the cognitive basis for social cooperation, division of labor, and political organization.
- ****Ijāzah**** — Formal certification/license granted by a teacher authorizing a student to transmit a specific text or body of knowledge.
- ****Mālikī school**** — One of four major Sunni schools of jurisprudence (*fiqh*), predominant in North/West Africa; Khaldun served as its Grand Qadi in Cairo.
- ****Dinar / Dirham**** — Gold and silver coins of the Islamic monetary system; Khaldun insisted on fixed weights (1 dinar = 1 mithqal ≈ 4.25g; 7 dinars = 10 dirhams) to preserve intrinsic value.
- ****Isnād**** — Chain of transmitters validating a *hadith* or historical report; Khaldun broke with this method by evaluating content critically rather than relying solely on transmitter credibility.
- ****Rijal Hawa Rijal**** — An underground reformist party in Cairo with which Khaldun was allegedly linked late in life, leading to his arrest.

## Debates and open questions
- **Arab ancestry claim:** Khaldun insists on descent from the Hadhramaut tribe via Wa’il ibn Hujr. Some scholars accept this; others argue the emphasis serves to legitimize his status in a Berber-dominated political landscape.
- **Racial views:** Passages on the Zanj (sub-Saharan Africans) and Berber-Arab distinctions have been cited as racist. Abdelmajid Hannoum argues translator William McGuckin de Slane inserted a racial ideology into *Histoire des Berbères* that distorts Khaldun’s original sociological categories.
- **Originality vs. synthesis:** Debate continues over whether Khaldun’s cyclical theory, labor value, and taxation insights are original discoveries or brilliant syntheses of earlier Islamic thought (e.g., al-Farabi, al-Mawardi, the *Ikhwan al-Safa*).
- **Direct influence on Europe:** No evidence Khaldun’s works were known in Europe before the 17th-century Ottoman intermediaries (Kâtip Çelebi, Mustafa Naima) and 19th-century Orientalist translations (Silvestre de Sacy). Parallels with Machiavelli, Vico, Smith, Marx, and Comte are treated as convergent precedent, not transmission.
- **Reception in Egypt:** Contemporary critics (notably Ibn Hajar al-ʿAsqalānī) accused him of historical inaccuracy, disorganization, and violating judicial customs. Only Ibn al-Azraq engaged substantively with his theory. Al-Maqrīzī praised the *Muqaddimah* but may have misunderstood its methodology.
- **Autobiography’s reliability:** Muhsin Mahdi argues Khaldun’s silence on his conflict with Ibn al-Khaṭīb reveals later self-recognition of misjudging Muhammad V; others read it as political discretion.
