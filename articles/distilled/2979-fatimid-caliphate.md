# Fatimid Caliphate

## Overview
The Fatimid Caliphate (909–1171 CE) was a major Isma'ili Shi'a dynasty that ruled across North Africa and the Middle East, with Egypt as its heartland. It is significant for establishing a powerful Shi'a empire rivaling the Sunni Abbasid Caliphate, fostering a vibrant multicultural society, and leaving a lasting architectural and intellectual legacy, particularly through Cairo and Al-Azhar Mosque.

## Timeline
- **909** — Abdallah al-Mahdi Billah proclaimed first Fatimid Caliph in Ifriqiya.
- **921** — Capital established at al-Mahdiyya (Tunisia).
- **969** — General Jawhar conquers Egypt for Caliph al-Mu'izz.
- **973** — Caliph al-Mu'izz moves the capital to the newly founded Cairo.
- **1004–1021** — Reign of Caliph al-Hakim, marked by autocratic rule and persecution of non-Muslims.
- **1060s** — Civil war between ethnic army factions and the "Mustansirite Hardship" famine.
- **1074** — Badr al-Jamali becomes military vizier, temporarily reviving the state.
- **1094** — Death of Caliph al-Mustansir leads to a schism between Nizari and Musta'li Isma'ilis.
- **1099** — Crusaders capture Jerusalem from the Fatimids.
- **1171** — Saladin abolishes the Fatimid Caliphate, founding the Ayyubid dynasty.

## Body

### Origins and Rise to Power
The Fatimids emerged from the Isma'ili Shi'a movement, which believed in a line of hidden Imams descended from the Prophet Muhammad's daughter Fatima. In 899, the movement's leader, Abdallah, declared himself the awaited messianic Imam, causing a split with the Qarmatians. His missionary (da'i), Abu Abdallah al-Shi'i, successfully rallied the Kutama Berbers in Ifriqiya (modern Tunisia/Algeria). Between 902 and 909, Abu Abdallah's forces overthrew the Aghlabid dynasty, which ruled nominally for the Abbasids. In 909, Abdallah al-Mahdi Billah was brought from Sijilmasa to become the first Fatimid Caliph, establishing a new Shi'a state.

### Consolidation and the Move to Egypt
The early Fatimid state was a Kutama Berber hegemony. After failed invasions of Egypt in the 910s–920s, the capital was moved to coastal al-Mahdiyya (921) and later al-Mansuriyya (948) for security. The caliphate consolidated control over the Maghreb and Sicily while rivaling the Umayyads of Cordoba. In 969, Caliph al-Mu'izz's general Jawhar conquered Egypt from the Ikhshidids. A new capital, Cairo (al-Qahira), was founded in 969. In 973, Caliph al-Mu'izz moved the court to Cairo, making Egypt the political, cultural, and economic center of the empire, which at its height included parts of North Africa, the Levant, and the Hejaz.

### Society and Administration
Fatimid society was pluralistic. Isma'ili Shi'ism was the state religion, but the majority of Muslims remained Sunni, and significant Christian (especially Coptic) and Jewish communities existed. Non-Muslims (dhimmis) paid the jizya tax but generally enjoyed tolerance and could hold high office, a policy that ensured administrative talent and revenue. The state promoted Isma'ili doctrine through a hierarchical missionary network (da'wa) and institutions like the Al-Azhar Mosque. The army was initially based on Kutama Berbers but later incorporated Turkish, Daylami, and Black African soldiers, leading to ethnic factionalism.

### Decline and Fall
Internal decay began with the long, detached reign of al-Mustansir (1036–1094), marked by civil war between army factions and severe famine. The powerful military vizierate, established by Badr al-Jamali (1074–1094), made the caliph a figurehead. External threats mounted: the Seljuks took Syria in the 1070s, and the First Crusade captured Jerusalem in 1099. A succession crisis after al-Mustansir's death split Isma'ili Islam into Nizari and Musta'li branches. The final century saw constant vizierial coups and Crusader incursions. In 1171, Saladin, the Ayyubid vizier, deposed the last Fatimid caliph, al-Adid, and restored Sunni Abbasid allegiance.

## Terms
- **Isma'ili Shi'a**: A branch of Shi'a Islam that follows a line of Imams descended from Isma'il ibn Ja'far, rather than his brother Musa al-Kazim.
- **Da'i (plural: du'at)**: A missionary or propagandist for the Isma'ili faith, responsible for spreading the doctrine and preparing for the Imam's return.
- **Kutama**: A Berber tribal confederation from eastern Algeria that formed the core military force of the early Fatimid state.
- **Dhimmi**: A non-Muslim subject of an Islamic state, granted protection and religious autonomy in exchange for paying the jizya tax.
- **Vizier (Wazir)**: A high-ranking minister or chief advisor, who under the later Fatimids became the de facto head of government.
- **Iqta**: A system of land grants given to military officers in lieu of salary, which later weakened central authority.
- **Majalis al-hikma**: "Sessions of Wisdom," secret lectures on Isma'ili doctrine held for initiates in the Fatimid palace.
- **Nizari**: The branch of Isma'ili Shi'ism that followed Nizar, son of Caliph al-Mustansir, after the 1094 succession crisis.
- **Musta'li**: The branch of Isma'ili Shi'ism that followed al-Musta'li, the son of al-Mustansir who became caliph.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent of popular conversion to Isma'ili Shi'ism in Egypt remains unclear, with sources reporting enthusiasm that later waned.
- The character and motivations of Caliph al-Hakim are heavily debated: traditional sources portray him as insane, while modern scholarship seeks explanations in political and social context.
- The precise nature of negotiations between the Fatimids and the First Crusade is uncertain, with some sources suggesting the Fatimids may have invited the Crusaders to attack the Seljuks.
