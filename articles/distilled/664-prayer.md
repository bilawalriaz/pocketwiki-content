# Prayer

## Overview  
Prayer is a deliberate act of communication with a deity, ancestor, or sacred power, ranging from formal liturgy to spontaneous utterance. It appears across virtually all religious traditions and serves purposes such as petition, praise, thanksgiving, and spiritual communion. Its forms, postures, and theological meanings vary widely, and scientific inquiry into its healing effects has produced inconclusive or contradictory results.

## Timeline  
- **c. 2600 BC** — Earliest known liturgical texts (e.g., Kesh temple hymn) addressed to deities
- **c. 2500 BC** — Egyptian Pyramid Texts contain spells and incantations to gods
- **Iron Age** — Polytheistic Greek and Roman religions develop highly ritualized prayer
- **c. 8th century AD** — Wessobrunn Prayer composed, possibly Christianized pagan origin
- **1872** — Francis Galton conducts statistical experiment on royal longevity and prayer
- **1988** — Double-blind study claims intercessory prayer improves cardiac recovery
- **2001** — Mayo Clinic double-blind study finds no effect of intercessory prayer
- **2006** — Meta-analysis concludes no discernible effect of prayer on healing

## Body  

### Forms and Practices  
Prayer takes many forms: liturgical or spontaneous, individual or communal, spoken, chanted, or sung. Physical postures include standing, kneeling, prostration, bowing, and hand gestures such as clasping or raising. Accompaniments may include anointing with oil, lighting candles, burning incense, facing a sacred direction, or fasting. Prayer can be structured (e.g., daily liturgies) or informal, embedded in daily life or reserved for special occasions.

### Historical Development  
The earliest attested prayers appear in ancient Mesopotamian and Egyptian texts, often as hymns or incantations. In ancient Greece and Rome, prayer was a legal and social act, governed by formulaic rituals and the principle *do ut des* (“I give so that you may give”). Ancestor worship was integral to polytheistic practice and persists in traditions like Shinto and Chinese folk religion. Medieval Christian prayer emphasized kneeling and clasped hands, evolving from earlier standing postures with raised arms.

### Approaches to Prayer  
Prayer is understood through several theological lenses: *direct petition* (appealing to a deity for specific outcomes), *educational* (shaping moral character rather than influencing outcomes), *rationalist* (using prayer as intellectual training toward divine understanding, as in Maimonides), and *experiential* (seeking direct mystical encounter, as in hesychasm or Sufism). Kabbalistic Judaism views prayer as affecting cosmic structure, while Christian mysticism integrates meditation and contemplation through practices like lectio divina.

### Major Religious Traditions  
In **Judaism**, prayer is structured around three daily services (Shacharit, Mincha, Ma’ariv), emphasizing intention (kavanah) and communal participation (minyan). **Christianity** centers on the Lord’s Prayer and includes diverse practices from liturgical recitation to charismatic glossolalia. **Islam** mandates five daily salah prayers facing Mecca, incorporating specific physical postures. **Hinduism** uses mantras and meditation directed toward deities or the formless Brahman. **Buddhism** treats prayer as aspiration and merit-transfer rather than supplication to a creator god. **Sikhism** emphasizes the Ardās, a standing prayer of supplication and gratitude. Other traditions, including **Baháʼí**, **Mandaean**, and **Zoroastrian**, maintain distinct daily prayer routines.

### Prayer and Healing  
Scientific studies on intercessory prayer and health have yielded inconsistent results. Early experiments, such as Galton’s 1872 study on royal longevity, found no effect. Later controlled trials, including the 2001 Mayo Clinic study and the 2006 STEP trial, reported no significant benefit—and some suggested harm—when subjects knew they were being prayed for. Critics argue that experimental designs fail to capture the personal, contextual nature of prayer, and that intercessors often object to scripted or impersonal protocols. Surveys indicate widespread lay use of prayer for health, with 43% of Americans reporting praying for their own health in 2002.

## Terms  
- **Kavanah**: Intention or inward focus required in Jewish prayer.  
- **Keva**: Fixed, ritualistic structure of prayer in Judaism.  
- **Do ut des**: Latin phrase meaning “I give so that you may give,” describing reciprocal prayer in ancient Rome.  
- **Salah**: The five daily ritual prayers in Islam.  
- **Hesychasm**: A contemplative prayer practice in Eastern Orthodoxy aimed at achieving union with God.  
- **Lectio divina**: A Christian practice of scriptural reading, meditation, and prayer.  
- **Mantra**: A repeated sacred sound or phrase used in Hindu and Buddhist prayer.  
- **Dua**: Personal supplication in Islam, distinct from ritual salah.  
- **Glossolalia**: Speaking in tongues, often associated with Pentecostal prayer.  
- **Ardās**: A Sikh prayer of supplication, traditionally recited standing with folded hands.

## Debates and Open Questions  
The efficacy of intercessory prayer for healing remains scientifically unresolved, with meta-analyses showing no effect and some studies suggesting negative outcomes. Theological debates persist over whether prayer should aim to change divine will or human disposition. Scholars also question whether “religious experience” is a universal category or a Western construct imposed on non-Western traditions. The role of ritualized versus spontaneous prayer, and the validity of measuring spiritual phenomena empirically, continue to divide researchers and believers.
