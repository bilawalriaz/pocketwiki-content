# Uruguay

## Overview
Uruguay is a small, stable democracy in southeastern South America, known for its progressive social policies, high quality of life, and early adoption of welfare state reforms. Its history is marked by a struggle for independence, periods of internal conflict, a civic-military dictatorship, and a successful return to democracy, shaping its modern identity as a secular, socially liberal nation.

## Timeline
- **1516** — First European explorer, Juan Díaz de Solís, reaches the region.
- **1680** — Portuguese establish Colônia do Sacramento.
- **1726** — Spanish found Montevideo as a military stronghold.
- **1811** — José Gervasio Artigas launches a revolt against Spanish rule.
- **1828** — Treaty of Montevideo grants Uruguay independence.
- **1830** — First constitution is adopted.
- **1903-15** — José Batlle y Ordóñez institutes major welfare and economic reforms.
- **1973** — Coup d'état establishes a civic-military dictatorship.
- **1985** — Return to civilian rule with national elections.
- **2013** — Uruguay becomes the first country to fully legalize cannabis.

## Body

### Prehistory and Colonial Rule
Human presence in the area dates back approximately 13,000 years. At the time of European contact, the predominant indigenous groups were the Charrúa, Chaná, and Guaraní, who were not organized into large political units. The region became a zone of contention between the Spanish and Portuguese empires. The Portuguese founded Colônia do Sacramento in 1680, and the Spanish established Montevideo in 1726. Uruguay's early history was shaped by fights for dominance in the Platine region.

### Independence and 19th-Century Conflict
Uruguay secured independence between 1811 and 1828 through a four-way struggle involving Spain, Portugal, the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata, and the Empire of Brazil. Following independence, politics were dominated by two parties: the conservative Blancos (Whites) and the liberal Colorados (Reds). These parties clashed in several civil wars, including the Guerra Grande (1839–1851), which involved foreign intervention. The late 19th century saw significant European immigration, primarily from Spain and Italy, which transformed the country's demographics and culture.

### Early 20th Century and Welfare State
President José Batlle y Ordóñez (terms 1903–07 and 1911–15) implemented pioneering economic, labor, and social reforms that laid the foundations of an advanced welfare state. This period of stability and reform earned Uruguay the reputation of being the "Switzerland of the Americas." The country experienced a coup in 1933 under President Gabriel Terra, which weakened some of these reforms.

### Civic-Military Dictatorship and Return to Democracy
Economic crises and the fight against the Tupamaros, a far-left urban guerrilla group, culminated in a 1973 coup d'état. The resulting civic-military dictatorship, which lasted until 1985, was marked by political repression, state terror, and economic decline. A 1980 referendum rejected a military-drafted constitution, leading to a planned return to civilian rule. National elections in 1984 began the transition back to democracy, which has been consolidated since.

### Modern Uruguay
Since the return to democracy, Uruguay has been a stable constitutional republic. It is highly ranked in international measures of democracy, transparency, and social progress. The country is socially liberal, having legalized same-sex marriage, abortion, and, in 2013, becoming the first nation to fully legalize cannabis. It is a high-income economy and a founding member of the United Nations, the OAS, and Mercosur.

## Terms
- **Blancos (Whites):** One of Uruguay's two traditional political parties, historically representing conservative, rural agricultural interests.
- **Colorados (Reds):** One of Uruguay's two traditional political parties, historically representing liberal, urban business interests.
- **Tupamaros:** A Marxist–Leninist urban guerrilla group active in Uruguay in the 1960s and early 1970s.
- **Civic-military dictatorship:** The period of authoritarian rule in Uruguay from 1973 to 1985, led jointly by civilian and military authorities.
- **Mercosur:** The Southern Common Market, a South American trade bloc founded in 1991, of which Uruguay is a founding member.
- **Rioplatense:** The dialect of Spanish spoken in the Río de la Plata region, including Uruguay and Argentina, characterized by *voseo* and *yeísmo*.
- **Asado:** A traditional social event and method of cooking meat over an open fire or grill, central to Uruguayan cuisine.
- **Candombe:** A folk dance and musical tradition of African origin, performed especially during Carnival.
- **Gaucho:** A skilled horseman and cattle herder of the South American pampas, an important figure in Uruguayan folklore and culture.

## Debates and Open questions
The source notes scholarly debate regarding the interpretation of the name "Uruguay," with several proposed meanings from the Guaraní language, including "bird-river" and "river of painted birds." The latter, proposed by poet Juan Zorrilla de San Martín, is culturally significant but considered dubious.
