# Neutron

## Overview
A neutron is a neutral subatomic particle, slightly heavier than a proton, found in atomic nuclei alongside protons. Its discovery in 1932 by James Chadwick unlocked the secrets of nuclear structure and reactions, directly enabling nuclear fission, nuclear power, and atomic weapons. Neutrons mediate the strong nuclear force that binds nuclei, govern isotope stability through beta decay, and serve as essential tools in research, medicine, and industry.

## Body

### Discovery and Early Models
The neutron's discovery resolved fundamental inconsistencies in early atomic models. Rutherford's 1920 nucleus model included neutral particles, but the prevailing assumption was that nuclei contained "nuclear electrons." Quantum mechanics invalidated this: confined electrons would exceed nuclear binding energies (Klein paradox, 1928), and proton-electron spin combinations couldn't explain observed nuclear spins. In 1931, Bothe and Becker observed penetrating radiation from alpha-bombarded beryllium, initially misidentified as gamma rays. Chadwick's 1932 experiments proved these were uncharged particles matching Rutherford's hypothesized neutron, earning him the 1935 Nobel Prize.

### Nuclear Structure and Stability
Atomic nuclei contain Z protons and N neutrons, bound by the nuclear force. Isotopes differ only in neutron number; nuclides with identical neutron numbers are isotones; those with equal mass numbers are isobars. Neutrons stabilize nuclei by offsetting proton-proton electromagnetic repulsion—essential for multi-proton nuclei. Within nuclei, neutrons can be stable due to quantum mechanical constraints: the Pauli exclusion principle prevents decay when all lower proton states are filled. Carbon-14 exemplifies this, decaying to nitrogen-14 via beta decay with a 5,730-year half-life.

### Composition and Decay
Neutrons are composite particles (baryons) of one up quark (+2/3e) and two down quarks (-1/3e each), held by gluon-mediated strong force. Free neutrons decay via the weak interaction: n⁰ → p⁺ + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ, with a mean lifetime of ~15 minutes. This beta decay involves quark flavor change through W boson emission. The reverse process (p⁺ → n⁰ + e⁺ + νₑ) occurs in proton-rich nuclei. Electron capture (p⁺ + e⁻ → n⁰ + νₑ) and inverse beta decay represent alternative transformations.

### Properties
Neutrons are spin-1/2 fermions with no net electric charge (experimental limit: -2(8)×10⁻²²e). Their magnetic moment (-1.93 μN) arises from quark motion and confirms composite structure. Neutron mass (1.00866491606 Da) is measured indirectly via deuteron formation energy. The Standard Model predicts a tiny electric dipole moment, undetectable with current technology but targeted by four active experiments. Antineutrons (baryon number -1) were discovered in 1956; neutron-antineutron oscillations remain unobserved but could explain cosmic matter dominance.

### Detection and Sources
Neutrons require indirect detection via nuclear interactions. Capture-based methods use high-cross-section nuclides (³He, ⁶Li, ¹⁰B) that emit detectable secondary particles. Elastic scattering detectors exploit energy transfer to light nuclei. Natural neutron background originates from cosmic ray muons (spallation) and spontaneous fission in crustal uranium/thorium. Artificial sources include californium-252 (spontaneous fission), alpha-beryllium reactions, and nuclear reactors. Research facilities employ research reactors and spallation sources for intense beams.

### Applications and Risks
Neutrons enable nuclear energy through chain reactions: neutron-induced fission of ²³⁵U or ²³⁹Pu releases ~10⁷ times more energy than chemical explosives. Neutron scattering facilities use thermal/cold neutrons for material analysis. Medical applications include fast neutron therapy (>20 MeV) and boron neutron capture therapy (¹⁰B + n → ⁷Li + α). Neutron activation analysis identifies elemental composition. Health risks arise from indirect ionization; hydrogen-rich materials (water, paraffin) provide effective shielding by slowing neutrons for absorption by ⁶Li.

## Terms
- **Nucleon**: Collective term for protons and neutrons in nuclei
- **Isospin**: Symmetry treating proton and neutron as quantum states of one particle
- **Beta decay**: Radioactive process where neutron converts to proton (or vice versa) via weak interaction
- **Neutron capture cross-section**: Probability of nucleus absorbing a neutron
- **Thermal neutron**: Neutron with kinetic energy ~0.025 eV (room temperature equivalent)
- **Moderator**: Material slowing neutrons via elastic scattering (e.g., water, graphite)
- **Spallation**: Process ejecting neutrons from nuclei via high-energy particle collisions
- **Pauli exclusion principle**: Quantum rule forbidding identical fermions from identical states
- **Nuclear shell model**: Theory organizing nucleons into discrete energy levels
- **Baryon number**: Quantum number conserved in strong/electromagnetic interactions (±1 for nucleons/antinucleons)

## Debates and Open Questions
The neutron electric dipole moment remains undetected despite Standard Model predictions; its measurement could reveal physics beyond the Standard Model. Neutron-antineutron oscillations, if observed, would explain cosmic baryon asymmetry. The exact quark mass contributions to neutron properties require relativistic quantum chromodynamics calculations. Free neutron lifetime measurements show discrepancies between beam and bottle experiments (~8.2-second difference), suggesting unknown decay channels or systematic errors. The stability of dineutrons and tetraneutrons as resonant states continues to be investigated for insights into nuclear force behavior.
