# Petroleum

## Overview
Petroleum (crude oil) is a naturally occurring, yellowish-black liquid mixture of hydrocarbons found in geological formations. As a fossil fuel formed over millions of years from the anaerobic decay of buried prehistoric organisms (primarily plankton and algae), it is a non-renewable resource. Petroleum is the foundation of modern industrial society: refined via distillation into fuels (gasoline, diesel, jet fuel), lubricants, and the petrochemical feedstocks for plastics, pharmaceuticals, fertilizers, and thousands of other products. Global consumption is approximately 100 million barrels per day. Its extraction, refining, and combustion are major drivers of anthropogenic climate change (releasing ~25% of global CO₂ emissions) and cause severe local environmental damage via spills and pollution. Control of petroleum reserves has shaped 20th- and 21st-century geopolitics, enriching "petrostates" and driving conflicts. While production may peak before 2035, the transition to renewable energy and electrification is expected to reduce dependency.

## Timeline
- **>4,300 years ago** — Sumerians use bitumen for boat construction; asphalt used in Babylonian walls and towers.
- **1st century BCE** — *I Ching* records discovery, extraction, and use of raw oil in China.
- **347 CE** — Bamboo-drilled oil wells operational in China.
- **7th century** — Petroleum used as key ingredient in Greek fire (Byzantine incendiary weapon).
- **9th century** — Oil fields exploited near Baku, Azerbaijan; described by al-Razi (10th c.) and Marco Polo (13th c.).
- **1415–1450** — Seneca people dig sophisticated oil pits (4.5–6 m deep) in Western Pennsylvania.
- **1846** — First modern hand-drilled well (accidental strike) at Bibi-Heybat, near Baku, by Russian engineers.
- **1853** — Ignacy Łukasiewicz hand-digs first intentional commercial well at Bóbrka, Poland; invents kerosene lamp.
- **1858** — First commercial well in North America at Oil Springs, Ontario (James Miller Williams).
- **1859** — Edwin Drake drills first engine-drilled well in Americas at Titusville, Pennsylvania, sparking global oil boom.
- **1870s** — Development of supertankers eventually reduces shipping share of oil price from 33% (1950s) to <3% (2010).
- **1930s** — Alfred Treibs confirms biological origin of petroleum via porphyrin analysis.
- **1973** — OPEC oil embargo against Western nations supporting Israel (Yom Kippur War) triggers global energy crisis.
- **1979** — Iranian Revolution causes second oil crisis; prices more than double, spurring non-OPEC production (Alaska, North Sea, Canada oil sands).
- **2018** — U.S. becomes world's largest producer due to hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling (tight oil).
- **2020** — BP Energy Outlook suggests peak oil demand reached, accelerated by COVID-19 pandemic and energy transition.

## Body

### Composition and Classification
Petroleum is a complex mixture of liquid, gaseous, and solid hydrocarbons. Light fractions include methane, ethane, propane, and butane; heavier fractions consist of alkanes (paraffins), cycloalkanes (naphthenes), and aromatic hydrocarbons. Alkanes C5–C8 yield gasoline; C9–C16 yield diesel, kerosene, and jet fuel; >C16 yield fuel oil, lubricants, paraffin wax (~C25), and asphalt (≥C35). Heteroatoms (sulfur, nitrogen, oxygen) and trace metals (vanadium, nickel) are present. Crude oil is classified by geographic origin (e.g., West Texas Intermediate, Brent Blend), API gravity (density: light/medium/heavy), and sulfur content (sweet <0.5% S, sour >0.5% S). Light, sweet crudes command premium prices due to higher gasoline yield and lower refining costs. Benchmark crudes (WTI, Brent, Dubai-Oman, OPEC Basket) serve as global pricing references, though actual traded volumes increasingly differ from benchmark specifications.

### Formation (Fossil vs. Abiogenic)
**Fossil origin (consensus):** Organic matter (zooplankton, algae) accumulates in anoxic lake/sea bottoms (<0.1 mg/L O₂). Anaerobic bacteria hydrolyze polysaccharides/proteins to sugars/amino acids, then ferment them to CO₂, methane, and fulvic acids. Below ~10 m, bacterial action ceases; increasing heat/pressure (diagenesis) polymerizes residues into **kerogen** (waxy solid, halfway to fuel). At ~1 km depth (50–150 °C, "oil window"), **catagenesis** (thermal cracking via radical rearrangements over millions of years) converts kerogen to liquid/gaseous hydrocarbons. Heat derives from radioactive decay (⁴⁰K, ²³²Th, ²³⁵U, ²³⁸U); geothermal gradient ~10–30 °C/km. Kerogen Type I (algal) yields oil; Type II (plankton) yields oil/gas; Type III (woody) yields gas. Above the oil window, oil cracks to gas. Migration traps oil in porous reservoir rocks capped by impermeable seal rocks.

**Abiogenic hypothesis:** Proposed by Russian scientists (mid-1850s) positing inorganic mantle origin. Geochemical evidence (biomarkers, isotopes) contradicts this as a significant commercial source; abiogenic methane exists but not in profitable oil accumulations.

### Reservoirs and Extraction
A petroleum reservoir requires: (1) mature source rock, (2) porous/permeable reservoir rock (sandstone, carbonate), (3) caprock seal. Fluids segregate by density: gas cap / oil column / water leg. Hydrocarbons migrate buoyantly until trapped. Recovery phases: **Primary** (natural reservoir pressure, <40% of US daily output); **Secondary** (waterflood/gas injection to maintain pressure, ~50%); **Tertiary/Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR)** (steam, CO₂, polymers to reduce viscosity, ~10%). **Unconventional reservoirs:** Oil sands (Canada, Venezuela) contain biodegraded, immobile bitumen/extra-heavy oil (3.6 trillion barrels in-place, ~2× conventional reserves); extracted via surface mining + hot water separation or in-situ steam injection (SAGD). Oil shales (US largest deposits) are immature source rocks containing kerogen; require retorting (heat/pressure) to generate synthetic oil. Tight oil (shale oil) produced via horizontal drilling + hydraulic fracturing ("fracking") to create permeability in low-porosity rock.

### Refining and Products
Fractional distillation separates crude into boiling-range fractions. Further conversion (cracking, reforming, alkylation) upgrades heavy fractions to lighter, higher-value products. **By volume:** ~84% fuels (gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, heating oil, LPG, bunker fuel). **By value:** Petrochemicals (ethylene, propylene, BTX aromatics) are precursors for plastics, synthetic rubber, fibers, pharmaceuticals, pesticides, fertilizers, solvents, refrigerants. Lubricants, waxes, asphalt, petroleum coke, sulfur/sulfuric acid are co-products. Modern medicine, agriculture, and materials science are deeply dependent on petrochemical building blocks.

### Industry, Trade, and Economics
Crude oil trades as futures on NYMEX and ICE (contracts = 1,000 barrel multiples, up to 9 years forward). Shipping costs fell from 33% of Persian Gulf–US price (1950s) to <3% (2010) due to supertankers. Top 2018 producers: US, Russia, Saudi Arabia. ~80% of accessible conventional reserves in Middle East (62.5% in "Arab five": Saudi Arabia, UAE, Iraq, Qatar, Kuwait). "Petrostates" derive outsized political/economic power from oil rents. Studies link high oil rents to increased corruption and erosion of political rights in producer nations. Price shocks (1973, 1979, 2008, 2020) trigger recessions, spur efficiency, and accelerate alternative energy investment.

### Environmental Effects
**Climate:** Petroleum combustion emits ~25% of annual global CO₂ (plus methane leaks). Atmospheric CO₂ >415 ppm (vs. 180–300 ppm pre-industrial). Arctic sea ice minimum halved since 1979. Ocean acidification (CO₂ uptake) reduces calcium carbonate saturation, threatening marine calcifiers. **Extraction:** Habitat fragmentation, produced water toxicity, induced seismicity (wastewater injection). **Spills:** Tanker accidents (Exxon Valdez, Deepwater Horizon, Amoco Cadiz) and pipeline leaks damage coastlines; sea spills spread over hundreds of km², coating wildlife. Cleanup relies on mechanical recovery, dispersants, in-situ burning; bioremediation by bacteria (e.g., *Alcanivibrio*) degrades hydrocarbons slowly. **Tarballs:** Weathered oil blobs persist on beaches; used to fingerprint spill sources. **Nitrogen heterocycles** (pyridine, quinoline) from refining/coal processing are water-soluble groundwater contaminants.

### Geopolitics and Conflict
Oil access drove WWII strategy (German drive for Baku). 1973 embargo and 1979 crisis reshaped global alliances. Iran–Iraq War, Gulf Wars, and 2020 Russia–Saudi price war rooted in resource control. OPEC (founded 1960) coordinates production to influence prices. "Age of Oil" may wane as renewables rise; GeGaLo index predicts power shift from exporters to importers/renewable-rich nations.

### Future: Peak Oil and Energy Transition
**Peak oil** (production peak followed by decline) predicted variously for 1989, 1995, 2000; delayed by demand destruction (recessions) and unconventional supply. Discovery peak: 1965; production > discoveries since 1980. BP 2020 Outlook: peak *demand* reached (2019) due to COVID + energy transition. Focus shifts from peak supply to peak demand. **Unconventional oil** (tight oil, oil sands, deepwater subsalt) raises production ceiling but requires high capex, technology, and energy input (low EROI for oil sands). **Transition:** Road transport (49% of oil use) shifting to EVs; aviation (8%) to biojet/synfuels; petrochemicals (17%) harder to decarbonize. Divestment campaigns threaten financing for new exploration. IEA/OPEC repeatedly revise 2020+ demand forecasts downward. Non-OPEC conventional supply stable; growth from US tight oil.

### Extraterrestrial Hydrocarbons
Titan (Saturn's moon): lakes of liquid methane/ethane/propane estimated at 300× Earth's proven oil reserves. Mars: Curiosity rover detected benzene and propane in 3-billion-year-old Gale Crater rocks (2015).

## Terms
- ****API gravity**** — Oil industry measure of density; higher API = lighter (less dense) crude.
- ****Bitumen**** — Extremely viscous, semi-solid form of petroleum (crude bitumen); requires heating/dilution to flow.
- ****Catagenesis**** — Thermal cracking of kerogen into liquid/gaseous hydrocarbons at 60–150+ °C over geological time.
- ****Cracking**** — Refining process breaking large hydrocarbon molecules into smaller, more valuable ones (thermal or catalytic).
- ****Diagenesis**** — Physical/chemical transformation of buried sediment into rock; here, conversion of organic matter to kerogen.
- ****Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR)**** — Tertiary methods (steam, CO₂, polymers) to increase oil mobility and recovery beyond waterflood.
- ****Kerogen**** — Waxy, insoluble organic solid in source rocks; precursor to petroleum via catagenesis.
- ****Oil window**** — Temperature/depth range (~60–150 °C, ~1–4 km) where kerogen generates liquid oil; below = immature, above = gas window.
- ****Petrochemicals**** — Chemical products derived from petroleum feedstocks (naphtha, LPG, gas oil) used to make plastics, fertilizers, drugs, etc.
- ****Tight oil**** — Light crude trapped in low-permeability shale/tight sandstone; produced via horizontal drilling + hydraulic fracturing.

## Debates and open questions
1. **Peak oil timing vs. peak demand:** Whether geological constraints (supply peak) or climate policy/technology (demand peak) will dominate future production trajectory. BP (2020) argues demand peaked in 2019; OPEC/IEA project plateau then decline post-2030.
2. **Abiogenic contribution:** While fossil origin is consensus, the extent of deep-Earth abiotic methane/hydrocarbon generation and its potential migration to reservoirs remains a minority research topic.
3. **Unconventional resource viability:** Net energy return (EROI) of oil sands and tight oil is lower than conventional; long-term economic/environmental sustainability debated.
4. **Petrochemical demand floor:** Even with transport electrification, petrochemicals (plastics, fertilizers) may sustain significant oil demand; circular economy/bioplastics penetration uncertain.
5. **Reserve transparency:** OPEC reserve revisions (1980s jumps without discoveries) and lack of independent audit create uncertainty in global reserve estimates and peak models.
6. **Geopolitical realignment:** Speed and stability of power shift from petrostates to renewable-energy leaders (GeGaLo index) depends on critical mineral supply chains (lithium, cobalt, rare earths) for transition technologies.
