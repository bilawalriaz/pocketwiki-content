# Plastic

## Overview
Plastics are synthetic or semisynthetic materials composed primarily of polymers—long molecular chains built from repeating monomer units. Their defining property, plasticity (from Greek *plastikos*, "capable of being shaped"), allows molding, extrusion, or pressing into diverse solid forms. Combined with low weight, durability, chemical resistance, and low-cost production, this adaptability has driven global production from 9.2 billion metric tons (1950–2017) to over 400 million tons annually by 2023, with projections exceeding 1.3 billion tons by 2060. Packaging (≈40%) and construction (≈20%) dominate usage. While plastics enable critical advances in medicine, sanitation, and lightweight transport, their environmental persistence creates severe pollution: only ~9% of all plastic ever made has been recycled, 14% incinerated, and the remainder accumulates in landfills and ecosystems as macro- and microplastics. Additives (up to 20 per product, 80% by volume in PVC) leach out, posing health and ecological risks. A UN treaty negotiation launched in 2022 aims for a legally binding agreement on plastic pollution by end of 2024.

## Timeline
- **c. 1600 BC** — Mesoamericans use natural rubber for balls, bands, figurines.
- **1839** — Charles Goodyear discovers vulcanization, hardening natural rubber.
- **1855** — Alexander Parkes invents Parkesine (cellulose nitrate), first man-made plastic; patented 1856.
- **1872** — Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) first created; commercial production begins late 1920s.
- **1893** — Auguste Trillat insolubilizes casein with formaldehyde, producing galalith.
- **1907** — Leo Baekeland invents Bakelite in New York, the first fully synthetic plastic; coins term "plastics."
- **1920s** — Hermann Staudinger establishes polymer science foundations; later called "father of polymer chemistry" (Nobel 1953). Herman Mark develops polymer physics; known as "father of polymer physics."
- **1930s** — BASF first produces polystyrene.
- **1933** — ICI researchers Reginald Gibson and Eric Fawcett discover polyethylene.
- **1941** — Calico Printers' Association (UK) discovers polyethylene terephthalate (PET); licensed to DuPont (US) and ICI.
- **1954** — Giulio Natta discovers polypropylene (manufactured from 1957); Dow Chemical invents expanded polystyrene.
- **1950–2017** — Estimated 9.2 billion metric tons of plastic produced; >50% since 2004.
- **1987** — Montreal Protocol adopted; prior to this, CFCs used in polystyrene manufacture contributed to ozone depletion.
- **2022** — UN Member States vote to establish Intergovernmental Negotiating Committee for a legally binding global treaty on plastic pollution (target draft end-2024).
- **2023** — Global production >400 million metric tons (preliminary figures); "plasticosis" disease discovered in seabirds from plastic ingestion.

## Body

### Structure and Classification
Most plastics consist of organic polymers with carbon-based backbones (often with oxygen, nitrogen, or sulfur). Side chains attached to the backbone determine properties. Plastics are classified by: chemical structure (acrylics, polyesters, silicones, polyurethanes, halogenated plastics); synthesis process (condensation, polyaddition, cross-linking); physical properties (hardness, density, glass transition temperature); and manufacturing relevance.

**Thermoplastics vs. thermosets:** Thermoplastics (PE, PP, PS, PVC) soften on heating and can be remolded repeatedly; they represent 86% of production. Thermosets (epoxy, polyimide, Bakelite, vulcanized rubber) irreversibly cross-link on curing; reheating causes decomposition.

**Commodity, engineering, high-performance:** Commodity plastics (PE, PP, PS, PVC, PMMA, PET, polyurethanes) are high-volume, low-cost, lower mechanical performance; ≈80% of production. Engineering plastics (ABS, HIPS, PC, PC+ABS, PMMA, silicones, urea-formaldehyde) replace metals in vehicles (50% volume, 12–17% weight), cutting weight and improving fuel efficiency 6–8%. High-performance plastics (aramids/Kevlar, UHMWPE, PEEK, PEI, PI, PTFE, PPS, PES, PVDF, LCPs, PBI, BMI, cyanate esters) withstand >150 °C, extreme chemical/mechanical stress; used in aerospace, medical implants, electronics.

**Amorphous vs. crystalline:** Amorphous plastics lack ordered structure; crystalline plastics (HDPE, PBT, PEEK) have regular spacing. Semi-crystalline plastics (PE, PP, PVC, nylons, polyesters, some polyurethanes) exhibit both a melting point and glass transition(s).

**Conductive polymers:** Doped polyacetylene reaches 80 kS/cm (copper: 60 MS/cm); practical conductive plastic: PEDOT:PSS.

**Biodegradable plastics & bioplastics:** Biodegradable plastics (mainly PLA) break down under biological factors (UV, moisture, bacteria); limited commercial success. Bioplastics derive from renewable plant materials (cellulose, starch); global bio-based capacity ≈327,000 tonnes/year vs. >150 million tonnes for petrochemical PE+PP (2015).

### Plastic Industry
**Production:** Polymerization of petrochemical monomers in large chemical plants (economies of scale). ~100 companies account for 90% output (BASF, Dow, ExxonMobil, SABIC, Sinopec, etc.). Since 2010, Asia leads; China 31% of resin production (2020). US invested >$200B in new plants (2010+). EU industry employs 1.6M, turnover >€360B/yr.

**Compounding:** Pure resin is rarely sold. Additives (stabilizers, plasticizers, dyes, flame retardants) blended in—typically ~20 per product, up to 80% volume in PVC. Thermosets compounded as liquids; thermoplastics melted (150–320 °C) and mixed via extrusion (laminar flow requires high shear). Masterbatches concentrate additives in carrier resin for later dilution.

**Converting:** Processes include injection molding, blow molding, rotational molding, casting, film blowing, spinning, 3D printing. Thermosets start liquid, then cure. Major consumer products: LDPE packaging (bags, film), HDPE containers (milk, shampoo), PET bottles—together ≈36% of use, mostly single-use (<1 day). Construction, textiles, transport, electrical goods use longer-lived plastics (5–20+ years).

**Consumption:** NAFTA 21%, China 20%, Western Europe 18% of global consumption. Per capita: NA 94 kg, EU 85 kg, China 58 kg (but high national total).

### Additives and Health Effects
Additives (400+ high-volume in EU; 5,500 globally identified) improve lifespan, workability, appearance. All plastics contain polymer stabilizers for melt-processing. Additives remain chemically distinct, leach during use, landfilling, or environmental release. Fragmentation into micro/nanoplastics mobilizes additives. US EPA review: of 3,377 chemicals potentially in plastic packaging, 68 ranked highest for human health hazards, 68 for environmental hazards.

**Recycling complications:** Additives persist in melt-recycling, causing inconsistent properties (e.g., mixed colors → brown). Brominated flame retardants from e-waste found in recycled toys as brominated dioxins (toxicity similar to chlorinated dioxins; developmental, neuro-, endocrine effects).

**Health:** Base polymers low toxicity (insoluble, high MW). Monomers can be toxic: vinyl chloride (PVC precursor) is a known human carcinogen (IARC). Plasticizers (adipates, phthalates/DEHP) leach from PVC; restricted in EU and US (children’s products). BPA (polycarbonate building block) is an estrogenic endocrine disruptor; leaches from can linings, dental sealants, bottles; linked in animal studies to weight gain, insulin resistance, potential cancer (FDA $30M investigation 2010). Phthalates banned in EU toys; US banned certain types 2009. Heavy metals (Cd, Cr, Pb, Hg) banned in many jurisdictions but still found in food packaging. Uncontrolled burning in developing countries emits dioxins, acid gases (POPs under Stockholm Convention). BPA in baby bottles banned in many regions but not all low-income countries.

**Plasticosis (2023):** Seabirds ingesting plastic develop scarred, inflamed digestive tracts, impairing digestion, growth, survival.

### Environmental Effects
**Waste:** Estimates: 1 billion tons discarded since 1950s; or 8.3B tons produced, 6.3B tons waste, 9% recycled. 2018: >343M tons waste, 90% post-consumer. Composition: 81% polymer resin, 13% fibers, 32% additives (overlapping categories). Top marine polluters: China, Indonesia, Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam. Ten rivers (Yangtze, Indus, Yellow, Hai, Nile, Ganges, Pearl, Amur, Niger, Mekong) carry 88–95% of riverine plastic to sea. More plastic in soil than oceans. 50–80% of marine debris is plastic.

**Microplastics:** Observed in seabird guts since 1960s; increasing concentrations in food chain; long-term effects poorly understood.

**Decomposition:** Main pathways: hydrolysis, photo-oxidation. Degradability correlates with functional groups (esters, amides). Polyolefins (PE, PP, PS) lack functional groups → degrade very slowly (PE via defect reactions). PET (polyester) degrades faster via ester hydrolysis/photooxidation. PVC degrades <300 °C, releasing HCl which autocatalyzes degradation; additives capture HCl. Microbial degradation known but not scalable; white rot fungus *P. chrysosporium* degrades Bakelite.

**Recycling:** Almost all mechanical (remelt/reform). Pyrolysis (>500 °C, no O₂) cracks plastics to hydrocarbon feedstocks/fuels.

**Greenhouse gases:** OECD: 1.8 Gt CO₂e (3.4% global) in 2019; could reach 4.3 Gt by 2060. Production emits fugitive methane; energy often fossil-based. But plastics can cut emissions (e.g., food-waste-reducing packaging). 2024 study: 15 of 16 applications, plastic incurs fewer GHG emissions than alternatives (glass, aluminum). Burning plastic releases black carbon (GWP up to 5,000× CO₂). 2025 UN talks: reducing production discussed as climate imperative (plastic = 3–5% emissions, may triple by 2060).

**Energy intensity:** Crude oil → plastic: 7.9–13.7 kWh/lb. Silicon: 29.2–29.8 kWh/lb; semiconductors: ~381 kWh/lb. Compare: iron 2.5–3.2, glass 2.3–4.4, steel 2.5–6.4, paper 3.2–6.4 kWh/lb.

**Incineration:** Municipal incinerators: >850 °C for ≥2 s (EU Industrial Emissions Directive), flue-gas treatment. Uncontrolled/open burning releases carcinogenic dioxins/furans.

**Biodegradation facilitation:** Bacterium *Blaptica dubia* (cockroach gut) degrades polystyrene; biodegradation products in feces. Research on bacterial inclusion or large-scale degradation.

**Ozone depletion:** Prior to the Montreal Protocol, CFCs were commonly used in the manufacture of polystyrene, contributing to depletion of the ozone layer.

### History
Evolution: natural plastics (gums, shellac, egg/blood proteins) → chemically modified naturals (rubber, cellulose, collagen, casein) → fully synthetics. 1600 BC: Mesoamerican rubber. Middle Ages: treated cattle horns (lantern windows); horn-like materials from casein+lye. 1839: Goodyear vulcanization. 1855: Parkesine (cellulose nitrate). 1872: PVC first made. 1893: Galalith (casein+formaldehyde). 1897: Casein plastic for blackboards (Krische/Spitteler). 1907: Bakelite (Baekeland). 1920s: Hermann Staudinger establishes polymer science foundations; later called "father of polymer chemistry" (Nobel 1953). Herman Mark develops polymer physics; known as "father of polymer physics." 1923: Durite Plastics (phenol-furfural). 1930s: Polystyrene (BASF). 1933: Polyethylene (ICI). 1941: PET (Calico Printers'). 1954: Polypropylene (Natta), expanded polystyrene (Dow). 1960s+: Polycarbonate, HDPE surge. 1980s–90s: Recycling, biodegradable plastics emerge. 2000s+: Bioplastics, microplastics awareness drive research/policy.

### Policy
March 2, 2022: UNEA-5.2 resolution "End plastic pollution: Towards an international legally binding instrument" establishes Intergovernmental Negotiating Committee (INC). Mandate: start work by end-2022, complete draft global legally binding agreement by end-2024.

## Terms
- **Polymer** — A large molecule composed of repeating structural units (monomers) linked by covalent bonds; the primary constituent of plastics.
- **Monomer** — A small molecule that can bind chemically to other monomers to form a polymer chain.
- **Thermoplastic** — A polymer that softens when heated and hardens when cooled, reversibly, allowing repeated molding (e.g., PE, PP, PVC).
- **Thermoset** — A polymer that irreversibly cross-links on curing; reheating causes decomposition, not melting (e.g., epoxy, Bakelite).
- **Commodity plastic** — High-volume, low-cost plastic with modest mechanical properties, used for packaging, containers, single-use items (e.g., PE, PP, PS, PVC, PET).
- **Engineering plastic** — Higher-performance plastic used for structural parts (vehicle components, machine parts); can replace metals.
- **High-performance plastic** — Polymer with exceptional thermal (>150 °C), chemical, and mechanical resistance for aerospace, medical, electronics (e.g., PEEK, PTFE, aramids).
- **Additive** — Chemical blended into plastic to modify properties (stabilizers, plasticizers, flame retardants, colorants); remains distinct and can leach.
- **Microplastic** — Plastic fragment <5 mm, formed by degradation of larger items or manufactured directly; pervasive in environment and food chain.
- **Bioplastic** — Plastic derived substantially from renewable biomass (cellulose, starch) rather than petrochemicals.
- **Pyrolysis** — Thermal decomposition of plastic at >500 °C in absence of oxygen, yielding hydrocarbon feedstocks or fuels.
- **Plasticosis** — Fibrotic disease in seabirds caused by chronic ingestion of plastic, leading to scarred, inflamed digestive tracts (identified 2023).

## Debates and open questions
- **Recycling efficacy:** Mechanical recycling retains additives, degrades properties, achieves only ~9% global rate; chemical recycling (pyrolysis) energy-intensive and not yet at scale.
- **Bioplastic trade-offs:** Land/water use for feedstocks vs. fossil carbon reduction; biodegradability often requires industrial composting, not marine/soil conditions.
- **Additive transparency:** Identities/concentrations rarely disclosed; 5,500+ additives globally, many unassessed for health/ecotoxicity; regulatory gaps in low-income countries.
- **Microplastic health impacts:** Long-term effects of dietary/inhalation exposure in humans poorly understood; bioaccumulation and vector effects for adsorbed pollutants debated.
- **Production caps vs. circularity:** 2025 UN negotiations pivot to reducing production (3–5% GHG, may triple) vs. industry focus on waste management/recycling; black carbon from burning adds climate urgency.
- **Degradation predictability:** Polymer structure dictates degradation pathway; polyolefins persist centuries, PVC autocatalyzes via HCl; no reliable "design-for-degradation" rules for all environments.
- **Equity:** Per capita consumption 94 kg (NA) vs. 58 kg (China); waste export to Global South; uncontrolled incineration exposes vulnerable populations to dioxins/POPs.
