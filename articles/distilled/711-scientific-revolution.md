# Scientific Revolution

## Overview

The Scientific Revolution (16th–17th centuries) was an irreversible break with ancient natural philosophy, fundamentally transforming how the natural world was investigated and understood. It marked the emergence of "modern" science through a more mechanistic worldview, increased integration with mathematics, and a focus on new evidence. Culminating in Newton's *Principia* (1687), it established the foundations of modern astronomy, physics, biology, and chemistry, shifting authority from ancient texts to observation, experimentation, and mathematical reasoning.

## Body

### Background and Foundations

The Scientific Revolution built upon medieval translations of Greek, Arabic, and Byzantine texts into Latin, accelerated during the 12th–13th centuries, combined with the emergence of medieval universities. By the 16th century, Aristotelian cosmology dominated: a geocentric, hierarchical universe with four terrestrial elements seeking natural places, surrounded by an unchanging celestial realm of aether in perfect circular motion. Ptolemy's *Almagest* provided the mathematical framework for planetary calculations. However, the European encounter with the Americas (absent from Ptolemy's maps) and Vespucci's *Mundus Novus* (1503) began undermining ancient authorities' reliability.

Christianity contributed to the rise of science through key figures' religious convictions—Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo, and Newton saw the universe's order as reflecting its Creator's perfection. Whitehead argued modern science inherited "faith" in human reason from medieval scholastics. Renaissance technology, especially Gutenberg's printing press (1440s) and optical glass development, enabled knowledge dissemination and precise observation.

### Astronomy: The First Modern Science

Tycho Brahe's observation of the 1572 supernova challenged Aristotelian cosmology, as the object showed no daily parallax (implying it was beyond the Moon) yet didn't move like planets. His *De Nova Stella* (1573) documented this. Kepler, using Brahe's data, proposed elliptical planetary orbits in *Astronomia Nova*, departing from circular motion. Galileo's telescopic observations—Jupiter's moons, Venus's phases, lunar mountains, and sunspots—provided persuasive evidence for heliocentrism and discredited Aristotelian philosophy. Newton's *Principia* derived Kepler's laws from universal gravitation, removing doubts about heliocentrism and unifying terrestrial and celestial mechanics.

### Scientific Method and Empiricism

The scientific method shifted from Aristotelian deduction to inductive and mathematical approaches championed by Descartes, Galileo, and Bacon. Bacon's *Novum Organum* (1620) proposed systematic experimentation and eliminative induction, arguing "knowledge and human power are synonymous." Empiricism, developed by Locke (*tabula rasa*), Berkeley, and Hume, emphasized experience-based knowledge. Galileo demonstrated that natural laws are mathematical, writing that "philosophy is written in the language of mathematics."

### Mechanical Philosophy and Mathematization

Mechanical philosophy rejected Aristotelian final causes, viewing nature as machines governed by matter and motion. Newton's theory of gravity faced criticism for "action at a distance," but his *Principia* established mathematical principles governing all motion. Mathematization advanced through Viète's algebraic notation (1591), Descartes's *La Géométrie* (1637), and Newton's calculus, enabling quantitative physical analysis.

### Institutionalization

The Royal Society of London (1660/1662) and French Academy of Sciences (1666) institutionalized scientific investigation. The Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions* (1665) established scientific priority and peer review. These institutions supported experimental research and knowledge dissemination.

### New Ideas in Biology, Medicine, and Chemistry

Vesalius revolutionized anatomy; Harvey demonstrated blood circulation. Paracelsus advanced chemical medicine. Boyle's *Sceptical Chymist* (1661) advocated experimental chemistry and atomic theory. Gilbert founded electrical science through *De Magnete* (1600), discovering electrical properties of materials.

### Mechanical Devices

Advances included Napier's logarithms, Oughtred's slide rule (1622), Pascal's calculator (1642), and Leibniz's pinwheel calculator (1685). Telescopes (Galileo, 1609; Newton's reflecting telescope, 1668), microscopes (van Leeuwenhoek), barometers (Torricelli, 1643), and steam engines (Savery, 1698; Newcomen) enabled new investigations.

## Terms

- **Heliocentrism**: Astronomical model placing the Sun, not Earth, at the center of the universe
- **Empiricism**: Philosophical approach emphasizing experience and observation as sources of knowledge
- **Mechanical philosophy**: 17th-century worldview explaining natural phenomena through matter in motion and physical causation
- **Inductive method**: Reasoning from specific observations to general principles, advocated by Bacon
- **Tabula rasa**: Locke's concept of the mind as a "blank tablet" at birth, shaped by experience
- **Corpuscular theory**: Newton's model describing light and matter as composed of minute particles
- **Universal gravitation**: Newton's law stating all matter attracts all other matter with force proportional to mass and inversely proportional to distance squared
- **Scientific method**: Systematic approach involving observation, hypothesis, experimentation, and mathematical analysis

## Debates and Open Questions

Historians debate whether the Scientific Revolution was a radical break or continuity of earlier trends. The "continuity thesis" (Duhem, Randall, Crombie) argues the revolution is a myth. Others suggest it was merely Renaissance rediscovery of classical knowledge. Arun Bala emphasizes non-Western influences (Arabic optics, Indian mathematics, Chinese technology). Recent scholarship highlights women's marginalization from formal scientific institutions, noting significant contributions in informal settings and questioning the male-dominated historical narrative.
