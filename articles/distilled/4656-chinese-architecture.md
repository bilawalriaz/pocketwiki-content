# Chinese architecture

## Overview
Chinese architecture is an enduring architectural tradition that has developed over millennia in China, characterized by its structural principles, symbolic use of space, and profound influence across East Asia. Its continuity, despite foreign invasions and modernization, makes it a key element of Chinese civilization and a major source of architectural styles in neighboring countries.

## Timeline
- **c. 5000–2000 BC** — Neolithic cultures (Yangshao, Hongshan, Longshan) develop early settlements and ritual centers.
- **c. 1600–1046 BCE** — Shang dynasty sees earliest raised platform foundations for elite buildings.
- **523 AD** — Construction of the Songyue Pagoda, China's oldest extant pagoda, using brick.
- **605 AD** — Completion of the Zhaozhou Bridge, an early example of stone architecture.
- **782–857 AD** — Construction of the oldest surviving wooden buildings: Nanchan Temple (782) and Foguang Temple (857).
- **1055 AD** — Liaodi Pagoda built, the tallest pre-modern building in China at 84 m.
- **1368–1644** — Ming dynasty establishes Beijing as the imperial capital and constructs the Forbidden City.
- **1930s–1940s** — Architectural debate over adapting Western modernism versus traditional forms.
- **1952–1954** — Soviet socialist realism influences Chinese architecture.
- **1964** — Mao Zedong advocates for a "mass design revolution movement" promoting indigenous rammed earth construction.
- **1973–1976** — Wall Reform Movement increases use of reinforced concrete and prefabricated materials.
- **2014** — Datong begins rebuilding its ancient city wall and traditional buildings.

## Body

### Core Characteristics and Principles
Chinese architecture is defined by bilateral symmetry, enclosed open spaces, a horizontal emphasis, and the use of cosmological concepts like *feng shui*. Structures are traditionally classified by type (e.g., pagodas, palaces) and vary based on status—imperial, commoner, or religious. Due to the primary use of perishable wood, historical knowledge often comes from miniature ceramic models and diagrams. The system has retained its principal characteristics for over four thousand years across a vast cultural sphere.

### Historical Development and Materials
Early construction used rammed earth and wood. From the Tang dynasty (618–907) onward, brick and stone became more common. The oldest surviving wooden buildings date to the Tang and Song dynasties. Structures are typically built on raised platforms, with timber frames using joinery and dowelling rather than nails. The *dougong* bracket system is a key structural and decorative feature. Roofs are a dominant element, with styles ranging from simple gabled to ornate sweeping curves reserved for temples and palaces.

### Architectural Types and Social Hierarchy
Architecture is strictly regulated by social class. Imperial buildings feature yellow roof tiles, hip roofs, and the exclusive use of the five-clawed dragon motif. Commoner homes follow a set pattern with a central shrine and wings for family members, with size and color legally reflecting the owner's status. Religious architecture varies: Buddhist monasteries often follow the imperial style, while Daoist temples typically follow commoner styles. Unique communal fortresses like the *Tulou* were built by Hakka communities for protection.

### Urban Planning and Cultural Significance
Urban planning is based on *feng shui* and the well-field system, exemplified by cities like Beijing and Chang'an. The home is a microcosm of cosmic and social order, embodying Neo-Confucian values of hierarchy and gender roles. *Feng shui* principles guide orientation and layout to channel cosmic energy (*qi*). The separation of inner (female) and outer (male) spaces reflects and reinforces social norms.

### Regional Variation and Influence
Distinct regional styles exist, such as Hui, Shanxi, Lingnan, and Hakka architecture, adapted to local terrain and climate. Chinese architecture has majorly influenced Japan, Korea, Vietnam, and Mongolia, particularly through the East Asian hip-and-gable roof design. It also influenced parts of Southeast and South Asia, including Thailand, Indonesia, and Sri Lanka.

## Terms
- **Bilateral symmetry**: A design principle where elements are mirrored on either side of a central axis, signifying balance.
- **Feng shui**: A geomantic practice of arranging spaces to harmonize with the flow of cosmic energy (*qi*).
- **Dougong**: An interlocking wooden bracket system used to support roofs and add ornamentation.
- **Siheyuan**: A traditional courtyard house with buildings surrounding an open central space.
- **Sky well (tianjing)**: A small, enclosed courtyard in southern Chinese architecture that provides light and ventilation.
- **Hip roof**: A roof where all four sides slope downwards to the walls, a style reserved for imperial buildings.
- **Rammed earth**: A construction technique using compacted earth, historically important for walls and foundations.
- **Tulou**: A communal, fortified earthen building, often circular, used by Hakka communities.
- **Jian (間)**: The space between two columns in a building; the number of *jian* indicated a building's importance.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The debate from the 1930s-1940s over whether to adapt Western modernism or use traditional forms with modern materials remains a central tension in Chinese architectural practice.
- The "Daqing Spirit" of the 1960s, which promoted rammed earth construction as an ideological ideal of communist society, represents a unique intersection of politics and building practice.
- The 2014 reconstruction of Datong's ancient city has sparked debate between preserving traditional aesthetics and concerns over authenticity and cost.
