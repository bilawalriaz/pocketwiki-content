# Evolution

## Overview

Evolution is the change in heritable characteristics of biological populations over successive generations, driven by natural selection and genetic drift acting on genetic variation. The scientific theory of evolution by natural selection was conceived independently by Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace in the mid-19th century and rests on observable facts: more offspring are produced than can survive, traits vary among individuals, different traits confer different survival and reproduction rates, and traits are heritable. All life on Earth shares a last universal common ancestor (LUCA) that lived approximately 3.5–3.8 billion years ago. Evolution has produced biodiversity at every level of biological organisation, with applications in medicine, agriculture, and computer science.

## Body

### Heredity and sources of variation

Inherited traits are controlled by genes; the complete set of genes in an organism is its genotype, while observable traits (structure and behaviour) form its phenotype. Phenotype arises from genotype interacting with environment—suntans are not inherited, but the ability to tan is. Heritable characteristics pass via DNA, a biopolymer whose base sequence encodes genetic information. Genes are DNA portions specifying functional units; different forms of a gene at a chromosome location (locus) are alleles. Mutations change DNA sequences, producing new alleles that may alter phenotype.

Genetic variation comes from mutations, reshuffling through sexual reproduction, and gene flow (migration between populations). Mutations are the ultimate source of variation; about half of mutations in protein-coding regions are deleterious, half neutral, and a small percentage beneficial. Gene duplication allows new genes to evolve from pre-existing ones—for example, four human eye light-sensing genes descend from a single ancestral gene. Sexual reproduction and homologous recombination (exchange of DNA between matching chromosomes) produce new allele combinations without altering allele frequencies. Gene flow includes horizontal gene transfer, common among bacteria and contributing to antibiotic resistance spread. Epigenetic inheritance systems—heritable changes not explained by DNA sequence—include DNA methylation, RNA interference, and prion conformation.

### Evolutionary forces

Natural selection embodies three principles: variation exists, different traits confer differential fitness, and traits are heritable. More offspring are produced than can survive, creating competition; organisms with advantageous traits pass them on more. Fitness is measured by the proportion of subsequent generations carrying an organism's genes, not total offspring. Selection can be directional (shift in average trait value), disruptive (extreme values favoured), or stabilising (extremes selected against). Natural selection acts at multiple levels—genes, cells, individuals, groups, species—and can be understood through ecosystems, where each population occupies a distinct niche.

Genetic drift is random fluctuation of allele frequencies due to sampling error, halting when alleles become fixed (disappear or replace others). The neutral theory of molecular evolution holds that most evolutionary changes result from fixation of neutral mutations by drift; the nearly neutral theory refines this for population size effects. Effective population size, usually smaller than total population, determines drift rate. Mutation bias—differences in expected rates of mutation types—can influence genome composition, though GC-biased gene conversion and AT-biased mutation in bacteria limit its scope. Genetic hitchhiking occurs when a beneficial allele's selective sweep carries linked neutral alleles to higher frequency. Sexual selection is a special case selecting traits that increase mating success, such as cumbersome antlers or bright colours, despite survival disadvantages.

### Natural outcomes

Adaptation is the process making organisms better suited to their habitat; an adaptive trait is the product. Adaptations arise through gradual modification of existing structures—bat wing bones resemble mouse feet and primate hands from common mammalian ancestry. Deep homology describes common genes controlling structurally different organs. Vestigial structures (pseudogenes, whale hip bones, human wisdom teeth) lost their original function. Exaptations are structures originally adapted for one function that became useful for another, such as the flattened head of the lizard *Holaspis guentheri* aiding gliding.

Coevolution is the cycle of selection and response between interacting species, as with rough-skinned newt tetrodotoxin production and garter snake resistance. Cooperation includes mutualistic plant–mycorrhizal fungi relationships and eusociality in insects, potentially evolving through kin selection (helping relatives carrying shared alleles). Speciation—species diverging into descendant species—occurs through reproductive isolation. Modes include allopatric (geographic isolation), peripatric (small isolated populations with founder effects), parapatric (no physical separation but reduced gene flow), and sympatric (no geographic isolation, rare). Hybridisation can produce new species, especially in plants via polyploidy (chromosome doubling). Punctuated equilibrium links rapid speciation to short fossil-record bursts interspersed with stasis. Extinction is the disappearance of entire species; the Permian–Triassic extinction killed approximately 96% of marine species, while the ongoing Holocene extinction has rates 100–1000 times background, with up to 30% of current species possibly extinct by mid-21st century.

### Applications and evolutionary history

Artificial selection has been used for thousands of years in domestication and now in genetic engineering and directed evolution (repeated mutation and selection for valuable proteins). Evolutionary theory informs medicine—pathogens and cancers evolve resistance to drugs—and computer science, where evolutionary algorithms optimise complex systems. The earliest undisputed life evidence dates from at least 3.5 billion years ago; common descent is deduced from geographic distributions, morphological similarities, vestigial traits, and nested classification. The fossil record and molecular clocks (mutation-based dating) reveal lineages; humans and chimpanzees share 98% of their genomes. Prokaryotes dominated for billions of years; endosymbiosis (bacteria engulfed by eukaryotic cells) produced mitochondria and chloroplasts. The Cambrian explosion (~538.8 million years ago) saw most modern animal types appear.

### History of evolutionary thought

Evolutionary ideas trace to pre-Socratic philosophers Anaximander and Empedocles, and later Lucretius. Medieval Aristotelianism viewed species as fixed forms in a divine order, though Ibn Khaldun (1377) asserted humans developed from "the world of the monkeys." The Scientific Revolution rejected fixed categories; Linnaeus (1735) classified species hierarchically but viewed them as divinely fixed. Lamarck's 1809 transmutation theory proposed progressive complexity and inheritance of acquired characteristics. Darwin, influenced by Malthus, developed natural selection from 1838; Wallace independently conceived the same theory in 1858. Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) led to wide acceptance. Marsh's fossil discoveries of horse ancestors and toothed birds provided strong support. Mendel's 1865 inheritance laws and Weismann's germ/soma distinction clarified heredity; the modern synthesis (1920s–1930s) unified natural selection, population genetics, and Mendelian inheritance. Further syntheses incorporated DNA structure (1953), molecular biology, and evolutionary developmental biology ("evo-devo").

## Terms

- **Allele**: Different form of a gene at a specific chromosome location (locus).
- **Genotype**: The complete set of genes within an organism's genome.
- **Phenotype**: The complete set of observable traits making up an organism's structure and behaviour.
- **Natural selection**: Process by which traits enhancing survival and reproduction become more common in successive generations.
- **Genetic drift**: Random fluctuation of allele frequencies within a population from one generation to the next.
- **Gene flow**: Exchange of genes between populations and species, including horizontal gene transfer.
- **Speciation**: Process where a species diverges into two or more descendant species.
- **Adaptation**: Process making organisms better suited to their habitat, or the trait resulting from this process.
- **Exaptation**: Structure originally adapted for one function that became useful for another.
- **Modern synthesis**: 1920s–1930s unification of natural selection, population genetics, and Mendelian inheritance.

## Debates and open questions

The relative importance of selection versus neutral processes (including drift) in driving evolutionary change is an area of current research. The neutral theory of molecular evolution has been debated since it does not fit some observed genetic variation; the nearly neutral theory offers a better-supported version. Whether mutations implicated in adaptation reflect common mutation biases is disputed. The role of extinction in evolution is not well understood and may depend on extinction type. The "species problem" concerns disagreement over species definitions—the Biological Species Concept faces challenges because genetic recombination among prokaryotes is not intrinsic to reproduction. Some researchers seek a unifying monistic species definition while others adopt pluralistic approaches. The importance of hybridisation in producing new animal species remains unclear.
