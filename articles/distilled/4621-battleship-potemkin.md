# Battleship Potemkin

## Overview
*Battleship Potemkin* is a 1925 Soviet silent film directed by Sergei Eisenstein. It dramatizes the 1905 mutiny on the Russian battleship *Potemkin* and is a foundational work of Soviet montage theory, a style of editing that creates meaning through the juxtaposition of shots. The film is celebrated for its technical innovation, particularly the "Odessa Steps" sequence, and is widely considered one of the greatest and most influential films ever made.

## Timeline
- **1905** — The historical mutiny on the battleship *Potemkin* occurs.
- **1925** — The film is commissioned for the 20th anniversary of the 1905 Revolution.
- **31 March 1925** — Filming begins in Leningrad.
- **21 December 1925** — First screening at the Bolshoi Theatre.
- **18 January 1926** — Moscow premiere.
- **1926** — International release, including a successful run in Berlin.
- **1930** — A version with added sound (voice dubbing) is released.
- **1950** — The film is restored with a new score by Nikolai Kryukov.
- **1958** — Voted the greatest film of all time at the Brussels World's Fair.
- **1976** — Reissued with a new score by Dmitri Shostakovich.
- **2004-2005** — A major restoration is completed, aiming to return to Eisenstein's original version.
- **2025** — A restored version with a score by Pet Shop Boys is released for the film's centenary.

## Body

### Production and Original Script
The film was commissioned by the Soviet government to commemorate the 20th anniversary of the 1905 Revolution. The original script by Nina Agadzhanova covered multiple revolutionary episodes. Director Sergei Eisenstein, facing time and weather constraints, abandoned this broad script to focus on a single episode: the mutiny on the *Potemkin*. He and co-writer Grigori Aleksandrov essentially created a new film from a few pages of the original script. Filming took place in Odessa, using non-professional actors and real locations, including the derelict battleship *Rostislav* for exterior shots and the cruiser *Komintern* for interiors.

### Film Style and Content
Eisenstein used the film to test his theories of montage, editing shots to produce a strong emotional and intellectual response in the viewer. The film is structured in five episodes, from the sailors' protest over maggot-infested meat to their final confrontation with the Tsarist fleet. The characterization is deliberately simple to clearly align audience sympathy with the revolutionary sailors. A famous montage example is the sequence of lion statues edited to appear to awaken, symbolizing revolutionary consciousness.

### The Odessa Steps Sequence
This scene, depicting Tsarist soldiers massacring unarmed civilians on a long flight of steps, is one of the most famous in cinema history. While based on real reports of troops firing on rioters in Odessa, the specific event as portrayed did not happen. The sequence is a masterclass in montage, using rhythmic editing to create a sense of relentless, machine-like violence. Its imagery, such as a baby carriage rolling down the steps, has been widely referenced and parodied in other works of art.

### Distribution, Censorship, and Legacy
The film initially struggled for distribution in the USSR until poet Vladimir Mayakovsky intervened. It became a huge international success, particularly in Germany. However, it was also banned in several countries, including the United Kingdom, the United States, and France, for its revolutionary content. Even in the Soviet Union, it was censored; a prologue with text by Leon Trotsky was later replaced with a quote from Lenin. The film's legacy is immense, influencing filmmakers from Orson Welles to Brian De Palma. It has been consistently ranked among the greatest films in polls by *Sight & Sound* and other publications.

## Terms
- **Soviet Montage Theory**: A filmmaking theory that argues meaning is created not within a single shot, but through the conflict or juxtaposition of different shots in editing.
- **Orthochromatic film stock**: Early black-and-white film that was sensitive to blue and green light but not red, causing red objects to appear black on screen.
- **Constructivism**: An artistic movement that originated in Russia, emphasizing geometric forms and industrial materials, seen in the film's posters by Aleksandr Rodchenko.
- **Intertitles**: Text cards inserted into silent films to convey dialogue or narrative information.
- **Cossacks**: Members of a group of predominantly East Slavic Orthodox Christian people who became known as members of democratic, self-governing, semi-military communities.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historical Accuracy**: The film's most famous scene, the Odessa Steps massacre, is a dramatic fabrication, though it was inspired by real, widespread violence in Odessa. This raises questions about the relationship between historical fact and cinematic myth-making.
- **Propaganda vs. Art**: Eisenstein intended the film as revolutionary propaganda, but its enduring status is based on its artistic and technical innovation. Critics debate whether its power derives more from its political message or its formal brilliance.
- **Authorial Intent vs. Censorship**: The film has been altered multiple times for political and commercial reasons, from Soviet censorship of Trotsky's words to international edits of violent scenes. The 2005 restoration aimed to recover Eisenstein's original vision, but the "definitive" version remains a subject of scholarly discussion.
