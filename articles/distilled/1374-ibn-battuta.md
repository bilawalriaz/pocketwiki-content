# Ibn Battuta

## Overview

Ibn Battuta (24 February 1304 – 1368/1369) was a Maghrebi Muslim traveller, explorer, and scholar from Tangier (present-day Morocco). Over 30 years (1325–1354), he visited much of Africa, Asia, and the Iberian Peninsula, travelling roughly 117,000 km (73,000 mi)—more than any other pre-modern explorer, surpassing Zheng He (c. 50,000 km) and Marco Polo (c. 24,000 km). Near the end of his life, he dictated an account of his journeys, *The Rihla*, which remains the primary—and often only—source for his adventures, offering a crucial window into the 14th-century Muslim world.

## Timeline

- **24 February 1304** — Born in Tangier into a family of Islamic legal scholars (qadis) of the Berber Lawata tribe.
- **14 June 1325** — Departs Tangier at age 21 for the hajj (pilgrimage) to Mecca; does not return for 24 years.
- **November 1326** — Completes first hajj in Mecca; decides to continue travelling rather than return home.
- **1326–1330** — Travels through Iraq, Iran, Arabia, Somalia, and the Swahili coast; visits Kilwa.
- **1330/1332** — Third hajj; sets off for Anatolia, intending to reach India and seek employment with the Sultan of Delhi.
- **1332/1334** — Visits Constantinople with the retinue of Princess Bayalun; meets Byzantine Emperor Andronikos III.
- **12 September 1333** — Reaches the Indus River; proceeds to Delhi, where Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq appoints him qadi (judge).
- **1341** — Leaves Delhi in charge of an embassy to China; attacked by bandits en route; detours to the Maldives, serving as chief judge for nine months.
- **1345** — Meets Shah Jalal in Sylhet (Bangladesh); travels to Sumatra and then to China (Quanzhou).
- **1348** — Returns to Damascus; learns his father died 15 years earlier; witnesses the Black Death's devastation across Syria, Palestine, and Egypt.
- **1349** — Returns to Tangier; discovers his mother died a few months before.
- **1351–1354** — Travels through Morocco, al-Andalus (Spain), and across the Sahara to the Mali Empire, visiting Timbuktu and Gao.
- **1354–1355** — Dictates *The Rihla* to Ibn Juzayy at the suggestion of the Marinid ruler Abu Inan Faris; dies in 1368 or 1369.

## Body

### Early life and first pilgrimage

All known details of Ibn Battuta's life come from *The Rihla*. He was of Arabised Berber descent, born into a family of qadis (Islamic legal scholars) in Tangier during the Marinid dynasty's reign. He would have studied at a Sunni Maliki school, the dominant education in North Africa at the time. In 1325, he set off for Mecca, travelling overland along the North African coast through Tlemcen, Béjaïa, and Tunis, usually joining caravans for safety. In Alexandria, two ascetic holy men prophesied he would become a world traveller. He chose the least-travelled route to Mecca via the Nile valley, but a local rebellion forced him back to Cairo; he then took a second detour to Damascus, where a holy man had prophesied he would reach Mecca only via Syria. He completed his first hajj in November 1326.

### Iraq, Iran, Arabia, and East Africa

After Mecca, Ibn Battuta joined a caravan to Iraq, visiting Najaf (mausoleum of Ali, the Fourth Caliph), then detouring into Iran—Wasit, Basra, Isfahan, and Shiraz—before returning to Baghdad in June 1327, where parts of the city still lay ruined from Hulagu Khan's 1258 invasion. He travelled north on the Silk Road to Tabriz, then back to Baghdad and Mosul, joining a pilgrim caravan for his second hajj. He remained in Mecca for roughly three years (c. 1327–1330), then sailed down the Red Sea to Yemen, visiting Zabīd, Ta'izz, and Aden. He visited Zeila and Mogadishu in Somalia, describing the latter as "an exceedingly large city" ruled by Sultan Abu Bakr ibn Shaikh 'Umar. He continued to the Swahili coast, visiting Mombasa and Kilwa (in present-day Tanzania), a key gold-trade centre, whose Sultan al-Hasan ibn Sulaiman he praised.

### Anatolia, Central Asia, and Constantinople

Seeking employment with the Sultan of Delhi, Ibn Battuta crossed Anatolia, where he was impressed by the hospitality of the *fityan* associations (semi-religious guilds of young artisans led by an *Akhil*). His itinerary in Anatolia is confused; historians believe he visited towns in a different order than described. He met Orhan, sultan of the Ottoman Beylik, describing him as "the greatest of the kings of the Turkmens." From Sinope, he sailed to the Crimean Peninsula, meeting Uzbeg Khan's travelling court. He reached Bolghar, his northernmost point, and considered travelling to the "land of darkness" (northern Siberia), where silent trade in furs occurred, but abandoned the idea. He joined Princess Bayalun's expedition to Constantinople, meeting Emperor Andronikos III and visiting Hagia Sophia. He then travelled through Bukhara and Samarkand, crossed the Hindu Kush (which he noted means "Hindu-slayer" because slaves died there from cold), and reached the Indus River on 12 September 1333.

### South Asia, Maldives, and Southeast Asia

In Delhi, Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq appointed Ibn Battuta a qadi, but he struggled to enforce Islamic law beyond the court. He witnessed a sati ceremony (ritual burning of a widow on her husband's funeral pyre). After six years of fluctuating favour, he left in 1341 in charge of an embassy to China, but was robbed and kidnapped by bandits en route. He detoured to the Maldives, where he was compelled to stay nine months as chief judge, enforcing strict punishments (public whipping for missing Friday prayer, amputation for robbers) that antagonised the rulers. He then visited Sri Lanka, survived a shipwreck and pirate attack, and returned to the Maldives before sailing to Chittagong and meeting the renowned Sufi Shah Jalal in Sylhet. In 1345, he reached the Samudra Pasai Sultanate in Sumatra, noting it marked the end of *Dar al-Islam* (Muslin-ruled territory). He then sailed to China, stopping at "Mul Jawa" (possibly Majapahit Java) and "Kaylukari" in "Tawalisi," where he met Princess Urduja—a location and figure of disputed historicity, possibly fictitious.

### China and return

In 1345, Ibn Battuta arrived in Quanzhou, praising local craftsmen, silk, porcelain, and paper money, but scholars note numerous errors in his account of China (e.g., confusing the Yellow River with the Grand Canal). He travelled to Guangzhou, Fuzhou, Hangzhou (which he called one of the largest cities he had seen), and Beijing, where he presented himself as the ambassador from Delhi. He noted the Great Wall was said to be sixty days' travel away, but found no one who had seen it. He returned to Quanzhou and sailed for Southeast Asia, losing much of his wealth to the crew. He returned overland, learning of the Ilkhanate's collapse and the Black Death's devastation (Cairo reportedly saw 1,100 deaths daily). He returned to Tangier in 1349, discovering his mother had died months earlier.

### Spain, North Africa, and Mali

In 1350, Ibn Battuta joined a group defending Gibraltar from Alfonso XI of Castile, but the king died of plague, so he toured al-Andalus, ending in Granada. In 1351, he crossed the Sahara to the Mali Empire, passing through the salt-mining town of Taghaza (buildings made of salt slabs). He met Mansa Suleyman, king since 1341, but disapproved of local dress customs and wrote critically of black Africans' "ill manners" and "feeble intellect." He recorded the earliest mention of the Mande xylophone (bala). He visited Timbuktu (then a small, relatively unimportant town) and Gao, and encountered a hippopotamus for the first time. He returned to Morocco in early 1354, accompanying a caravan transporting 600 female slaves.

### The Rihla and its historicity

In 1354, Ibn Battuta dictated his account to Ibn Juzayy, a scholar he had met in Granada. The full title translates as *A Masterpiece to Those Who Contemplate the Wonders of Cities and the Marvels of Travelling*, but it is commonly known as *The Rihla* (The Travels). Ibn Juzayy did not acknowledge his sources, copying passages from earlier travellers like Ibn Jubayr and Muhammad al-Abdari. Modern scholars doubt Ibn Battuta visited all the places he described, suspecting hearsay and plagiarism (e.g., his trip to Bolghar, Sanaa, and parts of China). The work was unknown outside the Muslim world until the early 19th century, when manuscripts were acquired and translated into English (Samuel Lee, 1829), French (Defrémery and Sanguinetti, 1853–1858), and later English (Hamilton Gibb, 1958–1994). In 2010, Tim Mackintosh-Smith reported finding manuscripts of Islamic law copied by Ibn Battuta in Damascus in 1326, corroborating the *Rihla*'s dating of his Syrian sojourn.

## Terms

- **Hajj**: The Islamic pilgrimage to Mecca, one of the Five Pillars of Islam.
- **Qadi**: An Islamic judge; Ibn Battuta's family profession and his own role in Delhi and the Maldives.
- **Rihla**: Literally "journey" or "travel"; the standard Arabic literary genre of travel accounts, and the common name for Ibn Battuta's book.
- **Fityan/Akhil**: Semi-religious associations of young artisans in Anatolia, led by a leader titled *Akhil*, who specialised in welcoming travellers.
- **Sati**: The ritual burning of a widow on her husband's funeral pyre, witnessed by Ibn Battuta in India.
- **Dar al-Islam**: Lands ruled by Muslims; Ibn Battuta noted Sumatra marked its eastern limit.
- **Mansa**: Title of the ruler of the Mali Empire (e.g., Mansa Suleyman).
- **Ilkhanate**: A Mongol Khanate ruling parts of Persia and the Middle East, which Ibn Battuta visited in the 1320s–1330s.
- **Zanj**: The medieval Arabic term for the Swahili coast region (Bilad al-Zanj, "Land of the Zanj").
- **Black Death**: The bubonic plague pandemic that struck the Middle East in 1348, which Ibn Battuta witnessed and described.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars dispute whether Ibn Battuta visited all the places he described. German scholar Ralph Elger views the *Rihla* as an important literary work but suspects much of its content is fiction compiled from other contemporary travel reports. Specific doubts include his trip up the Volga to Bolghar, his visit to Sanaa in Yemen, his journey from Balkh to Bistam, his route across Anatolia, and whether he actually visited China (sections may be plagiarised from earlier authors). His claim that a Maghrebi converted the Maldives to Islam is contradicted by the official Maldivian history, which credits a Tabrizi named Maulana Shaikh Yusuf Shams-ud-din. The locations of "Kaylukari" and "Tawalisi" (and Princess Urduja) are disputed, with some scholars (Sir Henry Yule, William Henry Scott) considering them entirely fictitious. However, Tim Mackintosh-Smith's 2010 field study found manuscripts corroborating the *Rihla*'s dating of Ibn Battuta's stay in Damascus, lending some support to its accuracy.
