# Acid

## Overview

An acid is a molecule or ion capable of either donating a proton (H⁺), known as a Brønsted–Lowry acid, or forming a covalent bond with an electron pair, known as a Lewis acid. In aqueous solutions, proton donors form hydronium ions (H₃O⁺) and are called Arrhenius acids. Acids are fundamental to chemistry and industry: they give sour tastes, turn blue litmus red, react with bases and certain metals to form salts, and serve as reagents in nearly all modern industrial processes, from fertilizer production to drug formulation.

## Timeline

- **1884** — Svante Arrhenius attributes acidity to hydrogen cations (H⁺) in water
- **1923** — Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry independently define acids as proton donors
- **1923** — Gilbert N. Lewis proposes acids as electron-pair acceptors
- **2011** — Annual world production of sulfuric acid reaches ~200 million tonnes

## Body

### Definitions and concepts

Modern definitions focus on the fundamental chemical reactions common to all acids. Most everyday acids are aqueous solutions, so the Arrhenius and Brønsted–Lowry definitions are most relevant. The Brønsted–Lowry definition is the most widely used; unless otherwise specified, acid–base reactions involve proton transfer from an acid to a base. Hydronium ions are acids under all three definitions. Alcohols and amines can be Brønsted–Lowry acids but also function as Lewis bases due to lone electron pairs on their oxygen and nitrogen atoms.

### Arrhenius acids

An Arrhenius acid increases the concentration of H⁺ ions when added to water. The free hydrogen nucleus (a proton) does not exist alone in water; it exists as hydronium (H₃O⁺) or other forms (H₅O₂⁺, H₉O₄⁺). An Arrhenius base increases hydroxide (OH⁻) concentration; hydronium and hydroxide react to form water, so increasing hydronium decreases hydroxide. In acidic solutions, hydronium concentration exceeds 10⁻⁷ moles per liter, giving a pH below 7.

### Brønsted–Lowry acids

Brønsted and Lowry generalized the Arrhenius theory to include non-aqueous solvents. A Brønsted–Lowry acid donates a proton to a base. This theory handles reactions the Arrhenius definition cannot: acetic acid donates a proton to ammonia without producing hydronium, and hydrogen chloride reacts with ammonia in benzene or as gases to form ammonium chloride without forming ions. Both are proton-transfer reactions that the Arrhenius definition fails to describe.

### Lewis acids

A Lewis acid accepts an electron pair from another species. For example, boron trifluoride (BF₃) accepts a lone pair from fluoride ion (F⁻) to form tetrafluoroborate. Many Lewis acids are not Brønsted–Lowry acids, and most Brønsted acids (like hydrogen chloride and acetic acid) cannot form covalent bonds with electron pairs and are not Lewis acids. In modern terminology, "acid" implicitly means Brønsted acid; Lewis acids are usually specified explicitly. Depending on context, a Lewis acid may also be called an oxidizer or electrophile.

### Dissociation and equilibrium

Acid reactions generalize as HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, where HA is the acid and A⁻ is the conjugate base; this is called protolysis. Acid–base conjugate pairs differ by one proton. The acid dissociation constant Kₐ equals the product of product concentrations divided by the reactant concentration. Stronger acids have higher Kₐ; because Kₐ spans many orders of magnitude, pKₐ = −log₁₀Kₐ is more commonly used, with stronger acids having smaller pKₐ values.

### Acid strength

A strong acid completely dissociates in water; a weak acid only partially dissociates. Examples of strong acids include hydrochloric, hydroiodic, hydrobromic, perchloric, nitric, and sulfuric acids. Key factors in deprotonation ease are the polarity of the H–A bond and the size of atom A. Sulfonic acids are a class of strong organic acids. Superacids are stronger than 100% sulfuric acid; examples include fluoroantimonic acid and magic acid. The strongest known acid is the helium hydride ion, with a proton affinity of 177.8 kJ/mol. While Kₐ measures an acid compound's strength, pH measures the strength of an aqueous acid solution.

### Chemical characteristics

Monoprotic acids donate one proton per molecule; examples include hydrochloric acid and acetic acid. Polyprotic acids donate more than one proton: diprotic acids (like sulfuric acid) have two dissociation constants (Kₐ₁ > Kₐ₂), and triprotic acids (like phosphoric acid and citric acid) have three (Kₐ₁ > Kₐ₂ > Kₐ₃). Successive Kₐ values differ because losing a proton becomes energetically less favorable as the conjugate base becomes more negatively charged. Fractional concentrations (α) of each species can be calculated from pH and K values; a plot of these against pH is a Bjerrum plot.

Neutralization is the reaction between an acid and a base producing a salt and water. It is the basis of titration, where a pH indicator shows the equivalence point. Neutralization does not always yield pH 7.0—only with similar acid and base strengths. Weak acid–weak base combinations produce weakly acidic or weakly basic salts. Solutions of weak acids and their conjugate-base salts form buffer solutions.

### Titration

Acid–base titration determines acid concentration by adding a strong base of known concentration until neutralization, indicated by a color change. The titration curve plots base volume against pH; pH always rises as base is added. For diprotic acids, there are two equivalence points and two midpoints where pH = pKₐ, with buffer regions between them.

### Applications

Sulfuric acid is the most-produced industrial chemical worldwide, used in fertilizers, detergents, batteries, and dyes. Acids react in neutralization to produce salts (e.g., ammonium nitrate fertilizer), remove rust via pickling, and serve as electrolytes in batteries. In food, citric acid occurs in citrus fruits, oxalic acid in tomatoes and rhubarb (toxic in high concentrations in rhubarb leaves and unripe carambolas), and ascorbic acid (Vitamin C) is essential for humans. Carbonic acid, added to soft drinks, decomposes into water and CO₂, causing fizz when opened. Acetylsalicylic acid (Aspirin) is used as a painkiller. In the body, hydrochloric acid aids digestion, amino acids build proteins, and carbonic acid maintains pH equilibrium. Acids catalyze industrial reactions, such as sulfuric acid in gasoline alkylation.

### Biological occurrence

Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) contain acidic phosphate groups and carry the genetic code. Amino acids have a central carbon bonded to a carboxyl group, an amino group, a hydrogen, and a variable R group that determines identity. At physiological pH (~7), free amino acids exist as zwitterions with net neutral charge, except those with acidic or basic side chains. Fatty acids form phospholipid bilayers in cell membranes. Hydrochloric acid in gastric juice hydrolyzes proteins and converts pepsinogen to pepsin; ants produce formic acid for defense. Acid–base equilibrium regulates breathing: CO₂ in blood forms carbonic acid, and the resulting pH decrease signals the brain to breathe faster. Many drugs, including ibuprofen, aspirin, and penicillin, are weak acids that cross cell membranes in their uncharged form.

## Terms

- **Brønsted–Lowry acid**: A species that donates a proton (H⁺) to a base
- **Arrhenius acid**: A substance that increases hydronium ion concentration when added to water
- **Lewis acid**: A species that accepts an electron pair from another species
- **Conjugate base**: The species formed when an acid loses a proton
- **Kₐ (acid dissociation constant)**: Equilibrium constant for acid dissociation; larger values mean stronger acids
- **pKₐ**: −log₁₀Kₐ; smaller values mean stronger acids
- **pH**: Negative logarithm of hydronium ion concentration; below 7 indicates acidity
- **Buffer solution**: A solution of a weak acid and its conjugate-base salt that resists pH changes
- **Zwitterion**: A molecule with net neutral charge carrying both positive and negative charges
- **Superacid**: An acid stronger than 100% sulfuric acid

## Debates and open questions

The Lewis definition is only marginally related to the Brønsted concept: many Lewis acids are not Brønsted acids, and most Brønsted acids are not Lewis acids. There is no single order of Lewis acid strengths—the relative acceptor strength depends on the base, requiring at least two properties (hardness and strength in Pearson's HSAB theory; electrostatic and covalent in Drago's ECW model) to define.
