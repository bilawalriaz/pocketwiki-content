# History of slavery

## Overview
Slavery is the practice of owning people as property, forcing them to work without pay. It has existed across nearly all human cultures and eras, from ancient civilizations to the modern day, with victims coming from diverse ethnic and religious groups. Its forms, legal status, and economic roles have varied dramatically, but it has consistently been a system of exploitation and social control, shaping economies, societies, and laws worldwide.

## Timeline
- **c. 10,000 BC** — Neolithic Revolution begins, creating conditions for mass chattel slavery.
- **c. 5400 BC** — Slavery institutionalized in early Mesopotamian civilizations.
- **c. 1750 BC** — Slavery referenced in the Mesopotamian Code of Hammurabi.
- **16th century** — European merchants, led by Portugal, initiate the transatlantic slave trade.
- **1794** — Slavery abolished in the French First Republic and its colonies.
- **1804** — Haiti becomes an independent republic after a successful slave rebellion.
- **1807** — Britain passes the Slave Trade Act, criminalizing the international slave trade within its empire.
- **1833** — Britain passes the Slavery Abolition Act, ending slavery in most of its empire.
- **1865** — Slavery legally abolished in the United States with the 13th Amendment.
- **1888** — Brazil abolishes slavery, the last nation in the Western Hemisphere to do so.
- **1926** — League of Nations adopts the Slavery Convention, a turning point in banning global slavery.
- **2019** — An estimated 40.3 million people worldwide are subject to some form of modern slavery.

## Body

### Origins and Ancient Practice
Slavery predates written records, emerging in hunter-gatherer societies but expanding significantly after the Neolithic Revolution (c. 10,000 BC) due to agricultural surpluses and population density. It was institutionalized in early civilizations like Sumer and Babylonia by c. 5400 BC. Ancient slavery was a mix of debt bondage, punishment for crimes, prisoners of war, and children born to slaves. It was widespread across Europe, Asia, Africa, and the Americas.

### The Atlantic Slave Trade and Plantation Economies
Beginning in the 16th century, European merchants, starting with the Portuguese, initiated the transatlantic slave trade. They purchased enslaved Africans from West African kingdoms, transporting them to colonies in the Americas. This trade was part of a triangular system: European goods went to Africa, enslaved people to the Americas, and plantation products like sugar and cotton back to Europe. The trade peaked in the late 18th century, with an estimated 12 to 20 million Africans shipped across the Atlantic, about 15% dying during the voyage. The system was driven by the demand for labor in plantation economies, particularly for sugar in the Caribbean and Brazil, and cotton in the United States.

### African Participation and the Trade's Impact
African states and elites played a key role in the slave trade, capturing and selling people from rival groups for European goods like guns and cloth. This participation was often driven by existing local systems of slavery and warfare. The trade profoundly disrupted African societies, fueling conflicts and undermining local economies. In regions like Senegambia and the Sokoto Caliphate, enslaved people made up a third to half the population by the 19th century.

### Abolition Movements and Legal End
Abolitionism grew in the 18th and 19th centuries, driven by moral arguments and slave resistance. Britain criminalized the international slave trade in 1807 and abolished slavery in its empire in 1833. The United States ended slavery in 1865 after the Civil War. Brazil was the last Western Hemisphere nation to abolish it in 1888. The Haitian Revolution (1791-1804) was the only successful large-scale slave rebellion, leading to the creation of an independent black republic. International efforts, like the 1926 Slavery Convention, continued the push for global abolition.

### Modern Slavery and Human Trafficking
Slavery persists in the 21st century through human trafficking, forced labor, and forced marriages. An estimated 40.3 million people were enslaved in 2019, generating $150 billion in annual profits. Forms include domestic servitude, forced labor in manufacturing and mining, and sexual slavery. Armed conflict and modern transportation have made trafficking easier. Regions like Sudan, Mauritania, and Niger report ongoing practices, and the Islamic State has enslaved Yazidi and Christian women.

## Terms
- **Chattel slavery**: A system where enslaved people are treated as personal property, bought, sold, and inherited.
- **Middle Passage**: The horrific sea voyage from Africa to the Americas, where enslaved Africans were transported in brutal conditions.
- **Triangular trade**: A 16th-19th century trade system linking Europe, Africa, and the Americas in a cycle of goods, enslaved people, and raw materials.
- **Maroon communities**: Settlements formed by escaped enslaved people, often in remote areas, who resisted recapture.
- **Abolitionism**: A movement to end slavery and the slave trade, prominent in the 18th and 19th centuries.
- **Forced labor**: Work or service extracted under threat of punishment, a key feature of modern slavery.
- **Indentured servitude**: A labor system where people work for a set period to pay off a debt, distinct from lifelong chattel slavery.
- **Helotry**: A form of serfdom in ancient Sparta where an entire population was enslaved to the state.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Economic impact of slavery**: Historians debate whether profits from the slave trade and plantations significantly financed the Industrial Revolution in Britain. Some argue it was a minor factor, while others contend it was foundational.
- **African agency in the trade**: Scholars discuss the extent to which African elites were active participants versus victims of the trade, and how the trade altered pre-existing African slavery systems.
- **Causes of abolition**: Was the end of slavery driven primarily by moral and humanitarian concerns, or by economic shifts that made it less profitable?
- **Global prevalence today**: The exact scale and nature of modern slavery are debated, with estimates varying and new forms like cyber-slavery emerging.
