# Architectural theory

## Overview
Architectural theory is the practice of thinking, discussing, and writing about architecture, encompassing its design, purpose, and cultural role. It is a foundational discipline taught in architecture schools and practiced by leading architects, evolving from ancient treatises to contemporary philosophical and digital explorations. Its history reflects shifts in technology, culture, and intellectual thought, influencing architectural styles and movements across centuries.

## Timeline
- **c. 27–23 BC** — Vitruvius writes *De architectura*, the only surviving major classical treatise on architecture.
- **c. 6th century CE** — Varahamihira's *Brihat Samhita* includes early Indian architectural principles (Vastu Shastra).
- **1103** — Li Jie publishes *Yingzao Fashi*, codifying Chinese architectural elements in Song dynasty China.
- **1414** — Rediscovery of Vitruvius's work profoundly influences Renaissance architects.
- **15th century** — Leon Battista Alberti writes *De re aedificatoria*, establishing Renaissance architectural theory.
- **1889** — Camillo Sitte publishes *City Planning According to Artistic Principles*, critiquing 19th-century urbanism.
- **1896** — Louis Sullivan's essay "The Tall Office Building Artistically Considered" introduces "form ever follows function."
- **1908** — Adolf Loos writes *Ornament and Crime*, demanding the elimination of ornament.
- **1920s–1930s** — Walter Gropius, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, and Le Corbusier develop theory for the International Style.
- **1970s onward** — Postmodern architecture reacts against the austerity of High Modern principles.
- **2000s onward** — Rise of "Digital" Architecture, including Biomimicry and Parametricism.
- **2010s** — Emergence of architectural theory based on social reproduction theory and care ethics.

## Body

### Antiquity and Medieval Transmission
Architectural theory in antiquity is best documented by Vitruvius's *De architectura*, which defined architecture through the triad of *firmitas* (firmness), *utilitas* (commodity), and *venustas* (delight). Its rediscovery in 1414 provided a scientific foundation for the Renaissance. Parallel traditions existed, such as India's Vastu Shastra texts, which integrated architecture with nature and geometry. During the Middle Ages, architectural knowledge was primarily transmitted orally or through master builders' lodges, with few written theories surviving, though exceptions include Li Jie's *Yingzao Fashi* in China and Villard de Honnecourt's portfolio in Europe.

### Renaissance to Enlightenment
The Renaissance saw a revival of classical theory, with Alberti's *De re aedificatoria* placing Vitruvian principles at the core of modern architecture. This classical foundation evolved through the Enlightenment, driven by new archaeological discoveries like Pompeii. Theorists such as Johann Joachim Winckelmann promoted Neoclassicism, while others like Julien-David Le Roy and Abbé Marc-Antoine Laugier contributed to a renewed interest in classical forms and primitive architectural ideals.

### 19th-Century Reactions and Foundations
The 19th century witnessed a reaction against Neoclassicism. Augustus Pugin and John Ruskin provided a moral basis for Gothic Revival architecture, while Horatio Greenough's functionalist ideas anticipated modernism. The Arts and Crafts movement, led by William Morris, emphasized craftsmanship and influenced Art Nouveau. On the continent, theorists like Eugène Viollet-le-Duc and Gottfried Semper spurred innovation, with Semper's concept of Realismus attracting an international following.

### 20th-Century Modernism and Postmodernism
The 20th century was dominated by the Modern Movement. Louis Sullivan's "form follows function" and Adolf Loos's rejection of ornament became central tenets. The International Style, theorized by Gropius, Mies van der Rohe, and Le Corbusier, aimed to use industrialized architecture to reshape society. Frank Lloyd Wright, while modern, offered a personal, nature-inspired alternative. Postmodernism later reacted against the perceived austerity of these modernist principles, seeking greater historical reference and complexity.

### Contemporary Theory
Contemporary architectural theory is pluralistic, engaging deeply with philosophy, cultural studies, and technology. Theorists like Peter Eisenman and Bernard Tschumi engage with post-structuralist thought, while others explore phenomenology, feminism, and digital methodologies. Since 2000, theory has addressed globalization and urbanism, with figures like Rem Koolhaas examining "Bigness." Digital trends include Biomimicry (emulating nature) and Parametricism (using computational algorithms). Recent frameworks incorporate social reproduction theory and care ethics, reflecting a broadening of architectural discourse.

## Terms
- **Vitruvian triad**: The three principles of architecture defined by Vitruvius: *firmitas* (structural adequacy), *utilitas* (functional adequacy), and *venustas* (beauty).
- **Vastu Shastra**: Ancient Indian texts describing principles of design, layout, and spatial geometry to integrate architecture with nature.
- **Neoclassicism**: An 18th-century architectural style inspired by renewed interest in classical Greek and Roman art and architecture.
- **International Style**: A mid-20th-century architectural movement characterized by rectilinear forms, light, taut plane surfaces, and open interior spaces.
- **Form follows function**: A principle stating that the shape of a building should be primarily based upon its intended purpose or function.
- **Parametricism**: A contemporary architectural style that uses computational design tools and algorithms to create complex, fluid forms.
- **Biomimicry**: The design methodology that seeks sustainable solutions by emulating nature's time-tested patterns and strategies.
- **Deconstructivism**: An architectural movement that encourages fragmentation and the manipulation of a structure's surface or skin.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The enduring relevance and interpretation of Vitruvian principles in modern and contemporary practice.
- The tension between universalizing modernist ideals (like the International Style) and contextual, regional, or historical approaches.
- The role and impact of digital technologies and algorithms on architectural creativity and theory.
- How emerging frameworks, such as care ethics and social reproduction theory, will reshape architectural priorities and urban design.
