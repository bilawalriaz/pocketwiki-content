# Classical period (music)

## Overview
The Classical period in Western art music, spanning roughly 1750 to 1820, represents a stylistic shift from the complex polyphony of the Baroque era to a clearer, more orderly aesthetic. It established foundational forms like the symphony and string quartet, and its emphasis on balance, structure, and homophonic texture (a clear melody over accompaniment) became the bedrock for later Western music.

## Timeline
- **c. 1750–1760** — Transition from Baroque style; composers experiment with simpler textures and dramatic expression.
- **1761** — Joseph Haydn composes his first symphonies in the new style.
- **1772** — Haydn's Opus 20 string quartets integrate Baroque polyphony with Classical style.
- **1781** — Haydn's Opus 33 string quartets establish a new, integrated ensemble style.
- **1781** — Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart arrives in Vienna, accelerating the style's development.
- **1780s** — Public taste solidifies around the standards of Haydn and Mozart; piano replaces harpsichord.
- **1790s** — A new generation of composers (Beethoven, Hummel) emerges, building on Classical foundations.
- **1805** — Ludwig van Beethoven's opera *Fidelio* premieres.
- **1808** — Beethoven's Symphony No. 5 and Symphony No. 6 premiere.
- **c. 1820** — The period concludes with the deaths of Beethoven (1827) and Schubert (1828), marking the style's eclipse.

## Body

### Style and Characteristics
The Classical style favored homophony—a clear melody over chordal accompaniment—over the dense polyphony of the Baroque. It embraced the *style galant*, emphasizing light elegance and clarity. Musical forms became more structured, with well-defined contrasts between keys and the development of sonata form, a structural principle reconciling melodic material with harmonic development. Orchestras grew in size and standardized, with the harpsichord's basso continuo role fading by 1775. The piano (fortepiano) replaced the harpsichord, allowing for dynamic expression (loud and soft).

### Historical Development
The period began with a transition (c. 1750–1775) where composers sought more dramatic expression than the Baroque's "doctrine of affections." Styles like *Empfindsamkeit* (sensitive style) used abrupt changes, which Classical masters like Haydn later made logically motivated. Haydn, working from the late 1750s, became the "father of the symphony" and string quartet, integrating Baroque techniques into new forms. Mozart, arriving in Vienna in 1781, applied this style to opera and the virtuoso concerto, merging Italian brilliance with Germanic cohesion.

By the 1790s, a new generation led by Beethoven emerged. Beethoven's works, like his *Eroica* Symphony (c. 1803–04), expanded the scale and ambition of Classical forms, using their language for unprecedented emotional depth and harmonic innovation, effectively bridging to the Romantic era.

### The First Viennese School
The term "First Viennese School" refers to the trio of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven, who worked in Vienna. While they knew each other and shared a stylistic foundation, they were not a collaborative school like later groups. Their collective work defined the mature Classical style in Vienna, which was also home to many other important composers of the era.

### Instruments and Ensembles
The standard Classical orchestra comprised a string section (violin, viola, cello, double bass) supplemented by woodwinds (flute, oboe, clarinet, bassoon), brass (natural horn, trumpet), and timpani. The keyboard shifted from harpsichord to fortepiano. Chamber music, especially the string quartet, gained prominence. The guitar also experienced a "golden age" of publication and instruction.

## Terms
- **Homophony**: A texture with a primary melody supported by chordal accompaniment.
- **Style galant**: A mid-18th-century style emphasizing lightness and elegance over Baroque grandeur.
- **Sonata form**: A structural principle for music, typically in three sections (exposition, development, recapitulation), central to Classical composition.
- **Basso continuo**: A Baroque practice of continuous bass line and chordal accompaniment, which declined during the Classical period.
- **Fortepiano**: An early piano whose hammers strike strings, allowing for dynamic variation (loud/soft), unlike the plucked harpsichord.
- **Empfindsamkeit**: The "sensitive style" of the mid-18th century, characterized by abrupt emotional and textural changes.
- **First Viennese School**: A term for the principal Classical composers Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven, who worked in Vienna.
- **Sturm und Drang**: A "storm and stress" phase in the arts (c. 1760s–70s) favoring dramatic emotionalism, influencing composers like Haydn.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise boundaries of the period are debated, with some scholars placing the start as early as 1730 or as late as 1770.
- The classification of Franz Schubert is ambiguous; he is sometimes included in the First Viennese School but is also seen as a bridge to Romanticism.
- The extent to which the "First Viennese School" represents a cohesive movement versus a geographical label for three towering individuals is a point of scholarly discussion.
