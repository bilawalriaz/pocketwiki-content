# Wu Zetian

## Overview

Wu Zetian (624–705) was the only woman in Chinese history to rule as emperor in her own right, founding the short-lived Zhou dynasty (690–705) after decades of de facto power as empress consort and dowager during the Tang dynasty. Her rise from concubine to sovereign broke centuries of patriarchal succession norms, and her reign marked a high point of Tang political power and cultural influence, even as it sparked enduring debates about gender, legitimacy, and governance.

## Timeline

- **624** — Birth of Wu Zhao in the Tang dynasty
- **649** — Becomes concubine of Emperor Gaozong after Emperor Taizong's death
- **655** — Elevated to empress consort; rivals Empress Wang and Consort Xiao killed
- **660** — Emperor Gaozong suffers stroke; Wu administers empire on his behalf
- **683** — Gaozong dies; Wu becomes empress dowager and regent for her sons
- **684** — Deposes Emperor Zhongzong; installs Emperor Ruizong
- **690** — Proclaims Zhou dynasty; assumes title of Huangdi (emperor)
- **697** — Suppresses major Khitan rebellion; secret police terror peaks
- **705** — Removed in Shenlong Coup; dies months later

## Body

### Early Life and Rise to Power

Wu Zetian was born into the Wu clan of Wenshui County, though her exact birthplace remains disputed among scholars. Her father, Wu Shiyue, was a prosperous official who served under the early Tang. Encouraged by her parents to study, she entered the palace at age 14 as a concubine to Emperor Taizong, receiving the rank of cairen. After Taizong’s death in 649, she was briefly consigned to a nunnery but was recalled by his successor, Emperor Gaozong, becoming his favored concubine and later empress in 655.

Her ascent was marked by ruthless elimination of rivals. In 655, she orchestrated the downfall of Empress Wang and Consort Xiao through accusations of witchcraft, securing her position as empress consort. Over time, she systematically purged officials who opposed her, including chancellors like Zhangsun Wuji and Chu Suiliang, consolidating power behind the throne.

### De Facto Rule and Political Control

From 660, following Emperor Gaozong’s debilitating illness, Wu governed in all but name, issuing edicts and making key decisions. She maintained control through a network of informants and secret police, notably figures like Lai Junchen and Suo Yuanli, who carried out false accusations, torture, and executions. The "Manual of Accusation," attributed to her regime, codified methods of interrogation and coercion.

She also restructured the bureaucracy, favoring merit-based appointments over hereditary aristocracy, particularly from the northwestern elite. Her reforms weakened the traditional power base of the Guanzhong aristocracy, shifting influence toward scholars and officials from the North China Plain.

### Founding the Zhou Dynasty

In 690, Wu deposed her son Emperor Ruizong and declared herself emperor, establishing the Zhou dynasty. This act defied Confucian succession laws and shocked the political establishment. To legitimize her rule, she adopted Buddhist prophecies, claiming to be the reincarnation of the Devi of Pure Radiance, and commissioned apocryphal sutras to justify her authority. She also performed major Daoist rituals, including sacrifices on Mount Tai, to assert the Mandate of Heaven.

Her reign saw significant cultural and religious patronage. She elevated Buddhism above Taoism, built temples nationwide, and supported Indian influences through monks and texts from Nalanda. She also fostered literary culture, sponsoring the "North Gate Scholars" and promoting new-style poetry.

### Governance and Military Campaigns

Wu’s reign coincided with the High Tang period, characterized by strong central authority and territorial expansion. She launched military campaigns against the Tibetan Empire and the Western Turkic Khaganate, achieving mixed results. In 692, her forces recaptured the Four Garrisons of Anxi from Tibet, but later defeats weakened her western frontier.

Domestically, she implemented land reforms using the equal-field system and issued edicts of grace to win popular support. She expanded the imperial examination system, opening it to commoners and strengthening meritocratic recruitment—a move that reshaped the bureaucratic elite.

### Decline and Fall

In her later years, Wu’s reliance on favorites, particularly the Zhang brothers, led to court factionalism and corruption. By 705, discontent among officials culminated in the Shenlong Coup, led by Li Xian and supported by chancellors like Yao Yuanzhi. The Zhang brothers were executed, and Wu was forced to abdicate. She died shortly thereafter, and the Tang dynasty was restored under Emperor Zhongzong.

## Terms

- **Huangdi**: Title meaning "imperial supreme ruler"; reserved for emperors; Wu was the only woman to assume it.
- **Hou**: Title meaning "sovereign" or "queen"; grammatically gender-neutral in Classical Chinese.
- **Empress Dowager**: Title for a retired empress who continues to wield influence, often as regent.
- **Secret Police**: Officials like Lai Junchen who used informants, torture, and false accusations to eliminate rivals.
- **Civil Service Examinations**: Merit-based tests for government service; expanded under Wu to include commoners.
- **Mandate of Heaven**: Concept legitimizing rule based on virtue; Wu used rituals to claim it.
- **Fubing System**: Military organization combining soldier-farming; used to maintain armies at low cost.
- **Juntian System**: Equal-field land distribution policy ensuring fair allocation to farmers.
- **North Gate Scholars**: Group of literary officials who served Wu directly, bypassing traditional chancellors.
- **Zhou Dynasty**: Wu’s self-proclaimed dynasty (690–705), named after the ancient Zhou, lasting only her lifetime.

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars debate the extent of Wu’s personal involvement in atrocities. Traditional histories accuse her of killing her daughter and multiple relatives, but modern historians argue these accounts were shaped by Confucian bias against female rule. The death of her daughter is now considered possibly natural, with rumors emerging centuries later.

Her political reforms, particularly the expansion of the examination system, are widely praised, yet some argue they were motivated primarily by necessity—she needed loyal officials outside the Li clan. The legitimacy of her Zhou dynasty is also contested; while contemporaries accepted it, later historians often dismissed it as an illegitimate interruption of Tang rule.

Her use of Buddhism and Daoism for political legitimacy remains a subject of analysis, with some viewing it as pragmatic and others as syncretic manipulation. The long-term impact of her social policies—particularly the weakening of aristocratic power—is still evaluated differently by scholars, with some seeing it as progressive and others as destabilizing.
