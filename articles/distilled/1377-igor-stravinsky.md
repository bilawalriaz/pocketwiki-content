# Igor Stravinsky

## Overview

Igor Fyodorovich Stravinsky (1882–1971) was a Russian-born composer and conductor, later a French (1934) and American (1945) citizen, widely considered one of the most important and influential composers of the 20th century. A pivotal figure in modernist music, he is best known for three revolutionary ballets composed for Sergei Diaghilev's Ballets Russes—The Firebird (1910), Petrushka (1911), and The Rite of Spring (1913)—the last of which caused a near-riot at its premiere due to its avant-garde nature and permanently changed how composers understood rhythmic structure. His career is typically divided into three periods: Russian (1913–1920), neoclassical (1920–1951), and serial (1954–1968), and his stylistic versatility influenced composers as diverse as Aaron Copland, Philip Glass, Béla Bartók, and Pierre Boulez. In 1998, Time magazine listed Stravinsky as one of the 100 most influential people of the century.

## Body

### Early life and education (1882–1909)

Stravinsky was born into a musical family in Oranienbaum, Russia. His father Fyodor was a bass at the Mariinsky Theater; his mother Anna was an amateur singer and pianist. The name "Stravinsky" is of Polish origin, deriving from the Strava river. He studied piano privately from age nine and regularly attended Mariinsky Theater performances from around age 10. Despite his musical passion, his parents expected him to study law at the University of Saint Petersburg, enrolling in 1901. During summer 1902, he met Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov, who agreed to personally advise him on compositions. After Fyodor died in 1902, Stravinsky became more independent and increasingly involved in Rimsky-Korsakov's circle. His first major task was the Piano Sonata in F-sharp minor (performed publicly in 1905); he also composed the Symphony in E-flat (finished first draft 1905) and Faun and Shepherdess (1907). Rimsky-Korsakov organized the first public premiere of Stravinsky's work in April 1907. Rimsky-Korsakov's death in June 1908 deeply affected Stravinsky, who composed Funeral Song in his memory.

### International fame: the three ballets (1909–1913)

Impresario Sergei Diaghilev, after hearing Stravinsky's Scherzo fantastique and Feu d'artifice in February 1909, commissioned him to orchestrate Chopin for the ballet Les Sylphides. When Diaghilev's Ballets Russes needed a new ballet with distinctly Russian music, he turned to Stravinsky after other composers declined. The Firebird premiered in Paris on 25 June 1910 to widespread acclaim. Petrushka followed on 13 June 1911 to equal acclaim. The Rite of Spring, depicting pagan rituals in Slavonic tribes, used avant-garde techniques including uneven rhythms, superimposed harmonies, atonality, and extensive instrumentation; its experimental nature caused a near-riot at its 29 May 1913 premiere. Soon after, Stravinsky was hospitalized for typhoid fever for five weeks.

### Russian period (1913–1920)

During World War I, the Stravinskys lived in Switzerland (1914–1920). His income from German royalties was cut off when his publisher suspended operations, forcing him to sell manuscripts and accept commissions. Stravinsky intensely researched Russian folk poetry, drawing on collections by Pyotr Kireevsky and Alexander Afanasyev. Renard (1916), an opera-ballet, used folk texts; L'Histoire du soldat (1918), a small-scale theater work for eleven musicians and two dancers written with Swiss novelist Charles F. Ramuz, mixed Russian folktales with popular forms like tango, waltz, ragtime, and chorale. Les noces, a ballet-cantata depicting Russian wedding traditions, was completed in 1923. In 1917, Stravinsky met Pablo Picasso in Rome; a commedia dell'arte they saw inspired the ballet Pulcinella (premiered 1920).

### Neoclassical period (1920–1951)

After the war, Stravinsky moved to France, living briefly with Coco Chanel before settling in Biarritz and later Nice. His neoclassical period turned toward techniques and themes from the classical period. Key works include the Octet (1923, using sonata form), Apollon musagète (1927, Greek mythology), Oedipus rex (1927, Latin libretto by Jean Cocteau), Persephone (1935), and Symphony of Psalms (1930, using Latin Vulgate psalms). In 1921, he signed a contract with player piano company Pleyel, creating piano roll arrangements of his music. After meeting Father Nicolas in 1926, Stravinsky reconnected with the Russian Orthodox Church and wrote his first religious composition, Pater Noster. He met violinist Samuel Dushkin in Germany, composing the Violin Concerto with his help. In 1934, Stravinsky became a French citizen. His last years in France were marked by the deaths of his daughter Ludmila, wife Yekaterina, and mother Anna from 1938–1939, and he moved to the United States shortly after World War II began.

### United States and serial period (1939–1971)

In the United States, Stravinsky delivered the Charles Eliot Norton Lectures at Harvard (published as Poetics of Music), married Vera de Bosset in 1940, and settled in Los Angeles. Money became scarce during the war, leading him to compose commercial works, including the Circus Polka (commissioned by Barnum and Bailey for elephant choreography by Balanchine) and the Scherzo à la russe. He allowed The Rite of Spring to be used in Disney's 1940 film Fantasia. In 1945, he became an American citizen and signed with Boosey & Hawkes. He met conductor Robert Craft in 1948; Craft became his assistant and collaborator. After The Rake's Progress (1951), an English-language opera based on William Hogarth's paintings with a libretto by W. H. Auden and Chester Kallman, Stravinsky turned toward serialism, influenced by the Second Viennese School music Craft introduced him to. In Memoriam Dylan Thomas (1954) was his first fully twelve-tone work; Canticum Sacrum (1956) was his first based on a tone row. His final major work was the Requiem Canticles (1966), performed at his funeral. Stravinsky died of pulmonary edema on 6 April 1971 in New York City.

## Terms

- **Ballets Russes**: Sergei Diaghilev's ballet company that premiered Stravinsky's first three ballets in Paris.
- **Twelve-tone technique**: Serial compositional method devised by Arnold Schoenberg using a fixed ordering of the twelve pitches; Stravinsky treated it more freely, repeating notes and reordering row cells.
- **Neoclassicism**: Period (1920–1951) when Stravinsky used techniques and themes from the classical period, modernizing older forms like sonata form and Baroque counterpoint.
- **Leit-harmony**: Stravinsky's term for a harmonic structure used in The Firebird, a portmanteau of leitmotif and harmony, juxtaposing whole-tone phrases (Firebird) with octatonic harmony (Koschei).
- **Bitonality**: Use of two keys simultaneously; Petrushka contains one of the first prominent uses in modern music via the "Petrushka chord."
- **Tone row**: An ordered sequence of the twelve pitches used in serial composition.
- **Octatonic harmony**: Harmony based on an eight-note scale alternating whole and half steps, associated with Koschei in The Firebird.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate the exact boundaries of Stravinsky's stylistic periods: musicologist Jeremy Noble dated the neoclassical period from 1920 with Symphonies of Wind Instruments, while Béla Bartók argued it "really starts with his Octet for Wind Instruments." Bartók also considered the Russian period to have begun in 1913 with The Rite of Spring. Stravinsky's constant stylistic changes confused many supporters, and academics saw his turn to serialism as not innovative enough, believing Schoenberg's death marked the end of twelve-tone music. However, later analysis judged his neoclassical style as "the harbinger of musical postmodernism," and his music stood out as a body of unique ingenuity after many modern styles fell out of fashion.
