# Mary Shelley

## Overview
Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley (1797–1851) was an English novelist, editor, and travel writer best known for *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus* (1818), a foundational work of science fiction that critiques the individualistic ambition of Romanticism and the Enlightenment faith in perfectibility. The daughter of political philosopher William Godwin and feminist pioneer Mary Wollstonecraft, Shelley was raised in an intellectual anarchist household but received little formal education. Her elopement at 16 with the married poet Percy Bysshe Shelley led to ostracism, debt, and the deaths of three of their four children. The conception of *Frankenstein* occurred during a famous 1816 summer at Lake Geneva with Lord Byron. After Percy’s drowning in 1822, Shelley supported her surviving son, Percy Florence, through a prolific career encompassing historical novels (*Valperga*, *Perkin Warbeck*), the apocalyptic *The Last Man*, biographical essays, and travelogues (*Rambles in Germany and Italy*). She also edited and canonized Percy’s poetry, often against censorship from her father-in-law. Recent scholarship recognizes her as a lifelong political radical who argued that cooperation and sympathy—particularly as practiced by women in the family—were the true mechanisms for social reform, challenging both her father’s rationalist anarchism and her husband’s Romantic individualism.

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Mary Godwin was born into a radical intellectual dynasty. Her mother, Mary Wollstonecraft, author of *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, died days after her birth; her father, William Godwin, authored *Political Justice* (1793), a foundational anarchist text arguing that marriage was a repressive monopoly (a view he later retracted). Godwin provided Mary a rich informal education—access to his library, intellectual visitors like Coleridge and Aaron Burr, and tutoring in history and languages—though he admitted he did not follow Wollstonecraft’s prescribed pedagogy. The 1801 marriage to Mary Jane Clairmont brought financial instability (the Godwins’ publishing firm failed) and a hostile stepmother who favored her own children. From 1812–1813, Mary stayed with the dissenting Baxter family in Scotland, an experience she later credited with fostering her imagination: "my true compositions... were born and fostered" beneath the trees there.

### Relationship with Percy Shelley and Continental Travels
In 1814, Mary (16) fell in love with Percy Shelley (21), a devotee of Godwin’s *Political Justice* who was estranged from his aristocratic family and married to Harriet. They met secretly at her mother’s grave in St Pancras churchyard. Godwin forbade the relationship, seeking to protect his daughter’s "spotless fame," but the couple eloped to France on 28 July 1814 with Claire Clairmont. The journey through post-Napoleonic France radicalized Mary’s pacifism: "The distress of the inhabitants... has given a sting to my detestation of war." Penniless and pregnant, they returned to England in September 1814 to face Godwin’s refusal to speak to them, Percy’s creditors, and the birth and death of their first daughter (1815). A brief financial reprieve followed Percy’s grandfather’s death, allowing a stay at Bishopsgate where *Alastor* was written and William was born (1816).

### The Geneva Summer and *Frankenstein*
In May 1816, the Shelleys and Claire (pregnant by Byron) joined Lord Byron and physician John Polidori at Villa Diodati, Lake Geneva. Incessant rain confined them indoors, where German ghost stories prompted Byron’s challenge to each write one. Mary’s anxiety lifted during a "waking dream" (astronomically dated to 2–3 am, 16 June 1816) after discussions on galvanism and the "principle of life." She envisioned a "pale student of unhallowed arts" animating a "hideous phantasm." With Percy’s encouragement, she expanded the tale into *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus*, published 1 January 1818. The novel critiques Romantic egotism: Victor Frankenstein, a Satanic/Promethean figure, abandons family for "relentless ambition... clothed as quest for truth," demonstrating the individual’s lack of control over history—a direct challenge to Godwinian perfectibility.

### Authorship of *Frankenstein*
The extent of Percy’s contribution remains debated. Mary wrote she owed him "not the suggestion of one incident... but for his incitement, it would never have taken the form in which it was presented." James Rieger argued Percy’s assistance was so extensive he was a "minor collaborator"; Anne K. Mellor countered he made only "technical corrections"; Charles E. Robinson equated his role to a standard publisher’s editor. Fiona Sampson (2018), examining the Bodleian notebooks, concluded Percy did "rather less than any line editor working in publishing today."

### Italian Years: Loss and Literary Productivity
The Shelleys left for Italy in March 1818, never to return. The period was marked by catastrophic child loss—Clara (1818, Venice) and William (1819, Rome)—plunging Mary into depression alleviated only by writing and the birth of Percy Florence (1819). Despite grief, Italy offered political freedom. Mary wrote *Valperga* (1823) to aid her impoverished father after Percy refused to assist him further regarding the novel. She also wrote the novella *Matilda* (suppressed by Godwin for incest themes), and plays *Proserpine* and *Midas*. She navigated Percy’s interest in other women (Sophia Stacey, Emilia Viviani, Jane Williams) while forming her own emotional ties, notably to Greek revolutionary Prince Mavrokordatos. The mysterious 1819 registration of a baby, Elena Adelaide Shelley, in Naples—claimed by dismissed servants to be Percy’s by Claire or Elise Foggi—remains unresolved; Elena died in 1820. In 1822, a pregnant Mary miscarried nearly fatally at Villa Magni; Percy saved her with an ice bath. Weeks later, he drowned sailing back from Livorno (8 July 1822).

### Widowhood, Editorial Labor, and Professional Authorship
Returning to England in 1823, Mary faced precarious finances. Sir Timothy Shelley (Percy’s father) initially agreed to support his grandson Percy Florence only if the boy were handed over to an appointed guardian; Mary rejected this instantly. She secured instead a limited annual allowance (£100, later £250), repayable when Percy Florence inherited the estate, though Sir Timothy refused to meet her and dealt only through lawyers. He threatened to stop the allowance if any biography of Percy were published. Mary circumvented this by editing *Posthumous Poems* (1824) and the canonical *Poetical Works* (1839), casting Percy as a lyrical sentimentalist rather than political radical to suit Victorian tastes. She restored atheistic passages in *Queen Mab* for the second edition, leading to Moxon’s prosecution for blasphemous libel. Critics accused her of miscopying and obscuring Percy’s voice, yet Donald Reiman acknowledges her editions remain essential sources. Simultaneously, she wrote *The Last Man* (1826), *Perkin Warbeck* (1830), *Lodore* (1835), *Falkner* (1837), five volumes of biographies for Lardner’s *Cabinet Cyclopaedia* (print runs ~4,000 vs. hundreds for novels), and the travelogue *Rambles in Germany and Italy* (1844). In 1827, she aided marginalized women by obtaining false passports for Isabel Robinson and Mary Diana Dods to embark on a life together in France as husband and wife. She also supported her aging father.

### Literary Themes: Gender, Politics, and Genre
Shelley deployed the **Godwinian novel** (confessional form exploring self/society), **historical novel** (Scott’s genre, feminized in *Valperga* and *Perkin Warbeck* to privilege female "reason and sensibility" over masculine conquest), and **Gothic** (to explore repressed female desire and censor her own authorial voice). *Frankenstein* functions as a "birth myth" (Moers) processing guilt over her mother’s death and her own maternal failures; as a critique of "unnatural modes of production" where man usurps female reproduction (Mellor); and as a challenge to Miltonic patriarchy (Gilbert & Gubar). *The Last Man* rejects Enlightenment progress and Romantic imagination, depicting a plague-emptied world where humanity is not central. Politically, Shelley remained a reformer: she celebrated the 1820 Spanish Liberal uprising, the 1830 Whig victory, and the Reform Act 1832. Scholars debate whether *Lodore* and *Falkner* signal conservatism (Poovey, Mellor: retreat to "separate sphere" of bourgeois family) or consistent radicalism (Bennett, Blumberg: challenge to patriarchal education and violent masculinity from the start). Her works consistently argue that "cooperation and sympathy, particularly as practised by women in the family, were the ways to reform civil society."

### Travelogues and Biographies as Political Intervention
*History of a Six Weeks' Tour* (1817) and *Rambles in Germany and Italy* (1844) use the travelogue form to map "personal and political landscape through the discourse of sensibility and sympathy." For Shelley, travel builds "a wider circle of sympathy... the uses of travel." *Rambles* critiques monarchy, class, slavery, and war while recording her "pilgrimage" to Percy’s Italian sites. Her biographies for Lardner (1832–39), written in Johnsonian style, emphasized domesticity and criticized male-dominated institutions like primogeniture. Greg Kucich argues these widely circulated volumes (4,000 copies each) made biography "one of her most influential political interventions" for feminist historiography.

### Reputation and Legacy
In her lifetime, reviewers missed her political edge. Posthumously, she was reduced to "Shelley’s Pygmalion" (Frederick Jones, 1945) and a one-novel author. Her son and daughter-in-law "Victorianised" her memory via censored letters; Trelawny questioned her intelligence and authorship. Theatrical (1823 *Presumption*) and cinematic adaptations (1910, 1931 Whale, 1957 Hammer, 1974 Brooks, 1994 Branagh) cemented *Frankenstein* in popular culture but obscured her broader oeuvre. Republication since the 1980s and Sunstein’s 1989 biography restored her status as a major Romantic figure: a versatile professional writer, political radical, and pioneering female author who declared, "I think that I can maintain myself, and there is something inspiriting in the idea."

## Terms
- **Godwinian novel**: A 1790s genre (exemplified by Godwin’s *Caleb Williams*) using Rousseauvian confession to explore the contradictory relations between the self and society; Shelley adopts its form but critiques its Enlightenment optimism.
- **Galvanism**: The contraction of muscle stimulated by electric current; a contemporary scientific fascination (e.g., Aldini’s experiments) that informed the "principle of life" discussions at Villa Diodati and the animation mechanism in *Frankenstein*.
- **The Modern Prometheus**: *Frankenstein*’s subtitle; references the Titan who stole fire (knowledge) for humanity and was eternally punished, paralleling Victor’s usurpation of divine creation.
- **Cabinet Cyclopaedia**: Dionysius Lardner’s popular encyclopedia series (1820s–30s) targeting middle-class self-education; Shelley contributed five volumes of literary biographies, her highest-circulation works.
- **Separate spheres**: The 19th-century ideology dividing public (male) and domestic (female) realms; scholars debate whether Shelley’s later novels retreat into this or subvert it by valuing feminine "cooperation and sympathy" as political tools.
- **Primogeniture**: The right of the firstborn son to inherit the entire estate; a frequent target of Shelley’s biographical critiques as a male-dominated institution.
- **Queen Mab**: Percy Shelley’s early atheistic epic poem; Mary fought to include its radical passages in her 1839 edition, resulting in her publisher’s blasphemy prosecution.
- **Birth myth**: Ellen Moers’s term for *Frankenstein*, reading the novel as Shelley’s processing of trauma from her mother’s death in childbirth and her own infant mortality.
- **Rambles in Germany and Italy**: Shelley’s 1844 epistolary travelogue, blending personal grief, political commentary on Italian nationalism, and the theory that sympathetic connection builds civil society.
- **Pygmalion**: Mythological sculptor who fell in love with his statue; used by critics to diminish Mary as merely Percy’s creation rather than an independent genius.

## Debates and open questions
- **Percy Shelley’s role in *Frankenstein***: Collaboration vs. editing. The manuscript shows Percy’s hand in revisions, but scholars disagree on whether this constitutes co-authorship (Rieger), standard editorial input (Robinson, Sampson), or thematic shaping (Mellor).
- **The Naples mystery (1819)**: The parentage of Elena Adelaide Shelley, registered by Percy in Naples. Theories include adoption, Percy’s child by Elise Foggi or Claire Clairmont, or Elise’s child by Byron. Mary denied knowledge; biographers remain divided.
- **Political trajectory—radical to conservative?**: Poovey and Mellor argue later novels (*Lodore*, *Falkner*) retreat into domestic conservatism and bourgeois family values. Bennett and Blumberg counter that Shelley consistently challenged patriarchal structures from *Frankenstein* onward, and her "reformist" stance was always distinct from Percy’s and Godwin’s radicalism.
- **Autobiographical vs. typological reading**: Critics (e.g., Clemit, Blumberg) resist reading characters as thinly veiled portraits (e.g., *Matilda* as Mary/Godwin/Percy), arguing Shelley created "types rather than portraits" to explore philosophical ideas.
- **Suppression of *Matilda***: Godwin refused to publish the novella (written 1819–20) due to its incest theme; it remained unpublished until 1959. The extent to which this censorship shaped Shelley’s subsequent authorial self-effacement is debated.
- **Nature of Shelley’s radicalism**: Whether her advocacy for "feminine affections" as the basis of social reform constitutes a distinct feminist political theory or an essentialist reinforcement of gender roles remains a central question in recent scholarship.
