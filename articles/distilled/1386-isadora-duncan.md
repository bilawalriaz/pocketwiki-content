# Isadora Duncan

## Overview
Angela Isadora Duncan (1877/1878–1927) was an American-born dancer and choreographer recognized as the "Mother of Modern Dance." Rejecting the rigid codification of classical ballet, she developed a technique based on natural, evolutionary movement—skipping, running, and leaping—originating from the solar plexus and inspired by Greek antiquity, nature, and American athleticism. She performed barefoot in loose Greek tunics, transforming dance from theatrical entertainment into a sacred, expressive art form. Her life was marked by artistic innovation, the founding of experimental schools in Germany, France, the U.S., and Soviet Russia, and profound personal tragedies, including the drowning deaths of her two eldest children. She died in Nice, France, when her silk scarf entangled in a car’s rear axle, breaking her neck.

## Body

### Early Life and Artistic Formation
Born in San Francisco, Duncan was the youngest of four children. Her father, a banker and mining engineer, faced financial ruin and scandal following a bank collapse; her mother divorced him, leaving the family in poverty. Duncan found formal schooling constricting and dropped out around age ten, earning money teaching dance with her siblings. In 1896, she joined Augustin Daly’s company in New York but clashed with the pantomime style and hierarchy. Brief study with ballet teacher Marie Bonfanti confirmed her rejection of rigid routine. Feeling unappreciated in America, she sailed for London in 1898, where drawing-room performances for the wealthy funded a studio. There, Greek vases and bas-reliefs at the British Museum crystallized her aesthetic. In Paris, the Louvre and the 1900 Exposition Universelle further shaped her vision, leading to salon performances for patrons like Marguerite de Saint-Marceaux.

### Career Breakthrough and European Tours
In 1902, Loie Fuller invited Duncan to tour Europe. This exposure cemented her reputation for a style emphasizing natural movement—skipping, running, jumping—over ballet’s rigidity. Despite mixed critical reception, she became a sensation, inspiring visual artists including Auguste Rodin, Antoine Bourdelle, and Arnold Rönnebeck. In 1910, she met Aleister Crowley, who praised her "magical consciousness" in gesture, and formed a lifelong bond with Mary Desti. A famous 1912 Bacchanalia hosted by designer Paul Poiret at Pavillon du Butard featured Duncan dancing on tables in a Greek gown for 300 guests.

### Schools and Educational Mission
Duncan viewed commercial touring as a distraction from her true mission: educating youth and creating beauty. She opened her first school in 1904 in Grunewald, Germany. It was a boarding school combining academics with dance, not intended to produce professionals. Six pupils—Anna, Maria-Theresa, Irma, Gretel, Lisa, and Erika—became known as the "Isadorables"; three later used the Duncan surname. A Paris school closed due to World War I. In 1914, she relocated to New York, backed by financier Otto Kahn, who provided the Century Theatre for productions like *Oedipus Rex*. In 1921, her leftist sympathies drew her to Soviet Russia, where she founded a Moscow school. When the Soviet government failed to support it, she returned to the West in 1924, leaving the school to her adopted daughter, Irma Doretta Henrietta Erih-Grimm Duncan.

### Philosophy and Technique
Duncan traced dance to its roots as a sacred art. Her technique rejected codified ballet vocabulary for "evolutionary" motion: each movement born organically from the previous, originating in the solar plexus. She cited the sea, Greek antiquity, folk dances, and American athleticism (great strides, leaps, lifted forehead) as inspirations. Costumes—white Greek tunics and bare feet—enabled the freedom of movement denied by corsets and pointe shoes. She sought to connect emotion and movement as the "divine expression of the human spirit," encompassing both joy and sorrow.

### Personal Life: Children, Relationships, and Tragedy
Duncan bore three children out of wedlock: Deirdre Beatrice (1906, father Gordon Craig) and Patrick Augustus (1910, father Paris Singer, heir to the sewing machine fortune). Both drowned in 1913 when their car plunged into the Seine. In despair, she conceived a son with sculptor Romano Romanelli in 1914; he died shortly after birth. She had a close, speculated-upon relationship with actress Eleonora Duse. In 1922, she married poet Sergei Yesenin, 18 years her junior, taking Soviet citizenship. The marriage collapsed; Yesenin returned to Moscow in 1923 and died by apparent suicide in 1925. A purported romantic relationship with poet Mercedes de Acosta, documented in letters, is disputed by Duncan’s friends and relatives as forged publicity. In her final years, Duncan struggled with depression, debt, and alcoholism, moving between Paris and the Mediterranean. Her autobiography *My Life* was published posthumously in 1927.

### Death
On September 14, 1927, in Nice, Duncan rode in an open Amilcar CGSS automobile owned by mechanic Benoît Falchetto. She wore a long, hand-painted red silk shawl by Roman Chatov, a gift from Mary Desti. The shawl entangled in the open-spoked rear wheel and axle, pulling her from the car and breaking her neck (some sources note near-decapitation). Desti claimed she warned Duncan immediately. Duncan’s reported last words were either "Adieu, mes amis. Je vais à la gloire!" or "Je vais à l'amour." Gertrude Stein remarked, "affectations can be dangerous." As a Soviet citizen, her will was the first of a Soviet national probated in the U.S. Her ashes rest at Père Lachaise Cemetery beside her children.

## Terms
- ****Modern Dance**** — A dance genre pioneered by Duncan emphasizing natural, expressive movement over the codified vocabulary and rigid technique of classical ballet.
- ****Solar Plexus**** — The anatomical center (network of nerves in the abdomen) Duncan identified as the source of all movement initiation.
- ****Evolutionary Motion**** — Duncan’s principle that each movement arises organically from the preceding one in a continuous, natural succession.
- ****Isadorables**** — The six female pupils (Anna, Maria-Theresa, Irma, Gretel, Lisa, Erika) from Duncan’s 1904 Grunewald school who carried on her legacy.
- ****Greek Tunic**** — A loose, white, sleeveless garment inspired by ancient Greek dress, worn barefoot to allow unrestricted movement.
- ****Bacchanalia**** — A festival re-creation hosted by Paul Poiret (1912) where Duncan danced on tables, evoking the rites of Bacchus/Dionysus.
- ****Amilcar CGSS**** — The open automobile model in which Duncan was a passenger at the time of her fatal accident.
- ****Isadora Duncan Syndrome**** — A medical term for injury or death caused by neckwear (scarves, necklaces) entangling in machinery.
- ****Century Theatre**** — A modern New York venue provided by financier Otto Kahn for Duncan’s 1914–1915 productions, including *Oedipus Rex*.
- ****Varshavianka**** — A 1924 dance routine composed by Duncan to the Polish revolutionary song "Whirlwinds of Danger."

## Debates and open questions
*   **Birth Date:** Sources conflict between May 26, 1877, and May 27, 1878.
*   **Last Words:** Witnesses differ on whether Duncan said "Je vais à la gloire" (I go to glory) or "Je vais à l'amour" (I go to love).
*   **Mercedes de Acosta Relationship:** The authenticity of letters suggesting a romantic affair is disputed; Duncan’s circle alleged forgery for publicity.
*   **Eleonora Duse Relationship:** Speculation exists regarding the nature of their intimacy during Duncan’s grief in Viareggio, but no confirmation exists.
*   **Legal Adoption of Isadorables:** Duncan did not legally adopt all six pupils, though three used her surname; the extent of formal guardianship remains unclear.
*   **Soviet School Support:** The reasons for the Soviet government’s failure to fulfill promises to Duncan’s Moscow school are not fully detailed in the source.
