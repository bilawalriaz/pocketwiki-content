# José de San Martín

## Overview
José de San Martín (1778–1850) was an Argentine general and the primary leader of the southern and central South American wars of independence from Spain. He is revered as a national hero in Argentina, Chile, and Peru for his strategic military campaigns that liberated these nations, most notably through the Crossing of the Andes and the naval expedition to Peru.

## Timeline
- **25 February 1778** — Born in Yapeyú, Corrientes (modern Argentina).
- **1783** — Family moves to Spain; begins military studies in Málaga.
- **1808** — Fights in the Peninsular War; contacts independence supporters in London.
- **9 March 1812** — Arrives in Buenos Aires to serve the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata.
- **3 February 1813** — Leads the Battle of San Lorenzo against royalist forces.
- **1814** — Commands the Army of the North; conceives the plan to liberate Peru via Chile.
- **6 September 1814** — Becomes Governor of Cuyo Province, organizing the Army of the Andes.
- **9 July 1816** — Congress of Tucumán declares independence of the United Provinces.
- **January–February 1817** — Leads the Crossing of the Andes with the Army of the Andes.
- **12 February 1817** — Wins the Battle of Chacabuco, liberating Santiago, Chile.
- **12 July 1821** — Appointed Protector of Peru after seizing Lima.
- **28 July 1821** — Peruvian independence is officially declared.
- **26 July 1822** — Meets Simón Bolívar at the Guayaquil Conference.
- **3 September 1822** — Resigns as Protector of Peru and departs for Chile.
- **17 August 1850** — Dies in Boulogne-sur-Mer, France.

## Body

### Early Life and European Military Career
Born in Yapeyú, San Martín moved to Spain as a child. He began a military career in 1789, fighting in North Africa, the War of the Second Coalition, and the War of the Oranges. During the Peninsular War (1808–1814) against Napoleonic France, he rose to lieutenant colonel. Influenced by Enlightenment ideas and secret societies, he resigned from the Spanish army and, in 1812, sailed to South America to join the independence movements.

### Argentine Campaign and the Army of the Andes
Upon arriving in Buenos Aires, San Martín organized the Regiment of Mounted Grenadiers and fought at the Battle of San Lorenzo (1813). After commanding the Army of the North, he devised a strategic plan: instead of attacking the royalist stronghold in Upper Peru directly, he would create a new army in Cuyo, cross the Andes to liberate Chile, and then attack Peru by sea. As Governor of Cuyo, he meticulously organized the Army of the Andes, mobilizing resources and manpower, including freed slaves. He successfully lobbied for the formal declaration of independence by the Congress of Tucumán in 1816 to legitimize the campaign.

### Liberation of Chile
In early 1817, San Martín led the Army of the Andes across the mountain range in six columns, a massive logistical feat. He defeated royalist forces at the Battle of Chacabuco (1817) and entered Santiago, where he declined the Chilean leadership in favor of Bernardo O'Higgins. After a setback at the Battle of Cancha Rayada (1818), he secured Chilean independence with a decisive victory at the Battle of Maipú (1818). He then focused on building a naval fleet for the next phase of the war.

### Protector of Peru and the Guayaquil Conference
San Martín led a naval expedition to Peru in 1820. Employing a strategy of attrition and political persuasion rather than direct assault, he entered Lima in 1821 and was declared Protector of Peru. He enacted reforms, including abolishing native servitude and the Inquisition, but did not fully abolish slavery. His campaign stalled due to a lack of reinforcements and internal disputes. In 1822, he met with Simón Bolívar at the Guayaquil Conference. The details are unknown, but afterward, San Martín resigned his command and left Peru, allowing Bolívar to complete its liberation.

### Later Life and Legacy
San Martín retired from public life, living in Europe. He supported the federalist cause in Argentina from afar, admiring Juan Manuel de Rosas. He died in France in 1850, and his remains were repatriated to Buenos Aires in 1880. He is celebrated as a founding father in Argentina, Chile, and Peru, with numerous statues and honors worldwide. His legacy as a brilliant strategist and selfless leader who prioritized independence over personal power is central to South American national identities.

## Terms
- **Army of the Andes**: The military force created and commanded by San Martín in Cuyo Province for the liberation of Chile and Peru.
- **Protector of Peru**: The title held by San Martín as the head of state of newly independent Peru from 1821 to 1822.
- **United Provinces of the Río de la Plata**: The former Spanish viceroyalty that became the basis for modern Argentina.
- **Royalist**: A soldier or supporter of the Spanish Crown during the wars of independence.
- **Peninsular War**: The 1808–1814 conflict in the Iberian Peninsula where Spanish forces, including San Martín, fought against Napoleonic France.
- **Lodge of Rational Knights**: A secret society San Martín joined in 1811, which promoted independence ideals among American-born Spaniards.
- **Caudillo**: A military and political leader, often of a province, in post-independence Spanish America.
- **Freedom of Wombs**: A legal principle enacted by San Martín in Peru that freed children born to enslaved mothers.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Reasons for leaving Spain**: Historians debate whether San Martín was motivated by patriotism, was acting as a British agent, or saw the South American wars as a continuation of the Enlightenment struggle against absolutism.
- **Guayaquil Conference**: The private discussions between San Martín and Bolívar in 1822 are unknown. Theories suggest they discussed combining forces, the form of government for liberated nations, or San Martín's possible offer to serve under Bolívar.
- **Execution of the Carrera brothers**: The responsibility for the 1818 execution of Chilean leaders Luis and Juan José Carrera is disputed, with some historians blaming San Martín and others O'Higgins.
- **San Martín's monarchism**: His support for establishing a monarchy in South America, possibly with an Inca descendant, was a political strategy to gain European recognition, but it remains a point of analysis regarding his political vision.
