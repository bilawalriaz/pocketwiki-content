# National flag

## Overview
A national flag is a symbol representing a nation, flown by its government and citizens. Its design, featuring specific colors and symbols, often carries meaning and can change after significant historical events. The concept evolved from military standards to become a key emblem of national identity and sovereignty.

- **13th century** — Republics of Genoa and Venice use maritime flags.
- **1606** — First Union Flag (Union Jack) created for Great Britain.

## Body

### History and Origins
Flags originated as military field signs, such as Roman *vexillum* standards and Viking "Raven banners." The practice of using flags to indicate country of origin outside warfare began with maritime flags in the 13th century. The Dutch flag, evolving from the 16th-century *Prinsenvlag*, is perhaps the oldest tricolour in continuous use. The Union Jack was created in 1606 to represent the union of England and Scotland, later incorporating Ireland in 1801. National flags began to be displayed in civilian contexts with the rise of nationalism in the late 18th century, exemplified by the U.S. and French flags. Most European nations standardized their flag designs in the 19th and early 20th centuries.

### Adoption and Change
A national flag's design is often described in a constitution or a separate flag law. Minor changes are typically legislative, while major changes are constitutional. Flag changes frequently follow regime changes, such as revolutions or civil wars, making them political symbols. For example, Germany's flag has changed with its government from the imperial tricolour to the Weimar Republic's black-red-gold, to the Nazi flag, and back to black-red-gold after World War II. Similarly, flags of Libya and Syria were abandoned after coups and later reinstated by opposition forces.

### Usage and Protocol
National flags have distinct types for land and sea. On land, they are categorized as civil (for anyone), state (for government), and war/military flags. Many countries use one design for all three. At sea, flags are called ensigns: civil, state, and naval. Strict protocol governs display: the national flag is flown in the position of honour, hoisted first and lowered last, and flown at equal height with other national flags. Flying a flag upside down is a traditional distress signal, though only the Philippines officially recognizes this.

### Design and Similarities
The art of flag design is called *vexillography*. Most flags are rectangular, with Nepal being a notable exception. Common colors include red, white, green, blue, yellow, and black; Jamaica is the only national flag with none of these. Many flags are similar, sometimes due to shared history (flag families). Examples include the Nordic Cross design used by Scandinavian countries, the Pan-African colors (red, yellow, green) in many African flags, and the Pan-Arab colors (red, white, black) in Arab nations. Similarities can cause confusion, such as between the flags of Italy and Mexico, which differ in shade, proportion, and the Mexican coat of arms.

## Terms
- **Vexillography**: The art and practice of designing flags.
- **Civil flag**: A flag flown by private citizens, not government entities.
- **State flag**: A flag used officially by government agencies.
- **War flag**: A flag used by military organizations.
- **Ensign**: A flag indicating a ship's nationality, flown at sea.
- **Canton**: The upper-left quarter of a flag, often containing a distinct design.
- **Tricolour**: A flag with three colored bands.
- **Nordic Cross**: A cross design shifted to the hoist side, used by Scandinavian countries.
