# Antarctic Peninsula

## Overview
The Antarctic Peninsula is the northernmost part of mainland Antarctica, extending about 1,300 km from the Weddell Sea. It is a region of dramatic climate change, hosting the continent's highest concentration of research stations and subject to overlapping territorial claims by Argentina, Chile, and the United Kingdom. Its relatively mild climate and accessible location make it a focal point for studying rapid environmental shifts and a hub for international scientific activity.

## Timeline
- **27 Jan 1820** — First likely sighting by a Russian expedition led by Fabian Gottlieb von Bellingshausen.
- **30 Jan 1820** — First charting of part of the peninsula (Trinity Peninsula) by a British expedition.
- **1832** — John Biscoe names the northern part "Graham Land."
- **1901–1904** — Swedish Antarctic Expedition explores parts of the peninsula.
- **1934–1937** — British Graham Land expedition concludes it is a peninsula, not an archipelago.
- **1940s** — First Antarctic research stations established during WWII (Operation Tabarin).
- **1964** — "Antarctic Peninsula" name agreed upon, dividing it into Graham Land (north) and Palmer Land (south).
- **1989** — Oil spill from the grounding of the Argentine ship ARA *Bahía Paraíso*.
- **2002** — Significant breakup of the Larsen Ice Shelf.
- **2020** — A large iceberg breaks off from the George VI Ice Shelf.
- **2025** — Scientists report a thriving marine ecosystem discovered under the 2020 iceberg's former location.

## Body

### Discovery, Naming, and Sovereignty
The peninsula was first sighted in 1820 and soon charted by British explorers. Naming disputes between the US ("Palmer Peninsula") and UK ("Graham Land") were resolved in 1964 by adopting "Antarctic Peninsula," with Graham Land to the north and Palmer Land to the south. Chile and Argentina also use their own names (O'Higgins Land and Tierra de San Martín, respectively). The peninsula is part of disputed and overlapping sovereignty claims by Argentina, Chile, and the UK, none of which are internationally recognized under the Antarctic Treaty System. Argentina maintains the most bases and personnel there.

### Geology and Geography
The peninsula is a mountainous continuation of the Andes, connected via the Scotia Arc. Its bedrock consists of a string of islands joined by a grounded ice sheet. Geology records a history from the Gondwana supercontinent, with volcanic activity linked to the subduction of the Phoenix Plate. The Drake Passage opened about 30 million years ago, separating Antarctica from South America. The landscape is Antarctic tundra, with glaciers flowing into ice shelves like the Larsen, which experienced major breakup in 2002. The highest point is Hope Peak at 3,239 m.

### Climate and Climate Change
The peninsula has Antarctica's mildest climate, classified as tundra. The western coast has a maritime Antarctic climate with temperatures above 0°C for several summer months. The region is experiencing rapid warming, with average temperatures rising by 0.5°C per decade over the last 50 years. This has caused decreased sea ice, glacier retreat and acceleration, and the disintegration of seven ice shelves in the last two decades. The climate shift is displacing the cold, dry continental climate with a warmer, moister maritime one.

### Flora, Fauna, and Ecosystems
Plant life is limited to mosses, lichens, algae, and two flowering plants (Antarctic hair grass and pearlwort) in milder areas. The marine ecosystem is highly productive, supporting krill, which are a key food source for seals (like the crabeater seal) and penguins. Penguin species include chinstrap, gentoo, Adélie, and a small emperor colony. The region is also home to various seabirds and whales. Recent research has documented ecosystem responses to warming, such as shifts in penguin populations and the discovery of a thriving deep-sea community under a recently detached iceberg.

### Paleoclimate and Glaciation
Fossil evidence shows the peninsula once had subtropical forests during the Cretaceous period. Progressive cooling over the last 37 million years, linked to falling CO2 levels, led to the replacement of forests with tundra and eventually a permanent ice sheet in the early Pliocene (about 5.3–3.6 million years ago). The peninsula was likely the last part of Antarctica to become fully glaciated. During the Last Glacial Maximum (20,000–18,000 years ago), the ice sheet was thicker and extended to the continental shelf edge. Deglaciation occurred mainly between 18,000 and 6,000 years ago.

## Terms
- **Antarctic Treaty System**: An international agreement that sets aside territorial claims, bans military activity and mineral mining, and promotes scientific cooperation in Antarctica.
- **Ice Shelf**: A thick, floating platform of ice that forms where a glacier or ice sheet flows down to a coastline and onto the ocean surface.
- **Drake Passage**: The body of water between South America's Tierra del Fuego and the South Shetland Islands of Antarctica.
- **Scotia Arc**: An undersea mountain range and island arc that connects the Andes of South America to the mountains of the Antarctic Peninsula.
- **Tundra**: A biome characterized by low temperatures, short growing seasons, and the absence of trees, dominated by mosses, lichens, and low shrubs.
- **Nunatak**: An exposed, rocky peak or ridge not covered with ice or snow within an ice field or glacier.
- **Gondwana**: An ancient supercontinent that included present-day South America, Africa, Antarctica, Australia, and the Indian subcontinent.

## Debates and Open questions
- The first European to land on the continent is disputed, with 19th-century seal hunter John Davis being a likely candidate, though records are unreliable.
- The precise impact of the peninsula's glacier retreat on future global sea level rise is uncertain; the IPCC has been unable to determine the greatest potential effect.
