# Al Capone

## Overview
Al Capone was an American gangster who became the most notorious crime boss of the Prohibition era. As leader of the Chicago Outfit, he built a vast criminal empire through bootlegging and violence, but his downfall came not from murder charges but from federal tax evasion. His story illustrates the rise of organized crime in 1920s America and the eventual federal response to combat it.

- **Nov 16, 1939** — Released from prison on parole due to deteriorating health.
- **Jan 25, 1947** — Dies of cardiac arrest at his home in Palm Island, Florida.

## Body

### Early Life and Rise in Chicago
Born to Italian immigrants in Brooklyn, Capone joined the Five Points Gang as a teenager. He moved to Chicago in 1919 to work for Johnny Torrio, who ran a criminal syndicate that illegally supplied alcohol. Capone became Torrio's right-hand man. After Torrio was severely wounded in a 1925 ambush by the rival North Side Gang, he retired and handed control of the organization, later known as the Chicago Outfit, to the 26-year-old Capone.

### Reign as Boss
Capone expanded the bootlegging business through extreme violence, including bombings that killed as many as 100 people in the 1920s. He secured political protection, notably from Mayor William Hale Thompson, and cultivated a public image as a "modern-day Robin Hood" by making charitable donations. His reign was marked by a bloody feud with the North Side Gang, which culminated in the 1929 Saint Valentine's Day Massacre. This event, where seven rivals were machine-gunned in broad daylight, shocked the public and damaged Capone's image, leading newspapers to dub him "Public Enemy No. 1."

### Federal Intervention and Trial
The Saint Valentine's Day Massacre prompted publisher Walter Strong to secretly ask President Herbert Hoover for federal action. This led to a multi-agency attack on Capone, including the deployment of incorruptible Prohibition agents like Eliot Ness. The key legal strategy, pioneered by Assistant Attorney General Mabel Walker Willebrandt, was to prosecute gangsters for tax evasion on their illegally earned income. A crucial piece of evidence was a letter from Capone's lawyer admitting his taxable income for certain years. In 1931, Capone was convicted on five counts of tax evasion and sentenced to 11 years in federal prison.

### Imprisonment and Death
Capone served time at Atlanta Penitentiary and then Alcatraz, where his health declined due to neurosyphilis, a complication of syphilis he likely contracted in his youth. He was paroled in 1939 due to his diminished mental capacity. He spent his final years at his Florida home, dying in 1947 at age 48. His death marked the end of an era, though the Chicago Outfit continued under new leadership.

## Terms
*   **Bootlegging:** The illegal manufacture, transportation, or sale of alcoholic beverages during Prohibition.
*   **Chicago Outfit:** The criminal syndicate based in Chicago that Capone led from 1925 to 1931.
*   **North Side Gang:** A rival Irish-American gang in Chicago, led successively by Dean O'Banion, Hymie Weiss, and Bugs Moran, which fought Capone for control of the city's criminal rackets.
*   **Prohibition:** The nationwide constitutional ban on the production, importation, transportation, and sale of alcoholic beverages from 1920 to 1933.
*   **Saint Valentine's Day Massacre:** The 1929 murder of seven members of the North Side Gang, widely attributed to Capone's organization.
*   **Tax Evasion:** The illegal non-payment or under-payment of taxes. Federal authorities used this charge to successfully prosecute Capone.
*   **Unione Siciliana:** A Sicilian-American benevolent society that was heavily infiltrated and corrupted by gangsters, including Capone's allies.
*   **Neurosyphilis:** A late-stage complication of syphilis that affects the brain and nervous system, leading to cognitive decline and death.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Direct Involvement in Crimes:** While Capone was widely assumed to have ordered the Saint Valentine's Day Massacre, he was at his Florida home at the time. Historians debate the extent of his direct involvement in specific acts of violence versus his role as an organizer.
*   **The Baseball Bat Story:** A famous tale claims Capone beat three traitorous henchmen to death with a baseball bat. Historians like Deirdre Bair question its veracity, while biographers Max Allan Collins and A. Brad Schwartz suggest the story, reported in contemporary press, may have been deliberately spread by the Outfit to enhance Capone's fearsome reputation.
*   **The "Secret Six":** Capone blamed his downfall on a vigilante group called the Secret Six. While they aided federal efforts by gathering intelligence, their direct contribution to the tax evasion case is debated, and they were later brought down by scandal.
