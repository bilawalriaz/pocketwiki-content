# Zaragoza

## Overview
Zaragoza is the capital of Aragon, Spain, and its fourth-largest city. Founded by Romans as Caesaraugusta, it became a major Moorish capital and later the heart of the Kingdom of Aragon. Its strategic location in the Ebro valley has made it a crossroads of cultures, economies, and conflicts for over two millennia.

## Body

### Roman and Medieval Foundations
The city began as the Iberian settlement of Salduie. The Romans founded Caesaraugusta between 25 and 11 BC as a veteran colony, establishing it as an important economic center with typical public buildings. After the Gothic conquest in the 5th century, the city saw little destruction. Following the Umayyad conquest, Zaragoza became the capital of the Upper March. After the collapse of the Caliphate of Córdoba, it flourished as the independent Taifa of Zaragoza, especially under the Banu Hud dynasty in the 11th century. It fell to the Almoravids in 1110 before its Christian conquest.

### Christian Conquest and Jewish Community
Alfonso I of Aragon captured Zaragoza on 18 December 1118, making it the capital of his kingdom. A significant Jewish community thrived in the medieval city, known for its yeshivas that studied philosophy alongside the Talmud. Notable figures included physicians and grammarians like Jonah ibn Janah. The community was spared the 1391 pogroms but was ultimately expelled following the Crown's 1492 edict, with most departing by late July of that year.

### Early Modern and Modern History
The city was decimated by plague in 1564. During the War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714), Zaragoza initially supported the Habsburg claimant but was taken by Bourbon forces in 1707, leading to the abolition of Aragonese institutions. It suffered two brutal sieges during the Peninsular War (1808–1809). The 1936 military coup succeeded easily, making it a key Francoist industrial center. Post-war development included a U.S. air base (until 1992) and a major General Motors factory (1982), which spurred rapid population growth.

### Geography and Climate
Zaragoza lies in the arid Ebro valley, at the confluence of the Ebro, Huerva, and Gállego rivers. Its municipality is the ninth largest in Spain by area. The climate is semi-arid (Köppen: BSk), with low annual precipitation (328 mm), hot summers, and cold winters often influenced by the northwest Cierzo wind.

### Economy and Transport
The economy is anchored by the automotive industry (Opel), household appliances (Balay), and railway manufacturing (CAF). The city is a major logistics hub, with the Plataforma Logística de Zaragoza (PLAZA) and a high-traffic cargo airport. It is well-connected by high-speed rail (AVE), with Madrid reachable in 75 minutes and Barcelona in about 90 minutes.

### Culture and Landmarks
Zaragoza is famous for the Basílica del Pilar, La Seo Cathedral, and the Aljafería Palace. The Mudéjar Architecture of Aragon, including several city churches, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The annual Fiestas del Pilar, centered on 12 October, is one of Spain's most celebrated festivals, featuring the iconic Flower Offering to St. Mary.

## Terms
- **Taifa**: An independent Muslim-ruled principality in the Iber Peninsula, especially after the collapse of the Caliphate of Córdoba.
- **Upper March**: A frontier province of the Caliphate of Córdoba, with Zaragoza as its capital.
- **Mudéjar**: A style of architecture using Islamic decorative motifs and techniques in Christian buildings, common in medieval Spain.
- **Nueva Planta**: The decrees issued by Philip V after 1707 that abolished the traditional laws and institutions of the Crown of Aragon.
- **AVE**: Spain's high-speed rail service, connecting major cities like Madrid, Barcelona, and Zaragoza.
- **Cierzo**: A cold, dry northwest wind that blows through the Ebro valley.
- **Köppen climate classification (BSk)**: A semi-arid or steppe climate, characterized by low precipitation.
- **Expo 2008**: A world's fair held in Zaragoza with the theme "Water and Sustainable Development."
