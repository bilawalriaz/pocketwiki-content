# São Paulo (state)

## Overview
São Paulo is the most populous and economically powerful state in Brazil, located in the Southeast Region. Named after Saint Paul of Tarsus, it is a major industrial, scientific, and agricultural hub, often called the "locomotive of Brazil" for its outsized contribution to the national GDP. Its history is defined by bandeirante exploration, coffee-driven growth, massive immigration, and rapid industrialization, making it a diverse and dynamic region.

## Timeline
- **c. 12,000 BC** — Region inhabited by Indigenous peoples.
- **1532** — Martim Afonso de Sousa establishes the first permanent Portuguese settlement, São Vicente.
- **1554** — Jesuit mission São Paulo dos Campos de Piratininga is founded, the origin of the capital city.
- **17th Century** — Paulista bandeirantes intensify interior exploration, expanding Portuguese territory.
- **1822** — Brazilian independence declared near São Paulo; the state becomes a major coffee producer.
- **1888** — Brazil abolishes slavery; European immigration to São Paulo accelerates.
- **1891** — New federal constitution; São Paulo's coffee elite forms a power-sharing "café com leite" deal with Minas Gerais.
- **1932** — Constitutionalist Revolution: São Paulo rebels against the federal government of Getúlio Vargas.
- **1934** — University of São Paulo (USP) is founded.
- **1945** — São Paulo becomes the largest industrial center in South America.
- **2022** — State population reaches 44.4 million, with a GDP accounting for ~31.2% of Brazil's total.

## Body

### Early History and Colonial Expansion
The area was inhabited by Amerindian peoples for millennia before Portuguese and Spanish explorers arrived in the early 16th century. The first permanent Portuguese settlement, São Vicente, was established in 1532. The capital's origins trace to a Jesuit mission founded in 1554. In the 17th century, *bandeirantes* (explorers) from São Paulo penetrated the interior, searching for enslaved people and mineral wealth. Their expeditions greatly expanded the territorial domain of the Portuguese Empire in South America, earning the state the nickname "Bandeirante State."

### Coffee, Immigration, and Industrialization
After Brazilian independence in 1822, São Paulo became a major coffee producer, creating a powerful rural oligarchy. The end of slavery in 1888 and government incentives led to massive European immigration, particularly from Italy, Portugal, and Spain, and later from Japan. By 1893, foreigners made up over 55% of the population. This influx provided labor for coffee plantations and a market for new industries. Under President Getúlio Vargas (1930–1945), the state pioneered industrialization, diversifying into textiles, food processing, and later automotive and aviation manufacturing. By 1945, it was South America's largest industrial center.

### Modern Economy and Society
Today, São Paulo has a strong, diversified economy with the highest GDP among Brazilian states. It is the country's largest industrial, scientific, and agricultural producer, leading in orange juice, sugar, and ethanol. The state is a major tourist destination, featuring UNESCO World Heritage sites, a vibrant cultural scene, and diverse natural landscapes. It has high social indices, including the second-highest Human Development Index (HDI) in Brazil and the lowest homicide rate. The population is highly diverse, descended from Portuguese colonizers, Indigenous peoples, Africans, and waves of immigrants from across the globe.

## Terms
- **Bandeirantes**: 17th-century explorers from São Paulo who ventured into the Brazilian interior, initially to capture Indigenous people for enslavement and later to search for mineral wealth, significantly expanding Portuguese territory.
- **Café com leite (coffee-and-milk)**: A political arrangement in the early Brazilian Republic where the coffee-producing elite of São Paulo and the dairy-rich elite of Minas Gerais alternated control of the federal presidency.
- **Constitutionalist Revolution (1932)**: An uprising by the population of São Paulo against the federal government of Getúlio Vargas, primarily demanding a new constitution. It was militarily defeated but led to political concessions.
- **Estado Novo**: The authoritarian regime established by Getúlio Vargas in 1937, which centralized power and lasted until 1945.
- **HDI (Human Development Index)**: A composite statistic of life expectancy, education, and per capita income indicators, used to rank regions by level of human development.
- **Import substitution**: An economic policy adopted in Brazil (and elsewhere) to reduce foreign dependency by fostering domestic industry through tariffs and other protections.
- **MMDC**: An acronym for the initials of four students (Martins, Miragaia, Dráusio, and Camargo) killed by government troops in May 1932, whose deaths sparked the Constitutionalist Revolution.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The role and legacy of the *bandeirantes* are debated; they are remembered both for their atrocities in enslaving Indigenous people and for their role in exploring and expanding Brazil's borders.
- The long-term economic and social consequences of the "café com leite" political dominance and the state's rapid, immigration-fueled industrialization are subjects of ongoing historical analysis.
- The causes and effects of the dramatic drop in São Paulo's homicide rate since the late 1990s (from 39.7 to 6.27 per 100,000 inhabitants by 2019) are studied for potential lessons in public security.
