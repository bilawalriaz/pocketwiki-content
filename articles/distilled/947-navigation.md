# Navigation

## Overview
Navigation is the science and practice of determining position and directing the movement of vehicles—ships, aircraft, land vehicles, and spacecraft—from one place to another. It encompasses four domains: marine, land, aeronautic, and space navigation, and relies on positioning relative to known references using instruments, charts, and increasingly electronic systems. The field has evolved from early open-ocean wayfinding using stars and wave patterns to modern satellite-based global positioning, fundamentally shaping exploration, trade, and transportation.

## Body

### Historical Development
Polynesian navigation represents the earliest known form of open-ocean navigation, relying on memory, observation of stars, wave patterns, and wildlife behavior. The Chinese compass, originating during the Han dynasty (c. 206 BC), revolutionized navigation when adopted for maritime use by the Song dynasty in the 11th century. The first usage of a compass recorded in Western Europe and the Islamic world occurred around 1190. By the late medieval period, instruments like the mariner’s astrolabe and quadrant enabled more precise celestial measurements. The Age of Discovery (15th century) saw systematic exploration powered by these tools, with Portuguese and Spanish navigators charting new sea routes across the globe. The first cross-staff used in navigation was known from the 14th century onwards, and its disadvantages were overcome with the invention of the backstaff in 1595 by John Davis.

### Core Concepts and Instruments
Latitude and longitude form the basis of geographic positioning. Latitude is measured as angular distance from the equator, traditionally determined by observing Polaris. Longitude, measured east or west of the prime meridian, historically posed greater challenges due to the need for accurate timekeeping. The marine chronometer, perfected by John Harrison in the 18th century, solved this problem by enabling precise time-stamping of celestial observations. The sextant became the primary tool for measuring angular distances between celestial bodies and the horizon, forming the backbone of celestial navigation. Various methods for calculation with sextant and chronometer evolved over time, beginning with the Duller method (1728), the Douwes method (1821), the Sumner method (1837), modified by Henry Raper (1844), and the Marc St Hilaire or intercept method (1877).

### Modern Navigation Systems
Electronic navigation emerged in the 20th century with radio-navigation systems like LORAN-C and Decca, alongside OMEGA, which was approved for development in 1968 and terminated on September 30, 1997. These were later superseded by satellite-based Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS). The U.S. GPS, operational since the late 20th century, provides global coverage with accuracy between 1 and 10 metres. The cost of maintaining the system is approximately US$750 million per year. Other systems include Russia’s GLONASS, the EU’s Galileo, and China’s BeiDou. Inertial navigation systems (INS), with the first inertial system considered to be the V-2 guidance system deployed by the Germans in 1942, compute position from motion sensors without external references and remain critical in submarines and long-range missiles. Integrated bridge systems on modern ships combine radar, ECDIS, AIS, and GNSS into unified platforms supporting autonomous-ready operations.

### Navigation Processes and Practices
Passage planning involves four stages—appraisal, planning, execution, and monitoring—as specified in International Maritime Organization Resolution A.893(21), Guidelines For Voyage Planning, and includes fifty elements of a comprehensive passage plan. It is legally the captain’s responsibility. Traditional celestial navigation includes daily routines such as maintaining dead reckoning plots, taking star and sun sights at morning and evening twilight, determining compass errors by azimuth observations, and computing noon meridian observations. Land navigation uses maps, compasses, and GNSS receivers, while pedestrian navigation involves orienteering and wayfinding. Underwater navigation relies on acoustic positioning systems like ultra-short baseline (USBL) for submarines and ROVs. Artificial intelligence is increasingly used to assist in position fixing, route optimization, and decision-making.

### Spatial Cognition and Wayfinding
Navigation also involves cognitive processes where humans form mental maps using egocentric and allocentric frames of reference. According to Dan Montello's space classification, there are four levels of space with the third being the environmental space. Barbara Tversky proposed a fourfold classification of navigable space: space of the body, space around the body, space of navigation and space of graphics. Wayfinding is categorized into aided (using maps, GPS) and unaided (relying on environmental cues) types. Directed wayfinding includes path following, path finding, path search, and path planning, depending on familiarity with the environment and destination. These cognitive models help explain how people navigate complex spaces like cities and unfamiliar terrain.

## Terms
- **Dead reckoning**: Estimating position based on course, speed, time, and distance traveled since the last known position.
- **Line of position (LOP)**: A line representing possible positions of a vessel derived from a single observation.
- **Sextant**: An optical instrument for measuring the angle between two visible objects, used in celestial navigation.
- **Marine chronometer**: A precision timepiece used aboard ships to provide accurate time for celestial observations.
- **GNSS (Global Navigation Satellite System)**: A network of satellites providing autonomous geo-spatial positioning with global coverage.
- **Inertial navigation system (INS)**: A dead reckoning system that computes position from motion sensors without external references.
- **Rhumb line (loxodrome)**: A path crossing all meridians at the same angle, appearing as a straight line on Mercator charts.
- **ECDIS (Electronic Chart Display and Information System)**: A navigation information system that displays electronic charts and provides position fixing.
- **Parallel indexing**: A radar technique using a line parallel to the ship’s course to maintain safe distance from hazards.
- **Celestial fix**: A position determined by the intersection of lines of position obtained from celestial observations.

## Debates and Open Questions
The increasing reliance on GNSS has sparked renewed interest in resilient navigation methods due to vulnerabilities from jamming and spoofing. There is ongoing debate about balancing automation with traditional skills, as over-reliance on technology may degrade navigators’ ability to operate independently. Additionally, the integration of AI in navigation raises questions about decision-making accountability and system reliability in critical situations. The future of autonomous maritime navigation also presents unresolved challenges in standardization, safety protocols, and human-machine interaction.
