# Chemical bond

## Overview
A chemical bond is the association of atoms or ions that forms molecules, crystals, and other structures, determining the structure and properties of matter. Bonds arise from electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions (ionic bonds), the sharing of electrons (covalent bonds), or the delocalization of electrons over a metal lattice (metallic bonds). All bonding is fundamentally described by quantum theory, though simplified models like the octet rule and VSEPR theory allow chemists to predict bond strength, directionality, and polarity. Strong (primary) bonds hold atoms together within molecules or lattices, while weak (secondary) intermolecular forces govern bulk physical properties like melting points.

- **Early 19th century** — Humphry Davy uses the voltaic pile to decompose compounds, suggesting a link between chemical bonding and electricity.

- **1904** — Hantaro Nagaoka proposes a planetary model of the atom with a massive positive center and revolving electrons.
- **1911** — Ernest Rutherford discovers the atomic nucleus surrounded by electrons.
- **1913** — Niels Bohr proposes a nuclear atom model with electron orbits and a model of the chemical bond involving a rotating electron ring.
- **1916** — Gilbert N. Lewis develops the concept of electron-pair bonds (single, double, triple).
- **1916** — Walther Kossel proposes a model of ionic bonding based on complete electron transfer, structured on Abegg's rule.

- **1933** — H. H. James and A. S. Coolidge calculate the dihydrogen molecule dissociation energy using explicit electron-electron distance functions, convincing the community of quantum theory's quantitative accuracy.

## Body

### Fundamental Nature of Chemical Bonds
Chemical bonds result from the attraction between negatively charged electrons and positively charged protons. When electrons are shared between two nuclei, constructive quantum mechanical wavefunction interference stabilizes the system. Bonded nuclei maintain an optimal **bond distance** that balances attractive forces (nucleus-electron) against repulsive forces (electron-electron, nucleus-nucleus). In a covalent bond, energy is released upon formation not primarily from potential energy reduction, but from a reduction in kinetic energy: electrons occupy a more spatially distributed orbital (longer de Broglie wavelength) compared to being confined near individual nuclei.

### Ionic Bonding
Ionic bonding occurs between atoms with a large electronegativity difference (typically >1.7), involving the transfer of one or more electrons from one atom to another. This creates oppositely charged ions (commonly −3e to +3e) held together by isotropic electrostatic attraction (Coulomb's law). Because attraction is non-directional, ions pack into **ionic crystals** where each ion is surrounded by oppositely charged neighbors at equal distances; no specific ion pairs exist. Ionic bonds are strong (high melting points) but brittle, as short-range forces cannot bridge cracks. In crystals like sodium cyanide (NaCN), ionic bonds exist between Na⁺ and CN⁻, while covalent bonds persist within the cyanide ion (C≡N). Upon melting or dissolving in water, ionic bonds break first, allowing ions to move freely, while covalent bonds within complex ions remain intact.

### Covalent Bonding
Covalent bonding involves the sharing of valence electrons between atoms. In **non-polar covalent bonds** (electronegativity difference 0–0.3), electrons are shared equally. In **polar covalent bonds** (difference 0.3–1.7), electrons are drawn closer to the more electronegative atom, creating a dipole moment. Bond order varies: a **single bond** shares one electron pair (sigma bond); a **double bond** shares two pairs (one sigma, one pi); a **triple bond** shares three pairs (one sigma, two pi). Quadruple bonds are rare, occurring only between certain transition metals. A **coordinate covalent (dipolar) bond** forms when both shared electrons originate from one atom (a Lewis base, electron-pair donor) donated to an empty orbital on another atom (a Lewis acid, electron-pair acceptor), common in transition metal complexes (e.g., Ag(NH₃)₂⁺). Covalent bonds are directional and anisotropic, allowing discrete molecules (e.g., CH₄) or extended network solids (e.g., diamond, quartz) with high strength and melting points.

### Metallic Bonding
In metallic bonding, atoms donate valence electrons to a delocalized "sea" of electrons permeating the metal lattice. The resulting positively charged metal cations are attracted to this electron cloud. This delocalization—an extreme case of electron sharing over a vast covalent network—explains classical metallic properties: high electrical and thermal conductivity (mobile electrons), luster (electron cloud reflects light), ductility and malleability (non-directional bonding allows planes of atoms to slide), and high tensile strength.

### Intermolecular Bonding (Weak Bonds)
Weak **intermolecular forces** act between discrete molecules or molecular units, influencing physical properties like melting and boiling points.
*   **Van der Waals forces** encompass interactions between closed-shell molecules, including Coulombic interactions between partial charges and Pauli repulsion between filled electron shells.
*   **Keesom forces** attract permanent dipoles of polar molecules.
*   **London dispersion forces** arise from transient induced dipoles in non-polar molecules.
*   **Hydrogen bonds** (A–H•••B) form when hydrogen is covalently bound to a highly electronegative atom (N, O, F), giving H a partial positive charge attracted to a lone pair on a neighboring electronegative atom B. They are responsible for the anomalously high boiling points of water and ammonia.
*   **Halogen bonds** are analogous interactions involving a halogen atom between two electronegative atoms.
At very short distances, repulsive forces between electron shells dominate.

### Theories of Chemical Bonding
*   **Pure Ionic Model:** Treats electrons as perfectly localized; described by classical electrostatics (Coulomb's law).
*   **Valence Bond (VB) Theory:** Conceptualizes bonds as localized electron pairs shared via overlap of atomic orbitals. Augmented by **orbital hybridization** (mixing orbitals to match molecular geometry) and **resonance** (delocalization over multiple structures). Spatially localized, chemically intuitive for reaction mechanisms.
*   **Molecular Orbital (MO) Theory:** Views bonding as delocalized orbitals extending over the whole molecule, constructed via **Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO)** adapted to molecular symmetry. Orbital energies are physically significant, directly linked to photoelectron spectroscopy data. More "natural" quantum mechanically.
*   **Complementarity:** VB and MO are competing but complementary frameworks; both can achieve high accuracy in principle, but differ at approximation levels. **Density Functional Theory (DFT)** has become a popular third approach for quantitative calculations.
*   **Bond Classification:** Covalent bonds are classified by symmetry: **sigma (σ) bonds** are symmetric about the internuclear axis; **pi (π) bonds** have a nodal plane containing the axis. Most bonds are intermediate **polar covalent**, blending ionic and covalent character based on electronegativity difference.

## Terms
- ****Bond distance**** — The optimal internuclear separation where attractive and repulsive forces balance.
- ****Electronegativity**** — The tendency of an atom to attract shared electrons in a chemical bond; used to estimate bond polarity and energy.
- ****Ionic bond**** — Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed by electron transfer; non-directional, forms crystal lattices.
- ****Covalent bond**** — Bond formed by sharing valence electrons between atoms; directional, exists as single, double, or triple bonds.
- ****Polar covalent bond**** — Covalent bond with significant ionic character due to unequal electron sharing (electronegativity difference 0.3–1.7).
- ****Coordinate covalent bond**** — Covalent bond where both shared electrons are donated by one atom (Lewis base) to an empty orbital on another (Lewis acid).
- ****Metallic bond**** — Bonding via a delocalized "sea" of electrons over a lattice of metal cations; non-directional, confers conductivity and malleability.
- ****Valence Bond (VB) Theory**** — Model describing bonds as localized electron pairs from overlapping atomic orbitals; uses hybridization and resonance.
- ****Molecular Orbital (MO) Theory**** — Model describing bonding via delocalized orbitals spanning the molecule, formed by Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO).
- ****Hydrogen bond**** — Strong dipole-dipole interaction (A–H•••B) where H is bound to N, O, or F and attracted to a lone pair on B; governs water's properties.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Continuum of bond types:** The source notes that bonding behaviors "merge into each other seamlessly" with "no clear line" between ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding, making strict classification arbitrary despite its utility.
*   **VB vs. MO Theory:** These frameworks offer competing but complementary insights. At lower levels of approximation, they yield different results; one may be better suited for specific systems or properties, but both can theoretically reach any desired accuracy.
*   **Quantitative vs. Qualitative Trade-off:** Early quantum calculations (Burrau, Heitler-London, James-Coolidge) proved quantum theory's quantitative correctness for simple systems, but mathematical complexity prevents exact analytic solutions for multi-electron molecules, necessitating approximate methods (VB, MO, DFT) whose relative computational efficiency and accuracy remain active considerations.
