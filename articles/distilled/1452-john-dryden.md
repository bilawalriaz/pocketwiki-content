# John Dryden

## Overview
John Dryden (1631–1700) was the dominant literary figure of Restoration England, serving as the first official Poet Laureate (1668) and historiographer royal (1670). He mastered the heroic couplet, establishing it as the standard English poetic form for the next century, and excelled across genres: satiric verse (*Absalom and Achitophel*, *Mac Flecknoe*), heroic tragedy (*All for Love*), literary criticism (*Of Dramatick Poesie*), and translation (Virgil’s *Aeneid*). A pragmatic writer who earned his living from publishers and the public rather than aristocratic patrons, Dryden navigated violent political shifts—from Puritan upbringing to Cromwell’s service, Royalist panegyrist, and finally Catholic convert under James II—losing his offices at the 1688 Glorious Revolution but cementing a legacy that shaped Alexander Pope, Samuel Johnson, and the very diction of 18th-century poetry.

## Body

### Education and Early Political Formation
Dryden’s schooling at Westminster (1644–50) under royalist headmaster Richard Busby instilled rhetorical discipline—weekly translations and *dissoi logoi* (arguing both sides of a question)—that shaped his lifelong dialectical method. The curriculum’s high Anglican royalism contrasted with his Puritan gentry family (grandfather Sir Erasmus Dryden, cousin of Jonathan Swift). At Trinity College, Cambridge (1650–54), the Puritan Master Thomas Hill restored his childhood ethos; he graduated top of his class. His first published poem (1649), an elegy for schoolmate Henry, Lord Hastings, cautiously referenced Charles I’s execution nearby, foreshadowing his ability to navigate dangerous politics.

### From Cromwell to the Restoration
Returning to London during the Protectorate, Dryden worked for Secretary of State John Thurloe, likely through cousin Sir Gilbert Pickering. He walked in Cromwell’s funeral procession (1658) alongside Milton and Marvell, then published *Heroic Stanzas* (1659), a restrained eulogy. With the 1660 Restoration, he pivoted decisively: *Astraea Redux* cast the Interregnum as chaos and Charles II as restorer of order. Two further panegyrics (1662) sought patronage, but he ultimately earned his living writing for booksellers and the public—a professional model that defined his career.

### Theatrical Career and Critical Theory
The 1660 theatre reopening made playwriting Dryden’s primary income (1660s–70s). After a shaky start (*The Wild Gallant*, 1663), he contracted to supply three plays yearly to the King’s Company, becoming a shareholder. He led Restoration comedy (*Marriage à la Mode*, 1673) and heroic tragedy (*Aureng-zebe*, 1675; *All for Love*, 1678), yet grew dissatisfied, feeling his talents wasted on “unworthy audiences.” During the 1665 plague closure he wrote *Of Dramatick Poesie* (1668), a dialogue among four contemporaries (Dryden as “Neander”) debating classical, French, and English drama. The essay exemplifies his independent, wide-reading criticism: he defended his own practice, questioned rhyme in serious drama (prologue to *Aureng-zebe*), and moved toward blank verse in *All for Love*.

### Satire, Religion, and Political Crisis
*Annus Mirabilis* (1667), a modern epic in pentameter quatrains on the Dutch War and Great Fire, secured the Laureateship. His greatest achievements came in satiric verse: *Mac Flecknoe* (1682), a mock-heroic assault on rival Thomas Shadwell; *Absalom and Achitophel* (1681) and *The Medal* (1682), allegorizing the Exclusion Crisis. *Religio Laici* (1682) defended Anglicanism; his 1683 *Plutarch’s Lives* introduced “biography” to English. In 1687 he converted to Catholicism, publishing *The Hind and the Panther* to celebrate. When James II fell in the 1688 Glorious Revolution, Dryden refused the new oaths, lost the Laureateship to Shadwell, and forfeited public offices.

### Translation as Vocation
Freed from court, Dryden turned to translation, finding it “far more satisfying than writing for the stage.” He rendered Horace, Juvenal, Ovid, Lucretius, Theocritus, and—most ambitiously—Virgil’s *Aeneid* (1697), published by subscription as a national event earning £1,400. He expanded Virgil’s ~10,000 lines into 13,700 English couplets, arguing Latin’s concision required amplification for English clarity and emotion. His method—“not so streight as Metaphrase, nor so loose as Paraphrase”—aimed at presumed authorial intent and smooth English rhythm. *Fables Ancient and Modern* (1700) added Homer, Ovid, Boccaccio, and modernized Chaucer.

### Poetic Style and the Heroic Couplet
Dryden established the heroic couplet as the dominant English verse form. He sought “precise and concentrated” expression, recreating natural speech rhythms within formal structures. In his *Religio Laici* preface he distinguished verse for instruction (“plain and natural, yet majestic”) from verse for passion (“florid, elevated”). He introduced alexandrines and triplets for variety, creating what Auden called “the master of the middle style”—a poetic diction that modeled 18th-century poetry. Johnson credited him with refining the language, improving sentiments, and tuning the numbers of English verse.

### The Preposition-Stranding Rule
In 1672 Dryden objected to Ben Jonson’s phrase “The bodies that those souls were frighted from,” creating the proscription against ending English sentences with prepositions. He often translated his drafts into Latin to test concision and elegance, then back-translated using Latin grammar; since Latin forbids terminal prepositions, he imposed the rule on English—a convention adopted by later writers.

## Terms
- ****Heroic couplet**** — A pair of rhyming iambic pentameter lines; Dryden standardized it as the principal English poetic form for satire, epic, and drama.
- ****Poet Laureate**** — Official court poet appointed by the monarch; Dryden was the first (1668), obliged to produce annual occasional verse for state events.
- ****Restoration comedy**** — Witty, sexually explicit comedy of manners performed after theatres reopened in 1660; Dryden’s *Marriage à la Mode* is a key example.
- ****Mock-heroic**** — Satiric mode applying epic grandeur to trivial subjects; *Mac Flecknoe* elevates the mediocre playwright Shadwell to a ridiculous “king” of dullness.
- ****Metaphrase / Paraphrase**** — Dryden’s translation terms: *metaphrase* = word-for-word literal rendering; *paraphrase* = free adaptation. He aimed between them.
- ****Alexandrine**** — A twelve-syllable iambic line (six feet); Dryden mixed these with pentameter couplets for rhythmic variety.
- ****Occasional verse**** — Poetry written for a specific public event (coronation, victory, death); Dryden’s nondramatic poems are largely occasional.
- ****Exclusion Crisis**** — 1679–81 political struggle to bar Catholic James, Duke of York, from succession; allegorized in *Absalom and Achitophel*.
- ****Glorious Revolution**** — 1688 deposition of James II and accession of William and Mary; Dryden’s refusal of allegiance oaths cost him the Laureateship.
- ****Preposition stranding**** — Ending a clause with a preposition (e.g., “frighted from”); Dryden invented the prescriptive ban against it in 1672.

## Debates and open questions
- **Sincerity of religious conversion**: Scholars dispute whether Dryden’s 1687 Catholicism was genuine conviction or political opportunism under James II.
- **Translation fidelity vs. domestication**: Critics (e.g., Mark Van Doren) accuse Dryden of padding Virgil; defenders cite his explicit theory that Latin concision *requires* English expansion for equivalent effect.
- **Literary reputation trajectory**: Wordsworth and Matthew Arnold dismissed Dryden as lacking “sensibility” and being “classics of our prose,” while T. S. Eliot hailed him as “the ancestor of nearly all that is best in the poetry of the eighteenth century” yet accused him of a “commonplace mind.”
- **Marriage and personal life**: Little evidence survives about the Drydens’ domestic relationship; Lady Elizabeth’s reported mental decline after his death and the childlessness of their three sons leave biographical gaps.
- **Authorship of the preposition rule**: Dryden’s 1672 objection to Jonson is the earliest recorded instance, but whether he *invented* the rule or codified an existing preference remains debated.
