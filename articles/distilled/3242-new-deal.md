# New Deal

## Overview
The New Deal was a series of economic, social, and political reforms enacted between 1933 and 1938 under President Franklin D. Roosevelt in response to the Great Depression. It represented a massive expansion of the federal government's role in the economy and society, aiming to provide relief for the unemployed, promote economic recovery, and enact lasting reforms to prevent future depressions. Its legacy is a permanent shift in American political and economic life, establishing a new role for federal intervention and creating a political coalition that dominated for decades.

## Timeline
- **1929–1933** — The Great Depression causes catastrophic economic collapse: manufacturing output falls by one-third, unemployment rises to 25%, and thousands of banks fail.
- **Nov. 1932** — Franklin D. Roosevelt wins the presidential election in a landslide over Herbert Hoover.
- **Mar. 1933** — Roosevelt takes office; declares a national bank holiday and sends the Emergency Banking Act to Congress.
- **1933–1934** — The "First New Deal" focuses on immediate relief and recovery, creating agencies like the CCC, AAA, NRA, and TVA.
- **1935–1938** — The "Second New Deal" focuses on longer-term reforms, creating the WPA, Social Security Act, and Wagner Act.
- **May 1935** — The Supreme Court declares the National Recovery Administration (NRA) unconstitutional.
- **1936** — The Supreme Court declares the Agricultural Adjustment Act (AAA) unconstitutional.
- **1937** — Roosevelt's "court-packing plan" fails in Congress.
- **1937–1938** — A sharp economic recession occurs, with unemployment jumping from 14.3% to 19.0%.
- **1938** — Republicans gain seats in Congress, forming a conservative coalition that blocks further New Deal legislation.
- **Dec. 1941** — U.S. entry into World War II finally achieves full employment through massive war spending.

## Body

### Origins and the First New Deal (1933–1934)
The New Deal was a response to the unprecedented economic crisis of the Great Depression, which saw bank failures, deflation, and mass unemployment. Roosevelt, influenced by Keynesian ideas of aggregate demand, argued for massive government intervention. During his first "Hundred Days," Congress passed a flurry of legislation. The **Emergency Banking Act** and the **Glass-Steagall Act** (creating the FDIC) stabilized the financial system. The **Agricultural Adjustment Administration (AAA)** sought to raise farm prices by paying farmers to reduce production. The **National Recovery Administration (NRA)** attempted to eliminate "cut-throat competition" by letting industries create "codes of fair competition." Public works programs like the **Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC)** and the **Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA)** provided jobs and developed infrastructure.

### The Second New Deal (1935–1938)
By 1935, with unemployment still high, the "Second New Deal" shifted focus to longer-term social and labor reforms. The **Social Security Act** established a permanent system of old-age pensions, unemployment insurance, and welfare for the disabled and dependent children. The **National Labor Relations Act (Wagner Act)** guaranteed workers the right to organize unions and bargain collectively. The **Works Progress Administration (WPA)** became the largest New Deal agency, employing millions to build public infrastructure and fund arts projects. The **Fair Labor Standards Act** of 1938 established a national minimum wage, a 40-hour workweek, and banned most child labor.

### Political Realignment and Legacy
The New Deal produced a major political realignment, creating the **New Deal coalition** of labor unions, urban ethnic minorities, African Americans, white Southerners, and intellectuals that made the Democratic Party dominant in national politics until the 1960s. An opposing conservative coalition largely controlled Congress from 1939 onward. The New Deal dramatically increased the power of the federal government and established the presidency as the center of authority. While it did not end the Depression—full employment was only achieved with World War II—it restored public confidence and created a lasting framework for federal social and economic policy.

## Terms
*   **New Deal:** The series of programs, public work projects, financial reforms, and regulations enacted by President Franklin D. Roosevelt between 1933 and 1938.
*   **Great Depression:** The severe worldwide economic depression that took place mostly during the 1930s, beginning in the United States.
*   **Keynesian Economics:** An economic theory advocating government intervention and deficit spending during recessions to stimulate aggregate demand and reduce unemployment.
*   **First New Deal:** The initial phase of Roosevelt's programs (1933–1934), focused on immediate relief, recovery, and financial reform.
*   **Second New Deal:** The later phase (1935–1938), which focused on more progressive social and labor reforms like Social Security and the Wagner Act.
*   **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC):** A government corporation created by the Glass-Steagall Act that insures bank deposits to prevent bank runs.
*   **Agricultural Adjustment Administration (AAA):** A New Deal agency that paid farmers subsidies to reduce crop production in order to raise agricultural prices.
*   **Works Progress Administration (WPA):** The largest New Deal agency, employing millions of jobseekers to carry out public works projects, including arts and literacy programs.
*   **Social Security Act:** A landmark 1935 law that established a system of old-age benefits for workers, benefits for victims of industrial accidents, unemployment insurance, and aid for dependent mothers and children.
*   **New Deal Coalition:** The alignment of voting blocs that supported the Democratic Party from the 1930s to the 1960s, including labor unions, minorities, and Southern whites.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians and economists continue to debate the New Deal's effectiveness. **Keynesians** argue it was a necessary intervention that halted the collapse but lacked sufficient deficit spending to fully end the Depression. **Monetarists**, like Milton Friedman, contend that the banking and monetary reforms (e.g., leaving the gold standard) were the crucial recovery factors. Some **conservative economists** argue New Deal policies like the NRA and AAA, which fostered cartels and restricted production, actually prolonged the Depression. **New Left historians** criticize the New Deal for being too favorable to capitalism and failing to address systemic inequalities, particularly for African Americans and women. A major scholarly debate concerns its impact on federalism: did it exacerbate tensions between federal and state governments, or did it create a cooperative system where states retained control over funding allocation?
