# Cardinal Mazarin

## Overview
Jules Mazarin (1602–1661) was an Italian cardinal and diplomat who became the chief minister of France under Louis XIII and the young Louis XIV. He succeeded Cardinal Richelieu, guiding France through the final stages of the Thirty Years' War and the internal rebellion of the Fronde. His diplomacy established France as the dominant European power and laid the groundwork for the modern state system through the Peace of Westphalia.

- **1630** — Achieves first major diplomatic success mediating the War of the Mantuan Succession.
- **1640** — Moves to Paris to enter the service of Cardinal Richelieu.
- **1641** — Made a cardinal.
- **4 December 1642** — Death of Richelieu; Mazarin becomes chief minister to Louis XIII.
- **14 May 1643** — Death of Louis XIII; Mazarin becomes chief minister to the regent Anne of Austria and the young Louis XIV.
- **1648** — The Fronde rebellion begins in Paris.
- **1648–1648** — Negotiates the Peace of Westphalia, ending the Thirty Years' War.
- **1653** — Returns triumphantly to Paris after the Fronde is suppressed.
- **7 November 1659** — Signs the Treaty of the Pyrenees with Spain, adding new provinces to France.
- **9 March 1661** — Dies in Paris; Louis XIV chooses not to appoint a new chief minister.

## Body

### Early Life and Papal Service
Born to Roman parents in Pescina, Giulio Mazzarino was educated by Jesuits and studied law in Spain. After returning to Rome, he entered papal service in 1628. He gained early fame mediating the War of the Mantuan Succession, where he brokered a last-minute peace between French and Spanish armies at Casale. This success led to the Treaty of Cherasco (1631) and brought him to the attention of Cardinal Richelieu of France, whom he first met in 1630. Mazarin served as a papal diplomat in France and Avignon, gradually aligning himself with French interests. He was made a cardinal in 1641 and entered Richelieu's service in Paris in 1640.

### Chief Minister and the Thirty Years' War
Following Richelieu's death in 1642, Mazarin became chief minister. When Louis XIII died in 1643, Mazarin served the regent Anne of Austria and the child king Louis XIV. He continued Richelieu's war against the Habsburgs, using French military victories to negotiate the Peace of Westphalia (1646–1648). This treaty ended the Thirty Years' War and established key principles of international law, including state sovereignty and legal equality among nations. Mazarin also expanded French influence in the Holy Roman Empire by settling Protestant princes in secularized territories and forming the League of the Rhine in 1658.

### The Fronde (1648–1653)
To finance the war, Mazarin imposed heavy taxes on Parisians and the nobility, sparking the Fronde rebellion in 1648. The uprising, led by the Parlement of Paris and later by nobles like the Prince de Condé, forced Mazarin and the court to flee Paris multiple times. The conflict had two phases: the Fronde of the Parliament (1648–1649) and the Fronde of the Princes (1650–1653). Mazarin skillfully manipulated the factions, eventually exiling himself to Germany while directing policy from abroad. With the help of the loyal general Turenne, Condé was defeated at the Battle of the Dunes (1658), and Mazarin returned to Paris in triumph in 1653.

### Final Diplomatic Victories and Legacy
Mazarin's final years were marked by major diplomatic successes. He negotiated the Treaty of the Pyrenees (1659), which added Roussillon, Cerdagne, and parts of the Low Countries to France. He also arranged the marriage of Louis XIV to Maria Theresa of Spain in 1660, ending the long Habsburg-Bourbon wars. As the de facto ruler for nearly two decades, Mazarin was a crucial patron of the arts, introducing Italian opera to Paris and amassing a vast art collection. He founded the Bibliothèque Mazarine, France's first public library, and the Collège des Quatre-Nations (now the Institut de France). He educated Louis XIV in practical statecraft, emphasizing dissimulation and control of passion. Upon his death in 1661, Louis XIV decided to rule without a chief minister, marking the start of personal absolutist rule.

## Terms
- **Chief Minister:** The principal minister of state, effectively the head of government, serving under the monarch.
- **Fronde:** A series of civil wars in France (1648–1653) between the crown and discontented nobles/parlements over taxation and authority.
- **Peace of Westphalia (1648):** The set of treaties ending the Thirty Years' War, establishing principles of state sovereignty and non-interference.
- **Treaty of the Pyrenees (1659):** Peace treaty between France and Spain that ended their long conflict and added territory to France.
- **League of the Rhine (1658):** A defensive alliance of German princes with France, designed to check Habsburg power in the Holy Roman Empire.
- **Parlement of Paris:** A high court of appeals and a political body that could register (approve) royal edicts, giving it a voice in governance.
- **Jansenism:** A Catholic theological movement emphasizing original sin and predestination, considered heretical by the papacy and opposed by Mazarin for its political implications.
- **Bibliothèque Mazarine:** The first public library in France, founded by Mazarin in 1643 and now part of the Institut de France.
- **Mazarinettes:** The seven nieces of Mazarin, whom he married into powerful European noble families to secure alliances.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact nature of Mazarin's early relationship with Richelieu is debated; Richelieu initially viewed him with suspicion, calling him "more Spanish and Savoyard."
- Historians debate the extent of Mazarin's personal wealth accumulation versus his service to the state; his fortune at death was immense (35 million livres).
- The role of Mazarin's Italian background and Catholic faith in shaping his policies and his conflicts with the Spanish-influenced papal court is a subject of scholarly analysis.
- The long-term impact of his financial policies, which contributed to the Fronde but also funded France's rise, is evaluated differently by historians focusing on state-building versus social unrest.
