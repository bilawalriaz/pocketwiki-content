# Imjin War

## Overview
The Imjin War (1592–1598) was a series of two Japanese invasions of Korea launched by Toyotomi Hideyoshi to conquer the Korean Peninsula and, ultimately, China. The conflict, which involved massive armies from Japan, Ming China, and Joseon Korea, ended in a military stalemate and Japanese withdrawal. It was a devastating regional war that reshaped East Asian politics, caused immense destruction in Korea, and led to significant cultural and technological transfers between the nations involved.

## Timeline
- **23 May 1592** — Japanese First Division under Konishi Yukinaga lands at Busan, beginning the first invasion.
- **12 June 1592** — Japanese forces capture the Korean capital, Hanseong (Seoul).
- **21 July 1592** — Korean Admiral Yi Sun-sin wins the Battle of Okpo, the first of many naval victories disrupting Japanese supply lines.
- **8 February 1593** — Ming Chinese forces under Li Rusong recapture Pyongyang from the Japanese.
- **26 January 1593** — Battle of Byeokjegwan: Ming advance on Hanseong is halted by a large Japanese force.
- **13 August 1592** — Decisive Korean naval victory at the Battle of Hansan Island forces a Japanese naval withdrawal.
- **1593–1596** — Truce period with failed peace negotiations between China and Japan.
- **1597** — Second Japanese invasion (Chŏngyu War) begins after talks collapse.
- **26 October 1597** — Admiral Yi Sun-sin wins the Battle of Myeongnyang with a vastly outnumbered fleet.
- **16 December 1598** — Final major naval battle at Noryang; both Yi Sun-sin and Ming commander Deng Zilong are killed.
- **Late 1598** — Death of Toyotomi Hideyoshi; Japanese forces ordered to withdraw from Korea.

## Body

### Background and Causes
By the late 16th century, Toyotomi Hideyoshi had unified Japan and sought to legitimize his rule and occupy his large samurai class by launching a continental invasion. His stated goal was the conquest of Ming China, with Korea as the necessary pathway. Diplomatic efforts failed when the Joseon court, a tributary state of Ming China, refused Japan's demand to allow safe passage for an invasion of China and rejected Hideyoshi's ultimatums. Korea, having enjoyed a long peace, underestimated Japan's military strength and unity.

### Military Balance and Command
The Japanese army was a professional force of battle-hardened soldiers, including samurai and ashigaru (conscripted infantry), armed with the tanegashima matchlock arquebus, which gave them a significant advantage in land battles. The Joseon military, though maintaining a standing structure, was poorly trained, equipped, and led due to prolonged peace and bureaucratic decay. Ming China, the regional superpower, eventually intervened with large armies, but its forces were often overstretched. At sea, the Korean navy, under the brilliant command of Admiral Yi Sun-sin, possessed superior, cannon-armed warships like the panokseon and the iconic "turtle ships," which dominated Japanese fleets reliant on boarding tactics.

### The First Invasion (1592–1593)
Japanese forces landed at Busan and advanced rapidly northward, capturing Hanseong within weeks and pushing into the northern provinces. The Korean regular army suffered a series of defeats. However, the Japanese advance was hampered by two key factors: the Korean navy's destruction of supply fleets and the widespread resistance of "righteous armies" (civilian militias). Ming intervention, culminating in the recapture of Pyongyang, forced the Japanese to consolidate in the south. A stalemate ensued, leading to a truce in 1593.

### The Second Invasion and Stalemate (1597–1598)
After peace negotiations collapsed, Japan launched a second invasion in 1597. A major Japanese naval victory at the Battle of Chilcheollyang, following the removal of Yi Sun-sin, allowed Japanese forces to advance into Jeolla Province. Yi Sun-sin was reinstated and, with only 13 ships, won the miraculous Battle of Myeongnyang, regaining naval initiative. On land, Ming and Joseon forces halted the Japanese advance at battles like Jiksan and Ulsan. The conflict settled into a stalemate in the southern coastal regions.

### Conclusion and Aftermath
Toyotomi Hideyoshi's death in 1598 led the Japanese Council of Five Elders to order a withdrawal. The final naval Battle of Noryang saw the destruction of much of the retreating Japanese fleet and the deaths of both Yi Sun-sin and Ming commander Deng Zilong. The war ended with the withdrawal of Japanese forces. The conflict devastated Korea, destroying over 60% of its farmland and causing massive civilian and military casualties. It weakened the Toyotomi clan, paving the way for Tokugawa Ieyasu's unification of Japan. Ming China was financially drained, contributing to its later collapse. Despite the destruction, the war facilitated cultural exchanges, including the introduction of arquebuses and chili peppers to Korea, and Korean pottery techniques to Japan.

## Terms
- **Arquebus (Tanegashima):** A matchlock firearm widely used by Japanese infantry, providing a key advantage in land battles.
- **Ashigaru:** Light infantry in the Japanese army, usually conscripted peasants armed with spears, bows, or arquebuses.
- **Panokseon:** The standard Korean warship, a large, multi-decked vessel armed with heavy cannons.
- **Turtle Ship (Geobukseon):** A heavily armored Korean warship, possibly with a spiked roof, used to ram and disrupt enemy formations.
- **Righteous Army (Uibyeong):** Korean civilian militias that formed to resist the Japanese invasion through guerrilla warfare.
- **Wajō:** Japanese coastal fortresses built in Korea during the second invasion to secure supply lines.
- **Tributary System:** The East Asian political order in which states acknowledged Chinese suzerainty in exchange for trade and legitimacy; Ming China intervened to protect its tributary, Joseon Korea.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact design and tactical effectiveness of the Korean "turtle ship" remain debated among historians, as no contemporary models survive.
- The primary cause of Korean defeats in early land battles is debated: whether it was the technological superiority of Japanese arquebuses, as argued by official Yu Sŏngnyong, or the poor discipline and morale of Korean troops, as suggested by other accounts.
- The strategic impact of Hideyoshi's death on the timing of the Japanese withdrawal is a point of analysis, as the Council of Five Elders kept it secret to maintain army morale.
