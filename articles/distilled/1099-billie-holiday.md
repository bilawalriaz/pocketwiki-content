# Billie Holiday

## Overview
Billie Holiday (1915–1959), born Eleanora Fagan, was an American jazz and swing singer whose innovative vocal style—modeled on jazz instrumentalists—revolutionized phrasing and tempo in popular music. Nicknamed "Lady Day" by saxophonist Lester Young, she achieved commercial success in the 1930s and 1940s with hits like "What a Little Moonlight Can Do" and "God Bless the Child," while her 1939 recording of "Strange Fruit" became a landmark protest against lynching. Her career was increasingly derailed by heroin addiction, legal persecution, and the loss of her New York City Cabaret Card (a license to perform in venues serving alcohol), which forced her into concert halls. Despite a deteriorating voice, she delivered acclaimed late-career performances at Carnegie Hall and recorded the critically praised *Lady in Satin* (1958). She died at 44 from cirrhosis and heart failure while under police guard for narcotics possession. Posthumously, she received four Grammy Awards and induction into the Grammy, Rock and Roll, and Rhythm & Blues Halls of Fame.

## Timeline
- **April 7, 1915** — Born Eleanora Fagan in Philadelphia to teenage parents Clarence Halliday and Sarah "Sadie" Fagan.
- **1925–1927** — Sent twice to House of the Good Shepherd, a Catholic reform school; traumatized by punishment involving a dead body.
- **1929** — Moves to Harlem, New York; begins singing in nightclubs.
- **November 1933** — Recording debut at age 18 with Benny Goodman ("Your Mother's Son-In-Law," "Riffin' the Scotch").
- **1935** — Signed to Brunswick by John Hammond; begins influential collaboration with pianist Teddy Wilson; appears in Duke Ellington's *Symphony in Black*.
- **1937–1938** — Tours as vocalist with Count Basie and Artie Shaw; becomes one of the first Black women to tour the segregated South with a white orchestra (Shaw).
- **April 20, 1939** — Records "Strange Fruit" for Commodore Records after Columbia refuses; song becomes a million-selling anthem against lynching.
- **1941** — Co-writes and records "God Bless the Child," her biggest hit (over a million sold).
- **August 1944** — Signs with Decca Records; records "Lover Man" with strings, a major pop crossover hit.
- **May 16, 1947** — Arrested for narcotics possession in New York; sentenced to Alderson Federal Prison Camp; loses Cabaret Card.
- **March 27, 1948** — Sold-out comeback concert at Carnegie Hall (2,700 tickets) shortly after prison release.
- **1952–1957** — Records for Verve/Clef (Norman Granz); first European tour (1954); publishes ghostwritten autobiography *Lady Sings the Blues* (1956).
- **1958** — Records final major album *Lady in Satin* with Ray Ellis orchestra.
- **March 1959** — Final European television appearance (*Chelsea at Nine* in London); final studio sessions for MGM Records.
- **May 31, 1959** — Hospitalized for liver and heart disease; handcuffed to bed by Federal Bureau of Narcotics agents.
- **July 17, 1959** — Dies at age 44 of pulmonary edema and heart failure caused by cirrhosis.

## Body

### Childhood and Early Hardship
Eleanora Fagan was born in Philadelphia to unwed teenagers Clarence Halliday (a jazz banjo player/guitarist) and Sarah "Sadie" Fagan. Her father abandoned the family early; her mother worked "transportation jobs" on railroads, leaving Holiday in Baltimore with relatives, primarily Martha Miller. Holiday’s autobiography *Lady Sings the Blues* (1956) is noted as inconsistent on early details, though largely confirmed by biographer Stuart Nicholson. At nine, truancy led to juvenile court and commitment to the House of the Good Shepherd, a Catholic reform school where nuns locked her in a room with a dead girl overnight, causing lasting trauma. Released in 1925, she worked in her mother’s restaurant, dropped out of school at 11, and in 1926 fought off a rapist, leading to a second stint at the reform school as a state witness. By 1929, she joined her mother in Harlem.

### Early Career and the Hammond/Wilson Era
In Harlem, Holiday adopted her stage name (from actress Billie Dove and her father) and sang in clubs with saxophonist Kenneth Hollan. Producer John Hammond heard her at Covan’s in 1933 and arranged her recording debut with Benny Goodman. Hammond declared she "sang like an improvising jazz genius." In 1935, Hammond signed her to Brunswick to record pop tunes with pianist Teddy Wilson for the jukebox trade. Allowed to improvise, Holiday manipulated melody to fit emotion with high skill. Their first sides included "What a Little Moonlight Can Do" (her "claim to fame") and "Miss Brown to You." Brunswick paid a flat fee, no royalties, but the records were commercial assets; "I Cried for You" sold 15,000 copies. Tenor saxophonist Lester Young, a boarder at her mother’s house, became a frequent accompanist; their rapport was so close Young said they sounded like "two of the same voices." He nicknamed her "Lady Day"; she called him "Prez."

### Big Bands, Segregation, and Artie Shaw
Late 1937 brought a brief stint with Count Basie’s band. Holiday chose her material and arrangements, cultivating a persona of a woman unlucky in love. Basie noted she "knew how she wanted to sound and you couldn't tell her what to do." She competed with Ella Fitzgerald (Chick Webb’s vocalist); a 1938 Savoy Ballroom battle saw magazines split on the winner. In early 1938, Holiday joined Artie Shaw’s orchestra, becoming one of the first Black women to tour full-time with a white band in the segregated South. Shaw defended her against racism, but she endured segregated facilities (service elevators, separate dining) and audience heckling. She recorded only one side with Shaw ("Any Old Time") due to label conflicts, though Shaw played on her 1936 Teddy Wilson sessions. She left Shaw in November 1938 after the Lincoln Hotel forced her to use the service elevator.

### "Strange Fruit" and Commodore Records
In 1939, Barney Josephson (proprietor of the integrated Café Society) introduced Holiday to "Strange Fruit," a poem by Jewish schoolteacher Abel Meeropol (pseudonym Lewis Allan) about lynching. Holiday performed it with a specific ritual: waiters silenced the crowd, lights dimmed to a single spotlight on her face, and on the final note all lights went out. Columbia deemed the subject too sensitive; Milt Gabler recorded it for Commodore on April 20, 1939. It sold a million copies (equivalent to a top-twenty hit), though Gabler credited the B-side "Fine and Mellow" for jukebox sales. Holiday said the song reminded her of her father’s death from a lung disorder after being denied medical treatment due to race. She kept it in her repertoire for 20 years.

### Commercial Peak and Decca Years
Milt Gabler became A&R for Decca and signed Holiday in August 1944. Her first session produced "Lover Man" (with strings, at her insistence), a major pop hit (#16 Pop, #5 R&B) that made her a staple in the pop market. She recorded "Don't Explain" (written after catching husband Jimmy Monroe with lipstick on his collar) and "Good Morning Heartache" (1946). In 1946, she filmed *New Orleans* with Louis Armstrong; racism and McCarthyism reduced her role, and her heroin use (supplied by lover Joe Guy) disrupted the set. By the late 1940s, critics noted a reliance on slow ballads and a lack of melodic variation.

### Legal Persecution, Prison, and Carnegie Hall
By 1947, Holiday earned $250,000 over three years and ranked second in the *DownBeat* poll. On May 16, 1947, she was arrested for narcotics possession. Her lawyer failed to appear; dehydrated and ill, she pleaded guilty and requested hospitalization. She was sentenced to Alderson Federal Prison Camp. The conviction cost her the Cabaret Card, barring her from alcohol-serving venues. (Scholarly debate exists: Johann Hari argues the Federal Bureau of Narcotics targeted her from 1939 due to "Strange Fruit"; Lewis Porter contends no federal campaign existed and Hari’s claim originated in his 2015 book.) Released early in March 1948, she agreed to a Carnegie Hall comeback concert organized by Ed Fishman. On March 27, 1948, she sang 32 songs to a sold-out house of 2,700—a record advance sale—despite having no current hit record. She fainted after the third curtain call when a hatpin in a gift of gardenias pierced her head. A subsequent Broadway show, *Holiday on Broadway*, sold out but closed in three weeks. She was arrested again in San Francisco in January 1949.

### Decline, Autobiography, and Final Recordings
The lost Cabaret Card and out-of-print records decimated her income; in 1958 she received a $11 royalty check. Her 1950s health deteriorated due to drugs, alcohol, and abusive relationships (she married mob enforcer Louis McKay in 1957). She toured Europe in 1954 and 1959. Her autobiography *Lady Sings the Blues*, ghostwritten by William Dufty, was published in 1956; scholar John Szwed (2015) argues it is generally accurate but legally censored, omitting relationships with Charles Laughton, Tallulah Bankhead, and Orson Welles. The accompanying LP featured re-recordings of hits. On November 10, 1956, she gave two celebrated Carnegie Hall concerts; narrator Gilbert Millstein described her transformation from frail, tinny-voiced rehearsal to "erect and beautiful" performer with "strength undiminished." Critic Nat Hentoff called it a night when she was "undeniably the best and most honest jazz singer alive." Her 1957 *Sound of Jazz* performance of "Fine and Mellow" with a dying Lester Young is legendary. Her final studio album, recorded for MGM in 1959 with Ray Ellis, was released as *Billie Holiday* then retitled *Last Recording*. *Lady in Satin* (1958, Columbia), also with Ellis, initially disappointed the conductor musically but moved him emotionally upon final mix.

### Illness and Death
By early 1959, Holiday had cirrhosis. She relapsed after stopping drinking. On May 31, 1959, she entered Metropolitan Hospital for liver and heart disease. Federal narcotics agents handcuffed her to the bed for possession. She received last rites July 15 and died July 17 at 3:10 a.m., age 44, of pulmonary edema and heart failure caused by cirrhosis. She died with $0.70 in the bank; her estate was $1,000. *The New York Times* ran a short, bylined obituary on page 15. Her funeral at the Church of St. Paul the Apostle was paid for by Michael P. Grace II; she was buried at Saint Raymond’s Cemetery in the Bronx.

### Artistry and Influence
Holiday’s vocal delivery was instantly recognizable. Lacking formal training, she used improvisational prowess to shape phrasing and tempo, aiming for her voice to sound like an instrument. She cited Louis Armstrong (especially the scat-clarinet duet on "West End Blues") and Bessie Smith as primary influences. She would have instrumentalists repeat improvised lines for her vocal use. Frank Sinatra called her "the greatest single musical influence on me" and "unquestionably the most important influence on American popular singing in the last twenty years" (1958).

### Scholarly Interpretations
Angela Y. Davis (*Blues Legacies and Black Feminism*) argues Holiday’s performances expressed personal autonomy and emotional independence for Black women, citing "T'ain't Nobody's Bizness If I Do" as challenging respectability politics. Davis frames "Strange Fruit" as one of the earliest and most influential musical statements against U.S. racial violence, noting Holiday persisted despite professional and legal pressure. These interpretations situate her within a tradition of African American musical protest.

## Terms
- ****Cabaret Card**** — A license required for performers to work in New York City venues that served alcohol; revoked after Holiday’s 1947 drug conviction, forcing her into concert halls.
- ****Commodore Records**** — Independent label owned by Milt Gabler; recorded Holiday’s "Strange Fruit" (1939) after Columbia refused due to the song’s anti-lynching subject matter.
- ****Improvisational phrasing**** — Holiday’s signature technique of manipulating melody and tempo spontaneously, modeled on jazz instrumentalists rather than standard vocal conventions.
- ****Lester Young ("Prez")**** — Tenor saxophonist, frequent accompanist, and close friend who nicknamed Holiday "Lady Day"; their musical rapport was described as "two of the same voices."
- ****"Strange Fruit"**** — Song based on Abel Meeropol’s poem about lynching; Holiday’s 1939 recording became a million-selling protest anthem performed with a specific theatrical ritual.
- ****Teddy Wilson**** — Pianist and bandleader; collaborated with Holiday on ~95 Brunswick/Vocalion recordings (1935–1938), establishing her early jazz standard repertoire.
- ****Artie Shaw**** — White bandleader who hired Holiday in 1938, making her one of the first Black women to tour the segregated South with a white orchestra; defended her against racism.
- ****Lady in Satin**** — Holiday’s 1958 Columbia album with a 40-piece orchestra arranged by Ray Ellis; noted for its emotional intensity despite her deteriorated voice.
- ****Federal Bureau of Narcotics (FBN)**** — Agency under Harry J. Anslinger that arrested Holiday in 1947 and 1959; debated whether they systematically targeted her for singing "Strange Fruit."
- ****God Bless the Child**** — Holiday’s 1941 co-written hit (with Arthur Herzog Jr.), inspired by a fight with her mother over money; sold over a million copies, Grammy Hall of Fame (1976).

## Debates and open questions
- **Federal targeting for "Strange Fruit":** Johann Hari (*Chasing the Scream*, 2015) argues the FBN targeted Holiday from 1939 specifically to silence her performance of "Strange Fruit." Lewis Porter disputes this, stating no evidence of a federal campaign exists prior to Hari’s book and that narcotics enforcement was the stated motive.
- **Autobiography reliability:** *Lady Sings the Blues* (1956), ghostwritten by William Dufty, is considered generally accurate by John Szwed (2015) but legally censored—omitting relationships with Charles Laughton, Tallulah Bankhead, and Orson Welles, and watering down other material.
- **Paternity:** Holiday’s birth certificate lists father as "Frank DeViese"; historians debate whether this is an error/anomaly or indicates a different father than Clarence Halliday.
- **Vocal decline vs. emotional depth:** Critics and Holiday herself noted her voice deteriorated in the 1950s (thin, limited range), yet many argue her late work (*Lady in Satin*, 1956 Carnegie Hall) achieved greater emotional profundity through phrasing and interpretive wisdom.
