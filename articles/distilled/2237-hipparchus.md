# Hipparchus

## Overview
Hipparchus (c. 190 – c. 120 BC) was a Greek astronomer, mathematician, and geographer, widely considered the greatest ancient astronomical observer and the founder of trigonometry. His work, which systematically incorporated Babylonian data and introduced quantitative geometric models, laid the foundations for later astronomy, most famously through his discovery of the precession of the equinoxes.

## Timeline
- **c. 190 BC** — Born in Nicaea, Bithynia.
- **162–127 BC** — Active period of astronomical observations.
- **c. 146 BC** — Made a key solstice observation used to calculate the tropical year.
- **141 & 139 BC** — Used lunar eclipses to verify Babylonian lunar periods.
- **127 BC** — Discovered the precession of the equinoxes by comparing his star positions with earlier records.
- **c. 120 BC** — Died on the island of Rhodes.

## Body

### Life and Legacy
Hipparchus was born in Nicaea and likely died on Rhodes, where he spent much of his later life. He is known to have worked between 162 and 127 BC. Although he wrote at least fourteen books, only his *Commentary on the Phaenomena of Eudoxus and Aratus* survives. Most knowledge of his work comes from later authors like Ptolemy, Strabo, and Pliny. He is often called the "father of astronomy."

### Mathematical Innovations
Hipparchus is recognized as the first to compile a trigonometric table, using the chord function to solve astronomical problems. This innovation allowed Greek astronomers to make precise quantitative models. He likely used a circle with a circumference of 21,600 units and a radius of 3,438 units, tabulating chords for angles in 7.5° increments. He also employed arithmetic techniques from Babylonian sources, expanding the mathematical toolkit available for astronomy and geography.

### Lunar and Solar Theory
Hipparchus developed the first geometric models for the Moon's non-uniform motion, using lunar eclipse observations to determine the parameters for both an eccentric circle and an epicycle model. For the Sun, he used equinox and solstice observations to determine that the tropical year was approximately 365¼ − 1/300 days, an estimate about 6 minutes per year longer than the modern value. He also modeled the Sun's apparent motion by placing the Earth off-center from the Sun's circular orbit.

### Distances and Sizes
In a lost work, *On Sizes and Distances*, Hipparchus used solar eclipse observations and lunar parallax to calculate the Moon's distance. He found its least distance to be 71 Earth radii and its greatest 83, while another method gave a mean distance of about 67⅓ Earth radii. He also estimated the Sun's distance and the relative sizes of the Sun, Earth, and Moon.

### Star Catalog and Precession
Late in his career, Hipparchus compiled the first comprehensive Western star catalog of about 850 stars, likely using an armillary sphere or similar instrument. He may have introduced the stellar magnitude system. His most famous discovery, the precession of the equinoxes, came from comparing his star positions with those of Timocharis and Aristillus about 150 years earlier, finding that Spica had shifted 2° relative to the equinox. He concluded the precession rate was at least 1° per century.

### Geography
Hipparchus critiqued the geographer Eratosthenes, insisting that geography must be based on astronomical measurements. He improved methods for determining latitude from star observations and proposed using simultaneous lunar eclipse observations to find longitude. He also argued against the idea that the Atlantic, Indian, and Caspian seas were connected.

## Terms
- **Precession of the equinoxes**: The slow westward shift of the equinoctial points along the ecliptic relative to the fixed stars.
- **Trigonometry**: The branch of mathematics dealing with the relationships between the sides and angles of triangles.
- **Chord function**: A trigonometric function used by Hipparchus, giving the length of the straight line subtended by a central angle in a circle.
- **Tropical year**: The time it takes for the Sun to return to the same position relative to the equinoxes (approx. 365.2422 days).
- **Sidereal year**: The time it takes for the Sun to return to the same position relative to the fixed stars (approx. 365.25636 days).
- **Parallax**: The apparent displacement of an object when viewed from different positions.
- **Deferent and epicycle**: Geometric models where a planet moves uniformly on a small circle (epicycle) whose center moves uniformly on a larger circle (deferent).
- **Stellar magnitude**: A system for ranking the brightness of stars, traditionally from 1 (brightest) to 6 (faintest).

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact source and direction of transmission of certain Babylonian astronomical periods (like the long draconitic month) between Hipparchus and Babylonian astronomers is debated.
- The precise instrument(s) Hipparchus used for his star catalog observations is unknown; it may have involved a mix of coordinate systems.
- The extent to which the star catalog in Ptolemy's *Almagest* is a direct copy of Hipparchus's work versus an enriched and partially re-observed version is a subject of ongoing scholarly analysis.
- A 2022 announcement of a potential fragment of Hipparchus's star catalog in a medieval manuscript was questioned in 2024, with the debate ongoing.
