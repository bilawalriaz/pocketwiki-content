# Olmecs

## Overview
The Olmec were an early major Mesoamerican civilization that flourished in the Gulf lowlands of modern-day Veracruz and Tabasco, Mexico, from roughly 1200 to 400 BC. They are considered one of the cradles of civilization in the Americas, establishing the first complex society in the region with monumental architecture, sophisticated art, and cultural practices that influenced subsequent Mesoamerican cultures for centuries.

## Timeline
- **c. 2500 BC** — Pre-Olmec cultures flourish in the region.
- **c. 1600–1500 BC** — Early Olmec ritual deposits found at El Manatí; rubber balls for the ballgame date to this period.
- **c. 1400 BC** — Distinctive Olmec culture fully appears at San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán.
- **c. 950–900 BC** — San Lorenzo declines and is largely abandoned; La Venta rises to prominence.
- **c. 900–400 BC** — La Venta is the dominant Olmec center.
- **c. 400–350 BC** — Major Olmec centers are abandoned; population in the eastern heartland drops precipitously.
- **c. 32 BC** — One of the earliest Long Count calendar dates appears on Stela C at Tres Zapotes.
- **1869** — First published description of an Olmec colossal head (Tres Zapotes Monument A).
- **1930s–40s** — Matthew Stirling conducts first scientific excavations, establishing Olmec antiquity.
- **2006** — The Cascajal Block, a potential early writing system, is discovered near San Lorenzo.
- **2018** — Mitochondrial DNA study confirms Olmec maternal lineage belongs to Native American haplogroup A.

## Body

### Origins and Heartland
The Olmec civilization emerged in the Gulf lowlands, an area of swampy, fertile alluvial soil. Its roots lie in earlier farming cultures of Tabasco (c. 5100–4600 BC). The first major center was San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán, where Olmec culture was fully established by c. 1400 BC. The productive environment supported a dense population and an elite class that commanded long-distance trade for luxury materials like jade from Guatemala and obsidian from highland sources.

### Major Centers and Decline
After San Lorenzo's decline around 900 BC—possibly due to internal upheaval or environmental changes—La Venta became the primary center. La Venta featured massive constructions like the Great Pyramid and opulent buried offerings. By c. 400 BC, the major Olmec centers were abandoned. The cause is debated, but likely involves severe environmental changes, such as river course alterations from tectonic activity or siltation, which disrupted the agricultural base. Successor cultures, like the Epi-Olmec at Tres Zapotes, emerged afterward.

### Art and Iconography
Olmec art is defined by a distinctive style. Its most iconic creations are the colossal basalt heads (up to 3.4 m tall), believed to be portraits of rulers. Other key artifacts include "altars" (likely thrones), free-standing sculptures, and stelae. Art often features werejaguar motifs, combining human and jaguar traits. "Olmec-style" jade face masks, though not found in controlled Olmec contexts, were valued by later cultures like the Aztecs. The Kunz axes are another ritual artifact type representing werejaguars.

### Innovations and Influence
The Olmec are credited with several "firsts" in Mesoamerica. They likely originated the Mesoamerican ballgame, evidenced by ancient rubber balls. They may have developed early writing systems, such as the symbols on the Cascajal Block (c. 900 BC) and the San Andrés glyphs (c. 650 BC). The Long Count calendar and the concept of zero may also be Olmec inventions. Olmec-style artifacts and iconography are found across Mesoamerica, from Central Mexico to Guatemala, indicating extensive trade networks and cultural influence, possibly through elite emulation.

### Society and Language
Olmec society was likely hierarchical, centered on powerful rulers who derived legitimacy from religious authority. However, it may have lacked institutions like a standing army. The ethnic and linguistic identity is unknown, but the Mixe-Zoquean language family is spoken in the historical heartland, leading many scholars to hypothesize the Olmec spoke an ancestral Mixe-Zoquean language. DNA studies of Olmec remains confirm a Native American maternal lineage.

## Terms
- **Heartland**: The core Olmec region in the Gulf lowlands of Veracruz and Tabasco.
- **Formative Period**: The era in Mesoamerican chronology (c. 2000 BC–AD 250) when complex societies and agriculture developed.
- **Colossal heads**: Massive basalt sculptures, up to 3.4 m tall, believed to be portraits of Olmec rulers.
- **Werejaguar**: A supernatural being in Olmec art, depicted as a hybrid of human and jaguar.
- **Long Count calendar**: A Mesoamerican calendar system that counts days from a mythical creation date, requiring the use of zero.
- **Mixe-Zoquean**: A language family spoken in the Olmec heartland region; a leading hypothesis for the Olmec language.
- **Epi-Olmec**: A post-Olmec culture (c. 400 BC–AD 250) that succeeded the Olmec in parts of the heartland.
- **Cascajal Block**: A serpentine slab with carved symbols (c. 900 BC) that may represent an early writing system.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Mother Culture vs. Sister Culture**: A major debate is whether the Olmec were the "mother culture" that spawned all later Mesoamerican civilizations, or a "sister culture" that developed contemporaneously with others. Evidence of older, sophisticated cultures (e.g., Zapotec at San José Mogote) challenges the mother culture hypothesis.
- **Cause of Decline**: The reason for the collapse of Olmec centers around 400 BC is uncertain. Leading theories point to environmental changes (river shifts, tectonic activity) or possibly volcanic eruptions forcing relocation.
- **Nature of Olmec Influence**: How Olmec style spread across Mesoamerica is debated. Theories include trade, colonization, artisan travel, or voluntary adoption by other elites to signify status.
- **Writing and Calendar Origins**: While the Olmec are candidates for inventing writing and the Long Count calendar, definitive proof is lacking. The Cascajal Block's authenticity and meaning are debated, and the calendar's earliest dates postdate the Olmec civilization's end.
- **Ethnic and Linguistic Identity**: The specific language and ethnicity of the Olmec remain unknown, though Mixe-Zoquean is the leading linguistic hypothesis.
