# Brain

## Overview
The brain is an organ that serves as the center of the nervous system in all vertebrate and most invertebrate animals. It consists of nervous tissue and is typically located in the head (cephalization), usually near organs for special senses such as vision, hearing, and olfaction. In vertebrates, it develops from the anterior neural tube into three primary vesicles—forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain—which differentiate into specialized structures such as the cerebral cortex, cerebellum, and brainstem. Neurons communicate via synapses using neurotransmitters, forming vast, plastic networks that underlie learning, memory, and cognition. While basic cellular mechanisms are conserved across species, brain size, complexity, and organization vary dramatically, reflecting evolutionary adaptations to ecological niches. The human brain is distinguished by a massive expansion of the neocortex, particularly the prefrontal cortex, enabling advanced executive functions.

## Timeline
- **700–650 Mya** — Common bilaterian ancestor (simple tubeworm with segmented body and anterior ganglion) appears in Cryogenian period.
- **>500 Mya** — First vertebrates appear during Cambrian period; resemble modern jawless fish (hagfish, lamprey).
- **445 Mya** — Jawed vertebrates appear.
- **350 Mya** — Tetrapods appear.
- **320 Mya** — Modern reptiles and mammals diverge from common ancestor.
- **310 Mya** — Amniotes appear.
- **200 Mya** — Mammaliaforms appear.
- **>5,000 years ago** — Oldest discovered brain found in Armenia (Areni-1 cave complex), preserved in skull of 12–14-year-old girl.
- **1986** — Complete "wiring diagrams" (connectome) of *C. elegans* created.
- **2010s–2020s** — *Drosophila* connectomes achieved; as of 2026: one full female larval connectome, one full adult male CNS connectome, one full adult female CNS connectome.
- **2024** — Large-scale single-cell sequencing of human prefrontal cortex (almost 3M nuclei, 388 individuals) identifies 28 cell types, ~500K cell type–specific regulatory elements, and ~1.5M single-cell expression QTLs.

## Body

### Cellular Structure
Brains comprise two primary cell classes: **neurons** (signal-transmitting cells) and **glial cells** (support, insulation, metabolic regulation, developmental guidance). Neurons possess a cell body, **dendrites** (receiving inputs), and an **axon** (transmitting outputs). Axons may be **myelinated** (wrapped in fatty myelin sheaths, increasing conduction speed to 1–100 m/s) or unmyelinated. **Synapses** are specialized junctions where an arriving **action potential** triggers release of a **neurotransmitter** that binds receptors on the target cell. The human brain contains ~100 trillion synapses; synapse strength can change based on activity patterns (**synaptic plasticity**), widely considered the basis of learning and memory. Grey matter contains neuron cell bodies; white matter consists of myelinated axon tracts.

### Evolution
**Bilaterian Ground Plan:** All bilaterians (animals with left/right symmetry) descend from a ~700–650 Mya ancestor with a nerve cord, segmental ganglia, and an anterior "brain." The forebrain likely fuses two ancestral systems: an apical sensory/hormonal system (specified by *six3/rx* genes) and a blastoporal locomotor system (axial patterning genes *nk2/sim*) that moved laterally to form the ventral nerve cord and dopaminergic forebrain regions.

**Invertebrates:** Arthropods (insects, crustaceans) and cephalopods (octopuses, squids) evolved complex brains independently from twin ventral nerve cords. *Drosophila* (fruit fly) and *C. elegans* (nematode, 302 neurons) are key genetic model organisms; *Aplysia* (sea slug) models cellular learning mechanisms.

**Vertebrates:** All vertebrate brains share a conserved embryonic three-vesicle plan (forebrain/prosencephalon, midbrain/mesencephalon, hindbrain/rhombencephalon). In fish/amphibians, regions remain similar in size; in terrestrial tetrapods, the forebrain expands dramatically, the hindbrain forms a large cerebellum, and the midbrain shrinks. **Brain-body scaling** follows a power law (exponent ~0.75); primates have brains 5–10× larger than predicted. **Key components:** Medulla (vital reflexes), pons (sleep, respiration, cranial nerves), hypothalamus (homeostasis, hormones), thalamus (relay, motivation), cerebellum (motor/cognitive precision, 10% volume/50% neurons), optic tectum/superior colliculus (spatial orienting), pallium/cerebral cortex (complex processing, six-layered neocortex in mammals), hippocampus (spatial memory, medial pallium homolog in all vertebrates), basal ganglia (action selection via disinhibition, dopamine-mediated reward), olfactory bulb (smell, reduced in primates).

**Reptiles:** Diverse morphologies (limbless snakes, armored turtles) correlate with neural variations (e.g., absent limb motor columns in snakes, infrared trigeminal innervation). Crocodilians have largest brain/body ratio and telencephalon; turtles largest diencephalon; lizards largest mesencephalon. All share elaborated brain organization (migrated neuronal nuclei) and conserved developmental transcription factors with mammals.

**Mammals:** Brains ~2× bird and ~10× reptile size at same body mass. Forebrain expansion creates a six-layered **neocortex** (vs. three-layered pallium in non-mammals), folding into gyri/sulci. Superior colliculus shrinks; visual functions shift to cortex. Cerebellum gains **neocerebellum** supporting cortex. Placental mammals possess a **corpus callosum** connecting hemispheres.

**Primates:** Encephalization Quotient (EQ) corrects for nonlinear brain-body scaling. Humans: EQ 7–8; other primates: EQ 2–3; dolphins: higher than non-human primates. Expansion driven by prefrontal cortex (executive function) and visual cortex (>30 areas, >50% neocortical surface).

### Development
Vertebrate neural development is conserved: ectoderm → neural plate → neural tube → three vesicles (prosencephalon, mesencephalon, rhombencephalon) → five vesicles (telencephalon, diencephalon, mesencephalon, metencephalon, myelencephalon). Neurons born in proliferative zones migrate to final positions. Axons navigate via **growth cones** responding to molecular cues (attraction/repulsion). Initial synaptic overproduction is refined by **activity-dependent pruning** (e.g., retinal waves guide retinotopic map formation in midbrain). In humans, most neurons generated prenatally; **adult neurogenesis** persists in olfactory bulb and hippocampal dentate gyrus. Genes set gross architecture; experience refines synaptic matrices (enriched environments → thicker cortex, higher synaptic density).

### Physiology
**Neurotransmitters:** **Glutamate** (ubiquitous, excitatory) and **GABA** (ubiquitous, inhibitory) dominate. Others are localized: serotonin (raphe nuclei), norepinephrine (locus coeruleus), dopamine (reward, basal ganglia), acetylcholine. **Dale's principle**: a neuron releases the same transmitter(s) at all its synapses. Psychoactive drugs target specific transmitter systems.

**Electrical Activity:** Synchronized neuronal firing generates detectable fields (EEG/MEG). States: delta waves (deep sleep), alpha (awake/inattentive), beta/gamma (active engagement). Seizures reflect failed inhibition (pathological high-amplitude spikes).

**Metabolism:** **Blood-brain barrier** (tight endothelial junctions) isolates brain metabolism; nutrients enter via cerebrospinal fluid/glymphatic clearance. Brain consumes 2–8% basal metabolism (20–25% in humans), mostly maintaining neuronal membrane potential. Primary fuel: glucose; alternatives: ketones, lactate, fatty acids.

### Function
**Perception:** Sensory receptors → spinal cord/brainstem nuclei → thalamus → cerebral cortex for feature extraction and multisensory integration. Modalities vary (e.g., infrared in snakes, magnetic in birds, electric in fish).

**Motor Control:** Hierarchical: spinal cord (reflexes, central pattern generators for locomotion) → medulla/pons (stereotyped: breathing, swallowing) → midbrain (red nucleus: limb coordination) → primary motor cortex (fine voluntary control via corticospinal/pyramidal tract). Secondary modulation by premotor cortex, basal ganglia (action selection), cerebellum (precision/timing). Autonomic nervous system controls smooth muscle.

**Sleep:** **Suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)** in hypothalamus houses circadian clock (24-hr *clock gene* rhythms), entrained by light via retinohypothalamic tract. SCN drives sleep-wake cycles via hypothalamus/brainstem (reticular formation → thalamus → cortex). Two phases: **REM** (dreaming, low norepinephrine/serotonin, high acetylcholine) and **NREM** (slow-wave sleep, synchronized cortical waves).

**Homeostasis:** Hypothalamus maintains internal parameters (temperature, glucose, osmolarity) via negative feedback. Sensors in vessel linings → hypothalamic nuclei → motor outputs (behavior) and pituitary hormone release.

**Motivation:** **Reward-punishment system** centered on **basal ganglia**. Basal ganglia tonically inhibit motor systems; disinhibition permits action. **Dopamine** signals reward prediction error; addictive drugs (cocaine, amphetamine, nicotine) elevate dopamine or enhance its effects.

**Learning and Memory:** **Synaptic plasticity** (long-term potentiation/LTP, discovered 1971) is primary mechanism. Types: **Working memory** (transient cell assemblies), **Episodic memory** (hippocampus-dependent, lifetime duration), **Semantic memory** (cortical, facts), **Instrumental learning** (basal ganglia, reward-based), **Motor learning** (cerebellum, premotor cortex, basal ganglia; microadjustment storage). **BDNF** and physical activity enhance plasticity.

### Research
**Approaches:** Neuroanatomy (stains, microscopy, connectomics), neurophysiology (electrodes, EEG, pharmacology), lesion studies (human stroke, targeted animal lesions), computational neuroscience (biophysical simulations, abstract neural networks), genetics/genomics (mouse knockouts, Cre-Lox, single-cell sequencing). **2024 milestone:** Almost 3M human prefrontal nuclei sequenced, revealing cell-type-specific regulatory networks linked to aging and neuropsychiatric disease.

**History:** Ancient debate: heart (Aristotle) vs. brain (Alcmaeon, Hippocratic Corpus, Galen). Galen: nerves carry "animal spirits." Renaissance: Descartes (hydraulic nerves, *res cogitans*). 1790s: Galvani (electricity triggers muscle contraction). Late 1800s: Golgi stain (Cajal) reveals neuron diversity. Early 1900s: Hodgkin/Huxley (action potential biophysics), Katz (synaptic electrochemistry). 1940s: Cybernetics (brain as information processor). 1959: "What the frog's eye tells the frog's brain" (feature detectors). 1960s: Hubel/Wiesel (visual cortex edge detectors). Late 1900s: "Decade of the Brain" (1990s). 2000s: Multi-electrode recording, optogenetics, connectomics, large-scale genomics.

### Society and Culture
**As food:** Animal brains consumed in various cuisines. **Rituals:** Neanderthal mortuary practices possibly included brain consumption. Fore people (Papua New Guinea) practiced funerary endocannibalism; transmitted **kuru** (prion disease).

## Terms
- ****Action potential**** — Brief electrochemical pulse (~1 ms) traveling along an axon at 1–100 m/s, triggering neurotransmitter release at synapses.
- ****Synapse**** — Specialized junction where an axon terminal releases neurotransmitter to bind receptors on a target cell; the fundamental unit of neural communication.
- ****Synaptic plasticity**** — Activity-dependent modification of synaptic strength (e.g., long-term potentiation); the primary cellular mechanism for learning and memory.
- ****Neurotransmitter**** — Chemical messenger (e.g., glutamate, GABA, dopamine) released at synapses to alter target cell electrical/chemical properties.
- ****Blood-brain barrier**** — Tight junctions of cerebral endothelial cells restricting passage of toxins, pathogens, and many drugs; necessitates glymphatic clearance.
- ****Neocortex**** — Six-layered cerebral cortex unique to mammals, evolved from three-layered pallium; site of high-level sensory, motor, and cognitive processing.
- ****Basal ganglia**** — Forebrain nuclei implementing action selection via tonic inhibition/disinhibition of motor systems; central to reward (dopamine) learning.
- ****Encephalization Quotient (EQ)**** — Measure of brain size relative to predicted size for a given body mass, correcting for nonlinear scaling; humans ~7–8.
- ****Connectome**** — Comprehensive map of neural connections (wiring diagram); completed for *C. elegans* (1986) and *Drosophila* CNS (2010s–2020s).
- ****Glymphatic system**** — Brain-wide waste clearance pathway using cerebrospinal fluid bulk flow, compensating for lack of conventional lymphatic drainage due to blood-brain barrier.

## Debates and open questions
- **Ur-bilaterian brain:** Whether the last common bilaterian ancestor possessed a true brain or only an apical sensory organ; brainless echinoderms/tunicates may represent secondary loss or ancestral state.
- **Homology mapping:** Precise correspondences between vertebrate and invertebrate brain regions (e.g., midbrain-hindbrain boundary to insect brains) remain unclear.
- **Neural code:** How ensembles of millions of neurons cooperate to produce cognition; population-level patterns (EEG) vs. single-unit computation.
- **Adult neurogenesis:** Functional significance of new neurons in human olfactory bulb and hippocampal dentate gyrus; extent in other regions.
- **Consciousness:** Mechanisms linking distributed neural activity to subjective experience; "hard problem" unresolved.
- **Computational modeling:** Trade-off between biophysical realism (intractable at whole-brain scale) and abstract models (may miss critical cellular computations); Human Brain Project approach contested.
- **Glial roles:** Expanding understanding of astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes beyond support to active modulation of synaptic transmission, plasticity, and metabolism.
