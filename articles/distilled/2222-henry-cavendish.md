# Henry Cavendish

## Overview
Henry Cavendish (1731–1810) was an English experimental chemist and physicist whose precise, quantitative work laid foundations for modern chemistry and physics. He is best known for discovering hydrogen, synthesizing water, and conducting the first accurate measurement of the Earth's density, an experiment that also allows calculation of the gravitational constant. His reclusive nature meant many of his discoveries, including anticipations of Ohm's law and Coulomb's law, were not published until long after his death.

## Timeline
- **10 October 1731** — Born in Nice, France.
- **1748–1751** — Attended St Peter's College, Cambridge, leaving without a degree.
- **1760** — Elected to the Royal Society and the Royal Society Club.
- **1766** — Published *On Factitious Airs*, describing "inflammable air" (hydrogen).
- **1777** — Discovered that exhaled air becomes "fixed air" (carbon dioxide).
- **1783** — Published on the synthesis of water by burning hydrogen in oxygen.
- **1785** — Conducted precise experiments on the composition of atmospheric air.
- **1787** — Adopted Lavoisier's antiphlogistic chemical theory.
- **1797–1798** — Performed the Cavendish experiment to measure Earth's density.
- **24 February 1810** — Died at Clapham, London.

## Body

### Chemistry Research
Cavendish was a leading "pneumatic chemist" who studied gases. In 1766, he identified "inflammable air" (hydrogen) as a distinct substance produced by acids on metals, correctly noting it formed water in a 2:1 ratio when burned. He later showed that air exhaled by mammals becomes carbon dioxide, not "phlogisticated air" as Joseph Priestley believed. His 1783 experiments on burning hydrogen in oxygen led him to conclude water was condensed from the air, a finding that sparked a priority dispute with James Watt. In 1785, he analyzed atmospheric air, finding it consisted of one part oxygen to four parts nitrogen, with a 1/120 residue of an unknown gas—later identified as argon by Ramsay and Rayleigh in the 1890s. Cavendish used the language of phlogiston theory but adopted Lavoisier's new chemistry by 1787, though he rejected Lavoisier's material theory of heat.

### Density of the Earth
In 1798, Cavendish published the results of his famous experiment to determine the Earth's density. Using a torsion balance designed by John Michell, he measured the gravitational attraction between small lead spheres (1.61 lb) and large stationary ones (350 lb). By isolating the apparatus to eliminate air currents and temperature effects, he calculated an average density of 5.48 times that of water (modern value ~5.51). This result, within 1% of the accepted figure, also provided a value for the gravitational constant (G) of 6.754 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², close to the modern 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹. Cavendish's stated goal was to measure density, not G or Earth's mass directly, though his data allows those calculations.

### Electrical and Heat Research
Cavendish conducted extensive, mostly unpublished work on electricity and heat. In the 1770s, he developed a mathematical theory of electricity based on an "electrical fluid," demonstrating experimentally that electric force follows an inverse-square law (anticipating Coulomb's law). He also explored concepts like electric potential and capacitance. His work on heat, described in a manuscript from the late 1780s, proposed a mechanical theory where heat results from the motion of matter, prefiguring thermodynamics and the conservation of energy. Much of this research was only published posthumously by James Clerk Maxwell in 1879.

## Terms
- **Pneumatic chemist**: A scientist specializing in the study of gases.
- **Phlogiston theory**: An 18th-century theory that combustion involves releasing a substance called phlogiston.
- **Antiphlogistic theory**: Lavoisier's theory replacing phlogiston with oxygen-based combustion.
- **Torsion balance**: An instrument that measures tiny forces by twisting a wire.
- **Eudiometer**: A device for measuring the purity or "goodness" of air for breathing.
- **Gravitational constant (G)**: The constant in Newton's law of gravitation, relating mass and distance to gravitational force.
- **Mechanical equivalent of heat**: The concept that heat and mechanical work are interchangeable forms of energy.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Priority of water synthesis**: Cavendish's 1783 experiment on burning hydrogen preceded James Watt's publication, leading to a dispute over who first discovered water's composition.
- **Interpretation of the Cavendish experiment**: While often described as measuring G or Earth's mass, Cavendish's own goal was to determine Earth's density; the other values are derived from his data.
- **Cavendish's personality**: Modern commentators, including Oliver Sacks, have speculated he may have been autistic based on his extreme shyness and social avoidance.
