# Voltaire

## Overview
François-Marie Arouet (1694–1778), known by his pen name Voltaire, was a French Enlightenment writer, philosopher, and satirist whose works championed civil liberties, religious tolerance, and freedom of speech. His prolific output—over 20,000 letters and 2,000 books and pamphlets—made him one of the first internationally celebrated authors. Through sharp wit and relentless critique of dogma, Voltaire challenged the Catholic Church, absolute monarchy, and social injustice, leaving an enduring legacy as a symbol of free thought and rationalism.

- **1778** — Returned to Paris; died on 30 May

## Body

### Early Life and Rise to Fame
Voltaire was born into a bourgeois family in Paris. His father, a minor official, wanted him to pursue law, but Voltaire gravitated toward literature. His early satirical writings led to imprisonment, including a stint in the Bastille for mocking the Régent. His debut play, *Œdipe* (1718), brought him fame and recognition from European royalty.

### Exile in England and Intellectual Awakening
In 1726, Voltaire was exiled to England after a violent confrontation with the aristocrat Guy Auguste de Rohan-Chabot. During his two-and-a-half-year stay, he encountered British constitutional monarchy, religious tolerance, and Newtonian science. He met prominent figures like Alexander Pope and Jonathan Swift, and developed a deep appreciation for Shakespeare. These experiences shaped his worldview and informed his later critiques of French absolutism.

### Château de Cirey and Scientific Collaboration
Returning to France, Voltaire settled at the Château de Cirey with Émilie du Châtelet, a brilliant mathematician and physicist. Together, they studied Newton’s works, conducted experiments, and built a vast library. Voltaire became a key advocate for Newtonian physics in France, helping displace Cartesian theories. Their collaboration produced significant philosophical and scientific contributions, though personal tensions eventually arose.

### Prussia and Frederick the Great
In 1750, Voltaire accepted an invitation from Frederick the Great to live at the Prussian court. Initially welcomed, he completed *Micromégas*, a pioneering work of science fiction. However, conflicts with court officials and a falling-out with Frederick led to his abrupt departure in 1753. His travels back to France were marked by further disputes and delays.

### Geneva, Ferney, and Later Years
Settling in Geneva briefly, Voltaire clashed with local religious authorities over theater restrictions. He then purchased an estate at Ferney, where he spent the last two decades of his life. There, he wrote *Candide*, a scathing satire of Leibnizian optimism, and the *Dictionnaire philosophique*, a collection of essays critiquing organized religion and promoting reason. He also championed justice in high-profile cases like that of Jean Calas, a Protestant wrongly executed for murder.

### Religious and Philosophical Views
A deist, Voltaire believed in a rational, benevolent God but rejected revealed religion and biblical literalism. He advocated for religious tolerance and separation of church and state, though his views on Judaism and Islam were often critical and reflective of Enlightenment prejudices. His famous phrase “écrasez l’infâme” encapsulated his call to crush superstition and oppression.

### Literary Legacy
Voltaire’s extensive corpus includes plays, poems, novels, histories, and philosophical treatises. His historical writings emphasized social customs and cultural achievements over military conquests, influencing modern historiography. His sharp irony and advocacy for human rights cemented his status as a central figure of the Enlightenment.

## Terms
- **Deism**: Belief in a creator based on reason rather than revelation.
- **Philosophe**: A term used in 18th-century France for Enlightenment thinkers.
- **Optimism (Leibnizian)**: The idea that this world is the "best of all possible worlds."
- **Censorship**: Government control over publication and expression.
- **Constitutional Monarchy**: A monarchy limited by laws and representative institutions.
- **Newtonian Physics**: Theories of motion and universal gravitation developed by Isaac Newton.
- **Polygenism**: The discredited theory that different human races had separate origins.
- **Meritocracy**: A system in which advancement is based on ability and achievement.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the extent of Voltaire’s anti-Semitism, with some arguing his criticisms were aimed at religious orthodoxy rather than Jews themselves. His complex relationship with Frederick the Great remains a subject of analysis, particularly regarding their reconciliation after years of estrangement. Additionally, debates persist over whether his advocacy for civil liberties extended fully to all marginalized groups, given his controversial views on race and non-Christian religions.
