# Remote sensing

## Overview
Remote sensing is the acquisition of information about an object or area without physical contact, typically using satellite or airborne sensors to detect electromagnetic radiation. It is a foundational tool for Earth observation, enabling monitoring of environmental changes, resource management, and security applications across disciplines like geography, ecology, and meteorology.

## Timeline
- **1858** — G. Tournachon (Nadar) takes the first aerial photograph from a balloon over Paris.
- **WWI** — Systematic aerial photography is developed for military surveillance and reconnaissance.
- **Early 1960s** — Evelyn Pruitt coins the term "remote sensing" to describe new data streams beyond aerial photography.
- **1972** — Landsat 1, the first satellite for systematic Earth observation, is launched.
- **1974–77** — LACIE (Large Area Crop Inventory Experiment) is the first major project using Landsat for agricultural statistics.
- **1999** — IKONOS, the first commercial satellite collecting very high-resolution imagery, is launched.

## Body

### Methods and Platforms
Remote sensing is broadly divided into **passive** and **active** methods. Passive sensors detect naturally emitted or reflected energy, with sunlight being the most common source. Active sensors emit their own signal (e.g., radar or lidar) and measure the reflected return. Data is collected from orbital platforms (satellites) and airborne systems (aircraft, drones), allowing access to dangerous or inaccessible areas without disturbance.

### Data Characteristics and Processing
The quality of remote sensing data is defined by four key resolutions: **spatial** (pixel size), **spectral** (number and width of frequency bands), **radiometric** (intensity levels), and **temporal** (revisit frequency). Raw data requires processing, including **georeferencing** (aligning images to a map coordinate system), **radiometric correction** (adjusting for uneven illumination), and **atmospheric correction** (removing haze effects). Data is often categorized into processing levels, from raw (Level 1) to analysis-ready products (Level 3).

### Key Applications
Applications are vast and include:
*   **Environmental Monitoring:** Tracking deforestation, glacial changes, and natural disasters.
*   **Agriculture and Forestry:** Estimating crop areas, monitoring forest health, and assessing land use.
*   **Oceanography and Hydrology:** Measuring sea level, ocean currents, and water quality.
*   **Geology and Geophysics:** Mineral exploration using gamma-ray or spectral data, and mapping seafloor features with altimeters.
*   **Urban and Security:** Monitoring population, infrastructure damage, and border areas.

### History and Development
The discipline evolved from early balloon photography to systematic military aerial surveillance in WWI and WWII. The Cold War accelerated development with specialized aircraft (e.g., U-2, SR-71) and the advent of satellite technology. The launch of Landsat in 1972 marked the beginning of global, civilian Earth observation. The term "remote sensing" was formalized in the 1960s, and the field continues to advance with new sensors and commercial high-resolution satellites.

## Terms
*   **Active Remote Sensing:** A method where a sensor emits its own energy pulse (e.g., radar, lidar) and measures the reflected signal from a target.
*   **Passive Remote Sensing:** A method where a sensor detects energy naturally emitted or reflected by a target, such as sunlight.
*   **Spatial Resolution:** The size of the area on the ground represented by a single pixel in an image.
*   **Spectral Resolution:** The number and specific wavelength intervals (bands) a sensor can record.
*   **Radiometric Resolution:** The sensor's ability to distinguish small differences in signal intensity (e.g., number of gray-scale levels).
*   **Temporal Resolution:** The frequency at which a sensor revisits and images the same location.
*   **Georeferencing:** The process of aligning remote sensing data to a known coordinate system on the Earth's surface.
*   **LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging):** An active sensor using laser pulses to measure distances, often for creating precise elevation models.
*   **Inverse Problem:** The principle of inferring an unknown state (e.g., temperature) from a related, measurable observation (e.g., spectral emissions).
