# Noah Webster

## Overview
Noah Webster (1758–1843) was an American lexicographer, educator, and political writer known as the "Father of American Scholarship and Education." He sought to forge a distinct American cultural identity separate from Britain by standardizing American English spelling, pronunciation, and usage through his bestselling "Blue-Backed Speller" (1783) and *An American Dictionary of the English Language* (1828). A Federalist, he believed a unified national language was essential for republican virtue and political stability, and he successfully lobbied for the first U.S. copyright laws to protect his works. His later life saw a shift from revolutionary optimism to religious conservatism and a controversial retreat from abolitionism.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into an established Connecticut family descended from colonial governors, Webster attended a poor local school that motivated his later educational reforms. Tutored in Latin and Greek at 14, he entered Yale at 15, graduating in 1778 amid Revolutionary War disruptions. His father mortgaged the farm for his tuition, but Webster drifted after graduation, finding a liberal arts education "disqualifies a man for business." He studied law under future Chief Justice Oliver Ellsworth, passing the bar in 1781, but wartime conditions prevented a legal career. He turned to teaching and writing, opening private schools and publishing essays justifying the Revolution.

### Political Theory and Federalist Advocacy
Webster viewed American nationalism as superior to European models, grounded in "universal toleration" and popular sovereignty. He applied Jean-Jacques Rousseau’s *Social Contract* to argue for a strong central government in *Sketches of American Policy* (1785), one of the earliest such arguments. He supported the Constitution vigorously, publishing the influential pamphlet "An Examination into the Leading Principles of the Federal Constitution" (1787) under the pen name "A Citizen of America." He emphasized widespread property ownership as a Federalist tenet and adapted Rousseau’s *Emile* to argue for female education, even writing two "fan fiction" sequels for his *Reader*.

### Federalist Editor and Public Intellectual
In 1793, Alexander Hamilton recruited Webster to New York to edit the Federalist newspaper *American Minerva* (later *Commercial Advertiser*), where he wrote the first known newspaper editorial. He defended the Washington and Adams administrations, neutrality policy, and Jay’s Treaty, while attacking the French Revolution and "Democratic-Republican Societies." Jeffersonian Republicans vilified him as a "pusillanimous... self-dubbed patriot." He authored two volumes on epidemics (1799), earning recognition as "America's first epidemiologist," and served in the Connecticut legislature (1800, 1802–1807). He helped found Amherst College (1812) and was elected to the American Philosophical Society (1827).

### School Books: The "Blue-Backed Speller"
Dissatisfied with British textbooks and overcrowded one-room schools, Webster published *A Grammatical Institute of the English Language* in three parts: a speller (1783), grammar (1784), and reader (1785). He argued American language should follow "republican principles"—popular usage, not aristocratic standards—and rejected the prerequisite of Greek/Latin for English grammar. The Speller used a developmental structure anticipating Piaget, progressing from alphabet to complex sentences by age-appropriate stages. It sold 15 million copies by 1837 and 60 million by 1890, funding his dictionary work. It popularized American spellings (*defense, color, center*) and introduced secular "civics" content, replacing the biblical *New England Primer*. The *Reader* included patriotic orations, Rousseau sequels, and abolitionist essays. After 1840, the McGuffey Readers eclipsed his market share.

### Dictionary: Standardizing American English
Webster published a *Compendious Dictionary* in 1806, then spent 26 years expanding it. He learned 26+ languages to trace etymologies, though scholars like Charlton Laird and Thomas Pyles later criticized his grasp of Anglo-Saxon and German. Completed in Cambridge, England (1825), the 1828 *American Dictionary* contained 70,000 words (12,000 new), adding Americanisms like "skunk." Webster did not invent spellings like *color* or *center* but popularized existing variants based on "simplicity, analogy or etymology." The first edition sold only 2,500 copies, forcing him to mortgage his home. The 1840 second edition appeared in two volumes. Culturally conservative Federalists called it radical and vulgar; Republicans attacked Webster personally. Posthumously, it became essential for Emily Dickinson and influenced the *Imperial Dictionary* (1850). Merriam brothers acquired rights in 1843, founding the Merriam-Webster lineage.

### Views: Religion, Slavery, and Copyright
**Religion:** A freethinker in youth, Webster converted to Calvinistic orthodoxy in 1808. His 1828 Dictionary emphasized social control, submission to authority, and fear of God, containing the most Biblical definitions of any reference work. He declared "Education is useless without the Bible" and published a revised *Common Version* Bible (1833) and an apologetic work (1834).

**Slavery:** He founded the Connecticut Society for the Abolition of Slavery (1791) but by the 1830s opposed the abolitionist movement. His 1832 *History of the United States* omitted slavery’s role and included racist characterizations, declaring Anglo-Saxons "the only true Americans." In 1837, he condemned his abolitionist daughter Eliza, arguing Northerners could not legally interfere with the South and abolitionist preachers "deserve the penitentiary."

**Copyright:** Webster lobbied state legislatures (1782–1786) and Congress for copyright protection, securing laws in CT, NY, MA, VA, and others before the 1790 federal act. In 1826–1831, he personally lobbied Congress—enlisting Daniel Webster and others—to extend terms to match new British law, resulting in the Copyright Act of 1831, the first major revision of U.S. copyright law.

## Terms
- **Blue-Backed Speller**: Webster’s *The American Spelling Book* (1783), named for its blue cover; the bestselling American textbook of the 19th century, teaching reading, spelling, and civic values.
- **Federal Language**: Webster’s concept of a standardized American English serving as a nationalizing device to separate the U.S. culturally from Britain, balancing regularity and innovation.
- **Popular Sovereignty in Language**: The principle that linguistic standards should be set by general usage of the people, not aristocratic elites, mirroring republican government.
- **Compendious Dictionary (1806)**: Webster’s first, smaller dictionary, precursor to the comprehensive 1828 edition.
- **An American Dictionary of the English Language (1828)**: Webster’s magnum opus, 70,000 entries, standardizing American spelling/usage and adding Americanisms; basis for Merriam-Webster dictionaries.
- **Copyright Act of 1831**: First major revision of U.S. copyright law, extending terms to 28+14 years; passed after Webster’s decades of state and federal lobbying.
- **Connecticut Society for the Abolition of Slavery (1791)**: Organization Webster helped found; he later repudiated the abolitionist movement’s tactics and ideology.
- **Common Version (1833)**: Webster’s revised edition of the King James Bible, modernizing grammar and removing offensive language.
- **American Minerva (1793)**: New York’s first daily newspaper, founded and edited by Webster as a Federalist organ; published the first known newspaper editorial.
- **Sketches of American Policy (1785)**: Webster’s early political work applying Rousseau’s *Social Contract* to argue for a strong U.S. central government.

## Debates and open questions
- **Origin of American spellings**: Scholars debate whether Webster invented spellings like *color/centre* or merely popularized existing variants; John Algeo (2008) argues he selected pre-existing options on philological grounds.
- **Scholarly rigor of etymologies**: Contemporaries and later linguists (Laird, Pyles) criticized Webster’s command of Anglo-Saxon and German, questioning the scientific basis of his 1828 etymologies.
- **Paradox of language politics**: Jill Lepore (2008) notes Federalists attacked the dictionary as radical/vulgar for inclusivity, while Republicans attacked Webster as mad—revealing a clash between liberty and order in defining Americanism.
- **Abolitionism reversal**: Historians debate the causes of Webster’s shift from founding an abolition society (1791) to publishing a racist history textbook (1832) and condemning abolitionist preachers (1837), reflecting broader Northern complicity debates.
- **Secular vs. religious education**: The *Speller* pioneered secular civics ("secular catechism to the nation-state"), yet Webster later insisted "Education is useless without the Bible," embodying a tension in American educational philosophy.
- **Lexicography as poetry**: Nathan Austin argues Webster’s definitions possess a "lexical poetics" that influenced Emily Dickinson and American literature, raising questions about dictionaries as literary artifacts.
