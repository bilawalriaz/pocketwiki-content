# Tughril I

## Overview
Tughril (c. 993–1063) was a Turkoman chieftain who founded the Seljuk Empire, a pivotal medieval state that reshaped the political and religious landscape of the Middle East. By uniting nomadic Turkic tribes, defeating the Ghaznavids and Buyids, and restoring the Abbasid Caliphs to spiritual authority, he established a new model of Sunni Muslim rule that blended Turkic military power with Persianate administration and Islamic legitimacy.

## Timeline
- **c. 993** — Tughril is born in the Central Asian steppes.
- **1037** — Declared Sultan by the Seljuk dynasty after capturing Nishapur.
- **1040** — Decisive Seljuk victory at the Battle of Dandanqan ends Ghaznavid rule in Khorasan.
- **1042** — Conquers Ray, which he later makes his capital.
- **1052** — Enters Isfahan after a siege, making it his new capital.
- **1055** — Enters Baghdad at the Abbasid Caliph's invitation, ending Buyid rule.
- **1058** — Arslan al-Basasiri, backed by the Fatimids, seizes Baghdad and deposes the Caliph.
- **1060** — Defeats and executes his rebellious brother Ibrahim Inal.
- **1061** — Defeats and kills Arslan al-Basasiri, restoring the Abbasid Caliph.
- **1062** — Marries the daughter of Abbasid Caliph al-Qa'im bi-Amrillah.
- **1063** — Dies near Ray without a direct heir, leaving the empire to his nephew Alp Arslan.

## Body

### Rise and Consolidation in Khorasan
Tughril and his brother Chaghri Beg, grandson of the Seljuk dynasty's eponymous founder, began as mercenaries for various Central Asian powers. After being allowed to settle in Khorasan by the Ghaznavid Sultan Mahmud, they grew in strength. Following the death of Seljuk leaders, the tribe united under Tughril. In 1037, he was proclaimed Sultan. A series of wars with the Ghaznavids culminated in the decisive Battle of Dandanqan (1040), which broke Ghaznavid power and established the Seljuks as a major Middle Eastern force.

### Expansion Across Iran and the West
From his base in Khorasan, Tughril systematically expanded westward. He conquered regions like Gorgan, Tabaristan, and Azerbaijan, forcing local rulers to acknowledge his suzerainty. He moved his capital from Nishapur to Ray (c. 1042) and later to Isfahan (1052) after prolonged sieges. His brother Ibrahim Inal led the first major Seljuk campaigns into Byzantine Anatolia in 1048. This expansion was driven by a need for pasture, wealth, and the ambition to create a unified Islamic state under Sunni authority.

### Alliance with the Abbasid Caliphate
Tughril sought legitimacy from the Abbasid Caliph in Baghdad. After his victory at Dandanqan, he requested and received a decree of authority. In 1055, he entered Baghdad at the Caliph's invitation, ending the rule of the Shi'a Buyid dynasty. He arranged a marriage between his niece and the Caliph, strengthening ties. However, his general Arslan al-Basasiri, with Fatimid support, briefly seized Baghdad in 1058, deposing the Caliph. Tughril returned, crushed the revolt, and executed Basasiri in 1061, cementing his role as the Caliph's protector and the de facto ruler of the Sunni world.

### Governance and Religious Policy
Tughril established a new political model: the Sultan held absolute temporal and military power, while the Caliph retained only spiritual authority. He governed through a blend of Turkic tradition and Persianate administration. A devout Sunni, particularly of the Hanafi school, he and his vizier, Amid al-Mulk Kunduri, promoted Hanafi orthodoxy and actively persecuted Shi'a and other sects, including Ash'ari theologians. This policy aimed to secure the support of the Sunni majority and the Caliphate.

### Death and Succession
Tughril died in 1063 without a direct heir. He had designated his younger nephew Suleiman as successor, but the powerful faction supporting the more capable Alp Arslan (Chaghri Beg's elder son) prevailed. This succession struggle highlighted the ongoing tension between the Seljuk family's Turkic traditions of shared rule and the Persianate model of centralized, hereditary monarchy that Tughril had promoted.

## Terms
*   **Sultan:** An Islamic sovereign with absolute temporal authority, a title Tughril used to assert his political power.
*   **Khutbah:** The Friday sermon delivered in a ruler's name, a key symbol of political legitimacy in the Islamic world.
*   **Iqta' (fief):** A grant of land revenue given to military commanders in lieu of salary, a core institution of Seljuk administration.
*   **Buyids:** A Shi'a Daylamite dynasty that controlled Baghdad and much of Iran before the Seljuks.
*   **Fatimids:** A Shi'a Ismaili caliphate based in Egypt, rivals to the Abbasids and political opponents of Tughril.
*   **Hanafi school:** One of the four major Sunni schools of Islamic jurisprudence, favored by the Seljuks.
*   **Ash'ari:** A school of Sunni theology that was initially persecuted under Tughril's Hanafi-leaning policies.
*   **Shihna:** A military and administrative official appointed by the sultan to monitor and control the Caliph's court in Baghdad.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Succession Model:** Scholars debate the extent to which Tughril successfully imposed a centralized, hereditary Persianate model of kingship versus the traditional Turkic system of shared leadership among elite family members.
*   **Character Assessment:** Historical sources offer conflicting portrayals of Tughril, describing him as both a pious, just ruler who avoided bloodshed and as an oppressive leader whose army engaged in widespread plunder.
*   **Motives for Baghdad Intervention:** The precise sequence of events and motivations surrounding Tughril's entry into Baghdad in 1055—whether it was primarily a response to the Caliph's invitation or a calculated move to eliminate the Buyids and expand his own power—is detailed differently across sources.
