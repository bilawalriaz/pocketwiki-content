# Robert Schumann

## Overview
Robert Schumann (1810–1856) was a German composer, pianist, and music critic who epitomized the early Romantic era. Initially torn between law and music, a debilitating hand injury forced him to abandon a virtuoso career and focus on composition. His output—spanning piano cycles, Lieder, symphonies, chamber music, and the opera *Genoveva*—is defined by poetic imagination, literary allusions, and the projection of two contrasting artistic personas: the impetuous "Florestan" and the introspective "Eusebius." He co-founded the *Neue Zeitschrift für Musik*, using it to champion progressive composers like Chopin and the young Johannes Brahms. His marriage to the pianist Clara Wieck in 1840, after a bitter legal battle with her father, provided emotional stability that fueled his most prolific period. Chronic mental illness, likely congenital, led to a suicide attempt in 1854 and two years of confinement in a sanatorium, where he died at 46. While his late works were long dismissed as products of decline, modern scholarship and historically informed performance have rehabilitated their reputation, affirming his influence on composers from Brahms and Mahler to Debussy and Rihm.

## Timeline
- **8 June 1810** — Born in Zwickau, Saxony, to August Schumann (bookseller/publisher) and Johanna Christiane.
- **1828–1830** — Studies law at Leipzig and Heidelberg Universities; secretly pursues music, hearing Paganini and Italian opera.
- **1830** — Commits to music career; begins piano study with Friedrich Wieck; publishes Op. 1 (*Abegg Variations*).
- **1831–1834** — Hand injury ends pianist ambitions; studies counterpoint with Heinrich Dorn; invents Florestan/Eusebius personas.
- **1834** — Co-founds *Neue Zeitschrift für Musik*; edits it for ten years.
- **1835–1839** — Composes major piano cycles: *Carnaval*, *Symphonic Studies*, *Kreisleriana*, *Davidsbündlertänze*, *Kinderszenen*.
- **1836–1840** — Courts Clara Wieck; overcomes Friedrich Wieck's opposition via lawsuit; marries 12 Sept 1840.
- **1840** — *Liederjahr* (Year of Song): writes over half his total Lieder output, including *Dichterliebe* and *Frauenliebe und Leben*.
- **1841–1842** — Turns to orchestral music (Symphony No. 1 "Spring") and chamber music (Piano Quintet, three String Quartets).
- **1843** — Severe mental crisis; recovers to compose oratorio *Das Paradies und die Peri*; teaches at Leipzig Conservatory.
- **1844–1845** — Tour of Russia; moves to Dresden; health improves; completes Piano Concerto and Symphony No. 2.
- **1848–1850** — Composes opera *Genoveva* (premiered 1850); appointed Music Director in Düsseldorf.
- **1850–1853** — Composes Symphony No. 3 "Rhenish" and Cello Concerto; meets Brahms (1853), hails him in "Neue Bahnen."
- **27 Feb 1854** — Attempts suicide by jumping into Rhine; admitted to Endenich sanatorium near Bonn.
- **29 July 1856** — Dies at Endenich, aged 46; cause recorded as pneumonia.

## Body

### Early Life and the Turn to Music
Schumann was born into a literary household; his father August was a successful publisher and translator of Byron and Scott, granting Robert early immersion in Romantic literature, particularly Jean Paul, whose fantasy and sensibility shaped his aesthetic. He was not a child prodigy, but showed early pianistic talent for portraying character in melody. After his father’s death in 1826, his mother pressed him toward law. He enrolled at Leipzig (1828) and Heidelberg (1829), but neglected lectures for music, discovering Schubert and hearing Paganini. In 1830, declaring his life a "twenty-year struggle between poetry and prose," he secured his mother’s reluctant blessing to study piano with Friedrich Wieck in Leipzig, aiming for a virtuoso career within three years.

### The 1830s: Injury, Journalism, and the Davidsbündler
A progressive paralysis of the right hand (cause uncertain) ended his performing hopes by 1832, redirecting him to composition and criticism. His early piano works—*Papillons* (Op. 2), *Carnaval* (Op. 9), *Symphonic Studies* (Op. 13)—employed musical cryptograms (e.g., the A-S-C-H motif) and programmatic narratives. He formalized his dual artistic nature as **Florestan** (impetuous) and **Eusebius** (poetic), using these personas in his writings for the *Neue Zeitschrift für Musik*, which he edited from 1835. He founded the imaginary **Davidsbündler** (League of David), a fellowship of like-minded artists fighting musical philistinism. During this decade he befriended Mendelssohn (who premiered his rediscovered Schubert "Great" C major Symphony in 1839) and fell in love with Wieck’s daughter Clara, a celebrated pianist. Wieck’s vehement opposition triggered a four-year legal battle, resolved only by a court ruling allowing their marriage in 1840.

### The 1840s: Liederjahr, Symphonies, and Chamber Music
Marriage brought domestic stability and a creative explosion. 1840 was his *Liederjahr*: he composed over 130 songs, including the cycles *Dichterliebe* (Heine), *Frauenliebe und Leben* (Chamisso), and two *Liederkreise* (Heine/Eichendorff), establishing an equal partnership of voice and piano with significant preludes/postludes. In 1841 he tackled orchestral music; Mendelssohn premiered Symphony No. 1 "Spring." 1842 became his chamber music year: after studying Haydn and Mozart, he produced three String Quartets, the Piano Quintet (dedicated to Clara, quoting her theme), and the Piano Quartet, standardizing the piano+string quartet ensemble for later composers. A severe mental breakdown in early 1843 interrupted his momentum, but he recovered to compose the successful secular oratorio *Das Paradies und die Peri* and teach at Mendelssohn’s new Leipzig Conservatory. A taxing 1844 Russian tour with Clara worsened his health (insomnia, auditory disturbances, phobias), prompting a move to Dresden. There, studying counterpoint with Clara, he completed the Piano Concerto (1845) and Symphony No. 2 (1846), and began the opera *Genoveva* (finished 1848).

### The 1850s: Düsseldorf, Brahms, and Decline
*Genoveva* premiered in Leipzig (1850) but failed; critics found it neither tuneful enough for the public nor progressive enough for Wagnerians. Seeking income, Schumann accepted the Düsseldorf Music Directorship (1850). His shyness and mental instability made conducting and administration impossible; relations with the orchestra deteriorated, forcing his resignation in 1853. Despite this, he composed the "Rhenish" Symphony No. 3 and Cello Concerto in 1850, and revised earlier works (Symphony No. 4, *Symphonic Studies*). In 1853, the 20-year-old Johannes Brahms arrived with a letter from Joachim. Schumann, overwhelmed, hailed him in his final *Neue Zeitschrift* article "Neue Bahnen" as the messiah of German music. Brahms became a crucial support to Clara as Schumann’s health collapsed. In February 1854, Schumann threw himself into the Rhine; rescued, he entered Dr. Richarz’s sanatorium at Endenich. The director barred Clara from visiting for over two years, allowing only friends like Brahms and Joachim. Schumann died there in 1856.

### Works: Piano, Song, and Orchestra
**Solo Piano:** Schumann’s reputation rests most securely here. He moved from virtuoso showpieces (*Abegg Variations*) to the Romantic **character piece cycle**—interrelated miniatures with fanciful titles (*Carnaval*, *Kreisleriana*, *Kinderszenen*, *Waldszenen*). These blend Florestan/Eusebius contrasts, literary allusions, and ciphers (musical codes for names). He also wrote didactic works (*Album für die Jugend*) and lighter salon pieces he privately deemed "feeble."
**Songs (Lieder):** Ranked with Schubert, Brahms, and Wolf as a master of the German Lied. His 300+ settings favor later Romantics (Heine, Eichendorff, Mörike) over Goethe/Schiller. The 1840 cycles are landmarks; complete cycle performances became standard only posthumously (first *Dichterliebe* in 1861 with Brahms at piano). His accompaniments are quasi-independent piano commentaries.
**Orchestral:** Four symphonies. No. 1 "Spring" (1841) was initially praised; No. 4 originated as a 1841 D minor symphony, heavily revised in 1851 (Brahms preferred the lighter original). No. 2 (1846) is classically structured; No. 3 "Rhenish" (1850) has five movements with pictorial elements. The Piano Concerto (1845), evolving from a 1841 *Phantasie*, is a repertoire staple, its opening juxtaposing Florestan vigor and Eusebius lyricism. The Cello Concerto (1850) and Concert Piece for Four Horns (1849) remain performed; the Violin Concerto (1853) is rarer. Conductors from Mahler to Gardiner have debated his orchestration, with historically informed performance (smaller forces) improving modern reception.
**Chamber:** The Piano Quintet and Quartet (1842) are masterpieces; the Quintet’s "public" brilliance contrasts the Quartet’s intimacy. Three Piano Trios (1847, 1851) and late Violin Sonatas (1851) follow. He avoided string quartets after Op. 41, daunted by Beethoven. He wrote flexibly for unusual ensembles (e.g., *Adagio and Allegro* Op. 70 for horn/violin/cello).
**Opera and Choral:** *Genoveva* (1850) failed due to its non-dramatic, Lied-like structure; defenders (Harnoncourt) argue it creates a new genre, while detractors find it static. The oratorio *Das Paradies und die Peri* (1843) was his greatest lifetime success, admired by Wagner and Tchaikovsky. *Szenen aus Goethes Faust* (1844–53), a hybrid oratorio/opera, was never performed complete in his life.

### Legacy and Reception
During his life, Schumann was famed for piano music and songs; orchestral and operatic works were marginal. A posthumous "late style" stigma persisted (Draeseke: "started as a genius and ended as a talent"), attributed to mental decline or conservative technique. Since the late 20th century, period-performance practice and increased recordings have rehabilitated late works. His influence is vast: **French** (Fauré, Debussy, Ravel—*Kinderszenen* inspired *Children's Corner* and *Ma mère l'Oye*); **Russian** (Rubinstein, Tchaikovsky); **German** (Mahler, Strauss, Schoenberg, Rihm). He was a figurehead for the conservative "War of the Romantics" faction (Clara, Brahms, Joachim, Hanslick) against Liszt/Wagner. The Clara/Brahms edition (1879–87) suppressed late works; the New Schumann Complete Edition (49 vols., finished 2023) restores them. Zwickau preserves his birthplace as a museum; the International Schumann Competition and Joan Chissell Prize honor him.

## Terms
- ****Florestan / Eusebius**** — Schumann’s two invented artistic alter egos: Florestan represents the impetuous, passionate, extroverted side; Eusebius the dreamy, introspective, poetic side. They appear as characters in his criticism and as musical signatures in his compositions.
- ****Davidsbündler (League of David)**** — An imaginary fellowship of musicians created by Schumann (named for the biblical David fighting the Philistines) to represent progressive artistic ideals against musical conservatism; members included his real friends writing under pseudonyms in his journal.
- ****Musical Cryptogram**** — A sequence of notes derived from letters of a name or word (e.g., A-S-C-H for the town Asch in *Carnaval*; A-B-E-G-G for the fictional Countess Abegg in Op. 1), used by Schumann as structural and personal motifs.
- ****Character Piece**** — A short instrumental work, usually for piano, depicting a specific mood, scene, or character (e.g., *Kinderszenen*). Schumann elevated the cycle of interrelated character pieces into a major Romantic form.
- ****Liederjahr (Year of Song)**** — 1840, when Schumann composed over half his total Lieder output (approx. 130 songs), including the major cycles *Dichterliebe*, *Frauenliebe und Leben*, and two *Liederkreise*, triggered by his marriage to Clara.
- ****Neue Zeitschrift für Musik**** — Music journal co-founded by Schumann in 1834; he edited it for ten years. It championed Romantic composers (Chopin, Berlioz, Brahms) and propagated his aesthetic theories via the Florestan/Eusebius dialogues.
- ****Historically Informed Performance (HIP)**** — An approach using period instruments, smaller orchestral forces, and period techniques. Applied to Schumann from the 1990s onward, it clarified textures and rehabilitated the reputation of his orchestration.
- ****War of the Romantics**** — Mid-to-late 19th-century aesthetic conflict: Schumann/Clara/Brahms/Joachim/Hanslick (absolute music, Beethoven tradition) vs. Liszt/Wagner/Draeseke (program music, chromatic harmony, "music of the future").
- ****Das Paradies und die Peri**** — Schumann’s secular oratorio (1843), based on Thomas Moore’s *Lalla Rookh*. His most popular large-scale work during his life, later neglected, now revived as a masterpiece of exotic Romanticism.
- ****Endenich Sanatorium**** — Private asylum near Bonn where Schumann lived from March 1854 until his death in July 1856, after a suicide attempt. Director Dr. Richarz restricted family visits, believing they hindered recovery.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Nature of mental illness:** The source notes the condition "may have been congenital," affecting his father and sister. Modern retrospective diagnoses (bipolar disorder, syphilis, brain tumor) are not in the source text; the source records only symptoms (melancholia, auditory hallucinations, phobias, paralysis) and the sanatorium director’s treatment philosophy.
*   **Quality of late works (post-1845):** A 19th/early-20th century consensus (Draeseke, Fuller Maitland) held that declining health and orthodox technique degraded his inspiration. The source reports a modern reversal (Hall, Holliger) attributing the earlier neglect to performance tradition and lack of period practice, but notes the view "is still his piano works and songs from the 1830s and 1840s on which his reputation is primarily based."
*   **Orchestration competence:** Contemporary critics and later conductors (Mahler, Toscanini, Szell) routinely re-orchestrated his symphonies, deeming them clumsy or "inflated piano music." The scholar Julius Harrison argued alterations are fruitless, as the "essence" lies in his personal faults. The source cites HIP (smaller forces) as the reason for improved modern regard.
*   **Genoveva’s dramatic viability:** The source presents opposing views: the consensus (Jensen, Kobbé) calls it undramatic and unstageworthy ("an evening of Lieder"); Nikolaus Harnoncourt argues critics misjudged it by conventional operatic standards; Victoria Bond (1987 US stage premiere) found it "full of high drama and supercharged emotion" and "very stageworthy."
*   **Clara and Brahms’s editorial suppression:** The 1879–87 complete edition deliberately omitted late works deemed tainted by insanity. The New Complete Edition (2023) restores them, but the source does not detail specific suppressed pieces or the exact criteria used by the editors.
