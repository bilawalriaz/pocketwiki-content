# History of Argentina

## Overview
Argentina's history spans pre-Columbian settlements to the present, marked by Spanish colonization, independence struggles, internal conflicts, economic booms and crises, and a turbulent path to democracy. Its development was shaped by European immigration, agricultural exports, the rise and fall of Peronism, military dictatorships, and ongoing economic volatility.

## Timeline
- **c. 13,000 years ago** — First human settlements in southern Patagonia.
- **1516** — Spanish expedition led by Juan Díaz de Solís arrives at the Río de la Plata.
- **1776** — Spanish Crown establishes the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata.
- **1810** — May Revolution ousts the Spanish viceroy, beginning the independence process.
- **1816** — Congress of Tucumán declares independence from Spain.
- **1853** — Constitution of 1853 establishes a federal republic.
- **1880** — Julio Argentino Roca becomes president; Buenos Aires is federalized as the capital.
- **1912** — Sáenz Peña Law establishes universal, secret, and mandatory male suffrage.
- **1930** — Military coup overthrows President Hipólito Yrigoyen, starting the "Infamous Decade."
- **1946** — Juan Perón is elected president, launching the Peronist era.
- **1976** — Military coup establishes the "National Reorganization Process" dictatorship.
- **1983** — Democracy is restored with the election of Raúl Alfonsín.
- **2001** — Severe economic crisis leads to political collapse and multiple presidents in one week.
- **2023** — Javier Milei, a far-right outsider, wins the presidential election.

## Body

### Pre-Columbian and Colonial Era
The region was sparsely populated by hunter-gatherers and some agricultural societies. The Inca Empire conquered parts of the northwest in the late 15th century. Spanish exploration began in 1516; the first Buenos Aires settlement (1536) was abandoned. Permanent colonization started later with Córdoba (1573) and a second Buenos Aires (1580). Initially part of the Viceroyalty of Peru, Spain created the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata in 1776 to counter Portuguese expansion. Buenos Aires grew as a commercial port despite Spanish trade restrictions. British invasions in 1806-07 were repelled locally, boosting creole confidence.

### Independence and Civil Wars
The Napoleonic invasion of Spain in 1808 created a power vacuum, leading to the May Revolution of 1810. A long war for independence followed, led by figures like Manuel Belgrano and José de San Martín, who crossed the Andes to liberate Chile and Peru. Independence was formally declared in 1816. After defeating Spain in 1824, the new nation descended into civil wars between Unitarians (centralists) and Federalists (provincial autonomists). The dominant federalist Juan Manuel de Rosas ruled Buenos Aires Province from 1829 to 1852. His defeat at the Battle of Caseros led to the 1853 Constitution, establishing a federal system. Buenos Aires initially seceded but rejoined in 1862 under President Bartolomé Mitre.

### Conservative Republic and Rise of the Radicals (1880–1930)
From 1880, the National Autonomist Party (PAN) dominated politics, presiding over economic growth based on agricultural exports and massive European immigration. This era saw the "Conquest of the Desert" (1878-85), a military campaign that subdued indigenous populations and opened Patagonia. Social tensions led to the formation of the Radical Civic Union (UCR), which demanded universal male suffrage. The Sáenz Peña Law (1912) granted this, and in 1916, the UCR's Hipólito Yrigoyen won the presidency. His governments (1916-22, 1928-30) promoted social reforms but also faced labor unrest. A 1930 military coup ended this democratic period.

### Peronism, Dictatorship, and Democracy (1930–1983)
The "Infamous Decade" (1930-43) was marked by electoral fraud. A 1943 military coup brought Juan Perón to power. His populist movement, Peronism, emphasized workers' rights and state intervention. He was overthrown in 1955, leading to unstable civilian rule where Peronism was banned. A 1966 military coup faced growing guerrilla violence. Perón returned in 1973, but his death in 1974 left his wife Isabel to preside over economic collapse. The 1976 military coup launched the "National Reorganization Process," a dictatorship responsible for the "Dirty War," during which an estimated 30,000 people were "disappeared." The regime's defeat in the 1982 Falklands War led to a return to democracy in 1983.

### Modern Argentina (1983–Present)
Democracy was restored under Raúl Alfonsín (1983-89), who addressed human rights crimes but struggled economically. The 1990s under Carlos Menem saw neoliberal reforms. A catastrophic economic crisis in 2001 caused political collapse. The subsequent Kirchner governments (2003-2015) oversaw economic recovery by rejecting IMF policies but faced corruption accusations. Mauricio Macri (2015-19) implemented market-oriented reforms. Alberto Fernández (2019-23) governed during the COVID-19 pandemic and economic turmoil. In 2023, Javier Milei, a libertarian outsider, won the presidency promising radical spending cuts to combat hyperinflation and poverty.

## Terms
- **Caudillo**: A political-military leader, often a provincial strongman, prominent in 19th-century Argentina.
- **Peronism**: A populist political movement based on Juan Perón's legacy, emphasizing social justice, economic nationalism, and state intervention.
- **Dirty War (Guerra Sucia)**: The period of state terrorism (c. 1974-1983) under military rule, characterized by the "disappearance" of political dissidents.
- **Import Substitution Industrialization (ISI)**: An economic policy aimed at reducing foreign dependency by developing domestic industries.
- **Corralito**: A 2001 measure that froze bank deposits to prevent a run on the banks during a severe economic crisis.
- **Infamous Decade (Década Infame)**: The period from 1930 to 1943, characterized by electoral fraud and corruption.
- **National Reorganization Process (Proceso de Reorganización Nacional)**: The name given by the 1976-1983 military dictatorship to its regime.
- **Sáenz Peña Law (1912)**: Established universal, secret, and mandatory male suffrage in Argentina.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Interpretations of Juan Manuel de Rosas**: Liberal historiography often portrays him as a dictator, while revisionist historians support him for defending national sovereignty.
- **The "Dirty War" and its Legacy**: Debates continue over the exact number of victims (officially around 30,000 "disappeared"), civilian complicity, and the balance between justice and reconciliation.
- **Economic Model**: A persistent debate exists between proponents of neoliberal policies (like Macri's) and those favoring state intervention and social welfare (like the Kirchners').
- **Role of the Military**: Argentina's history of frequent military coups raises ongoing questions about the armed forces' proper role in a democratic society.
