# Peopling of the Americas

## Overview
The peopling of the Americas refers to the initial human migration from Asia into North and South America, a foundational event in human prehistory. It is a complex, multi-disciplinary topic involving archaeology, genetics, and geology, with ongoing scholarly debate about the precise timing, routes, and source populations of the first migrants. Understanding this process is key to explaining the origins and diversity of Indigenous peoples of the Americas.

## Timeline
- **c. 36,000–25,000 BP** — Genetic models suggest a Beringian population split from Siberian groups and became isolated.
- **c. 26,000–19,000 BP** — Last Glacial Maximum (LGM); sea levels drop, creating the Beringia land bridge.
- **c. 21,000 BP** — Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets coalesce, blocking an inland migration corridor.
- **c. 16,000–15,000 BP** — Pacific coastal route becomes ice-free; earliest widely accepted archaeological sites in the Americas date to this period.
- **c. 14,000 BP** — Swan Point (Alaska) shows undisputed human habitation; ice-free corridor begins to open.
- **c. 13,000–12,600 BP** — Clovis culture flourishes in North America.
- **c. 11,500 BP** — Ice-free corridor becomes viable for human migration; Beringia is submerged.

## Body

### Migration Routes and Timing
The primary debate centers on how and when people first entered the Americas. The **Clovis First theory**, which proposed migration via an inland ice-free corridor around 13,000 years ago, has been largely superseded. Evidence from pre-Clovis sites like Monte Verde, Chile (14,500 BP) and Buttermilk Creek, Texas (15,500 BP) indicates earlier arrival. Two main routes are now considered: an **interior route** through an ice-free corridor between the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets, which opened around 13,000–12,000 BP, and a **Pacific coastal route**, which may have been viable by 16,000 BP. The coastal route, possibly involving maritime travel, is supported by the rapid southward spread of populations and the discovery of the mtDNA haplogroup D4h3a along the Pacific coast. The precise date of first arrival remains debated, with evidence ranging from ~15,000 BP to potentially earlier.

### Evidence and Chronology
Archaeological evidence has progressively pushed back the date of human presence. The **White Sands footprints** in New Mexico, dated to between 21,000 and 23,000 years ago, provide compelling but debated evidence for a pre-LGM presence. Other contested sites include Chiquihuite Cave, Mexico (artifacts dated to 26,000 BP, but their human origin is disputed) and the Cerutti Mastodon site, California (130,000 BP, rejected by most scholars). The oldest undisputed evidence of habitation is from Swan Point, Alaska, at 14,000 BP. Genetic studies offer complementary timelines, with models proposing migration into Beringia between 40,000 and 25,000 BP, followed by isolation and later dispersal into the Americas.

### Source Populations and Genetics
Genetic analysis indicates that Indigenous Americans descend from a founding population formed in Siberia by the admixture of **Ancient North Eurasians** and an ancient East Asian population. The exact location of this admixture is debated, with theories placing it in southern Siberia or Northeastern Siberia (the **"Beringian standstill hypothesis"**). Mitochondrial DNA haplogroups A, B, C, D, and X are characteristic of Native Americans, with subclades like D4h3a tracing coastal migration. A small, unexplained Australasian genetic component (~3%) is found in some Amazonian populations and the ancient Lagoa Santa specimen, though overall genetic evidence supports a single major migration from Beringia.

### Environmental Context
The migration was shaped by the **Wisconsin glaciation**. During the LGM, lowered sea levels created the Beringia land bridge, but extensive ice sheets blocked inland routes. The environment was a cold, herb-dominated tundra. As glaciers retreated, coastal and then inland corridors opened. The **ice-free corridor** between the ice sheets did not become ecologically viable for human passage until about 11,500 BP, after the earliest accepted archaeological sites. The deglaciation also exposed a productive coastal plain with kelp forests, which may have facilitated a maritime migration route.

## Terms
- **Paleo-Indians**: The earliest known peoples of the Americas, present before roughly 10,000 years ago.
- **Beringia**: The land bridge that connected northeastern Siberia and western Alaska during periods of low sea level.
- **Last Glacial Maximum (LGM)**: The period of peak glaciation during the last ice age, c. 26,000 to 19,000 years ago.
- **Clovis First theory**: The now-disputed hypothesis that the Clovis culture represents the earliest human presence in the Americas.
- **Ice-free corridor**: A passable route between the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets in interior North America.
- **Ancient North Eurasians (ANE)**: A genetically distinct population from Upper Paleolithic Siberia that contributed ancestry to Native Americans.
- **mtDNA haplogroups**: Genetic lineages traced through mitochondrial DNA, used to map maternal ancestry and migration routes.
- **Lithic artifacts**: Stone tools or debris, key evidence for identifying prehistoric human activity.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Timing of first arrival**: Is the earliest presence ~15,000 years ago (based on widely accepted sites) or significantly earlier, as suggested by contested evidence like White Sands?
- **Migration route(s)**: Did the first migrants primarily use an inland ice-free corridor, a coastal maritime route, or both at different times?
- **Source population dynamics**: Where exactly did the ancestral Native American population form, and was it a single admixture event or multiple?
- **Interpretation of contested sites**: Are finds like the Chiquihuite Cave stones or the Cerutti Mastodon site evidence of human activity?
- **Genetic anomalies**: What explains the small Australasian genetic component in some Amazonian populations?
