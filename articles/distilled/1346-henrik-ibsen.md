# Henrik Ibsen

## Overview

Henrik Ibsen (1828–1906) was a Norwegian playwright widely regarded as the father of modern drama and the foremost dramatist of the 19th century. He pioneered theatrical realism, moving from early lyrical and surreal works to later plays that exposed the moral and social contradictions of bourgeois society. His works, written in Dano-Norwegian and published by Gyldendal, include *Brand*, *Peer Gynt*, *A Doll's House*, *Ghosts*, *An Enemy of the People*, *The Wild Duck*, *Rosmersholm*, *Hedda Gabler*, and *When We Dead Awaken*. Ibsen's plays sparked international controversy for their unflinching examination of family, morality, and individualism, influencing writers such as George Bernard Shaw, Oscar Wilde, and James Joyce.

## Body

### Early Life and Family Background

Ibsen was born into the merchant elite of Skien, a prosperous port town in Telemark. His father, Knud Ibsen, was a timber and luxury goods merchant, and his mother, Marichen Altenburg, came from a prominent local family. The Ibsen family was deeply embedded in the Paus family network, part of an educated, upper-class elite that traced its roots to merchants, shipowners, and civil servants since the 16th century. Contrary to romanticized early biographies, modern scholarship—particularly by Jørgen Haave and Jon Nygaard—has refuted claims of severe childhood hardship, arguing instead that Ibsen enjoyed a comfortable upbringing within Norway’s patrician class. His parents’ close familial relationship, sometimes described as near-sibling-like, may have influenced themes of intimacy and transgression in plays like *Rosmersholm*.

### Career Development and Exile

At fifteen, Ibsen left school to work as a pharmacist in Grimstad, where he began writing plays. After an unsuccessful attempt to enter university, he committed fully to theater, working at Det norske Theater in Bergen and later becoming director of the Christiania Theatre. In 1864, he left Norway for Italy in self-imposed exile, spending 27 years in Rome, Dresden, and Munich. This period saw his rise to international prominence with *Brand* (1865) and *Peer Gynt* (1867), the latter influenced by Kierkegaard. During exile, he developed the concept of the "drama of ideas," embedding personal philosophy into dramatic form.

### Major Works and Thematic Evolution

Ibsen’s early works, such as *Peer Gynt*, featured lyrical and surreal elements. After abandoning verse, he embraced prose realism, producing plays that scrutinized the façades of bourgeois life. *A Doll’s House* (1879) critiqued marital roles and gender expectations, while *Ghosts* (1881) tackled venereal disease and inherited moral corruption. *An Enemy of the People* (1882) shifted focus from domestic to communal conflict, portraying the individual versus the masses. Later works like *The Wild Duck* (1884) and *Hedda Gabler* (1890) delved into psychological complexity, exploring internal moral struggles rather than overt social denunciation. Ibsen considered *Emperor and Galilean* (1873) his masterpiece, though it received little contemporary acclaim.

### Reception and Legacy

Ibsen’s plays caused uproar across Europe and America, particularly from 1879 onward. While early critics portrayed him as a misunderstood avant-gardist, scholars like Narve Fulsås and Tore Rem argue that he enjoyed significant popular and critical success in Norway. His works were translated into multiple languages and remained central to intellectual discourse. He was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature three times (1902–1904). Beyond Europe, Ibsen profoundly influenced Japanese drama, helping launch the modern Shingeki movement. Today, he is commemorated globally through festivals, awards, and institutions, including the Ibsen Museum in Oslo and the annual Delhi Ibsen Festival.

## Terms

- **Drama of ideas**: Ibsen’s term for plays that explore philosophical and moral questions through character and plot.
- **Theatrical realism**: A movement emphasizing realistic depiction of everyday life and ordinary people, pioneered by Ibsen.
- **Patrician elite**: The educated, upper-class families in Norway, including the Ibsens and Pauses, who dominated commerce and public office.
- **Problem play**: A genre of drama addressing controversial social issues, exemplified by Ibsen’s works.
- **Self-imposed exile**: Ibsen’s 27-year absence from Norway (1864–1891), spent primarily in Italy and Germany.
- **Dano-Norwegian**: The written standard language Ibsen used for his plays, reflecting Norway’s linguistic ties to Denmark.
- **Summons of the Ideal**: A concept in *The Wild Duck* representing the pursuit of absolute truth, embodied by the character Gregers Werle.
- **Franc tireur**: A term Ibsen used to describe himself as a lone observer critiquing society from the margins.

## Debates and Open Questions

**Childhood and Biography**: Early biographies depicted Ibsen as a self-made genius who overcame poverty and familial tyranny. Modern scholarship, led by Jørgen Haave and Jon Nygaard, challenges this narrative, asserting that Ibsen came from a privileged background and had a happy childhood. The extent to which his plays reflect personal experience versus artistic invention remains debated.

**Literary Interpretation**: Some scholars advocate for biographical readings of Ibsen’s works, linking characters directly to family members. Others, including Haave, caution against naive literal interpretations, arguing that dramatizing family dynamics does not equate to direct representation.

**Political Views**: Ibsen expressed anarchist sympathies, advocating for the abolition of the state. However, his political stance evolved, and he later distanced himself from radical movements, maintaining a skeptical stance toward both left and right ideologies.

**Reception History**: While Ibsen was controversial in his time, recent scholarship questions the myth of widespread hostility, suggesting instead that he achieved considerable popularity and respect in Norway during his lifetime.
