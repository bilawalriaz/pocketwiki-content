# Mikhail Lermontov

## Overview
Mikhail Lermontov (1814–1841) was a Russian Romantic poet, novelist, and painter, widely regarded as the most significant Russian literary figure after Alexander Pushkin’s death. Born into a noble family of Scottish descent, he was raised by his domineering grandmother after his mother’s early death and his father’s estrangement. His fragile health and isolated upbringing fostered a introspective, rebellious personality. Lermontov’s work synthesizes Byronic Romanticism with Russian realism, founding the tradition of the psychological novel through *A Hero of Our Time* (1840). His poem "Death of the Poet" (1837), condemning high society for Pushkin’s death, brought him fame and exile to the Caucasus—a region he loved since childhood and which became the central landscape of his imagination. He died at age 26 in a duel with a fellow officer, Nikolai Martynov.

## Body

### Family Origins and Early Childhood
Lermontov descended from the Scottish Learmonth family, tracing back to Yuri (George) Learmonth, a 17th-century officer who settled in Russia. Family legend claimed descent from the 13th-century poet Thomas the Rhymer. His father, Captain Yuri Petrovich Lermontov, married Maria Mikhaylovna Arsenyeva, a wealthy Stolypin heiress, in a union opposed by her mother, Elizaveta Arsenyeva. After Maria’s death from tuberculosis in 1817 (aged 21), a bitter custody battle ensued. Arsenyeva secured guardianship until Lermontov turned 16, effectively exiling his father, who died of consumption in 1831. This feud informed Lermontov’s early drama *Menschen und Leidenschaften* (1830).

Raised in luxury at Tarkhany (Penza Oblast), Lermontov received a multilingual home education (French, German, English), musical training, and art instruction. However, he suffered from scrofula and rickets (causing bow-leggedness), limiting his physical activity and deepening his introspection. His grandmother’s control isolated him from peers and his father, fostering a "lonely and withdrawn" character noted by biographers. Trips to the Caucasus in 1819, 1820, and 1825 for his health imprinted the mountains as a "sacred" landscape and sparked his first romantic infatuation at age nine.

### Education and Literary Awakening
In 1827, Lermontov moved to Moscow, entering the University’s boarding school for nobility in 1829. Tutored by poet Alexey Merzlyakov, he devoured Russian classics (Derzhavin, Zhukovsky, Pushkin) and European Romantics, particularly Lord Byron, to whom he remained devoted despite tutors’ attempts to redirect him. By 1829, he had written early Romantic poems like *The Corsair*, *The Culprit*, and the first version of *The Demon*, showing strong Pushkin and Byron influence. He also developed a talent for caricature and sardonic wit, earning a reputation for "poisonous" epigrams.

At Moscow University (1830–1832), Lermontov avoided radical student circles (Belinsky, Herzen, Stankevich), preferring an aristocratic clique that nonetheless found him aloof and obnoxious. He participated in the 1831 "Malov scandal" (a student riot against a professor) but escaped punishment. His father’s death in 1831 triggered suicidal ideation, reflected in his dramas ending in protagonist suicide. Politically engaged verses like *The Prophecy* and *The Sail* emerged, alongside masterpieces *The Angel* (1831) and *The Sail* (1831). Clashes with professors led him to leave in June 1832 with a two-year certificate.

### Military Service and St. Petersburg Society (1832–1837)
Unable to transfer to St. Petersburg University, Lermontov entered the elite School of Cavalry Junkers under family pressure, graduating in November 1832 as a cornet in the Life-Guard Hussar Regiment. The regiment’s culture of drinking, gambling, and "wenching" clashed with his literary ambitions, which he concealed. A riding accident broke his knee, leaving a permanent limp. He circulated pornographic and sadistic "Junkers poems" in manuscript, damaging his reputation among polite society.

Despite this, he wrote seriously: finishing *The Demon*, *Boyarin Orsha*, *The Tambov Treasurer's Wife*, and the play *Masquerade* (1835). Through friend Svyatoslav Rayevsky, he met editor Andrey Krayevsky. His first authorized publication, *Khadji-Abrek* (1835), appeared without his consent. By 1836, he was a fixture in high society, supported lavishly by his grandmother, yet leading a "double life" of dissipation and secret literary labor.

### "Death of the Poet" and First Exile (1837)
Pushkin’s death in a duel (January 1837) ignited Lermontov’s outrage at society’s sympathy for the killer, Georges d'Anthès. He composed *Death of the Poet* in minutes; its final 16 lines accused the court "pillars" of complicity, depicting them as "hangmen who kill liberty, genius, and glory" facing divine judgment. The poem spread instantly, hailing him as "Pushkin’s heir." Nicholas I, receiving an anonymous copy subtitled "The Call for Revolution," ordered Lermontov’s arrest. He was jailed in the Peter and Paul Fortress, then exiled as a cornet to the Nizhegorodsky Dragoons in the Caucasus. During the investigation, Lermontov implicated Rayevsky, who received a harsher punishment (deportation to Olonets).

### First Caucasus Exile and Creative Peak (1837–1838)
The Caucasus suited Lermontov: he studied local languages (Kumyk), painted, and wrote prolifically. He traveled the Caucasian line from Kizlyar to Taman, visited Georgia, and met Decembrists (Alexander Odoyevsky) and Georgian intellectuals (Alexander Chavchavadze). Though some Decembrists found his views "chaotic" and "low-brow," he produced *The Song of the Merchant Kalashnikov* (published 1837 after Zhukovsky intervened with the censor), *Borodino*, and *Valerik*. Transferred to the Grodno Regiment at Nizhny Novgorod via General Benckendorff’s intercession, he returned to St. Petersburg a celebrity in late 1838.

### *A Hero of Our Time* and Second Exile (1838–1841)
Back in the capital, Lermontov joined the Hussar Guard and began *A Hero of Our Time* (1840), a novel of five linked stories centered on Pechorin, a disenchanted, doomed nobleman. Published in *Otechestvennye Zapiski*, it pioneered Russian psychological realism. His 1840 poetry collection (published by Glazunov) included *Heavenly Clouds, Eternal Travelers*.

Bored and ill-tempered in society, Lermontov dueled Ernest de Barante (son of the French ambassador) in February 1840 at the same spot where Pushkin fell. Slightly wounded, he was jailed; visitor Vissarion Belinsky admired his poetry but puzzled over his "dual personality." Grand Duke Mikhail Pavlovich reduced the sentence to Caucasus exile, framing the duel as defending Russian honor. Assigned to the Tengin Infantry Regiment on the Chechen front (Imam Shamil’s forces), Lermontov commanded a Cossack unit in the July 1840 Battle of Valerik River, earning official praise for "great courage" but criticism from officers for boasting and leading a "gang of reckless thugs."

### Final Months and Death
In early 1841, Lermontov visited St. Petersburg on leave, enjoying his happiest period—writing daily, reciting to salons, planning a literary journal. However, he failed to report to his commander, attending a ball instead where he inadvertently snubbed the Tsar’s daughters. War Minister Count Kleinmichel ordered him back to the Caucasus within 24 hours. A gypsy fortune-teller (who had predicted Pushkin’s death) told him: "You will get your retirement, but of such a kind after which you won't ask for more."

Arriving in Pyatigorsk in May 1841, Lermontov feigned illness to avoid duty, spending weeks in a social whirl. His cadet-school friend Nikolai Martynov, affecting a Circassian costume and romantic hero pose reminiscent of Lermontov’s own character Grushnitsky, became the target of Lermontov’s merciless teasing. On July 25 (O.S. July 13), Martynov challenged him. The duel occurred July 27 (O.S. July 15) at the foot of Mount Mashuk. Lermontov allegedly intended to fire into the air; Martynov shot first, killing him instantly. He was buried without military honors on July 30; thousands attended. In January 1842, Nicholas I permitted reburial at Tarkhany. His grandmother suffered a stroke and died in 1845.

### Literary Style and Major Works
Lermontov’s career spanned only six years but produced over 30 long poems, 600 short poems, a novel, and five dramas. His early work (late 1820s) imitated Byron’s "outcast avenger" hero. By the early 1830s, poems like *The Angel* and *The Sail* achieved lyrical perfection, blending intimacy with metaphysical yearning. He experimented with prose in the unfinished *Vadim* (1832), set during Pugachev’s Rebellion, marking a turn toward history and folklore.

Two strands defined his 1830s poetry: stark historical poems on medieval Russia (*The Song of the Merchant Kalashnikov*, *Borodino*) and lush, ethnographic Caucasian tales (*Ismail-Bey*, *Mtsyri*). *The Song of the Merchant Kalashnikov* was praised by Belinsky for merging with the "spirit of folklore"; his *Cossack Lullaby* entered actual folk tradition. *The Demon* (begun 1829, finished 1839), a fallen angel’s tragic love for a Georgian maiden, was banned as "atheistic" until 1856 but inspired Vrubel’s paintings and Rubinstein’s opera. *Mtsyri* (1839), a monk choosing fatal freedom over monastic safety, paired with *The Demon* as studies of the "riotous human spirit."

*A Hero of Our Time* (1840) is his masterpiece. Its non-chronological structure and psychological depth divided critics: Nabokov called the prose "dry and drab," "crude as Stendhal’s," while Mirsky deemed it "the best Russian prose ever written... transparent, absolutely adequate to the context." The novel’s title became a byword for the Russian intelligentsia’s existential dilemmas.

### Private Life and Relationships
Lermontov’s romantic life was turbulent and often cruel. His first love (1825) was a nine-year-old girl in the Caucasus. At 16, he pursued his cousin Yekaterina Sushkova, who mocked him as a "clumsy bow-legged boy"; he later exacted public revenge by seducing and discarding her in 1834. An affair with Natalya Ivanova (1830) ended when she chose a richer suitor. His deepest attachment was to another cousin, Varvara Lopukhina (later Bakhmetyeva), whose forced marriage to an older man devastated him; she inspired *Princess Ligovskaya* and the characters Princess Mary and Vera in *A Hero of Our Time*. As a hussar, he cultivated a "Don Juan" reputation, feigning love to "upset matches," though some scandals (e.g., involving Adèle Hommaire de Hell) were fabrications. By 1840, he reportedly sickened of this persona. Biographer John Garrard noted Lermontov viewed his romantic suffering as fated: "fate had ordained it."

## Terms
- ****Byronic hero**** — A literary archetype (from Lord Byron) of a charismatic, rebellious, isolated outcast who defies society; central to Lermontov’s early poetry and Pechorin in *A Hero of Our Time*.
- ****Psychological novel**** — A genre focusing on characters’ inner motives, contradictions, and mental states; Lermontov’s *A Hero of Our Time* founded this tradition in Russia.
- ****Caucasus**** — Mountainous region between the Black and Caspian Seas; Lermontov’s spiritual homeland, setting for his major poems and *A Hero of Our Time*, symbolizing freedom and sublime nature.
- ****Decembrists**** — Russian aristocrats who led a failed 1825 uprising against Nicholas I; exiled to Siberia/Caucasus, some met Lermontov during his first exile.
- ****Scrofula / Rickets**** — Childhood diseases (tuberculous lymphadenitis and vitamin D deficiency) causing Lermontov’s frail health, bow-leggedness, and lifelong physical limitations.
- ****Junkers poems**** — Obscene, sadistic verses circulated in manuscript among cadet-school peers (1832–1834); damaged Lermontov’s reputation and barred his work from polite female readership for decades.
- ****Otechestvennye Zapiski**** — Influential literary journal edited by Andrey Krayevsky; published *A Hero of Our Time* and Lermontov’s late poetry.
- ****Pechorin**** — Protagonist of *A Hero of Our Time*; a "superfluous man"—cynical, bored, self-analytical, destructive—embodying Lermontov’s generation’s spiritual crisis.
- ****Thomas the Rhymer**** — Legendary 13th-century Scottish prophet-poet (Thomas Learmonth); Lermontov’s family claimed descent, commemorated by a 2014 monument in Earlston, Scotland.
- ****Superfluous man**** — A 19th-century Russian literary type: an aristocratic intellectual alienated from society, unable to act meaningfully; Pechorin is the archetype.

## Debates and open questions
- **Autobiography vs. fiction**: Critics debate how far Lermontov’s "confessional" poems and Pechorin reflect his true self versus Romantic posing. Nabokov emphasized the prose’s "crude" artifice; Mirsky its "transparent" perfection.
- **Political stance**: *Death of the Poet* was read as revolutionary, yet Lermontov mocked Decembrist ideals and avoided radical circles. Scholars dispute whether he was a covert dissident or an apolitical aristocrat channeling spleen.
- **Caucasus representation**: Lermontov idealized mountain tribes’ "centuries-proven customs" and freedom, contrasting Pushkin’s view of European influence as progressive. Critics argue whether this reflects genuine ethnographic respect or Romantic exoticism.
- **Duel circumstances**: Lermontov’s alleged intent to fire in the air (deloping) relies on second-hand accounts; Martynov’s motive—humiliation versus honor—remains contested.
- **Literary legacy**: Russian tradition canonizes him as Pushkin’s direct heir; Western scholars (e.g., Lewis Bagby) stress the "unfinished," contradictory persona blurring life and Byronic performance.
