# Umar

## Overview
Umar ibn al-Khattab (c. 584–644) was the second Rashidun caliph, ruling from 634 until his assassination. A senior companion and father-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad, he is a central figure in early Islamic history. His caliphate saw the unprecedented expansion of the Islamic state and the establishment of its administrative and social foundations, making him one of the most influential Muslim rulers.

## Timeline
- **c. 584** — Born in Mecca to the Banu Adi clan of the Quraysh.
- **616** — Converts to Islam after initially opposing Muhammad.
- **622** — Participates in the Migration (Hijra) to Medina.
- **624-630** — Fights in nearly all of Muhammad's battles, including Badr, Uhud, and the conquest of Mecca.
- **June 632** — After Muhammad's death, helps secure the caliphate for Abu Bakr at the Saqifah assembly.
- **634** — Succeeds Abu Bakr as caliph.
- **636-637** — Muslim forces defeat the Byzantines at Yarmouk and conquer Jerusalem.
- **637-639** — Conquers the Sasanian Empire, including the decisive Battle of al-Qadisiyyah (636) and Battle of Nahavand (642).
- **638** — Introduces the Hijri calendar.
- **644** — Assassinated by the Persian slave Abu Lu'lu'a Firuz.

## Body

### Early Life and Conversion
Umar was born in Mecca into a family of modest standing but prominent men. He was literate, trained in martial arts, and known for his physical strength and fiery temper. Initially a fierce opponent of Islam, he set out to kill Muhammad in 616. However, after a confrontation with his sister Fatima and brother-in-law who were reciting the Quran, he converted. His conversion was a turning point for the early Muslim community, as he began to pray openly at the Kaaba, challenging the Quraysh's persecution.

### Service Under Muhammad and the First Caliphate
Umar participated in the Migration to Medina and fought in all of Muhammad's major battles. After Muhammad's death in 632, he played a key role in the Saqifah assembly, helping to secure the caliphate for Abu Bakr. He served as Abu Bakr's chief adviser and was nominated by him as successor before his death in 634.

### The Caliphate: Administration and Expansion
As caliph, Umar transformed the Islamic state into a centralized bureaucracy. He established a cabinet, appointed provincial governors based on merit, and implemented strict oversight to prevent corruption. He created the Bayt al-mal (public treasury) for social welfare and standardized state life, including the adoption of the Hijri calendar. His reign saw massive military expansion, conquering the Levant, Egypt, and the Sasanian Empire, extending the caliphate from Libya to the Indus River.

### Jerusalem and Religious Policy
In 637, Umar personally accepted the surrender of Jerusalem. He issued the Pact of Umar, guaranteeing the safety of lives, property, and places of worship for Christians and Jews, and prohibiting forced conversion. In a famous act of respect for religious spaces, he declined to pray inside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre to prevent its future conversion into a mosque.

### Assassination and Succession
In 644, Umar was assassinated by Abu Lu'lu'a Firuz, a Persian slave, likely over a tax dispute. On his deathbed, he appointed a committee of six senior companions to choose his successor, leading to the selection of Uthman ibn Affan.

## Terms
- **Rashidun Caliphs**: The first four "rightly guided" caliphs after Muhammad, including Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, and Ali.
- **Saqifah**: The assembly hall where the early Muslim community gathered to choose the first caliph after Muhammad's death.
- **Muhajirun**: The early Muslims who migrated from Mecca to Medina with Muhammad.
- **Ansar**: The native Muslims of Medina who supported the Muhajirun.
- **Bayt al-mal**: The public treasury or financial institution of the early Islamic state.
- **Hijri Calendar**: The Islamic lunar calendar, introduced by Umar, beginning with the Migration in 622 CE.
- **Pact of Umar**: A treaty guaranteeing the rights and protection of non-Muslims in conquered territories.
- **Amsar**: Garrison cities built to house Muslim soldiers, separating them from local populations.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Succession at Saqifah**: Historians debate the legitimacy and process of the selection of Abu Bakr. Some, like Wilferd Madelung, argue Umar and Abu Bakr mounted a political coup against Ali's claim.
- **Treatment of Ali and Fatima**: Shia sources claim Umar used force to secure Ali's allegiance, leading to the death of Fatima. Sunni sources generally reject this account, stating Ali pledged allegiance without grievance.
- **Assassination Motive**: While a tax dispute is the commonly cited motive for Abu Lu'lu'a's assassination of Umar, some modern scholars suggest broader grievances against non-Arab captives may have played a role.
- **Historical Reliability**: Much of the early Islamic history was transmitted orally and written down later. Modern scholarship seeks to corroborate traditional narratives with archaeological and external sources, noting gaps between historical fact and later religious or political constructions.
