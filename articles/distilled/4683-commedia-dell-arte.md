# Commedia dell'arte

## Overview
Commedia dell'arte was a form of professional, improvised Italian theatre that flourished across Europe from the 16th to 18th centuries. It is foundational to modern Western theatre, introducing professional actresses, standardized stock characters, and a blend of scripted scenarios with improvisation. Its influence extended to opera, ballet, and visual arts.

## Timeline
- **c. 1551** — First recorded commedia dell'arte performances in Rome.
- **1564** — Lucrezia Di Siena named in a contract, considered the first known Italian actress.
- **1568** — The Gelosi troupe becomes a distinct company.
- **c. 1570** — Andrea Calmo creates the character Il Magnifico, a precursor to Pantalone.
- **Early 17th C.** — Flaminio Scala publishes scenarios, helping to codify and legitimize the form.
- **1697** — Italian comedians are expelled from France, altering the form's development there.
- **1797** — Napoleon outlaws commedia dell'arte in Venice to suppress its political use.
- **1979** — Commedia dell'arte is revived in Venice.

## Body

### Origins and Characteristics
Commedia dell'arte emerged from Italian theatre, with possible links to ancient Roman Atellan Farces and medieval traditions. Its defining features are masked "types" (stock characters), improvised performances based on scripted scenarios, and the use of *lazzi* (rehearsed comic routines). Performances were by professional troupes, often outdoors in public spaces like piazzas. The form featured the first known professional actresses in Europe since antiquity, such as Isabella Andreini.

### Troupes and Performance
Companies, or *compagnie*, were itinerant, traveling in carts to perform for new audiences at fairs, courts, and towns. A typical troupe had about ten performers, including women, and was supported by staff like carpenters and prompters. Famous troupes included I Gelosi, Confidenti, and Fedeli. They faced persecution from authorities who viewed them as vagabonds (*vagabondi*), forcing them to move frequently. Troupes were often protected by nobility, such as the King of France for the Gelosi.

### Characters and Costumes
Characters were divided into four main groups: *Zanni* (servants/clowns like Arlecchino/Harlequin), *Vecchi* (wealthy old men like Pantalone and il Dottore), *Innamorati* (young lovers), and *Il Capitano* (braggart soldiers). Each had a distinct, exaggerated costume and mask (for most male roles) that instantly identified their social type and dialect. Female characters, like the *servetta* (maid), were typically unmasked.

### Evolution and Influence
The form evolved from pure improvisation to more structured acts by the 17th century. It heavily influenced French theatre, notably Molière, and spread across Europe, adapting to local cultures. In England, it inspired Punch and Judy puppet shows from the character Pulcinella. Its stock characters and plots entered opera buffa (e.g., Rossini, Verdi), ballet (e.g., Stravinsky's *Pulcinella*), and visual art (e.g., Watteau, Picasso). The form was outlawed in Venice in 1797 and not revived there until 1979.

## Terms
- **Commedia dell'arte**: Professional Italian theatre using masked stock characters and improvised performance based on scenarios.
- **Lazzi**: Rehearsed comic routines or gags inserted into improvised performances.
- **Scenario**: A written plot outline for a commedia performance, detailing key events and character entrances/exits.
- **Zanni**: Stock character group representing servants and clowns, often the source of physical comedy.
- **Vecchi**: Stock character group representing wealthy, often foolish, old men.
- **Innamorati**: Stock character group representing young, upper-class lovers.
- **Maschere**: The Italian term for the masked characters, which became synonymous with all commedia characters.
- **Compagnie**: The professional troupes of actors that performed commedia dell'arte.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise origins of commedia dell'arte are debated, with proposed links to Roman Atellan Farces, medieval jongleurs, and Carnival traditions.
- The exact point at which characters began wearing masks is uncertain, though it is strongly associated with Carnival conventions.
- Scholars debate the extent to which the form was purely improvised versus following structured performance practices, especially as it evolved.
