# William James

## Overview
William James (1842–1910) was a foundational American philosopher and psychologist, often called the "father of American psychology." He co-founded the philosophical school of pragmatism and was a key figure in functional psychology, profoundly influencing modern thought on truth, free will, religion, and the nature of the self.

## Timeline
- **1842** — Born in New York City to theologian Henry James Sr.
- **1861** — Begins scientific studies at Harvard's Lawrence Scientific School.
- **1865** — Joins a scientific expedition to the Amazon; returns early due to illness.
- **1869** — Earns M.D. from Harvard but never practices medicine.
- **1872** — Joins the informal "Metaphysical Club" at Harvard.
- **1875–76** — Teaches the first experimental psychology course in the U.S. at Harvard.
- **1890** — Publishes his landmark work, *The Principles of Psychology*.
- **1896** — Delivers his influential lecture, "The Will to Believe."
- **1902** — Publishes *The Varieties of Religious Experience*.
- **1907** — Publishes *Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking*.
- **1910** — Dies in Chocorua, New Hampshire.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Born into a wealthy, intellectual family, James initially pursued art before switching to medicine at his father's urging. Chronic health issues and intellectual searching led him to abandon medicine for philosophy and psychology. After earning his M.D. in 1869, he spent his entire academic career at Harvard, where he introduced the first psychology courses and became a leading proponent of functionalism and pragmatism.

### Pragmatism and the Theory of Truth
James, with Charles Sanders Peirce, established pragmatism, a philosophy that defines truth by its practical consequences. He argued that the "cash value" of an idea lies in its usefulness and workability in lived experience. Truth is not static but "happens" to an idea as it is verified through practice. This approach seeks to resolve metaphysical disputes by examining their practical differences, as in his famous "squirrel" example.

### Free Will and Determinism
James defended free will against hard determinism (the belief that all events are necessitated) and soft determinism (compatibilism, which he called a "subterfuge"). He proposed a two-stage model: first, "chance" presents a set of undetermined possibilities; then, "choice" selects one based on the individual's character and feelings. He argued that the existence of moral regret and judgment pragmatically supports the reality of free will.

### Psychology: Emotion and Self
In *The Principles of Psychology*, James outlined a theory of instincts and, with Carl Lange, developed the James-Lange theory of emotion. This theory posits that we feel emotions *because* of our physiological responses to stimuli (e.g., we run from a bear, then feel fear), not the other way around. He also divided the self into the "I" (the thinking, subjective self) and the "Me" (the empirical self, comprising material, social, and spiritual components).

### Philosophy of Religion and Mysticism
In *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, James analyzed personal religious experiences, arguing they should be studied over institutions. He defined mystical states by four criteria: ineffability (cannot be described), noetic quality (insightful), transiency, and passivity. While validating these experiences for the individual, he maintained a pragmatic, non-dogmatic approach, advocating for "over-beliefs" that help us live fuller lives.

## Terms
- **Pragmatism**: A philosophical school that defines truth and meaning by their practical consequences and workability in experience.
- **Functionalism**: A school of psychology focusing on the purpose and adaptive functions of consciousness and behavior.
- **Radical Empiricism**: James's view that the world and experience cannot be objectively analyzed because the observer's mind affects the empirical approach to truth.
- **James-Lange Theory of Emotion**: The theory that emotions are the mind's perception of physiological changes that follow an arousing stimulus.
- **Stream of Consciousness**: James's concept describing the mind as a continuous, flowing stream of thoughts and sensations.
- **Will to Believe**: James's doctrine defending the right to adopt a belief (e.g., religious) without sufficient evidence, if it yields practical benefits.
- **Pure Ego**: The "I" self—the subjective, thinking self that provides a sense of continuous identity.
- **Empirical Self**: The "Me" self—the self as known through experience, divided into material, social, and spiritual components.
- **The Metaphysical Club**: An informal discussion group at Harvard (c. 1872) where James, Peirce, and others developed pragmatism.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Interpretation of Pragmatism**: Scholars debate whether James intended a full theory of truth or a more instrumentalist method. Richard Rorty argued against viewing it as a truth theory, while others like Susan Haack disagree.
- **Definition of "Practical Consequences"**: Critics note James never clearly defined how to measure or interpret the "practical consequences" central to his pragmatism.
- **Psychical Research**: James's support for telepathy and mediumship (e.g., Leonora Piper) was criticized by contemporaries like James McKeen Cattell as unscientific, though James himself rejected the "spirit-control" hypothesis.
- **Free Will Model**: His two-stage model separating chance from choice has been influential but continues to be analyzed and debated in philosophy and science.
