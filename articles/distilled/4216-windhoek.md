# Windhoek

## Overview
Windhoek is the capital and largest city of Namibia, serving as the nation's economic, political, and cultural hub. Located on the central Khomas Highland plateau, its development from a hot spring settlement to a modern capital reflects the region's colonial history and post-independence growth.

## Timeline
- **1840** — Jonker Afrikaner establishes an Orlam settlement at a hot spring site.
- **1844** — First known mention of the name "Windhoek" in a letter.
- **1890** — Colonial Windhoek is founded by Major Curt von François; the Alte Feste fort is built.
- **1915** — German colonial rule ends; South Africa takes control during World War I.
- **1920** — Territory placed under a League of Nations mandate administered by South Africa.
- **1958** — World's first potable water reuse facility is introduced.
- **1965** — Windhoek formally receives town privileges.
- **1971** — General contract workers' strike begins, opposing apartheid and the labour system.
- **1990** — Namibia gains independence; Windhoek remains the national capital.

## Body

### History and Etymology
The city developed at a site of hot springs known to local pastoral tribes. The name "Windhoek" is most commonly derived from the Afrikaans words for "wind" and "corner," though another theory links it to the Winterhoek Mountains in South Africa. Jonker Afrikaner settled there in 1840, building a stone church, but wars between the Nama and Herero tribes destroyed the settlement by the 1870s. It was refounded in 1890 by German colonial forces under Major Curt von François, who established a garrison strategically positioned between warring groups. Development accelerated after 1907 with migration from the countryside and European settlement. The city was administered by South Africa from 1915, first under military rule and then as a League of Nations mandate. Major public works occurred after World War II, including road tarring and the pioneering water reuse plant. A significant 1971 strike in Windhoek aimed to abolish the contract labour system and apartheid. Since Namibian independence in 1990, the city has experienced accelerated growth.

### Economy and Administration
Windhoek is the administrative, commercial, and industrial centre of Namibia. It hosts nearly all national enterprises, government ministries, and major financial and media entities. The city provides over half of the country's non-agricultural employment, with particularly high national shares in utilities, transport, and finance. Its municipal budget nearly equals that of all other Namibian local authorities combined. The Windhoek Correctional Facility is the largest of the country's seven major prisons.

### Geography and Climate
The city is surrounded by rocky, mountainous terrain to the south, east, and west, making expansion challenging. The northern Brakwater area is the primary feasible direction for growth. Windhoek has a hot semi-arid climate with over 300 sunny days per year. The annual average temperature is above 18 °C (64 °F), with mild conditions due to its altitude. The average annual precipitation is 377.8 mm (14.87 in), mostly falling in the summer.

### Transport
Windhoek is served by two airports: Eros Airport for smaller craft and Hosea Kutako International Airport for major flights. The city is a road and rail hub, with paved access roads to Rehoboth, Gobabis, and Okahandja. Public transport is limited, with taxis being the primary mode of intra-city travel.

### Demographics and Politics
The population was 486,186 in 2023, growing due to internal migration. In 2010, the population was 65% black, 18% other, and 17% white. Afrikaans and German are used as lingua francas in public life, though the government uses English. Windhoek is governed by a multi-party municipal council. The SWAPO party won the 2015 election but lost its majority in the 2020 election, where several opposition parties gained seats.

### Culture and Education
Known as Namibia's art capital, Windhoek houses the National Art Gallery, National Theatre, and National Museum, which includes the historical Alte Feste and the Owela Museum. The city has numerous other museums and is the site of most places of worship, predominantly Christian. Notable architecture includes the Alte Feste, the three castles (Heinitzburg, Sanderburg, Schwerinsburg), and the post-independence Supreme Court building. Windhoek is the centre for higher education, hosting the University of Namibia (UNAM) and the Namibia University of Science and Technology (NUST), among others.

## Terms
- **Orlam**: A group of mixed-heritage people from the Cape Colony who migrated north.
- **Schutztruppe**: The German colonial protection corps.
- **League of Nations Class C mandate**: A territory administered by another state under international supervision, considered not yet ready for self-governance.
- **Potable reuse**: The process of treating wastewater to drinking water standards.
- **Hot semi-arid climate (BSh)**: A climate classification with hot temperatures and low, seasonal rainfall.
- **Lingua franca**: A language used for communication between people who do not share a native language.
- **Contract labour system**: A system of employment in Namibia under apartheid that restricted workers' rights and mobility.

## Debates and Open Questions
The etymology of the city's name is debated, with theories linking it to either local geography ("windy corner") or ancestral mountains in South Africa. The city's planned expansion to cover over 5,000 km² would make it one of the world's largest by area, raising questions about development feasibility given its low population density and geographical constraints.
