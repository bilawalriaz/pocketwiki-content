# John Wycliffe

## Overview
John Wycliffe (c. 1328–1384) was an English scholastic philosopher, theologian, and Catholic priest whose radical critique of ecclesiastical wealth, papal authority, and sacramental theology made him a pivotal precursor to the Protestant Reformation. He argued that *dominium* (lordship/authority) derives from God and is forfeited by mortal sin, meaning sinful clergy had no right to property or political power. This theory of "civil dominion" justified royal seizure of church assets, earning him powerful patrons like John of Gaunt. Wycliffe rejected transubstantiation in favor of a view resembling consubstantiation, emphasized scripture as the sole authoritative guide to truth (*sola scriptura* in principle), and taught a strong doctrine of predestination defining the true Church as the invisible body of the elect. His followers, derogatorily called Lollards, disseminated his ideas through vernacular preaching and Bible translations. Though protected by nobility and royal politics during his life, he was condemned posthumously by the Council of Constance (1415), which ordered his works burned and his remains exhumed and scattered.

## Timeline
- **c. 1328** — Born in Hipswell, Yorkshire (date disputed; sources range from 1324 to post-1331).
- **c. 1345** — Enters Oxford; influenced by Thomas Bradwardine’s Augustinian theology and the Black Death (1348).
- **1351** — Ordained priest.
- **1356** — Completes B.A. at Merton College; writes *The Last Age of the Church*, predicting the world’s end based on plague as judgment on unworthy clergy.
- **1361** — Master of Balliol College; receives living of Fillingham.
- **1365–1366** — Warden of Canterbury Hall; removed by monastic Archbishop Simon Langham; appeal to Rome rejected (1371).
- **1372** — Receives doctorate in theology.
- **1374** — Receives crown living of Lutterworth; serves on royal commission to Bruges negotiating with papal envoys; connects with John of Gaunt.
- **1376–1377** — Publishes *De civili dominio*, arguing church must divest property and clergy live in apostolic poverty.
- **1377** — Pope Gregory XI condemns 19 articles; Wycliffe summoned to St Paul’s; protected by John of Gaunt; riot ensues.
- **1378** — Writes *De incarcerandis fidelibus* opposing ecclesiastical imprisonment; Great Schism begins; Wycliffe’s anti-papal rhetoric hardens.
- **1381** — Peasants’ Revolt; Wycliffe’s "poor priests" implicated in preaching social equality; Wycliffe defines Eucharistic doctrine (rejecting transubstantiation); loses Gaunt’s support.
- **1382** — "Earthquake Synod" at London condemns 24 propositions (10 heretical, 14 erroneous); Oxford scholars forced to recant; Wycliffe suffers stroke.
- **1384** — Dies at Lutterworth (31 December) after stroke during Mass.
- **1401** — Statute *De heretico comburendo* makes heresy treason; mandates burning Lollard books.
- **1415** — Council of Constance declares Wycliffe a heretic; orders works burned and remains exhumed.
- **1428** — Remains exhumed, burned, and ashes cast into River Swift on papal orders.

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Wycliffe was born in the North Riding of Yorkshire, likely in the mid-1320s. He arrived at Oxford around 1345, where the Augustinian theology of Thomas Bradwardine (Archbishop of Canterbury, 1349) and the trauma of the Black Death (1348) shaped his worldview. He interpreted the plague not merely as divine judgment on the laity, but as an indictment of a corrupt, unworthy clergy whose high mortality rate left the church staffed by disreputable replacements. This conviction fueled his lifelong insistence on clerical poverty and scriptural authority. He progressed through Merton and Balliol Colleges, becoming a doctor of theology by 1372, while navigating the fierce rivalry between secular clergy and monastic orders (mendicant friars) that dominated Oxford politics.

### Political Theology: *Dominium* and Civil Dominion
Wycliffe’s philosophical system centered on *dominium* (lordship): all legitimate authority derives from God and is conditional upon the holder’s state of grace. In *De dominio Divino* (c. 1373), he argued that since God is the ultimate Lord, human lords (kings, popes, priests) hold authority only as stewards; mortal sin forfeits this right. Applied politically in *De civili dominio* (1377), this meant the Church—deemed sinful due to its wealth and worldliness—must surrender its temporal possessions to the Crown, and clergy must live in apostolic poverty. This theory provided intellectual cover for John of Gaunt and the nobility to challenge ecclesiastical wealth and jurisdiction (e.g., annates, ecclesiastical courts). Wycliffe further argued in *De incarcerandis fidelibus* that the Church lacked coercive power to imprison the excommunicated, appealing to secular authority for redress—a direct challenge to canon law.

### Conflict with Church and State (1377–1382)
Wycliffe’s attacks on clerical wealth initially aligned him with the Crown. In 1377, Pope Gregory XI condemned 19 articles from *De civili dominio*. At a hearing at St Paul’s, John of Gaunt physically defended Wycliffe, sparking anti-clerical riots. The death of Edward III and the accession of Richard II (1377) shifted the political landscape. Gregory XI issued a bull with 18 condemned theses, but Wycliffe avoided trial due to the Great Schism (1378) and the intervention of the Queen Mother, Joan of Kent. However, his theological radicalization—specifically his rejection of transubstantiation in *De Eucharistia* (1379)—alienated Gaunt and the aristocracy. The Peasants’ Revolt (1381), fueled partly by Wycliffe’s itinerant "poor priests" who preached against all hierarchy (secular and ecclesiastical), terrified the establishment. Archbishop William Courtenay convened the "Earthquake Synod" (1382), condemning 24 Wycliffite propositions. Though the Commons blocked a heresy statute, the King ordered the arrest of dissenters, and Oxford was purged of Wycliffite leadership.

### Doctrinal Distinctives: Scripture, Sacraments, and the Church
Wycliffe elevated Scripture as the sole, literally true authority (*omnis veritas est ex scriptura*), arguing it contained all truth necessary for salvation. His hermeneutic was rigorously literalist (e.g., interpreting Psalm 22:6 to mean Christ was begotten without sexual contact, like a worm). He defined the Church not as the visible hierarchy but as the *invisible church of the elect*—the totality of those predestined to blessedness. This ecclesiology undermined the papacy, the priesthood, and the sacramental system. On the Eucharist, he rejected transubstantiation (the annihilation of bread’s substance) for a view often termed consubstantiation: the bread remains bread substantially while becoming sacramentally Christ’s body. He rejected confession, indulgences, and monastic orders as unscriptural, advocating the dissolution of monasteries. His soteriology is debated: earlier scholars attributed to him a Protestant-like "faith alone" justification, but modern analysis suggests he held a complex compatibilism, affirming both divine necessity and human free will (e.g., a child walking requires total assistance yet acts willingly).

### The Vernacular Bible and the Lollards
Wycliffe is traditionally credited with initiating the first complete Middle English Bible translation (Wycliffe’s Bible), based on the Vulgate. An Early Version (c. 1382), possibly involving Nicholas of Hereford (OT) and Wycliffe (NT), was revised into a Late Version (1388, 1395) by John Purvey. Over 200 manuscripts survive, indicating wide circulation. However, recent scholarship minimizes Wycliffe’s direct translational role, suggesting he inspired the project through his biblicism but that the translations were collaborative, orthodox Catholic efforts later adopted by Lollards. The Lollards ("mumblers"), Wycliffe’s lay and clerical followers, preached "God’s law" using these texts. They were a diverse movement: not all were Wycliffites, not all Wycliffites were anti-Catholic, and the label was later conflated by opponents. The 1401 statute *De heretico comburendo* and the 1408 Constitutions of Oxford (banning unauthorized vernacular Scripture) targeted this diffusion.

### Posthumous Condemnation and Legacy
Wycliffe died in 1384, unexcommunicated but marginalized. The Council of Constance (1415) formally condemned him as a heretic, ordering his works burned and bones exhumed. In 1428, his remains were dug up, burned, and cast into the River Swift. Despite this, his influence persisted: his *De ecclesia* and *De potentate papae* directly shaped Jan Hus in Bohemia; his political theology resonated in 16th-century debates (e.g., Salamanca Dominicans rejecting native enslavement as Wycliffite error); and his name became synonymous with Bible translation (Wycliffe Global Alliance, Wycliffe Hall Oxford). He is commemorated in the Anglican calendar on 31 December.

## Terms
- ****Dominium**** — Lordship or authority; Wycliffe’s core concept that all rightful authority derives from God and is forfeited by mortal sin.
- ****Civil Dominion** (*De civili dominio*)** — Wycliffe’s political theory that the Church, being in sin, must surrender temporal property to the State; clergy must live in apostolic poverty.
- ****Lollards**** — Derogatory name ("mumblers") for Wycliffe’s followers (late 14th–16th c.); preached vernacular scripture, anticlericalism, and lay piety.
- ****Transubstantiation**** — Catholic doctrine that the substance of bread/wine becomes Christ’s body/blood while accidents remain; Wycliffe rejected this.
- ****Consubstantiation**** — View (attributed to Wycliffe) that Christ’s body is present *in* the bread, which remains substantially bread.
- ****Invisible Church**** — Wycliffe’s definition of the true Church as the body of the predestined elect, distinct from the visible institutional hierarchy.
- ****Sola Scriptura (Principle)**** — Wycliffe’s dictum: "All truth necessary to faith is in Scripture, and the more necessary, the more expressly."
- ****Peasants’ Revolt (1381)**** — Major uprising partly blamed on Wycliffe’s "poor priests" preaching social equality; turned elite opinion against Wycliffe.
- ****Earthquake Synod (1382)**** — Ecclesiastical council at London where an earthquake was interpreted as divine approval for condemning 24 Wycliffite propositions.
- ****De heretico comburendo (1401)**** — English statute equating heresy with treason, mandating burning of unrepentant heretics and Lollard books.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Direct role in Bible translation:** Did Wycliffe personally translate any portion of the Middle English Bible, or did he merely inspire the project? Manuscript evidence shows multiple translators; linguistic analysis challenges sole authorship.
*   **Soteriology – Justification by Faith?** Early Protestant historians (e.g., Thomas James, 1608) claimed Wycliffe taught *sola fide*; modern scholars (Stanford Encyclopedia, Lahey) argue his writings lack a developed doctrine of justification and instead emphasize a compatibilist predestination preserving human freedom.
*   **Nature of Eucharistic presence:** Is Wycliffe’s view accurately labeled "consubstantiation," or a unique "sacramental realism" where the bread remains substance but becomes Christ’s body "sacramentally"?
*   **Lollard continuity:** To what extent did the 15th-century Lollard movement reflect Wycliffe’s actual teachings vs. evolving popular anticlericalism? The Twelve/Thirty-Seven Conclusions (c. 1396) omit vernacular Scripture, a supposed Wycliffite hallmark.
*   **Philosophical nominalism vs. realism:** Wycliffe defended propositional realism (universals exist in God’s mind) against Ockhamist nominalism; scholars debate how this metaphysics underpins his ecclesiology and politics.
