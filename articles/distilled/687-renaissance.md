# Renaissance

## Overview
The Renaissance (c. 1300–1600) was a European cultural movement originating in Italy that revived classical Greek and Roman learning, reshaping art, science, politics, and religion. Its intellectual core was **humanism**—a method of studying ancient texts in their original languages to recover the "genius of man"—which shifted focus from medieval scholasticism toward empirical observation and civic engagement. The movement began in Florence, fueled by urban wealth, merchant-republic politics, and the patronage of families like the Medici, before spreading north via the printing press (c. 1440). It produced breakthroughs in linear perspective, anatomy, navigation, and astronomy (Copernicus), while humanist textual criticism of the New Testament (Erasmus, Valla) directly catalyzed the Protestant Reformation. Historians debate whether the Renaissance was a sharp break from the Middle Ages or a medieval continuation, and whether it represented "progress" given concurrent wars, witch-hunts, and gender inequality.

## Timeline
- **c. 1250–1300** — **Proto-Renaissance** in Italy; Dante, Giotto, and Nicola Pisano anticipate classical revival.
- **1348–1350** — **Black Death** devastates Europe; Florence loses ~50% population, altering labor economics and worldviews.
- **1396** — **Manuel Chrysoloras** invited to teach Greek in Florence, launching the Greek phase of humanism.
- **1401** — **Ghiberti vs. Brunelleschi** competition for Florence Baptistery doors; often cited as Renaissance art's symbolic start.
- **c. 1440** — **Gutenberg** invents movable-type printing press in Mainz; 270 European cities have presses by 1500.
- **1453** — **Fall of Constantinople** drives Greek scholars and manuscripts to Italy.
- **1486** — **Pico della Mirandola** publishes *Oration on the Dignity of Man*, a humanist manifesto.
- **1492** — **Columbus** reaches Americas; **Vespucci** later identifies "New World" (1503), undermining Ptolemaic geography.
- **1517** — **Luther** posts Ninety-five Theses; Reformation begins, fueled by humanist textual criticism.
- **1527** — **Sack of Rome** by Charles V's troops; conventional end of Italian High Renaissance.
- **1543** — **Copernicus** publishes *De revolutionibus* (heliocentrism); **Vesalius** publishes *De humani corporis fabrica* (modern anatomy).
- **1550** — **Vasari** coins *rinascita* in *Lives of the Artists*, defining three artistic phases.

## Body

### Origins and Italian Context
The Renaissance emerged from the crisis of the Late Middle Ages in Italy’s unique urban landscape. Unlike feudal Europe, 15th-century Italy was highly urbanized, dominated by independent city-republics (Florence, Venice, Genoa, Milan) whose merchant elites—exemplified by the Medici in Florence—patronized arts and learning. These cities sat amid Roman ruins, providing physical proximity to antiquity. Political thinkers like Bruni and Palmieri celebrated civic liberty and republican virtue (Lorenzetti’s *Allegory of Good Government*, 1338–40), while the Black Death (1348–50) halved Florence’s population, raising labor value, lowering rents, and shifting focus toward earthly life. The fall of Constantinople (1453) accelerated the influx of Greek scholars and manuscripts, but the recovery of Latin classics (Cicero, Livy) by Petrarch, Salutati, and Poggio Bracciolini had begun a century earlier.

### Humanism: Method and Education
Renaissance humanism was less a philosophy than a **philological method**: studying Greek and Latin literary, historical, and oratorical texts in the original, using reason and empirical evidence rather than medieval scholastic dialectic. The curriculum, *Studia Humanitatis*, comprised poetry, grammar, history, moral philosophy, and rhetoric. Humanists like Valla and Erasmus applied textual criticism to the Greek New Testament, exposing errors in the Latin Vulgate and enabling Reformation theology. Education aimed to produce the *uomo universale* (universal man)—intellectually and physically excellent, capable of honorable action in any sphere. Libraries became public institutions for exchange; the Vatican and Corvinian (Buda) libraries amassed secular classics alongside theology.

### Art and Architecture
Art broke with medieval symbolism toward **naturalism**. Giotto (d. 1337) treated painting as a "window into space"; Brunelleschi formalized **linear perspective** (c. 1420), codified by Alberti (*De pictura*, 1435). Masters—Leonardo (anatomy, light), Michelangelo (sculptural form), Raphael (harmonious composition)—became paradigms. In the Low Countries, van Eyck perfected **oil painting** on canvas, enabling luminous color and durability. Architecture revived Roman orders (Tuscan, Composite) and Vitruvian principles: Brunelleschi’s Florence Cathedral dome (1420–36) and Alberti’s Sant’Andrea, Mantua, exemplify the integration of columns, pilasters, and entablatures. The High Renaissance climaxed in the collaborative rebuilding of St. Peter’s Basilica (Bramante, Michelangelo, Raphael, Sangallo, Maderno).

### Science, Navigation, and Medicine
Humanist reverence for Aristotle and Ptolemy initially reinforced geocentrism, but empirical observation eroded it. **Nicholas of Cusa** (c. 1450) argued for an infinite, centerless universe. **Copernicus** (*De revolutionibus*, 1543) placed the Sun at the center. **Leonardo** pioneered controlled experiments in hydraulics, anatomy, and aerodynamics, earning the title "father of modern science" (Capra). In medicine, **Vesalius** (*De humani corporis fabrica*, 1543) corrected Galen via direct dissection; **Berengario da Carpi** added illustrations (1521). The **Paracelsian movement** rejected humoral theory for chemical remedies. New World botany (maize, guaiac for syphilis) expanded pharmacopeia via Spanish/Portuguese exploration. **Luca Pacioli** (1494) codified double-entry bookkeeping, founding modern accounting.

### Religion and the Reformation
The Renaissance unfolded against Christian backdrop; most art served the Church. Yet humanist textual work (Valla’s *Annotations on the New Testament*, Erasmus’s Greek edition, 1516) undermined papal authority by exposing doctrinal corruptions. The Western Schism (1378–1417) and Conciliarism had already weakened the papacy; Alexander VI’s corruption (simony, nepotism) fueled dissent. **Luther’s** 95 Theses (1517) attacked indulgences, sparking the Reformation—a direct outgrowth of humanist philology and individual conscience. The Catholic Counter-Reformation (Council of Trent, 1545) responded, but the unity of Western Christendom was permanently fractured.

### Spread Beyond Italy
- **France**: Imported by Charles VIII (1495); Francis I brought Leonardo, Cellini, and built Fontainebleau. French humanists: Rabelais, Montaigne, Ronsard.
- **Germany/Low Countries**: Printing press (Gutenberg, c. 1450) and Dürer preceded Italian influence. Humanism fused with Reformation (Erasmus, Luther). Vesalius (anatomy), Mercator (cartography), Bruegel (genre painting) flourished.
- **England**: Elizabethan era (late 16th c.) saw literary flowering (Shakespeare, Marlowe, Spenser) and music (Byrd, Tallis); visual arts lagged. Bacon pioneered empirical science.
- **Hungary**: First northern kingdom to adopt Renaissance (Quattrocento). Matthias Corvinus (r. 1458–90) built Bibliotheca Corviniana (second only to Vatican), hired Italian artists, and hosted humanists (Bonfini, Janus Pannonius).
- **Poland**: "Golden Age" under Jagiellonians; Copernicus studied at Kraków; Polish became East-Central Europe’s lingua franca.
- **Portugal/Spain**: Portugal led navigation (Nunes, Zacuto, *Manueline* architecture); Camões epicized empire in *Os Lusíadas*. Spain produced Cervantes (*Don Quixote*, first Western novel), mystics (Teresa of Ávila, John of the Cross), and El Greco.

### Historiography and Debates
- **Terminology**: Vasari (1550) used *rinascita*; Michelet (1855) popularized "Renaissance" as a scientific age (Columbus to Galileo); Burckhardt (1860) defined it as 14th–mid-16th century Italy, birthplace of modern individuality.
- **Continuity vs. Break**: Medievalists stress "thousand ties" (Panofsky) linking eras; Burckhardt/Michelet saw a veil lifted. Huizinga (*Autumn of the Middle Ages*, 1919) argued Renaissance *declined* from High Middle Ages by freezing Latin and ignoring vernacular vitality.
- **Progress?**: Marxist historians view it as superstructural reflection of feudalism→capitalism transition. Critics note worsened poverty, warfare, witch-hunts, Machiavellian politics, and gender inequality (Kelly). Sarton/Thorndike downplay scientific originality.
- **Periodization**: "Early modern" now preferred by political/economic historians to avoid "rebirth from Dark Ages" teleology. "Long Renaissance" extends to 17th century outside Italy. Other "renaissances" identified: 12th-century, Carolingian, Ottonian, Timurid, Harlem, Disney.

## Terms
- ****Humanism**** — A scholarly method emphasizing study of Greek/Latin literary, historical, and oratorical texts in original languages, using reason and empirical evidence rather than scholastic dialectic.
- ****Studia Humanitatis**** — The five-part humanist curriculum: poetry, grammar, history, moral philosophy, and rhetoric.
- ****Uomo universale**** — "Universal man"—Greco-Roman ideal of combined intellectual, physical, and civic excellence, goal of humanist education.
- ****Linear perspective**** — Mathematical system (formalized by Brunelleschi/Alberti) for depicting three-dimensional space on a flat surface via converging orthogonals to a vanishing point.
- ****Movable-type printing**** — Gutenberg’s c. 1440 invention using reusable metal type, enabling mass book production (20M+ volumes by 1500) and rapid idea diffusion.
- ****Conciliarism**** — Reform movement (post-Western Schism) asserting general councils’ authority over the pope.
- ****Heliocentrism**** — Copernican model (1543) placing the Sun, not Earth, at the center of planetary motion.
- ****Humoral theory**** — Galenic medical framework (dominant until 19th c.) balancing four bodily fluids (blood, phlegm, yellow/black bile); challenged by Vesalius and Paracelsians.
- ****Manueline**** — Portuguese architectural style (early 16th c.) blending Gothic, Renaissance, and maritime motifs, financed by spice-trade wealth.
- ****Early modern**** — Historians’ preferred term for c. 1500–1800, highlighting transition from medieval to modern without "rebirth" teleology.

## Debates and open questions
1. **Periodization**: Does the Renaissance begin c. 1300 (Proto-Renaissance), 1401 (Baptistery competition), or later? Does it end in 1527 (Sack of Rome), 1545 (Trent), or 1600+ ("long Renaissance")?
2. **Discontinuity vs. continuity**: Was it a sharp break from the Middle Ages (Burckhardt) or an organic evolution with "a thousand ties" (Panofsky)?
3. **Geography of origin**: Why Florence? Competing theories: Medici patronage, civic republicanism, merchant wealth, Byzantine refugee scholars, or "Great Men" chance.
4. **Progress narrative**: Did the Renaissance improve human welfare? Counter-evidence: intensified witch-hunts, religious wars, gender restriction, economic recession (Lopez), scientific stagnation (Sarton).
5. **Term validity**: Is "Renaissance" a coherent historical category or a 19th-century construct (Michelet/Burckhardt) imposing unity on diverse, conflicting local cultures (Starn)?
6. **Agency of "Great Men" vs. structural forces**: Were figures like Leonardo products of unique genius or of systemic conditions (urban capitalism, patronage networks, printing)?
7. **Global entanglements**: How did Ottoman expansion, American colonization, and Asian trade shape the Renaissance, rather than merely receiving its output?
