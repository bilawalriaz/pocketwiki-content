# Great Artesian Basin

## Overview
The Great Artesian Basin (GAB) is the world's largest and deepest artesian basin, a vast underground water reservoir underlying 22% of the Australian continent. It is the sole source of fresh water for much of inland Australia, making it a critical lifeline for agriculture, communities, and ecosystems. Its management involves complex coordination across multiple states and territories, and it faces significant environmental pressures from resource extraction.

## Timeline
- **Triassic, Jurassic, early Cretaceous** — Sandstone aquifer layer deposited by continental erosion.
- **Post-deposition** — Marine sedimentary rock forms a confining layer over the sandstone.
- **Pre-European** — Mound springs discharge GAB water, supporting endemic species and Aboriginal communities.
- **1878** — European discovery: a shallow bore near Bourke, NSW, produces flowing water.
- **1886-1887** — Similar flowing bores discovered at Back Creek (Qld) and near Cunnamulla (Qld).
- **1915** — 1,500 bores provide 2,000 megalitres of water per day.
- **1982** — Roxby Downs (Indenture Ratification) Act permits Olympic Dam mine to extract GAB water.
- **1988** — Olympic Dam mine commences operations.
- **2007** — Australian Commonwealth Government announces funding to address unregulated/abandoned bores.
- **2009** — Queensland Gas Company's hydraulic fracturing incident connects aquifers, raising contamination concerns.
- **2020** — Great Artesian Basin Strategic Management Plan published.
- **May 2024** — Queensland Government rejects a carbon capture project and bans the practice in the GAB.

## Body

### Geology and Hydrology
The GAB's water is held in a sandstone aquifer laid down during the Triassic, Jurassic, and early Cretaceous periods. This layer was later covered by marine sedimentary rock, creating a confining layer that traps the water. The basin is up to 3,000 metres deep and contains an estimated 64,900 cubic kilometres of groundwater. Most recharge water enters from high ground near the eastern edge and flows very slowly (1–5 metres per year) toward the south and west. Groundwater age ranges from several thousand years in the north to nearly 2 million years in the south-western discharge zones.

### Historical Use and Extraction
Prior to European settlement, mound springs sustained unique ecosystems and Aboriginal communities. European discovery from 1878 onward allowed the settlement of vast inland areas for pastoralism. Boreholes tap the pressurised aquifer, often without pumps. However, extraction is essentially a mining operation, with recharge far less than current use. Output has dropped from 2,000 megalitres per day in 1915 to 1,500 megalitres per day today, from a mix of flowing and mechanically-pumped bores. This over-extraction has caused many mound springs to dry up, likely leading to species extinctions. Major users include the Olympic Dam mine and the Birdsville geothermal power station.

### Management and Environmental Concerns
The GAB spans four jurisdictions with different laws, coordinated by the Great Artesian Basin Coordinating Committee (GABCC). A major environmental threat is contamination from coal seam gas extraction. A 2009 hydraulic fracturing ("fracking") incident by the Queensland Gas Company connected aquifers and potentially released toxic chemicals. Studies have found heavy metals like lead, arsenic, and uranium in groundwater contaminated by such activities. Another concern was a proposed carbon capture project by Glencore, which critics argued would acidify the water and release heavy metals. This proposal was rejected and the practice banned in the GAB in 2024.

## Terms
- **Artesian Basin:** A large underground water reservoir (aquifer) where water is confined under pressure between layers of impermeable rock.
- **Aquifer:** A body of permeable rock that can contain or transmit groundwater.
- **Confining Layer:** An impermeable layer of rock that traps water within an aquifer.
- **Mound Spring:** A spring where dissolved minerals build up over time to form a mound, typically found at the discharge points of artesian basins.
- **Recharge:** The process by which water enters an aquifer from the surface.
- **Discharge:** The process by which water exits an aquifer, often through springs.
- **Hydraulic Fracturing (Fracking):** A drilling technique that fractures rock with high-pressure fluid to release trapped gas or oil.
- **Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS):** A technology to capture carbon dioxide emissions and store them underground.

## Debates and Open questions
- The long-term impact of hydraulic fracturing chemicals on aquifer health, agriculture, and human populations remains a significant concern.
- The sustainability of current extraction rates, which far exceed natural recharge, poses a critical question for the basin's future viability.
- The effectiveness of whole-of-basin management across different state and territorial legislative frameworks is an ongoing challenge.
