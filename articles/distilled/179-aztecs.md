# Aztecs

## Overview
The Aztecs were a Mesoamerican civilization flourishing in central Mexico from c. 1300 to 1521, defined by Nahuatl-speaking ethnic groups organized into city-states (*altepetl*). The Aztec Empire formed in 1427 as the Triple Alliance of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan, which defeated the Tepanec hegemony. Tenochtitlan became the dominant partner, extracting taxes from client states through indirect hegemonic control rather than territorial garrisoning. The empire reached its zenith in 1519 before Spanish conquistadors led by Hernán Cortés, allied with indigenous enemies of the Mexica, besieged and destroyed Tenochtitlan in 1521. Aztec society featured a strict nobility-commoner divide, intensive chinampa agriculture, a complex calendric system, and a state religion centered on human sacrifice to sustain the cosmic order. Post-conquest, Nahua elites mediated Spanish rule, while disease decimated the population. Aztec symbols and the Nahuatl language remain central to Mexican national identity.

## Timeline
- **c. 1300** — Nahuatl-speaking groups, including the Mexica, migrate into the Valley of Mexico.
- **1325** — Mexica found Tenochtitlan on islets in Lake Texcoco after a prophetic vision.
- **1376** — Acamapichtli elected first *Huey Tlatoani* of Tenochtitlan, founding the royal dynasty.
- **1427–1428** — Triple Alliance (Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, Tlacopan) defeats Azcapotzalco; Aztec Empire begins.
- **1440–1469** — Reign of Motecuzoma I: major territorial expansion, consolidation of tribute system, and "Flower Wars" against Tlaxcala.
- **1486–1487** — Ahuitzotl expands empire, rebuilds Great Temple; mass sacrifice at inauguration reported.
- **1502** — Motecuhzoma Xocoyotzin (Moctezuma II) becomes *Tlatoani*; expands empire to maximum extent.
- **1519** — Hernán Cortés lands; allies with Tlaxcala; enters Tenochtitlan in November.
- **1520** — Smallpox epidemic devastates population; Moctezuma II killed during uprising; Cuitláhuac succeeds briefly.
- **13 Aug 1521** — Fall of Tenochtitlan; Cuauhtémoc captured; Spanish hegemony established.
- **1521–1565** — Colonial transition: puppet *cuauhtlatoque* installed, then dynastic rule restored under Spanish vassalage.
- **1545, 1576** — Major epidemic waves (likely cocoliztli) cause further catastrophic population decline.

## Body

### Definitions and Terminology
The term "Aztec" derives from *aztēcatl* ("people from Aztlán"), a mythical northern homeland. It was not an endonym; the dominant group called themselves *Mēxihcah* (Mexica). Alexander von Humboldt popularized "Aztec" in 1810 as a collective term for peoples linked to the Mexica state; William H. Prescott cemented it globally in 1843. Scholars debate its utility: Robert H. Barlow preferred "Culhua-Mexica"; Pedro Carrasco argued for "Tenochca Empire" to reflect political reality. Broadly, "Aztec" refers to Nahuatl-speaking peoples of the late postclassic period (Mexica, Acolhua, Tepanec, Chalca, Xochimilca, etc.) sharing cultural traits. Linguistically, "Aztecan" denotes the Nahuatl branch of Uto-Aztecan.

### Sources of Knowledge
Understanding relies on archaeology (e.g., Templo Mayor excavations), pre-Hispanic pictorial codices (glyphic writing), and early colonial alphabetic texts. Key sources include the *Codex Mendoza* (c. 1541), detailing tribute, conquests, and society; the *Florentine Codex* (1545–1576), a bilingual encyclopedia by Bernardino de Sahagún and indigenous informants covering religion, history, and natural history; and annals by Nahua historians (Chimalpahin, Alva Ixtlilxochitl). Spanish accounts (Cortés, Bernal Díaz del Castillo) and friars' chronicles (Motolinia, Torquemada, Durán) provide external perspectives, though often biased. Modern scholarship combines archaeology, ethnohistory, and linguistics.

### Pre-Imperial History: Migration and Foundation
Nahua peoples migrated into central Mexico from the northwest, possibly displacing Oto-Manguean speakers after Teotihuacan's fall (6th century CE). Postclassic (post-900 CE) city-states like Tula, Tenayuca, and Colhuacan rose. The Mexica arrived late, serving as mercenaries for Azcapotzalco and Colhuacan. After expulsion from Chapultepec and Tizapan, they settled on Lake Texcoco islets in 1325, guided by a vision of an eagle on a cactus. Acamapichtli became first *tlatoani* (ruler) in 1376. For 50 years, Tenochtitlan paid tribute to the Tepanec empire of Azcapotzalco under Tezozomoc, growing wealthy from shared conquest spoils.

### Formation and Expansion of the Triple Alliance (1427–1519)
Tezozomoc's death (1426) triggered a succession crisis. Itzcoatl (r. 1427–1440) allied with Texcoco (Nezahualcoyotl) and Tlacopan to destroy Azcapotzalco (1428). The Triple Alliance formed; Tenochtitlan soon dominated. Itzcoatl conquered the southern lake chinampa zone (Xochimilco, Culhuacan) and Morelos. Motecuzoma I (r. 1440–1469) expanded into Veracruz, Oaxaca, and Guerrero, instituting "Flower Wars" (*xochiyaoyotl*) against Tlaxcala, Cholula, and Huexotzinco—ritualized conflicts for captives and exhaustion. His advisor Tlacaelel (*Cihuacoatl*) reformed laws, solidified noble (*pipiltin*) power, and established the practice of reinstalling conquered rulers bound by fealty. Axayacatl (r. 1469–1481) conquered Tlatelolco (1473) but was crushed by the Tarascan state (1478–79). Tizoc (r. 1481–1485) failed militarily. Ahuitzotl (r. 1486–1502) suppressed rebellions, expanded to the Gulf and Oaxaca, built garrisons against Tarascans, and inaugurated the Great Temple with massive reported sacrifices. Motecuhzoma Xocoyotzin (r. 1502–1520) pushed to the Pacific and Chiapas, intensified Flower Wars, and restricted commoner (*macehualtin*) mobility and luxury consumption.

### Spanish Conquest and Colonial Transition (1519–1565)
Cortés landed in 1519, allied with Tlaxcala, and entered Tenochtitlan. Moctezuma was seized; Spanish power grew. After Moctezuma's death (1520) and a smallpox epidemic, Cuitláhuac ruled 80 days. Cuauhtémoc led the defense during the 1521 siege. Tenochtitlan fell on 13 August 1521; Cuauhtémoc was captured, later tortured and executed (1525). The Spanish maintained Cuauhtémoc nominally, then installed puppet *cuauhtlatoque* (non-dynastic regents: Tlacotzin, Motelchiuhtzin, Xochiquentzin). In 1538, Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza restored dynastic rule (Huanitzin, Tehuetzquititzin, Cecetzin, Cipac) to legitimize Spanish authority via indigenous intermediaries. By 1565, direct Spanish governorships replaced the *tlatoani* office.

### Social and Political Organization
**Classes:** Nobility (*pipiltin*, ~5% of population) held hereditary privileges: land, luxury goods, corvée labor, high office. Commoners (*macehualtin*) were farmers, artisans, warriors, traders; 80% non-agricultural in later stages. Landless laborers (*mayehqueh*) worked lords' estates. Slaves (*tlacotin*) were not a hereditary caste. Warriors gained status via captives.
**Family/Gender:** Bilateral kinship; women owned/inherited property. Gender roles were separate but framed as complementary: men farmed/traded/fought; women managed domestic sphere, but also worked as merchants, healers, priests. Noble polygamy and marriage alliances were political tools.
**Altepetl & Calpolli:** The *altepetl* ("water-mountain") was the sovereign city-state, ruled by a *tlatoani*, comprising a capital and subject settlements. It was the primary ethnic/political identity. In the Basin, *altepetl* subdivided into *calpolli*—territorial/kinship units organizing commoner labor, land use (non-private), and local governance. In Morelos, a typical *altepetl* held 10,000–15,000 people over 70–100 km².
**Imperial Structure:** A hegemonic, discontinuous confederacy. The Triple Alliance demanded taxes (not tribute) from client *altepetl*, restoring local rulers who cooperated. Control relied on elite intermarriage and ideology. Strategic frontier provinces received garrisons/puppet rulers; subject provinces provided economic stability. The *altepetl* remained the effective local unit.

### Economy
**Agriculture:** Maize-based, intensified by *chinampas*—artificial lake islands yielding ~7 crops/year. One hectare fed ~20 people; 9,000 hectares could feed 180,000. Irrigation, aqueducts, and human waste fertilizer supported Tenochtitlan's density. Lake provided fish, amphibians, waterfowl; few domestic animals (turkeys, dogs).
**Crafts/Trade:** Specialized production (ceramics, obsidian, featherwork, gold/jade). *Pochteca* guilds conducted long-distance trade for luxuries, supervised markets. Markets were highly regulated (daily in cities, every 5 days in towns); Tlatelolco drew 60,000 daily. Currency: cacao beans (small purchases), *quachtli* cotton cloth lengths (large; 20 supported a commoner/year). Land/labor generally not commodities.
**Taxation:** Conquered *altepetl* paid yearly taxes in valued local goods (feathers, cloth, food, firewood). Taxes burdened commoners; nobles benefited via indirect rule. Trade brought exotic goods (obsidian, bronze from Tarascans) to all classes.

### Urbanism
**Tenochtitlan:** Built on islets with a symmetrical four-quadrant plan centered on the walled sacred precinct. Great Temple (Templo Mayor) rose 50m; palaces, schools (*calmecac*), ballcourt, skull rack (*tzompantli*) surrounded it. Canals enabled transport. Population estimates: 200,000–700,000 (including Tlatelolco/suburbs). **Templo Mayor:** Double pyramid with shrines to Tlaloc (rain) and Huitzilopochtli (sun/war). Enlarged by successive rulers; orientation embodied Mexica cosmovision as the universe's navel. **Other Cities:** Texcoco (25,000), Cholula (largest pyramid), Tlaxcala (confederacy of four), Cuauhnahuac, Tollocan. Shared layout: central plaza, twin-stair pyramid facing west.

### Religion and Cosmology
**Metaphysics:** Centered on *teotl*—dynamic, self-generating sacred energy. Monistic pantheism (supreme *Ometeotl*) among elites; polytheistic practice popularly.
**Deities:** Four pillars: Tlaloc (rain), Huitzilopochtli (sun/war, Mexica patron), Quetzalcoatl (wind/knowledge), Tezcatlipoca (night/fate). Great Temple shrines mirrored sacred mountains Tonacatepetl (Tlaloc) and Coatepec (Huitzilopochtli). Dozens of minor deities; pantheon expanded via conquest.
**Mythology:** *Legend of the Suns*: four previous eras destroyed; fifth sun created by self-sacrifice of a minor god, sustained by divine blood. Earth made from Cipactli (crocodile) by Tezcatlipoca/Quetzalcoatl; humans from bones ground by Cihuacoatl, animated by Quetzalcoatl's blood. Huitzilopochtli guided Mexica migration; defeated sister Coyolxauhqui at Coatepec (reenacted at Great Temple).
**Calendar:** Dual system: 260-day *tonalpohualli* (divinatory) and 365-day *xiuhpohualli* (solar/agricultural). 52-year cycle (*Xiuhmolpilli*) marked by New Fire Ceremony: all fires extinguished, new fire drilled on sacrificial victim, distributed to *calpolli*. Fear of *tzitzimimeh* (star demons) during the night without fire.
**Human Sacrifice:** Cosmic necessity: gods/humans sacrifice to continue life. Scale debated: 1487 reconsecration claimed 80,400 (likely exaggerated); annual estimates 1,000–20,000. Cannibalism occurred (captor consumed flesh) but extent disputed. Ideological function: political theater displaying power, deterring rebellion, legitimizing divine authority of rulers.

### Art and Cultural Production
**Writing:** Logographic + phonetic syllabic signs (e.g., tooth *tlantli* = syllable *tla*). Not fully syllabic like Maya; phoneticism may have increased post-conquest. Used for names, places, tribute lists; narratives via iconographic sequences (footprints = travel, burning temple = conquest).
**Poetry/Song:** Highly valued; genres: *yaocuicatl* (war), *teocuicatl* (gods), *xochicuicatl* (flowers/poetry). Key device: parallelism/diphrasisms (e.g., *in xochitl in cuicatl* = "flower and song" = poetry). Surviving collections: *Cantares Mexicanos*, *Romances de los señores de la Nueva España*. Authors include Nezahualcoyotl (Texcoco), Cuacuauhtzin, Macuilxochitzin (female).
**Ceramics:** Orange/red/polychrome wares. Black-on-orange sequence: Aztec I–II (c. 1100–1350), III (1350–1520), IV (colonial). Everyday vessels: *comalli* (griddles), *caxitl* (bowls), *molcaxitl* (mortars). Fired in simple kilns.
**Sculpture:** Stone (andesite, basalt); wood rare. Monumental: Calendar Stone (Sunstone), Coatlicue statue, Coyolxauhqui Stone. Ritual types: *cuauhxicalli* (eagle/jaguar heart vessels), *temalacatl* (gladiatorial sacrifice stones, e.g., Stone of Tizoc). Originally painted polychrome, inlaid with precious materials.
**Featherwork (*amanteca*):** Elite craft; mosaics on shields, cloaks, banners. Feathers (quetzal from Guatemala/Honduras) via trade/tax. Adhesive: orchid bulbs. Fewer than 10 original pieces survive.

### Colonial Period (1521–1821)
Mexico City built over Tenochtitlan. Aztec warriors served as Spanish auxiliaries, spreading Nahuatl. Indigenous *gobernadores* (often former *tlatoque*) ruled *cabeceras* (head towns) under *República de indios*, mediating taxes/labor for *encomenderos*. Elites retained status via conversion/loyalty; Moctezuma's descendants gained Spanish titles (Duke of Moctezuma). **Population Collapse:** Smallpox (1520–21), cocoliztli (1545, 1576). Pre-contact estimates range 1–30 million for central Mexico; 16th-century decline 50–90%. **Continuity/Change:** *Altepetl* became *cabeceras* with *cabildos* (town councils). *Congregación* forced resettlement for evangelization/labor control. Nahua social structure persisted under Spanish overlay.

### Legacy
**National Identity:** 19th-century shift from "barbarians" to "original sons of soil." Creole patriots (Sigüenza y Góngora, Clavijero) used Aztec history against European denigration. 1821 independence: Aztec eagle on cactus became national coat of arms. Liberals (*Indigenistas*) vs. conservatives (*Hispanistas*) debated indigenous heritage. Porfirio Díaz (1876–1911) funded archaeology, erected Cuauhtémoc monument. Revolution (1910–20) fueled *indigenismo*; Diego Rivera's murals ubiquitous. Modern critique (Octavio Paz) of state appropriation of Aztec symbols.
**Scholarship:** Humboldt sparked European interest. Kingsborough published codices (bankrupting himself). Prescott's *Conquest of Mexico* (1843) shaped popular views. International Congress of Americanists (1875) institutionalized field.
**Language/Place/Names:** Nahuatl spoken by 1.5 million. Hundreds of loans in Mexican Spanish/global languages (chocolate, tomato, chili, avocado, tamale, coyote). Nahuatl place names ubiquitous in Mexico; Metro stations honor Moctezuma II, Cuauhtémoc.
**Cuisine:** Global staple crops (corn, beans, chili, tomato, avocado) with Nahuatl names. Marketing uses Aztec imagery for authenticity.
**Ethnicity:** "Aztec" largest self-identified American Indian group in 2020 US Census.
**Popular Culture:** Quetzalcoatl names pterosaur *Quetzalcoatlus*; appears in games/films. Jennings' *Aztec* novels. Films: *The Other Conquest* (2000), *Retorno a Aztlán* (1989). 1970s B-movies: "Aztec mummy" tropes.

## Terms
- ****Altepetl**** — Nahuatl for "water-mountain"; the sovereign city-state, primary unit of political organization and ethnic identity, ruled by a *tlatoani*.
- ****Calpolli**** — Subdivision of *altepetl* in the Basin of Mexico; a territorial/kinship unit organizing commoner land use, labor, and local governance.
- ****Chinampa**** — Artificial agricultural island in lake shallows, built from layered mud/vegetation; highly fertile, enabling year-round, multi-crop harvests.
- ****Cihuacoatl**** — "Snake Woman"; title of the principal advisor/deputy to the *Huey Tlatoani* (e.g., Tlacaelel), overseeing internal administration and justice.
- ****Cuauhtlatoque**** — "One who speaks like an eagle"; non-dynastic interim regents appointed by Spanish (1525–1536) or pre-conquest for conquered territories.
- ****Huey Tlatoani**** — "Great Speaker"; title of the emperor of the Triple Alliance, based in Tenochtitlan.
- ****Macehualtin**** — Commoner class; originally peasants, later included artisans, warriors, traders; could gain noble privileges via martial prowess.
- ****Pipiltin**** — Hereditary nobility (~5% of population); held land, luxury rights, corvée authority, high office.
- ****Pochteca**** — Professional long-distance merchant guilds; traded luxury goods, supervised markets, acted as spies/diplomats.
- ****Quachtli**** — Standardized cotton cloth lengths used as currency for large purchases; grades worth 65–300 cacao beans.
- ****Temalacatl**** — Carved stone disk for gladiatorial sacrifice; war captives tied to it and fought armed warriors (e.g., Stone of Tizoc).
- ****Teotl**** — Central Nahua metaphysical concept: a single, dynamic, self-generating sacred energy/force manifest as deities.
- ****Tlacotin**** — Slaves; status
