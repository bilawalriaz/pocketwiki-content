# War on terror

## Overview
The War on Terror is a global military campaign initiated by the United States after the September 11, 2001 attacks. It represents a shift from traditional state-on-state warfare to a prolonged conflict against non-state terrorist networks and the regimes that harbor them, fundamentally reshaping international security, law, and civil liberties.

## Timeline
- **Sep 11, 2001** — Al-Qaeda attacks the World Trade Center and Pentagon, killing 2,977 people.
- **Sep 14, 2001** — U.S. Congress passes the Authorization for Use of Military Force (AUMF).
- **Oct 7, 2001** — U.S.-led coalition invades Afghanistan, beginning Operation Enduring Freedom.
- **Mar 20, 2003** — U.S.-led coalition invades Iraq.
- **May 1, 2003** — President Bush declares major combat operations in Iraq have ended.
- **May 2, 2011** — U.S. special forces kill Osama bin Laden in Pakistan.
- **Mar 2009** — U.S. Defense Department renames operations from "Global War on Terror" to "Overseas Contingency Operation."
- **Aug 30, 2021** — U.S. completes withdrawal from Afghanistan.
- **Feb 28, 2026** — U.S. and Israel launch joint military strikes in Iran.

## Body

### Origins and Objectives
The campaign was a direct response to the 9/11 attacks by al-Qaeda. President George W. Bush framed it as a "war on terror" against a "radical network of terrorists and every government that supports them." The initial legal basis was the 2001 AUMF, which authorized force against those responsible for 9/11 and those harboring them. The Bush administration's objectives included defeating terrorist organizations, ending state sponsorship of terrorism, and preventing future attacks.

### Major Military Operations
The first theater was Afghanistan, where the U.S. and allies ousted the Taliban regime in late 2001. The conflict evolved into a long-term insurgency, with NATO's International Security Assistance Force (ISAF) deployed in 2001. The U.S. withdrew its last troops in August 2021, after the Taliban retook Kabul.

In 2003, the U.S. invaded Iraq, citing alleged weapons of mass destruction and links to terrorism—claims later contested. The invasion led to the fall of Saddam Hussein's government but sparked a violent insurgency and sectarian conflict. The U.S. combat mission officially ended in 2010, but forces returned in 2014 to fight the Islamic State (IS).

Following the 2014 territorial expansion of the Islamic State, the U.S.-led Operation Inherent Resolve was launched to defeat IS in Iraq and Syria. The campaign also expanded to other regions, including the Philippines, the Horn of Africa, and the Sahel, targeting al-Qaeda affiliates and other militant groups.

### Evolution and Current Phase
The term "war on terror" was gradually abandoned by the U.S. government. The Obama administration shifted to "Overseas Contingency Operations" and later defined the enemy as specific networks like al-Qaeda. However, the underlying conflict continued. The 2020s saw a transition from large-scale occupations to more targeted operations, intelligence-sharing, and regional partnerships.

The conflict has recently expanded. In 2025, the U.S. initiated Operation Southern Spear in Latin America, applying post-9/11 legal frameworks to combat narcoterrorism. In 2026, tensions with Iran escalated into direct U.S. and Israeli military strikes, marking a significant widening of the conflict.

### Human and Financial Costs
The Costs of War Project estimates the post-9/11 wars have caused over 4.5 million deaths (direct and indirect) and displaced 38 million people. The financial cost to the U.S. Treasury exceeds $8 trillion. The campaign has been marked by significant civilian casualties, the use of drone warfare, and controversial practices like extraordinary rendition and detention at Guantanamo Bay.

### Criticism and Controversy
The war has been criticized on multiple fronts. Critics argue the term "war" is a misnomer for a tactic, and that the conflict has been used to justify infringements on civil liberties (e.g., the Patriot Act), human rights abuses, and perpetual war. The legality of actions like drone strikes and the detention of "enemy combatants" has been challenged. Security analysts often contend there is no military solution to terrorism, emphasizing the need for political and diplomatic solutions.

## Terms
- **Authorization for Use of Military Force (AUMF):** A 2001 law authorizing the U.S. president to use force against those responsible for the 9/11 attacks and associated forces.
- **Operation Enduring Freedom:** The official name for the U.S.-led war in Afghanistan and related global operations (2001–2014).
- **Operation Inherent Resolve:** The U.S.-led military campaign against the Islamic State in Iraq and Syria, begun in 2014.
- **Extraordinary Rendition:** The U.S. government-sponsored abduction and extrajudicial transfer of a detainee to a foreign country for interrogation.
- **Enemy Combatant:** A term used by the Bush administration to designate individuals captured in the war on terror who were not afforded prisoner-of-war protections.
- **Overseas Contingency Operation (OCO):** The term adopted by the Obama administration in 2009 to replace "Global War on Terror."
- **Narcoterrorism:** The convergence of illegal drug trade and terrorism, a concept applied in the 2025 Operation Southern Spear.
- **Islamic State (IS):** A militant Islamist group that declared a caliphate in 2014 and became a primary adversary of the U.S.-led coalition.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Terminology and Legitimacy:** Was "war on terror" an appropriate or accurate framework, or did it obscure the nature of the conflict and enable overreach?
- **Effectiveness:** Has the military campaign reduced global terrorism, or has it fueled instability and radicalization?
- **Legal and Ethical Boundaries:** To what extent did measures like the Patriot Act, Guantanamo Bay, and drone strikes violate international law and human rights?
- **Endgame and Legacy:** Can the conflict be considered "won," or has it evolved into a permanent state of low-intensity global conflict? The expansion into narcoterrorism and conflict with Iran suggests the latter.
