# African literature

## Overview
African literature encompasses both oral traditions ("orature") and written works in African and Afro-Asiatic languages, with a history spanning from at least the fourth century AD. It is distinguished by a traditional integration of art and social purpose, where beauty serves to communicate important truths and build community. Its development reflects Africa's historical phases, from pre-colonial manuscript traditions to colonial-era slave narratives and post-colonial explorations of independence and identity.

## Timeline
- **c. 500 AD** — The Garima Gospels, one of the oldest surviving bibles, are written in Ge'ez in Ethiopia.
- **14th century AD** — The *Kebra Negast* ("Book of Kings") is compiled in Ethiopia.
- **1728** — *Utendi wa Tambuka* ("The Story of Tambuka"), an early Swahili literary work, is dated.
- **1789** — Olaudah Equiano publishes *The Interesting Narrative*, a seminal slave narrative.
- **1911** — Joseph Ephraim Casely Hayford publishes *Ethiopia Unbound*, likely the first African novel in English.
- **1935** — H.I.E. Dhlomo publishes *The Girl Who Killed to Save*, the first English-language African play.
- **1948** — Léopold Sédar Senghor publishes the first anthology of French-language African poetry.
- **1958** — Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* is published, gaining worldwide critical acclaim.
- **1962** — Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o writes *The Black Hermit*, the first East African drama.
- **1986** — Wole Soyinka becomes the first post-independence African to win the Nobel Prize in Literature.
- **2000** — The Caine Prize for African Writing is established.

## Body

### Oral and Pre-colonial Traditions
African literature has deep roots in oral traditions, or "orature," a term coined by Pio Zirimu. This includes prose (myths, historical tales, trickster stories), poetry (praise songs, epics), and musical forms. Storytelling often uses call-and-response techniques. Written literature also flourished pre-colonially, notably in Ge'ez in Ethiopia, in Arabic and local languages in the Sahel (e.g., the 300,000+ manuscripts of Timbuktu), and in Swahili on the East African coast. A recent project documented over 4,500 manuscripts from the Horn of Africa, revealing extensive scholarly networks.

### Colonial Period
During colonization, African writers began using European languages. Key early works include the first English-language novel, *Ethiopia Unbound* (1911), and the first English-language play, *The Girl Who Killed to Save* (1935). Slave narratives were a prominent form for Western audiences. The late colonial period saw literature increasingly focus on themes of liberation and independence, exemplified by the négritude movement in francophone Africa. Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* (1958) became a globally acclaimed analysis of colonialism's impact. Many writers faced persecution; for example, Ken Saro-Wiwa was executed in 1995.

### Post-colonial and Contemporary Literature
Following independence in the 1950s and 1960s, African literature expanded dramatically. Writers explored themes like the clash between tradition and modernity, individualism and community, and political development. Female representation has significantly increased. Major accolades include Nobel Prizes for Wole Soyinka (1986), Naguib Mahfouz (1988), and Abdulrazak Gurnah (2021), and the Booker Prize for Ben Okri (1991). Contemporary developments include a break from academic styles, the rise of literary festivals, and the impact of the internet, which has fostered digital platforms like OkadaBooks and journals like *Brittle Paper*.

## Terms
- **Orature**: Oral literature, a term coined by Pio Zirimu, encompassing prose, poetry, and song transmitted verbally.
- **Trickster tale**: A folktale featuring a small, cunning animal (e.g., Anansi the spider) that uses wit to overcome larger foes.
- **Négritude**: A literary and ideological movement, led by figures like Léopold Sédar Senghor, that affirmed Black African culture and identity.
- **Slave narrative**: An autobiographical account written by an enslaved person, often for Western audiences, detailing their experiences.
- **Call-and-response**: A storytelling technique common in African oral traditions where the audience participates by responding to the narrator.
- **Griot**: A West African storyteller, praise singer, or oral historian, often a hereditary musician.
- **Ge'ez**: An ancient South Semitic language of Ethiopia, used for religious and literary texts.
- **Manuscript tradition**: The practice of producing and preserving handwritten texts, as seen in centers like Timbuktu.

## Debates and Open questions
The source highlights ongoing scholarly discussions about the definition and scope of African literature. A central debate concerns whether literature should be defined by a separation of art from content (a European view) or an inclusive approach where art serves social and communicative purposes (a traditional African view). The scale and diversity of pre-colonial written production, such as the manuscripts of Timbuktu and the Horn of Africa, continue to be documented, challenging earlier narratives that emphasized orality alone.
