# Post–World War II economic expansion

## Overview
The post–World War II economic expansion, also called the Golden Age of Capitalism, was a period of exceptional worldwide economic growth and stability from the late 1940s to 1973. It was characterized by high GDP growth, full employment, and rising living standards across both developed and developing nations, fundamentally reshaping the global economy and society.

## Timeline
- **1945** — End of World War II; beginning of postwar reconstruction.
- **1947** — Marshall Plan begins, pumping over $12 billion into Western Europe. |
- **1948** — German currency reform accelerates the Wirtschaftswunder.
- **1950** — Economic historians generally agree the "golden age" begins.
- **1956** — U.S. Federal Aid Highway Act authorizes the Interstate Highway System.
- **1958** — West Germany is a founding member of the European Economic Community.
- **1971** — Collapse of the Bretton Woods monetary system; Nixon closes the gold window.
- **1973** — Arab oil embargo triggers the first oil shock; widely marks the end of the golden age.
- **1973–75** — 1973–75 recession and stock market crash conclude the expansion.

## Body

### Global Economic Climate and Causes
The boom saw OECD members average over 4% real GDP growth annually in the 1950s and nearly 5% in the 1960s. This growth was unusually stable, with far fewer financial crises than in preceding or subsequent periods. Key causes included high productivity growth from wartime technological advances (automation, new agricultural methods), the adoption of Keynesian economic policies emphasizing government spending and demand management, and international cooperation through institutions like the United Nations and the Bretton Woods system. The Marshall Plan was crucial for European recovery. "Financial repression"—government policies keeping interest rates low and often negative in real terms—helped service and reduce public debt. Wealth redistribution via progressive taxation and wartime rationing, which persisted post-war, also contributed to more equitable growth.

### National Experiences
The expansion was global but manifested differently by country. The United States saw its GDP grow from $228 billion in 1945 to nearly $1.7 trillion in 1975, with growth distributed fairly evenly across classes. West Germany experienced the *Wirtschaftswunder* (economic miracle), with industrial production doubling from 1950 to 1957. Japan's postwar recovery, boosted by the Korean War, led to the "Japanese economic miracle," making it a major power in steel, cars, and electronics. France's *Trente Glorieuses* (1947–1973) averaged 5% annual growth. Italy had its *Miracolo economico* with record growth rates in the early 1960s. Even war-devastated nations like South Korea and Austria, and relatively unaffected ones like Sweden, experienced considerable growth. The Soviet Union also saw rapid growth and technological achievements, such as the first satellite, until the mid-1960s when resources shifted to military projects.

### Effects and Decline
The boom enabled profound social and political changes, including the baby boom, the rise of the welfare state, decolonization, the space race, and the counterculture of the 1960s. In the U.S., it fueled suburbanization and a belief in technocratic solutions to social problems. The period ended abruptly in the early 1970s due to a confluence of events: the collapse of Bretton Woods, the 1973 oil crisis which doubled oil prices, and the ensuing 1973–75 recession. This crisis accelerated the shift to a post-industrial economy and exposed vulnerabilities in Western manufacturing to new global competition.

## Terms
- **Keynesian economics**: An economic theory advocating government intervention through fiscal policy (spending and taxation) to manage demand and achieve full employment.
- **Bretton Woods system**: A postwar international monetary order that established fixed exchange rates and institutions like the IMF to promote global financial stability.
- **Financial repression**: Government policies that keep nominal interest rates low, often below inflation, to reduce the real value of public debt and encourage borrowing.
- **Wirtschaftswunder**: The rapid reconstruction and growth of West Germany's economy from the early 1950s.
- **Trente Glorieuses**: The thirty years of strong economic growth in France from 1945 to 1975.
- **Marshall Plan**: A U.S. program providing over $12 billion in aid to rebuild Western European economies after World War II.
- **Full employment**: An economic state where all available labor resources are being used in the most efficient way possible, with unemployment at a very low, non-inflationary level.
- **Import substitution**: A trade and economic policy aiming to replace foreign imports with domestic production.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the causes of the boom. Keynesian economists attribute it to deliberate government policies of demand management and social spending. Others emphasize structural factors like postwar reconstruction, technological catch-up, and the unique geopolitical conditions of the Cold War. The role of "military Keynesianism"—stabilization through high military spending—is also debated. Furthermore, scholars question whether the golden age was a unique, non-repeatable historical anomaly or if its lessons can be applied to contemporary economic challenges.
