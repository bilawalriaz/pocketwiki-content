# Reformation

## Overview
The Reformation was a major 16th-century theological movement in Western Christianity that posed a religious and political challenge to the papacy and the authority of the Catholic Church hierarchy. Sparked by Martin Luther’s 1517 protest against the sale of indulgences, it challenged papal authority and Catholic doctrines—especially the role of good works in salvation—giving rise to Protestantism. The movement diversified rapidly due to the printing press, producing distinct traditions: Lutheranism, the Reformed (Calvinist) churches, Anglicanism, and various Radical (Anabaptist) groups. The Catholic Church responded with the Counter-Reformation, formalized at the Council of Trent (1545–1563). The ensuing European wars of religion saw the deaths of between seven and seventeen million people. The Thirty Years’ War (1618–1648) devastated much of Germany, killing between 25 and 40% of its population. The Peace of Westphalia confirmed the Peace of Augsburg, added Calvinism as a legal option, guaranteed private worship and limited public exercise for minorities, and ended papal political veto power over European sovereigns.

## Body

### Precursors and Context
**Calamities and Late Medieval Piety**  
Europe’s demographic collapse after the Black Death (mid-14th century) intensified anxieties about salvation. Popular piety focused on purgatory, indulgences, Masses for the dead, and the *danse macabre*. Laypeople organized in confraternities and the *devotio moderna* emphasized interior spirituality and lay education. The clergy, legally separated from laity by Gregorian Reform, held sacramental monopoly; pluralism and non-residence were common. The papacy, recovering from the Western Schism (1378–1417), acted as a Renaissance princely power, funding projects like St. Peter’s Basilica through indulgence sales.

**Humanism and Textual Criticism**  
Humanists’ *ad fontes* ("back to the sources") drive recovered Greek manuscripts and applied philology to Scripture. Lorenzo Valla exposed the *Donation of Constantine* as a forgery; Erasmus published a Greek-Latin New Testament revealing Vulgate errors. These tools undermined papal claims and enabled vernacular Bible translations, allowing laypeople to challenge clerical interpretations.

**Pre-Reformation Dissent**  
John Wycliffe (d. 1384) and Jan Hus (d. 1415) attacked clerical wealth, transubstantiation, and papal authority, anticipating *sola scriptura*. Hus’s execution sparked the Hussite Wars; the Bohemian Brethren survived as a non-hierarchical church. The Lollards persisted in England. These movements proved reformist energy existed long before Luther.

### The German Reformation and Lutheranism
**Luther’s Breakthrough**  
Luther, an Augustinian monk tormented by *Anfechtung* (spiritual dread), developed a theology of justification by faith alone (*sola fide*) through study of Augustine and Paul. His 1517 Theses targeted the indulgence preacher Johann Tetzel’s aggressive tactics. The conflict escalated: Luther denied papal authority to remit sins, burned the bull *Exsurge Domine* (1520), and refused to recant at Worms (1521). Protected by Frederick the Wise, who vetoed the imperial ban and arranged Luther’s staged abduction, he translated the New Testament into German at Wartburg, standardizing the language.

**Print, Preaching, and Urban Adoption**  
The printing press accelerated Luther’s reach; he was responsible for about one-fifth of all works printed in Germany in the first third of the 16th century. Woodcuts by Lucas Cranach the Elder communicated ideas to the illiterate. Imperial free cities—Strasbourg, Ulm, Nuremberg—adopted reform early, driven by magistrates or merchant elites. Evangelical preachers offered the Eucharist in both kinds and attacked clerical privilege, resonating with anti-clericalism.

**Radicals, Peasants, and Princely Consolidation**  
Andreas Karlstadt and the Zwickau prophets pushed iconoclasm and adult baptism; Luther condemned them as fanatics. The Peasants’ War (1524–25) invoked Gospel liberty against seigneurial oppression; Luther’s *Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants* urged princely slaughter. Thereafter, Luther accepted princes as "emergency bishops" to impose order. The first Evangelical state church was created in Ducal Prussia (1525); Electoral Saxony followed (1525). The 1529 Protest at Speyer birthed the label "Protestant." The Marburg Colloquy failed to reconcile Luther’s corporeal Real Presence with Zwingli’s memorialism.

### The Swiss Reformation: Zwingli, Calvin, and the Radicals
**Zwingli’s Civic Reformation**  
In Zürich, priest Huldrych Zwingli (from 1516) secured magistrate backing for disputations. By 1526: images removed, Latin Mass replaced by German communion service, fasting and clerical celibacy abolished. He viewed the Eucharist as a memorial, rejecting both transubstantiation and Luther’s ubiquity doctrine. Zwingli died at the 1531 Battle of Kappel; Heinrich Bullinger succeeded him, seeking Eucharistic consensus.

**Anabaptists and the Schleitheim Articles**  
Conrad Grebel, George Blaurock, and Felix Manz broke with Zwingli over infant baptism and church-state separation. In 1525 they practiced believers’ baptism, earning the name Anabaptists. The 1527 Schleitheim Articles codified pacifism, separation from the world, the ban (shunning), and communal economics. Persecuted by Catholics and Protestants alike (Manz drowned 1527), survivors migrated to Moravia (Hutterites) and the Low Countries (Mennonites under Menno Simons).

**Calvin and the International Reformed Movement**  
French exile John Calvin systematized Reformed theology in the *Institutes* (1536, final 1559), emphasizing God’s sovereignty, total depravity, and double predestination. Invited to Geneva (1541), he established the *Ecclesiastical Ordinances*: four offices (pastors, doctors, elders, deacons), a consistory for moral discipline, and a church-state partnership. Geneva became the "Protestant Rome," training ministers via its academy (Theodore Beza, rector). Calvin’s theology spread to France (Huguenots), the Netherlands, Scotland, Hungary, and Poland.

### The English and Scottish Reformations
**Henry VIII’s Royal Supremacy**  
Denied an annulment by Rome, Henry VIII (r. 1509–47) used Parliament to declare himself Supreme Head of the Church of England (1534). Monasteries were dissolved (1536–40), funding the Crown. The Six Articles (1539) retained transubstantiation and clerical celibacy. Under Edward VI (1547–53), Cranmer’s *Book of Common Prayer* and Forty-two Articles moved the church toward Reformed theology. Mary I (1553–58) restored Catholicism, burning ~300 Protestants. Elizabeth I (1558–1603) crafted a *via media*: the 1559 Settlement restored royal supremacy, a revised Prayer Book with ambiguous Eucharistic language, and the Thirty-nine Articles. Puritans demanded further purification; Presbyterians and Congregationalists contested episcopal governance.

**Scotland’s Kirk**  
John Knox, a Calvinist, led the 1560 Reformation Parliament to abolish papal jurisdiction and the Mass. The Kirk adopted Presbyterian polity (governance by elders), though Episcopalianism persisted under royal pressure.

### The Catholic Reformation and Council of Trent
**Internal Reform and New Orders**  
Shocked by the sack of Rome (1527) and Protestant gains, Pope Paul III (1534–49) appointed reform-minded cardinals (Contarini, Pole, Carafa). The Society of Jesus (Jesuits), founded by Ignatius of Loyola (approved 1540), became the vanguard: schools, missions, and polemics against Protestantism.

**Trent’s Doctrinal Settlement**  
The Council of Trent (1545–47, 1551–52, 1562–63) reaffirmed: Scripture *and* Tradition as authority; the Vulgate as authentic; seven sacraments; transubstantiation; justification by faith *and* works; purgatory, indulgences, and saintly intercession. It mandated episcopal residence, diocesan seminaries, a revised breviary, missal, and catechism (Roman Catechism, 1566). Papal authority was centralized via Roman congregations (including the Index of Forbidden Books).

### Religious Wars and Political Settlements
**The Schmalkaldic Wars and Peace of Augsburg (1555)**  
Charles V defeated the Schmalkaldic League (1547) but could not enforce the *Augsburg Interim*. Maurice of Saxony’s revolt forced the 1555 Peace: *cuius regio, eius religio* for Catholics and Lutherans only; ecclesiastical reservation protected prince-bishoprics; free cities must tolerate both confessions.

**French Wars of Religion (1562–1598)**  
Huguenot nobility (Coligny, Condé, Navarre) challenged Guise dominance. The 1572 St. Bartholomew’s Day Massacre killed thousands. Henry IV (Navarre) converted to Catholicism ("Paris is worth a mass"), defeated the Catholic League, and issued the Edict of Nantes (1598): limited worship rights, fortified towns, and public office access for Huguenots.

**The Dutch Revolt (1566–1648)**  
Iconoclasm (1566) triggered Alba’s terror. William of Orange led the northern provinces to independence (Union of Utrecht, 1579; Act of Abjuration, 1581). The Dutch Republic practiced relative toleration; the southern Netherlands remained Catholic under Spanish rule.

**Eastern Europe: Transylvania and Poland**  
Transylvania’s 1568 Edict of Torda recognized four confessions (Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, Unitarian). Poland’s 1573 Warsaw Confederation guaranteed noble religious freedom; the Polish Brethren (Socinians) developed anti-Trinitarian theology.

**The Thirty Years’ War and Peace of Westphalia (1648)**  
Bohemian revolt (1618) triggered a continental war. Habsburg counter-reformation measures devastated Germany (25–40% population loss). Westphalia confirmed the Peace of Augsburg, added Calvinism as a legal option, guaranteed private worship and limited public exercise for minorities, and ended papal political veto power over European sovereigns.

### The Reformation Beyond Germany
**Scandinavia**  
Monarchs imposed Lutheranism: Denmark-Norway-Iceland (Christian III, 1536), Sweden-Finland (Gustav Vasa, 1527–93). Iceland’s last Catholic bishop, Jón Arason, was executed (1550).

**France**  
Huguenots declined after 1598; Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes (1685), causing 150,000–200,000 to flee. A Lutheran minority persisted in Alsace.

**Spain and Italy**  
The Spanish Inquisition (est. 1478) and Index suppressed Protestantism early; few adherents, mostly intellectuals, were executed or exiled. In Italy, the Waldensians aligned with Calvinism (1532) but survived only in Alpine valleys. Italian exiles (Vermigli, Ochino, Sozzini) fueled Reformations in Poland and Transylvania.

**Eastern Orthodox Encounter**  
Patriarch Cyril Lucaris (1629) published a Calvinistic *Confessio* in Geneva; condemned by Orthodox synods (1638, 1642, 1672).

## Terms
- ****Justification by faith alone (*sola fide*)**** — Protestant doctrine that sinners are declared righteous by God solely through trust in Christ’s merit, not human works.
- ****The three *solae***** — *Sola scriptura* (Scripture alone as final authority), *sola fide* (faith alone), *sola gratia* (grace alone)—summary of core Protestant consensus.
- ****Magisterial Reformation**** — Mainstream Protestant movements (Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican) that cooperated with civil magistrates to enforce religious uniformity.
- ****Radical Reformation / Anabaptists**** — Groups rejecting state churches, infant baptism, and oaths; practicing believers’ baptism, pacifism, and often communal economics.
- ****Counter-Reformation / Catholic Reformation**** — Catholic response: doctrinal clarification at Trent, new orders (Jesuits), episcopal reform, Index, and missions to reclaim territory.
- *****Cuius regio, eius religio***** — Principle (legalized 1555) that a ruler determines the official religion of his territory; subjects must conform or emigrate.
- ****Consistory**** — Calvinist church court (pastors + elders + magistrate) enforcing moral discipline and doctrinal conformity.
- ****Double predestination**** — Calvin’s doctrine that God eternally elects some to salvation and reprobates others to damnation, independent of human merit.
- ****Nicodemites**** — Crypto-Protestants (or crypto-Catholics) who outwardly conformed to the dominant religion while secretly holding dissenting views.
- ****Peace of Westphalia (1648)**** — Treaties ending the Thirty Years’ War; recognized Calvinism, fixed 1624 as normative year for church ownership, secularized international law.

## Debates and open questions
- **Periodization**: Scholars dispute start (1517 vs. earlier reform currents) and end dates (1555 Augsburg, 1648 Westphalia, or "never ended").
- **Causality**: How much did printing, pre-existing social networks, economic grievances, or theological ideas drive the spread? A 2020 study argues Luther’s personal networks mattered more than the press for early adoption.
- **Continuity vs. Rupture**: Was the Reformation a sharp break from medieval Catholicism or an intensification of late-medieval reform trends (Peter Marshall’s view)?
- **Confessionalization thesis**: Did state churches (Lutheran, Reformed, Catholic) impose top-down discipline creating modern subjects, or did popular piety drive change from below?
- **Protestant ethic and capitalism**: Weber’s thesis linking Calvinist anxiety to capitalist spirit remains contested; recent economic history finds mixed evidence.
- **Violence and toleration**: Why did confessional coexistence succeed in some polities (Transylvania, Dutch Republic) but fail catastrophically in others (France, Germany)?
- **Global legacy**: How did Reformation categories (Protestant/Catholic) shape colonial missions, indigenous conversions, and modern denominationalism (45,000+ groups)?
