# John Maynard Keynes

## Overview
John Maynard Keynes (1883–1946) was an English economist whose work founded Keynesian economics, the dominant school of macroeconomic thought from the late 1930s to the 1970s. He challenged the neoclassical tenet that free markets automatically ensure full employment, arguing instead that aggregate demand drives economic activity and that rigid wages prevent automatic recovery from slumps. In *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936), he demonstrated that economies can settle into stable low-employment equilibria, necessitating government fiscal and monetary intervention. His ideas shaped the post-WWII Bretton Woods system and the "golden age" of capitalism. Though displaced by monetarism in the 1970s amid stagflation, Keynesian policy resurged during the 2008 financial crisis and underpins modern New Keynesian macroeconomics.

## Timeline
- **5 June 1883** — Born in Cambridge, England, to economist John Neville Keynes and social reformer Florence Ada Keynes.
- **1904** — Graduates King’s College, Cambridge, with a first-class B.A. in mathematics.
- **1906–1908** — Serves as a clerk in the India Office; resigns to return to Cambridge.
- **1911** — Becomes editor of *The Economic Journal*; publishes *Indian Currency and Finance* (1913).
- **1915–1919** — Works at the Treasury during WWI; manages allied credit and currency acquisition.
- **1919** — Attends Versailles Peace Conference as Treasury representative; resigns in protest; publishes *The Economic Consequences of the Peace*.
- **1923** — Publishes *A Tract on Monetary Reform*, advocating domestic price stability over the gold standard.
- **1925** — Marries Russian ballerina Lydia Lopokova.
- **1930** — Publishes *A Treatise on Money*; argues saving exceeding investment causes unemployment.
- **1936** — Publishes *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, founding modern macroeconomics.
- **1937** — Suffers a heart attack, limiting direct participation in subsequent theoretical debates.
- **1940** — Publishes *How to Pay for the War*, advocating compulsory saving over deficit finance.
- **1942** — Elevated to hereditary peerage as Baron Keynes of Tilton.
- **1944** — Leads British delegation at Bretton Woods Conference; proposes International Clearing Union and *bancor* (overruled by US).
- **1946** — Dies of a heart attack on 21 April at Tilton, East Sussex, aged 62.
- **1970s** — Keynesianism challenged by stagflation and monetarist critiques (Friedman, Lucas).
- **2008–2009** — Keynesian resurgence informs global fiscal stimulus responses to the financial crisis.

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Keynes was raised in an upper-middle-class academic household in Cambridge. His father, John Neville Keynes, was a university lecturer in moral sciences; his mother, Florence Ada Keynes, became the town’s second female mayor. Educated at Eton (1897–1902) and King’s College, Cambridge (1902–1904), he excelled in mathematics and classics. At Cambridge, he joined the secretive Cambridge Apostles debating society and fell under the influence of philosopher G. E. Moore, whose ethic valuing "the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects" remained Keynes’s "religion under the surface." Though trained in mathematics, Alfred Marshall persuaded him toward economics. His only formal economics instruction was one term of graduate lectures. Biographer Robert Skidelsky argues Keynes’s early optimism—rooted in late-Victorian imperial confidence and a belief in governance by cultural unity—shaped his lifelong faith in the ability of educated officials to solve practical problems.

### World War I and the Versailles Turning Point
In 1915, Keynes joined the Treasury, where his "nerve and mastery became legendary" in managing inter-allied credit and scarce currencies (notably breaking the peseta market to lower costs). As a conscientious objector, he continued government work. At the 1919 Versailles Peace Conference, he argued against crippling German reparations, warning they would impoverish Central Europe, destroy export markets for the victors, and sow "the decay of the whole civilized life of Europe." Excluded from high-level talks by the "Heavenly Twins" (Lord Sumner and Lord Cunliffe), he failed to prevent a treaty he viewed as morally and economically disastrous. His resignation and *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919) made him famous but anti-establishment. He predicted hyperinflation and future conflict; while some historians (e.g., Ruth Henig) argue the treaty was not economically harsh in practice, Keynes’s followers point to the 1923 hyperinflation and the collapse of the Weimar Republic as validation.

### The 1920s: Probability, Gold Standard, and Early Policy Advocacy
Keynes published *A Treatise on Probability* (1921), pioneering interval probability and decision-weight approaches. He attacked post-war deflation in *A Tract on Monetary Reform* (1923), urging countries to target domestic price stability even at the cost of currency depreciation. He opposed Britain’s 1925 return to the gold standard at pre-war parity (engineered by Chancellor Winston Churchill), arguing it forced deflationary policy when expansion was needed to cure unemployment. In *The Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill*, he warned the gold standard would depress British industry—a forecast borne out until Britain abandoned gold in 1931. During this decade, his pro-stimulus views had limited policy impact; Hyman Minsky later judged his theoretical justification "muddled."

### The Great Depression and *The General Theory*
The 1930 *Treatise on Money* (two volumes) linked unemployment to saving exceeding investment when interest rates are too high, and criticised financial indices for misrepresenting purchasing power. As the Depression deepened, Keynes advocated counter-cyclical public spending in *The Means to Prosperity* (1933), introducing the **multiplier effect** (adapted from R.F. Kahn’s employment multiplier). He corresponded with President Roosevelt, though significant US influence came only after 1939.

*The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936) provided the theoretical core. Keynes rejected **Say’s Law** ("supply creates its demand") and the neoclassical assumption that flexible wages ensure full employment. He introduced **price stickiness**: workers resist nominal wage cuts, so labour markets do not clear. Consequently, **aggregate demand** (consumption + investment) determines output and employment. If demand is insufficient, the economy can rest in a stable low-employment equilibrium. Government must raise expenditure—via public works or lower interest rates—to escape the trap. He also reinterpreted inflation as "taxation by currency depreciation," arguing the standard of value should be a deliberate policy choice. The book founded modern macroeconomics, though Keynes’s 1937 heart attack left formalisation to others (Hicks, Samuelson, Modigliani), producing the **neoclassical synthesis** (IS–LM model) that Post-Keynesians argue distorts his emphasis on radical uncertainty.

### Shift to Protectionism and Trade Theory
Initially a free-trader under Marshall, Keynes turned protectionist after 1929. He argued the gold standard’s rigidity and downward wage stickiness made the Ricardian **comparative advantage** assumptions (full employment, perfect labour mobility, automatic adjustment) unrealistic. In *National Self-Sufficiency* (1933), he wrote: "let goods be homespun whenever it is reasonably and conveniently possible, and, above all, let finance be primarily national." He proposed tariffs (10–15%) to eliminate trade deficits, arguing surplus countries impose a "negative externality" on deficit partners by weakening global aggregate demand. At Bretton Woods, his **Keynes Plan** proposed an International Clearing Union issuing a supranational currency (**bancor**) with symmetric pressure on surplus and deficit nations. The US (Harry Dexter White) rejected symmetry; the IMF/World Bank placed adjustment burden solely on deficit countries. J. Bradford DeLong notes Keynes was later proven correct on nearly every point where he was overruled.

### World War II and Postwar Architecture
In *How to Pay for the War* (1940), Keynes advocated compulsory saving (forced loans from workers) over deficit spending to finance the war without inflation. He served on the Bank of England Court of Directors (1941–1946) and led the British delegation at Bretton Woods (1944). Despite his failing health, he secured favourable terms for the 1945 Anglo-American loan. His final hope, expressed to Henry Clay in 1946, was that Adam Smith’s "invisible hand" might yet solve Britain’s postwar difficulties—a striking concession from the architect of managed capitalism.

### Inflation, Alienation, and Ethical Views
Keynes preferred mild inflation to deflation ("better to disappoint the rentier" than hurt working families) but warned in *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* that currency debasement "engages all the hidden forces of economic law on the side of destruction." He did not deeply engage with **alienation**; in *Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren* (1931), he predicted growth would eventually make the pursuit of money irrelevant, allowing lives of leisure—a vision partially realised in wealth but not in the elimination of alienation. A lifelong Liberal, he supported eugenics (treasurer of Cambridge Eugenics Society 1911; director of British Eugenics Society 1937–1944), linking population "quality" to economic planning, but opposed compulsory sterilisation. He rejected Marxism as scientifically erroneous and declared in 1925: "the class war will find me on the side of the educated bourgeoisie." He supported Zionism, helped Jewish friends (e.g., Wittgenstein), and condemned Nazism once its aggression was clear, criticising the Left for pacifist wavering in 1939–1940.

### Investments and Arts Patronage
A successful investor, Keynes rebuilt a fortune nearly wiped out in the 1929 crash (net worth ~£500,000 at death, ~£24m in 2024). Managing King’s College endowment from the 1920s, he pioneered **value investing** and **diversification** ("opposed risks" moving in opposite directions), shifting from land/bonds to small-cap dividend stocks, outperforming the market by 6–8% annually for 25 years. He chaired National Mutual Life Assurance (1921–1938), legitimising active equity management for insurance funds. As a Bloomsbury Group member, he funded the Cambridge Arts Theatre, the Royal Opera House, Sadler’s Wells Ballet, and founded the Arts Council of Great Britain (1946). He collected modern art (Cézanne, Picasso, Matisse) and Newton’s manuscripts, calling Newton "the last of the magicians."

## Terms
- ****Aggregate demand**** — Total spending in the economy (consumption + investment + government spending + net exports); the key determinant of output and employment in Keynesian theory.
- ****Price stickiness**** — The resistance of nominal wages and prices to adjust downward, preventing labour markets from clearing and enabling persistent unemployment equilibria.
- ****Say’s Law**** — The classical proposition that "supply creates its own demand"; Keynes rejected this as a special case valid only under 19th-century conditions.
- ****Multiplier effect**** — The process by which an initial increase in spending (e.g., government public works) generates a larger total increase in income and employment through successive rounds of consumption.
- ****Comparative advantage**** — Ricardian theory that countries should specialise in goods they produce relatively efficiently; Keynes criticised its assumptions (full employment, perfect labour mobility) as unrealistic in the 1930s.
- ****Bancor**** — Keynes’s proposed supranational currency for the International Clearing Union at Bretton Woods, designed to symmetrically penalise trade surpluses and deficits.
- ****Neoclassical synthesis**** — The post-WWII fusion of Keynesian macroeconomics with neoclassical microfoundations (IS–LM model), dominant until the 1970s.
- ****Stagflation**** — Simultaneous high inflation and high unemployment (1970s), which contradicted the Phillips curve and fuelled monetarist critiques of Keynesianism.
- ****New Keynesian economics**** — 1990s–2000s framework providing microfoundations (rational expectations + nominal rigidities/imperfect competition) for Keynesian policy conclusions.
- ****Liquidity preference**** — Keynes’s theory that interest rates are determined by the supply of and demand for money (liquidity), not solely by saving and investment.

## Debates and open questions
- **Versailles reparations**: Historians debate whether the treaty was economically harsh (Keynes’s view) or manageable with US loans (Henig, Schuker). Keynes’s prediction of disaster is cited by followers; critics note Germany received net capital inflows.
- **Theoretical fidelity of the neoclassical synthesis**: Post-Keynesians (Minsky, Davidson) argue IS–LM models erase Keynes’s emphasis on **fundamental uncertainty** and the non-ergodic nature of economic systems.
- **1970s stagflation cause**: Monetarists (Friedman) blame excessive Keynesian demand management; Keynesians (Kuttner, Pugh) cite the collapse of Bretton Woods capital controls, the Vietnam War’s deficit financing, or supply shocks (oil).
- **Protectionism vs. free trade**: Keynes’s 1930s turn to tariffs and managed trade remains contested; mainstream economics largely reverted to comparative advantage after Bretton Woods, though trade imbalance concerns resurfaced post-2008.
- **Eugenics legacy**: Scholars debate whether Keynes’s eugenics advocacy was a marginal prejudice or integral to his vision of "managed" social and economic planning.
- **2008 stimulus efficacy**: Economists remain divided on the magnitude of fiscal multipliers, the risk of debt overhang, and whether Keynesian coordination prevented a second Great Depression or merely delayed adjustment.
