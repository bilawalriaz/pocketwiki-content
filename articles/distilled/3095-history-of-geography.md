# History of geography

## Overview
Geography is the study of the Earth's surface, its features, and the relationship between humans and their environment. Its history traces the evolution from ancient descriptions of the known world to a modern academic discipline, reflecting changing cultural, religious, and scientific paradigms. Understanding this history reveals how different societies have conceptualized space, place, and their own position within the world.

## Timeline
- **c. 9th century BC** — Oldest known world maps date from ancient Babylon.
- **c. 600 BC** — The Babylonian *Imago Mundi* world map is created.
- **c. 276–194 BC** — Eratosthenes first uses the term "geography" and calculates Earth's circumference.
- **c. 300–350 AD** — The *Vishnudharmottara Purana* in India contains chapters on physical and human geography.
- **5th century BC** — The earliest known Chinese geographical writing, the *Yu Gong*, appears.
- **6th century AD** — Byzantine geographers like Stephanus of Byzantium and Hierocles produce key works.
- **1025 AD** — Persian scholar Al-Biruni describes a polar equidistant projection of the celestial sphere.
- **1154 AD** — Al-Idrisi creates the advanced world map *Tabula Rogeriana* for Norman Sicily.
- **1507 AD** — Martin Waldseemüller's map is the first to name the continent "America."
- **1845 AD** — Alexander von Humboldt begins publishing his influential work *Kosmos*.
- **1888 AD** — The National Geographic Society is founded in the United States.
- **1950s** — The "quantitative revolution" begins in geography, emphasizing statistical methods.

## Body

### Ancient Foundations
Early geographical knowledge was often intertwined with mythology and religion. Ancient Egypt viewed the Nile as the center of the world, while Babylonian maps like the *Imago Mundi* depicted a flat Earth surrounded by a "bitter river." The Greeks initiated a more scientific approach; Homer's works contained geographical data, and philosophers like Thales and Anaximander pondered the Earth's shape and created early maps. Herodotus provided extensive descriptions of the known world, and Pythagoras first proposed a spherical Earth, an idea later verified empirically by Aristotle. Eratosthenes calculated the Earth's circumference with great precision.

### Classical and Medieval Synthesis
The Hellenistic and Roman periods saw geography become a tool of empire. Alexander the Great's campaigns expanded Greek knowledge eastward, while Roman engineers used sophisticated surveying for their vast road network. Key works by Strabo and Ptolemy synthesized classical knowledge, though Ptolemy's geocentric model and smaller Earth circumference estimate would influence cartography for centuries. After the fall of Rome, geographical knowledge in Europe regressed, but it was preserved and advanced in the Byzantine Empire and the Islamic world. Islamic scholars like Al-Biruni and Al-Idrisi made major advances in mathematical geography and mapmaking, building on Greek, Persian, and Indian sources.

### Early Modern to 19th Century
The Age of Exploration (15th-17th centuries) shattered medieval geographical conceptions. Voyages by Columbus, da Gama, and Magellan revealed the Americas and global oceanic connections, leading to the Columbian Exchange. This era revived interest in accurate mapping, exemplified by the Waldseemüller map (1507) and Gerardus Mercator's projections. In the 18th and 19th centuries, geography became institutionalized as a university discipline. Thinkers like Immanuel Kant separated it from theology, while Alexander von Humboldt and Carl Ritter established it as a holistic, empirical science. Humboldt's *Kosmos* aimed to synthesize all natural knowledge, and the founding of societies like the Royal Geographical Society (1830) marked its professionalization.

### 20th Century Themes and Debates
The 20th century saw geography engage with several major themes. **Environmental determinism**, the theory that the physical environment directly shapes human societies, was prominent but later widely repudiated as unscientific and prone to bias. **Regional geography** focused on descriptive studies of specific places. The **quantitative revolution** of the 1950s introduced statistical methods and spatial analysis, laying the groundwork for GIS. In reaction, **critical geography** emerged in the 1970s, applying Marxist, feminist, and postmodern theories to examine power, inequality, and social justice in spatial contexts. This led to a persistent split between "social-cultural" and "spatial-analytical" approaches within the discipline.

## Terms
- **Cartography**: The science and art of making maps.
- **Environmental determinism**: The theory that a people's physical, mental, and moral habits are directly caused by their natural environment.
- **Geodesy**: The science of accurately measuring and understanding the Earth's geometric shape, orientation in space, and gravity field.
- **Gnomon**: The part of a sundial that casts a shadow; used by ancient Greeks for astronomical measurements.
- **Physiography**: The study of physical geography and the natural features of the Earth's surface.
- **Possibilism**: The theory that the natural environment provides constraints and possibilities, but human culture is shaped by social and cultural factors.
- **Quantitative revolution**: A period in geography (1950s-60s) emphasizing statistical methods, spatial analysis, and the formulation of testable theories.
- **Regional geography**: The study of the unique characteristics of a specific region, integrating physical and human elements.
- **Spatial analysis**: The study of the distribution of phenomena and processes across space.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which the physical environment determines human culture and development remains a contested issue, with modern critiques of environmental determinism.
- There is an ongoing methodological and philosophical split within geography between quantitative, spatial-analytical approaches and qualitative, critical, and humanistic approaches.
- The discipline continues to debate its core focus: whether it is primarily the study of spatial patterns, the relationships between humans and their environment, or the analysis of places and regions.
