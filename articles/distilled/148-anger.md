# Anger

## Overview

Anger (also called wrath or rage) is an intense emotional state involving a strong, uncomfortable, and non-cooperative response to a perceived provocation, hurt, or threat. It triggers physical effects like increased heart rate, elevated blood pressure, and higher levels of stress hormones adrenaline and noradrenaline, and is linked to the fight-or-flight response. Modern psychologists view anger as a normal, natural emotion with functional value for survival and cooperation, but uncontrolled anger can harm personal and social well-being. The study of anger spans psychology, neuroscience, philosophy, and religion, with debates over its intrinsic value and the effects of suppressing versus expressing it.

## Timeline

- **Ancient period** — Greek philosophers (Galen, Seneca, Aristotle) debate anger's value; Seneca calls it "worthless even for war," Aristotle sees use against injustice
- **Medieval period** — Aquinas, Ibn Sina, and others elaborate on anger; al-Ghazali argues animals possess anger; wrath becomes one of the Seven Deadly Sins in Catholicism
- **c. 1200** — Medieval authors define gender differences via humoral theory: men choleric (hot/dry, aggressive), women phlegmatic (cold/wet, passive)
- **1975** — Raymond Novaco begins publishing influential literature on anger, stratifying it into cognitive, somatic-affective, and behavioral modalities
- **1981** — Study using modeling, behavior rehearsal, and videotaped feedback shows increases in anger control among aggressive youth
- **1992** — Los Angeles riots cited as example of sudden, explosive release of suppressed anger
- **2005** — Study shows angry subjects retrospectively rated terrorism risks after 9/11 as low
- **2007** — Matsumoto study finds observers cannot distinguish ethnic-specific expressions of anger
- **2010** — Fernandez formulates CBAT (cognitive behavioral affective therapy); Dutch study shows anger increases desire for objects
- **2021** — Gallup World Poll finds 23% of adults experienced a lot of anger, up from 18% in 2014
- **2023** — Study reveals five-factor model of anger from 46 subscales of self-report measures

## Body

### Psychology and sociology

Psychologists recognize three types of anger: hasty and sudden anger (connected to self-preservation, shared with animals, episodic), settled and deliberate anger (reaction to perceived deliberate harm or unfair treatment, episodic), and dispositional anger (related to character traits like irritability and sullenness). Anger can mobilize psychological resources toward correcting wrongs and promoting social justice, but in strong form impairs information processing and cognitive control. There is a sharp distinction between anger and aggression—anger can activate aggression but is neither necessary nor sufficient for it.

### Neuropsychological perspective

The human infant initially struggles indiscriminately against any restraining force; later, the fighting reaction becomes limited to stimuli whose hurting or restraining influence can be thrown off by physical violence. Brain regions activated when recognizing threat include the salience network (dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and anterior insula cortex) and subcortical areas (thalamus, amygdala, brain stem). Neuroimaging studies show the lateral orbitofrontal cortex is the most consistently activated region during anger, associated with approach motivation.

### Differences between related concepts

Raymond Novaco stratified anger into three modalities: cognitive (appraisals), somatic-affective (tension and agitations), and behavioral (withdrawal and antagonism). Annoyance and rage are imagined at opposite ends of an emotional continuum. Rage problems are conceptualized as "the inability to process emotions or life's experiences" due to insufficiently developed emotion regulation capacity or temporary loss from recent trauma. Anger viewed as a protective response to perceived threat is considered positive; the negative expression is aggression, which can manifest as antisocial personality disorder and intermittent explosive disorder.

### Characteristics and expression styles

William DeFoore described anger as a pressure cooker: "we can only suppress or apply pressure against our happy for so long before it erupts." A simple trichotomy of anger expression is passive, aggressive, and assertive anger. Passive anger includes dispassion, evasiveness, defeatism, obsessive behavior, psychological manipulation, secretive behavior, and self-blame. Aggressive anger includes bullying, destruction, grandiosity, hurtfulness, risk-taking, selfishness, threats, unjust blaming, unpredictability, and vengeance. Assertive anger includes blame, punishment, and sternness. Ephrem Fernandez identified six dimensions of anger expression: direction, locus, reaction, modality, impulsivity, and objective.

### Ethnicity and culture

Research on anger across cultures shows mixed findings. Matsumoto (2007) found observers could not distinguish ethnic-specific expressions of anger. Hatfield, Rapson, and Le (2009) found differences in expression frequency across ethnicities, with Europeans showing the lowest frequency of negative emotion expression. Mabry and Kiecolt (2005) found Black Americans did not feel or express more anger than Whites after controlling for sex and age. Deffenbacher and Swaim (1999) found White non-Hispanic Americans expressed more verbal aggression than Mexican Americans, with no significant difference in physical aggression.

### Causes

Anger arises when people sense offense to themselves or loved ones, are certain about the nature and cause of the angering event, are convinced someone else is responsible, and feel they can influence the situation. Psychotherapist Michael C. Graham defines anger in terms of expectations: anger results when we are "expecting the world to be different than it is." People rarely find more than one cause for their anger, though anger can have multicausal origins including remote events. Sleep deprivation and internal infections can also cause anger. According to cognitive consistency theory, anger is caused by inconsistency between desired and perceived situations.

### Cognitive effects

Anger reduces cognitive ability and accurate processing of external stimuli—dangers seem smaller, actions less risky, ventures more likely to succeed. Angry people make riskier decisions and less realistic risk assessments. Unlike sadness and fear, anger promotes correspondence bias (blaming behavior on nature rather than circumstances), reliance on stereotypes, and less attention to details. Angry people anticipate more anger-causing events, place more blame on others (creating a feedback loop), and focus attention only on anger-causing events rather than all negative events. A 2010 Dutch study found anger-primed subjects expressed more desire for random objects than fear-primed subjects.

### Expressive strategies

Display of anger can be feigned or exaggerated and serves as an effective manipulation strategy for social influence. Larissa Tiedens found that people expressing anger were perceived as powerful, stubborn, and dominant, and were attributed higher social status. Studies show people concede more to angry opponents than happy ones in negotiations, analyzing the opponent's emotion to conclude about their limits.

### Coping strategies

Conventional therapies involve restructuring thoughts and beliefs, typically within cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) or rational emotive behavior therapy (REBT). "Stress inoculation" teaches relaxation skills and cognitive controls. The skills-deficit model states poor social skills render a person incapable of expressing anger appropriately; social skills training and aggression replacement training have shown significant reductions in anger. Fernandez's CBAT (cognitive behavioral affective therapy) sequences techniques in three phases: prevention, intervention, and postvention. Certain psychiatric medications may help control anger symptoms, including SSRI antidepressants like sertraline, anticonvulsant mood stabilizers, antipsychotics like aripiprazole and risperidone, and benzodiazepines like midazolam, though findings are mixed.

Suppression of anger may have harmful effects—it may find another outlet such as physical symptoms or become more extreme. Examples include the Los Angeles riots of 1992 and Francine Hughes, who killed her abusive husband after 13 years of domestic abuse. Some psychologists criticize the catharsis theory of aggression (which suggests releasing pent-up anger reduces aggression). Studies link suppressed anger to hypertension, coronary artery disease, cancer, irritable bowel syndrome, eating disorders, depression, and higher suicide rates.

### Dual threshold model

The Dual Threshold Model of Anger in organizations (Geddes and Callister, 2007) explains anger expression outcomes. The "expression threshold" is crossed when an employee conveys felt anger to those able to address the situation; the "impropriety threshold" is crossed when expression becomes socially or culturally inappropriate. Negative outcomes occur when employees suppress anger (failing to cross the expression threshold) or "double cross" both thresholds. Positive outcomes occur when expression stays between the thresholds, prompting productive exchanges.

### Physiology

Emotions are generated by multiple brain structures. The amygdala performs rapid, minimal evaluative processing of emotional significance; the thalamus relays information to the neocortex for discrimination of stimulus features. External expression includes physiological responses: rib cage tenses, breathing becomes faster and irregular, the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activates, heart rate and blood pressure increase, blood flows to hands, perspiration increases, the face flushes, nostrils flare, and the jaw tenses.

### Measuring anger

The Gallup World Poll asks people in over 140 countries whether they experienced anger during a lot of the day yesterday; 23% said yes in 2021, up from 18% in 2014. The Spielberger State-Trait Anger Expression Inventory and Novaco Anger Scale and Provocation Inventory are widely used self-report measures. A 2023 study of 46 subscales revealed five factors: anger-arousal, anger-rumination, frustration-discomfort, anger-regulation, and socially constituted anger.

### Philosophical perspectives

Ancient Greek philosophers generally showed hostile attitudes toward uncontrolled anger. Seneca regarded anger as a kind of madness, "worthless even for war," while Aristotle ascribed value to anger arising from perceived injustice. Seneca advised avoiding anger by remembering its faults, checking speech and impulses, and daily self-inquisition; Galen added finding a guide and teacher. Both agreed anger control should start in childhood. Medieval philosophers elaborated on these views; al-Ghazali argued animals possess anger, while Ibn Sina, Roger Bacon, and Thomas Aquinas disagreed. Medieval beliefs held that those prone to anger had excess yellow bile or choler. Aquinas felt justified anger was not a sin. Medieval gender theory held men were choleric (hot, dry, dominant, aggressive) and women phlegmatic (cold, wet, passive, submissive). Modern perspectives differ from ancient ones in two ways: early philosophers were not concerned with harmful effects of anger suppression, and recent studies consider gender differences. Immanuel Kant rejects revenge as vicious; David Hume argues lack of anger can be "evidence of weakness and imbecility." Soraya Chemaly argues anger is "a critically useful and positive emotion" and a "powerful force for political good."

### Religious perspectives

In Judaism, anger is a negative trait; Jacob condemned his sons' anger, and restraining oneself from anger is noble. Maimonides rules that one who becomes angry is as though they had worshipped idols. In Catholicism, wrath is one of the Seven Deadly Sins, though the Catechism states anger as a passion is neither good nor evil—it becomes sinful when directed against an innocent person, unduly unbending, or desiring excessive punishment. Some Christian writers valued anger caused by injustice. Protestant writers like Andrew D. Lester see anger as "a spiritual friend" and argue expressing anger toward God can deepen the relationship. In Hinduism, anger is equated with sorrow as a form of unrequited desire; the Bhagavad Gita regards greed, anger, and lust as signs of ignorance leading to bondage. In Buddhism, anger is "being unable to bear the object, or the intention to cause harm to the object," listed as one of the five hindrances; the Dalai Lama states anger is a destructive emotion and not a virtuous one. In Islam, anger is considered instigated by Satan, and suppression of anger is deemed praiseworthy; the Quran instructs people to restrain their anger. In many religions, anger is attributed to God or gods; the Hebrew Bible says opposition to God's will results in God's anger.

## Terms

- **Fight or flight response**: Physiological reaction to threat involving increased heart rate and stress hormones
- **Salience network**: Brain regions (dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, anterior insula cortex) activated when recognizing threat
- **CBT/REBT**: Cognitive behavioral therapy and rational emotive behavior therapy, schools of therapy that restructure thoughts to reduce anger
- **Correspondence bias**: Tendency to blame behavior on nature rather than circumstances, increased by anger
- **Catharsis theory**: Theory that releasing pent-up anger reduces aggression, criticized by some psychologists
- **Dual Threshold Model**: Model explaining workplace anger outcomes via "expression" and "impropriety" thresholds
- **Humoral theory**: Medieval belief that temperament results from balance of four qualities (heat, coldness, moistness, dryness) and humors
- **Kleshas**: In Buddhism, three root causes of bondage: greed, hatred, and delusion
- **CBAT**: Cognitive behavioral affective therapy, integrative anger treatment with prevention, intervention, and postvention phases
- **Choleric**: Medieval term for those prone to anger, believed to have excess yellow bile

## Debates and open questions

Ancient philosophers disagreed over anger's intrinsic value: Seneca held it "worthless even for war," while Aristotle ascribed value to anger from perceived injustice. Modern psychologists, unlike earlier writers, point out possible ill effects of suppressing anger on well-being and interpersonal relationships. Some psychologists criticize the catharsis theory of aggression, while others maintain suppression does not eliminate anger. Scholars debate whether anger expression is genetically determined—distinguishing genetic from environmental factors requires further research and actual measurement of specific genes and environments. Research on ethnic differences in anger expression shows conflicting results depending on methodology and comparison groups.
