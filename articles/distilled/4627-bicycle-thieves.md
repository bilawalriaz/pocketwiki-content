# Bicycle Thieves

## Overview
*Bicycle Thieves* (1948) is a foundational Italian neorealist film directed by Vittorio De Sica. It depicts a poor father's desperate search for his stolen bicycle in post-WWII Rome, a loss that threatens his only chance at employment. The film is celebrated for its raw realism, use of non-professional actors, and profound social commentary, cementing its status as one of the most influential and acclaimed films in cinema history.

## Body

### Production and Neorealism
*Bicycle Thieves* is the quintessential work of Italian neorealism, a movement emphasizing realism through location shooting and non-professional actors. Director Vittorio De Sica, unable to secure studio funding, financed the film himself. He and screenwriter Cesare Zavattini loosely adapted Bartolini's novel, focusing on the title and core plot device. The cast included factory worker Lamberto Maggiorani as the father and 8-year-old Enzo Staiola, discovered selling flowers on the street, as the son. This approach aimed to portray the authentic poverty and unemployment of post-war Italy.

### Plot and Themes
The narrative follows Antonio Ricci, who secures a job posting bills but requires a bicycle. His wife Maria pawns her dowry sheets to redeem his old one. On his first day, the bicycle is stolen. The police offer little help, forcing Antonio and his son Bruno to search the city's markets and streets. Their increasingly hopeless quest exposes the indifference of society and institutions. The film culminates in Antonio's own attempted theft of a bicycle, leading to his public humiliation and a moment of compassion from the bicycle's owner. The story explores themes of poverty, desperation, and the erosion of dignity.

### Reception and Legacy
Initial Italian reception was hostile, with critics and the novel's author feeling it portrayed Italians negatively. International reviews, however, were overwhelmingly positive, praising its universal human drama. The film has since been canonized, consistently ranking high in *Sight & Sound* polls and appearing on numerous "greatest films" lists. It has profoundly influenced global cinema, cited as a major inspiration by directors like Satyajit Ray, Ken Loach, and Martin Scorsese, and is considered a cornerstone of the Italian neorealist canon.

## Terms
*   **Italian Neorealism:** A film movement (c. 1943-1952) characterized by stories set among the poor and working class, filmed on location, and using non-professional actors to depict the harsh realities of post-WWII Italy.
*   **Location Shooting:** Filming on actual sites rather than on constructed studio sets, a key tenet of neorealism for achieving authenticity.
*   **Non-professional Actor:** An individual cast in a film role without prior formal acting training, often chosen for their naturalistic appearance and connection to the character's social reality.
*   **Dowry:** Property or money brought by a bride to her husband at marriage; Maria pawns her prized dowry bedsheets to help her family.
*   **Carabiniere:** A member of the Italian national gendarmerie, a military police force with jurisdiction throughout the country.
*   **Honorary Award:** A special Academy Award given to recognize extraordinary distinction, in this case for the most outstanding foreign language film of 1949.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Title Translation:** The original Italian title *Ladri di biciclette* is plural ("Bicycle Thieves"). The common English title *The Bicycle Thief* (singular) is considered a poor translation by purists, though some critics argue its power as a title.
*   **Fidelity to Source:** Author Luigi Bartolini felt the film betrayed his novel, which featured a middle-class intellectual protagonist and focused on the breakdown of civil order, not a working-class man's personal tragedy.
*   **Artistic Choices:** Director Luchino Visconti criticized De Sica's decision to use a professional actor to dub over the non-professional lead's dialogue, seeing it as a break from neorealist principles.
