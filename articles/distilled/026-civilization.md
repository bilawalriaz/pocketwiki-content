# Civilization

## Overview

A civilization is any complex society characterized by the development of the state, urbanization, and standardized symbolic systems of communication, namely writing systems. Civilizations are organized around densely populated settlements, divided into hierarchical social classes with a division of labour, often with a ruling elite and subordinate urban and rural populations engaged in intensive agriculture, mining, manufacture, and trade. The concept matters because it describes the most complex form of human social organization, tracing the emergence of cities, states, writing, and concentrated power from the Neolithic Revolution to the present, while also raising questions about progress, decline, and cultural identity.

## Timeline

- **c. 11,000 BCE** — Natufian culture in the Levant shows earliest planting of cereal crops
- **c. 9,130 BCE** — Earliest Neolithic technology at Göbekli Tepe in Western Asia
- **c. 7,400 years ago** — Earliest civilizations develop in Mesopotamia
- **c. 7,000 BCE** — Independent Neolithic revolutions begin in northwestern South America and Mesoamerica
- **5500–4200 BCE** — Solnitsata, possibly the oldest known town in Europe, is built
- **c. 3600 BCE** — Chalcolithic civilizations rise in Mesopotamia, expanding into Bronze Age kingdoms
- **c. 3500 BCE** — Urbanization begins in the Caral-Supe civilization of coastal Peru
- **c. 1200 BCE** — Iron Age begins after the Bronze Age collapse; Olmec civilization emerges in North America
- **8th–3rd century BCE** — Axial Age, a critical transitional phase leading to classical civilization
- **c. 1500 CE** — Major transition to modernity begins in Western Europe

## Body

### History of the concept

The English word "civilization" comes from the French *civilisé* ('civilized'), from Latin *civilis* ('civil'), related to *civis* ('citizen') and *civitas* ('city'). The abstract noun meaning "civilized condition" came in the 1760s from French; the first known French use was in 1757 by Victor de Riqueti, marquis de Mirabeau, and the first English use is attributed to Adam Ferguson in his 1767 *Essay on the History of Civil Society*. The word was opposed to barbarism or rudeness, in the active pursuit of progress characteristic of the Age of Enlightenment. In the late 1700s and early 1800s, during the French Revolution, "civilization" was used in the singular to mean the progress of humanity as a whole; the plural "civilizations" became common only in the later 20th century.

Already in the 18th century, civilization was not always seen as improvement. Rousseau, in *Emile*, argued that civilization, being more rational and socially driven, is not fully in accord with human nature. This view, developed in Germany by Johann Gottfried Herder and later philosophers such as Kierkegaard and Nietzsche, saw cultures as natural organisms defined by pre-rational "folk spirit," while civilization, though more rational and materially successful, was unnatural and led to "vices of social life" such as guile, hypocrisy, envy, and avarice. Leo Strauss, having fled Germany, argued in New York during World War II that this opinion of civilization was behind Nazism and German militarism.

### Characteristics

Social scientists such as V. Gordon Childe have named traits distinguishing civilizations from other societies: means of subsistence, settlement patterns, forms of government, social stratification, economic systems, literacy, and other cultural traits. Andrew Nikiforuk argues that "civilizations relied on shackled human muscle" and considers slavery a common feature of pre-modern civilizations. All civilizations have depended on agriculture, with the possible exception of some early Peruvian civilizations that may have depended on maritime resources. The traditional "surplus model" postulates that cereal farming produces stored surplus, especially with intensive techniques like irrigation and crop rotation. Research from the *Journal of Political Economy* contradicts this, arguing horticultural gardening was more productive but only cereal farming produced civilization because the yearly harvest could be taxed, allowing a taxing elite and urban development.

A food surplus permits specialization: soldiers, artisans, priests, and others with specialized careers, creating a division of labour. Civilizations have distinctly different settlement patterns—the word is sometimes defined as "living in cities"—and more complex political structures, namely the state. Morton Fried and Elman Service classified human cultures into four categories: hunter-gatherer bands (generally egalitarian); horticultural–pastoralist societies with two inherited classes; chiefdoms with several inherited classes; and civilizations with complex hierarchies and institutional government. Economically, civilizations display complex patterns of ownership and exchange, using money as a medium of exchange by the early Iron Age. Writing, developed first in Sumer, is considered a hallmark of civilization, though the Inca civilization functioned without it, using knotted strings called *quipus*.

The transition to complex economies does not necessarily mean improved living standards: males in the Middle Ages (c. 500–1500 CE) were taller than those in the preceding Roman Empire or succeeding Early Modern Period (c. 1500–1800 CE), and 19th-century Plains Indians were taller than their "civilized" American and European counterparts.

### As a contrast with other societies

The idea of civilization implies progression from a previous "uncivilized" state. Cultures defining themselves as "civilized" often did so in contrast to others, calling them barbarians, savages, and primitives. The modern Western idea developed as a contrast to indigenous cultures encountered during European colonization of the Americas and Australia. In the 19th century, European culture as "civilized" and superior became fully developed, and civilization became a core part of European identity, sometimes used to justify dominating other cultures. In Australia, British settlers justified displacing Indigenous Australians by observing that the land appeared uncultivated and wild. Since early modern times, Eurocentric ideals of "civilization" have been widely imposed through coercion and dominance.

### Cultural identity

"Civilization" can also refer to the culture of a complex society. Civilizations tend to develop intricate cultures, including state-based decision-making, literature, professional art, architecture, organized religion, and complex customs of education and control. The intricate culture tends to spread and influence other cultures, a classic example being Chinese civilization's influence on Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. Many civilizations are large cultural spheres containing many nations and regions; the civilization in which someone lives is that person's broadest cultural identity. International law, the United Nations, and UNESCO aim to preserve cultural heritage and identity, especially in war. According to Karl von Habsburg of Blue Shield International, destruction of cultural assets is part of psychological warfare targeting the opponent's cultural identity.

Philosopher Oswald Spengler used the German word *Kultur* for what many call "civilization," believing a civilization's coherence is based on a single primary cultural symbol, with cultures experiencing cycles of birth, life, decline, and death. Arnold J. Toynbee traced the rise and decline of 21 civilizations and five "arrested civilizations," attributing decline to the failure of a "creative minority" through moral or religious decline. Samuel P. Huntington defines civilization as "the highest cultural grouping of people and the broadest level of cultural identity people have short of that which distinguishes humans from other species."

### Complex systems

Systems theorists view civilizations as complex systems—networks of cities defined by economic, political, military, diplomatic, social, and cultural interactions. Trade networks were, until the nineteenth century, much larger than cultural or political spheres; the Silk Road and Indian Ocean routes linked the Roman Empire, Persia, India, and China 2000 years ago. David Wilkinson proposed that economic and military-diplomatic integration of Mesopotamian and Egyptian civilizations created a "Central Civilization" around 1500 BCE, which later expanded globally through European colonization. Many theorists argue the world has become integrated into a single "world system" through globalization, though there is debate over when this integration began.

### History and fall of civilizations

The notion of human history as a succession of "civilizations" is modern. Mesopotamia is the site of the earliest civilizations, developing from 7,400 years ago, and has been evaluated by Beverley Milton-Edwards as having "inspired some of the most important developments in human history including the invention of the wheel, the building of the earliest cities and the development of written cursive script." The "urban revolution"—a term introduced by Childe in the 1930s—from the 4th millennium BCE marked the beginning of accumulated economic surpluses, associated with the state monopoly of violence, a warrior class, endemic warfare, hierarchies, and human sacrifice. The Bronze Age collapse was followed by the Iron Age around 1200 BCE, culminating in the Axial Age (8th–3rd century BCE). A major transition to modernity began approximately 1500 CE in Western Europe.

Civilizations traditionally end either by incorporation into another expanding civilization or by collapsing into simpler living. Explanations for collapse include: Ibn Khaldun's suggestion of nomadic invasions; Edward Gibbon's view that Rome's decline was "the natural and inevitable effect of immoderate greatness"; Joseph Tainter's theory of diminishing returns to complexity; Jared Diamond's five reasons (environmental damage, climate change, dependence on long-distance trade, violence, and societal responses); and Peter Turchin's "fiscal-demographic" model, where population growth leads to fiscal problems, famine, epidemics, and collapse. Peter Heather argues Rome fell because centuries of contact with barbarians made them more sophisticated adversaries. Bryan Ward-Perkins argues from archaeological evidence that Rome's collapse had deleterious impacts on living standards. Arthur Demarest argues the Maya collapse resulted from erratic, complex events including soil fertility loss, drought, and violence. Thomas Homer-Dixon considers the energy return on investments central to limiting civilization survival. Feliks Koneczny asserted civilizations cannot be mixed into hybrids and that an inferior civilization given equal rights within a highly developed one will overcome it.

### Future and non-human civilizations

Samuel P. Huntington argues the 21st century will be characterized by a clash of civilizations, though this is challenged by Edward Said, Muhammed Asadi, and Amartya Sen. Ronald Inglehart and Pippa Norris argue the "true clash" between the Muslim world and West is caused by Muslim rejection of Western liberal sexual values. Cultural historian Morris Berman argues the United States has crossed a threshold where collapse is inevitable due to extreme individualism, expansion, and material wealth pursuit. Derrick Jensen defines civilization as "a culture... that both leads to and emerges from the growth of cities," arguing it is intrinsically harmful and unsustainable. The Kardashev scale classifies civilizations by energy harnessed, though an alternative view suggests future civilization may be characterized by technological minimalism and efficiency rather than ever-increasing energy consumption.

The current scientific consensus is that humans are the only animal species on Earth with the cognitive ability to create civilizations. The silurian hypothesis considers whether an industrial civilization could be detected in the geological record. Astronomers speculate about alien intelligent civilizations using the Drake equation and search for "technosignatures"; the proposed field "xenoarchaeology" concerns study of non-human civilization artifacts.

## Terms

- **State**: A complex political structure with institutionalized government, more stratified than other societies
- **Neolithic Revolution**: The transition to agriculture and sedentary settlement, critical to modern civilization
- **Urban revolution**: Childe's term for the 4th-millennium BCE development of cities, surpluses, and state structures
- **Surplus model**: Theory that cereal farming produces stored food surplus enabling specialization
- **Quipus**: Knotted strings of different lengths and colours used by the Inca for recording, in place of writing
- **Axial Age**: Period from the 8th to 3rd century BCE, a critical transitional phase to classical civilization
- **Kardashev scale**: Hypothetical classification of civilizations by the amount of energy they can harness
- **Technosignatures**: Technological traces searched for as evidence of alien intelligent civilizations
- **Silurian hypothesis**: Thought experiment on whether an industrial civilization could be detected in the geological record

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether the surplus model or the appropriability of cereal harvests explains civilization's emergence; whether writing is necessary for civilization (the Inca counterexample); whether civilization improves living standards (stature evidence suggests not always); when globalization began and what integration type is key; whether civilizations decline due to moral failure (Toynbee), diminishing returns (Tainter), environmental factors (Diamond), or fiscal dynamics (Turchin); whether civilizations can mix (Koneczny says no); and whether the 21st century will see a "clash of civilizations" (Huntington) or whether that framing dangerously ignores other identities (Sen).
