# Çatalhöyük

## Overview
Çatalhöyük is a large Neolithic and Chalcolithic proto-city settlement in southern Anatolia (modern Turkey), occupied from around 7500 BC to 5600 BC. It is a key archaeological site for understanding early permanent human settlements, notable for its dense, egalitarian community structure, rich symbolic culture, and well-preserved domestic architecture. Its study has significantly shaped debates on the origins of social complexity, religion, and gender roles in prehistoric societies.

## Timeline
- **c. 7500 BC** — Initial occupation of the Eastern Mound begins.
- **c. 7000 BC** — Settlement flourishes at its peak.
- **c. 5600 BC** — Occupation shifts to the Western Mound (Early Chalcolithic period).
- **1958** — Site discovered by James Mellaart during a regional survey.
- **1961–1965** — First major excavations led by Mellaart.
- **1993** — Excavations resume under Ian Hodder.
- **2012** — Inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2018** — Hodder-led excavations conclude.
- **2023** — A museum opens on the site.
- **2025** — Discovery of the "House of the Dead" mortuary structure announced.

## Body

### Archaeology and Excavation
The site consists of two mounds: the larger Eastern Mound (Neolithic) and the smaller Western Mound (Chalcolithic). Excavations by James Mellaart (1961–1965) revealed 18 successive building layers, establishing the site's importance. After a hiatus due to Mellaart's professional scandal, Ian Hodder led extensive excavations from 1993 to 2018, applying new digital and reflexive methodologies. Recent work includes the 2025 discovery of a "House of the Dead" with remains of 20 individuals, suggesting ritual use. Key finds include rare preserved textiles, thousands of clay figurines (mostly animal), and obsidian tools indicating trade.

### Settlement and Social Structure
Çatalhöyük was a dense honeycomb of mudbrick houses with no streets; access was via ladders through rooftops, which served as communal spaces. The population is estimated between 600 and 800 people during its Middle phase. The layout suggests a highly communal, egalitarian society with no obvious public buildings or palaces. Recent genetic studies (2025) indicate the society was initially organized along matrilineal lines, with households passing from mother to daughter. Burials within houses, often beneath floors or platforms, show evolving social bonds from close kin to more unrelated individuals over time.

### Culture and Symbolism
The settlement is rich in symbolic expression, with vivid murals and figurines found throughout. Common motifs include hunting scenes, geometric designs, and animals like aurochs and leopards. A famous mural depicting twin peaks (possibly Mount Hasan) has been debated as the world's oldest map. Animal skulls, especially cattle, were mounted on walls. The dead were buried in flexed positions, sometimes with skulls later removed and plastered, a practice with parallels in the Levant. Burial decorations show a sex-based divide, with males associated with red cinnabar and females with blue/green pigments.

### Religion and the "Mother Goddess" Debate
The site is famous for its female figurines, which original excavator Mellaart interpreted as evidence of a dominant "Mother Goddess" cult. However, later excavations under Hodder found that most figurines depict animals, with fewer than 5% being female. Hodder argues there is little evidence for a matriarchal society, stating men and women had equal social status. The interpretation of figurines remains debated, with some, like a unique skeleton-backed female figurine, suggesting complex symbolism related to life and death rather than a simple fertility cult.

### Economy and Subsistence
The economy combined agriculture, animal management, and hunting. Cultivated crops included various wheats, barley, and peas. Evidence shows the domestication and penning of cattle and sheep, with dairy consumption confirmed at the Western Mound. Hunting of wild animals and fishing remained important. Tools were primarily made from locally sourced obsidian, which was also a major trade good exchanged for items like Mediterranean shells and Syrian flint. While often cited as egalitarian, some evidence suggests emerging inequalities in the distribution of certain goods like storage units and quern-stones.

## Terms
- **Tell**: An artificial mound formed by the accumulated debris of long-term human settlement.
- **Neolithic**: The final stage of the Stone Age, characterized by the development of agriculture and permanent settlements.
- **Chalcolithic**: The "Copper Age," a transitional period between the Neolithic and Bronze Age.
- **Matrilineality**: A social system in which descent and inheritance are traced through the mother's line.
- **Intramural inhumation**: Burial of the dead within the boundaries of a settlement, often under house floors.
- **Post-processual archaeology**: A theoretical approach emphasizing the interpretation of archaeological evidence within its cultural and symbolic context, often associated with Ian Hodder.
- **Aurochs**: An extinct species of large wild cattle native to Eurasia.
- **Obsidian**: A volcanic glass used for making sharp tools; a key trade commodity at Çatalhöyük.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Social Organization**: Was Çatalhöyük a matriarchal society centered on a Mother Goddess, as Mellaart proposed, or a more egalitarian community with balanced gender roles, as Hodder's work suggests?
- **Symbolism of Figurines**: What is the true meaning of the abundant figurines? Are they primarily religious idols, teaching tools, or symbols with varied meanings related to life, death, and community?
- **The "Map" Mural**: Does a famous wall painting represent a landscape with Mount Hasan volcano, or is it a decorative design, such as a leopard skin?
- **Economic Equality**: To what extent was the society truly egalitarian? Evidence points to both shared tools and emerging inequalities in wealth transmission.
- **Burial Practices**: What do the changing patterns of burial (from kin-based to more diverse groups) and the removal of skulls reveal about evolving social and ritual life?
