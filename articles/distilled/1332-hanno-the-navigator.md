# Hanno the Navigator

## Overview
Hanno the Navigator was a Carthaginian explorer and probable member of the aristocratic Magonid family who led a major naval expedition down the West African coast in the 5th century BC. His voyage, recorded in a *periplus* (a navigational log or "circumnavigation" account), is the earliest surviving first-person narrative of Atlantic exploration. The expedition aimed to found colonies and secure trade routes—particularly for gold—beyond the Pillars of Hercules (Strait of Gibraltar). The distance reached remains fiercely debated: estimates range from southern Morocco to as far as Gabon, with the text’s vagueness and probable deliberate obfuscation by Carthage to protect trade secrets preventing consensus.

## Timeline
- **5th century BC** — Hanno lives; consensus places his expedition in this century.
- **5th century BC (c.)** — Greek translation of the Punic *periplus* produced, abridged to 101 lines.
- **c. 430 BC** — Herodotus describes Carthaginian trade on the Moroccan coast but likely does not know of Hanno’s specific voyage.
- **c. 86–160 AD** — Arrian summarizes Hanno’s voyage in *Anabasis of Alexander* (Indica), noting a 35-day eastward sail then a difficult turn south.
- **c. 23–79 AD** — Pliny the Elder references the *periplus*, claiming Hanno reached Arabia (considered unrealistic by modern scholars).
- **146 BC** — Roman destruction of Carthage; skins of "Gorillai" kept in Temple of Juno reportedly survive until this date.
- **1559** — Conrad Gessner publishes first Latin translation of the Greek *periplus* in Zurich.
- **1847** — Gorilla species scientifically described and named after the *Gorillai* encountered by Hanno.
- **1955** — Raymond Mauny argues ancient ships could not sail far past Cape Bojador due to winds/currents preventing return.
- **20th–21st century** — Scholarly debate continues on maximum extent: Senegal (consensus minimum), Gambia, Sierra Leone, Cameroon, or Gabon.

## Body

### Identity and Historical Context
Hanno’s exact identity is obscured by the commonness of the name in Carthage. Ancient sources variously call him *basileus* (Greek for "king," but used for high officials), *dux* (leader/general), or *imperator* (commander). Modern consensus identifies him as a 5th-century BC aristocrat of the Magonid family, possibly the son of Hamilcar I. The expedition was a state enterprise: Carthage dispatched 60 ships carrying 30,000 people (though some scholars argue 5,000 is more plausible) to found *Libyophoenician* (Carthaginian) colonies and explore the Atlantic coast of Africa. The primary strategic driver was likely securing the West African gold trade, a foundation of Carthaginian wealth.

### The Periplus: Textual History and Reliability
The *Periplus* is a rare extant exploration account written by the explorer, but the original Punic text is lost. The sole surviving versions are Greek manuscripts (9th and 14th century) translating a 5th-century BC Greek abridgement of 101 lines. This transmission chain introduces contradictions and errors. Scholars argue the Carthaginians deliberately falsified geographic details in the official account to protect trade monopolies: outsiders could not locate the described places, but Carthaginians could still claim the achievement. Furthermore, the narrative ends abruptly after the "Gorillai" encounter without describing the return voyage. Scholar Oikonomides theorizes the Greek translation used an incomplete Punic manuscript, and the final lines were inserted to mask the gap.

### The Voyage: Route and Colonization
The *periplus* summary describes Hanno sailing from Carthage through the Pillars of Hercules, founding or repopulating seven colonies along the Moroccan coast. At the river Lixus, he allied with the Lixitae tribe and established a settlement at the island of Kerne. Pushing south, the fleet encountered increasingly hostile environments—savage men, wild beasts, and unintelligible languages. They sighted an "immense opening of the sea" with fires, a bay called the "Horn of the West," and lands of many flames dominated by a very tall mountain. The voyage terminated at the "Horn of the South" after provisions ran out.

### Geographic Debates: How Far South?
The vagueness of the *periplus* yields distance estimates from under 1,100 km (680 mi) to over 4,800 km (3,000 mi).
*   **Consensus Minimum:** Most agree the expedition reached at least **Senegal** (River Senegal or Cape Verde region).
*   **Southern Morocco Limit:** Raymond Mauny (1955) argued square-sailed ships without stern rudders could not beat the northerly winds and Canary Current to return from south of Cape Bojador (Western Sahara). He places the furthest point at the River Drâa, attributing southern artifacts to later voyages.
*   **Gulf of Guinea Candidates:** Proponents of a farther reach cite the "chariot of the gods" mountain description. **Mount Cameroon** (4,040 m, active volcano with lava entering the sea) matches the "burning heat, streams of lava" (Arrian) and "flames" (*periplus*) better than **Mount Kakoulima** in Guinea (890 m), though Warmington prefers Kakoulima as Cameroon is deemed too distant for the described timeline.
*   **Gabon Hypothesis:** Some analyses suggest the route extended to Gabon, but this remains a minority view due to the immense logistical difficulty of the return journey for oared vessels.

### The Gorillai Encounter
At the "Horn of the South," Hanno found an island inhabited by hairy, savage people his Lixite interpreters called *Gorillai*. Attempts to capture males failed; three females were caught but killed due to ferocity. Their skins were deposited in the Temple of Juno (Tanit/Astarte) in Carthage, where Pliny the Elder claimed they survived until 146 BC. In 1847, the gorilla (*Gorilla gorilla*) was scientifically named after this account, though the *periplus* description (possibly chimpanzees or a mythologized tribe) does not definitively identify the species.

### Ancient Reception
*   **Herodotus (c. 430 BC):** Describes silent barter trade on the Moroccan coast but shows no knowledge of Hanno’s specific voyage.
*   **Arrian (c. 130 AD):** Provides a distinct summary: 35 days sailing east into the "Outer Sea," then a turn south met with water shortage, heat, and lava flows—corroborating the volcanic mountain description.
*   **Pliny the Elder (c. 77 AD):** Claims Hanno circumnavigated Africa to Arabia, a claim modern scholarship rejects as a confusion of Hanno’s voyage with the legendary Phoenician circumnavigation sponsored by Necho II.

### Legacy and Historiography
Hanno’s voyage attracted intense European scholarly interest from the 16th century onward, paralleling the Age of Discovery. The lunar crater *Hanno* and appearances in modern media (e.g., *Civilization VI*) commemorate him. The central historiographical problem remains the *periplus*’s function: was it a genuine log, a propaganda piece, or a deliberately obfuscated trade secret? The lack of archaeological evidence for Mediterranean trade south of Mogador Island (Essaouira) keeps the debate open.

## Terms
- ****Periplus**** — A navigational manuscript listing ports, coastal landmarks, and distances; literally "a sailing around" or circumnavigation account.
- ****Punic**** — The Phoenician dialect spoken in Carthage; the original language of Hanno’s account.
- ****Libyophoenician**** — Term for Carthaginian colonies or citizens of mixed Libyan (North African) and Phoenician descent.
- ****Magonid**** — A dominant aristocratic family in Carthage (6th–4th century BC) to which Hanno likely belonged.
- ****Pillars of Hercules**** — Ancient name for the promontories flanking the Strait of Gibraltar (Calpe/Abila), marking the Mediterranean's western limit.
- ****Lixitae / Lixites**** — A nomadic tribe encountered near the River Lixus (modern Loukkos, Morocco) who acted as interpreters for Hanno.
- ****Kerne**** — An island base established by Hanno; often identified with Herne Island (Dragon Island) near Dakhla or an island in the Bay of Arguin.
- ****Gorillai**** — The name given by Hanno’s interpreters to the hairy, savage people (or apes) encountered at the voyage’s end; etymological source for "gorilla."
- ****Canary Current**** — A southward-flowing ocean current off Northwest Africa; creates a navigational barrier for square-rigged ships attempting a northward return.
- ****Mount Cameroon**** — Active volcano (4,040 m) in Cameroon; candidate for the "chariot of the gods" mountain described by Hanno/Arrian due to lava entering the sea.

## Debates and open questions
1.  **Maximum Southern Latitude:** Did Hanno reach only the Draa River (southern Morocco), the Senegal River, the Gambia, Sierra Leone, Mount Cameroon, or Gabon? The "consensus" of at least Senegal is contested by Mauny’s nautical arguments.
2.  **Mountain Identification:** Does the "chariot of the gods" / "flames and lava" describe Mount Cameroon (volcanic, matches Arrian) or Mount Kakoulima (closer, non-volcanic)?
3.  **Personnel Figures:** Is the figure of 30,000 colonists/crew a scribal error, an exaggeration, or a plausible figure for a mass colonization effort? (Alternative: 5,000).
4.  **Textual Integrity:** Is the *periplus* a good-faith abridgement, a deliberately falsified "trade secret" document, or a translation of an already truncated Punic original?
5.  **Gorillai Identity:** Were the *Gorillai* great apes (gorillas or chimpanzees), a hirsute human tribe, or a mythological construct?
6.  **Return Voyage Feasibility:** How did oared ships overcome the prevailing northerly winds and the Canary Current to return from the Gulf of Guinea? Mauny argues it was nearly impossible without massive crews and provisioning not evidenced in the text.
