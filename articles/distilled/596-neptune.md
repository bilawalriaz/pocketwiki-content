# Neptune

## Overview
Neptune is the eighth and farthest known planet from the Sun, an ice giant whose discovery in 1846 was the first ever predicted mathematically rather than observed directly. Its existence was inferred from gravitational perturbations in Uranus's orbit, leading to independent calculations by John Couch Adams and Urbain Le Verrier, and subsequent telescopic confirmation by Johann Gottfried Galle. Neptune is notable for its dynamic weather, supersonic winds, a tilted and offset magnetic field, a faint ring system, and a large retrograde moon, Triton.

## Timeline
- **c. 1612–1613**: Galileo records positions later identified as Neptune, mistaking it for a star.
- **1821**: Alexis Bouvard publishes Uranus's orbital tables, revealing discrepancies.
- **1843–1846**: John Couch Adams calculates a predicted position for an unknown perturbing planet.
- **1845–1846**: Urbain Le Verrier independently predicts Neptune's position.
- **23 September 1846**: Johann Gottfried Galle observes Neptune within 1° of Le Verrier's prediction.
- **1846**: Triton, Neptune's largest moon, discovered by William Lassell.
- **1984**: Neptune's ring system detected via stellar occultation.
- **25 August 1989**: Voyager 2 conducts the only spacecraft flyby of Neptune.
- **2006**: IAU reclassifies Pluto as a dwarf planet, restoring Neptune as the outermost planet.
- **2018**: New dark storm systems identified on Neptune.
- **2023**: James Webb Space Telescope detects H₃⁺ in Neptune's atmosphere, indicating infrared aurorae.

## Body

### Discovery and Naming
Neptune was discovered through mathematical prediction. After Alexis Bouvard noted anomalies in Uranus's orbit, both John Couch Adams and Urbain Le Verrier independently calculated the position of a perturbing planet. On 23 September 1846, Johann Gottfried Galle observed Neptune near Le Verrier's predicted location, validating the power of celestial mechanics. A nationalistic dispute arose between France and Britain over credit, eventually resolved in favor of joint recognition. Initially called "the planet exterior to Uranus," it was named Neptune after the Roman god of the sea, following a suggestion by Friedrich Bessel and approval by the international community.

### Physical Characteristics and Structure
Neptune is an ice giant with a mass 17.15 times that of Earth and an equatorial radius of 24,764 km. Its atmosphere, composed primarily of hydrogen and helium with traces of methane, gives it a faint blue color due to Rayleigh scattering and methane absorption. The interior consists of a small rocky core, a thick mantle of supercritical "ices" (water, ammonia, methane), and an outer atmosphere. The mantle may contain exotic phases such as ionic and superionic water, and possibly diamond rain formed from methane decomposition under extreme pressure. Neptune radiates 2.61 times more energy than it receives from the Sun, though the source of this internal heat remains debated.

### Rotation and Magnetosphere
Neptune rotates once every 16.11 hours, with an axial tilt of 28.32°, producing 40-year-long seasons. Its atmosphere exhibits differential rotation, with equatorial zones rotating slower than the magnetic field. The magnetic field is highly tilted (47°) and offset from the planet's center by at least 0.55 planetary radii, resembling Uranus's field. This unusual geometry likely arises from dynamo action in a thin shell of electrically conducting fluid in the mantle. The magnetosphere extends far into space, with a bow shock at 34.9 planetary radii and a tail stretching beyond 72 radii.

### Atmosphere and Climate
Neptune's atmosphere is dynamically active, with the strongest sustained winds in the Solar System reaching up to 2,100 km/h. Weather patterns include Great Dark Spots—anticyclonic storms analogous to Jupiter's Great Red Spot—and high-speed "scooter" clouds. The atmosphere is stratified into troposphere, stratosphere, and thermosphere, with complex chemistry driven by solar UV radiation and internal heat. Notably, Neptune's thermosphere reaches anomalously high temperatures (~750 K), possibly due to magnetic field interactions or gravity wave dissipation. The planet's weather activity is influenced by seasonal changes and solar cycles, with high-altitude clouds appearing and disappearing over decades.

### Orbital Motion and Observation
Neptune orbits the Sun at an average distance of 30.1 AU, completing one orbit every 164.8 years. Its orbit is nearly circular (eccentricity 0.0087) and inclined 1.77° relative to Earth. Due to its distance, Neptune appears as a tiny disk (2.2–2.4 arcseconds) and is only visible through telescopes. Ground-based observations improved significantly with adaptive optics in the 1990s, and the Hubble Space Telescope has provided continuous monitoring since the 1990s. Neptune undergoes retrograde motion every 367 days and brightens slightly as it approaches perihelion.

### Satellite System and Rings
Neptune has 16 known moons, dominated by Triton, which accounts for over 99.5% of the system's mass. Triton is unique in having a retrograde orbit, suggesting capture, and is slowly spiraling inward due to tidal forces. Other notable moons include Nereid, with one of the most eccentric orbits in the Solar System, and Proteus, the largest irregularly shaped moon. Neptune's ring system consists of five main components: Galle, Le Verrier, Lassell, Arago, and Adams, with Adams containing prominent arcs maintained by gravitational interactions with the moon Galatea.

### Exploration
Voyager 2 remains the only spacecraft to visit Neptune, conducting a flyby on 25 August 1989. The mission revealed Neptune's active weather, complex magnetic field, and multiple rings. Future exploration includes proposed orbital missions such as Neptune Odyssey and Triton-focused orbiters, as well as China's potential Tianwen-5 mission targeting Neptune by 2058. The James Webb Space Telescope continues to provide new insights into Neptune's atmospheric composition and dynamics.

## Terms
- **Ice giant**: A planet composed primarily of volatile substances heavier than hydrogen and helium, such as water, ammonia, and methane.
- **Supercritical fluid**: A state of matter where distinct liquid and gas phases do not exist, occurring under high temperature and pressure.
- **Differential rotation**: A rotation where different parts of a planet or star rotate at different rates.
- **Dynamo action**: The process by which a celestial body generates a magnetic field through the motion of electrically conductive fluids.
- **Retrograde orbit**: An orbit that moves in the opposite direction to the planet's rotation.
- **Resonance**: A gravitational relationship where two orbiting bodies exert regular, periodic influence on each other.
- **Arcs**: Segments of planetary rings that appear incomplete due to gravitational confinement by nearby moons.
- **Thermosphere**: The upper layer of a planet's atmosphere where temperatures increase with altitude due to absorption of high-energy radiation.
- **Troposphere**: The lowest layer of a planet's atmosphere where weather occurs and temperature decreases with altitude.
- **Lagrangian points**: Positions in space where gravitational forces and orbital motion balance, allowing objects to remain stable relative to two larger bodies.

## Debates and Open Questions
Several unresolved questions surround Neptune. The source of its internal heat, which exceeds solar input by a factor of 2.61, remains unknown, particularly given Uranus's lack of similar heating despite structural similarities. The mechanism behind Neptune's anomalously hot thermosphere (~750 K) is also debated, with candidates including magnetic field interactions and gravity wave dissipation. The stability and evolution of Neptune's ring arcs challenge classical orbital mechanics, requiring ongoing gravitational confinement by Galatea. Additionally, the origin of Neptune's highly tilted and offset magnetic field is uncertain, with hypotheses ranging from unique dynamo processes to early giant impacts. The long-term fate of Triton, which is spiraling inward and will eventually be torn apart at the Roche limit, raises questions about the future evolution of the Neptunian system.
