# Lydia

## Overview
Lydia was an Iron Age kingdom in western Anatolia (modern Turkey) that became a major power in the 7th-6th centuries BC. It is historically significant for its wealthy capital Sardis, its role in regional conflicts with empires like Assyria, Media, and Persia, and its pioneering development of coinage, which revolutionized ancient commerce. After its conquest by Persia in 546 BC, it remained an important province under successive empires.

- **133 BC** — Lydia becomes part of the Roman province of Asia.
- **296 CE** — Lydia is re-established as a separate Roman province under Diocletian.

## Body

### Origins and Early Kingdom
Lydia's early history is obscure, with its territory part of the Late Bronze Age Arzawa polity. The Lydian people appear to have established political cohesion around 800 BC. Literary accounts mention two mythical early dynasties, the house of Atys and the Heraclids, but these lack historical credibility. The first demonstrably historical king is Gyges (c. 680 BC), who founded the Mermnad dynasty.

### The Mermnad Empire (c. 680–546 BC)
Gyges seized power during a period of Cimmerian invasions that destroyed the neighboring Phrygian kingdom. He consolidated Lydia, attacked Greek cities, and allied with Egypt. After his death in a Cimmerian attack in 644 BC, his successors Ardys and Sadyattes faced continued invasions. Alyattes (r. 635–585 BC) finally defeated the Cimmerians with Scythian help, expanded eastward into Phrygia, and fought a five-year war with the Median Empire that ended with the solar eclipse of 585 BC.

Alyattes' son Croesus (r. c. 560–546 BC) expanded the empire to control all western Anatolia. He maintained alliances with Media, Egypt, and Babylon. After the Persian king Cyrus the Great overthrew Croesus's brother-in-law, the Median king Astyages, Croesus attacked Persian territory. Cyrus retaliated, defeating and capturing Sardis by 546 BC, ending Lydian independence.

### Persian, Hellenistic, and Roman Rule
Lydia became a satrapy (province) of the Achaemenid Persian Empire. It later passed to Alexander the Great, then to the Seleucid and Attalid dynasties. In 133 BC, it was bequeathed to Rome, becoming part of the wealthy province of Asia. Under Emperor Diocletian in 296 CE, Lydia was revived as a separate, smaller Roman province. It remained part of the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire until its capture by Turkish beyliks in the 14th-15th centuries.

### Coinage and Economy
The Lydians are credited with inventing coinage. The first coins, minted under Alyattes (c. 591–560 BC), were made of electrum (a natural gold-silver alloy). These early coins, stamped with a lion's head, included denominations like the trite (1/3 stater). Croesus later introduced the first standardized gold and silver coins (the Croeseid) and a bimetallic system. However, early coins were likely too valuable for daily retail, which was later facilitated by small silver fractions from Greek cities.

### Religion and Culture
Early Lydian religion was polytheistic and centered on nature fertility. Its pantheon included native deities like Artimus (a variant of Artemis), the storm god Lews, and the frenzy god Pakiš. The religion showed connections to both Anatolian and Greek traditions. After the Persian conquest, native traditions persisted. Lydia later became an important center for Judaism and early Christianity, with a significant synagogue discovered at Sardis.

## Terms
- **Satrapy**: A province of the ancient Persian Empire, governed by a satrap.
- **Electrum**: A naturally occurring alloy of gold and silver, used in the earliest Lydian coins.
- **Mermnad dynasty**: The ruling dynasty of Lydia from Gyges (c. 680 BC) to Croesus (546 BC).
- **Cimmerians**: A nomadic people from the Pontic steppe who invaded Anatolia in the 7th century BC.
- **Battle of the Eclipse**: A battle in 585 BC between Lydia and Media, halted by a solar eclipse, leading to a peace treaty.
- **Croeseid**: The first standardized gold coin, issued by King Croesus.
- **Trite**: A Lydian coin denomination, approximately one-third of a stater.
- **Artimus**: The chief goddess of the Lydian pantheon, a variant of the Aegean-Balkan nature goddess later associated with Artemis.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact date of the fall of the Lydian kingdom to Persia is debated; traditional dates of 547 BC are questioned, with some scholars suggesting it may have occurred after 539 BC.
- The origin of the Etruscan civilization is a long-standing debate. Herodotus claimed they migrated from Lydia, but Dionysius of Halicarnassus and modern scholarship, including linguistic and genetic studies, overwhelmingly support an indigenous Italian origin, finding no evidence of a Lydian migration.
- The precise dating of the first Lydian electrum coins is highly debated, with estimates ranging from 700 BC to 550 BC.
