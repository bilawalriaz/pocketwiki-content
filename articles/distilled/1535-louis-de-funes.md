# Louis de Funès

## Overview
Louis de Funès (1914–1983) was a French actor and comedian whose high-energy performances, elastic facial expressions, and signature portrayal of authoritarian yet sycophantic *petit bourgeois* characters made him the most bankable star in French cinema history. Though a shy, devout Catholic in private life, his screen persona—defined by hyperactivity, bad faith, and explosive anger—resonated deeply with 1960s Western societies navigating shifting hierarchies. He achieved international fame across Europe, the Eastern Bloc, and the Middle East, yet remained largely unknown in the English-speaking world save for the 1973 release of *The Mad Adventures of Rabbi Jacob*. His decades-long struggle from bit parts to superstardom, largely forged through collaborations with directors Jean Girault and Gérard Oury, culminated in a legacy commemorated by two museums and a namesake rose variety.

## Body

### Early Life and Family Background
Louis de Funès was born in Courbevoie to a Spanish noble family originating from Funes (Basque Counts de Galarza) and a Galician mother of Portuguese descent. His parents eloped to France in 1904 after their families opposed the marriage in conservative Restoration Spain; his father, a former lawyer, became a diamond cutter. Known as "Fufu," de Funès spoke French, Spanish, and English, attended Lycée Condorcet, but dropped out and drifted through menial jobs. He worked as a jazz pianist in Pigalle, where his grimacing amused customers, and studied acting for a year at the Simon school, meeting future star Daniel Gélin. A 1936 marriage to Germaine Carroyer produced a son, Daniel, but ended in divorce in 1942. During the Occupation, he continued piano studies and met secretary Jeanne Barthelémy de Maupassant; they married in 1943 and had two sons, Patrick (later a doctor) and Olivier (an Air France pilot and actor who appeared in several of his father's films).

### Theatrical Career and Film Apprenticeship (1945–1962)
De Funès began in theatre, maintaining stage work even after film fame, culminating in the play *Oscar* (later adapted to film). His film debut came in 1945 via Daniel Gélin's connection: a 40-second porter role in *La Tentation de Barbizon*. For the next 20 years, he performed roughly 130 roles, over 80 as minor parts or extras. He maintained a grueling daily routine: morning dubbing (notably for Italian comic Totò), afternoon film shoots, evening theatre. From 1945–1955, he appeared in 50 films, often uncredited. A turning point arrived in 1956 with a small but noticed role as black-market butcher Jambier in Claude Autant-Lara's *La Traversée de Paris*. Throughout this "apprenticeship," his wife Jeanne and Daniel Gélin encouraged him to overcome his fear of rejection, managing his career through lean years when he considered himself too short, bald, and skinny for leading roles.

### Stardom and the Girault/Oury Collaborations (1963–1974)
Stardom erupted unexpectedly at age 49 with Jean Girault's *Pouic-Pouic* (1963), securing top billing for all subsequent films. Girault cast him as the scheming, sycophantic gendarme Cruchot in *Le gendarme de Saint-Tropez* (1964), launching a six-film series. Simultaneously, Gérard Oury paired him with Bourvil—calm, naive foil to de Funès's hyperactive crank—in *Le Corniaud* (1965) and *La Grande Vadrouille* (1966). The latter drew 17.27 million admissions, remaining the highest-grossing French film ever. The duo's chemistry defined an era; a planned third Oury film, *La Folie des grandeurs* (1971), recast Yves Montand after Bourvil's 1970 death, yet succeeded. De Funès also co-starred with Jean Marais, Mylène Demongeot, Jean Gabin, Fernandel, and Annie Girardot. Between 1964–1979, he topped the French annual box office seven times; in 1968, all three of his releases placed in the year's top ten.

### Style and Persona
De Funès's comic engine was the friction between his diminutive stature (1.64 m / 5 ft 4½ in) and volcanic energy. Nicknamed "the man with forty faces per minute," he specialized in humorously excitable, middle-aged authority figures—petty tyrants who kissed up to superiors while persecuting subordinates. This *petit bourgeois* archetype, rooted in bad faith and uncontrolled rage, resonated with 1960s societal anxieties. His physical comedy—facial contortions, tics, hyperactivity—produced maximal effect when paired with Bourvil's placid goodness. In his later *The Miser* (1980), these traits were muted, percolating beneath a more restrained Harpagon.

### Later Career, Health Decline, and Death
A 1975 heart attack forced cancellation of *Le Crocodile* (where he would have played a dictator). After recovery, he starred in Claude Zidi's *L'Aile ou la cuisse* (1976), a nuanced food-critic role considered among his best, and opposite rising comic Coluche. He fulfilled a longtime dream adapting Molière's *The Miser* in 1980. His final film, *Le Gendarme et les Gendarmettes*, released in 1982. Two heart attacks, attributed to the physical strain of his stage antics, preceded a fatal third on 27 January 1983. Despite a requested private funeral, over 3,000 mourners packed the Saint-Martin church in Le Cellier; he was buried in the Cimetière du Cellier on the Château de Clermont grounds.

### Legacy and Honors
De Funès was made Chevalier of the Légion d'honneur in 1973. A rose variety bears his name, cultivated in the Château de Clermont garden where a monument stands. The French postal service issued a commemorative stamp in 1998. He appears in French comics (*Lucky Luke*, *Clifton*) and inspired the character Skinner in Pixar's *Ratatouille* (2007). Museums opened at his former residence, Château de Clermont (2013), and in Saint-Raphaël (2019), cementing his status as a French cultural icon.

## Terms
- **Petit bourgeois**: A social class comprising small business owners, merchants, and low-level professionals; de Funès's signature characters embodied this class's anxiety, sycophancy toward authority, and tyranny toward subordinates.
- **Sycophant**: A person who acts obsequiously toward someone important to gain advantage; the defining trait of de Funès's gendarme Cruchot and similar roles.
- **Dubbing (post-synchronization)**: The process of replacing original dialogue with translated or corrected audio; de Funès earned steady early income dubbing Italian star Totò for French releases.
- **Walk-on / Extra**: A minor, often non-speaking screen role; de Funès performed over 80 such parts before earning leads.
- **Top billing**: The most prominent credit position, reserved for the star; guaranteed to de Funès after *Pouic-Pouic* (1963).
- **Box-office draw / Bankable actor**: A performer whose name alone guarantees ticket sales; de Funès holds the record in French cinema history.
- **Légion d'honneur (Chevalier)**: France's highest order of merit; de Funès received the knight rank in 1973.
- **Fantômas trilogy**: A 1964–1967 film series where de Funès played Commissioner Juve opposite Jean Marais's master criminal.
- **Gendarmes series**: Six films (1964–1982) directed by Jean Girault starring de Funès as the opportunistic gendarme Cruchot.
- **La Grande Vadrouille (1966)**: Gérard Oury's WWII comedy co-starring Bourvil; highest-grossing French film ever (17.27 million admissions).
