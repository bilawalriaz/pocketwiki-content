# Constantinople

## Overview
Constantinople, modern Istanbul, was the capital of the Roman and Byzantine Empire from 330 to 1453, and then of the Ottoman Empire until 1922. Its strategic location at the crossroads of Europe and Asia made it a pivotal center of trade, culture, and power for over a millennium, shaping the history of the Mediterranean and beyond.

## Timeline
- **c. 657 BC** — Greek colonists from Megara found the city of Byzantium.
- **324** — Emperor Constantine I refounds the city as "New Rome."
- **330** — Constantinople is consecrated as the new capital of the Roman Empire.
- **413-414** — Theodosius II builds the massive Theodosian Walls.
- **532** — The Nika Riots devastate the city; Hagia Sophia is destroyed.
- **537** — Justinian I inaugurates the new Hagia Sophia cathedral.
- **1204** — The Fourth Crusade sacks Constantinople; the Latin Empire is established.
- **1261** — Michael VIII Palaiologos recaptures the city for the Byzantine Empire.
- **1453** — Ottoman Sultan Mehmed II conquers Constantinople after a siege.
- **1922** — The Ottoman sultanate is abolished; the capital moves to Ankara in 1923.

## Body

### Foundation and Early History
The site was first settled as the Greek colony of Byzantium around 657 BC. Its strategic position on the Bosporus made it a prosperous but often contested trading city. In 324, Emperor Constantine I chose it as the site for his new capital, "New Rome," which was ceremonially consecrated in 330. Constantine divided the city into 14 regions and adorned it with public works, including the Great Palace, Hippodrome, and the main street, the Mese. The city's initial administrative status was lower than Rome's, but it grew rapidly.

### The Byzantine Capital and Justinian's Era
Constantinople became the undisputed capital after the fall of the Western Roman Empire in the 5th century. Its most famous fortifications, the Theodosian Walls, were built in 413-414 and proved nearly impregnable for centuries. Under Emperor Justinian I (527-565), the city reached a peak of architectural splendor. After the Nika Riots of 532 destroyed the original Hagia Sophia, Justinian commissioned the current cathedral, a masterpiece of Byzantine architecture. His reign also saw major legal reforms and military campaigns launched from the city. The population may have reached 500,000, but was later devastated by the Plague of Justinian in 541-542.

### Survival, Schism, and Decline
Constantinople withstood major sieges by Avars, Persians, and Arabs (674-678 and 717-718), aided by its walls and the use of Greek fire. The 8th and 9th centuries were marked by the Iconoclast controversy, which caused internal strife and worsened relations with the West. The city's fortunes fluctuated, but it remained a major center. After the Battle of Manzikert in 1071, Seljuk Turks overran Anatolia, the empire's heartland. The Komnenian dynasty (1081-1185) staged a recovery, but growing Italian merchant influence and internal weakness set the stage for disaster.

### The Fourth Crusade and Latin Interregnum
In 1204, the Fourth Crusade diverted to Constantinople. The city was sacked and looted on an unprecedented scale, with its art treasures destroyed or stolen. The Crusaders established the Latin Empire, which lasted until 1261. The city's population and infrastructure declined severely during this period. The Byzantine Empire was fragmented into successor states, the most important being the Empire of Nicaea.

### The Fall and Ottoman Era
After recapture in 1261, the restored Byzantine Empire was a shadow of its former self, controlling little beyond Constantinople and its environs. The city's population dwindled to perhaps 50,000 by 1453. On May 29, 1453, after a seven-week siege, Ottoman Sultan Mehmed II conquered the city using massive cannons. He made it his capital, Kostantiniyye, repopulating it with Muslims, Christians, and Jews. The Hagia Sophia was converted into a mosque. The city remained the Ottoman capital until the empire's dissolution.

## Terms
*   **Byzantium:** The original Greek colony on the site, founded c. 657 BC.
*   **Theodosian Walls:** The massive triple-line fortifications built in the early 5th century that protected the city from landward attack for over a millennium.
*   **Hagia Sophia:** The cathedral of the Ecumenical Patriarch, rebuilt by Justinian I after 532, and a supreme example of Byzantine architecture.
*   **Nika Riots:** A violent 6th-century rebellion in the Hippodrome that led to widespread destruction and the rebuilding of Hagia Sophia.
*   **Greek Fire:** An incendiary weapon used by the Byzantine navy, crucial in defending the city from Arab sieges.
*   **Fourth Crusade:** The 1204 Crusade that sacked Constantinople, leading to the Latin Empire and a permanent schism between Eastern and Western Christianity.
*   **Ecumenical Patriarch:** The spiritual leader of the Eastern Orthodox Church, based in Constantinople.
*   **Mese:** The main colonnaded street of Constantinople, running from the Augustaion to the Golden Gate.
*   **Varangian Guard:** An elite imperial bodyguard unit, primarily of Norse and later Anglo-Saxon warriors, active from the 10th century.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact population of Constantinople at its various peaks (e.g., under Justinian or in the 12th century) is debated, with scholarly estimates varying widely.
*   The precise causes and sequence of events during the Nika Riots are reconstructed from sources with potential biases.
*   The long-term impact of the Iconoclast controversy on the relationship between the Eastern and Western Churches is a subject of ongoing historical analysis.
