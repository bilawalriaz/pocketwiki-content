# Bass guitar

## Overview
The electric bass guitar is the lowest-pitched member of the guitar family, similar in appearance to an electric guitar but with a longer neck and scale length. Since the mid-1950s, it has largely replaced the double bass in popular music due to its lighter weight, smaller size, easier portability, frets for easier intonation, and electromagnetic pickups for amplification. It is a foundational instrument in modern music, requiring external amplification to be heard.

## Timeline
- **1930s** — Paul Tutmarc develops the first modern electric bass guitar, the Audiovox "Model 736 Bass Fiddle."
- **1951** — Fender begins mass-producing the Precision Bass (P-Bass).
- **1953** — Gibson releases the EB-1, the first short-scale violin-shaped electric bass.
- **1953** — Monk Montgomery possibly becomes the first to record with the electric bass.
- **1958** — Gibson releases the hollow-body EB-2 bass.
- **1960** — Fender introduces the Jazz Bass (J-Bass) with an offset waist body.
- **1966** — The first commercial fretless bass guitar, the Ampeg AUB-1, is introduced.
- **1972** — Alembic establishes the "boutique" or high-end custom bass market.
- **1974** — Music Man introduces the StingRay, the first widely produced bass with active electronics.
- **Mid-1970s** — Five-string basses with a low "B" string are introduced.
- **1975** — Anthony Jackson commissions the first six-string bass (low B, high C).

## Body

### Design and Construction
The electric bass guitar typically has four strings, though five-, six-, and seven-string models exist. It is tuned the same as the double bass (E, A, D, G), one octave lower than the four lowest strings of a standard guitar. The scale length—the vibrating string length from nut to bridge—defines its size: 30" (76 cm) or less is short scale, 34" (86 cm) is standard, and 35" (89 cm) is long scale, which is common on 5-string models for better low-B resonance. The instrument is played with fingers, thumb, or a pick.

### Sound Production
The bass is acoustically quiet and requires amplification via electromagnetic or piezo-electric pickups. Pickups convert string vibrations into an electrical signal sent to an amplifier. Strings consist of a steel, nickel, or alloy core wound with smaller-gauge wire. Common winding types—roundwound, flatwound, halfwound, coated, tapewound, and taperwound—significantly affect the instrument's sound and are often chosen for specific musical genres.

### History and Development
The first mass-produced electric bass was the Fender Precision Bass (1951), which revolutionized music by being portable, less prone to feedback, and easier to play in tune due to its frets. Early pioneers included Monk Montgomery, Bill Black, and James Jamerson. Competitors like Gibson (EB-1, 1953) and Höfner (500/1, 1956) soon followed. The 1960s saw an explosion of models, including the Fender Jazz Bass (1960) and the first commercial fretless bass (1966).

In the 1970s, innovation focused on electronics and extended range. Alembic pioneered high-end, custom basses with active electronics (pre-amplification and equalization) for greater tonal control. The Music Man StingRay (1974) was the first widely produced active bass. This era also introduced five- and six-string models, expanding the instrument's range.

## Terms
- **Scale length:** The length of the vibrating string between the nut and bridge saddles, determining the instrument's size and string tension.
- **Pickup:** A device attached to the body that converts string vibrations into an electrical signal for amplification.
- **Active electronics:** Onboard powered circuitry (pre-amplifier and equalizer) that boosts the signal and allows for greater tonal control.
- **Fretless bass:** A bass guitar whose neck lacks frets, allowing for smooth glissando and a tone closer to the double bass.
- **Roundwound/Flatwound:** Common string winding types with circular and rounded-square cross-sections, respectively, producing different tonal characteristics.
- **Neck-through-body:** A construction method where the neck wood extends through the entire body, often associated with enhanced sustain.
- **Short/Long scale:** Refers to the scale length; long scale (34"+) is standard, while short scale (<32") offers easier playability.
