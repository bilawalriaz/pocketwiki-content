# Draco (legislator)

## Overview
Draco was an ancient Athenian legislator, traditionally active c. 625–600 BC, who created Athens' first written legal code. His laws replaced a system of oral tradition and blood feud with a publicly posted code, but were infamous for their extreme harshness, establishing the death penalty for most offenses. While most of his laws were later repealed, his homicide law remained in effect, and his name gave rise to the modern term "draconian" for severe rules.

## Timeline
- **c. 621/620 BC** — Draco establishes his legal code during the archonship of Aristaechmus.
- **Early 6th century BC** — Solon repeals all of Draco's laws except those concerning homicide.
- **409/8 BC** — An inscription is made preserving part of Draco's homicide law.
- **18th century onward** — The adjective "draconian" enters English to describe severe laws.

## Body

### The Draconian Constitution
Draco's laws (θεσμοί, *thesmoi*) were the first written constitution of Athens. To ensure public knowledge, they were inscribed on wooden tablets called axones (ἄξονες) and later on four-sided pyramidal steles called kyrbeis (κύρβεις). The code's major innovations included making all laws public and written, distinguishing between murder and involuntary homicide, and establishing a harsh, uniform penalty structure. The death penalty was prescribed for even minor offenses, such as stealing a cabbage. According to tradition, Draco justified this by stating he had no greater punishment for more serious crimes.

### Homicide Law and Legacy
Draco's homicide law was the only part of his code not repealed by the reformer Solon. In 409 BC, Athenians decided to revise their laws, and a fragmentary inscription from that year preserves part of Draco's homicide law. It states that the victim's relatives must prosecute a killer. The preserved text suggests that unintentional homicide was punished by exile, but the punishment for intentional homicide is unclear from the inscription. Draco's legacy is primarily linguistic; the adjective "draconian" (δρακόντειος, *drakónteios*), used in English since 1777, denotes excessively severe legislation or punishment.

### Historical Authenticity and the Council
Scholars debate Draco's historicity. The 10th-century *Suda* records a folkloric story of his death by suffocation from gifts thrown by supporters. Some, like Karl Julius Beloch, have hypothesized he was not a person but a personification of a sacred serpent (*drakon*), with laws issued in its name. However, most scholars believe he was a real lawgiver. Aristotle attributes to Draco the introduction of the Council of Four Hundred, a body chosen by lot, and the extension of the franchise to all free men who could provide military equipment. This claim is considered by some modern scholars, like Welwei (1998), to be untrue.

## Terms
*   **Draconian:** An adjective derived from Draco's name, meaning excessively harsh, severe, or cruel, especially regarding laws or punishments.
*   **Axones (ἄξονες):** Wooden tablets on which Draco's laws were first inscribed, possibly designed to pivot for reading.
*   **Kyrbeis (κύρβεις):** Four-sided pyramidal steles that replaced the axones to preserve Draco's laws.
*   **Thesmoi (θεσμοί):** The Greek term for the laws or ordinances established by Draco.
*   **Archon:** A chief magistrate in ancient Athens; Draco's code was established during the archonship of Aristaechmus.
*   **Areopagus:** A council in Athens that served as a guardian of the laws and a court of appeal.
*   **Council of Four Hundred:** A governing body introduced by Draco, chosen by lot, distinct from the Areopagus.
*   **Mina:** A unit of weight and currency used in ancient Greece, used to define property qualifications for office.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Historicity:** Was Draco a real historical figure, or was he a later invention? Some scholars, like Karl Julius Beloch, propose he was a personification of a sacred serpent, with laws attributed to its priests.
*   **Scope of Laws:** While his homicide law is partially known, the specific content and penalties of Draco's other laws are entirely unknown.
*   **Homicide Law Details:** The preserved inscription of the homicide law is fragmentary and ambiguous. It is unclear whether it specified a punishment for intentional homicide or if the exile penalty applied only to unintentional cases.
*   **Political Reforms:** Aristotle's claim that Draco extended the franchise and created the Council of Four Hundred is not based on authentic tradition and is disputed by modern scholarship.
