# Roman Polanski

## Overview
Roman Polanski (born 18 August 1933) is a Polish-French filmmaker whose career spans over six decades and 23 feature films, earning him an Academy Award for Best Director (*The Pianist*, 2002), a Palme d'Or, a Golden Bear, ten César Awards, and three BAFTAs. His work is defined by psychological tension, claustrophobic atmospheres, and themes of persecution and alienation—sensibilities forged by surviving the Holocaust as a child in the Kraków Ghetto and the 1969 murder of his pregnant wife, Sharon Tate, by the Manson Family. In 1977, Polanski was arrested in the U.S. for drugging and raping a 13-year-old girl; he pleaded guilty to unlawful sex with a minor but fled to Europe in 1978 before sentencing, fearing the judge would renege on a plea bargain. He remains a fugitive from U.S. justice, protected by French citizenship, and has faced subsequent allegations from multiple women. This legal status has restricted his movements, complicated financing, and led to his 2018 expulsion from the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences, yet he has continued directing internationally, most recently *The Palace* (2023).

## Body

### Early Life and the Holocaust
Born in interbellum Paris, Polanski moved with his family to Kraków in 1937. Following the 1939 Nazi invasion, the family was forced into the Kraków Ghetto. His mother, four months pregnant, was deported to Auschwitz and murdered in the gas chamber; his father was sent to Mauthausen. Polanski escaped the ghetto in 1943, surviving by assuming a false Catholic identity with the help of Polish Catholics, including Stefania and Jan Buchala (later honored as Righteous Among the Nations). He witnessed extreme violence, including being used for target practice by German soldiers. Biographers link these childhood traumas—constant fear, confinement, and the loss of his mother—to the palpable dread and "tangible atmospheres" of his later films. After the war, he reunited with his father in Kraków, living briefly in a primitive village without electricity before pursuing cinema.

### Polish Beginnings and International Breakthrough (1955–1968)
Polanski graduated from the National Film School in Łódź (1959), where shorts like *Two Men and a Wardrobe* (1958) gained recognition. His feature debut, *Knife in the Water* (1962), scripted with Jerzy Skolimowski, was a rare post-war Polish film without a war theme; its Western success and Oscar nomination launched his international reputation. Finding the French industry xenophobic, he moved to the UK, directing three films with collaborator Gérard Brach: *Repulsion* (1965), a psychological horror starring Catherine Deneuve influenced by Buñuel and Hitchcock; *Cul-de-sac* (1966), a Beckettian tragicomedy; and *The Fearless Vampire Killers* (1967), a color Panavision vampire parody shot in fairy-tale landscapes evoking Marc Chagall. On the latter, he met Sharon Tate; they married in January 1968.

### Hollywood Peak and Tragedy (1968–1977)
Paramount brought Polanski to the U.S. for *Rosemary's Baby* (1968), which he adapted from Ira Levin’s novel in three weeks. The film’s box-office success cemented his status as a major commercial director. In August 1969, while Polanski worked in London, the pregnant Tate and four friends were murdered by the Manson Family. Polanski called his absence that night his "greatest regret," stating the event pivoted his personality from optimism to "ingrained pessimism." He channeled this into *Macbeth* (1971), a graphically violent adaptation funded by Hugh Hefner, and *Chinatown* (1974), a neo-noir masterpiece inspired by the California Water Wars, nominated for 11 Oscars and later preserved in the National Film Registry. *The Tenant* (1976), in which Polanski starred as a Polish immigrant in Paris, completed the loose "Apartment Trilogy" exploring alienation and psychic breakdown.

### Exile and European Period (1978–2001)
After fleeing the U.S. in 1978, Polanski worked exclusively in Europe. *Tess* (1979), dedicated to Tate and starring Nastassja Kinski, became the most expensive French film to date, winning three Oscars and Césars for Best Picture/Director. *Pirates* (1986), an homage to Errol Flynn swashbucklers, was a critical and financial failure. *Frantic* (1988), a Hitchcockian thriller starring Harrison Ford and future wife Emmanuelle Seigner, failed commercially but earned critical praise. *Bitter Moon* (1992) and *Death and the Maiden* (1994, from Ariel Dorfman’s play) explored dark psycho-sexual dynamics. *The Ninth Gate* (1999) with Johnny Depp returned to occult thriller territory.

### Late Career Resurgence (2002–Present)
*The Pianist* (2002), adapting Władysław Szpilman’s Holocaust memoir, resonated deeply with Polanski’s own history. It won the Palme d'Or at Cannes and the Academy Award for Best Director (accepted by Harrison Ford). Subsequent films include *Oliver Twist* (2005), *The Ghost Writer* (2010—a political thriller winning six European Film Awards), *Carnage* (2011, from Yasmina Reza’s play), *Venus in Fur* (2013, French-language), *Based on a True Story* (2017), and *An Officer and a Spy* (2019, on the Dreyfus affair), which won the Venice Grand Jury Prize and a record fifth César for Best Director. *The Palace* (2023), a black comedy set on New Year's Eve 1999, premiered at Venice amid protests regarding Polanski’s inclusion alongside Woody Allen and Luc Besson; financing shifted to Italian company Rai Cinema after French sources withdrew.

### Legal History and Allegations
**1977 Arrest and Flight:** Polanski was arrested for the sexual assault of 13-year-old Samantha Gailey (later Geimer). He pleaded guilty to "unlawful sexual intercourse with a minor" under a plea bargain for probation. After serving 42 days of a 90-day psychiatric evaluation, he learned Judge Laurence J. Rittenband intended to impose a 50-year sentence. Polanski fled to France on 31 January 1978. As a French citizen, he is protected from extradition.

**Subsequent Legal Battles:** Geimer sued in 1988; a confidential settlement was completed by 1997. Geimer has since publicly opposed further prosecution. In 2009, Polanski was arrested in Switzerland on a U.S. request; after two months' jail and house arrest, the Swiss rejected extradition in 2010. Polish courts rejected U.S. extradition requests in 2015 and 2016, ruling he had served his punishment. An Interpol Red Notice (1978) restricted travel to France, Switzerland, and Poland, though his name was later removed from Interpol's public list.

**Additional Allegations:** Since 2010, multiple women have accused Polanski of assaults dating from the 1970s: Charlotte Lewis (1983, age 16), Renate Langer (1972, age 15), Marianne Barnard (1975, age 10), Valentine Monnier (1975, age 18), and a 2023 civil suit (settled 2024) alleging a 1973 rape. Polanski denies all, often attributing renewed scrutiny to Harvey Weinstein’s influence. He was acquitted of defaming Lewis in France in May 2024.

**Institutional Consequences:** Expelled from the Academy in May 2018 under revised Standards of Conduct post-#MeToo; a 2020 lawsuit for reinstatement failed. His wife Emmanuelle Seigner declined an Academy invitation in protest. The 2020 César win for *An Officer and a Spy* sparked protests, walkouts, and tear gas clashes outside the ceremony.

## Terms
- ****Kraków Ghetto**** — A sealed district in Nazi-occupied Kraków where Jews were forcibly confined; site of Polanski's childhood imprisonment and his mother's deportation to Auschwitz.
- ****Mauthausen**** — A network of 49 German concentration camps in Austria where Polanski's father was imprisoned; he survived.
- ****Righteous Among the Nations**** — An honorific from Yad Vashem (Israel's Holocaust memorial) for non-Jews who risked their lives to save Jews; awarded to Stefania and Jan Buchala, who sheltered Polanski.
- ****Plea Bargain**** — An agreement where a defendant pleads guilty to a reduced charge; Polanski accepted "unlawful sex with a minor" expecting probation, but fled when the judge signaled rejection.
- ****Interpol Red Notice**** — A request to law enforcement worldwide to locate and provisionally arrest a person pending extradition; issued for Polanski in 1978, limiting his travel for decades.
- ****Apartment Trilogy**** — Critical grouping of *Repulsion* (1965), *Rosemary's Baby* (1968), and *The Tenant* (1976) linked by themes of isolation, paranoia, and psychological disintegration within domestic spaces.
- ****California Water Wars**** — Early 20th-century disputes over Los Angeles water rights; historical basis for *Chinatown*'s plot.
- ****Dreyfus Affair**** — 1894–1906 French political scandal where Jewish officer Alfred Dreyfus was wrongly convicted of treason; subject of *An Officer and a Spy* (2019).
- ****Palme d'Or**** — Highest prize at the Cannes Film Festival; won by Polanski for *The Pianist* (2002).
- ****César Award**** — French national film award; Polanski holds the record for most Best Director wins (five: *Tess*, *The Pianist*, *The Ghost Writer*, *Venus in Fur*, *An Officer and a Spy*).

## Debates and open questions
- **Art vs. Artist:** The central debate concerns whether Polanski’s cinematic achievements can or should be separated from his crimes and fugitive status. Festival directors (e.g., Venice’s Alberto Barbera) argue for distinguishing the "responsibilities of the man and those of the artist," while protesters and organizations (#MeToo, Time’s Up) contend that honoring him normalizes impunity for sexual violence.
- **Judicial Misconduct vs. Flight:** The 2008 documentary *Roman Polanski: Wanted and Desired* alleged Judge Rittenband engaged in ex parte communications and reneged on the plea deal. A 2009 California appellate court acknowledged "considerable evidence" of misconduct but ruled flight was not Polanski’s only legal remedy. The victim, Samantha Geimer, has consistently argued the legal process harmed her more than the assault and supports dismissal.
- **Extradition Sovereignty:** France and Poland have refused U.S. extradition requests (Poland’s Supreme Court dismissed the government’s appeal in 2016), citing dual citizenship, time elapsed, and procedural fairness. This raises questions about international legal cooperation for historic sex crimes.
- **Victim’s Agency:** Geimer’s public forgiveness and opposition to prosecution clash with legal principles that crimes against minors are offenses against the state, not solely the victim. Her 2023 meeting with Polanski and statement that he "served his sentence" complicates public discourse.
- **Cultural Boycott:** The 2023 Venice inclusion of Polanski, Woody Allen, and Luc Besson intensified debate over whether institutions should platform artists facing credible abuse allegations, balancing presumption of innocence (in new allegations) against admitted guilt (1977 case).
