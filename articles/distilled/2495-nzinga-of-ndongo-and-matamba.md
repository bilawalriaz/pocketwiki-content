# Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba

## Overview
Nzinga (c. 1583–1663) was a paramount ruler of the Ambundu kingdoms of Ndongo and Matamba in present-day northern Angola. She is a major historical figure in Atlantic Creole culture, remembered for her intelligence, political and diplomatic wisdom, and military tactics during a period of rapid growth in the African slave trade and Portuguese imperial encroachment.

## Timeline
- **c. 1583** — Nzinga is born into the royal family of Ndongo.
- **1617** — Her brother Mbandi becomes king, orders her son killed and her sterilized.
- **1621** — Nzinga is sent as an ambassador to the Portuguese in Luanda, negotiates a peace treaty, and is baptized.
- **1624** — After her brother's death, she assumes power as Ngola (king) of Ndongo.
- **1626** — Portugal declares war on Ndongo after fomenting rebellion.
- **1628** — Nzinga's army is severely depleted; she goes into exile.
- **1631–1635** — She conquers the neighboring Kingdom of Matamba.
- **1641** — She forms an alliance with the Dutch West India Company, which has captured Luanda.
- **1648** — The Portuguese recapture Luanda; the Dutch leave Angola.
- **1656** — A peace treaty is signed between Nzinga and Portugal.
- **17 December 1663** — Nzinga dies; her sister Kambu succeeds her.

## Body

### Early Life and Political Background
Nzinga was born around 1583, the daughter of Ngola Kilombo of Ndongo. Her name derives from the Kimbundu verb *kujinga* ("to twist"), as the umbilical cord was wrapped around her neck at birth. As a child, she received military and political training, displaying aptitude with a battle axe and participating in governance duties. She was also taught to read and write Portuguese by missionaries.

During her youth, Ndongo faced crises from Portuguese encroachment and Imbangala (fierce warrior bands) invasions. The Portuguese established a trading post in Luanda in 1575 and waged brutal wars, seizing territory and slaves. By the time Nzinga's father became king in 1593, the kingdom's power was greatly diminished.

### Rise to Power and Early Wars
In 1621, Nzinga's brother Mbandi sent her as an ambassador to negotiate with the Portuguese. She secured a peace treaty and was baptized as Ana de Sousa. After Mbandi's death in 1624, she became Ngola of Ndongo, facing opposition from male claimants who argued a woman could not rule. She consolidated power by eliminating rivals and strategically using her royal lineage.

Her treaty with the Portuguese collapsed as they reneged on terms and supported a rival king, Hari. In 1626, Portugal declared war. After military defeats, Nzinga was expelled from Ndongo by 1628.

### Exile, Alliance, and Conquest of Matamba
In exile, Nzinga married the Imbangala warlord Kasanje to gain military support. She adopted Imbangala customs, combining them with her Mbundu heritage to create a new, capable army. Between 1631 and 1635, she conquered the Kingdom of Matamba, which had a tradition of female leadership, providing a more stable power base. She used Matamba to finance her wars through the slave trade.

### Alliance with the Dutch and Later Campaigns
In 1641, Nzinga allied with the Dutch, who had seized Luanda. This allowed her to reclaim large parts of Ndongo until the Portuguese recaptured Luanda in 1648. She continued fighting the Portuguese until a peace treaty was signed in 1656. The treaty ceded coastal lands to Portugal but recognized Nzinga as ruler, granted her a slave trade monopoly, and secured the release of her sister.

### Later Years and Legacy
In her final years, Nzinga rebuilt Matamba, reformed its legal code, and pursued Christian diplomacy, corresponding with Pope Alexander VII. She ensured a smooth succession by appointing her sister Kambu as heir. She died on 17 December 1663. Her legacy as a symbol of resistance and leadership endures in Angola and beyond.

## Terms
- **Ngola**: The title for the ruler of Ndongo, etymological root of "Angola."
- **Imbangala**: Tribal bands of warriors known for ferocity and religious fervor, who formed warbands and were sometimes hired as mercenaries.
- **Sobas**: Ndongan noblemen who provided tribute (provisions, soldiers, slaves) to the king.
- **Kijikos**: A servile caste of slaves traditionally owned by Ndongan royalty.
- **Lunga**: A large bell used by Ndongan war captains as a symbol of power.
- **Kilombo**: A war camp, specifically the large camp Nzinga built with 80,000 members.
- **Maji a samba**: An Imbangala initiation rite involving anointing with oil made from a crushed infant.
- **Atlantic Creole culture**: A cultural synthesis emerging from interactions among Africans, Europeans, and others in the Atlantic world.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Succession legitimacy**: Nzinga's right to rule was contested because she and her brother were children of slave wives, not the first wife, and because she was female. She countered by emphasizing her direct bloodline.
- **Imbangala practices**: Sources disagree on the intricacies and extent of Imbangala rites and laws (*ijila*), including the extent of Nzinga's participation in rituals like the *maji a samba*.
- **Portuguese portrayal**: Early Portuguese accounts, like those of Capuchin priests Cavazzi and Gaeta, praised her skills but included pejorative statements (e.g., cannibalism) without evidence, shaping later Western depictions.
- **Historical portrayal**: Modern historians debate whether to view Nzinga as a populist hero or a tyrant, with Linda Heywood cautioning against simplistic labels and emphasizing her use of culture, diplomacy, and war to secure her kingdom.
