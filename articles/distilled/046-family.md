# Family

## Overview
The family is a group of people related by consanguinity (recognized birth) or affinity (marriage or other relationship), forming the basis for social order. Historically, most human societies use the family as the primary purpose of attachment, nurturance, and socialization. Anthropologists classify family organizations as matrifocal, patrifocal, conjugal (nuclear), avuncular, or extended. The family is also an important economic unit, and the field of genealogy traces family lineages through history.

- **1884** — Friedrich Engels publishes *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State*, expanding Morgan's economic hypothesis.
- **1948** — UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights declares the family "the natural and fundamental group unit of society."
- **1965–1995** — Divorce rates climb drastically, increasing the number of single-parent families.
- **1970–2011** — Divorce legalized in predominantly Roman Catholic countries: Italy (1970), Portugal (1975), Brazil (1977), Spain (1981), Argentina (1987), Ireland (1996), Chile (2004), Malta (2011).
- **1978** — Council of Europe Convention on Legal Status of Children Born out of Wedlock comes into force.
- **1978–2015** — China's one-child policy in place.
- **1980** — Multigenerational family living in the US reaches a low point (about 1 in 8 people).
- **1985** — Swiss referendum guarantees women legal equality within marriage (in force January 1988).
- **2016** — One in five people in the US lives in a multigenerational family.

## Body

### Social functions and size
One primary function of the family is providing a framework for the production and reproduction of persons biologically and socially, through sharing food, care and nurture (nurture kinship), jural rights, and moral ties. From a child's perspective, the family is a "family of orientation" that locates children socially and aids enculturation; from a parent's perspective, it is a "family of procreation" aimed at producing and socializing children. In societies with a sexual division of labor, marriage is necessary for an economically productive household. C. C. Harris notes the western conception of "family" is ambiguous and confused with "household"; Olivia Harris argues this confusion reflects capitalist familial ideology that insists nuclear family members live together. Total fertility rate varies from 6.76 children per woman in Niger to 0.81 in Singapore (as of 2015); fertility is below replacement in all Eastern and Southern European countries and particularly high in Sub-Saharan Africa.

### Types of families
Historically, the most common family type was multigenerational—grandparents, parents, and children living together. In the US this declined after World War II, reaching a low in 1980, then rising to one in five people by 2016, driven by demographic changes and the Boomerang Generation. In Canada, about 6% lived in multigenerational families as of 2016, with numbers rising due to Aboriginal and immigrant families and high housing costs. A conjugal (nuclear) family includes only spouses and unmarried minor children; some sociologists distinguish conjugal families (relatively independent of kindred) from nuclear families (closer ties to kindred). A single-parent family consists of one parent (widowed, divorced, or never married) with children; shared-parenting arrangements may improve children's well-being compared to sole custody. A matrifocal family centers on a mother and her children, with fathers intermittently present; the term was coined in Guiana. Extended family has two meanings: a synonym for consanguineal family, or "kindred" (an egocentric network beyond the domestic group). Family of choice (or "found family") is common in LGBT communities, among veterans, abuse survivors, and those without contact with biological parents; it differentiates from "family of origin." Blended families (stepfamilies) involve remarried parents bringing children from former families; most US households are now non-traditional under definitions requiring a breadwinner father and stay-at-home mother. Monogamous families allow one official partner at a time (serial monogamy permitted), prohibited from bigamy. Polygamous families include polygyny (one man, multiple wives), polyandry (one woman, multiple husbands), and group marriage; polygyny is practiced mainly in parts of the Middle East and Africa, while polyandry was traditionally practiced in Himalayan areas.

### Kinship terminology
Lewis Henry Morgan (1818–1881) performed the first survey of kinship terminologies in *Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity of the Human Family*, distinguishing classificatory systems (not distinguishing lineal and collateral relationships) from descriptive systems (which do). He identified six patterns: Hawaiian (only sex and generation), Sudanese (no two relatives share a term), Eskimo (also distinguishes lineal from collateral), Iroquois (also distinguishes siblings of opposite sexes in the parental generation), Crow (matrilineal with "skewing"), and Omaha (patrilineal, like Crow). Most Western societies employ Eskimo terminology, which emphasizes the nuclear family and assumes the mother's husband is the biological father. Collateral relatives include siblings, aunts/uncles, nieces/nephews, and cousins, distinguished by degrees of collaterality and generation (removal). Aggregate relatives (by marriage) use "-in-law" tags; Indian English uses "co-sibling" for a sibling-in-law who is the spouse of one's sibling.

### Descent systems
Patrilineality (agnatic kinship) traces family membership through the father's lineage, involving inheritance of property, rights, names, or titles through male kin. Matrilineality traces membership through the mother's lineage, contrasting with the more common patrilineal pattern. Bilateral descent traces through both paternal and maternal sides equally; it is found among some groups in West Africa, India, Australia, Indonesia, Melanesia, Malaysia, and Polynesia, and may help members survive in extreme environments by relying on two dispersed family sets.

### History of theories
Early scholars applied Darwin's biological evolution to family systems. Morgan's *Ancient Society* (1877) inspired Engels' *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884), which argued economic factors transformed primitive community into class-divided society; this theory dominated until the 1980s, when structural functionalism gained acceptance. Contemporary society views the family as a haven from the world, but Zinn and Eitzen argue the protective image has waned—today the family is "more compensatory than protective." Unhappily married couples are at 3–25 times the risk of clinical depression. Scholars dispute claims of a "golden age" of family, noting desertion and illegitimate children existed in the past. In the postmodern family, married couples have dropped below half of all American households; marriages are increasingly based on love rather than economic or social gain. Many countries have reformed family laws to accommodate diverse models, including the UK's Family Law (Scotland) Act 2006 and Ireland's Civil Partnership Act 2010.

### Domestic violence and honor
Domestic violence (DV) is violence within the family, defined differently across medical, legal, political, and social contexts. The Council of Europe defines it as physical, sexual, psychological, or economic violence within the family or between current/former spouses. The 1993 UN Declaration identified DV as one of three contexts of violence against women. Family violence is broader, including child abuse, elder abuse, and other violent acts. The WHO defines child maltreatment as physical and emotional ill-treatment, sexual abuse, neglect, and exploitation; five subtypes are physical abuse, sexual abuse, neglect, emotional abuse, and exploitation. Elder abuse is "a single, or repeated act, or lack of appropriate action, occurring within any relationship where there is an expectation of trust which causes harm or distress to an older person." Abuse of parents by children is common but under-reported. Forced and child marriages, practiced particularly in Asia and Africa, are associated with high rates of domestic violence. Family honor can lead to honor killings—homicide of a family member believed to have brought shame, for reasons such as refusing an arranged marriage, disapproved relationships, sex outside marriage, or rape.

### Economic and legal issues
Families often form a sharing economy with common ownership. Dowry is property a wife or her family gives to the husband; bride price is paid by the groom's family to the bride's parents; dower is property given to the bride herself, remaining under her control. Some countries offer married couples taxation advantages; property regimes include community property (each owns half at divorce). The right to family is enshrined in the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Reproductive rights include deciding the number, timing, and spacing of children; states have used coercive methods such as bans on contraception or China's one-child policy. Legal reforms have addressed gender equality (abolishing coverture), divorce, and children born outside marriage. The Philippines still does not allow divorce.

### Health, politics, and balance
Family medicine is a specialty devoted to comprehensive care based on knowledge of the patient in family and community context. Maternal mortality is defined by WHO as death during pregnancy or within 42 days of termination; it has dropped dramatically in Western countries but remains serious in Africa and Asia. Infant mortality (death before age one) and child mortality (before age five) have decreased significantly in modern times. Some governments promote male-dominated family structures—the Civil Code of Iran states the husband is exclusively head of the family—while others promote gender-equal policies. Work–family balance involves prioritizing between work and family, reflected in maternity and paternity leave policies. Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights protects private and family life, subject to restrictions "necessary in a democratic society."

### Criticism and social justice
Ayn Rand compared partiality toward consanguinity with racism, calling the worship of family "a crudely primitive first installment on the worship of the tribe," and advocated a childfree lifestyle. Social justice in family relations is controversial; John Stuart Mill's *The Subjection of Women* was a notable exception advocating women's rights within marriage. Second-wave feminists argued "the personal is political," challenging the nuclear family and arguing state reluctance to intervene in domestic violence violates women's human rights. Global trends include over 40% of US babies born outside marriage in 2013, and rising childlessness in Europe and Japan; however, Swedish statistics from 2013 showed fewer children experiencing parental separation and increased marriages, suggesting the nuclear family was no longer in decline there.

## Terms
- **Consanguinity**: Relation by recognized birth or "blood."
- **Affinity**: Relation by marriage or other relationship.
- **Matrifocal family**: A family centered on a mother and her children, with fathers intermittently present.
- **Nuclear family**: A conjugal family of spouses and unmarried minor children.
- **Enculturation**: The process by which children learn their culture's social norms.
- **Classificatory kinship**: Systems that do not distinguish lineal and collateral relationships.
- **Descriptive kinship**: Systems that do distinguish lineal and collateral relationships.
- **Patrilineality**: Tracing family membership through the father's lineage.
- **Matrilineality**: Tracing family membership through the mother's lineage.
- **Bilateral descent**: Tracing family membership through both parents equally.
- **Domestic violence**: Violence within the family, defined variably by context.
- **Dowry**: Property a wife or her family gives to the husband at marriage.
- **Bride price**: Property paid by the groom's family to the bride's parents.
- **Dower**: Property given to the bride herself, remaining under her control.

## Debates and open questions
- Whether the western conception of "family" is meaningfully distinct from "household" (C. C. Harris) or whether the confusion reflects capitalist ideology (Olivia Harris).
- Whether family structures of the past were superior to today's; Zinn and Eitzen argue "there is no golden age of the family."
- Whether the family is "declining" depends on one's definition of "family"; married couples have dropped below half of US households.
- Whether kinship is universally based on "blood" or better understood through living together, sharing food, and care (recent anthropological arguments).
- Whether the nuclear family is in decline globally; Swedish data since the 2000s suggest it may no longer be declining there.
