# John A. Macdonald

## Overview
Sir John A. Macdonald (1815–1891) was the first prime minister of Canada and the dominant figure of Canadian Confederation. He served as prime minister for nearly 19 years, guiding the new nation through its formative decades by building a national government, a transcontinental railway, and a protective economic policy. His legacy is complex, marked by foundational nation-building alongside controversial policies toward Indigenous peoples and Chinese immigrants.

## Timeline
- **1815** — Born in Glasgow, Scotland; family immigrates to Kingston, Upper Canada in 1820.
- **1844** — Elected to the legislature of the Province of Canada for Kingston.
- **1857** — Becomes premier of the Province of Canada.
- **1864** — Forms the Great Coalition with rival George Brown to pursue federation.
- **1867** — Becomes first prime minister of the Dominion of Canada on July 1.
- **1870** — Annexes Rupert's Land and the North-Western Territory; Manitoba becomes a province.
- **1871** — British Columbia joins Confederation, with a promise of a transcontinental railway.
- **1873** — Resigns as prime minister due to the Pacific Scandal.
- **1878** — Returns to power, implementing the National Policy tariff and railway construction.
- **1885** — North-West Rebellion led by Louis Riel is suppressed; Riel is executed.
- **1885** — Canadian Pacific Railway is completed.
- **1891** — Dies in office on June 6 after serving a sixth majority government.

## Body

### Early Life and Legal Career
Born in Scotland, Macdonald immigrated to Kingston, Upper Canada, in 1820. After leaving school at 15, he was articled to a lawyer and called to the Bar in 1836. He built a prominent legal practice in Kingston, taking on high-profile cases and becoming a public figure. He served briefly in the militia during the Rebellions of 1837.

### Rise to Colonial Leadership
Macdonald entered politics in 1844, representing Kingston. He became a key figure in the unstable political system of the Province of Canada, serving as premier from 1857. The political deadlock between Canada West and Canada East, combined with fears of American expansionism following the Civil War, created a crisis. In 1864, Macdonald agreed to a Great Coalition with his rival George Brown to pursue federal union.

### Architect of Confederation
Macdonald was a leading figure at the Charlottetown, Quebec, and London Conferences that negotiated the terms of union. The British North America Act, 1867, created the Dominion of Canada, and Macdonald became its first prime minister. His first term focused on consolidating the new nation, including annexing the North-Western Territory and Rupert's Land, and bringing British Columbia and Prince Edward Island into Confederation.

### First Premiership and the Pacific Scandal
As prime minister, Macdonald established the North-West Mounted Police and expanded Canada westward. His government fell in 1873 over the Pacific Scandal, in which his party accepted bribes from businessmen seeking the Canadian Pacific Railway charter. He resigned, and the Liberals took power.

### Return to Power and National Policy
Macdonald returned to office in 1878, championing the National Policy—a program of high protective tariffs to build Canadian industry, rapid construction of the Canadian Pacific Railway, and western agricultural development. The railway, completed in 1885, was a monumental but costly project that nearly bankrupted the private syndicate building it, requiring government loans for completion.

### Controversial Policies and Final Years
Macdonald's later years were marked by the suppression of the North-West Rebellion (1885) and the execution of Métis leader Louis Riel, which alienated many French Canadians. His government implemented the Chinese head tax and established the residential school system for Indigenous children, based on the 1879 Davin Report advocating "aggressive assimilation." He won three more majority elections before dying in office in 1891.

## Terms
- **Confederation**: The process (1864–1867) by which the British North American colonies federated to form the Dominion of Canada.
- **Great Coalition**: A political alliance (1864) of rival parties in the Province of Canada, led by Macdonald and George Brown, formed to achieve federation and political reform.
- **National Policy**: Macdonald's economic program, implemented from 1879, combining high protective tariffs, western settlement, and transcontinental railway construction.
- **Canadian Pacific Railway (CPR)**: The transcontinental railway completed in 1885, a key promise to British Columbia for joining Confederation and a tool for national unity and western development.
- **Pacific Scandal**: A political corruption scandal (1873) in which Macdonald's Conservative Party accepted bribes from railway financiers, leading to his resignation.
- **North-West Rebellion**: An 1885 uprising led by Louis Riel and the Métis in present-day Saskatchewan, suppressed by the Canadian government.
- **Davin Report**: An 1879 report that recommended the creation of industrial boarding schools for Indigenous children, forming the basis of the Canadian Indian residential school system.
- **Chinese head tax**: A tax imposed on Chinese immigrants to Canada, first enacted in 1885, to discourage Chinese labour after the railway's completion.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historical Legacy**: Macdonald is consistently ranked among Canada's greatest prime ministers for his role in Confederation and nation-building. However, his legacy is heavily contested due to his government's policies toward Indigenous peoples (including the residential school system) and Chinese immigrants. This has led to the removal of his name from prizes and the removal or vandalism of his statues.
- **Execution of Louis Riel**: The decision to execute Riel in 1885 remains deeply divisive, seen by some as necessary to uphold law and order and by others as a politically motivated act that alienated French Canadians and ignored Riel's mental state.
- **Role in Residential Schools**: Macdonald's government initiated the residential school system based on the Davin Report's model of "aggressive assimilation." The Truth and Reconciliation Commission later concluded this system amounted to cultural genocide.
