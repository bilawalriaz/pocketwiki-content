# History of sociology

## Overview
Sociology is the scientific study of society, social institutions, and social relationships. It emerged as a formal discipline in the 19th century, primarily from Enlightenment thought, to analyze the profound changes brought by modernity, capitalism, and secularization. Its development involved a shift from philosophical speculation to empirical research, establishing itself as a key social science for understanding human interaction and social structures.

## Timeline
- **14th century** — Ibn Khaldun writes *Muqaddimah*, advancing early social-scientific reasoning on social cohesion and conflict.
- **1780s** — Emmanuel Joseph Sieyès coins the term "sociologie."
- **1838** — Auguste Comte gives sociology its modern definition, calling it the "queen science."
- **1893** — Émile Durkheim publishes *The Division of Labour in Society*.
- **1895** — Durkheim founds the first European sociology department at the University of Bordeaux.
- **1897** — Durkheim's *Suicide* pioneers modern social research.
- **1905** — The American Sociological Association is founded; Lester Frank Ward is its first president.
- **1919** — Max Weber establishes a sociology department at LMU Munich.
- **1923** — The Institute for Social Research (Frankfurt School) is founded in Germany.
- **1937** — Talcott Parsons' *The Structure of Social Action* consolidates the American sociological tradition.
- **1959** — C. Wright Mills publishes *The Sociological Imagination*.
- **1989** — Kimberlé Crenshaw develops the concept of intersectionality.

## Body

### Antecedent History
Sociological reasoning has ancient roots, with proto-sociological observations in Greek texts and early social pattern analysis in 13th-century China. A significant precursor is the 14th-century work of Ibn Khaldun, who theorized social cohesion (*asabiyyah*) and social conflict. The term "sociology" was first coined in the late 18th century by Emmanuel Joseph Sieyès.

### 19th-Century Founding
Sociology was formally defined and institutionalized in the 19th century. Auguste Comte established it as a positivist science seeking universal laws through observation. Henri de Saint-Simon advocated for scientists to guide societal progress. Karl Marx, though rejecting positivism, developed historical materialism, analyzing society through economic structures and class conflict. Émile Durkheim solidified sociology's empirical basis, studying "social facts" like suicide rates and establishing the first academic department. In Germany, Max Weber introduced interpretive understanding (*Verstehen*) and analyzed rationalization, while Georg Simmel explored social forms and urban life. Herbert Spencer, though influential, is noted for his social Darwinist views.

### Institutionalization and Diversification
Formal academic departments emerged in Europe and North America in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The University of Chicago became a major hub, developing symbolic interactionism. The discipline diversified into various theoretical perspectives. Structural functionalism, advanced by Talcott Parsons, viewed society as a system of interrelated parts. Critical theory, from the Frankfurt School, blended Marxism with psychoanalysis to critique modernity. Later, structuralism, post-structuralism, and postmodernism challenged grand narratives, with thinkers like Michel Foucault analyzing power relations.

### Late 20th and 21st Century Developments
Recent sociology has focused on globalization, risk society (Ulrich Beck), and structuration theory (Anthony Giddens). Intersectionality, developed by Kimberlé Crenshaw, became a key framework for analyzing overlapping systems of discrimination. Methodologically, quantitative analysis has been enhanced by new statistical techniques and software, while social network analysis has become a pervasive paradigm. The discipline continues to debate the balance between positivist and interpretive approaches.

## Terms
- **Positivism**: The theory that authentic knowledge can only be derived from positive confirmation of theories through strict, scientifically and quantitatively based methods.
- **Antipositivism (Verstehen)**: A methodological approach emphasizing the need for sociologists to understand the subjective meanings and cultural contexts that guide human action.
- **Social Facts**: Concepts, such as suicide rates or legal systems, that are attributed to society at large and exert control over individuals, as defined by Émile Durkheim.
- **Asabiyyah**: A concept from Ibn Khaldun referring to social cohesion, group solidarity, or tribalism that binds a group together.
- **Historical Materialism**: Karl Marx's theory that the economic organization of society (the material base) fundamentally shapes its social, political, and intellectual structures (the superstructure).
- **Rationalization**: Max Weber's concept describing the process by which modern society becomes increasingly organized around efficiency, calculability, and control, leading to "disenchantment."
- **Gemeinschaft and Gesellschaft**: Ferdinand Tönnies' terms for two types of social association: *Gemeinschaft* (community) characterized by traditional, close-knit bonds, and *Gesellschaft* (society) characterized by impersonal, rational, and individualistic ties.
- **Intersectionality**: A framework, developed by Kimberlé Crenshaw, for understanding how various social and political identities (e.g., race, gender, class) combine to create unique modes of discrimination and privilege.
- **Structural Functionalism**: A macro-level theoretical perspective that views society as a complex system whose parts work together to promote solidarity and stability.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Positivism vs. Antipositivism**: A core methodological debate concerns whether sociology should emulate the natural sciences (positivism) or focus on interpreting subjective meaning (antipositivism).
- **Structure vs. Agency**: A persistent question is the extent to which social structures determine individual behavior versus the capacity of individuals to act freely (agency).
- **Canon Formation**: The "canon" of classical sociologists (Durkheim, Marx, Weber) is debated, with arguments for greater inclusion of figures like Harriet Martineau, W.E.B. Du Bois, and feminist theorists.
- **Disciplinary Boundaries**: Sociology's relationship with other social sciences and its autonomy from applied social research methods remain topics of discussion.
