# Electronic music

## Overview
Electronic music is a broad group of genres that use electronic instruments, circuitry-based technology, or computers in their creation. It encompasses both pure electronic instruments (like synthesizers) and electromechanical ones (like the electric guitar). Its development from late 19th-century experiments to a dominant force in popular culture is a story of technological innovation and artistic experimentation, fundamentally changing how music is composed, produced, and consumed.

## Timeline
- **Late 19th C.** — First electronic musical devices developed.
- **1920s-30s** — Commercial electronic instruments (theremin, ondes Martenot) introduced; first compositions written.
- **1944** — Halim El-Dabh creates earliest known tape music in Egypt.
- **1948** — Pierre Schaeffer begins *musique concrète* in Paris.
- **1953** — Karlheinz Stockhausen composes *Studie I*, first purely electronic music in Germany.
- **1957** — Max Mathews develops MUSIC I, a pioneering computer music program.
- **1968** — Wendy Carlos's *Switched-On Bach* popularizes the Moog synthesizer.
- **1970s** — Synthesizers and drum machines (e.g., Moog, TR-808) influence popular music genres like disco and synth-pop.
- **1983** — MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface) standard finalized, enabling instrument communication.
- **1980s** — Digital synthesizers (Yamaha DX7) and samplers become mainstream; electronic music dominates pop.
- **1990s** — Affordable technology makes electronic music production widespread; electronic dance music (EDM) grows.
- **2000s** — Software-based production (DAWs, plugins) democratizes music creation.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development (Late 19th Century – 1930s)
The first electronic instruments, like the Telharmonium (c. 1897), were large, experimental devices. The 1920s and 30s saw more practical, commercially produced instruments like the theremin and ondes Martenot. Early composers used them within orchestras, but avant-garde figures like Edgard Varèse and John Cage began exploring their unique sonic possibilities, including microtonality and noise. Parallel developments in recording technology, such as electrical recording (1925) and sound-on-film, allowed composers to experiment with splicing and layering recorded sounds.

### The 1940s and 1950s: Tape Music and Studio Foundations
The practical audio tape recorder (unveiled 1935, improved post-WWII) was a revolutionary tool. In 1944, Halim El-Dabh created *The Expression of Zaar*, considered the earliest tape music composition. In Paris, Pierre Schaeffer developed *musique concrète* (1948), which involved editing recorded natural and industrial sounds. In Germany, the WDR Studio for Electronic Music (opened 1953) pioneered *elektronische Musik*, synthesizing sounds entirely from electronic signals, as in Stockhausen's *Studie I* (1953). These two approaches—*musique concrète* (using recorded sounds) and *elektronische Musik* (using generated sounds)—formed the foundational dichotomy of early electronic art music. Studios were established worldwide, including the Columbia-Princeton Electronic Music Center (USA) and NHK Studio (Japan).

### Expansion and Popularization (1960s – 1970s)
The 1960s saw the rise of live electronics and the first computer-generated music (CSIRAC, 1951; Ferranti Mark I, 1951). Japanese manufacturers like Roland and Korg began producing influential instruments, including early drum machines. The Moog synthesizer, popularized by Wendy Carlos's *Switched-On Bach* (1968), entered popular music with bands like the Beatles and Doors. By the 1970s, synthesizers and drum machines became central to new genres like krautrock (Kraftwerk), synth-pop, and disco. The development of affordable, portable synthesizers like the Minimoog (1970) made electronic sounds accessible to a wider range of musicians.

### The Digital Revolution and Mainstream Dominance (1980s – 1990s)
The 1980s were defined by digital technology. The MIDI standard (1983) allowed instruments and computers to communicate. Digital synthesizers like the Yamaha DX7 (1983) and affordable samplers made new sounds ubiquitous in pop music. Drum machines like the Roland TR-808 became foundational to hip-hop and electronic dance music. By the 1990s, increasingly affordable technology made electronic music production a part of popular culture. Genres like house, techno, and trance emerged, and large-scale electronic music festivals began.

### The 2000s and Beyond: Democratization and Software
The 21st century has been marked by the dominance of software. Digital Audio Workstations (DAWs) like Ableton Live and software synthesizers (plugins) allow high-quality production on a single laptop. This has democratized music creation, leading to a massive increase in home-produced music shared online. New forms of performance, like laptop performance ("laptronica") and live coding, have emerged. The modular synthesizer has also seen a revival.

## Terms
- **Electromechanical Instrument:** An instrument with mechanical parts (e.g., strings, hammers) that generate sound, which is then processed electrically (e.g., electric guitar, Hammond organ).
- **Pure Electronic Instrument:** An instrument that generates sound entirely through circuitry, with no acoustic source (e.g., synthesizer, theremin).
- **Musique Concrète:** A form of electroacoustic music that uses recorded sounds (natural or industrial) as raw material, edited and manipulated in a studio.
- **Elektronische Musik:** A genre, originating in Germany, that synthesizes sounds entirely from electronically generated signals, rather than using recorded sounds.
- **MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface):** A technical standard (finalized 1983) that allows electronic instruments, computers, and other devices to communicate and synchronize.
- **Sampler:** An electronic instrument that plays back recorded sounds (samples), which can be pitch-shifted and manipulated.
- **Drum Machine:** A programmable electronic musical instrument that creates percussion sounds and rhythms.
- **Synthesizer:** An electronic instrument that generates audio signals, which can be shaped and modified to create a wide variety of sounds.
- **Digital Audio Workstation (DAW):** Software used for recording, editing, mixing, and producing audio files, forming the core of modern music production.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source text highlights a foundational scholarly debate: the distinction between *musique concrète* (using recorded sounds) and *elektronische Musik* (using synthesized sounds). This dichotomy, established in the 1950s, shaped early electronic music philosophy. Another ongoing theme is the tension between electronic music as academic art music and its role in popular culture, a divide that began narrowing in the 1970s and has largely dissolved with the mainstream success of electronic genres.
