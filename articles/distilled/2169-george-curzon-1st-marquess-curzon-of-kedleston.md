# George Curzon, 1st Marquess Curzon of Kedleston

## Overview
George Nathaniel Curzon (1859–1925) was a dominant British imperial statesman whose career spanned the height of the British Raj and the post-WWI reshaping of the Middle East. As Viceroy of India (1899–1905) and Foreign Secretary (1919–1924), he was a key architect of British policy in Asia, known for his administrative reforms, geopolitical foresight, and a personality that inspired both admiration and intense dislike.

## Body

### Early Life and Political Rise
Born into the Derbyshire aristocracy, Curzon was educated at Eton and Oxford. A spinal injury from adolescence left him requiring a metal corset for life. He entered Parliament in 1885 and quickly gained a reputation as a brilliant, if pompous, orator. His extensive travels through Russia, Central Asia, and the Far East (1888–1895) produced influential books that framed Russia as the primary threat to British India, shaping his geopolitical outlook.

### Viceroy of India (1899–1905)
Curzon's viceroyalty was marked by energetic reform and imperial assertion. He reorganized the administration of the North-West Frontier Province, pursued a policy of "forceful control mingled with conciliation," and undertook the restoration of the Taj Mahal. He championed the rights of Indian subjects, notably pressing for severe punishment of British soldiers who attacked Indians. However, his 1905 Partition of Bengal, intended to weaken nationalist sentiment, instead revitalized the Indian National Congress. His tenure ended in resignation after a power struggle with the Commander-in-Chief, Lord Kitchener, over military authority.

### Foreign Secretary (1919–1924)
Appointed Foreign Secretary in 1919, Curzon navigated the complex post-war settlement. He proposed the Curzon Line for Poland's eastern border and was central to Middle Eastern policy, overseeing the division of the British Mandate of Palestine and the creation of the Emirate of Transjordan. He was the chief Allied negotiator of the 1922 Treaty of Lausanne, which defined modern Turkey's borders. His tenure was often hampered by a difficult relationship with Prime Minister David Lloyd George, who viewed him as pompous but relied on his expertise.

### Personal Life and Legacy
Curzon's first wife, Mary Leiter, died in 1906, a profound personal loss. He later married Grace Hinds. Despite his accomplishments, including saving historic buildings like Bodiam Castle, his career was marked by a sense of unfulfilled potential. He was famously passed over for Prime Minister in 1923, partly due to opposition from peers like Arthur Balfour who argued a peer should not hold the office in a democratic age. He died in 1925, leaving a complex legacy as a brilliant but often abrasive imperial administrator.

## Terms
*   **Viceroy of India:** The representative of the British monarch in India, effectively the head of the British administration.
*   **Partition of Bengal (1905):** The division of the Bengal Presidency into two provinces by Lord Curzon, which sparked massive nationalist protest.
*   **Curzon Line:** A proposed demarcation line for Poland's eastern border, suggested by Curzon in 1919.
*   **Treaty of Lausanne (1922):** The peace treaty that defined the borders of modern Turkey after the Turkish War of Independence.
*   **Great Game:** The strategic rivalry between the British and Russian Empires for influence in Central Asia during the 19th and early 20th centuries.
*   **Swadeshi movement:** An Indian nationalist movement promoting domestic production and boycotting British goods, intensified by the Partition of Bengal.
*   **Imperial Cadet Corps (ICC):** A corps founded by Curzon in 1901 to give Indian princes military training, a precursor to Indianizing the officer corps.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Assessment of his Viceroyalty:** Historians debate whether Curzon was a progressive reformer who cared for Indian welfare or a staunch imperialist whose policies (like the Partition) ultimately strengthened the independence movement.
*   **Reasons for being passed over for PM:** Scholars analyze whether the 1923 decision was primarily due to constitutional convention (a peer not leading the Commons), personal animosity from figures like Balfour, or Curzon's own unpopular character.
*   **Effectiveness as Foreign Secretary:** There is debate over how much his Middle Eastern policies, particularly regarding Palestine, sowed the seeds for future conflict, and whether his influence was constrained by Lloyd George.
