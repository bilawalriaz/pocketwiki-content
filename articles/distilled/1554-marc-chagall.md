# Marc Chagall

## Overview
Marc Chagall (1887–1985) was a Russian-French modernist artist of Jewish ancestry whose work synthesized Cubism, Symbolism, and Fauvism into a unique, poetic visual language rooted in memories of his childhood in Vitebsk. He is celebrated as a pioneer of modern art and the "quintessential Jewish artist of the twentieth century," creating paintings, stained glass, ceramics, tapestries, and stage designs that blended folk imagery with avant-garde techniques. His life spanned the Russian Revolution, two World Wars, and the Holocaust; he fled Nazi-occupied France for the United States in 1941 before returning to France in 1948. Chagall’s art remained a "long dreamy reverie" of Eastern European Jewish life, using floating figures, vibrant color, and biblical motifs to express a universal message of love, suffering, and resilience.

## Body

### Early Life and Artistic Formation
Chagall was born into a Hasidic Jewish family in Lyozna, near Vitebsk, a city where over half the 66,000 residents were Jewish. The Pale of Settlement confined Jews to this region, fostering a distinct *shtetl* culture that became Chagall’s lifelong visual vocabulary. His father, a herring porter, and observant parents immersed him in a world of prayer and folklore. Chagall later wrote that he felt his Jewish identity "at every step," experiencing pogroms where he narrowly escaped violence by denying his faith. Barred from regular schools by quotas, he attended a Jewish religious school until his mother bribed a headmaster (50 roubles) to admit him to a Russian high school at age 13. A classmate’s drawing sparked a "revelation"; Chagall began copying library images and declared his intent to become a painter. In 1906, he studied briefly under realist Yehuda Pen in Vitebsk alongside future avant-gardists El Lissitzky and Ossip Zadkine, but rejected academic portraiture. He moved to Saint Petersburg in 1906, using a friend’s passport to bypass residency restrictions, and studied at the Zvantseva School under Léon Bakst, a Jewish designer for the Ballets Russes who became a crucial role model. In 1909, he met Bella Rosenfeld, his future wife and muse.

### Paris and the Synthesis of Modernism (1910–1914)
Chagall arrived in Paris in 1910, finding Cubism dominant but alien to his "ripe color gift" and poetic sentiment. He enrolled at Académie de La Palette and frequented the Louvre, admiring Rembrandt, Chardin, and the Impressionists. He learned gouache and developed a personal symbolism—ghostly floating figures, giant fiddlers, transparent wombs—painting Vitebsk memories in Fauvist and Cubist keys. Poets Blaise Cendrars and Guillaume Apollinaire championed him before painters did; Apollinaire dubbed his work *surnaturel*. Chagall synthesized Cubism’s fragmentation, Symbolism’s mystery, and Fauvism’s color into a style that art historian James Sweeney called a "reawakening of a poetry of representation," while André Breton noted the "triumphant return of the metaphor." Despite these affiliations, Chagall resisted movement labels, insisting his symbols were a private language.

### Russia: Revolution, Avant-Garde, and Theater (1914–1922)
Chagall returned to Vitebsk in 1914 to marry Bella, but World War I closed the border. He married in 1915; their daughter Ida was born in 1916. He worked for the War Industry Committee in Petrograd and exhibited in Moscow and St. Petersburg, gaining recognition. The 1917 Revolution initially offered opportunity: Anatoly Lunacharsky appointed him Commissar of Arts for Vitebsk (1918). Chagall founded the People’s Art College and the Vitebsk Museum of Modern Art, hiring Pen, Lissitzky, and Kazimir Malevich. However, Suprematist faculty (Malevich, Lissitzky) rejected Chagall’s "bourgeois individualism," seizing the school in 1920. Chagall resigned, moving to Moscow to design murals for the State Jewish Chamber Theater (1920–21), creating a 9x24-foot backdrop called "Hebrew jazz in paint" that became a landmark of modern theater design. Famine and civil war forced a move to Malakhovka, where he taught war orphans and illustrated David Hofstein’s *Grief*. In 1922, he secured an exit visa, writing his autobiography *My Life* while waiting.

### France, Bible Illustrations, and the Nazi Threat (1923–1941)
Back in Paris, Chagall found his pre-war works left in Berlin lost. Dealer Ambroise Vollard commissioned etchings for Gogol’s *Dead Souls*, La Fontaine’s *Fables*, and the Bible. For the Bible project (1931–39, published 1956), Chagall traveled to Palestine (1931), stating he "did not see the Bible, I dreamed it." He studied Rembrandt and El Greco in Amsterdam, producing 105 plates blending divine narrative with human intimacy. Meanwhile, Hitler’s rise triggered the Nazi "degenerate art" campaign; Chagall’s work was mocked as "green, purple, and red Jews shooting out of the earth." After Germany occupied France, the Vichy regime stripped Chagall of citizenship (October 1940). Unaware of the full danger, the Chagalls were trapped until daughter Ida and Alfred Barr (MoMA) secured their place on a rescue list. Varian Fry and Hiram Bingham IV smuggled them out via Marseille; they were arrested but released after Fry’s intervention. They sailed from Lisbon on the *Mouzinho* (June 1941), reaching New York the day before Germany invaded the USSR.

### United States: War, Grief, and Theater (1941–1948)
In New York, Chagall felt isolated by language but found community among émigré artists (Mondrian, Breton). Pierre Matisse became his dealer, staging successful shows. In 1942, he designed sets and costumes for the ballet *Aleko* (music by Tchaikovsky, story by Pushkin) in Mexico City, where he connected with "primitive" Mexican art; the premiere earned 19 curtain calls. He also designed Stravinsky’s *Firebird* (1945). The war and Holocaust haunted him: he learned Vitebsk was destroyed and painted Crucifixion scenes as Jewish suffering metaphors. In a 1944 speech, he condemned Christian silence and praised Soviet Russia for saving Jews. Bella’s sudden death in September 1944 devastated him; he stopped painting for months, later writing she joined "the millions of Jewish victims" in his mind. He began a seven-year relationship with Virginia Haggard (daughter David McNeil born 1946). After Paris’s liberation, he published a letter mourning Bella and celebrating freedom’s return.

### Late Career in France: Monumental Art and Stained Glass (1948–1985)
Chagall returned to France in 1948, settling in the Côte d'Azur near Matisse and Picasso. Picasso reportedly said, "When Matisse dies, Chagall will be the only painter left who understands what color is." He married Vava Brodsky in 1952. At nearly 70, he embraced stained glass, exploiting natural light’s interaction with color. Major commissions included: Assy (1956); Metz Cathedral (1958–60); the **Jerusalem Windows** (1962) for Hadassah Medical Center—twelve towering panels representing the tribes of Israel, called "jewels of translucent fire"; the UN *Peace* window (1964) for Dag Hammarskjöld; Fraumünster Zürich (1967); St. Stephan’s Mainz (1978–), a symbol of Jewish-Christian reconciliation in the former "capital of European Jewry"; All Saints Tudeley (1963–78), the only church with a full Chagall window set; Chichester Cathedral (1978); and the *America Windows* (1977) at the Art Institute of Chicago. In 1963–64, he painted the vast Paris Opéra ceiling (2,400 sq ft, 440 lbs of paint), honoring composers; initial controversy over a "Russian Jew" decorating a French monument dissolved into unanimous acclaim. He also created massive murals for the Metropolitan Opera (1966), tapestries for the Knesset (woven by Yvette Cauquil-Prince), ceramics, and sculptures.

### Art Styles and Techniques
**Color:** Chagall’s color was structural and emotional, not descriptive. Raymond Cogniat wrote colors "sculpt and animate the volume of shapes... suggest movements, planes and rhythms," conveying "explosive movement with the simplest use of colors." **Subject Matter:** His repertoire remained fixed on Vitebk memories—musicians, lovers floating, acrobats, animals, flowers—blending reality and fantasy. The circus/clown motif evolved from joy to prophetic anxiety as Europe darkened. **Jewish Themes:** He fused modernist techniques (Fauvism, Cubism) with Hasidic folk culture, giving "grim life... romantic overtones of a charmed world." The *Fiddler on the Roof* symbolized Jewish spiritual resilience. Though not observant, he used Jewish and Christian imagery for a "universal message," viewing the Bible as "the greatest source of poetry." **Stained Glass:** This medium allowed "intense and fresh colors" animated by shifting light. The Jerusalem Windows epitomized his ability to "transform material into light," serving as a "transparent partition between my heart and the heart of the world."

## Terms
- **Pale of Settlement**: The western region of the Russian Empire (modern Ukraine, Belarus, Poland, Lithuania, Latvia) where Jews were legally confined from the late 18th century to 1917.
- **Shtetl**: A small Jewish market-village in Eastern Europe with distinct communal institutions (schools, hospitals, markets), largely destroyed in the Holocaust.
- **Hasidic Judaism**: A mystical, ecstatic Orthodox Jewish movement originating in the 18th century; central to Vitebsk’s culture and Chagall’s spiritual imagery.
- **École de Paris**: The loose community of modernist artists (many foreign-born) working in Paris in the early 20th century; Chagall was a key figure.
- **Suprematism**: A Russian abstract art movement founded by Malevich, focused on basic geometric forms (squares, circles); clashed with Chagall’s figurative approach at the Vitebsk Art College.
- **Degenerate Art (Entartete Kunst)**: Nazi term for modern art deemed "un-German," Jewish, or Bolshevik; over 20,000 works confiscated from German museums in 1937.
- **Varian Fry Rescue Operation**: A US-backed effort (1940–41) led by journalist Varian Fry and Vice-Consul Hiram Bingham IV to smuggle ~2,000 artists/intellectuals from Vichy France using forged visas.
- **Gouache**: An opaque watercolor technique Chagall learned in Paris, used for vivid, matte Russian scenes.
- **Catalogue Raisonné**: A comprehensive, annotated listing of all known works by an artist; an online version for Chagall was launched in 2023 by the Association des Amis de Marc Chagall.
- **Kaddish**: The Jewish prayer for the dead; recited over Chagall’s coffin by a stranger, as he died without Jewish rites.

## Debates and open questions
- **Jewish Identity vs. Universalism**: Chagall credited his Jewish roots as his artistic source ("the hassidic spirit is still the basis") yet insisted his work represented "not the dream of one people but of all humanity." Scholars debate whether his Christian imagery (Crucifixions, church windows) reflects genuine syncretism, assimilation pressure, or a strategic universalism.
- **Art Historical Classification**: Critics struggle to place Chagall: he is claimed by Surrealism (Breton), Expressionism, and the École de Paris, yet rejected all labels. His "illogical and fantastic" juxtapositions resist standard modernist narratives of abstraction.
- **Relationship with the Soviet Avant-Garde**: His brief tenure as Vitebsk Commissar (1918–20) ended in conflict with Malevich and Lissitzky. Historians debate whether Chagall’s pluralistic "collective of independently minded artists" was politically naive or a deliberate alternative to state-imposed aesthetic uniformity.
- **Stained Glass and Site Specificity**: Chagall designed windows for cathedrals (Reims, Metz, Zürich, Mainz) and synagogues (Jerusalem). The tension between his Jewish identity and Christian commissions—and his disappointment that the Jerusalem Windows used artificial light—raises questions about artistic control in sacred architecture.
- **Provenance and Restitution**: Multiple Nazi-looted Chagalls have surfaced in recent decades (Menzel v. List, 1969; Gurlitt trove, 2013; MoMA’s *Over Vitebsk*, restituted 2021). Ongoing research continues to clarify ownership histories of works displaced during 1933–1945.
