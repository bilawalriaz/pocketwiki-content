# Early Muslim conquests

## Overview
The early Muslim conquests (c. 622–750 CE) were a series of rapid military expansions initiated by the Prophet Muhammad and continued by the Rashidun and Umayyad Caliphates. These campaigns fundamentally reshaped the political and cultural map of the ancient world, destroying the Sasanian Empire, drastically reducing the Byzantine Empire, and establishing Islamic rule from the Iberian Peninsula to Central Asia. Their success was enabled by a combination of ideological unity, the exhaustion of the rival empires, and effective Arab military tactics, leading to the creation of a vast, new Islamic civilization.

## Timeline
- **622** — Muhammad establishes the first Islamic state in Medina.
- **632** — Death of Muhammad; Abu Bakr becomes the first caliph, uniting Arabia after the Ridda Wars.
- **634–641** — Conquest of Syria from the Byzantine Empire, culminating in the Battle of Yarmuk (636).
- **636** — Battle of al-Qadisiyyah leads to the Arab-Muslim conquest of Iraq and the Sasanian capital, Ctesiphon.
- **639–642** — Conquest of Egypt from the Byzantine Empire under Amr ibn al-As.
- **642** — Battle of Nahavand seals the fate of the Sasanian Empire.
- **656–661** — First Fitna (civil war) ends with the founding of the Umayyad Caliphate.
- **674–751** — Conquest of Transoxiana (Central Asia) against Turkic and Sogdian resistance.
- **698** — Arab forces capture Carthage, ending Byzantine rule in North Africa.
- **711–714** — Conquest of most of the Iberian Peninsula (Hispania) under Tariq ibn Ziyad.
- **711–714** — Conquest of Sindh (modern Pakistan) under Muhammad bin Qasim.
- **732** — Frankish victory at the Battle of Tours halts major Muslim expansion into Western Europe.

## Body

### Background and Causes
Pre-Islamic Arabia was a region of tribal societies, with the Byzantine and Sasanian Empires competing for influence through client kingdoms like the Ghassanids and Lakhmids. Both empires were severely weakened by decades of mutual warfare and plague, creating a power vacuum. The unification of the Arabian Peninsula under Islam after 632 provided a new, ideologically motivated force. Historians cite this imperial exhaustion, combined with the new political and ideological coherence of the Muslim state, as primary factors for the conquests' success.

### Major Campaigns and Military Success
Muslim armies, often outnumbered but highly mobile and motivated, achieved rapid victories. In Syria, the Byzantines were defeated at Yarmuk (636), leading to the loss of the province by 641. Egypt fell swiftly between 639 and 642, providing a crucial grain base for the caliphate. The Sasanian Empire collapsed after defeats at al-Qadisiyyah (636) and Nahavand (642), with the last Sasanian king killed in 651. Expansion continued into North Africa, the Iberian Peninsula, and Central Asia, though it faced stronger resistance in mountainous regions like Anatolia and Afghanistan.

### The First Fitna and the Umayyad Caliphate
The assassination of Caliph Uthman in 656 sparked the First Fitna, a civil war that halted conquests. It ended with the establishment of the Umayyad dynasty in 661, which moved the capital to Damascus. The Umayyads resumed expansion, launching naval campaigns against Byzantium and conquering North Africa, Iberia, and Transoxiana. Their rule saw the creation of a vast empire but also internal tensions between Arab and non-Arab Muslims.

### Aftermath: Society, Taxation, and Religion
The conquests led to large-scale Arab migration and the establishment of garrison towns. Early policy, under Caliph Umar, allowed local elites and religions to persist in exchange for tribute (jizya) and land taxes (kharaj). There was no mass forced conversion initially; conversion to Islam was gradual and driven by social and economic incentives. Over time, Islamic jurists formalized the status of non-Muslims (dhimmis) as protected but subordinate "People of the Book" (Christians and Jews), who paid the jizya. This system, along with the blending of Arab, Byzantine, and Sasanian elements, formed the basis of the new Islamic civilization.

## Terms
- **Rashidun Caliphate**: The first four "rightly guided" caliphs (632–661) who succeeded Muhammad.
- **Umayyad Caliphate**: The first hereditary Muslim dynasty (661–750), based in Damascus.
- **Jizya**: A poll tax levied on non-Muslim subjects (dhimmis) of the Islamic state.
- **Kharaj**: A land tax, initially applied to all agricultural land, later specifically to non-Muslims.
- **Dhimmi**: A protected non-Muslim subject in an Islamic state, granted religious autonomy in exchange for paying the jizya.
- **Fitna**: A period of civil war and schism within the Muslim community, notably the First Fitna (656–661).
- **Mawali**: Non-Arab converts to Islam who became clients (protégés) of Arab tribes or patrons.
- **Ghassanids/Lakhmids**: Arab Christian client kingdoms of the Byzantine and Sasanian Empires, respectively, that acted as buffer states before the Islamic conquests.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Motivations for Conquest**: Historians debate whether the conquests were primarily religiously motivated, a form of secular Arab expansionism, or driven by economic factors like migration.
- **Impact of Taxation on Conversion**: Scholars disagree on whether the jizya was a primary incentive for conversion to Islam or if social status and other factors were more significant.
- **Nature of Early Islamic Rule**: Revisionist historians question the traditional narrative, suggesting the early Islamic state and its conquests may have been constructed retrospectively from diverse regional sources.
- **End of the Conquests**: The halt of rapid expansion by the mid-8th century is attributed to natural barriers, stronger resistance, diminishing returns, and a shift in caliphal priorities toward administration.
