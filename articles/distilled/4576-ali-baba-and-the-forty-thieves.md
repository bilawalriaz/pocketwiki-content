# Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves

## Overview
"Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves" is a folk tale from the *One Thousand and One Nights* collection. It tells of a poor woodcutter who discovers a thieves' secret treasure cave and must outwit them with the help of a clever slave-girl. The story is one of the most popular and widely adapted tales from the Arabian Nights, known globally for its themes of cleverness, loyalty, and the dangers of greed.

## Timeline
- **18th century** — Antoine Galland adds the tale to his French translation of *One Thousand and One Nights*, based on stories told by Hanna Diyab.
- **1901** — Richard F. Burton includes the tale in the supplemental volumes of his English translation.
- **2004** — Folklorist Hans-Jörg Uther revises the Aarne-Thompson Index, subsuming tale type 676 ("Open Sesame") under type 954 ("The Forty Thieves").
- **2016** — The story's elements appear in the video game *Persona 5*.
- **2023** — The "Forty Thieves" questline appears in *Assassin’s Creed Mirage*.

## Body

### Textual History and Origin
The tale was added to the *One Thousand and One Nights* by its 18th-century French translator, Antoine Galland. He heard it from a Syrian storyteller named Hanna Diyab in Paris. The earliest known text is Galland's French version. An Arabic manuscript discovered by Duncan Black MacDonald was later found to be a counterfeit. Scholars debate the story's ultimate origin, with some pointing to Eastern elements like the hidden treasure chamber and magic formula as evidence of an independent, pre-literary tradition.

### Plot Summary
Ali Baba is a poor woodcutter whose wealthy brother Cassim resents him. While working, Ali Baba overhears forty thieves opening their treasure cave with the magic phrase "open sesame." He enters and takes a modest amount of gold. His brother Cassim, learning the secret, greedily enters the cave but forgets the exit phrase and is killed by the returning thieves. Ali Baba retrieves the body, and his clever slave-girl Morgiana devises a plan to stitch the corpse together for a proper burial, fooling the community.

The thieves discover someone knows their secret and track down Ali Baba's house. Morgiana repeatedly foils their assassination attempts: first by marking all houses similarly, then by chipping all doorsteps. Finally, the thieves' leader hides his men in oil jars brought to Ali Baba's house. Morgiana discovers the plot and kills the hidden thieves with boiling oil. She later identifies the disguised leader at a feast and kills him with a dagger during a dance. Grateful, Ali Baba frees Morgiana and marries her to his son, becoming the sole keeper of the treasure cave's secret.

### Tale Type and Analysis
The story is classified in the Aarne-Thompson-Uther (ATU) index as type 954, "The Forty Thieves." It combines elements of two older tale types: one about a poor man discovering a magic cave (ATU 676) and another about a girl outwitting robbers hiding in containers. The tale has achieved "almost universal diffusion," with variants found across Europe, Africa, and the Americas, suggesting a deep oral tradition alongside its literary source.

### Popular Culture and Adaptations
The tale has been adapted into countless forms worldwide, including stage pantomimes, films, television series, music, and video games. Notable adaptations include the 1916 musical *Chu Chin Chow*, numerous Indian films in various languages, the 1959 Soviet puppet film, and the 1996 Disney film *Aladdin and the King of Thieves*. The name "Ali Baba" has also entered broader culture, used as slang during the Iraq War and as the namesake for the Chinese Alibaba Group.

## Terms
- **One Thousand and One Nights**: A collection of Middle Eastern folk tales compiled in Arabic during the Islamic Golden Age.
- **Aarne-Thompson-Uther (ATU) Index**: A system used by folklorists to classify and categorize folktales by their core narrative elements.
- **Pantomime**: A type of musical comedy stage production, popular in Britain, often based on fairy tales.
- **Maronite**: An Eastern Catholic sect of Christianity; Hanna Diyab was a Syrian Maronite storyteller.
- **Zero-knowledge proofs**: A cryptographic concept often explained using the parable of "Ali Baba's Cave."

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the tale's ultimate origin. While its first appearance is in Galland's 18th-century literary collection, folklorists like Ulrich Marzolph and Ton Dekker suggest an "independent strand" of oral tradition may have existed earlier, based on the story's Eastern elements and widespread variants. The authenticity of the Arabic manuscript discovered by Duncan Black MacDonald is settled; it was a counterfeit.
