# Futurism

## Overview
Futurism was an early 20th-century Italian artistic and social movement that glorified modernity, speed, technology, and violence, while aggressively rejecting the past. Founded by poet Filippo Tommaso Marinetti, it sought to liberate Italy from its historical weight and became influential across multiple art forms, though its later association with Fascism complicated its legacy.

## Timeline
- **5 Feb 1909** — Marinetti publishes the *Manifesto of Futurism* in *La gazzetta dell'Emilia*.
- **11 Apr 1910** — *Technical Manifesto of Futurist Painting* is published.
- **1912** — First Futurist exhibition outside Italy held at Bernheim-Jeune gallery in Paris.
- **1913** — Boccioni creates sculpture *Unique Forms of Continuity in Space*; Russolo writes *The Art of Noises*.
- **1914** — Personal quarrels create a rift between the Milan and Florence Futurist groups.
- **1915** — Italy enters WWI; many Futurists enlist. Boccioni is killed in 1916.
- **1918** — Marinetti founds the Futurist Political Party.
- **1919** — Futurist Political Party is absorbed into Mussolini's Fasci Italiani di Combattimento.
- **1929** — Manifesto *Perspectives of Flight* launches the Aeropainting (*aeropittura*) movement.
- **1944** — Death of Marinetti marks the end of the organized movement.

## Body

### Origins and Core Philosophy
Futurism was founded in Milan in 1909 by Filippo Tommaso Marinetti. Its founding manifesto, published in *Le Figaro*, expressed a "passionate loathing of everything old" and celebrated dynamism, speed, technology, youth, and violence. The movement aimed to glorify modernity and repudiate the cult of the past, including traditional Italian Renaissance art. The Futurists published numerous manifestos on topics from painting to cuisine, detailing their beliefs and disdain for tradition.

### Artistic Style and Practice
Early Futurist painters initially used Divisionism, a technique of breaking light into stippled dots. After contact with Cubism in Paris around 1911, they adopted its fragmented forms to express dynamism and "universal dynamism." Key works include Boccioni's *The City Rises* (1910) and Balla's *Dynamism of a Dog on a Leash* (1912), which depicted motion through multiplied forms. Boccioni also applied Futurist ideas to sculpture, as in *Unique Forms of Continuity in Space* (1913). The movement practiced in all media, including architecture, music, and literature.

### Political Alignment and Fascism
Futurism was intensely patriotic and admired violence from its outset. In 1913, Marinetti published a political manifesto, and the movement campaigned against the Austro-Hungarian Empire. After WWI, many Futurists, including Marinetti, supported Benito Mussolini's Fascist movement, hoping it would modernize Italy. Marinetti's Futurist Political Party merged into the National Fascist Party in 1919. While Futurism was not made the official state art, its association with the regime brought it official acceptance, especially in architecture. This link damaged many Futurists' reputations after WWII.

### Second Futurism and Aeropainting
After WWI, Marinetti revived the movement in what art historians call "Second Futurism." A major expression was *aeropittura* (Aeropainting), launched in 1929. This style used the new perspective of flight as subject matter, ranging from realistic propaganda to abstraction. Artists like Gerardo Dottori and Fortunato Depero explored aerial landscapes and the technology of aviation.

### International Spread
Parallel movements emerged elsewhere. Russian Futurism, a literary and visual arts movement, featured figures like Vladimir Mayakovsky and Kazimir Malevich. Its style, Cubo-Futurism, combined Cubist forms with Futurist dynamism, but ideologically, many Russian Futurists embraced communism. Armenian Futurism, founded by Kara-Darvish, sought to apply Western Futurism to an Armenian context, later looking for inspiration in a "New East."

### Legacy and Influence
Futurism influenced later movements including Art Deco, Constructivism, Surrealism, and Vorticism. Its ideals of youth, speed, and technology persist in modern culture, from cinema (e.g., *Blade Runner*) to music. The movement is considered extinct as an organized force, but its ideas continue to provoke reaction and inspire artists, including Neo-Futurists in theatre and architecture.

## Terms
- **Divisionism**: A painting technique of breaking color into dots and stripes, adopted by early Futurists.
- **Cubo-Futurism**: The main style of Russian Futurist painting, combining Cubist forms with Futurist themes of movement.
- **Parole in libertà**: "Word autonomy"; a Futurist poetic style rejecting meter, syntax, and punctuation.
- **Intonarumori**: Acoustic noise generators invented by Luigi Russolo for Futurist music concerts.
- **Aeropittura**: "Aeropainting"; a Second Futurist style focused on aerial perspectives and flight.
- **Photodynamism**: A photographic technique using long exposures to capture the trajectory of movement, theorized by Anton Giulio Bragaglia.
- **Passéism**: An attitude or trend opposing Futurism by valuing the past and tradition.
- **Plastic Dynamism**: The Futurist concept of representing the dynamic relationship between objects and their environment.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The degree to which Futurism's early radicalism was compromised by its later alliance with Fascism remains a central point of scholarly debate.
- The movement's relationship with other avant-gardes, like Cubism, is complex; while influenced by it, Futurists insisted on depicting motion and dynamism, unlike the more static Cubist works.
- The role and recognition of female Futurists, such as Benedetta Cappa Marinetti and Valentine de Saint-Point, within a movement whose founding manifestos expressed misogyny, is an ongoing area of study.
