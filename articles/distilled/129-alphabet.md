# Alphabet

## Overview

An alphabet is a writing system that uses a standard set of symbols, called letters, to represent particular sounds—specifically phonemes, the smallest sound segments that can distinguish one word from another in a language. This distinguishes alphabets from syllabaries, which assign symbols to spoken syllables, and logographies, which assign symbols to words or morphemes. The first letters emerged in Ancient Egypt as pronunciation aids for hieroglyphs, but the first fully phonemic script was Proto-Sinaitic, which led to the Phoenician alphabet—the ancestor of most modern scripts. In a narrower scholarly sense, "true" alphabets represent both consonants and vowels with independent letters, a distinction that makes the Greek alphabet the first true alphabet, since Phoenician was an abjad (consonant-only).

## Body

### Origins in Egypt and the Near East

The Ancient Egyptian writing system had about 24 uniliteral hieroglyphs—glyphs providing one sound—used as pronunciation guides for logograms, to write grammatical inflections, and later to transcribe loan words and foreign names. This system was used until the 5th century AD, when it was forgotten after pagan temples were closed, until the discovery of the Rosetta Stone. Cuneiform, used for several ancient languages including Sumerian, saw its last known use in 75 AD.

In the Middle Bronze Age, the Proto-Sinaitic script appeared c. 1840 BC in Egyptian turquoise mines in the Sinai Peninsula, apparently left by Canaanite workers. In 1999, John and Deborah Darnell discovered an earlier version at Wadi el-Hol, dated c. 1800 BC, adapted from Egyptian hieroglyphs datable to c. 2000 BC. This script had no vowel characters and was probably originally a syllabary. The best-attested Bronze Age alphabet is Ugaritic, invented before the 15th century BC—an alphabetic cuneiform script with 30 signs, including three indicating the following vowel.

### Phoenician and Its Descendants

Proto-Sinaitic developed into the Phoenician alphabet before c. 1050 BC. The Phoenician script was probably the first phonemic script, with only about two dozen distinct letters—simple enough for traders to learn and able to write different languages because it recorded words phonemically. It spread across the Mediterranean. By the 10th century BC, Canaanite and Aramaic distinguished themselves; Aramaic gave rise to Hebrew. The South Arabian alphabet, a sister script, is the ancestor of Geʽez. The Greek alphabet was the first to give vowels independent letterforms, choosing letters for sounds that did not exist in Greek. The Euboean form of Greek was carried to Italy c. 800–600 BC, producing the Etruscan alphabet and then Latin, which spread across Europe with the Roman Republic and became the world's most widely used script.

Other notable scripts include Elder Fuþark (from c. 100 CE, ultimately derived from Phoenician), the Old Hungarian script, and Glagolitic—created by Saints Cyril and Methodius for Old Church Slavonic and, with Greek uncial, the basis of Cyrillic. In Asia, most alphabetic scripts of India and Eastern Asia descend from Brahmi, believed to be a descendant of Aramaic. Hangul, created in 1443 by Sejong the Great, is a featural alphabet where letter designs reflect place of articulation; it places letters in syllable clusters with equal dimensions, allowing mixed-script writing.

### Types of Alphabets

Linguists distinguish "true" alphabets from abjads and abugidas based on vowel treatment. Abjads (Arabic, Hebrew, Syriac) have letters for consonants and leave most vowels unexpressed. Abugidas are consonant-based but indicate vowels with diacritics—systematic graphic modifications. True alphabets (Latin, Cyrillic, Hangul) represent both consonants and vowels. The boundaries are not always clear: Sorani Kurdish uses the Arabic script but writes vowels mandatorily, making it a true alphabet; the ʼPhags-pa script of the Mongol Empire was based on Tibetan abugida but writes vowel marks after the preceding consonant. For tonal languages, scripts vary: some disregard tone (Somali), most use diacritics (Vietnamese, Thai), and some have separate tone letters (Hmong, Zhuang).

### Alphabetical Order and Acrophony

Most alphabets have a standard ordering for collation—listing words in alphabetical order. A dozen Ugaritic tablets from the 14th century BC preserve two sequences: the ABCDE order later used in Phoenician (continuing in Hebrew, Greek, Cyrillic, Latin) and the HLĦM order preserved in Geʽez. Both have been stable for at least 3000 years. The Brahmic family uses a phonology-based order arranged by how and where sounds are produced in the mouth. Latin alphabets vary in treating modified letters: Icelandic treats accented letters as distinct; Spanish formerly did for ñ, ll, and ch but changed in 1994 and 2010; German ignores umlauts in collation except in telephone directories.

Acrophony—naming letters after words beginning with that sound—is used in Samaritan, Aramaic, Syriac, Hebrew, Greek, and Arabic. Latin abandoned it, referring to letters by adding a vowel (usually ⟨e⟩). The names of F, L, M, N, S remain the same in English and French because "short" vowels were unaffected by the Great Vowel Shift, while A, B, C, D differ due to that shift.

### Orthography and Pronunciation

When an alphabet is adopted for a language, an orthography develops mapping letters to phonemes. A perfectly phonemic orthography would have one-to-one correspondence, but this is rarely achieved. Spanish and Finnish come close; English deviates greatly because the Great Vowel Shift occurred after its orthography was established and because of numerous loanwords. Languages may fail one-to-one correspondence through digraphs and trigraphs (German "tsch"), multiple spellings for one phoneme (modern Greek writes [i] six ways), silent letters (Thai เบียร์ retains a silent /r/), sandhi, dialect variation, and distinct symbol sets for foreign words. Some countries undertake spelling reforms: Turkey switched from Arabic to Latin script, and Kazakhstan is transitioning from Cyrillic to Latin after making a version in 2021. The International Phonetic Alphabet is the standard system linguists use to represent sounds independently of orthography.

## Terms

- **Phoneme**: The smallest sound segment that can distinguish one word from another in a language.
- **Abjad**: A segmental script with letters for consonants that leaves most vowels unexpressed (e.g., Arabic, Hebrew).
- **Abugida**: A consonant-based script indicating vowels with diacritics (e.g., Devanagari, Geʽez).
- **Syllabary**: A writing system assigning symbols to spoken syllables rather than individual phonemes.
- **Logography**: A writing system assigning symbols to words, morphemes, or other semantic units.
- **Acrophony**: Naming letters after words that begin with that letter's sound.
- **Collation**: The ordering of items, such as words in a dictionary, in a defined sequence.
- **Diacritic**: A systematic graphic modification of a letter, often indicating vowels or tone.
- **Digraph/Trigraph/Tetragraph**: Combinations of two, three, or four letters representing a single phoneme.
- **Featural alphabet**: An alphabet where letter designs derive from articulatory features, as in Hangul.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether the earliest alphabets had a defined sequence; some scripts today, like Hanuno'o, are learned in no particular order. Orly Goldwasser has connected the "illiterate turquoise miner graffiti" theory to the origin of the alphabet, suggesting the first alphabet may have been developed by workers rather than scribes. The Proto-Sinaitic script was originally probably a syllabary, and whether it or the Wadi el-Hol script should be considered the first alphabet depends on how one defines "alphabet" in the narrow sense. The categorisation of scripts into alphabets, abjads, and abugidas is not always clear-cut, as some scripts shift categories depending on language use, and no established names yet exist for distinguishing how scripts treat tone.
