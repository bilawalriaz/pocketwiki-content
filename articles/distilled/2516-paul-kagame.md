# Paul Kagame

## Overview
Paul Kagame is a Rwandan politician and former military officer who has been the president of Rwanda since 2000. He is a central figure in modern Rwandan history, having commanded the Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF) to end the 1994 genocide and subsequently leading the country's reconstruction and economic development, though his rule is marked by authoritarianism and allegations of human rights abuses.

## Timeline
- **23 October 1957** — Born in Tambwe, Ruanda-Urundi (now Rwanda).
- **1959** — Family flees to Uganda during the Rwandan Revolution.
- **1981** — Joins Yoweri Museveni's rebel army in the Ugandan Bush War.
- **October 1990** — RPF invades Rwanda; Kagame takes command after leader Fred Rwigyema's death.
- **August 1993** — Arusha Accords signed, ending the Rwandan Civil War and establishing a transitional government.
- **6 April 1994** — Assassination of President Habyarimana triggers the Rwandan genocide.
- **18 July 1994** — RPF captures Gisenyi, ending the genocide and the civil war.
- **March 2000** — Becomes President of Rwanda following the resignation of Pasteur Bizimungu.
- **2003** — New constitution approved by referendum; Kagame wins first presidential election.
- **2015** — Constitutional referendum allows Kagame to run for additional terms.
- **2018-2019** — Serves as Chairperson of the African Union.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Career
Born to a Tutsi family, Kagame fled to Uganda as a child during the 1959 revolution that ended Tutsi political dominance. In Uganda, he joined Yoweri Museveni's rebel army, fighting in the Ugandan Bush War and rising to head military intelligence. In 1990, he co-led the RPF invasion of Rwanda to secure the return of Tutsi refugees. After the death of RPF leader Fred Rwigyema, Kagame took command, reorganized the forces, and waged a guerrilla war that led to the 1993 Arusha Accords.

### The Rwandan Genocide and Civil War
The genocide began after President Habyarimana's assassination on 6 April 1994. Kagame resumed the civil war, and the RPF systematically defeated government forces, ending the genocide in July 1994. The RPF's victory came at a cost; scholars and human rights groups document that RPF forces killed thousands of civilians, predominantly Hutu, during and after the conflict. Kagame became Vice President and Minister of Defence, controlling the army while a transitional government was established.

### Post-Genocide Leadership and Congo Wars
As de facto leader, Kagame focused on security and rebuilding. He confronted a Hutu refugee crisis in neighboring Zaire (now DRC), where former genocidal forces were rearming. This led to Rwanda's involvement in the First Congo War (1996-97), which toppled dictator Mobutu Sese Seko. The Second Congo War (1998-2003) erupted after Rwanda and Uganda fell out with the new Congolese government. These conflicts caused millions of deaths and were accompanied by allegations of Rwandan exploitation of Congolese minerals and support for insurgencies, charges Kagame denies.

### Presidency and Domestic Policy
Kagame assumed the presidency in 2000. He oversaw the adoption of a new constitution in 2003 and has won four presidential elections (2003, 2010, 2017, 2024) with over 90% of the vote, though none were rated free or fair by international observers. His domestic agenda, "Vision 2020" and later "Vision 2050," prioritized national development, resulting in significant economic growth, improved healthcare, and expanded education. However, his government is considered authoritarian, with severe restrictions on political opposition, free speech, and civil liberties.

### Foreign Policy
Kagame has cultivated close ties with the United States and the United Kingdom, which have been major donors. Relations with France were poor until 2009. He is a member of the East African Community and the Commonwealth of Nations. His relationship with the DRC remains tense due to ongoing accusations of Rwandan support for rebel groups like M23, leading to periodic aid suspensions from Western countries. He has also praised China's investment model in Africa.

## Terms
- **Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF):** A rebel group formed by Tutsi refugees that invaded Rwanda in 1990 and ended the 1994 genocide.
- **Arusha Accords:** A 1993 peace agreement that ended the Rwandan Civil War and established a transitional power-sharing government.
- **Vision 2020:** A national development strategy launched by Kagame to transform Rwanda into a middle-income country by 2020.
- **First Congo War (1996-97):** A conflict in which Rwandan-backed rebels overthrew Zaire's dictator Mobutu Sese Seko.
- **Second Congo War (1998-2003):** A major war in central Africa, often called "Africa's World War," involving Rwanda and multiple other nations.
- **M23:** A rebel group active in the eastern DRC, which a UN report alleged was commanded by Rwanda's defence minister.
- **Vision 2050:** Kagame's long-term development plan for Rwanda to become a high-income country by 2050.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Role in the Genocide's Start:** The cause of President Habyarimana's assassination is disputed. Some theories implicate Hutu extremists, while others, including French and Spanish judges, allege Kagame and the RPF were responsible.
- **RPF War Crimes:** The extent of RPF killings of civilians during and after the genocide is debated. While documented by human rights groups and scholars, the exact death toll is uncertain, with estimates ranging from tens to hundreds of thousands.
- **Legitimacy of Elections:** International observers consistently find Kagame's elections lack freedom and fairness, citing repression of opposition and irregularities. Scholars debate whether Kagame would win without these tactics.
- **Involvement in Congo:** Rwanda's alleged support for insurgencies in the DRC and exploitation of its minerals remains a major point of contention, with periodic UN reports and government denials.
- **Authoritarian Rule vs. Development:** A central debate is whether Kagame's authoritarian governance is a necessary trade-off for Rwanda's stability and rapid economic development, or if it fundamentally undermines human rights and long-term peace.
