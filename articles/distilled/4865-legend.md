# Legend

## Overview
A legend is a genre of folklore consisting of a narrative about human actions, believed or perceived to have occurred in history. It is distinguished by its verisimilitude (appearance of being true or real), often featuring human protagonists and sometimes miracles, while maintaining a connection to a historical or topographical basis. Legends are vital cultural narratives that reflect and reaffirm a group's values, evolving over time to remain relevant.

## Timeline
- **c. 1340** — The word "legend" enters English from Old French, initially meaning a narrative of an event.
- **1510s** — The noun "legendary" appears, meaning a collection of legends.
- **1613** — English Protestants begin using "legend" to imply a fictitious story, especially of a saint.
- **1866** — Jacob Grimm defines the fairy tale as "poetic, legend historic."
- **1925** — Friedrich Ranke characterizes folk legend as "a popular narrative with an objectively untrue imaginary content."
- **1928** — Helmut de Boor notes legend is comparatively amorphous and realistic versus folktale.
- **1968** — The term "urban legend" first appears in print.
- **1981** — Jan Harold Brunvand popularizes the study of urban legends with *The Vanishing Hitchhiker*.
- **1990** — Timothy R. Tangherlini proposes a modern professional definition of legend.

## Body

### Definition and Characteristics
A legend is a short, traditional, episodic narrative performed conversationally. It reflects a symbolic representation of folk belief and collective experiences, serving to reaffirm the values of the group to which it belongs. Legends often operate in a realm of uncertainty, neither fully believed nor resolutely doubted by participants. They are frequently distinguished from myths by their focus on human beings as main characters and their perceived historical basis, whereas myths generally do not have such a basis. The Brothers Grimm defined legend as "folktale historically grounded."

### Etymology and Scholarly Evolution
The term "legend" is a loanword from Old French, derived from Medieval Latin *legenda* ("things to be read"). Its meaning shifted from a simple narrative to connotations of being "undocumented" or "spurious." Early scholars like Jacob Grimm and Friedrich Ranke focused on the literary narrative. Later approaches, especially after the 1960s, enriched the study by considering performance, social context, and anthropological insights. Scholars like Ernst Bernheim and Gordon Allport linked legend to rumor, a connection further blurred by William Hugh Jansen's distinction between "short-term" and "long-term" legends.

### Christian and Oral Traditions
In a narrow Christian sense, *legenda* referred to hagiographical accounts (lives of saints) read in church, often collected in a *legendary*. Because these accounts included many miracles, the term "legend" broadened to refer to any story set in a historical context but containing supernatural or fantastic elements. History preserved orally over many generations can take on a more narrative or mythological form, as seen in traditions like those of the African Great Lakes.

### Urban Legends and Related Concepts
Urban legends are a modern folklore genre, rooted in popular culture, comprising fictional stories often presented as true with macabre or humorous elements. The term was popularized by Jan Harold Brunvand in 1981 to show that folklore exists in modern societies and can illuminate contemporary culture. Legend is distinguished from myth by its necessary historical or topographical connection. It is also distinct from fable, which exceeds realism (e.g., talking animals). When retold as fiction, a legend's authentic qualities can fade, as in Washington Irving's literary transformation of a local tale into *The Legend of Sleepy Hollow*.

## Terms
- **Verisimilitude**: The appearance of being true or real within a narrative.
- **Ecotypified**: Adapted to a specific local environment or culture through repeated performance.
- **Hagiography**: A biography of a saint or religious figure, often idealizing the subject.
- **Folktale**: A traditional narrative, often anonymous, that is more highly structured and less historically grounded than a legend.
- **Myth**: A traditional narrative, often involving gods or supernatural beings, that explains natural phenomena or cultural practices and is not typically tied to a specific historical basis.
- **Urban legend**: A modern fictional story, often with a macabre or humorous twist, that is presented as a true account and spreads through popular culture.
- **Legendry**: A collection or corpus of legends.
- **Rumour**: A currently circulating story or report of uncertain or doubtful truth, which shares staying power with some legends.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars have long debated the precise categorization of legends, seeking a content-based system similar to the Aarne–Thompson folktale index. The distinction between legend and rumour is considered by some to be effectively obliterated, as both can persist as long-term cultural phenomena. The relationship between legend and other narrative forms like myth, folktale, and fable remains a subject of analysis, particularly regarding the role of historical basis, realism, and supernatural elements.
