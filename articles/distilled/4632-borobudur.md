# Borobudur

## Overview
Borobudur is a 9th-century Mahayana Buddhist temple in Central Java, Indonesia, and the largest Buddhist temple in the world. Constructed by the Sailendra Dynasty, it is a monumental stupa and pilgrimage site whose design blends indigenous Javanese ancestor worship with Buddhist cosmology. After being abandoned and buried under volcanic ash, it was rediscovered in the 19th century and has undergone major restoration, now standing as a UNESCO World Heritage Site and a powerful symbol of Indonesia's cultural heritage.

## Timeline
- **c. 780–833** — Construction of Borobudur in five stages under the Sailendra and Sanjaya dynasties.
- **824** — Karangtengah inscription mentions a sacred building, possibly Borobudur.
- **842** — Tri Tepusan inscription mentions a *Kamūlān* (sacred building) likely referring to Borobudur.
- **928–1006** — Mataram kingdom capital moves to East Java; likely period of Borobudur's abandonment.
- **1814** — Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles is informed of the monument's location, leading to its rediscovery.
- **1835** — The entire complex is fully unearthed under Christiaan Lodewijk Hartmann.
- **1907–1911** — First major restoration led by Theodoor van Erp.
- **1975–1982** — Massive joint Indonesian government and UNESCO restoration project.
- **1991** — Borobudur is listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2010** — The temple is damaged by ashfall from the eruption of Mount Merapi.
- **2012** — Recorded in the Guinness Book of World Records as the world's largest Buddhist temple.

## Body

### Etymology and Location
The name "Borobudur" has multiple proposed etymologies, including "ancient Boro," "vihara of Buddha on a high place," or a corruption of *Vihara Buddha Uhr*. The temple is located in the fertile Kedu Plain, between twin volcanoes and two rivers. Three Buddhist temples—Borobudur, Pawon, and Mendut—are aligned in a straight line, suggesting a ritual relationship. The hypothesis that Borobudur was once surrounded by a lake has been largely dismissed by archaeological studies.

### Construction and Abandonment
Construction began c. 780 under the Sailendra Dynasty and was completed c. 833 by the Sanjaya Dynasty, indicating a period of peaceful coexistence between Hindu and Buddhist patrons. The temple's purpose is not recorded in contemporary inscriptions. It was likely abandoned between the 10th and 14th centuries, possibly due to the capital's move to East Java or the later conversion of Java to Islam. It lay hidden under volcanic ash and jungle until its rediscovery.

### Rediscovery and Restoration
Rediscovered in 1814 under British administration, the monument was gradually cleared. Early Dutch-led restorations in the early 20th century addressed structural instability but introduced new problems. A major international campaign from 1975 to 1982, led by Indonesia and UNESCO, involved dismantling and cleaning over a million stones to stabilize the foundation and improve drainage. The temple has since faced threats from vandalism, earthquakes, and volcanic eruptions, requiring ongoing conservation.

### Architecture and Design
Borobudur is built as a single large stupa on a natural hill, forming a giant mandala. It has nine platforms: six square and three circular, topped by a central dome. The structure is divided into three symbolic realms of Buddhist cosmology: *Kāmadhātu* (world of desires, the base), *Rūpadhatu* (world of forms, the square terraces), and *Arūpadhatu* (formless world, the circular terraces). A hidden base, discovered in 1885, contains 160 narrative relief panels. The monument is constructed from andesite stone without mortar, using knobs and dovetails for joints.

### Reliefs and Statues
Borobudur contains approximately 2,670 bas-relief panels, one of the world's largest collections of Buddhist reliefs. The narrative panels, arranged in 11 series, depict the Buddha's life (*Lalitavistara*), his previous lives (*Jataka* and *Avadana*), and the spiritual journey of Sudhana (*Gandavyuha*). The hidden base illustrates the law of karma. Originally adorned with 504 Buddha statues, many are now damaged or missing. The statues display five different *mudrās* (hand gestures), each representing a cardinal direction and one of the Five Dhyani Buddhas.

## Terms
- **Candi**: Indonesian term for ancient temple or shrine structure.
- **Stupa**: A hemispherical structure containing relics, used as a place of meditation.
- **Mandala**: A symbolic diagram representing the universe, used in Tantric Buddhism.
- **Mudrā**: A symbolic hand gesture used in Buddhist iconography.
- **Pradakshina**: The ritual of clockwise circumambulation around a sacred object.
- **Anastylosis**: A restoration method where a ruined structure is reassembled using original materials.
- **Andesite**: A gray volcanic stone used in the temple's construction.
- **Kāmadhātu**: The Buddhist realm of desires, represented by Borobudur's base.
- **Rūpadhatu**: The Buddhist realm of forms, represented by the temple's square terraces.
- **Arūpadhatu**: The formless realm, represented by the circular upper terraces.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The original name of Borobudur is uncertain, with several competing etymological theories.
- The reason for the temple's abandonment remains a mystery, with theories ranging from volcanic activity to religious and political shifts.
- The "three realm" cosmological division of the temple's terraces, while conventional, is questioned by some scholars for lacking direct textual or narrative support in the monument itself.
- The purpose of encasing the "hidden foot" reliefs is debated, with explanations ranging from structural concerns to symbolic or religious reasons.
- The design of the original pinnacle (*chattra*) atop the main stupa is unknown, as the reconstructed version was later dismantled.
