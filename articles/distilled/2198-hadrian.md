# Hadrian

## Overview
Hadrian (r. 117–138) was a Roman emperor of the Nerva–Antonine dynasty, renowned for his extensive travels, ambitious building projects, and a policy shift from military expansion to consolidating and defending the empire's borders. His reign marked a turn towards cultural unification under a Hellenic ideal and the establishment of a more autocratic, bureaucratic system of government, though his relationship with the Senate remained deeply strained.

## Timeline
- **24 January 76** — Born Publius Aelius Hadrianus in Italica, Hispania Baetica.
- **c. 100/101** — Marries Vibia Sabina, grandniece of Emperor Trajan.
- **117** — Succeeds Trajan as emperor following a contested adoption.
- **118** — Orders the execution of four leading senators, earning the Senate's lifelong enmity.
- **122** — Initiates construction of Hadrian's Wall in Britannia.
- **128–132** — Extensive travels in Greece and the East; establishes the Panhellenion league.
- **130** — His lover Antinous drowns in the Nile; Hadrian founds Antinoöpolis in his memory.
- **132–135** — Suppresses the Bar Kokhba revolt in Judaea, ending Jewish political independence.
- **136** — Adopts Lucius Aelius Caesar as his heir; dedicates the Temple of Venus and Roma.
- **138** — Adopts Antoninus Pius as successor; dies on 10 July at Baiae.

## Body

### Early Life and Rise to Power
Born to a senatorial family from Italica, Hadrian was orphaned at ten and became a ward of his cousin, the future emperor Trajan. He pursued a traditional military and political career (*cursus honorum*), serving as a military tribune and later as a liaison to the Senate. His marriage to Trajan's grandniece, Vibia Sabina, was likely arranged by Trajan's wife, Plotina, who may have favored Hadrian as successor. Trajan died in 117 without a clear, legally sound adoption; Hadrian's succession was secured by a document signed by Plotina, leading to rumors of illegitimacy.

### Emperor: Policy and Travels
Hadrian immediately reversed Trajan's expansionist policies, withdrawing from Mesopotamia and other indefensible territories to focus on stabilizing borders. He spent over half his reign traveling the provinces, intervening directly in local affairs, and commissioning major building projects like Hadrian's Wall and the rebuilding of the Pantheon in Rome. His travels aimed to foster a unified, cosmopolitan empire with a shared Hellenic culture, supporting provincial towns and promoting Greek cities like Athens. This policy alienated Roman traditionalists and the Senate, who saw him as remote and authoritarian.

### Military and Administrative Reforms
Hadrian's military strategy emphasized defense and preparedness, developing permanent border fortifications (*limites*) and using specialized auxiliary troops (*numeri*). He systematized Roman law through the jurist Salvius Julianus, creating the Perpetual Edict and establishing a permanent imperial legal council (*consilia principis*), which shifted power from the Senate to a new equestrian bureaucracy. He codified social hierarchy, distinguishing between the privileged *honestiores* and the lower-status *humiliores*, and enacted reforms limiting the abuse of slaves.

### Cultural Interests and the Antinous Cult
A passionate Philhellene (lover of Greek culture), Hadrian promoted Athens as a cultural capital and established the Panhellenion, a league of Greek cities. His deep grief over the death of his lover, the Greek youth Antinous, led to the creation of a widespread, popular cult in Antinous's honor, with temples and festivals across the empire. He was also a prolific builder, hunter, and poet, and was the first Roman emperor to wear a beard, setting a fashion for centuries.

### Final Years and Succession
Hadrian's last years were marked by chronic illness and a troubled succession. After his first adopted heir, Lucius Aelius Caesar, died in 138, he adopted Antoninus Pius, requiring him to adopt Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus as future heirs. Hadrian died in 138 and was deified by the Senate only after pressure from Antoninus, reflecting their enduring hostility.

## Terms
- **Cursus honorum**: The sequential order of public offices held by aspiring politicians in the Roman Republic and Empire.
- **Philhellene**: A person who admires or supports Greek culture, language, and history.
- **Limites (limes)**: The fortified borders or frontiers of the Roman Empire.
- **Numeri**: Ethnic, non-citizen auxiliary troops in the Roman army, often used for specialized, low-intensity warfare.
- **Consilia principis**: The permanent legal advisory council of the Roman emperor, staffed by salaried aides.
- **Honestiores**: A legal classification for Roman citizens of high status (senators, equestrians, etc.) who received lighter punishments.
- **Humiliores**: A legal classification for Roman citizens of low status, who faced harsher punishments for the same offenses.
- **Panhellenion**: A league of Greek cities established by Hadrian, centered in Athens, to promote a unified Hellenic culture.
- **Perpetual Edict**: A codification of Roman law under Hadrian that fixed the legal actions of praetors into permanent statutes.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Legitimacy of Succession**: The exact circumstances of Trajan's adoption of Hadrian are disputed; ancient sources conflict on whether the adoption document was genuine or fabricated by Plotina.
- **Causes of the Bar Kokhba Revolt**: While traditionally linked to Hadrian's ban on circumcision, modern scholars debate this, suggesting other factors like heavy-handed administration, land grants to Roman colonists, and messianic fervor were primary causes.
- **Character and Motives**: Historians debate whether Hadrian was a benevolent, cosmopolitan reformer or a secretive, authoritarian autocrat. His relationship with the Senate is consistently portrayed as hostile, but the depth of his personal cruelty versus political necessity is analyzed differently.
- **Antinous's Death**: The circumstances of Antinous's drowning in the Nile in 130 are unknown, with theories ranging from accident and suicide to ritual sacrifice.
