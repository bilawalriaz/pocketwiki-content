# Karl Marx

## Overview
Karl Marx (1818–1883) was a German philosopher, economist, and revolutionary socialist whose analysis of capitalism and theory of historical materialism became the foundation of Marxism. He argued that human history is driven by class struggle rooted in the material conditions of production, predicting that capitalism’s internal contradictions—specifically the exploitation of the proletariat by the bourgeoisie—would inevitably lead to its collapse and replacement by a classless, stateless communist society. Co-authoring *The Communist Manifesto* (1848) with Friedrich Engels and writing the critique of political economy *Das Kapital* (1867–1894), Marx provided the theoretical framework for revolutionary movements that shaped the 20th century, while his methodological innovations established him as a principal architect of modern sociology and social science.

## Timeline
- **5 May 1818** — Born in Trier, Kingdom of Prussia, to Heinrich Marx and Henriette Pressburg.
- **1841** — Earns Ph.D. in philosophy from University of Jena with thesis on Democritean vs. Epicurean philosophy.
- **1842–1843** — Edits *Rheinische Zeitung* in Cologne; paper suppressed by Prussian government under Russian pressure.
- **1843–1845** — Lives in Paris; meets Friedrich Engels (Aug 1844); writes *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844* and *Theses on Feuerbach*; expelled by French government.
- **1845–1848** — Resides in Brussels; writes *The German Ideology* (unpublished in lifetime) and *The Poverty of Philosophy*; joins Communist League.
- **21 Feb 1848** — *The Communist Manifesto* published in London.
- **1848–1849** — Returns to Cologne during Revolutions of 1848; edits *Neue Rheinische Zeitung*; expelled from Prussia, then France; moves to London.
- **1852–1862** — Serves as European correspondent for the *New-York Daily Tribune*.
- **1859** — Publishes *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*.
- **1864** — Elected to General Council of the International Workingmen's Association (First International).
- **1867** — Publishes Volume I of *Das Kapital*.
- **1871** — Writes *The Civil War in France* defending the Paris Commune.
- **1875** — Writes *Critique of the Gotha Programme*.
- **14 Mar 1883** — Dies in London, stateless; buried in Highgate Cemetery.
- **1885, 1894** — Engels publishes Volumes II and III of *Das Kapital* posthumously.

## Body

### Intellectual Formation and Early Radicalism
Marx’s intellectual trajectory began in the Young Hegelian circles of Berlin, where he adopted Hegel’s dialectical method but inverted its idealism, arguing that material conditions shape consciousness rather than the reverse. His 1841 doctoral thesis on ancient materialism signaled a break with theology. Barred from an academic career by Prussian repression of the Young Hegelians, Marx turned to radical journalism, editing the *Rheinische Zeitung* until its 1843 suppression. In Paris (1843–1845), he synthesized three core influences: Hegel’s dialectics, French utopian socialism (Saint-Simon, Fourier), and British political economy (Smith, Ricardo). Meeting Friedrich Engels in 1844 cemented a lifelong collaboration; Engels’ study of the English working class convinced Marx that the proletariat was the revolutionary agent. During this period, Marx drafted the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*, detailing alienated labour, and the *Theses on Feuerbach* (1845), which formulated historical materialism: "philosophers have only interpreted the world... the point is to change it."

### The Communist Manifesto and the 1848 Revolutions
Expelled from France, Marx settled in Brussels (1845–1848), where he and Engels wrote *The German Ideology* (published 1932), breaking definitively with Young Hegelian idealism and "true socialism" to establish materialism as history’s motor force. They joined the Communist League, transforming it from a secret society into an open political party. *The Communist Manifesto* (1848) declared that "the history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles," predicting the bourgeoisie would produce its own "grave-diggers" in the proletariat. During the 1848 Revolutions, Marx returned to Cologne, editing the *Neue Rheinische Zeitung* and advocating a tactical alliance with the bourgeoisie against feudalism. After the revolutions’ defeat, he was expelled from Prussia and France, settling permanently in London in 1849.

### London Exile, Journalism, and *Das Kapital*
In London, Marx lived in poverty sustained largely by Engels’ subsidies. He served as European correspondent for the *New-York Daily Tribune* (1852–1862), writing on global politics, the American Civil War, and Indian colonialism. The 1857 global economic crisis (Panic of 1857) validated his theory of capitalism’s cyclical crises. Marx spent decades in the British Museum researching political economy. He published *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859) and *Das Kapital* Volume I (1867), analyzing capital’s "laws of motion": the extraction of surplus value from labour power, the tendency of the rate of profit to fall, and the system’s inherent instability. Volumes II and III were edited by Engels posthumously (1885, 1894). The *Grundrisse* notebooks (1857–58) and *Theories of Surplus Value* (published later as a "fourth volume") contain extensive drafts of this critique.

### The First International and Late Political Theory
Marx was a leading figure in the International Workingmen's Association (First International, 1864–1876), battling the anarchist faction led by Mikhail Bakunin over revolutionary strategy and state power. The 1871 Paris Commune—where workers seized Paris for two months—prompted Marx’s *The Civil War in France*, hailing the Commune as the prototype of the "dictatorship of the proletariat" (a transitional workers' state). In the *Critique of the Gotha Programme* (1875), he criticized compromises with Ferdinand Lassalle’s state socialism, distinguishing a lower phase of communism ("from each according to ability, to each according to work") from a higher phase ("from each according to ability, to each according to need"). In correspondence with Vera Zasulich (1881), he contemplated whether Russia’s peasant commune (*mir*) could bypass capitalism, concluding it required insulation from external capitalist pressures.

### Core Theoretical Concepts
**Historical Materialism:** Societies develop through modes of production (forces + relations of production). The economic base (production relations) determines the superstructure (law, politics, ideology). Transitions occur when productive forces outgrow existing relations, causing revolutionary rupture (e.g., feudalism to capitalism).
**Class Struggle & Exploitation:** Under capitalism, the bourgeoisie owns the means of production; the proletariat sells labour power. Surplus value—the difference between the value labour creates and the wage paid—is the source of profit and exploitation. This relation generates alienation: workers lose control over their product, labour process, species-being, and other humans.
**Ideology & False Consciousness:** The ruling class controls the production of ideas, presenting particular interests as universal. Commodity fetishism obscures social relations behind thing-like market relations.
**Revolution & Transition:** Capitalism’s contradictions (crises, falling profit rate, proletarian immiseration) foster class consciousness. The proletariat must seize state power (dictatorship of the proletariat) to socialize the means of production, abolishing classes and the state. Marx allowed for peaceful transition in strong democracies (Britain, US, Netherlands) but deemed force necessary where peaceful means were blocked.

## Terms
- ****Historical Materialism**** — Marx’s theory that material economic conditions (the mode of production) fundamentally determine social structure, political institutions, and consciousness; history progresses through class struggles rooted in these conditions.
- ****Mode of Production**** — The unity of *forces of production* (technology, labour, raw materials) and *relations of production* (property relations, class structure); distinct historical epochs (feudalism, capitalism) are defined by their specific modes.
- ****Bourgeoisie / Proletariat**** — The two main classes under capitalism: the bourgeoisie owns the means of production; the proletariat, lacking means, must sell its labour power for wages.
- ****Surplus Value**** — The value created by workers beyond the value of their labour power (wages); appropriated by the capitalist as profit, it is the basis of exploitation in Marx’s critique of political economy.
- ****Alienation (Entfremdung)**** — The separation of the worker from the product of labour, the labour process, their "species-being" (creative human essence), and other humans; a structural result of wage labour under capitalism.
- ****Commodity Fetishism**** — The perception of social relations between people as objective, thing-like relations between commodities; masks the exploitation inherent in the wage relation.
- ****Base and Superstructure**** — The economic base (relations of production) conditions the superstructure (legal, political, religious, artistic, philosophical forms); the superstructure in turn stabilizes the base.
- ****Dictatorship of the Proletariat**** — The transitional revolutionary state in which the working class holds political power to suppress bourgeois resistance and socialize the means of production, preceding a classless, stateless communist society.
- ****Ideology**** — Ideas that reflect the interests of a ruling class but appear universal and eternal; serves to legitimize the existing social order and obscure exploitation.
- ****Primitive Accumulation**** — The historical process (enclosures, colonialism, slavery) separating producers from the means of production, creating the propertyless proletariat necessary for capitalism’s emergence.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Determinism vs. Agency:** Scholars dispute whether Marx’s "laws of motion" imply rigid economic determinism (inevitable collapse) or a tendency requiring conscious proletarian agency (the "point is to change it").
*   **The "Transformation Problem":** A technical debate in Marxian economics concerning the mathematical consistency of transforming labour values into prices of production in Volume III of *Das Kapital*.
*   **Nature of the Dictatorship of the Proletariat:** Whether Marx envisioned a democratic workers' state (Paris Commune model) or a centralized party dictatorship; later Marxist-Leninist practice diverged significantly from his sparse descriptions.
*   **Russia and Non-Western Paths:** Marx’s late letters (e.g., to Vera Zasulich) suggest the Russian *mir* might bypass capitalism, contradicting his stagist schema; debate persists on whether this was a theoretical revision or a tactical concession.
*   **Ecological Critique:** Modern scholars (e.g., John Bellamy Foster) argue Marx developed a metabolic rift theory of ecological crisis; others contend his productivism mirrors capitalist growth obsession.
*   **Antisemitism Allegations:** "On the Jewish Question" (1844) uses antisemitic tropes to critique bourgeois society; scholars debate whether this reflects personal prejudice, a rhetorical strategy targeting Bruno Bauer, or an immanent critique of liberal emancipation.
*   **Legacy of 20th-Century States:** The relationship between Marx’s theory and the authoritarian practices of states claiming Marxism (USSR, Maoist China) remains contested: distortion, logical outcome, or product of specific material conditions (backwardness, war, encirclement).
