# Moldavia

## Overview
Moldavia was a historical principality in Eastern Europe, existing from the 14th century until 1859. It was a key state in the region, initially independent and later an autonomous vassal of the Ottoman Empire. Its territory, centered between the Eastern Carpathians and the Dniester River, is now divided among Romania, the Republic of Moldova, and Ukraine. Moldavia's history is defined by its struggle for autonomy, cultural development, and eventual union with Wallachia to form the basis of modern Romania.

## Timeline
- **c. 1359** — Bogdan I crosses the Carpathians, establishing an independent Moldavian principality.
- **1457–1504** — Reign of Stephen the Great, a period of military success and cultural flourishing.
- **1484** — Stephen the Great is forced to cede Chilia and Cetatea Albă to the Ottomans.
- **1711** — Dimitrie Cantemir's anti-Ottoman revolt fails; leads to the Phanariote era of Greek-appointed princes.
- **1775** — The Habsburg Empire annexes the northern region of Bukovina.
- **1812** — The Russian Empire annexes the eastern half of Moldavia, Bessarabia.
- **1829** — The Treaty of Adrianople ends Russian occupation and grants Moldavia autonomy, including its own military.
- **1859** — Alexandru Ioan Cuza is elected prince of both Moldavia and Wallachia, creating a personal union.
- **1862** — The United Principalities are formally created, ending the Principality of Moldavia's independent existence.

## Body

### Foundation and Early History
The principality was founded in the mid-14th century by Vlach (Romanian) leaders from Maramureș. Bogdan I established independence from Hungary in 1359. The state was initially divided into Țara de Sus ("Upper Land") and Țara de Jos ("Lower Land"). Early princes navigated conflicts with Hungary, Poland, and the Golden Horde. The region saw early Christian communities and was home to various peoples, including Vlachs, Slavs, and later, German and Armenian settlers.

### The Golden Age and Ottoman Vassalage
Under Stephen the Great (1457–1504), Moldavia reached its peak, successfully defending against Ottoman, Hungarian, and Polish invasions, most notably at the Battle of Vaslui (1475). However, after 1484, he was forced to accept Ottoman suzerainty. The 16th and 17th centuries saw increasing Ottoman control, economic decline, and political instability. The Phanariote era (1711–1822) brought Greek princes appointed by the Ottoman Porte, marked by corruption and high taxes, but also some Enlightenment-inspired reforms like the abolition of serfdom in 1749.

### Fragmentation and Union
The 18th and 19th centuries saw Moldavia lose territory: Bukovina to the Habsburgs (1775) and Bessarabia to Russia (1812). After the Russo-Turkish War (1828–1829), Russian influence grew, introducing the modernizing Organic Statute. A nationalist movement, the Partida Națională, campaigned for union with Wallachia. Following the 1848 revolutions and the Crimean War, the Great Powers permitted ad hoc assemblies. In 1859, Alexandru Ioan Cuza was elected prince of both principalities, creating a personal union that evolved into the United Principalities of Romania in 1862.

### Society and Economy
Moldavian society was hierarchical, with a boyar nobility, free peasants (răzeși), and serfs. Slavery of Roma and Tatar peoples existed until its abolition in stages during the 1840s and 1850s. The economy was agricultural, based on grain and cattle trade, but suffered under Ottoman monopolies and heavy taxation. The military relied on light cavalry tactics and, later, mercenaries. A fleet was established on the Danube after 1829.

## Terms
- **Voivode/Prince**: The ruler of the principality.
- **Boyars**: The land-owning aristocracy.
- **Suzerainty**: A relationship where a state has control over another's foreign policy, while allowing internal autonomy.
- **Phanariotes**: Greek administrators from the Phanar district of Constantinople who ruled the Danubian Principalities under Ottoman auspices (1711–1822).
- **Organic Statute**: A constitutional framework imposed by Russia (1831) that modernized administration in the principalities.
- **Partida Națională**: A trans-boundary political group campaigning for the union of Moldavia and Wallachia.
- **Răzeși**: Freeholding yeomen peasants with land rights.
- **Robi**: Slaves, primarily of Roma ethnicity, in Moldavian society.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The ethnic identity of the early medieval Bolohoveni people is debated; Romanian scholars identify them as Vlachs, while archaeological evidence suggests they were Slavic.
- The exact etymology of the name "Moldavia" is unknown, with multiple competing theories (legendary, Gothic, Slavic, or from a local landowner).
- The historical population of the principality in the 15th century is estimated broadly between 250,000 and 600,000, as no census was conducted until 1769–1774.
