# Universe

## Overview

The universe is the totality of all existence: all matter, energy, space, and time, from sub-atomic particles to entire galactic filaments. Since the early 20th century, cosmology has established that the universe began in a hot, dense state called the Big Bang about 13.8 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since. The observable portion is about 93 billion light-years in diameter, but the total size is unknown. Modern cosmology describes a universe composed mostly of mysterious dark energy and dark matter, with ordinary matter—stars, planets, and life—making up only a small fraction.

## Timeline

- **c. 3000–1200 BCE** — Earliest written records of astronomy from Ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia
- **4th c. BCE** — Eudoxus proposes first coherent geocentric model with 27 celestial spheres
- **3rd c. BCE** — Aristarchus of Samos proposes the first known heliocentric model
- **1543 era** — Copernicus revives heliocentrism, placing the Sun at the center
- **1915** — Einstein formulates general relativity, beginning modern physical cosmology
- **1917** — Einstein applies general relativity to model the universe's structure
- **1922–1923** — Hubble proves Andromeda and Triangulum are galaxies outside the Milky Way
- **1998** — Two groups measure the deceleration parameter as negative (~−0.55), showing expansion is accelerating
- **2015** — Best value for the age of the universe: 13.799 ± 0.021 billion years

## Body

### Definition and etymology

The physical universe has been defined as "The totality of all space and time; all that is, has been, and will be." Some philosophers include ideas and abstract concepts like mathematics in the definition. The word derives from the Latin *universus*, meaning 'combined into one'. Ancient Greek synonyms included *to pan* ('the all'), *to holon* ('all things'), and *ho kosmos* ('the world, the cosmos').

### Chronology and the Big Bang

The prevailing model is the Big Bang theory, based on general relativity and assumptions of homogeneity and isotropy. The earliest state was extremely hot and dense; the universe cooled during expansion. An intense period called cosmic inflation is postulated to explain observations and set initial conditions for the Lambda-CDM model (the standard model with a cosmological constant and cold dark matter). Within the first fraction of a second, all Standard Model particles were in equilibrium. As the universe cooled, it underwent phase transitions analogous to water freezing. About one minute after the Big Bang, protons and neutrons combined via Big Bang nucleosynthesis for about 15 minutes, producing helium, small amounts of deuterium, and traces of lithium.

After about 377,000 years, the universe cooled enough for electrons and nuclei to form the first stable atoms—called recombination for historical reasons. The universe became transparent, and the released photons form the cosmic microwave background (CMB). Around 47,000 years, matter's energy density exceeded that of radiation, ending the radiation-dominated era. Tiny density fluctuations led dark matter to concentrate, attracting ordinary matter to form the first stars (Population III) after 100–300 million years. These massive, short-lived stars reionized the universe between about 200–500 million and 1 billion years and seeded heavier elements. After about 9.8 billion years, dark energy's density exceeded matter's, beginning the present dark-energy-dominated era in which expansion accelerates.

### Physical properties

The observable universe has a proper distance of 46 billion light-years from Earth to its edge, giving a diameter of about 93 billion light-years. This exceeds 13.8 billion light-years because the edge and Earth have moved apart. The Milky Way is roughly 87,400 light-years in diameter; Andromeda is about 2.5 million light-years away. Whether the total universe is finite or infinite is unknown.

The expansion is inferred from the redshift of distant galaxies. Analyses of Type Ia supernovae indicate the expansion is accelerating. The mass–energy density, equivalent to about 5 protons per cubic meter, allowed expansion for 13.8 billion years. Before 1998, cosmologists expected the deceleration parameter to be positive; it was measured at approximately −0.55, implying the scale factor's second derivative has been positive for the last 5–6 billion years.

Modern physics organizes events into spacetime, originating with special relativity. Observers moving relative to each other disagree on time and distance between events but agree on the speed of light and the interval (c²T² − D²). General relativity explains gravity as curvature of spacetime caused by energy content; orbits are bodies falling freely through curved background. The Einstein field equations relate matter distribution to spacetime curvature. The universe appears to be a smooth continuum of three spatial dimensions and one time dimension.

The density parameter Omega (Ω) determines spatial geometry: flat, open, or closed if Ω equals, is less than, or is greater than 1. Observations from COBE, WMAP, and Planck suggest the universe is infinite in extent with finite age, supporting flat FLRW (Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker) models.

### Composition

The mass–energy density is about 68% dark energy, 27% dark matter, and 5% ordinary matter. Neutrinos contribute less than 0.3% and electromagnetic radiation about 0.005%. The universe has 10 billion times more matter than antimatter; the cause of this baryon asymmetry is unknown. Average density is about 1 proton per 200 litres, with vast voids of exceptionally low density.

Dark energy is an unknown form of energy hypothesized to permeate space, causing accelerated expansion. Its density (~7 × 10⁻³⁰ g/cm³) is much less than ordinary matter within galaxies but dominates because it is uniform. Proposed forms include the cosmological constant (constant energy density, equivalent to vacuum energy) and scalar fields like quintessence or moduli.

Dark matter is invisible to the entire electromagnetic spectrum but inferred from gravitational effects. It constitutes about 26.8% of total mass–energy and 84.5% of total matter. Other than neutrinos, it has not been detected directly.

Ordinary matter (4.9% of mass–energy) includes atoms, ions, electrons, stars, and interstellar gas. Visible stars and gas account for less than 10% of ordinary matter. It exists in states including solid, liquid, gas, plasma, and condensates. It is composed of quarks and leptons: protons (two up quarks, one down quark), neutrons (two down, one up), and electrons (a lepton). Big Bang nucleosynthesis formed light elements up to lithium and beryllium within about 20 minutes; heavier elements came from stellar and supernova nucleosynthesis.

Elementary particles are described by quantum mechanics and exhibit wave–particle duality. The Standard Model covers electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions, predicting the Higgs boson. It does not accommodate gravity. Hadrons (baryons like protons and neutrons, mesons like pions) formed during the hadron epoch from about 10⁻⁶ seconds after the Big Bang. Leptons (electrons, muons, taus, neutrinos) dominated during the lepton epoch starting about 1 second after the Big Bang. The photon epoch began about 10 seconds after the Big Bang; about 380,000 years later, photons decoupled, forming the CMB whose tiny temperature variations were the seeds of all structure.

### Cosmological models

General relativity (1915) describes gravity as spacetime curvature. Under the cosmological principle, the Friedmann equations govern the scale factor's evolution. Curvature determines global geometry: positive curvature gives a finite universe (like a 3D sphere); flat or negative curvature gives a spatially infinite universe. The fate depends on curvature and the cosmological constant: a sufficiently dense positive-curvature universe would recollapse in a Big Crunch; flat or negatively curved universes expand indefinitely toward a Big Freeze and heat death. Observations suggest the universe is close to flat, with accelerating expansion possibly leading to a Big Rip.

Multiverse hypotheses propose our universe is one of many disconnected spacetimes. Max Tegmark and Brian Greene have proposed classification schemes; Tegmark calculated our nearest "doppelgänger" is 10^10^115 metres away. The physical basis of these ideas has been challenged.

### Historical conceptions

Many cultures have creation myths: world eggs (Kalevala, Pangu, Brahmanda Purana), single creators (Genesis, Atum, Coatlicue), unions of deities (Rangi and Papa), crafting from corpses (Tiamat, Ymir), or from fundamental principles (Brahman and Prakrti).

Pre-Socratic Greeks proposed primordial materials (arche): Thales (water), Anaximander (apeiron), Anaximenes (air), Heraclitus (fire), Empedocles (four elements), and Democritus (atoms in a void). Parmenides emphasized changelessness; Zeno posed motion paradoxes. Indian philosopher Kanada developed atomism; Buddhist Dignāga proposed point-sized, durationless atoms of energy. Temporal finitism was inspired by Abrahamic creation doctrines; John Philoponus argued against infinite past, followed by Al-Kindi, Saadia Gaon, and Al-Ghazali.

Early astronomy came from Egypt and Mesopotamia (c. 3000–1200 BCE). Greek models were geocentric: Eudoxus used 27 spheres, Aristotle 55. Aristarchus first proposed heliocentrism; Seleucus later supported it, possibly via tides. The Aristotelian model dominated for two millennia until Copernicus revived heliocentrism. Newton unified earthly and celestial laws. Olbers' paradox noted an infinite star-filled universe would make the night sky bright; the Jeans instability (1902) clarified gravitational collapse issues. Kant speculated nebulae were separate galaxies; Hubble proved this in 1922–1923 using Cepheid variables, establishing the universe contains many galaxies.

## Terms

- **Big Bang**: The prevailing model of the universe's origin as an extremely hot, dense state that expanded and cooled.
- **Cosmic microwave background (CMB)**: The photons released when atoms first formed, still observable today as highly isotropic microwave radiation at ~2.72548 kelvins.
- **Dark energy**: An unknown form of energy permeating space, causing the expansion of the universe to accelerate.
- **Dark matter**: Hypothetical matter invisible to the electromagnetic spectrum, inferred from gravitational effects; constitutes most of the universe's matter.
- **Lambda-CDM model**: The standard cosmological model with a cosmological constant (Lambda) and cold dark matter.
- **Cosmological principle**: The hypothesis that the large-scale universe is homogeneous and isotropic, looking the same from all vantage points.
- **General relativity**: Einstein's geometric theory of gravity (1915) describing gravity as curvature of spacetime.
- **Redshift**: The stretching of light from distant galaxies indicating they are receding from us.
- **Nucleosynthesis**: The formation of atomic nuclei, either in the Big Bang (producing light elements) or in stars (producing heavier elements).
- **Observable universe**: The spatial region from which light can reach us, limited by the particle horizon; about 93 billion light-years in diameter.

## Debates and open questions

Many competing hypotheses exist about the ultimate fate of the universe and about what, if anything, preceded the Big Bang. The nature of both dark energy and dark matter is unknown. The cause of the observed baryon asymmetry (10 billion times more matter than antimatter) is not known. The fine-tuned universe hypothesis—that life requires fundamental constants to lie within a narrow range—is debated, including whether the question is even logically meaningful. The physical basis of multiverse ideas has been challenged. Whether the total universe is finite or infinite remains unknown.
