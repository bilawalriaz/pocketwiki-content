# Partition of India

## Overview
The Partition of India in 1947 was the division of British India into two independent dominions, India and Pakistan, based on religious majorities. It was a pivotal event that ended British colonial rule, triggered one of the largest mass migrations in history, and created enduring geopolitical tensions in South Asia.

## Timeline
- **1905** — Partition of Bengal by Viceroy Curzon, sparking nationalist protests.
- **1906** — Founding of the All-India Muslim League in Dacca.
- **1916** — Lucknow Pact: Congress and Muslim League agree on a joint self-government proposal.
- **1937** — Provincial elections: Congress forms governments in most provinces; Muslim League performs poorly in Muslim-majority regions.
- **1940** — Lahore Resolution: Muslim League formally demands independent Muslim-majority states.
- **1942** — Quit India Resolution: Congress launches civil disobedience; its leaders are jailed.
- **1945** — Labour Party wins UK election, deciding to decolonize India.
- **1946** — Provincial elections: Muslim League wins majority of Muslim seats. Cabinet Mission Plan proposed and later collapses.
- **1946** — Direct Action Day (16 August) leads to the Great Calcutta Killings and widespread communal violence.
- **1947** — 3 June: Mountbatten announces partition plan. 14-15 August: India and Pakistan gain independence.
- **1947** — 17 August: Radcliffe Line boundary is published.
- **1971** — Secession of East Pakistan as Bangladesh (a later partition of Pakistan, not India).

## Body

### Background and Political Developments
The idea of a separate Muslim homeland evolved over decades. The 1905 Partition of Bengal and the founding of the Muslim League in 1906 marked early political organization. The two-nation theory—the assertion that Hindus and Muslims are distinct nations—became the ideological foundation for the Pakistan Movement. Political deadlock intensified after the 1937 provincial elections, where the Congress's refusal to share power with the League in the United Provinces alienated Muslim elites. During World War II, the Muslim League's support for the British war effort while Congress leaders were jailed allowed it to consolidate its claim as the sole representative of Indian Muslims.

### The Path to Partition
The 1945 UK election brought a Labour government committed to decolonization. The 1946 Indian elections gave the Muslim League a mandate for Pakistan. The Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946 proposed a loose federation to keep India united, but it collapsed after Congress leader Jawaharlal Nehru rejected the grouping of provinces. The subsequent Direct Action Day by the League triggered horrific communal violence, notably the Great Calcutta Killings, which escalated into a cycle of retaliatory massacres across northern India. By 1947, the last Viceroy, Lord Mountbatten, concluded that a swift partition was necessary to avoid civil war.

### The Partition and Its Violence
The Mountbatten Plan of 3 June 1947 formalized the division. The Radcliffe Line, drawn hastily by Sir Cyril Radcliffe, partitioned the provinces of Punjab and Bengal along district-wide religious majorities. The announcement of the boundary on 17 August, two days after independence, exacerbated chaos. The partition displaced between 12 and 20 million people, creating a massive refugee crisis. Estimates of deaths range from 200,000 to 2 million, with most scholars accepting approximately 1 million. The worst violence occurred in Punjab, where organized ethnic cleansing led to the near-total removal of religious minorities on both sides of the new border. Violence also erupted in Bengal, Sindh, Delhi, and princely states like Jammu and Kashmir.

### Aftermath and Legacy
The new governments were unprepared for the scale of migration and violence. Millions of refugees were resettled, with India building new housing colonies in cities like Delhi and Pakistan receiving the majority of Muslims in its western and eastern wings. The partition created a legacy of hostility between India and Pakistan, leading to multiple wars and the ongoing Kashmir conflict. It also left a profound cultural impact, inspiring extensive literature, film, and art that continue to explore the trauma and human cost of the division.

## Terms
- **Partition of India**: The 1947 division of British India into the independent dominions of India and Pakistan.
- **Two-Nation Theory**: The ideology that Hindus and Muslims are two distinct nations, forming the basis for the demand for Pakistan.
- **Muslim League**: Political party that became the primary advocate for a separate Muslim homeland.
- **Cabinet Mission Plan (1946)**: A British proposal for a united, federal India with grouped provinces; its collapse made partition likely.
- **Direct Action Day**: 16 August 1946, called by the Muslim League, which triggered the Great Calcutta Killings and widespread communal violence.
- **Mountbatten Plan (3 June Plan)**: The final British proposal for the partition of India and the timeline for independence.
- **Radcliffe Line**: The boundary drawn by Sir Cyril Radcliffe to divide Punjab and Bengal between India and Pakistan.
- **Princely States**: Semi-autonomous territories under British suzerainty, given the choice to join India, Pakistan, or remain independent at partition.
- **Dyarchy**: A system of dual government introduced by the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms (1919), where some subjects were transferred to Indian ministers while others remained with British officials.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **British Responsibility**: Scholars debate whether the British "divide and rule" policy inevitably led to partition or if it was a result of intractable Hindu-Muslim differences and political failures.
- **Haste of Withdrawal**: Historians question whether Lord Mountbatten's rushed timeline (advancing independence from June 1948 to August 1947) unnecessarily increased the scale of violence and suffering.
- **Inevitability**: Some argue partition was not inevitable but became so due to the breakdown of the Cabinet Mission Plan and the escalation of communal violence after 1946.
- **Role of Popular Imagination**: Historian Venkat Dhulipala suggests Pakistan was popularly imagined as a "New Medina" (an ideal Islamic state), indicating broader social support beyond elite politics.
- **Impact on Women**: The partition led to mass abduction and rape of women; post-war recovery efforts were fraught, and some scholars note complex, sometimes emancipatory, effects on women's social roles in the aftermath.
