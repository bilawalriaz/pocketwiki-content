# Wales

## Overview
Wales is a country and constituent nation of the United Kingdom, on Great Britain's western side. It has a distinct Celtic cultural and linguistic identity, shaped by periods of independence, conquest, and modern devolution. Its history features native kingdoms, English conquest, industrial transformation, and a contemporary political settlement granting self-governance.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Permanent settlement begins after the last ice age.
- **5th century** — Roman withdrawal leads to distinct Welsh culture among Celtic Britons.
- **c. 942** — Hywel Dda unites most of Wales and codifies Welsh law.
- **1055–1063** — Gruffudd ap Llywelyn briefly unites all of Wales.
- **1283** — Edward I completes conquest; Statute of Rhuddlan ends independence.
- **1400–1415** — Owain Glyndŵr leads major revolt against English rule.
- **1535–1542** — Laws in Wales Acts annex Wales into England.
- **18th–19th c.** — Industrial Revolution transforms Wales, especially South Wales coalfield.
- **1925** — Plaid Cymru, Welsh nationalist party, is founded.
- **1998** — Government of Wales Act creates devolved assembly (now Senedd).
- **2011** — Referendum grants assembly primary law-making powers.
- **2020** — Assembly renamed Senedd Cymru (Welsh Parliament).

## Body

### History and Identity
Distinct Welsh culture emerged among Celtic Britons after the Roman withdrawal in the 5th century. Various kingdoms followed, with Hywel Dda (c. 942) unifying most of Wales and codifying its laws. Brief unification under Gruffudd ap Llywelyn (1055–1063) preceded Norman incursions and a long struggle between Welsh princes and English kings, ending with Edward I's 1283 conquest. Welsh identity persisted through revolts like Owain Glyndŵr's (1400–1415) and was formally integrated into England by the Laws in Wales Acts (1535–1542). The 19th century saw distinctive Welsh politics evolve from Liberalism to socialism and the Labour Party. Nationalist movements grew in the 20th century, leading to devolution and the Senedd's creation in 1998.

### Geography and Climate
Wales is mountainous, bordered by the Irish Sea, England, the Bristol Channel, and the Celtic Sea. Its landscape was shaped by the last ice age, with highest peaks in Snowdonia (Eryri), including Snowdon (Yr Wyddfa) at 1,085 m. The country has over 2,700 km of coastline and numerous islands, the largest being Anglesey. It has a changeable, maritime climate and is one of Europe's wettest countries. Three national parks and five Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty protect its diverse landscapes.

### Government and Politics
Wales is a country within the United Kingdom, represented in the UK Parliament by 32 MPs. Devolution in 1998 established the unicameral Senedd, holding powers over health, education, and agriculture. The Welsh Government, led by a First Minister, is drawn from and accountable to the Senedd. Wales is divided into 22 principal council areas for local government. The legal system is English law, though the Senedd can pass primary legislation on devolved matters.

### Economy
Wales transitioned from an agricultural to an industrial economy in the 19th century, driven by coal mining and metallurgy. The South Wales Coalfield's exploitation caused rapid population growth. Since heavy industry's decline, the economy has shifted to public sector, services, and tourism. Agriculture is largely livestock-based, making Wales a net exporter of animal produce. In 2018, GDP was £75 billion. Wales is a net exporter of electricity, with over half its energy needs met by renewables in 2021.

### Demographics and Languages
The 2021 census recorded a population of 3.1 million, with two-thirds in South Wales. The population is predominantly white (93.8%), with growing urban ethnic diversity. Both Welsh and English are official languages. English is spoken by almost all, while Welsh is spoken by 17.8% (538,300 people), concentrated in the north and west. The Welsh language saw long decline but has been supported by revitalization efforts since the 20th century.

### Culture and Society
Wales has a distinctive culture with its own language, mythology (e.g., the Mabinogion), and literary tradition dating to the 6th century. Known as "the land of song," it has a strong choral tradition and the annual National Eisteddfod festival. Rugby union is a major sport and national symbol. Key symbols include the red dragon (Y Ddraig Goch), the leek, and the daffodil. Saint David's Day is celebrated on 1 March. Four UNESCO World Heritage Sites are located in Wales.

## Terms
- **Senedd (Senedd Cymru):** Devolved unicameral parliament of Wales, established 1998, renamed 2020.
- **Devolution:** Statutory delegation of powers from UK Parliament to the Senedd.
- **Cymry / Cymru:** Welsh names for the Welsh people and Wales, from a Brythonic word meaning 'fellow-countrymen'.
- **Eisteddfod:** Traditional Welsh festival of literature, music, and performance; the National Eisteddfod is largest.
- **Mabinogion:** Collection of medieval Welsh prose stories, cornerstone of Celtic mythology.
- **Plaid Cymru:** Welsh nationalist political party, founded 1925.
- **Laws in Wales Acts (1535–1542):** Acts annexing Wales, introducing English law, and integrating it into England.
- **Welsh 3000s:** The 14 (or 15) mountains in Wales over 3,000 feet (910 m), in Snowdonia.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent of national identity is debated, with census data showing complex mixes of "Welsh only," "British only," and dual identities, varying by region.
- The future of the Welsh language remains key, with debates over its use in education, public life, and revitalization policy effectiveness.
- The economic impact of devolution and the fiscal relationship with the UK government, including funding and investment debates (e.g., HS2), are ongoing.
