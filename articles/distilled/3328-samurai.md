# Samurai

## Overview
The samurai were Japan's professional warrior class, serving as retainers to feudal lords from the medieval period until the Meiji Restoration. Originating as mounted archers and landowners, they evolved into a hereditary administrative class during the Tokugawa peace, embodying a blend of martial skill, Confucian learning, and bureaucratic duty. Their institutional dissolution in the late 19th century marked Japan's transition to a modern nation-state.

## Timeline
- **792 AD** — Imperial conscript army is dismantled, creating reliance on professional warriors.
- **814–825 AD** — Minamoto and Taira clans are formed from expelled imperial family members.
- **1180–1185** — Genpei War: Minamoto clan defeats the Taira, leading to the first shogunate.
- **1192** — Minamoto no Yoritomo is appointed shogun, establishing the Kamakura shogunate.
- **1467–1477** — Onin War devastates Kyoto, weakening the Ashikaga shogunate and leading to the Sengoku period.
- **1543** — Portuguese introduce matchlock muskets to Japan.
- **1588–1591** — Toyotomi Hideyoshi enacts the Sword Hunt and Separation Edict, codifying the samurai class.
- **1600** — Battle of Sekigahara: Tokugawa Ieyasu's victory leads to Tokugawa rule.
- **1603** — Tokugawa Ieyasu is declared shogun, beginning 250 years of peace.
- **1868** — Meiji Restoration: Shogunate is abolished, beginning the dismantling of samurai status.
- **1877** — Satsuma Rebellion: Last major armed revolt by former samurai is defeated.
- **1947** — Legal status of kazoku (nobility) and shizoku (former samurai) is abolished.

## Body

### Origins and Rise (700–1180 AD)
The samurai emerged as the imperial conscript army was phased out by 792 AD due to its ineffectiveness and unpopularity. Wealthy provincial families who could afford private training in mounted archery became the de facto military force. The growth of tax-exempt estates (shōen) enriched these warrior families, who donated land to avoid taxes. The Minamoto and Taira clans, formed from expelled imperial family members, became powerful warrior houses, setting the stage for direct conflict.

### The Genpei War and First Shogunate (1180–1333)
Rivalry between the Minamoto and Taira clans culminated in the Genpei War (1180–1185). Minamoto no Yoritomo's victory led to the establishment of the Kamakura shogunate in 1192, a parallel military government. Yoritomo created the gokenin class of warrior vassals who served the shogun in exchange for privileges and land recognition. The samurai class proper was a subclass below the gokenin, often serving as sub-vassals.

### Civil War and Unification (1333–1603)
The Ashikaga shogunate (1336–1467) saw the rise of shugo daimyo (military governors) who became powerful regional lords. The Onin War (1467) plunged Japan into the Sengoku period, a century of civil war where social mobility allowed some commoners, like Toyotomi Hideyoshi, to rise to samurai status. The introduction of mass-produced muskets in 1543 transformed warfare, increasing the role of ashigaru (foot soldiers). Unification was achieved by Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and Tokugawa Ieyasu, with Hideyoshi legally codifying the samurai class and separating them from the peasantry.

### Tokugawa Shogunate (1603–1868)
After the Battle of Sekigahara (1600), the Tokugawa shogunate established prolonged peace. Samurai transitioned from warriors to hereditary bureaucrats and administrators in castle towns. Neo-Confucianism emphasized strict social hierarchy and lord-retainer loyalty. The samurai's right to bear two swords became a symbolic emblem of status. Despite the peace, some samurai served as mercenaries in Southeast Asia or as diplomatic ambassadors before Japan's isolation (sakoku).

### Dissolution (1868–1947)
Facing Western pressure, the Tokugawa shogunate fell in 1868. The Meiji government, led by former samurai, dismantled the class's institutional basis through reforms: domains were abolished (1871), stipends commuted, and conscription introduced (1873). Former samurai became kazoku (nobility) or shizoku. Economic hardship led to rebellions, notably the Satsuma Rebellion (1877), whose defeat confirmed the new government's authority. The legal status of shizoku persisted until 1947.

### Culture and Philosophy
Samurai culture blended martial arts with literary and artistic pursuits like waka poetry, noh theater, and the tea ceremony. Bushido, the "way of the warrior," was influenced by Confucianism (emphasizing loyalty), Buddhism (especially Zen, for mental discipline), and Shinto. Literacy was high among the samurai, who maintained personal libraries (buke bunko). Women managed households and were trained in weapons like the naginata for defense, though their primary role was often political marriage.

## Terms
- **Bushi**: The proper Japanese term for a professional warrior.
- **Samurai**: Derived from "saburau" (to serve), meaning a retainer or vassal to a lord.
- **Daimyo**: Feudal lords who ruled domains and commanded samurai retainers.
- **Shogun**: The supreme military ruler, a title held by the head of the dominant samurai clan.
- **Gokenin**: Direct warrior vassals of the shogun, especially in the Kamakura period.
- **Ashigaru**: Low-ranking foot soldiers, often conscripted peasants, who became important in mass warfare.
- **Bushido**: The informal code of conduct and values for the samurai, emphasizing loyalty, honor, and martial skill.
- **Kazoku**: The hereditary peerage created in 1869 for former daimyo and court nobles.
- **Shizoku**: The status group for former samurai after the Meiji Restoration, recognized until 1947.
- **Rōnin**: A samurai without a lord or master.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which Zen Buddhism was truly central to samurai philosophy versus being a later Western projection is debated. Scholars note it was one of many Buddhist schools followed, not necessarily the dominant one for all samurai.
- The historical accuracy and social prevalence of "bushido" as a formal, practiced code during the samurai's heyday is questioned, with much of its codification occurring in the peaceful Edo period.
- The degree to which the samurai's legendary social mobility and meritocracy during the Sengoku period was a reality versus a later mythologization is a point of scholarly discussion.
