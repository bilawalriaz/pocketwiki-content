# Realism (arts)

## Overview
Realism is the attempt to represent subject-matter truthfully, avoiding artificiality, exaggeration, and the supernatural. While often used interchangeably with naturalism, the terms differ: naturalism seeks to depict objects with minimal distortion (tied to linear perspective and illusionism), whereas Realism refers specifically to a mid-19th-century art movement originating in France after the 1848 Revolution. Led by Gustave Courbet, it rejected Romanticism and academic idealization to focus on the mundane lives of commoners, driven by leftist politics. The impulse toward realism—resisting idealization, perfecting illusionism, and depicting ordinary subjects—has a long history across visual arts, literature, theatre, cinema, and opera.

## Timeline
- **4th century BCE** — Sculptor Demetrius of Alopece prefers realism over ideal beauty.
- **5th century BC** — Legend of Zeuxis painting grapes so lifelike birds peck at them (per Pliny).
- **15th century** — Early Netherlandish painters (Campin, van Eyck) achieve high naturalism/illusionism using oil techniques.
- **Early 15th century** — Scientific linear perspective developed in Italy.
- **1434** — Jan van Eyck paints the *Arnolfini Portrait*, detailing middle-class interiors.
- **16th century** — Rise of large-scale genre scenes of working people (Aertsen, Beuckelaer, Bruegel).
- **17th century** — Dutch Golden Age painting refines landscape and light depiction; Caravaggisti use "naturalist" style in Italy.
- **1848** — French Revolution sparks the Realist movement in France.
- **1863** — Critic Jules-Antoine Castagnary coins "Naturalist school" as a quasi-scientific, apolitical offshoot of Realism.
- **1860s–1871** — Peredvizhniki (Wanderers) form in Russia, spreading Realism.
- **Late 19th century** — Literary Realism (Zola, Flaubert, James) and Theatrical Realism (Chekhov, Ibsen) flourish.
- **1890** — Mascagni's *Cavalleria rusticana* marks peak of operatic *verismo*.
- **Post-WWII** — Italian Neorealism (De Sica, Rossellini, Visconti) creates politically oriented cinema.

## Body

### Visual Arts: Definitions and Distinctions
In visual art, "realistic" describes accurate visual appearance (illusionism), while "realist" concerns subject matter. Naturalism (or mimesis/illusionism) is the precise depiction of scenes and objects, peaking in 15th-century Early Netherlandish painting (Robert Campin, Jan van Eyck). Paradoxically, 19th-century Realist painters like Courbet were *less* noted for precise visual rendering than academic painters, who used high skill to depict contrived, imaginary historical scenes. Realism rejected this academic idealization in favor of contemporary, unvarnished reality.

### Resisting Idealization
The tension between observing nature and idealizing forms dates to antiquity. Demetrius of Alopece (4th c. BCE) favored realism; Roman Republican portraiture valued *verism* (truthful depiction), while emperors preferred Greek idealism. In Christian art, a recurring "realism" emphasized Christ’s physical suffering to stimulate meditation, peaking in Late Medieval Germany and Spanish Baroque sculpture. Renaissance theorists debated the balance: Leonardo da Vinci championed pure nature study, while Leon Battista Alberti and Michelangelo advocated selecting ideal beauty. In 17th-century Italy, the debate polarized between the classical-idealist Carracci and the "naturalist" Caravaggisti, who set religious scenes in contemporary backstreets. In the 19th century, Castagnary defined Naturalism as a quasi-scientific, apolitical sub-movement of Realism ("truth balanced with science"), adopted by Émile Zola. The term later became a catch-all for non-academic, non-Impressionist art (Barbizon School, Düsseldorf School, American Regionalism).

### Illusionism: The Technical History
Accurate depiction of anatomy, perspective, light, and color has a long trajectory. Upper Paleolithic art achieved lifelike animals. Greek art advanced anatomy; Roman painting developed effective (if unscientific) perspective and *verism* in portraiture. Late Antiquity rejected illusionism for expressive force. Classical standards returned in the Early Renaissance, aided by oil painting techniques (Netherlands, 15th c.; Italy, 1470s) and scientific perspective (Italy, early 15th c.). Dutch Golden Age painting (17th c.) mastered landscape, weather, and natural light. Still life advanced illusionism, though Netherlandish flower painting conventionally combined blooms from all seasons. Portraiture often idealized subjects until the 19th-century shift toward unvarnished truth.

### Depiction of Ordinary Subjects
Everyday subjects were historically marginal—confined to manuscript margins (drolleries), backgrounds, or small scale—due to art’s cost and religious/political commissioning. Early Netherlandish painting lowered the portrait threshold to prosperous merchants (e.g., *Arnolfini Portrait*, 1434), detailing interiors laden with symbolism. *Labours of the Months* cycles developed landscape and working-class depiction. The 16th century saw large kitchen/market scenes (Pieter Aertsen, Joachim Beuckelaer, Annibale Carracci) and Bruegel’s panoramic peasant life. The 17th century exploded with genre painting across Europe: Dutch subgenres, Italian *Bamboccianti*, Spanish *bodegones*, and unidealized peasants in history paintings (Ribera, Velázquez). 18th-century prints often comic/moralistic; mid-19th century onward emphasized poverty, though painters favored rural over urban poor until Impressionists depicted Paris streets. Industrial subjects appeared rarely (Wright of Derby) until late 19th-century commissions by industrialists. American Realism (early 20th c.) continued this tradition.

### The Realist Movement (Mid-19th Century)
Reacting against Romanticism and History painting, French Realism centered on "real" life: common laborers in ordinary surroundings. Chief exponents: Gustave Courbet, Jean-François Millet, Honoré Daumier, Jean-Baptiste-Camille Corot. They used unprettified detail to depict ordinary contemporary existence, paralleling naturalist literature (Zola, Balzac, Flaubert). Equivalents arose across the West: Russia’s *Peredvizhniki* (Wanderers, 1860s onward) with Ilya Repin and Ivan Shishkin; Britain’s Hubert von Herkomer and Luke Fildes addressing social issues.

### Literature
Literary Realism is "the faithful representation of reality," focusing on middle/lower-class daily life without romantic idealization. It assumes an objective, ontologically independent reality knowable through the senses (roots in Descartes, Locke, Thomas Reid). It reacted against Romanticism and is often derided as "bourgeois realism." Victorian writers practiced it; its rigidities prompted the modernist revolt (c. 1900), which attacked bourgeois social order with anti-rationalist, anti-realist programs.

### Theatre
Theatrical realism emerged in 19th-century European drama, linked to the Industrial Revolution, science, and photography. It directed attention to social/psychological problems of ordinary life, portraying characters as victims of larger forces—ordinary, impotent, lacking answers. Anton Chekhov used "camera works" for uninflected slices of life. Melodramatic and realistic forms blended throughout the 19th–20th centuries. In the US, dramatic realism preceded fictional realism by two decades (late 1870s/1880s), championed by William Dean Howells and Henry James. Post-WWII, theatrical realism collapsed into nihilism and the absurd.

### Cinema
Italian Neorealism (post-WWII: De Sica, Visconti, Rossellini) created politically oriented cinema using realist approaches. Two film realisms exist: **Seamless realism** uses narrative/technique to create a "reality effect"; **Aesthetic realism** (French 1930s, André Bazin 1950s) acknowledges film cannot fix meaning, using location shooting, natural light, and non-professionals to let viewers choose readings. Aesthetic realists employ long shots, deep focus, and eye-level angles to reduce manipulation. French *cinéma vérité* (1960s), British/French/German New Waves (1950s/60s "kitchen sink" dramas), and Siegfried Kracauer’s theory (realism as cinema’s primary function) further developed the tradition.

### Opera
*Verismo* (post-Romantic, 1890s–early 1900s) brought literary naturalism (Zola, Flaubert, Ibsen) to opera. Italian composers (Mascagni, Leoncavallo, Giordano, Cilea, Puccini) presented gritty, flawed lower-class protagonists in true-to-life drama. Mascagni’s *Cavalleria rusticana* (1890) and Leoncavallo’s *Pagliacci* (infidelity, revenge, violence) are landmarks. Verdi’s *Luisa Miller* and *La traviata* are seen as precursors. In Britain, Gilbert and Sullivan’s *Iolanthe* offered a realistic depiction of nobility amid fantastical elements.

## Terms
- ****Naturalism**** — In visual art, the precise, detailed depiction of objects with minimal distortion; tied to linear perspective and illusionism. In 19th-century theory, a quasi-scientific, apolitical offshoot of Realism.
- ****Illusionism**** — The accurate rendering of visual appearances (anatomy, perspective, light, color) to create a lifelike effect.
- ****Mimesis**** — Synonym for naturalism/illusionism: the imitation of nature in art.
- ****Verism**** — Roman Republican commitment to truthful, unidealized portraiture.
- ****Idealization**** — The artistic practice of correcting nature to conform to standards of beauty (e.g., Greek models), opposed by Realism.
- ****Genre painting**** — Depictions of everyday life and ordinary people at work or leisure, prominent in 17th-century Dutch art and 19th-century Realism.
- ****Realist movement**** — Mid-19th-century French movement (Courbet, Millet) rejecting Romanticism/academicism for unvarnished contemporary subjects, often politically motivated.
- ****Verismo**** — Late 19th-century Italian operatic style adopting literary naturalism, featuring lower-class protagonists and gritty drama.
- ****Italian Neorealism**** — Post-WWII cinematic movement (De Sica, Rossellini) using location shooting and non-actors for politically oriented social realism.
- ****Aesthetic realism (film)**** — Theory (Bazin) that film cannot fix meaning; uses long takes, deep focus, and location shooting to allow viewer interpretation.

## Debates and open questions
- **Realism vs. Naturalism**: The source notes the terms are "not necessarily synonymous" and used "indiscriminately" for decades; scholars debate whether Courbet or the Impressionists better fit "Naturalism."
- **Idealization vs. Observation**: The Renaissance debate (Leonardo vs. Alberti/Michelangelo) over whether art should depict nature purely or select ideal beauty persisted for centuries and underpins the Realist rupture.
- **Politics of Realism**: 19th-century Naturalism explicitly tried to avoid the leftist politics motivating Realism, but "not wholly successfully"; later art historians contest the political valence of both labels.
- **Illusionism's Trajectory**: The source traces illusionism from Paleolithic to Dutch Golden Age, noting its rejection in Late Antiquity and revival in the Renaissance, but the causal links between technical progress and artistic intent remain interpretive.
- **Theatrical Realism's Origins**: Some cite photography as the basis; others argue dramatic realism is far older, demonstrated by principles of dramatic form matching physical reality.
- **Film Realism Typology**: The distinction between "seamless" (manipulative authenticity) and "aesthetic" (viewer freedom) realism remains a live theoretical framework.
