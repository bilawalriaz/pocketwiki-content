# Ocean

## Overview

The ocean is the interconnected body of salt water covering approximately 70.8% of Earth, containing 97% of Earth's water. It is the primary component of Earth's hydrosphere, acting as a huge heat reservoir for Earth's energy budget, carbon cycle, and water cycle, forming the basis for global climate and weather patterns. The ocean is essential to life, harboring most of Earth's animals and protist life; it originated photosynthesis and therefore Earth's atmospheric oxygen, and still supplies half of it. The ocean faces major threats from human activities, including marine pollution, overfishing, and the effects of climate change, which include ocean warming, ocean acidification, and sea level rise.

## Timeline

- **c. 4.28–3.8 billion years ago** — Geological evidence (pillow basalt, greenstone belts) indicates liquid water existed on Earth
- **Hadean eon** — Oceans thought to have formed; possibly caused emergence of life
- **3 billion years ago** — Life within the ocean evolved, prior to life on land
- **c. 125,000 years ago** — Last global "warm spell": seas about 5.5 m higher than now
- **Last ice age** — Glaciers covered ~1/3 of land; oceans ~122 m lower than today
- **c. 3 million years ago** — Oceans possibly up to 50 m higher than now
- **1951** — Challenger II surveyed Mariana Trench; named "Challenger Deep"
- **1960** — Bathyscaphe Trieste reached bottom of Mariana Trench with crew of two
- **1999–2000** — Southern Ocean recognized by U.S. Board on Geographic Names (1999) and International Hydrographic Organization (2000)
- **2020** — Researchers reported sufficient water to fill oceans may have been on Earth since planetary formation began
- **2023** — High Seas Treaty signed; enables marine protected areas in international waters

## Body

### Terminology and divisions

"The ocean" or "the sea" used without specification refers to the interconnected world ocean. Strictly, a "sea" is a body of water partly or fully enclosed by land; there is no sharp distinction between seas and oceans, though seas are generally smaller. The term "World Ocean" was coined in the early 20th century by Russian oceanographer Yuly Shokalsky. The word "ocean" comes from Oceanus, the Greek Titan personifying an enormous river encircling the world.

By convention, the World Ocean is divided into five major oceans (descending by area): Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern (Antarctic), and Arctic. This five-ocean model crystallized in the early 21st century when the Southern Ocean, delineated by the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, was recognized internationally. Seawater covers about 361,000,000 km². The ocean's furthest pole of inaccessibility, "Point Nemo" in the South Pacific, is roughly 2,688 km from nearest land.

### Natural history and formation

Scientists believe a sizable quantity of water was in the material forming Earth, though water molecules escaped gravity more easily when Earth was less massive (atmospheric escape). During formation, Earth possibly had magma oceans; outgassing, volcanism, and meteorite impacts produced an early atmosphere of carbon dioxide, nitrogen, and water vapor. After Earth's surface cooled, water vapor condensed, forming first oceans—possibly hotter than today and green due to high iron content. A pillow basalt sample from the Isua Greenstone Belt shows water existed 3.8 billion years ago; rocks in the Nuvvuagittuq Greenstone Belt, Quebec, dated 3.8 or 4.28 billion years old, also show evidence of water. In August 2020, researchers reported sufficient water to fill the oceans may have always been on Earth since formation; in this model, greenhouse gases kept oceans from freezing when the young Sun had only 70% of current luminosity.

Plate tectonics, post-glacial rebound, and sea level rise continually change coastlines. In mid-ocean, magma thrusts between plates forming mid-oceanic ridges; convection currents drive plates apart. One oceanic plate may slide beneath another (subduction), forming deep trenches, causing earthquakes, and forcing magma up to create underwater mountains and volcanic island chains. Every ocean basin has a mid-ocean ridge; together they form the global mid-oceanic ridge system, the longest mountain range at 65,000 km. As of 2024, just over 26% of the ocean floor has been mapped at higher resolution than satellites provide.

### Physical properties

Ocean temperatures depend on solar radiation. In the tropics, surface temperatures can exceed 30 °C; near poles with sea ice, equilibrium is about −2 °C. Deep ocean temperatures range between −2 °C and 5 °C everywhere. The average depth is 3,688 meters; nearly half of marine waters exceed 3,000 meters. "Deep ocean" (below 200 m) covers about 66% of Earth's surface. The deepest region is the Mariana Trench, with maximum depth estimated around 10,935 meters.

Oceanographers classify the ocean into vertical and horizontal zones. The photic zone extends to where light intensity is 1% of surface value (approximately 200 m in open ocean); photosynthesis occurs here, making it the most biodiverse. Below lies the mesopelagic (twilight) zone where photosynthesis is unlikely to achieve net growth, then the aphotic deep ocean with no sunlight. The pelagic aphotic zone divides by depth/temperature into mesopelagic (above a 12 °C thermocline at 700–1,000 m in tropics), bathypelagic (10–4 °C, roughly 700–4,000 m), abyssopelagic (to ~6,000 m), and hadalpelagic (6,000–11,000 m, oceanic trenches). Boundaries between water masses are called thermoclines (temperature), haloclines (salinity), chemoclines (chemistry), and pycnoclines (density). The average temperature of the world ocean is 3.9 °C because deep cold water predominates.

Tides are primarily driven by the Moon's gravitational tidal forces, modulated by the Sun, Earth's rotation, and continents. Tidal cycles take about 12.5 hours. Spring tides (higher range) occur when Sun and Moon align; neap tides when misaligned. Open-ocean tidal ranges are under 1 meter but exceed 10 meters in some coastal areas; the Bay of Fundy and Ungava Bay reach 16 meters. Tides differ from storm surges, where high winds and low pressure raise water dramatically.

Ocean currents are continuous, directed flows caused by wind, the Coriolis effect, and temperature/salinity differences. Major currents include the Gulf Stream, Kuroshio Current, Agulhas Current, and Antarctic Circumpolar Current. Wind-driven currents are confined largely to the top hundreds of meters; at depth, thermohaline circulation drives motion. The Atlantic meridional overturning circulation (AMOC) is driven by cooling of polar surface waters, creating dense water that sinks; deep water may be isolated from the surface for hundreds or thousands of years. Currents transfer heat from tropics to poles, moderating coastal climates. Climate projections suggest AMOC has weakened since preindustrial times and will likely weaken further over the 21st century.

### Chemical properties

Salinity measures total dissolved salts. Average ocean chlorinity is about 19.2‰; average salinity is about 34.7‰. Salinity is higher where evaporation exceeds precipitation (e.g., Mediterranean at 38‰) and lower where precipitation exceeds evaporation (polar regions). Sea ice formation excludes salt, increasing residual seawater salinity. Between 1950 and 2019, high-salinity regions became more saline while low-salinity regions freshened; the Pacific and Southern Oceans likely freshened while the Atlantic became more saline.

Ocean water contains dissolved gases—oxygen, carbon dioxide, nitrogen—exchanged at the surface; solubility depends on temperature and salinity. All gases are more soluble in colder water. Photosynthesis in surface waters releases oxygen and consumes carbon dioxide; bacterial decomposition of sinking organic matter consumes oxygen and releases carbon dioxide at depth. The oceans are a major carbon sink; coastal habitats (mangroves, saltmarshes) are termed "Blue carbon" ecosystems. Some deep areas have very low oxygen (oxygen minimum zones or hypoxic waters), worsened by climate change.

Global mean surface pH is approximately 8.05–8.08 (slightly alkaline), down from about 8.2 over the past 300 million years and from ~8.15 in 1950 to ~8.05 in 2020. Atmospheric CO₂ absorbed by oceans forms carbonic acid, which dissociates to release hydrogen ions, lowering pH (ocean acidification). Deep waters naturally have pH as low as 7.8 from organic matter degradation; surface waters can reach 8.4 in productive areas. Several distinct pH scales exist; differences between scales may reach 0.14 units. Alkalinity—the balance of proton acceptors and donors—buffers pH; carbonate, bicarbonate, and borate ions are the significant contributors, with the first two exceeding 95%. CO₂ absorption does not affect alkalinity.

Elements in ocean water have wide concentration ranges: sodium and chloride at grams per liter; iron at nanograms per liter. Residence time is the average time an element stays dissolved before removal. Sodium has residence times of millions of years (unreactive, soluble); iron and aluminium have residence times of only hundreds of years (insoluble, reactive). Nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, potassium) are essential for life; nitrate and phosphate have residence times of 10,000 and 69,000 years respectively; potassium, 12 million years. Phosphate from agriculture and sewage eventually sinks to the ocean floor, making mineable phosphate a non-renewable resource.

### Marine life and human uses

Life within the ocean evolved 3 billion years before life on land. Ocean life includes most animal phyla—many found only in marine environments (sponges, cnidarians, comb jellies, brachiopods, echinoderms)—plus cephalopods, crustaceans, fish, sharks, cetaceans, seabirds, sea turtles, sea grasses, mangroves, algae (including kelp and nori source Pyropia), bacteria, archaea, and fungi.

Humans use oceans for navigation, warfare, shipping, trade, fishing, aquaculture, leisure, power generation, offshore drilling, deep-sea mining, and desalination. Containerization greatly boosted shipping efficiency, a major factor in globalization. The biggest global commercial fisheries are anchovies, Alaska pollock, and tuna; a 2020 FAO report stated 34% of fish stocks were overfished in 2017. Fish accounted for 17% of global animal protein intake. Marine energy includes tidal power, ocean thermal energy, and wave power; offshore wind has higher speeds but higher construction costs. "Freedom of the seas" (17th-century principle) is enshrined in the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS). The International Maritime Organization (ratified 1958) handles maritime safety and shipping-related pollution.

### Protection

Ocean protection includes marine protected areas (MPAs), pollution prevention policies, sustainable seafood support, and ocean clean-up projects. In 2021, 43 scientists published a framework for evaluating MPA protection levels. In March 2023, a legally binding High Seas Treaty was signed, enabling marine protected areas in international waters and supporting the 30% protection target by 2030; it was adopted by 193 UN Member States.

## Terms

- **Photic zone**: Upper ocean layer where light intensity is at least 1% of surface value (~200 m); where photosynthesis occurs
- **Thermohaline circulation**: Deep-ocean circulation driven by temperature and salinity density differences
- **Coriolis effect**: Apparent deflection of moving objects due to Earth's rotation, influencing current direction
- **Halocline**: Zone of rapid salinity increase with depth
- **Ocean acidification**: pH drop caused by absorption of atmospheric CO₂ forming carbonic acid
- **Residence time**: Average time an element stays dissolved in ocean water before removal
- **Pelagic zone**: Open ocean's water column from surface to ocean floor
- **Subduction**: Process where one oceanic plate slides beneath another, forming trenches
- **Alkalinity**: Balance of proton acceptors and donors in seawater; buffers pH
- **Marine protected area (MPA)**: Protected ocean region created to safeguard ecosystems

## Debates and open questions

The origin of Earth's oceans remains unknown. The timing of first liquid water is debated: rocks dated at 3.8 billion years by one study and 4.28 billion by another show water evidence, but if oceans existed earlier, evidence may have been destroyed by crustal recycling. A 2020 model suggests sufficient water may have been present since planetary formation began, kept liquid by greenhouse gases despite the young Sun's lower luminosity. The ocean as a whole will never be fully explored; some estimate only 5% has been explored, and as of 2024 just over 26% of the seafloor is mapped at high resolution.
