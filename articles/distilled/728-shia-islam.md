# Shia Islam

## Overview

Shia Islam is the second-largest branch of Islam, representing approximately 10–13% of the global Muslim population (roughly 200–260 million faithful followers as of 2026). Rooted in the belief that Muhammad explicitly designated his cousin and son-in-law Ali ibn Abi Talib as his rightful successor, Shia Islam diverges from Sunni Islam primarily over the question of legitimate leadership (imamate) after the Prophet's death in 632 CE. This foundational dispute, centered on the events at Ghadir Khumm and the meeting at Saqifa where Abu Bakr was secured as caliph, shaped centuries of theological, political, and cultural development, making Shia Islam not merely a sect but a comprehensive religious tradition with distinct doctrines, practices, and institutions.

## Timeline

- **632 CE** — Death of Muhammad; dispute over succession at Saqifa leads to Abu Bakr's caliphate
- **656–661** — First Fitna (civil war); Ali's caliphate marked by battles of the Camel, Siffin, and Nahrawan
- **661** — Assassination of Ali; Muawiyah establishes Umayyad dynasty
- **680** — Battle of Karbala; martyrdom of Husayn ibn Ali and 72 followers
- **909** — Foundation of Fatimid Caliphate in North Africa
- **1501–1736** — Safavid dynasty in Persia causes significant changes, including transformation of Twelver Shia Islam from predominantly Arab to predominantly Persian
- **18th–20th centuries** — Specific persecution events including 1514 Ottoman massacre, 1802 Wahhabi sack of Karbala, Saddam's crackdown, 2011 Malaysia ban, and ISIS persecution

## Body

### Origins and Foundational Dispute

Shia Islam emerged from the "partisans" (shīʿat) of Ali ibn Abi Talib, who believed Muhammad designated Ali as his successor at Ghadir Khumm, declaring "Anyone who has me as his mawla, has Ali as his mawla." When Muhammad died in 632 CE, Ali and the Prophet's family were occupied with funeral arrangements while Abu Bakr, Umar, and Abu Ubaidah convened the Saqifa meeting, securing Ali's exclusion from leadership. Shia Muslims view this as a violation of divine designation, while Sunnis regard it as a legitimate political succession. The term "Shia" itself derives from shīʿatu ʿAlī ("followers of Ali").

### The Martyrdom of Husayn and Karbala

The Battle of Karbala in 680 CE marks the definitive split between Shia and Sunni Islam. After Muawiyah's death, his son Yazid demanded allegiance from Husayn ibn Ali, who refused, viewing it as betrayal of the treaty between Hasan ibn Ali and Muawiyah. Intercepted en route to Kufa, Husayn and his followers were killed, with Husayn's head delivered to Yazid in Damascus. This event established the central Shia narrative of resistance against tyranny and is commemorated annually on Ashura (10th of Muharram).

### The Imamate and Spiritual Authority

Shia theology centers on the imamate—the belief that divine guidance continues through infallible (ma'sum) descendants of Ali through Fatima, Muhammad's daughter. Unlike Sunni Islam, which sees succession as a political matter, Shia Islam views it as a theological necessity: divine justice requires that Allah not leave humanity without infallible guidance. The imams possess esoteric knowledge and interpret both the Quran and the Prophet's teachings. The three principal Shia branches are Twelverism, Isma'ilism, and Zaydism. Twelvers believe in twelve imams, ending with the hidden Mahdi; Ismailis follow a different line; Zaydis recognize Zayd ibn Ali as the fifth imam, though there is also a group called Zaydi Wasitis who are Twelvers.

### Theological Distinctives

Shia theology emphasizes divine justice (ʿadl) as a fundamental principle with no direct equivalent in mainstream Sunni thought—it is one of the five uṣūl al-dīn in Twelver doctrine, elevated to the status of a foundational creed alongside monotheism, prophethood, imamate, and resurrection. This contrasts with Ashari Sunni theology, which subordinates moral categories to divine will. The doctrine of amr bayn al-amrayn ("a matter between two matters") resolves the tension between divine decree and human free will, affirming genuine human agency within Allah's sustaining will. The concept of ismah (infallibility) extends to prophets, imams, and Fatima, ensuring reliable religious guidance. The Zaydi Shias, who do not attribute ismah to the Imams, represent an important exception to this doctrine.

### Occultation and Eschatology

Twelver Shia Islam believes the twelfth imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, entered occultation (ghaybah) in 874 CE and will return as the eschatological redeemer alongside Jesus (ʿIsa) to establish justice before the Day of Judgment. This belief shapes Shia political theology, particularly the concept of guardianship of the Islamic jurist (wilayat al-faqih), institutionalized in modern Iran. The occultation period necessitates qualified jurists (mujtahids) to interpret religious law through ijtihad (independent reasoning).

### Jurisprudence and Practice

The Jaʽfari school, named after the sixth imam Jaʿfar al-Sadiq, governs Twelver Shia practice. It recognizes four sources of law: the Quran, the Sunnah (including imamic traditions), consensus (ijmaʿ), and the intellect (ʿaql). Shia practice differs from Sunni Islam in prayer timing (allowing combination of prayers), the inclusion of Ali in the Shahada, and the emphasis on commemorative rituals during Muharram. The institution of marjaʿ al-taqlid (source of emulation) allows believers to follow qualified jurists in religious matters. The canonical Shia hadith collections, known as the Kutub al-Arbaʿa (Four Books), include al-Kulayni's al-Kāfī (d. 329 AH), al-Saduq's Man La Yahduruhu al-Faqih (d. 381 AH), and al-Tusi's Tahdhib al-Ahkam and al-Istibsar (d. 460 AH).

### Demographics and Modern Context

Shia Muslims form majorities in Iran (90%), Iraq (65%), and Azerbaijan (55%), and significant populations in Lebanon, Bahrain, Yemen, Turkey, Pakistan, and Afghanistan. Iran remains the only country where Shia Islam serves as the foundation of both legal and governmental systems. Historical persecution, including massacres under Ottoman rule and destruction of holy sites by Wahhabi forces, continues to influence Shia-Sunni relations. Contemporary Shia communities face varying degrees of discrimination and marginalization in Sunni-majority contexts.

## Terms

- **Imamate**: The Shia doctrine of divinely appointed, infallible spiritual and political successors to Muhammad, beginning with Ali ibn Abi Talib
- **Ismah**: The concept of infallibility or divine protection from error and sin, attributed to prophets, imams, and Fatima in Shia theology
- **ʿAdl**: Divine justice, a fundamental Shia theological principle asserting that God acts in accordance with moral categories recognizable by human intellect
- **Ghaybah**: The occultation, particularly the Twelver belief that the twelfth imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, is hidden and will return eschatologically
- **Ahl al-Bayt**: The purified family of Muhammad, including Ali, Fatima, Hasan, Husayn, and their descendants, regarded as infallible inheritors of divine knowledge
- **Shahada**: The Islamic profession of faith; the Shia version includes the phrase "Ali-un-Waliullah" (Ali is the friend of God)
- **Marjaʿ al-taqlid**: A source of emulation; a qualified Shia jurist whose legal rulings ordinary believers follow
- **Jaʽfari jurisprudence**: The Shia school of Islamic law named after Jaʿfar al-Sadiq, recognizing four sources including the intellect (ʿaql)
- **Ashura**: The 10th day of Muharram commemorating the martyrdom of Husayn ibn Ali at Karbala
- **Nass**: Divine designation or appointment, particularly the Shia belief that Muhammad was divinely commanded to appoint Ali as successor

## Debates and Open Questions

Several scholarly debates persist regarding Shia Islam's origins and development. Western historians have variously characterized early Shia Islam as primarily political faction versus religious movement, with some arguing this reflects anachronistic Western separations of religion and politics. The interpretation of the Ghadir Khumm proclamation remains contested, with Sunni scholars viewing it as general affirmation of Ali's merit while Shia Muslims see it as explicit designation of succession. The theological development of ismah and the imamate evolved over centuries, with some arguing these concepts were retrofitted to justify political positions. Contemporary debates include the role of wilayat al-faqih in Shia political theory, the extent of Shia-Sunni reconciliation possibilities, and the impact of modern sectarian conflicts on traditional Shia identity formation.
