# Alps

## Overview
The Alps are a major mountain range in Europe, stretching approximately 1,200 km across eight countries. Formed by the collision of the African and Eurasian tectonic plates, they are a defining geographic, climatic, and cultural feature of the continent, influencing weather patterns, providing water to lowland Europe, and hosting a distinct multicultural identity and a dominant tourism industry.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Formation of the Alps begins with the collision of the African and Eurasian plates.
- **c. 3300 BC** — Ötzi the Iceman, a Neolithic mummy, dies on a glacier at the Austrian–Italian border.
- **218 BC** — Hannibal crosses the Alps with an army during the Second Punic War.
- **1291** — The Federal Charter of 1291 is signed by four Alpine cantons, a foundational act for Switzerland.
- **1786** — The first ascent of Mont Blanc is made.
- **1800** — Napoleon crosses the Great St. Bernard Pass with an army of 40,000.
- **1865** — The first ascent of the Matterhorn marks the end of the "Golden Age of Alpinism."
- **1882** — The 15 km-long Saint Gotthard Railway Tunnel opens.
- **1924** — The first Winter Olympic Games are held in Chamonix, France.
- **2016** — The Gotthard Base Tunnel, the world's longest railway tunnel (57.1 km), opens.

## Body

### Geology and Formation
The Alps formed over tens of millions of years as the African and Eurasian tectonic plates collided, a process called the Alpine orogeny. This caused extreme shortening, thrusting, and folding of marine sedimentary rocks into high peaks like Mont Blanc and the Matterhorn. The range is geologically subdivided into the Western, Eastern, and Southern Alps based on rock composition and nappe structures (stacked sheets of rock). The core consists of layers of European, African, and oceanic (Tethyan) origin. The region remains geologically active, with ongoing uplift and earthquakes typically between magnitude 6 and 7.

### Geography and Hydrology
The Alps are a crescent-shaped range about 800 km long and 200 km wide, with a mean peak height of 2.5 km. They are divided into the Western and Eastern Alps, with the highest peaks in the west (Mont Blanc, 4,810 m) and lower ones in the east. The range provides up to 90% of the water to lowland Europe, with major rivers like the Rhine, Rhône, and Po originating from Alpine runoff. This water supports drinking supplies, irrigation, and over 550 hydroelectric power plants. Glaciers, which once covered 1,817 km² in 1876, have shrunk significantly due to climate change, impacting future water storage.

### Climate and Ecology
The Alps create distinct climatic zones, from the temperate colline zone (500–1,000 m) to the glacial zone above the snow line (2,400–3,000 m). They divide European weather, creating a wet north and dry south. The region hosts 13,000 plant species, including alpine gentians and edelweiss, which are adapted to harsh conditions. Wildlife includes 30,000 species, such as the alpine ibex, chamois, marmots, and golden eagles. Human activity, like deforestation and ski-run construction, has altered ecosystems.

### Human History and Culture
Human habitation dates to the Palaeolithic era. By the 6th century BC, Celtic cultures like La Tène were established. The Romans built roads and settlements, and medieval monasteries provided shelter. The Alps fostered a strong cultural identity with traditional practices like farming, cheesemaking, and woodworking. The population of 14 million is multicultural and linguistically diverse, with numerous dialects. Tourism, which began in the 19th century, became the dominant industry by the late 20th century, with 120 million annual visitors.

### Tourism and Transportation
Tourism expanded significantly after World War II, centered on winter sports and summer scenery. The Alps have hosted multiple Winter Olympic Games. Transportation infrastructure includes 4,200 km of roads and extensive railways, with major tunnels like the Gotthard Base Tunnel facilitating flat routes through the mountains. Some villages, like Zermatt, are car-free. The industry faces concerns about cultural loss and sustainability, with climate change reducing high-altitude ski resorts.

## Terms
- **Alpine orogeny**: The mountain-building process that formed the Alps through tectonic plate collision.
- **Nappe**: A large, sheet-like body of rock that has been moved far from its original position by faulting or folding.
- **Flysch**: Sedimentary rock formed during mountain building, consisting of alternating layers of sandstone and shale.
- **Tree line**: The edge of the habitat at which trees are capable of growing; above it, the climate is too cold.
- **Permafrost**: Ground that remains completely frozen for at least two years straight.
- **Isostatic rebound**: The rise of land masses after the removal of the weight of ice sheets or glaciers.
- **Tracht**: Traditional folk costume, such as lederhosen for men and dirndls for women, worn in German-speaking Alpine regions.
- **Almabtrieb**: The traditional festival celebrating the descent of cattle from high alpine pastures in mid-September.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise mechanisms and relative contributions of different forces (e.g., isostatic rebound, mantle convection, ongoing plate convergence) to the present-day uplift of the Alps are difficult to quantify and likely vary in space and time.
- The impact of climate change on Alpine water storage, river runoff, and the future of glacier-dependent ecosystems and tourism is a major ongoing concern.
- The effects of diverting river water for snowmaking in ski resorts on the broader Alpine hydrology are not yet fully understood.
