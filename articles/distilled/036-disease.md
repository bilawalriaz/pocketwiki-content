# Disease

## Overview

A disease is an abnormal condition that adversely affects the structure or function of part or all of an organism and is not immediately due to external injury. Diseases are medical conditions associated with specific signs and symptoms, caused by external factors like pathogens or internal dysfunctions (e.g., immune system problems producing immunodeficiency, allergies, or autoimmune disorders). The study of disease is called pathology, which includes etiology (the study of cause). The four main types are infectious, deficiency, hereditary, and physiological diseases; the deadliest in humans are ischemic heart disease, stroke, and COPD.

## Timeline

- **1882** — Bacterial cause of tuberculosis discovered, ending earlier ascriptions to heredity, lifestyle, or moral failings
- **2004** — WHO calculated 932 million years of potential life lost to premature death
- **2004** — WHO calculated 1.5 billion disability-adjusted life years lost to disease and injury
- **Present** — ICD-11 is the latest World Health Organization classification of diseases

## Body

### Core Concepts and Terminology

The term "disease" broadly refers to any condition impairing normal body functioning, often specifically meaning infectious diseases caused by pathogenic agents (viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoa, multicellular organisms, and prions—aberrant proteins). An infection producing no clinically evident impairment (like normal gut bacteria) is not a disease, but an asymptomatic infection expected to produce symptoms later is. Non-infectious diseases include most cancers, heart disease, and genetic disease.

Key distinctions: an **acquired disease** begins during one's lifetime (versus **congenital disease** present at birth); an **acute disease** is short-term; a **chronic disease** persists, often at least six months; a **genetic disease** results from one or more genetic mutations (sometimes inherited, sometimes de novo/random); a **hereditary disease** is a genetic disease passed through families; an **iatrogenic disease** is caused by medical intervention; an **idiopathic disease** has unknown cause; an **incurable disease** cannot be cured but is not necessarily terminal; a **primary disease** is the root cause while a **secondary disease** is a sequela (complication) of it; a **terminal disease** inevitably results in death.

**Illness** and **sickness** are generally synonyms for disease, but "illness" can specifically refer to the patient's subjective experience. A person can have a disease without being ill (asymptomatic condition) or be ill without disease (medicalizing normal experiences). Symptoms of illness often reflect evolved "sickness behavior"—lethargy, depression, loss of appetite, sleepiness, hyperalgesia (increased pain sensitivity), and inability to concentrate—that helps clear infection.

A **disorder** is a functional abnormality that may or may not show signs and symptoms; it is considered more value-neutral and less stigmatizing than "disease." A **medical condition** is a broad concept including all diseases, lesions, and disorders, plus non-pathological conditions receiving medical treatment (like pregnancy); the DSM psychiatric manual uses "general medical condition" to mean everything except mental disorders. **Morbidity** (from Latin *morbidus*, "sick") is a diseased state or poor health; comorbidity is the simultaneous presence of two or more conditions. **Pathosis** or **pathology** is synonymous with disease. A **syndrome** is an association of signs and symptoms that often occur together, whether or not the cause is known (e.g., Down syndrome has one cause; Parkinsonian syndrome has multiple). **Predisease** is a subclinical or prodromal vanguard of disease (e.g., prediabetes); its identification is contentious because there is seldom a clear line between legitimate concern and over-medicalization driven by conflicts of interest.

### Types by Body System and Stages

**Mental illness** is a broad label for conditions involving affective or emotional instability, behavioral dysregulation, or cognitive dysfunction, including major depression, generalized anxiety disorders, and schizophrenia; it can be biological or psychological in origin. An **organic disease** is caused by physical or physiological change to tissue or organs, commonly contrasted with mental disorders.

In infectious disease, the **incubation period** is time between infection and symptom appearance; the **latency period** is time between infection and ability to spread to others. Some viruses exhibit **viral latency**—hiding dormant in the body, as varicella zoster virus causes chickenpox acutely then may later cause shingles. A **clinical disease** produces characteristic signs and symptoms (AIDS is the clinical stage of HIV infection). A **cure** ends a condition; **remission** is the disappearance, possibly temporary, of symptoms. **Progressive disease** worsens until death, serious debility, or organ failure; **stable disease** does not get better or worse. **Refractory disease** resists treatment more than normal. **Subclinical disease** (silent/asymptomatic) precedes symptom onset. The **terminal phase** is between the disease process and active dying. **Recovery** is the repairing of physical processes and resumption of healthy functioning.

By extent: **localized disease** affects one part (athlete's foot); **disseminated disease** has spread (metastatic in cancer); **systemic disease** affects the entire body (influenza, high blood pressure).

### Classification, Causes, and Prevention

Diseases may be classified by cause, pathogenesis (mechanism), symptoms, or organ system involved. A chief difficulty in nosology (disease classification) is that diseases often cannot be clearly defined when cause or pathogenesis is unknown, so diagnostic terms often reflect only symptoms. The most used classification is the World Health Organization's ICD, currently in its 11th edition.

Causes include microorganisms, genetics, environment, or combinations. Infectious diseases are transmitted by hand-to-mouth contact, insect bites, contaminated food or water, or sexually. Social determinants of health—the social conditions in which people live—greatly influence well-being, as recognized by the WHO and Public Health Agency of Canada; climate change can exacerbate infectious disease transmission. When a disease's cause is poorly understood, societies tend to mythologize it. One should not confuse a pathogen (cause) with the disease itself (e.g., West Nile virus causes West Nile fever). Types of causes include airborne, foodborne, infectious (contagious diseases spread person-to-person; communicable diseases spread but not necessarily through everyday contact), lifestyle (increasing with industrialization and longevity), and non-communicable (heart disease, cancer).

Many diseases can be prevented through sanitation, proper nutrition, adequate exercise, vaccinations, and other self-care and public health measures.

### Treatments and Epidemiology

Treatments (or therapies) are efforts to cure or improve disease, including medications, surgery, medical devices, and self-care. Preventive healthcare avoids disease in the first place; a treatment or cure is applied after a problem starts. Cures are a subset of treatments that reverse diseases completely; many incurable diseases remain treatable. Pain management uses an interdisciplinary approach to relieve pain and improve quality of life.

Epidemiology studies factors that cause or encourage diseases. It is a cornerstone of public health research and evidence-based medicine, ranging from outbreak investigation to statistical modeling. Epidemiologists study syndemics (disease interactions in populations) and rely on biology, biostatistics, Geographic Information Science, and social sciences. A challenge is defining diseases—without agreed definitions, researchers report different numbers of cases. Morbidity databases, like the European Hospital Morbidity Database, compile hospital discharge data submitted to the WHO Regional Office for Europe.

**Disease burden** is measured by financial cost, mortality, morbidity, or other indicators. **Years of potential life lost (YPLL)** estimates years shortened by disease but does not account for disability before death. **Quality-adjusted life years (QALY)** and **disability-adjusted life years (DALY)** account for whether the person was healthy after diagnosis, showing burden on those who are sick but live normal lifespans. In the developed world, heart disease and stroke cause the most loss of life, but neuropsychiatric conditions like major depressive disorder cause the most years lost to sickness.

### Society and Culture

How society responds to disease is the subject of medical sociology. A condition may be considered a disease in some cultures but not others (obesity was associated with prosperity in many African regions; epilepsy is seen as spiritual gifts among the Hmong). Sickness confers the "sick role"—social legitimization of benefits like illness benefits and work avoidance—but obligates the sick person to seek treatment and recover. Most religions grant exemptions from duties to the sick (e.g., fasting on Yom Kippur or Ramadan). Identifying a condition as a disease has significant social and economic implications, as with the controversial recognition of repetitive stress injury and PTSD. Diseases of poverty and diseases of affluence vary by time and place; diabetes can be associated with both. The term "leper" still evokes social stigma.

Language shapes the experience of disease. Military metaphors dominate: disease is an enemy to be fought, the patient a warrior. Journey metaphors describe illness as travel "on the road to recovery." Some metaphors are disease-specific (slavery for addiction). Diseases are used as metaphors for social ills—"cancer" for anything endemic and destructive; tuberculosis symbolized transcendence in 19th-century literature but became an emblem of poverty in the 20th century after its cause was understood.

## Terms

- **Etiology**: The study of the cause of a disease
- **Pathogen**: A disease-causing microorganism (bacteria, virus, protozoa, fungus, or prion)
- **Prion**: An aberrant protein that can cause disease
- **Sequelae**: A complication or condition caused by a prior (primary) disease
- **Nosology**: The classification of diseases
- **Iatrogenic**: Caused by medical intervention
- **Idiopathic**: Having an unknown cause or source
- **Comorbidity**: The simultaneous presence of two or more medical conditions
- **Syndemic**: The interaction of diseases within a population
- **Mortality rate**: The proportion of people dying during a given time interval (contrasted with morbidity rate)

## Debates and Open Questions

The nosology (classification) and epistemology of predisease is contentious: there is seldom a bright line differentiating legitimate concern for subclinical status from conflict-of-interest-driven over-medicalization (by pharmaceutical manufacturers) or de-medicalization (by insurers). Identifying legitimate predisease enables useful prevention, but labeling healthy people can cause overtreatment. One review proposed three criteria for predisease: high risk of progression, actionability for risk reduction, and benefit outweighing harm. Additionally, epidemiology faces the challenge that different groups use significantly different definitions for poorly understood diseases, leading to divergent case counts and reported characteristics.
