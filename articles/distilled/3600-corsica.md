# Corsica

## Overview
Corsica is a mountainous Mediterranean island and one of France's 18 regions, known for its distinct Italian-influenced culture and a long history of autonomy movements. Its strategic location and unique identity have made it a crossroads of Mediterranean powers, leading to a complex political landscape that balances French administration with strong local nationalism.

## Timeline
- **238 BC** — Incorporated into the Roman Republic as a province with Sardinia.
- **1284** — Genoese influence begins after defeating Pisa at the Battle of Meloria.
- **1729** — Corsican Revolution for independence from Genoa begins.
- **1755** — Independent Corsican Republic proclaimed under Pasquale Paoli.
- **1769** — France annexes Corsica after Genoa cedes it to Louis XV.
- **1975** — Corsica is split into two French departments; armed conflict with nationalists begins.
- **2014** — Major nationalist faction (FLNC-UC) announces a truce.
- **2018** — The two departments merge into a single territorial collectivity.
- **2022** — Murder of nationalist Yvan Colonna reignites unrest and conflict.

## Body
### History and Political Status
Corsica was ruled by the Republic of Genoa from 1284 until 1755, when it seceded to form a self-proclaimed Italian-speaking republic. Genoa ceded the island to France in 1768 to settle debts, leading to French annexation in 1769. The future Emperor Napoleon Bonaparte was born in Corsica that same year. The island's historical ties to Tuscany have left a lasting Italian cultural imprint. Today, Corsica is a territorial collectivity of France with more extensive autonomy than other regions, including a Corsican Assembly with limited executive powers. A long-running armed conflict between French forces and Corsican nationalist groups, most notably the National Liberation Front of Corsica (FLNC), began in 1975. Major factions declared truces in 2014 and 2016, but violence resumed in 2022 following the murder of jailed nationalist Yvan Colonna.

### Geography and Ecology
Formed about 250 million years ago, Corsica is the most mountainous island in the Mediterranean, with a single mountain chain comprising two-thirds of its landmass. Its highest peak is Monte Cinto at 2,706 m. The island has a Mediterranean climate and is divided into four ecological zones by altitude, from coastal scrubland to uninhabited alpine zones. About 40% of its area is protected, including the Parc naturel régional de Corse and the UNESCO-listed Scandola Nature Reserve. The island's native Pleistocene fauna, including the Corsican giant shrew and Sardinian pika, went extinct after human arrival, replaced by introduced species like the European mouflon and Corsican red deer.

### Demographics, Language, and Culture
As of January 2026, Corsica had a population of 365,636. French is the official and most widely spoken language, but Corsican (Corsu), an Italo-Dalmatian language closely related to medieval Tuscan, is also recognized. Italian was the official language until 1859. The language is divided into two main varieties, Cismuntanu and Ultramuntanu, and is considered endangered, with only about 10% of the population speaking it natively. Corsican culture retains strong Italian elements, visible in cuisine (featuring chestnuts, brocciu cheese, and wild boar), surnames, and traditions.

### Economy and Transport
Corsica's economy relies on agriculture, tourism, and public administration. Key exports include granite, cork, cheese, wine, and olive oil. The island is connected to mainland France and Italy by ferry services from ports like Bastia and Ajaccio. It has four international airports and a 232 km metre-gauge railway network linking Bastia, Ajaccio, and Calvi.

## Terms
- **Territorial collectivity**: A French administrative division with more autonomy than a standard region or department.
- **FLNC (National Liberation Front of Corsica)**: A Corsican nationalist paramilitary group that used armed conflict to pursue independence or greater autonomy.
- **Italo-Dalmatian language**: A group of Romance languages, including Corsican and Italian, spoken in parts of Italy and the Adriatic.
- **Cismuntanu and Ultramuntanu**: The two main dialectal varieties of the Corsican language, divided by a geographic line.
- **Parc naturel régional de Corse**: A large protected area in Corsica's interior, established in 1972.
- **Vendetta**: A traditional Corsican code requiring deadly revenge for offenses against family honor.
- **Maquis (machja)**: The dense, shrubby Mediterranean vegetation covering much of Corsica's hillsides.

## Debates and open questions
The primary ongoing debate concerns Corsica's political future: the extent of autonomy it should have within France, versus full independence. While polls show a majority of Corsicans oppose independence, there is strong support for greater self-governance. The French government has expressed willingness to discuss "a form of autonomy" but opposes full independence. The 2003 referendum narrowly rejected a plan to merge the departments and increase autonomy, but the issue remains central to Corsican politics. The resumption of violence in 2022 complicates these discussions.
