# Subutai

## Overview
Subutai (c. 1175–1248) was the primary military strategist for Genghis Khan and Ögedei Khan, directing over 20 campaigns that conquered more territory than any other commander in history. His innovative strategies, coordinating armies hundreds of kilometers apart, were instrumental in the expansion of the Mongol Empire, the largest contiguous empire in human history. He is regarded as one of the greatest military commanders in history and the single greatest in Mongolian history.

## Timeline
- **c. 1175** — Born near the upper Onon River in modern Mongolia.
- **1197** — First independent command against the Merkit at age 22.
- **1211** — Participates in the initial Mongol invasion of Jin China.
- **1212** — Takes the fortress of Huan by storm, his first major independent exploit.
- **1217** — Defeats the Merkit and Cuman-Kipchak confederation on the Chu River.
- **1220–1223** — Leads the "Great Raid" with Jebe, circling the Caspian Sea and defeating a Rus' army at the Kalka River.
- **1226** — Commands the flank army in the invasion of the Xi Xia kingdom.
- **1231–1234** — Directs the final conquest of Jin China, culminating in the siege of Kaifeng.
- **1236–1240** — Directs the Mongol conquest of the Rus' principalities.
- **1241–1242** — Commands the Mongol invasion of Central Europe, winning the Battle of Mohi in Hungary.
- **1248** — Dies at his home near the Tuul River at age 72.

## Body

### Early Life and Rise to Command
Subutai was born into a family with long-standing ties to Temujin (the future Genghis Khan). His father, Jarchigudai, had supplied food to Temujin, and his elder brother Jelme was a general and close companion. Subutai left his clan at age 14 to join Temujin's army, rising rapidly through merit. He was appointed to the prestigious post of Genghis Khan's ger (yurt) door guard, giving him access to strategy meetings. He apprenticed under elite generals like Jebe and Muqali before earning independent command. Genghis Khan counted him among his "Four Dogs of war."

### Military Innovations and Strategy
Subutai was a major innovator in the art of war. He routinely coordinated large armies separated by vast distances, using deception to mislead foes and strike from unexpected directions. He bypassed strong fortifications until organized resistance was destroyed, and used siege engines even in field battles, as at the Battle of Mohi where stonethrowers cleared enemy crossbowmen. His tactics were decisive and fluid, using heavy firepower to create openings for rapid cavalry charges, as seen at the Kalka River where a 9-day retreat was followed by an immediate, crushing charge. He mastered creating divisions within enemy ranks using spies and psychological warfare.

### Central Asian and Chinese Campaigns
Subutai played key roles in the Mongol invasions of Central Asia and China. In 1219, he marched with Genghis Khan through the Kyzylkum Desert to outflank the Khwarizm Empire. He and Jebe then pursued the Khwarizm Shah, preventing him from rallying his forces. In the campaign against the Jin dynasty (1231–1234), Subutai outmaneuvered fortified lines by dividing Mongol forces and invading from the south through the Song Empire. He used feints, supply raids, and attrition to isolate and destroy the main Jin army at the Battle of Sanfengshan, before conducting an eight-month siege of the capital Kaifeng.

### The Great Raid and Conquest of Rus'
The "Great Raid" (1220–1223) with Jebe was a reconnaissance-in-force of unprecedented scale. They defeated the Kingdom of Georgia, crossed the Caucasus Mountains, and used diplomacy to isolate and defeat steppe coalitions. At the Kalka River, they destroyed a combined Rus' and Cuman army. Later, directing the full invasion of Rus' (1236–1240), Subutai used giant encirclement campaigns and winter attacks to defeat the Volga Bulgars and the Rus' principalities in detail, including the Grand Duke of Vladimir at the Battle of the Sit River.

### Invasion of Central Europe
The invasion of Europe (1241–1242) was planned and commanded by Subutai. He sent spies ahead and launched a five-pronged winter attack to confuse and defeat European armies piecemeal. While a northern force won the Battle of Legnica in Poland, Subutai personally led the central column into Hungary. At the Battle of Mohi, he used a feint at a bridge to fix Hungarian attention, then secretly crossed the Sajo River with a main force to envelop them. He left a deliberate gap in his encirclement to lure the Hungarians into a disorganized retreat, where they were slaughtered. The campaign devastated Hungary's population and agriculture before being halted by the death of Ögedei Khan.

### Final Years and Legacy
In his final years, Subutai helped elect Güyük as Great Khan and, at age 71, was placed in charge of a campaign against the Song dynasty. He died in 1248. His descendants, like Uriyangkhadai and Aju, continued Mongol conquests. Subutai's strategic innovations—dispersing forces along multiple axes and rapidly coalescing to defeat enemies in detail—were lost to the West for centuries but later influenced Soviet deep battle doctrine and modern maneuver warfare theory.

## Terms
- **Tumen**: A Mongol army unit of approximately 10,000 men.
- **Ger**: A portable felt tent, or yurt, used by nomadic peoples of the Central Asian steppes.
- **Kurultai**: A traditional Mongol and Turkic assembly to decide on major issues, including the election of a leader.
- **Scorched earth**: A military strategy of destroying anything useful to the enemy, including crops and infrastructure.
- **Reconnaissance-in-force**: A large-scale military operation to test enemy defenses and gather intelligence, not necessarily to conquer territory.
- **Circumvallation**: A siege line built by attackers around a besieged place to prevent relief and escape.
- **Deep battle**: A Soviet military doctrine emphasizing coordinated operations across a broad front to penetrate and disrupt enemy defenses at depth.
- **Maneuver warfare**: A military strategy that emphasizes speed, surprise, and movement to defeat an enemy by dislocating their critical vulnerabilities.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Subutai's ethnic background**: Some historical accounts claim he belonged to the Uriankhai clan (reindeer people), making him an outsider. Recent scholarship discounts this as a literary trope, noting his family's long association with Genghis Khan's lineage.
- **Reason for the Mongol withdrawal from Europe (1242)**: The papal envoy Carpini alleged the army withdrew to elect a new Great Khan after Ögedei's death, over Subutai's objections. The historian Rashid al-Din noted Batu and Subutai only heard of the death after beginning withdrawal, and a Cuman revolt in Russia may have also been a factor.
- **The fate of the "Great Raid" rearguard**: It is likely that Jebe, Subutai's partner, was killed by Kipchaks during the sacrifice of the 1,000-man rearguard at the Kalka River, though this is not explicitly confirmed.
