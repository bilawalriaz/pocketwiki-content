# John Muir

## Overview
John Muir (1838–1914) was a Scottish-born American naturalist, author, and early environmental advocate whose writings and activism were instrumental in the preservation of Yosemite Valley and the creation of the U.S. National Park system. Known as the "Father of the National Parks," his philosophy of complete preservationism—valuing nature for its spiritual and intrinsic worth—profoundly shaped American conservation and the modern environmental movement.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born in Dunbar, Scotland, Muir emigrated to Wisconsin in 1849. His strict religious upbringing fostered a deep, albeit evolving, spirituality. He attended the University of Wisconsin–Madison, where a botany lesson sparked his lifelong passion for natural history. An 1867 eye injury in a factory accident, which temporarily blinded him, prompted a resolve to dedicate his life to exploring nature. He subsequently walked to Florida and eventually traveled to California in 1868.

### Exploration and Scientific Work
Muir settled in California, where he became captivated by Yosemite Valley. He lived there for years, working as a shepherd and guide while conducting extensive geological and botanical studies. He championed the theory that glaciers, not catastrophic earthquakes, had sculpted Yosemite, a view initially disputed but later supported by his discovery of an active alpine glacier in 1871. He also studied the ecology of Giant Sequoia groves. Between 1879 and 1899, he made seven trips to Alaska, exploring and documenting its glaciers and landscapes.

### Activism and Preservation
Muir's advocacy was crucial in establishing Yosemite National Park in 1890, though he was dismayed it remained under state control. In 1892, he co-founded the Sierra Club to protect the Sierra Nevada. His friendship with President Theodore Roosevelt led to a pivotal 1903 camping trip in Yosemite, which helped convince Roosevelt to place the valley under federal protection. Muir's philosophy of preservationism—advocating that nature be kept completely off-limits to exploitation—clashed with Gifford Pinchot's conservationism, which promoted sustainable resource use. This divide defined early 20th-century environmental policy. Muir's final major battle was the unsuccessful campaign to prevent the damming of Hetch Hetchy Valley, which was authorized in 1913.

### Writing and Philosophy
Muir's writings, born from intense labor and revision, were not just scientific records but spiritual and aesthetic interpretations of nature. He viewed the natural world as a "Book of Nature" revealing divine truth, seeing wilderness as a "home" and a source of "mountain baptism." His work, influenced by transcendentalists like Emerson and Thoreau, aimed to change readers' perception of nature from a resource to a sacred entity. He published over 300 articles and 12 books during his lifetime.

### Personal Life and Legacy
In 1880, Muir married Louisa Strentzel and managed her family's large fruit ranch in Martinez, California, for a decade, though he frequently returned to the mountains. He became a U.S. citizen in 1903. His legacy includes the Sierra Club (now with over 2.4 million members), numerous national parks, and a profound influence on environmental thought. His writings continue to inspire conservation efforts and are celebrated for shaping America's relationship with its wild landscapes.

## Terms
- **Preservationism**: The philosophy, championed by Muir, that natural areas should be protected from all human exploitation and development.
- **Conservationism**: The philosophy, associated with Gifford Pinchot, advocating for the sustainable, managed use of natural resources for long-term human benefit.
- **Sierra Club**: A prominent American environmental organization co-founded by Muir in 1892 to help protect the Sierra Nevada.
- **Transcendentalism**: A philosophical movement emphasizing the inherent goodness of people and nature, which influenced Muir's spiritual view of the wilderness.
- **Glacial Theory**: Muir's scientifically contested and later proven idea that glaciers carved major landscape features like Yosemite Valley.
- **Hetch Hetchy Valley**: A valley in Yosemite National Park dammed for San Francisco's water supply, a defeat that galvanized the modern environmental movement.
- **Complete Preservationism**: Muir's belief that plants, animals, and natural resources should be completely off-limits to human exploitation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Muir's Racial Views**: Muir's legacy is complicated by derogatory comments about Native Americans and African Americans in his early writings, though his views reportedly evolved later in life. The Sierra Club has acknowledged this "complex and controversial legacy" and its impact on people of color.
- **Preservation vs. Conservation**: The fundamental debate between Muir's preservationism and Pinchot's conservationism over the proper human relationship with wilderness remains a central tension in environmental policy.
- **Impact of Native Americans**: Muir saw wilderness as pristine and free from human influence, not recognizing that landscapes he loved had been shaped by Native American practices like controlled burns for millennia.
