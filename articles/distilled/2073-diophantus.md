# Diophantus

## Overview
Diophantus of Alexandria (fl. c. 250 CE) was a Greek mathematician whose major work, *Arithmetica*, established the foundation for algebra by systematically solving arithmetic problems through equations. His methods, which focused on finding integer solutions to polynomial equations, directly influenced the development of algebra in the medieval Islamic world and Renaissance Europe, and his name is immortalized in the term "Diophantine equations."

## Timeline
- **c. 170 BCE – 350 CE** — Probable lifespan of Diophantus, based on references by Hypsicles and Theon of Alexandria.
- **c. 250 CE** — Diophantus flourishes; his work is later quoted by Theon of Alexandria (4th century).
- **9th century CE** — *Arithmetica* is translated into Arabic by Qusta ibn Luqa, influencing Islamic algebra.
- **1575** — First published Latin edition of *Arithmetica* by Xylander.
- **1621** — Bachet's influential Latin edition of *Arithmetica* is published.
- **1670** — Fermat's son publishes an edition containing his father's famous "Last Theorem" annotation.
- **1968** — Four lost books (IV-VII) of *Arithmetica* are discovered in an Arabic translation in Iran.
- **1994** — Andrew Wiles provides a proof for Fermat's Last Theorem, a problem inspired by Diophantus.

## Body

### Biography and Epitaph
The exact dates of Diophantus's life are uncertain, but he likely lived in the 3rd century CE. The only specific biographical detail comes from a mathematical puzzle in the *Greek Anthology* (5th or 6th century CE) which describes his life in fractions of his age. Solving the puzzle yields an age of 84, though this information cannot be confirmed.

### The Arithmetica
Diophantus's principal work, *Arithmetica*, is a collection of 290 algebraic problems. Originally in thirteen books, ten survive: six in Greek (Books I-III, VIII-X) and four in an Arabic translation (Books IV-VII). The work is the earliest extant text to solve arithmetic problems using algebraic methods, though Diophantus did not invent algebra himself. He introduced a symbolic notation for unknowns and their powers, using abbreviations and a three-step solution process: naming the unknown, simplifying the equation, and solving it. Unlike modern notation, his system lacked symbols for operations and placed coefficients after variables.

### Mathematical Content and Method
*Arithmetica* focuses on finding integer solutions to equations with integer coefficients, now called Diophantine equations. Most problems lead to quadratic equations, which Diophantus categorized into three types based on the arrangement of positive terms, as he did not use zero or negative coefficients. He accepted rational (fractional) solutions but rejected negative or irrational results as "absurd." He also explored simultaneous quadratic equations and problems involving squares and cubes. Notably, he observed that numbers of the form 4n+3 cannot be the sum of two squares and may have known that every number is the sum of four squares, a result later proven by Lagrange.

### Other Works and Influence
Diophantus wrote other works, including *On Polygonal Numbers* (incomplete) and the lost *Porisms* and *On Parts*. His work was studied in Late Antiquity by Neoplatonic scholars like Hypatia. After being lost in Western Europe during the Middle Ages, *Arithmetica* was reintroduced via Arabic translations and Latin editions, profoundly influencing European algebra. Pierre de Fermat's study of the 1621 edition led to his famous "Last Theorem." Diophantus's concept of "adequality" (approximate equality) also influenced Fermat's method for finding maxima.

## Terms
- **Diophantine equation**: A polynomial equation with integer coefficients for which only integer solutions are sought.
- **Arithmetica**: The major work of Diophantus, a collection of algebraic problems solved through equations.
- **Indeterminate equation**: An equation with infinitely many solutions; Diophantus sought specific integer solutions.
- **Determinate equation**: An equation with a unique solution.
- **Al-jabr and al-muqābala**: Medieval Arabic terms for algebraic simplification procedures, similar to Diophantus's methods.
- **Polygonal numbers**: Numbers represented as dots forming regular polygons, a topic of interest to the Pythagoreans.
- **Adequality (parisotēs)**: A term coined by Diophantus for approximate equality, later used by Fermat.
- **Logistic**: A branch of mathematics distinct from arithmetic, focusing on calculation, where Diophantus's work was placed by later scholars.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Exact dates**: Diophantus's lifespan is debated, with estimates ranging from 170 BCE to 350 CE.
- **Scope of knowledge**: It is uncertain whether Diophantus had a proof that every number is the sum of four squares or merely conjectured it.
- **Fermat's claim**: Scholars debate whether Fermat actually had a valid proof for his Last Theorem, which he claimed was too large for the margin of his *Arithmetica*.
- **Lost works**: The content of lost works like *Porisms* and *On Parts* is only partially known from references.
