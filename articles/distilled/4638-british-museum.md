# British Museum

## Overview
The British Museum is a public institution in London dedicated to human history, art, and culture. Established in 1753, it was the world's first public national museum. Its permanent collection of over eight million objects documents human culture from its beginnings to the present, making it one of the largest and most comprehensive in the world. The museum's growth was largely fueled by British colonisation and global exploration, and it remains a major centre for scholarship and public education, though its collections are subject to ongoing debates about repatriation.

## Timeline
- **1753** — British Museum Act 1753 establishes the museum, based on Sir Hans Sloane's collection.
- **1759** — Museum opens to the public in Montagu House.
- **1802** — Rosetta Stone acquired; building expansion plans begin.
- **1816** — Elgin Marbles (Parthenon Sculptures) acquired by the museum.
- **1857** — Round Reading Room opens.
- **1881** — Natural History collections separated, forming the Natural History Museum.
- **1973** — British Library Act separates the library from the museum.
- **1997** — British Library moves to St Pancras, freeing space.
- **2000** — Queen Elizabeth II Great Court opens.
- **2025** — Museum receives 6,440,120 visitors, the second most in the UK.

## Body

### Foundation and Early Growth
The museum was founded on the bequest of Sir Hans Sloane, whose vast collection of 71,000 objects was offered to the nation for £20,000. The British Museum Act 1753 also incorporated the Cottonian and Harleian libraries, creating a national museum and library. It opened in 1759 in Montagu House. Early expansion focused on natural history and books, but the 1772 acquisition of Sir William Hamilton's Greek vases marked a turn towards antiquities. The 19th century saw major acquisitions like the Rosetta Stone (1802) and the Elgin Marbles (1816), alongside the construction of Sir Robert Smirke's neoclassical building.

### Expansion and Specialisation
Throughout the 19th and 20th centuries, the museum's collections grew dramatically through excavations and purchases, often linked to the British Empire. Key additions included Assyrian sculptures from Nimrud and Nineveh, the Sutton Hoo ship burial treasures, and the Standard of Ur. The natural history collections were moved to a new building in 1881, becoming the Natural History Museum. The British Library was formally separated in 1973 but remained in the building until 1997. Its departure allowed for the creation of the Great Court in 2000.

### Governance and Building
The museum is a non-departmental public body sponsored by the Department for Culture, Media and Sport. It is governed by a board of 25 trustees. The main building, designed by Sir Robert Smirke, is a Greek Revival structure with a famous Ionic colonnade. Major architectural additions include Sydney Smirke's Round Reading Room (1857) and the Great Court (2000), the largest covered public square in Europe. The museum covers over 92,000 m² but displays less than 1% of its collection at any time.

### Departments and Collections
The museum's collections are organised into departments. The Department of Egypt and Sudan holds over 100,000 objects, including mummies and the Rosetta Stone. The Department of Greece and Rome houses the Parthenon Marbles and other Classical antiquities. The Department of the Middle East contains the world's largest collection of Mesopotamian artefacts outside Iraq, including Assyrian palace reliefs. Other departments cover Prints and Drawings; Britain, Europe and Prehistory; Asia; Africa, Oceania and the Americas; and Coins and Medals. Conservation and scientific research are conducted in-house.

### Controversies and Criticism
The museum faces significant criticism over the provenance of key objects. The Elgin Marbles (claimed by Greece), Benin Bronzes (claimed by Nigeria), and Rosetta Stone (claimed by Egypt) are among the most contested. The museum argues that its collections are held in trust for global humanity and that the British Museum Act 1963 prevents the disposal of objects. Other controversies include protests over sponsorship by the oil company BP and major thefts of artefacts, including a significant incident in 2023 involving a staff member.

## Terms
- **Non-departmental public body**: A UK government organisation that is not part of a government department but is sponsored by one.
- **Repatriation**: The return of cultural property to its country or place of origin.
- **Cuneiform**: An ancient system of writing using wedge-shaped marks, used in Mesopotamia.
- **Numismatics**: The study or collection of currency, including coins, tokens, and paper money.
- **Ethnography**: The scientific description of the customs of individual peoples and cultures.
- **Provenance**: The origin and chain of ownership of an object, crucial for establishing legitimacy.
- **Spoliation**: The act of plundering, especially in wartime; often refers to Nazi-looted art.
- **Great Court**: The central covered square of the museum, redesigned by Foster and Partners and opened in 2000.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the ethics and legality of possessing artefacts acquired during the colonial era or under contested circumstances. Countries like Greece, Nigeria, and Egypt continue to press for the return of major items. The museum maintains that its universal collection serves a broader educational purpose and is protected by law. A secondary debate involves the museum's financial relationships, particularly its sponsorship by BP, which critics argue conflicts with its educational mission. The 2023 thefts have also raised serious questions about the museum's security and governance.
