# Aaron Copland

## Overview  
Aaron Copland (1900–1990) was an American composer, pianist, conductor, teacher, and writer whose accessible, open-harmony style defined mid-20th-century American classical music. Known as the "Dean of American Music," he fused modernist technique with folk and jazz influences to create works like *Appalachian Spring*, *Billy the Kid*, and *Fanfare for the Common Man*, shaping both concert and film music.

## Body  

### Early Life and Training  
Born into a Conservative Jewish family of Lithuanian immigrants, Copland grew up in Brooklyn above his father’s dry-goods store. His mother encouraged music lessons; his sister Laurine gave him early piano training. At 16, hearing Paderewski inspired him to pursue composition. He studied harmony and theory with Rubin Goldmark (1917–1921), gaining grounding in the Germanic tradition but feeling constrained by its limits.

### Paris and Boulanger  
In 1921, Copland traveled to Paris, studying at the Fontainebleau School with Isidor Philipp and Paul Vidal before switching to Nadia Boulanger. Her rigorous, open-minded mentorship broadened his musical horizons and fostered his eclectic taste. He absorbed modernist trends, attended performances, and began writing music criticism. Returning to the U.S. in 1924, he committed to a career as a composer despite financial struggles during the Depression.

### Shift to Accessibility  
Initially writing in a modernist idiom, Copland found little public or financial success. The Great Depression prompted a stylistic shift toward accessibility, aligning with the German concept of *Gebrauchsmusik*—music serving both artistic and utilitarian purposes. Works like *El Salón México* (1932–1936) and *Billy the Kid* (1938) used folk tunes and simplified harmonies to evoke American themes, achieving widespread acclaim.

### Signature Works and Nationalism  
The 1940s marked Copland’s peak productivity. *Appalachian Spring* (1944), *Rodeo* (1942), and *Fanfare for the Common Man* (1942) became patriotic standards. His "populist" style employed open diatonic harmonies and folk-derived melodies, often syncopated or reharmonized, to create a distinctly American sound. Critics praised his ability to blend simplicity with structural sophistication, though some accused him of pandering.

### Film Scores and Public Engagement  
Copland composed scores for *Of Mice and Men* (1939), *The Heiress* (1949), and others, integrating his personal style rather than following Hollywood conventions. He avoided leitmotifs and understated emotional cues, instead matching themes to action. His film work expanded his audience and reinforced his image as a voice of American democracy.

### Serial Techniques and Later Career  
In the late 1940s, Copland explored twelve-tone methods after encountering Boulez and Webern. Unlike Schoenberg, he treated tone rows as melodic/harmonic sources, not structural absolutes. Major serial works include the *Piano Quartet* (1950), *Piano Fantasy* (1957), *Connotations* (1961), and *Inscape* (1967). From the 1960s, he focused increasingly on conducting, recording his own and others’ works, though he lamented creative stagnation.

### Political Context and Legacy  
Copland’s leftist affiliations led to FBI surveillance and blacklisting during the McCarthy era. He was included on an FBI list of 151 artists thought to have Communist associations, and *A Lincoln Portrait* was withdrawn from the 1953 inaugural concert for President Eisenhower. Though investigated, he was never charged. His music remained influential, earning him the Presidential Medal of Freedom (September 14, 1964, presented by President Lyndon B. Johnson) and National Medal of Arts (1986). He died of Alzheimer's disease and respiratory failure on December 2, 1990, in North Tarrytown, New York (now Sleepy Hollow); his estate funded the Aaron Copland Fund for Composers.

## Terms  
- **Gebrauchsmusik**: German term meaning "utility music," composed for practical use rather than concert performance.  
- **Twelve-tone technique**: A method of musical composition devised by Arnold Schoenberg using all twelve chromatic tones without a tonal center.  
- **Populist style**: Copland’s accessible musical idiom incorporating folk, jazz, and open harmonies to reflect American identity.  
- **Vernacular style**: Copland’s term for his approach rooted in everyday American musical expression.  
- **Dodecaphony**: Synonym for twelve-tone composition.  
- **Leitmotif**: A recurring musical theme associated with a character or idea, commonly used in opera and film scores.  
- **Serialism**: Broader term encompassing twelve-tone and other systematic compositional approaches.  
- **Open harmony**: Sparse, diatonic chord structures that create a sense of space and clarity.  
- **Americanism in music**: Stylistic traits reflecting national identity through folk, jazz, and landscape-inspired elements.  
- **Dean of American Music**: Informal title recognizing Copland’s central role in defining American classical music.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Copland’s embrace of accessibility sparked debate: while some saw it as democratic and patriotic, others—including composer David Diamond—accused him of "selling out" to commercialism. His use of serial techniques in later works divided critics, with some praising innovation and others viewing it as inconsistent with his earlier voice. His political associations during the Red Scare raised questions about artistic integrity versus social responsibility. Additionally, interpretations of his sexuality and its influence on his work remain underexplored due to his guarded personal life.
