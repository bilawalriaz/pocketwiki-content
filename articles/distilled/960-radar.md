# Radar

## Overview
Radar (Radio Detection and Ranging) is a radiodetermination system that transmits radio or microwave signals and analyzes reflected echoes to determine the range, angle, and radial velocity of objects. Developed secretly by multiple nations before and during World War II, radar provided a decisive military advantage—most notably enabling the Royal Air Force to win the Battle of Britain via the Chain Home early-warning network. A pivotal breakthrough was the British cavity magnetron (1940), which enabled compact, high-resolution microwave radar. Today, radar underpins air traffic control, weather forecasting, maritime navigation, automotive safety, astronomy, and remote sensing, increasingly employing digital signal processing, phased arrays, and machine learning to extract signals from noise.

## Timeline
- **1886** — Heinrich Hertz demonstrates radio waves reflect off solid objects.
- **1904** — Christian Hülsmeyer patents the *telemobiloscope*, the first device to detect distant metallic objects via radio waves (ship detection, no ranging).
- **1922** — U.S. Navy researchers Taylor and Young detect signal fading caused by ships passing through a radio beam on the Potomac River.
- **Dec 1934** — Robert M. Page (U.S. Naval Research Laboratory) demonstrates the first elementary pulsed radar apparatus.
- **Feb 1935** — Daventry Experiment (UK): Watson-Watt and Wilkins detect a bomber using BBC transmitter signals, proving aircraft detection feasibility.
- **1935–1936** — Pulsed radar demonstrated in Germany (Kühnhold/GEMA) and UK (Watson-Watt team); Chain Home station deployment begins.
- **1939** — Chain Home network operational across UK; Soviet RUS-1/RUS-2 Redut radars enter mass production (607 units total).
- **1940** — Tizard Mission shares cavity magnetron with U.S., enabling centimetric radar; U.S. Navy coins acronym "RADAR".
- **1941–1945** — MIT Radiation Laboratory develops microwave radar; Page invents monopulse technique (1943).
- **1943** — First Soviet airborne radar, Gneiss-2, enters service on Pe-2 dive bombers.
- **1960s** — Pulse compression introduced, combining long-pulse energy with short-pulse resolution; phased arrays deployed (Mammut, Safeguard).
- **2017** — NOAA plans national network of multi-function phased array radars for meteorology and flight monitoring.

## Body

### Early Experiments and Precursors (1886–1934)
The physics foundation was laid by Heinrich Hertz (1886), who showed radio waves reflect off conductors. Alexander Popov (1895–1897) observed interference from a passing ship but did not pursue it. Christian Hülsmeyer (1904) built the first practical detection device, the *telemobiloscope*, using a spark-gap transmitter, horn antenna, and parabolic reflector to detect ships in fog; it earned patents but was rejected by German military officials. In 1922, U.S. Navy researchers A. Hoyt Taylor and Leo C. Young noted signal fading from ships on the Potomac and proposed detection use, but the Navy did not act. Eight years later, Lawrence A. Hyland observed similar fading from aircraft at the Naval Research Laboratory (NRL), prompting a patent and research proposal. In the UK, W.A.S. Butement and P.E. Pollard built a 50 cm pulsed test unit (1931), recorded as the first official British record of the technology later used in Chain Home Low.

### Pre-War Development and the Birth of Operational Radar (1934–1939)
Pulsed radar—the basis of modern systems—was first demonstrated by Robert M. Page at NRL in December 1934. Parallel developments followed in Germany (Rudolf Kühnhold/GEMA, May 1935) and the UK (Watson-Watt team, June 1935). The UK effort accelerated after the Air Ministry asked Watson-Watt to evaluate a rumored German "death ray"; his assistant Arnold Wilkins calculated it impossible but recalled aircraft-induced radio fading. The resulting Daventry Experiment (26 Feb 1935) detected a bomber via BBC shortwave signals, impressing Hugh Dowding and unlocking funding. Watson-Watt became superintendent of Bawdsey Research Station (1936), where the Chain Home (CH) network was designed. CH used powerful floodlighting transmitters and radio direction finders rather than steerable beams, allowing rapid deployment with existing tech. By 1940, CH covered the entire UK, feeding the "Dowding System" of fighter control that proved decisive in the Battle of Britain. France (CSF, 1934), the USSR (P.K. Oshchepkov, RAPID/RUS-1/RUS-2), and Japan also pursued radar, though French and Soviet systems used continuous-wave operation lacking full pulsed performance. Oshchepkov’s arrest slowed Soviet progress; only 607 Redut stations were built during the war.

### World War II: Cavity Magnetron and Microwave Radar
The cavity magnetron, developed in the UK, generated high-power microwaves, enabling compact, sub-meter-resolution radars for aircraft interception and naval gunnery. Britain shared it with the U.S. via the 1940 Tizard Mission. Alfred Lee Loomis established the secret MIT Radiation Laboratory (1941–45), which became the primary Allied microwave radar developer. Robert Page introduced the monopulse technique (1943), improving angle tracking accuracy. Wartime needs drove specialized radars: airborne interception (night fighters), air-to-surface-vessel (maritime patrol), and navigation aids like Oboe for RAF Pathfinders. Watson-Watt advised U.S. air defense after Pearl Harbor (1941).

### Principles of Operation
**Signal and Illumination:** A transmitter emits radio waves (illumination) toward a target. Conductive materials (metal, seawater, wet ground) reflect strongly; radar-absorbent materials reduce reflection (stealth). **Reflection Physics:** Scattering depends on wavelength vs. target size. Long wavelengths (early radar) exceed target size, yielding vague signals via Rayleigh scattering. Modern centimetric/millimetric waves (cm or less) resolve small objects. Corner reflectors (three mutually perpendicular surfaces) return energy directly to source, used on boats for visibility; stealth aircraft avoid right angles. **Radar Range Equation:** Received power $P_r \propto P_t G_t A_r \sigma F^4 / R^4$ (for monostatic radar). The $R^{-4}$ dependence makes distant returns extremely weak. **Doppler Effect:** Radial motion shifts frequency by $F_D = 2 F_T V_R / C$ (active radar). This enables velocity measurement and moving-target discrimination but introduces ambiguity if $|F_D| \ge F_R/2$ (Nyquist limit), where $F_R$ is pulse repetition frequency. **Polarization:** Horizontal, vertical, linear, or circular polarization discriminates target types (e.g., circular minimizes rain clutter; linear highlights metal). **Limiting Factors:** Range is bounded by line-of-sight (radar horizon), maximum unambiguous range ($c/2F_R$), and sensitivity (radar equation). Noise (internal thermal/shot noise, external background) sets the detection floor; signal processing (integration, pulse-Doppler filtering) improves signal-to-noise ratio. Clutter (ground, sea, weather, chaff, multipath) is mitigated by Doppler processing, moving-target indicators, polarization selection, constant false-alarm rate (CFAR) gain control, and terrain maps. Jamming (intentional or not) exploits the one-way path advantage; countermeasures include narrow mainlobes, sidelobe suppression, frequency hopping, and polarization agility.

### Signal Processing
**Distance Measurement:** *Transit time* (pulse ranging): $R = c \Delta t / 2$. Pulse width limits minimum range; pulse repetition frequency limits maximum unambiguous range. Modern radars use dual PRFs or staggered pulses for wide range coverage. *Frequency modulation (FM/CW):* Frequency difference between transmitted and received signals gives range; used in radar altimeters (AZUSA, MISTRAM). *Pulse compression:* Long, frequency-modulated pulses provide energy; matched filtering on receive compresses them to short pulses for resolution and SNR gain—universal on modern large radars. **Speed Measurement:** Coherent transmitters enable Doppler velocity measurement (radial component only). Continuous-wave (CW) radar measures speed without pulsing (traffic enforcement). Pulsed radar uses phase change between pulses. Advanced transforms (wavelet, chirplet) analyze time-frequency structure. **Pulse-Doppler Processing:** Range gates are Doppler-filtered independently, separating targets by velocity. Essential for "look-down/shoot-down" (detecting low-flying targets against ground clutter) and weather radar (radial wind/precipitation). Requires medium PRF, phase coherence, and range-ambiguity resolution. **Plot/Track Extraction:** Plot extractors discard spurious returns; track algorithms (Nearest Neighbour, Probabilistic Data Association, Multiple Hypothesis Tracking, Interactive Multiple Model) correlate plots over time to estimate trajectories for air traffic control, fire control, and missile guidance.

### Engineering Implementation
**Components:** Transmitter (oscillator: klystron, magnetron, solid-state + modulator), waveguide, duplexer (T/R switch), receiver (matched filter), signal/data processor, display, antenna controller, user interfaces. **Antenna Designs:** *Parabolic reflector:* Symmetric (pencil beam, high gain, e.g., NEXRAD weather radar) or spoiled (fan beam: narrow azimuth/wide elevation for 2D surveillance, or narrow elevation/wide azimuth for height finders). *Slotted waveguide:* Mechanically scanned, low cost, low wind load—common on ships/airports. *Phased array:* Electronic beam steering via phase shifters across many elements. No moving parts, multi-target tracking, graceful degradation. Used in Aegis, Patriot, B-1B, MiG-31, F-22 (AESA). NOAA plans national phased-array weather network. **Frequency Bands:** Traditional WWII code names (L, S, C, X, Ku, K, Ka, mm-wave) persist. Shorter wavelengths → smaller antennas, higher resolution, but greater atmospheric attenuation, component complexity, and aperture-efficiency trade-offs. **Modulators:** *High-voltage switch* (thyratron + pulse-forming network) for non-coherent magnetrons (pulsed power). *Hybrid mixers* (waveform generator + exciter) for coherent klystron/solid-state amplifiers, enabling intrapulse modulation and pulse compression. **Coolant:** High-power microwave tubes (klystrons, TWTs >1 kW) require liquid cooling (deionized water) to manage heat and prevent plasma-induced FM noise degrading Doppler performance. Coolanol (silicate ester) used in 1970s military radars but phased out due to flammability, toxicity, and environmental concerns (Navy P2 program).

### Configurations and Regulations
**Configurations:** Bistatic, continuous-wave (CW), Doppler, FM-CW, monopulse, passive (uses external illuminators), planar array, pulse-Doppler, synthetic-aperture radar (SAR), over-the-horizon (OTH) with chirp. **Regulation:** ITU Radio Regulations (Art. 1.100) define radar as a radiodetermination system comparing reference signals with reflected/retransmitted signals, classified by radiocommunication service (radiolocation, radiolocation-satellite).

## Terms
- ****Cavity Magnetron**** — High-power vacuum tube generating microwaves via electron interaction with resonant cavities; enabled compact, high-resolution WWII radar.
- ****Pulse Compression**** — Technique using long, frequency-modulated pulses for energy, then matched filtering on receive to compress pulse width for resolution and SNR gain.
- ****Phased Array**** — Antenna with many elements whose phases are electronically controlled to steer beam without moving parts; allows multi-target tracking and rapid scanning.
- ****Radar Cross-Section (σ)**** — Measure of a target's reflectivity (scattering coefficient), determining echo strength; units m².
- ****Pulse Repetition Frequency (PRF)**** — Rate at which radar transmits pulses; determines maximum unambiguous range ($c/2\cdot\text{PRF}$) and Doppler Nyquist limit.
- ****Monopulse**** — Angle-measurement technique comparing simultaneous beams (sum/difference) for high-precision tracking; invented by Robert Page (1943).
- ****Clutter**** — Unwanted echoes from ground, sea, weather, birds, chaff, or multipath; mitigated by Doppler, polarization, CFAR, and terrain maps.
- ****Synthetic-Aperture Radar (SAR)**** — Coherent processing of returns from a moving platform to synthesize a large aperture, achieving high cross-range resolution for imaging.
- ****Duplexer**** — T/R switch protecting receiver from high-power transmit pulse while sharing a single antenna.
- ****Radar Horizon**** — Effective line-of-sight limit due to Earth's curvature and atmospheric refraction; limits low-altitude detection range.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origin Priority:** Multiple nations (UK, US, Germany, USSR, France, Japan, Netherlands) developed radar independently pre-WWII; exact "first" claims vary by criterion (pulsed vs. continuous-wave, demonstration vs. deployment).
- **Soviet Development Impact:** Arrest of P.K. Oshchepkov (1939) severely curtailed USSR radar production (only 607 Redut stations); counterfactual impact on Eastern Front air defense debated.
- **Clutter vs. Sensitivity Trade-offs:** CFAR and Doppler processing improve detection in clutter but can mask targets embedded in strong clutter or with near-zero radial velocity.
- **Phased Array Cost/Reliability Balance:** Electronic steering eliminates mechanical failure but increases component count; gradual degradation vs. sudden failure modes affect lifecycle cost models.
- **Quantum Noise Floor:** Ultimate detection limit set by thermal noise ($k_B T B$) and quantum nature of current (electron shot noise); matched filtering compresses energy but cannot overcome single-electron quantization.
- **Automotive Radar Standardization:** As driverless vehicles proliferate, interference between dense automotive radars and spectrum allocation (77–81 GHz) remain unresolved regulatory/technical challenges.
- **Over-the-Horizon Radar (OTH) Viability:** Ionospheric propagation enables beyond-line-of-sight detection but suffers from clutter, geomagnetic disturbances, and low resolution; operational utility for strategic vs. tactical use debated.
