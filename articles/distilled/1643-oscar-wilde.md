# Oscar Wilde

## Overview
Oscar Wilde (1854–1900) was an Irish author, poet, and playwright who became the most celebrated dramatist of late-Victorian London and a leading figure in the Aestheticism movement, which championed "art for art's sake." After a brilliant academic career at Trinity College Dublin and Magdalen College, Oxford—where he absorbed the influence of Walter Pater and John Ruskin—Wilde cultivated a public persona as a flamboyant wit and dandy. He achieved massive commercial success with society comedies like *Lady Windermere’s Fan* (1892) and *The Importance of Being Earnest* (1895), and scandal with his Gothic novel *The Picture of Dorian Gray* (1890). At the height of his fame, a libel suit against the Marquess of Queensberry—father of Wilde’s lover, Lord Alfred Douglas—backfired, exposing Wilde’s homosexual relationships. He was convicted of "gross indecency" in 1895 and sentenced to two years’ hard labour. Imprisonment broke his health and spirit; he wrote the profound letter *De Profundis* and the poem *The Ballad of Reading Gaol* (1898) in exile in France, where he died in poverty at 46. Posthumously pardoned in 2017, he is now widely regarded as the greatest playwright of the Victorian era.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Wilde was born into an Anglo-Irish intellectual family in Dublin. His father, Sir William Wilde, was a renowned ear and eye surgeon and antiquarian; his mother, Jane, wrote revolutionary poetry under the pseudonym "Speranza" and hosted a celebrated salon. Wilde excelled in classics at Portora Royal School, then at Trinity College Dublin (1871–74), where he won the Berkeley Gold Medal and worked with Professor J.P. Mahaffy on *Social Life in Greece*. At Magdalen College, Oxford (1874–78), he read Greats, won the Newdigate Prize for his poem "Ravenna," and graduated with a double first. At Oxford, he fell under the spell of Walter Pater’s *Studies in the History of the Renaissance*, which taught that life should be lived as a work of art, and John Ruskin, who argued art must serve moral good. Wilde also briefly embraced Freemasonry and seriously considered converting to Catholicism, though his father threatened to cut off funds. He cultivated an aesthetic persona—long hair, peacock feathers, blue china—becoming the visible embodiment of the "art for art's sake" movement.

### Apprenticeship and American Tour (1880s)
After Oxford, Wilde moved to London, publishing *Poems* (1881) to mixed reviews. In 1882, impresario Richard D’Oyly Carte sent him on a lecture tour of the United States and Canada to promote the Gilbert and Sullivan operetta *Patience*, which satirized aestheticism. Wilde lectured on "The English Renaissance" and interior decoration for nearly a year, famously claiming "life imitates art." He was both celebrated and mercilessly caricatured in the press, often with anti-Irish and racist imagery. Returning to London, he married Constance Lloyd in 1884. They renovated a house at 16 Tite Street, Chelsea, and had two sons. Wilde edited *The Woman’s World* magazine (1887–89), raising its tone with serious articles on politics and culture, but grew bored with administration. In 1886, he met Robert Ross, who, by Wilde’s later account, initiated him into homosexual sex. Wilde also frequented the Café Royal, Hatchards, and Harrods, establishing himself as a central figure in London’s literary and social scene.

### Prose Writing and *The Picture of Dorian Gray* (1886–1891)
Wilde published fairy tales (*The Happy Prince and Other Tales*, 1888) and the story collection *Lord Arthur Savile’s Crime* (1891). His critical essays, collected in *Intentions* (1891), include *The Decay of Lying* and *The Critic as Artist*, dialogues asserting that art is superior to life and criticism is itself an art. In *The Soul of Man Under Socialism* (1891), he argued for the abolition of private property and a society freed by mechanization for artistic creation—a vision George Orwell later summarized as "a world populated by artists." His only novel, *The Picture of Dorian Gray*, appeared in *Lippincott’s Monthly Magazine* (July 1890). It tells of a man who remains youthful while his portrait ages and records his corruption—a "Faustian bargain." Critics denounced its "decadence and homosexual subtext"; Wilde defended it, declaring art is neither moral nor immoral, only well or badly made. He revised it for book publication in 1891, adding a preface of epigrams ("Books are well written, or badly written. That is all.").

### Theatrical Triumphs (1892–1895)
Wilde turned to drama, writing *Salomé* (1891) in French for Sarah Bernhardt. The Lord Chamberlain banned it for depicting biblical characters. Undeterred, Wilde wrote four society comedies. *Lady Windermere’s Fan* (1892) subverted Victorian morality with "collusive concealment" rather than exposure. *A Woman of No Importance* (1893) and *An Ideal Husband* (1895) followed. *The Importance of Being Earnest* (14 Feb 1895), his masterpiece, is a "trivial comedy for serious people" built on "bunburying" (inventing fictitious personas to escape social duties). It lacks the stock "woman with a past" and overt decadence of earlier works, presenting idle *cultivés* in drawing-room farce. H.G. Wells praised its "Gilbertian" humour spangled with Wilde’s unique wit. The play’s opening night was a triumph, cementing Wilde’s reputation.

### The Queensberry Feud and Trials
In 1891, Wilde met Lord Alfred Douglas, an Oxford undergraduate. Their intense, indiscreet relationship drew the fury of Douglas’s father, the Marquess of Queensberry, creator of modern boxing rules. Queensberry harassed Wilde, leaving a card at his club in February 1895: "For Oscar Wilde, posing somdomite [sic]." Against friends’ advice, Wilde sued for criminal libel. At the Old Bailey (April 1895), Queensberry’s counsel, Edward Carson—Wilde’s fellow Dubliner and Trinity alumnus—cross-examined Wilde on his works and his friendships with working-class youths. Wilde’s wit faltered when pressed on kissing a servant boy; he admitted the boy’s ugliness was relevant, prompting Carson’s trap. Facing witnesses ready to testify to sexual acts, Wilde dropped the prosecution. Queensberry was acquitted, his accusation deemed "true in substance," leaving Wilde bankrupt.

Wilde was immediately arrested for "gross indecency" (homosexual acts not amounting to buggery). At his first trial (April 1895), he delivered a famous defense of "the love that dare not speak its name," citing David and Jonathan, Plato, and Shakespeare. The jury deadlocked. At the retrial (May 1895), before Mr Justice Wills, Wilde and Alfred Taylor were convicted and sentenced to two years’ hard labour—the maximum. The judge called it "totally inadequate for a case such as this."

### Imprisonment and *De Profundis*
Wilde served his sentence at Pentonville, Wandsworth, and Reading Gaol. "Hard labour" meant treadmill walking and picking oakum. His health collapsed; he ruptured an eardrum in a fall, an injury later contributing to his death. Transferred to Reading through the intervention of reformer Richard Haldane, he was known only as "C.3.3." The hanging of fellow prisoner Charles Thomas Wooldridge (July 1896) inspired *The Ballad of Reading Gaol*. Granted writing materials, Wilde composed a 50,000-word letter to Douglas, *De Profundis* (Jan–Mar 1897). In it, he dissects their relationship, blaming Douglas for his vanity and himself for moral weakness: "I am here for having tried to put your father in prison." The letter traces a spiritual journey from aesthetic hedonism to a Christianity of suffering: "I wanted to eat of the fruit of all the trees in the garden of the world... My only mistake was that I confined myself so exclusively to the trees of what seemed to me the sun-lit side of the garden."

### Exile and Death (1897–1900)
Released in May 1897, Wilde sailed for Dieppe, adopting the name "Sebastian Melmoth." He wrote two letters to the *Daily Chronicle* advocating prison reform. *The Ballad of Reading Gaol* (1898), published under "C.3.3.," was a commercial success, its refrain "each man kills the thing he loves" linking the executed murderer to the poet. Wilde briefly reunited with Douglas in Rouen and Naples, but families forced their separation. Constance died in 1898. In Paris, Wilde lived in poverty at the Hôtel d'Alsace, drinking heavily, his writing joy gone. He assisted Émile Zola during the Dreyfus Affair, passing on a document implicating the real traitor, Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy. By November 1900, he developed meningitis, likely from the old prison ear injury. On 29 November, Robert Ross summoned Fr Cuthbert Dunne, who conditionally baptised Wilde into the Catholic Church and administered Extreme Unction. Wilde died on 30 November 1900, aged 46. He was buried in Bagneux; in 1909 his remains moved to Père Lachaise Cemetery, where Jacob Epstein’s modernist tomb—commissioned by Ross—became a pilgrimage site.

## Terms
- **Aestheticism**: Late-19th-century movement championing "art for art's sake," prioritizing beauty and sensory experience over moral or didactic purpose; associated with Walter Pater and Wilde.
- **Greats (Literae Humaniores)**: Oxford’s rigorous undergraduate course in classics, ancient history, and philosophy; Wilde took a double first in it.
- **Newdigate Prize**: Annual Oxford poetry prize; Wilde won in 1878 for "Ravenna."
- **Faustian bargain**: A pact trading spiritual integrity for worldly gain; in *Dorian Gray*, the protagonist’s soul for eternal youth.
- **Bunburying**: Wilde’s coinage in *The Importance of Being Earnest* for inventing a fictitious persona or obligation to escape social duties.
- **Gross indecency**: Legal term under Section 11 of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885, criminalizing homosexual acts between men short of buggery (anal intercourse).
- **Libel Act 1843**: Statute allowing a defendant in a criminal libel case to avoid conviction by proving the truth of the libel and "public benefit" in publishing it.
- **De Profundis**: Latin for "out of the depths" (Psalm 130); Wilde’s 50,000-word prison letter to Lord Alfred Douglas, published posthumously.
- **The Ballad of Reading Gaol**: Wilde’s 1898 poem on prison brutality, published under his cell number "C.3.3.," contrasting the executed murderer with the collective suffering of convicts.
- **Alan Turing Law**: Informal name for the Policing and Crime Act 2017, which posthumously pardoned men convicted of historical homosexual offences no longer crimes.

## Debates and open questions
- **Cause of death**: Richard Ellmann (1987) attributed Wilde’s meningitis to syphilis; Merlin Holland (grandson) and Wilde’s physicians attributed it to an old suppuration of the right ear from the prison fall, treated for years, with no syphilitic evidence.
- **Nature of Wilde’s sexual relationships with youths**: The 2000 libel trial transcript suggests Wilde engaged with teenagers (e.g., Alphonse Conway, 16; Walter Grainger, 16). Antony Edmonds argues Wilde would face prosecution today for paying for sex with minors (age of consent was 16 in 1895). The source notes the age of consent had been raised from 13 to 16 only in 1885.
- **Authorship of *De Profundis* edits**: Robert Ross published an abridged version in 1905; the full, unexpurgated text appeared only in 1962. Douglas denied receiving a copy.
- **Wilde’s political stance**: *The Soul of Man Under Socialism* advocates anarchist-individualist socialism (abolition of property, no government), distinct from Fabian state socialism; it inspired Russian revolutionaries but was suppressed under Stalin.
- **Literary merit of *Dorian Gray***: Critics range from Robert McCrum (Guardian: top 100 English novels) to Robin McKie (technically mediocre, plot device unexploited). Wilde himself called the plot "as old as the history of literature."
