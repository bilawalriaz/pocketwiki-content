# Hip-hop

## Overview
Hip-hop is a genre of popular music and associated culture that emerged in the early 1970s from African-American and Afro-Caribbean communities in New York City. It is defined by a synthesis of techniques, most notably rapping, and is culturally significant as a voice for the disenfranchised, commenting on social issues while borrowing from its environment. Its evolution from local block parties to the bestselling music genre in the United States by 2017 demonstrates its profound global influence.

## Timeline
- **August 11, 1973** — Kool Herc hosts a party at 1520 Sedgwick Avenue, often cited as the birth of hip-hop.
- **1979** — The Sugarhill Gang's "Rapper's Delight" becomes a Top 40 hit, bringing hip-hop to a national audience.
- **1982** — Grandmaster Flash and the Furious Five release "The Message," pioneering socially conscious rap.
- **1986** — Run-DMC's *Raising Hell* becomes the first hip-hop album certified platinum.
- **1988** — N.W.A's *Straight Outta Compton* establishes gangsta rap and Los Angeles as a hip-hop capital.
- **1989** — The first Grammy Award for Best Rap Performance is awarded.
- **1990** — MC Hammer's *Please Hammer, Don't Hurt 'Em* becomes the first hip-hop album certified diamond.
- **1992** — Dr. Dre's *The Chronic* popularizes the G-funk subgenre.
- **1996-1997** — The murders of Tupac Shakur and The Notorious B.I.G. mark the end of the East Coast–West Coast rivalry.
- **2007** — Kanye West's *Graduation* outsells 50 Cent's *Curtis*, signaling a shift in hip-hop's mainstream sound.
- **2017** — Hip-hop/R&B surpasses rock as the most consumed musical genre in the U.S.

## Body

### Origins and Early Culture (1970s)
Hip-hop emerged from block parties in the Bronx, where DJs like Kool Herc extended instrumental breaks from disco and funk records to create continuous dance music. These "breakbeats" provided the platform for breakdancers ("B-boys" and "B-girls") and MCs (Masters of Ceremonies) who initially hyped the crowd with chants and call-and-response. The culture comprised four elements: DJing, MCing (rapping), breakdancing, and graffiti art. Afrika Bambaataa's Universal Zulu Nation helped transform the scene into a positive social movement, channeling gang energy into creative competition.

### Old School and Mainstream Breakthrough (1979–1986)
Hip-hop transitioned from a live performance art to a recorded genre with the 1979 release of "Rapper's Delight." Early recordings were often disco-inflected. The "new school" emerged in the mid-1980s, characterized by drum machine minimalism, more aggressive lyrics, and cohesive albums. Artists like Run-DMC and LL Cool J brought hip-hop to mainstream pop charts, with Run-DMC's *Raising Hell* (1986) becoming the genre's first platinum album.

### Golden Age and Regional Diversification (1986–1997)
The period from the mid-1980s to mid-1990s is known as hip-hop's "golden age," marked by innovation, diversity, and critical acclaim. This era saw the rise of gangsta rap, which reflected inner-city violence and social commentary, exemplified by N.W.A. It also saw the emergence of alternative and jazz rap from the Native Tongues movement. Regional scenes flourished, notably on the East Coast (Wu-Tang Clan, Nas) and West Coast (Dr. Dre, Snoop Dogg), and in the South (Geto Boys, Outkast). The era was marred by the East Coast–West Coast rivalry, which culminated in the deaths of Tupac Shakur and The Notorious B.I.G.

### Commercial Dominance and the Internet (1997–2010s)
In the late 1990s and 2000s, hip-hop became commercially dominant, often pairing rappers with R&B singers. The "bling era" emphasized materialism, while the South rose to prominence with crunk and later trap music. The mid-2000s saw a decline in physical album sales due to digital piracy, but the internet also democratized distribution. The "blog era" allowed artists like Kanye West and Kid Cudi to build followings online, leading to a more introspective and alternative mainstream sound.

### Streaming Era and Global Reach (2010s–Present)
The 2010s were dominated by streaming platforms and the global spread of trap music, characterized by its dark atmosphere and Roland TR-808 drum machine sounds. Artists like Drake and Migos achieved massive success via SoundCloud and Spotify. By 2017, hip-hop became the most consumed genre in the U.S. The culture has been reinvented worldwide, with distinct scenes in Latin America, Europe, Africa, and Asia, all acknowledging its roots in the Bronx.

## Terms
- **Breakbeat:** An extended instrumental section of a record, looped by a DJ to provide a continuous rhythm for dancing.
- **MC (Master of Ceremonies):** The person who raps, hypes the crowd, and interacts with the audience at a hip-hop event.
- **Turntablism:** The art of manipulating sounds and creating new music using turntables and a DJ mixer.
- **Scratching:** A DJ technique where a vinyl record is moved back and forth on a turntable to produce percussive or rhythmic sounds.
- **Sampling:** The practice of taking a portion of a pre-existing sound recording and reusing it in a new song.
- **Gangsta rap:** A subgenre of hip-hop that reflects the violent environment of inner-city American black youths, often with explicit lyrics about crime and street life.
- **G-funk:** A subgenre of gangsta rap that emerged in the early 1990s, characterized by slow, heavy beats and melodic synthesizers.
- **Trap music:** A subgenre that originated in the Southern U.S., featuring double or triple-time hi-hats, heavy kick drums from the TR-808, and dark lyrical themes.
- **Bling era:** A period in the late 1990s and 2000s characterized by commercially oriented hip-hop that emphasized materialism and luxury.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise origin of the term "hip-hop" is debated, with multiple figures like Lovebug Starsky and Keef Cowboy claiming credit.
- The impact of gangsta rap remains contentious; critics argue it glorifies violence, while proponents like Sister Souljah argue it exposes societal contradictions.
- The rise of trap music has sparked debate about lyrical depth, with some artists and critics lamenting a perceived decline in lyricism, a style sometimes pejoratively labeled "mumble rap."
