# Abidjan

## Overview
Abidjan is the largest city and economic capital of Ivory Coast, serving as a major cultural and financial hub for West Africa. Its rapid growth from a small fishing village to a metropolis of over 6 million people reflects its colonial history, strategic port, and role as the center of Ivorian industry and commerce.

## Timeline
- **1898** — French colonists select the site for Abidjan after yellow fever epidemics force a move from Grand-Bassam.
- **1903** — Abidjan officially becomes a town.
- **1931** — Construction of a new wharf and the first floating bridge connecting Plateau and Treichville.
- **1934** — Abidjan is designated the capital of the French colony of Ivory Coast.
- **1951** — Completion of the Vridi Canal connects the Ébrié Lagoon to the sea, enabling Abidjan to become a major seaport.
- **1960** — Ivory Coast gains independence; Abidjan becomes the administrative and economic center.
- **1983** — Yamoussoukro is designated the official political capital, but Abidjan remains the economic capital.
- **2002–2007** — First Ivorian Civil War impacts the city's security.
- **2010–2011** — Second Ivorian Civil War and crisis; major conflict and violence in Abidjan.
- **2014** — Henri Konan Bédié Bridge, a toll bridge, is completed.
- **2021** — Census records Abidjan's population at 6.3 million.

## Body

### Colonial Foundation and Growth
Abidjan began as a small Atchan fishing village. French colonists chose its location in 1898 for its safety from yellow fever. It became a town in 1903 and the colony's capital in 1934, replacing Bingerville. The construction of the Vridi Canal in 1951 was pivotal, transforming Abidjan into a deep-water port and the financial center of West Africa by the late 1950s. The city was laid out on a grid plan, with segregated zones for colonists and natives.

### Post-Independence and Modern Challenges
After independence in 1960, Abidjan experienced explosive population growth, from 180,000 to over 1.2 million by 1978. This period saw the development of modern districts like Cocody. However, from the 1980s, the city's fortunes declined due to economic crisis and corruption. The political capital was moved to Yamoussoukro in 1983, but Abidjan retained all major government institutions and foreign embassies. The city was severely affected by the Ivorian Civil Wars (2002–2007 and 2010–2011), which caused significant instability and violence.

### Geography and Climate
Abidjan is located on the Gulf of Guinea, bisected by the Ébrié Lagoon. It is divided into northern and southern halves by the lagoon. The climate is tropical wet and dry (Köppen: Aw), with a long rainy season from March to July and a short one from September to December. Climate change projections suggest Abidjan's future climate may resemble that of Manila, with significant risks from sea-level rise.

### Administration and Society
The city is governed as the Autonomous District of Abidjan, created in 2001, which includes 10 central communes and 4 outlying sub-prefectures. French is the official language, but the colloquial "français de Treichville" is widely spoken. The population is religiously diverse, with Christianity (47.7%) and Islam (46.0%) as the largest groups. Abidjan is a major center for education, hosting the Université Félix Houphouët-Boigny.

### Economy and Infrastructure
Abidjan is the economic engine of Ivory Coast and a hub for West Africa. Its port is one of the largest in the region, handling 60% of the country's industrial activity. Major industries include food processing (cocoa, coffee), textiles, chemicals, and oil refining. The city is the seat of the regional stock exchange (BRVM) and hosts numerous international financial institutions. Transportation includes an international airport, a railway to Ouagadougou, and a network of bridges, with a metro system planned for 2028.

### Culture and Tourism
Known as the "Manhattan of the tropics," Abidjan is a vibrant cultural center. It is a major producer of West African music, giving rise to genres like Zouglou and Coupé-Décalé. The city hosts numerous festivals, including the International Jazz Festival and FESNACI. Key cultural sites include the National Museum of Civilisations, Banco National Park, and the lively nightlife districts of Treichville and Yopougon.

## Terms
- **Ébrié Lagoon**: The large body of water on which Abidjan is built, separating its northern and southern halves.
- **Vridi Canal**: A 15m-deep canal completed in 1951 that connects the Ébrié Lagoon to the Gulf of Guinea, enabling deep-sea port access.
- **Autonomous District of Abidjan**: The administrative division governing Abidjan since 2001, comprising 10 communes and 4 sub-prefectures.
- **Plateau**: The central business district of Abidjan, home to modern skyscrapers and key government institutions.
- **Coupé-Décalé**: A popular dance music genre that originated in Abidjan in the early 2000s.
- **Zouglou**: An Ivorian popular music genre that emerged in the 1990s.
- **BRVM (Bourse Régionale des Valeurs Mobilières)**: The regional stock exchange for the West African Economic and Monetary Union, headquartered in Abidjan.
- **SOTRA (Société des Transports Abidjanais)**: The public company responsible for urban transportation in Abidjan via bus and taxi.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The city's official status remains a point of discussion: while Yamoussoukro is the political capital, Abidjan is universally recognized as the economic capital and hosts all major government institutions and foreign embassies.
- The impact of climate change, particularly sea-level rise, poses a significant long-term threat to the city's infrastructure and population, with projected damages in the tens of billions of dollars.
- Urban planning challenges persist, including managing rapid population growth, infrastructure rehabilitation, and addressing issues like urban squalor and traffic congestion.
