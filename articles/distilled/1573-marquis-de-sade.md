# Marquis de Sade

## Overview
Donatien Alphonse François, Marquis de Sade (1740–1814), was a French nobleman, writer, and political activist whose life and work became synonymous with sexual violence and philosophical nihilism. He spent roughly 32 years of his adult life imprisoned in royal fortresses, revolutionary prisons, and insane asylums—first for *lettres de cachet* (royal warrants for detention without trial) obtained by his family to suppress scandals involving prostitutes and servants, and later for political moderatism during the Reign of Terror and for pornography under Napoleon. His major libertine novels—*The 120 Days of Sodom*, *Justine*, *Juliette*, and *Philosophy in the Bedroom*—combine graphic depictions of rape, torture, and murder with materialist, atheist dialogues arguing that nature sanctions the unrestrained pursuit of passion, including destruction and domination. The term "sadism" derives from his name. Scholars debate whether his documented crimes (beating a housemaid, poisoning prostitutes with Spanish fly, abusing adolescent servants) constitute clinical sadism or reflect the libertine culture of the aristocracy, and whether his philosophical defenses of violence are satire, irony, or sincere nihilism. Twentieth-century intellectuals variously claimed him as a precursor to Nietzsche, Freud, surrealism, anarchism, socialism, and totalitarianism, while feminists such as Andrea Dworkin and Roger Shattuck argue that his cultural rehabilitation normalizes violence against women.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Career (1740–1763)
Sade was born into a 13th-century noble family at the Hôtel de Condé in Paris. His father, a diplomat, fell from royal favor and into debt; his mother, a lady-in-waiting to the Princess of Condé, eventually entered a convent. Sade’s childhood was marked by violent rages and isolation: at age four he was sent to his grandmother in Avignon, then to his libertine uncle, the Abbé de Sade, at Saumane. Educated at the Jesuit college Louis-le-Grand (1750–1754) and the Chevaux-légers military academy (1754–1755), he was commissioned at 15 and served in the Seven Years' War, rising to captain by 1759. Despite a good military record, his gambling, womanizing, and refusal to court superiors ended his promotion prospects. Discharged in 1763, he resisted an arranged marriage to the wealthy bourgeois Renée-Pélagie de Montreuil, having been rejected by his beloved Laure de Lauris, but relented under family pressure. The marriage produced two sons and a daughter but quickly soured; Sade called his wife "too cold and too devout."

### Scandals and Royal Imprisonment (1763–1790)
Four major scandals defined this period, each involving sexual violence and blasphemy, and each resolved through royal intervention (*lettres de cachet*) secured by his mother-in-law, Madame de Montreuil, who became his implacable enemy.

1.  **Testard Affair (1763):** Sade hired prostitute Jeanne Testard, blasphemed using a chalice and crucifix, demanded she trample a crucifix, and threatened her with weapons. Arrested on the king’s order, he was released after a month following his father’s plea.
2.  **Arcueil Affair (1768):** Sade lured widow Rose Keller to Arcueil, locked her up, whipped her with a cat-o'-nine-tails, cut her with a penknife, poured hot wax on wounds, and threatened death. Keller escaped; Madame de Montreuil paid her to withdraw the complaint. The king issued a *lettre de cachet*; the *parlement* accepted a royal pardon but Sade remained detained at his family’s request.
3.  **Marseilles Affair (1772):** With servant Latour, Sade staged an orgy with four prostitutes involving flagellation and anal sex. He gave them aniseed pastilles laced with Spanish fly (cantharides), a toxic aphrodisiac. Two women fell critically ill. Sade and Latour were sentenced to death *in absentia* for sodomy and poisoning and burnt in effigy. Sade fled to Italy with his sister-in-law, Anne-Prospère, but was arrested in the Fortress of Miolans (Sardinia); he escaped in 1773.
4.  **La Coste & Treillet Affairs (1774–1777):** At his Provençal château, Sade engaged in orgies with five adolescent female servants (c. 15 years old). Families filed kidnapping and seduction charges; one girl died. Servant Nanon Sablonnière was falsely accused of theft and imprisoned via *lettre de cachet* to silence her. In 1776, servant Catherine Treillet’s father fired a pistol at Sade after three servants fled alleging solicitation. Lured to Paris by false news of his mother’s illness, Sade was arrested on the standing *lettre de cachet* (13 Feb 1777).

Imprisoned at Vincennes (1777–1784) and the Bastille (1784–1789), Sade wrote prolifically: *Voyage d'Italie*, *Dialogue Between a Priest and a Dying Man*, *The 120 Days of Sodom* (fair copy at Bastille), *Aline and Valcour*, *The Misfortunes of Virtue*, and *Eugénie de Franval*. In 1789, protesting curtailed exercise, he shouted through a megaphone that warders were murdering prisoners, prompting transfer to Charenton asylum. The National Assembly abolished *lettres de cachet* in March 1790; he was released 2 April.

### Revolution, Terror, and Literary Career (1790–1801)
Freed, Sade separated from his wife, took the name "Louis Sade, man of letters," and began a lifelong relationship with actress Marie-Constance Quesnet. He published *Justine* (1791) anonymously and engaged in revolutionary politics. Initially a constitutional monarchist, he shifted to radical republicanism as the monarchy fell, joining the Section des Piques. He served as commissioner for health/charity and delivered a funeral oration for Marat and Le Peletier (1793). His petition to the Convention against religion likely alienated Robespierre. Arrested in December 1793 for "moderatism" and "feigned patriotism," he was scheduled for execution on 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794) but survived—by bribery or bureaucratic error—the day Robespierre fell. Released in October 1794, he published *Philosophy in the Bedroom*, *Aline and Valcour*, *Juliette*, and *The New Justine* (1795–99). Plagued by debt, an erroneous émigré listing, and the loss of La Coste proceeds to his ex-wife, he lived in poverty until his émigré status was revoked in 1799. *Crimes of Love* (1800), published under his name, drew hostile reviews identifying him as the author of *Justine* and *Juliette*.

### Final Incarceration at Charenton (1801–1814)
Napoleon’s Consulate cracked down on immorality. In March 1801, Sade was arrested at his publisher’s office. Police Minister Joseph Fouché ordered detention without trial, deeming pornography laws insufficient and a trial too publicizing. After attempting to seduce young prisoners at Sainte-Pélagie, he was diagnosed with "libertine dementia" and moved to Bicêtre, then Charenton asylum. Director Abbé de Coulmier practiced "moral treatment," allowing Sade to write, stage plays (with professional actors and inmates in a 200-seat theater built 1805), and host balls attended by Napoleonic elite. Sade completed the ten-volume *Les Journées de Florbelle* (1807, seized by police) and three conventional historical novels. Official opposition to Coulmier’s methods led to temporary solitary confinement (1810), but privileges were restored. In 1813, performances were banned; Sade began a sexual relationship with Madeleine Leclerc, the teenage daughter of a Charenton employee. He died 2 December 1814 of gangrenous fever. His son burned his remaining manuscripts, including *Les Journées de Florbelle*.

### Political, Religious, and Philosophical Views
Interpretation is hindered by the "polyphony" of Sade’s fiction (Phillips): characters voice conflicting arguments, often using satire, parody, and irony, making authorial intent elusive.

*   **Materialism & Determinism:** Influenced by La Mettrie, d’Holbach, and Hume, Sade’s libertines deny God, free will, and objective morality. The universe is matter in perpetual flux; human character is determined by natural laws. Punishing libertines for nature-determined acts is pointless.
*   **Nature as Norm:** Libertines substitute Nature for God, viewing it as a destructive force whose laws (violence, domination, reproduction through destruction) must be obeyed. Crime—murder, theft, torture—is natural and pleasurable; virtues (charity, pity) are unnatural conventions.
*   **Nihilism:** Crocker argues Sade constructed the first "complete system of nihilism": the only value is egotistical passion, primarily the sex drive linked to destruction and domination. A hierarchy of criminal pleasure culminates in torturing/murdering children.
*   **Politics:** Gorer calls him the "first reasoned socialist" for denying property rights and seeing all elites united against the people. Marshall sees an anarchist precursor (libertarian, contemplating a lawless society), though Sade ultimately advocated minimal laws. Lever, Bongie, and Gray view him as an opportunist: feudalist before 1789, monarchist in 1791, republican in 1792–93—always defending aristocratic privilege and his property.
*   **Totalitarianism:** Camus (1951) argues Sade’s absolute moral license—passions ruling, destruction necessary for creation—leads to a war of all against all, requiring the few to enslave the many, thus prefiguring totalitarianism in freedom’s name.
*   **Religion:** John Gray sees Sade’s philosophy as fundamentally religious, replacing God with Nature while railing against Nature’s destructive impulses.

### Critical Reception and Cultural Influence
**Contemporary/19th Century:** Hostile. *Moniteur* called *Oxtiern* a "revolting atrocity"; *Journal Général de France* found *Justine* disgusting despite "rich imagination." Krafft-Ebing (1886) clinicalized "sadism" as sexual pathology. Baudelaire, Flaubert, Stendhal, Byron, and Poe admired him; Swinburne found him funny; Anatole France called him boring.

**20th Century Onward:** Explosion of interest post-1960s unexpurgated editions. Apollinaire: "freest spirit"; Breton: surrealist for "total liberation." Seen as precursor to Nietzsche, Freud, Nazism (Queneau). Beauvoir (1951): second-rank writer but valuable for rethinking human relations. Barthes (1971): influential textual analysis resisting biographical readings. **Feminist split:** Carter (1978) and Paglia (1990) praise Sade for empowering female sexuality/satirizing Rousseau; Le Brun reads him as poetry. Dworkin (1981) and Shattuck (1996) condemn rehabilitation: Sade depicts women as natural prostitutes, men as having a natural right to rape; his "moral nihilism" harms "unformed minds" and enters culture at "highest intellectual and lowest criminal levels" (serial killers Brady and Bundy read him). Onfray calls hero-worship "intellectually bizarre" given his history as a "sexual delinquent."

**Cultural Legacy:** Influenced 19th-century writers (Stendhal, Baudelaire, Dostoevsky, Nietzsche, Stirner) and 20th-century thought (Bataille, Foucault). *Sexual sadism disorder* (DSM) named after him: arousal from non-consensual infliction of extreme pain/suffering. Artistic works: *Marat/Sade* (Weiss, 1964), *Madame de Sade* (Mishima, 1965), *Salò* (Pasolini, 1975), *Quills* (Kaufman, 2000). Commercial success: US edition of *Justine* and *Philosophy in the Bedroom* sold 350,000 copies (1965–1990). Entered French *Bibliothèque de la Pléiade* (1990); Guyotat (2014): "our Shakespeare... same sense of tragedy."

### Writing Corpus
*   **Libertine Novels:** *The 120 Days of Sodom* (written 1785, pub. 1899), *Justine* (two versions, 1791, 1797–99), *Philosophy in the Bedroom* (dialogue novel, 1795), *Juliette* (1797–99). Combine graphic sex/violence (incest, sodomy, flagellation, coprophilia, necrophilia, child rape/murder) with didactic dialogues on philosophy, religion, politics. Genres: pornography, Gothic, moral tale, dark fairy tale, satire.
*   **Other Fiction:** *Dialogue Between a Priest and a Dying Man* (1782, non-pornographic, outlines themes: atheism, materialism, nature, relativist morality). *Aline and Valcour* (epistolary, 1795). *The Misfortunes of Virtue* (novella, basis for *Justine*). *Crimes of Love* (1800, 11 tales, non-pornographic, themes of incest/disaster). Three conventional historical novels at Charenton (1812–13).
*   **Plays:** ~20 written in prison; *Oxtiern* and *Le Suborneur* staged professionally (1791–92, failed); others staged at Charenton. Phillips: "overwritten melodramas" of little critical interest.
*   **Essays/Tracts:** "Reflections on the Novel" (1800 preface: rules for novelists, violated in his own libertine works). Political: "Address to the King" (1791, constitutional monarchy), "Idea on the method for the sanctioning of laws" (1792, local ratification), Eulogy for Marat/Le Peletier (1793). Debated as sincere vs. expedient parody.
*   **Letters/Journals:** 200+ prison letters (mostly to wife); journals 1807–08, 1814. Bongie: "lasting literary achievement"; Seaver: reveal the "enigmatic" man; Phillips: Sade fictionalized himself in these too.

### Legacy and Manuscripts
Descendants suppressed his legacy until mid-20th century. Comte Xavier de Sade reclaimed the title, discovered manuscripts at Condé-en-Brie (1948), enabling Gilbert Lely’s biography (1952, 1957) and Jean-Jacques Pauvert’s 30-volume edition (1966). Pléiade editions from 1990. Family sold Château de Condé (1983); Pierre Cardin restored La Coste for festivals. Manuscripts scattered in libraries; many lost or destroyed by his son (1814). Scholarly debate persists on whether Sade/violent pornography influenced later crimes (Brady, Bundy).

## Terms
- ****Lettre de cachet**** — A royal warrant in pre-revolutionary France allowing arrest and detention without trial, often used by families to control scandalous relatives.
- ****Libertine**** — A person, especially in 18th-century France, who rejects conventional moral and religious restraints, particularly in sexual behavior; also a literary genre (libertine novel) combining eroticism with philosophical argument.
- ****Sadism**** — Term coined by Krafft-Ebing (1886) from Sade’s name; clinically, *sexual sadism disorder* is arousal from inflicting extreme pain/suffering/humiliation non-consensually.
- ****Materialism (Philosophical)**** — The doctrine that only matter exists; God, soul, and free will are illusions; all phenomena (including human behavior) are determined by physical laws.
- ****Determinism**** — The view that every event, including human action, is the inevitable result of preceding causes and natural laws; negates moral responsibility.
- ****Nihilism**** — The rejection of all religious and moral principles, often with the belief that life is meaningless; Crocker argues Sade built the first "complete system" grounding value solely in egotistical passion.
- ****Moral Treatment**** — Early 19th-century asylum approach (Coulmier at Charenton) emphasizing humane conditions, work, theater, and social interaction over chains and darkness.
- ****Moderatism**** — During the French Revolution, a charge against those deemed insufficiently radical; Sade was arrested for this in 1793 despite his republican activism.
- ****Émigré**** — A French noble or supporter of the monarchy who fled France during the Revolution; their property was confiscated. Sade
