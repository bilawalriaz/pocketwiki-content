# John Coltrane

## Overview
John Coltrane (1926–1967) was an American jazz saxophonist, bandleader, and composer whose innovations in harmony, rhythm, and spirituality made him one of the most influential figures in 20th-century music. Beginning in the bebop and hard bop traditions, he pioneered modal jazz and "sheets of sound"—a dense, rapid improvisational style—before leading the avant-garde into free jazz. His career traced a trajectory from sideman for Miles Davis and Thelonious Monk to leader of the "Classic Quartet," which produced the spiritual landmark *A Love Supreme* (1965). In his final years, Coltrane pursued an increasingly ecstatic, dissonant sound influenced by global religious traditions, leaving a legacy recognized by a posthumous Pulitzer Prize and canonization by the African Orthodox Church.

## Timeline
- **Sep 23, 1926** — Born in Hamlet, North Carolina; raised in High Point.
- **Jun 5, 1945** — Hears Charlie Parker perform, a pivotal influence.
- **Aug 6, 1945** — Enlists in the Navy; serves in Hawaii, records first informal sessions (Jul 1946).
- **1946–1954** — Studies at Granoff School of Music (Dennis Sandole); switches to tenor sax (1947); tours with King Kolax, Eddie Vinson, Dizzy Gillespie, Earl Bostic, Johnny Hodges.
- **Oct 1955 – Apr 1957** — Joins Miles Davis’s "First Great Quintet"; records *Cookin'*, *Relaxin'*, *Workin'*, *Steamin'*; fired due to heroin addiction.
- **Jul–Dec 1957** — Works with Thelonious Monk at the Five Spot; overcomes addiction ("spiritual awakening").
- **1958** — Rejoins Davis (sextet); Ira Gitler coins "sheets of sound"; records *Milestones* and *Kind of Blue*.
- **1960** — Records *Giant Steps* (Atlantic), featuring "Coltrane changes"; forms first quartet with McCoy Tyner, Steve Davis, Elvin Jones.
- **1961** — Moves to Impulse! Records; records *My Favorite Things* (soprano sax debut); Village Vanguard residency with Eric Dolphy, Reggie Workman.
- **1962–1965** — "Classic Quartet" (Tyner, Jimmy Garrison, Jones) records *Ballads*, *Impressions*, *Crescent*, and *A Love Supreme* (Dec 1964).
- **1965** — Records *Ascension* (large ensemble free jazz); adds Pharoah Sanders, Rashied Ali (second drummer); Tyner and Jones depart.
- **1965–1967** — Leads second quartet (Alice Coltrane, Garrison, Ali, Sanders); records *Meditations*, *Expression*, *Interstellar Space*.
- **Jul 17, 1967** — Dies of liver cancer at Huntington Hospital, Long Island, age 40.
- **2007** — Awarded special Pulitzer Prize for "masterful improvisation, supreme musicianship and iconic centrality to the history of jazz."

## Body

### Early Development and Military Service (1926–1946)
Coltrane was born in Hamlet, North Carolina, and raised in High Point. After his father, aunt, and grandparents died in late 1938, his mother raised him. He played clarinet and alto horn in a community band before switching to alto saxophone, inspired by Lester Young and Johnny Hodges. In June 1943, he moved to Philadelphia, where his mother bought him his first saxophone. He studied at the Ornstein School of Music (1944–45) and played his first professional gigs in a cocktail lounge trio. A defining moment occurred on June 5, 1945, when he saw Charlie Parker perform: "The first time I heard Bird play, it hit me right between the eyes." To avoid the Army draft, he enlisted in the Navy on August 6, 1945. Stationed at Pearl Harbor, he played in the Melody Masters swing band—treated as a guest performer because the band was all-white—and recorded his first known sessions in Hawaii on July 13, 1946.

### Post-War Apprenticeship and "Sheets of Sound" (1946–1958)
Discharged in August 1946, Coltrane used the G.I. Bill to study theory with guitarist Dennis Sandole at the Granoff School of Music into the early 1950s. He switched to tenor saxophone in 1947 while with Eddie Vinson. Philadelphia pianist Hasaan Ibn Ali was cited by saxophonist Odean Pope as the key to Coltrane’s melodic system. Coltrane practiced obsessively—reportedly "25 hours a day"—falling asleep with the horn in his mouth or fingering silently for hours. He played with Dizzy Gillespie, Earl Bostic, and Johnny Hodges in the early 1950s. In October 1955, Miles Davis hired him for the "First Great Quintet" (Red Garland, Paul Chambers, Philly Joe Jones). The group’s 1956 marathon sessions yielded four classic albums. Coltrane’s heroin addiction led to his dismissal in April 1957. He then joined Thelonious Monk at the Five Spot (July–December 1957), a period credited with refining his "sheets of sound" technique—rapid, compressed runs using uneven note groupings (fives, sevens) to fit complex chord changes. He rejoined Davis in December 1957, staying until April 1960 and contributing to *Milestones* and *Kind of Blue*.

### Atlantic Period and the First Quartet (1959–1961)
Coltrane’s Atlantic debut as a leader, *Giant Steps* (1960), featured only his compositions. The title track’s harmonic cycle—"Coltrane changes"—became a benchmark for improvisational difficulty. In 1960, he formed his first standing quartet for a Jazz Gallery gig, stabilizing with pianist McCoy Tyner (a Philadelphia friend), bassist Steve Davis, and drummer Elvin Jones. *My Favorite Things* (1961) marked his recording debut on soprano saxophone, an unconventional choice for modern jazz. The instrument’s higher register expanded his tenor approach: "By playing on the soprano... I found myself doing the same thing [on tenor]... the willingness to change and just try to play as much of the instrument as possible."

### Impulse! Years: Village Vanguard and the Classic Quartet (1961–1965)
Impulse! bought Coltrane’s contract in May 1961, reuniting him with engineer Rudy Van Gelder. By early 1961, Reggie Workman replaced Davis on bass, and Eric Dolphy joined as second horn. The quintet’s Village Vanguard residency (recorded extensively) revealed a radical new direction influenced by Indian ragas, modal jazz, and free jazz. Saxophonist John Gilmore (Sun Ra’s band) was a catalyst: after hearing him, Coltrane reportedly said, "He's got it! Gilmore's got the concept!" Coltrane experimented with two basses (Workman plus Art Davis or Donald Garrett) to create a "suspended rhythm," heard on *Olé Coltrane* and *Africa/Brass*. Critics were divided; *DownBeat* labeled Coltrane and Dolphy "anti-jazz" in 1961, and French audiences booed him.

In 1962, Dolphy left and Jimmy Garrison replaced Workman, forming the "Classic Quartet" (Tyner, Garrison, Jones). Coltrane moved toward harmonically static, modally open forms that allowed expansive rhythmic and motivic improvisation. Studio albums grew more conservative (*Ballads*, *Duke Ellington & John Coltrane*, *John Coltrane and Johnny Hartman*), but live sets (*Impressions*, *Live at Birdland*, *Newport '63*) balanced standards with exploration. A March 6, 1963 session, long thought lost (master tape destroyed), was released in 2018 as *Both Directions at Once*. The quartet’s masterpiece, *A Love Supreme* (December 1964), is a four-part suite expressing Coltrane’s faith. The final movement, "Psalm," is a musical setting of his poem to God, with one note per syllable. The album was composed at his Dix Hills, Long Island home. It was performed live only three times (Antibes 1965, Seattle 1965).

### Late Period: Avant-Garde and the Second Quartet (1965–1967)
After *A Love Supreme*, Coltrane absorbed the dissonance of Albert Ayler, Ornette Coleman, and Sun Ra. Early 1965 recordings (*The John Coltrane Quartet Plays*, *Transition*, *Sun Ship*) show abstracted playing: multiphonics, overtones, altissimo register, and a mutated "sheets of sound." In June 1965, he recorded *Ascension*, a 38-minute collective improvisation with ten musicians (including Pharoah Sanders, Freddie Hubbard, Marion Brown, John Tchicai). Sanders joined the band in September 1965; his vocabulary—multiphonics, growls, "high register squeals" imitating the human cry—pushed the music further. Critic Gary Giddins noted followers now faced "a player who appeared to have little contact with earth."

By late 1965, Rashied Ali joined as second drummer. McCoy Tyner left (unable to hear himself over two drummers) after *Meditations*; Elvin Jones departed in early 1966 ("only poets can understand it"). Both later expressed deep respect: Jones compared Coltrane to Einstein; Tyner called him "a scientist in a lab." The second quartet—Alice Coltrane (piano), Garrison (bass), Ali (drums), Sanders (tenor)—played marathon concerts (solos often exceeding 15 minutes). Coltrane explored flute and alto saxophone (a Yamaha prototype). Final 1967 sessions yielded *Expression*, *Stellar Regions*, and the duo album *Interstellar Space* with Ali. His last performance was a radio broadcast for the Olatunji Center in April 1967.

### Artistry: Instruments and Concept
Coltrane began on alto, switched to tenor in 1947, and added soprano in 1960. His preference for the tenor’s upper register stemmed from alto horn and clarinet training; his "sound concept" was set higher than the instrument’s normal range. Soprano practice expanded his tenor range downward and upward. He experimented with flute after Eric Dolphy’s death (Dolphy’s mother gave him the instruments). Coltrane had a strong affinity for drums, often keeping a spare kit onstage for solos ("Pursuance," "The Drum Thing") and stating, "I feel the need for more time, more rhythm all around me... with more than one drummer, the rhythm can be more multi-directional." His tenor (Selmer Mark VI, 1965) and soprano (Selmer Mark VI, 1962) were auctioned in 2005 for the John Coltrane Foundation.

### Personal Life and Spirituality
Raised Christian (both grandfathers were A.M.E. Zion ministers), Coltrane’s spirituality deepened through his first wife, Naima (Juanita Grubbs), a Muslim convert. They married in 1955; he adopted her daughter Syeeda. "Naima" was his favorite composition. They separated in 1963; he moved out with only "his clothes and his horns." In 1957, he experienced a "spiritual awakening" that helped him overcome heroin and alcohol addiction, writing in *A Love Supreme*’s liner notes: "by the grace of God, a spiritual awakening which was to lead me to a richer, fuller, more productive life." He declared, "I believe in all religions" (*Meditations* liner notes).

In 1963, he met pianist Alice McLeod; they had three sons (John Jr., 1964; Ravi, 1965; Oran, 1967) and married in 1966 after his divorce. Alice shared his interest in Indian philosophy. Post-*A Love Supreme* titles reflect spiritual exploration: *Ascension*, *Om*, *Meditations*, *Selflessness*. His library included the *Bhagavad Gita*, *Autobiography of a Yogi*, the Qur'an, the Bible, Kabbalah, and texts on Hinduism, Krishnamurti, African history, Plato, Aristotle, and Zen Buddhism. *Om* (October 1965) incorporates chants from the *Bhagavad Gita* and *Tibetan Book of the Dead*. Coltrane believed music could harness mystical forces to produce specific emotional or physical effects: "If I want it to rain, it will start right away to rain... if one of my friends is ill, I'd like to play a certain song and he will be cured."

### Veneration and Legacy
After his death, the Yardbird Temple in San Francisco (named for Charlie "Yardbird" Parker) worshipped Coltrane as God incarnate. Affiliating with the African Orthodox Church, they became St. John Coltrane African Orthodox Church, the only African Orthodox church using his music and lyrics as liturgy. Rev. F.W. King stated, "God dwells in the musical majesty of his sounds." *The New York Times* noted the church’s message of "deliverance through divine sound" aligns with Coltrane’s experience—he reportedly heard God’s voice during withdrawal. In 1966, asked what he hoped to be in five years, Coltrane replied, "a saint." He is depicted in the *Dancing Saints* icon at St. Gregory of Nyssa Episcopal Church (San Francisco) and listed among historical Black saints at Saint Barnabas Episcopal Church (Newark). Documentaries include *The Church of Saint Coltrane* (1996) and *Chasing Trane* (2016). Honors: DownBeat Hall of Fame (1965), Grammy Lifetime Achievement (1997), Pulitzer Special Citation (2007), North Carolina Music Hall of Fame (2009). His Philadelphia home is a National Historic Landmark (1999); his Dix Hills home is on the National Register of Historic Places (2007).

## Terms
- **Sheets of sound**: Term coined by Ira Gitler (1958) for Coltrane’s dense, rapid improvisational style using uneven note groupings (fives, sevens) to navigate chord changes at high speed.
- **Coltrane changes**: Harmonic substitution cycle (major thirds) used in the title track of *Giant Steps* (1960), creating a notoriously difficult improvisational framework.
- **Modal jazz**: Improvisational approach using scales (modes) rather than chord progressions as the primary harmonic structure; central to Coltrane’s 1960s work.
- **Free jazz / The New Thing**: Avant-garde movement (led by Ornette Coleman) abandoning fixed harmony, tempo, and form; Coltrane adopted its principles from 1965 onward.
- **Multiphonics**: Extended technique producing multiple simultaneous pitches on a single-reed instrument; used heavily in Coltrane’s late period.
- **Altissimo register**: Playing above the standard range of the saxophone; a feature of Coltrane’s post-1965 vocabulary.
- **Classic Quartet**: Coltrane’s 1962–1965 group with McCoy Tyner (piano), Jimmy Garrison (bass), Elvin Jones (drums); produced *A Love Supreme*.
- **Second Quartet**: Coltrane’s 1965–1967 group with Alice Coltrane (piano), Jimmy Garrison (bass), Rashied Ali (drums), Pharoah Sanders (tenor).
- **A Love Supreme**: Four-part suite (December 1964) expressing Coltrane’s devotion; "Psalm" movement sets his poem to God note-for-syllable.
- **African Orthodox Church / St. John Coltrane Church**: San Francisco congregation that canonized Coltrane as a saint; uses his music as liturgy.

## Debates and open questions
- **Cause of death**: Biographer Lewis Porter speculates hepatitis (possibly from heroin needles) led to cirrhosis and liver cancer; Frederick J. Spencer attributes it to needle use "or the bottle, or both," noting the "seeds of cancer were sown in his days of addiction."
- **LSD use**: There is speculation Coltrane used LSD in 1965, informing the "cosmic" transcendence of his late music; Nat Hentoff likened his and Sanders’s playing to "speaking with 'the gift of tongues.'"
- **Critical reception of late work**: Critics and audiences were sharply divided in 1961–1967; *DownBeat*’s "anti-jazz" label and French booing contrast with later acclaim. The split between "accessible" studio albums and radical live performances remains a focus of scholarly analysis.
- **Authorship of "Psalm" poem**: The *A Love Supreme* liner notes present the poem as Coltrane’s original text; its precise theological sources (Christian, Islamic, universalist) are debated.
- **Impact of two-bass/two-drummer ensembles**: Coltrane’s rationale (rhythmic suspension, multi-directional time) is documented, but the musical efficacy and influence on later jazz rhythm sections remain discussed.
