# German Empire

## Overview
The German Empire (1871–1918), also known as Imperial Germany or the Second Reich, was a federal constitutional monarchy formed by the unification of German states under Prussian leadership. It rapidly became Europe's leading industrial and scientific power, but its authoritarian political structure, aggressive foreign policy under Kaiser Wilhelm II, and ultimate defeat in World War I led to its collapse and the rise of the Weimar Republic.

## Body

### Foundation and Political Structure
The Empire was founded on 18 January 1871, uniting the North German Confederation with the south German states (excluding Austria). Its constitution, effective 16 April 1871, created a federal parliamentary monarchy. Executive power was vested in the Kaiser (Emperor), who appointed the Chancellor and was supreme commander of the armed forces. The legislature consisted of the *Reichstag*, elected by universal male suffrage, and the *Bundesrat* (Federal Council), representing the 25 constituent states. In practice, Prussia dominated the Empire; its king was the Kaiser, and its chancellor was usually also the Imperial Chancellor. The political system blended authoritarian rule with democratic elements, though Prussia's weighted voting system ensured conservative control.

### The Bismarck Era (1871–1890)
Chancellor Otto von Bismarck shaped the early Empire. Domestically, he pursued "revolutionary conservatism," using social welfare programs (health, accident, and old-age insurance in the 1880s) to bind workers to the state and counter socialism. He also launched the *Kulturkampf* (1871–1880), a campaign against Catholic Church influence that ultimately mobilized Catholic voters. Foreign policy focused on preserving European peace and isolating France through a complex alliance system, including the Dual Alliance (1879) and the Three Emperors' League (1881). Bismarck was reluctant to acquire colonies but did so in the 1880s under public pressure.

### The Wilhelmine Era and *Weltpolitik*
After Bismarck's dismissal in 1890, Kaiser Wilhelm II pursued *Weltpolitik* ("world politics"), a more aggressive and expansionist foreign policy. This abandoned Bismarck's careful balance of power, leading to Germany's growing isolation. Germany engaged in colonial expansion, notably in Africa and China, and built a powerful navy to rival Britain's, straining relations. Domestically, industrialization boomed, making Germany Europe's largest economy by 1913. The Social Democratic Party (SPD) grew significantly, becoming the largest party in the Reichstag by 1912, despite government repression.

### World War I and Collapse
Germany entered World War I in 1914, offering Austria-Hungary a "blank check" of support. The initial Schlieffen Plan for a quick victory over France failed, leading to stalemate on the Western Front. Germany had success on the Eastern Front, culminating in the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (1918) with Russia. However, the Allied naval blockade caused severe food shortages, and unrestricted submarine warfare brought the United States into the war in 1917. Military offensives in 1918 failed, and with its allies collapsing and revolution spreading at home, Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated on 9 November 1918. The Empire was succeeded by the Weimar Republic.

## Terms
- **Kaiser**: The German Emperor, head of state with extensive executive and military powers.
- **Chancellor**: Head of government, appointed by and responsible to the Kaiser.
- **Reichstag**: The federal parliament elected by universal male suffrage.
- **Bundesrat**: The federal council representing the governments of the constituent states.
- **Weltpolitik**: Kaiser Wilhelm II's aggressive, expansionist foreign policy aimed at global power status.
- **Kulturkampf**: Bismarck's 1870s campaign to reduce the influence of the Catholic Church.
- **Junkers**: The Prussian landed aristocracy, a dominant conservative elite in politics and the military.
- **Sonderweg**: A historiographical thesis arguing Germany followed a "special path" to modernity that facilitated authoritarianism and Nazism.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Sonderweg Thesis**: Historians debate whether the Empire's authoritarian structures and failure to develop a stable parliamentary democracy created a "special path" that directly led to Nazism, or if this view overemphasizes continuity.
- **Responsibility for WWI**: The extent of Germany's (and specifically the "blank check") responsibility for causing World War I remains a major scholarly debate, with some historians like Fritz Fischer arguing for primary German culpability.
- **Bismarck's Legacy**: Scholars assess whether Bismarck's alliance system was a coherent peace-preserving strategy or an inherently unstable structure that collapsed after his departure.
- **Military Dictatorship in 1918**: Historians disagree on whether the wartime military leadership under Hindenburg and Ludendorff constituted a full "military dictatorship," noting the Reichstag retained some influence.
