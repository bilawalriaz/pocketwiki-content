# North America

## Overview

North America is a continent in the Northern and Western hemispheres, bordered by the Arctic, Atlantic, and Pacific Oceans, with a southeastern connection to South America and the Caribbean Sea. It is the third-largest continent by area (about 24.7 million km², ~16.5% of Earth's land) and fourth by population (over 592 million in 2021, ~7.5% of the world's total). Its history spans indigenous habitation dating back at least 20,000 years, Norse exploration around 1000 CE, and European colonization beginning in 1492, which shaped its present-day cultural, linguistic, and economic landscape.

## Timeline

- **c. 27,000–14,000 years ago** — Traditional theory holds hunters entered via the Bering Land Bridge; alternative theories suggest coastal sailing from Beringia ~13,000 years ago
- **c. 9550–9050 BCE** — Clovis culture appears in modern New Mexico, one of the oldest known cultures
- **c. 1000 CE** — Earliest verifiable Norse settlement in North America, at Newfoundland (Vinland); Leif Erikson (c. 970–1020) is thought to have visited
- **1492** — Christopher Columbus's voyages begin the transatlantic exchange and European colonization
- **1513** — Juan Ponce de León visits and names La Florida
- **1521** — Hernán Cortés conquers the Aztec Empire
- **1607** — First permanent English settlement at Jamestown, Virginia
- **1763** — France cedes its North American territories east of the Mississippi to Britain after the Seven Years' War
- **1776** — Thirteen Colonies declare independence from Britain on 4 July
- **1783** — Treaty of Paris ends the American Revolutionary War, recognizing U.S. sovereignty
- **1803** — Louisiana Purchase: Napoleon sells France's remaining North American claims to the U.S.
- **1867** — Canada unifies as a dominion; Russia sells Alaska to the U.S.
- **1913** — Panama Canal completed, connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans

## Body

### Name and Definition

The Americas were named after Amerigo Vespucci, the first European to suggest they were a landmass unknown to Europeans. In 1507, cartographer Martin Waldseemüller placed "America" on South America; the name was later extended to North America. Gerardus Mercator used "America" for the entire Western Hemisphere in 1538 and called North America "America or New India" in 1569. The Spanish Empire called its territories "Las Indias," overseen by the Council of the Indies.

The United Nations recognizes three subregions: Northern America, Central America (including Mexico), and the Caribbean. In trade contexts like NAFTA, "North America" refers to Canada, Mexico, and the U.S. The six-continent model, prevalent in Latin America, uses "North America" more narrowly. The continent divides culturally into Anglo-America and Latin America, with Francophone regions including Quebec, Saint Pierre and Miquelon, and Louisiana.

### Pre-Columbian Era

The specifics of initial settlement are debated: the traditional theory posits hunters crossing the Bering Land Bridge from Siberia, while a growing view suggests sailing from Beringia ~13,000 years ago. The oldest petroglyphs date from 15,000–10,000 years before present. Indigenous peoples were divided into many polities across culture areas—from bison hunters of the Great Plains to farmers of Mesoamerica—classified by language families like Athabaskan and Uto-Aztecan. The Inuit likely arrived later, evidenced by the Dorset culture's disappearance and replacement by the Thule people.

Southern groups domesticated crops now used worldwide, including tomatoes, squash, and maize. The Mayans developed writing, pyramids, a complex calendar, and the concept of zero around 400 CE. By the Spanish arrival, political dominance had shifted to the Aztec Empire, conquered in 1521.

### Post-Contact, 1492–1910

European arrival brought curiosity, trade, cooperation, and resistance from indigenous peoples. Populations declined substantially due to Eurasian diseases like smallpox (to which natives lacked immunity) and violent conflicts. Several linguistic groups died out. Spain established settlements in the Caribbean in the 1490s, then conquered the Aztecs and built cities in Mexico and Central America. France developed Saint-Domingue as a sugar colony; Britain took Barbados and Jamaica. Britain delayed mainland settlement for a century after Spain, focusing first on Ireland.

The first permanent English settlement was Jamestown (1607), followed by the Thirteen Colonies. The first permanent French settlement was Quebec City (1608). After the Seven Years' War (1763), France ceded its eastern territories to Britain; Spain gained lands west of the Mississippi. The American Revolution began in 1775; the Declaration of Independence was adopted 4 July 1776, and the Treaty of Paris (1783) recognized U.S. sovereignty.

Westward expansion followed: the Louisiana Purchase (1803), the Adams–Onís Treaty settling the Spanish boundary (1819), Mexican independence (1821), and the Mexican–American War, which gained vast territory. Russia sold Alaska to the U.S. in 1867. Canada unified as a dominion in 1867. The U.S. aided Panama's separation from Colombia and completed the Panama Canal in 1913.

### Geography and Geology

North America's only land connection to South America is at the Darien Gap in Panama. The continent sits primarily on the North American Plate; western Mexico and parts of California lie on the Pacific Plate along the San Andreas Fault. Four great regions exist: the Great Plains, the mountainous west (including the Rockies), the Canadian Shield plateau, and the varied eastern region with the Appalachians. The highest peak is Denali in Alaska. The USGS places the geographic center near Balta, North Dakota.

Laurentia, the geologic core, formed 1.5–1.0 billion years ago. North America was part of Pangaea, then Laurasia, separating from Eurasia in the mid-Cretaceous. The Appalachians formed ~480 mya; the Rockies formed during the Laramide orogeny (80–55 mya). The Isthmus of Panama formed ~12–15 mya; the Great Lakes were carved by receding glaciers ~10,000 years ago. Canada has extensive mineral resources, including the Sudbury Basin (an ancient meteorite impact crater). Central America is geologically active, with major earthquakes and volcanic eruptions.

### Economy and Demographics

North America's GDP per capita was $41,830 in October 2016 (IMF), making it the richest continent. The U.S. has the world's largest economy; Canada and Mexico follow. NAFTA (1994) formed one of the world's largest trade blocs, replaced by USMCA in 2018. CARICOM (1973) and the Central American Common Market (1961) structure Caribbean and Central American trade.

The continent is ethnically diverse, with Whites, Mestizos, and Blacks as the largest groups. English, Spanish, and French dominate; Danish and Dutch appear in Greenland and the Dutch Caribbean. About 6 million in Mexico, 372,000 in the U.S., and 225,000 in Canada speak indigenous languages. Christianity is the largest religion; the U.S. has the world's largest Christian population (~247 million). The most populous countries are the U.S. (342.9 million), Mexico (126 million), and Canada (41.5 million). The largest cities are Mexico City and New York City, the only ones exceeding eight million.

### Culture

U.S. and English Canada share cultural similarities; French Canada has a legally protected distinct culture. The U.S. Southwest exhibits significant Hispanic cultural elements. Canada is a federated parliamentary democracy under a constitutional monarchy; its constitution dates to 1867, with amendment power only from 1982. The Quebec Act of 1774 guaranteed Catholic Francophone rights. Canada limits non-Canadian broadcasting content; Quebec mandates French signage. Latino culture is strong in the southwestern U.S., New York, and Florida. Northern Mexico is influenced by U.S. culture. Greenland shares cultural ties with Canada's indigenous peoples and has strong Danish ties. Canada, Mexico, and the U.S. jointly host the 2026 FIFA World Cup; lacrosse is a national sport in Canada.

## Terms

- **Bering Land Bridge**: Land connection between Siberia and Alaska, crossed by early migrants (traditionally 27,000–14,000 years ago)
- **Clovis culture**: Early indigenous culture (c. 9550–9050 BCE) in modern New Mexico
- **Vinland**: Norse name for North America, referenced in sagas; site of the earliest verifiable European settlement (~1000 CE)
- **NAFTA**: North American Free Trade Agreement (1994) among Canada, Mexico, and the U.S.; replaced by USMCA in 2018
- **Laurentia**: Ancient craton forming North America's geologic core (1.5–1.0 billion years old)
- **Laramide orogeny**: Mountain-building period (80–55 mya) that formed the Rockies
- **Anglo-America**: English-speaking regions of North America (Canada, U.S., sometimes Belize and the Commonwealth Caribbean)
- **Latin America**: Regions where Romance languages predominate, generally south of the U.S.
- **Pan-American Highway**: Road network of nearly 48,000 km through mainland nations; the U.S.–Mexico portion is roughly 26,000 km
- **CARICOM**: Caribbean Community and Common Market, signed 1973 by 15 Caribbean nations

## Debates and open questions

The exact timing and route of the first human settlement of North America remain unresolved: the traditional theory posits land-bridge crossing from 27,000–14,000 years ago, while a growing view suggests sailing from Beringia ~13,000 years ago. The geographic center of North America is disputed—the USGS states no government agency has established a monumented point, though a field stone obelisk in Rugby, North Dakota claims to mark it. The southern limit of the continent is also debated: the political boundary lies at the Darien Gap in Panama, but some geologists place it physiographically at the Isthmus of Tehuantepec in Mexico.
