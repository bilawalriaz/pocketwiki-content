# Elizabeth Cady Stanton

## Overview
Elizabeth Cady Stanton (1815–1902) was a foundational leader of the American women's rights movement. She was the primary organizer of the 1848 Seneca Falls Convention and the main author of its Declaration of Sentiments, which demanded equal rights, including suffrage. Her decades-long partnership with Susan B. Anthony was central to the movement's development, though her later views on race and religion sparked significant controversy.

## Timeline
- **1840** — Attends World Anti-Slavery Convention in London; women delegates are barred from participating.
- **1848** — Organizes the Seneca Falls Convention; authors the Declaration of Sentiments.
- **1851** — Meets Susan B. Anthony, beginning a lifelong partnership.
- **1863** — Co-founds the Women's Loyal National League to campaign for the abolition of slavery.
- **1866** — Co-founds the American Equal Rights Association (AERA) for universal suffrage.
- **1868** — Co-founds the National Woman Suffrage Association (NWSA) and begins publishing *The Revolution*.
- **1869** — The AERA splits over the Fifteenth Amendment; Stanton and Anthony form the NWSA.
- **1878** — Helps introduce the federal women's suffrage amendment that later becomes the 19th Amendment.
- **1890** — Becomes first president of the merged National American Woman Suffrage Association (NAWSA).
- **1895** — Publishes *The Woman's Bible*, a controversial critique of religion's role in women's oppression.
- **1902** — Dies in New York City, 18 years before the passage of the 19th Amendment.

## Body

### Early Life and Activism
Born into a prominent New York family, Stanton received an education superior to most women of her era, studying law books with her father and attending the Troy Female Seminary. Her activism was sparked by the 1840 World Anti-Slavery Convention, where she witnessed the exclusion of female delegates. In 1848, she channeled her discontent into organizing the Seneca Falls Convention. The convention's Declaration of Sentiments, modeled on the U.S. Declaration of Independence, listed grievances against male dominance and controversially demanded the right to vote, which became a central tenet of the movement.

### Partnership with Susan B. Anthony
In 1851, Stanton met Susan B. Anthony, forming a pivotal partnership. Stanton was the intellectual and writer, while Anthony was the organizer and speaker. Their collaboration was crucial during the Civil War, when they led the Women's Loyal National League in the largest petition drive in U.S. history to abolish slavery. After the war, they co-founded the American Equal Rights Association to campaign for suffrage for both African Americans and women.

### The Suffrage Split and Later Career
The proposed Fifteenth Amendment, which would enfranchise Black men but not women, caused a bitter split in the movement. Stanton and Anthony opposed it, insisting on universal suffrage. Stanton sometimes used elitist and racially condescending language in her arguments, drawing criticism from allies like Frederick Douglass. They formed the rival National Woman Suffrage Association (NWSA), while Lucy Stone's faction created the American Woman Suffrage Association (AWSA). The groups merged in 1890 into the NAWSA, with Stanton as its first president, though she soon stepped back from leadership.

### Writing and Religious Critique
Stanton was a prolific writer. She co-authored the first three volumes of the *History of Woman Suffrage* and published her memoirs, *Eighty Years and More*. Her most controversial work was *The Woman's Bible* (1895), which critically examined the Bible's portrayal of women, arguing it reflected ancient prejudice rather than divine will. The book alienated many in the suffrage movement, which formally distanced itself from it, and contributed to Stanton's later marginalization.

## Terms
*   **Declaration of Sentiments:** The document Stanton authored for the 1848 Seneca Falls Convention, outlining grievances against gender inequality and demanding equal rights.
*   **Coverture:** A common law doctrine where a married woman's legal identity was subsumed by her husband's, limiting her property and contractual rights.
*   **American Equal Rights Association (AERA):** Post-Civil War organization founded by Stanton and Anthony to campaign for suffrage for all citizens regardless of race or sex.
*   **Fifteenth Amendment:** U.S. Constitution amendment (1870) prohibiting voting discrimination based on race, but not sex, causing a split in the suffrage movement.
*   **National Woman Suffrage Association (NWSA):** Organization formed by Stanton and Anthony in 1869, which opposed the Fifteenth Amendment and focused on a federal suffrage amendment.
*   **American Woman Suffrage Association (AWSA):** Rival suffrage organization formed in 1869 by Lucy Stone, which supported the Fifteenth Amendment and pursued a state-by-state strategy.
*   **National American Woman Suffrage Association (NAWSA):** The merged organization formed in 1890 from the NWSA and AWSA, with Stanton as its first president.
*   **The Woman's Bible:** Stanton's 1895 work that critically re-examined biblical passages about women, arguing they were products of patriarchal prejudice.
*   **New Departure:** A legal strategy arguing the 14th Amendment implicitly granted women the right to vote, which was ultimately rejected by the Supreme Court.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Stanton's Racial Views:** Scholars debate the extent to which Stanton's opposition to the Fifteenth Amendment was based on principled insistence on universal suffrage versus personal elitism and racial prejudice, as evidenced by her use of terms like "Sambo."
*   **Impact of *The Woman's Bible*:** Historians analyze how the controversy surrounding this book affected Stanton's legacy and the suffrage movement's public image and internal cohesion.
*   **Legacy and Recognition:** Stanton's role was often overshadowed by Susan B. Anthony's in the decades after her death. Her full recognition as a central figure was largely a product of the second-wave feminist movement in the 1960s.
