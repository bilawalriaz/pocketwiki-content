# Rabindranath Tagore

## Overview
Rabindranath Tagore (1861–1941) was a Bengali polymath—poet, novelist, playwright, composer, philosopher, painter, and social reformer—who reshaped Bengali literature, music, and art through Contextual Modernism. In 1913, he became the first Indian and non-European to win a Nobel Prize in any category, and the first lyricist to win the Nobel Prize in Literature, for *Gitanjali* (Song Offerings), praised for its accessible spiritual idealism. A humanist and internationalist, he denounced British colonial rule and narrow nationalism, famously renouncing his knighthood after the 1919 Jallianwala Bagh massacre. He founded Visva-Bharati University at Santiniketan to realise an educational ideal fusing Eastern and Western traditions beyond rote learning. His vast legacy includes the national anthems of India ("Jana Gana Mana") and Bangladesh ("Amar Sonar Bangla"); the Sri Lankan national anthem was inspired by his work, and "Banglar Mati Banglar Jol" is the state anthem of West Bengal. He composed some 2,230 songs (*Rabindra Sangeet*), and produced a body of work spanning fiction, drama, essays, and late-life painting.

## Timeline
- **7 May 1861** — Born in Jorasanko mansion, Calcutta, youngest of 13 surviving children to Debendranath Tagore and Sarada Devi.
- **Feb 1873** — After *upanayan* rite at age eleven, tours India with father for several months; visits father's Shantiniketan estate and Amritsar (Golden Temple) before reaching Himalayan hill station of Dalhousie.
- **1877** — At age sixteen, publishes first substantial poems under pseudonym *Bhānusiṃha* ("Sun Lion"), accepted as lost classics; debuts short story "Bhikharini" ("The Beggar Woman").
- **1878–1880** — Studies in England (Brighton, University College London); returns degree-less, resolving to reconcile European and Brahmo traditions.
- **1883** — Marries Mrinalini Devi (aged 10); they have five children, two dying in childhood.
- **1890–1895** — Manages ancestral estates in Shelaidaha (now Bangladesh); "Sadhana period" yields over half the 84 stories of *Galpaguchchha*; meets Baul singer Lalon Shah.
- **1901** — Founds ashram and experimental school at Santiniketan; wife and two children die there in subsequent years.
- **1912** — Translates *Gitanjali* into English; shares with W.B. Yeats and Ezra Pound in London.
- **Nov 1913** — Awarded Nobel Prize in Literature; first Indian and non-European laureate.
- **1915** — Receives knighthood from King George V.
- **1916** — Escapes assassination narrowly by Indian expatriates during stay in a San Francisco hotel.
- **1919** — Renounces knighthood in protest against Jallianwala Bagh massacre.
- **1921** — Establishes Institute for Rural Reconstruction (Shriniketan) with Leonard Elmhirst near Santiniketan.
- **1921–1932** — Extensive global travels (Europe, Americas, East Asia, Persia, Iraq, Sri Lanka); meets Einstein, Mussolini, Bergson, Rolland, others.
- **1930s** — Campaigns against untouchability; successfully opens Guruvayoor Temple to Dalits; writes late prose-poems and science-inflected works (*Visva-Parichay*).
- **7 Aug 1941** — Dies aged 80 in Jorasanko mansion, Calcutta.

## Body

### Family Background and Early Formation
The Tagores were Pirali Brahmins (a historically stigmatised sub-caste) with ancestral roots in Jessore and Bardhaman; the surname *Tagore* is an anglicisation of *Thakur*, originally *Kushari*. Debendranath Tagore, Rabindranath’s father, was a leader of the Brahmo Samaj and the Bengal Renaissance. The Jorasanko household was a cultural hub hosting literary magazines, theatre, and both Bengali and Western classical music. Rabindranath, nicknamed "Rabi," was raised largely by servants after his mother’s early death and his father’s frequent travel. He avoided formal schooling—lasting a single day at Presidency College—preferring tutoring from his brother Hemendranath (swimming, gymnastics, judo, wrestling) and roaming the family estates. A formative 1873 tour with his father exposed him to Sikh *gurbani* at the Golden Temple in Amritsar, inspiring poems on Sikh gurus, and to Kālidāsa’s classical poetry in Dalhousie.

### Shelaidaha: The Zamindar-Poet and the "Sadhana" Period
In 1890, Tagore began managing the family’s vast estates in Shelaidaha, travelling the Padma River on the family barge. As *Zamindar Babu*, he collected token rents and immersed himself in village life, an experience that grounded his literature in the "voluptuous poverty" of rural Bengal. He encountered the Baul folk singer Lalon Shah via Gagan Harkara, popularising Lalon’s songs, which emphasised inward divinity and rebellion against orthodox *bhadralok* society. The years 1891–1895, named after his magazine *Sadhana*, were his most productive for fiction: he wrote over half the 84 stories of *Galpaguchchha*, inventing the Bengali short story genre. Key stories like "Kabuliwala," "Kshudita Pashan," and "Atithi" examined the lives of the downtrodden with unprecedented penetrative depth.

### Santiniketan, *Gitanjali*, and the Nobel Prize
In 1901, Tagore moved to Santiniketan to found an ashram-school (later Visva-Bharati) centred on a marble prayer hall (*The Mandir*), teaching under trees via a *brahmacharya* system of personal guru guidance. Personal tragedies struck: his wife and two children died there; his father died in 1905. In 1912, he translated his 1910 Bengali collection *Gitanjali* into English prose poems. In London, W.B. Yeats wrote the preface; Ezra Pound and the India Society championed it. The Swedish Academy awarded the 1913 Nobel Prize in Literature for the "idealistic—and for Westerners—accessible nature" of this translated body. He was knighted in 1915 but renounced it in 1919 after the Jallianwala Bagh massacre, writing to Viceroy Chelmsford that "badges of honour make our shame glaring."

### Rural Reconstruction, Politics, and Anti-Nationalism
With agricultural economist Leonard Elmhirst, Tagore founded Shriniketan (1921) to "free villages from the shackles of helplessness and ignorance" through "vitalising knowledge," seeking to moderate Gandhi’s *Swaraj* protests, which he feared caused a "mental... colonial decline." He campaigned against "abnormal caste consciousness" and untouchability in the early 1930s, penning Dalit heroes and successfully opening Guruvayoor Temple to Dalits. Politically, he opposed imperialism but lampooned the Swadeshi movement (*The Cult of the Charkha*, 1925), arguing independence must not deny learning from abroad. He urged self-help and education over "blind revolution." He escaped assassination—and only narrowly—by Indian expatriates during his stay in a San Francisco hotel in late 1916; the plot failed when his would-be assassins fell into an argument. His songs "Chitto Jetha Bhayshunyo" and "Ekla Chalo Re" became anthems of the independence movement; the latter was favoured by Gandhi, with whom Tagore mediated the Gandhi–Ambedkar dispute on separate electorates.

### Global Travels and Cosmopolitan Dialogue
Between 1878 and 1932, Tagore visited over thirty countries on five continents. His 1912–1913 London stay launched his global fame. He lectured in Japan and the US (1916–17), denouncing nationalism ("Nationalism in India" admired by Romain Rolland). In 1924–25, he visited Peru, Mexico, and Argentina (hosted by Victoria Ocampo). A 1926 meeting with Mussolini in Rome ended when Tagore denounced Fascism in *The Manchester Guardian*. He recovered in Hungary (Lake Balaton), toured Southeast Asia (1927) exploring Indian cultural legacies (Borobudur), and undertook a near-year-long Europe/US tour (1930–31), lecturing at Oxford (Hibbert Lectures), visiting Dartington Hall, Denmark, Switzerland, Germany, and the Soviet Union. In 1932, hosted by Reza Shah Pahlavi in Persia, he wrote that Asian nations would carry lamps converging on a "common ray of knowledge." He interacted with Einstein, Bergson, Frost, Mann, Shaw, Wells, and Rolland.

### Late Creative Phase: Science, Painting, and Final Poems
In his "twilight years" (1932–1941), Tagore scrutinised orthodoxy. He rebuked Gandhi for calling the 1934 Bihar earthquake "seismic karma" for Dalit oppression. He took up painting seriously at sixty; exhibitions toured Europe (debut Paris). Likely red-green colour blind, his works showed strange colour schemes, influenced by Malanggan scrimshaw, Haida carvings, and Max Pechstein’s woodcuts. The Nazis removed his paintings from Berlin’s Crown Prince Palace in 1937, listing five as "degenerate art" (1941–42). His remit expanded to science (*Visva-Parichay*, 1937), weaving biology, physics, and astronomy into poetry and stories (*Se*, *Tin Sangi*, *Galpasalpa*). Chronic illness struck in late 1937 (coma) and late 1940 (final decline). His last poem, dictated 30 July 1941, reads: "Today my sack is empty. I have given completely whatever I had to give."

## Terms
- ****Bengal Renaissance**** — A cultural, social, intellectual, and artistic movement in Bengal (late 18th–early 20th c.) advocating rationalism, social reform, and literary modernisation; the Tagore family was at its forefront.
- ****Brahmo Samaj / Brahmo traditions**** — A monotheistic Hindu reform movement founded by Raja Ram Mohan Roy; Debendranath Tagore was a leader. It rejected idol worship and caste rigidity, influencing Tagore’s universalism.
- ****Contextual Modernism**** — Tagore’s approach to art and literature: modernising Bengali forms by spurning rigid classical strictures and absorbing relevant global influences without blind imitation.
- ****Gitanjali (Song Offerings)**** — Tagore’s 1910 Bengali poetry collection (1912 English prose translation by the author); won the 1913 Nobel Prize in Literature for its "profoundly sensitive, fresh and beautiful" verse.
- ****Galpaguchchha**** — Three-volume collection of 84 short stories (written largely 1891–1895, "Sadhana period"); established the Bengali short story genre.
- ****Rabindra Sangeet**** — Tagore’s ~2,230 songs, merging fluidly with his literature; influenced by *thumri* style and classical *ragas*; includes national anthems of India and Bangladesh.
- ****Sadhana period**** — 1891–1895, named after Tagore’s magazine *Sadhana*; his most fecund phase for short fiction, written while managing estates in Shelaidaha.
- ****Santiniketan / Visva-Bharati**** — Ashram-school founded 1901 at Santiniketan ("Abode of Peace"); became Visva-Bharati University (foundation 1918, inaugurated 1921), aiming to connect India and the world beyond nationalism.
- ****Shriniketan**** — Institute for Rural Reconstruction (1921) near Santiniketan, co-founded with Leonard Elmhirst; focused on village uplift through education and cooperative self-help.
- ****Upanayan**** — Hindu coming-of-age rite (sacred thread ceremony) marking initiation into student life; Tagore underwent this at age eleven before touring India with his father.

## Debates and open questions
- **Translation and global reception**: W.B. Yeats and Ezra Pound initially championed Tagore’s English *Gitanjali*, but Yeats later attacked his English translations as "sentimental rubbish" that "wrecked his reputation." E.M. Forster noted *The Home and the World*’s charms "vanished in translation." Scholars debate whether Tagore’s global stature suffers from inadequate translations of his Bengali originals.
- **Nationalism vs. internationalism**: Tagore denounced political nationalism (especially the Swadeshi movement) as a "menace," advocating a universal humanism. Critics (including some Indian nationalists) charged this undermined the independence struggle; Gandhi valued his songs but differed on methods. The tension between cultural patriotism and political anti-nationalism remains a central interpretive debate.
- **Gandhi–Tagore disputes**: They clashed on the 1934 Bihar earthquake (Gandhi: divine retribution for untouchability; Tagore: unscientific superstition), on the spinning wheel (*charkha*) as economic strategy, and on the role of Western science/education. Their dialogue frames competing visions of Indian modernity.
- **Caste and social reform**: Tagore campaigned against untouchability (opening Guruvayoor Temple) and wrote Dalit protagonists, yet his family’s Pirali Brahmin background and his own position within *bhadralok* society complicate assessments of his radicalism.
- **Painting: late bloom or eccentricity?**: Tagore began painting at sixty; works were exhibited in Europe but removed by Nazis as "degenerate art." Critics debate whether his visual art constitutes a significant modernist oeuvre or a synesthetic extension of his poetry, especially given his likely colour blindness.
- **Science and poetry in late work**: *Visva-Parichay* (1937) and stories like *Se* weave contemporary physics and biology into narrative. Scholars differ on whether this reflects a genuine integration of scientific epistemology or a poetic metaphorics.
