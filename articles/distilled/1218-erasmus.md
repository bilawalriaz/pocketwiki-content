# Erasmus

## Overview

Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam (c. 1466–1536) was a Dutch Catholic priest, humanist, theologian, and philologist whose scholarly editions of the New Testament and Church Fathers profoundly shaped both the Protestant Reformation and the Catholic Counter-Reformation. Writing in elegant Latin and Greek, he advocated for reform from within the Church through textual criticism, pastoral theology, and a return to early Christian sources. His works—including *The Praise of Folly*, *On Free Will*, and his New Testament annotations—made him the most widely read author of the early 16th century, accounting for 10–20% of European book sales by the 1530s. Caught between Catholic and Protestant reformers, Erasmus promoted peace, tolerance, and a moderate Christian humanism rooted in classical learning and scriptural study.

## Timeline

- **c. 1466** — Born in Rotterdam (date disputed)
- **1487** — Enters Augustinian canonry at Stein
- **1499–1500** — First visit to England; meets Colet and More
- **1501–1504** — Studies in France; writes *Enchiridion*
- **1506–1509** — Travels in Italy; discovers Valla’s New Testament notes
- **1511–1515** — Second major stay in England; writes *Praise of Folly*
- **1516** — Publishes first Latin-Greek New Testament with annotations
- **1521** — Settles permanently in Basel
- **1525** — Publishes *On Free Will*; begins controversy with Luther
- **1529** — Leaves Catholic Basel after Mass banned; moves to Freiburg
- **1535** — Returns to Basel; dies 12 July 1536

## Body

### Scholarship and Textual Criticism

Erasmus revolutionized biblical scholarship through rigorous philological methods. His *Adagia* (1500) compiled thousands of classical proverbs, becoming foundational for Renaissance education. In 1516, he published the first printed Greek New Testament with a revised Latin Vulgate and extensive annotations, modeled on Lorenzo Valla’s earlier work. Though he denied intending a definitive critical edition, his text became the basis for most Textus Receptus translations, including Luther’s German Bible and the King James Version. Working closely with printer Johann Froben in Basel, Erasmus supervised production personally, ensuring accuracy through iterative proof corrections. His editions of Church Fathers and classical authors like Plutarch and Cicero reintroduced ancient thought to Western Europe.

### Religious Reform and Theology

Erasmus advocated reforming the Catholic Church from within, emphasizing moral renewal over doctrinal rupture. He criticized monastic corruption, clerical luxury, and superstitious practices while defending core doctrines like transubstantiation. His *Philosophia Christi*—a life modeled on Christ’s teachings—prioritized peace, mercy, and inner devotion over ritualistic externalism. He promoted pastoral care through accessible scripture and practical ethics, writing extensively on marriage, confession, and preparation for death. Though initially sympathetic to Luther’s critique of indulgences, Erasmus broke with him over salvation doctrine, defending synergism (human cooperation with grace) against Luther’s monergism (divine sovereignty alone).

### Pacifism and Political Thought

Peace was central to Erasmus’s worldview. He condemned warfare between Christian rulers and advocated for diplomatic solutions, arguing that true courage lay in restraint rather than conquest. In *The Complaint of Peace* and *The Education of a Christian Prince*, he urged rulers to govern justly, avoid militarism, and pursue concord. He opposed the Ottoman threat not through crusade but through moral reform of Christendom. His political philosophy emphasized consent of the governed and checks on princely power, blending classical republican ideals with Christian ethics. He famously declared that “war is sweet to those who have never tasted it,” reflecting his belief that conflict stemmed from princely ambition rather than necessity.

### Literary Style and Irony

Erasmus wrote in a spontaneous yet polished Latin style, blending classical rhetoric with Christian themes. His use of irony—especially in *The Praise of Folly*—allowed him to critique society subtly, often praising folly to expose vice. His letters, written daily in his own hand, reveal his wit, erudition, and personal relationships. He employed dialogues and colloquies to engage readers while avoiding direct confrontation. His concept of *copia*—abundant expression through varied adages and tropes—enriched Renaissance Latin prose but sometimes led to misinterpretation when irony was missed. Critics accused him of ambiguity, yet Erasmus saw nuance as essential to pastoral persuasion.

### Controversies and Legacy

Erasmus faced criticism from both Catholics and Protestants. Catholic theologians attacked his views on monasticism and scripture, while Protestants like Luther condemned his defense of free will and traditional sacraments. Despite this, he remained committed to Catholic unity, opposing schism even as friends like Thomas More suffered execution under Henry VIII. His middle path disappointed partisans on both sides, yet his influence endured. By the 1570s, “everyone had assimilated Erasmus to one extent or another.” Modern assessments recognize him as a pivotal figure in shaping early modern Christianity, bridging medieval piety and Renaissance humanism.

## Terms

- **Philologist**: Scholar specializing in historical texts, languages, and their transmission.
- **Synergism**: Doctrine holding that salvation involves cooperation between divine grace and human will.
- **Monergism**: Doctrine asserting that salvation is entirely God’s work, independent of human choice.
- **Textus Receptus**: The Greek New Testament text used by Protestant reformers, largely derived from Erasmus’s editions.
- **Philosophia Christi**: Erasmus’s ideal of Christian living based on Christ’s teachings, especially the Sermon on the Mount.
- **Copia**: Literary theory emphasizing abundant, varied expression using classical adages and tropes.
- **Irenicism**: Promotion of religious unity and tolerance among Christian denominations.
- **Patristic**: Relating to the Church Fathers, early Christian theologians and writers.
- **Vulgate**: The standard Latin translation of the Bible used in the Western Church.
- **Humanist**: Renaissance scholar emphasizing classical learning, rhetoric, and moral philosophy.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several aspects of Erasmus’s life and thought remain contested. His exact birth year is uncertain—he calculated it as 1466, but remembered ages suggest 1469. The authenticity of his early biographical account (*Epistle to Grunnius*) is debated, as it may contain fictional elements. Historians disagree on whether he truly accepted the Lady Margaret’s Professorship of Divinity at Cambridge. His sexual orientation and relationships, particularly with Servatius Rogerus, are subjects of scholarly discussion given the intimate tone of their correspondence. The extent of his antisemitism and anti-Islamic sentiment varies in interpretation, with some scholars finding harsh prejudice and others contextualizing his views within medieval Christian norms. Finally, debates persist over whether Erasmus enabled the Reformation through his textual scholarship or inadvertently hindered genuine reform by advocating excessive compromise with Catholic authorities.
