# Cinema of Japan

## Overview
The cinema of Japan (日本映画, Nihon eiga) is one of the world's oldest and most influential film industries, originating in the late 1890s. It achieved global acclaim during its "Golden Age" in the 1950s and has since become a dominant force in animation, with anime films consistently topping international box offices and critical polls. Its history reflects Japan's cultural traditions, wartime experiences, and post-war modernization.

## Timeline
- **1896** — Kinetoscope first shown in Japan.
- **1897** — Vitascope and Cinematograph introduced; first Japanese films shot.
- **1908** — Director Shōzō Makino begins career, pioneering the jidaigeki genre.
- **1918** — Pure Film Movement gains momentum with films like *The Glow of Life*.
- **1930** — First Japanese feature-length talkie, *Fujiwara Yoshie no furusato*.
- **1939** — Film Law passed, increasing state control over cinema.
- **1943** — Akira Kurosawa's feature debut, *Sugata Sanshiro*.
- **1950** — Kurosawa's *Rashomon* wins Golden Lion at Venice, introducing Japanese cinema to the world.
- **1954** — *Seven Samurai* and *Godzilla* are released, defining the Golden Age.
- **1984** — Hayao Miyazaki's *Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind* launches Studio Ghibli's rise.
- **1997** — *Princess Mononoke* becomes a massive hit; J-horror boom begins with *Ringu*.
- **2001** — *Spirited Away* wins Academy Award for Best Animated Feature.
- **2020** — *Demon Slayer: Mugen Train* becomes Japan's highest-grossing film.
- **2023** — *The Boy and the Heron* and *Godzilla Minus One* win Academy Awards.

## Body

### Early Development and Silent Era
Japanese cinema began with imported Western technology in the 1890s, but early films were heavily influenced by traditional theater like kabuki. Silent films employed *benshi*, live narrators who explained the story. The Pure Film Movement of the 1910s criticized this theatrical style and advocated for more cinematic techniques, leading to the use of actresses and editing cuts. By the mid-1920s, popular samurai films (*jidaigeki*) and the rise of major studios like Shochiku established a thriving domestic industry.

### Wartime, Occupation, and the Golden Age
During World War II, the government used cinema for propaganda. After Japan's surrender in 1945, the American occupation's censorship body (SCAP) banned militaristic themes, temporarily halting *jidaigeki* production. The 1950s became the "Golden Age," as directors like Akira Kurosawa, Yasujirō Ozu, and Kenji Mizoguchi created internationally acclaimed masterpieces. Films like *Rashomon* (1950), *Tokyo Story* (1953), and *Seven Samurai* (1954) won major festival awards and are considered among the greatest ever made. This era also saw the birth of the *kaiju* (monster) genre with *Godzilla* (1954).

### New Wave, Decline, and the Rise of Anime
The 1960s saw the peak of film production and the emergence of the Japanese New Wave, with directors like Nagisa Oshima and Shohei Imamura creating socially conscious, avant-garde films. The 1970s brought a decline in theater attendance due to television, leading studios to produce more violent or sexual content. The 1980s saw the rise of anime as a major cultural export, with Hayao Miyazaki's Studio Ghibli and Katsuhiro Otomo's *Akira* (1988) gaining worldwide followings.

### Contemporary Cinema and Global Impact
Since the 1990s, Japanese cinema has experienced a resurgence, often called a "second Golden Age," driven by the global popularity of anime and acclaimed live-action directors like Hirokazu Koreeda and Takeshi Kitano. The J-horror genre, beginning with *Ringu* (1998), became an international phenomenon. Anime now dominates the Japanese box office; as of 2025, the top 16 highest-grossing Japanese films worldwide are all animated. Recent films like *Demon Slayer: Mugen Train* (2020) and *The Boy and the Heron* (2023) have broken records and won top international awards, including Academy Awards.

## Terms
- **Jidaigeki**: Period films set in the Edo period (1603–1868) or earlier, often featuring samurai.
- **Gendaigeki**: Films set in the contemporary era, the opposite of *jidaigeki*.
- **Kaiju**: "Strange creature" films, a genre of monster movies exemplified by *Godzilla*.
- **Tokusatsu**: Live-action films that make heavy use of special effects, often involving costumed superheroes or monsters.
- **Benshi**: Live narrators who accompanied and explained silent films in Japanese theaters.
- **Pure Film Movement**: A 1910s reform movement advocating for more cinematic techniques over theatrical storytelling.
- **Pinku eiga (Pink film)**: Low-budget, softcore pornographic films that became a training ground for many directors.
- **Nuberu bagu (Japanese New Wave)**: A film movement from the late 1950s to early 1970s characterized by social criticism and avant-garde techniques.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The source notes that the "Golden Age" is widely considered the 1950s, but the 1990s and 2000s are also referred to as a "second Golden Age," suggesting ongoing debate about the industry's peak periods.
- The impact of government censorship, first under SCAP and later through domestic laws like the 1939 Film Law, on artistic expression is a recurring historical theme.
- The role and recognition of women in Japanese cinema, both as directors and subjects, is highlighted as an important area of study, with a 2009 symposium dedicated to the topic.
