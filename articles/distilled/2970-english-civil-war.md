# English Civil War

## Overview
The English Civil War (1642–1651) was a series of conflicts between Royalists supporting King Charles I and Parliamentarians over the balance of power in the Kingdom of England. Part of the wider Wars of the Three Kingdoms, it resulted in the king's execution, the establishment of a republic, and ultimately a restored monarchy with a stronger Parliament, setting England on a path toward parliamentary monarchy.

## Timeline
- **1642** — Charles I raises his standard at Nottingham, beginning the First English Civil War.
- **1645** — Parliament creates the New Model Army, which wins decisive victories at Naseby and Langport.
- **1646** — Royalist defeat; Charles I surrenders to the Scottish army, ending the First Civil War.
- **1648** — Second English Civil War ends with Parliamentarian victory at Preston.
- **1649** — Charles I is tried for treason and executed; the Commonwealth of England is proclaimed.
- **1650** — Charles II is crowned King of Scotland, promising a Presbyterian church.
- **1651** — Parliamentarians defeat Charles II at Worcester, ending the Third English Civil War.
- **1660** — The Restoration: Charles II returns to the throne, restoring the monarchy.

## Body

### Background and Causes
The conflict stemmed from long-standing tensions between the Stuart monarchy and Parliament. King James I and his son Charles I believed in the Divine Right of Kings, while Parliament asserted its traditional role, especially in taxation. Charles's "Personal Rule" (1629–1640) without Parliament, his imposition of extra-parliamentary taxes like ship money, and his promotion of High Anglican religious policies alienated many. The immediate trigger was Charles's attempt to impose a new prayer book on Presbyterian Scotland in 1637, which sparked the Bishops' Wars. To fund an army against the Scots, Charles was forced to recall Parliament in 1640, leading to a constitutional crisis.

### The First English Civil War (1642–1646)
War began in 1642 after failed negotiations and Charles's attempt to arrest five MPs. Early Royalist cavalry successes, led by Prince Rupert, were countered by the disciplined New Model Army under Oliver Cromwell and Thomas Fairfax. Key Parliamentarian victories at Marston Moor (1644) and Naseby (1645) destroyed Charles's main armies. The war ended in 1646 with Charles surrendering to the Scots, who later handed him to Parliament.

### The Interregnum and Second Civil War (1646–1649)
Victory exposed divisions between moderate Parliamentarians, the New Model Army, and Scottish Presbyterians. Charles secretly negotiated with the Scots, leading to the Second Civil War in 1648. Royalist uprisings and a Scottish invasion were crushed by the New Model Army. In response, the Army purged Parliament (Pride's Purge), tried Charles I for treason, and executed him in January 1649. England became a republic, the Commonwealth.

### The Third English Civil War and Aftermath (1649–1660)
The conflict expanded as Charles II was crowned in Scotland and allied with Covenanters. Cromwell defeated Scottish forces at Dunbar (1650) and Worcester (1651). Cromwell also led a brutal campaign in Ireland, suppressing Royalists and confiscating Catholic land. The Commonwealth and later the Protectorate under Cromwell proved unstable. After Cromwell's death, the monarchy was restored in 1660 with Charles II, but Parliament's power was permanently strengthened.

## Terms
- **Divine Right of Kings**: The doctrine that a monarch's authority comes directly from God, not from the people or Parliament.
- **Personal Rule**: The period (1629–1640) when Charles I governed without summoning Parliament.
- **Ship Money**: An extra-parliamentary tax levied by Charles I, which became a major grievance.
- **New Model Army**: The professional, centrally organized Parliamentarian army created in 1645.
- **Covenanters**: Scottish Presbyterians who signed the National Covenant (1638) to resist Charles I's religious reforms.
- **Rump Parliament**: The remnant of the Long Parliament after Pride's Purge in 1648, which tried and executed Charles I.
- **Commonwealth**: The republican government of England from 1649 to 1653 and 1659 to 1660.
- **Restoration**: The 1660 return of the monarchy under Charles II.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians debate the war's primary cause. The **Whig** view saw it as a struggle for parliamentary rights against royal tyranny. The **Marxist** interpretation, led by Christopher Hill, framed it as a bourgeois revolution. **Revisionists** in the 1970s emphasized contingency and local loyalties, challenging both theories. More recently, the **"Wars of the Three Kingdoms"** perspective argues the conflict cannot be understood apart from events in Scotland and Ireland. Another debate centers on whether it was fundamentally a **religious war**, as argued by John Morrill, or a constitutional one.
