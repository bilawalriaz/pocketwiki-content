# Goethe's Faust

## Overview
*Faust* is a tragic play in two parts by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, widely considered his magnum opus and the greatest work of German literature. It explores the consequences of a scholar's pact with the devil, evolving from a personal tragedy about knowledge and desire into a broader allegory about human striving, society, and redemption.

## Timeline
- **c. 1772–1775** — Goethe develops the earliest version, the *Urfaust*.
- **1790** — *Faust, a Fragment* is published.
- **1806** — Goethe completes a preliminary version of Part One.
- **1808** — Part One is published.
- **1828–29** — Revised edition of Part One, the last edited by Goethe.
- **1831** — Goethe finishes writing Part Two.
- **1832** — Part Two is published posthumously.
- **1886** — A copy of the lost *Urfaust* manuscript is discovered.
- **1938** — World premiere of both parts, unabridged, in Dornach, Switzerland.

## Body

### Development and Structure
The work evolved over decades. The *Urfaust* (c. 1772–1775) is an early form with 22 scenes, mostly in rhymed verse. Its manuscript is lost, but a copy was found in 1886. The first printed version was a *Fragment* in 1790. Part One was completed in 1806 and revised until 1829. Part Two, which occupied Goethe's final years, was finished in 1831 and published posthumously. The two parts are often subtitled "Part One" and "Part Two" in English.

### Faust, Part One: The "Small World"
Part One is set in a local, temporal milieu. The story begins with a wager in Heaven between God and Mephistopheles (the Devil) over the soul of Heinrich Faust, a scholar disillusioned with the limits of human learning. In his study, Faust makes a pact: Mephistopheles will serve him on Earth, and in return, Faust will serve him in the afterlife. The deal is sealed when Faust signs with his blood. The wager specifies that if Mephistopheles can grant Faust a moment of transcendence so perfect he wishes it to last forever, Faust will instantly die and belong to the Devil. With Mephistopheles' help, Faust seduces Gretchen (Margaret). This leads to tragedy: Gretchen's mother dies, her brother is killed by Faust, and Gretchen drowns her illegitimate child. She is imprisoned for murder. Faust tries to free her, but she refuses to escape. Voices from Heaven announce her salvation ("Sie ist gerettet"), a change from the harsher ending of the *Urfaust* ("Sie is condemned").

### Faust, Part Two: The "Wide World"
Part Two shifts focus from Faust's personal soul to broader social, historical, and philosophical themes. It is rich in classical allusion and consists of five acts. Faust's journey continues through allegorical adventures. Ultimately, he loses his wager with Mephistopheles but is saved by divine mercy. Angels declare the play's central theme: "He who strives on and lives to strive / Can earn redemption still." This conclusion resolves the outcome of the wagers from Part One.

### Translations and Adaptations
*Faust* has been widely translated and adapted. Notable English translations include those by Anna Swanwick (1850, 1878), Bayard Taylor (1870–71), and Walter Kaufmann (who translated Part One fully but only selected scenes from Part Two). The work has inspired countless musical and film adaptations, from operas by Gounod and Boito to films by F. W. Murnau and Alexander Sokurov.

## Terms
*   ***Urfaust***: The earliest known version of Goethe's *Faust*, developed between 1772 and 1775.
*   **Mephistopheles**: The Devil, who wagers with God that he can corrupt Faust.
*   **Gretchen (Margaret)**: Faust's love interest in Part One; her tragedy forms the core of the first part.
*   **Pact**: The agreement where Faust trades his soul to Mephistopheles for earthly knowledge and experience.
*   **Wager**: The specific condition within the pact: if Faust ever wishes a moment to last forever, he will die and belong to the Devil.
*   **Transcendence**: The state of surpassing ordinary limits, which Faust seeks and which becomes the key to the wager.
*   **"Small World" / "Wide World"**: Terms describing the shift in scope from Part One's personal drama to Part Two's universal allegory.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Attribution of Translations**: The authorship of an 1821 English translation is disputed. Some scholars attribute it to Samuel Taylor Coleridge, while others provide evidence against this, citing Coleridge's denials.
*   **Political Interpretation**: Boris Pasternak's 1950 Russian translation was attacked in the Soviet journal *Novy Mir* for allegedly distorting Goethe's ideas to defend "pure art" and for emphasizing irrational elements over progressive social meaning.
*   **Completeness of Part Two**: Translator Walter Kaufmann objected to translating all of Part Two, believing its attempt to imitate Greek poetry in German could not be effectively transposed into English.
