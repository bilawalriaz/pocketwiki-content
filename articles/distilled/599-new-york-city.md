# New York City

## Overview
New York City, the most populous U.S. city with over 8.8 million residents as of 2020, sits at the mouth of the Hudson River on New York Harbor. It serves as the demographic and economic core of the Northeast megalopolis and the world's largest metropolitan economy, functioning as a global hub for finance, media, culture, and diplomacy. Its five boroughs—Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx, and Staten Island—were consolidated into a single city in 1898, creating a dense, linguistically diverse metropolis where over 800 languages are spoken.

## Body

### Early History and Colonial Foundations
Before European contact, the area was inhabited by Munsee-speaking Lenape people, whose homeland of Lenapehoking included present-day Manhattan, Staten Island, and parts of Long Island. Italian explorer Giovanni da Verrazzano first documented the harbor in 1524, followed by Spanish captain Estêvão Gomes in 1525. Henry Hudson rediscovered the harbor in 1609 for the Dutch East India Company, leading to Dutch colonization. The Dutch established a permanent settlement in 1624 and founded New Amsterdam in 1626. Peter Minuit famously purchased Manhattan from the Lenape in 1626 for 60 guilders (~$900 in 2018), though legends of a $24 bead purchase are disproven.

### Dutch and English Rule
Under Dutch rule, the colony grew slowly until the patroon system of 1628 attempted to attract settlers through land grants. Peter Stuyvesant governed from 1647, expanding the population from 2,000 to 8,000 but earning a reputation as a despotic leader. In 1664, the English seized New Amsterdam without resistance, renaming it New York after the Duke of York (later King James II). The Dutch briefly recaptured it in 1673, renaming it New Orange, before returning it to England in 1674. English rule brought yellow fever epidemics and the establishment of New York as a major slave-holding port, with 42% of households enslaving Africans by 1730. The 1735 trial of John Peter Zenger for seditious libel helped establish press freedom in North America.

### American Revolution and National Capital
During the American Revolution, New York was the site of the largest battle—Battle of Long Island in 1776—and the British made it their military headquarters. The city became a haven for Loyalists and escaped slaves, with up to 10,000 freedmen crowding British lines. After independence, New York served as the national capital from 1785 to 1790, hosting George Washington's inauguration and the drafting of the Bill of Rights. The capital moved to Philadelphia in 1790, but New York continued growing, reaching 3.43 million residents by 1900.

### Industrialization and Immigration
The 19th century transformed New York through immigration and infrastructure. The Erie Canal (completed 1825) connected the port to the interior, while the Commissioners' Plan of 1811 established Manhattan's street grid. Irish and German immigration surged, especially during the Great Famine, with over 200,000 Irish arriving by 1860. Tammany Hall dominated local politics. The Civil War era saw Draft Riots in 1863, where ethnic tensions erupted into violence against Black residents and elites, killing at least 120 people. The 1886 dedication of the Statue of Liberty and the opening of Ellis Island made the city the primary gateway for 14 million immigrants.

### Modern Consolidation and Global Ascendancy
The 1898 consolidation created the five-borough city, unified by the 1904 subway. The early 20th century saw New York become the world's largest urban area by the 1920s and the first megacity by the 1930s. The Harlem Renaissance flourished during Prohibition, while Wall Street emerged as the world's premier financial center. Post-WWII, the United Nations headquarters (1952) and abstract expressionism solidified New York's cultural dominance. The 1970s fiscal crisis nearly bankrupted the city, but financial sector growth in the 1980s restored prosperity. The September 11 attacks in 2001 killed 2,753 people and reshaped Lower Manhattan's skyline with the new World Trade Center.

### Contemporary Challenges
Recent decades have seen Hurricane Sandy (2012), the COVID-19 pandemic (2020), and ongoing affordability crises. The city remains the most expensive in the U.S., with Manhattan producing the highest county GDP. Climate change has prompted major infrastructure investments, including $15 billion in federal funding for resiliency projects. Despite challenges, NYC continues leading in finance, media, technology, and culture.

## Terms
- **Patroon system**: Dutch colonial land grant program offering land and autonomy to wealthy settlers who brought 50 colonists
- **Consolidation**: The 1898 merger of Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx, and Staten Island into a single city government
- **Megacity**: An urban area with over 10 million residents; NYC was the first in the early 1930s
- **Fintech**: Financial technology sector centered on Wall Street and expanding throughout Manhattan
- **Urban heat island effect**: The phenomenon where cities experience higher nighttime temperatures due to pavement and buildings
- **Gini coefficient**: Statistical measure of income inequality (NYC's was 0.55 in 2022)
- **Stop-and-frisk**: NYPD policy allowing police to detain and search individuals, declared unconstitutional in 2013
- **Right-to-shelter law**: NYC policy guaranteeing housing for anyone who needs it, regardless of status
- **Stonewall riots**: 1969 protests against police raids at the Stonewall Inn, catalyzing the modern LGBTQ+ rights movement
- **Commissioners' Plan of 1811**: Urban planning document that established Manhattan's rectangular street grid

## Debates and Open Questions
The $24 glass beads legend for Manhattan's purchase remains widely believed despite being disproven. The effectiveness of the NYPD's stop-and-frisk program in reducing crime continues to be debated, as crime rates declined even after its 2013 court ruling. The city's response to climate change, including proposed seawalls and coastal barriers, faces questions about feasibility and cost-effectiveness. Whether NYC's post-pandemic recovery will restore its pre-2020 economic dominance remains uncertain, particularly regarding office vacancy rates and remote work trends. The long-term viability of congestion pricing (implemented January 2025) as a solution to traffic and transit funding is still being evaluated.
