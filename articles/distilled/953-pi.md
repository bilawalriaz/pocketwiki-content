# Pi

## Overview
Pi (π) is a mathematical constant defined as the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter in Euclidean geometry, approximately equal to 3.14159. It is an irrational number (cannot be expressed as a ratio of integers) and a transcendental number (not a root of any non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients), which proves the impossibility of "squaring the circle" with compass and straightedge. π appears ubiquitously across mathematics and physics—geometry, trigonometry, number theory, statistics, Fourier analysis, quantum mechanics, and general relativity—often due to its connection to circles, periodicity, and the Gaussian integral. The symbol π was introduced by William Jones in 1706 and popularized by Leonhard Euler.

## Body

### Fundamentals: Definition and Properties
π is defined as the ratio of a circle's circumference *C* to its diameter *d* (*π = C/d*), constant for all circles in Euclidean geometry. Because circumference is an arc length, formal definitions rely on calculus: Karl Weierstrass (1841) defined π as the integral ∫₋₁¹ dx/√(1−x²). To avoid integral calculus in early curricula, alternative definitions are used: π is twice the smallest positive zero of the cosine function (defined via power series or differential equations), or the unique positive real such that the complex exponential *exp(z)* equals 1 at *z = 2πi k* (k ∈ ℤ).

π is **irrational** (proven by Lambert, 1768): it cannot equal a ratio of integers; its decimal expansion is infinite and non-repeating. Common rational approximations include 22/7 and 355/113. The **irrationality measure** (how well π is approximated by rationals) is known to lie between that of *e* and Liouville numbers. π is **transcendental** (proven by Lindemann, 1882, via the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem): it is not a root of any non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients. This implies π cannot be constructed with compass/straightedge, making "squaring the circle" impossible. It is unknown whether π and *e* are algebraically independent (Schanuel's conjecture would resolve this). The digits of π appear **normal** (all finite digit sequences occur with equal asymptotic frequency), but this remains unproven; statistical tests on trillions of digits show no deviation from randomness.

### History of Computation
**Polygon Era (Antiquity–1600s):** Archimedes (c. 250 BC) established the method of exhaustion, bounding π via perimeters of inscribed/circumscribed polygons up to 96 sides. This geometric approach dominated for over a millennium. Zu Chongzhi (c. 480 AD) reached 7-digit accuracy using a 12,288-sided polygon and Liu Hui's geometric series acceleration. Jamshīd al-Kāshī (1424) achieved 16 digits with a 3×2²⁸-sided polygon. Ludolph van Ceulen (1596–1630) computed 35 digits, leading to the name "Ludolphian number" in Germany.

**Infinite Series (1600s–1900s):** The development of calculus and infinite series revolutionized computation. Viète (1593) gave the first infinite product; Wallis (1655) gave another. Newton (1665–66) used an arcsine series for 15 digits. Gregory and Leibniz (1671–73) discovered the arctangent series (*arctan z = z − z³/3 + z⁵/5 − ...*), which equals π/4 at *z=1* but converges impractically slowly. Machin (1706) combined arctan(1/5) and arctan(1/239) for rapid convergence (100 digits). Machin-like formulae remained the standard for 250 years, culminating in Ferguson's 620 digits (1946) by hand. Ramanujan (1914) discovered elegant, rapidly convergent series based on modular equations, anticipating modern algorithms.

**Computer Era (1949–present):** ENIAC computed 2,037 digits in 1949 using an arctan series. The million-digit barrier fell in 1973. Two breakthroughs around 1980 accelerated progress: (1) **Iterative algorithms** (Brent–Salamin AGM method, 1975–76), which *multiply* correct digits per step (doubling, quadrupling, or quintupling), though requiring more memory; (2) **Fast multiplication algorithms** (Karatsuba, Toom–Cook, FFT-based), critical because multiplication dominates runtime. The Chudnovsky formula (1987), a Ramanujan-type series yielding ~14 digits/term, became the standard for record-setting (1 billion digits 1989, 10 trillion 2011, 100 trillion 2022). **Spigot algorithms** (1995, Rabinowitz/Wagon; BBP algorithm, Plouffe) generate digits sequentially without reuse; the BBP formula allows extraction of arbitrary hexadecimal digits without computing predecessors, enabling verification of record computations.

### Role and Characterizations in Mathematics
**Geometry & Trigonometry:** π appears in areas/volumes of circles, spheres, ellipses, cones, tori (e.g., circle area *πr²*, sphere volume *4/3πr³*). By Barbier's theorem, all curves of constant width *w* have perimeter *πw*. Angles in radians: full circle = 2π rad; sin/cos period = 2π.

**Analysis & Eigenvalues:** π is the smallest eigenvalue (wavenumber) of the vibrating string problem (*f'' + λf = 0*, *f(0)=f(1)=0*), giving fundamental mode *sin(πx)*. It is the optimal constant in **Wirtinger's inequality** (*π²∫|f|² ≤ ∫|f'|²*) and the **isoperimetric inequality** (*4πA ≤ P²*, equality for circle). These generalize to **Sobolev** and **Poincaré inequalities** in higher dimensions.

**Fourier Analysis & Uncertainty:** The canonical Fourier transform *f̂(ξ) = ∫ f(x)e⁻²πixξ dx* places π in the exponent. The **Heisenberg uncertainty principle** (Δx Δp ≥ h/4π) follows from the Fourier transform's properties; π is the unique constant making the Gaussian *e⁻πx²* its own Fourier transform. The **Stone–von Neumann theorem** underlies this uniqueness.

**Probability & Statistics:** The Gaussian (normal) density *f(x) = (1/σ√2π) e⁻⁽ˣ⁻ᵐ⁾²/²σ²* contains π via the Gaussian integral *∫₋∞^∞ e⁻ᵘ² du = √π*. The central limit theorem explains π's ubiquity. The Cauchy distribution *g(x) = 1/(π(x²+1))* has entropy ln(4π); its integral defines π. The **Hilbert transform** *Hf(t) = (1/π) p.v. ∫ f(x)/(x−t) dx* requires π for unitarity on L²(ℝ).

**Complex Analysis:** **Cauchy's integral formula**: ∮_γ dz/(z−z₀) = 2πi for *z₀* inside Jordan curve γ. The residue theorem generalizes this: ∮ g(z) dz = 2πi Σ Res(g). The factor 2πi arises from deforming γ to a circle and integrating in polar coordinates.

**Number Theory:** Euler's solution to the Basel problem: ζ(2) = π²/6. This implies the probability two random integers are coprime is 6/π² ≈ 61%. The Riemann zeta functional equation involves π: ζ(s) = 2ˢ πˢ⁻¹ sin(πs/2) Γ(1−s) ζ(1−s). Also exp(−ζ'(0)) = √2π.

**Gamma Function & Stirling:** Γ(1/2) = √π; Γ(n) = (n−1)!. Volumes of n-balls: *Vₙ(r) = πⁿ/² rⁿ / Γ(n/2+1)*. **Stirling's approximation**: *n! ~ √(2πn) (n/e)ⁿ*, derived from the gamma function.

**Topology:** Gauss–Bonnet theorem: ∫_Σ K dA = 2π χ(Σ), linking total curvature to Euler characteristic χ. For a sphere (χ=2), area = 4π.

**Physics & Vector Calculus:** π appears in Coulomb's law, Gauss's law, Maxwell's equations, Einstein field equations. Examples: pendulum period *T ≈ 2π√(L/g)*; Heisenberg uncertainty *Δx Δp ≥ h/4π*; Euler buckling *F = π²EI/L²*; Stokes' drag *F = 6πηRv*; Newtonian potential in ℝ²: *Φ = (1/2π) log|x|*, in ℝ³: *Φ = −1/(4π|x|)* (denominator is sphere area).

**Fractals:** In the Mandelbrot set, near the neck (−0.75, 0), iterations to divergence × ε → π as ε→0; near the cusp (0.25+ε, 0), iterations × √ε → π.

### Outside Mathematics
**Physical Phenomena:** π appears in formulae due to circular/spherical symmetry (pendulum, quantum uncertainty, buckling, fluid drag, electromagnetism). The vacuum permeability μ₀ was defined exactly as 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m until 2019.

**Memorization (Piphilology):** Guinness record: 70,000 digits (Rajveer Meena, 2015). Techniques: mnemonics (word-length poems/piems), pattern recognition, method of loci. Constrained writing: *Cadaeic Cadenza* (3,835 digits), *Not a Wake* (10,000 digits).

**Popular Culture:** Pi Day (March 14, 3/14); Pi Approximation Day (July 22, 22/7). Tau Day (June 28) advocates τ=2π as more natural. Indiana Pi Bill (1897) attempted to legislate π=3.2 (failed in Senate). Palais de la Découverte "pi room" displays 707 digits (based on Shanks' 1873 error-containing calculation). *Contact* (Sagan) suggests a message in π's digits; *Star Trek* uses π computation to halt a rogue computer.

## Terms
- ****Irrational number**** — A real number that cannot be expressed as a ratio *a/b* of two integers; its decimal expansion is infinite and non-repeating.
- ****Transcendental number**** — A number that is not a root of any non-zero polynomial equation with rational coefficients; all transcendentals are irrational.
- ****Normal number**** — A number whose infinite digit sequence in a given base contains every finite block of digits with equal asymptotic frequency (conjectured for π, unproven).
- ****Method of exhaustion**** — Ancient geometric technique (Archimedes) for finding area/perimeter by inscribing/circumscribing polygons with increasing sides.
- ****Infinite series**** — Sum of infinitely many terms; enabled high-precision π calculation from the 17th century onward (e.g., Gregory–Leibniz, Machin, Chudnovsky).
- ****Iterative algorithm**** — Recurrence relation where each step uses prior outputs to produce a result converging to the target; e.g., Brent–Salamin AGM doubles digits per iteration.
- ****Spigot algorithm**** — Algorithm that yields digits of π sequentially without reusing them (like a dripping tap); BBP variant allows direct extraction of arbitrary hexadecimal digits.
- ****Gaussian integral**** — ∫₋∞^∞ e⁻ˣ² dx = √π; fundamental to probability, Fourier analysis, and the normal distribution.
- ****Euler's identity**** — *eⁱᵖ + 1 = 0*, linking five fundamental constants (e, i, π, 1, 0); derived from Euler's formula *eⁱᵠ = cos φ + i sin φ* with φ=π.
- ****Riemann zeta function**** — ζ(s) = Σ n⁻ˢ (Re(s)>1); ζ(2)=π²/6 (Basel problem); functional equation involves π and Γ; central to prime number distribution.

## Debates and open questions
- **Normality of π:** It is widely believed but unproven that π is a normal number (all digit blocks occur equally often in every base). Statistical tests on trillions of digits support the conjecture.
- **Algebraic independence of π and e:** Unknown whether π and *e* are algebraically independent (i.e., no non-zero polynomial *P(x,y)* with rational coefficients satisfies *P(π,e)=0*). Schanuel's conjecture implies they are.
- **Irrationality measure of π:** The exact irrationality measure (supremum of μ such that |π − p/q| < q⁻ᵐ has infinitely many solutions) is unknown; bounds place it between the measures of *e* and Liouville numbers.
- **Optimal computation algorithms:** While Chudnovsky series and AGM methods dominate current records, the theoretical limits of digit-extraction algorithms (like BBP) and memory-time tradeoffs remain active research.
- **Tau vs. Pi (τ = 2π):** A minority advocates τ (radians per full turn) as the fundamental circle constant, arguing it simplifies formulae (e.g., *eⁱᵗ = 1*, area = ½τr²). This has not displaced π in mainstream mathematics.
