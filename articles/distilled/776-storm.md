# Storm

## Overview  
A storm is a disturbed state of the atmosphere or an astronomical body’s environment, marked by strong winds, precipitation, lightning, or other severe weather. Storms arise from pressure differentials—low-pressure centers surrounded by high pressure—and can profoundly affect human society through flooding, wind damage, storm surge, and wildfire, while also offering benefits like drought relief and recreational snow. They occur on Earth and other planetary bodies, ranging from local dust devils to Jupiter’s Great Red Spot, and have shaped history, culture, and scientific understanding.

## Body  

### Formation and Classification  
Storms form when a low-pressure center develops within a high-pressure system, creating wind and storm clouds like cumulonimbus. Localized low pressure from rising hot air can spawn smaller phenomena such as dust devils and whirlwinds. Meteorologically, a terrestrial storm requires winds of 24.5 m/s (Beaufort 10+) or higher, though common usage is broader. Storms last 12 to 200 hours depending on geography and season.

### Types  
Storms vary widely: **blizzards** bring gale-force winds and heavy snow; **bomb cyclones** are rapidly intensifying mid-latitude cyclones; **derechos** are long-lived straight-line wind events; **tornadoes** are violent land-based whirlwinds; **tropical cyclones** (hurricanes, typhoons) are warm-core systems fueled by condensing moist air. Other types include **ice storms**, **hailstorms**, **dust storms**, **firestorms**, **squalls**, and **nor’westers**.

### Extraterrestrial Storms  
Storms exist beyond Earth. Jupiter’s Great Red Spot—an anticyclone larger than Earth—has persisted over 340 years. Neptune had a similar feature, the Great Dark Spot. Mars experiences planet-wide dust storms, observed in detail by Mariner 9 in 1971. Saturn’s Dragon Storm was imaged by Hubble in 1990 and 1994. Two extrasolar planets—HD 209458 b and HD 80606 b—host storms with winds up to 17,700 km/h.

### Effects on Human Society  
Storms cause shipwrecks, structural damage, power outages, and displacement. Tropical cyclones account for 83% of storm damage despite being only 21% of landfalls. Storm surge causes 90% of tropical cyclone deaths. Lightning ignites wildfires and acid rain; hail damages crops and property; snow disrupts transport and utilities. From 1991–2023, storms caused $720 billion in global damage. Agriculture suffers from hail and flooding but benefits from snow insulation and tropical rainfall. Aviation faces risks from hail and wind shear, leading to FAA mandates for detection systems after 1980s accidents.

### Cultural and Historical Impact  
Storms feature in mythology (Noah’s Flood, *Gilgamesh*), literature (*The Tempest*, *The Wizard of Oz*), and art (Turner, Aivazovsky). They’ve influenced historical events, such as Spain’s 1565 victory at Fort Caroline. Films like *The Perfect Storm* and *The Johnstown Flood* dramatize real disasters. Music and fine arts frequently depict storms as symbols of nature’s power.

## Terms  
- **Beaufort scale**: A measure of wind speed; Force 10+ indicates storm conditions (≥24.5 m/s).  
- **Bomb cyclone**: A mid-latitude cyclone undergoing rapid intensification.  
- **Derecho**: A widespread, long-lived straight-line windstorm linked to thunderstorms.  
- **Firestorm**: A conflagration generating its own wind system, often from wildfires or bombings.  
- **Storm surge**: Abnormal sea-level rise during a cyclone, causing coastal flooding.  
- **Tropical cyclone**: A warm-core storm system fueled by heat from condensing moist air.  
- **Wind shear**: A shift in wind speed/direction with height, hazardous to aircraft.  
- **Acid rain**: Precipitation with low pH caused by nitric oxide and other pollutants.  
- **Cumulonimbus**: A dense, towering cloud associated with thunderstorms.  
- **Nor'easter**: A strong cold-air cyclone affecting the northeastern U.S. and eastern Canada.

## Debates and Open Questions  
The definition of a blizzard remains debated, particularly regarding the temperature threshold, which has been dropped in some U.S. contexts. The classification of what constitutes a "storm" varies between strict meteorological criteria and popular usage. Whether planetary storms like Jupiter’s Great Red Spot are truly analogous to terrestrial storms is a topic of ongoing scientific discussion. The long-term behavior and dissipation mechanisms of extraterrestrial storms, such as those on Saturn and Mars, remain under study.
