# Bacteria

## Overview

Bacteria are ubiquitous, mostly free-living prokaryotic microorganisms—single-celled organisms lacking a nucleus—typically a few micrometres in length. They were among the first life forms on Earth and inhabit nearly every habitat, playing vital roles in nutrient cycling, nitrogen fixation, and decomposition. While most bacteria are harmless or beneficial (in human guts, sewage treatment, food production, and biotechnology), several species cause major infectious diseases, and antibiotic resistance is a growing problem. Once classified as plants, bacteria are now recognized as one of three domains of life, distinct from Archaea and Eukarya.

## Body

### Origin and evolution

The ancestors of bacteria were unicellular microorganisms that appeared about 4 billion years ago. For roughly 3 billion years, bacteria and archaea dominated life. Bacterial fossils like stromatolites exist, but their lack of distinctive morphology prevents dating specific species; gene sequences instead reconstruct bacterial phylogeny, indicating bacteria diverged first from the archaeal/eukaryotic lineage. The most recent common ancestor of bacteria and archaea was probably a hyperthermophile living about 2.5–3.2 billion years ago. Bacteria also drove the second great divergence: eukaryotes arose when proto-eukaryotic cells engulfed alphaproteobacterial symbionts to form mitochondria or hydrogenosomes. Later, some eukaryotes engulfed cyanobacteria-like organisms, forming chloroplasts—a process called primary endosymbiosis.

### Morphology and cellular structure

Bacterial cells are typically 0.5–5.0 micrometres long, about one-tenth the size of eukaryotic cells. Most are spherical (cocci) or rod-shaped (bacilli); others are curved (vibrio), spiral (spirilla), or tightly coiled (spirochaetes). Shape is determined by the cell wall and cytoskeleton and affects nutrient acquisition, attachment, swimming, and predator escape. Most bacteria are single cells, but some form chains, clusters, or multicellular structures; starved myxobacteria use quorum sensing to aggregate into fruiting bodies containing ~100,000 cells, with some differentiating into resistant myxospores. Bacteria also form biofilms—dense surface aggregations up to half a metre deep—which protect cells and make them much harder to kill, a major medical concern.

Bacteria lack a membrane-bound nucleus; their genetic material is typically a single circular chromosome in an irregularly shaped nucleoid. They lack membrane-bound organelles but may have protein-bound ones like carboxysomes, plus a cytoskeleton. Energy generation occurs across the cell membrane via electrochemical gradients. The cell wall, made of peptidoglycan (polysaccharide chains cross-linked by peptides), is essential and targeted by penicillin. Gram-positive bacteria have thick peptidoglycan walls; Gram-negative bacteria have thin walls plus a second lipid membrane. Extracellular structures include flagella (for motility), fimbriae and pili (for attachment and conjugation), and glycocalyx capsules (for protection). Some Gram-positive genera form endospores—highly resistant dormant structures that can survive extreme stress, including outer space conditions, for millions of years.

### Metabolism and growth

Bacterial metabolism is classified by energy source (phototrophic from light, chemotrophic from chemical oxidation), electron donors (lithotrophs use inorganic compounds, organotrophs use organic), and carbon source (heterotrophs use organic carbon, autotrophs fix carbon dioxide). Diazotrophs fix nitrogen gas using nitrogenase. Sulfate-reducing bacteria produce toxic methylmercury in the environment. Bacteria reproduce by binary fission, with some populations doubling every 17 minutes. Growth follows four phases: lag (adaptation), logarithmic (rapid exponential growth), stationary (nutrient depletion), and death.

### Genetics

Most bacteria have a single circular chromosome, ranging from 160,000 base pairs (Carsonella ruddii) to 16.0 Mbp (Minicystis rosea); some have linear chromosomes or multiple chromosomes. Almost all are haploid, so a single inactivating mutation eliminates protein production. Bacteria are clonal but evolve through mutations and genetic recombination. They transfer DNA via three mechanisms: transformation (uptake of exogenous DNA), transduction (bacteriophage-mediated transfer), and conjugation (direct cell contact). Transfer between different species—horizontal gene transfer—can spread antibiotic resistance.

### Behaviour and communication

Many bacteria are motile using flagella, rotating structures powered by electrochemical gradients. Bacteria show swimming and tumbling (a three-dimensional random walk), twitching motility via type IV pili, and gliding motility. They respond to stimuli through chemotaxis, phototaxis, and magnetotaxis. Some bacteria communicate via quorum sensing, coordinating gene expression based on population density; biofilms can have over 500 times increased resistance to antibacterial agents compared to individual cells.

### Classification and identification

Modern classification emphasizes molecular systematics—guanine-cytosine ratio, genome-genome hybridization, and rRNA gene sequencing—because lateral gene transfer makes morphology and metabolism unreliable. Historically, bacteria were classed as plants ("Schizomycetes"); molecular work revealed two prokaryotic domains, Bacteria and Archaea, which with Eukarya form the three-domain system. Identification uses Gram staining (developed 1884), culture techniques, and increasingly DNA-based methods like polymerase chain reaction. Only ~2% of bacterial species have been fully studied; estimates of total diversity range from 10⁷ to 10⁹ species.

### Interactions with other organisms

Bacteria form commensal, mutualistic, and parasitic relationships. Commensals like Escherichia coli live harmlessly in the human gut but can cause infections elsewhere. Predatory bacteria such as Myxococcus xanthus kill and consume other microorganisms. Mutualisms include interspecies hydrogen transfer between anaerobic bacteria and methanogenic archaea, and nitrogen fixation in the rhizosphere (root zone). Human gut flora synthesizes vitamins, ferments carbohydrates, and inhibits pathogens. Nearly all animal life depends on bacteria for vitamin B12 synthesis. Pathogenic bacteria cause tetanus, typhoid, syphilis, cholera, leprosy, and tuberculosis; they produce endotoxins (from broken cells) and exotoxins (released by bacteria). Antibiotics are bacteriocidal (killing) or bacteriostatic (growth-preventing); their use in farming contributes to antibiotic resistance.

### Technology and industry

Lactic acid bacteria have been used for thousands of years in fermented foods (cheese, yogurt, soy sauce, vinegar, wine). Bacteria degrade hydrocarbons in oil spill cleanup (e.g., after the 1989 Exxon Valdez spill) and industrial toxic wastes. Bacillus thuringiensis serves as an environmentally friendly biological pesticide. Bacteria are workhorses of molecular biology, used to produce therapeutic proteins like insulin, and are preserved in Biological Resource Centres.

### History of bacteriology

Leeuwenhoek first observed bacteria in 1676 but did not recognize them as distinct, calling all microorganisms "animalcules"; no one saw them again for over a century. Ehrenberg introduced "bacterium" in 1828. Pasteur disproved spontaneous generation in 1859; Koch proved the germ theory (Nobel 1905) and established Koch's postulates. Ehrlich developed the first antibiotic in 1910. Woese's 1977 discovery that archaea are evolutionarily distinct from bacteria, based on 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing, established the three-domain system.

## Terms

- **Prokaryote**: Organism lacking a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; includes Bacteria and Archaea
- **Peptidoglycan**: Cell wall polymer of polysaccharide chains cross-linked by peptides; unique to bacteria
- **Gram stain**: 1884 test classifying bacteria by cell wall structure; thick peptidoglycan stains purple (Gram-positive), thin stains pink (Gram-negative)
- **Endospore**: Highly resistant dormant structure formed by some Gram-positive bacteria, surviving extreme stress
- **Quorum sensing**: Cell-density-dependent communication where bacteria coordinate gene expression via molecular signals
- **Horizontal gene transfer**: Transfer of DNA between different bacterial species, potentially spreading antibiotic resistance
- **Binary fission**: Asexual reproduction where a bacterium divides into two identical daughter cells
- **Biofilm**: Dense surface-attached bacterial community, often highly resistant to antibiotics
- **Nitrogen fixation**: Conversion of nitrogen gas to nitrogenous compounds by diazotrophs using nitrogenase
- **Three-domain system**: Classification dividing life into Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya

## Debates and open questions

Most named bacteria (43,000+ species) have never been studied; ~75% have no academic research devoted to them, and 90% of studies focus on less than 1% of species. Even in the best-studied bacterium, E. coli, a quarter of its 4,000 genes remain poorly characterized. Estimates of total bacterial diversity range from 10⁷ to 10⁹ species, possibly off by many orders of magnitude. Cavalier-Smith has argued that Archaea and Eukaryotes evolved from Gram-positive bacteria, a contested claim. The earliest life on land may have been bacteria some 3.22 billion years ago, but this is debated.
