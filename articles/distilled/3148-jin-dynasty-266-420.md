# Jin dynasty (266–420)

## Overview
The Jin dynasty (266–420 CE) was an imperial Chinese dynasty that reunified China after the Three Kingdoms period but was soon fractured by civil war and non-Han rebellions. Its collapse led to the Sixteen Kingdoms era in the north and the establishment of the Eastern Jin in the south, marking a major demographic and cultural shift as northern Chinese migrated southward.

## Timeline
- **263** — Sima Zhao's Cao Wei conquers Shu Han, ending one of the Three Kingdoms.
- **266** — Sima Yan (Emperor Wu) usurps the throne from Cao Huan, founding the Jin dynasty.
- **280** — Western Jin conquers Eastern Wu, reunifying China and ending the Three Kingdoms period.
- **291–306** — War of the Eight Princes devastates the empire through civil war among Sima princes.
- **304** — Cheng-Han and Han-Zhao declare independence; the Upheaval of the Five Barbarians begins.
- **311** — Han-Zhao sacks Luoyang, capturing Emperor Huai.
- **316** — Han-Zhao captures Chang'an and executes Emperor Min, ending the Western Jin.
- **317** — Sima Rui (Emperor Yuan) reestablishes the Jin dynasty at Jiankang, beginning the Eastern Jin.
- **383** — Eastern Jin defeats Former Qin at the Battle of Fei River, recovering lands south of the Yellow River.
- **420** — General Liu Yu usurps the throne, ending the Jin dynasty and founding the Liu Song dynasty.

## Body

### Founding and Unification
The Jin dynasty was founded by Sima Yan, who succeeded his father Sima Zhao as regent of Cao Wei. In 266, he forced the last Wei ruler to abdicate, establishing the Jin. In 280, he conquered the rival state of Eastern Wu, reunifying China for the first time since the Han dynasty. Emperor Wu upheld Confucian primogeniture by naming his disabled eldest son as heir, while empowering princes to protect the dynasty—a decision that later backfired.

### Decline and Fall of the Western Jin
After Emperor Wu's death in 290, his disabled son Emperor Hui's reign saw Empress Jia seize power, leading to corruption and the War of the Eight Princes (291–306). This civil war weakened the central government, enabling the "Five Barbarians" (non-Han ethnic groups) to rebel. In 304, northern states like Han-Zhao declared independence. Han-Zhao sacked Luoyang in 311 and captured Chang'an in 316, executing the last Western Jin emperor. The imperial court and many Han Chinese fled south.

### Eastern Jin and Northern Conflicts
In 317, Sima Rui reestablished the dynasty at Jiankang (modern Nanjing). The Eastern Jin was dominated by powerful immigrant gentry clans (menfa), whose authority often rivaled the emperor's. The dynasty launched campaigns to recover the north, most successfully at the Battle of Fei River (383), where a Jin force defeated the Former Qin, causing its collapse. However, internal rebellions and court paranoia often undermined these efforts.

### Migration and Administration
The fall of the north caused massive migration, with one in eight northerners moving south. These immigrants (qiaoren) were exempt from taxes and registered on white paper (baiji), while natives were on yellow paper (huangji). The court created "lodged" administrative divisions (qiaozhou, qiaojun, qiaoxian) as nominal governments-in-exile for northern territories, both to assert legitimacy and appease refugees. The "tu duan" policy later aimed to integrate these groups by registering people based on their current residence.

### Religion and Culture
Taoism was polarized: the court repressed popular sects like the Tianshi Dao (Way of the Celestial Masters) but supported official schools. Buddhism grew in popularity due to the era's chaos, with monks engaging in scholarly debates with elites. The Jin is noted for its greenish celadon porcelain, often decorated with animal and Buddhist figures.

## Terms
- **Menfa (門閥)**: Gentry clans that dominated Eastern Jin politics, limiting imperial authority.
- **Qiaoren (僑人)**: "Lodged people"; northern Han Chinese refugees who migrated south.
- **Baiji (白籍)**: White paper registers for tax-exempt northern immigrants.
- **Huangji (黃籍)**: Yellow paper registers for native southern residents.
- **Tu duan (土斷)**: Policy to register people based on current residence to integrate immigrants.
- **Qiaozhou/Qiaojun/Qiaoxian**: Lodged administrative divisions (province, commandery, county) created for northern refugees, serving as nominal governments-in-exile.
- **Five Barbarians**: Exonym for non-Han ethnic groups (e.g., Xiongnu, Jie, Di, Qiang, Xianbei) who rebelled and established states in the north.
- **Battle of Fei River (383)**: Key victory where Eastern Jin defeated the Former Qin, leading to the latter's collapse.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which Emperor Wu's policy of empowering princes directly caused the War of the Eight Princes is debated.
- The effectiveness and implementation of the "tu duan" policy in integrating immigrants versus appeasing natives remains a scholarly discussion.
- The balance of power between the Eastern Jin emperors and the dominant gentry clans (like the Wang and Xie) is analyzed as a defining feature of the period.
