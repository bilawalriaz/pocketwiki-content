# Lycurgus

## Overview
Lycurgus is the legendary lawgiver credited with creating Sparta's entire social and political system, known as *eunomia* ("good order"). He is a foundational figure in the "Spartan mirage"—the idealization of Sparta as a timeless, austere, and perfectly ordered state. However, as a historical person, he is almost certainly mythical, and the reforms attributed to him were largely invented or reinterpreted centuries after his supposed lifetime to justify Sparta's institutions.

## Timeline
- **c. 885 BC** — One ancient date (Ephorus/Eratosthenes) for the Great Rhetra, Lycurgus's foundational law code.
- **c. 804/821 BC** — Another ancient date (from Thucydides) for the institution of Lycurgus's reforms.
- **c. 776 BC** — Date based on the Disc of Iphitos, which links Lycurgus to the first Olympic Games (likely a 4th-century BC forgery).
- **Late 7th/Early 6th c. BC** — The Lycurgan legend likely emerges in Sparta, after the poet Tyrtaeus, to explain Spartan success.
- **c. 550 BC** — Coinage introduced to Greece, making any Lycurgan law banning precious metals anachronistic.
- **c. 500 BC** — Most social reforms attributed to Lycurgus (e.g., wedding customs, syssitia rules) likely date to this period.
- **3rd c. BC** — Reformist kings Agis IV and Cleomenes III invoke Lycurgus to justify their land redistribution policies.
- **2nd c. AD** — Plutarch writes his *Life of Lycurgus*, consolidating earlier disparate legends.

## Body

### Historicity and Chronology
Almost nothing is known for certain about Lycurgus, including when he lived. Ancient sources are profoundly inconsistent, placing him anywhere from the 11th to the 8th century BC. The earliest source, Herodotus (5th century BC), records oral traditions. The absence of Lycurgus in the earliest Spartan poetry (Tyrtaeus) leads most modern historians to doubt his existence. The Great Rhetra, the document containing his reforms, is not genuine and contains anachronistic content. Its true date is debated, but it likely emerged in the 7th century BC to ennoble Sparta's institutions.

### Attributed Political and Military Reforms
Lycurgus is credited with creating Sparta's constitution, including the *gerousia* (council of elders) and the *ephorate* (magistrates). However, the Great Rhetra does not mention the ephorate, suggesting it was a later addition. He is also credited with establishing the *syssitia* (mandatory mess halls), which became central to Spartan military and social life. These halls likely evolved from private clubs (*andreia*) into their classical form shortly before 500 BC through sumptuary restrictions and compulsory contributions.

### Attributed Economic Reforms
A core myth is that Lycurgus redistributed land equally among citizens (*homoioi*). This is a later invention, first attested during the reformist reigns of Agis IV and Cleomenes III. The poet Tyrtaeus (7th century BC) already opposed land distribution, proving inequality existed early. Lycurgus is also said to have banned gold and silver for a cumbersome iron currency to prevent wealth accumulation. This is anachronistic, as coinage was not introduced to Greece until the 550s BC. Evidence shows Spartans used precious metals and engaged in trade.

### Attributed Social Reforms
Lycurgus is credited with unique customs like staged bride capture, wife-sharing for eugenic purposes, and sumptuary burial laws. Archaeological and poetic evidence (e.g., from Alcman, 7th century BC) shows these customs postdate him, emerging between 600-500 BC. He is also credited with the *agoge* (state education system) and the *crypteia* (secret police killing helots), which likely developed in the 7th century BC alongside the ephorate.

### Legacy and the Spartan Mirage
In Sparta, Lycurgus was honoured as a god, his cult providing divine sanction for the state's laws. The "Spartan mirage" idealized his creation as a perfect, timeless system. This myth was constantly reinvented; later reformers claimed to be restoring his "true" laws. In the modern era, Lycurgus was admired by thinkers like Rousseau for his austere civic morality but criticized by others like Diderot for creating a "dismal monastery." In the 19th and 20th centuries, his legacy was co-opted by German nationalist and Nazi ideology to promote militarism and racial purity.

## Terms
- **Eunomia**: "Good order"; the ideal of a well-regulated, lawful society attributed to Lycurgus's reforms.
- **Great Rhetra**: The supposed foundational law code of Sparta, attributed to Lycurgus but likely a later fabrication.
- **Syssitia**: Mandatory public mess halls for all Spartan citizen men, central to social and military life.
- **Gerousia**: The Spartan council of elders, a key political institution.
- **Ephorate**: A board of five annually elected overseers with significant political power.
- **Homoioi**: "Equals"; the official term for Spartan full citizens (*spartiates*).
- **Agoge**: The rigorous state education and training system for Spartan boys.
- **Helots**: The subjugated, state-owned serf population of Sparta.
- **Spartan Mirage**: The idealized and often fictional image of Sparta as a perfectly ordered, austere state.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historicity**: Did Lycurgus exist at all? Most modern historians consider him mythical.
- **Chronology**: When did the Great Rhetra originate? Dates range from the 9th to the 7th century BC.
- **Authorship of Reforms**: Which, if any, of the attributed institutions can be linked to a single lawgiver? Most reforms likely evolved over centuries.
- **Purpose of the Myth**: Was the Lycurgan legend created to explain Spartan success, to provide divine legitimacy, or to serve as a political tool for later reformers?
