# Broadcasting

## Overview
Broadcasting is the one-to-many distribution of audio and audiovisual content to dispersed audiences via electronic mass communications media, typically radio waves. It evolved from early wireless telegraphy (Marconi, 1894) into a dominant mass medium for entertainment and news starting in the 1920s with commercial AM radio, followed by television in the 1930s. The field encompasses government-managed public services, private commercial networks, and community stations, funded by advertising, license fees, or subscriptions. A defining legal distinction (U.S. CFR Title 47) separates broadcasting—transmissions intended for the general public—from private two-way telecommunications. The industry transitioned from analog to digital signals in the 2000s and now includes streaming and webcasting, with global one-way broadcast reception capacity quadrupling between 1986 and 2007.

- **1894** — Guglielmo Marconi begins developing wireless telegraphy using radio waves.
- **1901** — Marconi demonstrates transatlantic radio wave transmission.
- **1906** — Experimental radio broadcasting begins.
- **1920** — Commercial AM radio broadcasting begins; becomes major mass medium in 1920s.
- **1925** — John Logie Baird demonstrates mechanical television (Nipkow disk) at Selfridges, London.
- **1927** — Philo Farnsworth demonstrates first promising cathode-ray tube television.
- **1929** — BBC begins experimental television broadcasts using Baird's system.
- **1930s** — Stereo FM broadcasting development begins in U.S.; commercial television broadcasting starts.
- **1937** — First regular television broadcasts begin.
- **1960s** — Satellite broadcasting initiated.
- **1970s** — Satellite broadcasting enters general industry usage; FM displaces AM as dominant standard in UK.
- **1980s** — Direct Broadcast Satellites (DBS) emerge.
- **1993–1994** — Webcasting of video and audio streams begins.
- **2000s** — Broadcasters switch from analog to digital signals; streaming digital technology referred to as broadcasting.

## Body

### Definition and Legal Distinctions
Broadcasting distributes content via a one-to-many model using electromagnetic waves (over-the-air/terrestrial), cable, satellite, or internet streaming. The U.S. Code of Federal Regulations (Title 47, Part 97) defines it strictly as "transmissions intended for reception by the general public," excluding private or two-way transmissions like amateur (ham) radio and citizens band (CB) radio. Over-the-air broadcasting generally requires a license; cable transmissions may not, though regulations vary by country.

### Historical Development: Radio
Marconi’s wireless telegraphy (1894–1901) established the physics of radio. World War I accelerated military radio development, enabling post-war commercial AM broadcasting (1920s). World War II further advanced radio for navigation, radar, and aircraft communication. FM broadcasting, developed in the 1930s (U.S.) and 1970s (UK), eventually displaced AM as the dominant commercial standard due to higher fidelity.

### Historical Development: Television
Mechanical television (Baird, 1925, Nipkow disk) allowed BBC experimental broadcasts in 1929. Electronic television, based on Karl Braun’s cathode-ray tube and perfected by Philo Farnsworth (1927), became the 20th-century standard. Post-WWII, television expanded using VHF/UHF spectrum. Satellite broadcasting began in the 1960s, with Direct Broadcast Satellites (DBS) emerging in the 1980s.

### Analog to Digital Transition
Originally, all broadcasting used analog signals (continuous voltage variations analogous to sound/light pressure). In the 2000s, the industry switched to digital signals (sampled, quantized discrete values), enabling HD Radio, Digital Audio Broadcasting (DAB), satellite radio, Digital Radio Mondiale (DRM), and digital television. This transition imposed bandwidth and dynamic range constraints but increased capacity and quality.

### Transmission Methods
A high-powered tower transmits a high-frequency electromagnetic wave modulated with audio/video data. Receivers tune to the frequency and demodulate the signal. Historical methods include:
*   **Telephone broadcasting (1881–1932):** Théâtrophone systems and telephone newspapers over wires.
*   **Radio broadcasting (exp. 1906, comm. 1920):** Audio via radio waves; stations link via networks for syndication/simulcast.
*   **Television broadcasting (exp. 1925, comm. 1930s):** Video added to radio model.
*   **Cable radio/TV (1928/1932):** Coaxial cable distribution, later originating cable-only channels.
*   **Satellite (DBS c. 1974, radio c. 1990):** Direct-to-home programming.
*   **Webcasting (video c. 1993, audio c. 1994):** Internet streaming mixing traditional and dedicated content.

### Economic Models
*   **Commercial:** For-profit, funded by advertising airtime sales and subscription fees (cable/pay-TV).
*   **Public:** Non-profit, publicly owned; funded by license fees, government grants, corporate underwriting (FCC-restricted, no "call to action"), and audience donations (e.g., NPR, PBS, CPB in the U.S.).
*   **Community:** Owned/operated by community groups for local programming; usually non-profit cooperatives.
*   **Internet Webcast:** Audience purchases virtual gifts converted to currency for anchors/platforms; common in live streaming.

### Recorded vs. Live Forms
Early U.S. radio networks forbade prerecorded shows (1930s–40s), requiring live repeats for time zones. Exceptions included the Hindenburg disaster (1937) and WWII war correspondence. Recording allows editing, slow-motion, and error correction but risks "spoilers." Prerecording was also used for propaganda control (Germany 1940s, Radio Moscow 1980s). "Live-to-tape" records performances before a studio audience for later broadcast. Distribution paths include studio-to-transmitter links, satellites (simulcast), physical media (tape, DVD), and final delivery via antenna, cable, or internet multicasting.

### Social Impact
Broadcasting functions as **dissemination**: a one-way flow from one source to a large audience without dialogue exchange. The message, once released, may be altered by gatekeepers (e.g., government) and absorbed unpredictably by audiences (listen, analyze, ignore). Content is sequenced via a **schedule**. Broadcasting addresses an "open-ended destination," assembling audiences into masses (e.g., loyal radio show followers). Receivers generally cannot respond immediately, though modern opt-in/opt-out tools offer some control.

### Broadcast Engineering
A subset of electrical, computer, and IT engineering covering the entire **airchain** (studio, transmitter, remote broadcasts). It integrates audio engineering and RF (radio frequency) engineering. Stations employ broadcast engineers, though one engineer may serve a station group or work contractually in small markets.

## Terms
- ****Broadcasting**** — One-to-many distribution of audio/audiovisual content to dispersed audiences via electronic mass communications media.
- ****Over-the-air (OTA) / Terrestrial broadcasting**** — Transmission from station to home receivers via radio waves; usually requires a license.
- ****Analog signal**** — Continuous signal where instantaneous voltage varies continuously with the source information (e.g., sound pressure).
- ****Digital signal**** — Representation of information as a sampled sequence of quantized discrete values.
- ****Modulation / Demodulation**** — Process of encoding information onto a high-frequency carrier wave (modulation) and extracting it at the receiver (demodulation).
- ****Airchain**** — The complete signal path from studio production through transmitter to antenna radiation.
- ****Dissemination**** — One-way communication flow from a single source to a large audience without dialogue exchange.
- ****Simulcast**** — Simultaneous broadcast of the same programming across multiple stations or platforms.
- ****Direct Broadcast Satellite (DBS)**** — Satellite system designed for direct-to-home reception by the public, distinct from studio network uplinks.
- ****Underwriting**** — Funding model for public broadcasting where corporate grants are acknowledged via FCC-restricted spots that cannot advocate products or include a "call to action."

## Debates and open questions
*   **Definition boundaries:** Whether streaming digital technology (webcasting) legally and functionally qualifies as "broadcasting" remains an evolving classification (noted as "increasingly referred to as broadcasting" in the 2000s).
*   **Licensing parity:** Regulatory inconsistency persists regarding whether cable and internet transmissions require licenses equivalent to over-the-air broadcasting.
*   **Spectrum allocation:** The shift from analog to digital (2000s) and the rise of wireless broadband create ongoing contention for finite VHF/UHF spectrum.
*   **Public vs. commercial funding:** The sustainability of public broadcasting models (license fees vs. government allocation vs. underwriting) remains a policy debate, particularly regarding editorial independence.
*   **Propaganda and control:** The historical use of prerecording for state propaganda (1940s Germany, 1980s Radio Moscow) raises enduring questions about content integrity in live vs. recorded formats.
