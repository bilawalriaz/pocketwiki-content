# Lise Meitner

## Overview
Lise Meitner (1878–1968) was an Austrian-Swedish nuclear physicist whose theoretical interpretation of Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann's experimental data was instrumental in the discovery of nuclear fission. Her work, conducted despite facing significant gender and antisemitic barriers, laid the foundation for nuclear energy and atomic weapons. Her exclusion from the Nobel Prize awarded solely to Hahn remains a prominent historical injustice.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into a Jewish family in Vienna, Meitner showed an early interest in science. After women were admitted to universities, she passed the *matura* exam in 1901 and entered the University of Vienna. Inspired by Ludwig Boltzmann, she earned her doctorate in physics in 1906, becoming the second woman to do so at the university. Her early research on alpha particle scattering contributed to Ernest Rutherford's nuclear model of the atom.

### Career in Berlin and Collaboration with Hahn
In 1907, Meitner moved to Berlin, where Max Planck allowed her to attend his lectures. She began a lifelong collaboration with chemist Otto Hahn, initially working unpaid in a converted woodshop. They discovered the radioactive recoil technique and new isotopes. In 1912, they moved to the new Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Chemistry (KWI), where Meitner worked without pay as a "guest." During World War I, she served as an X-ray nurse. In 1917, she and Hahn isolated the mother isotope of actinium, which she named protactinium. In 1922, she became the first woman to receive a habilitation in physics in Prussia, and in 1926, she became Germany's first female physics professor. Her research on beta decay spectra helped confirm the need for a neutral particle, later named the neutrino.

### Nazi Persecution and Escape
The rise of the Nazi regime progressively stripped Meitner of her positions due to her Jewish ancestry. The 1938 *Anschluss* (annexation of Austria) made her a stateless person. With the help of Dutch physicist Dirk Coster and colleagues, she fled to the Netherlands on July 13–14, 1938. She eventually settled in Stockholm, working at Manne Siegbahn's laboratory, though she faced professional isolation and financial difficulty.

### Discovery of Nuclear Fission
In mid-1938, Hahn and Strassmann in Berlin bombarded uranium with neutrons and found chemical evidence of barium, an element much lighter than uranium. Hahn wrote to Meitner with these puzzling results. During a Christmas walk with her nephew Otto Robert Frisch, Meitner applied the liquid-drop model of the nucleus to interpret the data. They calculated that the uranium nucleus could become unstable and split, with the mass difference converting to energy via Einstein's equation *E=mc²*. Frisch coined the term "fission" for this process. Their theoretical explanation, published in *Nature* in February 1939, confirmed Hahn and Strassmann's chemical discovery and revealed the immense energy potential of splitting the atom.

### Later Life and Legacy
Meitner continued her research in Sweden, working on nuclear reactor design. She declined to join the Manhattan Project, stating, "I will have nothing to do with a bomb!" She received numerous honors, including the Enrico Fermi Award in 1966, but was famously excluded from the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry awarded solely to Hahn. She retired in 1960 and moved to the UK. Element 109, meitnerium, is named in her honor.

## Terms
- **Nuclear Fission:** The process in which the nucleus of a heavy atom, such as uranium, splits into two or more smaller nuclei, releasing a large amount of energy.
- **Liquid-drop Model:** A model of the atomic nucleus treating it as a drop of incompressible fluid, used by Meitner and Frisch to explain how a nucleus could split.
- **Protactinium (Pa):** A radioactive element (atomic number 91) discovered by Meitner and Hahn in 1917.
- **Habilitation:** A post-doctoral qualification required in Germany to become a university professor and independent researcher.
- **Anschluss:** The annexation of Austria by Nazi Germany in March 1938.
- **Neutrino:** A neutral, nearly massless subatomic particle proposed by Wolfgang Pauli to explain the continuous energy spectrum in beta decay.
- **Auger-Meitner Effect:** The emission of an electron from an atom following the absorption of an X-ray, independently discovered by Meitner and Pierre Victor Auger.
- **Transuranium Elements:** Chemical elements with atomic numbers greater than 92 (uranium).

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nobel Prize Exclusion:** The primary debate concerns why Meitner was omitted from the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry awarded to Hahn. Sealed Nobel Committee records suggest a combination of disciplinary bias (chemistry vs. physics), political obtuseness, and the poor relationship between Meitner and the influential Swedish physicist Manne Siegbahn, who was on the physics committee.
- **Relative Contributions:** While Hahn performed the crucial chemical experiments identifying barium, Meitner and Frisch provided the essential physical interpretation that explained the phenomenon as nuclear fission and calculated the energy release. The Nobel Committee's failure to adequately assess this interdisciplinary contribution is seen as a key reason for the oversight.
