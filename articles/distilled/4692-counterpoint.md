# Counterpoint

## Overview
Counterpoint is the art of combining two or more independent melodic lines that are harmonically interdependent yet distinct in rhythm and contour. It is a foundational technique in Western classical music, particularly from the Renaissance through the Baroque period, and is taught through a structured pedagogical system called species counterpoint.

## Timeline
- **c. 1532** — Giovanni Maria Lanfranco describes a precursor to species counterpoint.
- **1619** — Lodovico Zacconi first codifies species counterpoint.
- **1725** — Johann Joseph Fux publishes *Gradus ad Parnassum*, defining the five species.
- **1856–1915** — Sergei Taneyev develops a mathematical theory of counterpoint.
- **1923** — Igor Stravinsky's *Octet* uses linear counterpoint, associated with neoclassicism.

## Body

### General Principles and Development
Counterpoint focuses on the interaction of independent melodies, with harmony as a secondary result. The functional independence of voices is paramount; parallel motion that fuses voices is prohibited. The technique developed strongly in the European Renaissance and Baroque periods. Related compositional forms include the round, canon, and the complex fugue, all examples of imitative counterpoint, where a melodic idea is repeated across voices.

### Species Counterpoint
Species counterpoint is a strict pedagogical system where students write against a fixed melody (*cantus firmus*) in five progressive stages of complexity. Codified by Fux in 1725, the species are: 1) note against note; 2) two notes against one; 3) four notes against one; 4) notes offset as suspensions; and 5) florid counterpoint, combining all previous species. Core rules for all species include beginning and ending on perfect consonances, favoring contrary motion, and avoiding parallel fifths and octaves. Each species introduces specific rules for handling dissonance and melodic embellishment.

### Free and Linear Counterpoint
From the Baroque period onward, composers increasingly used free counterpoint, which relaxes strict species rules. It allows chromaticism, dissonant chords, and greater rhythmic freedom, focusing on how independent lines relate to each other rather than to a cantus firmus. Linear counterpoint, a 20th-century neoclassical approach, prioritizes the integrity of individual melodic lines over harmonic considerations, sometimes creating unconventional vertical sonorities.

### Dissonant Counterpoint
Theorized by Charles Seeger, dissonant counterpoint reverses traditional rules, making dissonance the norm and consonance the exception. It was used by composers like Ruth Crawford-Seeger and John Cage to create a "purified" musical effect.

## Terms
- **Counterpoint**: The relationship between two or more simultaneous, independent melodic lines.
- **Species Counterpoint**: A pedagogical system for teaching counterpoint through five progressive stages of complexity.
- **Cantus Firmus**: A fixed, pre-existing melody (Latin for "fixed melody") against which other parts are written in species counterpoint.
- **Imitative Counterpoint**: A texture where two or more voices repeat a version of the same melodic idea.
- **Free Counterpoint**: A less restrictive style where composers focus on the interaction of independent lines without a cantus firmus.
- **Linear Counterpoint**: A technique prioritizing the horizontal integrity of melodic lines over vertical harmonic considerations.
- **Dissonant Counterpoint**: A method that reverses traditional rules, establishing dissonance as the norm.
- **Suspension**: A note held over from a previous beat to create a dissonance that resolves downward by step (central to fourth species).
- **Parallel Motion**: Movement of two voices in the same direction by the same interval; parallel fifths and octaves are forbidden in strict counterpoint.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the origins and precise rules of counterpoint. For example, while Fux's rules are seminal, later theorists introduced small modifications. The application of counterpoint principles to non-Western and microtonal music is an area of ongoing theoretical extension, as seen in the work of Octavio Agustín. The balance between strict linear independence and harmonic effect remains a central aesthetic consideration in compositional practice.
