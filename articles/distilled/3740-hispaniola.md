# Hispaniola

## Overview
Hispaniola is the second-largest island in the Caribbean, located in the Greater Antilles. It is uniquely divided between two sovereign nations: the Spanish-speaking Dominican Republic in the east and the French/Creole-speaking Haiti in the west. The island is historically significant as the site of the first permanent European settlements in the Americas, which led to profound demographic, ecological, and political changes that shaped the entire Western Hemisphere.

## Timeline
- **c. 6000 BC** — Archaic Age people arrive from mainland Americas.
- **1st millennium BC** — Arawakan-speaking ancestors of the Taíno migrate into the Caribbean.
- **December 5, 1492** — Christopher Columbus lands on the island, which he names Española.
- **1493** — La Isabela, the first European settlement, is founded.
- **1498** — Santo Domingo, the oldest permanent European settlement in the Americas, is founded.
- **1503** — Spanish Crown legalizes the *encomienda* system of forced indigenous labor.
- **1516** — First sugar mill in the New World is established on Hispaniola.
- **1605** — Spanish forced relocation of inhabitants to Santo Domingo leaves the rest of the island open to foreign pirates.
- **1697** — Treaty of Ryswick: Spain formally cedes the western third of the island to France, creating Saint-Domingue.
- **1791** — Major slave revolt begins in Saint-Domingue, leading to the Haitian Revolution.
- **1804** — Haiti declares independence, becoming the first free Black republic.
- **1844** — Dominican Republic declares independence from Haitian rule.
- **1915-1934** — United States occupies Haiti; occupies the Dominican Republic from 1916-1924.

## Body

### Indigenous Peoples and Spanish Colonization
At the time of European contact, Hispaniola was inhabited by several Taíno kingdoms. Columbus's arrival in 1492 initiated Spanish colonization, beginning with the fort La Navidad and the settlement La Isabela. The Spanish established Santo Domingo in 1498 as a base for further conquest. The indigenous population was devastated by forced labor in gold mines under the *encomienda* system, violence, and European diseases like smallpox, leading to a population collapse of up to 95% within a century. To replace this labor force, the Spanish began importing enslaved Africans in 1503, leading to the first major slave revolt in the Americas in 1521.

### Division of the Island and Colonial Economies
By the early 17th century, Spanish neglect allowed French, English, and pirates to establish bases on the island's north and west coasts. France formally established the colony of Saint-Domingue in the west, which became a brutally efficient sugar-producing economy powered by enslaved Africans. It grew to be the wealthiest colony in the Caribbean. Spain retained the eastern part as the Captaincy General of Santo Domingo. The island was formally divided by the 1697 Treaty of Ryswick.

### Independence and the Formation of Two Nations
The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) resulted in the independence of Saint-Domingue as Haiti. In 1804, Haiti's leader Jean-Jacques Dessalines adopted the indigenous name "Haiti" for the new nation. The eastern part of the island changed hands several times before declaring independence as the Dominican Republic in 1844 after a war against Haitian rule. The 19th century saw periods of Spanish re-annexation and U.S. intervention. In the 20th century, both nations endured long periods of dictatorship (e.g., the Duvaliers in Haiti, Trujillo in the Dominican Republic) and U.S. military occupations. In recent decades, the Dominican Republic has developed a significantly larger economy, while Haiti has faced persistent political instability, natural disasters, and extreme poverty.

### Geography and Ecology
Hispaniola is mountainous, with five major ranges. Its highest point is Pico Duarte (3,101 m) in the Dominican Republic. The island has four distinct ecoregions, including moist and dry forests and pine forests. Climate varies with altitude and topography, from hot and humid lowlands to cooler highlands. The island is home to endemic fauna like the Hispaniolan hutia and solenodon. Haiti has experienced severe deforestation, while the Dominican Republic has implemented successful reforestation policies.

## Terms
*   **Greater Antilles:** The largest islands in the Caribbean, including Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica, and Puerto Rico.
*   **Taíno:** The Arawakan-speaking indigenous people who were the dominant group on Hispaniola at the time of European contact.
*   **Cacique:** A leader or chief of a Taíno kingdom.
*   **Encomienda:** A Spanish colonial system granting settlers the right to demand labor or tribute from indigenous people.
*   **Saint-Domingue:** The French colony on the western third of Hispaniola, known for its sugar plantations and enslaved labor force.
*   **Captaincy General of Santo Domingo:** The official name for the Spanish-controlled territory in eastern Hispaniola.
*   **Haitian Revolution (1791–1804):** A successful slave revolt that led to the independence of Haiti and the abolition of slavery there.
*   **Maroons:** Escaped enslaved Africans who formed independent communities.
*   **Mestizo/Mulatto:** Terms for people of mixed European and Indigenous American or African ancestry, respectively.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Pre-contact Population:** Estimates for the Taíno population of Hispaniola in 1492 vary widely, from tens of thousands to as high as 750,000 or more, with ongoing scholarly debate.
*   **Authenticity of "Quisqueya":** Historians disagree on whether the name "Quisqueya" (meaning "mother of the lands") is an authentic indigenous name or a later invention by Spanish chroniclers.
*   **Causes of Taíno Decline:** While disease, violence, and forced labor are all cited, the relative weight of each factor in the catastrophic population decline remains a subject of historical analysis.
*   **Haiti's Forest Cover:** Media reports often claim Haiti has only 2% forest cover, but recent environmental analysis suggests the figure is closer to 30%, though this is still a dramatic decrease from historical levels.
