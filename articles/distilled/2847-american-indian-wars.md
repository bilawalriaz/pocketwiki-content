# American Indian Wars

## Overview
The American Indian Wars were a series of conflicts spanning from the 17th to the early 20th centuries between various Native American tribes and European colonial powers, the United States, and briefly the Republic of Texas and Confederate States. Driven primarily by settler and government desire for tribal lands, these wars resulted in massive land cessions, forced relocations, and a catastrophic decline in the Native American population, fundamentally shaping the geography and demographics of North America.

## Timeline
- **1609–1701** — Beaver Wars between the Iroquois and French-allied Algonquians.
- **1675–78** — King Philip's War in New England.
- **1763–66** — Pontiac's War in the Great Lakes region.
- **1775–1783** — American Revolutionary War, with many Native nations allied to the British.
- **1794** — Battle of Fallen Timbers ends the Northwest Indian War.
- **1813** — Tecumseh killed at the Battle of the Thames during the War of 1812.
- **1830** — Indian Removal Act authorizes the exchange of Native lands east of the Mississippi for western lands.
- **1838–39** — Cherokee forced removal known as the Trail of Tears.
- **1862** — Dakota War of 1862 in Minnesota.
- **1864** — Sand Creek Massacre of Cheyenne and Arapaho.
- **1876** — Battle of the Little Bighorn.
- **1890** — Wounded Knee Massacre.

## Body

### Colonial Periods (1609–1774)
Conflicts began with early colonial settlements and were often intertwined with European rivalries. Tribes like the Iroquois allied with different European powers (e.g., French, British) based on trade and strategic interests. Major wars included the Pequot War (1636–38), King Philip's War (1675–78), and Pontiac's War (1763–66), which frequently involved alliances between colonists and Native nations against other tribes and colonial powers.

### East of the Mississippi (1775–1842)
Following the American Revolution, the new U.S. government inherited conflicts with tribes allied to Britain. The Northwest Indian War (1785–95) ended with the Treaty of Greenville, ceding much of Ohio. The War of 1812 saw a major pan-tribal confederacy under Tecumseh ally with the British; his death in 1813 crippled this resistance. The Indian Removal Act of 1830 authorized the president to negotiate land exchanges, leading to the forced relocation of the "Five Civilized Tribes" from the Southeast, most infamously the Cherokee Trail of Tears. The Second Seminole War (1835–42) was the longest and most costly war the U.S. Army waged against Native Americans.

### West of the Mississippi (1804–1924)
As settlement expanded westward, conflicts intensified. The U.S. government increasingly confined tribes to reservations through treaties and military force. Key conflicts included the Dakota War of 1862, the Colorado War culminating in the Sand Creek Massacre (1864), and the Great Sioux War of 1876–77, which included the Battle of the Little Bighorn. General Philip Sheridan's winter campaigns (1868–69) were pivotal in breaking Plains Indian resistance. The wars in the Southwest, particularly the Apache Wars, continued until Geronimo's surrender in 1886. The Wounded Knee Massacre (1890) is often cited as the symbolic end of the major wars, though scattered conflicts continued into the 1920s.

### Effects on Population and Historiography
The Native American population in the contiguous United States declined from an estimated 600,000 in 1800 to about 250,000 by 1890 due to disease, warfare, and forced removal. Historiography has evolved; 1970s ethno-history sought Native perspectives, while later scholars debated whether U.S. policies constituted genocide. Revisionist historians like Jeffrey Ostler argue some events meet the modern legal definition of genocide.

## Terms
- **Indian Removal Act (1830):** U.S. law authorizing the president to negotiate treaties exchanging Native American lands east of the Mississippi for lands in the west.
- **Reservation:** A designated area of land managed by a Native American tribe under the U.S. Department of the Interior, established through treaties and legislation.
- **Pan-tribal confederacy:** An alliance of multiple Native American tribes formed for a common military or political purpose, such as Tecumseh's confederacy.
- **Treaty of Greenville (1795):** Agreement that ended the Northwest Indian War, ceding Ohio and part of Indiana to the United States.
- **Trail of Tears:** The forced relocation of the Cherokee and other tribes from the Southeast to Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma) in the 1830s, resulting in thousands of deaths.
- **Ghost Dance:** A religious movement among Plains tribes in the late 19th century that predicted the restoration of their lands and way of life, leading to tensions culminating at Wounded Knee.
- **Sand Creek Massacre (1864):** An attack by Colorado militia on a peaceful Cheyenne and Arapaho encampment, killing about 200 people, mostly women and children.
- **Genocide:** The deliberate killing of a large group of people, especially those of a particular ethnic group or nation; its application to the Indian Wars is debated by scholars.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate whether aspects of U.S. policy, including forced removals, warfare, and reservation confinement, constitute genocide. Historian Jeffrey Ostler argues some events fit the modern legal definition, while others contend the conflicts varied too much across time and place for such a broad label. The exact population of Native Americans before European contact remains unknown, with estimates ranging widely. The historiography continues to evolve, with increasing focus on Native American perspectives and the long-term cultural impacts of the wars.
