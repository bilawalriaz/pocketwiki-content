# Cecil B. DeMille

## Overview
Cecil B. DeMille (August 12, 1881 – January 21, 1959) was an American filmmaker and actor who directed 70 feature films between 1914 and 1958, establishing himself as the most commercially successful producer-director in film history. A founding father of American cinema, DeMille pioneered Hollywood filmmaking with *The Squaw Man* (1914), the first full-length feature shot in Hollywood, and defined the epic spectacle genre through biblical and historical narratives like *The Ten Commandments* (1923 and 1956). His work, marked by grand scale, showmanship, and visual innovation, shaped the transition from silent to sound cinema and influenced generations of directors.

## Timeline
- **August 12, 1881** — Born in Ashfield, Massachusetts; raised in New York City
- **1900** — Began stage career with Charles Frohman’s company
- **1913** — Co-founded Jesse L. Lasky Feature Play Company with Lasky, Sam Goldfish, and East Coast businessmen; moved to Hollywood
- **1914** — Directed *The Squaw Man*, first feature-length film made in Hollywood
- **1923** — Released *The Ten Commandments* (silent), Paramount’s highest-grossing film for 25 years until DeMille broke the record again
- **1924** — Left Paramount amid disputes over budgets and creative control
- **1927** — Directed *The King of Kings*, a biblical epic viewed over 800 million times around the world
- **1932** — Returned to Paramount; *The Sign of the Cross* became first sound film to integrate all aspects of cinematic technique
- **1949** — *Samson and Delilah* became highest-grossing film of 1950
- **1950** — Received Academy Honorary Award for 37 years of brilliant showmanship
- **1952** — *The Greatest Show on Earth* won Academy Award for Best Picture
- **1953** — Received Irving G. Thalberg Memorial Award and Directors Guild of America Lifetime Achievement Award
- **1956** — Released 1956 *The Ten Commandments*, final and most iconic film
- **January 21, 1959** — Died; buried at Hollywood Forever Cemetery

## Body

### Early Life and Theater Career
Born into a theatrical family—his father Henry was a playwright and actor, and his mother Beatrice a literary agent—DeMille was immersed in drama from youth. After his father’s death in 1893, Beatrice ran a school and later became a prominent play broker. DeMille studied at the American Academy of Dramatic Arts and began acting on Broadway under Charles Frohman. Though active in theater, he struggled with playwriting and grew disillusioned with stage limitations, especially after David Belasco plagiarized his work on *The Return of Peter Grimm*.

### Transition to Film and Founding Hollywood
In 1913, DeMille partnered with Jesse Lasky, Sam Goldfish (later Samuel Goldwyn), and a group of East Coast businessmen to create the Jesse L. Lasky Feature Play Company. Their first production, *The Squaw Man* (1914), was filmed in a Hollywood barn and became the first feature-length film shot there, marking Hollywood’s rise as the film capital. The film’s success led to the creation of Paramount Pictures through a merger with Adolph Zukor’s company. DeMille’s early silent films showcased dramatic lighting techniques inspired by Belasco, earning them labels like “Rembrandt lighting.”

### Silent Era Success and Scandals
Throughout the 1910s and 1920s, DeMille directed numerous hits including *The Cheat* (1915), *Male and Female* (1919), and *The Ten Commandments* (1923). His films often blended sensationalism with social commentary, reflecting themes of gender dynamics and class conflict. However, increasing scrutiny over moral content led to censorship pressures. In 1924, DeMille left Paramount amid disputes over budgets and creative control, briefly forming DeMille Pictures Corporation before returning in 1932.

### Sound Era and Epic Spectacles
DeMille adapted well to sound films, innovating with microphone booms and camera cranes. His return to Paramount with *The Sign of the Cross* (1932) marked a comeback, followed by ambitious projects like *The Greatest Show on Earth* (1952), which won Best Picture. His 1949 *Samson and Delilah* redefined biblical epics with its lush visuals and controversial themes. The 1956 *Ten Commandments* remake solidified his legacy as a master of spectacle, featuring groundbreaking special effects such as the parting of the Red Sea.

### Personal Life and Political Views
DeMille was known for his authoritarian directing style and complex personal life, including a long marriage to Constance Adams and several mistresses. A conservative Republican, he opposed unionization and supported anti-communist causes, serving on the board of the National Committee for a Free Europe. He also contributed to military efforts during both world wars and designed Air Force Academy uniforms.

### Legacy and Influence
DeMille’s impact extends beyond box office success. He influenced directors like Martin Scorsese, Steven Spielberg, and Alfred Hitchcock, and his films remain culturally significant. Despite critical dismissal during his lifetime, his reputation experienced a renaissance in the 2010s. His archives are preserved at institutions like Brigham Young University, and landmarks like the Hollywood Heritage Museum honor his contributions.

## Terms
- **Lasky Lighting**: Dramatic, low-key lighting technique used by DeMille to create striking visual contrasts, marketed as “Rembrandt lighting.”
- **Neo-Naturalism**: A thematic approach in DeMille’s films emphasizing conflicts between human laws and natural forces.
- **Office-Directing**: DeMille’s method of working with actors in private meetings rather than giving instructions on set.
- **Hays Code**: A moral censorship system introduced in response to public outcry against immoral film content in the 1920s.
- **Technicolor**: A color motion picture process adopted by DeMille in later films, notably *North West Mounted Police* (1940).
- **VistaVision**: A widescreen format used in *The Ten Commandments* (1956) to enhance visual grandeur.
- **Auteur Theory**: A critical concept identifying directors as the primary creative force behind films; DeMille was initially excluded but later reevaluated.
- **Cecil B. DeMille Award**: An annual Golden Globe recognizing lifetime achievement in film, named in his honor.
- **Famous Players–Lasky**: The merged entity of DeMille’s Lasky Company and Zukor’s Famous Players, later becoming Paramount Pictures.
- **Lost City of Cecil B. DeMille**: Archaeological site near Guadalupe, California, containing artifacts from the 1923 *Ten Commandments* set.

## Debates and Open Questions
DeMille’s artistic merit remains contested. Critics historically dismissed his work as shallow spectacle, while supporters argue his visual innovations and thematic consistency qualify him as an auteur. His political conservatism and alleged antisemitism, particularly following *The King of Kings*, sparked controversy, though he donated profits to charity in response. Scholars debate whether his early silent films or later epics better represent his genius, with some praising his pioneering techniques and others criticizing his outdated aesthetics. The availability of only 20 of his silent films on DVD highlights ongoing preservation challenges.
