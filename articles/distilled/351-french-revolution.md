# French Revolution

## Overview

The French Revolution (1789–1799) was a decade of radical political and societal transformation that dismantled the *Ancien Régime*—the feudal, monarchical, and aristocratic system—and replaced it with a succession of republican governments. Triggered by a state bankruptcy caused by regressive taxation, costly wars, and elite resistance to reform, the Revolution began with the calling of the Estates-General in 1789. It progressed through a constitutional monarchy, the abolition of feudalism and church privileges, and the establishment of the First Republic in 1792. Internal revolt and foreign war led to the Committee of Public Safety and the Reign of Terror (1793–94), during which roughly 16,600 people were executed. The Thermidorian Reaction produced the Directory (1795–99), an unstable five-man executive reliant on the military. The Revolution ended with Napoleon Bonaparte’s coup of 18 Brumaire (November 1799), establishing the Consulate. Its legacy includes the end of feudalism in France, the spread of nationalist and democratic ideals across Europe, and the introduction of mass conscription and secular legal codes that reshaped modern warfare and governance.

## Body

### Causes and the Crisis of the *Ancien Régime*

The Revolution stemmed from a convergence of long-term structural pressures and short-term triggers. Between 1715 and 1789, France’s population grew from 21 to 28 million, expanding the middle class (almost 10% of the population) but immiserating wage labourers and tenant peasants. An economic recession from 1785 and catastrophic harvests in 1787–88 spiked food prices and unemployment. The state faced a debt crisis: by 1788, half of revenue serviced debt, largely from the 1778–83 Anglo-French War. The tax system was the core structural flaw—rates varied by region, collection was farmed out to private *tax farmers*, and the nobility and clergy were largely exempt. Reform attempts by ministers Calonne (1786) and Brienne (1787) foundered on the *parlements* (regional high courts dominated by nobility), which insisted only an Estates-General could authorize new taxes. This institutional deadlock forced Louis XVI to summon the Estates-General for May 1789. Simultaneously, Enlightenment critiques of privilege, the example of the American Revolution, and scandals like the Affair of the Diamond Necklace delegitimized the monarchy and aristocracy in educated public opinion.

### The Estates-General and the National Assembly (1789)

The Estates-General convened at Versailles on 5 May 1789. The Third Estate (610 deputies, mostly lawyers and office-holders) demanded voting by head rather than by estate, which would allow them to outvote the clergy (303) and nobility (322). When the king and privileged orders resisted, the Third Estate unilaterally verified its credentials and declared itself the **National Assembly** on 17 June, claiming sole legitimacy to represent the nation. Locked out of their hall on 20 June, deputies swore the **Tennis Court Oath** not to disperse until a constitution was established. Under pressure from popular demonstrations and mutinies in the French Guards, Louis XVI ordered the clergy and nobility to join the Assembly on 27 June, effectively surrendering the *Ancien Régime*’s corporate structure.

### Constitutional Monarchy and the Reconstruction of France (July 1789 – September 1792)

**Abolition of Feudalism and the Declaration of Rights.** The dismissal of popular minister Necker on 11 July triggered the Storming of the Bastille (14 July), saving the Assembly from dissolution. In early August, the **August Decrees** abolished feudal dues (affecting 25% of farmland), tithes, venal office, and provincial privileges. The **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen** (26 August) defined liberty, property, security, and resistance to oppression as natural rights, establishing popular sovereignty and equality before the law.

**The Church and the State.** The Assembly nationalized church lands (≈10% of France) to back a new paper currency, *assignats*. The **Civil Constitution of the Clergy** (July 1790) made priests and bishops elected state employees, requiring an oath of loyalty. Only 24% complied, creating a schism between "juring" and "refractory" clergy that alienated devout regions (Vendée, Brittany) and fueled counter-revolution.

**Political Fractures.** The 1791 Constitution created a unicameral Legislative Assembly with a suspensive royal veto. Suffrage was limited to "active citizens" (males over 25 paying taxes equal to three days' labour), disenfranchising the poor. The **Le Chapelier Law** (June 1791) banned guilds and worker associations. Political clubs proliferated: the **Jacobins** (radical, centralised) and **Feuillants** (constitutional monarchists) emerged from the same society. The king’s attempted flight to Varennes (June 1791) shattered trust in the monarchy; the subsequent Champ de Mars Massacre (July 1791), where the National Guard fired on republican petitioners, polarized the capital.

**War and the Fall of the Monarchy.** In April 1792, the Legislative Assembly declared war on Austria, urged by Girondins (Brissotins) seeking to spread revolution and by royalists hoping to restore authority. Military defeats and the Brunswick Manifesto threatening Paris triggered the **Insurrection of 10 August 1792**: *fédérés* (volunteers) and the Paris National Guard stormed the Tuileries. The Assembly suspended the king and called a National Convention elected by universal male suffrage.

### The First Republic and the Reign of Terror (September 1792 – July 1794)

**Proclamation and Regicide.** The Convention abolished the monarchy on 22 September 1792 (Year One). The French victory at Valmy (20 September) secured the Republic. Louis XVI was tried and executed on 21 January 1793, prompting Britain, the Dutch Republic, Spain, and others to join the **War of the First Coalition**.

**The Committee of Public Safety.** Facing invasion, federalist revolts (Lyon, Marseille, Bordeaux), and the Vendée civil war, the Convention created the **Committee of Public Safety** (April 1793). After the Girondins were expelled (June 1793), the Committee—dominated by Robespierre, Saint-Just, and Couthon—became the de facto executive. The **Law of Suspects** (Sept 1793) and **Law of 22 Prairial** (June 1794) suspended due process. Approximately 16,600 death sentences were issued by the Revolutionary Tribunal; thousands more died in prison or in summary repression (notably the *noyades* at Nantes). Price controls (*Maximum*) and *assignat* hyperinflation wrecked the economy.

**The Terror’s Logic and Collapse.** The Terror targeted internal enemies (Hébertists, Dantonists) as well as counter-revolutionaries. Robespierre’s attempt to impose a civic religion (Cult of the Supreme Being) and his isolation from the Committee led to his overthrow on **9 Thermidor Year II (27 July 1794)**. He and 19 allies were executed the next day.

### The Thermidorian Reaction and the Directory (1794–1799)

**Constitution of Year III.** The Thermidorians drafted a conservative constitution (ratified August 1795) establishing a **bicameral legislature** (Council of 500, Council of Ancients) and a five-man **Directory** executive. The "law of two-thirds" retained 600 Conventionnels to ensure stability. Suffrage was restricted by property qualifications.

**Instability and Reliance on the Army.** The Directory faced royalist uprisings (Vendémiaire 1795, crushed by Napoleon), Jacobin resurgence (1798), and the **Conspiracy of the Equals** (Babeuf, 1796) demanding the 1793 Constitution. Chronic inflation persisted until the *assignat* was withdrawn in 1796. The regime survived by annulling elections (Fructidor 1797, Floréal 1798) using the army. Generals—Napoleon, Joubert, Hoche—became political arbiters. The **War of the Second Coalition** (1798) and directory corruption eroded legitimacy.

**The Coup of 18 Brumaire.** Director Sieyès, seeking a "sword" to restructure the regime, allied with Napoleon (returned from Egypt) and his brother Lucien (President of the 500). On 9 November 1799, troops dispersed the Councils; the Directory was replaced by the **Consulate** (Napoleon, Sieyès, Ducos), ending the revolutionary period.

### The French Revolutionary Wars (1792–1802)

The Revolution transformed warfare. The *levée en masse* (August 1793) mobilized 750,000–800,000 men by 1794, dwarfing coalition armies. War aims shifted from dynastic disputes to ideological expansion: "natural frontiers" (Rhine, Alps, Pyrenees) and sister republics (Batavian, Cisalpine, Helvetic). French armies lived off occupied territories, making generals politically powerful. The Directory’s inability to demobilize without economic collapse perpetuated conflict. By 1797, France controlled the Left Bank of the Rhine and northern Italy; the Treaty of Campo Formio (1797) confirmed Austrian recognition.

### Slavery and the Colonies

Saint-Domingue (Haiti) held 500,000 of the empire’s 700,000 slaves. The 1789 Declaration excluded colonies under planter pressure. In 1791, a massive slave revolt erupted. Commissioners Sonthonax and Polverel abolished slavery in Saint-Domingue (1793) to secure Black allegiance against British/Spanish invasion. The Convention ratified **universal abolition on 4 February 1794**, granting citizenship to all colonial residents. Toussaint Louverture emerged as leader in Saint-Domingue. However, the 1793 Constitution was suspended, and post-1794 "emancipation" often compelled former slaves to remain on plantations for wages. Napoleon reinstated slavery in 1802 (outside source context), but the 1794 decree was the first general abolition by a major power.

### Women and the Revolution

Women participated actively: the October March on Versailles, the Society of Revolutionary Republican Women (price controls, cockade laws), and political writing (Olympe de Gouges’ *Declaration of the Rights of Woman*, 1791). The 1791 and 1793 constitutions denied them political rights. In October 1793, the Jacobin government banned all women’s clubs, arguing their place was private. Women also led counter-revolutionary resistance to dechristianization in rural areas, defending traditional Catholicism.

### Economic Policies

The Revolution abolished feudal dues, tithes, tax farming, and guilds. Church and émigré lands were sold as *biens nationaux* to finance the state via *assignats*. However, war, loss of colonial trade (British blockade), and excessive *assignat* issuance caused hyperinflation (3,500% by 1795). Price controls (the *Maximum*) created black markets. The *assignat* was replaced by the gold *Franc germinal* in 1803. Land redistribution created a nation of small peasant proprietors, though economic historians debate whether fragmentation initially hurt or helped productivity.

### Impact and Legacy

**France.** The Revolution permanently ended feudalism, noble privilege, and venal office. It established equality before the law, secularized the state (civil marriage, divorce, inheritance reform), and created a nationalist citizenry. The *Code Napoléon* (1804) codified these gains. Despite Bourbon (1815) and Vichy (1940) attempts to reverse republicanism, the privileges of 1789 were never restored.

**Europe.** French armies exported the Revolution: abolition of serfdom, guilds, and Jewish ghettos; introduction of the Napoleonic Code; secularization of church lands. Studies show occupied territories experienced faster urbanization and growth post-1850. The Revolution sparked the "Revolution Controversy" in Britain (Burke vs. Paine/Wollstonecraft), inspired the United Irishmen (1798), and catalyzed German nationalism.

**Historiography.** Early accounts were ideological (Burke, Paine). 19th-century strands: reactionary, democratic-republican (Michelet), liberal (Guizot, Tocqueville). Mid-20th century saw Marxist dominance (Lefebvre, Soboul: class struggle, *sans-culottes* as proto-proletariat). Revisionists (Cobban, Furet) argued it was a political conflict won by conservative property owners, not a bourgeois revolution. Since the 1990s, Marxist models have faded; cultural, regional, and transnational approaches prevail.

## Terms

- ****Ancien Régime**** — The pre-1789 French political and social system characterized by absolute monarchy, feudal privileges, and a society divided into three estates (clergy, nobility, commoners).
- ****Estates-General**** — A representative assembly of the three estates, last convened in 1614; its 1789 meeting triggered the Revolution.
- ****National Assembly**** — The revolutionary body formed by the Third Estate on 17 June 1789, claiming sole authority to represent the nation and write a constitution.
- ****Assignats**** — Paper currency issued 1789–1796, backed by confiscated church lands; hyperinflation destroyed their value.
- ****Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790)**** — Law subordinating the Catholic Church to the state, requiring clergy to swear an oath of loyalty; caused a major schism.
- ****Sans-culottes**** — Literally "without knee-breeches"; urban workers and artisans who wore trousers instead of aristocratic breeches, forming the radical popular base in Paris.
- ****Girondins / Brissotins**** — Loose faction of deputies (led by Brissot) favoring war and federalism; purged from the Convention in June 1793.
- ****Montagnards / The Mountain**** — Radical deputies (Robespierre, Danton, Marat) sitting on high benches in the Convention; dominated the Committee of Public Safety.
- ****Committee of Public Safety**** — Twelve-man executive committee created April 1793; under Robespierre became the de facto government during the Terror.
- ****Reign of Terror (Sept 1793 – July 1794)**** — Period of state-sanctioned violence: ~16,600 executed by Revolutionary Tribunal, thousands more killed summarily, to suppress internal and external enemies.
- ****Thermidorian Reaction**** — The coup of 9 Thermidor Year II (27 July 1794) overthrowing Robespierre, ending the Terror, and shifting policy toward conservatism.
- ****Directory (1795–1799)**** — Five-man executive established by the Constitution of Year III; relied on military to survive coups and war.
- ****Coup of 18 Brumaire (9 Nov 1799)**** — Napoleon Bonaparte’s overthrow of the Directory, establishing the Consulate and ending the Revolution.
- ****Levée en masse (Aug 1793)**** — Universal conscription decree mobilizing all able-bodied men; created massive citizen armies that transformed European warfare.
- ****Biens nationaux**** — "National goods"—confiscated church and émigré lands sold to finance the Revolution; created a class of peasant landowners.

## Debates and open questions

*   **Ideological vs. Circumstantial Origins:** Jonathan Israel argues the "radical Enlightenment" drove the Revolution; Alfred Cobban counters that revolutionaries improvised practical solutions to immediate crises, not pre-conceived theories.
*   **Class Character:** Marxist historians (Lefebvre, Soboul) viewed it as a bourgeois revolution against feudalism. Revisionists (Cobban, Furet) argue the bourgeoisie was not a coherent class and the outcome entrenched conservative property owners, retarding capitalism.
*   **Necessity of the Terror:** Was the Terror an inevitable product of revolutionary ideology and utopian goals (Furet), or a contingent response to war, invasion, and economic collapse (middle position)?
*   **Economic Impact of Land Redistribution:** Did the fragmentation of estates into small peasant farms hurt early 19th-century productivity (negative view) or eventually facilitate human capital investment and growth (positive view)?
*   **Women’s Agency:** Historians debate whether the Revolution offered women a genuine political opening subsequently crushed, or whether their exclusion was structural from the start (e.g., *active/passive* citizen distinction).
*   **Colonial Paradox:** The 1794 abolition of slavery was a radical first, yet the Revolution maintained coercive labor regimes in the colonies and Napoleon reinstated slavery in 1802—how to weigh the universalist decree against the practice?
*   **End Date:** While 9 November 1799 (Brumaire) is the conventional endpoint, some argue the Revolution continued until 1815 (Waterloo) or 1871 (Paris Commune), or that its institutional legacy persists in the Fifth Republic.
