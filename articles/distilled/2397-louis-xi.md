# Louis XI

## Overview
Louis XI (1423–1483), called "the Prudent," was King of France from 1461 to 1483. He is a pivotal figure in French history for transforming a weak monarchy into a centralized state by outmaneuvering powerful feudal lords, especially the Dukes of Burgundy, and laying the administrative foundations for the modern French government. His reign marked the effective end of the Hundred Years' War and the beginning of France's emergence as a dominant European power.

## Timeline
- **3 July 1423** — Born in Bourges to Charles VII and Marie of Anjou.
- **1440** — Participates in the failed Praguerie revolt against his father.
- **1446** — Banished by Charles VII; establishes his own court in Dauphiné.
- **1451** — Marries Charlotte of Savoy without his father's consent.
- **1456** — Flees to Burgundy after Charles VII sends an army to Dauphiné.
- **22 July 1461** — Becomes King of France upon the death of Charles VII.
- **1464** — Establishes a royal postal relay system across France.
- **1467** — Charles the Bold becomes Duke of Burgundy, Louis's chief rival.
- **1475** — Signs the Treaty of Picquigny with Edward IV of England, ending the Hundred Years' War.
- **5 January 1477** — Death of Charles the Bold at the Battle of Nancy; Louis seizes Burgundian territories.
- **1482** — Treaty of Arras settles the Burgundian inheritance with Maximilian I.
- **30 August 1483** — Dies at the Château de Plessis-lez-Tours, succeeded by his son Charles VIII.

## Body

### Early Life and Rebellion
Louis was born during the Hundred Years' War, when English forces held northern France. He grew up in a weakened kingdom and despised his father, Charles VII, as weak. In 1440, at age 16, he joined the Praguerie, a short-lived revolt to depose Charles VII. The king forgave the rebels but banished Louis in 1446 due to his ceaseless intrigues. Louis ruled his province of Dauphiné independently and, in 1451, married Charlotte of Savoy against his father's will, a union that began French involvement in Italian affairs. In 1456, he fled to Burgundy, finding refuge with Duke Philip the Good, Charles VII's greatest enemy.

### Ascension and Domestic Reform
Louis became king in 1461. He pursued his father's goal of limiting the power of feudal dukes and barons with greater success. He reformed the tax system, suppressed rebellious vassals, and appointed talented men of humble origin to government, favoring the rising merchant class. A key innovation was the 1464 creation of a royal postal relay system, which gave him rapid communication across the realm and earned him the nickname "the Universal Spider." He traveled constantly to oversee local governance and promote trade. He also abolished the Pragmatic Sanction of 1438, reasserting papal authority over the French church.

### Feud with Burgundy
Louis's reign was dominated by conflict with the Duchy of Burgundy. He initially bought territories from Philip the Good, angering the heir, Charles the Bold. After a period of war and a humiliating forced treaty at Péronne in 1468, Louis worked systematically to destroy Burgundian power. He allied with Charles's enemies, such as the citizens of Liège. After Charles the Bold was killed at the Battle of Nancy in 1477, Louis seized key Burgundian territories, including Burgundy itself and Picardy. The subsequent conflict with Charles's heir, Mary of Burgundy, and her husband Maximilian I, was settled by the 1482 Treaty of Arras, which returned the original Burgundian duchy to France.

### Foreign Policy and Legacy
Louis skillfully isolated Burgundy by signing the Treaty of Picquigny with England in 1475, formally ending the Hundred Years' War. After Burgundy's fall, he turned his attention to Italy, leveraging his marriage alliance with Savoy and forging new relationships with Milan, Naples, and Venice. He died in 1483, having united France and established a strong, centralized monarchy. His administrative reforms, particularly in communication and governance, helped transition France from the Middle Ages to the early modern period.

## Terms
- **Praguerie**: A 1440 revolt by French nobles, including Louis, against King Charles VII.
- **Dauphiné**: A province in southeastern France, traditionally held by the heir to the French throne.
- **Pragmatic Sanction**: A 1438 decree by Charles VII that established a French Gallican Church with limited papal control; abolished by Louis XI.
- **League of the Public Weal**: A 1465 rebellion of French nobles against Louis XI, led by his brother.
- **Treaty of Picquigny**: A 1475 agreement between Louis XI and Edward IV of England that ended English claims in France and the Hundred Years' War.
- **Order of St. Michael**: A chivalric order founded by Louis XI in 1469 to enhance royal prestige.
- **Treaty of Arras**: A 1482 agreement between Louis XI and Maximilian I that settled the Burgundian inheritance.
- **Bourgeoisie**: The non-noble, merchant and professional class that Louis XI favored in his administrative reforms.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact nature of Louis's childhood marriage to Margaret of Scotland is debated; while it was politically arranged and unhappy, the personal feelings of the couple are unknown.
- Louis's reputation is complex: he is praised as a "civil reformer" and unifier of France but also criticized for his secretive, treacherous methods. Niccolò Machiavelli later condemned his reliance on Swiss mercenaries over a national infantry.
- The long-term impact of his Italian marriage alliance is debated; while it initiated French involvement, the major Italian Wars began under his son, Charles VIII.
