# J. M. W. Turner

## Overview
Joseph Mallord William Turner (1775–1851) was an English Romantic painter who transformed landscape painting from a lowly genre into a vehicle for the sublime, becoming the youngest Royal Academician in England at age 27. A child prodigy from a working-class Covent Garden background, he achieved early commercial success by mastering and imitating Old Masters, but his mature style—characterized by luminous, atmospheric washes and a near-abstract dissolution of form—alienated conservative critics who dismissed his work as "blots" or signs of failing eyesight. His radical experiments with light, colour, and the elemental forces of nature, particularly in oil paintings that adopted watercolour transparency, directly anticipated French Impressionism and modern abstraction. Upon his death, he bequeathed to the nation about 300 oil paintings, 30,000 watercolours and drawings, and 280 sketchbooks, many of which are displayed at London's Tate Britain and National Gallery museums.

## Body

### Childhood and Early Training
Turner was born to a barber-wigmaker father and a mother from a butcher family who suffered severe mental illness, eventually dying in Bethlem Hospital ("Bedlam") in 1804. His father recognized his talent early, displaying and selling the boy's drawings from the shop window. Between ages 10 and 14, Turner was sent to live with relatives in Brentford, Margate, and Sunningwell. In Margate and Sunningwell, he produced sustained series of drawings and a surviving sketchbook, establishing the *modus operandi* of his career: pencil sketches and watercolours made on location (*en plein air*) serving as the foundation for finished studio works. He briefly worked for architects Thomas Hardwick, James Wyatt, and Joseph Bonomi the Elder, producing architectural studies, before Hardwick advised him to focus on painting. In 1789, he entered the Royal Academy Schools and studied under topographical draughtsman Thomas Malton, learning to copy and colour outline prints—a thriving commercial trade that funded his education.

### Academic Ascent and Early Patronage
Turner progressed rapidly at the Academy. His watercolour *A View of the Archbishop's Palace, Lambeth* was exhibited in 1790. Early works focused on architecture and topography, influenced by travels to Wales. In 1793, he showed the oil *The Rising Squall, Hot Wells*, which foreshadowed his later climatic effects. At age 21, his first oil, *Fishermen at Sea*, a dramatic moonlit seascape, was praised as a summary of 18th-century marine art. *Dutch Boats in a Gale* (1801) "took the Academy by storm," earning him election as a full Royal Academician at 27, the youngest artist in the institution's history to achieve the highest academic recognition in England. Despite this, his lowly social origins drew snobbery from patrician Academicians like Sir George Beaumont, who publicly called him a "little Reptile" and coined the pejorative "white painter."

Commercially astute, Turner imitated Old Masters (Claude Lorrain, Canaletto, Rembrandt, Titian, Poussin, Rubens, Watteau) to satisfy wealthy collectors, following Joshua Reynolds's doctrine. This "chameleon tendency" secured commissions from patrons like William Beckford (150 guineas for *The Fifth Plague of Egypt*, 1799) and the Duke of Bridgewater (250 guineas for *Dutch Boats in a Gale*, 1801). Crucial early support came from Dr. Thomas Monro, an alienist and amateur artist, at whose house Turner copied works by John Robert Cozens, learning that watercolour could prioritize mood over topographic information. Walter Ramsden Fawkes of Farnley Hall (Yorkshire) and George Wyndham, 3rd Earl of Egremont at Petworth House (West Sussex) became lifelong patrons; Petworth still holds 20 Turners.

### The *Liber Studiorum* and Printmaking
From 1806 to 1819, Turner devoted 12 years to the *Liber Studiorum* (Book of Studies), a set of 71 mezzotint prints (plus etchings) intended to elevate landscape painting's status. Loosely modelled on Claude Lorrain's *Liber Veritatis*, Turner categorized landscape into six types: Marine, Mountainous, Pastoral, Historical, Architectural, and Elevated or Epic Pastoral. The project was a critical success and a major factor in legitimizing landscape as high art. Turner mastered multiple printmaking techniques, but favoured mezzotint for its tonal richness.

### European Travel and the Italian Turning Point
The 1802 Peace of Amiens allowed Turner's first continental trip: Paris (studying the Louvre), Switzerland, and later, extensive Italian tours. His 1819 Grand Tour—Turin, Milan, Como, Venice, Rome, Naples—was pivotal. Venice, with its unique light and water, became his most enduring subject, inspiring masterpieces like *Venice, from the Porch of Madonna della Salute* (1835) and *The Sun of Venice Going to Sea* (1843). Alpine scenery, particularly the 1816 "Year Without a Summer" (caused by Mount Tambora's eruption), provided spectacular atmospheric effects that fed his fascination with nature's violent power.

### The Thames and *En Plein Air* Practice
The River Thames was Turner's constant subject and geographic anchor. Born yards from it, he lived successively in Isleworth, Hammersmith (where he fitted a boat as a floating studio), Twickenham (Sandycombe Lodge), and finally Chelsea. In 1805, he systematically sketched the river from Oxford to the Estuary. Working outdoors, he developed "painterly abbreviations"—gestural brushstrokes that distilled moving water and light into their essence—techniques impossible in a studio. This direct observation fueled his revolutionary handling of paint.

### Mature Style: Light, Atmosphere, and the Vortex
Turner was a prolific artist, producing about 550 oil paintings and over 2,000 watercolours during his lifetime. Financial independence allowed him to innovate. His mature style fused watercolour transparency with oil paint, creating "chromatic palette and broadly applied atmospheric washes." He used a vortex compositional structure (e.g., *Snow Storm: Hannibal and his Army Crossing the Alps*, 1812) to convey nature's destructive energy. In works like *The Slave Ship* (1840) and *Rain, Steam and Speed* (1844), objects dissolve into shimmering colour and light. Critics were baffled: John Eagles called the 1840 exhibits "absurd extravagances"; Beaumont dismissed them as "blots"; others speculated he was losing his sight. John Ruskin, however, hailed him as the artist who could "stirringly and truthfully measure the moods of Nature." Monet and Pissarro, seeing these works in London in 1870, recognized him as a direct precursor to Impressionism.

### Materials and Technical Recklessness
Turner experimented obsessively with unstable paint mixtures and fugitive organic pigments like carmine, ignoring experts' advice to use durable mineral colours. He prioritized immediate visual brilliance over longevity. Ruskin lamented the rapid fading; by 1930, significant deterioration was documented in both oils and watercolours.

### Later Life, Relationships, and Death
Turner never married. His father lived with him for 30 years as studio assistant; his 1829 death triggered deep depression. He had two daughters, Evelina and Georgiana, with housekeeper Sarah Danby. From 1846, he lived secretly in Chelsea as "Mr. Booth" or "Admiral Booth" with widow Sophia Caroline Booth. He formed a profound late friendship with the young clergyman-artist Edward Thomas Daniell, who provided spiritual comfort; Daniell's early death in 1842 devastated Turner. Turner died at Sophia Booth's house in 1851 and was buried in St Paul's Cathedral.

### The Turner Bequest and Contested Legacy
Turner bequeathed ~300 oils, 30,000 works on paper, 280 sketchbooks, and a cash endowment for "decayed artists" to the nation. His will was contested; cousins inherited the cash, the Royal Academy received funds for the Turner Medal, and the art went to the National Gallery. Parliament later allowed dispersal to provincial museums (1873), contravening his wish for a dedicated gallery. The bulk of the Bequest moved to the Duveen Wing (Tate Britain) in 1910 and the Clore Gallery in 1987, though key works remain at the National Gallery. Collector Henry Vaughan bequeathed a comprehensive watercolour collection to public galleries in 1899, stipulating January-only display to prevent fading—a conservation pioneer.

## Terms
- ****Royal Academician (RA)**** — The highest rank of membership in the Royal Academy of Arts, London; Turner was elected at 27, the youngest ever in England.
- *****Liber Studiorum***** — Turner's published series of 71 mezzotint/etching prints (1806–1819) categorizing landscape into six genres, modelled on Claude Lorrain's *Liber Veritatis*.
- *****En plein air***** — French for "in the open air"; the practice of painting outdoors directly from nature, foundational to Turner's working method.
- ****Mezzotint**** — An intaglio printmaking technique allowing rich tonal gradations (dark to light) without line work; Turner's preferred print medium.
- ****The Sublime**** — An aesthetic category (theorized by Burke) denoting overwhelming, terrifying natural power (storms, avalanches, vastness); central to Turner's mature vision.
- ****Vortex composition**** — A swirling, centrifugal arrangement of forms (clouds, water, figures) used by Turner to visualize chaotic energy and the destructive force of nature.
- ****Fugitive pigment**** — A colourant that fades or changes chemically when exposed to light/air (e.g., carmine); Turner used these for brilliance despite impermanence.
- ****Grand Tour**** — The traditional educational journey through Europe (especially Italy) undertaken by British aristocrats and artists; Turner's 1819 tour was transformative.
- ****Turner Bequest**** — The ~300 oils, 30,000 works on paper, and 280 sketchbooks left to the British nation in 1851, now housed primarily at Tate Britain and the National Gallery.
- ****Impressionism precursor**** — Turner's late dissolution of form into light and colour anticipated the French Impressionists (Monet, Pissarro), who studied his works in London in 1870.

## Debates and open questions
- **Bequest dispersal vs. unity:** Turner mandated a single dedicated gallery; Parliament and the Tate split the collection (National Gallery vs. Tate Britain) and lend works internationally, violating his explicit conditions.
- **Authenticity of late works:** The extreme abstraction of his final oils (e.g., *Norham Castle, Sunrise* series) raises questions: are they finished paintings, unfinished sketches, or deliberate experiments? The Tate's 1966 "Turner: The Late Works" exhibition re-evaluated them as finished masterpieces.
- **Paternity of Sarah Danby's daughters:** Turner is "believed to be the father" of Evelina and Georgiana, but no definitive documentary proof exists; the relationship was concealed during his lifetime.
- **Cause of stylistic radicalism:** Critics then and now debate whether his late style stemmed from failing eyesight (cataracts), deliberate theoretical innovation, or the influence of contemporary science (Goethe's colour theory, volcanic sunsets).
- **Attribution of *Liber Studiorum* plates:** Distinguishing Turner's own etching/mezzotint work from that of his hired engravers (e.g., Charles Turner, William Say) remains a technical connoisseurship challenge.
- **Conservation vs. access:** Henry Vaughan's 1899 stipulation (display only in January) set an early conservation standard; modern museums balance this against public demand for year-round viewing of light-sensitive watercolours.
