# An Lushan rebellion

## Overview
The An Lushan Rebellion (755–763) was a devastating civil war that marked the turning point of the Tang dynasty (618–907). It began as a commandery rebellion led by the general An Lushan, who sought to overthrow the Tang and establish his own Yan dynasty. The conflict caused massive depopulation, permanently weakened central imperial authority, and led to the rise of autonomous regional warlords, setting the stage for the dynasty's eventual collapse.

## Timeline
- **16 Dec 755** — An Lushan mobilizes his army from Fanyang, beginning the rebellion.
- **18 Jan 756** — Yan forces capture the Eastern Capital, Luoyang.
- **5 Feb 756** — An Lushan declares himself Emperor of the new Yan dynasty.
- **7 Jul 756** — Tang forces are defeated at Tong Pass, opening the road to Chang'an.
- **Jul 756** — Yan forces capture the imperial capital, Chang'an.
- **12 Aug 756** — Emperor Xuanzong abdicates; Crown Prince Li Heng becomes Emperor Suzong.
- **29 Jan 757** — An Lushan is assassinated by his son, An Qingxu.
- **Winter 757** — Tang and Uyghur allies recapture Chang'an and Luoyang.
- **10 Apr 759** — Shi Siming kills An Qingxu and usurps leadership of the Yan dynasty.
- **18 Apr 761** — Shi Siming is killed by his son, Shi Chaoyi.
- **Winter 762** — Tang forces retake Luoyang for the second time.
- **17 Feb 763** — Shi Chaoyi commits suicide, ending the rebellion.

## Body

### Background and Outbreak
The rebellion occurred during a period of Eurasian political turmoil. An Lushan, a general of Sogdian-Turkic origin, had risen to become a favorite of Emperor Xuanzong and was appointed *jiedushi* (military governor) of three northern commanderies, giving him control of a massive army. He exploited court discontent, natural disasters, and the absence of strong palace troops. On 16 December 755, he marched south from Fanyang, capturing Luoyang in early 756 and declaring the Yan dynasty.

### Course of the War
The rebellion spanned the reigns of three Tang emperors: Xuanzong, Suzong, and Daizong. After capturing Luoyang, Yan forces were halted at the Battle of Yongqiu, preventing their advance south. However, through court intrigue, the Tang generals defending the critical Tong Pass were removed, and their replacement was defeated, allowing Yan to capture Chang'an in July 756. Emperor Xuanzong fled to Sichuan, during which the Mawei Incident led to the forced death of his consort Yang Guifei. The new emperor, Suzong, rallied Tang forces with crucial aid from Uyghur mercenaries and Arab allies. The war featured brutal sieges, like that of Suiyang, where Tang defenders practiced cannibalism to hold out. The Yan leadership was unstable; An Lushan was killed by his son in 757, who was then killed by his ally Shi Siming in 759. Shi Siming was in turn killed by his own son, Shi Chaoyi, in 761. The rebellion finally ended with Shi Chaoyi's suicide in 763.

### Aftermath and Consequences
The rebellion caused catastrophic depopulation, with census figures showing a drop from over 52 million people in 755 to under 17 million in 764, though this reflects both mortality and a breakdown in census systems. To secure peace, the Tang court pardoned and retained surrendered Yan generals as autonomous *jiedushi*, creating the semi-independent *fanzhen* (regional military commissions) that plagued the dynasty's remainder. The Tang permanently lost control of the Western Regions to the Tibetan Empire. The economic center of China shifted south to the Jiangnan region, which had been spared the worst destruction. The dynasty's weakened state led to later revolts, such as the Four Garrisons Rebellion (781), and ultimately to the Huang Chao Rebellion (874–884), which destroyed its power.

## Terms
- **Jiedushi**: A regional military governor position that, after the rebellion, often held autonomous, hereditary power.
- **Fanzhen**: Autonomous regional military commissions or provinces that emerged after the rebellion, reducing central control.
- **Yan dynasty**: The short-lived rebel state (755–763) established by An Lushan and his successors.
- **Huihe (Uyghur)**: A Turkic people whose Khaganate provided crucial military aid to the Tang in exchange for trade concessions and marriage alliances.
- **Sogdian**: An Iranian people from Central Asia, prominent in Silk Road trade; many, including An Lushan, were active in Tang China.
- **Mawei Incident**: The mutiny of imperial troops at Mawei Inn in 756, which forced Emperor Xuanzong to order the execution of his consort Yang Guifei.
- **Siege of Suiyang**: A prolonged (Jan–Oct 757) and brutal siege where Tang defenders held out against Yan forces, preventing their advance south.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Death Toll**: The census drop of ~36 million is debated. Historians argue it reflects not only war casualties but also a breakdown in census accuracy, the removal of untaxed classes, and the loss of northern provinces from the tax rolls. Revised estimates of direct war deaths range from 13 million downward.
- **Role of Foreign Allies**: The extent to which Uyghur and Arab intervention was decisive versus merely helpful is a point of analysis. Their aid came at the cost of significant economic concessions and political influence.
- **Cause of Tang Decline**: While the rebellion is the clear catalyst, scholars debate whether the subsequent *fanzhen* system was an inevitable outcome or a specific policy failure in how the court managed surrendered generals.
