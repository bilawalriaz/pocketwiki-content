# Syracuse, Sicily

## Overview
Syracuse is a historic city on Sicily's southeastern coast, founded by Corinthians in 733 BC. It rose to become one of the largest and most powerful cities of the classical world, rivaling Athens, and was the birthplace of Archimedes. Its strategic importance led to successive control by Romans, Byzantines, Arabs, Normans, and Spanish, each leaving architectural and cultural layers. Today, it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, recognized for its exceptional testimony to three millennia of Mediterranean history.

## Timeline
- **c. 733 BC** — Founded by Corinthians under Archias on the island of Ortygia.
- **5th–4th c. BC** — Golden age under tyrants like Dionysius I and Hiero II; rival of Carthage and Athens.
- **212 BC** — Conquered by Rome after a siege led by Archimedes.
- **663–668** — Becomes capital of the Byzantine Empire under Constans II.
- **878** — Falls to Arab siege, ending its role as Sicily's capital.
- **1085** — Recaptured by Normans under Robert Guiscard.
- **1542 & 1693** — Devastated by major earthquakes; the 1693 event leads to Baroque rebuilding.
- **3 Sep 1943** — Armistice of Cassibile, ending hostilities between Italy and Allies, signed nearby.
- **2005** — Designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site with the Necropolis of Pantalica.

## Body

### Ancient Foundation and Classical Power
Syracuse was founded in 733 BC by Greek colonists from Corinth. It rapidly expanded, subjugating local Sicel populations and becoming a major power in Magna Graecia. The city was ruled by a series of powerful tyrants, including Gelon, Dionysius I, and Hiero II, who extended Syracusan influence across Sicily and the Mediterranean. Syracuse was a fierce rival of the Phoenician city of Carthage, leading to the Greco-Punic Wars. It successfully repelled a major Athenian invasion during the Peloponnesian War. The city was a cultural hub, hosting figures like Plato, and was the birthplace of the mathematician Archimedes, who died during the Roman conquest in 212 BC.

### Medieval and Early Modern Transformations
After the Roman conquest, Syracuse remained the chief city of Sicily. It became the capital of the Byzantine Empire from 663 to 668 under Constans II. The Arab siege and capture in 878 led to a long period of decline, with Palermo replacing it as the island's capital. The Normans captured the city in 1085, establishing it as a county. Under Spanish rule from the 16th century, Syracuse was heavily fortified against Ottoman threats. The catastrophic 1693 earthquake destroyed much of the city, leading to its reconstruction in the distinctive Sicilian Baroque style.

### Modern Era and World War II
Syracuse experienced political turmoil during the Napoleonic era, with British forces garrisoning the city. It joined the movement for Italian unification, surrendering to Garibaldian forces in 1860. In World War II, the city was heavily bombed and was one of the first in Sicily liberated by the Allies in July 1943. The secret Armistice of Cassibile, which led to Italy's surrender, was signed nearby on 3 September 1943. The post-war period saw the development of a major petrochemical complex in the area, which brought economic activity but also environmental concerns.

### Cultural and Architectural Legacy
Syracuse's identity is deeply tied to its layered history. Its ancient Greek heritage includes the Neapolis Archaeological Park, with its theatre and quarries. The island of Ortygia, the original city core, features the Cathedral (built over the Temple of Athena) and the Fountain of Arethusa. The city's architecture is a palimpsest, from medieval structures to the Baroque palaces and churches rebuilt after 1693. It is renowned for its cuisine, part of the Mediterranean diet, and traditions like the Feast of Saint Lucy. The city remains a center for academic and cultural institutions, including the Siracusa International Institute for Criminal Justice and Human Rights.

## Terms
*   **Magna Graecia:** The name for the coastal areas of Southern Italy that were extensively settled by Greek colonists.
*   **Tyrant:** In the Greek context, a sole ruler who seized power unconstitutionally, not necessarily a cruel one.
*   **Polis:** The Greek term for a city-state, the fundamental political unit of ancient Greece.
*   **Latomie:** Ancient stone quarries, later used as prisons; the most famous in Syracuse is the "Ear of Dionysius."
*   **Baroque:** An artistic and architectural style of the 17th and 18th centuries characterized by grandeur, drama, and movement.
*   **Autocephalous:** A church that is self-governing and does not recognize any higher ecclesiastical authority.
*   **Armistice of Cassibile:** The secret agreement signed on 3 September 1943, which ended hostilities between the Kingdom of Italy and the Allied forces.
*   **Petrochemical complex:** An industrial facility that processes petroleum and natural gas into chemical products.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Etymology of Syracuse:** The origin of the city's name is uncertain, with hypotheses deriving it from Siculian, Proto-Indo-European, or Semitic languages.
*   **Population of Ancient Syracuse:** Historians debate the exact population at its peak, with estimates ranging widely, though it was undoubtedly one of the largest cities in the classical world.
*   **Role of the Syracusan Church:** The city's early church was autocephalous, leading to historical disputes between the Latin (Papal) and Byzantine (Patriarchate of Constantinople) ecclesiastical authorities over its jurisdiction.
