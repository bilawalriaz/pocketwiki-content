# Chronology

## Overview
Chronology is the science of arranging events in their order of occurrence in time, essential for establishing the sequence of historical, geological, and archaeological events. It relies on methods like radiocarbon dating and dendrochronology to determine absolute ages, and on synchronism to correlate events across different cultures or regions. This discipline underpins our understanding of the past by providing a temporal framework for all historical and scientific inquiry.

## Body

### Methods and Related Fields
Chronology is the science of locating historical events in time. It relies on **chronometry** (timekeeping) and **historiography** (the study of historical methods). Key scientific dating methods include **radiocarbon dating**, which estimates the age of organic material by measuring carbon-14, and **dendrochronology**, which dates trees by analyzing growth rings. Dendrochronology is used to calibrate radiocarbon dating curves.

### Calendars and Eras
A **calendar** is a system of organizing days, while an **era** is a coherent system of numbered years. The most widespread is the Christian era, founded by Dionysius Exiguus (c. 500) and later extended by Bede to include BC years. The **epoch** is the starting point of an era. The *Ab Urbe condita* era (from the founding of Rome, traditionally 753 BC) was used by some Roman historians but was not widely adopted until later. The **astronomical era**, which includes a leap year zero, was developed in the 18th century to simplify calculations.

### Chronology in Prehistory
Without written records, archaeologists in the late 19th century developed relative chronologies using pottery styles. William Flinders Petrie pioneered **seriation** (sequence dating) in Egyptology by grouping contemporary artifacts. The study involves **classification** (creating descriptive categories) and **typology** (analyzing changes to create sequences). After the mid-20th century, laboratory techniques constantly refined these chronologies, with multiple dating methods used to cross-check results for reliability.

### Synchronism
The fundamental problem of chronology is **synchronism**: synchronizing events to relate them to a common time scale. Eusebius's *Chronicon* (325 AD) synchronized events from nine kingdoms using parallel columns, dated from *Anno Mundi* (from the creation of the world, computed as 5199 BC). Later chronographers like George Syncellus and Joseph Justus Scaliger elaborated on this work. Scaliger reconstructed the lost *Chronicon* and invented the **Julian Day**, a unified time scale for historians and astronomers. Events can also be synchronized through archaeological finds or astronomical events, like the Eclipse of Thales.

## Terms
- **Chronology**: The science of arranging events in their order of occurrence in time.
- **Chronometry**: The science of measuring time accurately.
- **Historiography**: The study of historical methods and the writing of history.
- **Radiocarbon dating**: A method for estimating the age of formerly living things by measuring the proportion of carbon-14.
- **Dendrochronology**: The science of dating trees by analyzing growth rings.
- **Seriation**: A relative dating method that places artifacts in a sequence based on style changes.
- **Synchronism**: The correlation of events from different cultures or regions to a common time scale.
- **Epoch**: The date (usually a year) when an era begins.
- **Julian Day**: A continuous count of days used as a unified time scale by historians and astronomers.

## Debates and Open questions
The source notes that the *Ab Urbe condita* era was not as widely used by Romans as earlier editors made it appear, indicating a scholarly debate about its historical prevalence. The reliability of conclusions drawn from a single, unsupported dating technique is questioned, emphasizing the need for corroborative evidence from multiple methods.
