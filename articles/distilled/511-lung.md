# Lung

## Overview
The lungs are the primary respiratory organs in air-breathing vertebrates, responsible for gas exchange—extracting oxygen into the bloodstream and releasing carbon dioxide. In humans, the paired lungs occupy the thoracic cavity, with the right lung larger (three lobes) than the left (two lobes) to accommodate the heart. Air travels through the trachea, branching bronchi, and bronchioles to reach millions of microscopic alveoli, where a thin blood–air barrier enables diffusion. The lungs receive a dual blood supply: deoxygenated blood via the pulmonary circulation for gas exchange, and oxygenated blood via the bronchial circulation for tissue nourishment. Breathing is driven by the diaphragm and intercostal muscles expanding the thoracic cavity. Beyond respiration, the lungs filter blood clots, regulate blood pressure via the renin–angiotensin system, maintain acid–base balance, and enable vocalization. Development begins in the embryonic foregut, with alveolar formation continuing into early adulthood. Major diseases include obstructive conditions (asthma, COPD), restrictive diseases (pulmonary fibrosis), infections (pneumonia), and cancer, strongly linked to smoking.

## Timeline
- ****4th week of gestation**** — Lung bud appears as an outpouching of the foregut; laryngotracheal groove forms.
- ****5th week**** — Right bronchial bud forms three secondary buds; left forms two (defining future lobes).
- ****6th–16th week**** — Major lung elements form (bronchi, bronchioles); tissue becomes highly vascularized.
- ****16th–26th week**** — Bronchi enlarge; bronchioles and alveolar ducts develop; terminal bronchioles form.
- ****26th week – birth**** — Blood–air barrier established; Type I and II alveolar cells appear; surfactant production begins (~7th month), enabling potential survival if premature.
- ****Birth**** — First breath inflates lungs; fetal lung fluid absorbed; ductus arteriosus closes; pulmonary circulation activates.
- ****Early adulthood**** — Alveolar multiplication completes; lung maturation finishes.

## Body

### Gross Anatomy and Structure
Human lungs are conical organs situated in the thoracic cavity flanking the heart. The right lung has three lobes (upper, middle, lower) separated by an oblique and a horizontal fissure; the left lung has two lobes (upper, lower) separated by an oblique fissure, with the lingula serving as a homologue to the right middle lobe. Each lobe comprises bronchopulmonary segments (supplied by a segmental bronchus and artery), which subdivide into lobules. The hilum is the central recession where bronchi, vessels, nerves, and lymph nodes enter, forming the root of the lung. The lungs are enclosed by the pleural sac: the visceral pleura covers the lung surface, and the parietal pleura lines the rib cage; the pleural cavity between them contains lubricating fluid for frictionless sliding. The right lung is heavier (standard reference: men 155–720 g, women 100–590 g) than the left (men 110–675 g, women 105–515 g). Mediastinal surfaces bear impressions from adjacent structures: the right lung shows grooves for the azygos vein, superior/inferior vena cavae, and esophagus; the left lung shows a deep cardiac impression, and grooves for the aortic arch, descending aorta, and left subclavian artery.

### Microanatomy and Histology
The respiratory tract terminates in alveoli, the functional parenchyma. Connective tissue rich in elastin and collagen provides elasticity (compliance) and recoil; elastin concentrates at alveolar openings and junctions. Alveolar walls contain pores of Kohn allowing inter-alveolar airflow. The respiratory epithelium is ciliated pseudostratified columnar with goblet cells (secreting mucin), basal cells, club cells (in terminal bronchioles), and macrophages; it secretes airway surface liquid (ASL) for mucociliary clearance. Pulmonary neuroendocrine cells (PNECs, ~0.5% of epithelium) cluster at airway junctions, sense inspired gas composition, and secrete serotonin, dopamine, and neuropeptides. Bronchi are held open by cartilage rings/plates; bronchioles lack cartilage, relying on smooth muscle (absent in respiratory bronchioles, termed membranous bronchioles). The respiratory zone begins at respiratory bronchioles, forming the acinus (up to 10 mm diameter) containing alveolar ducts, sacs, and alveoli. The secondary pulmonary lobule (30–50 primary lobules) is the smallest grossly visible unit, supplied by a terminal bronchiole and enclosed by an interlobular septum. Alveoli are lined by Type I pneumocytes (squamous, 95% surface area, gas exchange, non-dividing) and Type II pneumocytes (cuboidal, secrete surfactant and lining fluid, proliferate and differentiate into Type I). Alveolar macrophages clear debris and pathogens.

### Blood Supply and Innervation
The lungs possess a dual circulation. The **pulmonary circulation** carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle via the pulmonary arteries to alveolar capillaries for gas exchange, returning oxygenated blood to the left atrium. The **bronchial circulation** (systemic) supplies oxygenated blood to airways and supporting tissue via bronchial arteries (usually three: two left, one right) branching from the aorta. Lung blood volume averages 450 mL (~9% of total), fluctuating widely and capable of autotransfusion during hemorrhage. Innervation is autonomic: parasympathetic via the vagus nerve (acetylcholine causes bronchoconstriction and glandular secretion); sympathetic tone via norepinephrine on beta-2 adrenoceptors causes bronchodilation. The respiratory center in the brainstem drives breathing via the phrenic nerve (cervical plexus) to the diaphragm.

### Development
Lungs derive from the ventral foregut (laryngotracheal groove) during the 4th week. Branching morphogenesis—driven by FGF10 (promotes epithelial branching), inhibited by SHH, with roles for FGFR2b and BMP4—generates the bronchial tree through repeated tip bifurcation. By week 5, secondary buds establish the lobar pattern (3 right, 2 left). Weeks 6–16 form conducting airways and vascularization. Weeks 16–26 see bronchiolar and alveolar duct development. From week 26 to birth, the blood–air barrier forms: Type I cells for gas exchange and Type II cells secreting surfactant (reducing surface tension to permit alveolar expansion). Surfactant appearance (~month 7) marks viability. Vitamin A deficiency disrupts alveolar septation, epithelial differentiation, extracellular matrix, and basement membrane, predisposing to respiratory disease. At birth, lungs are fluid-filled; the first breath (within ~10 seconds) triggers fluid absorption, pulmonary vascular resistance drop, and spontaneous breathing. Alveoli number only ~1/6 of adult count at birth, multiplying into early adulthood; capillary network maturation permits normal growth.

### Physiology: Gas Exchange and Mechanics
Gas exchange occurs across the blood–air barrier (0.5–2 μm thick) folded into ~300–500 million alveoli, yielding 70–145 m² surface area. Lungs cannot expand actively; inspiration requires thoracic volume increase via diaphragm contraction (primary) and intercostal muscles lifting the rib cage. Expiration is passive at rest (muscle relaxation), leaving functional residual capacity (FRC ~2.5–3.0 L). Forced expiration recruits accessory muscles, reducing FRC but leaving ~1 L residual volume. Pulmonary stretch receptors in airway smooth muscle trigger the Hering–Breuer reflex to prevent over-inflation. The conducting zone warms air to 37°C, humidifies, and cleanses via mucociliary clearance (cilia beating mucus toward pharynx).

### Non-Respiratory Functions
Lungs maintain homeostasis: endothelial ACE converts angiotensin I to II, regulating blood pressure; expelling CO₂ regulates acid–base balance. They filter venous thrombi, metabolize/excrete bioactive substances (prostaglandins, leukotrienes, serotonin, bradykinin), and absorb/modify/excrete drugs. They provide airflow for speech and paralanguage. Emerging research suggests a role in platelet biogenesis.

### Gene and Protein Expression
~75% of ~20,000 human protein-coding genes are expressed in lung; <200 are lung-enriched, <20 highly specific. Highest specific expression: surfactant proteins (SFTPA1, SFTPB, SFTPC) and napsin in Type II pneumocytes; DNAH5 (dynein) in ciliated cells; SCGB1A1 in goblet cells.

### Clinical Significance
**Inflammation/Infection:** Pneumonia (alveolar), bronchitis/bronchiolitis (airway), pleurisy (pleura). Causes: bacteria, viruses (TB major bacterial cause), fungi (Aspergillus in immunocompromised), hantavirus (rodent-borne). Alcohol chronically desensitizes cilia, reducing mucociliary clearance and macrophage numbers, increasing infection susceptibility.  
**Vascular:** Pulmonary embolism (DVT-derived clot in pulmonary arteries); pulmonary hypertension (elevated pulmonary artery pressure); granulomatosis with polyangiitis (small vessel vasculitis). Lung contusion (trauma) causes alveolar hemorrhage. Pleural effusion, pneumothorax (air), hemothorax (blood) compress lung.  
**Obstructive Diseases:** Asthma, bronchiectasis, COPD (chronic bronchitis, emphysema) feature airway obstruction from inflammation/constriction. Diagnosed by spirometry (FEV1/FVC ratio). Managed by trigger avoidance, bronchodilators, corticosteroids. Smoking is primary cause of chronic bronchitis/emphysema; elastase/alpha-1 antitrypsin imbalance drives emphysema; basal cell disarray underlies COPD progression.  
**Restrictive Diseases:** Pulmonary fibrosis replaces functional tissue with fibrous connective tissue (occupational, autoimmune, drug-induced). Severe cases require mechanical ventilation.  
**Cancer:** Primary (small-cell, non-small-cell carcinoma) or metastatic. Smoking is major risk factor. Staged by CT/biopsy; treated with surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, or palliation. Screening recommended for high-risk US populations.  
**Congenital:** Cystic fibrosis, pulmonary hypoplasia, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, infant respiratory distress syndrome (surfactant deficiency). Azygos lobe (variation) complicates thoracoscopy.  
**Pneumothorax:** Air in pleural space uncouples lung from chest wall (traumatic or spontaneous, e.g., scuba diving breath-hold ascent).  
**Examination:** Palpation and auscultation of posterior, lateral, anterior lung fields; triangle of auscultation (thin musculature) enhances sound. Wheezing suggests asthma/COPD.  
**Function Testing:** Spirometry measures volumes (tidal volume 500–750 mL, vital capacity, residual volume) and flows (FEV1). Plethysmography measures FRC (cannot be measured by exhalation alone). Total lung capacity 4–6 L (lower in females, smokers, shorter individuals; increased by training up to 40%, modified by pollution). Diffusing capacity measures gas transfer efficiency.

### Comparative Anatomy
**Birds:** Small, rigid "circulatory lungs" connected to 8–9 air sacs extending into bones. Unidirectional flow: air sacs → parabronchi (millions of parallel passages with atria for gas exchange via cross-current blood flow) → air sacs → expiration. Air sacs ventilate lungs but are poorly vascularized. Paleopulmonic parabronchi (unidirectional) in all birds; some have neopulmonic (bidirectional) regions.  
**Reptiles:** Single central bronchus with large alveolar pockets (sponge-like). Snakes/limbless lizards: right lung dominant; amphisbaenians: left dominant. Crocodilians/monitor lizards: unidirectional flow with air sacs (hepatic piston ventilation via diaphragmaticus muscle). Turtles: forelimb/pectoral girdle pumping.  
**Amphibians:** Simple balloon-like lungs; gas exchange mainly on outer surface (low efficiency). Buccal pumping (positive pressure) forces air in. Cutaneous respiration supplements gas exchange. Lungless salamanders and caecilian *Atretochoana* respire via skin/mouth, limiting size.  
**Fish:** Lungs in coelacanths (vestigial), bichirs (paired sacs), lungfish (complex septated chambers, homologous to tetrapod lungs). Lungs ventral to esophagus (vs. dorsal swim bladder in ray-finned fish).  
**Invertebrates:** Convergent evolution: book lungs (arachnids), branchiostegal lungs (coconut crab), mantle cavity lung with pneumostome (pulmonate snails/slugs).

### Evolutionary Origins
Lungs and fish gas bladders are homologous, originating as esophageal outpocketings in bony fish (Osteichthyes) for air-gulping in hypoxia. In ray-finned fish (Actinopterygii), most evolved into closed gas bladders (physoclistous); some retain open connection (physostome). In basal bony fish (gar, bichir, bowfin, lobe-finned), sacs function as lungs. Lobe-finned fish (Sarcopterygii) gave rise to tetrapods; thus, vertebrate lungs are homologous to fish gas bladders, not gills.

## Terms
- ****Alveoli**** — Microscopic air sacs at the terminus of the respiratory tree where gas exchange occurs across a thin blood–air barrier.
- ****Bronchopulmonary segment**** — A discrete, surgically resectable unit of lung supplied by its own segmental bronchus and artery; subdivisions of lobes.
- ****Buccal pumping**** — A positive-pressure breathing mechanism (used by amphibians and early tetrapods) where the floor of the mouth forces air into the lungs.
- ****Compliance**** — The distensibility of lung tissue; the ease with which lungs expand under pressure, conferred by elastin in connective tissue.
- ****FEV1/FVC ratio**** — The ratio of forced expiratory volume in 1 second to forced vital capacity; used to distinguish obstructive (low ratio) from restrictive (normal/high ratio) lung disease.
- ****Hilum**** — The central depression on the medial lung surface where bronchi, vessels, nerves, and lymph nodes enter/exit (the root of the lung).
- ****Pleural cavity**** — The potential space between the visceral and parietal pleurae containing lubricating pleural fluid for frictionless movement.
- ****Pulmonary circulation**** — The circuit carrying deoxygenated blood from the right heart to the lungs for gas exchange and returning oxygenated blood to the left heart.
- ****Surfactant**** — A lipoprotein complex secreted by Type II pneumocytes that reduces surface tension at the air–liquid interface, preventing alveolar collapse.
- ****Type I / Type II pneumocytes**** — Alveolar epithelial cells: Type I (squamous, 95% surface area, gas exchange, non-dividing); Type II (cuboidal, secrete surfactant, proliferate/differentiate into Type I).

## Debates and open questions
- **Total alveolar surface area:** Estimates vary widely (50–75 m² to 70–145 m²); the "tennis court" analogy is disputed as overstated.
- **Lung microbiota role:** The significance of the dynamic lung microbiome in homeostasis and diseases like asthma/COPD is under active investigation.
- **Platelet biogenesis:** The extent and mechanism of lung contribution to platelet production remain research topics.
- **COPD pathogenesis:** The precise sequence from smoking-induced basal cell disarray to emphysema and malignant transformation is being elucidated.
- **Fetal lung fluid clearance:** The exact mechanisms and timing of fluid absorption at birth involve multiple interacting pathways.
- **Vitamin A threshold:** The precise levels and timing of vitamin A sufficiency required for normal alveolar development are not fully defined.
