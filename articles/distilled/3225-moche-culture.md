# Moche culture

## Overview
The Moche civilization was a complex society that flourished along the northern coast of Peru from approximately 100 to 800 AD. It is renowned for its sophisticated irrigation systems, monumental adobe pyramids (huacas), and highly expressive art, particularly its detailed ceramics and metalwork. The Moche are not considered a monolithic empire but rather a collection of autonomous polities sharing a common culture, whose society was socially stratified and whose practices included significant ritual human sacrifice.

## Timeline
- **c. 100–300 AD** — Early Moche period; emergence of the culture.
- **c. 300–600 AD** — Middle Moche period; expansion and flourishing.
- **c. 500–800 AD** — Late Moche period; urban nucleation and collapse.
- **c. 300 AD** — Burial of the Lord of Sipán, a high-ranking Moche elite.
- **c. 450 AD** — Burial of the Lady of Cao, a high-status female.
- **c. 563–594 AD** — Severe climatic events (possibly a super El Niño) disrupt the region.
- **c. 600–700 AD** — Pampa Grande becomes a major northern Moche center.
- **c. 700 AD** — Moche establish control over the neighboring Virú culture.
- **1899–1900** — First archaeological excavations at Huaca de la Luna by Max Uhle.
- **1987** — Discovery of the intact tomb of the Lord of Sipán.
- **2005** — Discovery of the mummified Lady of Cao.

## Body

### Society and Political Structure
The Moche were an agriculturally based society whose wealth and social stratification were founded on a sophisticated network of irrigation canals that diverted river water to desert lands. This agricultural surplus allowed for social specialization. Scholars contend the Moche were not a single state but likely a group of autonomous polities sharing a common culture, evidenced by their iconography and monumental architecture. Two distinct regions, Southern and Northern Moche, have been identified, each probably corresponding to a different political entity with its own ruling dynasties. Centralized control over the entire Moche area appears to have been infrequent.

### Art and Material Culture
Moche material culture is distinguished by its highly decorated and standardized ceramics, which used mold technology for mass production. Pottery depicted a wide range of social activities, including war, agriculture, and erotica, and included realistic portrait vessels. The Moche also developed advanced metalworking techniques, including electrochemical plating and depletion gilding to cover copper in gold or silver. The discovery of the Tombs of Sipán in 1987 revealed a wealth of metal objects, proving they were closely tied to elite power. Textiles, woven from cotton and wool, also indicate social hierarchy, with more complex and dyed garments belonging to elites.

### Religion and Ritual
Human sacrifice played a significant role in Moche religious practices, as indicated by iconography and skeletal remains. Rituals involved elite actors, monumental settings, and possibly the consumption of blood. The victims may have been losers of ritual battles or warriors captured in territorial conflicts. A recurring figure in their iconography is the "Decapitator," often depicted as a spider or winged creature holding a knife and a severed head. Sacrifices may have been linked to rites of ancestral renewal and agricultural fertility.

### Collapse
The demise of the Moche political structure is attributed to several factors. Studies of Andean ice cores reveal severe climatic events between 563 and 594 AD, possibly a super El Niño, which caused intense flooding followed by decades of drought. This would have disrupted the canal-based irrigation critical to Moche agriculture. However, Moche polities survived beyond 650 AD in some valleys, where later settlements show fortifications and defensive works, suggesting social unrest and conflict over scarce resources rather than a complete demise from climate alone.

## Terms
- **Huaca**: A monumental adobe pyramid or ceremonial complex, such as the Huaca del Sol and Huaca de la Luna.
- **Regional Development Epoch**: The archaeological period in Peru (c. 200 BC–600 AD) during which the Moche flourished.
- **Polity**: A politically organized unit; the Moche are thought to have consisted of several autonomous polities.
- **Stirrup spout**: A distinctive bottle spout design common in Moche ceramics.
- **Tumi**: A crescent-shaped metal knife used in Moche sacrificial rituals.
- **Electrochemical replacement plating**: A metalworking technique used by the Moche to cover copper with a thin layer of gold or silver.
- **Depletion gilding**: A process where the surface of an alloy is treated to enrich it with a precious metal, creating a gold-like appearance.
- **Liminal space**: An in-between or transitional space, such as a sunken step, which held significance in Moche ceremonies.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Political Organization**: Scholars debate whether the Moche were a unified state or a collection of autonomous polities sharing a common culture.
- **Sacrificial Victims**: There is disagreement over whether victims of Moche sacrifice were losers of ritual battles among elites or warriors captured in territorial conflicts.
- **Cause of Collapse**: Theories for the Moche decline emphasize environmental change (severe El Niño events), social unrest, and inter-elite conflict, with no single cause universally accepted.
- **Terminology**: Historian Waldemar Espinoza Soriano argues the term "Mochica culture" is erroneous and should be reserved for the Lambayeque culture.
