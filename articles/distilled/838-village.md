# Village

## Overview  
A village is a permanent human settlement smaller than a town, typically with 500–2,500 inhabitants, historically rooted in subsistence agriculture and communal living. Villages have shaped social, economic, and cultural life across continents, from ancient Afghanistan to modern rural India, and remain significant in global demographic and administrative systems. Their evolution—from traditional agrarian communities to modern administrative units—reflects broader patterns of urbanization, industrialization, and cultural preservation.

## Timeline  
- **c. 13th century** — Shtetlekh (Jewish villages) begin appearing in Eastern Europe
- **1804** — Dan Fodio’s Jihad reshapes village governance in Northern Nigeria
- **1900** — ~46% of Germans live in rural areas; villages dominate settlement patterns
- **1911** — Encyclopædia Britannica documents village communities globally
- **1960s–1970s** — Soviet central planning drives village depopulation in Russia
- **1982** — Les Plus Beaux Villages de France founded to preserve heritage villages
- **2001** — I Borghi più belli d’Italia established to protect Italian villages
- **2002** — Bulgaria census records 2.4 million villagers; migration trends analyzed
- **2011** — India census reports 649,481 villages; 69% population lives in them
- **2017** — Pakistan census shows 64% rural population, mostly in villages
- **2023** — 361 Italian villages listed as “Most Beautiful”; 172 in France

## Body  

### Definitions and Characteristics  
Villages are permanent settlements with fixed dwellings clustered closely together, distinguishing them from dispersed settlements. While often rural, the term *urban village* applies to certain urban neighborhoods. In Great Britain, a hamlet gained village status by building a church. Traditional villages supported subsistence agriculture, artisan fishing, or crafts, with homes grouped for sociability and defense. Transient villages also exist, particularly among nomadic or semi-nomadic groups.

### Regional Variations  
In **Afghanistan**, the *deh* is a mid-sized settlement larger than a *qala* but smaller than a *shār*. In **India**, Gandhi famously declared, “The soul of India lives in its villages,” and the 2011 census counted 649,481 villages, with 236,004 having fewer than 500 residents. **Pakistan** defines rural areas as those outside urban boundaries, with villages called *deh* or *gaaon*. In **Kazakhstan**, 42.7% of citizens lived in 8,172 *auyls* (villages) per the 2009 census.

In **East Asia**, villages serve as administrative subdivisions under townships (*China*, *Taiwan*), while in **Southeast Asia**, terms like *kampung* (Brunei, Indonesia, Malaysia) and *desa* (Indonesia) denote village-level governance. Indonesian *desa* follow adat (customary law), whereas *kelurahan* reflect modern administration. In the **Philippines**, “village” often refers to gated private subdivisions, distinct from *barangays*, the official government unit.

In **Europe**, villages vary widely. In **Slavic regions**, *selo* and *ves* denote villages, with *selo* often indicating a church-centered community. **Russia** distinguishes *selo* (with a church) from *derevnya* (without), and Soviet-era policies relocated villagers into larger settlements. **Ukraine** treats villages as the lowest administrative unit under *hromada* councils. **Finland** used land register villages (*rekisterikylä*) until 2014. **France** classifies communes by density; **Germany** defines villages as group settlements in rural areas; **Italy** lacks a legal definition but informally caps villages at 2,000 residents. **Spain** and **Portugal** use *pueblo* and *aldeia*, respectively, often tied to religious festivals and communal spaces.

In the **UK**, villages traditionally required a church and lacked town halls or mayors. Many evolved into dormitories or pit villages during industrialization. **Lebanon** and **Syria** feature mountainous villages retaining Aramaic names and agrarian economies. In **Africa**, Nigerian villages were governed by *Magajis* under emirs post-1804, while South African villages range from 500 to 2,000 residents.

### Urbanization and Change  
The Industrial Revolution drew villagers into cities for factory work, spurring village growth into towns and enabling labor specialization. Urbanization continues globally, though not always linked to industrialization. In **Russia**, 1960s–70s central planning relocated villagers to collective farms. In **Bulgaria**, 2002 data showed 23% migration from village to city. In **Germany**, rural populations dropped from 46% in 1900 to 23% by 2015. Many European villages now function as tourist destinations (*selski turizam* in Bulgaria) or second-home communities (*dacha settlements* in Russia).

### Cultural Significance  
Villages embody cultural identity. The **Ashkenazi shtetl** symbolizes Eastern European Jewish life, memorialized in literature and film (*Fiddler on the Roof*). In **Vietnam**, villages center on communal houses worshipping tutelary deities, with customs overriding royal law (“Phép vua thua lệ làng”). In **Indonesia**, *nagari* (Minangkabau) and *banjar* (Bali) reflect ethnic social structures. In **Māori New Zealand**, the *pā* was a fortified hilltop village. In **Australia** and **North America**, “village” now often denotes planned communities or unincorporated hamlets, detached from rural origins.

## Terms  
- **Comonym**: A village name derived from Greek *kōmē* (village) and *onyma* (name).  
- **Deh**: A mid-sized Afghan settlement between *qala* and *shār*.  
- **Kampung**: A Malay/Indonesian village, often with fewer than 10,000 people.  
- **Selo**: A Slavic village, historically distinguished by the presence of a church.  
- **Shtetl**: A small Jewish market town in Eastern Europe.  
- **Ves**: An archaic Slavic term for village, preserved in place names.  
- **Derevnya**: A Russian rural settlement without a church, contrasted with *selo*.  
- **Hromada**: Ukraine’s administrative unit governing villages.  
- **Gotong royong**: A Malay/Indonesian communal mutual-aid tradition.  
- **Pit village**: A British settlement built around a coal mine.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- The etymology of *dedina* (Slovak) is debated: possibly from *dediť* (“to inherit”) or linked to Sanskrit *deśá*.  
- Whether villages should be defined by population, function, or administrative status remains contested across countries.  
- The future of European villages amid ongoing rural depopulation and urbanization is uncertain.  
- The role of traditional village governance (*adat*, *penghulu*) in modern nation-states is increasingly marginalized.
