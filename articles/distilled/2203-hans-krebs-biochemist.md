# Hans Krebs (biochemist)

## Overview
Sir Hans Krebs (1900–1981) was a German-British biochemist whose discoveries of the urea cycle and the citric acid cycle fundamentally advanced the understanding of cellular respiration, the process by which cells extract energy from food. His work, which earned a Nobel Prize, provided the biochemical foundation for understanding metabolism in nearly all aerobic organisms.

## Body

### Early Life and Career in Germany
Krebs studied medicine at the Universities of Göttingen, Freiburg, and Berlin, earning his MD in 1925. He shifted his focus from clinical practice to biochemical research, working under Otto Warburg from 1926 to 1930. In 1931, at the University of Freiburg, he and student Kurt Henseleit discovered the urea cycle, the metabolic pathway for converting toxic ammonia into urea for excretion. This was the first metabolic cycle to be identified.

### Exile and Discovery of the Citric Acid Cycle
Following the Nazi rise to power, Krebs was dismissed from his position in 1933 due to his Jewish ancestry. He was rescued by Sir Frederick Gowland Hopkins and recruited to the University of Cambridge. In 1935, he moved to the University of Sheffield, where he had greater resources. Using a Warburg manometer to measure oxygen consumption in tissue slices, Krebs and William Arthur Johnson systematically tested hypotheses about glucose metabolism. In 1937, they established the sequence of the citric acid cycle (also called the Krebs or TCA cycle), the central metabolic pathway that allows aerobic cells to generate far more ATP (energy currency) from food than anaerobic processes like glycolysis. His initial manuscript was rejected by *Nature*, but he published the full discovery in the journal *Enzymologia*.

### Later Work and Legacy
Krebs moved to the University of Oxford in 1954, where he continued research until his retirement in 1967. In 1957, with Hans Kornberg, he discovered the glyoxylate cycle, a variation of the citric acid cycle found in plants, bacteria, and fungi. He received numerous honours, including a knighthood in 1958 and the Copley Medal in 1961. His legacy is commemorated by research institutes, lectureships, and scholarships in his name.

## Terms
*   **Cellular respiration:** The biochemical process in living cells that extracts energy from food and oxygen to power life processes.
*   **Urea cycle (Krebs–Henseleit cycle):** The metabolic pathway that converts toxic ammonia into urea for excretion; the first metabolic cycle discovered.
*   **Citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle):** A central sequence of chemical reactions in aerobic cells that breaks down molecules to produce ATP, the cell's energy currency.
*   **ATP (Adenosine triphosphate):** The primary energy-carrying molecule in cells.
*   **Glyoxylate cycle:** A variation of the citric acid cycle found in plants, bacteria, protists, and fungi, allowing them to convert fats into carbohydrates.
*   **Warburg manometer:** An instrument for measuring oxygen consumption in tissue samples, crucial to Krebs's experimental work.
*   **Glycolysis:** An anaerobic process (without oxygen) that breaks down glucose for energy, yielding less ATP than the citric acid cycle.
