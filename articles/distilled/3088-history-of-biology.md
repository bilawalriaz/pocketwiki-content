# History of biology

## Overview
The history of biology traces the study of living systems from ancient practical knowledge to a unified modern science. It matters because it shows how biology evolved from scattered observations in medicine and natural history into a coherent discipline through key conceptual revolutions—like cell theory and evolution by natural selection—and technological advances, such as microscopy and DNA sequencing.

## Timeline
- **c. 10,000 BCE** — Neolithic Revolution: domestication of plants and animals begins.
- **c. 1600 BCE** — Edwin Smith and Ebers Papyri written in Ancient Egypt.
- **c. 1500 BCE** — Ayurveda originates in ancient India.
- **4th century BCE** — Aristotle classifies animals; Theophrastus writes on botany.
- **1543** — Andreas Vesalius publishes *De humani corporis fabrica*.
- **1628** — William Harvey describes blood circulation.
- **1665** — Robert Hooke publishes *Micrographia*.
- **1670s-1680s** — Antonie van Leeuwenhoek observes microorganisms.
- **1735** — Carl Linnaeus publishes his taxonomy.
- **1859** — Charles Darwin publishes *On the Origin of Species*.
- **1866** — Gregor Mendel publishes work on inheritance.
- **1900** — Mendel's laws rediscovered by Carl Correns.
- **1930s** — Modern synthesis unites genetics and natural selection.
- **1953** — Watson and Crick propose DNA double helix structure.
- **1972** — First recombinant DNA experiment by Paul Berg.
- **2000** — First draft of the human genome announced.

## Body

### Ancient and Medieval Foundations
Early biological knowledge was practical, focused on medicine and agriculture. Ancient Egyptian papyri (c. 1600 BCE) document surgical and pharmaceutical practices. In Mesopotamia, animal anatomy was studied for divination, and medicine combined magical and herbal treatments. Independent traditions emerged in China (Taoist alchemy, yin-yang theory) and India (Ayurveda, c. 1500 BCE), which classified organisms and developed surgery. Greek natural philosophy, especially Aristotle's empirical observations and classification of 540 animal species, became the dominant framework. His concept of the *scala naturae* (Great Chain of Being) and teleological view of nature persisted for centuries. Galen's medical authority remained unchallenged in Europe until the Renaissance, while Islamic scholars like Avicenna preserved and advanced Greek works.

### Renaissance to Enlightenment: Empiricism and Classification
The Renaissance revived empirical study. Vesalius (1543) replaced Galenic authority with human dissection. Microscopy, pioneered by Hooke (1665) and dramatically improved by Leeuwenhoek (1670s), revealed the "micro-world" of bacteria and cells. The 17th-18th centuries focused on systematizing nature. Linnaeus created binomial nomenclature (1735), while Buffon suggested species mutability. Natural theology, arguing from design, encouraged natural history but entrenched teleology. The rise of mechanical philosophy began replacing the "nature as organism" metaphor with "nature as machine."

### 19th Century: Disciplines and Evolution
Biology emerged as a unified field. The term "biology" was introduced around 1800. Key developments included:
- **Biogeography & Evolution:** Humboldt linked organisms to environment. Cuvier established extinction via comparative anatomy. Lyell's uniformitarian geology provided deep time. Darwin (1859) and Wallace independently proposed evolution by natural selection, synthesizing evidence from geology, biogeography, and embryology.
- **Cell Theory & Physiology:** Schleiden and Schwann (1838-39) proposed cells as life's basic unit; Virchow added "all cells from pre-existing cells" (1860s). Microscopy revealed cell structures (nucleus, chromosomes). Pasteur disproved spontaneous generation, leading to germ theory (Koch). Experimental physiology (Bernard) analyzed life as chemical/physical processes, challenging vitalism (Wöhler synthesized urea, 1828).

### 20th Century: Genetics, Molecules, and Synthesis
The rediscovery of Mendel's laws (1900) launched genetics. Morgan's fruit fly work (1910s) linked genes to chromosomes. The "modern synthesis" (1930s) unified Mendelian genetics with natural selection via population genetics (Fisher, Haldane, Wright). Molecular biology rose after Watson and Crick's DNA structure (1953). The "central dogma" (DNA → RNA → protein) and genetic code cracking (1961-66) defined the field. Recombinant DNA technology (1970s) enabled genetic engineering. The Human Genome Project (1988-2000) epitomized the genomic era. Ecology became quantitative (Odum, 1950s), and evolutionary theory expanded (punctuated equilibrium, neutral theory).

### Late 20th-21st Century: Integration and New Frontiers
Disciplines converged. Organismal biologists adopted molecular tools; molecular biologists studied gene-environment interplay. Genomics and proteomics reversed earlier reductionism. New fields emerged: bioinformatics, synthetic biology, and astrobiology. Technological advances in computing, imaging, and data analysis enabled systems-level understanding of life.

## Terms
- **Scala naturae:** The "Great Chain of Being," a hierarchical classification of life from plants to humans, prevalent from Aristotle to the 18th century.
- **Cell theory:** The principle that (1) all living things are made of cells, (2) the cell is the basic unit of life, and (3) all cells arise from pre-existing cells.
- **Germ theory of disease:** The theory that many diseases are caused by microorganisms.
- **Natural selection:** The process where organisms with favorable traits are more likely to survive and reproduce, proposed by Darwin and Wallace.
- **Modern synthesis:** The early 20th-century fusion of Mendelian genetics with Darwinian evolution by natural selection.
- **Central dogma of molecular biology:** The framework describing the flow of genetic information from DNA to RNA to protein.
- **Recombinant DNA:** DNA molecules formed by laboratory methods of genetic recombination to bring together genetic material from multiple sources.
- **Genomics:** The study of the complete set of genes (genome) of an organism.
- **Bioinformatics:** The use of computational tools to analyze biological data, especially large datasets like genomes.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Teleology vs. Mechanism:** The debate over whether biological phenomena are best explained by purpose (Aristotle, natural theology) or physical/chemical causes (mechanical philosophy) persisted until the 19th century.
- **Extinction and Species Mutability:** The acceptance of extinction (Cuvier) and the mutability of species (Buffon, Lamarck) was contentious before Darwin.
- **Mendelism vs. Biometrics:** Early 20th-century conflict between Mendelian discrete inheritance and continuous variation studied by biometricians was resolved by the modern synthesis.
- **Neutral Theory vs. Selectionism:** Motoo Kimura's neutral theory (1968) proposed that most molecular evolution is driven by genetic drift, not natural selection, sparking ongoing debate about the relative importance of selection and contingency in evolution.
- **Molecular vs. Organismal Biology:** The "Molecular Wars" (late 20th century) saw tension between reductionist molecular approaches and holistic organismal/evolutionary perspectives, a divide that has since lessened with integrative fields.
