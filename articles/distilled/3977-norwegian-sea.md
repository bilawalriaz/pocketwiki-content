# Norwegian Sea

## Overview
The Norwegian Sea is a deep marginal sea located northwest of Norway, between the North Sea and the Greenland Sea. It is a critical region for global climate regulation due to its role in ocean currents, and it supports rich fisheries and significant offshore oil and gas reserves. Its year-round ice-free conditions, despite its high latitude, are a direct result of warm Atlantic currents.

## Timeline
- **c. 250 million years ago** — Formation begins as the Eurasian and North American plates start to separate.
- **c. 8,000 years ago** — The Storegga Slide, a major submarine landslide, induces a tsunami.
- **1607** — Jan Mayen island is discovered; it becomes a base for Dutch whalers.
- **1610** — The Muscovy Company begins hunting walrus and whales near Spitsbergen.
- **1773** — First depth measurements are performed by Constantine Phipps.
- **1876–1878** — First systematic oceanographic research expedition aboard the *Vøringen*.
- **1920s–1970s** — Herring stock increases, then collapses due to climate shifts and overfishing.
- **1976** — Norway establishes a 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zone.
- **1993** — Norway starts undersea oil production in the Norwegian Sea.
- **2007** — Gas production begins at the major Ormen Lange field.

## Body

### Formation and Geography
The Norwegian Sea formed about 250 million years ago as the Eurasian and North American plates rifted apart. Its present continental slope marks the ancient border between Norway and Greenland. The last ice age shaped its coasts, with glaciers carving fjords and extending continental slopes. The sea floor is mostly deep, averaging about two kilometres, with two major basins separated by a ridge. The southern basin is larger and deeper, reaching 3,500–4,000 metres. Submarine ridges, like the Scotland-Greenland Ridge (avg. 500m deep), separate it from adjacent seas.

### Hydrology and Climate
Four major water masses meet here, driving currents vital for global climate. The warm, salty North Atlantic Current flows in, releasing heat that keeps the sea ice-free and moderates Norway's climate. Its flow is twice as strong in winter as summer. Cold, deep water from the Greenland Sea enters via the Fram Strait. This deep water, Norwegian Sea Deep Water (NSDW), is cold (below 0°C), saline, and nutrient-rich but oxygen-poor. The interaction between warm surface water and cold air in winter is crucial for Arctic climate. The sea is a transition zone between boreal and Arctic conditions.

### Flora and Fauna
The sea is a transition zone for marine life. Phytoplankton blooms peak in May, dominated by diatoms. Zooplankton, especially copepods like *Calanus finmarchicus*, are the base of the food web. Extensive cold-water coral reefs of *Lophelia pertusa* on the continental slopes provide critical habitat but are threatened by trawling. The coastal waters are vital spawning grounds for Atlantic herring and cod. Fish stocks, particularly herring, have fluctuated dramatically due to climate and overfishing. Whales, including minke and orca, are common, following fish stocks. Seabirds like puffins were severely impacted by the herring collapse.

### Human Activities
Norway, Iceland, and Denmark/Faroe Islands share the territorial waters. Fishing, especially for cod at Lofoten, has been practiced for centuries but suffered from overfishing. Whaling was intensive from the 17th to early 20th centuries, decimating bowhead and blue whale populations. The sea is now a major source of oil and gas, with fields like Ormen Lange producing significant natural gas via long undersea pipelines. Navigation is important for the Northern Sea Route to Asia and was a critical Allied supply route to Russia in WWII. During the Cold War, it was a strategic gateway for the Soviet Navy to the Atlantic, leading to intense naval activity and the sinking of the nuclear submarine *Komsomolets* in 1989.

## Terms
- **Marginal sea:** A sea partially enclosed by islands, archipelagos, or peninsulas, adjacent to or mostly enclosed by a particular ocean.
- **Continental shelf:** The extended perimeter of a continent and associated coastal plain, which is submerged under relatively shallow seas.
- **Thermohaline circulation:** Global density-driven circulation of the oceans, driven by differences in water temperature (thermo) and salinity (haline).
- **Sverdrup (Sv):** A unit of flow used to measure ocean currents, equal to one million cubic metres per second.
- **Isotherm:** A line on a map or chart connecting points having the same temperature at a given time or on average over a given period.
- **Phytoplankton:** Microscopic marine plants that drift on the ocean surface and are the foundation of the marine food web.
- **Zooplankton:** The animal component of plankton, consisting of small drifting animals that feed on phytoplankton.
- **Exclusive economic zone (EEZ):** A sea zone prescribed by the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea over which a state has special rights regarding the exploration and use of marine resources.

## Debates and Open questions
- The relative importance of the Norwegian Sea's large heat capacity versus the Gulf Stream and its extensions as the primary source of Norway's mild winters is a subject of recent research and conclusion.
- The impact of climate change on the delicate balance between Atlantic and Arctic species in this transition zone is an ongoing area of study.
- The future of fish stocks, particularly the recovery of herring and the decline of capelin and cod, remains uncertain under varying climate and fishing pressure scenarios.
- The environmental risk posed by the radioactive material aboard the sunken submarine *Komsomolets* is a persistent concern.
