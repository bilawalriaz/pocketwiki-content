# 20th-century classical music

## Overview
20th-century classical music refers to Western art music composed between 1901 and 2000. This period is defined by an unprecedented divergence of styles, moving away from the unified practices of the previous centuries. It was a century of radical experimentation, where composers broke with traditional tonality, explored new technologies, and drew influences from jazz and folk music, resulting in a landscape without a single dominant style.

## Timeline
- **c. 1900** — Late Romantic style (Mahler, Strauss) and Impressionism (Debussy) dominate.
- **c. 1909-1912** — Arnold Schoenberg develops atonality, breaking with traditional tonality.
- **1913** — Luigi Russolo publishes *The Art of Noises*, a Futurist manifesto for incorporating noise into music.
- **1921-1923** — Schoenberg develops the twelve-tone technique.
- **After WWI** — Neoclassicism emerges, reviving forms from the 17th and 18th centuries (Stravinsky, Prokofiev).
- **1940s-50s** — Pierre Schaeffer pioneers *musique concrète*, using recorded sounds as raw material.
- **1950s** — John Cage introduces chance (aleatory) elements into composition.
- **1960s** — Minimalism begins, featuring repetition and gradual processes (Riley, Reich, Glass).
- **Late 20th C.** — Eclecticism, polystylism, and postmodern reactions to avant-garde styles become prominent.

## Body

### The Break from Tradition and Stylistic Pluralism
At the turn of the century, music was characterized by late Romanticism and Impressionism. A pivotal moment was the widespread break with traditional tonality in the first decade, which led to an unprecedented "linguistic plurality" of styles. This fragmentation meant no single movement dominated the century. Composers reacted to and extended earlier styles in diverse ways, leading to parallel developments like Expressionism, Neoclassicism, and later, Minimalism.

### Major Movements and Techniques
**Impressionism**, led by Claude Debussy in France, reacted against German Romanticism, emphasizing sensuous experience over intellectual or ethical themes. **Neoclassicism**, prominent between the World Wars, sought to revive the balanced forms of the 17th and 18th centuries, often with modern harmonic twists. **Expressionism**, developed by Schoenberg and his disciples (the Second Viennese School), used atonality to convey intense emotion. **Futurism**, an Italian movement, advocated for incorporating everyday noises into music, influencing later developments in electronic and experimental music.

### Technological and Procedural Innovation
The century saw the rise of **electronic music**, enabled by recording technology and magnetic tape. This included *musique concrète* (using recorded natural sounds) and live electronic music. Composers like John Cage pioneered **aleatory** (chance) music and the **prepared piano**. **Minimalism** emerged as a reaction to complex serialism, using repetition and simple processes. Later, **spectral music** analyzed sound spectra to create compositions.

### Cultural and National Influences
Important cultural trends informed musical styles. **Primitivism** influenced early works by Stravinsky and Prokofiev. **Nationalism** remained a strong force, with composers like Vaughan Williams incorporating folk music. In the United States, an **American vernacular style** developed, blending classical forms with jazz and popular idioms, notably in the works of Gershwin and Copland. Composers under communist regimes, like Shostakovich, often had to navigate political constraints like socialist realism.

## Terms
- **Atonality**: Music that avoids a tonal center or key, developed by Arnold Schoenberg.
- **Twelve-tone technique**: A compositional method devised by Schoenberg that uses all twelve notes of the chromatic scale in a fixed order (a "tone row") to avoid tonal hierarchy.
- **Neoclassicism**: A movement reviving the forms and textures of the 17th and 18th centuries, often with modern harmonic language.
- **Musique concrète**: A form of electroacoustic music that uses recorded sounds from the environment as raw material.
- **Aleatory**: Music in which some element of the composition is left to chance or to the determination of the performer(s).
- **Minimalism**: A style characterized by the use of repetition, steady pulse, and consonant harmony, often with gradual transformation.
- **Spectral music**: A compositional approach that uses the analysis of sound spectra as the basis for harmony and orchestration.
- **Polystylism**: The use of multiple, often contrasting, styles within a single composition.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The dating of **postmodernism** in music is debated; some place its start as early as 1930, while others see it as a later 20th-century reaction to modernism.
- The boundaries and definitions of terms like **experimental music** and **electronic music** are fluid and contested.
- The relationship between **modernism and postmodernism** is complex, with figures like John Cage claimed by both camps.
