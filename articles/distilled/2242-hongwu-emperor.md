# Hongwu Emperor

## Overview
The Hongwu Emperor (r. 1368–1398), born Zhu Yuanzhang, founded the Ming dynasty. Rising from peasant origins during the Yuan dynasty's collapse, he unified China through military conquest and implemented sweeping reforms that centralized power, restructured society, and established enduring institutions. His reign restored native Chinese rule and set the template for imperial governance until the Qing dynasty's end.

- **1382** — Conquers Yunnan; Empress Ma dies
- **1388** — Lan Yu defeats Mongols at Buir Lake
- **1392** — Crown Prince Zhu Biao dies; grandson Zhu Yunwen named heir
- **1393** — Major purge following Lan Yu's execution
- **24 Jun 1398** — Hongwu Emperor dies; succeeded by grandson Jianwen Emperor

## Body

### Rise from Poverty to Rebellion
Zhu Yuanzhang grew up amid epidemics, famines, and uprisings under Yuan rule. After his family died in 1344, he spent years as a wandering novice monk before joining the Red Turban rebels in 1352. His leadership and military skill enabled rapid advancement. He captured Nanjing in 1356, building a powerful regional regime while adopting Yuan administrative practices. Though nominally subordinate to Red Turban figurehead Han Lin'er, Zhu increasingly acted independently, defeating rivals and declaring himself King of Wu in 1364.

### Unification Wars (1360–1368)
Three major powers divided the Yangtze River Basin: Zhu's state, Chen Youliang's Han regime, and Zhang Shicheng's domain. The decisive Battle of Lake Poyang in September 1363 saw Zhu's fleet defeat Chen's larger force, killing Chen. By 1365, Zhu had absorbed Chen's territories. He then conquered Zhang Shicheng, besieging Suzhou for ten months before capturing it in October 1367. On 23 January 1368, Zhu proclaimed himself emperor and established the Ming dynasty.

### Northern Campaigns and Limits of Expansion
Ming armies drove the Yuan emperor from Dadu (Beijing) in September 1368 and continued campaigns into Shanxi, Shaanxi, and Gansu. A 1370 offensive defeated Mongol forces and captured the Yuan emperor's family, though the new ruler escaped. A massive 1372 offensive into Mongolia ended in defeat for two of three Ming armies, shattering hopes of controlling the entire Yuan Empire. The Ming shifted to defensive postures in the north, though successful campaigns conquered Sichuan (1371), Yunnan (1381–82), and Liaodong (1387–88).

### Administrative Reforms
The Hongwu Emperor restructured government to maximize imperial control. Initially following the Yuan model with a Central Secretariat and Grand Chancellors, he abolished the chancellorship entirely in 1380 after executing Grand Chancellor Hu Weiyong for treason. The six ministries were placed directly under imperial authority, and the Chief Military Commission was split into five separate bodies. He established the Embroidered Uniform Guard as secret police. These reforms fragmented state authority, eliminating coup risks but weakening long-term governance capacity.

### Social and Economic Policies
The emperor pursued a vision of a simple agricultural society. He confiscated land from wealthy landowners and Buddhist monasteries, redistributing it to peasants. The lijia system of local self-government was implemented nationwide by 1381. The Weisuo military system made soldiers hereditarily obligated to serve, with each family providing one member per generation. The army was self-sufficient through soldier-farmers. Taxes were kept low—targeting 3% of harvest—but collection in kind and long-distance transportation created heavy burdens.

### Control of Merchants and Foreign Trade
Merchants were placed at the bottom of the four-occupations hierarchy and excluded from civil service examinations. Strict regulations controlled their movements, requiring travel documents for journeys over 100 li (58 km). The government banned private foreign trade entirely, destroying ships and blocking ports under the policy that "not even a piece of wood should sail across the sea." Foreign relations operated through the tribute system, where neighboring states sent missions recognizing Ming superiority and received goods in return.

### Later Reign: Purges and Succession
The emperor's eldest son and heir, Zhu Biao, died in 1392, creating succession uncertainty. The emperor appointed his young grandson Zhu Yunwen as heir and launched massive purges to eliminate the military nobility that might threaten the boy's succession. Approximately 100,000 people were killed in political purges during his reign. The most notable purge in 1393 followed the execution of General Lan Yu. The emperor died on 24 June 1398, and his grandson ascended as the Jianwen Emperor, whose subsequent attempts to curb princely power triggered civil war and his own overthrow by the Hongwu Emperor's fourth son, Zhu Di (the Yongle Emperor).

## Terms
- **Red Turban Rebellion**: Anti-Yuan uprising beginning in 1351, fueled by secret societies including the White Lotus; Zhu Yuanzhang joined in 1352
- **Weisuo system**: Hereditary military organization where soldiers served in guards (wei) of 5,600 men, divided into battalions and companies; families provided one soldier per generation
- **Lijia system**: Local self-government structure dividing households into units (jia) that rotated responsibility for tax collection and public services
- **Yellow Registers**: Government records tracking households and population for taxation and corvée obligations
- **Fish-Scale Registers**: Land surveys recording parcel locations, quality, ownership, and tax quotas
- **Grand Secretariat (Zhongshu Sheng)**: Central administrative body led by Grand Chancellors; abolished by Hongwu in 1380
- **Tribute system**: Diplomatic framework where foreign states sent missions acknowledging Ming superiority, receiving Chinese goods in return
- **Jinshi**: Highest degree in the civil service examination system
- **Embroidered Uniform Guard (Jinyiwei)**: Secret police force directly subordinate to the emperor
- **Great Ming Code**: Legal code based on the Tang Code of 653, compiled from 1364 and finalized in 1397

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Despotism vs. pragmatism**: Modern historians debate whether the Hongwu Emperor's centralization and purges reflected paranoia and mental illness, or were rational responses to his impoverished upbringing and the genuine threat of military conspiracies
- **Legacy of administrative reforms**: While the abolition of the chancellorship strengthened imperial power, scholars note it weakened long-term governance capacity, creating a tension between autocratic control and effective administration that persisted through the dynasty
- **Effectiveness of anti-merchant policies**: Despite severe restrictions on trade and merchants, contemporary observers noted that commercial profits far exceeded agricultural income, suggesting the policies failed to suppress market activity
- **Succession planning**: The decision to bypass his sons in favor of a grandson remains debated—whether it reflected genuine attachment to his heir or a calculated attempt to prevent the military nobility from gaining power through a new emperor's gratitude
