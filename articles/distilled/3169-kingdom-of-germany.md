# Kingdom of Germany

## Overview
The Kingdom of Germany (Latin: *Regnum Teutonicum*) was the original core territory of the Holy Roman Empire, evolving from the eastern Frankish kingdom created by the Treaty of Verdun in 843. It consisted of several stem duchies and, from 962, its elected kings claimed the imperial title, theoretically uniting it with Italy and Burgundy. The kingdom's distinct "German" identity and terminology emerged gradually, shaped by political conflicts like the Investiture Controversy and the weakening of imperial power outside its core lands.

## Timeline
- **843** — Treaty of Verdun splits the Carolingian Empire, creating East Francia (the future Kingdom of Germany).
- **870** — Treaty of Meerssen divides Lotharingia between East and West Francia.
- **880** — Treaty of Ribemont gives Lotharingia to East Francia, setting a long-lasting border.
- **911** — Death of Louis the Child, last Carolingian king of East Francia; stem duchies elect Conrad I.
- **919** — Henry the Fowler elected king; early use of "Kingdom of the Germans" recorded.
- **962** — Otto I crowned Emperor, formally linking the German kingship to the imperial title.
- **c. 1028** — Henry III elected King of the Romans, a title used before imperial coronation.
- **1075-1122** — Investiture Controversy; Pope Gregory VII uses *Regnum Teutonicorum* to define Henry IV's realm.
- **1122** — Concordat of Worms legally distinguishes the emperor's authority in the "German kingdom" from other imperial parts.
- **1250** — Post-Hohenstaufen period; emperors' power wanes, strengthening identification of the empire with Germany.
- **1508** — Maximilian I adopts the title "emperor elect," and "King of the Romans" becomes the heir-apparent's title.
- **1512** — The term "Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation" becomes more common.

## Body

### Origins and Structure
The Kingdom of Germany originated as East Francia, one of three kingdoms created from the Carolingian Empire by the Treaty of Verdun in 843. It comprised several regions with distinct identities, which became the "stem duchies": Saxony, Franconia, Thuringia, Bavaria, and Swabia. Lotharingia was incorporated after 925. These duchies, while having internal solidarity, acknowledged the kingdom's unity after the last Carolingian king died in 911, electing Conrad I. The Ottonian dynasty later worked to preserve the duchies as crown offices, though they became hereditary by the reign of Henry IV.

### Emergence of "German" Terminology
The eastern kingdom was initially called *Francia Orientalis* (East Francia). The term "German" (*Teutonicus*) emerged to distinguish its Germanic-speaking peoples from Romance-speaking Franks. Early references, like a 919 annal entry mentioning a "Kingdom of the Germans," are debated. The Ottonians, as non-Frankish rulers, may have adopted the "Teutonic" label to legitimize their rule over all northern Alpine peoples. By the late 11th century, during the Investiture Controversy, Pope Gregory VII used *Regnum Teutonicorum* to define Henry IV's realm as a distinct territorial kingdom, separate from Italy. This terminology gained traction among German writers, reflecting a growing sense of a distinct political community.

### Imperial Title and Political Reality
From Otto I's coronation in 962, German kings claimed the imperial title, theoretically ruling a transnational empire. In practice, the "Teutonic" lands remained the empire's administrative core. The Archbishop of Mainz was ex officio arch-chancellor of Germany, though only the German chancery functioned. As imperial influence in Italy and Burgundy waned, the titles distinguishing these kingdoms fell out of use. To emphasize their universal claim, emperors from the 12th century used the title *rex Romanorum* (King of the Romans). However, the Concordat of Worms (1122) legally recognized the emperor's separate authority within the "German kingdom."

### Late Medieval and Early Modern Developments
After the Hohenstaufen dynasty collapsed in the 13th century, emperors were often confined to German affairs, and terms like *Regnum Alemanniae* (Kingdom of Alamannia/Germany) appeared. Proposals to create a hereditary German kingdom separate from the empire were rejected due to attachment to the imperial title. From 1250, German identity became more closely linked to the empire as a whole. The term "Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation" emerged in 1474. The Imperial Circles, established after 1500, effectively defined Germany against non-German imperial territories like Italy and Bohemia. In 1508, Maximilian I's adoption of "emperor elect" formalized the separation of the German royal election from imperial coronation.

## Terms
- **Stem Duchies**: The large, semi-autonomous duchies (Saxony, Bavaria, Franconia, Swabia, Lotharingia) that formed the core regions of the Kingdom of Germany.
- **Regnum Teutonicum**: Latin for "Kingdom of the Germans," a term used from the 11th century to denote the German core of the Holy Roman Empire.
- **Investiture Controversy**: A conflict (c. 1075–1122) between popes and Holy Roman Emperors over the appointment of church officials, which helped define the territorial "German kingdom."
- **Rex Romanorum**: "King of the Romans," a title adopted by German kings-elect to emphasize their claim to the imperial throne before papal coronation.
- **Concordat of Worms (1122)**: Agreement ending the Investiture Controversy, which legally distinguished the emperor's authority in the "German kingdom" from his authority elsewhere in the empire.
- **Imperial Circles (Reichskreise)**: Administrative districts established in the early 16th century that effectively defined the German lands within the Holy Roman Empire.
- **Translatio Imperii**: The medieval concept of the transfer of imperial authority from Rome to the Franks and then to the Germans, used to justify the empire's universal claims.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origin of "German" Identity**: Historians debate whether the concept of a "German" kingdom dates to the 10th century or emerged later, in the 11th century, partly due to papal terminology during the Investiture Controversy.
- **Nature of the Stem Duchies**: Scholarly debate exists over whether the stem duchies were truly "tribal" entities with continuous solidarity from the Migration Period or were largely Frankish institutions reshaped after Charlemagne.
- **Separation from the Empire**: There were persistent proposals, such as a possible discussion between Pope Nicholas III and Rudolf I, to create a hereditary German kingdom independent of the Holy Roman Empire, but these were rejected due to the strong attachment to the imperial title.
