# Giotto

## Overview
Giotto di Bondone (c. 1267–1337) was an Italian painter and architect from Florence whose work marked the transition from the Byzantine style to the Renaissance. His naturalistic approach to figure drawing, emotional expression, and spatial composition broke with medieval conventions and influenced generations of artists. His masterwork, the Scrovegni Chapel frescoes in Padua (completed c. 1305), are considered a cornerstone of Early Renaissance art.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Giotto was likely born in Florence to Bondone, a blacksmith. The traditional story of his discovery by Cimabue—finding him drawing sheep—comes from Vasari and is considered legendary by modern scholars. Similarly, the anecdote of Giotto drawing a perfect red circle for the Pope may be apocryphal. His training remains uncertain; while Vasari claimed he was Cimabue’s pupil, earlier sources suggest otherwise. Around 1290, Giotto married Ricevuta di Lapo del Pela (known as 'Ciuta'), the daughter of Lapo del Pela of Florence. The marriage produced four daughters and four sons, one of whom, Francesco, became a painter.

### Career
By 1301, Giotto owned property in Florence and held multiple commissions. He worked in Rome (1297–1300), where he painted the Stigmatization of St. Francis (c. 1297), one of the few signed panels attributed to him. The attribution of the Upper Church frescoes at Assisi to Giotto is hotly debated. Technical analysis in 2002 found white lead in some Assisi frescoes—absent from known Giotto works—suggesting multiple Roman artists were involved. The Stigmatization motif in his Louvre panel may reflect influence from Assisi. According to Vasari, Giotto's earliest works were for the Dominicans at Santa Maria Novella, including a fresco of The Annunciation and an enormous suspended Crucifix, dated to about 1290. By the early 1300s, Giotto also painted the Badia Polyptych, now in the Uffizi, Florence.

### Scrovegni Chapel
Commissioned by Enrico degli Scrovegni around 1305, the chapel serves as family worship space and venue for an annual mystery play. The frescoes depict the Life of the Virgin and Life of Christ, emphasizing salvation and the Virgin Mary. The 37-scene cycle is arranged in three tiers, guiding viewers left to right. Allegorical figures of the seven virtues appear in grisaille, and quatrefoil Old Testament scenes frame the narrative. The expense of the ultramarine blue pigment used required it to be painted on top of the already-dry fresco (a secco) to preserve its brilliance. That is why it has disintegrated faster than the other colours, which were painted on wet plaster and have bonded with the wall. An example of the decay can clearly be seen on the robe of the Virgin, in the fresco of the Nativity.

### Style
Giotto’s figures are solidly three-dimensional, clothed in naturally hanging garments rather than stylized drapery. He employed foreshortening and inward-facing figures to create spatial illusion. His emotional realism—seen in the shame of soldiers, gossipers on the road to Egypt, and the shepherds’ sideways glances—distinguishes his work. The Adoration of the Magi may reference Halley’s Comet (1301), inspiring the 1986 spacecraft named after him.

### Mature Works
In Assisi (1306–1311), Giotto painted the Lower Church transept frescoes, showing stylistic development from Padua. In Florence, the Ognissanti Madonna (c. 1310) is the only universally accepted panel painting, though its original placement is debated. He also painted around the same time the Dormition of the Virgin, now in the Berlin Gemäldegalerie, and the Crucifix in the Church of Ognissanti. The Peruzzi and Bardi Chapels in Santa Croce display his evolving use of architecture and narrative arrangement. The Peruzzi Chapel, painted a secco, is severely deteriorated but influenced later artists like Masaccio and Michelangelo. The Bardi Chapel depicts the life of Francis of Assisi, following a similar iconography to the frescoes in the Upper Church at Assisi, dating from 20 to 30 years earlier.

### Later Works and Death
In Naples (1330–1333), Giotto served as court painter under King Robert of Anjou. He returned to Florence in 1334 to design the cathedral’s campanile (bell tower), begun July 18, 1334. Construction continued after his death by Andrea Pisano and Francesco Talenti, completed in 1359. His final work was the Podestà Chapel in the Bargello. Giotto died in January 1337.

### Burial and Legacy
Vasari records Giotto buried in Florence Cathedral beside the entrance, marked by a white marble plaque. Excavations in the 1970s uncovered bones beneath Santa Reparata (directly beneath the cathedral), which forensic analysis tentatively linked to Giotto based on paint-related chemicals in the skeleton. However, the remains indicate a man about 4 feet tall, possibly suffering from congenital dwarfism—contradicting some traditional portraits. Skeptics, including Franklin Toker, question the identification.

## Terms
- **A secco**: Painting on dry plaster surface; less durable than true fresco (buon fresco).
- **Buon fresco**: Technique of painting on wet lime plaster, creating permanent bond with wall.
- **Grisaille**: Monochrome painting in gray tones, often simulating sculpture.
- **Giottesque**: Style characterized by naturalistic figures, emotional expression, and spatial awareness.
- **Foreshortening**: Artistic technique creating illusion of depth by shortening forms along the line of sight.
- **Connoisseurship**: Method of art attribution based on expert judgment of style and quality.
- **Polyptych**: Multi-panel altarpiece, typically hinged and decorated with religious scenes.
- **Mystery play**: Medieval theatrical performance depicting biblical stories, often performed annually.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Assisi attribution**: Whether Giotto painted the Upper Church St. Francis frescoes remains disputed. Technical evidence suggests multiple Roman artists, with no confirmed Giotto contribution.
- **Birthplace**: Traditional accounts cite Colle Vespignano, but documentary evidence points to Florence.
- **Training**: Vasari’s claim of Cimabue apprenticeship is considered legendary; actual training unknown.
- **Burial remains**: 1970s excavation bones tentatively identified as Giotto, but physical evidence (dwarfism, height) conflicts with traditional portraits.
- **Ognissanti Madonna placement**: Whether intended for main altar or choir screen remains unresolved.
- **Santa Croce chapel dating**: Bardi and Peruzzi Chapel frescoes’ chronology is disputed due to stylistic variations.
- **Stefaneschi Triptych**: Designed c. 1320, but style seems unlikely for Giotto or his normal Florentine assistants.
- **Baroncelli Chapel**: Completed 1328 but now believed to be mostly by assistants including Taddeo Gaddi.
