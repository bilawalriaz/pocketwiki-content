# Sociology

## Overview
Sociology is the scientific study of human society, examining social behavior, relationships, interaction, and culture. It spans micro-level analyses of individual agency to macro-level analyses of social systems and structure. The discipline emerged in the early 19th century as a response to modernity’s challenges—industrialization, urbanization, secularization, and rationalization—seeking to apply scientific rationalism to social life. Its theoretical core is multi-paradigmatic, historically rooted in the tension between positivism (modeling social science on natural science) and antipositivism (emphasizing subjective meaning and interpretation). Sociology informs social policy, welfare, and diverse sectors including politics, education, business, and medicine.

## Timeline
- **1780** — Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès coins the term "sociology" in an unpublished manuscript.
- **1838** — Auguste Comte independently defines sociology as a positivist science of society in *Course in Positive Philosophy*.
- **1875** — William Graham Sumner teaches the first U.S. college course entitled "Sociology" at Yale.
- **1892** — Albion Small establishes the world’s first formal Department of Sociology at the University of Chicago.
- **1895** — Émile Durkheim establishes the first European department of sociology at the University of Bordeaux; publishes *Rules of the Sociological Method*.
- **1896** — W. E. B. Du Bois founds the Atlanta Sociological Laboratory, the first scientific school of sociology, at Atlanta University.
- **1897** — Durkheim publishes *Suicide*, a seminal study using statistical analysis to distinguish sociological from psychological explanation.
- **1905** — Max Weber publishes *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*; American Sociological Association (ASA) founded.
- **1919** — Weber establishes the first sociology department in Germany at LMU Munich, advancing antipositivist methodology.
- **1923** — Institute for Social Research (Frankfurt School) founded at University of Frankfurt.
- **1949** — International Sociological Association (ISA) founded.
- **1966** — Peter L. Berger and Thomas Luckmann publish *The Social Construction of Reality*, reinvigorating the sociology of knowledge.
- **1985** — Mark Granovetter publishes "Economic Action and Social Structure: The Problem of Embeddedness," consolidating new economic sociology.

## Body

### History and Foundations
Sociological reasoning predates the discipline, appearing in ancient Greek philosophy (Socrates, Plato, Aristotle), Confucian thought, and the 14th-century *Muqaddimah* of Ibn Khaldun, whom some consider the father of sociology. The term "sociology" (from Latin *socius*, companion, and Greek *logia*, study of) was coined by Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès in 1780 and defined by Auguste Comte in 1838. Comte envisioned a "social physics" (later ceded to Adolphe Quetelet) to unify history, psychology, and economics through positivism—the view that authentic knowledge derives only from scientific method. Though later theorists rejected Comte’s rigid "three stages" of human understanding, his insistence on sociology as a distinct science justified Durkheim in regarding him as the discipline’s founder.

Karl Marx, while rejecting Comtean positivism, developed a "science of society" grounded in historical materialism and class struggle, earning recognition as a principal architect of sociology. Herbert Spencer, the era’s most popular sociologist, applied evolutionary biology to society, coining "survival of the fittest" and advocating laissez-faire governance; his influence prompted Durkheim to define his own ideas in opposition.

Institutionalization began in the 1890s. Albion Small founded the first department at the University of Chicago (1892) and the *American Journal of Sociology* (1895). W. E. B. Du Bois established the Atlanta Sociological Laboratory (1896), pioneering urban and Black sociology. Durkheim institutionalized the discipline in Europe at the University of Bordeaux (1895), refining positivism into a methodology based on objective *social facts*—external, coercive realities like laws and norms. His *Suicide* (1897) demonstrated that varying suicide rates between Catholics and Protestants stemmed from social integration levels, not individual psychology, establishing structural functionalism. Max Weber, the third principal architect, established antipositivist sociology in Germany (1919), arguing for *Verstehen* (interpretive understanding) of subjective *social action* and *ideal types*.

Early 20th-century expansion saw departments founded in the UK (LSE, 1904), Germany (1909 association), Poland (1920), and the Frankfurt School (1923). Talcott Parsons later consolidated the "canon" (Durkheim, Weber, Pareto, Marshall) in the U.S., driving structural-functionalism’s dominance until the 1960s.

### Theoretical Traditions
**Positivism and Antipositivism.** Positivism seeks to study society using natural science methods: empiricism, objectivity, and causal laws. Durkheim refined this via *social facts*; modern *instrumental positivism* (Paul Lazarsfeld) prioritizes methodological rigor, quantitative methods, and middle-range theory over epistemology. Antipositivism, rooted in Hegel and Marx, argues the social world differs irreducibly from the natural world due to human meaning. Weber defined sociology as the interpretive understanding of *social action* via *Verstehen*. Ferdinand Tönnies distinguished *Gemeinschaft* (community, unity of will) from *Gesellschaft* (society, instrumental association), separating pure (deductive) from applied (inductive) sociology. Georg Simmel added micro-level analyses of social forms and individuality.

**Classical Theory (Four Traditions).** Randall Collins identifies four lineages:
1.  **Functionalism** (Comte, Durkheim, Spencer, Radcliffe-Brown): Views society as an organism where institutions function to maintain stability and solidarity. Emphasizes social evolution toward complexity.
2.  **Conflict Theory** (Marx, Weber, Simmel): Focuses on inequality, power struggles, and structural contradiction (e.g., class struggle) rather than cohesion.
3.  **Symbolic Interactionism** (Weber, Simmel, Mead, Cooley, Chicago School): Society is constructed through micro-level interactions using symbols; reality is a shared, fluid mosaic. Ethnomethodology (Garfinkel) extends this to study how people produce social order.
4.  **Utilitarianism / Rational Choice** (Homans, Blau, Coleman): Actors maximize self-interest based on preferences, knowledge, and decision rules; embedded in exchange theory.

**20th-Century Developments.** Post-WWII U.S. sociology shifted from the Chicago School’s interactionism to survey research (Lazarsfeld) and Parsons’ grand structural-functionalism at Harvard. By the 1960s, functionalism collapsed, yielding a "baffling variety" of perspectives: phenomenology, critical theory (Frankfurt School), ethnomethodology, structuralism, post-structuralism. The *Pax Wisconsana* (University of Wisconsin) described a mid-century truce favoring empirical middle-range theory over grand theory. Structuralism (Giddens, Lévi-Strauss) analyzed culture as sign systems; network structuralism (Harrison White) analyzed social relations as patterned networks. Post-structuralism (Foucault) rejected humanist assumptions, intersecting with postmodernism.

**Central Theoretical Problems.** Sociology grapples with three inherited dichotomies:
1.  **Subjectivity/Objectivity:** How intersubjectivity is achieved; the sociologist’s position *inside* the object of study (Bourdieu’s reflexivity).
2.  **Structure/Agency:** Whether social structures (class, gender) determine behavior or individuals exercise free will; central to explaining social reproduction of inequality.
3.  **Synchrony/Diachrony:** Static analysis of structure (Saussure’s *langue*) vs. dynamic analysis of process (*parole*); historical sociology vs. survey research.

### Research Methodology
Methods divide into **qualitative** (direct observation, interviews, textual analysis; prioritizes contextual depth) and **quantitative** (statistical analysis of large samples; prioritizes generalizability). Quantitative methods dominate U.S. sociology (2:1 ratio in top journals); qualitative methods prevail in British journals. **Sampling** uses probability (random, stratified) or non-probability (convenience, snowball) methods to infer population parameters.

Key methods include: **Archival/Historical research**; **Content analysis** (coding texts via grounded theory/QDA software); **Experimental research** (isolating variables via randomization); **Longitudinal studies** (tracking over time); **Participant observation** (ethnography); **Program evaluation** (assessing policy effectiveness); **Survey research** (questionnaires/interviews).

**Computational sociology** employs simulations, AI, text mining, social network analysis (SNA), and sequence analysis to model complex social processes bottom-up. It bridges natural and social science, exporting concepts like network centrality.

### Subfields
**Culture.** *Sociology of culture* treats culture as a dependent variable; *cultural sociology* sees all social phenomena as inherently cultural. The "cultural turn" (1960s) elevated culture’s status. Sub-areas include art/literature (Bourdieu, Frankfurt School) and cultural studies (Birmingham School: power, production/consumption reciprocity).

**Criminality, Deviance, Law.** Criminology draws on sociology/psychology. Sociology of deviance studies norm violation (formal crime, informal deviance); Merton’s typology links structural strain to deviance. Sociology of law (Durkheim: law as "visible symbol" of solidarity) examines law’s interaction with social change (e.g., incarceration’s role in racial stratification).

**Communications & Digital Sociology.** Analyzes Internet, new media, and digital devices. Studies online communities (network analysis, virtual ethnography), organizational change, and the shift to informational society (Castells). Digital sociology expands scope beyond the Internet to all digital media impacts.

**Economic Sociology.** Originating with Jevons (1879), Durkheim, Weber, Simmel. *New economic sociology* (Granovetter, 1985) centers on *embeddedness*: economic action occurs within ongoing social relations. SNA is primary method; key concepts include *strength of weak ties* (Granovetter) and *structural holes* (Burt). Sociology of work examines technology, globalization, labor markets, and inequality.

**Education.** Studies how institutions shape outcomes. Coleman Report (1966) found family background outweighs school resources; catalyzed desegregation busing.

**Environment.** Environmental sociology studies human-nature interactions, metabolic rift (Marx/Foster), risk, and disaster. Human ecology links natural, social, and built environments.

**Social Pre-wiring.** Studies fetal social behavior (twin ultrasounds), arguing newborns inherit genetic wiring for social interaction (evident from 14th week gestation), predating socialization.

**Family, Gender, Sexuality.** Family as primary socialization unit; feminist sociology critiques patriarchy and intersectionality (gender/race/class).

**Health, Illness, Body.** Sociology of health/illness focuses on social effects/attitudes; medical sociology on profession/institutions. Sociology of the body covers embodiment, biotechnology, genetics. Subfield on death/dying (Davies, Kearl).

**Knowledge & Science.** Sociology of knowledge (Mannheim, Berger/Luckmann: *Social Construction of Reality*) links thought to social context. Sociology of science (Merton, Latour) studies science as social activity; basis for Science and Technology Studies (STS).

**Leisure.** Studies free time organization (sport, tourism); originated with Veblen’s *Theory of the Leisure Class*.

**Peace, War, Conflict.** Military sociology studies the military as a social group (coerced collective action, civilian-military relations).

**Political Sociology.** Historically studied political organization/society relations (voting, Lazarsfeld). Now examines power, identity formation, social movements, NGOs, stateless entities, human rights.

**Population/Demography.** Studies size, composition, change; links to ecology, migration (mobilities), epidemiology.

**Public Sociology.** (Burawoy) Engages publics beyond academia; a style of sociology, not a method.

**Race/Ethnic Relations.** Studies racism, segregation, policy (assimilationism, multiculturalism, anti-racism); intersects stratification, postcolonial theory.

**Religion.** Studies practices, roles, secularization (Durkheim’s *Suicide* origin; Weber’s four texts). Methodological atheism assumed.

**Social Change/Development.** Macrosociological/historical-comparative methods. Theories: Marx (material base → culture), Weber (culture → material), Durkheim (sociocultural evolution). World-systems theory (Wallerstein) globalizes Marx. Connell critiques "Northern Theory" bias.

**Social Networks.** Structures of nodes (actors) and ties (relations). Avoids discrete units; focuses on how tie structure constitutes actors. Integrates geography (sociomapping).

**Social Psychology (Sociological).** Micro-scale actions: inequality, group dynamics, prejudice, identity. Prominent user of experimental method.

**Stratification, Poverty, Inequality.** Hierarchical arrangement (class, caste). Functionalism: hierarchy stabilizes. Conflict: hierarchy blocks mobility. Marx: class via means of production (bourgeoisie/proletariat). Weber: three dimensions—Property (class), Prestige (status), Power (party). Bourdieu: cultural/symbolic capital.

**Urban/Rural/Community Sociology.** Urban: metropolitan life, planning (Du Bois, Simmel, Chicago School, Sassen’s global city). Rural: non-metropolitan, overlaps environment. Community: analyzes associations (Tönnies’ *Gemeinschaft/Gesellschaft*), social capital.

### Interdisciplinary Relations
Sociology overlaps with social anthropology (UK/Europe: non-Western focus, now converging), political science, economics, social work (sociology diagnoses root causes; social work implements interventions), and philosophy. New fields (communication, cultural studies, demography, literary theory) draw on sociological methods. Irving Louis Horowitz (1994) and Randall Collins argue the discipline suffers fragmentation, ideological theory, and loss of coherence/core. Despite this, sociologists dominated the 2007 *Times Higher Education* list of most-cited humanities authors (Foucault, Bourdieu, Giddens, Goffman, Habermas, Weber, Latour).

## Terms
- ****Positivism**** — The methodological principle that sociology should emulate natural science, relying on empiricism, objectivity, and the scientific method to establish authentic knowledge.
- ****Antipositivism**** — The view that the social world requires distinct methods (interpretive understanding, *Verstehen*) because human action is driven by subjective meanings, not just external causes.
- ****Social Facts**** — (Durkheim) Objective, external, and coercive social phenomena (norms, laws, currents) that exist independently of individual manifestations and form sociology’s proper empirical object.
- ****Verstehen**** — (Weber) Interpretive understanding: a systematic process where the researcher relates to a cultural group on their own terms to grasp the subjective meaning of social action.
- ****Ideal Type**** — (Weber) A hypothetical, analytical construct (simplification) of complex social phenomena used as a measuring rod to compare empirical reality.
- ****Structural Functionalism**** — A macro-theoretical paradigm viewing society as a system of interdependent parts (institutions) working together to maintain stability, solidarity, and evolutionary adaptation.
- ****Conflict Theory**** — A macro-paradigm emphasizing inequality, power differentials, and struggle over resources as the engine of social structure and change.
- ****Symbolic Interactionism**** — A micro-theoretical tradition viewing society as the product of everyday interactions where individuals use symbols to construct shared, fluid realities.
- ****Embeddedness**** — (Granovetter) The concept that economic actions and relations are structurally situated within, and shaped by, ongoing networks of social relations.
- ****Social Construction of Reality**** — (Berger & Luckmann) The process by which individuals and groups, through interaction, create and institutionalize shared meanings that come to be experienced as objective fact.
- ****Gemeinschaft / Gesellschaft**** — (Tönnies) *Gemeinschaft*: community based on unity of will, kinship, tradition. *Gesellschaft*: society/association based on instrumental, contractual, self-interested ties.
- ****Agency / Structure**** — The core ontological debate: *Agency* is the capacity of individuals to act independently; *Structure* refers to recurrent patterned arrangements (class, gender) that constrain or enable action.
- ****Synchrony / Diachrony**** — (Saussure/Lévi-Strauss) *Synchrony*: static analysis of a system at a single point in time. *Diachrony*: dynamic analysis of processes and change over time.
- ****Middle-Range Theory**** — (Merton) Abstract statements generalizing from segregated hypotheses and empirical regularities, rather than from an abstract idea of the social whole.
- ****Reflexivity**** — (Bourdieu) The sociologist’s critical self-awareness of their own position within the social world they study, necessary to avoid unconscious bias.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Unity vs. Fragmentation:** Whether sociology possesses a coherent "core discipline" (Horowitz, Collins) or has irreversibly fractured into specialties with little mutual regard.
*   **Positivism vs. Interpretation:** The enduring epistemological divide: whether quantitative, natural-science modeling (dominant in the U.S.) or qualitative, meaning-centered analysis (stronger in Europe/UK) provides superior sociological knowledge.
*   **Structure vs. Agency:** The ontological stalemate: how to theoretically integrate structural determination (inequality reproduction) with individual voluntarism without reducing one to the other.
*   **Grand Theory vs. Middle-Range/Empiricism:** The *Pax Wisconsana* consensus that grand theory is a "waste of time" versus calls (e.g., Duane Rousselle) for its return to counter scientific pragmatism.
*   **Northern Theory Bias:** Raewyn Connell’s critique that sociological canon and research prioritize Global North perspectives, lacking adequate theories of imperialism/colonialism and blinding the discipline to Global South realities.
*   **Public vs. Professional Sociology:** Burawoy’s tension between sociology as a dialogue with publics (public sociology) and sociology as an academic conversation addressing peers (professional sociology).
*   **Relevance to Policy:** The recurring charge (Horowitz) that ideological theory has rendered sociology irrelevant to practical policy-making, driving talent to other fields.
