# Labrador Sea

## Overview
The Labrador Sea is an arm of the North Atlantic Ocean situated between the Labrador Peninsula and Greenland. It is a marginal sea of major oceanographic importance, as it is a primary source of the cold, deep water mass known as the North Atlantic Deep Water, which plays a critical role in global ocean circulation.

## Timeline
- **c. 60 million years ago** — Separation of the North American and Greenland plates begins, initiating the formation of the Labrador Sea.
- **c. 40 million years ago** — The process of sea-floor spreading between the plates stops.
- **c. 500 BC – 1300 AD** — Southern coast inhabited by Dorset, Beothuk, and Inuit settlements; Dorset tribes later replaced by Thule people.
- **1978** — Shrimp fisheries begin in the sea.
- **1992** — Cod fishing is halted due to depleted populations near the Labrador and West Greenland banks.

## Body

### Formation and Geography
The Labrador Sea formed during the Paleocene epoch (starting about 60 million years ago) as the North American and Greenland tectonic plates separated. This process of sea-floor spreading stopped about 40 million years ago. The sea is flanked by continental shelves and connects northward to Baffin Bay via the Davis Strait. Its depth varies from about 3,400 meters where it joins the Atlantic to less than 700 meters towards Baffin Bay.

### Oceanography and Deep Water Formation
The sea features a major anticlockwise water circulation. The West Greenland Current brings warmer, saline water north, while the cold, less saline Baffin Island Current and Labrador Current flow southward along the Canadian coast, carrying icebergs. A key feature is the Northwest Atlantic Mid-Ocean Channel (NAMOC), a 3,800 km-long turbidity current channel system on the sea floor.

The Labrador Sea is a critical formation region for North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW). Specifically, it produces the Labrador Sea Water (LSW), a distinct layer within the NADW. LSW is characterized by low salinity (34.84–34.89 parts per thousand), low temperature (3.3–3.4 °C), high oxygen content, and high homogeneity. Its potential density (27.76–27.78 mg/cm³) causes it to sink and spread, forming a major component of the world's largest identifiable water mass.

### Ecology and Resources
The sea's northern and western parts are ice-covered from December to June, providing breeding grounds for pinnipeds (seals and walrus). It is a vital feeding area for numerous cetacean species, including blue, fin, humpback, and the critically endangered North Atlantic right whale. It hosts one of two primary populations of sei whales.

Fisheries have historically targeted cod, shrimp, haddock, herring, lobster, and flatfish. Cod populations were depleted by the 1990s, leading to a fishing moratorium in 1992. Coastal fauna includes species like the Labrador wolf, woodland caribou, moose, and polar bear (occasionally). The extinct Labrador duck was once common. Coastal flora includes black spruce, tamarack, dwarf birch, and Labrador tea.

## Terms
- **Marginal Sea:** A sea partially enclosed by islands, archipelagos, or peninsulas, adjacent to or widely open to the open ocean.
- **Turbidity Current Channel:** A submarine channel formed and maintained by dense, sediment-laden underwater currents (turbidity currents).
- **North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW):** A cold, dense water mass that flows at great depth in the Atlantic Ocean, a key component of global thermohaline circulation.
- **Labrador Sea Water (LSW):** The top layer of the NADW, formed in the Labrador Sea; characterized by low salinity, low temperature, and high oxygen.
- **Potential Density:** The density a parcel of water would have if moved adiabatically (without heat exchange) to a reference pressure, used to determine water mass stability.
- **Pinnipeds:** A widely distributed and diverse clade of carnivorous, fin-footed, semiaquatic marine mammals (seals, sea lions, walruses).
- **Semi-diurnal Tides:** Tides with two nearly equal high waters and two nearly equal low waters each day.
