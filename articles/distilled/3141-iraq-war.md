# Iraq War

## Overview
The Iraq War (2003–2011) was a protracted armed conflict initiated by a U.S.-led coalition invasion that toppled Saddam Hussein's Ba'athist government. It began as part of the "War on Terror" following the September 11 attacks, based on claims of Iraqi weapons of mass destruction (WMDs) and links to al-Qaeda—both later proven false. The invasion led to a prolonged insurgency, sectarian civil war, and lasting geopolitical consequences, including the rise of the Islamic State.

## Timeline
- **Oct 2002** — U.S. Congress passes Iraq Resolution, authorizing force.
- **19 Mar 2003** — U.S.-led coalition invasion of Iraq begins.
- **9 Apr 2003** — Baghdad falls; Saddam Hussein's regime collapses.
- **1 May 2003** — President Bush declares "end of major combat operations."
- **13 Dec 2003** — Saddam Hussein captured.
- **22 Feb 2006** — Bombing of al-Askari Mosque triggers severe sectarian civil war.
- **30 Dec 2006** — Saddam Hussein executed.
- **10 Jan 2007** — President Bush announces a "troop surge" of ~20,000 additional forces.
- **31 Dec 2011** — Last U.S. combat troops withdraw from Iraq.
- **Jun 2014** — Islamic State (ISIL) captures Mosul, leading to U.S. re-engagement.

## Body
### Rationale and Invasion
The Bush administration framed the invasion as part of the post-9/11 War on Terror. Key justifications included Iraq's alleged WMD programs and ties to al-Qaeda. A September 2002 U.S. intelligence report noted knowledge of Iraq's WMD programs was weak and relied on "analytic assumptions." The 9/11 Commission (2004) found no credible evidence linking Saddam Hussein to al-Qaeda. Despite UN weapons inspectors finding no evidence of active WMD programs, the U.S. and UK pushed for UN Security Council authorization, which was blocked. The invasion began on 19 March 2003 with a "shock and awe" bombing campaign, followed by a ground invasion that quickly toppled the regime.

### Post-Invasion Chaos and Insurgency
The fall of Baghdad created a power vacuum. The U.S.-led Coalition Provisional Authority (CPA) made critical errors, including de-Ba'athification (Order 1) and disbanding the Iraqi Army (Order 2), which alienated Sunnis and fueled the insurgency. Widespread looting and a breakdown in order followed. An insurgency, initially in the "Sunni Triangle," expanded into a sectarian civil war, particularly after the 2006 bombing of the al-Askari Mosque. The CPA transferred sovereignty to an interim Iraqi government in June 2004, but violence escalated.

### The Surge and Withdrawal
In 2007, President Bush deployed an additional 170,000 troops (the "surge") under General David Petraeus. This, combined with the "Anbar Awakening" (Sunni tribes turning against al-Qaeda) and a ceasefire by Shia cleric Muqtada al-Sadr, helped reduce violence. A 2008 Status of Forces Agreement set a timeline for U.S. withdrawal, completed in December 2011. However, the Iraqi government's sectarian policies under Prime Minister Nouri al-Maliki alienated Sunnis, contributing to the rise of the Islamic State (ISIL), which captured Mosul in 2014 and prompted the return of U.S. forces.

### Legality and Criticism
The invasion's legality was widely questioned. UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan stated it was "not in conformity with the UN Charter" and was illegal. The 2016 UK Chilcot Report concluded the war was unnecessary and that peaceful alternatives had not been exhausted. The primary rationale of WMDs was discredited when none were found. The war's human and financial costs were immense, with estimates of Iraqi deaths ranging from 150,000 to over one million, and U.S. costs projected to exceed $2.9 trillion by 2050.

## Terms
- **De-Ba'athification**: CPA Order 1, which removed members of Saddam Hussein's Ba'ath Party from public office, contributing to Sunni disenfranchisement.
- **Coalition Provisional Authority (CPA)**: The transitional government of Iraq established by the U.S.-led coalition from April 2003 to June 2004.
- **Insurgency**: An armed rebellion against the occupying coalition forces and the new Iraqi government, emerging after the invasion.
- **Sectarian Civil War**: Widespread violence between Iraq's Shia majority and Sunni minority, which intensified after 2006.
- **Troop Surge**: The 2007 deployment of an additional ~20,000 U.S. troops to Iraq to combat the insurgency and stabilize the country.
- **Anbar Awakening**: A movement in 2006-2007 where Sunni tribes in Anbar Province allied with U.S. forces against al-Qaeda in Iraq.
- **Status of Forces Agreement (SOFA)**: A 2008 pact between the U.S. and Iraq that mandated the withdrawal of all U.S. combat forces by December 2011.
- **Islamic State (ISIL/ISIS)**: A jihadist extremist group that emerged from al-Qaeda in Iraq, exploiting the power vacuum and sectarian tensions to seize territory in 2014.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Intelligence Failure**: The extent to which the Bush administration deliberately manipulated or ignored intelligence about WMDs and al-Qaeda links remains a subject of investigation and debate.
- **Causation of Sectarian Violence**: Scholars debate whether the CPA's policies (de-Ba'athification, disbanding the army) were the primary cause of the insurgency and civil war, or if deeper sectarian divisions were the main driver.
- **Impact of the Surge**: Analysts disagree on whether the 2007 troop surge was the primary reason for the subsequent drop in violence, or if other factors like the Sunni Awakening and Sadr's ceasefire were more decisive.
- **Long-Term Geopolitical Consequences**: The war's role in destabilizing the region, empowering Iran, and creating the conditions for the rise of ISIL is a major point of historical and political analysis.
