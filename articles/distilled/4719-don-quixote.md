# Don Quixote

## Overview
*Don Quixote* is a novel by Miguel de Cervantes, published in two parts in 1605 and 1615. It is considered a founding work of Western literature and the first modern novel, telling the story of a minor nobleman who loses his sanity reading chivalric romances and sets out as a knight-errant. Its exploration of reality versus illusion, its innovative metafictional structure, and its profound influence on language and literature make it one of the most important books in history.

## Timeline
- **1605** — Publication of *Don Quixote*, Part One.
- **1614** — A spurious sequel, Avellaneda's *Second Volume*, is published.
- **1615** — Cervantes publishes the genuine *Don Quixote*, Part Two.
- **1616** — Cervantes dies, shortly after Part Two's publication.
- **1620** — First complete English translation (by Thomas Shelton) is published.
- **2002** — Norwegian Nobel Institute survey votes *Don Quixote* the greatest work of fiction.

## Body

### Plot and Structure
The novel follows Alonso Quijano, a hidalgo (minor nobleman) from La Mancha who reads so many chivalric romances that he decides to become a knight-errant, renaming himself Don Quixote. He recruits the simple farm laborer Sancho Panza as his squire. In Part One, Don Quixote imposes his fantasy on reality, seeing inns as castles and windmills as giants. Sancho, initially grounded in reality, gradually adopts some of his master's worldview. Part Two is a sequel in which characters have read Part One and are familiar with the protagonists. A duke and duchess play elaborate pranks on the pair, and Don Quixote eventually regains his sanity before dying, renouncing his chivalric delusions.

### Style and Language
Cervantes wrote in Early Modern Spanish, using linguistic contrast for humor: Don Quixote speaks in an archaic Old Castilian style copied from his books, while other characters use contemporary Spanish. The novel is episodic and blends farce with profound themes. Its title character's name is an ironic pun; "quixote" refers to a piece of armor for the thighs, and the suffix "-ote" denotes something large and grotesque. The work pioneered metafiction, with Cervantes claiming to translate the story from an Arabic manuscript by the fictional historian Cide Hamete Benengeli.

### Themes and Interpretation
The central theme is the conflict between idealism and reality, or illusion and truth. Don Quixote's journey is often interpreted as a battle against the "reality principle." The novel is a satire of chivalric romances but also a compassionate study of individualism and the human need to find meaning. The dynamic between the idealistic Quixote and the pragmatic Sancho creates a lasting literary motif. The phrase "tilting at windmills" originates from an iconic scene and means attacking imaginary enemies.

### Sources and Historical Context
Cervantes drew on sources like the Spanish chivalric novel *Amadís de Gaula* and the Italian poem *Orlando furioso*. His own experience as a galley slave in Algiers influenced the work. The novel reflects Spanish society of the time: a declining world power with a bankrupt treasury, where the old hidalgo class and its ideals of chivalry were becoming obsolete.

### Publication and Legacy
Part One was an immediate success, with editions printed across Europe and the Americas. The publication of a pirated sequel by Avellaneda in 1614 prompted Cervantes to write and publish his own Part Two in 1615. The novel has been translated into countless languages and is one of the best-selling books in history. It gave English the adjective "quixotic" (meaning foolishly impractical and romantic) and the idiom "tilting at windmills."

## Terms
- **Hidalgo**: A member of the lower Spanish nobility, a rank below that of a knight.
- **Knight-errant (caballero andante)**: A wandering knight in medieval chivalric romance, seeking adventure to prove his prowess and defend the weak.
- **Chivalric romance**: A literary genre popular in the late Middle Ages, featuring idealized tales of knights, courtly love, and adventure.
- **Metafiction**: Fiction that self-consciously addresses the devices of fiction, often by breaking the fourth wall or incorporating stories about storytelling.
- **Quixotic**: Adjective meaning exceedingly idealistic, unrealistic, and impractical, derived from the character's name.
- **Tilting at windmills**: An idiom meaning to fight imaginary enemies or pursue a futile, idealistic cause.
- **Picaresque**: A genre of prose fiction that depicts the adventures of a roguish hero of low social class who lives by his wits in a corrupt society.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The "Best" Book Passage**: The meaning of the priest's statement in Chapter VI that *Tirant lo Blanch* is "the best book in the world" is heavily debated and called "the most difficult passage of Don Quixote."
- **Avellaneda's Identity**: The true identity of Alonso Fernández de Avellaneda, author of the spurious 1614 sequel, remains unknown despite many theories.
- **Location of the "Place in La Mancha"**: Cervantes deliberately left the opening village unnamed, sparking centuries of debate. A 2004 academic study proposed Villanueva de los Infantes, but the question is unresolved.
- **Translation Fidelity**: With numerous English translations, scholars debate which best captures Cervantes' style, with no single version considered ideal for all purposes.
