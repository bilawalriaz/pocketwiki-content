# Rudolf Nureyev

## Overview
Rudolf Nureyev (1938–1993) was a Soviet-born ballet dancer and choreographer widely regarded as the preeminent male ballet dancer of the 20th century. Born on a Trans-Siberian train to a Tatar Muslim family, he trained at the Vaganova Academy in Leningrad and rose rapidly through the Kirov Ballet. His 1961 defection in Paris—the first by a Soviet artist during the Cold War—created an international sensation and reshaped the global ballet landscape. Nureyev’s technical brilliance, charisma, and intense stage presence elevated the male dancer from a supporting role to a co-equal star. He formed a legendary partnership with Margot Fonteyn at The Royal Ballet, directed the Paris Opera Ballet (1983–1989), and staged influential productions of classics like *Sleeping Beauty*, *Giselle*, and *La Bayadère*. Despite a late start in formal training, his relentless perfectionism set a new standard for male technique. He died of AIDS complications in 1993, leaving a substantial legacy through the Rudolf Nureyev Foundation.

## Body

### Early Life and Training
Nureyev was born in 1938 near Lake Baikal while his mother travelled to join his father, a Red Army political commissar. Raised in a Tatar Muslim family with three older sisters in Ufa, he discovered dance through Bashkir folk performances and a childhood viewing of *Song of the Cranes*. World War II disrupted Soviet cultural life, delaying his formal training until 1955, when, at the unusually late age of 17, he entered the Vaganova Academy. Ballet master Alexander Pushkin became his mentor, housing Nureyev and accelerating his development to compensate for the delayed start.

### Kirov Ballet and Defection
Graduating in 1958, Nureyev joined the Kirov Ballet immediately as a principal, dancing 15 roles in three years, frequently partnering Ninel Kurgapkina. His rebellious nature—evident in a 40-minute protest over costume in *Don Quixote*—clashed with artistic director Konstantin Sergeyev. Despite KGB surveillance, he was permitted to tour Paris in 1961, where his performances electrified audiences. At Le Bourget Airport, ordered to return to Moscow under false pretenses (a Kremlin performance, then a fabricated maternal illness), Nureyev refused, suspecting imprisonment. With help from French police and socialite Clara Saint, he requested asylum on 16 June 1961, becoming the first Soviet artist to defect during the Cold War.

### The Royal Ballet and the Fonteyn Partnership
Nureyev debuted with The Royal Ballet in February 1962, partnering Margot Fonteyn, 19 years his senior. Their chemistry—described as "one body, one soul"—revitalized the company and ballet’s popular appeal; critics dubbed him a "pop dancer" and "Nijinsky reborn." They premiered Kenneth MacMillan’s *Romeo and Juliet* (1965) and Frederick Ashton’s *Marguerite and Armand*, their signature piece. The partnership endured for decades, concluding with a 1988 performance when Fonteyn was 69. Nureyev remained with the Royal Ballet until 1970, then as Principal Guest Artist, while touring globally with companies including the National Ballet of Canada (staging *Sleeping Beauty* in 1972) and American Ballet Theatre.

### Paris Opera Ballet Directorship
Granted Austrian citizenship in 1982, Nureyev assumed directorship of the Paris Opera Ballet in 1983. He revitalized a struggling company, mentoring a generation of future stars—Sylvie Guillem, Manuel Legris, Isabelle Guérin, and others—while continuing to dance and choreograph. His productions of *Sleeping Beauty*, *Swan Lake*, and *Romeo and Juliet* entered the repertoire. Despite advancing illness, he completed a final *La Bayadère* in 1992, using a photocopy of Minkus’s original score obtained during a 1989 visit to Leningrad.

### Illness and Final Years
Nureyev tested positive for HIV in 1984 but continued working. In 1987, Mikhail Gorbachev permitted a visit to his dying mother. His last stage appearance was the 1992 *La Bayadère* premiere, where he received France’s highest cultural honor. He conducted *Romeo and Juliet* in New York in May 1992 despite pericarditis, declining further treatment in July. He died on 6 January 1993 at Notre Dame du Perpétuel Secours hospital; his funeral at the Palais Garnier featured music from *Giselle* and his ballet shoes cast into the grave.

### Technique and Artistic Influence
Nureyev’s late entry into formal training (age 17 vs. the standard 9) drove a lifelong obsession with technical perfection. Colleagues described his standard as "21 out of 20." He transformed the male dancer’s role, expanding male choreography in classical productions and bridging ballet and modern dance—collaborating with Martha Graham and performing both styles when such crossover was rare. His directorship at the Paris Opera produced a lineage of ballet directors (Legris, Hilaire, Jude). Mikhail Baryshnikov praised his "instinctive intelligence, constant curiosity, and extraordinary discipline."

### Personal Life and Relationships
Nureyev’s sexuality is described as bisexual or homosexual; he had heterosexual relationships early on but later engaged in promiscuous gay encounters. He met Erik Bruhn in 1961; they became a couple and had a volatile on-and-off relationship until 1966, remaining friends until Bruhn’s death in 1986. In 1969, he met Wallace Potts; they lived together and Potts filmed his work, but Potts left in 1975 after discovering Nureyev’s infidelity. In 1979, Nureyev met Robert Tracy; they were lovers for two and a half years, and Tracy became his secretary and lived at Nureyev’s Dakota apartment until 1993. Their relationship ended contentiously: Nureyev’s lawyer sent Tracy three eviction notices the week before Nureyev died, and Tracy later received a $600,000 settlement from the Foundation in exchange for silence. Nureyev was known for a volatile temper, impatience with hierarchy, and fierce loyalty to friends. He collected Oriental carpets, antique textiles, and fine art, auctioned posthumously for the Foundation.

## Terms
- **Vaganova Academy**: The associate school of the Mariinsky (Kirov) Ballet in Leningrad (St. Petersburg), where Nureyev trained from age 17.
- **Kirov Ballet**: The Soviet-era name for the Mariinsky Ballet in Leningrad; Nureyev’s first professional company (1958–1961).
- **Defection**: The act of abandoning allegiance to one’s country for political asylum; Nureyev’s 1961 defection in Paris was the first by a Soviet artist in the Cold War.
- **Principal Dancer**: The highest rank in a ballet company; Nureyev skipped the corps de ballet and entered the Kirov directly at this level.
- **Pas de deux**: A dance for two, typically a male and female; the core vehicle for the Fonteyn-Nureyev partnership.
- **Paris Opera Ballet**: The world’s oldest national ballet company; Nureyev directed it from 1983–1989, revitalizing its repertoire and dancers.
- **Rudolf Nureyev Foundation**: Established 1975 (renamed 1994) to manage his estate (~$33M), fund grants, and preserve his choreographic legacy.
- **Commandeur de l'Ordre des Arts et des Lettres**: France’s highest cultural honor, awarded to Nureyev onstage at his final premiere (8 Oct 1992).
- **Pericarditis**: Inflammation of the sac around the heart; a complication of Nureyev’s AIDS that hospitalized him in 1992.
- **Tatar**: A Turkic ethnic group native to the Volga-Ural region; Nureyev identified strongly with his Tatar heritage and Muslim upbringing.

## Debates and open questions
- **Sexuality labels**: Sources differ on whether Nureyev is best described as bisexual (given early heterosexual relationships) or homosexual; his own statements and behavior reflected fluidity and promiscuity.
- **Defection motives**: While the immediate trigger was KGB pressure at Le Bourget, the extent to which artistic freedom vs. personal safety drove his decision remains interpreted variously.
- **Artistic authorship**: His stagings of *Swan Lake*, *Sleeping Beauty*, and *La Bayadère* closely followed Mariinsky/Petipa traditions but introduced significant male choreography; scholars debate the balance of restoration vs. innovation.
- **AIDS disclosure**: Nureyev denied his diagnosis publicly for years; the timing and manner of his eventual acknowledgment (only confirmed the morning of his death) reflect the stigma of the era.
- **Legacy in Russia**: His reputation was officially restored post-Soviet era (name re-entered Mariinsky history, monuments erected), yet the 2017 Bolshoi ballet *Nureyev* was cancelled and later dropped due to "gay propaganda" laws, indicating ongoing cultural tension.
