# History of Australia

## Overview
Australia's history spans over 65,000 years, from the arrival of Aboriginal Australians through European exploration, British colonisation, federation, and its development into a modern, multicultural democracy. It is marked by colonisation's profound impact on Indigenous peoples, the growth of a national identity, and Australia's evolving role in the Asia-Pacific.

## Timeline
- **c. 50,000–65,000 years ago** — First ancestors of Aboriginal Australians arrive from Maritime Southeast Asia.
- **1606** — Dutch ship *Duyfken* makes first documented European landing.
- **1770** — Lieutenant James Cook charts east coast, claims it for Britain.
- **1788** — First Fleet arrives; British penal colony established at Sydney Cove.
- **1851** — Gold rushes begin in New South Wales and Victoria.
- **1901** — Commonwealth of Australia proclaimed; six colonies federate.
- **1914–1918** — Australia fights in World War I; Gallipoli campaign.
- **1939–1945** — Australia fights in World War II; Japanese attacks on mainland.
- **1945** — Post-war immigration program begins; "populate or perish" policy.
- **1966** — Decimal currency introduced; White Australia policy begins dismantling.
- **1975** — Whitlam government dismissed in constitutional crisis.
- **2023** — Referendum on Indigenous Voice to Parliament is rejected.

## Body

### Indigenous Prehistory and Society
Aboriginal Australians settled the continent from 50,000 to 65,000 years ago, developing complex societies with unique artistic, spiritual, and legal systems centred on the Dreaming. They used fire-stick farming to manage the landscape. Torres Strait Islanders arrived from Papua New Guinea around 2,500 years ago. At European contact, Aboriginal society consisted of around 600 distinct groups with 250 languages, organised into egalitarian, semi-nomadic bands and clans.

### Early European Exploration and Colonisation
Dutch navigators explored the western and southern coasts in the 17th century, naming the continent New Holland. In 1770, James Cook charted the east coast for Britain. The First Fleet established a penal colony at Sydney Cove in 1788. The colony struggled initially but expanded after Governor Macquarie's reforms and the crossing of the Blue Mountains in 1813. Other colonies were established: Van Diemen's Land (Tasmania) in 1803, Swan River (Western Australia) in 1829, South Australia in 1836, and Queensland in 1859. Convict transportation ended between 1840 and 1868.

### Impact on Indigenous Populations
British settlement caused a catastrophic decline in the Aboriginal population due to introduced diseases like smallpox, violent frontier conflict, and dispossession. Estimates of deaths from conflict between 1788 and 1928 range from 17,000 to 30,000 Aboriginal people. Colonial policies evolved from early attempts at assimilation to protectionism, involving segregation on reserves and missions. The policy of removing Aboriginal children of mixed descent from their families, later known as the Stolen Generations, was practiced from the late 19th century until the 1970s.

### From Self-Government to Federation
Gold rushes in the 1850s brought prosperity and population growth. The colonies gained responsible government in the 1850s, developing parliamentary democracies. A growing nationalist movement, concerns about defence, and economic inefficiencies drove the federation movement. After referendums in the 1890s, the six colonies federated to form the Commonwealth of Australia on 1 January 1901.

### Nationhood and World Wars
The new nation's first laws included the Immigration Restriction Act 1901, the cornerstone of the White Australia policy. Australia fought as part of the British Empire in World War I, suffering heavy casualties at Gallipoli and on the Western Front, fostering a distinct national identity. In World War II, the threat from Japan led to a strategic shift towards the United States, with Prime Minister Curtin declaring Australia "looks to America." The post-war period saw a massive immigration program from Europe and sustained economic growth.

### Post-War Transformation
The post-war boom was driven by manufacturing, immigration, and suburban expansion. The White Australia policy was gradually dismantled from the 1960s and formally abolished in 1973, leading to multiculturalism. Indigenous rights advanced with the 1967 referendum and the Mabo native title decision in 1992. Politically, the period saw the end of long Liberal-Country Party dominance (1949–72), the reformist Whitlam government (1972–75), and economic restructuring under Hawke and Keating (1983–96). Australia engaged in the Vietnam War and later peacekeeping operations in East Timor and the Solomon Islands.

### Contemporary Australia
Since the 1990s, Australia has experienced uninterrupted economic growth, becoming highly multicultural with 30% of the population born overseas. Political leadership has seen long-serving governments under Howard (1996–2007) and shorter terms for Rudd, Gillard, Abbott, Turnbull, and Morrison. Key issues include climate change policy, asylum seeker management, Indigenous reconciliation (including the rejected 2023 Voice referendum), and navigating relations with China and the United States through alliances like AUKUS.

## Terms
- **Federation**: The process by which the six self-governing British colonies united to form the Commonwealth of Australia in 1901.
- **White Australia Policy**: A set of historical policies that aimed to forbid people of non-European ethnic origin, especially Asians and Pacific Islanders, from immigrating to Australia, starting with the Immigration Restriction Act 1901.
- **Stolen Generations**: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children who were forcibly removed from their families by Australian federal and state government agencies and church missions, under acts of their respective parliaments, from approximately 1910 to 1970.
- **Mabo Decision**: A 1992 High Court of Australia ruling that recognised native title in Australian common law for the first time, overturning the doctrine of *terra nullius* (land belonging to no one).
- **ANZUS**: The security treaty between Australia, New Zealand, and the United States, signed in 1951.
- **Multiculturalism**: A policy of cultural pluralism, adopted from the 1970s, that encourages the maintenance of cultural diversity within a framework of social cohesion and equality.
- **Native Title**: The recognition by Australian law that Indigenous Australians have rights and interests to land and waters according to their traditional laws and customs.
- **AUKUS**: A trilateral security partnership between Australia, the United Kingdom, and the United States, announced in 2021, focused on the Indo-Pacific region.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Motives for Colonisation**: There is a longstanding debate over whether the primary reason for establishing a penal colony at Botany Bay was the pressing need to solve Britain's penal management crisis or broader imperial strategic and economic goals.
- **Frontier Conflict Death Toll**: Historians debate the total number of Aboriginal and settler deaths in frontier conflicts, with estimates for Aboriginal deaths ranging from 17,000 to over 30,000.
- **Impact of Assimilation Policies**: The extent and legacy of policies of assimilation and the forced removal of Indigenous children remain deeply contested, with ongoing calls for truth-telling and reparations.
- **Economic Recovery from the Great Depression**: The effectiveness of the Lyons government's policies in steering Australia's recovery from the Great Depression is debated, with some arguing recovery was due more to external factors.
- **Role of the US Alliance**: The strategic implications and costs of Australia's close alliance with the United States, particularly in the context of rising tensions with China, continue to be a subject of political and public debate.
