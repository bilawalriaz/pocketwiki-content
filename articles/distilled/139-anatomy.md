# Anatomy

Anatomy (from Greek *anatomē*, "dissection") is the branch of morphology concerned with the study of the internal and external structure of organisms and their parts. It is an old science, having its beginnings in prehistoric times, and is inherently tied to developmental biology, embryology, comparative anatomy, evolutionary biology, and phylogeny—the processes by which anatomy is generated over immediate and long-term timescales. Anatomy and physiology, which study structure and function respectively, make a natural pair of related disciplines and are often studied together; human anatomy is one of the essential basic sciences applied in medicine. The discipline is divided into macroscopic (gross) anatomy, examined with unaided eyesight, and microscopic anatomy, which uses optical instruments to study tissues (histology) and cells.

## Body

### Etymology and definition

Derived from the Greek *anatomē* "dissection" (from *anatémnō*, "I cut up"), anatomy is the scientific study of the structure of organisms including their systems, organs, and tissues. It is distinct from physiology and biochemistry, which deal with the functions of parts and the chemical processes involved. The discipline subdivides into gross anatomy (structures visible to the naked eye, including superficial or surface anatomy) and microscopic anatomy (histology and embryology). Regional anatomy studies interrelationships of structures in a specific body region; systemic anatomy studies structures forming a discrete body system. Methods include dissection, endoscopy (a video camera-equipped instrument inserted through a small incision), and angiography (visualizing blood vessels via X-rays or magnetic resonance angiography). The term "anatomy" commonly refers to human anatomy, but includes other animals (zootomy) and plants (plant anatomy).

### Animal tissues

The animal kingdom contains multicellular, heterotrophic, motile organisms. Most animals (eumetazoans) have bodies differentiated into separate tissues, an internal digestive chamber, and a blastula stage in embryonic development; metazoans do not include sponges, which have undifferentiated cells. Animal cells lack cell walls and chloroplasts. All cells derive from embryonic germ layers: diploblastic animals have two (ectoderm, endoderm); triploblastic animals have three (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm). Animal tissues group into four basic types: connective, epithelial, muscle, and nervous.

**Connective tissue** is fibrous, made of cells scattered among an extracellular matrix, and often called fascia. Main types include loose connective tissue, adipose tissue, fibrous connective tissue, cartilage, and bone. The matrix contains collagen, the chief protein, which organizes and maintains tissues. The matrix can form an exoskeleton (a thickened, rigid cuticle stiffened by mineralization or protein cross-linking) or an endoskeleton (internal, present in all developed animals).

**Epithelial tissue** consists of closely packed cells bound by cell adhesion molecules. Cells can be squamous (flat), cuboidal, or columnar, resting on a basal lamina. Types are modified for function: ciliated epithelium lines the respiratory tract; microvilli occur in the small intestine; keratinized stratified squamous epithelium covers the skin, with keratinocytes making up to 95% of skin cells.

**Muscle tissue** (myocytes) produces force and motion. Three main types exist: smooth muscle (no striations, contracts slowly, found in organs like the uterus and blood vessels), skeletal muscle (contracts rapidly, limited extension, attached to bone), and cardiac muscle (found only in the heart). Obliquely striated muscle, intermediate between the other two, is found in earthworms.

**Nervous tissue** comprises neurons that transmit information. In simple animals like cnidarians, nerves form a nerve net; in most animals they organize into bundles. Higher animals have a central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) and a peripheral nervous system, divided into the somatic (voluntary) and autonomic (involuntary) systems.

### Vertebrate anatomy

All vertebrates share a basic body plan with chordate characteristics: a notochord (stiffening rod), a dorsal neural tube, pharyngeal arches, and a tail posterior to the anus. The defining characteristic is the vertebral column; in most vertebrates the notochord becomes the nucleus pulposus of intervertebral discs, though sturgeons and coelacanths retain it into adulthood. Jawed vertebrates have paired appendages (fins or legs), considered homologous because the same underlying skeletal structure was inherited from a last common ancestor—an argument Charles Darwin used for evolution.

**Fish anatomy**: The body divides into head, trunk, and tail. The skeleton is cartilage (cartilaginous fish) or bone (bony fish). The heart has two chambers pumping blood through gills in a single circulatory loop. Sharks and rays are basal fish with cartilaginous skeletons, dorso-ventrally flattened bodies, five pairs of gill slits, and no swim bladder. Bony fish have a bony skeleton, an operculum protecting gills, overlapping scales, and a swim bladder for maintaining depth.

**Amphibian anatomy**: Frogs, salamanders, and caecilians are tetrapods (though caecilians and some salamanders lack or have reduced limbs). Their hearts have three chambers (two atria, one ventricle). They breathe by buccal pumping and supplement with gas exchange through moist skin. Frogs have robust pelvic girdles and long hind legs; salamanders resemble lizards with long tails; caecilians are limbless and burrow.

**Reptile anatomy**: Turtles, tuataras, lizards, snakes, and crocodiles are tetrapods (snakes and some lizards lack limbs). Their bones are better ossified than amphibians', and horny scales create a waterproof layer. They have internal fertilization and amniotic eggs. Turtles have protective shells; tuataras have a parietal eye and primitive brain; lizards have flexible jaws; snakes have extreme jaw flexibility to swallow prey whole; crocodilians have four-chambered hearts, unlike other reptiles.

**Bird anatomy**: Birds are endothermic tetrapods with wings adapted for flight. Their long bones are thin, hollow, and light, with air sac extensions from the lungs occupying bone centers. They have no teeth, a horn-covered beak, and feathers that are outgrowths of the epidermis. A single uropygial gland produces oil that waterproofs feathers.

**Mammal anatomy**: Mammals mostly have four limbs situated below the trunk. Their teeth are differentiated and coated in prismatic enamel, shed once (milk teeth) or not at all. They have three middle ear bones, hair, sweat glands, and mammary glands producing milk. The heart has four chambers; nitrogenous waste is excreted primarily as urea. Most are viviparous with a placenta; monotremes (platypus, echidnas) lay eggs; marsupials have a short fetal stage with development completed in the pouch.

**Human anatomy**: Humans have a head, neck, trunk (thorax and abdomen), two arms and hands, and two legs and feet. Medical students learn gross and microscopic anatomy through models, textbooks, and dissection of cadavers. Human anatomy is taught regionally or systemically; Gray's Anatomy has been reorganized from systems to regional format. Physicians, especially surgeons and those in histopathology and radiology, require thorough anatomical knowledge.

### Invertebrate anatomy

Invertebrates constitute about 95% of animal species, from unicellular eukaryotes like *Paramecium* to complex animals like the octopus and lobster. None has a backbone. Metazoans have epithelium and connective tissue as the most basic tissue types. Exoskeletons are composed of chitin in arthropods, calcium carbonate in molluscs and brachiopods, and silica in diatoms and radiolaria. Marcello Malpighi, the father of microscopical anatomy, discovered that plants had tubules similar to those in insects.

**Arthropod anatomy**: Arthropods are the largest phylum, with over a million known species. Insects have segmented bodies with a hard exoskeleton of chitin, organized into head, thorax, and abdomen. The head bears antennae, compound eyes, ocelli, and mouthparts; the thorax has three pairs of legs and one or two pairs of wings. Spiders (arachnids) have four pairs of legs, a cephalothorax and abdomen, no wings or antennae, and mouthparts called chelicerae often connected to venom glands.

### Other branches

Surface anatomy studies anatomical landmarks visible from exterior contours, enabling medics to gauge deeper structures. Comparative anatomy compares structures across different animals. Artistic anatomy studies body proportions for artistic reasons.

### History

**Ancient**: The Edwin Smith Papyrus (1600 BCE) described the heart, vessels, brain, meninges, and cerebrospinal fluid. Hellenistic Alexandria housed the biggest medical library and great patronage from the Ptolemaic dynasty. Herophilus and Erasistratus (3rd century BCE) pioneered human dissection using cadavers of condemned criminals. Herophilus classified the pulse, discovered arteries had thicker walls than veins, identified the prostate gland, and recognized the brain as the "seat of intellect." Erasistratus distinguished sensory and motor nerves, described the brain's structure, and named the epiglottis and heart valves. In the 2nd century, Galen wrote the final influential anatomy treatise of ancient times, based mostly on dog anatomy, which became the only anatomical textbook for a thousand years.

**Medieval to early modern**: Anatomy developed little until the sixteenth century. Between 1275 and 1326, Mondino de Luzzi and others at Bologna carried out the first systematic human dissections since ancient times; Mondino's *Anatomy* (1316) was the standard textbook for a century. Leonardo da Vinci made many anatomical sketches. Andreas Vesalius (1514–1564) published *De humani corporis fabrica* in 1543, considered the founder of modern human anatomy. In England, anatomy was the subject of the first public lectures in any science.

**Late modern**: Medical schools in the United States needed cadavers, leading to body snatching; the Anatomy Act of 1832 halted the practice in Britain. Sir John Struthers set up the system of three years of "pre-clinical" academic teaching. Ignaz Semmelweis discovered that puerperal fever was caused by medical students going from the dissecting room to examine women in childbirth; washing hands in chlorinated lime dramatically reduced incidence. The advent of microscopy, achromatic lenses, the microtome, and staining techniques advanced histology. The electron microscope allowed research into ultrastructure, and X-ray diffraction gave rise to molecular anatomy in the 1950s. Non-invasive techniques including X-rays, magnetic resonance imaging, computed tomography, and ultrasound have enabled examination of internal structures in unprecedented detail.

## Terms

- **Histology**: The study of tissues on a microscopic scale.
- **Gross anatomy**: The examination of body parts using unaided eyesight; also called macroscopic anatomy.
- **Notochord**: A stiffening rod present in all vertebrates at some point in their lives, mostly in the embryonic stage.
- **Eumetazoans**: Animals with bodies differentiated into separate tissues, having an internal digestive chamber and a blastula stage.
- **Extracellular matrix**: The inorganic material among which connective tissue cells are scattered; contains collagen.
- **Endotherm**: An animal that maintains body temperature internally, such as birds.
- **Viviparous**: Giving birth to live young, as most mammals do.
- **Chelicerae**: Mouthparts of spiders, often connected to venom glands.
- **Buccal pumping**: A respiratory pump action in amphibians where air is drawn into the buccopharyngeal region then forced into the lungs.

## Debates and open questions

The source notes that anatomy is "a complex and dynamic field that is constantly evolving as discoveries are made." Historically, Herophilus's claim that the brain was the "seat of intellect" contradicted Aristotle's view that it was a "cooling chamber." The source also records that Galen's drawings, based mostly on dog anatomy, became the only anatomical textbook for a thousand years, implying questions about the accuracy of human anatomy knowledge during that period. The historian Marie Boas is quoted as finding the slowness of anatomical progress before the sixteenth century "as mysteriously slow as its development after 1500 is startlingly rapid," a scholarly puzzle about why the field stagnated for so long.
