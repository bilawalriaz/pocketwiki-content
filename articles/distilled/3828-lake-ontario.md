# Lake Ontario

## Overview
Lake Ontario is the easternmost and smallest by surface area of North America's five Great Lakes, forming a vital link in the Great Lakes–St. Lawrence Seaway system. It serves as the final outlet for the Great Lakes chain to the Atlantic Ocean and is a critical ecological, economic, and historical resource for both Canada and the United States.

## Timeline
- **c. 11,000 BP** — First archaeological evidence of human presence in the region.
- **c. 4000 BP** — Lake Ontario reaches its modern water level.
- **1615** — Étienne Brûlé is believed to be the first European to reach the lake.
- **1673** — Fort Frontenac is established by the French at what is now Kingston, Ontario.
- **1784** — The first private commercial vessel, the *Good Intent*, operates on the lake.
- **1816** — The *Frontenac*, the first steamer on the Great Lakes, is launched.
- **1818** — The Rush–Bagot Treaty demilitarizes the Great Lakes.
- **1857** — Propeller ships outnumber side-wheel steamers on the lake.
- **1934** — The lake freezes over completely for the last recorded time.
- **1954** — Marilyn Bell becomes the first person to swim across the lake.
- **2016** — A $150-million cleanup project begins at the contaminated Randle Reef. |
- **2024** — The Lake Ontario National Marine Sanctuary is designated in U.S. waters.

## Body

### Geography and Geology
Lake Ontario is the 13th-largest lake in the world, with a surface area of 7,340 square miles (19,011 km²) and a maximum depth of 802 feet (244 m). It is the lowest in elevation of the Great Lakes at 243 feet (74 m) above sea level. The lake basin was carved by the Wisconsin ice sheet during the last ice age. As the ice retreated, the lake was once much higher (Lake Iroquois) and later briefly became a bay of the Atlantic Ocean (Champlain Sea). The land is still rebounding from the ice's weight, causing the lake bed to tilt southward, which accelerates shoreline erosion on the southern shore.

### Climate and Ecology
The lake's climate is moderated by its waters, creating microclimates that support fruit-growing regions on its shores. It is a major source of lake-effect snow, particularly on its southeastern shore and the Tug Hill Plateau. The lake's ecology is characterized by high biodiversity, especially in its wetlands, which are vital for migratory birds and fish. However, these ecosystems are threatened by stabilized water levels, pollution, and invasive species. As the most downstream Great Lake, it accumulates pollution from the entire basin, making it the most environmentally stressed of the five Great Lakes.

### Human History and Settlement
The name "Ontario" derives from the Wyandot (Huron) word for "beautiful lake." The lake was a border between the Wendat and Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy before European contact. French and later British colonial powers established trading posts and forts, leading to conflicts like the War of 1812. After demilitarization, the lake became a hub for commerce and shipbuilding. Today, the Canadian shore is densely populated, with the Golden Horseshoe conurbation including Toronto and Hamilton, while the American shore is largely rural, centered on Rochester.

### Navigation and Economy
The lake is a key part of the Great Lakes Waterway, connected to the Atlantic via the St. Lawrence Seaway and to Lake Erie via the Welland Canal. It also links to other waterways like the Trent-Severn and Oswego Canals. Major ports include Toronto and Hamilton, with Hamilton being a center for steel production. Historically, the lake supported an industrial-scale fishery, but today only recreational fishing remains.

## Terms
*   **Seiche:** A standing wave in an enclosed or partially enclosed body of water, with Lake Ontario having a natural rhythm of about eleven minutes.
*   **Lake-effect snow:** Snowfall produced when cold winds pass over the warmer lake waters, picking up moisture and depositing it as heavy snow on downwind shores.
*   **Drumlin:** An elongated hill in the shape of an inverted spoon or half-buried egg, formed by glacial ice acting on underlying unconsolidated till.
*   **Eutrophication:** The process by which a body of water becomes overly enriched with minerals and nutrients, inducing excessive growth of algae.
*   **Haudenosaunee Confederacy:** A group of six Native American nations (also known as the Iroquois League) historically based in the region.
*   **Rush–Bagot Treaty:** An 1818 agreement between the U.S. and Britain that limited naval armaments on the Great Lakes.
*   **Golden Horseshoe:** A densely populated and industrialized region surrounding the western end of Lake Ontario in Ontario, Canada.
*   **Stone hooker:** A type of scow used in the 19th and early 20th centuries to rake flat shale fragments from the lake floor for construction.

The source notes ongoing ecological challenges, such as the impact of stabilized water levels on wetland diversity and the invasion of cattails.
