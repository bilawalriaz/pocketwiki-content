# Politics

## Overview

Politics is the activity of settling affairs in an organized society, usually concerned with resolving issues via a government. It involves methods such as promoting views, negotiation, making laws, and exercising force, and operates at levels from clans to sovereign states to the international sphere. The branch of social science studying politics is political science. The word often carries a negative connotation, but can also be used positively, as in a "political solution" that is compromising and non-violent.

## Timeline

- **c. 3000 BC** — Early dynastic Sumer and Egypt arise as the first states with defined borders
- **4th century BC** — Greek city-states grant citizenship rights; Athens develops direct democracy
- **1430** — The singular "politic" first attested in English
- **1648** — Peace of Westphalia begins the modern international system
- **18th century** — Emer de Vattel lays out the principle of non-interference in domestic affairs
- **19th century** — Nationalism rises; Germany and Italy unify; nation-state model spreads
- **After WWI** — Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman Empires dissolve; League of Nations founded
- **After WWII** — League of Nations replaced by the United Nations
- **20th century** — Political globalization proceeds via intergovernmental organizations

## Body

### Etymology

The English word "politics" derives from the Greek *polis* ("city"). Aristotle's *Politiká* used the Ancient Greek term *politiká* ('affairs of the cities'). In the mid-15th century, it was rendered in Early Modern English as *Polettiques*, becoming *Politics* in Modern English. The singular "politic" first appeared in English in 1430, from Middle French *politique*, itself from Latin *politicus* and Greek *politikos* ('citizen').

### Approaches

Adrian Leftwich distinguished views of politics by how extensive or limited their perception of the "political" is. The extensive view sees politics across all human social relations; the limited view restricts it to contexts like governance. A feminist perspective argues traditionally non-political sites should be political—encapsulated in the slogan "the personal is political." Politics may also be defined by the use of power, as argued by Robert A. Dahl.

Some perspectives view politics empirically as power exercise (political realism), others as a social function with a normative basis (political moralism). For moralists like Hannah Arendt, Aristotle held that "to be political…meant that everything was decided through words and persuasion and not through violence." Realists, including Machiavelli, Hobbes, and Lasswell, see politics as reflecting human desire for rule and self-interest.

Agonism argues politics is essentially conflict between interests. Elmer Schattschneider claimed "at the root of all politics is the universal language of conflict"; Carl Schmitt saw the essence as the friend/foe distinction. Michael Laver offered a mixed view: "Politics is about the characteristic blend of conflict and co-operation…Pure conflict is war. Pure co-operation is true love."

### History

Early human bands and tribes lacked centralized political structures—stateless societies. Ancient civilizations had frontiers rather than definite boundaries; early dynastic Sumer and Egypt were first to define borders. Up to the 12th century, many lived in non-state societies ranging from egalitarian bands to stratified chiefdoms.

State formation theories include voluntary theories (shared rational interest, often tied to agriculture) and the hydraulic hypothesis (states result from needing large-scale irrigation). Conflict theories hold that states form through oppression of one group by another; some argue warfare was critical.

The first states arose around 3000 BC from the Uruk period (Sumer) and Predynastic Egypt. Egyptians, Romans, and Greeks first explicitly formulated political philosophy and rationally analyzed institutions; prior states were justified by religious myths. Greek city-states granted citizenship to free populations; Athens combined this with direct democracy.

The Peace of Westphalia (1648) began the modern international system, with external powers avoiding interference in domestic affairs—a principle laid out by Emer de Vattel in the mid-18th century. The 19th-century rise of nationalism bolstered the "Westphalian" doctrine, assuming legitimate states correspond to nations. Classic multinational empires included the Austrian, French, Russian, Spanish, Ottoman, and British Empires; Caliphates developed similarly in the Muslim world after Muhammad's death in 632.

Most theories see the nation state as a 19th-century European phenomenon, facilitated by state-mandated education, mass literacy, and mass media. Some scholars (Steven Weber, Michel Foucault, Jeremy Black) argue the nation state was an inadvertent byproduct of 15th-century discoveries in political economy, capitalism, cartography, and map-making. Germany and Italy unified partly through nationalist campaigns; German unification was preceded by the Zollverein customs union. Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points promoted national self-determination, leading to dissolution of the Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman Empires after WWI. Decolonization created new nation states in the Third World.

Political globalization began in the 20th century through intergovernmental organizations. The League of Nations (after WWI) was replaced by the United Nations (after WWII). Regional integration includes BRICS, the African Union, ASEAN, the European Union, and Mercosur. International institutions include the International Criminal Court, IMF, and WTO.

### Political science

Political science comprises three subfields: comparative politics (comparing constitutions, political actors, legislatures), international relations (interactions between nation-states and transnational organizations), and political philosophy (justice, legitimacy, liberty). It draws on economics, law, sociology, history, philosophy, geography, psychology, psychiatry, anthropology, and neurosciences.

### Political system

David Easton defined a political system as "the interactions through which values are authoritatively allocated for a society." Governments are generally monarchies or republics (presidential, semi-presidential, or parliamentary); republics are most common. Regimes can be classified by who rules: democracy (many), oligarchy (few), or autocracy (one). By vertical integration: confederations, federations, and unitary states.

The sovereign state was defined by Max Weber as having a monopoly on violence within its territory; the Montevideo Convention requires a defined territory, permanent population, government, and capacity for international relations. Stateless societies have little concentrated authority. Anarchism advocates society without states; Marxism holds the state would "wither away" in a post-capitalist society.

Constitutions are written documents limiting government powers; an unwritten constitution is continually written by legislative and judiciary branches. England set the fashion of written constitutions during the Civil War but abandoned them after the Restoration; they were later taken up by American Colonies, France after the Revolution, and the rest of Europe.

Political culture describes how culture impacts politics. Lucian Pye defined it as "the set of attitudes, beliefs, and sentiments, which give order and meaning to a political process." Trust is a major factor; postmaterialism concerns non-material issues like human rights and environmentalism. Political dysfunctions include gridlock and voter suppression. "Proxy politics" refers to influence through proxy parties or proxy wars.

### Levels of politics

Macropolitics covers issues affecting an entire political system or interactions between systems. Global politics crosses national borders, including cities, nation-states, multinational corporations, and NGOs. International relations may be peaceful (diplomacy) or violent (war). States with strong influence are superpowers; the world order is affected by the balance of power. Domestic politics includes public policy executed by state bureaucracy.

Mesopolitics describes intermediary structures like political parties. Parties seek political power through campaigns, education, or protest, espousing an ideology with a written platform. Party systems can be multiparty, two-party, dominant-party, or one-party. According to Duverger's law, first-past-the-post systems likely lead to two-party systems; proportional representation creates multiparty systems.

Micropolitics describes individual actors' actions within the political system—often called political participation.

### Political values

Democracy is a system where outcomes depend on participants' actions, with inherent uncertainty; no single force controls outcomes. Three conceptions exist: aggregative, deliberative, and radical.

Aggregative democracy solicits and aggregates citizen preferences, primarily through voting. Minimalism (Schumpeter) holds citizens cannot "rule" because they lack clear views. Direct democracy proponents argue citizens should vote directly on legislation; participation socializes and educates citizens and checks elites. Anthony Downs (1957, *An Economic Theory of Democracy*) suggested ideological parties act as mediating brokers.

Robert Dahl's polyarchy requires free and open elections selecting representatives; but poverty may prevent full democracy. Ronald Dworkin argued "democracy is a substantive, not a merely procedural, ideal." Deliberative democracy requires authentic deliberation—free from distortions of unequal power—before voting by majority rule.

Equality means all people have the same social status, including human rights, dignity, and access to social goods. It requires absence of legally enforced class or caste boundaries and discrimination based on inalienable identity aspects.

The left–right spectrum dates from the French Revolution: supporters of the republic sat on the left, monarchy supporters on the right. The left is generally progressivist; the far-left supports revolutionary means (Communism, Marxism), while center-left favors reform (social democracy). The right is conservative; the far-right is reactionary (Fascism, Nazism). Norberto Bobbio held the left seeks to eradicate social inequality while the right sees it as resulting from natural inequalities. Christian Democracy claims to combine left and right; Fascist Terza Posizione and Peronism claimed to be above the divide.

Political freedom includes negative liberty (freedom from coercion) and positive liberty (absence of disabling conditions, fulfillment of enabling conditions). Authoritarianism subjugates individual rights to group goals; libertarianism holds the individual as sovereign, with anarchists advocating total abolition of the state. Classical liberalism stresses individual freedom, property rights, free markets, and limited government, as in the writings of John Locke, Adam Smith, and others.

## Terms

- **Polis**: Greek city-state; root of the word "politics"
- **Stateless society**: Society not governed by a state, with little concentrated authority
- **Hydraulic hypothesis**: Theory that states formed from the need to build large-scale irrigation projects
- **Westphalian doctrine**: Principle that states are independent agents and external powers should not interfere in domestic affairs
- **Zollverein**: Customs union preceding German unification
- **Polyarchy**: Dahl's term for societies with institutions and procedures leading to democracy, especially free elections
- **Duverger's law**: First-past-the-post systems tend toward two-party systems; proportional representation toward multiparty systems
- **Negative liberty**: Freedom from oppression, coercion, and unreasonable external constraints
- **Positive liberty**: Absence of disabling conditions and fulfillment of enabling conditions
- **Postmaterialism**: Degree a political culture concerns non-material issues like human rights and environmentalism

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether state formation can be generalized: some seek universal theories (voluntary vs. conflict theories), while others argue each case should be treated on its own. The origin of the nation state is contested—some see it as a 19th-century European phenomenon, while others argue it was an inadvertent byproduct of 15th-century intellectual discoveries in political economy and cartography. The extensive vs. limited view of what counts as "political" remains contested, as does the moralism/realism distinction—whether politics is fundamentally about ethics or about power and self-interest.
