# Sofia

## Overview
Sofia is the capital and largest city of Bulgaria, situated in the Sofia Valley at the foot of Vitosha mountain. With a history of human habitation dating back to at least 7000 BC, it has been a significant political, economic, and cultural center for millennia, serving as the capital of the Roman province of Dacia Mediterranea, a major city in the Bulgarian Empires, and the regional capital of the Ottoman province of Rumelia. Its selection as the capital of modern Bulgaria in 1879 initiated a period of rapid growth, and it remains the country's primary hub for government, economy, and culture.

## Timeline
- **c. 7000 BC** — Earliest evidence of human habitation in the area.
- **29 BC** — Roman Republic conquers the Celtic tribe Serdi, establishing the city of Serdica.
- **311 AD** — Emperor Galerius issues the Edict of Toleration in Serdica, legalizing Christianity.
- **809** — Khan Krum incorporates Serdica into the First Bulgarian Empire; it becomes known as Sredets.
- **1018** — Byzantine Empire conquers Bulgaria, ending the First Bulgarian Empire's rule over Sredets.
- **1194** — Sredets is reincorporated into the restored Second Bulgarian Empire.
- **1382/1383** — Ottoman Empire captures Sredets after a siege.
- **1530** — Sofia becomes the capital of the Ottoman province of Rumelia Eyalet.
- **1878** — Russian forces liberate Sofia from Ottoman rule during the Russo-Turkish War.
- **1879** — Sofia is selected as the capital of the newly established Third Bulgarian State.
- **1944** — Sofia is occupied by the Soviet Red Army; a communist government takes power in 1945.
- **1990** — Bulgaria transitions from a People's Republic to a Republic, marking the end of communist rule.

## Body

### History and Names
The area has been inhabited since the Neolithic period, attracted by hot springs. The Celtic tribe Serdi gave their name to the city, first recorded as Serdica in the 1st century BC. It became a significant Roman city, capital of the Diocese of Dacia, and a place where Christianity was early legalized. After periods of destruction and rebuilding, it was incorporated into the First Bulgarian Empire in 809 and renamed Sredets. The city alternated between Bulgarian and Byzantine rule until its conquest by the Ottomans in the late 14th century. Under Ottoman rule, it was the capital of Rumelia Eyalet. The modern name "Sofia" derives from the medieval Saint Sofia Church and was officially adopted after liberation in 1878, though the Slavic name Sredets was also used for a time.

### Geography and Climate
Sofia is located in the Sofia Valley, surrounded by mountains including Vitosha to the south and the Balkan Mountains to the north. It is the third-highest capital city in Europe. The city is known for its 49 mineral and thermal springs. It has a humid continental climate with cold, snowy winters and warm summers. The valley's geography limits air circulation, leading to significant air pollution from particulate matter and traffic, a persistent environmental challenge.

### Government and Economy
As the national capital, Sofia is the seat of all branches of the Bulgarian government. It is the country's economic hub, hosting most major Bulgarian and international companies. The economy is dominated by the service sector (88.6%), with dynamic growth in information technology and high-value manufacturing. Sofia is a regional IT hub and was ranked by Forbes as a top global city for startups due to low corporate tax and fast internet.

### Culture and Demographics
Sofia concentrates Bulgaria's leading cultural institutions, including the National Opera and Ballet, the Ivan Vazov National Theatre, and numerous museums. The city is a "triangle of religious tolerance," with close proximity of major Christian, Muslim, and Jewish temples. The Boyana Church, a UNESCO World Heritage site, is a key cultural landmark. The population is approximately 1.3 million, making it home to about 17.9% of Bulgaria's total population. The city is ethnically diverse, with a majority of ethnic Bulgarians and significant minorities.

### Transport and Infrastructure
Sofia is a major transport hub for international railway and road networks. Public transport includes an extensive bus, tram, and trolleybus network, and a metro system that opened in 1998 and continues to expand. Vasil Levski Sofia Airport is the main international gateway. The city has a comprehensive district heating system and faces challenges with traffic congestion and road maintenance.

## Terms
*   **Serdica/Sredets:** The ancient and medieval names for the city, derived from the Celtic tribe Serdi and a Slavic word meaning "middle," respectively.
*   **Rumelia Eyalet:** The Ottoman Empire's largest and most important province in Europe, for which Sofia served as the capital from 1530 to 1836.
*   **Edict of Toleration:** A 311 AD decree issued by Emperor Galerius in Serdica that ended the persecution of Christians and implicitly legalized the religion.
*   **Chitalishte:** A traditional Bulgarian cultural institution, combining a library, community center, and theater; the first in Sofia was inaugurated in 1867.
*   **Shopi:** The local term for the inhabitants of the rural suburbs around Sofia, who speak Western Bulgarian dialects.
*   **Pan-European Transport Corridors:** Major international transport routes; three (IV, VIII, and X) cross Sofia.
*   **Beta global city:** A ranking by the Globalization and World Cities Research Network indicating Sofia's importance in the global economic system.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Name Dispute:** After liberation in 1878, there was a debate over whether the new capital should be named Sofia or the Slavic Sredets. A compromise was reached, but Sofia eventually became the sole official name.
*   **Air Pollution Management:** The effectiveness of measures to combat Sofia's severe air pollution is a subject of ongoing concern and criticism, as noted by the European Court of Auditors and the European Commission.
*   **Urban Development:** The end of communist-era planning has led to debates about "chaotic and unrestrained construction" in the city, contrasting with the organized development of previous eras.
