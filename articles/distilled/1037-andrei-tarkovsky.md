# Andrei Tarkovsky

## Overview
Andrei Tarkovsky (1932–1986) was a Soviet film director and screenwriter widely regarded as one of cinema’s greatest auteurs. His seven feature films—marked by metaphysical themes, extremely long takes, and a "sculpting in time" aesthetic that prioritizes emotional resonance over narrative efficiency—explore faith, memory, and the artist’s spiritual duty against a backdrop of modern materialism. After early acclaim for *Ivan’s Childhood* (1962), escalating censorship and production battles over *Andrei Rublev* (1966), *Mirror* (1975), and *Stalker* (1979) drove him into exile in 1979. His final works, *Nostalghia* (1983) and *The Sacrifice* (1986), were made in Italy and Sweden. He died of lung cancer in Paris, possibly linked to toxic locations used during *Stalker*’s filming. His theoretical book *Sculpting in Time* (1986) codified his view of cinema as a medium that alters the viewer’s experience of time.

## Timeline
- **4 April 1932** — Born in Zavrazhye, Ivanovo Industrial Oblast, to poet Arseny Tarkovsky and Maria Vishnyakova.
- **1937–1943** — Father leaves family in 1937; volunteers for WWII in 1941; returns in 1943 with leg wound (later amputated due to gangrene). Family evacuates to Yuryevets during war.
- **1951–1952** — Studies Arabic at Oriental Institute; drops out in 1952 to work as prospector; participates in year-long taiga expedition to river Kureyka.
- **1954–1960** — Enrolls at State Institute of Cinematography (VGIK) under Mikhail Romm; marries Irma Raush (1957); graduates with diploma film *The Steamroller and the Violin* (1960).
- **1962** — Directs debut feature *Ivan’s Childhood*; wins Golden Lion at Venice Film Festival; son Arseny born.
- **1966** — Completes *Andrei Rublev*; Soviet authorities suppress wide release until 1971; wins FIPRESCI prize at Cannes 1969.
- **1970** — Divorces Irma Raush; marries Larisa Kizilova; son Andrei Jr. born.
- **1972** — Releases *Solaris* (adapted from Stanisław Lem); wins Grand Prix Spécial du Jury at Cannes.
- **1975** — Releases *Mirror*; Soviet authorities assign "third category" limited distribution.
- **1979** — Completes *Stalker* after ruined negatives and heart attack; wins Prize of the Ecumenical Jury at Cannes; leaves USSR for Italy.
- **1983** — Releases *Nostalghia* (Italy); wins FIPRESCI and Ecumenical Jury prizes at Cannes; Soviet lobbying blocks Palme d’Or.
- **10 July 1984** — Announces at Milan press conference he will not return to USSR; son Andrei Jr. still not allowed to leave.
- **28 Aug 1985** — Processed as Soviet defector at Latina refugee camp, Italy.
- **1986** — Publishes *Sculpting in Time*; shoots *The Sacrifice* in Sweden with Sven Nykvist; diagnosed with terminal lung cancer at year's end.
- **Jan 1986** — Begins treatment in Paris; son Andrei Jr. finally allowed to leave Soviet Union and joins him.
- **29 Dec 1986** — Dies in Neuilly-sur-Seine, France; buried in Sainte-Geneviève-des-Bois.
- **1990** — Posthumously awarded Lenin Prize.

## Body

### Childhood and Formative Experiences
Tarkovsky’s upbringing fused aristocratic lineage (Polish noble paternal grandfather; Russian noble maternal grandmother) with Soviet disruption. His father, poet Arseny Tarkovsky, left the family in 1937, subsequently volunteering for the army in 1941. He returned home in 1943, having been awarded the Order of the Red Star after being shot in one of his legs, which he would eventually need to have amputated due to gangrene. Wartime evacuation to Yuryevets, a year hospitalized with tuberculosis (1947–48), and a strained paternal relationship became core material for *Mirror*. A poor student, he studied Arabic at the Oriental Institute from 1951 to 1952. He did not finish his studies and dropped out in 1952 to work as a prospector for the Academy of Science Institute for Non-Ferrous Metals and Gold. He participated in a year-long research expedition to the river Kureyka near Turukhansk in the Krasnoyarsk Province. During this time in the taiga, Tarkovsky decided to study film.

### Film Education and Early Influences
At VGIK (1954–60), mentor Mikhail Romm granted students unusual autonomy during the Khrushchev Thaw, which opened Soviet screens to Italian neorealism, French New Wave, and Japanese cinema (Kurosawa, Mizoguchi, Ozu). Tarkovsky cited Bresson, Bergman, Buñuel, Dreyer, and Dovzhenko as formative; he called Dovzhenko’s *Earth* his "cinematic university." He co-wrote early scripts with classmate Andrei Konchalovsky, graduating with *The Steamroller and the Violin* (1960), which won a New York Student Film Festival prize.

### Soviet-Era Features: Censorship and Compromise
*Ivan’s Childhood* (1962), inherited from a stalled project, brought immediate international fame (Venice Golden Lion). *Andrei Rublev* (1966), a 15th-century icon painter’s biography budgeted at over 1 million rubles, faced years of state suppression; Tarkovsky cut multiple versions before a truncated 1971 Soviet release, though a 1969 Cannes screening won the FIPRESCI prize. *Solaris* (1972), a Stanisław Lem adaptation co-written with Friedrich Gorenstein, earned the Cannes Grand Prix Spécial du Jury but frustrated Tarkovsky, who disliked sci-fi’s "comic book" trappings. *Mirror* (1975), a fragmented autobiographical collage incorporating his father’s poems, was relegated to "third category" distribution (workers’ clubs, few prints), endangering his career. *Stalker* (1979), adapted from the Strugatsky brothers’ *Roadside Picnic*, suffered ruined exterior negatives, a cinematographer change (Georgy Rerberg to Alexander Knyazhinsky), and Tarkovsky’s 1978 heart attack. He rejected readings of the film as political allegory or impenetrable mystery.

### Exile and Final Films
After shooting the documentary *Voyage in Time* (1982) with Tonino Guerra in Italy, Tarkovsky made *Nostalghia* (1983) with Italian RAI funding after Mosfilm withdrew. Soviet lobbying at Cannes to block the Palme d’Or solidified his resolve never to return. He staged *Boris Godunov* at London’s Royal Opera House (1983), then announced permanent Western residence in Milan (1984). At that time, his son Andriosha was still in the Soviet Union and not allowed to leave the country. *The Sacrifice* (1986), shot in Sweden with Bergman alumni (cinematographer Sven Nykvist, actor Erland Josephson), explored apocalypse and redemption. During production, in a particularly poignant scene in the making-of documentary *Directed by Andrei Tarkovsky*, Tarkovsky expresses his sentiments on death—he claims himself to be immortal and has no fear of dying. Ironically, at the end of the year Tarkovsky was diagnosed with terminal lung cancer. In January 1986, he began treatment in Paris and was joined there by his son, Andre Jr, who was finally allowed to leave the Soviet Union. The film won Cannes’ Grand Prix Spécial du Jury, FIPRESCI, and Ecumenical Jury prizes, accepted by his son.

### Theoretical Framework: "Sculpting in Time"
Tarkovsky argued cinema’s unique property is recording time in real time; long takes and minimal cuts let viewers *feel* time passing, lost, and the relation between moments. Up to *Mirror*, he explored this theory; afterward, he pursued Aristotelian unities (single action, place, day). He dismissed color film as a "commercial gimmick" (1966), using it sparingly for emphasis (e.g., *Andrei Rublev*’s color epilogue of icons; sepia in *Stalker*). Recurring motifs—water, fire, rain indoors, levitation, reflections, bells, candles—serve as photogenic symbols of self-reflection and the spiritual realm. He viewed the artist’s duty as ascetic self-surrender to a higher purpose, mirroring characters in *Andrei Rublev*, *Stalker*, and *The Sacrifice*.

### Spiritual and Philosophical Orientation
Tarkovsky’s eclectic spirituality drew from Christian mysticism, existentialism (Dostoevsky), paganism, anthroposophy, and Eastern traditions (Taoism, Zen). He rejected institutional labels, stating the film itself mattered more than his personal creed. He critiqued Western rationalism, materialism, and egoism as antithetical to artistic ideals of beauty, truth, and selflessness—a critique sharpened during exile. His films stage a dualistic conflict between Western decay and Eastern spiritual wholeness. He believed the artistic image is humanity’s sole creation born of self-surrender, evidence that humans are made in God’s likeness.

### Collaborators and Visual Style
Cinematographer Vadim Yusov (1958–72) shaped the visual language of the Soviet features; Tarkovsky spent days preparing single long takes, often requiring only one. For *The Sacrifice*, Sven Nykvist (Bergman’s longtime DP) initially chafed at Tarkovsky directing actors through the camera but called the collaboration a career highlight. Ingmar Bergman called Tarkovsky "the greatest," who "invented a new language, true to the nature of film, as it captures life as a reflection, life as a dream." Bergman also noted the risk of self-repetition ("self-karaoke") in late-career auteurs, including Tarkovsky.

### Death and Posthumous Recognition
Tarkovsky died 29 December 1986 in Paris; his funeral was held at Alexander Nevsky Cathedral, burial in Sainte-Geneviève-des-Bois. His wife Larisa, actor Anatoly Solonitsyn, and sound designer Vladimir Sharun all died of similar cancers; Sharun attributed them to chemical exposure upstream from the *Stalker* shoot. Posthumous honors include the 1990 Lenin Prize, the Andrei Tarkovsky Memorial Prize (first recipient Yuri Norstein), a minor planet (3345 Tarkovskij), and the Yuryevets museum (1996). His diaries (*Time Within Time*, 1989) and *Sculpting in Time* remain foundational texts.

## Terms
- **Sculpting in Time**: Tarkovsky’s theory that cinema’s essence is altering the viewer’s experience of time through long takes and minimal editing, making time itself the medium’s material.
- **Long take**: An extended, uninterrupted shot (often minutes long) that preserves real-time duration and spatial continuity, central to Tarkovsky’s style.
- **Khrushchev Thaw**: Post-1953 relaxation of Soviet cultural restrictions allowing limited exposure to foreign art, crucial for Tarkovsky’s film education.
- **Third category**: Soviet distribution classification severely limiting screenings to workers’ clubs and third-class cinemas, denying returns and risking accusations of wasting state funds.
- **The Zone**: In *Stalker*, a forbidden, anomalous area granting wishes; became a metaphor for artistic/spiritual quest in later criticism (e.g., Chris Marker’s *Sans Soleil*).
- **Ecumenical Jury**: Independent Cannes jury awarding films with spiritual/moral depth; Tarkovsky won its prize three times, a record.
- **FIPRESCI prize**: Award by the International Federation of Film Critics; Tarkovsky won at Venice (*Ivan’s Childhood*), Cannes (*Andrei Rublev*, *Nostalghia*, *The Sacrifice*).
- **Aristotelian unities**: Classical dramatic principles (action, place, time) Tarkovsky adopted post-*Mirror* to concentrate narrative structure.
- **Self-karaoke**: Bergman’s term for late-career auteurs repeating their own stylistic signatures; he acknowledged Tarkovsky’s susceptibility.
- **Lenin Prize**: One of the USSR’s highest state honors, awarded posthumously to Tarkovsky in 1990 during Glasnost/Perestroika.

## Debates and open questions
- **Cause of cancer**: Whether Tarkovsky’s (and Larisa Tarkovskaya’s, Anatoly Solonitsyn’s) lung cancer resulted from toxic chemical exposure at the *Stalker* filming locations (downstream from a chemical plant) remains unproven but is argued by sound designer Vladimir Sharun.
- **Political allegory in *Stalker***: Tarkovsky explicitly rejected readings of the film as a political allegory or impenetrable mystery, yet critics persist in interpreting the Zone as a metaphor for Soviet dissent or spiritual resistance.
- **Autobiography vs. fiction in *Mirror***: The film’s fragmented blend of personal memory, newsreel, and father’s poems resists stable categorization; scholars debate whether it constitutes a new autobiographical form or a deliberate dissolution of genre.
- **Color vs. monochrome aesthetics**: Tarkovsky’s 1966 dismissal of color as a "gimmick" contrasts with his later selective use (icon epilogue, sepia sequences); the tension between his theoretical stance and practice is unresolved.
- **Late style: development or repetition?**: Bergman’s "self-karaoke" critique raises whether *Nostalghia* and *The Sacrifice* deepen Tarkovsky’s project or reiterate it in exile, a debate informed by his shift to Aristotelian unities and Western crews.
- **Solaris as adaptation**: Tarkovsky’s dissatisfaction with Lem’s novel and the sci-fi genre complicates assessments of *Solaris* as either a failed genre exercise or a deliberate subversion of it.
