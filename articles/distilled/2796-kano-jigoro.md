# Kanō Jigorō

## Overview
Kanō Jigorō (1860–1938) was a Japanese educator and martial artist who founded judo, transforming traditional jūjutsu into a modern system of physical, mental, and moral education. His innovations, including the dan ranking system and the codification of judo's principles, made it one of the first Japanese martial arts to gain international recognition and an official Olympic sport. Beyond judo, he was a pivotal figure in Japanese education and international sports, serving as the first Asian member of the International Olympic Committee (IOC).

## Timeline
- **10 December 1860** — Born in Mikage, Japan (now part of Kobe).
- **1877** — Entered Tokyo Imperial University.
- **1882** — Graduated and founded the Kodokan jūjutsu dojo with 12 mats.
- **1883** — Received menkyo (teaching credential) in Kitō-ryū jūjutsu.
- **1888** — Co-presented a lecture on jūjutsu to the Asiatic Society of Japan.
- **1909** — Became the first Asian member of the International Olympic Committee (IOC).
- **1915** — Defined judo as "the way of the highest or most efficient use of both physical and mental energy."
- **1922** — Authorized Gichin Funakoshi's karate exhibition in Tokyo; founded the Kodokan Cultural Association.
- **1927** — Created the "Maximum-Efficiency National Physical Education" system.
- **4 May 1938** — Died at sea while traveling as an IOC member.

## Body

### Early Life and Jūjutsu Training
Born to a sake-brewing family, Kanō was educated in neo-Confucian classics and Western languages. Bullied for his small stature, he sought self-defense training and began studying Tenjin Shin'yō-ryū jūjutsu under Fukuda Hachinosuke in 1877. Fukuda emphasized free practice (*randori*) over ritual forms. After Fukuda's death, Kanō studied under Iso Masatomo, a specialist in kata and striking (*atemi*). He later learned the throwing techniques of Kitō-ryū from Iikubo Tsunetoshi, who granted him a full teaching credential (*menkyo*) in 1883. This synthesis of schools provided the technical foundation for his new art.

### Founding and Development of Kodokan Judo
In 1882, Kanō established the Kodokan dojo, initially teaching a system he named "jūdō" (the gentle way). He combined Kitō-ryū's throwing techniques with Tenjin Shin'yō-ryū's choking and pinning techniques. The Kodokan grew from a dozen students to over a thousand *dan*-graded members by 1911. Kanō's pedagogical innovations included the *dan* ranking system to indicate proficiency and the use of black and white belts. He codified judo's dual purpose: as a martial art (*shobu-ho*) and as a system for physical and moral development (*rentai-ho* and *shushin-ho*), encapsulated in his mottoes "maximum efficiency, minimal effort" (*seiryoku zen'yō*) and "mutual welfare and benefit" (*jita kyōei*).

### Educational and International Career
Kanō earned a living as a prominent educator, serving as president of Tokyo Higher Normal School (1900–1920) and director of primary education for the Ministry of Education (1898–1901). He championed the inclusion of judo and kendo in Japanese public schools. In 1909, he became the first Asian IOC member, representing Japan at multiple Olympic Games and leading Japan's bid for the 1940 Olympics. While he was passive about including judo in the Olympics, viewing it as more than a sport, his international work significantly raised its profile. He also promoted other sports, helped systematize karate in Japan, and introduced Western strength training methods.

### Philosophy and Legacy
Kanō envisioned judo as a path to self-perfection and societal contribution. He established the Kodokan Cultural Association in 1922 to promote judo's philosophical principles. His "Maximum-Efficiency National Physical Education" (1927) applied judo principles to general fitness. He died in 1938, but his legacy endured as judo spread globally, becoming an Olympic sport for men in 1964 and for women in 1992. He was inducted into the International Judo Federation Hall of Fame in 1999.

## Terms
- **Jūjutsu**: The traditional Japanese martial art of grappling, the precursor to judo.
- **Randori**: Free-form practice or sparring, a core training method in judo.
- **Kata**: Pre-arranged forms or patterns of techniques, used to preserve and teach traditional movements.
- **Dan**: A ranking system indicating a practitioner's relative skill level, pioneered by Kanō.
- **Menkyo**: A traditional Japanese license or certificate granting full teaching authority in a martial art.
- **Seiryoku zen'yō**: Kanō's principle of "maximum efficiency, minimal effort" in the use of mind and body.
- **Jita kyōei**: Kanō's principle of "mutual welfare and benefit" for oneself and others.
- **Kodokan**: The headquarters and governing body of judo, founded by Kanō in 1882.
- **Atemi**: Striking techniques targeting vital areas, part of traditional jūjutsu but not emphasized in judo *randori*.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact cause of Kanō's death in 1938 is officially listed as pneumonia, but other sources suggest food poisoning. Allegations of assassination by poisoning have surfaced, linked to his opposition to Japanese militarism, though no contemporary documentation supports this claim.
- Kanō's personal stance on including judo in the Olympics was "rather passive," viewing it as a principle of life rather than a mere sport. This contrasts with judo's eventual status as a core Olympic sport, raising questions about how his vision aligns with its modern competitive form.
