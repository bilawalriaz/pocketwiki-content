# Michael Faraday

## Overview
Michael Faraday (1791–1867) was an English chemist and physicist whose experimental discoveries established the foundations of electrochemistry and electromagnetism. Despite minimal formal education and limited mathematical training, he discovered electromagnetic induction, diamagnetism, and the laws of electrolysis, and invented the first electric motor and dynamo. His concept of the electromagnetic field—visualized as "lines of force"—revolutionized physics, later formalized mathematically by James Clerk Maxwell into the field theory underpinning modern technology. Faraday also made major contributions to chemistry, discovering benzene, liquefying gases, and standardizing terminology such as *anode*, *cathode*, and *ion*. A devout Sandemanian Christian, he declined honours including a knighthood and the presidency of the Royal Society, devoting his later life to public service, scientific education, and ethical advocacy.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Faraday was born into a poor Glasite (Sandemanian) family; his father was a blacksmith. With only basic schooling, he entered a seven-year bookbinding apprenticeship at age 14. He exploited access to books—particularly Isaac Watts’s *The Improvement of the Mind* and Jane Marcet’s *Conversations on Chemistry*—to educate himself. He joined the City Philosophical Society, where he attended lectures and discussed scientific topics, developing a focused interest in electricity.

### Entry into Science and Mentorship by Davy
In 1812, Faraday attended lectures by Humphry Davy at the Royal Institution, taking meticulous notes which he bound and sent to Davy. Impressed, Davy hired him as Chemical Assistant in March 1813 after an accident damaged Davy’s eyesight. Faraday accompanied Davy on an 1813–1815 European tour, meeting leading scientists. Their relationship later strained after Faraday published his 1821 electromagnetic rotation results without acknowledging Davy or William Hyde Wollaston, leading to a temporary reassignment away from electromagnetism.

### Electromagnetism and Electromagnetic Induction
Following Hans Christian Ørsted’s 1820 discovery of electromagnetism, Faraday built two devices producing continuous "electromagnetic rotation" (1821), including the homopolar motor, establishing the principle of the electric motor. After a hiatus improving optical glass, he resumed research in 1831. Wrapping two insulated coils around an iron ring, he observed that a current in one coil induced a momentary current in the other—**mutual inductance**. He further showed that moving a magnet through a wire loop (or vice versa) generated current, proving a **changing magnetic field produces an electric field**. This **Faraday’s law of induction** became one of Maxwell’s equations and the basis of field theory. He constructed the first electric dynamo, the ancestor of modern generators.

### Unity of Electricity and Field Concept
In 1832, Faraday investigated "static," battery, and "animal electricity," concluding that the perceived distinct "kinds" of electricity were illusory. He proposed a single "electricity" where varying quantity and intensity (current and voltage) produced different phenomena. Late in his career, he hypothesized that electromagnetic forces extended into empty space around conductors—a **field concept** rejected by contemporaries but later validated by Maxwell and Heinrich Hertz.

### Diamagnetism and Magneto-Optics
In 1845, Faraday discovered **diamagnetism**: many materials exhibit weak repulsion from a magnetic field. Simultaneously, he found that a magnetic field aligned with a light beam rotates the plane of polarization of linearly polarized light—the **Faraday effect**—demonstrating a fundamental link between light and magnetism. In 1862, he attempted to detect magnetic splitting of spectral lines (the Zeeman effect) but lacked sufficient apparatus; Pieter Zeeman succeeded in 1897, crediting Faraday.

### Electrochemistry and Chemical Discoveries
Faraday formulated the **laws of electrolysis** (quantitative relationships between electric charge and chemical change). He popularized terminology proposed by William Whewell: *anode*, *cathode*, *electrode*, *ion*, and *electrolyte*. He discovered **benzene** (bicarburet of hydrogen) and **tetrachloroethylene** (perchloroethylene), synthesized hexachloroethane, determined the composition of chlorine clathrate hydrate, liquefied several gases (supporting the view that gases are vapours of low-boiling liquids), and invented an early **Bunsen burner**. In 1857, he observed that gold colloids exhibited optical properties distinct from bulk gold, arguably the first observation of quantum size effects and the birth of nanoscience.

### Faraday Cage and Static Electricity
In 1836, the **ice pail experiment** showed that charge resides only on the exterior of a charged conductor and that exterior fields cancel inside. He built a 12-foot wooden frame on glass supports, lined with paper and wire mesh, electrified it, and demonstrated safety inside—proving electricity was a force, not an imponderable fluid. This **Faraday cage** principle underpins electrostatic shielding.

### Royal Institution, Public Service, and Science Communication
Faraday held successive roles at the Royal Institution: Assistant Superintendent (1821), Laboratory Director (1825), and first Fullerian Professor of Chemistry (1833, life tenure). He founded the **Friday Evening Discourses** (1825) and delivered **nineteen Christmas Lectures** (1827–1860), notably *The Chemical History of a Candle* (1848), to inspire youth and fund the Institution. His lecturing philosophy emphasized a "flame... kept alive with unremitting splendour" and urged audiences to "philosophise" on mechanisms (e.g., why ice floats).

His public service included: forensic investigation with Charles Lyell of the 1846 Haswell colliery explosion (first linking coal dust to explosion severity, demonstrating ventilation prevention—ignored until the 1913 Senghenydd disaster); lighthouse optics and electric lighting trials with Chance Brothers; corrosion protection for ships; pollution studies at Swansea and the Royal Mint; and a famous 1855 letter to *The Times* on the Thames "Great Stink." He refused to develop chemical weapons for the Crimean War (1853–56) on ethical grounds and declined to publish lectures, insisting live experiments were essential for genuine understanding.

### Honours, Character, and Legacy
Faraday declined a knighthood (religious objection to worldly reward) and twice refused the Royal Society presidency. He accepted honorary degrees and foreign academy memberships (American, Swedish, French, Dutch). A nervous breakdown in 1839 interrupted but did not end his work. He retired to Hampton Court in 1858. Albert Einstein kept his portrait alongside Newton and Maxwell; Ernest Rutherford called him "one of the greatest scientific discoverers of all time." The SI unit of capacitance, the **farad**, and the Faraday constant (charge per mole of electrons) bear his name. His field concept, once rejected, became the cornerstone of modern physics.

## Terms
- ****Electromagnetic induction**** — The production of an electromotive force (voltage) across a conductor when it experiences a changing magnetic field; discovered by Faraday in 1831.
- ****Mutual inductance**** — The induction of a current in one coil by a changing current in a nearby, magnetically coupled coil; demonstrated by Faraday’s iron-ring apparatus.
- ****Faraday’s law of induction**** — A quantitative law (later one of Maxwell’s equations) stating that the induced electromotive force in a closed circuit equals the negative rate of change of magnetic flux through the circuit.
- ****Diamagnetism**** — A weak, negative magnetic susceptibility causing materials to be repelled by a magnetic field; discovered by Faraday in 1845.
- ****Faraday effect**** — The rotation of the plane of polarization of linearly polarized light passing through a material subjected to a magnetic field parallel to the light path; discovered 1845.
- ****Laws of electrolysis**** — Faraday’s two quantitative laws: (1) mass of substance altered at an electrode is proportional to charge passed; (2) masses altered by the same charge are proportional to chemical equivalent weights.
- ****Faraday cage**** — A conductive enclosure that blocks external static and non-static electric fields by redistributing charge on its exterior, cancelling interior fields; demonstrated 1836.
- ****Homopolar motor**** — A direct-current motor producing continuous rotation via the Lorentz force on a conductor in a magnetic field; Faraday’s 1821 device was the first.
- ****Electric dynamo**** — A generator converting mechanical rotation into electrical energy via electromagnetic induction; Faraday built the first in 1831.
- ****Field concept**** — The idea that electromagnetic influences extend through space as "lines of force" rather than acting instantaneously at a distance; Faraday’s visual model, later mathematized by Maxwell.

## Debates and open questions
- **Priority and credit for electromagnetic rotation (1821):** Faraday published without acknowledging Davy or Wollaston, causing a Royal Society controversy that strained his mentorship with Davy and may have delayed his electromagnetic research for years.
- **Rejection of the field concept:** Faraday’s proposal that electromagnetic forces exist in empty space around conductors was dismissed by contemporary scientists who favored action-at-a-distance models; acceptance required Maxwell’s mathematics (1860s) and Hertz’s experimental verification of electromagnetic waves (1887–88).
- **Coal dust explosion hazard (1846):** Faraday and Lyell’s forensic proof that coal dust amplified the Haswell colliery explosion—and that ventilation could prevent it—was ignored by mine owners for over 60 years until the 1913 Senghenydd disaster killed 439.
- **Mathematical vs. experimental philosophy:** Faraday’s lack of formal mathematics (limited to simple algebra) led some peers to undervalue his theoretical insights; Maxwell later declared Faraday "a mathematician of a very high order" for his geometric reasoning via lines of force.
- **Ethical stance on weapons:** Faraday’s refusal to develop chemical weapons for the Crimean War (1853–56) on religious/ethical grounds was a rare early instance of a scientist declining state military work on principle.
