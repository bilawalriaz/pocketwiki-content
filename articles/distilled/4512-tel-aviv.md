# Tel Aviv

## Overview
Tel Aviv is Israel's most populous city and its economic and technological center, founded in 1909 as a modern Jewish suburb of the ancient port of Jaffa. It grew rapidly under British rule, became the temporary seat of Israeli government, and evolved into a global high-tech hub and cultural capital known for its vibrant nightlife, liberal atmosphere, and UNESCO-listed Bauhaus architecture.

## Timeline
- **1909** — Founded as Ahuzat Bayit by 66 Jewish families on sand dunes north of Jaffa.
- **1910** — Renamed Tel Aviv, after the Hebrew translation of Herzl's novel *Altneuland*.
- **1921** — Granted township status within the Jaffa Municipality.
- **1925** — Patrick Geddes draws up a master plan for the city's development.
- **1934** — Granted independent municipal status, separate from Jaffa.
- **1948** — Israeli Declaration of Independence proclaimed; city serves as temporary government center.
- **1950** — Fully reunified with Jaffa under the name Tel Aviv-Yafo.
- **2003** — The "White City" is designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2023** — The Red Line of the Tel Aviv Light Rail opens.

## Body

### Foundation and Early Growth
Tel Aviv was established in 1909 by the Yishuv (Jewish residents of Palestine) as a modern, hygienic alternative to the crowded conditions of Jaffa. The name, chosen in 1910, means "Spring Mound," symbolizing renewal atop ancient layers. Under Mayor Meir Dizengoff, it grew rapidly, adopting the 1925 Geddes Plan, which emphasized a garden city layout with a grid of streets and public parks. Jewish immigration, especially from Nazi Germany in the 1930s, fueled expansion, and the city became a center for Bauhaus architecture, creating the "White City."

### Municipal Independence and Statehood
Tel Aviv became independent from Jaffa in 1934. During the 1947–1949 Palestine war, fighting erupted between Jewish Tel Aviv and Arab Jaffa. After Israel's declaration of independence in 1948, Tel Aviv served as the temporary government center until functions moved to Jerusalem in 1949. The cities were fully unified in 1950 as Tel Aviv-Yafo. The city faced attacks during the war, including Egyptian airstrikes and the Altalena Affair, a confrontation between the IDF and Irgun.

### Urban Decline and Revival
Tel Aviv's population peaked at 390,000 in the early 1960s but then declined due to suburbanization, high property prices, and urban decay. By the late 1980s, it had a reputation as a "dying" city. Revival began in the 1990s with an influx of immigrants from the former Soviet Union, many with technical skills, which helped establish Tel Aviv as a high-tech hub. The city's finances and population recovered, and by the 2000s, it attracted a younger demographic and experienced gentrification.

### Modern Economy and Culture
Today, Tel Aviv is a global city with a beta+ ranking and a leading startup ecosystem. It has the highest cost of living in the world and is a major tourist destination. The city is a center for arts, entertainment, and LGBT culture, hosting a large annual pride parade. Its economy is driven by high-tech, finance, and tourism, with the Tel Aviv Stock Exchange as the country's only one.

## Terms
- **Yishuv**: The Jewish community in Palestine before the establishment of the State of Israel.
- **Aliyah**: Immigration of Jews to the Land of Israel.
- **Geddes Plan**: A 1925 master plan for Tel Aviv based on the garden city movement, emphasizing green spaces and a hierarchical street grid.
- **Bauhaus/International Style**: A modernist architectural style characterized by functional design, clean lines, and lack of ornamentation, prominent in Tel Aviv's "White City."
- **Silicon Wadi**: A nickname for the high-tech cluster in the Tel Aviv metropolitan area, analogous to Silicon Valley.
- **Gush Dan**: The metropolitan area centered on Tel Aviv, Israel's most populous region.
- **Mutasarrifate of Jerusalem**: An administrative district of the Ottoman Empire that included Jaffa before 1917.
- **Kibbutz**: A collective community in Israel, traditionally agricultural.

## Debates and Open Questions
The article notes the ongoing debate over the status of Jerusalem, which affects whether Tel Aviv is considered Israel's most populous city. It also references historical tensions between religious and secular communities, as well as the demographic and social impacts of gentrification in neighborhoods like Jaffa and Florentin. The city's future development plans, including skyscraper construction and transportation projects like the light rail and proposed metro, involve balancing growth with preservation of its architectural heritage.
