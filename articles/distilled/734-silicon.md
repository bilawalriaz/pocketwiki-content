# Silicon

## Overview

Silicon (Si, atomic number 14) is a hard, brittle, blue-grey metalloid and semiconductor, the second most abundant element in the Earth's crust (~28% by mass). Its high oxygen affinity delayed isolation until 1823 (Berzelius), yet its semiconductor properties, low cost, and stable oxide have made it the foundation of modern electronics, defining the "Silicon Age." Beyond technology, silicon is essential in biology (diatom frustules, plant cell walls) and industry (concrete, steel, silicones).

## Body

### Discovery and Early Chemistry

Silicon's strong affinity for oxygen prevented early isolation. Lavoisier (1787) recognized silica as an oxide but could not reduce it. Davy (1808) named it "silicium" believing it metallic. Gay-Lussac and Thénard (1811) produced impure amorphous silicon via potassium and silicon tetrafluoride, but did not characterize it. Thomas Thomson (1817) renamed it silicon, reflecting its nonmetallic nature. Berzelius (1823–24) successfully purified silicon and synthesized silicon tetrachloride, earning credit for discovery. Deville later produced crystalline silicon via aluminum reduction of silicon chloride. Wöhler (1857–58) discovered volatile silicon hydrides; Friedel and Crafts (1863) made the first organosilicon compound. Early 20th-century work by Stock and Kipping detailed silane and organosilicon chemistry.

### Physical and Atomic Properties

A silicon atom has 14 electrons ([Ne]3s²3p²), with four valence electrons forming sp³ hybrids and tetrahedral SiX₄ compounds. Melting point: 1414 °C; boiling point: 3265 °C — second only to boron among nonmetals/metalloids. Silicon crystallizes in a diamond cubic lattice (space group 227), contracting on melting like water ice. It has no stable allotropes at standard pressure, but high-pressure forms include Si–VII (hexagonal close-packed at ~40 GPa) and BC8 (body-centered cubic, metastable at low pressure). Pure silicon is an intrinsic semiconductor with a small band gap; its resistivity decreases with temperature. Doping with phosphorus (n-type) or boron (p-type) enables transistor action via p–n junctions.

### Chemistry and Compounds

Bulk silicon is inert but reactive at high temperatures, forming a protective SiO₂ layer below 900 °C. It reacts with halogens (F₂ at RT, Cl₂ at ~300 °C), dissolves in hot alkali to form silicates, and alloys with most metals when molten. Unlike carbon, silicon shows weaker catenation (Si–Si bond energy ~226 kJ/mol vs C–C ~356 kJ/mol), fewer multiply bonded compounds (double bond rule), but allows hypervalence (e.g., SiF₆²⁻). Silicon resembles germanium more than carbon due to d-block contraction. Its chemistry is dominated by tetrahedral silicates and silicon dioxide.

### Occurrence

Silicon is the 8th most abundant element in the universe and constitutes ~27.2% of the Earth's crust (after oxygen). It is concentrated in silicate minerals (>90% of crust), with differentiation separating core (Fe-Ni-S), mantle (olivine, pyroxenes), and crust (aluminosilicates, quartz). Oceanic silicon fluxes include river input, groundwater, aeolian dust (300–900 Mt/yr, 80–240 Mt as particulate Si), and hydrothermal activity. Isotopic gradients exist between Atlantic and Pacific deep waters (>0.3‰ ³⁰Si difference).

### Production

Metallurgical-grade silicon (95–99%) is produced by carbothermal reduction of quartz in electric arc furnaces, yielding ferrosilicon (80% of global output; China leads at 4.6 Mt/yr). Semiconductor-grade silicon (>99.9999999%) is made via reduction of trichlorosilane or silicon tetrachloride, purified by fractional distillation and zone refining, then grown as single crystals via the Czochralski process. Polycrystalline and upgraded metallurgical-grade (UMG) silicon serve solar and large-area electronics.

### Applications

**Electronics:** Silicon wafers host >90% of semiconductor devices. Doped monocrystalline silicon forms transistors, integrated circuits, solar cells, and photonics components. Silicon photonics uses it as a Raman laser medium. Quantum dots (2–1000 nm) enable displays, sensors, and luminescent concentrators via size-tunable photoluminescence.

**Industrial:** Silicates dominate construction (Portland cement, concrete, ceramics, glass). Silicon carbide serves as abrasive and high-strength ceramic. Silicones (polymers of Si–O) are used in sealants, medical implants, and thermal greases. Ferrosilicon deoxidizes steel and enables thin-section casting.

**Alloys:** Al-silicon alloys (12% Si eutectic) reduce thermal contraction in automotive castings. Silicon improves hardness and wear resistance.

### Biological Role

Diatoms, radiolarians, and sponges build silica skeletons. Plants absorb orthosilicic acid via xylem, depositing amorphous silica in cell walls, enhancing strength, reducing herbivory, and triggering defense pathways. Rice is a notable accumulator. Silicon may be essential for human connective tissue (elastin/collagen synthesis) and bone health, though deficiency is hard to demonstrate. Marine diatoms drive oceanic silicon cycling, exporting biogenic silica via marine snow.

## Terms

- **Metalloid**: Element with intermediate properties between metals and nonmetals; silicon exhibits both.
- **Semiconductor**: Material with conductivity between conductor and insulator; silicon's band gap allows controlled doping.
- **p–n junction**: Interface between n-type and p-type semiconductors; basis of diodes and transistors.
- **Doping**: Introducing impurities (e.g., boron, phosphorus) to alter electrical properties.
- **Ferrosilicon**: Iron-silicon alloy (variable Si content) used in steelmaking.
- **Czochralski process**: Method of growing single-crystal silicon by pulling from molten silicon.
- **Zone refining**: Purification technique using moving temperature gradients.
- **Biogenic silica**: Silica produced by living organisms (e.g., diatom frustules).
- **Siloxane**: Compound with alternating silicon and oxygen atoms; backbone of silicones.
- **Band gap**: Energy difference between valence and conduction bands in semiconductors.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Essentiality in humans**: Silicon is considered potentially essential for connective tissue formation, but definitive deficiency syndromes remain unproven due to its ubiquity.
- **Silicon vs. carbon biochemistry**: Astrobiology debates whether silicon could support alternative life; its weaker catenation and bond energies limit complex molecule stability compared to carbon.
- **Oceanic silicon cycling**: While riverine input dominates coastal silicon flux, the role of aeolian dust in open-ocean productivity and its isotopic signature evolution remain active research areas.
- **Silicon quantum dot toxicity**: Despite being metal-free and non-toxic, long-term biocompatibility and environmental impact of silicon nanomaterials require further study.
