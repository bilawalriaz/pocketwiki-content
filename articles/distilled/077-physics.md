# Physics

Physics is the scientific study of matter, its fundamental constituents, its motion and behavior through space and time, and the related entities of energy and force. It is one of the most fundamental scientific disciplines, often called the "fundamental science" because all branches of natural science are constrained by its laws. Physics aims to describe natural phenomena in terms of simpler root causes and to connect these causes together, a process of "unifying" forces that continues today. Advances in physics have directly enabled transformative technologies, from television and computers to nuclear weapons and industrialization.

## Timeline

- **Before 3000 BCE** — Early civilizations (Sumerians, Egyptians, Indus Valley) develop predictive knowledge of celestial motions
- **650–480 BCE** — Natural philosophy originates in Greece; pre-Socratic philosophers like Thales propose natural causes for events
- **384–322 BCE** — Aristotle writes his treatise "Physics"; Aristotelian physics becomes influential for ~two millennia
- **6th century CE** — John Philoponus challenges Aristotelian science; Islamic scholarship later develops optics (Ibn al-Haytham's Book of Optics)
- **16th–17th centuries** — Scientific Revolution: heliocentric model replaces geocentric; Kepler's planetary laws (1609–1619); Galileo's telescopic work
- **Late 17th century** — Newton unifies laws of motion and universal gravitation; Newton and Leibniz develop calculus
- **19th century** — Thermodynamics, electromagnetism, and mechanics mature into "classical physics"; some experimental results remain inexplicable
- **Early 20th century** — Planck's quantum theory and Einstein's relativity launch modern physics
- **2012** — CERN announces detection of a particle consistent with the Higgs boson

## Body

### Ancient astronomy and natural philosophy

Astronomy is one of the oldest natural sciences. Early civilizations before 3000 BCE, including the Sumerians, ancient Egyptians, and the Indus Valley Civilization, had predictive knowledge of the motions of the Sun, Moon, and stars, though explanations were often unscientific. According to Asger Aaboe, Western astronomy's origins lie in Mesopotamia, descended from late Babylonian astronomy. Natural philosophy began in Greece during the Archaic period (650–480 BCE) when pre-Socratic philosophers like Thales rejected non-naturalistic explanations and proposed ideas verified by reason and observation. Atomism, proposed by Leucippus and Democritus, was found correct approximately 2000 years later.

### Aristotelian and medieval physics

Aristotle (384–322 BCE) wrote a substantial treatise on "Physics" in the 4th century BCE. His approach mixed limited observation with logical deduction but did not rely on experimental verification. He explained motion and gravity with the theory of four elements (air, fire, water, earth), each having its own natural place; heavier objects would fall faster, with speed proportional to weight. His laws of motion included the notion that violent motion's speed depends on the force applied. The problem of motion led to the philosophical notion of a "prime mover" as the ultimate source of all motion. After the Western Roman Empire fell in the fifth century, intellectual pursuits declined in western Europe, but the Byzantine Empire continued advancing learning. Islamic scholarship inherited Aristotelian physics and developed it further, notably in optics and vision through Ibn al-Haytham's Book of Optics, which presented light rays as an alternative to ancient Greek visual rays and applied controlled experiments verifying laws of refraction and reflection.

### Scientific Revolution and classical physics

Physics became a separate science when early modern Europeans used experimental and quantitative methods. Major developments included replacing the geocentric model with the heliocentric Copernican model, Kepler's laws of planetary motion (1609–1619), Galileo's telescopic work, and Newton's unification of laws of motion and universal gravitation. Newton and Leibniz independently developed calculus, which Newton applied to physical problems. During the Industrial Revolution, research into thermodynamics, chemistry, and electromagnetics resulted from increased energy needs. By the end of the 19th century, these theories matched many observations and became "classical physics." However, several results remained inexplicable: the undetectable luminiferous aether, blackbody radiation intensity mismatches, and unexpected electron emission from illuminated metals.

### Modern physics

Modern physics began in the early 20th century with Max Planck's quantum theory and Einstein's relativity. Classical mechanics predicted that the speed of light depends on the observer's motion, conflicting with Maxwell's equations; Einstein's special relativity resolved this by allowing a constant speed of light. Planck proposed that material oscillators can only be excited in discrete steps proportional to their frequency, correcting black-body radiation problems. This, along with the photoelectric effect and discrete electron orbital energy levels, led to quantum mechanics, pioneered by Heisenberg, Schrödinger, and Dirac. The Standard Model of particle physics was derived from this work. Classical physics accurately describes systems with length scales greater than the atomic scale and motions much slower than light; outside this domain, classical predictions fail.

### Core theories and branches

Central theories used by all physicists include classical mechanics, quantum mechanics, thermodynamics and statistical mechanics, electromagnetism, and special relativity. Classical physics includes mechanics (statics, kinematics, dynamics; solid and fluid mechanics), acoustics, optics, heat and thermodynamics, and electricity and magnetism. Modern physics concerns behavior under extreme conditions or very large/small scales; atomic and nuclear physics study matter at the smallest scale where chemical elements can be identified, while particle physics (high-energy physics) deals with the most basic units of matter. The two chief modern theories—quantum theory (concerned with discrete nature at atomic and subatomic levels) and relativity (describing phenomena in moving frames of reference)—present a different picture of space, time, and matter than classical physics.

Major contemporary fields include nuclear and particle physics, condensed matter physics, atomic/molecular/optical (AMO) physics, astrophysics, and applied physics. Particle physics studies elementary constituents described by the Standard Model, which accounts for 12 known particles of matter (quarks and leptons) interacting via gauge bosons. Nuclear physics studies atomic nuclei, with applications in nuclear medicine, magnetic resonance imaging, and radiocarbon dating. Condensed matter physics, the largest field, deals with macroscopic properties of "condensed" phases like solids, liquids, superfluids, and Bose–Einstein condensates; the term was coined by Philip Anderson in 1967. Astrophysics applies physics to stellar structure, evolution, and cosmology; the Big Bang model was confirmed by nucleosynthesis success and the 1964 discovery of the cosmic microwave background.

### Research methods and current frontiers

Physicists use the scientific method to test theories, comparing implications with experimental conclusions. Theorists develop mathematical models predicting future results; experimentalists test predictions and explore new phenomena. Phenomenologists work at the interplay of theory and experiment. Current research includes high-temperature superconductivity in condensed matter physics, evidence for physics beyond the Standard Model (notably massive neutrinos), and the nature of dark matter and dark energy. Many everyday phenomena involving complexity, chaos, or turbulence remain poorly understood, including sandpile formation and water droplet shapes; these have received growing attention since the 1970s due to modern mathematical methods and computers.

## Terms

- **Classical physics**: Traditional branches (mechanics, thermodynamics, electromagnetism) well-developed before the 20th century; accurate for systems larger than atomic scale moving slower than light
- **Modern physics**: Physics incorporating quantum mechanics and relativity; concerns behavior under extreme conditions or very small/large scales
- **Quantum mechanics**: Theory concerned with discrete nature of phenomena at atomic and subatomic levels, including particle-wave complementarity
- **Relativity**: Theory describing phenomena in frames of reference in motion; special relativity excludes gravity, general relativity includes it
- **Standard Model**: Framework describing 12 known particles of matter (quarks and leptons) interacting via strong, weak, and electromagnetic forces through gauge bosons
- **Condensed matter physics**: Study of macroscopic properties of "condensed" phases (solids, liquids, superfluids) where particle numbers are extremely large and interactions strong
- **Natural philosophy**: Pre-17th-century unified study of physics, chemistry, biology, and mathematics branches
- **Scientific law**: Concise verbal or mathematical statement expressing a fundamental principle of a theory
- **Phenomenologist**: Physicist studying complex experimental phenomena and relating them to fundamental theory

## Debates and open questions

Physics beyond the Standard Model remains an active research area, with theories such as supersymmetry yet to be proven or disproven; the Large Hadron Collider has found the Higgs boson, but future research aims to test supersymmetry. An important unsolved theoretical problem is high-temperature superconductivity. The nature of dark matter and dark energy remains mysterious. Many complex everyday phenomena—sandpile formation, nodes in trickling water, water droplet shapes, surface tension catastrophes, self-sorting in shaken heterogeneous collections—remain poorly understood despite seeming solvable by dynamics and mechanics. Philosophers of physics debate issues including the nature of space and time, determinism, and metaphysical outlooks such as empiricism, naturalism, and realism; some theorists hold that logical truths and mathematical reasoning depend on the empirical world, while others maintain mathematics concerns abstract patterns beyond the real world.
