# Western Front (World War I)

## Overview
The Western Front was the primary theatre of World War I on land, where the German and French armies, along with their allies, fought from 1914 to 1918. It became defined by brutal trench warfare after the initial German advance was halted in 1914, leading to a deadlock that cost millions of lives in futile offensives. The front's eventual resolution in 1918, through a combination of new tactics, technology, and the arrival of American forces, directly led to Germany's defeat and the war's end.

## Timeline
- **Aug 1914** — German invasion of Belgium and Luxembourg begins the Western Front.
- **Sep 1914** — First Battle of the Marne halts the German advance; trench warfare begins.
- **Apr 1915** — First large-scale use of poison gas by Germany at Second Battle of Ypres.
- **Feb–Dec 1916** — Battle of Verdun; German attempt to "bleed France white" fails with ~700,000 combined casualties.
- **Jul–Nov 1916** — Battle of the Somme; British and French offensive results in over a million combined casualties.
- **Apr 1917** — Nivelle Offensive fails, leading to widespread French Army mutinies.
- **Jul–Nov 1917** — Third Battle of Ypres (Passchendaele); costly British advance in Flanders.
- **Nov 1917** — Battle of Cambrai; first large-scale use of tanks by the British.
- **Mar–Jul 1918** — German Spring Offensives (Operation Michael) make significant gains but fail to win the war.
- **Aug–Nov 1918** — Entente Hundred Days Offensive leads to the collapse of the German army.
- **11 Nov 1918** — Armistice signed, ending hostilities on the Western Front.

## Body

### The War of Movement and Trench Warfare (1914)
The war began with Germany executing a modified Schlieffen Plan, invading neutral Belgium to outflank French defenses. This brought Britain into the war. After a series of battles (the "Battle of the Frontiers"), the German advance was halted at the First Battle of the Marne in September 1914. Both sides then attempted to outflank each other in the "Race to the Sea," resulting in a continuous line of fortified trenches from the North Sea to Switzerland. This static front, where territory was measured in yards, would define the war for three years. German occupation of industrial regions in northern France dealt a serious blow to the French economy.

### Deadlock and New Weapons (1915–1917)
From 1915 to 1917, both sides launched massive offensives (e.g., Verdun, the Somme, Passchendaele) that used artillery bombardments and infantry assaults but resulted in catastrophic casualties for minimal gains. The defensive advantages of trenches, machine guns, and barbed wire proved overwhelming. To break the deadlock, new technologies were introduced. Germany first used lethal chlorine gas at Ypres in 1915, leading to an escalation in chemical warfare with phosgene and mustard gas. Aircraft evolved from reconnaissance to dedicated fighters, creating an "air war." The British introduced the tank at the Somme in 1916, though its impact was initially limited.

### The Turning Point (1917–1918)
The stalemate began to crack in 1917. The German withdrawal to the fortified Hindenburg Line shortened their front. The failed Nivelle Offensive caused mutinies in the French Army, shifting the main burden to the British. The entry of the United States into the war in April 1917 promised crucial manpower. In 1918, the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk freed German divisions from the Eastern Front. Germany launched a final, massive Spring Offensive using new "infiltration tactics," achieving the deepest advance since 1914 but ultimately failing to win the war. The exhausted German army was then broken by the Entente's Hundred Days Offensive, aided by over two million arriving American troops, leading to the Armistice.

## Terms
*   **Schlieffen Plan:** Germany's pre-war strategy for a two-front war, involving a rapid invasion of France through Belgium before turning east to face Russia.
*   **Trench Warfare:** A form of fighting using continuous ditches and fortifications, which created a static front and made offensive action extremely costly.
*   **No Man's Land:** The dangerous, contested ground between opposing trench lines.
*   **Creeping Barrage:** An artillery tactic where a curtain of shellfire moves forward in a planned sequence, with infantry advancing immediately behind it for cover.
*   **Infiltration Tactics (Stosstruppen):** German stormtrooper tactics using small, elite units to bypass strongpoints and attack enemy rear areas, used effectively in the 1918 Spring Offensive.
*   **Hindenburg Line:** A massive, fortified German defensive position built in 1916–17 behind the Noyon Salient to shorten and strengthen their front.
*   **Hundred Days Offensive:** The final Allied offensive from August to November 1918 that led to the German collapse and the Armistice.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The strategic wisdom of the battles of Verdun and the Somme is heavily debated. While they inflicted massive casualties on the German army, the Entente losses were also staggering, leading some historians to question if the war of attrition was a viable strategy.
*   The causes and scale of the 1917 French Army mutinies are analyzed to understand the limits of soldier endurance and the impact on French military capability for the rest of the war.
*   The "Stab-in-the-back" myth (Dolchstoßlegende), which claimed the German army was undefeated in the field but betrayed by civilians, is a key historical debate about post-war German politics and the rise of Nazism.
