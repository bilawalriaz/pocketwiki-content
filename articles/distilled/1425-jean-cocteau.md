# Jean Cocteau

## Overview
Jean Cocteau (1889–1963) was a French polymath—poet, playwright, novelist, designer, film director, visual artist, and critic—whose multidisciplinary output defined 20th-century avant-garde culture. He insisted that all his diverse works, from novels and plays to films and drawings, constituted forms of *poésie* (poetry), blurring the boundaries between artistic media. A central figure in Parisian modernism, he collaborated with Picasso, Stravinsky, Satie, and Diaghilev, influencing Surrealism, Dada, and the group of composers known as Les Six. His *Orphic Trilogy* of films (*The Blood of a Poet*, *Orpheus*, *Testament of Orpheus*) and his novel *Les Enfants Terribles* remain landmarks of modernist myth-making. Openly homosexual in an era of repression, he wove homoerotic themes throughout his work, while his wartime associations with German intellectuals and praise for Nazi sculptor Arno Breker led to post-war collaboration charges, though he was cleared.

## Timeline
- **5 July 1889** — Born in Maisons-Laffitte, Yvelines, to a socially prominent Parisian family.
- **1898** — Father, Georges Cocteau, dies by suicide when Cocteau is nine.
- **1909** — Publishes first poetry collection, *Aladdin's Lamp*, at age nineteen.
- **1912** — Collaborates with Léon Bakst on *Le Dieu bleu* for the Ballets Russes.
- **1917** — *Parade* premieres: ballet with scenario by Cocteau, sets by Picasso, music by Erik Satie, libretto by Apollinaire; produced by Sergei Diaghilev.
- **1918** — Meets poet Raymond Radiguet; begins intense friendship and literary collaboration.
- **1923** — Radiguet dies suddenly; Cocteau begins opium use, later describing addiction and withdrawal in *Opium: Diary of a Cure* (1929).
- **1929** — Publishes *Les Enfants Terribles*, written in a week during opium weaning.
- **1930** — Directs first film, *The Blood of a Poet* (released 1932); stages *La Voix Humaine*.
- **1934** — Stages *La Machine Infernale*, considered his greatest theatrical work.
- **1940–1944** — During Nazi occupation, participates in "round-table" at Georges V Hotel with German and French intellectuals; publishes *Salut à Breker* (1942) praising Nazi sculptor Arno Breker.
- **1946** — Releases *Beauty and the Beast*, starring Jean Marais.
- **1950** — Releases *Orpheus*, second film in the Orphic Trilogy.
- **1955** — Elected to the Académie Française and The Royal Academy of Belgium.
- **1960** — Releases *Testament of Orpheus*, final film of the Orphic Trilogy, featuring Picasso and Yul Brynner.
- **11 Oct 1963** — Dies of a heart attack at his château in Milly-la-Forêt, aged 74; buried in the Chapelle Saint-Blaise des Simples.

## Body

### Early Life and Bohemian Circles
Born into a wealthy family, Cocteau was traumatized by his father’s suicide in 1898. He left home at fifteen and published *Aladdin's Lamp* (1909), quickly earning the nickname "The Frivolous Prince" in Bohemian circles. By his early twenties, he associated with literary giants Marcel Proust, André Gide, and Maurice Barrès. World War I service as a Red Cross ambulance driver introduced him to Guillaume Apollinaire, Pablo Picasso, and Amedeo Modigliani, forging the network that would drive his interdisciplinary career.

### The Ballets Russes and *Parade*
Sergei Diaghilev, impresario of the Ballets Russes, challenged Cocteau to "astonish me," resulting in *Parade* (1917). This landmark ballet fused cubist sets by Picasso, a libretto by Apollinaire, and Erik Satie’s score incorporating typewriters and sirens. The scandalous premiere cemented Cocteau’s role as a catalyst for avant-garde synthesis. He subsequently influenced the composer collective Les Six (including Poulenc, Milhaud, and Honegger) and frequented the bar Le Boeuf sur le Toit, which he helped name.

### Raymond Radiguet and Opium
In 1918, Cocteau met the teenage prodigy Raymond Radiguet. He mentored Radiguet, secured his military exemption, and engineered the publication and prize-winning success of *Le Diable au corps*. Radiguet’s sudden death in 1923 devastated Cocteau, though accounts differ on his immediate reaction; he did not attend the funeral and left for Monte Carlo with Diaghilev. There, a chance meeting with Louis Laloy initiated his opium addiction. The drug and his brutal 1929 withdrawal—documented in *Opium: Diary of a Cure* with pen-and-ink illustrations—radically altered his literary style. *Les Enfants Terribles* was written in a single week during this weaning. Catholic philosopher Jacques Maritain supported his recovery, prompting a temporary return to the sacraments and the founding of the journal *Le Roseau d'Or*.

### Theatre and Early Film (1926–1939)
Cocteau dominated the Parisian stage in the 1930s. *La Voix Humaine* (1930), a monologue for a woman on a telephone, and *Les Parents terribles* (1938), a family drama, were major successes. *La Machine Infernale* (1934), his retelling of the Oedipus myth, is widely regarded as his theatrical masterpiece. He also wrote the libretto for Stravinsky’s *Oedipus rex* (1927). His first film, *The Blood of a Poet* (1930), though now canonical in surrealist cinema, was rejected by the Surrealists themselves as insufficiently orthodox.

### The Occupation and Collaboration Controversy
A self-described pacifist and antiracist who signed a 1940 petition against antisemitism, Cocteau nonetheless fell under the influence of Arno Breker, Hitler’s favored sculptor, who portrayed the Führer as a pacifist art patron. During the occupation, Cocteau joined a "round-table" of French and German intellectuals at the Georges V Hotel, including Ernst Jünger and Carl Schmitt. His 1942 article *Salut à Breker* effusively praised the sculptor, leading to post-war collaboration charges. He was cleared, having used his contacts in a failed attempt to save friends like Max Jacob. Later, aligning with communists such as Louis Aragon, he praised Joseph Stalin as the era’s only great politician.

### The Orphic Trilogy and Cinematic Legacy
Post-war, Cocteau pioneered avant-garde French cinema, influencing the coming New Wave. *Beauty and the Beast* (1946), starring his muse and adopted son Jean Marais, combined poetic visual effects with fairy-tale narrative. *Orpheus* (1950) transposed the myth to contemporary Paris, using mirrors as portals to the underworld. *Testament of Orpheus* (1960), financed partly by Yul Brynner, served as a meta-fictional summation of his life and art, featuring cameos by Picasso and Luis Miguel Dominguín. These three films constitute the *Orphic Trilogy*, exploring the poet’s relationship with death and inspiration.

### Visual Art, Religious Works, and Private Life
In the 1950s, Cocteau turned to mural painting, decorating the Chapelle Saint-Pierre in Villefranche-sur-Mer (1956) and the marriage hall at Menton’s Hôtel de Ville (1957). He never concealed his homosexuality; the semi-autobiographical *Le Livre blanc* (1928) explicitly addresses his "love of boys" and societal persecution. His longest relationships were with actors Jean Marais and Édouard Dermit, the latter formally adopted. Homoerotic imagery permeates his drawings, films, and the 1947 edition of Jean Genet’s *Querelle de Brest*, which featured 29 explicit Cocteau illustrations.

### Death and Epitaph
Cocteau suffered a severe heart attack in April 1963. His health declined further, attributed by biographer Roger Peyrefitte to a devastating breach with his longtime patron Francine Weisweiller. He died on 11 October 1963, the day after Édith Piaf’s death—a coincidence often mythologized as death from grief. Per his wishes, he is buried beneath the floor of the Chapelle Saint-Blaise des Simples in Milly-la-Forêt. His epitaph reads: *Je reste avec vous* ("I stay with you").

## Terms
- **Poésie (Cocteau’s definition)**: Cocteau’s term unifying all his creative output—novels (*poésie de roman*), plays (*poésie de théâtre*), criticism (*poésie critique*), drawings (*poésie graphique*), and films (*poésie cinématographique*)—as variations of poetry.
- **Orphic Trilogy**: Three films—*The Blood of a Poet* (1930), *Orpheus* (1950), *Testament of Orpheus* (1960)—using the Orpheus myth to explore the artist’s descent into the subconscious and confrontation with death.
- **Les Six**: A group of six French composers (Auric, Durey, Honegger, Milhaud, Poulenc, Tailleferre) influenced by Cocteau’s manifesto *Le Coq et l'Arlequin* (1918), advocating clarity and popular culture over Wagnerian excess and Impressionist vagueness.
- **Ballets Russes**: Itinerant ballet company founded by Sergei Diaghilev; the primary vehicle for Cocteau’s early interdisciplinary collaborations (*Le Dieu bleu*, *Parade*).
- **Opium: Diary of a Cure (1929)**: Illustrated memoir documenting Cocteau’s 1929 withdrawal from opium addiction, blending minute-by-minute physical description with philosophical reflection.
- **Jean Marais (1913–1998)**: French actor, Cocteau’s muse, lover, and adopted son; starred in *Beauty and the Beast*, *Orpheus*, *Ruy Blas*, and *The Eternal Return*.
- **Arno Breker (1900–1991)**: German sculptor favored by Hitler; convinced Cocteau of Hitler’s pacifism, leading to Cocteau’s controversial 1942 article *Salut à Breker*.
- **Jacques Maritain (1882–1973)**: Catholic philosopher who guided Cocteau through opium withdrawal and back to the Church; co-founder of *Le Roseau d'Or*.
- **La Machine Infernale (1934)**: Cocteau’s stage adaptation of the Oedipus myth, structured as a mechanical tragedy where fate operates like an infernal machine; considered his greatest play.
- **Académie Française**: France’s preeminent literary academy; Cocteau elected in 1955, marking institutional recognition of his multidisciplinary career.

## Debates and open questions
- **Nature of the Radiguet relationship**: Contemporaries and scholars debate whether Cocteau’s intense bond with the teenage Radiguet was romantic/sexual; Cocteau actively denied a sexual component.
- **Reaction to Radiguet’s death**: Conflicting accounts claim either stunned despair triggering opium addiction or calculated detachment (skipping the funeral, departing for Monte Carlo); Cocteau later called the addiction coincidental.
- **Wartime collaboration**: The extent of Cocteau’s complicity remains contested. He praised Nazi sculptor Arno Breker and attended German-hosted salons, yet signed anti-antisemitism petitions, attempted to save Jewish friends, and claimed his "politics are non-existent." He was officially cleared but critically tainted.
- **Surrealist rejection of *The Blood of a Poet***: The Surrealist movement, led by André Breton, rejected Cocteau’s first film as insufficiently surrealist, highlighting tensions between Cocteau’s aestheticized myth-making and Surrealism’s revolutionary automatism.
- **Cause of final decline**: Biographers dispute whether Cocteau’s fatal heart attack resulted from grief over Édith Piaf’s death (apocryphal), long-term health deterioration, or the emotional rupture with patron Francine Weisweiller.
