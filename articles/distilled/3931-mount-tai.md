# Mount Tai

## Overview
Mount Tai (Tai Shan) is a mountain of immense historical and cultural significance in Shandong province, China. As the easternmost of the Five Sacred Mountains, it has been a site of imperial worship and pilgrimage for over 3,000 years, symbolizing sunrise, birth, and renewal. Its unique blend of natural and cultural features led to its designation as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987.

## Timeline
- **c. 30 million years ago** — Mount Tai's current outline formed during the mid-Cenozoic uplift.
- **c. 1831 BC or 1652 BC** — The Mount Tai earthquake, the first recorded earthquake in Chinese history.
- **c. 2070–1600 BC** — During the Xia dynasty, the mountain was known as Mount Dai.
- **c. 1600–1046 BC** — Religious worship of Mount Tai begins during the Shang dynasty.
- **219 BC** — Qin Shi Huang holds a ceremony on the summit, proclaiming imperial unity.
- **666 AD** — Emperor Gaozong of Tang holds Feng and Shan sacrifices with international representatives.
- **1907** — The "Most Revered of the Five Sacred Mountains" inscription is carved.
- **1987** — Mount Tai is designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Geological Formation and Natural Features
Mount Tai is a tilted fault-block mountain, higher to the south, formed from ancient rocks like gneiss and granite. Its stratum, the Taishan Complex, is the oldest paleo-metamorphic formation from the Cambrian Period in eastern China. The mountain's current form resulted from uplift that began in the Proterozoic Era and accelerated in the mid-Cenozoic. It features a vertical climate gradient, with vegetation covering about 80% of its area, including 989 seed plant species. Notable trees include the 2,100-year-old Han Dynasty Cypresses.

### History of Worship and Imperial Ritual
Human presence at Mount Tai dates to the Paleolithic period. Worship began at least 3,000 years ago, evolving into the Feng and Shan sacrifices—imperial rites where emperors paid homage to Heaven on the summit (Feng) and Earth at the foot (Shan). The first recorded emperor to perform these rites was Qin Shi Huang in 219 BC. The sacrifices reached their highest importance during the Han dynasty. A total of 72 emperors are recorded as having visited the mountain. The tradition involved inscribing stone tablets to mark "great peace."

### Cultural and Religious Significance
Mount Tai is central to Chinese religion, hosting deities like the Dongyue Emperor (Great Deity of Mount Tai) and Bixia Yuanjun (Goddess of the Blue Dawn). The Dai Temple at its foot, first built in the Qin dynasty, is a palace-style complex housing the 1009 mural "The God of Mount Tai Making a Journey." The mountain is adorned with over 1,800 stone inscriptions and steles. The climb to the summit involves 6,660 stone steps, including the famous 1,827-step "Shibapan" (18 levels). It remains a major pilgrimage and tourist site, attracting about six million visitors in 2003.

## Terms
- **Feng and Shan sacrifices**: An ancient imperial rite where the emperor worshipped Heaven on a mountain summit (Feng) and Earth at its base (Shan).
- **Taishan Complex**: The ancient, metamorphic rock stratum forming the foundation of Mount Tai, dating to the Archean Era.
- **Dai Miao**: The Temple of the God of Mount Tai at the mountain's foot, designed like an imperial palace.
- **Bixia Yuanjun**: The "Goddess of the Blue Dawn," a major deity associated with Mount Tai.
- **Shibapan**: The "18 levels," a steep section of 1,827 stone steps on the main climbing route.
- **UNESCO World Heritage Site**: A location designated for its outstanding universal cultural or natural value.

## Debates and Open questions
- The exact date of the first recorded earthquake at Mount Tai is debated, with sources citing either 1831 BC or 1652 BC.
- The purpose of the Wordless Stele (Wúzì Bēi) is uncertain; theories suggest the inscription was either intentionally left blank or eroded by weather.
