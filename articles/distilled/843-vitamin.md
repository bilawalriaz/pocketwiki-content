# Vitamin

## Overview  
Vitamins are organic molecules—often existing as families of related compounds called vitamers—that organisms cannot synthesize in sufficient amounts and must obtain through diet. They support essential metabolic functions: regulating growth and differentiation (vitamin A), acting as hormones (vitamin D), serving as enzyme cofactors (B-complex), and functioning as antioxidants (vitamins C and E). Deficiencies cause diseases like scurvy and beriberi, while excess intake can lead to toxicity. Their discovery between 1910 and 1948 revolutionized nutrition science and public health policy, leading to food fortification and widespread supplementation.

## Body  

### Discovery and Early Research  
The concept of vitamins emerged from observations linking diet to disease. Ancient Egyptians used liver for night blindness (vitamin A deficiency). During the Age of Discovery, sailors suffered scurvy due to lack of fresh produce. James Lind’s 1747 citrus trials proved dietary prevention. In 1881, Nikolai Lunin showed milk contained unknown life-essential factors beyond known macronutrients. Takaki Kanehiro demonstrated beriberi was dietary in 1884, though he wrongly attributed it to protein deficiency. Christiaan Eijkman later linked polished rice to polyneuritis in chickens, identifying the anti-beriberi factor (B1). Frederick Hopkins proposed “accessory factors” necessary for health.

### Isolation and Naming  
In 1910, Umetaro Suzuki isolated a water-soluble complex from rice bran, naming it aberic acid (later Orizanin), but failed to gain recognition due to poor translation. In 1912, Casimir Funk independently isolated the same complex and proposed the name “vitamine,” believing it cured beriberi. Jack Cecil Drummond dropped the final “e” in 1920 after realizing not all vitamins were amines. The term became synonymous with Hopkins’ accessory factors.

### Nobel Recognition  
Multiple researchers received Nobels for vitamin-related work: Adolf Windaus (1928, Chemistry) for sterol-vitamin connections; Eijkman and Hopkins (1929, Medicine) for vitamin discovery; Paul Karrer and Norman Haworth (1937, Chemistry) for carotenoid and flavin chemistry; Albert Szent-Györgyi (1937, Medicine) for vitamin C; Edward Doisy and Henrik Dam (1943, Medicine) for vitamin K; Richard Kuhn (1938, Chemistry) for B2 and B6; and George Whipple, George Minot, William Murphy (1934), Alexander Todd (1957), Dorothy Hodgkin (1964) for B12 research. George Wald (1967, Medicine) studied vitamin A’s role in vision.

### Classification and Function  
Thirteen vitamins are recognized: four fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) and nine water-soluble (eight B vitamers and C). Fat-soluble vitamins accumulate in tissues, risking hypervitaminosis, while water-soluble ones are excreted more readily. Vitamins regulate gene expression, hormone-like signaling, enzymatic reactions, and oxidative balance. They are vital for fetal development, childhood growth, and adult cellular maintenance.

### Intake and Deficiency  
Most vitamins come from diet, though some are synthesized endogenously (e.g., vitamin D via sunlight, vitamin K by gut flora). Deficiencies vary in onset: vitamins A, D, and B12 can remain stored for months or years, whereas niacin stores deplete within weeks. Primary deficiencies stem from inadequate intake; secondary ones arise from malabsorption, lifestyle factors, or medications. Common deficiency diseases include beriberi (B1), pellagra (B3), scurvy (C), rickets (D), and neural tube defects (folate).

### Excess and Toxicity  
Hypertoxicity occurs with fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and certain water-soluble ones (B3, B6, folate). The EU and other nations set tolerable upper intake levels (ULs). In 2016, over 63,000 vitamin overdose cases were reported to U.S. poison control centers, mostly involving children under five.

### Regulation and Marketing  
Governments regulate supplements differently. The U.S. Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (1994) classifies supplements as food, placing safety responsibility on manufacturers. The EU requires proof of safety for non-prescription sales. Food fortification programs (e.g., folic acid in flour) aim to prevent deficiencies. Public campaigns promoted vitamin-rich foods, sometimes misleadingly (e.g., “Wonder Bread builds strong bodies 8 ways”).

### Naming Anomalies  
Letters F–J were skipped because those compounds were reclassified, discarded, or merged into the B-complex. Vitamin K derives from the Danish word *Koagulation*. Some proposed vitamins (e.g., B17/laetrile, B21) lack scientific validation. Terms like “vitamin N” (nature exposure) and “vitamin I” (ibuprofen) are informal slang.

## Terms  
- **Vitamer**: A group of closely related molecules that collectively form a vitamin (e.g., tocopherols and tocotrienols for vitamin E).  
- **Fat-soluble vitamin**: Vitamins absorbed with dietary fats, stored in liver and adipose tissue (A, D, E, K).  
- **Water-soluble vitamin**: Vitamins dissolved in water, excreted in urine, requiring regular replenishment (B-complex, C).  
- **Hypervitaminosis**: Toxicity from excessive vitamin intake, typically affecting fat-soluble vitamins.  
- **Food fortification**: Mandatory addition of nutrients to staple foods to prevent deficiencies.  
- **Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL)**: Maximum daily intake unlikely to cause adverse effects.  
- **Primary deficiency**: Insufficient vitamin intake from diet.  
- **Secondary deficiency**: Impaired absorption or utilization despite adequate intake.  
- **Anti-vitamin**: Compound that inhibits vitamin absorption or action (e.g., avidin blocking biotin).  
- **Bioavailability**: Proportion of a nutrient absorbed and utilized by the body.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- Whether choline should be classified as a vitamin remains debated.  
- The efficacy of multivitamin supplementation for healthy individuals lacks strong evidence.  
- Discrepancies exist between national dietary guidelines (e.g., vitamin C recommendations range from 40 mg/day in India to 155 mg/day in the EU).  
- Many proposed vitamins (e.g., B17, B21) are not scientifically validated.  
- Long-term effects of high-dose supplementation are poorly understood.  
- Regulatory frameworks vary globally, with inconsistent oversight of supplement safety and labeling.
