# Ancient Carthage

## Overview
Ancient Carthage was a major Phoenician civilization and empire based in North Africa, dominating the western Mediterranean through maritime trade and military power for centuries. It grew from a small Tyrian colony into a wealthy republic and formidable rival of Rome, culminating in the devastating Punic Wars. Its destruction in 146 BC ended Phoenician political power and paved the way for Roman hegemony, but its legacy influenced Roman culture and persists through archaeological and historical study.

## Timeline
- **c. 814 BC** — Carthage founded as a Tyrian colony in present-day Tunisia.
- **7th century BC** — Carthage becomes independent after Phoenicia's conquest by the Neo-Assyrian Empire.
- **509 BC** — First treaty signed between Carthage and Rome, demarcating spheres of influence.
- **480 BC** — First Sicilian War: Carthage defeated by Syracuse at the Battle of Himera.
- **264–241 BC** — First Punic War: Conflict with Rome over Sicily ends with Carthaginian defeat.
- **241–238 BC** — Mercenary War: Revolt by unpaid Carthaginian troops after the First Punic War.
- **218–201 BC** — Second Punic War: Hannibal's campaigns in Italy, ending with Roman victory at Zama.
- **149–146 BC** — Third Punic War: Carthage is besieged and destroyed by Rome.
- **44 BC** — Julius Caesar founds Roman Carthage on the site of the destroyed city.

## Body

### Foundation and Independence
Carthage was settled around 814 BC by merchants from Tyre, a Phoenician city-state. Its strategic location in the Gulf of Tunis provided access to fertile land and major trade routes. Following Phoenicia's subjugation by foreign powers, Carthage became independent in the mid-6th century BC. Leveraging its naval power, it grew into a hegemon, controlling a network of colonies, vassals, and satellite states across the western Mediterranean, including parts of North Africa, Iberia, and islands like Sicily and Sardinia.

### Government and Politics
Carthage developed a unique republican system combining elements of oligarchy, democracy, and monarchy. It was led by two annually elected **sufetes** (judges) with executive and judicial power, but no military command. A council of elders (**Adirim**) held broad powers, and a popular assembly could vote on deadlocked matters. A separate tribunal, the **One Hundred and Four**, oversaw and punished generals. This system, praised by Aristotle for its balance, featured a separation of political and military offices, though powerful families like the Barcids could dominate both spheres.

### Economy and Society
Carthage's economy was based on extensive maritime trade, manufacturing, and advanced agriculture. It monopolized the tin trade and produced goods like purple dye, textiles, and metalwork. Its society was urbanized and stratified, with a wealthy merchant elite, a class of citizen-soldiers, and a large population of foreign mercenaries and subject peoples, particularly Libyans. Agriculture became increasingly vital, especially after the Second Punic War, with innovations in farming techniques.

### Military
Carthage maintained one of the ancient world's largest navies, crucial for protecting trade routes. Its army was primarily composed of foreign mercenaries and auxiliaries from diverse regions (Iberians, Gauls, Numidians, etc.), led by professional generals. The **Sacred Band** was an elite citizen-soldier unit. Carthage famously used war elephants in battle. The military was separate from the political government, with generals subject to review by the One Hundred and Four.

### Religion and Culture
The Carthaginians practiced a polytheistic Phoenician religion, with **Baal Hammon** and **Tanit** as supreme deities. They engaged in religious syncretism, incorporating gods from other cultures. Archaeological evidence from **tophets** (sacred precincts) suggests the practice of child sacrifice, though this is debated. Their culture valued commerce, seafaring, and agriculture, as reflected in the writings of Mago on farming.

### Conflict with Rome and Destruction
Centuries of rivalry with Rome over trade and territory led to the three Punic Wars. The Second Punic War (218–201 BC) saw Hannibal's famous invasion of Italy, but ended with a decisive Roman victory. After the Third Punic War (149–146 BC), Rome besieged and utterly destroyed Carthage, selling its survivors into slavery. The site was later rebuilt as a Roman city.

## Terms
- **Sufete**: A chief magistrate in Carthage, analogous to a Roman consul, elected annually from the elite class.
- **Adirim**: The "mighty ones," a supreme council of elders in Carthage with broad administrative and financial powers.
- **One Hundred and Four**: A judicial tribunal that oversaw and punished Carthaginian generals after their campaigns.
- **Punic**: The term for Phoenicians and their culture in the western Mediterranean, derived from the Latin word for Phoenician.
- **Thalassocracy**: A state with primary power based on the sea and naval dominance.
- **Tophet**: A sacred precinct or cemetery in Punic cities, containing urns with cremated remains, associated with possible child sacrifice.
- **Baal Hammon**: The chief male deity of Carthage, a god of fertility and the sky.
- **Tanit**: The chief female deity of Carthage, a goddess of war and fertility, often called the "Face of Baal."

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Child Sacrifice**: Historical accounts and archaeological evidence from tophets are debated. Some scholars argue the remains are from natural infant deaths and ritual burials, while others see confirmation of sacrificial practices, especially during crises.
- **Political Evolution**: The exact timing and nature of Carthage's transition from a monarchy to an oligarchic republic are unclear, with evidence pointing to reforms in the 6th or 7th century BC.
- **Nature of the Empire**: The degree of direct control Carthage exerted over its territories is uncertain. Its empire was likely a hegemonic network of treaties, alliances, and tributary relationships rather than a centralized state.
- **Citizenship**: The criteria for Carthaginian citizenship and the political rights of different social classes, including Libyans and other subject peoples, are not well understood.
