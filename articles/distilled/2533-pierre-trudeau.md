# Pierre Trudeau

## Overview
Pierre Elliott Trudeau was the 15th Prime Minister of Canada, serving two non-consecutive terms from 1968 to 1979 and 1980 to 1984. A dominant figure in Canadian politics for over 15 years, he is remembered for his intellectual force, charismatic personality, and transformative policies that reshaped Canada's social fabric and constitutional framework. His legacy is defined by the introduction of official bilingualism and multiculturalism, the patriation of the Constitution with the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, and his staunch federalist stance against Quebec separatism, though his economic policies and centralizing tendencies remain controversial.

## Timeline
- **October 18, 1919** — Born in Outremont, Quebec.
- **1965** — Elected to the House of Commons as a Liberal.
- **1967-1968** — Serves as Minister of Justice, liberalizing divorce, abortion, and homosexuality laws.
- **April 20, 1968** — Sworn in as Prime Minister after winning the Liberal leadership.
- **June 25, 1968** — Wins first majority government, fueled by "Trudeaumania."
- **October 1970** — Invokes the War Measures Act during the October Crisis.
- **1971** — Announces Canada's Multiculturalism Policy.
- **1972** — Wins a minority government; introduces the capital gains tax.
- **1974** — Wins a majority government after defeating a no-confidence motion.
- **1975** — Establishes Petro-Canada and introduces wage and price controls.
- **1976** — Canada joins the G7; Parti Québécois wins Quebec provincial election.
- **1979** — Loses election to Joe Clark's Progressive Conservatives.
- **February 1980** — Wins a majority government, returning to power.
- **May 20, 1980** — Federalists win the Quebec sovereignty referendum.
- **October 1980** — Introduces the National Energy Program (NEP).
- **April 17, 1982** — Constitution Act, 1982, including the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, is proclaimed.
- **February 29, 1984** — Announces his retirement as Liberal leader.
- **September 28, 2000** — Dies in Montreal at age 80.

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Born to a wealthy French-Canadian family, Trudeau was educated at elite Jesuit institutions. He studied law at the Université de Montréal and later at Harvard, the Paris Institute of Political Studies, and the London School of Economics. His travels and studies exposed him to Keynesian economics and personalist philosophy, shaping his belief in individual rights over state power and his opposition to nationalism. During World War II, he opposed conscription for overseas service, reflecting the prevailing sentiment in French Canada.

### Rise to Power
Trudeau emerged as a prominent critic of Quebec's conservative Union Nationale government, co-founding the dissident journal *Cité Libre*. He joined the Liberal Party in 1965, believing the social democratic NDP could not achieve power. As Minister of Justice (1967-68), he gained national attention by liberalizing social laws, famously stating there was "no place for the state in the bedrooms of the nation." His charisma sparked "Trudeaumania," leading to his election as Liberal leader and Prime Minister in 1968.

### First Premiership (1968–1979)
Trudeau's first government focused on social reform and asserting federal authority. He implemented official bilingualism through the *Official Languages Act* and introduced a national multiculturalism policy. His response to the 1970 October Crisis, in which he invoked the War Measures Act to combat the FLQ, was controversial but widely supported. Economically, his government expanded social programs, introduced the capital gains tax, and created Petro-Canada. However, large deficits, high inflation, and the National Energy Program (NEP) generated significant opposition, particularly in Western Canada, fostering "Western alienation." He won elections in 1968, 1972 (minority), and 1974 (majority) before losing to Joe Clark in 1979.

### Second Premiership and Constitutional Legacy (1980–1984)
After defeating Clark's minority government in 1980, Trudeau's second term was dominated by the economy and the constitution. He led the federalist campaign to victory in the 1980 Quebec sovereignty referendum. His government introduced the NEP, which Alberta Premier Peter Lougheed called "the most discriminatory action taken by a federal government against a particular province." His crowning achievement was the patriation of the Constitution in 1982. The *Constitution Act, 1982*, which included the *Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms*, achieved full Canadian sovereignty but was signed without Quebec's consent, a point of lasting acrimony. He retired in 1984, citing a "walk in the snow."

### Post-Political Life and Legacy
In retirement, Trudeau practiced law and remained an influential voice, successfully opposing the Meech Lake and Charlottetown constitutional accords. He is ranked highly by scholars as a prime minister who maintained national unity but is a divisive figure. Admirers praise his intellect and defense of a unified, bilingual Canada. Critics accuse him of arrogance, economic mismanagement, and centralizing power at the expense of regional and provincial interests. His eldest son, Justin Trudeau, became Prime Minister in 2015.

## Terms
- **Official Bilingualism:** The policy, enacted via the *Official Languages Act* (1969), making French and English the co-equal official languages of the federal government.
- **Multiculturalism:** A 1971 policy recognizing Canada's cultural diversity within a bilingual framework, the first of its kind in the world.
- **War Measures Act:** A federal law granting the government emergency powers, invoked by Trudeau during the 1970 October Crisis to arrest suspected FLQ members without trial.
- **Patriation:** The process of transferring the power to amend Canada's Constitution from the British Parliament to Canadian authorities, completed in 1982.
- **Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms:** A bill of rights entrenched in the Constitution Act, 1982, guaranteeing civil rights and liberties.
- **National Energy Program (NEP):** A 1980 federal policy to secure oil self-sufficiency and redistribute energy revenues, fiercely opposed by oil-producing provinces like Alberta.
- **Western Alienation:** A political sentiment in Western Canada stemming from the perception that federal policies, especially the NEP, unfairly favored Central Canada.
- **October Crisis:** The 1970 kidnapping of a British diplomat and a Quebec minister by the FLQ separatist group, leading to the invocation of the War Measures Act.
- **Trudeaumania:** The media term for the intense public excitement and celebrity-like status Trudeau attracted during his rise to power in 1968.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Economic Management:** Scholars debate whether Trudeau's deficit spending and state intervention (like the NEP) were necessary responses to global crises or caused long-term economic harm and regional division.
- **Constitutional Legacy:** The patriation of the Constitution is celebrated for entrenching rights but criticized for being done without Quebec's consent, which some argue fueled subsequent sovereignty movements.
- **Use of the War Measures Act:** The necessity and proportionality of invoking the Act during the October Crisis remain debated, with critics viewing it as an overreach of state power.
- **Centralization vs. Federalism:** Trudeau's vision of a unified, bilingual state is seen by admirers as essential for national unity and by critics as an attempt to diminish provincial autonomy and cultural distinctiveness, particularly in Quebec and the West.
