# La Malinche

## Overview
La Malinche (c. 1500–c. 1529), also known as Marina or Malintzin, was a Nahua woman from the Gulf Coast of Mexico who became Hernán Cortés's primary interpreter, advisor, and consort during the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire (1519–1521). Her multilingual skills were crucial to the Spanish campaign, and her son with Cortés is considered one of the first Mestizos (people of mixed European and Indigenous ancestry). Her legacy is deeply contested in Mexican culture, viewed variously as a traitor, a victim, or a symbolic mother of the nation.

## Timeline
- **c. 1500** — Born in the altepetl (town-state) of Olutla, near the Coatzacoalcos River.
- **c. 1508–1512** — Sold or kidnapped into slavery as a child, eventually reaching the Maya port of Potonchán.
- **1519** — Given to Hernán Cortés by the Maya of Potonchán; her language skills are discovered.
- **1519** — Acts as interpreter during the first meeting with Moctezuma's emissaries at San Juan de Ulúa.
- **1519** — Helps negotiate the critical alliance with the Tlaxcalans.
- **1519** — Allegedly uncovers a plot against the Spanish in Cholula, leading to a preemptive massacre.
- **1519** — Present at the Spanish entry into Tenochtitlan, translating between Cortés and Moctezuma.
- **1521** — Tenochtitlan falls; her son, Martín Cortés, is born in 1522.
- **1524–1526** — Accompanies Cortés to Honduras as an interpreter during a rebellion.
- **Before 1529** — Marries Spanish hidalgo Juan Jaramillo; dies before February 1529.

## Body

### Background and Early Life
Malinche's birth name is unknown. She was baptized Marina and later called Malintzin by the Nahua, a form that was heard by the Spanish as "Malinche." The title Tenepal, often attached to her name, likely means "one who speaks vigorously." She was born around 1500 into a noble family in Olutla, a tributary state east of the Aztec Empire. As a child, she was sold into slavery, passed through Xicalango, and eventually acquired by Chontal Maya in Potonchán, where she learned Chontal and possibly Yucatec Maya.

### Role in the Conquest
In 1519, after a battle at Potonchán, Malinche was among twenty enslaved women given to the Spanish. Cortés took her as a consort and discovered her ability to speak Nahuatl. She became his essential interpreter, first working with Gerónimo de Aguilar (who spoke Yucatec Maya and Spanish) to create a translation chain. Her skills were vital in negotiating alliances, such as with the Tlaxcalans, and in communicating with Moctezuma. She is credited with uncovering a supposed plot in Cholula, though modern historians suggest this story may have been fabricated to justify the subsequent massacre. She was present at the Spanish entry into Tenochtitlan in 1519 and the city's fall in 1521.

### Later Life and Legacy
After the conquest, Malinche lived in Coyoacán and bore Cortés a son, Martín. She accompanied Cortés to Honduras (1524–1526) and later married Juan Jaramillo. She died before 1529. Her role has been debated for centuries. Conquistador accounts, like Bernal Díaz del Castillo's, emphasize her indispensable importance. Indigenous sources, such as the *Lienzo de Tlaxcala*, sometimes portray her as a powerful, independent figure. Historians disagree on her agency: some see her as a skilled diplomat, while others argue her choices were made for survival within a context of enslavement. In modern Mexico, she is a complex icon—symbolizing both the treacherous *malinchista* (one who prefers foreign culture) and the mother of the mestizo people.

## Terms
- **Altepetl**: A Mesoamerican political-territorial unit, often translated as "town-state."
- **Mestizo**: A person of mixed European and Indigenous American ancestry.
- **Nahuatl**: The language of the Aztec Empire and the lingua franca of central Mesoamerica.
- **Tecpillahtolli**: A formal, reverential register of the Nahuatl language used by nobility.
- **Hidalgo**: A Spanish nobleman of lower rank.
- **Malinchista**: A Mexican term for a person perceived as betraying their culture by favoring foreign influences.
- **Chontal Maya**: An Indigenous group of the Gulf Coast; their language was one Malinche learned.
- **Probanza**: A Spanish legal proceeding to establish facts or claims.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Her Agency and Motives**: Was Malinche a willing collaborator, a pragmatic survivor, or a victim of circumstance? Historians debate the extent of her autonomy and whether her diplomacy was intentional or a means of survival.
- **The Cholula Plot**: Did Malinche genuinely uncover a conspiracy against the Spanish, or was the story a later fabrication to justify the massacre and strengthen alliances with the Tlaxcalans?
- **Her Historical Impact**: Some historians argue she was the "real conqueror" due to her diplomatic skills, while others, like Inga Clendinnen, suggest key communications were nonverbal and her role has been overstated.
- **Cultural Symbolism**: In Mexico, she remains a polarizing figure—viewed as a foundational mother of the nation or as the archetypal traitor, a debate that continues in feminist and postcolonial scholarship.
