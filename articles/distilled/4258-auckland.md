# Auckland

## Overview
Auckland is New Zealand's largest and most populous city, located on the North Island. It is the nation's primary economic hub and a major multicultural metropolis, known for its unique geography between two harbours and its volcanic landscape. Its growth from a Māori settlement to a modern "super city" reflects broader patterns of colonialism, immigration, and economic development in New Zealand.

## Timeline
- **c. 1350** — Auckland isthmus first settled by Māori.
- **1840** — Treaty of Waitangi signed; Ngāti Whātua Ōrākei gifts land for a new capital.
- **1841** — Auckland officially declared New Zealand's capital.
- **1865** — Capital status transferred to Wellington.
- **1959** — Auckland Harbour Bridge opens, connecting the North Shore.
- **1986** — Immigration policy changes allow increased Asian immigration.
- **2010** — Auckland's multiple councils amalgamate into a single "super city" (Auckland Council).
- **2023** — Census records Auckland's urban population as 1,402,275.

## Body

### Early History and Colonial Foundation
The Auckland isthmus was settled by Māori around 1350, valued for its fertile volcanic soil. By the early 1700s, the Te Waiohua confederation was the main tribal group, later supplanted by Ngāti Whātua Ōrākei. The Māori population peaked at an estimated 20,000 before European arrival. Following the 1840 Treaty of Waitangi, Ngāti Whātua Ōrākei gifted land to Governor William Hobson, who founded Auckland as the new capital in 1840. The city grew as a military and commercial base, with its population rising from 1,500 in 1841 to over 12,000 by 1864.

### Modern Development and Economic Shift
Auckland lost its capital status to Wellington in 1865 but continued to grow as a port and service centre for regional logging, gold mining, and later, dairy farming. In the 20th century, transport evolved from trams to a car-dominated system, enabling suburban expansion. Economic deregulation in the mid-1980s shifted the national economic nerve centre from Wellington to Auckland. The city's demographic transformation began with 1986 immigration policy changes, leading to a significant increase in Asian and Polynesian populations.

### Geography and Volcanic Field
Auckland is situated on a narrow isthmus between the Waitematā Harbour (Pacific Ocean) and the Manukau Harbour (Tasman Sea). The landscape is defined by the Auckland Volcanic Field, comprising at least 53 volcanic centres formed over the last ~193,000 years. This monogenetic field (each volcano erupts once) is fed by mantle magma and is unrelated to the Taupō Volcanic Zone. The most recent eruption was at Rangitoto c. 1450 AD. The volcanic cones were historically used as Māori pā (fortified villages) and later quarried for building materials.

### Demographics and Culture
Auckland is New Zealand's most multicultural city. The 2023 census recorded its urban population as 1,402,275, with a median age of 35.1 years. Ethnic composition (people could identify with multiple groups) was 44.0% European, 34.9% Asian, 18.7% Pasifika, and 12.2% Māori. In 2016, it had the world's fourth-largest foreign-born population (39%). The city is home to the world's largest urban Polynesian population. Religious diversity includes 35.7% Christian, 6.1% Hindu, and 3.3% Muslim affiliations.

### Economy and Infrastructure
Auckland is New Zealand's major financial and commercial centre, with a 2025 GDP estimated at $167 billion NZD. Key industries include professional services, manufacturing, retail, and tourism. The city is served by Auckland Airport (handling 18.5 million passengers in 2024) and major ports. Transport is heavily car-dependent, with a network of motorways and four suburban railway lines. The City Rail Link project aims to improve public transport. Housing affordability is a significant issue, with median prices far exceeding national averages.

## Terms
- **Pā**: A Māori fortified village or settlement, often built on strategic elevated sites like volcanic cones.
- **Isthmus**: A narrow strip of land connecting two larger land areas, bordered by water on both sides. Auckland is built on one.
- **Monogenetic volcanic field**: A volcanic area where each volcano erupts only once, usually over a short period, before activity ceases.
- **Super city**: The colloquial term for the single Auckland Council local authority created in 2010 by amalgamating previous councils.
- **Pasifika**: A term referring to people of Pacific Island heritage, a major demographic group in Auckland.
- **Māori**: The indigenous Polynesian people of New Zealand.
- **Pākehā**: A Māori term for New Zealanders of European descent.
- **Kūmara**: The Māori word for sweet potato, a traditional crop cultivated in volcanic soils.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Housing Crisis**: There is significant public debate over the causes of Auckland's high housing costs, often citing a lack of land supply, easy credit, and the city's high liveability.
- **Future Volcanic Activity**: The Auckland Volcanic Field remains active, and future eruptions are considered a threat, though the location of any new eruption is unknown.
- **Transport Policy**: Auckland's historical pro-automobile policies have led to high car dependency and congestion, sparking ongoing debate about the effectiveness and future of public transport investments like the City Rail Link.
