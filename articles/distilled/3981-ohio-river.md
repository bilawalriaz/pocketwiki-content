# Ohio River

## Overview
The Ohio River is a 981-mile-long river in the United States, formed by the confluence of the Allegheny and Monongahela rivers at Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. It flows southwest to join the Mississippi River at Cairo, Illinois, serving as a major tributary and a historic boundary between the Midwestern and Southern U.S. The river is vital for transportation, industry, and drinking water, but it is also one of the nation's most polluted waterways.

## Timeline
- **c. 2.5–3 million years ago** — The Ohio River begins forming from glacial activity and ancient river systems.
- **1693** — Arnout Viele leads the first known European exploration of the upper Ohio Valley.
- **1729** — Gaspard-Joseph Chaussegros de Léry conducts the first survey and mapping of the river.
- **1749** — The Ohio Company is established to settle and trade in the Ohio Valley.
- **1763** — The Treaty of Paris ends the French and Indian War; France cedes territory east of the Mississippi to Britain.
- **1778** — Louisville is founded at the Falls of the Ohio.
- **1783** — The Treaty of Paris grants the entire Ohio Valley to the United States.
- **1803** — The Louisiana Purchase secures U.S. access to the Mississippi River and Gulf of Mexico.
- **1818** — The National Road reaches Wheeling, Virginia (now West Virginia), connecting the river to the East.
- **1830** — The Louisville and Portland Canal opens, bypassing the Falls of the Ohio for navigation.
- **1929** — The river is "canalized" with locks and dams, creating a series of pools for navigation.
- **1967** — The Silver Bridge at Point Pleasant, West Virginia, collapses, killing 46 people.
- **2023** — A Norfolk Southern train derailment in East Palestine, Ohio, raises major pollution concerns.

## Body

### Etymology and Early Significance
The name "Ohio" derives from the Seneca word *Ohi:yo'*, meaning "good river." Native Americans, including the Mississippian culture, used the river as a major transportation and trading route for millennia. European exploration began in the late 17th century, with figures like Arnout Viele (1693) and surveyors like Gaspard-Joseph Chaussegros de Léry (1729) mapping its course.

### Colonial and Early American History
The river became a focal point of colonial conflict between Britain and France, culminating in the French and Indian War. After 1763, it served as a boundary for Native American lands and later as the southern border of the U.S. Northwest Territory. In the early 19th century, it was considered the western extension of the Mason–Dixon line, dividing free and slave states. Thousands of enslaved people crossed it to freedom via the Underground Railroad.

### Navigation and Engineering
The river's natural navigation was obstructed by the Falls of the Ohio, a 26-foot drop near Louisville. The Louisville and Portland Canal (1830) and later the McAlpine Locks and Dam bypassed this barrier. Since the "canalization" project of 1929, the Ohio is no longer free-flowing but is divided into 21 pools by 20 locks and dams for navigation and power generation.

### Geography and Climate
The Ohio River is the largest tributary of the Mississippi by volume. Its drainage basin covers 203,940 square miles across 14 states. It acts as a climatic transition zone between humid continental and humid subtropical climates, freezing regularly in Pittsburgh but remaining ice-free year-round near its confluence with the Mississippi.

### Ecology and Pollution
The river supports diverse ecosystems but has suffered significant species decline due to pollution and invasive species like zebra mussels. It is consistently ranked as one of the most polluted rivers in the U.S., with major contaminants including mercury, nitrates from farm runoff, and per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) from industrial discharge. A 2020 report found heavy industry dumped over 41 million pounds of toxic pollution into its watershed.

### Economy and Communities
The Ohio River is a major industrial corridor for barge traffic carrying oil, steel, and other goods. Cities along its banks include Pittsburgh, Cincinnati, Louisville, and Evansville. However, many communities in the Appalachian portion of its basin face deep poverty and environmental injustice, bearing a disproportionate burden of pollution-related health issues.

## Terms
- **Canalization**: The engineering process of building locks and dams to create a navigable, controlled waterway.
- **Drainage basin**: The area of land where all water drains into a common outlet, like a river.
- **Falls of the Ohio**: A series of rapids near Louisville where the river drops 26 feet, historically a major navigation barrier.
- **Humid continental climate**: A climate with warm to cool summers and cold winters, with significant seasonal temperature variation.
- **Humid subtropical climate**: A climate with hot, humid summers and mild winters.
- **Locks and dams**: Structures built to raise and lower water levels, allowing boats to navigate changes in elevation.
- **PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances)**: Synthetic chemicals known as "forever chemicals" that persist in the environment and are linked to health problems.
- **Underground Railroad**: A network of secret routes and safe houses used by enslaved African Americans to escape into free states and Canada.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Pollution Responsibility**: There is ongoing debate over the enforcement of environmental regulations and the responsibility of industries versus regulatory agencies like the EPA for the river's persistent pollution.
- **Environmental Justice**: Scholars and activists question the fairness of how economically disadvantaged communities along the river bear the brunt of industrial pollution and its health consequences.
- **Legacy of Industrialization**: The long-term ecological and health impacts of historical pollution from coal, steel, and chemical industries remain a subject of study and concern.
