# Nefertiti

## Overview
Nefertiti was the Great Royal Wife of Pharaoh Akhenaten during Egypt's 18th Dynasty (c. 1370–1330 BC). She and her husband led a radical religious revolution, promoting the exclusive worship of the sun disc Aten. Her historical significance is amplified by scholarly debate over whether she ruled as a female pharaoh after Akhenaten's death, and by the global fame of her iconic sculpted bust.

## Timeline
- **c. 1370 BC** — Nefertiti is born.
- **c. 1353 BC** — Marries Amenhotep IV (later Akhenaten); becomes Great Royal Wife.
- **c. 1348 BC** — Akhenaten moves the capital to Akhetaten (Amarna); Nefertiti's name changes to Neferneferuaten-Nefertiti.
- **c. 1346 BC** — Nefertiti is depicted with unprecedented prominence, possibly as a co-regent.
- **c. 1336 BC** — Akhenaten dies in his 17th regnal year.
- **c. 1336–1334 BC** — Nefertiti possibly rules as the female pharaoh Neferneferuaten.
- **c. 1334 BC** — Tutankhaten (later Tutankhamun) ascends the throne; Amarna is abandoned.

## Body

### Religious Revolution and Royal Partnership
Nefertiti first appears in scenes in Thebes during the early reign of Amenhotep IV. She and the king promoted Atenism, a religion centered on the sun disc Aten, shifting Egypt from polytheism to a system possibly described as monolatry (worship of one god without denying others). The capital was moved to the new city of Akhetaten (Amarna) around Year 4 of Akhenaten's reign. Nefertiti was depicted with exceptional frequency and in roles typically reserved for the king, such as smiting enemies, indicating her high political status.

### Possible Reign as Pharaoh
A key 2012 discovery—an ink inscription from Year 16 of Akhenaten's reign—confirms Nefertiti was alive and held her title as Great Royal Wife until near the end of his reign. This evidence supports the theory that she became the female pharaoh Neferneferuaten after Akhenaten's death, ruling briefly before the young Tutankhamun. Scholars like James P. Allen now identify Neferneferuaten as Nefertiti, arguing she was appointed to secure the succession. Her reign may have initiated a partial return to traditional religion.

### Death, Burial, and Identification
Nefertiti's intended burial was likely in the Royal Tomb at Amarna, but she was probably not interred there. Her actual burial place is unknown. Several mummies have been proposed as hers, including the "Younger Lady" from KV35 (later identified as Tutankhamun's mother) and a mummy from KV21B. None are definitively identified. In 2015, a theory proposed her tomb was hidden behind Tutankhamun's, but radar scans found no chambers.

### The "Dakhamunzu" Letter
A Hittite document records a letter from an Egyptian queen ("Dakhamunzu") whose husband had died, asking for a Hittite prince to marry. The sender's identity is debated; candidates include Nefertiti, Meritaten, or Ankhesenamun. Recent chronology adjustments make Nefertiti a plausible candidate, as she held significant power.

## Terms
*   **Atenism:** The religious system promoted by Akhenaten and Nefertiti, focusing on the worship of the sun disc Aten.
*   **Great Royal Wife:** The principal wife of the pharaoh, a title of high political and religious status.
*   **Co-regent:** A ruler who shares power with the reigning monarch.
*   **Neferneferuaten:** The throne name (prenomen) of the female pharaoh who ruled after Akhenaten, widely believed to be Nefertiti.
*   **Amarna:** The modern name for the site of Akhetaten, the capital city built by Akhenaten.
*   **Ushabti:** A funerary figurine placed in tombs to serve the deceased in the afterlife.
*   **Cartouche:** An oval ring enclosing a royal name, used in hieroglyphs.
*   **Monolatry:** A religious system that worships one god without denying the existence of others.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Parentage:** Nefertiti's parents are unknown. The official Ay is a likely father, but this is based on titles and speculation, not direct evidence.
*   **Identity of Neferneferuaten:** While many scholars now identify the female pharaoh as Nefertiti, some argue it could be her daughter Meritaten.
*   **The Dakhamunzu Letter:** The identity of the Egyptian queen who wrote to the Hittites remains uncertain, with Nefertiti being a strong candidate.
*   **Burial and Remains:** The location of Nefertiti's tomb and the identity of her mummy are unresolved mysteries.
