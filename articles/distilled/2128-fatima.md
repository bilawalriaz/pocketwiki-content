# Fatima

## Overview
Fatima bint Muhammad (c. 605/15–632 CE), daughter of the Islamic prophet Muhammad and his wife Khadija, is a central figure in Islam, especially revered in Shia tradition. She is the wife of Ali ibn Abi Talib (the fourth Rashidun caliph and first Shia imam) and mother of Hasan and Husayn (the second and third Shia imams). Her life and the events following Muhammad's death are pivotal in the Sunni-Shia divide, particularly regarding the succession to Muhammad and the status of his family (Ahl al-Bayt). She is viewed as an archetype of piety, compassion, and enduring suffering, and it is through her lineage that Muhammad's family line continues.

## Timeline
- **c. 605/15 CE** — Fatima is born in Mecca to Muhammad and Khadija.
- **c. 623–625 CE** — Fatima marries Ali ibn Abi Talib in Medina.
- **632 CE** — Muhammad dies; succession crisis leads to Abu Bakr becoming caliph.
- **632 CE** — Fatima contests Abu Bakr's claim to the caliphate and her inheritance of Fadak.
- **632 CE** — An altercation occurs at Fatima's house involving Umar and Ali's supporters.
- **632 CE** — Fatima dies within six months of Muhammad, at a young age.

## Body

### Early Life and Marriage
Fatima was born in Mecca to Khadija. Her exact birth year is debated: Sunni sources often place it in 605 CE, while Twelver Shia sources suggest c. 612–615 CE. She grew up during the early persecution of Muslims and lost her mother Khadija in childhood. She married her cousin Ali around 1–2 AH (623–625 CE). Their marriage, performed by Muhammad, is seen as spiritually significant. They lived in poverty initially, with both performing hard labor. Their circumstances improved later, and they had several children, including sons Hasan and Husayn.

### Dispute over Succession and Inheritance
Following Muhammad's death in 632 CE, Abu Bakr was chosen as caliph at the Saqifa assembly, an event from which Muhammad's family was largely absent. Fatima, supporting Ali's claim to leadership based on Muhammad's designation at Ghadir Khumm, contested Abu Bakr's authority. A central dispute was over Fadak, a village whose revenues had supported Muhammad's family. Fatima claimed it was a gift from her father, but Abu Bakr seized it, citing a hadith that prophets do not leave inheritance. Fatima protested, delivering the "Sermon of Fadak" and quoting Quranic verses on inheritance. Abu Bakr rejected her claim, and she demanded her general inheritance from Muhammad's estate, which he also denied based on the same hadith.

### Conflict with the Caliphate and Death
In the aftermath of the Saqifa affair, Umar, acting on Abu Bakr's orders, led a group to Ali's house to secure his pledge of allegiance. Shia sources allege this raid involved violence, resulting in Fatima's injury and miscarriage of her son Muhsin. Sunni sources generally reject the allegations of violence and miscarriage. Fatima died within six months of Muhammad, at a young age (18 or 27, per Shia and Sunni sources). The cause of death is disputed: Sunnis attribute it to grief, while Shia sources link it to injuries from the raid. She reportedly never reconciled with Abu Bakr and Umar. Following her will, Ali buried her secretly at night, and her exact burial place remains uncertain.

### Legacy and Descendants
Fatima is the progenitor of all of Muhammad's surviving descendants. Her children with Ali were Hasan, Husayn, and daughters Zaynab and Umm Kulthum. The fate of a third son, Muhsin, is contested (miscarriage vs. natural death). Her descendants are given the honorific titles *sayyid* or *sharif*. The Fatimid dynasty (909–1171 CE) claimed descent from her. She is venerated as "Mistress of the Women of Paradise" and is a symbol of piety and suffering, especially in Shia Islam, where her pain is seen as redemptive.

## Terms
- **Ahl al-Bayt**: "People of the House." In Shia Islam, this specifically refers to Muhammad, Fatima, Ali, Hasan, and Husayn. In Sunni Islam, it often includes Muhammad's wives.
- **Fadak**: A village near Medina whose revenues were a point of contention between Fatima and Caliph Abu Bakr over inheritance rights.
- **Hadith**: A recorded saying or action of the Islamic prophet Muhammad.
- **Imam**: In Shia Islam, the divinely appointed, infallible leader of the Muslim community after Muhammad, beginning with Ali.
- **Mahr**: A mandatory gift or payment from the groom to the bride in an Islamic marriage.
- **Rashidun**: The first four "rightly guided" caliphs after Muhammad: Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, and Ali.
- **Sayyid/Sharif**: Honorific titles for descendants of the Prophet Muhammad.
- **Saqifa**: The courtyard where the Ansar (Medinan natives) gathered to choose a successor to Muhammad, leading to the selection of Abu Bakr.
- **Shia**: A major branch of Islam that believes leadership after Muhammad should have passed directly to Ali and his descendants.
- **Sunni**: The largest branch of Islam, which holds that the caliphate was rightfully determined by community consensus.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause of Fatima's Death**: Sunnis attribute it to grief, while Shia sources claim it resulted from injuries sustained during a raid on her house by Umar.
- **The Raid on Fatima's House**: The nature and extent of the confrontation are heavily disputed. Shia sources allege violence and miscarriage; Sunni sources generally deny this, though some acknowledge a confrontation.
- **Inheritance of Fadak**: Whether Fadak was a personal gift to Fatima from Muhammad or part of his public estate is a core dispute, hinging on the interpretation of Quranic verses and the authenticity of the hadith of prophetic non-inheritance.
- **Composition of the Ahl al-Bayt**: A key theological difference: Shia restrict it to the "People of the Cloak" (Muhammad, Ali, Fatima, Hasan, Husayn), while many Sunni views include Muhammad's wives.
- **Fatima's Reconciliation**: Whether Fatima reconciled with Abu Bakr and Umar before her death is debated, with Sunni reports of reconciliation often viewed by Shia as later inventions.
