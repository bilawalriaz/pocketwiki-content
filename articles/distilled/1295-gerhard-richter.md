# Gerhard Richter

## Overview

Gerhard Richter (b. 1932) is a German visual artist whose career spans photorealistic paintings, abstract works, photographs, glass sculptures, and installations. Emerging from post-war East Germany and later resettling in West Germany, Richter co-founded the anti-style *Kapitalistischer Realismus* (Capitalist Realism), critiquing both Socialist Realism and Western consumer culture. His work oscillates between representation and abstraction, often using photography as a starting point, and he has become one of the most influential and commercially successful contemporary artists.

## Body

### Early Life and Political Context

Born in Dresden during the final years of the German Empire, Richter grew up under National Socialism. His father, a teacher, was compelled to join the Nazi Party but remained apolitical. Family trauma—including the death of an aunt in a Nazi euthanasia program—deeply influenced his later artistic engagement with German history. After apprenticing as a sign painter and being rejected from art school for being “too bourgeois,” he studied in Dresden before fleeing to West Germany in 1957.

### Artistic Beginnings and Capitalist Realism

In Düsseldorf, Richter joined the Kunstakademie and co-coined *Kapitalistischer Realismus* with Sigmar Polke and Konrad Lueg. This anti-style appropriated advertising aesthetics to critique both Soviet Socialist Realism and Western consumerism. His early photo-paintings, rendered in blurred grayscale, questioned memory, media, and historical narrative. Works like *Helga Matura* (1966) and *Forty-eight Portraits* (1971–72) exemplify this phase.

### Photo-Paintings and the Blur

From the 1960s through the 1970s, Richter painted from found and self-taken photographs, applying a distinctive blur technique. This method obscured clarity, evoking uncertainty and the mediation of images. Series such as *18 October 1977*, based on the deaths of Red Army Faction members, used blurred newspaper photos to reflect on state violence and ideological conflict. The blur became a signature device, destabilizing perception and meaning.

### Abstract Work

Beginning in the late 1960s, Richter produced grey monochromes and, from 1976, titled works simply *Abstract Painting*. Using brushes, scrapers, and later a homemade squeegee, he built layered compositions through gestural addition and subtraction. These works balanced spontaneity with control, often resembling landscapes despite their non-representational nature. The squeegee technique, refined in the 1980s, became central to his mature abstraction.

### Color Charts and Conceptual Systems

Starting in 1966, Richter explored color charts as systematic, impersonal sources. Collaborating with friend Blinky Palermo, he used chance operations and mathematical permutations to arrange hues. Series like *256 Colours* (1973–74) and *4900 Colours* (2007) reduced painting to modular grids, questioning authorship and aesthetic hierarchy. These works paralleled his stained-glass designs, including the Cologne Cathedral window (2007), composed of 11,500 randomly arranged colored squares.

### Glass, Mirrors, and Sculpture

Since 1967, Richter has worked with glass, beginning with *Four Panes of Glass*. His transparent and mirrored sculptures challenge spatial perception and viewer interaction. Monumental mirrors and glass panels, such as *Spiegel I* and *Spiegel II* (1989), create immersive environments. Later installations, like the Dia Art Foundation’s glass sculpture (2002), refract light and space, extending his exploration of materiality and illusion.

### Still Lifes and Historical Memory

In the 1980s, Richter painted *Candles* and *Skulls*, referencing vanitas traditions. These works juxtaposed quiet beauty with mortality, reflecting on loss and remembrance. His *Birkenau* series (2014), based on clandestine Auschwitz photographs, marked a return to explicit historical trauma. Donated to the International Auschwitz Committee, these paintings confront the limits of representation in depicting genocide.

### Prints, Drawings, and Collaborations

Active in printmaking from 1965 to 1974, Richter experimented with screenprint, photolithography, and collotype to achieve a “non-art” aesthetic. His drawings, often mechanically assisted, reveal a self-critical approach to hand-drawing. Collaborations with composers like Steve Reich (*Firenze*, 1999) and musicians such as Sonic Youth highlight cross-media influences.

### Commissions and Public Works

Though typically avoiding private commissions, Richter accepted projects like the Siemens *Domplatz, Mailand* (1968) and the Reichstag wall piece (1998). His church windows—Cologne Cathedral (2007) and Tholey Abbey (2020)—demonstrate his ability to merge conceptual rigor with spiritual symbolism. He waived fees for the Cologne window, emphasizing public access over personal gain.

## Terms

- **Kapitalistischer Realismus**: An anti-style of art co-introduced by Richter, Sigmar Polke, and Konrad Lueg, critiquing both Socialist Realism and Western consumer culture.
- **Blur**: A painting technique used by Richter to obscure photographic source material, evoking uncertainty and the mediation of images.
- **Atlas**: An ongoing, encyclopedic collection of approximately 4,000 photographs and illustrations mounted on panels, begun in 1961.
- **Squeegee Paintings**: Abstract works created using a homemade tool to drag and scrape paint, producing layered, dynamic surfaces.
- **Overpainted Photographs (*Übermalte Fotografien*)**: A series where Richter drags wet paint over photographs, merging abstraction with figuration.
- **Color Chart Paintings**: Systematic compositions based on industrial color charts, exploring chance, permutation, and conceptual order.
- **Vanitas**: A still-life tradition symbolizing the transience of life; referenced in Richter’s *Candles* and *Skulls* series.
- **Memento Mori**: Latin for “remember you must die”; theme in Richter’s candle and skull paintings.
- **Capitalist Realism**: See *Kapitalistischer Realismus*.
- **Stained Glass Windows**: Richter’s abstract designs for Cologne Cathedral and Tholey Abbey, integrating modernist form with sacred space.

## Debates and Open Questions

Richter’s relationship with his East German past remains debated. While he escaped in 1957, his early murals and portraits reflect a complex negotiation with Socialist Realism and state ideology. Scholars question whether his later abstract works fully resolve or merely aestheticize historical trauma. The *18 October 1977* series sparked controversy for its detached treatment of RAF deaths, raising questions about the ethics of representing political violence. Additionally, Richter’s market success—with paintings selling for tens of millions—contrasts with his stated indifference to price, prompting discussion about the commodification of dissent in contemporary art.
