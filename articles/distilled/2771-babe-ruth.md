# Babe Ruth

## Overview
George Herman "Babe" Ruth (1895–1948) was an American professional baseball player who transformed the sport from a low-scoring, strategic game into one dominated by the home run. His career spanned 22 seasons (1914–1935), primarily with the Boston Red Sox and New York Yankees. He is widely regarded as one of the greatest sports heroes in American culture and a foundational figure in baseball's modern era.

## Body

### Early Life and Development
Ruth was sent at age seven to St. Mary's Industrial School for Boys, a reformatory and orphanage, where he lived for most of his youth. He learned baseball there, excelling as a pitcher and hitter, and was mentored by Brother Matthias Boutlier. In 1914, he was signed by the minor-league Baltimore Orioles and quickly sold to the Boston Red Sox.

### Boston Red Sox Career (1914–1919)
Ruth began as a star left-handed pitcher, winning 89 games with a 2.19 ERA. He was a key member of three World Series championship teams (1915, 1916, 1918). However, he desired to play every day. In 1918, manager Ed Barrow began using him as an outfielder when not pitching. In 1919, Ruth broke the single-season home run record with 29, signaling a shift in his role and the sport's style.

### Sale to the Yankees and the "Live-Ball Era"
After the 1919 season, Red Sox owner Harry Frazee sold Ruth to the New York Yankees for $100,000 and a loan. This transaction is popularly blamed for Boston's subsequent 86-year championship drought, known as the "Curse of the Bambino." Ruth's power hitting with the Yankees helped usher in baseball's "live-ball era," a shift from a low-scoring, strategic game to one where the home run became a major factor. This change was aided by rule changes and the use of livelier baseballs starting in 1920.

### New York Yankees Stardom (1920–1934)
Ruth became the face of the Yankees, leading them to seven American League pennants and four World Series titles. He repeatedly broke his own single-season home run record, culminating in 60 in 1927 with the legendary "Murderers' Row" lineup. His massive popularity drew record crowds, boosted baseball's national appeal, and led to the construction of Yankee Stadium in 1923. His career home run total reached 714.

### Final Years and Retirement
Ruth's last full season with the Yankees was 1934. He finished his career with the Boston Braves in 1935, hitting three home runs in his final game. Despite his fame, he was denied a major league managerial position, likely due to his well-publicized off-field behavior. He remained a public figure, supporting the war effort during World War II, before dying of cancer in 1948.

## Terms
- **Dead-ball era**: The period before 1920 characterized by low-scoring games, few home runs, and a focus on strategy like bunting and base-stealing.
- **Live-ball era**: The period beginning around 1920, marked by higher-scoring games and a dramatic increase in home runs, influenced by rule changes and livelier baseballs.
- **Murderers' Row**: The nickname for the New York Yankees' powerful lineup, particularly the 1927 team featuring Ruth and Lou Gehrig.
- **Curse of the Bambino**: A popular superstition explaining the Boston Red Sox's 86-year championship drought (1918–2004) as punishment for selling Ruth to the Yankees.
- **Slugging percentage**: A statistic measuring a batter's power, calculated as total bases divided by at-bats.
- **On-base percentage**: A statistic measuring how frequently a batter reaches base via hits, walks, or hit-by-pitches.
- **Called shot**: The legendary moment in the 1932 World Series where Ruth allegedly pointed to center field before hitting a home run to that spot.
- **Nasopharyngeal cancer**: The rare cancer at the base of the skull that caused Ruth's death.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact cause of Ruth's severe 1925 illness, dubbed "the bellyache heard 'round the world," remains a mystery, with theories ranging from alcohol abuse to other ailments.
- The authenticity of Ruth's "called shot" in the 1932 World Series is perpetually debated, with no definitive proof of his intent.
- The primary reason Ruth was never offered a major league managerial job is attributed by historians to his flamboyant personal life and perceived inability to manage himself, though this prevented him from being fairly evaluated.
