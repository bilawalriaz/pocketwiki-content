# Flood

## Overview
A flood is an overflow of water that submerges normally dry land, caused by factors such as heavy rainfall, snowmelt, storm surges, or infrastructure failure. Floods are the single most destructive hazard for global agriculture, causing over USD 1.5 trillion in losses between 1991 and 2023, and pose escalating risks due to climate change intensifying the water cycle and rising sea levels. Human alterations—deforestation, wetland drainage, and impervious urban surfaces—amplify frequency and severity by reducing natural absorption and accelerating runoff. While catastrophic to infrastructure, health, and economies, periodic flooding also provides ecological benefits, recharging groundwater, depositing fertile sediment, and sustaining floodplain biodiversity.

## Timeline
- **At least 6,000 years ago** — Humans begin systematic attempts to understand and manage floodplain mechanisms.
- **1991–2023** — Floods cause >USD 1.5 trillion in agricultural losses globally (FAO).
- **2007** — Bangladesh flood destroys >1 million houses.
- **2010–2011** — Queensland floods (Australia) reveal hazard models based solely on flow velocity/depth are insufficient due to debris dynamics.
- **2015** — HEC-RAS, TUFLOW, and 1D/2D hydraulic models widely available for floodplain mapping.
- **Present** — Global Flood Monitoring System (GFMS) uses NASA/GP satellite data to forecast flooding at 12 km resolution globally, 1 km for inundation maps.

## Body

### Types of Floods
**Areal flooding** occurs on flat or low-lying land when rainfall or snowmelt exceeds infiltration and runoff capacity. Water accumulates in depressions and floodplains; saturated soil, frozen ground, or impervious surfaces (concrete, roofs) halt infiltration. Endorheic basins (closed drainage) flood when precipitation exceeds evaporation.

**River (fluvial) flooding** happens when flow exceeds channel capacity. *Slow-rising floods* dominate large rivers with vast catchments, driven by sustained rain, snowmelt, or monsoons. *Rapid/flash floods* prevail in small, steep, or impermeable basins, or normally dry channels (arroyos), triggered by intense thunderstorms or sudden dam/landslide/glacier release. A hybrid *overland flooding* occurs in extremely flat terrain (e.g., Red River Valley, former Lake Agassiz bed, slope ~5 in/mi), where spring melt spills overbank and travels miles unpredictably across the glacial lakebed.

**Coastal flooding** results from storm surges (tropical or extratropical cyclones) combining with high tides and waves, or from tsunamis. Surge is "an additional rise of water generated by a storm, over and above predicted astronomical tides." Estuary flooding combines surge, low pressure, large waves, and high river flows. Climate change (sea-level rise, stronger storms) and coastal population growth intensify damages.

**Urban flooding** arises when drainage systems are overwhelmed by intense rainfall on impervious surfaces. **Intentional flooding** is a hydraulic engineering tool: agricultural (paddy rice), river-management (diverting water to sacrificial areas, e.g., Dutch *overlaten*), or military (creating obstacles; *controlled* if civilians evacuated, reversible, ecological impact minimized—e.g., Dutch Water Lines; *uncontrolled* if not—e.g., WWII Inundation of Walcheren).

### Causes and Amplifying Factors
**Upslope factors** (watershed): The single most important determinant of flood magnitude is upstream watershed area. For small watersheds (<30 mi² / 80 km²), rainfall intensity ranks second; for large ones, main channel slope ranks second. *Time of Concentration*—time for runoff from the farthest point to reach the controlling channel—defines the critical rainfall duration (minutes for urban drains, days for river basins). Precipitation conversion to runoff ranges from near 0% (light rain on dry ground) to 170% (warm rain on snowpack).

**Downslope factors** (channel/coast): Flow elevation is controlled by channel geometry (depth, slope, sediment), restrictions (bridges, canyons), and downstream boundary conditions (ocean, tidal fluctuations, surge). Vegetation, ice, debris, or structures alter effective geometry. *Coincidence* of factors—warm rain on snowpack, ice jams, beaver dam failures—creates extremes exceeding simple statistical models. Debris (woody vegetation, structures) dynamically modifies channel geometry; 2010–11 Queensland data showed velocity/depth criteria alone cannot capture debris-entrainment hazards.

**Human & climate drivers**: Draining wetlands (natural sponges) and paving surfaces increase runoff volume and speed. ~30% of precipitation becomes runoff; higher with snowmelt. Climate change intensifies the water cycle (more extreme rainfall) and raises sea levels, increasing coastal and fluvial flood risk.

### Negative Impacts
**Infrastructure & society**: Floods demolish buildings, bridges, roads, utilities. Catastrophic riverine floods stem from dam failures, landslides, earthquakes, or volcanic eruptions (lahars, outburst floods). Tsunamis cause catastrophic coastal flooding.

**Economic**: Annual global damages reach billions USD. US: >$7B/year. Bangladesh 2007: >1M houses destroyed. Wet basements lower property values 10–25%. ~40% of US small businesses never reopen post-flood (FEMA). Agriculture: crop loss, livestock death, food shortages, price spikes. Tourism declines, rebuilding costs soar. Psychological trauma (depression, PTSD) is a leading long-term health outcome.

**Health**: Direct fatalities: drowning, trauma from debris. Indirect: dehydration, heat stroke, heart attacks when medical access is cut. Injuries spike during rescue/recovery. Waterborne diseases (cholera, hepatitis A/E, typhoid, giardia, cryptosporidium) surge from sewage contamination and lost treatment. Vector-borne diseases (malaria, dengue, West Nile, yellow fever) rise in residual standing water. Mold in chronically wet homes increases adverse respiratory outcomes 30–50% in coastal/wetland areas; linked to allergic rhinitis and asthma.

### Positive Impacts (Benefits)
Smaller, periodic floods recharge groundwater, deposit nutrient-rich sediment, and enhance soil fertility—foundational to ancient civilizations (Tigris-Euphrates, Nile, Indus, Ganges, Yellow River). They supply water in arid regions, kill pests, maintain river-corridor ecosystems and floodplain biodiversity, boost lake/river biomass and fisheries, provide spawning habitat (e.g., weather fish), and support bird populations. Flood-prone regions favor hydropower viability.

### Flood Management & Protection
**Structural**: Detention basins, levees, bunds, reservoirs, weirs, dams (US Army Corps of Engineers network). Coastal: sea walls, beach nourishment, barrier islands. Riparian erosion control slows meander migration.

**Non-structural / Green Infrastructure**: Repair/expand sewers and stormwater systems; reduce impervious surfaces via porous paving, natural drainage, wetlands (SUDS). Convert flood-prone zones to parks/playgrounds. Ordinances: on-site stormwater retention, elevated/flood-proofed buildings. Property-level: re-grading, rain barrels, sump pumps, check valves.

**Emergency**: Sandbags, portable inflatable tubes when defenses fail. "Turn Around, Don't Drown" (US NWS).

**Safety Planning**: Site critical facilities (hospitals, emergency ops) outside hazard zones. Design unavoidable structures (bridges) to withstand flooding. Use land-use planning to reserve high-risk zones for temporary uses. Planning pillars: historical observation, statistical/hydrologic/hydraulic modeling, inundation mapping, long-term regulation, engineering design, forecasting/emergency response, real-time warning.

**Clean-up Safety**: Hazards—electrical, carbon monoxide, musculoskeletal, thermal stress, vehicles, fire, drowning, hazardous materials, biological waste, sharp debris, remains. PPE: hard hats, goggles, heavy gloves, life jackets, watertight steel-toe boots.

### Flood Prediction & Risk Assessment
**Statistical**: Annual maximum flow series estimate recurrence intervals (e.g., 100-year flood). Regional regression transfers estimates to ungauged sites.

**Hydraulic models**: 1D (channel levels), 2D (floodplain depths), or coupled (HEC-RAS, TUFLOW, CREST-iMAP, Inunda). Used for floodplain mapping, insurance, channel design. Can invert observed inundation to estimate flow rate.

**Basin models**: Couple land-surface processes (rainfall→runoff) with reach models. Forecast runoff hydrographs for design storms; assess land-use/climate change impacts. Complexity limits full-process understanding at scale.

**Operational forecasting (US)**: Integrates USGS stream gauges, cooperative networks, automated sensors, NOAA NOHRSC snow data, hydroelectric data, with Quantitative Precipitation Forecasts (QPF) for daily/as-needed forecasts. NWS coordinates with Environment Canada for transboundary basins (e.g., St. Lawrence).

**Global Flood Monitoring System (GFMS)**: NASA/GP satellite precipitation + land-surface model (vegetation, soil, terrain) → soil moisture & streamflow. Outputs: rainfall, streamflow, depth, flooding stats every 3 hrs at 12 km; 5-day forecasts; 1 km inundation maps.

**Forecast accuracy**: Requires long historical flow–rainfall records + real-time catchment storage (reservoir capacity, groundwater, aquifer saturation). Radar rainfall estimates and weather forecasting are critical. Urban rule-of-thumb (NWS): ≥1 in (25 mm) rain in ~1 hr initiates significant ponding on impervious surfaces. Flash Flood Guidance and Headwater Guidance issued routinely.

**Risk definition**: Probability × consequence (hazard × vulnerability). High population density on coasts and floodplains + climate change = rising coastal/fluvial risk.

## Terms
- ****Areal flood**** — Flooding on flat/low land where rainfall/snowmelt exceeds infiltration/runoff capacity, accumulating in depressions.
- ****Flash flood**** — Rapid-onset flood (minutes–hours) with little warning, typically from intense rain on small/steep/impermeable basins or sudden dam/ice release.
- ****Storm surge**** — Abnormal rise of water generated by a storm (cyclone), over and above predicted astronomical tides.
- ****Time of Concentration**** — Time for runoff from the most distant point in a watershed to reach the channel point controlling flooding downstream.
- ****Overland flooding**** — Hybrid river/areal flood in extremely flat terrain (e.g., Red River Valley) where water leaves the channel and travels miles overland unpredictably.
- ****Overlaten**** — Intentionally lowered segments in Dutch levees that divert floodwater to sacrificial areas for river management.
- ****100-year flood**** — Flood with ~1% annual exceedance probability (~63% chance in any 100-year period); standard design benchmark in many developed countries.
- ****Green infrastructure / SUDS**** — Sustainable Urban Drainage Systems: porous paving, wetlands, natural channels that reduce impervious surfaces and mimic natural absorption.
- ****GFMS**** — Global Flood Monitoring System: satellite-driven (NASA/GPM) global flood forecasting and mapping tool at 12 km / 1 km resolution.
- ****Lahar**** — Volcanic mudflow/debris flow that can cause catastrophic riverine flooding.

## Debates and open questions
- **Model adequacy**: Physical process models of full drainage basins remain incomplete; process interactions under extreme climates are poorly understood. Basin models combine well-understood point processes with uncertain large-scale dynamics.
- **Hazard criteria**: Field data (2010–11 Queensland) show velocity/depth/momentum thresholds alone cannot capture risks from large, mobile debris entrained in flow.
- **Coincidence vs. statistics**: Extreme floods often arise from coincident factors (rain-on-snow, ice jams, beaver dams) that simplistic precipitation-runoff models underestimate.
- **Climate attribution**: While climate change intensifies the water cycle and raises seas, quantifying its precise contribution to specific flood frequency/severity changes remains an active research area.
- **Managed retreat vs. structural defense**: Balancing "room for the river" (sacrificial floodplains, overlaten) against hardening defenses in the face of rising risk and entrenched development.
- **Urban pluvial prediction**: High-resolution forecasting of flash flooding in cities requires sub-hourly rainfall data and detailed drainage network models not yet universally available.
