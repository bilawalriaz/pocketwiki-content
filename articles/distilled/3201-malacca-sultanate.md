# Malacca Sultanate

## Overview
The Malacca Sultanate (c. 1400–1511) was a Malay Muslim kingdom in modern Malaysia. It became Southeast Asia's dominant maritime power and entrepôt, controlling the Strait of Malacca trade route. Its 15th-century golden age established Malay culture, language, and Islam as defining regional features, shaping the Malay world for centuries.

## Timeline
- **c. 1400** — Parameswara (Iskandar Shah) founds Malacca after fleeing Singapura's fall.
- **1405–1433** — Ming China establishes friendly relations; Admiral Zheng He visits multiple times.
- **1424–1444** — Sultan Muhammad Shah converts to Islam, Islamising the court and administration.
- **1446 & 1456** — Malacca repels two major invasions from Siam's Ayutthaya kingdom.
- **1459–1477** — Sultan Mansur Shah's reign sees expansion to Pahang, Sumatra, and the Riau Islands.
- **1471** — Conflict with Đại Việt (Vietnam) after its invasion of Champa.
- **1509** — Portuguese explorer Diogo Lopes de Sequeira arrives; a plot against him fails.
- **1511** — Portuguese under Afonso de Albuquerque conquer Malacca.
- **1521** — Exiled Sultan Mahmud Shah dies in Kampar, Sumatra.
- **1520s** — His sons establish the successor states of Perak and Johor.
- **1641** — Dutch and Johor forces capture Malacca from the Portuguese.

## Body

### Founding and Early Growth
Founded c. 1400 by Parameswara, a prince from Singapura, Malacca grew at the Bertam River's mouth, protected by the Orang Laut (sea people). A tributary relationship with Ming China provided security from Siamese threats. The port attracted Arab, Indian, and Chinese traders. The third ruler, Muhammad Shah, converted to Islam in the 1430s, Islamising state customs and bureaucracy. Key institutions were created, including the *Undang-Undang Laut Melaka* (Maritime Laws) and four *Shahbandars* (harbour masters) for foreign trading communities.

### Golden Age and Expansion
Malacca peaked in the mid-to-late 15th century under sultans Muzaffar Shah and Mansur Shah. It repelled Siamese invasions in 1446 and 1456, securing independence. Mansur Shah expanded territory to include Pahang, parts of Sumatra (Jambi, Indragiri, Siak), and the Riau-Lingga islands. The sultanate became the region's primary Islamic learning and trade centre, handling Maluku spices and Indian textiles. The court developed a sophisticated administration: the Sultan, advised by a *Bendahara* (vizier), supported by the *Temenggung* (security chief) and *Laksamana* (naval chief).

### Portuguese Conquest and Aftermath
European spice demand led Portugal to target Malacca. After a failed 1509 attempt, a Portuguese armada under Afonso de Albuquerque conquered the city in August 1511. The last sultan, Mahmud Shah, was exiled. The Portuguese built *A Famosa* fortress and disrupted trade, causing Malacca's decline. The conquest enraged Ming China, leading to decades of conflict. The exiled sultan's sons later established Perak and Johor, resisting the Portuguese until the Dutch captured Malacca in 1641.

### Administration and Society
The centralized monarchy vested absolute power in the Sultan, whose right to rule (*daulat*) blended pre-Islamic and Islamic sovereignty concepts. Key offices included the *Bendahara* (chief minister), *Penghulu bendahari* (treasurer), *Temenggung* (security chief), and *Laksamana* (naval commander). The legal system combined Hindu-Buddhist traditions, Islamic law, and indigenous *adat* (custom). Society was cosmopolitan, with foreign traders in enclaves under their *Shahbandars*.

### Islam and Cultural Legacy
Islam, firmly established by the mid-15th century, transformed society, redefining kingship and fostering ties with Muslim polities. Malacca became a major Islamic scholarship centre, spreading the faith across the archipelago. It standardised Classical Malay as a lingua franca and Jawi script for administration and literature. Its court traditions, architecture, and customs set a cultural template for subsequent Malay-Muslim sultanates, contributing to regional "Malayisation."

### Trade and Economy
Prosperity stemmed from strategic location and secure trade. As an entrepôt, it collected eastern spices (cloves, nutmeg) and redistributed western goods like Indian textiles and Chinese porcelain. The port provided warehouses, security, and efficient administration. Unique tin ingots served as local currency. The economy relied heavily on imported rice due to limited domestic production.

## Terms
- **Daulat**: Divine sovereignty or the sacred right of a ruler, blending pre-Islamic and Islamic ideas.
- **Bendahara**: Highest-ranking minister and chief advisor to the Sultan, similar to a vizier.
- **Laksamana**: Head of the navy and chief emissary, responsible for maritime security and law.
- **Shahbandar**: Harbour master administering affairs for a specific group of foreign traders.
- **Undang-Undang Laut Melaka**: Maritime Laws of Malacca, a code governing trade and naval conduct.
- **Orang Laut**: "Sea people" who served as naval patrol and provided trade route security.
- **Entrepôt**: A port for importing, storing, and re-exporting goods as a central trade hub.
- **Malayisation**: The spread of Malay culture, language, and Islam to become dominant across Maritime Southeast Asia.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Founding Date and Founder**: The conventional date is c. 1400, but earlier dates are proposed. Sources debate the first ruler's identity: Portuguese and Chinese sources name Parameswara, while the Malay Annals call him Iskandar Shah. Some accept Parameswara's son was Megat Iskandar Shah.
- **Islamisation Timeline**: Sources disagree on conversion timing. Tomé Pires claimed Parameswara's son converted at age 72, while the Malay Annals state Islam was adopted under Muhammad Shah (r. 1424–1444). It was firmly established by Muzaffar Shah's reign (r. 1445–1459).
- **Succession and Early Rulers**: Discrepancies in Malay, Chinese, and Portuguese sources regarding early sultans' names, number, and reign details create chronological uncertainty.
