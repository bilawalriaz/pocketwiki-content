# Teotihuacan

## Overview
Teotihuacan was a major ancient Mesoamerican city in the Valley of Mexico, reaching its zenith in the first half of the first millennium CE. It was the largest city in the pre-Columbian Americas and a powerful cultural and economic center whose influence extended across the region. The city is renowned for its monumental pyramids, complex urban planning, and mysterious collapse, making it a key subject for understanding pre-Aztec Mesoamerican civilization.

## Timeline
- **c. 600 BCE** — First human settlements in the Teotihuacan area.
- **c. 100 BCE** — Teotihuacan begins to develop into a major urban center.
- **1–150 CE** — Explosive population growth; Pyramid of the Sun completed by 100 CE.
- **250–350 CE** — Shift in political power from the Temple of the Feathered Serpent to the Avenue of the Dead complex.
- **378 CE** — Teotihuacano warlord Sihyaj K'ahk' conquers Tikal in the Maya region.
- **450 CE** — City reaches its peak population and influence.
- **535–536 CE** — Extreme weather events may have contributed to later decline.
- **c. 550 CE** — Major monuments are sacked and systematically burned.
- **650–750 CE** — Teotihuacan's decline as a major power; population decreases.
- **1987 CE** — Site designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Origins and Growth
The city's early history is mysterious. It began as a religious center around the first century CE, possibly growing after the eruption of the Xitle volcano displaced populations from the rival center of Cuicuilco. By 200 CE, it had become the largest metropolis in Mesoamerica, with a population that stabilized around 100,000 by 300 CE. Its growth was fueled by agricultural productivity from *chinampas* (raised-bed fields) and its role as a hub for trade, especially in obsidian.

### Society and Culture
Teotihuacan was a multi-ethnic city, with neighborhoods like Tlailotlacan (Oaxacan migrants) and Barrio de Los Comerciantes (Gulf Coast merchants). Society was stratified into elites, intermediates, and laborers, all living in distinct multi-family apartment compounds. The city's art, especially its vibrant murals, glorified deities and nature rather than individual rulers, suggesting a possible collective governance. Religion centered on deities like the Great Goddess and the Feathered Serpent, with human sacrifice practiced during building dedications.

### Influence and Collapse
At its height, Teotihuacan's influence was vast, evidenced by its architectural style (*talud-tablero*) and iconography found at distant Maya sites like Tikal and Copán. The nature of this influence—whether through direct conquest or cultural diffusion—is debated. The city's collapse in the 6th-7th centuries CE is attributed to a combination of factors: internal social strife, possible drought linked to climate events of 535–536, and disruptions in trade. Its fall led to a power vacuum filled by regional centers like Xochicalco.

### Archaeology and Modern Site
The site was never fully lost and was a pilgrimage destination for the Aztecs, who named it. Major excavations began in the early 20th century. Key discoveries include a man-made tunnel beneath the Temple of the Feathered Serpent, sealed around 200 CE, containing offerings like greenstone statues and liquid mercury. The city's layout, centered on the Avenue of the Dead, was astronomically aligned. Today, it is a major tourist attraction but faces threats from urban development and unauthorized construction.

## Terms
- **Talud-tablero**: An architectural style featuring a sloping panel (*talud*) surmounted by a rectangular frame (*tablero*), characteristic of Teotihuacan and found across Mesoamerica.
- **Chinampas**: "Floating gardens," a system of raised-bed agriculture used in the swampy areas of the Valley of Mexico to support large populations.
- **Obsidian**: A volcanic glass used to create sharp tools and luxury items; Teotihuacan controlled its trade and production.
- **Avenue of the Dead**: The central thoroughfare of Teotihuacan, flanked by ceremonial platforms and leading to the major pyramids.
- **Multi-family residential compounds**: The primary housing units in Teotihuacan, organized by social status and often containing workshops.
- **Great Goddess of Teotihuacan**: A primary deity associated with water, fertility, and the earth, frequently depicted in murals.
- **Feathered Serpent (Quetzalcoatl)**: A major deity linked to wind, air, and learning, whose temple is a central monument.
- **UNESCO World Heritage Site**: A designation for cultural or natural sites of outstanding universal value, granted to Teotihuacan in 1987.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ethnicity of Inhabitants**: Scholars debate whether the founders were Nahuas, Otomi, Totonac, or a multi-ethnic coalition.
- **Nature of Governance**: The absence of royal palaces or depictions of individual rulers suggests a possible collective or bureaucratic political system, contrasting with other Mesoamerican states.
- **Cause of Collapse**: Theories include internal uprising, invasion, ecological disaster from drought, or a combination of these factors.
- **Extent of Influence**: Whether Teotihuacan was a direct imperial power controlling distant regions like the Maya area, or its influence was primarily through prestige and cultural diffusion, remains debated.
