# Idi Amin

## Overview
Idi Amin was a Ugandan military officer and dictator who ruled as the third president of Uganda from 1971 to 1979. His brutal regime, marked by ethnic persecution, political repression, and economic mismanagement, is estimated to have caused the deaths of between 100,000 and 500,000 people. His overthrow in 1979 followed a disastrous invasion of Tanzania, and his rule remains a defining period of turmoil in modern Ugandan history.

## Timeline
- **30 May 1928** — Idi Amin is born in Kampala, Uganda.
- **1946** — Joins the British colonial King's African Rifles as a cook.
- **25 January 1971** — Seizes power in a military coup, overthrowing President Milton Obote.
- **August 1972** — Orders the expulsion of Uganda's Asian population, crippling the economy.
- **June 1976** — Allows hijackers to land at Entebbe Airport, leading to a major international crisis.
- **25 June 1976** — Declared President for Life.
- **November 1978** — Ugandan troops invade Tanzania's Kagera Region.
- **April 1979** — Tanzanian and rebel forces capture Kampala; Amin flees into exile.
- **16 August 2003** — Dies in exile in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Career
Amin's early life is poorly documented, with conflicting accounts of his birth year and parentage. He joined the British colonial army, the King's African Rifles, in 1946, serving in Kenya and rising to the rank of Warrant Officer. After Uganda's independence in 1962, he remained in the army, becoming its commander in 1966. His loyalty to President Milton Obote was demonstrated in 1966 when he led a violent attack on the Kabaka's palace, killing over 1,000 people.

### Rise to Power and Rule
A rift developed between Amin and Obote, leading Amin to stage a successful coup on 25 January 1971 while Obote was abroad. He initially promised a caretaker government but soon declared himself President for Life. His rule was characterized by extreme brutality, establishing military tribunals and secret police agencies like the State Research Bureau to torture and kill perceived enemies. He purged the army and targeted ethnic groups like the Acholi and Lango, as well as intellectuals, politicians, and religious leaders. In 1972, he expelled Uganda's Asian population, which controlled much of the economy, leading to economic collapse.

### International Relations and Downfall
Amin's foreign policy shifted from pro-Western to being backed by Libya, the Soviet Union, and East Germany. His support for the 1976 Entebbe hijacking and his expulsion of Asians severely damaged Uganda's international standing. In 1978, facing internal army mutinies, he ordered an invasion of Tanzania's Kagera Region. Tanzania counter-attacked, and with Ugandan exile forces, captured Kampala in April 1979, forcing Amin into exile. He lived in Libya, Iraq, and finally Saudi Arabia until his death.

## Terms
- **King's African Rifles:** The British colonial army regiment in which Amin served before Uganda's independence.
- **Coup d'état:** A sudden, violent, and illegal seizure of power from a government.
- **State Research Bureau:** The notorious secret police agency established by Amin to enforce his rule through torture and assassination.
- **Operation Entebbe:** The 1976 Israeli commando raid to rescue hostages from a hijacked plane at Uganda's Entebbe Airport.
- **Organisation of African Unity (OAU):** An intergovernmental organization designed to promote solidarity among African states, which Amin chaired in 1975.
- **Economic War:** Amin's 1972 policy of expropriating properties owned by Asians and Europeans, leading to economic collapse.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact death toll of Amin's regime is unknown, with estimates ranging from 80,000 to 500,000.
- The circumstances of the 1978 invasion of Tanzania are debated: some sources state Amin ordered it to distract from domestic problems, while others suggest he lost control of his army and sanctioned it retroactively.
- Amin's legacy in Uganda is complex; while internationally condemned as a brutal dictator, some Ugandans, particularly in his home region, have viewed him as a patriot who challenged colonial and foreign influence.
