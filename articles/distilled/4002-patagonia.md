# Patagonia

## Overview
Patagonia is a vast geographical region in southern South America, shared by Argentina and Chile, known for its dramatic landscapes from the Andes to the Atlantic. It matters as a unique ecological zone, a historical frontier of colonization, and a modern economic area defined by resources like oil, sheep, and tourism. Its history involves indigenous cultures, European exploration, and nation-building conflicts that shaped its current political and cultural identity.

## Timeline
- **c. 12,500 BC** — Earliest confirmed human presence at Monte Verde, Chile.
- **1520** — Ferdinand Magellan's expedition explores the coast and names the region "Patagonia."
- **1534** — Spanish Crown claims the territory as part of the Governorate of New Léon.
- **1843** — Chile founds Fuerte Bulnes at the Strait of Magellan.
- **1860** — French adventurer Orelie-Antoine de Tounens proclaims the Kingdom of Araucanía and Patagonia.
- **1870s** — Argentina executes the "Conquest of the Desert" military campaigns against indigenous peoples.
- **1881** — Argentina and Chile sign the Boundary Treaty, dividing Patagonia.
- **1902** — British mediation finalizes the border between the two nations.
- **1907** — Oil is discovered near Comodoro Rivadavia, Argentina.
- **1994** — An arbitration tribunal resolves the last border dispute, granting Argentina sovereignty over the Southern Patagonia Icefield.

## Body

### Etymology and Identity
The name "Patagonia" derives from "patagón," a term used by Magellan in 1520 to describe the native Tehuelche people, whom his expedition believed to be giants. An alternative theory suggests a link to the Greek region Paphlagonia via a character in a 1512 chivalric romance. The region's toponymy reflects its mixed heritage, with indigenous, Spanish, and Welsh influences. Many inhabitants identify as "Patagonian" rather than Latino, emphasizing a distinct regional culture.

### Indigenous Peoples and Early Exploration
Before European contact, Patagonia was inhabited by various groups, including hunter-gatherers like the Tehuelche on the plains and maritime peoples like the Yámana in the south. Agriculture was limited. Spanish explorers, beginning with Magellan in 1520, claimed the territory but established few lasting settlements, focusing on strategic control. Early European accounts fueled myths of "Patagonian giants," a belief that persisted for centuries.

### Colonization and Conflict
In the 19th century, newly independent Argentina and Chile aggressively expanded southward. This led to violent conflicts with indigenous populations, culminating in Argentina's "Conquest of the Desert" (1870s), which subjugated native peoples. The 1881 Boundary Treaty formally divided Patagonia between the two nations. Colonization by settlers from Chile, Europe (notably Welsh communities), and Argentina disrupted indigenous societies and economies.

### Physical Geography and Climate
Patagonia's landscape is divided by the Andes. The west features fjords, glaciers, and temperate rainforests, while the east consists of arid steppes and plateaus. The climate is predominantly cool and dry, with extreme variations: the western coast receives heavy precipitation (up to 7,000 mm annually), while the eastern plains are semi-arid (as low as 200 mm). Strong westerly winds are a defining feature.

### Fauna and Geology
The region's fauna includes the guanaco, Darwin's rhea, and the Patagonian mara. Marine life is significant, with the Valdés Peninsula being a UNESCO site for whales and seals. Geologically, Patagonia's origin is debated; it may be an allochthonous terrane that separated from Antarctica. The area is rich in fossils, including giant dinosaurs like *Argentinosaurus*, and contains major hydrocarbon reserves in formations like Vaca Muerta.

### Modern Economy
The economy is based on natural resources. Argentine Patagonia relies on sheep farming, oil, and gas extraction. Chilean Patagonia's economy is dominated by fishing, salmon aquaculture, and tourism. Tourism has grown significantly, attracting visitors to glaciers and national parks. Foreign investment in land for conservation or agriculture has sparked social and political controversies.

## Terms
- **Allochthonous terrane**: A fragment of crustal material formed elsewhere and accreted onto a continent.
- **Conquest of the Desert**: A controversial late-19th century Argentine military campaign to subdue indigenous peoples in Patagonia and the Pampas.
- **Huincul Fault**: A major geological discontinuity often proposed as the northern limit of Patagonia.
- **Malones**: Coordinated raids by indigenous groups, particularly Mapuche, to steal cattle from Argentine settlements.
- **Steppe**: A vast, flat, arid grassland with little tree cover, characteristic of eastern Patagonia.
- **Vaca Muerta**: A major geological formation in the Neuquén Basin containing vast unconventional oil and gas reserves.
- **Yámana (Yaghan)**: An indigenous maritime people of the southernmost islands of Tierra del Fuego.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origin of the Patagonian Landmass**: Geologists debate whether it is an allochthonous terrane that drifted from Antarctica (proposed by Víctor Ramos) or of parautochthonous (nearby) origin (argued by R.J. Pankhurst).
- **Northern Boundary**: The precise northern limit of Patagonia is not universally defined, with common definitions using the Colorado and Barrancas rivers or the Huincul Fault.
- **Impact of Colonization**: The extent and intentionality of population decline among indigenous groups following European colonization and the "Conquest of the Desert" remain subjects of historical analysis.
