# Corporation

## Overview
A corporation is a legal entity authorized by the state to act as a single person—distinct from its human members—with the capacity to own property, contract, sue, and be sued. Its defining modern features are **limited liability** (shareholders risk only their investment) and **legal personality** (the entity survives its members in perpetuity). Originating in Roman *collegia* and medieval chartered bodies, the corporation evolved from a privileged, state-granted monopoly instrument of mercantilism into a freely registrable vehicle for private capital accumulation. This shift, codified in British legislation between 1844 and 1862 and later exported globally, enabled the mobilization of dispersed savings for industrial-scale enterprise, fundamentally reshaping economic organization and the relationship between capital, labor, and the state.

## Timeline
- **49–44 BC** — *Lex Julia* requires Roman Senate/Emperor approval for *collegia* to act as legal bodies.
- **527–565 AD** — Justinian’s *Corpus Juris Civilis* codifies Roman corporate law (*Universitas*, *corpus*, *collegium*).
- **11th–14th c.** — Glossators and commentators revive corporate concept in Europe via Justinian’s texts.
- **1347** — Stora Kopparberg (Sweden) receives charter from King Magnus Eriksson; often cited as oldest commercial corporation.
- **1600** — English East India Company chartered by Queen Elizabeth I; granted trade monopoly east of Cape of Good Hope.
- **1602** — Dutch East India Company (VOC) chartered; issues tradable share certificates on Amsterdam Stock Exchange with limited liability.
- **1720** — British Bubble Act prohibits unchartered companies; South Sea Bubble bursts, damaging corporate reputation.
- **1825** — Bubble Act repealed amid Industrial Revolution pressure for legal reform.
- **1844** — Joint Stock Companies Act creates Registrar; enables incorporation by registration (two-stage, £5 each) without charter.
- **1855** — Limited Liability Act allows investors to cap liability at unpaid share amount (for companies >25 members).
- **1856** — Joint Stock Companies Act codifies registration and limited liability into single statute.
- **1862** — Companies Act consolidates prior statutes; remains in force through *Salomon* decision.
- **1892** — Germany introduces *Gesellschaft mit beschränkter Haftung* (GmbH): separate legal personality with limited liability even for single-member firms.
- **1897** — *Salomon v. Salomon & Co.* (House of Lords) confirms separate legal personality and distinct corporate liability.
- **1896/1899** — New Jersey (1896) then Delaware (1899) enact "enabling" general incorporation laws in US; Delaware dominates after NJ repeal (1913).
- **1980s onward** — Wave of privatization and deregulation transfers state-owned enterprises to corporate form globally.

## Body

### Origins and Early Forms
Roman law recognized corporate entities (*Universitas*, *corpus*, *collegium*) including municipalities, religious cults, burial clubs, and trade guilds. Following the *Lex Julia* (49–44 BC), such bodies required Senate or Imperial authorization. They could hold property, contract, sue, and act through representatives. The concept was preserved in Justinian’s *Corpus Juris Civilis* (527–565) and revived in 11th–14th century Europe by glossators (notably Bartolus de Saxoferrato and Baldus de Ubaldis), who linked the corporation to the "body politic" metaphor. Medieval Europe saw incorporation of churches, municipalities (e.g., City of London Corporation), and the Stora Kopparberg mining community (chartered 1347). Early business collaboration otherwise relied on common-law partnerships, which arose automatically when persons acted together for profit, and on regulated guilds.

### Mercantilism and the Chartered Monopoly
In the 17th century, Dutch and English states used chartered companies as instruments of colonial expansion. The Dutch East India Company (VOC, 1602) defeated Portuguese forces in the Moluccas, issued paper share certificates tradable on the Amsterdam Stock Exchange, and granted limited liability in its charter. The English East India Company (1600) received a monopoly east of the Cape of Good Hope, became deeply integrated with British military and naval policy, and delivered extraordinary returns (near 150% by 1711). The South Sea Company (1711), backed by the Treaty of Utrecht asiento, triggered Britain’s first speculative bubble; its collapse in 1720 (shares falling from £1,000 to under £100) prompted the Bubble Act 1720, which banned unchartered companies and froze incorporation policy for over a century. Stewart Kyd’s 1794 treatise defined the corporation as an artificial body with perpetual succession, capacity to hold property, contract, sue, and exercise political rights per its design.

### Development of Modern Company Law
**Deregulation.** Adam Smith’s *Wealth of Nations* (1776) criticized corporate management of others’ money, favoring private entrepreneurship. The 1825 repeal of the Bubble Act began gradual liberalization, but incorporation remained cumbersome (royal charter or private Act of Parliament), forcing many large ventures into unincorporated associations with impractical litigation requirements. William Gladstone’s 1843 committee produced the **Joint Stock Companies Act 1844**, creating the Registrar of Joint Stock Companies and enabling incorporation by a simple two-stage registration (£5 + £5) for the first time.

**Limited Liability.** The 1844 Act conferred legal personality but not limited liability; members faced unlimited losses. The **Limited Liability Act 1855** (championed by Robert Lowe) capped shareholder liability at the unpaid portion of their shares (for firms >25 members; insurance excluded until 1862). *The Economist* initially dismissed its importance but later ranked the invention of limited liability alongside Watt and Stephenson as a pillar of the Industrial Revolution. The **Joint Stock Companies Act 1856** and **Companies Act 1862** consolidated registration and limited liability, triggering a railway boom and subsequent wave of insolvencies that reignited debate over accountability.

**Further Developments.** Germany’s 1892 *GmbH* law extended separate personality and limited liability to single-owner firms, influencing global legislation. The 1897 **Salomon v. Salomon & Co.** decision definitively confirmed the corporation’s separate legal personality, insulating owners from corporate debts. In the US, incorporation required legislative acts until New Jersey (1896) and Delaware (1899) passed enabling statutes; Delaware prevailed after New Jersey’s 1913 repeal. Late 19th century saw holding companies, mergers, dispersed shareholders, and antitrust responses. The 20th century brought worldwide registration-based incorporation laws, fueling pre- and post-WWI booms, followed by conglomerate formation and, from the 1980s, mass privatization and deregulation of state-owned enterprises.

### Ownership and Control
A corporation is theoretically owned and controlled by its members. In a **joint-stock company**, members are **shareholders**; ownership, profit (dividends), and voting rights are proportional to shares held. In **non-stock corporations** (e.g., worker cooperatives, credit unions), membership criteria are set by the constitutive document. Day-to-day control is delegated: common-law countries favor a **single board of directors** (mixing executive and non-executive directors for supervision); civil-law countries often use a **two-tier structure** (supervisory board and managing board). **Co-determination** (e.g., Germany) mandates worker-elected board representation.

### Formation
Historically by royal charter or private Act; today by **registration** with the relevant government. Registration is the prerequisite for limited liability. The process typically requires filing **articles of incorporation** (nature of business, authorized stock, director details), after which directors adopt **bylaws** for internal governance. A corporation must designate a principal address and a **registered agent** to accept legal service of process. Under the **internal affairs doctrine**, the law of the incorporation jurisdiction governs internal disputes (shareholders vs. directors/officers). Operating in other jurisdictions requires registration as a **foreign corporation** and appointment of a local registered agent; external affairs (employment, contracts, torts) are subject to host-state law. Corporations generally cannot own their own stock except as **treasury stock** (repurchased shares, treated as unissued capital, not an asset).

### Naming
Corporations have distinct names. Historically names referenced board members (e.g., "President and Fellows of Harvard College"); modern names need not. Canada permits numbered names (e.g., "12345678 Ontario Limited"). Most jurisdictions require a suffix denoting corporate status or limited liability (e.g., "Inc.", "Ltd.", "LLC")—mandatory in some, optional in others (e.g., California)—to put third parties on constructive notice of limited liability. Names must be unique within the registering jurisdiction; a name is a unique identifier only when combined with the state of incorporation, hence legal citations include the state. Some jurisdictions bar "company" alone as a corporate designator because it may denote a partnership or sole proprietorship.

### Personhood
Though not human, corporations are recognized as **legal persons** in several countries: they can own property, sue/be sued, exercise human rights against individuals and the state, and be liable for human rights violations. They can be dissolved by statute, court order, or shareholder action; insolvency typically leads to restructuring rather than liquidation. In the UK, corporations can be convicted of criminal offenses (fraud, corporate manslaughter). Critics (e.g., Joel Bakan) argue the legal mandate to prioritize corporate profit creates a "psychopathic" personality that externalizes harms to employees, the public, and nature. David Runciman contends corporate personhood clarifies the modern state’s structure, blurring the state/individual dichotomy and reframing citizens as political stakeholders.

## Terms
- ****Legal personality / Legal person**** — Status allowing an entity to hold rights and duties (own property, contract, sue, be sued) distinct from its human members.
- ****Limited liability**** — Rule capping a shareholder’s financial loss at the amount invested (or unpaid on shares); personal assets are shielded from corporate debts and torts.
- ****Joint-stock company**** — Corporation whose capital is divided into transferable shares; members are shareholders with rights proportional to holdings.
- ****Non-stock corporation**** — Corporation that cannot issue shares; owners are members (e.g., cooperatives, credit unions) per its constitutive rules.
- ****Charter**** — Historical instrument (royal grant or legislative act) creating a corporation; replaced by registration in modern law.
- ****Board of directors**** — Elected/appointed body that oversees management; in common-law countries a single board mixes executive and non-executive directors.
- ****Two-tier board**** — Civil-law structure separating a supervisory board (oversight) from a managing board (operations).
- ****Co-determination**** — Statutory right of workers to elect a fraction of the board of directors (e.g., Germany).
- ****Articles of incorporation**** — Founding document filed on registration stating the corporation’s purpose, authorized stock, and initial directors.
- ****Internal affairs doctrine**** — Principle that the law of the state of incorporation governs internal corporate disputes (shareholders vs. managers).
- ****Foreign corporation**** — A corporation operating outside its state of incorporation; must register locally and appoint a registered agent.
- ****Treasury stock**** — Shares repurchased by the corporation; treated as unissued capital, not an asset on the balance sheet.
- ****GmbH**** — *Gesellschaft mit beschränkter Haftung*: German limited-liability company form (1892) allowing separate personality with a single owner.
- ****Salomon principle**** — Rule from *Salomon v. Salomon & Co.* (1897) confirming the corporation’s separate legal personality and distinct liability from its owners.
- ****Enabling statute**** — General incorporation law (e.g., New Jersey 1896, Delaware 1899) allowing formation by registration without legislative act.
- ****Privatization**** — Sale of state-owned enterprises to private corporations, often accompanied by deregulation (1980s onward).

## Debates and open questions
- **Accountability vs. limited liability:** Since the 1855 Act, critics argue limited liability lets businessmen escape responsibility for failing or harmful enterprises; proponents credit it with mobilizing capital for industrialization.
- **Corporate purpose:** Whether the legal mandate to maximize shareholder profit (per Bakan) inherently produces psychopathic externalization of social/environmental costs, or whether stakeholder governance models can align corporate and public interests.
- **Personhood scope:** The extent to which corporations should enjoy constitutional/human rights (e.g., political speech, religious liberty) versus bearing corresponding duties and criminal liability.
- **Regulatory competition:** Whether the "race to the bottom" (or "race to the top") among jurisdictions (e.g., Delaware dominance) optimizes corporate law or undermines worker/creditor protections.
- **State–corporation boundary:** Runciman’s view that corporate personhood blurs the state/individual dichotomy raises unresolved questions about democratic accountability when private entities wield quasi-public power.
