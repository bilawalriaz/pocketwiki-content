# Sandro Botticelli

## Overview

Sandro Botticelli (c. 1444–1446 – May 17, 1510) was an Italian painter of the Early Renaissance, active in Florence. Though best known today for mythological subjects like *The Birth of Venus* and *Primavera*, he also painted many religious works, including numerous Madonnas, and portraits. His posthumous reputation suffered until the late 19th century, when the Pre-Raphaelites rediscovered him; since then his linear grace and idealized figures have made him an icon of Renaissance art. His career spans the 1470s–1490s, moving from early success to a late, more personal and "archaic" style that turned against the emerging High Renaissance manner of Leonardo da Vinci.

## Timeline

- **c. 1444–1446** — Born in Florence, in the Borgo Ognissanti neighbourhood
- **c. 1461–1462** — Apprenticed to Fra Filippo Lippi, leading Florentine painter
- **1469** — Lippi dies; Botticelli has his own workshop; commissioned *Fortitude* (1470)
- **1474** — Paints *Saint Sebastian*; invited to Pisa to fresco the Camposanto (no work survives)
- **c. 1475–76** — *Adoration of the Magi* for Santa Maria Novella, marking climax of early style
- **1478** — Fresco of Pazzi conspirators' execution (lost); first major fresco commission
- **1481–82** — Summoned to Rome by Pope Sixtus IV; paints three scenes in the Sistine Chapel
- **c. 1482–85** — *Primavera* and *The Birth of Venus* painted; his most successful decade
- **1485** — Bardi Altarpiece finished; framed by Giuliano da Sangallo
- **c. 1487** — San Barnaba Altarpiece, showing elements of emotional late style
- **1490s** — Style becomes more personal and mannered; follows Savonarola's influence
- **1500–1501** — *Mystical Nativity*, his only dated painting (1501 inscription)
- **1510** — Dies in Florence, buried in Ognissanti Church

## Body

### Early life and training

Botticelli was born in Florence's Borgo Ognissanti, the youngest of four surviving children of a tanner, Mariano di Vanni d'Amedeo Filipepi. His father's tax returns place his birth between 1444 and 1446. In 1460 his father became a gold-beater, a trade that brought contact with artists; Vasari reports Botticelli was initially trained as a goldsmith. The nickname "Botticelli" ("little barrel") came from his brother Giovanni's round stature. From c. 1461–62 he apprenticed to Fra Filippo Lippi, learning intimate compositions with melancholic figures, clear contours, and slight light contrasts. He probably left Lippi's workshop by April 1467; his presence in the workshops of the Pollaiuolo brothers or Verrocchio is speculated but unproven.

### Career before Rome

By 1469 Botticelli had his own workshop. His earliest securely dated work is *Fortitude* (1470), completing a series of the Seven Virtues begun by Piero del Pollaiuolo. In 1472 he took on Filippino Lippi (his master's son) as apprentice; their works from these years are often hard to distinguish. His earliest surviving altarpiece (c. 1470–72) shows mastery of composition. The *Saint Sebastian* (1474) reflects close study of the human body and Early Netherlandish landscape influence. The *Adoration of the Magi* (c. 1475–76) contains portraits of the Medici family and was praised by Vasari, spreading his reputation. In 1478 he frescoed the executed Pazzi conspirators (lost), a "pittura infamante" (defaming painting) that may have led to his Roman summons. His surviving *Saint Augustine* fresco (1480) for Ognissanti is regarded as his finest by scholar Ronald Lightbown.

### Sistine Chapel

In 1481 Pope Sixtus IV summoned Botticelli and other Florentine and Umbrian artists to fresco the Sistine Chapel, likely part of a peace deal after the Pazzi War. Botticelli contributed three of fourteen large scenes (*Temptations of Christ*, *Youth of Moses*, *Punishment of the Sons of Corah*), plus papal portraits and lunette paintings. Vasari implies Botticelli had overall charge, but modern scholars think Pietro Perugino more likely. Botticelli imposed a triptych-like composition, dividing scenes into central and flanking groups; Christ or Moses appears multiple times in each. He returned to Florence in 1482.

### Mythological subjects of the 1480s

*Primavera* (c. 1482) and *The Birth of Venus* (c. 1485) are not a pair but are discussed together; both are in the Uffizi. They were virtually unprecedented in Western art since antiquity as large-scale classical mythology subjects. Art historians debate their meanings: emulation of ancient painters, wedding contexts, Renaissance Neoplatonism (which gave divine love a central philosophical place), and the identity of commissioners. Both were seen by Vasari at the Villa di Castello, owned by Lorenzo di Pierfrancesco de' Medici; a 1975-discovered 1499 inventory suggests *Primavera* was for his townhouse and *Birth of Venus* for another site. The smaller *Venus and Mars* (National Gallery, London) was likely a spalliera (wall panel) for the Vespucci family, whose name means "little wasps" (wasps buzz around Mars' head). A four-panel *Nastagio degli Onesti* series was commissioned by Antonio Pucci in 1483 for a marriage.

### Religious paintings after Rome

Botticelli returned from Rome with an enhanced reputation. The Bardi Altarpiece (1485, Berlin) shows a Madonna in a hortus conclusus (closed garden), with banderoles (ribbons) carrying biblical verses. The San Barnaba Altarpiece (c. 1487) begins his emotional late style. His largest altarpiece, the San Marco Altarpiece, retains its full predella (five panels). The Cestello Annunciation (1489–90) shares sombre colouring with two Lamentations (c. 1490–95). The Pala delle Convertite (c. 1491–93, Courtauld Gallery) depicts the Holy Trinity for an institution for ex-prostitutes. After c. 1493–95 he painted no more large religious works.

### Madonnas and tondos

Botticelli painted Madonnas throughout his career; these were a steady income source. He was one of the first to use the round tondo format (typically 115–145 cm across), associated with palaces. His Virgins are idealized and richly dressed. Savonarola's complaint that painters "made the Virgin appear dressed as a whore" may have affected his style. The Magnificat Madonna (c. 1483, Uffizi) shows Mary writing the Magnificat. One Madonna and Child with Angels Carrying Candlesticks (1485/1490) was destroyed in 1945 when Berlin's Friedrichshain flak tower burned.

### Portraits

Botticelli painted relatively few portraits; many attributed to him are workshop productions. Idealized female portraits probably do not represent specific people, despite traditional links to Simonetta Vespucci (d. 1476). Women appear in profile, men in three-quarter pose. Lightbown attributes only about eight individual portraits, most before c. 1475. He inserted self-portraits and Medici portraits into larger works.

### Dante, printing and manuscripts

Botticelli had a lifelong interest in Dante. He designed illustrations for the 1481 printed *Divine Comedy*, engraved by Baccio Baldini—unprecedented for a leading painter—but only 19 of 100 engravings were made, and Baldini's skill was inadequate. He later began a luxury parchment manuscript with 93 surviving pages (now divided between the Vatican and Berlin), mostly underdrawings, never completed. He may also have illustrated Savonarola's pamphlets, almost all destroyed.

### Medici patronage

The Medici ruled Florence throughout Botticelli's life until 1494. Lorenzo il Magnifico (head from 1469) patronized the humanist and Neoplatonist circle influencing Botticelli's mythologies, though Lorenzo himself seems to have commissioned little from him. Botticelli worked more for Lorenzo's cousins and Medici allies like Tommaso Soderini and Antonio Pucci. Giuliano de' Medici, assassinated in the 1478 Pazzi conspiracy, appears in portraits and is linked to theories about figures in the mythologies.

### Last years

Vasari claims Botticelli became a follower of the moralistic friar Girolamo Savonarola (preaching 1490–1498), gave up painting, and fell into poverty. Modern scholars reject this: the *Mystical Nativity* (dated 1501) came after Savonarola's death, and in 1502 Isabella d'Este's agent found Botticelli available but she preferred Perugino. He produced little after 1501. Late works include the *Calumny of Apelles* (c. 1494–95), *Story of Virginia* and *Story of Lucretia* (c. 1500), and the *Mystic Crucifixion* (damaged, attribution uncertain). His late style shows expressive distortion, non-naturalistic colour, and a reversion to Gothic conventions—opposite to Leonardo's High Renaissance direction. He died in May 1510, buried in Ognissanti.

### Later reputation

Botticelli's reputation was eclipsed longer than any other major European artist. Vasari's Life (1550) was disapproving and confused him with Francesco Botticini. The *Birth of Venus* was displayed in the Uffizi from 1815 but little mentioned. The *Mystical Nativity* was exhibited in Manchester in 1857, viewed by over a million people. The Pre-Raphaelites and Walter Pater revived interest; the first monograph appeared in 1893. Herbert Horne's 1908 monograph remains a landmark. The asteroid 29361 Botticelli (discovered 1996) is named after him.

## Terms

- **Tondo**: A round painting format, especially associated with Botticelli's Madonnas.
- **Sacra conversazione**: An altarpiece showing the Madonna and Child with saints in a unified space.
- **Pittura infamante**: "Defaming painting," a Florentine custom of depicting traitors' executions.
- **Spalliera**: A painting made to fit into furniture or wall panelling.
- **Predella**: The lower panels of an altarpiece, often showing narrative scenes.
- **Hortus conclusus**: "Closed garden," a traditional symbolic setting for the Virgin Mary.
- **Banderole**: A ribbon carrying text, used in religious paintings.
- **Renaissance Neoplatonism**: A philosophical movement giving divine love a central place, influencing Botticelli's mythologies.
- **Piagnoni**: "Snivellers," followers of Savonarola.
- **Marzocco**: The heraldic lion symbol of Florence.

## Debates and open questions

- **Sistine Chapel leadership**: Whether Botticelli or Perugino directed the project; Vasari implies Botticelli, modern scholars favour Perugino.
- **Commissioners of the mythologies**: Whether *Primavera* and *Birth of Venus* were painted for the Villa di Castello or other sites; a 1499 inventory suggests different locations.
- **Savonarola's influence**: Vasari claims Botticelli gave up painting and destroyed secular works; modern scholars doubt this, noting the *Mystical Nativity* postdates Savonarola's death.
- **Sexuality**: A 1502 charge that "Botticelli keeps a boy" (sodomy accusation) was never prosecuted; scholars debate whether it reflects his homosexuality or was political slander.
- **Attribution**: Many works, especially Madonnas, are debated between master and workshop; Lightbown sees a sharp division, others disagree.
- **Simonetta Vespucci**: Traditional claims that she modeled for his female figures are considered unlikely by modern scholars.
