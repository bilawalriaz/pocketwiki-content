# Eratosthenes

## Overview
Eratosthenes of Cyrene (c. 276 – c. 195/194 BC) was a Greek polymath and chief librarian at the Library of Alexandria. He is foundational to geography, which he helped define, and is best known for the first accurate calculation of Earth's circumference. His work bridged mathematics, astronomy, and chronology, establishing methods that influenced science for centuries.

## Timeline
- **c. 276 BC** — Born in Cyrene (modern Libya).
- **c. 260s BC** — Moves to Athens for advanced studies under various philosophers.
- **246 BC** — Appointed chief librarian at the Library of Alexandria by Ptolemy III.
- **c. 240s–230s BC** — Corresponds with Archimedes; conducts major geographical and mathematical work.
- **c. 196 BC** — Dies in Alexandria, reportedly by voluntary starvation after losing his sight.

## Body

### Life and Reputation
Born in Cyrene, Eratosthenes studied in Athens for about twenty years, learning from Stoic, Cynic, and Platonic teachers. In 246 BC, he was summoned to Alexandria to head the Library of Alexandria, a position that included tutoring the future Ptolemy IV. His wide-ranging expertise earned him the nickname "Pentathlos" (all-rounder), but critics called him "Beta" (second) for not being the foremost expert in any single field. He died around age 80 in Alexandria.

### Astronomical and Geographical Contributions
Eratosthenes' most famous achievement was calculating Earth's circumference. Using the difference in shadow angles at noon on the summer solstice between Alexandria and Syene (modern Aswan), he measured an angle of 7.2° (1/50 of a circle). Multiplying the reported distance between the cities (5,000 stadia) by 50, he arrived at 250,000 stadia—remarkably close to the modern value. His method contained two fortuitous errors that canceled out: Syene is not exactly on the Tropic of Cancer nor on the same meridian as Alexandria.

He also calculated the obliquity of the ecliptic (the tilt of Earth's axis) with high accuracy and devised a calendar with a 365-day year, adding a leap day every four years. In geography, his three-volume *Geographica* was the first to use a grid of parallels and meridians to map the known world, dividing it into five climate zones and recording over 400 cities.

### Mathematics and Other Works
In mathematics, he introduced the Sieve of Eratosthenes, an efficient algorithm for finding prime numbers. He proposed a mechanical solution to the problem of doubling the cube using a device called the *Mesolabio*. His work *Platonikos* explored mathematical proportions in Plato's philosophy and music theory. He also founded scientific chronology, using historical records to date events like the Trojan War (to 1184 BC) and establish a timeline based on Olympic victors.

## Terms
- **Polymath**: A person of wide-ranging learning and expertise.
- **Library of Alexandria**: The ancient world's largest repository of knowledge, where Eratosthenes served as chief librarian.
- **Geography**: The study of Earth's surface, a term coined by Eratosthenes.
- **Stadia (singular: stadion)**: An ancient Greek unit of distance; the exact length is debated but was about 157–185 meters.
- **Obliquity of the ecliptic**: The angle between the planes of Earth's equator and its orbit around the Sun.
- **Sieve of Eratosthenes**: A simple algorithm for finding all prime numbers up to a given limit.
- **Armillary sphere**: A model of objects in the sky, consisting of a framework of rings representing celestial coordinates.
- **Chronology**: The arrangement of events in the order of their occurrence in time.

## Debates and Open questions
- The precise length of the "stadia" Eratosthenes used is unknown, leading to uncertainty about the exact accuracy of his circumference calculation.
- The method he used to determine the obliquity of the ecliptic is not recorded and remains speculative.
- The authenticity of a letter attributed to him on doubling the cube is disputed.
- The full content and structure of his lost *Geographica* are reconstructed only from fragments in later authors like Strabo.
