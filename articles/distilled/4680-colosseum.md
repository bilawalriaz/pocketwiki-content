# Colosseum

## Overview
The Colosseum is an elliptical amphitheatre in Rome, Italy, and the largest ancient amphitheatre ever built. Constructed by the Flavian dynasty emperors Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian, it was the premier venue for public spectacles like gladiatorial contests and animal hunts. Its design and scale made it a symbol of Imperial Roman power, and it remains an iconic landmark and major tourist attraction today.

## Timeline
- **64 AD** — Great Fire of Rome devastates the area; Emperor Nero later builds his Domus Aurea palace with an artificial lake on the site.
- **c. 70–72 AD** — Emperor Vespasian begins construction of the amphitheatre on the filled-in site of Nero's lake.
- **80 AD** — Completed under Emperor Titus; inaugural games are held.
- **81–96 AD** — Emperor Domitian adds the hypogeum (underground network) and a top gallery.
- **217 AD** — A major fire damages the wooden upper levels; repairs continue for decades.
- **399 & 404 AD** — Emperor Honorius bans gladiatorial fights.
- **443 AD** — A major earthquake causes significant damage.
- **1349 AD** — Another great earthquake collapses the outer south side.
- **1749 AD** — Pope Benedict XIV consecrates the site as a Christian shrine, forbidding its use as a quarry.
- **1993–2000** — A major restoration programme is completed.
- **2013–2016** — A full cleaning and restoration of the façade is sponsored by Tod's.

## Body

### Construction and Purpose
The Colosseum was built on the site of Nero's private lake, a populist gesture to return land to the public. Construction, begun by Vespasian around 70–72 AD and completed by Titus in 80 AD, was funded by spoils from the First Jewish–Roman War. The structure, made of travertine, tuff, and concrete, could hold an estimated 50,000 to 80,000 spectators. It was used for gladiatorial contests (*munera*), animal hunts (*venatio*), executions, and possibly mock sea battles (*naumachiae*), though the latter is debated due to lack of physical evidence.

### Design and Structure
The free-standing elliptical building featured a complex system for crowd management, including 80 numbered entrances and *vomitoria* (passageways) for rapid entry and exit. Seating was strictly stratified by social class, from the emperor and senators at the bottom to the poor and women in the top wooden gallery. The arena floor covered the *hypogeum*, a two-level underground network of tunnels and cages added by Domitian, which housed gladiators and animals and contained machinery for lifts and scenery. A retractable awning (*velarium*) shaded spectators.

### Later History and Symbolism
After the fall of Rome, the Colosseum was repurposed as housing, a fortress, and a quarry for building materials. It suffered severe damage from earthquakes, notably in 1349. In the 18th century, Pope Benedict XIV consecrated it as a Christian site, believing it to be a place of martyrdom, though historical evidence for widespread martyrdom there is lacking. Today, it is a global symbol of Rome, a major tourist site, and a venue for papal ceremonies like the Good Friday *Via Crucis* procession. It is also used as a backdrop for concerts and as a symbol in the campaign against capital punishment.

## Terms
*   **Amphitheatre:** An oval or circular open-air venue with tiered seating surrounding a central performance area.
*   **Flavian dynasty:** The Roman imperial dynasty (69–96 AD) comprising emperors Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian, who built the Colosseum.
*   **Hypogeum:** The elaborate underground network of tunnels, cages, and machinery beneath the arena floor.
*   **Velarium:** A large, retractable canvas awning that shaded spectators from the sun.
*   **Vomitoria:** Passageways in the seating tiers that allowed spectators to enter and exit quickly.
*   **Munera:** Gladiatorial contests, privately funded spectacles with a religious element.
*   **Venatio:** Animal hunts featuring wild beasts imported from across the empire.
*   **Naumachia:** Staged mock sea battles, a practice debated for the Colosseum due to logistical questions.
*   **Spolia:** Architectural materials taken from older buildings for reuse.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Mock Sea Battles:** Ancient accounts describe the arena being flooded for naval displays, but scholars debate its practicality due to the lack of waterproofing evidence and the later construction of the hypogeum.
*   **Christian Martyrdom:** While tradition holds the Colosseum as a major site of Christian martyrdom, historical records and physical evidence are scarce. Many scholars believe most martyrdoms occurred elsewhere in Rome, such as the Circus Maximus.
*   **Capacity:** Ancient sources cite a capacity of 87,000, but modern estimates typically range from 50,000 to 80,000.
