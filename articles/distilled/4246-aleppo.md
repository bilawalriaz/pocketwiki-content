# Aleppo

## Overview
Aleppo is one of the world's oldest continuously inhabited cities, located in northern Syria. Its historical significance stems from its strategic position as a major trading hub on the Silk Road between the Mediterranean and Mesopotamia. The city's long history is marked by periods of great prosperity and devastating conflict, most recently the Syrian civil war, which caused massive destruction.

- **301–286 BC** — Hellenic settlement named Beroea established by Seleucus Nicator.
- **637** — Captured by Rashidun Muslims under Abu Ubaidah ibn al-Jarrah.
- **944** — Becomes capital of the independent Hamdanid Emirate.
- **1260** — Captured and sacked by the Mongols under Hulagu.
- **1516** — Incorporated into the Ottoman Empire.
- **1869** — Economic decline begins after the opening of the Suez Canal diverts trade.
- **1920** — Declared capital of the French-mandated State of Aleppo.
- **1946** — Gains independence as part of Syria; rivalry with Damascus intensifies.
- **2012–2016** — The Battle of Aleppo devastates the city during the Syrian civil war.

## Body

### Ancient and Medieval History
Aleppo's history begins in the third millennium BC as the Amorite city of Ḥalab, capital of the kingdom of Yamhad. It was later ruled by Hittites, Assyrians, and Arameans. After Alexander the Great's conquest in 333 BC, it was refounded as the Greek city Beroea. Following the Arab conquest in 637 AD, it became a major Islamic city. Under the Hamdanid dynasty (944–1002), it was a center of culture and commerce. The city was a key prize in the Crusades and was ruled by the Zengids and Ayyubids before its catastrophic sack by the Mongols in 1260. It was later recovered by the Mamluks.

### Ottoman Era and Decline
Aleppo entered the Ottoman Empire in 1516 and became its third-largest city after Constantinople and Cairo. Its prosperity peaked in the 16th and 17th centuries as a terminus for the Silk Road, hosting numerous European consulates. The city's economy declined after the 18th century due to the fall of Safavid Iran (disrupting the silk trade) and, decisively, the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869, which diverted trade to sea routes. Post-WWI borders, which cut Aleppo off from its northern hinterland and port, further marginalized the city relative to Damascus.

### Modern Era and Civil War
After Syrian independence, Aleppo rivaled Damascus for political and economic dominance. The city was a major industrial and commercial center until the Syrian civil war. Anti-government protests began in 2011, escalating into the devastating Battle of Aleppo (2012–2016). The city was divided between government and rebel forces, leading to massive destruction, including of the historic Old City. The government, with Russian support, recaptured the entire city by December 2016. An estimated 31,000 people were killed in the conflict. In November 2024, the city was captured by Syrian opposition groups led by Hayat Tahrir al-Sham.

### Architecture and Culture
Aleppo's Old City, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, is characterized by its medieval Citadel, vast covered souqs (including the world's largest), khans (caravanserais), mosques, and churches. The al-Jdaydeh Christian quarter was established outside the walls in the 15th century. The city is renowned for its distinctive cuisine, featuring numerous varieties of kibbeh and kebab, and its musical tradition, particularly the Qudud Halabiya, inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list.

## Terms
*   **Silk Road:** Ancient network of trade routes connecting the East and West, on which Aleppo was a key western terminus.
*   **Amorites:** Semitic-speaking people who founded the kingdom of Yamhad with Aleppo as its capital in the 2nd millennium BC.
*   **Beroea:** The Greek name given to Aleppo during the Hellenistic period under the Seleucid Empire.
*   **Hamdanid Emirate:** A Shia Muslim dynasty that ruled Aleppo from 944, fostering a golden age of culture and trade.
*   **Mamluks:** A warrior caste that ruled Egypt and Syria, recapturing Aleppo from the Mongols in 1260.
*   **Souq:** A traditional marketplace or bazaar, often covered, forming a central part of the Old City's economy.
*   **Khan (Caravanserai):** A roadside inn where travelers could rest and recover, typically surrounding a courtyard, common in Aleppo's souqs.
*   **Citadel of Aleppo:** A massive fortified palace in the center of the Old City, built atop a large artificial mound with origins in the 3rd millennium BC.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The identification of the ancient city of **Armi**, mentioned in the Ebla tablets, with Aleppo is contested by historians.
*   The extent to which post-Ottoman border changes, rather than internal factors, caused Aleppo's decline relative to Damascus is a subject of historical analysis.
*   The causes of the 1850 sectarian violence in Aleppo are debated; some scholars argue it was driven by economic tensions from Tanzimat reforms rather than pure religious conflict.
