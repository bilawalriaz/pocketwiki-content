# Northern and Southern dynasties

## Overview
The Northern and Southern dynasties (420–589 AD) was a period of political division in China following the Sixteen Kingdoms and Eastern Jin. While marked by civil war, it was also a time of significant cultural flourishing, technological advancement, and the spread of Buddhism and Daoism. It saw large-scale Han Chinese migration south of the Yangtze and accelerated sinicization (adoption of Han Chinese culture) among non-Han groups in the north. The period ended with reunification by the Sui dynasty.

## Timeline
- **420** — Liu Yu founds the Liu Song dynasty, ending the Eastern Jin and beginning the Southern dynasties.
- **439** — Northern Wei unifies the north, ending the Sixteen Kingdoms and beginning the Northern and Southern dynasties period.
- **c. 471–499** — Emperor Xiaowen of Northern Wei enacts major sinicization reforms, including moving the capital to Luoyang.
- **523** — Rebellion of the Six Garrisons begins in Northern Wei, leading to its eventual split.
- **534–535** — Northern Wei splits into Eastern Wei and Western Wei.
- **550** — Eastern Wei is replaced by the Northern Qi dynasty.
- **557** — Western Wei is replaced by the Northern Zhou dynasty.
- **577** — Northern Zhou conquers Northern Qi, reunifying the north.
- **581** — Yang Jian usurps the throne, founding the Sui dynasty.
- **589** — Sui dynasty conquers the Chen dynasty, reunifying China.

## Body

### Background and Division
The period emerged from the collapse of the Western Jin dynasty (266–416 AD). The War of the Eight Princes (291–306) weakened the Jin, allowing the "Five Barbarians" (nomadic groups resettled in the north) to seize power. The Jin court fled south, reestablishing as the Eastern Jin in 318. The north fragmented into the Sixteen Kingdoms. In 420, the Jin general Liu Yu seized the throne, founding the Liu Song dynasty and starting the Southern dynasties. In 439, the Northern Wei, founded by the Xianbei Tuoba tribe, unified the north, starting the Northern dynasties.

### Northern Dynasties: Sinicization and Conflict
The Northern dynasties were dominated by the Xianbei-founded Northern Wei. Under Emperor Xiaowen (r. 471–499), the court enacted sweeping sinicization reforms: adopting Han clothing, language, surnames, and intermarrying with Han elites. The capital was moved to Luoyang. These reforms caused a rift between the sinicized aristocracy and the traditional steppe military. This tension, combined with social unrest, led to the Rebellion of the Six Garrisons (523) and the split of Northern Wei into Eastern and Western Wei in 534–535. These were soon replaced by the Northern Qi (550) and Northern Zhou (557). The Northern Zhou, which briefly revived Xianbei warrior culture, conquered the Northern Qi in 577 but was overthrown by the Sui in 581.

### Southern Dynasties: Aristocracy and Instability
The Southern dynasties (Liu Song, Southern Qi, Liang, Chen) were ruled by military strongmen from lowly backgrounds who gradually stripped power from the great aristocratic families (shijia). The Liang dynasty (502–557) reached a cultural peak under Emperor Wu, a patron of Buddhism, but was devastated by the Hou Jing Rebellion (548–552). The final Chen dynasty (557–589) was weakened by internal strife and the rise of a money-based economy, which undermined the old landowning aristocracy. It was conquered by the Sui in 589.

### Culture and Society
The period saw a mass migration of Han Chinese to the south, transforming the Yangtze valley from a frontier into a major cultural center. Buddhism and Daoism flourished, with two essential Daoist canons written. Technological advances included the stirrup (enabling heavy cavalry), medicine, astronomy, and mathematics (exemplified by Zu Chongzhi). Literature and arts thrived, with southern styles being flowery and northern styles more direct. The introduction of Sanskrit phonology influenced the study of Chinese language and poetry.

## Terms
- **Sinicization**: The process by which non-Han peoples adopted Han Chinese culture, language, and customs.
- **Xianbei**: A nomadic people who founded the Northern Wei and other northern dynasties.
- **Shijia**: The great, powerful aristocratic families that dominated political and social life in the Southern dynasties.
- **Equal-field system**: A land redistribution system introduced by Northern Wei to increase tax revenue and agricultural output.
- **Neo-Daoism (Xuanxue)**: A philosophical movement blending Daoist and Confucian ideas, prominent in the Southern dynasties.
- **Pianwen**: A style of flowery, rhymed prose popular in Southern dynasty literature.
- **Mahayana Buddhism**: A major branch of Buddhism that became widespread in China during this period.
- **Rebellion of the Six Garrisons**: A 523 revolt in Northern Wei that led to its fragmentation.
