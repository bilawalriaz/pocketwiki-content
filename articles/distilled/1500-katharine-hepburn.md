# Katharine Hepburn

## Overview
Katharine Hepburn (1907–2003) was an American actress whose six-decade career established her as a defining figure of 20th-century cinema. Raised by progressive parents in Connecticut, she cultivated a public persona of fierce independence and athleticism that mirrored the strong-willed, sophisticated women she portrayed on screen. After an early Oscar win for *Morning Glory* (1933), a string of commercial failures led exhibitors to label her "box office poison" in 1938. She engineered a comeback by buying out her RKO contract and securing the film rights to *The Philadelphia Story* (1940), which revived her stardom. At MGM, she formed a 26-year professional and romantic partnership with Spencer Tracy, producing nine films. In later decades, she transitioned to Shakespearean stage work and mature screen roles, winning three more Oscars for *Guess Who's Coming to Dinner* (1967), *The Lion in Winter* (1968), and *On Golden Pond* (1981)—a record four Best Actress wins. Hepburn shunned Hollywood convention, wearing trousers, avoiding publicity, and living independently after a brief early marriage. Her unconventional lifestyle and portrayals of autonomous women influenced shifting perceptions of gender roles, leading the American Film Institute to name her the greatest female star of classic Hollywood in 1999.

## Body

### Early Life and Formative Trauma
Hepburn was the second of six children raised in a progressive Hartford household. Her father, a urologist, founded the New England Social Hygiene Association; her mother headed the Connecticut Woman Suffrage Association and campaigned for birth control with Margaret Sanger. The children were encouraged to debate freely and test physical limits—Hepburn became an avid golfer, swimmer, and tennis player. At 13, she discovered her beloved brother Tom dead by hanging during a visit to New York. The family denied suicide, calling it an experiment gone wrong. The trauma made Hepburn nervous and withdrawn; she dropped out of school, was tutored privately, and used Tom’s birthday (November 8) as her own until revealing her true birth date in her 1991 autobiography. She attended Bryn Mawr College (1924–1928), initially struggling academically and socially, but found her footing in theater, winning the lead in *The Woman in the Moon* her senior year.

### Stage Struggles and Hollywood Arrival (1928–1934)
After graduation, Hepburn joined a Baltimore repertory company but was fired from her first starring role on opening night for lateness and rushed lines. She cycled through Broadway productions—*These Days*, *Holiday*, *Death Takes a Holiday*, *Art and Mrs. Bottle*—often fired or replaced, once told by a playwright she had "no talent." Her breakthrough came in Philip Barry’s *The Warrior's Husband* (1932), where her athleticism (leaping down stairs with a stag over her shoulder) and aggressive energy earned raves. A Hollywood scout arranged a screen test for *A Bill of Divorcement* (1932); director George Cukor, impressed by her oddity and a simple gesture of picking up a glass, advocated for her. She demanded $1,500/week—high for an unknown—and won a long-term RKO contract. Her third film, *Morning Glory* (1933), won her an Oscar, followed by *Little Women* (1933), a massive hit. A Broadway return in *The Lake* (1933) ended in disaster: producer Jed Harris’s poor direction and refusal to rehearse led to scathing reviews; Hepburn paid $14,000 of her savings to close the show, a lesson in career autonomy.

### Career Crisis and Self-Made Revival (1934–1940)
A string of period dramas—*The Little Minister*, *Break of Hearts*, *Mary of Scotland*, *A Woman Rebels*, *Quality Street*—flopped. Hepburn’s press hostility (refusing interviews, snapping at reporters), boyish dress, and "arrogant" reputation alienated the public. David O. Selznick denied her *Gone with the Wind*, citing no sex appeal. *Stage Door* (1937) and Howard Hawks’s *Bringing Up Baby* (1938) drew critical praise but weak box office; the latter cemented her "box office poison" label. Rather than accept a B-movie, Hepburn bought out her RKO contract for $75,000. She starred in *Holiday* (1938) at Columbia, another flop, then left Hollywood to star in Barry’s new play *The Philadelphia Story*, written to showcase her mix of humor, aggression, and vulnerability. Howard Hughes bought the film rights for her; she sold them to MGM on condition she star, choose director Cukor, and co-stars James Stewart and Cary Grant. The 1940 film was a smash, with *Time* declaring "Come on back, Katie, all is forgiven," earning her a third Oscar nomination.

### The Tracy-Hepburn Partnership and MGM Years (1942–1952)
*Woman of the Year* (1942) launched the Tracy-Hepburn screen duo and their secret 26-year romance. Tracy, married but separated, refused divorce; the relationship remained hidden. Hepburn devoted herself to managing his alcoholism and depression, often mothering him. At MGM, they made *Keeper of the Flame* (1942), *Without Love* (1945), *The Sea of Grass* (1947), *State of the Union* (1948), and *Adam's Rib* (1949)—the last a hit written for them by Garson Kanin and Ruth Gordon. Hepburn’s career slowed as she prioritized Tracy; she did no stage work 1942–1950. Her anti-communist stance in the late 1940s led to a nine-month blacklist and picketing at *Song of Love* (1947) screenings. In 1950, she tackled Shakespeare’s Rosalind in *As You Like It* on Broadway, selling out 148 shows. *The African Queen* (1951), shot on location in the Belgian Congo, earned her a fifth Oscar nomination and Bogart his only win, proving her draw without Tracy. *Pat and Mike* (1952), the second Kanin-Gordon vehicle, became her favorite Tracy film. Exhausted, she starred in Shaw’s *The Millionairess* in London and Broadway (1952), but a film adaptation failed to materialize—her "biggest disappointment."

### Artistic Peak and Mature Persona (1953–1967)
Free of MGM, Hepburn chose David Lean’s *Summertime* (1955), earning an Oscar nomination for a lonely woman’s Venetian affair; a canal fall gave her a chronic eye infection. She toured Australia with the Old Vic (1955–56) in *Merchant of Venice*, *Taming of the Shrew*, *Measure for Measure*. *The Rainmaker* (1956) brought another nomination; she noted she was "playing me"—the "maiden aunt" archetype of independent, unmarried women finding agency through romance. *The Iron Petticoat* (1956) she called her worst film. *Desk Set* (1957) reunited her with Tracy but flopped. She returned to Shakespeare in Stratford, CT (*Merchant*, *Much Ado*). *Suddenly, Last Summer* (1959) with Elizabeth Taylor was a "miserable experience" clashing with director Mankiewicz, but earned an eighth nomination. *Long Day's Journey Into Night* (1962), filmed for a tenth of her salary, she called the greatest American play and her best screen work, winning Cannes Best Actress and an Oscar nomination.

### Late Career Triumphs and Television Turn (1967–1994)
Hepburn paused five years to care for ailing Tracy. *Guess Who's Coming to Dinner* (1967), their ninth film, addressed interracial marriage; Tracy died 17 days post-wrap. Hepburn won her second Oscar, 34 years after the first, feeling it honored Tracy too. *The Lion in Winter* (1968) brought a third (tied with Streisand) and a BAFTA. *The Madwoman of Chaillot* (1969) failed. She starred in the Broadway musical *Coco* (1969–70), nervous but praised for "guts," earning a Tony nomination. The 1970s brought television: *The Glass Menagerie* (1973, Emmy nomination), *Love Among the Ruins* (1975, her only Emmy win with Olivier). *Rooster Cogburn* (1975) with Wayne was moderate. A hip fracture during *A Matter of Gravity* tour (1976) didn’t stop her; she performed in a wheelchair. *On Golden Pond* (1981) with Henry Fonda became the year’s second-highest grosser; she dove into Squam Lake at 74, winning a fourth Oscar and second BAFTA. *The West Side Waltz* (1981) earned a second Tony nod. Television films dominated her 80s: *Mrs. Delafield Wants to Marry* (1986), *Laura Lansing Slept Here* (1988). Her autobiography *Me: Stories of My Life* (1991) topped bestseller lists. Final theatrical film: *Love Affair* (1994, age 87, supporting role). Final role: *One Christmas* (1994, TV).

### Personal Life: Independence and Contradictions
Hepburn married once, Ludlow Ogden Smith (1928–1934), calling herself "a terrible pig" for exploiting his devotion. She declined remarriage and children, deeming motherhood a full-time commitment she wouldn't make. Affairs with agent Leland Hayward and Howard Hughes ended as she prioritized career. The Tracy relationship was the core of her emotional life; she never attended his funeral (1967) out of respect for his wife, speaking publicly only after Louise Tracy’s death (1983). Hepburn was an atheist ("we should be kind to each other"), a dedicated Democrat, pro-choice, and practiced Schweitzer’s "Reverence for Life." She wore trousers and went barefoot decades before fashion accepted it, citing stockings as the reason. Her privacy was fierce—wrestling a camera from a photographer—yet she craved recognition. In later years she opened up, giving a landmark *Dick Cavett Show* interview (1973).

### Acting Style and Screen Persona
Hepburn was not instinctive but rigorously prepared: studying texts, learning co-stars' lines, rehearsing extensively, performing her own stunts. She involved herself in scripts, costumes, lighting. Her characters—wealthy, intelligent, strong—followed a formula (per Kanin): a "stuck-up" woman humbled by an "earthy" force or cataclysm, revealing vulnerability, ultimately "restored to a safe position within the status quo." Critics debated her versatility: some saw a compelling personality playing herself; others, a limited range masked by charisma. Her clipped patrician voice became iconic. She acknowledged the overlap: "I think I'm always the same... I liked material that showed that personality."

### Legacy and Memorials
Hepburn is enshrined in *Women Who Changed the World*, *Encyclopædia Britannica*’s "300 Women Who Changed the World," and AFI’s "Greatest American Screen Legend" (female). Academics credit her with bringing feminist issues to classical Hollywood and modeling the "modern woman"—assertive, independent, trouser-wearing. Four films (*African Queen*, *Philadelphia Story*, *Bringing Up Baby*, *Guess Who's Coming to Dinner*) appear on AFI’s 100 Greatest American Films. She received Kennedy Center Honors (1990), Screen Actors Guild Lifetime Achievement (1979), and a Council of Fashion Designers Lifetime Achievement (1986). Memorials include Katharine Hepburn Place (NYC), the Katharine Houghton Hepburn Center at Bryn Mawr (awarding an annual Medal), the Katharine Hepburn Cultural Arts Center in Old Saybrook, CT, and a USPS "Legends of Hollywood" stamp. Portrayals include Cate Blanchett’s Oscar-winning turn in *The Aviator* (2004) and the one-woman play *Tea at Five*.

## Terms
- **Box office poison**: A 1938 label by the Independent Theatre Owners of America applied to stars (including Hepburn) whose films lost money, making them commercially toxic.
- **Studio system**: The Hollywood model (1920s–1950s) where studios controlled actors via long-term contracts; Hepburn broke hers by buying out RKO.
- **Screwball comedy**: A 1930s–40s genre of fast-paced, witty romances featuring battle-of-the-sexes dynamics; Hepburn starred in *Bringing Up Baby* and *The Philadelphia Story*.
- **Technicolor**: A saturated color film process; *The African Queen* (1951) was Hepburn’s first Technicolor film.
- **Method acting**: An instinctive, emotion-memory technique; Hepburn explicitly rejected this, favoring rigorous textual study and rehearsal.
- **Maiden aunt archetype**: Hepburn’s self-described screen persona in the 1950s–60s: mature, independent, unmarried women finding agency through romance (*The African Queen*, *Summertime*, *The Rainmaker*).
- **House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC)**: A 1940s–50s congressional committee investigating alleged communist influence; Hepburn’s opposition to its Hollywood blacklist led to a nine-month work drought.
- **Kennedy Center Honors**: Annual U.S. arts lifetime achievement award; Hepburn received it in 1990.
- **Reverence for Life**: Albert Schweitzer’s ethical philosophy emphasizing respect for all living things; Hepburn practiced it but rejected religion/afterlife.
- **AFI 100 Years...100 Stars**: American Film Institute’s 1999 ranking naming Hepburn the greatest female screen legend of classic Hollywood.

## Debates and open questions
- **Sexual orientation**: Biographer William J. Mann (2007) argued Hepburn was lesbian/bisexual; her niece Katharine Houghton denied evidence; friend Liz Smith (2017 documentary) attested she was. Hepburn joked about rumors but never confirmed.
- **Acting versatility vs. personality**: Critics (e.g., David Macaray, Helen Lawrenson) contend Hepburn played essentially the same character—her own personality—across roles, questioning her range versus her star power.
- **Feminist legacy**: While celebrated for modeling independence, some feminists criticized her public statements that women "cannot have it all" (career and family), viewing it as reinforcing limitations.
- **Tracy relationship dynamics**: Hepburn described "absolute bliss" but also mothering an alcoholic, depressed man who refused divorce; scholars debate whether this was empowerment or self-erasure.
- **Later work quality**: Her 1970s–90s television films were popular but critically dismissed; debate persists whether they extended her legacy or diluted it.
