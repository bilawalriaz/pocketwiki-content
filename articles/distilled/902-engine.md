# Engine

## Overview
An engine or motor is a machine that converts one or more forms of energy—potential, heat, chemical, electric, or nuclear—into mechanical motion. Engines are central to transportation, industry, and power generation because mechanical energy drives work such as propulsion, cutting, grinding, crushing, and mixing. Heat engines, which transform thermal energy into work, dominate practical applications, while electric, pneumatic, hydraulic, and molecular motors cover specialized roles. The evolution from ancient water wheels and steam pumps to modern internal combustion and electric motors reflects centuries of engineering progress driven by efficiency, power density, and environmental concerns.

## Body

### Classification and Energy Sources
Engines are categorized by the energy source they accept and the motion they produce. Heat engines convert thermal energy into mechanical work through thermodynamic cycles; examples include internal combustion engines, steam engines, and gas turbines. Electric motors use electrical energy interacting with magnetic fields to generate motion. Pneumatic motors rely on compressed air, hydraulic motors on pressurized fluid, and clockwork motors on elastic energy. Biological systems employ molecular motors like myosin, which use chemical energy directly without heat as an intermediate.

### Heat Engines and Combustion
Heat engines operate by converting heat into work. In internal combustion engines, fuel combustion in a sealed chamber generates high-pressure gas that expands against pistons or turbine blades, producing mechanical motion. External combustion engines heat a separate working fluid—steam, gas, or organic liquid—through a wall or heat exchanger. Air-breathing engines use atmospheric oxygen to burn fuel, offering better specific impulse than rockets but requiring continuous airflow. Non-combustive heat engines include nuclear plants using reactor heat to drive steam turbines and thermoacoustic engines that convert temperature differences into sound waves.

### Historical Development
Ancient civilizations used human, animal, water, and wind power through simple machines like capstans, treadmills, and water wheels. Hero of Alexandria is credited with many wind and steam-powered machines in the 1st century AD, including the Aeolipile. Medieval Islamic engineers advanced gear systems and water-raising devices, with al-Jazari pioneering crank mechanisms. Taqi al-Din in 1551 and Giovanni Branca in 1629 described rudimentary steam turbine devices. The Chinese invented the solid rocket motor in the 13th century. The Industrial Revolution marked a turning point: Thomas Savery's original steam engines were not mechanical engines but pumps. The Watt steam engine (1763–1775) evolved from the 1712 Newcomen design, enabling mechanized factories and railways. Internal combustion engines emerged in the 19th century, with de Rivaz and the Niépce brothers testing engines in 1807, Barsanti and Matteucci's free-piston engine (1853–57), Lenoir's 1860 model, and the 1877 Otto cycle providing compact, high-power alternatives to steam.

### Automobile Engines
Karl Benz's 1886 automobile popularized lightweight gasoline engines operating on the four-stroke Otto cycle. Diesel engines, more thermally efficient, became standard for trucks and buses. Innovations included turbocharging, electronic fuel injection, and variable valve timing. Engine configurations ranged from 1- to 16-cylinder designs, with flat ("boxer") engines offering balance advantages. The largest internal combustion engine, the Wärtsilä-Sulzer RTA96-C, powers container ships with over 80 MW output. Modern trends emphasize downsizing, hybridization, and emission controls to meet efficiency and environmental standards.

### Electric and Alternative Motors
Electric motors convert electrical energy into motion via magnetic field interactions. They dominate applications from household appliances to industrial machinery and ship propulsion. Efficiency improvements focus on reducing stator and rotor losses, optimizing magnetic materials, and minimizing friction. Pneumatic and hydraulic motors serve niche roles in tools and heavy machinery. Hybrid systems combine multiple energy sources, such as battery-electric and internal combustion, to optimize performance and reduce emissions.

### Performance Metrics
Engine performance is evaluated by speed (rpm), torque (rotational force), power (work rate), efficiency (output/input ratio), and noise levels. Electric motors typically exceed 90% efficiency and operate quietly, while internal combustion engines produce significant noise and lower thermal efficiency. Thrust-producing engines like turbofans and rockets generate substantial acoustic energy due to high-velocity exhaust interacting with ambient air. Optimal combustion efficiency in passenger vehicles is reached with a coolant temperature of around 110 °C (230 °F).

## Terms
- **Heat engine**: A device converting thermal energy into mechanical work via thermodynamic cycles.
- **Internal combustion engine**: An engine where fuel burns inside a combustion chamber, directly driving pistons or turbines.
- **External combustion engine**: An engine where an external heat source heats a working fluid to produce motion.
- **Air-breathing engine**: A combustion engine using atmospheric oxygen instead of carrying an oxidizer.
- **Torque**: A rotational force calculated as the product of applied force and distance from the rotation axis.
- **Specific impulse**: A measure of thrust efficiency, especially relevant in jet and rocket engines.
- **Thermodynamic cycle**: A series of processes that convert heat into work, such as the Otto or Rankine cycle.
- **Prime mover**: A component that transforms fluid pressure or flow changes into mechanical energy.
- **Turbocharging**: Forcing compressed air into an engine to increase power output.
- **Hybrid motor**: A system combining two or more distinct energy sources, such as electric and internal combustion.

## Debates and Open Questions
Several unresolved issues persist in engine technology. The environmental impact of internal combustion engines remains contentious, particularly regarding CO₂ emissions and NOₓ production from hydrogen-air combustion. While electric motors offer higher efficiency and lower local emissions, their global footprint depends on electricity generation sources. Diesel engines produce lower hydrocarbon and CO2 emissions, but greater particulate and NOx pollution, than gasoline engines, and are also 40% more fuel efficient than comparable gasoline engines. The viability of pneumatic motors in transportation is limited by efficiency losses. Additionally, the definition of "zero-emission" varies: strict interpretations require pure hydrogen-oxygen combustion, while broader definitions allow water vapor and exclude fuel cells. Exhaust gas from a spark ignition engine consists of nitrogen 70 to 75% (by volume), water vapor 10 to 12%, carbon dioxide 10 to 13.5%, hydrogen 0.5 to 2%, oxygen 0.2 to 2%, carbon monoxide 0.1 to 6%, unburnt hydrocarbons and partial oxidation products 0.5 to 1%, nitrogen monoxide 0.01 to 0.4%, nitrous oxide <100 ppm, sulfur dioxide 15 to 60 ppm, traces of other compounds, and other particles. Carbon monoxide is highly toxic, and can cause carbon monoxide poisoning. Catalytic converters can reduce toxic emissions, but not eliminate them. Resulting greenhouse gas emissions, chiefly carbon dioxide, from the widespread use of engines in the modern industrialized world is contributing to the global greenhouse effect – a primary concern regarding global warming. Future developments may hinge on advances in battery technology, alternative fuels, and regulatory frameworks balancing performance, cost, and sustainability.
