# George C. Marshall

## Overview
George C. Marshall (1880–1959) was a preeminent American soldier-statesman whose career spanned both World Wars and the early Cold War. As U.S. Army Chief of Staff during World War II, he was the principal organizer of the massive American military expansion and a key architect of Allied victory. Later, as Secretary of State, he championed the Marshall Plan, a pivotal economic aid program for post-war Europe, for which he received the Nobel Peace Prize. His career exemplifies the transition from military leadership to high-level diplomacy in the 20th century.

- **5 Jun 1947** — Outlined the European Recovery Program (Marshall Plan) in a speech at Harvard University.
- **7 Jan 1949** — Resigned as Secretary of State due to ill health.
- **Sep 1950** — Appointed U.S. Secretary of Defense.
- **1953** — Awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for the Marshall Plan.
- **16 Oct 1959** — Died in Washington, D.C.

## Body

### Early Career and World War I
Marshall graduated from VMI in 1901 and was commissioned in 1902. He served in the Philippines and held various instructional and staff positions, graduating first in his Army Staff College class in 1908. During World War I, he served with the 1st Division in France, helping to plan its combat operations. He gained recognition for his planning of the Battle of Cantigny (1918) and later served on the staff of the American Expeditionary Forces headquarters, where he was a key planner of the Meuse-Argonne Offensive.

### Chief of Staff and World War II
Appointed Chief of Staff in September 1939, Marshall organized the largest military expansion in U.S. history, growing the Army from 189,000 to over eight million soldiers. He coordinated Allied operations in both Europe and the Pacific and was instrumental in selecting many of the war's top American commanders, including Dwight D. Eisenhower and Omar Bradley. His leadership was central to the Allied victory, earning him the moniker "organizer of victory" from Winston Churchill. He was promoted to the five-star rank of General of the Army in 1944.

### Post-War Diplomacy and the Marshall Plan
After a failed 1945-47 mission to mediate the Chinese Civil War, Marshall became Secretary of State in 1947. His most significant achievement was advocating for the Marshall Plan, a U.S. program to rebuild European economies and prevent the spread of communism. The plan, announced in 1947, provided billions in aid and is credited with stabilizing post-war Europe. Marshall resigned in 1949 due to exhaustion. He later served as Secretary of Defense during the Korean War (1950-51), where he helped restore military morale and supported the relief of General Douglas MacArthur.

## Terms
- **General of the Army**: The highest rank in the U.S. Army, a five-star rank equivalent to a field marshal, created during World War II.
- **Marshall Plan**: Officially the European Recovery Program, a U.S. initiative providing over $13 billion in economic aid to rebuild Western European economies after World War II.
- **Chief of Staff of the Army**: The senior uniformed Army officer responsible for organizing, training, and equipping all active and reserve Army units.
- **American Expeditionary Forces (AEF)**: The United States Armed Forces sent to Europe in World War I.
- **Meuse-Argonne Offensive**: The final Allied offensive of World War I on the Western Front, a major battle involving over a million American troops.
- **Chinese Civil War**: The conflict between the Kuomintang (Nationalists) and the Chinese Communists, which paused during WWII and resumed afterward, ending in Communist victory in 1949.
- **Korean War**: A conflict (1950-1953) between North Korea (supported by China and the USSR) and South Korea (supported by the UN, primarily the U.S.).

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Pearl Harbor Intelligence Failure**: A Congressional investigation criticized Marshall for delaying warnings to commanders in Hawaii based on intercepted Japanese messages, though the final report did not single him out for fault.
- **Replacement System in WWII**: Marshall's shift from a unit-rotation plan to an individual replacement system for combat troops is criticized for harming unit cohesion and soldier morale, with some historians arguing it was a significant tactical error.
- **Recognition of Israel**: As Secretary of State, Marshall strongly opposed President Truman's decision to recognize the new state of Israel in 1948, fearing it would cause war in the Middle East and was politically motivated.
- **Delayed Recognition of Soviet Threat**: Some historians, like Wilson Miscamble, argue Marshall was slow to recognize the dangers posed by the Soviet Union, not fully grasping the threat until April 1947.
