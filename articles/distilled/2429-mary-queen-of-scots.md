# Mary, Queen of Scots

## Overview
Mary, Queen of Scots (1542–1587) was the Catholic monarch of a Protestant kingdom whose claim to the English throne made her a perpetual threat to her cousin, Elizabeth I. Her life was defined by the religious and political turmoil of the Reformation, a series of ill-fated marriages, and a 19-year imprisonment in England that ended with her execution for treason. Her story is a central drama of the 16th-century struggle for power between Catholic and Protestant Europe.

## Timeline
- **8 December 1542** — Born at Linlithgow Palace; becomes Queen of Scotland six days later.
- **1548** — Sent to France for safety and education; betrothed to the Dauphin Francis.
- **24 April 1558** — Marries Francis, Dauphin of France.
- **5 December 1560** — Husband Francis II dies; Mary becomes a widow.
- **19 August 1561** — Returns to Scotland after 13 years in France.
- **29 July 1565** — Marries her half-cousin, Henry Stuart, Lord Darnley.
- **9 March 1566** — Darnley and conspirators murder her secretary, David Rizzio.
- **19 June 1566** — Her son, the future James VI and I, is born.
- **10 February 1567** — Lord Darnley is murdered in an explosion at Kirk o' Field.
- **15 May 1567** — Marries James Hepburn, 4th Earl of Bothwell, widely suspected of Darnley's murder.
- **24 July 1567** — Forced to abdicate in favour of her infant son, James VI.
- **16 May 1568** — Flees Scotland after defeat at the Battle of Langside; lands in England.
- **25 October 1586** — Found guilty of plotting to assassinate Elizabeth I.
- **8 February 1587** — Executed at Fotheringhay Castle.

## Body

### Early Life and French Marriage
Mary was the only surviving legitimate child of King James V. She became queen at six days old, and Scotland was governed by regents during her childhood. In 1548, at age five, she was sent to France for safety and education, escaping the "Rough Wooing"—a military campaign by England's Henry VIII to force a marriage between Mary and his son. She married the Dauphin Francis in 1558, and upon his accession in 1559, became Queen consort of France. Her claim to the English throne, as the great-granddaughter of Henry VII, was recognized by many Catholics, who considered the Protestant Elizabeth I illegitimate. Francis's death in 1560 left Mary a widow at 18.

### Return to Scotland and Rule
Mary returned to a Scotland transformed by the Reformation. As a Catholic monarch in a now-Protestant kingdom, she adopted a pragmatic policy of tolerance, accepting the established Protestant church and retaining Protestant advisors like her half-brother, the Earl of Moray. Her personal rule was marked by an attempt to balance factions, but her claim to the English throne remained a source of tension with Elizabeth I. After failed marriage negotiations with various European princes, she married her Catholic half-cousin, Lord Darnley, in 1565. The union, which produced a son, James, quickly soured due to Darnley's arrogance and jealousy of her Italian secretary, David Rizzio. In 1566, Darnley conspired in Rizzio's murder, an act that destroyed their marriage.

### Downfall and Abdication
Following the birth of her son, Mary's husband Darnley was murdered in an explosion in February 1567. Suspicion fell on the Earl of Bothwell, whom Mary married three months later after his acquittal for the murder. This marriage, seen as scandalous by both Protestant and Catholic nobles, provoked a rebellion. Mary was imprisoned at Lochleven Castle and forced to abdicate in July 1567 in favour of her infant son, James VI. After a failed attempt to regain her throne, she fled to England in 1568, seeking protection from Elizabeth I.

### Captivity and Execution
Mary's arrival in England placed Elizabeth in a difficult position: Mary was a fellow anointed queen and a potential heir, but also a Catholic figurehead for plots against Elizabeth's Protestant rule. Elizabeth confined Mary in various castles for 18 and a half years. A key piece of evidence against Mary was the "casket letters," documents purportedly proving her complicity in Darnley's murder, though their authenticity remains debated. In 1586, Mary was implicated in the Babington Plot, a scheme to assassinate Elizabeth. She was tried for treason, convicted, and executed at Fotheringhay Castle in 1587 after Elizabeth signed her death warrant.

## Terms
*   **Regent:** A person appointed to administer a state because the monarch is a minor, absent, or incapacitated.
*   **Rough Wooing:** The military campaign led by England's Henry VIII in the 1540s to force a marriage between Mary and his son, Edward.
*   **Dauphin:** The title given to the heir apparent to the throne of France.
*   **Crown Matrimonial:** A title that would have made Darnley a co-sovereign of Scotland with the right to keep the throne if he outlived Mary.
*   **Casket Letters:** A set of documents, including letters and sonnets, presented as evidence of Mary's guilt in the murder of her husband, Darnley.
*   **Bond of Association:** A 1584 agreement, promoted by Elizabeth's government, that bound signatories to kill anyone who plotted against Elizabeth, even if it meant assassinating her potential successor.
*   **Babington Plot:** A 1586 conspiracy to assassinate Elizabeth I and place Mary on the English throne, which led directly to Mary's trial and execution.

## Debates and open questions
*   **The Casket Letters:** Their authenticity is the central historical controversy. Historians are divided on whether they are complete forgeries, altered genuine letters, or written by Mary to Bothwell. The originals were likely destroyed in 1584.
*   **Mary's Complicity:** There is no concrete proof of her direct involvement in Darnley's murder. Accusations rest on circumstantial evidence and the disputed casket letters. Historian Jenny Wormald argued Mary was a "tragic failure" unable to cope with her circumstances, while others see her as a pawn of scheming nobles.
*   **Elizabeth's Role:** Elizabeth's handling of Mary's captivity and execution is debated. She appeared to vacillate, possibly to avoid the stain of executing a fellow monarch while allowing her council to act decisively.
