# Budapest

## Overview
Budapest is the capital and primate city of Hungary, serving as the nation's political, economic, and cultural heart. Situated on the River Danube, it is a major European metropolis and global city, renowned for its historical architecture, thermal baths, and vibrant cultural life. Its strategic location at the center of the Pannonian Basin has made it a crossroads of trade and history for millennia.

## Timeline
- **1st century AD** — Roman settlement Aquincum becomes capital of Pannonia Inferior.
- **Late 9th century** — Magyar tribes settle the area, displacing earlier inhabitants.
- **1241** — The area is pillaged by the Mongols.
- **1361** — Buda becomes the capital of Hungary.
- **1541** — Buda falls to the Ottoman Empire, beginning 150 years of rule.
- **1686** — Buda is reconquered by the Holy League, ending Ottoman rule.
- **1848** — The Hungarian Revolution of 1848 begins in the capital.
- **1849** — The Chain Bridge, the first permanent bridge across the Danube, opens.
- **1873** — Buda, Pest, and Óbuda are officially unified to form Budapest.
- **1896** — The first underground railway on the European continent opens.
- **1918** — Austria-Hungary dissolves after World War I.
- **1944-45** — The city suffers major damage during the Siege of Budapest in WWII.
- **1956** — The Hungarian Revolution of 1956 is crushed by Soviet forces.
- **1987** — Buda Castle and the Danube banks are designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Etymology and History
The city's name derives from the 1873 unification of Buda, Pest, and Óbuda. The origins of "Buda" and "Pest" are debated; theories for Buda include a Turkic personal name or a legendary founder named Bleda, while "Pest" may come from a Slavic word for cave. The area's history began with a Celtic settlement, later the Roman town of Aquincum. After the Magyar settlement in the 9th century, Buda became the royal capital in 1361 and a Renaissance center under King Matthias Corvinus. Ottoman rule lasted from 1541 to 1686, after which the city entered a period of prosperity. The 19th century saw rapid growth, culminating in the 1873 unification and Budapest's role as the co-capital of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. The 20th century brought devastation in World War II, followed by Soviet occupation and the 1956 revolution. After the fall of communism in 1989, the city underwent significant renewal.

### Geography and Climate
Budapest is strategically located in the Pannonian Basin, bisected by the Danube. The city contrasts the hilly Buda side (west) with the flat Pest side (east). It is rich in green areas and possesses a unique geothermal system, with over 100 medicinal springs producing 70 million liters of thermal water daily. The climate is transitional between humid temperate and humid continental, with warm summers and chilly winters.

### Architecture and Sights
The city's architecture spans from Roman ruins to contemporary buildings, with a low-rise skyline protected by legislation. Key styles include Gothic (Matthias Church), Renaissance (influenced by King Matthias), Ottoman (Rudas Baths), Baroque, Neoclassical (Chain Bridge), and Art Nouveau. Major landmarks include the neo-Gothic Parliament Building, St. Stephen's Basilica, and the Buda Castle Quarter, all part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Budapest is also known as the "spa capital of Europe," with historic baths like Széchenyi and Gellért.

### Economy and Infrastructure
Budapest is a significant economic hub, generating over 40% of Hungary's GDP. It is a center for finance, banking, tourism, and innovation, hosting many international organizations. The city has a major international airport and an extensive public transport system, including the historic M1 metro line (1896). Trams 4 and 6 are among the busiest in the world. The Danube remains an important waterway.

### Culture and Demographics
The city is a major cultural center with over 200 museums, numerous theaters, and a vibrant culinary scene blending Hungarian and international influences. It is home to a large Jewish community and the second-largest synagogue in the world. The population is predominantly Hungarian, with a growing number of foreign residents. Budapest attracts millions of international visitors annually, drawn to its history, spas, and events like the Sziget Festival.

## Terms
- **Primate city**: A city that is disproportionately larger and more influential than others in its country, serving as its economic, cultural, and political center.
- **Pannonian Basin**: A large basin in Central Europe, historically a crossroads of cultures and trade, where Budapest is centrally located.
- **Aquincum**: The Roman town that was the capital of Pannonia Inferior, whose ruins are now part of Budapest's Óbuda district.
- **Spolia**: The reuse of earlier building materials or architectural elements in new construction, seen in Budapest's Inner City Parish Church.
- **Panelház**: Prefabricated concrete apartment blocks built during the communist era, common in Eastern Europe.
- **Ruin bars**: Bars and clubs established in the ruins of abandoned buildings or vacant lots in Budapest's old Jewish quarter, known for their eclectic decor.
- **BKK (Budapesti Közlekedési Központ)**: The Centre for Budapest Transport, the unified public transport authority for the city.
- **UNESCO World Heritage Site**: A landmark or area with legal protection by an international convention administered by UNESCO, recognizing its cultural or natural significance.

## Debates and Open Questions
The etymologies of "Buda" and "Pest" remain obscure and debated, with multiple theories involving Turkic, Slavic, and legendary origins. The city's architectural future presents a debate between preserving its historic low-rise cityscape and the pressure to develop high-rise buildings. The political landscape shows tension between the nationalist government in Budapest and the opposition-led city administration.
