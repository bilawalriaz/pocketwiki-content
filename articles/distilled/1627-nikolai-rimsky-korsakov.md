# Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov

## Overview
Nikolai Andreyevich Rimsky-Korsakov (1844–1908) was a Russian composer and a central member of The Five, a group dedicated to forging a distinct nationalistic style of classical music. Initially an autodidact and naval officer, he became Professor of Composition and Orchestration at the Saint Petersburg Conservatory in 1871, undertaking a rigorous three-year program of self-education in Western theory to remedy his technical gaps. He mastered and codified orchestration, producing a seminal textbook completed posthumously by his son-in-law Maximilian Steinberg. His best-known works—*Scheherazade*, *Capriccio Espagnol*, and the *Russian Easter Festival Overture*—exemplify his use of musical orientalism, folk song, and brilliant instrumental color. As a teacher of over 250 students, including Igor Stravinsky, Sergei Prokofiev, and Ottorino Respighi, he bridged the gap between the self-taught nationalism of Glinka and The Five and the professionally trained composers who became the Russian norm. His editing of works by Mussorgsky and Borodin brought them into the repertoire but remains controversial for altering the original composers' intentions.

## Body

### Early Life and Naval Career
Rimsky-Korsakov was born in Tikhvin, 200 km east of Saint Petersburg, into a noble family with a history of military service. His older brother Voin, a navigator 22 years his senior, inspired a poetic love of the sea through tales of his exploits. Though he showed aural talent, the young Nikolai was indifferent to piano lessons, preferring literature. At age 12, prompted by Voin, he entered the School for Mathematical and Navigational Sciences. While a cadet, he studied piano with Feodor Kanille, who introduced him to the music of Mikhail Glinka and Robert Schumann and encouraged his compositional ambitions. In November 1861, Kanille introduced the 18-year-old to Mily Balakirev, César Cui, and Modest Mussorgsky—the core of The Five. Balakirev became his mentor, guiding the composition of his First Symphony. Rimsky-Korsakov completed and orchestrated three movements before embarking on a 32-month cruise aboard the *Almaz* (1862–1865). At sea, he studied Berlioz’s *Treatise on Instrumentation* and acquired scores at every port, but the isolation eventually dulled his musical drive, and he nearly abandoned composition for a naval life.

### Mentorship, The Five, and Early Orchestral Works
Returning to Saint Petersburg in May 1865, Rimsky-Korsakov reconnected with Balakirev, who insisted he finish the First Symphony. The premiere in December 1865 established his reputation. Balakirev’s mentorship was intense and controlling; he frequently recomposed students' works at the piano, demanding frequent recasting. Under this guidance, Rimsky-Korsakov produced *Overture on Three Russian Themes*, *Fantasia on Serbian Themes*, and the initial versions of *Sadko* and *Antar*, which cemented his fame as an orchestrator. He socialized closely with The Five, particularly Alexander Borodin and Mussorgsky (with whom he shared an apartment in 1871–72). The group’s aesthetic—championed by critic Vladimir Stasov—valued Glinka, Schumann, and Berlioz, while dismissing Mendelssohn, Mozart, Haydn, and Bach as outdated or mechanical. Rimsky-Korsakov absorbed these judgments uncritically. His orchestration skills were in high demand: Balakirev, Cui, and Alexander Dargomyzhsky all asked him to orchestrate their works.

### Professorship, Marriage, and Academic Transformation
In 1871, director Mikhaíl Azanchevsky appointed the 27-year-old Rimsky-Korsakov Professor of Composition and Orchestration at the Conservatory, despite his lack of formal training (he could not harmonize a chorale or name chords). Advised by Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky to study, Rimsky-Korsakov took a three-year sabbatical from original composition, teaching himself counterpoint, fugue, and harmony from textbooks while staying ahead of his students. He emerged a fervent convert to academic training, revising all his pre-1874 works—including *Sadko* and *Antar*—in pursuit of technical perfection. He also mastered conducting through the Orchestra Class. In 1872, he married Nadezhda Purgold, a Conservatory-trained pianist who became his closest musical critic and collaborator, arranging his works for piano four-hands. In 1873, he became Inspector of Naval Bands, a civilian post allowing him to resign his commission; he used the role to study wind and brass instruments intensively, informing his orchestration textbook.

### Creative Crisis and Folk Song Studies
Rimsky-Korsakov’s new academic rigor provoked a backlash from The Five, who viewed his Third Symphony and String Quartet as dryly pedantic "German" exercises. Tchaikovsky privately feared he might become bogged down in "contrapuntal tricks." Two projects restored his creative voice: compiling two folk song collections (1874, 1876) from singer Tvorty Filippov and other sources, and editing Glinka’s orchestral scores with Balakirev and Anatoly Lyadov. The folk collections provided a vast reservoir of authentic material and models for "fakeloric" passages. Editing Glinka forced a note-by-note engagement with his idol’s instrumentation, which Rimsky-Korsakov called a "beneficent discipline" leading him back to modern music. Encouraged by Nadezhda, he composed *May Night* (1878–79) and *The Snow Maiden* (1880–81), writing in a transparent, folk-like idiom inspired by Glinka and "casting off the shackles of counterpoint."

### The Belyayev Circle and Programmatic Masterpieces
In 1882, Rimsky-Korsakov met timber magnate Mitrofan Belyayev, a Slavophile patron who founded the "Belyayev circle." Unlike The Five, these composers (including Glazunov and Lyadov) combined nationalism with Western academic training. Belyayev funded the Russian Symphony Concerts (1886–87), which Rimsky-Korsakov co-conducted. For these concerts, he wrote his three most famous orchestral works: *Scheherazade*, *Capriccio Espagnol*, and the *Russian Easter Festival Overture*. These pieces replaced contrapuntal density with virtuoso figuration and luxuriant orchestration, employing a "mosaic" technique of juxtaposed instrumental colors—primary hues augmented by sophisticated effects, some learned from Wagner’s *Ring* (heard in Saint Petersburg in 1889). The *Ring* impressed Rimsky-Korsakov deeply, turning his focus almost exclusively to opera for the remainder of his career.

### Conservatism, Editing The Five, and Late Operas
From 1889 onward, Rimsky-Korsakov’s "musical conscience" grew increasingly conservative. He rejected the innovations of Richard Strauss and Claude Debussy (dismissing *Estampes* as having "no technique, even less imagination") and eventually turned critical of Wagner and his own former compatriots. He revised Mussorgsky’s *Boris Godunov* (1895) and completed Borodin’s *Prince Igor* (with Glazunov), but his edits—removing perceived harmonic "errors" and structural flaws—later drew accusations of pedantry and distortion of the originals' originality. A second creative drought (1892), caused by neurasthenia and family tragedies, ended with Tchaikovsky’s death in 1893. Rimsky-Korsakov then composed roughly one opera every 18 months until 1908 (11 total), including *Christmas Eve*, *The Tale of Tsar Saltan* (source of "Flight of the Bumblebee"), *The Legend of the Invisible City of Kitezh*, and *The Golden Cockerel*. He viewed operas as primarily musical rather than dramatic works, achieving dramatic success through deliberate non-theatricality.

### 1905 Revolution and Final Years
A lifelong liberal, Rimsky-Korsakov supported student demonstrators at the Conservatory during the 1905 Revolution. He wrote open letters condemning administrative repression, leading to his dismissal and the expulsion of ~100 students. Faculty (including Glazunov and Lyadov) and over 300 students resigned or walked out in protest; he was reinstated by December under new director Glazunov. He retired in 1906. His final opera, *The Golden Cockerel*, a satire on monarchy and the Russo-Japanese War, was banned by censors and premiered only in 1909, after his death. In 1907, he conducted Diaghilev’s "Historical Russian Concerts" in Paris, a triumph for Russian music in Europe. There, he hissed at Strauss’s *Salome* and recoiled from Debussy’s *Pelléas et Mélisande*, acknowledging he was a "convinced kuchkist" whose era had passed. Angina, worsened by the 1905 stresses, killed him in 1908.

### Compositional Style and Harmonic Language
Rimsky-Korsakov openly acknowledged his debts: whole-tone scales and orientalism from Balakirev; harmonic adventurousness from Liszt; structural models from Glinka. He developed whole-tone and octatonic scales for "fantastic" (supernatural) operatic passages, tempering radical harmonies with strict self-imposed rules ("musical conscience"). This made him both progressive (using scales considered adventurous in the West) and conservative (controlling their deployment), a stance later contrasted with the radical freedom of his student Stravinsky. His orchestration evolved from the sparse textures of *Sadko*/*Antar* to the brilliant mosaics of the 1880s works, where themes are assigned to pure instrumental groups (e.g., trombones in unison) accompanied by intricate, interlocking patterns in strings, woodwinds, and percussion.

### Operas: Categories and Aesthetics
His 15 operas fall into three groups: **Historical dramas** (*The Maid of Pskov*, *Mozart and Salieri*, *The Tsar's Bride*); **Folk operas** (*May Night*, *Christmas Eve*); and **Fairy tales/legends** (*The Snow Maiden*, *Sadko*, *Kashchey the Deathless*, *The Tale of Tsar Saltan*, *The Golden Cockerel*). He sought novelty in each work. Critics note a tension: his music is "exquisite" but can turn "cold and pellucid," lacking dramatic heat—a flaw he may have cultivated, insisting operas were musical works first. Yet they succeed dramatically through musical brilliance, evoking a world of "Russian East, supernaturalism, exoticism, and Slavic pantheism."

### Legacy: The Transitional Figure
Rimsky-Korsakov is the pivotal link between the autodidact nationalism of Glinka/The Five and the professionalized Russian conservatory system. Vladimir Stasov claimed Russian musicians disdained "book learning," but Rimsky-Korsakov had taught it for a decade by 1882. He privately disparaged his former colleagues' technical deficiencies (Balakirev: "insufficient technique"; Borodin: "difficulty"; Mussorgsky: "sloppily and often incoherently"), acknowledging his luck in "bethinking himself in time." By the 1890s, conservatory training was universal for Russian composers; the faculties were dominated by his students and the "New Russian School." He taught ~250 students at the Conservatory (plus private pupils like Stravinsky), including Glazunov, Lyadov, Prokofiev, Respighi, and Mykola Lysenko. His pedagogy was systematic: harmony/counterpoint → form → technique/orchestration → piano proficiency, after which "studies would be over."

### Folklore, Pantheism, and Ritual
Moving beyond The Five’s reliance on the *protyazhnaya* (drawn-out melismatic lyric song), Rimsky-Korsakov turned to "calendar songs" tied to specific pagan rituals. His folk song collections deepened his fascination with sun-worship and Slavic pantheism, influenced by Alexander Afanasyev’s *The Poetic Outlook on Nature by the Slavs*. He integrated seasonal calendar songs and *khorovodi* (ceremonial dances) into *May Night* and *The Snow Maiden*. Musicologists recognize his folklore operas as ecumenical explorations of the tension between paganism and Christianity and the 17th-century Old Believer schism.

## Terms
- ****The Five (Moguchaya kuchka)**** — A circle of Russian nationalist composers (Balakirev, Cui, Mussorgsky, Borodin, Rimsky-Korsakov) active 1860s–1870s, advocating a distinctly Russian style based on folk song and orientalism, opposing Western academicism.
- ****Musical Orientalism**** — The use of exotic harmonic, melodic, and rhythmic elements (e.g., whole-tone, octatonic scales, augmented seconds) to evoke the "East" or the supernatural, a hallmark of The Five’s style.
- ****Protyazhnaya**** — A "drawn-out" Russian folk song type characterized by extreme rhythmic flexibility, asymmetrical phrases, and tonal ambiguity; the primary folk model for The Five before Rimsky-Korsakov turned to calendar songs.
- ****Calendar Songs**** — Folk songs tied to specific ritual occasions in the agricultural/religious year (e.g., spring rituals, harvest); Rimsky-Korsakov used these extensively in *The Snow Maiden* and *May Night* for their pantheistic connections.
- ****Khorovodi**** — Ceremonial round dances performed during folk rituals; incorporated by Rimsky-Korsakov into operas like *The Snow Maiden* to depict communal pagan rites.
- ****Belyayev Circle**** — A later generation of nationalist composers (Glazunov, Lyadov, etc.) patronized by Mitrofan Belyayev; unlike The Five, they embraced Western conservatory training alongside Russian folk idioms.
- ****Musical Conscience**** — Rimsky-Korsakov’s term for his self-imposed critical faculty, which enforced strict rules on harmonic experimentation (e.g., controlling octatonic/whole-tone usage) and drove his revisions of his own and others' works.
- ****Fakelore**** — A term used in the source to describe passages composed by Rimsky-Korsakov in imitation of authentic folk style, modeled on the material he collected in his folk song anthologies.
- ****Inspector of Naval Bands**** — A civilian post (1873–1884) allowing Rimsky-Korsakov to study wind/brass instruments and band orchestration practically, directly feeding his textbook *Principles of Orchestration*.
- ****Neurasthenia**** — A late-19th-century medical diagnosis (symptoms: rushes of blood to head, confusion, memory loss, obsessions) attributed to Rimsky-Korsakov during his 1892 creative drought.

## Debates and open questions
- **Editing of Mussorgsky:** Rimsky-Korsakov revised Mussorgsky’s works (e.g., *Boris Godunov*, *Night on Bald Mountain*) to correct perceived harmonic errors and structural flaws. While this popularized Mussorgsky, modern scholars (citing Pavel Lamm’s manuscript analysis) argue he distorted the originals' radical originality; Mussorgsky’s unedited versions now often replace Rimsky-Korsakov’s editions in performance.
- **Completion of Borodin’s *Prince Igor*:** Rimsky-Korsakov and Glazunov discarded ~20% of Borodin’s manuscripts. The result is debated as a three-way collaboration rather than a faithful realization of Borodin’s intent; the chaotic state of the manuscripts makes a definitive urtext extremely difficult.
- **Conservatism vs. Innovation:** Rimsky-Korsakov pioneered octatonic/whole-tone techniques later central to Stravinsky and Debussy, yet he personally rejected their music as lacking technique. Scholars debate whether his "musical conscience" was a necessary discipline or a limitation that prevented him from fully realizing the implications of his own harmonic discoveries.
- **Autodidact vs. Academic:** The Five’s ideology rejected conservatory training, yet Rimsky-Korsakov became its most effective exponent. The irony—highlighted by Richard Taruskin—is that by professionalizing Russian composition, he effectively ended the era of the autodidact nationalist composer that The Five embodied.
