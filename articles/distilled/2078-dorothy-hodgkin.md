# Dorothy Hodgkin

## Overview
Dorothy Hodgkin (1910–1994) was a pioneering English chemist who advanced X-ray crystallography to determine the structures of complex biomolecules, including penicillin, vitamin B12, and insulin. Her work was foundational for structural biology and earned her the 1964 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, making her the only British woman scientist to win a Nobel in the sciences.

- **1928** — Entered Somerville College, Oxford, to study chemistry.
- **1932** — Graduated with first-class honours; began PhD at Cambridge under J.D. Bernal.
- **1934** — Began experiencing rheumatoid arthritis; returned to Oxford.
- **1937** — Married Thomas Lionel Hodgkin; awarded PhD.
- **1945** — Published first steroid structure (cholesteryl iodide) and solved penicillin structure.
- **1947** — Elected Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS).

- **1953** — Banned from entering the US; one of the first to see the DNA double helix model.
- **1956** — Appointed Royal Society Wolfson Research Professor.
- **1964** — Awarded Nobel Prize in Chemistry for work on vitamin B12.
- **1969** — Elucidated the structure of insulin.
- **1976** — Became President of the Pugwash Conference.
- **29 July 1994** — Died in Ilmington, Warwickshire.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born to archaeologists in Cairo, Hodgkin spent much of her childhood in England, separated from her parents. Her mother fostered her interest in science, giving her a book on X-ray crystallography at age 16. After grammar school, she entered Somerville College, Oxford, in 1928. She then completed a PhD at Cambridge under John Desmond Bernal, where she applied X-ray crystallography to biological substances like pepsin.

### Career and Key Discoveries
Hodgkin returned to Oxford in 1934, eventually becoming a fellow and tutor in chemistry. Her career was defined by solving the structures of complex biomolecules. In 1945, she determined the structure of penicillin, confirming the presence of a β-lactam ring. She then tackled vitamin B12, a large molecule containing cobalt; its structure was published in 1955–56. Her most prolonged project was insulin, which she began studying in 1934 but did not solve until 1969, after 35 years of work, paving the way for its mass production.

### Personal Life and Politics
Hodgkin was diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis in 1934, which progressively disabled her hands and feet, but she remained scientifically active. She married historian Thomas Hodgkin in 1937, and they had three children. Due to her and her husband's leftist political associations, she was banned from the US in 1953. She was a lifelong peace activist, serving as president of the Pugwash Conference from 1976 to 1988 and accepting the Lenin Peace Prize in 1987. She maintained international scientific contacts, notably with researchers in China and the USSR.

### Legacy and Honours
Hodgkin received numerous honours, including the Order of Merit (1965) and the Copley Medal (1976). She was Chancellor of the University of Bristol from 1970 to 1988. Her legacy includes buildings named after her, the Dorothy Hodgkin Fellowship for early-career scientists, and commemorative stamps. She died in 1994 after a stroke.

## Terms
- **X-ray crystallography**: A technique using X-ray diffraction patterns to determine the atomic and molecular structure of a crystal.
- **β-lactam ring**: A four-membered lactam (cyclic amide) ring structure, a key component of the penicillin molecule.
- **Pleochroic**: A property of some crystals where they appear to be different colors when viewed from different angles under polarized light.
- **Rheumatoid arthritis**: A chronic autoimmune disorder causing inflammation and deformity in joints.
- **Pugwash Conference**: An international organization of scientists and public figures working to reduce the danger of armed conflict and the threat of nuclear war.
- **Order of Merit**: A prestigious award from the British monarch for exceptional achievement in the arts, sciences, or public services.
- **Copley Medal**: A scientific award given by the Royal Society of London for outstanding research in any branch of science.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the pepsin experiment, the first application of X-ray crystallography to a biological substance, is "largely credited to Hodgkin," though she always maintained that her mentor J.D. Bernal took the initial photographs and provided key insights. Her political activities, including writing a foreword for a book fraudulently attributed to Elena Ceaușescu, highlight the complex intersection of science and politics during the Cold War.
