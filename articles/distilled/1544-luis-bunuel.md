# Luis Buñuel

## Overview
Luis Buñuel Portolés (1900–1983) was a Spanish-Mexican filmmaker widely regarded as one of cinema’s most influential figures. His career, spanning the 1920s to the 1970s across France, Mexico, and Spain, fused avant-garde **surrealism**—a movement seeking to unlock the unconscious through irrational juxtaposition—with sharp political and anti-clerical satire. After shocking Paris with the Dalí collaborations *Un Chien Andalou* (1929) and *L'Age d'Or* (1930), he spent the 1940s and 50s in Mexico mastering commercial filmmaking while embedding subversive themes in films like *Los Olvidados* (1950). His return to international art cinema yielded masterpieces such as *Viridiana* (1961), *The Exterminating Angel* (1962), *Belle de Jour* (1967), and *The Discreet Charm of the Bourgeoisie* (1972), which dismantled bourgeois rituals and religious hypocrisy with a rigorous, economical visual style.

## Timeline
- **22 Feb 1900** — Born in Calanda, Aragon, Spain.
- **1917–1925** — Studies at University of Madrid; befriends Salvador Dalí and Federico García Lorca at Residencia de Estudiantes; forms core of "Generación del 27".
- **1925–1929** — Moves to Paris; assists Jean Epstein; works as film critic; writes *Un Chien Andalou* with Dalí in six days.
- **1929** — *Un Chien Andalou* premieres; Buñuel and Dalí admitted to André Breton’s Surrealist group.
- **1930** — *L'Age d'Or* premieres; banned by Paris police and Vatican pressure; Buñuel breaks with Dalí.
- **1931–1933** — Joins Communist Party of Spain (later denied); directs pseudo-documentary *Las Hurdes: Tierra Sin Pan* (1933), banned by Republic and Franco.
- **1934–1936** — Works anonymously as producer/director for Filmófono in Madrid; makes four commercial films.
- **1936–1939** — Spanish Civil War: coordinates Republican film propaganda in Paris/Geneva; Lorca executed (1936).
- **1938–1946** — Hollywood and New York: dubbing for Warner Bros.; edits anti-fascist films at MoMA; resigns after Dalí’s autobiography brands him "Antichrist".
- **1946–1960** — Mexican period: 21 films. *Gran Casino* (1947) fails; *El Gran Calavera* (1949) hits; *Los Olvidados* (1950) wins Best Director at Cannes 1951.
- **1954–1960** — International co-productions: *Robinson Crusoe* (1954), "revolutionary triptych" (*Cela s'appelle l'aurore*, *La Mort en ce jardin*, *La Fièvre Monte à El Pao*), *The Young One* (1960).
- **1961** — *Viridiana* wins Palme d'Or at Cannes; banned in Spain for 17 years; Vatican denounces it.
- **1962–1965** — *The Exterminating Angel* (1962); *Diary of a Chambermaid* (1964, first Carrière/Silberman collaboration); *Simón del desierto* (1965).
- **1967** — *Belle de Jour* becomes biggest commercial hit.
- **1969–1974** — "Heresy" and surrealist late works: *The Milky Way* (1969), *Tristana* (1970), *Discreet Charm of the Bourgeoisie* (1972, Oscar winner), *The Phantom of Liberty* (1974).
- **1977** — Final film, *That Obscure Object of Desire*, uses two actresses for one role; wins National Society of Film Critics Award.
- **29 Jul 1983** — Dies in Mexico City from diabetes complications.

## Body

### Early Formation and Surrealist Breakthrough (1900–1930)
Buñuel’s upbringing in Calanda—a town he claimed remained medieval until WWI—and a strict Jesuit education forged a lifelong tension between religious ritual and rational revolt. At the Residencia de Estudiantes in Madrid, he formed the nucleus of the **Generación del 27** with Dalí and Lorca. A screening of Fritz Lang’s *Der müde Tod* (1921) convinced him cinema was the "true means of expression." In Paris, after a contentious apprenticeship with impressionist director Jean Epstein, Buñuel and Dalí created *Un Chien Andalou* (1929). Financed by Buñuel’s mother, the 16-minute short deployed **Freudian** dream logic—most famously a razor-sliced eyeball—under a single rule: "no idea or image that might lend itself to a rational explanation... would be accepted." Intended to outrage the artistic vanguard, it instead became a sensation, securing their entry into André Breton’s Surrealist circle.

Their follow-up, *L'Age d'Or* (1930), commissioned by the aristocratic de Noailles, fractured the partnership. Buñuel, radicalizing leftward, sought to undermine bourgeois institutions; Dalí, drifting toward fascism and aristocracy, wanted mere scandal. The film’s blasphemous finale (linking Christ to the Marquis de Sade) incited fascist riots, a police ban, and the de Noailles’ withdrawal under Vatican threat. The film vanished until 1981. Buñuel’s brief Hollywood stint (MGM, 1930) ended in mutual disdain; he refused to "waste time listening to one of the whores" (Lili Damita) and returned to Spain.

### Political Engagement and Ethnographic Experiment (1931–1939)
The Second Republic’s anti-clerical turbulence drew Buñuel into politics; he joined the Communist Party in 1931. Declining an ethnographic mission to Africa, he instead read Maurice Legendre’s study of Las Hurdes, Spain’s poorest region, and directed *Las Hurdes: Tierra Sin Pan* (1933). Funded by anarchist lottery-winner Ramón Acín, the film juxtaposed horrific poverty with a detached travelogue narration and incongruous Brahms, creating what critic Mercè Ibarz termed a **"surrealist documentary"**—a subversive propaganda tool that awakened "marxist materialism" from orthodoxy (Catherine Russell). Banned by both Republic and Franco, it remains a categorization puzzle.

Buñuel then spent 1934–36 at Filmófono in Madrid, directing four popular musicals/comedies anonymously to protect his surrealist reputation, though historian Manuel Rotellar confirms Buñuel controlled all creative decisions. During the Civil War (1936–39), he coordinated Republican film propaganda in Paris, cataloging footage, smuggling tracts, and supervising the documentary *España 1936*. Lorca’s 1936 murder haunted him for life. With Franco’s victory, Buñuel fled to the US via Hollywood (1938).

### American Exile and MoMA Propaganda (1938–1946)
Hollywood rejected Buñuel’s "normal, solid" demeanor; he survived on dubbing and gag-writing (Chaplin’s *The Great Dictator*). In New York, MoMA curator Iris Barry hired him for the **Office of the Coordinator of Inter-American Affairs (OCIAA)**, editing captured Nazi films (e.g., *Triumph of the Will*) into anti-fascist shorts for Latin America. His friend Gustavo Pittaluga noted Buñuel "created maybe 2,000 remarkable works" from "anodyne documentaries." Dalí’s 1942 memoir *The Secret Life of Salvador Dalí* denounced Buñuel as a Communist atheist, prompting Archbishop Spellman to demand MoMA expel "the Antichrist." Budget cuts forced Buñuel’s resignation. He returned to Hollywood for Warner Bros. dubbing (1944–45), confronting Dalí but not shooting him, and abandoned a lifelong *Under the Volcano* adaptation.

### Mexican Apprenticeship and Mastery (1946–1960)
Producer Óscar Dancigers lured Buñuel to Mexico during its **Golden Age** (3rd largest industry, 1,500 theaters). After the flop *Gran Casino* (1947), Buñuel honed a rapid, low-cost technique (125 shots, 16 days) on *El Gran Calavera* (1949), a hit. This bought him "a degree of freedom" for *Los Olvidados* (1950). Rejecting melodramatic ennoblement, Buñuel portrayed street children as "ruthless predators" in a brutal milieu. The premiere provoked walkouts, egg-throwing at the screen, and calls for citizenship revocation. Dancigers added a "happy ending" and a universalizing prologue; the film was pulled in three days.

Octavio Paz championed *Los Olvidados* at Cannes 1951, securing Best Director and FIPRESCI prizes. UNESCO later called it "the most important document in Spanish about the marginal lives of children." Buñuel made 21 Mexican films, often generic *churros* imposed by the industry, yet critics detect a sustained meditation on **sexual pathology** (*Él*, *Ensayo de un crimen*), **machismo** (*El Bruto*), **fantasy/reality blurring** (*Subida al cielo*), **women’s disruptive status** (*Susana*), and **religious absurdity** (*Nazarín*, *Simón del desierto*). Many passion projects (Rulfo’s *Pedro Páramo*, Golding’s *Lord of the Flies*) remained unfilmed.

### International Co-productions and the "Revolutionary Triptych" (1954–1960)
Buñuel seized chances to work abroad. *Adventures of Robinson Crusoe* (1954) was his first color film, shot bilingually in Manzanillo. In France, he made a "revolutionary triptych" (Raymond Durgnat): *Cela s'appelle l'aurore* (1956), *La Mort en ce jardin* (1956), and *La Fièvre Monte à El Pao* (1959)—each studying armed revolt against right-wing dictatorship. The last starred dying Gérard Philipe; both men admitted they didn't know why they were making it, Buñuel confessing he "took everything... as long as it wasn't humiliating." *The Young One* (1960), a US/Mexico co-production on racism and desire in the Deep South, won a Cannes special award but faced vicious US hostility.

### Spanish Return: Scandal and Mastery (1961–1965)
Carlos Saura’s UNINCI group and actress Silvia Pinal (backed by husband Gustavo Alatriste) offered Buñuel "absolute freedom" in Spain. *Viridiana* (1961), a lurid tale of rape, incest, and sacrilege, passed the censor with minor changes—until Buñuel substituted an "even more immoral" final scene. Smuggled negatives reached Paris for editing. It shared the **Palme d'Or** at Cannes 1961. The Vatican denounced it; Spain banned it for 17 years; production companies UNINCI and Film 59 were dissolved.

Buñuel followed with *The Exterminating Angel* (1962), where dinner guests inexplicably cannot leave—a bourgeois trap mirroring *Discreet Charm*’s later inability to eat. In France, producer Serge Silberman (Holocaust survivor) initiated the **Buñuel-Carrière-Silberman** partnership. *Diary of a Chambermaid* (1964) cast Jeanne Moreau over Pinal, ending Pinal’s marriage per her later claim. Carrière, initially deferential, was ordered by Silberman to "stand up to Buñuel"; he became the essential counter-voice for 15 years. *Simón del desierto* (1965) was Buñuel’s last Mexican feature.

### Late Surrealist Satires (1966–1977)
*Belle de Jour* (1967), adapting Joseph Kessel, became Buñuel’s biggest hit. He researched Madrid brothels but clashed with imposed star Catherine Deneuve. *The Milky Way* (1969), inspired by Godard’s *La Chinoise*, sent two pilgrims through time to enact Catholic heresies; banned in Italy, saved by Church intervention. *Tristana* (1970), from Galdós’s "weakest" novel, faced 8 years of censorship delays; Deneuve and Buñuel reconciled.

*The Discreet Charm of the Bourgeoisie* (1972) originated from Silberman’s forgotten dinner party. Affluent friends repeatedly fail to eat; Buñuel used video playback for complex crane/tracking shots, editing in-camera in one day. It won the **Oscar for Best Foreign Language Film**, prompting Buñuel’s contempt for the "2500 idiots" voting. *The Phantom of Liberty* (1974), a 12-episode relay-race narrative, was his "most Surrealist film," a tribute to Benjamin Péret. *That Obscure Object of Desire* (1977), from Pierre Louÿs, cast two actresses (Ángela Molina, Carole Bouquet) as the same woman after Maria Schneider’s dismissal—Buñuel credited two dry martinis; Carrière claimed prior authorship. It was his final film.

### Personal Life, Style, and Legacy
Buñuel married gymnastics teacher Jeanne Rucar (1934), defying Epstein’s warning; they had two sons. A lifelong **hypnotist**, he equated cinema with hypnosis: darkness and rapid cuts "weaken the spectator's critical intelligence." His directing was **extremely economical**—few weeks, minimal deviation from script, almost no wasted footage—drawing comparisons to Hitchcock’s pre-planned precision. Ingmar Bergman wrote, "Buñuel nearly always made Buñuel films"; John Huston called them instantly recognizable. Octavio Paz defined his work as "the marriage of the film image to the poetic image, creating a new reality... scandalous and subversive."

Honors: Career Golden Lion (Venice 1982), Moscow Honorable Prize (1979), National Prize for Arts (1977), two Nobel Literature nominations (1968, 1972). Seven films in *Sight & Sound*’s 2012 top 250; 15 in *They Shoot Pictures, Don't They?* top 1,000 (2nd only to Godard). Influenced Woody Allen, Gaspar Noé, Yorgos Lanthimos, Wes Anderson, Jonathan Glazer. Retrospectives at Bonn (1994), Madrid (1996), Cannes centenary (2000). Salle Buñuel at Palais des Festivals; Centro Buñuel Calanda museum; schools in Zaragoza and Neuilly-sur-Seine. *NYT* obituary: "iconoclast, moralist, and revolutionary... leader of avant-garde surrealism in his youth and a dominant international movie director half a century later."

## Terms
- ****Surrealism**** — An avant-garde movement (led by André Breton) seeking to resolve dream and reality into a "super-reality" via irrational juxtaposition and automatic techniques.
- ****Generación del 27**** — Influential group of Spanish poets/artists (including Lorca, Dalí, Buñuel) centered at Madrid’s Residencia de Estudiantes c. 1920s.
- ****Freudian**** — Relating to Sigmund Freud’s psychoanalysis; *Un Chien Andalou* uses dream logic, displacement, and sexual symbolism derived from Freud.
- ****Surrealist documentary**** — Term (Mercè Ibarz) for *Las Hurdes*: layered sound, juxtaposed narrative forms (travelogue, pedagogy), subversive use of filmed documents as propaganda.
- ****Churros**** — Mexican industry slang for cheap, hastily made genre films; Buñuel often assigned these but infused them with subversive themes.
- ****Revolutionary triptych**** — Raymond Durgnat’s term for three 1950s co-productions (*Cela s'appelle l'aurore*, *La Mort en ce jardin*, *La Fièvre Monte à El Pao*) studying armed revolt vs. right-wing dictatorship.
- ****Palme d'Or**** — Top prize at Cannes Film Festival; *Viridiana* (1961) shared it with *Une aussi longue absence*.
- ****Auteur**** — A filmmaker whose personal creative vision dominates a film; Bergman/Huston cited Buñuel as a definitive example.
- ****Video playback monitor**** — Device allowing director to review footage instantly; Buñuel first used it on *Discreet Charm* (1972) for complex crane/tracking shots and in-camera editing.
- ****Relay-race narrative**** — Structure of *The Phantom of Liberty* (1974): 12 episodes linked by passing a character from one segment to the next.

## Debates and open questions
- **Spanish citizenship**: Sources dispute whether Buñuel renounced Spanish citizenship in 1949 to become Mexican, or retained both privately.
- ***The Beast with Five Fingers* (1946)**: Buñuel claimed in *My Last Sigh* he contributed a disembodied-hand scene uncredited; Library of Congress scholar Brian Taves challenges this.
- ***Tristana* (1970) interpretation**: Scholar Beth Miller argues critics consistently miss its "socialist-feminist statement," rendering it the most underrated Buñuel film.
- **Dual casting in *That Obscure Object of Desire* (1977)**: Buñuel credited a martini-inspired epiphany; Carrière claimed he proposed it earlier during scenario development.
- **Dalí’s role in *L'Age d'Or***: Buñuel and Dalí fell out over politics (Dalí’s fascist leanings) and personal jealousy (Gala); Dalí had no hand in shooting but later claimed the film as a deliberate anti-Catholic attack.
- **MoMA departure (1942)**: Dalí’s book triggered Archbishop Spellman’s "Antichrist" accusation; simultaneous Hollywood trade-paper campaign cut MoMA film budget 66%, forcing Buñuel out—extent of coordination debated.
- **Filmófono authorship**: Rotellar insists Buñuel directed four films credited to José Luis Sáenz de Heredia; titular director confirmed Buñuel chose shots and controlled editing.
- **Catholic Church vs. *The Milky Way* (1969)**: Banned in Italy; Church hierarchy intervened to lift ban—unusual reversal suggesting tactical or doctrinal nuance.
