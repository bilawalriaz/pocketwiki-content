# Diego Rivera

## Overview
Diego Rivera (1886–1957) was a Mexican painter whose monumental frescoes defined the Mexican Mural Movement and projected the country’s post-revolutionary identity onto the international stage. Sponsored by the post-revolutionary government of Álvaro Obregón, Rivera adapted Italian Renaissance fresco technique and pre-Columbian visual language to create public art narrating Mexican history, class struggle, and industrial progress. His Marxist politics fueled both his subject matter and his most famous controversies, notably the 1933 destruction of *Man at the Crossroads* at Rockefeller Center for its portrait of Lenin. Despite expulsion from the Mexican Communist Party and friction with Stalinist factions, Rivera remained a committed leftist, later joining the esoteric order AMORC. His volatile marriage to Frida Kahlo—twice married, twice divorced—became a defining biographical axis. By his death, the Mexican state had declared his works *monumentos históricos*; his 1931 painting *The Rivals* held the auction record for a Latin American work ($9.76 million, 2018) until 2021.

## Body

### Artistic Formation and European Avant-Garde
Rivera began drawing at three and entered the Academy of San Carlos at ten. Governor Teodoro A. Dehesa Méndez sponsored his European study from 1907. In Madrid he trained with Eduardo Chicharro; in Paris he settled in Montparnasse, frequenting La Ruche alongside Amedeo Modigliani (who painted his portrait in 1914), Ilya Ehrenburg, Chaïm Soutine, Max Jacob, and Moise Kisling. From 1913 to 1917 Rivera adopted **Cubism**—the fragmented, multi-perspective style pioneered by Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque—before pivoting around 1917 to a **Post-Impressionist** idiom inspired by Paul Cézanne, employing simple forms and vivid color patches. This period established his technical fluency but left him searching for a public, socially engaged art.

### The Mexican Mural Program and Signature Style
The 1910 Revolution created a cultural opening. In 1921, new Education Minister José Vasconcelos launched a state-sponsored mural program to forge a unified national identity through public art. Rivera returned from Italy, where he had studied Renaissance frescoes, to join José Clemente Orozco, David Alfaro Siqueiros, Rufino Tamayo, and Jean Charlot. His first major mural, *Creation* (1922), used **encaustic** (pigment suspended in hot wax) at the National Preparatory School; he carried a pistol against right-wing student opposition. He soon switched exclusively to **buon fresco** (pigment on wet plaster), developing a native style: large, simplified figures, bold colors, and explicit Aztec compositional influence. The 124-fresco cycle at the Secretariat of Public Education (1922–28) codified this vocabulary. Narrative density became a hallmark: murals functioned like Maya stelae, encoding historical allegory—e.g., *En el Arsenal* juxtaposing Tina Modotti, Julio Antonio Mella, and Vittorio Vidali amid arms distribution.

### Chapingo and the Agrarian Mythos
At the National School of Agriculture (Chapingo, 1925–27), Rivera painted *Tierra Fecundada* (Fertile Land) in the chapel. The soffit joins hammer and sickle with a star; a propagandist points to another hammer-and-sickle. A woman holds an ear of corn in each hand, evoking the Aztec maize goddess per critic Antonio Rodríguez. Corpses of Emiliano Zapata and Otilio Montaño fertilize the maize field above; a central sunflower “glorifies those who died for an ideal and are reborn… into the fertile cornfield of the nation” (Rodríguez). Guadalupe Marín appears as a fertile nude goddess; their daughter Guadalupe as a cherub. The chapel fuses Marxist iconography with indigenous cosmology, rendering revolution as cyclical regeneration.

### Detroit Industry and the Machine Age
The 1932–33 *Detroit Industry Murals* (27 panels at the Detroit Institute of Arts, funded by Edsel Ford) transpose this mythic framework onto Ford’s River Rouge complex. North and south walls depict engine and transmission production; upper registers show raw material extraction; side panels portray medical technology and agriculture. The cycle synthesizes **Taylorist** industrial choreography with Rivera’s compositional grandeur, celebrating collective labor while subtly critiquing capitalist alienation. During 1950s McCarthyism, a placard in the courtyard defended the murals’ artistic merit while denouncing Rivera’s politics as “detestable.”

### Rockefeller Center, Lenin, and Censorship
In 1933 John D. Rockefeller Jr. commissioned *Man at the Crossroads* for Rockefeller Center. The three-panel fresco contrasted capitalism and socialism; a central controller figure flanked by wealthy revelers and a Lenin-led workers’ march. After press outcry and Rockefeller’s demand to replace Lenin, Rivera refused. The management company destroyed the mural (though rumors persist it was merely plastered over). Rivera was ordered to leave the U.S.; Chicago’s World’s Fair commission was cancelled. Archibald MacLeish and E. B. White published poetic responses. In 1934 Rivera recreated the work as *Man, Controller of the Universe* at Mexico City’s Palacio de Bellas Artes, asserting artistic sovereignty over patronage.

### Pan American Unity and Late Synthesis
Invited by architect Timothy L. Pflueger for the 1940 Golden Gate International Exposition, Rivera painted *Pan American Unity* (ten panels, completed Nov 29, 1940) before live audiences. The mural fuses pre-Columbian mythology, Aztec sculpture, Bay Area architecture (Pflueger’s buildings), and portraits of Kahlo, woodcarver Dudley C. Carter, and actress Paulette Goddard planting a white tree. African-American artist Thelma Johnson Streat assisted. The work embodies Rivera’s “continental” vision: technological modernity rooted in indigenous heritage, a synthesis he termed “Pan Americanism.”

### Political Trajectory: Communism, Trotsky, and AMORC
In the autumn of 1922, Rivera participated in the founding of the Revolutionary Union of Technical Workers, Painters and Sculptors, and later that year he joined the Mexican Communist Party (including its Central Committee). In the autumn of 1927, Rivera went to Moscow, Soviet Union, having accepted a government invitation to take part in the celebration of the 10th anniversary of the October Revolution. Although commissioned to paint a mural for the Red Army Club in Moscow, in 1928 Rivera was ordered by authorities to leave the country because, he suspected, of "resentment on the part of certain Soviet artists". He returned to Mexico. In 1929, after the assassination of president-elect Álvaro Obregón the previous year, the government suppressed the Mexican Communist Party. That year Rivera was expelled from the party because of his suspected **Trotskyite** sympathies (Leon Trotsky lived with Rivera and Kahlo months later). The *En el Arsenal* mural’s inclusion of Stalinist assassin Vittorio Vidali beside victim Julio Antonio Mella fueled accusations of Rivera’s foreknowledge. In 1926 Rivera became a member of **AMORC** (Ancient Mystical Order Rosae Crucis), an occult organization founded by American occultist Harvey Spencer Lewis. In 1926, Rivera was among the founders of AMORC's Mexico City lodge, called Quetzalcoatl after the pre-Columbian deity. He painted an image of Quetzalcoatl for the local temple. In 1954, seeking Communist Party readmission, he defended AMORC as “essentially materialist… based on ancient Egyptian occult knowledge from Amenhotep IV and Nefertiti,” claiming he had infiltrated a “Yankee” organization. The Party, equating AMORC with Freemasonry, rejected him.

### Personal Life: Marriages, Identity, and Belief
Rivera’s full name—Diego María de la Concepción Juan Nepomuceno Estanislao de la Rivera y Barrientos Acosta y Rodríguez—reflects Spanish naming conventions. He claimed converso (forced-convert Jewish) ancestry through his mother, writing in 1935: “My Jewishness is the dominant element in my life… sympathy with the downtrodden masses.” His heritage was Spanish, Amerindian, African, Italian, Jewish, Russian, and Portuguese. An atheist, he inscribed “God does not exist” in *Dreams of a Sunday in the Alameda* (held back nine years until he painted it out). Four wives: Angelina Beloff (1911, div. 1921), Guadalupe Marín (1922, div. 1929), Frida Kahlo (1929, div. 1939, remarried 1940), Emma Hurtado (1955). Children: son Diego (1916–18) with Beloff; daughter Marika (1918/19) with Maria Vorobieff-Stebelska; daughters Ruth and Guadalupe with Marín. His autobiography, *My Life, My Art* (1960, with Gladys March), includes a dubious claim of ritual cannibalism in 1904 (“women’s brains in vinaigrette”), widely regarded as fabrication.

## Terms
- ****Fresco (buon fresco)**** — Technique of applying water-based pigment onto freshly laid wet plaster; as plaster carbonates, pigment becomes integral with wall.
- ****Encaustic**** — Paint medium using heated beeswax mixed with pigment; used by Rivera for *Creation* (1922) before switching to fresco.
- ****Cubism**** — Early-20th-century avant-garde movement (Picasso, Braque) fragmenting objects into geometric planes showing multiple viewpoints simultaneously; Rivera practiced it 1913–1917.
- ****Post-Impressionism**** — Late-19th/early-20th-century reaction against Impressionism’s naturalism, emphasizing structure (Cézanne), symbolic color (Gauguin), and expressive form; Rivera adopted it c. 1917.
- ****Mexican Mural Movement**** — State-sponsored public-art program (from 1921) deploying monumental wall paintings to construct post-revolutionary national identity; Rivera, Orozco, Siqueiros its “Big Three.”
- ****Trotskyism**** — Marxist current following Leon Trotsky, opposing Stalin’s “socialism in one country” and bureaucratic degeneration; Rivera’s sympathy caused his 1929 Communist Party expulsion.
- ****AMORC (Ancient Mystical Order Rosae Crucis)**** — Rosicrucian esoteric order founded 1915 by Harvey Spencer Lewis; Rivera joined 1926, co-founded Mexico City lodge Quetzalcoatl, cited it in failed 1954 Party readmission bid.
- ****La Calavera Catrina**** — Zinc-etching skeleton lady by José Guadalupe Posada (c. 1910–13); Rivera elaborated her into a full-bodied nationalist icon in *Dream of a Sunday Afternoon* (1946–47).
- ****Converso**** — Jews forced to convert to Catholicism in 15th–16th-century Spain; Rivera claimed maternal converso ancestry as formative to his solidarity with the oppressed.
- ****Pan Americanism**** — Rivera’s late concept (embodied in *Pan American Unity*, 1940) fusing hemispheric indigenous heritage with industrial modernity as basis for cultural unity.

## Debates and open questions
- **Cannibalism claim**: Rivera’s autobiography asserts he ate human flesh (cadavers from the morgue) in 1904; scholars treat this as fabrication or performance, but its rhetorical function—linking taboo-breaking to revolutionary futurism—remains debated.
- **Rockefeller Center destruction**: Whether *Man at the Crossroads* was physically demolished or plastered over (and potentially recoverable) is unconfirmed; no physical trace has been found.
- **Vidali/Mella foreknowledge**: Rivera’s inclusion of assassin Vittorio Vidali and victim Julio Antonio Mella in *En el Arsenal* (1928) before Mella’s 1929 murder fuels speculation about Rivera’s complicity or prescience; no evidence resolves it.
- **AMORC vs. Communism**: Rivera’s simultaneous Rosicrucian membership and Marxist militancy—defended as infiltration, described as “materialist” occultism—exposes tensions between esoteric spirituality and dialectical materialism that historians interpret variously as syncretism, opportunism, or genuine philosophical hybridity.
- **Auction record provenance**: *The Rivals* (1931) sold for $9.76M (Christie’s, 2018) from the Rockefeller collection; its status as record-holder for Latin American art (until 2021) raises questions about market canonization versus art-historical centrality.
