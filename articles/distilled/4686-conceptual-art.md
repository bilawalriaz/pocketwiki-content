# Conceptual art

## Overview
Conceptual art is a movement where the idea or concept behind the work is considered more important than the finished aesthetic object. Emerging in the 1960s, it challenged traditional definitions of art by questioning the role of the artist, the necessity of a physical artifact, and the commodification of art in the market. Its legacy is profound, influencing much of contemporary art practice.

## Timeline
- **1917** — Marcel Duchamp submits *Fountain*, a signed urinal, pioneering the readymade and challenging art's definitions.
- **1952** — John Cage premieres *4′33″*, a composition of silence, shifting focus to the idea and context of art.
- **1961** — Henry Flynt coins the term "concept art" in a Fluxus publication.
- **1965** — Joseph Kosuth creates *One and Three Chairs*, exploring the relationship between object, image, and definition.
- **1967** — The first wave of conceptual art begins, with groups like Art & Language forming.
- **1968** — Lawrence Weiner issues his "Declaration of Intent," stating the work need not be physically built.
- **1969** — Joseph Kosuth publishes *Art after Philosophy*, a key manifesto.
- **1970** — The first dedicated conceptual art exhibition, *Conceptual Art and Conceptual Aspects*, is held in New York.
- **1990s** — The term "conceptual art" gains popular UK usage, often denoting contemporary art not using traditional painting or sculpture.
- **1999** — Tracey Emin is nominated for the Turner Prize for *My Bed*, a work of personal documentation.

## Body

### Origins and Precursors
Conceptual art has roots in early 20th-century modernism, particularly Marcel Duchamp's readymades—ordinary objects designated as art. His *Fountain* (1917) demonstrated that an artist's choice and idea could define a work, not its manual creation. In 1956, Isidore Isou proposed art that could only exist conceptually. The term "concept art" was first used by philosopher Henry Flynt in 1961, though its meaning later shifted when adopted by artists like Joseph Kosuth and the Art & Language group, who focused on critical inquiry over traditional objects.

### Core Principles and Critique
Conceptual art emerged in the 1960s partly as a reaction against formalism, the theory championed by critic Clement Greenberg that art should refine to its essential, medium-specific qualities (e.g., flatness in painting). Conceptual artists rejected this, arguing that the idea was paramount. Sol LeWitt defined it as art where "all of the planning and decisions are made beforehand and the execution is a perfunctory affair." This approach also critiqued the art market and gallery system by dematerializing the art object—often replacing it with documentation, text, or instructions—thereby challenging art as a commodity.

### Language and Method
A central concern for early conceptual artists was language. Artists like Lawrence Weiner, Joseph Kosuth, and Art & Language used text as the primary medium, allowing words to signify art in their own right, rather than as part of a visual composition. This shift was influenced by mid-20th-century linguistic philosophy. Methodologically, many works took the form of written instructions (e.g., Yoko Ono's *Grapefruit*), emphasizing that the idea could be executed by anyone, or not at all. The work's existence was often in its documentation or the viewer's understanding.

### Legacy and Contemporary Practice
The first major wave of conceptual art lasted from the late 1960s to the late 1970s. Its influence is seen in subsequent movements like Neo-conceptualism in the 1980s and 90s, including the Young British Artists. Contemporary artists continue its concerns with anti-commodification, social critique, and ideas as medium, often in installation, performance, or digital art. The term "conceptual art" is now sometimes broadly used to describe any contemporary art not focused on traditional craft.

## Terms
- **Conceptual Art**: Art where the idea or concept is prioritized over traditional aesthetic and material concerns.
- **Readymade**: An ordinary, manufactured object presented as art, a term coined by Marcel Duchamp.
- **Formalism**: An art theory, notably by Clement Greenberg, that art should focus on the essential, formal qualities of its medium (e.g., flatness in painting).
- **Dematerialization**: The conceptual art strategy of reducing or eliminating the physical art object in favor of ideas, documentation, or text.
- **Art & Language**: A British conceptual art collective founded in 1968, known for using text and critical inquiry.
- **Neo-conceptualism**: Art practices from the 1980s onward that derive from the conceptual art movement of the 1960s and 70s.
- **Event Score**: A simple set of written instructions for an action, a central feature of Fluxus art.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Defining the Term**: The meaning of "conceptual art" is contested. As artist Mel Bochner noted, "concept" can be confused with "intention," and the term has been broadly applied to much contemporary art, diluting its original radical aims.
- **Relationship to Formalism**: Scholars debate whether conceptual art was a radical break from Greenberg's formalist modernism or a continuation of its "dematerializing" logic.
- **The Role of Documentation**: A persistent question is whether documentation (texts, photos) of a conceptual work *is* the art, or merely a record of it.
- **Art vs. Craft**: Conceptual art explicitly favors the "art" side of the art/craft dichotomy, engaging historical discourse over manual skill, a preference that continues to shape contemporary practice.
