# Empire style

## Overview
The Empire style was an early-19th-century design movement in architecture, furniture, and decorative arts, representing the second phase of Neoclassicism. It flourished from 1799 to 1815 during Napoleon I's rule, using art and architecture to idealize his leadership and the French state. The style spread from France across Europe and to the United States, where it influenced local movements like the Federal style.

## Timeline
- **c. 1790s** — Architects Percier and Fontaine, after studying in Rome, become leading furniture designers in Paris.
- **1799–1804** — The Consulate period sees the transitional Consulate style, introducing Empire motifs inspired by military campaigns.
- **1804–1815** — The Empire style flourishes under Napoleon I's First French Empire.
- **1806–1836** — The Arc de Triomphe is built in Paris, a key Empire-style structure.
- **Post-1815** — After Napoleon's fall, the style continues with minor adaptations and sees later revivals.
- **1870** — The style is revived as a national style in Italy following unification.
- **1980s** — A revival of the Empire style occurs.

## Body
### Origins and Philosophy
The Empire style emerged from the preceding Directoire style, which was a more austere Neoclassicism evoking the Roman Republic. The Empire style rejected this austerity, turning instead to the "florid opulence of Imperial Rome." It was created by architects Charles Percier and Pierre Fontaine, who drew inspiration from ancient Greek and Roman empires. The style was a form of propaganda, intended to present Napoleon as a liberator and the French state as enlightened, much like the Neoclassical architecture of the American Federal style.

### Architecture and Interiors
Empire architecture used simple timber frames, often veneered in expensive mahogany, and was adorned with ormolu (gilded bronze) details. Famous French examples include the Arc de Triomphe and La Madeleine in Paris, which emulated Roman edifices. The style also spread to Imperial Russia, used in victory memorials like the Kazan Cathedral. Interiors featured spacious rooms with symmetrically arranged motifs, Corinthian pilasters, and decorative friezes. Key historic sites with homogeneous Empire ensembles include the Château de Malmaison and the Royal Palace of Amsterdam.

### Motifs and Spread
Empire ornamentation was governed by strict symmetry. Common motifs included imperial emblems like the eagle, bee, and Napoleon's initials "N" and "I," alongside Greco-Roman symbols such as laurel wreaths, swans, and sphinxes. Egyptian Revival motifs, like scarabs and obelisks, were popular early in the period. The style spread internationally: General Bernadotte introduced it to Sweden (as the Karl Johan style), and it was adapted in the UK, Germany, and the US into local forms like the Regency and Biedermeier styles. A bombastic variant, Stalinist Empire style, was later exported by the Soviet Union.

## Terms
- **Neoclassicism**: An artistic and architectural style inspired by the art and culture of classical antiquity (Greece and Rome).
- **Directoire style**: The austere, minimalist Neoclassical style that preceded the Empire style in France.
- **Ormolu**: Gilded bronze used for furniture mounts and decorative embellishments.
- **Percier and Fontaine**: The two French architects credited as the creators of the Empire style.
- **Corinthian**: An ornate classical architectural order characterized by columns with elaborate, leafy capitals.
- **Pilaster**: A flat, rectangular column that projects slightly from a wall, used for decoration.
- **Frieze**: A horizontal decorative band, often with sculpted or painted motifs, located at the top of a wall or above a door.
- **Biedermeier**: A Central European style of furniture and interior design from the early 19th century, related to the Empire style.
- **Federal style**: An American architectural and design style (c. 1780–1830) influenced by Neoclassicism and the Empire style.
