# Cardinal Richelieu

## Overview
Cardinal Richelieu (1585–1642) was a French prelate and statesman who served as chief minister to King Louis XIII. He is pivotal for transforming France into a centralized, powerful state and for redirecting the Thirty Years' War from a religious conflict to a struggle against Habsburg hegemony, laying the groundwork for French dominance in Europe.

## Timeline
- **1585** — Born in Paris to a lesser noble family.
- **1607** — Consecrated Bishop of Luçon.
- **1616** — Appointed Secretary of State for foreign affairs.
- **1622** — Made a cardinal.
- **1624** — Becomes chief minister to Louis XIII.
- **1627–28** — Leads the siege of the Huguenot stronghold La Rochelle.
- **1629** — Created Duke of Richelieu; defeats Huguenot rebels, ending their political power.
- **1630** — Survives the "Day of the Dupes," securing his power against Marie de Médici.
- **1635** — Founds the Académie Française.
- **1642** — Dies; succeeded by Cardinal Jules Mazarin.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Born into minor nobility, Richelieu entered the clergy to secure the family's Bishopric of Luçon. He first gained political prominence at the 1614 Estates-General. He rose through court factions, serving Queen Mother Marie de Médici, but lost power after her overthrow in 1617. He regained influence by mediating between Marie and Louis XIII, and after the death of the king's favorite in 1621, he was made cardinal in 1622. He became indispensable during Huguenot rebellions and was appointed chief minister in 1624.

### Domestic Policy: Centralizing Royal Authority
Richelieu's primary goal was to consolidate power under the monarchy. He systematically dismantled the feudal nobility's power by abolishing the office of Constable of France and ordering the razing of private fortified castles. He ruthlessly suppressed noble conspiracies, executing the Duke of Montmorency in 1632. To break the political and military power of the Huguenots, he besieged La Rochelle (1627–28) and defeated them militarily, though the Peace of Alais (1629) preserved their religious freedoms while revoking their political privileges. He established a network of spies and used intendants (royal officials) to bypass local authorities and enforce taxes like the taille, funding his policies.

### Foreign Policy and the Thirty Years' War
Richelieu's foreign policy was driven by *raison d'état* (reason of state), prioritizing French national interest over religious solidarity. His main objective was to check the power of the Habsburg dynasty, which ruled Spain and the Holy Roman Empire. Despite being a Catholic cardinal, he openly subsidized Protestant powers like the Dutch Republic and Sweden against the Habsburgs. He allied France with Sweden via the Treaty of Bärwalde (1631). This strategy redirected the Thirty Years' War from a religious conflict to a nationalist struggle against Habsburg hegemony, ultimately draining Habsburg resources and positioning France for continental dominance.

### Patronage and the New World
Richelieu was a major patron of the arts and learning. He renovated the Sorbonne, founded the Académie Française (1635) to regulate the French language, and built the Palais-Cardinal (later Palais-Royal). He supported playwrights like Pierre Corneille and amassed a vast art collection. In colonial affairs, he promoted New France, founding the Compagnie des Cent-Associés (1627) and negotiating the 1632 Treaty of Saint-Germain-en-Laye to recover Quebec. His policies encouraged peaceful coexistence and integration with Indigenous peoples, who were considered "natural Frenchmen" if converted.

### Final Years and Legacy
Richelieu's later years were marked by illness and continued conspiracies, including a plot by his protégé Cinq-Mars, which he uncovered and crushed. He died in 1642, having named Jules Mazarin as his successor. His legacy is the creation of a strong, centralized French state and the foundation of French foreign policy that led to the hegemony of Louis XIV. He is considered a father of the modern nation-state and the modern secret service.

## Terms
- **Chief Minister:** The principal minister of state, holding effective executive power under the monarch.
- **Huguenots:** French Protestants, whose political and military power Richelieu dismantled.
- **Habsburg Dynasty:** A powerful European royal house ruling Spain and the Holy Roman Empire, the primary rival to French ambitions.
- **Raison d'état:** The principle that the interests and security of the state justify actions that might otherwise be immoral or illegal.
- **Intendants:** Royal officials appointed by the crown to administer provinces, enforce laws, and collect taxes, bypassing local authorities.
- **Estates-General:** A representative assembly of the three estates (clergy, nobility, commoners) of France, convened by the king.
- **Thirty Years' War (1618–1648):** A major European conflict initially between Protestant and Catholic states, which evolved into a wider struggle for political dominance.
- **Day of the Dupes (1630):** A political crisis where Louis XIII ultimately reaffirmed his support for Richelieu over his mother, Marie de Médici.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which Richelieu's policies were driven by personal ambition versus a coherent vision for French statecraft is debated.
- His alliance with Protestant powers against Catholic Habsburgs created a lasting tension between his role as a prince of the Church and his actions as a statesman, raising questions about the primacy of religious versus national loyalty.
- Historians assess whether his harsh authoritarian measures (censorship, espionage, executions) were necessary for state-building or primarily served to maintain his personal power.
