# Yongle Emperor

## Overview
The Yongle Emperor (r. 1402–1424), born Zhu Di, was the third emperor of China's Ming dynasty. He seized power in a civil war and is remembered for moving the capital to Beijing, sponsoring massive cultural and maritime projects, and conducting aggressive foreign policy. His reign is often considered the "second founding" of the Ming, marking a shift toward a more centralized, militarized, and outward-looking empire.

## Timeline
- **1360** — Zhu Di is born, fourth son of the Hongwu Emperor.
- **1370** — Granted the title Prince of Yan.
- **1380** — Relocates to Beiping (modern Beijing) to guard the northern border.
- **1399** — Launches the Jingnan campaign rebellion against the Jianwen Emperor.
- **1402** — Captures Nanjing and ascends the throne as the Yongle Emperor.
- **1403** — Designates Beijing as a secondary capital.
- **1405–1421** — Eunuch Zheng He leads six major maritime expeditions.
- **1407** — The Yongle Encyclopedia compilation is completed.
- **1410** — Personally leads first campaign into Mongolia, defeating the Eastern Mongols.
- **1415** — Reconstruction of the Grand Canal is completed.
- **1420** — Beijing is officially declared the principal capital.
- **1424** — Dies while returning from his fifth Mongol campaign.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Zhu Di was the fourth son of the Ming founder, the Hongwu Emperor. He was made Prince of Yan and stationed in Beiping, where he gained military experience and popularity. After the Hongwu Emperor's death in 1398, his nephew, the Jianwen Emperor, attempted to curb the power of imperial princes. In 1399, Zhu Di rebelled, claiming to be restoring order. After a three-year civil war known as the Jingnan campaign, he captured the capital Nanjing in 1402 and declared himself emperor.

### Domestic Policy and Administration
The Yongle Emperor's reign is often called the "second founding" of the Ming. He established a new hereditary military nobility loyal to the emperor, replacing the powerful generals of his father's era. He created the Grand Secretariat, an advisory body that became the core of the civil administration, and maintained a relatively stable bureaucracy with long-serving ministers. He relied heavily on eunuchs for intelligence and military commands, establishing the feared "Eastern Depot" secret police. He also systematized Neo-Confucian texts for civil service examinations, which were held every three years.

### The New Capital and Grand Canal
His most significant project was relocating the capital from Nanjing to Beijing, which he saw as his power base and a strategic location near the northern frontier. Construction, employing hundreds of thousands of workers, was completed in 1420. To supply the new capital, he oversaw the reconstruction of the Grand Canal, finished in 1415. This massive engineering project created a continuous waterway for shipping grain from the south, stimulating economic growth along its route but ending the sea transport of grain.

### Culture and Scholarship
To bolster his legitimacy, the Yongle Emperor promoted himself as a Confucian patron. He commissioned the compilation of the Yongle Encyclopedia, a vast collection of all known books, involving over 2,000 scholars. He also sponsored the creation of official commentaries on Confucian classics for use in education and examinations. He supported Buddhism, particularly Tibetan Buddhism, to help pacify non-Chinese peoples within the empire.

### Foreign Policy and Military Campaigns
The Yongle Emperor pursued an active foreign policy to assert Ming hegemony. He sent diplomatic missions and military expeditions to Manchuria, Korea, Japan, and Central Asia. His most famous ventures were the seven maritime expeditions led by the eunuch admiral Zheng He, which reached Southeast Asia, India, Persia, and East Africa. The Mongols remained the greatest threat; he personally led five campaigns into Mongolia, but these were ultimately unsuccessful and costly. He also invaded Đại Việt (northern Vietnam) in 1406, annexing it, but faced persistent rebellions that continued until his death.

## Terms
- **Jingnan campaign**: The civil war (1399–1402) in which Zhu Di overthrew his nephew, the Jianwen Emperor.
- **Grand Secretariat**: An advisory body created by the Yongle Emperor that became the de facto core of the Ming civil administration.
- **Grand Canal**: A major inland waterway system reconstructed under the Yongle Emperor to supply Beijing with grain from the south.
- **Yongle Encyclopedia**: A massive compilation of books commissioned by the Yongle Emperor, one of the largest encyclopedias in history.
- **Treasure ships**: The large, advanced vessels used in the maritime expeditions led by Admiral Zheng He.
- **Eastern Depot**: A secret police agency established in 1420, run by eunuchs, to monitor officials and the public.
- **Tributary system**: The diplomatic framework where foreign states acknowledged Ming suzerainty in exchange for trade and titles.
- **Neo-Confucianism**: The state orthodoxy promoted by the Yongle Emperor, which systematized Confucian thought for education and governance.

## Debates and open questions
- **Legitimacy**: Historians debate the extent to which the Yongle Emperor's rewriting of history and harsh purges were necessary to secure his rule versus being excessive.
- **Strategic impact**: Scholars argue whether his decision to move the capital to Beijing and withdraw forward garrisons from the northern frontier was a strategic mistake that weakened Ming defenses long-term.
- **Maritime policy**: The discontinuation of Zheng He's voyages after the Yongle era is debated as a turning point, with some viewing it as a lost opportunity for global engagement and others as a pragmatic fiscal decision.
- **Succession**: The conflict between his sons Zhu Gaochi and Zhu Gaoxu created lasting instability, raising questions about the emperor's handling of the succession.
