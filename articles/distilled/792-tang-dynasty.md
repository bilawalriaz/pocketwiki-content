# Tang dynasty

## Overview  
The Tang dynasty (618–907 CE) was a golden age of Chinese civilization marked by territorial expansion, cultural flourishing, and technological innovation. Founded by the Li family after the collapse of the Sui, it experienced political interruption under Empress Wu Zetian (690–705) and eventual decline following the An Lushan rebellion (755–763). Renowned for poetry, cosmopolitan cities like Chang’an, and a merit-based bureaucracy, the Tang shaped East Asian culture and governance for centuries.

## Timeline  
- **618** — Li Yuan declares himself emperor, founding the Tang
- **626** — Xuanwu Gate Incident: Li Shimin kills brothers, becomes Emperor Taizong
- **690–705** — Empress Wu Zetian usurps power, establishing Wu Zhou
- **755–763** — An Lushan rebellion devastates central authority
- **763** — Rebellion ends; jiedushi gain autonomy
- **845** — Emperor Wuzong suppresses Buddhism
- **874–884** — Huang Chao rebellion further weakens the dynasty
- **907** — Zhu Wen deposes Emperor Ai, ending the Tang

## Body  

### Establishment and Early Rule  
The Tang were founded by the Li family, of Han and partial Xianbei descent, after exploiting Sui decline. Emperor Gaozu (Li Yuan) declared himself emperor in 618. His son Taizong seized power in 626 via the Xuanwu Gate Incident, killing two brothers to secure the throne. Taizong expanded territory through campaigns against the Eastern and Western Turks, destroyed the Eastern Turkic Khaganate, and promoted Buddhism while compiling a new legal code.

### Wu Zetian’s Usurpation  
Empress Wu Zetian rose from concort to emperor, ruling as China’s only female sovereign from 690 to 705. She manipulated court politics, eliminated rivals, and legitimized her rule through the *Great Cloud Sutra*. Her reign weakened the Northwestern aristocracy and broadened political participation, though her rule was marked by purges and factional strife.

### Emperor Xuanzong and the Golden Age  
Xuanzong’s 44-year reign (712–756) marked the Tang’s peak. He promoted Buddhism, Taoism, and foreign trade, while abolishing the death penalty in 747. However, his reliance on non-Chinese generals and aggressive foreign policy sowed instability. The An Lushan rebellion erupted in 755, led by a mixed Sogdian-Turkic general, devastating northern China and killing tens of thousands.

### Decline and Fall  
The An Lushan rebellion crippled central authority. Regional military governors (*jiedushi*) gained hereditary control, eroding civil governance. Natural disasters, agrarian revolts, and the Huang Chao rebellion (874–884) further weakened the state. Zhu Wen, a former rebel turned general, seized Chang’an in 901, assassinated the emperor in 904, and declared the Later Liang in 907, ending the Tang.

### Administration and Politics  
The Tang refined the civil-service examination system, emphasizing Confucian classics and literary skill. The Three Departments and Six Ministries structure became a lasting bureaucratic model. The equal-field system allocated land to soldiers and peasants, but its breakdown after 755 led to land concentration and peasant displacement.

### Culture and Society  
Chang’an, the capital, was the world’s largest city, hosting diverse ethnicities and religions. The Tang were a golden age of poetry, with Li Bai, Du Fu, and Wang Wei defining Chinese verse. Woodblock printing, invented during this period, spread knowledge widely. Buddhism thrived until Wuzong’s persecution in 845. Women enjoyed relatively liberal rights, especially in urban areas, and played roles in religion and culture.

### Military and Foreign Policy  
The Tang maintained vast armies to control Inner Asia and the Silk Road. They defeated the Western Turks, fought Tibet, and clashed with the Abbasid Caliphate at the Battle of Talas (751). The protectorate system extended influence into Central Asia. Maritime trade flourished, linking China to the Middle East and Africa.

### Economy  
The Silk Road and maritime routes enriched the empire. Salt monopolies became major revenue sources. The Grand Canal facilitated grain transport. Cities like Yangzhou thrived as commercial hubs. Technological advances included the compass, gunpowder, and papermaking.

### Science and Technology  
Woodblock printing revolutionized information dissemination. Mechanical clocks, chain pumps, and gas cylinders showcased engineering prowess. Medical texts classified herbal remedies, and alchemical experiments advanced materials science.

## Terms  
- **Jiedushi**: Regional military governors with autonomous power.  
- **Fubing**: Tang military system combining soldier-farming.  
- **Equal-field system**: Land allocation policy linking military service to farmland.  
- **Jinshi**: Highest civil-service degree, testing literary and governance skills.  
- **Chang’an**: Capital city, among the world’s largest at its height.  
- **Woodblock printing**: Technique enabling mass production of texts.  
- **Mandate of Heaven**: Doctrine justifying dynastic rule based on virtue.  
- **Tributary system**: Framework for foreign relations with neighboring states.  
- **Civil-service exams**: Merit-based recruitment for government posts.  
- **Silk Road**: Network of trade routes connecting China to Eurasia.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- **Wu Zetian’s legitimacy**: Traditional historiography vilifies her, but modern scholars debate her effectiveness and motivations.  
- **An Lushan’s impact**: Some argue the rebellion was catastrophic, while others suggest long-term structural issues contributed to decline.  
- **Aristocratic decline**: Recent scholarship challenges the notion that Huang Chao’s rebellion destroyed the aristocracy, arguing decline began earlier.  
- **Population estimates**: Discrepancies exist between census data and scholarly reconstructions of Tang demographics.  
- **Tang influence on Japan**: The extent of direct cultural transmission versus independent development remains debated.
