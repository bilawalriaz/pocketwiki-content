# Printing

## Overview
Printing is a process for mass reproducing text and images using a master form or template. It evolved from early ink rubbings on stone tablets in sixth-century China to woodblock printing on paper and textiles, then to movable type (porcelain, wood, and metal) in East Asia, and finally to the mechanized printing press using metal alloy type and oil-based ink developed by Johannes Gutenberg in Europe c. 1450. This technology drastically reduced the cost and increased the speed of book production, enabling the Renaissance, the Scientific Revolution, and the spread of literacy. Subsequent industrial innovations—rotary presses, offset lithography, and digital printing—further scaled output, while 3D printing extended the concept to additive manufacturing of physical objects.

## Body

### Woodblock Printing
Woodblock printing originated in China as a method for textiles and later paper. The earliest ink-squeeze rubbings and potential stone blocks date to the mid-sixth century. Mechanical woodblock printing on paper began in the 7th century under the Tang dynasty and spread across East Asia. Japan printed the *Hyakumantō Darani* around 770; Korea produced the *Pure Light Dharani Sutra* before 751 (estimated no later than 704). By the ninth century, printing on paper flourished; the *Diamond Sutra* (868) is the earliest completely surviving dated printed book. By the tenth century, print runs reached 400,000 copies for some sutras, and skilled printers achieved 2,000 double-page sheets per day. The technique spread to Korea, Japan, Turpan, Vietnam, Persia, and Russia, reaching Europe by c. 1400 for old master prints and playing cards.

In the Islamic world (900–1444), wooden and tin block printing (*ṭarsh*) was used by street magicians (Banū Sāsān/Ghurabā') for mass-produced amulets with minuscule text (0.1 cm high), and officially for Fatimid tax receipts, Granada commercial stamps, Almohad decrees, Ilkhanid paper money, and Hajj certificates. Block printing vanished from the Islamic world after 1436/1444 without explanation; migrating Egyptian Romani Ghurabā' may have transmitted the craft to Central Europe.

In Europe, block printing on cloth was common by 1300. With paper availability c. 1400, it shifted to small woodcut religious images and playing cards, produced in large numbers from 1425. Mid-15th-century block-books (woodcut text and images on single blocks) emerged as cheaper alternatives to manuscripts; the *Ars moriendi* and *Biblia pauperum* were bestsellers. Scholars debate whether block-books preceded or followed movable type (estimated 1440–1460).

### Movable-Type Printing
Movable type uses individual reusable pieces (metal, wood, ceramic) cast from matrices. Around 1040, Bi Sheng in China created the first system using porcelain (clay) type, which broke easily. By 1298, Wang Zhen carved more durable wooden type and developed revolving tables with number-association for efficient Chinese typesetting. Despite this, woodblock printing (xylography) remained dominant in China because it was cheaper and more efficient for thousands of characters. Copper movable type appeared in the 12th century for Northern Song paper money.

In Korea (Goryeo dynasty), bronze movable type was invented c. 1230 using a coin-casting method: characters cut in beech wood, pressed into clay moulds, bronze poured, then polished. The *Jikji* (1377) is the earliest extant metal-type book. Eastern metal movable type spread to Europe between the late 14th and early 15th centuries; historians note the Korean method was "extremely similar to Gutenberg's," and a Chinese-Korean technique or report likely traveled westward.

### The Printing Press
Around 1450, Johannes Gutenberg introduced Europe's first movable-type printing system in Mainz. He innovated: casting type from a lead-tin-antimony-copper-bismuth alloy (still used today) via matrix and hand mould; adapting the screw press; formulating oil-based ink; and creating softer, more absorbent paper. Work began c. 1436 with partners Andreas Dritzehen and paper-mill owner Andreas Heilmann. Compared to woodblock, press-based movable type was faster, more durable, yielded uniform lettering, and spurred typography/font design. The high-quality, relatively low-cost Gutenberg Bible (1455) proved the system's superiority for Western alphabets. The press spread rapidly across Europe, fueling the Renaissance and global knowledge dissemination.

### Rotary Printing Press
In 1843, Richard M. Hoe invented the steam-powered rotary press in the U.S., using impressions curved around a cylinder to print on continuous paper rolls. Hoe's design ran 2,000 revolutions/hour (8,000 pages/hour). By 1891, presses at the *New York World* and *Philadelphia Item* produced 90,000 4-page sheets/hour or 48,000 8-page sheets. William Bullock later improved rotary drum printing. Modern rotary technologies include sheetfed offset, rotogravure, and flexographic printing. The transition to rolled paper enabled continuous feed and massive mass production.

### Conventional Printing Technologies
All printing separates image areas (printing) from non-image areas (non-printing). Four main process categories exist:
- **Planographics**: Image and non-image on same plane; separation chemical/physical (offset lithography, collotype, screenless printing).
- **Relief**: Image areas raised; non-image recessed (flexography, letterpress).
- **Intaglio**: Image areas etched/engraved below surface; non-image on plane (steel die engraving, gravure, etching, collagraph).
- **Porous/Stencil**: Ink passes through fine mesh; stencil blocks non-image areas (screen printing, stencil duplicator, risograph).

**Crop marks** indicate trim lines to eliminate blank margins; the trimmed excess is **bleed**.

**Letterpress** (relief): Movable type locked in a press bed, inked, and pressed onto paper. Invented by Gutenberg mid-15th century; standard for text until offset printing replaced it in the late 20th century; now revived artisanally.

**Offset** (planographic/lithographic): Right-reading image on plate inked, offset to rubber blanket (becomes mirror), then transferred to substrate (right-reading again). Relies on oil-water repulsion. Plate, blanket, and impression cylinders standard. Most books/newspapers use offset lithography today.

**Gravure** (intaglio): Image comprises tiny depressions (cells) on copper-plated steel cylinder (chromed). Cells filled with ink; doctor blade scrapes excess; rubber roller presses paper into cells. Used for long, high-quality runs: magazines, catalogues, packaging, fabric, wallpaper, stamps, decorative laminates. Viscosity control ensures color accuracy.

**Flexography** (relief): Photopolymer plates. Dominates flexible packaging, corrugated board, labels, newspapers (80% US market, 50% Europe, 20% Asia), competing with gravure.

**Other techniques**: Dye-sublimation; inkjet (small runs, variable data, diverse substrates); laser/toner (offices, transactional, variable-data direct mail); pad printing (3D surfaces); screen printing (textiles, tiles, uneven surfaces); intaglio (currencies); thermal printing (labels, baggage tags, deli tickets).

### Impact of German Movable-Type Printing Press
**Quantitative**: European book output rose from a few million to ~1 billion copies in under four centuries after Gutenberg.

**Religious**: Samuel Hartlib (1641) predicted printing would spread knowledge so common people would know their rights and resist oppression. In the Muslim world, Arabic-script printing was opposed early modern period due to calligraphy's prestige; Hebrew/Armenian printing permitted. First Ottoman movable type was Hebrew (1493). Sultan Selim I (1515) made printing punishable by death; Murad III (late 16th c.) allowed non-religious Arabic books (mostly imported). Ibrahim Muteferrika established first Arabic press in Ottoman Empire (operated until 1742, 17 utilitarian works). Printing became common in Islamic world only in 19th century. In Germanic states, Hebrew printers excluded from guilds; Hebrew printing flourished in Italy from 1470 (Rome, Bari, Pisa, Livorno, Mantua) under licensed titles (*con licenza de superiori*). Church and crown regulated content; printing "wrong" material carried extreme penalties (e.g., William Carter hanged 1584 for pro-Catholic pamphlet in Protestant England).

**Social**: Print broadened access to knowledge, enabled cumulative intellectual progress, and assured ideas would not be lost (Acton 1895). Elizabeth Eisenstein: print created sustained uniform reference and allowed comparison of incompatible views. Asa Briggs and Peter Burke identify five reading types emerging with print: *Critical* (general access enables independent opinion); *Dangerous* (seen as rebellious, especially for women stirring emotions/love notes); *Creative* (interpretation diverging from author intent); *Extensive* (shift from intensive cover-to-cover reading to selective excerpts across topics); *Private* (solitary reading replaces group oral reading, linked to individualism). Occupational structure shifted: printers (literate artisans) emerged; scribes declined; proof-correcting, bookselling, and librarianship grew.

**Educational**: Universities transformed in language of scholarship, libraries, curriculum, pedagogy. *Language*: Latin dominated pre-print; vernacular expanded post-print, though Latin remained international until 18th century. *Curriculum*: Professors could compare multiple authors; textbooks printed at varying difficulty levels.

### Digital Printing
By 2005, digital printing accounted for ~9% of 45 trillion pages printed annually. Subdivided by format: small format (up to ledger size, offices/libraries); wide format (up to 3'/914mm rolls, drafting/design). Technologies: blueprint/chemical; daisy wheel (pre-formed characters); dot-matrix (dot arrays); line printing; heat transfer (fax/receipts); inkjet/bubble-jet; electrophotography (toner to charged image); laser (xerography, pixel-by-pixel laser); solid ink (melted sticks). Toner systems generally more economical long-term despite higher upfront cost than inkjet. Professional digital presses (Xerox iGen3, Kodak Nexpress, HP Indigo, InfoPrint 5000) rival offset quality, handle variable data. Digital offset/direct imaging presses auto-convert files to plates but cannot insert variable data. Small press/fanzines use digital; pre-photocopying era used spirit duplicator, hectograph, mimeograph.

### 3D Printing
3D printing (additive manufacturing, rapid prototyping, fabricating) creates physical objects from 3D digital models by depositing thin material layers. 1980s: limited to functional/aesthetic prototypes ("rapid prototyping"). By 2019, precision, repeatability, and material range reached industrial-production viability. Key advantage: complex geometries infeasible by hand, including hollow parts and internal truss structures for weight reduction. Fused deposition modeling (FDM)—continuous thermoplastic filament—is the most common process as of 2020.

## Terms
- **Woodblock printing (xylography)**: Relief printing technique where text/images carved into a wooden block, inked, and pressed onto substrate; originated in China for textiles/paper.
- **Movable type**: System using individual reusable type pieces (metal, wood, ceramic) cast from matrices, allowing flexible composition vs. fixed blocks.
- **Matrix**: Master mould (struck by letterpunch) used to cast multiple identical type pieces.
- **Hand mould**: Adjustable device for casting type pieces from molten alloy using a matrix, enabling mass production of uniform sort.
- **Oil-based ink**: Ink using linseed oil vehicle (Gutenberg's innovation) adhering to metal type and transferring cleanly to paper, unlike water-based inks for woodblocks.
- **Screw press**: Adapted wine/olive press mechanism applying even vertical pressure for printing; basis of Gutenberg's press.
- **Rotary press**: Press using curved plates on cylinders printing on continuous rolls; steam-powered (Hoe 1843) enabled high-speed mass production.
- **Offset lithography**: Planographic process where image transfers from plate to rubber blanket to paper; relies on oil-water repulsion; dominant for books/newspapers.
- **Intaglio (gravure)**: Printing from recessed cells etched/engraved into cylinder; high-quality, long runs (magazines, packaging, stamps).
- **Flexography**: Relief printing using flexible photopolymer plates; dominant for packaging, labels, newspapers.
- **Digital printing**: Direct imaging from digital files without static plates; includes inkjet, laser/toner, electrophotography; enables variable data.
- **3D printing (additive manufacturing)**: Layer-by-layer fabrication of physical objects from digital models; FDM (thermoplastic filament) most common.

## Debates and open questions
- **Block-book chronology**: Whether European block-books (c. 1440–1460) preceded or followed movable type; majority view holds they followed.
- **Transmission of movable type to Europe**: Extent to which Korean/Chinese metal-type technology or reports thereof influenced Gutenberg; scholars note "extremely similar" methods but direct transmission unproven.
- **Disappearance of Islamic block printing**: Why *ṭarsh* printing vanished after 1436/1444 without explanation; possible role of migrating Ghurabā' in European transmission.
- **Quantitative impact estimates**: European book output rise from "a few million to around one billion copies within less than four centuries" is an estimate; precise figures debated.
- **3D printing industrial viability**: As of 2019, some processes considered viable for industrial production, but scope, materials, and economic thresholds remain evolving.
