# Spanish Empire

## Overview  
The Spanish Empire (1492–1976) was the first truly global empire, spanning the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Oceania at its height of 13.7 million km². Born from the 1492 union of Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile, it was financed by American silver and driven by Catholic evangelization, becoming the dominant European power of the early modern period. Its collapse began with 19th-century independence movements and ended with the 1898 Spanish-American War, leaving a lasting linguistic, religious, and cultural legacy across the globe.

## Timeline  
- **1492**: Columbus reaches the Americas; Granada falls, completing the Reconquista  
- **1494**: Treaty of Tordesillas divides the New World between Spain and Portugal  
- **1519–1521**: Cortés conquers the Aztec Empire; Tenochtitlan falls in 1521  
- **1532**: Pizarro captures the Inca Empire at Cajamarca  
- **1580–1640**: Iberian Union unites Spanish and Portuguese crowns  
- **1700**: War of Spanish Succession begins after Charles II’s death  
- **1759–1763**: Seven Years' War; Spain loses Havana and Manila to Britain  
- **1808**: Napoleon’s invasion triggers Spanish American wars of independence  
- **1898**: Spanish-American War ends with Spain ceding Cuba, Puerto Rico, Philippines, and Guam  

## Body  

### Origins and Early Expansion  
The empire began with the dynastic marriage of Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile in 1469, creating a personal union that consolidated Iberian power. Their completion of the Reconquista with the fall of Granada in 1492 enabled overseas expansion. Columbus’s 1492 voyage, backed by the Catholic Monarchs, opened the Americas to European colonization. The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) granted Spain exclusive rights to most of the New World, while Portugal retained Africa and Asia. Early settlements in the Caribbean, such as Santo Domingo (1496), established patterns of colonization that replicated Castilian institutions. The conquest of the Canary Islands (completed by 1496) provided a model for subjugating indigenous populations, with over 90% of Guanche people killed or enslaved by 1504.

### Habsburg Ascendancy and Global Dominion  
Charles V inherited a vast transatlantic empire in 1516, including Spanish territories in the Americas, Italian kingdoms, the Low Countries, and Austrian Habsburg lands. His reign marked the peak of Habsburg power, but also the beginning of overextension. The conquests of the Aztec (1521) and Inca (1532) empires brought enormous wealth through silver mining at Potosí and Zacatecas, financing European wars. Philip II (r. 1556–1598) expanded Spanish control to the Philippines (1565) and briefly ruled Portugal (1580–1640), creating the first "empire on which the sun never sets." However, constant warfare in Europe, the Netherlands, and against the Ottomans drained resources. The Spanish Armada’s defeat in 1588 symbolized the limits of Habsburg naval power. Economic strain from silver imports caused inflation, debated by scholars at the School of Salamanca, while smuggling undermined the mercantile system.

### Bourbon Reforms and Decline  
The War of Spanish Succession (1701–1714) ended with the Bourbon Philip V ascending the throne, implementing centralizing reforms like the Nueva Plata decrees to strengthen royal authority. The Bourbons moved colonial administration from Seville to Cádiz (1717), promoted legal trade through *comercio libre* (1778), and reformed the military and Church. These changes sparked resistance, including the Tupac Amaru uprising (1780) and comunero revolts in New Granada. Despite economic growth in the 18th century—especially silver production in Mexico—the benefits were unevenly distributed, deepening social tensions. Spain’s defeat in the Seven Years' War (losing Havana and Manila) exposed military weaknesses, while British and French encroachment in the Pacific Northwest highlighted declining global influence.

### Independence and Final Collapse  
Napoleon’s 1808 invasion of Spain created a legitimacy crisis, triggering independence movements across Spanish America. Leaders like Simón Bolívar and José de San Martín led successful revolts, with Mexico achieving independence in 1821. By 1826, Spain had lost nearly all American territories except Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines. The Spanish-American War (1898) marked the final collapse: the U.S. destroyed Spain’s fleets at Manila Bay and Santiago de Cuba, forcing cession of remaining overseas territories. Spain retained only African possessions, including Spanish Sahara and Equatorial Guinea, until the mid-20th century.

## Terms  
- **Encomienda**: A labor system granting colonists the right to extract tribute and labor from indigenous people in exchange for protection and Christianization.  
- **Repartimiento**: A rotational forced labor draft requiring indigenous communities to supply workers for mines and plantations.  
- **Flota**: The Spanish treasure fleet system protecting transatlantic silver shipments.  
- **Casa de Contratación**: The Seville-based council regulating colonial trade and navigation.  
- **Nueva Plata decrees**: Bourbon-era laws centralizing power and abolishing regional privileges in the former Crown of Aragon.  
- **Comercio libre**: 1778 policy allowing Spanish American ports to trade freely with each other and Spanish ports.  
- **Caudillismo**: A political system dominated by strongman leaders, rooted in colonial administrative weakness.  
- **Mestizo**: A person of mixed European and indigenous ancestry, forming a distinct social category in colonial society.  
- **Peninsular**: Spaniards born in Spain, who dominated colonial administration over American-born creoles.  
- **Quinto real**: The crown’s 20% tax on precious metal production from colonial mines.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Scholars debate whether Spanish colonial rule was primarily exploitative or developmental. Humboldt’s 1800s observations suggested indigenous peoples in New Spain were wealthier than European peasants, challenging narratives of universal oppression. The role of silver wealth in Spain’s economic decline remains contested—some argue it enriched northern Europe while impoverishing Spain, while others emphasize its role in funding European wars. The effectiveness of Bourbon reforms in preventing independence is debated, with some viewing them as too little, too late, and others seeing them as provocations that accelerated colonial rebellion. The extent to which the Spanish-American War represented a sudden collapse versus a long-declining empire is also disputed.
