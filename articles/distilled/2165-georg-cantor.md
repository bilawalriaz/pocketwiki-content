# Georg Cantor

## Overview
Georg Cantor (1845–1918) was a German mathematician who founded set theory, a foundational branch of mathematics. He revolutionized the understanding of infinity by proving that infinite sets can have different sizes, a concept initially met with fierce resistance but now central to modern mathematics.

## Timeline
- **1845** — Born in Saint Petersburg, Russian Empire.
- **1867** — Earned his doctorate in number theory from the University of Berlin.
- **1874** — Published his first paper on set theory, proving the real numbers are uncountable.
- **1879–1884** — Published a series of six articles introducing his transfinite set theory.
- **1883** — Published *Grundlagen*, a major monograph defending and expanding his theory.
- **1884** — Suffered his first major bout of depression.
- **1891** — Introduced his famous diagonal argument.
- **1895–1897** — Published his last significant papers on set theory.
- **1899** — Hospitalized; his youngest son died suddenly.
- **1904** — Shaken by Julius König's attempted refutation of his work at a major congress.
- **1918** — Died in a sanatorium in Halle, Germany.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Cantor was born in Saint Petersburg to a Lutheran family. After his father's illness, the family moved to Germany. He excelled in mathematics, earning his doctorate in Berlin in 1867. He spent his entire academic career at the University of Halle, becoming a full professor in 1879. His work on trigonometric series led him to investigate the nature of infinite sets.

### Development of Set Theory
Cantor's foundational work began in 1874 with a proof that the set of real numbers is "more numerous" than the set of natural numbers, demonstrating that infinite sets can have different sizes. He established that two sets have the same "size" if a one-to-one correspondence exists between them. He defined countable (denumerable) and uncountable (nondenumerable) sets and introduced the power set (the set of all subsets of a set), proving its size is always strictly larger than the original set. This work, published in a series of papers from 1879 to 1884, established the theory of transfinite cardinal and ordinal numbers, extending arithmetic to infinite quantities.

### Controversy and Later Work
Cantor's ideas faced intense opposition, most notably from Leopold Kronecker, who rejected actual infinity and non-constructive proofs. This conflict likely contributed to Cantor's recurring bouts of depression. Despite this, he continued his work, introducing the diagonal argument in 1891 and formulating the continuum hypothesis—the conjecture that there is no set whose size is between that of the natural numbers and the real numbers. He also distinguished between the "transfinite" (increasable infinities) and the "absolute" infinite. His later years were marked by personal tragedy and hospitalizations, though he remained active in mathematical societies.

### Philosophy and Legacy
Cantor, a devout Lutheran, saw his work on the absolute infinite as divinely inspired. He engaged in debates with theologians who feared his transfinite numbers challenged God's unique infinity. Philosophically, he believed mathematics possessed "freedom" to posit concepts free of contradiction. His work was initially controversial but gained recognition, exemplified by David Hilbert's defense: "No one shall expel us from the paradise that Cantor has created." Set theory, once counter-intuitive, became a fundamental language for all of mathematics.

## Terms
- **Set theory**: The branch of mathematics that studies sets, or collections of objects, forming a foundational theory for mathematics.
- **One-to-one correspondence**: A pairing between elements of two sets where each element of one set is paired with exactly one element of the other, and vice versa, used to define equal "size."
- **Countable set**: A set that can be put into a one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers (e.g., the rational numbers).
- **Uncountable set**: A set that cannot be put into a one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers (e.g., the real numbers).
- **Power set**: The set of all possible subsets of a given set.
- **Cardinal number**: A number representing the "size" or cardinality of a set, extended by Cantor to infinite sets (denoted by ℵ, aleph).
- **Ordinal number**: A number representing the order type of a well-ordered set, extended to transfinite ordinals (denoted by ω, omega).
- **Continuum hypothesis**: Cantor's conjecture that there is no set whose cardinality is strictly between that of the natural numbers and the real numbers.
- **Diagonal argument**: Cantor's 1891 proof technique demonstrating the uncountability of the real numbers and used to prove his theorem about power sets.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Philosophical and religious opposition**: Cantor's theory of actual infinity was opposed by constructivists (like Kronecker) and intuitionists (like Poincaré and Brouwer) who rejected non-constructive existence proofs. Some theologians initially saw it as challenging God's unique infinity, though others like Konstantin Gutberlet and Cardinal Franzelin eventually accepted it.
- **The continuum hypothesis**: Cantor believed this was true but could not prove it. It was later shown (by Gödel in 1940 and Cohen in 1963) to be independent of standard Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with the axiom of choice (ZFC), meaning it can be neither proved nor disproved within that system.
- **Paradoxes and foundational crises**: Cantor's work led to paradoxes like Russell's paradox, which revealed inconsistencies in early set theory and prompted the development of axiomatic systems (e.g., Zermelo's 1908 axioms) to avoid them.
