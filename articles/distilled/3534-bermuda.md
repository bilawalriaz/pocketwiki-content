# Bermuda

## Overview
Bermuda is a British Overseas Territory in the North Atlantic Ocean, an archipelago of 181 islands with a population of around 64,000. It is a self-governing parliamentary democracy with a high-income economy dominated by international business and tourism. Its history as a strategic naval base and its unique cultural blend of British, African, and West Indian influences make it a distinct entity within the British realm.

## Timeline
- **Early 1500s** — Discovered by Spanish explorer Juan de Bermúdez; uninhabited.
- **1609** — English ship *Sea Venture* wrecked, leading to permanent settlement.
- **1612** — Official English settlement begins; St. George's Town founded.
- **1616** — First slaves (from the West Indies) arrive in Bermuda.
- **1684** — Somers Isles Company charter revoked; Bermuda becomes a Crown Colony.
- **1775** — Bermudians secretly supply gunpowder to American rebels.
- **1815** — Royal Naval Dockyard construction begins, solidifying its military role.
- **1930s** — Tourism emerges as a key industry after agricultural decline.
- **1941** — US military bases established under the Destroyers-for-Bases Agreement.
- **1967** — Universal adult suffrage adopted.
- **1995** — US and Canadian military bases close; independence referendum rejected.
- **2020** — Flora Duffy wins Bermuda's first Olympic gold medal (triathlon).

## Body

### Discovery and Settlement
Bermuda was discovered in the early 1500s by the Spanish but was not settled due to its dangerous reefs and reputation as the "Isle of Devils." Permanent English settlement began in 1612 following the 1609 shipwreck of the *Sea Venture*, which was en route to the Jamestown colony. The islands were initially administered by the Somers Isles Company, which promoted agriculture, particularly tobacco. However, Bermudians soon turned to maritime trades like shipbuilding, privateering, and the salt trade after the company's charter was revoked in 1684.

### Slavery and Demographics
Slavery was introduced in 1616, with the first enslaved people arriving from the West Indies. The enslaved population, which included both Africans and Native Americans, grew over time, leading to frequent rebellions and restrictive laws. Bermuda's population is a mix of Black (52%) and White (31%) Bermudians, with significant ancestry from Britain, Ireland, Portugal, and West Indian immigrants. The Portuguese community, originating from the Azores and Madeira, has been a major demographic influence for over 160 years.

### Political History and Governance
Bermuda has a long history of self-governance, with its House of Assembly established in 1620. It is a British Overseas Territory where the UK retains responsibility for defence and foreign affairs. The territory has its own bicameral parliament and premier as head of government. A 1995 referendum decisively rejected independence. Bermuda is an associate member of the Caribbean Community (CARICOM), a decision that sparked debate due to its minimal trade with the region.

### Economy and Strategic Importance
Bermuda's economy shifted from agriculture to tourism in the early 20th century, and by the late 1970s, international business, especially insurance and reinsurance, became the dominant sector, accounting for about 85% of GDP. The territory has no personal income tax but relies on import duties and payroll taxes; a corporate tax for large multinationals was introduced in 2025. Historically, Bermuda was a vital Imperial fortress and Royal Navy base, known as the "Gibraltar of the West." Its strategic importance continued through both World Wars, with US military bases operating until 1995.

### Geography and Climate
The archipelago sits atop ancient volcanic seamounts, with a limestone cap formed by coral and sand. Its climate is humid subtropical, moderated by the Gulf Stream, resulting in mild winters and warm summers. Bermuda is prone to hurricanes but is often protected by its northern position and coral reefs. Fresh water is scarce and collected from rainfall.

## Terms
- **British Overseas Territory**: A territory under the jurisdiction and sovereignty of the United Kingdom, but not part of it.
- **Somers Isles Company**: The joint-stock company that administered Bermuda from 1615 until its charter was revoked in 1684.
- **House of Assembly**: Bermuda's lower house of parliament, established in 1620, making it one of the world's oldest legislatures.
- **Premier**: The head of government in Bermuda, appointed by the governor.
- **Governor**: The representative of the British monarch in Bermuda, responsible for defence and foreign affairs.
- **CARICOM**: The Caribbean Community, a socio-economic bloc of nations; Bermuda is an associate member.
- **Bermudian English**: The predominant language, with British English conventions used in formal writing.
- **Gombey**: Troupes performing a traditional dance that incorporates African, Caribbean, British, and Native American influences.
- **Cahow (Bermuda petrel)**: A seabird thought extinct since the 1620s, rediscovered in 1951 and now a conservation focus.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Independence**: The 1995 referendum saw a large majority vote against independence, but the issue remains a topic of political discussion.
- **CARICOM Membership**: Bermuda's 2003 decision to join CARICOM was controversial, with opponents arguing it was detrimental due to negligible trade and cultural differences.
- **Citizenship and Immigration**: Historical changes to British nationality laws have affected Bermudians' rights of abode in the UK, a point of ongoing sensitivity.
