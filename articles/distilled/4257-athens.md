# Athens

## Overview
Athens is the capital and largest city of Greece, a major Mediterranean metropolis and one of the world's oldest cities with a recorded history spanning over 3,400 years. It is historically significant as the birthplace of democracy, Western philosophy, and classical arts, and remains the economic, political, and cultural heart of modern Greece.

## Timeline
- **11th–7th millennia BC** — Earliest human presence in the Cave of Schist.
- **c. 3000 BC** — Athens has been continuously inhabited for at least 5,000 years.
- **1400 BC** — Settlement becomes an important Mycenaean centre.
- **508 BC** — Cleisthenes introduces democracy in Athens.
- **490 BC** — Athenians defeat Persians at the Battle of Marathon.
- **480 BC** — Greek victory at the Battle of Salamis; Athens is sacked by Persians.
- **431–404 BC** — Peloponnesian War; Athens is defeated by Sparta.
- **338 BC** — Athens is defeated by Philip II of Macedon at Chaeronea.
- **1834** — Athens is chosen as the capital of the modern Greek state.
- **1896** — Athens hosts the first modern Olympic Games.
- **2004** — Athens hosts the Summer Olympic Games for the second time.

## Body

### Antiquity and Classical Era
Athens has been continuously inhabited since at least 3000 BC and was a major Mycenaean centre by 1400 BC. Social unrest in the 6th century BC led to Solon's reforms, which paved the way for democracy under Cleisthenes in 508 BC. Athens became a leading naval power, helping to defeat the Persian Empire in the Greco-Persian Wars. Its Golden Age under Pericles saw the construction of the Parthenon and the flourishing of drama, philosophy, and history. Imperial ambitions via the Delian League led to the Peloponnesian War, which Athens lost to Sparta. Despite later defeats by Macedon, its cultural influence remained profound.

### Roman, Byzantine, and Ottoman Rule
Under Rome, Athens was a free city famed for its schools. The city declined after the Western Roman Empire fell and was sacked by Germanic tribes in 267 AD. It remained a centre of learning, especially Neoplatonism, until Emperor Justinian I banned pagan philosophy in 529 AD. As part of the Byzantine Empire, Athens experienced periods of growth and was sacked by Slavs in 582 and Saracens in 896. After the Fourth Crusade (1204), it was ruled by Latin Crusaders until the Ottoman conquest in 1458. Under Ottoman rule, the city's population and importance declined sharply. The Parthenon was severely damaged in 1687 when a Venetian bombardment ignited Ottoman gunpowder stored inside.

### Modern Capital
Athens was captured by Greek insurgents in 1822 during the War of Independence and became the capital of the new Greek state in 1834. Designed as a neoclassical capital, it grew slowly until the 1920s, when refugees from Asia Minor swelled its population. The city hosted the first modern Olympics in 1896. It suffered under Axis occupation and famine during World War II, followed by civil war. Rapid, often unplanned, growth in the 1950s-60s led to severe pollution, which was significantly mitigated by infrastructure projects in the 1990s and for the 2004 Olympics.

### Geography and Climate
Athens sprawls across the Attica Basin, bounded by four mountains. It has a hot-summer Mediterranean climate (Köppen: Csa) and is known for intense summer heat due to an urban heat island effect. The city's geography contributes to temperature inversions that can trap pollution, though air quality has improved since the 1990s.

### Economy and Transport
Athens is Greece's financial capital and a major economic centre in Southeast Europe. Its port, Piraeus, is Europe's second-busiest passenger port. The city is a major transport hub with an international airport, an extensive metro and tram network, and motorways connecting it to the rest of Greece. Tourism is a leading economic contributor.

### Culture and Society
Athens is a global archaeological hub, home to the National Archaeological Museum and the Acropolis Museum. Its architectural landscape blends ancient, neoclassical, and modern styles, with strict height laws preserving views of the Acropolis. The city has a vibrant cultural scene with numerous theatres, concert halls, and entertainment districts. It is home to major universities and is the centre of Greek sports, with clubs like Panathinaikos and Olympiacos.

## Terms
*   **Acropolis:** The ancient citadel on a rocky outcrop above the city, containing the Parthenon and other significant buildings.
*   **Attica Basin:** The central plain where Athens is located, bounded by mountains.
*   **Delian League:** An association of Greek city-states led by Athens, originally to fight Persia, which evolved into an Athenian empire.
*   **Frankokratia:** The period of Latin (Western European) rule in Greece following the Fourth Crusade (1204).
*   **Hellenistic World:** The Greek-influenced cultures and regions that emerged after the conquests of Alexander the Great.
*   **Kizlar Agha:** The chief black eunuch of the Ottoman Sultan's harem, who administered Athens in the 17th–18th centuries.
*   **Neoplatonism:** A philosophical school of antiquity that blended Platonic and other influences, which flourished in Athens.
*   **Peloponnesian War:** A major ancient Greek conflict (431–404 BC) between the Delian League (led by Athens) and the Peloponnesian League (led by Sparta).

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact impact of Emperor Justinian I's 529 AD ban on pagan philosophy teaching in Athens is much debated by historians.
*   The origin of the city's name is debated; modern scholars generally agree the goddess Athena was named after the city, not vice versa, but the name's root is possibly pre-Greek.
