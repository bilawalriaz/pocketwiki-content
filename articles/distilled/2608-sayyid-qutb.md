# Sayyid Qutb

## Overview
Sayyid Qutb (1906–1966) was an Egyptian author, educator, and leading member of the Muslim Brotherhood whose prison writings became foundational texts for modern Islamist ideology. His radical reinterpretation of Islamic concepts, particularly his diagnosis of contemporary Muslim societies as being in a state of *jahiliyyah* (pre-Islamic ignorance), provided the ideological framework for later militant jihadist movements. His execution by the Nasser regime cemented his status as a martyr for many Islamists.

## Timeline
- **9 October 1906** — Born in Musha, Asyut Province, Egypt.
- **1922** — Publishes first article in the literary magazine *al-Balagh*.
- **1948–1950** — Studies in the United States; publishes *Social Justice in Islam* (1949).
- **Early 1950s** — Joins the Muslim Brotherhood after returning to Egypt.
- **July 1952** — Welcomes the Free Officers' coup led by Gamal Abdel Nasser.
- **1954** — Imprisoned following an alleged plot to assassinate Nasser.
- **1964** — Released from prison; rearrested in August 1965.
- **29 August 1966** — Executed by hanging after a trial for plotting to overthrow the state.

## Body

### Early Life and Literary Career
Born to a politically active family, Qutb memorized the Quran by age 10 and developed a critical view of traditional religious education. He moved to Cairo, receiving a British-style education and beginning a career as a teacher and literary critic. His early work was secular, focusing on literature and education. A pivotal shift occurred after a two-year scholarship in the United States (1948–1950), where he developed a profound distaste for Western materialism, individualism, and what he perceived as its spiritual emptiness, as detailed in his essay "The America that I Have Seen."

### Ideological Transformation and Prison Writings
Qutb's experiences in America and the brutality he witnessed in Nasser's prisons (1954–1964) catalyzed a radical shift toward Islamism. He concluded that both Western civilization and secular Arab nationalism were forms of *jahiliyyah*. His most influential works, the Quranic commentary *Fi Zilal al-Qur'an* (*In the Shade of the Qur'an*) and the manifesto *Ma'alim fi al-Tariq* (*Milestones*), were composed in prison. In *Milestones*, he argued that the entire Muslim world had reverted to *jahiliyyah* because it had abandoned God's sovereignty (*Hakamiyya*) for human-made laws. He called for a revolutionary vanguard to overthrow these un-Islamic systems and implement a complete social and political order based on *sharia* (Islamic law).

### Political Philosophy and Legacy
Qutb's mature philosophy demanded the total implementation of *sharia* as the sole basis for governance, rejecting both Western democracy and secular nationalism. He advocated for offensive jihad as a necessary means to abolish the *jahili* system. His execution made him a martyr, and his ideas profoundly influenced subsequent generations of Islamists. His brother, Muhammad Qutb, promoted his work in Saudi Arabia, where it influenced figures like Ayman al-Zawahiri and Osama bin Laden. Qutb's thought also impacted the 1979 Iranian Revolution, with his works translated into Persian by figures like Ali Khamenei, though later Iranian scholars have often criticized his ideas as extremist.

## Terms
- **Jahiliyyah**: Qutb's term for a state of pre-Islamic ignorance and barbarism, which he applied to all contemporary societies, including Muslim ones, that did not fully implement divine law.
- **Sharia**: Islamic law derived from the Quran and Sunna, which Qutb argued should be the complete and sole basis for all aspects of life and governance.
- **Tawhid**: The Islamic principle of the oneness of God, which Qutb linked directly to political sovereignty (*Hakamiyya*), meaning God alone should rule.
- **Hakamiyya**: The concept of God's absolute sovereignty and right to legislate, which Qutb argued was violated by any human-made legal or political system.
- **Vanguard**: A revolutionary elite, inspired by Leninist theory, tasked with leading the struggle to re-establish an Islamic society through preaching and jihad.
- **Takfir**: The act of declaring a Muslim to be an apostate. Qutb's ideas were seen as providing a basis for widespread *takfir* of secular Muslims and rulers.
- **Shura**: Consultation, a Quranic principle often cited by Islamists to support democratic ideas. Qutb argued it only meant the ruler should consult some of the ruled, not that it mandated elections.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origins of Radicalism**: Scholars debate whether Qutb's radicalism stemmed primarily from his experiences in American society, the torture he endured in Nasser's prisons, or a longer intellectual evolution.
- **Interpretation of *Milestones***: There is significant disagreement over whether Qutb's call for a vanguard and jihad was a literal blueprint for violent revolution or a more metaphorical call for spiritual and social reform.
- **Theological Legitimacy**: Mainstream Sunni scholars, such as those at Al-Azhar, have declared Qutb's ideas heretical (*deviant*), particularly his sweeping *takfir* of Muslim societies and his rejection of centuries of Islamic scholarship.
- **Influence vs. Distortion**: Some scholars, like John Calvert, argue that the association of Qutb solely with al-Qaeda and terrorism "monopolizes and distorts" his broader contribution to Islamic political thought and social criticism.
