# Slavery in Africa

## Overview
Slavery in historical Africa encompassed diverse forms, from domestic servitude and debt bondage to chattel slavery and military conscription. Its scale and nature were transformed by the trans-Saharan, Indian Ocean, and Atlantic slave trades, which exported millions of Africans and reshaped economies and societies. The eventual abolition of these trades and internal slavery, driven by external pressure and internal resistance, was a protracted process whose legacy continues to influence demographic, economic, and social patterns.

- **8th–9th centuries AD** — Muslim participation in the African slave trade expands.
- **1441** — Portuguese explorer Antão Gonçalves buys enslaved Africans in Guinea.
- **1501** — First enslaved Africans arrive in Hispaniola in the Americas.
- **1658** — Formal slave system begins at the Dutch Cape Colony.
- **1807** — Britain passes the Abolition of the Slave Trade Act.
- **1833** — Britain passes the Slavery Abolition Act, freeing slaves in most colonies.
- **1889–90** — Brussels Anti-Slavery Conference focuses on suppressing slavery.
- **1926** — League of Nations adopts the Slavery Convention.
- **1960s–1970s** — Chattel slavery is formally abolished in Saudi Arabia, Yemen, and Oman.

## Body

### Forms and Practices
Slavery in Africa was not monolithic. It included **chattel slavery**, where slaves were property (common in the Nile Valley and North Africa); **domestic service**, where slaves lived within households and retained some freedoms; **pawnship** (debt bondage), prevalent in West Africa; and **military slavery**, where conscripted units served patrons. Slaves were also used for sacrifice, particularly in 19th-century states like Dahomey. The relationship between slaves and masters was often complex, influenced by kinship structures, with some slaves eventually being integrated into the master's lineage.

### Regional Practices
Slavery varied by region. In North Africa, it dates to ancient Egypt and persisted through Roman, Islamic, and Ottoman periods, with Barbary pirates capturing Europeans. In West Africa, slavery expanded dramatically with the Atlantic trade, with states like the Ashanti and Dahomey heavily involved. In the Horn of Africa, Ethiopian Christian kings and Somali Muslim sultanates enslaved Nilotic and Bantu peoples. The Great Lakes and Southeast African regions were deeply involved in the Indian Ocean trade, with Zanzibar as a major hub. In Southern Africa, the Dutch Cape Colony established a formal slave society, while Portuguese Mozambique became a key export center.

### The Trans-Saharan, Indian Ocean, and Atlantic Slave Trades
These trades were transformative forces. The trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trades, driven by Arab and later European merchants, moved millions of Africans to the Middle East, North Africa, and Asia over centuries. The Atlantic slave trade, beginning in the 15th century, forcibly transported an estimated 12.8 million people to the Americas between 1450 and 1900. Fueled by European demand for plantation labor, these trades created a massive export-oriented slave system. African elites and states often participated, with some economies becoming dependent. The trades caused significant demographic disruption, warfare, and economic reorientation within Africa.

### Abolition and Legacy
Abolition was a gradual, uneven process. European nations began banning the trade in the early 19th century, with Britain leading naval enforcement. Internal slavery was abolished later, often under colonial rule, though authorities frequently prioritized regulation over immediate freedom. The anti-slavery movement was used as justification for the "Scramble for Africa." Despite legal abolition, slavery and slavery-like practices persisted into the 20th century and, in some regions, continue illegally today. The demographic and economic effects are heavily debated, with scholars arguing over the long-term impact on Africa's development and the role of slave trade profits in European industrialization.

## Terms
- **Chattel slavery**: Slavery where the enslaved person is treated as property.
- **Pawnship**: Debt bondage where a person is pledged as collateral.
- **Mamluks**: Slave soldiers in the Islamic world who could rise to power.
- **Trans-Saharan slave trade**: Trade of enslaved Africans across the Sahara to North Africa and the Middle East.
- **Atlantic slave trade**: Forced transportation of enslaved Africans to the Americas, primarily 16th–19th centuries.
- **Zanj**: Arabic term for Bantu-speaking peoples from East Africa, often enslaved.
- **Assimilative slavery**: Slavery where individuals could be integrated into the master's kinship group.
- **Inboekstelsel**: 19th-century Southern African system where captured children were registered as laborers until adulthood.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Demographic Impact**: Scholars debate the scale of population loss and its long-term effects. Walter Rodney argued it was a demographic disaster that permanently disadvantaged Africa, while others like David Eltis compare it to European emigration rates and question its relative impact.
- **Economic Effect**: There is debate on whether the slave trade devastated African economies by disrupting production and political stability, or whether it also stimulated certain trade and brought in goods that raised living standards.
- **Role in European Industrialization**: The "Williams thesis" argues that profits from the slave trade and slavery were crucial to financing Britain's Industrial Revolution. This is contested by historians who claim the profits were a small fraction of the British economy.
- **Abolition Motives**: Historians debate whether abolition was primarily driven by humanitarian moralism or by changing economic incentives and imperial strategies.
