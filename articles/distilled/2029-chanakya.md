# Chanakya

## Overview
Chanakya was a legendary Brahmin scholar and strategist credited with orchestrating the rise of Chandragupta Maurya and the founding of the Maurya Empire in ancient India. While no contemporary historical records of him exist, his story is preserved in four major legendary narratives from the 4th to 11th centuries CE. He is traditionally identified with Kauṭilya, the purported author of the *Arthashastra*, a foundational treatise on statecraft and economics, though modern scholarship considers this identification a later development.

## Timeline
- **c. 4th–3rd cent. BCE** — Traditional period of Chanakya and Chandragupta's activities.
- **4th–8th cent. CE** — Composition of Vishakhadatta's play *Mudrarakshasa*.
- **5th–6th cent. CE** — Earliest Buddhist source for the legend, the *Mahavamsa*, is composed.
- **11th cent. CE** — Kashmiri versions of the legend appear in *Kathasaritsagara* and *Brihat-Katha-Manjari*.
- **12th cent. CE** — Jain version by Hemachandra, *Parishishtaparvan*, is written.
- **150 BCE–50 CE** — Oldest textual layers of the *Arthashastra* are composed.
- **50–125 CE** — The "Kauṭilya Recension" of the *Arthashastra* is compiled.
- **175–300 CE** — The *Arthashastra* undergoes its final "Śāstric Redaction."
- **1905** — The manuscript of the *Arthashastra* is rediscovered by Rudrapatna Shamasastry.

## Body

### Legendary Narratives
There is no documented historical information about Chanakya; all accounts are legendary. Scholar Thomas Trautmann identifies four distinct versions of the Chanakya-Chandragupta legend. In all versions, Chanakya is insulted by the Nanda king, vows revenge, and installs Chandragupta as the new emperor after the Nanda's overthrow.

The **Buddhist version** (5th–6th cent. CE) portrays Chanakya as a Brahmin from Takshashila who, after being humiliated by Emperor Dhana Nanda, discovers the young Chandragupta playing "king" among children. He trains Chandragupta, initially fails to conquer the empire by attacking the capital directly, and succeeds only after learning to conquer border villages first. The narrative includes the story of Chanakya saving the unborn Bindusara by cutting open the dying Empress Durdhara's womb.

The **Jain version** (12th cent. CE, based on 1st–8th cent. CE sources) presents Chanakya as a lay Jain (*shravaka*) who is kicked off the emperor's throne by a servant. He finds Chandragupta, tests his loyalty, and forms an alliance with King Parvataka. After conquering the Nanda empire, Parvataka is killed by a *visha kanya* (poison girl), leaving Chandragupta as sole ruler. This version details Chanakya's consolidation of power and his eventual death by fire, orchestrated by the jealous minister Subandhu.

The **Kashmiri version** (11th cent. CE) features a minister named Shakatala, whose 100 sons starve to death so he can live for revenge. Shakatala uses the vengeful Chanakya to destroy a usurper emperor who had possessed the real Nanda's body.

The **Mudrarakshasa version** (4th–8th cent. CE) is a largely fictional Sanskrit play. It focuses on Chanakya's intricate political and espionage maneuvers to defeat the Nanda minister Rakshasa and secure Chandragupta's throne, culminating in Rakshasa's surrender and appointment as Chandragupta's prime minister.

### Identification with Kauṭilya and the Arthashastra
Chanakya is conventionally identified with Kauṭilya, the author of the *Arthashastra*. However, contemporary scholarship rejects this as a later development. The *Arthashastra* is now considered a compilation of multiple texts from the early centuries of the Common Era, centuries after the Mauryan period. The identification likely served to add prestige to the work. The text's author is named Kauṭilya, with one verse referring to him as Vishnugupta. The link to the Mauryan prime minister is made in the text's penultimate paragraph, which is considered a later interpolation. The Guptas may have promoted this connection to legitimize their rule as successors to the Mauryas.

## Terms
- **Arthashastra**: An ancient Indian treatise on statecraft, economic policy, and military strategy, attributed to Kauṭilya.
- **Nanda dynasty**: The ruling dynasty of Magadha that preceded the Maurya Empire.
- **Maurya Empire**: The first pan-Indian empire, founded by Chandragupta Maurya with Chanakya's guidance.
- **Pataliputra**: The capital city of the Nanda and Maurya empires.
- **Visha kanya**: A "poison girl," a woman trained to assassinate through physical contact.
- **Svayamvara**: A practice where a woman chooses her husband from among assembled suitors.
- **Shravaka**: A lay follower in Jainism.
- **Shikha**: The topknot worn by Brahmins, symbolizing their religious vows.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historicity**: The existence of Chanakya as a historical individual is unverified; he is known only through legendary narratives.
- **Authorship of the Arthashastra**: The identification of Chanakya with Kauṭilya, the text's author, is widely rejected by modern scholars as a later tradition.
- **Age of the Arthashastra**: The text's composition is debated, with scholarly consensus placing its final form between 175–300 CE, not the Mauryan period.
- **Relationship between legends**: Scholars debate which version of the Chanakya-Chandragupta legend is oldest and most historically reliable, with some arguing the Jain version is more consistent.
