# East–West Schism

## Overview
The East–West Schism, or Great Schism, is the formal break of communion between the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox Churches beginning in 1054. It culminates centuries of political, cultural, and theological divergence. The schism remains unresolved; both churches recognize each other as sister churches but not in full communion.

## Timeline
- **325** — Council of Nicaea establishes patriarchal order, with Rome first.
- **381** — Council of Constantinople elevates Constantinople to second honor after Rome.
- **451** — Council of Chalcedon grants Constantinople equal privileges to Rome (Canon 28), which Rome rejects.
- **1054** — Mutual excommunications between Cardinal Humbert (for Pope Leo IX) and Patriarch Michael I Cerularius.
- **1182** — Massacre of Latins in Constantinople deepens animosity.
- **1204** — Fourth Crusade sacks Constantinople, establishing a Latin Empire and patriarchate.
- **1274** — Second Council of Lyon attempts reunion; rejected by the East.
- **1439** — Council of Ferrara–Florence agrees to reunion; rejected by Eastern clergy and people.
- **1453** — Fall of Constantinople to the Ottomans; the Ecumenical Patriarchate becomes an autonomous millet.
- **1870** — First Vatican Council defines papal infallibility, rejected by the East.
- **1965** — Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras I nullify the 1054 excommunications.

## Body

### Underlying Causes and the 1054 Event
The schism resulted from long-standing ecclesiological and theological disputes, exacerbated by geopolitical tensions. Key issues included the *Filioque* clause, the use of leavened vs. unleavened bread in the Eucharist, and the scope of papal authority. The immediate trigger was the 1053 closure of Latin churches in Constantinople by Patriarch Michael I Cerularius. In 1054, papal legates led by Cardinal Humbert excommunicated Cerularius; he in turn excommunicated the legates. The validity of these acts is debated, as Pope Leo IX had died before their execution. The breach was not immediately recognized as permanent, but events like the 1204 Sack of Constantinople hardened the division.

### Ecclesiological Disputes
The core conflict is over church governance. The Catholic Church developed papal primacy, asserting the Pope's universal jurisdiction and infallibility (defined in 1870). The Eastern Orthodox Church maintains "eucharistic ecclesiology," where each local church with its bishop is fully the Church, and all bishops are ontologically equal. The Pope is seen as *primus inter pares* (first among equals) with a primacy of honor, not authority. This fundamental difference—centralized versus synodal authority—remains the primary obstacle to reunion.

### Theological Differences
While both sides accuse the other of heresy, the Catholic Church officially views the Orthodox as schismatic, not heretical. Key disputes include:
*   **The Trinity:** The West added *Filioque* ("and the Son") to the Creed, stating the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father *and the Son*. The East rejects this as a unilateral innovation, maintaining the Spirit proceeds from the Father alone.
*   **Original Sin and Salvation:** Eastern theology emphasizes ancestral sin as inherited sickness and death, while Western theology stresses inherited guilt. The Catholic dogma of the Immaculate Conception (1854) is rejected by the East.
*   **Theology and Experience:** Orthodox theologians argue Western theology relies too much on scholastic rationalism, while Eastern theology is grounded in the experiential knowledge of God (*theoria*) through practices like hesychasm.
*   **Purgatory and Hell:** The Catholic doctrine of purgatory is not accepted. Orthodox theology speaks of an intermediate state but rejects purifying fires.

### History After 1054 and Modern Relations
The schism deepened through the Crusades, the establishment of Latin hierarchies in the East, and the rise of competing national churches. Reunion was attempted at councils like Lyon (1274) and Florence (1439) but failed. In the 20th century, the Second Vatican Council (1962-65) initiated dialogue. In 1965, the 1054 excommunications were lifted. The Joint International Commission for Theological Dialogue began in 1980. While gestures of goodwill continue, deep divisions on papal authority and theology persist. The status of Eastern Catholic Churches (uniates) remains a point of contention.

## Terms
*   **Filioque:** Latin for "and (from) the Son," added to the Nicene Creed by the West, stating the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father *and the Son*. Rejected by the East.
*   **Eucharistic Ecclesiology:** The Eastern Orthodox view that each local church celebrating the Eucharist with its bishop constitutes the whole, catholic Church.
*   **Primus Inter Pares:** Latin for "first among equals," the Orthodox understanding of the Pope's role as a bishop with a primacy of honor, not universal jurisdiction.
*   **Theoria:** In Eastern theology, the direct, experiential vision or knowledge of God, achieved through ascetic practice.
*   **Hesychasm:** A mystical tradition in Eastern Orthodox monasticism emphasizing inner stillness and the Jesus Prayer to achieve *theoria*.
*   **Caesaropapism:** The subordination of church authority to the political authority of the emperor, a feature of the Byzantine Empire.
*   **Adiaphora:** Matters of Christian practice considered theologically indifferent—neither commanded nor forbidden by scripture.
*   **Uniate:** A historical term for Eastern Catholic Churches in communion with Rome but retaining Eastern liturgical practices; considered pejorative by many Orthodox.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **The Date of the Schism:** While 1054 is conventional, scholars debate whether the true break occurred later, such as in 1204 or even in the 18th century when sacramental communion was finally severed.
*   **The Nature of the Divide:** Catholics officially view the Orthodox as schismatic, while many Orthodox theologians view Catholic doctrines (e.g., *Filioque*, papal infallibility, Immaculate Conception) as heretical, making the divide theological.
*   **Prospects for Reunion:** The primary stumbling block is papal primacy. The Catholic "maximum demand" is Orthodox acceptance of the 1870 definition of papal primacy. The Orthodox "maximum demand" is Catholic rejection of that definition and the *Filioque*. Pope Benedict XVI suggested a compromise where the East accepts Catholic legitimacy in its second-millennium development, and the West accepts Eastern Orthodoxy in its traditional form, but this remains speculative.
