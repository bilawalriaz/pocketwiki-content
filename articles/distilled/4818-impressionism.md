# Impressionism

## Overview
Impressionism was a 19th-century French art movement that broke from academic tradition by emphasizing the immediate visual experience of a scene. It prioritized the effects of light, color, and movement over detailed realism, using visible brushstrokes and often painting outdoors (*en plein air*). The movement challenged the established art world and laid the groundwork for modern art, influencing music, literature, and global artistic practices.

## Timeline
- **1863** — The *Salon des Refusés* is held, showcasing rejected works including Manet's *Le déjeuner sur l'herbe*.
- **1874** — The first Impressionist exhibition is held at Nadar's studio; critic Louis Leroy coins the term "Impressionism" satirically.
- **1874-1886** — Eight independent Impressionist exhibitions are held in Paris.
- **1870s-1880s** — The movement gains prominence and public recognition despite initial critical hostility.
- **1880s** — Post-Impressionism begins to develop as artists like Van Gogh and Gauguin react against Impressionist tenets.
- **1886** — The eighth and final Impressionist exhibition is held, featuring Seurat's pointillist work.
- **1899** — Alfred Sisley, a core Impressionist, dies in poverty.
- **1986** — Tamar Garb's *Women Impressionists* is published, helping to rectify the historical omission of female artists from the movement.

## Body

### Origins and Rebellion
Impressionism emerged in 1860s Paris as a rebellion against the French *Académie des Beaux-Arts*, which dominated the official *Salon de Paris* and valued historical, religious, and portrait subjects rendered with precise, blended brushwork. A group of young painters, including Claude Monet, Pierre-Auguste Renoir, Alfred Sisley, and Frédéric Bazille, met while studying under Charles Gleyre. They shared an interest in painting contemporary life and landscapes directly from nature, influenced by artists like Eugène Boudin and Johan Jongkind. Their frequent meetings at the Café Guerbois were often led by the older Édouard Manet.

### The First Exhibition and Critical Response
After repeated Salon rejections, the artists founded the *Société anonyme des artistes peintres, sculpteurs, graveurs, etc.* to exhibit independently. Their first exhibition in April 1874 featured 30 artists. The critical response was largely hostile. Critic Louis Leroy's satirical review in *Le Charivari*, which mocked Claude Monet's *Impression, Sunrise*, inadvertently gave the movement its name. The artists eventually adopted the term "Impressionist," despite their diverse styles, united by a spirit of independence. The group exhibited together eight times between 1874 and 1886, though membership shifted, with artists like Cézanne and Renoir occasionally defecting to submit to the Salon.

### Techniques and Innovation
Impressionists developed a distinct technical approach to capture fleeting moments. They used short, thick brushstrokes (*impasto*) and applied pure, unmixed colors side-by-side to create vibrant optical effects, avoiding smooth blending. They often omitted black, creating dark tones by mixing complementary colors. A key innovation was painting *en plein air* (outdoors) to study natural light directly, often painting shadows with blue tones reflected from the sky. This was facilitated by new technology: pre-mixed paints in tin tubes and commercially available synthetic pigments like cobalt blue and cadmium yellow. They typically painted on light-colored grounds, a shift from the traditional dark grounds.

### Content and Composition
The Impressionists depicted modern, everyday life—middle-class leisure, urban scenes, and landscapes including factories—moving away from idealized historical or mythological subjects. Their compositions were often asymmetrical and cropped, resembling a snapshot, influenced by the rise of photography and Japanese *ukiyo-e* prints. Photography challenged painters to move beyond mere realistic reproduction, encouraging them to express subjective perception. This focus on the artist's personal vision of a moment, rather than a detailed record, was a fundamental shift.

### Female Impressionists
Women artists like Berthe Morisot, Mary Cassatt, Eva Gonzalès, and Marie Bracquemond faced significant social and professional barriers. They were excluded from the informal gatherings where ideas were formed and were often limited to domestic subjects. Despite this, Morisot and Cassatt participated in multiple exhibitions and developed individual styles. Their work often depicted women in domestic settings, but they avoided cliché, showing subjects engaged in activities or deep in thought. Their contributions were largely omitted from art historical textbooks until the late 20th century.

### Commercial Success and Legacy
The artists gained little financial reward from the early exhibitions, but their dealer, Paul Durand-Ruel, played a crucial role in promoting their work in France and abroad. By the early 1880s, some artists like Monet and Pissarro achieved financial security. Impressionist methods became commonplace in Salon art by the 1890s. The movement directly inspired Post-Impressionism and was a precursor to Fauvism and Cubism. Its influence spread globally, leading to national schools of Impressionism in countries like the United States, Australia, and Japan, and analogous movements in music (e.g., Debussy, Ravel) and literature.

## Terms
- **En plein air**: The practice of painting outdoors, directly from nature, to capture the effects of natural light.
- **Impasto**: A technique where paint is laid on an area of the surface in very thick layers, usually thick enough that the brush or painting-knife strokes are visible.
- **Salon de Paris**: The official annual art exhibition of the *Académie des Beaux-Arts* in Paris, which set the standards for artistic success in France.
- **Académie des Beaux-Arts**: The French institution that preserved traditional painting standards and controlled the Salon.
- **Simultaneous contrast**: A principle of color theory where placing two colors side-by-side makes them appear more vivid and distinct to the viewer.
- **Post-Impressionism**: A term for the art movement that followed Impressionism in the 1880s, characterized by a move away from naturalistic depiction toward symbolic content and emotional expression.
- **Japonism**: The influence of Japanese art, especially *ukiyo-e* woodblock prints, on Western art, noted for its bold colors, asymmetrical compositions, and flat areas of color.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise boundaries of the movement are debated, as the artists had diverse styles and temperaments, unified more by their independence than a single doctrine.
- The extent of the influence of visual Impressionism on its musical counterpart is debatable; composer Claude Debussy disavowed the term.
- The role and recognition of female Impressionists have been a subject of scholarly re-evaluation, as they were historically marginalized in art historical narratives.
- The categorization of artists like Paul Cézanne, who participated in early exhibitions but later developed a distinct style, illustrates the difficulty of applying fixed labels to the movement's evolution.
