# Tikal

## Overview
Tikal was one of the largest and most powerful city-states of the pre-Columbian Maya civilization, located in the rainforests of Guatemala's Petén region. It dominated the Maya lowlands politically, economically, and militarily during the Classic Period (c. 200–900 AD), interacting with distant powers like Teotihuacan. Its long dynastic history, monumental architecture, and eventual collapse make it a key site for understanding Maya civilization and its decline.

## Timeline
- **c. 1000 BC** — Earliest traces of agriculture at the site.
- **c. 400–300 BC** — Major construction begins in the Late Preclassic period.
- **1st century AD** — Dynastic rulership founded, possibly by Yax Ehb Xook.
- **14 January 378 AD** — King Chak Tok Ichʼaak is killed; Siyaj Kʼakʼ arrives from Teotihuacan.
- **13 September 379 AD** — Yax Nuun Ahiin I, son of Teotihuacan's ruler, installed as king.
- **426 AD** — Tikal founds the distant city of Copán.
- **562 AD** — Tikal is defeated by Calakmul and Caracol, beginning the "Tikal hiatus."
- **629 AD** — Tikal founds the military outpost of Dos Pilas.
- **682 AD** — Jasaw Chan Kʼawiil I ends the hiatus and resumes monument construction.
- **695 AD** — Jasaw Chan Kʼawiil I defeats Calakmul, ending its dominance.
- **810 AD** — Temple III, the last major pyramid, is built.
- **869 AD** — Stela 11, the last monument erected at Tikal, is dedicated.
- **10th century** — The site is largely abandoned.

## Body

### Etymology and Geography
The modern name "Tikal" likely derives from a Yucatec Maya phrase meaning "at the waterhole." Hieroglyphic inscriptions refer to the ancient city as **Yax Mutal** ("First Mutal"). The city is located in the Petén Basin, about 303 km north of Guatemala City. It covered over 16 square kilometers with about 3,000 structures built on limestone ridges above swampy lowlands. The site had no natural water sources and relied on a system of ten reservoirs for rainwater collection.

### Rise and Teotihuacan Influence
Tikal rose to prominence in the Early Classic period. A pivotal event occurred in 378 AD when the native king was killed and a new dynasty was installed by Siyaj Kʼakʼ, a general likely from the distant metropolis of **Teotihuacan**. This new dynasty, including Yax Nuun Ahiin I, rapidly Mayanized and made Tikal a key Teotihuacan ally. Tikal's power expanded, founding colonies like Dos Pilas and influencing distant cities like Copán. This era saw the construction of fortifications and the establishment of a regional trade network.

### Rivalry, Hiatus, and Resurgence
A long-running rivalry with the city of **Calakmul** defined much of Tikal's history. In 562 AD, Tikal was comprehensively defeated by a Calakmul-Caracol alliance, leading to the "Tikal hiatus"—a period of over a century with no new monuments or inscriptions. The hiatus ended in 682 AD when Jasaw Chan Kʼawiil I resumed construction. In 695 AD, he decisively defeated Calakmul, capturing its king and ending its supremacy. This victory ushered in Tikal's final period of architectural flourishing under rulers like Yikʼin Chan Kʼawiil.

### Collapse and Abandonment
By the 9th century, the Classic Maya collapse affected Tikal. Population peaked and then sharply declined, likely due to overpopulation, agrarian failure, and environmental degradation. Reservoirs became contaminated with mercury from cinnabar paint and algae blooms. Central authority collapsed, satellite cities declared independence, and the last monument was erected in 869 AD. The elite palaces were burned, and by the end of the 10th century, the city was abandoned to the rainforest.

### Modern Exploration and Significance
The ruins were first explored by a Guatemalan expedition in 1848. Tikal National Park was established in 1955. Major excavations by the University of Pennsylvania (1956–1970) mapped the city and restored key structures. In 1979, it was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Tikal is one of Guatemala's most important tourist attractions and has been used as a filming location for movies like *Star Wars*.

## Terms
- **Classic Period:** The period of Maya civilization from c. 200 to 900 AD, characterized by the peak of city-states, monumental architecture, and hieroglyphic writing.
- **Emblem Glyph:** A hieroglyphic title identifying a specific polity or kingdom; Tikal's was Mutul.
- **Kʼatun:** A unit of time in the Maya Long Count calendar, equal to 20 years.
- **Kaloomteʼ:** A high-ranking royal title, possibly meaning "high king," used by rulers of Tikal and Calakmul.
- **Tikal Hiatus:** A period from the late 6th to late 7th century with a lapse in monument construction and inscriptions at Tikal, following its defeat.
- **Sacbeob (causeways):** Raised limestone roads linking different parts of a Maya city.
- **Talud-tablero:** An architectural style featuring a sloping panel (talud) topped by a vertical panel (tablero), characteristic of Teotihuacan.
- **Chultun:** A subterranean, bottle-shaped chamber used for storage.

## Debates and Open questions
- **Population Estimates:** Figures for Tikal's peak population vary widely, from 10,000 to as high as 90,000 inhabitants, with some archaeologists believing the higher estimates are too extreme.
- **Cause of Collapse:** While overpopulation and agrarian failure are primary theories, recent research suggests a meteorological drought and the contamination of water reservoirs with mercury and cyanobacteria may have been critical factors.
- **Nature of Earthworks:** The purpose of Tikal's extensive earthworks is debated; originally thought to be defensive, recent research suggests they may have primarily served as a water collection system.
