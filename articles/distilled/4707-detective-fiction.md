# Detective fiction

## Overview
Detective fiction is a genre of crime and mystery fiction centered on an investigator solving a crime, most often murder. It emerged in the mid-19th century and has remained a globally popular literary form, shaping cultural archetypes of the rational investigator and influencing how societies think about justice, evidence, and truth.

## Timeline
- **c. 13th century** — "The Three Apples" in *One Thousand and One Nights* is considered an early detective story archetype.
- **1748** — Voltaire's *Zadig* features a protagonist performing feats of analysis.
- **1841** — Edgar Allan Poe publishes "The Murders in the Rue Morgue," introducing C. Auguste Dupin, the first fictional detective.
- **1868** — Émile Gaboriau publishes *Monsieur Lecoq*, pioneering the detective minutely examining a crime scene.
- **1868** — Wilkie Collins publishes *The Moonstone*, often called the first great English detective novel.
- **1878** — Anna Katharine Green's *The Leavenworth Case* popularizes the genre in America.
- **1887** — Arthur Conan Doyle creates Sherlock Holmes in *A Study in Scarlet*.
- **1920s-1930s** — The "Golden Age" of detective fiction, dominated by British writers like Agatha Christie and Dorothy L. Sayers.
- **1929** — Ronald Knox codifies rules for detective fiction in his "Decalogue."
- **1930s** — Dashiell Hammett and Raymond Chandler pioneer the American "hardboiled" subgenre.
- **1965** — Satyajit Ray creates the Bengali detective Feluda.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
The genre's roots are debated, with scholars noting precursors in ancient texts like the biblical story of Susanna and the Elders, though some critics argue these are merely puzzles. Early Arabic literature, particularly *One Thousand and One Nights*, contains stories like "The Three Apples" that feature a crime, an investigation, and a resolution, anticipating modern detective fiction. In China, the *Gong'an* (court case) fiction genre featured historical judges like Bao Gong solving crimes, differing from Western traditions by often revealing the criminal at the start and incorporating supernatural elements.

The English-language genre is conventionally considered to have begun with Edgar Allan Poe's "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" (1841), which established the archetype of the brilliant, eccentric detective using "ratiocination" (complex reasoning). This was followed by pioneers like Wilkie Collins, whose *The Moonstone* (1868) established many classic conventions of the detective novel, including the "locked room" mystery and a cast of false suspects. Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes stories, beginning in 1887, became the most famous early example, defining the consulting detective who uses observation and deduction.

### The Golden Age and Global Spread
The interwar period (1920s-1930s) is known as the Golden Age of Detective Fiction, characterized by the "whodunit" puzzle plot, where the reader is challenged to identify the murderer from a closed circle of suspects. Authors like Agatha Christie, Dorothy L. Sayers, and Ngaio Marsh standardized conventions, often set in country houses. Christie, the best-selling novelist of all time at her death, created iconic detectives like Hercule Poirot and Miss Marple.

The genre spread globally, adapting to local cultures. In China, translations of Sherlock Holmes in the 1890s inspired native writers like Cheng Xiaoqing, who created the detective Huo Sang. In Japan, Edogawa Rampo founded the modern mystery genre, later evolving into the "social school" of Seicho Matsumoto. In India, characters like Byomkesh Bakshi (1932) and Feluda (1965) became cultural icons.

### Subgenres and Modern Evolution
Several distinct subgenres emerged. The "hardboiled" school, pioneered by Dashiell Hammett and Raymond Chandler in the 1930s, featured cynical private investigators in gritty urban settings, focusing on social realism over puzzle-solving. The "police procedural" realistically depicts the work of law enforcement. The "inverted" detective story, or "howcatchem," reveals the crime and perpetrator at the start, focusing on the investigation.

Modern detective fiction often explores social issues through its mysteries, as seen in the works of Tony Hillerman (Native American communities) and Walter Mosley (African American communities). The genre also faces criticism for implausible coincidences (e.g., amateur detectives constantly encountering murder) and the impact of technology like mobile phones on traditional plot devices.

## Terms
- **Whodunit:** A plot-driven mystery where the reader is given clues to deduce the perpetrator's identity before the final reveal.
- **Hardboiled:** A subgenre featuring a cynical, tough private investigator in a realistic, often violent urban setting.
- **Police procedural:** A subgenre focusing on the realistic, day-to-day work of police officers solving crimes.
- **Locked room mystery:** A puzzle where a crime is committed under seemingly impossible circumstances, such as in a sealed room.
- **Ratiocination:** The process of complex, logical reasoning used by detectives like Dupin and Holmes to solve crimes.
- **Golden Age:** The period from the 1920s to the 1930s, considered the classic era of the whodunit puzzle plot.
- **Inverted detective story:** A narrative that reveals the crime and criminal at the beginning, focusing on how the detective solves it.
- **Gong'an fiction:** An early Chinese genre of detective stories centered on a magistrate-judge solving cases.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the true origins of detective fiction, with arguments for ancient texts, early Arabic stories, or the 19th-century Western works of Poe and Collins. There is also contention over which work is the "first" English detective novel, with candidates including *The Moonstone*, *The Notting Hill Mystery* (1862-63), and *The Trail of the Serpent* (1861). The genre continually grapples with the tension between puzzle-driven plots and social realism, and how technological advancements affect the plausibility of traditional mystery narratives.
