# Norman Borlaug

## Overview
Norman Borlaug (1914–2009) was an American agronomist whose development of high-yield, disease-resistant wheat varieties is credited with saving over a billion people from starvation and sparking the Green Revolution. His work dramatically increased food production in Mexico, South Asia, and later Africa, fundamentally altering global agriculture and food security.

## Timeline
- **1914** — Born in Saude, Iowa.
- **1937** — Earned B.S. in forestry from University of Minnesota.
- **1942** — Earned PhD in plant pathology and genetics.
- **1944** — Moved to Mexico to head the Cooperative Wheat Research and Production Program.
- **1953** — Acquired Japanese dwarf wheat Norin 10, key to developing semi-dwarf varieties.
- **1963** — Mexico became a net wheat exporter.
- **1965** — Imported 550 tons of seed to Pakistan and India amid food shortages.
- **1968** — Wheat yields in Pakistan and India nearly doubled from 1965 levels.
- **1970** — Awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for contributions to world food supply.
- **1984** — Began teaching at Texas A&M University; later helped launch African agricultural projects.
- **2006** — Received the Congressional Gold Medal.
- **2009** — Died in Dallas, Texas.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Borlaug grew up on an Iowa farm and was influenced by his grandfather to pursue education. After initially failing the University of Minnesota entrance exam, he earned a forestry degree in 1937. A lecture by plant pathologist E.C. Stakman redirected his focus to plant pathology, leading to his PhD in 1942. He worked briefly at DuPont before being recruited in 1944 to lead a wheat research program in Mexico, a joint venture of the Rockefeller Foundation and the Mexican government.

### Wheat Research in Mexico
In Mexico, Borlaug faced initial hostility from farmers due to crop losses from stem rust. He pioneered "shuttle breeding," growing wheat in two distinct seasons and locations (central highlands and Sonora) to accelerate development and create adaptable varieties. He developed multiline wheat varieties—mixtures of pure lines with different disease resistance genes—to combat evolving pathogens. A breakthrough came in 1953 with the acquisition of the Japanese semi-dwarf wheat Norin 10. Borlaug crossed it with his disease-resistant strains to create high-yield, lodging-resistant varieties like Pitic 62. By 1963, 95% of Mexico's wheat used these semi-dwarf varieties, making the country self-sufficient and a net exporter.

### The Green Revolution in South Asia
In the mid-1960s, Borlaug's seeds were introduced to India and Pakistan, which faced severe food shortages. Despite logistical nightmares—including customs delays, the Watts riots, and the outbreak of the Indo-Pakistani War—the seeds were planted. Initial yields were record-breaking. By 1968, wheat yields in Pakistan had nearly doubled (from 4.6 to 7.3 million tons), and India's yields rose from 12.3 to 20.1 million tons. This success, termed the "Green Revolution" by William Gaud in 1968, averted predicted mass famines. Borlaug was awarded the 1970 Nobel Peace Prize for this work, which he accepted as a symbol of agriculture's role in peace.

### Later Work and the Borlaug Hypothesis
Borlaug later applied his methods to Africa through the Sasakawa Africa Association, though progress was hampered by a lack of infrastructure. He advocated for the "Borlaug hypothesis": that increasing yields on existing farmland reduces pressure to convert forests into new cropland. He was a staunch supporter of agricultural biotechnology and pesticides, arguing they were essential for feeding a growing population, a stance that drew criticism from environmentalists who cited issues like increased debt for farmers and reduced biodiversity.

### Criticisms and Legacy
Critics, including journalist John Vidal and activist Vandana Shiva, argued the Green Revolution increased social inequality, debt, and environmental harm. Borlaug dismissed most critiques, stating his work was a necessary step, not a utopia. He warned that without continued yield increases and population control, future food shortages would be catastrophic. He founded the World Food Prize in 1986 and remained active in research until his death in 2009.

## Terms
- **Green Revolution**: The period of significant increases in agricultural production, particularly in developing nations, during the mid-20th century, driven by high-yield crop varieties and modern techniques.
- **Semi-dwarf wheat**: Wheat varieties bred to be shorter and stronger-stalked to support larger grain heads without collapsing (lodging) under the weight of fertilizer-induced growth.
- **Shuttle breeding**: A technique of growing crops in two different locations and seasons per year to accelerate breeding cycles and develop adaptable varieties.
- **Multiline varieties**: Mixtures of several genetically similar pure plant lines, each with different disease resistance genes, grown together to minimize crop loss from evolving pathogens.
- **Borlaug hypothesis**: The idea that increasing agricultural productivity on existing farmland can help control deforestation by reducing the demand to clear new land for farming.
- **CIMMYT**: The International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center (Centro Internacional de Mejoramiento de Maíz y Trigo), the research institution in Mexico where Borlaug worked.
- **Norin 10**: A Japanese dwarf wheat variety whose genes were crucial for developing Borlaug's high-yield, semi-dwarf wheat.
- **Ug99**: A strain of wheat stem rust fungus identified as a major global threat, which Borlaug helped mobilize international research against.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Impact on Inequality**: Did the Green Revolution primarily benefit large agribusinesses and exacerbate debt and inequality among smallholder farmers?
- **Environmental Cost**: To what extent did the reliance on chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and monocultures cause long-term environmental damage?
- **Population vs. Technology**: Borlaug initially stressed curbing population growth but later emphasized technology's capacity to feed 10 billion people. This shift reflects an ongoing debate about whether food security is primarily a production or distribution/population issue.
- **Applicability in Africa**: Why did Borlaug's methods achieve rapid success in Asia but face greater obstacles in Africa, and what does this reveal about the necessary preconditions (e.g., infrastructure, markets) for agricultural transformation?
