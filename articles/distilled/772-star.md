# Star

## Overview  
A star is a self-gravitating, luminous sphere of plasma whose energy comes from nuclear fusion in its core. Stars form from collapsing molecular clouds, shine for most of their lives by fusing hydrogen into helium, and end as white dwarfs, neutron stars, or black holes depending on their initial mass. They are the primary factories of elements heavier than lithium and shape the structure and chemical evolution of galaxies.

## Body  

### Formation and Evolution  
Stars form when regions of molecular clouds become gravitationally unstable (Jeans instability), often triggered by shock waves from nearby massive stars or cloud collisions. Collapse produces a protostar surrounded by a protoplanetary disk; gravitational energy heats the core until hydrogen fusion ignites. Pre-main-sequence stars (T Tauri for <2 M☉, Herbig Ae/Be for higher masses) emit jets that help disperse surrounding gas. Stellar evolution is governed primarily by initial mass: very low-mass stars (<0.5 M☉) are fully convective and evolve directly into helium white dwarfs; low-mass stars (0.5–2.25 M☉) become red giants, undergo helium flash, and shed planetary nebulae; intermediate-mass stars (2.25–8 M☉) ignite helium without a flash; massive stars (>8 M☉) fuse successively heavier elements up to iron, then explode as supernovae.

### Structure and Physics  
A stable star maintains hydrostatic equilibrium between inward gravity and outward pressure from core fusion. Energy flows outward via radiation in the radiation zone and convection in the convection zone. The photosphere is the visible surface; above it lie the chromosphere, transition region, and corona—superheated plasma whose existence depends on a convective envelope. Nuclear fusion converts mass to energy via E=mc². In Sun-like stars, the proton-proton chain dominates; massive stars use the carbon-nitrogen-oxygen cycle. Heavier elements form via the triple-alpha process and subsequent burning stages (neon, oxygen, silicon), ending in iron-56 production.

### Classification and Observation  
Stars are classified by spectral type (O, B, A, F, G, K, M) based on temperature and absorption-line patterns, with luminosity classes (I–VII) indicating size. The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram plots luminosity against temperature, revealing evolutionary tracks. Apparent magnitude measures observed brightness; absolute magnitude (at 10 parsecs) reflects intrinsic luminosity. Stellar properties—mass, age, metallicity, distance, motion—are derived from spectra, photometry, and astrometry.

### Distribution and Multiplicity  
Stars form in groups within galaxies; most are in binary or multiple systems. Binary interactions (Roche lobe overflow, mass transfer) drive phenomena like Type Ia supernovae and blue stragglers. Star clusters (open and globular) contain coeval populations. Stellar kinematics—radial velocity and proper motion—reveal galactic structure and stellar origins. The nearest star to Earth after the Sun is Proxima Centauri at 4.25 light-years.

## Terms  
- **Main sequence**: Phase where stars fuse hydrogen in their cores; accounts for ~90% of stellar lifetime.  
- **Hydrostatic equilibrium**: Balance between gravitational contraction and outward pressure from fusion.  
- **Jeans instability**: Critical density threshold at which a gas cloud collapses under self-gravity.  
- **Spectral classification**: System categorizing stars by temperature and spectral-line features (O–M).  
- **Metallicity**: Abundance of elements heavier than helium in a star; influences evolution and planet formation.  
- **Protostar**: Early stage of star formation before sustained hydrogen fusion begins.  
- **Hertzsprung-Russell (HR) diagram**: Plot of stellar luminosity vs. temperature, used to study evolution.  
- **Supernova**: Catastrophic explosion of a massive star’s core, producing heavy elements and compact remnants.  
- **White dwarf**: Dense stellar remnant supported by electron degeneracy pressure; end state of low- to intermediate-mass stars.  
- **Stellar nucleosynthesis**: Process by which stars create elements heavier than lithium through nuclear fusion.

## Debates and Open Questions  
The role of binary interactions in massive star evolution remains disputed: some argue single-star models cannot explain observed populations of luminous blue variables and Wolf–Rayet stars, while others propose mass transfer in binaries as the key mechanism. The exact upper mass limit for star formation (~150 M☉) is uncertain, with some observations suggesting mergers in dense clusters may bypass this limit. The nature of Population III stars—the first generation after the Big Bang—is still being investigated, with recent evidence pointing to their existence in high-redshift galaxies. Whether all stars initially form as binaries is debated, though recent studies of star-forming regions suggest a high binary fraction.
