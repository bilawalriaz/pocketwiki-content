# Coal

## Overview
Coal is a combustible sedimentary rock formed from ancient plant matter (peat) subjected to heat and pressure over millions of years, a process called coalification. It is primarily carbon with variable hydrogen, sulfur, oxygen, and nitrogen. Coal powered the Industrial Revolution and remains a dominant energy source, supplying roughly a quarter of global primary energy and over a third of electricity as of 2020. However, its extraction and combustion are the largest single source of anthropogenic carbon dioxide, driving climate change, and cause severe air pollution responsible for an estimated 800,000 premature deaths annually. While international agreements target a phase-down of unabated coal use to meet Paris Agreement goals, consumption hit a record in 2024, driven largely by China (which mines half the world’s coal) and India, though peak demand is expected before 2030.

## Timeline
- **25,000–23,000 BC** — Oldest intentional use of black coal documented at Ostrava, Petřkovice (Czech Republic).
- **4000 BC** — Neolithic inhabitants in Shenyang, China, carve ornaments from black lignite.
- **1000 BC** — Coal from Fushun mine (China) used to smelt copper.
- **c. 371–287 BC** — Theophrastus writes *On Stones*, earliest European reference to coal as fuel.
- **2nd Century AD** — Romans exploiting major coalfields in England and Wales; used for iron smelting and heating.
- **1253** — Charter of King Henry III references "Seacoal Lane" in London; coal shipped from Newcastle for Westminster Abbey by 1257.
- **13th Century** — Exhaustion of surface coal drives development of underground shaft mining and adits ("pitcoal").
- **1700** — Britain mines five-sixths of world’s coal; steam engine begins displacing water wheels.
- **1947** — UK coal mining workforce peaks at ~750,000 miners.
- **2015** — Last deep coal mine in the UK closes.
- **2020** — UN Secretary-General calls for end to new coal plants; coal supplies ~25% of world primary energy.
- **2021** — Glasgow Climate Pact agrees to "phase down" unabated coal power.
- **2024** — Record global coal consumption; ~15 Gt CO₂ emitted (over 25% of global GHG). Peak demand expected before 2030.

## Body

### Formation and Coalification
Coal forms from dead vegetation in low-lying wetlands (coal forests) protected from oxidation by mud or acidic water, converting first to peat. Deep burial by sediments subjects peat to heat and pressure over millions of years, driving off water, methane, and carbon dioxide, increasing carbon content. **Temperature is the dominant factor** over pressure or time: sub-bituminous coal forms at 35–80 °C, while anthracite requires 180–245 °C. Ninety percent of coal beds date to the Carboniferous and Permian periods. Paradoxically, this occurred during the Late Paleozoic icehouse; glaciation lowered sea levels, exposing vast continental shelves and river deltas that became widespread wetlands. Contributing factors included high oxygen (>30%) causing charcoal-forming wildfires, high CO₂ boosting plant growth, and lycophyte trees with determinate growth (rapid carbon turnover). A debated hypothesis suggests a 60-million-year "lag" between the evolution of lignin (woody stems) ~360 Mya and the evolution of lignin-degrading fungi ~300 Mya, allowing wood to accumulate; phylogenetic analysis supports fungal enzyme evolution ~200 Mya. Tectonically, the Central Pangean Mountains created year-round equatorial precipitation, preserving peat. Coal also exists in Precambrian strata, presumed derived from algae. **Cyclothems**—repeating sedimentary sequences including coal seams—reflect glacial sea-level cycles flooding and exposing continental shelves.

### Chemistry and Classification
Coalification proceeds via **dehydration** (removing H₂O), **decarboxylation** (removing CO₂), and **demethanation** (removing CH₄), converting aliphatic compounds to aromatic and eventually polyaromatic structures resembling graphene. **Macerals** are microscopically identifiable coalified plant parts: *vitrinite* (woody tissue), *lipinite* (spores/algae), *inertite* (charred wood), and *huminite* (precursor to vitrinite). Rank increases with metamorphic grade: **Peat** → **Lignite** (brown coal, lowest rank, highest health harm) → **Sub-bituminous** → **Bituminous** (dense, banded, "steam coal") → **Anthracite** (hard, glossy, highest carbon) → **Graphite**. **Hilt’s Law** states rank increases with depth in a vertical thermal gradient, though lateral metamorphism (e.g., igneous sills) can alter rank locally. The critical commercial distinction is **thermal coal** (steam coal, burned for electricity) vs. **metallurgical coal** (coking coal, baked to make coke for steel).

### History of Use
Early use was sporadic: Stone Age Europe, Neolithic China (ornaments, copper smelting), Roman Britain (widespread mining, heating, ironworking). In Britain, "seacoal" (washed ashore) shifted to "pitcoal" (mined) by the 13th century as surface sources exhausted. A pivotal domestic transition to coal hearths occurred in London (late 16th/early 17th century), preceding industrial adoption. The **Industrial Revolution** cemented coal’s dominance; the steam engine overcame watermill site limits. Britain dominated early production (1700: 5/6 of world output). Coal underpinned 19th/20th-century industry and the European Coal and Steel Community (EU predecessor). UK deep mining ended in 2015.

### Modern Uses
**Electricity (68% of 2022 use):** Thermal coal is pulverized, burned in boilers to raise steam for turbines (25–50% efficiency). **Integrated Gasification Combined Cycle (IGCC)** plants gasify coal to syngas (CO + H₂) for gas turbines, achieving up to 94% combined heat/power efficiency with less local pollution. **Coke/Steel (10–15% of use):** Metallurgical coal is baked anaerobically at ~1,000 °C to produce coke, a porous carbon residue that reduces hematite (Fe₂O₃) to iron in blast furnaces. **Chemicals:** Coal gasification produces syngas feedstock for methanol, hydrogen, ammonia, urea, olefins, etc., competitive when oil/gas prices are high; water-intensive, concentrated in China. **Liquefaction:** Direct conversion to synthetic gasoline/diesel via hydrogenation (Fischer–Tropsch); emits more CO₂ than oil refining unless combined with biomass and CCS. **Gasification:** Syngas for power (IGCC), fuels (Fischer–Tropsch), or chemicals (methanol-to-gasoline).

### Industry and Markets
~8,000 Mt produced annually (90% hard coal, 10% lignite); ~50% from underground mines (higher accident risk). Industry employs ~2.7 million. **China mines ~50%, India ~10%.** Top exporters (2022): Indonesia (471 Mt), Australia (344 Mt), Russia (224 Mt). Top importers: China, India, Japan. Metallurgical coal commands a volatile premium over thermal coal due to low-sulfur requirements. **Market trends:** China consumed 4,520 Mt in 2022 (>50% global); its policy drives global trends. IEA projects global coal trade declining ~12% by 2026 (thermal -16%, met +2%) due to domestic production growth in China/India and phase-outs in Europe. Renewables increasingly undercut existing coal plants (already cheaper in Global North; early 2020s in China; late 2020s in SE Asia). Stranded asset risk looms for coal-heavy utilities.

### Health Impacts
Mining/processing pollutes air/water. Combustion emits NOx, SO₂, particulates (PM2.5), and heavy metals. **~800,000 premature deaths/year globally** (mostly India/China). SO₂ forms PM2.5, the deadliest air pollutant. Specific outcomes: asthma, strokes, reduced intelligence, heart disease, mercury poisoning, lung cancer. Europe’s annual health costs: ~€43 billion. **Coalworker’s pneumoconiosis ("black lung")** kills ~1,500 US former miners yearly. Coal ash (~10% of coal) contains mercury, arsenic, uranium, thorium; stored locally, it leaches toxins and radioactivity. UNSCEAR estimates coal plants release more radioactivity than nuclear plants.

### Environmental Damage
**Water:** Mining alters groundwater/table/acidity; fly ash spills (e.g., Kingston Fossil Plant) contaminate land/water; plants consume vast water volumes, stressing scarce regions (e.g., Thar Desert). **Acid rain:** ~100 Tg/S SO₂ emitted in 2014 (half from coal); oxidizes to H₂SO₄, causing ecosystem acidification; aerosols briefly mask warming but precipitate in weeks vs. centuries for CO₂. **Land:** Subsidence over abandoned tunnels; **underground fires** (thousands burning, e.g., Brennender Berg since 1668) emit CO₂, cause subsidence, ignite wildfires. China’s coal fires burn ~120 Mt/year, emitting 360 Mt CO₂ (2–3% of global fossil fuel CO₂). **Coke byproducts** (ammonia, tar) pollute waterways if discharged.

### Climate Change
Coal is the **largest long-term driver of climate change**. Coal plants were the single largest contributor to CO₂ emissions growth in 2018 (40% of fossil fuel CO₂, >25% total). 2016: 14.5 Gt CO₂ from coal. Emission intensity: ~1 tonne CO₂/MWh (double gas). IPCC advises most reserves must stay underground to limit warming to 1.5–2°C, requiring early retirement of hundreds/thousands of plants. Methane from mining adds forcing.

### Pollution Mitigation and Carbon Capture
**Pre-combustion:** Refined coal (dewatered, dedusted) improves efficiency. **Post-combustion:** Flue-gas desulfurization (scrubbers), selective catalytic reduction (NOx), electrostatic precipitators (particulates). **Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS):** Captures CO₂ from flue gas for underground burial. **Track record:** 1972–2017 plans for 161 Mt/yr capture; **98% failed by 2021** due to cost, liability, social acceptance. **As of 2024: only 4 coal plants + 1 gas plant operate CCS globally.** **"Clean coal"**: Term evolved from acid rain scrubbers to include CCS; criticized as misleading—even with controls, impact remains high. **"Abated coal"**: 2023 UN agreement to "phase down unabated coal"; criticized as undefined. IPCC defines abated as capturing ≥90% of lifecycle emissions.

### Economics and Politics
2018 investment: $80B (mostly sustaining, not new mines). Long-term climate damages: trillions/year; highest social cost of carbon in India/US. Air pollution costs: US ~$200/ton stored; EU €43B/yr. **Subsidies (2021):** ~$19B direct; G20 provides ≥$63.9B/yr (finance, fiscal, SOE). Indonesia caps domestic coal prices; EU bans state aid for new plants (2020) and existing (2025). **Stranded assets:** Risk of carbon bubble (e.g., China Energy Investment). State utilities in Global South (Eskom, PLN, EGAT, etc.) still building/planning plants. **Politics:** China, India, Indonesia, Vietnam, Turkey, Bangladesh face criticism for new plants undermining Paris Agreement. Pacific Islands criticized Australia (2019). G7 agreed (2021) to end new direct international coal finance.

## Terms
- ****Coalification**** — The geological process converting dead vegetation (peat) into coal via heat, pressure, and time.
- ****Macerals**** — Microscopic, coalified plant components retaining original morphology (e.g., vitrinite, liptinite, inertite).
- ****Rank**** — Classification by metamorphic grade/carbon content: Peat → Lignite → Sub-bituminous → Bituminous → Anthracite → Graphite.
- ****Thermal Coal (Steam Coal)**** — Coal burned to generate steam for electricity generation.
- ****Metallurgical Coal (Coking Coal)**** — Coal heated anaerobically to produce coke, used as fuel/reductant in blast furnaces for steelmaking.
- ****Syngas**** — Synthesis gas (CO + H₂) produced by coal gasification; feedstock for power, fuels, chemicals.
- ****Coke**** — Porous, high-carbon residue from baking metallurgical coal; reduces iron ore in blast furnaces.
- ****Cyclothem**** — Repeating sedimentary sequence (including coal seams) driven by glacial sea-level cycles.
- ****Hilt’s Law**** — Observation that coal rank increases with depth in a vertical thermal gradient.
- ****Unabated Coal**** — Coal used without interventions substantially reducing lifecycle GHG emissions (IPCC: <90% capture).

## Debates and open questions
*   **Lignin lag hypothesis:** Whether the Carboniferous coal peak resulted from a ~60-million-year delay in fungal lignin degradation (supported by phylogenetic enzyme dating ~200 Mya) vs. climatic/tectonic drivers (Central Pangean Mountains, high O₂/CO₂, lycophyte ecology).
*   **CCS viability:** Whether carbon capture can overcome 98% historical project failure rates, cost barriers, liability gaps, and social opposition to scale meaningfully.
*   **"Abated" definition:** Lack of a standardized threshold for "abated coal" in the 2023 UN agreement risks loopholes (e.g., minimal capture rates labeled as abatement).
*   **Peak coal timing:** IEA expects peak demand before 2030, but Chinese policy shifts, Indian growth, and Global South energy security needs create uncertainty.
*   **Stranded asset cascade:** Whether continued state-backed plant construction in the Global South will trigger a systemic financial "carbon bubble" burst.
