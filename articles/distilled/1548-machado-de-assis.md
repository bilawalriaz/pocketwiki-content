# Machado de Assis

## Overview
Joaquim Maria Machado de Assis (1839–1908) is widely regarded as the greatest writer of Brazilian literature. Born in Rio de Janeiro to a poor family of mixed African and Portuguese descent, he rose from autodidactic obscurity to become a foundational figure in Brazilian Realism and the first president of the Brazilian Academy of Letters (1897–1908). His early romantic novels gave way to a mature, innovative realist style marked by irony, metafictional narration, and incisive critiques of the social hypocrisies of Imperial and early Republican Brazil. His masterpieces—*Memórias Póstumas de Brás Cubas* (1881), *Quincas Borba* (1891), and *Dom Casmurro* (1899)—along with the short story "A Missa do Galo" (1893), established a literary legacy celebrated by critics such as Harold Bloom, who ranked him among the 100 greatest geniuses of literature.

- **20 Oct 1904** — Wife Carolina dies after 35 years of marriage.
- **29 Sep 1908** — Dies in Rio de Janeiro.

## Body

### Origins and Autodidactic Rise
Machado de Assis was born in 1839 in the Livramento country house in Rio de Janeiro, protected by his godmother, the widow of a senator. His father was a wall painter and grandson of freed slaves; his mother was a Portuguese immigrant from the Azores. He received minimal formal education, studying briefly in public schools and a girls' school where his stepmother worked, while learning French at night from a baker. His literary career began at age 15 when Francisco de Paula Brito published his poem "Ela" in 1855. He soon secured a position as a typographer's apprentice at the *Imprensa Oficial*, where director Manuel Antônio de Almeida encouraged his writing. Through the newspaper milieu, he entered liberal intellectual circles, befriending Francisco Otaviano and Quintino Bocaiúva, and taught himself English, German, and later Greek, absorbing influences from Sterne, Shakespeare, Byron, and Swift.

### Bureaucratic Career and Early Romantic Phase
Machado advanced through the civil service, working at the Ministry of Agriculture, Trade, and Public Works, eventually heading a section. He published two poetry collections, *Falenas* (1870) and *Americanas* (1875), and five "romantic novels" between 1872 and 1878 (*Ressurreição*, *A Mão e a Luva*, *Helena*, *Iaiá Garcia*). While popular with the public, critics deemed them mediocre. The 1864 death of his father and the subsequent death of his mentor José de Alencar triggered epileptic attacks and a deep melancholia, prompting a stylistic rupture. His 1867 appointment to the *Diário Oficial* by Emperor Dom Pedro II—whom he venerated as a humble, learned patriot—provided financial stability and the honor of knighthood in the Order of the Rose (promoted to officer in 1888).

### Marriage and Racial Identity
In 1868, Machado met Carolina Augusta Xavier de Novais, sister of a colleague. Despite his stammer, shyness, and the disapproval of her white family due to his African descent (he is perceived in Brazil as *pardo*, though Harold Bloom classified him as the greatest Black writer in Western literature), they married in 1869. The union lasted 35 years until her death in 1904 and was described as a "perfect married life"; they had no children. Machado avoided public political engagement, drawing criticism from abolitionists like José do Patrocínio and later writer Lima Barreto for his silence on slavery and his interracial marriage.

### The Realist Masterpieces and the Republic
The 1881 publication of *Memórias Póstumas de Brás Cubas* inaugurated Machado’s mature phase. Narrated by a deceased protagonist, the novel introduced a "skeptical and realistic tone," metafictional wit, and a dialogue between Romantic subjectivism and Realist objectivism. Critics identify his later works—*Quincas Borba* (1891), *Dom Casmurro* (1899), *Esaú e Jacó* (1904), and *Memorial de Aires* (1908)—as masterpieces of literary Impressionism and innovative realism. The 1889 military coup that established the Republic caught Machado, a loyal monarchist, by surprise; he famously resisted the removal of Pedro II's portrait from his office. The new regime sharpened his critical lens, leading him to write novels that historians like Sidney Chalhoub view as expositions of the social, political, and economic dysfunction of late Imperial Brazil. Roberto Schwarz argues Machado inverts narrative conventions to expose the hypocrisies of 19th-century Brazilian society.

### Brazilian Academy of Letters and Final Years
In 1897, alongside monarchist intellectuals like Joaquim Nabuco and Afonso Celso, Machado founded the Brazilian Academy of Letters, serving as its president until his death. He secured a permanent headquarters for the Academy in 1905. Following Carolina's death in 1904, he sank into depression and died in 1908. Posthumous collections of his chronicles, criticism, and plays continued to appear for decades.

### Narrative Style and Critical Reception
Machado’s style is defined by direct address to the reader (breaking the "fourth wall"), irony, and a tender yet incisive view of human nature. Critical interpretation remains divided: Abel Barros Baptista classifies him as an "anti-realist" negating objective representation, while John Gledson sees a faithful depiction of Brazilian reality via daring technique. G. Reginald Daniel posits a sophisticated mediation between Romanticism and Realism-Naturalism, evolving toward Impressionism. Despite inclusion on Harold Bloom’s list of 100 literary geniuses and admiration from writers like Salman Rushdie, Carlos Fuentes, and Woody Allen, Machado remains relatively unknown in the English-speaking world compared to other Latin American authors, despite multiple translations sponsored by figures like Susan Sontag and Elizabeth Hardwick.

## Terms
- **Autodidactism**: Self-education without formal schooling; Machado’s primary method of learning languages and literature.
- **Pardo**: A Brazilian racial classification for mixed-race individuals (typically European, African, and Indigenous ancestry); how Machado is generally perceived in Brazil.
- **Memórias Póstumas de Brás Cubas** (1881): Machado’s pivotal novel narrated by a dead man ("Posthumous Memoirs"), marking the shift from Romanticism to a skeptical, metafictional Realism.
- **Metafiction**: Fiction that self-consciously addresses its own artificiality; a hallmark of Machado’s mature style (e.g., the narrator addressing the reader directly).
- **Brazilian Academy of Letters (Academia Brasileira de Letras)**: Founded in 1897 by Machado and peers to cultivate Brazilian language and literature; he served as its first president.
- **Liberal Monarchist**: Machado’s political stance: support for constitutional monarchy under Pedro II, religious freedom, and civil liberties, opposed to both clerical conservatism and republicanism.
- **Literary Impressionism**: A late 19th-century style emphasizing subjective perception and fragmented reality; critics identify this in Machado’s later novels like *Dom Casmurro*.
- **"A Missa do Galo"** (1893): Short story ("Midnight Mass") widely considered the masterpiece of Brazilian short fiction, noted for its subtle ambiguity.
- **Order of the Rose (Ordem da Rosa)**: Brazilian imperial order of chivalry; Machado was knighted (1867) and made an officer (1888) by Pedro II.
- **Fourth Wall**: The imaginary barrier between fiction and audience; Machado frequently breaks it by speaking directly to the reader.

## Debates and open questions
- **Realist vs. Anti-Realist**: Critics disagree whether Machado’s work constitutes a faithful representation of Brazilian reality (Gledson) or a philosophical negation of representation itself (Baptista).
- **Racial Identity and Politics**: Scholars debate the extent to which Machado’s mixed-race identity and silence on abolition shaped his literary persona; some argue his avoidance of racial themes was strategic survival, others see it as a limitation.
- **Periodization of Style**: Daniel’s thesis of a dialogue between Romanticism and Realism evolving into Impressionism is contested by those who see a sharper break in 1881 or a consistent anti-realist project throughout.
- **Canonical Status Abroad**: Despite high critical acclaim (Bloom, Sontag, Rushdie), Machado’s limited commercial success in English translation remains a subject of discussion regarding the reception of non-European modernisms.
