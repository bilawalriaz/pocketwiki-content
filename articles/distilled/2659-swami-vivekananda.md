# Swami Vivekananda

## Overview
Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902) was an Indian Hindu monk and chief disciple of the mystic Ramakrishna. He is a pivotal figure for introducing Vedanta and Yoga to the Western world, raising interfaith awareness, and helping elevate Hinduism to the status of a major world religion. His work also significantly influenced the Hindu revivalist movement and Indian nationalism.

## Timeline
- **12 January 1863** — Born as Narendranath Datta in Calcutta, British India.
- **1881** — First meets Ramakrishna, who becomes his primary spiritual guide.
- **16 August 1886** — Ramakrishna dies; Narendra and disciples form the first monastic order.
- **December 1886** — Takes formal monastic vows, adopting the name Vivekananda.
- **1888–1893** — Travels extensively across India as a wandering monk.
- **11 September 1893** — Delivers landmark speech at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago.
- **1894** — Founds the Vedanta Society of New York.
- **1 May 1897** — Founds the Ramakrishna Mission in Calcutta for social service.
- **1899** — Establishes Vedanta Society of San Francisco; second visit to the West.
- **4 July 1902** — Dies at Belur Math, Calcutta, aged 39.

## Body

### Early Life and Spiritual Formation
Born into an aristocratic Bengali family, Narendranath Datta received a broad education in both Western philosophy and Indian scriptures. His early spiritual quest was influenced by the Brahmo Samaj, a reformist movement. In 1881, he met Ramakrishna, who became his guru. Despite initial skepticism, Narendra accepted Ramakrishna after his father's death left the family in poverty, finding solace in spiritual practice. Following Ramakrishna's death in 1886, Narendra and other disciples founded the Baranagar Math, the first monastery of the Ramakrishna order.

### Travels and the Parliament of Religions
As a wandering monk from 1888, Vivekananda traveled across India, witnessing widespread poverty. In 1893, he traveled to the United States to attend the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago. His opening address, beginning "Sisters and brothers of America," received a standing ovation and introduced Hindu spiritual thought, advocating for religious tolerance and universal acceptance. The speech made him a renowned figure, dubbed the "cyclonic monk from India."

### Teachings and Western Mission
Vivekananda lectured widely across the US and UK, establishing Vedanta Societies. He adapted Hindu philosophy for Western audiences, synthesizing classical yoga and Advaita Vedanta. His book *Raja Yoga* (1896), an interpretation of Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, was highly influential in shaping the modern Western understanding of yoga. He emphasized that each soul is potentially divine and that all religions are different paths to the same goal.

### Social Reform and Indian Nationalism
In India, Vivekananda founded the Ramakrishna Mission (1897) to combine spiritual training with social service, education, and humanitarian work. He addressed social issues like poverty and the caste system, and his fervent nationalism helped awaken a new sense of Indian identity. He believed a country's future depends on its people and sought to bring noble ideas to the doorstep of the poor.

## Terms
- **Vedanta**: A school of Hindu philosophy based on the Upanishads, exploring the nature of ultimate reality (Brahman) and the self (Atman).
- **Yoga**: A spiritual discipline aiming to achieve a state of harmony between mind and body; Vivekananda popularized its philosophical aspects.
- **Advaita Vedanta**: A non-dualistic school of Vedanta philosophy, asserting that the individual soul and the ultimate reality are one.
- **Sannyasin**: A Hindu ascetic or monk who has renounced worldly life.
- **Brahmo Samaj**: A 19th-century reformist Hindu movement that rejected polytheism and idol worship.
- **Neo-Vedanta**: A modern interpretation of Hinduism, particularly Advaita Vedanta, that seeks compatibility with Western thought.
- **Samadhi**: A state of intense concentration and meditative consciousness, considered a means to liberation.
- **Karma Yoga**: The yoga of selfless action, one of the paths to spiritual liberation.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the extent of Vivekananda's innovation. While often labeled a proponent of Neo-Vedanta, some argue his core ideas, like the emphasis on direct spiritual experience over scripture, are present in earlier medieval Advaitic literature. There is also discussion about the relative influence of the Brahmo Samaj versus Ramakrishna on his early development.
