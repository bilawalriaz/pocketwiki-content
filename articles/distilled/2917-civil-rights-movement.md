# Civil rights movement

## Overview
The civil rights movement was a decades-long struggle by African Americans and their allies to end legalized racial segregation, discrimination, and disenfranchisement in the United States. It achieved landmark federal legislation in the 1960s, fundamentally altering American law and society, though informal racism and systemic inequalities persisted.

## Timeline
- **1865-1870** — Reconstruction Amendments (13th, 14th, 15th) abolish slavery and grant citizenship and voting rights to Black men.
- **1896** — Supreme Court upholds "separate but equal" doctrine in *Plessy v. Ferguson*.
- **1954** — Supreme Court rules school segregation unconstitutional in *Brown v. Board of Education*.
- **1955-56** — Montgomery bus boycott follows Rosa Parks' arrest.
- **1960** — Sit-in movement begins in Greensboro, NC.
- **1961** — Freedom Rides test desegregation of interstate travel.
- **1963** — Birmingham campaign and March on Washington.
- **1964** — Civil Rights Act of 1964 is signed into law.
- **1965** — Selma voting rights marches; Voting Rights Act of 1965 is signed.
- **1968** — Civil Rights Act of 1968 (Fair Housing Act) is signed; Martin Luther King Jr. is assassinated.

## Body

### Background and Legal Foundations
The movement's origins trace to the Reconstruction era, which granted new constitutional rights to African Americans. After 1877, Southern states systematically stripped these rights through Jim Crow laws enforcing segregation and disenfranchisement, upheld by the Supreme Court's 1896 *Plessy v. Ferguson* decision. Early 20th-century efforts by organizations like the NAACP focused on litigation. The pivotal legal victory came in 1954 with *Brown v. Board of Education*, which declared school segregation unconstitutional and overturned the "separate but equal" doctrine.

### Direct Action and Nonviolent Protest
After *Brown*, the movement expanded from legal challenges to mass nonviolent direct action. The 1955-56 Montgomery bus boycott, sparked by Rosa Parks, demonstrated the power of economic boycotts and brought Martin Luther King Jr. to prominence. Tactics evolved to include sit-ins (starting in 1960), Freedom Rides (1961) to test interstate travel desegregation, and large-scale campaigns like the 1963 Birmingham campaign, where police brutality against protesters, including children, galvanized national opinion. The 1963 March on Washington, where King delivered his "I Have a Dream" speech, showcased the movement's scale and moral appeal.

### Legislative Victories and Federal Intervention
Sustained pressure, often met with violent repression, forced federal action. President John F. Kennedy proposed civil rights legislation in 1963. After his assassination, President Lyndon B. Johnson secured its passage as the Civil Rights Act of 1964, banning discrimination in public accommodations and employment. The 1965 Selma to Montgomery marches, and the brutal attack on marchers known as "Bloody Sunday," led directly to the Voting Rights Act of 1965, which outlawed discriminatory voting practices. The Fair Housing Act of 1968 prohibited discrimination in housing.

### Evolution and Internal Debates
The movement was not monolithic. While King and the SCLC advocated nonviolent civil disobedience, other groups like the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) grew more militant. The Black Power movement, influenced by Malcolm X, emphasized racial pride, self-defense, and economic empowerment, critiquing the integrationist focus of earlier leaders. The Black Panther Party, founded in 1966, combined community programs with armed self-defense. These shifts reflected frustration with slow progress and persistent violence.

### Backlash and Unfinished Work
The movement faced fierce resistance, from "massive resistance" in the South to white backlash in the North over housing and school integration. Urban riots in the mid-to-late 1960s, fueled by poverty and police brutality, eroded some white support. While legal segregation ended, informal racism and systemic inequalities in housing, education, and criminal justice remained deeply entrenched.

## Terms
- **Jim Crow laws**: State and local statutes that enforced racial segregation in the Southern United States from the late 19th century until 1965.
- **Disenfranchisement**: The systematic removal of the right to vote from a group of people, achieved in the South through poll taxes, literacy tests, and other barriers.
- **Nonviolent direct action**: A strategy of public, nonviolent protest—such as boycotts, sit-ins, and marches—designed to create a crisis and force negotiation.
- **Civil disobedience**: The nonviolent refusal to obey unjust laws as a form of peaceful protest.
- **Freedom Rides**: Intercacial bus trips through the South in 1961 to test the desegregation of interstate travel facilities.
- **Black Power**: A slogan and philosophy emphasizing racial pride, self-sufficiency, and equality for people of African descent, often advocating for more militant tactics.
- **Voting Rights Act of 1965**: Federal legislation that outlawed discriminatory voting practices and provided for federal oversight of elections in areas with a history of voter suppression.
- **Civil Rights Act of 1964**: Landmark federal law that prohibited discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin in employment and public accommodations.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The relative effectiveness of nonviolent protest versus armed self-defense or Black Power militancy remains a subject of historical analysis.
- The extent to which the movement's successes were driven by grassroots organizing versus the leadership of prominent figures like Martin Luther King Jr. is debated.
- Scholars examine the complex and sometimes strained alliances between the civil rights movement and other groups, such as Jewish Americans and the political left.
- The movement's legacy is contested, with ongoing debates about whether its goals of true equality have been achieved or if systemic racism persists in new forms.
