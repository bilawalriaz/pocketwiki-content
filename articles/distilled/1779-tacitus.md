# Tacitus

## Overview
Publius Cornelius Tacitus (c. AD 56–c. 120) was a Roman historian and politician, widely regarded as one of the greatest Roman historians. His major works, the *Annals* and the *Histories*, provide a continuous narrative of the Roman Empire from the death of Augustus (AD 14) to the death of Domitian (AD 96), offering critical insights into the psychology of imperial power and the corruption of the Roman governing class.

## Timeline
- **c. AD 56–57** — Born to an equestrian family; birthplace unknown.
- **AD 77 or 78** — Marries Julia Agricola, daughter of the general Gnaeus Julius Agricola.
- **AD 81 or 82** — Begins political career as a quaestor under Emperor Titus.
- **AD 88** — Becomes praetor and a member of the priestly college of quindecimviri.
- **c. AD 89–93** — Serves in the provinces, possibly commanding a legion.
- **AD 97** — Becomes suffect consul under Emperor Nerva; delivers funeral oration for Lucius Verginius Rufus.
- **AD 98** — Publishes *Agricola* and *Germania*.
- **AD 100** — Prosecutes the corrupt proconsul Marius Priscus with Pliny the Younger.
- **c. AD 105** — Publishes the *Histories*.
- **AD 112–113** — Serves as governor of the Roman province of Asia.
- **c. AD 117** — Publishes the *Annals*; death occurs after this date, possibly as late as AD 125–130.

## Body

### Life and Career
Details of Tacitus's life are scarce, known from his works, Pliny the Younger's letters, and an inscription. He was born to an equestrian family, possibly from Gallia Narbonensis, and studied rhetoric in Rome. He married Julia Agricola in AD 77/78 and advanced through the political *cursus honorum* (the sequential order of public offices) under the Flavian emperors. His experience during Domitian's reign of terror (AD 81–96) profoundly shaped his historical perspective, instilling a deep hatred of tyranny. He reached the height of his political career as suffect consul in AD 97 and later governed the province of Asia (AD 112–113).

### Major Historical Works
Tacitus's two principal works, the *Histories* and the *Annals*, were intended as a continuous 30-book narrative of the Roman Empire from AD 14 to AD 96. He wrote the *Histories* first, covering the Year of the Four Emperors (AD 69) and the Flavian dynasty, but only the first four books and part of the fifth survive. The *Annals*, his final work, covers the Julio-Claudian emperors from the death of Augustus. Significant portions are lost, but the surviving text is an invaluable record. The *Annals* contains one of the earliest extra-Biblical references to Jesus of Nazareth, in the context of Nero's persecution of Christians.

### Monographs
Tacitus wrote three works with a narrower focus. The *Agricola* (c. AD 98) is a biography of his father-in-law, the general Gnaeus Julius Agricola, and includes an account of the Roman conquest of Britain. The *Germania* is an ethnographic study of the Germanic tribes beyond the Rhine, describing their lands, laws, and customs. The *Dialogus de oratoribus* is an early work on oratory, written in a Ciceronian style and dedicated to Fabius Iustus.

### Literary Style and Historical Approach
Tacitus's Latin prose is famously dense, concise, and epigrammatic, often praised for its "grave, concise, and pithily eloquent" style. He owed a significant debt to the historian Sallust. His historical method is chronological but focuses intensely on the motives and psychology of political actors, blending factual narrative with moral insight. He claimed to write "without either anger or zeal" (*sine ira et studio*), yet his work is deeply preoccupied with the corruption of the Senate under the emperors and the dangers of unchecked power. His portrayal of emperors like Tiberius is complex, shifting from relative respect to condemnation.

### Sources and Influence
Tacitus used official Roman records like the *Acta Senatus* (Senate minutes) and *Acta Diurna* (daily gazette), as well as earlier historians such as Cluvius Rufus, Fabius Rusticus, and Pliny the Elder. His works are a primary source for the period and influenced later historians like Edward Gibbon. His accounts of Jewish customs and the early Christian movement remain historically significant.

## Terms
- **Cursus honorum**: The sequential order of public offices held by aspiring politicians in the Roman Republic and Empire.
- **Equestrian**: The social class of wealthy non-senators, from which Tacitus's family originated.
- **Suffect consul**: A consul elected to replace a consul who died or was removed from office during a term.
- **Quindecimvir**: A member of a priestly college of fifteen men responsible for maintaining the Sibylline Books and organizing the Secular Games.
- **Acta Senatus**: The official minutes of the sessions of the Roman Senate.
- **Acta Diurna**: A daily gazette of public and private events in ancient Rome.
- **Epistolarium**: A collection of letters, used by Tacitus as a historical source.
- **Exitus illustrium virorum**: A genre of literature detailing the deaths of famous men, often those who opposed emperors.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Tacitus's Neutrality**: Scholars debate whether his claim to write *sine ira et studio* ("without anger or zeal") is credible, given the evident bitterness and irony in his political analysis, likely shaped by his experiences under Domitian.
- **Interpretation of His Politics**: His works have been interpreted both as supporting and rejecting the imperial system, leading to the scholarly divide between "Black" (pessimistic) and "Red" (optimistic) Tacitists.
- **Completeness of the *Annals***: It is unknown whether Tacitus completed the *Annals*; the work breaks off in AD 66, and he died before writing his planned histories of Nerva and Trajan.
- **Authenticity of the *Dialogus***: Its distinct Ciceronian style has led to periodic questions about its authorship, though it is now generally accepted as an early work of Tacitus.
