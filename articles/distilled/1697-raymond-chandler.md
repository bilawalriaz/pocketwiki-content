# Raymond Chandler

## Overview
Raymond Chandler (1888–1959) was an American-British novelist and screenwriter who, alongside Dashiell Hammett, founded the hardboiled school of detective fiction. After losing his oil executive career to alcoholism during the Great Depression, he began writing pulp stories at age 44, creating the iconic private detective Philip Marlowe. His seven novels, including *The Big Sleep* (1939) and *The Long Goodbye* (1953), redefined the genre by prioritizing character, atmosphere, and lyrical prose over plot mechanics. Chandler’s essay "The Simple Art of Murder" established the moral code of the genre: a detective who is "not himself mean" walking "mean streets." His screenwriting work on *Double Indemnity* (1944) and *The Blue Dahlia* (1946) helped shape film noir, while his Marlowe novels became templates for cinematic adaptation.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born in Chicago in 1888, Chandler moved to England with his Irish-born mother in 1900 after his alcoholic father abandoned the family. He received a classical education at Dulwich College (1901–1907), where alumni included P. G. Wodehouse and C. S. Forester. He became a British subject in 1907 to sit the civil service examination, passing and taking an Admiralty post, but resigned after a year, disliking the "servility." An encounter with the talented but doomed poet Richard Barham Middleton, who later committed suicide, convinced Chandler he lacked the talent for a literary career. He worked briefly as a journalist for the *Daily Express* and *The Westminster Gazette* before returning to America in 1912, eventually settling in Los Angeles in 1913.

### Military Service and Business Career
In 1917, Chandler enlisted in the Canadian Expeditionary Force (50th Reinforcement Battalion), seeing trench combat in France with the 7th Battalion C.E.F. (British Columbia Regiment). He survived the Spanish flu pandemic and was training for the Royal Air Force when the war ended. Back in Los Angeles, he began an affair with Pearl "Cissy" Pascal, a married woman 18 years his senior. After his mother’s death in 1923, they married in 1924. Chandler built a successful career in the oil industry, rising to vice president of the Dabney Oil Syndicate by 1931. However, his alcoholism, absenteeism, affairs with subordinates, and threatened suicides led to his dismissal in 1932, at age 44, during the Great Depression.

### Literary Career: The Marlowe Novels
Desperate for income, Chandler taught himself pulp fiction by dissecting Erle Stanley Gardner stories, taking five months to write his first sale, "Blackmailers Don't Shoot" (1933), for *Black Mask*. His debut novel, *The Big Sleep* (1939), introduced Philip Marlowe, a first-person narrator who combined toughness with a chivalric moral code. Chandler published seven novels in his lifetime: *Farewell, My Lovely* (1940), *The High Window* (1942), *The Lady in the Lake* (1943), *The Little Sister* (1949), *The Long Goodbye* (1953), and *Playback* (1958). *The Long Goodbye* was hailed as arguably the first mystery since Hammett’s *The Glass Key* to qualify as a "serious and significant mainstream novel." An eighth novel, *Poodle Springs*, was completed by Robert B. Parker in 1989.

### Screenwriting and Hollywood
Literary success pulled Chandler into Hollywood. He co-wrote *Double Indemnity* (1944) with Billy Wilder, earning an Academy Award nomination; Wilder credited Chandler with much of the film’s memorable dialogue. His only produced original screenplay, *The Blue Dahlia* (1946), earned a second nomination, though Chandler claimed he could only finish it while drunk, aided by round-the-clock secretaries. He clashed violently with Alfred Hitchcock on *Strangers on a Train* (1951), leading Hitchcock to theatrically discard Chandler’s drafts, though Chandler retained lead credit. Chandler’s prose style—marked by sharp, lyrical similes ("Dead men are heavier than broken hearts")—and his Marlowe character became foundational to the film noir aesthetic.

### Later Life, Death, and Posthumous Reunion
Cissy’s death in 1954 devastated Chandler. He neglected her cremated remains, which sat in a mausoleum basement for 57 years. His depression and drinking worsened; he attempted suicide in 1955 and saw his writing quality decline. He regained U.S. citizenship in 1956 and was elected president of the Mystery Writers of America. He died in 1959 at Scripps Memorial Hospital. Buried at Mount Hope Cemetery without instructions, he was separated from Cissy until 2010, when scholar Loren Latker petitioned successfully to reunite them. On February 14, 2011, their ashes were interred together under a shared stone reading: "Dead men are heavier than broken hearts."

### Views on Pulp Fiction and Craft
In his 1950 introduction to *Trouble Is My Business*, Chandler argued that *Black Mask* stories prioritized scene over plot: "The ideal mystery was one you would read if the end was missing." He contended the hardboiled genre posited that justice requires an individual to force it into being, creating a fantasy of compressed action and logic. He viewed the rigid pulp formulas as a challenge: "To exceed the limits of a formula without destroying it is the dream of every magazine writer who is not a hopeless hack."

### Critical Reputation and Legacy
Critics including W. H. Auden, Evelyn Waugh, and Ian Fleming praised Chandler’s prose; Fleming called his dialogue "some of the finest written in any prose today." Paul Levine described his style as the "literary equivalent of a quick punch to the gut." However, Chandler faced contemporary criticism for being either too violent or too soft, and modern critics like Patrick Anderson cite rambling plots (Chandler allegedly didn't know who killed the chauffeur in *The Big Sleep*) and problematic depictions of race, gender, and sexuality. Despite this, his evocation of 1930s–40s Los Angeles (Bay City = Santa Monica, Gray Lake = Silver Lake) remains definitive. His legacy includes Raymond Chandler Square in Hollywood (Historic Cultural Monument #597), the Raymond Chandler Award (Noir in Festival), and the adjective "Chandleresque."

## Terms
- **Hardboiled**: A style of crime fiction featuring cynical, tough protagonists, graphic violence, and colloquial dialogue; Chandler and Hammett are its primary founders.
- **Philip Marlowe**: Chandler’s series detective, a "complete man and a common man" who adheres to a personal code of honor on "mean streets."
- **Black Mask**: The leading pulp magazine of the 1920s–30s where Chandler published his first stories and honed his craft.
- **Pulp Fiction**: Inexpensive fiction magazines printed on wood pulp paper; the primary market for early hardboiled detective stories.
- **Film Noir**: A cinematic style (1940s–50s) characterized by cynicism, fatalism, and low-key lighting; Chandler’s screenplays and novels were central to its development.
- **The Simple Art of Murder**: Chandler’s 1944 canonical essay defining the moral and aesthetic principles of the hardboiled detective.
- **Chandleresque**: An adjective describing writing that imitates Chandler’s distinctive blend of hardboiled toughness, lyrical simile, and moral complexity.
- **Dabney Oil Syndicate**: The company where Chandler worked as a vice president until his 1932 dismissal for alcoholism and misconduct.
- **Canadian Expeditionary Force (C.E.F.)**: The Canadian overseas military force in WWI; Chandler served in the 7th Battalion (British Columbia Regiment) in France.
- **Poodle Springs**: Chandler’s unfinished final novel, completed by Robert B. Parker and published in 1989.

## Debates and open questions
- **Plot coherence**: Critics debate whether Chandler’s plots are structurally incoherent (famously, he reportedly did not know who murdered the Sternwood chauffeur in *The Big Sleep*) or whether the "plotlessness" is a deliberate feature prioritizing atmosphere and character.
- **Social attitudes**: Scholars contest the extent to which Chandler’s depictions of Black, female, and homosexual characters reflect period-typical prejudice versus authorial malice, with Patrick Anderson calling him a "rather nasty man at times."
- **Literary status**: The tension remains between Chandler’s pulp origins and his acceptance into the literary canon; Pauline Kael argued he remained "locked in the conventions of pulp writing," while others cite *The Long Goodbye* as a breakthrough into "serious" literature.
- **Marlowe’s morality**: Readers debate whether Marlowe’s "knight-errant" code is a genuine moral compass or a romanticized facade for a cynical operator, a tension Parker summarized as "an innocent who knows better, a Romantic who is tough enough to sustain Romanticism."
