# Luanda

## Overview
Luanda is the capital and largest city of Angola, serving as the nation's primary port, industrial, and cultural center. Founded by the Portuguese in 1576, it is one of Africa's oldest colonial cities and was a major hub for the slave trade to Brazil. Its modern history is defined by rapid, unplanned growth due to the Angolan Civil War (1975–2002) and a recent, oil-fueled reconstruction boom that has made it one of the world's most expensive cities.

## Body

### Colonial Foundation and the Slave Trade
Founded in 1576, Luanda was Portugal's primary base in Angola. It became the center of the transatlantic slave trade to Brazil from c. 1550 until its abolition in 1836. During this period, the Portuguese made few territorial conquests beyond the immediate hinterland. The 19th century brought an economic revolution after the slave trade ended and ports opened to foreign shipping, leading to exports of palm oil, coffee, cotton, and other goods. By 1850, Luanda was one of the most developed Portuguese cities outside Europe.

### 20th Century: Growth and Independence
Under Portugal's Estado Novo dictatorship, Luanda grew from a town of 61,208 in 1940 to a wealthy city of 475,328 by 1970. It was largely unaffected by the Portuguese Colonial War (1961–1974). After Angolan independence in 1975, the start of the Civil War caused most white residents to flee. The city's population swelled with internal refugees, overwhelming its infrastructure and leading to the rapid expansion of slums known as *musseques*.

### 21st Century: Reconstruction and Inequality
Following the end of the Civil War in 2002, high oil revenues fueled a major reconstruction effort. New highways, social housing, and a new international airport (opened 2023) have been developed. However, this growth has been marked by extreme inequality. Luanda is consistently ranked as one of the most expensive cities in the world for expatriates, while about 53% of its inhabitants live in poverty with inadequate access to water and electricity.

### Geography and Climate
The city is divided into the old Baixa de Luanda and the newer Cidade Alta. It has a hot semi-desert climate (Köppen: BSh) that is surprisingly dry due to the cooling Benguela Current. Annual rainfall is low (405 mm) but highly variable. Climate change projections suggest Luanda will face significant sea-level rise, placing it among the most vulnerable major African cities.

### Demographics and Culture
The population is primarily from the Ambundu, Ovimbundu, and Bakongo ethnic groups. Portuguese is the official language, alongside Bantu languages like Kimbundu. The city is Angola's cultural epicenter, hosting institutions like the National Museum of Slavery and the annual Luanda International Jazz Festival.

### Economy and Transport
Luanda's economy is dominated by the oil industry, though it has a manufacturing sector producing food, beverages, textiles, and cement. The Port of Luanda is the country's largest and one of Africa's busiest. Transport infrastructure is poor but undergoing reconstruction. The main public transit consists of privately owned minibuses called *candongueiros*. The Luanda railway connects the city to Malanje.

## Terms
- **Musseques:** Slums or shanty towns that expanded dramatically around Luanda due to wartime migration.
- **Estado Novo:** The authoritarian dictatorship that ruled Portugal from 1933 to 1974.
- **Benguela Current:** A cold ocean current flowing north along Angola's coast, which creates Luanda's dry climate.
- **Köppen climate classification:** A system for classifying climates; BSh denotes a hot semi-desert climate.
- **Candongueiro:** A privately owned minibus taxi, a primary form of public transport in Luanda.
- **Baixa de Luanda:** The lower, historic part of the city near the port.
- **Cidade Alta:** The upper, newer part of the city.
- **RCP 4.5:** A "moderate" climate change scenario used in projections, where global warming reaches ~2.5–3°C by 2100.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The source notes that the slave trade involved "local merchants and warriors who profited," indicating a complex internal dynamic beyond simple victimization.
- The city's future is tied to climate change vulnerability. Projections for sea-level rise and associated damages vary widely based on emission scenarios (RCP 4.5 vs. RCP 8.5) and potential ice sheet instability, with costs potentially reaching hundreds of billions of dollars.
- The effectiveness of government efforts to overcome poverty through job creation and housing is an ongoing challenge, given the scale of inequality and infrastructure deficits.
