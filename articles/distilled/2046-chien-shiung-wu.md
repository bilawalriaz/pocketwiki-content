# Chien-Shiung Wu

## Overview
Chien-Shiung Wu (1912–1997) was a Chinese-American experimental physicist whose work was foundational to nuclear and particle physics. She is best known for the Wu experiment, which proved that parity is not conserved in weak interactions, a discovery that overturned a fundamental law of physics and paved the way for the Standard Model. Her career spanned the Manhattan Project, leading research at Columbia University, and advocacy for women in science.

## Timeline
- **May 31, 1912** — Born in Liuhe, Taicang, Jiangsu, China.
- **1936** — Emigrates to the United States, enrolling at the University of California, Berkeley.
- **June 1940** — Completes Ph.D. at Berkeley; thesis on bremsstrahlung and radioactive xenon.
- **May 30, 1942** — Marries physicist Luke Chia-Liu Yuan.
- **March 1944** — Joins the Manhattan Project at Columbia University.
- **1949** — First to experimentally confirm quantum entanglement using photons (EPR paradox).
- **1956** — Conducts the Wu experiment, proving parity violation in weak interactions.
- **1957** — Lee and Yang win the Nobel Prize for the theory; Wu is not awarded.
- **1958** — Becomes the first woman tenured physics professor at Columbia University.
- **1963** — Experimentally confirms the conserved vector current (CVC) hypothesis.
- **1975** — First female president of the American Physical Society.
- **1978** — Awarded the inaugural Wolf Prize in Physics.
- **February 16, 1997** — Dies in New York City.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born to a progressive father who founded a school for girls, Wu was encouraged in science. She excelled academically, graduating first in her class from National Central University in Nanjing (1934) after initially studying mathematics. She moved to the U.S. in 1936, choosing UC Berkeley over the University of Michigan due to its more liberal environment and superior facilities, including the cyclotron. At Berkeley, she completed her Ph.D. in 1940 under the supervision of Ernest O. Lawrence and Emilio Segrè, with a thesis on beta decay and radioactive xenon isotopes.

### Manhattan Project and Beta Decay Expertise
During World War II, Wu joined the Manhattan Project at Columbia University. Her expertise in xenon isotopes, from her Ph.D. research, proved critical in solving a problem with the B Reactor at the Hanford Site, where the fission product Xe-135 was acting as a neutron poison. After the war, she established herself as the world's leading authority on beta decay. In 1949, she demonstrated that previous experimental discrepancies with Fermi's theory of beta decay were due to flawed experimental setups, not theoretical errors.

### The Wu Experiment and Parity Violation
In the mid-1950s, theoretical physicists Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen Ning Yang proposed that the law of conservation of parity might not hold for the weak nuclear force. Wu designed and executed the definitive experiment to test this. Using cryogenic techniques to cool cobalt-60 atoms and align their nuclear spins with a magnetic field, she observed that the beta particles emitted during decay were not symmetric. This 1957 result proved parity violation, confirming Lee and Yang's theory. While Lee and Yang received the 1957 Nobel Prize, Wu's crucial experimental contribution was omitted, a decision later widely criticized. She was awarded the inaugural Wolf Prize in Physics in 1978.

### Later Career and Advocacy
Wu continued her research, experimentally confirming the conserved vector current (CVC) hypothesis in 1963, a key step toward the Standard Model. She became the first female president of the American Physical Society in 1975 and was a vocal advocate against gender discrimination in science. She also engaged in political advocacy, protesting human rights issues in Taiwan and China. Wu retired in 1981 and was posthumously honored with numerous awards, including a U.S. postage stamp in 2021.

## Terms
- **Parity**: A symmetry operation that mirrors a physical system. Conservation of parity meant the laws of physics were thought to be identical in a mirror-image world.
- **Weak Interaction**: One of the four fundamental forces, responsible for radioactive decay. The Wu experiment showed it does not conserve parity.
- **Beta Decay**: A type of radioactive decay in which an atomic nucleus emits a beta particle (an electron or positron). Wu was a leading expert on this process.
- **Cobalt-60**: A radioactive isotope used in the Wu experiment as a source of beta particles.
- **Cryogenics**: The production and behavior of materials at very low temperatures. Wu used liquid gases to cool cobalt-60 atoms to near absolute zero.
- **Manhattan Project**: The U.S. research and development undertaking during WWII that produced the first nuclear weapons. Wu contributed to uranium enrichment.
- **Conserved Vector Current (CVC) Hypothesis**: A theory in particle physics about the symmetry of the weak force, which Wu experimentally confirmed.
- **Quantum Entanglement**: A phenomenon where particles become linked and the state of one instantly influences the other, regardless of distance. Wu provided early experimental confirmation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nobel Prize Omission**: The decision to award the 1957 Nobel Prize in Physics to Lee and Yang but not to Wu for her experimental confirmation of their theory is considered a significant oversight. Wu received 23 Nobel nominations between 1958 and 1974 but never won.
- **Legacy and Recognition**: While Wu was highly respected by peers, her recognition lagged behind theoretical physicists for decades. Her story highlights historical gender bias in scientific awards and recognition.
