# James Cook

## Overview
Captain James Cook (1728–1779) was a British Royal Navy officer, explorer, and cartographer whose three Pacific voyages (1768–1779) dramatically expanded European geographical and scientific knowledge. Rising from a farm labourer’s son to a master navigator, Cook produced charts of unprecedented accuracy—most notably completing the first recorded circumnavigation of New Zealand and the first European contact with eastern Australia and the Hawaiian Islands. His voyages resolved major geographical questions, disproving the existence of a habitable *Terra Australis* in temperate latitudes and charting the northwest American coast. Cook pioneered scurvy prevention through diet and hygiene, losing no crew to the disease on his first voyage, and tested the marine chronometer (K1) that solved the longitude problem. His encounters with Indigenous peoples were complex: guided by Admiralty orders to cultivate friendship, he also used lethal force when threatened, resulting in at least 45 Indigenous deaths. Cook was killed in Hawaii in 1779 during a dispute over a stolen cutter. His legacy remains contested—celebrated as a supreme navigator and scientist, yet criticised as an agent of colonialism and the devastating impacts of European contact on Pacific peoples.

## Timeline
- **7 Nov 1728** — Born in Marton, Yorkshire.
- **1745–1755** — Apprenticed to merchant navy (Walkers, Whitby); studies navigation, astronomy; serves in Baltic and North Sea coal trade.
- **17 June 1755** — Enlists in Royal Navy as able seaman aboard HMS *Eagle*.
- **1758–1759** — Seven Years’ War: surveys St. Lawrence entrance (Siege of Louisbourg, Siege of Quebec); learns plane-table surveying from Samuel Holland; produces first engraved chart (Gaspé Bay).
- **1763–1767** — Commands HMS *Grenville*; surveys Newfoundland coast; observes 1766 solar eclipse to determine longitude; results communicated to Royal Society.
- **25 Aug 1768 – 12 Jul 1771** — **First voyage** (HMS *Endeavour*): observes 1769 transit of Venus at Tahiti; circumnavigates and charts New Zealand; first European landing on east coast of Australia (Botany Bay); runs aground on Great Barrier Reef; returns via Batavia (heavy dysentery losses).
- **1772–1775** — **Second voyage** (HMS *Resolution* & *Adventure*): crosses Antarctic Circle (17 Jan 1773); reaches 71°10′S (Jan 1774), blocked by pack ice; disproves temperate *Terra Australis*; visits Easter Island, Tonga, New Caledonia, South Georgia, South Sandwich Islands; tests K1 chronometer; awarded Copley Medal for scurvy prevention paper.
- **1776–1779** — **Third voyage** (HMS *Resolution* & *Discovery*): searches for North-West Passage from Pacific side; first European sighting of Hawaiian Islands (18 Jan 1778); charts NW American coast to Bering Strait (70°41′N); returns to Hawaii (Nov 1778).
- **14 Feb 1779** — Killed at Kealakekua Bay, Hawaii, during attempt to kidnap King Kalaniʻōpuʻu to recover stolen cutter.
- **4 Oct 1780** — Surviving crew returns to England under John Gore.

## Body

### Early Life and Merchant Navy Apprenticeship
James Cook was born in Marton, Yorkshire, the second of eight children of a Scottish farm labourer. In 1736 the family moved to Great Ayton, where a local landowner funded his schooling. At 16 he was apprenticed to a shopkeeper in Staithes but soon moved to Whitby, joining the coal-trade fleet of John and Henry Walker. As a merchant navy apprentice aboard colliers like the *Freelove*, he sailed the English coast and Baltic, rigorously studying algebra, geometry, trigonometry, navigation, and astronomy. He earned his master’s licence in 1752 and served as mate on the *Friendship*, gaining experience in shallow-water navigation.

### Royal Navy and the Seven Years’ War
In 1755, declining a merchant command, Cook joined the Royal Navy at Wapping as Britain mobilised for the Seven Years’ War. He served aboard HMS *Eagle*, saw action against French warships, and passed his master’s examination in 1757. Posted to HMS *Pembroke* in North America, he participated in the 1758 capture of Louisbourg. There he met army surveyor Samuel Holland, who taught him plane-table surveying; the two collaborated on charts of the St. Lawrence entrance, blending land-surveying with hydrography. In 1759 Cook’s soundings and updated charts enabled Wolfe’s nighttime troop crossing and victory at the Plains of Abraham. His first engraved chart, Gaspé Bay, appeared in 1759.

### Newfoundland Surveys and Scientific Recognition
At war’s end Cook was appointed master of HMS *Grenville* to chart Newfoundland’s rugged coast (1763–1767). He employed local pilots, survived a powder-horn explosion that scarred his right hand (1764), and grounded on uncharted rocks. In 1766 he precisely timed a solar eclipse, sending data to astronomer John Bevis; the resulting longitude difference (Newfoundland vs. Oxford) was published by the Royal Society in 1767 and used in his sailing directions. His Newfoundland charts exceeded contemporary accuracy and brought him to Admiralty and Royal Society attention.

### First Voyage (1768–1771): *Endeavour*
**Objectives & Ship.** Jointly sponsored by the Navy and Royal Society, the voyage’s public aim was observing the 1769 transit of Venus at Tahiti; secret orders directed a search for *Terra Australis* and claiming lands for Britain. Cook, 39, was promoted lieutenant and given the collier *Earl of Pembroke*, renamed *Endeavour*—sturdy, shallow-draught, and capable of being careened. The complement included astronomer Charles Green, naturalist Joseph Banks (with a retinue of seven), and artists.

**Tahiti & Society Islands.** Arriving April 1769, the transit was observed. Cook witnessed surfing, clashed over sacred marae stones and thefts (seizing canoes as ransom), and detained a chief to recover deserters. He claimed Raiatea-Taha’a, Huahine, Borabora, Tupai, and Maupiti as the Society Islands.

**New Zealand.** Sailing south, Cook landed at Poverty Bay (Oct 1769). With Tahitian priest Tupaia interpreting, he made first European contact with Māori; initial encounters turned violent. He circumnavigated both main islands, proving they were separated by a strait (Cook Strait), and mapped nearly the entire coastline. He claimed Mercury Bay and Queen Charlotte Sound (a future base).

**Australia & Return.** Convinced no southern continent existed there, Cook turned west, sighting Point Hicks (19 Apr 1770)—first European sighting of Australia’s east coast. He charted northward, landing at Botany Bay (29 Apr), where Gweagal men opposed them. After a week, *Endeavour* ran aground on the Great Barrier Reef (11 Jun); 27 hours of lightening and fothering freed her. Seven weeks of repairs at Endeavour River yielded first European kangaroo observation. At Possession Island (Aug 1770) Cook claimed the surveyed coast for Britain. The return via Batavia caused a dysentery epidemic: 30 deaths. *Endeavour* reached England 12 Jul 1771; Cook was promoted commander.

### Second Voyage (1772–1775): *Resolution* & *Adventure*
**Objective & Ships.** Commissioned by the Royal Society to determine *Terra Australis*’s existence. Cook chose two Whitby colliers: *Resolution* (his command) and *Adventure* (Tobias Furneaux). *Resolution* carried the K1 chronometer (Kendall’s copy of Harrison’s H4), azimuth compass, ice anchors, and a seawater distiller. Naturalists Johann Reinhold Forster and son Georg replaced Banks; astronomer William Wales managed K1.

**Antarctic Search.** From Cape Town (Nov 1772) they sailed east between 50°S–70°S. Crews melted icebergs for fresh water (Nov 1772). On 17 Jan 1773 they crossed the Antarctic Circle—first recorded Europeans to do so. On 18 Jan they unknowingly passed within 75 miles of Antarctica. Fog separated the ships (Feb 1773); *Adventure* lost 20 men to scurvy (Furneaux ignored Cook’s dietary rules). They reunited at Queen Charlotte Sound (May 1773), traded with Māori, and Cook noted disease transmission risks.

**Pacific Circuit & Separation.** Resuming eastward, they visited Tahiti and Tonga. A storm separated them again (Oct 1773); Cook waited three weeks, then continued alone. *Adventure* arrived five weeks later; ten crew were killed by Māori in a violent encounter (Dec 1773), bodies partially burned. Furneaux returned to Britain without Cook. Cook later defended Māori character.

**Southern Sweep & Return.** *Resolution* made a vast anti-clockwise loop: far south to 71°10′S (Jan 1774), blocked by pack ice; Cook wrote no man would attempt penetration. He suffered severe gastrointestinal illness (Feb–Mar 1774), recovering after eating dog soup and emetics (after pufferfish poisoning in New Caledonia, Sep 1774). He visited Easter Island, Tonga, New Caledonia (first European landing, claimed for Britain), and returned to Queen Charlotte Sound. Learning of *Adventure*’s tragedy only vaguely, he sailed home via South Georgia (charted, claimed Jan 1775) and South Sandwich Islands. The voyage proved no polar landmass reached beyond ~50°S, ending the *Terra Australis* hypothesis. Cook was promoted post-captain, granted Greenwich Hospital post, elected FRS, and awarded the Copley Medal.

### Third Voyage (1776–1779): *Resolution* & *Discovery*
**Objective & Cover.** Primary goal: find a North-West Passage from the Pacific. A simultaneous Atlantic-side expedition (Richard Pickersgill) was planned. The Admiralty’s public cover was returning Polynesian Mai to Tahiti. Cook commanded *Resolution*; Charles Clerke commanded *Discovery*. Key officers: William Bligh (master), George Vancouver (midshipman), John Webber (artist), David Samwell (surgeon’s mate).

**Pacific Leg.** Via Kerguelen Island (Dec 1776, found French message in bottle), Tasmania, and New Zealand (Feb 1777, site of *Adventure* massacre—Cook declined revenge), they reached Tahiti (returned Mai, observed human sacrifice). At Mo’orea (Oct 1777) Cook destroyed canoes and huts over a stolen goat. They sighted Hawaii (18 Jan 1778)—first European landing at Waimea, Kauaʻi, and Niʻihau. Hawaiians prostrated before Cook; a crewman killed a Hawaiian, angering Cook. He noted cultural parallels with Tahiti (language, marae, religion) and named the archipelago Sandwich Islands.

**North American Coast.** Cook sailed northeast, sighting Oregon (Cape Foulweather), missing the Strait of Juan de Fuca, and entering Nootka Sound (Vancouver Island, Mar–Apr 1778). He charted the coast to the Bering Strait, identifying Cook Inlet (Alaska). By Aug 1778 he crossed the Arctic Circle into the Chukchi Sea, reaching 70°41′N before pack ice blocked him. He sailed west to Siberia, then back to Bering Strait, closing the gap between Russian (west) and Spanish (south) explorations. Cook’s health and temper deteriorated; crew tensions rose.

**Hawaii & Death.** Returning to Hawaii (Nov 1778), Cook anchored at Kealakekua Bay (Hawaiʻi Island, Jan 1779), met King Kalaniʻōpuʻu during the Makahiki festival (honouring god Lono). Many Hawaiians believed Cook was Lono incarnate; *Resolution*’s mast formation resembled ritual artefacts. After a month, a gale broke *Resolution*’s foremast; they returned for repairs. Relations deteriorated: thefts, quarrels. On 13 Feb 1779 a cutter was stolen. Next day Cook attempted to kidnap the king as ransom. A crowd gathered; news arrived of a chief shot across the bay. Hawaiians attacked with stones, clubs, daggers. Cook fired, killed one, then was overwhelmed. Cook and four marines died; 17 Hawaiians killed.

**Aftermath.** Hawaiians took bodies inland. A priest mediated; some remains (identified by the 1764 hand scar) were returned 19 Feb and buried at sea. Clerke took command, charted islands per Cook’s plan, failed again at the North-West Passage (blocked by ice). Clerke died of tuberculosis (Aug 1779); John Gore brought the ships home (4 Oct 1780).

### Science, Technology, and Seamanship
Cook’s seamanship allowed three global voyages without losing a ship. He was an expert surveyor, hydrographer, and cartographer, proficient with theodolite, plane table, and sextant. His Newfoundland charts surpassed Royal Navy charts a century later. On the first voyage he used the new *Nautical Almanac* (lunar distances) for longitude; on the second and third he tested the K1 chronometer, which performed superbly, solving the longitude problem. He carried naturalists who collected over 3,000 plant species (first voyage) and produced interdisciplinary geographic works (second voyage). Cook and Banks noted Polynesian linguistic and cultural similarities, hypothesising a common Asian origin.

### Health and Disease
Cook pioneered scurvy prevention: wort, frequent fresh food resupply, strict hygiene (washing, airing bedding). On the first circumnavigation he lost zero crew to scurvy—exceptional for the era. He presented a paper to the Royal Society and received the Copley Medal. However, his crews carried syphilis, gonorrhoea, tuberculosis, malaria, dysentery, smallpox, influenza, and hepatitis to Tahiti, Hawaii, British Columbia, and New Zealand, causing population declines among peoples with no immunity. Cook issued orders barring women from ships and forbidding infected crew from sexual contact, but these were widely ignored.

### Indigenous Peoples: Conflict, Cooperation, and Cross-Cultural Exchange
**Conflict & Cooperation.** Admiralty orders required friendship, civility, trade, and reporting; the Royal Society advised avoiding violence, using lethal force only as a last resort. Cook initiated contact with gift-giving, name exchange, hongi, and relied on Polynesian intermediaries (Tupaia, Hitihiti, Mai). Yet he wrote that if peaceful landing failed, “we land nevertheless and maintain the footing… by the Superiority of our fire arms.” He ordered warning shots and small shot (less lethal), but crew sometimes disobeyed, using musket balls. At least 45 Indigenous people were killed by Cook’s crew (two by Cook); 15 crew killed by Indigenous people (including Cook). Worst violence: New Zealand (voyages 1 & 2) and Hawaii (voyage 3). Theft of official property triggered severe responses: hostage-taking, canoe destruction, floggings, ear-cropping. Crew criticised Cook for leniency; Polynesian advisers sometimes urged harsher punishment. Ceremonial friendships with chiefs entangled Cook in local politics and obligations he did not fully grasp.

**Chief or Deity.** In Polynesia Cook was often seen as *ariki* (high chief), embodying *atua* (gods) due to his leadership, crew deference, weapon power, and ceremonial friendships (exchanging genealogies, names, symbols, merging *mana*). In Hawaii, his arrival during Makahiki and *Resolution*’s rigging led many to view him as the god Lono.

**Trade & Introduction of Species.** Cook bartered iron nails, beads, copper, knives, cloth for food (fish, pigs, fruit, breadfruit). Crew traded individually for curiosities and sex. He carried livestock (pigs, goats, cattle, horses, rabbits, poultry, sheep) for crew consumption, breeding pairs on visited islands, and gifts. He planted European gardens (wheat, vegetables, citrus, melons, pineapple, grape) for Indigenous benefit and future British visitors.

**Cross-Cultural Exchange.** Polynesian words entered English (*tabu*, *tatu*); crew adopted “heiva” (Tahitian performance) for Cook’s anger, called him “Toote.” Polynesians learned English; Tupaia and Mai became proficient. Crew embraced tattooing (later a sailor tradition). Polynesians adopted European foods; crew ate dog. Artists exchanged styles; Tupaia learned English drawing. Boxing, wrestling, music, and dance were shared. Tupaia advised on Polynesian navigation, helping chart South Pacific islands. Mai became a celebrity in England, an unofficial ambassador. British and Polynesian religious ceremonies were mutually observed; a Hawaiian shrine burial became a cross-cultural ritual. After Cook’s death, his memory and remains were incorporated into Hawaiian rituals for decades.

**Cook’s Observations.** Cook described Māori as brave, noble, benevolent; Aboriginal Australians as happier than Europeans due to equality and sufficiency, needing no trade—views reflecting Enlightenment “noble savage” ideas but contradicting British prejudices. He questioned whether European contact benefited Indigenous peoples (1773 journal: “we debauch their Morals… introduce… wants and perhaps diseases”). He viewed Māori cannibalism as a custom of disunity, not savagery. He argued Polynesians shared common ancestry and long-voyage traditions. He refuted Dampier on Australians, Bougainville on Tahitian communal property, and British myths on Tahitian women’s promiscuity. Yet he sometimes used derogatory terms and judged without deep inquiry.

### Personal Life and Character
Cook married Elizabeth Batts (1762), daughter of his mentor Samuel Batts. They had six children (1763–1776); all died without issue. Elizabeth secured a posthumous coat of arms (1785)—the only known posthumous grant. Historians describe Cook as profoundly competent, patient, persistent, ambitious but not overly so, hot-tempered at incompetence, secretive, decisive, irreligious, chaste, strict, frugal, sober, fearless, calm in danger, capable of self-denial, but obstinate when flexibility was needed.

### Legacy
**Commemorations.** Monuments worldwide (Cambridge, Sydney, Melbourne); institutions (James Cook University, Townsville; James Cook University Hospital, Middlesbrough); RRS *James Cook* (2006); NASA spacecraft named after his ships; 400+ stamps, dozens of coins. Annual reenactment at Cooktown since 1959, supported by Guugu Yimithirr people, celebrating a reconciliation spear-gift after a turtle dispute. Around the 250th anniversary (2018–2020), memorials in Australia, New Zealand, Canada, Hawaii were vandalised; calls for removal/modification citing colonialism.

**Ethnographic Collections.** Largest: Cook-Forster Collection (University of Göttingen). Australian Museum (Sydney) holds 250+ objects, mostly Polynesian. Indigenous campaigns seek repatriation; debates frame Cook’s representation and artefact display within decolonisation of museums.

**Reputation & Influence.** Immediate tributes (Cowper, Goethe) praised humble origins, skill, leadership, science, humanity. Banks promoted Cook to support Australian colonisation (First Fleet 1788); colonists adopted Cook as founding father. In Hawaii, missionaries (1830s) disparaged Cook as idolater and disease-spreader, shaping native historiography. Bicentennial (1970s) revived interest; late 20th century brought Indigenous perspectives and acknowledgement of contact’s harms. Today Cook is widely regarded as a supreme explorer whose voyages expanded geography and enabled British Pacific engagement, but for many Indigenous peoples he symbolises violent encounter, disease, and non-consensual land claims. Scholars (Tombs, Thomas, Williams) acknowledge harms but argue Cook should not bear responsibility for post-mortem colonial policies.

## Terms
- ***Terra Australis Incognita*** — Hypothesised “unknown southern land” believed to balance northern continents; Cook’s voyages disproved its existence in temperate latitudes.
- **Transit of Venus** — Rare astronomical event (Venus crossing the Sun’s disk); 1769 observation from Tahiti was the public aim of Cook’s first voyage, used to calculate solar parallax.
- **Plane-table surveying** — Land-surveying technique using a flat table with sighting rule to plot features directly in the field; learned from Samuel Holland at Louisbourg (1758).
- **Fothering** — Emergency leak repair: hauling a sail (often filled with oakum/wool) under a ship’s hull to cover and slow a leak; used on *Endeavour* after Great Barrier Reef grounding.
