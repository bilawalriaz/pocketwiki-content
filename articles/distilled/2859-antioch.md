# Antioch

## Overview
Antioch, founded in 300 BC in northern Syria (modern Antakya, Turkey), was one of the most important cities of the ancient eastern Mediterranean. It served as the capital of the Seleucid Empire, a major Roman metropolis, a cradle of early Christianity, and a Crusader principality, making it a pivotal center of political, religious, and cultural exchange for centuries.

## Timeline
- **300 BC** — Founded by Seleucus I Nicator as part of a network of four cities to secure his territory.
- **240 BC** — Became the sole capital of the Seleucid Empire after the Battle of Ancyra.
- **63 BC** — Passed to the Roman Republic under Pompey, becoming the capital of Roman Syria.
- **115 AD** — Devastated by a massive earthquake during Trajan's visit.
- **256 AD** — Sacked by the Persians under Shapur I, with ~100,000 killed or deported.
- **327 AD** — Emperor Constantine began building the Great Church (Domus Aurea).
- **362 AD** — Emperor Julian's visit highlighted tensions between pagans and Christians.
- **526 AD** — Severely damaged by an earthquake; its port, Seleucia Pieria, never recovered.
- **540 AD** — Sacked by the Sassanian Persians under Khosrow I, who deported much of the population.
- **637 AD** — Conquered by the Rashidun Caliphate, beginning Islamic rule.
- **969 AD** — Recaptured by the Byzantine Empire.
- **1098 AD** — Conquered by Crusaders, becoming the capital of the Principality of Antioch.
- **1268 AD** — Captured and razed by the Mamluks under Baibars, ending the Crusader state.

## Body

### Foundation and Hellenistic Era
Seleucus I Nicator founded Antioch in 300 BC on the Orontes River in the fertile Amuq plain to secure his newly conquered territory. The city was named for his father, Antiochus. It was laid out in a grid plan and expanded into four walled quarters (Tetrapolis) by his successors. Antioch became the western capital of the Seleucid Empire, a multicultural hub of Greeks, Macedonians, and Jews. It was renowned for its beauty and the nearby suburb of Daphne, but its location on a fault line made it prone to devastating earthquakes, the first recorded in 148 BC.

### Roman Period and Rise of Christianity
After 63 BC, Antioch flourished as the capital of Roman Syria, favored by emperors as an eastern capital. It grew to over 500,000 inhabitants, the third-largest city in the Roman Empire. Major Roman additions included a hippodrome, aqueducts, and forums. The city was a chief center of early Christianity; the term "Christian" first emerged there. It became one of the original five patriarchates, with Saint Peter traditionally considered its first bishop. The city experienced periods of tension, such as during Emperor Julian's visit in 362 AD, and was sacked by Persians in 256 AD.

### Byzantine, Arab, and Seljuk Eras
Repeated earthquakes (526, 528 AD) and the Sassanian sack of 540 AD severely diminished Antioch. It was renamed Theopolis by Justinian I but never fully recovered. Conquered by the Rashidun Caliphate in 637 AD, it became a frontline city between the Umayyad/Abbasid Caliphates and the Byzantine Empire, leading to further decline. The Byzantines recaptured it in 969 AD, but it was taken by the Seljuk Turks in 1084 AD, setting the stage for the Crusades.

### Crusader Principality and Fall
The First Crusade captured Antioch in 1098 after a brutal siege, establishing the Principality of Antioch. The principality was a vassal state often caught between the Byzantine Empire, Armenian Kingdom, and Muslim powers. It faced internal strife, attacks from Nur ad-Din, and the effects of the Second Crusade. After a period of Armenian influence, the Mamluk sultan Baibars captured and destroyed the city in 1268, massacring or enslaving its population and ending the Latin presence in Syria.

## Terms
*   **Diadochi:** The successors of Alexander the Great who fought for control of his empire after his death in 323 BC.
*   **Seleucid Empire:** A Hellenistic state founded by Seleucus I, ruling much of the Near East from its capitals at Antioch and Seleucia.
*   **Pentarchy:** The early Christian concept of the five major patriarchates: Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem.
*   **Comes Orientis:** A high-ranking Roman official, the "Count of the East," who governed the Diocese of the East from Antioch.
*   **Theme:** A Byzantine military-administrative province.
*   **Doux:** The Byzantine governor of a major city or theme.
*   **Principality of Antioch:** A Crusader state established after the First Crusade, lasting until 1268.
*   **Mamluk:** A warrior class that ruled Egypt and Syria from 1250 to 1517.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Population Figures:** Ancient sources give varying numbers for Antioch's population at different times (e.g., 500,000 at its peak, 200,000 free adults under Ignatius, 150,000 total in 363 AD), making precise demographic history difficult.
*   **The 490/491 CE Riots:** Historical accounts of anti-Jewish violence differ significantly between Greek and Slavonic manuscript traditions, creating uncertainty about the sequence of events and the role of the city's Blue and Green factions.
*   **Julian's Visit:** The degree of religious harmony or tension in Antioch during Emperor Julian's 362 AD visit is debated, with sources like Ammianus Marcellinus highlighting conflict between the emperor's paganism and the city's Christian majority.
