# Lake

## Overview
A lake is a naturally occurring, relatively large body of water localized in a basin surrounded by dry land, distinct from oceans, lagoons, and ponds. Lakes cover roughly 2.5 million km² (under 2% of Earth’s surface) but hold almost all surface freshwater. They form through diverse geological processes—tectonics, volcanism, glaciation, fluvial action, solution, landslides, wind, shoreline processes, organic activity, and meteorite impacts—and are classified by origin, thermal stratification, water chemistry, and seasonal permanence. Lakes are temporary features on geological timescales, eventually filling with sediment or spilling out of their basins. Human-made lakes (reservoirs) serve water supply, power, agriculture, and recreation. Limnology, the study of inland waters, examines lakes’ physical, chemical, and biological zones. Extraterrestrial lakes exist on Titan (hydrocarbon) and formerly on Mars.

## Timeline
- **c. 4860 BC** — Collapse of Mount Mazama forms caldera later filled by Crater Lake (Oregon, USA).
- **c. 20,000 years ago** — Last glaciation creates Tal-y-llyn Lake (Wales) via landslide; shapes most northern European and North American lake basins.
- **1911** — Earthquake-triggered landslide forms Sarez Lake (Tajikistan) behind Usoi Dam.
- **1957** — G. Evelyn Hutchinson publishes *A Treatise on Limnology*, establishing the 11-major-type classification by origin (76 subtypes).
- **1959** — Hebgen Lake earthquake creates Quake Lake (Montana, USA) by landslide damming.
- **2005 (spring)** — Unusually heavy rains temporarily fill a lake in Death Valley (USA); evaporates by summer.
- **3 June 2005** — Lake Beloye (Nizhny Novgorod Oblast, Russia) drains completely in minutes via subsurface shift.
- **1990–2020** — Over half of the world’s large lakes shrink, driven partly by climate change and water diversion.
- **Present** — Cassini–Huygens mission confirms liquid ethane/methane lakes on Titan (e.g., Kraken Mare, ~400,000 km²).

## Body

### Definition and basic characteristics
A lake is a water-filled basin on land, separate from the ocean though possibly connected by outflowing rivers. Most are freshwater; some are saline (salt lakes) with salinity exceeding seawater. Lakes vary widely in area and volume but collectively cover ~2.5×10⁶ km². No universal scientific criterion distinguishes lakes from ponds; size-based thresholds (2–40 hectares) are commonly used. Lakes differ from lagoons (shallow, tidally influenced coastal pools) and reservoirs (artificially controlled for human use).

### Distribution
Most lakes are freshwater and concentrated in the Northern Hemisphere at higher latitudes. Canada has an estimated 31,752 lakes >3 km² and ≥2 million total; Finland has 168,000 lakes ≥500 m² (57,000 ≥10,000 m²). The total number of lakes globally is undetermined because small ponds dominate counts but are unmapped; one study estimated 304 million lakes and ponds, 91% ≤1 hectare. Yet >99% of lake water volume resides in <100 large lakes, because volume scales superlinearly with area. Endorheic lakes lack outflow, losing water only by evaporation/seepage. Extraterrestrial lakes exist on Titan; Mars has dry paleolake beds and one confirmed subglacial lake.

### Classification by origin (Hutchinson, 1957)
Hutchinson’s landmark system recognizes 11 major types (76 subtypes):
- **Tectonic lakes**: Formed by crustal deformation (faulting, tilting, folding, warping). Rift lakes (e.g., Lake Baikal, Central African Rift lakes) occupy rift valleys. Uplifted sea-floor basins yield the Caspian Sea, Aral Sea, and Pontocaspian lakes. Crustal extension creates alternating grabens/horsts, disrupting drainage and forming endorheic salt lakes (e.g., Great Salt Lake, Dead Sea) in arid zones. Sag ponds arise from faulting.
- **Volcanic lakes**: Occupy craters, maars, or calderas (e.g., Crater Lake, Oregon, from Mount Mazama’s collapse c. 4860 BC). Also form when lava flows or lahars dam rivers (e.g., Malheur Lake, Oregon). Crater lakes most closely approximate a circular shape.
- **Glacial lakes**: Created by direct glacial/ice-sheet action—carved rock basins, morainic/outwash dams, drift basins. Most numerous globally; most northern European and North American lakes are glacial legacies. Subtypes: proglacial, subglacial, finger lakes, epishelf lakes (freshwater dammed behind ice shelves, mostly Antarctica).
- **Fluvial (riverine) lakes**: Produced by running water. **Oxbow lakes** (crescent-shaped) form when meandering rivers cut off horseshoe bends. **Fluviatile dams** occur when tributary sediment blocks the main river. **Lateral lakes** form when main-river sediment blocks a tributary (often via levees). **Floodplain lakes** (four types) arise from floodplain basin creation and are flushed during high floods.
- **Solution lakes**: Basins formed by dissolution of soluble bedrock (karst). Cavities collapse into sinkholes; if groundwater is near surface, they fill as solution/karst lakes (e.g., Dalmatian coast, Florida). Limestone caves host underground lakes.
- **Landslide lakes**: River valleys blocked by mudflows, rockslides, or screes. Common in mountains; typically short-lived. Quake Lake (1959) and Sarez Lake (1911, Usoi Dam still holding) are examples. Tal-y-llyn Lake (Wales) dates to last glaciation (~20 ka).
- **Aeolian lakes**: Wind-formed in arid zones or as relict paleoclimate indicators. Basins dammed by wind-blown sand, interdunal lakes between dunes, deflation basins (e.g., Moses Lake, Washington; Badain Jaran Desert, China).
- **Shoreline lakes**: Blocked estuaries or uneven beach-ridge accretion by longshore currents. Include maritime coastal lakes (drowned estuaries), lakes between tombolos/spits, lakes cut off from larger lakes by bars, and lakes divided by converging spits.
- **Organic lakes**: Created by plants/animals; rare, small, ephemeral. Basins from beaver dams, coral structures, vegetation dams. **Peat lakes**: partly decomposed plant material holds vegetated surface below water table; low nutrients, acidic, low bottom oxygen.
- **Artificial (anthropogenic) lakes**: Human-made by damming rivers, rerouting water, or filling excavations. Uses: drinking water, hydroelectricity, flood control, agriculture/aquaculture, recreation. Upper Silesia (Poland, >4,000 water bodies) and Lusatian Lake District (Germany) are anthropogenic lake districts. Sudarshana Lake (Gujarat, India) dates to Chandragupta Maurya’s reign.
- **Meteorite (impact) lakes**: Formed by extraterrestrial impacts. Lonar Lake (India), Lake El’gygytgyn (Siberia), Pingualuit Crater Lake (Quebec). Can preserve long paleoclimatic sediment records.

### Other classification methods
- **Thermal stratification** (Forel, modified by Hutchinson & Löffler): Density maximum at 4°C creates layers—hypolimnion (cold bottom), metalimnion (transition), epilimnion (warm surface). **Holomictic** lakes mix completely (subtypes: amictic, cold/warm monomictic, dimictic, polymictic, oligomictic). **Meromictic** lakes have unmixed layers; deep layer lacks dissolved oxygen, preserving undisturbed sediments. Stratification can also be salinity-driven (halocline/chemocline).
- **Seasonal water-level variation**: Ephemeral/intermittent (seasonal fill/dry), dry lake (irregular), perennial (year-round), playa lake (shallow, intermittent in arid zones), vlei (South African shallow seasonal lake).
- **Water chemistry**: Acid lake (pH <6.5; highly acidic <5.5; sources: mine pits, igneous landscapes, peat bogs, volcanic craters, acid rain). Salt/saline/brine lake (endorheic, arid, high neutral salts; e.g., Great Salt Lake, Dead Sea). Alkali sink/flat (shallow, periodically flooded, evaporitic minerals). Salt pan (small depression, evaporates to salt deposit). Saline pan (ephemeral acid saline lake with bottom crust).
- **Non-water liquids**: Lava lakes (molten basalt in vents/craters). Hydrocarbon lakes (liquid ethane/methane on Titan, detected by Cassini–Huygens).

### Paleolakes
Lakes that existed under past hydrological conditions. Identified by relict shorelines (paleoshorelines), lacustrine sediments, and fossils. **Former lakes** no longer exist (e.g., Owens Lake, California; common in Basin and Range). **Shrunken lakes** persist but reduced (e.g., Lake Agassiz → remnants Lake Winnipeg, Lake Winnipegosis). Scientific/economic value: shaped basin floors/piedmonts; sediments hold geologic/paleontologic data; pre-Quaternary deposits host oil shale, shale gas, petroleum/natural gas source rocks; shore strata sometimes contain coal.

### Physical and chemical characteristics
Lake level changes reflect input (precipitation, catchment runoff, groundwater, artificial) vs. output (evaporation, surface/groundwater outflow, human extraction). Nutrient richness categories: **oligotrophic** (nutrient-poor, clear, low plant growth), **mesotrophic** (moderate), **eutrophic** (enriched, algal blooms), **hypertrophic** (excessive, poor clarity, devastating blooms, low oxygen; often from fertilizer runoff). Thermal stratification creates thermoclines; temperate lakes mix seasonally (turnover), oxygenating depths. Deep tropical lakes never reach 4°C at surface, so no mixing; deep layers become anoxic, CO₂-saturated (sometimes SO₂ from volcanism). Rare mixing events (earthquakes, landslides) trigger **limnic eruptions**—rapid gas release (e.g., Lake Nyos, Cameroon); CO₂, heavier than air, can asphyxiate downslope settlements. Lake beds (inorganic/organic) influence nutrient availability. Varved sediments (annual black/white layers) track paleoevents. Lakes moderate local climate via water’s high specific heat (4,186 J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹): daytime cooling (sea breeze), nighttime warming (land breeze).

### Limnology and biological zones
Limnology studies inland waters and ecosystems. Three main zones: **littoral** (sloped near shore), **photic/open-water** (sunlight abundant), **profundal/benthic** (deep, little light). Light penetration (Secchi depth, measured with 20-cm Secchi disk) depends on turbidity from suspended sedimentary/biological particles (algae, detritus). Colors: yellow/brown from decaying plants, green from algae, reddish-brown from iron oxides in shallow water. Bottom-feeding detritivorous fish stir mud; piscivorous fish reduce planktivores, increasing algae (trophic cascade). Biological subzones: epilittoral (above water), littoral (to max macrophyte depth), littoriprofundal (transition, algae/bacteria), profundal (sediment, no vegetation). Algal communities: epipelic (sediments), epilithic (rocks), epipsammic (sand), epiphytic (macrophytes), epizooic (animals), metaphyton (littoral, unattached).

### Disappearance
Lakes infill with sediment, becoming wetlands (swamp/marsh). Reeds accelerate closure via peat formation; peat fires can reverse it (dynamic equilibrium). Fire suppression has artificially converted many shallow lakes to marshes. Turbid lakes and those with many herbivorous fish disappear slower. Succession: plant mats → peat moss/fen → trees → forest. **Seasonal disappearance**: intermittent/ephemeral lakes in karst (e.g., Lake Cerknica, Slovenia; Lag Prau Pulte, Graubünden) or endorheic basins after heavy rain (Death Valley 2005; Sevier Lake, Utah). **Rapid disappearance**: Lake Beloye (Russia, 2005) drained in minutes via subsurface shift to Oka River. **Permafrost thaw**: shrinking/disappearance of hundreds of large Arctic lakes in western Siberia as thaw allows drainage. **Human causes**: Aral Sea “murdered” by irrigation diversions; 1990–2020 >50% of large lakes shrank (climate change, water use).

### Extraterrestrial lakes
**Titan** (Saturn’s moon): only body with confirmed large lakes. Cassini–Huygens detected liquid ethane/methane. Kraken Mare (~400,000 km², ~5× Lake Superior), Ligeia Mare (~150,000 km², ~2× Lake Superior). **Io** (Jupiter’s moon): possible liquid sulfur lakes in volcanic calderas (lava analogs). **Mars**: one confirmed subglacial lake near south pole; geologic evidence for ancient surface lakes. **Moon**: dark basaltic plains (lacus) once thought to be water lakes.

### Notable lakes (records)
- **Largest by surface area**: Caspian Sea (371,000 km²; geographically a lake).
- **Largest freshwater by area**: Lake Michigan–Huron (117,400 km², hydrologically one lake); if split, Lake Superior (82,100 km²).
- **Deepest & largest by volume (freshwater)**: Lake Baikal (1,637 m max, 749 m mean, 23,600 km³ volume; 630 km long).
- **Oldest**: Lake Baikal (~25–30 Ma), then Lake Tanganyika; Lake Maracaibo debated (now sea-connected).
- **Longest**: Lake Tanganyika (~660 km centerline; 3rd by volume, 2nd oldest, 2nd deepest at 1,470 m).
- **Highest (any size)**: Ojos del Salado crater lake (6,390 m).
- **Highest large (>250 km²)**: Pumoyong Tso, Tibet (5,018 m, 290 km²).
- **Highest navigable**: Lake Titicaca (3,812 m; largest in South America).
- **Lowest**: Dead Sea (−418 m; hypersaline).
- **Longest coastline**: Lake Michigan–Huron (~5,250 km excl. inner islands); Lake Huron alone (~2,980 km) still leads.
- **Largest island in a lake**: Manitoulin Island (Lake Michigan–Huron, 2,766 km²); Lake Manitou on it is largest lake-on-island-in-lake.
- **Largest lake on an island**: Nettilling Lake, Baffin Island (5,542 km²).
- **Largest naturally draining two directions**: Wollaston Lake.
- **Largest resurgent caldera lake**: Lake Toba, Sumatra.
- **Largest within a single city**: Lake Wanapitei, Sudbury, Ontario (since 2001 boundaries; formerly Lake Ramsey).
- **Only saltwater lake with crocodiles**: Lake Enriquillo, Dominican Republic.
- **Largest with no islands**: Lake Bernard, Ontario (claim).
- **Most shoreline per area**: Lake Saimaa/Saimaa basin, Finland (~15,000 km total).
- **Largest in one country**: Lake Michigan (USA); if Michigan–Huron counted as one, Great Bear Lake (Canada).
- **Largest lake-on-island-in-lake-on-island**: Crater Lake on Vulcano Island in Lake Taal on Luzon, Philippines.
- **Northernmost named**: Upper Dumbell Lake, Nunavut (82°28′N; 5.2 km SW of Alert).
- **Ancient lakes (>1 Ma)**: only 20 globally.

## Terms
- ****Endorheic lake**** — A lake with no natural outflow, losing water only by evaporation and/or underground seepage.
- ****Thermal stratification**** — Layering of lake water by temperature/density: epilimnion (warm surface), metalimnion (transition), hypolimnion (cold bottom).
- ****Holomictic**** — Lake that achieves uniform temperature/density and mixes completely at least once per year.
- ****Meromictic**** — Lake with permanently unmixed layers; deep layer lacks dissolved oxygen, preserving undisturbed sediments.
- ****Limnic eruption**** — Sudden release of dissolved gases (CO₂, CH₄, SO₂) from deep water during rare mixing, potentially causing mass asphyxiation.
- ****Oxbow lake**** — Crescent-shaped fluvial lake formed when a meandering river cuts off a horseshoe bend.
- ****Paleolake**** — A lake that existed in the past under different hydrological conditions; identified by relict shorelines and sediments.
- ****Secchi depth**** — Depth at which a 20-cm black-and-white Secchi disk disappears; measures water transparency/turbidity.
- ****Trophic state (oligo/meso/eu/hypertrophic)**** — Classification by nutrient richness and resulting plant/algal growth: oligotrophic (low), mesotrophic (moderate), eutrophic (high), hypertrophic (excessive).
- ****Varve**** — Annual sediment pair (dark winter organic layer, light summer mineral layer) used for paleochronology.

## Debates and open questions
- **Lake vs. pond definition**: No internationally accepted scientific or political boundary; size thresholds (2–40 ha) are arbitrary and discipline-dependent.
- **Lake Maracaibo status**: Considered by some the second-oldest lake; others argue it has become a lagoon/bay due to sea-level connection.
- **Global lake count**: Undetermined because small ponds (<1 ha) are abundant but unmapped; estimates (e.g., 304 million) rely on extrapolation.
- **Mars surface lakes**: Geologic evidence suggests ancient lakes, but only one subglacial lake is confirmed; extent and duration of past surface water debated.
- **Aral Sea recovery**: Whether partial restoration is feasible given ongoing irrigation demands and climate trends remains contested.
- **Permafrost-thaw lake loss**: Magnitude and rate of Arctic lake drainage from thawing permafrost are active research areas.
