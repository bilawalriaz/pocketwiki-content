# English literature

## Overview
English literature encompasses works written in English from the English-speaking world. Its 1,400-year development reflects the language's evolution from Old to Modern English, shaped by history, cultural exchange, and the British Empire. It is a global tradition with major contributions from writers across the former Empire and the United States.

## Timeline
- **c. 450** — Anglo-Saxon settlement begins; Old English emerges.
- **c. 1000** — Surviving manuscript of *Beowulf* is dated.
- **1066** — Norman Conquest; French becomes the court language.
- **c. 1387–1400** — Geoffrey Chaucer writes *The Canterbury Tales*.
- **1476** — William Caxton introduces the printing press to England.
- **1590–1596** — Edmund Spenser publishes *The Faerie Queene*.
- **1611** — The King James Bible is published.
- **1667** — John Milton publishes *Paradise Lost*.
- **1719** — Daniel Defoe publishes *Robinson Crusoe*.
- **1798** — Wordsworth and Coleridge publish *Lyrical Ballads*.
- **1922** — Joyce publishes *Ulysses*; Eliot publishes *The Waste Land*.
- **1997** — J.K. Rowling publishes *Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone*.

## Body

### Old English Literature (449–1066)
Old English literature encompasses works written after the Anglo-Saxon settlement. Genres include epic poetry, hagiography, and chronicles. The most famous work is *Beowulf*, a national epic set in Scandinavia. Oral tradition was strong, and most authors are anonymous, though Cædmon, Bede, Alfred the Great, and Cynewulf are known. Classical texts were adapted, such as King Alfred's translation of Boethius' *Consolation of Philosophy*.

### Middle English Literature (1066–1485)
Following the Norman Conquest, French became the elite language, and Middle English emerged as the vernacular. Religious literature remained popular, and new genres like romance appeared. Major 14th-century writers include William Langland (*Piers Plowman*), the Pearl Poet (*Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*), and Geoffrey Chaucer, whose *The Canterbury Tales* helped legitimize Middle English. The period saw the first complete English Bible translations (Wycliffe's Bible) and ended with the spread of the Chancery Standard and the printing press. Medieval theatre evolved from liturgical enactments into mystery, miracle, and morality plays.

### Tudor and Stuart Periods (1485–1714)
The Tudor period saw the English Renaissance, influenced by classical learning and the Reformation. Thomas More's *Utopia* (1516) and the poetry of Thomas Wyatt and Henry Howard introduced new forms. The Elizabethan era was the height of Renaissance drama, dominated by William Shakespeare. Other major dramatists included Christopher Marlowe and Ben Jonson. The Stuart period continued with Shakespeare's later works and saw the rise of Metaphysical poets like John Donne. The King James Bible (1611) became a literary standard. After the Interregnum, the Restoration brought a revival of drama and the rise of the novel, with Aphra Behn's *Oroonoko* (1688) as an early example. John Milton's epic *Paradise Lost* (1667) was a landmark.

### Georgian and Romantic Eras (1714–1837)
The Georgian era began with Augustan literature, featuring satire and the development of the novel by writers like Daniel Defoe and Jonathan Swift. The Age of Sensibility followed, with Samuel Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755) and the sentimental novel. Romanticism, reacting to the Industrial Revolution, emphasized emotion, nature, and individualism. Key poets include William Wordsworth, Samuel Taylor Coleridge, Lord Byron, Percy Bysshe Shelley, and John Keats. The novel was advanced by Sir Walter Scott's historical romances and Jane Austen's social realism. American literature diverged, with early works by Benjamin Franklin and Thomas Paine.

### Victorian Era (1837–1901)
The Victorian era saw the novel become the leading genre, often serialised and addressing social issues. Charles Dickens, William Makepeace Thackeray, the Brontë sisters, George Eliot, and Thomas Hardy were major novelists. Sage writing, a genre of social criticism, was developed by Thomas Carlyle and John Ruskin. Poetry was led by Alfred, Lord Tennyson, and Robert Browning, who perfected the dramatic monologue. Genre fiction emerged, including detective stories (Wilkie Collins), science fiction (H. G. Wells), and fantasy (George MacDonald). American literature grew in stature with writers like Mark Twain, Henry James, and Emily Dickinson.

### 20th Century
Modernism, emerging from disillusionment with Victorian certainty, was influenced by new ideas in philosophy and science. Early modernists included Joseph Conrad, W. B. Yeats, and T. S. Eliot. The novel was revolutionised by stream-of-consciousness techniques in the works of James Joyce (*Ulysses*, 1922) and Virginia Woolf. Between the wars, writers like Aldous Huxley and George Orwell explored dystopian themes. Post-1945, literature saw the rise of the "Angry Young Men" in drama, the Theatre of the Absurd (Samuel Beckett), and the continued dominance of the novel with writers like Graham Greene and Iris Murdoch. Postmodernism, characterised by fragmentation and metafiction, emerged later. Literature from the Commonwealth of Nations, including authors from Nigeria, India, and the Caribbean, became a major part of the English literary tradition.

## Terms
- **Old English**: The earliest historical form of the English language, spoken in Anglo-Saxon England from c. 450 to 1066.
- **Middle English**: The form of the English language used from the Norman Conquest (1066) until the late 15th century.
- **Vernacular**: The language or dialect spoken by the ordinary people in a particular country or region.
- **Alliterative verse**: A form of verse that uses alliteration as the principal structural device, common in Old and Middle English poetry.
- **Chancery Standard**: A written form of English that emerged in the late 15th century from the government offices in London, helping to standardise the language.
- **Stream of consciousness**: A narrative mode that attempts to depict the multitudinous thoughts and feelings which pass through the mind.
- **Augustan literature**: A literary style of the early 18th century in Britain, characterised by satire, order, and classical allusion.
- **Romanticism**: An artistic and literary movement of the late 18th and early 19th centuries that emphasised emotion, individualism, and nature.
- **Modernism**: A broad movement in the arts and literature of the early 20th century, characterised by a deliberate break with traditional forms.
- **Postmodernism**: A late 20th-century movement characterised by skepticism, irony, and a rejection of grand narratives.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise date and authorship of *Beowulf* are debated, with composition dated between the 8th and early 11th centuries.
- The beginning of the novel in English is impossible to satisfactorily date, with candidates ranging from works in the Restoration period to the 18th century.
- The transition from modernism to postmodernism in English literature is contested, with no agreement on when it occurred.
- The definition, scope, and importance of postmodern literature are subjects of ongoing debate.
