# Modern era

## Overview

The modern era is the current historical period of human history, originally applied to European and Western history after classical antiquity and the Middle Ages, often from around 1500. Today, historians commonly use "early modern" for the period from the Middle Ages to the 19th century, and "modern" for events from the 19th century to today; the period since 1945 is also called contemporary history. The modern era is marked by transformative developments in science, politics, warfare, and technology, including the French Revolution, the Industrial Revolution, and the transition from nationalism toward a liberal international order. It has also been an Age of Discovery and globalization, during which European powers and their colonies strengthened their political, economic, and cultural colonization of the rest of the world, permanently changing how people live.

## Timeline

- **c. 1450s** — Gutenberg's moveable-type printing press ushers in the information age
- **1453** — Ottoman conquest of Constantinople; fall of the Byzantine Empire
- **1492** — Columbus's first voyage to the Americas initiates sustained Europe–New World contact
- **1517** — Martin Luther posts the 95 Theses, igniting the Reformation
- **1543** — Copernicus proposes heliocentrism in *De revolutionibus*
- **1618–1648** — Thirty Years' War devastates Europe; Peace of Westphalia (1648) establishes state sovereignty
- **c. 1760** — Industrial Revolution begins in Britain with textile mechanization and the steam engine
- **1789–1799** — French Revolution overthrows the Bourbon monarchy
- **1914–1918** — World War I; collapse of four empires
- **1939–1945** — World War II; Holocaust; atomic bombings; ~62 million deaths
- **1945–1991** — Cold War between the US-led West and Soviet-led East

## Body

### Terminology and periodization

Eras cannot easily be defined for periodization, the process of categorizing the past into named blocks of time for analysis. The year 1500 is an approximate starting point because many major events changed the Western world around then: the fall of Constantinople (1453), Gutenberg's printing press (1450s), Columbus's voyage (1492), and the Reformation (1517). The term "modern" was coined shortly before 1585 to describe the beginning of a new era. The term "early modern" was long thought to have been invented in the 1930s or mid-20th century, gaining traction in the 1960s; however, Justus Nipperdey pointed to its widespread usage by American historians around 1900, noting that by the interwar years it permeated textbooks, seminars, conferences, and job descriptions. The difference between "early modern" and "modern" was defined by the French Revolution and Industrial Revolution. The European Renaissance (14th–16th centuries), starting in Italy, marks the transition from the Late Middle Ages into early modern history.

"Modernity" and "modernism" refer to a new way of thinking distinct from medieval thought. "Postmodernism," coined in 1949, describes a movement in art rather than a historical period; "postmodernity" was later coined to describe major changes in the 1960s in economy, society, culture, and philosophy. These terms stem from European history; in worldwide usage (China, India, Islam), they are applied differently, often in the context of contact with European culture in the Age of Discovery.

### Early modern period (c. 1400–1800)

The Renaissance and Reformation (c. 1400–1550) saw Gutenberg's printing press (c. 1450s), the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople (1453), and Columbus's first voyage (1492). Amerigo Vespucci's *Mundus Novus* (1503) was the first printed articulation that the western lands were a new continent, not Asia's edge. Machiavelli's *The Prince* (c. 1513) founded modern political philosophy; Luther's 95 Theses (1517) ignited the Reformation; Copernicus's heliocentrism (1543) and the Council of Trent (1545, beginning the Catholic Counter-Reformation) followed. Mercantilism rose as the prevailing economic theory.

The Scientific Revolution and early Baroque (c. 1550–1700) included Tycho Brahe's observation of a new star (1572), the defeat of the Spanish Armada (1588, securing English naval supremacy), Kepler's elliptical orbits (1609), Galileo's telescopic observations (1610), the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648), and the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which established state sovereignty. Newton's *Principia* (1687) formulated the laws of motion and universal gravitation; the Glorious Revolution (1688) affirmed parliamentary supremacy; Louis XIV's reign (1643–1715) epitomized absolutism.

The 18th century saw the War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714), the War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748), and the Seven Years' War (1756–1763), the first truly global conflict. The Industrial Revolution began c. 1760 in Britain. The American Revolution (1775–1783) produced US independence; the French Revolution (1789–1799) overthrew the Bourbon monarchy. The Ottoman Old Regime (1703–1789) was followed by Ottoman decline and modernization.

### 19th century

Historians sometimes define a 19th-century era from 1815 (Congress of Vienna) to 1914 (World War I); Eric Hobsbawm defined a "long nineteenth century" from 1789 to 1914. The Spanish, Portuguese, and Ottoman Empires declined; the Holy Roman Empire was dissolved in 1806; the Mughal Empire effectively ended after the Indian Rebellion of 1857. The Napoleonic era (1799–1815) began with Napoleon's coup and ended at Waterloo; the Congress of Vienna (1814–1815) sought to restore the pre-Revolutionary balance of power.

The Industrial Revolution replaced manual labor with industry and machinery, beginning with textile mechanization and iron-making, enabled by canals, roads, and railways. Steam power (fuelled by coal) underpinned production increases; all-metal machine tools (early 19th century) facilitated further manufacturing. Its dating is debated: Hobsbawm held it "broke out" in the 1780s, felt by the 1830s–1840s; T. S. Ashton placed it roughly 1760–1830. Its impact is often compared to the Neolithic Revolution. The First Industrial Revolution gave way to the Second around 1850, with steam-powered ships, railways, the internal combustion engine, and electric power.

After the Napoleonic Wars, the British Empire became the world's leading power, controlling one-quarter of the world's population and one-third of its land area, enforcing a Pax Britannica. Slavery was greatly reduced: after a slave revolt in Haiti, Britain forced the Barbary pirates to stop enslaving Europeans, passed the Slavery Abolition Act 1833, and charged its navy with ending the global slave trade. Slavery was abolished in Russia (1861), the US (Emancipation Proclamation, 1863), and Brazil (1888). The Scramble for Africa was formalized at the Berlin West Africa Conference (1884–1885), where European powers claimed African spheres of influence. Electricity, steel, and petroleum fuelled the Second Industrial Revolution, enabling Germany, Japan, and the US to become great powers; Russia and China failed to keep pace, causing social unrest.

### 20th century

The 20th century was distinguished by unprecedented pace and global scale of change. Technology and medicine impacted even the Global South; large-scale industry and centralized media enabled brutal dictatorships, while increased communications contributed to democratization. Developments included airplanes, space exploration, nuclear technology, genetics, and the Information Age. Major political developments included the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, two world wars, and the Cold War; the British Empire lost political power over Commonwealth countries via the Statute of Westminster, the Canada Act 1982, and the Australia Act 1986, as well as the independence of India, Pakistan, South Africa, and Ireland.

World War I (July 1914 – 11 November 1918) pitted the Allied Powers (British Empire, France, Russia until March 1918, Japan, and the US after 1917) against the Central Powers (German, Austro-Hungarian, and Ottoman Empires). It caused the disintegration of four empires and radical map changes. Fighting occurred along the Western Front's trench system, on the Eastern Front, at sea, and—for the first time—from the air. Over 9 million soldiers died; more died of the worldwide influenza outbreak than in hostilities. World War I created a decisive break with the old world order and was an important factor in World War II.

The Interwar period (1918–1939) included the Roaring Twenties, the Great Depression, and the rise of communism in Russia and fascism in Italy and Germany. World War II (1939–1945) was the largest and deadliest war in history, culminating in the Holocaust and ending with the atom bomb. It began on 1 September 1939 with Germany's invasion of Poland; the UK and France declared war within two days, and the Soviet Union joined Germany on 17 September per the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact. Germany invaded western Europe in May 1940; France surrendered six weeks later. The Tripartite Pact (27 September 1940) formed the Axis; Germany invaded the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941, creating a two-front war. Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941, bringing the US and China into the war. Italy surrendered in September 1943; Germany in May 1945; Japan on 2 September 1945 after the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Around 62 million died (estimates vary), about 60% civilians; the Soviet Union lost ~23 million, China ~10 million, Poland ~5.6 million (16% of its population). The Holocaust was the deliberate, systematic murder of millions of Jews and other groups by the Nazi regime. After the war, Europe split into NATO-aligned Western and Warsaw Pact-aligned Eastern spheres; power shifted to the US and Soviet Union.

The Cold War (1945–1991) dominated politics between the "West" (US, Western Europe, Japan) and "East" (Soviet Union, Eastern Europe, China), ending with the Soviet collapse. The Korean War, Vietnam War, Soviet–Afghan War, and Gulf War impacted political life; the 1960s counterculture and the rise of computers changed society.

### Post–Cold War era (1990s)

At the end of the 20th century, more technological advances had been made than in all preceding history; computers, the Internet, and other technologies radically altered daily life. Problems included the widening gap between rich and poor nations, disease (West Nile, Avian influenza, malaria, and HIV/AIDS epidemics in southern Africa), increased globalization and Americanization causing anti-Western feelings (especially in the Middle East), and the spread of English as a global language. Terrorism, dictatorship, and nuclear weapons proliferation—including fears that terrorists had already acquired nuclear weapons—required immediate attention.

## Terms

- **Early modern period**: The period after the Middle Ages up to the 19th century, distinguished from "modern" by the French and Industrial Revolutions.
- **Periodization**: The process of categorizing the past into quantified, named blocks of time for analysis.
- **Modernity/Modernism**: New ways of thinking distinct from medieval thought.
- **Postmodernism**: Coined 1949; a movement in art, not a historical period.
- **Mercantilism**: The prevailing economic theory of the early modern period.
- **Pax Britannica**: British-enforced peace and trade promotion after the Napoleonic Wars.
- **Scramble for Africa**: The formal division of African spheres of influence among European powers, initiated at the Berlin Conference (1884–1885).
- **Holocaust**: The deliberate, systematic murder of millions of Jews and other "unwanted" groups by the Nazi regime during World War II.
- **Cold War**: The post-1945 political rivalry between the US-led West and Soviet-led East, ending with the Soviet collapse in 1991.

## Debates and open questions

- The dating of the Industrial Revolution is debated: Hobsbawm held it "broke out" in the 1780s and was not fully felt until the 1830s–1840s; Ashton placed it roughly between 1760 and 1830.
- The origin of the term "early modern" is contested: long thought to be 1930s or mid-20th century, Nipperdey documented its widespread use by American historians around 1900.
- Whether the Holocaust was intended from the war's beginning or planned later is debated; persecution of Jews extended well before the war (e.g., Kristallnacht).
- Whether the gap between rich and poor nations can be fixed is disputed: some argue wealth is finite, others that powerful nations are not doing enough to help developing countries.
