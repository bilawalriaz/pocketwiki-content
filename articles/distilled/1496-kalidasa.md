# Kalidasa

## Overview
Kālidāsa (4th–5th century CE) is widely regarded as ancient India’s greatest poet and playwright, defining the Classical Sanskrit literary tradition. His seven authenticated works—three plays, two epic poems (*mahākāvyas*), and two lyric poems—draw on Hindu Puranas and philosophy to explore love, duty, and the interplay of nature and human emotion. He likely flourished at the Gupta court of Chandragupta II (r. 380–415 CE), a period of cultural efflorescence, though his exact dates and birthplace remain debated. His *Abhijñānaśākuntalam* became the first Sanskrit drama known in Europe, profoundly influencing Goethe and subsequent world literature.

## Timeline
- **4th–5th century CE** — Kālidāsa’s active period; likely court poet to Chandragupta II (Gupta era).
- **c. 473 CE** — Earliest paleographical evidence: Mandsaur Sun temple inscription contains verses imitating *Meghadūta* and *Ṛtusaṃhāra* (author unnamed).
- **634 CE** — Aihole inscription (Karnataka) first mentions Kālidāsa by name alongside poet Bhāravi.
- **10th century** — Kashmirian scholar Vallabhadeva writes earliest surviving commentaries on Kālidāsa.
- **15th century** — Kolāchala Mallinātha Suri composes influential commentaries under Vijayanagara king Deva Rāya II.
- **1791 CE** — Sir William Jones publishes first English translation of *Abhijñānaśākuntalam*.
- **1792 CE** — Jones publishes *Ṛtusaṃhāra* in original Sanskrit.
- **1899–1990** — Māni Mādhava Chākyār choreographs Kālidāsa plays in Koodiyattam tradition (Kerala).
- **1910** — Gustav Holst premieres *The Cloud Messenger*, choral work based on *Meghadūta*.

## Body

### Life and Historical Context
Almost nothing certain is known of Kālidāsa’s biography; inferences come solely from his works. Tradition links him to a king named Vikramāditya. While a legendary Vikramāditya supposedly ruled Ujjain c. 1st century BCE, most scholars identify the patron as the historical Chandragupta II (r. 380–415 CE), placing Kālidāsa in the Gupta "Golden Age." His career may have extended into the reigns of Kumāragupta I (414–455 CE) and Skandagupta (455–467 CE). The 634 CE Aihole inscription is the earliest epigraph to name him, confirming his canonical status by the early 7th century.

### Birthplace Theories
Geographical allusions in his poems have spawned rival theories, none universally accepted.
*   **Ujjayini (Malava):** Historian V. V. Mirashi argues the poet’s detailed, affectionate description of Ujjayini in the *Meghadūta*—urging a cloud to detour there—marks it as his home. Mirashi notes Kali worship was prevalent in Malava during the 4th–5th centuries, fitting the name *Kālidāsa* ("servant of Kali").
*   **Kashmir:** Scholar Lachhmi Dhar Kalla (1926) cites references to Himalayan flora (saffron, deodar), fauna (musk deer), geography (tarns, glades), and Kashmiri-specific legends (e.g., the *Nilamata Purana* figure Nikumbha in *Raghuvaṃśa*). He reads *Śakuntalā* as an allegory for Pratyabhijñā philosophy (Kashmir Shaivism) and suggests Huna invasions forced the poet south. Mirashi counters that the Huns invaded Kashmir only in the 6th century (post-dating Kālidāsa), that the *Nilamata Purana* borrows *from* Kālidāsa, and that the *Rājataraṅgiṇī* omits him from Kashmir’s literary greats.
*   **Vidarbha:** Indologist Peter Peterson points to the *Vaidarbhī* style and Vidarbha princesses (Mālavikā, Indumatī) as evidence. Mirashi dismisses the style as non-exclusive to the region.
*   **Central India (Dashapura/Vidisha):** Scholars Hara Prasad Shastri and S. M. Paranjape argue for Dashapura (Mandsaur) or Vidisha based on minute topographical knowledge and a possible link to 6th-century king Yaśodharman. Mirashi rejects the Yaśodharman synchronism as too late.
*   **Bengal, Uttarakhand, Mithila:** Local traditions claim Bengal (based on Kali-worship etymology and legends Mirashi calls "old wives' tales"), Kaviltha village in Uttarakhand (annual festival, memorial committee founded 1973), and Kalidas Dih in Bihar’s Madhubani district.

### Authentic Works
Modern consensus, based on literary analysis, attributes seven works to a single author:
**Epics (*Mahākāvya*)**
*   *Kumārasambhava* ("Birth of Kumāra/Kārtikeya"): Narrates Pārvatī’s birth, penance, marriage to Śiva, and the birth of their son Kumāra, destined to slay the demon Tāraka.
*   *Raghuvaṃśa* ("Dynasty of Raghu"): Chronicles the Solar Dynasty kings from Dilīpa to Agnivarṇa, emphasizing ideal kingship (*rājadharma*).

**Lyric Poems (*Khaṇḍakāvya*)**
*   *Meghadūta* ("Cloud Messenger"): A *yakṣa* exiled to central India sends a message to his beloved in Alakā via a monsoon cloud. Composed in the lyrical *mandākrāntā* metre, it is celebrated for its fusion of landscape and longing.
*   *Ṛtusaṃhāra* ("Gathering of Seasons"): Describes the six Indian seasons through the lens of erotic love (*śṛṅgāra*).

**Plays (*Nāṭaka*)**
*   *Mālavikāgnimitra*: King Agnimitra loves Mālavikā, a servant girl revealed as a Vidarbha princess, resolving the queen’s jealousy.
*   *Abhijñānaśākuntalam* ("Recognition of Śakuntalā"): King Duṣyanta marries hermit-girl Śakuntalā; a curse by sage Durvāsas makes him forget her until a lost signet ring is recovered. Widely considered his masterpiece.
*   *Vikramorvaśīya* ("Ūrvaśī Won by Valour"): King Purūravas loves celestial nymph Ūrvaśī; after trials including her transformation into a vine, a curse is lifted allowing them to remain on earth.

### Disputed Authorship and "False Kalidasas"
Several scholars (M. Srinivasachariar, T. S. Narayana Sastri) argue the seven works reflect three distinct authors named Kālidāsa: Mātṛgupta (plays), Medharudra (epics and *Meghadūta*), and Kotijit (*Ṛtusaṃhāra* and minor works). Sastri lists six additional historical figures bearing the name (e.g., Padmagupta, Yamakakavi). Indologist Siegfried Lienhard catalogs numerous poems falsely attributed to Kālidāsa (e.g., *Nalodaya*, *Śyāmala-Daṇḍaka*, *Sṛtabodha*). K. Krishnamoorthy suggests "Vikramāditya" and "Kālidāsa" became generic titles for "patron king" and "court poet."

### Legends
Popular folklore, unsupported by historical evidence, depicts Kālidāsa as an illiterate shepherd transformed into a genius by the goddess Kali (explaining his name). Another legend claims he was murdered in Lanka (Sri Lanka) by treachery while visiting King Kumāradāsa.

### Influence and Reception
Kālidāsa became the archetype of Sanskrit literature. The 7th-century poet Bāṇabhaṭṭa praised his "honey-laden" sayings; Jayadeva called him *kavikulaguru* (lord of poets). The *Daśam Granth* (Sikh scripture) lists him among seven Brahma avatars. His influence permeates later Sanskrit and vernacular Indian literature; Rabindranath Tagore’s monsoon poetry echoes *Meghadūta*’s romanticism. The 13th-century Old Javanese *kakawin* *Sumanasāntaka* by Mpu Monaguṇa adapts *Raghuvaṃśa* (Aja-Indumatī episode) into a 1,100-stanza epic reflecting Javanese life.

In the West, Sir William Jones’s 1791 English translation of *Abhijñānaśākuntalam* (rendered into German 1792) electrified Herder and Goethe, the latter hailing its fusion of nature, moral purity, and sovereign grace. Wilhelm von Humboldt praised his depiction of nature’s influence on lovers. This reception spurred 19th/20th-century European art (Camille Claudel’s *Shakuntala* sculpture, Gustav Holst’s *The Cloud Messenger*, 1910) and Indian stage/film adaptations (Koodiyattam performances by Māni Mādhava Chākyār; numerous Hindi, Kannada, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali films 1955–1983).

### Commentarial Tradition
Hundreds of pre-modern Sanskrit commentaries exist, though only a fraction are published. The earliest surviving are by 10th-century Kashmirian Vallabhadeva; the most studied are 15th-century Mallinātha Suri’s. These texts reveal textual fluidity from centuries of manuscript copying and parallel oral traditions. Modern critical editions (e.g., Barbara Stoler Miller, 1984) and translations (Arthur W. Ryder, Project Gutenberg, Clay Sanskrit Library) maintain accessibility.

## Terms
- ****Mahākāvya**** — "Great poem"; the Sanskrit epic genre characterized by cantos (*sarga*), ornate style, and prescribed themes (e.g., *Raghuvaṃśa*, *Kumārasambhava*).
- ****Khaṇḍakāvya**** — "Minor poem"; a shorter lyric or narrative poem in a single metre (e.g., *Meghadūta*, *Ṛtusaṃhāra*).
- ****Mandākrāntā**** — A Sanskrit metre of 17 syllables per quarter-verse, noted for slow, lyrical movement; used for *Meghadūta*.
- ****Yaṣṭi**** — A nature spirit/demi-god (here, the exiled protagonist of *Meghadūta*).
- ****Pratyabhijñā**** — "Recognition"; a school of Kashmir Shaivism positing the self’s recognition of its identity with Śiva; Kalla argues *Śakuntalā* allegorizes this.
- ****Vaidarbhī**** — A poetic style (*rīti*) associated with the Vidarbha region, characterized by sweetness and clarity; cited in birthplace debates.
- ****Upamā**** — Simile; Kālidāsa is proverbial for mastery of *upamā* ("Upamā Kālidāsasya").
- ****Rājadharma**** — The duty/ethics of kingship, a central theme of *Raghuvaṃśa*.
- ****Koodiyattam**** — Kerala’s UNESCO-listed Sanskrit theatre tradition; preserved Kālidāsa’s plays in performance for centuries.
- ****Kakawin**** — Old Javanese narrative poem in Sanskrit-derived metres; *Sumanasāntaka* adapts *Raghuvaṃśa*.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Single vs. multiple authorship:** Whether the seven canonical works represent one genius or a "Kālidāsa" brand shared by several poets (Mātṛgupta, Medharudra, Kotijit, etc.) remains unresolved.
*   **Precise dating:** The 4th–5th century CE window (Gupta era) is consensus, but exact reign(s)—Chandragupta II only, or extending to Kumāragupta I/Skandagupta—is uncertain.
*   **Birthplace:** No theory (Ujjayini, Kashmir, Vidarbha, Dashapura, Vidisha, Bengal, Uttarakhand, Mithila) commands agreement; each relies on selective internal evidence countered by rival scholarship.
*   **Textual stability:** Commentaries reveal significant variation across manuscripts; reconstructing an ur-text is complicated by oral/written transmission interplay.
*   **Vikramāditya’s historicity:** Whether the patron "Vikramāditya" refers to Chandragupta II, the legendary 1st-century BCE king, Yaśodharman (6th century), or a generic title affects the entire chronological framework.
