# Wilhelm Röntgen

## Overview
Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen was a German experimental physicist who discovered X-rays in 1895, a breakthrough that revolutionized medicine and physics. He was the first Nobel Prize in Physics laureate (1901) for this discovery, which he deliberately left unpatented to benefit society. His work directly inspired further research into radioactivity and atomic physics.

## Biography
### Early Life and Education
Röntgen was born in Prussia and moved to the Netherlands as a child, rendering him stateless. After being unfairly expelled from a technical school in Utrecht, he studied mechanical engineering at the Federal Polytechnic School in Zurich, earning his Ph.D. in 1869. He was a protégé of physicist August Kundt, following him to academic positions at the Universities of Würzburg and Strasbourg.

### Career
Röntgen held professorships at the Universities of Strasbourg, Giessen, Würzburg, and finally Munich. He regained German citizenship in 1888 after 40 years of statelessness. He planned to emigrate to the United States for a position at Columbia University, but World War I altered his plans, and he remained in Munich.

### Personal Life
He married Anna Bertha Ludwig in 1872 after a long engagement opposed by his father. They adopted a niece, Josephine, in 1887. Röntgen was a member of the Dutch Reformed Church. He died in 1923, having spent his final years in financial difficulty due to post-WWI inflation.

## Discovery of X-rays
In November 1895, while experimenting with cathode ray tubes at the University of Würzburg, Röntgen observed a fluorescent screen glowing despite being shielded from visible light. He investigated this phenomenon, which he termed "X-rays" (using "X" for the unknown), and discovered they could pass through opaque materials. Within weeks, he produced the first radiographic image—a picture of his wife's hand, which famously prompted her to exclaim, "I have seen my death!" His paper, published on 28 December 1895, caused immediate excitement in the scientific community. He deliberately refused to patent his discovery, wanting it to be freely available for practical use.

## Recognition and Legacy
Röntgen received numerous awards, including the Rumford Medal (1896) and the first Nobel Prize in Physics (1901). He was a member of many scientific academies. His discovery directly influenced Henri Becquerel's work on radioactivity and the subsequent research of Marie and Pierre Curie. Element 111, roentgenium (Rg), is named in his honor. World Radiography Day is celebrated annually on 8 November, the anniversary of his discovery.

## Terms
- **X-rays**: A form of electromagnetic radiation with a wavelength range that allows them to pass through many materials opaque to visible light, discovered by Röntgen.
- **Cathode rays**: Streams of electrons observed in vacuum tubes, the investigation of which led to the discovery of X-rays.
- **Fluorescence**: The emission of light by a substance that has absorbed light or other electromagnetic radiation.
- **Radiograph**: An image produced on a sensitive surface by a form of radiation other than visible light, such as an X-ray image.
- **Roentgenium (Rg)**: A synthetic chemical element with atomic number 111, named in honor of Wilhelm Röntgen.
