# Heian period

## Overview
The Heian period (794–1185) was the final era of classical Japanese history, marked by the decline of Chinese influence and the flourishing of a distinct national culture. It is considered the peak of the Japanese imperial court, renowned for its art, literature, and the rise of the samurai class, which ultimately ended aristocratic rule.

## Timeline
- **794** — Emperor Kammu moves the capital to Heian-kyō (modern Kyoto).
- **838** — Imperial missions to Tang China end, accelerating cultural independence.
- **858** — Emperor Seiwa begins the rule of the Fujiwara clan.
- **895** — Sugawara no Michizane halts embassies to China.
- **1000–1008** — Murasaki Shikibu writes *The Tale of Genji*.
- **1068** — Emperor Go-Sanjō, the first non-Fujiwara-mothered emperor in centuries, overthrows Fujiwara dominance.
- **1087** — Emperor Shirakawa abdicates, beginning the *insei* (cloistered emperor) system.
- **1156** — The Hōgen Rebellion ends Fujiwara power and the *insei* system.
- **1185** — The Taira clan is defeated in the Genpei War; Minamoto no Yoritomo becomes the first shōgun.

## Body

### Political Structure and the Fujiwara Regency
The period began when Emperor Kammu moved the capital to Heian-kyō to strengthen imperial authority. However, real power soon passed to the Fujiwara clan, who intermarried with the imperial family. By the 10th century, Fujiwara regents (Sesshō and Kampaku) ruled as "hereditary dictators," enthroning and dethroning emperors at will. This system was challenged by Emperor Go-Sanjō in 1068 and later supplanted by the *insei* system, where retired emperors governed from behind the scenes. The Fujiwara's decline allowed the military class to enter court politics, culminating in the Hōgen Rebellion (1156).

### Rise of the Samurai
As the central government's control over land and taxation weakened, local landholders (*shōen* holders) developed private armies for protection. These professional warriors, the samurai, gained influence through military titles and connections to court families like the Taira and Minamoto. The Fujiwara, Taira, and Minamoto clans fought for control, with Taira no Kiyomori seizing power after 1156. His dominance ended with the Genpei War (1180–1185), after which Minamoto no Yoritomo established the Kamakura shogunate, creating a bakufu (military government) that ruled Japan for centuries.

### Culture and Literature
The decline of Chinese influence fostered *kokufū bunka* (national culture). Two Japanese syllabaries, katakana and hiragana, emerged, enabling a boom in vernacular literature. Court ladies, less educated in Chinese, wrote famous works like *The Tale of Genji* and *The Pillow Book*. Poetry was a vital social skill, and calligraphy was prized. Art shifted from Chinese imitation to the vivid *yamato-e* style depicting court life. Buddhism flourished with the esoteric Tendai and Shingon sects, which emphasized rituals and were patronized by the aristocracy.

### Economics and Society
The economy relied on barter and rice as a primary unit of exchange, as currency circulation collapsed. The *shōen* system allowed aristocratic estates to grow, evading central taxation and control. This decentralization weakened the imperial government. While the era was largely peaceful, crime was widespread due to ineffective policing. The aristocracy, or *Ryōmin* ("Good People"), numbered only about 5,000 in a population of five million, enjoying wealth and cultural pursuits while the samurai class rose from the provinces.

## Terms
- **Heian-kyō**: The "Capital of Peace and Tranquility," modern Kyoto, established as the capital in 794.
- **Fujiwara**: A powerful aristocratic family that controlled the throne through regency and intermarriage for much of the period.
- **Shōen**: Tax-free private estates that grew in independence, undermining central authority.
- **Samurai**: The emerging military class of provincial warriors who served as guards for estates and clans.
- **Insei**: The "cloistered emperor" system where retired emperors governed behind the scenes.
- **Kokufū bunka**: The "national culture" that developed as Chinese influence waned.
- **Hiragana & Katakana**: Japanese syllabaries developed from Chinese characters, enabling vernacular literature.
- **Yamato-e**: A style of Japanese painting depicting native themes, contrasting with earlier Chinese-influenced art.
- **Bakufu**: The "tent government" of the shōgun, established after 1185.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the *shōen* system's growth is a key factor in the decline of imperial control, but the exact mechanisms and pace of this decentralization are part of the historical narrative. The transition from court to military rule is presented as a gradual process driven by economic and social changes, with the Genpei War as the decisive turning point.
