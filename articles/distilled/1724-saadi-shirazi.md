# Saadi Shirazi

## Overview
Saadi (c. 1209/10–1291/92) was a Persian Sufi poet from Shiraz, widely regarded as one of the greatest figures in classical Persian literature and honored with the title "Master of Speech." His two masterworks, the verse collection *Bustan* (The Orchard, 1257) and the prose-and-verse *Gulistan* (The Rose Garden, 1258), synthesize Sufi ethics with pragmatic wisdom, earning him enduring influence across the Islamic world and in the West, where thinkers from Goethe to Emerson admired his moral clarity. Saadi’s life spanned the fragmentation of the Salghurid dynasty in Fars and the imposition of Mongol Ilkhanid rule; his poetry navigates this turbulence, mourning the fall of Baghdad (1258) while counseling successive rulers. His most famous aphorism, *Bani Adam* ("The children of Adam are limbs of one body"), inscribed at the United Nations, encapsulates his humanist vision of shared essence and mutual responsibility.

## Timeline
- **1209/10** — Birth in Shiraz (date uncertain; under Salghurid rule since 1148).
- **c. 1223/24** — Leaves Shiraz for Baghdad to study after father’s death and brief deposition of Salghurid ruler Sa’d I.
- **c. 1224–1256** — Extensive travels across Iraq, Syria, Palestine, and Hejaz; likely did not reach India or Khorasan.
- **1257** — Returns to Shiraz; completes *Bustan*, dedicated to Salghurid ruler Abu Bakr ibn Sa’d.
- **1258** — Completes *Gulistan*; composes elegies on the Mongol sack of Baghdad and death of Caliph al-Musta’sim.
- **1260** — Abu Bakr ibn Sa’d dies; Saadi elegizes him and his son Sa’d II (died 12 days later).
- **1263** — Salghurid prince Saljuk Shah ibn Salghur killed by Mongols; Saadi composed poems for his brief reign.
- **1268–1272** — Writes counsel treatises and qasidas for Shiraz governor Amir Ankyanu.
- **c. 1287–1289** — Last datable poem addressed to administrator Majd-al-Din Rumi.
- **1291/92** — Death in Shiraz (scholarly debate: 1291 per Safa/Hamdallah Mustawfi; 1292 per Nafisi).
- **1765** — Carsten Niebuhr visits dilapidated tomb; later renovated by Karim Khan Zand (r. 1751–1779).
- **2005** — *Bani Adam* carpet installed at UN headquarters in New York.

## Body

### Background and Name
Saadi’s full name and lineage remain uncertain due to variant manuscript traditions. The earliest external source, Ibn al-Fuwati (d. 1323), records a 1262 letter addressing him as "Muslih al-Din Abu Muhammad Abd-Allah ibn Musharrif." Western scholars (Browne, Arberry, Rypka, Davis) often prefer "Musharrif al-Din ibn Muslih al-Din Abd-Allah," while Iranian scholars Zabihollah Safa and Paul E. Losensky, relying on a 1326 compilation preface by Ali ibn Ahmad ibn Abu Bakr Bisotun and the poet Jami (d. 1492), reconstruct his name as "Abu Muhammad Musharrif al-Din Muslih ibn Abd-Allah ibn Musharrif." His pen name "Saadi" likely signals allegiance to the Salghurid dynasty, whose rulers bore the name "Sa’d" (or "Banu Sa’d"); scholar Abdolhossein Zarrinkoob argues the name demonstrated loyalty to this Turkoman-origin Persianate house that ruled Fars from 1148.

### Education and Travels
Saadi received early education from his father, who instilled tolerance; orphaned in adolescence, he departed for Baghdad c. 1223/24. Scholar Badiozzaman Forouzanfar notes parallels between Saadi’s teachings and those of Sufi master Shihab al-Din Yahya ibn Habash Suhrawardi, suggesting a possible association. After studies, Saadi traveled for nearly 30 years. His works claim dramatic episodes—killing a temple priest in India, capture by Crusaders in Syria—but Iranologist Homa Katouzian, followed by Losensky, judges these rhetorically crafted and historically unlikely. Katouzian concludes Saadi likely visited Iraq, Syria, Palestine, and the Arabian Peninsula, but not Khorasan, India, or Kashgar. Losensky warns that reconstructing a precise itinerary from his poetry is "misguided."

### Return to Shiraz and Political Context
Saadi returned to Shiraz in 1257, already famous from circulating ghazals. He quickly dedicated *Bustan* (1257) and *Gulistan* (1258) to Salghurid ruler Abu Bakr ibn Sa’d (r. 1226–1260), crediting him with restoring peace. The Salghurid collapse accelerated: Abu Bakr acknowledged Mongol suzerainty in 1256/57; his death in 1260 triggered rapid succession crises (Sa’d II died 12 days later; child ruler Muhammad I ibn Sa’d under mother Tarkhan Khatun). Saadi elegized the dead and praised the living. By 1263, Mongol pressure installed Abu Bakr’s nephews; Saljuk Shah ibn Salghur’s five-month reign ended when Mongols killed him after a drunken revolt. Power passed to Abish Khatun, forced to marry Möngke Temür, son of Ilkhan Hulagu Khan (r. 1256–1265), effectively annexing Shiraz.

Saadi mourned the 1258 Mongol destruction of Baghdad and death of Abbasid caliph al-Musta’sim in Arabic and Persian qasidas, yet also composed poems honoring the new Mongol authorities and their Persian administrators. His works for governor Amir Ankyanu (1268–1272)—four qasidas and the prose treatise *Dar tarbiat-e yaki az moluk*—are "counsel and warnings concerning the proper conduct of rulers" rather than conventional panegyrics (Losensky). He also praised chancery chief Shams al-Din Husayn Alakani and the Juvayni brothers (Shams al-Din Juvayni, Ilkhanate finance minister; Ata-Malik Juvayni, historian of the Mongols). Two treatises in his collected works describe a meeting with the Juvaynis and Ilkhan Abaqa (r. 1265–1282) at Tabriz returning from Hajj, though these texts are not by Saadi. A *qit'a* collection, the *Sahebiya*, honors Shams al-Din Juvayni.

### Works: Bustan and Gulistan
*Bustan* (The Orchard, 1257), entirely in epic-meter verse, illustrates cardinal virtues (justice, liberality, modesty, contentment) through stories and reflections on dervish ecstatic practices. *Gulistan* (The Rose Garden, 1258), mainly prose with interspersed poems, juxtaposes the precariousness of court dependence with dervish freedom. Its anecdotes blend aphorisms, advice, and humor, revealing "profound awareness of the absurdity of human existence." A famous passage urges learning a trade: "a profession is a living fountain and permanent wealth... whereas those who have no trade will glean crumbs." *Bustan* also contains a vivid first-person battle narrative against Mongols: a warrior describes arrows "raining like hail," surrender "like a fish... caught by the hook," and the futility of armor when "Fortune averted her face."

### Ghazals and Arabic Works
Saadi composed roughly 700 ghazals, collected in four books: *Tayyibat*, *Bada’i*, *Khawatim*, and *Ghazaliyat-e Qadim*. The last contains youthful passion; *Khawatim* reflects old-age asceticism and mysticism; the middle two blend both and are artistically superior. Foroughi separated mystical/didactic ghazals into a chapter titled "Admonitions." Saadi favored circular meters (e.g., *fa’alātun fā’ilātun fa’alātun fā’ilātun*) and is credited with bringing the ghazal form to its peak alongside Hafez. His Arabic output—several qasidas, qit’as, and verses—has no independent volume; a 2011 collection (*Saadi's Arabic Poems*) compiles them. Edward Browne judged them average; Musa Anwar finds them respectable in content and structure but notes grammatical errors.

### Bani Adam
The aphorism *Bani Adam* (Gulistan, Chapter 1) asserts human unity: "The children of Adam are limbs of one body, created from one essence. If one limb suffers, the others cannot rest. If you feel no sympathy for others’ misery, you do not deserve the name 'human.'" The version with *yekdigar* ("one another") appears in earliest manuscripts (per Habib Yaghmai) and is standard in Iran—on a UN carpet (2005), the 500-rial coin (2008), and the 100,000-rial note (2010). Translations by Vahid Dastjerdi, Ali Salami, Richard Jeffrey Newman, and UN Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon vary slightly. Coldplay used the Persian text in a 2019 song; British Columbia Minister Bowinn Ma recited a version in parliament.

### Legacy and Poetic Style
Saadi embodies dual personas: the Sufi Sheikh (spiritual, *Bustan*’s "delicate and soothing" images) and the travelling merchant (mundane, *Gulistan*’s "graphic" concrete realism)—"two almond kernels in the same shell." His prose is "simple but impossible to imitate," built on synonymy, homophony, oxymoron, internal rhythm, and external rhyme. Western reception began with André du Ryer’s partial French *Gulistan* (1634) and Adam Olearius’s complete German *Bustan* and *Gulistan* (1654). Goethe’s *West-Östlicher Divan* is the chief work influenced by Saadi. Hegel praised Persian pantheistic poetry’s "higher and freer development," naming Saadi the master of the didactic turn before Rumi’s mysticism. Pushkin quoted Saadi in *Eugene Onegin*; La Fontaine drew on *Gulistan* for fables; Benjamin Franklin borrowed a *Bustan* parable unknowingly; Emerson compared Saadi’s wisdom to the Bible. Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot’s middle name honors Saadi (chosen by his father Lazare). Voltaire adopted "Saadi" as a nickname. President Obama quoted *Bani Adam* in a 2009 Nowruz message. A Mercury crater was named for Saadi in 1976.

### National Commemoration and Mausoleum
Saadi Day is observed annually on 1 Ordibehesht (21 April, 20 April in leap years), the date Saadi states he finished *Gulistan* in 1256 (the text says 1258; the commemoration follows his stated completion date). Crowds gather at his tomb in Shiraz. The mausoleum, dilapidated in 1765 (per Niebuhr), was renovated by Karim Khan Zand, who added an iron railing and a brick-and-plaster structure.

## Terms
- **Bustan** (The Orchard): Saadi’s 1257 verse collection in epic meter, illustrating virtues and dervish life through didactic stories.
- **Gulistan** (The Rose Garden): Saadi’s 1258 prose-and-verse work mixing anecdotes, aphorisms, and poems on ethics and human folly.
- **Ghazal**: Amatory ode or lyric poem in monorhyme couplets; Saadi wrote ~700, peaking the form with Hafez.
- **Qasida**: Formal ode, often panegyrical; Saadi wrote Persian and Arabic qasidas for rulers and on public events (e.g., fall of Baghdad).
- **Qit'a**: Monorhyme poem (single rhyme throughout); shorter than a qasida, used for occasional verse.
- **Salghurids**: Persianate Turkoman dynasty ruling Fars from Shiraz (1148–1260s); Saadi’s early patrons.
- **Ilkhanate**: Mongol khanate in Persia (1256–1335); succeeded Salghurids, ruled through Persian administrators like the Juvaynis.
- **Suhrawardi (Shihab al-Din Yahya ibn Habash)**: Sufi philosopher (d. 1191); Forouzanfar suggests doctrinal links to Saadi.
- **Bani Adam**: "Children of Adam"; Saadi’s most famous aphorism on human unity, from *Gulistan* Chapter 1.
- **Khanqah**: Sufi lodge or convent; the setting for the "Sheikh preaching" in Saadi’s dual-persona metaphor.

## Debates and open questions
- **Birth year**: 1209 vs. 1210 (most scholars accept either; source says "1209 or 1210").
- **Full name**: Multiple reconstructions exist—Ibn al-Fuwati (1262), early manuscripts (1328), 1326 compilation preface, and Jami (d. 1492) differ on order of *Muslih*, *Musharrif*, *Abd-Allah*, and kunya *Abu Muhammad*.
- **Travel veracity**: Autobiographical claims (India, Crusader captivity) are considered rhetorical fictions by Katouzian and Losensky; exact itinerary unrecoverable.
- **Death year**: 1291 (Safa, based on Hamdallah Mustawfi’s *Tarikh-i guzida*, 1330) vs. 1292 (Nafisi); Losensky accepts either, noting year-end death complicates chronicles.
- **Arabic poetry quality**: Browne calls it average; Musa Anwar rates it respectable but notes grammatical errors.
- **Bani Adam manuscript variant**: *yekdigar* ("one another") in earliest manuscripts vs. *yek peykar* ("one body") in later editions and most English translations.
