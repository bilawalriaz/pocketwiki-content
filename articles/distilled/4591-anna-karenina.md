# Anna Karenina

## Overview
*Anna Karenina* is a novel by Leo Tolstoy, first published in book form in 1878, often considered among the greatest works of world literature. It interweaves the tragic story of Anna Karenina's adulterous affair with the parallel spiritual journey of Konstantin Levin, exploring themes of family, faith, society, and the contrasts between rural and urban life in Imperial Russia.

## Timeline
- **1875–1877** — Novel serialized in *The Russian Messenger*
- **1878** — Published in book form
- **c. 1870s** — Story set against reforms of Emperor Alexander II
- **Part 1** — Anna arrives in Moscow to reconcile her brother Stiva and his wife Dolly; meets Vronsky
- **Part 2** — Anna and Vronsky begin an affair in St. Petersburg
- **Part 4** — Anna gives birth to daughter Annie; Vronsky attempts suicide; they flee to Italy
- **Part 5** — Anna and Vronsky return to Russia; Anna is socially ostracized
- **Part 7** — Anna becomes increasingly jealous and paranoid; commits suicide by throwing herself under a train
- **Part 8** — Vronsky volunteers for the Serbian revolt; Levin finds spiritual meaning

## Body

### Plot and Structure
The novel begins with the famous line, "Happy families are all alike; every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way." It opens with Prince Stepan "Stiva" Oblonsky's infidelity causing a family crisis, which his sister Anna Karenina helps to resolve. In Moscow, Konstantin "Kostya" Levin proposes to Princess Kitty Shcherbatskaya, but she rejects him, believing the wealthy Count Vronsky will propose. At a ball, Vronsky dances with Anna instead, revealing his love for her. Anna returns to her husband, Alexei Karenin, in St. Petersburg, but Vronsky pursues her, and they begin an affair.

The story follows two parallel narratives. Anna's affair scandalizes society. After a difficult childbirth, she and Vronsky flee to Italy, leaving behind her son Seryozha. Upon returning to Russia, Anna is shunned by high society while Vronsky retains his social freedom. Her growing jealousy and paranoia lead to morphine addiction and, ultimately, suicide by throwing herself under a train. Meanwhile, Levin struggles with agricultural management, spiritual doubt, and his love for Kitty. They marry, and after witnessing the birth of his son and speaking with a peasant, Levin finds faith and meaning in life.

### Themes and Style
The novel explores hypocrisy, jealousy, faith, family, marriage, and the agrarian connection to land versus urban life. Trains are a recurring motif, with several key events occurring at railway stations. The story is set against the backdrop of rapid societal transformations following the liberal reforms of Emperor Alexander II, including the emancipation reform of 1861, judicial and military reforms, and the rise of railroads and industry. These contemporary developments are debated by the characters.

Levin is considered a semi-autobiographical portrayal of Tolstoy, reflecting his beliefs and life events. Tolstoy's style is seen as transitional between realism and modernism. The novel's epigraph is "Vengeance is mine; I will repay" from Romans 12:19.

### Reception and Translations
The novel received immediate acclaim. Fyodor Dostoevsky called it "perfect" and unmatched in European literature. William Dean Howells and Matthew Arnold praised its realism and psychological depth. Vladimir Nabokov described it as "one of the greatest love stories in world literature."

Numerous English translations exist, with notable versions by Constance Garnett (1901), Aylmer and Louise Maude (1918), Rosemary Edmonds (1954), and Richard Pevear and Larissa Volokhonsky (2000). Scholars debate their merits, with the Garnett-Kent-Berberova revision and Pevear/Volokhonsky version often recommended. The title has been translated as both *Anna Karenin* (using the masculine surname form) and *Anna Karenina* (the direct transliteration of the Russian feminine form).

## Terms
- **Serialization**: Publication of a novel in sequential installments in a periodical before book publication.
- **Emancipation reform of 1861**: The abolition of serfdom in Russia, a key liberal reform under Alexander II.
- **Zemstvo**: An elected local government body introduced in Imperial Russia.
- **Pan-Slavism**: A political movement advocating for the unity of Slavic peoples.
- **Pietist**: A member of a religious movement emphasizing personal devotion and inner spirituality.
- **Morphine addiction**: Anna's dependency on the narcotic, which she begins using to aid sleep.
- **Alter ego**: A fictional character considered to be a representation of the author (Levin for Tolstoy).
- **Ekphrasis**: A literary description of or commentary on a visual work of art.

## Debates and Open questions
- **Translation choices**: Scholars debate the best English translation, comparing accuracy, readability, and scholarly apparatus. The choice between "Karenin" and "Karenina" reflects a debate over naturalizing Russian names into English.
- **Levin as Tolstoy's alter ego**: While widely accepted, the degree to which Levin represents Tolstoy's own views is a subject of literary analysis.
- **Tolstoy's dissatisfaction**: Tolstoy expressed anguish and hatred for the novel while finishing it, stating it should be "scrapped, and melted down, thrown away."
- **The Anna Karenina principle**: The novel has inspired a scientific principle named after it, though this is not detailed in the source text.
