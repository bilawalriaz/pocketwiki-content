# Ancient Greek architecture

## Overview
Ancient Greek architecture, flourishing from c. 900 BC to the 1st century AD, is renowned for its temples and public buildings, which established a formal vocabulary of orders and proportions that profoundly influenced Western architecture. Its development was shaped by Greece's geography, climate, and humanist philosophy, leading to structures designed as sculptural entities within the landscape, emphasizing balance, symmetry, and optical refinement.

## Timeline
- **c. 2800–c. 1100 BC** — Minoan civilization on Crete, known for elaborate palaces.
- **c. 1500–1100 BC** — Mycenaean civilization on the Peloponnese, characterized by citadels and fortifications.
- **c. 1100–900 BC** — Greek Dark Age following the collapse of Minoan and Mycenaean cultures.
- **c. 600 BC** — Earliest substantial stone architectural works appear; Doric order well-defined by c. 600 BC.
- **c. 525–480 BC** — Earliest surviving open-air theatres date from this period.
- **448–432 BC** — Construction of the Parthenon in Athens, a pinnacle of High Classical architecture.
- **323 BC** — Death of Alexander the Great marks the end of the Hellenic period and start of the Hellenistic period.
- **334 BC** — Corinthian order first used externally on the Choragic Monument of Lysicrates.
- **174 BC–132 AD** — Construction of the Temple of Zeus Olympius in Athens, a major Corinthian temple.

## Body

### Influences and Historical Context
Greek architecture evolved from earlier Minoan and Mycenaean cultures, though much technical knowledge was lost after their collapse around 1100 BC. The rocky landscape provided abundant limestone and high-quality marble, enabling precise architectural and sculptural detail. The maritime climate encouraged outdoor living, influencing the placement of temples on hilltops and theatres on natural slopes. Greek humanist philosophy, which placed mankind at the center and valued order and reason, drove a search for perfection and beauty in architectural form, rather than the mere application of rules.

### Architectural Character and Building Types
The core architectural form is trabeated (post and lintel), derived from earlier wooden structures. The most iconic building is the rectangular temple, which housed a cult statue and served as a treasury, with worship occurring at an outdoor altar. Temples were typically raised on a stepped base (stylobate) and surrounded by columns. Other key public buildings include the open-air theatre, the agora (public square) with its surrounding stoa (colonnade), the bouleuterion (town council building), and the stadium. Domestic architecture centered on houses with inward-facing rooms arranged around a central courtyard.

### Structural Principles and Temple Design
Temples were built from stone blocks (ashlar masonry) with minimal mortar, often secured with metal clamps. The structure consists of columns supporting an entablature (architrave, frieze, and cornice) and a triangular pediment. Architects employed sophisticated optical refinements to counteract visual illusions; for example, columns featured a slight convex curve (entasis), and major lines like the stylobate were subtly curved. The Parthenon exemplifies this, with its stylobate rising about 65 mm at the center. Temple plans are classified by column arrangement, such as peripteral (surrounded by a single colonnade) or dipteral (double colonnade).

### The Classical Orders
Greek formal architecture is defined by three orders, each with distinct column and entablature proportions and details. The **Doric order**, the oldest, features sturdy columns with no base and a simple cushion-like capital; its frieze is divided into triglyphs and metopes. The **Ionic order**, from Ionia, has slender columns with bases and voluted (scroll-like) capitals; its frieze is often a continuous band. The **Corinthian order**, a later, more ornate variant of the Ionic, is distinguished by its tall capital decorated with acanthus leaves. These orders governed the entire design of a building.

### Decoration and Sculpture
Architectural decoration was integral, evolving from painted terracotta revetments on early wooden temples to extensive stone sculpture. Sculpture was placed in specific zones: metopes and pediments in the Doric order, and continuous friezes in the Ionic. Styles progressed from the rigid Archaic period to the idealized lifelikeness of the High Classical period (e.g., the Parthenon sculptures by Phidias) and the dramatic, emotional flair of the Hellenistic period (e.g., the Pergamon Altar frieze).

## Terms
- **Trabeated**: A structural system using posts (columns) and lintels (horizontal beams).
- **Entablature**: The horizontal structure above the columns, comprising the architrave, frieze, and cornice.
- **Pediment**: The triangular gable at the end of a temple roof, often filled with sculpture.
- **Stylobate**: The top step of a temple's base, upon which the columns stand.
- **Entasis**: A slight convex curve in a column's profile to correct the optical illusion of concavity.
- **Doric Order**: The earliest and most austere of the three Classical orders, with sturdy, baseless columns.
- **Ionic Order**: A more slender order with voluted capitals and columns that have bases.
- **Corinthian Order**: The most ornate order, with tall capitals decorated with acanthus leaves.
- **Crepidoma**: The stepped platform or base of a temple.
- **Temenos**: The sacred precinct or enclosure around a temple.
