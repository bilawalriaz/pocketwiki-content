# Tigris

## Overview
The Tigris is one of the two great rivers that define Mesopotamia, flowing from Turkey through Iraq to the Persian Gulf. It has been central to the development of civilizations, religions, and modern geopolitics, but now faces significant environmental and political threats.

## Timeline
- **c. 2900 BC** — City of Lagash is irrigated by the Tigris via a canal.
- **Ancient times** — The river nurtures the Assyrian Empire and major cities like Nineveh and Ctesiphon.
- **c. 1858** — Baháʼu'lláh writes *The Hidden Words* while exiled in Baghdad on the Tigris.
- **1932–1959** — The Tigris features on the coat of arms of Iraq.
- **2014** — The "Geneva Consensus On Tigris River" is reached between Iraq and Turkey.
- **February 2016** — Warnings are issued that Mosul Dam could collapse, risking major cities.

## Body

### Etymology and Name
The name "Tigris" derives from Old Persian *Tigrā*, via Akkadian *Idiglat* and Sumerian *Idigna*, which likely meant "the swift river." This name contrasts it with the slower Euphrates. The river is known by various names in regional languages, including Kurdish *Ava Mezin* ("the Great Water").

### Geography and Navigation
The Tigris is 1,750 km long, rising in Turkey's Taurus Mountains. It flows through Southeastern Turkey, forms a short border with Syria, and then traverses Iraq. Near its confluence with the Euphrates, it splits into several channels and marshes before joining the Euphrates to form the Shatt al-Arab waterway. The river is a vital transport route; shallow-draft vessels reach Baghdad, while rafts are used downstream from Mosul.

### Management and Modern Threats
The Tigris is heavily dammed in Iraq and Turkey for irrigation and flood control, with Mosul Dam being Iraq's largest. Turkish dam projects are controversial due to environmental impacts and reduced downstream flow. Water is also used as political leverage during conflicts. A major concern is the potential collapse of Mosul Dam, which could cause catastrophic flooding in cities including Mosul, Tikrit, Samarra, and Baghdad, endangering up to 1.5 million people.

### Religious and Mythological Significance
The Tigris holds deep religious importance. In Sumerian mythology, it was created by the god Enki. It is deified as Aranzah in Hittite and Hurrian myths. The river appears in the Old Testament as one of the four rivers from Eden and as the site of a vision by Daniel. It is mentioned in Islamic texts and is the setting for writings by the founder of the Baháʼí Faith.

## Terms
- **Mesopotamia**: The historical region between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, often called the "cradle of civilization."
- **Shatt al-Arab**: The waterway formed by the confluence of the Tigris and Euphrates, flowing to the Persian Gulf.
- **Distributary**: A stream that branches off and flows away from a main river channel.
- **Mosul Dam**: The largest dam in Iraq, built on the Tigris, noted for its structural risks.
- **Geneva Consensus On Tigris River**: A 2014 agreement between Iraq and Turkey to share data on river flows.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the geopolitical and environmental impact of Turkish dam construction on downstream water flow and quality in Iraq. Another open question is the long-term sustainability of the river given climate change, poor water management, and regional instability. The risk of a Mosul Dam collapse remains a critical, unresolved safety concern.
