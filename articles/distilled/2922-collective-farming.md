# Collective farming

## Overview
Collective farming is a system where multiple farmers manage their holdings jointly, typically as either member-owned agricultural cooperatives or state-run farms. It has been a central policy tool for communist governments seeking to restructure rural economies, often with significant social and economic consequences, including forced displacement, famine, and debates over efficiency versus private farming.

## Timeline
- **Pre-20th century** — Communal land systems exist among the Aztec (calpulli), Wyandot, Haudenosaunee, and Russian peasant communes (mir/obshchina).
- **1917** — Mexican Constitution abolishes feudal rights and establishes ejidos, communal farms on purchased land.
- **1927–1933** — Soviet Union under Stalin implements forced collectivization, leading to dekulakization and famine (1932–33).
- **1945–1962** — Communist regimes in Eastern Europe (Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, East Germany) and Asia (China, North Korea, Vietnam) adopt collective farming.
- **1948–1962** — Romania's collectivization is enforced, often violently, until its virtual eradication of private farming.
- **1948–1961** — Hungary attempts collectivization multiple times, finally succeeding in the early 1960s under János Kádár.
- **1949–1969** — Czechoslovakia implements collectivization in three stages, officially ending with the 1960 constitution.
- **1953–1958** — China transitions through mutual aid groups and cooperatives to people's communes during the Great Leap Forward, contributing to the Great Chinese Famine (1959).
- **1973–1975** — Tanzania's Operation Vijiji forcibly villagizes the population to implement ujamaa collective farming.
- **1986** — Vietnam begins Đổi Mới reforms, encouraging private enterprise while maintaining state land ownership and many collective cooperatives.
- **1989** — Fall of communism in Czechoslovakia leads to collapse of most collective farms due to loss of subsidies and foreign competition.

## Body

### Pre-20th-Century Communal Systems
Before state-mandated collectivization, various societies practiced communal land management. In Mexico, the Aztec calpulli system granted families rights to farm communally owned land. In North America, the Wyandot and Haudenosaunee used communal ownership with usufruct rights, where land use was tied to active cultivation and could be redistributed by councils. In Imperial Russia, the obshchina or mir was a peasant commune that held land collectively, periodically reallocating strips to households for tax purposes.

### Collectivization in the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe
The Soviet Union pioneered forced collectivization under Stalin in the late 1920s to boost production and eliminate the kulak class. This process, part of the first five-year plan, destroyed traditional farming systems and caused a devastating famine (1932–33) that killed millions, particularly in Ukraine. Agricultural production did not surpass pre-collectivization levels until 1940. After World War II, Soviet-style collectivization was imposed across Eastern Europe. In Romania, it was a violent process lasting until 1962. In Hungary, it failed twice before succeeding in the early 1960s. Czechoslovakia's collectivization occurred in stages, leading to low productivity, resource waste, and environmental damage, though it eventually eliminated rural poverty. East Germany's Landwirtschaftliche Produktionsgenossenschaft (LPG) transferred land from former Junker estates to state-controlled entities.

### Collectivization in Asia
China's collectivization began in 1953, progressing through cooperatives to people's communes during the Great Leap Forward (1958–1962). Unlike the Soviet model, it was initially more voluntary and smoother, but flawed policies and natural disasters led to the Great Chinese Famine, causing nearly 30 million deaths. Collectivization facilitated state control for rapid industrialization. After Mao's death, Deng Xiaoping's reforms introduced the household responsibility system, which increased production. In Vietnam, collectivization was pushed from 1958, and after reunification in 1976, private ownership was abolished. The 1986 Đổi Mới reforms encouraged private enterprise, but over 50% of farms remain collective cooperatives. North Korea's collective system collapsed in the late 1990s, contributing to a severe famine.

### Other Models and Modern Cooperatives
Collective farming also exists outside communist states. In Israel, kibbutzim (voluntary collective communities) began in 1909. In Mexico, the ejido system provides collective land-use rights. In Europe, cooperative farming is common, with cooperatives holding significant market shares (e.g., 40% in the EU). In India, some villages practice collective farming for religious offerings. In Canada and the U.S., Hutterites have farmed communally since the 16th century. Modern movements like community-supported agriculture also represent voluntary collective models.

## Terms
- **Collectivization**: The process of aggregating individual farmholdings into jointly managed farms.
- **Agricultural cooperative**: A farm owned and operated by its member-farmers as a collective enterprise.
- **State farm**: A farm owned and directly managed by a centralized government.
- **Kolkhoz**: A type of collective farm in the Soviet Union.
- **Sovkhoz**: A state-owned farm in the Soviet Union.
- **Dekulakization**: The Soviet policy of repressing and deporting wealthier peasants (kulaks) to enforce collectivization.
- **Ejido**: A Mexican system of communal farmland with use rights granted to individual families.
- **Obshchina/mir**: A traditional Russian peasant commune that held land collectively.
- **Kibbutz**: A voluntary collective community in Israel, often focused on agriculture.
- **Household responsibility system**: China's post-Mao reform that allowed individual farming within the collective framework.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Efficiency**: The source notes a 1975 Soviet article found private plots (less than 1% of land) produced 27% of agricultural value, making them roughly 40 times more efficient than collective farms.
- **Voluntary vs. Forced**: Scholars debate whether collectivization in places like China was largely voluntary and smooth, unlike the brutal Soviet model, or if coercion was still present.
- **Causes of Famine**: In China, the Great Chinese Famine is attributed to flawed policies (e.g., Lysenkoism, Four Pests Campaign), natural disasters, and political radicalism/career incentives within the party.
- **Long-term Viability**: After the fall of communism, many collective farms in Eastern Europe collapsed due to loss of subsidies and inability to compete, leading to significant drops in agricultural employment.
