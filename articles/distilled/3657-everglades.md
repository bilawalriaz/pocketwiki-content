# Everglades

## Overview
The Everglades is a vast, slow-moving river of grass in southern Florida, unique for its sheetflow hydrology and subtropical ecosystems. It has been profoundly altered by human drainage and development, leading to massive habitat loss and triggering one of the world's most ambitious and costly ecological restoration efforts.

## Timeline
- **c. 15,000 years ago** — Humans first arrive in the Florida peninsula.
- **1848** — First proposal to drain the Everglades for agriculture.
- **1882** — First drainage canals constructed.
- **1947** — Everglades National Park dedicated; Marjory Stoneman Douglas publishes *The Everglades: River of Grass*.
- **1948** — Central and Southern Florida Flood Control Project (C&SF) approved by Congress.
- **1962-1971** — Kissimmee River straightened into the C-38 canal.
- **1986** — Major algal bloom in Lake Okeechobee signals water quality crisis.
- **1992** — Kissimmee River Restoration project approved by Congress.
- **2000** — Comprehensive Everglades Restoration Plan (CERP) signed into law.
- **2017** — Python Elimination Program implemented by South Florida Water Management District.

## Body

### Geology and Hydrology
The Everglades ecosystem is built on a porous limestone shelf. Water from Lake Okeechobee flows south as a wide, shallow sheet (sheetflow) at about 0.5 miles per day, creating a "River of Grass." This slow movement, influenced by the underlying rock, defines the region's plant communities and hydroperiod (the length of time an area is flooded). The system is sustained primarily by atmospheric processes, with evapotranspiration accounting for 70–90% of water entering undeveloped wetlands.

### Native Peoples and Exploration
Humans have inhabited the region for 15,000 years. Before European contact, the Calusa and Tequesta tribes dominated. They were gradually displaced by the Seminole, a new tribe formed from Creek and other groups who resisted U.S. removal in the 19th-century Seminole Wars. Early explorers described the area as an impenetrable swamp, a perception that fueled later desires to "tame" it.

### Drainage and Development
A national push for progress led to systematic drainage beginning in the late 19th century. The 1850 Swamp and Overflowed Lands Act gave states control of wetlands. Developer Hamilton Disston's 1881 land purchase and early canals spurred Florida's economy. Governor Napoleon Bonaparte Broward championed drainage in the early 1900s. The construction of the Hoover Dike (1930-37) and the C&SF project (post-1948) built over 1,400 miles of canals and levees, diverting water to cities and creating the Everglades Agricultural Area (EAA) for sugarcane. Approximately 50% of the original Everglades was developed.

### Ecological Decline and Restoration
By the 1970s, degradation was severe, prompting conservation efforts. UNESCO and the Ramsar Convention designated it a Wetland of Global Importance. A proposed jetport was blocked after environmental studies. Restoration began in the 1980s with the Kissimmee River backfilling. The 2000 CERP, the most comprehensive restoration plan in history, aimed to store water, improve quality, and restore natural flow. Progress has been slow, hampered by funding and political issues. Key threats include nutrient pollution (phosphorus), invasive species like the Burmese python and melaleuca tree, and climate change-induced sea-level rise.

## Terms
- **Sheetflow**: The slow, wide, shallow flow of water across the Everglades' limestone shelf, defining it as a river.
- **Hydroperiod**: The seasonal pattern of water level fluctuations in a wetland, determining which plants and animals can survive.
- **Peat**: A soil type formed from accumulated, partially decayed plant matter in consistently flooded, low-oxygen conditions.
- **Periphyton**: A complex mixture of algae, cyanobacteria, and microbes that forms mats in the Everglades, crucial for marl soil formation.
- **Marl**: A calcitic soil formed in areas with shorter hydroperiods, where periphyton absorbs calcium from water.
- **Invasive Species**: Non-native organisms that aggressively outcompete native species, disrupting the ecosystem.
- **Comprehensive Everglades Restoration Plan (CERP)**: A 30-year, multi-billion-dollar federal-state partnership approved in 2000 to restore natural water flow and quality.
- **Everglades Agricultural Area (EAA)**: A region south of Lake Okeechobee drained for farming, primarily sugarcane, which is a major source of nutrient pollution.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Pace and Prioritization of CERP**: Critics argue restoration projects benefit urban areas first, while the central Everglades ecosystem continues to degrade. The U.S. National Research Council has graded many ecosystem attributes as "degraded" to "significantly degraded."
- **Sugar Industry vs. Restoration**: The future of sugarcane farming in the EAA remains contentious, as it is a major economic driver but also a primary source of phosphorus pollution.
- **Climate Change Adaptation**: How to restore the ecosystem while accounting for sea-level rise, increased hurricane intensity, and changing rainfall patterns is a major ongoing challenge.
- **Invasive Species Control**: The effectiveness and cost of managing pervasive invasives like pythons, melaleuca, and Brazilian pepper is a continuous struggle.
