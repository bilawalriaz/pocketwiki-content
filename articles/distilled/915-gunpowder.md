# Gunpowder

## Overview
Gunpowder, the earliest known chemical explosive, is a granular mixture of potassium nitrate (saltpeter), charcoal, and sulfur. It is classified as a low explosive because it deflagrates (burns quickly) rather than detonates, making it ideal as a propellant in firearms and artillery but less effective for shattering rock. Invented in China during the Tang dynasty as a byproduct of alchemical experiments seeking an elixir of immortality, gunpowder spread across Eurasia and revolutionized warfare, industry, and technology. Its use declined with the rise of smokeless powders in the late 19th century, though it remains in niche applications such as fireworks and historical reenactments.

## Body

### Composition and Chemistry
Gunpowder consists of three components: potassium nitrate (KNO₃) as the oxidizer, charcoal (mostly carbon) and sulfur as fuels. The standard composition by weight is 75% saltpeter, 15% charcoal, and 10% sulfur, though variations exist for specific uses. Blasting powder, for example, uses 70% nitrate, 14% charcoal, and 16% sulfur. Sulfur lowers the ignition temperature, increasing combustion rate. Modern gunpowder grains are often coated with graphite to prevent static buildup. The combustion reaction is complex, producing solids (potassium carbonate, sulfate, sulfide), gases (CO₂, N₂, CO), and water vapor. Gunpowder releases about 3 megajoules per kilogram, significantly less than TNT (4.7 MJ/kg) or gasoline (10.4 MJ/kg when accounting for oxygen mass).

### Historical Development in China
Gunpowder originated in China during the Tang dynasty, first documented in 808 AD in the *Taishang Shengzu Jindan Mijue*. Taoist alchemists likely discovered it accidentally while seeking an elixir of immortality. The Chinese name "huǒ yào" ("fire medicine") reflects its medicinal origins. By the 11th century, the *Wujing Zongyao* recorded early formulas, though these contained insufficient saltpeter for explosive effects. Military applications evolved from incendiary projectiles (904 AD) to bombs, fire lances, and eventually metal-barreled hand cannons by the late 13th century. The Jin-Song Wars (12th century) saw widespread use of fire lances and bombs, with iron-cased bombs appearing in the early 13th century.

### Spread to the Middle East and Europe
Knowledge of gunpowder reached the Islamic world between 1240 and 1280, likely via Mongol invasions. Hasan al-Rammah's 13th-century text *al-Furūsiyya wal-Manāsib al-Ḥarbiyya* included 107 recipes, some nearly matching modern ideal proportions. The term "midfa" described early gunpowder weapons, evolving from flamethrowers to cannons. In Europe, Roger Bacon documented gunpowder in 1267, and by 1346 it was manufactured at the Tower of London. The late 14th-century corning technique—forming damp powder into grains—dramatically improved performance, increasing power by 30–300%.

### European Advancements and Industrial Use
European manufacturers refined purification methods and standardized production. The Renaissance saw two schools of pyrotechnic thought in Italy and Nuremberg. Antoine Lavoisier revolutionized French gunpowder production in the 1770s by ending the oppressive *droit de fouille* (right to dig) and instituting scientific management. The 19th century brought further innovations: Lammot du Pont's graphite-coated sodium nitrate powder (1857), and systematic grading by grain size (F through FFFFg). Industrial applications dominated 19th-century production, with more powder used in mining and construction than warfare.

### Decline and Modern Status
The late 19th century introduction of smokeless powder marked gunpowder's decline. Most British manufacturers merged into Explosives Trades Limited (post-WWI), later becoming part of Imperial Chemical Industries. The last British gunpowder factory closed in 1976. Today, gunpowder survives in historical firearms, fireworks, and specialized applications. Modern substitutes like Pyrodex and Triple Seven offer reduced fouling while maintaining traditional charge measurements.

## Terms
- **Deflagration**: Rapid combustion at subsonic speeds, characteristic of low explosives like gunpowder
- **Detonation**: Supersonic combustion producing a shockwave, characteristic of high explosives
- **Brisance**: The shattering effect of an explosive, determined by the speed of its reaction
- **Corning**: The process of forming gunpowder into grains to improve combustion and handling
- **Saltpeter**: Potassium nitrate (KNO₃), the oxidizing agent in gunpowder
- **Fire lance**: Early Chinese weapon that combined a gunpowder charge with a spear-like tube
- **Serpentine**: Original fine-grained European gunpowder, ground to flour-like consistency
- **Midfa**: Arabic term for early gunpowder weapons, evolving from flamethrowers to cannons
- **Mealed powder**: Finely ground gunpowder used for priming and ignition
- **Smokeless powder**: Modern propellant with different burning characteristics, largely replacing gunpowder

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians debate the exact transmission routes of gunpowder technology from China to the West. While literary and archaeological evidence supports a Chinese origin, the rapid spread across Eurasia remains puzzling—occurring over decades rather than centuries like paper, compass, and printing. Translation difficulties complicate early records: the Arabic word "naft" shifted from meaning naphtha to gunpowder, and Chinese "pào" evolved from trebuchet to cannon. Some historians question claims of early Islamic cannon use (e.g., at Ain Jalut 1260) due to ambiguous terminology. The role of Mongols as transmitters is debated, with some arguing for direct Chinese-Islamic contact. Additionally, the interpretation of early European references—such as the "long lance" at the Battle of Mohi (1241)—remains contested, with accounts variously describing gas attacks, early cannons, or non-gunpowder weapons.
