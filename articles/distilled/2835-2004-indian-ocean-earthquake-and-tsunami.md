# 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami

## Overview
The 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami was a catastrophic undersea megathrust earthquake and subsequent tsunami that struck on December 26, 2004. It is one of the deadliest natural disasters in recorded history, killing an estimated 227,898 people across 14 countries. The event was significant not only for its immense human toll but also for its unprecedented scale, as the earthquake was one of the most powerful ever recorded, and the tsunami it generated affected coastlines across the entire Indian Ocean basin.

## Timeline
- **26 Dec 2004** — A Mw 9.2–9.3 earthquake strikes off the coast of Aceh, Indonesia, at 07:58:53 local time.
- **26 Dec 2004** — A massive tsunami, with waves up to 30 m high, devastates coastal communities around the Indian Ocean.
- **28 Dec 2004** — The Free Aceh Movement (GAM) declares a cease-fire following the widespread devastation.
- **Early 2005** — A worldwide humanitarian response begins, with donations totaling over US$14 billion. |
- **15 Aug 2005** — A peace agreement is signed between the Indonesian government and GAM, explicitly citing the tsunami as a justification.
- **Early 2005** — The Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System is formed to provide early warnings for the region.

## Body

### The Earthquake
The earthquake, known as the Sumatra–Andaman earthquake, was caused by a rupture along the fault boundary where the Indian plate subducts beneath the Burma plate. It had a magnitude of Mw 9.2–9.3, making it the most powerful earthquake ever recorded in Asia and one of the strongest globally since 1900. The rupture was the longest ever observed, extending 1,200–1,300 km, and lasted at least ten minutes. The sudden vertical rise of the seafloor by several metres displaced an estimated 30 km³ of water, triggering the tsunami. The event was so massive it caused the entire planet to vibrate and slightly altered Earth's rotation, shortening the day by 2.68 microseconds.

### The Tsunami
The displaced water generated a tsunami that radiated across the Indian Ocean. In deep water, the waves traveled at 500–1,000 km/h but were only about 600 mm high. Upon reaching shallow coastal waters, they slowed and grew into destructive waves up to 30 m high. The tsunami struck some coastlines within minutes (Indonesia) and others hours later (Somalia, South Africa). The lack of a tsunami warning system in the Indian Ocean meant nearly all victims were taken by surprise. The waves caused catastrophic damage in Indonesia, Sri Lanka, India, Thailand, and as far away as East Africa.

### Impact and Humanitarian Response
The disaster killed an estimated 227,898 people, with Indonesia suffering the most (around 170,000 deaths). About one-third of the dead were children. The tsunami also killed up to 9,000 foreign tourists. The environmental impact was severe, contaminating freshwater supplies with salt and destroying ecosystems like mangroves and coral reefs. Economically, local fishing and tourism industries were devastated. A massive international humanitarian aid effort followed, with over US$14 billion donated globally. The disaster also had political consequences, helping to broker a long-sought peace in the Aceh conflict.

## Terms
- **Megathrust earthquake**: An earthquake occurring at subduction zones where one tectonic plate is being forced under another, capable of producing the world's largest earthquakes.
- **Subduction zone**: A geological boundary where two tectonic plates converge, and one plate sinks beneath the other into the Earth's mantle.
- **Sunda megathrust**: The specific fault zone where the Indian plate subducts beneath the Burma plate, responsible for the 2004 earthquake.
- **Tsunami**: A series of ocean waves caused by a large-scale, sudden displacement of the ocean floor, typically from an earthquake.
- **Teletsunami**: A tsunami that causes damage far from its source, often across an entire ocean basin.
- **Run-up**: The maximum vertical height above sea level that a tsunami reaches on land.
- **Inundation**: The horizontal distance inland that a tsunami wave travels.
- **Foreshock**: A smaller earthquake that precedes a larger earthquake in the same area.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Magnitude Estimation**: The earthquake's magnitude has been revised in various studies, with estimates ranging from Mw 9.1 to 9.3, with Mw 9.2–9.3 being the most representative.
- **Future Hazard Assessment**: Before 2004, it was thought that giant earthquakes required young, buoyant crust, fast plate convergence, and the absence of back-arc basins. The 2004 event challenged these assumptions, requiring a reassessment of earthquake hazard in similar regions.
- **Warning Systems**: The lack of an Indian Ocean tsunami warning system was a major factor in the high death toll. The subsequent creation of the Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System in 2005 was a direct response, but its effectiveness and coverage remain subjects of ongoing evaluation.
- **Historical Precedent**: Scientific evidence suggests major tsunamis in the region occur every few hundred years, with the last comparable event around A.D. 1300–1450, indicating a long recurrence interval for such catastrophes.
