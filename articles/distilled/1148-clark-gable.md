# Clark Gable

## Overview
William Clark Gable (February 1, 1901 – November 16, 1960) was an American actor often referred to as the "King of Hollywood." He appeared in a total of 67 theatrically released motion pictures across a variety of genres during a 37-year career, three decades of which he spent as a leading man. In 1999, the American Film Institute ranked Gable as the seventh-greatest male screen legend of classical Hollywood cinema. Gable won the Academy Award for Best Actor for his role in Frank Capra's *It Happened One Night* (1934) and earned nominations in the same category for portraying Fletcher Christian in Frank Lloyd's *Mutiny on the Bounty* (1935) and Rhett Butler in Victor Fleming's *Gone with the Wind* (1939). For his comedic performances in George Seaton's *Teacher's Pet* (1958) and Walter Lang's *But Not for Me* (1959), Gable received nominations for the Golden Globe Award for Best Actor in a Motion Picture – Musical or Comedy. His other notable films include William A. Wellman's *Call of the Wild* (1935), George Sidney's *Key to the City* (1950), and John Ford's *Mogambo* (1953). His final onscreen role was as an aging cowboy in John Huston's *The Misfits* (1961). Gable was one of the most consistently bankable stars in the history of Hollywood, appearing 16 times on Quigley Publishing's annual Top Ten Money Making Stars Poll. He died of a heart attack at the age of 59.

## Body

### Early Life and Rise to Stardom
Born into a modest Ohio family, Gable lost his mother early and was raised by his father and stepmother, who emphasized grooming and discipline. After working odd jobs and taking acting lessons from Josephine Dillon—who also became his first wife—he moved to Hollywood in 1924. Initially typecast as a villain or extra, he gained attention through stage roles and small film parts before landing a contract with MGM in 1931.

### MGM Stardom and Iconic Roles
At MGM, Gable quickly rose to prominence, starring in *The Painted Desert* (1931), *Red Dust* (1932), and *Strange Interlude* (1932). His chemistry with co-stars like Joan Crawford and Jean Harlow solidified his image as a leading man. In 1934, he won the Academy Award for Best Actor for *It Happened One Night*, a screwball comedy that redefined romantic genres. His performance as Rhett Butler in *Gone with the Wind* (1939) earned him an Academy Award nomination, and remains legendary, particularly for the line "Frankly, my dear, I don't give a damn."

### World War II Service
Following the death of his wife Carole Lombard in a plane crash in 1942, Gable enlisted in the U.S. Army Air Forces. He served as head of a motion picture unit with the 351st Bomb Group, flying five combat missions, including one to Germany, as an observer-gunner in B-17 Flying Fortresses between May 4 and September 23, 1943, earning the Distinguished Flying Cross and Air Medal. His service was both patriotic and personal, reflecting his deep connection to country and loss.

### Post-War Career and Later Years
After the war, Gable returned to film with mixed results. While *Mogambo* (1953) was a commercial success, his later films struggled to match earlier acclaim. He left MGM in 1954, seeking creative control and better roles. His final film, *The Misfits* (1961), released posthumously, was critically praised as one of his finest performances.

### Personal Life and Legacy
Gable married five times, including a brief but happy marriage to Carole Lombard. He had a daughter, Judy Lewis, with actress Loretta Young, a relationship kept secret until decades after his death. Politically conservative, he co-founded the Motion Picture Alliance for the Preservation of American Ideals. He died in 1960 at age 59, remembered as a symbol of classic Hollywood masculinity.

## Terms
- **Screwball Comedy**: A subgenre of comedy featuring absurd situations and romantic entanglements, often involving class conflict.
- **Pre-Code Era**: Period in Hollywood before strict censorship enforcement, allowing more adult themes in films.
- **Motion Picture Unit**: Military units during WWII that produced training and propaganda films.
- **Distinguished Flying Cross**: U.S. military decoration awarded for heroism or extraordinary achievement in aerial flight.
- **Hollywood Blacklist**: Systematic exclusion of individuals in the entertainment industry suspected of communist affiliations.
- **Bankable Star**: An actor whose name or presence guarantees box office success.
- **Matinee Idol**: A male performer popular with audiences, especially young women.
- **Leading Man**: Male actor who plays the primary romantic interest or hero in a film.
- **Method Acting**: Acting technique emphasizing emotional memory and psychological realism.
- **Studio System**: Hollywood production model dominated by major studios controlling all aspects of filmmaking.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Rhett Butler Casting**: Gary Cooper initially turned down the role, reportedly predicting the film's failure. Whether Gable was truly reluctant or simply playing to type remains debated.
- **Relationship with Loretta Young**: The nature of Gable and Young's relationship and the circumstances of Judy Lewis's conception remain contested, with conflicting accounts about consent.
- **Impact of WWII Service**: Some argue Gable's military service enhanced his public image, while others suggest it disrupted his career momentum.
- **Later Career Decline**: Critics like David Thomson questioned whether Gable's post-war film choices matched his earlier stature, sparking debate over his lasting relevance.
- **Cultural Legacy**: Gable's portrayal of masculinity continues to be analyzed—celebrated by some as timeless, criticized by others as outdated.
