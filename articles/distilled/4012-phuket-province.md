# Phuket province

## Overview
Phuket is Thailand's largest island and a southern province, historically a strategic tin-trading port on the India-China route. Today, it is a major global tourism destination, with an economy that transitioned from tin mining to rubber and then to tourism. Its history includes significant European contact, a celebrated defense against Burmese invasion, and modern challenges like the 2004 tsunami and the COVID-19 pandemic.

## Timeline
- **1545** — Portuguese explorer Fernão Mendes Pinto provides early European accounts of the island, then called Junk Ceylon.
- **1680s** — Dutch, English, and French compete for tin trade; France gains a monopoly in 1685.
- **1688** — French influence in Siam is reduced after a revolution.
- **1785** — Burmese invasion repelled by heroines Thao Thep Kasattri and Thao Si Sunthon.
- **1876** — Chinese laborer uprising in tin mines occurs amid declining tin prices.
- **1933** — Monthon Phuket is dissolved; Phuket becomes a separate province.
- **1980s** — Tourism development begins in earnest on west coast beaches.
- **2004** — Indian Ocean tsunami causes significant damage and fatalities.
- **2017** — Phuket welcomes ~10 million tourists, generating ~385 billion baht in revenue.
- **2020** — COVID-19 pandemic leads to lockdowns, severely impacting tourism.

## Body

### History and Name
The name "Phuket" derives from the Malay word *Bukit*, meaning 'hill'. Historically inhabited by Austronesian Malays, the island was later settled by Siamese kingdoms. It was formerly known as Thalang (from Malay *Telong*, 'cape') and to Westerners as Junk Ceylon. Its location on a major trade route led to frequent European contact from the 16th century, though it was never colonized. In the 17th century, European powers competed for its tin resources. A pivotal moment came in 1785 when, during the "Nine Armies' Wars," the sisters Thao Thep Kasattri and Thao Si Sunthon successfully defended Thalang from a Burmese siege by tricking the enemy into overestimating the island's defenses. In the 19th century, under King Chulalongkorn, Phuket became the administrative center for southern tin provinces. The modern economy shifted from tin to rubber and, since the 1980s, primarily to tourism.

### Geography and Climate
Phuket Island, located in the Andaman Sea, is Thailand's largest island at 543 km². It is mountainous, with a range running north-south. The highest point is traditionally cited as Khao Mai Thao Sip Song (529 m), though an unnamed peak may reach 542 m. About 60% of the island is covered with forests, rubber, and palm oil plantations. The west coast has sandy beaches, while the east coast is muddy. Key protected areas include the Khao Phra Thaeo No-Hunting Area and Sirinat National Park, which contains a sea turtle nesting site. The climate is tropical monsoon, with a dry season (December–March) and a wet season. Average annual rainfall is about 2,200 mm.

### Demographics and Economy
Phuket's population is diverse, including Buddhists, a significant Muslim minority (about 20%), and people of Chinese ancestry, notably the Peranakan "Phuket Babas." The 2010 census recorded 525,018 people, but the actual population, including unregistered migrants and expatriates, is estimated to be much higher. The economy is dominated by tourism, which in 2017 generated nearly 14% of Thailand's total tourism revenue. Rubber plantations are also important. Phuket is a major hub for luxury yachting and was listed as a top global retirement destination in 2005. The tourism sector faced a downturn in early 2019 due to fewer arrivals and hotel oversupply, but revenues still increased slightly.

### Transportation and Infrastructure
Phuket International Airport (HKT) underwent a major expansion to increase capacity to 12.5 million passengers annually. The island has no direct rail link; the nearest stations are ~230 km away. Local transport includes songthaews, buses, motorcycle taxis, and ride-share services. A planned tram network aims to connect the airport to Chalong. Ferry services operate to neighboring islands like Phi Phi and Koh Lanta.

### Culture and Attractions
Phuket's culture reflects its diverse heritage. Key festivals include the annual Thao Thep Krasattri and Thao Si Sunthon Fair (March 13), the Chinese Vegetarian Festival (September/October) with its ascetic rituals, and the Ghost Festival. Major attractions include the 45-meter-tall Big Buddha statue, the Two Heroines Monument, Wat Chalong temple, and the historic Sino-Portuguese architecture of Old Phuket Town. Beaches like Patong, Karon, and Kata are central to tourism.

## Terms
- **Changwat**: Thai word for province.
- **Monthon**: An administrative division used in Thailand from 1893 to 1933.
- **Peranakan/Phuket Baba**: A community of descendants of Chinese immigrants, particularly tin miners, who integrated with local Malay culture.
- **Songthaew**: A passenger vehicle, typically a converted pickup truck with two benches.
- **Tambon**: A Thai administrative sub-district.
- **Tin monopoly**: A government-granted exclusive right to trade in tin, a key historical resource for Phuket.
- **Tsunami-detection buoy**: Part of an Indian Ocean warning system installed after the 2004 disaster.
- **Vegetarian Festival**: A Chinese-origin festival in Phuket involving a nine-day vegetarian diet and rituals for purification.

## Debates and Open questions
- The exact population of Phuket is debated, with official census figures (e.g., 360,905 in 2012) differing widely from estimates of actual residents (e.g., ~2 million in 2025) due to unregistered migrants and expatriates.
- The total number of hotel rooms in Phuket is uncertain, with estimates ranging from 40,000 to over 93,000, highlighting the difficulty in quantifying tourism infrastructure.
