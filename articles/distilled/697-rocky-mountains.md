# Rocky Mountains

## Overview
The Rocky Mountains are the largest mountain system in North America, stretching approximately 3,000 miles from northern British Columbia to New Mexico. They formed between 80 and 55 million years ago during the Laramide orogeny through shallow-angle subduction of oceanic plates beneath the North American plate, and have since been shaped by glaciation and erosion. The range holds significant ecological, cultural, and economic value, supporting diverse wildlife, Indigenous and settler histories, and industries such as mining, tourism, and forestry.

## Body

### Geography
The Rocky Mountains are the easternmost part of the North American Cordillera, extending from the Liard River in British Columbia to the headwaters of the Pecos River in New Mexico. The range varies in width from 110 to 480 kilometers and includes numerous subranges such as the Front Range, Wind River Range, and Teton Range. The Continental Divide runs through the Rockies, separating waters flowing to the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. The highest peak is Mount Elbert in Colorado at 4,401 meters (14,440 feet). Population density is low, averaging four people per square kilometer, though growth occurred rapidly between 1950 and 1990.

### Geology
The oldest rocks in the Rockies are Precambrian metamorphic formations, overlain by Paleozoic sedimentary layers deposited in ancient shallow seas. The Ancestral Rocky Mountains formed around 300 million years ago during the Pennsylvanian period. The modern Rockies arose during the Laramide orogeny (80–55 million years ago), driven by shallow subduction of the Farallon Plate beneath North America. This process caused widespread crustal deformation and uplift. Glaciation during the Pleistocene and Holocene epochs carved U-shaped valleys and cirques. Alternative tectonic models, including a "hit-and-run" collision hypothesis, have been proposed to explain some uplift patterns.

### Ecology and Climate
The Rockies span latitudes from 59°N to 35°N, creating varied climates and ecosystems. Biotic zones range from prairie below 550 meters to alpine tundra above the tree line, which varies from 3,700 m (12,000 ft) in New Mexico to 760 m (2,500 ft) at the northern end of the Rockies (near the Yukon). Ten forested zones are recognized, defined by dominant tree species such as ponderosa pine, Douglas fir, and whitebark pine. Wildlife includes elk, moose, grizzly bears, wolves, and mountain goats. Many species declined due to European-American settlement, though some, like the bald eagle and gray wolf, have partially recovered through conservation efforts.

### History
Indigenous peoples inhabited the Rockies after the last ice age, including the Apache, Blackfoot, Crow, Shoshone, and Ute. Paleo-Indians hunted mammoths and ancient bison, practicing seasonal migration between plains and mountains. European exploration began with Spanish incursions in the 16th century, followed by French-Canadian fur traders in the 18th century. Alexander Mackenzie crossed the range in 1793, and the Lewis and Clark Expedition mapped it from 1804 to 1806. Fur trading posts, including those established by the North West Company and Hudson’s Bay Company, marked early European presence. The Oregon Trail brought settlers from the 1840s onward, and gold rushes in the mid-19th century spurred rapid development. National parks and forest reserves were established in the late 19th and early 20th centuries to preserve scenic landscapes. In 1905, U.S. President Theodore Roosevelt extended the Medicine Bow Forest Reserve to include the area now managed as Rocky Mountain National Park.

### Economy
Mining has been a cornerstone of the Rocky Mountain economy, with major production of copper, gold, silver, molybdenum, and coal. The Climax mine in Colorado was the world’s largest molybdenum producer, employing over 3,000 workers. Abandoned mines with their wakes of mine tailings and toxic wastes dot the Rocky Mountain landscape. In one major example, eighty years of zinc mining profoundly polluted the river and bank near Eagle River in north-central Colorado. High concentrations of the metal carried by spring runoff harmed algae, moss, and trout populations. An economic analysis of mining effects at this site revealed declining property values, degraded water quality, and the loss of recreational opportunities. The analysis also revealed that cleanup of the river could yield $2.3 million in additional revenue from recreation. In 1983, the former owner of the zinc mine was sued by the Colorado Attorney General for the $4.8 million cleanup costs; five years later, ecological recovery was considerable. Coalbed methane, extracted via dewatering or hydraulic fracturing, supplies 7 percent of the natural gas used in the U.S., particularly from the San Juan and Powder River Basins. Agriculture relies on dryland farming, irrigation, and transhumance—moving livestock between seasonal pastures. Tourism is a major industry, drawing millions annually to national parks such as Yellowstone, Banff, and Jasper for hiking, skiing, and outdoor recreation.

### Hazards
The Rockies pose risks including encounters with bears and mountain lions, extreme weather, avalanches, and falling rocks. Steep terrain makes falls potentially fatal, with documented fatalities occurring in 1993, 2007, 2015, and 2018. Search and rescue operations are common, but comprehensive fatality statistics are not maintained by the U.S. Forest Service.

## Terms
- **Laramide orogeny**: A period of mountain-building (80–55 million years ago) caused by shallow subduction of oceanic plates beneath North America.
- **Continental Divide**: A north-south line along the Rocky Mountains separating river systems flowing to the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans.
- **Alpine tundra**: A high-elevation biome above the tree line, characterized by low temperatures and sparse vegetation.
- **Transhumance**: The seasonal movement of livestock between high-elevation summer pastures and low-elevation winter ranges.
- **Biotic zone**: An ecological region defined by climate, vegetation, and indicator species.
- **Subduction**: The process by which one tectonic plate moves beneath another, often triggering mountain-building.
- **Cirque**: A bowl-shaped depression carved by glaciers at the head of a valley.
- **Coalbed methane**: Natural gas trapped in coal seams, extracted through dewatering or fracturing.

## Debates and Open Questions
The exact northern terminus of the Rockies remains debated, with Canadian definitions excluding ranges like the Brooks Range, while the Geological Society of America includes them as the "Arctic Rockies." The mechanism behind Laramide uplift is also contested; while shallow-angle subduction is the traditional explanation, some researchers advocate for a "hit-and-run" tectonic collision model involving oblique plate interactions. Additionally, the full extent of prehistoric Indigenous impact on mammal populations and fire regimes is still under investigation.
