# History of Mesopotamia

## Overview
Mesopotamia, meaning "Between the Rivers" (the Tigris and Euphrates), is a historical region in the modern Middle East, primarily in Iraq. It is considered a "cradle of civilization" because it was home to some of the world's earliest major urban societies, writing systems, and legal codes. Its history spans from prehistoric human occupation to the Arab Muslim conquests in the 7th century AD, pieced together from archaeology and, later, written records.

## Timeline
- **c. 10,000–8,700 BC** — Pre-Pottery Neolithic A (PPNA) period; introduction of agriculture.
- **c. 6500–3800 BC** — Ubaid period; earliest known period on the southern alluvial plain.
- **4th millennium BC** — Uruk period; emergence of urban life and early cuneiform script.
- **c. 2900–2350 BC** — Early Dynastic period; Sumerian city-states (e.g., Ur, Uruk) vie for power.
- **c. 2350–2170 BC** — Akkadian Empire; Sargon creates first empire, Akkadian language spreads.
- **c. 2112–2004 BC** — Ur III Empire; last great Sumerian empire, high state control.
- **c. 1894 BC** — Founding of Babylon as an independent state by Amorites.
- **c. 1792–1750 BC** — Reign of Hammurabi of Babylon; unifies Mesopotamia, famous law code.
- **c. 1720–1076 BC** — Middle Assyrian period; Assyria becomes major power, overthrows Mitanni.
- **c. 1650–1155 BC** — Kassite dynasty rules Babylon; longest-lasting Babylonian dynasty.
- **911–612 BC** — Neo-Assyrian Empire; largest empire to that date, vast territorial control.
- **620–539 BC** — Neo-Babylonian Empire; Nabopolassar rebels, destroys Nineveh (612 BC).
- **539–330 BC** — Achaemenid Persian rule; Mesopotamia flourishes as a satrapy.
- **330 BC–1st century AD** — Hellenistic (Seleucid) and then Parthian rule.
- **3rd–7th centuries AD** — Sassanid Persian rule; Christianity flourishes in Assyria.
- **630s–651 AD** — Arab Muslim conquest; end of ancient Mesopotamian political entities.

## Body

### Prehistory and Early Urbanization
Human settlement began in the foothills of the Taurus and Zagros Mountains. The Pre-Pottery Neolithic periods (c. 10,000–6800 BC) saw the transition to agriculture and animal domestication. Monumental structures like Göbekli Tepe (c. 10,000 BC) were built by hunter-gatherers. Later, distinct cultures like the Halaf (known for painted pottery) and Ubaid emerged. The Ubaid culture spread from the south, possibly through adoption rather than invasion, laying the groundwork for urbanization. The Uruk period (4th millennium BC) saw the rise of cities like Uruk and the invention of cuneiform writing.

### The Third Millennium BC: Sumerians and Akkadians
The Early Dynastic period (c. 2900–2350 BC) was characterized by competing Sumerian city-states. The Sumerian language is a language isolate, and their origins are debated. By 2600 BC, cuneiform had developed into a syllabic script. The Akkadian Empire (c. 2350–2170 BC), founded by Sargon, created the first large empire and saw Akkadian become a lingua franca. After its fall to the Guti, the Ur III Empire (c. 2112–2004 BC) re-established Sumerian dominance with unprecedented state control before being overthrown by Elamites and Amorites.

### The Second Millennium BC: Amorites, Kassites, and Assyria
The Isin-Larsa period saw Amorite dynasties dominate the south. Babylon was founded in 1894 BC and rose to prominence under Hammurabi (c. 1792–1750 BC), who conquered much of Mesopotamia. In the north, Assyria repelled Amorites but was later ruled by the Amorite Shamshi-Adad I. The Middle Assyrian period (c. 1720–1076 BC) saw Assyria become a major imperial power, overthrowing the Mitanni Empire. The Kassites ruled Babylon for over four centuries (c. 1650–1155 BC) but left few records. The period ended with widespread invasions and collapse (c. 1200 BC).

### The First Millennium BC: Imperial Assyria and Babylon
The Neo-Assyrian Empire (911–612 BC) was the largest and most powerful empire yet, conquering Egypt, Babylon, and the Levant. It fell in 612 BC to a coalition of Babylonians, Medes, and Scythians. The Neo-Babylonian Empire (620–539 BC), under rulers like Nebuchadnezzar II, succeeded it but was conquered by the Persian Achaemenid Empire in 539 BC. Mesopotamia then passed through Hellenistic (Seleucid), Parthian, and Sassanid Persian rule. Christianity and Mandaeanism emerged, and Assyrian Christian traditions persisted. The region was finally incorporated into the Islamic Caliphate in the 7th century AD.

## Terms
- **Cuneiform**: The earliest known system of writing, developed from pictographs in the late 4th millennium BC, using wedge-shaped marks on clay tablets.
- **Sumerian**: A language isolate (unrelated to any other known language) spoken in southern Mesopotamia until the 1st millennium AD, when it was replaced by Akkadian.
- **Akkadian**: A Semitic language that became the lingua franca of Mesopotamia from the Akkadian Empire onward, written in cuneiform.
- **Amorites**: A Semitic people who established dynasties across Mesopotamia in the 2nd millennium BC, including the First Dynasty of Babylon.
- **Kassites**: A people of unknown linguistic origin who ruled Babylon for over four centuries (c. 1650–1155 BC).
- **Mitanni**: A Hurrian-speaking empire in northern Mesopotamia (c. 1500–1300 BC) that was a major power before being overthrown by Assyria.
- **Stratigraphy**: The archaeological study of layered deposits (strata) to establish a relative chronology, where newer layers are typically on top of older ones.
- **Middle Chronology**: A widely used chronological framework for the 3rd and 2nd millennia BC, though its dates are debated and vary by up to 150 years from other proposals.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Chronology**: The absolute dating of the 3rd and 2nd millennia BC is highly debated, with "Ultra-long," "Long," "Middle," "Short," and "Ultra-short" chronologies proposed, differing by up to 150 years. The Middle Chronology remains common despite uncertainties.
- **Sumerian Origins**: The origins of the Sumerians are unknown, as their language is a language isolate with no clear external relatives.
- **Ubaid Expansion**: The spread of the Ubaid culture from south to north Mesopotamia is debated; it may have been through cultural adoption rather than invasion, as there is no archaeological hiatus between the preceding Halaf culture and the Northern Ubaid.
- **Early Dynastic History**: The Sumerian King List, once a key source, is now considered unreliable for reconstructing Early Dynastic political history.
