# Urban design

## Overview
Urban design is an interdisciplinary field focused on shaping the physical form of cities and regions, considering broader economic, social, and environmental values. It connects architecture, landscape architecture, and urban planning to organize community environments, with the goal of creating equitable, beautiful, performative, and sustainable urban spaces.

## Timeline
- **c. 5th century BC** — Hippodamus of Miletus, considered the "father of European urban planning," develops the grid plan.
- **9th century** — English towns like Southampton and Wareham are designed on grids for defense (Burghal Hidage).
- **12th century** — The burgage system brings self-organizing design to medieval Western European towns.
- **1898** — Sir Ebenezer Howard publishes *Garden Cities of To-morrow*, initiating the garden city movement.
- **1909** — The University of Liverpool offers the first academic course on urban planning.
- **1928** — The International Congresses of Modern Architecture (CIAM) is founded.
- **1956** — The term "urban design" is first used at a Harvard University conference.
- **1961** — Jane Jacobs publishes *The Death and Life of Great American Cities*; Kevin Lynch publishes *The Image of the City*.
- **1980s** — New Urbanism emerges to combat suburban sprawl.
- **1990s** — Landscape urbanism and sustainable urbanism theories surface.
- **2010s** — Movements like Black Lives Matter challenge design thinking regarding equity and public space.

## Body

### Theory and Interdisciplinary Nature
Urban design theory primarily concerns the design and management of public space—the streets, plazas, parks, and infrastructure used by the general public. It is an interdisciplinary field that borrows knowledge from architecture, landscape design, urban planning, civil engineering, sociology, urban economics, and other social and natural sciences. Key theorists include Jane Jacobs, Kevin Lynch, and Christopher Alexander.

### Historical Development
Urban design has been practiced throughout history, with ancient examples in Classical Chinese, Roman, and Greek cultures. The early modern era saw Renaissance and Enlightenment influences, with Spanish colonial cities often planned. The 19th century brought industrialization, public health concerns, and the rise of landscape architecture with figures like Frederick Law Olmsted. Modern urban design emerged around 1900 to mitigate industrial-age consequences, led by visionaries like Sir Ebenezer Howard.

### 20th Century Professionalization and Paradigm Shifts
Urban planning became professionalized in the early 1900s, with the first academic courses and official acts like the UK's Housing, Town Planning, &c. Act 1909. The automobile reshaped city layouts. Post-WWII, the term "urban design" was coined (1956), and influential works by Jacobs, Lynch, and others critiqued modernism and introduced concepts like "serial vision" and urban "legibility." The field has undergone major paradigm shifts, including the formation of Team 10 (focusing on "Human Association"), the rise of environmental awareness (Brundtland Report), and challenges to modernism (demolition of Pruitt-Igoe). More recent shifts include neoliberalism's impact on public space and movements like Black Lives Matter emphasizing spatial justice.

### New Approaches and Current Trends
Contemporary urban design includes several distinct approaches. **New Urbanism** (1980s) promotes compact, walkable, mixed-use neighborhoods to combat sprawl. **Landscape urbanism** views the city as interconnected ecological fields rather than just buildings. **Everyday urbanism** focuses on the lived experience of residents. **Tactical urbanism** uses short-term, low-cost interventions for long-term change. **Sustainable urbanism** seeks balance between economy, equity, and environment. Current trends emphasize walkability, climate resilience, and creating long-lasting, livable environments.

## Terms
- **Public Space:** The totality of spaces used freely by the general public, such as streets, plazas, and parks.
- **Legibility:** The ease with which people can understand the layout of a place (concept developed by Kevin Lynch).
- **Serial Vision:** The concept that the urban landscape is experienced as a series of related spaces (coined by Gordon Cullen).
- **Garden City:** A planned community combining town and country, as envisioned by Ebenezer Howard.
- **New Urbanism:** An approach promoting walkable, mixed-use, and dense neighborhoods to reduce sprawl.
- **Landscape Urbanism:** A theory arguing the city is constructed of ecologically rich horizontal fields, not just objects and buildings.
- **Space Syntax:** A method to predict how movement patterns in cities affect vitality and behavior (developed by Bill Hillier).
- **Transit-Oriented Development (TOD):** A design concept for medium-density living centered around public transit (developed by Peter Calthorpe).
- **Planner's Triangle:** A diagram highlighting conflicts between economic development, environmental protection, and social equity (created by Scott Campbell).

## Debates and Open Questions
A central debate exists between **New Urbanism** and **Landscape Urbanism**. New Urbanism advocates for the neighborhood as the basic building block, emphasizing density and walkability, but is criticized as nostalgic and culturally problematic. Landscape urbanism proposes landscape as the foundation, embracing horizontality and adaptability, but is critiqued for limited application (mostly urban parks). **Everyday urbanism** argues for grassroots improvements over master-planned interventions. Underlying these debates is the ongoing challenge of integrating principles from multiple theories and ensuring interdisciplinary collaboration to address issues like climate change, social justice, and equity in the built environment.
