# Encyclopedia

## Overview
An encyclopedia is a comprehensive reference work that provides detailed, factual summaries of knowledge across all or specific fields of study. Unlike dictionaries, which focus on word definitions and etymology, encyclopedias aim to explain concepts, subjects, and disciplines in depth. They have evolved over 2,000 years from hand-copied manuscripts to digital, open-source platforms like Wikipedia, fundamentally changing how knowledge is compiled, accessed, and shared.

## Timeline
- **1st century AD** — Pliny the Elder compiles *Natural History*, an early encyclopedic work.
- **c. 600–625** — Isidore of Seville writes *Etymologiae*, the first major Christian encyclopedia.
- **1408** — The *Yongle Encyclopedia* is completed in China, becoming the largest paper encyclopedia.
- **1470** — A scribal error in a Latin manuscript creates the single word "encyclopaedia."
- **1493** — The *Nuremberg Chronicle* is printed, an early illustrated encyclopedia.
- **1704** — John Harris publishes *Lexicon Technicum*, introducing the modern alphabetical format.
- **1751–1772** — The French *Encyclopédie*, edited by Diderot and d'Alembert, is published.
- **1768–present** — The *Encyclopædia Britannica* is first published in Scotland.
- **1993** — Microsoft launches *Encarta*, a landmark CD-ROM multimedia encyclopedia.
- **2001** — Wikipedia is launched, pioneering the free, online, collaboratively edited encyclopedia.

## Body

### Etymology and Definition
The word "encyclopedia" derives from the Greek phrase *enkúklios paideía*, meaning "general education" or "complete knowledge." A 15th-century scribal error combined the two words into a single term. An encyclopedia is defined as a work that comprehensively treats a branch of knowledge or all knowledge, typically through articles arranged alphabetically or by subject. Its core purpose is to convey accumulated, reliable information on a topic, often including maps, illustrations, and bibliographies.

### Characteristics and Evolution
The modern encyclopedia evolved from the dictionary in the 18th century, adopting its alphabetical structure but providing much more extensive treatment of subjects. Four major elements define an encyclopedia: its subject matter (general or specialized), its scope (breadth and depth of knowledge), its method of organization (alphabetical or hierarchical), and its method of production (from state-sponsored to for-profit). The advent of digital media has enabled new organizational possibilities, such as hyperlinks and search functions, and has democratized authorship and access.

### Pre-Modern and Early Printed Encyclopedias
Before printing, encyclopedias were rare, hand-copied works for scholars and patrons. Pliny the Elder's *Natural History* (1st century AD) and Isidore of Seville's *Etymologiae* (c. 600–625) were foundational Western examples. China produced massive encyclopedic works like the *Yongle Encyclopedia* (1408). The printing press, introduced from Asia, allowed wider diffusion. Early printed works like the *Nuremberg Chronicle* (1493) integrated text and images. The 18th century saw the creation of the modern alphabetical format with John Harris's *Lexicon Technicum* (1704) and the influential French *Encyclopédie*.

### Digital and Online Encyclopedias
The late 20th century saw encyclopedias published on CD-ROMs and DVDs, such as Microsoft's *Encarta* (1993–2009), which featured multimedia elements. The 21st century is dominated by online encyclopedias. The concept of a free, internet-based encyclopedia began with proposals like Interpedia (1993) and GNUPedia (1999). Wikipedia, launched in 2001, became the world's largest and most prominent example, using a wiki model for open, collaborative editing. It has vastly expanded the accessibility, authorship, and variety of encyclopedia entries, with over 60 million articles in more than 300 languages.

## Terms
*   **Encyclopedia**: A reference work providing comprehensive summaries of knowledge, either general or specialized, through detailed articles.
*   **Dictionary**: A linguistic reference work that primarily lists words and their definitions, focusing on etymology, meaning, and usage.
*   **General encyclopedia**: An encyclopedia containing articles on topics in every field of knowledge.
*   **Specialized encyclopedia**: An encyclopedia covering a wide range of topics within a particular field, such as medicine or law.
*   **Wiki**: A website that allows collaborative editing of its content and structure by its users.
*   **CD-ROM encyclopedia**: An encyclopedia published on a compact disc for use with a personal computer, often featuring multimedia.
*   **Open-source**: Software or content for which the original source code or material is made freely available and may be redistributed and modified.
*   **Leishu**: A traditional Chinese genre of reference work or encyclopedia, often organized thematically.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source text notes the ongoing, blurred distinction between encyclopedias and dictionaries, as both can contain factual information about the subjects they define. The rise of digital and open-source models like Wikipedia has also shifted traditional notions of authority and reliability in encyclopedic knowledge, moving from expert-compiled works to collaboratively edited platforms.
