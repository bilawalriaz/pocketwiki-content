# Andrew Carnegie

## Overview
Andrew Carnegie (1835–1919) was a Scottish-born American industrialist who led the expansion of the U.S. steel industry in the late 19th century, becoming one of the richest men in history. After selling Carnegie Steel to J.P. Morgan in 1901 for $303.45 million (forming U.S. Steel, the first billion-dollar corporation), he devoted his final 18 years to philanthropy, giving away roughly $350 million (approx. $6.9 billion in 2025 dollars)—nearly 90% of his fortune. His 1889 "Gospel of Wealth" argued that the rich have a moral obligation to distribute surplus wealth for public benefit during their lifetimes, endorsing progressive taxation and estate taxes. He funded over 3,000 public libraries, established major institutions (Carnegie Mellon University, Carnegie Institution for Science, Carnegie Endowment for International Peace), and pursued world peace, though his legacy is complicated by the 1889 Johnstown Flood (linked to his South Fork Club membership) and the violent 1892 Homestead Strike at his steel plant.

## Body

### Early Life and Entry into Business
Carnegie was born in a one-room weaver’s cottage in Dunfermline, Scotland. His maternal uncle, Scottish political leader George Lauder Sr., deeply influenced him as a boy by introducing him to Robert Burns' writings and historical Scottish heroes such as Robert the Bruce, William Wallace, and Rob Roy. Lauder's son, also named George Lauder, grew up with Carnegie and later became his business partner in the United States. His father’s handloom business collapsed under industrial mechanization, and the family borrowed money from George Lauder Sr. to emigrate in 1848. In Allegheny, Pennsylvania, Carnegie worked 12-hour days as a bobbin boy, then as a telegraph messenger. He memorized Pittsburgh’s business geography and learned to read telegraph signals by ear—a skill that led to promotion. Colonel James Anderson’s Saturday-night library access shaped Carnegie’s self-education; he later credited this as the model for his library philanthropy.

### Railroad Apprenticeship and Capital Accumulation
At 18, Carnegie joined the Pennsylvania Railroad under Thomas A. Scott, becoming Western Division Superintendent at 24. The railroad was America’s first big business; Carnegie mastered management, cost control, and the value of vertical integration. Scott and railroad president J. Edgar Thomson facilitated Carnegie’s first investments—often insider deals in companies doing business with the railroad (Adams Express, Woodruff Sleeping Car). Carnegie reinvested returns in railroad-related industries (iron, bridges, rails), building capital while supplying the railroad and giving Scott and Thomson stakes in his ventures.

### Civil War and Transition to Iron
During the Civil War, Scott (now Assistant Secretary of War) appointed Carnegie Superintendent of Military Railways and Union telegraph lines in the East. Carnegie restored rail access to Washington, D.C., and managed troop transport. The war demonstrated the strategic necessity of rail, telegraph, and iron production. After the war, Carnegie left the railroad to concentrate on ironworks, forming Keystone Bridge Works and Union Ironworks. He leveraged railroad connections to secure contracts and supplied steel for the landmark Eads Bridge (completed 1874), a proof-of-concept for structural steel.

### Steel Empire: Innovation and Integration
Carnegie’s fortune rested on two innovations: (1) adopting the Bessemer process to burn carbon from pig iron rapidly and cheaply, dropping steel prices and capturing the rail market; (2) vertical integration—owning raw material sources (Lake Superior ore fields, coal mines, coke ovens), transport (railways, lake steamships), and manufacturing. The 1883 Homestead Steel Works purchase brought a 425-mile railway and tributary coal/iron fields. By 1889, U.S. steel output exceeded the UK’s; Carnegie owned a large share. He lobbied for protective tariffs (earning millions annually) and manipulated markets with railroad barons rather than competing freely.

### Sale to U.S. Steel and Retirement
In 1901, at 65, Carnegie prepared for retirement by converting enterprises into joint-stock companies. J.P. Morgan, seeking an integrated steel trust to eliminate duplication, negotiated the buyout through Charles M. Schwab. Carnegie's share of this amounted to $225.64 million (in 2025, $8.73 billion), which was paid to him in the form of 5%, 50-year gold bonds. U.S. Steel became the world’s first billion-dollar corporation (capitalized at $1.4B, 4% of U.S. GDP). Carnegie retired from business.

### Philanthropy: Libraries, Education, Science, and Peace
Carnegie’s giving followed his "Gospel of Wealth": surplus wealth is a public trust to be administered during life. His flagship project was public libraries—3,000 built across the English-speaking world, funded on condition that localities provide land and operating budgets. Inspired by Enoch Pratt’s Baltimore library, Carnegie saw libraries as engines of self-improvement. He founded Carnegie Institute of Technology (now Carnegie Mellon), Carnegie Institution for Science (Washington, D.C.), and Carnegie Trust for Universities of Scotland ($10M, exceeding total government funding for Scottish universities). He funded the Hooker Telescope at Mount Wilson, 7,000 church organs, Carnegie Hall, the Peace Palace at The Hague, and the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace. He established pension funds for Homestead workers and college professors (precursor to TIAA-CREF) and the Carnegie Hero Fund for civilian heroism. The Carnegie Corporation of New York (1911) was created to perpetuate his giving.

### Controversies: Johnstown Flood and Homestead Strike
Carnegie was a member of the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club, whose poorly maintained dam collapsed in 1889, killing 2,209 in Johnstown. Club members (including partner Henry Clay Frick) formed a relief committee but avoided public accountability; lawsuits were defeated by attorneys Philander Knox and James Hay Reed. Carnegie later donated a replacement library.

The 1892 Homestead Strike was a 143-day confrontation at Carnegie Steel’s main plant. With Carnegie in Scotland, Frick—staunchly anti-union—locked out the Amalgamated Association after demanding a 22% wage cut. Frick imported strikebreakers and 300 Pinkerton agents; a battle left 10 dead. The state militia restored order; anarchist Alexander Berkman wounded Frick in an assassination attempt. Operations resumed with non-union labor. Carnegie’s reputation suffered permanently, despite his physical absence.

### Intellectual Influences and Contradictions
Carnegie claimed discipleship to Herbert Spencer’s evolutionary philosophy ("All is well since all grows better"), which emphasized individual rights, laissez-faire, and "survival of the fittest"—including allowing the unfit to perish. Yet Carnegie’s practices diverged: he pursued market concentration (buying competitors, vertical integration) that Spencer called "commercial murder"; he accepted protective tariffs Spencer condemned as taxation for a minority; and he built vast philanthropic institutions Spencer dismissed as corrupt mechanisms that interfere with natural selection. Carnegie reconciled this by arguing concentration of capital was an evolutionary necessity and that charity should enable self-help (libraries, parks) rather than relieve suffering indiscriminately.

### World Peace and Anti-Imperialism
Influenced by John Bright, Carnegie opposed military intervention and American colonialism. He opposed Philippine annexation (1898), offering $20 million to the Philippines so that the Filipino people could purchase their independence from the United States, and joined the Anti-Imperialist League. He funded the Peace Palace (1913) and the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace (1910), believing scientific investigation of war’s causes and codification of international law could abolish war. The 1914 outbreak of WWI shattered his optimism, but his ideas informed the post-war League of Nations.

## Terms
- **Bessemer process**: A steelmaking technique (adopted by Carnegie) that blows air through molten pig iron to oxidize impurities rapidly, enabling cheap mass production of steel.
- **Vertical integration**: Business strategy of controlling all stages of production—from raw material extraction (ore, coal) through transport and manufacturing—to reduce costs and secure supply.
- **Gospel of Wealth**: Carnegie’s 1889 doctrine that the rich are trustees of surplus wealth and must distribute it for public benefit during their lifetimes; endorsed progressive and estate taxation.
- **Horizontal integration**: (Implied) Consolidation of competitors at the same production stage; Carnegie merged with or bought out rival steel firms to dominate the market.
- **Pinkerton agents**: Private security operatives (Pinkerton National Detective Agency) hired by Frick to protect strikebreakers at Homestead; their clash with workers caused 10 deaths.
- **U.S. Steel Corporation**: The trust formed by J.P. Morgan in 1901 through the buyout of Carnegie Steel and other major producers; first billion-dollar corporation.
- **Carnegie Corporation of New York**: Endowed in 1911 as a perpetual philanthropic vehicle to continue Carnegie’s giving after his death.
- **Survival of the fittest**: Spencerian evolutionary concept Carnegie invoked to justify laissez-faire competition, though his philanthropy contradicted Spencer’s rejection of aid to the "unfit."
- **Anti-Imperialist League**: Organization (1898) opposing U.S. annexation of the Philippines; Carnegie served as vice-president.
- **TIAA-CREF**: Teachers Insurance and Annuity Association–College Retirement Equities Fund, evolved from Carnegie’s 1905 pension fund for college professors.

## Debates and open questions
- **Philanthropy as justification**: Historians debate whether Carnegie’s giving genuinely redressed the social costs of his labor practices (wage suppression, 12-hour days, anti-unionism) or served to legitimize the extreme wealth concentration he engineered.
- **Spencerian consistency**: Scholars note the tension between Carnegie’s professed laissez-faire Spencerianism and his reliance on tariffs, insider railroad deals, and market manipulation—raising questions of sincere belief vs. rhetorical convenience.
- **Library design and control**: While Carnegie funded 3,000 libraries, historians (e.g., Van Slyck, White) argue the buildings reinforced paternalistic civic pride and that library boards (often dominated by mill managers) controlled reading material, limiting worker autonomy.
- **Homestead responsibility**: Though Carnegie was in Scotland during the 1892 strike, he authorized Frick’s hardline strategy; debate persists over his moral culpability for the violence and union-breaking.
- **Peace advocacy vs. war profits**: Carnegie’s steel mills supplied armor plate for the U.S. Navy expansion in the 1880s–90s while he publicly championed pacifism and arbitration—a contradiction he never fully resolved.
