# Casablanca

## Overview
Casablanca is Morocco's largest city and its primary economic and business center. Located on the Atlantic coast, it is the most populous city in the Maghreb region and a major financial and industrial hub for Africa. Its history, from an ancient Berber settlement to a modern metropolis, reflects Morocco's complex colonial past and its post-independence development.

## Timeline
- **7th century BC** — Founded and settled by Berbers.
- **1468** — Portuguese bombard and destroy the town of Anfa.
- **1756–1790** — Sultan Mohammed ben Abdallah rebuilds the town, naming it ad-Dār al-Bayḍāʾ.
- **1906** — Treaty of Algeciras formalizes French preeminence in Morocco.
- **August 1907** — French bombard the city in response to tribal resistance.
- **March 1912** — Treaty of Fez establishes the French Protectorate.
- **November 1942** — American forces capture Casablanca during Operation Torch.
- **January 1943** — Casablanca hosts the Anfa Conference between Churchill and Roosevelt.
- **1956** — Morocco gains independence from France.
- **2003** — Multiple suicide bomb attacks strike the city.
- **November 2023** — Casablanca joins UNESCO's Creative Cities Network.

## Body

### Etymology and Early History
The settlement was originally called **Anfa** by its Berber founders. The origin of the name is debated, with theories pointing to Tamazight (Berber) roots meaning "hill" or "promontory." After the Portuguese destroyed the town in 1468, they rebuilt it and called it **Casa Branca** ("White House"). When Sultan Mohammed ben Abdallah rebuilt the city in the 18th century, he gave it the Arabic name **ad-Dār al-Bayḍāʾ**, also meaning "The White House." The Spanish later calqued this as "Casablanca," which became the common name under the French protectorate.

### Colonial Era and World War II
The 19th century saw population growth as Casablanca became a key wool exporter. French influence was formalized by the 1906 Treaty of Algeciras, leading to a violent bombardment of the city in 1907 after local resistance. French rule was established in 1912, and the city was planned as a colonial port with a European *ville nouvelle* (new town) separate from the medina. During World War II, American forces captured the city from Vichy France in 1942. It later hosted the 1943 Anfa Conference, where the Allies adopted the doctrine of "unconditional surrender."

### Post-Independence and Modern Challenges
Morocco's independence in 1956 began a new era. Casablanca became a center of anti-colonial activism and later faced social unrest, including major riots in 1965 and 1981. The city is a departure point for significant Jewish emigration to Israel. In the 21st century, it has grappled with terrorism, poverty, and urban inequality, while also pursuing development projects and women's rights reforms, such as the 2004 **mudawana** (family law).

### Economy and Infrastructure
Casablanca is the engine of Morocco's economy, hosting the headquarters of major national and international companies. The **Port of Casablanca** is one of Africa's largest artificial ports. The city is a significant financial center, home to the **Casablanca Stock Exchange**, Africa's third-largest by market capitalization. Its economy is diverse, with major industries including phosphate export, fishing, textiles, and electronics. The city is expanding its transport network with a tramway, bus rapid transit, and a planned regional express rail (RER) ahead of the 2030 FIFA World Cup.

### Demographics and Culture
The population is predominantly Arab and Berber Muslim. Historically, Casablanca was home to a large European Christian community and a major Jewish community, which has since dwindled. The city's culture is vibrant, with a significant music scene (including hip-hop and traditional chaabi), active theater and film industries, and a rich architectural heritage blending Moroccan, Art Deco, and modernist styles. The **Hassan II Mosque** is a major landmark and tourist attraction.

## Terms
- **Medina**: The traditional, walled old quarter of a North African city.
- **Ville nouvelle**: The "new town" built outside the medina during the French colonial period, typically with European-style planning.
- **Mellah**: The historic Jewish quarter in a Moroccan city.
- **Mudawana**: The Moroccan family law code, reformed in 2004 to expand women's rights.
- **Chaouia plain**: The fertile coastal plain where Casablanca is located.
- **Calque**: A word or phrase borrowed from another language by literal, word-for-word translation (e.g., "Casablanca" from "Casa Branca").
- **Zawiya**: An Islamic religious school or lodge, often centered on a Sufi saint.
- **RER (Regional Express Network)**: A planned commuter rail system for the Casablanca metropolitan area.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact etymology of the original name "Anfa" is uncertain, with competing Berber, Hebrew, and Arabic theories.
- The origin of the name "Casablanca" is unclear, with legends involving a white-washed structure or a Sufi saint, but no definitive explanation.
- The human cost of the 1907 French bombardment is debated, with estimates of Moroccan casualties ranging from 600 to as high as 15,000 dead and wounded.
- The death toll from the 1981 Bread Riots is disputed, with the government reporting 66 killed and opposition figures claiming over 600.
