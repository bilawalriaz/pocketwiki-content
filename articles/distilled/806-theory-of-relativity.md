# Theory of relativity

## Overview

The theory of relativity, comprising special relativity (1905) and general relativity (1915), both formulated by Albert Einstein, revolutionized physics by replacing Newton's classical mechanics. It introduced 4-dimensional spacetime, relativity of simultaneity, time dilation, length contraction, and mass-energy equivalence (E=mc²). These theories became foundational to modern cosmology, predicting phenomena such as black holes, neutron stars, and gravitational waves, and enabling practical technologies like GPS.

## Body

### Development and Acceptance

Einstein published special relativity in 1905, building on work by Michelson, Lorentz, Poincaré, and others. Max Planck and Hermann Minkowski contributed subsequent developments. The term "theory of relativity" emerged from Planck's "Relativtheorie" in 1906, with Bucherer first using "Relativitätstheorie." By the 1920s, special relativity was widely accepted and essential to atomic, nuclear, and quantum physics. General relativity, developed between 1907 and 1915, initially seemed less useful—offering only minor corrections to Newtonian gravity and lacking experimental testability. Its complex mathematics limited understanding to a few specialists. Around 1960, new mathematical techniques and astronomical discoveries (quasars, cosmic microwave background, pulsars, black hole candidates) revitalized the field, with observations confirming the theory's predictions.

### Special Relativity

Special relativity describes spacetime structure based on two postulates: (1) the principle of relativity—the laws of physics are identical in all inertial frames; and (2) the constancy of light speed—all observers measure the same speed of light regardless of motion. These postulates contradict classical mechanics but align with experiments. Key consequences include relativity of simultaneity, time dilation (moving clocks run slower), length contraction (objects shorten in motion direction), a universal speed limit (light speed), and mass-energy equivalence (E=mc²). The theory replaces Galilean transformations with Lorentz transformations, fundamentally altering concepts of space and time.

### General Relativity

General relativity is Einstein's theory of gravitation, developed from the equivalence principle: accelerated motion and gravitational fields are physically indistinguishable. This implies free fall is inertial motion, not gravitational force as in Newton's theory. To resolve contradictions with classical mechanics, Einstein proposed curved spacetime, formulated using Riemannian geometry developed with mathematician Marcel Grossmann. The 1915 Einstein field equations relate spacetime curvature to mass, energy, and momentum. Consequences include gravitational time dilation (clocks run slower in stronger fields), orbital precession (observed in Mercury and binary pulsars), light deflection by gravity, frame-dragging by rotating masses, and cosmic expansion. Solutions to the field equations are metric tensors defining spacetime topology and inertial motion.

### Experimental Evidence

Einstein classified relativity as a "principle-theory"—derived from empirical facts rather than speculative models. Special relativity's key validations came from three experiments: the Michelson-Morley experiment (1881, 1887) showing light speed isotropy and null aether wind detection; the Kennedy-Thorndike experiment (1932) confirming light speed constancy across inertial frames; and the Ives-Stilwell experiment (1938, 1941) verifying time dilation via the transverse Doppler effect. General relativity was confirmed through Mercury's orbital precession, solar light deflection, gravitational redshift, equivalence principle tests, and frame-dragging measurements.

### Modern Applications

Relativistic effects are critical for practical engineering. GPS, GLONASS, and Galileo satellite systems must account for both special and general relativistic effects—satellites move at different speeds and experience different gravitational potentials than Earth-bound receivers. High-precision timekeeping, electron microscopes, and particle accelerators also require relativistic corrections to function properly.

## Terms

- **Inertial frame of reference**: A coordinate system where objects not subject to forces move at constant velocity.
- **Lorentz transformations**: Mathematical equations replacing Galilean transformations, relating space and time coordinates between inertial frames.
- **Equivalence principle**: The foundational concept that gravitational effects are locally indistinguishable from acceleration.
- **Metric tensor**: Mathematical object defining spacetime geometry and how distances are measured.
- **Gravitational time dilation**: Phenomenon where clocks run slower in stronger gravitational fields.
- **Relativity of simultaneity**: The principle that simultaneous events in one frame may not be simultaneous in another.
- **Luminiferous aether**: Hypothetical medium once believed necessary for light propagation, disproven by Michelson-Morley.
- **Principle-theory**: Scientific framework starting from empirical regularities rather than underlying mechanisms.

## Debates and Open Questions

The interpretation of the Michelson-Morley null result remained debated—while it showed light speed isotropy, it alone could not definitively disprove aether theory or validate special relativity. The FitzGerald-Lorentz contraction hypothesis was an ad hoc attempt to preserve aether theory without theoretical foundation. Whether the velocity of the solar system affects light speed measurements was considered too coincidental to accept as an explanation for Kennedy-Thorndike results. The distinction between principle-theories and constructive theories remains a methodological consideration in physics.
