# Johann Strauss II

## Overview
Johann Strauss II (1825–1899) was an Austrian composer and violinist who earned the title "The Waltz King" by popularizing the Viennese waltz throughout the 19th century. Overcoming his father Johann Strauss I's opposition and a professional rivalry fueled by the 1848 revolutions, he composed over 500 dance pieces—including "The Blue Danube" and "Tales from the Vienna Woods"—and operettas such as *Die Fledermaus* and *Der Zigeunerbaron*. His music bridged popular entertainment and high art, earning admiration from composers like Wagner, Brahms, and Richard Strauss, and establishing a legacy maintained today by the Vienna New Year's Concert.

## Body

### Early Life and Paternal Opposition
Johann Strauss II was born into a musical dynasty; his father, Johann Strauss I, was a famous composer who actively discouraged his son's musical ambitions, preferring a banking career. The elder Strauss discovered the boy secretly practicing violin with Franz Amon, his orchestra's first violinist, and whipped him severely, ostensibly to spare him the hardships of a musician's life. The family dynamic shifted in 1844 when the father abandoned them for a mistress, Emilie Trampusch, allowing the son to study theory with Professor Joachim Hoffmann and composer Joseph Drechsler. Armed with testimonials from violin teacher Anton Kollmann (ballet répétiteur at the Vienna Court Opera), the 19-year-old secured a license to perform and recruited his first orchestra at the Zur Stadt Belgrad tavern. His paternal great-grandfather was a Hungarian Jew—a fact the Nazis, who lionised Strauss's music as "so German", later tried to conceal.

### Debut, Revolution, and Succession
Johann I's influence blocked his son from major Viennese venues, but Dommayer's Casino in Hietzing risked a contract. The debut in October 1844 drew unanimous critical praise, with *Der Wanderer* hailing the continuation of the Strauss name. The first major appointment for the young composer was the honorary position of "Kapellmeister of the 2nd Vienna Citizen's Regiment", left vacant following Joseph Lanner's death two years before. The 1848 revolutions sharpened the family rivalry: the son supported the revolutionaries, leading to his arrest for playing "La Marseillaise" and the denial of the court post *KK Hofballmusikdirektor* by the monarchy. The father remained loyal, composing the "Radetzky March" for the Habsburg marshal. Upon his father's death from scarlet fever in 1849, Johann II merged the orchestras, toured extensively, and composed patriotic marches for the new Emperor Franz Josef I to repair his standing with the court.

### International Fame and Court Appointment
Strauss toured Austria, Poland, and Germany, eventually surpassing his father's fame. A nervous breakdown in 1853 forced a seven-week hiatus, during which his brother Josef—persuaded to leave engineering—conducted the orchestra. From 1856 to 1865, Strauss held lucrative summer engagements at the Vauxhall Pavilion in Pavlovsk, Russia. He finally attained the coveted *KK Hofballmusikdirektor* position in 1863 after previous rejections due to his political record. In 1862, his youngest brother Eduard joined as conductor, solidifying a family enterprise that dominated Viennese dance music. A 1872 American tour culminated in a "Monster Concert" of over 1,000 performers at Boston's World's Peace Jubilee, where a valet famously clipped the composer's Newfoundland dog to supply "authentic Strauss hair" to fans.

### Operetta and Stage Works
While dance music remained his staple, Strauss achieved lasting success in operetta (light opera with spoken dialogue). His most famous stage works are *Die Fledermaus* (The Bat), *Eine Nacht in Venedig* (A Night in Venice), and *Der Zigeunerbaron* (The Gypsy Baron). Numerous waltzes and polkas derived from operetta themes—such as "Cagliostro-Walzer" and "Rosen aus dem Süden"—became independent hits. He also composed one grand opera, *Ritter Pázmán*, and was working on the ballet *Aschenbrödel* (Cinderella) at his death.

### Marriages and Personal Life
Strauss married three times. His first wife, singer Henrietta Treffz (m. 1862), managed his career until her death in 1878. Six weeks later, he married actress Angelika Dittrich; their incompatibility and her indiscretions led him to seek a divorce. The Roman Catholic Church refused an annulment, prompting Strauss to convert, change nationality, and become a citizen of Saxe-Coburg-Gotha in 1887 to marry his third wife, Adele Deutsch. Adele revitalized his late creativity, yielding the "Kaiser-Walzer," "Kaiser Jubiläum," and the operettas *Der Zigeunerbaron* and *Waldmeister*.

### Rivals, Admirers, and Musical Relationships
Strauss dominated the dance market but faced rivals: Carl Michael Ziehrer and Émile Waldteufel (dominant in Paris) in dance music, and Jacques Offenbach in operetta. Philipp Fahrbach initially blocked his court appointment. Conversely, he was revered by major composers. Richard Wagner admired his "Wein, Weib und Gesang"; Richard Strauss (no relation) cited his "laughing genius of Vienna" while writing *Der Rosenkavalier*. Johannes Brahms was a close friend; Strauss dedicated "Seid umschlungen, Millionen!" to him. Brahms famously inscribed a fan with measures from "The Blue Danube" labeled "Unfortunately, NOT by Johannes Brahms."

### Death, Legacy, and the Fate of the Archives
Strauss died in 1899 of pleuropneumonia, buried in Vienna's Zentralfriedhof. His legacy was institutionalized by Clemens Krauss's 1929 all-Strauss Vienna Philharmonic concert, founding the annual New Year's Concert tradition. The family orchestra ended in 1901 when Eduard disbanded it after a global tour of 840 cities. In a drastic measure to prevent other composers from claiming the family's works—motivated by a pact with Josef and rivalry with Ziehrer—Eduard burned the original orchestral archives in a furnace factory in Vienna's Mariahilf district in 1907. Consequently, many modern performances rely on reconstructed scores. Two Vienna museums preserve his memory: his former Praterstrasse residence and the Strauss Museum.

## Terms
- **Waltz**: A ballroom dance in 3/4 time; the primary genre Strauss elevated to art music.
- **Operetta**: A light theatrical genre with spoken dialogue, songs, and dances; less serious than opera.
- **KK Hofballmusikdirektor**: "Music Director of the Royal Court Balls"; the prestigious imperial appointment Strauss secured in 1863.
- **Kapellmeister**: A conductor or music director; here, an honorary regimental title Strauss held early in his career.
- **Vorgeiger**: The practice of leading an orchestra while playing the violin, a Strauss family tradition.
- **Pavlovsk Seasons**: Annual summer concerts (1856–1865) at the Vauxhall Pavilion near Saint Petersburg, a major income source.
- **Quadrille**: A square dance for four couples, frequently composed by Strauss for court balls.
- **Gradual**: A liturgical chant in the Catholic Mass; Strauss's only sacred work, *Tu qui regis totum orbem* (1844), is one.
- **Saxe-Coburg-Gotha**: An Ernestine duchy in Germany; Strauss became a citizen in 1887 to circumvent Catholic marriage laws.
- **Aschenbrödel**: The ballet *Cinderella*, unfinished at Strauss's death, later completed by Josef Bayer.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authenticity of performing editions**: Because Eduard Strauss destroyed the original orchestral archives in 1907, scholars debate how closely modern editions and the Vienna New Year's Concert repertoire reflect Johann II's original orchestrations and intentions.
- **Motivation for archive destruction**: While the stated reason was a pact with Josef to prevent plagiarism, the source notes this "may also have been fueled by Strauss's rivalry with... Carl Michael Ziehrer," leaving the primary motive open to interpretation.
- **Political opportunism vs. conviction**: The source describes Strauss's 1848 revolutionary stance as "professionally disadvantageous," yet his later patriotic marches for Franz Josef I were "probably to ingratiate himself," raising questions about the sincerity of his political allegiances.
- **Paternity of the "Waltz King" title**: While the source attributes the popularization of the waltz to Johann II, the foundational role of Johann I and Joseph Lanner (whose regimental post Johann II inherited) suggests a more complex lineage for the genre's dominance.
