# Parthian Empire

## Overview
The Parthian Empire (247 BC–224 AD), also known as the Arsacid Empire, was a major Iranian political and cultural power centered in ancient Iran. Founded by Arsaces I of the Parni tribe, it grew into a vast realm controlling the Silk Road trade route between the Roman Empire and Han China. The empire was notable for its decentralized government, its role as Rome's primary eastern rival, and its synthesis of Hellenistic and Iranian cultures, which laid the groundwork for the subsequent Sasanian Empire.

## Timeline
- **c. 247 BC** — Arsaces I leads the Parni tribe to conquer the region of Parthia from the Seleucid satrap Andragoras.
- **c. 171–132 BC** — Mithridates I greatly expands the empire, conquering Media and Mesopotamia from the Seleucids.
- **53 BC** — Parthian forces under Surena decisively defeat the Roman army of Crassus at the Battle of Carrhae.
- **40–39 BC** — Parthian forces under Pacorus I capture the Roman Levant, except Tyre.
- **20 BC** — A diplomatic exchange occurs: Rome returns Parthian princes in exchange for legionary standards lost at Carrhae.
- **63 AD** — After a war, Tiridates I is ceremonially crowned King of Armenia by the Roman emperor Nero in Rome.
- **114–117 AD** — Roman Emperor Trajan invades and temporarily annexes Armenia and Mesopotamia, reaching the Persian Gulf.
- **161–166 AD** — In the Roman–Parthian War, Romans capture and burn the Parthian capitals of Seleucia and Ctesiphon.
- **197–198 AD** — Roman Emperor Septimius Severus again invades Mesopotamia, capturing Seleucia and Ctesiphon.
- **224 AD** — Ardashir I of Persis defeats and kills the last Parthian king, Artabanus IV, establishing the Sasanian Empire.

## Body

### Origins and Expansion
The Arsacid dynasty was founded by Arsaces I, chieftain of the Parni tribe, who conquered the Parthia region in 247 BC. The empire's major expansion occurred under Mithridates I (r. c. 171–132 BC), who seized Media and Mesopotamia from the declining Seleucid Empire. This established the Parthians as a major power, stretching from the Euphrates to the borders of India. The empire's location on the Silk Road made it a vital center for trade between Rome and China, enriching it through taxation on goods like silk, spices, and exotic animals.

### Relations with Rome and Armenia
The Parthians' primary western rival was Rome, with conflict often centering on control of the Kingdom of Armenia. A defining moment was the Battle of Carrhae (53 BC), where Parthian horse archers destroyed a Roman army. Relations fluctuated between war and uneasy peace, with Rome repeatedly invading Mesopotamia and capturing the Parthian capitals of Seleucia and Ctesiphon. The Arsacids maintained firm control over Armenia for much of their history, with the Armenian Arsacid dynasty surviving the fall of the main empire. Frequent internal civil wars between Parthian contenders ultimately weakened the empire more than foreign invasions.

### Government and Society
The Parthian government was highly decentralized compared to the earlier Achaemenid Empire. The "King of Kings" ruled over a network of semi-autonomous vassal kingdoms and provinces. A powerful hereditary nobility, including great families like the House of Suren, held significant influence and could even depose kings. The empire was culturally heterogeneous, blending Greek and Iranian traditions. While Greek was an official language, the native Parthian language and Aramaic were also widely used. Art and architecture, such as the monumental iwan (arched hall), fused Hellenistic and Iranian elements, with a distinctive artistic style characterized by frontal portraiture.

### Religion and Culture
Religion in the Parthian Empire was polytheistic and syncretic, with Greek and Iranian deities often equated (e.g., Zeus with Ahura Mazda). The extent of official patronage for Zoroastrianism is debated, though it gained prominence later. The empire saw the rise of Manichaeism, a syncretic faith blending Zoroastrian, Christian, and Buddhist elements. Parthian culture was transmitted orally through court minstrels (gōsān), with epic tales later written down in the Sasanian period. Royal women, while polygamous kings had multiple queens, could own property and accompanied armies, though they were rarely depicted in art.

## Terms
*   **Arsacid Empire:** Another name for the Parthian Empire, derived from its founding dynasty, the Arsacids.
*   **Cataphract:** A heavily armored cavalryman, both rider and horse clad in mail, who served as the elite striking force of the Parthian army.
*   **Hellenistic:** Referring to the Greek-influenced culture that spread across the Near East after the conquests of Alexander the Great.
*   **Iwan:** A signature Parthian architectural form: a large, barrel-vaulted audience hall open on one side.
*   **King of Kings:** The title adopted by the Arsacid monarchs, claiming the legacy of the Achaemenid Persian Empire.
*   **Parthian shot:** A cavalry tactic where horse archers feign retreat, then turn to shoot at pursuing enemies.
*   **Satrapy:** A province of an ancient Persian empire, governed by a satrap.
*   **Silk Road:** The ancient network of trade routes connecting China and the Mediterranean, over which the Parthians controlled key segments.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact ethnic origins of the founder, Arsaces I, are debated; some scholars argue he was a native Parthian rather than an invading Parni.
*   The precise year of the empire's founding (247 BC) is uncertain, with theories linking it to the loss of Seleucid control, Arsaces becoming Parni chief, or his conquest of Parthia.
*   The extent of official Arsacid patronage of Zoroastrianism is unclear, with evidence for and against it being the state religion before the Sasanians.
*   The reliability of Greco-Roman historical sources is questioned, as they were written by rivals and often ignore social and cultural aspects of Parthian life.
