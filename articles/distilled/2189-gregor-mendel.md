# Gregor Mendel

## Overview
Gregor Mendel (1822–1884) was an Austrian friar and scientist whose meticulous experiments with pea plants in the 1850s and 1860s established the fundamental laws of heredity. Though his work was ignored during his lifetime, its rediscovery in 1900 laid the foundation for modern genetics, explaining how traits are passed from parents to offspring through discrete units we now call genes.

## Timeline
- **20 July 1822** — Born in Heinzendorf bei Odrau, Silesia, Austrian Empire.
- **1843** — Joins the Augustinian Order of Saint Augustine, taking the name Gregor.
- **1851–1853** — Studies at the University of Vienna.
- **1856–1863** — Conducts pea plant hybridization experiments in the monastery garden.
- **1865** — Presents his findings to the Natural History Society of Brno.
- **1866** — Publishes *Versuche über Pflanzenhybriden* ("Experiments on Plant Hybridization").
- **1868** — Becomes abbot of St. Thomas' Abbey, ending his scientific work.
- **6 January 1884** — Dies in Brno from chronic nephritis.
- **1900** — Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns, and Erich von Tschermak independently rediscover and verify Mendel's laws.
- **2021** — Mendel's body is exhumed; his genome is sequenced.
- **2025** — The last three of the seven genes Mendel studied are identified in the pea genome.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Mendel was born into a German-speaking farming family. He entered the Augustinian monastery in Brno partly to secure an education without financial burden. He trained as a priest and later worked as a substitute high school teacher, but failed the final exams for certification twice. In 1851, he was sent to the University of Vienna for formal studies in physics and natural history, where he was influenced by professors like Christian Doppler. He returned to his abbey in 1853 as a physics teacher.

### The Pea Plant Experiments
Between 1856 and 1863, Mendel cultivated and tested approximately 28,000 pea plants in the monastery's garden. He focused on seven distinct traits (e.g., seed shape, flower color, plant height) that were inherited independently. By cross-breeding true-breeding varieties and meticulously tracking traits through generations, he discovered predictable patterns. For example, crossing true-breeding yellow and green pea plants produced only yellow-seeded offspring in the first generation, but green seeds reappeared in a 1:3 ratio in the next generation. This led him to formulate the concepts of "dominant" and "recessive" traits and to propose the existence of invisible "factors" (now called genes) that determine inheritance. His work established the Law of Segregation and the Law of Independent Assortment.

### Initial Reception and Rediscovery
Mendel presented his results in 1865 and published them in 1866, but the scientific community largely ignored them, viewing the work as a study of hybridization rather than fundamental inheritance. The significance of his discoveries was not recognized until 1900, when three botanists—Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns, and Erich von Tschermak—independently verified his findings. This rediscovery sparked the modern age of genetics, though it initially faced opposition from the biometric school of thought, which favored statistical analysis of continuous variation. The debate was eventually resolved, and Mendelian genetics was integrated with Darwinian evolution in the modern synthesis of the 1930s and 1940s.

### Later Life and Legacy
After becoming abbot in 1868, Mendel's administrative duties and a dispute with civil authorities over taxation ended his scientific work. He died in 1884. His papers were burned by his successor. The genes underlying the seven pea traits he studied were finally identified between 2004 and 2025. In 2021, his body was exhumed, and his genome was sequenced, revealing a predisposition to heart problems.

## Terms
*   **True-breeding:** A plant that, when self-pollinated, always produces offspring with the same trait.
*   **Dominant:** A trait that is expressed in an organism even if only one copy of the gene for it is present.
*   **Recessive:** A trait that is expressed only when two copies of the gene for it are present.
*   **F1 generation:** The first filial generation, the immediate offspring of a cross between two true-breeding parents.
*   **F2 generation:** The second filial generation, produced by self-pollinating or crossing F1 individuals.
*   **Law of Segregation:** Mendel's first law, stating that the two alleles (gene variants) for a trait separate during gamete formation.
*   **Law of Independent Assortment:** Mendel's second law, stating that alleles for different traits are distributed to gametes independently of one another.
*   **Genotype:** The genetic makeup of an organism.
*   **Phenotype:** The observable physical characteristics of an organism, determined by its genotype and environment.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **The Mendelian Paradox:** In 1936, statistician Ronald Fisher analyzed Mendel's data and concluded the results were statistically too close to the expected ratios, suggesting possible falsification or unconscious bias. This created a paradox, as Mendel's character made deliberate fraud unlikely. Subsequent analyses by scholars like Hartl and Fairbanks have largely rejected Fisher's statistical argument, suggesting his interpretation was flawed.
*   **Reason for Initial Neglect:** Scholars debate why Mendel's work was ignored for over 35 years. Factors include its publication in an obscure journal, its mathematical approach being foreign to biologists of the time, and the prevailing theory of "blending inheritance" which Mendel's discrete model contradicted.
*   **Lysenkoism:** In the Soviet Union and China, Mendelian genetics was officially rejected in favor of Lamarckism (the inheritance of acquired characteristics) under the policy of Lysenkoism, leading to the persecution of geneticists and agricultural failures.
