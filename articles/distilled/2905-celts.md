# Celts

## Overview
The Celts were a diverse collection of Indo-European peoples in Europe and Anatolia, defined by their use of Celtic languages and shared cultural traits. Their history spans from Bronze Age linguistic origins, through continental spread and conflict with Rome, to cultural survival in Atlantic regions. Studying the Celts is central to understanding pre-Roman Western Europe and modern Celtic nations.

## Timeline
- **c. 1200 BC** — Urnfield culture flourishes in central Europe, a proposed origin for proto-Celtic.
- **c. 800–500 BC** — Hallstatt culture develops, associated with early Celts.
- **c. 450 BC** — La Tène culture begins, the classic archaeological expression of Celtic art.
- **517 BC** — First recorded use of the name 'Celts' (Keltoi) by Hecataeus of Miletus.
- **6th century BC** — Earliest undisputed Celtic inscriptions (Lepontic) appear in northern Italy.
- **390 BC** — Celts sack Rome after the Battle of the Allia.
- **3rd century BC** — Celtic settlement reaches central Anatolia (Galatia).
- **58–51 BC** — Julius Caesar's Gallic Wars conquer most of Gaul.
- **1st century AD** — Most Celtic territories become part of the Roman Empire.
- **c. 500 AD** — Celtic culture becomes largely restricted to Ireland, Britain, and Brittany.
- **8th century AD** — Celtic literary tradition begins with Old Irish texts.
- **1707** — Edward Lhuyd first uses the modern English word 'Celt' in a scholarly context.

## Body

### Origins and Theories
Celtic origins are debated. The traditional "Celtic from the East" theory posits proto-Celtic arose in the late Bronze Age Urnfield culture (c. 1200 BC), spreading with Hallstatt and La Tène cultures. This is challenged by "Celtic from the West," suggesting an earlier Atlantic coastal origin spreading east, and "Celtic from the Centre," proposing emergence in Bronze Age Gaul. Linguistic evidence shows the earliest inscriptions are Lepontic (6th century BC) in Italy and Celtiberian (2nd century BC) in Iberia, not the Hallstatt/La Tène heartland.

### Distribution and Society
Celts were widespread. In Gaul (modern France), they were conquered by Rome in the 1st century BC. In Iberia, groups included Celtiberians, Gallaeci, and Celtici. In central Europe, they were displaced by Germanic tribes. They also expanded into Southeast Europe and Anatolia. Insular Celts in Britain and Ireland included Britons, Gaels, and Picts. Society was tribal, often led by kings, and divided into a warrior aristocracy, an intellectual class (including druids), and the general populace. They engaged in extensive trade and used complex coinage.

### Conflict with Rome and Romanisation
Celts frequently clashed with Rome, including the sack of Rome in 390 BC and the Gallic Wars. By the 1st century AD, most continental Celtic lands were Roman provinces. This led to Romanisation, where native elites adopted Roman language, customs, and government, eventually causing the loss of Celtic languages on the continent. In contrast, Insular Celts in Ireland and northern Britain remained outside the empire, preserving their languages and culture.

### Religion and Art
Ancient Celts practiced polytheism overseen by druids, who served as priests, judges, and lore-keepers. They venerated deities associated with natural features and made votive offerings in water and wetlands. After Romanisation, Romano-Celtic syncretic deities emerged. Celtic art, particularly the La Tène style, features intricate geometric decoration, spirals, and stylized motifs. Later Insular art, like the Book of Kells, blended Celtic, Roman, and Germanic elements.

### Legacy and Modern Identity
Following the Roman period, Celtic culture survived in Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Cornwall, Brittany, and the Isle of Man. These regions developed distinct Gaelic and Brittonic languages. A modern Celtic identity was constructed during the 19th-century Celtic Revival. Today, several Celtic languages are spoken or revived, and "Celtic" refers to the languages and cultures of these six modern "Celtic nations."

## Terms
- **Proto-Celtic**: Reconstructed ancestor of all Celtic languages, dated to the Late Bronze Age.
- **Hallstatt culture**: Early Iron Age culture (c. 800–500 BC) in central Europe, associated with early Celts.
- **La Tène culture**: Late Iron Age culture (from c. 450 BC) known for its distinctive art, the classic archaeological expression of the Celts.
- **Druid**: Priestly and intellectual class in ancient Celtic societies, responsible for religion, education, and law.
- **Gauls**: Celtic peoples inhabiting modern France, Belgium, and parts of Switzerland and northern Italy.
- **Insular Celts**: Celtic-speaking peoples of the British and Irish islands and their descendants.
- **Continental Celts**: Celtic-speaking peoples of mainland Europe before Romanisation.
- **Ogham**: Early Medieval alphabet used for inscriptions in Primitive Irish and other Celtic languages.
- **Torc**: Large neck ring, often gold, worn as a prestigious ornament in Celtic cultures.
- **Oppidum**: Large, fortified Iron Age settlement, particularly associated with Celts of Gaul.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origins**: Primary debate between "Celtic from the East" (Urnfield/Hallstatt) and newer "Celtic from the West" or "Celtic from the Centre" models.
- **Definition**: Interrelationships of ethnicity, language, and culture are unclear; "Celt" is primarily a linguistic label.
- **Insular Celtic Identity**: Debate over whether Iron Age people of Britain and Ireland should be called "Celts," as classical writers often did not use the term.
- **Tartessian Language**: Hypothesis that Tartessian was Celtic is widely rejected; it is often regarded as unclassified.
- **Genetic Continuity**: Genetic studies show links between modern Celtic-speaking populations and Iron Age groups, but also indicate primary genetic makeup was established by Bronze Age migrations, complicating a simple genetic definition.
