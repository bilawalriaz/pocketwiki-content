# Rwanda

## Overview
Rwanda is a densely populated, landlocked country in East Africa with a complex history of monarchy, colonialism, and genocide. Its modern identity is defined by the aftermath of the 1994 genocide and subsequent reconstruction under a centralized government, which has driven rapid economic growth and development while maintaining tight political control.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Early Iron Age settlers and ancestors of the Twa inhabit the region.
- **15th century** — King Gihanga establishes the Kingdom of Rwanda.
- **Mid-18th century** — The Kingdom of Rwanda, under the Tutsi Nyiginya clan, becomes dominant.
- **1897** — Germany establishes a colonial presence, ruling through the existing monarchy.
- **1916** — Belgium invades and takes control during World War I.
- **1935** — Belgium introduces identity cards classifying people as Hutu, Tutsi, or Twa.
- **1959** — The Hutu Revolution leads to violence and the overthrow of the monarchy.
- **1962** — Rwanda gains independence as a Hutu-dominated republic.
- **1973** — Juvénal Habyarimana seizes power in a military coup.
- **1990** — The Tutsi-led Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF) invades from Uganda, starting a civil war.
- **April 6, 1994** — President Habyarimana is assassinated, triggering the Rwandan genocide.
- **July 1994** — The RPF ends the genocide with a military victory.
- **2000** — Paul Kagame becomes President of Rwanda.

## Body

### Early History and Kingdom
The territory was first settled by hunter-gatherers, ancestors of the Twa. Later migrations brought farmers and herders, including Bantu groups. Social organization evolved from clans into kingdoms. By the mid-18th century, the Kingdom of Rwanda, ruled by the Tutsi Nyiginya clan, became dominant. Under King Kigeli Rwabugiri in the 19th century, the kingdom expanded and implemented administrative systems like *ubuhake* (cattle clientship) and *uburetwa* (forced labor), which deepened the divide between the Hutu and Tutsi populations.

### Colonial Era and Independence
Germany colonized Rwanda in 1897, ruling indirectly through the monarchy. Belgium took control in 1916 and later administered it as a League of Nations mandate. The Belgians solidified ethnic divisions by introducing identity cards in 1935 and initially favored the Tutsi elite. In 1959, a Hutu uprising led to widespread violence and the abolition of the monarchy. Rwanda gained independence in 1962 as a republic dominated by the Hutu majority, leading to cycles of violence and Tutsi exile.

### Genocide and Civil War
In 1990, the RPF, a Tutsi-led rebel group, invaded from Uganda, sparking a civil war. A 1993 peace accord was fragile. On April 6, 1994, the assassination of President Habyarimana triggered the Rwandan genocide. Over approximately 100 days, Hutu extremists killed between 500,000 and 1,000,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu. The RPF resumed its offensive and took control of the country by mid-July 1994, ending the genocide. The RPF government has since pursued national reconciliation and economic development.

### Politics and Government
Rwanda is a de facto one-party state ruled by the RPF. President Paul Kagame has been in power since 2000. The constitution mandates a multi-party system but prohibits politics based on ethnicity. The government is highly centralized and authoritarian, with low corruption but significant restrictions on civil liberties and political opposition. Rwanda's legal system blends civil law with traditional *Gacaca* courts, which were used to process genocide cases.

### Economy and Development
Rwanda's economy was devastated by the 1994 genocide but has since experienced strong growth, driven by services, agriculture, and tourism. Coffee and tea are major exports. Tourism, especially mountain gorilla tracking, is a leading foreign exchange earner. Despite progress, poverty remains significant, with 30.5% of the population affected by multidimensional poverty as of 2024. The country has achieved near-universal financial inclusion through mobile money.

### Demographics and Society
Rwanda is one of Africa's most densely populated countries. The population is young and predominantly rural. The people are collectively known as the Banyarwanda, comprising three subgroups: Hutu (84%), Tutsi (15%), and Twa (1%). The government now discourages ethnic distinctions. Kinyarwanda is the national language, with English, French, and Swahili as official languages. Christianity is the largest religion.

## Terms
- **Banyarwanda**: The collective term for the people of Rwanda, sharing a single language and cultural heritage.
- **Hutu, Tutsi, Twa**: The three traditional subgroups within the Banyarwanda people. Their origins are debated, but they were historically distinguished by social class and occupation.
- **Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF)**: The Tutsi-led rebel group that ended the 1994 genocide and has ruled Rwanda since.
- **Gacaca**: A traditional community-based justice system revived to process the massive backlog of genocide cases.
- **Ubuhake**: A pre-colonial system of cattle clientship where Tutsi patrons granted privileged status to clients in exchange for service.
- **Umuganda**: A monthly national day of mandatory community service.
- **Mutuelles de santé**: A community-based health insurance scheme covering over 90% of the population.
- **Kinyarwanda**: The principal national language spoken by nearly all Rwandans.

## Debates and open questions
- The origins and historical nature of the Hutu and Tutsi distinction are debated. Some scholars argue they were separate racial groups, while others contend the categories were fluid social classes that were rigidified by colonial policies.
- The effectiveness and fairness of the *Gacaca* courts used after the genocide are debated, with criticism focusing on whether they met international legal standards.
- While Rwanda's government is praised for low corruption and economic development, it is criticized by human rights groups for extensive political repression, including arbitrary detention and restrictions on freedoms.
