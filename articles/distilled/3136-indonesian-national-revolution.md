# Indonesian National Revolution

## Overview
The Indonesian National Revolution (1945–1949) was a four-year armed conflict and diplomatic struggle that ended Dutch colonial rule and established the independent Republic of Indonesia. It was a pivotal decolonization conflict that combined guerrilla warfare, internal social upheaval, and international diplomacy, ultimately reshaping the political landscape of Southeast Asia.

## Timeline
- **17 Aug 1945** — Sukarno and Hatta proclaim Indonesian independence.
- **Oct–Nov 1945** — Battle of Surabaya becomes a national symbol of resistance.
- **Nov 1946** — Linggadjati Agreement: Netherlands recognizes Republican authority over Java and Sumatra.
- **Jul 1947** — Dutch launch "Operation Product," a major military offensive.
- **Jan 1948** — Renville Agreement establishes a ceasefire line.
- **Sep 1948** — Madiun Affair: Communist-led uprising is crushed by Republican forces.
- **Dec 1948** — Dutch launch "Operation Kraai," capturing Yogyakarta and the Republican government.
- **Mar 1949** — Republican "General Offensive" recaptures Yogyakarta briefly.
- **Dec 1949** — Netherlands transfers sovereignty to the Republic of the United States of Indonesia.

## Body

### Background and Proclamation
Indonesian nationalism grew in the early 20th century through groups like Budi Utomo and the Indonesian National Party. The Japanese occupation (1942–1945) was a crucial catalyst, as it destroyed Dutch infrastructure and elevated nationalist leaders like Sukarno. Following Japan's surrender on 15 August 1945, youth groups (*pemuda*) pressured Sukarno and Hatta to declare independence on 17 August 1945. A power vacuum ensued, leading to the *Bersiap* period (1945–46), marked by revolutionary fervor and violence against perceived enemies, with death toll estimates ranging from 3,500 to 30,000.

### Allied Intervention and Early Conflict
The weakened Netherlands could not immediately reassert control. Allied forces, primarily British, were tasked with restoring order and repatriating Japanese troops. Clashes erupted as Republicans resisted the return of Dutch authority. The Battle of Surabaya (October–November 1945) was the bloodiest single engagement; despite a Republican military defeat, it galvanized national resistance and international sympathy. The British withdrew by November 1946, leaving 55,000 Dutch troops to land in Java.

### Diplomacy and Military Stalemate
Dutch forces regained major cities but could not control the countryside. International diplomacy became central. The Linggadjati Agreement (1946) was followed by the Dutch "Operation Product" (1947) and the UN-brokered Renville Agreement (1948). The Republic faced severe economic hardship and political crises, including the communist Madiun Affair (1948) and the Islamist Darul Islam rebellion. In December 1948, the Dutch launched "Operation Kraai," capturing the Republican capital Yogyakarta and its leadership. However, they failed to defeat guerrilla resistance, and the offensive triggered strong international condemnation, notably from the United States, which threatened to cut off Marshall Plan aid.

### Transfer of Sovereignty
Under intense international pressure, the Dutch agreed to negotiations. The Dutch–Indonesian Round Table Conference (1949) resulted in the Netherlands transferring sovereignty over the Dutch East Indies (except Dutch New Guinea) to the federal Republic of the United States of Indonesia (RUSI) on 27 December 1949. The federal structure was short-lived; by August 1950, it was dissolved into the unitary Republic of Indonesia.

### Internal Turmoil and Impact
The revolution involved significant internal social upheaval. "Social revolutions" challenged traditional aristocracies, sometimes violently. The conflict caused immense suffering, with Indonesian deaths estimated between 97,421 and 100,000, and over 4,000 Dutch soldiers killed. Seven million people were displaced on Java and Sumatra. Economically, the revolution caused shortages, inflation, and left the new republic with a substantial colonial debt. Politically, it established the Indonesian army's central role and set the stage for future political trends.

## Terms
*   ***Pemuda***: Indonesian for "male youth"; radical, politicized groups who drove the initial revolutionary struggle.
*   ***Bersiap***: The chaotic period (1945–46) after the Japanese surrender, marked by revolutionary violence and the seizure of power by Indonesian nationalists.
*   ***PETA*** (*Pembela Tanah Air*): The Japanese-formed Indonesian volunteer army; its disbanded members formed the core of the early Republican military.
*   ***Van Mook Line***: An artificial demarcation line drawn by the Dutch in 1948, which became the de facto ceasefire line after the Renville Agreement.
*   ***Raja***: Traditional local rulers or aristocrats, many of whom had collaborated with the Dutch and faced challenges during the revolution.
*   **Round Table Conference**: The 1949 diplomatic conference in The Hague that finalized the transfer of sovereignty from the Netherlands to Indonesia.
*   ***Darul Islam***: An Islamist insurgency movement that declared an independent Islamic state in 1948, opposing the secular Republican government.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Casualty Figures**: Precise Indonesian death tolls are uncertain, with estimates for the *Bersiap* period alone ranging from 3,500 to 30,000.
*   **Dutch Responsibility**: A 2022 Dutch historical review concluded that the Netherlands used "systematic and excessive violence" that was "deliberate" and "condoned at every level," a finding that led to formal apologies.
*   **Role of External Powers**: The decisive impact of U.S. diplomatic and economic pressure on the Dutch decision to withdraw is a key point of analysis.
*   **Legacy of Violence**: The revolution entrenched a culture of political violence and established the military's influential role in Indonesian politics, the effects of which persist.
