# Movie theater

## Overview
A movie theater (or cinema) is a commercial venue with auditoriums designed for the public exhibition of films. It is a significant cultural and entertainment institution, evolving from early precursors to become a global industry with complex technology, design, and business models. The theater experience is defined by projected images on a large screen with synchronized sound, typically accompanied by concessions.

## Timeline
- **1892-1900** — Émile Reynaud gives public screenings of animated movies at the Musée Grévin in Paris.
- **1894** — First public Kinetoscope parlor opens in New York City, a commercial motion picture house.
- **1895** — First commercial public screening of films using the Cinématographe at the Salon Indien du Grand Café in Paris.
- **1895** — First building constructed for dedicated motion picture demonstration opens in Atlanta, Georgia.
- **c. 1905-1915** — Nickelodeons, small storefront theaters charging five cents, flourish in the United States.
- **1957** — The Elgin Theatre in Ottawa becomes the first venue to offer two films on different screens, pioneering the multiplex concept.
- **1963** — Stanley Durwood of American Multi-Cinema pioneers the modern multiplex in the U.S.
- **1988** — The Kinepolis in Brussels, Belgium, opens as the first megaplex with 25 screens.
- **2009** — D-Box Technologies releases its motion seat system to theaters.
- **2010s** — Digital cinema projection becomes the majority standard, replacing physical film prints.

## Body

### Terminology and Design
The venue is called a "movie theater" in North America and a "cinema" in other Anglophone countries. The terms derive from Greek and French roots related to movement and viewing. Traditional design features a single auditorium with padded seats on a sloped floor, a foyer with a box office, and a concession stand. Modern multiplexes, a concept developed in Canada in the 1950s, contain multiple auditoriums sharing a single foyer. The largest complexes, called megaplexes, can have up to thirty screens. "Stadium seating," with sharply raked rows for clear sight lines, dates to the 1920s and is now common.

### History and Evolution
Early film screenings occurred in existing vaudeville theaters and other darkened venues. Dedicated movie houses emerged in the late 1890s and early 1900s, followed by the nickelodeon boom. The multiplex model, which allowed more efficient operation, became the norm from the 1960s onward, leading to the decline of single-screen theaters. Drive-in theaters, an outdoor format popular in the 1950s and 1960s, have largely been replaced, with about 300 remaining in the U.S. Alternative venues have included video salons in the 1980s and various outdoor or mobile cinemas.

### Technology and Formats
Projection technology has evolved from early systems to digital cinema, which uses digital files instead of heavy film reels. Sound systems now commonly include subwoofers for low-pitched sounds. Specialized presentation formats include 3D film, which uses polarized glasses to create depth perception and saw waves of popularity in the 1950s and 2010s. IMAX uses oversized screens and 70mm film or dual digital projectors for a larger, higher-resolution image. Premium Large Formats (PLF) are auditoriums with high-end amenities like large screens, laser projection, and immersive sound, often branded by exhibitors. Some theaters also offer motion-controlled seating with haptic effects.

### Programming and Presentation
Theaters are classified by their programming: first-run theaters show new mainstream releases; second-run or discount theaters show older films at lower prices; and repertory or arthouse theaters show alternative, classic, or independent films. A typical modern presentation includes commercial advertisements, movie trailers, and then the feature film. In the early decades of sound film, programs often included newsreels and short films. Some countries have a tradition of intermissions for long films.

### Business and Economics
Revenue comes from ticket sales and concessions, with concessions like popcorn and soda being highly profitable. Ticket pricing often uses economic price discrimination, with lower prices for matinees, children, students, and seniors. The industry faces competition from home entertainment options like streaming services and high-quality home theater systems, which have contributed to a long-term decline in ticket sales since the 1950s. Box office revenue is heavily "front-loaded," with films earning most of their receipts in the opening weeks.

### Controversies and Accessibility
Common controversies include the volume of trailers and films, the cost of concessions, and pre-film advertising. Copyright piracy via camcording is a major concern, with theaters employing various countermeasures. Accessibility features include hearing-impaired audio tracks, audio description for the visually impaired, and, in some regions, sign language video tracks.

## Terms
- **Multiplex**: A movie theater complex containing multiple auditoriums, typically sharing a single foyer and box office.
- **Megaplex**: A very large multiplex, generally with 20 or more screens.
- **Nickelodeon**: An early, small movie theater, common in the U.S. from about 1905 to 1915, that charged five cents for admission.
- **Drive-in**: An outdoor movie theater where patrons watch from their cars.
- **IMAX**: A proprietary system using oversized screens and specialized projectors for a larger, higher-resolution image.
- **Premium Large Format (PLF)**: A non-proprietary term for auditoriums with high-end amenities like large screens, laser projection, and immersive sound.
- **First-run theater**: A theater that shows new mainstream films during their initial release.
- **Second-run theater**: A theater that shows films after their initial run, often at a lower ticket price.
- **Repertory/Arthouse theater**: A theater that shows alternative, classic, or independent films.
- **Digital Cinema Package (DCP)**: The digital equivalent of a film print, containing the movie's video, audio, and subtitle files.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The uniform pricing of movie tickets, despite varying production costs and demand, is noted as an economics puzzle, often attributed to the unpredictability of a film's success.
- The impact of simultaneous theatrical and streaming releases on the traditional movie-going experience and theater revenue is a ongoing concern.
- The rise of home theater systems with large screens and high-quality sound presents a persistent competitive challenge to the theatrical experience.
