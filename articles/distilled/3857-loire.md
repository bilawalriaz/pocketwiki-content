# Loire

## Overview
The Loire is the longest river in France, stretching 1,006 km (625 mi) from the Massif Central to the Atlantic Ocean. It drains over a fifth of France's land and is historically vital for trade, agriculture, and culture. Its valley, known as the "Garden of France," is famed for its châteaux and vineyards, and a central section is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Geography and Hydrology
The Loire rises in the Cévennes range of the Massif Central at about 1,408 m (4,619 ft). It flows north to Orléans, then west to the Atlantic at Saint-Nazaire. Its drainage basin covers 117,054 km² (45,195 sq mi). The river is divided into three zones: the Upper Loire (source to Allier confluence), the middle Loire Valley (Allier to Maine confluence), and the Lower Loire (Maine to estuary). Its average discharge is 863 m³/s (30,500 cu ft/s), but flow varies greatly seasonally, from as low as 10 m³/s (350 cu ft/s) in late summer to over 8,000 m³/s (280,000 cu ft/s) during floods. Major floods occurred in 1856, 1866, and 1911. The river has few dams, with the Villerest Dam (1985) being key for flood control.

### Ecology and Conservation
The Loire hosts high biodiversity, including over 100 phytoplankton species and nearly all of France's freshwater fish species (about 57). Migratory fish like Atlantic salmon, sea trout, and shads are common. The great Loire salmon population declined from ~100,000 in the 19th century to below 100 by the 1990s, leading to a fishing ban in 1984 and restoration efforts that increased numbers to ~500 by 2005. The estuary is critical for migratory birds. Conservation efforts, led by groups like WWF and the Loire Vivante network, successfully opposed major dam projects in the 1980s-90s. The Natural Loire River Plan (1994) led to the decommissioning of three dams to restore ecosystems.

### Human History and Navigation
Human habitation dates to the Middle Palaeolithic. The Gauls used the Loire as a trade route by 600 BC. After Roman conquest in 56 BC, cities like Aurelianis (Orléans) developed. Christianity spread from the 3rd century. Vikings raided in the 9th century. During the Hundred Years' War (1337–1453), the Loire was a strategic border. The Renaissance brought architectural flourishing. Historically, the river was a major transport artery; by 1700, Nantes was France's leading inland port. Navigation declined with railways from the 1850s. Today, navigable sections include the maritime Loire (Saint-Nazaire to Nantes) and canals like the Canal latéral à la Loire.

### The Loire Valley and Châteaux
The Loire Valley, a 280 km (170 mi) stretch, is famed for its cultural landscape. It contains over a thousand châteaux, evolving from medieval fortresses to Renaissance palaces. Architectural styles range from the 10th-century donjons to Baroque and Neoclassical designs. Notable examples include Chambord, Amboise, and Chenonceau. The valley is also a premier wine region, with appellations like Sancerre, Vouvray, and Muscadet. Winemaking dates to the 1st century AD. The river moderates the climate, aiding viticulture. The central valley was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2000 for its cultural landscape.

## Terms
- **Massif Central**: A highland region in south-central France, the source of the Loire.
- **Alluvium**: Soil, sand, or gravel deposited by flowing water.
- **Tributary**: A river that flows into a larger river.
- **Estuary**: The tidal mouth of a large river, where the sea meets freshwater.
- **Gabare**: A shallow-draught flat-bottomed boat used on French rivers.
- **Château**: A large French country house or castle, especially in the Loire Valley.
- **AOC (Appellation d'origine contrôlée)**: A French certification for agricultural products, including wine, guaranteeing origin and production methods.
- **Natura 2000**: A network of nature protection areas under the European Union's Habitats Directive.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The primary debate covered is the conflict between development (dam construction) and conservation in the late 20th century, which resulted in a shift toward ecosystem restoration.
- The source text notes the Loire is "constantly under threat of losing its status as the last wild river in France" due to its length and navigability, implying ongoing tension between use and preservation.
