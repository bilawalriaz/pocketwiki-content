# Hideki Tojo

## Overview
Hideki Tojo was a Japanese military officer and politician who served as Prime Minister of Japan from 1941 to 1944 during World War II. His leadership was defined by aggressive expansionism, the decision to attack Pearl Harbor, and the subsequent war against the Allied powers. He was later convicted of war crimes and executed, becoming a symbol of Japan's wartime militarism.

- **23 December 1948** — Executed by hanging after being convicted of war crimes.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Career
Tojo was born in 1884 to a military family of samurai descent. He pursued a military career, graduating from the Imperial Japanese Army Academy in 1905. His early experiences, including the unpopular Treaty of Portsmouth (1905) and a visit to the United States, fostered a lasting dislike of Americans. He rose through the ranks, becoming a key member of the Tōseiha ("Control Faction") within the Army, which advocated for a totalitarian "national defense state" and viewed the United States as a future enemy. He gained prominence in the 1930s, serving as chief of staff of the Kwantung Army in Manchuria and overseeing operations during the 1937 invasion of China.

### Rise to Power and Decision for War
In 1940, Tojo was appointed Army Minister. He was a strong advocate for the Tripartite Pact with Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy. As diplomatic tensions with the United States escalated over Japan's expansion in China and French Indochina, Tojo emerged as a hawkish voice in the cabinet, arguing that compromise would be seen as weakness. When Prime Minister Konoe resigned in October 1941, Tojo was chosen as his successor, partly due to his perceived loyalty to Emperor Hirohito. Despite being ordered to re-examine policy, Tojo's government ultimately decided on war. He misrepresented the U.S. "Hull note" as an ultimatum to justify the decision. On 7 December 1941, Japan attacked Pearl Harbor.

### Premiership and World War II
As Prime Minister, Tojo held multiple portfolios and presided over a government that mobilized the nation for "total war." His foreign policy aimed to establish the "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere." While initially successful, the tide turned after the Battle of Midway in June 1942. Tojo's authority was based on the Emperor's support, but he had to negotiate with the Navy, which often worked at cross-purposes. His government was responsible for numerous war crimes, including the massacre and starvation of prisoners of war and civilians. Military setbacks, including the failed invasion of India (1944) and the loss of Saipan (1944), eroded his support among the elite. Following the disaster at Saipan, Emperor Hirohito withdrew his support, forcing Tojo to resign in July 1944.

### Arrest, Trial, and Execution
After Japan's surrender in 1945, Tojo was arrested for war crimes. He attempted suicide but survived. At the International Military Tribunal for the Far East (the Tokyo Trials), he was found guilty of waging wars of aggression and permitting inhumane treatment of prisoners. He accepted full responsibility for the war. Historians note that U.S. General Douglas MacArthur's staff worked to shift ultimate responsibility for the war onto Tojo to protect Emperor Hirohito from prosecution. Tojo was executed by hanging on 23 December 1948.

## Terms
- **Tōseiha ("Control Faction"):** A militaristic faction within the Imperial Japanese Army that favored working within the system to create a totalitarian state for total war, as opposed to the more radical Kōdōha.
- **Kempeitai:** The military police of the Imperial Japanese Army, used for internal security and suppressing dissent.
- **Kokutai:** A Japanese political concept referring to the nation's polity or identity, centered on the Emperor as a divine, unbroken lineage.
- **Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere:** A concept used to justify Japanese expansionism in Asia and the Pacific, ostensibly as a pan-Asian alliance free from Western influence.
- **Imperial General Headquarters:** The supreme command organization for the Japanese military during WWII, consisting of Army and Navy leaders under the nominal authority of the Emperor.
- **Jushin:** The "elder statesmen," a group of senior advisors to the Emperor who traditionally had influence over major appointments and policies.
- **International Military Tribunal for the Far East (Tokyo Trials):** The post-WWII trial held in Tokyo to prosecute Japanese leaders for war crimes, similar to the Nuremberg Trials in Germany.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Tojo's Role vs. the Emperor's:** Historians debate the extent of Emperor Hirohito's responsibility for the war. Some, like Herbert Bix, argue that U.S. authorities, particularly General MacArthur, deliberately exonerated the Emperor by shifting ultimate blame onto Tojo during the Tokyo Trials.
- **The "Hull Note" as an Ultimatum:** Tojo's government characterized the U.S. diplomatic note of November 1941 as an ultimatum to justify war. Historical analysis shows it was a tentative proposal without a deadline, making this characterization a key point of contention regarding the justification for the attack on Pearl Harbor.
- **Tojo's Authority:** While often portrayed as a dictator, Tojo's power was contingent on the Emperor's support and required negotiation with other military branches, especially the Navy, which often opposed his decisions.
