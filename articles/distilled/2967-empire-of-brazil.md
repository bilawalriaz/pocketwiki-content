# Empire of Brazil

## Overview
The Empire of Brazil was a 19th-century constitutional monarchy that encompassed the territories of modern Brazil and Uruguay until 1828. It was notable for its relative political stability, economic growth, and parliamentary system in a region otherwise marked by dictatorships and instability. Its history traces a path from independence through internal turmoil, consolidation, and a golden age, ending with a military coup that established a republic.

## Timeline
- **7 Sep 1822** — Prince Pedro declares Brazil's independence from Portugal.
- **12 Oct 1822** — Pedro is acclaimed as Emperor Pedro I of Brazil.
- **1828** — The Cisplatine War ends with the secession of Cisplatina (later Uruguay).
- **7 Apr 1831** — Emperor Pedro I abdicates in favor of his son, Pedro II.
- **1834** — The Additional Act grants greater autonomy to provincial governments.
- **1840** — Pedro II is declared of age, ending the Regency period.
- **1850** — The Eusébio de Queirós Law effectively ends the transatlantic slave trade.
- **1864-1870** — The Paraguayan War, a major regional conflict, ends in victory for Brazil.
- **1871** — The Law of Free Birth is passed, freeing children born to enslaved women.
- **13 May 1888** — The Golden Law abolishes slavery completely.
- **15 Nov 1889** — A military coup overthrows Emperor Pedro II, establishing a republic.

## Body

### Independence and Early Years
Brazil's path to independence began in 1808 when the Portuguese royal family fled Napoleon's invasion and relocated to Rio de Janeiro. In 1815, Brazil was elevated from a colony to a kingdom within the United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil, and the Algarves. After King John VI returned to Portugal in 1821, his son Pedro, as regent, faced Portuguese attempts to revoke Brazil's autonomy. Influenced by advisors like José Bonifácio de Andrada, Pedro declared independence on 7 September 1822 and was crowned Emperor Pedro I. The ensuing war of independence concluded with Portuguese recognition in 1825.

### Regency and Consolidation
Pedro I's reign was marked by conflict with parliament over the monarch's role and the loss of Cisplatina (Uruguay) after the Cisplatine War. Unable to manage crises in both Brazil and Portugal, he abdicated in 1831 for his young son, Pedro II. The ensuing Regency period was unstable, with regional civil wars erupting due to a weak central government. The Additional Act of 1834, intended to quell dissent by granting provinces more autonomy, often exacerbated local rivalries. By 1840, politicians accepted that a strong, neutral emperor was needed for stability, leading to Pedro II being declared of age.

### The Golden Age
Pedro II's personal rule, beginning in 1840, ushered in an era of internal peace and economic prosperity. The Empire successfully navigated crises involving British pressure over the slave trade and regional conflicts, culminating in victory in the Platine War (1851–52) and the Paraguayan War (1864–70). This period saw significant infrastructure development, including railroads and telegraphs, and a surge in European immigration. The "Law of Free Birth" (1871) began the legal dismantling of slavery, which was completed by the Golden Law of 1888.

### Decline and Fall
The monarchy's decline stemmed from several factors. The "Law of Free Birth" alienated conservative slave-owning elites. Emperor Pedro II grew weary and made no effort to secure the monarchy's future. His heir, Princess Isabel, was not considered a suitable ruler by the male-dominated political establishment. Meanwhile, republican and positivist ideas gained traction within the army. On 15 November 1889, a military coup led by republican officers overthrew the emperor with little public resistance, establishing the First Brazilian Republic.

### Government and Society
Brazil was a constitutional monarchy with a bicameral parliament (the General Assembly). The emperor held significant executive and moderating powers but governed through a Council of Ministers. A 1834 reform granted provincial assemblies substantial autonomy. Elections were relatively democratic for the era, with a broad (though property-based) male franchise. Society was multi-ethnic, with a large enslaved population until abolition. The Catholic Church was the state religion, though other faiths were practiced privately. A merit-based nobility was created to honor service to the Empire.

## Terms
- **Constitutional Monarchy:** A system of government where a monarch acts as head of state within the parameters of a constitution.
- **Regency:** A period of rule by a regent or committee of regents, governing in place of a monarch who is a minor or absent.
- **Additional Act (1834):** A constitutional amendment that granted greater legislative and administrative autonomy to Brazil's provinces.
- **General Assembly:** The bicameral national parliament of the Empire, consisting of the Senate and the Chamber of Deputies.
- **Council of Ministers:** The executive cabinet, headed by the President of the Council of Ministers (prime minister), responsible for day-to-day governance.
- **Law of Free Birth (1871):** Legislation that declared all children born to enslaved women in Brazil to be free.
- **Golden Law (1888):** The law that abolished slavery in Brazil without any compensation to slave owners.
- **Positivism:** A philosophical system that regards society as governed by natural laws and advocated for a scientific, often republican, form of government.

## Debates and Open questions
- The primary responsibility for the monarchy's fall is often attributed to Emperor Pedro II's passivity and failure to prepare a successor or defend the institution in his later years.
- Historians debate the extent to which the abolition of slavery directly caused the monarchy's end by turning powerful economic elites against the crown.
- The role of the military, influenced by positivism, is central to the coup, but scholars examine whether the republic was the intended goal of all conspirators or if a dictatorship was the immediate aim.
