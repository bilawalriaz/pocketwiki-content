# Satyajit Ray

## Overview

Satyajit Ray (1921–1992) was an Indian film director, screenwriter, author, illustrator, and composer, widely considered one of the greatest and most influential filmmakers in cinema history. He is celebrated for works including *The Apu Trilogy* (1955–1959), *The Music Room* (1958), *The Big City* (1963), *Charulata* (1964), and the *Goopy–Bagha* trilogy (1969–1992). Ray's films are known for their humanism, deceptive simplicity, and deep underlying complexity, and he was a cultural icon in India and Bengali communities worldwide.

## Timeline

- **1921** — Born in Calcutta to author Sukumar Ray and Suprabha Ray.
- **1943** — Started as a junior visualiser at a British advertising agency; began designing book covers for Signet Press.
- **1947** — Co-founded the Calcutta Film Society.
- **1950** — Sent to London; viewing *Bicycle Thieves* (1948) determined him to become a filmmaker.
- **1955** — Released *Pather Panchali*, his first film, winning eleven international prizes.
- **1956** — *Aparajito* won the Golden Lion at the Venice Film Festival.
- **1959** — Completed *Apur Sansar*, ending *The Apu Trilogy*.
- **1964** — Released *Charulata*, his personal favourite film.
- **1969** — Directed *Goopy Gyne Bagha Byne*, one of his most commercially successful films.
- **1983** — Suffered a heart attack while working on *Ghare Baire*, severely limiting his productivity.
- **1992** — Received an Honorary Academy Award; died on 23 April at age 70.

## Body

### Early life and education

Ray's ancestry traces back at least ten generations to Ramsunder Deo (born in the middle of the sixteenth century), who migrated to Sherpur in East Bengal and was granted a jagir (a feudal land grant). His grandfather Upendrakishore Ray Chowdhury (born 1863) was a writer, publisher, and leader of the Brahmo Samaj who set up a printing press. His father Sukumar Ray was a pioneering Bengali writer of nonsense rhyme and children's literature. Sukumar died when Satyajit was two, and Ray grew up in his grandfather's house, attracted to printing processes from an early age.

Ray studied at Ballygunge Government High School and completed a BA in economics at Presidency College, Calcutta. In 1940, his mother insisted he study at Visva-Bharati University in Santiniketan, founded by writer Rabindranath Tagore. There he came to appreciate Oriental art and read three books that influenced him to become a serious student of film-making: Paul Rotha's *The Film Till Now* and two books on theory by Rudolf Arnheim and Raymond Spottiswoode. He dropped out in 1942, as he could not feel inspired to become a painter.

### Visual artist and path to film

In 1943, Ray started working at D.J. Keymer, a British advertising agency, as a junior visualiser. He also started a second job for the Signet Press, designing book covers with complete artistic freedom, establishing himself as a leading Indian typographer and book-jacket designer. He designed covers for works including Bibhutibhushan Bandyopadhyay's *Pather Panchali* (a children's version), which deeply influenced him and became the subject of his first film.

In 1949, Ray married Bijoya Das, his first cousin; their son Sandip also became a film director. That year, French director Jean Renoir came to Calcutta to shoot *The River*, and Ray helped him find locations, telling Renoir about his idea to film *Pather Panchali*. In 1950, Ray was sent to London for six months, where he watched 99 films. Vittorio De Sica's neorealist *Bicycle Thieves* (1948) had the most profound effect; Ray later said he walked out of the theatre determined to become a filmmaker.

### The Apu years (1950–1959)

Ray decided to adapt *Pather Panchali*, a 1928 semi-autobiographical Bildungsroman about a small boy in a Bengal village. The film had no script, only Ray's drawings and notes, and was shot over two and a half years with an inexperienced crew and mostly amateur actors. After unsuccessful attempts to find producers, Ray shot with personal savings, refusing funding that required script changes or supervision. American director John Huston saw excerpts and recognised "the work of a great film-maker". With a loan from the West Bengal government, the film was completed and released in 1955 to critical acclaim, though some critics, including Bosley Crowther of *The New York Times*, criticised its loose structure.

*Aparajito* (1956), the second in the trilogy, depicts the struggle between a young man's ambitions and his loving mother; it won the Golden Lion at Venice. Ray had not planned a trilogy, but after being asked about the idea in Venice, he completed *Apur Sansar* (1959), introducing actors Soumitra Chatterjee and Sharmila Tagore. The film opens with Apu in near-poverty in Calcutta and becomes involved in an unusual marriage; critics Robin Wood and Aparna Sen thought it a major achievement ending the trilogy.

### From Devi to Charulata (1959–1964)

Ray made films about the British Raj, a documentary on Tagore, a comic film, and his first original screenplay. *Devi* (1960) examined superstition in society, starring Sharmila Tagore as a young wife deified by her father-in-law. In 1961, on Prime Minister Nehru's insistence, Ray made a documentary on Rabindranath Tagore for the poet's birth centennial, which Ray said took as much work as three feature films. That year, Ray revived *Sandesh*, the children's magazine his grandfather founded, writing stories and essays that became a steady income source.

*Kanchenjungha* (1962) was Ray's first colour film and first original screenplay, telling of an upper-class family arranging their youngest daughter's engagement in Darjeeling. *Charulata* (1964), based on Tagore's short story *Nastanirh*, portrays a lonely wife in 19th-century Bengal developing feelings for her brother-in-law. Ray said it contained the fewest flaws among his work and was his only work which, given a chance, he would make exactly the same way; it earned him a Silver Bear for Best Director at Berlin.

### New directions (1965–1982)

In this period, Ray took on fantasy, science fiction, detective stories, and historical dramas, exploring contemporary Indian life. *Nayak* (1966) follows a screen hero's inner conflict during a train journey. In 1967, Ray wrote a script for *The Alien*, based on his short story "Bankubabur Bandhu"; Ray later claimed Steven Spielberg's *E.T.* (1982) plagiarised this script, a claim Spielberg denied. *Goopy Gyne Bagha Byne* (1969), a musical fantasy based on his grandfather's children's story, was one of his most commercially successful films.

The Calcutta trilogy—*Pratidwandi* (1970), *Seemabaddha* (1971), and *Jana Aranya* (1975)—focused on repression, with male protagonists encountering the forbidden. *Shatranj Ke Khilari* (1977), Ray's first feature in a language other than Bengali, was set in Lucknow a year before the 1857 Indian Rebellion and commented on British colonisation. In 1980, Ray made *Hirak Rajar Deshe*, a sequel to *Goopy Gyne Bagha Byne*, alluding to India during The Emergency.

### Later days and death (1983–1992)

In 1983, Ray suffered a heart attack while working on *Ghare Baire* (1984), an adaptation about the dangers of fervent nationalism, completed with his son's help. In 1990, he directed *Shakha Proshakha*, about an honest old man learning of his sons' corruption. His last film, *Agantuk* (The Stranger), provokes questions about civilisation when a long-lost uncle visits his niece. Ray was an atheist, a heavy smoker but non-drinker, and valued work above all, working 12-hour days. Twenty-four days before his death, he received an Honorary Academy Award via video-link, calling it the "best achievement of [my] movie-making career". He died on 23 April 1992.

### Other works

Ray created two popular fictional characters: Feluda the sleuth and Professor Shonku the scientist. He wrote nonsense verse, humorous stories, and short stories exploring the macabre and suspense. His film essays were published as *Our Films, Their Films* (1976) and *Bishoy Chalachchitra* (1976). He designed four typefaces for roman script named Ray Roman, Ray Bizarre, Daphnis, and Holiday script, which won an international competition in 1971, and designed most of his film posters, combining folk art and calligraphy.

### Filmmaking style and influences

Ray was influenced by Jean Renoir, Vittorio De Sica, and Old Hollywood directors like John Ford, Billy Wilder, and Ernst Lubitsch. He learned freeze frames from François Truffaut and jump cuts from Jean-Luc Godard. Ray considered script-writing integral to direction and initially refused to make films in any language other than Bengali. His art director Bansi Chandragupta was so important that Ray would always write scripts in English before creating a Bengali version so Chandragupta could read it. Ray composed his own scores starting with *Teen Kanya*, and his narrative structures reflect musical forms like sonata and fugue. He cast actors from diverse backgrounds and varied his direction intensity based on actor skill.

## Terms

- **Bildungsroman**: A novel about the maturation of a protagonist.
- **Neorealism**: An Italian film movement using amateur actors and realistic settings, exemplified by *Bicycle Thieves*.
- **Jagir**: A feudal land grant.
- **Brahmo Samaj**: A 19th-century Bengali religious and social movement.
- **Montage**: A film editing technique assembling shots to condense narrative, pioneered by Sergei Eisenstein.
- **Zamindars**: Landlords in British India, depicted in *The Music Room*.
- **Bounce lighting**: A technique reflecting light from cloth to create diffused, realistic light.
- **Hyperlink cinema**: A narrative structure with interconnecting stories, which *Kanchenjungha* anticipated.

## Debates and open questions

Critics have debated whether Ray's films are anti-modern, lacking the experimentation of contemporaries like Godard; some find them glacially slow, while Kurosawa defended them as "flowing composedly, like a big river". Socialists claimed Ray was not "committed" to the downtrodden, while others accused him of glorifying poverty in *Pather Panchali* and *Ashani Sanket*. In 1980, MP Nargis Dutt accused Ray of "exporting poverty", wanting films representing "Modern India". Ray harshly criticised colleague Mrinal Sen's film *Akash Kusum* for attacking "easy targets", though Ray continued making films on that same demographic. The *E.T.* plagiarism claim against Spielberg remains contested, with Spielberg denying it.
