# Knut Hamsun

## Overview
Knut Hamsun (1859–1952) was a Norwegian novelist, poet, and playwright awarded the 1920 Nobel Prize in Literature for *Growth of the Soil*. He is widely regarded as a pioneer of psychological literature and literary modernism, introducing techniques such as stream of consciousness and interior monologue that influenced writers including Franz Kafka, Thomas Mann, and Ernest Hemingway. His early "Neo-Romantic" novels (*Hunger*, *Mysteries*, *Pan*, *Victoria*) focused on the irrational intricacies of the human mind, while his later "Nordland novels" employed Norwegian new realism to depict rural life with dialect and irony. Hamsun’s legacy is profoundly complicated by his ardent support for Nazi Germany during World War II; he met Adolf Hitler, gifted his Nobel medal to Joseph Goebbels, and wrote a eulogy for Hitler after his death. Charged with treason in 1945, he avoided conviction due to alleged mental impairment but was heavily fined in 1948, a controversy that continues to polarize Norwegian cultural memory.

## Body

### Early Life and Formative Trauma
Born Knud Pedersen in the Gudbrandsdalen valley, Hamsun moved north to Hamarøy at age three. At nine, he was sent to live with his uncle Hans Olsen, who ran a post office. Olsen subjected him to beatings and starvation, treatment Hamsun later identified as the cause of his lifelong "chronic nervous difficulties." Escaping back to Lom in 1874, he spent five years working odd jobs—shop assistant, peddler, shoemaker's apprentice, sheriff's assistant, and teacher—before becoming a ropemaker's apprentice at 17 and beginning to write. Businessman Erasmus Zahl provided crucial financial support; Hamsun later modeled the character Mack (in *Pan*, *Dreamers*, *Benoni*, *Rosa*) on him. A sojourn in America (1880s) yielded his first non-fiction work, *The Cultural Life of Modern America* (1889), which already displayed his virulent racism and Anglophobia.

### Literary Innovation and the Neo-Romantic Revolt
Hamsun explicitly rejected the dominant realism and naturalism of his time, arguing literature’s proper object was the "whisper of blood, and the pleading of bone marrow"—the intricacies of the human mind. His breakthrough novel, *Hunger* (1890), depicts a starving writer’s descent into near-madness in Kristiania (Oslo) through internal monologue and bizarre logic, presaging Kafka and twentieth-century modernism. He became the "leader of the Neo-Romantic revolt," followed by *Mysteries* (1892), *Pan* (1894), and *Victoria* (1898). These works feature the "perpetual wanderer," an itinerant stranger insinuating himself into small rural communities. Hamsun’s prose is marked by rapturous depictions of Norwegian woodlands and coastline, reflecting a pantheistic vision where mankind and nature share a mystical bond ("No one knows God... man knows only gods").

### The Nordland Novels and Nobel Recognition
In his later "Nordland novels," Hamsun shifted toward Norwegian new realism, portraying everyday rural life with local dialect, irony, and humour. *Growth of the Soil* (1917), a two-volume epic of pioneer farming in the north, was hailed as his "monumental work." H.G. Wells praised it, and it secured the 1920 Nobel Prize in Literature. Other major sequences include the *Wanderer Trilogy* (1906–1912), *Benoni and Rosa* (1908), *Children of the Age* and *Segelfoss Town* (1913–1915), and *The August Trilogy* (1927–1933). He published only one poetry collection, *The Wild Choir* (1904), set to music by several composers.

### World War II, Collaboration, and Trial
From youth, Hamsun held racist, anti-egalitarian, and Anglophobic views. During the German occupation of Norway (1940–1945), he actively supported the Nazi war effort, writing articles asserting "the Germans are fighting for us" and courting high-ranking officers. Joseph Goebbels noted in his diary Hamsun’s "unshakable" faith in German victory. Hamsun met Hitler in 1943; the meeting grew heated when Hamsun complained about civilian administrator Josef Terboven and requested the release of imprisoned Norwegians—Otto Dietrich called it the only time someone got a word in edgeways with Hitler, attributing it to Hamsun’s deafness. In 1943, Hamsun gifted his Nobel medal to Goebbels (its whereabouts remain unknown). A week after Hitler’s suicide, Hamsun published a eulogy calling him "a warrior for mankind... a prophet of the gospel of justice."

After liberation, Hamsun was detained (June 1945) and committed to a psychiatric hospital. A psychiatric examination concluded he had "permanently impaired mental faculties," leading prosecutors to drop treason charges in favor of a civil liability case. In 1948, Norway’s Supreme Court fined him 325,000 kroner (reduced from 575,000) for alleged membership in the collaborationist party Nasjonal Samling and moral support of the Germans, though he was cleared of direct Nazi affiliation. Whether he was a party member or mentally impaired remains debated. His final book, *On Overgrown Paths* (1949), written in semi-imprisonment at Landvik, fiercely rebuts the psychiatrists and judges, serving as evidence to many of his lucidity.

### Personal Life
Hamsun married Bergljot Bech in 1898; they had a daughter, Victoria, and divorced in 1906. In 1909 he married Marie Andersen (1881–1969), a promising actress who abandoned her career. They had four children (Tore, Arild, Ellinor, Cecilia) and lived as farmers in Hamarøy before buying Nørholm manor in Landvik (1918), where Hamsun wrote undisturbed, though he often travelled to write in spartan conditions. Marie authored two memoirs about their life together.

### Legacy and Cultural Reception
Hamsun’s literary stature is immense: Isaac Bashevis Singer called him "the father of the modern school of literature... The whole modern school of fiction in the twentieth century stems from Hamsun." Thomas Mann saw him as a descendant of Dostoevsky and Nietzsche. A 1954 fifteen-volume edition was superseded by a 27-volume edition (2009) with linguistic modernization. Three homes and the Knut Hamsun Centre (2009) operate as museums. Yet Norwegians remain torn: as a 2009 biographer noted, "We can't help loving him, though we have hated him all these years.... That's our Hamsun trauma. He's a ghost that won't stay in the grave." His works have inspired 25 film/TV adaptations since 1916, including the acclaimed *Hunger* (1966), and the biopic *Hamsun* (1996) starring Max von Sydow.

## Terms
- **Stream of consciousness**: Narrative technique presenting the continuous, often chaotic flow of a character’s thoughts and sensations; pioneered by Hamsun in *Hunger* (1890).
- **Interior monologue**: A form of stream of consciousness rendering a character’s inner speech directly; central to Hamsun’s psychological realism.
- **Neo-Romanticism**: Late 19th-century literary movement rejecting realism/naturalism in favor of subjectivity, the irrational, and the sublime; Hamsun led this revolt in Scandinavia.
- **Pantheism**: The belief that God and the universe/nature are identical; Hamsun’s work reflects a mystical unity of mankind and the Norwegian landscape.
- **Perpetual wanderer**: Recurring Hamsun archetype—an itinerant stranger/narrator who enters and disrupts small rural communities.
- **Nordland novels**: Hamsun’s later works set in northern Norway, employing new realism, local dialect, irony, and humour to depict rural life.
- **Nasjonal Samling**: Norwegian fascist collaborationist party (1933–1945) led by Vidkun Quisling; Hamsun was fined for alleged membership/moral support.
- **Quisling regime**: The collaborationist government headed by Vidkun Quisling during the German occupation (1940–1945); Hamsun supported it.
- **Anglophobia**: Fear or hatred of England/Britain; a lifelong driver of Hamsun’s politics, evident from his 1889 American travelogue onward.
- **On Overgrown Paths** (1949): Hamsun’s final book, a polemical memoir written in semi-imprisonment defending his sanity and attacking his prosecutors.

## Debates and open questions
- **Mental competence in 1945–1948**: The psychiatric evaluation declaring "permanently impaired mental faculties" was politically convenient; *On Overgrown Paths* (1949) demonstrates sharp lucidity, leaving historians divided on whether impairment was genuine, exaggerated, or a legal fiction.
- **Nasjonal Samling membership**: Hamsun denied party membership; the 1948 civil verdict fined him for "alleged membership" without criminal conviction. Archival evidence remains inconclusive.
- **Motives for collaboration**: Scholars debate the relative weight of lifelong Anglophobia, anti-egalitarian racism, distaste for parliamentary democracy, personal vanity, and genuine ideological affinity with Nazism.
- **Separation of art and artist**: The "Hamsun trauma" in Norway epitomizes the unresolved tension between celebrating a foundational modernist innovator and condemning a Nazi collaborator; the 2009 state-sponsored 150th-anniversary celebrations reignited this debate publicly.
