# Robert Burns Woodward

## Overview
Robert Burns Woodward (1917–1979) was a preeminent American organic chemist who revolutionized the field by demonstrating that complex natural products could be synthesized through rational, principle-based planning rather than trial and error. His work established the modern era of total synthesis, and his theoretical insights with Roald Hoffmann provided fundamental rules for predicting reaction stereochemistry.

## Timeline
- **April 10, 1917** — Born in Boston, Massachusetts.
- **1933** — Entered MIT; received B.S. (1936) and Ph.D. (1937).
- **1937–1938** — Junior Fellowship at Harvard University.
- **Early 1940s** — Published "Woodward's rules" for UV spectroscopy in structure elucidation.
- **1944** — Synthesized the alkaloid quinine with William von Eggers Doering.
- **Late 1940s–1960s** — Synthesized numerous complex natural products (e.g., strychnine, reserpine, chlorophyll).
- **1965** — Awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for achievements in organic synthesis.
- **Early 1960s–1973** — Led the collaborative total synthesis of vitamin B12 with Albert Eschenmoser.
- **1973** — Published the Woodward–Hoffmann rules with Roald Hoffmann.
- **July 8, 1979** — Died in Cambridge, Massachusetts, while working on erythromycin synthesis.

## Body

### Early Work and the Quinine Synthesis
Woodward's early career focused on applying instrumental techniques, like ultraviolet spectroscopy, to determine molecular structures, creating empirical "Woodward's rules." His 1944 synthesis of quinine was a landmark. He realized that synthesizing the precursor quinotoxine would provide a route to quinine, based on earlier work by Paul Rabe. This synthesis demonstrated that organic synthesis could be a rational science guided by principles of reactivity, moving beyond mere trial and error.

### The "Woodwardian Era" of Total Synthesis
Woodward pioneered the use of physical organic chemistry principles, such as those developed by Ingold and Robinson, as a predictive framework for synthesis. He synthesized a series of complex natural products previously thought impossible to create in the lab, including strychnine, reserpine, chlorophyll, and cephalosporin. His work was characterized by meticulous planning, the use of new spectroscopic techniques (IR, NMR), and a focus on stereoselective synthesis—producing molecules with a specific three-dimensional configuration. This approach, often described as elegant, set a new standard for the field.

### Vitamin B12 and Theoretical Contributions
The total synthesis of vitamin B12, published in 1973, was a monumental collaborative effort with Albert Eschenmoser involving nearly 100 researchers. Its complexity convinced chemists that any natural product could be synthesized with sufficient planning. During this work, Woodward and Roald Hoffmann developed the Woodward–Hoffmann rules, which use molecular orbital symmetry to predict the stereochemical outcome of organic reactions. Hoffmann shared the 1981 Nobel Prize for this work; Woodward had died in 1979.

### Other Contributions and Legacy
Woodward also contributed to structure determination (e.g., of penicillin's beta-lactam ring, confirmed by Dorothy Hodgkin) and, with Geoffrey Wilkinson, proposed the structure for ferrocene, helping launch organometallic chemistry. He trained over 200 students and authored nearly 200 publications. His encyclopedic knowledge and ability to connect disparate chemical ideas were hallmarks of his career.

## Terms
- **Total synthesis:** The complete chemical synthesis of a complex organic molecule from simple, commercially available precursors.
- **Stereochemistry:** The study of the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms in molecules and its effect on their properties and reactions.
- **Stereoselective synthesis:** A chemical reaction that preferentially forms one stereoisomer over others.
- **Ultraviolet (UV) spectroscopy:** An analytical technique that measures the absorption of UV light by molecules, used to deduce structural information.
- **Woodward's rules:** Empirical rules correlating the UV absorption maxima of conjugated organic compounds with their molecular structure.
- **Diels–Alder reaction:** A fundamental carbon-carbon bond-forming reaction between a conjugated diene and a substituted alkene.
- **Woodward–Hoffmann rules:** A set of rules based on orbital symmetry that predict the stereochemical course of pericyclic reactions.
- **Organometallic chemistry:** The study of chemical compounds containing at least one chemical bond between a carbon atom of an organic molecule and a metal.

## Debates and Open questions
- The correct structure of penicillin was a major point of contention during World War II. Woodward initially endorsed an incorrect structure before supporting the beta-lactam structure, which was ultimately proven correct by Dorothy Hodgkin's X-ray crystallography in 1945.
- Some historians and Woodward himself believed he should have shared the 1973 Nobel Prize awarded to Geoffrey Wilkinson and Ernst Otto Fischer for work on ferrocene and organometallic chemistry.
