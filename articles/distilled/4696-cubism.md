# Cubism

## Overview
Cubism was a revolutionary early-20th-century avant-garde art movement that began in Paris. It fundamentally changed painting and visual arts by depicting subjects from multiple perspectives simultaneously, rather than a single viewpoint. Considered the most influential art movement of the 20th century, its innovations in form and abstraction sparked developments in music, ballet, literature, and architecture.

## Timeline
- **1907** — Pablo Picasso paints *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon*, a proto-Cubist work.
- **1908** — Critic Louis Vauxcelles describes Braque's work as reducing forms to "cubes."
- **1911** — First organized Cubist group exhibition at the Salon des Indépendants (Salle 41).
- **1912** — First declared group exhibition of Cubism worldwide at Galeries Dalmau, Barcelona.
- **1912** — Marcel Duchamp's *Nude Descending a Staircase, No. 2* causes a scandal at the Salon des Indépendants.
- **1912** — Picasso creates the first Cubist collage; Braque pioneers *papiers collés*.
- **1912** — Metzinger and Gleizes publish *Du "Cubisme"*, the first theoretical treatise on the movement.
- **1912** — The Salon de la Section d'Or exhibition in Paris exposes Cubism to a wide audience.
- **1913** — Guillaume Apollinaire publishes *Les Peintres Cubistes*.
- **1913** — The Armory Show in New York introduces Cubism to the American public.
- **1917-1920** — "Crystal Cubism" emerges, characterized by geometric planes and order, partly as a response to World War I.

## Body

### Origins and Proto-Cubism
Cubism emerged in Paris in the early 1900s, pioneered by Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque. A primary influence was the late work of Paul Cézanne, who simplified natural forms into geometric solids like cylinders, spheres, and cones. Picasso's 1907 painting *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon*, influenced by Iberian and African art, is often considered a proto-Cubist work for its fractured forms and multiple viewpoints. The term "Cubism" was coined by critic Louis Vauxcelles in 1908 after viewing Georges Braque's *Houses at l'Estaque*, which he described as reducing forms to "cubes."

### Analytic and Synthetic Cubism
Scholars divide Cubism into phases. **Analytic Cubism** (c. 1910–1912) was a radical period where subjects were broken down into complex, faceted planes, often in monochromatic colors. **Synthetic Cubism** (c. 1912–1914) followed, characterized by simpler shapes, brighter colors, and the introduction of collage—incorporating materials like newspaper and wallpaper into artworks. This phase remained vital until around 1919.

### The Salon Cubists vs. Kahnweiler's Artists
A key distinction existed between two groups. Picasso, Braque, Juan Gris, and Fernand Léger were supported by dealer Daniel-Henry Kahnweiler, allowing them to experiment privately. In contrast, the "Salon Cubists" (including Jean Metzinger, Albert Gleizes, Robert Delaunay, and Henri Le Fauconnier) built their reputation through public exhibitions at the Salons. They emphasized multiple perspective and simultaneity—the representation of different views at once—often applied to large-scale modern-life subjects. Their 1911 Salon des Indépendants exhibition brought Cubism to public attention for the first time.

### Theoretical Development and Abstraction
The movement's theory was formalized in the 1912 manifesto *Du "Cubisme"*, which explained the concept of observing a subject from multiple points in space and time simultaneously. While Picasso and Braque resisted total abstraction, other Cubists like František Kupka and those labeled Orphists (e.g., Delaunay, Léger) pushed toward nonrepresentational art. Marcel Duchamp, inspired by Cubism, developed the concept of the "ready-made" by presenting ordinary objects as art.

### Crystal Cubism and Later Developments
During World War I (1914–1918), a shift toward clarity and geometric order led to "Crystal Cubism." After the war, with support from dealer Léonce Rosenberg, Cubism remained a central artistic issue until the mid-1920s. It influenced numerous movements, including Futurism, Constructivism, De Stijl, and Art Deco. The movement's impact extended to sculpture, where artists like Alexander Archipenko and Jacques Lipchitz explored positive/negative space, and to architecture, inspiring geometric forms and the rejection of historical styles.

### Influence in Other Fields
Cubism's principles of fragmentation and multiple perspectives influenced literature (e.g., Gertrude Stein's repetitive prose), poetry (Guillaume Apollinaire), ballet (Picasso's sets for *Parade*), and graphic design. Its formal innovations were a catalyst for the development of modern art globally.

## Terms
- **Analytic Cubism:** The first phase of Cubism (c. 1910–1912), characterized by the breakdown of subjects into complex, faceted planes, often in muted colors.
- **Synthetic Cubism:** The second phase (c. 1912–1914), featuring simpler shapes, brighter colors, and the incorporation of collage materials like newspaper.
- **Collage:** A modern art form introduced by Cubism, involving the assembly of different materials (e.g., paper, fabric) onto a surface.
- **Simultaneity:** A Cubist concept of representing multiple viewpoints or moments in time within a single image, challenging Renaissance linear perspective.
- **Multiple Perspective:** The technique of depicting a subject from several angles at once to show it in a greater context.
- **Papiers collés:** A collage technique using cut or torn pieces of paper, pioneered by Braque and Picasso.
- **Orphism:** A term coined by Guillaume Apollinaire for a more abstract, colorful offshoot of Cubism associated with artists like Robert Delaunay.
- **Section d'Or (Golden Section):** A collective of Cubist artists (active 1911–1914) who sought to distinguish their work from Picasso and Braque's and link Cubism to traditional proportions.
- **Ready-made:** An ordinary manufactured object presented as a work of art, a concept developed by Marcel Duchamp.
- **Crystal Cubism:** A post-1914 style emphasizing geometric planes, clarity, and order, often linked to a conservative turn after World War I.

## Debates and Open questions
- **Origins and Definition:** There is debate over whether *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* is truly the first Cubist work, and about the restrictive definition of Cubism that centers only on Picasso and Braque, potentially marginalizing the contributions of the Salon Cubists.
- **Theoretical Interpretation:** The assertion that Cubist depiction supports the flatness of the canvas (made by Kahnweiler) was later criticized by art historians like Clement Greenberg.
- **Influence vs. Independence:** Scholars question the extent to which the Salon Cubists depended on Picasso and Braque's techniques or developed them independently from Cézanne and other sources.
- **Historical Phasing:** The terms "Analytic" and "Synthetic" are historical impositions applied after the fact; the phases were not designated as such by the artists at the time.
