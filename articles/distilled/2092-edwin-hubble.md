# Edwin Hubble

## Overview
Edwin Hubble was an American astronomer who fundamentally transformed our understanding of the cosmos. He proved that "nebulae" were actually separate galaxies beyond the Milky Way and established the relationship between galactic distance and recession velocity, providing the first observational evidence for the expanding universe.

## Timeline
- **1889** — Born in Marshfield, Missouri.
- **1910** — Earned B.S. from University of Chicago.
- **1910-1913** — Rhodes Scholar at Oxford, studying law.
- **1917** — Earned Ph.D. in astronomy from University of Chicago.
- **1919** — Joined staff at Mount Wilson Observatory.
- **1924** — Proved Andromeda Nebula was a separate galaxy using Cepheid variables.
- **1929** — Published relationship between galaxy distance and redshift (Hubble's law).
- **1953** — Died in San Marino, California.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Hubble was a gifted athlete who initially studied law at Oxford to honor his father's wishes. After his father's death in 1913, he returned to the U.S., taught high school, and then pursued astronomy, earning his Ph.D. in 1921. His dissertation was on faint nebulae.

### Career and Discoveries
Hubble spent his career at Mount Wilson Observatory, using its powerful telescopes. His key discovery was that objects like the Andromeda Nebula were separate galaxies, not part of the Milky Way. This was proven in 1924 by measuring distances to Cepheid variable stars within them. He later devised the Hubble sequence for classifying galaxy shapes.

In 1929, Hubble established a linear relationship between a galaxy's distance and its redshift (its light shifted toward longer wavelengths), now known as Hubble's law. This implied the universe is expanding. Although Georges Lemaître had proposed this theoretically two years earlier, Hubble provided the crucial observational evidence. Hubble himself remained skeptical of the expansion interpretation, favoring the idea that redshift represented an unknown principle of nature.

### Later Work and Personal Life
During World War II, Hubble worked on ballistics research for the U.S. Army. He suffered a heart attack in 1949 and died in 1953. His burial site remains unknown. He campaigned unsuccessfully for astronomy to be eligible for the Nobel Prize in Physics during his lifetime.

## Terms
- **Cepheid variable**: A type of star whose brightness pulsates with a regular period, used as a "standard candle" to measure astronomical distances.
- **Redshift**: The displacement of spectral lines toward longer (redder) wavelengths, indicating an object is moving away from the observer.
- **Hubble's law**: The observation that the recessional velocity of a galaxy is proportional to its distance from Earth.
- **Hubble constant**: The current rate of expansion of the universe, derived from Hubble's law.
- **Extragalactic astronomy**: The study of objects and phenomena outside the Milky Way galaxy.
- **Standard candle**: An astronomical object with a known intrinsic luminosity, used to calculate distance based on its apparent brightness.
- **Hubble sequence**: A classification scheme for galaxies based on their visual appearance (e.g., spiral, elliptical).

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Priority for Hubble's law**: Georges Lemaître published the redshift-distance relation theoretically in 1927, two years before Hubble's 1929 observational paper. A 1931 English translation of Lemaître's paper omitted the key section, a redaction later found to have been made by Lemaître himself.
- **Hubble's skepticism**: Despite his law implying expansion, Hubble maintained until his death that the redshift might not represent true recession velocity, favoring the model of a static universe.
- **Nobel Prize eligibility**: Hubble spent his later career trying to have astronomy recognized as part of physics to qualify for the Nobel Prize. The committee changed the rules shortly after his death.
