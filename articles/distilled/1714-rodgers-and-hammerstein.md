# Rodgers and Hammerstein

## Overview
Composer Richard Rodgers (1902–1979) and lyricist-dramatist Oscar Hammerstein II (1895–1960) formed the most influential partnership in 20th-century musical theater. Between 1943 and 1959, they created a string of integrated musicals—most famously *Oklahoma!*, *Carousel*, *South Pacific*, *The King and I*, and *The Sound of Music*—that replaced the star-driven, revue-style shows of the 1920s and 1930s with dramas where song, dance, and dialogue advanced a unified plot. Their work initiated Broadway’s "golden age," earned 34 Tony Awards, 15 Academy Awards, two Pulitzer Prizes, and established a template for serious, socially conscious musical storytelling that influenced successors like Stephen Sondheim. Of the other four shows the pair produced on Broadway during their lifetimes, *Flower Drum Song* was well-received, and none was a critical or commercial flop.

## Timeline
- **1920–21** — Rodgers, Hart, and Hammerstein collaborate on Columbia University Varsity Shows (*Fly With Me*, *You'll Never Know*).
- **1943** — *Oklahoma!* opens on Broadway (March 31), runs 2,212 performances; first original cast recording; special Pulitzer Prize 1944.
- **1943** — Hammerstein writes book and lyrics for *Carmen Jones* (stage version of Bizet's *Carmen* with African American characters in the contemporary South).
- **1945** — *Carousel* opens on Broadway (April 19); film *State Fair* released (only original film score).
- **1947** — *Allegro* opens on Broadway.
- **1949** — *South Pacific* opens on Broadway (April 7); wins Pulitzer Prize for Drama 1950.
- **1951** — *The King and I* opens on Broadway (March 29).
- **1953** — *Me and Juliet* opens on Broadway (May 28); four R&H shows run simultaneously on Broadway (*Oklahoma!*, *South Pacific*, *The King and I*, *Me and Juliet*).
- **1955** — *Pipe Dream* opens on Broadway.
- **1957** — Television musical *Cinderella* airs live on CBS (March 31), viewed by 107+ million.
- **1958** — *Flower Drum Song* opens on Broadway; first major musical with a mostly Asian cast.
- **1959** — *The Sound of Music* opens on Broadway (November 16); last R&H collaboration.
- **1960** — Hammerstein dies (August); Rodgers writes lyrics for two new songs in 1965 film of *The Sound of Music*.
- **1965** — Film of *The Sound of Music* released; wins five Oscars including Best Picture.

## Body

### Previous Work and Partnerships
Before uniting, both men had major careers. Rodgers wrote over two decades of witty, melodic shows with Lorenz Hart, including *A Connecticut Yankee* (1927), *Babes in Arms* (1937), and *Pal Joey* (1940). Hammerstein co-wrote the operettas *Rose-Marie* (1924), *The Desert Song* (1926), and *The New Moon* (1928), then partnered with Jerome Kern on *Show Boat* (1927), a landmark integration of book and score. At Columbia University, Rodgers and Hammerstein collaborated on the 1920 Varsity Show, *Fly With Me*. The songs for the show were originally written by Rodgers (a freshman) and Lorenz Hart. Hammerstein, who was on the judging committee, added two songs in the revising stage. The three men collaborated again on the 1921 Varsity Show, *You'll Never Know*, with Hammerstein as "Director of Production." By the early 1940s, Hart’s alcoholism made him unreliable; Rodgers approached Hammerstein, and both had independently wanted to musicalize Lynn Riggs’s play *Green Grow the Lilacs*.

### Oklahoma! and the Integrated Musical
*Oklahoma!* (1943) revolutionized the form. Rather than using songs as diversions, every number advanced plot or character; dance (choreographed by Agnes de Mille) served narrative function. The show opened without stars, prioritizing material over celebrity, and ran a then-record 2,212 performances. It produced the first original cast album (Decca Records), standardizing the practice. A 1955 film introduced the Todd-AO 70 mm widescreen process. The special 1944 Pulitzer Prize recognized its dramatic cohesion.

After their initial success with *Oklahoma!*, the pair took a break from working together and Hammerstein concentrated on the musical *Carmen Jones*, a stage version of Bizet's *Carmen* with the characters changed to African Americans in the contemporary South, for which he wrote the book and lyrics. The musical was adapted to the screen in 1954, and scored a Best Actress Oscar nomination for leading lady Dorothy Dandridge.

### Carousel and Tragic Innovation
*Carousel* (1945), adapted from Ferenc Molnár’s *Liliom*, centered on an antihero (Billy Bigelow) and included domestic violence—a rarity for musical comedy. It replaced the traditional overture with the instrumental "Carousel Waltz" and featured extended musical scenes blending sung, spoken, and danced material. The 1956 film reunited *Oklahoma!* leads Gordon MacRae and Shirley Jones.

### State Fair: Sole Original Film Score
*State Fair* (1945) was the only score Rodgers and Hammerstein wrote directly for film. A Technicolor remake of a 1933 Will Rogers vehicle, it won their sole shared Oscar for "It Might as Well Be Spring." A 1962 remake failed; a stage version reached Broadway only in 1996.

### South Pacific: Social Conscience on Stage
*South Pacific* (1949), drawn from James A. Michener’s *Tales of the South Pacific*, tackled racism explicitly. The song "You've Got to Be Carefully Taught"—asserting prejudice is learned—provoked controversy; Georgia legislators tried to ban the show as "Moscow-inspired." Rodgers and Hammerstein refused cuts. The show won the 1950 Pulitzer Prize for Drama (shared with co-writer Joshua Logan). Mary Martin and opera star Ezio Pinza led the original cast.

### The King and I: Historical Drama and Star Power
Based on Margaret Landon’s *Anna and the King of Siam*, *The King and I* (1951) starred Gertrude Lawrence and unknown Yul Brynner. Brynner’s portrayal became iconic, winning an Oscar for the 1956 film (Deborah Kerr’s singing dubbed by Marni Nixon). The King and I was followed by *Me and Juliet*, which opened at the Majestic Theatre on May 28, 1953. When *Oklahoma!* returned to Broadway on August 31, 1953, with *The King and I*, *Me and Juliet* and *South Pacific* all still playing, Rodgers and Hammerstein had four shows appearing on Broadway at once.

### Cinderella: Television Event
Their only original TV musical, *Cinderella* (1957), aired live on CBS with Julie Andrews (Rodgers: "What won us over was the chance to work with Julie"). It drew over 107 million viewers. Only a black-and-white kinescope survives. Subsequent TV versions aired in 1965, 1997, and a Broadway adaptation opened in 2013.

### Flower Drum Song: Cultural Milestone
*Flower Drum Song* (1958), from C. Y. Lee’s novel, was directed by Gene Kelly and featured a mostly Asian cast—unprecedented for Broadway. Though less celebrated than the "big five," it was a solid success. A 2002 revival by David Henry Hwang rewrote the book while retaining the score and immigrant themes.

### The Sound of Music: Final Triumph
*The Sound of Music* (1959), based on the von Trapp family’s escape from the 1938 Anschluss, starred Mary Martin and Theodore Bikel. Hammerstein died in 1960; for the 1965 film (Julie Andrews, Christopher Plummer), Rodgers wrote both music and lyrics for two new songs ("I Have Confidence," "Something Good"). The film won five Oscars, including Best Picture, and became the highest-grossing film adaptation of a Broadway musical. "Edelweiss" was the duo’s final song.

### Legacy
Rodgers and Hammerstein redefined musical theater: shows became "integrated musicals" where book, score, dance, and design served a unified dramatic purpose. Their "formula musical" structure—baritone lead, soprano lead, tenor and alto supports—streamlined casting but drew criticism for predictability (a pattern rooted in Viennese operetta). Scholars mark *Oklahoma!* as the dividing line between pre- and post-golden-age theater. Stephen Sondheim cited them as crucial influences. Their catalog remains among the most licensed and revived worldwide. *Allegro* (1947), *Me and Juliet* (1953), and *Pipe Dream* (1955) are rarely staged and their artistic merit remains contested.

### On Television and Film
The pair appeared on the debut of *Toast of the Town* (1948, later *The Ed Sullivan Show*), a 1954 tribute special, and as mystery guests on *What's My Line?* (1956). They cameoed in MGM’s *Main Street to Broadway* (1953) and the *South Pacific* film trailer (1958).

### Social Issues
Beneath accessible melodies, their works addressed serious themes: domestic violence (*Carousel*), racism and interracial marriage (*South Pacific*), sexism and class (*The King and I*), and fascism (*The Sound of Music*). This maturity distinguished them from the frivolous musical comedies of the 1920s–30s.

## Terms
- **Integrated musical**: A show in which songs, dances, and dialogue are woven into a unified narrative rather than presented as standalone entertainments.
- **Original cast recording**: An album recorded by the Broadway cast using the show’s orchestrations; *Oklahoma!* (1943) was the first.
- **Formula musical**: A term describing R&H’s recurring structural patterns (predictable plot arcs, fixed vocal casting: baritone lead, soprano lead, tenor support, alto support).
- **Antihero**: A protagonist lacking conventional heroic qualities; Billy Bigelow in *Carousel* is an early musical-theater example.
- **Anschluss**: The 1938 annexation of Austria by Nazi Germany, the historical backdrop of *The Sound of Music*.
- **Todd-AO**: A 70 mm widescreen film process first used for the 1955 *Oklahoma!* movie.
- **Kinescope**: A film recording of a live television broadcast; the only surviving record of the 1957 *Cinderella*.
- **Princess Theatre musicals**: Intimate, character-driven shows (1915–18) by Kern, Bolton, and Wodehouse that prefigured integration.
- **Operetta**: A light opera with spoken dialogue; Viennese models like *The Merry Widow* influenced R&H’s casting formulas.
- **Pulitzer Prize for Drama**: Awarded to *Oklahoma!* (1944, special) and *South Pacific* (1950).

## Debates and open questions
- **Formula vs. innovation**: Critics dispute whether R&H’s recurring character types and plot structures represent a reliable craft or a creative limitation.
- **Cultural representation**: *The King and I* and *Flower Drum Song* are re-examined for Orientalist tropes; the 2002 *Flower Drum Song* revival rewrote the book to address stereotypes.
- **Authorship balance**: Scholars debate the division of dramatic vs. musical initiative—Hammerstein typically wrote the book and lyrics first, Rodgers set them to music, but the collaboration was unusually fluid.
- **Canon hierarchy**: The "big five" (*Oklahoma!*, *Carousel*, *South Pacific*, *The King and I*, *The Sound of Music*) dominate revivals and scholarship; *Allegro* (1947), *Me and Juliet* (1953), and *Pipe Dream* (1955) are rarely staged and their artistic merit remains contested.
