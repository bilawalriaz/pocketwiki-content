# Robert Millikan

## Overview
Robert Andrews Millikan (1868–1953) was a pivotal American experimental physicist whose Nobel Prize-winning work measured the elementary charge of the electron and validated Einstein's photoelectric effect. He was also a transformative leader in science education and administration, most notably at the California Institute of Technology (Caltech), though his legacy is now complicated by his association with the eugenics movement.

- **1895** — Receives Ph.D. from Columbia University.

- **1913** — Publishes seminal results of the oil drop experiment.

- **1921** — Becomes Director of Caltech's Norman Bridge Laboratory of Physics.
- **1923** — Awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics.
- **1930s** — Leads research on cosmic rays at Caltech.
- **1953** — Dies in San Marino, California.
- **2020-2023** — Caltech and other institutions begin removing his name from buildings and awards due to his eugenics advocacy.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Millikan was educated at Oberlin College and Columbia University, where he earned his Ph.D. in 1895. He began his career teaching physics, a subject he initially learned from a textbook to teach a preparatory course. He later studied in Germany and joined the University of Chicago, where he became a professor in 1910. In 1917, he was recruited by astronomer George Ellery Hale to help transform Throop College of Technology into a major research institution, which was renamed the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) in 1920. Millikan became its key scientific leader, serving as Director of the Norman Bridge Laboratory of Physics and Chairman of the Executive Council from 1921 until his retirement in 1955.

### The Oil Drop Experiment
In 1909, Millikan, with graduate student Harvey Fletcher, began the oil drop experiment to measure the charge of a single electron. While J.J. Thomson had discovered the electron's charge-to-mass ratio, the actual charge was unknown. Millikan's apparatus suspended charged oil droplets against gravity between two metal electrodes. By measuring the forces on many droplets, he demonstrated that their charges were integer multiples of a fundamental value (1.592 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulomb), proving charge is quantized. This work, for which he received the 1923 Nobel Prize, was initially contested by Felix Ehrenhaft. A controversy exists over Millikan's selective use of data, which he argued was based on complete observational series, to reduce statistical error.

### The Photoelectric Effect
Millikan was initially skeptical of Albert Einstein's 1905 photon theory of light. He conducted a decade-long experimental program to test it, building specialized equipment to prepare clean metal surfaces. His 1914 results precisely confirmed Einstein's photoelectric equation, though Millikan himself remained unconvinced of the theoretical interpretation for years. His work provided crucial experimental evidence for the photon concept and allowed him to measure the Planck constant.

### Cosmic Rays and Later Work
At Caltech, Millikan's research focused on cosmic rays, a term he coined. In the 1930s, he debated Arthur Compton over their composition, arguing they were high-energy photons ("birth cries of atoms") rather than charged particles. Compton was later proven correct. During World War I, Millikan served as Vice Chairman of the National Research Council, working on anti-submarine devices. He also contributed to the League of Nations' Committee on Intellectual Cooperation and chaired a committee on earthquake protection after the 1933 Long Beach earthquake.

### Legacy and Controversy
Millikan was a devout Christian who argued for harmony between science and religion. However, he was also a proponent of eugenics, serving as a trustee of the Human Betterment Foundation and making statements praising the "Anglo-Saxon" population of his community. In the 21st century, this association has led to the removal of his name from numerous institutions, including Caltech's Millikan Library (now Caltech Hall), a professorship, and awards from the American Association of Physics Teachers. This removal has been debated by some scholars who argue his beliefs were within the norms of his time.

## Terms
- **Elementary charge (e):** The fundamental electric charge carried by a single proton or, equivalently, the magnitude of the charge carried by a single electron.
- **Photoelectric effect:** The emission of electrons when light shines on a material, a phenomenon that supported the particle theory of light.
- **Cosmic rays:** High-energy radiation, mainly consisting of protons and atomic nuclei, that constantly bombards Earth from outer space.
- **Quantization:** The concept that a physical property, like electric charge, can only exist in discrete, indivisible units rather than a continuous range.
- **Eugenics:** A discredited pseudoscientific belief in improving the genetic quality of a human population, often through discriminatory practices.
- **Planck constant (h):** A fundamental constant in quantum mechanics relating the energy of a photon to its frequency.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Data Selection:** Was Millikan's exclusion of some data points from his oil drop experiment legitimate scientific judgment or "cosmetic surgery" to reduce error and avoid controversy?
- **Interpretation of Results:** Why did Millikan, despite providing definitive experimental proof for Einstein's photoelectric equation, resist accepting the photon theory of light for over a decade?
- **Legacy Reckoning:** Should historical scientific figures be judged by the moral standards of their time, and how should institutions balance honoring scientific contributions against advocacy for harmful ideologies like eugenics?
