# British Raj

## Overview
The British Raj was the period of direct rule by the British Crown over the Indian subcontinent, lasting from 1858 to 1947. It began after the Indian Rebellion of 1857 ended the East India Company's control and ended with the partition of British India into the independent dominions of India and Pakistan. This era saw the development of modern infrastructure, the rise of Indian nationalism, and the eventual transfer of power amid widespread communal violence.

## Timeline
- **28 June 1858** — Rule transferred from the East India Company to the British Crown.
- **1885** — Indian National Congress founded.
- **1905** — Partition of Bengal by Lord Curzon; Swadeshi movement begins.
- **1906** — All-India Muslim League founded in Dacca.
- **1916** — Lucknow Pact between Congress and Muslim League; Home Rule Leagues founded.
- **1919** — Jallianwala Bagh massacre; Government of India Act (Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms) passed.
- **1920** — Non-cooperation movement launched by Gandhi.
- **1930** — Salt Satyagraha led by Gandhi.
- **1935** — Government of India Act passed, providing for provincial autonomy.
- **1942** — Quit India Movement launched; Congress leaders arrested.
- **15 August 1947** — Independence and partition into India and Pakistan.

## Body

### Post-Rebellion Governance and Infrastructure
After the 1857 Rebellion, the British reorganized the Indian army and integrated Indian princes into the political system. The Raj invested heavily in infrastructure, building the world's largest irrigation system and a vast railway network, which was the fourth largest globally by 1920. These projects, along with canals, were designed to facilitate the extraction of raw materials and the import of British goods, intertwining the Indian and British economies. However, the financial risks were borne by Indian taxpayers, and few skilled jobs were created for Indians.

### Rise of Nationalism and Political Reform
A new Indian middle class, educated in British-founded universities, formed the core of the nationalist movement. The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, initially debated British policy and the economic "drain" of wealth. The partition of Bengal in 1905 sparked the Swadeshi movement, a boycott of British goods. Political reforms, such as the Morley-Minto Reforms (1909) and Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms (1919), introduced limited Indian participation in legislatures but maintained British control. The Jallianwala Bagh massacre (1919) radicalized the movement, leading to Gandhi's campaigns of non-cooperation and civil disobedience.

### World War II and the Path to Independence
World War II was a watershed. The Viceroy declared war on India's behalf without consultation, prompting Congress ministries to resign. The Muslim League, supporting the war effort, grew in strength and demanded a separate Muslim state, formalized in the 1940 Lahore Resolution. The war effort expanded Indian industry and the military, but also led to the Quit India Movement (1942), which was suppressed. Post-war, naval mutinies and Labour's election victory in Britain accelerated the timeline for independence. The failure of negotiations over partition led to the creation of India and Pakistan in August 1947, accompanied by massive communal violence and displacement.

### Economy and Society
The Raj's economy was characterized by the deindustrialization of traditional sectors like textiles due to British imports, alongside the growth of new industries like Tata's steel. Agriculture remained dominant, with famines causing millions of deaths, though irrigation projects later improved food production. A modern education system created a professional class but also fueled nationalism. Public health challenges included cholera, plague, and malaria, with significant mortality despite some medical advances.

## Terms
- **British India**: The territories directly administered by the British Crown.
- **Princely States**: Over 500 semi-autonomous regions under indirect British rule, with their own hereditary rulers.
- **Satyagraha**: Gandhi's philosophy of non-violent resistance, meaning "truth-force."
- **Swadeshi**: The movement promoting Indian-made goods and boycotting foreign products.
- **Diarchy**: The system under the 1919 Act where some government departments were transferred to Indian ministers while key ones remained with British officials.
- **Purna Swaraj**: The demand for complete independence, adopted by the Congress in 1929.
- **Two-Nation Theory**: The idea, advanced by the Muslim League, that Hindus and Muslims were distinct nations requiring separate states.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians debate the economic impact of the Raj. The "Nationalist school" argues it impoverished India, while others, like P.J. Marshall, suggest the British were part of a continuing Indian elite system and did not cause a sharp break from Mughal-era prosperity. The long-term intention of British rule—whether to develop or exploit India—remains contested. The historiography of education and social reform also continues to be reassessed.
