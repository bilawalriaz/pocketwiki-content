# Archaea

## Overview

Archaea (singular: archaeon) is one of the three domains of life, alongside Bacteria and Eukarya. Initially classified as bacteria (as "archaebacteria"), archaea were recognized as a distinct domain in 1977 by Carl Woese and George E. Fox based on ribosomal RNA gene sequencing. Archaea are prokaryotes (lacking membrane-bound organelles) that are genetically and biochemically distinct from bacteria—most notably in their ether-linked membrane lipids, their unique metabolism of methanogenesis, and their archaellum motility structure. They are abundant across Earth's habitats, from extreme environments (hot springs, salt lakes) to oceans, soils, and the human gut, and are critical to global carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling.

- **2015** — *Lokiarchaeum* discovered; most closely related to eukaryotes known at that time
- **2017** — "Asgard" superphylum proposed as sister group to TACK and more closely related to eukaryotes
- **2020** — *Promethearchaeum syntrophicum* (Asgard archaea) reported as possible link between prokaryotes and eukaryotes

## Body

### Discovery and classification

For much of the 20th century, prokaryotes were classified by biochemistry, morphology, and metabolism. In 1965, Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling proposed using gene sequences to determine relationships—the phylogenetic approach still used today. The first archaea isolated were methane-producing species in the genera *Methanobacterium* and *Methanococcus* in the 1930s–1940s, though they were classified as bacteria. In 1977, Carl Woese and George E. Fox separated archaea from bacteria based on ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes, citing three lines of evidence: lack of peptidoglycan in cell walls, two unusual coenzymes, and 16S rRNA gene sequencing results. Woese, Otto Kandler, and Mark Wheelis later proposed the three-domain system (Eukarya, Bacteria, Archaea)—the "Woesian Revolution."

The word "archaea" comes from Ancient Greek meaning "ancient things," as the first representatives were methanogens assumed to reflect Earth's primitive atmosphere. For a long time, archaea were seen as extremophiles, but by the end of the 20th century, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detection from environmental samples revealed them in non-extreme environments, showing them to be abundant and diverse.

### Classification and phylogeny

Classification of archaea is rapidly moving and contentious. Current systems rely heavily on ribosomal RNA gene sequences (molecular phylogenetics). Most cultured species belong to two main kingdoms: Methanobacteriati and Thermoproteati (formerly TACK). Other groups include *Nanoarchaeum equitans* (discovered 2003, phylum Nanoarchaeota, reassigned to Nanobdellota in 2023), the proposed "Korarchaeota" (now Thermoproteota), and ARMAN organisms (Micrarchaeota and Parvarchaeota, discovered 2006). The "TACK" superphylum (2011) was proposed as related to eukaryote origins; the "Asgard" superphylum (2017) was proposed as more closely related to the original eukaryote. The "DPANN" superphylum (2013) groups nano-sized archaea with extremely small cells and genomes, possibly including obligate symbionts or parasites—though some analyses suggest DPANN is not monophyletic, with the grouping caused by long branch attraction (LBA).

The species concept is controversial: Ernst Mayr's definition (reproductively isolated interbreeding group) does not apply since archaea reproduce asexually. Horizontal gene transfer is high between lineages. Some researchers group individuals into species-like populations based on similar genomes and infrequent gene transfer (as in *Ferroplasma*), but studies in *Halorubrum* found significant transfer between less-related populations. Estimates of phyla range from 18 to 23, of which only 8 have cultured representatives.

### Origin and evolution

Earth is about 4.54 billion years old; life began at least 3.5 billion years ago. Earliest evidence includes biogenic graphite in 3.7-billion-year-old rocks in Western Greenland and microbial mat fossils in 3.48-billion-year-old sandstone in Western Australia. Chemical fossils of unique archaeal lipids have been detected in rocks up to 3.8 billion years old (Isua district, Greenland), suggesting the archaeal lineage may be the most ancient on Earth. Woese argued the three domains diverged early from an ancestral colony, possibly before cells evolved, when unrestricted lateral gene transfer was possible. The last common ancestor of bacteria and archaea may have been a thermophile.

Woese's 16S rRNA comparison method showed archaea were genetically more similar to eukaryotes than prokaryotes, despite structural similarity to bacteria, indicating Archaea and Eukarya shared a more recent common ancestor. Unique archaeal features include ether-linked membrane lipids (more chemically stable than ester linkages, aiding survival in extreme environments) and methanogenesis (methane production), which plays a pivotal role in the carbon cycle. It is theorized both domains originated at deep-sea alkaline hydrothermal vents, with the lipid divide possibly dating to the origin of life.

### Relationship to bacteria and eukaryotes

Most metabolic pathway genes are common between Archaea and Bacteria, while most gene-expression genes are common between Archaea and Eukarya. Archaeal cell structure is most similar to Gram-positive bacteria (single lipid bilayer, thick sacculus). One proposal suggests archaea evolved from Gram-positive bacteria in response to antibiotic selection pressure—archaea are resistant to antibiotics produced primarily by Gram-positive bacteria, and these antibiotics target genes distinguishing archaea from bacteria. This could also explain adaptation to extreme environments as escape from antibiotic-producing organisms (the Neomura hypothesis).

The evolutionary relationship between archaea and eukaryotes remains unclear. The standard hypothesis states eukaryotes arose through symbiogenesis—fusion of an archaean and a eubacterium forming mitochondria. The eocyte hypothesis instead posits Eukaryota emerged relatively late from Archaea. *Lokiarchaeum* (discovered 2015, named for Loki's Castle hydrothermal vent in the Arctic Ocean) was the most eukaryote-related archaeon known, called a transitional organism. Sister phyla ("Thorarchaeota," "Odinarchaeota," "Heimdallarchaeota") comprise the "Asgard" supergroup.

### Morphology and structure

Archaea range from 0.1 to over 15 micrometers in diameter, occurring as spheres, rods, spirals, or plates. Unusual shapes include lobed cells in *Sulfolobus*, needle-like filaments in *Thermofilum*, rectangular rods in *Thermoproteus* and *Pyrobaculum*, and flat square cells in *Haloquadratum walsbyi*. *Thermoplasma* and *Ferroplasma* lack cell walls and resemble amoebae. Some species form aggregates or filaments up to 200 μm long; *Pyrodictium* produces hollow tubes called cannulae connecting cells. Most archaea possess a cell wall assembled from surface-layer proteins forming an S-layer (a rigid protein array like chain mail). Unlike bacteria, archaea lack peptidoglycan; Methanobacteriales have pseudopeptidoglycan, chemically distinct (lacking D-amino acids and N-acetylmuramic acid). Archaella (archaeal flagella) are driven by rotatory motors powered by a proton gradient but evolved from bacterial type IV pili, unlike bacterial flagella (which share ancestry with the type III secretion system); archaella are synthesized by adding subunits at the base.

Archaeal membranes are unusual in four ways: (1) glycerol-ether lipids instead of glycerol-ester lipids; (2) mirror-image stereochemistry (sn-glycerol-1-phosphate vs. sn-glycerol-3-phosphate), suggesting entirely different enzymes developed early in life's history; (3) isoprenoid chains with side-branches and rings instead of straight-chain fatty acids; (4) some archaea fuse lipid tails into a monolayer (bolaamphiphiles), making membranes more rigid—as in *Ferroplasma*, aiding survival in highly acidic habitats.

### Metabolism

Archaea use diverse energy sources. Chemotrophs obtain energy from inorganic compounds (sulfur, ammonia), including nitrifiers, methanogens, and anaerobic methane oxidizers, using redox reactions to generate ATP through chemiosmosis. Phototrophs use sunlight but no known archaea perform oxygen-generating photosynthesis. Methanogens produce methane in anaerobic environments (swamps), using unique coenzymes (coenzyme M, methanofuran); acetotrophic archaea (order Methanosarcinales) break down acetic acid into methane and carbon dioxide, playing a major role in biogas production. Autotrophs fix carbon via a modified Calvin cycle, the 3-hydroxypropionate/4-hydroxybutyrate cycle, the reverse Krebs cycle (Thermoproteota), or the reductive acetyl-CoA pathway (Methanobacteriati). Phototrophic Halobacteria use light-activated ion pumps (bacteriorhodopsin, halorhodopsin) to generate ion gradients converted to ATP by ATP synthase—a form of photophosphorylation.

### Genetics

Archaea usually have a single circular chromosome, sometimes in multiple copies. The largest known genome (as of 2002) was *Methanosarcina acetivorans* at 5,751,492 base pairs; the smallest is *Nanoarchaeum equitans* at 490,885 base pairs with ~537 protein-encoding genes. Up to 15% of proteins encoded by any archaeal genome are unique to the domain, most with unknown function. Transcription resembles eukaryotic transcription (RNA polymerase close to eukaryotic RNA polymerase II), while translation shows signs of both bacterial and eukaryotic equivalents. Most archaeal genes lack introns, though introns occur in tRNA and rRNA genes. Horizontal gene transfer occurs via cytoplasmic bridges (*Haloferax volcanii*) and DNA damage-induced aggregation (*Sulfolobus* species), which may provide homologous recombination repair—possibly a primitive form of sexual interaction. Archaeal viruses form a diverse virosphere (15–18 DNA-based families), including bottle-, spindle-, coil-, and droplet-shaped viruses; most maintain chronic infections without killing host cells.

### Reproduction and behavior

Archaea reproduce asexually by binary or multiple fission, fragmentation, or budding. Cell division in *Sulfolobus* shows characteristics of both bacterial and eukaryotic systems. Methanobacteriati use the FtsZ protein (contracting ring), while cren- and thaumarchaea use the Cdv machinery related to eukaryotic ESCRT-III. Archaea do not make spores, though some Haloarchaea form thick-walled structures resistant to osmotic shock (not reproductive). Quorum sensing (cell-density communication) exists in archaea, with LuxR proteins similar to bacterial versions. Archaea form biofilms (sessile communities producing extracellular polymeric substances) through stages of attachment, micro-colony formation, maturation, and dispersal.

### Ecology

Archaea may represent about 20% of microbial cells in oceans and vast numbers in seafloor sediment. Extremophiles include halophiles (salt lakes, outnumbering bacteria above 20–25% salinity), thermophiles (above 45 °C), hyperthermophiles (above 80 °C; *Methanopyrus kandleri* Strain 116 reproduces at 122 °C, the highest recorded for any organism), alkaliphiles, and acidophiles (*Picrophilus torridus* grows at pH 0). Archaea are also common in cold oceanic environments and plankton (up to 40% of microbial biomass). In seafloor sediments, archaea make up at least 12.8% of living cells below 1 meter depth; viral lysis of archaea accounts for up to one-third of total microbial biomass killed, releasing ~0.3–0.5 gigatons of carbon per year. Archaea carry out nitrogen cycling (nitrification, denitrification, nitrogen fixation), sulfur cycling (releasing sulfur from rocks; *Sulfolobus* contributes to acid mine drainage), and carbon cycling (methanogens as decomposers in anaerobic ecosystems). Well-characterized interactions are mutual or commensal: methanogens aid protozoa in ruminant and termite digestive tracts; *Cenarchaeum symbiosum* is an endosymbiont of a sponge; *Methanobrevibacter smithii* is the most common archaean in the human gut. No clear archaeal pathogens were known as of 2003, though *Nanoarchaeum equitans* may be parasitic on *Ignicoccus hospitalis*, and *Candidatus* Sukunaarchaeum mirabile appears to be a specialized parasite of a dinoflagellate host.

### Significance in technology and industry

Extremophile archaea provide enzymes functioning under harsh conditions. Thermostable DNA polymerases (Pfu DNA polymerase from *Pyrococcus furiosus*) revolutionized molecular biology by enabling PCR. Amylases, galactosidases, and pullulanases functioning above 100 °C allow high-temperature food processing (low-lactose milk and whey). These enzymes are stable in organic solvents, useful in green chemistry and structural biology. Methanogenic archaea are vital in sewage treatment (anaerobic digestion, biogas production). Acidophilic archaea show promise for extracting metals from ores (gold, cobalt, copper). Archaea host archaeocins—a new class of potentially useful antibiotics differing in structure from bacterial antibiotics, possibly with novel modes of action.

## Terms

- **Prokaryote**: Organism lacking membrane-bound organelles; a "grade" of life including bacteria and archaea, not a natural group
- **16S rRNA**: Ribosomal RNA molecule central to protein production; its sequence is used for phylogenetic classification
- **Methanogenesis**: Metabolic production of methane, unique to archaea
- **Ether lipids**: Membrane lipids with ether bonds (vs. ester bonds in bacteria/eukaryotes), more chemically stable
- **Archaellum**: Archaeal flagellum, evolved from bacterial type IV pili, synthesized at the base
- **S-layer**: Rigid array of surface-layer proteins covering the cell wall
- **Horizontal gene transfer**: Transfer of genes between lineages, common in archaea
- **Quorum sensing**: Cell-density communication via signaling molecules (e.g., AHLs)
- **Extremophile**: Organism thriving in extreme environments (high temperature, salinity, acidity, etc.)
- **Symbiogenesis**: Theory that eukaryotes arose from fusion of an archaean and a eubacterium (forming mitochondria)

## Debates and open questions

- Whether DPANN forms a monophyletic group or is an artifact of long branch attraction
- Whether species designations have practical meaning given high horizontal gene transfer and asexual reproduction
- The exact relationship between Asgard archaea and eukaryotes, and whether eukaryotes emerged early (standard hypothesis) or late (eocyte hypothesis) from archaea
- Whether archaea evolved from Gram-positive bacteria in response to antibiotic selection pressure (Neomura hypothesis)
- The function of cannulae in *Pyrodictium* colonies
- Whether the apparent grouping of archaeal and eukaryotic lipids in ancient rocks reflects true archaeal ancestry
- The role of archaea in ocean ecology and global biogeochemical cycles remains largely unexplored since most marine species are uncultured
