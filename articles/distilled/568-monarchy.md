# Monarchy

## Overview
A monarchy is a form of government in which a single head of state—the monarch—rules for life, with power typically passing hereditarily within a royal family (dynasty). Historically dominant, monarchies peaked in the mid-19th century before declining sharply as republics replaced them, particularly after World Wars I and II. Today, 43 sovereign nations retain a monarch. Most are constitutional monarchies where the ruler serves a ceremonial role under a constitution, though absolute monarchies persist in roughly a dozen states. A 2020 study argues monarchy arose from an efficiency advantage in governing large territories before modern communications; a 2023 study suggests it persists because monarchies can democratize via constitutional transition without destabilizing leadership, appealing to opposition groups seeking both democracy and stability.

## Timeline
- **c. 3100 BCE** — Narmer reigns as Pharaoh of Ancient Egypt; Enmebaragesi rules as Sumerian King of Kish c. 2600 BCE—earliest evidenced monarchies.
- **509 BCE** — Roman Republic established, abolishing monarchy.
- **500 BCE** — Athenian democracy established, replacing monarchic rule.
- **1649** — Parliament of England temporarily overthrows the English monarchy.
- **1776 / 1789** — American and French Revolutions accelerate modern anti-monarchism and republicanism.
- **1918–1945** — World Wars I and II trigger widespread abolition of monarchies in favor of republics.
- **1980** — Sweden adopts equal (full cognatic) primogeniture, allowing the eldest child regardless of sex to inherit.
- **1993** — Cambodian monarchy restored under peace agreement after Khmer Rouge deposition.
- **2008** — Bhutan transitions to a constitutional monarchy.
- **2013** — United Kingdom adopts absolute primogeniture for Commonwealth realms (effective for heirs after 2011).
- **2017** — Saudi King Salman designates his son as heir, shifting from agnatic seniority (brother-to-brother) to father-to-son succession.
- **2025** — Forty-three sovereign nations have a monarch, including fifteen Commonwealth realms sharing King Charles III.

## Body

### Etymology and Concept
The term derives from Greek *monarkhia* ("ruling of one," from *monos* "alone" + *arkhein* "to rule"), entering English via Old French and Late Latin in the mid-14th century as "sovereignty" or "absolute power." By the mid-15th century, *monarch* meant a sole, autocratic ruler for life. Polybius (2nd century BCE) classified monarchy as one of three "benign" forms (with aristocracy and democracy), opposed to "malignant" tyranny, oligarchy, and ochlocracy. In antiquity, monarchy contrasted with republics where executive power rested with citizen assemblies.

### Historical Development
Monarchy evolved from prehistoric chiefdoms into early state formations in Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley, and China. Early monarchs often held sacral functions and claimed divine ancestry, establishing the divine right of kings. Some ancient monarchies were elective (e.g., Sumerian assemblies, Frankish elections). Antiquity saw the first abolitions in favor of republican assemblies (Rome 509 BCE, Athens 500 BCE). The 4th-century BCE *Arthashastra* codified monarchic ethics in India.

From the 17th century, parliamentarism (Icelandic Commonwealth, Swiss *Landsgemeinde*, medieval town privileges) and revolutionary republicanism (English Civil War 1649, American Revolution 1776, French Revolution 1789) challenged monarchy. This birthed the modern ideological divide: *republicanism* (advocacy for abolition) versus *monarchism* (advocacy for retention). The 19th century saw polarized politics between radical anti-monarchism and reactionary monarchism. The 20th century brought mass abolition after the World Wars. Hybrid forms appeared historically: the Dutch Republic (monarchical republic), Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth and Sweden’s Age of Liberty (1719–1772) as republican monarchies.

### Characteristics and Role
Monarchies center on hereditary reign for life, with the heir raised within a royal household and trained for rule. Most historical monarchs have been male, but female monarchs (*queen regnant*, distinct from *queen consort*) have ruled. Hereditary succession systems vary: **primogeniture** (eldest child inherits), **agnatic primogeniture** (male-line only, often via Salic law excluding females), **semi-Salic law** (females inherit only upon extinction of male lines), **agnatic seniority** (succession passes to eldest brother before children, as in pre-2017 Saudi Arabia), and **tanistry** (semi-elective, weighing merit). Since 1980, European monarchies have adopted equal primogeniture (Sweden, Netherlands, Norway, Belgium, Denmark, Luxembourg, UK).

Non-hereditary forms exist: **elective monarchies** (Cambodia, Malaysia, UAE, Papacy) where an electoral college chooses the monarch for life or a term; **self-proclaimed monarchies** where leaders assume royal titles (Napoleon I 1804, Bokassa 1976, Yuan Shikai 1915); and **usurpation** via coup or fraudulent lineage claims (e.g., pseudo-Smerdis in Herodotus). Legitimacy is often ritualized through coronations, insignia, and titles, especially to resolve succession crises or legitimize new dynasties.

### Powers of the Monarch
**Absolute monarchies** (~12 states): monarch rules as autocrat with unrestricted power. **Constitutional monarchies** (majority): monarch’s power is limited by constitution; typically a ceremonial figurehead symbolizing national unity and continuity. **Semi-constitutional monarchies**: monarch retains substantial political authority under a democratic constitution (e.g., Liechtenstein, Monaco). The term "parliamentary monarchy" distinguishes ceremonial systems from semi-constitutional ones.

### Current Monarchies (as of 2025)
**Commonwealth realms (15):** Antigua and Barbuda, Australia, Bahamas, Belize, Canada, Grenada, Jamaica, New Zealand, Papua New Guinea, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, Solomon Islands, Tuvalu, United Kingdom—all share King Charles III as head of state, evolved from the British Empire.

**European constitutional monarchies:** Andorra (unique diarchy: French President and Bishop of Urgell as co-princes), Belgium, Denmark, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden—fully democratic with limited/ceremonial monarchs.

**European semi-constitutional monarchies:** Liechtenstein (Prince can veto laws, appoint/dismiss officials; 2003 constitution strengthened powers), Monaco (Prince appoints minister of state, government council, judges).

**Muslim world monarchies:** Bahrain, Brunei, Jordan, Kuwait, Malaysia, Morocco, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, UAE. Brunei, Oman, Saudi Arabia are absolute; Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar, UAE are "mixed" (representative bodies exist but monarch retains most power); Jordan, Malaysia, Morocco are constitutional.

**East/Southeast Asian constitutional monarchies:** Bhutan (transitioned 2008), Cambodia (restored 1993 after Khmer Rouge), Thailand (20th-century transition), Japan (world’s oldest continuous monarchy, legendary from 660 BCE; Emperor stripped of political power post-WWII, authority vested in National Diet).

**Other unique cases:** Eswatini (diarchy: King *Ngwenyama* as administrative head, Queen Mother *Ndlovukati* as spiritual head); Vatican City (Pope as elected absolute monarch, not hereditary); Samoa (parliamentary republic with hereditary *matai* chiefs dominating parliament, not a formal monarchy); North Korea (Kim family described as de facto hereditary dictatorship/absolute monarchy, enshrined in 2013 party rules, though officially a republic); Syria (al-Assad family 1971–2024 similarly categorized).

## Terms
- ****Monarchy**** — Government with a hereditary (or occasionally elective) head of state ruling for life.
- ****Dynasty**** — A royal family continuing across several generations.
- ****Primogeniture**** — Succession system where the eldest child inherits the throne.
- ****Agnatic primogeniture / Salic law**** — Succession limited to male-line descendants; females excluded.
- ****Semi-Salic law**** — Females may inherit only upon extinction of all male-line descendants.
- ****Agnatic seniority**** — Succession passes to the monarch’s eldest brother before the monarch’s children.
- ****Tanistry**** — Semi-elective hereditary system weighing merit and seniority.
- ****Elective monarchy**** — Monarch chosen for life or a term by an electoral college (e.g., Papacy, Malaysia).
- ****Constitutional monarchy**** — Monarch’s powers limited by constitution; usually ceremonial.
- ****Absolute monarchy**** — Monarch rules with unrestricted autocratic power.
- ****Semi-constitutional monarchy**** — Monarch retains substantial political powers under a democratic constitution.
- ****Queen regnant**** — Female monarch ruling in her own right (vs. *queen consort*, wife of a king).
- ****Diarchy**** — Two individuals sharing head-of-state authority (Andorra, Eswatini).
- ****Commonwealth realms**** — Independent sovereign states sharing the British monarch as head of state.

## Debates and open questions
- **Efficiency origin vs. obsolescence:** The 2020 study posits monarchy arose from coordination efficiency in large pre-modern territories and declined when communications/transport technology reduced that advantage. The 2023 study counters that monarchy persists because it uniquely accommodates democratization without leadership destabilization. These explanations are not mutually exclusive but emphasize different causal mechanisms (structural efficiency vs. strategic adaptability).
- **Classification boundaries:** Samoa’s *matai*-based parliament and North Korea’s hereditary dictatorship blur the line between republic and monarchy. The source notes North Korea is *de facto* monarchic but *de jure* a republic; Samoa is constitutionally a parliamentary republic despite hereditary political elites.
- **Future of semi-constitutional monarchies:** Whether states like Liechtenstein and Monaco will follow the European trend toward purely ceremonial monarchy or retain political powers remains open.
- **Succession reform momentum:** Equal primogeniture has spread across Europe since 1980, but many Muslim-world and Asian monarchies retain male-preference or agnatic systems; pressure for further reform is unresolved.
