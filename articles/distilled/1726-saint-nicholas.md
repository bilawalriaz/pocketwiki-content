# Saint Nicholas

## Overview
Saint Nicholas of Myra (c. 270–343) was a Greek Christian bishop in the Roman province of Lycia (modern Turkey) whose reputation for secret charity and miracle-working made him one of Christianity’s most venerated saints. Almost nothing about him is historically certain; the earliest surviving biography was written nearly 500 years after his death and likely incorporates legends from pagan figures such as Apollonius of Tyana. His cult expanded dramatically after Italian merchants translated his major relics from Myra to Bari in 1087, making him accessible to Western pilgrims and Crusaders. From his Dutch epithet *Sinterklaas* evolved the modern figure of Santa Claus.

## Timeline
- **c. 270** — Traditional birth in Patara, Lycia (Asia Minor).
- **c. 300–310** — Pilgrimage to Egypt and Palestine; elected Bishop of Myra upon return.
- **303–311** — Imprisoned during Diocletianic Persecution; released under Constantine.
- **325** — Listed as attendee at First Council of Nicaea (attested only in later lists; absent from contemporary accounts).
- **6 Dec 343** — Traditional date of death in Myra.
- **c. 450** — Theodosius II orders Church of St. Nicholas built in Myra over his tomb.
- **c. 510–515** — Theodore Lector includes "Nicholas of Myra of Lycia" on a list of Nicaea attendees.
- **9th c.** — Michael the Archimandrite writes earliest complete surviving *Life of Saint Nicholas*.
- **1087** — Merchants from Bari remove major bones from Myra; enshrined in new Basilica di San Nicola (1089).
- **1100** — Venetian sailors take remaining bone fragments from Myra to San Nicolò al Lido during First Crusade.
- **1950s** — Scientific examination of Bari relics reveals male, ~70+ years, 1.68 m tall, severe arthritis, healed broken nose.
- **2017** — Radiocarbon dating of a pelvis fragment (Lyon) confirms 4th-century origin; anatomical compatibility with Bari/Venice remains suggested.

## Body

### Historical Sources and Scholarly Debate
No contemporary writings mention Nicholas. The earliest references (6th century) indicate an established cult: Theodosius II built a church over his tomb in Myra (c. 450), Procopius notes Justinian I renovated Constantinopolitan churches dedicated to him (c. 490), and Theodore Lector lists him at Nicaea (510–515). Eustratius of Constantinople (c. 583) cites a lost *Life* describing the "three generals" miracle. The earliest complete biography is by Michael the Archimandrite (early 9th century), ~500 years post-mortem. Historians dispute its reliability: Cann and Jones consider it the only account with historical truth; Lendering notes its lack of a "conversion narrative" suggests reliance on pre-9th-century sources, but also observes striking parallels with Philostratus’s 3rd-century *Life of Apollonius of Tyana* (e.g., saving innocents from execution, giving dowries), arguing Christian hagiographers adapted pagan stories.

### Family, Generosity, and Early Travels
Tradition places Nicholas’s birth in Patara to wealthy Greek Christians (parents named either Epiphanius/Johanna or Theophanes/Nonna). After their deaths in an epidemic, he distributed their wealth. The signature legend—first attested in Michael the Archimandrite—describes him secretly throwing three purses of gold through a window over three nights to provide dowries for three daughters of an impoverished man, saving them from prostitution. English argues for a historical kernel (early attestation, unique among saints); Lendering contrasts it with Apollonius’s similar act, noting Nicholas’s explicit motive to save women aligns with 4th-century Christianity’s elevated view of women. Nicholas reportedly calmed a storm at sea (becoming patron of sailors) and lived in a crypt near Bethlehem (now Church of St. Nicholas, Beit Jala).

### Bishop of Myra and Miracles
Elected bishop when he was the first priest to enter the church after the incumbent’s death. Imprisonment under Diocletian and release under Constantine is plausible but unattested in early sources. Early miracles include: (1) halting the execution of three innocent men condemned by Governor Eustathius, pushing the executioner’s sword down and exposing a bribed juror; (2) appearing in dreams to Constantine and Consul Ablabius to free three generals (Ursos, Nepotianos, Herpylion) falsely accused of treason. Later versions interweave these: the generals aid Nicholas in stopping Eustathius’s execution, then are themselves imprisoned by Ablabius until Nicholas’s dream intervention. Both stories parallel Apollonius legends.

### Council of Nicaea (325)
Nicholas appears on three early attendee lists (Theodore Lector, 151st name), but only on longer lists (~300 names), not shorter ones (~200). He is absent from accounts by Athanasius and Eusebius, who were present. Two scholarly possibilities: (1) he did not attend, but a later editor added him because his absence seemed odd; (2) he attended, but his name was later removed. A 14th-century legend claims he slapped Arius, was defrocked and imprisoned, then restored by Christ and Mary. Greydanus dismisses it as historically valueless due to late attestation; Lendering defends it as an embarrassing detail unlikely to be invented. The scene appears in Eastern Orthodox icons and 1660s paintings in Bari’s basilica.

### Later Miracles: Famine and Resurrection
During a famine (311–312), Nicholas persuaded sailors to offload wheat for Myra; the cargo weight miraculously remained unchanged upon delivery to Constantinople. A late medieval legend (absent from early *Lives*) tells of a butcher pickling three murdered children in brine to sell as pork; Nicholas resurrected them with the sign of the cross. Lendering calls it historically valueless; English notes it became wildly popular in the Late Middle Ages, driving iconography of Nicholas with three children and a barrel—later misinterpreted as patronage of children and brewers.

### Relics: Translation, Division, and Scientific Analysis
**Gemile:** Recent archaeology suggests Nicholas may have been first entombed in a 4th-century rock-cut church on Gemile Island ("St. Nicholas Island"), 20 miles from Patara.  
**Myra:** Mid-7th century Arab naval threats prompted transfer to Myra (40 km inland). Relics exuded *manna* (myrrh), believed miraculous.  
**Bari (1087):** Amid Seljuk Turkish conquest of Anatolia and the 1054 East–West Schism, Bari merchants seized the major bones over Greek Orthodox objections. English calls it a "holy robbery"; Bari calls it a rescue. Pope Urban II enshrined them in the Basilica di San Nicola (1089). The translation sparked Western veneration; Crusaders mustering at Bari (1096) spread his cult. Manna still seeps annually (extracted 6 Dec); natural explanations include capillary seawater transfer (tomb is below sea level).  
**Venice (1100):** Venetian fleet en route to First Crusade took remaining fragments to San Nicolò al Lido. Scientific studies confirm Bari and Venice remains are anatomically compatible (same individual).  
**Dispersal:** Small fragments distributed across Europe (Normandy, Flanders, Lorraine, Rome, etc.). Irish tradition claims relics at Thomastown, Co. Kilkenny (likely a local priest). 2024: Russian Orthodox Church gifted a relic to Baku.  
**Science:** 1950s examination (Luigi Martino) found male, 70+, 1.68 m, slender, severe spinal/pelvic arthritis, healed broken nose (consistent with Diocletianic torture). 2004/2014 facial reconstructions (Wilkinson, Face Lab). 2017 radiocarbon dating of a pelvis fragment (Lyon) confirmed 4th-century origin; Higham and Kazan suggest it may belong to the Bari/Venice individual (left pubis vs. Bari’s left ilium), but DNA testing is lacking.

### Veneration, Celebrations, and Santa Claus Evolution
Patron of sailors, merchants, children, prisoners, etc. Eastern Orthodox: celebrated Thursdays (Octoechos), feast days for translation (9, 10, 20 May), nativity (29 Jul), death (6 Dec). Western: 6 Dec (Julian 19 Dec). Medieval England: "boy bishop" ceremonies. Belgium/France: nuns left anonymous gifts for the needy on 6 Dec. Low Countries: sailors bought gifts at Nicholas fairs for children, establishing him as gift-giver. Dutch *Sinterklaas* brought to New Amsterdam; evolved into Santa Claus (St. Nicholas Ave/Terrace/Park in Harlem recall Dutch origins). Honored in Lutheran, Anglican, and Episcopal calendars (6 Dec).

### Iconography
Eastern Orthodox: elderly bishop, white fluffy beard, balding, omophorion, Gospel Book; sometimes Christ (Gospel) and Theotokos (omophorion) over shoulders referencing Nicaea legend; occasionally in boat rescuing sailors. Bari depictions: dark-skinned, emphasizing foreign origin to enhance city’s prestige. Roman Catholic: bishop’s vestments, mitre, crozier; three purses/coins/balls for dowry miracle. Three gold balls later interpreted as oranges, spawning legend he comes from Spain with winter fruit.

## Terms
- **Arianism**: 4th-century Christological doctrine (denying Son’s co-eternity with Father) opposed at Nicaea.
- **Diocletianic Persecution** (303–311): Empire-wide suppression of Christianity under Diocletian; Nicholas traditionally imprisoned then.
- **Manna / Myrrh**: Clear, rose-scented liquid exuding from Nicholas’s relics, collected annually in Bari; believed miraculous.
- **Nicene Creed**: Statement of Trinitarian faith adopted at First Council of Nicaea (325); Nicholas traditionally a signer.
- **Sinterklaas**: Dutch folkloric figure based on Saint Nicholas; direct predecessor of Santa Claus.
- **Translation (of relics)**: Ceremonial transfer of a saint’s remains to a new shrine; Bari celebrates 9 May as Nicholas’s translation.
- **Octoechos**: Eastern Orthodox liturgical book containing hymns for the eight tones; includes weekly Thursday hymns to Nicholas.
- **Slava**: Serbian annual family patron-saint feast; Nicholas (19 Dec) is the most common.
- **Apollonius of Tyana**: 1st-century Neopythagorean philosopher; Philostratus’s *Life* (early 3rd c.) shares multiple motifs with Nicholas legends.
- **Boy Bishop**: Medieval English custom (6 Dec) where a boy assumed episcopal functions during Christmastide.

## Debates and open questions
- **Historicity**: No contemporary evidence; earliest *Life* is 9th-century. How much, if any, narrative reflects a real 4th-century bishop?
- **Nicaea attendance**: Name on longer lists only; absent from Athanasius/Eusebius. Did he attend, was he added later, or removed later?
- **Arius slap**: 14th-century legend. Embarrassing detail (supports authenticity per Lendering) vs. extreme lateness (rejects per Greydanus).
- **Relic authenticity**: Bari and Venice remains are anatomically compatible; 2017 pelvis fragment is 4th-century. Without DNA, cannot confirm all belong to the same historical individual.
- **Manna origin**: Miraculous exudate vs. capillary seawater infiltration (tomb below sea level in harbor city).
- **Gemile vs. Myra as first burial**: Archaeological evidence (4th-century church, name painted, "St. Nicholas Island" toponym) challenges traditional Myra-first view.
- **Legendary borrowing**: Extent to which Nicholas’s *Life* adapts Apollonius of Tyana stories (dowry gift, saving condemned, demon tree) vs. independent Christian tradition.
