# Bharatanatyam

## Overview
Bharatanatyam is a classical Indian dance form originating from Tamil Nadu, India, that expresses South Indian religious and spiritual themes, particularly of Shaivism and Hinduism. Recognized by the Sangeet Natak Akademi, it is considered the oldest classical dance tradition in India, with theoretical foundations in ancient texts. Its modern revival in the 20th century transformed it from a temple-based art into a globally performed classical tradition, symbolizing Indian cultural identity.

## Timeline
- **c. 500 BCE – 500 CE** — The *Natya Shastra*, a foundational Sanskrit text on performance arts, is compiled, describing dance theory, gestures, and postures.
- **c. 171 CE** — The Tamil epic *Silappatikaram* references dance, describing a dancer's training regimen.
- **6th–9th centuries CE** — Temple sculptures (e.g., Kanchipuram) suggest dance was a refined performance art by the mid-1st millennium CE.
- **c. 12th century** — The southern gateway of the Chidambaram temple is carved with 108 *karanas* (dance poses) from the *Natya Shastra*.
- **1892** — The "anti-dance movement" is launched by Christian missionaries and British officials, accusing the dance of being a front for prostitution.
- **1910** — The British colonial government in the Madras Presidency bans temple dancing, leading to a decline in the tradition.
- **1932** — E. Krishna Iyer and Rukmini Devi Arundale propose renaming the dance from *Sadiraattam* to *Bharatanatyam* to restore respect.
- **1955** — State-sponsored dance festivals are introduced in independent India, showcasing Bharatanatyam on a national platform.

## Body

### Origins and Theoretical Foundations
The theoretical basis of Bharatanatyam is found in the *Natya Shastra* (c. 500 BCE–500 CE) and the Tamil text *Kootha nool* from the *Tholkappiyam* (250 BCE). The *Natya Shastra* describes the theory of *Tāṇḍava* dance (associated with Shiva), *rasa* (sentiment), *bhāva* (expression), gestures, and postures, framing dance as a spiritual expression. Historical references appear in Tamil epics like *Silappatikaram* (c. 2nd century CE) and *Manimegalai* (c. 6th century CE). Temple sculptures from the 6th to 12th centuries, such as the 108 *karanas* at Chidambaram, illustrate the dance's ancient roots and its connection to Shaivite iconography.

### Colonial Decline and Anti-Dance Movement
During British colonial rule, classical Indian dance forms were ridiculed and discouraged. Christian missionaries and colonial officials labeled the dance as indecent and associated it with prostitution, launching the "anti-dance movement" in 1892. This culminated in a 1910 ban on temple dancing by the Madras Presidency, which severely disrupted the tradition. The ban stemmed from colonial perspectives that viewed the dance as incompatible with 19th-century ideals of modernity and morality.

### Post-Colonial Revival and Standardization
The 1910 ban sparked protests and a revival movement led by Indian nationalists and artists. Figures like E. Krishna Iyer and Rukmini Devi Arundale championed the dance, questioning colonial stereotypes and working to decouple it from its association with devadasis (temple dancers). The revival was influenced by Western ideas of democratizing art, aiming to make it accessible to middle- and upper-class women. Rukmini Devi Arundale is credited with developing the Kalakshetra style and helping standardize the dance. The Thanjavur Quartet had earlier formalized the basic structure of modern Bharatanatyam. By the mid-20th century, it was successfully revived as a mainstream classical art, becoming a hallmark of Indian national identity.

### Performance Structure and Elements
Bharatanatyam is traditionally a solo performance (*ekaharya*) accompanied by Carnatic music and a singer, often the dancer's guru acting as the *nattuvanar* (conductor). The repertoire follows three categories from the *Natya Shastra*: *nritta* (pure, abstract dance), *nritya* (expressive dance conveying meaning through gestures), and *natya* (dramatic dance). A full performance, like an *arangetram* (debut), follows a set sequence (*margam*) including pieces like *Pushpanjali* (offering), *Jatiswaram* (pure dance with melody), *Varnam* (the core, expressive centerpiece), and *Tillana* (climactic pure dance). The style is noted for its fixed upper torso, bent legs (*aramandi*), and a vocabulary of hand gestures (*hasta* or *mudra*).

### Costume and Symbolism
The costume resembles a Tamil Hindu bridal dress, with a brightly colored sari, elaborate jewelry, and bells (*salangai*) on the ankles. Male dancers typically wear a white *dhoti*. Symbolism is central, with gestures and expressions conveying *rasa* (emotional taste) and *bhava* (mood) from Hindu epics. Communication is achieved through four aspects: *Angika* (body), *Vachika* (voice/music), *Aharya* (costume/makeup), and *Sattvika* (emotional connection). The dance incorporates elements of yoga and is considered a form of *Bhakti Yoga* (devotional practice).

## Terms
- **Bani**: A term describing the dance technique and style specific to a guru or school.
- **Aramandi**: The basic standing position in Bharatanatyam, characterized by bent knees and a flexed posture.
- **Nritta**: Pure, abstract dance focusing on rhythmic movement and form without narrative.
- **Nritya**: Expressive dance that uses gestures and expressions to convey a story or emotion.
- **Natya**: Dramatic dance that incorporates elements of drama and storytelling.
- **Hasta/Mudra**: Symbolic hand gestures used to communicate meaning and emotions.
- **Abhinaya**: The art of expression in Indian aesthetics, using body, speech, and mind to convey meaning.
- **Rasa**: The emotional taste or sentiment evoked in the audience through a performance.
- **Bhava**: The mood or psychological state expressed by the dancer.
- **Arangetram**: A solo debut performance marking the completion of a dancer's initial training.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Devadasi Origins**: Some scholars argue Bharatanatyam descends from ancient *devadasi* (temple dancer) culture, while modern scholars question this due to lack of direct evidence, suggesting courtesan dancing is a later phenomenon from the 16th–17th centuries.
- **Colonial Impact**: The extent to which the colonial ban and anti-dance movement shaped the modern revival and "sanitization" of the dance is debated, with revivalists both resisting and incorporating Western influences.
- **Authenticity vs. Evolution**: The standardization of Bharatanatyam in the 20th century and its adaptation to modern stages (including non-religious themes) raise questions about balancing tradition with contemporary expression.
