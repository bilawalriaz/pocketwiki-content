# Capital city

## Overview
A capital city is the primary municipality that serves as the seat of government for a country or subnational division, typically housing its central political institutions. Its status is often defined by law or constitution, though many long-standing capitals are recognized by convention. The selection, location, and symbolism of capitals are deeply tied to a nation's history, politics, and identity.

## Timeline
- **c. 2500 BC** — Damascus is cited as the earliest existing capital.
- **1st century BC** — The Latin phrase "Roma Caput Mundi" (Rome, head of the world) is used by the poet Ovid.
- **1682–1789** — The Palace of Versailles serves as the seat of government for the Kingdom of France, while Paris remains the traditional capital.
- **1790** — The U.S. Residence Act approves the creation of Washington, D.C. as a compromise capital.
- **1812** — Helsinki replaces Turku as the capital of Finland under the Russian Empire.
- **1834** — The ruined city of Athens is made the capital of newly independent Greece.
- **1865** — Wellington becomes the capital of New Zealand, replacing Auckland for a more central location.
- **1911** — Delhi is formally made the capital of British India.
- **1997** — Akmola (renamed Astana in 1998) becomes the capital of Kazakhstan.
- **2005** — Naypyidaw is founded and designated the capital of Myanmar.

## Body

### Origins and Terminology
The word "capital" derives from the Latin *caput*, meaning "head." Historically, major economic centers often became political capitals through conquest or federation, as seen with ancient Babylon, Rome, and Constantinople. In medieval Western Europe, governments were often itinerant (wandering). Capitals attract skilled administrators and may also be religious centers, like Rome or Jerusalem. The earliest known capital is Damascus (c. 2500 BC). While many modern capitals are legally designated, others like London and Paris are capitals by convention.

### Modern Capitals and Their Location
Most modern capitals are located near a country's geographic center for accessibility and defense, or as a compromise between rival cities. Examples of compromise capitals include Canberra (Australia), Washington, D.C. (U.S.), and Ottawa (Canada). In federations like Germany, Russia, and the U.S., each state has its own capital. In unitary states with multiple constituent nations, like the UK, one city (London) often serves as both the national and a constituent capital.

### Planned Capitals
Governments sometimes build new, planned capitals to house the seat of government. These are often created to relieve overcrowding in an old capital or as a geographic compromise. Examples include Brasília, Canberra, and Washington, D.C. The latter was founded via the Compromise of 1790 to balance power between Northern and Southern states.

### Unusual Arrangements
Some nations have multiple capitals or separate the official capital from the seat of government. South Africa has three capitals: Pretoria (administrative), Cape Town (legislative), and Bloemfontein (judicial). In others, like Bolivia (Sucre vs. La Paz) and the Netherlands (Amsterdam vs. The Hague), the constitutional capital differs from where the government primarily operates. A few states, like Monaco and Singapore, are city-states with no distinct capital. Disputed capitals exist in divided territories, such as Nicosia in Cyprus and Jerusalem, claimed by both Israel and Palestine.

### Symbolism and Military Strategy
Capitals are powerful symbols of the state. Their selection can be highly symbolic, such as making ruined Athens the capital of independent Greece or moving the Russian capital to European-oriented Saint Petersburg. A "forward capital" is placed in a peripheral location for strategic or economic reasons, like Islamabad or Naypyidaw. Militarily, capturing a capital was historically decisive in highly centralized states (e.g., ancient China), but less so in decentralized ones where resistance could continue from provinces.

## Terms
- **Capital City**: The municipality holding primary status as the seat of government for a country or subnational division.
- **Seat of Government**: The physical location where a government's offices and meeting places are concentrated.
- **Unitary State**: A state governed as a single entity, where the central government is supreme.
- **Federation**: A political entity characterized by a union of partially self-governing states or regions under a central government.
- **Itinerant Government**: A government that moves from place to place, common in medieval Europe.
- **Forward Capital**: A capital city deliberately located in a geographically or demographically peripheral area for strategic or economic reasons.
- **Compromise Capital**: A new capital city chosen as a neutral location between two or more rival cities or regions.
- **De facto Capital**: A capital that is the actual seat of government in practice, even if not the official or constitutional capital.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The final status of Jerusalem is a major point of contention, with most countries considering it unsettled pending negotiations.
- The legal status of capitals is debated in some countries; for example, the Portuguese constitution does not mention Lisbon as the capital.
- The effectiveness of relocating capitals (e.g., Myanmar to Naypyidaw, Kazakhstan to Astana) for political or economic goals is an ongoing discussion.
