# Trajan

## Overview
Trajan (r. 98–117 AD) was a Roman emperor remembered as one of the "Five Good Emperors" and for presiding over the Roman Empire's greatest territorial expansion. A military emperor from Hispania, his reign combined massive building projects, social welfare programs, and ambitious conquests that pushed the empire to its maximum extent, though his later Parthian campaign proved overextended and was largely reversed by his successor.

## Timeline
- **18 September 53** — Born Marcus Ulpius Traianus in Italica, Hispania Baetica
- **89 AD** — Supports Emperor Domitian against revolt of Antonius Saturninus
- **91 AD** — Serves as ordinary consul
- **97 AD** — Adopted as heir by Emperor Nerva
- **28 January 98** — Succeeds Nerva as emperor
- **101–102** — First Dacian War; reduces Dacia to client kingdom
- **105–106** — Second Dacian War; annexes Dacia as Roman province
- **106** — Annexes Nabataean Kingdom, creating province of Arabia Petraea
- **113–117** — Parthian campaign; conquers Armenia, Mesopotamia, reaches Persian Gulf
- **116–117** — Diaspora Revolt in eastern provinces
- **c. 9 August 117** — Dies at Selinus while returning to Rome

## Body

### Rise to Power
Born to a distinguished senatorial family of Italic origin settled in Spain, Trajan rose through military ranks on Rome's frontiers. He gained prominence supporting Domitian against revolt in 89 AD and later served as governor of Germania and Pannonia. When the elderly, childless Nerva faced a Praetorian Guard revolt in 96 AD, he adopted Trajan as successor in 97 AD—possibly under pressure, as some sources suggest. Trajan's accession in 98 AD was orderly, though he first toured the Rhine and Danube frontiers rather than rushing to Rome, and had the hostile Praetorian Prefect Aelianus executed.

### Relations with the Senate and Greek East
Trajan cultivated an image of republican virtue while concentrating power. He encouraged the Senate to "share" imperial governance but made key provinces imperial to bypass senatorial mismanagement. The Senate awarded him the title *optimus princeps* ("best ruler"). In the Greek East, Trajan managed local oligarchies carefully: he recalled exiles, returned confiscated property, but appointed *correctores* (imperial auditors) to curb extravagant civic spending that threatened solvency. He admitted over fourteen Eastern notables to the Senate, blending traditional Roman rule with pragmatic inclusion of wealthy Greek elites.

### Building Projects and Social Programs
Trajan was a prolific builder, with architect Apollodorus of Damascus designing his Forum—the largest in Rome—financed by Dacian war booty. He built a massive Danube bridge, roads including the Via Traiana, and expanded the Circus Maximus. His *alimenta* welfare program provided cash, food, and subsidized education to poor Italian children, funded through Dacian spoils and estate taxes. In 107, he devalued the denarius (reducing silver content from 93.5% to 89.0%), enabling increased military and civil spending.

### Conquest of Dacia
Dacia, under King Decebalus, had been an unstable client kingdom since Domitian's reign. Trajan's first war (101–102) forced Decebalus to accept client status, but Decebalus soon rearmed and plotted against Rome. The second war (105–106) ended with the destruction of Dacia's capital Sarmizegetusa, Decebalus's suicide, and annexation of the territory as a province. The conquest yielded enormous wealth—over half a million slaves and rich gold mines—and was commemorated by Trajan's Column in Rome. The province, garrisoned by one legion, served as a forward base but was eventually abandoned during the third-century crisis.

### Parthian Campaign and Death
In 113, Trajan invaded Parthia over the Armenian succession, possibly motivated by territorial ambition, trade route control, or emulation of Alexander the Great. He annexed Armenia, then conquered Mesopotamia and Assyria, reaching the Persian Gulf in 116—where he declared he was "too old" to match Alexander's conquests. However, revolts erupted across the newly conquered territories and in the Jewish diaspora. Trajan's health failed during the siege of Hatra; he died at Selinus in August 117. His successor Hadrian immediately abandoned Mesopotamia as indefensible, though other conquests were retained.

## Terms
- **Optimus princeps**: "Best ruler," an honorary title bestowed by the Senate on Trajan, appearing on coins from 105 AD
- **Alimenta**: A Roman welfare program providing cash, food, and education to poor children in Italy, formalized under Trajan
- **Correctores**: Imperial auditors appointed by Trajan to oversee finances of nominally free Greek cities
- **Rex amicus**: "Friend king," a term for client kings who ruled under Roman suzerainty
- **Tetarte**: A 25% tax on goods entering the Roman Empire
- **Second Sophistic**: A cultural movement of Greek literary revival during the Roman Imperial period
- **Denarius**: Standard Roman silver coin; Trajan reduced its silver content in 107 AD
- **Legatus legionis**: A legion commander, typically of senatorial rank
- **Praetorian Guard**: Elite Roman military unit serving as the emperor's bodyguard
- **Dacicus**: A victory title awarded to Trajan after his first Dacian War

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Motives for the Parthian War**: Scholars debate whether Trajan sought control of Indian trade routes, territorial expansion, strategic defensibility, or personal glory. Some historians consider commercial motives anachronistic.
- **Nerva's adoption of Trajan**: Was it voluntary or coerced by military pressure? Pliny's writings hint at imposition, and some scholars call the "natural continuity" between reigns an after-the-fact fiction.
- **Pliny's correspondence**: Whether Pliny's letters to Trajan reflect genuine intimacy or merely official, possibly secretary-drafted mail remains disputed.
- **Diaspora Revolt scope**: Whether the 116–117 Jewish revolt included Judea proper or only eastern diaspora communities lacks clear archaeological evidence.
- **Trajan's succession**: Dio claims Plotina forged or delayed Trajan's death to ensure Hadrian's adoption—possibly reflecting contemporary rumor or gendered political anxieties.
- **Legacy assessment**: Historians remain divided between viewing Trajan as an ideal ruler (Paribeni, Heuss) versus an overambitious militarist (Gibbon, Mommsen, Veyne).
