# Chavín culture

## Overview
The Chavín culture was a pre-Columbian civilization that flourished in the northern Andean highlands of Peru from approximately 900 to 250 BCE. It represents the main culture of the Early Horizon period, characterized by a widespread religious cult, advanced architecture, and a distinctive artistic style that influenced many contemporary societies along the Peruvian coast and highlands. Its ceremonial center, Chavín de Huántar, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and a key to understanding early Andean social complexity and religious authority.

## Timeline
- **c. 900–500 BCE** — Construction of the "Old Temple" at Chavín de Huántar; first phase of Chavín art.
- **c. 900–500 BCE** — Urabarriu stage: dispersed population, diverse diet, ceramics influenced by other cultures.
- **c. 500–400 BCE** — Chakinani stage: population migrates to surround the ceremonial center; increased trade.
- **c. 500–200 BCE** — Construction of the "New Temple"; second phase of Chavín art.
- **c. 400–250 BCE** — Jarabarriu stage: dramatic population increase, proto-urban settlement, social specialization.
- **c. 250 BCE** — End of the Chavín cultural horizon.

## Body

### Achievements and Economy
The Chavín demonstrated advanced engineering, notably at Chavín de Huántar, with a complex drainage system that used water flow to create roaring sounds emulating a jaguar. They used non-local materials like white granite and black limestone, requiring organized labor for transport. Metallurgy included refined gold work and soldering. The domestication of camelids (llamas) was crucial; they were used as pack animals and for fiber and meat. The production of *ch'arki* (llama jerky) was a main economic resource for trade. Agriculture focused on potatoes, quinoa, and maize, supported by irrigation systems.

### Architecture
Chavín de Huántar is the primary site, featuring both internal (galleries, passages) and external (plazas, mounds) architecture. The "Old Temple" (c. 900–500 BCE) and "New Temple" (c. 500–200 BCE) were built in at least 15 phases. The design incorporated a U-shaped arrangement with an east-west axis. Internal galleries were labyrinthine, windowless, and designed to disorient visitors. Key artifacts like the Lanzón were integrated into the architecture. Construction used quartzite, sandstone, granite, and limestone, with platforms built over earlier debris.

### Art and Iconography
Chavín art is the first widespread, recognizable style in the Andes, known for "mythical realism" and complex iconography featuring human-animal interactions. It often depicted exotic creatures like jaguars and eagles. The technique of "contour rivalry" made designs intentionally difficult to interpret, likely for elite priests. Major artifacts include the Tello Obelisk (possibly depicting a creation myth), tenon heads (projecting fanged jaguar carvings), and the 4.53-meter Lanzón (a fanged deity shaft in the Old Temple). Art frequently showed the use of psychoactive plants like the San Pedro cactus.

### Sphere of Influence
The Chavín culture had a wide influence, stretching from Piura to Paracas and from the northern to southern highlands. This is evidenced by Chavín-style renovations and iconography at distant sites like Pacopampa, Caballo Muerto, and Garagay. Warfare was not a significant element; social control may have been exercised through religious pressure and control of water resources. Evidence of warfare is found only in contemporaneous sites outside Chavín influence.

### Social Structure and Religion
Power was held by a shaman elite who legitimized authority through claimed divine connections. The use of psychotropic drugs, complex rituals, and restricted access to sacred galleries supported this hierarchy. Ritual spaces evolved from public plazas to private galleries, indicating a shift toward more exclusive practices. Rituals included processions, offerings (e.g., smashed obsidian, ceramics), fire ceremonies, and music using strombus shell trumpets. The Lanzón deity was central, possibly an ancestral oracle. Art reflected hybrid deities and cosmological binaries.

## Terms
- **Early Horizon**: The period in Andean chronology (c. 900–200 BCE) characterized by the spread of the Chavín religious style.
- **Chavín de Huántar**: The primary archaeological site of the Chavín culture, featuring monumental temple architecture.
- **Contour rivalry**: An artistic technique where lines can be interpreted in multiple ways, making designs complex and esoteric.
- **Tenon heads**: Stone carvings of fanged heads that project from walls, a hallmark of Chavín art.
- **Lanzón**: A 4.53-meter carved granite shaft depicting a fanged deity, central to the Old Temple.
- **Tello Obelisk**: A carved stone shaft possibly depicting a creation myth with "cat-dragon" or cayman figures.
- **Psychotropic drugs**: Psychoactive substances, such as mescaline from the San Pedro cactus, used in rituals.
- **U-shaped pattern**: A common architectural layout for Chavín temples, enclosing a central plaza.
- **Proto-urban**: A settlement pattern with a central ceremonial core and surrounding satellite communities.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact nature of Chavín language is unknown; some propose it was a form of Proto-Quechuan, while others date Proto-Quechuan to a later period.
- The function of the underground galleries is debated: were they primarily for exclusive rituals, or also for burial (as suggested by 2018 findings of face-down bodies)?
- The role of maize in the Chavín economy is debated; carbon isotope analysis shows the diet was mainly C3 plants (potatoes, quinoa), not maize.
- The extent to which psychotropic drugs were used—only by elites or more widely—and their precise role in rituals is unclear.
- Whether Chavín de Huántar was the culture's birthplace or just a major center is uncertain.
