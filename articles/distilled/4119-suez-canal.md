# Suez Canal

## Overview
The Suez Canal is a 193.3 km artificial sea-level waterway in Egypt connecting the Mediterranean and Red Seas. It is a critical global trade route, dividing Africa and Asia, and significantly shortens maritime journeys between Europe and Asia. Its construction, control, and strategic importance have been central to geopolitical and economic history since the 19th century.

## Timeline
- **c. 1878–1839 BCE** — Earliest attempts to link the Nile and Red Sea via canals, attributed to Pharaohs Senusret III or Ramesses II.
- **c. 610–595 BCE** — Pharaoh Necho II begins a canal project but does not complete it.
- **c. 522–486 BCE** — Persian King Darius I completes a canal from the Nile to the Red Sea.
- **1854–1856** — Ferdinand de Lesseps obtains a concession from Egypt to build a modern canal.
- **1859–1869** — Construction of the Suez Canal by the Suez Canal Company.
- **17 November 1869** — The Suez Canal officially opens.
- **26 July 1956** — Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalizes the canal.
- **October–November 1956** — The Suez Crisis: UK, France, and Israel invade Egypt; canal is blocked.
- **5 June 1967** — Egypt closes the canal at the start of the Six-Day War; it remains shut for 8 years.
- **6 October 1973** — Egyptian army crosses the canal in Operation Badr during the Yom Kippur War.
- **5 June 1975** — The canal reopens after extensive mine-clearing operations.
- **6 August 2015** — The expanded "New Suez Canal" opens, allowing two-way traffic in a central section.
- **23–29 March 2021** — The container ship *Ever Given* runs aground, blocking the canal for six days.

## Body

### Precursors and Ancient Canals
The idea of a waterway between the Mediterranean and Red Sea is ancient. Canals linking the Nile to the Red Sea via Wadi Tumilat were built and rebuilt by various rulers, including Darius I, Ptolemy II, and Trajan. These canals fell into disuse due to silt accumulation and political changes. Napoleon's 1798 expedition rediscovered remnants of an ancient canal but mistakenly concluded the Red Sea was higher than the Mediterranean, making a sea-level canal seem impossible.

### Construction and Opening (1854–1869)
French diplomat Ferdinand de Lesseps secured a concession from Egypt's ruler Sa'id Pasha to form the Suez Canal Company. Despite British opposition, construction began in 1859. The project used forced labor (corvée) until 1864 and employed tens of thousands of workers. The canal, designed without locks, opened on 17 November 1869. It immediately transformed global trade by providing a direct route between Europe and Asia, bypassing the long voyage around Africa.

### Control, Nationalization, and Conflict
The canal was operated by a European-owned company until 1956, when President Nasser nationalized it to fund the Aswan Dam. This triggered the Suez Crisis, a military conflict involving the UK, France, and Israel. The canal was blocked and later cleared with UN assistance. It was closed again from 1967 to 1975 following the Six-Day War and the subsequent War of Attrition. After the 1973 Yom Kippur War, international mine-clearing operations allowed its reopening in 1975. The canal is now operated by the state-owned Suez Canal Authority.

### Modern Expansion and Operation
The canal's capacity has been repeatedly increased. A major expansion completed in 2015 created a new 35 km parallel channel, allowing two-way traffic and aiming to double daily ship transit capacity. The canal is 193.3 km long, 24 m deep, and 205 m wide. Ships transit in scheduled convoys, a process taking 11–16 hours. In 2021, over 20,600 vessels used the canal. A significant incident occurred in March 2021 when the *Ever Given* container ship ran aground, blocking global trade for six days.

### Economic and Environmental Impact
The canal dramatically reduced shipping times between Europe and Asia, boosting Mediterranean ports and global commerce. It is a vital choke point for global trade, especially for oil. Environmentally, the canal created a salt-water corridor between two seas, leading to "Lessepsian migration"—the colonization of the eastern Mediterranean by over a thousand Red Sea species, which has significantly altered the local ecosystem.

## Terms
- **Sea-level waterway**: A canal built at the same level as the connecting seas, requiring no locks.
- **Corvée**: Forced labor, used extensively during the canal's initial construction.
- **Suez Canal Company**: The original European-owned concessionary company that built and operated the canal until its nationalization in 1956.
- **Suez Crisis (1956)**: A military conflict triggered by Egypt's nationalization of the canal, involving the UK, France, and Israel.
- **Lessepsian migration**: The migration of marine species from the Red Sea to the Mediterranean via the Suez Canal, named after Ferdinand de Lesseps.
- **Suezmax**: The maximum size of ship that can transit the Suez Canal when fully laden.
- **Convoy system**: The method of organizing ship traffic through the canal in scheduled groups.
- **New Suez Canal**: A major expansion project completed in 2015, adding a parallel channel to allow two-way traffic.

## Debates and open questions
- **Historical death toll**: Estimates of worker deaths during construction vary widely, from under 1,000 to 120,000, with modern analysis suggesting the lower end of the range.
- **Environmental impact**: The long-term ecological consequences of the ongoing Lessepsian migration and the impact of the 2015 expansion on species transfer are subjects of ongoing scientific concern and study.
- **Strategic alternatives**: The viability of alternative trade routes, such as the Northern Sea Route through the Arctic or land-based "dry canals," as competitors to the Suez Canal is a recurring topic in geopolitical and economic analysis.
