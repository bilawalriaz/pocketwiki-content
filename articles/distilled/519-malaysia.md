# Malaysia

## Overview
Malaysia is a federal constitutional elective monarchy in Southeast Asia, comprising 13 states and three federal territories split by the South China Sea into Peninsular Malaysia and East Malaysia (Borneo). A former British colony that gained independence in 1957 and formed the current federation in 1963, it is a multiethnic, megadiverse nation of over 34 million people. Its politics are defined by the *bumiputera* system—constitutionally enshrined affirmative action favouring the Malay majority and indigenous groups over Chinese and Indian minorities. The economy has transitioned from agriculture to manufacturing and services, ranking as a newly industrialised, upper-middle-income market. Malaysia is a founding member of ASEAN, the OIC, and the Non-Aligned Movement.

## Body

### Etymology and Early History
The name *Malaysia* combines "Malays" with the Latin-Greek suffix *-ia* ("land of the Malays"). Early references include *Malayadvipa* in the Sanskrit *Vayu Purana* (first millennium CE) and *Malayu Kulon* in Ptolemy’s *Geographia* (2nd century). The term *Melayu* originally referred to the Sungai Melayu (river) and later the Melayu Kingdom in Sumatra, becoming associated with Srivijaya. It evolved into an ethnonym during the 15th-century rise of the Malacca Sultanate, where Islamisation fused ethnic and religious identity (*Melayu* becoming interchangeable with *Melakans*). European usage narrowed *Malayos* to Malacca’s ruling class, later expanding to the wider cultural group. Before colonisation, the peninsula was *Tanah Melayu* ("Malay Land"). The modern name was proposed by French navigator Dumont d’Urville (1831) and English ethnologist George Earl (1850). The 1957 state chose "Federation of Malaya"; "Malaysia" was adopted in 1963 upon the inclusion of Singapore, North Borneo, and Sarawak (the *si* suffix theorised to represent these additions).

Human habitation dates back 40,000 years; the peninsula’s first inhabitants were likely Negritos. The region participated in the Maritime Jade Road (2000 BC–1000 AD). Indian and Chinese traders arrived by the 1st century AD, establishing ports and introducing Hinduism and Buddhism (Sanskrit inscriptions appear by the 4th–5th century). Langkasuka (2nd–15th century) and the Buddhist Srivijayan empire (7th–13th century) dominated early history. The Hindu-Buddhist Majapahit empire wrested control in the 13th–14th century. In the early 15th century, Parameswara, a prince linked to Srivijaya, founded the Malacca Sultanate. His conversion to Islam accelerated the religion’s spread, and Malacca became a pivotal entrepôt.

### Colonial Era and the Road to Independence
Portugal conquered Malacca in 1511; the Dutch seized it in 1641. British involvement began in 1786 with the lease of Penang. They acquired Singapore (1819) and Malacca (1824, Anglo-Dutch Treaty), forming the Straits Settlements crown colony (1826). By the 20th century, the Federated Malay States (Pahang, Selangor, Perak, Negeri Sembilan) accepted British Residents; the Unfederated Malay States accepted British advisers. In Borneo, the Brooke family ruled Sarawak (1842–1946) as White Rajahs; North Borneo (Sabah) became a British protectorate (1877–78). British rule encouraged mass Chinese and Indian immigration for tin mining and rubber plantations.

The Klang War (1867–74) and Larut Wars (1861–74)—disputes over chieftaincies and tin revenue involving Malay chiefs and Chinese secret societies—disrupted trade and prompted British intervention, formalised by the Pangkor Treaty (1874). Japan occupied the region (1942–45), heightening ethnic tensions and stimulating nationalism. Post-war, the Malayan Union (1946) proposed equal citizenship and reduced sultanate powers; fierce Malay opposition forced its replacement by the Federation of Malaya (1948), restoring sultanate autonomy. The Malayan Emergency (1948–60) saw a predominantly Chinese Communist insurgency defeated by Commonwealth forces.

### Formation of Malaysia and Post-Independence Politics
Malaya gained independence on 31 August 1957. A plan to federate with North Borneo (Sabah), Sarawak, and Singapore was delayed by a UN referendum (requested by Indonesia and Sarawak opposition) but realised on 16 September 1963. Indonesia opposed the federation (*Konfrontasi*); the Philippines claimed Sabah. Singapore was expelled in 1965. Racial tensions erupted in the 13 May 1969 riots. In response, PM Tun Abdul Razak launched the New Economic Policy (NEP) to increase *bumiputera* economic share via affirmative action in education, employment, and business.

Under PM Mahathir Mohamad (1981–2003), rapid industrialisation shifted the economy from agriculture to manufacturing; mega-projects included the Petronas Towers, North–South Expressway, Multimedia Super Corridor, and the new administrative capital Putrajaya. The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis caused a sharp recession but recovery followed. The 1MDB scandal (2015) implicated PM Najib Razak in massive embezzlement, leading to the historic 2018 election defeat of the Barisan Nasional coalition (in power since 1957). A subsequent political crisis (2020–22) saw three PMs in quick succession amid the COVID-19 pandemic. The November 2022 election produced the first hung parliament; Anwar Ibrahim formed a grand unity government.

### Government and Politics
Malaysia is the only federal country in Southeast Asia. The head of state is the *Yang di-Pertuan Agong* (King), elected for a five-year term by and from the nine hereditary Malay rulers (rotation by informal agreement). The King’s role has been largely ceremonial since 1994 constitutional amendments. The head of government is the Prime Minister, who must command a majority in the lower house (House of Representatives, 222 members, five-year term, first-past-the-post). The upper house (Senate, 70 members) comprises 26 state-elected and 44 royally appointed senators. Voting age was lowered to 18 in 2019.

The legal system is based on English common law. The Federal Court is the apex, with separate High Courts for Peninsular and East Malaysia. A special court hears cases involving royalty. Judicial independence has been questioned. Race is central to politics: the NEP and its successor, the National Development Policy, grant *bumiputera* (Malays and indigenous groups, ~70% of citizens) preferential access to housing, education, contracts, and equity. This generates interethnic resentment. Debate persists over Islamism vs. secularism; Kelantan’s state-level Islamic criminal laws (hudud) remain unenforced federally. Malaysia is classified as a "flawed democracy" (Democracy Index) and "partly free" (Freedom House). Corruption remains significant (1MDB scandal involved alleged theft of $3.5+ billion).

### Administrative Divisions
Malaysia comprises 13 states and three federal territories (Kuala Lumpur, Labuan, Putrajaya). Eleven states and two territories are in Peninsular Malaysia; two states (Sabah, Sarawak) and one territory (Labuan) in East Malaysia. Nine peninsular states retain hereditary monarchs; four have appointed Governors. States have unicameral assemblies led by Chief Ministers. Sabah and Sarawak enjoy greater autonomy, including separate immigration controls. Secessionist rhetoric occasionally surfaces (Penang, Johor, Kelantan, Sabah, Sarawak) over federal intervention, development disparities, and oil royalties, but no serious movements exist. Local government (cities, municipalities, districts) is appointed by state governments (or federal for territories), though regulated by the federal Ministry of Housing and Local Government.

### Foreign Relations and Military
Foreign policy emphasises neutrality, regional stability (ASEAN centrality), and national sovereignty. Malaysia is a founding member of ASEAN, OIC, and NAM; member of UN, APEC, Commonwealth, and D-8. It avoids conflict with China over South China Sea claims but has condemned Chinese incursions into its waters/airspace. Maritime disputes exist with Indonesia, Singapore (land reclamation), and the Philippines (dormant Sabah claim). Brunei and Malaysia resolved land claims (2009).

The Malaysian Armed Forces (Army, Navy, Air Force) are volunteer (age 18); spending is ~1.5% GDP. Malaysia participates in UN peacekeeping (MALBATT) and the Five Power Defence Arrangements (with UK, Australia, NZ, Singapore). It conducts joint exercises with the US, China, India, Japan, and others. Border security has been tightened against spillover from southern Philippines/Thailand militancy.

### Human Rights
Homosexuality is illegal (punishable by caning/imprisonment); LGBT individuals face vigilante violence. Anwar Ibrahim’s sodomy trials were deemed politically motivated by UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention, Amnesty International, and Human Rights Watch. Human/sex trafficking are significant problems. The mandatory death penalty was abolished in July 2023 (Abolition of Mandatory Death Penalty Act 2023), granting judges discretion; natural life imprisonment was replaced with 30–40 year terms. Press freedom fluctuates; laws like the Printing Presses and Publications Act restrict expression.

### Geography
Malaysia (330,803 km²) is the 66th largest country, uniquely straddling mainland Asia and the Malay Archipelago. Peninsular Malaysia (40% land area) runs 740 km north-south, divided by the Titiwangsa Mountains (granite, heavily forested, karst landscape, source of river systems; highest peak Mount Korbu, 2,183 m). Coastal plains max 50 km wide; coastline ~1,931 km (harbours only on west). East Malaysia (Borneo) has a 2,607 km coastline; interior dominated by the Crocker Range. Mount Kinabalu (4,095 m) in Kinabalu National Park (UNESCO site) is the highest peak. Sarawak hosts the Mulu Caves (world’s largest cave system, UNESCO site) and the Rajang River (longest in Malaysia). The Strait of Malacca carries ~40% of global trade. Tanjung Piai (Johor) is mainland Asia’s southernmost tip. Climate is equatorial with two monsoons (SW April–Oct, NE Oct–Feb); high humidity, ~250 cm annual rainfall. Climate change threatens sea-level rise, flooding, and droughts.

### Biodiversity and Conservation
Malaysia is one of 17 megadiverse countries (~20% of world animal species; high endemism). It hosts ~210 mammals, 620+ birds (Peninsular), 250 reptiles, 150 frogs, thousands of insects, ~14,500 flowering plants, ~8,500 vascular plants (Peninsular), ~15,000 (East). East Malaysian forests may hold 2,000 tree species (240/hectare). Rafflesia (world’s largest flower, 1 m diameter) is present. Marine biodiversity is exceptional: Sipadan waters among richest globally; Sulu Sea hosts 600 coral and 1,200 fish species. ~4,000 fungi species recorded (many more likely undiscovered).

As of 2007, ~two-thirds forested (some 130 million years old), dominated by dipterocarps. Deforestation for logging and palm oil is severe: >80% of Sarawak’s rainforest logged; >60% of peninsula cleared; forests predicted extinct by 2020 at then-current rates. Species extinctions (e.g., *Begonia eiromischa*) have occurred. Marine threats: dynamite/poison fishing, uncontrolled tourism (Leatherback turtles down 98% since 1950s). Government aims to balance growth and protection but accused of favouring business. 28 national parks established (23 in East Malaysia). Anti-trafficking talks held with Brunei and Indonesia.

### Economy
Malaysia is an open, state-oriented, newly industrialised market economy (34th nominal GDP, 28th PPP). Structure (2017): Services 53.6%, Industry 37.6%, Agriculture 8.8%. Unemployment low (3.4%, 2024). Key sectors: manufacturing (electronics, automotive—22nd largest globally), petroleum, palm oil (top global producer), and tourism (3rd largest GDP contributor, 15.9% in 2019, 26.1M arrivals). The Strait of Malacca facilitates trade. Malaysia is an OPEC+ member. Chinese Malaysians (~25% population) hold ~70% market capitalisation, linked to the "bamboo network" of overseas Chinese businesses. High-tech exports reached $92.1B (2020), 2nd in ASEAN. Ranked 34th Global Innovation Index (2025), 32nd Global Competitiveness (2022). Islamic banking hub with highest female participation in sector.

### Infrastructure
Rail: 2,783 km (state-run). Roads: 238,823 km (26th largest globally, 2016). Inland waterways: 7,200 km (22nd longest). 114 airports; Kuala Lumpur International (KLIA) is 12th busiest in Asia. Port Klang is 13th busiest container port globally. Flag carrier: Malaysia Airlines. Telecoms: 2nd in SEA (after Singapore); 4.7M fixed, 30M+ cellular subscribers; urban-rural divide persists. 200+ industrial parks (incl. Technology Park Malaysia, Kulim Hi-Tech Park). Water: >95% access (90% groundwater). Energy: Dominated by Tenaga Nasional; National Grid connects Peninsular Malaysia; Sarawak Energy and Sabah Electricity serve East. Capacity >29,728 MW (2013); generation 140,985 GWh. Primary sources: oil and natural gas (4th largest reserves in Asia-Pacific).

### Demographics
Population: 32.4M (2020), 42nd globally; growth ~1.54%/yr (2012 est.). Density: 96/km². 70.3% aged 15–64; 8.1% 65+. 90.1% citizens. Ethnic breakdown (citizens): *Bumiputera* 70.6% (Malays—Muslim, Malay custom, ancestry—dominant politically; plus non-Malay indigenous: Dayaks, Kadazan-Dusun, Melanau, Bajau in Sabah/Sarawak; Orang Asli on peninsula), Chinese 22.1%, Indian 6.5% (mostly Tamil). *Bumiputera* definition varies by state. Citizenship not automatic by birth; dual citizenship prohibited. Biometric *MyKad* issued at 12. 80% population on Peninsula; 75.1% urban. ~3M migrant workers (~10%); Sabah NGOs estimate 2M illegal immigrants in Sabah alone. ~171,500 refugees/asylum seekers (mostly Myanmar, Philippines, Indonesia). RELA volunteer militia enforces immigration law (controversial history).

### Religion
Constitution: Islam is "religion of the Federation"; freedom of religion guaranteed but Muslims face restrictions on conversion/apostasy. 2020 Census: Islam 63.5%, Buddhism 18.7%, Christianity 9.1%, Hinduism 6.1%, Confucianism/Taoism/Chinese traditional 1.3%, None/Other 2.7%. Sarawak, Penang, Kuala Lumpur have non-Muslim majorities. Sunni Shafi'i is dominant madhhab; 18% non-denominational Muslims. Ethnic-religious correlation high: Chinese ~83.6% Buddhist; Indians ~86.2% Hindu; Non-Malay *bumiputera* ~46.5% Christian, 40.4% Muslim. Syariah courts govern Muslims on personal law (marriage, inheritance, apostasy) per Shafi'i school; no jurisdiction over non-Muslims or general criminal/civil law. Civil courts do not hear Islamic matters.

### Languages
Official: Malaysian Malay (*Bahasa Melayu*, standardised Malay; Latin/Rumi script official, Jawi not prohibited). English: active second language (official in Sarawak); *Manglish* colloquial creole widely used in business. 137 living languages total; 111 indigenous. Peninsular: 41 languages. Orang Asli languages endangered/extinct (e.g., Low Country Semang). East Malaysia: Iban (Sarawak), Dusunic/Kadazan (Sabah). Chinese dialects: Mandarin, Cantonese, Hokkien. Tamil (Indians). Creoles: Portuguese-based Malaccan Creole, Spanish-based Chavacano.

### Health
Two-tier system: universal public (highly subsidised) and private. Ministry of Health main provider. Considered among Asia’s most developed; drives medical tourism. Spending: 3.83% GDP (2019). Life expectancy: 76 (74M/78F, 2020). Infant mortality: 7/1000. Fertility: 2.0 (below 2.1 replacement). Leading death cause: coronary artery disease (17%, 2020), then pneumonia (11%). High traffic fatality rate globally. Smoking major issue.

### Education
Non-compulsory kindergarten → 6 years compulsory primary → 5 years optional secondary. Primary: National schools (Malay medium) vs. Vernacular schools (Chinese/Tamil medium). Secondary culminates in Malaysian Certificate of Education (SPM). Matriculation programme (1999) allows university entry; 10% quota for non-*bumiputera*.

### Culture
Multi-ethnic, multicultural, multilingual. Influences: Indigenous, Chinese, Indian, Persian, Arabic, British. Minimal assimilation due to "social contract" and government structure. Cultural disputes with Indonesia (shared heritage). 1971 National Cultural Policy: culture based on indigenous peoples, may incorporate suitable elements, Islam must play a part, Malay language promoted. Non-Malays resent perceived cultural suppression.
