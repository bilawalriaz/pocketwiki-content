# Economics

## Overview

Economics is a social science that studies the production, distribution, and consumption of goods and services, focusing on the behaviour and interactions of economic agents and how economies work. It is broadly divided into microeconomics, which analyses individual agents (households, firms, buyers, sellers) and markets, and macroeconomics, which analyses economies as systems where production, distribution, consumption, savings, and investment interact. The discipline evolved from "political economy" and now applies its analytical methods to diverse subjects including crime, education, the family, law, politics, war, and the environment.

## Timeline

- **1776** — Adam Smith publishes *The Wealth of Nations*, described as "the effective birth of economics as a separate discipline."
- **1798** — Thomas Robert Malthus uses diminishing returns to explain low living standards.
- **1817** — David Ricardo focuses on income distribution and states the principle of comparative advantage.
- **1848** — John Stuart Mill distinguishes between the market's roles in allocating resources and distributing income.
- **1867** — Karl Marx publishes the first volume of *Das Kapital*.
- **1870–1910** — Neoclassical economics forms, integrating supply and demand and rejecting the labour theory of value.
- **1890** — Alfred Marshall publishes *Principles of Economics*, extending analysis to the microeconomic level.
- **1932** — Lionel Robbins defines economics as the study of human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means.
- **1936** — John Maynard Keynes publishes *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, ushering in contemporary macroeconomics.
- **1950s–1960s** — Monetarism emerges under Milton Friedman.
- **1970s** — New classical economists introduce rational expectations.
- **1980s** — New Keynesian economists adopt rational expectations but emphasize market failures.
- **By 2000s** — The new neoclassical synthesis emerges, integrating rational expectations with nominal rigidities.
- **After 2008** — Macroeconomic research places greater emphasis on the financial system and behavioural economics.

## Body

### Definitions of economics

The earlier term for the discipline was "political economy," derived from the Ancient Greek *oikonomia*, meaning the "way (nomos) to run a household (oikos)." Adam Smith (1776) defined it as "an inquiry into the nature and causes of the wealth of nations." Jean-Baptiste Say (1803) defined it as the science of production, distribution, and consumption of wealth. Alfred Marshall (1890) extended this to "a study of man in the ordinary business of life." Lionel Robbins (1932) offered what has been termed "[p]erhaps the most commonly accepted current definition": "Economics is the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses." Robbins described this as analytical rather than classificatory, focusing on a particular aspect of behaviour imposed by scarcity. Some critics found the definition overly broad, but from the 1960s such comments abated as rational-choice modelling expanded the domain of the subject. Many economists, including Nobel Prize winners James M. Buchanan and Ronald Coase, reject the method-based definition and prefer subject-matter definitions like Say's.

### Classical political economy

Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) identified land, labour, and capital as the three factors of production, distinct from the physiocratic view that only agriculture was productive. Smith discussed the benefits of specialisation by division of labour and the "invisible hand," whereby individuals pursuing their own gain frequently promote that of society. Malthus (1798) argued that human population tends to increase geometrically while food production increases arithmetically, leading to diminishing returns and chronically low wages. David Ricardo (1817) focused on the distribution of income among landowners, workers, and capitalists, positing an inherent conflict between landowners and labour/capital. He was the first to state and prove the principle of comparative advantage, according to which each country should specialise in producing goods in which it has a lower relative cost of production. John Stuart Mill (1848) parted company with earlier classical economists on the inevitability of the market system's distribution of income, arguing society must intervene.

### Marxian economics

Marxian economics descends from classical economics and derives from the work of Karl Marx, whose *Das Kapital* was published in 1867. Marx focused on the labour theory of value, which held that the value of an exchanged commodity was determined by the labour that went into its production, and the theory of surplus value, which demonstrated how workers were only paid a proportion of the value their work had created. Marx wrote that these were mechanisms used by capital to exploit labour.

### Neoclassical economics

Neoclassical economics formed from about 1870 to 1910. The term "economics" was popularised by Alfred Marshall and Mary Paley Marshall as a concise synonym for "economic science." Neoclassical economics systematically integrated supply and demand as joint determinants of both price and quantity in market equilibrium. It rejected the labour theory of value in favour of a marginal utility theory of value on the demand side and a more comprehensive theory of costs on the supply side. In the 20th century, neoclassical theorists departed from measuring total utility for a society, opting instead for ordinal utility, which posits behaviour-based relations across individuals. Agents are assumed to act rationally, have stable preferences, and the capability of making a choice.

### Keynesian economics and post-WWII developments

Keynesian economics derives from John Maynard Keynes's *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936), which focused on determinants of national income in the short run when prices are relatively inflexible. Keynes attempted to explain why high labour-market unemployment might not be self-correcting due to low "effective demand." After World War II, Keynesian economics was dominant in the US and its allies, while Marxian economics dominated in the Soviet Union. Monetarism emerged in the 1950s and 1960s under Milton Friedman, contending that monetary policy was more important than fiscal policy for stabilisation. New classical economists (Robert Lucas, Thomas Sargent, Edward Prescott) introduced rational expectations in the 1970s. New Keynesian economists (George Akerlof, Janet Yellen, Gregory Mankiw, Olivier Blanchard) adopted rational expectations but emphasized market failures and price/wage rigidity. By the 2000s, a new neoclassical synthesis emerged, leading to dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models now standard in most central banks.

### Methodology

Mainstream economic theory relies upon analytical economic models. In microeconomics, principal concepts include supply and demand, marginalism, rational choice theory, opportunity cost, budget constraints, utility, and the theory of the firm. Economic theories are frequently tested empirically through econometrics using economic data. Controlled experiments are difficult and uncommon in economics; instead, broad data is observationally studied. The field of experimental economics is growing, and behavioural economics has found that some axioms are not entirely correct. Statistical methods such as regression analysis are common.

### Microeconomics

Microeconomics examines how entities interact within a market to create a market system. Market structures include perfect competition (where every participant is a "price taker") and imperfect competition, including monopoly (one seller), duopoly (two sellers), oligopoly (few sellers), monopolistic competition (many sellers of differentiated goods), monopsony (one buyer), and oligopsony (few buyers). Production is the conversion of inputs into outputs. Economic efficiency measures how well a system generates desired output; a widely accepted standard is Pareto efficiency, reached when no further change can make someone better off without making someone else worse off. The production–possibility frontier (PPF) graphically represents scarcity, opportunity cost, and efficiency. Specialisation is considered key to economic efficiency, based on comparative advantage. The theory of supply and demand explains how prices coordinate the amounts produced and consumed. Market equilibrium occurs where quantity supplied equals quantity demanded. Firms combine labour and capital and can achieve economies of scale. Game theory, dating from the 1944 classic by John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern, considers strategic interactions between agents. Market failure encompasses information asymmetries, natural monopoly, public goods, and externalities.

### Macroeconomics

Macroeconomics examines the economy as a whole, including national income and output, the unemployment rate, and price inflation. Growth economics studies factors explaining economic growth, including the rate of investment, population growth, and technological change. The Great Depression of the 1930s spurred the creation of macroeconomics as a separate discipline. The unemployment rate measures the percentage of workers in the labour force who are unemployed; types include frictional, structural, and cyclical unemployment. Money is a means of final payment and unit of account. Monetary policy is the set of actions central banks take to achieve their broader objectives, typically by setting a target for its policy rate. Fiscal policy involves governments adjusting spending and taxation to alter aggregate demand, though effects can be limited by crowding out and Ricardian equivalence. Economic inequality, measured using the Gini coefficient among other methods, has been linked to political and social instability.

## Terms

- **Microeconomics**: The branch of economics analysing individual agents and markets, their interactions, and outcomes.
- **Macroeconomics**: The branch analysing economies as systems where production, distribution, consumption, savings, and investment interact.
- **Comparative advantage**: The principle that each country should specialise in producing goods in which it has a lower relative cost of production.
- **Labour theory of value**: The classical and Marxian theory that the value of an exchanged commodity is determined by the labour that went into its production.
- **Rational expectations**: The notion introduced by new classical economists that agents form expectations based on all available information.
- **Opportunity cost**: What must be given up to obtain something; the cost of one good in terms of units of another good forgone.
- **Pareto efficiency**: A standard reached when no further change can make someone better off without making someone else worse off.
- **Market failure**: Problems that undermine standard economic assumptions, including information asymmetries, natural monopoly, public goods, and externalities.
- **Monetary policy**: The set of actions central banks take to achieve broader objectives, typically by setting a target for the policy rate.
- **Fiscal policy**: Government adjustment of spending and taxation to alter aggregate demand.

## Debates and open questions

There is no single agreed definition of economics. Robbins's scarcity-based definition has been criticised as overly broad, failing to limit its scope to market analysis, and as not accounting for the macroeconomics of high unemployment. Many economists, including Nobel Prize winners James M. Buchanan and Ronald Coase, reject the method-based definition in favour of subject-matter definitions. Ha-Joon Chang has argued that Robbins's definition makes economics peculiar because all other sciences define themselves by their area of inquiry rather than methodology. Empirical testing in economics is typically regarded as less rigorous than controlled experimentation, and conclusions more tentative. The use of commonly accepted methods need not produce a consensus on a particular question, given different tests, data sets, and prior beliefs.
