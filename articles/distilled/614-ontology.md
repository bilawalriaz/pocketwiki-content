# Ontology

## Overview
Ontology is the philosophical study of being, investigating the most general features of reality, the categories into which entities fall, and the relations among them. It seeks a comprehensive inventory of what exists—distinguishing particulars from universals, concrete from abstract objects, and substances from properties—while addressing whether existence comes in degrees or modes. Ontology underpins metaphysics, logic, and the sciences, and its applied branches provide the conceptual frameworks used in computer science, genetics, and the social sciences to structure domain knowledge.

## Timeline
- **c. 700 BCE** — Upanishads discuss the divine foundation of the universe and the oneness or manyness of ultimate reality.
- **6th century BCE** — Presocratic philosophers in Greece propose rational first principles (e.g., water, fire) as the source of all things.
- **c. 515–450 BCE** — Parmenides explicitly distinguishes being from non-being, often regarded as the founder of ontology.
- **427–347 BCE** — Plato develops the theory of Forms, positing unchangeable perfect forms as more real than imitating matter.
- **384–322 BCE** — Aristotle introduces a ten-category system with substance as the primary kind of being.
- **3rd century CE** — Neoplatonism posits the One as an ineffable source more basic than being; Xuanxue in China explores being and non-being.
- **980–1037 CE** — Avicenna identifies God as a necessary being and all else as contingent.
- **1224–1274 CE** — Thomas Aquinas refines distinctions between existence/essence, substance/accidents, and matter/form.
- **c. 1287–1347 CE** — William of Ockham formulates Ockham’s Razor: prefer the ontology with the fewest entities.
- **1596–1650** — René Descartes establishes substance dualism: mind and matter as distinct, interacting substances.
- **1632–1677** — Baruch Spinoza proposes substance monism: a single entity identical to God and nature.
- **1679–1754** — Christian Wolff delimits ontology as a distinct discipline separate from other metaphysics.
- **1724–1804** — Immanuel Kant limits knowledge to appearances; ontology becomes a system of pure concepts of understanding.
- **1861–1947** — Alfred North Whitehead develops process ontology: events/processes as fundamental.
- **1891–1970** — Rudolf Carnap argues ontological questions are relative to linguistic frameworks.
- **1908–2000** — W. V. O. Quine ties ontology to the ontological commitments of our best scientific theories.
- **1941–2001** — David Lewis defends modal realism: possible worlds are as concrete as the actual world.
- **Late 20th century** — Applied ontology expands in computer and information science for domain modeling and data interoperability.

## Body

### Definition and scope
Ontology is the branch of philosophy that studies being, existence, and the fundamental categories of reality. Unlike individual sciences (e.g., biology, astronomy), which restrict themselves to limited domains, ontology aims to characterize reality as a whole in its most general aspects. The term derives from Greek *ontos* (being) and *logia* (study of), coined in the 17th century. Its exact relation to metaphysics is disputed: traditionally ontology is a subdiscipline (general metaphysics) distinct from special metaphysics (God, mind, value); alternatively, ontology provides the inventory of reality while metaphysics examines the structure of the inventoried entities; some treat the terms as synonyms.

### Basic concepts

#### Being
Being encompasses all reality and every entity. It contrasts with non-being, and whether it admits substantive analysis is debated. Proposals include: being as a universal property (criticized because properties presuppose being); the Eleatic principle (“power is the mark of being”—only causally efficacious entities exist); and Berkeley’s immaterialism (“to be is to be perceived”). Categories of being—substance, property, relation, state of affairs, event—aim to inventory reality. Aristotle held that entities in different categories exist in distinct ways; Duns Scotus insisted on a univocal mode of being. Contemporary consensus: existence is binary, not degreed.

#### Particulars and universals
Particulars (individuals) are unique, non-repeatable entities (Socrates, Mars). Universals are general, repeatable entities (the color green, circularity) that express features shared by particulars. Universals appear as properties (features of single entities) or relations (connections among multiple entities). Properties divide into essential (must have, e.g., three-sidedness for a triangle) and accidental (can lack, e.g., redness). Relations divide into internal (grounded in the relata’s properties, e.g., resemblance) and external (not so grounded, e.g., spatial relations). Substances are particulars that underlie and support properties/relations, persist through change, and can exist independently. States of affairs are complex particulars with multiple components (e.g., “Socrates is wise”); those corresponding to reality are facts, the truthmakers of statements. Events are particulars occurring in time, usually involving change; processes are sequences of events.

#### Concrete and abstract objects
Concrete objects exist in space and time, have causal powers, and are mereologically complex (parts/wholes studied by mereology). Abstract objects (e.g., the number 7) lack spatiotemporal location and causal powers; their existence and nature are debated. Fictional objects (e.g., the One Ring) and intentional objects (objects of mental states, e.g., the Loch Ness Monster as thought of) raise further ontological questions: are they abstract, artifacts, or something else?

#### Other concepts
Ontological dependence: entity A cannot exist without entity B (e.g., an apple’s surface depends on the apple). Independence marks fundamentality. Related to metaphysical grounding. Ontological commitment: entities a person or theory says exist (e.g., belief in God commits one to God). Quine–Putnam indispensability argument: best scientific theories commit us to numbers, supporting mathematical Platonism. Modality: possibility (true in some possible world) and necessity (true in all possible worlds) analyzed via possible-world semantics and modal logic. Identity: qualitative (exact similarity) vs. numerical (one and the same entity); synchronic (at a time) vs. diachronic (across time). Kripke: discovered identities (e.g., “Water is H₂O”) are necessary because “H₂O” is a rigid designator.

### Branches
- **Pure ontology**: abstract topics about being as a whole, not domain-restricted.  
- **Applied (domain) ontology**: applies ontological theories to specific domains (matter, mind, numbers, social kinds).  
  - *Social ontology*: studies social kinds (money, gender, language) as useful constructions lacking mind-independent reality of natural kinds.  
  - *Information/computer science*: formal frameworks (e.g., Gene Ontology, Suggested Upper Merged Ontology) for structured data storage and interoperability.  
- **Formal ontology**: studies objects’ abstract structures/forms using formal logic; contrasts with material ontology (domain-specific features, e.g., geometry, biology).  
- **Descriptive vs. prescriptive ontology**: descriptive articulates ordinary conceptual schemes; prescriptive seeks better conceptualizations.  
- **Analytic vs. speculative ontology**: analytic determines what kinds of things *could* exist; speculative determines what *actually* exists.  
- **Meta-ontology**: studies ontology’s concepts, assumptions, methods (“what does it mean to exist?”). Related to Heidegger’s fundamental ontology (meaning of being).

### Schools of thought

#### Realism and anti-realism
- **Ontological realism**: objective, mind-independent facts determine which ontological theories are true.  
- **Ontological anti-realism/deflationism** (Carnap): ontological statements are framework-relative; no framework-independent facts.  
- **Universals**: Platonic realism (universals exist independently of mind and particulars); Aristotelian/moderate realism (universals exist only if instantiated); conceptualism (universals exist only as mental concepts); nominalism (no universals, only particulars).  
- **Mathematical objects**: Platonism (abstract mathematical objects exist independently); conventionalism (truths by definition); game formalism (mathematics as rule-governed symbol manipulation).  
- **Modal realism** (Lewis): possible worlds are concrete and real; anti-realists treat them as abstract/fictional.  
- **Scientific realism**: unobservables (electrons, forces) exist as described by theories; anti-realism: theories are predictive instruments.  
- **Moral realism**: mind-independent moral facts; anti-realism: relativism (subjective/cultural) or nihilism (no moral facts).

#### By number of categories
- **Monocategorical**: one fundamental category (e.g., nominalism: only concrete particulars; bundle theory: only properties).  
- **Polycategorical**: two or more basic categories; systems of categories inventory highest genera.  
- **Monism vs. dualism**: monism—one fundamental kind/substance (materialism: only matter; idealism: only mental; neutral monism: both derivative); dualism—mind and matter as independent principles.  
- **Monism vs. pluralism** (entity count): monism—single all-encompassing entity (Advaita Vedanta); pluralism—many distinct entities.

#### By fundamental categories
- **Substance-attribute ontology**: unanalyzable substances characterized by universals (properties/relations).  
- **Substratum theory**: objects = featureless substratum (bare particular) + properties.  
- **Stuff ontologies**: continuous, infinitely divisible stuff, not distinct entities.  
- **Process ontology** (Whitehead): processes/events fundamental; reality is dynamic.  
- **Bundle theories**: objects are bundles of co-present properties; traditional (universals) vs. trope (particular properties).  
- **Relationalism / ontic structural realism**: relations fundamental; objects either absent or structure-dependent.  
- **Fact ontologies**: facts (states of affairs) are fundamental constituents; objects/properties/relations exist only as fact-components.  
- **Historical category systems**: Aristotle (10), Vaisheshika (6), Kant (12 in four classes), Peirce, Husserl, Chisholm, Lowe.

#### Others
- **Constituent vs. relational ontologies**: constituent—objects have internal structure (properties as parts, e.g., bundle theory); relational—properties externally related (pin-cushion analogy).  
- **Hierarchical vs. flat ontologies**: hierarchical—levels of fundamentality (particles > macroscopic objects; substances > properties); flat—all entities exist on same level (object-oriented ontology).  
- **Endurantism vs. perdurantism**: endurantism—3D objects wholly present at each time; perdurantism—4D objects with temporal parts (change = different qualities in different parts).  
- **Differential ontology** (poststructuralist): difference privileged over identity; identity is a secondary determination.  
- **Object-oriented ontology** (speculative realism): objects as fundamental building blocks; flat ontology; objects exist independently of human thought.

### Methods
No single standard method; meta-ontology studies the diverse approaches.
- **Conceptual analysis**: decompose concepts into necessary/sufficient conditions to decide existence questions (e.g., numbers).  
- **Eidetic variation** (phenomenology): imaginatively vary features to isolate essences.  
- **Transcendental method**: from the fact that an entity exists, deduce conditions for its possibility.  
- **Intuitions & thought experiments**: non-inferential impressions as foundations; counterfactual scenarios (e.g., philosophical zombies) to test mind–body relations.  
- **Naturalistic methods** (Quine): analyze ontological commitments of best scientific theories; science as most reliable reality description.  
- **Principles of theory choice**: Ockham’s Razor (simplicity in basic types or entities); explanatory power; proximity to common sense.  
- **Ontological engineering** (applied ontology): delineate scope, define concepts/relations in formal language, validate with test data; methodologies include Cyc, Grüninger & Fox, METHONTOLOGY; sometimes adapt upper ontologies (e.g., BFO, SUMO).

### Related fields
- **Logic**: formal ontology uses logical systems; existential quantifier (∃) expresses existence; some seek a formal “ontologese” to avoid natural-language ambiguities.  
- **Information/computer science**: ontologies as conceptual schemes for databases, knowledge representation, and interoperability (e.g., Protein Ontology, Environment Ontology, FOAF).  
- **Anthropology** (“ontological turn” since 1990s): cross-cultural understandings of being; Indigenous ontologies often ascribe intentionality to non-humans (animism), emphasize interconnectedness and harmony.  
- **Theology**: ontological argument (Anselm)—God defined as greatest conceivable being must exist; Heidegger’s critique of “ontotheology” (using God as foundational principle of reality).

### History
Ancient roots: Upanishads (c. 700 BCE) on unity/plurality of ultimate reality; Samkhya (dualist: consciousness/matter); Vaisheshika (six categories); Chinese Taoism (Laozi, 6th c. BCE: Tao, yin/yang) and Xuanxue (3rd c. CE: being/non-being). Greek Presocratics (6th c. BCE) seek rational first principles; Parmenides distinguishes being/non-being; Plato’s Forms; Aristotle’s categories and substance; Neoplatonism (the One). Medieval: problem of universals—Boethius, Abelard (conceptualism), Aquinas (existence/essence, transcendentals), Scotus (univocal being, haecceity), Ockham (razor). Arabic-Persian: Avicenna (necessary/contingent being). Indian: Advaita Vedanta (8th c., non-dualism: only Brahman exists); Navya-Nyāya (13th c., non-existence/negation). Chinese: Neo-Confucianism (9th c., *li* as ground of being). Modern: Descartes (substance dualism); Spinoza (monism: God = nature); Leibniz (monads); Locke (substratum); Wolff (ontology as distinct discipline); Berkeley (idealism: to be is to be perceived). Kant (appearances vs. things-in-themselves; categories of understanding); Hegel (being = thought); Schopenhauer (will); Bradley (absolute spirit); Vivekananda/Aurobindo (Advaita developments). 20th century: Husserl (phenomenology); Heidegger (meaning of being via human existence); Sartre (being/nothingness); Hartmann (four-level hierarchy); Meinong (nonexistent objects); Russell/Wittgenstein (logical atomism/fact ontology); Whitehead (process); Carnap (linguistic frameworks); Quine (scientific commitments); Lewis (modal realism). Late 20th century onward: applied ontology in computer/information science.

## Terms
- ****Particular**** — A unique, non-repeatable entity (e.g., Socrates, the Taj Mahal).
- ****Universal**** — A general, repeatable entity that can be instantiated by many particulars (e.g., the color green, circularity).
- ****Substance**** — A particular entity that underlies and supports properties/relations, persists through change, and can exist independently.
- ****Property**** — A feature or quality possessed by an entity; divided into essential (must have) and accidental (can lack).
- ****Relation**** — A way two or more entities connect; internal (grounded in relata’s properties) or external (not so grounded).
- ****State of affairs**** — A complex particular composed of entities (e.g., an individual + a property); if it corresponds to reality, it is a **fact**.
- ****Event**** — A particular occurring in time, usually involving change; a **process** is a sequence of events.
- ****Concrete object**** — An entity existing in space and time with causal powers (e.g., a tree).
- ****Abstract object**** — An entity outside space and time, lacking causal powers (e.g., the number 7).
- ****Ontological commitment**** — An entity that a person or theory asserts exists; used to compare theories (Quine).
- ****Possible world**** — A complete, consistent way things could have been; used to analyze possibility (true in some world) and necessity (true in all).
- ****Endurantism**** — Material objects are 3D, wholly present at each moment of their existence.
- ****Perdurantism**** — Material objects are 4D, extended in time with temporal parts; only one part is present at any moment.
- ****Ockham’s Razor**** — Principle of theory choice: prefer simpler ontologies (fewer basic types or entities).
- ****Applied ontology**** — Use of ontological theories to build formal conceptual frameworks for specific domains (e.g., databases, genetics).

## Debates and open questions
- **Mode of being**: Do entities in different categories exist in distinct ways (Aristotle) or univocally (Scotus)?  
- **Degrees of existence**: Is existence binary or do some entities have a “higher degree” of being (Plato)?  
- **Universals**: Do they exist mind-independently (Platonic/Aristotelian realism), only mentally (conceptualism), or not at all (nominalism)?  
- **Abstract objects**: Do numbers, sets, propositions exist? If so, how do we know them (Benacerraf’s epistemological challenge)?  
- **Nonexistent objects**: Can there be objects that do not exist (Meinong) or does “there are” entail existence (Russell/Quine)?  
- **Fundamental category**: Is reality fundamentally substances, stuff, processes, bundles, facts, or structures?  
- **Monism vs. pluralism**: Is there one all-encompassing entity (Advaita) or many?  
- **Mind–body**: Dualism (independent substances/properties), materialism, idealism, neutral monism?  
- **Persistence**: Endurantism vs. perdurantism vs. stage theory?  
- **Ontological realism vs. anti-realism**: Are there framework-independent facts about what exists (Carnap’s challenge)?  
- **Methodology**: Can ontology be pursued a priori (intuition, conceptual analysis) or only naturalistically (via science)?  
- **Social kinds**: Are money, gender, institutions mind-dependent constructions or do they have objective reality?  
- **Modal realism**: Are possible worlds concrete (Lewis) or abstract/fictional?
