# Leaves of Grass

## Overview
*Leaves of Grass* is a poetry collection by Walt Whitman, first self-published in 1855 and continually revised until his death in 1892. It is a foundational work of American literature, celebrated for its free verse, its celebration of the self, democracy, and the body, and its controversial, candid depictions of sensuality. Its evolution from a slim volume of 12 poems to a final "deathbed edition" of nearly 400 poems mirrors Whitman's own life and the changing American landscape.

## Timeline
- **1855** — First edition of *Leaves of Grass* self-published on July 4; 12 poems, no author name.
- **1855** — Ralph Waldo Emerson sends Whitman a letter praising the book as "wit and wisdom."
- **1856** — Second edition published, featuring a blurb from Emerson's letter on the cover.
- **1860** — Third edition published by Thayer & Eldridge; includes major new poems.
- **1865** — *Drum-Taps*, a collection of Civil War poems, is published separately.
- **1867** — Fourth edition incorporates *Drum-Taps* and its sequel into *Leaves of Grass*.
- **1881** — Seventh edition published in Boston; considered by many the definitive version.
- **1882** — Boston authorities declare the book "obscene literature"; Whitman refuses to censor it.
- **1891-92** — Final "deathbed edition" is prepared and published, containing almost 400 poems.

## Body

### Publication and Evolution
The first edition was a 95-page, self-published volume of 12 untitled poems, printed in Brooklyn. Whitman paid for and helped typeset it, and it featured an engraving of him in work clothes but no author name. The title was a pun: "grass" was publisher slang for minor works, and "leaves" meant pages. The book's expansion was driven by Whitman's lifelong revisions. The 1856 edition used a quote from Emerson's praise as a cover blurb, a pioneering marketing move. Later editions grew significantly, incorporating new sections like *Drum-Taps* (Civil War poems) and culminating in the 1892 "deathbed edition," which Whitman declared his complete and final poetic utterance.

### Content and Style
Whitman's poetry is characterized by free verse, rejecting traditional rhyme and meter to create a sprawling, cataloguing style. The poems celebrate the individual ("Song of Myself"), the body ("I Sing the Body Electric"), American democracy, and the material world over spiritual themes. The collection's evolution included adding titles to early poems and organizing later works into sections like "Calamus." Key poems added over time include "Crossing Brooklyn Ferry," "Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking," and Lincoln elegies like "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd."

### Controversy and Censorship
From its first publication, *Leaves of Grass* was controversial for its explicit sexual imagery and sensual themes. Early critics called it "filth" and attacked Whitman personally. The most significant censorship attempt came in 1882, when Boston's district attorney, urged by the New England Society for the Suppression of Vice, demanded the removal of several poems and changes to others, labeling the book "obscene literature." Whitman refused, and his publisher initially dropped the book. Whitman found a new publisher, and the resulting publicity from the Boston ban boosted sales.

### Legacy and Cultural Impact
Despite initial derision, *Leaves of Grass* became recognized as a central work of American poetry. Its themes of democracy, equality, and the common person have been adopted by various movements. In the early 20th century, socialist publications used it to champion the working class. During World War II, the U.S. government distributed Whitman's poetry to soldiers to inspire patriotism. The work has also been claimed for racial equality, as noted by Langston Hughes. However, Whitman's nationalism has also been criticized for being exclusionary. The collection has permeated popular culture, referenced in films (*Dead Poets Society*, *Bull Durham*), television (*Breaking Bad*), and music.

## Terms
*   **Free Verse:** Poetry that does not rhyme or follow a regular meter, a hallmark of Whitman's style.
*   **Catalogue:** A poetic technique of listing items, people, or ideas in rapid succession, used by Whitman to convey expansiveness.
*   **Transcendentalism:** A philosophical movement emphasizing individualism, intuition, and the inherent goodness of people and nature, which influenced Whitman.
*   **Phrenology:** A pseudoscience popular in the 19th century that linked mental faculties to skull shape; Whitman used its concept of "adhesiveness" for friendship.
*   **Deathbed Edition:** The final, 1892 edition of *Leaves of Grass*, prepared by Whitman shortly before his death.
*   **Blurb:** A promotional quote on a book's cover; Whitman pioneered this by using Emerson's letter on the 1856 edition.
*   **Drum-Taps:** Whitman's 1865 collection of Civil War poems, later incorporated into *Leaves of Grass*.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Edition Count:** Scholars disagree on whether to count six or nine separate editions, depending on whether a new set of type is required for a new edition.
*   **Whitman's Nationalism:** While his work is celebrated for its democratic ideals, critics like Nathanael O'Reilly argue his imagined America is "arrogant, expansionist, hierarchical, racist and exclusive."
*   **Borrowed Material:** Despite his proclamation of "original energy," scholars have found Whitman borrowed phrases from newspapers and science books, complicating claims of pure originality.
