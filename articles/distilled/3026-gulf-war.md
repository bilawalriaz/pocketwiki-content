# Gulf War

## Overview
The Gulf War (1990–1991) was an armed conflict between Iraq and a 42-nation coalition led by the United States, triggered by Iraq's invasion and annexation of Kuwait. It was the largest military alliance since World War II and introduced new military technologies and live media coverage from the front lines. The war's aftermath included severe environmental damage, long-term health issues for veterans, and set the stage for the 2003 Iraq War.

## Timeline
- **2 August 1990** — Iraq invades and occupies Kuwait.
- **6 August 1990** — UN Security Council imposes economic sanctions on Iraq (Resolution 661).
- **7 August 1990** — US begins Operation Desert Shield, deploying troops to Saudi Arabia.
- **29 November 1990** — UN Security Council Resolution 678 authorizes "all necessary means" if Iraq does not withdraw by 15 January 1991.
- **17 January 1991** — Coalition launches Operation Desert Storm with a massive aerial and naval bombardment of Iraq and Kuwait.
- **29 January 1991** — Iraq attacks and briefly occupies the Saudi city of Khafji (Battle of Khafji).
- **24 February 1991** — Coalition launches a decisive ground assault, liberating Kuwait and advancing into Iraq.
- **26 February 1991** — Iraqi forces begin retreating; the "Highway of Death" bombing occurs.
- **28 February 1991** — Coalition declares a ceasefire after 100 hours of ground combat.
- **3 March 1991** — Formal ceasefire agreement signed.
- **March–April 1991** — Iraqi government suppresses uprisings in the south and north.

## Body

### Background and Invasion
The conflict stemmed from long-standing border disputes, Iraqi debt from the Iran–Iraq War (much owed to Kuwait), and Iraqi accusations that Kuwait was overproducing oil to drive down prices, harming Iraq's economy. Following failed negotiations, Iraq invaded Kuwait on 2 August 1990, occupying it within two days and annexing it as its 19th province. The invasion was met with immediate international condemnation.

### Coalition Building and Diplomacy
The UN Security Council passed a series of resolutions demanding withdrawal and imposing sanctions. A broad coalition of 42 countries was formed, with the US, Saudi Arabia, the UK, and Egypt as major contributors. Diplomatic efforts, including a Soviet-proposed ceasefire, were rejected by the coalition, which insisted on Iraq's unconditional withdrawal. A public relations campaign, later revealed to include fabricated testimony about Iraqi atrocities, helped build support for military intervention in the US.

### The Air Campaign and Scud Attacks
Operation Desert Storm began on 17 January 1991 with a five-week aerial bombardment. Iraq fired 88 Scud missiles at Israel and Saudi Arabia, hoping to provoke an Israeli response that would fracture the Muslim-majority coalition. This strategy failed, as Israel, pressured by the US, did not retaliate. The coalition flew over 100,000 sorties, using precision-guided munitions and stealth aircraft (F-117 Nighthawk) to destroy Iraqi military infrastructure.

### Ground War and Liberation of Kuwait
The ground campaign, Operation Desert Saber, began on 24 February 1991. Coalition forces executed a "left-hook" maneuver, sweeping into Iraq while Marines and Arab forces advanced into Kuwait. The campaign featured some of the largest tank battles in American history, including the Battle of 73 Easting and the Battle of Medina Ridge. Kuwait was declared liberated on 26 February, and a ceasefire was declared on 28 February after 100 hours of ground combat.

### Aftermath and Consequences
In the aftermath, the Iraqi government brutally suppressed Shia and Kurdish uprisings, killing an estimated 25,000 to 100,000 people. Coalition forces established no-fly zones over northern and southern Iraq. The war caused catastrophic environmental damage, including over 600 oil well fires and a massive oil spill. Many coalition veterans reported illnesses, later termed Gulf War syndrome, linked to exposure to depleted uranium and chemical weapons agents. The UN Special Commission (UNSCOM) sought to dismantle Iraq's weapons of mass destruction programs. The continued US military presence in Saudi Arabia after the war became a stated motivation for the 9/11 attacks.

## Terms
- **Operation Desert Shield**: The US-led military buildup and defense of Saudi Arabia from August 1990 to January 1991.
- **Operation Desert Storm**: The US name for the air and land conflict from 17 January to 28 February 1991.
- **Scud**: A Soviet-designed tactical ballistic missile used by Iraq to attack coalition targets in Israel and Saudi Arabia.
- **Precision-guided munition**: A "smart bomb" with guidance systems to hit specific targets, reducing collateral damage.
- **Gulf War syndrome**: A range of chronic illnesses reported by veterans of the war, with debated causes.
- **Highway of Death**: The name given to a stretch of highway in Iraq where a retreating Iraqi convoy was destroyed by coalition air attacks.
- **No-fly zone**: Airspace over a region where military aircraft are prohibited, established by the coalition over parts of Iraq after the war.
- **Depleted uranium**: A dense, slightly radioactive byproduct of uranium enrichment used in armor-piercing tank rounds and cannon shells.

## Debates and Open questions
- **Causes of Gulf War syndrome**: Research points to exposure to low levels of sarin nerve agent from bombed chemical weapons facilities, depleted uranium, and other toxins, but the exact cause remains debated.
- **Civilian casualties**: Estimates of Iraqi civilian deaths during the war and subsequent uprisings vary widely, from tens of thousands to over 200,000, with studies offering conflicting data on long-term health impacts.
- **Media and propaganda**: The war's media coverage was tightly controlled by the military. The revelation that key public relations stories, like the "incubator babies" testimony, were fabricated raised questions about the role of propaganda in justifying the conflict.
- **Strategic decision to stop**: The decision by President George H. W. Bush not to march on Baghdad and overthrow Saddam Hussein is debated, with critics arguing it left a dangerous regime in power and supporters claiming it prevented a costly occupation and preserved the coalition.
