# 1755 Lisbon earthquake

## Overview
The 1755 Lisbon earthquake was a catastrophic natural disaster that struck on All Saints' Day, combining a massive earthquake, tsunami, and firestorm to destroy much of Lisbon and the Portuguese Empire. It profoundly impacted European philosophy, politics, and the development of modern seismology, becoming a pivotal event in the Age of Enlightenment.

## Timeline
- **1 November 1755** — A magnitude 7.7+ earthquake strikes Lisbon at ~09:40 local time, followed by a tsunami and firestorm.
- **4 December 1755** — Chief engineer Manuel da Maia presents rebuilding plans for Lisbon to the king and Marquis of Pombal.
- **1756** — Immanuel Kant publishes three texts attempting a natural explanation for the earthquake.
- **1758** — King Joseph I survives an assassination attempt, leading to reprisals against the aristocracy.
- **1780** — The Casa Pia institution is founded to provide shelter and schooling to children in need.

## Body

### The Earthquake, Tsunami, and Fire
The earthquake lasted between three and a half to six minutes, causing wide fissures. About 40 minutes later, a tsunami engulfed the harbor and downtown, followed by two more waves. Candles lit for the holiday started a firestorm that burned for hours. The disaster affected a vast area: coastal towns in the Algarve were heavily damaged, shocks were felt as far as Finland and North Africa, and tsunamis struck the coasts of Morocco, Spain, Ireland, and possibly Brazil. The epicenter is debated but is located in the Atlantic Ocean west-southwest of Cape St. Vincent.

### Casualties and Damage
An estimated 30,000–40,000 people died in Lisbon, with another 10,000 possibly dying in Morocco. The total death toll in Portugal, Spain, and Morocco may have been 40,000 to 50,000. Approximately 85% of Lisbon's buildings were destroyed, including the Royal Ribeira Palace, its 70,000-volume library, and hundreds of irreplaceable artworks and historical records. The Royal Hospital of All Saints burned with hundreds of patients inside.

### Relief and Reconstruction
The royal family survived, but King Joseph I developed a lifelong fear of living within walls. Prime Minister Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo, the Marquis of Pombal, organized the response, famously stating the goal was to "bury the dead and heal the living." Corpses were buried at sea to prevent disease, and the army enforced order. Pombal chose a plan to completely rebuild Lisbon's Baixa district with wide, rectilinear streets. The new "Pombaline" buildings were among Europe's earliest seismically protected constructions, tested using wooden models and marching troops.

### Philosophical and Political Impact
The earthquake, occurring on a major religious holiday, sparked intense debate about divine judgment. It influenced Enlightenment thinkers: Voltaire used it to critique optimism in *Candide*, Rousseau argued it showed the dangers of dense cities, and Kant published early scientific theories about its natural causes. Politically, Pombal's competent response consolidated his power and weakened the old aristocracy, though it also fueled resentment that led to a later assassination attempt on the king.

### Birth of Modern Seismology
Pombal ordered a systematic inquiry, sending questionnaires to parishes across Portugal to gather objective data on the earthquake's timing, effects, and damage. This large-scale, factual investigation is considered a foundational step in the development of modern seismology.

## Terms
- **Theodicy**: A branch of theology and philosophy that attempts to reconcile the existence of a benevolent God with the presence of evil and suffering in the world.
- **Moment magnitude scale**: A modern scale used by seismologists to measure the size of earthquakes in terms of the energy released.
- **Pombaline**: The architectural and urban planning style implemented in the rebuilding of Lisbon under the Marquis of Pombal, characterized by wide streets and early seismic-resistant design.
- **Seismology**: The scientific study of earthquakes and the propagation of elastic waves through the Earth.
- **Sebastianism**: A Portuguese messianic belief in the return of King Sebastian, who disappeared in 1578; the earthquake caused a surge in its following.
- **Manueline**: A distinctively Portuguese late Gothic architectural style from the 16th century, many examples of which were destroyed in the earthquake.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact location of the earthquake's epicenter remains a subject of debate among seismologists and geologists.
- The precise death toll is uncertain due to vague contemporary reports and the difficulty of separating casualties from a subsequent earthquake series on 18–19 November.
- The long-term philosophical impact is analyzed differently; for example, Werner Hamacher argues it shook the foundational certainty of Western philosophy.
