# Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period

## Overview
The Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period (907–979 CE) was an era of political fragmentation in China following the collapse of the Tang dynasty. It is defined by the rapid succession of five short-lived dynasties in the north and the concurrent existence of multiple, more stable kingdoms in the south. This period of division and warfare ultimately set the stage for the reunification under the Song dynasty and a significant economic and cultural shift toward southern China.

## Timeline
- **907** — Tang dynasty ends; Zhu Wen founds the Later Liang.
- **923** — Li Cunxu founds the Later Tang, conquering the Later Liang.
- **936** — Shi Jingtang founds the Later Jin with Khitan aid, ceding the Sixteen Prefectures.
- **947** — Liu Zhiyuan founds the Later Han after the Khitans withdraw.
- **951** — Guo Wei founds the Later Zhou.
- **960** — Zhao Kuangyin seizes power, founding the Song dynasty.
- **979** — Song conquers the Northern Han, ending the period.

## Background
The period's roots lie in the weakening of Tang central authority after the An Lushan Rebellion (755–763 CE). Regional military governors (*jiedushi*) gained de facto independence, controlling their own armies, wealth, and hereditary administrations. By the early 10th century, these warlords declared themselves rulers, leading to constant warfare. Historian Hugh Clark models the era in three stages: chaotic warlord fighting (880–910), stabilization and dynastic proclamation (910–950), and forceful reunification by the Later Zhou and Song (950–979).

## The Five Dynasties (North)
The "Five Dynasties" refer to the succession of regimes that claimed the Mandate of Heaven by controlling the Central Plain. The first, the **Later Liang (907–923)**, was founded by Zhu Wen, a former Tang general. It was overthrown by the **Later Tang (923–936)**, founded by Li Cunxu of the Shatuo Turks. The **Later Jin (937–947)**, also Shatuo, was founded by Shi Jingtang, who ceded the Sixteen Prefectures to the Khitan Liao dynasty for military support. The brief **Later Han (947–951)** was another Shatuo regime. The final dynasty, the **Later Zhou (951–960)**, was founded by the Han Chinese general Guo Wei. His successor, Chai Rong, began reunification efforts. In 960, general Zhao Kuangyin staged a coup, founding the Song dynasty and ending the Five Dynasties era. These northern regimes were characterized by frequent coups and were disproportionately led by military families from the northeast, some of mixed Han-Turkic ancestry.

## The Ten Kingdoms (South)
In contrast to the north, southern China was divided among more concurrent and generally more stable kingdoms. These states, founded by military men often of low social origin, promoted economic development and culture to legitimize their rule. Key kingdoms included:
*   **Wu** and its successor **Southern Tang**, which dominated the middle Yangtze.
*   **Wuyue**, known for its learning and longevity.
*   **Min**, **Southern Han**, and **Former/Later Shu**, which controlled coastal and Sichuan regions.
*   **Jingnan**, a small, weak state that maintained good relations with northern dynasties.
*   **Northern Han**, a Shatuo-led holdout in Shanxi protected by the Liao.
The southern economies prospered through trade, agriculture, and infrastructure. The Song dynasty gradually conquered these kingdoms between 963 and 979.

## Culture and Law
Culturally, the period was a transition from the Tang's internationalism to the Song's more national culture. Southern states preserved artistic and literary traditions that were later incorporated into the Song. Innovations included "white ceramics" and Taoist-inspired landscape painting. Legally, the era is noted for judicial abuses, severe torture, and a departure from Tang legal procedures, though the Tang code remained the statutory foundation.

## Terms
*   **Five Dynasties**: The five successive dynasties (Later Liang, Tang, Jin, Han, Zhou) that controlled the Central Plain in northern China.
*   **Ten Kingdoms**: The most powerful of the concurrent dynastic states established in southern China after the Tang's fall.
*   **Jiedushi**: Regional military governors who gained hereditary, autonomous control over their provinces during the late Tang, leading to fragmentation.
*   **Mandate of Heaven**: The traditional Chinese belief that heaven grants the right to rule to a just leader; its "sharing" was a pragmatic concept during this period.
*   **Shatuo Turks**: A Turkic people whose military leaders founded three of the Five Dynasties (Later Tang, Later Jin, Later Han).
*   **Sixteen Prefectures**: Strategic territories in modern northern Hebei and Beijing ceded by the Later Jin to the Khitan Liao dynasty.
*   **Flying Money (*feiqian*)**: Early bank drafts or certificates of deposit developed during this period to address currency shortages.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The traditional count of "Ten" Kingdoms is debated; historian Bo Yang counts eleven, excluding Northern Han.
*   The historian Hugh Clark's three-stage model provides a framework for understanding the period's political evolution from chaos to reunification.
*   The extent to which the northern dynasties' rulers, despite claiming imperial status, dealt with each other as "sibling states" in diplomatic equality is a noted historical concept.
*   The period's legacy is viewed through contrasting northern and southern lenses, with Song-era histories written from a northern viewpoint often portraying the southern kingdoms as illegitimate.
