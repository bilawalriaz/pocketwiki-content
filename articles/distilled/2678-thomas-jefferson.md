# Thomas Jefferson

## Overview
Thomas Jefferson (1743–1826) was a Founding Father, the third U.S. President, and the principal author of the Declaration of Independence. He was a central figure in the American Revolution, the expansion of the United States through the Louisiana Purchase, and the development of American political philosophy. His legacy is complex, marked by his advocacy for liberty and his lifelong ownership of enslaved people.

## Body

### Early Life and Revolutionary Career
Born into Virginia's planter class, Jefferson was educated at the College of William & Mary, where he studied law and the Enlightenment ideas of Locke, Bacon, and Newton. He began his political career in the Virginia House of Burgesses. As a delegate to the Second Continental Congress, he was the primary author of the Declaration of Independence (1776), articulating the principle that "all men are created equal." He later served as Governor of Virginia and, in 1785, as U.S. Minister to France.

### Political Rise and Presidency
Jefferson's political philosophy, which favored agrarian democracy and states' rights, clashed with Alexander Hamilton's Federalist vision of a strong central government and national bank. This conflict led to the formation of the Democratic-Republican Party. After serving as the first Secretary of State and then Vice President under John Adams, Jefferson won the presidency in 1800. His administration reduced the national debt, cut taxes, and doubled the nation's size with the Louisiana Purchase (1803). He authorized the Lewis and Clark Expedition to explore the new territory. His second term was marked by foreign policy challenges, including the Embargo Act of 1807, which aimed to protect U.S. shipping but caused economic distress.

### Slavery and the Hemings Relationship
Jefferson's relationship with slavery is a central paradox of his life. He wrote that slavery was a moral evil and proposed gradual emancipation, yet he enslaved over 600 people during his lifetime and never freed most of them. He freed only a few individuals in his lifetime and in his will. Since 1802, it has been alleged that he fathered children with Sally Hemings, an enslaved woman at Monticello. A 1998 DNA study and subsequent historical research have led most scholars to conclude that Jefferson was the father of her son Eston Hemings and likely all six of her children.

### Later Life and Legacy
After his presidency, Jefferson founded the University of Virginia, designing its curriculum and architecture. He rekindled a famous correspondence with his old rival John Adams. Both men died on July 4, 1826, the 50th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence. Jefferson is consistently ranked among the top U.S. presidents for his advocacy of religious freedom, the Louisiana Purchase, and his role in shaping American democracy.

## Terms
- **Democratic-Republican Party:** The political party founded by Jefferson and James Madison, which advocated for states' rights, agrarian democracy, and a limited federal government, opposing the Federalist Party.
- **Louisiana Purchase:** The 1803 acquisition of the Louisiana Territory from France for $15 million, which doubled the geographic size of the United States.
- **Embargo Act of 1807:** A law signed by Jefferson that banned all American ships from trading with foreign nations, intended to pressure Britain and France but which severely damaged the U.S. economy.
- **Lewis and Clark Expedition:** The 1804–1806 expedition commissioned by Jefferson to explore the newly acquired Louisiana Territory and find a route to the Pacific Ocean.
- **Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom:** A 1786 law drafted by Jefferson that disestablished the Church of England in Virginia and guaranteed freedom of religion, a precursor to the First Amendment.
- **Marbury v. Madison:** The 1803 Supreme Court case that established the principle of judicial review, allowing courts to declare laws unconstitutional, which arose from a political battle during Jefferson's presidency.
- **Sally Hemings:** An enslaved woman at Monticello with whom Jefferson is now widely believed to have had a long-term relationship and fathered several children.
- **Three-Fifths Compromise:** A 1787 agreement that counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for congressional representation and taxation, which helped secure Jefferson's victory in the 1800 election.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Interpretations of Slavery:** Scholars debate whether Jefferson was a proto-abolitionist whose efforts were thwarted by political compromise or a hypocrite who entrenched slavery to protect his wealth and social status.
- **Constitutionality of the Louisiana Purchase:** Jefferson himself had doubts about the federal government's constitutional authority to acquire new territory, a question that remains a point of historical discussion.
- **The Embargo Act's Effectiveness:** Historians assess whether the Embargo Act was a principled stand for neutrality or a disastrous policy that crippled the American economy and failed to change British or French behavior.
- **Jefferson's Religious Beliefs:** While he championed religious freedom, his personal beliefs—rejecting the Trinity, miracles, and the resurrection—have led to ongoing debate about whether he was a Deist, a Unitarian, or something else entirely.
