# Chinua Achebe

## Overview
Chinua Achebe (1930–2013) was a Nigerian novelist, poet, and critic widely regarded as the father of modern African literature. His debut novel, *Things Fall Apart* (1958), challenged Western narratives about Africa by centering Igbo perspectives and remains the most widely read African novel. Achebe’s work fused Igbo oral traditions with English prose to explore colonialism, tradition, masculinity, and postcolonial identity, profoundly shaping global perceptions of African literature.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into a Christian family in Ogidi, Achebe was immersed in both Igbo traditional culture and colonial Christianity. His father, Isaiah Achebe, was a Protestant convert who retained respect for Odinani (Igbo traditional religion). Storytelling, masquerades, and religious syncretism shaped Achebe’s worldview. At Government College Umuahia and later University College Ibadan (now University of Ibadan), he studied English, history, and theology after abandoning medicine. Exposure to Western literature—including Joyce Cary’s *Mister Johnson*—deepened his critique of colonial representations of Africa.

### Literary Career and Major Works
Achebe worked for the Nigerian Broadcasting Service (NBS) in Lagos, where he developed skills in dialogue and narrative structure. *Things Fall Apart* (1958) depicted precolonial Igbo society and its disruption by British rule, countering Eurocentric portrayals. The novel’s success led to *No Longer at Ease* (1960), exploring post-independence disillusionment, and *Arrow of God* (1964), examining cultural clash through a priest’s downfall. *A Man of the People* (1966), a satirical critique of postcolonial corruption, eerily predicted the 1966 Nigerian coup. His final novel, *Anthills of the Savannah* (1987), addressed military dictatorship.

### Political Engagement and Biafran War
During the 1967–1970 Nigerian Civil War, Achebe supported Biafran independence and served as its ambassador, advocating internationally for humanitarian aid. He co-founded Citadel Press with Christopher Okigbo to promote Nigerian literature. Post-war, he edited *Okike*, a journal promoting African arts, and engaged in Nigerian politics through the People’s Redemption Party. Disillusioned by corruption, he withdrew from partisan politics but remained vocal on social justice.

### Postcolonial Critique and Academic Life
In 1975, Achebe delivered his seminal lecture "An Image of Africa," critiquing Joseph Conrad’s *Heart of Darkness* as racist and dehumanizing. The talk sparked intense debate but became foundational in postcolonial studies. After a paralyzing car accident in 1990, he joined Bard College, teaching for over a decade before moving to Brown University. He consistently defended writing in English while advocating for African voices and cultural authenticity.

### Style and Themes
Achebe’s fiction draws on Igbo oral traditions—proverbs, folktales, and communal storytelling—to craft a distinctly African narrative voice. He used English strategically, arguing it enabled cross-cultural communication while allowing him to “push back” linguistic boundaries to express African thought. Themes include the tension between tradition and modernity, the impact of colonialism, gender roles, and the moral complexities of postcolonial leadership. His tragic endings reflect both African and classical influences, emphasizing collective responsibility over individual heroism.

## Terms
- **Odinani**: Traditional Igbo religious beliefs and practices.
- **Agbala**: An Igbo term for a man without title, often associated with weakness or femininity.
- **Heinemann African Writers Series**: A publishing initiative launched in 1962 to promote African literature globally.
- **Postcolonial discourse**: Scholarly analysis of the cultural, political, and psychological effects of colonialism.
- **Oral tradition**: The passing of stories, histories, and cultural values through spoken word.
- **Yam farmer**: A symbolic figure in Igbo society representing strength, masculinity, and cultural identity.
- **Umuofia**: A fictional Igbo village setting in *Things Fall Apart*.
- **Biafra**: The southeastern region of Nigeria that declared independence in 1967, leading to civil war.

## Debates and Open Questions
Achebe’s portrayal of gender has drawn criticism for reinforcing patriarchal norms, though scholars like Ajoke Mimiko Bestman argue his work critiques misogyny through characters like Okonkwo. His rejection of the “father of African literature” label reflects ongoing debates about authorship, authenticity, and Western categorization. The controversy surrounding his critique of Conrad highlights tensions in interpreting colonial-era texts. Additionally, his stance on writing in English versus indigenous languages remains debated among African writers and scholars. Finally, his political activism and support for Biafra continue to influence discussions on literature’s role in postcolonial resistance.
