# Tijuana

## Overview
Tijuana is the most populous city in Baja California and northern Mexico. Located on the U.S.–Mexico border adjacent to San Diego, it forms the core of the San Diego–Tijuana metropolitan area, one of the world's largest bi-national conurbations. Its history and economy are fundamentally shaped by its border location, leading to rapid growth, economic dynamism, and significant challenges with crime.

## Timeline
- **1829** — Santiago Argüello establishes Rancho Tía Juana, the origin of the city's name.
- **1848** — End of Mexican–American War; the international border is established near Tijuana.
- **11 July 1889** — Founding of the city of Tijuana.
- **1920s** — U.S. Prohibition makes Tijuana a major destination for American tourists seeking gambling and alcohol.
- **1928** — The Agua Caliente Touristic Complex opens, becoming a legendary resort.
- **1935** — President Cárdenas outlaws gambling in Baja California, leading to Agua Caliente's decline.
- **1965** — The Border Industrialization Program launches, spurring *maquiladora* growth.
- **1982** — The Tijuana Cultural Center (CECUT) opens, marking a shift towards cultural tourism.
- **2007–2010** — Intense cartel-related violence, with homicides peaking in 2010.
- **2018** — Tijuana records 2,253 homicides, one of the highest rates globally.

## Body

### Name and Origins
The city's name derives from "Rancho Tía Juana," established in 1829. The most accepted theory is that "Tía Juana" adapts the Kumeyaay word *Tiwan* ("by the sea"). The land was originally inhabited by the Kumeyaay people. Spanish colonists mapped the coast in the 16th–17th centuries, but significant settlement began after the 1829 land grant.

### 19th and Early 20th Century: Border Town and Tourism
Following the Mexican–American War (1848), Tijuana became a border town. Urban development began with its 1889 founding. From the late 19th century, it attracted American tourists, a trend that exploded during Prohibition in the 1920s. The Agua Caliente complex, opened in 1928, epitomized this era until gambling was outlawed in 1935. The population grew from about 22,000 in 1940 to over 65,000 in 1950.

### Late 20th Century: Industrialization and Modernization
The 1965 Border Industrialization Program transformed Tijuana's economy by attracting foreign investment and *maquiladoras* (assembly plants). This industrial boom drew migrants from across Mexico, causing the population to surge from under 500,000 in 1980 to nearly 1 million by 1985. In the 1970s–80s, channeling the Tijuana River created land for the modern Zona Río business district, symbolizing the city's evolution into a modern metropolis.

### 21st Century: Growth, Violence, and Cultural Renaissance
The 21st century has been marked by extreme contrasts. Tighter U.S. border controls after 9/11 and escalating drug violence, particularly between 2007 and 2010, damaged tourism. Homicide rates remain among the world's highest, driven by cartel activity. Concurrently, Tijuana has emerged as a major manufacturing center, especially for medical devices, and a cultural hotspot known for its cuisine, craft beer, and art scene. The city continues to grow rapidly, largely through unregulated hillside settlement, posing infrastructure and safety risks.

### Geography and Climate
Tijuana is Mexico's westernmost city, nestled among hills and canyons. The intermittent Tijuana River flows through a central valley. The climate is hot semi-arid (Köppen BSh) with mild, wet winters and warm, dry summers. Air pollution can occur during temperature inversions, but frost and snow are extremely rare.

### Demographics and Crime
Tijuana has a diverse, cosmopolitan population, including large communities from other Mexican states, Asia, and the United States. Rapid growth fueled by migration leads to high poverty levels. The city is the birthplace of the Tijuana Cartel and has been severely impacted by the Mexican drug war. In 2018, it recorded 2,253 homicides, and in 2022, it was reported to have the world's second-highest homicide rate.

### Economy and Tourism
The economy is based on tourism, manufacturing, and trade. Tijuana is a dominant North American manufacturing hub, hosting hundreds of *maquiladoras*. It is the world's most visited border city, with over 50 million annual border crossings with San Diego. Tourism centers on shopping, dining, nightlife, and medical tourism. The city is also a major center for the Baja Med culinary movement.

### Culture and Entertainment
Tijuana's cultural identity has evolved from its tourist-town roots. The 1982 opening of CECUT was a milestone. The city has a vibrant art scene, active nightlife, and significant musical history, from the Tijuana Brass in the 1960s to modern Nortec. It is also known for its red-light district, Zona Norte.

## Terms
- **Maquiladoras:** Foreign-owned assembly plants that import materials duty-free for assembly and export.
- **Conurbation:** A large metropolitan area formed by merging cities, like San Diego–Tijuana.
- **Kumeyaay:** The indigenous people of the San Diego-Tijuana region.
- **Border Industrialization Program:** A 1965 Mexican initiative to attract foreign investment via *maquiladoras*.
- **Zona Río:** Tijuana's modern central business district, built on reclaimed river land.
- **CECUT:** The Tijuana Cultural Center, a major arts institution.
- **Baja Med:** A culinary movement blending Mexican, Mediterranean, and Asian flavors.
- **Nortec:** A music genre blending Norteño and techno, originating in Tijuana.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact meaning of "Tijuana" is debated, with the Kumeyaay-derived "by the sea" being most accepted versus the folk etymology "Aunt Jane."
- The true population is uncertain, with discrepancies between census data (e.g., 1.3 million in 2010) and city estimates (e.g., 1.6–2 million in 2010).
- The causes behind high-profile violence, such as the 1994 assassination of presidential candidate Luis Donaldo Colosio, remain subjects of speculation.
