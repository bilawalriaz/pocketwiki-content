# Túpac Amaru II

## Overview
Túpac Amaru II (c. 1742–1781) was an Indigenous Peruvian cacique (chieftain) who led a massive rebellion against Spanish colonial rule in 1780–1781. Though not initially seeking independence, his revolt against oppressive labor and tax systems became a foundational symbol of Indigenous resistance and social justice in the Americas, inspiring later independence movements and 20th-century leftist politics.

## Timeline
- **c. 1742** — Born José Gabriel Condorcanqui Noguera in Surimana, Cusco province.
- **1760** — Married Micaela Bastidas Puyucahua.
- **After father's death** — Inherited caciqueship of Tungasuca and Pampamarca; became a wealthy muleteer.
- **Late 1770s** — Experienced economic crisis; petitions to colonial authorities for relief were ignored.
- **4 November 1780** — Captured and executed Corregidor Antonio de Arriaga, launching the rebellion.
- **16 November 1780** — Decreed abolition of slavery for Black people in Spanish America.
- **18 November 1780** — Won Battle of Sangarará, killing 578 Spanish soldiers.
- **Early 1781** — Failed to capture Cusco; rebellion began to falter.
- **April 1781** — Betrayed and captured by his own officers.
- **18 May 1781** — Executed by dismemberment in Cusco's Plaza de Armas after witnessing his family's deaths.
- **1781–1782** — Rebellion continued under his cousin Diego Cristóbal Túpac Amaru and others.
- **1968–1975** — Declared a national hero by Peru's revolutionary government under Juan Velasco Alvarado.

## Body

### Early Life and Grievances
Born into the Quechua nobility, José Gabriel Condorcanqui was a direct descendant of Túpac Amaru I, the last Inca ruler of Vilcabamba. Educated by Jesuits, he inherited a chieftainship and amassed wealth as a merchant. As a recognized cacique, he mediated between his communities and Spanish authorities. He repeatedly petitioned to alleviate burdens like the *mita* (forced draft labor for mines) and excessive taxes, but his requests were ignored in Tinta, Cusco, and Lima. Witnessing widespread economic distress and exploitation, he adopted the name Túpac Amaru II to claim his royal Inca lineage and organize resistance.

### The Rebellion
The revolt began on 4 November 1780 with the execution of the abusive Corregidor Antonio de Arriaga. Túpac Amaru II's initial proclamation framed the rebellion as a protest against colonial abuses, not independence. He sought to abolish oppressive systems like the *mita*, forced goods distribution, and *obrajes* (textile workshops). On 16 November 1780, he decreed the abolition of slavery. The movement attracted tens of thousands of Indigenous peasants, artisans, and women, including his wife Micaela Bastidas, who commanded troops. Ideologically, it blended demands for reform within the colonial system with visions of restoring the Inca Empire (*Tahuantinsuyo*) as an egalitarian society based on the *ayllu* (communal agricultural system).

### Defeat and Execution
After early victories, the rebellion faltered. A critical failure was the inability to capture Cusco, where Spanish reinforcements repelled his army. Betrayed by two officers, Túpac Amaru II was captured in April 1781. He was forced to watch the execution of his wife, son, and relatives before his own brutal dismemberment by horses in Cusco's main plaza on 18 May 1781. His body parts were displayed across the region to deter further revolt. Despite his death, the rebellion continued under his cousin Diego Cristóbal and others until 1782.

### Legacy and Recognition
Though the rebellion failed, it marked the first large-scale Indigenous uprising in the Spanish colonies and instilled a new sense of Indigenous nationalism. Its legacy was reinterpreted over time. In the 20th century, particularly during the military government of Juan Velasco Alvarado (1968–1975), Túpac Amaru II was elevated to a national hero symbolizing social justice. His name has been adopted by political movements worldwide, including the Uruguayan Tupamaros guerrillas and the Peruvian MRTA insurgent group. He remains a potent symbol of resistance in Peru and across the Americas.

## Terms
- **Cacique/Curaca**: An Indigenous chief or leader recognized within the colonial administrative system.
- **Mita**: A Spanish colonial system of forced draft labor, especially for silver mining in places like Potosí.
- **Corregidor**: A Spanish provincial governor and judge, often responsible for collecting tribute and enforcing labor drafts.
- **Encomienda**: A colonial labor system granting a Spanish colonist the right to demand tribute and labor from Indigenous people; abolished in 1720 but replaced by other exploitative systems.
- **Ayllu**: The traditional Andean communal social and economic unit based on collective land ownership and agriculture.
- **Sapa Inca**: The supreme ruler of the Inca Empire.
- **Tahuantinsuyo**: The name for the Inca Empire, meaning "the four regions together."
- **Obrajes**: Colonial-era textile workshops, often using forced Indigenous labor.
- **Bourbon Reforms**: A series of administrative and economic changes implemented by the Spanish Bourbon monarchy in the 18th century to centralize control and increase revenue, often worsening conditions for colonized peoples.
- **Creole (Criollo)**: A person of Spanish descent born in the Americas.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nature of the Rebellion**: Scholars debate whether Túpac Amaru II's goal was initially to reform the colonial system from within or to overthrow it entirely. His proclamations often appealed to the Spanish king, suggesting a reformist stance, while his adoption of the Inca title implied a separatist vision.
- **Ideological Complexity**: The rebellion contained diverse elements, from conservative demands for a return to pre-reform conditions to radical, proto-egalitarian ideas that sought to "turn the world upside down" by abolishing racial hierarchies and forced labor.
- **Role of Women**: The significant participation of women like Micaela Bastidas and Bartola Sisa (who led after Túpac Katari's capture) is increasingly studied, challenging traditional narratives focused solely on male leaders.
- **Legacy and Symbolism**: The reinterpretation of Túpac Amaru II by 20th-century leftist and nationalist governments raises questions about the appropriation of historical figures for contemporary political agendas.
