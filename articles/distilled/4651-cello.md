# Cello

## Overview
The violoncello, or cello, is a medium-low pitched bowed string instrument of the violin family, essential to classical music as both a solo and ensemble instrument. It provides the bass voice in string quartets and is a core member of the symphony orchestra, known for its wide expressive range from bass to soprano registers.

## Timeline
- **c. 1500** — Violin family instruments, including cello-sized ones, emerge distinct from the viola da gamba family.
- **c. 1530** — Earliest depictions of the violin family in Italy show three sizes: violin, viola, and cello.
- **1538 (possible)** — The first bass violin, a direct ancestor to the cello, is possibly invented.
- **1607** — Claudio Monteverdi refers to the instrument as "basso de viola da braccio" in *Orfeo*.
- **c. 1700** — Italian players popularize the cello in northern Europe.
- **c. 1750** — The size and design of the cello begin to be standardized.
- **1741** — Michel Corrette publishes one of the earliest cello manuals.
- **c. 1845** — Adrien Servais introduces the endpin for greater stability.
- **1920s-30s** — Aluminum cellos are produced by Alcoa and G.A. Pfretzschner.
- **1989** — Michael Bach begins developing a curved bow (BACH.Bow) for polyphonic playing.

## Body

### Etymology and History
The name "cello" is a shortened form of the Italian "violoncello," meaning "little violone" (big viola). The instrument is part of the viola da braccio ("viola for the arm") family, distinct from the viola da gamba family. Contrary to a popular misconception, the cello did not evolve from the viola da gamba but existed alongside it for about two and a half centuries. The earliest surviving cellos were made by Andrea Amati. The modern cello's design, including the endpin, was standardized around 1750, replacing earlier methods where the instrument was held between the player's calves.

### Construction and Design
A traditional cello is made from carved wood, typically with a spruce top and maple back, sides, and neck. Key components include the bridge (which transfers string vibrations), f-holes (for sound projection), an internal bass bar and soundpost, and a tailpiece with fine tuners. The endpin, introduced c. 1845, supports the instrument on the floor. Modern cellos have higher string tension than Baroque cellos, producing a louder, more projecting tone. Bows are traditionally made from pernambuco wood with horsehair.

### Tuning and Playing Technique
The cello's four strings are tuned in perfect fifths: C2, G2, D3, and A3 (an octave below the viola). Music is primarily written in bass clef, using tenor and treble clefs for higher passages. The player sits, supporting the cello with the endpin, and uses the left hand to stop strings on the fingerboard and the right hand to bow or pluck (pizzicato). Techniques include vibrato, harmonics, glissando, and various bowing styles like legato and staccato.

### Repertoire and Modern Use
The cello has a vast solo repertoire, including J.S. Bach's six unaccompanied Suites and concertos by composers like Vivaldi, Haydn, Dvořák, and Elgar. In orchestras, it provides low-register harmony and occasional solos. It is also central to chamber music, particularly string quartets. In the 20th century, virtuoso Mstislav Rostropovich inspired many new works. The cello appears in popular music (e.g., The Beatles, Pink Floyd), jazz, world music, and "cello rock" ensembles like Apocalyptica.

## Terms
- **Violoncello (Cello):** A medium-low pitched bowed string instrument of the violin family.
- **Basso Continuo:** A Baroque-era musical practice where a bass instrument (like cello) and a chordal instrument (like harpsichord) provide harmonic support.
- **Endpin:** A retractable spike at the base of the cello that supports the instrument on the floor.
- **Soundpost:** A small wooden dowel wedged inside the cello between the top and back plates, crucial for sound transmission.
- **Pizzicato:** A technique where strings are plucked with the fingers instead of bowed.
- **Vibrato:** A slight, rapid oscillation in pitch used for expressive effect.
- **Wolf Tone:** An acoustic anomaly causing a harsh, growling sound when a played note resonates with the cello's body.
- **Luthier:** A specialist who builds and repairs stringed instruments.
