# Cao Cao

## Overview
Cao Cao (c. 155–220 AD) was a Chinese warlord, statesman, and poet who rose to power during the Eastern Han dynasty's collapse. He controlled the Han central government, laying the foundation for the state of Cao Wei, established by his son Cao Pi. His life marks the transition to the Three Kingdoms period and remains a subject of scholarly debate and cultural fascination.

## Timeline
- **c. 155** — Born in Qiao County, Pei State.
- **184** — Begins career during the Yellow Turban Rebellion; appointed Chancellor of Jinan State.
- **189** — Raises an army to oppose Dong Zhuo after he seizes power.
- **196** — Receives Emperor Xian, establishes a new capital at Xuchang, and takes control of the Han government.
- **200** — Defeats rival warlord Yuan Shao at the decisive Battle of Guandu.
- **207** — Unifies northern China after campaigns against Yuan Shao's heirs and the Wuhuan tribes.
- **208** — Appointed Imperial Chancellor; defeated at the Battle of Red Cliffs by Sun Quan and Liu Bei.
- **211** — Defeats the Guanzhong coalition led by Ma Chao and Han Sui at the Battle of Tong Pass.
- **213** — Created Duke of Wei by Emperor Xian.
- **216** — Elevated to King of Wei.
- **220** — Dies in Luoyang; succeeded by his son Cao Pi.
- **220** — Cao Pi forces Emperor Xian to abdicate, founding Cao Wei and ending the Eastern Han.

## Body

### Rise to Power and Control of the Han
Cao Cao began as an Eastern Han official, gaining prominence in the 190s by recruiting followers in Yan Province. His pivotal move came in 196 AD when he received the figurehead Emperor Xian, establishing a new capital at Xuchang. This allowed him to control the central government nominally under the emperor, issuing orders in his name and implementing the *tuntian* (agricultural colony) system to sustain his armies.

### Military Campaigns and Unification of the North
Throughout the 190s, Cao Cao waged wars against rivals like Lü Bu, Yuan Shu, and Zhang Xiu. His triumph over Yuan Shao at the Battle of Guandu in 200 AD was a turning point. Over the next seven years, he defeated Yuan Shao's heirs and allies, unifying northern China by 207 AD.

### Southern Campaign and Stalemate
In 208 AD, after becoming Imperial Chancellor, Cao Cao launched a southern expedition. He was decisively defeated at the Battle of Red Cliffs by the alliance of Sun Quan and Liu Bei, preventing unification and establishing a tripartite balance of power. Subsequent attempts to annex southern lands failed.

### Later Campaigns and Honors
Cao Cao consolidated power, defeating a coalition at Tong Pass (211 AD) and seizing Hanzhong from Zhang Lu in 215 AD, though he later lost it to Liu Bei. He received increasing honors, becoming Duke of Wei in 213 AD and King of Wei in 216 AD. He died in 220 AD, and his son Cao Pi soon forced Emperor Xian to abdicate, establishing the Cao Wei dynasty.

### Historical Sources and Legacy
The primary source is Chen Shou's *Records of the Three Kingdoms*, annotated by Pei Songzhi. These draw on favorable sources like the *Book of Wei* and hostile ones like the *Cao Man zhuan*. Cao Cao was an accomplished poet, contributing to the Jian'an style. Opinions are divided: he is praised as a brilliant leader and poet but condemned as a treacherous tyrant, a stereotype solidified in the *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*.

## Terms
- **Tuntian**: Agricultural colonies established by Cao Cao to produce grain for his armies by resettling refugees on state-owned land.
- **Imperial Chancellor (Chengxiang)**: The highest Han ministerial office, held by Cao Cao from 208 AD, granting formal administrative control.
- **Three Kingdoms**: The period (220–280 AD) after the Han, characterized by the tripartite division among Cao Wei, Shu Han, and Eastern Wu.
- **Jian'an style**: A poetic style named after the Jian'an era (196–220 AD), associated with Cao Cao and his sons, known for vigor and emotional depth.
- **Xiaolian**: A Han civil service recommendation system based on moral character and reputation.
- **Wuhuan**: A nomadic people in northern China involved in regional politics, against whom Cao Cao campaigned.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historical vs. Literary Image**: A major debate concerns the gap between the historical Cao Cao and the treacherous villain portrayed in the *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* and traditional opera.
- **Legitimacy of His Rule**: Scholars debate whether Cao Cao was a loyal minister preserving the Han or a de facto usurper. His acceptance of titles like King of Wei while the emperor reigned is central.
- **Authenticity of His Tomb**: A tomb discovered in Anyang in 2009, claimed to be Cao Cao's, sparked controversy. While officially endorsed, some experts have questioned its authenticity, though the academic community has largely accepted it.
