# Strait of Hormuz

## Overview
The Strait of Hormuz is a strategically vital waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the open ocean. It is the world's most important oil chokepoint, with approximately 20% of global seaborne oil and 25% of liquefied natural gas (LNG) trade passing through it annually. Its control is a major point of geopolitical tension, particularly between Iran and the United States, and its disruption can cause severe global energy supply shortages.

## Timeline
- **1st century AD** — Described but unnamed in the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*.
- **15th–18th centuries** — Portuguese presence and control, followed by disputes with England.
- **1959** — Iran expands its territorial sea to 12 nautical miles (nmi).
- **1971** — Iran seizes the Greater and Lesser Tunbs islands, extending control over navigation channels.
- **1972** — Oman expands its territorial sea to 12 nmi, fully enclosing the strait's narrowest section.
- **1980s** — The "Tanker War" during the Iran–Iraq War, with attacks on commercial shipping.
- **1988** — U.S. Navy shoots down Iran Air Flight 655 over the strait.
- **2015** — Iran's IRGCN seizes the Marshall Islands–flagged container ship *Maersk Tigris*.
- **2019** — Attacks on oil tankers *Front Altair* and *Kokuka Courageous*; Iran seizes the *Stena Impero*.
- **2025** — Iran threatens to block the strait in response to Israeli attacks; U.S. strikes Iranian nuclear sites.
- **2026** — Following the assassination of Iran's supreme leader, Iran's IRGC closes the strait, causing a major global crisis.

## Body

### Strategic Importance and Navigation
The strait is about 104 miles (167 km) long and varies in width from 60 miles (97 km) to 24 miles (39 km). It is the sole sea passage from the Persian Gulf, making it critical for the energy exports of Gulf states like the UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, and Iraq. A traffic separation scheme (TSS) in Omani waters organizes ship flow, but very large vessels often must use Iranian waters due to shallow depths near the UAE. The strait is completely enclosed by the territorial waters of Iran and Oman, creating a complex legal situation regarding transit passage.

### Legal and Sovereignty Disputes
Both Iran and Oman have made declarations that conflict with the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS). Oman requires prior permission for foreign warships. Iran has enacted laws requiring permission for warships, submarines, and nuclear-powered ships to exercise innocent passage. The U.S., which has not ratified UNCLOS, contests these claims and asserts the right of transit passage. Iran has periodically threatened to close the strait, most notably during tensions in 2008, 2011–2012, and 2018–2019, often in response to sanctions or military threats.

### The 2026 Iran War and Closure
The strait became a central theater during the 2026 Iran War. Following U.S. strikes on Iranian nuclear sites and the assassination of Iran's supreme leader, the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) officially closed the strait in early March 2026. This action, which included deploying mines and threatening to fire on ships, caused a collapse in tanker traffic, restricted over 90% of shipments, and led to a global energy crisis. The closure prompted a U.S. naval blockade and intense diplomatic efforts, with Iran eventually agreeing to reopen the strait as part of ceasefire negotiations in April 2026, though the situation remained volatile.

### Smuggling and Alternative Routes
The strait is a major route for illicit trade, with goods smuggled from the UAE to Iran via the Omani exclave of Khasab to evade sanctions. In response to the threat of closure, regional states have developed alternative pipelines. The UAE's Habshan–Fujairah pipeline (capacity ~2 million barrels per day) bypasses the strait entirely. Saudi Arabia has also reopened the IPSA pipeline to the Red Sea. However, these pipelines cannot replace the strait's total capacity, and all LNG must still transit by ship.

## Terms
*   **Chokepoint:** A narrow passage along a key trade route that is strategically important and vulnerable to closure or disruption.
*   **UNCLOS (United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea):** An international treaty establishing a legal framework for all marine and maritime activities, including rights of passage through straits.
*   **Transit Passage:** A right under UNCLOS allowing ships and aircraft to pass freely through straits used for international navigation, which cannot be suspended.
*   **Innocent Passage:** A more restricted right under UNCLOS allowing foreign ships to pass through a state's territorial waters only if the passage is not prejudicial to peace, good order, or security.
*   **Traffic Separation Scheme (TSS):** A maritime traffic management system that separates opposing streams of traffic into defined lanes to reduce collision risks.
*   **IRGC (Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps):** A branch of Iran's military responsible for protecting the Islamic system and controlling Iran's strategic weapons, including its naval forces in the Persian Gulf.
*   **JCPOA (Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action):** The 2015 nuclear deal between Iran and world powers, from which the U.S. withdrew in 2018.

## Debates and Open questions
*   **Legal Authority:** A central debate is whether Iran or Oman can legally restrict passage through the strait, given their territorial claims and declarations that conflict with customary international law and UNCLOS provisions on transit passage.
*   **Iran's Capability and Will:** Scholars debate the feasibility and duration of a full Iranian blockade. Some analyses suggest Iran could impede traffic for a month, while others argue its own economic dependence on the strait for imports and exports makes a prolonged closure logistically and economically self-defeating.
*   **Strategic Calculus:** Analysis describes Iran's policy as "double-faced": it acts as a security provider in normal times but uses the strait as a tool for brinkmanship and deterrence when facing existential threats, a strategy constrained by its own reliance on the waterway.
