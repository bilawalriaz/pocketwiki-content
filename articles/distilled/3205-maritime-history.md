# Maritime history

## Overview
Maritime history studies human interaction with the sea, covering navigation, trade, naval warfare, shipbuilding, and seafaring's social and cultural impacts. This global, interdisciplinary field traces how maritime activity shaped civilizations, economies, and geopolitical power from prehistory to the present.

## Timeline
- **c. 50,000 BC** — First sea voyages reach Greater Australia (Sahul).
- **c. 3500–3000 BC** — Earliest sailing vessel depictions in Mesopotamia.
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Austronesian expansion from Taiwan begins.
- **15th–17th c.** — Age of Discovery: European voyages establish global trade routes.
- **1588** — Spanish Armada defeated by England.
- **1805** — Battle of Trafalgar establishes British naval supremacy.
- **1862** — Battle of Hampton Roads: first ironclad clash ends Age of Sail.
- **1914–1918** — WWI introduces unrestricted submarine warfare.
- **1939–1945** — Battle of the Atlantic and Pacific War redefine naval strategy.
- **1999** — Panama Canal transferred to Panamanian control.
- **21st c.** — Rise of piracy, Arctic competition, and stealth technology.

## Body

### Historiography and Approaches
Maritime history encompasses fishing, naval history, trade, navigation, and culture. Scholars divide into "Traditionalists" (academic-focused) and "Utilitarians" (policy-influencing). Recent work shifts from ship minutiae ("rivet counting") to studying human users and sea travel as part of wider transport history. "Sub-Oceanic History" examines the ocean's interior as a historically produced space.

### Prehistory and Ancient Seafaring
Watercraft were used prehistorically, with voyages to Australia c. 50,000 years ago. The oldest known boat is the Pesse canoe (c. 8040–7510 BCE). Ancient sailing emerged in Mesopotamia (c. 3500–3000 BCE) and Egypt (c. 3100 BCE). The Austronesian expansion (from c. 3000 BCE) used technologies like the crab claw sail to colonize the Indo-Pacific. Rome developed military and trade ships, while Phoenicians reportedly circumnavigated Africa c. 600 BCE.

### Age of Navigation and Trade Networks
By 1000 BCE, Austronesians traded regularly with Asia. Vikings developed oceagoing longships by c. 190 AD. Chinese sea-going ships, based on Austronesian designs, emerged in the 10th century. Key tools included the mariner's astrolabe (used by 1481) and magnetic compass (China by 1088, Europe by c. 1190). Major medieval networks included the Hanseatic League (13th–17th centuries) and Italian maritime republics (e.g., Venice, Genoa), which reconnected Europe with Asia and fostered early capitalism.

### Age of Discovery and European Expansion
Driven by bullion shortages and Ottoman trade control, Europeans developed the caravel and carrack. The Portuguese pioneered the sea route to India, while Columbus's 1492 voyage initiated widespread colonization of the Americas. This Columbian Exchange integrated global economies and rose colonial empires as public-private ventures. Concurrently, China's Ming dynasty launched Zheng He voyages (1405–1433) before turning inward.

### Age of Sail and Naval Warfare
The Age of Sail (c. 1571–1862) was dominated by sailing ships. Key events include the Spanish Armada's defeat (1588) and Battle of Trafalgar (1805). The triangular trade linked Europe, Africa, and the Americas in exchanging slaves, sugar, and rum. Piracy was a major hazard until navies like the British Royal Navy suppressed it by the 1720s. Life at sea was harsh, with captains prioritizing owners and cargo. Passenger travel improved with 19th-century government regulations.

### Transition to Steam and Modern Navies
Steam propulsion replaced sail in the 19th century, enabled by the triple-expansion engine and iron hulls. The first ironclad, *Gloire*, launched in 1859, and the Battle of Hampton Roads (1862) marked the end of wooden warships. The Greek War of Independence (1821–1829) highlighted naval power's strategic importance. By the late 19th century, steam was predominant, and navies adopted torpedoes and mines.

### 20th-Century Conflicts and Technology
WWI saw the first aircraft carriers and unrestricted submarine warfare, countered by the convoy system. WWII's Battle of the Atlantic was pivotal against U-boats, while the Pacific War was defined by carrier battles. The latter 20th century introduced nuclear propulsion, sonar, and blockades like the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962). The Falklands War (1982) demonstrated naval task forces' continued importance.

### 21st-Century Challenges
Modern issues include piracy (annual losses of $13–$16 billion), Arctic resource and shipping route competition, and stealth ship development. Social changes include women serving as admirals and submarine crew members.

## Terms
- **Thalassocracy**: A state with primary power based on the sea.
- **Entrepôt**: A port city for importing, storing, and re-exporting goods.
- **Caravel**: A small, maneuverable 15th-century sailing ship for exploration.
- **Carrack**: A large, ocean-going 15th-century sailing ship for trade and warfare.
- **Clipper**: A fast 19th-century sailing ship for long-distance trade.
- **Ironclad**: A mid-19th-century steam-powered warship with iron/steel armor.
- **Submarine**: A watercraft capable of independent underwater operation.
- **Convoy system**: Grouping merchant ships under naval escort for protection.
- **Stealth ship**: A vessel designed to evade radar, sonar, or other detection.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise date and nature of first seafaring voyages to Australia remain unknown.
- The extent and impact of pre-Columbian transatlantic contact is debated.
- The authorship and veracity of Amerigo Vespucci's published accounts are disputed.
- The effectiveness and morality of historical naval blockades, such as Britain's in WWI, are subjects of ongoing analysis.
