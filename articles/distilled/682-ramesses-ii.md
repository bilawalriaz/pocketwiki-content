# Ramesses II

## Overview
Ramesses II (c. 1303–1213 BC), third pharaoh of the Nineteenth Dynasty, is widely regarded as the most powerful ruler of Egypt’s New Kingdom. His 66-year reign (1279–1213 BC) featured extensive military campaigns against the Hittites, Nubians, and Libyans, culminating in the earliest known surviving peace treaty (c. 1259 BC). He transformed the landscape with massive building projects—including a new capital, Pi-Ramesses, and the temples of Abu Simbel and the Ramesseum—and celebrated an unprecedented thirteen or fourteen Sed festivals. Though popularly identified as the pharaoh of the Exodus, Egyptologists and historians reject this association due to a lack of archaeological evidence and chronological incompatibility.

## Timeline
- **c. 1303 BC** — Birth of Ramesses II.
- **c. 1292 BC** — Grandfather Ramesses I becomes pharaoh; Ramesses II (~age 11) enters royal court.
- **c. 1290 BC** — Father Seti I becomes pharaoh; Ramesses II (~age 14) named prince regent.
- **31 May 1279 BC** — Accession to the throne (III Shemu day 27).
- **Year 2 (1277 BC)** — Decisive naval victory over Sherden pirates and Lukka/Shekelesh peoples.
- **Year 4 (1275 BC)** — First Syrian campaign; captures Hittite vassal Amurru; erects stela at Nahr el-Kalb.
- **1 May 1274 BC** — Battle of Kadesh (Year 5) against Muwatalli II; tactical Egyptian victory but strategic stalemate; retreat from Syria.
- **Year 7 (1272 BC)** — Third Syrian campaign; splits army; recaptures Upi (Damascus region) and reaches Tunip.
- **Year 8–9 (1271–1270 BC)** — Campaigns to Dapur in northern Amurru; erects statue; gains prove ephemeral as territory reverts to Hittites.
- **Year 10 (1269 BC)** — Second campaign against Dapur; six sons participate.
- **Year 18 (1261 BC)** — Stele erected at Beth Shean (19 January).
- **Year 21 (1259 BC)** — Treaty of Kadesh signed with Ḫattušili III (10 November); dual-language (hieroglyphic/cuneiform) copies survive.
- **1255 BC** — Inauguration of Abu Simbel temples with Queen Nefertari.
- **Year 30 (1249 BC)** — First Sed festival (jubilee).
- **13 August 1213 BC** — Death (Year 67, II Akhet day 6), aged ~90; reign of 66 years, 74 days.
- **1881** — Mummy discovered in Royal Cache (TT320).
- **2021** — Mummy moved to National Museum of Egyptian Civilization (Pharaohs’ Golden Parade).

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Ramesses II was not born a prince. His grandfather, Ramesses I, was a vizier and military officer appointed successor by Horemheb; at the time, Ramesses II was about eleven. Upon Ramesses I’s death, his son Seti I became king and designated Ramesses II prince regent around age fourteen. Ramesses ascended the throne at 25 on 31 May 1279 BC (III Shemu day 27). Early in his reign, he focused on domestic building projects, founding the Delta city of Pi-Ramesses as a new capital and staging point for Levantine campaigns.

### Military Campaigns
**Sherden Pirates (Year 2):** Ramesses ambushed and captured Sherden sea raiders—likely from Ionia, Anatolia, or Sardinia—along the Mediterranean coast. Many Sherden were incorporated into the pharaoh’s personal bodyguard, identifiable by horned helmets and Naue II swords.

**Syrian Campaigns & Battle of Kadesh:** Ramesses sought to reclaim territories lost to the Hittites and emulate his father Seti I. In Year 4, he captured the Hittite vassal Amurru. In Year 5 (1274 BC), he marched to Kadesh, where Muwatalli II ambushed him. Egyptian reinforcements routed the Hittite chariotry, but Ramesses, unable to sustain a siege, withdrew. Though he claimed victory, scholars consider the campaign a strategic stalemate; Hittites soon occupied Damascus.

**Later Syrian Campaigns (Years 7–10):** In Year 7, Ramesses split his army: one force under son Amun-her-khepeshef captured Edom-Seir and Moab; Ramesses took Jerusalem, Jericho, and recaptured Upi. In Years 8–9, he pushed north to Dapur and Tunip—territory unseen by Egyptians since Thutmose III (~120 years prior)—but gains evaporated within a year. Year 10 saw a second Dapur campaign with six sons participating. By Year 18, Egyptian presence was marked by a stele at Beth Shean.

**Peace Treaty with the Hittites (Year 21, 1259 BC):** A diplomatic crisis erupted when Ḫattušili III, who had deposed his nephew Muršili III, demanded the exile’s extradition from Egypt. Ramesses denied knowledge, nearly triggering war. Instead, both sides concluded the Treaty of Kadesh, recorded in Egyptian hieroglyphs and Hittite cuneiform. Uniquely, each version claims the other sued for peace. The treaty established a stable northern frontier; the harbour town of Sumur (north of Byblos) marked Egypt’s northernmost garrison. No further Canaanite campaigns are recorded.

**Nubian Campaigns:** Ramesses campaigned south of the First Cataract into Nubia, a colony for 200 years. At ~22, he fought alongside sons Amun-her-khepeshef and Khaemwaset. Campaigns are commemorated at temples in Beit el-Wali, Gerf Hussein, and Kalabsha; one relief depicts him charging alone against tribes.

**Libyan Campaigns:** Egyptian forts along a 300 km Mediterranean stretch (e.g., Zawyet Umm El Rakham) indicate military control over Libyan territory. Detailed battle accounts are absent; records may refer to Seti I’s earlier campaigns or generalized claims of conquest.

### Sed Festivals
By Year 28, Ramesses elevated Amun above other gods. He celebrated his first Sed festival (royal jubilee) at Year 30 (1249 BC), traditionally held to rejuvenate the pharaoh’s strength. Breaking the standard three-year interval, he held them every two years at times, totaling thirteen or fourteen—more than any other pharaoh.

### Building Projects and Monuments
Ramesses launched the most ambitious building program since the pyramids (c. 1,500 years prior), “covering the land with buildings.” He deeply engraved reliefs to resist usurpation and enhance solar symbolism (linking to Ra). Art served as propaganda, depicting victories over foreigners. His cartouches appear even on monuments he did not build. He expanded gold mining at Akuyati (Wadi Allaqi).

**Pi-Ramesses:** New capital in the eastern Delta (“Domain of Ramesses, Great in Victory”), dominated by temples and a palace with a zoo. Motives uncertain; possibly proximity to Canaan/Syria. Misidentified as Tanis until 20th-century work located it near modern Qantir (~30 km south). Only colossal statue feet remain above ground.

**Ramesseum:** Mortuary temple in western Thebes (between Qurna and the desert). Oriented northwest-southeast; featured two courts, massive pylons, a 17 m, 1,000+ tonne syenite seated statue (fragments remain), and a hypostyle hall (41 × 31 m) with 39 of 48 columns standing. Walls depict Kadesh “victories” and the god Min. Storerooms and a scribal school surrounded the complex.

**Abu Simbel:** Inaugurated 1255 BC with Nefertari. Two rock-cut temples; the Great Temple’s facade features four 20 m colossal seated statues of Ramesses. Designed to deify the living king. Discovered 1813 (Burckhardt); entered 1817 (Belzoni).

**Other Nubian Monuments:** Temples at Beit el-Wali (early campaigns), Derr, Gerf Hussein (relocated to New Kalabsha), and additions to the Amun temple at Jebel Barkal (founded under Thutmose III).

**Later Discoveries:** Colossal statue (83 tonnes) moved from Memphis to Cairo (1955), then Grand Egyptian Museum (2006). 2018: possible royal booth/seat at Matariya. 2019: red granite “Ka” statue bust at Mit Rahina (first granite Ka statue found). 2023: fragment of original granite sarcophagus identified (reused by 21st Dynasty priest Menkheperre). 2024: gold sword with Ramesses’ signature found at a Nile fort.

### Death and Burial
Ramesses died 13 August 1213 BC (Year 67, II Akhet day 6), aged ~90, after 66 years and 74 days. He suffered severe dental abscesses, arthritis, arteriosclerosis, and heart disease; CT scans show a pronounced stoop. Cause of death unknown; jaw abscess infection is a leading hypothesis.

**Mummy:** Originally buried in KV7 (Valley of the Kings). Looting prompted priests to move the body: first to queen Ahmose Inhapy’s tomb, then 72 hours later to high priest Pinedjem II’s tomb—recorded on coffin linen. Discovered 1881 in TT320 (Royal Cache) in a reused wooden coffin. Now in National Museum of Egyptian Civilization (moved 2021 via Pharaohs’ Golden Parade).

Physical analysis: ~1.7 m tall, aquiline nose, strong jaw, white/auburn hair dyed light red by henna (Maspero) or naturally red (Bucaille/Ceccaldi). Red hair linked to deity Set; family connections noted (father Seti I = “follower of Set”; grandfather Ramesses I = High Priest of Set). Forensic debate: Ceccaldi (1976) concluded “Berber type,” fair-skinned; Cheikh Anta Diop disputed hair morphology as ethnic indicator. 1976: mummy sent to Paris for fungal treatment (full military honours; no passport issued). 2004 study: diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (not ankylosing spondylitis) caused hunched back. Mandibular abscess noted.

**Royal Family Burials:**
- **Nefertari (QV66):** Discovered 1904 (Schiaparelli). Magnificent wall paintings (Book of the Dead chapters 17, 144, 146); astronomical ceiling; looted in antiquity.
- **KV5 (Sons):** Rediscovered 1995 (Kent Weeks). Largest tomb in Valley of Kings (~150 corridors/chambers found by 2006; up to 200 estimated). Held mummies of ~52 sons; inscriptions name Meryatum, Sety, Amun-her-khepeshef, and “Generalissimo Ramesses.” Thoroughly looted; no intact burials or sarcophagi found.

### Exodus Association
Despite frequent portrayal in media (e.g., *The Ten Commandments*, *The Prince of Egypt*, *Exodus: Gods and Kings*), scholars reject Ramesses II as the Exodus pharaoh. Egyptologist Mostafa Waziry (2023) and historian Lester L. Grabbe cite: no archaeological evidence for Israelite slave labor or the Exodus in Egyptian records; Pi-Ramesses construction shows no foreign slave labor; Ramesses’ firm control of Canaan into Syria contradicts a devastated Egypt; Hyksos ruled earlier. The association stems from his historical prominence and the biblical name “Raamses” (Exodus 1:11).

## Terms
- ****Sed festival (Heb-Sed)**** — Royal jubilee traditionally held in Year 30 and every three years after to ritually rejuvenate the pharaoh’s strength and legitimacy.
- ****Prince regent**** — Heir apparent appointed to exercise royal authority during the reigning king’s lifetime; Ramesses II held this role under Seti I from ~age 14.
- ****Naue II sword**** — A type of European/Anatolian cut-and-thrust sword (c. 13th century BC) adopted by Egyptians and depicted in Kadesh reliefs; carried by Sherden bodyguards.
- ****Vassal state**** — A polity subordinate to a suzerain power; Amurru was a Hittite vassal before Ramesses captured it.
- ****Cuneiform**** — Wedge-shaped script on clay tablets used for Hittite and Akkadian; the Treaty of Kadesh survives in both hieroglyphic (Egyptian) and cuneiform (Hittite) versions.
- ****Royal Cache (TT320)**** — A concealed tomb shaft near Deir el-Bahri where priests reburied multiple New Kingdom royal mummies (including Ramesses II) to protect them from looting; discovered 1881.
- ****Ka statue**** — A statue intended to house the *ka* (vital essence/soul) of the deceased; the 2019 granite bust is the first royal Ka statue found in stone (previous example: wood, 13th Dynasty).
- ****Pi-Ramesses (Per-Ramesses)**** — “Domain of Ramesses, Great in Victory”; new capital founded in the eastern Delta, serving as military staging point for Levantine campaigns.
- ****Ramesseum**** — Ramesses II’s mortuary temple in western Thebes; famed for its colossal syenite statue (>1,000 tonnes) and Kadesh reliefs.
- ****Abu Simbel**** — Rock-cut temple complex in Nubia; Great Temple facade features four 20 m seated colossi of Ramesses; aligned so sunlight illuminates inner sanctuary statues twice yearly.

## Debates and open questions
- **Exodus identification:** Source explicitly states scholars (Mostafa Waziry, Lester L. Grabbe) dispute the popular association; no Egyptian evidence supports Israelite slave labor or a mass exodus during his reign.
- **Kadesh outcome:** Egyptian sources claim victory; modern consensus views it as a tactical Egyptian success but strategic stalemate, with Hittites retaining Syria.
- **Mummy’s red hair:** Maspero attributed color to henna dye; Bucaille and Ceccaldi argued for natural red hair (implying family trait and Set association); Cheikh Anta Diop contested hair morphology as an ethnic marker.
- **Libyan campaign attribution:** Unclear if coastal forts reflect Ramesses II’s own actions or Seti I’s earlier campaigns; no detailed battle accounts survive for Ramesses.
- **Chronology of later Syrian campaigns:** Source notes “precise chronology… not clear” for campaigns after Year 7 due to monuments lacking regnal dates.
- **Cause of death:** Unknown; leading hypothesis is fatal infection from a mandibular dental abscess, but not certain.
- **Sarcophagus fate:** Original granite sarcophagus fragment identified 2023 (reused by 21st Dynasty priest Menkheperre); location of the complete original sarcophagus (and whether it was ever used) remains open.
