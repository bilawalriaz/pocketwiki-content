# Car

## Overview  
A car (automobile) is a motor vehicle with wheels, typically seating 1–8 people and transporting passengers rather than cargo. There are over 1.6 billion cars in use worldwide as of 2025. The modern car emerged in 1886 when Carl Benz patented the Benz Patent-Motorwagen, though earlier steam and electric prototypes date to the 18th century. Cars reshaped global mobility, economics, and urban form, but also generate significant environmental, health, and social costs.

## Timeline  
- **c. 1672** — Ferdinand Verbiest builds a steam-powered model car for the Kangxi Emperor
- **1769** — Nicolas-Joseph Cugnot constructs the first full-scale steam-powered road vehicle
- **1808** — François Isaac de Rivaz designed and constructed the first internal combustion-powered automobile
- **1886** — Carl Benz patents the Benz Patent-Motorwagen, marking the birth of the modern car
- **1901** — Ransom Olds introduces the first mass-produced car, the Oldsmobile Curved Dash
- **1908** — Ford Model T launches, becoming the first mass-affordable car
- **1913** — Henry Ford implements the moving assembly line at Highland Park
- **1920s** — Nearly all cars become mass-produced; marketing begins shaping design
- **1970s–2020s** — Electric and hybrid vehicles gain commercial traction amid climate concerns

## Body  

### Etymology  
The word *car* derives from Latin *carrus* or Gaulish *karros*, originally referring to any wheeled horse-drawn vehicle. “Motor car” (1895) is standard in British English; “automobile” (from Greek *autos* “self” and Latin *mobilis* “movable”) is common in North America.

### Early Development  
Steam-powered vehicles preceded internal combustion engines. Nicolas-Joseph Cugnot’s 1769 steam tricycle is widely credited as the first full-scale self-propelled vehicle. In 1808, François Isaac de Rivaz designed and constructed the first internal combustion automobile. Carl Benz’s 1886 Patent-Motorwagen is considered the foundation of the modern car. Bertha Benz’s 1888 road trip demonstrated its practicality. Around the same time, Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach developed competing technologies, leading to the Mercedes brand.

### Mass Production  
Ransom Olds pioneered large-scale production in 1901 using stationary assembly lines. Henry Ford revolutionized this in 1913 with the moving assembly line, reducing build time from over 12 hours to under two. This method, termed “Fordism,” spread globally. By 1930, companies without assembly lines often failed. Shared platforms and components became standard, enabling cost efficiency and variety.

### Propulsion and Fuels  
Most cars in the early 2020s use internal combustion engines (ICE) burning petrol or diesel. Alternatives include hybrid, plug-in electric, and hydrogen vehicles. Electric cars, which were invented early in the history of the car, became commercially available in the 2000s and widespread in the 2020s. As of 2025, one in four cars sold is electric but, despite rapid growth, less than one in twenty cars on the world's roads were fully electric and plug-in hybrid cars by the end of 2024.

### Design and Features  
Cars include controls for driving, comfort, and safety—steering wheel, pedals, gear shifts, lights, and climate systems. Modern cars feature airbags, ABS, navigation, and infotainment. Physical controls are increasingly replaced by touchscreens and electronic systems. Weight has increased due to safety features and batteries, typically ranging from 1 to 3 tonnes. Body styles vary widely: sedans, hatchbacks, SUVs, and sports cars accommodate different seating and cargo needs.

### Safety  
Traffic collisions remain the leading cause of injury-related deaths globally. Standard safety tests include Euro NCAP and IIHS evaluations. Regulations now mandate data recorders and automated braking in some regions. Early fatalities include Mary Ward (1869) and Henry Bliss (1899).

### Environmental Effects  
As of 2024, cars produce 10% of global carbon dioxide emissions. They contribute to air pollution via exhaust, brake/tire wear, and microplastics. Habitat fragmentation and roadkill affect wildlife. Governments impose emission standards and low-emission zones. By 2035, all newly sold cars in the European market must be Zero-emissions vehicles. Electric cars produce roughly half the lifetime emissions of petrol/diesel models as of 2023.

### Social and Economic Impact  
Mass car ownership boosted mobility and economic growth, especially in the U.S., Australia, and Europe. However, it also caused congestion, urban sprawl, and social inequities. Low-income communities face barriers to employment due to car dependency. Air pollution from cars increases risks of stroke, heart disease, COPD, and childhood asthma. Urban planning around cars reduces walkability and independence for non-drivers.

### Emerging Technologies  
Battery improvements, lidar sensors, wireless charging, and connected car systems are advancing rapidly. Autonomous vehicles operate as robotaxis in limited areas. Car-sharing services reduce private ownership. New materials like carbon fiber and aluminum aim to lighten vehicles. Open-source car designs exist but are not widespread.

### Industry  
The automotive industry produces over 56 million cars annually (as of 2020), with China leading manufacturing. The sector faces pressure to decarbonize, driven by legislation like the EU’s “Fit for 55” package. Competition grows from public transit and shared mobility solutions.

## Terms  
- **Internal Combustion Engine (ICE):** Engine that burns fuel internally to generate power.  
- **Regenerative Braking:** System that recovers kinetic energy during braking and stores it in a battery.  
- **Fordism:** Manufacturing approach combining high wages with high efficiency, pioneered by Henry Ford.  
- **Zero-Emission Vehicle (ZEV):** Vehicle emitting no tailpipe pollutants, including electric and hydrogen cars.  
- **Low-Emission Zone (LEZ):** Urban area restricting access for high-polluting vehicles.  
- **Assembly Line:** Production system where each worker performs a specialized task as the product moves along a conveyor.  
- **Plug-in Hybrid Electric Vehicle (PHEV):** Car combining an ICE with a rechargeable electric battery.  
- **Telematics:** Technology enabling vehicle communication and data exchange, used in navigation and diagnostics.  
- **Urban Sprawl:** Low-density, car-dependent urban expansion increasing infrastructure and environmental costs.  
- **Car Sharing:** Service allowing short-term vehicle rental without ownership.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- Who truly invented the automobile: Benz, Daimler, or earlier pioneers like de Rivaz?  
- Should the phase-out of fossil fuel cars be accelerated to meet climate targets?  
- How can cities balance car convenience with sustainability and equity?  
- Will autonomous vehicles achieve broad deployment, and what regulatory changes are needed?  
- Are electric vehicles truly cleaner when accounting for battery production and electricity sources?
