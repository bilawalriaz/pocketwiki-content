# Surgery

## Overview
Surgery is a medical specialty employing manual and instrumental techniques to diagnose or treat pathological conditions, alter bodily functions, reconstruct or alter appearance, or remove unwanted tissues. It encompasses a wide range of procedures—from emergency interventions to elective cosmetic surgeries—and requires a coordinated team including surgeons, anesthetists, nurses, and technologists. The practice is governed by principles of aseptic technique and involves preoperative preparation, intraoperative execution, and postoperative care. Surgery has evolved from ancient practices like trepanation to modern precision techniques, and remains a critical component of global healthcare, though access remains uneven worldwide.

## Timeline
- **Prehistoric** — Trepanation practiced to treat intracranial conditions
- **c. 2650 BC** — Ancient Egyptians perform dental abscess drainage
- **First millennium BCE** — Sushruta Samhita documents surgical procedures in India
- **c. 400 BCE** — Hippocrates stated in the oath "I will not use the knife, even upon those suffering from stones, but I will leave this to those who are trained in this craft"
- **1530s–1590** — Ambroise Paré advances battlefield surgery in France
- **1761–1824** — John Hunter pioneers scientific surgery in Scotland
- **1840s** — Ether and chloroform introduce practical anesthesia
- **1847** — Ignaz Semmelweis noticed that medical students fresh from the dissecting room were causing excess maternal death compared to midwives
- **1860s** — Joseph Lister developed antiseptic techniques after learning of Pasteur's work on micro-organisms
- **1895** — Wilhelm Röntgen discovers X-rays for diagnostic imaging
- **2015** — Lancet Commission on Global Surgery (LCoGS) published the landmark report titled "Global Surgery 2030: evidence and solutions for achieving health, welfare, and economic development"

## Body

### Definitions and Classifications
A procedure is generally considered surgical if it involves cutting tissues to treat disease or injury. Surgeries are classified by urgency (elective, urgent, emergency), purpose (bariatric, exploratory, plastic, cosmetic, reconstructive, therapeutic, curative), type of procedure (excision, amputation, bypass, grafting, implantation, repair, resection, transplantation), body system (cardiac, neuro, orthopedic, etc.), invasiveness (open, hybrid, minimally invasive), and equipment used (cryosurgery, electrosurgery, laser, robotic). Terminology follows standardized suffixes: *-ectomy* (removal), *-otomy* (cutting into), *-oscopy* (endoscopic), *-ostomy* (stoma creation), *-plasty* (reconstruction), *-myotomy* (muscle cutting), and *-orraphy* (repair).

### Surgical Settings and Care Phases
Surgery occurs in inpatient, outpatient, or office-based settings. In 2011, of the 38.6 million hospital stays in U.S. hospitals, 29% included at least one operating room procedure. The process includes preoperative care (examination, testing, consent, NPO orders), intraoperative management (anesthesia, sterile technique, procedural steps), and postoperative care (monitoring, pain management, rehabilitation). Aseptic technique governs all phases, requiring sterilized instruments, sterile attire, and hand hygiene. Postoperative complications include infections, pain, cognitive dysfunction, and prolonged recovery.

### Special Populations and Global Context
Older adults face elevated risks due to frailty. One frailty scale uses five items: unintentional weight loss, muscle weakness, exhaustion, low physical activity, and slowed walking speed. A healthy person scores 0; a very frail person scores 5. Compared to non-frail elderly people, people with intermediate frailty scores (2 or 3) are twice as likely to have post-surgical complications, spend 50% more time in the hospital, and are three times as likely to be discharged to a skilled nursing facility instead of to their own homes. People who are frail and elderly (score of 4 or 5) have even worse outcomes, with the risk of being discharged to a nursing home rising to twenty times the rate for non-frail elderly people. Pediatric surgery requires developmental considerations, while vulnerable populations (incarcerated, cognitively impaired) need special consent protocols. Globally, Halfdan T. Mahler, the 3rd Director-General of the World Health Organization (WHO), first brought attention to the disparities in surgery and surgical care in 1980 when he stated: "the vast majority of the world's population has no access whatsoever to skilled surgical care and little is being done to find a solution." The 2015 Lancet Commission identified 143 million annual unmet surgical needs and $12.3 trillion in projected economic losses by 2030. The lack of adequate surgical and anesthesia care has resulted in 33 million individuals every year facing catastrophic health expenditure. The WHO's resolution WHA68.15 and the National Surgical Obstetric and Anesthesia Plan (NSOAP) aim to expand access through infrastructure development and policy frameworks.

### Historical Development
Surgical history spans millennia, beginning with trepanation and advancing through ancient civilizations. Key milestones include Sushruta's texts, Galen's innovations, and medieval Islamic contributions by Al-Zahrawi, who pioneered neurosurgery and obstetric techniques. The Renaissance emphasized empirical dissection via Vesalius, while Paré improved amputation methods. The 18th–19th centuries saw scientific rigor introduced by Hunter, followed by anesthesia (1840s), antisepsis (Semmelweis, Lister), and diagnostic imaging (X-rays, 1895). These advances laid the foundation for modern surgical practice.

## Terms
- **Aseptic technique**: Strict separation of sterile and unsterile elements to prevent infection.
- **Anastomosis**: Surgical connection between two hollow structures (e.g., blood vessels, intestines).
- **Bellwether procedures**: Essential surgeries (laparotomy, caesarean section, open fracture care) indicating minimum emergency care capacity.
- **Catastrophic health expenditure**: Out-of-pocket costs exceeding 40% of household income.
- **Elective surgery**: Non-urgent procedure scheduled at patient/facility convenience.
- **Frailty scale**: Five-item assessment (weight loss, weakness, exhaustion, low physical activity, walking speed) predicting surgical outcomes in elderly patients.
- **Ligation**: Tying off blood vessels or ducts to prevent bleeding or flow.
- **Reoperation**: Return to surgery for planned or unplanned reasons (e.g., complications, margin clearance).
- **Stoma**: Permanent or semi-permanent opening in the body (e.g., colostomy).
- **Weekday effect**: Higher mortality risk for elective surgeries performed later in the week or on weekends.

## Debates and Open Questions
Several areas remain contentious: routine preoperative chest X-rays are discouraged for low-risk patients due to risks of misdiagnosis and overtreatment; evidence is insufficient to confirm whether preemptive opioid administration reduces postoperative pain; and the use of topical antibiotics on surgical wounds is debated despite Cochrane findings showing reduced infection rates. Additionally, the "weekday effect" in surgical mortality—linked to reduced weekend staffing and service availability—remains under investigation. Globally, scaling surgical access in low-resource settings raises questions about cost-effectiveness versus competing health priorities, though studies suggest surgical interventions rival established public health measures in DALYs averted.
