# St. Lawrence Seaway

## Overview
The St. Lawrence Seaway is a binational system of locks, canals, and channels that allows oceangoing vessels to travel from the Atlantic Ocean to the Great Lakes, reaching as far inland as Duluth, Minnesota. It is a critical infrastructure for North American trade, enabling the transport of bulk cargo like grain and steel, though its economic and environmental impacts are subjects of ongoing debate.

## Timeline
- **1824–1829** — First Welland Canal constructed with small locks.
- **1890s** — First proposals for a deep-water seaway are made.
- **1932** — Treaty of intent signed by Canada and the U.S., but fails U.S. Senate ratification in 1934.
- **1951** — Canada announces it will build the seaway alone, leading to U.S. agreement.
- **May 13, 1954** — U.S. President Eisenhower signs the Wiley-Dondero Seaway Act, authorizing joint construction.
- **1959** — The St. Lawrence Seaway officially opens to navigation.
- **1998** — The Canada Marine Act restructures the Canadian portions of the seaway.
- **2011** — A U.S. Army Corps of Engineers study to expand the seaway is scrapped due to budget issues.

## Body

### Development and Construction
The seaway was preceded by a series of canals, most notably the Welland Canal, whose locks were progressively enlarged to accommodate larger ships. Proposals for a comprehensive deep-waterway began in the 1890s, often linked to hydroelectric power development. Decades of negotiations between the U.S. and Canada were stalled by political opposition, particularly from Atlantic coast ports and railroads. The seaway, built at a cost of C$470 million, opened in 1959.

### Operation and Dimensions
The seaway consists of seven locks on the St. Lawrence River and eight on the Welland Canal. The locks are 766 feet (233.5 m) long, 80 feet (24.4 m) wide, and 30 feet (9.14 m) deep, but the maximum vessel size is slightly smaller (the "Seawaymax" limit). Channel depths vary, from 41 feet (12.5 m) downstream of Quebec City to 27 feet (8.2 m) upstream of Montreal. These dimensions mean only about 10% of modern oceangoing ships can traverse the entire system. The seaway is managed by the St. Lawrence Seaway Management Corporation (Canada) and the Great Lakes St. Lawrence Seaway Development Corporation (U.S.).

### Trade and Economic Impact
The seaway handles 40–50 million tonnes of cargo annually, with about 50% traveling to/from international ports. Key exports include grain, while inbound cargo includes steel. Its opening is often linked to the decline of the Erie Canal, though railroads had already diminished its importance. The seaway's economic benefits are debated; while it boosted Great Lakes ports, changing global trade patterns (e.g., Europe no longer being a major grain importer) have shifted shipping to Gulf and West Coast ports.

### Ecology and Environment
Construction required flooding areas to create Lake St. Lawrence, submerging several communities known as "The Lost Villages." A major adverse effect has been the introduction of invasive species, like the zebra mussel, which have damaged ecosystems and infrastructure. The seaway also provides recreational opportunities, including boating and scuba diving among shipwrecks. Low water levels in the Great Lakes in recent years have posed challenges for navigation.

## Terms
- **Seawaymax**: The maximum vessel size that can transit the seaway locks: 740 ft (225.6 m) long, 78 ft (23.8 m) wide, and 26.5 ft (8.1 m) deep.
- **Welland Canal**: A key canal in the seaway system that bypasses Niagara Falls, connecting Lake Ontario and Lake Erie.
- **Binational**: Jointly managed by two countries (Canada and the United States).
- **Hydroelectric**: Relating to the generation of electricity from the energy of falling or flowing water.
- **Invasive species**: Non-native organisms whose introduction causes ecological or economic harm.
- **Panamax**: The maximum size of ship that can fit through the original locks of the Panama Canal.
- **Bulk cargo**: Unpackaged goods like grain, coal, or ore transported in large quantities.
- **Draft**: The vertical distance between the waterline and the bottom of a ship's hull.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Economic Justification**: Did the seaway's benefits justify its cost, given subsequent changes in global trade and the prior decline of the Erie Canal?
- **Environmental Impact**: How can the negative effects of invasive species and altered water levels be mitigated?
- **Future Viability**: Should the seaway be expanded to accommodate larger modern ships, or are the costs and environmental risks too high?
- **Climate Resilience**: How will increasingly frequent low water levels in the Great Lakes affect the seaway's operations and regional economies?
