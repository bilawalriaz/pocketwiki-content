# Chord (music)

## Overview
A chord is the simultaneous sound of two or more musical notes, forming the fundamental building block of harmony in Western music. Chords and their progressions provide the structural and emotional foundation for melodies, evolving from incidental byproducts of polyphony to primary, functional elements that define musical style across centuries.

## Timeline
- **16th century** — Seventh chords begin to appear in music.
- **17th century** — Spelling of "chord" standardized to avoid confusion with "cord."
- **Baroque era** — Figured bass notation develops; dominant seventh chords become a fixture.
- **18th century** — Roman numeral analysis for chords is introduced.
- **19th century** — Hugo Riemann develops the theory of functional harmony.
- **20th century** — Chords expand greatly in complexity, incorporating added tones, dissonance, and new structures like tone clusters.

## Body

### Definition and Evolution
A chord is defined as the sound of two or more notes played together. The concept of harmony, initially any two-note combination, evolved during the Renaissance to mean the simultaneous sound of three notes. A chord progression is a sequence of harmonies with a specific purpose, such as reinforcing the tonic (home key) or modulating (changing key). The study of harmony focuses on these progressions and the principles governing their connection.

### Historical Development
Chords emerged as distinct elements from polyphonic (multi-voiced) music between the 16th and 18th centuries. In early organum, chords were incidental results of melodic lines. The Baroque era saw melodies often accompanied simply by chords, with the dominant seventh chord becoming a key functional element. As tonality expanded, composers used chromatic chords (like the Neapolitan) for their sonic qualities, not just their function. The 20th century greatly expanded chordal vocabulary, with jazz developing a particularly eclectic range.

### Structure and Notation
A common triad (three-note chord) consists of a root, third, and fifth. Chords are identified by their intervals and basic qualities: major, minor, diminished, and augmented. Extended chords (like ninths, elevenths, and thirteenths) are built by stacking thirds and are central to jazz. Chords can be inverted, meaning the root is not the lowest note; Jean-Philippe Rameau developed the standard nomenclature for inversions. Notation methods include figured bass (Baroque), Roman numerals, the Nashville Number System, and alphabetical symbols.

### Function and Analysis
Chords are often analyzed by their relationship to the tonic and dominant scale degrees, a concept formalized by Hugo Riemann as functional harmony. Traditional part-writing rules, inherited from polyphony, govern how chord tones (like the dissonant tritone in a dominant seventh) resolve. As music became more chromatic, composers borrowed chords from other keys and used altered chords to obscure traditional root relationships, sometimes asserting tonality through emphasis alone.

## Terms
- **Chord**: The simultaneous sound of two or more musical notes.
- **Triad**: A three-note chord consisting of a root, third, and fifth.
- **Arpeggio**: A chord whose notes are sounded successively rather than simultaneously.
- **Chord Progression**: A sequence of chords with a specific harmonic purpose.
- **Figured Bass**: A Baroque shorthand notation where numbers above a bass note indicate the harmony to be played.
- **Roman Numeral Analysis**: A system labeling chords by their scale degree (e.g., I, IV, V).
- **Nashville Number System**: A 20th-century shorthand using numbers to represent chord roots relative to a key.
- **Inversion**: A chord where the root is not the lowest sounding note.
- **Functional Harmony**: A theory analyzing chords by their relationship to the tonic or dominant.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that chords can be "spelled and analyzed in a dizzying number of ways," with the most essential aspect being "how they sound." This implies an ongoing tension between theoretical analysis and aural perception in understanding harmony.
