# Pope Leo I

## Overview
Pope Leo I (c. 391–461), known as Leo the Great, served as Bishop of Rome from 440 to 461. He is the first of three popes designated "the Great" and a Doctor of the Church. His pontificate defined the Church’s response to collapsing imperial authority in the West and Christological controversy in the East. Leo asserted papal primacy as the heir of Peter the Apostle, using Roman legal concepts of inheritance (*vicarius*) to argue that the Bishop of Rome holds unique, universal jurisdiction. Theologically, his *Tome* established the doctrine of the hypostatic union—Christ as one person in two natures, divine and human, "without confusion or division"—which became the standard of orthodoxy at the Council of Chalcedon (451). Politically, he negotiated with Attila the Hun (452) and the Vandal king Genseric (455), mitigating the destruction of Rome. His reign marks the decisive transition of the papacy from a spiritual office into a central governing institution of Western Christendom.

## Timeline
- **c. 391** — Born in Tuscany, son of Quintianus/Quintilianus.
- **431** — As deacon, receives dedication of John Cassian’s anti-Nestorian treatise; involved in Cyril of Alexandria’s appeal to Rome.
- **440** — 29 September: Elected Pope unanimously while on imperial mission to Gaul.
- **443–444** — Condemns and eradicates Manichaeans in Rome; writes treatise against Priscillianists (447).
- **445** — 6 June: Obtains imperial decree from Valentinian III enforcing papal primacy and extradition of recalcitrant bishops.
- **449** — *Tome* presented at Second Council of Ephesus ("Robber Synod"); ignored; Flavian deposed.
- **451** — Council of Chalcedon accepts *Tome* as orthodox; defines hypostatic union; Leo rejects canons elevating Constantinople.
- **452** — Leads embassy to Attila the Hun; Attila withdraws from Italy.
- **455** — Negotiates with Vandal king Genseric; prevents burning of Rome and spares major basilicas.
- **461** — 10 November: Dies; buried in portico of Old St. Peter’s Basilica (first pope buried there).
- **1754** — Proclaimed Doctor of the Church by Benedict XIV.

## Body

### Early Life and Rise
Born to a Tuscan aristocratic family, Leo was a deacon by 431, prominent enough for John Cassian to dedicate a treatise against Nestorius to him at Leo’s suggestion. He corresponded with Cyril of Alexandria regarding jurisdictional disputes and was dispatched by Emperor Valentinian III to Gaul to mediate between the general Aëtius and the magistrate Albinus—a sign of imperial confidence. While abroad, Pope Sixtus III died (11 August 440), and Leo was unanimously elected on 29 September.

### Pastoral Governance and Heresy
Leo immediately enforced doctrinal discipline. In Aquileia, he censured the readmission of Pelagians without formal abjuration, ordering a synod to enforce repudiation. In Rome, he identified a Manichaean cell (possibly refugees from Vandal Africa), preached against them (late 443), and secured civil trials where suspects confessed under torture. By early 444, he declared the sect eradicated and burned their books. He similarly combated Priscillianism in Spain, writing a detailed treatise (21 July 447) and urging a general council to purge the episcopate. Pastorally, he organized charity during famines and refugee crises, linking fasting to almsgiving on Ember days (*Quattuor tempora*). During his reign, the title "Pope" shifted from a generic term for any bishop to designate exclusively the Bishop of Rome.

### Papal Authority: Theory and Practice
Leo grounded papal primacy in Petrine succession and Roman law. He styled himself the unworthy *heres* (heir) and *vicarius* (deputy) of Peter, holding apostolic authority by inheritance and obligated to Peter’s example. Christ remained the ultimate source of grace, but Peter’s unique relationship with Christ mandated a single Petrine seat toward which the universal Church must converge.

He operationalized this theory across the Empire:
*   **Gaul:** He deposed Hilary of Arles, who claimed a subordinate primacy, securing a decree from Valentinian III (6 June 445) recognizing Roman primacy based on Peter’s merits, Rome’s dignity, and the Council of Nicaea. The decree authorized provincial governors to forcibly extradite bishops refusing a papal summons.
*   **Alexandria:** He insisted Patriarch Dioscorus conform to Roman practice, arguing Mark the Evangelist (Peter’s disciple) could have taught no other tradition.
*   **North Africa:** He regulated discipline in isolated Mauretania Caesariensis, forbidding lay appointments to the episcopate.
*   **Italy & Illyria:** He rebuked Sicilian bishops for deviating from Roman baptismal custom and reprimanded the vicar of Thessalonica, Anastasius, for overstepping delegated authority, insisting reserved powers belonged to Rome alone.
*   **East:** He influenced the selection of Timothy Salophakiolos as Patriarch of Alexandria over the Miaphysite Timotheus Aelurus.

In 450, Emperor Theodosius II became the first to address the Bishop of Rome as "Patriarch of the West," a title retained (with a brief 2006–2024 interruption) to the present. By 447, Leo argued heretics deserved death, lest human and divine law collapse.

### The *Tome* and the Council of Chalcedon
At the Second Council of Ephesus (449), Leo’s legates delivered the *Tome*—a letter to Flavian of Constantinople adhering to Augustinian western Christology. The council, dominated by Dioscorus, ignored it, deposed Flavian, and was later branded the "Robber Synod." Leo repudiated its acts at a Roman synod (October 449) and demanded an ecumenical council in Italy.

At Chalcedon (451), the *Tome* was read and acclaimed: "Peter has spoken thus through Leo." The Council defined the hypostatic union: Christ is one person (*hypostasis*) in two natures (*physeis*), divine and human, united "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation." It condemned Eutyches (who denied Christ’s true humanity) and Nestorius (who divided Christ’s person). Leo accepted the dogmatic decrees but firmly rejected Canon 28, which granted Constantinople privileges equal to Rome based on the city’s civil status, insisting ecclesiastical rank derived from apostolic foundation, not political importance.

### Christology, Soteriology, and Mariology
Leo’s nearly 100 sermons and 150 letters center on Christ’s person (Christology) and saving work (Soteriology). He insisted on the *integrality* of both natures: if Christ were only divine, the crucifixion, burial, and resurrection would be divine fictions, destroying human hope for resurrection. Mary’s role is determined by Christology: she is *Theotokos* (Mother of God) because the one born is the divine Word, yet she gave him true human flesh from the line of David. For Leo, Christ’s presence extends into the Church—Scripture, Tradition, liturgy, the believer’s life, and the council—making the Church the ongoing locus of the Incarnation.

### Oriental Orthodox Schism
Leo’s "two natures after the union" formula was rejected by those loyal to Cyril of Alexandria’s *mia physis* ("one nature of the Word incarnate"). After Dioscorus’s death, the See of Alexandria split. The anti-Chalcedonian faction, led by Timothy II, condemned the *Tome* and Chalcedon at the Third Council of Ephesus, forming the nucleus of the Oriental Orthodox Churches (Miaphysites). They confess Christ as 100% human and 100% divine in one nature without mingling or confusion, reciting daily: "Christ's divinity parted not from His humanity, not for a single moment nor a twinkling of an eye." Modern Coptic scholars (e.g., El-Moharraky, Girgis) argue the schism was exacerbated by political and ecclesiastical rivalry over patriarchal precedence, not merely theology.

### Leo and Attila (452)
Following the Battle of the Catalaunian Plains (451), Attila invaded Italy, sacking Aquileia. Valentinian III sent an embassy—Gennadius Avienus, Memmius Aemilius Trygetius, and Leo—to Attila’s camp near Lake Garda. Attila withdrew. Ancient sources (Prosper of Aquitaine) credit Leo’s personal authority; Priscus records Attila’s men feared the fate of Alaric (d. 410); Paul the Deacon adds a miraculous vision. Modern historians (e.g., J.B. Bury) emphasize material factors: plague in the Hun camp, food shortages, and the arrival of Eastern Roman troops sent by Marcian. The embassy’s success was likely multifactorial; Leo’s moral authority was a component, but not the sole cause.

### Vandal Sack of Rome (455)
Leo could not prevent Genseric’s sack, but he and his clergy met the Vandals, securing a reprieve from murder and arson. The basilicas of St. Peter, St. Paul, and St. John were spared as refuges. Leo subsequently aided Rome’s reconstruction, restoring St. Peter’s.

### Death and Legacy
Leo died 10 November 461, requesting burial near St. Peter’s tomb in the portico of Old St. Peter’s—the first papal burial there. In 688, Sergius I translated his relics to the south transept. Benedict XIV declared him a Doctor of the Church (1754); Benedict XVI called his papacy "undoubtedly one of the most important in the Church's history." Thirteen subsequent popes have taken his name (latest: Leo XIV). Feast days: 10 November (West, Anglican), 18 February (East), 28 June (translation of relics).

## Terms
- ****Hypostatic Union**** — The doctrine, defined at Chalcedon (451), that Jesus Christ is one person (*hypostasis*) subsisting in two complete natures, divine and human, united "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation."
- ****Tome of Leo**** — Leo’s 449 letter to Flavian of Constantinople articulating western Christology (two natures in one person); rejected at Ephesus II, accepted as orthodox at Chalcedon.
- ****Miaphysitism**** — The Christological position (held by Oriental Orthodox) that Christ has one (*mia*) composite nature, fully divine and fully human, without confusion; rejects Chalcedon’s "two natures" formula as Nestorian.
- ****Dyophysitism**** — The Chalcedonian position: Christ exists in two distinct natures (*dyo physeis*) after the union.
- ****Monophysitism**** — The heresy (associated with Eutyches) that Christ’s human nature was absorbed into the divine, leaving only one divine nature; condemned at Chalcedon.
- ****Petrine Primacy**** — The doctrine that the Bishop of Rome holds supreme authority over the Church by succession from Peter the Apostle, whom Christ made the "rock" and head of the apostles.
- ****Vicarius Petri**** — "Vicar of Peter"; Leo’s legal term for the pope as Peter’s deputy and heir, exercising Peter’s authority by Roman law of inheritance (*heres*).
- ****Robber Synod** (*Latrocinium*)** — Leo’s term for the Second Council of Ephesus (449), where Dioscorus manipulated proceedings to depose Flavian and ignore Leo’s *Tome*; repudiated at Chalcedon.
- ****Patriarch of the West**** — Title first used by Theodosius II (450) for the Bishop of Rome, signifying his jurisdiction over the Western Church; used by popes until 2006, restored 2024.
- ****Quattuor tempora**** — The four sets of Ember days (quarterly fasts); Leo linked these fasts to charity and almsgiving as pastoral practice.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Attila’s withdrawal (452):** Sources conflict. Prosper credits Leo’s spiritual authority; Priscus cites fear of Alaric’s fate; Bury and modern historians prioritize plague, famine, and Marcian’s troop movements. The relative weight of Leo’s diplomacy vs. material constraints remains unresolved.
*   **Nature of papal authority:** Leo claimed universal jurisdiction by divine right (Petrine succession) and Roman law (*heres/vicarius*). The extent to which this was accepted *de facto* by other patriarchs (especially Constantinople) versus imposed by Western imperial decree is debated.
*   **Chalcedon’s reception:** The "two natures" formula split the Church permanently. Scholars debate whether the schism was primarily theological (Cyril’s *mia physis* vs. Leo’s *duo physeis*) or political (Alexandria vs. Rome/Constantinople rivalry), with Coptic historians emphasizing the latter.
*   **Coercion of heretics:** Leo’s endorsement of the death penalty for heretics (447) and use of torture against Manichaeans raise questions about the continuity between his theology of unity and his praxis of force.
*   **Authenticity of the *Tome* at Ephesus II:** The council’s acts claim the *Tome* was not read; Leo’s legates protested. Historians debate whether this was procedural suppression or a genuine oversight, affecting the legitimacy of the "Robber Synod" label.
