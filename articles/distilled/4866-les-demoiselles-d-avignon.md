# Les Demoiselles d'Avignon

## Overview
*Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* (1907) is a seminal oil painting by Pablo Picasso, depicting five nude female prostitutes in a brothel. It is a foundational work of modern art, marking a radical break from traditional European painting and directly paving the way for Cubism. Its confrontational style and use of non-Western artistic influences made it revolutionary and deeply controversial upon its creation.

## Timeline
- **c. 1906** — Picasso begins preparatory sketches for the painting, influenced by Iberian sculpture and African art.
- **March 1907** — Picasso completes the painting in his studio in the Bateau-Lavoir, Montmartre, Paris.
- **July 1916** — First public exhibition at the Salon d'Antin in Paris, organized by André Salmon.
- **1916** — Salmon renames the painting from Picasso's preferred *Le Bordel d'Avignon* to *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon*.
- **1924** — Picasso sells the painting to designer Jacques Doucet for 25,000 francs.
- **1937** — The painting is exhibited in New York at the Jacques Seligman & Co. gallery.
- **1939** — The Museum of Modern Art (MoMA) in New York acquires the painting for $24,000. |

## Body

### Creation and Rivalry
Picasso created *Les Demoiselles* during a period of intense artistic development, having moved through his Blue and Rose Periods. The painting was conceived during a direct rivalry with Henri Matisse, who was then the perceived leader of the avant-garde. Picasso sought to surpass Matisse's recent works, such as *Le bonheur de vivre*, by turning to "primitive" art for inspiration. The shock and impact of *Les Demoiselles* propelled Picasso to the center of the modern art world, effectively ending the Fauvist movement led by Matisse.

### Influences and Style
The painting synthesizes multiple artistic influences. The figures on the left show the influence of ancient Iberian sculpture, while the two figures on the right, with their mask-like faces, are directly inspired by African tribal art. Picasso also drew from the work of Paul Cézanne, particularly his geometric simplification of form, and the dramatic compositions of El Greco. The painting rejects traditional Renaissance perspective, using a compressed, two-dimensional picture plane. This violent overturning of established conventions is considered the logical starting point for Cubism, the revolutionary style Picasso and Georges Braque would develop.

### Reception and Legacy
Initially seen only by Picasso's close circle, the painting was met with shock and disapproval. Friends like Matisse and Braque were initially troubled, though Braque later studied it intently, leading to his collaboration with Picasso. It was first publicly exhibited in 1916, where it was deemed immoral. Its true revolutionary impact was recognized in the 1920s and 1930s. Art historian John Richardson called it "the first unequivocally 20th-century masterpiece" and "the cornerstone of 20th-century art." It is now in the permanent collection of MoMA and is frequently cited as the most influential work of art of the last century.

## Terms
- **Primitivism**: A style in Western art that draws inspiration from the art and cultures of Africa, Oceania, and the Americas.
- **Cubism**: An avant-garde art movement, pioneered by Picasso and Braque, that uses geometric shapes and multiple perspectives to depict subjects.
- **Fauvism**: An early 20th-century art movement characterized by strong, non-naturalistic color, led by Henri Matisse.
- **Iberian sculpture**: Art from pre-Roman Spain, which influenced Picasso's use of simplified, mask-like forms.
- **Verisimilitude**: The appearance of being true or real; in art, the faithful representation of visual reality.
- **Salon d'Antin**: The Parisian exhibition space where *Les Demoiselles* was first shown publicly in 1916.
- **Bateau-Lavoir**: The name of Picasso's studio building in Montmartre, Paris, where he painted *Les Demoiselles*.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The role of African art**: Picasso denied the influence of African masks during the painting's creation, but later accounts and evidence (like photographs of his studio) contradict this. Scholars debate the exact nature and timing of this influence.
- **Interpretation of the figures**: The meaning of the five women is widely debated. Some see them as a simple brothel scene, while others, like Leo Steinberg, argue the painting is a complex meditation on the "trauma of the gaze," where the viewer is confronted by the prostitutes. Suzanne Preston Blier interprets them as global figures representing different races and eras in an evolutionary framework.
- **Relationship to Cubism**: While often called the first Cubist painting, art historian Douglas Cooper argues it is not yet Cubist in its expressionist violence, but is the essential starting point from which Cubism grew.
