# Taxonomy (biology)

## Overview
Taxonomy is the scientific discipline of naming, defining, and classifying biological organisms based on shared characteristics, with modern approaches emphasizing evolutionary relationships and common ancestry. It produces a hierarchical system of taxa (singular: taxon) organized into ranked categories such as domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species. Rooted in Carl Linnaeus’s 18th-century binomial nomenclature, taxonomy has evolved from descriptive morphology to a synthesis of molecular genetics, cladistics, and phylogenetics, serving as the foundation for biodiversity research and conservation biology.

## Timeline
- **c. 1500 BC** — Egyptian wall paintings show early plant taxonomy
- **384–322 BC** — Aristotle classifies organisms by anatomical attributes
- **1583** — Andrea Cesalpino publishes *De Plantis*, first major post-Linnaean taxonomy
- **1735** — Linnaeus publishes *Systema Naturae*, introducing binomial nomenclature
- **1753** — Linnaeus publishes *Species Plantarum*, starting point for valid plant names
- **1758** — Linnaeus publishes *Systema Naturae* 10th ed., starting point for animal names
- **1859** — Darwin’s *On the Origin of Species* shifts taxonomy toward evolutionary relationships
- **1970s** — Cladistic methodology becomes dominant, emphasizing monophyly
- **1977** — Carl Woese proposes three-domain system (Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya)

## Body

### Definition and Scope
Taxonomy involves conceiving, naming, and classifying groups of organisms. It is often considered a sub-discipline of systematics, which also includes phylogenetics and nomenclature. Disagreements persist over whether biological nomenclature belongs to taxonomy or systematics. In 1970, Michener et al. defined systematics as encompassing naming, description, preservation, classification, identification, and evolutionary history, with taxonomy covering only the first four functions. The term "taxonomy" was coined in 1813 by de Candolle.

### Taxonomic Revision and Characters
A taxonomic revision analyzes variation patterns within a taxon using morphological, anatomical, palynological, biochemical, or genetic data. Monographs are comprehensive revisions covering entire taxa globally. Taxonomic characters are the observable traits used to infer phylogenetic relationships. These may include structural features, biochemical markers, or DNA sequences.

### Alpha and Beta Taxonomy
Alpha taxonomy refers to the identification, description, and naming of taxa, especially species. Originally meaning morphological taxonomy, it now includes modern techniques. Beta taxonomy involves classifying species into higher-order groups and arranging them hierarchically. William Bertram Turrill introduced "alpha taxonomy" in 1935, distinguishing it from broader taxonomic goals involving ecology, genetics, and phylogeny.

### Microtaxonomy and Macrotaxonomy
Microtaxonomy addresses the species problem—how to define species—while macrotaxonomy studies higher-ranked groups (subgenus and above) using phylogenetic nomenclature. Both reflect ongoing debates about the nature of species and higher taxa.

### Historical Development
Pre-Linnaean taxonomy was descriptive and utilitarian, focusing on medicinal or agricultural plants. Aristotle classified organisms by body plans, dividing life into plants and animals. Medieval taxonomy followed Aristotelian logic, incorporating the great chain of being. The Renaissance saw advances via microscopy, with Cesalpino and Ray producing detailed classifications. Linnaeus formalized binomial nomenclature and hierarchical ranks. Post-Darwin, taxonomy embraced evolutionary relationships, leading to phyletic systems and eventually cladistics.

### Modern Classification
Modern taxonomy integrates Linnaean ranks with evolutionary principles. Cladistics, emerging in the 1960s, requires taxa to be monophyletic (including all descendants of a common ancestor). Synapomorphies (shared derived traits) diagnose clades. Paraphyletic and polyphyletic groups are discouraged. The PhyloCode offers an alternative rank-free nomenclature, though Linnaean ranks remain widely used. Molecular data, especially from Woese’s rRNA studies, reshaped higher-level classification with the three-domain system.

### Applications and Challenges
Taxonomy underpins biodiversity studies and conservation. Taxonomic descriptions require formal publication, unique Latin names, type specimens, and diagnostic features. Author citations credit original describers. Phenetics, based on overall similarity, has been largely replaced by cladistics. Databases like the Catalogue of Life and Encyclopedia of Life digitize taxonomic knowledge. Enigmatic taxa with uncertain affinities may be labeled *incertae sedis*.

## Terms
- **Taxon**: A named group of organisms at any rank in a classification system.
- **Monophyletic**: A group including an ancestor and all its descendants.
- **Paraphyletic**: A group excluding some descendants of the common ancestor.
- **Polyphyletic**: A group not including the most recent common ancestor.
- **Synapomorphy**: A shared derived character state indicating common ancestry.
- **Binomial nomenclature**: A two-part naming system (genus + species) introduced by Linnaeus.
- **Alpha taxonomy**: The practice of identifying, describing, and naming species.
- **Beta taxonomy**: The classification of species into higher taxonomic groups.
- **Clade**: A group of organisms believed to comprise all evolutionary descendants of a common ancestor.
- **Incertae sedis**: A term indicating uncertain taxonomic placement.

## Debates and Open Questions
Debates persist over the relationship between taxonomy and systematics, particularly regarding the role of nomenclature. The integration of molecular and morphological data sometimes yields conflicting phylogenies. The three-domain system proposed by Woese remains influential but contested, with alternatives like Cavalier-Smith’s Neomura hypothesis challenging its assumptions. The PhyloCode’s adoption versus traditional Linnaean ranks is unresolved. Additionally, the species concept remains contentious, with microtaxonomy grappling with definitions across diverse organisms. The scalability and standardization of taxonomic databases also pose ongoing challenges.
