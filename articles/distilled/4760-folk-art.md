# Folk art

## Overview
Folk art encompasses visual art made within a folk culture, typically objects with practical utility crafted by artisans trained in a popular tradition rather than fine art. It reflects a community's cultural life and heritage, with forms passed down through generations, and is distinct from naive art or ethnographic art from traditional societies. The concept emerged in the 19th century, often carrying nostalgia for pre-industrial society, and continues to be hand-crafted today.

## Timeline
- **1894** — Alois Riegl publishes *Volkskunst, Hausfleiss, und Hausindustrie*, emphasizing individual creativity within folk traditions.
- **1951** — Barbara Jones organizes the *Black Eyes and Lemonade* exhibition in London, popularizing folk and mass-produced objects alongside contemporary art.
- **1962** — George Kubler publishes *The Shape of Time*, describing historical change through the continuum of objects and images.
- **1966** — The U.S. National Endowment for the Arts (NEA) begins funding, prioritizing support for folk festivals.
- **1967** — The NEA's first grant is made to the National Folk Festival Association.

## Body

### Characteristics of Folk Art Objects
Folk art objects are a subset of material culture—tangible items experienced through the senses. They are valued for technical execution of existing forms, where skill is evident in precision, decoration, or beauty. These objects are typically handcrafted in one-off production, making each piece unique. The design and production are learned through informal or formal apprenticeships within a cultural framework, not through self-teaching. The community recognizes the object as a known type, often with utilitarian origins, and individual pieces reference other works in the culture while showing exceptional execution. The aesthetic reflects the community's cultural biases, balancing collective tradition with individual innovation.

### Folk Artists and Training
Folk artists work within established cultural norms but contribute individual creativity, preventing traditions from stagnating. Training historically occurred through apprenticeships with local craftsmen, such as blacksmiths or stonemasons. As tools became less accessible, training shifted to technical or applied arts schools. Informal teaching outside institutions has opened the genre to artists facing barriers to formal education, like Canadian folk artist Maud Lewis, who became famous despite a congenital illness.

### Materials, Forms, and Crafts
Folk art uses locally available materials and reproduces familiar shapes and forms. It includes a wide range of objects, from everyday items to decorative pieces, crafted by artisans using traditional methods. Examples include quilting, ornamental picture framing, decoy carving, basket weaving, and santos carving.

### Influence on Mainstream Art
Folk art has inspired mainstream artists. Pablo Picasso was influenced by African tribal sculptures and masks, while Natalia Goncharova drew from Russian popular prints called luboks. The 1951 *Black Eyes and Lemonade* exhibition in London showcased folk and mass-produced objects alongside contemporary art, contributing to the rise of pop art in Britain.

### Supporting Organizations and Recognition
Organizations like UNESCO and the International Organization of Folk Art (IOV) support folk art through festivals and cultural events to promote international understanding. In the U.S., the National Endowment for the Arts (NEA) identifies and supports folk art fellows in crafts like quilting, ironwork, and pottery, based on authenticity, excellence, and significance within a tradition. Folklife festivals worldwide encourage education and community engagement.

## Terms
- **Folk art**: Visual art made in the context of folk culture, typically with practical utility and crafted within a popular tradition.
- **Material culture**: Tangible objects that can be handled and re-experienced, forming a subset of cultural artifacts.
- **Apprenticeship**: A system of training where skills are learned from a master craftsman through practice.
- **Utilitarian**: Designed for practical use in daily life, not purely decorative.
- **Naive art**: Art created by self-taught artists, often with a simplistic style, overlapping with but distinct from folk art.
- **Volkskunst**: German term for folk art, emphasizing its roots in community traditions.
- **Lubok**: A traditional Russian popular print, often used as inspiration in mainstream art.
- **Folklife**: The cultural life of a community, including both tangible and intangible traditions.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Definitions of folk art vary, with contested overlap with "naive art" and debates about its use in non-European contexts.
- The term originated in Europe and developed differently in the United States, leading to divergent cultural interpretations.
- The balance between individual creativity and community norms in folk art production is a subject of scholarly discussion, as noted by Alois Riegl.
