# Nigeria

## Overview  
Nigeria, officially the Federal Republic of Nigeria, is a West African country located between the Sahel and the Gulf of Guinea. With a population exceeding 242 million, it is the most populous nation in Africa and the sixth most populous globally. Comprising 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory, Nigeria is a federal republic with a diverse population of over 250 ethnic groups, predominantly the Hausa, Yoruba, and Igbo. Its history spans millennia, from ancient civilizations like the Nok culture to British colonial rule and post-independence challenges including civil war and military dictatorships. Today, Nigeria is a regional power and one of Africa’s largest economies, driven largely by oil production and a rapidly growing tech sector.

## Timeline  
- **c. 1500 BC – 200 AD**: Nok civilization flourishes; earliest iron smelting in Africa.  
- **10th century**: Kingdom of Nri consolidates among the Igbo; Kano Chronicle records Hausa city-states.  
- **15th–19th centuries**: Benin, Oyo, and Sokoto Caliphate rise as major regional powers.  
- **1804–1808**: Fulani Jihads led by Usman dan Fodio establish the Sokoto Caliphate.  
- **1885–1914**: British colonization; Southern and Northern Nigeria Protectorates merged in 1914.  
- **1960**: Independence as the Federation of Nigeria under Prime Minister Abubakar Tafawa Balewa.  
- **1967–1970**: Nigerian Civil War (Biafran War) results in 1–3 million deaths.  
- **1966–1999**: Series of military coups and dictatorships, including the Abacha regime (1993–1998).  
- **1999**: Return to civilian rule with the Fourth Republic; Olusegun Obasanjo becomes president.  
- **2015**: Muhammadu Buhari elected, ending PDP rule; first peaceful transfer of power.  
- **2023**: Bola Tinubu wins disputed election amid ongoing security and economic challenges.

## Body  

### Pre-Colonial Foundations  
Nigeria’s earliest known civilization was the Nok culture (c. 1500 BC – 200 AD), renowned for its terracotta sculptures and early ironworking. By the 10th century, powerful kingdoms emerged: the Hausa city-states in the north, the Kingdom of Nri among the Igbo, and the Yoruba city-state of Ile Ife, which became the spiritual center of the Ife Empire. The Kingdom of Benin, founded by the Edo people, thrived from the 15th to the 19th centuries as a major trade hub. These societies developed complex political systems, artistic traditions, and extensive trade networks long before European contact.

### Colonial Conquest and Consolidation  
British interest in Nigeria intensified in the 19th century, driven by the suppression of the transatlantic slave trade and the desire for raw materials. The Royal Niger Company, chartered in 1885, facilitated British control over the interior. Key military campaigns included the conquest of Benin in 1897 and the Anglo-Aro War (1901–1902). In 1900, the British government assumed direct control, establishing the Southern and Northern Nigeria Protectorates. The fusion of these territories in 1914 created the modern state of Nigeria. British colonial policy relied heavily on indirect rule, particularly in the north, where traditional leaders were co-opted into the administrative structure.

### Independence and Early Republic  
Nigeria gained independence on 1 October 1960 as a federation within the Commonwealth, with Nnamdi Azikiwe as ceremonial president and Abubakar Tafawa Balewa as prime minister. However, deep ethnic and regional divisions soon destabilized the new nation. The discovery of oil in Oloibiri in 1956 shifted economic focus to the Niger Delta, exacerbating inequalities between regions. Political tensions culminated in a series of coups in 1966, followed by a counter-coup that heightened north-south divisions. In 1967, the Eastern Region declared independence as the Republic of Biafra, triggering a brutal civil war that lasted until 1970.

### Military Rule and Economic Transformation  
After the civil war, Nigeria experienced alternating periods of military rule and brief democratic interludes. The 1970s oil boom brought unprecedented revenue, but mismanagement and corruption undermined development. General Murtala Muhammed’s regime (1975–1976) initiated administrative reforms, while General Olusegun Obasanjo oversaw a transition to civilian rule in 1979. The Third Republic was short-lived; General Muhammadu Buhari seized power in 1983, followed by General Ibrahim Babangida, whose regime was marked by authoritarianism and economic liberalization. The annulment of the 1993 presidential election sparked widespread protests, leading to the brutal dictatorship of General Sani Abacha (1993–1998). His regime was characterized by human rights abuses and rampant corruption until his death in 1998.

### Democratic Transition and Contemporary Challenges  
The Fourth Republic began in 1999 with the election of Olusegun Obasanjo, who implemented significant democratic reforms despite criticisms of electoral malpractice. Subsequent administrations under Umaru Yar’Adua, Goodluck Jonathan, and Muhammadu Buhari faced persistent challenges, including corruption, insurgency, and economic instability. The Boko Haram insurgency, which began in 2009, has claimed tens of thousands of lives and displaced millions. Meanwhile, Nigeria’s economy has struggled with diversification, relying heavily on oil exports despite efforts to develop agriculture and technology sectors. The 2023 election of Bola Tinubu marked a continuation of democratic governance, albeit amid concerns over electoral integrity and security.

### Geography and Environment  
Nigeria spans 923,769 square kilometers, bordered by Niger, Chad, Cameroon, and Benin. Its landscape includes the fertile Niger and Benue river valleys, the Jos Plateau, and the vast Niger Delta. The country’s climate ranges from tropical rainforest in the south to arid savanna in the north. Environmental degradation, particularly deforestation and oil pollution in the Niger Delta, poses significant challenges. Climate change and desertification threaten agricultural productivity, especially in the Sahel region.

### Society and Culture  
Nigeria’s population is religiously divided, with approximately 50% Muslim and 45% Christian, alongside adherents of traditional African religions. The country’s cultural output is globally influential, with Nollywood ranking as the world’s second-largest film industry. Nigerian music, from Fela Kuti’s Afrobeat to contemporary Afrobeats artists like Burna Boy and Wizkid, has gained international acclaim. Literature, too, has produced Nobel laureates such as Wole Soyinka and celebrated authors like Chinua Achebe. Despite these achievements, Nigeria grapples with systemic issues such as poverty, inequality, and human rights violations, particularly affecting women and marginalized communities.

## Terms  
- **Federal Republic**: A state in which power is divided between a central government and constituent political units (states).  
- **Indirect Rule**: A colonial administrative system where local leaders are used to govern on behalf of the colonial power.  
- **Sokoto Caliphate**: A 19th-century Islamic state established by the Fulani jihads under Usman dan Fodio.  
- **Nollywood**: The Nigerian film industry, known for its prolific output and global reach.  
- **Boko Haram**: An Islamist extremist group active since 2009, responsible for widespread violence in northeastern Nigeria.  
- **Fourth Republic**: The current democratic era in Nigeria, beginning in 1999 after decades of military rule.  
- **Sharia Law**: Islamic legal system applied in some northern Nigerian states, governing personal and criminal matters for Muslims.  
- **OPEC**: Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, of which Nigeria is a member, influencing global oil prices.  
- **Griot**: A West African storyteller, historian, and oral tradition keeper, integral to many Nigerian cultures.  
- **Pidgin English**: A creole language widely spoken in Nigeria, serving as a lingua franca among diverse ethnic groups.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Several contentious issues persist in Nigeria. The legitimacy of the 2023 presidential election remains disputed, with opposition candidates challenging the results in court. The effectiveness of Nigeria’s anti-corruption efforts, particularly under the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC), is debated amid allegations of political bias. The future of the Niger Delta remains uncertain, as oil extraction continues to fuel environmental destruction and conflict despite government initiatives. Additionally, the role of traditional rulers in modern governance and the integration of Sharia law in northern states raise questions about secularism and human rights. Finally, Nigeria’s ability to diversify its economy away from oil dependence and address its growing population’s needs will determine its trajectory in the 21st century.
