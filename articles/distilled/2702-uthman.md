# Uthman

## Overview
Uthman ibn Affan (c. 573/576–656 CE) was the third Caliph of the Rashidun Caliphate, ruling from 644 until his assassination. A wealthy merchant, early convert to Islam, and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad, his reign was marked by significant territorial expansion and the standardization of the Quran. However, his perceived nepotism and administrative policies led to widespread discontent and a violent revolt, culminating in his death and a major crisis in the early Muslim community.

## Timeline
- **c. 573/576** — Born in Hejaz to the wealthy Banu Umayya clan.
- **611** — Converts to Islam in Mecca, becoming one of the earliest followers.
- **615** — Migrates to Abyssinia (Ethiopia) with his wife Ruqayya to escape persecution.
- **622** — Participates in the migration (Hijra) to Medina.
- **628** — Acts as Muhammad's envoy during the Treaty of Hudaybiyya.
- **644** — Elected Caliph after the death of Umar.
- **c. 650** — Orders the compilation of the standardized Uthmanic codex of the Quran.
- **655** — Muslim navy defeats the Byzantine fleet at the Battle of the Masts.
- **656** — Assassinated in Medina by rebels after a siege of his house.

## Body

### Early Life and Companionship of Muhammad
Uthman was born into the affluent Banu Umayya clan of the Quraysh tribe. He inherited significant wealth, which he invested in trade, becoming one of the wealthiest men in Mecca. He converted to Islam in 611, shortly after Abu Bakr. To escape persecution, he and his wife Ruqayya (daughter of Muhammad) migrated to Abyssinia in 615, returning to Mecca four years later. In 622, they migrated to Medina. Uthman later married two more of Muhammad's daughters, earning him the title "Dhū al-Nurayn" (Possessor of Two Lights). He was known for his exceptional generosity, financing key military campaigns like the Expedition of Tabuk.

### Election and Caliphate
Following Caliph Umar's death in 644, a committee of six companions was appointed to choose a successor. After negotiations, Uthman was elected, partly because he agreed to follow the precedent of the first two caliphs. His reign saw the Caliphate's expansion into Persia, North Africa, and the Iberian Peninsula. He implemented economic reforms, increasing public allowances and lifting restrictions on Arab land ownership, which fostered growth. His most enduring act was ordering the compilation of a single, authoritative text of the Quran (the Uthmanic codex) around 650 to preserve its unity as Islam spread.

### Revolt and Assassination
Uthman's final years were marred by growing opposition, fueled by accusations of nepotism in appointing governors (many from his Umayyad clan) and arbitrary rule. Rebels from Egypt, Kufa, and Basra gathered in Medina, besieging his house in 656. Uthman refused to allow his supporters to fight, seeking to avoid civil war. On 17 June 656, rebels scaled the walls and assassinated him while he was reciting the Quran. His burial was hurried and conducted with little ceremony due to the siege.

## Terms
- **Rashidun Caliphate:** The first four caliphs of Islam (632–661 CE), considered by Sunni Muslims to be "rightly guided."
- **Caliph:** The political and religious successor to the Prophet Muhammad, leading the Muslim community.
- **Uthmanic codex:** The standardized version of the Quran compiled under Caliph Uthman's orders, which became the universal text.
- **Muhajirun:** The early Muslims who migrated from Mecca to Medina with Muhammad.
- **Ansar:** The Muslim residents of Medina who supported the migrating Muhajirun.
- **Dhū al-Nurayn:** An honorific title for Uthman, meaning "Possessor of Two Lights," for marrying two of Muhammad's daughters.
- **Hijra:** The migration of Muhammad and his followers from Mecca to Medina in 622 CE, marking the start of the Islamic calendar.
- **Bay'ah al-Ridwan:** The "Pledge of the Tree," a pledge of allegiance given to Muhammad by his companions at Hudaybiyya.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Causes of the Revolt:** The reasons for the anti-Uthman movement are disputed. Sunni tradition often attributes it to the personal ambition of rebels and external agitators, while Shia and some modern historians emphasize Uthman's nepotism, centralized policies, and deviations from the precedents of his predecessors.
- **Legacy and Assessment:** Uthman is revered in Sunni tradition as the third Rightly Guided Caliph and a martyr. In Shia tradition, he is often viewed less favorably for consolidating Umayyad power and altering the governance established by Muhammad and the first caliphs.
- **Compilation of the Quran:** While Uthman's role in standardizing the Quran is central to Sunni belief, Shia sources hold that the Quran was compiled during Muhammad's lifetime, and Uthman's work was merely a preservation effort.
