# Charles K. Kao

## Overview
Sir Charles K. Kao (1933–2018) was a Hong Kong-born electrical engineer whose pioneering work in the 1960s on the transmission of light through glass fibres laid the essential groundwork for modern fibre-optic telecommunications. His research demonstrated that the high signal loss in existing fibres was due to impurities, not a fundamental flaw, and proposed a threshold for practical communication, igniting a global race to develop low-loss fibres. This breakthrough enabled the high-speed data transmission that underpins the Internet and the World Wide Web.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Kao was born in Shanghai in 1933 and moved to Hong Kong in 1949. He graduated from St. Joseph's College in 1952. Although he qualified for the University of Hong Kong, it did not offer electrical engineering, so he went to London. He earned a B.Sc. from Woolwich Polytechnic in 1957 and a Ph.D. from University College London in 1965, with doctoral research conducted while working at Standard Telecommunication Laboratories (STL).

### Foundational Work in Fibre Optics
In the 1960s at STL in Harlow, England, Kao and his team investigated why existing optical fibres had extremely high signal loss (over 1,000 dB/km). In a seminal 1966 paper with George Hockham, they proposed that this loss was caused by impurities in the glass, not by fundamental physical effects, and that the intrinsic limit for glass attenuation could be below 20 dB/km. This conclusion, initially widely disbelieved, launched a worldwide effort to produce ultra-pure glass. Kao identified fused silica (SiO₂) as an ideal candidate. By 1969, he and M. W. Jones measured the intrinsic loss of bulk-fused silica at 4 dB/km, proving the viability of ultra-transparent glass. He also advocated for single-mode fibres for long-distance communication and envisioned the future of submarine fibre-optic cables.

### Later Career and Recognition
Kao joined the Chinese University of Hong Kong in 1970 to establish its electronics department. He later held senior roles at ITT Corporation in the United States and was an adjunct professor at Yale University. From 1987 to 1996, he served as Vice-Chancellor of CUHK. In 2009, he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics "for groundbreaking achievements concerning the transmission of light in fibres for optical communication." He was knighted in 2010. Kao suffered from Alzheimer's disease from 2004 and died in Hong Kong in 2018.

## Terms
- **Fibre optics:** The technology of transmitting information as pulses of light through thin strands of glass or plastic.
- **Attenuation:** The reduction in strength of a signal over a distance, measured in decibels per kilometer (dB/km).
- **Decibel per kilometer (dB/km):** A unit measuring the loss of signal power over a one-kilometer length of fibre.
- **Fused silica (SiO₂):** A high-purity form of glass identified by Kao as ideal for low-loss optical fibres.
- **Single-mode fibre:** An optical fibre designed to carry only a single ray of light (mode), enabling higher bandwidth and longer transmission distances compared to multi-mode fibre.
- **Multi-mode fibre:** An optical fibre that allows multiple light paths (modes), typically used for shorter distances.
- **Intrinsic loss:** The fundamental, minimum light loss inherent to a pure material, as opposed to loss caused by impurities.
- **Standard Telecommunication Laboratories (STL):** The research centre of Standard Telephones and Cables (STC) in Harlow, England, where Kao conducted his pioneering work.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that Kao's initial proposal that glass fibres could replace copper wires for long-distance communication was "widely disbelieved" at the time. The primary scholarly debate was over the cause of high light loss in fibres—whether it was a fundamental physical limit or due to removable impurities. Kao's work conclusively argued for the latter, shifting the research focus to material purity.
