# Electric guitar

## Overview
An electric guitar is a guitar that requires external amplification to be heard at performance volumes. It uses electromagnetic pickups to convert string vibrations into electrical signals, which are reproduced as sound by loudspeakers. Invented in the early 1930s, it became the most important instrument in popular music by the 1950s and 1960s, driving the development of genres like electric blues, rock and roll, and heavy metal.

## Timeline
- **1931** — George Beauchamp designs the "Frying Pan," the first commercially marketed electrically amplified string instrument.
- **1932** — Commercial production begins by the Ro-Pat-In Corporation (later Rickenbacker).
- **1935** — Rickenbacker releases the Electro-Spanish Ken Roberts, the first full-scale electric guitar.
- **1936** — Gibson markets its first production electric guitar, the ES-150.
- **1940** — Les Paul builds his "log guitar" solid-body prototype.
- **1950** — Fender introduces the Esquire/Broadcaster (later Telecaster), the first mass-produced solid-body guitar.
- **1952** — Gibson releases the Gibson Les Paul model.
- **1954** — Fender introduces the Stratocaster.
- **Late 1970s** — Floyd Rose develops "locking" nuts and bridges for vibrato systems.

## Body

### History and Development
Early experiments with amplifying string instruments began in the 1910s-1920s. The demand for louder guitars in large big band ensembles led to the first commercial models, which were hollow archtop acoustics with electromagnetic pickups. The "Frying Pan" lap steel (1932) was the first successful product. Early solid-body experiments included Les Paul's 1940 "log" and Vivi-Tone's 1934 model, but the first mass-produced solid-body was Fender's Telecaster (1950). Solid-body designs eliminated feedback issues common with amplified hollow bodies. Key models like the Gibson Les Paul (1952) and Fender Stratocaster (1954) defined the instrument's future.

### Types and Construction
Electric guitars are primarily categorized by body type. **Solid-body** guitars, like the Fender Stratocaster, have no resonant air cavity and depend entirely on amplification, avoiding feedback. **Semi-acoustic** (or semi-hollow) guitars have a hollow body, often with a solid center block to reduce feedback, and are used in jazz, blues, and rock. **Electric acoustic** guitars are standard acoustic guitars with built-in pickup systems for amplification.

Construction varies greatly. The **body** is typically hardwood (alder, mahogany, maple). The **neck** can be bolt-on, set-in (glued), or neck-through, and its **scale length** (e.g., 25.5" for Fender, 24.75" for Gibson) affects string tension and feel. **Pickups** are magnets wound with wire that convert string vibration into an electrical signal. **Single-coil** pickups are bright but prone to hum; **humbucker** (double-coil) pickups cancel hum and produce a richer tone. **Bridge** systems include fixed, floating, and vibrato (whammy bar) types, which affect tuning stability and pitch-bending capability.

### Roles in Music
In rock and related genres, the electric guitar typically serves two roles. The **rhythm guitarist** plays chord progressions and riffs, forming part of the rhythm section. The **lead guitarist** plays instrumental melodies, fills, and solos. In smaller groups, one guitarist may switch between both roles.

## Terms
- **Pickup:** A device, usually a magnet wrapped in wire, that converts the vibration of metal strings into an electrical signal.
- **Solid-body:** A guitar made of a solid block of wood, designed to minimize acoustic resonance and feedback when amplified.
- **Semi-acoustic:** A guitar with a hollow body and electromagnetic pickups, offering a blend of acoustic and electric tones.
- **Humbucker:** A double-coil pickup designed to cancel (or "buck") electromagnetic interference (hum), producing a warmer, thicker sound.
- **Scale length:** The vibrating length of the strings from the nut to the bridge, which affects string tension, playability, and tone.
- **Vibrato bar (whammy bar):** A lever attached to the bridge that allows the player to temporarily change string tension, bending pitch up or down.
- **Bolt-on neck:** A neck attached to the guitar body with screws, allowing for easier adjustment or replacement.
- **Tremolo:** An effect that creates a pulsating change in volume; often confused with vibrato, which is a change in pitch.

## Debates and Open Questions
The degree to which the choice of body wood affects the tone of an electric guitar's amplified signal is a subject of ongoing debate. Many players and builders believe wood choice is highly significant, while others argue the difference is subtle or negligible compared to electronics and amplification. The article notes that formal investigations confirming or refuting these claims are scarce.
