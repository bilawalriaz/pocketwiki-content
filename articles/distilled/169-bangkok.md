# Bangkok

## Overview

Bangkok, known in Thai as Krung Thep Maha Nakhon and colloquially as Krung Thep, is the capital and most populous city of Thailand. It is an extreme primate city, dwarfing all other Thai urban centres in size and national economic importance, and is among the world's top tourist destinations. The city traces its roots to a 15th-century trading post, became the site of two successive capitals (Thonburi in 1767 and Rattanakosin in 1782), and was the heart of Siam's modernization in the late 19th century. Today, Bangkok is a regional force in finance, business, transport, healthcare, and pop culture, while struggling with traffic congestion, subsidence, flooding, and the effects of climate change.

## Body

### History

Bangkok began as a village on the Chao Phraya River's west bank under Ayutthaya rule, serving as a customs outpost with forts on both riverbanks. After Ayutthaya fell to the Burmese in 1767, King Taksin established his capital at the town, founding the Thonburi Kingdom. In 1782, King Phutthayotfa Chulalok (Rama I) moved the capital to Rattanakosin Island on the eastern bank, founding the Rattanakosin Kingdom; the City Pillar was erected on 21 April 1782, regarded as the city's foundation date.

Bangkok's economy grew through international trade, first with China, then with Western merchants from the early to mid-19th century. As Siam faced Western pressure, Kings Mongkut (r. 1851–68) and Chulalongkorn (r. 1868–1910) introduced steam engines, printing, rail transport, utilities, formal education, and healthcare. After absolute monarchy was abolished in 1932, Bangkok became the centre of power struggles. During World War II, the city was subjected to Allied bombing after Thailand allied with Japan, but grew rapidly post-war due to US aid and investment. US military R&R boosted tourism and established sex tourism. Following the US withdrawal from Vietnam in 1973, Japanese investment led export-oriented manufacturing growth. The 1997 Asian financial crisis stalled development. The city has seen repeated protests: student uprisings in 1973 and 1976, anti-military demonstrations in 1992, and frequent street protests since 2006, including those around Thaksin Shinawatra (2006–2013) and a student-led movement in 2020.

### Name

The origin of "Bangkok" is unclear. *Bang* means "a village on a stream"; it may derive from *Bang Ko* ("island village") or *Bang Makok*, after *Elaeocarpus hygrophilus*, a plant with olive-like fruit—supported by Wat Arun's former name, Wat Makok. Officially the town was Thonburi Si Mahasamut ("city of treasures gracing the ocean"); Bangkok was likely a colloquial name adopted by foreign visitors. When Rama I founded the new capital, it inherited Ayutthaya's ceremonial name. The full ceremonial name, composed of Pali and Sanskrit roots, translates as "The city of angels, great city of immortals, magnificent city of the Nine Gems, seat of the King, city of royal palaces, home of gods incarnate, erected by Vishvakarman at Indra's behest." Listed in Guinness World Records as the world's longest place name (168 letters), it is remembered by many Thais through the 1989 song "Krung Thep Maha Nakhon" by Asanee–Wasan. Today the city is officially Krung Thep Maha Nakhon, colloquially Krung Thep, meaning "City of Angels."

### Government

Bangkok is governed by the Bangkok Metropolitan Administration (BMA), a special administrative area at provincial level with a directly elected governor serving four-year terms. The governor and four appointed deputies form the executive; the Bangkok Metropolitan Council, elected by districts, is the legislative body. The first gubernatorial election in nine years was held on 22 May 2022 after a lapse following the 2014 coup, won by Chadchart Sittipunt. The city has fifty districts (*khet*), subdivided into 180 sub-districts (*khwaeng*). The BMA oversees infrastructure, planning, transport, drainage, waste, education, medical, and rescue services. Bangkok hosts all branches of national government, including the Government House, Parliament House, and courts; Sappaya-Sapasathan is the world's largest parliament building. The city seal shows Indra riding Airavata, a divine white elephant; the tree symbol is *Ficus benjamina*, and the giant barb is the aquatic animal.

### Geography

Bangkok covers 1,568.7 km² in the Chao Phraya River delta, with an average elevation of only 1.5 m above sea level. Originally swampland, it was drained via canals (*khlong*) from the 16th to 19th centuries, earning the nickname "Venice of the East." The geology features soft marine clay ("Bangkok clay") over an aquifer system; groundwater pumping caused subsidence reaching 120 mm/year in 1981, now reduced to 10–30 mm/year, though parts of the city are 1 m below sea level. This increases flood risk; severe floods occurred in 1995 and 2011. The clay amplifies long-period earthquake ground motion, making tall buildings vulnerable despite the city not being seismically active; seismic codes were only added in 2007. Coastal location makes Bangkok vulnerable to sea-level rise; an OECD study estimated 5.138 million people may face coastal flooding by 2070. The climate is tropical savanna (Köppen Aw) with hot, rainy, and cool seasons; the highest recorded temperature was 41.0 °C on 7 May 2023, the lowest 9.9 °C in January 1955. NASA's Climate Impact Group projects days at or above 32 °C rising from 193 in 1960 to 276 in 2018, and 297–344 by 2100.

### Economy

Bangkok is Thailand's economic centre, with 2022 output of 5.747 trillion baht (US$164 billion) and per-capita GDP more than double the national average. Wholesale/retail trade is the largest sector (24% of gross provincial product), followed by manufacturing, real estate, transport, and financial intermediation. The automotive industry around Greater Bangkok is Southeast Asia's largest production hub. The Stock Exchange of Thailand has 648 listed companies (2011) with combined market capitalization of 8.485 trillion baht. The Globalization and World Cities Research Network ranks Bangkok as an "Alpha-" world city. Seventeen Thai companies are on the Forbes 2000, all based in the capital, including PTT, Thailand's only Fortune Global 500 company. Income inequality is substantial, with a Gini coefficient of 0.48, though absolute poverty is low (0.64% vs. 7.75% nationally).

### Tourism and Culture

Bangkok is among the world's top tourist destinations; MasterCard ranked it first in 2018 with over 20 million overnight visitors, and Euromonitor ranked it first in 2024 with 32.4 million visitors. Major sights include the Grand Palace, Wat Phra Kaew, Wat Pho, Wat Arun, and the Jim Thompson House. The city has a reputation as a major sex tourism destination despite prostitution being technically illegal. Street vendors are ubiquitous—possibly over 100,000—though a 2015–16 crackdown evicted nearly 15,000 vendors from 39 public areas. Bangkok was designated UNESCO World Book Capital for 2013 and a "Design City" in 2022. It is the centre of Thailand's media industry, hosting all national newspapers and broadcasters. The city has hosted the Asian Games four times (1966, 1970, 1978, 1998), the most of any city, plus the inaugural Southeast Asian Games (1959) and 2012 FIFA Futsal World Cup.

### Transport

Bangkok's road network suffers chronic congestion; road surface area is only 8% of the city (vs. 20–30% in Western cities). The first expressway opened in 1981. Three rapid transit systems operate: BTS Skytrain (since 1999), MRT Metro, and SRT commuter lines; the Airport Rail Link opened in 2010. A Mass Rapid Transit Master Plan aims for 508 km of lines by 2029. Two airports serve the city: Suvarnabhumi (opened 2006, served 52.8 million passengers in 2015) and Don Mueang (reopened for domestic flights 2007, international low-cost carriers 2012). Water transport remains significant: the Chao Phraya Express Boat carries about 19,834 passengers daily (2023). Traffic is the main air pollution source; PM2.5 levels rose to unhealthy levels in January–February 2018. Congestion pricing was announced in 2024.

### Health, Education, and Safety

Bangkok has long been Thailand's education centre, with the country's five oldest universities (Chulalongkorn, Thammasat, Kasetsart, Mahidol, Silpakorn) founded between 1917 and 1943. Ramkhamhaeng University (1971) was Thailand's first open university. Medical resources are concentrated in the capital: in 2000, Bangkok had 39.6% of the country's doctors, 42 public hospitals, 98 private hospitals, and 4,063 clinics. An estimated 200,000 medical tourists visited in 2011, making Bangkok the most popular global destination for medical tourism. Crime rates are moderate; by the late 1980s, Bangkok's crime rates were about four times the rest of the country. Political demonstrations are common; the March–May 2010 crackdown killed 92. A 2015 bombing at the Erawan shrine killed 20. Traffic accidents caused 456 deaths in 2010. Up to 300,000 stray dogs roam the city, and rabies is prevalent.

## Terms

- **Primate city**: A city that dominates its country's urban hierarchy, being disproportionately larger and more important than all others.
- **Rattanakosin Kingdom**: The Thai kingdom founded by Rama I in 1782 with its capital at Bangkok.
- **Khlong**: A canal in Thailand; historically the primary transport route in Bangkok.
- **Khet**: A district of Bangkok, equivalent to an *amphoe* (district) in other Thai provinces.
- **Bangkok clay**: The soft marine clay layer averaging 15 m thick underlying the city, contributing to subsidence and earthquake vulnerability.
- **Subsidence**: The gradual sinking of land, caused here by extensive groundwater pumping.
- **Tuk-tuk**: A motorized three-wheeled auto rickshaw used as a taxi.
- **Soi**: A side street or lane branching off a major road in Bangkok.
- **PM2.5**: Atmospheric particulate matter under 2.5 micrometres, a key air pollution measure.
- **Gini coefficient**: A measure of income inequality, where 0 is perfect equality and 1 is maximal inequality.

## Debates and open questions

The origin of the name "Bangkok" is debated: it may derive from *Bang Ko* ("island village") or *Bang Makok* (after the olive-fruit-bearing plant *Elaeocarpus hygrophilus*). The city faces existential questions about its future: subsidence, flooding, and sea-level rise have prompted calls to move the capital—proposed unsuccessfully during World War II to Phetchabun, studied for Nakhon Nayok in the 2000s, and for Chachoengsao in 2017. There are fears the city may be submerged by 2030, though a 2019 *Nature Communications* study corrected earlier elevation models and concluded up to 12 million Thais face annual flooding. The balance between development and preserving the city's character—evident in the street vendor crackdown—remains contested.
