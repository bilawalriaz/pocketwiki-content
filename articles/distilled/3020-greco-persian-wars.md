# Greco-Persian Wars

## Overview
The Greco-Persian Wars (499–449 BC) were a series of conflicts between the Greek city-states and the Achaemenid Persian Empire. They began with the Ionian Revolt and escalated into two major Persian invasions of Greece, culminating in decisive Greek victories that preserved Greek independence and shifted the balance of power in the Aegean. The wars shaped the political landscape of classical Greece, leading to the rise of the Athenian-led Delian League and setting the stage for later internal Greek conflicts.

## Timeline
- **547 BC** — Cyrus the Great conquers the Greek-inhabited region of Ionia.
- **499 BC** — Aristagoras incites the Ionian Revolt against Persian rule.
- **498 BC** — Ionians, aided by Athens and Eretria, capture and burn Sardis.
- **494 BC** — Persian victory at the Battle of Lade crushes the Ionian Revolt.
- **490 BC** — Persian force under Datis and Artaphernes raids Eretria and lands at Marathon.
- **490 BC** — Athenians and Plataeans defeat the Persians at the Battle of Marathon.
- **480 BC** — Xerxes I leads the second Persian invasion; Greeks are defeated at Thermopylae.
- **480 BC** — Greek fleet defeats the Persian fleet at the Battle of Salamis.
- **479 BC** — Greek alliance routs the Persian army at the Battle of Plataea.
- **479 BC** — Greek fleet destroys the remnants of the Persian fleet at the Battle of Mycale.
- **478 BC** — Greeks capture Byzantium, leading to Spartan withdrawal and the formation of the Delian League.
- **466 BC** — Delian League achieves a double victory at the Battle of the Eurymedon, liberating Ionia.
- **451 BC** — Greek expedition to Cyprus marks the effective end of major hostilities.

## Body

### Origins and the Ionian Revolt
The conflict's roots lay in the Persian conquest of the Greek cities of Ionia in Asia Minor. The Persians ruled through appointed Greek tyrants, which fostered discontent. In 499 BC, the tyrant Aristagoras of Miletus, facing removal, sparked the Ionian Revolt. After initial success, including the burning of Sardis with Athenian and Eretrian aid, the revolt was decisively crushed by 493 BC. The Persian king Darius I vowed to punish Athens and Eretria and to secure the empire's western frontier by conquering mainland Greece.

### The First Persian Invasion (492–490 BC)
The first invasion consisted of two campaigns. In 492 BC, Mardonius re-subjugated Thrace and Macedon but was forced to withdraw after a naval disaster. In 490 BC, a new force under Datis and Artaphernes sacked Eretria before landing at Marathon. There, the Athenians, led by Miltiades, achieved a shocking victory, demonstrating that the Persian army could be defeated. This victory boosted Greek morale and delayed further invasion for a decade.

### The Second Persian Invasion (480–479 BC)
After Darius's death, his son Xerxes I prepared a massive invasion. In 480 BC, he crossed into Europe. The Greeks formed a defensive alliance led by Sparta and Athens. A Spartan force under King Leonidas I made a legendary stand at the pass of Thermopylae, buying time for the Greek fleet. Though Thermopylae fell, the Greek navy inflicted a critical defeat on the Persians at the naval Battle of Salamis. In 479 BC, the confederated Greek army decisively routed the Persian land army at Plataea, while the Greek fleet destroyed the Persian fleet at Mycale, effectively ending the invasion.

### The Delian League and the End of the Wars
Following the Persian withdrawal, Sparta lost interest in the campaign, and leadership passed to Athens, which formed the Delian League in 478 BC to continue the war. For three decades, the League campaigned to remove Persian garrisons from the Aegean and Asia Minor. Key victories, like the double victory at the Eurymedon in 466 BC, liberated Ionia. A disastrous Athenian-led intervention in an Egyptian revolt (c. 460 BC) halted major campaigns. A final expedition to Cyprus in 451 BC, following a truce in Greece, marked the end of open conflict. Some ancient sources mention a formal Peace of Callias, though its existence and terms are debated.

## Terms
- **Achaemenid Empire**: The First Persian Empire, founded by Cyrus the Great, which ruled from the Indus Valley to Egypt.
- **Hoplite**: A heavily armed Greek foot soldier, typically a middle-class citizen who could afford his own armor (helmet, breastplate, greaves, and a large round shield called an *aspis*).
- **Phalanx**: A military formation of infantry standing shoulder-to-shoulder in ranks, presenting a wall of spears and shields.
- **Trireme**: A warship powered by three banks of oars, the dominant naval vessel in the Mediterranean during this period.
- **Satrap**: A provincial governor in the Persian Empire.
- **Delian League**: An alliance of Greek city-states formed in 478 BC under Athenian leadership to continue the war against Persia, later evolving into the Athenian Empire.
- **Ostracism**: A procedure in Athenian democracy where citizens could vote to exile a prominent politician for ten years without loss of property.
- **Pentekontaetia**: The "Fifty Years" (479–431 BC) between the Persian Wars and the Peloponnesian War, a period of relative peace and Athenian ascendancy.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The Peace of Callias**: Ancient sources disagree on whether a formal peace treaty was signed between Athens and Persia around 449 BC. Some historians accept it, while others consider it a later invention.
- **Troop Numbers**: The figures given by Herodotus for Xerxes' army (e.g., 2.5 million) are widely rejected by modern scholars as exaggerated; estimates for the Persian force at Plataea often center around 200,000.
- **Herodotus's Reliability**: While his work is the primary source, some details, especially precise numbers and dates, are viewed with skepticism. His account of the betrayal at Thermopylae by Ephialtes is also questioned as possible folklore.
- **The Battle of Mycale**: Some modern historians doubt Herodotus's account that the battle occurred on the same day as Plataea, suggesting it may have happened later after news of the victory arrived.
