# Smallpox

## Overview
Smallpox was a highly contagious and often fatal infectious disease caused by the variola virus (genus *Orthopoxvirus*). It is the only human disease to have been globally eradicated, certified by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1980 after a decade-long intensive vaccination and surveillance campaign. Historically, smallpox killed an estimated 300–500 million people in the 20th century alone, with a case-fatality rate of approximately 30% for the severe *Variola major* form. Survivors frequently suffered permanent disfiguring scars, and blindness was a common complication. The disease spread primarily through prolonged face-to-face contact via respiratory droplets. Prevention relied on vaccination, pioneered by Edward Jenner in 1796 using the related cowpox/vaccinia virus, which conferred cross-protective immunity. Two live variola virus stocks remain in WHO-sanctioned laboratories in the US and Russia, sparking ongoing debate regarding their destruction versus retention for research.

## Body

### Classification and Clinical Forms
Smallpox presentation varied by viral strain and host immunity. The Rao classification defines four principal types of *Variola major* infection in unvaccinated individuals:
*   **Ordinary (90%+ of cases):** Discrete or confluent rash progressing synchronously: macules → papules → vesicles → pustules → scabs over 2–3 weeks. Case-fatality ~30% overall; confluent rash (lesions merging) raises fatality to ~62%.
*   **Modified:** Occurs in previously vaccinated persons (≈25% of breakthrough cases). Lesions fewer, evolve faster, more superficial, rarely fatal.
*   **Malignant (Flat, 5–10% of cases):** Lesions remain flush with skin, soft/velvety, mature slowly. Severe prodrome, prolonged high fever, extensive enanthem. Nearly always fatal (death days 8–12), mostly in children; thought due to deficient cell-mediated immunity.
*   **Hemorrhagic (≈2%):** Severe bleeding into skin/mucosa/viscera; "black pox" (*variola nigra*). **Early (fulminant):** Petechiae/bleeding before rash; death days 5–7, ~100% fatal; more common in pregnancy (16% of cases). **Late:** Bleeding in early eruptive phase; coagulation cascade failure; fatality 90–95%.

*Variola minor* (alastrim) caused milder disease (<1% mortality) but spread efficiently due to ambulant patients. Infection conferred immunity to *Variola major*. Two rare forms, *variola sine eruptione* (fever without rash, in vaccinated) and subclinical infections, were also documented.

### Cause, Virology, and Evolution
Smallpox is caused by *Variola virus* (family *Poxviridae*, subfamily *Chordopoxvirinae*, genus *Orthopoxvirus*). Virions are large, brick-shaped (300–350 nm), with a linear double-stranded DNA genome (~186 kbp) featuring inverted terminal repetitions (ITRs) forming a continuous loop. Uniquely among human DNA viruses, poxviruses replicate in the cytoplasm, encoding their own DNA-dependent RNA polymerase.

Four orthopoxviruses infect humans: variola, vaccinia, cowpox, monkeypox. Variola infects only humans naturally (no animal reservoir or asymptomatic carrier state), a critical factor enabling eradication. Two main clades exist: *Variola major* (severe, spread from Asia 400–1,600 years ago) and *Variola minor/alastrim* (mild, diverged from West African ancestral strain 1,400–6,300 years ago). Molecular clock estimates for divergence from a rodent virus (*Taterapox*) range widely (3,000–50,000+ years ago) due to calibration uncertainties. Strain diversification occurred primarily in the 18th–19th centuries.

### Transmission and Pathogenesis
Transmission required close contact, primarily via inhalation of respiratory droplets from oral/nasal/pharyngeal mucosa of an infected person. Fomite transmission (contaminated bedding/clothing) occurred but was rare. Airborne spread in enclosed settings (buildings, transport) was documented but uncommon. Seasonal peaks in winter/spring (temperate zones); year-round in tropics.

Pathogenesis: Virus invades respiratory mucosa → migrates to regional lymph nodes (initial multiplication) → primary viremia (~day 3–4) → multiplication in spleen, bone marrow, lymph nodes → secondary viremia (~day 12) → seeding of skin/mucous membranes → rash onset. Infectivity peaked during first week of rash (intact pustules); waned as scabs formed (days 7–10); contagious until last scab fell off. No transmission during prodrome.

### Diagnosis
Clinical definition: Acute fever onset followed by firm, deep-seated vesicles/pustules in same developmental stage (centrifugal distribution: face → extremities → trunk; palms/soles involved). **Differential:** Chickenpox (centripetal distribution, spares palms/soles, lesions asynchronous/varying sizes). **Laboratory confirmation:** Electron microscopy (brick-shaped virions identical across orthopoxviruses); Guarnieri bodies (cytoplasmic inclusion bodies) on skin biopsy; viral culture on chorioallantoic membrane (pock morphology); PCR/RFLP for strain characterization; serology (ELISA).

### Prevention: Vaccination
**Variolation (Inoculation):** Pre-1796 practice using *Variola minor* material (scabs/pus). Practiced in China (10th–16th c.), India, Africa, Ottoman Empire. Introduced to Europe early 18th c. (Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, Cotton Mather). Mortality 0.5–2% vs. 20–30% for natural smallpox, but risked transmitting wild virus.

**Vaccination (Jenner, 1796):** Used cowpox (*vaccinia*) material—safer, no smallpox transmission risk. 19th century: cowpox replaced by *vaccinia* virus (origin unknown, genetically distinct from both cowpox and variola). **Modern vaccine:** Live vaccinia virus administered via bifurcated needle (invented Benjamin Rubin, repurposed D.A. Henderson 1967) causing localized "take" (Jennerian vesicle → pustule → scab → scar). **Efficacy:** ~95% prevention. High immunity 3–5 years, waning thereafter; revaccination extends protection. Historical data: fatality 1.3% if vaccinated <10 yrs prior, 7% (11–20 yrs), 11% (>20 yrs) vs. 52% unvaccinated. **Risks:** Serious non-fatal reactions ~1/1,000; life-threatening ~14–500/million; death ~1–2/million (postvaccinial encephalitis, progressive vaccinia). Routine vaccination ceased in US (1972), Europe (1970s), globally (1986) as disease risk fell below vaccine risk. Current use: lab workers, military, mpox prevention. **Newer vaccines:** ACAM2000 (live vaccinia, Vero cell culture, FDA 2007) stockpiled.

### Treatment
**Post-exposure prophylaxis:** Vaccination within 3 days prevents/modifies disease; 4–7 days may modify severity. **Supportive care:** Wound/infection control, fluid therapy, ventilator support. Flat/hemorrhagic types managed as shock (fluid resuscitation); confluent cases akin to extensive burns. **Antivirals (post-eradication development):** Cidofovir (IV, nephrotoxic, suggested 2004). Tecovirimat (FDA approved 2018, first specific anti-smallpox drug; resistance mutations observed during 2022–23 mpox outbreak). Brincidofovir (FDA approved 2021).

### Prognosis and Complications
**Mortality:** *Variola major* ~30% overall; *Variola minor* ~1%. Ordinary discrete <10%; semi-confluent 25–50%; confluent 50–75%; malignant/hemorrhagic 90–100%. Children <1 yr: 40–50%. Death typically days 10–16 (ordinary); day 6 (early hemorrhagic, heart failure/pulmonary edema); days 8–10 (late hemorrhagic/flat, sepsis/electrolyte loss). **Complications:** Pitted scars (65–80% survivors, face prominent); ocular (2% cases: keratitis, corneal ulcer → blindness 35–40% affected eyes); encephalitis (1/500, adults); osteomyelitis variolosa (2–5% children, symmetrical bony lesions → limb deformities); respiratory (bronchitis/pneumonia, day 8, viral/bacterial); secondary bacterial skin infection (uncommon).

### History: Emergence and Global Spread
Earliest credible evidence: Egyptian mummy Ramses V (1145 BCE), Indian texts (1500 BCE), Chinese texts (1122 BCE). Likely endemic in India for 2000+ years. Spread: China → Japan (6th c., 735–737 epidemic killed ~1/3 population); Southwest Asia/Europe timing unclear—absent from Greek/Roman literature, Bible; possibly Antonine Plague (165–180 CE); Rhazes (9th c. Persia) first definitive description differentiating from measles/chickenpox. Crusades (population growth/mobility) established endemicity in Europe by 16th c. (30% mortality).

**Columbian Exchange:** No pre-Columbian evidence in Americas. Introduced Hispaniola (1507), Mexico (1520, Spanish settlers). Native Americans lacked immunity; case fatality up to 90%. Decimated Aztec (Cuitláhuac died 1520) and Inca (Huayna Capac died 1527 → civil war) empires, facilitating conquest. Plymouth (1633) devastated Native Americans and colonists. Australia (1789, 1829) principal cause of Aboriginal death 1780–1870. By mid-18th c., endemic globally except Australia/isolated islands. 18th c. Europe: ~400,000 deaths/year; 1/3 blindness cases; up to 10% Swedish infants died annually. Variolation reduced impact among wealthy late 18th c.; vaccination drove real decline late 19th c. *Variola minor* spread globally early 20th c. (US, UK, South Africa), becoming dominant and lowering mortality.

### History: Eradication
**Early efforts:** Jenner (1796) → regional elimination attempts. Balmis expedition (1803) to Spanish colonies. First compulsory vaccination: Thurgau (1806), Lucca/Piombino (1806), Baden (1809), Prussia (1815), Sweden (1816), German Empire (1874). UK banned variolation (1842), mandated vaccination (1853). US federal program for Native Americans (1832); state mandates (Massachusetts 1843 onward). Northern Europe eliminated by 1900; industrialized nations low incidence by 1914.

**Global campaign:** 1950 PAHO hemisphere effort (success except 4 S. American countries). 1958 USSR proposes global initiative (WHA11.54). 1959–1966: modest funding ($100k/yr), 2M deaths/yr, disappointing progress. 1966: Smallpox Eradication Unit formed under Donald Henderson. 1967: WHO intensifies ($2.4M/yr), adopts Karel Raška’s surveillance/containment ("ring vaccination"): isolate cases, vaccinate contacts. Key advantages: humans only reservoir, no carriers, heat-stable vaccine, bifurcated needle. Soviet Union donated 1.5B doses (1958–1979); by 1973 >80% vaccine produced in developing countries.

**Final push:** Last major European outbreak Yugoslavia 1972 (175 cases, 35 deaths, martial law/quarantine). By end 1975, only Horn of Africa. Intensive campaign Ethiopia/Somalia 1977 (Frank Fenner). Last *Variola major*: Rahima Banu, Bangladesh, Oct 1975. Last *Variola minor* (indigenous): Ali Maow Maalin, Somalia, 26 Oct 1977. Certified Dec 1979; WHA endorsed May 1980. Cost 1967–1979: ~US$300M (1/3 from developed world); US recoups investment every 26 days in averted costs. Eradication called "one of the noblest and best things we have ever done."

### Post-Eradication Events
1978: Janet Parker (UK lab photographer) died from lab-acquired infection → all stocks consolidated to two WHO BSL-4 labs: CDC (Atlanta) and VECTOR (Koltsovo, Russia). WHO recommended destruction (1986), deadlines postponed (1993, 1999). 2002: WHA permits temporary retention for research. 2010 WHO review: no essential public health purpose served by retention. **Incidents:** 2003 scabs found in 1888 book (Santa Fe); 2014 viable vials (1954) found in FDA cold storage (Bethesda) → transferred to CDC, destroyed 2015. 2017: Horsepox synthesized (U. Alberta, ~$100k) demonstrating feasibility of variola synthesis → dual-use concerns. 2019: Gas explosion at VECTOR (no breach).

### Society and Culture
**Biological Warfare:** 1763 Fort Pitt (Pontiac's War): British (Amherst, Bouquet, Ecuyer) gave smallpox-infected blankets to Delaware delegation; effectiveness unknown. Accounts of use in American Revolutionary War. Disputed theory: Royal Marines used smallpox in New South Wales 1789 (Warren vs. Carmody/chickenpox hypothesis). WWII: UK, US, Japan (Unit 731) researched weaponization; abandoned due to vaccine availability. 1947: USSR established weapons factory (Zagorsk). 1971: Aral Sea (Vozrozhdeniya Island) open-air test caused fatal outbreak (lab technician infected ship → Aralsk). 1991: US/UK inspection of Biopreparat met with evasion. 1992: Defector Ken Alibek alleged 20-ton stockpile of weaponized variola (possibly vaccine-resistant) at Zagorsk; unverified. 1997: Russia moved samples to VECTOR; concern over scientist proliferation post-USSR collapse.

**Notable Cases:** Monarchs: Louis XV (d. 1774), Mary II, Joseph I, Louis I, Peter II, Ulrika Elenora. Rulers: Ramses V, Kangxi (survived), Shunzhi, Tongzhi, Komei (d. 1867), Date Masamune (lost eye), Cuitláhuac (d. 1520), Huayna Capac (d. 1527). US Presidents: Washington (1751), Jackson, Lincoln (1863). Others: Guru Har Krishan (d. 1664), Jonathan Edwards (d. 1758, post-inoculation), Stalin (scarred, retouched photos), Ferenc Kölcsey (lost eye). Dynastic impacts: Louis XV succession cleared by smallpox deaths; Peter II death; Catherine the Great inoculated self/son (1768), promoted empire-wide (2M by 1800). Qing dynasty protocols protected Manchus.

**Tradition/Religion:** Deities worshipped to appease/avert disease: China (T'ou-Shen Niang-Niang, "heaven flower" euphemism, ugly masks at New Year); Yoruba (Sopona/Obaluaye, "one who kills by covering with pustules," priests controlled worship, banned by British for alleged spreading); India (Shitala, broom/cool water, shrines, cooling foods on roofs). **Red treatment:** Near-universal belief smallpox demons feared red; patients/rooms decorated red. Practiced 12th c. Europe (Charles V, Elizabeth I). Afforded scientific credibility by Niels Finsen (red light reduces scarring); persisted to 1930s.

## Terms
- ****Variolation**** — Early immunization method using material from *Variola minor* pustules (scabs/pus) to induce mild infection and immunity; mortality 0.5–2%. Also called inoculation.
- ****Vaccination**** — Jenner's method (1796) using cowpox/vaccinia virus to confer cross-protective immunity without risk of smallpox transmission; safer than variolation.
- ****Ring Vaccination**** — Eradication strategy: intensive surveillance to detect cases, then vaccination of all close contacts (ring) to interrupt transmission chains.
- ****Variola major**** — Severe, historically dominant clade of smallpox virus; ~30% case-fatality rate; cause of vast majority of historical mortality.
- ****Variola minor (Alastrim)**** — Mild clade of smallpox virus; <1% mortality; patients often ambulant, facilitating spread; infection conferred immunity to *Variola major*.
- ****Orthopoxvirus**** — Genus of *Poxviridae* including variola, vaccinia, cowpox, monkeypox; brick-shaped, cytoplasmic replication, linear dsDNA genome.
- ****Guarnieri Bodies**** — Characteristic cytoplasmic inclusion bodies (viral replication sites) seen in poxvirus infections; identified via hematoxylin-eosin staining of skin biopsies.
- ****Viremia**** — Presence of virus in the bloodstream; in smallpox, primary viremia (~day 3–4) seeds internal organs, secondary viremia (~day 12) seeds skin/mucosa.
- ****Enanthem**** — Rash on mucous membranes (mouth, throat); in smallpox, appears days 12–15 before skin rash, releases virus into saliva.
- ****Bifurcated Needle**** — Two-pronged needle (invented Benjamin Rubin, adapted D.A. Henderson 1967) for percutaneous vaccine delivery; holds dose by surface tension, enables rapid, low-cost administration.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Virus Stock Destruction:** Whether to destroy the two remaining WHO-sanctioned variola stocks (CDC, VECTOR). Pro-destruction: eliminates lab accident/terrorism risk; stocks not needed for outbreak response (vaccines exist). Pro-retention: potential need for antiviral/vaccine development against engineered threats; 2010 WHO review concluded no essential public health
