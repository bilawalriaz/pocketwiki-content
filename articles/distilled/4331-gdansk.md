# Gdańsk

## Overview
Gdańsk is a major Polish seaport on the Baltic coast, historically a crossroads of Polish, German, and Kashubian cultures. Its strategic location made it a contested prize for centuries, shaping its identity through periods of Polish, Teutonic, Prussian, and German rule. The city is globally significant as the birthplace of the Solidarity movement, which helped end communist rule in Eastern Europe.

## Timeline
- **997** — First written mention as *urbs Gyddanyzc* in the life of St. Adalbert.
- **1308** — Teutonic Knights seize the town, incorporating it into their monastic state.
- **1343** — Treaty of Kalisz: Teutonic Order holds Gdańsk as a grant from the Polish Crown.
- **1454** — City pledges allegiance to Polish King Casimir IV, beginning the Thirteen Years' War.
- **1457** — King grants the Great Privilege, giving Gdańsk full autonomy within Poland.
- **1793** — Annexed by Prussia in the Second Partition of Poland.
- **1920** — Becomes the Free City of Danzig under League of Nations protection.
- **1939** — Battle of Westerplatte marks the start of World War II.
- **1945** — City captured by Soviet/Polish forces; post-war population exchange begins.
- **1980** — Gdańsk Shipyard strike leads to the founding of the Solidarity trade union.

## Body

### Early History and Medieval Autonomy
The settlement began in the 9th century, becoming a trade center annexed by Poland's Piast dynasty around 975. After a period of rule by the Samboride dynasty, the Teutonic Knights seized the town in 1308. The Order rebuilt it and, by the mid-14th century, it thrived as a Hanseatic League port exporting grain and timber via the Vistula. In 1454, Gdańsk rebelled against the Order and rejoined the Kingdom of Poland. The 1457 Great Privilege granted it extensive autonomy, including its own laws, mint, and tax-free trade within Poland-Lithuania, cementing its role as the Polish Crown's principal seaport.

### Prussian, German, and Free City Eras
Gdańsk was annexed by Prussia in 1793 and became part of the German Empire in 1871. After World War I, the Treaty of Versailles created the Free City of Danzig (1920–1939), a semi-autonomous city-state under League of Nations oversight but with Poland controlling its foreign affairs and harbor access. The rise of Nazism brought anti-Polish repression. On 1 September 1939, the German battleship *Schleswig-Holstein* fired on the Polish garrison at Westerplatte, one of the first acts of World War II.

### Post-War Transformation and Solidarity
Captured in 1945, the heavily damaged city was annexed by Poland. Its German population was expelled and replaced by Polish settlers, including those from former eastern Polish territories annexed by the USSR. The historic center was reconstructed to emphasize its pre-Prussian Polish character. In the 1970s and 1980s, the city's shipyards became centers of anti-communist protest. The 1980 Gdańsk Shipyard strike, led by Lech Wałęsa, founded the Solidarity (Solidarność) trade union, a movement that challenged communist rule across Eastern Europe and helped precipitate its collapse in 1989.

## Terms
*   **Hanseatic League:** A medieval commercial and defensive confederation of merchant guilds and market towns in Northern Europe.
*   **Teutonic Order:** A German medieval military order that established a monastic state in the Baltic region.
*   **Great Privilege (1457):** A charter from Polish King Casimir IV granting Gdańsk extensive autonomy, including self-governance and trade rights.
*   **Free City of Danzig (1920–1939):** A semi-autonomous city-state created by the Treaty of Versailles, under League of Nations protection but with Poland controlling its foreign policy and port.
*   **Solidarity (Solidarność):** An independent trade union and social movement founded in Gdańsk in 1980, which played a central role in ending communist rule in Poland.
*   **Tricity (Trójmiasto):** The metropolitan area formed by Gdańsk, Gdynia, and Sopot.
*   **Pomeranian Voivodeship:** The administrative province (voivodeship) of which Gdańsk is the capital.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **The 1308 Massacre:** The scale of the Teutonic Knights' massacre after seizing Gdańsk is debated, with estimates of victims ranging from 60 to 1,000.
*   **Cultural Identity:** The historical and cultural connections between Gdańsk and the Kashubian region are a subject of controversy among Kashubians themselves.
*   **Post-War Reconstruction:** The deliberate effort to "de-Germanize" the city's architecture during post-war rebuilding has been a point of historical and ideological discussion.
