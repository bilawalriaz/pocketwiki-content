# Michelangelo

## Overview
Michelangelo Buonarroti (1475–1564) was an Italian sculptor, painter, architect, and poet whose mastery across disciplines defined the High Renaissance. Born near Arezzo and raised in Florence, he absorbed classical ideals and Humanist philosophy at the Medici court before establishing his reputation in Rome with the *Pietà* (1499) and *David* (1504). Though he considered himself a sculptor, his frescoes on the Sistine Chapel ceiling (1508–1512) and *The Last Judgment* (1536–1541) revolutionized Western painting through their dynamic anatomy and complex theological programs. As architect of St. Peter’s Basilica from 1546, he centralized Bramante’s plan and designed the iconic dome, completed posthumously. His *terribilità*—awe-inspiring emotional intensity—and expressive physicality catalyzed Mannerism. He was the first Western artist with a biography published during his lifetime, documented extensively through correspondence, poetry, and three contemporary lives.

## Body

### Early Formation and Medici Patronage (1475–1494)
Michelangelo was born in Caprese, where his father Ludovico served briefly as podestà. The family returned to Florence; after his mother’s death (1481), he lived with a stonecutter’s family in Settignano, gaining early familiarity with marble. He rejected grammar studies under Francesco da Urbino, preferring to copy masters like Giotto and Masaccio. In 1488, he apprenticed with Domenico Ghirlandaio, mastering fresco technique. Lorenzo de’ Medici recruited him to the Platonic Academy (1490–1492), where Neoplatonists Marsilio Ficino and Pico della Mirandola shaped his intellectual outlook. Under sculptor Bertoldo di Giovanni, he produced his first marble reliefs, *Madonna of the Stairs* and *Battle of the Centaurs*. A blow from fellow pupil Pietro Torrigiano disfigured his nose c. 1492.

### First Roman Sojourn and the *Pietà* (1496–1501)
Following Lorenzo’s death (1492) and the Medici expulsion (1494), Michelangelo worked in Bologna on the Shrine of St. Dominic, studying Jacopo della Quercia’s reliefs—later echoed in the Sistine ceiling. In Florence, he carved a *Cupid* artificially aged to pass as an antique; Cardinal Raffaele Riario discovered the fraud but invited the sculptor to Rome (1496). There, his *Bacchus* was rejected by Riario but bought by banker Jacopo Galli. In 1497, Cardinal Jean de Bilhères commissioned the *Pietà*, completed at age 24. The work’s technical perfection and emotional restraint—Mary’s youth, Christ’s peaceful repose—established his fame. It remains his only signed sculpture.

### Florentine Triumph: *David* and Lost Cartoons (1501–1505)
Returning to Florence, Michelangelo accepted the Guild of Wool’s commission to finish a colossal *David* from a flawed block abandoned by Agostino di Duccio. Completed in 1504, the 5.17-meter marble embodied civic liberty (*libertas*) through tense contrapposto and concentrated gaze. A committee including Leonardo da Vinci and Botticelli placed it in the Piazza della Signoria. Simultaneously, he was commissioned to paint the *Battle of Cascina* in the Palazzo Vecchio, opposing Leonardo’s *Battle of Anghiari*. Neither fresco was executed; both cartoons were lost, though copies by Bastiano da Sangallo and Rubens preserve their compositions. He also painted the *Doni Tondo* (Uffizi), whose twisting figures and saturated color anticipate the Sistine ceiling.

### The Julius II Tomb and Sistine Ceiling (1505–1512)
Pope Julius II summoned Michelangelo to Rome in 1505 for a massive freestanding tomb with forty statues. Frequent interruptions and clashes with the pope led Michelangelo to flee to Florence in 1506; he returned under pressure. The tomb was never finished as conceived; the reduced version in San Pietro in Vincoli centers on *Moses* (1516), flanked by the *Rebellious Slave* and *Dying Slave* (Louvre). In 1508, Julius redirected him to the Sistine Chapel ceiling. Michelangelo replaced the prescribed Twelve Apostles with a vast program: nine Genesis scenes framed by prophets, sibyls, and *ignudi* (idealized nude youths). Over four years, he painted 500+ square meters and 300+ figures, evolving from detailed narrative groups (e.g., *Drunkenness of Noah*) to monumental, simplified forms (*Separation of Light from Darkness*, painted in a day). *The Creation of Adam* became an icon of divine-human contact.

### Medici Projects in Florence (1513–1534)
Under Medici popes Leo X (1513–1521) and Clement VII (1523–1534), Michelangelo worked at San Lorenzo. Leo’s façade commission (1516–1520) was cancelled after quarrying at Pietrasanta. The Medici Chapel (New Sacristy, 1520–1534) houses tombs of Giuliano, Duke of Nemours, and Lorenzo, Duke of Urbino, each with allegorical pairs: *Night/Day* and *Dusk/Dawn*. The *Medici Madonna* crowns the ensemble. For the Laurentian Library (1524), he designed a revolutionary vestibule with recessed columns and a lava-like staircase, a seminal Mannerist space. During the 1527–1530 republican siege, he directed fortifications. After the Medici restoration, Clement VII sentenced him to death; he hid for two months in a corridor beneath the New Sacristy (rediscovered 1975), drawing on the walls. Pardoned, he left Florence permanently in 1534.

### *The Last Judgment* and Pauline Chapel (1534–1550)
In Rome, Michelangelo befriended the poet Vittoria Colonna. Clement VII commissioned *The Last Judgment* (1534–1541) on the Sistine altar wall. Christ appears as a massive, beardless, nude Apollo-like judge; the saved rise, the damned fall. Nudity provoked charges of sacrilege; at the Council of Trent (1564), Daniele da Volterra was hired to paint draperies (*braghe*). The *Crucifixion of St. Peter* and *Conversion of Saul* in the Cappella Paolina (1542–1550) are dense, introspective narratives; in the former, Michelangelo depicts himself as a disillusioned onlooker.

### St. Peter’s Basilica and Final Architecture (1546–1564)
Appointed chief architect in 1546 at age 71, Michelangelo reverted to Bramante’s Greek-cross plan, simplifying and strengthening the piers to support a hemispherical (later ovoid) dome. He designed the exterior’s giant order and rippling cornice. The dome’s supporting ring was underway at his death; Giacomo della Porta completed it (1588–1590) with a steeper profile. He also designed the Campidoglio pavement (oval with star pattern), the Palazzo Farnese upper floor, Santa Maria degli Angeli (converting the Baths of Diocletian), and the Porta Pia.

### Late Sculpture and Poetry
In old age, Michelangelo carved unfinished *Pietàs* reflecting on mortality. The *Florentine Pietà* (c. 1547–1555) includes a self-portrait as Nicodemus; he damaged it in frustration. The *Rondanini Pietà* (1552–1564), worked on six days before death, was carved to near-abstraction. He wrote over 300 poems, many to the nobleman Tommaso de’ Cavalieri (met 1532), forming the first major modern corpus of male-to-male love poetry. Later sonnets to Vittoria Colonna address spiritual themes. His grandnephew censored the pronouns in the 1623 edition; original genders were restored in 1893.

## Terms
- **terribilità**: A quality of awe-inspiring, overwhelming emotional intensity in Michelangelo’s figures, admired by contemporaries.
- **ignudi**: The twenty seated male nudes on the Sistine ceiling, serving as decorative and structural supports for the narrative scenes.
- **contrapposto**: A pose where weight rests on one leg, creating asymmetrical tension in hips and shoulders; used by Michelangelo to imply latent energy.
- **non finito**: The deliberate or circumstantial leaving of a work unfinished, revealing the sculptor’s process of “liberating” the figure from the block.
- **Mannerism**: A post-High Renaissance style (c. 1520–1600) characterized by artificiality, elongation, and instability, directly inspired by Michelangelo’s late manner.
- **Platonic Academy**: A Humanist circle in Florence sponsored by the Medici, where Michelangelo encountered Neoplatonic philosophy linking beauty to divine love.
- **cartoon**: A full-scale preparatory drawing for a fresco or painting, transferred to the wall by pouncing or incision.
- **Sibyls**: Female prophets of classical antiquity (e.g., Cumaean, Libyan) depicted on the Sistine ceiling alongside Hebrew prophets as pagan precursors to Christ.
- **giant order**: An architectural order (columns/pilasters) spanning two or more stories, used by Michelangelo at St. Peter’s and the Campidoglio for monumental unity.
- **presentation drawings**: Highly finished drawings given as gifts (e.g., *Pietà for Vittoria Colonna*), not studies for execution.

## Debates and open questions
- **Sexuality and poetry**: Whether the sonnets to Tommaso de’ Cavalieri express homoerotic desire or Neoplatonic spiritual friendship remains contested; the 1623 censorship and 1893 restoration frame the debate.
- **Sistine ceiling authorship**: The extent of assistant participation in the *buon fresco* execution (vs. Michelangelo’s sole hand) is debated, though he reportedly dismissed most assistants early on.
- **Julius II tomb reduction**: The degree to which the final monument (San Pietro in Vincoli) reflects Michelangelo’s intent vs. contractual compromises by heirs and popes is unclear.
- **Dome profile**: Whether Michelangelo intended a hemispherical or ovoid dome for St. Peter’s is disputed; della Porta’s steeper execution may alter the structural and visual logic.
- **Late Pietàs’ meaning**: The *Florentine* and *Rondanini Pietàs* are read variously as penitential self-portraits, theological meditations on the Incarnation, or formal experiments anticipating modern abstraction.
- **Laurentian Library vestibule dating**: The staircase’s final form (built 1559 by Ammannati from a clay model) may deviate from Michelangelo’s 1520s design, complicating attribution of its Mannerist innovations.
