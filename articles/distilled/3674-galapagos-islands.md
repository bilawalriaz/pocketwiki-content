# Galápagos Islands

## Overview
The Galápagos Islands are a volcanic archipelago in the Eastern Pacific, belonging to Ecuador. They are globally renowned for their exceptional biodiversity and high number of endemic species, which were studied by Charles Darwin and became foundational to his theory of evolution by natural selection. The islands are a UNESCO World Heritage Site and a critical area for conservation and scientific research.

## Timeline
- **c. 8-90 million years ago** — First islands form over the Galápagos hotspot.
- **10 March 1535** — Accidental European discovery by Bishop Tomás de Berlanga.
- **1684** — English pirate Ambrose Cowley creates the first thorough map, naming islands.
- **12 February 1832** — Ecuador formally annexes the islands.
- **15 Sep – 20 Oct 1835** — HMS Beagle visits; Charles Darwin studies geology and wildlife.
- **1959** — Ecuador declares 97.5% of the land area a national park; Charles Darwin Foundation founded.
- **1973** — Galápagos Province is officially established.
- **1978** — UNESCO designates the islands a World Heritage Site.
- **1986** — A marine reserve is declared around the islands.
- **2007** — UNESCO places the islands on the List of World Heritage in Danger.
- **2010** — UNESCO removes the islands from the danger list.

## Body

### Geology and Formation
The Galápagos sit on the Nazca tectonic plate, which moves eastward over a stationary mantle plume known as the Galápagos hotspot. This process has created a chain of volcanoes for at least 20 million years. The western islands, like Fernandina and Isabela, are younger, taller, and more volcanically active, while the eastern islands are older and more eroded. The archipelago features numerous active volcanoes with large calderas, such as Sierra Negra on Isabela, which experienced significant uplift before its 2018 eruption.

### Human History and Settlement
There is no firm evidence of pre-Columbian human visitation. European discovery occurred in 1535, but the islands were largely ignored until the 17th century, when they became a base for pirates and whalers. These visitors introduced invasive species like goats and rats, severely damaging ecosystems. Ecuador annexed the islands in 1832, and the first permanent settlement was established. Charles Darwin's 1835 visit on HMS Beagle was part of a British survey and proved crucial to his later work. In the 20th century, the U.S. built a military base on Baltra during WWII, later transferred to Ecuador. Tourism grew from the 1960s onward, becoming central to the local economy.

### Ecology and Biodiversity
The islands' isolation led to the evolution of unique endemic species, including giant tortoises, marine iguanas, flightless cormorants, and Darwin's finches. The terrestrial environment ranges from arid lowlands with cacti to humid highlands with forests. The surrounding marine reserve, shaped by converging ocean currents, supports rich biodiversity, including penguins and albatrosses. The primary ecological threats are introduced invasive species (plants and animals) and the impacts of a growing human population and tourism.

### Conservation and Modern Challenges
Formal protection began in 1959 with the national park, followed by the marine reserve in 1986. The Charles Darwin Foundation provides scientific research to guide management. Major conservation successes include the eradication of feral goats from several islands. However, challenges persist, including illegal fishing, the introduction of new invasive species, and the pressures of tourism. The islands were temporarily placed on UNESCO's danger list in 2007 due to these threats but were removed in 2010 after progress was made.

## Terms
- **Endemic species**: A species found naturally in only one specific geographic area.
- **Hotspot**: A location on Earth's surface over a mantle plume, leading to intense volcanic activity.
- **Shield volcano**: A broad, gently sloping volcano built from fluid lava flows.
- **Caldera**: A large cauldron-like hollow that forms shortly after the emptying of a magma chamber in a volcanic eruption.
- **Marine reserve**: A protected area of ocean where activities like fishing are restricted to conserve marine life.
- **Invasive species**: A non-native organism that causes ecological or economic harm.
- **El Niño**: A climate pattern describing unusual warming of surface waters in the eastern tropical Pacific Ocean.
- **Mestizo**: A person of combined European and Indigenous American descent.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Pre-Columbian visitation**: Archaeological evidence is debated. While some artifacts were found, a 2016 reanalysis suggests they may have been deposited after Spanish contact, and no evidence of permanent settlement exists.
- **Conservation vs. Development**: The islands face ongoing tension between protecting their unique ecosystems and supporting the local economy, which is heavily dependent on tourism and fishing.
- **Effectiveness of protection**: While significant progress has been made, the recurring threat of invasive species and the impacts of climate events like El Niño raise questions about the long-term efficacy of conservation measures.
