# José Rizal

## Overview
José Rizal (1861–1896) was a Filipino polymath, writer, and nationalist whose novels and essays exposed the injustices of Spanish colonial rule, inspiring the Philippine Revolution. He is widely regarded as a national hero of the Philippines, though no official proclamation formally declares him as such. His life and works are central to understanding the birth of Filipino national identity and the struggle for independence.

## Timeline
- **June 19, 1861** — Born in Calamba, Laguna, Philippines.
- **1882** — Secretly travels to Spain to study medicine and liberal arts.
- **1887** — Publishes his first novel, *Noli Me Tángere*, in Berlin.
- **1891** — Publishes his second novel, *El filibusterismo*, in Ghent.
- **July 6, 1892** — Arrested and exiled to Dapitan, Mindanao, after founding La Liga Filipina.
- **August 1, 1896** — Leaves Dapitan for Cuba to serve as a doctor.
- **October 6, 1896** — Arrested in Barcelona after the Philippine Revolution breaks out.
- **December 30, 1896** — Executed by firing squad at Bagumbayan (now Luneta Park).

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into a family of Chinese, Japanese, and Tagalog descent, Rizal was the seventh child of leaseholders on a Dominican hacienda. His mother, Teodora, taught him to read at age three. After early schooling in Biñan, he excelled at the Ateneo Municipal de Manila, graduating with a Bachelor of Arts in 1877. He then studied philosophy and medicine at the University of Santo Tomas, but left for Spain in 1882 without his parents' consent to pursue further studies.

### Studies and Activism in Europe
In Madrid, Rizal studied medicine and philosophy while joining the Propaganda Movement, a group advocating political reforms for the Philippines under Spain. He became a prolific writer, contributing essays and poems to the newspaper *La Solidaridad*. His most influential works were his two novels: *Noli Me Tángere* (1887), which exposed the abuses of Spanish friars, and its sequel, *El filibusterismo* (1891), which had a more revolutionary tone. These books angered colonial authorities and educated Filipinos alike.

### Exile in Dapitan and Return to Manila
In 1892, Rizal returned to Manila and founded La Liga Filipina, a civic organization. He was arrested days later and exiled to Dapitan. During his four-year exile, he practiced medicine, established a school, and conducted scientific research. In 1895, he met Josephine Bracken, an Irish woman who became his common-law wife. He was granted permission to serve as a doctor in Cuba in 1896 but was arrested en route after the Philippine Revolution, inspired by his writings, had begun.

### Trial and Execution
Rizal was tried by a military court for rebellion, sedition, and conspiracy. Despite disavowing the ongoing revolution in a manifesto, he was convicted and sentenced to death. He was executed by a squad of Filipino soldiers on December 30, 1896. His final poem, *Mi último adiós*, was hidden in an alcohol stove and later became a symbol of patriotism.

### Legacy and Controversy
Rizal's execution made him a martyr for the cause of independence. His remains were later exhumed and reinterred at the Rizal Monument in Luneta Park. A major controversy surrounds a document allegedly showing he retracted his anti-Catholic views before his death; its authenticity is fiercely debated. He is honored with a national holiday, and the Rizal Law mandates the study of his life and works in schools. His status as the "national hero" is popularly accepted but was not established by a formal law.

## Terms
*   **Propaganda Movement:** A political reform movement in the 1880s-90s, led by Filipino expatriates in Europe, that advocated for representation in the Spanish Cortes and other liberal reforms.
*   **La Liga Filipina:** A civic organization founded by Rizal in 1892 to promote social reforms through legal means; it was quickly disbanded by Spanish authorities.
*   **Katipunan:** A secret revolutionary society founded by Andrés Bonifacio that used Rizal's name and writings to inspire the Philippine Revolution.
*   **Noli Me Tángere:** Rizal's first novel (1887), a social commentary on the abuses of Spanish colonial rule and the Catholic Church.
*   **El filibusterismo:** Rizal's second novel (1891), a sequel to *Noli Me Tángere* with a more radical, revolutionary theme.
*   **Mi último adiós:** Rizal's famous final poem, written before his execution, expressing love for his country and willingness to die for it.
*   **Retraction controversy:** The ongoing historical debate over the authenticity of a document in which Rizal allegedly retracted his anti-clerical writings before his execution.
*   **Rizal Law (Republic Act No. 1425):** A 1956 Philippine law requiring all educational institutions to include courses on Rizal's life, works, and writings.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **National Hero Status:** While popularly revered, Rizal was never officially declared the national hero by law or executive order. Some historians argue his status was promoted by American colonizers as a model of peaceful reform, in contrast to more radical figures like Andrés Bonifacio.
*   **Stance on Revolution:** Rizal publicly condemned the 1896 revolution, but his writings inspired it. Eyewitness accounts of his private views are conflicting, with some suggesting he supported a well-prepared uprising.
*   **The Retraction:** The debate over whether Rizal retracted his anti-Catholic beliefs before his execution remains unresolved. Supporters cite eyewitness testimony and the document itself, while doubters point to a lack of corroborating evidence and questions about the document's authenticity.
