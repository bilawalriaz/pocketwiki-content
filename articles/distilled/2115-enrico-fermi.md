# Enrico Fermi

## Overview
Enrico Fermi (1901–1954) was an Italian-American physicist whose work was foundational to nuclear physics and the atomic age. He excelled in both theoretical and experimental physics, making seminal contributions to statistical mechanics, quantum theory, and nuclear reactions. He is best known for creating the world's first artificial nuclear reactor and for his central role in the Manhattan Project, which developed the first atomic bombs.

## Timeline
- **29 September 1901** — Born in Rome, Italy.
- **1922** — Earns doctorate from University of Pisa at age 20.
- **1926** — Becomes professor of theoretical physics at Sapienza University of Rome.
- **1933–34** — Develops theory of the weak interaction, introducing the "neutrino."
- **1934** — Discovers that slow neutrons are more effective at inducing radioactivity.
- **1938** — Awarded Nobel Prize in Physics; emigrates to the United States to escape Italian racial laws.
- **2 December 1942** — Leads team that achieves the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction (Chicago Pile-1).
- **16 July 1945** — Present at the Trinity test, the first detonation of a nuclear weapon.
- **1945–54** — Post-war work at University of Chicago and Argonne National Laboratory; serves on AEC advisory committee.
- **28 November 1954** — Dies of stomach cancer in Chicago.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Fermi showed early aptitude for physics and mathematics, mentored by family friend Adolfo Amidei. He entered the Scuola Normale Superiore in Pisa, where he studied under Luigi Puccianti and published his first scientific works on topics like relativistic electrodynamics. After earning his doctorate in 1922, he studied in Germany with Max Born and in the Netherlands with Paul Ehrenfest, meeting leading physicists like Heisenberg and Einstein.

### Theoretical Breakthroughs in Rome
In 1926, Fermi was appointed to one of Italy's first theoretical physics chairs at the University of Rome. He developed **Fermi–Dirac statistics** (1926), describing particles that obey the Pauli exclusion principle, now called **fermions**. He then formulated a theory of **beta decay** (1933–34), introducing the **neutrino** and describing the **weak interaction**, one of the four fundamental forces. His theory was initially rejected by the journal *Nature*.

### Experimental Discoveries and the Nobel Prize
Switching to experimental work in 1934, Fermi bombarded elements with neutrons. He discovered that **slow neutrons** were far more effective at inducing radioactivity than fast ones, a key insight he described with the **Fermi age equation**. When bombarding uranium, he believed he had created new transuranic elements, for which he received the 1938 Nobel Prize. This conclusion was later proven incorrect; the products were actually from **nuclear fission**, a process he had initially dismissed.

### The Manhattan Project
Fermi left Italy in 1938 for the United States. After the discovery of fission was confirmed, he worked at Columbia University and then the University of Chicago. He led the construction of **Chicago Pile-1**, the first nuclear reactor, which achieved a self-sustaining chain reaction on 2 December 1942. During the war, he contributed to the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos, heading the F Division and helping design the **B Reactor** at Hanford, where he solved a critical shutdown problem caused by **xenon poisoning**. He was present at the Trinity test, using his "Fermi method" to estimate the bomb's yield.

### Post-War Career and Legacy
After the war, Fermi helped establish the Institute for Nuclear Studies (later the Enrico Fermi Institute) at Chicago. He served on the Atomic Energy Commission's General Advisory Committee, opposing the development of the hydrogen bomb on moral and technical grounds. He continued research in particle physics, particularly on **pions** and **muons**, and speculated on the origin of **cosmic rays**. He died in 1954 at age 53.

## Terms
- **Fermi–Dirac statistics**: A statistical model describing the distribution of particles (fermions) that obey the Pauli exclusion principle.
- **Fermions**: Particles (like electrons, protons, and neutrons) that obey the Pauli exclusion principle and Fermi–Dirac statistics.
- **Neutrino**: A neutral, nearly massless particle proposed by Fermi to explain energy conservation in beta decay.
- **Weak interaction**: One of the four fundamental forces of nature, responsible for radioactive decay like beta decay.
- **Slow neutrons**: Neutrons that have been slowed down (moderated) and are more easily captured by atomic nuclei.
- **Fermi age equation**: A diffusion equation Fermi developed to describe the slowing down of neutrons.
- **Nuclear fission**: The splitting of a heavy atomic nucleus into lighter nuclei, releasing energy.
- **Chicago Pile-1 (CP-1)**: The first artificial nuclear reactor, built by Fermi's team, which achieved the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction.
- **Xenon poisoning**: The shutdown of a nuclear reactor caused by the buildup of the neutron-absorbing fission product xenon-135.
- **Fermi method**: A technique for making quick, order-of-magnitude estimates using basic principles and simple calculations.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Discovery of fission**: Fermi's initial Nobel Prize-winning work was based on the incorrect conclusion that he had created transuranic elements. The correct interpretation of nuclear fission was later established by others, including Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch.
- **Hydrogen bomb opposition**: Fermi strongly opposed the development of the hydrogen bomb on both moral and technical grounds after the first Soviet atomic test in 1949.
- **Fermi paradox**: Fermi mused about the contradiction between the high probability of extraterrestrial life and the lack of evidence for it, a question that remains open.
