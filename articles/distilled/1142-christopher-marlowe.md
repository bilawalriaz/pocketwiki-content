# Christopher Marlowe

## Overview

Christopher Marlowe (baptised 26 February 1564 – 30 May 1593) was an English playwright, poet, and translator of the Elizabethan era, widely regarded as the foremost dramatist in London in the years preceding his mysterious death. He pioneered the use of blank verse in English drama, establishing a stylistic standard that influenced contemporaries including Shakespeare. His plays—featuring overreaching protagonists and themes of humanism, violence, and religious skepticism—were enormously successful, yet his brief adult life was marked by controversy, espionage allegations, and unresolved questions surrounding his death.

## Timeline

- **26 Feb 1564** — Baptised at St George's Church, Canterbury
- **c. 1580s** — Studied at Corpus Christi College, Cambridge; translated Ovid’s *Amores*
- **1587** — Awarded MA by Cambridge after Privy Council intervention; *Tamburlaine* first performed
- **1589** — Arrested in Flushing, Netherlands, on counterfeiting charges
- **1592** — Implicated in fatal quarrel with Thomas Watson; imprisoned in Newgate
- **11 May 1593** — Privy Council orders arrest of authors of anti-foreign libels
- **20 May 1593** — Marlowe presents himself to the Privy Council
- **30 May 1593** — Killed in Deptford; buried 1 June at St Nicholas Churchyard

## Body

### Early Life and Education

Born in Canterbury to shoemaker John Marlowe and Katherine Arthur, Marlowe was the second of nine children. He attended The King's School, Canterbury on scholarship and entered Corpus Christi College, Cambridge at age 16, also via scholarship, with expectations of entering the clergy. However, he abandoned this path, earning his BA in 1584. His mastery of Latin led him to translate Ovid’s *Amores*, though copies were later burned in 1599 for indecency. In 1587, the university hesitated to grant him an MA due to rumors of his intent to join the English seminary at Rheims—an act forbidden under Elizabeth I’s 1585 penal laws. The Privy Council intervened, praising his “faithful dealing” and “good service,” though the nature of that service remains unspecified and has fueled speculation about early state involvement.

### Adult Life and Espionage Allegations

Little concrete information survives about Marlowe’s adult life beyond legal records and literary works. He was variously described as a spy, brawler, heretic, magician, duellist, and rake. Scholars debate whether his alleged atheism and Catholic sympathies were genuine or fabricated to aid covert operations. In 1587, unusually long absences from Cambridge and lavish spending beyond his scholarship income suggest external funding—possibly from government service. Some link him to the tutor “Morley” for Arbella Stuart in 1589, though legal records place him in London during that period. His arrest in Flushing in 1592 for alleged coin counterfeiting may have disrupted a mission to infiltrate Catholic plotters like William Stanley. These incidents support theories of royal-sanctioned espionage, possibly under Sir Francis Walsingham.

### Philosophy and Religious Controversy

Marlowe was reputed to be an atheist—a dangerous stance in Protestant England, where such views were equated with sedition. Accusations intensified amid fears of the “School of Night,” a supposed circle of radical thinkers. After his arrest in 1593, informer Richard Baines recorded eighteen blasphemous statements attributed to Marlowe, including claims that Christ was a bastard and that Scripture contained contradictions. While some historians argue these were fabricated to discredit him, others believe they reflect real subversive beliefs. Thomas Kyd, also imprisoned, corroborated reports of Marlowe’s irreligiosity. Whether authentic or performative, these accusations contributed directly to the charges that led to his final confrontation with the Privy Council.

### Sexuality

Claims of Marlowe’s homosexuality persist, though scholars caution against applying modern identity categories. Baines reported Marlowe saying, “all they that love not Tobacco & Boies were fools,” interpreted as referencing male same-sex relations. Literary evidence includes homoerotic passages in *Hero and Leander* and the sympathetic portrayal of Edward II’s relationship with Piers Gaveston. J.B. Steane found no evidence for Marlowe’s homosexuality, while others note the prevalence of such themes in his writing. Contemporary accounts may reflect posthumous rumor rather than fact, but the literary treatment suggests deliberate engagement with non-heteronormative desire.

### Death and Its Mysteries

On 30 May 1593, Marlowe died in Deptford at the home of widow Eleanor Bull, alongside Ingram Frizer, Nicholas Skeres, and Robert Poley—all connected to the Walsingham intelligence network. A coroner’s inquest, discovered in 1925 by Leslie Hotson, concluded that Marlowe was stabbed above the eye during a dispute over the bill (“the Reckoning”) and that Frizer acted in self-defense. The jury accepted this version, and Frizer was pardoned. However, doubts remain due to the questionable credibility of the witnesses—professional liars and agents—and inconsistencies in the timeline and wound description. Numerous alternative theories propose political assassination, jealousy, or a staged death. Despite Hotson’s confidence in the official account, many scholars continue to question its veracity, leaving Marlowe’s death one of history’s enduring mysteries.

## Terms

- **Blank verse**: Unrhymed iambic pentameter, adopted by Marlowe as a dramatic standard.
- **Privy Council**: Advisory body to the English monarch; involved in matters of state security and espionage.
- **Anglican clergy**: Established church officials; Marlowe was expected to join them but did not.
- **Counterfeiting**: Illegal production of currency; Marlowe was accused in the Netherlands.
- **Atheism**: Denial of divine existence; considered treasonous in Elizabethan England.
- **Agent provocateur**: Undercover operative who infiltrates groups to provoke or expose wrongdoing.
- **Stationers' Register**: Official record of published works; used to control printing and copyright.
- **Foul paper**: Author’s original manuscript draft; referenced in textual analysis of plays.
- **Anti-Catholic laws**: Statutes enacted by Elizabeth I to suppress Catholic practices and loyalty abroad.
- **Rakehell**: A dissolute or immoral person; a label applied to Marlowe in contemporary descriptions.

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars remain divided on several key aspects of Marlowe’s life and death. The authenticity of his alleged atheism and blasphemies is hotly debated—were they genuine beliefs or strategic fabrications to protect his espionage work? The circumstances of his death continue to generate controversy. While the 1925 coroner’s report provides an official narrative, critics point to the unreliable character of the witnesses and anomalies in the physical evidence. Alternative theories range from political assassination by the Privy Council to accidental death during a debt-related altercation. Additionally, questions persist regarding his possible collaboration with Shakespeare, the extent of his involvement in statecraft, and the true authorship of certain plays attributed to him. Given the scarcity of reliable documentation, many of these issues likely will never be definitively resolved.
