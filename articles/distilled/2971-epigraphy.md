# Epigraphy

## Overview
Epigraphy is the study of inscriptions (epigraphs) as physical writing, focusing on identifying, dating, and classifying graphemes (written symbols) within their cultural and historical contexts. It is a primary auxiliary science of history and archaeology, providing direct evidence from literate cultures. The field is distinct from history, which interprets the events recorded in inscriptions, and from palaeography, which studies ancient handwriting in ink.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Earliest known inscriptions appear.
- **6th century BC** — Athenian laws inscribed on wooden tablets; earliest stone law codes like Gortyna.
- **5th century BC** — "Tribute lists" of the Delian League inscribed in Athens.
- **2nd century BCE** — Rosetta Stone created (multilingual stele).
- **1st century BCE** — Augustus's autobiographical record (Monumentum Ancyranum) composed.
- **1825–1877** — *Corpus Inscriptionum Graecarum* published in Berlin.
- **1863** — *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum* begun by Theodor Mommsen et al.
- **19th century** — Jean-François Champollion and Thomas Young decipher Egyptian hieroglyphs using the Rosetta Stone.
- **20th century** — Significant progress in deciphering Maya hieroglyphs.

## Body

### Definition and Scope
Epigraphy is the science of analyzing inscriptions as writing. It excludes the historical significance of a text as a document and its artistic value. An epigraph can range from a single mark to a lengthy document. The media are diverse, including engravings on stone or metal, impressions in wax, and painting on ceramics. Durability is often accidental. The character of the writing is studied separately from the nature of the text itself; for example, public inscriptions differ from private economic records like Mycenaean Linear B tablets.

### History of the Discipline
The science developed steadily from the 16th century, initially focusing on Latin inscriptions. Key figures include Georg Fabricius, Theodor Mommsen, and Louis Robert. Major projects include the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum* (begun 1863), the largest collection of Latin inscriptions, and the *Inscriptiones Graecae*, its modern Greek counterpart. Other corpora exist for Etruscan, Celtic, Iranian, and Mesopotamian inscriptions. The decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs was achieved via the Rosetta Stone, and Maya script has been largely deciphered in modern times.

### Greek Inscriptions
Greek inscriptions are classified by content. They include:
*   **Political and Social:** Public decrees, which follow stereotyped formulae allowing precise dating; treaties and arbitration records; and *proxenia* decrees, appointing citizens to assist foreigners.
*   **Economic:** Detailed public accounts, treasure lists, and building specifications (e.g., for the Parthenon) that reveal state finance and technical processes.
*   **Educational:** Records of the *ephebic* system, which evolved from military training into a cosmopolitan university.
*   **Honorary:** Decrees granting privileges or erecting statues for services to the state.
*   **Artistic:** Signatures of artists on statues and vases, crucial for art history.
*   **Funerary:** The most numerous class, ranging from simple name inscriptions to metrical epitaphs.

### Latin Inscriptions
Latin inscriptions are more standardized in form and content across the Roman Empire. A major challenge is the extensive use of abbreviations. Common materials include stone, marble, bronze, and stamped bricks or tiles. Categories parallel Greek inscriptions but include distinct Roman elements:
*   **Religious:** Dedications, records of priestly colleges, and ritual calendars.
*   **Political:** Laws, senatorial decrees (*senatus consulta*), and imperial edicts (e.g., Diocletian's Price Edict).
*   **Military:** Tombstones, discharge certificates, and altar dedications that collectively reveal imperial recruitment policy.
*   **Honorary:** Inscriptions listing an individual's career (*cursus honorum*) in a fixed order.
*   **Funerary:** Extremely common, often headed *D.M.* (*Dis Manibus*), providing data on population, trades, and longevity.
*   **Infrastructure:** Milestones and boundary stones (*termini*).

### Sanskrit Inscriptions
Sanskrit epigraphy traces the language's use from the 1st century BCE in Brahmi script. A turning point was the mid-2nd century CE inscription of Rudradaman I, which established a poetic eulogy style. Under the Gupta Empire, Sanskrit became the predominant language for royal records. Inscriptions from South India (e.g., Nagarjunakonda) show its use in Buddhist and Shaivite contexts. From the 4th century CE, Sanskrit inscriptions spread to Southeast Asia, where Indic scripts evolved into local scripts like Khmer and Javanese.

## Terms
*   **Epigraphy:** The study of inscriptions as writing.
*   **Grapheme:** A basic unit of a writing system (e.g., a letter or symbol).
*   **Corpus:** A comprehensive collection of inscriptions (e.g., *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum*).
*   **Palaeography:** The study of ancient handwriting, typically in ink.
*   **Proxenos:** A citizen appointed by a foreign state to assist its visitors, akin to a consul.
*   **Cursus honorum:** The sequential order of public offices held by a Roman politician.
*   **Eponymous archon:** The chief magistrate of Athens, whose name was used to date a year.
*   **Prytany:** The period during which a tribe held the presidency in the Athenian council.
*   **Ideography:** The study of inscriptions representing ideas or concepts.
