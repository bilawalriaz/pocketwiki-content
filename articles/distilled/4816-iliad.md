# Iliad

## Overview
The *Iliad* is one of two major ancient Greek epic poems attributed to Homer, composed in dactylic hexameter around the 8th century BC. It is a foundational work of Western literature, depicting events from the final weeks of the Trojan War, focusing on the hero Achilles' wrath and its devastating consequences. The poem explores enduring themes of glory, fate, pride, and the human cost of war, while also providing a detailed, if stylized, view of ancient Greek warfare and society.

## Timeline
- **c. 12th century BC** — The historical backdrop: the Late Bronze Age collapse and the era of the Trojan War.
- **c. 8th century BC** — Scholarly consensus places the composition of the *Iliad* in this period.
- **630 BC** — *Terminus ante quem* for the poem's existence, evidenced by its reflection in art and literature.
- **1489** — The first printed edition (*editio princeps*) is published in Florence.
- **1920s** — Milman Parry launches the oral-formulaic theory of Homeric composition.
- **1951** — Richmond Lattimore publishes a influential English translation.
- **1990** — Robert Fagles publishes a widely read modern English translation.
- **2015** — Caroline Alexander publishes the first full-length English translation by a woman.
- **2023** — Emily Wilson publishes a new English translation in unrhymed iambic pentameter.

## Body

### Synopsis and Narrative
The poem is set during the tenth year of the Trojan War. It begins with the "wrath of Achilles" (μῆνις), sparked when King Agamemnon dishonors him by seizing his war prize, Briseis. Achilles withdraws from battle, leading to catastrophic Achaean losses. The narrative follows the war's tide as it turns against the Greeks, the death of Achilles' companion Patroclus (who fights in Achilles' armor), and Achilles' vengeful return. He kills the Trojan hero Hector and desecrates his body. The epic concludes with King Priam's journey to ransom Hector's corpse, a moment of shared grief and temporary truce. Notably, the poem does not depict the fall of Troy or the Trojan Horse.

### Composition and Authorship
The poem's composition, historicity, and authorship are subjects of the "Homeric Question." It was likely composed in Homeric Greek, a literary mixture of dialects. The oral-formulaic theory, developed by Milman Parry and Albert Lord, argues the poem's formulaic epithets and repetitive structures are artifacts of an oral tradition, where poets composed from memory and improvisation. The earliest complete manuscript is the 10th-century Venetus A.

### Themes and Religion
Critical themes include *kleos* (imperishable glory), *timē* (honor), fate (*kēr*), and pride. The Olympian gods are anthropomorphic and actively intervene in the war, often driven by personal loyalties and quarrels stemming from the Judgment of Paris. While they influence events, they are bound by fate, which determines ultimate outcomes like the deaths of Hector and Achilles. The poem's depiction of warfare includes detailed infantry combat, chariot tactics, and heroic duels, though scholars debate its accuracy for the Bronze Age.

### Cultural Influence and Legacy
The *Iliad* was central to ancient Greek education and culture, performed by rhapsodes at festivals. Its influence permeated the Hellenistic, Roman, and Byzantine worlds. During the Middle Ages, the Trojan War story survived in the West through Latin retellings like the *Ilias Latina*. The Renaissance saw a return to Homer, and the poem remains the cornerstone of the Western literary canon. It has inspired countless adaptations in literature, art, film, and music, from Shakespeare's *Troilus and Cressida* to modern novels and films.

## Terms
- **Dactylic hexameter**: The meter of the epic, consisting of lines with six "feet," each typically a long syllable followed by two short ones.
- **Homeric Greek**: The literary dialect used by Homer, a blend of Ionic and other Greek dialects.
- **Kleos (κλέος)**: Glory or fame, especially the immortal renown won by heroic deeds.
- **Mēnis (μῆνις)**: The "wrath" or "rage" of Achilles, the poem's central theme.
- **Nostos (νόστος)**: The homecoming of a warrior, a theme explored more in the *Odyssey*.
- **Timē (τιμή)**: The honor or respect a warrior earns through his accomplishments.
- **Rhapsode**: A professional performer who recited epic poetry at festivals.
- **Phalanx**: A formation of infantry fighting in close rank, mentioned in the poem but debated as a historical tactic for the Trojan War era.
- **Aspis (ἀσπίς)**: The round shield carried by Greek warriors.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The Homeric Question**: Was the *Iliad* composed by a single author (Homer) or compiled from earlier oral traditions? Did the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* share the same author?
- **Oral vs. Written**: Was the poem composed orally and later written down, or was it a written composition from the start? The oral-formulaic theory strongly supports the former.
- **Historicity**: How much of the poem reflects actual Mycenaean-era history versus later Iron Age (Homer's own time) society and warfare? The Catalogue of Ships, for example, seems to reflect pre-Dorian invasion geography.
- **Fate and Divine Will**: Can the gods in the *Iliad* alter fate, or are they also bound by it? The poem presents Zeus as capable of changing fate but choosing not to.
- **Heroism**: Is Achilles, the protagonist, the true "hero" of the poem, or does Hector, with his loyalty and defense of family, embody a more heroic ideal? The text leaves this open to interpretation.
