# Lubumbashi

## Overview
Lubumbashi is the second-largest city in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) and the capital of Haut-Katanga Province. Founded as Élisabethville in 1910, it is the economic and mining hub of the country's mineral-rich southeast, near the Zambian border. Its history reflects the colonial exploitation of copper, post-independence secessionist conflict, and ongoing regional instability.

## Timeline
- **1910** — Belgian government establishes Élisabethville, named for Queen Elisabeth.
- **1920s** — Major investments in copper mining and rail infrastructure spur rapid growth.
- **1941** — Miners' strike against forced labor is violently suppressed.
- **1957** — City holds first free municipal elections; nationalists win majority.
- **1960** — City becomes capital of secessionist State of Katanga during Congo Crisis.
- **1966** — President Mobutu renames city "Lubumbashi."
- **1997** — Rebel leader Laurent-Désiré Kabila declares himself president from Lubumbashi.
- **1999-2003** — Lubumbashi serves as the DRC's legislative capital.
- **2010** — Major prison break frees 960 inmates, including a militia leader.

## Body

### Colonial Foundation and Mining Economy
The Belgian colonial government founded Élisabethville in 1910 near the Etoile du Congo copper mine. The Comité Spécial du Katanga (CSK), a semi-private concessionary company, held its headquarters there. Huge 1920s investments in mining and rail (e.g., the Elisabethville-Port Francqui line) made Katanga a world copper producer. The city's population grew from ~30,000 in 1930 to 180,000 in 1957. Colonial society was rigidly segregated: the city center was reserved for Europeans, while Congolese lived in designated "indigenous quarters" like Quartier Albert (now Kamalondo). Despite this, the mining economy made Élisabethville the most prosperous region in the Congo, with a relatively high standard of living for black Africans by the late 1950s.

### Post-Independence Conflict and Renaming
Élisabethville was the capital of the secessionist State of Katanga (1960-1963) during the Congo Crisis. After the secession was defeated, the city was renamed Lubumbashi in 1966 by Mobutu Sese Seko. It experienced ethnic violence in the early 1990s and was captured by rebels in 1997. From 1999 to 2003, it temporarily served as the DRC's legislative capital to foster national unity.

### Modern Economy and Infrastructure
Lubumbashi remains the mining capital of the DRC, hosting headquarters for major mining companies. The DRC produces over 3% of the world's copper and half its cobalt, mostly from Katanga. The city is a commercial and industrial center with manufactures including textiles, food products, and copper smelting. It is a transport hub with an international airport and connections via Transafrican Highway 9 and the Cape to Cairo Railway network.

### Demographics and Culture
French is the official language, but Kiswahili (specifically the Kingwana dialect) is the main spoken lingua franca. The population is predominantly Christian. Cultural life includes the Biennale of Lubumbashi art exhibition, the National Museum, and a popular zoo. Music is vibrant, often mixing languages like Swahili, Lingala, and French. Football is the most popular sport, with TP Mazembe being a highly successful continental club.

## Terms
- **Comité Spécial du Katanga (CSK):** A semi-private Belgian concessionary company with major land and mining privileges in Katanga.
- **Centre extra-coutumier:** A colonial administrative experiment (from 1933) for limited indigenous self-governance, which ultimately failed.
- **Cité indigène:** A segregated residential area for Congolese under Belgian rule, separated from the European city center.
- **Kingwana:** A dialect of Kiswahili spoken in eastern DRC, including Lubumbashi.
- **Transafrican Highway 9 (TAH 9):** A major road connecting Lubumbashi to Lusaka and Kolwezi.
- **Biennale of Lubumbashi:** A biennial contemporary art exhibition showcasing regional artists.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes a scholarly debate on the relative strength of contemporary art in Lubumbashi versus Kinshasa. Art historian Bogumił Jewsiewicki argues that Lubumbashi's popular art scene is weaker and has not produced an internationally renowned figure like Kinshasa's Chéri Samba.
