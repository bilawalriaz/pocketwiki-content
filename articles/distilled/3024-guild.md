# Guild

## Overview
A guild is a professional association of artisans or merchants regulating a craft or trade within a territory, granting members exclusive rights. Originating in ancient civilizations, guilds became extensive in medieval Europe, controlling production, quality, and training, influencing economic and civic life. Their legacy persists in modern professional associations and licensing systems echoing guild structures of exclusivity and self-regulation.

## Timeline
- **c. 2254–2218 BC** — Naram-Sin of Akkad promulgates common Mesopotamian standards used by artisan guilds.
- **c. 1755–1750 BC** — Code of Hammurabi includes laws regulating wages for shipbuilders and shipmasters.
- **Ancient Rome** — Collegia (guilds) like the corpus naviculariorum operate but decline with the Roman Empire.
- **High Middle Ages** — Merchant and craft guilds proliferate across Europe, regulating trade and urban governance.
- **13th century** — Women participate as full masters in select guilds in cities like Rouen.
- **17th–18th centuries** — Exclusively female guilds proliferate in Paris, Rouen, and Cologne.
- **1791** — French Revolution abolishes guilds in France.
- **19th century** — Most European guilds dissolve due to industrialization and Enlightenment critiques.
- **1878** — City and Guilds of London Institute established to advance technical education.
- **1927** — Pharmacy Guild of Australia founded.
- **1998** — Thomas Malone proposes modern guild structures for independent workers in the information economy.
- **2005** — U.S. Department of Justice files antitrust lawsuit against National Association of Realtors for guild-like practices.

## Body

### Early History and Medieval Role
Guilds trace to ancient Mesopotamia, where standards were set for artisans, and to Roman collegia, voluntary merchant associations. These early forms collapsed with the Roman Empire. In the High Middle Ages, guilds re-emerged in European cities, evolving from religious fraternities. They regulated trade, ensured product quality, and protected members' economic interests through monopolies on local production. Guilds also had social and political roles, influencing municipal governments, maintaining welfare funds, and reinforcing communal life. Their authority derived from charters granting legal privileges, shaping urban economies around controlled hierarchies.

### Women's Participation
Women's involvement in medieval guilds was diverse but constrained. Most guilds were male-dominated, often allowing women entry only through marriage or as widows. However, evidence shows women engaged widely: London silkwomen could inherit property, and Parisian guilds like surgeons and glass-blowers were open to them. In Rouen, women held full master status in some guilds. Historians debate whether participation declined in the early modern period. Alice Clark's "decline thesis" argues women became marginalized, but later scholars like Clare Crowston note women remained active and formed independent guilds. Regional contrasts exist, with declines documented in parts of Germany.

### Fall of the Guilds
Enlightenment thinkers like Adam Smith criticized guild monopolies for inhibiting free trade and innovation. As nation-states expanded, new patent systems weakened guild control. The French Revolution abolished guilds in 1791, and industrialization made them less viable across Europe by the 19th century. Historians debate their economic impact: some view them as monopolistic and rent-seeking, while others argue they facilitated training and quality control.

### Guilds Outside Europe
Similar organizations developed globally: śreṇi in ancient India, Akhiya fraternities in the Ottoman Empire, gongsuo in late-imperial China, za and kabunakama in Japan, pochteca in the Aztec Empire, and Igun in Benin Kingdom. These varied in form but shared roles in regulating crafts and trade.

### Modern Guilds and Quasi-Guilds
Professional associations today replicate guild structures, requiring apprenticeships and certifications for fields like law, medicine, and engineering. Licensing and accreditation practices, often resulting from lobbying, act as modern "guild-privileges," limiting entry and enforcing norms. Critics argue these practices protect public good but can also deter outsiders, enhancing professionals' prestige and earnings. The university system, originating as scholars' guilds, persists through accreditation linked to federal aid, potentially limiting competition. In the information economy, proposals for guild-like structures aim to protect independent workers, while patents serve as guild privileges restricting newcomers.

### International Examples
In Europe, guilds survive as livery companies in London, ceremonial and charitable organizations with regulatory roles. In Germany, Innungen exist as voluntary trade associations, and Handwerkskammern provide regional craft governance. In India, professional guilds include the Indian Medical Association. In North America, guilds appear as labor unions (e.g., The Newspaper Guild) and in industries like film (e.g., Screen Actors Guild), real estate (e.g., National Association of Realtors), law (state bar associations), and medicine (e.g., American Medical Association). Australia has guilds like the Pharmacy Guild, which sets standards for pharmacists.

## Terms
- **Guild**: A professional association of artisans or merchants regulating a craft or trade, often granting exclusive rights within a territory.
- **Collegium**: A type of guild in Ancient Rome, any association or corporation acting as a legal entity, often voluntary merchant groups.
- **Apprenticeship**: A formal system of training where learners work under skilled masters to gain expertise in a trade.
- **Journeyman**: A skilled worker who has completed apprenticeship but is not yet a master, often employed by masters.
- **Master**: A fully qualified guild member who can train apprentices and operate independently.
- **Letters Patent**: Official documents granting legal privileges, such as monopolies, to guilds.
- **Quasi-guild**: Modern organizations, like professional associations, that emulate guild structures through licensing and accreditation.
- **Accreditation**: A process of evaluation and approval, often by peer institutions, that grants legitimacy and access to benefits like federal aid.
- **Livery Company**: A surviving form of medieval guild in London, with ceremonial, charitable, and sometimes regulatory roles.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Economic Impact**: Historians debate whether guilds were monopolistic and rent-seeking or beneficial for training, quality control, and technological adaptation.
- **Women's Participation**: Scholars disagree on whether women's roles in guilds declined in the early modern period, with regional variations complicating the narrative.
- **Modern Licensing**: There is debate over whether contemporary licensing and accreditation practices serve public good or primarily protect professionals by limiting competition, akin to historical guild privileges.
- **Patents and Innovation**: Questions arise about whether patents, as modern guild privileges, hinder innovation, especially in fields like agriculture where public investment may underpin breakthroughs.
