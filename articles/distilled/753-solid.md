# Solid

## Overview
A solid is a fundamental state of matter characterized by structural rigidity and resistance to external forces, where constituent atoms or molecules are closely packed with minimal kinetic energy. Solids form when matter cools below its melting point (or sublimation point), adopting either ordered crystalline lattices or disordered amorphous structures. The macroscopic properties of a solid—mechanical, thermal, electrical, and optical—are dictated by its microscopic bonding type (ionic, covalent, metallic, or van der Waals) and structural hierarchy. Understanding solids underpins solid-state physics, materials science, and engineering, enabling technologies from construction and metallurgy to semiconductors and nanotechnology.

## Body

### Definition and General Behavior
Solids resist compression and shape alteration due to tightly bound atoms possessing the least kinetic energy per particle of any phase. They do not flow like liquids or expand like gases. Heating increases atomic vibration and volume (thermal expansion), though less than in fluids. Phase transitions occur at the melting point (solid to liquid) or sublimation point (solid to gas); the pathway depends on whether ambient pressure exceeds the substance's triple point pressure. Most solids are denser than their liquid phases, but water, gallium, and plutonium are exceptions. Non-porous solids strongly resist compression due to electron cloud repulsion; upon pressure release, they rapidly re-expand, storing energy that can fracture rigid containers (e.g., freezing water in steel).

### Microscopic Structure: Crystalline vs. Amorphous
Solids are classified by atomic arrangement. **Crystalline solids** (metals, ice, salt) feature atoms in regular, repeating geometric lattices (e.g., body-centered cubic) that can extend over large scales (single crystals like diamond) or exist as **polycrystalline** aggregates of many crystallites (most metals, ceramics). **Amorphous solids** (glass, polystyrene) lack long-range order. Formation conditions dictate structure: slow cooling favors crystallization; rapid freezing favors amorphous states. Single-element crystalline phases are **allotropes** (e.g., diamond/graphite); multi-atom molecular phases are **polymorphs** (e.g., calcite/aragonite). Many macroscopic objects are **composites** of distinct substances (e.g., rock as mineral aggregate, wood as cellulose fibers in lignin matrix).

### Classes of Solids by Bonding
**Metals** (left of boron–polonium line) exhibit metallic bonding: a lattice of positive ions in a delocalized electron "sea," conferring high electrical/thermal conductivity, opacity, luster, ductility, and strength. **Alloys** (e.g., steel, ≤2.1% carbon in iron) enhance properties. **Minerals** are naturally occurring crystalline solids (mostly silicates) forming Earth's crust. **Ceramics** (inorganic oxides, nitrides, carbides) are chemically inert, high-temperature resistant (1,000–1,600 °C), hard, strong in compression, but brittle (low fracture toughness *K*<sub>Ic</sub>). Advanced ceramics (silicon carbide, transparent alumina) enable cutting tools, armor, and lasers. **Glass-ceramics** are formed as glass then partially crystallized (~70% crystalline) to achieve near-zero thermal expansion and high thermal shock resistance (up to 1,000 °C). **Organic solids** (wood, polymers) rely on carbon–hydrogen chemistry. **Polymers** form via polymerization of monomers; synthetic plastics (polyethylene, nylons) and biopolymers (cellulose, proteins) exhibit tunable properties via functional groups. **Composite materials** combine a matrix (e.g., polymer, ceramic) with a dispersed phase (fibers, particles) for synergistic properties (e.g., steel-reinforced concrete, RCC for Space Shuttle leading edges). **Semiconductors** (Si, Ge, GaAs) have resistivity between metals and insulators; current flows via electrons and holes, enabling transistors, solar cells, and integrated circuits. **Nanomaterials** (<100 nm) show size-dependent properties: gold nanoparticles melt at ~300 °C (vs. 1,064 °C bulk), exhibit red color, and provide high surface area for catalysis, batteries, and solar cells. **Biomaterials** (bone, nacre) inspire synthetic designs via structural hierarchy, multifunctionality, self-healing, and self-assembly.

### Physical Properties
**Mechanical:** Solids deform under stress; strain is deformation relative to original size. **Elasticity** (reversible, Hooke’s law, Young’s modulus), **viscoelasticity** (time-dependent, hysteresis), and **plasticity** (irreversible beyond yield stress) describe response modes. Key metrics: tensile/compressive/shear strength, fracture toughness, ductility, hardness. Refractory materials retain strength at high temperatures. **Thermal:** Atomic lattice vibrations (phonons) govern thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity. **Electrical:** Conductors (metals), insulators (ceramics, glasses), and semiconductors differ by charge carriers (electrons, holes, ions). **Superconductors** (Sn, Al, doped semiconductors, ceramics) exhibit zero resistivity below a critical temperature. **Dielectrics** (plastics) concentrate electric fields for capacitors. **Electro-mechanical:** **Piezoelectricity** (voltage from stress, reversible) occurs in crystals (quartz) and polymers (PVDF), enabling sensors and actuators. **Optical:** Materials transmit (glass) or reflect (metals) light; wavelength-selective transmission enables filters. Trade-offs exist between optical bandpass and mechanical durability (e.g., sapphire missile domes weaken >600 °C). **Opto-electronic:** Solar cells convert light to electricity via photo-generation and separation of charge carriers (photovoltaic effect), used in remote power, satellites, and grid-tied arrays.

### Fields of Study
**Solid-state physics** (major branch of condensed matter physics) investigates fundamental properties. **Solid-state chemistry** focuses on synthesis, structure, and reactions. **Materials science** bridges composition, processing, structure, and properties for engineering applications.

## Terms
- ****Melting point**** — Intrinsic temperature at which a solid transitions to a liquid at a given pressure.
- ****Sublimation point**** — Temperature at which a solid transitions directly to a gas (occurs when pressure is below triple point pressure).
- ****Crystalline solid**** — Solid with atoms arranged in a regular, repeating geometric lattice (e.g., metals, salt).
- ****Amorphous solid**** — Solid lacking long-range atomic order (e.g., glass, polystyrene).
- ****Polycrystalline**** — Material composed of many small crystallites (grains) rather than a single crystal.
- ****Allotrope**** — Different structural forms of the same element in the solid state (e.g., diamond and graphite for carbon).
- ****Polymorph**** — Different crystal structures of the same molecular compound (e.g., calcite and aragonite for CaCO₃).
- ****Metallic bonding**** — Electrostatic attraction between a lattice of positive ions and a delocalized "sea" of valence electrons.
- ****Fracture toughness (K<sub>Ic</sub>)**** — Ability of a material with microstructural flaws to resist crack propagation; low in brittle ceramics.
- ****Piezoelectricity**** — Generation of voltage in response to mechanical stress (and reverse shape change from voltage) in certain crystals/polymers.

## Debates and open questions
- **Ceramic engine feasibility:** Manufacturing ceramic parts with sufficient precision and durability for gas turbine engines remains difficult and costly due to microscopic flaws causing crack proliferation during sintering.
- **Infrared dome trade-off:** A persistent conflict exists between optical bandpass (transmission range) and mechanical durability/thermal stability in missile dome materials (e.g., sapphire weakens >600 °C); transparent ceramics and nanocomposites are potential solutions.
- **Nanomaterial property scaling:** Traditional solids exhibit altered properties (melting point, color, strength) at nanoscale; the full implications for energy storage (batteries, supercapacitors), catalysis, and photovoltaics are under active development.
- **Biomimetic synthesis:** Translating biological principles of structural hierarchy, self-healing, and self-assembly into scalable chemical synthesis of high-performance synthetic materials is an emerging strategy.
