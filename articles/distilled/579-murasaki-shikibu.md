# Murasaki Shikibu

## Overview
Murasaki Shikibu (c. 973 – c. 1014/1025) was a Japanese novelist, poet, and lady-in-waiting during the Heian period, best known as the author of *The Tale of Genji* (c. 1000–1012), widely considered one of the world’s first novels. Born into a middling branch of the dominant northern Fujiwara clan, she received an exceptional education in Chinese classics—normally reserved for men—under her scholar-father, Fujiwara no Tametoki. After a brief marriage to Fujiwara no Nobutaka (c. 998–1001) and the birth of a daughter, she was widowed and likely began writing *Genji* in grief and solitude. Her literary reputation drew the attention of Fujiwara no Michinaga, the de facto ruler, who summoned her c. 1005 to serve Empress Shōshi, his daughter, as a companion-tutor. At court, Murasaki taught Shōshi Chinese literature in secret, completed *Genji* using court life as material, and kept a diary revealing her alienation from court rituals and rivalry with fellow writer Sei Shōnagon. She retired with Shōshi to the Lake Biwa region after Emperor Ichijō’s death in 1011. Her work, written in *kana* (Japanese syllabary) rather than Chinese, cemented Japanese as a literary language and remains a foundational text of world literature.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Murasaki was born c. 973 into the northern Fujiwara clan, which dominated court politics through imperial marriages and regencies. Her branch had fallen to middle ranks—provincial governors—yet retained literary prestige: her great-grandfather Fujiwara no Kanesuke and grandfather were renowned poets allied with Ki no Tsurayuki, a key figure in promoting Japanese verse. Her father, Fujiwara no Tametoki, was a respected Chinese scholar and State Academy graduate. Unusually, Murasaki was raised in her father’s household (rather than her mother’s) after her mother’s early death, alongside her brother Nobunori. While Nobunori studied Chinese for a government career, Murasaki listened and mastered the classics, prompting her father’s lament: “What a pity she was not born a man!” She also learned music, calligraphy, and Japanese poetry. This education was transgressive: Heian women were excluded from Chinese, the language of government, and expected to use *kana*, the new Japanese syllabary. Murasaki’s fluency in both positioned her uniquely to bridge the masculine world of Chinese learning and the feminine world of vernacular literature.

### Marriage and Widowhood
Heian aristocratic women lived in seclusion, marrying at puberty and remaining in their natal homes while husbands visited. Murasaki defied convention by staying unmarried until her mid-to-late twenties. In 996 she traveled with her father to Echizen Province—a rare journey for a noblewoman—returning c. 998 to marry Fujiwara no Nobutaka, an older second cousin with multiple households and a reputation for extravagance. Their daughter Kenshi was born in 999; Nobutaka died of cholera in 1001. As a widow with servants, Murasaki had leisure to read *monogatari* (tales) like *The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter* and *The Tales of Ise*. Scholars debate whether she began *Genji* before or after his death; her diary records profound grief: “The thought of my continuing loneliness was quite unbearable.” A later legend claims she retreated to Ishiyama-dera at Lake Biwa and conceived *Genji* while moon-gazing, though historians dismiss this as fiction. She circulated chapters privately, gaining a reputation that reached Michinaga.

### Court Life and Rival Salons
Heian court culture peaked in the early 11th century. Kyoto’s population reached ~100,000, but nobles were isolated in the palace, preoccupied with aesthetic minutiae—poetry, textiles, fragrance, color combinations. Women expressed emotion through art; those lacking taste lost standing. Michinaga consolidated power after 995 by marrying four daughters to emperors. In 1000 he placed his daughter Shōshi (c. 12) in Emperor Ichijō’s harem, later elevating her to Empress (chūgū) alongside the existing Empress Teishi (kōgō)—an unprecedented dual empress system. To build a rival salon to Teishi’s (served by Sei Shōnagon, author of *The Pillow Book*), Michinaga recruited talented women for Shōshi, including Izumi Shikibu, Akazome Emon, and Murasaki (c. 1005–1006).

Murasaki’s diary reveals sharp rivalry. She disparaged Izumi Shikibu’s poetry as derivative and Sei Shōnagon as “dreadfully conceited” for flaunting Chinese characters. Shōnagon’s *Pillow Book* may have been propaganda for Teishi’s court; scholars suggest Michinaga commissioned Murasaki to write *Genji* as a counter-work showcasing Shōshi’s salon. The two writers differed temperamentally: Shōnagon was witty and outspoken; Murasaki was withdrawn, sensitive, and hid her Chinese learning, calling it “pretentious.” Murasaki earned the nickname “The Lady of the Chronicles” (Nihongi no tsubone) after a courtier praised her learning during a reading of *Genji*; though meant mockingly, she was privately flattered. She taught Shōshi Chinese ballads by Bai Juyi in secret—“almost subversive” in a culture that barred women from Chinese—writing in her diary of “reading with Her Majesty … very secretly, in odd moments.” Murasaki disliked court revelry and the “drunken, stupid” men, though she endured Michinaga’s persistent advances (he once stole a *Genji* chapter). His patronage supplied paper, ink, and calligraphers essential for her manuscript.

### Later Life, Death, and Works
When Emperor Ichijō died in 1011, Shōshi retired to a Fujiwara mansion in Biwa; Murasaki accompanied her, recorded there in 1013. Her father returned from Echigo in 1014, possibly for her funeral; most scholars accept 1014 as her death date (age 41), though Bowring and Waley argue she may have lived until 1025, attending ceremonies for Shōshi’s son, Emperor Go-Ichijō. Her brother died c. 1011; her father became a monk at Miidera, dying in 1029. Her daughter entered court in 1025 as wet nurse to future Emperor Go-Reizei, becoming the celebrated poet Daini no Sanmi.

Three works survive: *The Tale of Genji* (54 chapters, ~1100 pages), *The Diary of Lady Murasaki* (c. 1008–1010), and *Poetic Memoirs* (128 poems, published 1206 by Fujiwara no Teika). *Genji*, written in *kana*, is the supreme achievement of Heian prose. It blends *waka* diary traditions, earlier *monogatari*, Chinese histories, and narrative poetry into a distinctly modern novel form. Set in the late 9th–early 10th centuries, it strips fantasy from the genre, focusing on the “shining prince” Genji’s loves and the central Heian theme *mono no aware*—the pathos of impermanence, invoked over a thousand times. Keene calls it “the supreme work of Japanese fiction”; McCullough finds it transcends genre and age, universalizing Heian court romance. By 1021 it was complete and sought in the provinces; Emperor Ichijō had it read aloud despite being in Japanese.

### Legacy
Murasaki’s influence is unbroken. By the 12th century her work was required reading for court poets; the *Genji Monogatari Emaki* (c. 1110–1120) is the earliest surviving illustrated scroll. In the 17th century, *Genji* became emblematic of Confucian female virtue; *genji-e* (Genji-themed art) adorned dowry sets and wedding gifts for nobles and shoguns. Ukiyo-e masters (Hiroshige, Kiyonaga, Utamaro) popularized it for samurai and commoners. Murasaki herself became a cultural icon, depicted at Ishiyama Temple gazing at the moon. Modern media—films, manga, comics—continually adapt *Genji*. The 2000 yen note (2000) features her and *Genji*; Kyoto celebrated the millennium in 2008. Waley’s 1925–1933 translation drew comparisons to Austen, Proust, and Shakespeare. Mulhern likens Murasaki to Shakespeare: both defined their eras (Heian court, Elizabethan England) and generated endless criticism. *Genji* remains, in Shirane’s words, “unmatched by any other Japanese text or artifact” in its thousand-year multimedia afterlife.

## Terms
- **Heian period** (794–1185): Classical Japanese era centered on the imperial court in Heian-kyō (Kyoto), noted for aristocratic culture and vernacular literature.
- **Fujiwara clan**: Aristocratic family that dominated politics through regencies (sesshō, kampaku) and imperial marriages from the 9th–11th centuries.
- **kana**: Japanese syllabary developed c. 9th century from abbreviated Chinese characters, enabling writing in the vernacular; used by women for literature.
- **monogatari**: “Tale” or narrative fiction genre; early examples include *The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter*; *Genji* elevated it to psychological realism.
- **nyōbō**: Lady-in-waiting; court woman serving an empress or high-ranking consort, often a literary salon participant.
- **mono no aware**: “The pathos of things”; aesthetic sensitivity to impermanence and transience, central theme of *Genji*.
- **chūgū / kōgō**: Titles for concurrent empresses created by Michinaga: *chūgū* (“Inner Palatine”) for Shōshi, *kōgō* (“Lustrous Heir-bearer”) for Teishi.
- **Genji Monogatari Emaki**: 12th-century illustrated handscroll of *Genji* (4 scrolls, 19 paintings, 20 calligraphy sheets), earliest surviving *Genji* art.
- **genji-e**: Artworks depicting scenes from *Genji* or Murasaki; used on dowry items, screens, prints; symbolized cultural status and marital virtue.
- **Poetic Memoirs**: Murasaki’s collection of 128 poems, arranged biographically, preserved in a 1206 edition by Fujiwara no Teika.

## Debates and open questions
- **Personal name**: Unknown; possibly Fujiwara no Kaoruko (Kyōshi), mentioned in a 1007 diary, but unconfirmed.
- **Date of death**: Most scholars accept 1014 (father’s sudden return), but Bowring and Waley argue for 1025 based on possible attendance at later court ceremonies.
- **Marriage dynamics**: Richard Bowring sees harmony; Haruo Shirane detects resentment in her poems.
- **Michinaga relationship**: Waley asserts a romance; Bowring denies she was a concubine but acknowledges pressure and flirtation (diary records him stealing a *Genji* chapter).
- **Ishiyama-dera legend**: The moon-gazing inspiration story is widely dismissed as later fiction, though the temple displays a “Genji room” and relics.
- **Genji’s composition timeline**: Uncertain whether earliest chapters predate widowhood; likely written over a decade (c. 1000–1012) across marriage, widowhood, and court service.
- **Rivalry with Sei Shōnagon**: They never served concurrently (Teishi died 1001), but Murasaki’s diary criticism may reflect salon politics or genuine literary disagreement.
- **Chinese language stance**: Murasaki hid her fluency at Shōshi’s court (hostile to women’s Chinese study) yet used it secretly to teach Shōshi; scholars debate whether this was subversion, pragmatism, or internalized misogyny.
