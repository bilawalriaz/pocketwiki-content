# H. P. Lovecraft

## Overview
Howard Phillips Lovecraft (1890–1937) was an American writer of weird, horror, fantasy, and science fiction whose work, largely published in pulp magazines during his lifetime, gained posthumous recognition as foundational to 20th-century supernatural horror. His fiction articulates **cosmicism**—the philosophy that humanity is an insignificant, accidental speck in a vast, indifferent, mechanistic universe—often through the **Cthulhu Mythos**, a shared fictional universe of alien deities, forbidden texts (such as the *Necronomicon*), and decaying New England settings ("Lovecraft Country"). Lovecraft’s life was marked by family tragedy (his father and mother were institutionalized), financial decline, chronic illness, and a voluminous correspondence that sustained the "Lovecraft Circle" of writers. His legacy is complicated by virulent racist and antisemitic views, which he partially moderated late in life but never fully renounced, and which remain central to modern scholarly debate.

## Body

### Early Life, Family Tragedy, and Formative Influences
Lovecraft was born into a Providence family of English descent with substantial means derived from his maternal grandfather, Whipple Van Buren Phillips. In 1893, his father suffered a psychotic episode and was committed to Butler Hospital, dying five years later of general paresis (late-stage syphilis), a fact Lovecraft may have been shielded from or later obscured. Raised by his mother, aunts, and grandparents, Lovecraft was a precocious, sickly child. Whipple became his intellectual center, encouraging classical literature and inventing original "weird tales" of "winged horrors" that seeded Lovecraft’s imagination. Early exposure to Gustave Doré’s illustrations for *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* triggered recurring nightmares of "night-gaunts," which appeared in his fiction decades later. By age eight, astronomy and chemistry captivated him; he self-published scientific journals via hectograph. The 1896 death of his grandmother plunged the household into mourning that "never fully recovered," and the 1904 death of Whipple—followed by the collapse of the family fortune—forced a move to a duplex and triggered suicidal ideation, averted only by scientific curiosity.

### Education, Breakdown, and Amateur Journalism
Lovecraft attended high school intermittently, excelling in chemistry and physics but suffering "near breakdowns." In 1908, a severe "nervous collapse" (attributed by later scholars to possible chorea minor or hysteroid seizure/atypical depression) ended his formal education; he never graduated or attended Brown University as planned. Isolated with his mother, he wrote poetry—some racist, unpublished—and in 1913 sparked a year-long feud in *Argosy*’s letters column criticizing writer Fred Jackson. This drew the attention of Edward F. Daas, who recruited him into the United Amateur Press Association (UAPA) in 1914. Lovecraft immersed himself in amateur journalism, advocating archaic British English, Anglophilia, and amateurism over commercialism. He served as UAPA vice-president (1915) and president (1917), using his platform for xenophobic critiques of immigration and pro-Allied war advocacy. Encouraged by W. Paul Cook, he turned to prose, publishing "The Alchemist," "The Tomb" (Poe-influenced), and "Dagon" (first story with mature Mythos themes).

### Mother’s Death, Dream Cycle, and Mythos Emergence
Lovecraft’s mother suffered a nervous breakdown in 1918–1919, describing hallucinations of "weird and fantastic creatures"; she was committed to Butler Hospital in March 1919 and died in May 1921. Lovecraft’s initial grief gave way to relief at newfound independence and improved health. In 1919, discovering Lord Dunsany shifted his style toward fantasy: the "Dream Cycle" ("The White Ship," "The Doom That Came to Sarnath," "The Cats of Ulthar," "Celephaïs") emerged. By late 1920, the Cthulhu Mythos coalesced with "Nyarlathotep," "The Crawling Chaos," and definitively "The Nameless City" (1921), introducing the couplet "That is not dead which can eternal lie / And with strange aeons even death may die." "The Outsider" (1921) became a heavily analyzed, multiply interpretable story. At a 1921 amateur convention, he met Sonia Greene, whom he married in 1924.

### New York Marriage, Crisis, and Return to Providence
Greene, a widow seven years his senior, relocated Lovecraft to Brooklyn. He joined the "Kalem Club" of intellectual friends who urged submissions to *Weird Tales*. Editor Edwin Baird accepted many stories, including the Houdini-ghostwritten "Under the Pyramids." However, Greene’s business failed, assets vanished in a bank collapse, and Lovecraft—lacking marketable skills—could not support them. He declined the *Weird Tales* editorship (relocating to Chicago). New editor Farnsworth Wright rejected his work. Greene’s traveling work separated them; Lovecraft’s Red Hook apartment was burgled. In 1925–26, he wrote "The Horror at Red Hook" and "He," the latter explicitly declaring New York a "mistake" of "horror and oppression." He outlined "The Call of Cthulhu" and wrote the seminal essay "Supernatural Horror in Literature." Emaciated and despairing, he returned to Providence in April 1926.

### Final Providence Period: Masterworks, Correspondence, and Decline
Back in Providence (10 Barnes St., later 66 Prospect St.), Lovecraft produced his most celebrated works: *The Dream-Quest of Unknown Kadath* (repudiating Dunsany), *The Case of Charles Dexter Ward*, "The Call of Cthulhu," *At the Mountains of Madness*, *The Shadow over Innsmouth*, and *The Shadow Out of Time*. He engaged in extensive revision and ghostwriting for peers (including Houdini’s *The Cancer of Superstition*, halted by Houdini’s 1926 death). His correspondence exploded: the "Lovecraft Circle" (Clark Ashton Smith, August Derleth, Robert E. Howard, Frank Belknap Long, etc.) shared motifs, settings, and fictional entities with his encouragement. Howard’s 1936 suicide devastated him. Lovecraft’s politics shifted toward a non-Marxist, elitist socialism after the Depression; he supported FDR but deemed the New Deal insufficient. He never supported himself by writing, often abandoning stories after one rejection; *The Shadow over Innsmouth* (1936) was his only book publication in life (400 copies, error-ridden, ~200 bound). Diagnosed with terminal intestinal cancer in February 1937, he kept a scientific diary of his decline until death on March 15.

### Personal Views: Politics, Atheism, and Race
**Politics:** Early "Tory" conservatism, Anglophilia, anti-democracy, and pro-aristocracy gave way in the 1930s to a socialism outlined in "Some Repetitions on the Times" (1933): state resource control, shorter hours, higher wages, unemployment insurance, and an "aristocracy of intellectuals" (he used "fascism" descriptively for this). He supported FDR, initially praised Hitler as a cultural preserver (criticizing only racial *descent* vs. *culture* basis), but cooled after his neighbor reported anti-Jewish violence in Germany.

**Atheism:** In "A Confession of Unfaith" (1922), he traced a path from Protestant upbringing → brief Greco-Roman paganism (adopting the name "Abdul Alhazred") → 1902 astronomical revelation of cosmic insignificance → confirmed atheism and cosmic pessimism, influenced by Nietzsche and Mencken.

**Race:** The most controversial aspect. Lovecraft was a white supremacist who esteemed only English/English-descent people. Early works advocated a "strong color line"; fiction allegorized miscegenation (e.g., Deep Ones in *The Shadow over Innsmouth*). He married Greene, a Jewish woman he considered "well assimilated." By the 1930s, views moderated toward cultural preservationism ("make the Germans more German..."), influenced by travel (positive remarks on Québécois/First Nations cultures), but prejudices persisted.

### Literary Influences
Grandfather’s Gothic tales; childhood reading: *One Thousand and One Nights*, Hawthorne, Mandeville, Polo (travelogues influenced settings/gaps in science). **Edgar Allan Poe**: "God of Fiction," unity of effect, direct quotes in *At the Mountains of Madness*. **Lord Dunsany** (1919): greatest career impact; spawned Dream Cycle; later repudiated as unnatural style. **Arthur Machen, Algernon Blackwood**: 1920s influences. **Decadents, Puritans, Aesthetic Movement** (per Barton Levi St. Armand): fused into a "New England Decadent" identity, polarizing artificial paradise vs. oneiric visions. **Science**: Amateur astronomy (Ladd Observatory), biology, geology, physics → materialist cosmicism. Non-Euclidean geometry and Einsteinian relativity used to render horror scientifically plausible ("The Colour Out of Space" as peak). **Dreams**: Not transcribed but structurally influential; *The Dream-Quest of Unknown Kadath* and "The Silver Key" deconstruct dream/reality boundaries, compared to Jungian archetypes.

### Core Themes
**Cosmicism**: Humanity is insignificant in a meaningless, mechanical universe. Forces are indifferent, not malevolent. Articulated 1921, fully fictionalized by 1926 ("The Call of Cthulhu"). Protagonists *confirm* known insignificance rather than *discover* it.

**Forbidden Knowledge**: Happiness requires ignorance. Seeking hidden truth (self-knowledge, family history, cosmic reality) causes madness/destruction (*The Case of Charles Dexter Ward*, *The Shadow Out of Time*). Horror is internalized psychological impact.

**Civilizational Decline**: Fixation on Western terminal decline. Oswald Spengler’s *Decline of the West* (1920s) provided framework: politics, economics, science, art as interdependent cyclical decay. Central to *At the Mountains of Madness*.

**Science as Horror Vehicle**: Merged supernatural horror with contemporary science. Einsteinian physics, non-Euclidean geometry, biology used to explain monsters scientifically ("The Dreams in the Witch House," *The Shadow Out of Time*). Mathematics invokes "otherness" (Tom Hull).

**Lovecraft Country**: Fictionalized New England (Arkham≈Oakham, Innsmouth≈Newburyport, Dunwich≈Greenwich). Names/geography altered for mood/realism; locations variable per story needs. Quabbin Reservoir creation mirrored in "The Colour Out of Space."

## Terms
- ****Cosmicism**** — Lovecraft’s literary philosophy: humanity is an insignificant, accidental speck in a vast, indifferent, mechanistic universe; no supernatural meaning exists.
- ****Cthulhu Mythos**** — Posthumous term for Lovecraft’s interconnected stories sharing alien deities (Great Old Ones/Outer Gods), forbidden texts (*Necronomicon*), and New England settings; Lovecraft called it "Yog-Sothothery."
- ****Necronomicon**** — Fictional grimoire by the "mad Arab" Abdul Alhazred, containing forbidden lore on the Mythos entities; recurring plot device.
- ****Lovecraft Country**** — Fictionalized New England region (Arkham, Innsmouth, Dunwich, Kingsport, Miskatonic University) serving as primary Mythos setting; based on real Massachusetts towns.
- ****Dream Cycle**** — Early series (1919–1921+) of Dunsany-influenced fantasies set in a shared dreamworld accessible to sensitive dreamers (e.g., Randolph Carter).
- ****Lovecraft Circle**** — Network of correspondents (Clark Ashton Smith, August Derleth, Robert E. Howard, Frank Belknap Long, etc.) who shared fictional elements with Lovecraft’s encouragement.
- ****Forbidden Knowledge**** — Theme: seeking hidden truths (cosmic, genealogical, self) leads to madness/destruction; happiness lies in ignorance.
- ****Night-gaunt**** — Faceless, winged nightmare beings from Lovecraft’s childhood (triggered by Doré illustrations); later incorporated into Dream Cycle fiction.
- ****Cosmic Indifferentism**** — Lovecraft’s self-description: the universe is not hostile but utterly indifferent to humanity; horror stems from this indifference.
- ****Arkham House**** — Publisher founded 1939 by August Derleth and Donald Wandrei to preserve Lovecraft’s works; controlled copyright claims for decades.

## Debates and open questions
- **Copyright status**: No definitive evidence of renewed copyrights; most scholars (S. T. Joshi, Chris J. Karr) consider works public domain, but a reconstituted literary estate (1998) asserts ownership and licenses publications. Joshi withdrew his public-domain conclusion in later years.
- **August Derleth’s editorial legacy**: Derleth imposed a moralistic cosmology (Good Elder Gods vs. Evil Outer Gods, elemental associations) contrary to Lovecraft’s amoral indifference; his Arkham House control shaped reception until 1970s scholarly correction.
- **Racism vs. literary merit**: Central critical tension. Early critics (Edmund Wilson) dismissed work as "bad taste"; later scholars (Joshi, Dirda, King) affirm mastery of atmosphere and influence. Modern debate centers on whether racist content is separable from cosmic themes, and how to contextualize the World Fantasy Award’s 2015 trophy change.
- **Nature of 1908 breakdown**: Undiagnosed; theories include chorea minor, hysteroid seizure (atypical depression), or physical/mental combination; Lovecraft’s letters describe headaches, insomnia, nervous weakness, and sensory avoidance.
- **Father’s illness**: Lovecraft claimed insomnia/overwork caused paralysis; death certificate states general paresis (syphilis). Unknown if Lovecraft was deceived or later misrepresented facts.
- **Extent of dream transcription**: Popular 1990s notion that stories were dream transcripts is rejected by scholars; dreams provided structural/atmospheric influence, not verbatim content.
- **Political evolution**: Sincerity and consistency of late socialism debated—elitist, anti-Marxist, anti-democratic, using "fascism" positively for intellectual aristocracy; shift motivated by Depression’s societal harm and observation that wealthy class failed aristocratic ideal.
