# Diocletian

## Overview
Diocletian (r. 284–305) was a Roman emperor who ended the Crisis of the Third Century by stabilizing the empire through radical administrative, military, and economic reforms. His creation of the Tetrarchy (rule of four) permanently split imperial authority and established a new autocratic model of government. Though his system collapsed after his abdication, his reforms fundamentally reshaped the Roman state and enabled its survival for another 150 years.

## Timeline
- **284** — Proclaimed emperor by the army after the death of Numerian; defeats rival Carinus in 285.
- **286** — Appoints Maximian as co-emperor (Augustus) to rule the West.
- **293** — Establishes the Tetrarchy by appointing Galerius and Constantius as junior Caesars.
- **296–298** — Suppresses a revolt in Egypt led by Domitius Domitianus.
- **298** — Galerius defeats the Sassanid Persians, leading to the favorable Peace of Nisibis.
- **301** — Issues the Edict on Maximum Prices to combat inflation; it fails.
- **303** — Launches the Great Persecution, the last and largest official persecution of Christians.
- **305** — Voluntarily abdicates, the first Roman emperor to do so.
- **311/312** — Dies in retirement at his palace in Dalmatia.

## Body

### Rise to Power and the Tetrarchy
Diocles, a soldier of low birth from Dalmatia, rose through the military ranks. After the mysterious death of Emperor Numerian in 284, he was proclaimed emperor by the troops, taking the name Diocletian. He defeated Numerian's brother Carinus in 285 to become sole ruler. To manage the vast empire and avoid the instability of sole rule, he appointed his fellow officer Maximian as co-emperor (Augustus) in 286. In 293, he further delegated power by creating the Tetrarchy, appointing two junior Caesars (Galerius and Constantius) under himself and Maximian. This system divided the empire into four administrative units, each ruled by a tetrarch, with a clear line of succession.

### Military Campaigns and Foreign Policy
Diocletian's reign was marked by constant campaigning to secure the empire's borders. He fought the Sarmatians and Carpi along the Danube, the Alamanni on the Rhine, and suppressed usurpers in Egypt. His greatest foreign policy success was the war against the Sassanid Persian Empire. After an initial Roman defeat, his Caesar Galerius won a decisive victory, leading to the Peace of Nisibis in 299. This treaty gave Rome control over Armenia and key territories east of the Tigris, securing a favorable and lasting peace.

### Administrative and Legal Reforms
Diocletian enacted the most sweeping administrative reforms in Roman history. He doubled the number of provinces to nearly 100, grouping them into twelve dioceses governed by vicarii. This separated civil and military authority, with new military commanders (duces) overseeing frontier defense. He vastly expanded the bureaucracy, which some sources claim doubled in size. Legally, he issued over 1,200 rescripts (imperial rulings) and initiated the first official codification of Roman law with the Codex Gregorianus and Codex Hermogenianus. To fund his enlarged army and administration, he implemented a new, standardized tax system based on regular censuses of land (iugum) and heads (caput).

### Economic Policies and the Great Persecution
Diocletian's economic reforms had mixed results. He restored the coinage but faced renewed inflation, prompting the failed Edict on Maximum Prices in 301, which attempted to fix prices for over 1,000 goods. His most infamous policy was the Great Persecution (303–312), the last and bloodiest official persecution of Christians. Initiated after a divination ceremony blamed Christians, it involved destroying churches, scriptures, and executing resisters. The persecution ultimately failed to eradicate Christianity, which became the empire's preferred religion under Constantine just two decades later.

### Abdication and Legacy
Weakened by illness, Diocletian abdicated on 1 May 305, the first emperor to do so voluntarily. He retired to his palace in Dalmatia (now Split, Croatia), where he famously tended his gardens. His tetrarchic system quickly collapsed into civil war among his successors. Despite this, his administrative structure, tax system, and autocratic model of imperial rule were adopted and refined by Constantine and endured for centuries. His reign marked the definitive end of the classical Roman Empire and the beginning of the Dominate, a more centralized and bureaucratic state.

## Terms
- **Tetrarchy**: The system of "rule by four" established by Diocletian, with two senior Augusti and two junior Caesars governing distinct regions of the empire.
- **Augustus**: The title for a senior Roman emperor.
- **Caesar**: The title for a junior emperor and designated heir within the Tetrarchy.
- **Diocese**: A large administrative grouping of Roman provinces, governed by a vicarius.
- **Vicarius**: A deputy governor of a diocese, appointed by the praetorian prefect.
- **Dux**: A military commander responsible for frontier defense, separate from civil administration.
- **Rescript**: An authoritative legal ruling issued by the emperor in response to a petition.
- **Iugum/Caput**: The units of Diocletian's tax system, based on land productivity and population, respectively.
- **Edict on Maximum Prices**: Diocletian's 301 decree attempting to curb inflation by fixing prices, which was largely ignored and failed.
- **Peace of Nisibis**: The 299 treaty with Persia that gave Rome control over Armenia and key eastern territories.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact cause of Numerian's death (assassination vs. natural causes) and Diocletian's potential involvement remain unclear.
- Scholars debate whether Maximian's assumption of the Augustus title in 286 was authorized by Diocletian or a usurpation later legitimized.
- The effectiveness and enforcement of the Edict on Maximum Prices are disputed; ancient sources like Lactantius may exaggerate its negative impacts.
- The degree to which Diocletian, rather than his more zealous colleague Galerius, drove the Great Persecution of Christians is debated, with some sources placing primary responsibility on Galerius.
