# Robert Louis Stevenson

## Overview
Robert Louis Stevenson (1850–1894) was a Scottish novelist, essayist, poet, and travel writer whose adventure romances—*Treasure Island* (1883), *Kidnapped* (1886), and *Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde* (1886)—made him a global celebrity. Born in Edinburgh to a dynasty of lighthouse engineers, he rejected the family profession for literature despite chronic respiratory illness (likely bronchiectasis or sarcoidosis, not tuberculosis as contemporaries thought). His marriage to American Fanny Van de Grift Osbourne led to years of travel in search of health, culminating in settlement at Vailima, Samoa (1890). There, witnessing colonial rivalry among Britain, Germany, and the United States, his writing shifted from romance to a darker realism (*The Beach of Falesá*, *The Ebb-Tide*) and he became a political advocate for Samoan autonomy. He died of a probable brain haemorrhage at 44. Long dismissed as a children’s author, late-20th-century scholarship restored his reputation as a peer of Conrad and James; he ranks 26th among the world’s most-translated writers.

## Timeline
- **13 Nov 1850** — Born Robert Lewis Balfour Stevenson in Edinburgh to lighthouse engineer Thomas Stevenson and Margaret Isabella Balfour.
- **1866** — First publication, *The Pentland Rising: A Page of History, 1666*, printed by his father.
- **1867–1871** — Studies engineering at University of Edinburgh; joins Speculative Society; decides on literary career (1871).
- **1873** — Meets Sidney Colvin and Fanny Sitwell in England; declared atheist to horrified parents.
- **1875** — Qualifies as advocate (lawyer) but never practises.
- **1876** — Meets Fanny Van de Grift Osbourne at Grez-sur-Loing, France.
- **1878–1879** — Publishes first travel books *An Inland Voyage* and *Travels with a Donkey in the Cévennes*; travels to California, nearly dying en route.
- **May 1880** — Marries Fanny (now divorced) in San Francisco; honeymoon at Mount Saint Helena (*The Silverado Squatters*).
- **1884–1887** — Settles in Bournemouth (“Skerryvore”); writes *Treasure Island*, *Kidnapped*, *Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde*, *A Child’s Garden of Verses* while largely bedridden.
- **1888** — Charters yacht *Casco*; begins three-year Pacific voyage (Hawaii, Gilbert Islands, Tahiti, Samoa).
- **Dec 1889** — Arrives Apia, Samoa; purchases Vailima estate (Jan 1890).
- **1892** — Publishes *A Footnote to History*, chronicle of Samoan civil war and great-power interference.
- **1893–1894** — Writes realist Pacific fiction (*The Beach of Falesá*, *The Ebb-Tide*) and sequel *Catriona*; begins *Weir of Hermiston*.
- **3 Dec 1894** — Dies at Vailima, aged 44; buried on Mount Vaea.

## Body

### Family background and childhood
Stevenson was born into a professional dynasty: his father Thomas, grandfather Robert, and uncles Alan and David were all eminent lighthouse engineers. His mother’s family, the Balfours, were gentry and Church of Scotland ministers. A sickly only child, he spent much of boyhood in bed or at rural retreats (Bridge of Allan, Colinton, Swanston Cottage), dictating stories before he could read. His father proudly paid for his first publication at 16, a historical account of the 1666 Covenanters’ rising.

### Education and rejection of engineering
Stevenson’s formal schooling was fragmented by illness: Mr Henderson’s School, Edinburgh Academy, a term at Spring Grove (Isleworth), and Robert Thomson’s private school. At Edinburgh University (1867) he nominally studied engineering but avoided lectures, preferring the Speculative Society debating club and amateur dramatics at Professor Fleeming Jenkin’s house. Annual lighthouse inspection trips with his father (Orkney, Shetland, Erraid, Wick) supplied travel material but confirmed his lack of engineering aptitude. In April 1871 he announced his intention to write; his father accepted the decision on condition he read law as a fallback. He passed the Scottish bar in 1875 but never practised.

### Religious crisis and Bohemian years
In 1873, his father discovered the constitution of the LJR (Liberty, Justice, Reverence) Club, which opened “Disregard everything our parents have taught us.” Stevenson declared himself an atheist, devastating his parents. Yet by 1878 he wrote a conciliatory letter affirming a “manifest God” and Christianity as a “wise, noble… doctrine of life.” He never resumed church attendance in Scotland but later taught Sunday School in Samoa and wrote prayers. Critics debate whether he reverted to faith or merely valued the King James Bible’s literary cadence. Meanwhile he adopted Bohemian dress, frequented cheap pubs and brothels, and published “An Apology for Idlers” (1877), defending idleness as a “radiating focus of goodwill” and a proof of the “Liveableness of Life.”

### Literary circles and marriage
In late 1873 Stevenson met Sidney Colvin (lifelong literary adviser) and Fanny Sitwell. Through Colvin he entered London literary circles: Andrew Lang, Edmund Gosse, Leslie Stephen, and poet W. E. Henley (wooden-legged, energetic, later model for Long John Silver). A canoe trip with friend Sir Walter Simpson became *An Inland Voyage* (1878). At Grez-sur-Loing (1876) he met Fanny Osbourne, an American magazine writer separated from her philandering husband. They became lovers in 1877; she returned to California in 1878. Stevenson followed in 1879, travelling steerage and overland by train (*The Amateur Emigrant*, *Across the Plains*), arriving near death in Monterey. Nursed back to health, he struggled in San Francisco until Fanny, now divorced, joined him. They married in May 1880.

### Productive invalidism in Bournemouth
Seeking sea air, the Stevensons settled at “Skerryvore” in Bournemouth (1884–1887). Largely bedridden—“like a weevil in a biscuit”—Stevenson wrote his most famous works: *Treasure Island* (serialized 1881, book 1883), *A Child’s Garden of Verses* (1885), *Kidnapped* and *Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde* (both 1886), and *Underwoods* (1887). Henry James became a daily visitor after moving nearby. Stevenson debated James on the “art of fiction,” arguing the novel cannot rival life’s complexity but creates a “harmonious pattern” by “half-shut[ting] his eyes against the dazzle.” He saw himself in Walter Scott’s tradition—storytelling that transports—rejecting French realism’s “sordidness.”

### Pacific voyages and political awakening
After his father’s death (1887), Stevenson sailed the *Casco* from San Francisco (June 1888). The sea air temporarily restored his health. He befriended King Kalākaua and Princess Kaʻiulani in Hawaii, visited the Molokaʻi leper colony (gifting a piano), and stayed months with tyrant-chief Tem Binoka on Abemama (Gilbert Islands). In December 1889 he reached Apia, Samoa, and bought the Vailima estate. Immersing himself in Samoan culture—taking the name *Tusitala* (“Teller of Tales”), translating *The Bottle Imp* into Samoan—he witnessed three imperial powers (Britain, Germany, USA) manipulating clan rivalries. He abandoned romance for reportage: *A Footnote to History* (1892) chronicles the first Samoan Civil War; letters to *The Times* exposed European misconduct with “ironic twist.” He allied with chief Mataafa against German-backed Malietoa, warning chiefs that without “occupying and using” their land through roads, gardens, and wise trade, “others will… cast you into outer darkness,” citing parallels in Hawaii, Ireland, and the Scottish Highlands.

### Final realist turn and unfinished masterpiece
At Vailima Stevenson wrote ~700,000 words. *The Beach of Falesá* (1892) presents a prejudiced, unheroic Scots copra trader; villains are duplicitous whites. He called it “the first realistic South Seas story,” capturing the “smell and look” of the islands. *The Ebb-Tide* (1894, with Lloyd Osbourne) depicts three derelict whites in Papeete, a “microcosm of imperialist society” where moral edges are “blurred.” Critics note parallels with Conrad’s early fiction. Simultaneously he returned to Scottish historical romance with *Catriona* (1893), sequel to *Kidnapped*. Exhausted but driven by Vailima’s expenses, he began *Weir of Hermiston* (1896), set in 18th-century Scotland amid social breakdown mirroring Samoa. “It’s so good that it frightens me,” he reportedly said; it remains unfinished at his death.

### Death and burial
On 3 December 1894, straining to open a wine bottle (or making mayonnaise), Stevenson exclaimed “What’s that?”, asked if his face looked strange, and collapsed. He died within hours, aged 44, likely of a brain haemorrhage; 2000 research suggests hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (Osler-Rendu-Weber syndrome) explaining his lifelong pulmonary bleeding and early death. Samoans kept night vigil, bore him on their shoulders to Mount Vaea, and buried him on land donated by British Vice-Consul Thomas Trood. His epitaph, from his poem “Requiem,” reads: “Home is the sailor, home from sea…” The western face bears Ruth 1:16–17.

### Critical reputation
Stevenson was celebrated in life by peers (James, Conan Doyle, Kipling, Proust). In the 20th century he was relegated to children’s/horror genres, excluded from the *Oxford Anthology* (1973) and *Norton Anthology* (1968–2000). Virginia Woolf (daughter of his mentor Leslie Stephen) and Leonard Woolf dismissed him. Late-20th-century re-evaluation restored him as a literary theorist, essayist, social critic, and Pacific historian, peer to Conrad and James. Roger Ebert argued he belongs “on the same shelf with Joseph Conrad and Jack London instead of in between Winnie the Pooh and Peter Pan.” The Index Translationum ranks him 26th worldwide (2018), ahead of Wilde and Poe.

## Terms
- **Speculative Society**: Exclusive Edinburgh University debating club where Stevenson formed key friendships (Charles Baxter, Fleeming Jenkin).
- **Covenanters**: 17th-century Scottish Presbyterians who resisted royal imposition of episcopacy; subject of Stevenson’s first publication and nurse’s tales.
- **Bronchiectasis / sarcoidosis**: Modern medical retrospective diagnoses for Stevenson’s chronic “weak chest,” replacing contemporary tuberculosis diagnosis.
- **Tusitala**: Samoan for “Teller of Tales,” the name Stevenson adopted at Vailima.
- **Lockean sense**: Reference to John Locke’s labour theory of property—land ownership justified by “improving management and labour.”
- **Seasteading**: Libertarian ideal of autonomous maritime communities; critics see *The Ebb-Tide*’s Attwater’s island fishery as anticipating this.
- **Social Darwinism**: Ideology applying “survival of the fittest” to human societies; some readings detect it in *Treasure Island* and *Kidnapped*’s orphan-versus-rabble struggles.
- **Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (Osler-Rendu-Weber syndrome)**: Genetic vascular disorder identified in 2000 research as possible cause of Stevenson’s symptoms and death.
- **Requiem**: Stevenson’s poem (“Under the wide and starry sky…”) inscribed on his tomb.
- **Vailima**: Stevenson’s 314-acre Samoan estate, now a museum; name means “five waters.”

## Debates and open questions
- **Nature of his illness**: Contemporaries diagnosed tuberculosis; modern scholars favour bronchiectasis or sarcoidosis; 2000 study proposes hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia.
- **Religious stance**: Did his 1878 conciliatory letter signal genuine faith, or only aesthetic attachment to biblical language? He taught Sunday School in Samoa but wrote the sceptical “Pulvis et Umbra” (1888).
- **Political ideology**: Conservative (cousin Graham Balfour), individualist/Social Darwinist (critics of *Treasure Island*), libertarian (citing “The Day After Tomorrow”), or anti-imperialist radical (Julia Reid, citing Samoan activism)? Evidence supports multiple readings.
- **Collaboration with Fanny Osbourne**: *The Dynamiter* (1885) and *The Wrong Box*, *The Wrecker*, *The Ebb-Tide* (with Lloyd Osbourne) raise questions about her editorial and compositional role.
- **Literary status**: Why was he canonically excluded mid-century? Was it genre prejudice, the “children’s author” label, or modernist rejection of romance? The reversal since the 1980s remains a case study in reputation formation.
