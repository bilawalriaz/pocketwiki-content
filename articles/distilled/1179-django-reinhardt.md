# Django Reinhardt

## Overview
Jean "Django" Reinhardt (1910–1953) was a French jazz guitarist and composer of Manouche Romani descent, widely regarded as the first major European jazz talent and one of the greatest guitarists in history. After a fire severely burned his left hand in 1928, he developed a revolutionary technique using only his index and middle fingers for soloing, creating a virtuosic style that fused Romani musical traditions with American swing. With violinist Stéphane Grappelli, he co-founded the Quintette du Hot Club de France (1934), the first major jazz ensemble featuring string instruments exclusively. Despite Nazi persecution of Romani people during the occupation of France, Reinhardt survived and continued performing, composing standards like "Nuages" and "Minor Swing." His influence spans jazz, rock, and country, inspiring figures from Charlie Byrd and Wes Montgomery to Jeff Beck, Jimmy Page, and Willie Nelson.

## Body

### Early Life and Musical Foundations
Reinhardt was born into a Manouche Romani family in Belgium but raised in encampments near Paris. His father used his wife's surname to avoid French conscription. Largely self-taught and illiterate until adulthood, Reinhardt began on violin before receiving a banjo-guitar at 12. He learned by mimicking local virtuosos like Jean "Poulette" Castro and his uncle Guiligou. By 15, he was earning a living busking in cafés with his brother Joseph, playing popular *bal musette* dance music alongside accordionists. He had not yet encountered jazz, though American expatriate bands like Billy Arnold's were active in Paris.

### The 1928 Fire and Technical Reinvention
At 17, Reinhardt married Florine "Bella" Mayer per Romani custom. His first recordings in 1928 attracted British bandleader Jack Hylton, who offered him a job. Before starting, a candle ignited celluloid flowers in their caravan, engulfing it in flames. Reinhardt suffered burns over half his body; doctors recommended amputating his right leg (he refused, walking with a cane) and declared he would never play guitar again—the fourth and fifth fingers of his left hand were badly damaged. During 18 months of recovery, he retaught himself using only his index and middle fingers for solo lines, reserving the injured fingers for chord work. His brother Joseph bought him a six-string steel-strung acoustic guitar, enabling his comeback.

### Discovery of Jazz and the Quintette du Hot Club de France
After separating from his wife, Reinhardt traveled France with girlfriend Sophie "Naguine" Ziegler. In 1931, he met Stéphane Grappelli, a violinist who had heard Joe Venuti and Eddie Lang play with Paul Whiteman's orchestra in 1928. Introduced to American jazz via Émile Savitry's record collection (Louis Armstrong, Duke Ellington, Venuti, Lang, Lonnie Johnson), Reinhardt resolved to become a jazz professional. In summer 1934, both played in Louis Vola's band at the Hôtel Claridge. Pierre Nourry, secretary of the Hot Club de France, invited them to form the Quintette du Hot Club de France—an all-string ensemble (lead guitar, violin, rhythm guitars, bass) unique in jazz. From 1934–1939, they became Europe's most innovative jazz group, recording extensively for Decca (US) and French labels, and performing at the artistic salon R-26. Reinhardt also recorded with visiting Americans: Coleman Hawkins, Benny Carter, Rex Stewart, Adelaide Hall, and jammed with Louis Armstrong. In 1938, the quintet triumphed in London, where Eddie Cantor kissed Reinhardt's hand onstage.

### Wartime Survival and Creative Expansion
When WWII began, the quintet was touring the UK. Reinhardt returned immediately to Paris; Grappelli remained in London. Reinhardt reformed the group with clarinetist Hubert Rostaing replacing Grappelli. As a Romani jazz musician, he faced existential danger: Nazis systematically murdered 600,000–1.5 million Romani, enforced sterilization, and required ID triangles. Jazz was condemned as "degenerate" by Hitler and Goebbels, yet occupied France maintained a vibrant jazz scene on Radio Paris and Radio France, fueled by the *Zazou* youth subculture and the departure of American musicians. Reinhardt became Europe's most famous jazz musician, working steadily but under constant threat. A jazz-loving Luftwaffe officer, Dietrich Schulz-Köhn, once secured his release after a capture during an escape attempt. During this period, Reinhardt experimented with amplification for big-band formats, composed a Mass for the Gypsies and a symphony (dictated to an assistant, as he could not read music), and wrote "Rythme Futur" hoping to appease Nazi tastes. His 1940 composition "Nuages" became a liberation anthem in Paris, selling over 100,000 copies and earning three encores at Salle Pleyel.

### United States Tour and Postwar Struggles
In autumn 1946, Reinhardt toured the US as guest soloist with Duke Ellington's orchestra, debuting at Cleveland Music Hall and playing two nights at Carnegie Hall (six curtain calls the first night). Though proud, he was not fully integrated—playing only a few tunes at show's end with no special arrangements. He then held a residency at Café Society Uptown in New York, but a borrowed electric guitar hampered his delicacy. Promised California work never materialized; he returned to France in February 1947. Postwar, Reinhardt became notoriously unreliable—missing concerts to "walk to the beach" or refusing to leave bed—yet continued improvising at R-26 with Grappelli. In 1949, he recorded over 60 tunes in Rome with an Italian rhythm section, reuniting with Grappelli on his acoustic Selmer-Maccaferri. He declined Benny Goodman's renewed offer to join him in the US.

### Final Years and Bebop Integration
In 1951, Reinhardt retired to Samois-sur-Seine near Fontainebleau. He adopted an amplified Selmer (Stimer pickup), and his "Nouvelle Quintette" with young modernists like altoist Hubert Fol (in the Charlie Parker mold) began fusing bebop vocabulary with his melodic style. His playing grew more linear and "horn-like," with longer sustain and less reliance on acoustic "gypsy tricks." Critics like Wayne Jefferies argue this "electric period" reveals Reinhardt as a major bebop guitarist whose "infallible technique" and "advanced harmonic sense" reached heights "Christian and many other Bop musicians never came near." His final recordings (March 1953) captured this evolution. On 16 May 1953, walking home from Fontainebleau–Avon station after a Paris gig, he collapsed from a brain hemorrhage; a doctor arrived a day later; he was declared dead at 43.

### Technique and Musical Approach
Reinhardt's technique was forged by his injury. Using a plectrum for volume (pre-amplification), he developed a left-hand system based on three-note chords, each serving multiple conventional inversions. His ring and little fingers could fret high strings for chord tones but not articulate independently; he also used his thumb on the low string. Melodic runs relied on two-note-per-string arpeggios (index/middle fingers) shifting vertically up the neck, avoiding conventional "box" positions. He employed octave melodies, tremolo chords mimicking horn sections, natural/artificial harmonics, dissonances, chromatic runs spanning the full fretboard, two- and three-octave arpeggios, advanced chords, and flattened-fifth usage predating bebop. Hugues Panassié (1942) called his technique "vastly superior," combining "inconceivable velocity" with "sparkling and ravishing" melodic invention. Billy Neil and E. Gates (1945) emphasized the emotional core: "profound rational knowledge of his instrument" fused with "love for music." John Jorgenson notes the "personality," "joy," and "intensity" that make his playing "infectious." In his later electric period (c. 1946+), amplification enabled longer sustain, quieter passages, and a more linear, Christian-influenced approach, though he retained his originality and ferocious swing.

### Family and Musical Dynasty
Reinhardt's first son, Lousson (Henri Baumgartner), played bebop in the 1950s–60s, lived traditionally, and recorded little. His second son, Babik (b. 1943), became a respected contemporary jazz guitarist, recording albums until his 2001 death. Brother Joseph initially quit music after Django's death but returned; his son Markus is a Romani-style violinist. The third generation includes David Reinhardt (Babik's son), leading his own trio, and Dallas Baumgartner (Lousson's great-grandson), a low-profile traveling guitarist. Distant relative Schnuckenack Reinhardt (d. 2006) promoted gypsy jazz in Germany.

### Legacy and Influence
Reinhardt wrote nearly 100 songs. After a decade of obscurity (1950s bebop/rock dominance), a revival began in the mid-1960s, spawning annual festivals (Festival Django Reinhardt at Samois-sur-Seine since 1983, DjangoFests globally, "Django in June" at Smith College). Classical guitarist Julian Bream and country legend Chet Atkins (who ranked him #1 among ten greatest 20th-century guitarists) championed him. US jazz guitarists Charlie Byrd (primary influence) and Wes Montgomery studied him. Rock icons Jeff Beck ("by far the most astonishing guitar player ever," "superhuman"), Jimmy Page ("fantastic"), Andrew Latimer, Denny Laine, and Jimmy McCulloch cited him. Jerry Garcia and Tony Iommi—both missing fingertips—drew inspiration from his disability triumph. Garcia (1985) stressed Django's "fullness of expression": "every note have a specific personality... I really haven't heard it anywhere but with Django." Willie Nelson called him life-changing: "I studied his technique. Even more, I studied his gentleness."

### Cultural Footprint
Tributes include John Lewis's jazz standard "Django" (1954), Dickey Betts's "Jessica" (Allman Brothers), Willie Nelson/Merle Haggard's album *Django and Jimmie* (2015), and Lillebjørn Nilsen's "Tanta til Beate" (1982). He appears in novels (*The Magic Strings of Frankie Presto*), graphic novels (*Jeangot*, *Django Main de Feu*), films (*Sweet and Lowdown*, *The Triplets of Belleville*, *Head in the Clouds*, *Djangomania!*, *Hugo*, *Django* 2017), video games (*Mafia*, *BioShock*), and TV (*The Backyardigans*). Honors: 66th/76th in "Greatest Belgian" polls (2005), Belgian 10-Euro commemorative coin (2010), and namesake of the Django web framework and WordPress 3.1.

## Terms
- ****Manouche**** — A subgroup of the Romani people (often called "Gypsies") in France; Reinhardt's ethnic community.
- ****Bal musette**** — A style of French popular dance music featuring accordion, often played in cafés and dance halls; Reinhardt's early professional milieu.
- ****Banjo-guitar**** — A six-string hybrid instrument tuned like a guitar but with a banjo resonator body; Reinhardt's first main instrument (1928 recordings).
- ****Quintette du Hot Club de France**** — The all-string jazz ensemble (lead guitar, violin, two rhythm guitars, bass) co-founded by Reinhardt and Grappelli in 1934; Europe's first major jazz group.
- ****Selmer-Maccaferri**** — A distinctive acoustic guitar model (large D-shaped or oval soundhole, steel strings) favored by Reinhardt; became iconic in gypsy jazz.
- ****Gypsy jazz** (or *jazz manouche*)** — The genre pioneered by Reinhardt, blending Romani musical traditions, French musette, and American swing; characterized by string-only ensembles, percussive rhythm (*la pompe*), and virtuosic improvisation.
- ****La pompe**** — The driving, percussive rhythm guitar technique in gypsy jazz (fast up-down strums mimicking a drum), providing harmonic and rhythmic foundation.
- ****Zazous**** — A French youth subculture of the 1940s who embraced jazz, flamboyant fashion, and resistance to Vichy/Nazi conformity; swelled Hot Club audiences.
- ****Bebop**** — A modern jazz style (1940s+) featuring fast tempos, complex harmonies, and extended improvisation; Reinhardt assimilated its vocabulary in his final years.
- ****Stimer pickup**** — An early magnetic pickup fitted to acoustic guitars; Reinhardt adopted it c. 1946 for amplification, enabling his "electric period" sound.

## Debates and open questions
- **Origin of "Django"**: Sources suggest it may be Romani for "I awake," or simply a Walloon diminutive of "Jean"; no consensus.
- **Wartime survival**: How Reinhardt navigated Nazi-occupied Paris as a famous Romani jazz musician remains debated—scholars cite his fame, protection by jazz-loving German officers (e.g., Dietrich Schulz-Köhn), and the ambiguous status of jazz in Vichy France, but the full extent of his compromises or resistance is unclear.
- **Electric period assessment**: Reinhardt's post-1946 amplified/bebop recordings have historically received less attention than his pre-war acoustic output; recent revival (Rosenberg Trio, Biréli Lagrène, scholarship by Wayne Jefferies) argues they represent a major, underrated bebop contribution.
- **Unrecorded compositions**: Several youthful waltzes (e.g., "Montagne Sainte-Genevieve," "Gagoug") survived only in associates' repertoires; a Mass for the Gypsies was incomplete (8-minute extract exists). Their musical significance is still being explored.
- **Literacy and composition method**: Reinhardt could not read/write music; he dictated compositions to assistants. The degree to which this shaped his harmonic structures versus his ear-based improvisational genius is a musicological question.
