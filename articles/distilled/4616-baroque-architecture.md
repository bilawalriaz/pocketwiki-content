# Baroque architecture

## Overview
Baroque architecture is a highly decorative and theatrical style that originated in late 16th-century Italy. It was initially promoted by the Catholic Church, particularly the Jesuits, as a visual tool to inspire awe and counter the Protestant Reformation. The style evolved through distinct phases, spreading across Europe and its colonies, and eventually developing into the even more ornate Rococo variant.

## Timeline
- **c. 1584** — Early Baroque begins with the Church of the Gesù in Rome.
- **1603** — Santa Susanna, a key early Baroque church, is completed in Rome.
- **1615–1645** — Luxembourg Palace in Paris introduces the Italian palace style to France.
- **1625–1675** — High Baroque period flourishes in Rome with architects like Bernini and Borromini.
- **1668–1678** — Construction of the Palace of Versailles begins, defining French Baroque grandeur.
- **1675–1750** — Late Baroque period sees the style spread across Europe and to the colonies.
- **c. 1730** — Rococo, a more elaborately decorative variant, appears in Central Europe.
- **1748–1755** — Nuruosmaniye Mosque complex marks the full emergence of Ottoman Baroque.

## Body

### Origins and Early Baroque (1584–1625)
Baroque architecture emerged in Rome as a Catholic Counter-Reformation tool, designed to inspire collective awe and contrast with the severe style of earlier churches. It used dramatic effects like **quadratura** (illusionistic ceiling painting) to create the impression of looking into heaven. Early masterpieces include Giacomo della Porta's Church of the Gesù (consecrated 1584) and Carlo Maderno's façade for St. Peter's Basilica (completed 1612). The style was soon imported to Paris, with the Luxembourg Palace (1615–1624) by Salomon de Brosse being an early example.

### High Baroque (1625–1675)
This period, centered in Rome under patrons like Pope Urban VIII, saw the style reach its peak. Architects Gian Lorenzo Bernini, Francesco Borromini, and Pietro da Cortona created buildings, interiors, and public spaces characterized by movement, curved forms, and theatrical light. Bernini's colonnade for St. Peter's Square (1656–57) and Borromini's San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane (1634–1646) are landmarks. In France, a more ordered, classical variant emerged under Louis XIV, exemplified by the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles.

### Late Baroque and Regional Variants (1675–1750)
The style spread throughout Europe, developing distinct national characteristics. In Central Europe, architects like Fischer von Erlach and Balthasar Neumann created grandiose churches and palaces. In Spain and its colonies, the ornate **Churrigueresque** style emerged. The Dutch Republic developed a restrained, sober Baroque reflecting Protestant values. By the early 18th century, the highly decorative **Rococo** variant appeared, with Balthasar Neumann's Würzburg Residence (1749–51) as a key example. The style also reached Russia, the Ottoman Empire (as Ottoman Baroque), and the Spanish and Portuguese colonies in the Americas.

## Terms
- **Baroque:** A decorative and theatrical architectural style emphasizing drama, movement, and emotional intensity.
- **Quadratura:** Trompe-l'œil ceiling painting combined with sculpture to create the illusion of three-dimensional space, often depicting heavens.
- **Churrigueresque:** An extremely ornate Spanish and Spanish Colonial Baroque style named after the Churriguera family of architects.
- **Rococo:** An even more elaborately decorative variant of Baroque that flourished in Central Europe in the early 18th century.
- **Solomonic Column:** A twisted column, often used in Baroque architecture to create an illusion of upward motion.
- **Cartouche:** An ornamental, sculpted frame or scroll-like element used to decorate surfaces.
- **Chiaroscuro:** The use of strong contrasts between light and dark for dramatic effect.
- **Ottoman Baroque:** A style that emerged in 18th-century Istanbul, integrating European Baroque elements into Ottoman architecture.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes a scholarly debate regarding Ottoman Baroque, challenging the view that it was merely derivative and inauthentic. Instead, it is explained as a conscious endeavor to refashion Istanbul into a modern city with a globally resonant architectural mode, part of a larger move to reaffirm the empire’s status.
