# Melody

## Overview
A melody is a linear succession of musical tones perceived as a single entity, combining pitch and rhythm. It is the foreground element of music, often carrying the primary theme and emotional expression, and serves as a fundamental tool for communication and memory in musical composition.

## Timeline
- **Pre-20th century** — European music melodies featured fixed, recurring pitch and duration patterns.
- **18th century** — Johann Philipp Kirnberger argued melody is the ultimate goal of all harmony.
- **19th century** — Richard Wagner popularized the leitmotif, a recurring melody associated with an idea, person, or place.
- **20th century** — Melodies began utilizing a greater variety of pitch resources, including the chromatic scale, and qualitative dimensions like timbre gained structural importance.
- **1984** — Paul Narveson noted that over three-quarters of melodic topics had not been thoroughly explored.
- **20th-21st century** — Contemporary music lessened the primary importance of pitch and duration, often emphasizing timbre and dynamics to create linear form.

## Body

### Definition and Elements
A melody is a combination of pitch and rhythm, perceived as a single entity. It is the foreground to the background accompaniment. Its essential elements are duration, pitch, and quality (timbre), along with texture and loudness. Melodies are often described by their motion (conjunct or disjunct), pitch range, tension and release, continuity, cadence, and shape. They typically consist of musical phrases or motifs that are repeated throughout a composition.

### Function and Historical View
Melody has been considered the primary goal of music. Johann Philipp Kirnberger argued that all harmony exists to serve beautiful melody. Composer Marcus Paus compared melody to a scent for the senses, stating it carries personality and serves as a messenger from the author to the audience. Before the 20th century, European and popular music melodies relied on fixed, recurring patterns of pitch and duration.

### Evolution and Stylistic Variation
The 20th century saw melodies employ a wider variety of pitch resources, with the chromatic scale becoming widely used alongside the diatonic scale. Composers also began assigning structural roles to qualitative dimensions like timbre, which were previously reserved for pitch and rhythm. Different musical styles use melody distinctively: jazz uses a "head" melody as a starting point for improvisation; rock and folk music often center on verse and chorus melodies; Indian classical music relies heavily on melody and rhythm without chord changes; and Balinese gamelan uses heterophony, simultaneous variations of a single melody. Western classical music often develops an initial theme into variations or layers multiple melodies in polyphony, such as in a fugue.

### Contemporary Developments
In contemporary music of the 20th and 21st centuries, the primary importance of pitch and duration has lessened, with quality (timbre) gaining prominence. Examples include musique concrète and klangfarbenmelodie (a technique where melody is created by changing timbre). Works like Elliott Carter's *Eight Etudes and a Fantasy* and György Ligeti's *Aventures* create linear form through dynamics or recurring phonetics rather than traditional pitch sequences.

## Terms
- **Melody**: A linear succession of musical tones perceived as a single entity.
- **Pitch**: The perceived frequency of a sound, determining its highness or lowness.
- **Rhythm**: The pattern of durations of notes and silences in music.
- **Timbre**: The quality or color of a musical sound that distinguishes it from others of the same pitch and loudness.
- **Motif**: A short melodic fragment or idea that is repeated and developed.
- **Leitmotif**: A recurring melody associated with a specific idea, person, or place, popularized by Richard Wagner.
- **Polyphony**: A musical texture consisting of two or more simultaneous, independent melodic lines.
- **Heterophony**: A musical texture where multiple performers simultaneously play variations of a single melodic line.
- **Diatonic scale**: A seven-note scale (e.g., major or minor) using only the pitches of a specific key.
- **Chromatic scale**: A twelve-note scale that includes all the pitches within an octave.

## Debates and Open Questions
The relative significance of melody versus harmony has been a historical point of debate, with figures like Kirnberger asserting melody's primacy. Scholar Paul Narveson noted in 1984 that the majority of melodic topics had not been thoroughly explored, suggesting ongoing areas for study. The evolving role of melody in contemporary music, where traditional pitch and duration may be secondary to timbre and dynamics, continues to challenge and expand its definition.
