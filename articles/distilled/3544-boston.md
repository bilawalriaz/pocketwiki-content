# Boston

## Overview
Boston is the capital and most populous city of Massachusetts, serving as the cultural and financial center of New England. Founded in 1630, it played a pivotal role in the American Revolution and has since evolved into a global leader in higher education, research, and biotechnology. Its economy is driven by finance, professional services, information technology, and government, with a population of 675,647 as of the 2020 census.

## Timeline
- **1630** — Founded on Shawmut Peninsula by English Puritan settlers.
- **1634** — Boston Common established as the nation's first public park.
- **1635** — Boston Latin School founded, the nation's first public school.
- **1770** — Boston Massacre occurs, fueling revolutionary sentiment.
- **1773** — Boston Tea Party takes place, escalating tensions with Britain.
- **1775-1776** — Siege of Boston during the American Revolutionary War.
- **1822** — Town of Boston officially becomes the City of Boston.
- **1897** — Tremont Street subway opens, the first subway system in the U.S.

- **2013** — Boston Marathon bombing occurs, killing 3 and injuring ~264.

## Body

### Founding and Early History
Before European colonization, the region was inhabited by the Massachusett people. The first European settler was Anglican cleric William Blaxton, who invited Puritan colonists to share the Shawmut Peninsula in 1630. The settlement was named "Boston" by Isaac Johnson after his hometown in Lincolnshire, England. Puritan ethics and a focus on education shaped the early city, leading to the founding of Boston Latin School in 1635. By the mid-18th century, Boston was the largest town in the Thirteen Colonies but faced economic stagnation as New York and Philadelphia surpassed it.

### American Revolution
Boston was a hotbed of revolutionary activity against British policies. Key events include the 1770 Boston Massacre and the 1773 Boston Tea Party, where colonists destroyed a shipment of tea to protest taxation. The British response with the Intolerable Acts led to the Battles of Lexington and Concord in 1775. The subsequent Siege of Boston (1775–1776) ended when Patriot forces, led by George Washington, placed cannons on Dorchester Heights, forcing the British to evacuate on March 17, 1776.

### 19th Century Growth and Immigration
After the Revolution, Boston thrived as a port but later shifted toward manufacturing. The city expanded significantly through land reclamation and the annexation of neighboring towns. A major wave of immigration, particularly from Ireland following the Great Famine, transformed the city's ethnic composition. By the late 19th century, distinct immigrant neighborhoods emerged, and the "Boston Brahmins" were regarded as the nation's social and cultural elite.

### 20th Century to Present
The early 20th century saw architectural growth but later economic decline as factories became obsolete. Urban renewal projects in the mid-20th century, like the construction of Government Center, displaced many residents. The economy recovered by the 1970s, driven by high-rise development and leading medical and educational institutions. The 21st century has marked Boston as a global leader in biotechnology and higher education, though it has faced challenges like the 2013 Marathon bombing and debates over gentrification.

## Terms
- **Puritans**: English Protestant settlers who founded Boston in 1630, emphasizing religious reform and education.
- **Shawmut Peninsula**: The original landmass on which Boston was founded.
- **Boston Brahmins**: The old, elite Boston families who dominated the city's social and cultural life in the 19th century.
- **Land Reclamation**: The process of creating new land from sea, marshes, or riverbeds, which significantly expanded Boston's area.
- **Annexation**: The incorporation of adjacent towns into the city, which increased Boston's size in the 19th and early 20th centuries.
- **Urban Renewal**: Government-led redevelopment of urban areas, often involving demolition, which reshaped parts of Boston in the mid-20th century.
- **Gentrification**: The process where wealthier individuals move into urban neighborhoods, increasing property values and often displacing lower-income residents.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The effectiveness and social cost of mid-20th century urban renewal projects, such as the demolition of the West End, remain debated.
- The long-term impacts of gentrification on Boston's neighborhoods and affordability are ongoing concerns.
- The city's strategies for mitigating sea-level rise and climate change impacts are a critical and evolving policy challenge.
