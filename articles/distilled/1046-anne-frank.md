# Anne Frank

## Overview
Annelies Marie Frank (12 June 1929 – c. February or March 1945) was a German-born Jewish diarist whose writings from hiding during the Nazi occupation of the Netherlands became one of the most widely read documents of the Holocaust. Born in Frankfurt, she fled to Amsterdam with her family in 1934 after Hitler’s rise to power. In July 1942, at age 13, she and her family went into hiding in a secret annex behind her father’s office on the Prinsengracht. Over the next two years, she recorded her thoughts, fears, and hopes in a red-checked diary. Arrested in August 1944, she was eventually deported to Bergen-Belsen, where she died of typhus in February or March 1945. Her father, Otto Frank—the sole survivor of the family—published her diary in 1947, transforming her into a global symbol of resilience and human dignity amid genocide.

## Body

### Early Life and Emigration
Anne Frank was born in Frankfurt to Edith and Otto Frank, members of a Reform Jewish family living in an assimilated community. Her early years were marked by intellectual curiosity fostered by her parents’ extensive library. In 1933, following Hitler’s appointment as chancellor, the family began fleeing persecution. Otto moved ahead to Amsterdam to establish a business, while Edith took the children to stay with relatives in Aachen before rejoining him. By late 1934, the entire family had settled in Amsterdam, joining hundreds of thousands of German Jews seeking refuge.

### Life in the Netherlands
In Amsterdam, Anne attended the Montessori School until anti-Jewish laws forced her transfer to the Jewish Lyceum in 1941. Meanwhile, Otto Frank expanded his business ventures, including founding Pectacon, which employed Hermann van Pels—a future companion in hiding. The family remained stateless despite long-term residence in the Netherlands. As deportations intensified in mid-1942, the Franks prepared to go into hiding.

### The Secret Annex
On 6 July 1942, the Frank family entered the Secret Annex—a concealed space behind a bookcase in Otto’s office building. They were soon joined by the Van Pels family and dentist Fritz Pfeffer. Supported by four trusted employees known as “helpers,” the group endured nearly two years of confinement. Anne chronicled daily life, interpersonal dynamics, and personal growth in her diary, which she addressed to “Kitty.” Her reflections reveal increasing maturity, emotional complexity, and literary ambition.

### Arrest and Deportation
On the morning of 4 August 1944, the Secret Annex was stormed by a group of German uniformed police (Grüne Polizei) led by SS-Oberscharführer Karl Silberbauer of the Sicherheitsdienst. All eight inhabitants were arrested and held at the House of Detention before being sent to Westerbork transit camp and then to Auschwitz. Upon arrival at Auschwitz, Otto Frank was separated from his family. Those deemed able to work were admitted into the camp; those deemed unfit for labour were immediately killed. Anne, who had turned 15 three months earlier, was one of the youngest spared from her transport. In October 1944, Anne and Margot were transferred to Bergen-Belsen. Edith Frank was left behind and died of disease, starvation and exhaustion at Auschwitz.

### Death and Liberation
Anne Frank died at the Bergen-Belsen concentration camp in February or March 1945. Although the specific cause is unknown, there is evidence to suggest that she died from a typhus epidemic that spread through the camp. Witnesses later testified that Margot fell from her bunk in her weakened state and was killed by the shock, and that Anne died a day after Margot. Their exact dates of death remain unknown. Both were buried together in a mass grave. Of the approximately 107,000 Jews deported from the Netherlands, fewer than 5,000 survived.

### Publication and Legacy
After the war, Otto Frank returned to Amsterdam and discovered that Miep Gies and Bep Voskuijl had preserved Anne’s diary. Encouraged by Anne’s expressed desire to be a writer, he edited and published the manuscript in 1947. The book gained international acclaim, inspiring plays, films, and educational programs worldwide. It has been translated into over 70 languages and remains a cornerstone of Holocaust education.

## Terms
- **Secret Annex**: A hidden chamber behind a bookcase in Otto Frank’s office on Prinsengracht, Amsterdam, where the Frank family and others hid during WWII.
- **Westerbork Transit Camp**: A Nazi transit camp in the Netherlands through which over 100,000 Jews passed en route to extermination camps.
- **Auschwitz**: A major Nazi concentration and extermination camp in occupied Poland where many Jews from the Netherlands were killed upon arrival.
- **Bergen-Belsen**: A Nazi concentration camp in northern Germany where Anne Frank and her sister Margot died of typhus in February or March 1945.
- **Holocaust**: The systematic genocide of six million European Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators during WWII.
- **Diary of Anne Frank**: Anne’s personal journal documenting her life in hiding, published posthumously and considered one of the most important literary works of the Holocaust.
- **Helpers**: Four employees of Otto Frank’s company who secretly supported the occupants of the Secret Annex by supplying food, news, and moral support.
- **Typhus**: A bacterial disease spread by lice, rampant in Nazi camps and responsible for thousands of deaths, including Anne Frank’s.

## Debates and Open Questions
The identity of the informant who betrayed the Secret Annex remains disputed. Various theories have emerged over decades:
- In 2015, researchers suggested Nelly Voskuijl, Bep Voskuijl’s sister, may have betrayed the group due to ideological opposition to aiding Jews.
- A 2016 report from the Anne Frank House proposed that the raid might have stemmed from investigations into ration-card fraud rather than deliberate betrayal.
- In 2018, another theory implicated Ans van Dijk, a Dutch Jew who collaborated with the Gestapo and betrayed at least 145 people.
- In 2022, a controversial investigation led by former FBI agent Vince Pankoke named Arnold van den Bergh, a member of Amsterdam’s Jewish Council, as the suspected informant. However, historians criticized the methodology and sources, leading to retractions and scholarly rejection of the claim.
Despite numerous investigations, no definitive proof has confirmed any single informant.
