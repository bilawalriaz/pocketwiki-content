# Richard Wagner

## Overview
Wilhelm Richard Wagner (1813–1883) was a German composer, theatre director, and essayist who revolutionised opera through his concept of the *Gesamtkunstwerk* ("total work of art"), fusing music, poetry, visual arts, and drama into a continuously sung narrative driven by *leitmotifs*—recurring musical phrases associated with characters, ideas, or objects. Unlike most composers, Wagner wrote his own libretti, which he called "poems." His mature works, particularly the four-opera cycle *Der Ring des Nibelungen* and *Tristan und Isolde*, pushed harmonic language to its limits, introducing extreme chromaticism and shifting tonal centres that anticipated 20th-century atonality. To realise his vision, he built the Bayreuth Festspielhaus, a dedicated festival theatre with a hidden orchestra pit and darkened auditorium. Wagner’s legacy is inseparable from his virulent antisemitism, expressed in essays like "Judaism in Music" (1850) and arguably encoded in his stage characters, and his posthumous appropriation by the Nazi regime, which continues to fuel debate over the separation of his art from his ideology.

## Timeline
- **22 May 1813** — Born in Leipzig, Confederation of the Rhine.
- **1833** — Composes first complete opera, *Die Feen*; appointed choirmaster at Theatre Würzburg.
- **24 Nov 1836** — Marries actress Christine Wilhelmine "Minna" Planer.
- **1839–1842** — Lives in Paris; completes *Rienzi* and *Der fliegende Holländer*.
- **20 Oct 1842** — *Rienzi* premieres successfully at Dresden Court Theatre; appointed Royal Saxon Court Conductor.
- **1843–1845** — Premieres *Der fliegende Holländer* (1843) and *Tannhäuser* (1845) in Dresden.
- **May 1849** — Participates in failed May Uprising in Dresden; flees into exile (Zürich).
- **1849–1852** — Publishes major theoretical essays: "The Artwork of the Future," "Judaism in Music," "Opera and Drama."
- **1848–1852** — Drafts and completes libretti for the *Ring* cycle (*Das Rheingold*, *Die Walküre*, *Siegfried*, *Götterdämmerung*).
- **1854** — Reads Schopenhauer’s *The World as Will and Representation*; calls it the most important event of his life.
- **1857–1864** — Interrupts *Ring* composition to write *Tristan und Isolde* and *Die Meistersinger von Nürnberg*.
- **1864** — King Ludwig II of Bavaria becomes patron; settles Wagner’s debts and funds productions in Munich.
- **10 Jun 1865** — *Tristan und Isolde* premieres in Munich (conducted by Hans von Bülow).
- **1866–1870** — Lives at Villa Tribschen (Lucerne); completes *Die Meistersinger*; Cosima von Bülow leaves husband for Wagner; they marry 25 Aug 1870.
- **1871** — Chooses Bayreuth for festival theatre; town donates "Green Hill" site.
- **13 Aug 1876** — Bayreuth Festspielhaus opens with first complete *Ring* cycle (conducted by Hans Richter).
- **26 May 1882** — *Parsifal* premieres at second Bayreuth Festival.
- **13 Feb 1883** — Dies of heart attack in Venice, aged 69; buried at Villa Wahnfried, Bayreuth.

## Body

### Early Life and Musical Awakening
Wagner was born in Leipzig to a police clerk, Carl Friedrich Wagner, who died six months later. His mother Johanna then lived with actor-playwright Ludwig Geyer (whom Wagner believed was his biological father until age fourteen), moving to Dresden. Geyer’s theatrical influence sparked Wagner’s early ambition as a playwright; his first work, the tragedy *Leubald* (1826), imitated Shakespeare and Goethe. Hearing Weber conduct *Der Freischütz* (1821) and Beethoven’s 7th and 9th Symphonies (1828) redirected him toward music. Studies with Christian Gottlieb Müller (harmony) and Theodor Weinlig (composition) followed; Weinlig published Wagner’s Piano Sonata in B-flat as Op. 1. A performance by soprano Wilhelmine Schröder-Devrient in 1829 became Wagner’s ideal of music-drama fusion, igniting an "almost demonic fire."

### Apprenticeship, Marriage, and Paris (1833–1842)
Wagner held brief posts in Würzburg, Magdeburg, and Königsberg. His early operas *Die Feen* (1833, Weberian) and *Das Liebesverbot* (1836, after Shakespeare) failed commercially, leading to bankruptcy. He married Minna Planer in 1836; the marriage was tempestuous, marked by separations and her eventual depression. Fleeing creditors in Riga (1839), a stormy sea passage to London inspired *Der fliegende Holländer*. In Paris (1839–1842), Wagner survived by hack writing and arranging operas for Schlesinger, while completing *Rienzi* and *Der fliegende Holländer*. He also drafted early prose sketches for "music drama" (e.g., *A Pilgrimage to Beethoven*).

### Dresden and Revolutionary Exile (1842–1858)
*Rienzi*’s 1842 Dresden premiere brought Wagner the Royal Saxon Court Conductorship. He staged *Der fliegende Holländer* and *Tannhäuser* there, but his involvement with left-wing nationalists (August Röckel, Mikhail Bakunin) and radical thinkers (Proudhon, Feuerbach) led to his participation in the 1849 May Uprising. Warrants forced flight to Zürich. In exile, Franz Liszt premiered *Lohengrin* (1850) in Weimar. Wagner survived on a pension from Julie Ritter and later support from Otto Wesendonck. This period produced his foundational theoretical essays: *The Artwork of the Future* (1849) defined the *Gesamtkunstwerk*; *Judaism in Music* (1850) launched his public antisemitism; *Opera and Drama* (1851) outlined the music-dramatic principles for the *Ring*. He completed the *Ring* libretti (1848–1852) and began composing *Das Rheingold* (1853–54) and *Die Walküre* (1854–56).

### Schopenhauer, *Tristan*, and the Wesendonck Affair
Introduced to Arthur Schopenhauer’s philosophy by Georg Herwegh in 1854, Wagner adopted the view that music is a direct expression of the metaphysical Will, elevating music’s status above the drama—a shift from his earlier theory. Simultaneously, he fell in love with Mathilde Wesendonck, wife of his patron Otto. This passion inspired *Tristan und Isolde* (1857–59) and the *Wesendonck Lieder* (five songs, two explicitly "studies for Tristan"). Work on the *Ring* halted for twelve years. Wagner conducted in London (1855), meeting Queen Victoria. The affair collapsed in 1858 when Minna intercepted a letter; Wagner fled to Venice, then Paris (1859–61), where a revised *Tannhäuser* failed amid Jockey Club protests and anti-Austrian politics.

### Munich Patronage and *Ring* Completion (1862–1871)
The political ban lifted in 1862. In 1864, 18-year-old King Ludwig II of Bavaria, an ardent admirer, summoned Wagner to Munich, cleared his debts, and pledged to stage his works. *Tristan* premiered in 1865 (conducted by Hans von Bülow), delayed by bailiffs and the soprano’s hoarseness. Wagner began an affair with Bülow’s wife Cosima (Liszt’s daughter), producing three children (Isolde, Eva, Siegfried) before their 1870 marriage. The scandal forced Wagner’s departure from Munich (1865); Ludwig installed him at Villa Tribschen, Lucerne. There Wagner completed *Die Meistersinger* (1867) and resumed the *Ring*, finishing *Siegfried* and *Götterdämmerung* (1874). He republished "Judaism in Music" under his own name (1869), provoking protests.

### Bayreuth: The Festival Theatre (1871–1876)
Wagner chose Bayreuth for a dedicated festival house. The town donated land; "Wagner societies" raised funds; Ludwig finally provided a loan (1874) when the project neared collapse. The Festspielhaus, incorporating ideas from Gottfried Semper, introduced innovations: a sunken, covered orchestra pit (making the orchestra invisible) and a darkened auditorium. The family home, Wahnfried, was completed 1874. The first Bayreuth Festival (1876) presented the complete *Ring* in three cycles. Reactions ranged from Edvard Grieg’s "divinely composed" to *Le Figaro*’s "dream of a lunatic." Friedrich Nietzsche, previously a devotee, broke with Wagner over perceived German nationalist pandering. The festival ran a 150,000-mark deficit.

### Final Years and *Parsifal* (1876–1883)
Wagner composed *Parsifal* (1877–82), his only work written specifically for the Festspielhaus, designated a *Bühnenweihfestspiel* ("festival play for the consecration of the stage"). It blends the Holy Grail legend with Buddhist renunciation (via Schopenhauer). Financing remained precarious; Wagner sold rights to unpublished works (including *Siegfried Idyll*) to Schott. Late essays ("Religion and Art," 1880; "Heroism and Christianity," 1881) revealed a reactionary turn, aligning Christianity with German nationalism and reiterating antisemitism. *Parsifal* premiered 26 May 1882; Wagner, suffering severe angina, conducted the final performance unseen. He died of a heart attack in Venice on 13 February 1883.

### Operatic Output: Three Phases
**Early Works (to 1842):** *Die Feen*, *Das Liebesverbot*, *Rienzi*. Conventional "Grand Opera" style (Spontini/Meyerbeer influence); Wagner later disowned them.
**Romantic Operas (1843–1851):** *Der fliegende Holländer*, *Tannhäuser*, *Lohengrin*. Transitional works developing thematic handling and orchestration; earliest in the "Bayreuth canon."
**Music Dramas (1851–1882):**
- *Der Ring des Nibelungen* (1848–74): Four operas (*Das Rheingold*, *Die Walküre*, *Siegfried*, *Götterdämmerung*) based on Norse/Germanic myth (Edda, Volsunga Saga, Nibelungenlied). Uses *Stabreim* (alliterative verse). *Das Rheingold* approaches the *Gesamtkunstwerk* ideal (no arias); *Die Walküre* retains "operatic" moments (Siegmund’s aria). Later acts grow more chromatic and leitmotivically complex.
- *Tristan und Isolde* (1859): Harmonic language pushes tonality to breaking point (the "Tristan chord"); widely seen as the gateway to atonality.
- *Die Meistersinger von Nürnberg* (1868): Only mature comedy; warm humanity but strong nationalist/antisemitic overtones (Beckmesser).
- *Parsifal* (1882): "Diaphanous score"; idiosyncratic Christian imagery; controversial for eroticism and perceived ideological coding.

### Non-Operatic Music and Prose
Few non-operatic works: Symphony in C (age 19), *Faust* Overture, *Siegfried Idyll* (chamber piece, 1870, for Cosima’s birthday), *Wesendonck Lieder*, *Centennial March* (1876). Wagner wrote prolifically: autobiography *Mein Leben* (1870–80, private edition), political/philosophical essays, and voluminous correspondence (est. 10,000–12,000 items). Collected editions were censored by Wagner and later Cosima; a critical edition began in 2013.

## Terms
- ****Gesamtkunstwerk**** — "Total work of art"; Wagner’s ideal fusion of music, poetry, visual arts, dance, and stagecraft into a unified dramatic experience.
- ****Leitmotif**** — A short, recurring musical phrase associated with a specific character, place, idea, or object, used to structure the drama and reveal psychological depth.
- ****Music Drama**** — Wagner’s term (later rejected by him) for his mature operas, where continuous sung narrative replaces distinct arias/recitatives, and orchestra equals singers in dramatic importance.
- ****Stabreim**** — Highly alliterative rhyming verse-pairs used in old Germanic poetry; Wagner adopted this for the *Ring* libretti.
- ****Bayreuth Festspielhaus**** — Wagner’s custom-built festival theatre in Bayreuth (opened 1876), featuring a hidden orchestra pit and darkened auditorium to focus attention on the stage.
- ****Tristan Chord**** — The opening harmony of *Tristan und Isolde* (F–B–D♯–G♯); its ambiguous resolution symbolises the breakdown of traditional tonality.
- ****Bühnenweihfestspiel**** — "Festival play for the consecration of the stage"; Wagner’s designation for *Parsifal*, implying a quasi-ritual performance exclusive to Bayreuth.
- ****Volksgeist**** — "National spirit"; term used in "Judaism in Music" to argue Jews were alien to German culture and incapable of genuine artistic creation.
- ****Wahnfried**** — Wagner’s villa in Bayreuth (completed 1874), named for "delusion" (*Wahn*) and "peace" (*Friede*); now the Richard Wagner Museum.
- ****Bayreuth Canon**** — The ten mature operas (*Holländer*, *Tannhäuser*, *Lohengrin*, *Ring* cycle, *Tristan*, *Meistersinger*, *Parsifal*) staged at Bayreuth per Wagner’s wishes.

## Debates and open questions
- **Antisemitism in the operas:** Scholars dispute whether characters like Alberich, Mime, Beckmesser, and Klingsor function as antisemitic caricatures, despite lacking explicit Jewish identification in librettos.
- **Schopenhauer’s impact:** Debate continues on whether Schopenhauer caused Wagner to elevate music above drama in the later *Ring* operas, or merely validated an existing compositional trajectory.
- **Nazi appropriation:** The extent to which Wagner’s ideas (vs. Houston Stewart Chamberlain’s reinterpretations) directly influenced Nazi ideology remains contested; the Nazi hierarchy largely disliked the operas, but Hitler exploited them for propaganda.
- **Performance in Israel:** An informal ban on public Wagner performances in Israel persists due to antisemitism/Nazi associations, though court challenges and occasional concert performances occur.
- **Political interpretation:** The *Ring* is read variously as a socialist critique of capitalism (George Bernard Shaw), a reactionary nationalist myth, or a psychological/Jungian allegory (Robert Donington).
- **Authorship of *Mein Leben*:** The autobiography was heavily edited by Wagner and later suppressed by Cosima; the first unexpurgated German edition appeared only in 1963.
