# Photography

## Overview

Photography is the practice of creating images by recording light, either electronically via image sensors or chemically via light-sensitive materials like film. It emerged from centuries of optical and chemical discovery, fundamentally changing art, science, documentation, and communication. The medium evolved from early camera obscura observations through analog film processes to today’s dominant digital technologies, reshaping how humans perceive and preserve reality.

## Body

### Etymology and Early Concepts

The term *photography* derives from Greek φωτός (*phōtós*, “light”) and γραφή (*graphé*, “drawing”), meaning “drawing with light.” Though independently coined by multiple figures—including Hércules Florence in 1834 and John Herschel in 1839—the earliest known public use appeared in the German newspaper *Vossische Zeitung* on February 25, 1839. Pioneers like Niépce, Daguerre, and Talbot did not adopt the term, instead naming their methods heliography, daguerreotype, and calotype respectively.

### Precursor Technologies

Photography synthesizes two key historical developments: the camera obscura and light-sensitive chemistry. Ancient Chinese philosophers noted the phenomenon; Aristotle and Euclid described it mathematically around the 5th century BCE. Ibn al-Haytham (965–1040) provided the first correct geometric analysis of the camera obscura and explored afterimages. Renaissance painters adopted it for realistic rendering. Chemically, silver nitrate (discovered by Albertus Magnus) and silver chloride (by Georg Fabricius) laid groundwork for light-reactive materials. Thomas Wedgwood attempted to fix images in 1802 but failed due to lack of permanence.

### Invention and Early Processes

Nicéphore Niépce produced the first permanent photoetching in 1822 and the earliest surviving photograph—*View from the Window at Le Gras*—in 1826, requiring days-long exposure. Partnering with Louis Daguerre, Niépce improved sensitivity before dying in 1833. Daguerre shifted focus to silver halides, developing the daguerreotype by 1837. Announced publicly on January 7, 1839, it reduced exposure times to minutes. Around the same time, William Fox Talbot developed the calotype, producing paper negatives capable of generating multiple positive prints—a foundational principle for future photography.

### Development of Film and Emulsions

Frederick Scott Archer’s wet plate collodion process (1851) became dominant until gelatin dry plates replaced it in the 1870s. Gabriel Lippmann introduced natural-color photography via light interference in 1891, earning a Nobel Prize in 1908. Flexible roll film, introduced by Kodak in 1885, democratized amateur photography. Nitrate film gave way to safer cellulose acetate ("safety film") starting in 1908. Hurter and Driffield’s work on emulsion sensitivity led to standardized film speed measurements.

### Color Photography

James Clerk Maxwell demonstrated the three-color separation method in 1861, forming the basis of all practical color processes. Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii applied this technique extensively in Russia. Dye sensitization by Hermann Vogel (1873) expanded spectral sensitivity. The Lumière brothers’ Autochrome (1907) was the first commercially viable color process. Kodachrome (1935) and Agfacolor Neu (1936) refined multilayer emulsions. Instant color film arrived with Polaroid in 1963. Color film remained prevalent until digital displaced it in the early 21st century.

### Digital Photography

Sony’s Mavica (1981) used a CCD but stored analog video signals. The first true digital camera, Fujifilm’s DS-1P (1988), saved images digitally. Kodak’s DCS 100 (1991) marked the birth of commercial digital photography. Unlike chemical photography, digital allows extensive post-processing. Today, over 99% of photographs are taken digitally, primarily via smartphones.

### Techniques and Applications

Cameras range from pinhole devices to advanced digital systems. Specialized techniques include stereoscopy (3D imaging), dual photography, ultraviolet/infrared capture, light field imaging, and photogrammetry. Photography serves diverse fields: art, journalism, science, forensics, commerce, and personal expression.

### Social and Cultural Impact

Susan Sontag critiqued photography’s power to objectify and manipulate perception. Ethical debates persist regarding manipulation, privacy, and desensitization. Photography influences tourism (“tourist gaze”), law enforcement, and legal frameworks governing rights and restrictions.

## Terms

- **Camera Obscura**: A darkened chamber or box with a small aperture projecting an inverted external image.
- **Latent Image**: An invisible photographic image formed by exposure but not yet developed.
- **Negative**: A photographic image where tones are reversed, used to produce positive prints.
- **Daguerreotype**: An early direct-positive process on a silver-coated copper plate, announced in 1839.
- **Calotype**: A paper-based negative-positive process invented by William Fox Talbot, patented in 1841.
- **Collodion Process**: A wet-plate technique using collodion as a binder for light-sensitive salts.
- **Film Speed**: A measure of a film’s sensitivity to light, quantified by systems like ISO.
- **Color Separation**: Capturing an image through red, green, and blue filters to reconstruct full color.
- **Digital Sensor**: An electronic device (e.g., CCD or CMOS) converting light into electrical signals.
- **Post-Processing**: Altering captured images after recording, common in digital workflows.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Coinage of “Photography”**: Whether Hércules Florence, John Herschel, or others should be credited with coining the term remains debated.
- **Inventor of Photography**: Claims vary among Niépce, Daguerre, Talbot, and Florence, depending on criteria such as permanence, reproducibility, and recognition.
- **Artistic Status**: Historically contested whether photography qualifies as fine art; resolved gradually through gallery acceptance and critical discourse.
- **Ethical Manipulation**: Ongoing tension between documentary integrity and creative freedom in photojournalism and digital editing.
- **Cultural Representation**: Photography’s role in shaping identity, power dynamics, and cross-cultural perception continues to evolve with technology and globalization.
