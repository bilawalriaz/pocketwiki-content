# Kingdom of Armenia (antiquity)

## Overview
The Kingdom of Greater Armenia was an ancient state in the Armenian Highlands that existed from 331 BC to 428 AD. It was a significant power in the Ancient Near East, often serving as a crucial buffer state between the Roman and Parthian (and later Sassanid) empires. Its history is marked by periods of imperial expansion, particularly under Tigranes the Great, and its adoption of Christianity as a state religion in 301 AD, making it the first country to do so.

## Timeline
- **331 BC** — The Orontid dynasty establishes the Kingdom of Armenia after Alexander the Great's conquest of Persia.
- **189 BC** — The Artaxiad dynasty takes power after the Seleucid Empire's defeat by Rome.
- **83–69 BC** — Tigranes the Great expands Armenia into a vast empire, ending the Seleucid Empire.
- **66 BC** — Armenia becomes a Roman client kingdom after Pompey's defeat of Mithridates VI of Pontus.
- **52 AD** — Tiridates I of the Parthian Arsacid dynasty is proclaimed King of Armenia.
- **63 AD** — Treaty of Rhandeia establishes that Armenian kings will be from the Arsacid dynasty but crowned as clients of Rome.
- **252 AD** — The Sassanid Empire occupies Armenia.
- **287 AD** — Tiridates III is restored to power with Roman support.
- **301 AD** — Tiridates III proclaims Christianity as the state religion of Armenia.
- **387 AD** — The Kingdom of Armenia is partitioned between the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) and Sassanid Empires.
- **428 AD** — The last Arsacid king is deposed, ending independent Armenian statehood until the 9th century.

## Body

### Origins and the Orontid Dynasty
The kingdom's roots lie in the Satrapy of Armenia, a province of the Achaemenid Persian Empire formed from the territory of the earlier Kingdom of Urartu. After Alexander the Great's conquest, the Orontid dynasty, who had served as Achaemenid satraps, became independent kings in 321 BC. The dynasty was eventually overthrown around 200 BC by Artaxias I, a Seleucid commander who founded the Artaxiad dynasty.

### The Artaxiad Dynasty and Imperial Zenith
The Artaxiad period saw Armenia reach its greatest territorial extent. After the Seleucid Empire was weakened by Rome, Artaxias I reunited the Armenian Highlands. His grandson, Tigranes the Great, created a short-lived empire from 83 to 69 BC by conquering the remnants of the Seleucid Empire and other neighboring territories. After Tigranes' defeat by Rome, the Artaxiad kings ruled as Roman clients until their overthrow in 12 AD.

### The Arsacid Dynasty and Roman-Parthian Contention
Following the Artaxiad overthrow, the Arsacid dynasty of Armenia was founded in 52 AD by Tiridates I, a member of the Parthian Arsacid royal house. For most of this period, Armenia was a contested buffer zone between Rome and Parthia. The Treaty of Rhandeia (63 AD) formalized a compromise: Armenian kings would be from the Parthian Arsacid line but would be crowned as clients of Rome. This arrangement was periodically disrupted by wars, such as the Roman-Parthian War of 55–63 AD and the campaign of Emperor Lucius Verus in 162–165 AD.

### Conversion to Christianity and Final Partition
In the early 4th century, King Tiridates III converted to Christianity, traditionally in 301 AD, making Armenia the first state to adopt it officially. This event culturally distinguished Armenia from its Zoroastrian Persian neighbors. The kingdom's final decades were marked by partition. In 387 AD, it was divided between the Byzantine and Sassanid Empires. The last Arsacid king was deposed in 428 AD, ending the kingdom's independence until the Bagratid dynasty emerged in the 9th century.

## Terms
- **Satrapy:** A province of the ancient Achaemenid (Persian) Empire, governed by a satrap.
- **Hellenistic Kingdoms:** States influenced by Greek culture that emerged after the death of Alexander the Great.
- **Client Kingdom:** A state that maintains nominal independence but is under the political, economic, and military control of a larger empire.
- **Buffer Zone:** A territory lying between two larger, often rival, powers, serving to reduce direct conflict.
- **Cataphract:** A heavily armored cavalryman, a unit common in ancient Near Eastern armies.
- **Nakharar:** The hereditary Armenian nobility who held political and military power.
- **Marzpan:** A governor of a frontier province in the Sassanid Empire.
- **Interpretatio Graeca:** The Greek practice of identifying foreign gods with their own (e.g., equating Aramazd with Zeus).

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact date of Armenia's conversion to Christianity is traditionally 301, but some scholars debate whether it occurred later, around 314.
- The reliability of ancient sources (like Josephus) regarding the size of Tigranes the Great's army is questioned, with numbers likely being exaggerated.
- The extent to which the Armenian nobility was divided between pro-Roman and pro-Parthian factions during the Arsacid period is a subject of ongoing historical analysis.
