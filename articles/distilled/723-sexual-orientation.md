# Sexual orientation

## Overview

Sexual orientation refers to an enduring pattern of romantic and/or sexual attraction to others, typically categorized as heterosexuality, homosexuality, bisexuality, and sometimes asexuality. It is distinct from sexual identity (self-conception) and sexual behavior (actions), though these may or may not align. Scientific consensus holds that sexual orientation is not a choice and is influenced by a combination of biological factors—particularly genetics, prenatal hormones, and fetal brain development—with little to no evidence supporting social or environmental causes such as parenting or childhood experiences. The concept is studied across multiple disciplines, including biology, psychology, anthropology, sociology, and law, and remains subject to scholarly debate regarding its definition, measurement, and cultural interpretation.

## Timeline

- **1860s** — Karl Heinrich Ulrichs proposes early classification of sexual orientation (dionings, urnings, urano-dionings).
- **1896** — Magnus Hirschfeld introduces a two-scale (A/B) model measuring homosexual and heterosexual desire independently.
- **1948** — Alfred Kinsey publishes *Sexual Behavior in the Human Male*, introducing the Kinsey Scale (0–6).
- **1953** — Kinsey extends the scale in *Sexual Behavior in the Human Female*.
- **1978** — Fritz Klein publishes the Klein Sexual Orientation Grid (KSOG), adding multidimensional and temporal analysis.
- **1990s** — Ray Blanchard and Anthony Bogaert identify the fraternal birth order effect linked to maternal immune response.
- **2001** — Twin study suggests genes are not a major factor in sexual orientation.
- **2010** — Twin study finds homosexuality influenced by both genes and environment.
- **2012** — Genome-wide linkage study identifies associations on chromosomes Xq28 and 8.
- **2014** — Large-scale genetic study published, supporting polygenic influence on male sexual orientation.
- **2017** — Biochemical evidence supports the maternal immunization hypothesis (anti-NLGN4Y antibodies).
- **2019** — Genome-wide association study of 493,001 individuals finds hundreds of genetic variants linked to same-sex behavior.

## Body

### Definitions and Distinguishing Features

Sexual orientation traditionally includes heterosexuality, homosexuality, and bisexuality, with asexuality sometimes classified as a fourth category. It involves enduring patterns of attraction—emotional, romantic, and/or sexual—and often includes a sense of identity and community belonging. The American Psychological Association defines it as an enduring pattern of emotional, romantic, and/or sexual attractions to men, women, or both sexes.

Sexual orientation differs from sexual identity (one’s internal sense of self) and sexual behavior (actual sexual acts). These components may not align; for instance, a person might be attracted to the same sex but identify as heterosexual and engage only in opposite-sex relationships—a state referred to as *discordance*. The term *closeted* describes individuals whose non-heterosexual orientation does not match their outward identity or behavior.

The term *sexual preference* overlaps with sexual orientation but implies voluntary choice, which the APA discourages due to its heteronormative bias. John Money introduced "sexual orientation" to replace "sexual preference," emphasizing that attraction is not a matter of free will.

### Alternative Terminologies: Androphilia and Gynephilia

Androphilia (attraction to masculinity) and gynephilia (attraction to femininity) offer alternatives to gender-binary terms like homosexual and heterosexual. These terms are particularly useful in describing transgender and intersex individuals without assigning sex-based labels. For example, a trans man attracted to men could be described as androphilic, avoiding confusion over whether he is classified as a heterosexual woman or homosexual man.

These terms also help navigate cultural contexts where Western categories like "homosexual" do not apply. Sociologist Johanna Schmidt notes that Samoan fa'afafine individuals resist classification under Western sexual orientation frameworks. Critics like Bruce Bagemihl argue that applying "heterosexual" or "homosexual" to trans people based on assigned sex at birth ignores their gender identity.

### Gender, Transgender Identity, and Conformance

Historically, sexual orientation was viewed as tied to the individual’s own sex, with gender-nonconforming traits assumed to accompany same-sex attraction. This view, held by figures like Freud and Hirschfeld, framed homosexuality as "sexual inversion." However, by the late 20th century, gender identity became recognized as distinct from sexual orientation.

Transgender and cisgender individuals can be attracted to any gender. Studies show that many gay men and lesbians exhibited cross-sex-typed behaviors in childhood. Non-binary understandings of sex and gender add complexity, prompting calls for more nuanced models that incorporate fluidity and multiple dimensions beyond binary constructs.

### Fluidity and Stability

Sexual orientation is generally stable for most people, but some experience shifts over time, especially women. The APA distinguishes between sexual orientation (innate attraction) and sexual orientation identity (which may evolve). Identity can change due to personal growth, social context, or life experiences, even if underlying attractions remain consistent.

### Causes of Sexual Orientation

#### Biological Factors

Biological theories dominate current scientific thinking. Research points to genetic influences, prenatal hormone exposure, and brain structure differences. No single cause has been identified, but evidence supports a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors.

**Genetic Factors**: Twin studies show mixed results. A 2001 study suggested genes play a minor role, while a 2010 study found both genetic and shared environmental influences. A 2012 genome-wide linkage study linked male homosexuality to regions on Xq28 and chromosome 8. A 2019 study of nearly half a million individuals found hundreds of genetic variants associated with same-sex behavior, though no single gene accounts for a large effect.

**Hormonal Influences**: The hormonal theory posits that prenatal exposure to androgens affects fetal brain development, shaping later sexual orientation. Since the default pathway is female, Y-chromosome-driven androgen production shifts development toward male-typical patterns.

**Birth Order Effect**: The fraternal birth order (FBO) effect shows increasing likelihood of homosexuality in males with each older brother from the same mother. This is attributed to the maternal immunization hypothesis: maternal antibodies against Y-linked proteins (e.g., NLGN4Y) accumulate with each male pregnancy, potentially altering brain masculinization. The effect increases odds by 38–48% per older brother and may account for 15–29% of gay male cases. Left-handedness nullifies the effect.

#### Social Hypotheses

There is no credible evidence that parenting styles, childhood trauma, or early experiences determine sexual orientation. Past psychoanalytic theories linking homosexuality to poor parenting or abuse have been discredited as rooted in prejudice rather than science.

### Assessment and Measurement

Measuring sexual orientation is challenging due to definitional ambiguity and social norms. Three primary components are assessed:

1. **Attraction**: Feelings of sexual or romantic interest.
2. **Behavior**: Actual sexual acts.
3. **Identity**: Self-labeling (e.g., gay, straight).

These dimensions rarely align perfectly. Surveys show that attraction-based estimates of same-sex orientation are higher than behavior- or identity-based ones. For example, assessing attraction yields twice the prevalence of homosexuality compared to identity labels.

#### Historical Models

- **Kinsey Scale (0–6)**: Measures orientation along a continuum from exclusively heterosexual (0) to exclusively homosexual (6). Praised for rejecting binaries, criticized for conflating attraction and behavior.
- **Klein Grid (KSOG)**: Expands on Kinsey by evaluating seven dimensions across past, present, and ideal timelines.
- **SASO (Sell Assessment)**: Separates attraction, behavior, and identity into distinct scales, prioritizing attraction as the core indicator.

### Cultural Perspectives

Cross-cultural research indicates that sexual orientation itself is largely independent of culture, but expression and identification vary widely. Western societies emphasize identity labels (gay, lesbian), while some cultures define sexuality through roles (active/passive) rather than orientation. Language barriers complicate cross-cultural comparisons, and terms like "homosexual" may lack equivalents elsewhere.

Perceptions of sexual orientation influence treatment and policy. In the U.S., 15.6% of hate crimes in 2004 were motivated by sexual orientation bias. Anti-discrimination laws protect perceived orientation in many jurisdictions.

### Demographics

Western surveys suggest ~93% of men and ~87% of women identify as completely heterosexual, with small percentages identifying as bisexual or homosexual. Lifetime prevalence of same-sex behavior varies globally, from 3–5% in East Asia to 6–20% in Latin America. Asexuality affects roughly 1% of the population.

### Social Constructionism

Some scholars, following Michel Foucault, argue that sexual orientation is a historically constructed identity rather than a fixed trait. Foucault contended that "homosexuality" as an identity emerged in the 18th century alongside state surveillance and capitalist norms. Others counter that same-sex attraction has existed across cultures and epochs, differing mainly in labeling and social acceptance.

### Law, Politics, and Theology

Legal and theological debates often hinge on whether sexual orientation is innate or chosen. Scientific consensus supports its biological basis, undermining arguments that it reflects moral failing or lifestyle choice. Legal scholars note that definitions of sexual orientation must suit specific contexts (e.g., employment law vs. marriage rights).

## Terms

- **Sexual Orientation**: Enduring pattern of romantic/sexual attraction; distinct from identity and behavior.
- **Sexual Identity**: One’s internal sense of self regarding sexuality.
- **Discordance**: Mismatch between orientation, identity, and/or behavior.
- **Closeted**: Person whose orientation conflicts with public identity or behavior.
- **Androphilia/Gynephilia**: Attraction to masculinity/femininity; alternatives to gender-binary terms.
- **Fraternal Birth Order Effect**: Increased chance of homosexuality in males with older brothers.
- **Maternal Immunization Hypothesis**: Maternal antibodies affect fetal brain development.
- **Kinsey Scale**: 0–6 continuum measuring heterosexual-to-homosexual orientation.
- **Klein Grid (KSOG)**: Multidimensional assessment of orientation across time.
- **Social Constructionism**: Theory that sexual orientation is culturally and historically constructed.

## Debates and Open Questions

Key unresolved issues include:

- **Definition**: Whether asexuality qualifies as a sexual orientation or represents absence thereof.
- **Causation**: Despite strong biological evidence, no single mechanism explains all variation in sexual orientation.
- **Measurement**: Which component—attraction, behavior, or identity—is most valid for research and clinical use.
- **Stability**: Extent to which orientation changes over time, particularly among women.
- **Cultural Universality**: Whether Western categories adequately capture global diversity in sexual expression.
- **Ethical Implications**: How scientific findings should inform policies on conversion therapy, anti-discrimination protections, and religious freedom.
