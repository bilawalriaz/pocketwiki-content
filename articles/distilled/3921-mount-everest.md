# Mount Everest

## Overview
Mount Everest, known as Sagarmāthā in Nepal and Qomolangma in Tibet, is the highest mountain on Earth above sea level, standing at 8,848.86 m (29,031 ft 8+1⁄2 in). It is a major climbing destination that attracts both expert mountaineers and commercial expeditions, but its extreme altitude, harsh weather, and the "death zone" above 8,000 m make it exceptionally dangerous. The mountain's history involves early surveying, pioneering ascents, and ongoing debates about commercialization, safety, and environmental impact.

## Timeline
- **1856** — Peak XV (Everest) is announced as the world's highest peak at 8,840 m (29,002 ft) by the Great Trigonometrical Survey.
- **1921** — First British reconnaissance expedition reaches 7,000 m (22,966 ft) on the North Col.
- **1922** — First expedition to climb above 8,000 m (26,247 ft), reaching 8,321 m (27,300 ft).
- **1924** — George Mallory and Andrew Irvine disappear during a summit attempt, sparking debate about whether they reached the top.
- **1953** — Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay make the first documented ascent via the Southeast Ridge.
- **1960** — First reported ascent from the North Ridge by a Chinese team.
- **1975** — Junko Tabei becomes the first woman to summit.
- **1978** — Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler make the first ascent without supplemental oxygen.
- **1980** — First winter ascent by a Polish team.
- **1996** — Eight climbers die in a blizzard, highlighting dangers of commercial guiding.
- **2014** — An avalanche kills 16 Nepali guides, the deadliest single event on the mountain at the time.
- **2015** — An earthquake triggers an avalanche that kills 18 at Base Camp; no summits occur for the first time since 1974.
- **2020** — Nepal and China jointly announce the new official height of 8,848.86 m.

## Body

### Naming and Surveys
The mountain was identified as the world's highest during the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India. In 1856, Surveyor General Andrew Waugh announced it as Peak XV, naming it after his predecessor, Sir George Everest, despite local names like Sagarmāthā and Qomolangma being in use. The height has been measured multiple times, with the most recent joint survey in 2020 establishing the official elevation as 8,848.86 m. The mountain is geologically composed of three formations, with its summit rock being Ordovician limestone containing marine fossils, a fact recognized by the International Union of Geological Sciences.

### Early Expeditions and First Ascent
British expeditions in the 1920s pioneered the North Ridge route from Tibet. The first confirmed successful ascent was made in 1953 by Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay via the Southeast Ridge in Nepal. This route became the standard after China closed the Tibetan border in 1950. The 1960 Chinese ascent from the North Ridge established the second main route.

### Climbing Routes and Dangers
The two main routes are the Southeast Ridge (Nepal) and the North Ridge (Tibet). The Southeast Ridge involves crossing the dangerous Khumbu Icefall and ascending through the "death zone" above 8,000 m, where oxygen levels are about a third of sea level, leading to risks of altitude sickness, frostbite, and exhaustion. Most climbers use supplemental oxygen, though about 5% ascend without it. The Hillary Step on the Southeast Ridge and the Second Step on the North Ridge are key technical obstacles.

### Commercialization and Modern Climbing
Since the 1990s, commercial guiding has made the summit accessible to less experienced climbers for fees ranging from $35,000 to over $200,000. This has led to overcrowding, as seen in the 1996 disaster and the 2019 season with record numbers of summits and deaths. Debates continue about the ethics of guiding inexperienced clients and the environmental impact, including waste management issues like human excrement and abandoned equipment.

### Significant Disasters and Events
The 1996 disaster, where eight climbers died in a blizzard, brought global attention to the risks of commercial expeditions. The 2014 avalanche and 2015 earthquake were the deadliest events, killing 16 and 18 people respectively. Other notable incidents include the 2006 death of David Sharp, which sparked debate about climber ethics, and the 2019 season marked by deadly crowding.

## Terms
- **Death Zone**: Altitudes above 8,000 m (26,000 ft) where oxygen pressure is insufficient to sustain human life for extended periods.
- **Khumbu Icefall**: A treacherous, constantly shifting glacier section on the Southeast Ridge route, requiring ladders and ropes to navigate.
- **Supplemental Oxygen**: Bottled oxygen used by most climbers above 8,000 m to mitigate the effects of hypoxia.
- **Summit Fever**: A psychological state where a climber's desire to reach the summit overrides judgment about safety and turnaround times.
- **Geoid**: The shape of the Earth's surface that best fits mean sea level, used as the reference for measuring mountain height.
- **Trigonometrical Survey**: A method of determining positions and elevations of points using angles measured from known locations, used to first measure Everest.
- **Sagarmāthā**: Nepali name for Everest, meaning "goddess of the sky."
- **Qomolangma**: Tibetan name for Everest, meaning "holy mother."

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Mallory and Irvine**: Whether George Mallory and Andrew Irvine reached the summit in 1924 before their deaths remains unconfirmed and is a subject of ongoing speculation.
- **Commercialization Ethics**: There is significant debate about the morality of guiding inexperienced climbers up Everest, with critics arguing it endangers lives and degrades the mountain's spirit, while proponents note economic benefits for Nepal.
- **Bottled Oxygen**: The use of supplemental oxygen is controversial; purists argue it allows unqualified climbers to attempt the summit, while others see it as a necessary safety tool.
- **Height Measurement**: While the 2020 joint survey established a new official height, the mountain's elevation is dynamic due to tectonic activity, and the exact measurement from rock versus snow cap has been a point of past disagreement between Nepal and China.
