# Claude Bernard

## Overview
Claude Bernard (1813–1878) was a foundational French physiologist whose experimental work established key principles of modern biology and medicine. He is most famous for originating the concept of the *milieu intérieur* (internal environment), the theoretical basis for homeostasis, and for his discoveries in digestion, metabolism, and the nervous system. His rigorous experimental methodology and advocacy for the scientific method in medicine made him a pivotal figure in the transition from speculative to evidence-based physiology.

## Timeline
- **12 July 1813** — Born in Saint-Julien, near Villefranche-sur-Saône, France.
- **1834** — Moves to Paris to pursue medicine after being dissuaded from a literary career.
- **1841** — Becomes a laboratory assistant (*préparateur*) at the Collège de France under François Magendie.
- **1847** — Appointed deputy-professor at the Collège de France.
- **1855** — Succeeds Magendie as full professor of physiology.
- **1857** — First describes carbon monoxide's affinity for hemoglobin.
- **1865** — Publishes *An Introduction to the Study of Experimental Medicine*, outlining his scientific philosophy.
- **1868** — Accepts a professorship at the Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle; admitted to the Académie Française.
- **10 February 1878** — Dies in Paris; receives a rare public state funeral.

## Body

### Early Life and Career Path
Bernard initially pursued literature but was advised to study medicine. He became an intern at the Hôtel-Dieu de Paris, where he met the influential physiologist François Magendie. This connection led to a position as Magendie's laboratory assistant and later his successor at the Collège de France. His marriage in 1845 was arranged for financial convenience, providing dowry funds for his experiments.

### Major Scientific Discoveries
Bernard's research focused on the digestive and nervous systems. He discovered the pancreas's crucial digestive role, identifying a fluid containing a lipolytic enzyme that breaks down fats. His most famous work revealed the liver's glycogenic function—its ability to produce sugar, which advanced the study of diabetes. He also discovered the vasomotor system, demonstrating that nerves control blood vessel dilation and constriction, thereby regulating circulation.

### Concept of the *Milieu Intérieur*
Bernard's central theoretical contribution was the concept of the *milieu intérieur*, or "internal environment." He argued that complex organisms maintain a stable, independent internal physiological state despite external environmental changes. This principle of physiological balance is the direct precursor to the term "homeostasis," coined later by Walter Cannon.

### Methodology and Philosophy
Bernard championed the experimental method in medicine, emphasizing that evidence must override prevailing theories. In his 1865 book, he argued that theories are merely hypotheses to be tested by facts and criticized scientists for cherry-picking data. His work relied heavily on vivisection, a practice that caused personal and professional controversy, including opposition from his own family and some colleagues.

## Terms
- **Milieu intérieur**: The "internal environment" of the body, a stable physiological state that Bernard argued is relatively independent of the external world.
- **Homeostasis**: The state of steady internal, physical, and chemical conditions maintained by living systems; a term coined by Walter Cannon based on Bernard's concept.
- **Glycogenic function**: The liver's ability to produce and store sugar (glycogen), a discovery critical to understanding metabolism and diabetes.
- **Vasomotor system**: The system of nerves that controls the constriction or dilation of blood vessels, regulating blood flow and pressure.
- **Lipolytic enzyme**: An enzyme that breaks down lipids (fats); Bernard identified such an enzyme in pancreatic secretions.
- **Vivisection**: Surgical experimentation on living animals, a primary but controversial method used by Bernard.
- **Experimental medicine**: An approach to medicine based on rigorous, objective experimentation and the scientific method, as advocated by Bernard.

## Debates and Open Questions
Bernard's glycogenic theory, which posited that the liver synthesizes sugar, was directly challenged by physiologist Frederick Pavy, who argued that blood sugar originated from dietary intake rather than hepatic synthesis. This debate persisted for decades until biochemical evidence confirmed Bernard's interpretation. Later research by von Mering and Minkowski on pancreatic diabetes further refined and validated his conclusions about glucose regulation.
