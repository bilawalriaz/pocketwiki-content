# Lute

## Overview
The lute is a plucked string instrument with a neck and a deep, round back, historically significant as the premier solo and accompanying instrument in European music from the Medieval to Baroque eras. Its evolution from ancient Near Eastern prototypes into a complex family of instruments reflects centuries of cultural exchange and musical innovation.

## Timeline
- **c. 3100 BC** — Earliest known depiction of a lute-like instrument on a Mesopotamian cylinder seal.
- **224–651** — The Sasanian Empire develops the short-necked barbat lute in Bactria/Gandhara.
- **711** — The Moors bring the oud (from the barbat) to Andalusia, Spain.
- **1140** — Lutes are depicted in the ceiling paintings of Palermo's Cappella Palatina.
- **Late 15th C.** — Earliest surviving lute music appears in Italian manuscripts.
- **c. 1500** — Lutenists begin transitioning from plectrum to finger-plucking, enabling complex polyphony.
- **16th C.** — The lute becomes the premier solo instrument; the vihuela coexists in Iberia.
- **Early 17th C.** — French lutenists develop new tunings and the *style brisé* (broken style).
- **c. 1670** — The "Baroque" or D-minor tuning scheme becomes standard in much of Europe.
- **1800** — The lute falls out of common use, superseded by keyboard instruments.
- **Early 20th C.** — A revival of interest in early music begins, led by pioneers like Arnold Dolmetsch.
- **Late 20th C.** — The early music movement boosts lute performance and historically informed construction.

## Body

### Origins and Early History
The lute family is defined by a body and a neck that stretches the strings. Musicologist Curt Sachs distinguished between ancient long-necked lutes (like the tanbur) and the short-necked variety that became the ancestor of the European lute. Archaeological evidence, including a c. 3100 BC Mesopotamian seal, pushes the lute's existence back millennia. The crucial development occurred in Bactria and Gandhara, where a short, almond-shaped lute emerged. This Gandharan lute, with its pear-shaped body and lateral pegs, is the "venerable ancestor" of the Islamic, Sino-Japanese, and European lute families.

### Transmission to Europe
The short-necked lute entered Europe via two main routes. The Moors introduced the oud to Spain in 711, where it merged with existing Roman traditions. The instrument also spread through Sicily, brought by Byzantine or Muslim musicians, and was depicted in Norman royal art by 1140. By the 14th century, lutes had spread throughout Italy and German-speaking lands. The lute's construction and repertoire were further shaped by its adoption in courts and by the development of specific regional schools.

### Evolution from Medieval to Baroque
Medieval lutes were small, four- or five-course instruments plucked with a quill. By the late 15th century, players switched to finger-plucking to perform polyphonic music, leading to an increase in courses (pairs of strings). The lute was the dominant solo instrument of the Renaissance. During the Baroque era, the number of courses grew to 14 or more, requiring structural innovations like extended necks for bass strings (theorbo, archlute). The lute's role gradually shifted to basso continuo accompaniment before falling out of favor after 1800.

### Construction and Design
Lutes are made almost entirely of wood. The soundboard (belly) is a thin, resonant spruce plate with a decorated sound hole called a "rose." The back (shell) is a deep, rounded bowl assembled from thin hardwood ribs. The neck is light wood with an ebony fretboard, and the pegbox is typically angled back. Frets are loops of gut tied around the neck. Strings were historically gut, now often nylon or synthetic, arranged in courses. The highest course (chanterelle) is usually a single string. The lute's design makes it extremely light for its size.

### Tuning and Repertoire
Lute tuning was complex and varied by era, region, and instrument size. A standard 6-course Renaissance lute was tuned in fourths with a major third between the third and fourth courses. By the Baroque era, the D-minor tuning scheme became common. The repertoire includes intabulations of vocal works, dances, and complex polyphonic fantasias. Key composers include Francesco da Milano (Renaissance), John Dowland (English), and Silvius Leopold Weiss (Baroque). Much music was improvised and never written down.

### Modern Revival
The lute was revived in the 20th century through the early music movement. Pioneers like Julian Bream and Diana Poulton spurred interest. Initially, lutes were built like heavy classical guitars, but scholarship led to lighter, historically accurate replicas. Today, many types of historical lutes are played by professional lutenists, and new compositions are being written for the instrument.

## Terms
- **Course**: A pair of strings on a lute, tuned in unison or octaves. The highest course is often a single string (chanterelle).
- **Fret**: A loop of gut tied around the neck, which the player presses the string against to change its pitch.
- **Luthier**: A maker of lutes and other stringed instruments.
- **Oud**: The fretless, short-necked lute of the Middle East, ancestor of the European lute.
- **Rose**: The decorated sound hole carved into the lute's soundboard.
- **Style brisé**: A French Baroque lute technique using broken, arpeggiated textures.
- **Tablature (tab)**: A form of musical notation indicating finger placement on the instrument's neck rather than specific pitches.
- **Theorbo/Archlute**: Large Baroque lutes with extended necks and extra bass strings for continuo playing.
- **Vihuela**: A guitar-shaped instrument from Renaissance Spain, tuned like a lute.

## Debates and open questions
- The precise etymology of "lute" and "oud" is debated, with theories linking it to Arabic, Persian, Sanskrit, or Syriac roots.
- The exact point at which the short-necked lute lineage diverged from the long-necked lute in the ancient Near East is not fully resolved.
- The extent to which specific composers like Johann Sebastian Bach wrote idiomatically for the lute versus the keyboard *lautenwerk* (lute-harpsichord) is a matter of scholarly discussion.
