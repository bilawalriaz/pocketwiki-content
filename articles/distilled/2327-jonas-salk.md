# Jonas Salk

## Overview
Jonas Salk was an American virologist who developed one of the first successful polio vaccines, a breakthrough that led to the near-eradication of the disease. His work, and his decision not to patent the vaccine, made him a global public figure and established a model for large-scale public health initiatives.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Salk was born to Jewish immigrant parents in New York City. He attended the competitive Townsend Harris Hall Prep School before enrolling at the City College of New York (CCNY), where he earned a chemistry degree in 1934. He then attended New York University School of Medicine, where he became focused on research rather than clinical practice, taking a year to study biochemistry. His interest in virology began during a postgraduate elective in Thomas Francis' laboratory at the University of Michigan, where he later worked on an influenza vaccine for the army.

### Polio Research and Vaccine Development
In 1947, Salk secured a professorship at the University of Pittsburgh. In 1948, the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis funded his project to determine if there were more than three types of poliovirus. Salk chose to develop a vaccine using a "killed" (inactivated) virus, which he believed was safer than the live, attenuated virus approach being pursued by others like Albert Sabin. After successful animal tests, he conducted the first human trials in 1952. A massive 1954 field trial involving about one million children, known as the "polio pioneers," confirmed the vaccine's efficacy. It was announced as safe on April 12, 1955, leading to immediate global vaccination campaigns. By 1959, the vaccine was in use in about 90 countries, and domestic transmission of polio was eliminated in the U.S. within 25 years.

### Public Figure and the Salk Institute
The vaccine's success made Salk an instant celebrity, a status he found burdensome. When asked who owned the patent, he famously replied, "Could you patent the sun?" Lawyers had determined the vaccine was not patentable due to prior art. In 1963, he founded the Salk Institute for Biological Studies in La Jolla, California, designed by architect Louis Kahn. The institute was conceived as a "Socratic academy" to foster collaboration between scientists, humanists, and artists. Salk continued research there on cancer and autoimmune diseases and, in his later years, worked on an AIDS vaccine.

### Biophilosophy and Later Work
Salk was known as the "Father of Biophilosophy," which he defined as applying a biological and evolutionary perspective to philosophical and social problems. He argued that humanity was shifting from a focus on death control to birth control and health. He authored several books on this theme, including *Man Unfolding* and *The Survival of the Wisest*. He remained a vigorous campaigner for mandatory childhood vaccination, calling it a "moral commitment."

## Terms
*   **Killed (inactivated) virus vaccine:** A vaccine containing virus particles that have been destroyed, so they cannot cause disease but can still provoke an immune response.
*   **Attenuated live virus vaccine:** A vaccine containing a weakened form of the virus that can replicate but does not cause serious illness.
*   **Polio pioneers:** The approximately one million children who participated in the 1954 field trial of the Salk vaccine.
*   **National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis:** The organization (later the March of Dimes) that funded Salk's polio research.
*   **Biophilosophy:** Salk's term for applying a biological, evolutionary viewpoint to philosophical, cultural, and social issues.
*   **Salk Institute for Biological Studies:** The research center founded by Salk in 1963 to investigate biological phenomena and foster interdisciplinary collaboration.

The Salk vaccine was used first and was instrumental in controlling polio, while the Sabin vaccine, coming into use in 1961, was easier to administer and helped in global eradication efforts.
