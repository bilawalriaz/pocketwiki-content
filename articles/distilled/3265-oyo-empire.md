# Oyo Empire

## Overview
The Oyo Empire was a powerful Yoruba state in West Africa, located in present-day western Nigeria and Benin. It became the largest Yoruba-speaking state and one of the most politically important kingdoms in Western Africa from the late 16th to the early 18th century, exerting influence over neighboring regions through its military, particularly its cavalry, and its sophisticated political system.

## Timeline
- **c. 1300** — Founding of Oyo by Oranmiyan, a prince from Ile-Ife.
- **14th century** — Oyo grows into a formidable inland power.
- **c. 1535** — Nupe invasion forces the Oyo dynasty into exile in Borgu for about 80 years.
- **Early 17th century** — Oyo is re-established with a more centralized and militaristic government.
- **1680** — The empire spans over 150,000 square kilometers.
- **1682** — Oyo begins raiding southward, eventually reaching the coast.
- **1728** — First major Oyo invasion of the Kingdom of Dahomey.
- **1748** — Dahomey is finally subjugated and becomes a tributary.
- **1754** — Dynastic intrigues begin with Prime Minister Gaha, weakening the empire.
- **1817** — Ilorin, under Afonja, secedes from the Oyo Empire.
- **1823** — Dahomey defeats Oyo, ending its hegemony.
- **1835** — The capital, Oyo-Ile, is destroyed by the Fulani of Ilorin.

## Body

### Origins and Pre-Imperial Period
According to oral tradition, the Oyo Empire was founded by Oranmiyan, a prince from the Yoruba kingdom of Ile-Ife. He established the first capital at a site called Ajaka. Early rulers included Ajaka, who was deposed for lacking military virtues, and his brother Shango, later deified as the god of thunder. By the end of the 14th century, Oyo had expanded significantly. However, around 1535, the Nupe people invaded, sacked the capital, and forced the ruling dynasty into an 80-year exile in Borgu.

### Imperial Expansion and Zenith
After returning from exile in the early 17th century, Oyo rebuilt with a stronger military, adopting cavalry and armor from their Nupe enemies. They constructed a new capital, Oyo-Igboho, and later repopulated the original capital, Oyo-Ile. Under leaders like Eguguojo and Orompoto, Oyo conquered much of Yorubaland and secured its borders. The empire reached its height in the 18th century, spanning over 150,000 square kilometers. Its power was based on agricultural wealth, trans-Saharan trade, and the collection of tribute from subjugated kingdoms, including the Fon Kingdom of Dahomey, which was invaded multiple times before being forced into submission in 1748.

### Political Structure
The Oyo Empire developed a sophisticated system of checks and balances. The Alaafin (king) was the supreme ruler but was checked by the Oyo Mesi, a seven-member council led by the Bashorun (prime minister). The Bashorun could compel the Alaafin to commit ritual suicide if he lost the council's favor. The Ogboni, a secret society of elders, provided a further check on the power of both the Alaafin and the Oyo Mesi. The empire was not purely hereditary; the Oyo Mesi selected the Alaafin from descendants of Oranmiyan. A unique tradition required the crown prince (Aremo) to commit suicide upon his father's death to prevent succession conflicts.

### Military
Oyo's military dominance was built on its professional cavalry, which was rare among Yoruba states. The army, commanded by the Aare-Ona-Kakanfo, could field over 100,000 horsemen. Infantry were also skilled, particularly as archers. The military used a combination of metropolitan forces, tributary levies, and a semi-standing army of specialist cavalry soldiers called the Eso. A culture of victory was so entrenched that defeat often carried the duty of suicide.

### Decline and Fall
Decline began with internal political strife, notably the intrigues of Prime Minister Gaha in the mid-18th century, who forced multiple Alaafins to suicide. Failed military campaigns under Alaafin Abiodun and his subsequent murder by his son further weakened the state. The secession of Ilorin in 1817, aided by Fulani Muslims, was a critical blow. The capital, Oyo-Ile, was destroyed in 1835. The empire also suffered from the end of the Atlantic slave trade, which reduced its revenue, and from the Fulani Jihad that established the Sokoto Caliphate. By the mid-19th century, Oyo had lost its prominence, and its people became victims of slave raids by neighboring states.

## Terms
- **Alaafin**: The king or supreme ruler of the Oyo Empire, meaning "owner of the palace."
- **Oyo Mesi**: The seven-member council of state that advised the Alaafin and held legislative power, acting as a check on his authority.
- **Bashorun**: The head of the Oyo Mesi and prime minister, who held significant power, including the ability to force the Alaafin's suicide.
- **Ogboni**: A powerful secret society of elders that represented popular opinion and religious authority, checking the power of the Alaafin and Oyo Mesi.
- **Aare-Ona-Kakanfo**: The supreme military commander of the Oyo Empire, required to live on a frontier province.
- **Eso**: A semi-standing army of 70 specialist cavalry war chiefs nominated by the Oyo Mesi.
- **Aremo**: The crown prince of Oyo, who by tradition had to commit suicide upon his father's death.
- **Bere**: A festival celebrating peace and the successful rule of the Alaafin, held at regular intervals.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact origins and historical development of Oyo's cavalry are unclear, though it may have been influenced by neighboring Nupe, Borgu, or Hausa states.
- Scholars debate how much of Oyo's sophisticated political structure existed before the Nupe invasion and exile, or was developed afterward.
- The precise causes and timeline of the empire's decline are complex, involving internal political instability, economic shifts from the slave trade, and external pressures like the Fulani Jihad.
