# D. W. Griffith

## Overview
D. W. Griffith (1875–1948) was an American film director whose innovations in narrative structure and editing fundamentally shaped cinema. He pioneered techniques such as cross-cutting and close-ups, and directed landmark works including *The Birth of a Nation* (1915) and *Intolerance* (1916). Despite his artistic influence, his legacy is deeply contested due to the racist content of *The Birth of a Nation*, which glorified the Ku Klux Klan and reinforced white supremacist narratives.

## Body

### Early Life and Entry into Film
Born into a poor Kentucky family, Griffith lost his father at age 10 and left school to work in retail and later as an actor in touring companies. In 1907, he moved to New York hoping to sell a script to Edison Studios; though rejected, he was cast in *Rescued from an Eagle's Nest*. This led him to pursue acting, appearing as an extra before joining Biograph in 1908.

### Innovation at Biograph
At Biograph, Griffith began directing after Wallace McCutcheon Sr. fell ill. He directed 48 shorts in 1908 alone, experimenting with crosscutting and camera placement. His 1909 film *The Cricket on the Hearth* used parallel editing inspired by Dickens. In 1910, he shot *In Old California*, the first film made in Hollywood. His first feature, *Judith of Bethulia* (1914), was produced despite Biograph’s skepticism about feature-length films.

### The Birth of a Nation and Its Aftermath
Griffith directed *The Clansman* (later *The Birth of a Nation*) in 1915 under Reliance-Majestic Studios. Based on Thomas Dixon Jr.’s novel, it portrayed slavery as benign and the KKK as heroes. The film was a massive box-office success but sparked riots in northern cities and prompted NAACP efforts to ban it. It is widely credited with contributing to the KKK’s revival. In response to criticism, Griffith made *Intolerance* (1916), depicting persecution across four historical periods. Though artistically ambitious, it was a financial failure that ruined Griffith personally.

### United Artists and Later Career
In 1919, Griffith co-founded United Artists with Chaplin, Pickford, and Fairbanks to give creators independence from commercial studios. He continued directing features like *Broken Blossoms* (1919), *Way Down East* (1920), and *Orphans of the Storm* (1921), but struggled financially. After leaving United Artists in 1924, he made only a few sound films, none successful. He received an honorary Oscar in 1936 and was posthumously honored with a Hollywood Walk of Fame star in 1960.

## Terms
- **Crosscutting**: Technique of interweaving scenes from separate storylines to build tension or show simultaneity.
- **Feature film**: A long-form motion picture, typically over 40 minutes, as opposed to short films.
- **Silent film**: Movies produced without synchronized dialogue or sound, relying on visuals and intertitles.
- **Blockbuster**: A highly profitable and popular film, often with wide appeal.
- **Dunning School**: A discredited academic interpretation of Reconstruction that portrayed Southern Democrats sympathetically and justified Jim Crow.
- **United Artists**: A film studio founded in 1919 by Griffith, Chaplin, Pickford, and Fairbanks to allow creative autonomy.
- **Biograph**: The American Mutoscope and Biograph Company, an early film studio where Griffith began directing.
- **National Film Registry**: A U.S. program selecting films deemed culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant for preservation.

## Debates and Open Questions
Griffith’s legacy remains deeply divided. While celebrated for technical innovations and narrative mastery—earning praise from filmmakers like Chaplin, Hitchcock, and Eisenstein—his work is also condemned for propagating racist ideology. *The Birth of a Nation* is frequently cited as instrumental in reviving the KKK and normalizing white supremacist views. The Directors Guild of America renamed its top honor from the "D. W. Griffith Award" to the "Lifetime Achievement Award" in 1999, citing his role in fostering racial stereotypes. Scholars continue to debate whether his artistic contributions can be separated from the harmful social impact of his most famous work.
