# Chinese art

## Overview
Chinese art encompasses visual art forms originating in or practiced by Chinese artists, characterized by an unusual continuity of tradition and a deep consciousness of its own history. It includes major forms like painting, calligraphy, sculpture, ceramics, and architecture, which often share motifs and are integrated to create unified environments. The art's evolution reflects China's dynastic history, philosophical influences, and engagement with both internal traditions and external influences, making it a key lens for understanding Chinese culture and society.

## Timeline
- **c. 6th millennium BC** — Neolithic Yangshao culture produces early pottery with painted decorations.
- **c. 3300–2300 BC** — Liangzhu culture develops a sophisticated jade carving tradition.
- **c. 1500 BC onward** — Bronze Age begins; Shang dynasty creates elaborate ritual bronze vessels.
- **221–209 BC** — Qin Shi Huang's Terracotta Army is assembled for his mausoleum.
- **1st century CE** — Buddhism arrives in China, later profoundly influencing sculpture and art.
- **4th century AD** — Wang Xizhi establishes the high point of semi-cursive calligraphy.
- **581–907 CE** — Sui and Tang dynasties see Buddhist sculpture evolve towards lifelike expression.
- **960–1279 CE** — Song dynasty is regarded as the golden age of Chinese ceramics and landscape painting.
- **1368–1644 CE** — Ming dynasty sees the rise of the Shanghai School and the perfection of blue-and-white porcelain.
- **1840–1912** — Late Qing period sees Western influence and the emergence of the Shanghai School.
- **1949–1976** — Socialist Realism dominates; traditional arts are suppressed during the Cultural Revolution.
- **1980s–present** — Rehabilitation of traditional forms and emergence of contemporary avant-garde movements.

## Body

### Neolithic and Early Dynastic Foundations
Early Chinese art began with Neolithic pottery, such as that from the Yangshao culture, which featured painted designs with spiritual significance. The Liangzhu culture developed a refined jade tradition. The Bronze Age, beginning with the Xia and Shang dynasties, produced ritual bronze vessels noted for their intricate zoomorphic decoration, like the taotie motif. These bronzes shifted from religious to aesthetic objects over time. The Qin dynasty's Terracotta Army (210–209 BC) demonstrated unprecedented monumental realism, while the Han dynasty saw the first monumental stone sculptures and the use of jade burial suits.

### The Influence of Buddhism and Scholarly Arts
Buddhism's arrival around the 1st century CE led to a major development in Buddhist sculpture, which evolved from symbolic Northern dynasties styles to the lifelike naturalism of the Tang dynasty. Concurrently, the scholar-official class elevated ink wash painting and calligraphy to the highest art forms. Painting, done with brush and ink on paper or silk, emphasized capturing the spirit or "rhythm" of nature, especially in landscape (shanshui) painting. The Song dynasty is considered the "Great age of Chinese landscape," while Yuan dynasty painters, often retreating from Mongol rule, revived older styles and developed the "Four Great Masters" tradition.

### Ceramics, Decorative Arts, and Architecture
Chinese ceramics show continuous development, with the Song dynasty's monochrome wares and the Ming dynasty's blue-and-white porcelain being particularly celebrated. Jingdezhen has been the porcelain capital since at least the Yuan dynasty. Decorative arts, including jade carving, lacquerware, and cloisonné, were executed with great skill. Chinese architecture, characterized by its emphasis on horizontal axis and timber-frame construction, influenced styles across East Asia. The Ming dynasty furniture, with its elegant proportions and sophisticated joinery, is highly regarded.

### Modern and Contemporary Transformations
The late Qing and early 20th century saw modernization efforts, with artists like Xu Beihong and Liu Haisu introducing Western techniques. After 1949, Socialist Realism became the official style, and traditional arts were suppressed during the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976). Since the 1980s, traditional forms have been rehabilitated, and contemporary avant-garde movements have gained international recognition. Artists like Ai Weiwei and Zhang Xiaogang engage critically with China's heritage and rapid social change.

## Terms
- **Ink wash painting (Shui-mo):** A style using brush and black or colored ink, often associated with scholar-officials, emphasizing spirit and atmosphere over precise detail.
- **Gong-bi:** A meticulous, highly detailed painting style often used for figural or narrative subjects, practiced by court or workshop artists.
- **Shanshui:** Literally "mountain-water," the traditional Chinese term for landscape painting, which aims to capture the essence and rhythm of nature.
- **Taotie:** A common motif on ancient Chinese bronze vessels, depicting a symmetrical, frontal mythological being.
- **Cong and Bi:** Ritual jade objects from the Neolithic Liangzhu culture; cong are cylindrical tubes, and bi are flat discs with a central hole.
- **Mortise-and-tenon joinery:** A complex interlocking wood joint system used in traditional Chinese furniture, requiring no nails or glue.
- **Chinoiserie:** The European interpretation and imitation of Chinese artistic traditions, popular in the 18th century.
- **Shanghai School (Haipai):** A late Qing dynasty painting school that catered to a commercial market, using vivid colors and popular themes while retaining traditional techniques.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise religious and social significance of symbols and designs on Neolithic Yangshao pottery is debated.
- The early significance and origin of the taotie motif on Shang bronzes remain unclear, with myths about it only recorded later.
- The extent to which contemporary Chinese art is a genuine expression versus a product constructed for the international art market and curators is a point of controversy.
