# Arithmetic

Arithmetic is the elementary branch of mathematics that studies numbers and their operations, primarily addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division, and in a wider sense also exponentiation, extraction of roots, and logarithms. It is one of the earliest forms of mathematics education and forms the basis for many other branches of mathematics, such as algebra, calculus, and statistics, as well as for sciences like physics and economics. The practice of arithmetic is at least thousands of years old, with ancient civilizations developing numeral systems around 3000 BCE, and its modern form was shaped by Greek abstract study, Indian and Arab developments of the decimal system, and the axiomatic foundations established in the 19th century.

## Timeline

- **c. 43,000 years ago** — Lebombo bone, possibly the oldest arithmetic artifact (disputed)
- **c. 22,000–30,000 years ago** — Ishango bone, possibly an arithmetic artifact (disputed)
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Egyptians and Sumerians invent numeral systems for practical problems
- **c. 1800 BCE** — Babylonians develop the first positional numeral system (radix 60)
- **7th–6th centuries BCE** — Greek mathematicians (Thales, Pythagoras) begin abstract study of numbers and proofs
- **c. 628 CE** — Brahmagupta writes down rules for zero and negative numbers
- **12th–13th centuries** — Fibonacci popularizes the decimal numeral system in the Western world
- **17th century** — First mechanical calculators invented; John Napier discovers logarithms
- **18th–19th centuries** — Euler and Gauss lay foundations of modern number theory; Dedekind–Peano axioms formulated
- **20th century** — Electronic calculators and computers revolutionize arithmetic speed and accuracy

## Body

### Definition and related fields

The term arithmetic derives from the Latin *arithmetica*, from the Ancient Greek *arithmos* ('number') and *arithmetike tekhne* ('the art of counting'). Its precise definition is disputed: a narrow characterization limits it to natural numbers, while the more common view includes integers, rational numbers, real numbers, and sometimes complex numbers. Some definitions restrict it to numerical calculations, while wider senses include the study of how the concept of numbers developed, properties of and relations between numbers, and the axiomatic structure of operations. Arithmetic is closely related to number theory, and some authors use the terms as synonyms, though number theory is more specifically restricted to the study of integers and their properties such as divisibility, factorization, and primality.

### Numbers and numeral systems

The main kinds of numbers are natural numbers (1, 2, 3, ...), whole numbers (which include 0), integers (positive and negative whole numbers), rational numbers (expressible as a ratio of two integers, such as 1/2), and real numbers (which include both rational and irrational numbers). Irrational numbers, like √2 and π, cannot be expressed as fractions and have infinite non-repeating decimal representations. Numbers can also be distinguished as cardinal (expressing quantity, answering "how many?") or ordinal (indicating position, answering "what position?").

Numeral systems are either positional or non-positional. All early systems were non-positional, where a digit's value does not depend on its position; examples include the unary system, Egyptian hieroglyphics, and Roman numerals. Positional systems have a radix that acts as a multiplicand for each position. The decimal (Hindu–Arabic) system has radix 10, while the binary system used by computers has radix 2. The earliest positional system was developed by the ancient Babylonians with radix 60.

### Operations

The most important operations are addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division, with further operations including exponentiation, extraction of roots, and logarithm. Two key concepts are identity elements (which cause no change, like 0 for addition and 1 for multiplication) and inverse elements (which combine with another element to yield the identity, like −6 for 6 in addition). Subtraction is the inverse of addition, and division is the inverse of multiplication. Addition and multiplication are both commutative (order of arguments doesn't matter) and associative (order of operations in a series doesn't matter); exponentiation and logarithm are neither.

### Types of arithmetic

Integer arithmetic deals with positive and negative whole numbers, using techniques like addition with carries, long multiplication, and long division. It is not closed under division—7 divided by 2 is not an integer—so results may be rounded or retain a remainder. Rational number arithmetic operates on fractions, and unlike integer arithmetic, it is closed under division (as long as the divisor is not 0). Real number arithmetic includes irrational numbers and is closed under exponentiation with positive bases. Since irrational numbers have infinite non-repeating decimals, calculations often use truncation or rounding. Floating-point arithmetic, used by computers, approximates real numbers but can violate laws like associativity due to rounding errors.

Other types include modular arithmetic (operating on a finite set, like clock faces), interval arithmetic (operations on ranges of values), vector and matrix arithmetic, and compound unit arithmetic. Non-Diophantine arithmetics violate traditional intuitions, with equations like 1 + 1 = 1, which can describe real-world situations such as raindrops merging.

### Axiomatic foundations

Axiomatic foundations provide a small set of laws from which all fundamental properties of numbers can be derived. The Dedekind–Peano axioms, first formulated by Richard Dedekind and refined by Giuseppe Peano, axiomatize natural number arithmetic using only the primitive concepts of 0, natural number, and successor. Set-theoretic constructions represent each natural number as a unique set (0 as the empty set, 1 as {0}, etc.), with integers as ordered pairs of natural numbers, rational numbers as pairs of integers, and real numbers constructed via Dedekind cuts.

### History

Early civilizations used numbers for practical purposes like commerce and tax records but lacked an abstract concept of number. The ancient Greeks initiated abstract study and introduced rigorous proofs, distinguishing classes of numbers like even, odd, and prime, and discovering irrational numbers. The ancient Indians developed zero as a number for calculations (rules written by Brahmagupta around 628 CE) and the positional decimal system. Arab mathematicians refined and spread this system to the Western world, where it was popularized by Fibonacci. The 17th century saw the first mechanical calculators and the discovery of logarithms. The 18th and 19th centuries brought modern number theory (Euler, Gauss) and axiomatic foundations (Cantor's set theory, Dedekind–Peano axioms). The 20th century's electronic calculators and computers revolutionized arithmetic.

### In various fields

Arithmetic education is one of the first forms of mathematics education, starting with concrete scenarios like counting beads and calculating change, then progressing to abstract understanding of different number types and advanced operations. The psychology of arithmetic studies how humans and animals learn about numbers, including the concept of numeracy—the capability to comprehend and apply numerical concepts. The philosophy of arithmetic explores the nature and ontological status of numbers, with views including Platonism (numbers exist independently of mind), intuitionism (mathematical objects are mental constructions), logicism (mathematical truths reduce to logical truths), and formalism (mathematical principles are rules of symbol manipulation). Arithmetic is relevant to daily life, business, engineering, cryptography, and most sciences.

## Terms

- **Natural numbers**: Whole numbers starting from 1, excluding 0 and negatives; also called counting numbers.
- **Rational numbers**: Numbers expressible as a ratio of two integers, like 1/2.
- **Irrational numbers**: Numbers that cannot be expressed as fractions, like √2 and π.
- **Positional numeral system**: A system where a digit's position determines its value, using a radix like 10 (decimal) or 2 (binary).
- **Identity element**: The element that causes no change when applied in an operation (0 for addition, 1 for multiplication).
- **Inverse element**: The element that combines with another to yield the identity (e.g., −6 is the additive inverse of 6).
- **Commutativity**: A property where changing the order of arguments doesn't affect the result (true for addition and multiplication).
- **Associativity**: A property where the order of operations in a series doesn't matter (true for addition and multiplication).
- **Modular arithmetic**: Arithmetic on a finite set where results outside the set are adjusted back, like clock faces with a modulus of 12.
- **Floating-point arithmetic**: A computer method approximating real numbers with limited precision, which can introduce rounding errors.

## Debates and open questions

- The precise definition of arithmetic is disputed: some restrict it to natural numbers only, while others include integers, rational numbers, real numbers, and complex numbers; some limit it to numerical calculations, while others include the study of number concepts and axiomatic structures.
- Whether the Lebombo bone (c. 43,000 years ago) and Ishango bone (c. 22,000–30,000 years ago) are the oldest arithmetic artifacts is disputed; a basic sense of numbers may predate these findings and might even have existed before language.
- Some mathematicians do not distinguish between natural and whole numbers, including 0 in the set of natural numbers.
- In philosophy, there is debate over whether arithmetic truths are knowable a priori (by thinking alone) or, as naturalist philosophers like Quine argue, grounded in the sensory world described by empirical sciences.
