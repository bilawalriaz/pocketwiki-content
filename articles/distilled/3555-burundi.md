# Burundi

## Overview
Burundi is a small, landlocked country in East Africa with a population of over 14 million. It is one of the world's poorest nations and has a history marked by ethnic conflict, civil war, and political instability. Its significance lies in its complex social structure, its role in regional conflicts, and its ongoing struggle for peace and development.

## Timeline
- **Late 16th century** — The Kingdom of Burundi (Urundi) emerges as a centralized state.
- **1885** — Burundi becomes part of German East Africa.
- **1916** — Belgian forces occupy Burundi during World War I.
- **1924** — Ruanda-Urundi becomes a Belgian League of Nations mandate.
- **1 July 1962** — Burundi gains independence from Belgium as a constitutional monarchy.
- **November 1966** — A coup d'état abolishes the monarchy, establishing a republic under military rule.
- **1972** — A genocide of the Hutu population by the Tutsi-dominated army occurs.
- **1993** — Melchior Ndadaye becomes the first Hutu president; his assassination triggers a civil war.
- **2000** — The Arusha Peace and Reconciliation Agreement is signed.
- **2005** — A new constitution is approved, and Pierre Nkurunziza is elected president.
- **2015** — President Nkurunziza's decision to seek a third term sparks protests and a failed coup.
- **2020** — Evariste Ndayishimiye is elected president after Nkurunziza's death.

## Body

### Pre-Colonial and Colonial Era
The Kingdom of Burundi, ruled by a monarch (*mwami*) and a princely aristocracy (*ganwa*), was characterized by a hierarchical political system and tributary economic exchange. The social structure included the Twa, Hutu, and Tutsi, but status was not purely ethnic; it was fluid, with wealth and livestock influencing social classification. The Germans colonized the region in the late 19th century. After World War I, Belgium administered Burundi and Rwanda together as a League of Nations mandate, later a UN trust territory. The Belgians preserved the kingdom's institutions while introducing administrative changes.

### Independence and Ethnic Conflict
Burundi became independent in 1962 as a monarchy. Political instability followed, including the assassination of popular nationalist Prince Louis Rwagasore in 1961. A 1965 coup attempt by Hutu officers failed, leading to Tutsi reprisals. In 1966, a series of coups ended the monarchy, establishing a one-party republic under Tutsi military rule. This period saw two major genocides: the 1972 mass killings of Hutus by the Tutsi-dominated army, and the 1993 mass killings of Tutsis by Hutu rebels following the assassination of the first Hutu president, Melchior Ndadaye. These events, along with other conflicts, killed an estimated 250,000 people between 1962 and 1993.

### Civil War and Peace Process
Ndadaye's assassination in 1993 sparked a 12-year civil war between Hutu rebels and the Tutsi-dominated army, killing approximately 300,000 people. Peace talks, mediated by African leaders, led to the 2000 Arusha Agreement. This power-sharing deal was integrated into a new constitution in 2005. A UN peacekeeping mission (2004-2006) helped oversee the transition. The 2005 constitution established a complex power-sharing system to manage ethnic tensions, with mandated representation for Hutu, Tutsi, and Twa groups.

### Post-Conflict and Contemporary Issues
Since 2005, the Hutu-led CNDD-FDD party has dominated politics. President Pierre Nkurunziza's 2015 decision to seek a third term led to violent protests, a failed coup, and a humanitarian crisis, with widespread human rights abuses documented. A 2018 constitutional referendum could have extended his rule to 2034, but he did not run again. He died in 2020, and his successor, Evariste Ndayishimiye, was elected. Burundi remains one of the world's poorest countries, with an economy based on subsistence agriculture. It faces severe challenges including poverty, corruption, poor infrastructure, and environmental degradation like deforestation.

## Terms
- **Ubugabire**: A patron-client relationship in the pre-colonial kingdom where the populace received royal protection in exchange for tribute and land tenure.
- **Ganwa**: The princely aristocracy of the Kingdom of Burundi, headed by the king (*mwami*).
- **Arusha Agreement**: A 2000 peace and reconciliation deal that ended the civil war and established a power-sharing government.
- **CNDD-FDD**: The National Council for the Defense of Democracy – Forces for the Defense of Democracy, the dominant Hutu-led political party since 2005.
- **FRODEBU**: The Front for Democracy in Burundi, a Hutu-dominated party that won the 1993 election.
- **Kirundi**: The sole national language of Burundi, spoken by virtually the entire population.
- **Mwami**: The title for the traditional monarch of the Kingdom of Burundi.
- **Twa, Hutu, Tutsi**: The three main ethnic groups in Burundi, with Hutu comprising about 81%, Tutsi 18%, and Twa less than 1% of the population.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The early history and the precise nature of the roles and social distinctions between the Twa, Hutu, and Tutsi peoples are debated among academics.
- The origins of the "culture of impunity" in Burundi are contested, with analysts placing its beginning at different points: 1965, 1972, or the late 1980s.
- The effectiveness and legacy of the complex ethnic power-sharing architecture enshrined in the 2005 constitution remain a subject of analysis, as the country continues to face authoritarianism and human rights concerns.
