# Mutapa Empire

## Overview
The Mutapa Empire was a powerful Shona state on the northern Zimbabwean Plateau, flourishing from the 15th to the 19th centuries. It was a major center of gold production and trade, interacting extensively with Swahili and Portuguese merchants. Its history illustrates the dynamics of state formation, the impact of European contact, and the resilience of Shona political and religious institutions amidst internal conflict and external pressure.

## Timeline
- **Early 15th c.** — Mutota, an elephant hunter, conquers Dande and establishes the Mutapa Kingdom's capital.
- **c. 1490** — Changamire I kills Mwenemutapa Mukombero, seizing the kingship after a succession conflict.
- **1506** — Portuguese gain control of the trading port of Sofala.
- **1561** — Jesuit missionary Gonçalo da Silveira is killed at the court of Mwenemutapa Negomo.
- **1571** — Portuguese expedition under Francisco Barreto invades Mutapa, aiming to control its gold.
- **1629** — Portuguese defeat Mwenemutapa Kapararidze, installing Mavhura as a vassal ruler.
- **1683** — Changamire Dombo of the Rozvi decisively defeats the Portuguese and later conquers the Kingdom of Butua.
- **1693** — Dombo attacks and destroys Portuguese feiras (marketplaces) on the Zimbabwean Plateau.
- **1760s** — The Mutapa state fragments, with the core territory confined to the Chidima region.
- **1885** — Portugal annexes Chidima, ending the Mutapa Empire's political independence.

## Body

### Origins and Early Expansion
The Mutapa state emerged in the 14th-15th centuries as Shona-speaking Karanga groups migrated north from the declining Great Zimbabwe. The hunter-warrior Mutota consolidated power in the Dande region, securing salt and trade routes. His successor, Matope, expanded the empire militarily, conquering the Tonga and Tavara peoples and gaining control of Zambezi trade routes. This early period established the kingdom's core territory and its reliance on controlling regional trade.

### Portuguese Contact and Conflict
The Portuguese arrived in the region in the early 16th century, initially as Muslim Swahili traders were supplanted by Portuguese settlements along the Zambezi. Relations were complex, involving trade, diplomacy, and conflict. A major turning point was the 1561 killing of Jesuit missionary Gonçalo da Silveira, which prompted a failed Portuguese invasion in 1571. In the 17th century, Portuguese intervention in succession disputes led to the installation of vassal kings like Mavhura, who signed a treaty of vassalage in 1629. This period saw the establishment of Portuguese feiras (marketplaces) and the beginning of prazos (land grants) in the Zambezi Valley.

### Internal Strife and the Rise of the Rozvi
The empire was weakened by frequent succession conflicts, often exacerbated by Portuguese interference. A major crisis occurred in the late 17th century with the rise of Changamire Dombo, leader of the Rozvi. Dombo, a former cattle keeper for the Mwenemutapa, built a power base and decisively defeated the Portuguese in 1683. He later conquered the Kingdom of Butua and, in 1693, destroyed the Portuguese feiras on the plateau, effectively ending their direct influence in the Mutapa heartland for decades. This period marked a shift in power away from the Mwenemutapa.

### Decline and Final Years
From the 18th century, the Mutapa state fragmented. Succession wars between the Nyamhandu and Boroma royal houses weakened central authority, and the capital moved into the Zambezi Valley. The state became a confederation of semi-autonomous chiefs. By the mid-19th century, the Mwenemutapa's power was largely symbolic, confined to the Chidima region. Portuguese influence revived through the prazo system. The empire's end came in the late 19th century when Portugal annexed Chidima in 1885, and subsequent colonial partitioning by Britain and Portugal extinguished its political sovereignty.

## Terms
- **Mwenemutapa**: The royal title meaning "Lord of the Conquered Land," applied to both the king and the empire.
- **Zimbabwe**: A stone-walled royal court or capital, from the Shona term for "large house of stone."
- **Mhondoro**: A powerful ancestral royal spirit, believed to be immortal and to communicate through a medium (svikiro).
- **Kuruva**: A tax or fee paid by foreign traders for the right to trade within Mutapa.
- **Mupeto**: A declaration of limited war, often used to seize unpaid kuruva from traders.
- **Feira**: A marketplace or trading post, established by Muslims and later Portuguese.
- **Prazo**: A large land grant given by the Portuguese Crown to settlers in the Zambezi Valley.
- **Kuronzera**: A system of cattle loaning that constituted a form of vassalage and wealth distribution.
- **Mambo**: The Shona term for king or chief.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Founding Figures**: The historicity of early rulers like Mutota is debated; some scholars suggest he may be a symbolic figure representing multiple rulers.
- **Succession System**: While theoretically adelphic (rotating among brothers), the practical reality was frequent violent conflict, which scholars like Stan Mudenge have quantified.
- **Rozvi Origins**: There is scholarly disagreement on whether Changamire Dombo, founder of the Rozvi Empire, descended from the earlier Changamire I.
- **Impact of Portuguese Contact**: Historians debate the degree to which Portuguese trade and intervention caused the empire's decline versus internal factors like succession disputes.
