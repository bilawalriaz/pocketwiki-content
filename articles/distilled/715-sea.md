# Sea

## Overview
The sea is the interconnected global system of saltwater bodies covering 71% of Earth's surface, containing 97.2% of its known water. It regulates climate through currents and the carbon cycle, hosts immense biodiversity, and has shaped human civilization through navigation, trade, and culture. Modern challenges include pollution, overexploitation, and climate change impacts like acidification and sea-level rise.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Austronesians begin maritime expansion in Southeast Asia
- **1872–1876** — HMS Challenger expedition establishes oceanography as a science
- **1960** — Trieste reaches deepest point in Mariana Trench (~10,915 m)
- **1994** — UNCLOS (third version) enters into force
- **2012** — James Cameron pilots Deepsea Challenger to Mariana Trench

## Body

### Definition and Scope
The term "sea" refers both to the entire interconnected oceanic system (Atlantic, Pacific, Indian, Southern, Arctic) and to specific smaller bodies like the Mediterranean or Red Sea. The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea defines all ocean water as "sea." Seas are generally smaller than oceans and often partially enclosed by land, though exceptions like the Sargasso Sea exist.

### Physical Science
Earth's 1.335 billion km³ of seawater contains about 35‰ salinity (grams of dissolved solids per liter), with sodium chloride comprising 85% of solids. Salinity varies with evaporation, precipitation, and ice formation. Seawater is slightly alkaline (pH ~8.2), but ocean acidification has lowered it below 8.1 due to CO₂ absorption. Temperature ranges from over 30°C at the surface in tropics to −2°C near polar ice, with deep water consistently between −2°C and 5°C.

### Ocean Dynamics
Surface currents, driven by wind and the Coriolis effect, form gyres that moderate climate. The thermohaline circulation ("global conveyor belt") moves cold, dense water from polar regions through all oceans over ~1,000 years. Waves form from wind friction, with rogue waves reaching over 25 meters. Tsunamis, caused by underwater earthquakes, landslides, or impacts, travel fast in deep water but amplify in shallow zones. Tides result from lunar and solar gravitational forces, creating twice-daily cycles.

### Geological Framework
The seabed consists of oceanic crust (dense basalt, 5–10 km thick) floating on the mantle via tectonic plates. Mid-ocean ridges form where magma rises between diverging plates, while subduction zones create trenches and volcanic islands. The Mariana Trench reaches 10.994 km depth. Continental shelves, slopes, and abyssal plains define coastal and deep-sea topography.

### Human Interaction
Humans have navigated seas since prehistoric times, with major exploration milestones including Polynesian expansion, Viking voyages, and 15th-century European circumnavigation. Oceanography began with Captain Cook's voyages and the Challenger expedition. Maritime law (UNCLOS) governs territorial waters, EEZs (200 nautical miles), and resource rights. Seas support fishing, aquaculture, trade (over $4 trillion annually), energy (offshore wind, tidal), and leisure industries.

### Environmental Challenges
Marine pollution includes plastic debris (Great Pacific Garbage Patch), oil spills, nutrient runoff causing dead zones, and heavy metal accumulation. Overfishing has reduced fish stocks by 80% in industrialized areas. Climate change causes warming, acidification, deoxygenation, sea-level rise (1.8 mm/year), and Arctic ice loss. Conservation efforts include fishing quotas and marine protected areas.

## Terms
- **Salinity**: Concentration of dissolved salts in water, measured in parts per thousand (‰)
- **Thermohaline circulation**: Global deep-water current system driven by temperature and salinity differences
- **Gyre**: Large system of rotating ocean currents, typically circular
- **Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ)**: Maritime zone extending 200 nautical miles from baseline, granting coastal states resource rights
- **Ocean acidification**: Decrease in ocean pH due to atmospheric CO₂ absorption forming carbonic acid
- **Bathymetry**: Mapping and study of ocean floor topography
- **Frazil ice**: Small ice crystals that form in supercooled seawater during initial freezing
- **Longshore current**: Coastal current flowing parallel to shore, created by waves approaching at an angle
- **Rip current**: Narrow, fast-moving channel of water flowing away from shore
- **Upwelling**: Process where deep, nutrient-rich water rises to the surface

## Debates and Open Questions
The precise location where life originated in the sea remains debated—Miller-Urey experiments suggested a chemical "soup" in open water, while alternatives propose volcanic hot springs, clay sediments, or deep-sea hydrothermal vents. The rate and severity of future ocean acidification and deoxygenation depend on climate mitigation efforts. The full ecological impacts of microplastics and deep-sea mining remain unknown. Disputes over maritime boundaries (e.g., Sea of Japan naming) have delayed ratification of updated IHO guidelines.
