# Friedrich List

## Overview
Daniel Friedrich List (1789–1846) was a German economist and political theorist who founded the nationalist theory of political economy, arguing that a nation’s true wealth lies in the development of its productive powers rather than in immediate exchange value. He contended that free trade benefits only nations of roughly equal industrial development; therefore, "infant industries" in less developed nations like Germany and the United States require temporary protective tariffs—viewed as investments in future productivity—to achieve economic sovereignty. A forefather of the German Historical School, List championed the *Zollverein* (German customs union) and railway construction as "Siamese twins" of national unification. His *National System of Political Economy* (1841) influenced the industrialization of Meiji Japan and Tsarist Russia under Sergei Witte, and later developmentalist policies in South Korea and China.

## Body

### Early Career and the German Customs Union
List entered the Württemberg bureaucracy in 1805, rising rapidly under reformist minister Karl August von Wangenheim. By 1817, despite lacking a degree, he was appointed professor at Tübingen, where he argued that universities ignored "national economy"—agriculture, manufacturing, mining, and trade—in favor of obsolete legal training. His 1818 book *Die Staatskunde und Staatspraxis Württembergs* and journalism in the *Volksfreund aus Schwaben* drew government suspicion. In 1819, he organized the General German Trade and Industry Association, the first modern German business lobby. He formulated its petition to the Federal Assembly, famously describing Germany’s thirty-eight customs lines as ligatures on a body’s limbs. The Bundestag rejected the petition, and List lost royal favor, resigning his professorship to become the association’s managing director. Though immediate lobbying failed, Wangenheim later used List’s plans to realize a South German customs union in 1828.

### Imprisonment, American Exile, and the Turn to Protectionism
Elected to the Württemberg parliament in 1820, List attacked bureaucratic despotism in his "Reutlingen Petition" (1821), demanding local self-government. The conservative majority stripped his immunity; he was sentenced to ten months at Hohenasperg fortress in 1822. He fled for two years but returned to serve the sentence in 1824. Pardoned in 1825 on condition of emigration, he settled in Pennsylvania. After a failed farming attempt, he edited the *Reading Adler* (1826–30), discovered coal, and co-founded the Little Schuylkill Railroad (1831), becoming an early US rail pioneer. In the US, List abandoned Adam Smith’s free trade doctrines. Influenced by Alexander Hamilton, Daniel Raymond, and French liberal protectionists like Adolphe Thiers and Jean-Antoine Chaptal, he argued in *Outlines of American Political Economy* (1827) that nations lagging behind England (like the US and Germany) needed protective tariffs to develop domestic industrial sovereignty. He supported Andrew Jackson’s 1828 election, gaining US citizenship and a consulship at Leipzig (1833), which provided diplomatic immunity from Württemberg prosecution.

### Railway Pioneer and *Vormärz* Liberalism
In Leipzig (1833–37), List made railway promotion his life’s work, calling railways and customs union the "Siamese twins" of German development. He published a widely distributed paper proving the Leipzig–Dresden line’s profitability, establishing the organizational model (committees, concessions, share issuance) copied across Germany. He founded the *Eisenbahnjournal* (1835–37) to advocate a national network. Simultaneously, he co-edited the *Rotteck-Welckersches Staatslexikon* (1834), the "basic book of *Vormärz* liberalism," contributing entries on industry, technology, and labor. Tensions with co-editor Carl Theodor Welcker forced his exit. The Panic of 1837 wiped out his American investments, ending his financial independence and forcing a move to Paris.

### The National System of Political Economy
In Paris (1837–40), List wrote for the *Allgemeine Zeitung* and developed his mature theory. *The Natural System of Political Economy* (1837) revived his German reputation. His magnum opus, *The National System of Political Economy* (1841), synthesized his views: economic development results from legal, social, and political factors, not just exchange. He defined three systems: "individual economics" (Smith/Say), "cosmopolitan economics" (universal free trade), and "national economics" (his own). The nation, defined by language, culture, and constitution, stands between the individual and humanity; private interests must be subordinated to national maintenance and strength. He argued Smith’s system was actually a "mercantile/exchange-value system" that ignored the reality of unequal industrial development. List’s "infant industry" argument held that a nation should begin with free trade to develop agriculture, switch to protection to build manufacturing, and return to free trade only once industries are internationally competitive. The temporary tariff cost is an "investment in industrial education." He also outlined stages of development: pastoral life → agriculture → agriculture with manufactures → agriculture, manufactures, and commerce combined. Only temperate-zone nations, he claimed, could reach the higher stages; tropical regions naturally supply raw materials.

### Later Advocacy and Death
Returning to Germany in 1840 after his son Oskar’s death, List settled in Augsburg. He criticized the 1834 *Zollverein* for favoring Prussia and advocated an "educational tariff" against British superiority; the *Zollverein* adopted moderate protective tariffs on iron and yarn in 1844. Württemberg restored his "civic honor" in 1841, but he declined editorship of the *Rheinische Zeitung* (the role went to Gustav Höfken; Karl Marx eventually took the post) and a Russian offer. He founded the *Zollvereinsblatt* (1843) to push for *Zollverein* expansion. As the *Zollverein* trended toward free trade and his health failed, his publisher withdrew; he funded the paper himself. A final trip to England seeking a commercial alliance failed. Bankrupted by another US crisis and suffering severe depression ("melancholy"), he committed suicide in Kufstein in 1846.

## Terms
- ****National System of Political Economy**** — List’s theory that the state must actively foster national productive powers through protective tariffs, infrastructure, and education, subordinating individual exchange interests to collective national development.
- ****Infant Industry Argument**** — The claim that new industries in developing nations require temporary tariff protection to achieve economies of scale and competitiveness against established foreign rivals; the tariff is an "investment" in future productivity.
- ****Zollverein**** — The German Customs Union (formalized 1834) eliminating internal tariffs among member states; List advocated it from 1819 as a prerequisite for German industrialization.
- ****Productive Powers**** — List’s core metric of national wealth: the full, many-sided development of a nation’s capacity to produce (skills, technology, infrastructure, institutions), not its current stock of exchange value.
- ****Stages of Economic Development**** — List’s schema for temperate nations: 1. Pastoral life; 2. Agriculture; 3. Agriculture + manufactures; 4. Agriculture + manufactures + commerce. The state must legislate the transitions.
- ****Cosmopolitan Economics**** — List’s term for the classical free-trade doctrine (Smith, Say) that treats humanity as a single economic unit, ignoring the political reality of sovereign nations with unequal development.
- ****Educational Tariff**** — A protective duty designed not to shield inefficiency permanently, but to "educate" domestic industry until it can compete globally; distinct from revenue or retaliatory tariffs.
- ****Vormärz**** — The "pre-March" period (1815–1848) of German liberal and nationalist agitation preceding the 1848 revolutions; List’s *Staatslexikon* was a key intellectual text of this era.
- ****German Historical School**** — An approach to economics (founded by List, Wilhelm Roscher, Bruno Hildebrand) emphasizing historical context, institutional evolution, and national specificity over universal abstract laws.
- ****Siamese Twins**** — List’s metaphor for the inseparable linkage of a national railway network and a customs union as the dual physical and legal infrastructure of German economic unity.

## Debates and open questions
- **American influence on List’s "National System":** Ha-Joon Chang (2002) argues List’s US exile (1825–33) and exposure to Hamilton/Clay’s "American System" decisively shaped his mature protectionism. Arno Daastøl (2011) counters that List advocated a German customs union in 1819 and his US views were pragmatic, influenced by French liberal protectionists (Thiers, Chaptal), not dogmatic adoption of Hamilton.
- **List vs. Marx on development:** Thomas Nipperdey and Roman Szporluk contrast List’s nationalist developmentalism (state-led industrialization, protection, nation-building) with Marx’s focus on class struggle and international proletariat; Angus Maddison notes Marx ignored "catching-up" policy problems List centered.
- **Applicability to tropical/colonial economies:** List claimed only temperate nations could achieve higher industrial stages, assigning the tropics a "natural monopoly" in raw materials. Mauro Boianovsky (2011) and Erik Reinert debate whether this was a descriptive observation or a normative justification for core-periphery inequality.
- **Legacy in 20th-century developmental states:** Scholars (Linebarger, Helleiner) trace List’s direct influence on Meiji Japan, Sergei Witte’s Russia, Ireland (Arthur Griffith), South Korea (Ha-Joon Chang), and post-Mao China (Deng Xiaoping), debating whether these represent faithful applications or selective appropriations of his nationalist framework.
- **Suicide and mental state:** The 1846 autopsy cited "melancholy" negating free will, permitting Christian burial. Historians debate whether his depression was clinical, reactive to financial ruin and political marginalization, or exacerbated by the failure of his "long view" to gain traction in his lifetime.
