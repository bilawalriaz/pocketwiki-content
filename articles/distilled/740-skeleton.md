# Skeleton

## Overview
A skeleton is the structural support framework of an animal's body, enabling posture, movement, and protection of internal organs. Skeletons vary widely across the animal kingdom, from external shells to internal bone frameworks, reflecting diverse evolutionary solutions to mechanical and physiological demands.

## Timeline
- **Pre-Cambrian** — Hydrostatic and pliant skeletons evolve in soft-bodied organisms
- **Cambrian (~540 MYA)** — Exoskeletons appear in arthropods
- **Ordovician (~485 MYA)** — Vertebrate jawless fish with endoskeletons diversify
- **Devonian (~416 MYA)** — Bony fish (Osteichthyes) and tetrapods emerge
- **Carboniferous** — Amniotes evolve, leading to reptiles, birds, and mammals
- **Jurassic–Cretaceous** — Birds evolve hollow, lightweight bones for flight

## Body

### Classification and Types
Skeletons are categorized by composition, location, and rigidity. Solid skeletons are made of bone, cartilage, or cuticle; fluid-filled hydrostatic skeletons rely on pressurized body fluids. Location divides them into endoskeletons (internal) and exoskeletons (external). Rigidity ranges from pliant (elastic, e.g., jellyfish mesoglea) to rigid (e.g., arthropod chitin).

### Exoskeletons
Exoskeletons are rigid outer coverings, common in arthropods (chitin-based) and molluscs (calcium carbonate shells). They provides protection, muscle attachment, and sensory functions. Growth requires molting (ecdysis). On land, exoskeletons limit size due to weight; aquatic forms like the southern giant clam can be massive.

### Endoskeletons
Endoskeletons are internal mineralized structures derived from mesodermal tissue, found in chordates, echinoderms, and sponges. They range from simple support (sponges) to complex muscle-attachment systems. Vertebrates possess bone-based endoskeletons with repeated segmental units (e.g., vertebrae, ribs).

### Hydrostatic Skeletons
Hydrostatic skeletons are fluid-filled cavities bounded by muscle and connective tissue, found in jellyfish, flatworms, nematodes, and earthworms. Movement occurs via alternating muscle contractions and expansions, transmitting force through fluid pressure.

### Vertebrate Skeletons
Vertebrate skeletons are endoskeletons primarily of bone, with cartilage playing a key role in joints and fetal development. Bones are rigid organs of mineral (hydroxyapatite) and organic matrix, producing blood cells and storing calcium/phosphate. Embryonic bones form from ectodermal and mesodermal skeletogenic cells.

#### Amphibians and Reptiles
Turtles evolved shells from ribcages. Snakes and caecilians have over 300 vertebrae, far exceeding lizards' typical 65.

#### Birds
Bird bones are hollow and lightweight, reducing flight costs. Skeletal fusion and specialized shapes endure flight stress. Beaks replace heavy jaws; hatchlings use egg teeth to exit eggs.

#### Fish
Fish skeletons are cartilaginous (Chondrichthyes: sharks, rays) or bony (Osteichthyes). The vertebral column is the main element; fins are supported by rays, not directly attached to the spine.

#### Mammals
Marine mammals lost hind limbs or fused them into tail fins; whale cervical vertebrae are fused for swimming stability.

#### Humans
The human skeleton has ~206 bones (variable due to fusion), comprising axial and appendicular regions. It supports organs, anchors muscles, and protects the brain, heart, and spinal cord. Sexual dimorphism exists: males have larger, heavier bones; females have wider pelvises for childbirth. Full skeletal maturity occurs around age 20.

### Invertebrate Skeletons
Invertebrates lack vertebrae but use diverse skeletal forms. Arthropods have chitinous exoskeletons requiring molting. Echinoderms possess calcite endoskeletons with stereom structure. Molluscs produce shells via mantle secretion. Sponges use siliceous or calcareous spicules.

### Cartilage
Cartilage is avascular connective tissue with chondrocytes in an extracellular matrix of Type II collagen, proteoglycans, and water. Types include hyaline, elastic, and fibrocartilage. Nutrient diffusion sustains chondrocytes; repair is slow.

## Terms
- **Endoskeleton**: Internal skeletal structure, typically of bone or cartilage, to which organs and muscles attach.
- **Exoskeleton**: External rigid covering providing protection and muscle attachment, e.g., arthropod cuticle.
- **Hydrostatic skeleton**: Flexible internal support using fluid pressure within body cavities.
- **Ecdysis**: Periodic molting process in arthropods for growth.
- **Stereom**: Porous, calcite-based skeletal structure unique to echinoderms.
- **Chondrocyte**: Cell type responsible for maintaining cartilage matrix.
- **Osteoblast/Osteoclast**: Bone-forming and bone-resorbing cells, respectively.
- **Axial/Appendicular skeleton**: Axial refers to central body axis (skull, spine); appendicular to limbs and girdles.
- **Sesamoid bone**: Small bone embedded in tendons, e.g., patella.
- **Perichondrium**: Connective tissue layer surrounding cartilage.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Whether the number of human bones (206) should account for variable fusions (e.g., sacrum, coccyx).
- The evolutionary transition from exoskeletons to endoskeletons in vertebrates remains debated.
- The functional significance of sexual dimorphism in human pelvic anatomy beyond obstetric demands.
