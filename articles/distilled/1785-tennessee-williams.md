# Tennessee Williams

## Overview

Tennessee Williams (1911–1983) was an American playwright and screenwriter, considered alongside Eugene O'Neill and Arthur Miller one of the three foremost playwrights of 20th-century American drama. His major successes—*The Glass Menagerie* (1944), *A Streetcar Named Desire* (1947), *Cat on a Hot Tin Roof* (1955), *Sweet Bird of Youth* (1959), and *The Night of the Iguana* (1961)—drew heavily from his dysfunctional family and personal struggles. His work is notable for its poetic dialogue, exploration of sexuality and mental instability, and its reflection of his own life experiences.

## Body

### Early Life and Family

Williams was born in Columbus, Mississippi, to Edwina Dakin and Cornelius Coffin "C. C." Williams, a traveling shoe salesman who became an alcoholic. His mother was the daughter of an Episcopal priest, and Williams spent much of his early childhood in his grandfather's rectory. A near-fatal case of diphtheria left him frail and confined to home for a year, and his father considered him effeminate and weak. His sister Rose, later diagnosed with schizophrenia, and his unhappy mother's intense focus on him shaped his writing. Critics agree Williams drew from his dysfunctional family throughout his career.

When Williams was eight, the family moved to St. Louis after his father's promotion. His mother's search for better housing and his father's drinking caused frequent moves. At 16, he won third prize for an essay in *Smart Set* magazine; a year later his short story appeared in *Weird Tales*. These early publications brought little recognition.

### Education and Early Career

From 1929 to 1931, Williams attended the University of Missouri, where he was bored and distracted, entering writing contests for income. His first submitted play, *Beauty Is the Word* (1930), about rebellion against religious upbringing, earned him honorable mention as the first freshman to receive such recognition. After failing a military training course, his father pulled him from school and put him to work at the International Shoe Company factory. Hating the monotony, Williams wrote prodigiously, setting a goal of one story per week. By his 24th birthday, he had suffered a nervous breakdown and left the job; memories of a factory co-worker contributed to the character Stanley Kowalski in *A Streetcar Named Desire*.

He enrolled at Washington University in St. Louis (1936), then transferred to the University of Iowa, graduating with a B.A. in English in 1938. He later studied at the Dramatic Workshop of The New School in New York. Around 1939, he adopted "Tennessee Williams" as his professional name, acknowledging his Southern roots.

### Rise to Fame

In 1939, with agent Audrey Wood's help, Williams received a $1,000 Rockefeller Foundation grant for *Battle of Angels*, produced unsuccessfully in Boston in 1940. He moved to New Orleans to write for the Works Progress Administration (WPA), a New Deal federal program. The grant attracted Hollywood's attention, earning him a six-month MGM contract at $250 weekly.

*The Glass Menagerie* (1944), developed from his short story "Portrait of a Girl in Glass," premiered in Chicago, moved to New York, and became an instant hit, winning the New York Drama Critics' Circle Award. *A Streetcar Named Desire* (1947) cemented his reputation. Between 1948 and 1959, seven more plays reached Broadway. By 1959, Williams had earned two Pulitzer Prizes, three New York Drama Critics' Circle Awards, three Donaldson Awards, and a Tony Award. Many plays were adapted to film in the 1950s and early 1960s.

### Later Years and Decline

In the 1960s and 1970s, Williams experienced personal turmoil and theatrical failures. His increasing alcohol and drug consumption, plus poor collaborator choices, damaged his work's quality. Plays including *Kingdom of Earth* (1967), *In the Bar of a Tokyo Hotel* (1969), and *Vieux Carré* (1978) were box office failures. His last play, *A House Not Meant to Stand* (1982), ran only 40 performances despite positive reviews.

### Personal Life and Relationships

Williams remained close to his sister Rose, who was lobotomized in 1943 after being diagnosed with schizophrenia, requiring lifelong institutionalization. Williams moved her to a private institution and gave her royalties from his plays for her care. Her treatment may have contributed to his alcoholism and drug dependence.

By the late 1930s, Williams began exploring his homosexuality. His relationships included Kip Kiernan (1940), Pancho Rodríguez y González (1946–1947), and Frank Merlo (1948–1962), his enduring 14-year partnership. Merlo provided stability and happiness until infidelities and drug abuse ended the relationship; Merlo died of lung cancer in 1963. After Merlo's death, Williams descended into catatonic depression and increasing drug use, including amphetamine injections from Dr. Max Jacobson ("Dr. Feelgood") and the sedative Seconal. In the 1970s, he had a lengthy relationship with Robert Carroll, a Vietnam War veteran in his 20s, who received a bequest in Williams's will alongside sister Rose.

### Death and Legacy

Williams died on February 25, 1983, at age 71 in his Hotel Elysée suite. Initially reported as choking on a plastic cap, the medical examiner later corrected the cause to toxic levels of secobarbital ingested via the cap. His will requested burial at sea near where poet Hart Crane died, but his brother Dakin arranged burial at Calvary Cemetery in St. Louis. Most of his estate went to the University of the South in Sewanee, Tennessee, with the bulk held in trust for his sister; after Rose's death in 1996, the university received about $7 million for a creative writing program.

Posthumous recognition includes the Harry Ransom Center's 2011 centennial exhibit "Becoming Tennessee Williams," induction into the Poets' Corner at the Cathedral of Saint John the Divine (2009), the annual Tennessee Williams/New Orleans Literary Festival (since 1986), a U.S. Postal Service stamp (1995), and a star on the St. Louis Walk of Fame. The Tennessee Williams Theatre in Key West, Florida, and the Tennessee Williams Welcome Center in Columbus, Mississippi, honor his legacy.

## Terms

- **Memory play**: A play that presents events through a character's recollection, as in *The Glass Menagerie*
- **Lobotomy**: A surgical procedure severing brain connections, performed on Williams's sister Rose in 1943
- **Works Progress Administration (WPA)**: New Deal federal program employing writers during the Depression
- **New York Drama Critics' Circle Award**: Major theater award Williams won three times
- **Pulitzer Prize for Drama**: Awarded to *A Streetcar Named Desire* (1948) and *Cat on a Hot Tin Roof* (1955)
- **Secobarbital**: A barbiturate sedative that caused Williams's fatal overdose
- **Alpha Tau Omega**: Fraternity Williams joined at University of Missouri but did not fit in with

## Debates and Open Questions

The 1955 Pulitzer Prize for *Cat on a Hot Tin Roof* was controversial: the jury preferred Clifford Odets's *The Flowering Peach*, and *Cat* was initially considered the weakest of five nominees. Board chairman Joseph Pulitzer Jr. overruled the jury after considerable discussion. Additionally, biographers debate whether Blanche DuBois in *A Streetcar Named Desire* was based on Williams's sister Rose, though the character's mental deterioration is widely seen as inspired by Rose's struggles.
