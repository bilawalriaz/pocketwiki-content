# Ontario

## Overview
Ontario is Canada's most populous and second-largest province, located in Central Canada. It is the country's economic engine, home to its capital (Ottawa) and largest city (Toronto), and its history is defined by Indigenous habitation, French and British colonialism, and rapid industrialization.

## Timeline
- **c. 11,000 years ago** — Paleo-Indians first settle the lands of Ontario.
- **1610** — The colony of the Pays d'en Haut is formally established as a French administrative dependency.
- **1763** — France cedes most of its North American possessions to Britain in the Treaty of Paris.
- **1791** — The Constitutional Act splits Quebec into Upper Canada (southwest) and Lower Canada (east).
- **1812–1814** — The War of 1812 sees American invasions of Upper Canada repelled by British, Canadian, and First Nations forces.
- **1841** — The Act of Union merges Upper and Lower Canada into the Province of Canada; Upper Canada becomes Canada West.
- **1867** — The British North America Act creates the Dominion of Canada, with Ontario as one of its four founding provinces.
- **1889** — The Canada (Ontario Boundary) Act confirms Ontario's boundary, extending it to the west and north.
- **1904** — The Ford Motor Company of Canada is established, marking the rise of the province's automotive industry.
- **1912** — Regulation 17 severely limits French-language schooling in Ontario.
- **1976** — The election of the Parti Québécois in Quebec drives many businesses and English-speakers to Ontario.

## Body

### Geography and Climate
Ontario is bordered by Manitoba, Hudson Bay, Quebec, and five U.S. states. Its geography is dominated by the Canadian Shield in the north and the fertile Great Lakes-Saint Lawrence Valley in the south. The province's climate varies from humid continental in the south to subarctic in the far north, with the Great Lakes significantly moderating temperatures in Southern Ontario. Northern Ontario covers 87% of the land area but contains only 6% of the population.

### History
The land was first inhabited by Paleo-Indians around 11,000 years ago. By the 1400s, large Iroquoian and Algonquian settlements existed. French explorers mapped the region in the early 17th century, calling it the Pays d'en Haut, and established a fur trade alliance with most Indigenous groups. After the British victory in the Seven Years' War, the 1763 Treaty of Paris transferred the territory to Britain. Following the American Revolution, thousands of United Empire Loyalists settled in the region, leading to the creation of Upper Canada in 1791. Political unrest culminated in the failed Upper Canada Rebellion of 1837. The subsequent Act of Union (1840) merged Upper and Lower Canada. Ontario became a founding province of the Dominion of Canada in 1867. Under Premier Oliver Mowat (1872–1896), the province fought for and won its current boundaries and emerged as an industrial powerhouse, fueled by railways, mining, and hydroelectric power.

### Demographics and Economy
As of the 2021 census, Ontario's population was over 14 million, with 94% living in Southern Ontario. The population is diverse, with 34.3% identifying as visible minorities. English is the de facto official language, though French-language services are guaranteed in designated areas. Ontario is Canada's leading manufacturing province, particularly in automobiles, steel, and food processing. The economy also relies on mining and forestry in the north, finance and IT in the Greater Toronto Area, and tourism. Agriculture, while using a small percentage of the workforce, remains significant in the south.

### Government and Politics
Ontario operates as a parliamentary democracy within a constitutional monarchy. The Legislative Assembly of Ontario, located at Queen's Park, has 124 seats. The leader of the party with the most seats becomes the Premier. The province has four main political parties: the Progressive Conservative, Liberal, New Democratic, and Green parties. As of 2025, the Progressive Conservatives hold a majority government.

### Culture and Infrastructure
Ontario's culture is shaped by its diverse population and includes major institutions like the Royal Ontario Museum and the Toronto Symphony Orchestra. Transportation is extensive, with Toronto Pearson International Airport being Canada's busiest, a vast 400-series highway network, and the Saint Lawrence Seaway for cargo. The province has four publicly funded school systems (English and French public and Catholic).

## Terms
- **Pays d'en Haut**: The French term for the "Upper Country," the vast territory administered by New France that included present-day Ontario.
- **Upper Canada**: The British colony that existed from 1791 to 1841 in the southern part of modern-day Ontario.
- **United Empire Loyalists**: American colonists who remained loyal to the British Crown during the American Revolution and migrated to British North America.
- **Responsible Government**: A system where the executive branch (the Premier and cabinet) is responsible to the elected legislature, not the monarch's representative.
- **Canadian Shield**: A large area of exposed Precambrian rock that covers much of Northern and Central Ontario, rich in minerals but with thin soil.
- **Golden Horseshoe**: A densely populated and industrialized region surrounding the western end of Lake Ontario.
- **Franco-Ontarian**: A person whose native language is French and who lives in Ontario.
- **CANDU reactor**: A Canadian-designed pressurized heavy-water reactor used for nuclear power generation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The source notes controversy over the **Ring of Fire** mineral deposit in Northern Ontario, specifically whether the province can afford the CAD$2.25 billion cost to build a road to the site, which is valued at CAD$60 billion.
- The long-term impact of **Regulation 17** (1912), which limited French-language education, and its eventual repeal in 1927 on Franco-Ontarian communities is a point of historical significance.
- The economic shift away from manufacturing, highlighted by the loss of 300,000 manufacturing jobs from 2003–2013 and competition from low-cost economies, presents an ongoing challenge for the province's economy.
