# Aleutian Islands

## Overview
The Aleutian Islands are a volcanic archipelago in the Northern Pacific Ocean, forming a 1,200-mile arc between Alaska and Russia. They are geologically active, ecologically unique, and historically significant as the site of the only North American land battles during World War II. The islands are the homeland of the Unangan (Aleut) people and represent a remote, harsh environment shaped by tectonic forces and oceanic climate.

## Timeline
- **c. 56 Ma** — The Aleutian island arc begins forming from subduction of the Pacific Plate under the North American Plate.
- **c. 9,000 BP** — The earliest known archaeological sites indicate human occupation of the eastern Aleutians.
- **1741** — Russian explorers Vitus Bering and Aleksei Chirikov discover the islands, leading to Russian colonization.
- **1867** — The United States purchases Alaska, including the Aleutian Islands, from Russia.
- **June 3, 1942** — Japanese forces invade and occupy the islands of Attu and Kiska.
- **May 1943** — U.S. forces recapture Attu in a bloody battle.
- **August 1943** — Allied forces invade Kiska, only to find the Japanese have already evacuated.
- **1965-1971** — The U.S. conducts underground nuclear weapons tests on Amchitka Island.
- **1976** — The Aleutian Islands are designated a UNESCO biosphere reserve.

## Body

### Geology and Geography
The Aleutian Islands are the northernmost part of the Pacific Ring of Fire, formed by the subduction of the Pacific Plate beneath the North American Plate beginning in the Early Eocene (55–50 Ma). The archipelago consists of 14 main volcanic islands and 55 smaller ones, with 57 volcanoes. The islands are divided into six groups, from the Fox Islands in the east to the Commander Islands (which belong to Russia) in the west. The largest island is Unimak. The terrain is mountainous and rugged, with steep coasts and active volcanic cones.

### Climate and Ecology
The climate is oceanic, characterized by moderate temperatures, heavy rainfall, and near-constant fog. The southern islands have a subpolar oceanic climate, while the northern areas are subarctic. The Aleutian Low, a semi-permanent low-pressure system, dominates the region in winter. The growing season is short, and the islands are largely devoid of native trees due to strong winds, instead supporting dense growth of shrubs and grasses. The ecosystem is largely unspoiled but threatened by introduced species. Sea otters are a keystone species, maintaining kelp forests by controlling sea urchin populations.

### Human History and Culture
The indigenous Unangan (Aleut) people settled the islands in a migration from east to west over thousands of years, developing a marine-adapted culture with advanced boat-building and weaving skills. Russian explorers arrived in 1741, leading to colonization, exploitation, and sporadic conflict. The Russian period also saw the establishment of Orthodox Christianity and the adoption of some Unangan technologies by the Russians. The U.S. acquired the islands in 1867. During World War II, the Japanese occupied Attu and Kiska in 1942, prompting the U.S. to relocate most Unangan civilians. The subsequent battles to retake the islands were the only land invasions of North America during the war. The post-war era saw nuclear testing on Amchitka and the passage of the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act in 1971.

## Terms
- **Aleutian Arc**: The chain of volcanic islands formed by the subduction of the Pacific Plate under the North American Plate.
- **Unangan**: The indigenous people of the Aleutian Islands, also known as Aleut.
- **Baidarka**: The traditional Aleut kayak, adopted by Russian colonists for sea otter hunting.
- **Aleutian Low**: A semi-permanent low-pressure area that forms over the islands in winter, influencing North Pacific weather.
- **Keystone Species**: A species (like the sea otter) that has a disproportionately large effect on its ecosystem relative to its abundance.
- **Subduction**: A geological process where one tectonic plate sinks beneath another, often creating volcanic arcs.
- **UNESCO Biosphere Reserve**: A designation for areas of ecological significance that balance conservation with sustainable use.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact timing of the first human settlement is debated, with evidence for occupation as early as 14,000 years ago, though the earliest confirmed sites date to approximately 9,000 years before present.
- The biogeographical and cultural classification of the islands is ambiguous; while they have affinities to North America, some sources associate them with Oceania due to their remote Pacific location.
