# Mojave Desert

## Overview
The Mojave Desert is a 54,000-square-mile arid region in the Southwestern United States, primarily in southeastern California and southwestern Nevada. It is the smallest and driest of the four major North American deserts, known for its extreme temperatures, basin and range topography, and unique flora and fauna, including the endemic Joshua tree. The desert is ecologically significant, supports major human activities like recreation and military training, and faces conservation challenges from development and climate change.

## Body

### Geography and Climate
The Mojave Desert is bordered by the Sierra Nevada, Sonoran Desert, and defined by the San Andreas and Garlock faults. Its landscape features parallel mountain ranges, valleys, alluvial fans (bajadas), and dry lake beds (playas). The climate is characterized by extreme seasonal temperature variations, with hot summers and cold, windy winters. Annual precipitation averages 2 to 6 inches, mostly from Pacific storms between November and April. Historically, low fuel loads prevented a natural fire regime, but invasive annual grasses have recently increased fire frequency.

### Geology
The desert's exposed geology is complex, with rocks dating back at least 1.7 billion years. The region formed under shallow Precambrian seas and was later shaped by the subduction of the Farallon Plate. Around 16 million years ago, crustal stretching began, creating the Basin and Range province. This process ripped apart the Death Valley area 2–3 million years ago. During the Ice Age, glacial lakes like Lake Manly filled these basins; their evaporation over the last 10,500 years concentrated salts and created the current desert environment.

### Ecology
The Mojave Desert hosts exceptional endemic flora, most notably the Joshua tree (*Yucca brevifolia*), a key indicator species. Other common plants include creosote bush, brittlebush, and various cacti. Fauna includes the desert tortoise, bighorn sheep, and the Mojave rattlesnake. While animal endemism is lower than for plants, unique species include the Devils Hole pupfish and the Kelso Dunes Jerusalem cricket. Plant communities are often distinct to specific geographic features.

### Human History and Development
Before European contact, Native American tribes like the Mohave lived in the desert. Exploration began in the 18th century, followed by American expansion in the 19th century. In recent decades, urbanization (e.g., Las Vegas, Lancaster-Palmdale) and military installations (e.g., Edwards Air Force Base) have grown significantly. Human development, including agriculture, mining, and renewable energy projects like the Desert Sunlight Solar Farm, threatens wildlife and habitats. The desert is a major tourism destination, featuring Death Valley and Joshua Tree National Parks.

### Conservation
The Mojave Desert is one of the best-protected ecoregions in the U.S., largely due to the California Desert Protection Act of 1994, which created major parks and wilderness areas. However, the southwest and central east portions remain threatened by off-road vehicles, development, and grazing. The World Wildlife Fund considers the desert relatively "stable/intact." Further protection was provided by the 2013 Desert Renewable Energy Conservation Plan, which designated 4.2 million acres of public land as protected wilderness.

## Terms
- **Basin and Range topography:** A landscape of parallel mountain ranges separated by flat valleys or basins.
- **Playa:** A dry lake bed in a desert basin where water collects and evaporates, leaving salt deposits.
- **Bajada:** A broad, sloping alluvial fan formed by sediment deposited at the base of a mountain.
- **Endemic:** A species native to and found only in a specific, limited geographic area.
- **Indicator species:** A species whose presence, absence, or abundance reflects a specific environmental condition, like the Joshua tree for the Mojave Desert.
- **Unconformity:** A gap in the geological record where rock layers are missing due to erosion or non-deposition.
- **Subduction zone:** A tectonic boundary where one tectonic plate sinks beneath another, often creating volcanoes and mountains.
- **Colluvium:** Loose, unconsolidated sediments that have been deposited at the base of a slope by rainwash or gravity.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact boundaries of the Mojave Desert are less distinctive to the east due to the absence of a clear indicator species like the Joshua tree.
- The impact of large-scale renewable energy developments on desert ecosystems and cultural landscapes is an ongoing concern, highlighted by incidents like the death of an endangered bird at a solar farm.
- The long-term effects of invasive annual grasses on the desert's natural fire regime and native plant communities are a significant area of ecological study.
