# European integration

## Overview
European integration is the process of political, legal, social, regional, and economic unification among European states, primarily advanced through the European Union (EU). It seeks to foster peace, stability, and prosperity by pooling sovereignty and creating shared institutions, representing a major experiment in supranational governance.

## Timeline
- **1951** — Treaty of Paris establishes the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC).
- **1957** — Treaties of Rome create the European Economic Community (EEC) and Euratom.
- **1965** — Merger Treaty combines the institutions of the ECSC, EEC, and Euratom.
- **1973** — First enlargement: Denmark, Ireland, and the United Kingdom join the EEC.
- **1992** — Maastricht Treaty formally establishes the European Union.
- **1999** — The euro is launched as an accounting currency.
- **2002** — Euro banknotes and coins enter circulation.
- **2004** — Major enlargement: 10 countries, mostly from Central and Eastern Europe, join the EU.
- **2007** — Bulgaria and Romania join the EU.
- **2013** — Croatia joins the EU.
- **2020** — The United Kingdom withdraws from the EU (Brexit).

## Body

### Historical Precursors and Interwar Ideas
The concept of a unified Europe has historical precedents, including the Roman Empire, the Holy Roman Empire, and the Napoleonic Empire. After World War I, thinkers like Richard von Coudenhove-Kalergi (advocating a conservative, Christian Europe) and French Prime Minister Aristide Briand (proposing a European federation to the League of Nations) revived the idea. These interwar movements were halted by the Great Depression and the rise of fascism.

### Post-WWII Foundation and Theories
The devastation of World War II created strong political will for integration to prevent future conflicts. Winston Churchill called for a "United States of Europe" in 1946, though he initially envisioned Britain as a sponsor rather than a member. The foundational theory was **neofunctionalism**, which argued that integration in one sector (like coal and steel) would create functional pressures ("spillover") for integration in others. This was countered by **intergovernmentalism**, which emphasized that nation-states and their governments remained the key actors.

### The European Union and Core Policies
The EU, established by the 1992 Maastricht Treaty, is the primary vehicle for integration. Its core economic policies include the **Single Market** (guaranteeing free movement of goods, services, capital, and people) and the **Eurozone** (a monetary union with a common currency). The **Schengen Area** has abolished internal border controls among most member states. Social and political integration includes programs like Erasmus for student mobility and the Charter of Fundamental Rights.

### Wider European Cooperation and Future Challenges
Integration extends beyond the EU through bodies like the **Council of Europe** (focused on human rights and legal standards) and the **OSCE** (focused on security). Regional cooperation exists in areas like the Nordic Council and the Visegrád Group. The future of integration is debated, with discussions on "finalité politique" (the ultimate political goal). Challenges include balancing national sovereignty with deeper union, managing enlargement to candidate countries in Eastern Europe, and responding to geopolitical events like Russia's war in Ukraine.

## Terms
- **Neofunctionalism:** A theory where integration in one policy area creates pressures for integration in adjacent areas ("spillover").
- **Intergovernmentalism:** A theory emphasizing that nation-states and their governments are the primary actors in integration decisions.
- **Single Market:** The EU's internal market ensuring the free movement of goods, services, capital, and people.
- **Eurozone:** The group of EU member states that have adopted the euro as their common currency.
- **Schengen Area:** An area of 27 European countries that have abolished internal border controls.
- **Council of Europe:** An international organization focused on human rights, democracy, and rule of law, separate from the EU.
- **European Economic Area (EEA):** An agreement allowing non-EU states (Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway) to participate in the Single Market.
- **Multi-level Governance:** A concept describing how power is shared between supranational (EU), national, and subnational authorities.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Sovereignty vs. Supranationality:** A core debate is whether deeper integration requires a transfer of sovereignty to EU institutions (a federalist view) or should remain a cooperation between sovereign states (an intergovernmentalist view).
- **Finalité Politique:** There is no agreed-upon end goal for integration; debates continue on whether the EU should evolve into a federal state or remain a union of states.
- **Enlargement vs. Deepening:** The EU faces the challenge of simultaneously integrating new member states (enlargement) while deepening political and economic union among existing members.
- **Democratic Deficit:** Scholars and citizens debate whether EU institutions are sufficiently democratic and accountable to the public.
