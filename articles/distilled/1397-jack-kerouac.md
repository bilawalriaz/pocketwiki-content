# Jack Kerouac

## Overview
Jack Kerouac (1922–1969) was an American novelist and poet who, alongside William S. Burroughs and Allen Ginsberg, pioneered the Beat Generation—a literary movement defined by spontaneous prose, jazz-influenced rhythms, and a search for spiritual meaning amid post-war conformity. His 1957 novel *On the Road* became the movement’s defining text, mythologizing cross-country travel and friendship with Neal Cassady while embedding a deeply Catholic, mystical worldview beneath its surface hedonism. Kerouac’s "spontaneous prose" method—typing on continuous scrolls to mimic jazz improvisation and Buddhist breath—produced a vast, autobiographical *roman à clef* oeuvre exploring Buddhism, his French-Canadian heritage, and the tension between asceticism and addiction. Though he despised the 1960s counterculture his work inspired, his influence permeates music (Bob Dylan, The Doors), literature, and the very lexicon of American bohemia.

## Timeline
- **March 12, 1922** — Born Jean-Louis Lebris de Kérouac in Lowell, Massachusetts, to French-Canadian parents.
- **1926** — Older brother Gerard dies of rheumatic fever; event later fictionalized in *Visions of Gerard*.
- **1940** — Enrolls at Columbia University on a football scholarship; meets future Beat figures.
- **1942–1943** — Serves as Merchant Mariner; writes first novel *The Sea Is My Brother* (published 2011). Briefly serves in Navy Reserves; discharged on psychiatric grounds (schizoid personality).
- **August 22, 1944** — Arrested as material witness in David Kammerer murder; marries Edie Parker for bail (annulled 1948).
- **1950** — First published novel, *The Town and the City*, appears under name "John Kerouac."
- **April 1951** — Types *On the Road* on a 120-foot tracing-paper scroll in a three-week spontaneous session.
- **1954** — Discovers *A Buddhist Bible* at San Jose Library; begins intensive study of Buddhism.
- **1955–1956** — Lives with sister in North Carolina; writes *Some of the Dharma* and *Wake Up: A Life of the Buddha*.
- **September 1957** — Viking Press publishes *On the Road* (heavily edited); Gilbert Millstein’s *New York Times* review makes Kerouac famous.
- **1958** — Publishes *The Dharma Bums*; moves to Northport, NY, with mother.
- **1959** — Appears on *The Steve Allen Show*; narrates film *Pull My Daisy*.
- **1968** — Final TV appearance on *Firing Line* with William F. Buckley Jr.; splits with Ginsberg over counterculture.
- **October 21, 1969** — Dies in St. Petersburg, Florida, at 47 from esophageal hemorrhage caused by cirrhosis.

## Body

### Early Life and French-Canadian Roots
Kerouac was born in Lowell, Massachusetts, to Léo-Alcide Kéroack and Gabrielle-Ange Lévesque, French Canadians from Quebec. He spoke only French at home until age six, retaining a marked accent into his late teens. His surname traces to Brittany (Kervoach, near Morlaix), though family legends claimed descent from a Baron François Louis Alexandre Lebris de Kerouac. The 1926 death of his older brother Gerard, whom Kerouac later viewed as a guardian angel, profoundly shaped his spirituality. His mother, a devout Catholic, instilled a faith that persisted alongside his later Buddhist studies; his father abandoned religion for drinking and gambling after Gerard’s death. A childhood mystical experience during confession—hearing God promise salvation despite a life of suffering—fused with Gerard’s vision of the Virgin Mary to form the bedrock of Kerouac’s syncretic worldview.

### Formation of the Beat Circle
At Columbia, Kerouac met Allen Ginsberg, Lucien Carr, and William S. Burroughs. The 1944 murder of David Kammerer by Carr (allegedly in self-defense against obsessive stalking) entangled Kerouac and Burroughs as material witnesses; they collaborated on the unpublished novel *And the Hippos Were Boiled in Their Tanks*. Kerouac’s brief marriage to Edie Parker secured his bail. During WWII, he served as a Merchant Mariner (1942) and briefly in the Navy Reserves, receiving a psychiatric discharge for "schizoid personality" after stating, "I just can't stand it; I like to be by myself." These years produced his first novel, *The Sea Is My Brother*, which he dismissed as a "crock."

### *On the Road* and Spontaneous Prose
After the poorly selling *The Town and the City* (1950), Kerouac developed his signature method. Influenced by bebop (Charlie Parker, Dizzy Gillespie), the "Joan Anderson letter" from Neal Cassady, and Buddhist concepts of breath, he typed *On the Road* in April 1951 on a 120-foot scroll of taped tracing paper—no paragraph breaks, minimal revision. The novel fictionalized 1947–1950 road trips with Cassady (Dean Moriarty), framing them as a Catholic quest for God: "I found him in the sky... THERE IS NO OTHER WAY OUT FOR THE HOLY MAN: HE MUST SWEAT FOR GOD." Viking Press demanded extensive cuts (sexual content, real names) before 1957 publication. The scroll version, published in 2007, restores the explicit text and real identities.

### Buddhism and Mid-Career Works
In 1954, Kerouac discovered Dwight Goddard’s *A Buddhist Bible* at the San Jose Library. He studied *The Diamond Sutra* intensively in 1955, cycling through the six Pāramitās weekly. Living with his sister "Nin" in North Carolina (1955–56), he wrote *Some of the Dharma*—an experimental treatise referencing Milarepa, Sariputa, and Dogen—and the biography *Wake Up: A Life of the Buddha* (published 2008). *The Dharma Bums* (1958), typed on a ten-foot teleprinter roll, chronicled his 1956 mountain climbing with Gary Snyder and fire-lookout stint on Desolation Peak. However, criticism from Zen authorities Ruth Fuller Sasaki, Alan Watts, and D.T. Suzuki devastated him; he declared, "I'm not a Buddhist any more," and descended into heavier drinking.

### Fame, Politics, and Decline
The 1957 *New York Times* review anointed Kerouac "voice of a new generation," a role he resisted: "I'm not a beatnik. I'm a Catholic." He coined "Beat Generation" from Herbert Huncke’s slang "beat" (down-and-out), imbuing it with beatific connotations. Politically conservative yet apolitical at heart, he supported Robert A. Taft (1952) and Barry Goldwater (1964), watched McCarthy hearings while smoking marijuana, and viewed Marxism as an "illusory tangent." Fame brought violence (a 1957 beating outside the San Remo Cafe), exploitation (the TV series *Route 66*), and alienation from Ginsberg and the emerging hippie movement, which he called "spiteful." His sister died in 1964; his mother suffered a stroke in 1966. Cassady died in Mexico in 1968.

### French-Language Writing
Kerouac wrote significant unpublished fiction in French, which he called "Canuck French": *La nuit est ma femme* (Feb–Mar 1951, preceding the *On the Road* scroll), *Sur le chemin* (Dec 1952, written in Mexico in five days), and portions of *Maggie Cassidy* (1953). These were collected in *La vie est d'hommage* (2016). Scholars note *La nuit est ma femme* as a direct French-language precursor to the English scroll.

### Death and Estate
On October 20, 1969, in St. Petersburg, Florida, Kerouac vomited blood from esophageal varices caused by cirrhosis. Surgery failed due to a non-clotting liver; he died the next morning at 47. An untreated hernia from a bar fight may have contributed. He was buried in Lowell’s Edson Cemetery. His estate, worth $91 at death, was valued at $10 million by 1998. Literary executorship passed from brother-in-law John Sampas to his son.

## Terms
- **Beat Generation**: Literary movement coined by Kerouac from Herbert Huncke’s slang "beat" (exhausted, down-and-out), redefined with beatific/spiritual connotations; included Kerouac, Ginsberg, Burroughs.
- **Spontaneous Prose**: Kerouac’s method—continuous typing on scroll paper, minimal revision, long dashes replacing periods to mimic jazz improvisation and Buddhist breath; outlined in "Belief and Technique for Modern Prose" (30 maxims).
- **Roman à clef**: Novel with real people thinly disguised as characters; Kerouac’s primary mode (Cassady = Dean Moriarty, Ginsberg = Carlo Marx, etc.).
- **The Diamond Sutra**: Core Buddhist text for Kerouac; he studied it in a weekly cycle of the six Pāramitās (perfections) plus Samādhi, hoping to condition mind to emptiness.
- **Scroll Manuscript**: The 120-foot tracing-paper roll on which *On the Road* was typed in April 1951; published unexpurgated in 2007.
- **Canuck French**: Kerouac’s term for his heritage French dialect; used in unpublished novels *La nuit est ma femme* and *Sur le chemin*.
- **Desolation Peak**: Fire lookout post in North Cascades, Washington, where Kerouac spent summer 1956; basis for *Desolation Angels* and sections of *The Dharma Bums*.
- **Joan Anderson Letter**: Neal Cassady’s 1950 letter to Kerouac, whose free-associative style catalyzed spontaneous prose.
- **Pāramitās**: Six Buddhist perfections (generosity, ethics, patience, effort, meditation, wisdom) that structured Kerouac’s 1955 study cycle.
- **Schizoid Personality**: Psychiatric diagnosis on Kerouac’s 1943 Navy discharge; characterized by detachment and preference for solitude.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Spontaneity vs. Preparation**: Critics (Truman Capote: "That's not writing, it's typing") dispute the "spontaneous" label given Kerouac’s years of journal outlining and the scroll’s premeditated construction.
- **Catholicism vs. Buddhism**: Scholars debate whether Kerouac synthesized or oscillated between traditions; his 1957 claim that *On the Road* is "a story about two Catholic buddies roaming the country in search of God" complicates its countercultural reception.
- **Political Stance**: Kerouac endorsed right-wing candidates (Taft, Goldwater) and supported McCarthy, yet rejected ideological labels; biographers dispute whether this reflects conservatism, contrarianism, or a traditionalist Catholic worldview.
- **French Works’ Significance**: The 2016 publication of *La vie est d'hommage* raises questions about whether Kerouac’s French writing represents a distinct, perhaps more authentic voice than his English prose.
- **Legacy vs. Intent**: Kerouac despised the hippie movement and "beatnik" stereotype his work spawned; the tension between his Catholic mysticism and his cultural legacy as a rebellion icon remains unresolved.
