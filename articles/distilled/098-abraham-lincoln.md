# Abraham Lincoln

## Overview

Abraham Lincoln (February 12, 1809 – April 15, 1865) was the 16th president of the United States, serving from 1861 until his assassination. He led the Union through the American Civil War, defeated the Confederacy, and played a major role in the abolition of slavery. Born in a Kentucky log cabin, he was largely self-educated and rose from frontier lawyer to president, becoming the first Republican president. His victory prompted Southern secession, and his wartime leadership—including the Emancipation Proclamation and the Gettysburg Address—defined his legacy as a national martyr and one of America's most revered presidents.

## Body

### Early life and family

Lincoln was the second child of Thomas Lincoln and Nancy Hanks Lincoln, raised on Sinking Spring Farm and Knob Creek Farm near Hodgenville, Kentucky. His father bought multiple farms but could not get clear property titles, losing hundreds of acres in legal disputes. In 1816 the family moved to Indiana, where titles were more reliable. Nancy died from milk sickness in 1818; Thomas remarried Sarah Bush Johnston in 1819, whom Abraham called "Mama." His sister Sarah died in childbirth in 1828.

Lincoln's formal schooling totaled less than 12 months by age 15, but he was an avid reader. As a teenager he worked as a farmhand and took a flatboat trip to New Orleans, where seeing slave markets left an indelible impression. In 1830 the family moved to Illinois. Lincoln became distant from his father, who lacked interest in education; he refused to attend his father's deathbed or funeral in 1851.

Lincoln married Mary Todd in 1842 after a turbulent courtship that included a broken engagement. They had four sons: Robert (the only one to live to maturity), Eddie (died 1850, probably of tuberculosis), Willie (died 1862 at the White House), and Tad (died 1871 of edema). Lincoln suffered from "melancholy," now thought to be clinical depression.

### Early career and law

After working at a New Salem general store, Lincoln served as a captain in the Illinois Militia during the Black Hawk War (1832) without seeing combat. He lost his first state house campaign but won in 1834. He served four terms, championing the Illinois and Michigan Canal, expanded suffrage to all White men, and led the move of the state capital to Springfield.

Admitted to the Illinois bar in 1836, Lincoln practiced law in Springfield, partnering with John T. Stuart, Stephen T. Logan, and William Herndon. He handled "virtually every kind of business that could come before a prairie lawyer," including the landmark bridge case *Hurd v. Rock Island Bridge Company*. In 1849 he received a patent for a riverboat flotation device—the only president to hold a patent. He appeared before the Illinois Supreme Court in 411 cases.

In the U.S. House (1847–1849), Lincoln opposed the Mexican–American War, demanding President Polk identify the exact "spot" where Mexican soldiers shed American blood—earning the nickname "spotty Lincoln." He supported the Wilmot Proviso banning slavery in territories won from Mexico.

### Rise to the presidency

The Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854, allowing territorial popular sovereignty on slavery, alarmed Northerners and split the Whig Party. Lincoln returned to politics, delivering an estimated 175 speeches against slavery's extension. He joined the new Republican Party in 1856. In the 1858 Senate race, he delivered his House Divided Speech ("A house divided against itself cannot stand") and debated Stephen Douglas seven times. Though Republicans won more popular votes, Democrats won more seats, and Douglas was re-elected.

Lincoln denounced the *Dred Scott* decision (1857), which declared the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional, as a Democratic conspiracy to support the Slave Power. His February 1860 Cooper Union speech argued the Founding Fathers had restricted slavery in the territories and insisted morality required opposition to slavery. He won the 1860 Republican nomination on the third ballot as "The Rail Candidate" and was elected with 39.8% of the popular vote, entirely from the North and West.

### Presidency and Civil War

After Lincoln's election, seven Southern states seceded and formed the Confederate States of America. Lincoln refused to recognize the Confederacy and rejected the Crittenden Compromise. He supported the Corwin Amendment protecting slavery where it existed. His first inaugural address appealed to the South, declaring he had "no purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere with the institution of slavery in the States where it exists."

Lincoln selected a "team of rivals" cabinet, including main opponent William Seward as Secretary of State. When Fort Sumter ran low on food, Lincoln sent provisions; Confederate forces fired first on April 12, 1861. Lincoln called for 75,000 militiamen, forcing border states to choose sides. He suspended habeas corpus, leading to 15,000 civilian detentions without trial; Chief Justice Taney ruled in *Ex parte Merryman* that only Congress could suspend it, but Lincoln persisted.

Lincoln took executive control of the war, imposing a naval blockade, disbursing funds before appropriation, and selecting generals. He replaced ineffective commanders—McClellan, Burnside, Hooker—until Ulysses S. Grant's victories at Vicksburg and Chattanooga led Lincoln to promote him to commander of all Union armies. The Trent Affair nearly caused war with Britain; Lincoln defused it by releasing Confederate envoys.

### Emancipation and Gettysburg

Lincoln overrode two generals' emancipation orders but signed congressional acts banning slavery in federal territories (June 1862) and allowing seizure of slaves from disloyal owners (July 1862). After the Union victory at Antietam, he issued the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation (September 22, 1862), declaring slaves in rebelling states free as of January 1, 1863. The final Proclamation freed slaves in ten states not under Union control. It made enlisting freedmen official policy.

On November 19, 1863, Lincoln delivered the Gettysburg Address, asserting the nation was "dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal" and that government "of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth." It became the most quoted speech in American history.

### Second term and Reconstruction

Lincoln ran for re-election in 1864 under the National Union Party label with War Democrat Andrew Johnson as running mate. Victories at Atlanta and in the Shenandoah Valley turned public opinion; he won 55.1% of the popular vote. His second inaugural address—"With malice toward none; with charity for all"—is considered among America's semi-sacred texts.

Reconstruction began before the war ended. Lincoln's Amnesty Proclamation (December 1863) offered pardons to Confederates who signed oaths of allegiance. He supported the 10% plan for Louisiana reestablishing statehood, which Radicals denounced as too lenient; they passed the Wade–Davis Bill, which Lincoln pocket-vetoed. He pressed for the Thirteenth Amendment abolishing slavery, which passed the House on January 31, 1865, and was ratified in December 1865. Lee surrendered at Appomattox on April 9, 1865.

### Assassination and legacy

John Wilkes Booth, a Confederate spy, shot Lincoln at Ford's Theatre on April 14, 1865, five days after Appomattox. Lincoln died at 7:22 am on April 15. His body traveled by funeral train from Washington to Springfield, Illinois, with hundreds of thousands paying respects. Booth was killed two weeks later.

Lincoln redefined republicanism, presenting the Declaration of Independence's equality principle as foundational. His religious views are debated: he was a skeptic as a young man but expressed growing dependence on God after his sons' deaths; he never joined a church. Scholars consistently rank him among the greatest presidents, though some Black intellectuals since the late 1960s have disputed the "Great Emancipator" title, noting his ethnic slurs and opposition to social equality. His portrait appears on the penny and $5 bill, and he is memorialized at the Lincoln Memorial, Mount Rushmore, and the Abraham Lincoln Presidential Library and Museum.

## Terms

- **Kansas–Nebraska Act (1854)**: Law allowing territorial popular sovereignty on slavery; split the Whigs and spurred Lincoln's political return.
- **Popular sovereignty**: Doctrine allowing each territory's electorate to decide slavery's status.
- **Emancipation Proclamation (1863)**: Declared slaves in rebelling states free; made abolition a Union war goal.
- **Habeas corpus**: Legal right to challenge detention; Lincoln suspended it, allowing arrests without charges.
- **Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)**: Supreme Court ruling that the Missouri Compromise was unconstitutional, infringing on slave owners' property rights.
- **Wilmot Proviso**: Failed 1846 proposal to ban slavery in territories won from Mexico.
- **Copperheads**: Anti-war Democrats who criticized Lincoln for refusing to compromise on slavery.
- **Radical Republicans**: Faction demanding harsh treatment of secession and faster abolition.
- **Thirteenth Amendment (1865)**: Constitutional amendment abolishing slavery and involuntary servitude.
- **Reconstruction**: Post-war process of reintegrating Southern states and defining freed slaves' status.

## Debates and open questions

- Whether Lincoln's relationship with Ann Rutledge was romantic: historians dispute the evidence; David Herbert Donald says it "cannot be reconstructed from the record."
- Lincoln's religious beliefs: sources differ—his law partner called him an "unbeliever," while a pastor asserted he avowed belief in Divine Authority; scholars say his personal faith "necessarily remain[s] a puzzle."
- Whether Lincoln's health was declining before assassination: photographs suggest weight loss; proposed conditions include Marfan syndrome or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2B.
- Whether Lincoln deserved the title "Great Emancipator": since the late 1960s, some Black intellectuals (e.g., Lerone Bennett Jr.) called him a White supremacist, citing ethnic slurs and opposition to social equality; defenders cite his condemnation of slavery and role in abolition.
- Whether Lincoln was moving toward more radical Reconstruction at his death: Phillip S. Paludan suggests yes; Eric Foner imagines possible agreement on federal protection for civil rights plus limited Black suffrage.
