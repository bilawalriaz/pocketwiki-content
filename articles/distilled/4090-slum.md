# Slum

## Overview
A slum is a densely populated urban area characterized by substandard housing, inadequate infrastructure, and poverty. It is a critical global issue, as slums represent the failure of urban planning and economic systems to provide basic needs for a growing urban population, leading to severe health, safety, and social challenges.

## Timeline
- **c. 1825** — New York City's Five Points, considered the first U.S. slum, is established.
- **1845** — The term "back slum" appears in London, likely originating as British slang.
- **1848** — The Public Health Act is passed in the UK, responding to conditions in slums.
- **1862** — The Peabody Trust is founded in London to provide decent housing.
- **1920** — Rio de Janeiro documents its first slum in a census.
- **1950s-1970s** — Major slum clearance and public housing programs are undertaken in Europe and the U.S.
- **1972** — John Turner's theoretical writings inspire the "slum upgrading" approach.
- **2008** — Over 50% of the world's population lives in urban areas, accelerating slum growth.
- **2012** — UN-Habitat reports 863 million people live in slums in the developing world.

## Body

### Definition and Characteristics
The United Nations defines slums as informal settlements lacking one or more key conditions: access to improved water, improved sanitation, sufficient living area, housing durability, and security of tenure. They are characterized by substandard, often self-built housing using inadequate materials, severe overcrowding, and a lack of basic infrastructure like paved roads, reliable electricity, and formal law enforcement. Insecure land tenure is a core feature, with dwellings often built on land without clear title or permission.

### Historical Development
Before the 19th century, rich and poor often lived in the same districts. Rapid industrialization and urbanization in the 19th century led to wealthy residents moving out of city centers, leaving poorer populations behind in overcrowded areas like London's East End. Slums were common in Europe and the U.S. until the early 20th century, with major clearance and public housing programs emerging post-World War II. In the developing world, slums grew significantly in the 20th century due to rural-urban migration and are now a predominant urban feature.

### Causes of Formation and Growth
Slums form due to a combination of factors. **Rural-to-urban migration** is a primary driver, as people seek better economic opportunities but often lack the skills or resources for formal housing. **Rapid urbanization** outpaces a government's ability to plan and provide infrastructure and affordable housing. **Economic stagnation, poverty, and high unemployment** force people into the **informal economy**, which thrives in slums. **Poor urban planning**, **colonial legacies** of segregation, and **social conflicts** or **natural disasters** also create and expand slums. **Politics** can perpetuate slums, as some leaders rely on slum populations for political support.

### Major Problems
Slum residents face severe, interconnected challenges. **Health crises** are rampant due to overcrowding, poor sanitation, and contaminated water, leading to high rates of infectious diseases like cholera and HIV/AIDS, as well as child malnutrition. **Violence and crime** are significant concerns, often linked to the illicit informal economy and insufficient law enforcement. **Vulnerability to hazards** is high, as slums are frequently located in areas prone to floods, landslides, or fires, with construction too poor to withstand disasters. The **informal economy** provides livelihoods but offers no security, legal protection, or social mobility.

### Countermeasures and Responses
Strategies to address slums include **slum removal**, which is often criticized for ignoring root causes; **slum relocation**, which can disrupt livelihoods and social networks; and **slum upgrading**, which aims to improve infrastructure and tenure security in situ, though long-term success is mixed. The most historically effective approach in developed nations has been **citywide infrastructure development** combined with **public housing** and reliable mass transit, which helped clear major slums in the U.S. and Europe. The UN advocates for vigorous urban planning, poverty reduction, and infrastructure development to achieve "cities without slums."

## Terms
- **Slum**: A densely populated urban area of substandard housing and poverty, lacking basic services.
- **Informal Economy**: Economic activity that is unregistered, untaxed, and unregulated by the government.
- **Security of Tenure**: The legal right of a person or group to occupy land or housing without fear of eviction.
- **Slum Upgrading**: A strategy to improve slum conditions by providing basic infrastructure and services in place.
- **Rural-to-Urban Migration**: The movement of people from countryside areas to cities, often in search of work.
- **Agglomeration Economies**: The benefits firms and people gain by locating near one another in cities.
- **Insecure Tenure**: The lack of formal, legal rights to the land or housing one occupies.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Effectiveness of Slum Upgrading**: While initially promising, many upgrading projects show disappointing long-term results, with conditions sometimes returning to pre-upgrade levels. It is debated whether this approach can lead to sustainable improvement without addressing underlying poverty and the informal economy.
- **Role of Politics**: Scholars debate the extent to which political interests, including patronage networks within slums, actively maintain slums by resisting relocation or formalization efforts that threaten existing power structures.
- **Impact of Relocation**: The success of relocation programs is contested. While some, like in Morocco, have used social mediation, others are criticized for uprooting communities and severing their connection to livelihoods, leading to resident opposition.
