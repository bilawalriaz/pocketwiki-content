# Kavango–Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area

## Overview
The Kavango–Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area (KAZA TFCA) is the world's second-largest transboundary conservation area, spanning 520,000 km² across five Southern African countries. It protects critical ecosystems of the Upper Zambezi and Okavango basins and supports the world's largest population of approximately 250,000 African elephants. The project demonstrates international cooperation in conservation and tourism, aiming to manage shared natural resources across national borders.

## Timeline
- **2003** — Ministers from the five partner countries meet in Katima Mulilo, Namibia, to discuss the project.
- **2006** — The Southern African Development Community (SADC) endorses KAZA as a regional project.
- **2006** — The five countries sign a memorandum of understanding at Victoria Falls, Zimbabwe.
- **August 18, 2011** — The heads of government sign a treaty, formally establishing the conservation area.
- **March 15, 2012** — The KAZA TFCA is officially opened in Katima Mulilo.
- **2014** — Lions are studied throughout the area.
- **November 2014** — Zambia and Zimbabwe introduce a common KAZA visa for free movement within the area.

## Body

### Establishment and Purpose
The initiative was conceived by the Shoshong Constituency Foundation and the World Wide Fund for Nature (WWF), inspired by earlier transboundary tourism and resource management concepts. Formal political commitment began with a 2003 ministerial meeting. The project gained official regional backing from SADC in 2006, leading to a memorandum of understanding. The legal framework was finalized with a treaty signed by all five national governments in 2011, and the area was officially opened in 2012. The primary goal is coordinated management of a vast, interconnected landscape for conservation and sustainable development.

### Geography and Composition
The TFCA covers 520,000 km², distributed among its member states: 30% in Botswana, 25% in Zambia, 17% in Angola, and 14% each in Namibia and Zimbabwe. Its core is the confluence of the Zambezi and Chobe Rivers, where four countries meet. The area incorporates 287,132 km² of pre-existing protected areas, including major national parks such as Chobe, Hwange, and Victoria Falls. This network of parks and reserves forms the backbone of the conservation area.

### Funding and Management
Financial support for KAZA comes from international development partners, including the KfW Development Bank, the German government, the World Bank, the Netherlands, and Sweden. Management involves cross-border cooperation, exemplified by the 2014 introduction of a common KAZA visa by Zambia and Zimbabwe to facilitate tourist movement. Ecological studies, such as the 2014 lion research, inform ongoing management strategies across the transboundary landscape.

## Terms
- **Transfrontier Conservation Area (TFCA):** A conservation area that spans the boundaries of two or more countries, requiring international cooperation for management.
- **Memorandum of Understanding (MoU):** An agreement between parties that outlines an intended common line of action, often a precursor to a formal treaty.
- **SADC:** The Southern African Development Community, a regional intergovernmental organization that endorsed the KAZA project.
- **Caprivi Strip:** A narrow, panhandle-shaped region of northeastern Namibia that is part of the KAZA area.
- **Okavango Delta:** A vast inland river delta in Botswana, a key ecosystem within the KAZA TFCA.
- **Transboundary:** Extending across a border or boundaries of multiple countries.
- **KfW Development Bank:** A German state-owned development bank that provides financial support for the project.
