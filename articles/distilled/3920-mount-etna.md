# Mount Etna

## Overview
Mount Etna is an active stratovolcano on Sicily's east coast, Italy. It is one of the world's most active and tallest volcanoes in Europe, with a height of 3,403 m (11,165 ft) as of September 2024. Its frequent eruptions, fertile soils, and proximity to populated areas make it a significant geological feature and a designated UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Timeline
- **c. 500,000 years ago** — Volcanic activity begins beneath the sea off ancient Sicily.
- **c. 8,000 years ago** — Catastrophic collapse of the eastern flank creates the Valle del Bove and a tsunami.
- **122 BCE** — Violent Plinian summit eruption devastates Catania; Rome exempts its population from taxes for ten years.
- **1669** — Most destructive historical eruption; lava destroys villages and reaches Catania's walls.
- **1928** — Flank eruption destroys the village of Mascali.
- **1991–1993** — Longest 20th-century eruption; lava threatens Zafferana Etnea but is diverted.
- **2002–2003** — Major eruption destroys tourist stations; ash falls in Libya.
- **2008–2009** — Longest flank eruption since 1991–93, lasting 417 days.
- **2013** — Added to the UNESCO World Heritage List.
- **2021** — Height of the Voragine crater surpasses the Southeast Crater after summer eruptions.
- **2024** — Height reaches 3,403 m (11,165 ft) in September; significant eruptions occur in July and August.

## Body

### Etymology and Mythology
The name "Etna" likely derives from the Greek *aíthō* ("to burn") or a Phoenician word for "furnace." In mythology, it was the forge of the god Vulcan/Hephaestus and the prison of the monster Typhon. The name "Mongibello" combines Romance and Arabic words for "mountain." In Arthurian legend, the volcano was associated with the otherworldly realm of Morgan le Fay and King Arthur.

### Geological History and Eruption Patterns
Volcanic activity began about 500,000 years ago. The volcano's growth was interrupted by major collapses, including the formation of the Valle del Bove c. 8,000 years ago. Eruptions occur at five summit craters or from over 300 flank vents. Since 1600, at least 60 flank eruptions have occurred, nearly half since 1900. Flank eruptions are more hazardous as they can occur near inhabited areas.

### Historical Eruptions
The first known report is from c. 1400 BCE. An eruption in 396 BCE reportedly halted a Carthaginian advance. The 1669 eruption was the most destructive historically, though contemporary accounts record no fatalities from the lava itself. Only 77 deaths are certainly attributable to Etna's eruptions in historical times, the last in 1987.

### Modern Activity (1923–Present)
Etna is in a near-constant state of activity. Notable 20th-century eruptions include 1928 (destroying Mascali) and 1991–1993 (where lava was diverted from Zafferana Etnea). The 2002–2003 eruption was massive, with ash reaching Libya. Recent decades feature frequent summit and flank eruptions, often disrupting air travel at Catania Airport. The volcano's height varies with summit activity; the Voragine crater became the highest point after summer 2024 eruptions.

## Terms
- **Stratovolcano**: A conical volcano built up by many layers (strata) of hardened lava and tephra.
- **Caldera**: A large cauldron-like hollow that forms shortly after the emptying of a magma chamber in a volcanic eruption.
- **Plinian eruption**: A highly explosive volcanic eruption characterized by a towering column of gas and ash.
- **Flank eruption**: An eruption occurring on the side of a volcano, as opposed to at the summit.
- **Tephra**: Fragmentary material ejected by a volcanic eruption.
- **Pyroclastic flow**: A fast-moving current of hot gas and volcanic matter that flows down the side of a volcano.
- **Lava tube**: A natural conduit formed by flowing lava which moves beneath the hardened surface of a lava flow.
- **Strombolian eruption**: A moderate volcanic eruption with relatively mild blasts of incandescent lava fragments.
- **VEI (Volcanic Explosivity Index)**: A measure of the explosiveness of volcanic eruptions, from 0 (non-explosive) to 8 (mega-colossal).

## Debates and Open Questions
The etymology of "Etna" is debated, with Greek, Siculian, and Phoenician origins proposed. The cause of a famine in 43 BCE, once linked to an Etna eruption in 44 BCE, is now more likely attributed to an eruption of Mount Okmok. The exact number of fatalities from the 1669 eruption is uncertain, with modern research suggesting the commonly cited figures of 15,000–20,000 may be confused with the 1693 Sicily earthquake.
