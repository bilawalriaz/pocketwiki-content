# Appalachian Mountains

## Overview
The Appalachian Mountains are a vast, ancient mountain range in eastern North America, stretching over 2,000 miles from Alabama to Newfoundland. They form the physical and ecological backbone of the eastern continent, influencing climate, biodiversity, and human settlement patterns. Formed by a series of tectonic collisions over hundreds of millions of years, they are among the oldest mountains on Earth, now significantly eroded but still a major geographic and cultural barrier.

## Timeline
- **c. 1.1 billion years ago** — Grenville Orogeny begins with the collision of cratons to form supercontinent Rodinia.
- **c. 600-560 million years ago** — Breakup of Rodinia creates the Iapetus Ocean and volcanic activity in the Blue Ridge region.
- **c. 500-470 million years ago** — Taconic Orogeny: An oceanic plate collides with North America, building early mountains.
- **c. 375-359 million years ago** — Acadian Orogeny: Collisions with the Avalonia Terrane further build the northern Appalachians.
- **c. 270 million years ago** — Alleghenian Orogeny: North America and Africa collide, forming the supercontinent Pangea and the Central Pangean Mountains.
- **1528** — Spanish explorers encounter the Apalachee tribe, giving the range its name.
- **1565** — First known use of the name "Appalachian" for the mountain range on a map.
- **1859** — Discovery of commercial petroleum in western Pennsylvania starts the U.S. oil industry.
- **1977** — Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act is passed to regulate coal mining.

## Body

### Etymology and Naming
The name "Appalachian" derives from the Apalachee, a Native American tribe encountered by Spanish explorers in Florida in 1528. The name was first applied to the mountains on maps in the 1560s but did not become the common term for the entire range until the late 19th century, competing with names like "Allegheny." Pronunciation varies regionally, with a common debate between "latch" and "lay" sounds for the third syllable.

### Geography and Geology
The range is geologically defined by a series of ancient orogenies (mountain-building events). The foundational rocks were formed during the Grenville Orogeny over a billion years ago. The modern Appalachians were shaped by three later collisions: the Taconic, Acadian, and Alleghenian orogenies, which culminated in the formation of the supercontinent Pangea. Subsequent erosion over 240 million years reduced the once-Alpine peaks to their current rugged, lower elevations. The range is divided into physiographic provinces, including the Blue Ridge, Valley and Ridge, and the Appalachian Plateau. It contains major mineral resources, notably anthracite and bituminous coal, and its drainage patterns form the Eastern Continental Divide.

### Ecology
The Appalachians are a hotspot of biodiversity due to their north-south orientation, which allowed species to migrate during climate shifts. Forests vary by latitude and elevation, from boreal spruce-fir forests in the north and at high elevations to diverse deciduous hardwood forests in the south. Iconic flora includes red spruce, Fraser fir, sugar maple, and oak-chestnut forests (now dominated by oaks after the chestnut blight). Fauna includes black bears, white-tailed deer, and a remarkable diversity of salamanders, which represent a large portion of the forest's animal biomass. Threats include invasive species like the hemlock woolly adelgid and historical impacts from logging and coal mining.

### Human Impact and Economy
The mountains have historically been a barrier to east-west travel, shaping U.S. expansion. Their economy was long based on logging and coal mining, which attracted migrants and created the distinct Appalachian cultural region. The decline of these industries led to economic hardship, but many communities have shifted to manufacturing, services, and tourism. The Appalachian Trail, a 2,175-mile hiking path from Georgia to Maine, is a major recreational and economic asset.

## Terms
- **Orogeny**: A mountain-building event caused by tectonic plate collisions.
- **Craton**: A stable, ancient part of a continental lithosphere.
- **Physiographic region**: A landform division based on geology, topography, and history.
- **Ridge and Valley**: A province of folded sedimentary rock creating parallel ridges and valleys.
- **Dissected plateau**: A plateau that has been deeply eroded by rivers, creating rugged terrain.
- **Boreal forest**: A northern coniferous forest dominated by spruce and fir.
- **Mesophytic**: A forest type with moderate moisture, supporting diverse hardwoods.
- **Appalachian bald**: A treeless summit, either grassy or heath-covered, with debated origins.

## Debates and Open questions
- The precise origin of Appalachian balds is debated; they may result from a combination of Pleistocene climate, megafauna grazing, Native American burning, and later livestock grazing.
- The exact pronunciation of "Appalachian" is a point of regional debate and identity.
- The ecological future of the range is uncertain due to ongoing threats from invasive species, climate change, and the legacy of extractive industries.
