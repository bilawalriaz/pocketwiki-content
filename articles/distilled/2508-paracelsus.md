# Paracelsus

## Overview
Paracelsus (1493–1541) was a Swiss physician, alchemist, and philosopher of the German Renaissance who challenged traditional Galenic medicine. He pioneered the use of chemicals and minerals in treatment, emphasized observation and experience, and is considered the father of toxicology. His work laid the foundation for modern chemistry and medicine, though his ideas were often intertwined with hermetic and astrological beliefs.

## Timeline
- **1493** — Born Theophrastus Bombast von Hohenheim in Einsiedeln, Switzerland.
- **c. 1515-16** — Receives medical doctorate from the University of Ferrara.
- **1517–24** — Travels extensively across Europe, working as an army surgeon.
- **1527** — Appointed city physician in Basel; lectures in German and publicly burns works of Galen and Avicenna.
- **1528** — Leaves Basel after threats; the name "Paracelsus" first appears in print.
- **1530** — Completes *Paragranum*, a major work on medical philosophy.
- **1536** — Publishes *Die große Wundarzney* ("The Great Surgery Book").
- **1537** — Completes *Astronomia magna*, a treatise on hermeticism and divination.
- **1541** — Dies in Salzburg, Austria.

## Body

### Biography and Career
Born to a physician father, Paracelsus received early education in botany, medicine, and mining. After earning his doctorate, he traveled widely, gaining practical experience as a military surgeon. His brief tenure as a professor in Basel (1527–28) was marked by radical acts, such as lecturing in German instead of Latin and burning ancient medical texts to protest established authority. He spent his later years as an itinerant physician and writer, often facing opposition from the medical establishment.

### Philosophy and Scientific Approach
Paracelsus rejected the ancient Greek theory of the four humours (blood, phlegm, black bile, yellow bile) as the cause of disease. Instead, he proposed the *tria prima*: three principles—sulphur (combustibility), mercury (liquidity), and salt (solidity)—which he believed composed all matter and whose imbalance caused illness. He integrated hermetic philosophy, viewing the human body as a microcosm reflecting the macrocosm of the universe. He argued that disease was caused by external agents and that "like cures like," meaning a substance causing symptoms in a healthy person could cure similar symptoms in a sick one.

### Medical and Chemical Contributions
Paracelsus introduced chemical and mineral-based remedies into medicine, advocating for specific treatments over general purges. He is credited with coining the terms "chemistry," "gas," and "alcohol." His principle of toxicology, "Sola dosis facit venenum" ("Only the dose makes the poison"), established that any substance can be toxic depending on the dosage. He advocated for wound cleanliness, anticipating antisepsis, and recognized the inherited nature of syphilis. He also pioneered the study of occupational diseases in miners.

### Legacy and Influence
After his death, Paracelsus's unpublished works were edited and widely read, inspiring the medical movement known as Paracelsianism. His ideas were embraced by Rosicrucians, who viewed him as a prophet. While his alchemical and astrological views remained controversial, his emphasis on observation, experience, and chemical therapy profoundly influenced the development of modern medicine and chemistry.

## Terms
- **Tria prima**: Paracelsus's theory that all matter is composed of three principles: sulphur, mercury, and salt.
- **Microcosm-macrocosm**: The hermetic belief that the human body (microcosm) is a reflection of the universe (macrocosm).
- **Doctrine of signatures**: The idea that plants resembling body parts can treat ailments of those parts.
- **Galenic medicine**: The ancient system of medicine based on the four humours, which Paracelsus opposed.
- **Hermeticism**: A philosophical tradition emphasizing the unity of the universe and the correspondence between the spiritual and material worlds.
- **Spagyrics**: Paracelsus's term for his approach to medicine, combining alchemy, astrology, and religion.
- **Laudanum**: An opium-based preparation attributed to Paracelsus, though its exact original recipe is unknown.
- **Antisepsis**: The practice of preventing infection by keeping wounds clean, which Paracelsus advocated.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The authenticity of many works attributed to Paracelsus is disputed, as numerous "Pseudo-Paracelsian" writings circulated after his death.
- Scholars debate the extent to which his medical innovations were separate from or dependent on his alchemical and astrological beliefs.
- The origin and meaning of his adopted name "Paracelsus" are uncertain, with interpretations ranging from "surpassing Celsus" to a Latinization of his birthplace.
