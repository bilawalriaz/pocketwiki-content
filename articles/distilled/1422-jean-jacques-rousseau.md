# Jean-Jacques Rousseau

## Overview
Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778) was a Genevan philosopher, writer, and composer whose radical critique of civilization reshaped modern political, educational, and literary thought. He argued that humans are naturally good but corrupted by society, private property, and the arts and sciences, which foster inequality and dependence. His major works—*Discourse on Inequality* (1755), *The Social Contract* (1762), *Émile* (1762), and *Julie* (1761)—established foundations for democratic theory, child-centered education, and Romanticism. His concept of the "general will" (the collective sovereign will aiming at the common good) influenced the French Revolution, while his autobiographical *Confessions* (1782) pioneered the modern genre of introspective memoir. Persecuted for religious heterodoxy and hounded across Europe, Rousseau died in 1778, leaving a legacy claimed by revolutionaries, romantics, and critics of modernity alike.

- **1749–1750** — Wins Académie de Dijon prize for *Discourse on the Arts and Sciences*, arguing progress corrupts morals.
- **1752** — Opera *Le devin du village* performed for Louis XV; refuses royal pension.
- **1754** — Returns to Geneva; reconverts to Calvinism; regains citizenship.
- **1755** — Publishes *Discourse on Inequality*; contributes "Political Economy" article to *Encyclopédie*.
- **1761** — Publishes novel *Julie, ou la nouvelle Héloïse*, a bestseller inspiring Romanticism.
- **April–May 1762** — Publishes *The Social Contract* and *Émile*; both condemned and burned in Paris and Geneva; arrest warrant issued.
- **1762–1765** — Flees to Neuchâtel (Prussia), Môtiers, Île de St.-Pierre; expelled by Bernese authorities.
- **Jan 1766** — Arrives in England with David Hume; public quarrel erupts (fueled by Horace Walpole hoax letter).
- **May 1767** — Returns to France under alias; lives near Grenoble; completes *Confessions*.
- **1770** — Public readings of *Confessions* halted by police at Madame d'Épinay's request.
- **1772** — Publishes *Considerations on the Government of Poland*; writes *Rousseau, Judge of Jean-Jacques*.
- **24 Oct 1776** — Knocked down by a Great Dane in Paris; suffers concussion and neurological decline.
- **1777–1778** — Composes *Reveries of the Solitary Walker*.
- **2 July 1778** — Dies of cerebral bleeding (apoplectic stroke) at Ermenonville.
- **11 Oct 1794** — Remains transferred to the Panthéon in Paris.

## Body

### Youth and Formative Experiences
Rousseau was born in Geneva, a Calvinist city-state governed by an oligarchic Small Council despite theoretical popular sovereignty. His mother died days after his birth; his father, a watchmaker, fled Geneva after a legal dispute when Rousseau was ten, leaving him with relatives. Apprenticed to an engraver who beat him, Rousseau fled at 15 to Savoy, where the noblewoman Françoise-Louise de Warens converted him to Catholicism (costing his Genevan citizenship) and became his patron, lover, and intellectual gateway. Largely self-educated, he devoured Plutarch’s *Lives*, which instilled a "free and republican spirit," and witnessed local militias, which he idealized as expressions of popular sovereignty versus mercenary armies. These experiences—Genevan political tension, maternal loss, paternal abandonment, and de Warens’s maternal tutelage—shaped his lifelong themes: the corruption of natural goodness by artificial society, the legitimacy of popular sovereignty, and the longing for authentic community.

### The Turn to Philosophy and the *Discourses*
In Paris (1742), Rousseau failed to monetize a musical notation system but entered Diderot’s circle. In 1749, walking to visit the imprisoned Diderot, he had a "revelation" that the arts and sciences degrade morality rather than improve it. His prize-winning *Discourse on the Arts and Sciences* (1750) launched his fame. He expanded this thesis in *Discourse on Inequality* (1755): the first person to fence land and say "This is mine" founded civil society and unleashed wars, crimes, and misery. In the state of nature, humans possess *amour de soi* (self-preservation) and *pitié* (empathy), living solitary, equal, and free. Social life, agriculture, metallurgy, and private property created dependence, inequality, and *amour-propre* (vanity/status-seeking), corrupting natural goodness. Rousseau denied humans are naturally wicked (contra Hobbes); rather, society makes them so. This "conjectural history" was not literal anthropology but a normative device to judge existing institutions.

### Political Theory: *The Social Contract*
*The Social Contract* (1762) asks how humans can live under law yet remain free. The solution: each alienates all natural rights to the community, receiving civil liberty and property rights in return. Sovereignty resides in the **general will** (*volonté générale*)—the collective will aiming at the common good, distinct from the "will of all" (sum of private interests). The general will is inalienable, indivisible, and infallible regarding its object (the common good), though citizens may err in discerning it. Government (magistrates) is merely an executive agent; the sovereign (the people assembled) legislates. Rousseau insisted on direct democracy in small city-states (like Geneva, ideally reformed), rejecting representation as surrender of freedom. "Forced to be free" means obeying laws one prescribed to oneself as a citizen. The work’s quasi-mathematical structure derives political legitimacy from axioms of equality and subordination to the general will.

### Education and *Émile*
*Émile* (1762) is a treatise on raising a natural man in corrupt society. Education follows developmental stages: infancy (0–12) guided by impulse and natural consequences; pre-adolescence (12–16) where reason awakens; adolescence (16+) where moral autonomy and a manual trade (e.g., carpentry) are cultivated. The tutor arranges environments so the pupil learns from experience, not precept. The famous "Profession of Faith of a Savoyard Vicar" (Book IV) defends a natural, deistic religion based on conscience and the wonder of creation, rejecting original sin and revelation—causing the book’s condemnation in Catholic France and Calvinist Geneva. Sophie, Émile’s destined wife, is educated for domestic subordination, reflecting Rousseau’s view that the patriarchal family (wife dependent, husband self-governing) is the necessary private foundation for the public political order. Feminists from Mary Wollstonecraft onward have condemned this gender asymmetry.

### Religion, Deism, and the *Confessions*
Rousseau oscillated between Catholicism (youth) and Calvinism (Genevan return), but his mature view was an emotional deism: God is immanent in nature’s beauty, distinct from society’s corruption. He affirmed religion’s necessity for social cohesion but denied original sin ("there is no original perversity in the human heart"). His "civil religion" (*Social Contract*, Book IV) mandates minimal dogmas (God, afterlife, sanctity of social contract) while tolerating all sects that tolerate others. This indifferentism outraged both churches. The *Confessions* (completed 1770, published 1782) invented modern autobiography: unflinching self-exposure (including abandoning five children to foundling hospitals) framed as moral anatomy. Public readings in Paris (1770–71) captivated audiences until police halted them. *Reveries of the Solitary Walker* (1776–78) continued this introspective, botanical, and meditative project.

### Music and Aesthetics
Rousseau composed seven operas (notably *Le devin du village*, 1752), motets, and a melodrama (*Pygmalion*). He championed Italian melody over French harmony (Querelle des Bouffons), arguing melody expresses free feeling—anticipating Romantic aesthetics. He invented a numbered notation system and a "boustrophedon" staff layout. His musical writings influenced Gluck and Mozart; Beethoven later arranged a duet from *Le devin du village*. Rousseau eventually renounced theater as incompatible with moral rigor.

### Final Years and Posthumous Reception
Hounded from Geneva, Bern, and France, Rousseau found temporary refuge in Prussia (Môtiers), then England (1766) with Hume. A hoax letter (by Horace Walpole) and press attacks triggered a paranoid rupture with Hume. Returning to France (1767), he lived under aliases, married Thérèse Levasseur (1768), and completed major works. A 1776 carriage accident caused neurological decline. He died at Ermenonville in 1778. Initially buried on the Île des Peupliers, he was reinterred in the Panthéon (1794) near Voltaire. His influence spans the French Revolution (Robespierre cited the general will), Romanticism (Goethe, Wordsworth, Tolstoy), progressive education (Pestalozzi, Montessori, Dewey), and modern democratic theory—while critics from Burke to Arendt blamed his "general will" for totalitarian tendencies.

## Terms
- ****amour de soi**** — Natural, benign self-preservation instinct; distinct from *amour-propre*.
- ****amour-propre**** — Vanity or pride derived from comparative status; a social corruption of *amour de soi*.
- ****pitié**** — Natural empathy for one’s species; a pre-rational check on egoism in the state of nature.
- ****state of nature**** — Hypothetical pre-social condition of equality, freedom, and solitude; used as normative benchmark.
- ****perfectibility**** — Uniquely human capacity to improve oneself and one’s condition through free choice; source of both progress and corruption.
- ****general will (volonté générale)**** — The sovereign collective will aiming at the common good; inalienable, indivisible, and distinct from the sum of private wills.
- ****civil religion**** — Minimal state-mandated creed (God, afterlife, social contract sanctity, tolerance) to bind citizens without sectarian dogma.
- ****natural consequences**** — Pedagogical method where children learn from the direct results of their actions rather than imposed punishment.
- ****Socinianism / Unitarianism**** — Anti-Trinitarian theology (denying original sin and divine revelation) voiced by the Savoyard Vicar in *Émile*.
- ****Querelle des Bouffons**** — 1750s Parisian debate pitting French harmonic tradition against Italian melodic opera; Rousseau championed the Italians.

## Debates and open questions
- **Totalitarian thesis**: Does the general will legitimize coercion of dissenters? Critics (Constant, Arendt, Talmon) see seeds of totalitarian democracy; defenders (e.g., from *Discourse on Political Economy*) stress it protects individuals *against* the mass.
- **Gender and the family**: Is Sophie’s subordination essential to Rousseau’s system or a contingent bias? Feminists argue it undermines his egalitarianism; some scholars see it as structural to his public/private distinction.
- **Historicity of the state of nature**: Rousseau called his conjectural history "hypothetical"; debate persists whether it is a thought experiment, a stadial history, or a mythic critique.
- **Representation vs. direct democracy**: Rousseau rejected representation as incompatible with sovereignty; modern democracies rely on it. Can his principles scale?
- **Religious indifferentism vs. civil religion**: Does the civil religion’s coercive minimalism contradict his plea for toleration in *Émile*?
- **Autobiographical reliability**: The *Confessions* is the primary source for his youth; scholars note chronological confusion and self-mythologizing, though archives largely corroborate key events.
- **Economic thought**: Interpretations range from agrarian autarkist (Schumpeter) to theorist of regulated commerce (Hont, Hanley, Li).
