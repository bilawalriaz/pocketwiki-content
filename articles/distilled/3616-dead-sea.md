# Dead Sea

## Overview
The Dead Sea is a hypersaline, landlocked lake bordered by Jordan, Israel, and the West Bank. It is the lowest point on Earth's land surface and one of the world's saltiest bodies of water, making it a unique geological, historical, and therapeutic site. Its extreme salinity and receding shoreline due to human activity make it a focal point for environmental concern.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — The Sedom Lagoon, a precursor to the Dead Sea, is inundated by Mediterranean waters.
- **c. 70,000–14,000 BP** — Lake Lisan, a larger predecessor, exists with levels 100–250 m higher than today.
- **150 BCE–70 CE** — The Dead Sea Scrolls are written and hidden in caves near Qumran.
- **66–73 CE** — The First Jewish–Roman War culminates in the siege of Masada on its western shore.
- **1929** — The Palestine Potash Company is granted a concession to extract minerals.
- **1947–1956** — Dead Sea Scrolls are discovered in caves near Qumran.
- **1964** — Israel's National Water Carrier diverts water from the Jordan River, accelerating the Dead Sea's recession.
- **1978–79** — A major mixing event homogenizes the previously stratified lake.
- **2021** — The Red Sea–Dead Sea Water Conveyance project is officially abandoned.
- **2025** — The lake's surface is measured at 439.78 m below sea level.

## Body

### Geography and Formation
The Dead Sea lies in the Jordan Rift Valley, a tectonic depression formed by the Dead Sea Transform fault. It is an endorheic basin, meaning it has no outlet streams. Its main tributary is the Jordan River, though its flow has been heavily diverted. The lake's extreme depth (304 m) and low elevation are results of ongoing tectonic activity. Geologists debate whether the basin is a true rift zone or formed by a "step-over" discontinuity along the transform fault.

### Geology and Chemistry
The lake formed from the ancient Sedom Lagoon, which was repeatedly flooded by the Mediterranean Sea. As the lagoon became landlocked, evaporation created thick salt deposits, including the 2.3 km thick rock salt layer beneath Mount Sodom. The Dead Sea's salinity is approximately 34.2%, making it 9.6 times saltier than the ocean. Its unique mineral composition is dominated by magnesium chloride (50.8%) and sodium chloride (30.4%), unlike ocean water which is mostly sodium chloride. This high salinity creates a density (1.24 kg/litre) that allows for easy floating.

### Human History and Settlement
The region has been inhabited for millennia, with biblical cities like Sodom and Gomorrah traditionally located near its shores. It served as a refuge and resource site, with bitumen (asphalt) used for Egyptian mummification. During the Hellenistic and Roman periods, the Essene sect settled at Qumran, producing the Dead Sea Scrolls. The area saw conflict during the First Jewish–Roman War, ending with the siege of Masada. Modern settlement includes Israeli hotels at Ein Bokek and Jordanian resorts on the eastern shore.

### Industry and Tourism
The Dead Sea is a major source of potash and bromine. The Dead Sea Works (Israel) and Arab Potash (Jordan) use evaporation ponds in the southern basin to extract minerals, generating billions in annual revenue. Tourism is a key industry, with hotels on both the Israeli and Jordanian sides. The unique climate, with high atmospheric pressure and reduced UV radiation, supports health tourism focused on climatotherapy and balneotherapy for conditions like psoriasis.

### Recession and Environmental Concerns
The Dead Sea's surface area has shrunk from 1,050 km² in 1930 to 605 km² today, with the water level dropping over 1 meter per year. This is primarily due to the diversion of the Jordan River for agriculture and industry. The receding water has caused groundwater levels to drop, leading to the formation of thousands of sinkholes along the shoreline, which have forced the closure of some resorts. A major project to pipe water from the Red Sea was abandoned in 2021, leaving the lake's future uncertain.

## Terms
- **Endorheic Basin:** A closed drainage basin that retains water and allows no outflow to other external bodies of water.
- **Hypersaline:** Having a very high concentration of dissolved salts, typically higher than that of seawater.
- **Dead Sea Transform (DST):** A left-lateral-moving transform fault forming part of the boundary between the African and Arabian tectonic plates.
- **Evaporites:** Sedimentary rocks formed by the evaporation of saline water, such as rock salt (halite) and gypsum.
- **Potash:** A potassium-rich salt used primarily as an agricultural fertilizer.
- **Climatotherapy:** Treatment that exploits local climatic features like temperature, humidity, and sunshine.
- **Sinkholes:** Cavities in the ground caused by the collapse of a surface layer, often due to the dissolution of underlying soluble rock like salt.
- **Carnallite:** A potassium magnesium chloride mineral used as a source of potassium.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact geological mechanism creating the Dead Sea's low elevation is debated: is it a true rift zone or a result of a "step-over" discontinuity along the transform fault?
- The precise cause of the dramatic drop in the lake's level around 10,000 years ago is unknown, though it is ruled out to be volcanic activity.
- The long-term viability of the Dead Sea's ecosystem and the potential environmental impact of any large-scale water replenishment project remain open questions.
