# George Berkeley

## Overview
George Berkeley (1685–1753) was an Anglo-Irish philosopher, writer, and Anglican clergyman whose theory of **immaterialism**—also called subjective idealism—denies the existence of material substance. His central claim, expressed in the Latin phrase *esse est percipi* ("to be is to be perceived"), holds that objects exist only insofar as they are perceived by minds, ultimately sustained by God’s eternal perception. A pioneer of British empiricism and a major figure in 18th-century philosophy, Berkeley influenced thinkers like Hume and Kant, and his critiques of Newtonian physics and calculus shaped early modern debates in science and mathematics.

## Body

### Immaterialism and the Nature of Reality
Berkeley argued that only two kinds of entities exist: **spirits** (active, perceiving minds) and **ideas** (passive, perceived contents). He rejected the notion of material substance, asserting that physical objects are merely collections of ideas caused by God and perceived by finite minds. His famous dictum *esse est percipi* encapsulates this view: nothing exists independently of being perceived. To counter the objection that unperceived objects would cease to exist, Berkeley invoked God as the infinite mind that perpetually perceives all things.

### Critique of Abstraction and Perception
Berkeley criticized the empiricist concept of **abstraction**, arguing that one cannot form an idea of an object without its sensible qualities (e.g., color, shape). This critique underpins his rejection of abstract material objects. In his *New Theory of Vision*, he challenged the Optic Writers’ assumption that spatial depth is directly seen, proposing instead that distance judgments arise from learned associations between visual and tactile sensations.

### Philosophy of Science and Mathematics
Berkeley opposed Newton’s concepts of absolute space, time, and motion, viewing forces like gravity as “occult qualities” beyond empirical observation. In *The Analyst* (1734), he famously derided infinitesimals in calculus as “ghosts of departed quantities,” arguing that the method lacked rigorous logical foundations. Though critical of its metaphysics, he acknowledged its practical utility.

### Theology and Moral Philosophy
A devout Christian, Berkeley saw God as the immediate cause of sensory experience and the guarantor of order in nature. In *A Discourse on Passive Obedience* (1712), he defended political submission to lawful authority based on divine command and natural law, anticipating elements of utilitarian thinking while maintaining a deontological stance against resistance.

### Legacy and Influence
Berkeley’s ideas profoundly influenced later philosophers, including Hume and Kant. His works gained renewed attention post–World War II due to their relevance to 20th-century issues in epistemology, language, and perception. In America, his name adorns institutions such as UC Berkeley and Berkeley College at Yale, reflecting his lasting cultural impact.

## Terms

- **Immaterialism**: Berkeley’s doctrine denying material substance; reality consists solely of minds and ideas.
- **Esse est percipi**: Latin for “to be is to be perceived”; core principle of Berkeley’s idealism.
- **Subjective Idealism**: The view that reality is mentally constructed; synonymous with immaterialism.
- **Occult Qualities**: Hidden forces (like Newtonian gravity) that Berkeley argued lie beyond empirical verification.
- **Abstraction**: The process of forming general ideas by mentally separating qualities from particular instances; Berkeley rejected this as impossible.
- **Primary vs. Secondary Qualities**: Locke’s distinction between intrinsic properties (shape, size) and subjective ones (color, taste); Berkeley extended skepticism to both.
- **Fluxion**: Newton’s term for infinitesimal change in calculus; Berkeley criticized its logical coherence.
- **Passive Obedience**: The moral duty not to resist civil or ecclesiastical authority, defended by Berkeley.
- **Theory of Signs**: Berkeley’s attempt to explain motion and matter through symbolic representation rather than occult forces.
- **Empiricism**: The philosophical stance that knowledge derives primarily from sensory experience.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Consistency of Immaterialism**: Critics argue that Berkeley’s reliance on God to sustain unperceived objects introduces an unverifiable entity into his ontology, undermining his empirical foundations.
- **Relativity Argument**: Berkeley’s claim that perception varies with observer position seems to conflict with *esse est percipi*, raising questions about whether relative perceptions can ground objective reality.
- **Abstract Ideas**: While Berkeley denies the possibility of conceiving matter apart from sensation, some argue that abstract thought is essential to scientific reasoning.
- **Calculus Critique**: Modern interpretations suggest Berkeley misunderstood Leibnizian calculus; nonetheless, his concerns contributed to later formalizations by Cauchy and Weierstrass.
- **Political Philosophy**: Berkeley’s defense of passive obedience clashes with emerging liberal democratic ideals, prompting debate over the compatibility of divine-right theory with secular governance.
- **Legacy of Slavery**: Recent reassessments highlight Berkeley’s ownership of enslaved people and defense of slavery, complicating his moral and intellectual legacy.
