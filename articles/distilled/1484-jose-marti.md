# José Martí

## Overview
José Julián Martí Pérez (1853–1895) was a Cuban nationalist, poet, journalist, and political theorist revered as the "Apostle of Cuban Independence." Through prolific writing and transnational organizing, he unified the Cuban émigré community—particularly tobacco workers in Florida—into a cohesive revolutionary force, founding the Cuban Revolutionary Party (CRP) in 1892 to wage a democratic war against Spanish colonial rule. Martí’s ideology fused liberal republicanism, anti-imperialism, and a vision of a multiracial, egalitarian republic, while warning that U.S. expansionism posed a greater long-term threat to Latin American sovereignty than Spanish colonialism. He died in combat at the Battle of Dos Ríos in 1895, becoming a martyr whose legacy has been claimed by both the Cuban communist government and its dissident opponents.

- **1892–94** — Extensive organizing tours through Florida, U.S., Caribbean, and Central America; meets Gómez and Maceo to plan uprising.

- **19 May 1895** — Killed at Battle of Dos Ríos; buried in Santa Ifigenia Cemetery, Santiago de Cuba.

## Body

### Early Formation and Exile (1853–1878)
Martí’s political consciousness formed early. His mentor Rafael María de Mendive introduced him to Krausist liberalism, emphasizing spirituality, solidarity, and democratic participation. The 1868 Ten Years' War catalyzed his activism; at 16, he was imprisoned for a letter criticizing a peer who joined the Spanish army. The brutal conditions—chains lacerated his legs—forced a transfer to Isla de Pinos and eventual exile to Spain (1871). In Madrid and Zaragoza, he earned law degrees, published *Political Imprisonment in Cuba* to sway Spanish public opinion, and protested the 1871 execution of eight medical students. When the First Spanish Republic (1873) reaffirmed Cuba as inseparable from Spain, Martí replied with "The Spanish Republic and the Cuban Revolution," arguing Cubans constituted a distinct people with separate trade, customs, and aspirations, united only by "ties of fraternity and love." Prevented from returning to Cuba, he migrated to Mexico and Guatemala (1875–78), teaching literature, writing for *Revista Universal*, and marrying Carmen Zayas Bazán.

### The New York Years: Journalism, Theory, and Organization (1881–1894)
Expelled from Venezuela for criticizing dictator Antonio Guzmán Blanco, Martí settled in New York (1881), becoming a prolific correspondent for Latin American newspapers (*La Nación*, *La Opinion Liberal*) and a translator for Appleton & Co. He served as consul for Uruguay, Argentina, and Paraguay, resigning in 1891 after Spanish diplomatic pressure. His 1881 article on the Guiteau trial was later anthologized in *The Library of America*. Martí’s political theory matured in this period: he opposed slavery as a Spanish crime, warned that the U.S. sought to buy Cuba for its strategic Gulf position and industrial markets, and argued Latin America must modernize by adopting U.S. work ethic and educational pragmatism while rejecting its political corruption, racism, and imperialism. He broke with generals Máximo Gómez and Antonio Maceo in 1884, fearing a military dictatorship would replace Spanish rule; he insisted a democratic civilian structure—the Cuban Revolutionary Party (CRP)—must precede and command any military action.

### Founding the Cuban Revolutionary Party and the 1895 War
Martí’s "Nuestra América" (1891) articulated a Latin American identity rooted in autochthonous institutions rather than imported French or U.S. codes. In January 1892, the *Bases del Partido Revolucionario* were ratified in Key West; Martí was elected delegate and launched *Patria* as the party organ. He toured Florida tobacco factories, delivering speeches like "Con Todos, y para el Bien de Todos" (Nov 1891) that united workers across race and class—considered his greatest political achievement. The CRP platform demanded a multiracial republic with universal suffrage, equitable land distribution, and civilian control over the military. Martí coordinated with Gómez (Montecristi, June 1893) and Maceo (Costa Rica, July 1893) for a simultaneous uprising. The *Plan de Fernandina* (arms shipment from Florida) was intercepted by U.S. authorities in Jan 1895, but the *Grito de Baire* launched the war on 24 Feb 1895. Martí and Gómez issued the *Manifesto de Montecristi* (Mar 1895), defining the revolution as a democratic, non-racial, anti-annexationist struggle.

### Final Campaign and Death
Martí landed at Playitas (11 Apr 1895) with a small expedition. The anticipated popular uprising failed to materialize immediately. On 19 May, at Dos Ríos, General Gómez ordered a retreat against a strong Spanish position; Martí, separated from the rearguard and conspicuous in a black jacket on a white horse, charged forward shouting "Joven, ¡a la carga!" He was killed instantly. The Spanish exhumed his body to confirm his identity and buried him in Santiago de Cuba. His death devastated the rebel leadership, but the war continued until U.S. intervention in 1898.

### Political Ideology: Liberalism, Anti-Imperialism, and the *Patria*
Martí’s liberalism was radicalized by Latin American conditions: nationalist, anti-imperialist, and influenced by Georgism (land-value tax). He viewed Cuban independence as unnatural under Spain but feared U.S. annexation more, writing that U.S. expansionism was Spanish America’s "greatest danger." He admired U.S. civil liberties, work ethic, and public education, urging Latin Americans to send representatives to learn industrial techniques, yet condemned U.S. political corruption, racism, and the "carnival atmosphere" of elections. His revolutionary tactic centered on the *patria* (fatherland) as a moral community transcending race and class. The CRP was designed to prevent the autonomist *Partido Autonomista* from settling for home rule. Martí insisted the military subordinate to civilian democratic goals, envisioning a republic with "enlightened and virtuous politicians" and an economy developing Cuba’s productive resources for all citizens.

### Writings and Modernismo
Martí’s output—essays, journalism, poetry, a novel (*Amistad funesta*), and the children’s magazine *La Edad de Oro* (1889)—spans over fifty volumes. He wrote for newspapers across the hemisphere: *El Diablo Cojuelo* (Cuba), *Revista Universal* (Mexico), *Revista Venezolana* (founded by him), *La Nación* (Buenos Aires), and *The Hour* (U.S.). His style blends aphorism, complex syntax, and "dioramic" chronicles, making him a founder of *Modernismo*. He viewed prose as a civic tool to elevate ideas; poetry as the language of permanent intuition. *Versos Sencillos* (1891), written in Haines Falls, NY, is autobiographical; its verses were later adapted into "Guantanamera." *La Edad de Oro* aimed to teach children "real magic" of industry and science. As a translator (English, French, Italian, Latin, Greek), he insisted translation "should be natural, so that it appears that the book were written in the language to which it has been translated."

## Terms
- ****Apostle of Cuban Independence**** — Honorific for Martí, reflecting his role as the moral and intellectual leader of the 1895 independence movement.
- ****Cuban Revolutionary Party (CRP)**** — Political party founded by Martí in 1892 (*Bases del Partido Revolucionario*) to organize the war of independence under civilian, democratic control.
- ****Modernismo**** — Spanish American literary movement (c. 1880–1920) characterized by subjective language, musicality, and aesthetic renewal; Martí is a key precursor.
- ****Nuestra América**** — Martí’s 1891 essay arguing for a Latin American identity based on autochthonous history, culture, and institutions, resisting U.S. cultural/political hegemony.
- ****Patria**** — Fatherland; Martí’s concept of a moral-political community uniting Cubans across race/class; also the name of the CRP’s official newspaper (1892).
- ****Plan de Fernandina**** — 1895 arms-smuggling operation from Fernandina, Florida, intercepted by U.S. authorities, delaying the expeditionary launch.
- ****Ten Years' War (1868–78)**** — First Cuban war for independence; ended by the Pact of Zanjón (1878), which granted limited reforms but not independence or abolition of slavery.
- ****Versos Sencillos**** — Martí’s 1891 poetry collection, written in New York; source of the lyrics for "Guantanamera."
- ****Krausism**** — Philosophy of Karl Christian Friedrich Krause, emphasizing rational spirituality, academic freedom, and social solidarity; influenced Martí via mentor Mendive.
- ****Manifesto de Montecristi**** — March 1895 proclamation by Martí and Gómez defining the revolution’s democratic, anti-racist, anti-annexationist principles.

## Debates and open questions
- **Martí’s political legacy**: The post-1959 Cuban government claims Martí as a forerunner of socialism and single-party rule, citing his anti-imperialism and social justice concerns. Dissidents and liberal scholars counter that Martí was a classical liberal committed to multi-party democracy, civil liberties, and the U.S. republican model (minus imperialism), noting his explicit criticisms of Marx and authoritarianism.
- **Race and the revolution**: Martí insisted on a raceless republic ("Cuban means more than white, more than black, more than mulatto"), yet some historians argue the 1895 leadership remained predominantly white and that Martí’s "raceless" rhetoric may have suppressed Afro-Cuban demands for specific redress.
- **U.S. relations**: Martí’s simultaneous admiration for U.S. civil society and fear of U.S. imperialism creates interpretive tension: was his anti-annexationism a tactical necessity or a deep philosophical conviction about Latin America’s distinct civilizational path?
- **Authorship and canon**: Martí published no books in life; his *Obras Completas* were assembled posthumously from scattered periodicals, letters, and diaries. Critical editions (1983 onward) continue to recover unknown texts, raising questions about the stability of the canon and the authenticity of attributed works.
