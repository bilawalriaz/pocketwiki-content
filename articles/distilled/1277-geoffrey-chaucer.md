# Geoffrey Chaucer

## Overview

Geoffrey Chaucer (born in London, most likely in the early 1340s; by some accounts, including his monument, he was born in 1343 – 25 October 1400) was an English poet, philosopher, astronomer, and civil servant whose works, especially *The Canterbury Tales*, established him as a foundational figure in English literature. Writing in Middle English during an era dominated by Latin and Anglo-Norman French, Chaucer legitimized English as a literary language and influenced its standardization. His career spanned diplomacy, bureaucracy, and court life, and his scientific treatise *A Treatise on the Astrolabe* demonstrates his expertise in contemporary learning.

- **1400** — Dies on 25 October; buried in Westminster Abbey

## Body

### Early Life and Family Background

Chaucer was born into a family of vintners and merchants in London. His father, John Chaucer, was a successful wine merchant with royal connections. The family's financial stability is evidenced by a 1324 kidnapping attempt involving his father, resolved with a £250 fine. Chaucer’s surname derives from the French *chaucier*, meaning a maker of shoes or chausses. His upbringing in a merchant family provided him access to literacy and administrative skills crucial for his later career.

### Public Service and Diplomatic Career

Chaucer’s official career began in 1357 as a page to Elizabeth de Burgh, Countess of Ulster, through his father's connections—a common medieval form of apprenticeship for boys into knighthood or prestige appointments. De Burgh was married to Lionel of Antwerp, Duke of Clarence, son of Edward III; this position brought the teenage Chaucer into the close court circle, where he remained for the rest of his life. He traveled to France during the Hundred Years' War with Lionel of Antwerp and was captured at Reims in 1360, with Edward III paying £16 for his ransom. Over the next decades, he served as a diplomat, undertaking missions to France, Spain, and Italy. In 1374, Edward III granted him a daily gallon of wine—a rare honor—likely for poetic service. From 1374 to 1386, he was Comptroller of the Customs for London, overseeing port revenues. He was an associate of eight of the 11 men prosecuted for treason by the Lords Appellant in 1388. In 1379–1380, Thomas Staundon filed legal action against Chaucer for unlawfully employing Cecily Chaumpaigne, though the case was never prosecuted. In 1389, he became Clerk of the King’s Works, managing royal building projects until 1391. He was also appointed keeper of the lodge at the King's Park in Feckenham Forest in Worcestershire, a largely honorary appointment. In September 1390, he was robbed and possibly injured while conducting business, leading him to stop work as Clerk of the King's Works on 17 June 1391.

### Literary Output and Major Works

Chaucer’s major works were composed primarily during his tenure as comptroller. *The Book of the Duchess* (c. 1368–70) is an elegy for Blanche of Lancaster, John of Gaunt’s wife. *Troilus and Criseyde* and *The Legend of Good Women* reflect his engagement with courtly love themes. *The Canterbury Tales*, begun in the 1380s, remains his most celebrated work, showcasing a diverse array of characters and social commentary. He also translated Boethius’ *Consolation of Philosophy* and *The Romance of the Rose*. His scientific writing includes *A Treatise on the Astrolabe*, dedicated to his ten-year-old son Lewis, considered one of the earliest examples of technical writing in English.

### Relationship with John of Gaunt

Chaucer was closely associated with John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster, serving under his patronage. Their families became linked when Gaunt married Katherine Swynford (de Roet) in 1396, making her Chaucer’s sister-in-law. *The Book of the Duchess* honors Blanche of Lancaster, Gaunt’s first wife. Chaucer’s poem *Fortune* also appears to reference Gaunt and the political dynamics of the time, including the 1390 ordinance requiring dual ducal consent for royal gifts.

### Religious Beliefs and the Retraction

Chaucer’s works engage deeply with Christian themes. The Parson in *The Canterbury Tales* embodies idealized Christianity, while figures like the Friar, Summoner, and Pardoner are satirized. The "Retraction," appended to *The Canterbury Tales*, includes a plea for forgiveness for worldly writings, listing works like *Troilus*, *The Book of the Duchess*, and *Parlement of Foules*. Its sincerity is debated, with some scholars viewing it as a final literary device rather than genuine repentance.

### Influence on the English Language

Chaucer played a pivotal role in legitimizing Middle English for literary expression. His use of the London dialect helped standardize English, though the court and bureaucracy were equally influential. He introduced nearly 2,000 words to English, including "acceptable," "arsenic," and "aspect." His metrical innovations, such as the rhyme royal and five-stress line, shaped English poetry. However, the Great Vowel Shift altered pronunciation significantly after his death, complicating modern readings.

### Reception and Editorial History

Chaucer’s contemporaries, including Thomas Hoccleve and John Gower, praised his work. His influence grew through the medieval and early modern periods, with 16th-century editors like William Thynne compiling his works and expanding the canon with apocryphal texts. These additions often reflected Protestant reinterpretations, casting Chaucer as a proto-Reformer. The 19th century saw scholarly rigor emerge, with Walter William Skeat establishing authoritative texts. Modern scholarship continues to explore his themes, structure, and audience.

## Terms

- **Middle English**: The variety of English spoken between c. 1100 and c. 1500, used by Chaucer and other late medieval writers.
- **Astrolabe**: A medieval instrument for solving problems related to time, latitude, and astronomy; Chaucer wrote a treatise explaining its use.
- **Rhyme Royal**: A stanzaic form consisting of seven lines in iambic pentameter with a specific rhyme scheme, invented by Chaucer.
- **Five-stress line**: A metrical line with five stressed syllables, a precursor to modern iambic pentameter.
- **Appellant**: A party appealing a legal decision; the Lords Appellant opposed Richard II in 1388.
- **Lollard**: Followers of John Wycliffe advocating religious reform; attracted to Chaucer’s critiques of clergy.
- **Apocryphal**: Works falsely attributed to a known author; many texts were added to Chaucer’s canon in early print editions.
- **Great Vowel Shift**: A phonetic change in English pronunciation occurring between the 14th and 18th centuries, affecting how Chaucer’s works sound today.
- **Diplomatic edition**: A scholarly edition reproducing a manuscript exactly, including spelling and layout; pioneered by the Chaucer Society.
- **Retraction**: A passage appended to *The Canterbury Tales* in which Chaucer asks for forgiveness for his secular writings.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several aspects of Chaucer’s life and work remain debated. The exact date and location of his birth are uncertain, though tradition places it in 1343 in London. The identity of the narrator in *The Canterbury Tales* and the extent of Chaucer’s personal views versus his characters’ perspectives are contested. The authenticity of the "Retraction" is questioned, with some scholars seeing it as a literary device. The attribution of works like *The Equatorie of the Planetis* to Chaucer is disputed. Additionally, the political motivations behind early editorial expansions of his canon, particularly the inclusion of Lollard texts, reflect broader tensions in interpreting his legacy. The influence of Italian poets like Petrarch and Boccaccio on his work is acknowledged, but the specifics of these encounters remain speculative.
