# Infinity

## Overview

Infinity, denoted by ∞ (the infinity symbol), is the concept of something boundless, endless, or larger than any assignable quantity. It has been a central topic of philosophical debate since antiquity and was rigorously incorporated into mathematics from the 17th century onward, culminating in Georg Cantor's theory of infinite sets in the late 19th century, which showed that infinities come in distinct sizes.

## Timeline

- **c. 610–546 BC** — Anaximander uses "apeiron" (unbounded) as an early concept of infinity.
- **c. 495–430 BC** — Zeno of Elea presents paradoxes involving infinite divisibility.
- **c. 300 BC** — Euclid proves the infinitude of primes, avoiding the term "infinity."
- **1655** — John Wallis introduces the symbol ∞ in *De sectionibus conicis*.
- **1656** — Wallis uses infinite series and products in *Arithmetica infinitorum*.
- **1699** — Newton writes on equations with infinite terms in *De analysi*.
- **1821** — Cauchy defines limits, resolving Zeno-type paradoxes via infinite series.
- **Late 19th century** — Cantor develops set theory and the theory of transfinite numbers.
- **1878** — Cantor proves that the number of points on a line equals the number of points in any finite-dimensional space.

## Body

### Ancient and Early Concepts

Ancient cultures, including the Greeks and Indians, approached infinity philosophically rather than formally. Anaximander introduced "apeiron" as an indefinite, unbounded principle. Aristotle distinguished *potential infinity* (a process that can continue indefinitely) from *actual infinity* (a completed infinite totality), rejecting the latter due to paradoxes. This "horror of the infinite" influenced Euclid, who phrased the infinitude of primes as "more than any assigned multitude." Zeno's paradoxes, like "Achilles and the Tortoise," highlighted contradictions in infinite divisibility. These were resolved in 1821 by Cauchy's limit definition, showing that infinite series like $10 + 0.1 + 0.001 + \cdots$ converge to finite values.

### Indian Mathematics

The Jain text *Surya Prajnapti* (c. 4th–3rd century BCE) classified numbers into enumerable, innumerable, and infinite, each subdivided into three orders, reflecting early systematic treatment of infinity.

### 17th Century and Calculus

European mathematicians began using infinite quantities systematically. Wallis introduced ∞ in 1655 and used it in area calculations. Newton (1699) worked with infinite series. Leibniz viewed infinitesimals and infinities as ideal entities governed by the Law of Continuity. In real analysis, ∞ denotes unbounded limits, not real numbers. The extended real number system adds +∞ and −∞ to the reals, enabling two-point or one-point compactifications. In complex analysis, ∞ represents an unsigned limit; adding it to the complex plane yields the Riemann sphere, allowing division by zero ($z/0 = ∞$ for nonzero $z$).

### Set Theory and Transfinite Numbers

Cantor revolutionized infinity by treating it mathematically. He defined ordinal and cardinal numbers, introducing ℵ₀ (aleph-null), the cardinality of natural numbers. Infinite sets are *Dedekind infinite* if they can be put into one-to-one correspondence with a proper subset. Cantor proved no largest cardinal exists and that the continuum (real numbers) has greater cardinality than naturals: $\mathbf{c} = 2^{\aleph_0} > \aleph_0$. Among the axioms of Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory, on which most of modern mathematics can be developed, is the axiom of infinity, which guarantees the existence of infinite sets. The Continuum Hypothesis (CH), stating no cardinal lies between ℵ₀ and **c**, is independent of ZFC set theory.

### Nonstandard Analysis

The original formulation of infinitesimal calculus by Newton and Leibniz used infinitesimal quantities. In the second half of the 20th century, this treatment was put on a rigorous footing through various logical systems, including smooth infinitesimal analysis and nonstandard analysis. In the latter, infinitesimals are invertible, and their inverses are infinite numbers. The infinities in this sense are part of a hyperreal field; there is no equivalence between them as with the Cantorian transfinites. For example, if H is an infinite number in this sense, then H + H = 2H and H + 1 are distinct infinite numbers.

### Geometry and Infinite Dimensions

Until the 19th century, geometry avoided actual infinity. Projective geometry introduced points at infinity to unify parallel and intersecting lines. With set theory, lines became infinite sets of points. Infinite-dimensional vector spaces arise in functional analysis, and topological spaces of infinite dimension exist (e.g., iterated loop spaces). Fractals exhibit self-similar infinite structure, such as the Koch snowflake with infinite perimeter but finite area.

### Finitism and Logic

Finitism, championed by Kronecker, rejects actual infinity. In logic, infinite regress arguments challenge justifications. First-order logic uses compactness and Löwenheim–Skolem theorems to construct non-standard models with infinite properties.

### Applications

In physics, infinite constructs like plane waves are idealized. Cosmology debates whether the universe is spatially infinite; the first published proposal that the universe is infinite came from Thomas Digges in 1576. Eight years later, in 1584, Giordano Bruno proposed an unbounded universe in *On the Infinite Universe and Worlds*. WMAP data suggest flat topology, possibly implying infinitude, though finite flat topologies are also possible. The multiverse hypothesis posits infinite universes. In computing, IEEE 754 defines ±∞ for overflow and division by zero. Programming languages support infinity constants for sorting and sentinel use. Infinite loops execute indefinitely. In art, vanishing points model infinity; Escher explored it visually. Infinite chess uses unbounded boards. Lakoff frames infinity as a metaphor based on the sequence <1, 2, 3, …>.

## Terms

- **Apeiron**: Ancient Greek term meaning "unbounded" or "indefinite," used by Anaximander.
- **Potential vs. Actual Infinity**: Potential = process continuing indefinitely; actual = completed infinite totality.
- **Dedekind Infinite**: A set equinumerous with a proper subset of itself.
- **Cardinal Number**: Measures the size ("how many") of a set.
- **Ordinal Number**: Represents order type of well-ordered sets.
- **Aleph-null (ℵ₀)**: Smallest transfinite cardinal; cardinality of natural numbers.
- **Cardinality of the Continuum (c)**: Cardinality of real numbers; equals $2^{\aleph_0}$.
- **Continuum Hypothesis (CH)**: Conjecture that no cardinal exists strictly between ℵ₀ and **c**.
- **Riemann Sphere**: One-point compactification of the complex plane, adding a point at infinity.
- **Extended Real Number System**: Reals augmented with +∞ and −∞, supporting limit operations.

## Debates and Open Questions

Whether the physical universe is spatially infinite remains unresolved. The Continuum Hypothesis cannot be proved or disproved within ZFC. Whether actual infinity should be accepted in mathematics divides finitists from classical mathematicians. The nature of infinity in cognitive science is debated, with Lakoff proposing metaphorical foundations.
