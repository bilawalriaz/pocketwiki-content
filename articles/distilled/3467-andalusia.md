# Andalusia

## Overview
Andalusia is the southernmost and most populous autonomous community of Spain, located on the Iberian Peninsula. It is a historical nationality with a rich cultural identity shaped by successive civilizations, including Romans, Visigoths, and, most significantly, the Moors during the Islamic Golden Age. Its geography, from fertile river valleys to high mountains, has made it a major agricultural region and a crossroads of European and African influences.

## Timeline
- **c. 716** — The name "Al-Andalus" first appears on coins minted by the new Muslim government of Iberia.
- **10th century** — Córdoba, capital of Al-Andalus, surpasses Constantinople as Europe's largest city and a center of learning.
- **13th century** — The Crown of Castile conquers the Guadalquivir Valley.
- **1492** — The Nasrid Kingdom of Granada, the last Muslim territory, is conquered, completing the Reconquista.
- **1833** — The eight modern provinces of Andalusia are established.
- **1918** — The Assembly of Ronda, a gathering of Andalusian nationalists, approves the region's flag.
- **1978** — The Spanish Constitution recognizes Andalusia as a nationality.
- **1980** — A referendum approves the Statute of Autonomy.
- **1981** — Andalusia becomes an autonomous community under the Statute of Autonomy.
- **2007** — A reformed Statute of Autonomy is approved by popular referendum, reaffirming Andalusia as a "historic nationality."

## Body

### Geography and Climate
Andalusia is divided into three main geographic zones: the Sierra Morena mountains in the north, the Baetic System in the south and east, and the fertile Guadalquivir valley between them. The region has a hot-summer Mediterranean climate, with the Guadalquivir valley being the hottest area of Europe. There is significant climatic variation, from the driest place in Europe (Cabo de Gata) to areas with very high rainfall (Sierra de Grazalema). The terrain includes the Iberian Peninsula's highest peak, Mulhacén, and the Tabernas Desert.

### History and Etymology
The name "Andalusia" derives from the Arabic "Al-Andalus," the term for Muslim-controlled Iberia. Its exact etymology is debated, with theories linking it to the Vandals, Goths, or pre-Roman tribes. The region's history is a succession of influences: Phoenicians, Romans (as the province of Baetica), Visigoths, and then a long period of Moorish rule that left a profound cultural legacy. After the Christian Reconquista, completed in 1492, Andalusia's ports prospered from trade with the New World. Chronic social inequality led to recurring unrest in the 19th and 20th centuries.

### Government and Politics
Andalusia is one of Spain's 17 autonomous communities, with its own parliament and government (the Junta de Andalucía). Its 1981 Statute of Autonomy, based on Article 151 of the Spanish Constitution, was the first to follow that specific path to autonomy. The 2007 Statute reaffirms Andalusia as a "historic nationality." The capital is Seville, while the High Court of Justice is seated in Granada. Blas Infante is recognized as the "Father of the Andalusian Nation."

### Economy
The economy has transitioned from being traditionally agricultural to one dominated by the service sector, especially tourism. While agriculture remains culturally important and employs about 8% of the workforce, its contribution to GDP is modest. The industrial sector is less developed than the Spanish average. Tourism is a major driver, with the Costa del Sol and cultural sites attracting millions of visitors annually. Andalusia's GDP per capita remains one of the lowest in Spain.

### Culture and Society
Andalusian culture is a fusion of its diverse history. Flamenco music and dance, originating in the 18th century with strong Romani influences, is a defining cultural expression. The region is also known for bullfighting, Hispano-Moorish architecture (e.g., the Alhambra), and vibrant festivals like Seville's Feria de Abril and Holy Week processions. Andalusian Spanish is widely spoken and has influenced American Spanish. Roman Catholicism is the predominant religion, with strong traditions of Marian devotion.

## Terms
- **Al-Andalus**: The Arabic name for the Iberian Peninsula under Muslim rule (711–1492).
- **Reconquista**: The centuries-long period of Christian reconquest of the Iberian Peninsula from Muslim rule.
- **Taifa**: An independent Muslim-ruled principality in the Iberian Peninsula, especially after the fragmentation of the Caliphate of Córdoba.
- **Flamenco**: A traditional Andalusian art form combining song (cante), dance (baile), and guitar (toque).
- **Junta de Andalucía**: The regional government of the autonomous community of Andalusia.
- **Statute of Autonomy**: The organic law that defines the powers and institutions of an autonomous community in Spain.
- **Costumbrismo andaluz**: A 19th-century literary and artistic genre depicting traditional Andalusian customs and folklore.
- **Majismo**: A cultural trend of the 18th century involving the adoption of traditional, lower-class Spanish dress and manners.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The etymology of "Al-Andalus" is disputed, with theories involving the Vandals, Goths, or pre-Roman substrate.
- The precise historical meaning and territorial extent of the terms "Andalusia" and "Al-Andalus" have varied over centuries.
- The 2007 Statute of Autonomy cites the 1919 Andalusianist Manifesto describing Andalusia as a "national reality" but does not formally endorse that specific formulation.
