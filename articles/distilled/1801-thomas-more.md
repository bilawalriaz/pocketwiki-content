# Thomas More

## Overview
Sir Thomas More (1478–1535) was an English statesman, humanist scholar, and Catholic martyr who served as Lord Chancellor under King Henry VIII. He is best known for his 1516 work *Utopia*, which describes an ideal society, and for his refusal to accept the king's supremacy over the English Church, which led to his execution. His life and death have made him a enduring symbol of conscience versus state power.

## Timeline
- **7 February 1478** — Born in London to Sir John More and Agnes Graunger.
- **1492–1494** — Studies at the University of Oxford, introduced to humanism.
- **1504** — Elected to Parliament; first opposes King Henry VII's tax demands.
- **1505** — Marries Jane Colt; begins legal and political career.
- **1516** — Publishes *Utopia* in Latin.
- **1521** — Knighted; becomes Under-Treasurer of the Exchequer.
- **1523** — Elected Speaker of the House of Commons.
- **1529** — Appointed Lord Chancellor after Cardinal Wolsey's fall.
- **1532** — Resigns as Lord Chancellor in protest over the Submission of the Clergy.
- **1534** — Refuses the Oath of Supremacy; imprisoned in the Tower of London.
- **1 July 1535** — Tried for treason and found guilty.
- **6 July 1535** — Executed at Tower Hill.

## Body

### Early Life and Humanism
Born into a successful London legal family, More served as a page to Archbishop John Morton, who promoted the "New Learning" (humanism). He studied at Oxford, mastering Latin and Greek, before pursuing law at Lincoln's Inn. A close friend of Desiderius Erasmus, More was a central figure in English humanism, blending classical learning with Christian piety. He considered becoming a monk but chose a secular life, marrying twice and raising a large, intellectually vibrant family.

### Political Career and Lord Chancellorship
More entered Parliament in 1504 and rose through royal service under Henry VIII, becoming a trusted advisor. He was knighted in 1521 and made Lord Chancellor in 1529. As Chancellor, he was known for his rapid judicial work and implemented public health measures like the Statute of Sewers (1532). His tenure was dominated by the king's break with Rome. More supported papal supremacy and refused to acknowledge Henry as head of the Church of England or the annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon. He resigned in 1532 to avoid endorsing these actions.

### Campaign Against the Reformation
More was a staunch defender of Catholic orthodoxy and viewed the Protestant Reformation as a dangerous heresy. He wrote extensive polemics against reformers like Martin Luther and William Tyndale, notably debating Tyndale's English translation of the New Testament. As Lord Chancellor, he actively prosecuted suspected heretics, though the extent of his personal involvement in torture is historically debated. He approved of the burning of obstinate heretics, seeing it as a defense of both faith and social order.

### Trial and Execution
After refusing the 1534 Oath of Supremacy, More was imprisoned. At his trial, he defended his silence as consent, arguing that "qui tacet consentire videtur" (one who keeps silent seems to consent). He was convicted on the perjured testimony of Richard Rich, who claimed More verbally denied the king's supremacy. More was executed at Tower Hill, declaring he died "the king's good servant, and God's first."

## Terms
*   **Humanism:** A Renaissance intellectual movement emphasizing classical learning, critical thinking, and human potential.
*   **Lord Chancellor:** The highest judicial officer in England, presiding over the Court of Chancery and acting as a chief legal advisor to the monarch.
*   **Oath of Supremacy:** A 1534 oath requiring subjects to swear that the monarch, not the Pope, was the Supreme Head of the Church of England.
*   **Praemunire:** A medieval English law making it a crime to appeal to a foreign authority (like the Pope) in matters that could be handled by the king's courts.
*   **Star Chamber:** A powerful English court of the Tudor and Stuart periods, known for its secretive proceedings and use in enforcing royal policy.
*   **Utopia:** More's 1516 book describing an imaginary island society with communal property, religious tolerance, and rational laws, serving as a critique of contemporary Europe.
*   **Polemics:** Aggressive, controversial arguments against a particular opinion or doctrine, especially in theology.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **The Extent of Persecution:** Historians debate the severity of More's actions against heretics. Some, like Richard Marius, portray him as a zealous persecutor, while others, like Eamon Duffy, note he never presided at a heresy trial or condemned anyone to death himself.
*   **Torture Allegations:** Stories of More personally torturing heretics, popularized by John Foxe's *Book of Martyrs*, are largely doubted by modern historians like Diarmaid MacCulloch, who find no independent proof.
*   **The Nature of *Utopia*:** Scholars question whether More's description of an ideal society was a sincere proposal, a satirical critique, or a complex blend of both. The name itself (Greek for "no place") suggests irony.
*   **Conscience vs. Loyalty:** More's life is often framed as a conflict between his duty to the state and his duty to God. His resignation and silent refusal to swear the oath highlight this tension, which remains a central theme in his legacy.
