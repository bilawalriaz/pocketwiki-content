# Arctic Ocean

## Overview

The Arctic Ocean is the smallest and shallowest of the world's five oceanic divisions, spanning approximately 14,060,000 km² and surrounding the North Pole. It is the coldest ocean, mostly covered by sea ice year-round, and its salinity is the lowest of the five major oceans due to low evaporation, heavy freshwater inflow from rivers, and limited connection to higher-salinity oceans. The ocean matters globally because its sea ice reflects sunlight, its water masses drive global ocean circulation, and it holds significant undiscovered oil and gas resources, while warming twice as fast as the rest of the planet.

## Timeline

- **c. 17,000–50,000 years ago** — Humans cross the Bering land bridge into North America during the Wisconsin glaciation
- **325 BC** — Pytheas of Massilia records a journey north to "Thule," likely describing loose sea ice
- **11th century** — Novaya Zemlya discovered; 1596: Spitzbergen added to maps
- **1818–1845** — John Barrow promotes British exploration seeking the mythical "Open Polar Sea"
- **1893–1896** — Fridtjof Nansen makes the first nautical crossing of the Arctic Ocean (Fram Expedition)
- **1937** — Soviet manned drifting ice stations begin monitoring the Arctic
- **1958** — USS Nautilus makes the first nautical transit of the North Pole (submarine)
- **1969** — Wally Herbert leads the first surface crossing (dog sled, Alaska to Svalbard)
- **1977** — NS Arktika makes the first surface nautical transit of the North Pole
- **September 2012** — Arctic sea ice reaches a record minimum, 49% below the 1979–2000 average
- **16 July 2015** — Five nations sign a declaration to keep fishing vessels out of the central Arctic Ocean

## Body

### Human History

Human habitation in the North American polar region goes back at least 17,000–50,000 years. Falling sea levels during the Wisconsin glaciation allowed people to cross the Bering land bridge from Siberia to Alaska. Early Paleo-Eskimo groups included the Pre-Dorset (c. 3200–850 BC), Saqqaq culture of Greenland (2500–800 BC), Independence I and II cultures (c. 2400–1800 BC and c. 800–1 BC), and Groswater. The Dorset culture (500 BC–AD 1500) was the last major Paleo-Eskimo culture before the Thule people migrated east from Alaska; the Thule Tradition (c. 200 BC–AD 1600) produced the ancestors of the modern Inuit.

European knowledge of the Arctic was largely conjectural until recent centuries. Pytheas of Massilia (325 BC) described a land where the sun set only three hours daily and water was replaced by a congealed substance—probably loose sea ice ("growlers" or "bergy bits"). Early cartographers disagreed on whether the pole was land or water. The myth of an "Open Polar Sea" persisted into the late 19th century, promoted by John Barrow and claimed sighted by Elisha Kane and Isaac Israel Hayes, but was widely considered disproven by the early 1900s as explorers found thick, year-round ice. In the 20th century, the first surface crossing came in 1969 (Wally Herbert), the first submarine transit in 1958 (USS Nautilus), and the first surface nautical transit in 1977 (NS Arktika). During World War II, the European Arctic was heavily contested as Allies resupplied the Soviet Union. In the 21st century, US President Donald Trump has repeatedly proposed purchasing or annexing Greenland and Canada, citing national security, economic interests, and control of the Northwest Passage.

### Geography and Geology

The Arctic Ocean occupies a roughly circular basin of about 14,056,000 km², with a coastline of 45,390 km. It is surrounded by Eurasia (Russia, Norway), North America (Canada, Alaska), Greenland, and Iceland. It connects to the Pacific via the Bering Strait and to the Atlantic via the Greenland and Labrador Seas. Major islands include Greenland, the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, Svalbard, Novaya Zemlya, and Franz Josef Land. The Russian continental shelf consists of three smaller shelves—the Barents, Chukchi Sea, and Siberian Shelves—of which the Siberian Shelf is the largest such shelf in the world and holds large oil and gas reserves. The Chukchi shelf edge forms the US–Russia border per the 1990 USSR–USA Maritime Boundary Agreement.

The Lomonosov Ridge divides the deep basin into the Eurasian Basin (4,000–4,500 m deep) and Amerasian Basin (about 4,000 m). Average depth is 1,038 m; the deepest point is Molloy Hole in the Fram Strait at about 5,550 m. Geologically, the region was shaped by the Ellesmerian orogeny (Paleozoic), Jurassic–Triassic subsidence that created oil and gas reservoirs, Cretaceous opening of the Canadian Basin, and rifting of Pangea that opened the Arctic Ocean. The Gakkel Ridge spreading in the Paleocene–Eocene moved the Lomonosov Ridge away from land. The geology remains poorly explored due to ice and remote conditions.

### Oceanography

The Arctic Ocean has a unique water structure. The top layer (about 50 m) has lower salinity and temperature, fed by Siberian and Canadian rivers (Ob, Yenisei, Lena, Mackenzie), which float on denser water. Below lies a halocline (where salinity and temperature rise with depth). Three water masses exist: Arctic Bottom Water (below 900 m, the densest in the World Ocean, formed from shelf water and Greenland Sea Deep Water), Atlantic Water (150–900 m, the largest inflow, about 10 times the Pacific inflow, entering through the Fram Strait), and Arctic Surface Water (150–200 m). Pacific inflow passes through the Bering Strait at 0.8 Sverdrups. Outflow occurs mainly through the Fram Strait (about 9 Sv), influenced by the Coriolis force. Recent estimates (2016–2018) show oceanic heat flux has overtaken atmospheric flux in the eastern Eurasian Basin, with weakening halocline stratification and increasing mixing.

### Sea Ice and Climate

Sea ice extent has declined continuously, at 12.85% per decade since 1980 from an average winter value of 15,600,000 km². Seasonal variation is about 7,000,000 km², maximum in April, minimum in September. The ocean is virtually ice-locked from October to June. The climate is polar: winters have polar night and stable conditions; summers have midnight sun and temperatures slightly above 0 °C. Surface water temperature is fairly constant at about −1.8 °C. The Arctic is warming twice as fast as the rest of the planet. During the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (55 million years ago), the region reached average annual temperatures of 10–20 °C, supporting tropical lifeforms requiring surface temperatures over 22 °C.

### Biology and Resources

Primary production by ice algae and phytoplankton is limited to spring and summer (March/April to September) due to 2–6 months of midnight sun and polar night. Zooplankton (copepods, euphausiids) and ice-associated fauna (amphipods) are key primary consumers. Higher trophic levels vary by region: the Barents Sea has sub-Arctic species (herring, young cod, capelin), while ice-covered central regions have polar cod as a central predator. Apex predators include seals, whales, and polar bears. Endangered species include walruses and whales. The region holds petroleum, natural gas, placer deposits, polymetallic nodules, sand and gravel, fish, seals, and whales. The central political dead zone may hold 25% or more of the world's undiscovered oil and gas resources.

### Environmental Concerns

Arctic ice melting reduces the planet's average albedo (reflectivity), potentially causing global warming in a positive feedback mechanism. Research suggests the Arctic may become ice-free in summer by 2040. Warming may release fresh melt-water into the north Atlantic, disrupting global ocean current patterns. Reduced ice has increased polar bear–human encounters; a mass invasion of Novaya Zemlya in December 2018–February 2019 caused a state of emergency. Melting ice may also destabilize methane clathrates (frozen methane deposits), releasing a powerful greenhouse gas. Other concerns include radioactive contamination from Russian waste dumps in the Kara Sea, Cold War nuclear test sites, Camp Century contaminants, and the Fukushima disaster. In 2015, five nations agreed to keep fishing vessels out of a 1.1 million square mile central zone until better scientific knowledge exists.

## Terms

- **Halocline**: A layer where salinity and temperature both rise with increasing depth, separating fresher surface water from denser deep water.
- **Sverdrup (Sv)**: A unit of ocean flow equal to one million cubic meters per second.
- **Thermohaline forcing**: Circulation driven by differences in temperature and salinity (density) rather than wind.
- **Albedo**: The reflectivity of a surface; sea ice reflects sunlight, so its loss warms the planet.
- **Clathrate**: A frozen methane deposit stabilized by cold conditions; melting releases methane, a potent greenhouse gas.
- **Paleo-Eskimo**: Early indigenous Arctic cultures preceding the Thule people, ancestors of modern Inuit.
- **Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ)**: A sea zone over which a state has special rights to resources.
- **Pack ice**: Sea ice piled up by compression zones, moved by wind and ocean currents.
- **Polar night / midnight sun**: Periods of continuous darkness (winter) or daylight (summer) at high latitudes.

## Debates and open questions

- Whether the Arctic Ocean is an ocean or better described as the Arctic Mediterranean Sea, an estuary of the Atlantic, or the northernmost part of the world ocean—some oceanographers dispute the IHO classification.
- When the Arctic was last ice-free: estimates range from 65 million years ago (fossil evidence of plants) to 5,500 years ago, 8,000 years ago (ice and ocean cores), or 125,000 years ago (last interglacial).
- The geology of the Arctic Ocean remains poorly explored; the Gakkel Ridge rift is poorly understood and may extend into the Laptev Sea.
- The Lomonosov Ridge may contain up to 10 billion barrels of oil, but its extent and accessibility are uncertain.
- The political dead zone near the center of the sea is subject to a mounting dispute between the US, Russia, Canada, Norway, and Denmark over territorial claims and resource rights.
