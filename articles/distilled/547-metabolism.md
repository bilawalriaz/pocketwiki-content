# Metabolism

## Overview
Metabolism is the set of life-sustaining, enzyme-catalyzed chemical reactions within living organisms. It serves three main functions: converting food energy into usable cellular energy (ATP), synthesizing macromolecular building blocks (proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, carbohydrates), and excreting waste. Reactions are organized into pathways—catabolic pathways break down molecules to release energy, while anabolic pathways consume energy to build complex structures. Remarkably, core pathways like glycolysis and the citric acid cycle are conserved across all domains of life, likely originating in the last universal common ancestor. Metabolism is regulated to maintain homeostasis and responds to signals such as hormones; its dysregulation underlies diseases including diabetes and cancer.

## Timeline
- **4th century BC** — Aristotle proposes an open-flow model of metabolism involving transformation of food, heat release, and excretion.
- **1260 AD** — Ibn al-Nafis describes the body in a continuous state of dissolution and nourishment.
- **1614** — Santorio Santorio publishes first controlled human metabolism experiments, quantifying "insensible perspiration."
- **1828** — Friedrich Wöhler synthesizes urea from inorganic precursors, challenging vitalism.
- **19th century** — Louis Pasteur identifies "ferments" (enzymes) in yeast as catalysts of fermentation.
- **Early 20th century** — Eduard Buchner discovers cell-free enzymes, founding biochemistry.
- **1930s–1950s** — Hans Krebs elucidates the urea cycle, citric acid cycle, and glyoxylate cycle.
- **Late 20th century–present** — Genomics, metabolomics, and computational modeling enable reconstruction of genome-scale metabolic networks and metabolic engineering.

## Body

### Core Concepts and Thermodynamics
Metabolism comprises catabolism (breaking down compounds, releasing energy) and anabolism (building up compounds, consuming energy). Enzymes catalyze reactions, lowering activation energy and enabling regulation. They couple non-spontaneous anabolic reactions to spontaneous catabolic ones, allowing organisms to maintain low-entropy order by increasing environmental entropy, consistent with the second law of thermodynamics. The basal metabolic rate measures total energy consumed by these reactions. Organisms are classified by nutritional groups: energy source (light/phototrophs vs. chemical/chemotrophs), electron donor (organic/organotrophs vs. inorganic/lithotrophs), and carbon source (organic/heterotrophs vs. CO₂/autotrophs).

### Key Biochemicals and Coenzymes
Four molecular classes dominate: amino acids (proteins/enzymes), carbohydrates (energy storage/structure), lipids (membranes/energy), and nucleotides (genetic information/coenzymes). Polymers form via condensation reactions. Coenzymes act as group-transfer intermediates. **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate) is the universal energy currency, coupling catabolism to anabolism via phosphorylation. **NAD⁺/NADH** and **NADP⁺/NADPH** shuttle electrons; NAD⁺ functions mainly in catabolism, NADPH in anabolism. Vitamins often serve as coenzyme precursors (e.g., B3 → NAD⁺). Minerals act as electrolytes (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺) for osmotic balance and nerve function, or as enzyme cofactors (Fe, Zn).

### Catabolism: Energy Extraction
**Digestion** breaks macromolecules extracellularly: proteases yield amino acids; glycoside hydrolases yield monosaccharides. Microbes secrete enzymes; animals use specialized gut cells.  
**Carbohydrate catabolism**: Glycolysis converts glucose to pyruvate, generating ATP and NADH. Pyruvate enters mitochondria, becomes acetyl-CoA, and fuels the **citric acid cycle** (Krebs cycle), producing NADH/FADH₂ and CO₂. **Oxidative phosphorylation** uses the electron transport chain (mitochondrial inner membrane in eukaryotes) to pump protons, creating a gradient that drives ATP synthase. Without oxygen, pyruvate converts to lactate (animals) or ethanol (yeast), regenerating NAD⁺ for glycolysis. The pentose phosphate pathway generates NADPH and ribose-5-phosphate for biosynthesis.  
**Fat catabolism**: Hydrolysis yields glycerol (enters glycolysis) and fatty acids. **Beta-oxidation** cleaves fatty acids to acetyl-CoA, yielding more ATP per carbon than glucose.  
**Amino acid catabolism**: Transamination removes amino groups (urea cycle excretes nitrogen); carbon skeletons enter the citric acid cycle or gluconeogenesis.  
**Chemolithotrophy** (prokaryotes only): Oxidation of inorganic substrates (H₂, H₂S, Fe²⁺, NH₃) drives ATP synthesis. **Photosynthesis** captures light via reaction centers, creating proton gradients for ATP synthesis; in oxygenic photosynthesis (plants/cyanobacteria), water is the electron donor, releasing O₂.

### Anabolism: Biosynthesis
Anabolism proceeds in three stages: precursor synthesis, ATP-dependent activation, and polymerization.  
**Carbon fixation**: The Calvin–Benson cycle (RuBisCO enzyme) fixes CO₂ into glycerate-3-phosphate using photosynthetic ATP/NADPH. C₃, C₄, and CAM plants differ in CO₂ delivery adaptations. Prokaryotes use additional pathways (reverse citric acid cycle, acetyl-CoA carboxylation).  
**Carbohydrates**: **Gluconeogenesis** synthesizes glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors (pyruvate, lactate, amino acids) using non-glycolytic enzymes to avoid futile cycles. Animals cannot convert acetyl-CoA to glucose (lack glyoxylate cycle); during starvation, they produce ketone bodies for the brain. Plants/bacteria use the glyoxylate cycle to convert acetyl-CoA to oxaloacetate for gluconeogenesis. Polysaccharides assemble via glycosyltransferases using activated sugar-phosphates (e.g., UDP-glucose).  
**Lipids**: Fatty acid synthases polymerize acetyl-CoA. Animals/fungi use a single multifunctional Type I enzyme; plants/bacteria use separate Type II enzymes. **Isoprenoids/sterols** derive from isopentenyl pyrophosphate (mevalonate pathway in animals/archaea; non-mevalonate pathway in plants/bacteria).  
**Proteins**: Amino acids are activated as aminoacyl-tRNA (ATP-dependent) and polymerized by ribosomes reading mRNA. Mammals synthesize 11 nonessential amino acids; 9 essential ones require dietary intake.  
**Nucleotides**: *De novo* synthesis is energy-intensive (purines from glycine/glutamine/aspartate/formate; pyrimidines from glutamine/aspartate). Salvage pathways recycle preformed bases.

### Xenobiotics and Redox Balance
**Xenobiotics** (foreign compounds: drugs, toxins) are detoxified in three phases: (I) oxidation (cytochrome P450s), (II) conjugation to water-soluble groups (glucuronide, glutathione), (III) export. Microbes degrade a wider range, enabling bioremediation. **Oxidative stress** from reactive oxygen species (byproducts of oxidative phosphorylation/disulfide bond formation) is countered by antioxidants (glutathione) and enzymes (catalase, peroxidases).

### Regulation and Control
**Homeostasis** maintains constant internal conditions. **Intrinsic regulation**: pathways self-adjust via allosteric feedback (product inhibition, substrate activation). **Extrinsic regulation**: hormones (e.g., insulin) bind receptors, triggering second-messenger cascades (often phosphorylation). Insulin promotes glucose uptake, glycogen synthesis (activates glycogen synthase via dephosphorylation), and lipogenesis; glucagon/epinephrine oppose these. Control analysis distinguishes enzyme *regulation* (activity change) from *control* (flux impact).

### Evolution
Core pathways (glycolysis, citric acid cycle) exist in all three domains (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya), inherited from the **last universal common ancestor (LUCA)**—likely a prokaryotic methanogen with extensive metabolism. Retention reflects optimal efficiency (minimal steps, high yield). Pathway evolution mechanisms include: sequential enzyme addition, pathway duplication/divergence, and enzyme recruitment (creating evolutionary mosaics). Modular "bow-tie" architecture in bacteria uses few central intermediates to process diverse inputs/outputs. Parasites/endosymbionts often lose pathways, scavenging host metabolites.

### Investigation and Manipulation
Classical reductionism uses radioactive tracers to map pathways and purifies enzymes for kinetics. **Metabolomics** profiles the complete small-molecule set (metabolome). **Genome-scale metabolic models** integrate genomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic data to predict flux (e.g., human metabolic model for drug discovery). **Metabolic engineering** genetically modifies organisms (yeast, bacteria, plants) to optimize production of drugs (antibiotics), chemicals (1,3-propanediol, shikimic acid), or biofuels—reducing energy cost, increasing yield, minimizing waste.

## Terms
- ****Catabolism**** — Metabolic pathways that break down molecules, releasing energy (e.g., glycolysis, beta-oxidation).
- ****Anabolism**** — Metabolic pathways that synthesize complex molecules from simpler precursors, consuming energy (ATP).
- ****ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate)**** — Universal energy currency; couples exergonic catabolic reactions to endergonic anabolic ones via phosphate transfer.
- ****NAD⁺/NADH**** — Redox coenzyme pair; NAD⁺ accepts electrons (oxidized) in catabolism, NADH donates them (reduced) to the electron transport chain.
- ****Acetyl-CoA**** — Central metabolic intermediate; product of pyruvate oxidation, fatty acid beta-oxidation, and amino acid catabolism; enters citric acid cycle.
- ****Oxidative Phosphorylation**** — ATP synthesis driven by proton gradient across mitochondrial inner membrane (eukaryotes) or cell membrane (prokaryotes), generated by electron transport chain.
- ****Gluconeogenesis**** — Synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors (lactate, amino acids, glycerol); uses distinct enzymes to avoid futile cycling with glycolysis.
- ****Xenobiotics**** — Foreign compounds (drugs, toxins) lacking metabolic function; detoxified via oxidation, conjugation, and excretion.
- ****Bow-tie Architecture**** — Metabolic network structure where diverse inputs converge on few central intermediates (the "knot") enabling diverse outputs.
- ****LUCA (Last Universal Common Ancestor)**** — Hypothetical single-celled organism from which all extant life descends; possessed core metabolic pathways conserved today.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Pathway evolution mechanisms**: Relative contributions of sequential enzyme addition, pathway duplication/divergence, and enzyme recruitment remain unclear; genomic data suggest step-by-step evolution, but structural studies indicate pervasive enzyme recruitment across pathways.
- **LUCA's metabolism**: Whether LUCA was a methanogen, its exact metabolic repertoire, and whether metabolism originated in an "RNA world" or "iron-sulfur world" are unresolved.
- **Metabolic control distribution**: Identifying which enzymes exert flux control (vs. mere regulation) in complex networks is context-dependent and technically challenging.
- **Cancer metabolism**: The Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis) and oncometabolite roles are active therapeutic targets, but metabolic heterogeneity and plasticity complicate intervention.
- **Metabolic engineering limits**: Predicting systemic effects of genetic modifications remains difficult due to incomplete kinetic parameters, regulatory complexity, and host burden.
