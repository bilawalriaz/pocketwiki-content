# Aladdin

## Overview
Aladdin is a Middle-Eastern folk tale best known from *One Thousand and One Nights*, though it was not part of the original manuscripts. It was added by French translator Antoine Galland in 1710, based on a story told by Syrian storyteller Hanna Diyab. The tale of an impoverished boy who finds a magical lamp and a genie has become a global cultural phenomenon, adapted into countless books, films, and plays.

## Timeline
- **1709** — Antoine Galland meets Syrian storyteller Hanna Diyab in Paris.
- **1709–10** — Galland transcribes and publishes the story of Aladdin in his French translation of *The Nights*.
- **1885** — Sir Richard Francis Burton publishes an influential English translation.
- **1888** — Arabist Hermann Zotenberg publishes a critical edition of an Arabic manuscript version.
- **1992** — Disney releases its animated film *Aladdin*, which becomes a massive cultural touchstone.
- **2014** — Disney's musical stage adaptation opens on Broadway.
- **2019** — Disney releases a live-action remake of *Aladdin*.

## Body
### Origin and Manuscripts
The story is classified as an "orphan tale," not found in the original *Nights* manuscripts. Antoine Galland included it in his 1710 French translation, crediting no source. His diary later revealed he heard it from Hanna Diyab, a Maronite storyteller from Aleppo. Two Arabic manuscripts of Aladdin were later discovered in Paris, but scholar Muhsin Mahdi demonstrated they were likely "back-translations" of Galland's French text. Scholars Ruth Bottigheimer and Paulo Horta argue Diyab may be considered the original author, as parallels exist with his autobiography.

### Plot Summary
Set in "one of the cities of Ancient China," the story follows Aladdin, a poor young man. A sorcerer from the Maghreb poses as his uncle and tricks him into retrieving a magic lamp from a booby-trapped cave. Aladdin escapes with the help of a ring's genie and later discovers the lamp contains a far more powerful genie. Using the lamp's genie, he becomes wealthy, marries Princess Badroulbadour, and builds a magnificent palace. The sorcerer steals the lamp, but Aladdin, aided by the ring's genie and the princess's cunning, recovers it and defeats the sorcerer. He later foils a plot by the sorcerer's brother and eventually becomes sultan.

### Setting and Cultural Context
The story is explicitly set in China, yet its details—such as a Sultan, Muslim characters, and a Jewish merchant—are more consistent with a Middle Eastern setting. Scholars suggest "China" may have been used abstractly to mean an exotic, faraway land. Historical Muslim communities existed in China, and some speculate the setting could be Turkestan, where rulers adopted the title "Sultan of the East and China."

### Tale Type and Distribution
Aladdin is classified as tale type ATU 561 in the Aarne–Thompson–Uther Index, featuring a poor hero who finds a magical object (a lamp) that grants wishes. It is related to ATU 560 (The Magic Ring) and ATU 562 (The Spirit in the Blue Light). The tale has a wide distribution, with the greatest density of variants in Europe and the Mediterranean, and others found across the Middle East, Central Asia, India, and Southeast Asia.

## Terms
- **Orphan tale**: A story added to a collection (like *The Nights*) that was not part of its original core manuscripts.
- **Aarne–Thompson–Uther (ATU) Index**: A system for classifying folktales by their core motifs and plot types.
- **Genie (Jinn)**: A supernatural spirit in Arabian mythology, often bound to serve the holder of a magical object.
- **Maghreb**: The region of Northwest Africa, including modern Morocco, Algeria, and Tunisia.
- **Back-translation**: A translation of a text back into its original language, often from a secondary translation.
- **Pantomime**: A type of musical comedy theatrical production, popular in the UK, often based on fairy tales.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authorship**: Was Hanna Diyab the original author of the Aladdin story, or merely a source for Galland? Scholars Bottigheimer and Horta argue for Diyab's authorship based on autobiographical parallels.
- **Authenticity of Arabic Manuscripts**: Are the discovered Arabic manuscripts original sources or later "back-translations" of Galland's French version? Muhsin Mahdi's analysis supports the latter theory.
- **Setting**: Is the Chinese setting a literal geographic reference or a literary device for an exotic land? The historical presence of Muslim communities in China and the use of "China" as a generic term in Arabic literature are key points in this debate.
