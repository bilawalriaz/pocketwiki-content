# Kalahari Desert

## Overview
The Kalahari is a large semiarid sandy savanna in Southern Africa, covering 900,000 km² across Botswana, Namibia, and South Africa. It is not a true desert due to its vegetation cover but is defined by its red sand, lack of permanent surface water, and a complex climatic history that has shaped its unique ecosystems and human inhabitants.

## Timeline
- **c. 30,000–11,000 BP** — Wetter, cooler period with abundant vegetation and permanent water sources.
- **c. 10,000 BP** — Ancient Lake Makgadikgadi drains or dries out.
- **c. 11,000 BP** — Climate shifts to Holocene; rainfall decreases, establishing the modern semi-arid ecosystem.
- **20,000 years ago** — San people begin living in the Kalahari as hunter-gatherers.
- **1996** — De Beers evaluates diamond mining potential at Gope.
- **1997** — Eviction of San and Bakgalagadi tribes from the Central Kalahari Game Reserve begins.
- **2006** — Botswana's High Court rules the eviction unlawful.

## Body

### Geography and Climate
The Kalahari is drained by dry valleys, seasonally flooded pans, and the salt pans of Makgadikgadi and Etosha. The only permanent river is the Okavango, which forms a wildlife-rich delta. The climate varies from sub-humid in the north and east to "Kalaharian" semi-arid in the south and west. This subtropical climate features a dry season from April to September and a wet summer season. Its high altitude (600–1600 m) results in cooler temperatures than other subtropical deserts, with common winter frost. Rainfall ranges from 110 mm in the driest southwest to over 500 mm in the wetter north and east.

### Historical Climate and Hydrology
Geological evidence shows the Kalahari was once much wetter. During the late Pleistocene (c. 30,000–11,000 BP), it supported large paleolakes, river systems, and diverse fauna like elephants and hippopotamuses. The massive ancient Lake Makgadikgadi, which may have covered 120,000 km², dried up around 10,000 years ago. Huge subterranean water reserves, such as the Dragon's Breath Cave (the largest non-subglacial underground lake), are remnants of this wetter past.

### Vegetation and Flora
The Kalahari supports more life than a true desert. Its vegetation ranges from xeric savanna in the south and west to dry forests in the north and east. Typical plants include acacia trees, camelthorn, and various grasses. The kiwano fruit is endemic to the region. Vegetation cover is dense in most areas, except in the true semi-desert southwest and on seasonal salt pans.

### Fauna and Protected Areas
The Kalahari is home to large predators like lions, cheetahs, and African wild dogs, as well as antelopes, ostriches, and diverse birds of prey. Seasonal wetlands like the Makgadikgadi Pans attract tens of thousands of flamingos. Key protected areas include the Central Kalahari Game Reserve, Kgalagadi Transfrontier Park, and Khutse Game Reserve.

### Human Population and Conflict
The San people have lived in the Kalahari for 20,000 years as hunter-gatherers, obtaining water from plants and ostrich eggshells. Other inhabitants include Bantu-speaking Tswana and Kgalagadi groups. A major conflict arose in 1997 when the San and Bakgalagadi were evicted from the Central Kalahari Game Reserve, a move linked to diamond mining interests. A 2006 court ruling declared the eviction unlawful, though mining permits were still granted.

## Terms
- **Semiarid**: A climate with low, unreliable rainfall, supporting sparse vegetation.
- **Paleolake**: An ancient lake that has since dried up or significantly shrunk.
- **Xeric savanna**: A dry, grassy ecosystem with scattered trees, adapted to low moisture.
- **Halophytic**: Plants adapted to grow in saline (salty) conditions, like those on salt pans.
- **Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)**: A belt of low pressure near the equator where trade winds converge, causing seasonal rains.
- **Kalaharian climate**: A subtropical, semi-arid climate with hot summers and cool, frosty winters.
- **Hunter-gatherer**: A person who obtains food by hunting wild animals and gathering plants, rather than farming.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the conflict between conservation, indigenous rights, and economic development. The 1997 eviction of the San and Bakgalagadi tribes from the Central Kalahari Game Reserve, ostensibly for wildlife conservation but coinciding with diamond exploration, was ruled unlawful in 2006. However, the government still granted mining permits within the reserve, leaving the future of the indigenous populations and the protected area's integrity in question.
