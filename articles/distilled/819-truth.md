# Truth

## Overview  
Truth is the property of statements, beliefs, or propositions that accurately represent reality or facts. It is a foundational concept in philosophy, science, ethics, and many other fields, serving both as a goal of inquiry and a standard for correct belief. Over millennia, thinkers have proposed competing theories—correspondence, coherence, pragmatic, semantic, and deflationary—each offering a different account of what makes something true. The debate remains unresolved, complicated by paradoxes, domain-specific applications, and questions about objectivity versus relativity.

## Body  

### Truthbearers and Truthmakers  
Truth is typically attributed to **truthbearers**—entities like sentences, propositions, or beliefs capable of being true or false. Sentences are concrete linguistic expressions whose truth value can vary with context or interpretation. Propositions, as abstract meanings underlying sentences, aim to resolve ambiguity but raise questions about abstract objects. Beliefs, as mental states, directly link truth to cognition but are private and hard to analyze. **Truthmakers**, conversely, are real-world entities (facts, states of affairs) whose existence makes a truthbearer true. Truth conditions specify the circumstances under which a statement would be true, anchoring truth in reality rather than convention.

### Major Theories of Truth  
The **correspondence theory** holds that truth consists in matching facts or reality. It grounds truth objectively but struggles with domains lacking empirical facts (e.g., ethics, mathematics). The **coherence theory** defines truth as consistency within a system of beliefs, appealing to holistic reasoning but criticized for allowing coherent fictions. **Pragmatic theories**, rooted in thinkers like William James and C.S. Peirce, define truth by practical utility or consensus under ideal inquiry, risking subjectivity. The **semantic theory**, pioneered by Alfred Tarski, uses a metalanguage to define truth conditions via T-sentences, avoiding paradoxes but offering limited metaphysical insight. **Deflationary theories** argue that truth has no deep nature; expressions like “‘Snow is white’ is true” merely restate the original claim. **Pluralists** reject a single definition, asserting that truth varies by domain.

### Types of Truth  
Truths are categorized by domain and epistemic access. **A priori** truths are known independently of experience (e.g., math); **a posteriori** truths depend on sensory input. **Necessary** truths hold in all possible worlds; **contingent** truths depend on actual conditions. **Analytic** truths follow from meaning alone; **synthetic** truths require empirical verification. **Moral**, **aesthetic**, and **religious truths** often involve evaluative or transcendent claims, debated for their objectivity. **Scientific truths** emerge from rigorous testing, while **historical truths** face interpretive challenges. **Truth simpliciter** contrasts with context-relative truth found in possible worlds or models.

### Truth in Culture and Disciplines  
In **religion**, truth is often linked to divine reality—e.g., Christianity’s “I am the truth,” Islam’s Al-Haqq, Hinduism’s satya, and Buddhism’s two truths doctrine. **Art** expresses truth through aesthetic experience and symbolic meaning. In **law**, truth guides justice but must be proven through evidence and standards of proof. **Journalism** seeks factual accuracy amid misinformation and bias. **Psychology** studies how cognition acquires and distorts truth. In **computer science**, Boolean logic encodes truth as binary values. **Probability theory** quantifies uncertainty in degrees of belief.

### Historical Development  
Ancient civilizations explored truth’s nature: Greeks like Parmenides and Plato linked truth to reality; Aristotle developed early correspondence ideas. Medieval thinkers integrated truth with theology—Augustine equated truth with God; Aquinas saw it as conformity to divine ideas. Modern philosophers shifted focus to epistemology and language—Kant redefined truth as agreement between judgment and object; Nietzsche critiqued truth as socially constructed. Twentieth-century figures like Tarski, Davidson, and Putnam advanced formal and pluralist approaches, while postmodernists questioned objectivity altogether.

## Terms  

- **Truthbearer**: An entity (sentence, proposition, belief) that can be true or false.  
- **Truthmaker**: A real entity whose existence makes a truthbearer true.  
- **Correspondence Theory**: Truth is alignment between statements and facts.  
- **Coherence Theory**: Truth arises from logical consistency among beliefs.  
- **Pragmatic Theory**: Truth is defined by practical consequences or inquiry outcomes.  
- **Semantic Theory**: Truth analyzed via truth conditions using a metalanguage (T-sentences).  
- **Deflationary Theory**: Truth lacks intrinsic nature; linguistic role exhausts the concept.  
- **Bivalence**: The principle that every proposition is either true or false.  
- **Truth Value Gap**: A statement that is neither true nor false.  
- **Two Truths Doctrine (Buddhism)**: Distinction between conventional and ultimate truth.

## Debates and Open Questions  

- Is there a single, unified concept of truth, or does it vary by domain (**pluralism vs. monism**)?  
- Can truth be reduced to correspondence, coherence, or utility, or is it irreducibly complex?  
- Are moral, aesthetic, or religious truths genuinely objective, or culturally relative?  
- Do truth-value gaps exist—statements that are neither true nor false?  
- How should paradoxes like the **liar paradox** (“This statement is false”) be resolved?  
- Does truth require justification, or can a belief be true without being warranted?  
- Is truth always binary (**bivalent**), or do degrees of truth exist (**truthlikeness**, fuzzy logic)?  
- Should truth be grounded in reality (**realism**) or in epistemic practices (**anti-realism**)?
