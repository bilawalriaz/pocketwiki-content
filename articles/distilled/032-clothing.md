# Clothing

## Overview

Clothing consists of items worn on the body, typically made from textiles but also from animal skins, furs, and other natural or synthetic materials. It is a feature of every known human society, serving protective, physiological, social, and cultural functions—from thermal regulation and hazard protection to expressing social status, gender, and religious identity. Clothing production has grown into one of the world's largest industries, employing millions and facing challenges around working conditions and sustainability.

## Body

### Origin and history

Estimates of when humans began wearing clothes range from 40,000 to as many as 3 million years ago. A 2003 study suggested at least 100,000 years ago based on lice: body lice cannot survive outside clothing, so their divergence from head lice—estimated between 40,000 and 170,000 years before present—implies clothing adoption. However, recent transcriptome analyses found body and head lice "almost genetically identical," casting doubt on louse-based dating. Direct archaeological evidence in Morocco (2021) supports clothes making 120,000 years ago.

Early clothing likely consisted of fur, leather, leaves, or grass that was draped, wrapped, or tied. Knowledge remains inferential since clothing decays faster than stone or metal. Sewing needles of bone and ivory date to about 30,000 BC (Kostenki, Russia) and at least 50,000 years ago (Denisova Cave, Siberia). The oldest known woven garment is the Tarkhan dress, an over 5,000-year-old linen piece.

### Making clothing

Arctic cultures crafted garments exclusively from treated animal furs and skins; others used textiles woven, knitted, or twined from wool, linen, cotton, silk, hemp, and ramie. Hand fabric-making is labor-intensive, involving fiber preparation, spinning, and weaving. The textile industry was the first mechanized—with the powered loom—during the Industrial Revolution.

Two main construction approaches exist. Draping wraps uncut rectangles of cloth, as with the dhoti and sari (Indian subcontinent), Scottish kilt, and Javanese sarong, allowing various sizes to wear the same garment. The alternative involves measuring, cutting, and sewing, using patterns, tailors, and adjustable mannequins. Traditional European patterns used fabric conservatively, adding triangular gussets and reusing remnants for patchwork; modern fashion cuts less conservatively, leaving odd-shaped remnants sold as waste or turned into quilts.

### Clothing as comfort

Comfort encompasses aesthetic, thermophysiological, thermal, moisture, tactile, and pressure comfort. Aesthetic comfort involves visual perception influenced by color, construction, style, and fit. Thermophysiological comfort maintains moisture and heat balance; natural fibers are breathable and absorbent, while synthetics are hydrophobic and repel moisture. Thermal comfort requires the skin surface to stay between 28 and 30 °C (82–86 °F); below or above is discomforting. Moisture comfort fails when more than "50% to 65% of the body is wet." Tactile comfort relates to fabric smoothness, roughness, softness, and stiffness. Pressure comfort depends on sensory response to fabric structure; Lycra improves it.

### Functions

Clothing's most obvious function is protection from elements—wind, sunburn, cold, rain, and snow. Shelter reduces clothing need; coats and outer layers are removed indoors. Not all body coverings count as clothing: handbags are accessories, scarves are easily removed, jewelry is adornment, but corrective eyeglasses and sunglasses are not accessories due to protective function. Specialized clothing includes space suits, armor, diving suits, bee-keeper gear, motorcycle leathers, and high-visibility clothing. The distinction between clothing and protective equipment is not always clear-cut.

Clothing also has social implications: covering body parts social norms require covered, signaling status, and expressing personal style. Someone lacking means for appropriate clothing is described as worn, ragged, or shabby.

### Scholarship

Serious study of clothing functions began in the nineteenth century as European colonial powers encountered tropical environments. Key works include Flügel's *Psychology of Clothes* (1930) and Newburgh's *Physiology of Heat Regulation and The Science of Clothing* (1949). By 1968, environmental physiology had advanced, but clothing science changed little; Newburgh remains cited by contemporary authors developing thermoregulatory models.

Clothing history reveals much about past lives, but challenges exist: textiles decay, and costume collections focus on unique pieces, limiting study of everyday clothing.

### Cultural aspects

Gender differentiation in clothing is considered appropriate in most cultures, shown in styles, colors, fabrics, and types. In Western societies, skirts and dresses are women's clothing; neckties are men's. Trousers were once exclusively male but are now worn by both genders. Women may wear masculine clothing, but the reverse is unusual. Sumptuary laws in some cultures regulate required attire; Islam requires women to cover more than men, from headscarf to burqa.

Clothing signals social status. In ancient Rome, only senators wore Tyrian purple; in Hawaii, only high chiefs wore feather cloaks; in imperial China, only the emperor wore yellow. Modern societies signal status through rare or luxury items and peer pressure.

Religious clothing may be occupational, worn only during ceremonies or daily. Sikhs wear turbans as part of their religion. Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism, and Jainism emphasize cleanliness of religious dress. Jewish ritual requires rending one's upper garment in mourning. The Quran describes spouses as "clothing/covering (Libaas)" for each other (2:187). Biblical clothing passages include Adam and Eve's fig leaves, Joseph's coat of many colors, and Temple priests' specific garments.

### Contemporary clothing

The Western dress code has changed over 500+ years. Mechanization made cloth widely affordable; synthetic fabrics altered "stylish" definitions. Blue jeans became popular in the late twentieth century and are worn even at formal events. Designer licensing, pioneered by Pierre Cardin, Yves Saint Laurent, and Guy Laroche in the 1960s, became common from the 1970s.

Western styles became international by the early twenty-first century, a process beginning with European colonialism and perpetuated by Western media. Fast fashion—inexpensive, mass-produced Western clothing—is global, and donated used clothing reaches poor countries via charities. People still wear ethnic dress on special occasions: Korean hanboks at weddings, Tongan men combining T-shirts with tupenu wraps.

Sportswear includes shorts, T-shirts, leotards, tracksuits, and specialized wet suits, salopettes, and spandex base layers for wicking sweat. Paris set 1900–1940 fashion trends with drop-waist day dresses and bobbed hair. The early twenty-first century features diverse styles from haute couture to thrift store grunge.

### Political issues

Garment workers faced challenging conditions—long hours, lack of benefits and representation—often in sweatshops, mostly in developing countries but also industrialized nations. NGOs, designers (Katharine Hamnett, American Apparel, Veja, Quiksilver, eVocal, Edun), and campaign groups like the Clean Clothes Campaign sponsor awareness events. Outsourcing to low-wage countries became possible after the Multi Fibre Agreement (MFA), which placed textile import quotas, was abolished. Many countries fail to enforce International Labour Organization treaties fully; India has not ratified sections 87 and 92.

Fur use dates to prehistoric times. Indigenous Arctic peoples still use it for warmth, but in developed countries it marks expensive designer clothing. PETA and others call fur farming cruel. Copenhagen (2022) and London (2018) fashion weeks banned real fur; Gucci, Chanel, Versace, and Furla stopped using it; Canada Goose discontinued new coyote fur in 2020. Israel banned new real-fur sales in 2021 (except religious practice); California banned fur trapping in 2019, with a total new-fur sales ban effective 1 January 2023 (except sheep, cow, rabbit).

### Life cycle

Clothing suffers from body oils, sweat, sun, moisture, abrasion, dirt, and pests. Cleaning leather is difficult; bark cloth (tapa) cannot be washed without dissolving. Most cloth can be laundered and mended. Laundering methods range from pounding rocks to washing machines and dry cleaning; boiling, chemical cleaning, and ironing sterilize fabrics. Many formal clothes require ironing; knit casual wear and permanent press (treated with polytetrafluoroethylene) do not. Clothes are stored on hangers or folded; cedar, lavender, mothballs, and airtight containers deter pests like carpet beetles and clothing moths.

Non-iron shirt resin releases formaldehyde, causing contact dermatitis in some people; no disclosure requirements exist. In 2008, the U.S. Government Accountability Office found highest formaldehyde levels in non-wrinkle shirts and pants; a 1999 study found 7 of 27 shirts still exceeded 75 ppm (safe limit) after six months of washing.

Historically, mending was an art when cloth cost more than labor. Today, mass-manufactured clothing is cheaper than repair labor, so many buy new. Thrifty people still replace zippers and buttons; techniques include darning, invisible mending, and visible mending inspired by Japanese Sashiko.

An estimated 80–150 billion garments are produced annually. Unwearable clothing becomes quilts, rags, rugs, bandages; cellulose fibers can be recycled into paper. Used clothing is donated, sold at consignment shops and auctions, or collected industrially for shipment to poorer countries. Globally, used clothing is worth $4 billion; the U.S. leads exports at $575 million. Synthetics from petrochemicals are not renewable or biodegradable. Excess inventory is sometimes destroyed to preserve brand value.

### Global trade

EU member states imported €166 billion of clothes in 2018 (51% from outside the EU); exported €116 billion (77% within the EU). Global clothing exports reached US$790.1 billion in 2022, up 10.6% from 2021. China leads at US$178.4 billion (22.6% market share), followed by Bangladesh (US$40.8 billion), Vietnam (US$39.8 billion), India (US$36.1 billion), and Turkey (US$29.7 billion). Vietnam's exports reached US$39.8 billion in 2022, up 14.2%, with the U.S. taking 47.3% and the EU 24.6%.

## Terms

- **Textile**: Woven, knitted, or twined fabric made from animal or plant fibers
- **Thermophysiological comfort**: Clothing's capacity to maintain moisture and heat balance between body and environment
- **Sumptuary laws**: Regulations controlling what people may wear based on rank or gender
- **Multi Fibre Agreement (MFA)**: Treaty placing quotas on textile imports, deemed protectionist; its abolition enabled outsourcing
- **Fast fashion**: Inexpensive, mass-produced Western clothing distributed globally
- **Sweatshop**: Workplace with long hours, lack of benefits, and no worker representation
- **Permanent press**: Fabric treated with a coating (e.g., polytetrafluoroethylene) that suppresses wrinkles
- **Darning**: Mending technique for repairing holes or tears in fabric
- **Sashiko**: Japanese visible mending technique
- **Haute couture**: Expensive, high-fashion custom clothing

## Debates and open questions

The origin date of clothing is debated, ranging from 40,000 to 3 million years ago. Louse-based dating (at least 100,000 years ago) has been challenged by transcriptome analyses showing body and head lice are "almost genetically identical," suggesting body lice emergence may result from regulatory or epigenetic changes rather than clothing adoption. The distinction between clothing and protective equipment remains unclear, and whether textile production in developing nations is exploitative or beneficial is contested.
