# Benedict of Nursia

## Overview

Saint Benedict of Nursia (c. 480–547) was an Italian monk whose Rule of Saint Benedict became the foundational text of Western Christian monasticism. His emphasis on balance, moderation, and reasonableness persuaded most medieval religious orders to adopt his rule, making it the most influential monastic code in Western Christendom. Pope Paul VI declared him patron saint of Europe in 1964.

## Timeline

- **c. 480** — Benedict born in Nursia, Italy
- **c. 495–500** — Leaves Rome, settles as hermit in cave at Subiaco
- **c. 500** — Becomes abbot of monastery near Subiaco; attempt on his life by poisoned bread
- **c. 530** — Leaves Subiaco, founds 12 monasteries in the area
- **530** — Founds monastery at Monte Cassino
- **21 March 547** — Dies at Monte Cassino
- **1964** — Pope Paul VI declares Benedict patron saint of Europe

## Body

### Hagiography and Sources

The primary ancient account of Benedict's life comes from Pope Gregory I's *Dialogues* (Book Two), written around 593. Gregory intended his work as a *floretum*—an anthology of striking miracles of Italian holy men—not a modern biography. He based his anecdotes on direct testimony from Benedict's disciples: Constantinus, Honoratus, Valentinianus, and Simplicius. Gregory's approach reflects the medieval understanding of history as a branch of grammar and rhetoric, designed to teach spiritual lessons rather than provide chronological accuracy.

### Early Life and Hermitage

Benedict was born to a Roman noble family in Nursia. Sent to study in Rome, he found the academic environment unsatisfying and fled to Enfide (identified with modern Affile) with his nurse. There he encountered Romanus of Subiaco, who gave him the monastic habit. Benedict then lived as a hermit for three years in a cave above a lake near Subiaco, sustained by Romanus's visits.

### Leadership and Foundation of Communities

After his hermitage, Benedict was persuaded to lead a monastery near Subiaco, but the monks attempted to poison him. When his blessing caused the poisoned cup to shatter, he returned to his cave. A priest named Florentius later tried to kill him with poisoned bread, but a raven carried it away. To escape further temptations, Benedict left Subiaco around 530 and founded twelve monasteries in the vicinity before establishing the great monastery at Monte Cassino.

### The Rule of Saint Benedict

Benedict's Rule, comprising seventy-three short chapters, provides both spiritual guidance for communal monastic life and administrative structure for monastery management. More than half the chapters address obedience and humility; about a quarter regulate the Opus Dei (divine office); the remainder outline monastery governance. Benedictine asceticism is characterized by moderation rather than extreme rigor.

### Veneration and Legacy

Benedict died of fever at Monte Cassino on 21 March 547 and was buried alongside his sister Scholastica. His feast day is observed on 11 July in the Roman Calendar (moved from 21 March to avoid Lenten conflicts), while Eastern traditions commemorate him on 14 March. The early Middle Ages have been called "the Benedictine centuries" due to his profound influence on European civilization and culture.

## Terms

- **Opus Dei**: The divine office or canonical hours—set prayer times observed by monastic communities throughout the day and night.
- **Epieíkeia** (επιείκεια): Greek term meaning "spirit of balance, moderation and reasonableness" that characterizes Benedict's approach.
- **Floretum**: Latin term meaning "flower garden"; Gregory I described his Dialogues as an anthology of striking miracles.
- **Hagiography**: Biographical writing about saints, emphasizing their spiritual virtues and miracles rather than strict historical accuracy.
- **Confederation**: The structure of the modern Order of Saint Benedict—a loose association of autonomous congregations rather than a centralized order.
- **Aborted papal brief**: Official papal document; Pope Benedict XIV approved the Saint Benedict Medal through briefs in 1741 and 1742.
- **Raven**: In Benedict's legend, a bird that carried away poisoned bread, saving his life.

## Debates and Open Questions

The authenticity of Gregory I's *Dialogues* as a historical source is disputed, as it was written decades after Benedict's death and serves primarily as a spiritual portrait rather than factual biography. The exact origin of the Saint Benedict Medal remains uncertain despite its association with a 1647 witchcraft trial at Natternberg. The date of Benedict's birth (c. 480) and death (21 March 547) are based on tradition rather than contemporary records. The 1969 revision of the General Roman Calendar moved his memorial from 21 March to 11 July, creating ongoing uncertainty about the proper commemoration date.
