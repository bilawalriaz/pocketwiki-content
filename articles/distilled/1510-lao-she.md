# Lao She

## Overview
Lao She (1899–1966), born Shu Qingchun to a poor Manchu family in Beijing, was a foundational figure in modern Chinese literature. He is celebrated for his vivid, humorous, and ironic portrayals of urban life, particularly through the Beijing dialect, in major works such as the novel *Rickshaw Boy* (1936) and the play *Teahouse* (1957). His writing bridged the May Fourth Movement’s vernacular revolution (Baihua) and the demands of wartime resistance, earning him the title "People's Artist." His life ended tragically during the Cultural Revolution when, after public humiliation by Red Guards, he drowned in Taiping Lake; the exact circumstances—suicide or murder—remain unresolved.

## Body

### Early Life and Formative Trauma
Lao She was born into the Manchu Šumuru clan of the Plain Red Banner, a hereditary military caste. His father died in 1901 fighting the Eight-Nation Alliance during the Boxer Rebellion, leaving the family destitute. Lao She later recalled his mother’s factual accounts of "foreign devils" as more terrifying than fairy tales, instilling a lifelong anti-imperialist consciousness. Financial hardship forced him to leave high school early, but he secured admission to Beijing Normal University (1913–1918). The 1919 May Fourth Movement proved pivotal: it supplied the "new spirit and new literary language" (Baihua, or plain vernacular) that enabled his transition from administrator to writer.

### London Years and Literary Debut (1924–1929)
As a lecturer at the University of London’s School of Oriental Studies, Lao She immersed himself in English literature, particularly Charles Dickens, whose influence shaped his narrative structure and social empathy. He began writing seriously, publishing his first novel, *The Philosophy of Lao Zhang* (1926), modeled on *Nicholas Nickleby* but set among Beijing students. He adopted the pen name "Lao She" (meaning "abandoned self") during this period. His London experiences informed *Mr Ma and Son* (1929), a satirical depiction of Chinese immigrants in London’s Chinatown that critiques racial stereotypes through humor.

### Return to China, Satire, and Resistance (1930–1949)
Back in China, Lao She taught at universities in Jinan and Qingdao while producing major works. *Cat Country* (1932), a satirical fable set on Mars populated by cat-people, allegorized China’s stagnation and vulnerability to Japanese aggression (Manchuria 1931, Shanghai 1932); Lao She later deemed it an artistic failure. *Rickshaw Boy* (1936) cemented his reputation: it follows Xiangzi, a peasant-turned-rickshaw-puller in 1920s Beijing, whose repeated efforts at independence are crushed by structural forces, making the novel a timeless study of labor exploitation. In 1938, Lao She was elected leader of the All-China Resistance Association of Writers and Artists, uniting cultural workers against Japan. He spent 1946–1949 in the US on a State Department grant, aided by Pearl S. Buck (who translated *Rickshaw Boy* into a US bestseller), but chose to return to the new People’s Republic of China in December 1949.

### Post-1949: State Writer, *Teahouse*, and Baihua Advocacy
In the PRC, Lao She became a cultural establishment figure. He championed **Baihua** (plain vernacular) as the standard for modern written Chinese, arguing that accessible language was essential for mass mobilization and literary realism. His masterpiece *Teahouse* (1957) uses a single Beijing teahouse ("Yu Tai") across three acts (1898, 1910s, 1940s) to dramatize half a century of social collapse and failed reform. He also wrote the children’s opera *Treasure Boat* (1961) and revised older texts like *Classics for Girls* (1938) to align with wartime gender roles.

### Death and Posthumous Fate
When the Cultural Revolution erupted in 1966, Lao She was denounced as a counterrevolutionary. Red Guards paraded and beat him publicly at the Temple of Confucius. The official account states he committed suicide by drowning in Taiping Lake on 24 August 1966; scholar Leo Ou-fan Lee and others have raised the possibility of murder, but no definitive evidence exists. His relatives hid his manuscripts in coal piles and chimneys, preserving them for posthumous publication. He was rehabilitated in 1978.

### Legacy and Commemoration
Lao She’s former courtyard home in Beijing (19 Fengfu Lane, originally 10 Fengsheng Lane) became the Lao She Memorial Hall in 1999, preserving persimmon trees he planted. The biennial Lao She Literary Award (est. 2000) honors Beijing writers. The commercial Laoshe Tea House (opened 1988) trades on his name but caters to tourists. Numerous film and stage adaptations—*Rickshaw Boy* (1982), *Teahouse* (1982), *The Drum Singers* (1987), *Beneath the Red Banner* (2000 stage)—attest to his enduring canonical status.

## Terms
- ****Baihua**** — "Plain speech"; the vernacular written Chinese promoted by the May Fourth Movement to replace Classical Chinese (wenyan), which Lao She adopted early and advocated as All-China Resistance Association leader.
- ****Plain Red Banner**** — One of the Eight Banners, the hereditary military-administrative divisions of Manchu society; Lao She’s family belonged to the Šumuru clan of this banner.
- ****May Fourth Movement (1919)**** — Anti-imperialist cultural and political movement sparked by student protests; catalyzed the New Culture Movement’s shift to vernacular literature, directly enabling Lao She’s career.
- ****Rickshaw Boy (Camel Xiangzi)**** — Lao She’s 1936 novel; Xiangzi, a peasant-turned-rickshaw-puller in 1920s Beijing, embodies the structural doom of the urban poor despite individual striving.
- ****Teahouse**** — Lao She’s 1957 three-act play; set in the "Yu Tai" teahouse from 1898 to 1949, it uses ensemble dialogue to chronicle China’s social disintegration across three regimes.
- ****Cat Country**** — 1932 satirical fable/sci-fi novel; a visitor to Mars finds a decaying cat-people civilization, allegorizing China’s stagnation and response to Japanese invasion.
- ****All-China Resistance Association of Writers and Artists**** — United-front cultural organization founded 27 March 1938; Lao She served as its leader to mobilize writers against Japan.
- ****Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)**** — Mao Zedong’s decade of political purge; Lao She was among the intellectuals publicly humiliated ("struggle sessions"), leading to his death.
- ****Three-Self Patriotic Movement**** — State-sanctioned Protestant body (est. 1954) enforcing self-governance, self-support, self-propagation; Lao She argued Chinese Christianity must be "sinicized" and free of foreign control.
- ****Pearl S. Buck**** — American Nobel laureate novelist; sponsored Lao She’s 1946–1949 US stay and translated *Rickshaw Boy* (1945, unauthorized happy ending; later authorized versions).

## Debates and open questions
- **Cause of death**: Official sources record suicide by drowning (24 August 1966) following public humiliation; Leo Ou-fan Lee and others suggest murder by Red Guards, but no reliable evidence has emerged to confirm either version.
- **Artistic merit of *Cat Country***: Lao She himself considered the novel an artistic failure; critics remain divided on whether its allegorical mode succeeds as satire or collapses under didacticism.
- **Politics of *Teahouse***: Scholars debate whether the play’s panoramic pessimism implicitly critiques the Communist revolution it culminates in, or whether it serves the state’s narrative of "liberation" from feudal chaos.
- **Unauthorized *Rickshaw Boy* translation**: The 1945 US edition (translated by Evan King) appended a bowdlerized happy ending; the 1979 Jean James and 1981 Shi Xiaoqing translations restored the original tragic conclusion, raising questions about reception history in the West.
- **Three-Self Movement role**: Lao She’s endorsement of sinicized Christianity aligns with state policy, but critics argue the Three-Self Patriotic Movement primarily served government control over religion rather than genuine ecclesiastical independence.
