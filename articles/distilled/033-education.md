# Education

Education is the transmission of knowledge, skills, and character traits, occurring through formal schooling, structured non-formal programs, or unstructured informal daily experiences. It is a purposeful activity whose precise definition, aims, and distinction from indoctrination are debated. Education plays a crucial role in society by socializing individuals, driving economic growth, and promoting personal development, with organized institutions and international bodies like UNESCO shaping its practice and global access.

## Timeline

- **c. 9000 BCE** — Emergence of agriculture begins a slow shift toward educational specialization.
- **4th millennium BCE** — Invention of writing in Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley, and China enables formal education.
- **14th century** — Aztec civilization makes formal education mandatory for youth regardless of social class.
- **15th century** — Invention of the printing press reduces book costs, increasing literacy.
- **17th–19th centuries** — Age of Enlightenment emphasizes reason; public, compulsory, free education rises.
- **1970** — 28% of primary-school-age children worldwide do not attend school.
- **2015** — Primary-school-age children not attending school drops to 9%; Education for All is replaced by Sustainable Development Goal 4.
- **2020s** — COVID-19 pandemic forces widespread school closures and a shift to remote learning.

## Body

### Definitions

The term "education" derives from Latin *educare* ("to bring up") and *educere* ("to bring forth"). Theorists debate its exact nature, with some viewing it as a process (schooling, teaching) and others as the resulting mental states of educated persons. R. S. Peters outlines three essential features: imparting knowledge and understanding, doing so beneficially, and in a morally appropriate manner. However, precise definitions face criticism for excluding less common forms, leading some theorists to prefer definitions based on "family resemblance" or to argue the term is context-dependent.

"Thick" conceptions state education must lead to improvement, contrasting with value-neutral "thin" conceptions. Many scholars stress critical thinking to distinguish education from indoctrination, which merely instills beliefs. This distinction is not universally accepted, as some indoctrination may be necessary for young children before their minds are developed. Definitions can be teacher-centered, student-centered, or both.

### Types of Education

The most common division is formal, non-formal, and informal education. Formal education occurs in a complex, chronological, and hierarchical institutional framework (from primary school to university), is government-controlled, and is compulsory up to a certain age. Non-formal education is organized and purposeful but occurs outside the system (e.g., tutoring, fitness classes). Informal education is unsystematic, spontaneous learning through daily experiences, with no designated authority figure, such as learning a first language from parents.

Levels are defined by the UNESCO International Standard Classification of Education, based on age, duration, and content complexity. These include early childhood (level 0), primary (level 1), secondary (levels 2–3), post-secondary non-tertiary (level 4), and tertiary education (levels 5–8). Primary education teaches basic skills in reading, writing, and math and is compulsory in almost all countries. Secondary education is divided into lower and upper stages, with upper secondary often leading to a high school diploma. Tertiary education includes short-cycle, bachelor's, master's, and doctoral levels, leading to academic degrees.

Other distinctions include traditional vs. alternative education (e.g., Montessori, Waldorf, homeschooling), categories by learner age or sex, special education for students with disabilities, and classifications by teaching method (teacher-centered vs. student-centered). Autodidacticism is self-education without guidance. Education is also categorized by subject (e.g., science, language) and by funding source (state vs. private).

### Role in Society and Institutions

Education enables social cohesion by teaching cultural norms and basic skills like reading and ICT, and promotes civic participation in democracies. Economically, it creates productive members of society, stimulates growth, reduces poverty, and is associated with lower birth rates. It can also address global problems like climate change and prepare workforces for technological changes like automation. On an individual level, it promotes personal development, health, and well-being.

Organized institutions—schools, universities, and ministries of education—regulate aspects like school age, class times, teacher qualifications, and curricula. International organizations like UNESCO advocate for education as a human right (Convention on the Rights of the Child) and promote initiatives like Education for All. Non-governmental organizations, such as the International Association of Universities, also play a key role.

### Factors of Educational Success

Educational success is often measured by exam scores but also includes attendance, graduation, and post-school indicators. Psychological factors include motivation (intrinsic is more beneficial than extrinsic), intelligence (often measured by IQ, but Howard Gardner argues for multiple distinct types), and personality traits like conscientiousness. Sociological factors include socioeconomic status, ethnicity, and gender, which are linked to inequality and discrimination. Low socioeconomic status can lead to slower cognitive development and higher dropout rates. Gender inequalities have improved but persist, particularly in STEM fields. Stereotypes can become self-fulfilling prophecies.

Technology, school infrastructure, and teacher quality also significantly impact success. A meta-analysis by Engin Karadağ et al. concludes that school and teacher factors have the biggest impact. Parent involvement boosts achievement and self-esteem.

### Education Studies

Education studies is the main discipline investigating education, integrating insights from philosophy, psychology, sociology, and economics. It uses quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods research. Subfields include the philosophy of education (examining assumptions and aims), pedagogy (teaching methods), the psychology of education (how learning occurs), the sociology of education (socialization and inequality), the economics of education (resource allocation), comparative education (contrasting systems), and the history of education.

A central topic concerns the aims of education, which range from acquiring knowledge and fostering critical thinking to promoting obedience or piety. Educational ideologies are systems of assumptions that interpret and evaluate practices, ranging from teacher-centered to student-centered and conservative to progressive. Influential learning theories include behaviorism (learning as a change in behavior), cognitivism (change in cognitive structures), and constructivism (learning based on personal experience). Jean Piaget outlined four stages of child development: sensorimotor, pre-operational, concrete operational, and formal operational. Teaching methods vary by subject and learner, and include the use of instructional media and assessment.

### History

In prehistory, education was informal, occurring through enculturation, storytelling, and imitation. The invention of writing in the 4th millennium BCE allowed for storing information and the rise of formal education, which was restricted to intellectual elites. Ancient achievements include Plato's Academy and the Library of Alexandria. In the medieval era, education was shaped by religion, including the Catholic Church in Europe, madrasas in the Islamic world, and yeshivas in Jewish communities. The first European universities were founded, and guilds provided vocational education.

In the early modern period, the Renaissance and Enlightenment shifted education toward secularism and reason. The printing press increased literacy. Public education rose in the 18th and 19th centuries, making schooling free and compulsory. In the contemporary era, intergovernmental organizations promoted universal access, leading to a steady rise in primary education attendance. Standardized curricula and tests were introduced, and emerging technologies like the internet enabled new forms of online education, which became crucial during the COVID-19 pandemic.

## Terms

- **Formal education**: Structured learning within a complex institutional framework, like public schools, usually compulsory up to a certain age.
- **Non-formal education**: Organized and purposeful learning that occurs outside the formal schooling system, such as tutoring or fitness classes.
- **Informal education**: Unstructured, spontaneous learning through daily experiences, with no designated authority figure.
- **Indoctrination**: Instilling beliefs without fostering the rational ability to critically reflect on them, often contrasted with education.
- **Curriculum**: A plan of instruction or program of learning that guides students to achieve educational goals.
- **Pedagogy**: The subfield of education studies interested in teaching methods and practices.
- **Autodidacticism**: Self-education that happens without the guidance of teachers or institutions.
- **Behaviorism**: A learning theory that understands learning as a change in behavior in response to environmental stimuli.
- **Cognitivism**: A learning theory that sees learning as a change in cognitive structures and focuses on mental processes.
- **Constructivism**: A learning theory holding that learning is based on the personal experience of each individual and social interactions.

## Debates and open questions

- The precise definition of education is disputed, including whether it is a process or a mental state, and whether "thick" (improvement-focused) or "thin" (value-neutral) conceptions are correct.
- There is disagreement about the aims of education, such as whether to prioritize liberal values like freedom and critical thinking or qualities like obedience and piety, and whether the primary purpose is to benefit the individual or society.
- The distinction between education and indoctrination is debated, with some arguing that a clear line cannot be drawn because some indoctrination may be necessary in early childhood.
- It is controversial whether the educational systems of developed countries are superior and should be exported to less developed countries.
- The effectiveness of learning styles is criticized for ambiguous empirical evidence, and educational research has been found to have low reproducibility.
