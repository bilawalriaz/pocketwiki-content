# Cerrado

## Overview
The Cerrado is a vast tropical savanna ecoregion in central Brazil, covering 21% of the country's land area. It is the world's most biodiverse savanna, hosting thousands of endemic species, and plays a critical hydrological role by supplying water to major South American river basins. However, its conversion for agriculture and cattle ranching, driven by soil amendments and subsidies, has made it one of the most threatened biomes, with only a small fraction under effective protection.

## Timeline
- **Cretaceous period** — Cerrado vegetation is believed to have ancient origins, possibly predating the separation of Africa and South America.
- **1892** — Danish botanist Eugenius Warming provides the first detailed European account of the Cerrado in his book *Lagoa Santa*.
- **1950** — The Paraobepa National Forest, the first protected area in the Brazilian Cerrado, is established.
- **1961** — Chapada dos Veadeiros and Emas National Parks are established.
- **Mid-1960s** — Agricultural activities in the Cerrado begin to expand significantly after the transfer of Brazil's capital to Brasília.
- **1970-2010** — The population in the Cerrado region more than doubles, from 35.8 to 76 million.
- **Late 1990s-early 2000s** — A boom in new protected areas occurs, coinciding with the passage of Brazil's National System of Nature Conservation Units (SNUC) law in 2000.
- **2001** — Chapada dos Veadeiros and Emas National Parks are jointly designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site as the "Cerrado Protected Areas."
- **2012** — Changes to Brazil's Forest Code legally allow for the clearing of almost 40 million hectares of additional native vegetation in the Cerrado.
- **2019-2023** — Studies show approximately 19% of the Cerrado exhibits significant woody plant encroachment, leading to reduced plant diversity.

## Body

### Climate and Geography
The Cerrado has a semi-humid tropical climate with two dominant seasons: wet and dry. Annual temperatures average between 22 and 27 °C, with precipitation between 80–200 cm. The core areas are the Brazilian Highlands (Planalto). The biome contains the headwaters of major river basins like the Paraná-Paraguay, Araguaia-Tocantins, and São Francisco, as well as upper catchments of Amazon tributaries.

### Flora and Vegetation
The Cerrado is a mosaic of habitats, including forest savanna, wooded savanna, park savanna, and gramineous-woody savanna, interspersed with gallery forests along streams. Vegetation types range from open grasslands (campo sujo) to dense woodlands (cerradão). The region has high plant diversity, with about 800 tree species and a unique herbaceous layer. Fire, soil fertility, and hydrology are key factors shaping the vegetation. The soils are typically well-drained oxisols with low pH and calcium.

### Fauna
The Cerrado supports high vertebrate diversity, including 150 amphibian, 120 reptile, 837 bird, and 161 mammal species. Notable fauna includes the maned wolf, jaguar, giant otter, and hyacinth macaw. Gallery forests are crucial habitats for most mammals. The region also has a high density of leaf-cutter ants and the world's highest diversity of galling insects.

### Human History and Agriculture
Indigenous peoples like the Xavantes and Karajás historically used fire to manage the landscape. Large-scale agriculture began in the mid-1960s after researchers at Embrapa discovered that adding phosphorus and lime could make the soil fertile. This led to a boom in soybean, cattle, and grain production, making the Cerrado a major agricultural hub. However, this expansion, fueled by subsidies and corporate interests, has caused extensive deforestation and habitat loss.

### Conservation Challenges
The Cerrado is highly threatened, with over 37% of its area completely converted for human use and only about 8% effectively protected. Protected areas, managed at federal, state, and municipal levels, cover roughly 9% of the biome but face issues like overlapping jurisdictions and a lack of management plans. Private lands hold the majority of remaining native vegetation, but compliance with the Forest Code is inconsistent. Indigenous lands are also vital for conservation, preserving more native vegetation than some protected areas.

## Terms
- **Ecoregion:** A large area of land or water that contains a distinct assemblage of natural communities sharing a common geography, geology, and climate.
- **Endemism:** The state of a species being native to and restricted to a certain geographic area.
- **Gallery forest:** A narrow strip of closed-canopy forest that grows along streams or rivers in otherwise open savanna or grassland.
- **Oxisols:** A type of highly weathered, acidic tropical soil with low fertility, common in the Cerrado.
- **Legal Reserve:** A portion of a private rural property in Brazil that must be maintained with native vegetation under the Forest Code.
- **Conservation unit:** A protected area in Brazil, managed under the National System of Nature Conservation Units (SNUC).
- **Integral Protection (IP):** A category of Brazilian conservation unit where nature is protected and resource use is limited to recreation and tourism.
- **Sustainable Protection (SP):** A category of Brazilian conservation unit that aims to reconcile nature conservation with sustainable resource use.
- **Woody plant encroachment:** An increase in the density of woody plants (trees and shrubs) in a grassland or savanna ecosystem, often reducing plant diversity.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Effectiveness of protection:** There is debate over whether the current network of protected areas, with its small coverage and many sustainable-use units, is sufficient to conserve the Cerrado's biodiversity and ecosystem services.
- **Role of private land:** Given that most remaining native vegetation is on private property, the effectiveness of the Forest Code and incentive programs like the Low-Carbon Agriculture (ABC) program in ensuring conservation is a critical question.
- **Agricultural sustainability:** The long-term viability of the Cerrado's agricultural model is questioned, as deforestation can reduce regional rainfall and make farming less productive.
- **Conservation priorities:** The floristic heterogeneity of the Cerrado complicates the selection of representative areas for conservation, leading to ongoing collaborative efforts to identify biodiversity hotspots.
