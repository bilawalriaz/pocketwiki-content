# Seonjo of Joseon

## Overview
King Seonjo (r. 1567–1608) was the 14th monarch of the Joseon dynasty. His reign began with promising Confucian reforms but is primarily remembered for the devastating Imjin War (1592–1598) against Japan, which he failed to prevent due to political factionalism and poor leadership, leaving the kingdom devastated.

## Timeline
- **1552** — Born as Yi Yeon in Hanseong (Seoul).
- **1567** — Crowned king at age 16 after the death of his uncle, King Myeongjong.
- **1567–1575** — Early reign: promotes Confucian scholars and reforms the civil service exam.
- **c. 1575** — Political factions emerge: Westerners vs. Easterners.
- **1589** — Rebellion of Chŏng Yŏrip leads to a purge of over 1,000 scholars.
- **1591** — Toyotomi Hideyoshi requests passage through Korea to invade China.
- **April 13, 1592** — Japanese forces under Konishi Yukinaga invade Korea, beginning the Imjin War.
- **1592** — King Seonjo flees Seoul, first to Pyongyang, then to Uiju on the Chinese border.
- **1597** — Japanese forces invade Korea for a second time.
- **1598** — Death of Toyotomi Hideyoshi; Japanese forces withdraw from Korea.
- **1608** — King Seonjo dies; succeeded by his son, Gwanghaegun.

## Body

### Early Reign and Reforms
Seonjo ascended the throne as a teenager, aiming to restore stability after past corruption. He promoted the Sarim scholar-officials who had been persecuted in earlier purges and appointed renowned Confucian scholars like Yi Hwang and Yi I to office. He reformed the civil service examination to emphasize politics and history over literature, restoring the reputations of previously disgraced scholars. These actions initially earned him respect and brought a period of peace.

### Political Factionalism
The court split into two major factions: the conservative Westerners, led by Shim Eui-gyeom, and the reformist Easterners, led by Kim Hyowŏn. This East-West feud paralyzed governance. The Easterners later divided into Northern and Southern factions, further complicating politics. Factional infighting directly undermined national defense; when conflicting reports arrived from Japan in the early 1590s, the dominant Eastern faction dismissed warnings of invasion, leaving the military unprepared.

### The Imjin War (1592–1598)
In 1592, Japan invaded with a massive force. The unprepared Joseon army suffered rapid defeats, and King Seonjo fled the capital, which was plundered and burned. The Korean navy, led by Admiral Yi Sun-sin, achieved crucial victories that disrupted Japanese supply lines. Ming Chinese forces arrived to aid Joseon, and after fierce battles, a stalemate led to failed peace negotiations. Japan invaded again in 1597 but was ultimately thwarted by combined Korean and Ming resistance. The war ended with the Japanese withdrawal following Hideyoshi's death in 1598.

### Aftermath and Later Reign
The war left Korea devastated and its people starving. Seonjo attempted reconstruction through army training and tax reforms, but political turmoil and famine persisted. A succession dispute arose between his son Gwanghaegun and a newborn son, Grand Prince Yeongchang. Seonjo died in 1608, having failed to rebuild the nation he had led into catastrophe.

## Terms
- **Joseon**: The Korean dynasty that ruled from 1392 to 1897.
- **Sarim**: A group of Confucian scholar-officials who advocated for moral governance and reform.
- **Literati purges**: A series of political purges in the 15th and 16th centuries where rival scholar factions were persecuted.
- **Westerners/Easterners**: The two primary political factions that emerged in the Joseon court during Seonjo's reign.
- **Imjin War**: The Japanese invasions of Korea (1592–1598), named for the Imjin year in the Korean calendar.
- **Hangul**: The Korean alphabet, created in the 15th century. Seonjo used it in a public edict during the war.
- **Daewongun**: A title meaning "Great Prince of the Court," held by Seonjo's father.
- **Ming**: The Chinese dynasty that was Joseon's suzerain and ally during the Imjin War.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which Seonjo's personal indecisiveness and flight from the capital directly contributed to the kingdom's collapse versus the pre-existing weakness caused by factionalism.
- The accuracy of the conflicting intelligence reports from Japan (from Hwang Yun-gil and Kim Sŏngil) and whether the court's dismissal of warnings was solely due to factional bias.
- The long-term impact of the East-West factional system, which lasted 200 years and is cited as a contributing factor to the dynasty's eventual decline.
