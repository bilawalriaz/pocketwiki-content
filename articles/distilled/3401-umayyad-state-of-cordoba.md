# Umayyad state of Córdoba

## Overview
The Emirate and Caliphate of Córdoba was an Islamic state ruled by the Umayyad dynasty in al-Andalus (the Iberian Peninsula) from 756 to 1031. It was a major political, economic, and cultural power in medieval Europe, known for its urban sophistication, religious tolerance, and architectural achievements. Its collapse into smaller kingdoms (taifas) marked a turning point in the history of the peninsula.

## Timeline
- **711** — Umayyad conquest of the Visigothic Kingdom in Iberia begins.
- **756** — Abd al-Rahman I establishes an independent Umayyad Emirate in Córdoba.
- **929** — Abd al-Rahman III proclaims himself Caliph, establishing the Caliphate of Córdoba.
- **936–940** — Construction begins on the palatine city of Madinat al-Zahra.
- **976** — Death of Caliph Al-Hakam II; Almanzor becomes the effective ruler.
- **1002** — Death of Almanzor, leading to internal power struggles.
- **1009** — Deposition of Abd al-Rahman Sanchuelo triggers the Fitna of al-Andalus (civil war).
- **1031** — The Caliphate collapses, fracturing into independent taifa kingdoms.

## Body

### Political History
The state was founded by Abd al-Rahman I, an Umayyad prince who fled the Abbasid revolution in the Levant. He united local fiefdoms into an independent emirate, though rulers initially used titles like *emir* and nominally recognized Abbasid suzerainty. The realm expanded through raids and diplomacy, with its control over frontier regions often dependent on the individual ruler's strength.

Abd al-Rahman III proclaimed himself caliph in 929 to challenge the rival Fatimid Caliphate's religious authority. This period saw increased diplomatic ties, military campaigns in North Africa, and significant state revenue. His successor, Al-Hakam II, presided over a cultural peak but relied more on advisers.

The caliphate's power was later usurped by the *hajib* (court official) Almanzor, who made the young caliph Hisham II a figurehead. Almanzor professionalized the army, relying heavily on Berber and *saqaliba* (Slavic slave) troops, and launched frequent campaigns against Christian kingdoms. After his death, his sons' struggle for control led to civil war (the Fitna of al-Andalus) and the caliphate's disintegration into taifas by 1031.

### Society and Demographics
The population was a mix of Arabs, Berbers, indigenous converts to Islam (*Muwallads*), Christians, and Jews. Arabs formed the ruling elite, while *Muwallads* became the majority. The capital, Córdoba, was one of Europe's largest cities, with an estimated population of around 450,000 by 1000 CE.

Arabic spread primarily through conversion to Islam, though the process was gradual. By 961, an estimated 50% of the population had converted. A vernacular, Andalusi Arabic, coexisted with Andalusi Romance, which was spoken by lower classes and Arabized Christians (*Mozarabs*). Jews, previously persecuted under the Visigoths, enjoyed improved status and participated in court and trade.

### Religion and Law
The state promoted the Maliki school of Islamic jurisprudence. Christians and Jews were recognized as *dhimmis* (protected non-Muslims) who paid the *jizya* tax. The local Christian Church was partially integrated with the Umayyad administration, though its authority weakened. Conversion to Islam was driven by social and economic incentives, accelerating in the 10th century.

### Economy
The economy was monetized, with gold and silver coinage minted from the 10th century, fueled by trans-Saharan trade. State revenue came from trade, tributes from Christian kingdoms, and agriculture. An agricultural revolution introduced new crops (e.g., rice, oranges) and irrigation techniques like *norias* (waterwheels), boosting output and population growth. Jewish merchants played a key role in Mediterranean trade networks.

### Culture and Architecture
Córdoba was a center of learning, with translations of Greek texts and a vast royal library. The court patronized poets, historians, and scientists. Arts flourished, including ivory carving, silk production (*tiraz*), and metalwork.

Architecturally, the Great Mosque of Córdoba, founded in 785, is iconic for its hypostyle hall with double-tiered arches. The caliphal period saw the construction of Madinat al-Zahra, a lavish palace-city, and expansions to the mosque featuring ribbed domes and a gold-mosaic *mihrab* (prayer niche). These styles influenced later Moorish architecture.

## Terms
- **Emirate/Caliphate:** An Islamic state ruled by an *emir* (prince) or *caliph* (successor to the Prophet Muhammad).
- **Umayyad:** The dynasty that ruled the first Islamic caliphate in Damascus and later the independent emirate/caliphate in Córdoba.
- **Al-Andalus:** The Arabic name for the Iberian Peninsula under Muslim rule.
- **Muwallad:** An indigenous Iberian who converted to Islam.
- **Mozarab:** A Christian living under Muslim rule who adopted Arabic language and customs.
- **Dhimmi:** A non-Muslim citizen of an Islamic state who paid the *jizya* tax in exchange for protection.
- **Saqliba (singular: Saqlabi):** Slavic slaves, often used as soldiers or in court service.
- **Hajib:** A high-ranking court official, like a chamberlain or vizier.
- **Fitna:** A civil war or period of internal strife within the Muslim community.
- **Taifa:** An independent Muslim-ruled kingdom that emerged after the collapse of the Caliphate of Córdoba.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Population Estimates:** Figures for the caliphate's population vary widely, from 3 million to over 10 million, based on different methodologies and interpretations of historical sources.
- **Army Size:** Contemporary accounts of Almanzor's army size are contradictory, with estimates ranging from 12,000 to 600,000 troops. Modern scholars suggest a more realistic figure of 35,000–90,000.
- **Rate of Conversion:** The pace of Islamization is modeled but not precisely known. Richard Bulliet's model suggests 50% conversion by 950 CE, but this is an estimate.
- **Legitimacy:** The Caliphate of Córdoba's claim to the title of caliph was not accepted outside al-Andalus and its North African affiliates, challenging its universal Islamic authority.
