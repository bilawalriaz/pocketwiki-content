# Afghan conflict

## Overview
The Afghan conflict is a series of interconnected wars and insurgencies that have kept Afghanistan in a near-continuous state of armed conflict since the 1970s. It began with the end of a long period of monarchical stability and evolved through a communist coup, a Soviet invasion, a multi-sided civil war, the rise and fall of the Taliban, a U.S.-led invasion, and the Taliban's return to power. The conflict has been defined by foreign interventions, proxy wars, and internal power struggles, resulting in massive humanitarian crises and shaping regional geopolitics.

## Timeline
- **1973** — 1973 Afghan coup d'état overthrows King Mohammad Zahir Shah, ending the monarchy.
- **1978** — Saur Revolution: PDPA overthrows President Daoud Khan, establishing the Democratic Republic of Afghanistan.
- **1979** — Soviet–Afghan War begins with a large-scale Soviet military intervention.
- **1989** — Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan is completed.
- **1992** — Fall of the PDPA government; Peshawar Accord creates the Islamic State of Afghanistan, triggering civil war.
- **1996** — Taliban seizes Kabul, establishing the first Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan.
- **2001** — U.S.-led invasion following 9/11; Taliban government is toppled.
- **2020** — U.S.-Taliban Doha Agreement is signed, paving the way for U.S. withdrawal.
- **2021** — Taliban offensive leads to the collapse of the Islamic Republic and re-establishment of the Islamic Emirate.

## Body

### Prelude and the PDPA Era
The conflict's roots lie in the end of Afghanistan's relative peace under King Zahir Shah, who was overthrown in a 1973 coup by his cousin, Mohammad Daoud Khan. Khan's Republic of Afghanistan initially relied on the PDPA but later tried to curb its influence. This led to the violent 1978 Saur Revolution, where the PDPA seized power. The new government's radical reforms, such as land reform and women's equality, provoked fierce rural opposition. Internal PDPA power struggles, marked by mass executions under leader Hafizullah Amin, further destabilized the country.

### Soviet–Afghan War and its Aftermath
The PDPA's inability to control the insurgency prompted a Soviet invasion in December 1979. The Soviets installed a more moderate PDPA faction but faced a protracted war against the Afghan mujahideen, who were backed by the U.S., Pakistan, and Saudi Arabia. After a decade of stalemate, Soviet forces withdrew in 1989. The PDPA government, now under Mohammad Najibullah, initially survived but collapsed in 1992 following the Soviet Union's dissolution and internal defections, ending communist rule.

### Civil War and the Rise of the Taliban
The 1992 Peshawar Accord, meant to create a power-sharing government, failed, leading to a multi-sided civil war in Kabul. Pakistan backed Gulbuddin Hekmatyar's faction, while Saudi Arabia and Iran supported rival militias. Amid this chaos, the Taliban emerged in 1994 from religious schools in Pakistan. With Pakistani support, they captured Kabul in 1996, imposing a harsh interpretation of Islam. The anti-Taliban Northern Alliance, led by Ahmad Shah Massoud, controlled about 30% of the country and resisted until 2001.

### U.S. Invasion and the Islamic Republic
Following the 9/11 attacks by al-Qaeda, which was based in Taliban-controlled Afghanistan, the U.S. invaded in October 2001. The Taliban regime was quickly toppled, and a transitional authority led by Hamid Karzai was established. A new Islamic Republic of Afghanistan was formed via a 2004 constitution. Despite international reconstruction efforts and a NATO-led security mission, a resilient Taliban insurgency, supported by Pakistan's ISI, persisted. Negotiations, including the 2020 Doha Agreement between the U.S. and the Taliban, failed to bring peace.

### Taliban Return and Ongoing Conflict
Following the U.S. withdrawal in 2021, a rapid Taliban offensive led to the collapse of the Islamic Republic and the re-establishment of the Islamic Emirate. Resistance emerged, notably the National Resistance Front in Panjshir, but was largely suppressed. As of 2026, the internationally recognized government is the collapsed Islamic Republic, while the Taliban controls the country. Clashes continue with anti-Taliban groups and across borders with Pakistan, which accuses the Taliban of harboring militants.

## Terms
- **Mujahideen**: Islamic holy warriors; in this context, the various Afghan insurgent groups fighting against the PDPA government and later the Soviet forces.
- **PDPA (People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan)**: Afghanistan's communist party, which ruled the country from 1978 to 1992.
- **Northern Alliance**: A military-political alliance of anti-Taliban factions, officially the United Front, that resisted Taliban rule from 1996 to 2001.
- **Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan**: The theocratic government established by the Taliban, first from 1996 to 2001 and again from 2021 to the present.
- **Islamic Republic of Afghanistan**: The democratic government established in 2004 following the U.S. invasion, which was overthrown in 2021.
- **Loya Jirga**: A traditional Afghan grand assembly of leaders convened to decide on important national issues, such as adopting a constitution.
- **Inter-Services Intelligence (ISI)**: Pakistan's intelligence agency, which provided critical support to the Taliban and other mujahideen factions.
- **Doha Agreement**: A 2020 peace deal between the U.S. and the Taliban that stipulated a full U.S. withdrawal in exchange for Taliban counter-terrorism commitments.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent of Pakistan's ISI role in creating and sustaining the Taliban remains a central point of analysis, with sources describing it as providing "financial, logistical and military support."
- The causes of the rapid collapse of the Islamic Republic in 2021 are debated, with factors including the U.S. withdrawal, corruption, and the Taliban's military strategy.
- The future of international recognition for the Taliban government is uncertain, as the internationally recognized government is the collapsed Islamic Republic.
- The potential for a full-scale civil war involving multiple anti-Taliban resistance groups remains an open concern.
