# Human geography

## Overview
Human geography is the branch of geography that studies the spatial relationships between human communities, cultures, economies, and their environments. It uses qualitative and quantitative methods to understand how social interactions connect with the environment, drawing from disciplines like sociology and economics to explain how human activity shapes the spaces we inhabit.

## Timeline
- **1830** — Royal Geographical Society founded in England.
- **1854** — John Snow's cholera map provides an early example of health geography.
- **1883** — First professor of geography appointed in the United Kingdom.
- **1888** — National Geographic Society founded in the United States.
- **1904** — Association of American Geographers founded (renamed 2016).
- **1922** — Halford John Mackinder appointed professor of geography at the London School of Economics.
- **1960s** — The quantitative revolution applies statistical models to geography, critiquing regional geography's descriptive nature.
- **1970s-80s** — Rise of critical geography, including radical geography influenced by Marxism and humanistic geography.

## Body

### Historical Development
The institutional foundations of modern geography were laid in the 19th century with societies like the Royal Geographical Society (1830) and the National Geographic Society (1888). Early theoretical frameworks included environmental determinism, which posited that the natural environment directly shapes human habits, but this was criticized for lacking rigor and justifying racism. Regional geography later sought to describe unique regions holistically. The mid-20th century quantitative revolution introduced statistical and mathematical modeling, leading to modern tools like geographic information systems (GIS). From the 1970s, critical geography emerged, with radical geography (influenced by Marxism) and humanistic geography (emphasizing qualitative methods) challenging positivist approaches.

### Core Fields of Study
Human geography is divided into numerous subfields. **Cultural geography** studies the spatial variation of cultural phenomena like language and religion. **Economic geography** examines relationships between economic systems and the environment. **Political geography** analyzes the spatial outcomes of political processes. **Population geography** focuses on the distribution and migration of populations. **Urban geography** studies cities, considering their site, situation, and internal organization. Other key fields include **health geography** (applying geography to disease and healthcare), **historical geography** (studying past geographies), and **settlement geography** (examining urban and rural areas).

### Contemporary Approaches
Within each subfield, various philosophical approaches are applied. For example, a researcher might use feminist, Marxist, or postmodern theories to analyze urban spaces. This theoretical diversity allows human geography to address complex issues like inequality, identity, and environmental change from multiple perspectives, integrating insights from the discipline's historical evolution.

## Terms
- **Human geography:** The study of spatial relationships between human communities, cultures, economies, and their environments.
- **Environmental determinism:** The theory that people's habits are directly caused by their natural environment.
- **Quantitative revolution:** A mid-20th century shift in geography emphasizing statistical and mathematical models.
- **Critical geography:** A broad term for critiques of positivism in geography, including radical and humanistic approaches.
- **Radical geography:** A critical approach, often using Marxist theory, that seeks to explain social problems and propose solutions.
- **Humanistic geography:** An approach advocating qualitative methods to understand human experiences of place.
- **GIS (Geographic Information Systems):** Tools for capturing, storing, and analyzing spatial data, developed from quantitative methods.
- **Positivism:** A philosophical system that holds that knowledge should be based on observable, measurable facts and logical laws.

## Debates and Open questions
The source highlights ongoing debates, such as the critique of environmental determinism for its lack of rigor and use to justify racism. The quantitative revolution sparked debate over the scientific rigor of descriptive regional geography. Later, radical geographers critiqued the objectivity of positivism as a tool of capital, advocating for politically engaged research. These shifts reflect continuous debate over methodology, theory, and the discipline's purpose.
