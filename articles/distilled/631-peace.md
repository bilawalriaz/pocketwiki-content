# Peace

## Overview  
Peace denotes a state of harmony marked by the absence of hostility and violence, whether between individuals, groups, or nations. It is a central ideal across philosophies, religions, and ideologies, yet lacks a single universal definition, being shaped by cultural, historical, and contextual factors. The pursuit of peace has taken many forms—from ancient diplomacy and religious teachings to modern international institutions and peacekeeping efforts—reflecting humanity’s enduring struggle to reconcile security, justice, and human flourishing.

## Body  

### Etymology and Meaning  
The word “peace” derives from Anglo-French *pes* and Latin *pax*, meaning “compact” or “agreement.” In English, it entered use around 1300 as a translation of the Hebrew *shalom*, which encompasses not only peace but also justice, health, prosperity, and safety. Used as both a greeting and farewell across cultures (e.g., Arabic *salaam*, Hawaiian *aloha*), “peace” reflects a broad spectrum of meanings—from inner tranquility to societal harmony. Psychologically, inner peace—achieved through meditation, prayer, or self-reflection—is often seen as a precursor to behavioral peace, rooted in virtues like compassion, patience, and self-control.

### Historical Diplomacy and Conflict  
Peace has historically been forged through diplomacy, such as the 8th–7th century BCE marriages of Greek princesses to rulers of Phrygia and Lydia, which facilitated cultural exchange and technological diffusion like the Greek alphabet and coinage. However, peace has also been imposed by victors through coercion, as critiqued by Tacitus, who noted that Rome called its destructive conquests “peace.” Two forms of societal peace are recognized: mere absence of war, and a deeper form requiring justice, mutual respect, and good governance.

### Religious Traditions  
Religions have long promoted peace through doctrine and practice. In Christianity, Jesus is hailed as the “Prince of Peace,” and figures like St. Francis of Assisi advocated nonresistance. Hindu texts call for universal harmony, while Buddhism teaches that peace arises from ending suffering through the Four Noble Truths. Islam emphasizes *salam* (peace) as foundational, and Judaism links peace (*shalom*) to divine presence and the messianic age of unity. Religious art and symbolism often depict peace as a goddess—Eirene in Greece, Pax in Rome—holding symbols of abundance and authority.

### Ideologies and Movements  
Pacifism, in its absolute or pragmatic forms, rejects violence as a tool of conflict resolution. Gandhi’s *satyagraha*—truth-force or soul-force—became a model for nonviolent resistance, influencing leaders like Martin Luther King Jr. The non-aggression principle, upheld by libertarians and anarchists, condemns initiations of force. Inner peace, cultivated through meditation or prayer, is emphasized in Buddhist, Hindu, and contemplative Christian traditions as essential to outer harmony.

### Institutions and Organizations  
The League of Nations, formed in 1919, was the first major attempt at collective security but failed to prevent WWII. Its successor, the United Nations (1945), aims to maintain global peace through diplomacy, law, and peacekeeping missions. UN peacekeepers, known as “Blue Helmets,” enforce ceasefires and protect civilians, earning the Nobel Peace Prize in 1988. Other initiatives include the Nobel Peace Prize (established 1901), the Gandhi Peace Prize (1995), and educational scholarships like Rhodes and Fulbright, designed to foster cross-cultural understanding.

### Theories of Peace  
Multiple theories seek to explain how peace can be sustained. The balance of power, favored by realists, argues that no single state should dominate others. Democratic peace theory suggests democracies rarely fight each other. Free trade and economic interdependence are believed to reduce conflict, though exceptions exist. Territorial peace theory posits that settled borders promote democratic stability. Game theory models, like the “peace war game,” explore cooperation versus aggression dynamics. Socialist critiques argue capitalism fuels imperial rivalry, while Keynes advocated managed capitalism for stability. International law and organizations aim to replace state anarchy with structured cooperation.

### Measurement and Long Peace Periods  
Efforts to quantify peace include the Global Peace Index and Fragile States Index, ranking countries by safety, stability, and institutional strength. Notable long periods of peace include Pax Britannica (1815–1914), Pax Americana (post-WWII), and national neutrality in Sweden (since 1814) and Switzerland (since 1815). Historical examples include Japan’s Edo period (1603–1868) and the Roman Pax Romana.

## Terms  
- **Shalom**: Hebrew term meaning peace, completeness, and well-being; used as a greeting.  
- **Satyagraha**: Gandhi’s philosophy of nonviolent resistance based on truth-force.  
- **Blue Helmets**: UN peacekeeping forces deployed to conflict zones.  
- **Pax Romana**: Period of relative peace in the Roman Empire (c. 190 years).  
- **Pacifism**: Opposition to war or violence as means of resolving disputes.  
- **Non-aggression principle**: Ethical stance opposing initiation of force or fraud.  
- **Peacekeeping**: UN operations enforcing ceasefires and protecting civilians post-conflict.  
- **Democratic peace theory**: Hypothesis that democracies do not wage war against one another.  
- **Balance of power**: System where no single state dominates, promoting stability.  
- **Inner peace**: Mental and spiritual calm achieved through reflection or discipline.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Scholars debate whether peace is best achieved through strength (balance of power, deterrence) or moral persuasion (nonviolence, international law). The effectiveness of appeasement versus deterrence remains contested, especially regarding nuclear proliferation. Critics question whether economic interdependence truly prevents war, given historical counterexamples like World War I. The role of democracy in fostering peace is debated, as is the tension between state sovereignty and global governance. Additionally, the definition of peace itself—whether merely the absence of war or requiring justice and equity—remains unresolved.
