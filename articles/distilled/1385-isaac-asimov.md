# Isaac Asimov

## Overview
Isaac Asimov (c. January 2, 1920 – April 6, 1992) was an American writer and professor of biochemistry at Boston University, widely considered one of the "Big Three" science fiction authors alongside Robert A. Heinlein and Arthur C. Clarke. He authored or edited over 500 books spanning nine of ten Dewey Decimal categories, making him one of history’s most prolific and versatile writers. Asimov is best known for the *Foundation* series, which depicts the fall of a galactic empire and the science of **psychohistory** (a fictional statistical method for predicting mass human behavior), and the *Robot* series, which introduced the **Three Laws of Robotics** and the term **robotics** itself. His popular science works, written in a clear, historical style, earned him the reputation of a "Great Explainer." Asimov’s influence extends beyond literature; his concepts of robotics and psychohistory have shaped real-world fields like AI ethics and economics.

## Timeline
- **c. Jan 2, 1920** — Born in Petrovichi, Russian SFSR; family emigrates to U.S. in 1923.
- **1939** — First professional story sale, "Marooned Off Vesta," to *Amazing Stories*.
- **1941** — Publishes "Nightfall" (voted best SF short story ever, 1964/1968) and "Liar!" (coins "robotics").
- **1942–1944** — Publishes first *Foundation* stories in *Astounding Science Fiction*.
- **1948** — Earns Ph.D. in chemistry from Columbia University.
- **1950** — Publishes *I, Robot* (fix-up) and *Pebble in the Sky* (first novel).
- **1951–1953** — *Foundation* trilogy published by Gnome Press.
- **1957** — *Sputnik* launch triggers shift from fiction to popular science writing.
- **1958** — Begins monthly science column in *The Magazine of Fantasy & Science Fiction* (runs 399 issues).
- **1966** — *Foundation* trilogy wins Hugo Award for "Best All-Time Series."
- **1972** — *The Gods Themselves* wins Hugo, Nebula, and Locus Awards for Best Novel.
- **1982–1986** — Returns to *Foundation* with *Foundation's Edge* and *Foundation and Earth*, unifying Robot/Empire/Foundation universes.
- **1983** — Contracts HIV from blood transfusion during triple bypass surgery (kept secret until 2002).
- **Apr 6, 1992** — Dies in Manhattan of heart and kidney failure.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Asimov was born in Petrovichi, Russian SFSR, between October 1919 and January 1920. His parents, Russian Jews Anna and Judah Asimov, immigrated to the U.S. in 1923, settling in Brooklyn. Asimov taught himself to read at five, devouring pulp magazines from his parents' candy stores—a major influence on his career. He graduated high school at 15, attended Seth Low Junior College (a Columbia branch with ethnic quotas), and earned a B.S. (1939), M.A. (1941), and Ph.D. in chemistry (1948) from Columbia. During WWII (1942–1945), he worked as a civilian chemist at the Philadelphia Navy Yard alongside L. Sprague de Camp and Robert Heinlein. He served briefly in the Army (1945–1946) before accepting an instructorship in biochemistry at Boston University School of Medicine (1949). He was promoted to associate professor (1955), dismissed from teaching for lack of research (1958), but retained his title and was later promoted to full professor (1979).

### Science Fiction Career: The "Future History"
Asimov’s fiction career had two distinct phases. The early phase (1939–1958) produced the *Foundation* trilogy, the *Robot* stories (collected in *I, Robot*), and the *Galactic Empire* novels. The *Foundation* series introduced **psychohistory**, a mathematical sociology predicting the statistical behavior of masses. The *Robot* stories established the **Three Laws of Robotics**—hard-coded ethical constraints preventing robots from harming humans—and coined the term **robotics**. The *Galactic Empire* novels were set earlier in the same timeline.

After a 24-year hiatus writing mostly nonfiction, Asimov returned to fiction in 1982 with *Foundation's Edge*. He subsequently wrote sequels (*Foundation and Earth*) and prequels (*Prelude to Foundation*, *Forward the Foundation*) that explicitly linked the Robot, Empire, and Foundation universes into a single "future history," though the source notes "many inconsistencies in this unification." He also wrote the *Lucky Starr* juveniles under the pseudonym Paul French and the standalone novel *The Gods Themselves* (1972), which featured aliens and alien sex—subjects he previously avoided due to editor John W. Campbell’s preference for human superiority.

### Popular Science and Nonfiction
The 1957 launch of *Sputnik I* caused a U.S. "science gap" panic, prompting publishers to seek popular science. Asimov pivoted, writing only four SF novels between 1958 and 1982 but over 120 nonfiction books. His monthly column in *The Magazine of Fantasy & Science Fiction* (1958–1992) became his primary venue, collected into dozens of volumes (e.g., *The Intelligent Man's Guide to Science*, *Understanding Physics*). He explained concepts historically, tracing them to their simplest origins. His range covered chemistry, astronomy, mathematics, history, Shakespeare, and the Bible (*Asimov's Guide to the Bible*). He coined the term **spome** (a self-sustaining system) and proposed the **World Season Calendar** reform (1973). UNESCO ranks him the 24th-most-translated author globally.

### Writing Style and Method
Asimov wrote in an "informal," transparent style using short words and simple sentences, prioritizing clarity over literary ornamentation. He composed at 90 words per minute on a typewriter, imagining the ending first, then the beginning, letting the middle "work itself out." He refused major revisions ("like chewing used gum"), preferring to write a new piece. Critics like James Gunn noted his fiction plays on a "bare stage" with functional dialogue and minimal description, driven by exposition. However, Gunn acknowledged passages in *The Caves of Steel* reminiscent of Proust. Asimov often used complex, non-chronological narrative structures (e.g., *The Gods Themselves* starts at Chapter 6), which some readers found detrimental to clarity.

### Personal Life and Controversies
Asimov married Gertrude Blugerman in 1942; they had two children, separated in 1970, and divorced in 1973. He married psychiatrist and writer Janet O. Jeppson two weeks later. He was a claustrophile (loving small spaces), a teetotaler, afraid of flying (traveling by cruise ship later in life), and never learned to swim or ride a bicycle. He was active in humanist, skeptical (CSICOP), and literary (Baker Street Irregulars, Trap Door Spiders) organizations.

**Sexual Assault Allegations:** The source documents that Asimov frequently fondled, kissed, and pinched women at conventions without consent. Biographer Alec Nevala-Lee characterizes many encounters as "clearly non-consensual," noting Asimov defended himself by claiming women "cooperated." Contemporary editors and authors (Judith Merril, Harlan Ellison, Frederik Pohl, Edward L. Ferman) corroborate a pattern of harassment that contributed to a hostile environment for women in the male-dominated SF community. Asimov satirized this behavior in *The Sensuous Dirty Old Man* (1971).

### Illness and Death
Asimov suffered a heart attack in 1977 and underwent triple bypass surgery in December 1983, during which he contracted HIV from a blood transfusion. His status was concealed to protect his family from anti-AIDS prejudice. He died April 6, 1992, of heart and kidney failure; the HIV diagnosis was revealed publicly by his widow Janet in 2002.

## Terms
- ****Psychohistory**** — A fictional science in the *Foundation* series combining history, sociology, and mathematical statistics to predict the future behavior of very large populations (e.g., the Galactic Empire). Asimov later felt "psychosociology" would have been a better term.
- ****Three Laws of Robotics**** — Ethical constraints hardwired into Asimov's fictional robots: (1) A robot may not injure a human or allow harm through inaction; (2) A robot must obey human orders unless they conflict with the First Law; (3) A robot must protect its own existence unless it conflicts with the First or Second Law.
- ****Robotics**** — The technology of designing, constructing, and operating robots. Asimov coined the term in "Liar!" (1941), believing it was the natural analogue to *mechanics* or *hydraulics*; it is now standard technical terminology.
- ****Positronic**** — A fictional adjective describing technology based on positrons (antimatter electrons) rather than electrons, used for robot brains in Asimov's work. Coined in "Liar!" (1941).
- ****Spome**** — Acronym for "Space Object, Matter-Energy," coined by Asimov to describe a self-sustaining, closed ecological system capable of supporting life indefinitely in space.
- ****Fix-up Novel**** — A novel created by editing and connecting previously published short stories (e.g., *Foundation*, *I, Robot*, *The Martian Way*).
- ****Social Science Fiction**** — A term Asimov created to describe 1940s SF (led by him and Heinlein) focusing on speculation about the human condition rather than gadgets or space opera.
- ****World Season Calendar**** — Asimov's 1973 calendar reform proposal dividing the year into four 13-week seasons (A–D) plus a "Year Day," allowing dates like "D-73."
- ****CSICOP**** — Committee for the Scientific Investigation of Claims of the Paranormal (now Committee for Skeptical Inquiry); Asimov was a founding member and key figure lending it authority.
- ****Future History**** — A unified fictional timeline linking previously separate series; Asimov synthesized his Robot, Empire, and Foundation universes in the 1980s.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Unification Inconsistencies:** Asimov’s late-career merging of the Robot, Empire, and Foundation series created a unified "future history," but the source explicitly notes "many inconsistencies in this unification, especially in his earlier stories." Scholars debate whether the synthesis strengthens the narrative or exposes contradictions in his earlier, unplanned worldbuilding.
*   **Literary Merit vs. Clarity:** Critics (e.g., James Gunn, Joseph Patrouch) argue Asimov’s transparent, exposition-heavy style lacks literary depth, characterization, and sensory description. Defenders counter that clarity *is* a distinct style, and that works like *The Caves of Steel* achieve literary quality. The source notes "relatively little literary criticism on Asimov" compared to his output volume.
*   **Sexual Harassment Legacy:** Asimov’s documented pattern of non-consensual groping at conventions—defended by him as "cooperation" and satirized in his writing—raises ongoing debate regarding his personal legacy versus his professional contributions, particularly concerning the historical culture of SF fandom.
*   **HIV Disclosure Timing:** Asimov contracted HIV in 1983 but kept it secret until after his death (revealed 2002) due to fear of prejudice against his family. This raises ethical questions about public health disclosure by public figures during the early AIDS crisis.
*   **Campbell’s Influence on Alien Depictions:** Asimov avoided writing aliens for decades because editor John W. Campbell rejected a story where aliens were superior to humans, which Asimov interpreted as racial bias. This editorial constraint shaped a major absence in his work until *The Gods Themselves* (1972).
