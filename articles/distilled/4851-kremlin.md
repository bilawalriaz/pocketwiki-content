# Kremlin

## Overview
The Moscow Kremlin is a historic fortified complex in the center of Moscow, Russia. It has served as the political and spiritual heart of the Russian state for centuries, evolving from a medieval fortress to the official residence of the President of the Russian Federation. Its architecture and history reflect the major transitions of Russian power, from the Grand Dukes and Tsars to the Soviet government and the modern Russian state.

## Body

### Origins and Medieval Fortress
The site has been inhabited since at least the 2nd century BCE. The Slavic fortified structure, or "grad," was first recorded as the "grad of Moscow" and was expanded in 1156. After destruction by the Mongols in 1237, it was rebuilt in oak. In the 14th century, Dmitri Donskoi replaced the oak palisade with a white limestone citadel, which withstood a major siege. The word "Kremlin" first appeared in records in 1331.

### Residence of the Grand Dukes and Tsars
In the late 15th century, Grand Prince Ivan III initiated a major reconstruction, inviting Italian architects like Pietro Antonio Solari and Marco Ruffo. They designed the current walls, towers, and the Palace of Facets. Subsequent tsars, including Ivan the Terrible, added cathedrals and palaces. The Kremlin was briefly held by Polish forces during the Time of Troubles (1610–1612). After the Romanov dynasty was established, more churches and palaces were built. However, after a fire in 1682, Tsar Peter I grew to dislike the Kremlin and moved the capital to Saint Petersburg in 1703, leading to its neglect.

### Imperial Period and Napoleonic Damage
Though still used for coronations, the Kremlin was largely neglected until Catherine the Great commissioned a new residence in 1773. During the French invasion of 1812, Napoleon's forces occupied the Kremlin and, upon retreating, ordered it blown up, causing significant damage to walls, towers, and the Arsenal. Restoration followed under architect Osip Bove. In the mid-19th century, Tsar Nicholas I demolished older structures to build the current Grand Kremlin Palace (1839–1849) and the Armoury (1851).

### Soviet and Modern Era
After the 1917 Revolution, the Bolshevik government moved to the Kremlin. Lenin and later Stalin resided there, ordering the removal of tsarist symbols like golden eagles, which were replaced by red stars. Many historic churches and monasteries, including the Chudov Monastery and Ascension Convent, were demolished. During World War II, the complex was camouflaged to confuse German pilots. The State Kremlin Palace was built in 1959–1961 for party congresses. The Kremlin Museums were established in 1961, and the complex was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1990. Today, it is the official workplace of the Russian President.

## Terms
- **Kremlin**: A Russian term for a fortified complex or fortress within a city.
- **Metonym**: A figure of speech where a thing or concept is referred to by the name of something closely associated with it (e.g., "The Kremlin" for the Russian government).
- **Kremlinology**: The study of Soviet and Russian politics, historically based on analyzing public information from the Kremlin.
- **Grad**: An old Slavic term for a fortified settlement or town.
- **Palace of Facets**: The oldest secular building in the Kremlin, built in 1491, used for important state ceremonies.
- **Terem Palace**: A 17th-century palace that was the primary home of the tsars' family.
- **Grand Kremlin Palace**: The largest structure in the complex, built in the 19th century, now part of the presidential residence.
- **State Kremlin Palace**: A modern, 1960s arena built within the walls for large meetings and events.
- **Kremlin Wall Necropolis**: A burial site along the Kremlin wall for prominent Soviet figures.

## Debates and Open Questions
The article notes ongoing debate about restoration. The current director of the Kremlin Museums, Elena Gagarina, advocates for a full-scale restoration of the destroyed medieval cloisters and churches. However, recent work has focused on restoring the original interiors of the Grand Kremlin Palace, which were altered during Stalin's rule. This reflects a broader tension between preserving the complex's layered history and restoring specific historical periods.
