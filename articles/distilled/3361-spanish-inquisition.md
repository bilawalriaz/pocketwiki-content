# Spanish Inquisition

## Overview
The Spanish Inquisition was a judicial institution established in 1478 by the Catholic Monarchs, Ferdinand and Isabella, and authorized by Pope Sixtus IV. Its stated purpose was to maintain Catholic orthodoxy, but it became a powerful instrument of state control, primarily targeting Jewish and Muslim converts (*conversos* and *moriscos*) suspected of secretly practicing their former faiths. It prosecuted an estimated 150,000 people, with 3,000–5,000 executed, and played a central role in enforcing religious and social conformity across the Spanish Empire for over 350 years.

## Timeline
- **1478** — Pope Sixtus IV authorizes the Inquisition via the bull *Exigit sincerae devotionis affectus*.
- **1480** — First inquisitors, Miguel de Morillo and Juan de San Martín, are appointed.
- **1481** — First *auto de fe* (public penance ceremony) held in Seville; six people burned.
- **1483** — Tomás de Torquemada is appointed first Inquisitor General.
- **1492** — The Alhambra Decree orders the expulsion of all Jews from Spain.
- **1502** — Muslims in Castile are ordered to convert or leave.
- **1542** — The Inquisition is established in the Viceroyalty of Peru.
- **1569** — Tribunals are established in Lima and Mexico City.
- **1609** — King Philip III orders the expulsion of the Moriscos.
- **1680** — A major *auto de fe* in Madrid, attended by 50,000, sentences 118 people.
- **1813** — The Inquisition is abolished by the liberal Cortes of Cádiz.
- **1834** — The Inquisition is permanently abolished by Royal Decree.

## Body

### Origins and Early Targets
The Inquisition was created to address suspicions of "Crypto-Judaism" among *conversos* (Jews who had converted to Catholicism). Following the 1492 expulsion of Jews, it expanded to target *moriscos* (Muslim converts) and later Protestants, alumbrados (a mystical sect), and others accused of heresy. Its authority grew as it replaced the earlier Papal Inquisition, becoming a tool of the Spanish crown.

### Procedures and Punishments
The Inquisition operated through a system of denunciations, secret trials, and the use of torture to extract confessions. A key public ritual was the *auto de fe*, where sentences were read and the condemned were handed to secular authorities for punishment. Penalties ranged from penance and public flogging to imprisonment, galley servitude, and execution by burning. Property confiscation was a standard practice, funding the institution and the state.

### Expansion and Scope
The Inquisition's jurisdiction extended beyond Spain to its empire, including Southern Italy and the Americas. It prosecuted a wide range of offenses, including heresy, blasphemy, bigamy, sodomy, and witchcraft, though with varying intensity. It also enforced *limpieza de sangre* (blood purity) statutes, which institutionalized racial discrimination against those of Jewish or Muslim descent.

### Decline and Abolition
Enlightenment ideas in the 18th century challenged the Inquisition's authority, reducing its activity. It was abolished during Napoleon's occupation (1808–1812), briefly restored, and finally abolished permanently in 1834. The last execution for heresy occurred in 1826.

## Terms
*   ***Auto de fe***: A public ceremony where the Inquisition's sentences were pronounced and penances were performed.
*   ***Converso***: A Jew who had converted to Christianity, often under duress, and was a primary target of the early Inquisition.
*   ***Morisco***: A Muslim who had converted to Christianity, later targeted for suspected secret practice of Islam.
*   ***Limpieza de sangre***: "Blood purity" statutes that discriminated against those of Jewish or Muslim ancestry.
*   ***Edict of Grace***: A proclamation offering a period of amnesty for self-confession of heresy.
*   ***Calificadores***: Theologians within the Inquisition who determined if a defendant's actions constituted heresy.
*   ***Relaxation to the secular arm***: The formal handing over of a condemned heretic to civil authorities for execution.
*   ***Sanbenito***: A penitential garment worn by those convicted of heresy during an *auto de fe*.

## Debates and Open questions
*   **Scale of Violence**: Historians debate the total number of executions, with estimates ranging from 3,000 to 5,000. The Inquisition's death toll is often compared to secular courts and witch trials in other parts of Europe.
*   **Role of Torture**: The frequency and severity of torture are contested. Revisionist historians argue it was used less often and more cautiously than in secular courts, while others highlight its psychological impact and role in extracting confessions.
*   **Economic Motives**: A major debate centers on whether the Inquisition was primarily a religious institution or a tool for wealth confiscation. The systematic seizure of property from the accused, often the wealthy, suggests a significant financial motive.
*   **Long-Term Impact**: Scholars study the Inquisition's lasting effects on Spanish society, including its alleged role in stifling scientific inquiry, economic development, and social trust.
