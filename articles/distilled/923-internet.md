# Internet

## Overview  
The Internet is the global system of interconnected computer networks using the TCP/IP protocol suite for communication. It emerged from 1960s–70s U.S. Department of Defense research into packet switching and resource sharing, evolving from ARPANET into a decentralized, commercially driven global infrastructure. It supports services like the World Wide Web, email, streaming, and e-commerce, transforming communication, commerce, culture, and governance worldwide.

## Timeline  
- **1969**: ARPANET first connected nodes at UCLA and Stanford.  
- **1974**: Cerf & Kahn published TCP/IP proposal; “internet” used as shorthand for internetwork.  
- **1982**: TCP/IP standardized, enabling global network expansion.  
- **1986**: NSFNet launched, linking U.S. supercomputer centers.  
- **1990**: ARPANET decommissioned; Tim Berners-Lee created the World Wide Web.  
- **1995**: NSFNet decommissioned; Internet fully commercialized in the U.S.  
- **2006**: Internet included on USA Today's list of the New Seven Wonders.  
- **2011**: Global IPv4 address allocation pool exhausted.  
- **2016**: IANA stewardship transition ended U.S. oversight of DNS root zone.  
- **2018**: 80% of world population covered by 4G networks.  
- **2025**: ITU estimates 6 billion (74%) people use the Internet.

## Body  

### Origins and Development  
The Internet originated from Cold War-era research into time-sharing and packet switching. J.C.R. Licklider advocated a universal network; Paul Baran (RAND) and Donald Davies (NPL) independently developed packet-switching concepts. ARPANET, funded by DARPA, began in 1969 connecting UCLA and Stanford. By 1971, 15 nodes were linked. International collaboration began in 1973 with connections to Norway and University College London. The development of TCP/IP by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn in 1974 enabled diverse networks to interconnect, forming a true “network of networks.”

### Governance and Standards  
The Internet lacks centralized governance. The Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), a non-profit body open to all contributors, develops and maintains core protocols via RFC documents. ICANN manages the global IP address space and Domain Name System. Five Regional Internet Registries (RIRs) allocate IP addresses regionally. The Internet Society (ISOC), founded in 1992, supports technical communities including the IETF and IAB. The UN’s Internet Governance Forum (established 2005) facilitates policy dialogue.

### Infrastructure and Architecture  
The Internet comprises routers, cables, wireless links, and data centers. ISPs operate at tiered levels: Tier 1 providers peer directly; lower tiers buy transit. Content delivery networks (CDNs) and colocation centers optimize performance. The Internet Protocol Suite (TCP/IP) structures communication across four layers: application, transport, internet, and link. IPv4 uses 32-bit addresses (≈4.3 billion hosts); IPv6 uses 128-bit addresses to address exhaustion. Subnets divide networks logically using CIDR notation. Routing tables and BGP guide packet forwarding.

### Applications and Services  
The World Wide Web, built by Tim Berners-Lee in 1990, is the dominant service, using HTTP, HTML, and URIs. Browsers like Chrome and Firefox navigate hyperlinked documents. Email remains critical. VoIP enables internet telephony. File sharing uses peer-to-peer or mirror servers. Streaming media (Netflix, YouTube) dominates entertainment. Social media platforms enable global interaction. E-commerce integrates supply chains and consumer markets.

### Social and Cultural Impact  
The Internet reshaped social interaction through forums, instant messaging, and social networks. It enabled remote work, online education, and virtual collaboration. Educational resources span pre-school to post-doctoral levels. Wikis facilitate collective knowledge-building. However, it also introduced cyberbullying, online harassment, and psychological effects like the online disinhibition effect and Internet addiction. Language diversity online reflects ASCII origins but expands via Unicode.

### Economic and Political Dimensions  
E-commerce generated $16 trillion in transactions by 2013. Digital economy reached $20.4 trillion (~13.8% of global sales). Platforms like Airbnb and Uber disrupted traditional industries. Online advertising surpassed cable TV revenues by 2011. Politically, the Internet enabled grassroots organizing (e.g., Arab Spring) and e-government services. Yet censorship persists globally, with governments filtering content or imposing blackouts. Surveillance programs monitor traffic under laws like the U.S. CALEA.

### Security and Risks  
Malware—including viruses, worms, ransomware, botnets, and spyware—poses major threats. Cybercrime costs projected at $6 trillion annually by 2021. Surveillance agencies intercept data under legal mandates. Governments censor content via filters or blackouts. Critical infrastructure increasingly targeted. Energy consumption debates highlight environmental impacts, with streaming contributing significantly to CO₂ emissions.

## Terms  
- **TCP/IP**: Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol; foundational communication protocols enabling internetworking.  
- **ARPANET**: Advanced Research Projects Agency Network; precursor to the modern Internet.  
- **RFC**: Request for Comments; technical documents defining Internet standards.  
- **ICANN**: Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers; coordinates DNS and IP allocation.  
- **IETF**: Internet Engineering Task Force; develops open Internet standards.  
- **IPv4/IPv6**: Versions of the Internet Protocol; IPv6 addresses IPv4 address exhaustion.  
- **CDN**: Content Delivery Network; distributes web content efficiently.  
- **VoIP**: Voice over Internet Protocol; transmits voice calls over the Internet.  
- **RIR**: Regional Internet Registry; allocates IP addresses regionally.  
- **Netizen**: Active participant in online communities or civic digital life.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- **Governance**: Should the Internet remain decentralized or adopt more regulatory oversight?  
- **Fragmentation**: How will national firewalls and zero-rating practices affect global connectivity?  
- **Energy Use**: What role should “digital sobriety” play in reducing carbon footprints?  
- **Privacy vs. Security**: Can surveillance coexist with civil liberties?  
- **Digital Divide**: Will mobile access close gaps in developing regions?  
- **Interoperability**: How to manage IPv6 adoption amid IPv4 legacy systems?
