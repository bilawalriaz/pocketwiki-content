# Heinrich Heine

## Overview

Heinrich Heine (1797–1856) was a German poet, writer, and literary critic whose work bridged Romanticism and modernity. Born Jewish in Düsseldorf, he converted to Lutheranism in 1825 to pursue academic opportunities, a decision he later regretted. His early poetry, especially *Buch der Lieder* (1827), became foundational to the German Lied tradition, set to music by Schubert, Schumann, and others. His later prose and verse, marked by sharp irony and satire, critiqued German nationalism, authoritarianism, and bourgeois conformity. Exiled in Paris from 1831 until his death, Heine became a central figure of the Young Germany movement and a voice for liberal cosmopolitanism. His famous warning—"Where they burn books, they will ultimately burn people also"—echoed through history, notably during the Nazi book burnings of 1933.

## Body

### Early Life and Education

Born into a Jewish family in Düsseldorf under French occupation, Heine grew up amid the political upheaval of Napoleonic Europe. His father, Samson Heine, was a textile merchant whose business failed partly due to economic hardship under French rule. Despite his parents' lack of religious devotion, young Heine received both Jewish and Catholic schooling, learning French fluently and developing an early affinity for Rhenish folklore. Sent to Hamburg to apprentice in his uncle Salomon’s bank, he showed little interest in commerce and instead gravitated toward literature and radical politics.

At university—first in Bonn, then Göttingen, and finally Berlin—Heine embraced liberal ideals and clashed with conservative authorities. At Bonn, he participated in protests against the Carlsbad Decrees, repressive measures imposed by Metternich to suppress liberal activity. In Göttingen, he was expelled from a student fraternity for antisemitism and suspended after a duel. In Berlin, he encountered Hegel and joined intellectual salons, but remained skeptical of institutional religion and orthodoxy.

### Literary Beginnings and Conversion

Returning to Hamburg in 1825, Heine converted to Lutheranism, viewing it as a necessary step for professional advancement in a society where Jews faced systemic exclusion from academia and civil service. Though he later claimed he never truly abandoned his Jewish identity, the conversion opened doors professionally but brought no tangible benefits. His first major publication, *Buch der Lieder* (1827), combined lyrical beauty with subtle irony, distinguishing him from purely Romantic poets. The collection gained popularity when composers began setting his verses to music, embedding his work deeply in the classical art song tradition.

### Paris Years and Political Engagement

In 1831, Heine settled permanently in Paris, drawn by its cultural vibrancy and freedom from German censorship. Working as a foreign correspondent for the liberal newspaper *Allgemeine Zeitung*, he reported on French politics and society, publishing *Französische Zustände* (*Conditions in France*). He positioned himself as a mediator between German and French cultures, critiquing what he saw as Germany’s backwardness and spiritual stagnation in works like *Zur Geschichte der Religion und Philosophie in Deutschland* (*On the History of Religion and Philosophy in Germany*).

Heine aligned with the Young Germany movement, a group of radical exiles including Ludwig Börne and Karl Gutzkow. However, his relationship with Börne soured after he published a scathing memorial essay following Börne’s death in 1837, damaging his standing among fellow dissidents. Despite this, he maintained close ties with Karl Marx, contributing poems to Marx’s journal *Vorwärds*. While sharing a commitment to social justice, Heine rejected Marx’s deterministic materialism, favoring instead a vision rooted in sensual experience and aesthetic liberation.

### Later Work and Decline

From the 1840s onward, Heine’s poetry grew increasingly political, addressing themes of oppression, exile, and resistance. Works like *Die schlesischen Weber* (*The Silesian Weavers*) and *Deutschland: Ein Wintermärchen* (*Germany: A Winter’s Tale*) attacked Prussian militarism and bourgeois complacency. In 1848, he suffered a paralyzing illness—likely caused by chronic lead poisoning—that left him bedridden for the rest of his life. Confined to what he called his “mattress-grave,” he continued writing from his sickbed, producing some of his most reflective and biting prose and verse.

His final years were marked by declining health and financial instability, yet he remained intellectually active, engaging with contemporary debates and maintaining correspondence with friends across Europe. He died in 1856 and was buried in Montmartre Cemetery, his tomb inscribed with his poem “Wo?” (“Where?”).

## Terms

- **Feuilleton**: A short, often satirical journalistic piece, typically appearing in newspapers; Heine helped popularize the form in German media.
- **Lied**: An art song, usually for voice and piano, setting poetry to music; Heine’s poems were frequently adapted into Lieder by Romantic composers.
- **Young Germany (Junge Deutschland)**: A mid-19th-century literary and political movement advocating liberal reforms and opposing authoritarianism; Heine was associated with its ideals.
- **Carlsbad Decrees**: A set of suppressive laws enacted in 1819 by Metternich to curb liberal and nationalist movements in the German states.
- **Saint-Simonianism**: A French utopian socialist philosophy emphasizing meritocracy, scientific progress, and gender equality; briefly influential among Heine’s Parisian circle.
- **Tendenzdichter**: Political poets of the 1840s who used verse to critique social conditions; Heine both influenced and distanced himself from this trend.
- **Lutetia**: The Latin name for Paris; Heine used it as the title for his collected journalism about France.
- **Conversion**: Heine’s 1825 baptism into Lutheranism, undertaken to overcome legal barriers facing Jews in Prussian academia and civil service.

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars continue to debate the authenticity and implications of Heine’s conversion to Christianity. While he framed it pragmatically—as “the ticket of admission into European culture”—critics argue it represented a betrayal of his Jewish heritage. Others contend that his continued identification with Jewish history and critique of antisemitism complicate any simple reading of the act.

The extent of Heine’s influence on Nazi ideology remains contested. Though the regime officially condemned him as a degenerate Jew, some argue that elements of his nationalist rhetoric and anti-immigrant sentiment resonated with later fascist discourse. Conversely, others emphasize his enduring legacy as a symbol of intellectual freedom and resistance to tyranny.

His relationship with Karl Marx has also sparked debate. While both shared opposition to capitalism and authoritarianism, Heine’s emphasis on individual expression and aesthetic pleasure contrasted sharply with Marx’s revolutionary materialism. Their correspondence reveals mutual respect tempered by fundamental philosophical differences.

Finally, the interpretation of Heine’s “empty phrase”—a concept critiqued by Karl Kraus—remains contentious. Some view it as evidence of his complicity in the commodification of culture, while others see it as a deliberate artistic strategy that exposed the contradictions of modern life.
