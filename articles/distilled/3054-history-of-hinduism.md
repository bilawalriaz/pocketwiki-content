# History of Hinduism

## Overview
Hinduism is a diverse family of religious traditions native to the Indian subcontinent, with no single founder. It is not a single, unified religion but a synthesis of various cultures and traditions that emerged around the beginning of the Common Era. Its history is a complex process of assimilation, philosophical development, and adaptation, evolving from ancient Vedic rituals to a vast spectrum of beliefs and practices followed by over a billion people today.

## Timeline
- **c. 3300–1700 BCE** — Indus Valley Civilization flourishes; seals suggest possible proto-Hindu iconography.
- **c. 1750–500 BCE** — Vedic period begins with Indo-Aryan migrations; composition of the Rigveda and other Vedic texts.
- **c. 600–200 BCE** — "Second Urbanisation"; rise of Śramaṇa movements (Buddhism, Jainism) challenges Brahmanical orthodoxy.
- **c. 200 BCE–300 CE** — The "Hindu synthesis" develops, incorporating Śramaṇa and Bhakti ideas into Brahmanical fold via smriti literature.
- **c. 320–650 CE** — Gupta Empire period ("Golden Age"); codification of Hindu philosophical schools and flourishing of Sanskrit literature.
- **c. 650–1200 CE** — Late-Classical period; rise of Puranic Hinduism and decline of Buddhism in India.
- **c. 1200–1750 CE** — Medieval period under Hindu and Islamic rulers; Bhakti movement flourishes.
- **1526–1857** — Mughal Empire period; varying policies of tolerance and intolerance towards Hindus.
- **1674–1818** — Maratha Empire rises as a major Hindu political power.
- **1850–present** — Modern period; Hindu reform movements, Hindu Renaissance, and global spread via diaspora.

## Body

### Roots and Early Development
Hinduism's roots are a fusion of diverse traditions, including the historical Vedic religion of the Indo-Aryans, Śramaṇa (renouncer) traditions, and indigenous cultures like those of the Indus Valley Civilization. The Vedic religion (c. 1750–500 BCE) was a sacrificial system centered on hymns to nature deities (devas) and cosmic order (ṛta). The later Upanishads (c. 9th–8th centuries BCE) introduced philosophical concepts like Brahman (ultimate reality) and ātman (self), forming the basis of Vedanta.

### The Hindu Synthesis and Classical Period
Between c. 200 BCE and 300 CE, a "Hindu synthesis" emerged in response to the challenge of Buddhism and Jainism. Brahmins broadened their services, incorporating local traditions and Śramaṇa ideas into a new Brahmanical framework via smriti literature (texts based on memory, not direct revelation). This synthesis is reflected in the great epics (Ramayana, Mahabharata) and the Bhagavad Gita. During the Gupta Empire (c. 320–650 CE), the six orthodox schools of Hindu philosophy were codified, and the Bhakti movement, emphasizing personal devotion, began in South India.

### Puranic Hinduism and Medieval Developments
From c. 650 CE, Puranic Hinduism became the dominant form, using the Puranas to disseminate a mainstream ideology among diverse populations. This period saw the rise of major devotional sects for Vishnu and Shiva. The Bhakti movement intensified, with poet-saints across India composing devotional works. Philosophically, Advaita Vedanta, systematized by Shankara (8th century CE), proposed that the individual self (ātman) and ultimate reality (Brahman) are one, with the perceived world being māyā (illusion). Later teachers like Ramanuja promoted devotional theism in contrast to Shankara's abstract monism.

### Islamic Rule and Hindu Response
From the 13th century, Muslim conquests introduced new political and religious dynamics. While there were periods of conflict and temple destruction, particularly under Aurangzeb, there was also coexistence and syncretism. Hindu kingdoms like the Vijayanagara Empire and the Maratha Empire emerged as defenders of Hindu culture. The Bhakti movement continued to flourish, often transcending religious boundaries.

### Modern Hinduism and Global Spread
The British colonial period sparked a Hindu Renaissance and reform movements like the Brahmo Samaj and Arya Samaj, which sought to purify Hinduism and engage with modernity. Western Indology influenced the conceptualization of Hinduism as a unified religion. In the 20th century, teachers like Vivekananda and Prabhupada globalized Hindu philosophies like Vedanta and Yoga. The Indian diaspora has established Hindu communities worldwide. Politically, Hindu nationalism (Hindutva) has become a significant force in modern India.

## Terms
- **Brahman**: The ultimate, unchanging reality in Hindu philosophy, especially in Vedanta.
- **Ātman**: The individual self or soul, often considered identical to Brahman in Advaita Vedanta.
- **Dharma**: A complex term meaning cosmic law, duty, righteousness, or the essential nature of a thing.
- **Bhakti**: Devotion to a personal god, a major trend in Hinduism emphasizing emotional love and worship.
- **Smriti**: "Remembered" texts, including epics and law codes, considered secondary to the revealed Shruti scriptures.
- **Shruti**: "Heard" scriptures, primarily the Vedas, considered the highest authority in orthodox Hinduism.
- **Māyā**: The illusory or apparent nature of the phenomenal world in Advaita Vedanta.
- **Karma**: The principle that an individual's actions in this life determine their future existences.
- **Saṃsāra**: The cycle of birth, death, and rebirth from which Hindus seek liberation (moksha).
- **Purāṇa**: A genre of ancient texts containing myths, legends, and genealogies of gods, heroes, and kings.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origins**: The extent to which Hinduism is derived from the Indus Valley Civilization versus the Indo-Aryan Vedic tradition is debated.
- **Influence**: The nature and direction of influence between early Upanishads and Buddhist teachings is contested by scholars.
- **Shankara's Influence**: The historical prominence and widespread influence of the philosopher Shankara and Advaita Vedanta in early medieval India is questioned, with some scholars suggesting their fame was amplified later.
- **Muslim Rule Impact**: The historical impact of Muslim conquests on Hinduism is a subject of diverse viewpoints, ranging from narratives of widespread destruction to those emphasizing coexistence and voluntary conversion.
