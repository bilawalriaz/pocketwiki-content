# Kabuki

## Overview
Kabuki is a classical Japanese theatre form combining drama, dance, and music, renowned for its stylized performances, elaborate costumes, and distinctive makeup. Originating in the early 17th century, it evolved from female-led performances to an all-male art form, becoming a central part of Edo-period urban culture. Its historical significance and artistic value led to its recognition as a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2005.

## Timeline
- **1603** — Izumo no Okuni begins performing a new dance drama style in Kyoto, marking the origin of kabuki.
- **1629** — Women are banned from performing in kabuki, leading to the use of young boys.
- **c. 1653** — After further restrictions, adult male actors (yarō-kabuki) become the norm.
- **1673–1841** — The Genroku period sees kabuki's formalization, with key developments in play structure, acting, and makeup.
- **1723** — The shogunate bans plays about love suicides (shinju mono).
- **1842** — The Tenpō Reforms force major kabuki theatres to relocate to Saruwaka-chō in Asakusa.
- **1868** — The Meiji Restoration begins; kabuki returns to central Tokyo and adapts to modern influences.
- **1887** — Emperor Meiji sponsors a kabuki performance, signaling its elevated status.
- **1945** — Kabuki is briefly banned by post-WWII occupation forces.
- **1947** — The ban is lifted, though censorship remains.
- **1975** — Earphone guides are introduced to broaden audience appeal.
- **2005** — UNESCO proclaims kabuki an intangible heritage of outstanding universal value.
- **1986** — Ichikawa En-ō premieres "Super Kabuki," a modernized genre incorporating contemporary themes and technology.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
Kabuki was founded in 1603 by Izumo no Okuni, a former shrine maiden, who led a troupe of female dancers performing a new, simple style of dance drama in Kyoto. The form, initially featuring ribald themes, became popular in red-light districts like Yoshiwara. To maintain social order, shogunal authorities banned women from performing in 1629, followed by a ban on young boys around 1653. This led to the establishment of yarō-kabuki, performed by adult male actors, with cross-dressing male actors (onnagata) taking on female roles.

### The Genroku Period and Formalization
During the Genroku period (late 17th to early 18th century), kabuki's structure and traditions solidified. Playwright Chikamatsu Monzaemon, though primarily writing for bunraku (puppet theatre), created works adapted for kabuki. Key artistic elements emerged, including the mie pose (a dramatic, picturesque stance) and kumadori makeup (stylized facial lines indicating character traits). The form temporarily lost popularity to bunraku in the mid-18th century before resurging.

### Relocation and the Meiji Era
Repeated fires and the restrictive Tenpō Reforms (1842) forced major theatres to relocate to Saruwaka-chō, a period known for exaggerated performances. After the Meiji Restoration (1868), kabuki returned to central Tokyo. Playwrights and actors adapted to modern tastes and Western influences, improving its reputation. The Emperor sponsored a performance in 1887, cementing its status.

### Post-War and Modern Kabuki
Following a brief post-WWII ban, kabuki revived. Innovations like the Takechi Kabuki productions in the 1950s sparked renewed interest. Today, it remains popular, with stars appearing in film and television. Modern adaptations include "Super Kabuki," which uses advanced technology and adapts contemporary stories (e.g., anime), though this sparks debate about tradition versus innovation. Earphone guides (introduced 1975) and international tours have broadened its global reach.

### Stage, Appearance, and Performance
The kabuki stage features a hanamichi (a walkway extending into the audience) and technological innovations like revolving stages (mawari-butai), trap doors (seri), and flying effects (chūnori). Costumes are vibrant and layered, with kumadori makeup using colors to symbolize character traits (e.g., red for heroism, blue for villainy). Performances are categorized into jidaimono (historical plays), sewamono (domestic plays), and shosagoto (dance pieces). Dance emphasizes sleeve movement and rhythmic stamping, while music includes nagauta (song-based pieces with shamisen).

### Play Structure and Acting
Full-length plays typically follow a five-act structure based on the jo-ha-kyū pacing convention (slow start, acceleration, quick end). Kabuki focuses on actor-centric storytelling, sometimes prioritizing an actor's skill over plot continuity. Regional styles differed, with Edo kabuki being extravagant and Kamigata (Kyoto-Osaka) kabuki favoring realism. Actors use stage names passed down through lineages, with name-succession ceremonies (shūmei) marking career milestones.

## Terms
- **Onnagata**: A male actor specializing in female roles.
- **Kumadori**: Stylized stage makeup using lines to exaggerate facial features and indicate a character's nature.
- **Mie**: A dramatic, picturesque pose struck by an actor to establish character and elicit audience appreciation.
- **Hanamichi**: A long, raised walkway extending from the stage through the audience, used for entrances and key scenes.
- **Jidaimono**: A category of kabuki plays set in historical periods, often using past events as metaphors for contemporary issues.
- **Sewamono**: A category of kabuki plays focusing on domestic stories of commoners, often involving romance and family drama.
- **Shosagoto**: A kabuki play or segment emphasizing dance.
- **Nagauta**: A form of kabuki music featuring vocal accompaniment and shamisen, often used in dance pieces.
- **Yarō-kabuki**: The all-male form of kabuki established after bans on female and young male performers.
- **Jo-ha-kyū**: A traditional pacing convention in Japanese theatre where action starts slowly, speeds up, and ends quickly.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which modern adaptations like "Super Kabuki" preserve or dilute the 400-year history and traditional essence of the art form is a subject of ongoing controversy.
- The historical role of kabuki in relation to social class, sexuality, and censorship—particularly its origins in red-light districts and its navigation of shogunal restrictions—continues to be analyzed by scholars.
