# William Blackstone

## Overview
Sir William Blackstone (1723–1780) was an English jurist and judge whose *Commentaries on the Laws of England* (1765–1769) became the definitive description of English common law. His work provided a systematic, scholarly foundation for legal education and profoundly influenced the legal systems of the United States and other common-law countries.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Blackstone was the fourth and posthumous son of a prosperous London silk mercer. After his father's death, family fortunes declined, but he attended Charterhouse School as a "poor scholar." He excelled academically, particularly in Latin and poetry. In 1738, he entered Pembroke College, Oxford, where he studied a broad curriculum before switching to a Bachelor of Civil Law degree in 1740. He was admitted to the Middle Temple in 1741 and called to the Bar in 1746, though his early practice was slow due to a lack of connections and a contraction in court business.

### Oxford Career and Lectures
While dividing his time between London and Oxford, Blackstone became a dedicated administrator at All Souls College, simplifying its accounting system and overseeing building projects. In 1753, he launched a private lecture series on English law—the first of their kind—which was highly successful and earned him significant income. These lectures formed the basis for his 1756 *An Analysis of the Laws of England*, a pioneering structured introduction to the subject. In 1758, he became the first Vinerian Professor of English Law, a position created from Charles Viner's bequest. His inaugural lecture, *A Discourse on the Study of the Law*, was widely published. However, his tenure was marked by personal conflicts and unpopularity within the university, leading to his departure from Oxford.

### The *Commentaries* and Judicial Career
With growing fame and patronage from the Prince of Wales, Blackstone returned to a successful practice at the Bar and entered Parliament in 1761. After resigning the Vinerian Chair in 1765, he published his magnum opus, *Commentaries on the Laws of England*, in four volumes between 1765 and 1769. The work was a commercial and critical success, providing a complete overview of English law. Despite repeated earlier failures, he was appointed a judge in 1770, first briefly to the King's Bench and then permanently to the Common Pleas, where he served until his death. He was considered a conscientious but sometimes narrow judge.

### Legacy and Influence
Blackstone's *Commentaries* became the cornerstone of legal education in England and America, giving the law "a veneer of scholarly respectability." The work was reprinted extensively and was the primary law book used by American lawyers for nearly a century. It directly influenced key American legal figures, including Alexander Hamilton, John Marshall, and Abraham Lincoln, and remains cited in U.S. Supreme Court decisions. His formulation that "it is better that ten guilty persons escape than that one innocent suffer" (Blackstone's Ratio) is a fundamental principle of Anglo-American law. His work was criticized by contemporaries like Jeremy Bentham and later by Alexis de Tocqueville, but its impact on shaping common law and legal thought is widely acknowledged.

## Terms
- **Common Law**: The body of law derived from judicial decisions and custom, as opposed to statutes.
- **Vinerian Professor**: The holder of the first professorship of English law at Oxford, endowed by Charles Viner.
- **Commentaries on the Laws of England**: Blackstone's four-volume treatise providing a systematic overview of English law.
- **Blackstone's Ratio**: The principle that it is better for ten guilty persons to escape than for one innocent person to suffer.
- **Rotten Borough**: A parliamentary constituency with a very small electorate, allowing the seat to be easily controlled.
- **Serjeant-at-Law**: A member of an order of barristers at the English Bar, a rank now obsolete.
- **King's Bench / Common Pleas**: Two of the senior common-law courts in England at the time.
- **Assize**: Courts held periodically in English counties to administer civil and criminal justice.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Accuracy and Omissions**: Critics, including Jeremy Bentham, argued the *Commentaries* contained inaccurate statements of law and omitted key documents like the Constitutions of Clarendon and the 1689 Bill of Rights.
- **Judicial Performance**: While respected, Blackstone was considered a poor trial judge, being reversed on appeal more frequently than his peers.
- **Motivations for Lectures**: His move to give private lectures was partly driven by financial gain and the anticipated creation of the Vinerian Professorship, not solely by benevolence.
- **Relationship with Mansfield**: The exact nature of Blackstone's relationship with Lord Mansfield and the reasons for his brief transfer from the King's Bench are debated, with Bentham's account of conflict being considered dubious.
