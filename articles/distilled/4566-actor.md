# Actor

## Overview
An actor is a person who portrays a character in a production, performing in theatre, film, radio, or television. The profession has evolved from ancient ritual performances to a modern, specialized art form, with significant historical and ongoing debates about gender roles, terminology, and compensation.

## Timeline
- **534 BC** — Thespis becomes the first recorded actor in ancient Greece.
- **1564** — Lucrezia Di Siena is named on a contract, becoming the first known Italian actress.
- **1660** — Women are first allowed to perform on the English stage after the Restoration.
- **1895** — Henry Irving is knighted, marking full societal acceptance of actors in Britain.
- **1950s-60s** — The term "actor" begins its re-adoption as a gender-neutral term.
- **2010** — *The Observer* and *The Guardian* style guides recommend "actor" for all genders.

## Body

### Terminology and Gender
The word "actor" historically meant "one who does something" and only referred to theatrical performers in the 16th century. The term "actress" first appeared in 1608. For centuries after women entered the English stage in 1660, "actress" became the common term for female performers. Since the 1950s, "actor" has been increasingly used as a gender-neutral term, a practice endorsed by major style guides in 2010, though the term "actress" remains common in major awards. The gender-neutral term "player" was common in early film but is now largely archaic, though it persists in some theatre contexts.

### Historical Development
Acting began in ancient Greece with Thespis in 534 BC. In ancient Greece, only men performed. Ancient Rome allowed some female performers, but the profession largely died out in late antiquity. During the Middle Ages, acting was performed by amateurs, often for religious plays, and was viewed with distrust by the Church. The Renaissance saw the rise of professional actors, with Italian *commedia dell'arte* troupes employing women from the 16th century. In England, women were first allowed on stage after the Restoration in 1660. The 19th century saw acting become an honored profession with the rise of actor-managers like Henry Irving. The 20th century displaced the actor-manager model with corporate theatre ownership and specialization.

### Women in Acting
Women were barred from the stage in ancient Greece and medieval Europe. They began performing professionally in Renaissance Italy, Spain, and France in the 16th century. England was late to allow women, who first appeared regularly after 1660. In Northern and Eastern Europe, women often appeared with foreign companies decades before native professional theatre was established. In East Asia, men traditionally played female roles (e.g., *onnagata* in Japanese kabuki), with women reintroduced in the 19th century. In the Middle East and Egypt, early modern theatre companies, founded in the 1850s-1870s, initially had to hire non-Muslim women due to social stigma.

### Acting Techniques and Media
Classical acting integrates body, voice, and script analysis, based on theories from figures like Konstantin Stanislavski. Stanislavski's system and its derivative, Method acting, emphasize actors using personal experiences to portray characters truthfully. The Meisner technique focuses on reacting to other actors in the moment. Acting for different media requires specific skills: theatre actors learn stage directions and blocking; film actors must work with cameras and convey subtlety; television actors work with multiple cameras and understand "frame"; radio drama relies solely on voice, music, and sound effects.

### Compensation and Gender Pay Gap
Actor incomes vary widely. In 2024, the median hourly wage for U.S. actors was $23.33, with many lacking benefits. Some film actors earn tens of millions per production. A significant gender pay gap exists; in 2013, top-paid U.S. actresses earned 40 cents for every dollar earned by top-paid actors.

## Terms
- **Actor/Actress**: A person who portrays a character. "Actor" is increasingly used as a gender-neutral term.
- **Commedia dell'arte**: An Italian theatrical form using improvisation and stock characters, which early employed professional women.
- **Method acting**: A range of techniques, based on Stanislavski's system, where actors use personal experiences to develop a character.
- **Onnagata**: A male kabuki actor who specializes in female roles.
- **Player**: An archaic gender-neutral term for an actor, still used in some theatre contexts.
- **Stage directions**: Instructions in a script indicating movement and placement on stage (e.g., "stage left").
- **Blocking**: The precise movement and positioning of actors on stage.
- **Series regular**: A main actor on a television show, part of the permanent cast.

## Debates and Open Questions
The use of "actor" versus "actress" remains a debated issue within the profession, with no consensus. The gender pay gap in the film industry persists, with significant disparities based on gender and race. The practice of cisgender actors playing transgender or non-binary roles is a contemporary point of discussion.
