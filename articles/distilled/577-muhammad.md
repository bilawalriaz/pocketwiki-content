# Muhammad

## Overview
Muhammad (c. 570–632 CE) was an Arab religious, military, and political leader who founded Islam. Muslims regard him as the final prophet—the "Seal of the Prophets"—sent to confirm the monotheistic teachings of earlier figures such as Abraham, Moses, and Jesus. The revelations he reported receiving over 23 years form the Quran, Islam’s central scripture, while his normative practices (*sunnah*), preserved in *hadith* collections and biographical works (*sīrah*), constitute the second source of Islamic law and theology. Born into the Banu Hashim clan of the Quraysh tribe in Mecca, Muhammad began preaching publicly c. 613 after experiencing visions in the cave of Hira. Persecution drove him and his followers to migrate to Medina in 622 (the *Hijrah*), where he established a unified community (*ummah*), fought a series of battles against Meccan forces and local Jewish tribes, and eventually conquered Mecca in 630. By his death in 632, most of the Arabian Peninsula had embraced Islam. His legacy includes the rapid expansion of a new polity, enduring social and legal reforms, and a contested historical record that scholars reconstruct from sources written 150–250 years after his life.

## Body

### Biographical Sources and Historiography
The earliest surviving biographies (*sīrah*) date from the 8th–9th centuries CE (2nd–3rd centuries AH), notably Ibn Ishaq’s *Life of God’s Messenger* (c. 767), preserved in recensions by Ibn Hisham and al-Tabari. These works evolved from *maghāzī* (campaign narratives) and storytellers’ accounts (*quṣṣāṣ*), often hagiographic rather than strictly historical. *Hadith* collections—compiled later by scholars such as al-Bukhari and Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj—record Muhammad’s sayings and actions via chains of transmission (*isnād*); their authenticity is graded by Islamic scholars but viewed skeptically by many Western historians as potential fabrications supporting later theological or legal positions. The Quran itself mentions Muhammad by name only four times and provides limited biographical detail, focusing on his role as "Messenger of God" and referencing events like the migration to Yathrib and the Battle of Badr. Modern scholarship (e.g., John Burton) emphasizes the "oral gap" between Muhammad’s life and the written sources, concluding that "virtually nothing of use to the historian emerges... beyond the bare fact that he once existed," though others (e.g., Karen Armstrong) argue more is known about Muhammad than founders of other major religions.

### Meccan Period (c. 570–622)
**Early Life and Marriage.** Muhammad was orphaned young: father Abdullah died before his birth, mother Amina when he was six. Raised by grandfather Abd al-Muttalib and uncle Abu Talib, he gained a reputation as *al-Amīn* ("the trustworthy"). At 25, he managed a caravan for Khadija, a wealthy widow 15 years his senior; she proposed marriage, and they remained monogamous for 25 years until her death in 619. They had four daughters (Fatimah survived him) and two sons who died in infancy. In 605, Muhammad arbitrated a clan dispute over replacing the Black Stone during the Kaaba’s reconstruction, placing it on a cloak for joint lifting.

**First Revelations.** Around 610, during retreats in the cave of Hira, Muhammad reported a terrifying encounter with the angel Gabriel, who commanded him to "Read." After initial fear and suicidal distress, he was reassured by Khadija and her Christian cousin Waraqah ibn Nawfal, who identified the visitor as the divine messenger sent to Moses. Revelations paused after Waraqah’s death, causing further anguish, but resumed with Gabriel affirming Muhammad’s prophetic status. Early Quranic verses warned of divine punishment, promised rewards to believers, and stressed the Last Judgment.

**Public Preaching and Opposition.** From c. 613, Muhammad preached *tawḥīd* (God’s oneness), *islām* (submission), and *zakāt* (almsgiving), attracting mostly women, freedmen, slaves, and lower-status individuals. The Quraysh initially ignored him but grew hostile when he attacked their ancestral polytheism. They demanded miracles, mocked his poverty, and pressured Abu Talib to silence him. Abu Talib refused, declaring he would never abandon his nephew. A Quraysh delegation consulted Jewish rabbis in Yathrib, who posed three test questions (the Sleepers of the Cave, Dhu al-Qarnayn, the Spirit); Muhammad answered after a 15-day delay via revelation (Quran 18), but neither the rabbis nor the Quraysh converted.

**Persecution, Abyssinia, and Boycott.** In 615, Muhammad sent followers to the Christian kingdom of Aksum (Abyssinia) under the Negus Aṣḥama ibn Abjar; sources differ on whether there were one or two migrations. The so-called "Satanic Verses" incident—where Muhammad allegedly recited verses acknowledging pagan goddesses, then retracted them—is recorded by early biographers but rejected by later Muslim scholars and debated by Western ones. In 616, Quraysh imposed a commercial and marital boycott on Banu Hashim, lifted in 619. That "Year of Sorrow" saw the deaths of Khadija and Abu Talib. Abu Lahab, the new clan head, withdrew protection after Muhammad said his ancestors were in hell.

**Ta'if and the Night Journey.** Seeking allies, Muhammad traveled to Ta'if but was stoned and rejected. He returned to Mecca under the protection of Mut'im ibn 'Adiy. Around this low point, tradition places the *Isrāʾ* (night journey from Mecca to "the farthest place of worship," identified variously as Jerusalem, a celestial Kaaba, or al-Ji'rana) and *Miʿrāj* (ascension through heavens). The Quran (17:1) mentions a night journey from *al-Masjid al-Ḥarām* to *al-Masjid al-Aqṣā*; dating and sequence vary among sources (Ibn Sa'd, Ibn Hisham, al-Tabari).

**Migration to Medina.** Muhammad cultivated contacts with the Khazraj and Aws tribes of Yathrib, who hoped he could mediate their feud. In 621 and 622, delegations pledged allegiance at al-ʿAqaba (the "Pledge of War"). Muhammad sent Musʿab ibn ʿUmayr to teach Islam in Yathrib. Meccan Muslims emigrated in small groups (*Hijrah*); Muhammad left last, escaping an assassination plot by having Ali sleep in his bed. He and Abu Bakr hid in a cave for three days, reaching Yathrib (renamed Medina) on 4 September 622. Emigrants became *Muhājirūn*; Medinan helpers, *Anṣār*.

### Medinan Period (622–632)
**Building the Community.** Muhammad purchased land for a mosque (*masjid*) serving as residence, prayer hall, court, and welfare center. The *qibla* (prayer direction) initially faced Jerusalem. The call to prayer (*adhān*) was instituted after a companion’s dream; Bilal, a former Abyssinian slave, became the first *muʾadhdhin*. The Constitution of Medina—a unilateral proclamation or treaty drafted by Muhammad—regulated relations among Muslim, Jewish, and pagan tribes, guaranteeing Jewish religious freedom and collective defense of the oasis. Its exact nature, date, and relation to the expulsion of Jewish tribes remain debated.

**Conflict with Mecca and Jewish Tribes.** Meccans seized *Muhājirūn* property. Quranic permission to fight was granted. In February 624, the *qibla* was changed to Mecca. After several failed caravan raids, the Muslims won a surprising victory at **Badr** (March 624) against a larger Meccan force, killing leaders including Abu Jahl. Muhammad attributed victory to angels. The Banu Qaynuqa (a Jewish tribe) were besieged and expelled after a marketplace altercation; Abdullah ibn Ubayy interceded to spare their lives.

**Uhud and Nadir.** In 625, Abu Sufyan led a Meccan army to **Uhud**. Muslims initially held ground but fled when archers disobeyed orders; Muhammad was wounded. The defeat prompted a revelation permitting polygyny (up to four wives) to care for widows. Muhammad later besieged the Banu Nadir (another Jewish tribe) after claiming a divine warning of assassination; they surrendered and were expelled, their palm groves burned. Kaʿb ibn al-Ashraf, a Nadir poet inciting Meccans, was assassinated by Muhammad’s followers using deception.

**Trench and Qurayza.** In 627, Abu Sufyan assembled a 10,000-man confederacy. Salman the Persian suggested digging a trench—unknown in Arabian warfare—which neutralized cavalry. After a two-week siege, the coalition withdrew. The Banu Qurayza (the third major Jewish tribe) were accused of negotiating with the besiegers. After a 25-day siege, they surrendered. Saʿd ibn Muʿadh, an Aws ally, judged that all men be executed (600–900 killed), property distributed, women and children enslaved. Modern scholars (Arafat, Ahmad) dispute the scale, suggesting conflation with earlier Jewish massacres; Watt finds their arguments unconvincing.

**Hudaybiyya and Khaybar.** In 628, Muhammad attempted a pilgrimage (*ʿumra*) but was stopped at Hudaybiyya. A ten-year truce was signed: Muslims would return that year but could pilgrimage next; Quraysh defectors to Muhammad must be returned, but not Muslim defectors to Quraysh. The treaty recognized Muhammad’s political parity. Weeks later, he attacked **Khaybar**, a Jewish oasis 75 miles north. After a month-long siege, it fell. Jews remained as tenant farmers paying half their harvest. Kenana ibn al-Rabi was tortured and executed for hidden treasure; his widow Safiyya became Muhammad’s wife. Fadak surrendered on similar terms, becoming Muhammad’s personal property. A Jewish woman poisoned Muhammad at a feast; he survived but suffered lingering effects.

**Conquest of Mecca.** In 630, the Banu Bakr (Quraysh allies) attacked the Khuzaʿa (Muslim allies). Quraysh chose to annul the truce rather than pay blood money. Muhammad marched with 10,000 men; Mecca surrendered with minimal casualties. He declared amnesty (excepting a few named individuals), destroyed idols in the Kaaba (sparing images of Mary and Jesus per some reports), and most Meccans converted.

**Hunayn, Ta'if, and Tabuk.** The Hawazin and Thaqif tribes mobilized at Hunayn; Muhammad’s 12,000 men were ambushed but rallied to victory, capturing vast booty and families. He besieged Ta'if for 15–20 days without success. Distribution of Hunayn spoils favored new Quraysh converts (Abu Sufyan’s family received 100 camels each), angering the *Anṣār*; Muhammad placated them with camels from future raids. In late 630, he led an expedition to **Tabuk** (Byzantine border) during extreme heat; no battle occurred, but local chiefs submitted and paid *jizya*. Ta'if (Thaqif) converted after a year of pressure.

**Farewell Pilgrimage and Death.** In February 631, a revelation gave idolaters four months’ grace. In 632, Muhammad led the Hajj, delivering a sermon prohibiting usury and pre-Islamic vendettas, affirming Muslim brotherhood, and fixing the lunar calendar without intercalation. In June 632, he fell ill—severe headache, fever, leg weakness—possibly from the Khaybar poison. He died on 8 June 632 in Aisha’s house, buried there. His last words: "O God, forgive me and have mercy on me; and let me join the highest companions."

### Succession and Early Expansion
No clear successor was designated. Umar nominated Abu Bakr, who became first caliph. Opponents (Shīʿat ʿAlī) held that Ali ibn Abi Talib was designated at Ghadir Khumm. Abu Bakr suppressed the *Ridda* wars (apostasy rebellions) and launched conquests against the Byzantine and Sasanian empires—exhausted by decades of war and internally divided (e.g., non-Chalcedonian Christians alienated from Constantinople). Within a decade, Muslims conquered Mesopotamia, Syria, Egypt, and Persia, establishing the Rashidun Caliphate.

### Household
Muhammad’s marital life divides into pre-*Hijra* (Khadija only) and post-*Hijra* (multiple wives). After Khadija’s death, he married Sawda bint Zamʿa and Aisha (daughter of Abu Bakr), betrothed at 6–7, consummated at 9 per classical sources. Nine wives survived him. Aisha became a major *hadith* transmitter. Maria al-Qibtiyya bore a son, Ibrahim, who died at two. Zayd ibn Haritha, a slave gifted by Khadija, was adopted then disowned when Muhammad married Zayd’s ex-wife Zaynab bint Jahsh. Muhammad performed household chores and consulted his wives. He owned slaves, advocated humane treatment and manumission, but did not abolish the institution.

### Legacy
**Islamic Tradition.** The *Shahāda* ("There is no god but God; Muhammad is the Messenger of God") is Islam’s core creed, recited at birth, death, in prayer, and conversion. The *sunnah* (Muhammad’s precedent) details rituals (prayer, fasting, pilgrimage) absent from the Quran. Muslims venerate him as *raḥma* (mercy) to the worlds; his birthday (*Mawlid*) is widely celebrated (except in Wahhabi Saudi Arabia). Depictions are prohibited in Sunni/Ahmadi Islam to prevent idolatry; early Persian/Ottoman miniatures sometimes veiled his face; modern images in Turkey/Iran offend many Muslims elsewhere.

**Social Reforms.** Watt argues Muhammad responded to Mecca’s total situation—religious, economic, social, political. Lewis identifies two traditions: Muhammad the rebel (Mecca) and statesman (Medina). Historians generally agree Islam improved Arab norms: denounced aristocratic privilege, mandated *zakāt* for the poor, regulated slavery, enhanced women’s and children’s rights (e.g., inheritance, marriage consent), and replaced tribal vengeance with legal procedure.

**European Views.** Postel, Leibniz, Boulainvilliers, Rousseau, Carlyle, Goethe, and Napoleon praised Muhammad as a sincere legislator, conqueror, or nation-builder. Voltaire was mixed (fanatic in drama, legislator in history). Pastoret ranked him with Zoroaster and Confucius. Modern scholars (Watt, Bell) argue for his sincerity and good faith, noting sincerity ≠ correctness; Welch attributes his influence to firm vocational belief.

**Criticism.** Existed from the 7th century: Arab contemporaries rejected monotheism; Jewish tribes objected to Biblical appropriation and "Seal of Prophets" claim. Medieval Christians labeled him false prophet/Antichrist. Modern criticism targets his prophetic legitimacy, marriages (especially Aisha’s age), slave ownership, treatment of enemies (Qurayza executions), and psychological state.

**Sufism and Other Religions.** Sufis view Muhammad as the perfect human (*al-insān al-kāmil*) and spiritual pole; all orders trace descent to him. Baháʼís revere him as a Manifestation of God, superseded by Baháʼu'lláh. Druze count him among seven major prophets. Sikhs honor him as a divine messenger; Guru Nanak reportedly praised him in *janamsākhī* literature.

## Terms
- ****Quran**** — The central religious text of Islam, believed by Muslims to be the verbatim word of God revealed to Muhammad via the archangel Gabriel over 23 years.
- ****Hadith**** — Reports of Muhammad’s sayings, actions, and tacit approvals, transmitted via chains of narrators (*isnād*); second source of Islamic law after the Quran.
- ****Sīrah**** — Biographical literature on Muhammad’s life; earliest extant version is Ibn Ishaq’s (c. 767), preserved in later recensions.
- ****Hijrah**** — The migration of Muhammad and his followers from Mecca to Medina (Yathrib) in 622 CE; marks the start of the Islamic (Hijri) calendar.
- ****Ummah**** — The community of Muslims bound by faith rather than kinship; established in Medina under the Constitution of Medina.
- ****Jizya**** — A per-capita tax levied on non-Muslim adult males (*dhimmīs*) under Islamic rule in exchange for protection and exemption from military service.
- ****Khums**** — "One-fifth"; the portion of war booty reserved for the Prophet/state (Quran 8:41), later institutionalized in Islamic finance.
- ****Sunnah**** — The normative practice of Muhammad derived from *hadith* and *sīrah*; model for Muslim conduct covering ritual, law, ethics, and daily life.
- ****Shahāda**** — The Islamic declaration of faith: "There is no god but God; Muhammad is the Messenger of God"; first of the Five Pillars.
- ****Ridda Wars**** — "Wars of Apostasy" (632–633 CE): campaigns by Caliph Abu Bakr against Arabian tribes who rejected Islamic authority after Muhammad’s death.

## Debates and open questions
- **Historicity of early sources:** The 150–250-year gap between Muhammad’s life and the earliest surviving *sīrah*/*hadith* manuscripts leads scholars like John Burton to doubt recoverable historical detail beyond his existence, while others (e.g., Karen Armstrong) find the record comparatively robust.
- **Nature of the Constitution of Medina:** Disputed whether it was a unilateral proclamation or mutual treaty, a single document or composite, drafted before or after the Jewish expulsions, and its precise legal character.
- **Banu Qurayza massacre scale:** Ibn Ishaq reports 600–900 men executed; Arafat and Ahmad argue this conflates later Jewish massacre memories and that only fighters were killed or enslaved; Watt and Kister defend the general reliability of the tradition.
