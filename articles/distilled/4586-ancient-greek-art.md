# Ancient Greek art

## Overview
Ancient Greek art, produced from the Iron Age to the Roman conquest in 146 BC, is renowned for its development of naturalistic yet idealized depictions of the human body, particularly the nude male form. It established foundational principles in sculpture, architecture, and ornament that profoundly influenced Roman, European, and Asian art, making it a cornerstone of Western artistic tradition.

## Timeline
- c. 1000 BC | Start of the Geometric period, following the Greek Dark Ages.
- 7th century BC | Development of the Archaic style, including black-figure vase painting.
- c. 530 BC | Invention of the red-figure vase painting technique in Athens.
- c. 500 BC | Traditional dividing line between Archaic and Classical periods.
- 480–448 BC | Persian Wars, a catalyst for the Classical period.
- 336–323 BC | Reign of Alexander the Great, separating Classical from Hellenistic periods.
- 146 BC | Roman conquest of Greece at the Battle of Corinth.

## Body

### Periods and Stylistic Development
Greek art is conventionally divided into four periods: Geometric, Archaic, Classical, and Hellenistic. Transitions were gradual, with stylistic development occurring at different speeds across the Greek world. The Geometric period (c. 1000–750 BC) featured abstract designs on pottery. The Archaic period (c. 750–500 BC) saw the rise of monumental stone sculpture, influenced by Egyptian models, and the black-figure pottery style. The Classical period (c. 500–323 BC) introduced naturalistic poses and individual portraiture, while the Hellenistic period (323–146 BC) expanded Greek culture across Eurasia, leading to more emotional, diverse, and technically virtuosic art.

### Pottery
Pottery is the most abundantly surviving art form, with over 100,000 complete vessels. It was used for daily life, trade, and as grave goods. The two main figural styles were black-figure (figures painted in black slip on red clay) and red-figure (figures left in red on a black background), which reversed the earlier technique. By the 5th century BC, pottery production became an industry, and vase-painting declined as an art form. Key production centers were Corinth and later Athens, whose exports dominated the Mediterranean.

### Sculpture
Sculpture focused on the human form, which was considered both sacred and secular. Archaic sculpture featured the *kouros* (standing nude male youth) and *kore* (standing draped female), characterized by the "archaic smile." Classical sculpture achieved greater naturalism and depicted real individuals, as seen in the Parthenon Marbles. Hellenistic sculpture became more expressive, emotional, and diverse, introducing multi-figure groups and genre subjects. Materials included marble, bronze (high-status but rarely surviving), and chryselephantine (gold and ivory) for cult statues. Most sculpture was originally painted in bright colors (polychromy).

### Architecture
Greek architecture, primarily in stone, used a post-and-lintel system. It developed three architectural orders: Doric (austere), Ionic (decorative), and Corinthian (ornate). Temples were the most common surviving structures, built from limestone or marble. Public buildings like stoas, theatres, and agoras were central to civic life. The vocabulary of architectural ornament, including motifs like the meander and palmette, was shared with other arts and had a lasting global influence.

### Painting and Other Media
Panel and wall painting were highly valued but almost entirely lost. Knowledge comes from literary descriptions, copies, and vase painting. Techniques included encaustic (wax) and tempera. Mosaics, initially using pebbles and later tesserae, became popular in the Hellenistic period. Fine metalwork, including vessels and jewelry, was expertly crafted but mostly survives from the edges of the Greek world. Engraved gems were a luxury art, evolving from intaglio seals to relief cameos.

## Terms
- **Kouros/Kore**: Archaic statues of a standing nude male youth (*kouros*) or a standing draped female (*kore*).
- **Polychromy**: The practice of painting sculpture and architecture in multiple colors, common in antiquity but now largely faded.
- **Chryselephantine**: A technique for cult statues using gold (for garments/hair) and ivory (for flesh) over a wooden frame.
- **Doric/Ionic/Corinthian**: The three classical orders of architecture, distinguished by column design and ornament.
- **Black-figure/Red-figure**: The two primary techniques of Greek vase painting, where figures are painted in black slip or left in red on a black background, respectively.
- **Terracotta**: Fired clay, used for figurines, architectural decorations, and some large sculptures.
- **Ekphrasis**: A literary description of a work of art, a key source for lost Greek paintings.
- **Orientalizing**: A late 8th-century BC period influenced by Near Eastern motifs, preceding the full Archaic style.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The social function of pottery is debated: was most production for daily use or primarily for burial as grave goods?
- The relationship between vase painting and other arts is contested; some scholars now view it as a secondary medium copying lost metalwork designs.
- The existence and characteristics of a distinct "Alexandrian style" in Hellenistic sculpture are questioned.
- The extent of continuity between Bronze Age (Minoan/Mycenaean) wall painting and later Greek traditions is unclear.
