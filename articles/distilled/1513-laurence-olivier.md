# Laurence Olivier

## Overview
Laurence Olivier (1907–1989) was an English actor and director who, alongside John Gielgud, Ralph Richardson, and Michael Redgrave, dominated the mid-20th-century British stage. Over a six-decade career, he excelled in theatre, film, and television, creating a distinctive technique built on physical transformation and external detail ("working from the outside in"). He co-directed the Old Vic (1944–1948), founding a legendary company, and served as the inaugural director of the National Theatre (1963–1973), establishing it as a permanent state-subsidized institution. His film legacy includes a Shakespearean trilogy as actor-director (*Henry V*, *Hamlet*, *Richard III*) and acclaimed roles in *Wuthering Heights*, *Rebecca*, *Spartacus*, *The Shoes of the Fisherman*, and *Marathon Man*. He received a knighthood (1947), life peerage (1970), Order of Merit (1981), an Academy Award for *Hamlet*, two honorary Oscars, five Emmys, and four Golden Globes.

## Body

### Early Life and Theatrical Awakening
Born into a clerical family with no theatrical connections, Olivier was pushed toward acting by his father, a dramatic preacher whose rhetorical technique the boy studied closely. After a nomadic childhood, he attended All Saints, Margaret Street, where the Anglo-Catholic ritual appealed to his theatrical instinct; Ellen Terry predicted his greatness after seeing him as Brutus aged ten. His father enrolled him at the Central School of Speech Training and Dramatic Art (1924), where he won a scholarship but was an indifferent student. His professional apprenticeship came at the Birmingham Repertory Company (1926–1928), which he called his "university"; there he played diverse leads and forged a decisive friendship with Ralph Richardson.

### West End Stardom and Hollywood Struggles (1930–1938)
Noël Coward cast Olivier in *Private Lives* (1930), providing his first major West End success and mentoring him in discipline and literature. A brief, unhappy Hollywood contract (1931) saw him mimic Ronald Colman; he returned to London, where John Gielgud cast him in a record-breaking *Romeo and Juliet* (1935). Their friendship remained prickly, fueled by Olivier's resentment of critics favoring Gielgud's verse-speaking. In 1936, Olivier joined the Old Vic, playing Hamlet, Henry V, and Macbeth. In 1938, his Coriolanus was hailed as his "first incontestably great performance" by Robert Speaight; it was his last appearance on a London stage for six years. Simultaneously, he began an affair with Vivien Leigh while married to Jill Esmond, leading to both divorces and their marriage in 1940.

### War Years and the Shakespearean Film Trilogy
Lured by $50,000, Olivier played Heathcliff in *Wuthering Heights* (1939), learning to strip away theatrical artifice for screen realism under William Wyler. He followed with *Rebecca* (1940) and *Pride and Prejudice* (1940). During WWII, he served in the Fleet Air Arm and made propaganda films (*That Hamilton Woman*, *49th Parallel*, *The Demi-Paradise*). At the Ministry of Information's behest, he directed, produced, and starred in *Henry V* (1944), filmed in neutral Ireland. Released late in the war, it became a patriotic touchstone, earning an honorary Oscar for his "outstanding achievement as actor, producer and director."

### The Old Vic Triumvirate (1944–1948)
Released from naval service, Olivier, Richardson, and John Burrell rebuilt the bomb-damaged Old Vic into the "most famous theatre in the Anglo-Saxon world." Olivier's Richard III (1944) became the benchmark for the role for forty years. In a celebrated double bill (1945), he switched from the searing tragedy of *Oedipus Rex* to the farce of *The Critic* in one evening. The company toured Germany and played the Comédie-Française. However, the new chairman, Lord Esher, resented the stars' fame and dismissed the triumvirate in 1948, delaying the National Theatre's creation by a decade, according to biographers.

### Post-War Actor-Manager and *Richard III* Film
Olivier formed Laurence Olivier Productions, staging the English premiere of *A Streetcar Named Desire* (1949) with Leigh as Blanche—a role Gielgud feared mirrored her fragility. A lease on the St James's Theatre brought commercial successes (*Caesar and Cleopatra*, *Antony and Cleopatra*) but critical disappointment; Darlington judged Olivier miscast and "lost interest in his own acting." He directed his third Shakespeare film, *Richard III* (1955), assembling an "All-Sir Cast" (Gielgud, Richardson, Hardwicke). Critically bold but a box-office failure, it ended his plans for a *Macbeth* film.

### The *Entertainer* Pivot and Royal Court (1957–1963)
Stagnating in classical revivals, Olivier was revitalized by John Osborne's *The Entertainer* (1957). Playing the seedy comedian Archie Rice, he accessed "moments of the most wrenching pathos" behind a tacky façade, feeling "like a modern actor again." He met Joan Plowright during the run. He followed with a ferocious *Coriolanus* (1959) at Stratford and a disastrous *Rhinoceros* (1960) at the Royal Court, where he undermined director Orson Welles. He directed the Chichester Festival (1961–1963), achieving a celebrated *Uncle Vanya*, proving his administrative skill ahead of the National Theatre appointment.

### The National Theatre Directorship (1963–1973)
Appointed inaugural director, Olivier built a resident company at the Old Vic pending the South Bank building. He fostered future stars (Gambon, Smith, Hopkins, Jacobi) but was accused of "paranoid jealousy" toward peers; Gielgud, Richardson, and Ashcroft rarely appeared. He acted in thirteen plays, directing eight. His *Othello* (1965) divided critics: praised as an "anthology of acting" by Zeffirelli, condemned as "self-parody" and condescending by others. The role triggered his first bout of stage fright. His final Shakespearean stage role, Shylock (1970), drew polarized reviews. Bureaucratic delays kept the new building unbuilt until after his tenure; Peter Hall succeeded him in 1973 amid tactless board handling. The largest auditorium in the new complex was named the Olivier Theatre.

### Later Career, Honours, and Death
Plagued by thrombosis and dermatomyositis (a degenerative muscle disorder), Olivier shifted to cameo film roles (*Sleuth*, *Marathon Man*, *The Boys from Brazil*) and television (*The Moon and Sixpence*, *Long Day's Journey into Night*, *Love Among the Ruins*, *Cat on a Hot Tin Roof*, *A Little Romance*, *Jesus of Nazareth*, *Brideshead Revisited*, *King Lear*, *Wagner*, *War Requiem*). His TV *Lear* (1983) was hailed as a "breathtakingly pure" finale. He received a knighthood (1947), life peerage (1970), Order of Merit (1981), ten competitive Oscar nominations (winning Best Actor for *Hamlet*), two honorary Oscars, five Emmys, and four Golden Globes. The Laurence Olivier Awards were established in 1984. He died in 1989; his ashes rest in Poets' Corner.

### Technique and Reputation
Olivier's method was "working from the outside in": he relied on elaborate makeup, false noses, wigs, and distinct voices/accents, building characters from "a mass of tiny details." Critics like James Agate argued this displayed the "mechanism" rather than the "time of day." Kenneth Tynan contrasted him with Gielgud: "the best actor in the world from the neck up and Olivier from the neck down." Olivier distrusted Method acting, clashing with Marilyn Monroe and jokingly advising Dustin Hoffman to "try acting." Colleagues debated his greatness: Peter Hall thought Richardson the greater actor, but William Redfield called Olivier "the definitive actor of the twentieth century" due to "dedication, scholarship, practice, determination and courage." Hall ultimately deemed him "the supreme man of the theatre of our time" for founding the National Theatre.

## Terms
- **Old Vic**: A London theatre (south of the Thames) specializing in Shakespeare under Lilian Baylis; base for Olivier's triumvirate company (1944–1948) and the nascent National Theatre (1963–1976).
- **Triumvirate**: The three-headed management of the Old Vic (1944–1948) comprising Olivier, Ralph Richardson, and stage director John Burrell.
- **"Working from the outside in"**: Olivier's self-described technique: constructing a character through external physical details (makeup, voice, walk, costume) rather than internal psychological identification.
- **English Stage Company**: Avant-garde company at the Royal Court; produced John Osborne's *Look Back in Anger* and *The Entertainer*, reviving Olivier's stage career in 1957.
- **National Theatre**: Britain's state-subsidized flagship theatre company; Olivier was its founding director (1963–1973), based at the Old Vic before moving to the South Bank complex (opened 1976).
- **Dermatomyositis**: A rare inflammatory degenerative muscle disorder causing weakness and rash; plagued Olivier's final 15 years, limiting his ability to insure for long film shoots.
- **Shakespearean Film Trilogy**: Olivier's three films as actor-director: *Henry V* (1944), *Hamlet* (1948), *Richard III* (1955). *Hamlet* won Best Picture and Best Actor Oscars.
- **Life Peerage**: A non-hereditary title (Baron Olivier of Brighton, 1970) granting a seat in the House of Lords; Olivier sat as a crossbencher.
- **Order of Merit (OM)**: A personal gift of the monarch limited to 24 living recipients for "exceptionally meritorious service"; awarded to Olivier in 1981.
- **Laurence Olivier Awards**: Annual awards for West End theatre established by the Society of London Theatre in 1984, named in his honour.

## Debates and open questions
- **Othello (1965) and race**: Critics and scholars remain divided on Olivier's blacked-up performance. Jonathan Miller called it a "condescending view of an Afro Caribbean person"; Franco Zeffirelli called it an "anthology of everything discovered about acting." The production is a flashpoint in debates on racial representation and "color-blind" vs. "color-conscious" casting.
- **Dismissal of the Old Vic Triumvirate (1948)**: Biographers (Bragg, Miller) argue Lord Esher's sacking of Olivier, Richardson, and Burrell—driven by personal ambition and resentment of their fame—set back the National Theatre's foundation by at least a decade. The governors claimed they sought a "more mediocre company spirit" (per *The Guardian*).
- **Olivier vs. Gielgud vs. Richardson**: Contemporaries and historians debate the "great trinity" hierarchy. Gielgud was seen as spiritual/intellectual ("neck up"), Olivier as physical/visceral ("neck down"), Richardson as instinctive/natural. Peter Hall rated Richardson higher; William Redfield rated Olivier highest for professional dedication.
- **Method Acting vs. Technical Craft**: Olivier's public dismissal of Stanislavski/Method ("don't bring him onto the film set") and clashes with Monroe/Hoffman frame a transatlantic debate on acting pedagogy: external transformation vs. internal psychological immersion.
- **National Theatre Directorship**: Olivier's reluctance to cast peers (Gielgud, Richardson, Scofield) is viewed either as necessary focus on a resident ensemble or as "paranoid jealousy" (Robert Stephens). The board's handling of his succession (Hall appointed without consultation) remains a case study in institutional governance.
