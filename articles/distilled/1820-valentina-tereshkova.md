# Valentina Tereshkova

## Overview

Valentina Tereshkova (born 6 March 1937) is a Russian engineer, politician, and former Soviet cosmonaut who became the first woman in space on 16 June 1963, flying a solo mission aboard Vostok 6. She remains the only woman to have flown a solo space mission and the youngest woman to orbit Earth, at age 26. Her flight was a major Soviet propaganda victory in the Space Race and paved the way for future female spacefarers, though no Soviet woman would fly again until 1982. Tereshkova later became a prominent political figure in the Soviet Union and, after its collapse, a member of Russia's ruling United Russia party.

## Body

### Early Life

Tereshkova was born in the village of Bolshoye Maslennikovo, 270 km northeast of Moscow, to parents who had migrated from Belarus. Her father, a former tractor driver and tank sergeant, died in the Finnish Winter War when she was two. Her mother moved the family to Yaroslavl, where she worked at a cotton mill. Tereshkova began school at 8, graduated at 16, and worked at a tire factory and textile mill while taking correspondence courses, graduating from the Light Industry Technical School in 1960. She trained in skydiving at a local Aeroclub, keeping this secret from her family, and joined the Komsomol (Communist Youth League), serving as its secretary in 1960–1961.

### Selection and Training

Tereshkova had not expressed a desire to go to space; her skydiving experience was the key qualification. After Yuri Gagarin's 1961 flight, training director Nikolai Kamanin learned that American women were training as astronauts (Mercury 13) and wrote that the first woman in space must not be American. Five female cosmonauts were selected from 400 candidates screened by DOSAAF (the Soviet volunteer military-support society), with requirements including being a parachutist under 30, under 170 cm tall, and under 70 kg. Tereshkova was chosen on 16 February 1962. The women began training before the men to increase Soviet chances of launching first. Training included isolation, centrifuge, thermo-chamber, and decompression tests, plus MiG-15UTI jet fighter piloting and water recovery drills. They accepted commissions as officers on advice from male cosmonauts, making it harder to dismiss them. By December 1962, all five were junior lieutenants. Illness and poor performance eliminated two candidates, leaving Tereshkova, Irina Solovyova, and Valentina Ponomaryova. An original plan for two women flying on consecutive days was altered; Vostok 5 would carry male cosmonaut Valery Bykovsky, and Tereshkova was nominated for Vostok 6 on 21 May 1963. Kamanin called her "Gagarin in a skirt," and Premier Khrushchev confirmed her selection, pleased with her propaganda value as the daughter of a war-dead collective farm worker.

### Vostok 6 Flight

Vostok 6 launched faultlessly on 16 June 1963, two days after Vostok 5. Tereshkova's call sign was "Chaika" (Seagull), later given to asteroid 1671 Chaika. The two spacecraft came within 5 km of each other during her first orbit and communicated by radio; onboard cameras broadcast live footage on Soviet state television. Tereshkova photographed the horizon, later used to identify atmospheric aerosol layers. She logged more flight time than all American astronauts combined up to that date. Though she experienced nausea and discomfort, she orbited Earth 48 times in 2 days, 22 hours, and 50 minutes. As planned for all Vostok missions, she ejected from the capsule about four miles above Earth and parachuted down, landing 620 km northeast of Karaganda, Kazakhstan, on 19 June. She encountered violent wind gusts but landed safely, bruising her nose, and dined with local villagers who helped her out of her spacesuit.

### After the Flight

Tereshkova received a hero's welcome in Moscow, with Khrushchev awarding her the Hero of the Soviet Union medal and the Order of Lenin. She spoke from Lenin's Tomb, referencing her father's death in war. She requested that the government locate and publish where her father was killed; a monument was erected at the site in Lemetti, Karelia. She made 42 trips abroad between 1963 and 1970, meeting Fidel Castro in Cuba and Elizabeth II in 1964 while both were pregnant. She became a member of the World Peace Council (1966), the Supreme Soviet (1966–1974), the Central Committee (1969–1991), and the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet (1974–1989). Despite wanting to continue as a cosmonaut and engineer, her superiors appointed her leader of the Committee for Soviet Women in 1968, unwilling to risk another hero after Gagarin's death. The female cosmonaut corps was disbanded in 1969; no woman flew again until Svetlana Savitskaya in 1982. Tereshkova earned a doctorate in aeronautical engineering in 1977 and re-qualified for spaceflight but never flew again, remaining an instructor at the Yuri Gagarin Cosmonaut Training Center.

### Later Political Career

After the Soviet collapse, Tereshkova lost State Duma elections in 1995 and 2003 but was elected to the Yaroslavl Oblast Duma in 2008. In 2011, she was elected to the national State Duma for United Russia, supporting amendments to add "Orthodoxy is the basis of Russia's national and cultural identity" to the Constitution's preamble—a shift from her Soviet-era atheist views. She was re-elected in 2016 and 2021. In 2022, she voted for the Russian invasion of Ukraine, leading to sanctions from the US Treasury (September 2022) and the European Union (December 2022), freezing her assets and banning her from EU states. During drafting of the 2020 constitutional amendments, she proposed lifting presidential term limits for Vladimir Putin.

### Personal Life

Tereshkova married cosmonaut Andriyan Nikolayev on 3 November 1963, a marriage encouraged by space authorities as a "fairy-tale message to the country." Their daughter Elena, born 8 June 1964, was the first child of two space travelers. The marriage deteriorated; the couple divorced in 1982, and Tereshkova married surgeon Yuli Shaposhnikov, whom she had met during her medical re-qualification exams. They remained married until his death in 1999.

### Legacy

Numerous places and objects bear Tereshkova's name, including a square in Tver, a lunar crater on the far side of the Moon (1970), a planetarium in Yaroslavl (2011), and a museum near Yaroslavl (1975). Fashion designer Pierre Cardin credited photos of Tereshkova in her spacesuit for inspiring his Space Age styles. She was named "greatest woman achiever of the 20th century" by the International Women of the Year association and carried torches in the 2008 and 2014 Olympic relays. Following her support for Russia's actions in Ukraine, streets in Ukraine bearing her name were renamed under the 2015 decommunisation law; by 2023, no objects named after her remained in Ukraine, and a monument in Lviv was dismantled in November 2023 and transferred to the Museum of Totalitarian Regimes.

## Terms

- **Vostok programme**: The Soviet human spaceflight project that launched the first man and first woman into space; Vostok 6 was its final flight.
- **DOSAAF**: The All-Union Voluntary Society for Assistance to the Army, Air Force and Navy, which screened initial cosmonaut candidates.
- **Komsomol**: The Communist Youth League, in which Tereshkova served as secretary.
- **Hero of the Soviet Union**: The highest Soviet honor, awarded to Tereshkova after her flight.
- **Call sign**: A radio identifier; Tereshkova's was "Chaika" (Seagull).
- **State Duma**: The lower house of the Russian legislature, to which Tereshkova was elected in 2011, 2016, and 2021.
- **Decommunisation law**: Ukrainian legislation (2015) mandating removal of communist-era names and monuments, which led to renaming of streets honoring Tereshkova.
- **Specially Designated Nationals List**: A US Treasury sanctions list; Tereshkova was added in September 2022.
