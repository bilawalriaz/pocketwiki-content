# Kinshasa

## Overview
Kinshasa is the capital and largest city of the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), and one of the world's fastest-growing megacities. It is the leading economic, political, and cultural center of the nation, and its history reflects the broader colonial and post-colonial trajectory of the Congo, from a trading post to a sprawling metropolis marked by rapid growth, conflict, and resilience.

## Timeline
- **c. 1881** — Henry Morton Stanley establishes a trading post named Léopoldville.
- **1898** — Completion of the Matadi–Kinshasa portage railway, sparking rapid development.
- **1923** — Léopoldville becomes the capital of the Belgian Congo.
- **1960** — Congo gains independence; Léopoldville is the capital of the new nation.
- **1966** — President Mobutu renames Léopoldville to Kinshasa as part of an "Authenticity" campaign.
- **1991-1992** — Waves of looting severely damage the city's economic infrastructure.
- **1997** — Fall of Mobutu's regime; Kinshasa becomes the capital under Laurent-Désiré Kabila.
- **1998-2003** — The Second Congo War brings conflict and human rights abuses to the city.
- **2006** — A controversial presidential election triggers widespread unrest.
- **2015** — Kinshasa is designated a UNESCO City of Music.

## Body

### Origins and Colonial Development
The site was inhabited for centuries by Teke and Humbu people and was known as Nshasa. In 1881, Henry Morton Stanley established a trading post named Léopoldville on the banks of the Congo River. It flourished as the first navigable port above the Livingstone Falls. The completion of the Matadi–Kinshasa railway in 1898 and its elevation to colonial capital in 1923 accelerated its growth. Colonial urban planning enforced racial segregation, with separate "indigenous quarters" (*cités indigènes*) for Africans and a European administrative center in Kalina (now Gombe). A "neutral zone" buffer, now the Kinshasa Botanical Garden, was established in 1933 between the two areas.

### Post-Independence and the Mobutu Era
After independence in 1960, the city's population surged due to migration and political instability. In 1966, President Mobutu Sese Seko renamed it Kinshasa as part of a policy to renativize place names. The city grew rapidly, drawing people from across the country. Its economy, once robust with manufacturing and industry, began to decline in the 1970s and 1980s due to economic mismanagement and falling commodity prices.

### Conflict and Modern Challenges
The 1990s brought severe economic crisis, marked by devastating looting in 1991 and 1992. The First Congo War (1996-1997) ended with the fall of Mobutu, but the new regime under Laurent-Désiré Kabila was marked by human rights abuses, including extrajudicial killings and systematic torture in military prisons. The Second Congo War (1998-2003) saw Kinshasa become a battleground, with shelling from neighboring Brazzaville and rebel advances halted only by foreign military intervention. The city's infrastructure was severely damaged, and the informal economy became the primary source of employment for nearly 70% of the labor force. Today, Kinshasa faces challenges of rapid, often unplanned urban expansion, inadequate infrastructure, and social issues like crime and a large population of street children.

### Geography and Administration
Kinshasa covers 9,965 square kilometers on the southern bank of the Pool Malebo on the Congo River, forming a natural border with Brazzaville, the capital of the Republic of the Congo. The terrain is a mix of marshy alluvial plains and hilly areas. Administratively, it is both a city and one of the DRC's 26 provinces, divided into 24 communes. Over 90% of the province's land is rural, with urban growth concentrated in the west.

### Economy and Culture
Kinshasa generates approximately 85% of the DRC's GDP despite housing only about 13% of its population. The economy is dominated by the informal sector, with formal industry largely dismantled since the 1990s. The city is the world's largest Francophone urban area, with French as the official language and Lingala as a widespread lingua franca. It has a vibrant music scene, having launched the careers of many major figures of Congolese rumba, and was designated a UNESCO City of Music in 2015. Cultural trends like *La Sape* (a form of dandyism) are internationally recognized.

## Terms
*   **Cité indigène:** A segregated residential area for Africans established during the colonial period.
*   **Pool Malebo:** A large, lake-like expansion of the Congo River on which Kinshasa and Brazzaville are located.
*   **Lingala:** A Bantu language that serves as a major lingua franca in Kinshasa and the northwestern DRC.
*   **Zairianisation:** A policy of President Mobutu in the 1970s to renativize names and culture, including renaming the country Zaire and its capital Kinshasa.
*   **La Sape:** A cultural movement and fashion subculture in the Congo, characterized by extravagant dress.
*   **Informal sector:** The part of the economy not regulated or protected by the state, often involving unregistered jobs and small-scale trade.
*   **Commune:** A primary administrative subdivision of Kinshasa, of which there are 24.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact origin of the name "Kinshasa" is debated, with theories linking it to words for "salt bag" or "place of exchange" in local languages.
*   The city's rapid, largely unplanned expansion presents ongoing challenges for governance, service provision, and environmental sustainability.
*   The long-term impact of the massive Chinese infrastructure loan (2007-2008) on Kinshasa's development and local markets is a subject of analysis.
