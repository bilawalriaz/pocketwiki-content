# Hermann Weyl

## Overview
Hermann Weyl (1885–1955) was a German mathematician and theoretical physicist whose work profoundly influenced both fields. He is celebrated for unifying disparate areas of mathematics and physics, from the foundations of analysis and topology to the group-theoretic underpinnings of quantum mechanics. His career bridged the Göttingen tradition of mathematics with the new physics of relativity and quantum theory, making him one of the most influential mathematicians of the twentieth century.

## Timeline
- **1885** — Born in Elmshorn, Germany.
- **1904–1908** — Studies mathematics and physics at Göttingen and Munich; earns doctorate under David Hilbert.
- **1913** — Publishes *Die Idee der Riemannschen Fläche*; moves to ETH Zurich.
- **1918** — Publishes *Raum, Zeit, Materie* and introduces the concept of gauge theory.
- **1923–1938** — Develops the theory of compact groups and their representations.
- **1930** — Becomes Hilbert's successor at the University of Göttingen.
- **1933** — Leaves Germany for the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton due to Nazi rise.
- **1955** — Dies in Zürich, Switzerland.

## Body

### Mathematical Foundations and Analysis
Weyl began his career with work on the asymptotic distribution of eigenvalues, proving the Weyl law in 1911. In 1913, he provided a rigorous, topological foundation for Riemann surfaces in *Die Idee der Riemannschen Fläche*, a model for later manifold theory. He also made significant contributions to analytic number theory, developing criteria for uniform distribution mod 1 using exponential sums.

### Physics and Gauge Theory
Influenced by Albert Einstein, Weyl sought to unify gravity and electromagnetism geometrically. His 1918 book *Raum, Zeit, Materie* introduced the notion of a gauge, an unsuccessful attempt to model forces as properties of spacetime geometry. This concept later became fundamental to modern particle physics. He also proposed the Weyl equation in 1929 to describe massless fermions.

### Group Theory and Quantum Mechanics
From the 1920s onward, Weyl developed the representation theory of Lie groups, proving a fundamental character formula for compact groups. This work provided the mathematical framework for symmetry in quantum mechanics, including the treatment of spinors. His book *The Classical Groups* (1939) revolutionized invariant theory.

### Foundations of Mathematics and Philosophy
Weyl engaged deeply with the foundations of mathematics. In *Das Kontinuum* (1918), he developed a predicative analysis avoiding the axiom of choice and Cantor's infinite sets. He briefly adopted L. E. J. Brouwer's intuitionism, advocating a "revolution" in mathematics, but later found its restrictions too severe. By the end of his life, he viewed mathematics as a "symbolic construction," moving closer to a formalist position.

## Terms
- **Gauge theory:** A type of field theory where the Lagrangian is invariant under certain local transformations; Weyl's 1918 attempt to unify gravity and electromagnetism.
- **Weyl law:** A formula describing the asymptotic distribution of eigenvalues of the Laplacian on a compact domain.
- **Lie group:** A group that is also a differentiable manifold, with group operations that are smooth; fundamental for describing continuous symmetries.
- **Representation theory:** The study of abstract algebraic structures by representing their elements as linear transformations of vector spaces.
- **Intuitionism:** A philosophy of mathematics that asserts mathematical objects exist only if they can be constructed, rejecting the law of excluded middle.
- **Predicative analysis:** A form of analysis that avoids self-referential definitions, restricting the use of impredicative sets.
- **Weyl equation:** A relativistic wave equation describing massless fermions, a precursor to the Dirac equation.
- **Riemann surface:** A one-dimensional complex manifold; Weyl gave it a rigorous topological foundation.

## Debates and Open Questions
Weyl's philosophical journey was marked by significant shifts. He initially championed Brouwer's intuitionism against Hilbert's formalism, predicting a crisis in mathematics based on vague notions like real numbers. However, he later found intuitionism's restrictions "almost unbearable" and partially reconciled with Hilbert. His final position emphasized mathematics as a "symbolic construction." The ultimate foundations and meaning of mathematics remained, for him, an open question.
