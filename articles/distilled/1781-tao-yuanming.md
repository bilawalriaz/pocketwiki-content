# Tao Yuanming

## Overview

Tao Yuanming (365–427), also known as Tao Qian, was a Chinese poet of the Six Dynasties period and the foremost representative of Fields and Gardens poetry. He spent much of his life in reclusion, farming, writing poetry, and reflecting on his decision to withdraw from civil service. His simple, direct style contrasted with the literary norms of his time, yet from the Tang dynasty onward he was revered as a paragon of authenticity, and he is now regarded as one of the great world poets.

## Timeline

- **259–334** — Tao Kan, Tao Yuanming's great-grandfather, serves as a prominent Jin general and governor.
- **365** — Tao Yuanming is born in Chaisang (modern Jiujiang, Jiangxi); some scholars argue for 352.
- **383** — Battle of Fei River occurs during Tao's youth; Eastern Jin survives Former Qin invasion.
- **386** — Buddhist priest Huiyuan builds Donglin Monastery near Mountain Lu; the Tiger Creek incident occurs.
- **c. 394** — Tao begins his first government stint as State Officer of Rites, at about age 29.
- **397** — Emperor An is crowned; his disabilities leave real power with regents, beginning a period of corrupt rule.
- **403** — Huan Xuan forces Emperor An to abdicate and proclaims the Chu dynasty.
- **404/405** — Huan Xuan is killed in an uprising; Liu Yu restores Emperor An and rules as regent.
- **405** — Tao resigns his final post as Penze county magistrate after about 80 days, retiring to farming.
- **418/419** — Liu Yu has Emperor An assassinated and installs his brother as Emperor Gong.
- **419/420** — Liu Yu forces Gong to abdicate, ends the Jin dynasty, and founds the Liu Song dynasty.
- **427** — Tao Yuanming dies, generally held to be at age 63; if born in 352, he would have been 76.

## Body

### Names and identity

Tao changed his name in mid-life from Tao Yuanming to Tao Qian, meaning "hiding," a change thought to signal his final withdrawal from politics after the Eastern Jin's demise in 420. As a young man he used the self-invented sobriquet "Master of the Five Willows." During the Tang dynasty, the character *yuan* (渊) became taboo because it appeared in Emperor Gaozu's personal name, Li Yuan; writers substituted similar characters such as *shen* (深), leading to variant names like Shenming and Quanming.

### Political background and government service

Tao was born into the Eastern Jin dynasty (317–420), a state founded after the Western Jin collapsed when the Xiongnu and other "Five Barbarians" overran northern China. The dynasty was marked by nepotism, corruption, and violence, including the War of the Eight Princes (291–306). Tao served in government for over ten years across five stints, under the usurpers Huan Xuan and Liu Yu and the disabled Emperor An. His poems show growing tension between ambition and the desire for retreat. His final post, as Penze county magistrate in 405, lasted about eighty days; he resigned, reportedly saying he would not "bow like a servant in return for five pecks of grain," a phrase that entered common usage.

### Return to the fields

In spring 405, prompted by his sister's death and disgust at court corruption, Tao retired permanently to his small farmstead for the last 22 years of his life. He married twice; his first wife died when he was in his thirties. He had five sons, mentioned in his letter "A Letter to My Sons Yan, Etc.," which apologizes for the hardship his withdrawal caused them. His works show influences from Confucianism (he studied the Six Classics), Taoism (a desire to "return to Nature"), and Buddhism (lines like "Life is like an illusion; everything returns to emptiness"), though he never formally became a Buddhist.

### Works and legacy

Approximately 130 of Tao's works survive, mostly poems and essays depicting an idyllic pastoral life of farming and drinking. His poetry, termed "Fields and Gardens" poetry, urges withdrawal from official life and engagement with farming, while also showing a sense of duty to family. His simple style gained local fame first, then recognition in anthologies; by the Tang dynasty he was revered by poets like Li Bai and Du Fu. His prose piece "Peach Blossom Spring" (桃花源記) tells of a fisherman who discovers a utopian land hidden from the world but cannot find it again; the term *Tao Hua Yuan* became a standard Chinese word for utopia. His influence extended to Song dynasty poets like Su Shi and Xin Qiji, the 16th-century Korean poet Yi Hwang, and later to Beat poetry and 1960s American and European poetry.

### Critical appraisal

Zhong Rong (468–518) described Tao's style as "spare and limpid, with scarcely a surplus word." Su Shi (1037–1101) declared Tao the only poet he was particularly fond of, asserting that neither Cao Zhi, Liu Zhen, Bao Zhao, Xie Lingyun, Li Bai, nor Du Fu achieved his stature. Huang Tingjian (1045–1105) noted that reading Tao's poems in youth seems like "gnawing on withered wood," but after life experience, one realizes one's own decisions were made in ignorance. Lin Yutang (1895–1976) considered Tao "the most perfectly harmonious and well-rounded character in the entire Chinese literary tradition."

## Terms

- **Fields and Gardens poetry**: A genre of Chinese poetry celebrating rural life, of which Tao Yuanming is the foremost representative.
- **Six Dynasties period**: The era of Chinese history between the Han and Tang dynasties (roughly 220–589), marked by political fragmentation.
- **Five Barbarians (Wu Hu)**: Non-Han peoples whose uprisings overthrew Western Jin rule in northern China.
- **Yuefu**: A poetic form tracing its origin to the Han dynasty Music Bureau; a precursor to Tao's verse style.
- **Shi**: A regular line-length poetic form that Tao used, which foreshadowed Tang dynasty verse forms like *gushi* (old-style verse).
- **Peach Blossom Spring**: Tao's prose fable about a hidden utopia; the term became a standard Chinese word for utopia.
- **Wen Xuan**: A 6th-century literary anthology that included Tao's work, indicating his early fame.
- **Pure Land Buddhism**: A Buddhist tradition associated with Mountain Lu, where Huiyuan founded the Donglin Monastery.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate Tao Yuanming's birth year: the *Book of Jin* gives 365, but scholar Yuan Xingpei has argued for 352, which would make him 76 at death rather than 63. Versions of his biography in Chinese sources vary as to his name and age during historical events. Accounts of his second and third government stints also differ between sources, including Lu Qingli's *A Year-by-Year Biography of Tao Yuanming*. The exact circumstances of his resignation—whether due to his sister's death, disgust at court corruption, or the imminent imposition of an onerous supervisor—are variously reported.
