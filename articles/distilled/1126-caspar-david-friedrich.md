# Caspar David Friedrich

## Overview

Caspar David Friedrich (1774–1840) was a German Romantic landscape painter whose symbolic, emotionally charged works transformed landscape painting from a decorative backdrop into a vehicle for spiritual and existential contemplation. Born in Greifswald, he studied in Copenhagen before settling in Dresden, where he developed a style that used nature—misty mornings, barren trees, Gothic ruins, and silhouetted figures—to convey subjective, metaphysical responses to the world. His influence extended from 19th-century Romanticism through 20th-century Expressionism, Surrealism, and Abstract Expressionism, though his reputation suffered during periods of Nazi appropriation and modernist dismissal before a major revival from the 1970s onward.

## Body

### Early Life and Training

Friedrich was the sixth of ten children raised in a strict Lutheran household. His father, Adolf Gottlieb Friedrich, was a candle-maker and soap boiler. He experienced profound personal loss early: his mother died in 1781, two sisters in 1782 and 1791, and his brother Johann Christoffer drowned in 1787 after falling through ice—possibly while attempting to save Friedrich. These traumas deeply shaped his artistic sensibility.

He began formal art training in 1790 under Johann Gottfried Quistorp at the University of Greifswald, where he was introduced to theologian Ludwig Gotthard Kosegarten, who taught that nature reveals God. Quistorp also exposed him to Adam Elsheimer’s landscape-dominated religious works and nocturnal scenes. In Copenhagen (1794–1798), Friedrich studied under Christian August Lorentzen and Jens Juel, absorbing Dutch landscape traditions and Sturm und Drang influences.

### Career Development and Major Works

Settling in Dresden in 1798, Friedrich experimented with printmaking before focusing on ink, watercolor, and oil. Frequent trips to the Baltic coast, Bohemia, and the Harz Mountains informed his landscapes, which combined careful observation with memory-rendered atmospheric effects. His breakthrough came in 1805 when he won the Weimar competition with sepia drawings praised by Goethe.

In 1808, he completed *Cross in the Mountains*, his first major painting and one of his few religious commissions. Though controversial—for placing landscape in a sacred context—it established his approach of using nature to express spiritual themes. The work prompted Friedrich to articulate his philosophy: the artist must paint not only external reality but internal vision.

Political tensions influenced his career trajectory. After gaining favor with the Prussian Crown Prince, he surprisingly applied for Saxon citizenship in 1816, aligning with pro-French Saxon policies despite his anti-French artistic stance. His Germanic subjects clashed with prevailing pro-French sentiment, possibly hindering academic advancement.

### Artistic Themes and Innovations

Friedrich revolutionized landscape painting by making it a self-contained emotive subject rather than a backdrop for human drama. He pioneered the *Rückenfigur*—a figure seen from behind, contemplating the view—which invites viewers to identify with the figure and share in the sublime appreciation of nature. His concept of *die romantische Stimmungslandschaft* (romantic mood-landscape) infused scenery with psychological and spiritual resonance.

Recurring symbols included bare oak trees and stumps (death), crosses and clear skies (redemption), crescent moons (hope), and anchors on shorelines (spiritual salvation). Winter landscapes, rendered stark and lifeless, broke from tradition by omitting human activity, emphasizing nature’s solitude and stillness.

Political allegory appeared in works like *Old Heroes' Graves* (1812), referencing Arminius and fallen German heroes, and *Fir Forest with the French Dragoon and the Raven* (c. 1813), depicting a lost French soldier dwarfed by forest—a metaphor for anticipated defeat.

### Later Life and Decline

From the 1820s, Friedrich’s reputation declined as Romantic ideals fell out of fashion. He became increasingly reclusive, described as “the most solitary of the solitary.” Patronage dwindled, and by the 1830s he lived in relative poverty. A stroke in 1835 left him partially paralyzed, restricting him to watercolors and reworkings of earlier compositions. Despite continued support from Russian patrons—including the Grand Duke and poet Vasily Zhukovsky—he struggled financially until his death in 1840.

### Legacy and Reception

Friedrich’s influence spanned centuries. He inspired contemporaries like Johan Christian Dahl and later artists including Arnold Böcklin, Russian painters Arkhip Kuindzhi and Ivan Shishkin, and American luminaries such as Albert Pinkham Ryder. His rediscovery began in 1906 with a Berlin exhibition, influencing Expressionists, Surrealists, and Existentialists.

The Nazi regime’s appropriation of his work in the 1930s damaged his reputation, associating him with nationalist ideology. Critics like Kenneth Clark dismissed him as technically outdated. However, scholars such as Werner Hofmann and Helmut Börsch-Supan rehabilitated his standing by the 1970s, placing him within art-historical context rather than political narrative.

Modern reassessments recognize Friedrich as a master of psychological complexity—a believer haunted by doubt, a celebrant of beauty shadowed by darkness. His imagery continues to resonate across cultures, influencing Abstract Expressionists like Mark Rothko and inspiring writers such as Samuel Beckett.

## Terms

- **Rückenfigur**: A figure depicted from behind, typically contemplating a landscape, used to draw the viewer into shared contemplation.
- **Romantische Stimmungslandschaft**: Friedrich’s term for landscape imbued with romantic feeling and emotional atmosphere.
- **Sturm und Drang**: A German literary and artistic movement emphasizing emotional intensity and individual expression, influential during Friedrich’s Copenhagen studies.
- **Sublime**: An aesthetic concept involving awe, vastness, and transcendence, often evoked through nature in Romantic art.
- **Blut und Boden**: Nazi ideology emphasizing racial purity and connection to native soil; used to co-opt Romantic artists like Friedrich.
- **Tetschen Altar**: Another name for Friedrich’s 1808 painting *Cross in the Mountains*, created as an altarpiece for a Bohemian chapel.
- **Nocturne**: A painting or composition featuring night scenes, a genre Friedrich explored under Elsheimer’s influence.
- **Symbolism**: An artistic movement valuing allegory and inner meaning, which embraced Friedrich’s visionary landscapes in the late 19th century.
- **Abstract Sublime**: Term coined by Robert Rosenblum linking Romantic landscapes to Abstract Expressionist works exploring formless infinities.
- **Catalogue raisonné**: A comprehensive listing and documentation of an artist’s complete works, developed for Friedrich by later scholars.

## Debates and Open Questions

Interpretations of Friedrich’s personal psychology remain contested. While some view him as melancholic and isolated—reinforced by his reclusive later life and recurring motifs of death and solitude—others, like Hubertus Gassner, argue his work contains life-affirming elements rooted in Freemasonry and Christian mysticism.

The extent of Nazi instrumentalization versus genuine artistic merit remains debated. Though his imagery was exploited for nationalist propaganda, scholars continue to separate his original intent from later political misuse.

Questions persist regarding attribution and dating. Friedrich rarely titled or dated canvases, relying on a personal notebook now used by scholars. Some modern titles may reflect later interpretations rather than the artist’s own choices.

Finally, the precise influence of his early losses on his artistic development invites ongoing analysis. While trauma clearly shaped his themes, the degree to which biographical events directly correlate with specific symbolic choices remains open to interpretation.
