# American frontier

## Overview
The American frontier was the process of westward expansion across mainland North America, beginning with early 17th-century European settlements and ending with the admission of the last contiguous western territories as states in 1912. This era of migration and settlement, fueled by ideologies like "manifest destiny," profoundly shaped American culture, democracy, and national identity, with its legends and events becoming central to the nation's folklore through the Western genre.

## Timeline
- **1607** — Jamestown, Virginia, established as the first permanent English settlement.
- **1763** — Treaty of Paris ends French and Indian War; France cedes lands east of Mississippi to Britain.
- **1787** — Northwest Ordinance establishes a system for governing and admitting new western territories as states.
- **1803** — Louisiana Purchase doubles the size of the United States.
- **1848** — Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo ends Mexican-American War; Mexico cedes vast territories to the U.S.
- **1862** — Homestead Act grants free 160-acre plots to settlers; Pacific Railroad Act authorizes transcontinental railroad.
- **1869** — Transcontinental railroad completed at Promontory Summit, Utah.
- **1890** — U.S. Census Bureau declares the frontier line no longer exists.
- **1890** — Wounded Knee massacre marks the end of major Indian Wars.
- **1912** — Arizona and New Mexico admitted as states, the last of the contiguous territories.

## Body

### Periodization and the Frontier Thesis
Historians debate the exact dates of the frontier era, generally spanning from early colonial settlement to the early 20th century. A key sub-period is the "Old West" (c. 1865-1890). In 1893, historian Frederick Jackson Turner presented his influential "Frontier Thesis," arguing that the existence of a moving frontier of free land was the defining process of American civilization. It fostered democracy, individualism, and a unique national character by transforming European settlers into Americans. The U.S. Census Bureau defined "frontier" territory as having a population density of fewer than 2 people per square mile.

### Colonial and Early National Expansion
The frontier began as a zone of contact beyond coastal settlements. Different colonial powers (English, French, Spanish, Dutch) had distinct expansion patterns. After the American Revolution, the new nation organized western lands through policies like the Northwest Ordinance. Settlers pushed west of the Appalachians, often in conflict with Native American tribes. The Louisiana Purchase of 1803, secured by President Thomas Jefferson, opened the trans-Mississippi West and inspired exploration, such as the Lewis and Clark expedition.

### Manifest Destiny and Territorial Acquisition
The belief in "manifest destiny"—that the U.S. was destined to expand to the Pacific—drove aggressive expansion in the 1840s. The annexation of Texas (1845) and the subsequent Mexican-American War (1846-1848) resulted in the U.S. acquiring present-day California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, and parts of other states. The Oregon Trail became a major route for settlers heading to the Pacific Northwest. The California Gold Rush (1849) triggered a massive population boom in the West.

### The Trans-Mississippi West and the Indian Wars
The post-Civil War era saw rapid settlement of the Great Plains and Rocky Mountain region, facilitated by the Homestead Act and transcontinental railroads. This expansion led to intense conflict with Native American tribes, resulting in the Indian Wars. Key conflicts included Red Cloud's War, the Great Sioux War (featuring the Battle of the Little Bighorn), and the Apache Wars. The U.S. government established a system of reservations and military forts to control Native populations and protect settlers. The massacre at Wounded Knee (1890) is often cited as the symbolic end of this era of warfare.

### Society, Law, and the Cattle Industry
Frontier society was characterized by a mix of democracy, violence, and social flux. Cattle ranching became a major industry, with longhorn cattle driven north to railheads in "cowtowns" like Dodge City. These towns, along with mining camps, developed reputations for lawlessness, though historians note many had formal legal systems and gun control laws. Vigilante justice and range wars over land and grazing rights were common. The frontier also included diverse populations, such as African-American "Buffalo Soldiers," Chinese railroad workers, and Hispanic communities in the Southwest.

### End of the Frontier and Cultural Legacy
By 1890, the Census Bureau declared the frontier line closed, as settlement had become discontinuous. The last territories (Oklahoma, Arizona, New Mexico) achieved statehood by 1912. The frontier's history and mythology were heavily romanticized in dime novels, Wild West shows (like Buffalo Bill's), and later in the Western film genre. This "frontier myth" became a core part of American national identity, emphasizing themes of rugged individualism, freedom, and conflict between civilization and wilderness.

## Terms
- **Frontier Thesis**: Frederick Jackson Turner's 1893 theory that the frontier experience was the primary force shaping American democracy and character.
- **Manifest Destiny**: The 19th-century belief that the United States was divinely ordained to expand across the North American continent.
- **Homestead Act**: An 1862 law granting 160 acres of public land to settlers who improved and lived on it for five years.
- **Indian Wars**: A series of conflicts between the U.S. government/military and Native American tribes over land and resources in the 19th century.
- **Cattle Drive**: The long-distance movement of cattle on horseback from ranches in the Southwest to railheads in the Midwest.
- **Cowtown**: A town in the Great Plains that served as a center for the cattle trade, shipping, and entertainment for cowboys.
- **Vigilante**: A member of a self-appointed group that enforces law and order without legal authority, often through extrajudicial punishment.
- **Buffalo Soldiers**: Members of the all-African-American regiments of the U.S. Army stationed in the West after the Civil War.

## Debates and Open questions
- **Periodization**: Historians disagree on the exact start and end dates of the frontier era, with some citing 1890 (census declaration) and others 1912 (last statehood) as the end.
- **Turner's Thesis**: The Frontier Thesis remains highly debated. Critics argue it oversimplifies history, ignores the experiences of women and minorities, and downplays the role of the federal government and corporations.
- **New Western History**: Since the 1970s, a "New Western History" has challenged traditional narratives, focusing more on cultural conflict, environmental impact, and the experiences of Native Americans, Hispanics, and other marginalized groups rather than just white expansion.
- **Violence and Law**: The popular image of the "Wild West" as a place of constant gunfights and lawlessness is contested by historians who point to the prevalence of formal legal systems and relatively low homicide rates in many frontier communities.
