# Mechanical engineering

## Overview  
Mechanical engineering is the study and application of physical machines and mechanisms involving force and movement, integrating engineering physics, mathematics, and materials science to design, analyze, manufacture, and maintain mechanical systems. As one of the oldest and broadest engineering disciplines, it emerged formally during the 18th-century Industrial Revolution but has roots stretching back millennia. Today, it underpins technologies from robotics and medical devices to power generation and aerospace, continually expanding into fields like nanotechnology, mechatronics, and biomechanics.

## Body  

### Historical Foundations  
The application of mechanical principles can be traced to ancient civilizations. The six classic simple machines were known in the ancient Near East, with the wedge and inclined plane predating recorded history. The wheel was invented in Mesopotamia, though some sources suggest independent invention in Eastern Europe. Levers appeared around 3000 BC in the Near East, used in balance scales and Egyptian construction. Pulleys emerged in Mesopotamia by the early 2nd millennium BC. The Saqiyah, developed in the Kingdom of Kush during the 4th century BC, utilized animal power for water lifting. Bloomeries and blast furnaces appeared in Meroe during the 7th century BC. Water-powered machines like the water wheel originated in the Persian Empire by the 4th century BC. Greek contributions include Archimedes’ influence on mechanics and the Antikythera mechanism, an analog computer from the 2nd century BC. In China, innovations included Zhang Heng’s seismometer and improved water clocks, and Ma Jun’s differential gear chariot. Su Song integrated escapement mechanisms into his astronomical clock tower in the 11th century. During the Islamic Golden Age, Al-Jazari documented numerous mechanical devices in 1206. The 17th century saw breakthroughs with Huygens’ pendulum clock and Newton’s laws of motion and calculus, laying the mathematical groundwork for mechanical engineering science.

### Education and Professional Development  
Mechanical engineering programs typically span four to five years, leading to degrees such as B.Eng., B.Sc., or B.Tech. In the U.S., most programs are accredited by ABET to ensure standardized curricula. Canada follows CEAB accreditation, while Australia adheres to the Washington Accord via Engineers Australia. India requires a B.Tech. or B.E. degree, or an ITI trade certificate plus the All India Trade Test. Postgraduate options include Master of Engineering, Master of Science, and Doctor of Philosophy degrees, with the latter emphasizing research. Core coursework includes mathematics (calculus, differential equations, linear algebra), physics, chemistry, statics and dynamics, materials science, thermodynamics, fluid mechanics, heat transfer, machine design, manufacturing processes, and control systems. Specialized areas such as robotics, biomechanics, cryogenics, and automotive engineering are often offered as electives. Practical experience through internships, co-op programs, and research projects is common, especially in the U.S.

### Job Duties and Licensing  
Mechanical engineers research, design, develop, build, and test mechanical and thermal devices, including engines, tools, and machines. Their duties include analyzing problems, designing systems using CAD, prototyping, testing, refining designs, overseeing manufacturing, and managing teams. They work across industries—designing medical devices, batteries, power plants, HVAC systems, and transport vehicles. Licensing varies globally: in the U.S., engineers must pass the FE exam, gain four years of experience as an EIT, and pass the PE exam. In the UK, chartered status requires a master’s degree and peer-reviewed project. In Australia, registration as a Professional Engineer is mandatory in certain states. Licensed engineers are authorized to approve critical infrastructure designs, ensuring public safety and professional accountability.

### Subdisciplines and Core Principles  
Mechanics, the study of forces and their effects on matter, is foundational. Statics deals with stationary bodies under load; dynamics examines moving bodies and includes kinematics and kinetics. Mechanics of materials explores deformation under stress, while fluid mechanics studies fluid behavior. Structural analysis investigates failure modes—static and fatigue—and uses methods like finite element analysis (FEA) to predict performance. Thermodynamics governs energy transformations, with applications in engines, power plants, and HVAC systems. Thermal engineering integrates heat transfer and combustion science. Design and drafting rely on CAD and CAM tools to produce detailed technical drawings and manufacturing instructions. Mechatronics merges mechanical, electrical, and software engineering to create automated systems, exemplified by CD-ROM drives and industrial robots. Robotics applies mechatronics to build programmable machines for dangerous, precise, or repetitive tasks.

### Modern Tools and Research Frontiers  
Computer-aided engineering (CAE) suites enable virtual prototyping, simulation, and optimization, reducing reliance on physical prototypes. Tools like FEA and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model complex phenomena such as viscoelasticity and turbulent flow. On-demand platforms now provide remote access to specialized simulation expertise. Research areas include micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS), used in sensors and biomedical devices; friction stir welding, a solid-state joining technique for aerospace; composite materials offering high strength-to-weight ratios; and nanotechnology, exploring molecular-scale engineering. Biomechanics applies mechanical principles to biological systems, aiding prosthetics and tissue engineering. Acoustical engineering addresses noise control and sound quality in consumer and industrial products. These advancements reflect mechanical engineering’s ongoing evolution and interdisciplinary reach.

## Terms  
- **CAD (Computer-Aided Design)**: Software used to create 2D and 3D technical drawings and models.  
- **CAM (Computer-Aided Manufacturing)**: Systems that automate manufacturing processes using programmed instructions.  
- **CAE (Computer-Aided Engineering)**: Tools for simulation, analysis, and optimization of engineering designs.  
- **FEA (Finite Element Analysis)**: Computational method to estimate stress, strain, and deformation in structures.  
- **CFD (Computational Fluid Dynamics)**: Numerical simulation of fluid flow and heat transfer.  
- **HVAC (Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning)**: Systems regulating thermal comfort and indoor air quality.  
- **Statics**: Branch of mechanics dealing with forces on stationary bodies.  
- **Dynamics**: Study of forces and motion in moving bodies.  
- **Thermodynamics**: Science of energy transformation involving heat and work.  
- **Mechatronics**: Integration of mechanical, electronic, and software engineering for smart systems.

## Debates and Open Questions  
The origin of the wheel remains debated, with some sources attributing it solely to Mesopotamia and others suggesting independent invention in Eastern Europe. The extent of prehistoric knowledge of simple machines versus later formalization is also discussed. In modern times, the feasibility of molecular assemblers in nanotechnology remains speculative, representing an area of exploratory engineering rather than established practice.
