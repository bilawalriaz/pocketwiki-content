# Political party

## Overview
A political party is an organization that coordinates candidates to compete in elections and participate in governance, typically uniting members around shared ideological or policy goals. Political parties have become a central institution in nearly every modern country, structuring political competition in both democracies and autocracies, though democracies generally host more parties. Their emergence around the late 18th century transformed politics from elite-dominated contests into mass-mobilized systems, making them essential intermediaries between citizens and the state.

## Timeline
- **Ancient Greece** — Plato and Aristotle discuss political factions in Classical Athens
- **Late Roman Republic** — Populares and Optimates factions emerge
- **17th century** — Whig and Tory factions form in Britain after the Glorious Revolution
- **c. 1790s** — First modern party systems emerge in Britain and the United States
- **1848** — Revolutions across Europe accelerate spread of party-based politics
- **1950s–60s** — Rise of catch-all parties; decline of mass party membership begins
- **Post-1970s** — Cartel parties emerge with heavy state financing
- **Mid-20th century** — Decolonization brings party systems to newly independent states

## Body

### Definition and Structure
Political parties are collective entities that organize electoral competition under a shared label. In a narrow sense, they are the candidates running under a party banner; in a broader sense, they encompass the entire apparatus supporting those candidates—voters, volunteers, official organizations, and affiliated legislators. Parties are distinguished from parliamentary groups, factions, and advocacy groups primarily by their focus on electing candidates. Most countries legally define what qualifies as a political party.

Parties typically feature a leader (often becoming head of government if their party wins), party executives (secretary, chair), and general membership. Democratic parties usually select leaders through open, competitive processes, while autocratic parties tightly control succession. Party membership often involves dues, exclusivity agreements, and platform adherence.

### Historical Development
Modern political parties emerged in late 18th-century Britain and the United States. The UK's Conservative Party and the US Democratic Party are frequently cited as the world's oldest continuous parties. The First Party System in the US arose from 1790s debates over federal power, producing Federalists and Democratic-Republicans. By the 19th century, stable party systems developed in Europe, often organized around liberal-conservative divides or religious disputes, accelerated by the 1848 Revolutions.

The 20th century saw socialist parties disrupt traditional divides, and decolonization produced new party systems in Asia, Africa, and Latin America—often emerging from independence movements. Universal suffrage expanded party membership dramatically, solidifying parties' role as intermediaries between public and government.

### Causes of Political Parties
Three main theories explain party ubiquity. **Social cleavages** theory holds that parties emerge from pre-existing societal divisions (class, religion, ethnicity), organizing these into electoral competition. Critics argue this is empirically untestable and ignores parties' role in shaping cleavages themselves. **Incentive-based** explanations suggest parties coordinate candidates and legislators to prevent mutual harm and enable collective decision-making. **Psychological** theories propose parties serve as heuristics, allowing voters to simplify complex choices by evaluating parties rather than individual candidates—though party identification has weakened in recent decades.

### Types of Party Organizations
Political scientists distinguish several party types. **Elite parties** (19th century) had minimal organization, relied on wealthy donors, and prioritized electioneering over membership. **Mass parties** emerged with universal suffrage, mobilizing ordinary citizens, maintaining large memberships, and centralizing around ideological cleavages (e.g., German Social Democrats). **Catch-all parties** (1950s–60s) reduced ideological rigidity and member dependence to attract broader support. **Cartel parties** (post-1970s) rely heavily on state funding and marginalize members, functioning as "semi-state agencies." **Niche parties** focus on specific issues like environment or immigration, outside the traditional left-right spectrum. **Business-firm parties** center on individual political entrepreneurs rather than ideology.

### Party Systems
Party systems reflect the collective structure of parties within a country, influenced by electoral rules and legal constraints. **Non-partisan systems** ban or marginalize parties, as in Saudi Arabia or some local elections. **One-party systems** concentrate power in a single party, either through legal prohibition of others (Cuba, China) or dominant-party dynamics (Japan, South Africa). **Two-party systems** (US, UK) result from plurality electoral rules, as described by Duverger's law, though this relationship remains debated. **Multi-party systems** (India, Turkey, Brazil) arise from proportional representation, often producing coalition governments and greater representation, though potentially causing instability.

### Funding and Regulation
Party funding involves substantial sums—billions in major democracies—sourced from member dues, corporate donations, trade unions, self-funding candidates, and state allocations. Public funding aims to level electoral playing fields, while private funding advocates view donations as political expression. Regulations typically restrict donor eligibility, cap contributions and expenditures, and mandate financial transparency. Clientelism and vote-buying occur despite legal prohibitions.

### Ideology and Symbols
Parties adopt ideologies to signal policy intentions, differentiate from competitors, and persuade voters. Common ideologies include liberalism, conservatism, socialism, communism, nationalism, and environmentalism. Parties may organize along economic left-right axes, but also liberal-authoritarian, establishment-anti-establishment, or pluralist-anti-system dimensions. Non-ideological parties pursue patronage, clientelism, or individual advancement. Colors and symbols aid voter identification, with cross-national ideological color associations (though exceptions exist, notably US red/blue divisions).

## Terms
- **Catch-all party**: A party that broadens its ideology and reduces member dependence to attract wider support.
- **Cartel party**: A party that relies heavily on state funding and marginalizes members, functioning as a semi-state agency.
- **Duverger's law**: The observation that single-member district plurality systems tend to produce two-party systems.
- **Elite party**: A minimally organized party financed by wealthy donors, dominant before universal suffrage.
- **Mass party**: A party that mobilizes ordinary citizens, maintains large membership, and centralizes around ideological cleavages.
- **Niche party**: A party focused on specific issues outside the traditional left-right divide.
- **Party system**: The collective structure of parties within a country, shaped by electoral and legal institutions.
- **Social cleavages**: Pre-existing societal divisions (class, religion, ethnicity) that give rise to political parties.
- **Two-party-plus system**: A multi-party system where two dominant parties overshadow smaller ones.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate whether social cleavages truly cause parties or merely correlate with them, and whether parties instead shape cleavages over time. The empirical validity of Duverger's law remains contested, with some arguing institutional restrictiveness—not just electoral rules—determines party numbers. Whether multi-party systems are inherently more democratic than two-party systems is disputed, as is whether they produce better policy outcomes. The decline of mass party membership since the 1980s raises questions about parties' future intermediary role. Additionally, the extent to which catch-all and cartel parties have eroded democratic representation remains unresolved.
