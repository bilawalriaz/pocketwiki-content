# Textile

## Overview  
Textiles are fibre-based materials—fibres, yarns, filaments, threads, and fabrics—produced through methods like weaving, knitting, and bonding. They are divided into consumer textiles (prioritizing comfort and aesthetics) and technical textiles (prioritizing function). Their significance spans from basic clothing and shelter to advanced applications such as bulletproof vests, medical implants, and smart textiles with embedded sensors.

## Timeline  
- **c. 34,000 BCE** — Dyed flax fibres found in Georgia, earliest evidence of textile-like materials
- **c. 70,000 BCE** — First clothes likely made from animal skins
- **1765** — Spinning jenny invented in UK, beginning of textile industrialization
- **19th century** — Eisengarn (iron yarn) invented in Germany
- **1968** — Beta cloth, made from Teflon-coated glass fibre, used in U.S. space suits
- **1990s** — Nanotechnology introduced into textile finishing
- **2013** — Global textiles and apparel export market reached $772 billion |
- **2021** — Global fibre output rebounded to 113 million tons
- **2023** — Enzymes used to separate cotton from polyester for recycling

## Body  

### Definition and Classification  
Textiles encompass all fibre-based materials, including fibres, yarns, and fabrics. The term originally referred only to woven goods but now includes knitted, non-woven, and technical textiles. Consumer textiles emphasize aesthetics and comfort (e.g., clothing, furnishings), while technical textiles prioritize performance in applications like geotextiles, medical devices, and protective gear.

### Historical Development  
Textile production is among humanity’s oldest crafts. Early tools included spindles and whorls, later replaced by spinning wheels, possibly invented in China or India. Prehistoric textiles were made from plant fibres, animal skins, and felted materials. The Industrial Revolution transformed production: the spinning jenny (1765) marked the start of mechanization, followed by advances in weaving and finishing technologies.

### Fibre Sources and Properties  
Fibre is the smallest textile component, spun into yarn and woven or knitted into fabric. Natural fibres come from plants (cotton, flax, hemp), animals (wool, silk), or minerals (asbestos, basalt). Synthetic fibres, such as polyester and nylon, are made via chemical synthesis. Blends combine natural and synthetic fibres to enhance performance and reduce cost. Fibre properties—strength, flexibility, length-to-width ratio—determine the final product’s characteristics.

### Manufacturing and Finishing  
Textile manufacturing involves converting fibre into yarn, then into fabric via weaving, knitting, or bonding. Finishing processes—bleaching, dyeing, printing, and chemical treatments—add functionality and aesthetics. Techniques like batik, tie-dye, and woodblock printing create decorative patterns. Modern innovations include nanotechnology-based treatments for water resistance and wrinkle reduction.

### Applications and Significance  
Textiles serve diverse roles: clothing, upholstery, industrial filters, automotive components, and aerospace materials. Technical textiles include smart textiles with sensing or responsive capabilities. They are essential to daily life and critical in specialized fields like medicine and defense.

### Environmental and Health Impacts  
The textile industry is a major polluter, using vast amounts of water and releasing toxic dyes and chemicals. Synthetic fibres contribute to microplastic pollution. Health risks include respiratory issues from dyes and skin allergies from chemical finishes. Efforts to mitigate impact include enzymatic recycling and sustainable production methods.

## Terms  
- **Fabric**: A thin, flexible material made from yarn, fibres, or polymer films.  
- **Cloth**: Woven, knitted, or felted material, often referring to processed fabric.  
- **Greige goods**: Raw, unfinished textiles before processing.  
- **Piece goods**: Textiles sold in cut lengths, also called yard goods.  
- **Technical textiles**: Textiles designed for performance rather than appearance.  
- **Smart textiles**: Fabrics with integrated sensors, communication, or responsive functions.  
- **Spandex (Lycra)**: A stretchable synthetic fibre used in activewear.  
- **Beta cloth**: A fire-resistant fabric made from Teflon-coated glass fibre, used in space suits.  
- **Eisengarn**: A strong, lustrous yarn treated with starch and wax, invented in 19th-century Germany.  
- **Serviceability**: A textile’s ability to meet consumer needs in durability, comfort, and safety.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- The origin of the spinning wheel is debated, with claims pointing to either China or India.  
- The exact timeline of early human clothing remains uncertain, though evidence suggests use of animal skins at least 70,000 years ago.  
- The sustainability of synthetic fibres versus natural alternatives is an ongoing concern, particularly regarding microplastic emissions and recyclability.  
- The health impacts of formaldehyde and flame retardants in finished textiles require further study and regulation.
