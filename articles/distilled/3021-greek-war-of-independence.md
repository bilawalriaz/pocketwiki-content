# Greek War of Independence

## Overview
The Greek War of Independence (1821–1829) was a successful revolution by Greek nationalists against the Ottoman Empire, leading to the creation of an independent Greek state. It was a pivotal event in the decline of Ottoman power in Europe and the rise of modern nationalism, inspiring other subject peoples and drawing significant international attention and intervention.

## Timeline
- **1814** — Filiki Eteria (Society of Friends) founded to liberate Greece.
- **21 Feb 1821** — Revolt begins in the Danubian Principalities under Alexander Ypsilantis.
- **17 Mar 1821** — Maniots declare war on the Ottomans in the Peloponnese.
- **25 Mar 1821** — Traditional date for the start of the revolution in the Peloponnese.
- **Sep 1821** — Greeks capture Tripolitsa, a major Ottoman stronghold.
- **1822** — Massacre of Chios by Ottoman forces shocks Europe.
- **1825** — Ibrahim Pasha of Egypt lands in the Peloponnese, turning the tide for the Ottomans.
- **20 Oct 1827** — Allied (British, French, Russian) fleets destroy the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet at the Battle of Navarino.
- **1828** — French expeditionary force expels Egyptian troops from the Peloponnese.
- **3 Feb 1830** — The London Protocol recognizes Greece as an independent state.
- **1832** — Treaty of Constantinople defines Greece's borders and establishes Prince Otto as king.

## Body

### Background and Outbreak
Greek territory had been under Ottoman rule since the 15th century. Throughout the 18th and early 19th centuries, Greek national consciousness grew, influenced by the European Enlightenment and the French Revolution. Intellectuals like Rigas Feraios and Adamantios Korais promoted ideas of liberty and national identity. In 1814, the secret society Filiki Eteria was founded to organize a revolt. The revolution began in early 1821, with simultaneous uprisings in the Danubian Principalities (led by Alexander Ypsilantis) and the Peloponnese. The initial revolt in the Principalities was quickly crushed, but the uprising in the Peloponnese, led by figures like Theodoros Kolokotronis, gained control of the countryside.

### The War and Civil Strife
The early Greek successes were followed by a period of internal conflict and devastating Ottoman reprisals, including the execution of the Ecumenical Patriarch in Constantinople and the massacre on the island of Chios. The Greek war effort was hampered by factionalism, leading to two civil wars between 1823 and 1825. The Ottoman Empire, struggling to suppress the revolt, enlisted the powerful vassal Muhammad Ali of Egypt. In 1825, Ibrahim Pasha landed in the Peloponnese with a modern, well-trained army, bringing the revolution to the brink of collapse. He captured key cities and besieged Missolonghi, whose fall in 1826 after a heroic siege became a powerful symbol of Greek resistance in Europe.

### International Intervention and Independence
The Greek cause attracted widespread sympathy in Europe, known as Philhellenism, fueled by the perceived link to classical antiquity and reports of Ottoman atrocities. Financial and volunteer support flowed to the Greeks. Initially, the conservative Great Powers (Britain, France, Russia) opposed the revolution, but shifting public opinion and diplomatic calculations led to intervention. In 1827, Britain, France, and Russia signed the Treaty of London, demanding an armistice. When the Ottomans refused, their combined fleets annihilated the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet at the Battle of Navarino. This decisive action, followed by a French expedition that expelled Ibrahim's forces and a Russo-Turkish war, forced the Ottoman Empire to concede. Greek independence was formally recognized by the London Protocol of 1830, and its borders were defined in 1832.

## Terms
*   **Filiki Eteria:** A secret organization founded in 1814 with the aim of liberating Greece from Ottoman rule.
*   **Klephts:** Christian bandits who operated in the mountains, defying Ottoman authority and becoming symbols of resistance.
*   **Armatoloi:** Christian militias recruited by the Ottomans to police certain areas, often former klephts.
*   **Phanariotes:** A class of Greek elites from the Phanar district of Constantinople who held high offices in the Ottoman bureaucracy and Orthodox Church.
*   **Philhellenism:** The sympathy and support for the Greek cause shown by many Western Europeans, inspired by classical heritage and romantic nationalism.
*   **Millets:** The Ottoman system of granting autonomous religious communities (like the Orthodox Christian Rum millet) limited self-governance.
*   **Megali Idea:** The nationalist concept of uniting all Greek-inhabited territories into a single Greek state, which dominated Greek foreign policy for decades after independence.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The historicity of the traditional story of Metropolitan Germanos raising the banner of revolution at the Monastery of Agia Lavra on 25 March 1821 is debated by historians.
*   The extent to which the "barbarisation project"—the alleged Ottoman-Egyptian plan to deport the Greek population—was a real plan or a propaganda tool used to spur European intervention remains unclear.
*   Scholars debate whether massacres committed by Greek revolutionaries were retaliatory responses to prior Ottoman actions or simultaneous atrocities that marked the violent birth of the new nation.
