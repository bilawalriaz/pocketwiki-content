# Children's literature

## Overview
Children's literature encompasses stories, books, and poems created for young readers, ranging from picture books for the very young to young adult fiction. It serves to entertain, instruct, and socialize children, reflecting and shaping cultural values. Its development is tied to evolving concepts of childhood, from didactic moral instruction to imaginative entertainment, and it remains a field marked by ongoing debates about representation, ideology, and its impact on development.

## Timeline
- **1658** — John Amos Comenius publishes *Orbis Pictus*, considered the first picture book for children.
- **1697** — Charles Perrault publishes his first collection of fairy tales in France.
- **1744** — Mary Cooper publishes *Tommy Thumb's Pretty Song Book*, the first known nursery rhyme collection.
- **c. 1744-1760s** — John Newbery publishes *A Little Pretty Pocket-Book*, widely considered the first modern children's book aimed at entertainment.
- **1812** — Johann David Wyss publishes *The Swiss Family Robinson*.
- **1865** — Lewis Carroll's *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* is published, signaling a shift to imaginative, child-oriented literature.
- **1902** — Beatrix Potter publishes *The Tale of Peter Rabbit*, pioneering character merchandising and integrated text-and-image storytelling.
- **1922** — The American Library Association awards the first Newbery Medal for children's literature.
- **1937** — J.R.R. Tolkien publishes *The Hobbit*.
- **1950** — C.S. Lewis publishes *The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe*, the first in *The Chronicles of Narnia*.
- **1963** — Maurice Sendak's *Where the Wild Things Are* is published, emblematic of a new realism.
- **1997** — J.K. Rowling's *Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone* is published, launching a globally dominant series.

## Body

### Definition and History
Children's literature has no single definition but is broadly works intended to entertain or instruct young people. Its origins lie in oral traditions like fairy tales and songs shared with children before printing. The modern genre emerged in the 18th century alongside the developing concept of childhood, influenced by philosophers like John Locke (theory of *tabula rasa*, or blank slate) and Jean-Jacques Rousseau (natural development). Early works were often didactic, such as *The New England Primer* (1691). The "Golden Age" (late 19th/early 20th centuries) saw classics like *Alice in Wonderland* and *Peter Pan* that prioritized imagination over moralizing.

### National Traditions
Literature developed distinct national traditions. In the UK, the Victorian era produced classics like *Black Beauty* (1877) and the modern picture book, with illustrators like Beatrix Potter. The US saw growth after the Civil War, with works like *Little Women* (1868) and the establishment of major awards like the Newbery Medal (1922). In Russia, the state heavily controlled children's literature after the 1917 Revolution, promoting collectivist themes. Other significant traditions flourished in Scandinavia (e.g., Astrid Lindgren), Germany, and later in post-colonial nations like Nigeria and India, where writers adapted traditional oral forms.

### Classification and Illustration
Literature is classified by genre (e.g., picture books, fantasy, non-fiction) or intended age (e.g., picture books for ages 0–8, young adult for 12–18). Illustration is integral, especially for younger readers. The first illustrated children's book is considered *Orbis Pictus* (1658). Techniques evolved from woodcuts to chromolithography in the 19th century, with illustrators like Randolph Caldecott extending the meaning of the text. Modern printing allowed for full-color, painterly styles.

### Literary Criticism and Controversies
Criticism focuses on content, stereotypes, and ideology. Debates center on whether to alter or contextualize outdated racial stereotypes in classics like *The Secret Garden* or *Pippi Longstocking*. Scholars analyze imperialism and colonialism in works like *Babar* and *Curious George*. Gender representation is a major concern; studies show male characters have historically outnumbered female characters, and roles are often stereotyped. Critics like Kimberley Reynolds argue children's literature is never neutral, embedding cultural values and constructing concepts of childhood.

### Impact and Diversity
Children's books are critical for language, cognitive, social, and emotional development. However, diversity in publishing remains a key issue. Statistics from the US and UK show underrepresentation of BIPOC, LGBTQIA+, and disabled characters and creators, though recent years have seen incremental improvements and advocacy movements like We Need Diverse Books.

## Terms
- **Tabula rasa**: Locke's theory that the human mind is born a "blank slate," shaped by experience.
- **Golden Age**: The late 19th and early 20th centuries, noted for a flourishing of classic children's literature.
- **Didactic**: Intended to teach, particularly in moral or religious instruction.
- **Picture book**: A book where illustrations are as important as the text, typically for pre-readers.
- **Young adult (YA)**: Fiction written for readers aged approximately 12 to 18.
- **Chapbook**: A small, inexpensive pamphlet of stories or ballads, popular in the 17th-19th centuries.
- **Hornbook**: A paddle-shaped board with a lesson (e.g., alphabet) protected by a transparent sheet, used for teaching in early modern Europe and America.
- **Newbery Medal**: An annual US award for the most distinguished contribution to American literature for children.
- **Carnegie Medal**: An annual UK award for an outstanding book for children or young adults.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Should outdated and offensive content (e.g., racial stereotypes) in classic children's books be altered in new editions, preserved with contextual notes, or left unchanged?
- To what extent does children's literature construct and perpetuate cultural ideologies about gender, race, and childhood itself?
- How can the publishing industry improve representation and diversity both in the content of books and among authors and illustrators?
- What is the appropriate boundary for "children's literature," especially given works like the *Harry Potter* series that appeal to all ages?
