# Unification of Germany

## Overview
The Unification of Germany was the 19th-century process that created a single nation-state for German-speaking peoples, culminating in the proclamation of the German Empire in 1871. It resolved the long-standing "German dualism" between Prussia and Austria in favor of a "Lesser Germany" (Kleindeutsche Lösung) led by Prussia, fundamentally altering the European balance of power established after the Napoleonic Wars.

## Timeline
- **1815** — Congress of Vienna establishes the loose German Confederation under Austrian presidency.
- **1818** — Prussian Zollverein (customs union) is created, later expanding to include most German states.
- **1848-49** — German revolutions fail; Frankfurt Parliament's offer of an imperial crown is rejected by Prussia.
- **1864** — Second Schleswig War: Prussia and Austria defeat Denmark over the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein.
- **1866** — Austro-Prussian War ends with Prussian victory at Königgrätz; Austria is excluded from German affairs.
- **1867** — North German Confederation is formed under Prussian leadership, with a new constitution.
- **1870** — Franco-Prussian War begins after the Ems Dispatch incident.
- **1871** — German Empire is proclaimed at Versailles; Wilhelm I becomes Kaiser.
- **1871** — Treaty of Frankfurt ends war with France; France cedes Alsace-Lorraine.

## Body

### Foundations and Early Nationalism
Following the dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire in 1806, the Congress of Vienna created the German Confederation, a loose association of 39 states dominated by Austria. This period saw the rise of German nationalism, fueled by shared language and culture, and economic integration through the Prussian-led Zollverein customs union. Improved transportation, especially railways, further connected the German states. Liberal and nationalist movements, expressed in events like the Wartburg Festival (1817) and Hambach Festival (1832), sought political unity but were suppressed by conservative forces like the Carlsbad Decrees.

### The Failed Revolution and the Rise of Realpolitik
The Revolutions of 1848–49 represented a liberal attempt at unification. The Frankfurt Parliament drafted a constitution and offered a crown to Prussia's King Frederick William IV, who refused it, fearing opposition from other princes and the "clay" crown from a popular assembly. This failure shifted the momentum toward conservative forces. In Prussia, King Wilhelm I and his minister-president Otto von Bismarck pursued *Realpolitik*—a policy based on practical power rather than ideology. Bismarck famously stated that the great questions of the day would be decided "by iron and blood," not speeches.

### Wars of Unification
Bismarck orchestrated three wars to achieve unification. First, the 1864 Second Schleswig War against Denmark, fought jointly with Austria, secured the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein. The subsequent dispute over their administration provided the pretext for the 1866 Austro-Prussian War. Prussia's decisive victory at Königgrätz led to the Peace of Prague, which dissolved the German Confederation and excluded Austria, settling the "Lesser Germany" solution. Prussia then formed the North German Confederation. Finally, Bismarck manipulated the Hohenzollern candidature for the Spanish throne to provoke France into declaring war in 1870. The Franco-Prussian War saw Prussian-led German forces capture Napoleon III and besiege Paris. On 18 January 1871, in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, Wilhelm I was proclaimed German Emperor.

### Building the Nation-State
The new German Empire was a federal state of 25 states and one imperial territory. Its constitution, based on the North German model, featured a parliament (Reichstag) elected by universal male suffrage, but real power resided with the Kaiser and the chancellor (always also Prussia's minister-president), who was accountable only to the emperor. Prussia dominated the federal council (Bundesrat). Political unification was followed by efforts to forge a national culture, including the *Kulturkampf* (1872–78), a largely unsuccessful struggle against Catholic Church influence, and policies of Germanization targeting minorities like Poles. Historians like Heinrich von Treitschke promoted a "Borussian myth," casting Prussia as the destined unifier of Germany.

## Terms
- **Kleindeutsche Lösung**: The "Lesser Germany" solution for unification, excluding Austria and led by Prussia.
- **Großdeutsche Lösung**: The "Greater Germany" solution, which would have included Austria and its multi-ethnic empire.
- **Zollverein**: A customs union, initially Prussian, that promoted economic integration among German states.
- **Realpolitik**: A political system based on practical and material factors rather than theoretical or ethical objectives.
- **Kulturkampf**: The "culture struggle" of the 1870s, Bismarck's campaign to reduce the influence of the Catholic Church in the new empire.
- **Reichstag**: The parliament of the German Empire, elected by universal male suffrage.
- **Bundesrat**: The federal council representing the governments of the individual states, with Prussia holding a veto.
- **Sonderweg**: A historical hypothesis suggesting Germany followed a "special path" to modernity that led to Nazism, often linked to the failures of 1848.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians debate whether Bismarck had a master plan for unification or was an opportunist who skillfully exploited circumstances to expand Prussian power. The *Sonderweg* thesis, which argued that the failure of liberal revolution in 1848 led to a delayed, authoritarian unification and ultimately to the catastrophes of the 20th century, has been challenged by more recent scholarship. This newer view contends that Germany's development was not uniquely aberrant compared to other nations and that the 1848 liberals achieved lasting constitutional and social reforms later incorporated into the empire.
