# Urartu

## Overview
Urartu was an Iron Age kingdom in the Armenian highlands, centered around Lake Van. It emerged as a major power in the 9th century BC, rivaling the Neo-Assyrian Empire, and is noted for its sophisticated metalwork and large fortresses. Its history and legacy are central to understanding the transition from the Bronze Age to the Iron Age in the Near East and the origins of the Armenian people.

## Timeline
- **c. 1274 BC** — Assyrian king Shalmaneser I first records "Uruatri" as a region within the Nairi confederation.
- **c. 860–843 BC** — King Arame unifies the Nairi states into the Kingdom of Urartu.
- **c. 832–820 BC** — Sarduri I establishes a new dynasty, resists Assyria, and moves the capital to Tushpa (modern Van).
- **c. 820–800 BC** — Ispuini annexes Musasir, introduces the cult of the god Ḫaldi, and begins using the Urartian language in inscriptions.
- **c. 785–760 BC** — Argishti I expands the kingdom to its greatest extent, founding Erebuni Fortress (782 BC).
- **745 BC** — Assyrian king Tiglath-Pileser III defeats Urartian king Sarduri II.
- **714 BC** — King Rusa I is crushingly defeated by Sargon II of Assyria; the temple at Musasir is sacked.
- **c. 714–685 BC** — Argishti II restores Urartu's position but it is no longer a threat to Assyria.
- **612 BC** — The Medes and Babylonians conquer Assyria.
- **590 BC** — The Median king Cyaxares captures the Urartian capital of Van, effectively ending its sovereignty.

## Body

### Origins and Growth
Urartu emerged from the Nairi, a loose confederation of tribes in the Armenian highlands repeatedly attacked by Assyria. The kingdom unified under King Arame in the mid-9th century BC. After initial setbacks, Sarduri I consolidated power and established the capital at Tushpa. The kingdom grew rapidly during a period of Assyrian stagnation, expanding under kings Ispuini, Menua, and Argishti I. At its height in the 8th century BC, Urartu stretched from the Euphrates to Lake Urmia and was a dominant Near Eastern power.

### Conflict with Assyria and Decline
Urartu's rise brought it into frequent conflict with the Neo-Assyrian Empire. A major turning point came in 745 BC with the defeat of Sarduri II by Tiglath-Pileser III. In 714 BC, Urartu suffered a catastrophic defeat by Sargon II, losing the religious center of Musasir. The kingdom recovered under Argishti II and Rusa II but was weakened by constant raids from Cimmerian and Scythian invaders. By the reign of Sarduri III (645–635 BC), Urartu had become dependent on Assyria.

### Fall and Legacy
Urartu was ultimately destroyed by the Medes, who captured its capital in 590 BC. Some historians suggest it may have survived until the mid-6th century BC, falling to Cyrus the Great. The region was subsequently incorporated into the Achaemenid Persian Empire as the Satrapy of Armenia. Urartian elements, including names and possibly genetic lineage, persisted within the Armenian population. The kingdom's rediscovery in the 19th century, inspired by medieval Armenian histories, played a significant role in modern Armenian nationalism.

### Society and Culture
Urartu's economy was based on agriculture, supported by sophisticated state-managed irrigation systems. The state was ruled by a king who also served as chief priest. Art and architecture are characterized by sturdy stone fortresses, temples, and exceptional bronze metalwork, including weapons and ceremonial cauldrons. The Urartian pantheon was a mix of Hurrian, Akkadian, and local deities, headed by the god Ḫaldi.

### Language and Identity
The elite used the Urartian language, an ergative-absolutive language of the Hurro-Urartian family, written in Assyrian cuneiform. The language shows little development over 250 years, suggesting it may have been a formal written language rather than a widely spoken vernacular. Evidence indicates early contact and bilingualism with Proto-Armenian. The relationship between the Urartian state and the later Armenian people is complex; while Urartian identity faded, its legacy was absorbed into the emerging Armenian nation.

## Terms
- **Hurro-Urartian language family**: A small, extinct language family to which the Urartian and Hurrian languages belong.
- **Cuneiform**: A system of writing using wedge-shaped marks, adapted by Urartu from Assyria.
- **Nairi**: A confederation of tribes in the Armenian highlands that preceded and was absorbed by Urartu.
- **Tushpa**: The capital city of Urartu, located near modern Van, Turkey.
- **Musasir**: An important religious center of Urartu, home to the main temple of the god Ḫaldi.
- **Ergative-absolutive**: A grammatical alignment where the subject of an intransitive verb is treated like the object of a transitive verb.
- **Satrapy**: A province of the ancient Persian Achaemenid Empire, governed by a satrap.
- **Cimmerians and Scythians**: Nomadic peoples from the Eurasian steppe whose raids contributed to Urartu's decline.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Language of the populace**: Scholars debate whether Proto-Armenian was spoken by the general population while Urartian was only a formal written language, or if Urartian was the primary vernacular.
- **Relationship to Armenians**: The exact nature of the continuity between the Urartian kingdom and the later Armenian people is debated, with theories ranging from direct descent to cultural and genetic absorption.
- **Cause of final collapse**: Historians disagree on whether Urartu was destroyed by the Medes in 590 BC or survived until conquered by Cyrus the Great in the mid-6th century BC.
- **Ethnic composition**: The Urartian ruling class may have been a small elite governing a diverse population, with kings possibly from various ethnic backgrounds.
