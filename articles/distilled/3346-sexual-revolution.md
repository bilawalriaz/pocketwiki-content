# Sexual revolution

## Overview
The sexual revolution was a mid-20th century social movement in the Western world that challenged traditional codes of sexual behavior and interpersonal relationships. It was driven by advances in contraception, changing social norms, and academic research, leading to increased acceptance of premarital sex, homosexuality, and alternative sexualities, and profoundly influencing legal and cultural attitudes toward privacy, gender, and personal freedom.

## Timeline
- **1928** — Margaret Mead publishes *Coming of Age in Samoa*, challenging Western views on adolescent sexuality.
- **1948** — Alfred Kinsey publishes *Sexual Behavior in the Human Male*, revealing widespread sexual practices.
- **1953** — Hugh Hefner founds *Playboy* magazine.
- **1960** — The birth control pill is approved for contraceptive use in the U.S.
- **1965** — *Griswold v. Connecticut* legalizes contraception for married couples.
- **1969** — The Stonewall riots catalyze the modern gay rights movement.
- **1973** — *Roe v. Wade* legalizes abortion nationwide in the U.S.
- **1980s** — The AIDS crisis leads to a decline in the "free love" movement.

## Body

### Origins and Influences
The revolution's intellectual roots include Sigmund Freud's theory of unconscious sexual drives and Margaret Mead's ethnography suggesting sexual freedom eases adolescent development. Kinsey's surveys and later work by Masters and Johnson provided data showing common sexual behaviors were more varied than traditionally believed. Social movements like the counterculture, women's liberation, and gay rights provided the matrix for change, fueled by a belief that sexual repression was detrimental.

### Contraception and Legal Change
The development of reliable contraception, especially the birth control pill, was a primary catalyst, giving individuals greater reproductive control. This spurred landmark legal shifts, beginning with *Griswold v. Connecticut* (1965), which established a right to marital privacy. This precedent influenced later rulings on contraception for unmarried couples (*Eisenstadt v. Baird*, 1972), abortion (*Roe v. Wade*, 1973), and eventually LGBTQ+ rights (*Lawrence v. Texas*, 2003; *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 2015).

### Cultural and Popular Expression
The revolution manifested in popular culture through the successful legal challenges to obscenity laws, allowing the publication of erotic novels like *Lady Chatterley's Lover* and *Fanny Hill*. Sexually explicit films, particularly from Sweden, challenged international standards. Nonfiction sex manuals and feminist works like *Our Bodies, Ourselves* (1970) openly discussed sexuality and pleasure. Pornography became more commercialized, with films like *Deep Throat* (1972) achieving mainstream notoriety.

### Feminism and Social Movements
The sexual liberation movement was deeply intertwined with second-wave feminism, which sought to redefine female sexuality beyond pleasing men and fought for women's sexual autonomy. The gay rights movement gained momentum after the Stonewall riots, politicizing "coming out." These movements collectively challenged traditional gender roles and heteronormativity, though debates later emerged within feminism about the revolution's benefits for women.

### Legacy and Backlash
By the mid-1970s, premarital sex was widely accepted in the U.S. However, the revolution's optimism waned due to economic crises, the commercialization of sex, and the AIDS crisis in the 1980s. Some feminist critics, like Sheila Jeffreys, argued the revolution primarily benefited men. Scholarly debates question whether it was a true liberation or a form of "repressive desublimation" that distracted from deeper political change.

## Terms
- **Sexual liberation**: A social movement challenging traditional sexual norms and advocating for personal freedom in sexual expression and relationships.
- **Free love**: A movement advocating for the separation of the state from sexual matters like marriage and birth control, emphasizing personal freedom.
- **Birth control pill**: An oral contraceptive approved in 1960 that provided women with reliable, easy-to-use reproductive control.
- **Psychoanalysis**: A therapeutic method developed by Sigmund Freud to explore unconscious drives, particularly sexual ones.
- **Kinsey Reports**: Two influential books (1948, 1953) by Alfred Kinsey detailing the wide range of human sexual behavior based on extensive interviews.
- **Griswold v. Connecticut**: A 1965 U.S. Supreme Court case that ruled bans on contraception violated the right to marital privacy.
- **Stonewall riots**: A 1969 uprising at a gay bar in New York City that became a catalyst for the modern gay rights movement.
- **Repressive desublimation**: A concept (associated with Herbert Marcuse) suggesting that apparent sexual liberation may actually serve as a conservative force that saps energy for true social critique.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Was the sexual revolution a genuine liberation or a form of "repressive desublimation" that distracted from political change?
- Did the revolution primarily benefit men, as some feminist critics like Sheila Jeffreys argue, or did it advance women's sexual autonomy?
- Scholars debate the timing and nature of earlier "first sexual revolutions," such as during the Christianization of Rome, the Enlightenment, or the Roaring Twenties.
- The impact and commercialization of pornography during the revolution remain contested, with debates between anti-pornography feminists and pro-sex feminists.
