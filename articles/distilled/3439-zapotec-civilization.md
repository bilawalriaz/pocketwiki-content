# Zapotec civilization

## Overview
The Zapotec civilization was a major pre-Columbian indigenous culture that flourished in the Valley of Oaxaca, Mexico, from c. 700 BC to AD 1521. They built one of Mesoamerica's first major cities, Monte Albán, and developed a complex state, writing system, and calendar. Their legacy endures in the modern Zapotec peoples and their languages.

## Timeline
- **c. 700–500 BC** — Rosario phase ends; major settlements like San José Mogote lose population.
- **c. 500 BC** — Monte Albán is founded in a central "no-man's-land."
- **400–100 BC** — Monte Alban 1 phase: Zapotec state at Monte Albán begins to expand.
- **100 BC – AD 200** — Monte Alban 2 phase: Zapotec expansion peaks; they conquer/colonize provinces.
- **c. AD 200** — Zapotec influence extends from Quiotepec to Ocelotepec.
- **c. AD 700** — Monte Albán ceases to be the largest city in the southern Mexican highlands.
- **1458** — Aztecs conquer the Valley of Oaxaca.
- **1497–1502** — Last major battle between Aztecs and Zapotecs at Guiengola.
- **1521** — Zapotecs and Mixtecs submit to Spanish rule after Aztec defeat.
- **1526** — Zapotec territory becomes part of the Marquessate of the Valley of Oaxaca.
- **1535** — Bishopric of Oaxaca is established; Dominican friars increase presence.
- **2026** — A well-preserved 1,400-year-old Zapotec tomb is discovered in Oaxaca.

## Body

### Origins and Rise of Monte Albán
Zapotec civilization originated in the Y-shaped Central Valleys of Oaxaca in the late 6th century BC. The three valleys were initially divided among competing societies. After the decline of earlier settlements like San José Mogote, a new city, Monte Albán, was founded on a mountain overlooking the valleys. Its population likely came from San José Mogote. Archaeologists Joyce Marcus and Kent V. Flannery compare its formation to *synoikism* in ancient Greece: a centralization of populations in response to external threats, suggested by early fortifications.

### Expansion and Statehood
The Zapotec state based at Monte Albán expanded dramatically from 400 BC to AD 200. They conquered or colonized settlements far beyond the Oaxaca Valley, evidenced by the sudden adoption of Zapotec-style pottery in other regions. Building J at Monte Albán, with its carved "Conquest Slabs," is interpreted as a record of this expansion, showing glyphs of conquered provinces and their rulers. The state's power relied on both colonization and military conquest.

### Warfare and Spanish Conquest
In the 1450s, the Aztec Empire invaded and conquered the Valley of Oaxaca in 1458. The last major battle occurred at Guiengola (1497–1502). Upon learning of the Aztec defeat by the Spanish, Zapotec King Cosijoeza ordered his people not to resist. In 1521, they peacefully submitted to Hernán Cortés's forces, sparing the valley the devastation experienced by the Aztecs.

### Viceroyalty and Demographic Collapse
After 1526, the region became part of New Spain. Spanish settlers, Dominican friars, and new domestic animals arrived. A process of *mestizaje* (racial mixing) began. Devastating epidemics of diseases like smallpox and measles caused a catastrophic population decline, from an estimated 1.5 million in 1520 to 150,000 by 1650.

### Language and Writing
The Zapotec languages belong to the Oto-Manguean family. They are tonal, with up to four distinct tonemes (pitch patterns). The Zapotecs developed one of Mesoamerica's earliest logosyllabic writing systems, using glyphs for syllables. The earliest known artifact with this script is the "Danzante" stone (c. 500–600 BC) from San José Mogote. The script was used for calendar information and historical records but remains largely undeciphered.

### Religion and Society
Zapotec religion was polytheistic, with deities like Cocijo (rain god) and Pitao Cozobi (maize god) associated with agriculture and fertility. They practiced human sacrifice and used dedication rituals to sanctify structures, burying caches of symbolic items like jade (power), obsidian (sacrifice), and shells (underworld). Society became increasingly stratified and centralized as the population grew during the Monte Alban phases.

### Geography and Technology
The Zapotec heartland was the fertile Central Valleys of Oaxaca. They were a sedentary culture, building stone and mortar houses. To support population growth, they developed canal irrigation systems to water crops on the valley floor and foothills. This agricultural innovation allowed them to sustain their cities and pay tribute.

## Terms
- **Synoikism**: The historical process where dispersed populations consolidate into a single central city, often for mutual defense.
- **Logosyllabic writing**: A writing system where symbols represent both whole words (logograms) and syllables (syllabograms).
- **Toneme**: A phonemic difference in meaning in a language determined by the pitch or tone of a syllable.
- **Mestizaje**: The process of racial and cultural mixing between indigenous peoples and European settlers.
- **Polytheistic**: A religious system that believes in and worships multiple deities.
- **Dedication ritual**: A ceremony to consecrate a building or space, often involving the burial of symbolic offerings.
- **Exonym**: A name given to a people or group by outsiders (e.g., "Zapotec" from Nahuatl *tzapotēcah*).
- **Endonym**: The name a people or group uses for themselves (e.g., *Be'ena'a*, "The Cloud People").

## Debates and Open Questions
- The origin of Mesoamerican writing is debated: whether the Zapotec script (c. 500 BC) or earlier Olmec symbols (c. 650 BC) represent the first true writing.
- The interpretation of the glyphs on Building J at Monte Albán as a record of conquest is a prominent but specific archaeological theory.
- The exact causes and timeline of the decline of Monte Albán's political dominance around AD 700 are not fully detailed in the source.
