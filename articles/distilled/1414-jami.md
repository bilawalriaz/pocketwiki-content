# Jami

## Overview
Nūr ad-Dīn ʿAbd ar-Rahmān Jāmī (1414–1492) was a preeminent Sunni poet, theologian, and Sufi shaykh of the Timurid era, celebrated for synthesizing the metaphysical school of Ibn ʿArabī with the Naqshbandī order’s disciplined praxis. Based in Herat, he dominated the cultural and intellectual life of the Persianate world, producing roughly eighty-seven works spanning poetry, Sufi metaphysics, grammar, history, and irrigation engineering. His magnum opus, the *Haft Awrang* (Seven Thrones), reworks Niẓāmī’s narrative models to allegorize the Sufi path, while his commentaries—particularly on divine mercy and the grammar text *al-Kāfiya*—became standard curricula from Ottoman madrasas to Mughal India. Jami’s legacy defines the "Persianate" cultural sphere, bridging Central Asia, Iran, and the Indian subcontinent through a Persian literary language that transcended political borders.

## Timeline
- **7 Nov 1414** — Born in Kharjerd, Khorasan, to a Persian family originating from Dasht (Isfahan region).
- **c. 1420s** — Family migrates to Herat; studies Peripatetic philosophy, mathematics, logic, Arabic, and Islamic philosophy at the Nizāmiyya college.
- **c. 1430s** — Travels to Samarqand, the leading center of scientific study, to complete advanced education.
- **1453** — Formally assumes role of Sufi shaykh; begins teaching the "prophetic" vs. "mystic" distinction in Sufism.
- **c. 1470s–80s** — Composes major works: *Haft Awrang* (Seven Thrones), *Tuhfat al-Ahrār*, *Lawāʾiḥ*, *Nafahāt al-Uns*, and the *Kherad-nāma*.
- **c. 1485** — Completes *Kherad-nāma* (Book of Wisdom), reframing the Alexander Romance as philosophical anecdotes.
- **9 Nov 1492** — Dies in Herat; funeral led by the Timurid prince, attended by massive crowds.

## Body

### Biography and Intellectual Formation
Jami was born in Kharjerd but raised in Herat after his family’s migration. His father, Nizām al-Dīn Ahmad, a Sufi himself, provided his initial mentorship. At the Nizāmiyya institution in Herat, Jami mastered the standard curriculum of the age: Peripateticism (Aristotelian logic/philosophy), mathematics, natural sciences, Arabic, rhetoric, and Islamic philosophy. A pivotal period followed in Samarqand, then the Muslim world’s paramount hub for scientific studies, where he completed his formal education. Upon returning to Herat, he occupied a unique position at the Timurid court, engaging directly in politics, economics, and religious life. His *nisba* (pen name) shifted from "Dashti" (referencing his father’s hometown) to "Jami," a dual pun explained in verse: his birthplace was Jam, and his pen had "drunk from the cup" of the scholar Shaykh al-Islām Ahmad Jam. He maintained a celebrated friendship with the Turkic poet ʿAlīshīr Navāʾī, bridging Persian and Turkic literary cultures. Of his four sons, only Ẓiyāʾ al-Dīn Yūsuf survived infancy, for whom Jami composed the *Bahāristān*.

### Sufi Teachings and the School of Ibn ʿArabī
Jami’s Sufism was rooted in the *waḥdat al-wujūd* (unity of existence) metaphysics of Ibn ʿArabī, yet he critically modified it. He distinguished two spiritual archetypes: the "prophetic" spirit (engaged in worldly guidance) and the "mystic" spirit (absorbed in divine witnessing). Initiated into the Naqshbandī *Khwājagānī* lineage via a blessing from Khwāja Muḥammad Pārsā and discipleship under Saʿd al-Dīn Kāsgarī (whose granddaughter he married), Jami emphasized *tawḥīd* (divine unity) as an all-embracing unity of lover, beloved, and love itself. He famously dissented from Ibn ʿArabī on mutual dependence, asserting: "We and Thou are not separate... but we need Thee, whereas Thou dost not need us." For Jami, love for the Prophet Muḥammad was the prerequisite for the spiritual path; when a prospective disciple claimed never to have loved anyone, Jami replied, "Go and love first, then come to me." He systematized Sufi terminology—defining sainthood (*walāya*), the stages of the seeker (*sālik*), the "seekers of blame" (*malāmatī*), and the charismatic feats (*karāmāt*) of saints—while pursuing "permanent presence with God" through ceaseless remembrance, silence, and self-oblivion.

### Major Literary Works
Jami’s output of ~87 books and letters ranges from *ghazals* (lyric odes) to technical treatises. His *Dīvān* comprises three chronological collections: *Fatḥat al-Shabāb* (Youth), *Wāsiṭat al-ʿIqd* (Middle Age), and *Khātimāt al-Ḥayāt* (Old Age). The *Haft Awrang* (Seven Thrones), explicitly modeled on Niẓāmī’s *Khamseh*, contains seven *masnavīs* (rhymed couplet narratives). The fifth, *Yūsuf wa Zulaykhā* (Joseph and Potiphar’s Wife), is his most acclaimed poem, allegorizing the soul’s pursuit of Divine Beauty through Quranic narrative. *Salamān wa Absāl* uses a prince’s carnal desire for his wet-nurse to map Sufi stages of repentance and the ideal of the "Sufi-king"—a ruler who realizes his role as God’s true vicegerent. The *Kherad-nāma* (c. 1485) adapts the Alexander Romance (*Iskandarnāma*) into a compendium of wisdom anecdotes. In prose, *Lawāʾiḥ* (Flashes of Light) is a seminal treatise on Sufi metaphysics; *Nafahāt al-Uns* (Breaths of Fellowship) compiles biographies of Sufi saints; and his commentary *al-Fawāʾid al-Ḍiyāʾiyya* on Ibn al-Ḥājib’s Arabic grammar *al-Kāfiya* became a staple in Ottoman madrasas under his Turkish appellation, Molla Cami. Uniquely, his manual on irrigation engineering, complete with advanced calculations, remained a functional reference for Herat’s water department centuries later.

### Art, Patronage, and Manuscript Culture
Jami’s poetry was a primary driver of the luxury manuscript industry in Timurid Herat. His texts were frequently illustrated with miniatures of exceptional quality, reflecting the complexity of his allegories. This integration of dense mystical poetry with visual art was not yet standard practice; Jami’s works helped establish the convention where image and text jointly exegesis Sufi symbolism. The patronage of the Timurid court—especially Sultan Ḥusayn Bāyqarā and Navāʾī—ensured his manuscripts were produced with the highest artistic and calligraphic standards, disseminating his influence visually as well as textually.

### Reception and the Persianate World
Jami’s works became the backbone of Persianate education across the Safavid, Uzbek, Ottoman, and Mughal empires. In the Ottoman realm, his grammar commentary defined madrasa curricula; in Mughal India, Emperor Bābur called him "the foremost authority of the age," and subsequent emperors (Humāyūn, Akbar, Jahāngīr, Shāh Jahān) commissioned illustrated versions of *Yūsuf wa Zulaykhā* into the 19th century. His influence shaped Persianized Urdu and poets like Ghālib via the lineage of his pupil Bābā Fighānī. Despite an invitation from the Deccan minister Maḥmūd Gāvān, Jami refused to leave Khorasan, declaring he would not trade his homeland for "the black land of India"—a statement revealing the geo-cultural hierarchies within the Persianate sphere. Modern scholarship notes a sharp decline in Jami studies over the last half-century, mirroring a broader neglect of classical Persian and Islamic humanities.

## Terms
- ****Naqshbandī / Khwājagānī**** — A major Sunni Sufi order (tariqa) emphasizing silent dhikr (remembrance), strict adherence to Sharia, and the "Khwājagān" (Masters) lineage; Jami’s institutional affiliation.
- ****Ibn ʿArabī (d. 1240)**** — Andalusian mystic whose school of *waḥdat al-wujūd* (Unity of Existence) posits that all reality is a manifestation of the One; Jami’s primary theological framework.
- ****Waḥdat al-wujūd**** — "Unity of Existence": the doctrine that God is the only true Being; creation is a theophany (self-disclosure) of the Divine Names, not a separate reality.
- ****Masnavī / Mathnawī**** — A poem in rhyming couplets (aa, bb, cc...), the standard Persian form for long narrative, didactic, or allegorical poetry (e.g., *Haft Awrang*).
- ****Ghazal**** — A lyric ode of 5–15 couplets with a single rhyme and refrain, traditionally exploring themes of love (earthly/divine), wine, and the beloved; Jami composed three *divāns* of them.
- ****Sālik / Murīd**** — The "wayfarer" or disciple on the Sufi path (*ṭarīqa*) who strives (*mujāhada*) under a shaykh, distinct from the *wālī* (saint) who has arrived.
- ****Malāmatī**** — "Blame-seekers": a Sufi current that conceals piety and invites public reproach to purge hypocrisy (riyāʾ) and safeguard sincerity (ikhlāṣ).
- ****Karāmāt**** — Charismatic gifts or "miracles" granted to saints (*awliyāʾ*) by God’s grace, distinct from prophets' miracles (*muʿjizāt*); Jami catalogued their types.
- ****Persianate World**** — The transregional cultural sphere (c. 15th–19th c.) where Persian served as the lingua franca of high culture, administration, and literature across Iran, Central Asia, Anatolia, and South Asia.
- ****Timurid Renaissance**** — The cultural efflorescence (late 14th–15th c.) under Timur’s descendants in Herat/Samarqand, patronizing arts, sciences, and letters; Jami was its culminating figure.

## Debates and open questions
- **Ibn ʿArabī fidelity vs. innovation**: Scholars debate whether Jami is a faithful transmitter of the *Akbarian* (Ibn ʿArabī) school or a reformer who "Naqshbandī-ized" it—especially regarding his rejection of the mutual need between God and creation.
- **Political quietism vs. engagement**: Jami’s court role and irrigation work suggest active worldly engagement, yet his teachings stress withdrawal (*khalwa*) and "unawareness of one’s earthly state"; the tension between the "prophetic" and "mystic" archetypes in his own life remains contested.
- **Authorship and corpus stability**: With ~87 attributed works—some mere letters, others massive commentaries—a critical edition distinguishing authentic core texts from later accretions or student compilations is still lacking.
- **Decline of reception**: The source claims a "last half-century" neglect of Jami. Historians debate whether this stems from colonial-era curriculum reforms, the rise of vernacular national literatures (Turkish, Urdu, Pashto), or the marginalization of Persianate studies in modern academia.
- **Dating of *Haft Awrang***: While the source places major composition in the 1470s–80s, the exact chronology of the seven *masnavīs* and their relationship to shifting court politics under Sultan Ḥusayn Bāyqarā is not firmly established.
