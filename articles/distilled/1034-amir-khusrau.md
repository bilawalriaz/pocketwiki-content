# Amir Khusrau

## Overview
Amīr Khusrau (1253–1325) was an Indo-Persian Sufi poet, musician, and scholar who served the Delhi Sultanate across seven reigns, bridging Turkic, Persian, and Indian cultures. A disciple of the Chishti saint Nizāmuddīn Auliyā, he is credited with founding *qawwālī* (Sufi devotional singing), introducing the Persian *ghazal* to India, and inventing the *tarāna* vocal form and the *sitar*. His vast output—five *dīvāns*, five *masnavīs* in a *khamsa* (quintet), historical epics, and a multilingual glossary (*Khāliq Bārī*)—established a pluralistic literary identity that still shapes South Asian music and poetry.

## Body

### Family and Formative Years
Khusrau’s father, Amīr Saif ud-Dīn Mahmūd, belonged to the Turkic Hazāra-yi Lāchīn clan of Transoxiana. Fleeing Chinggis Khan’s invasion, the family reached Delhi via Balkh and received refuge from Sultan Shams ud-Dīn Iltutmish (r. 1211–36), himself of Central Asian origin. Saif ud-Dīn received a fief in Patiyali (1230) and married Bibi Daulat Naz, daughter of Rawat Arz, a Hindu convert to Islam and war minister to Sultan Ghiyās ud-Dīn Balban. Khusrau, born in 1253, lost his father at age eight but inherited fluency in Turkish, Persian, Arabic, and an early immersion in Sufism. He began composing poetry at nine and earned the sobriquet *Tūtī-yi Hind* (“Parrot of India”) for his eloquence. In 1273, when Khusrau was 20, his grandfather died, reportedly at age 113.

### Court Career Under the Mamluks (1271–90)
Khusrau’s first patron was Malik Chajju, nephew of Sultan Balban, whose court introduced him to the royal assembly. In 1276 Prince Bughra Khan became his patron; when Bughra Khan was appointed governor of Bengal (1277), Khusrau visited him there (1279) while compiling his second *dīvān*, *Wast ul-Hayāt*. He then served Balban’s eldest son, Khan Muhammad, in Multan (1281–85), a cosmopolitan gateway for scholars and merchants from Baghdad, Arabia, and Persia. After Khan Muhammad’s death fighting the Mongols (1285), Khusrau wrote two elegies. He briefly served Sultan Mu‘izz ud-Dīn Qaiqābād (1287–88), producing *Qirān us-Sa‘dain* (1288), a *masnavī* celebrating the reconciliation of Bughra Khan and his son. The Mamluk dynasty ended in 1290 when Jalāl ud-Dīn Fīrūz Khaljī seized the throne.

### Flourishing Under the Khaljīs (1290–1316)
Jalāl ud-Dīn Fīrūz Khaljī honored Khusrau with the title “Amīr” and the post of *Mushaf-dār* (keeper of the royal manuscripts). Khusrau’s *ghazals* were set to music and performed nightly. He completed *Miftāh ul-Futūh* (1290) praising the Sultan’s victories and his third *dīvān*, *Ghurrat ul-Kamāl* (1294). When ‘Alā’ ud-Dīn Khaljī ascended (1296), Khusrau wrote *Khazā’in ul-Futūh* recording the new ruler’s conquests and administration. His magnum opus, the *Khamsa-e-Khusrau* (1298), comprises five *masnavīs* modeled on Nizāmī Ganjavī’s quintet: *Matla‘ ul-Anwār* (ethical/Sufi, 3,310 verses in 15 days), *Khusrau-Shīrīn* (4,000 verses), *Laylā-Majnūn* (romance), *Āyina-yi Iskandarī* (Alexander the Great, 4,500 verses), and *Hasht-Bihisht* (legends of Sasanian king Bahrām V, including “The Three Princes of Serendip”). ‘Alā’ ud-Dīn rewarded him lavishly, though historian Ziyā’ ud-Dīn Baranī later criticized the Sultan for granting only 1,000 *tankas* instead of the governorships a poet of Khusrau’s caliber deserved.

In 1300, Khusrau’s mother and brother died. He honored his mother with the verse: “Where ever the dust of your feet is found is like a relic of paradise for me.” When ‘Alā’ ud-Dīn’s son Qutb ud-Dīn Mubārak Shāh Khaljī was born, Khusrau prepared his horoscope, which is included in the *masnavī Saqiana*.

### Spiritual Turn and Later Service (1310–25)
In 1310 Khusrau became a formal disciple of Nizāmuddīn Auliyā, the Chishti saint of Delhi. Under Quṭb ud-Dīn Mubārak Shāh (r. 1316–20) he completed *Duval Rānī–Khizr Khān* (1315–16), a tragic romance of a Vaghela princess, and *Nuh Sipihr* (1318), a nine-chapter *masnavī* whose third “sky” gives a detailed account of India’s environment, seasons, flora, fauna, and cultures. He also produced the prose works *I‘jāz-e-Khusravī* (five volumes), *Baqia-Naqia* (1317), and *Afzal ul-Fawā’id* (1319), the last a record of Nizāmuddīn’s teachings. After Mubārak Shāh’s murder (1320) and the brief usurpation of Khusrau Khan, Ghiyāth al-Dīn Tughluq established the Tughluq dynasty; Khusrau began the historical *masnavī Tughluq Nāma* (1320). His final anthology, *Nihāyat ul-Kamāl*, was compiled weeks before his death in October 1325, six months after Nizāmuddīn’s passing.

### The Shalimar Bagh Verse
A famous Persian couplet—“If there is a paradise on earth, it is this, it is this, it is this”—is popularly ascribed to Khusrau and linked to Kashmir’s Shalimar Garden (built under Jahāngīr). Historian Rana Safvi found no such inscription in the garden and traces the verse to Sa‘adullah Khan, a noble under Shāh Jahān; Jahāngīr himself is recorded as first uttering the phrase in praise of Kashmir.

### Contributions to Hindustani Music
**Qawwālī.** Khusrau fused Persian, Arabic, Turkic, and Indian singing traditions in the late 13th century to create *qawwālī*, Sufi devotional song characterized by a punctuated chorus, devotional refrain, and a lead singer executing fast *tāns* and complex *svara* combinations. His disciples split into *Qawwāls* (Muslim devotional repertoire) and *Kalāwants* (secular songs in *qawwālī* style).

**Tarāna and Trivat.** Musicologist Jaidev Singh calls *tarāna* entirely Khusrau’s invention. While *nirgit* songs using meaningless syllables (*śuṣk-akṣaras*) and mnemonic drum syllables (*pāṭ-akṣaras*) existed since Bharata’s *Nāṭyaśāstra*, Khusrau introduced Persian words with soft consonants arranged to convey meaning, adding a few Hindi words for sense. Legend holds he devised the form while secretly listening to the musician Gopāl Nāyak’s exposition in *rāg* Kadambak for six days, then reproducing it with mnemonic syllables on the seventh.

**Sitar.** Khusrau is credited with modifying the three-stringed *Tritantrī Vīṇā* into a *Setār* (Persian “three-stringed”), which evolved into the modern sitar.

### Literary Legacy and Language
Khusrau wrote primarily in Persian but used the term *Hindavī* (“of Hind/India”) for the vernacular that became Hindustani (Hindi-Urdu). He gave *Hindavī* shape in Islamic literature, composed a Punjabi war ballad, and knew Sanskrit. Many *Hindavī* verses attributed to him lack pre-18th-century manuscript evidence and show relatively modern language. His riddles (*pahelīs*), exploiting double entendre, remain a vibrant oral genre. The versified glossary *Khāliq Bārī* (Persian, Arabic, *Hindavī*) is often ascribed to him, though scholar Hafiz Mehmood Khan Shirani dated its completion to 1622 in Gwalior by Ẓiyā’ ud-Dīn Khusrau. Musicians credit him with six forms—*qaul, qalbana, naqsh, gul, tarāna, khyāl*—but evidence is insufficient.

## Terms
- ****Delhi Sultanate**** — Succession of Turkic-Afghan dynasties ruling Delhi (1206–1526); Khusrau served its Mamluk, Khaljī, and early Tughluq phases.
- ****Chishti Order**** — Major Sufi *ṭarīqa* in South Asia emphasizing music (*samā‘*), poverty, and service; Nizāmuddīn Auliyā was its leading 14th-century master.
- ****Qawwālī**** — Sufi devotional song genre fusing Persian/Arabic/Turkic/Indian elements; structured around chorus, refrain, and virtuosic lead vocals.
- ****Ghazal**** — Lyric poem in rhyming couplets with a refrain, typically treating love (earthly or divine); Khusrau introduced the Persian form to India.
- ****Masnavī**** — Narrative poem in rhyming couplets (aa bb cc…); Khusrau wrote historical, romantic, and ethical *masnavīs*.
- ****Khamsa**** — “Quintet”—five *masnavīs* grouped as a set; Khusrau’s *Khamsa-e-Khusrau* (1298) emulates Nizāmī Ganjavī’s classic quintet.
- ****Tarāna**** — Vocal composition using Persian words with soft consonants arranged to yield meaning, plus mnemonic syllables; invented by Khusrau per Jaidev Singh.
- ****Sitar**** — Plucked lute; traditionally derived from Khusrau’s modification of the three-stringed *Tritantrī Vīṇā* into a *Setār*.
- ****Hindavī**** — Khusrau’s term for the Indian vernacular (early Hindustani/Hindi-Urdu) written in Perso-Arabic script.
- ****Khāliq Bārī**** — Versified glossary of Persian, Arabic, and Hindavī words; attribution to Khusrau debated (possibly 17th-century compilation).

## Debates and open questions
- **Authorship of *Khāliq Bārī*:** Hafiz Mehmood Khan Shirani argues it was completed in 1622 by Ẓiyā’ ud-Dīn Khusrau in Gwalior, not by Amīr Khusrau.
- **Attribution of *Hindavī* verses:** No manuscript evidence predates the 18th century; the language appears relatively modern, raising doubts about direct authorship.
- **Invention of six musical forms (*qaul, qalbana, naqsh, gul, tarāna, khyāl*):** Musicians credit Khusrau, but the source notes “insufficient evidence for this.”
- **Shalimar verse provenance:** Popular tradition ascribes the “paradise on earth” couplet to Khusrau in Kashmir; scholarship (Rana Safvi) assigns it to Sa‘adullah Khan under Shāh Jahān.
- **Extent of ‘Alā’ ud-Dīn’s patronage:** Baranī claims the Sultan undervalued Khusrau (1,000 *tankas* only), while Khusrau’s own panegyrics describe lavish rewards; the discrepancy remains unresolved.
