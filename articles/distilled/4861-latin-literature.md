# Latin literature

## Overview
Latin literature encompasses all writings in the Latin language, from the earliest Roman works to modern scholarly texts. It began formally in 240 BC and flourished for centuries, forming the foundation of Western literary and intellectual tradition. Its study is essential for understanding Roman history, medieval thought, and the Renaissance revival of classical learning.

## Timeline
- **240 BC** — First Latin stage play performed, marking the start of formal Latin literature.
- **c. 272–222 BC** — Livius Andronicus adapts Greek drama; Gnaeus Naevius creates Roman historical tragedy.
- **c. 200 BC** — Quintus Ennius writes the *Annals*, adopting Greek dactylic hexameter for epic.
- **160 BC** — Cato the Elder writes *On Agriculture*, the first major Latin prose work.
- **2nd century BC** — Gaius Lucilius creates the genre of Roman satire.
- **81 BC–AD 17** — The "Golden Age" of Latin literature, from Cicero's first speech to Ovid's death.
- **27 BC–AD 14** — The Augustan Age: Virgil, Horace, and Ovid produce their masterworks.
- **c. AD 60** — Petronius writes the *Satyricon*, the first picaresque novel.
- **Early 2nd century AD** — Tacitus and Suetonius write major historical works.
- **4th–5th centuries AD** — Christian writers like Augustine and Jerome establish Christian Latin literature.
- **Renaissance onward** — Latin serves as the lingua franca for European scholars like Bacon, Spinoza, and Newton.

## Body

### Early Latin Literature (240 BC – 2nd century BC)
Formal Latin literature began in 240 BC with adaptations of Greek drama by Livius Andronicus, a Greek prisoner of war. Gnaeus Naevius continued this tradition and introduced Roman historical tragedy (*fabula praetexta*). Epic poetry developed with Naevius and Quintus Ennius, who adopted the Greek dactylic hexameter. Early comedy is well-represented by the extant plays of Plautus and Terence, who freely adapted Greek "New Comedy." Prose began with Cato the Elder's *On Agriculture* (160 BC). The period concluded with Gaius Lucilius, who invented Roman satire.

### The Golden Age (81 BC – AD 17)
This period is traditionally divided into the age of Cicero and the Augustan Age. Cicero mastered Latin prose through orations, philosophical works, and letters. Historians like Caesar and Sallust wrote in distinct styles. Lyric poetry emerged with Catullus, and Lucretius expounded Epicurean philosophy. The Augustan Age, under Emperor Augustus's patronage, saw the peak of Latin poetry. Virgil's *Aeneid* provided an epic foundation for Rome. Horace perfected lyric and satirical verse. Ovid's *Metamorphoses* became the primary source of classical mythology for centuries. Livy produced a monumental history of Rome.

### The Imperial Period (AD 14 – c. 200)
After Augustus, authors emphasized stylistic innovation. The Stoic philosopher Seneca wrote influential tragedies and moral essays. Epic poetry continued with Lucan's *Pharsalia* and Statius's *Thebaid*. Petronius's *Satyricon* pioneered the picaresque novel. Martial perfected the epigram, and Juvenal wrote biting satire. Historians Tacitus and Suetonius provided critical accounts of the early empire. The period's most famous novel was Apuleius's *The Golden Ass*.

### Latin in Later Periods
Pagan literature had a final flourish in the 3rd–5th centuries. Christian writers like Augustine, Jerome, and Prudentius laid the foundations of Christian Latin literature. During the Middle Ages, Latin remained the language of scholarship, especially during the Carolingian Renaissance and the Scholastic period. The Renaissance saw a return to classical Latin ("Neo-Latin"), which remained the lingua franca of European learning into the early modern era, used by figures like Descartes, Bacon, and Newton.

## Terms
- **Fabula palliata**: A Roman comedy adapted from a Greek original, with characters in Greek dress.
- **Fabula praetexta**: A Roman tragedy based on Roman historical or legendary subjects.
- **Saturnian meter**: An early, native Latin verse form used before the adoption of Greek meters.
- **Dactylic hexameter**: The six-foot meter borrowed from Greek epic poetry, which became the standard for Latin epic.
- **Rhetoric**: The art of effective or persuasive speaking and writing, central to Roman education and political life.
- **Inflected language**: A language where word forms change to express grammatical relationships (e.g., case, tense), allowing for flexible word order.
- **Lingua franca**: A common language used for communication between speakers of different native languages.
- **Neo-Latin**: The form of Latin used during and after the Renaissance, modeled on classical standards.

## Debates and Open Questions
The traditional dating of the "Golden Age" (81 BC–AD 17) is questioned by recent scholarship, which challenges the assumption that works from this period are inherently superior to those from earlier or later eras. The completeness and authorship of some major works, like Virgil's *Aeneid* (which he left unfinished), are subjects of ongoing scholarly discussion.
