# Diogenes

## Overview
Diogenes of Sinope (c. 413/403 – c. 324/321 BC) was a foundational figure of Cynicism, a Hellenistic philosophy advocating virtue through radical self-sufficiency (*autarkeia*) and living "according to nature" (*kata physis*) rather than social convention (*nomos*). Exiled from his native Sinope for debasing the coinage—an act later interpreted as a metaphor for his mission to "revalue the currency" of conventional values—he embraced voluntary poverty in Athens and Corinth. His provocative public behavior (living in a storage jar, begging, masturbating in the marketplace, carrying a lamp in daylight to "look for a man") served as performative arguments against artificial social norms. He coined the term *kosmopolitēs* ("citizen of the world"), rejecting the polis as the sole locus of identity. Though he wrote dialogues, tragedies, and a *Politeia* (Republic), none survive; his philosophy is known through anecdotes (*chreiai*) recorded by Diogenes Laertius and others. His legacy bifurcated: Zeno of Citium adapted his ethics into Stoicism, while later Cynics preserved his ascetic practice.

## Timeline
- **c. 413/403 BC** — Born in Sinope (Black Sea) to Hicesias, a money-changer (*trapezitēs*); receives elite education.
- **c. 360s–350s BC** — Involved in currency debasement scandal (accounts differ on culpability); exiles or flees Sinope.
- **c. 350s BC** — Arrives in Athens; becomes disciple of Antisthenes (student of Socrates); adopts ascetic lifestyle in a *pithos* (jar).
- **c. 338 BC** — Captured by pirates en route to Aegina; sold into slavery in Crete to Xeniades of Corinth; tutors Xeniades' sons.
- **338 BC** — Captured by Philip II at Battle of Chaeronea; released after impressing Philip with audacity.
- **336 BC** — Meets Alexander the Great at Corinth; famously asks him to "stand out of my sun."
- **336 BC** — Attends 111th Olympic Games; crowns himself victor with pine wreath.
- **c. 324/321 BC** — Dies in Corinth (most likely) aged 81–90; cause disputed (old age, suicide, raw octopus, dog bite).

## Body

### Early Life and the Currency Scandal
Diogenes was born into a privileged family in Sinope, a Milesian colony on the Black Sea. His father, Hicesias, was a *trapezitēs* (authorized money-changer), and Diogenes served as an *epimelētēs* (magistrate). A defining event was the charge of "debasing the currency" (*paracharattein to nomisma*). Ancient sources conflict: some blame the father, others Diogenes; some claim Diogenes fled while his father died in prison. Modern scholarship is divided between viewing this as historical fact (supported by debased Sinopean coins c. 350–340 BC bearing the name Hikesio) and a philosophical fiction symbolizing his later project of challenging conventional values. A likely apocryphal oracle story claims Apollo told him to "adulterate the currency," which he first took literally before realizing its figurative meaning—a parody of Socrates' Delphic oracle.

### Exile and Conversion
The loss of his citizenship was a catastrophic social death in the Greek world, denying burial among ancestors. Diogenes reframed this trauma as philosophical liberation, embracing exile as the ultimate detachment from worldly ties (*apatheia*). Plutarch notes the hardship "transformed him into a philosopher." An anecdote regarding his slave Manes—who escaped, prompting Diogenes to remark, "If Manes can live without Diogenes, why not Diogenes without Manes?"—illustrates the Cynic tenet that possession (even of a slave) is a form of enslavement.

### Life in Athens: Performance as Pedagogy
In Athens, Diogenes lived in a large ceramic storage jar (*pithos*) and begged for sustenance. He adopted the persona of a "dog" (*kynikos*), the etymological root of "Cynic," explaining he "fawns on those who give, barks at those who don't." His public transgressions—eating in the marketplace (forbidden by *nomos*), masturbating, urinating on bystanders, rolling in hot sand, hugging frozen statues—were not mere eccentricity but *anaideia* (shamelessness): deliberate demonstrations that social taboos are unnatural conventions. He carried a lamp in daylight claiming to "look for a man" (*sophos*, a wise man), implying the city was empty of virtue. He mocked Plato’s definition of man as a "featherless biped" by presenting a plucked rooster, forcing Plato to add "broad, flat nails." When a follower of Zeno of Elea argued motion was impossible, Diogenes simply walked away, privileging empirical reality over abstract paradox.

### Later Life in Corinth and the Alexander Encounter
After a probable pirate capture and enslavement to Xeniades of Corinth (whom he told to "buy me, he needs a master"), Diogenes settled near the Craneum gymnasium. At the 111th Olympiad (336 BC), he crowned himself victor, declaring he competed against human ills (anger, fear, desire). The most famous anecdote involves Alexander the Great, likely meeting in 336 BC at the Isthmus of Corinth. Finding Diogenes sunning himself, Alexander offered a boon. Diogenes replied, "Stand out of my sun." Alexander reportedly declared, "If I were not Alexander, I would be Diogenes," recognizing the philosopher’s absolute autonomy (*autarkeia*) as a kingship superior to his own imperial power.

### Death and Burial Disputes
Diogenes died in the late 320s BC (c. 324/321 BC), possibly the same night as Alexander (June 323 BC), though scholars consider this synchronism legendary. Causes cited by ancient sources vary wildly: suicide by holding breath (symbolic self-determination), eating raw octopus (to prove cooking unnecessary), fever, or infected dog bite. Modern scholars (e.g., Roubineau) favor old age in Corinth. True to his indifference to *nomos*, he ordered his body thrown to wild animals or into a ditch. When disciples objected he would not feel the bites if unaware, he retorted, "If I am unaware, how can they hurt me?" His followers fought over his burial; the Corinthians interred him near the western gate, and a statue was later erected in Sinope.

### Works: Lost Corpus and *Politeia*
Diogenes Laertius lists dialogues, letters, tragedies, and a *Politeia* (Republic). None survive. The *Politeia* advocated radical communalism: shared wives, cannibalism of the dead, and incest—views so scandalous later Stoics denied its authenticity. His tragedies (e.g., *Heracles*, *Oedipus*, *Thyestes*) used myth to explore Cynic themes (endurance, rejection of taboo). A collection of pseudepigraphic letters circulated in antiquity. The *chreiai* (anecdotes) attributed to him may be by disciples like Metrocles.

### Philosophy: Influences and Core Concepts
**Influences:** Diogenes claimed discipleship to Antisthenes (c. 446–366 BC), a student of Socrates. He adopted Antisthenes' prioritization of virtue over law, religious skepticism, and deeds over theory. From Socrates he took the public interrogation of false wisdom but replaced irony with abrasive confrontation (Plato called him "Socrates gone mad"). Heraclitus influenced the Cynic view of common life as a fog (*typhos*) of illusion. Exile shaped his identification with heroic outcasts like Heracles and Odysseus.

**Challenging *Nomos*:** Diogenes held that *physis* (nature) is the only valid standard. Social conventions—manners, property, marriage, patriotism—are artificial constraints. His "shameless" acts were logical proofs: if an act is natural (eating, sex), doing it publicly cannot be wrong; shame is a learned error.

**Cosmopolitanism:** Asked his origin, he replied *kosmopolitēs* ("citizen of the world"), likely coining the term. He argued the only true polity is the cosmos. This was not modern universal brotherhood but a negative critique: the *polis* is an accident of birth, not a natural hierarchy. Favorinus suggests it was a consolation for his own statelessness.

**Asceticism and *Autarkeia*:** He distinguished *actual poverty* (lack of resources) from *feeling of poverty* (dissatisfaction from unmet social expectations). A tyrant craving more is "poor"; Diogenes, needing nothing, was "rich without a single obol." He discarded his cup seeing a boy drink from his hands, realizing nature provided a vessel. *Autarkeia* (self-sufficiency) required courage, rational indifference to suffering, and passive endurance of fate. His treatise *On Wealth* argued true wealth is intellectual management of needs, not accumulation.

## Terms
- ****Cynicism**** — Hellenistic philosophy (from *kynikos*, "dog-like") founded by Antisthenes and Diogenes; advocates virtue through living according to nature (*physis*) and rejecting convention (*nomos*) via asceticism and shamelessness (*anaideia*).
- ****Nomós (Nomos)**** — Law, custom, or social convention; contrasted with *physis* (nature) as the artificial target of Cynic critique.
- ****Physis**** — Nature; the Cynic standard of value, held to be self-sufficient and universal, unlike variable human laws.
- ****Autarkeia**** — Self-sufficiency; the Cynic ideal of independence from external goods, social approval, and desire.
- ****Anaideia**** — Shamelessness; deliberate public violation of taboos to demonstrate their conventionality and cultivate indifference to reputation.
- ****Kosmopolitēs**** — "Citizen of the world"; Diogenes' self-designation rejecting the *polis* as the sole political identity in favor of cosmic citizenship.
- ****Pithos**** — Large ceramic storage jar; Diogenes' shelter in Athens, symbolizing radical minimalism.
- ****Typhos**** — "Mist" or "fog"; Heraclitean term adopted by Cynics for the cloud of illusion (custom, desire, false opinion) obscuring reality.
- ****Chreia**** — Brief anecdote or saying attributed to a philosopher; the primary literary vehicle for preserving Cynic teachings.
- ****Paracharattein to nomisma**** — "Debase/revalue the currency"; literal charge against Diogenes/Hicesias, reinterpreted as metaphor for overturning conventional values.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Historicity of the currency crime:** Did Diogenes/Hicesias actually counterfeit coins (supported by numismatic evidence c. 350–340 BC), or is the story a later allegory for his philosophical "revaluation of values"?
*   **Authenticity of writings:** Were the *Politeia*, tragedies, and letters genuine, or pseudepigrapha by disciples (Philiscus, Pasiphon) or later Cynics? Stoics denied the *Politeia*'s authenticity due to its immorality; Emperor Julian doubted the tragedies' authorship.
*   **Disciple of Antisthenes?** Diogenes Laertius asserts he studied under Antisthenes at the Cynosarges, but chronological gaps (Antisthenes died c. 366 BC; Diogenes likely arrived in Athens later) make this debated.
*   **Date and cause of death:** Sources conflict on year (324–321 BC), location (Corinth, Athens, Olympia), and cause (old age, suicide, octopus, dog bite). The synchronism with Alexander's death (June 323 BC) is widely considered legendary.
*   **Nature of Cosmopolitanism:** Was Diogenes' *kosmopolitēs* a positive political ideal (world state) or a purely negative critique of the *polis*? Most scholars argue the latter; he did not advocate universal brotherhood.
*   **Diogenes Syndrome:** The modern psychiatric label for severe self-neglect/hoarding is acknowledged as a misnomer; Diogenes was a social minimalist who sought company in the Agora, not a withdrawn hoarder.
