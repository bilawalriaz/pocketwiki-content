# Heavy metal music

## Overview
Heavy metal is a rock music genre emerging in the late 1960s and early 1970s, defined by a thick, monumental sound with distorted guitars, emphatic beats, and high volume. Evolving from blues, psychedelic, and acid rock, it has spawned numerous subgenres, becoming a global cultural phenomenon with a dedicated fanbase and a complex history of commercial success, underground scenes, and social controversy.

## Timeline
- **Late 1960s** — Genre roots form from blues rock, psychedelic rock, and acid rock.
- **1968** — Pioneering bands Led Zeppelin, Black Sabbath, and Deep Purple are founded.
- **1970** — Black Sabbath releases *Black Sabbath*, generally accepted as the first heavy metal album.
- **Mid-1970s** — Judas Priest evolves the genre by discarding blues influences; Motörhead introduces punk sensibility and speed.
- **Late 1970s** — New Wave of British Heavy Metal (NWOBHM) emerges with bands like Iron Maiden and Saxon.
- **1980s** — Glam metal becomes popular; underground scenes produce thrash, death, and black metal.
- **1986** — Metallica's *Master of Puppets* becomes the first thrash metal Platinum record.
- **1991** — Metallica's self-titled "Black Album" brings mainstream success and redefines metal.
- **Mid-1990s** — Nu metal and groove metal expand the genre's definition, incorporating grunge and hip-hop elements.
- **2000s** — Metalcore and deathcore emerge as commercial forces; Scandinavian metal scenes thrive.
- **2010s** — Djent develops as a progressive metal spinoff; women gain more prominence in the genre.

## Body

### Origins and Characteristics
Heavy metal's sound, built on distortion-heavy riffs and power chords, traces roots to 1950s blues guitarists. Its direct lineage began in the mid-1960s with British blues-rock bands like The Kinks and The Who experimenting with feedback and volume. This loud blues rock combined with psychedelic and acid rock formed its original basis. Key early influencers included Cream, whose unison riffing and double bass drumming were essential prototypes, and the Jimi Hendrix Experience. The genre was codified in the late 1960s and early 1970s by bands like Led Zeppelin, Black Sabbath, and Deep Purple, who established its core elements: loud, distorted guitars, emphatic rhythms, dense bass-and-drum sound, and vigorous vocals. The electric guitar is the key element, with solos serving as a primary means of expressing virtuosity. Drumming is defined by speed, power, and precision, often using double-kick pedals and blast beats.

### Mainstream Success and Subgenre Proliferation
In the late 1970s, the New Wave of British Heavy Metal (NWOBHM) re-energized the genre, emphasizing faster tempos and a less bluesy sound. This led to mainstream success in the 1980s, with glam metal (e.g., Mötley Crüe, W.A.S.P.) dominating charts and MTV. Simultaneously, underground scenes produced more aggressive styles. Thrash metal, pioneered by the "Big Four" (Metallica, Slayer, Megadeth, Anthrax), broke into the mainstream in the mid-1980s. Extreme subgenres like death metal and black metal remained largely subcultural, emphasizing guttural vocals, down-tuned guitars, and themes of violence, Satanism, or paganism. Other significant subgenres include power metal (focusing on epic melodies), doom metal (emphasizing slow tempos and melancholy), and progressive metal (adopting complex compositional approaches).

### Lyrical Themes and Cultural Impact
Lyrically, heavy metal has concentrated on dark subject matter, personal trauma, and wider social issues like war. Sex is also a recurring theme. The genre's themes have frequently been criticized as juvenile, misogynistic, or occult-focused, leading to legal battles and censorship attempts, such as those by the U.S. Parents Music Resource Center (PMRC) in the 1980s. In some predominantly Muslim countries, heavy metal has been denounced and its fans arrested. Despite this, a 2009 study suggests metal fans suffer from poor mental health at a rate similar to or lower than the general population. The genre has a strong, exclusionary subculture with its own codes of dress (long hair, black T-shirts, leather jackets) and authenticity, where fans are known as "metalheads" or "headbangers."

### Evolution and Global Reach
Since the 1990s, popular styles have expanded metal's definition. Grunge and alternative rock challenged glam metal's dominance, while nu metal (e.g., Korn, Slipknot) incorporated hip-hop and grunge elements for mainstream success in the late 1990s. In the 2000s and 2010s, metalcore, deathcore, and djent emerged as significant styles. Metal has a global reach, with particularly strong scenes in Scandinavia, Germany, and Japan. Women have been involved since the genre's conception but were largely marginalized until the 1990s and 2000s, when female-fronted bands like Nightwish, Within Temptation, and Evanescence gained prominence, though allegations of sexism within the culture persist.

## Terms
- **Power Chord:** A simplified guitar chord, typically based on a perfect fifth interval, that sounds powerful when played with distortion at high volume.
- **Blast Beat:** A very fast drum beat characterized by rapid, alternating hits on the snare and bass drum, common in extreme metal subgenres.
- **Death Growl:** A guttural, low-pitched vocal style used in death metal and other extreme genres.
- **NWOBHM:** New Wave of British Heavy Metal, a movement in the late 1970s and early 1980s that re-energized the genre with faster, more aggressive sounds.
- **Headbanging:** A physical gesture where fans rhythmically beat time with their head, often emphasized by long hair.
- **Moshing:** A style of dance at metal concerts where participants bump and jostle each other in a "pit" near the stage.
- **Tritone:** An interval spanning three whole tones, historically considered dissonant and nicknamed "the devil in music," used frequently in metal harmony.
- **Pedal Point:** A sustained bass note over which changing harmonies are played, creating tension and a heavy sound.
- **Corpsepaint:** Black-and-white face makeup worn by some black metal musicians to create a grim, corpse-like appearance.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Genre Definition:** There is ongoing debate about which bands truly qualify as "heavy metal" versus "hard rock," with distinctions often based on musical style, audience identification, and shifting definitions over time.
- **First Heavy Metal Band:** Critics disagree over who was the first heavy metal band, with most crediting British bands Led Zeppelin and Black Sabbath, though American commentators sometimes favor Led Zeppelin and British commentators favor Black Sabbath.
- **Lyrical Content and Social Impact:** The genre has faced persistent accusations of promoting misogyny, violence, and the occult, leading to legal and congressional scrutiny. Scholars and fans debate the validity of these claims and the context of the lyrics.
- **Authenticity and "Selling Out":** The metal subculture has a strong code of authenticity, where performers are expected to appear devoted to the music and loyal to the subculture, never "selling out" to mainstream appeal. This leads to internal debates about which artists are "poseurs."
- **Sexism in the Scene:** While women have gained more visibility, debates continue about the prevalence of sexism within the metal community and whether the genre empowers or marginalizes female fans and musicians.
