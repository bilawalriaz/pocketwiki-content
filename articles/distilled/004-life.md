# Life

## Overview

Life is the capacity in matter, formed of one or more units called cells, for processes such as cell signaling, homeostasis, metabolism, cell growth, adaptation, response to stimuli, and reproduction. All life eventually reaches a state of death. Defining life is complicated by viruses, which replicate only in host cells, and the possibility of extraterrestrial life, which could be very different from life on Earth. Life exists all over the Earth in air, water, and soil, with many ecosystems forming the biosphere; the life in a particular ecosystem is called its biota.

Life has been studied since ancient times, with theories such as Empedocles's materialism and Aristotle's hylomorphism. Life originated at least 3.5 billion years ago, resulting in a universal common ancestor, which evolved into all existing species via many extinct ones, some leaving fossils. Modern classification began with Carl Linnaeus's system of binomial nomenclature in the 1740s. Living things are composed of biochemical molecules formed mainly from a few core chemical elements, and all contain proteins and nucleic acids (DNA and RNA). The cell is the structural and functional unit of life. Life is only known to exist on Earth, but extraterrestrial life is thought probable, and artificial life is being simulated and explored.

## Body

### Definitions

Since there is no consensus for a definition of life, most current definitions in biology are descriptive. Life is considered a characteristic of something that preserves, furthers or reinforces its existence in the given environment. This implies all or most of the following traits: homeostasis (regulation of the internal environment, e.g., sweating to reduce temperature); organisation (being structurally composed of one or more cells); metabolism (transformation of energy, used to convert chemicals into cellular components [anabolism] and to decompose organic matter [catabolism]); growth (maintenance of a higher rate of anabolism than catabolism); adaptation (the evolutionary process whereby an organism becomes better able to live in its habitat); response to stimuli (e.g., phototropism, chemotaxis); and reproduction (asexual or sexual).

From a physics perspective, an organism is a thermodynamic system with an organised molecular structure that can reproduce itself and evolve as survival dictates. Life has been described as an open system which makes use of gradients in its surroundings to create imperfect copies of itself. A NASA committee, based on a suggestion by Carl Sagan, defined life as "a self-sustained chemical system capable of undergoing Darwinian evolution," but this has been criticised because a single sexually reproducing individual is not alive by this definition. Others take a living systems theory viewpoint: living things are self-organizing and autopoietic (self-producing). Stuart Kauffman's definition describes life as an autonomous agent capable of reproducing itself and completing at least one thermodynamic work cycle. Living systems are characterized by a multiscale, hierarchical organization, from molecular machines to the whole biosphere.

Death is the termination of all vital functions or life processes in an organism or cell. Determining when death has occurred is difficult, as cessation of life functions is often not simultaneous across organ systems, requiring conceptual lines between life and death. Whether viruses should be considered alive is controversial; they possess genes, evolve by natural selection, and replicate by self-assembly, but do not metabolise and require a host cell. Virus self-assembly within host cells may support the hypothesis that life could have started as self-assembling organic molecules.

### History of study

Some of the earliest theories of life were materialist. Empedocles (430 BC) argued that everything is made of four eternal elements: earth, water, air, and fire. Democritus (460 BC) thought the soul was composed of fiery atoms. Plato held that the world was organised by permanent forms. Mechanistic materialism was revived by René Descartes (1596–1650), who held that animals and humans were machines. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz emphasised the hierarchical organization of living machines, and Julien Offray de La Mettrie (1709–1750) developed this in L'Homme Machine. Charles Darwin's evolutionary theory (1859) is a mechanistic explanation for the origin of species by natural selection.

Hylomorphism, first expressed by Aristotle (322 BC), holds that everything has both matter and form, and the form of a living thing is its soul. There are three kinds of souls: vegetative (plants), animal (animals), and rational (man only). Aristotle believed the soul cannot exist without the body. This account is consistent with teleological explanations of life, which account for phenomena in terms of purpose; this direction of causality contradicts the scientific evidence for natural selection, which explains consequences by prior causes.

Spontaneous generation, the belief that living organisms can form without descent from similar organisms, was proposed by Aristotle and considered the best explanation for two millennia. It was decisively dispelled by Louis Pasteur's experiments in 1859, expanding on investigations by Francesco Redi. Vitalism, the belief in a non-material life-principle, originated with Georg Ernst Stahl (17th century) and remained popular until the mid-19th century. It was disproved in 1828 when Friedrich Wöhler prepared urea from inorganic materials, and further undermined by Hermann von Helmholtz's demonstration that no energy is lost in muscle movement, and Eduard Buchner's demonstration that alcoholic fermentation could occur in cell-free extracts of yeast.

### Development

The age of Earth is about 4.54 billion years. Life on Earth has existed for at least 3.5 billion years, with the oldest physical traces dating back 3.7 billion years. Estimates from molecular clocks place the origin of life around 4 billion years ago. The earliest evidence includes biogenic graphite in 3.7 billion-year-old rocks from Western Greenland, microbial mat fossils in 3.48 billion-year-old sandstone from Western Australia, "remains of biotic life" in 4.1 billion-year-old rocks in Western Australia, and putative microfossils up to 4.28 billion years old in Quebec, Canada, suggesting "an almost instantaneous emergence of life" after ocean formation 4.4 billion years ago.

Evolution is the change in heritable characteristics of biological populations over successive generations, resulting in new species and often the disappearance of old ones. It occurs when natural selection (including sexual selection) and genetic drift act on genetic variation. Fossils are preserved remains or traces of organisms from the remote past; a specimen is called a fossil if older than 10,000 years. Extinction is the process by which a species dies out, determined retrospectively after a period of apparent absence. Over 99% of all species that have ever lived are now extinct; mass extinctions may have accelerated evolution by providing opportunities for diversification.

### Environmental conditions

The diversity of life results from the interplay between genetic opportunity, metabolic capability, environmental challenges, and symbiosis. For example, the release of molecular oxygen by cyanobacteria as a by-product of photosynthesis induced global changes, posing novel evolutionary challenges because oxygen was toxic to most life at the time, and ultimately resulting in the formation of Earth's major animal and plant species. The biosphere is the global sum of all ecosystems, a closed system (apart from solar and cosmic radiation and heat from the interior of the Earth) and largely self-regulating. Organisms exist in every part of the biosphere, including soil, hot springs, inside rocks at least 19 km deep underground, the deepest parts of the ocean, and at least 64 km high in the atmosphere. Life forms have been observed to survive in the vacuum of space, thrive in the deep Mariana Trench, and live 800 m below the ice of Antarctica.

Organisms must tolerate a range of conditions defined as the "range of tolerance"; outside this are "zones of physiological stress" (survival and reproduction possible but not optimal) and "zones of intolerance" (survival and reproduction unlikely or impossible). Some microorganisms, called extremophiles, have evolved to withstand freezing, complete desiccation, starvation, high levels of radiation exposure, and other challenges, and excel at exploiting uncommon sources of energy.

### Classification

The first classification of organisms was made by Aristotle (384–322 BC), who grouped living things as either plants or animals based mainly on their ability to move. He distinguished animals with blood from those without, and divided each into five groups. This theory remained dominant for more than a thousand years. In ancient Indian medicine, the Sushruta Samhita classified substances into sthavara ("immobile") and jangama ("mobile"); the Charaka Samhita used a three-way division including artificial (samyogaja) categories. In ancient China, the Erya dictionary organized entries into 19 semantic categories including grasses, trees, insects, fish, birds, and beasts.

In the late 1740s, Carl Linnaeus introduced his system of binomial nomenclature. Fungi were originally treated as plants; Herbert Copeland classified them in his Protoctista, and Whittaker eventually gave them their own kingdom in his five-kingdom system. Advances in microscopy revealed new groups of life, leading to Ernst Haeckel's kingdom Protista, the splitting off of prokaryotes into Monera, and eventually the current three-domain system (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukaryota). The classification of eukaryotes, especially protists, is still controversial. The ability to sequence large numbers of complete genomes has allowed a metagenomic view of the whole tree of life, revealing that the majority of living things are bacteria and that all have a common origin.

### Composition

All life forms require core chemical elements: carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur—the elemental macronutrients for all organisms. Together these make up nucleic acids, proteins and lipids. Carbon is the most abundant, forming multiple, stable covalent bonds. DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) carries most of the genetic instructions used in the growth, development, functioning and reproduction of all known living organisms and many viruses. Most DNA molecules consist of two biopolymer strands coiled around each other to form a double helix, composed of nucleotides (a nitrogen-containing nucleobase—cytosine, guanine, adenine, or thymine—plus a sugar called deoxyribose and a phosphate group). Base pairing rules (A with T, C with G) bind the strands, and each strand contains all the information needed to recreate the other, enabling information preservation during reproduction.

Cells are the basic unit of structure in every living thing, and all cells arise from pre-existing cells by division. Cell theory was formulated by Henri Dutrochet, Theodor Schwann, Rudolf Virchow and others during the early nineteenth century. Prokaryote cells lack a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles; Bacteria and Archaea are the two domains of prokaryotes. Eukaryote cells have a distinct nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; all species of large complex organisms are eukaryotes. The conventional model is that eukaryotes evolved from prokaryotes, with main organelles forming through endosymbiosis between bacteria and the progenitor eukaryotic cell. Multicellular organisms may have first evolved through colonies of identical cells; true multicellular organisms have developed specialisations making members dependent on the remainder of the organism. About 800 million years ago, a minor genetic change in the enzyme GK-PID may have allowed organisms to go from single cell to many cells.

### In the universe and artificial

Though life is confirmed only on Earth, many think extraterrestrial life is probable or inevitable. Other planets and moons are being examined for evidence of having once supported simple life, and projects such as SETI try to detect radio transmissions from possible alien civilisations. The region around another main-sequence star that could support Earth-like life is known as the habitable zone; stars in the intermediate mass range such as the Sun may have a greater likelihood for Earth-like life to develop. The variables of the Drake equation are used to discuss conditions where civilisation is most likely to exist. Artificial life is the simulation of any aspect of life, as through computers, robotics, or biochemistry. Synthetic biology combines science and biological engineering, with the goal of designing and building engineered biological systems that process information, manipulate chemicals, produce energy, and maintain and enhance human health and the environment.

## Terms

- **Biota**: The assemblage of living things inhabiting a specific place and time, such as an ecosystem or biome.
- **Homeostasis**: Regulation of the internal environment to maintain a constant state.
- **Metabolism**: Transformation of energy, used to convert chemicals into cellular components (anabolism) and to decompose organic matter (catabolism).
- **Autopoietic**: Self-producing; a systemic definition of life where living things are self-organizing.
- **Hylomorphism**: Aristotle's theory that everything has both matter and form, with the form of a living thing being its soul.
- **Spontaneous generation**: The belief that living organisms can form without descent from similar organisms; disproved by Pasteur in 1859.
- **Vitalism**: The belief that there is a non-material life-principle; disproved by Wöhler's synthesis of urea in 1828.
- **Extremophiles**: Microorganisms that withstand freezing, desiccation, starvation, high radiation, and other extreme conditions.
- **Binomial nomenclature**: Linnaeus's system of naming species, introduced in the late 1740s.
- **Habitable zone**: The region around a star that could support Earth-like life on an Earth-like planet.

## Debates and open questions

- There is no consensus for a definition of life; at least 123 definitions have been compiled. Life is a process, not a substance, complicating definitions.
- Whether viruses should be considered alive is controversial; they possess genes and evolve but do not metabolise and require host cells.
- The NASA definition of life as "a self-sustained chemical system capable of undergoing Darwinian evolution" has been criticised because a single sexually reproducing individual is not alive by this definition.
- The classification of eukaryotes, especially protists, is still controversial; the Protozoa and Chromista do not form clades or natural groupings.
- The exact timing and mechanism of life's origin is debated, with evidence ranging from 3.5 to 4.28 billion years ago, and hypotheses attempting to explain the formation of a universal last common ancestor from simple organic molecules.
