# Lake Michigan

## Overview
Lake Michigan is one of North America's five Great Lakes and the only one located entirely within the United States. It is the world's largest freshwater lake by surface area when considered jointly with Lake Huron, to which it is hydrologically connected. The lake is a vital resource for drinking water, transportation, and recreation for the millions of people living along its shores.

## Timeline
- **c. 800 AD** — Decline of the Hopewell culture in the region.
- **Early 17th C.** — European explorers first encounter descendants of the Late Woodland Native Americans.
- **1634 or 1638** — French explorer Jean Nicolet is believed to be the first European to reach the lake.
- **1640s-1650s** — The Beaver Wars cause a major demographic shift, forcing populations to flee west and north of the lake.
- **1671** — A French Catholic mission is founded at St. Ignace.
- **1673** — Jacques Marquette and Louis Jolliet explore the lake and its tributaries.
- **1715** — Fort Michilimackinac is founded on the southern side of the Straits of Mackinac.
- **1781** — Fort Mackinac is founded on Mackinac Island.
- **19th Century** — The lake becomes integral to the development of Chicago and the Midwest, with 90% of Chicago's grain shipped east via the lake before the Civil War.
- **1985** — Scientist J. Val Klump becomes the first person to reach the lake's deep bottom via submersible.
- **2013** — Lake Michigan's water level dips to an all-time low since records began in 1918.
- **2018** — A Purdue University report details the steady warming of the lake's surface temperature since 1980.

## Body

### History and Human Settlement
The region was home to Native American cultures, including the Hopewell and later the Late Woodland peoples. By the early 17th century, European explorers encountered tribes such as the Ojibwe, Potawatomi, and others. The Straits of Mackinac became a crucial travel and fur-trade corridor, leading to the establishment of French missions and forts like Fort Michilimackinac (1715). In the 19th century, the lake was central to the economic growth of Chicago and the Midwest, serving as a primary shipping route for grain and other goods.

### Hydrology and Statistics
Lake Michigan is hydrologically a single body of water with Lake Huron, connected by the wide Straits of Mackinac. Together, they form the largest freshwater lake in the world by surface area. The lake is divided into northern and southern basins by the Milwaukee Reef. Its water levels fluctuate seasonally, with historical highs and lows recorded in 1986 and 2013, respectively. The lake's water levels are influenced by factors like snowpack and summer temperatures.

### Shoreline and Geography
The lake's 1,640-mile shoreline features the world's largest freshwater sand dune system, particularly along its eastern shore in Michigan and Indiana. The region is often called the "Third Coast." Beaches vary from sandy dunes to rocky shores. Major cities along the lake include Chicago, Milwaukee, and Gary. The lake contains numerous islands, with Beaver Island being the largest.

### Human Activities and Ecology
The lake supports a major sport and commercial fishery. Native species like lake trout declined due to invasive sea lampreys and overharvesting, leading to the successful introduction of Pacific salmon. However, invasive species like zebra mussels continue to alter the ecosystem. The lake is a critical shipping lane for bulk goods like iron ore and grain, though total shipping has trended downward. Two major car ferries, the SS Badger and Lake Express, connect Wisconsin and Michigan. Tourism and recreation, including water sports and lighthouse viewing, are significant industries.

## Terms
- **Hydrologically connected:** Describes two or more bodies of water that are functionally a single system due to a free and open connection.
- **Datum:** A fixed reference point for measuring water levels; for the Great Lakes, it is a specific elevation in feet above sea level.
- **Beaver Wars:** A series of 17th-century conflicts initiated by the Iroquois to control the fur trade with European colonies.
- **Coureurs des bois:** Unlicensed French-Canadian fur traders who operated in the North American interior.
- **Saint Lawrence Seaway:** A system of locks, canals, and channels that allows ocean-going vessels to travel from the Atlantic Ocean to the Great Lakes.
- **Invasive species:** Non-native organisms that cause ecological or economic harm upon introduction to a new environment.
- **Singing sands:** Beach sand with a high quartz content that emits a squeaking sound when walked upon.
- **Petoskey stones:** Fossilized coral stones that are the state stone of Michigan, found only in the Lake Michigan region.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the warming of Lake Michigan's surface temperature is a subject of study, with a 2018 report indicating steady increases since 1980. This trend is likely to adversely affect native species and habitats. The ecological impact of ongoing invasive species introductions, such as the round goby and quagga mussels, remains a major concern for the lake's ecosystem health.
