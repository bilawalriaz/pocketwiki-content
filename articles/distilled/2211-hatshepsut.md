# Hatshepsut

## Overview
Hatshepsut was the sixth pharaoh of Egypt's Eighteenth Dynasty, ruling as a female king from c. 1479 to c. 1458 BC. She is significant as one of the most successful and prolific female rulers of ancient Egypt, whose prosperous reign was later followed by a systematic attempt to erase her from the historical record.

## Timeline
- **c. 1505–1495 BC** — Born as eldest daughter of Thutmose I and Ahmose.
- **c. 1479 BC** — Thutmose II dies; Hatshepsut becomes regent for her stepson, Thutmose III.
- **c. 1472 BC** — By Year 7 of Thutmose III's reign, Hatshepsut assumes the full titulary of pharaoh, becoming senior co-ruler.
- **c. 1471 BC** — Year 9: Expedition to the Land of Punt.
- **c. 1460 BC** — Year 20: Last dated attestation of Hatshepsut as pharaoh.
- **c. 1458 BC** — Year 22 of Thutmose III: Hatshepsut ceases to reign; Thutmose III becomes sole ruler.
- **Late 18th Dynasty** — Systematic erasure of Hatshepsut's name and images from monuments begins.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Hatshepsut was the daughter of Thutmose I. After marrying her half-brother Thutmose II, she became Great Royal Wife. When Thutmose II died, his son Thutmose III was a child, and Hatshepsut initially ruled as regent. Several years into this regency, she assumed the full royal titulary and position of pharaoh, becoming a senior co-ruler alongside Thutmose III. To legitimize her rule in a patriarchal system, she adopted male pharaonic regalia, including a false beard, and was depicted with masculine traits. She also promoted a divine birth narrative, claiming the god Amun was her father.

### Reign and Accomplishments
Hatshepsut's reign was a period of peace and prosperity. She was a prolific builder, commissioning projects like the Karnak Temple Complex, the Red Chapel, and her famous mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari. She re-established trade networks disrupted by the Hyksos, most notably a successful expedition to Punt that brought back myrrh trees and other luxury goods. Her building projects and official inscriptions heavily promoted her achievements and divine right to rule.

### Death and Erasure
Hatshepsut presumably died in Year 22 of Thutmose III's reign. She was likely buried in tomb KV20, which she had prepared for herself and her father, Thutmose I. Towards the end of Thutmose III's reign and into the reign of his son Amenhotep II, a campaign was undertaken to remove Hatshepsut from official records. Her cartouches were chiseled off monuments, her statues were destroyed or buried, and her achievements were reassigned to other pharaohs. The exact motivation for this erasure is debated.

## Terms
*   **Pharaoh:** The title for the monarch of ancient Egypt, considered a divine ruler.
*   **Regent:** A person appointed to administer a state because the monarch is a minor, absent, or incapacitated.
*   **Queen Regnant:** A female monarch reigning in her own right.
*   **Great Royal Wife:** The principal wife of the pharaoh, a title of high status.
*   **Cartouche:** An oval frame enclosing a group of Egyptian hieroglyphs, typically representing the name of a monarch.
*   **Heb Sed:** A jubilee festival celebrated by a pharaoh after a long period of rule, traditionally after 30 years.
*   **Mortuary Temple:** A temple built for the afterlife cult of a deceased pharaoh.
*   **God's Wife of Amun:** A powerful priestly title held by royal women, especially in the New Kingdom.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Motivation for Erasure:** Scholars debate whether the erasure of Hatshepsut was due to personal resentment by Thutmose III, a political necessity to reassert traditional male succession, or a routine ritual deactivation of her monuments.
*   **Identification of Her Mummy:** A mummy (KV60A) was proposed to be Hatshepsut based on a matching tooth, but later analysis cast doubt on this identification, and DNA testing has been refused.
*   **The "Hatshepsut Problem":** Her successful reign and subsequent erasure created confusion in early Egyptology about the order of succession in the Eighteenth Dynasty, highlighting conflicts between power and traditional gender roles.
