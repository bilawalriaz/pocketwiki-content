# Las Meninas

## Overview
*Las Meninas* (1656) is a monumental oil painting by Diego Velázquez, the leading artist in the court of King Philip IV of Spain. Set in the Royal Alcázar of Madrid, it depicts the Infanta Margaret Theresa surrounded by her entourage, with Velázquez himself at work on a large canvas. The painting is celebrated for its complex composition, which creates an enigmatic relationship between the viewer, the artist, and the royal subjects, raising profound questions about reality, illusion, and representation. It is considered a masterpiece of the Spanish Golden Age and a focal point for scholarly debate.

## Timeline
- **1656** — Diego Velázquez paints *Las Meninas* in his studio in the Royal Alcázar of Madrid.
- **1666** — The painting is first described in an inventory by García de Medrano.
- **1724** — Antonio Palomino publishes a detailed description identifying several figures.
- **1734** — The painting is damaged in a fire that destroys the Alcázar; it is later restored.
- **1819** — The painting enters the collection of the newly founded Museo del Prado.
- **1843** — The Prado catalogue lists the work for the first time as *Las Meninas*.
- **1984** — The painting is cleaned under the supervision of John Brealey, removing a "yellow veil" of dust.

## Body

### Background and Provenance
Velázquez was appointed palace chamberlain in 1651, a high-status but time-consuming role. The painting is set in his studio, the Pieza Principal of the late Balthasar Charles's quarters. It depicts the five-year-old Infanta Margaret Theresa, the only surviving child of Philip IV and Mariana of Austria at the time. The work was referred to in early inventories as *La Familia* ("The Family"). It was damaged in the 1734 Alcázar fire and restored. The painting has been cut down on both sides and has lost some texture and hue over time due to pollution and visitors. A 1984 cleaning removed accumulated dust but provoked public protest.

### Subjects and Composition
The scene is set in a high-ceilinged room, presented as a perspective grid. In the center foreground stands the Infanta Margaret Theresa, attended by two ladies-in-waiting (*meninas*), a chaperone, a bodyguard, two dwarfs, and a dog. To the left, Velázquez portrays himself at a large canvas, looking outward. On the back wall, a mirror reflects the upper bodies of King Philip IV and Queen Mariana. The composition is organized around three focal points: the Infanta, Velázquez's self-portrait, and the reflected royal couple. Light from a window on the right and an open doorway in the background creates depth and defines these focal points. The vanishing point of the perspective is in the doorway, where the queen's chamberlain, Don José Nieto Velázquez, is paused on the steps.

### The Mirror and Spatial Ambiguity
The mirror on the back wall reflects the king and queen, who are presumed to be standing outside the picture space in the viewer's position. This creates a central ambiguity: the viewer may be seeing the scene from the royal couple's perspective, or the couple may be reflected from the canvas Velázquez is painting. Some scholars, like Joel Snyder, propose the mirror reflects the canvas itself, making the painting a "mirror of majesty." The mirror's flat, clear reflection contrasts with the convex mirror in Jan van Eyck's *Arnolfini Portrait*, which may have influenced Velázquez.

### Interpretation and Influence
The painting explores the relationship between illusion and reality, a key concern in 17th-century Spanish culture. Svetlana Alpers argues Velázquez claimed high status for the artist by depicting himself at work among royalty, asserting painting as a liberal art. Michel Foucault analyzed the painting's complex network of gazes and its representation of classical representation itself. The work has been influential, inspiring artists like Francisco Goya and Pablo Picasso, who painted a series of 58 interpretations in 1957.

## Terms
- **Meninas**: Spanish for "ladies-in-waiting"; the maids of honor attending the Infanta.
- **Infanta**: A daughter of the Spanish monarch, not in line for the throne.
- **Pentimenti**: Traces of earlier work or alterations made by the artist during painting.
- **Aposentador mayor del palacio**: The palace chamberlain, a high court office held by Velázquez.
- **Búcaro**: A small cup or vase, often of red clay, offered to the Infanta.
- **Order of Santiago**: A prestigious Spanish military order; the red cross on Velázquez's chest was added later.
- **Episteme**: A term used by Foucault to describe the underlying framework of knowledge in a particular era.
- **Liberal art**: An intellectual pursuit, like painting, distinguished from mechanical crafts.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The Mirror's Reflection**: Does the mirror show the king and queen posing for their portrait, or does it reflect the canvas Velázquez is painting? Scholars like Leo Steinberg and Joel Snyder offer competing accounts.
- **The Viewer's Position**: Is the viewer standing in the place of the king and queen, or is the royal couple standing beside the viewer? The gazes of Velázquez, the Infanta, and the dwarf Maribarbola complicate this.
- **The Painting's Subject**: Is it a portrait of the Infanta, a depiction of the royal family, or a meta-commentary on the act of painting and representation itself?
- **The Canvas Velázquez Paints**: Its size and subject are unknown. Some argue it is *Las Meninas* itself, creating a self-referential loop.
