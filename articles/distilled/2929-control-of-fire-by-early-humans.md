# Control of fire by early humans

## Overview
The control of fire by early humans was a pivotal technology that enabled geographic expansion, cultural innovation, and significant biological and social changes. It provided warmth, protection, light, and a means to cook food, which increased caloric intake and may have driven brain development. The process evolved gradually from opportunistic use of natural wildfires to the deliberate creation of fire.

## Timeline
- **c. 300,000–400,000 years ago** — Earliest definitive evidence for deliberate fire-making (igniting new fires) found at a Neanderthal site in Barnham, England.
- **c. 164,000 years ago** — Systematic use of fire for heat-treating silcrete stone for toolmaking at Pinnacle Point, South Africa.
- **c. 400,000–300,000 years ago** — Evidence for controlled fire becomes widespread and convincing.
- **c. 780,000 years ago** — Oldest likely evidence for cooking (heated fish teeth) found at Daughters of Jacob Bridge, Golan Heights.
- **c. 1.0–1.79 million years ago** — Earliest evidence for controlled use of fire in Wonderwerk Cave, South Africa.

## Body

### The Gradual Process of Control
The control of fire was not a single event but a multi-stage process. It began with a habitat shift to savannas where wildfires were common. Early humans likely first interacted with fire by foraging in recently burned areas. The next steps involved using residual hot spots, transporting fire from burned to unburned areas, and eventually maintaining fires for extended periods, possibly leading to base campsites. The ability to make fire on demand, likely with friction devices, was a later development. Each stage could be practiced opportunistically, habitually, or obligately.

### Archaeological Evidence
Evidence for fire use is categorized by geological period. The earliest, from the Lower Paleolithic (e.g., Wonderwerk Cave, 1.0–1.79 Mya), is often uncertain and debated. Middle Paleolithic evidence (e.g., Kalambo Falls, 180,000 BP; Zhoukoudian, 460,000–230,000 BP) is more widespread and includes hearths and heat-treated tools. The oldest definitive evidence for deliberate fire-making comes from a Neanderthal site in Barnham, England, dated to ~400,000 years ago, where baked earth, flint, and pyrite (used to strike sparks) were found.

### Impact on Human Evolution and Society
Fire had profound effects. It enabled geographic expansion into colder climates by providing warmth. It was used for hunting, toolmaking (e.g., heat-treating stone ~164,000 years ago), and creating art and ceramics. Socially, gathering around hearths may have extended the day, fostered language development, and required larger cooperative groups. The "cooking hypothesis" proposes that cooking food increased caloric and nutrient intake, allowing for the evolution of larger brains and smaller digestive systems. Critics argue that brain growth began before strong evidence for cooking and that the energetic gain from cooking may be overstated.

## Terms
- **Controlled fire**: The ability to use, maintain, and eventually create fire at will, as opposed to merely using natural wildfires.
- **Heat treatment**: The process of using fire to alter the properties of materials, such as making silcrete stone easier to flake for toolmaking.
- **Cooking hypothesis**: The theory that cooking food with fire provided the extra energy needed for human brain growth and other biological changes.
- **Homo erectus**: An extinct species of early human, living from about 2 million to 110,000 years ago, associated with early evidence of fire use.
- **Neanderthal**: An extinct species or subspecies of archaic humans who lived in Eurasia until about 40,000 years ago, with evidence of fire-making.
- **Pyrite**: A mineral ("fool's gold") that can be struck with flint to produce sparks for starting fires.
- **Silcrete**: A fine-grained rock that early humans heat-treated to improve its quality for making stone tools.
- **Micromorphological analysis**: A technique for studying microscopic layers of sediment to identify evidence like ash or burned bone.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Timing of cooking**: While some evidence suggests cooking as early as ~1.8 million years ago (based on physiological changes), definitive archaeological evidence for cooking is more recent (~780,000 years ago).
- **Significance for brain growth**: Critics of the cooking hypothesis argue that significant brain growth occurred in *Homo* species before strong evidence for widespread cooking, and that a shift to a meat-based diet may have been more critical.
- **Evidence interpretation**: Many early claims for fire control (e.g., at Chesowanja, Koobi Fora) are debated because natural processes (like wildfires or volcanic activity) could explain the findings.
