# Amazon River

## Overview

The Amazon River in South America is the largest river by discharge volume in the world and is disputed with the Nile as the longest river system. It represents about 20% of global riverine discharge into oceans, with an average discharge of roughly 215,000–230,000 cubic meters per second. The river and its basin—the largest drainage basin on Earth at approximately 7,000,000 square kilometers—support over 47 million people and more than 10% of all known species. Its history spans geological reversal, pre-Columbian complex societies, European exploration, colonial exploitation, and modern development challenges.

## Timeline

- **c. 7500–4000 BC** — Earliest shell mounds indicate habitation; associated with ceramic age cultures
- **1541** — Gonzalo Pizarro and Francisco de Orellana depart Quito seeking El Dorado; Orellana's expedition becomes the first European descent of the Amazon
- **1542** — Orellana reaches the river's mouth on 24 August, demonstrating navigability; river named after Amazon warriors
- **1637** — Pedro Teixeira becomes first European to travel up the entire river, arriving in Quito
- **1835–1840** — Cabanagem revolt against white ruling class; estimated 30–40% of Grão-Pará's 100,000 people died
- **1850** — Emperor Pedro II sanctions steam navigation on the Amazon; Viscount of Mauá organizes the navigation company
- **1867** — Brazil decrees the opening of the Amazon to all countries, effective 7 September
- **1911** — Rubber exports reach 44,000 tons annually; Iquitos grows from 200 (1851) to 20,000 (1900)
- **1940s** — President Getúlio Vargas drives interior development; WWII Allied rubber demand funds expansion
- **1970s** — National Integration Program relocates northeastern families; Trans-Amazonian Highway built but largely fails
- **2014** — Study identifies Cordillera Rumi Cruz (Mantaro drainage) as the most distant source, ~80 km farther than Nevado Mismi

## Body

### Geological history and origins

For millions of years the Amazon flowed west as part of a proto-Amazon-Congo system when Africa and South America were connected in western Gondwana. About 80 million years ago the continents split. The Andes' main uplift began 15 million years ago due to Nazca Plate subduction beneath the South American Plate; the rising Andes and linked Brazilian/Guyana shields blocked the river, creating a vast inland sea that became a swampy freshwater lake. Between 11 and 10 million years ago, waters cut through sandstone and the Amazon began flowing eastward, giving rise to the Amazon rainforest. The river originated as transcontinental in the Miocene (11.8–11.3 million years ago) and took present shape ~2.4 million years ago in the Early Pleistocene. A large aquifer paralleling the river, dubbed the Hamza River, was publicized in August 2011.

### Pre-Columbian era

Amazonian societies were deeply involved in the emergence of South America's highland agrarian systems; trade with Andean civilizations (including the Muisca and Incas) contributed to their social and religious development. Early settlements sat on low hills or mounds: shell mounds (dated 7500–4000 BC, all ceramic-age), artificial earth platforms (best represented by the Marajoara culture), and figurative mounds. Archaeologists estimate over 3 million indigenous people lived around the Amazon by 1541. Pre-Columbian inhabitants altered forest ecology through selective cultivation and fire, creating nutrient-rich dark soils called terra preta de índio, enabling large-scale agriculture; this practice may have begun ~11,000 years ago and may explain a band of lower rainfall. Complex chiefdoms developed towns; Marajó island may have supported 100,000 people with social stratification. Many tribes warred constantly; the Munduruku expansion in the 18th century dislocated the Kawahíb and attacked Brazilian settlements from 1770.

### European arrival and exploration

In March 1500, Vicente Yáñez Pinzón was the first documented European to sail up the Amazon, calling it Río Santa María del Mar Dulce for its freshwater pushing into the ocean. Francisco de Orellana made the first full descent: in 1541 he left Pizarro's expedition (searching for El Dorado and cinnamon) to find food, missed the intended confluence, and after mutiny threats changed the mission to discovery. He reached the Rio Negro confluence on 3 June 1542 and the Atlantic mouth on 24 August 1542. The name "Amazonas" came from native women warriors whose attack reminded Orellana of mythical Greek Amazons. Lope de Aguirre (1560) may have made a second descent, though historians debate whether it was the Amazon or Orinoco. Pedro Teixeira (1637) was first to travel up the entire river; António Raposo Tavares (1648–1652) led an overland expedition covering over 10,000 km. Czech Jesuit Samuel Fritz established ~40 mission villages and proposed (1707 map) the Marañón as the source, a view held through the 20th century. Scientific exploration followed: La Condamine (1743), Humboldt (1799–1804), Spix and Martius (1817–1820), Langsdorff (1826–1828), Bates and Wallace (1848–1859), and Spruce (1849–1864).

### Post-colonial exploitation and 20th-century development

In 1850 the Brazilian Amazon's population was perhaps 300,000 (175,000 Europeans, 25,000 slaves); Pará (Belém) had 10,000–12,000 inhabitants, Manaus 1,000–1,500. Steam navigation began in 1853 with four small steamers; the 1867 decree opened the river to all countries at defined points. Steamboat commerce and rubber demand made Iquitos a cosmopolitan center; diseases (typhus, malaria) killed an estimated 40,000 native Amazonians during the rubber boom. By 1900, exports included rubber, cacao, Brazil nuts, pelts, and forest products. Four centuries after discovery, cultivated area in the basin was probably under 65 km². In the 1940s, Vargas pushed interior development with WWII rubber demand funding. The 1960s "Operation Amazon" brought large-scale agriculture via credit and fiscal incentives. The 1970s National Integration Program relocated northeastern families and built the Trans-Amazonian Highway, but the program failed—settlers were unequipped for the rainforest ecosystem, and large road portions became derelict. Manaus, with 1.9 million people in 2014, is the largest city on the river (64% pardo, 32% white). Although the Amazon itself remains undammed, ~412 dams operate on tributaries (151 on six main tributaries); scientists worry further damming could block fish-spawning runs and cause "ecosystem collapse."

### Course, watershed, and flooding

The most distant source was long thought to be Nevado Mismi (5,597 m) in the Apurímac drainage; a 2014 study identified the Mantaro drainage's Cordillera Rumi Cruz instead, ~80 km farther upstream. The Apurímac joins the Mantaro to form the Ene, then Tambo, then Ucayali; the Ucayali–Marañón confluence begins the Amazon proper (called Solimões in Brazil above the Rio Negro confluence at Manaus). The Lower Amazon begins where Rio Negro's dark waters meet the sandy Solimões, running side by side without mixing for over 6 km. The mouth's width is disputed: including the Pará river and Marajó island frontage, the estuary is ~325 km wide; the main channel alone is ~15 km. The river's plume covers up to 1.3 million km², affecting salinity, pH, light penetration, and sedimentation in the tropical north Atlantic. No bridges cross the river's full width (the Manaus Iranduba Bridge spans the Rio Negro tributary). The basin covers ~40% of South America; flooding rises over 9 m annually, covering 110,000 km² in dry season and 350,000 km² in wet season. The pororoca, an undular tidal bore, can reach 7.6 m high and travel 800 km inland. Tributaries flood at different times; the Madeira rises and falls two months earlier than most. Depth averages 20–50 m in the lowermost section, up to 100 m in places. Large ocean steamers reach Manaus (1,500 km upriver); smaller vessels reach Iquitos (3,600 km).

### Biodiversity

The Amazon rainforest is the richest tropical forest in biodiversity, hosting more than 10% of all known species. The boto (Amazon river dolphin, Inia geoffrensis) is the largest river dolphin, growing to 2.6 m, changing from gray to pink to white with age, and using echolocation. The tucuxi (Sotalia fluviatilis) lives in both river and coastal waters; the Amazonian manatee (Trichechus inunguis) is a vulnerable herbivore confined to freshwater. The giant otter (Pteronura brasiliensis), a top carnivore, has declined due to habitat destruction and hunting and is on CITES Appendix I. The anaconda inhabits shallow waters; caimans and turtles also live there. More than 5,600 fish species were known as of 2011, with ~50 new species discovered yearly, including the arapaima (up to 4.6 m), arowana, piranha (30–60 species), candirú (parasitic catfish), and goliath catfish (up to 3.6 m, 200 kg). The electric eel and over 100 electric fish species (Gymnotiformes) inhabit the basin; the bull shark has been reported 4,000 km upriver at Iquitos. Key river microbes include Actinomycetota, Alpha-, Beta-, and Gammaproteobacteria, and Thermoproteota.

## Terms

- **Discharge**: Volume of water flowing past a point per unit time; the Amazon's average is ~215,000–230,000 m³/s.
- **Terra preta de índio**: "Indian black earth," nutrient-rich dark soil created by pre-Columbian burning and cultivation, enabling sustainable large-scale agriculture.
- **Anabranches**: Multiple channels into which a river divides, often very long, connected by natural canals.
- **Várzea**: "Flooded forests" inundated every rainy season when the river rises over 9 m.
- **Pororoca**: Undular tidal bore at the mouth, with a leading wave up to 7.6 m traveling up to 800 km inland.
- **Bifurcation**: A river that links two major river systems; the Casiquiare canal connects the Orinoco and Amazon basins.
- **Euryhaline**: Able to thrive in both salt and fresh water, as with the bull shark.
- **Metagenomics**: Technique for studying microbial communities directly from environmental samples, used to identify Amazon River microbes.

## Debates and open questions

The Amazon–Nile length dispute remains unresolved. Historic consensus favored the Nile as longest, but reported lengths vary widely: the Amazon from 6,275 to 6,992 km, the Nile from 5,499 to 7,088 km. A 2008 Brazilian INPE claim (Amazon 6,992 km, Nile 6,853 km) was never peer-reviewed; a 2009 peer-reviewed study concluded the Nile is longer (7,088 km vs. 6,575 km). Measurement depends on source position, mouth definition, scale, and technique. The most distant source is also debated: the 2014 Mantaro/Cordillera Rumi Cruz finding challenges the long-held Nevado Mismi/Apurímac consensus. The mouth's location and width are disputed due to the Pará river, furos channels, and Marajó island geography. Whether Lope de Aguirre descended the Amazon or Orinoco remains uncertain. The Encyclopædia Britannica states the final length remains open to interpretation and continued debate.
