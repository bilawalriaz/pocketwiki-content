# Western imperialism in Asia

## Overview
Western imperialism in Asia began in the 15th century as a search for new trade routes to bypass Ottoman-controlled overland paths. It evolved from coastal trading posts into vast colonial empires that reshaped the continent's political, economic, and social structures. The era peaked in the late 19th century and largely ended with decolonization following World War II, though its legacy persists in modern Asian states and international relations.

## Timeline
- **1488** — Bartolomeu Dias rounds the Cape of Good Hope for Portugal.
- **1498** — Vasco da Gama reaches India, establishing a sea route from Europe.
- **1511** — Portuguese capture Malacca, a key trade hub in Southeast Asia.
- **1602** — Dutch East India Company (VOC) is founded, consolidating Dutch trade power.
- **1757** — British East India Company victory at the Battle of Plassey begins dominance in India.
- **1763** — End of Seven Years' War; British eliminate French influence in India.
- **1842** — Treaty of Nanking cedes Hong Kong to Britain after the First Opium War.
- **1853** — US Commodore Perry forces Japan to open to trade.
- **1898** — US acquires the Philippines, Guam, and Puerto Rico from Spain.
- **1947** — Independence of India and Pakistan marks the start of major decolonization.
- **1954** — French defeat at Dien Bien Phu ends colonial rule in Indochina.
- **1999** — Handover of Macau from Portugal to China ends the last European colony in Asia.

## Body

### Early Exploration and Portuguese Dominance
European exploration, spurred by Ottoman closure of Silk Road routes, was pioneered by Portuguese navigators. Vasco da Gama reached India in 1498. Under leaders like Afonso de Albuquerque, Portugal established a maritime empire of fortified trading posts (*feitorias*) to control spice routes, conquering Goa (1510), Malacca (1511), and Hormuz. Spain established a colony in the Philippines (1565). Portuguese power declined in the 17th century due to overextension and competition.

### The Dutch and British Ascendancy
The Dutch East India Company (VOC), founded in 1602, displaced the Portuguese, capturing Malacca (1641) and Ceylon (1658) to monopolize the spice trade. The British East India Company, chartered in 1600, competed with the Dutch and French. After the Seven Years' War (1756–1763), Britain emerged as the dominant power in India. The decline of the Mughal Empire created a vacuum the Company filled through military force and diplomacy, culminating in direct Crown rule after the Indian Rebellion of 1857.

### The "New Imperialism" and Other Powers
The Industrial Revolution shifted European interest to raw materials and markets. The 1870s Long Depression intensified competition, leading to the "New Imperialism" of direct territorial control. Established powers (Britain, France, Netherlands) expanded their empires. New imperial powers emerged: Japan after the Meiji Restoration (1868), Germany, Russia, and the United States (after the Spanish-American War, 1898). France built an empire in Indochina from the 1850s. Russia expanded into Central Asia and the Far East, leading to the "Great Game" rivalry with Britain.

### Japan's Rise and World Wars
Japan's forced opening by the US (1853) led to rapid modernization. It defeated China (1895) and Russia (1905), acquiring Taiwan, Korea, and rights in Manchuria. Japan entered World War I as an ally, seizing German possessions. Its invasion of Manchuria (1931) and war with China (1937) aimed at creating a "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere." World War II shattered European prestige when Japan occupied most of Western colonial Asia, weakening European powers and energizing nationalist movements.

### Decolonization and the Cold War
After World War II, European powers lacked resources to maintain their empires. Nationalist movements demanded independence. Britain granted independence to India and Pakistan (1947) and Burma (1948). The Dutch East Indies became Indonesia (1949). France was defeated at Dien Bien Phu (1954), leading to independence for Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia. The US granted the Philippines independence in 1946. Decolonization was complicated by the Cold War competition. The last European colony, Portuguese Macau, was returned to China in 1999.

## Terms
*   **Feitoria:** A fortified trading post established by European powers in Asia, serving as a base for commerce and military control.
*   **Mercantilism:** An economic theory that trade generates wealth and is stimulated by the accumulation of profitable balances, which a government should encourage by means of protectionism.
*   **Sepoy:** An Indian soldier serving under British command, particularly in the British East India Company's army.
*   **Doctrine of Lapse:** A policy of annexation applied by the British East India Company whereby any princely state under the suzerainty of the Company would be annexed if the ruler was "manifestly incompetent or died without a male heir."
*   **Mission civilisatrice:** The French colonial ideology of a "civilizing mission," which claimed a duty to bring Western culture and Christianity to colonized peoples.
*   **Great Game:** The 19th-century strategic rivalry and conflict between the British Empire and the Russian Empire for influence in Central and South Asia.
*   **Meiji Restoration:** The 1868 political revolution in Japan that restored practical imperial rule and led to rapid modernization and industrialization.
*   **Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere:** The concept of a bloc of Asian nations led by the Japanese and free of Western powers, used as a justification for Japanese imperialism.

## Debates and Open questions
*   **Economic Impact:** Scholars debate whether colonialism was ultimately profitable for the colonizing powers or if the costs of administration and military control often outweighed the economic gains.
*   **Legacy of Borders:** The arbitrary borders drawn by colonial powers, particularly in the Middle East and South Asia, are widely seen as a root cause of enduring ethnic and political conflicts.
*   **Role of Nationalism:** Historians analyze the extent to which anti-colonial nationalism was a homegrown response to oppression versus an ideology imported from the West.
