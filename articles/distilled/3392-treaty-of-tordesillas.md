# Treaty of Tordesillas

## Overview
The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) was a landmark agreement between Spain and Portugal that divided newly discovered lands outside Europe along a meridian in the Atlantic Ocean. It was a direct response to the return of Christopher Columbus in 1493, aiming to prevent conflict between the two Catholic maritime powers by granting Spain rights to lands west of the line and Portugal rights to lands east. The treaty fundamentally shaped the colonial map of the Americas and Africa, though its vague terms led to ongoing disputes and required a later treaty to divide the Eastern Hemisphere.

## Timeline
- **7 June 1494** — Treaty of Tordesillas signed by Spain and Portugal.
- **2 July 1494** — Treaty ratified by Spain.
- **5 September 1494** — Treaty ratified by Portugal.
- **24 January 1506** — Treaty sanctioned by Pope Julius II.
- **1500** — Pedro Álvares Cabral lands in Brazil, claiming it for Portugal under the treaty.
- **22 April 1529** — Treaty of Zaragoza signed, establishing an antimeridian to divide the Eastern Hemisphere.
- **13 January 1750** — Treaty of Madrid supersedes the Treaty of Tordesillas, redefining colonial borders.

## Body

**Origins and Negotiation**
The treaty was negotiated after Columbus's 1493 return sparked a dispute with Portugal, which claimed the new lands under the 1479 Treaty of Alcáçovas and a 1481 papal bull. Pope Alexander VI initially issued bulls (Inter caetera, 1493) granting Spain lands west of a line 100 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands. Portugal, seeking to protect its African route and future claims to India, rejected this. Direct negotiations between the Spanish monarchs and King John II of Portugal resulted in the line being moved 270 leagues west, a compromise that gave Portugal the eastern part of what would become Brazil.

**The Line of Demarcation and Its Ambiguity**
The treaty specified a line 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands but did not define the league's length or which island to measure from. This vagueness led to decades of conflicting expert opinions. For example, in 1495, Jaime Ferrer placed the line at 42°25′ W, while the 1501 Cantino planisphere showed it at 42°30′ W. The 1529 Treaty of Zaragoza implicitly used a league equal to 4/70th of a degree, but maps like the 1529 Diogo Ribeiro map placed the line much further west. The lack of a joint survey meant the exact boundary remained debated.

**Enforcement and Colonial Impact**
Spain and Portugal largely respected the treaty in the Atlantic, with Spain gaining most of the Americas and Portugal securing Brazil (claimed in 1500) and its African and Asian routes. However, enforcement was inconsistent: Spain did not resist Portuguese expansion of Brazil across the meridian, while disputes arose in Asia. The treaty did not apply to Indigenous peoples, who did not acknowledge it. Protestant powers like England and the Netherlands rejected the division entirely.

**The Eastern Hemisphere: Treaty of Zaragoza**
The original treaty did not divide the globe. After Portugal discovered the valuable Moluccas (Spice Islands) in 1512, Spain argued the line extended worldwide. The failed Junta of Badajoz (1524) led to the Treaty of Zaragoza (1529), where Spain relinquished claims to the Moluccas for payment. It set an antimeridian 17° east of the Moluccas, giving Portugal control of Asia and Spain the Pacific. This created unequal hemispheres: Portugal's was roughly 191°, Spain's 169°.

**Later Supersession**
The Treaty of Tordesillas was effectively voided by the Treaty of Madrid (1750), which established new borders between Brazil and Spanish America. Spain acknowledged sovereignty over the Philippines, while Portugal gained the Amazon basin. This was later adjusted by the Treaty of El Pardo (1761) and the First Treaty of San Ildefonso (1777).

## Terms
- **Line of Demarcation:** The meridian 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands established by the treaty to divide Spanish and Portuguese spheres.
- **Antimeridian:** The line opposite the Tordesillas meridian on the globe, defined by the Treaty of Zaragoza to divide the Eastern Hemisphere.
- **Papal Bull:** A formal proclamation issued by a pope; the treaty superseded earlier bulls like *Inter caetera*.
- **League:** A unit of distance whose exact length was undefined in the treaty, leading to boundary disputes.
- **Moluccas:** The Spice Islands in modern Indonesia, a major point of contention between Spain and Portugal.
- **Cape Verde Islands:** The Portuguese archipelago from which the Tordesillas line was measured.
- **Treaty of Zaragoza (1529):** Agreement that set the antimeridian to divide the Eastern Hemisphere, resolving disputes over the Moluccas.
- **Treaty of Madrid (1750):** Treaty that superseded Tordesillas, redefining colonial borders in South America.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Exact Location of the Line:** The treaty's vagueness led to multiple proposed longitudes (e.g., 42°25′ W to 49°45′ W), with no definitive survey ever conducted.
- **Portuguese Knowledge of Brazil:** Some historians argue Portugal knew of Brazil's eastern bulge before Cabral's 1500 landing, suggesting his arrival was not accidental.
- **Enforcement in Asia:** Spain claimed the Tordesillas line divided the entire world, but Portugal successfully limited it to the Atlantic via papal bulls, leading to the Zaragoza agreement.
- **Legacy in Antarctica:** Chile invoked the treaty in the 20th century to defend Antarctic territorial claims, arguing it granted sovereignty to the pole.
