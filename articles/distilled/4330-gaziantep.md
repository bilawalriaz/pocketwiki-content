# Gaziantep

## Overview
Gaziantep, historically known as Aintab, is a major city in southeastern Turkey with a history spanning millennia. Its strategic location near the Syrian border has made it a crossroads of cultures and a contested territory throughout history, shaping its diverse population and rich cultural heritage. Today, it is Turkey's fifth-most populous city and a significant economic and industrial center, renowned for its cuisine and resilience, notably after the devastating 2023 earthquake.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Archaeological evidence of pistachio cultivation found near the city.
- **Hellenistic period** — Probable site of the city of Antiochia ad Taurum.
- **634–638 AD** — Region passes to the Umayyads after the Muslim conquest of the Levant.
- **1098** — Captured by Crusaders and united to the County of Edessa.
- **1400** — City besieged by Timur.
- **1516** — Captured by the Ottoman Empire before the Battle of Marj Dabiq.
- **1874** — Central Turkey College founded by American missionaries.
- **1895 & 1915** — Armenian community decimated by Hamidian massacres and the Armenian genocide.
- **1920–1921** — City besieged by Turkish forces during the Franco-Turkish War.
- **1921** — Renamed "Gazi Ayıntap" by Turkish parliament for its resistance.
- **1928** — Official name changed to Gaziantep.
- **2015** — Designated a UNESCO City of Gastronomy.
- **6 February 2023** — Severely damaged by the Turkey–Syria earthquake.

## Body

### History and Strategic Importance
Gaziantep's history is marked by its contested location. After the Muslim conquests, it was ruled by successive empires including the Umayyads, Abbasids, and Byzantines. From the 13th century, it became a frontier fortress hotly contested between the Mamluk Sultanate, the Dulkadirids (a Turkoman vassal state), and the Ilkhanate. The city was besieged multiple times, including by Timur in 1400. It fell to the Ottoman Empire in 1516, ending centuries of border warfare. Under the Ottomans, it became a commercial center on trade routes. After World War I, it was occupied by France and defended by Turkish irregular forces in a 10-month siege (1920–1921), after which it was renamed "Gazi" (warrior) Ayıntap in honor of its resistance.

### Demographics and Cultural Identity
Gaziantep has historically been a multi-ethnic city. By the late 19th century, it had a large Armenian community that played a significant role in its economy and culture, alongside Muslim Turks and a smaller Jewish minority. The Armenian community was destroyed during the Hamidian massacres (1895) and the Armenian genocide (1915). In the 20th century, the city saw an influx of Kurdish migrants. As of the 2020s, the population is predominantly Turkish, with a significant Kurdish minority (around 450,000) and a large population of Syrian refugees (around 470,000). The local Turkish dialect shows Armenian and Arabic influences.

### Economy and Cuisine
Gaziantep is an economic powerhouse for southeastern Turkey, with a large organized industrial zone and significant exports. It is a leading producer of machine-made carpets and pistachios, the latter being so central that the Turkish word for pistachio is "Antep fıstığı" (Antep nut). The city is internationally renowned for its cuisine, designated a UNESCO City of Gastronomy in 2015. Its culinary tradition, featuring dishes like various *köfte* (meatballs) and pilafs, is rich and historically attested. Gaziantep baklava holds a European protected designation of origin.

### Urban Development and Modern Challenges
The city's modern layout was significantly shaped by the 1938 Jansen Plan, which introduced railways, new boulevards, and a green belt along Alleben Creek. Subsequent plans in 1955, 1973, and 1990 guided its expansion into a polycentric metropolitan area. Gaziantep faced a major disaster on 6 February 2023, when the Turkey–Syria earthquake caused the collapse of around 900 buildings and heavy damage to historic sites like Gaziantep Castle. Due to its relative intactness and size, it became a regional hub for earthquake relief efforts.

## Terms
*   **Dulkadirids:** A Turkoman dynasty that ruled a principality in southeastern Anatolia as vassals of the Mamluk Sultanate and later the Ottoman Empire.
*   **Ilkhanate:** A Mongol khanate established in the 13th century that controlled parts of the Middle East, including brief periods in Gaziantep.
*   **Mamluk Sultanate:** A medieval Islamic empire of former slave soldiers that ruled Egypt and the Levant, competing with the Mongols and Turkomans for control of regions like Gaziantep.
*   **Sanjak:** An administrative division of the Ottoman Empire, smaller than a province (vilayet).
*   **Beglerbeglik:** A high-level province in the Ottoman Empire, governed by a *beylerbey* (lord of lords).
*   **Külliye:** An Ottoman social complex centered around a mosque, typically including a madrasa, hospital, and other public buildings.
*   **Kastel:** A subterranean fountain structure peculiar to Gaziantep, used for ablution, prayer, and relaxation.
*   **Bedesten:** A covered market or bazaar hall, typically for textiles or valuables, common in Ottoman cities.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Origin of the Name:** The etymology of "Antep" is debated. Theories include derivation from the Arabic/Aramaic *aīn* (spring) and *tab* (praise), or from Aramaic *Ayin dab* (spring of the wolf).
*   **Economic Records:** The apparent economic growth in Gaziantep in the 1530s, as indicated by Ottoman tax records, may partly reflect more accurate tax assessment or deliberate Ottoman policy of lowering taxes in newly conquered areas before raising them later, rather than purely organic growth.
*   **Legacy of the 1921 Resistance:** Academic Ümit Kurt argues that the Turkish resistance against the French occupation was also driven by local elites seeking to retain wealth confiscated from Armenians during the genocide, and to prevent Armenian repatriation, thereby shaping the city's post-war demographic and political landscape.
