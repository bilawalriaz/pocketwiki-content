# History of computing hardware

## Overview
The history of computing hardware traces the evolution from simple mechanical aids for calculation to the complex, ubiquitous digital systems of today. This progression, driven by the need for greater speed, precision, and automation, fundamentally transformed science, industry, and daily life, culminating in the personal computer and mobile device revolutions.

## Timeline
- **c. 2700–2300 BC** — The abacus is used in Babylonia.
- **c. 150–100 BC** — The Antikythera mechanism, an analog astronomical computer, is constructed in the Hellenistic world.
- **1642** — Blaise Pascal invents the Pascaline, a mechanical calculator.
- **1804** — Joseph Marie Jacquard develops a loom controlled by punched cards, a landmark in programmability.
- **1833** — Charles Babbage designs the Analytical Engine, the first general-purpose computing concept.
- **1936** — Alan Turing publishes "On Computable Numbers," describing the theoretical Turing machine.
- **1941** — Konrad Zuse's Z3 becomes the world's first working electromechanical programmable, fully automatic digital computer.
- **1944** — The Colossus, the world's first electronic digital programmable computer, becomes operational at Bletchley Park.
- **1945** — The ENIAC, the first electronic programmable computer built in the US, becomes operational.
- **1947** — The bipolar transistor is invented, enabling smaller, more reliable computers.
- **1948** — The Manchester Baby runs its first program, becoming the first electronic stored-program computer.
- **1958** — Jack Kilby demonstrates the first working integrated circuit.
- **1971** — Intel releases the 4004, the first single-chip microprocessor.
- **1975** — The Altair 8800 kit is announced, sparking the personal computer revolution.

## Body

### Early Mechanical and Analog Devices
For millennia, computation was aided by manual tools like the abacus and tally sticks. The Renaissance saw the invention of the slide rule (1620s) and mechanical calculators like Pascal's Pascaline (1642) and Leibniz's stepped reckoner (c. 1672). The Industrial Revolution enabled more complex machines; Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine (1833) was the first design for a general-purpose, programmable computer, though it was never built. Punched cards, adapted from Jacquard's loom (1804), became a key data input method, later used by Herman Hollerith for the 1890 US Census. In the first half of the 20th century, analog computers, which used continuous physical quantities (like voltage) to model problems, were prominent for tasks like tide prediction and fire control.

### The Advent of the Digital Computer
The theoretical foundation for modern computing was laid by Alan Turing's 1936 paper on the universal Turing machine. During World War II, the need for rapid calculation spurred the development of the first electronic digital computers. Konrad Zuse's Z3 (1941) was the first programmable, fully automatic digital computer. The British Colossus (1944) was the first electronic digital programmable computer, used for codebreaking. The American ENIAC (1945) was the first general-purpose electronic programmable computer. These machines were massive, used vacuum tubes, and were programmed by physically rewiring them.

### The Stored-Program Computer and Commercialization
A crucial advance was the stored-program concept, where instructions are held in the same memory as data, as proposed in the "von Neumann architecture" (1945). The Manchester Baby (1948) and EDSAC (1949) were the first working stored-program computers. This led to the first commercial computers like the Ferranti Mark 1 (1951) and UNIVAC I (1951). The invention of the transistor (1947) led to smaller, more reliable "second-generation" computers in the 1950s, such as the IBM 1401.

### Integrated Circuits and Microprocessors
The integrated circuit (IC), invented by Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce around 1958-1959, allowed many transistors on a single chip, creating "third-generation" computers. The MOSFET (1959) enabled semiconductor memory, replacing magnetic cores. The scaling of MOS technology led to large-scale integration (LSI) and the microprocessor—a complete CPU on a single chip. The Intel 4004 (1971) was the first commercial single-chip microprocessor. This innovation made possible the microcomputer, or personal computer (PC), which became ubiquitous in the 1980s and evolved into today's mobile devices.

## Terms
- **Analog Computer:** A computer that represents numbers using continuous physical quantities (e.g., voltage, rotation).
- **Digital Computer:** A computer that represents numbers using discrete digits, typically binary (0s and 1s).
- **Turing Machine:** A theoretical model of computation that defines an abstract machine which manipulates symbols on a strip of tape according to a table of rules.
- **Stored-Program Computer:** A computer architecture where program instructions and data are stored in the same memory.
- **Vacuum Tube:** An electronic component used in early computers to control electrical current, later replaced by transistors.
- **Transistor:** A semiconductor device used to amplify or switch electronic signals, forming the basis of modern electronics.
- **Integrated Circuit (IC):** An electronic circuit where components are fabricated on a single piece of semiconductor material.
- **Microprocessor:** An integrated circuit that contains the functions of a computer's central processing unit (CPU).
- **MOSFET:** Metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor, the fundamental building block of modern digital electronics and memory.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Inventor of the Mechanical Calculator:** A debate exists over whether Wilhelm Schickard (1623) or Blaise Pascal (1642) should be regarded as the inventor.
- **First Microprocessor:** The exact definition of "microprocessor" leads to contention over which device was the first, with candidates including the Four-Phase Systems AL-1 (1969), the Garrett AiResearch MP944 (1970), and the Intel 4004 (1971).
- **First Microcomputer:** Which specific product is considered the first microcomputer system is a matter of debate, with early examples including the R2E Micral N (1973) and the Altair 8800 (1975).
