# Konrad Lorenz

## Overview
Konrad Lorenz (1903–1989) was an Austrian zoologist and a founder of ethology, the study of animal behavior. He is best known for his work on imprinting in birds and for sharing the 1973 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. His career was marked by significant scientific contributions and a controversial involvement with the Nazi Party.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Lorenz was born into a wealthy family in Vienna. He studied medicine at Columbia University and the University of Vienna, earning an MD in 1928 and a PhD in zoology in 1933. As a student, he kept a wide variety of animals, developing a menagerie that would inform his later work.

### Scientific Work and Ethology
Lorenz is recognized as a founder of ethology. His most famous research concerned imprinting, an instinctive process where some nidifugous birds (birds that leave the nest early) bond with the first moving object they see after hatching. He demonstrated that this behavior allows geese to recognize their own species. Working with Tinbergen, he developed theories on instinctive behavior, including the concept of fixed action patterns triggered by environmental stimuli. He argued that behavior patterns should be studied as innate biological traits, like anatomical organs, and emphasized observing animals in their natural context.

### World War II and Nazi Involvement
Lorenz's work was interrupted by World War II. He joined the Nazi Party in 1938, stating his scientific work was devoted to National Socialist ideas. Drafted in 1941, he served as a military psychologist conducting racial studies in occupied Poland. He was captured by the Soviet Red Army in 1944 and spent four years as a prisoner of war in Soviet Armenia. After the war, he initially denied his party membership and knowledge of Nazi atrocities, though he later expressed regret for writings that used Nazi terminology to support eugenics ideas.

### Later Career and Legacy
After the war, Lorenz resumed research, leading institutes in Germany and Austria. He popularized ethology through books like *King Solomon's Ring* and *On Aggression*. In his later work, he warned that human technology and competition, driven by positive feedback, threatened ecological stability, unlike natural systems governed by negative feedback. He also argued that human senses provide a correct perception of the world, as evolution would have eliminated misleading senses. He died in 1989.

## Terms
- **Ethology**: The scientific study of animal behavior, especially in natural conditions.
- **Imprinting**: An innate process where certain animals form an attachment to the first moving object they see, typically within a critical period after birth.
- **Nidifugous**: Describing birds that leave the nest shortly after hatching.
- **Fixed Action Pattern**: An instinctive behavioral sequence that is relatively invariant and triggered by a specific stimulus.
- **Umwelt**: A concept from Jakob von Uexküll referring to the self-centered world or perceptual environment of an organism.
- **Supernormal Stimulus**: An artificial stimulus that elicits a stronger response than the natural stimulus for which the behavior evolved.
- **Positive Feedback**: A process where a system's output amplifies the original stimulus, potentially leading to runaway effects.
- **Negative Feedback**: A regulatory process where a system's output counteracts the original stimulus, promoting stability.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent of Lorenz's participation in Nazi racial science projects remains unclear.
- His early theoretical models, which leaned toward group selection, were later challenged by orthodox Darwinian theory emphasizing individual selection.
- The ethical implications of his Nazi-era writings and their relationship to his scientific work are a subject of ongoing historical scrutiny.
