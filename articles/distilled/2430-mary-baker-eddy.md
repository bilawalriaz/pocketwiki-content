# Mary Baker Eddy

## Overview
Mary Baker Eddy (1821–1910) was an American religious leader who founded the Church of Christ, Scientist, and authored its core text, *Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures*. Her teachings, which posited that sickness is an illusion of the mind curable through spiritual understanding, established Christian Science as a significant new religious movement. Her life and work remain subjects of both reverence and scholarly debate regarding her influences, mental health, and the origins of her theology.

## Body

### Early Life and Influences
Born into a strict Calvinist family, Eddy experienced periods of sudden illness in her youth. After early marriages and widowhood, she sought treatment from Phineas Quimby, a mesmerist who healed without medicine. Eddy attributed her own temporary improvements to his methods but later interpreted them through a religious lens, believing they demonstrated a divine healing power akin to that of Jesus. A pivotal moment occurred in 1866 after a severe fall on ice; she claimed that reading the Bible restored her to health, marking her spiritual discovery of "Christian Science." In the years following, she was associated with Spiritualism, participating in séances, though she later denounced the practice.

### Building a Church and Teachings
After divorcing her second husband, Eddy published *Science and Health* in 1875, which became the textbook of Christian Science. She taught her healing method to hundreds of students, many of whom became practitioners. In 1879, she and her students founded The Church of Christ, Scientist "to reinstate primitive Christianity and its lost element of healing." She established a college, authorized practitioners, and founded several periodicals, including the *Christian Science Journal* and *Christian Science Sentinel*. In 1908, at age 87, she founded *The Christian Science Monitor*. A core tenet of her teaching was the rejection of "malicious animal magnetism" (M.A.M.), the belief that destructive mental forces, akin to mesmerism, could cause harm—a concept that became controversial and led to accusations of paranoia.

### Later Life and Legacy
Eddy's later years were marked by controversy, including the 1907 "Next Friends" lawsuit, which challenged her mental competency; psychiatrists who examined her concluded she was capable of managing her affairs. Debates persist about her possible use of morphine and the extent of her engagement with ideas like British Israelism. She died in 1910. Eddy was inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame in 1995 as the first American woman to found a worldwide religion, and her book *Science and Health* has been recognized for its cultural impact.

## Terms
- **Christian Science**: A religious movement founded by Mary Baker Eddy that teaches that sickness is an illusion of the mind curable through spiritual understanding and prayer.
- **Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures**: The foundational textbook of Christian Science, written by Mary Baker Eddy, outlining its theology and healing practice.
- **Mesmerism**: A 19th-century practice involving the induction of a trance-like state to treat illness, associated with Phineas Quimby and a precursor to modern hypnosis.
- **Malicious Animal Magnetism (M.A.M.)**: A term used in Christian Science for the belief that destructive mental forces, derived from mesmerism, can cause harm; considered an illusion without real power.
- **The Mother Church**: The First Church of Christ, Scientist, in Boston, the central church of the Christian Science movement.
- **Christian Science Practitioner**: An individual authorized by the church to heal through spiritual prayer based on Christian Science principles.
- **Spiritualism**: A 19th-century religious movement based on the belief that the living can communicate with the spirits of the dead, often through mediums or séances.
- **Pastor Emeritus**: The title given to Mary Baker Eddy by the church, with the Bible and *Science and Health* serving as the dual pastor.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Influence of Phineas Quimby**: Scholars debate the extent to which Eddy's teachings were derived from the mesmerist Phineas Quimby, with some arguing for direct plagiarism and others emphasizing her distinct theological development.
- **Mental Health and Paranoia**: Eddy's belief in "malicious animal magnetism" has been cited by critics as evidence of paranoia or psychosis, while defenders view it as a theological concept and a form of vigilant prayer.
- **Use of Medicine**: There is controversy over whether Eddy secretly used morphine later in life, with biographers offering conflicting accounts of addiction versus normal medical practice.
- **Engagement with Eastern Religions and British Israelism**: Scholars debate whether Eddy was influenced by Hinduism or Buddhism, and the degree to which she was intrigued by British Israelism, a theory tracing Anglo-Saxon ancestry to the ancient Israelites.
