# Irenaeus

## Overview
Irenaeus (c. 130–c. 202 AD) was a Greek bishop of Lugdunum (modern Lyon, France) and a pivotal second-century theologian who shaped Christian orthodoxy by systematically refuting Gnosticism. A native of Smyrna who heard the preaching of Polycarp—a disciple of John the Evangelist—Irenaeus grounded his authority in an unbroken chain of apostolic succession. His seminal work, *Against Heresies* (c. 180 AD), established three pillars of orthodoxy: Scripture, apostolic tradition, and the teaching of the apostles' successors (the episcopate). He is the earliest surviving witness to explicitly affirm the fourfold Gospel canon (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John) and developed a "recapitulation" theology of atonement in which Christ, as the New Adam, sums up and sanctifies every stage of human life to reverse the Fall. Declared a Doctor of the Church by Pope Francis in 2022, Irenaeus remains a foundational figure for Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant traditions.

## Timeline
- **c. 130–140 AD** — Born in Smyrna (Anatolia); raised in a Christian family; hears Polycarp preach.
- **c. 155 AD** — Polycarp martyred; Irenaeus likely relocates to Gaul (Lyon) afterward.
- **177 AD** — Sent as a priest from Lyon to Rome with a letter to Pope Eleutherius regarding Montanism; avoids the Lyon persecution that kills Bishop Pothinus.
- **178 AD** — Returns to Gaul; succeeds Pothinus as second Bishop of Lugdunum (Lyon).
- **c. 180 AD** — Writes *Against Heresies* (Adversus haereses), refuting Valentinian Gnosticism and defining the fourfold Gospel canon.
- **190–191 AD** — Intervenes with Pope Victor I to prevent excommunication of Anatolian churches observing Quartodeciman Easter.
- **c. 202 AD** — Death (date uncertain); buried in Church of Saint John in Lyon (later renamed St. Irenaeus).
- **2022** — Declared 37th Doctor of the Church by Pope Francis; given title *Doctor unitatis* (Doctor of Unity).

## Body

### Biography and Ministry
Irenaeus was born in Smyrna (modern İzmir, Turkey) before Polycarp’s martyrdom c. 155 AD. Unusually for his era, he was raised in a Christian family rather than converting as an adult. He migrated to Lugdunum (Lyon), a major Gallic trade hub, where he served as a priest. In 177 AD, during a local persecution under Marcus Aurelius, the imprisoned clergy of Lyon dispatched him to Rome with a letter concerning the Montanist controversy. This mission spared him the persecution that claimed Bishop Pothinus and many others. Upon his return, Irenaeus was elected bishop. His episcopate focused on pastoral care, missionary activity in the Rhône valley (where he encountered followers of the Gnostic "Marcus the Magician"), and theological writing. The last recorded act of his life (reported by Eusebius c. 325 AD) was his 190/191 AD appeal to Pope Victor I for tolerance toward the Quartodeciman practice of celebrating Easter on 14 Nisan. He died around the turn of the third century; though regarded as a martyr by some traditions, no contemporary account of his death exists. His tomb in Lyon was destroyed by Huguenots in 1562; a heel bone fragment in Lyon Cathedral dates to the correct period, though two purported crania are medieval.

### Writings and Textual Legacy
Irenaeus’s principal surviving work is *Against Heresies* (five books, c. 180 AD). Book I catalogues Valentinian Gnostic mythology (tracing it to Simon Magus); Book II refutes its logic; Book III counters it with Gospel evidence and apostolic tradition; Book IV harmonizes the Old Testament and Gospel; Book V addresses resurrection and Paul’s letters. A second surviving work, *The Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching* (discovered in Armenian in 1904), serves as a catechetical summary for converts. Eusebius lists lost works including *On the Ogdoad*, *On the Monarchy* (on God not being the cause of evil), and a letter to Blastus on schism. No complete Greek manuscript of *Against Heresies* survives; the text is preserved in a full ancient Latin translation (likely 3rd century), 33 Syrian fragments, and a complete Armenian version of Books IV–V. His writings heavily influenced Hippolytus and Tertullian, though his chiliasm (belief in an earthly millennium) may have limited his reception in the Greek East.

### Scripture and the Fourfold Gospel
Irenaeus is the earliest witness to treat all four canonical Gospels as essential, authoritative Scripture. Before him, regional preferences varied (Anatolia favored John; Matthew was widely popular). He argued for exactly four Gospels—ordered John, Luke, Matthew, Mark in *Adversus haereses* III 11.8—possibly reacting to Marcion’s truncated Luke and the popularity of Tatian’s *Diatessaron* (a harmony c. 160–175), which itself implies the fourfold collection was not yet universally fixed. Irenaeus acknowledged that heterodox groups used only one Gospel or more than four. He identified John the Apostle as author of the Fourth Gospel and Luke the physician (Paul’s companion) as author of Luke-Acts. He accepted most of the New Testament (citing ~21 of 27 books, ~1,000 citations total, heavily favoring Paul’s 13 letters), while excluding Gnostic texts and possibly Philemon. He read Scripture through the "Rule of Faith" (a proto-creed resembling the Apostles’ Creed) and the public preaching of bishops, insisting his interpretive lineage traced back to the Apostles via Polycarp and John, whereas Gnostics claimed a secret oral tradition.

### Apostolic Authority and Succession
Against Gnostic claims of secret tradition, Irenaeus argued that the true faith is preserved in the public, unbroken succession of bishops in every apostolic church. He famously cited the Roman church—founded by Peter and Paul—as a preeminent example of this succession, listing its bishops up to his contemporary Eleutherius. This passage later became a *locus classicus* for Roman primacy claims. Irenaeus’s logic was not merely hierarchical but evidential: the unanimous agreement of geographically independent apostolic churches (Rome, Smyrna, Ephesus, etc.) on the same doctrine proved its authenticity, since collusion was impossible. The episcopate thus functioned as a "chain of custody" for orthodoxy.

### Theology: Unity of God and Salvation History
Irenaeus’s theology centers on the **unity and goodness of the one God**, contra the Gnostic system of divine emanations (Aeons) and a sharp distinction between the supreme Monad and the inferior creator Demiurge. Employing the **Logos theology** common in the second century, he described the Son and Spirit as the "two hands of God" by which the Father creates and redeems.

**Recapitulation (Anakephalaiosis):** The core of his soteriology. Humanity was created immature, intended to grow gradually into the divine likeness. The Fall arrested this maturation. God’s plan is a single, continuous history of salvation culminating in Christ, the **New Adam**, who "recapitulates" (sums up) every stage of human life—infancy to old age—sanctifying it by living it in perfect obedience. Where Adam disobeyed regarding a tree (Eden), Christ obeyed on a tree (Cross). Irenaeus pioneered the Eve-Mary parallel: Eve’s faithlessness undone by Mary’s faith. Salvation is effected primarily by the **Incarnation** itself: the immortal, incorruptible Word uniting to human nature communicates those qualities "like a benign infection," reversing death and corruption. The Crucifixion is integral but subordinate to the Incarnation as the climax of obedience.

**Christ’s Age and Ministry:** Against Gnostics who claimed Jesus preached only one year and died in his early 30s, Irenaeus argued from John’s Gospel (three distinct Passover visits, John 8:57’s implication Jesus was near 50) and "Elders" in Asia who knew John that Jesus lived into his 40s or 50s, thereby sanctifying old age.

**Use of Paul:** Irenaeus leans heavily on Romans 5 (Christ-Adam typology), Colossians (debt paid on a tree), and Ephesians 1 (redemption through blood). He reads Paul as teaching that the Mosaic Law revealed sin but could not cure it because humans are physical; salvation requires a physical Savior. Christ’s virgin birth parallels Adam’s creation from "virgin soil," effecting a physical restoration of humanity.

### Contrast with Valentinian Gnosticism
Valentinianism posited a tragic cosmic fall (Sophia’s error) producing the material world, redemption via secret knowledge (*gnosis*) for an elite "spiritual" class, and a tripartite humanity: *material* (unsavable), *psychic* (church members, saved by faith/works to a middle state), and *spiritual* (redeemed to the *pleroma* by shedding body and soul). Irenaeus countered that the Creator *is* the Father of Christ; the material world is good, not a mistake; salvation is for all humanity, not an elite; and the Incarnation *redeems* the body rather than escaping it. He referenced a Gnostic gospel portraying Judas positively (likely the *Gospel of Judas*, discovered 1970s, dated late 2nd century), illustrating the diversity of Gnostic sects. Modern scholarship (post-Nag Hammadi, 1945) generally judges Irenaeus’s account of Gnostic mythology as broadly accurate, though Elaine Pagels critiques his portrayal of Gnostics as uniformly libertine; Nag Hammadi reveals some sects were rigorously ascetic.

### Quartodeciman Controversy
In 190/191 AD, Pope Victor I sought to impose Roman Easter practice (Sunday observance) and excommunicate the Anatolian churches led by Polycrates, who maintained the "Quartodeciman" custom (celebrating on 14 Nisan, the Jewish Passover date). Irenaeus, though not a Quartodeciman himself, mediated vigorously, urging Victor to prioritize unity over uniformity, citing the precedent of earlier bishops (Anicetus, Polycarp) who disagreed peaceably. His intervention preserved communion.

## Terms
- ****Recapitulation (Anakephalaiosis)**** — Irenaeus’s theory of atonement: Christ "sums up" all humanity by living through every age and stage, reversing Adam’s disobedience through perfect obedience, thereby restoring the divine likeness.
- ****Rule of Faith**** — A summary of apostolic teaching (proto-creed) used as a hermeneutical key to interpret Scripture correctly; preserved in the public preaching of bishops.
- ****Apostolic Succession**** — The unbroken line of bishops traceable to the Apostles, serving as the guarantee of authentic tradition against Gnostic claims of secret knowledge.
- ****Demiurge**** — In Gnostic systems, a lower divine being who creates the material world; distinct from the supreme God (Monad). Irenaeus identifies the Demiurge with the one good God of the Old Testament.
- ****Pleroma**** — The "fullness" of the divine realm in Gnosticism, inhabited by Aeons; the destination of the "spiritual" class upon redemption.
- ****Quartodeciman**** — Christians who celebrated Easter on 14 Nisan (the Jewish Passover date) rather than the following Sunday; tolerated by Irenaeus against Victor I’s excommunication threat.
- ****Logos Theology**** — Second-century framework identifying the pre-existent Son (Word/Logos) as the agent of creation and revelation; Irenaeus calls the Son and Spirit the "two hands of God."
- ****Against Heresies (Adversus haereses)**** — Irenaeus’s five-book treatise (c. 180 AD) refuting Gnosticism (especially Valentinianism) and establishing the fourfold Gospel, apostolic succession, and recapitulation theology.
- ****Montanism**** — A prophetic movement (late 2nd century) emphasizing new revelation and strict asceticism; Irenaeus carried a letter from Lyon to Rome concerning it in 177 AD.
- ****Diatessaron**** — Tatian’s harmony of the four Gospels (c. 160–175); its popularity suggests the fourfold Gospel was not yet universally fixed when Irenaeus wrote.

## Debates and open questions
- **Date and place of death:** Unknown; traditionally c. 202 AD in Lyon, but no contemporary evidence confirms martyrdom or exact year.
- **Accuracy of Gnostic portrayal:** Post-Nag Hammadi scholarship debates whether Irenaeus fairly represented Gnostic diversity or polemically flattened it; consensus leans toward substantial accuracy on mythology, but criticism remains on moral characterizations (libertinism vs. asceticism).
- **Christ’s age:** Irenaeus’s claim Jesus lived to 40–50 (based on John 8:57 and "Elders") contradicts the standard chronology (c. 30–33 AD death); historians view this as a theological argument for recapitulation rather than historical fact.
- **Chiliasm (Millenarianism):** Irenaeus affirmed a literal earthly millennium (Book V); this may have caused his works to be neglected in the Greek East, where allegorical interpretation prevailed.
- **Roman Primacy:** His citation of the Roman church’s succession is debated: does it witness to a primacy of *honor* or *jurisdiction*? Later Catholic and Protestant polemics read it differently.
- **Pauline Authorship:** Irenaeus accepted all 13 Pauline epistles as authentic; modern critical scholarship disputes several (e.g., Pastorals, Ephesians, Colossians).
- **Canon Formation:** Whether Irenaeus *created* the fourfold Gospel canon or *ratified* an emerging consensus remains debated; the *Diatessaron*’s success suggests the latter.
