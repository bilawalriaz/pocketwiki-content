# Spanish–American War

## Overview
The Spanish–American War (April 21 – August 13, 1898) was a brief conflict between Spain and the United States that resulted in U.S. acquisition of Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines, and a protectorate over Cuba. It marked the end of Spanish colonial rule in the Americas and Asia, and established the United States as a major world power, sparking domestic debate over imperialism.

## Timeline
- **April 1895** — Cuban nationalists begin revolt against Spanish rule.
- **February 15, 1898** — USS *Maine* sinks in Havana Harbor after an explosion.
- **April 21, 1898** — U.S. begins blockade of Cuba; Spain severs diplomatic relations.
- **April 25, 1898** — U.S. Congress declares war has existed since April 21.
- **May 1, 1898** — U.S. Navy destroys Spanish fleet at Manila Bay, Philippines.
- **June 10, 1898** — First U.S. forces land in Cuba at Guantánamo Bay.
- **June 20, 1898** — Guam surrenders to U.S. forces without resistance.
- **July 1, 1898** — Battles of El Caney and San Juan Hill in Cuba.
- **July 3, 1898** — Spanish fleet destroyed at Battle of Santiago de Cuba.
- **July 25, 1898** — U.S. troops land in Puerto Rico.
- **August 12, 1898** — Armistice signed in Washington, D.C.
- **December 10, 1898** — Treaty of Paris signed, ending the war.

## Body

### Background and Path to War
The 19th century saw the decline of the Spanish Empire and the rise of the United States. In 1895, a Cuban revolt against Spanish rule was brutally suppressed, notably under General Valeriano Weyler's "reconcentration" policy. U.S. public opinion, inflamed by "yellow journalism" from newspapers like Hearst's *New York Journal* and Pulitzer's *New York World*, became sympathetic to the Cuban cause. Economic interests in Cuba and strategic desires for a canal in Central America also influenced U.S. policy. The mysterious sinking of the USS *Maine* in Havana Harbor on February 15, 1898, with 266 deaths, became the catalyst for war. After failed negotiations, the U.S. declared war on April 25, 1898, following a congressional resolution that included the Teller Amendment, disclaiming any U.S. intention to annex Cuba.

### The War in the Pacific
The U.S. targeted Spain's Pacific colonies. On May 1, Commodore George Dewey's Asiatic Squadron destroyed the Spanish fleet at Manila Bay. U.S. forces, aided by Filipino revolutionaries led by Emilio Aguinaldo, besieged Manila. Aguinaldo declared Philippine independence on June 12. U.S. troops captured Manila on August 13, unaware a peace protocol had been signed the day before. This action ended Filipino-American collaboration and led to the Philippine–American War. On June 20, the island of Guam was captured without a fight.

### The War in the Caribbean
The primary U.S. objective was to destroy Spanish forces in Cuba and capture Santiago de Cuba. U.S. troops landed in June and, after fierce battles at El Caney and San Juan Hill on July 1, besieged Santiago. The Spanish fleet was destroyed trying to escape the harbor on July 3. Santiago surrendered on July 17. U.S. troops also landed in Puerto Rico on July 25, meeting limited resistance before an armistice halted operations on August 13. A medical disaster, primarily a typhoid fever epidemic, killed far more American soldiers than combat.

### Peace and Aftermath
The Treaty of Paris (December 10, 1898) ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the U.S. for $20 million. Cuba became independent in 1902 but under a U.S. protectorate, with the Platt Amendment granting the U.S. intervention rights and a perpetual lease of Guantánamo Bay. The war ended nearly four centuries of Spanish empire in the Americas and Asia. It propelled the U.S. into global power status and ignited a fierce domestic debate over imperialism. In Spain, the defeat caused a national trauma and cultural reevaluation known as the Generation of '98.

## Terms
*   **Yellow Journalism:** Sensationalist and often exaggerated news reporting, used to sway public opinion, notably by Hearst and Pulitzer in the lead-up to war.
*   **Reconcentration:** A Spanish policy in Cuba of forcibly moving rural populations into fortified towns to isolate insurgents, which caused widespread suffering and fueled U.S. interventionist sentiment.
*   **Teller Amendment:** A U.S. congressional resolution (1898) disclaiming any intention to annex Cuba, promising to leave its government to its people.
*   **Platt Amendment:** A 1903 amendment to a Cuban constitution, imposed by the U.S., granting the U.S. the right to intervene in Cuban affairs and establish naval bases.
*   **Protectorate:** A state that is controlled and protected by another sovereign state, as Cuba was by the U.S. after the war.
*   **Ilustrados:** The educated, Spanish-speaking middle class in the Philippines who advocated for reforms and later independence.
*   **Generation of '98:** A Spanish cultural and intellectual movement that emerged after the war's defeat, reevaluating Spanish society and identity.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Cause of the War:** Historians debate the primary cause, weighing humanitarian concern for Cuba against economic interests, strategic ambitions, and the role of yellow journalism in manipulating public opinion.
*   **U.S. Imperialism:** The war's outcome sparked a major domestic debate over whether the U.S. should become a colonial power, with figures like Mark Twain opposing annexation of the Philippines.
*   **USS *Maine* Sinking:** The cause of the explosion remains debated. A U.S. Navy inquiry blamed an external mine, while a Spanish investigation cited an internal accident. Later studies have reached contradictory conclusions.
*   **Fate of the Philippines:** The U.S. decision to retain the Philippines, leading to the bloody Philippine–American War, is seen by some historians as a betrayal of the initial support for Filipino independence.
