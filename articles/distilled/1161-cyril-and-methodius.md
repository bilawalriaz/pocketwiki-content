# Cyril and Methodius

## Overview

Saints Cyril (827–869) and Methodius (815–885), born Constantine and Michael respectively, were Byzantine Christian theologians and missionaries known as the "Apostles to the Slavs." They devised the Glagolitic alphabet—the first script for Old Church Slavonic—and translated liturgical texts into the Slavic language, enabling Christian evangelization among the Early Slavs. Their work laid the foundation for Slavic literacy and influenced the development of the Cyrillic script, which became central to Eastern Orthodox Christianity and Slavic cultural identity.

## Body

### Early Life and Education

Cyril and Methodius were born in Thessalonica, then part of the Byzantine Empire (modern-day Greece). Their father, Leo, served as a military officer (*droungarios*) in the theme of Thessalonica. After their father’s death, the powerful minister Theoktistos became their protector and supported their education. Cyril studied under Photius, who later became Patriarch of Constantinople, and taught philosophy at the University of Magnaura. Methodius became a deacon and later abbot of a monastery. Both brothers took monastic names—Cyril upon becoming a monk in Rome, and Methodius at the Polychron Monastery near Mysian Olympus (Uludağ, Turkey).

Their exact ethnic origins remain debated—some scholars argue they were of Slavic descent, others Greek, possibly both.

### Mission to the Khazars

Around 860, Emperor Michael III and Patriarch Photius dispatched Cyril on a diplomatic and missionary journey to the Khazars, a Turkic people who had requested a scholar capable of engaging with Jews and Muslims. While some accounts suggest Methodius accompanied him, this may be a later addition. Cyril reportedly learned the Khazar language during his stay in Chersonesos (Crimea). Upon returning to Constantinople, he resumed teaching, while Methodius rose to prominence in imperial administration and monastic leadership.

### Mission to Great Moravia

In 862, Prince Rastislav of Great Moravia appealed to Constantinople for missionaries to evangelize his Slavic subjects. Though Christianity had already reached Moravia, Rastislav sought to reduce Frankish influence and establish an independent Slavic church. The Byzantines saw this as an opportunity to extend their cultural and religious reach into Central Europe.

The brothers arrived in Moravia and immediately began translating biblical and liturgical texts into Old Church Slavonic, a standardized form of the Slavic language. To facilitate this work, they created the Glagolitic alphabet—the first writing system for Slavic languages. This innovation allowed them to conduct services in the vernacular rather than Latin or Greek, making Christianity more accessible to the local population.

However, their efforts faced strong opposition from German clerics aligned with the Roman Church, who insisted on Latin liturgy and claimed jurisdiction over Moravian territories. Tensions escalated when the brothers traveled to Rome in 867 to seek papal approval for their Slavic liturgy.

### Journey to Rome and Death of Cyril

In Rome, Cyril and Methodius were celebrated for their scholarly achievements and spiritual dedication. Pope Adrian II formally endorsed their mission and authorized the use of the Slavic liturgy. Cyril was ordained a priest, and several of his disciples—including Saints Gorazd, Clement of Ohrid, and Naum—were also ordained. Sensing his impending death, Cyril became a Basilian monk and adopted the name Cyril, dying in Rome on 14 February 869. His remains were later interred in the Basilica di San Clemente.

### Second Mission of Methodius

After Cyril’s death, Methodius returned to missionary work, focusing initially on Pannonia (the Balaton Principality) due to instability in Great Moravia. Political upheaval followed: Rastislav was deposed by his nephew Svatopluk, who allied with the Franks. Methodius was arrested and imprisoned for two and a half years by German bishops who opposed his authority and Slavic liturgy.

Released in 873 through papal intervention, Methodius was named Archbishop of Sirmium with jurisdiction over Moravia and Pannonia. Despite a papal prohibition against using the Slavonic liturgy, he continued his mission. In 878, he was summoned to Rome again on charges of heresy and liturgical disobedience but was ultimately cleared and reinstated with permission to celebrate in Slavonic.

### Final Years and Legacy

Methodius died on 6 April 882 in Great Moravia. His death sparked renewed conflict between his followers and German clergy. Pope Stephen V rejected Methodius’ chosen successor, Gorazd, and appointed Wiching—a German bishop who suppressed the Slavic liturgy and exiled Methodius’ disciples.

Many of these exiles, including Clement of Ohrid and Saint Naum, found refuge in Bulgaria under Knyaz Boris I. There, they established the Ohrid and Preslav Literary Schools, which became centers of Slavic learning and helped develop the Cyrillic script—a modified version of Glagolitic based on Greek letters. These institutions played a crucial role in spreading Orthodox Christianity and Slavic culture throughout the Balkans and Kievan Rus’.

## Terms

- **Old Church Slavonic**: A standardized literary language based on the Slavic dialect spoken in Thessalonica; used for early translations of Christian scripture and liturgy.
- **Glagolitic Alphabet**: The first writing system created for Slavic languages, invented by Cyril and Methodius around 863; precursor to the Cyrillic script.
- **Cyrillic Script**: A writing system developed by disciples of Cyril and Methodius at the Preslav Literary School; derived from Greek and Glagolitic; adopted widely across Eastern Europe.
- **Apostles to the Slavs**: Title given to Cyril and Methodius for their pioneering evangelization of Slavic peoples.
- **Equal-to-Apostles**: Honorific title in the Eastern Orthodox Church granted to saints whose missionary work is deemed foundational to the Church.
- **Great Moravia**: A 9th-century Slavic state in Central Europe where Cyril and Methodius conducted their primary missionary activity.
- **Khazars**: A semi-nomadic Turkic people who ruled parts of southern Russia and interacted with Byzantium; recipients of Cyril’s earlier mission.
- **University of Magnaura**: An institution in Constantinople where Cyril studied and taught philosophy.
- **Ohrid Literary School**: A medieval center of Slavic literacy and theology founded by Clement of Ohrid in the late 9th century.
- **Preslav Literary School**: A Bulgarian academy where the Cyrillic script was refined and Old Church Slavonic texts were copied and taught.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Ethnic Origins**: Whether Cyril and Methodius were of Slavic or Greek origin remains contested among historians.
- **Authorship of Glagolitic**: While traditionally attributed to Cyril, some scholars question whether he alone invented it or collaborated with others.
- **Liturgical Source**: Uncertainty exists about whether the brothers used Roman or Byzantine liturgical models as templates for their Slavonic translations.
- **Bishopric of Cyril**: Some sources claim Cyril was ordained bishop before his death, though evidence is inconclusive.
- **Location of Methodius’ Burial**: The capital city of Great Moravia is unknown, leaving the precise location of his tomb uncertain.
- **Filioque Controversy**: Methodius defended omitting the Filioque clause from the Creed, aligning with Byzantine practice but drawing criticism from Latin clergy.
