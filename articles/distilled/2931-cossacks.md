# Cossacks

## Overview
The Cossacks are a predominantly East Slavic, Eastern Orthodox Christian people originating from the Pontic–Caspian steppe. Historically a semi-nomadic, militarized society, they served as irregular cavalry and border guards for various Eastern European states, most notably the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth and the Russian Empire. Their legacy is defined by a tradition of self-governance, military service, and a complex relationship with central authority, which has made them symbols of both freedom and repression in modern national identities.

## Timeline
- **13th century** — First written attestation of the name "Cossack" in the Codex Cumanicus.
- **1492** — First international mention: Crimean Khan complains to Lithuania about Cossack subjects.
- **16th century** — Major Cossack hosts form near the Dnieper, Don, Volga, and Ural rivers.
- **1648–1654** — Khmelnytsky Uprising leads to the creation of the Cossack Hetmanate.
- **1654** — Treaty of Pereyaslav places the Hetmanate under Russian suzerainty.
- **1671–1786** — Don Cossack Army exists as an autonomous military state under Moscow.
- **1775** — Russian Empire destroys the Zaporozhian Sich after Pugachev's Rebellion.
- **1917–1922** — Cossacks fight in the Russian Civil War; many are exiled or subjected to "de-Cossackization."
- **1936** — Soviet Union relaxes restrictions, allowing Cossacks to serve openly in the Red Army.
- **1943–1945** — Some Cossacks collaborate with Nazi Germany; others serve the USSR.
- **1988** — Soviet law permits the re-establishment of Cossack hosts.
- **2005** — Russian federal law recognizes Cossack hosts as a paramilitary service.

## Body

### Origins and Early History
The origins of the Cossacks are disputed. The name derives from the Turkic word *kazak*, meaning "free man." Early groups were likely a mix of East Slavs, Turks, and others inhabiting the steppe north of the Black Sea. By the late 15th century, the term applied to Slavic peasants fleeing serfdom who established self-governing, cavalry-based communities in the "Wild Fields." These groups, such as the Zaporozhian and Don Cossacks, organized along military lines into autonomous "hosts" (voisko), responsible for protecting territories of affiliated villages (stanitsas).

### Relations with Poland-Lithuania and Russia
Cossacks were valued as irregular troops but often clashed with central authorities. The Zaporozhian Cossacks, based in modern Ukraine, gained autonomy from Poland in 1649 but launched the Khmelnytsky Uprising (1648) against Polish rule. The resulting Treaty of Pereyaslav (1654) brought them under Russian protection, though tensions over autonomy persisted. The Russian Empire gradually incorporated Cossack hosts, transforming them into a privileged military estate (sosloviye) in exchange for border defense and police duties. This led to major rebellions, such as those led by Stenka Razin (1667–1671) and Yemelyan Pugachev (1773–1775), which were brutally suppressed.

### 19th and 20th Centuries
By the 19th century, Cossacks were integral to the Russian Empire's military and expansion into Siberia and Central Asia. They also served as a mounted police force. After the Russian Revolution, Cossack communities were divided; many fought for the White Army, while others joined the Reds. The Soviet policy of "de-Cossackization" led to mass repression, famine, and the dissolution of traditional structures. During World War II, Cossacks served on both sides. After the war, Cossack traditions were suppressed until the Perestroika era, when a revival began with the 1988 law allowing the re-establishment of hosts.

### Culture and Organization
Cossack society was heavily militarized and democratic, with leaders (atamans or hetmans) elected by assemblies (rada). They were renowned for their horsemanship and cavalry tactics. Distinctive uniforms, like the *cherkesska* coat, varied by host. Family life was relatively egalitarian, with women often managing communities during men's long military service. Cossack identity is now a mix of ethnic descent and cultural affiliation, with organizations operating in Russia, Ukraine, and diaspora communities.

## Terms
- **Ataman/Hetman**: Elected military and political leader of a Cossack host.
- **Host (Voisko)**: A large, autonomous Cossack military and territorial organization.
- **Stanitsa**: A Cossack village or settlement, the basic unit of a host.
- **Rada**: A Cossack assembly or council that elected leaders and made decisions.
- **Sosloviye**: A "social estate" or class in the Russian Empire; Cossacks became a military estate.
- **De-Cossackization**: Soviet policy of repression and dismantling Cossack communities after the Civil War.
- **Zaporozhian Sich**: The fortified capital and autonomous center of the Zaporozhian Cossacks.
- **Cherkesska**: A long, open-fronted coat with cartridge loops, traditional to Caucasian Cossack hosts.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origins**: The ethnic origins of the Cossacks are debated, with theories ranging from purely East Slavic to a hybrid of Slavic, Turkic, and other steppe peoples. Genetic studies show a predominantly East Slavic paternal gene pool for most hosts.
- **Identity**: There is no consensus on defining modern Cossack identity—whether it is strictly ethnic ("born" Cossacks) or can include initiated members. This affects demographic counts.
- **Historical Role**: Cossacks are interpreted both as freedom-loving pioneers and as instruments of imperial repression, particularly for their role in suppressing uprisings and pogroms.
