# Fraction

## Overview
A fraction represents a part of a whole or any number of equal parts, expressed as a numerator over a denominator (e.g., 3/4). Fractions are foundational in mathematics, representing rational numbers, ratios, and division, and are essential in arithmetic, algebra, and real-world applications like measurement and probability.

## Body

### Forms of Fractions
**Simple (common/vulgar) fractions** are written as a/b where a and b are integers and b ≠ 0. They can be **proper** (numerator < denominator) or **improper** (numerator ≥ denominator). A **unit fraction** has numerator 1 (e.g., 1/7), and a **dyadic fraction** has a denominator that is a power of two (e.g., 1/8).

**Mixed numbers** combine an integer and a proper fraction (e.g., 2 3/4). They can be converted to improper fractions: 2 3/4 = (2×4 + 3)/4 = 11/4.

**Decimal fractions** have denominators that are powers of ten, expressed via decimal notation (e.g., 0.75 = 75/100). **Percentages** use denominator 100 (e.g., 51% = 51/100).

### Historical Notions
**Egyptian fractions** express numbers as sums of distinct unit fractions (e.g., 5/7 = 1/2 + 1/6 + 1/21). Every positive rational number has infinitely many such representations.

**Complex fractions** contain fractions in numerator, denominator, or both (e.g., (1/2)/(1/3)). **Compound fractions** involve "of" operations, equivalent to multiplication (e.g., 3/4 of 5/7 = 3/4 × 5/7 = 15/28).

### Arithmetic Operations
Fractions follow commutative, associative, and distributive laws. 

- **Equivalent fractions**: Multiplying numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number preserves value (e.g., 1/2 = 2/4).
- **Simplifying**: Divide numerator and denominator by their greatest common divisor (GCD). The Euclidean algorithm finds GCDs efficiently.
- **Comparison**: Use common denominators or cross-multiplication (ad vs bc for a/b and c/d).
- **Addition/Subtraction**: Convert to common denominators: a/b + c/d = (ad + bc)/bd.
- **Multiplication**: Multiply numerators and denominators: (a/b) × (c/d) = (ac)/(bd). Cancellation simplifies before multiplying.
- **Division**: Multiply by the reciprocal: (a/b) ÷ (c/d) = (a/b) × (d/c).

### Abstract Mathematics
Fractions are formally defined as ordered pairs (a, b) of integers with b ≠ 0, subject to equivalence relation (a, b) ~ (c, d) iff ad = bc. Operations are defined component-wise. This construction yields the field of rational numbers Q.

**Algebraic fractions** extend this to polynomials (e.g., 3x/(x² + 2x − 3)). A **rational fraction** is one whose numerator and denominator are polynomials; an **irrational fraction** is one that is not rational, as, for example, one that contains the variable under a fractional exponent or root, as in √(x+2)/(x²−3).

**Radical expressions** in denominators are rationalized by multiplying by conjugates (e.g., 1/(√a + √b) × (√a − √b)/(√a − √b)).

### Decimal Conversion
Converting fractions to decimals uses long division. Terminating decimals occur when denominator divides a power of the base. Repeating decimals convert via algebraic methods or pattern recognition (e.g., 0.333... = 1/3).

## Terms
- **Numerator**: Top number indicating count of parts
- **Denominator**: Bottom number indicating parts per whole
- **Proper fraction**: |numerator| < |denominator|
- **Improper fraction**: |numerator| ≥ |denominator|
- **Reciprocal**: Fraction with numerator/denominator swapped
- **Unit fraction**: Fraction with numerator 1
- **Egyptian fraction**: Sum of distinct unit fractions
- **Complex fraction**: Fraction containing fractions
- **Rational number**: Number expressible as a/b with integers a, b (b ≠ 0)
- **Field of fractions**: Smallest field containing an integral domain

## Debates and Open Questions
- Terminology around **complex** vs **compound fractions** is inconsistent and considered outdated
- Whether **mixed numbers** should be emphasized in education beyond primary school remains debated
- The attribution of √2's irrationality discovery to Hippasus is "commonly though probably erroneously" made
- While the Persian mathematician Jamshīd al-Kāshī claimed to have discovered decimal fractions himself in the 15th century, J. Lennart Berggren notes that he was mistaken, as decimal fractions were first used five centuries before him by the Baghdadi mathematician Abu'l-Hasan al-Uqlidisi as early as the 10th century
