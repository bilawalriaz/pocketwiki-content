# Encyclopædia Britannica

## Overview
The Encyclopædia Britannica is a general-knowledge English-language encyclopaedia first published in 1768. It is renowned for its authoritative, expert-written content and was the longest-running in-print encyclopaedia in English, published for 245 years before ceasing print production in 2012 to focus exclusively on its online version.

## Timeline
- **1768–1771** — First edition published in Edinburgh, Scotland, in weekly instalments forming three volumes.
- **1875–1889** — Publication of the 9th edition, dubbed the "Scholar's Edition" for its high scholarship.
- **1901** — Britannica acquired by an American firm, beginning a shift toward a mass market.
- **1911** — Publication of the 11th edition, a landmark for scholarship and literary style.
- **1932** — Adoption of a "continuous revision" policy, updating articles on a schedule between editions.
- **1974** — Launch of the 15th edition, reorganized into the Micropædia, Macropædia, and Propædia.
- **1996** — Purchased by Swiss billionaire Jacqui Safra.
- **2005** — *Nature* journal publishes a study comparing Britannica and Wikipedia accuracy.
- **2012** — Announcement that the 2010 print edition would be the last; focus shifts to online.
- **2016** — Published exclusively as an online encyclopaedia at Britannica.com.

## Body

### History and Ownership
The Britannica was founded by Scottish printers Colin Macfarquhar and Andrew Bell. Its history is divided into five eras marked by changes in management. After early Scottish ownership, it was managed by the Edinburgh firm A. & C. Black (1827–1901). In 1901, it was acquired by American businessman Horace Hooper, introducing door-to-door sales. Ownership later included Sears Roebuck, the University of Chicago (via the Benton Foundation from 1943), and finally Swiss billionaire Jacqui Safra (from 1996).

### Editions and Structure
The encyclopaedia has been issued in 15 editions. Early editions grew rapidly in size. The 9th and 11th editions are particularly noted for their scholarship. In 1974, the 15th edition introduced a three-part structure: the **Micropædia** (short articles for quick reference), the **Macropædia** (long, in-depth articles), and the **Propædia** (a hierarchical outline of all knowledge). This structure was reorganized and indexed in 1985. The final 2010 print set comprised 32 volumes.

### Contributors and Editorial Policy
The Britannica has employed thousands of contributors, including eminent scholars and Nobel laureates like Milton Friedman and Carl Sagan. Articles are either written by named experts or by the editorial staff. The encyclopaedia has generally maintained British English spelling. Since 1936, it has followed a policy of continuous revision, with at least 10% of articles considered for update annually.

### Transition to Digital and Competition
Facing competition from digital sources like Microsoft Encarta and later the online, peer-produced Wikipedia, Britannica developed CD-ROM, DVD, and online versions. A 2005 *Nature* study found Britannica articles had fewer factual errors (2.92 per article) than Wikipedia (3.86), though Britannica disputed the methodology. In 2012, the company ceased print publication to focus on its online platform and other educational tools.

## Terms
- **Micropædia**: The section of the 15th edition containing short articles (generally <750 words) for quick fact-checking.
- **Macropædia**: The section containing long, authoritative articles (up to 310 pages) for in-depth study.
- **Propædia**: A single volume providing a hierarchical "Outline of Knowledge" to systematize all human knowledge.
- **Continuous revision**: A policy adopted in 1936 where articles are updated on a regular schedule, rather than waiting for a new edition.
- **Scottish Enlightenment**: An intellectual movement in 18th-century Scotland; the early Britannica is considered one of its legacies.
- **Scholar's Edition**: A nickname for the 9th edition (1875–1889), renowned for its high scholarly quality.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Accuracy vs. Wikipedia**: A 2005 *Nature* study suggested comparable error rates between Britannica and Wikipedia, sparking a public dispute. Britannica argued the study was flawed, while *Nature* stood by its methodology.
- **Editorial Criticism**: The initial 15th edition (1974) was criticized for its complex three-part structure and the elimination of an index, which was later rectified.
- **Outdated Content**: Despite continuous revision, critics have noted that longer Macropædia articles can become outdated, and information between the Micro- and Macropædia can be inconsistent.
- **Coverage Bias**: While praised for its depth, the Britannica has been criticized for uneven coverage, such as devoting significantly more space to Christianity than to other world religions.
