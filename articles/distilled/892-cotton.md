# Cotton

## Overview
Cotton is a soft, fluffy staple fiber composed almost entirely of cellulose that grows in a protective boll around the seeds of shrubs in the genus *Gossypium*. Native to tropical and subtropical regions globally, it was independently domesticated in both the Old and New Worlds, with archaeological evidence dating to the 7th–6th millennia BC in Peru and the 6th millennium BC in South Asia. The invention of the cotton gin in 1793 mechanized seed separation, drastically lowering production costs and enabling cotton to become the world's most widely used natural fiber. Today, India is the largest producer and the United States the largest exporter, with global production around 25 million tonnes annually. The crop's history is deeply entwined with colonialism, slavery, and the Industrial Revolution, while modern cultivation faces sustainability challenges regarding water use, pesticide reliance, and genetic modification.

## Body

### Biology and Types
Cotton is a perennial shrub grown as an annual to control pests, requiring a long frost-free season, sunshine, and 50–100 cm of rainfall (often supplemented by irrigation). Four species are commercially grown: *Gossypium hirsutum* (Upland cotton, 90% of world production, native to Central America/Mexico), *G. barbadense* (Extra-long staple, >5%, native to South America), *G. arboreum* (Tree cotton, <2%, native to India/Pakistan), and *G. herbaceum* (Levant cotton, <2%, native to Africa/Arabia). The two New World species dominate modern production due to their longer, stronger fibers. Fibers are single, elongated, dried multilayer cells developing on the seed surface, structurally defined by a cuticle, primary wall, winding layer (S1), secondary wall (S2, the main cellulose mass), and a central lumen.

### Early Domestication and Global Spread
Cotton was independently domesticated in the Americas and the Old World. In Peru, *G. barbadense* underpinned coastal cultures (Norte Chico, Moche, Nazca) from c. 4200 BC, traded as nets for fish. In Mexico, *G. hirsutum* was domesticated 3400–2300 BC; Aztec tribute records indicate massive annual volumes (~53,000 tonnes). In South Asia, mineralized threads at Mehrgarh (6th millennium BC) and textiles at Indus Valley sites (3rd millennium BC) mark early use. Cotton appeared in the Levant (Tel Tsaf, 5200 BCE) likely via Indus trade. In Africa, *G. herbaceum* may have been domesticated c. 5000 BC in eastern Sudan; the Kingdom of Kush (Meroë) developed a sophisticated cotton textile industry by the 4th century BC, exporting textiles and using cotton in elite funerary contexts. In China, cultivation began in Yunnan during the Han dynasty (207 BC–220 AD).

### Medieval Technology and European Entry
Handheld roller gins existed in India by the 6th century; dual-roller gins appeared in India and China by the 12th–14th centuries, spreading to the Mediterranean by the 16th. The spinning wheel emerged in the Islamic world (11th century) and reached India by 1350 (likely via Iran). Cotton entered Europe as an imported luxury via the Muslim conquest of Iberia and Sicily; weaving knowledge spread to northern Italy by the 12th century. Northern Europeans, unaware of the plant's nature, believed cotton grew on trees bearing lambs ("Vegetable Lamb of Tartary"), a myth reflected in terms like German *Baumwolle* ("tree wool"). By the 15th century, Venice, Antwerp, and Haarlem were major cotton trade hubs.

### Mughal India: Peak Pre-Industrial Production
Under the Mughal Empire (early 16th–early 18th century), India became the world's leading textile manufacturer, holding a 25% share of global textile trade in the early 18th century. Agrarian reforms incentivized cash crops (cotton, indigo). The worm gear roller gin (13th–14th c.) and crank handle innovations diffused widely; the spinning wheel lowered yarn costs. Bengal Subah (Dhaka) was the primary production center, producing piece goods, calicos, and muslins. Indian textiles dominated world markets from the Americas to Japan.

### The Industrial Revolution and the Rise of Britain
The English East India Company (EIC) imported cheap calico/chintz from the 1660s, overtaking the spice trade by value. Domestic wool/linen producers lobbied for protection, resulting in the 1700 and 1721 Calico Acts banning cotton cloth imports (raw cotton exempt). This exemption spurred domestic mechanization: Paul/Wyatt rollers (1738), Hargreaves' spinning jenny (1764), Arkwright's water frame (1769), Crompton's mule (1775). Repeal of the Acts in 1774 triggered massive mill investment; raw cotton demand doubled every decade into the 1840s. Manchester became "Cottonopolis." Britain outcompeted India by combining labor-saving technology with protectionist tariffs, while EIC rule deindustrialized India, converting it into a raw cotton supplier and captive market for British goods. Capital from Bengal (post-1757) financed British mills.

### King Cotton, Slavery, and the American Civil War
Eli Whitney's 1793 saw gin made US short-staple cotton profitable, reducing labor from 600+ to ~12 hours per bale. By the 1840s, US "King Cotton" (longer, stronger fibers of *G. hirsutum* and *G. barbadense*) supplanted Indian supply for British mills. Cotton became the backbone of the antebellum Southern economy, cultivated almost entirely by enslaved African Americans. The 1861–65 Civil War caused the Lancashire Cotton Famine (Union blockade + Confederate embargo). Britain and France invested heavily in Egyptian cotton; Viceroy Isma'il borrowed heavily. Post-war (1865), traders returned to cheap US cotton, bankrupting Egypt (1876) and precipitating British occupation (1882). Meanwhile, British India and Australia expanded raw cotton cultivation to fill the wartime gap, but under colonial policy restricting Indian manufacturing.

### Post-Slavery US and 20th Century Shifts
After 1865, sharecropping replaced slavery in the US South; hand-picking persisted until mechanical pickers emerged mid-20th century. Synthetic fibers (rayon 1890s, nylon 1936, polyester early 1950s) threatened cotton markets, causing crashes in cotton-exporting economies (e.g., Nicaragua) by the 1960s. Production recovered in the 1970s but fell to pre-1960 levels by the early 1990s. Today, the US remains a major exporter with large farms in Texas, California, and the Deep South.

### Modern Cultivation: Genetics, Water, and Sustainability
Genetically modified (GM) cotton, introduced 1996, expresses *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) toxin to kill lepidopteran pests (bollworms), reducing broad-spectrum insecticide use. By 2011, GM cotton covered 69% of global area (25M ha), led by India (10.6M ha, 88% of its cotton), US (4.0M ha), China (3.9M ha), Pakistan (2.6M ha), and Australia (95% GM). Studies on Bt cotton show conflicting results regarding secondary pest outbreaks and long-term pesticide reduction. Herbicide-tolerant (glyphosate-resistant) varieties also exist. Low-gossypol (edible seed) cotton was deregulated by USDA in 2018. Organic cotton (non-GM, no synthetic chemicals) grew >50%/year as of 2007 (265k bales in 24 countries). Cotton has a high water footprint: 8,000–10,000 L/kg globally, up to 22,500 L/kg in dry Indian regions. Irrigation dependence causes environmental crises, notably the Aral Sea desiccation and Uzbek desertification.

### Harvesting, Trade, and Economics
Mechanical harvesting (pickers or strippers) dominates in the US, Europe, and Australia; hand-picking persists in developing countries and Xinjiang, China (allegedly forced labor; Xinjiang produces >20% of world cotton). Top 2017 producers: India and China (~18.5M tonnes each); top exporters: US ($4.9B) and Africa ($2.1B). Africa's trade share doubled since 1980, dominated by smallholders and brokers like Dunavant Enterprises. US growers (25,000) receive ~$2B/year subsidies, sparking WTO disputes (Brazil–US cotton dispute) and "Cotton-4" (Benin, Burkina Faso, Chad, Mali) advocacy. Fair trade cotton initiated in 2005 (Cameroon, Mali, Senegal). Cotton trades as futures on ICE (CT) and NYMEX (TT).

### Uses and Byproducts
Textiles: terrycloth (towels), denim (jeans), cambric (work shirts), corduroy, seersucker, twill, socks, underwear, T-shirts, bed sheets (hypoallergenic). Blends with polyester (durability), elastane (stretch), linen (wrinkle resistance). Non-textile: fishing nets, coffee filters, tents, nitrocellulose (explosives), cotton paper, bookbinding. Cottonseed yields oil (human consumption), meal (ruminant feed; gossypol toxic to monogastrics), hulls (roughage). Linters (short fibers on seed post-ginning) used for paper, cellulose, and medical "cotton wool." Long staple (LS) and Extra-long staple (ELS) cottons (e.g., Egyptian, Pima/Supima, Indian "Kasturi") command premium prices for quality.

## Terms
- ****Staple fiber**** — A fiber of discrete length (as opposed to continuous filament); cotton staple length determines quality and yarn strength.
- ****Boll**** — The protective, rounded seed capsule of the cotton plant in which the fiber develops.
- ****Ginning**** — The mechanical process of separating cotton fibers (lint) from the seeds.
- ****Lint**** — The long, usable fibers removed from the seed during ginning; the primary raw material for textiles.
- ****Linters**** — Short, fine fibers remaining on the seed after ginning, used for paper, cellulose, and medical cotton wool.
- ****Gossypol**** — A toxic polyphenolic pigment naturally occurring in cotton glands; renders seed inedible for monogastrics but removed in oil refining.
- ****Extra-long staple (ELS)**** — Cotton with fiber length >34.9 mm (1 3/8 in), e.g., *G. barbadense* varieties (Pima, Egyptian), producing finer, stronger yarns.
- ****Bt cotton**** — Genetically modified cotton expressing *Bacillus thuringiensis* toxin to resist lepidopteran pests (bollworms).
- ****Spinning mule**** — A hybrid spinning machine (Samuel Crompton, 1775) combining the moving carriage of the spinning jenny with the rollers of the water frame, enabling high-quality, high-volume yarn production.
- ****Calico Act**** — British legislation (1700, 1721) banning import/sale of cotton cloth to protect domestic wool/linen industries; raw cotton exemption spurred domestic mechanization.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Bt cotton efficacy and resistance:** Studies conflict on whether Bt cotton sustainably reduces pesticide use or triggers secondary pest outbreaks (mirids) requiring renewed spraying; long-term resistance evolution in target pests remains a concern.
*   **Water sustainability:** Cotton's extreme water footprint (up to 22,500 L/kg in arid zones) drives aquifer depletion (Ogallala, Aral Sea) and desertification; viability under climate change is debated.
*   **Forced labor in Xinjiang:** Allegations of state-sponsored forced labor in China's Xinjiang region (producing >20% of global cotton) have triggered import bans (US UFLPA) and supply chain audits; verification remains difficult.
*   **Subsidy distortion:** Heavy US/EU/China subsidies depress global prices, harming unsubsidized African smallholders; WTO dispute settlement (Brazil case, Cotton-4 initiative) has not fully resolved trade distortions.
*   **GM vs. Organic trade-offs:** GM cotton reduces insecticide use but raises seed costs, corporate dependency, and gene-flow concerns; organic cotton avoids synthetics but yields are typically lower, requiring more land per unit of fiber.
*   **Genome complexity:** Sequencing tetraploid cotton (*G. hirsutum*, *G. barbadense*) remains challenging due to duplicated A and D subgenomes; complete high-quality assemblies are recent (post-2014) and essential for precision breeding.
