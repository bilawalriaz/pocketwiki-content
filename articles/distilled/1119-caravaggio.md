# Caravaggio

## Overview

Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio (1571–1610) was an Italian Baroque painter whose radical naturalism and dramatic use of light and shadow—chiaroscuro intensified into tenebrism—revolutionized Western art. Working directly from live models and rejecting idealized beauty, he infused religious and mythological scenes with visceral realism and psychological intensity. His turbulent personal life, marked by violence and exile, paralleled the emotional rawness of his art. Though controversial in his time, Caravaggio’s influence shaped the Baroque era and reverberates through modern painting.

## Body

### Early Life and Training (1571–1592)

Born in Milan, where his father, Fermo (Fermo Merixio), was a household administrator and architect-decorator to the marquess of Caravaggio, Caravaggio lost both his father and grandfather on the same day in 1577 during a plague outbreak. His mother raised her five children in poverty. She died in 1584, the same year he began his four-year apprenticeship to the Milanese painter Simone Peterzano, described in the contract of apprenticeship as a pupil of Titian. Exposure to Leonardo da Vinci’s *Last Supper* and Counter-Reformation imagery shaped his early aesthetic sensibilities.

### Rise in Rome (1592–1606)

In 1592, Caravaggio left Milan for Rome in flight after "certain quarrels" and the wounding of a police officer. The young artist arrived in Rome "naked and extremely needy... without fixed address and without provision... short of money." Working for Giuseppe Cesari, he painted flowers and fruit before forging friendships with Prospero Orsi and Mario Minniti. His breakthrough came with *The Fortune Teller* and *The Cardsharps*, which attracted the patronage of Cardinal Francesco Maria del Monte. Del Monte commissioned intimate chamber pieces like *The Musicians* and *Bacchus*, showcasing adolescent models with sensual immediacy.

Caravaggio’s religious works introduced unprecedented realism. *Penitent Magdalene* depicted the saint as a weeping woman, not an ethereal visionary. However, his radical naturalism sparked controversy. *Death of the Virgin* was rejected by the Carmelites for using a prostitute as the Virgin’s model and showing her bare legs. Despite rejections, his reputation soared with masterpieces like *The Calling of Saint Matthew*, *The Martyrdom of Saint Matthew*, and *The Incredulity of Saint Thomas*.

His volatile personality led to frequent brawls and legal troubles. In 1606, killing Ranuccio Tomassoni—possibly unintentionally—forced his exile.

### Exile and Death (1606–1610)

In Naples, protected by the Colonna family, Caravaggio painted *The Seven Works of Mercy* and *Madonna of the Rosary*. A brief stint in Malta yielded *Beheading of Saint John the Baptist* and *Saint Jerome Writing*, but a drunken brawl led to expulsion from the Knights of Malta.

In Sicily, he produced *Burial of St. Lucy* and *The Raising of Lazarus*, evolving toward freer brushwork and stark isolation of figures. Increasingly erratic behavior—sleeping armed, mocking peers—marked his final years.

Returning to Naples in 1609, he was involved in a violent clash, an attempt on his life, which seriously disfigured his face. Sending *Salome with the Head of John the Baptist* (featuring his own severed head) to Grand Master Wignacourt, he sought papal pardon. He died in 1610 under uncertain circumstances while on his way from Naples to Rome. Reports stated that he died of fever, possibly due to wounds. Later research published in 2018 concluded he died as the result of a wound sustained in a brawl in Naples, specifically from sepsis caused by Staphylococcus aureus.

### Artistic Innovation and Legacy

Caravaggio pioneered tenebrism, using sharp contrasts of light and dark to heighten drama. He worked rapidly from live models, often prostitutes and street youths, infusing sacred subjects with earthy humanity. His ability to capture decisive moments—Christ’s touch awakening Lazarus, Matthew’s startled self-recognition—defined Baroque narrative.

The Caravaggisti, including Orazio Gentileschi, Carlo Saraceni, and Jusepe de Ribera, spread his style across Europe. Though initially overshadowed by the Carracci in Italy, his influence flourished in Spain, Flanders, and the Netherlands. Rubens, Rembrandt, and Vermeer absorbed his chiaroscuro techniques.

Rediscovered in the 20th century by critics like Roberto Longhi, Caravaggio’s legacy endures as a bridge between Renaissance idealism and modern realism. André Berne-Joffroy declared: “What begins in the work of Caravaggio is, quite simply, modern painting.”

## Terms

- **Chiaroscuro**: Technique contrasting light and shadow to create volume and mood.
- **Tenebrism**: Dramatic intensification of chiaroscuro, with subjects emerging from deep darkness.
- **Caravaggisti**: Artists directly influenced by Caravaggio’s style and realism.
- **Baroque**: Artistic movement emphasizing emotion, movement, and dramatic lighting, emerging post-Mannerism.
- **Naturalism**: Realistic depiction of subjects without idealization.
- **Mannerism**: Elaborate, artificial style preceding Baroque, characterized by elongated forms and complex compositions.
- **Counter-Reformation**: Catholic Church’s 16th-century response to Protestant Reformation, promoting emotionally engaging religious art.
- **Corporal Works of Mercy**: Seven acts of charity in Christian tradition (feeding hungry, sheltering homeless, etc.).

## Debates and Open Questions

**Cause of Death**: Contemporary accounts cite fever; modern theories include malaria, lead poisoning, sepsis from untreated wounds, or deliberate poisoning by enemies.

**Sexuality**: Scholars debate whether Caravaggio’s relationships with male models and lovers indicate homosexuality or bisexuality. While circumstantial evidence supports same-sex intimacy, definitive proof remains elusive.

**Authenticity of Late Works**: Several recently discovered paintings, including a *Judith Beheading Holofernes* found in 2014 and an *Ecce Homo* attributed in 2021, spark scholarly disputes over attribution due to stylistic variations and lack of documentation.

**Influence vs. Rivalry**: While Caravaggio profoundly influenced Baroque art, some argue his impact was mediated through followers rather than direct emulation, complicating assessments of his immediate legacy.
