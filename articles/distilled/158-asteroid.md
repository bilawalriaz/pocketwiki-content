# Asteroid

## Overview

An asteroid is a minor planet—an object larger than a meteoroid (1 meter or larger) that is neither a planet nor an identified comet—orbiting within the inner Solar System or co-orbital with Jupiter (Trojan asteroids). Asteroids are rocky, metallic, or icy bodies with no atmosphere, broadly classified into C-type (carbonaceous), M-type (metallic), or S-type (silicaceous). They range from rubble piles under a kilometer across to Ceres, a dwarf planet almost 1000 km in diameter. A body is classified as a comet rather than an asteroid if it shows a coma (tail) when warmed by solar radiation, though recent observations suggest a continuum between these types. Of roughly one million known asteroids, most lie in the main asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter (2–4 AU from the Sun), with a total mass only 3% that of Earth's Moon. Near-Earth asteroids pose potential impact threats, exemplified by the Chicxulub impact widely thought to have caused the Cretaceous–Paleogene mass extinction.

## Body

### Terminology and Classification

In 2006, the IAU introduced "small Solar System body" as the preferred broad term for objects neither planet, dwarf planet, nor natural satellite. Historically, Ceres was first considered a planet; William Herschel coined "asteroid" from Greek *asteroeidēs* meaning "star-like." For two centuries, "asteroid" and "planet" were used interchangeably. The main difference from comets: comets show a coma due to sublimation of near-surface ices. Some minor planets later showed cometary activity, and some comets become asteroid-like when depleted of volatiles. Highly eccentric asteroids are probably dormant or extinct comets. Beyond Jupiter, objects are called centaurs (first found 1977), trans-Neptunian objects (first found 1992), Kuiper-belt objects, scattered-disc objects, and Oort cloud bodies. The Kuiper-belt bodies are called "objects" partly to avoid classifying them as asteroids or comets. In 2006, the IAU created the dwarf planet class for the largest minor planets; only Ceres (≈975 km across) from the asteroid belt qualifies.

### Discovery and Observation History

Ceres was discovered by Giuseppe Piazzi on 1 January 1801 while searching star catalogs; he observed it 24 times before illness interrupted. After its position became uncertain, Carl Friedrich Gauss, then 24, developed an efficient orbit determination method, enabling recovery on 31 December 1801. Ceres fit the Titius–Bode law almost perfectly, though Neptune's later discovery (1846) discredited the law. Three more asteroids were found by 1807, then none until 1845 when Karl Ludwig Hencke found 5 Astraea. By 1868, the 100th asteroid was discovered. Max Wolf's 1891 astrophotography technique—asteroids appearing as streaks on long-exposure plates—dramatically increased detection; he alone found 248 asteroids. Traditional discovery involved photographing sky regions, viewing pairs under a stereoscope to spot moving bodies, measuring positions, and sending data to the Minor Planet Center, which determines if observations tie into a single orbit.

### Naming and Designation

By 1851, rapid discovery demanded a new system. In 1852, 20 Massalia received a number and name. Starting 1892, asteroids were listed by year and capital letter; by 1893, letters were doubled (1893AA). A simple chronological numbering system was established in 1925. Currently, new asteroids receive provisional designations (e.g., 2002 AT4) with year and alphanumeric code; once orbits are confirmed, they receive numbers and possibly names (e.g., 433 Eros). The first asteroids received iconic symbols like planets; in 1851, Johann Franz Encke introduced a numbered circle as generic symbol. 20 Massalia was the first without an iconic symbol; none were created after 37 Fides (1855).

### Formation and Distribution

Many asteroids are shattered remnants of planetesimals that never grew into planets. When Jupiter neared its current mass, orbital resonances ejected over 99% of belt planetesimals. Asteroids larger than ~120 km likely accreted during that early era; smaller ones are collision fragments. Ceres and Vesta grew large enough to melt and differentiate. The Nice model suggests some Kuiper-belt objects were captured in the outer belt, possibly becoming D-type asteroids. The main belt contains an estimated 1.1–1.9 million asteroids larger than 1 km, though it is mostly empty space. The four largest objects (Ceres, Vesta, Pallas, Hygiea) account for ~62% of belt mass. Trojans share orbits with planets at Lagrangian points L4 and L5 (60° ahead/behind); over 7,000 Jupiter trojans are catalogued. Near-Earth asteroids (NEAs) are divided into Atiras (orbits inside Earth's), Atens (semi-major axis <1 AU, crossing Earth's orbit), Apollos (semi-major axis >1 AU, crossing), and Amors (orbits outside Earth's). Martian moons Phobos and Deimos may be captured asteroids or impact ejecta—their origin remains debated.

### Physical Characteristics

Asteroids range from ~1 m to Ceres at 940 km diameter. Most are irregularly shaped; the largest are roughly spherical protoplanets. The size distribution follows a power law with "bumps" at ~5 km and ~100 km. Most asteroids over 120 km are primordial; smaller ones are fragmentation products. Very few asteroids over 100 meters rotate faster than 2.2 hours—the limit where inertial force exceeds gravity—suggesting most are rubble piles. Asteroids darken and redden with age due to space weathering, mostly within the first hundred thousand years. Medium-sized asteroids like 253 Mathilde are saturated with radius-size craters. Ceres has fewer large craters than expected, likely due to viscous relaxation. Composition is inferred from albedo, surface spectrum, and density; every asteroid with moons studied so far is a rubble pile. Water ice was confirmed on 24 Themis (2009) and water vapor on Ceres (2014); solar winds can create water in irradiated rock. Amino acids and organic compounds, including sugar molecules like ribose (detected 2019), suggest asteroids may have seeded Earth with life's ingredients.

### Classification Systems

Asteroids are classified by orbital characteristics and reflectance spectra. Families (tight dynamical associations from catastrophic break-ups) were first recognized by Kiyotsugu Hirayama in 1918; 30–35% of belt bodies belong to families. The 1975 Chapman-Morrison-Zellner system had three categories: C-type (75%), S-type (17%), and U (unclassified). This expanded into the Tholen (1984, 14 types) and SMASS (2002, 24 types) taxonomies, both with broad C, S, and X categories. Spectral class does not perfectly correspond to composition. Active asteroids have asteroid-like orbits but comet-like tails; the first discovered was 7968 Elst–Pizarro. DART's impact made Dimorphos an active asteroid, losing ~1 million kg and developing a 10,000-km dust tail. Dark comets (first identified 2024) show non-gravitational acceleration without comae; ʻOumuamua was the first such object identified.

### Exploration and Threats

Ground-based observations provide limited data: visual magnitude, light curves (rotation and shape), and spectra of the thin surface layer. Radar imaging (Arecibo, Goldstone) gives detailed surface and orbital information for near-Earth asteroids. Space-based observatories like NEOWISE and Spitzer detected many more objects. Dedicated missions include NEAR Shoemaker (orbited and landed on Eros, 2001), Hayabusa (returned Itokawa samples, 2010), Dawn (orbited Vesta and Ceres), Hayabusa2 (returned Ryugu samples, 2020), OSIRIS-REx (returned Bennu samples, 2023), Lucy (Jupiter trojans), DART (deflection test, shortened Dimorphos's orbit by ~32 minutes), Psyche (metallic asteroid, arrival 2029), Hera (studying DART results), and Tianwen-2 (Kamoʻoalewa and 311P/PanSTARRS). Asteroid mining was proposed in the 1970s for rare materials and space construction. Impact threat awareness grew from the Alvarez hypothesis (Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction) and Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9's 1994 Jupiter impact. By 2011, an estimated 89–96% of NEAs ≥1 km had been discovered. Deflection strategies include fragmentation and delay; "Project Icarus" (1967) proposed six Saturn V rockets with nuclear warheads against 1566 Icarus.

## Terms

- **Meteoroid**: Object smaller than 1 meter across orbiting the Sun
- **Coma**: Tail or fuzzy atmosphere shown by comets when solar radiation sublimates near-surface ices
- **Titius–Bode law**: Now-discredited formula predicting planetary orbital distances; suggested a planet at 2.8 AU
- **Rubble pile**: Asteroid consisting of loose debris held together by gravity, not solid rock
- **Lagrangian points**: Positions L4 and L5, 60° ahead of and behind a planet, where trojans orbit stably
- **Albedo**: Measure of surface reflectivity; brightness at zero phase angle
- **Space weathering**: Process by which asteroids darken and redden with age from exposure to space
- **Protoplanet**: Large body that formed during solar system accretion but never became a planet
- **Regolith**: Loose granular surface material covering an asteroid
- **Yarkovsky effect**: Thermal force that perturbs asteroid orbits from uneven heat emission

## Debates and Open Questions

The origin of Martian moons Phobos and Deimos is unresolved: they may be captured carbonaceous asteroids or material ejected by an impact on Mars that reaccreted in orbit. The high porosity of Phobos (25–35% voids) and its phyllosilicate composition argue against asteroidal origin, while capture requires mechanisms for circularizing orbits that may not have had sufficient time. The boundary between asteroids and comets is increasingly blurred—observations suggest "a continuum" rather than a sharp division, with some objects classified as both. Spectral classification does not reliably indicate composition; asteroids of the same taxonomic class may differ in material. The internal structure of asteroids is inferred only indirectly from density measurements, satellite orbits, and Yarkovsky drift; the nature of internal porosity remains unclear.
