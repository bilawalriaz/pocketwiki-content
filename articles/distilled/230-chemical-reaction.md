# Chemical reaction

## Overview
A chemical reaction is a process that transforms one set of chemical substances (reactants) into another (products) by rearranging atoms and altering electron positions in chemical bonds, without changing atomic nuclei. These transformations are governed by thermodynamics (energy changes) and kinetics (reaction rates), and are symbolically represented by balanced chemical equations. Reactions are fundamental to industrial synthesis, biological metabolism, and environmental processes, and can be catalyzed to lower activation energy barriers.

## Timeline
- **Antiquity** — Combustion, fermentation, and ore reduction known; Greek philosophers propose Four-Element Theory.
- **c. 850–950** — Works attributed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān describe synthesis of ammonium chloride.
- **c. 1300** — Alchemists begin producing mineral acids (sulfuric, nitric) by heating sulfate/nitrate minerals.
- **1667** — Johann Joachim Becher proposes phlogiston theory.
- **1746** — Lead chamber process developed for large-scale sulfuric acid production.
- **1785** — Antoine Lavoisier disproves phlogiston theory, identifies combustion as reaction with oxygen.
- **1808** — Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac recognizes gases react in definite volume relationships.
- **1828** — Friedrich Wöhler synthesizes urea from inorganic precursors, refuting vitalism.
- **1909–1910** — Haber process developed for industrial ammonia synthesis.

## Body

### Fundamental Concepts
Chemical reactions involve the rearrangement of atoms via the breaking and forming of chemical bonds, accompanied by energy changes. Reactants are the starting substances; products are the resulting substances with different properties. Reactions proceed through a sequence of elementary steps (the reaction mechanism). Nuclear chemistry deals with reactions involving unstable nuclei where nuclear changes occur alongside electronic ones.

### Thermodynamics and Equilibrium
Reactions are driven by Gibbs free energy change (ΔG = ΔH − TΔS). Exergonic reactions (ΔG < 0) proceed spontaneously. Exothermic reactions release heat (ΔH < 0); endothermic reactions absorb heat (ΔH > 0). Most reactions are reversible, reaching chemical equilibrium when forward and reverse rates equalize and ΔG = 0. Le Chatelier's principle predicts equilibrium shifts: increasing pressure favors the side with fewer gas moles; removing products increases yield.

### Kinetics
Reaction rates depend on reactant concentration, surface area, pressure, temperature, activation energy, and catalysts. Higher temperature increases molecular energy and collision frequency. Catalysts lower activation energy by providing an alternative mechanism without being consumed. Rate laws describe concentration dependence (e.g., first-order: v = k[A]). The Arrhenius equation (k = k₀e^(−Ea/kBT)) models temperature dependence. Theories include collision theory, transition state theory, and RRKM theory.

### Reaction Classification
**Four basic types:** Synthesis (A + B → AB), Decomposition (AB → A + B), Single displacement (A + BC → AC + B), Double displacement (AB + CD → AD + CB).  
**Redox:** Involve electron transfer/oxidation state changes. Oxidation is electron loss (increase in oxidation state); reduction is electron gain (decrease). Agents predicted by electronegativity. Electrolytic reactions use electrical energy; batteries reverse this.  
**Combustion:** Reaction with oxidant (usually O₂), often hydrocarbons, releasing heat/light.  
**Acid–base:** Proton transfer (Brønsted–Lowry) or electron-pair exchange (Lewis). Neutralization yields salt.  
**Precipitation:** Formation of insoluble solid from solution exceeding solubility limit.  
**Complexation:** Ligands donate electron pairs to metal centers forming coordination complexes.  
**Photochemical:** Initiated by photon absorption (e.g., photosynthesis, vision).  
**Solid-state/Interface:** Slow due to low diffusion; accelerated by temperature and surface area.

### Organic Reaction Mechanisms
Organic reactions involve covalent bonds of carbon/heteroatoms. Key mechanisms:
- **Substitution:** Group replacement. Nucleophilic (SN1, SN2), Electrophilic (aromatic/aliphatic), Radical (chain reaction).
- **Addition/Elimination:** Change substituent count on carbon. E1/E2 elimination (carbocation vs. concerted); Electrophilic/Nucleophilic/Radical addition. Markovnikov's rule predicts regioselectivity.
- **Rearrangement/Pericyclic:** Carbon skeleton reorganization (Wagner-Meerwein, Cope). Cycloadditions (Diels-Alder [4+2]) governed by orbital symmetry (Woodward–Hoffmann rules).

### Biochemical Reactions
Metabolism comprises enzyme-catalyzed reactions. Enzymes bind substrates at active sites, using mechanisms like induced fit, acid/base catalysis, and proximity effects. Anabolism builds macromolecules (proteins, carbohydrates) using ATP energy derived from glucose oxidation or photosynthesis. Catabolism decomposes organics.

### Applications and Monitoring
Chemical engineering optimizes yield and efficiency using catalysts (heterogeneous/homogeneous). Niche applications include thermite welding. Monitoring: slow reactions via pH, spectroscopy, or radiotracers; fast reactions via ultrafast laser spectroscopy (femtosecond resolution).

## Terms
- **Reactant/Reagent** — Substance initially involved in a chemical reaction.
- **Product** — Substance formed as a result of a chemical reaction.
- **Elementary reaction** — Smallest division of a reaction; no intermediate products; involves one or two molecules.
- **Reaction mechanism** — Actual sequence of individual elementary reactions.
- **Activation energy (Ea)** — Minimum energy required to initiate a reaction by breaking bonds.
- **Catalyst** — Substance that increases reaction rate by lowering activation energy without being consumed.
- **Gibbs free energy (ΔG)** — Thermodynamic potential; ΔG < 0 indicates spontaneous reaction at constant T and P.
- **Equilibrium** — State where forward and reverse reaction rates are equal; ΔG = 0.
- **Redox reaction** — Reaction involving oxidation (electron loss/oxidation state increase) and reduction (electron gain/oxidation state decrease).
- **Enzyme** — Protein catalyst that accelerates specific biochemical reactions at an active site.

## Debates and open questions
- **Vitalism vs. Synthesis:** Historically debated whether organic compounds required a "vital force"; resolved by Wöhler's urea synthesis (1828).
- **Phlogiston vs. Oxygen theory:** Phlogiston theory (1667) disproven by Lavoisier (1785) who established combustion as reaction with oxygen.
- **Reaction mechanism elucidation:** Determining precise elementary steps (e.g., SN1 vs SN2, E1 vs E2) requires experimental kinetics and theoretical modeling (transition state theory, RRKM).
- **Catalyst design:** Optimizing heterogeneous vs. homogeneous catalysts for activity, selectivity, and separation remains an engineering challenge.
- **Ultrafast dynamics:** Observing transition states on femtosecond scales pushes the limits of laser spectroscopy.
