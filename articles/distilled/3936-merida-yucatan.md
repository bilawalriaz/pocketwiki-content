# Mérida, Yucatán

## Overview
Mérida is the capital of the Mexican state of Yucatán and the cultural and financial hub of the Yucatán Peninsula. Founded in 1542 on the site of the Maya city of Ti'ho, its identity is defined by a deep syncretism of Maya and Spanish colonial heritage. It is recognized as one of Mexico's safest and most livable cities, with a unique regional culture, cuisine, and language.

## Body

### History and Foundation
Mérida was founded in 1542 by Spanish conquistadors, including Francisco de Montejo the Younger, on the site of the Maya city of Ti'ho (also called Ichkanzihóo). Many carved stones from Ti'ho's ruins were used in early Spanish buildings, such as the cathedral. From colonial times through the mid-19th century, it was a walled city designed to protect Spanish settlers from Maya revolts. The late 19th century brought economic prosperity from henequen cultivation. In 1905, the first Korean immigrants to Mexico arrived to work on henequen plantations near the city.

### Geography and Climate
Located slightly inland on the Yucatán Peninsula, Mérida is near the center of the Chicxulub Crater. Its topography is very flat, only 9 meters above sea level, with no surface water but numerous cenotes (natural sinkholes). The climate is tropical savanna (Köppen: Aw), hot and humid, with an average annual high of 33.5°C (92.3°F). The rainy season runs from June to October.

### Culture and Society
Mérida's culture is a product of Maya and Spanish syncretism. It has the third-largest historic district in the Americas. The city is a major cultural center with museums, theaters, and the Yucatán Symphony Orchestra. Its unique regional identity is evident in its distinct cuisine (e.g., cochinita pibil, papadzules) and its Spanish dialect, which is heavily influenced by Yucatec Maya. The Maya language is still spoken by a third of the state's population, though it faces social stigma. The city hosts the Mérida English Library for expatriates.

### Economy and Governance
As the state capital, Mérida houses the Yucatán government offices. Its economy has grown at three times the national average, driven by investment and its coastal location. The World Bank ranked it fourth nationally for ease of starting a business. The city is governed by a municipal president and city council.

### Transportation
Mérida is a major transport hub. The Manuel Crescencio Rejón International Airport offers domestic and international flights. The main bus terminal (CAME) provides first-class services across southern Mexico. A new municipal bus system ("Va y Ven") is replacing older private operators, and a bus rapid transit system (Ie-Tram) opened in 2023. The Tren Maya railway connects it to Palenque and Cancún. Major federal highways link it to Progreso, Campeche, and Cancún.

## Terms
- **Syncretism**: The amalgamation of different religions, cultures, or schools of thought.
- **Peninsulares**: Spanish people born in Spain who later moved to the colonies in the Americas.
- **Criollos**: People of Spanish descent born in the Americas.
- **Henequen**: A fiber from an agave plant, historically used to make rope and twine.
- **Cenote**: A natural pit or sinkhole resulting from the collapse of limestone bedrock, exposing groundwater.
- **Yucatec Maya**: The Mayan language spoken by a significant portion of the population in the Yucatán Peninsula.
- **Mérida Initiative**: A security cooperation agreement between the U.S. and Mexico, established in 2007.
- **Tren Maya**: A passenger railway project connecting major cities and archaeological sites in southeastern Mexico.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes a debate regarding the origin of Mérida's nickname, "La Ciudad Blanca" (The White City). One theory attributes it to the white limestone used on colonial buildings. Another is a folk tale that the Spanish only allowed white-skinned Europeans to live in the city, enforced by arches at its entrance. However, the source states the first arches were not built until 1690, nearly 150 years after the city's foundation, casting doubt on this folklore.
