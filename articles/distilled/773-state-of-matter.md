# State of matter

## Overview

A state of matter is a distinct form in which matter can exist, distinguished by how its constituent particles (atoms, molecules, ions, electrons) are arranged and behave collectively. Four classical states—solid, liquid, gas, and plasma—are observable in everyday life, while many additional states arise under extreme conditions or through quantum effects. Understanding states of matter is central to physics, chemistry, and materials science because transitions between states (phase transitions) reveal fundamental physical laws and enable technologies from superconductors to liquid crystal displays.

## Timeline

- **c. 1925** — Einstein predicts Bose–Einstein condensation
- **1911** — Superconductivity discovered
- **1937** — Helium-4 found to form a superfluid below 2.17 K
- **1986** — High-temperature superconductivity discovered in ceramic oxides
- **1995** — First Bose–Einstein condensate produced experimentally (Cornell, Wieman, Ketterle)
- **2000** — Quark–gluon plasma first detected at CERN

## Body

### Classical States

**Solid:** Particles are tightly packed in fixed positions, vibrating in place. Solids have definite shape and volume. Crystalline solids exhibit long-range ordered, repeating atomic patterns (e.g., iron changes from body-centered cubic below 912 °C to face-centered cubic between 912–1,394 °C). Amorphous solids like glass lack this long-range order and are not thermal equilibrium ground states.

**Liquid:** Particles remain close but can move past one another, giving a definite volume but adapting to container shape. Liquids form when solids melt above their melting point. Water is a notable exception where liquid volume exceeds solid volume.

**Gas:** Particles are far apart and move freely, expanding to fill any container. Gases have no definite shape or volume. Above the critical temperature, a gas becomes a supercritical fluid where liquid-gas distinctions vanish; supercritical CO₂ is used in decaffeination.

**Plasma:** A gas containing free electrons and ions that respond to electromagnetic fields. Created by extreme heat or voltage, plasma is the most abundant state in the universe (99% of ordinary matter), found in stars and lightning.

### Phase Transitions

Phase transitions mark abrupt structural changes and define distinct states. Heating a solid melts it to liquid, which boils to gas, then ionizes to plasma. Intermediate stages during staged transitions are called mesophases, exploited in liquid crystal technology. Superconductivity and ferromagnetism also emerge via phase transitions.

### Non-Classical States

**Glass:** Amorphous solids undergoing a glass transition when heated. Thermodynamically metastable relative to crystals, with negligible conversion rates.

**Liquid Crystals:** Exhibit properties between liquids and solids. Nematic phases (e.g., para-azoxyanisole at 118–136 °C) flow like liquids but maintain directional molecular alignment and respond to polarized light.

**Magnetic States:** Ordered by spin alignment rather than spatial arrangement. Ferromagnets (e.g., iron) align all spins in one direction; antiferromagnets (e.g., NiO) have opposing equal spins canceling out; ferrimagnets (e.g., magnetite) have unequal opposing spins yielding net magnetization. Quantum spin liquids remain magnetically disordered even at low temperatures due to geometric frustration.

### Condensates and Superfluids

**Bose–Einstein Condensates (BEC):** Predicted in 1925, BEC forms when bosonic particles cool near absolute zero, causing a large fraction to occupy the same quantum state. Helium-4 becomes a superfluid below 2.17 K, exhibiting zero viscosity and quantized vortices. First gas-phase BEC achieved in 1995 using rubidium and sodium atoms.

**Fermionic Condensates:** Fermions pair into composite bosons that condense similarly. Superconductors exhibit zero electrical resistance and expel magnetic fields via the Meissner effect. High-temperature superconductivity (up to 164 K) was discovered in 1986 in ceramic oxides.

### High-Energy States

**Degenerate Matter:** Found in stellar cores, supported by the Pauli exclusion principle rather than thermal pressure. Electron-degenerate matter exists in white dwarfs; neutron-degenerate matter in neutron stars, where electrons combine with protons via inverse beta-decay.

**Quark Matter:** At extreme densities or temperatures, quarks become deconfined. Strange matter may exist in massive neutron stars. Quark–gluon plasma (QGP), a high-temperature phase where quarks move freely among gluons, was first detected at CERN in 2000. Unlike gas-like plasma, QGP flows like a liquid with strong interactions.

**Color-Glass Condensate:** A theoretical state in nuclei moving near light speed, where gluon density becomes extremely high, forming a "gluonic wall."

### Proposed States

Several theoretical states remain unconfirmed: **supersolids** (solids with superfluid properties), **string-net liquids** (topologically ordered materials), **superglasses** (combining superfluidity with frozen amorphous structure), **chain-melted states** (coexisting liquid and solid regions in metals under extreme conditions), **quantum Hall states** (quantized electrical transport), and **photonic matter** (photons acquiring effective mass through gas interactions).

## Terms

- **Phase transition:** Abrupt structural change between states of matter, marked by property changes.
- **Mesophase:** Intermediate phase during staged state transitions.
- **Supercritical fluid:** Substance above its critical temperature and pressure, where liquid-gas distinctions disappear.
- **Degenerate matter:** Matter supported by quantum mechanical pressure (Pauli exclusion) rather than thermal motion.
- **Bose–Einstein condensate:** State where bosons occupy the same quantum ground state at ultra-low temperatures.
- **Fermionic condensate:** Analogous to BEC but formed by paired fermions.
- **Meissner effect:** Complete expulsion of magnetic fields from a superconductor.
- **Quantum spin liquid:** Magnetically disordered state preserving disorder to very low temperatures.
- **Color-flavor locked (CFL) phase:** Theoretical high-density quark matter phase that is superconductive for color charge.

## Debates and Open Questions

- Whether supersolids constitute a distinct state of matter or an exotic property of solids remains debated.
- The existence and stability of strange matter in neutron stars lack direct evidence.
- The nature of the quark liquid at high densities but low temperatures is unknown.
- Color-glass condensate behavior at the Large Hadron Collider is still under investigation.
- The mechanism behind high-temperature superconductivity is not fully understood.
- Whether string-net liquids represent fundamental conditions of the universe requires further study.
- The Hagedorn temperature and Planck-scale states cannot currently be produced or described by existing theories.
