# Pink Floyd

## Overview
Pink Floyd are an English rock band formed in London in 1965 by Syd Barrett (guitar, vocals), Nick Mason (drums), Roger Waters (bass guitar, vocals) and Richard Wright (keyboards, vocals), with David Gilmour (guitar, vocals) joining at the end of 1967. Gaining an early underground following as one of the first British psychedelic groups, they were distinguished by their extended compositions, sonic experiments, philosophical lyrics, and elaborate live performances, becoming a leading progressive rock band. Internal tensions fractured the classic lineup: Barrett left in 1968 due to deteriorating mental health; personal tensions led to Wright leaving the band in 1981, followed by Waters in 1985. Gilmour and Mason continued as Pink Floyd, rejoined later by Wright. They produced the studio albums *A Momentary Lapse of Reason* (1987) and *The Division Bell* (1994), both backed by major tours. In 2005, Gilmour, Mason and Wright reunited with Waters for a performance at the global awareness event Live 8. Barrett died in 2006, and Wright in 2008. The last Pink Floyd studio album, *The Endless River* (2014), was based on unreleased material from the *Division Bell* recording sessions. In 2022, Pink Floyd released the song "Hey, Hey, Rise Up!" in protest of the Russian invasion of Ukraine. Pink Floyd also composed several film scores. With over 250 million records sold, they were inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame (1996) and awarded the Polar Music Prize (2008).

## Body

### Formation and the Syd Barrett Era (1963–1968)
The band originated in 1962–63 when architecture students Roger Waters and Nick Mason met at London Polytechnic, later joined by fellow student Richard Wright. After cycling through names (Sigma 6, the Meggadeaths, the Abdabs, the Screaming Abdabs, Leonard's Lodgers, the Spectrum Five, the Tea Set) and personnel, childhood friend Syd Barrett joined in late 1963. By late 1965, the quartet—Barrett, Waters, Mason, Wright—became the Pink Floyd Sound, named after bluesmen Pink Anderson and Floyd Council. Managed by Peter Jenner and Andrew King (Blackhill Enterprises), they became fixtures of the London underground scene at the UFO Club, pioneering light shows and extended instrumental improvisations.

Signed to EMI in February 1967, they released two hit singles, "Arnold Layne" and "See Emily Play," and the acclaimed debut *The Piper at the Gates of Dawn* (August 1967), almost entirely written by Barrett. However, Barrett’s heavy LSD use precipitated a severe mental breakdown. His behavior became erratic—standing motionless on stage, refusing to mime on TV—and a US tour was cut short. In December 1967, David Gilmour was recruited as a fifth member to cover Barrett’s guitar parts, with the intention of retaining Barrett as a non-performing songwriter. This proved unworkable; Barrett’s "Have You Got It Yet?" sessions deliberately sabotaged rehearsals. In March 1968, the band simply stopped picking him up for a gig, ending his tenure. Jenner and King departed to manage Barrett; Steve O'Rourke became the band's manager.

### Musical Transition and the "Classic" Lineup (1968–1972)
Post-Barrett, Waters emerged as the primary lyricist and conceptual architect. *A Saucerful of Secrets* (1968) mixed Barrett’s final track ("Jugband Blues") with Waters’s emerging epic style ("Set the Controls for the Heart of the Sun"). The band experimented wildly on *Ummagumma* (1969), a double LP of live tracks and solo experimental pieces, and *Atom Heart Mother* (1970), a collaboration with Ron Geesin featuring a side-long suite. Waters later disowned *Atom Heart Mother* as "rubbish."

*Meddle* (1971) marked a critical turning point, featuring the 23-minute "Echoes," which *Rolling Stone* called the "Zenith which the Floyd have been striving for." It confirmed Gilmour as a major musical force. The band also composed film scores (*The Committee* 1968, *More* 1969, *Zabriskie Point* 1970, *Obscured by Clouds* 1972), the latter yielding their first US chart hit ("Free Four") and introducing the EMS VCS 3 synthesizer.

### The Waters-Led Blockbusters (1973–1983)
*The Dark Side of the Moon* (1973), recorded at Abbey Road with engineer Alan Parsons, explored themes of madness, time, and modern pressure. Its iconic prism cover (Hipgnosis/George Hardie) and seamless song flow made it a cultural phenomenon: US No. 1, 741 weeks on the Billboard chart, >45 million sold. Wealth followed; the band switched US labels to Columbia for a $1M advance.

*Wish You Were Here* (1975) addressed the music industry’s cruelty and Barrett’s absence. During recording, Barrett visited the studio unrecognizably changed; Waters was deeply shaken. The album hit No. 1 in UK and US.

*Animals* (1977), based on Orwell’s *Animal Farm*, divided society into Dogs (capitalists), Pigs (corrupt leaders), and Sheep (the exploited). Tensions flared over royalties: Gilmour’s side-long "Dogs" earned less than Waters’s short "Pigs on the Wing." Wright received no writing credits, signaling his marginalization.

*The Wall* (1979), co-produced by Bob Ezrin, dramatized a rock star’s isolation behind a metaphorical wall, inspired by Waters’s father’s WWII death and Barrett’s decline. During sessions, Wright was fired for lack of contribution but stayed as a paid musician for the theatrical tour (featuring a 40-foot cardboard wall built during the show). The single "Another Brick in the Wall (Part 2)" hit No. 1 globally. The 1982 film *Pink Floyd – The Wall* (starring Bob Geldof) won two BAFTAs.

*The Final Cut* (1983), driven by the Falklands War, was a Waters-dominated anti-war requiem for his father. Gilmour objected to recycled *Wall* outtakes; his name was briefly removed from credits. *Rolling Stone* called it "essentially a Roger Waters solo album."

### Schism, Legal Battles, and the Gilmour Era (1985–1994)
Waters announced his departure in 1985, declaring Pink Floyd "a spent force" and suing to dissolve the partnership and veto the name. Discovering the partnership was never formalized, he sought a High Court injunction. Gilmour and Mason countersued; a 1987 settlement granted Gilmour/Mason perpetual rights to the name, while Waters retained rights to *The Wall* concepts. Waters later regretted the suit.

*A Momentary Lapse of Reason* (1987) began as a Gilmour solo project but became a Pink Floyd album. Wright rejoined on a weekly salary ($11,000); Gilmour co-wrote lyrics with Anthony Moore. Waters dismissed it as a "clever forgery." The ensuing tour was nearly derailed by Waters’s legal threats against promoters; the band famously modified their inflatable pig with genitalia to distinguish it from Waters’s version.

*The Division Bell* (1994) saw Gilmour, Mason, and Wright collaborating more equally (Wright earned five co-writes). Polly Samson (Gilmour’s future wife) co-wrote "High Hopes." The album hit No. 1 globally. The subsequent tour, featuring a full *Dark Side* performance, was the band’s last.

### Reunions, Final Works, and Legacy (2005–Present)
After years of public acrimony, Bob Geldof brokered a reunion for Live 8 (July 2005). The four-man performance (Gilmour, Mason, Waters, Wright) was emotionally charged; they embraced onstage. Gilmour subsequently ruled out further tours. Barrett died in 2006; Wright in 2008.

Gilmour and Mason mined *Division Bell* sessions with Wright to create *The Endless River* (2014), an ambient instrumental tribute to Wright. It debuted at No. 1 but had no tour. In 2018, Mason formed Nick Mason’s Saucerful of Secrets to perform early material.

Relations between Gilmour and Waters remained hostile. A 2022 *Animals* remix was delayed by liner-note disputes. In March 2022, Gilmour and Mason released "Hey, Hey, Rise Up!" featuring Ukrainian singer Andriy Khlyvnyuk, protesting the invasion of Ukraine. Waters criticized the single; Gilmour and his wife Polly Samson publicly denounced Waters. In October 2024, the band agreed to sell their recording catalogue to Sony Music for ~$400 million (excluding songwriting rights).

## Terms
- ****Psychedelic rock**** — A style of rock music inspired by psychedelic culture and hallucinogens, characterized by extended improvisation, sonic effects (feedback, echo), and surreal lyrics; Pink Floyd pioneered this in the UK underground (1966–67).
- ****Progressive rock**** — A genre emphasizing compositional complexity, conceptual themes, and studio experimentation over standard pop formats; Pink Floyd became a leading exponent in the 1970s.
- ****Concept album**** — An album unified by a central theme, narrative, or lyrical motif rather than a collection of unrelated songs; *The Dark Side of the Moon*, *Animals*, *The Wall*, and *The Final Cut* are key examples.
- ****Quadraphonic sound**** — A four-channel audio system projecting sound 360 degrees around the listener; Pink Floyd pioneered live use via the Azimuth Co-ordinator (1967) and custom PA (1972).
- ****Azimuth Co-ordinator**** — An early quadraphonic panning device (joystick-controlled) used by Wright to swirl sound around venues during performances of "See Emily Play" and later tours.
- ****Hipgnosis**** — Graphic design partnership (Storm Thorgerson, Aubrey Powell) responsible for iconic Pink Floyd album covers from *A Saucerful of Secrets* (1968) to *The Division Bell* (1994), excluding *The Wall*.
- ****Binson Echorec**** — A magnetic drum delay unit used extensively by Barrett and Gilmour for signature echo/feedback textures; central to the band’s early "sonic carpets."
- ****EMS VCS 3**** — A portable British synthesizer (purchased from BBC Radiophonic Workshop) used by Wright and Waters from *Obscured by Clouds* (1972) onward for textural effects.
- ****Holophonic system**** — An audio processing technique (used on *The Final Cut*, 1983) simulating 3D sound movement around the listener’s head via standard stereo headphones.
- ****Publius Enigma**** — An internet-based mystery/riddle campaign (1994) planted by the record company during the *Division Bell* tour, projecting "Enigma Publius" lights at concerts.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authorship and "Band" Identity**: To what extent is post-1985 Pink Floyd (Gilmour/Mason) a continuation vs. a new entity? Waters argued the band ended in 1985; courts and the marketplace upheld Gilmour/Mason’s right to the name.
- **Wright’s Contribution**: Wright’s role is often minimized (no writing credits on *Animals*/*The Wall*, salaried rehiring in 1987). However, Gilmour, Mason, and Waters (posthumously) acknowledged his "soulful" keyboard textures as essential to the band’s signature sound.
- **Barrett’s Legacy vs. Exploitation**: The band’s most famous works (*Wish You Were Here*, *The Wall*, *Shine On You Crazy Diamond*) center on Barrett’s tragedy. Critics debate whether this constitutes homage or commercial exploitation of a vulnerable former member.
- **Catalogue Sale (2024)**: The $400M sale to Sony Music (recordings/merch, not publishing) required unanimous consent after years of deadlock. The exclusion of songwriting rights leaves Waters (primary lyricist) and Gilmour (primary composer) with ongoing control—and potential conflict—over licensing.
- **"Hey, Hey, Rise Up!" (2022)**: Was this a genuine political statement or a legacy-brand exercise? Waters’s condemnation and the Gilmour/Samson public attack highlight whether the "Pink Floyd" name can ever represent a unified voice again.
