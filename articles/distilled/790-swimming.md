# Swimming

## Overview  
Swimming is the self-propulsion of a human through water using coordinated limb and body movements to generate buoyancy and hydrodynamic thrust. It serves purposes ranging from recreation, sport, and exercise to survival and professional work, and is among the most popular public recreational activities worldwide. The science of swimming involves principles of buoyancy, drag, and hydrodynamics, while its history spans prehistoric art, ancient texts, and modern Olympic competition.

## Timeline  
- **~7000 BCE** — Earliest swimming depicted in Stone Age paintings
- **2000 BCE** — Written references appear in texts like the Epic of Gilgamesh
- **450 BCE** — Herodotus notes drowning risks in naval warfare
- **~500 CE** — Talmud mandates teaching sons to swim
- **1538** — Nikolaus Wynmann publishes first known complete swimming book
- **1800** — Competitive swimming begins in Europe
- **1873** — John Arthur Trudgen introduces trudgen stroke to Western competition
- **1896** — Swimming included in first modern Summer Olympics
- **1930s** — Butterfly stroke developed
- **1953** — Butterfly recognized as separate competitive stroke
- **2022** — FINA renamed World Aquatics

## Body  

### Science  
Swimming depends on the near-neutral buoyancy of the human body, which has an average relative density of 0.98 compared to water. Buoyancy varies with body composition—higher fat content and saltier water increase floatation, while males, with greater muscle mass and lower center of gravity, tend to sink more easily. Water supports body weight, making swimming low-impact, but its density and viscosity create resistance (drag). Swimmers reduce drag through horizontal positioning, body roll, and full arm extension. Power increases drag cubically, so reducing resistance is more effective than increasing stroke force. Pre-swim exercises like arm swings and strength training (e.g., squats) enhance performance; a 2022 review found combined strength training improves short- and medium-distance swimming.

### Infant Swimming  
Newborns display an innate swimming and diving reflex until about ten months. Immersion triggers apnea, bradycardia, and peripheral vasoconstriction—spontaneously holding breath and slowing heart rate. Infant swimming classes reinforce these instincts to build muscle memory and water safety.

### Technique  
Strokes are repeated motions used for propulsion. The four competitive strokes are front crawl (fastest), breaststroke, backstroke, and butterfly. Untrained swimmers often use doggy paddle. Additional non-competitive strokes include sidestroke, used in lifesaving. Strokes can be modified to isolate limbs or accommodate disabilities.

### History  
Swimming is documented in prehistoric art and ancient literature. Coastal European tribes were noted for swimming prowess. The first complete swimming manual was written in 1538. Competitive swimming began in Europe around 1800. The trudgen and butterfly strokes emerged in the 19th and 20th centuries. Swimming entered the Olympics in 1896.

### Purpose  
People swim for recreation, fitness, rehabilitation, employment, or survival. It is a full-body aerobic exercise beneficial for cardiovascular health, muscle tone, flexibility, and joint conditions. However, it does not promote bone density due to its non-weight-bearing nature.

### Health  
Swimming improves cardiovascular health, muscle strength, and mental well-being. It is ideal for arthritis and joint injuries due to reduced joint stress. Outdoor ("wild") swimming is linked to higher well-being than pool swimming. Risks include chlorine inhalation, ear infections, and rare heart issues during exertion.

### Sport  
Competitive swimming involves racing over set distances using standardized strokes. Training includes daily sessions and a taper phase before competition. Governed by World Aquatics (formerly FINA), events occur in 25m and 50m pools. Related disciplines include diving, water polo, and open water swimming.

### Safety  
Drowning is the primary risk, with an average of 3,536 annual fatal unintentional drownings in the U.S. (2005–2014). Lifeguards supervise public areas. Water safety education, buddy systems, and emergency response training are essential.

### Occupation  
Occupational swimming includes military operations (e.g., Navy SEALs), marine biology research, rescue services, and professional sports. Military recruits in the U.S. Navy, Marine Corps, and Coast Guard must complete basic swim training.

### Risks  
Water-related deaths stem from panic, exhaustion, hypothermia, blunt trauma, and marine life encounters. Injuries include surfer’s ear, infections, and jellyfish stings. Electric shocks from eels and rays are rare but possible.

### Lessons  
Formal swimming instruction begins in infancy. Countries like Sweden and Finland mandate school swimming programs. In the U.S., the Infant Swimming Resource teaches roll-back-to-float techniques. Programs vary globally, with some integrating lessons into school curricula.

### Clothing and Equipment  
Swimwear ranges from casual boardshorts to competitive jammers and diveskins. Wetsuits provide thermal insulation and buoyancy. Accessories include goggles, earplugs, snorkels, kickboards, pull buoys, fins, paddles, and safety gear. Nudity is permitted in some European settings.

## Terms  
- **Buoyancy**: The upward force exerted by water that allows the body to float.  
- **Drag**: Resistance created by water opposing body motion.  
- **Hydrodynamics**: The study of forces and motion in fluids, applied to optimize swimming efficiency.  
- **Stroke**: A repeated limb and body movement pattern used to propel through water.  
- **Taper**: A training phase where intensity is reduced before competition to maximize power.  
- **Mammalian Diving Reflex**: A physiological response to cold water immersion, slowing heart rate and conserving oxygen.  
- **Trudgen**: An early competitive swimming stroke involving alternating arm circles and frog-like leg kicks.  
- **Roll-back-to-float**: A survival technique taught to infants to float face-up after falling into water.  
- **Wild Swimming**: Swimming in natural bodies of water such as rivers, lakes, or oceans.  
- **World Aquatics**: The international governing body for aquatic sports, formerly known as FINA.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- Whether strength training should be integrated into swim training programs remains debated, though recent studies support its benefits if volume is controlled.  
- The effectiveness of infant swimming lessons in preventing drownings is questioned, given that drowning remains a leading cause of child death despite high swimming literacy rates in some countries.  
- The impact of swimming on bone density continues to be studied, particularly regarding adolescent athletes.  
- The classification of the butterfly as a distinct stroke versus a variant of breaststroke was historically contested before its official recognition in 1953.
