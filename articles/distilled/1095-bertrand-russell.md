# Bertrand Russell

## Overview
Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) was a British philosopher, logician, and social reformer who co-founded analytic philosophy and logicism—the thesis that mathematics reduces to logic. His technical work, notably *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913) with Alfred North Whitehead and the essay "On Denoting" (1905), reshaped the philosophy of language, logic, and mathematics. Simultaneously, his aristocratic background and early orphanhood fueled a lifelong commitment to pacifism, anti-imperialism, nuclear disarmament, and freedom of thought, leading to imprisonment in WWI, the 1950 Nobel Prize in Literature, and leadership in the Russell–Einstein Manifesto (1955) and the Russell Vietnam War Crimes Tribunal (1966). His career exemplifies the tension between abstract logical rigor and radical political engagement.

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Russell was born into the liberal aristocracy; his grandfather, Lord John Russell, served twice as Prime Minister. Orphaned by age four, he was raised by his religiously conservative but socially progressive grandmother, Countess Russell, who instilled a sense of social justice. Adolescence was marked by loneliness and suicidal ideation, relieved by mathematics (Euclid at eleven) and the poetry of Shelley. By eighteen, reading Mill’s *Autobiography* ended his theism; he became an atheist, rejecting free will and immortality. At Cambridge (1890–1893), he absorbed the "revolt against idealism" led by Moore and Whitehead, shifting from mathematics to philosophy and adopting a realist, logical analysis of propositions.

### Logic, Logicism, and Analytic Philosophy
Russell’s technical legacy rests on three pillars. First, **logicism**: the thesis that all mathematical truths are logical tautologies. *The Principles of Mathematics* (1903) and *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913, with Whitehead) attempted to derive arithmetic from a few logical axioms, though Gödel later proved the system incomplete. Second, **Russell’s Paradox** (1901): the set of all sets that do not contain themselves both does and does not contain itself, exposing a contradiction in naive set theory and prompting type theory as a fix. Third, **"On Denoting" (1905)**: a theory of definite descriptions ("the present King of France") that dissolves puzzles about non-existent entities by analyzing them as quantified logical forms rather than referring expressions. These works founded **analytic philosophy**, emphasizing logical analysis of language as the primary philosophical method. Russell supervised Ludwig Wittgenstein, whose *Tractatus* extended and challenged his views.

### First World War and the Cost of Pacifism
Russell’s pacifism was absolute in WWI. He chaired the No-Conscription Fellowship, defended conscientious objectors, and spoke at the 1917 Leeds Convention of anti-war socialists. In 1916, Trinity College dismissed him under the Defence of the Realm Act; he refused to pay the £100 fine, hoping for prison, but friends bought his auctioned books. In 1918, he served six months in Brixton for publicly opposing US entry into the war. He found prison "agreeable"—uninterrupted reading and writing produced *Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy* and *The Analysis of Mind*. G. H. Hardy’s 1941 pamphlet *Bertrand Russell and Trinity* documents the Fellows’ opposition to his dismissal and his 1919 reinstatement, followed by resignation in 1920 amid personal turmoil (divorce and remarriage).

### Interwar Travels, Education, and Political Evolution
Russell visited Soviet Russia (1920), meeting Lenin and finding him "impishly cruel"; the trip ended his Bolshevik sympathies (*The Practice and Theory of Bolshevism*). He then lectured in China (1921–1922) with Dora Black, falling gravely ill. With Dora he founded **Beacon Hill School** (1927), an experimental progressive school rejecting both traditional rote learning and what he saw as permissive flaws in other progressive models. He stood unsuccessfully for Parliament as Labour candidate in Chelsea (1922, 1923). Early in this period he endorsed eugenic policies (health certificates for parents, sterilization of the "feebleminded"), a stance he later abandoned. By the 1930s he chaired the India League (1932–1939), advocating Indian independence, and moved toward guild socialism—social control of major means of production without state ownership.

### Second World War and the Shift to "Relative Political Pacifism"
Initially an appeaser (1937: "treat [Germans] as visitors"), Russell reversed course in 1940, judging Hitler’s domination a permanent threat to democracy. In 1943 he articulated **relative political pacifism**: war is a great evil, but sometimes the lesser of two evils. His US sojourn (1938–1944) included a court-blocked appointment at City College of New York (deemed "morally unfit" for views on sexual morality in *Marriage and Morals*), defended by Einstein and Dewey. Lectures at the Barnes Foundation became *A History of Western Philosophy* (1945), a commercial success securing his finances.

### Nuclear Disarmament and Cold War Activism
Post-1945, Russell’s focus shifted to nuclear peril. In 1948 he argued a preemptive Western nuclear strike on the USSR might be humanitarian if it prevented a later, extinction-level exchange—a view he soon retracted for mutual disarmament. The **Russell–Einstein Manifesto** (1955), signed by eleven leading scientists, called for abolition of nuclear weapons and led to the Pugwash Conferences. In 1960 he founded the **Committee of 100** for mass nonviolent civil disobedience against nuclear weapons; at 89 he served seven days in Brixton (1961). He opposed the Vietnam War as near-genocidal, co-organizing the **Russell Vietnam War Crimes Tribunal** (1966–1967) with Sartre. His final public statement (31 Jan 1970) condemned Israeli bombing in the War of Attrition and called for withdrawal to pre-1967 borders.

### Philosophy of Religion, Ethics, and Society
Russell described himself as an agnostic philosophically, atheist popularly. He viewed religion as superstition that impedes knowledge and fosters fear, causing wars and misery. Ethically, he moved from youthful utilitarianism to a pluralistic view; he championed **The Will to Doubt** (Alfred Henry Lloyd): all beliefs are fallible, truth grows by hearing all sides, controlling bias, and discarding failed hypotheses. Politically, he advocated a **scientific society** with world government to enforce peace, population control, shared prosperity, and civil liberties. He supported homosexual law reform (1958), a universal basic income (*Roads to Freedom*, 1918), and Palestinian rights, while recognizing Israel’s existence as a fact. His *Impact of Science on Society* (1951) warned that scientific dictatorship could perfect indoctrination ("snow is black") to make populations believe they are happy while miserable.

### Marriages and Personal Life
Four marriages: Alys Pearsall Smith (1894–1921, divorced); Dora Black (1921–1935, two children); Patricia Spence (1936–1952, one son Conrad); Edith Finch (1952–1970, happy union). Eldest son John suffered mental illness; daughter Katharine founded the Bertrand Russell Society (1974). Russell inherited the earldom in 1931, sitting in the House of Lords until death.

## Terms
- **Analytic philosophy**: A tradition, co-founded by Russell, emphasizing logical analysis of language and concepts as the primary philosophical method.
- **Logicism**: The thesis that mathematics is reducible to logic; all mathematical truths are logical tautologies.
- **Russell’s Paradox**: The contradiction arising from the set of all sets that do not contain themselves (if it contains itself, it shouldn’t; if it doesn’t, it should), undermining naive set theory.
- **Theory of descriptions**: Russell’s 1905 analysis of definite descriptions ("the F") as quantified logical forms (∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y=x))), eliminating reference to non-existent entities.
- **Type theory**: Russell’s hierarchical solution to his paradox, restricting sets to contain only members of lower "types" to avoid self-reference.
- **Relative political pacifism**: Russell’s 1943 stance: war is always a great evil, but in extreme circumstances (e.g., resisting Hitler) it may be the lesser of two evils.
- **Russell–Einstein Manifesto (1955)**: A public declaration by eleven eminent scientists, initiated by Russell, calling for nuclear disarmament and peaceful resolution of conflict.
- **Committee of 100 (1960)**: A British mass civil-disobedience movement against nuclear weapons, founded by Russell and Michael Scott.
- **Russell Vietnam War Crimes Tribunal (1966–1967)**: An investigative body organized by Russell and Sartre to document alleged US war crimes in Vietnam.
- **The Will to Doubt**: Alfred Henry Lloyd’s concept, endorsed by Russell, that all knowledge is provisional and scientific progress requires systematic doubt and openness to revision.

## Debates and open questions
- **Logicism’s viability**: Gödel’s incompleteness theorems (1931) showed *Principia Mathematica* cannot prove all arithmetic truths within its system; debate continues over whether logicism survives in a modified form.
- **Preemptive nuclear strike (1948)**: Russell’s brief endorsement of a first strike on the USSR remains contested—was it a genuine strategic judgment or a rhetorical deterrent?
- **Eugenics advocacy (1920s–1930s)**: Russell’s early support for sterilization and health certificates conflicts with his later humanism; scholars debate the extent and sincerity of his recantation.
- **World government vs. liberty**: His call for a "single supreme world government" to enforce peace raises unresolved tension between global coercion and the individual freedom he championed.
- **Ordinary language philosophy**: Russell’s later hostility to Wittgenstein’s and Ryle’s linguistic turn (e.g., *Words and Things* foreword) split the analytic tradition; the debate shaped mid-century philosophy.
- **Vietnam Tribunal legitimacy**: Critics called the Russell Tribunal a "show trial" lacking legal authority; defenders argue it filled a moral vacuum when official bodies failed to investigate.
