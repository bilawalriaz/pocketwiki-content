# Jonathan Edwards (theologian)

## Overview
Jonathan Edwards (1703–1758) was a pivotal American revivalist preacher, philosopher, and theologian whose work shaped the First Great Awakening and founded the New England theological tradition. He is celebrated as a foundational figure in American intellectual history, blending Puritan theology with Enlightenment thought, though his legacy is complicated by his ownership of enslaved people.

## Timeline
- **1703** — Born in East Windsor, Connecticut.
- **1716** — Enters Yale College at age 13.
- **1727** — Ordained minister in Northampton, Massachusetts; marries Sarah Pierpont.
- **1731** — Preaches "God Glorified in the Work of Redemption," his first public attack on Arminianism.
- **1733–35** — Leads a major revival in Northampton, documented in *A Faithful Narrative*.
- **1741** — Preaches "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" in Enfield, Connecticut.
- **1749** — Publishes *The Life of David Brainerd*.
- **1751** — Dismissed from Northampton; becomes pastor in Stockbridge and missionary to the Housatonic Indians.
- **1754** — Publishes *Freedom of the Will*.
- **1758** — Becomes president of the College of New Jersey (Princeton); dies from smallpox inoculation.

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Jonathan Edwards was the only son of a minister and received an excellent education, entering Yale College in 1716. He was profoundly influenced by John Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* and the scientific discoveries of Isaac Newton. Rather than seeing science as leading to deism, Edwards viewed the natural world as evidence of God's masterful design, a theme central to his later theology. He was ordained in 1727 and became the sole minister of Northampton after his grandfather's death in 1729.

### The First Great Awakening
Edwards became a key leader of the First Great Awakening, a religious revival movement. A major revival began in Northampton in 1733, during which nearly 300 youths joined the church. He documented this in *A Faithful Narrative* (1737). The movement faced criticism for emotional excesses, and a "suicide craze" among the spiritually distressed effectively ended its first wave. Edwards later collaborated with evangelist George Whitefield, whose 1739–40 tour reignited revival fervor. Edwards's most famous sermon, "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" (1741), was delivered during this second wave. He defended the revivals in works like *Distinguishing Marks of a Work of the Spirit of God* (1741) and later systematized his views on genuine conversion in *Religious Affections* (1746).

### Theological Works and Later Ministry
After his dismissal from Northampton in 1751 over disputes about communion qualifications, Edwards served as a pastor and missionary in Stockbridge, Massachusetts. There, he wrote his major philosophical treatises, including *Original Sin*, *The Nature of True Virtue*, *The End for Which God Created the World*, and *Freedom of the Will* (1754). He defended the institution of slavery for certain categories of people but rejected the Atlantic slave trade. In 1758, he became president of the College of New Jersey but died shortly after from a smallpox inoculation.

### Legacy and Influence
Edwards's followers, known as New Light Calvinists, included Samuel Hopkins and Joseph Bellamy, who propagated his "New England theology." His writings deeply influenced 19th-century American revivalism and foreign missions. His descendants became a prominent American family, including Vice President Aaron Burr. Modern scholarship has re-evaluated his work, emphasizing its philosophical depth and engagement with the Enlightenment. The Jonathan Edwards Center at Yale University maintains a critical edition of his works.

## Terms
- **Congregationalist**: A Protestant Christian tradition where local congregations are self-governing.
- **First Great Awakening**: A series of Christian revivals that swept the British colonies in North America during the 1730s and 1740s.
- **Arminianism**: A theological perspective emphasizing human free will in salvation, opposed by Edwards's Calvinist view of God's absolute sovereignty.
- **Half-Way Covenant**: A 1662 practice allowing baptized but unconverted church members to have their children baptized, which Edwards opposed.
- **New England theology**: A school of thought derived from Edwards that modified traditional Calvinism, emphasizing God's benevolence and moral agency.
- **Supply pastor**: A clergyman employed temporarily to minister to a church without being settled as its permanent pastor.
- **Complementarian views**: The belief that men and women have different but complementary roles and responsibilities in marriage and church leadership.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The "suicide craze"**: The extent and cause of suicides during the 1735 revival downturn are debated; the source notes at least two, including Edwards's uncle.
- **Edwards's preaching style**: The characterization of his preaching as "fire and brimstone" is contested; historical accounts suggest he spoke in a quiet, emotive voice.
- **The Northampton dismissal**: Scholarly research has questioned the traditional narrative that a list of names read by Edwards caused a major congregational uproar.
- **Slavery**: Edwards's ownership of enslaved people has prompted modern debate about reconciling his theological legacy with his participation in slavery.
