# Arnold Schoenberg

## Overview

Arnold Schoenberg (1874–1951) was an Austrian-American composer, theorist, and teacher who fundamentally reshaped 20th-century music through three revolutionary innovations: developing variation, the emancipation of dissonance, and twelve-tone composition. Born into a Jewish family in Vienna, he synthesized Brahmsian motivic development with Wagnerian harmonic expansion before abandoning tonality entirely, creating atonal music that expressed extreme emotional states. Fleeing Nazi persecution, he emigrated to the U.S., where his twelve-tone system became the foundation for post-war modernist composition, influencing generations of composers and establishing him as one of the most consequential and controversial figures in Western art music.

## Body

### Early Development and Late Romantic Synthesis (1874–1907)

Schoenberg was born into a lower-middle-class Jewish family in Vienna's historically Jewish Leopoldstadt district. Largely self-taught, he began composing as a child, arranging military band music and works by Bellini and Pleyel. His formal training began around 1894 with counterpoint studies under Alexander von Zemlinsky, who became a lifelong mentor and brother-in-law. In 1898, Schoenberg converted to Lutheran Christianity—a common pattern of Jewish assimilation—but maintained his Jewish identity, which became increasingly central as antisemitism intensified.

His early masterpieces synthesized the opposing aesthetics of Brahms and Wagner. *Verklärte Nicht*, Op. 4 (1899), developed leitmotif-like themes through "developing variation"—a technique Schoenberg identified primarily in Brahms's music—where motivic elements are perpetually dissolved, varied, and recombined. This approach created a unique position between Brahmsian motivic development and Wagnerian narrative continuity. The work's success brought Schoenberg to the attention of Gustav Mahler, whose Third Symphony profoundly converted him from initial dismissiveness to devotion.

### Crisis and Breakthrough: Toward Atonality (1907–1911)

The period 1907–1911 marked Schoenberg's most dramatic artistic crisis. His String Quartet No. 2 (1907–1908), dedicated to his wife Mathilde, extended chromatic harmony toward atonality while incorporating Stefan George's Symbolist poetry. The quartet's final movements added soprano voices setting George's "Litanei" and "Entrückung," using harmonies Schoenberg later described as "schwebende" (fluctuating) and "aufgehoben" (suspended). During his wife's brief abandonment for painter Richard Gerstl, Schoenberg composed "Du lehnest wider eine Silberweide" from *Das Buch der Hängenden Gärten*, Op. 15—possibly his first composition without traditional key references.

The summer of 1910 brought his *Harmonielehre* (Harmony Study), codifying his theoretical approach. In 1911, Mahler's funeral in Grinzing deeply affected him, and the final Six Little Piano Pieces echoed death knells with bell-like tolling. A neighbor's antisemitic abuse drove him to flee Vienna for Zemlinsky's home on Lake Starnberg, where he encountered Wassily Kandinsky and other Blaue Reiter painters, further integrating his musical work with contemporary visual art movements.

### Expressionist Berlin and World War I (1911–1918)

Moving to Berlin in September 1911, Schoenberg joined Herwarth Walden's Der Sturm circle alongside Else Lasker-Schüler, Franz Werfel, and other expressionist artists. His *Herzgewächse* (1911) appeared in the *Der Blaue Reiter Almanach* (1912), and he composed *Pierrot lunaire*, Op. 21, using Sprechstimme (speaking voice that realizes pitch only fleetingly) for Albertine Zehme's recitation of Giraud's poems. The work established the standard five-player Pierrot ensemble and positioned Schoenberg as a central figure in German expressionism.

World War I triggered Schoenberg's "war psychosis," during which he wrote of an imminent reckoning against French "kitschmongers" like Ravel and Stravinsky, keeping a weather diary believing cloud formations could predict outcomes. Conscripted in 1915 despite asthma, he served loyally in the 4th Infantry Regiment "Hoch- und Deutschmeister" until discharge in October 1916. He later joked these were his happiest years, free from the burden of being "notorious Schoenberg."

### Twelve-Tone Revolution and Emigration (1918–1933)

Returning to Vienna in 1918, Schoenberg founded the Society for Private Musical Performances to present modernist works without hostile audiences or critics. The society gave 353 performances before collapsing amid Austrian hyperinflation. In 1920, antisemitic expulsion from Mattsee resort—where he was demanded to prove he was not Jewish—intensified his Jewish consciousness. By 1921, he wrote Alma Mahler of working on "something completely new," ironically noting that German Aryans would have this innovation to thank for maintaining German musical supremacy.

In 1923, Schoenberg announced twelve-tone technique as a governing principle: "a method of composing with twelve tones which are related only with one another." All twelve pitches of the octave were regarded as equal, with no note or tonality emphasized. Berg, Webern, and pupil Hanns Eisler adopted the method, while Roberto Gerhard began studying with him. His earlier works remained "natural forerunners" requiring assimilation before his new ones could be understood.

### American Exile and Final Period (1933–1951)

The Nazi seizure of power in 1933 forced Schoenberg's emigration. While in France, he was warned against returning to Germany and formally returned to Judaism at a Paris synagogue, viewing his heritage as ineluctable opposition to Nazism. He adopted the spelling "Schoenberg" in deference to American practice upon arriving in October 1933.

Settling in Los Angeles in 1936, Schoenberg taught at USC and UCLA, becoming a professor in 1936 with a $5,100 annual salary. His circle included Otto Klemperer, Edgard Varèse, George Gershwin, and students like John Cage and Lou Harrison. During this period, he composed major twelve-tone works including the Violin Concerto, Op. 36, the Piano Concerto, Op. 42, and the satirical *Ode to Napoleon* (1942), which quoted Beethoven's fate motif alongside "La Marseillaise" before concluding with an E-flat-major triad.

Simultaneously, he revisited tonality in works like the *Suite for Strings* (1935) and the completed *Chamber Symphony No. 2* (1939). As news of the Holocaust emerged, he memorialized its victims in *A Survivor from Warsaw* (1947). His death on Friday, July 13, 1951—fulfilling his triskaidekaphobia—was hastened by superstitious dread following an astrologer's warning that his 76th year was critical (7+6=13).

## Terms

**Developing variation**: A compositional technique, identified primarily in Brahms's music, where motivic elements are perpetually dissolved, varied, and recombined throughout a work.

**Emancipation of dissonance**: The gradual removal of traditional dissonance-consonance hierarchies, allowing dissonant intervals to function independently of tonal resolution.

**Sprechstimme**: A vocal technique that realizes pitch only fleetingly, hovering between speaking and singing, used prominently in *Pierrot lunaire*.

**Twelve-tone technique**: A compositional method where all twelve pitches of the octave are regarded as equal, organized through ordered tone rows with no tonal center.

**Hexachordal combinatoriality**: A twelve-tone principle where the hexachords (six-note segments) of a row combine to form aggregates or create specific intervallic relationships.

**Free atonality**: Schoenberg's period (1908–1923) of composing without traditional keys or tonal centers, distinct from but leading to twelve-tone composition.

**Dodecaphony**: Another term for twelve-tone composition, emphasizing the use of all twelve chromatic pitches.

**Zeitoper**: "Opera of the time"—a German theatrical form of the 1920s characterized by topical, often satirical subjects and modern musical idioms.

## Debates and Open Questions

**Periodization**: Traditional divisions of Schoenberg's output into three periods (Late Romanticism, atonality, twelve-tone) are arguably arbitrary, as stylistic characteristics transcend these boundaries and important musical traits—especially motivic development—persist throughout his career.

**Twelve-tone strictness**: Contrary to its reputation for doctrinaire rigidity, Schoenberg's twelve-tone technique varied significantly according to compositional demands. The structures of *Moses und Aron* differ fundamentally from those of the *Phantasy for Violin and Piano*, Op. 47.

**Public reception**: Despite reverence among experts and generations of students, Schoenberg's music remained unloved by the public nearly a century after his birth. British attempts to popularize his work are considered to have failed, raising questions about whether his expectation that "grocers' boys would whistle serial music" was ever realistic.

**Nazi reception**: Some composers like Paul von Klenau defended twelve-tone technique using Nazi-aligned rhetoric, presenting it as disciplined, anti-individualist order while avoiding mention of Schoenberg. This raises questions about the relationship between musical technique and political ideology.

**The "poietic fallacy"**: Critics like Richard Taruskin argue Schoenberg committed the fallacy of prioritizing the act of composition over listener experience, measuring his value primarily through influence on other artists rather than aesthetic merit.

**Historical necessity**: Schoenberg's anecdote about being asked if he was and answering "Nobody wanted to be, someone had to be, so I let it be me" suggests his sense of historical necessity, but whether this reflects genuine conviction or retrospective mythmaking remains debated.
