# Crusades

## Overview
The Crusades were a series of military campaigns launched by the papacy between 1095 and 1291 to recover and defend the Holy Land from Muslim rule, forming the core of a wider "crusading movement" that expanded to target pagans, Christian dissidents, and political enemies of the papacy into the 18th century. The movement arose from a confluence of factors: a strengthened papacy, the theology of penitential warfare offering spiritual rewards (indulgences) for military service, aristocratic piety, and the Byzantine Empire's appeal for aid against Seljuk Turkish expansion. The First Crusade (1096–1099) established four Crusader states in the Levant—the County of Edessa, the Principality of Antioch, the County of Tripoli, and the Kingdom of Jerusalem—whose survival depended on continuous reinforcement from Europe. Over two centuries, the Crusader states faced increasing Muslim unity under leaders like Zengi, Nur al-Din, Saladin, and the Mamluks, while European enthusiasm waned due to repeated failures, the diversion of crusades to Constantinople (1204) and Egypt, and the papacy's use of crusading indulgences for European political conflicts. The Mamluks captured the last mainland stronghold, Acre, in 1291, ending Frankish rule in the Levant, though the crusading framework persisted elsewhere.

## Body

### Origins and Papal Theory
The Crusades emerged from the 11th-century Gregorian Reform, which strengthened papal authority and promoted the "liberty of the Church." Theologians under Pope Gregory VII developed the concept of penitential warfare, equating death in a just war with martyrdom, drawing on Augustine’s earlier just war theory. This provided the ideological basis for *crucesignati* (those signed with the cross) to receive indulgences—remission of temporal punishment for sin—in exchange for military service. Simultaneously, the Byzantine Empire, having lost Anatolia to the Seljuk Turks after the Battle of Manzikert (1071), appealed to the West for mercenaries. Pope Urban II transformed this request into a general expedition to reclaim Jerusalem, leveraging the popularity of pilgrimage and aristocratic anxiety over salvation.

### The First Crusade and Establishment of the Levantine States (1096–1109)
The First Crusade unfolded in two waves. The uncontrolled "People's Crusade" (1096), led by Peter the Hermit, massacred Jewish communities in the Rhineland and was annihilated by the Turks in Anatolia. The subsequent "Princes' Crusade" (1096–1099), led by nobles including Godfrey of Bouillon, Bohemond of Taranto, and Raymond of Saint-Gilles, numbered 60,000–100,000. Exploiting Seljuk disunity following the deaths of Sultan Malik-Shah and Vizier Nizam al-Mulk (1092), the crusaders captured Nicaea (1097), Antioch (1098), and Jerusalem (15 July 1099), slaughtering the cities' Muslim and Jewish populations. Godfrey of Bouillon became the first Latin ruler of Jerusalem. The victory established four Crusader states (Outremer), whose defence committed Western Europe to sustained crusading. Italian maritime republics (Genoa, Pisa, Venice) provided crucial naval support in exchange for trade privileges, securing the coastal cities of Acre, Beirut, Sidon, and Tripoli by 1109.

### Early Defence and the Second Crusade (1100–1149)
The Crusade of 1101, launched by Paschal II to reinforce the new states, ended in disaster in Anatolia, shattering the myth of crusader invincibility. For decades, only small expeditions arrived, yet they were pivotal for consolidation. The fall of Edessa to Zengi (26 December 1144) triggered the Second Crusade (1147–1149), preached by Bernard of Clairvaux and led by Kings Louis VII of France and Conrad III of Germany. The crusade expanded the theatre of operations: the *Divina dispensatione* bull authorized a Northern Crusade against the pagan Wends, and crusaders aided the Reconquista at Lisbon and Tortosa. However, the main armies were decimated in Anatolia by the Seljuks, and the siege of Damascus (July 1148) collapsed within days due to Nur al-Din’s approaching relief force. The failure devastated European morale and left the Crusader states vulnerable.

### The Rise of Muslim Unity and the Third Crusade (1150–1192)
Nur al-Din unified Muslim Syria (1154–1157) and made the conquest of Jerusalem a jihad imperative. His Kurdish general Shirkuh, and later his nephew Saladin, seized control of Fatimid Egypt (1169), abolishing the caliphate (1171). Saladin reunited Egypt and Syria by 1186. After Raynald of Châtillon violated a truce, Saladin crushed the Kingdom of Jerusalem’s army at the Battle of Hattin (4 July 1187), capturing Jerusalem (2 October) and reducing Frankish holdings to Tyre, Antioch, and Tripoli. The Third Crusade (1189–1192), led by Emperor Frederick I, Philip II of France, and Richard I of England, was financed by the "Saladin tithe" (a 10% income tax). Frederick drowned en route (1190); Philip returned early. Richard captured Acre (1191), won at Arsuf, and secured the coast from Tyre to Jaffa via the Treaty of Jaffa (1192), guaranteeing Christian pilgrim access, but failed to retake Jerusalem.

### The Crusader States' Decline and the Crusades of the 13th Century (1193–1244)
The Fourth Crusade (1202–1204), diverted by Venetian commercial interests and Byzantine dynastic disputes, sacked Constantinople (12 April 1204), establishing the Latin Empire and fatally weakening Byzantium; only a fraction of crusaders reached the Holy Land. The Fifth Crusade (1217–1221), planned by Innocent III at the Fourth Lateran Council, targeted Damietta in Egypt. After capturing the city (1219), the crusaders rejected Sultan al-Kamil’s offer of Jerusalem for withdrawal, advanced on Cairo, and were trapped by Nile floods, surrendering Damietta for a truce. The Sixth Crusade (1228–1229), led by the excommunicated Emperor Frederick II, achieved the unprecedented return of Jerusalem, Bethlehem, and Nazareth via the Treaty of Jaffa (1229) through diplomacy with al-Kamil, though the Temple Mount remained Muslim. The Barons' Crusade (1239–1241), led by Theobald of Champagne and Richard of Cornwall, exploited Ayyubid fragmentation to restore the kingdom to its greatest extent since 1187. These gains were erased when Khwarazmian mercenaries, allied with Egypt, sacked Jerusalem (July 1244) and annihilated the Frankish army at La Forbie (October 1244).

### The Mamluk Conquest and the End of Mainland Outremer (1245–1291)
Louis IX of France launched the Seventh Crusade (1248–1254) against Egypt. After capturing Damietta, the crusaders were defeated at Al Mansurah; Louis was captured and ransomed (800,000 bezants), surrendering Damietta. He remained in Acre for four years fortifying the coast. In Egypt, the Mamluks (slave-soldier elite) seized power (1250), founding a sultanate dedicated to expelling the Franks. Sultan Baybars (r. 1260–1277) systematically dismantled the Crusader states: he captured Caesarea, Haifa, Arsuf, Safed, Jaffa, Beaufort, and Antioch (1268). The Eighth Crusade (1270), led by Louis IX against Tunis, ended with his death from plague and a negotiated withdrawal. Prince Edward of England’s subsequent small expedition (1271–1272) secured a truce. Despite the Second Council of Lyon (1274) planning a major *passagium generale*, European politics—specifically Charles of Anjou’s ambitions in Sicily and Byzantium, the Sicilian Vespers (1282), and the Aragonese Crusade—diverted resources. Sultan Qalawun captured the Hospitaller fortress Margat (1285), Latakia (1287), and Tripoli (1289). His son Khalil besieged and captured Acre (28 May 1291); the last mainland stronghold, Château Pèlerin, fell on 14 August 1291.

### The Crusading Movement After 1291
The loss of Acre caused dismay but not a massive new expedition; the Gascon War (1294) ended English plans. Crusading became primarily a political instrument: Boniface VIII proclaimed crusades against personal enemies (the Colonna, Frederick III of Sicily); the Templars were suppressed (1312) under French pressure; the Hundred Years' War halted French-led projects. The final Holy Land crusade, the Alexandrian Crusade (1365), saw Peter I of Cyprus sack Alexandria for a week. Meanwhile, the movement persisted in other theatres: the Iberian Reconquista concluded with Granada’s fall (1492); the Northern Crusades in the Baltic continued until the 1410s; and the papacy sponsored Holy Leagues against the Ottoman Empire into the 17th century.

## Terms
- ****Crusade Indulgence**** — Spiritual reward granted by the papacy remitting temporal punishment for sins, offered to those who took the cross or funded a substitute.
- ****Crucesignati**** — "Those signed with the cross"; participants who sewed a cloth cross on their garments, legally bound to fulfill their vow.
- ****Passagium Generale**** — A large-scale, general expedition summoned by the pope for the recovery of the Holy Land, involving kings and high nobility.
- ****Passagium Particulare**** — A smaller, particular expedition, often led by individual nobles or military orders, becoming the dominant form in the late 13th century.
- ****Military Orders**** — Monastic-knightly confraternities (e.g., Templars, Hospitallers, Teutonic Knights) vowing poverty, chastity, obedience, and defence of pilgrims/Holy Land.
- ****Outremer**** — (French: "overseas") The collective term for the four Crusader states established in the Levant: Edessa, Antioch, Tripoli, and Jerusalem.
- ****Mamluks**** — Slave-soldier corps (mostly Turkic/Circassian) who seized power in Egypt (1250), establishing a sultanate that conquered the remaining Crusader states.
- ****Ayyubids**** — Kurdish dynasty founded by Saladin, ruling Egypt and Syria (1171–1250), who unified Muslim forces against the Crusader states.
- ****Latin Empire**** — Crusader state established in Constantinople (1204–1261) after the Fourth Crusade sacked the Byzantine capital.
- ****Saladin Tithe**** — A 10% levy on income and movable goods imposed in England and France (1188) to fund the Third Crusade.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Definition and Scope:** Historians debate whether "the Crusades" should be limited to the numbered expeditions to the Holy Land (1095–1291) or include the broader "crusading movement" (Iberia, Baltic, Albigensian, political crusades) continuing past 1291. Thomas Asbridge argues the term imposes a "misleading aura of coherence" on disparate early expeditions.
*   **Motivation:** The relative weight of genuine piety (penitential pilgrimage, desire for salvation) versus material gain (land, plunder, trade privileges) and political ambition remains a central historiographical question.
*   **Byzantine Relations:** The extent to which Byzantine appeals (Alexios I) triggered the First Crusade versus papal ambition to assert supremacy over the Eastern Church is contested, as is the blame for the Fourth Crusade's diversion (Venetian manipulation vs. Byzantine duplicity vs. crusader opportunism).
*   **Frederick II’s Sixth Crusade:** Scholars dispute whether Frederick’s diplomatic recovery of Jerusalem (1229) was a masterstroke or a hollow victory, given his excommunication, the treaty’s short duration (10 years), and the exclusion of key strategic fortresses.
*   **Mamluk vs. Mongol Factor:** The degree to which the Mamluk conquest of Outremer was inevitable due to Muslim unity and Frankish demographic weakness, versus the contingent impact of the Mongol invasions (which disrupted the region but were halted at Ain Jalut, 1260), is debated.
*   **Legacy and Modern Appropriation:** The Crusades are invoked in modern political rhetoric—by Islamist groups as a paradigm for "centuries-long struggle" against the West, and by Western right-wing groups as a symbol of resistance to Islam—raising questions about historical anachronism and the construction of "crusader" memory.
