# Food preservation

## Overview
Food preservation comprises processes that extend food shelf life by inhibiting microbial growth, slowing fat oxidation, and preventing enzymatic or visual deterioration. These methods reduce food waste, lower production costs, enhance food security, and support environmental sustainability. Preservation techniques range from ancient practices like boiling, fermentation, and freezing to modern industrial methods such as irradiation, high-pressure processing, and modified atmosphere packaging. Each method affects food quality differently, with some traditional techniques offering lower energy use and carbon footprints, while others may generate harmful compounds.

## Timeline
- **c. 12,000 BC** — Drying, the earliest form of curing, practiced in Asia and Middle East
- **Pre-agriculture** — Boiling, burial, fermentation, and freezing used for preservation
- **1806** — Nicolas Appert develops canning; adopted by French Navy
- **1862** — Louis Pasteur invents pasteurization
- **1864** — Pasteur establishes link between microorganisms, spoilage, and illness
- **2005** — High-pressure processing used commercially for juices, meats, and guacamole
- **2015** — WHO classifies processed meat as carcinogenic to humans

## Body

### Traditional Techniques
Traditional preservation methods predate agriculture and include boiling, burial, canning, confit, cooling, curing, drying, fermentation, freezing, heating, jellying, jugging, kangina, lye treatment, pickling, and sugaring. These methods often combine multiple approaches—such as salting, smoking, and fermentation—to enhance effectiveness. Many rely on natural environmental factors like cool temperatures, low oxygen, or desiccation to inhibit spoilage.

### Canning and Pasteurization
Canning, invented by Nicolas Appert by 1806, involves cooking food, sealing it in sterilized containers, and boiling to eliminate bacteria. The scientific basis was clarified in 1864 by Louis Pasteur, who linked microorganisms to food spoilage and disease. Pasteurization, developed in 1862, heats liquids like milk to ~70°C for 15–30 seconds, then rapidly cools them to prevent bacterial regrowth. Low-acid foods require pressure canning to prevent *Clostridium botulinum* contamination, which produces a lethal, heat-stable toxin undetectable by taste or smell.

### Fermentation and Biopreservation
Fermentation uses controlled microbial activity to produce acids or alcohol, creating environments hostile to spoilage organisms. It enhances nutrition and palatability while preserving foods like cheeses, wines, beers, sauerkraut, kimchi, and pickles. Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are key biopreservatives, producing antimicrobial substances like lactic acid, nisin, and hydrogen peroxide. Their use is integral to hurdle technology, which combines multiple preservation stresses to ensure safety and quality.

### Modern Industrial Techniques
Modern methods include aseptic processing, vacuum packing, freeze drying, preservative additives, irradiation, pulsed electric field (PEF) electroporation, modified atmosphere packaging, nonthermal plasma, high-pressure processing (HP), and hurdle technology. These techniques offer precise control over microbial load and shelf life while minimizing quality loss. Irradiation, endorsed by WHO and FAO, kills pests and pathogens without heating. HP retains nutrients and flavor while achieving sterilization. Hurdle technology intelligently combines hurdles—temperature, pH, water activity, preservatives—to optimize safety and sensory properties.

## Terms
- **Osmosis**: Movement of water across a semipermeable membrane from low to high solute concentration; used in curing to draw moisture from food.
- **Water potential**: Measure of water availability in food; reduced water potential inhibits microbial growth.
- **Pyrolysis products**: Chemical compounds formed during burning or heating; smoke deposits antimicrobial phenols like syringol and guaiacol on cured meats.
- **Log reduction**: Quantitative measure of microbial kill; e.g., a 5-log reduction reduces pathogens by 99.999%.
- **Hurdle technology**: Combining multiple preservation methods to achieve synergistic microbial control.
- **Aseptic processing**: Sterilizing food and packaging separately, then combining under sterile conditions.
- **Nonthermal plasma**: Ionized gas used to sterilize food surfaces without heat.
- **Biopreservation**: Using beneficial microbes or their metabolites to extend shelf life.
- **Modified atmosphere**: Altering gas composition around food to slow respiration and spoilage.
- **Plasmolysis**: Shrinkage of microbial cells due to water loss in hypertonic environments; caused by sugaring or salting.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Carcinogenicity of processed meats**: WHO (2015) classifies processed meats as carcinogenic, raising concerns about curing agents like nitrites.
- **Consumer acceptance of irradiation**: Despite WHO/FAA endorsement, public perception remains negative due to misconceptions about radioactivity.
- **Environmental trade-offs**: Traditional methods often have lower carbon footprints than energy-intensive modern techniques, but scalability and consistency vary.
- **Safety vs. quality in canning**: Underprocessing risks *C. botulinum* contamination, while overprocessing degrades nutritional value and texture.
- **Regulatory variation in irradiation**: Global policies range from no regulation to full bans, reflecting differing risk assessments.
- **Efficacy of PEF and HP**: These emerging technologies show promise but face limitations in cost, throughput, and standardization.
