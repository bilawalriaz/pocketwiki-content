# Longitude

## Overview
Longitude is a geographic coordinate specifying the east-west position of a point on Earth, measured in degrees from the prime meridian. Its precise determination was a critical scientific and navigational challenge for centuries, as it is directly linked to time measurement due to Earth's rotation. Solving the "longitude problem" revolutionized global navigation, cartography, and trade.

## Timeline
- **2nd century BC** — Hipparchus develops a 360° coordinate system and proposes using lunar eclipses to find longitude.
- **2nd century AD** — Ptolemy creates a mapping system with a prime meridian through the Canary Islands.
- **c. 900 AD** — Al-Battānī determines longitude difference between Antakya and Raqqa with <1° error using lunar eclipses.
- **1494 & 1504** — Christopher Columbus attempts to use lunar eclipses for longitude, with large errors.
- **1657** — Christiaan Huygens patents the pendulum clock, greatly improving timekeeping accuracy.
- **1714** — British Parliament passes the Longitude Act, offering prizes for a practical method at sea.
- **1767** — First *Nautical Almanac* is published, enabling the lunar distances method.
- **1773** — John Harrison receives final payment for his chronometers after parliamentary intervention.
- **1839-1844** — First working telegraphs established, later used for longitude determination via time signals.
- **1907-1910** — Wireless time signals for ships transmitted from Halifax and the Eiffel Tower.
- **Early 1990s** — GPS replaces radio navigation systems as the primary method for determining position.

## Body

### Ancient and Medieval Concepts
The concept of longitude originated with ancient Greek astronomers. Hipparchus (2nd century BC) established the 360° system and proposed comparing local times of lunar eclipses to find longitude. Ptolemy (2nd century AD) improved mapping but used poor data, overestimating the Mediterranean's length. After the Roman Empire's fall, Hindu and Muslim astronomers continued the work; al-Battānī (c. 900 AD) achieved high accuracy using lunar eclipses.

### The Longitude Problem at Sea
The invention of the telescope (early 17th century) and pendulum clock (1657) revolutionized land-based longitude determination. However, at sea, the marine environment made pendulum clocks unreliable, and immediate results were needed. European powers offered prizes, most famously the British Longitude Act of 1714. Two solutions emerged: the **lunar distances** method, using tables from the *Nautical Almanac* (first published 1767), and the **marine chronometer**, perfected by John Harrison over three decades. Chronometers became cheaper and simpler to use than lunar calculations, replacing them by 1850.

### Technological Advancements
The telegraph (1839/1844) enabled precise longitude determination on land by transmitting time signals. The U.S. Coast Survey used this to map vast areas. At sea, wireless telegraphy (radio) in the early 20th century allowed ships to receive time signals and adjust chronometers. After World War II, radio navigation systems using fixed beacons became standard until replaced by GPS in the early 1990s.

### Determination Methods and Values
Longitude is defined as 0° at the prime meridian, ranging to ±180°. It is expressed in degrees, minutes, and seconds (sexagesimal) or decimal degrees. The core principle for most methods is comparing local time to a reference time (e.g., at Greenwich), with a 15° longitude difference equaling a one-hour time difference. Historical methods include lunar distances, Jupiter's satellites, and chronometer transport. The length of a degree of longitude varies with latitude, shrinking from about 111.3 km at the equator to zero at the poles, as circles of latitude get smaller.

## Terms
- **Prime Meridian**: The meridian (line of longitude) defined as 0°, from which other longitudes are measured. The International Reference Meridian passes near Greenwich, London.
- **Meridian**: An imaginary semicircular line running from pole to pole, connecting points with the same longitude.
- **Lunar Distances**: A method of finding longitude by measuring the angle between the Moon and a celestial body, then comparing the observed time to tabulated predictions for a reference meridian.
- **Chronometer**: A highly accurate portable clock, developed by John Harrison, used to keep the time of a reference meridian while at sea.
- **Sexagesimal Notation**: A system of angular measurement using base 60, dividing degrees into minutes and seconds (e.g., 23° 27′ 30″).
- **Geodetic Normal**: The direction perpendicular to the idealized ellipsoidal surface of the Earth, used as a reference for coordinates like longitude.
- **Vertical Deflection**: The small angle between the geodetic normal and the direction of gravity, which can cause astronomical longitude to differ slightly from geodetic longitude.
