# Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire

## Overview
The Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire (1519–1521) was a pivotal event in the history of the Americas, marking the collision of the Aztec Triple Alliance and the Spanish Empire. Led by Hernán Cortés, a small force of Spanish soldiers and a large army of Indigenous allies overthrew the most powerful empire in Mesoamerica. The conquest was due to a combination of factors, including superior European weaponry, strategic alliances with oppressed Indigenous populations, and the devastating impact of European diseases like smallpox. Its success established Spanish rule in Mesoamerica, leading to the creation of New Spain and a profound transformation of the region's social and cultural landscape.

## Timeline
- **1428** — Creation of the Aztec Triple Alliance of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan.
- **1502** — Moctezuma II elected *huey tlatoani* (emperor) of the Aztec Triple Alliance.
- **1519** — Hernán Cortés lands on the Gulf coast; allies with Tlaxcala and other city-states.
- **8 Nov 1519** — Cortés and his forces enter the Aztec capital, Tenochtitlan.
- **Mid-1520** — Pedro de Alvarado massacres Aztec elites during a festival, sparking a major uprising.
- **30 June 1520** — "La Noche Triste" – Spanish and allies suffer heavy losses fleeing Tenochtitlan.
- **Mid-Oct 1520** — Smallpox epidemic devastates Tenochtitlan, killing Emperor Cuitláhuac.
- **Late Dec 1520** — Spanish-Tlaxcaltec forces return to the Valley of Mexico.
- **Late Apr 1521** — Spanish launch 13 brigantines onto Lake Texcoco, gaining naval control.
- **10 May 1521** — Start of the siege of Tenochtitlan.
- **13 Aug 1521** — Surrender of Tenochtitlan; capture of Emperor Cuauhtémoc.
- **Oct 1522** — Charles V names Cortés captain-general of New Spain.

## Body

### The Aztec Empire and Spanish Arrival
The Aztec Empire, led by Moctezuma II, ruled hegemonically through local rulers, its power resting on the perception of unchallengeable military supremacy. This system was inherently unstable and vulnerable to challenges. In 1519, Hernán Cortés led an expedition to Mexico, defying the authority of his superior, Governor Diego Velázquez of Cuba. Cortés established the colony of Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz and secured crucial alliances with Indigenous city-states, particularly the Tlaxcalans and Tetzcocans, who were rivals or subjects of the Aztecs. A key asset was Doña Marina (La Malinche), a multilingual Nahua woman who served as a translator and cultural intermediary.

### The March to Tenochtitlan and Initial Confrontation
After defeating an expedition sent to arrest him, Cortés marched inland with his Spanish and Indigenous allies. He massacred the population of Cholula, a major religious center, which had a chilling effect on other cities. On 8 November 1519, Cortés entered Tenochtitlan, where he was welcomed by Moctezuma. The Spanish took Moctezuma captive, using him as a puppet ruler. This arrangement collapsed after Pedro de Alvarado massacred Aztec nobles during a religious festival, triggering a city-wide uprising. Moctezuma was killed in the ensuing conflict, though sources disagree on whether he was killed by the Spanish or his own people.

### The Siege and Fall of Tenochtitlan
The Spanish and their allies were forced to flee Tenochtitlan during the "Noche Triste" (Sorrowful Night), suffering heavy casualties. They regrouped in Tlaxcala. A smallpox epidemic, beginning in September 1520, devastated the Aztec population, killing their new emperor, Cuitláhuac. Cortés returned in 1521 with a larger force and constructed brigantines to control Lake Texcoco. The ensuing siege lasted eight months, cutting off food and water. Tenochtitlan fell on 13 August 1521 with the capture of the last emperor, Cuauhtémoc. The city was almost totally destroyed, and its ruins became the site of Mexico City.

### Aftermath and Spanish Rule
The conquest led to the establishment of New Spain. The Spanish crown authorized the *encomienda* system, which granted colonists the right to Indigenous labor and tribute, building on pre-existing patterns of service. The Aztec education system was abolished and replaced by limited church education, and Catholic missionaries worked to convert the population, suppressing many Indigenous cultural and religious practices. The majority of Spanish colonists were single men who intermarried with Indigenous women, leading to the emergence of a mixed-race *mestizo* population.

## Terms
- **Huey Tlatoani**: The "Great Speaker" or emperor of the Aztec Triple Alliance.
- **Altepetl**: The basic political unit in Mesoamerica, a city-state with its own ruler and territory.
- **Encomienda**: A Spanish colonial system granting a colonist the right to the labor and tribute of a specific Indigenous community.
- **Noche Triste**: "Sorrowful Night," the disastrous Spanish retreat from Tenochtitlan on 30 June 1520.
- **Brigantine**: A small, maneuverable sailing ship; 13 were built by the Spanish to control Lake Texcoco during the siege.
- **Mestizo**: A person of combined European and Indigenous American ancestry.
- **New Spain**: The name given by the Spanish to their colonial territory in central Mexico, established after the conquest.
- **Florentine Codex**: A 16th-century ethnographic research project in Mesoamerica by Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún, documenting Aztec culture and the conquest.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Moctezuma's Captivity**: Modern scholars are skeptical of Spanish claims that Cortés immediately took Moctezuma captive upon entering Tenochtitlan, suggesting it may have happened later.
- **The Role of Omens**: Indigenous accounts describe eight supernatural omens that foretold the conquest. Scholars debate whether these events actually occurred or were *ex post facto* (retrospective) creations to explain the defeat.
- **The Quetzalcoatl Myth**: The idea that Moctezuma believed Cortés was the returning god Quetzalcoatl is debated. Some ethnohistorians argue Aztec leaders viewed the Spanish rationally as powerful outsiders, not as supernatural beings. The concept appears mainly in later European writings.
- **Causation of Conquest**: Historians debate the relative importance of factors like Spanish technological superiority (weapons, cavalry), Indigenous alliances, and the catastrophic impact of smallpox in enabling the Spanish victory.
