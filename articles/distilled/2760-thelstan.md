# Æthelstan

## Overview
Æthelstan (c. 894–939) was the first Anglo-Saxon ruler to unite all of England under his rule, conquering the Viking Kingdom of York in 927. He is widely regarded by modern historians as the first King of England and one of the greatest Anglo-Saxon monarchs, known for his military victories, centralized government, and extensive European diplomacy.

## Timeline
- **c. 894** — Born to Edward the Elder and his first wife, Ecgwynn.
- **924** — Accepted as king by Mercians after Edward the Elder's death; faced resistance in Wessex.
- **925** — Crowned King of the Anglo-Saxons at Kingston upon Thames.
- **927** — Conquered York, becoming the first ruler of all England; received submissions at Eamont.
- **934** — Invaded Scotland, forcing Constantine II to submit.
- **937** — Defeated a combined Scottish, Viking, and Strathclyde Briton army at the Battle of Brunanburh.
- **939** — Died at Gloucester; succeeded by his half-brother Edmund I.

## Body

### Accession and Early Reign
When Edward the Elder died in 924, Æthelstan was accepted as king in Mercia but faced opposition in Wessex, possibly due to questions about his mother Ecgwynn's status. His half-brother Ælfweard, who may have been favored in Wessex, died weeks later. Æthelstan was not crowned until September 925. In 927, he seized the Viking Kingdom of York after the death of its king, Sihtric, and received the submission of other British rulers, establishing himself as the first king of all England.

### Military Campaigns and Overlordship
Æthelstan's rule over northern Britain was resented. In 934, he invaded Scotland, likely due to broken treaties or disputes over Northumbrian territories. The campaign forced Constantine II to acknowledge his overlordship. In 937, a grand alliance of Olaf Guthfrithson (Viking King of Dublin), Constantine II, and Owain of Strathclyde invaded England. Æthelstan's victory at the Battle of Brunanburh was celebrated as a monumental triumph, though its long-term strategic impact is debated.

### Centralized Government and Law
Æthelstan created the most centralized government England had yet seen. He controlled charter production through a single scribe ("Æthelstan A") and summoned large councils (witan) that included rulers from Wales and Scotland, fostering a sense of national assembly. His numerous law codes show a preoccupation with social order, especially combating theft, and introduced early forms of tithing (joint responsibility groups for peacekeeping).

### Church, Learning, and European Relations
Æthelstan was deeply pious, a collector of relics, and a patron of learning. His court attracted international scholars and was a center for the revival of Latin scholarship and the hermeneutic style. He laid groundwork for the later Benedictine monastic reform. He arranged marriages for his sisters to continental rulers, including Otto (future Holy Roman Emperor), creating powerful alliances. He also acted as a foster-father to exiled princes like Alan II of Brittany.

## Terms
- **Witan**: The king's council, composed of bishops, ealdormen, and magnates, which advised the king and witnessed charters.
- **Ealdorman**: A high-ranking royal official governing a large region, analogous to an earl.
- **Hermeneutic style**: A complex, ornate Latin writing style characterized by rare words and convoluted sentences, promoted at Æthelstan's court.
- **Tithing**: A system of sworn groups of ten or more men jointly responsible for maintaining peace and presenting thieves.
- **Subregulus**: A Latin title meaning "under-king," used for rulers who acknowledged a superior overlord.
- **Circumscription cross**: A type of coin issued by Æthelstan after 927, inscribed "Rex Totius Britanniae" to proclaim his kingship of all Britain.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Succession Dispute**: Historians debate whether Edward the Elder intended a divided kingdom or if Æthelstan's initial difficulty in Wessex stemmed from his mother's status.
- **Battle of Brunanburh's Significance**: Some historians view it as a "pyrrhic victory" with limited long-term gains, while others see it as crucial for securing Anglo-Saxon hegemony.
- **"First King of England"**: While a common modern title, some scholars argue Æthelstan's claimed authority was over the whole island of Britain, not a defined "England," and that his hegemony was fragile.
- **Law Code Effectiveness**: Historians disagree on whether Æthelstan's frequent and harsh legislation was a sign of effective governance or an admission of failure to control social disorder.
