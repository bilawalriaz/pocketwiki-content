# Mannerism

## Overview
Mannerism is a European artistic style that emerged in Italy around 1520 as a reaction to the harmonious ideals of the High Renaissance. It is characterized by artificiality, compositional tension, and exaggerated elegance, prioritizing intellectual virtuosity over naturalistic representation. The style spread internationally, influencing painting, sculpture, architecture, and literature until it was largely replaced by the Baroque in the early 17th century.

## Timeline
- **c. 1510–1520** — Mannerism emerges in Florence and/or Rome, reacting to the perfection of High Renaissance art.
- **1527** — The Sack of Rome causes early Mannerist artists to flee, spreading the style across Europe.
- **c. 1530** — The "High Maniera" phase begins, emphasizing intellectual conceits and artifice.
- **1530** — Rosso Fiorentino brings Florentine Mannerism to Fontainebleau, founding the School of Fontainebleau.
- **1539** — Agnolo Bronzino becomes court painter to the Medici, exemplifying the "cold" Maniera style.
- **1568** — Giorgio Vasari publishes the second edition of his *Lives*, using the term *maniera* to describe his contemporary style.
- **c. 1580** — Mannerism wanes in Italy as artists like the Carracci brothers and Caravaggio revive naturalism.
- **Early 17th c.** — Northern Mannerism continues, with artists like Joachim Wtewael working in the style until the end of their careers.

## Body

### Origins and Development
Mannerism arose from a perceived crisis among young artists after the High Renaissance, where technical mastery seemed complete. They sought new goals by studying the works of masters like Michelangelo, whose *terribilità* (awe-inspiring grandeur) became a primary model. This led to an "art imitating art" rather than nature. The style is often divided into an early "anti-classical" phase (c. 1520–1540) and a mature "High Maniera" (c. 1530–1580), which stressed virtuosity and intellectual complexity.

### Spread and International Styles
After the Sack of Rome in 1527, Italian artists disseminated Mannerism across Europe, creating the first international style since the Gothic. Key centers included the School of Fontainebleau in France, the court of Rudolf II in Prague, and cities like Haarlem and Antwerp. Northern Mannerism, influenced by Italian prints and traveling artists, developed distinct characteristics and continued into the early 17th century.

### Characteristics and Theory
Mannerist art is defined by elongated figures, distorted perspective, irrational spaces, and a preference for compositional tension over balance. Artists used flat black backgrounds, dramatic lighting, and vivid, sometimes incongruous colors. Theorists like Giorgio Vasari and Gian Paolo Lomazzo codified the style, emphasizing *invenzione* (invention), *maniera* (virtuosic technique), and the artist's intellectual refinement.

### Sculpture and Architecture
Mannerist sculpture, exemplified by Benvenuto Cellini and Giambologna, featured elongated forms, multiple viewpoints, and the *figura serpentinata* (serpentine figure). Architecture, pioneered by Michelangelo, rejected Renaissance harmony for visual trickery, unexpected elements, and the use of the giant order. The style emphasized experimentation with form and space.

### Key Artists and Works
- **Jacopo da Pontormo**: Known for elongated forms and emotionally charged religious scenes, like *Joseph in Egypt*.
- **Agnolo Bronzino**: His portraits, such as *Venus, Cupid, Folly and Time*, are noted for their porcelain-like surfaces, intellectual allegory, and aloof elegance.
- **El Greco**: His work, like *Laocoön*, combines elongated anatomy, acidic color, and irrational space to express intense religious emotion.
- **Giuseppe Arcimboldo**: Created fantastical composite portraits, like *Vertumnus*, using humor and symbolic assemblage.

## Terms
- **Maniera**: Italian for "style" or "manner," referring to an artist's personal style or the sophisticated, elegant style of the period.
- **Terribilità**: A term describing the awe-inspiring grandeur and emotional intensity in Michelangelo's work, which Mannerists sought to emulate.
- **Figura serpentinata**: A Mannerist compositional device featuring a figure in a twisted, serpentine pose, designed to be viewed from multiple angles.
- **Invenzione**: The intellectual concept or inventive idea behind an artwork, valued in Mannerism for its cleverness and complexity.
- **Anti-classical**: A term for early Mannerism, describing its reaction against the harmonious ideals of High Renaissance classicism.
- **High Maniera**: The mature phase of Mannerism (c. 1530–1580) characterized by extreme artifice, intellectual conceits, and elegant elongation.
- **School of Fontainebleau**: The French Mannerist style established by Italian artists like Rosso Fiorentino at the Château de Fontainebleau.
- **Sfumato**: A technique of rendering soft, hazy contours, used by some Mannerists to create atmospheric effects.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Definition and Periodization**: Historians debate whether Mannerism is a distinct style, movement, or period, and some label it the "Late Renaissance." Its phases and boundaries remain contested.
- **Origins**: The cause of the shift from High Renaissance to Mannerism is debated. Once seen as a reaction to social and religious upheaval (e.g., the Reformation, Sack of Rome), this explanation has fallen out of favor, though the stylistic contrast is clear.
- **Scope**: The term is applied analogously to literature, music, and even late Gothic Northern European painting (Antwerp Mannerism), leading to debates about its precise application across disciplines.
