# Mind

## Overview

The mind is the totality of mental phenomena, encompassing conscious processes (awareness of external and internal circumstances) and unconscious processes (which influence without intention or awareness). It includes perception, memory, thought, imagination, motivation, emotion, attention, learning, and consciousness. The mind's exact nature is disputed, and the mind–body problem—explaining the relation between matter and mind—is a central challenge. The dominant philosophical position since the 20th century has been physicalism, holding that minds are aspects of material objects. The mind's evolutionary history is tied to nervous system development, and individual minds develop through learning and aging. The main fields studying the mind include psychology, neuroscience, cognitive science, and philosophy of mind.

## Timeline

- **4–3.5 billion years ago** — Minimal forms of information processing appear in earliest life forms (e.g., bacteria sensing environment).
- **More than 600 million years ago** — Nerve cells emerge with multicellular organisms.
- **600–550 million years ago** — Evolutionary bifurcation into radially symmetric (nerve net) and bilaterally symmetric (centralized) nervous systems.
- **About 540 million years ago** — Vertebrates evolve within bilaterally organized organisms.
- **About 200 million years ago** — Neocortex develops in mammals, enabling higher-order brain functions.
- **About 65 million years ago** — Primates evolve; brain size relative to body increases.
- **7–5 million years ago** — First hominins emerge.
- **300,000–200,000 years ago** — Anatomically modern humans appear.
- **Possibly 50,000–40,000 years ago** — Behavioral modernity: new mental abilities like abstract thinking and symbols emerge.
- **End of 19th century** — Psychology emerges as a scientific discipline from Wilhelm Wundt's experimental work.
- **20th century** — Neuroscience recognized as distinct discipline; physicalism becomes dominant; cognitive science emerges in second half.
- **1980s** — Cognitive science expands following neuroimaging advances.

## Body

### Definition and Forms

The term "mind" is sometimes used narrowly for cognitive functions (perception, reasoning, awareness, memory) and broadly to include feeling, motivation, and behavior. The words "psyche" and "mentality" are synonyms; "soul," "spirit," "cognition," "intellect," and "brain" overlap but differ. The modern English word derives from Old English *gemynd*, meaning 'memory'. Mental phenomena include perception (interpreting sensory information), memory (episodic, semantic, procedural; short-term vs. long-term), thinking (logical reasoning, problem-solving, decision-making), imagination, motivation, attention, and learning.

An influential distinction is between conscious and unconscious processes. Phenomenal consciousness involves direct qualitative experience; access consciousness involves information accessible to other mental processes. Unconscious processes operate without awareness; psychoanalysis (per Sigmund Freud) holds that repression keeps disturbing phenomena from consciousness. Mental states are also divided into qualitative states (qualia, like colors and pains) and propositional states (attitudes toward content, like belief or hope). States can be rational, irrational, or arational; dispositional (unexercised power) or occurrent (actively considered). Traditionally, the mind was divided into faculties (e.g., intellect and will); recent approaches use mental modules—inborn, automatic systems for specific domains (e.g., visual perception, language processing).

### Theories of the Nature of Mind

Theories seek the "mark of the mental"—criteria distinguishing mental from non-mental phenomena. Epistemic criteria say mental states are known directly and privately; some claim such knowledge is infallible. Another view asserts all mental states are conscious or accessible to consciousness, denying "deep unconsciousness." Intentionality theory holds that mental states refer to or represent something; only mental states have original intentionality, while words have derivative intentionality. Behaviorism defines mental states as dispositions to observable behavior. Functionalism characterizes mental states by their functional role (e.g., pain's role involves injury, moaning, desire to stop). Computationalism defines minds as information processors. Externalism emphasizes environmental dependency; the extended mind thesis states external things (like a diary) can be part of the mind. Enactivism and embodied cognition stress organism–environment interaction and bodily connection.

### Relation to Matter

The mind–body problem came to prominence with Descartes' metaphysical distinction; earlier philosophers did not see mind and body as contrasting. Substance dualism holds minds exist independently; property dualism sees different properties of the same individual. Monist views include idealism (everything is mental), neutral monism (fundamental reality is neither), and physicalism (everything is physical). Eliminative physicalism denies mental phenomena; reductive physicalism (including behaviorism and type identity theory) says mental states can be described by physics; non-reductive physicalism (functionalism) sees mental concepts as abstract functional roles. The hard problem of consciousness asks how physical states give rise to conscious experience.

The human brain has about 86 billion neurons communicating via synapses. The forebrain hosts higher mental functions: prefrontal cortex (executive functions), sensory cortex (sensory information), hippocampus (long-term memories), amygdala (emotions), motor cortex (voluntary movements), Broca's area (speech production). Neurotransmitters like dopamine (motivation) and serotonin (mood) influence mental states. Physical brain changes affect the mind: psychoactive drugs temporarily alter brain chemistry; neurodegenerative diseases and injuries cause permanent changes. Phineas Gage's prefrontal cortex damage changed his personality, though he partially recovered.

### Development

The mind evolved through natural selection. Minimal information processing existed 4–3.5 billion years ago; nerve cells emerged over 600 million years ago. Vertebrates evolved about 540 million years ago; the neocortex developed about 200 million years ago. Primates appeared about 65 million years ago, hominins 7–5 million years ago, modern humans 300,000–200,000 years ago. Theories of human intelligence include the social intelligence hypothesis (social life drove mental evolution) and ecological intelligence hypothesis (complex physical environment). Behavioral modernity possibly occurred 50,000–40,000 years ago.

Individual minds develop through learning and universal psychological stages. Jean Piaget's theory divides cognitive development into four stages: sensorimotor (birth–2 years), preoperational (until 7), concrete operational (until 11), and formal operational (abstract ideas). Adolescence involves identity crises and developing independence. Adulthood sees improved reasoning; later adulthood brings decline in learning complex tasks and memory.

### Non-Human Minds

It is controversial which animals have minds. Discontinuity views see animal minds as fundamentally different (lacking abstract rationality); continuity views emphasize similarities in degree. Key questions concern consciousness and sentience (capacity for suffering). Artificial minds: functionalism suggests computers could implement mental functions. The Turing test (Alan Turing, 1912–1954) tests whether a computer can pass as human in conversation. Weak AI is limited to specific tasks; strong AI (artificial general intelligence) would have all human mental capacities. Arguments against strong AI include John Searle's Chinese Room Argument and Hubert Dreyfus's Heideggerian critique.

### Mental Health and Disorder

Mental health involves internal equilibrium and well-being; negative definitions see it as absence of mental disorders. Disorders are abnormal patterns causing distress; norms are culturally controversial (e.g., homosexuality was historically considered a disorder). Types include anxiety disorders (e.g., social phobia, obsessive–compulsive disorder), mood disorders (e.g., bipolar disorder), personality disorders (e.g., paranoid personality disorder), psychotic disorders (e.g., schizophrenia), dissociative and eating disorders. The biopsychosocial model identifies biological, cognitive, and environmental causes. Treatments include psychotherapeutic methods (psychoanalysis, cognitive behavioral therapy, behavior therapy, humanistic therapies) and drug therapies (antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, anxiolytics).

### Fields of Inquiry

Psychology (emerged late 19th century from Wundt) studies mind and behavior; subfields include cognitive, biological, developmental, social, personality psychology. Methods include experiments (manipulating independent variables), correlational studies, surveys, case studies, longitudinal studies. Neuroscience studies the nervous system using neuroimaging: fMRI (blood flow), PET (metabolic changes), EEG (electrical activity), plus brain damage studies. Philosophy of mind uses conceptual analysis, thought experiments (Mary the color scientist, philosophical zombies, brain in a vat), and phenomenology; influential figures include Plato, Aristotle, Descartes, Hume, Kant, James, Ryle. Cognitive science (emerged mid-20th century) integrates psychology, neuroscience, philosophy, linguistics, AI; it conceptualizes minds as information processors and analyzes processes at abstract, algorithmic, and implementation levels.

### Relation to Other Fields

Epistemology addresses the problem of other minds (how we know others have minds). Anthropology studies cultural conceptualizations of mind; some cultures (e.g., Azande) allow thoughts to directly affect the world. Religion: Buddhists deny an enduring self; Hindus affirm a permanent self (chariot analogy); Christian philosophy intertwines mind and soul; Islamic thought distinguishes mind, spirit, heart, self; Daoism and Confucianism use "heart-mind." Education shapes minds through knowledge transmission. Pseudoscience: phrenology (correlating mental traits with skull bumps) failed scrutiny, but brain localization is accepted; parapsychology studies clairvoyance, telepathy, telekinesis.

## Terms

- **Mind–body problem**: The challenge of explaining the relation between matter and mind.
- **Physicalism**: The view that everything is physical; minds are aspects of material objects.
- **Qualia**: Qualitative sensory experiences like colors, sounds, pains.
- **Intentionality**: The property of mental states referring to or representing something.
- **Functionalism**: Mental states characterized by their functional role, not internal constitution.
- **Hard problem of consciousness**: Explaining how physical states give rise to conscious experience.
- **Neocortex**: Brain region responsible for higher-order functions, developed in mammals.
- **Turing test**: Procedure testing whether a computer can pass as human in conversation.
- **Strong AI**: Artificial general intelligence with all human mental capacities.
- **Biopsychosocial model**: Framework identifying biological, cognitive, and environmental causes of mental disorders.

## Debates and open questions

The exact nature of the mind is disputed, including whether the mark of the mental is epistemic access, consciousness, or intentionality. There is no consensus on which non-human animals have minds or whether artificial minds are possible (strong AI is controversial). The hard problem of consciousness—how physical states give rise to subjective experience—remains unsolved. The boundary of the mind is debated: externalism and the extended mind thesis claim the environment is part of mind, while others see it as internal. Whether all mental states are conscious or accessible to consciousness is contested, as is the existence of "deep unconsciousness." The content of norms for mental health is culturally controversial.
