# Transport

## Overview

Transport (British English) or transportation (American English) is the intentional movement of humans, animals, and goods from one location to another. It divides into infrastructure (fixed installations), vehicles (means of carrying), and operations (how services run). Transport enables trade, essential for civilization's development, but machine-propelled forms cause air pollution and use large amounts of land.

## Timeline

- **c. 8000 BCE** — Oxen used for transport
- **4th–3rd millennium BCE** — Horses domesticated
- **19th century** — Steam engine applied to rail and ships; optical telegraph makes communication independent of physical transport
- **c. 1900** — Combustion engine and automobile make road transport competitive again
- **1903** — Wright brothers demonstrate first successful controllable airplane
- **1914–1918** — After World War I, aircraft become a fast way to transport people and express goods over long distances
- **1950s** — Containerization introduced, fostering globalization; scientific spaceflight begins
- **1960s** — Commercialization of the jet engine makes international air travel accessible
- **1964** — Shinkansen high-speed rail introduced in Japan
- **1970s** — Spaceflight interest dwindles
- **2010** — Movement to privatize roads and other infrastructure gains ground in the U.S.

## Body

### Modes

A mode of transport is a method of travelling or transporting people or cargo, possibly involving one mode or several (inter-modal or multi-modal). Each mode has its own infrastructure, vehicles, operators, and operations, chosen based on cost, capability, and route. Governments regulate vehicle operation; infrastructure ownership can be public, private, or a partnership. Passenger transport may be public (scheduled services) or private; freight transport has focused on containerization, though bulk transport handles large volumes of durable items.

**Human-powered** transport uses muscle-power (walking, running, swimming), popular for cost-saving, leisure, exercise, and environmentalism, sometimes the only option in underdeveloped regions. **Animal-powered** transport uses working animals, ridden, as pack animals, or harnessed to pull sleds or wheeled vehicles; they remain useful in rough terrain.

**Air** transport includes fixed-wing aircraft (airplanes), generating lift from air movement over wings, and rotary-wing aircraft (helicopters). Fixed-wing aircraft range from small trainers to large airliners, most requiring airports with maintenance, refueling, and loading infrastructure. Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs or drones) range from under a metre to full-sized airplanes, used for package delivery. The aircraft is the second fastest transport method after the rocket; commercial jets reach up to 955 km/h (593 mph). Aviation has significant climate impacts: the IPCC estimates a commercial jet's flight has 2–4 times the climate effect of the same CO2 emissions at ground level. Aerostats (lighter-than-air craft like balloons and blimps) use helium or heated air for lift.

**Land** transport covers rail and road. **Rail** uses wheeled vehicles on tracks (two parallel steel rails anchored to ties on a ballast foundation). Trains consist of connected vehicles (rolling stock), usually hauled by a locomotive powered by steam, diesel, gas turbine, or electricity. Railed vehicles move with less friction than rubber tires, making trains energy efficient. High-speed rail reaches up to 350 km/h (220 mph); commercial maglev in Shanghai runs at 460 km/h (290 mph). Since the 1980s, container trains have dominated general freight. **Road** transport's most common vehicle is the automobile; other users include buses, trucks, motorcycles, bicycles, and pedestrians. As of 2015, there were 950 million passenger cars worldwide, projected to reach 2.5 billion by 2050. Road transport offers unique flexibility (changes in location, direction, speed, timing) enabling door-to-door service, but consumes large amounts of space, causes congestion, and is a main source of urban noise and air pollution.

**Water** transport uses watercraft (barges, boats, ships) over bodies of water. The 19th century saw the first steamships; most commercial ships now use internal combustion engines burning bunker fuel, while some (like submarines) use nuclear propulsion. Though slow, modern sea transport is highly efficient: nearly 35,000 commercial vessels carried 7.4 billion tons of cargo in 2007.

**Other modes** include micromobility (small electric vehicles), pipeline transport (for liquids, vapors, gases, and slurries), cable transport (aerial tramways, funiculars, elevators, ski lifts), drone transport (used for medicine in least developed countries), and spaceflight. Suborbital spaceflight could potentially do global point-to-point delivery in less than 90 minutes.

### Elements

**Infrastructure** consists of fixed installations allowing vehicles to operate: transport networks, terminals, and parking/maintenance facilities. Rail, pipeline, road, and cable transport require the entire way to be constructed; air and watercraft only need fixed infrastructure at terminals. Terminals (airports, ports, stations) are where passengers and freight transfer between modes. Financing can be public or private; transport is often a natural monopoly funded through taxation, with usage fees (landing fees, tolls) imposed by owners. Poor forecasting frequently causes a benefits shortfall for infrastructure projects.

**Vehicles** are non-living devices that move people and goods, providing their own propulsion (steam engine, combustion engine, electric motor, jet engine, rocket) unless pulled by cable or muscle-power. They need a system to convert energy into movement (wheels, propellers, air pressure). Vehicles are commonly staffed by a driver, though some systems are fully automated. Since 2016, connected and autonomous vehicles have emerged as "future mobility," but safety and cybersecurity concerns remain.

**Operation** of private transport is by the owner; public and freight transport are done through private enterprise, governments, or partnerships. Since the 1980s, many national airlines and railways have been privatized. International shipping remains highly competitive with little regulation, but ports can be public-owned.

**Policy** must evolve as cities grow—55% of the world's population lives in cities, expected to rise to 68% by 2050. Policy enforces order in transport, which is "by nature chaotic," helping reduce accidents and save lives.

### Functions

**Passenger transport** is divided into public (scheduled services on fixed routes) and private (chartered or ad hoc). Short-haul transport is dominated by automobiles and mass transit; long-haul uses automobiles, trains, ships, coaches, and aircraft. All passenger transport can be considered intermodal since it starts and ends with walking. Buses are the cheapest mode; taxis are very flexible but more expensive; demand-responsive transport sits in the middle.

**Medical transport** includes ambulances (road-going emergency vehicles providing out-of-hospital care) and air medical services (helicopters, propeller, or jet aircraft for aeromedical evacuation).

**Freight transport** is key to manufacturing value chains. Containerization (standardized ISO containers) has revolutionized trade since the 1950s by enabling automated handling and economies of scale. Bulk transport handles cargo that can be treated roughly (ore, coal, cereals, petroleum). Air freight carries less than one percent of world transport by volume but forty percent of its value, used for high-value-to-weight items like electronics and fashion clothing.

### Impact

Transport is a component of the tertiary sector in the three-sector model of economics. Road traffic accidents kill or injure nearly 1.35 million people every year worldwide. Transport is the largest drainer of energy, making sustainability a major issue. In lean thinking, transporting materials is one of the seven wastes (muda) that do not add value.

**Planning** uses forecasting models to predict future transport patterns. Transport engineering considers trip generation, trip distribution, mode choice, and route assignment. Automotive transport can be seen as a tragedy of the commons. Transport facilities consume land; pavement in cities can exceed 20 percent of total land use. Traditional mobility solutions are increasingly questioned by new types of analysis spanning environmental impact, public health, sociology, and economics.

**Safety** is important because transport accidents involve significant energy levels. Significant accidents are reviewed by law enforcement and independent investigators (e.g., NTSB in the U.S.). Statistics are analyzed to determine safety measures.

**Environment**: Transport burns most of the world's petroleum, creating air pollution and contributing to global warming; it is the fastest-growing emission sector. According to the IEA, transport accounts for more than one-third of CO2 emissions globally in the early 2020s. In 2022, global CO2 emissions from transport grew by more than 250 Mt to nearly 8 Gt, over 3% compared to 2021. Road transport is the largest contributor. Environmental regulations have reduced individual vehicle emissions, but this is offset by more vehicles and more use. City buses produce about 0.3 kg CO2 per passenger mile; commuter trains about 0.17 kg; long-distance trains about 0.19 kg. Airplane emissions trap more heat at high altitude due to a mix of greenhouse gases. Cities are prioritizing public transport, bicycles, and pedestrian movement, including 20-minute neighbourhoods and congestion charges.

**Sustainable development**: The UN recognized transport's role in sustainable development at the 1992 Earth summit. The transport sector contributes to a quarter of global greenhouse gas emissions. Sustainable transport is mainstreamed across several 2030 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 3, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12, 14). Meeting sustainable transport targets is particularly important to the Paris Agreement. Road network development has contributed to environmental degradation and, in some cases, loss of cultural traditions and marginalisation of indigenous peoples; air links have had an even more devastating impact.

### History

Humans' first ways to move were walking, running, and swimming. Animal domestication allowed hauling heavier loads and riding for greater speed. The wheel and sled made animal transport more efficient. Water transport was the only efficient way to move large quantities before the Industrial Revolution; the first watercraft were canoes cut from tree trunks or made of animal hides. Most trading cities grew up on rivers or sea-shores.

Until the Industrial Revolution, transport remained slow and costly. The 19th century brought the optical telegraph, the steam engine applied to rail, and the steamship. Around 1900, the combustion engine and automobile made road transport competitive again. The first "modern" highways were built in the 19th century with macadam; later tarmac and concrete dominated. After World War II, automobiles and airlines took higher shares of transport, reducing rail and water to freight and short-haul passenger services. In the U.S., most transport infrastructure was privately owned by joint-stock corporations in the 19th century, came under government control in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, culminating in Amtrak's creation, but a privatization movement gained ground by 2010.

## Terms

- **Inter-modal/multi-modal transport**: Transport involving more than one mode.
- **Containerization**: Standardization of ISO containers to enable automated handling and transfer between modes.
- **Bulk transport**: Shipping large volumes of durable, roughly-handled cargo (ore, coal, cereals, petroleum).
- **Aerostat**: A lighter-than-air aircraft (balloon or airship) using a low-density gas for lift.
- **Rolling stock**: Connected vehicles that operate on rails.
- **Maglev**: Magnetic levitation rail transport (commercial version in Shanghai runs at 460 km/h).
- **UAV (unmanned aerial vehicle)**: Autonomous or remotely-piloted airplane (drone).
- **Muda**: Japanese term for waste in lean thinking; transporting materials is one of seven wastes.
- **Tragedy of the commons**: Situation where individual flexibility/comfort deteriorates the natural and urban environment for all.
- **Natural monopoly**: A market where a single provider is most efficient; transport infrastructure is often one.

## Debates and open questions

- **Aviation's climate impact**: The IPCC estimates a commercial jet's flight has 2–4 times the climate effect of the same CO2 emissions at ground level due to different atmospheric chemistry and radiative forcing at higher altitudes. Hypermobility (frequent long-distance air travel) is a topic of research and governmental concern.
- **Infrastructure benefits shortfall**: Poor forecasting and overestimation of passenger numbers by planners frequently lead to a benefits shortfall for transport infrastructure projects.
- **Road development's mixed results**: Road network development has not always fulfilled its original intentions; it has contributed to environmental degradation and, in some cases, loss of cultural traditions and marginalisation of indigenous peoples. Air links have had an even more devastating impact.
- **Connected and autonomous vehicles**: Since 2016, these innovations are said to form "future mobility," but concerns remain on safety and cybersecurity.
- **Privatization**: A movement to privatize roads and other infrastructure has gained ground and adherents, reversing a century of government control.
