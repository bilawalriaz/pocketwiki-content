# Milk

## Overview
Milk is usually a white liquid food (but can be shades of yellow, cream, pink, or brown) produced by the mammary glands of lactating mammals, serving as the primary nutrition for infants before they can digest solid food. It contains proteins (casein and whey), fats, lactose, vitamins, minerals, and immune factors like antibodies in early milk (colostrum). Humans uniquely consume milk from other species—primarily cattle, but also buffalo, goats, sheep, and camels—into adulthood. This practice spread globally after the Neolithic domestication of dairy animals, though adult digestion requires lactase persistence, a genetic mutation that arose in Europe millennia ago. Industrialization in the 19th century centralized production, introducing pasteurization and homogenization to ensure safety and shelf life. Today, India is the world's largest producer, and over six billion people consume dairy, though lactose intolerance and allergies affect significant minorities.

## Timeline
- **9000–7000 BC** — Domestication of cattle, sheep, and goats in Southwest Asia; earliest dairying evidenced by lipid residues in pottery (7th millennium BC).
- **4th millennium BC** — "Secondary products revolution" (dairying, wool, traction) proposed by Andrew Sherratt; camels domesticated in central Arabia.
- **7000–5500 BC** — Dairy animals spread to South Asia; African cattle possibly independently domesticated 7000–6000 BC.
- **7000 BC onward** — Spread to Europe; reaches Britain and Scandinavia after 4000 BC.
- **1624** — Cattle imported to New England.
- **1840s–1850s** — Railway transport of milk from rural areas to London begins (St Thomas's Hospital contracts rail shipment 1846).
- **1863** — Louis Pasteur invents pasteurization; experimentally found heating young wine to 50–60 °C briefly kills microbes without sacrificing quality; originally used to prevent wine/beer souring.
- **1870s–1880s** — Glass milk bottles introduced (New York Dairy Co. 1877; Express Dairy Co. 1880; Thatcher's jar 1884).
- **1880s** — Commercial pasteurizing equipment produced in Germany; adopted in Copenhagen and Stockholm by 1885.
- **1892** — Auguste Gaulin invents homogenization in France; exhibited at 1900 Paris World's Fair.
- **1932** — Plastic-coated paper milk cartons introduced commercially.
- **1993** — FDA approves recombinant bovine somatotropin (rbST/rBGH) for increasing milk yield in US cows.
- **2011** — Global production ~730 million tonnes from 260 million dairy cows; India largest producer.

## Body

### Biological Role and Composition
Milk is an emulsion of butterfat globules in a water-based fluid carrying dissolved carbohydrates, proteins, and minerals. Its pH ranges 6.7–6.9. Lipids (33 g/L in full-fat milk) are secreted as membrane-bound globules (0.2–15 µm) containing triacylglycerols and fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K). Proteins (30–35 g/L in bovine milk) are 80% casein, organized into spherical micelles (~0.1 µm) stabilized by κ-casein on the surface; the remainder are whey proteins (e.g., lactoglobulin). Lactose (glucose + galactose) averages 4.8% in bovine milk, providing ~40% of calories. Minerals (calcium, phosphate, magnesium, etc.) interact strongly with casein; calcium phosphate nanoclusters are central to micelle structure, though models differ on whether they form a distinct submicellar lattice (older view) or nucleate protein assembly (de Kruif/Holt, Horne models). Raw milk also contains living cells, bacteria, vitamin C, and active enzymes.

### Infant Nutrition vs. Human Food Product
All infant mammals consume milk, usually via breastfeeding. Colostrum, the first secretion, is rich in antibodies and growth factors. WHO recommends exclusive human breastfeeding for six months, continuing to two years or more. Substituting goat's milk risks electrolyte imbalance, metabolic acidosis, and anemia. Adult human consumption of other mammals' milk is culturally widespread, especially in the West. Originally limited to children (adults lacked lactase), early societies fermented milk into cheese/yogurt to reduce lactose. A mutation for lactase persistence spread in northwestern Europe thousands of years ago, enabling milk as a fallback nutrition source. Today, humans milk cattle, sheep, goats, yaks, buffalo, horses, reindeer, and camels.

### History of Dairying
Domestication began independently in multiple regions (Mesopotamia 9000–7000 BC; Americas 3500–3000 BC). Cattle, sheep, goats were first domesticated in Southwest Asia. Lipid residue analysis shows dairying in early agriculture (7th millennium BC), challenging Sherratt's later "secondary products revolution" (4th millennium BC). Dairy animals spread to Europe (7000 BC onward) and South Asia (7000–5500 BC). Pastoral economies developed on the Pontic–Caspian steppe (4th millennium BC). In East/Southeast Asia, the Americas, and Australia, dairy was historically absent due to lack of domesticated dairy species or hunter-gatherer lifestyles; consumption rose only with European colonialism in the last 500 years. In medieval Europe, milk was called "virtuous white liquor" (safer than water) and "white blood" (thought to be diverted menstrual blood), affecting religious dietary rules.

### Industrialization and Processing
Urban growth and railways (mid-19th century) revolutionized supply. By 1900, Great Western Railway moved 25 million imperial gallons annually to London. Demand doubled/tripled in late 19th century. Adulteration banned 1875. Glass bottles (1870s), pasteurization (commercially adopted 1880s), and homogenization (invented 1892, spread over 30 years to Canada, US, Norway; universal in US by mid-20th century) standardized safety and texture. Pasteurization (HTST: 72 °C/15 sec) kills pathogens but reduces vitamin C (~20%), thiamin/B12 (~10%), soluble calcium/phosphorus (~5%). Microfiltration/ultrafiltration extend shelf life and alter composition (e.g., higher protein, lower lactose). UHT (138 °C/2–4 sec) yields six-month shelf life unrefrigerated but imparts a "cooked" taste and loses vitamin B1/C. Homogenization breaks fat globules to ~0.4 µm, preventing creaming; casein coats new surfaces, whitening milk and improving stability but requiring prior pasteurization to inactivate lipases.

### Global Production and Sources
2011: 85% cow milk; buffalo 11%, goat 2%, sheep 1.4%, camel 0.2%. India leads production and consumption (cattle and buffalo). EU produced 153.8 million tonnes (2013). Israel has highest yield (12,546 kg/cow/year) due to barn-fed energy-rich diets; New Zealand lowest (3,974 kg) from year-round grazing. US yield 9,954 kg (2010); India 1,154 kg; China 2,282 kg. Heat stress from climate change threatens yields: Swedish data show 0.2–0.54 kg daily loss at 20–30 °C; Chinese yields may drop 50% by 2070. Price volatility: 2007 spike from biofuel competition; 2010 US farm price $1.35/gal (below break-even for many).

### Nutrition and Health
Composition varies by species: human milk 1.1% protein, 4.2% fat, 7% lactose (72 kcal/100g); cow milk 3.4% protein, 3.6% fat, 4.6% lactose (66 kcal/100g). Seal/whale milk >50% fat. Cow milk DIAAS 1.43 (excellent protein quality); glycemic index 39 (low). One cup 2% milk provides 285 mg calcium (22–29% adult DRI). Homogenization/pasteurization improve digestibility by forming softer curds. No reliable evidence milk increases overall cancer risk; protective against bowel cancer. Cow's milk allergy affects 2–3% of infants (mostly outgrown; 0.4% persist). Lactose intolerance (lactase deficiency) causes GI symptoms; tolerance varies, often ≥1 cup if consumed with meals. Lactose-free milk made via enzymatic cleavage (lactase) or ultrafiltration tastes sweeter (glucose + galactose). Fermented products (yogurt, kefir) and acidophilus milk aid digestion. The US CDC recommends that children over the age of 12 months (the minimum age to stop giving breast milk or formula) should have two servings of milk products a day.

### Bovine Growth Hormone (rbST/rBGH)
FDA-approved 1993; recombinant BGH from E. coli increases yield and raises IGF-1. FDA, NIH, WHO deem milk from treated cows safe and indistinguishable. Labels claiming "no rBST" must state no significant difference shown. Cows on rBGH have higher mastitis rates, leading to bans in Canada, Australia, NZ, Japan, and EU (animal welfare).

### Varieties, Packaging, and Distribution
Varieties defined by fat content (whole, 2%, 1%, skim, half-and-half), lactose reduction, fortification (vitamin D standard; vitamin A in reduced-fat), flavorings, farming method (organic, grass-fed), and processing (raw, pasteurized, UHT). UHT milk dominates in parts of Europe/Latin America; sold unrefrigerated. Packaging shifted from glass to HDPE jugs, cartons, bags (Canada, India, South Africa), and aseptic cartons. UV-blocking materials prevent flavor degradation. Distribution: daily home delivery (milkmen) declined with refrigeration, supermarkets, and cars (UK: 2.5B L delivered 1996 → 637M L 2006). India uses milkmen (bicycles) and collection centers; 2011 survey found 70% samples non-compliant, 8% contained detergents.

### Spoilage, Fermentation, and Derived Products
Raw milk sours via lactic acid bacteria fermenting lactose → lactic acid, lowering pH, denaturing proteins. Controlled fermentation yields yogurt, cheese, kefir, sour cream, buttermilk, kumis. Pasteurization kills these bacteria, so spoilage later produces off-flavors and food-poisoning risk. Refrigeration (1–4 °C) slows spoilage. Powdered milk (<5% moisture) is most durable; freezing causes fat aggregation. Milk used in baking, cereals, coffee, milkshakes, espresso drinks.

### Cultural and Non-Culinary Aspects
Mythology: Greek Milky Way from Hera's milk. African/Asian butter traditionally from fermented milk. Religious: Bible "Land of Milk and Honey"; Qur'an 16:66 praises milk; Ramadan broken with milk/dates; Jewish Chalav Yisrael rules; Hindu/Jain Abhisheka libations. Language: "milksop" (effeminate man, 14th c.), "milquetoast" (timid person, from comic strip Caspar Milquetoast), "to milk" (exploit). 19th c. slang: cheap methanol drink, defraud, intercept telegrams. Non-culinary: diluted milk as fungicide (powdery mildew on grapes); milk paint (casein + lime/borax); hair/skin treatments (lactic acid exfoliation, keratin proteins); milk baths with additives.

### Interspecies Consumption
Not unique to humans: seagulls, sheathbills, skuas, western gulls, and feral cats observed stealing milk from elephant seal teats.

## Terms
- **Colostrum** — First milk secretion after birth, rich in antibodies, immune factors, and growth factors.
- **Lactase** — Enzyme that hydrolyzes lactose into glucose and galactose; declines after weaning unless lactase persistence mutation present.
- **Lactase persistence** — Genetic trait allowing continued lactase production into adulthood; arose in Europe thousands of years ago.
- **Casein micelles** — Spherical aggregates (~0.1 µm) of casein proteins stabilized by κ-casein on surface; carry calcium phosphate nanoclusters.
- **Whey proteins** — Water-soluble proteins (e.g., lactoglobulin) remaining in liquid after casein coagulation; do not form large structures.
- **Pasteurization (HTST)** — Heating milk to 72 °C for 15 seconds to kill pathogenic bacteria; standard for safety.
- **Homogenization** — High-pressure forcing of milk through narrow tubes to break fat globules (~0.4 µm), preventing cream separation.
- **UHT (Ultra-heat treatment)** — Heating to 138 °C for 2–4 seconds; destroys all bacteria, enabling 6-month shelf life unrefrigerated.
- **rbST / rBGH** — Recombinant bovine somatotropin; synthetic growth hormone injected to increase milk yield; approved US 1993.
- **DIAAS** — Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score; ≥1 indicates high-quality protein (cow milk = 1.43).

## Debates and open questions
- **Dairying origins**: Lipid residue evidence (7th millennium BC) challenges Sherratt's "secondary products revolution" (4th millennium BC) timeline; exact sequence of meat vs. dairy use debated.
- **Calcium phosphate in micelles**: Competing models (submicellar lattice vs. de Kruif/Holt nanocluster nucleation vs. Horne polymerization) disagree on internal micelle structure.
- **Climate change impact**: IPCC notes stagnation in China/West Africa may stem from heat stress; models project up to 50% yield loss in China by 2070, but adaptation potential uncertain.
- **rbST animal welfare**: Higher mastitis incidence leads to bans in EU, Canada, NZ, Australia, Japan; US FDA maintains safety but welfare concerns persist.
- **Milk as fungicide/fertilizer**: Effectiveness debated; some evidence for powdery mildew control on grapes, but not widely adopted.
- **India's future trade balance**: Despite being top producer, rising domestic demand may turn India into a net importer.
