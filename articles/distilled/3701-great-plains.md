# Great Plains

## Overview
The Great Plains is a vast, flat region of central North America stretching from the Rocky Mountains eastward across the central United States and southern Canada. Defined by its semi-arid climate, grassland ecology, and history of nomadic Indigenous cultures followed by agricultural settlement, the region is a critical area for understanding North American geography, ecology, and history. Its development was shaped by technological innovation, environmental challenges like the Dust Bowl, and ongoing debates about land use and sustainability.

## Timeline
- **c. 145–66 million years ago** — The region is covered by the Western Interior Seaway during the Cretaceous Period.
- **c. 25 million years ago** — Grasslands become widespread during the Miocene and Pliocene epochs.
- **c. 13,000 years ago** — Most megafauna (e.g., mammoths, saber-toothed cats) go extinct at the end of the Pleistocene.
- **c. 800 CE** — Corn cultivation allows the development of Mississippian mound-building cultures.
- **1540–1542** — First European contact via Spanish expeditions (Coronado, de Soto).
- **1680** — Pueblo Revolt leads to the spread of horses to Plains tribes.
- **1770** — Plains Indian horse culture is mature, consisting of mounted buffalo-hunting nomads.
- **1803** — United States acquires the region via the Louisiana Purchase.
- **1862** — U.S. Homestead Act encourages settlement with 160-acre land claims.
- **1870s** — Railroads expand, accelerating settlement and the near-extinction of bison.
- **1930s** — The Dust Bowl devastates the southern plains due to drought and poor farming practices.
- **1950s–present** — Extensive irrigation from the Ogallala Aquifer enables large-scale agriculture.

## Body

### Geography and Climate
The Great Plains is the western portion of the Interior Plains, extending about 500 miles (800 km) east to west and 2,000 miles (3,200 km) north to south. Its altitude rises gradually from about 600–1,200 feet (180–370 m) in the east to 4,000–6,000 feet (1,200–1,800 m) near the Rockies. The eastern boundary is climatic, roughly following the 20-inch (510 mm) annual rainfall line near the 97th meridian west. The region has a semi-arid to continental climate with cold winters, hot summers, and high winds. The southeastern portion is part of "Tornado Alley."

### Ecology and Paleontology
The Great Plains is characterized by prairie and grassland flora. Its fauna historically included immense herds of American bison, along with pronghorn, prairie dogs, and the swift fox. The region is a major fossil site; during the Cretaceous, it was covered by the Western Interior Seaway. Later, Miocene grasslands supported diverse megafauna, most of which went extinct around 13,000 years ago. Today, grasslands are among the least protected biomes, with much converted for agriculture.

### Human History to 1850
Paleo-Indians arrived thousands of years ago. By the 17th–18th centuries, various tribes (e.g., Sioux, Cheyenne, Comanche) inhabited the plains, with a mature mounted buffalo-hunting culture established by 1770. The fur trade, beginning with the Hudson's Bay Company (1670), and the Louisiana Purchase (1803) brought increased European contact and devastating diseases like smallpox, which killed a majority of some tribes by the 1830s.

### Settlement and Transformation (1850–1930)
The Homestead Act (1862) and railroads drove massive settlement. Six key technologies—the Colt revolver, barbed wire (1874), windmill, steel plow, railroad, and mechanized farm equipment—enabled the transition from open-range ranching to sedentary agriculture. This period saw the near-extinction of bison and the displacement of Plains Indians. The Progressive Era (late 19th–early 20th century) brought reforms like direct democracy and women's suffrage, often pioneered in Plains states.

### 20th Century to Present
The Dust Bowl of the 1930s, caused by drought and inappropriate cultivation, forced many farmers off the land. Post-1950s, irrigation from the Ogallala Aquifer made the southern plains a major agricultural exporter, though aquifer depletion is a concern. Rural population has declined significantly since 1920, leading to thousands of ghost towns. The region now contributes substantially to U.S. wind power, and proposals like the "Buffalo Commons" suggest returning some drier areas to native prairie.

## Terms
- **Interior Plains**: The vast physiographic division of central North America, of which the Great Plains is the western part.
- **High Plains**: A subregion of the Great Plains, characterized by higher elevation and semi-arid conditions.
- **Prairie**: The dominant grassland ecosystem of the Great Plains, also used as a regional term in Canada.
- **Homestead Act (1862)**: U.S. law granting 160 acres of public land to settlers for a small fee, contingent on cultivation and residency.
- **Ogallala Aquifer**: A massive underground water-bearing stratum beneath the southern Great Plains, crucial for irrigation.
- **Dust Bowl**: A period of severe dust storms in the 1930s that damaged the ecology and agriculture of the southern plains.
- **Buffalo Commons**: A proposal to return about 139,000 sq mi of drier Great Plains to native prairie due to unsustainable land use.
- **Progressive Era**: A period of social and political reform in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, with strong activity in Plains states.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ecological Boundaries**: The eastern boundary of the Great Plains is debated; it is primarily climatic (rainfall line) rather than topographic, and definitions vary between physiographic and ecological models.
- **"Grit, not Grass" Hypothesis**: Paleontological evidence suggests that the open, gritty habitat of the Miocene, not the grass itself, drove dietary changes in mammals, challenging the traditional link between grassland spread and grazer evolution.
- **Sustainability of Current Land Use**: The long-term viability of irrigated agriculture depleting the Ogallala Aquifer and the ongoing rural population decline prompt questions about future land use, including proposals like the Buffalo Commons.
