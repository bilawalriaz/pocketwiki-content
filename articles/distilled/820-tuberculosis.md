# Tuberculosis

## Overview  
Tuberculosis (TB) is a contagious bacterial infection, primarily caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, that most commonly affects the lungs but can spread to other organs. It is transmitted through airborne droplets from people with active pulmonary disease. While many infections remain latent and non-contagious, a portion progress to active disease, which can be fatal without treatment. TB has shaped human history, earning names like “consumption” and “white death,” and remains a leading infectious cause of death globally, with rising challenges from drug-resistant strains.

## Timeline  
- **~10,000–11,000 BCE** — Earliest skeletal evidence of TB in prehistoric remains
- **3000–2400 BCE** — TB detected in Egyptian mummies
- **1689** — Richard Morton links tubercles to pulmonary TB pathology
- **1832** — J. L. Schönlein coins the term “tuberculosis”
- **1865** — Jean Antoine Villemin proves TB transmission from humans to animals
- **1882** — Robert Koch identifies *M. tuberculosis*
- **1906** — Calmette and Guérin develop BCG vaccine
- **1946** — Streptomycin introduces effective antibiotic treatment
- **1993** — WHO declares TB a global health emergency
- **2014** — WHO launches End TB Strategy targeting 90% reduction in TB deaths by 2030

## Body  

### History and Identification  
TB has afflicted humans since antiquity, with evidence found in Neolithic remains and Egyptian mummies. Ancient texts from Hippocrates and the Vedas describe symptoms consistent with TB. For centuries, TB was known as “consumption,” associated with romanticism and artistic temperament. The disease was not recognized as a single condition until the 1820s. In 1865, Villemin demonstrated its contagious nature, and in 1882, Koch identified the causative bacillus, earning him the 1905 Nobel Prize.

### Treatments and Public Health  
Before antibiotics, TB was managed through sanatoria, fresh air, and rest—often with poor outcomes. Surgical interventions like pneumothorax were used to collapse infected lungs. The 1946 introduction of streptomycin revolutionized treatment. Public health campaigns promoted hygiene, ventilation, and pasteurization to reduce transmission. BCG vaccination, developed in 1906, became widely adopted after WWII.

### Drug-Resistant Tuberculosis  
Resistance emerged shortly after antibiotic use began. By the 1980s, multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB)—resistant to isoniazid and rifampicin—became a global concern. Extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) and totally drug-resistant TB (TDR-TB) represent further escalation. As of 2023, 3.2% of new TB cases are RR/MDR-TB. Treatment is longer, costlier, and more toxic, requiring second-line drugs.

### Signs and Symptoms  
Active pulmonary TB presents with chronic cough, hemoptysis, fever, night sweats, and weight loss. Extrapulmonary TB affects 15–20% of cases, involving sites like the pleura, CNS, lymph nodes, bones, and genitourinary system. Latent TB shows no symptoms but can reactivate, especially in immunocompromised individuals.

### Causes and Transmission  
*M. tuberculosis* is an aerobic, slow-growing bacillus with a lipid-rich cell wall, contributing to drug resistance and environmental survival. It spreads via airborne droplets from coughs or sneezes. Other pathogenic mycobacteria include *M. bovis*, *M. africanum*, and *M. canettii*. Risk factors include HIV, diabetes, malnutrition, smoking, and immunosuppressive therapy.

### Pathogenesis  
Inhaled bacilli reach alveoli and are phagocytosed by macrophages. *M. tuberculosis* survives by inhibiting phagosome-lysosome fusion. Immune cells form granulomas to contain infection, but bacilli persist. If immunity fails, granulomas break down, leading to active disease. Disseminated (miliary) TB occurs when bacteria enter the bloodstream.

### Diagnosis  
Diagnosis is challenging due to nonspecific symptoms and slow culture growth. Tests include the tuberculin skin test (TST), interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) like Xpert MTB/RIF, and chest X-rays. Definitive diagnosis requires detecting bacteria in sputum or tissue samples.

### Prevention  
Prevention includes screening high-risk groups, treating latent infections, and BCG vaccination. Airborne infection control (AIC) uses administrative, environmental, and respiratory protection measures. Global strategies include DOTS, the Stop TB Strategy, and the End TB Initiative.

### Treatment  
Standard treatment combines four first-line drugs (HRZE) for 6 months. MDR-TB requires second-line drugs for up to 24 months. WHO now recommends shorter 6–9 month regimens. Adherence is critical; directly observed therapy (DOT) improves compliance.

### Prognosis  
Untreated TB is fatal in ~67% of cases within three years. HIV co-infection drastically increases mortality and reactivation risk. Early diagnosis and treatment significantly improve outcomes.

### Epidemiology  
WHO estimates 10.7 million new cases and 1.23 million deaths annually (2024). TB disproportionately affects low-income populations, with 87% of cases in 30 high-burden countries. HIV co-infection affects 6.1% of TB patients. Seasonality and socioeconomic factors influence transmission.

### Society and Culture  
TB has inspired art, literature, and folklore. Known as the “romantic disease,” it influenced figures like Keats, Chopin, and Orwell. Stigma persists, especially in marginalized communities. Legal frameworks vary, with some countries enforcing mandatory notification or isolation.

### Global Programs  
WHO initiatives include DOTS (1995), Stop TB Strategy (2006), and End TB Strategy (2014). These aim to reduce incidence and deaths, expand access to diagnostics and treatment, and eliminate catastrophic costs.

### Research Directions  
Key areas include faster diagnostics, shorter treatments, better vaccines, and understanding host-pathogen interactions. BCG remains the only licensed vaccine despite variable efficacy. New candidates like MVA85A have shown limited success.

### Other Animals  
Zoonotic TB (*M. bovis*) affects cattle and wildlife. Reverse zoonosis has been documented in elephants. A One Health approach is needed to address all reservoirs.

## Terms  
- **Latent TB**: Asymptomatic infection with *M. tuberculosis*, non-contagious, detectable via TST or IGRA.  
- **Active TB**: Symptomatic disease, contagious, requiring treatment.  
- **MDR-TB**: Resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampicin.  
- **XDR-TB**: MDR-TB plus resistance to fluoroquinolones and second-line injectables.  
- **BCG Vaccine**: Live attenuated *M. bovis* vaccine, effective in children, less so in adults.  
- **Granuloma**: Immune structure walling off *M. tuberculosis*, central to latency.  
- **DOT**: Directly observed therapy ensuring treatment adherence.  
- **Xpert MTB/RIF**: Rapid NAAT detecting *M. tuberculosis* and rifampicin resistance.  
- **TST**: Tuberculin skin test measuring delayed-type hypersensitivity.  
- **IGRA**: Blood test detecting interferon-gamma release in response to TB antigens.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- **TDR-TB Definition**: No universally accepted criteria; described as resistance to all first- and second-line drugs.  
- **BCG Efficacy**: Highly variable protection, especially in adults; new vaccines urgently needed.  
- **Latent TB Treatment**: Optimal duration and candidates remain debated.  
- **Global Targets**: End TB milestones largely unmet; funding and equity gaps persist.  
- **Zoonotic Transmission**: Role of *M. bovis* in human disease understudied in many regions.
