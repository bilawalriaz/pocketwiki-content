# Göbekli Tepe

## Overview
Göbekli Tepe is a Neolithic archaeological site in southeastern Turkey, inhabited from around 9500 to 8000 BCE. It is renowned for its monumental circular structures containing the world's oldest known megalithic pillars, many decorated with animal reliefs. The site is pivotal for understanding the transition from hunter-gatherer societies to settled village life, as it was built by pre-agricultural communities, challenging previous assumptions about the causes of the Neolithic Revolution.

## Timeline
- **c. 9500–9000 BCE** — Earliest construction and occupation (late PPNA period).
- **Early 9th millennium BCE** — Significant expansion and modification of enclosures (early PPNB).
- **Late 9th millennium BCE** — Major landslides damage enclosures; some are abandoned.
- **c. 8000 BCE** — Main Neolithic occupation ends; smaller groups later live among ruins.
- **1963** — Site first noted in an archaeological survey.
- **1994** — Klaus Schmidt recognizes its significance; excavations begin in 1995.
- **2014** — Klaus Schmidt dies; research continues under new direction.
- **2018** — Designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Location and Environment
Göbekli Tepe is a tell (artificial mound) on a limestone plateau in the Taş Tepeler region of Şanlıurfa Province, overlooking the Harran plain. The climate was wetter during its occupation, supporting open steppe grassland with wild cereals (einkorn, wheat, barley) and herds of gazelle, sheep, and goats. The site's high location provided visibility and access to limestone for construction and flint for tools. A rainwater harvesting system with cisterns supplied water.

### Chronology and Building Phases
The site was occupied during the Pre-Pottery Neolithic (PPN), divided into PPNA (c. 9600–8800 BCE) and PPNB (c. 8800–7000 BCE). Radiocarbon dating establishes its use from 9500 to 8000 BCE. The revised chronology includes eight phases. The earliest phase (late 10th millennium BCE) features round-oval domestic structures and the first large circular enclosures. Subsequent phases show modifications to these enclosures, the erection of T-shaped pillars, and a shift toward rectangular domestic buildings. Landslides in the early PPNB damaged lower structures, leading to stabilization efforts and eventual abandonment of some enclosures.

### Architecture
The most prominent features are large circular (and later rectangular) enclosures, 10 to 30 meters in diameter, containing T-shaped limestone pillars. Four circles have been excavated; geophysical surveys indicate at least 20 exist. Each enclosure has two taller central pillars facing each other, with smaller pillars set in interior walls. Pillars were quarried nearby and transported using flint tools. Many pillars are carved with animal reliefs (snakes, foxes, boars, vultures) and abstract symbols. Some pillars have anthropomorphic features like arms and loincloths, suggesting they represent stylized human or divine figures. Domestic structures evolved from round-oval to rectangular plans over time.

### Interpretation and Debate
Klaus Schmidt initially interpreted Göbekli Tepe as a ritual sanctuary built by nomadic hunter-gatherers who congregated periodically. Butchered animal bones suggest feasting events timed with seasonal game availability. However, recent discoveries of domestic structures, cereal processing tools, and water systems challenge the "temple-only" narrative, indicating a settled or semi-sedentary community where ritual and daily life coexisted. The purpose of the megalithic structures remains unknown; they were likely roofed and underwent cycles of collapse, repair, and rebuilding. Claims of astronomical observatory function have been largely rejected.

## Terms
- **Tell**: An artificial mound formed by the accumulated debris of human settlement over time.
- **Pre-Pottery Neolithic (PPN)**: The early Neolithic period in Southwest Asia (c. 9600–7000 BCE) characterized by permanent settlements before the widespread use of pottery.
- **Megalith**: A large stone that has been used to construct a structure or monument, either alone or together with other stones.
- **T-shaped pillar**: A distinctive monolithic limestone pillar with a horizontal top, found at Göbekli Tepe and other regional sites, often interpreted as stylized anthropomorphic figures.
- **Anthropomorphic**: Having human characteristics or form.
- **Radiocarbon dating**: A method for determining the age of organic material by measuring the decay of the radioactive isotope carbon-14.
- **Old wood effect**: A dating error where radiocarbon dates are older than the event being dated because the wood used was from an old tree.
- **Skull cult**: A Neolithic practice involving the special treatment and display of human skulls, sometimes plastered or incised.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Function**: Was Göbekli Tepe primarily a regional ritual center for nomads, or a settled village with integrated communal and domestic spaces? The discovery of domestic structures has shifted interpretation toward a mixed-use site.
- **Labor and Social Organization**: Estimates for the workforce needed to build the site vary widely, from hundreds (suggesting a large, coerced labor force) to a dozen or two (within the capacity of a single community). This debate impacts theories about social hierarchy and the origins of complex society.
- **Cause of the Neolithic Revolution**: Does Göbekli Tepe support the idea that the desire to build monumental structures for ritual gatherings drove the adoption of agriculture, or did settled life precede and enable such projects?
- **Intentional Burial**: Were the large enclosures intentionally backfilled as part of a ritual, or were they buried by natural processes like landslides and subsequent human activity? The latter interpretation is now favored.
