# Wright brothers

## Overview
The Wright brothers, Orville (1871–1948) and Wilbur (1867–1912), were American aviation pioneers who invented, built, and flew the world's first successful motor-powered, controlled airplane. Their breakthrough was developing a practical three-axis control system, which remains the fundamental principle of aircraft control. Their work transformed aviation from theory into practical technology, though their legacy is marked by legal battles and competing historical claims.

## Body

### Early Life and Interest in Flight
Wilbur and Orville Wright were sons of a bishop who settled in Dayton, Ohio. Their mechanical skills were honed through work with printing presses and bicycles. A toy helicopter given by their father in 1878 sparked their interest in flight. After reading about Otto Lilienthal's gliding experiments, the brothers began serious aeronautical research in 1899, focusing on the unsolved problem of pilot control.

### The Three-Axis Control System
The Wrights' key insight was that control, not inherent stability, was critical. Observing birds, they developed "wing-warping" to control roll. Combined with a forward elevator for pitch and a rear rudder for yaw, this created a three-axis control system. After disappointing glider results in 1901, they built a wind tunnel to gather accurate aerodynamic data. Their 1902 glider, with a movable rudder linked to the wing-warping control, finally achieved true, controlled flight.

### The First Powered Flights
Using their wind tunnel data, the brothers designed efficient propellers and a lightweight engine with mechanic Charlie Taylor. On December 17, 1903, at Kitty Hawk, they made four flights. The first, by Orville, lasted 12 seconds and covered 120 feet. The longest, by Wilbur, lasted 59 seconds and covered 852 feet. The flights were witnessed by five people but received little initial public attention.

### Establishing Legitimacy and Public Demonstrations
From 1904–1905 at Huffman Prairie near Dayton, they developed the Wright Flyer III, the first practical airplane. However, secrecy and lack of press coverage led to skepticism. In 1908, public demonstrations by Wilbur in France and Orville for the U.S. Army proved their achievement. A crash at Fort Myer killed observer Lt. Thomas Selfridge, the first airplane fatality, but Orville recovered and completed the Army contract.

### Patent War and Business
In 1906, the Wrights received a patent for their control system. They formed the Wright Company in 1909 but became embroiled in a protracted patent lawsuit with Glenn Curtiss over ailerons. The legal battle consumed their energy and stifled innovation. The dispute was resolved in 1917 when the U.S. government pressured the industry to form a patent pool during World War I. The Wright and Curtiss companies merged in 1929.

### Later Years and Legacy
Wilbur died of typhoid fever in 1912, worn out by business and legal struggles. Orville sold the company in 1915 and later served on government aviation boards. A long feud with the Smithsonian Institution, which had claimed its Langley Aerodrome was capable of flight, ended only after Orville's death. The 1903 Wright Flyer was installed at the Smithsonian in 1948 with a label affirming the Wrights' primacy.

## Terms
- **Three-axis control:** The system of controlling an aircraft's rotation around its three axes: pitch, roll, and yaw.
- **Wing-warping:** The Wrights' method of twisting the wings to control roll, later superseded by ailerons.
- **Canard:** An aircraft configuration with the horizontal elevator in front of the main wings, as on the early Wright Flyer.
- **Adverse yaw:** The tendency of an aircraft to yaw opposite a banked turn, solved by the Wrights' movable rudder.
- **Smeaton coefficient:** A value in the lift equation for air pressure; the Wrights discovered the traditional value was incorrect.
- **Wind tunnel:** A tool to study air moving past objects; the Wrights' home-built tunnel provided crucial aerodynamic data.
- **Aspect ratio:** The ratio of a wing's span to its chord; the Wrights found higher ratios improved lift-to-drag.
- **Pusher configuration:** An aircraft design where the propeller is mounted behind the engine, as on the original Wright Flyer.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Competing claims:** The Wrights' status as first in flight is challenged by claims for earlier aviators like Gustave Whitehead and Clément Ader. Historians debate whether these earlier attempts constituted controlled, sustained flight.
- **Smithsonian feud:** The decades-long dispute centered on the Smithsonian's claim that the Langley Aerodrome was capable of flight, based on modified 1914 tests. The conflict was resolved with strict conditions for displaying the 1903 Flyer.
- **Impact of legal battles:** Scholars debate how much the Wrights' patent lawsuits against Glenn Curtiss stifled early American aviation development.
- **Wilbur's leadership:** While the brothers presented a unified front, biographers note Wilbur was the driving force in the early years of their research.
