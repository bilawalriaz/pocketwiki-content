# Climate

Climate is the long-term weather pattern in a region, typically averaged over 30 years, but more rigorously defined as the mean and variability of meteorological variables over periods from months to millions of years. It encompasses the state of the climate system—the atmosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere, lithosphere, and biosphere—and their interactions. Climate matters because it shapes ecosystems, biomes, and human societies, and understanding it is essential for distinguishing natural variability from human-caused change, predicting future conditions, and studying ancient environments through paleoclimatology.

## Body

### Definition and Measurement

Climate (from Ancient Greek *klima*, 'inclination') is commonly defined as weather averaged over a long period. The standard averaging period is 30 years, as defined by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), though other periods may be used. The WMO describes "climate normals" as reference points for comparing current trends to the past; a normal is the arithmetic average of a climate element over 30 years—long enough to filter out interannual variations like El Niño–Southern Oscillation, but short enough to show longer trends. The WMO originated from the International Meteorological Organization, which designated 1901–1930 as the reference period in 1934; the 1961–1990 normals now serve as the baseline, with 1991–2020 normals next. Variables collected include air temperature, pressure, precipitation, wind, humidity, visibility, cloud amount, solar radiation, soil temperature, and pan evaporation. The difference between climate and weather is summarized by: "Climate is what you expect, weather is what you get."

Nearly constant climate determinants include latitude, altitude, land-to-water proportion, and proximity to oceans and mountains, changing only over millions of years via plate tectonics. More dynamic determinants include the thermohaline circulation of the ocean, which warms the northern Atlantic by 5°C (9°F) compared to other basins; other ocean currents redistribute heat regionally. Vegetation coverage affects solar heat absorption, water retention, and rainfall. Atmospheric greenhouse gas levels (particularly carbon dioxide and methane) determine solar energy retention, leading to global warming or cooling.

### Climate Classification

Climate classifications categorize the world's climates and may correlate closely with biome classifications, as climate is a major influence on life. The most widely used scheme is the Köppen climate classification, first developed in 1899. Modern methods divide into genetic methods (focusing on causes, such as air mass frequency or synoptic weather disturbance locations) and empiric methods (focusing on effects, such as plant hardiness, evapotranspiration, or Köppen's biomes). The Thornthwaite system, in use since 1948, incorporates evapotranspiration with temperature and precipitation, used in studying biological diversity and climate change impacts; its major classifications are microthermal, mesothermal, and megathermal. The Bergeron and Spatial Synoptic Classification systems focus on air mass origins. A common shortcoming of these schemes is that they produce distinct boundaries rather than the gradual transitions more common in nature.

### Climate Record

Paleoclimatology studies ancient climates, seeking to explain variations for all parts of the Earth since its formation. Since direct observations were scarce before the 19th century, paleoclimates are inferred from proxy variables: non-biotic evidence like lake bed sediments and ice cores, and biotic evidence like tree rings and coral. It demonstrates periods of stability and change, and can indicate whether changes follow regular cycles.

The modern climate record relies on instruments like thermometers, barometers, and anemometers from the past few centuries. Instrument changes—observation frequency, known error, immediate environment, and exposure—must be considered when studying past centuries. Long-term records skew toward population centres and affluent countries; since the 1960s, satellites allow global records, including the Arctic and oceans.

### Climate Variability and Change

Climate variability describes variations in the mean state and other characteristics "on all spatial and temporal scales beyond that of individual weather events." Some variability occurs at random times (random variability or noise); periodic variability occurs regularly in distinct modes or climate patterns. Correlations exist between climate oscillations and astronomical factors (barycenter changes, solar variation, cosmic ray flux, cloud albedo feedback, Milankovic cycles) and ocean-atmosphere heat distribution modes. Natural oscillations may be masked by volcanic eruptions, impact events, proxy data irregularities, positive feedback processes, or anthropogenic emissions. The term "climate change" now implies long-term, human-caused change, whereas in the 1960s it referred to what is now called climate variability.

Climate change reflects changes in variability or average state over decades to millions of years, caused by internal Earth processes, external forces (e.g., sunlight intensity variations), or human activities. Scientists identify Earth's Energy Imbalance (EEI) as a fundamental metric of global change status. In environmental policy contexts, "climate change" often refers only to modern changes, including global warming, sometimes with a presumption of human causation as in the UNFCCC, which uses "climate variability" for non-human causes. Earth has undergone four major ice ages, with glacial periods (colder than normal) separated by interglacial periods. Snow and ice accumulation increases surface albedo, reflecting more solar energy and maintaining lower temperatures; greenhouse gas increases, such as from volcanic activity, can produce interglacial periods. Suggested ice age causes include continent positions, Earth's orbit variations, solar output changes, and volcanism—but these natural changes occur much more slowly than the present rate from human greenhouse gas emissions.

### Climate Models

Climate models use quantitative methods to simulate interactions and radiative energy transfer between the atmosphere, oceans, land surface, and ice through physics equations. They are used from studying weather and climate dynamics to projecting future climate. All models balance incoming short wave (visible) electromagnetic radiation with outgoing long wave (infrared) radiation; any imbalance changes average temperature. Models range in resolution from >100 km to 1 km; high-resolution global models require significant computational resources. Global models can be dynamically or statistically downscaled to regional models to analyze local impacts; examples include ICON or CHELSA (Climatologies at high resolution for the earth's land surface areas). Recent applications focus on consequences of increasing greenhouse gases, primarily carbon dioxide; these models predict an upward trend in global mean surface temperature, with the most rapid increase projected for higher northern latitudes. Models range from simple radiant heat transfer models treating Earth as a single point, to vertically expanded radiative-convective models, to complex coupled atmosphere–ocean–sea ice global climate models that solve full equations for mass and energy transfer.

## Terms

- **Climate normal**: Arithmetic average of a climate element over a 30-year period, used as a reference point for comparing trends.
- **Paleoclimatology**: Study of ancient climates using proxy variables like ice cores, tree rings, and sediments.
- **Proxy variables**: Indirect evidence of past climate, including non-biotic (sediments, ice cores) and biotic (tree rings, coral) sources.
- **Köppen climate classification**: Most widely used scheme, first developed in 1899, identifying climates associated with biomes.
- **Evapotranspiration**: Process incorporated in the Thornthwaite system; combines evaporation and plant transpiration, used to study biological diversity.
- **Thermohaline circulation**: Ocean circulation that warms the northern Atlantic by 5°C (9°F) compared to other basins.
- **Albedo**: Surface reflectivity; snow and ice accumulation increases albedo, reflecting more solar energy and maintaining lower temperatures.
- **Earth's Energy Imbalance (EEI)**: Fundamental metric of global change status, reflecting the balance between incoming and outgoing radiation.
- **Milankovic cycles**: Astronomical factors correlated with climate oscillations, related to Earth's orbital variations.
- **Downscaling**: Process of converting global climate models to regional models, dynamically or statistically, to analyze local impacts.

## Debates and open questions

The source notes that while the variables determining climate are numerous and interactions complex, there is general agreement that the broad outlines are understood, at least regarding historical climate change determinants. It also highlights that natural oscillations may be masked by volcanic eruptions, impact events, irregularities in climate proxy data, positive feedback processes, or anthropogenic emissions, complicating the identification of natural versus human-caused variability. The shifting definitions of "climate change" versus "climate variability" reflect ongoing terminological debates, particularly regarding human causation assumptions in policy contexts.
