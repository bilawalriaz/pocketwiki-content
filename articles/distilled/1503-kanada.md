# Kaṇāda

## Overview
Kaṇāda (also Kaṇabhaksha, Kaṇabhuj) was an ancient Indian philosopher and natural scientist who founded the **Vaisheshika** school, one of the six orthodox (āstika) systems of Hindu philosophy. Estimated to have lived between the 6th century and 2nd century BCE, he authored the *Vaiśeṣika Sūtra* (also *Kaṇāda Sūtra*), a foundational text presenting an **atomistic** (paramāṇu) ontology. Kaṇāda argued that the physical universe is composed of eternal, indivisible atoms that combine through heat to form complex substances. His system is a systematic realist metaphysics identifying six categories of existence (padārthas) and nine classes of substance (dravya). It provides a non-theistic path to *moksha* (liberation) through knowledge and *dharma*, defining the latter as that which yields material progress and the highest good. Vaisheshika later merged closely with the **Nyaya** school of logic.

## Timeline
- **6th–2nd century BCE** — Estimated lifetime of Kaṇāda (subject to scholarly debate).
- **c. 200 BCE – 1st century CE** — *Vaiśeṣika Sūtra* systematized and finalized (per critical editions of manuscripts discovered 1957, 1961).
- **1st–2nd century CE** — Kaṇāda’s doctrines quoted in *Mahāvibhāṣa* and *Jñānaprasthana* (Kushan Empire) and Buddhist texts of Aśvaghoṣa.
- **1961** — Scholar Dale Riepe reviews dating, placing Kaṇāda before 300 CE.
- **Classical Period** — Vaisheshika becomes closely associated with the Nyaya school (Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika).

## Body

### Lifetime and Textual History
The exact century of Kaṇāda remains debated. The *Vaiśeṣika Sūtras* reference rival schools Sāṃkhya and Mīmāṃsā but not Buddhism, leading some scholars to posit a 6th century BCE date. However, critical editions of manuscripts discovered by Thakur (1957) and Jambuvijayaji (1961) suggest the received text was finalized between 200 BCE and the start of the Common Era, though core doctrines may be older. By the 1st–2nd century CE, his ideas were cited in Sarvāstivāda Buddhist texts (*Mahāvibhāṣa*, *Jñānaprasthana*) and by Aśvaghoṣa. In Jain literature, he is called **Sad-uluka** ("the Uluka who propounded the six categories"), and his school is termed **Aulukya** philosophy (from *Uluka*, "owl," a nickname for Kaṇāda).

### Ontology: The Six Categories (Padārthas) and Nine Substances
Kaṇāda’s system posits six **padārthas** (nameable, knowable categories) sufficient to describe all reality, including observers:
1.  **Dravya** (Substance): The substrate for qualities and actions.
2.  **Guṇa** (Quality): Attributes residing in substances (e.g., colour, taste, smell, touch, number, size, pleasure, pain).
3.  **Karma** (Action/Motion): Physical movement (e.g., coupling, uncoupling, priority, posterity).
4.  **Sāmānya** (Generality/Universals): Common properties allowing classification.
5.  **Viśeṣa** (Particularity): Unique individuators distinguishing eternal entities (atoms, souls, space).
6.  **Samavāya** (Inherence): The eternal, inseparable relation binding a whole to its parts, a quality to a substance, or a universal to an individual.

There are **nine dravyas** (substances): four atomic (earth, water, fire/light, air) and five non-atomic, all-pervasive (space/ākāśa, time/kāla, direction/diśa, souls/Ātman, mind/manas). Individual souls (Ātman) are eternal, infinite in number, and temporarily inhabit material bodies.

### Atomism (Paramāṇu) and Physics
**Paramāṇu** (atom) is defined as an indestructible, indivisible particle of matter—the state beyond which no measurement (size, division) applies. Kaṇāda used **invariance arguments** to deduce atomic properties: because an atom lacks internal parts, it must be spherical (same in all dimensions). Atoms exist in two states: absolute rest or motion.

He postulated **four distinct kinds of atoms** corresponding to the four atomic substances (earth, water, fire, air). Crucially, **two have mass and two are massless**. Every composite substance contains all four kinds. Atoms differ **both qualitatively and quantitatively** (unlike Greek atomism, where atoms differ only quantitatively). Combinations begin with dyads (dvyaṇuka) and triads (tryaṇuka), driven by heat, yielding the empirical material world. Physics is central because all knowable reality is based on motion; the observer (subject) is explicitly distinct from the objective system studied.

### Observations, Causality, and Epistemology
In *Vaiśeṣika Sūtra* Chapter 5, Kaṇāda lists empirical phenomena—falling objects, rising fire/heat, upward grass growth, rainfall, thunderstorms, liquid flow, magnetic attraction—and classifies their causes into two types: **volition** (conscious effort) and **subject-object conjunction** (mechanical contact). This distinction reflects his epistemology: the observer (subject) is separate from objective reality, paralleling the Vedanta distinction between *Apara* (associational/empirical) and *Para* (deeper subjective) knowledge.

### Ethics, Dharma, and Liberation
Kaṇāda defines **Dharma** functionally: "that which brings about material progress and highest good." He asserts the Vedas are authoritative *because* they teach such Dharma, not the reverse (something is not Dharma merely because it is in the Vedas). Like Sāṃkhya, early Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, Yoga, Vedanta sub-schools, Jainism, and Buddhism, Vaisheshika is **non-theistic**: the universe evolves by law (not divine fiat), and humans can attain *moksha* through their own knowledge and piety, without God.

### Influence
Kaṇāda’s analytical categories (substance, quality, action, etc.) created a structural affinity with the **Nyaya** school of logic, leading to their historical synthesis as Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika. His ideas possibly influenced the medical scholar **Charaka** (*Charaka Samhita*). The concept of the observer-independent system anticipates themes in modern philosophy of physics.

## Terms
- ****Vaisheshika**** — One of six orthodox Hindu darśanas (philosophical systems), founded by Kaṇāda, specializing in metaphysics and atomistic physics.
- ****Vaiśeṣika Sūtra**** — Kaṇāda’s aphoristic treatise (also *Kaṇāda Sūtra*), finalized c. 200 BCE – 1st century CE, outlining ontology, atomism, and epistemology.
- ****Padārtha**** — "Category of existence"; the six fundamental ontological categories (substance, quality, action, generality, particularity, inherence).
- ****Dravya**** — Substance; the substratum in which qualities and actions inhere. Nine classes exist (four atomic, five non-atomic).
- ****Paramāṇu**** — Atom; the eternal, indivisible, indestructible minimal particle of matter, spherical by invariance, existing in states of rest or motion.
- ****Guṇa**** — Quality; a property (colour, taste, size, pleasure, pain, etc.) that resides in a substance but lacks independent existence.
- ****Karma**** — Action/Motion; physical movement (e.g., throwing, falling, coupling) distinct from the ethical "karma" of moral causality.
- ****Sāmānya**** — Generality/Universal; the recurrent property allowing multiple entities to be grouped into a class.
- ****Viśeṣa**** — Particularity; the unique individuator distinguishing otherwise identical eternal entities (atoms, souls, space).
- ****Samavāya**** — Inherence; the eternal, inseparable relation connecting a whole to its parts, a quality to its substance, or a universal to its instances.
- ****Ātman**** — Soul/Self; eternal, infinite in number, distinct from body/mind, the knower and enjoyer.
- ****Dharma**** — In Kaṇāda’s usage: that which yields material prosperity (*abhyudaya*) and spiritual liberation (*niḥśreyasa*).
- ****Moksha**** — Liberation from the cycle of rebirth; attained by knowledge of the true nature of reality (padārthas) and practice of Dharma.
- ****Aulukya / Sad-uluka**** — Alternate names for Vaisheshika and Kaṇāda found in Jain texts (*Uluka* = "owl," a nickname for Kaṇāda).

## Debates and open questions
*   **Chronology**: Estimates range from 6th century BCE (based on silence regarding Buddhism) to 2nd century BCE / 1st century CE (based on manuscript critical editions and external citations). No consensus exists.
*   **Independence of Atomism**: Whether Kaṇāda’s atomic theory developed independently of Greek atomism (Leucippus/Democritus) is argued from theoretical differences (qualitative vs. quantitative atomic differences), but direct historical contact cannot be proven or disproven.
*   **Textual Stratification**: The *Vaiśeṣika Sūtra* exists in multiple manuscript versions; the extent to which the surviving text reflects Kaṇāda’s original composition versus later systematization (c. 200 BCE – 1st century CE) is unresolved.
*   **Theistic vs. Non-theistic Origins**: While classical Vaisheshika is explicitly non-theistic (law-governed universe, self-effort *moksha*), later Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika synthesis (e.g., Udayana) introduced God (Īśvara) as the efficient cause of atomic combination. The transition point and motivation remain debated.
*   **Influence on Charaka**: The *Charaka Samhita* shares categorical terminology (e.g., *padārtha*, *dravya*, *guṇa*), but the direction and depth of influence—borrowing vs. shared intellectual milieu—is not definitively established.
