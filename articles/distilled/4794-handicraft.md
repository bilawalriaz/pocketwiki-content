# Handicraft

## Overview
Handicraft refers to traditional, manual production of practical and aesthetic items using skills passed through generations, distinct from machine-based mass production. It remains culturally significant, economically vital in many regions, and increasingly recognized for its social and educational value.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Dhokra metal casting practiced in India for over 5,000 years
- **c. 2000 BC** — Redware pottery tradition in Iranian Baluchistan dates back 4,000 years
- **Middle Ages** — Craft guild system formalizes apprenticeships and professional trades
- **Late 19th century** — Arts and Crafts movement emerges in Europe, North America, and Australia
- **1950s** — North Vietnam has over 100,000 handicraft enterprises
- **Early 1970s** — Handicraft production provides half of North Vietnam's local industrial output
- **1977** — South Vietnam has 700,000 handicraftsmen after reunification
- **2023** — Vietnam's handicraft exports reach US$2.2 billion, up 10.5% from 2022 |

## Body

### Definition and Characteristics
Handicraft encompasses creative activities using hands and skill with materials like textiles, clay, wood, and metal. Items are both practical and aesthetic, with individual artisanship as the paramount criterion—mass-produced goods are excluded. The term applies to traditional techniques, though some crafts are modern inventions or regional popularizations. Materials range from natural, indigenous resources to upcycled industrial components.

### Historical Roots and the Arts and Crafts Movement
Handicraft originates in rural material necessities of ancient civilizations. The Arts and Crafts movement, founded by William Morris and John Ruskin, idealized pre-industrial medieval societies where fulfillment came through creative handicraft, contrasting with industrial alienation. Originally, many crafts were professions under guild systems where apprentices trained for years. The Industrial Revolution reduced these roles, shifting handicraft toward hobby, folk art, and fine art.

### Education and Skill Development
Handicrafts are integrated into education systems worldwide. In Scandinavian countries, advanced handicrafts (slöjd in Swedish, käsityö in Finnish) are compulsory, teaching metal, textile, and woodwork to develop practical problem-solving skills. Secondary schools and universities increasingly offer handicraft electives alongside fine arts, with the distinction fading due to studio craft—professional fine artists using traditional techniques.

### Production and Economic Impact
Handicraft production is small-scale manual labor, historically common but largely supplanted by industrialization except for luxury goods. The "handmade effect" describes consumer preference for human-crafted items, especially for symbolic gifts conveying love. In pre-revolutionary Russia, handicrafts produced 30% of manufactured goods. In Vietnam, handicrafts remain economically crucial, with 600 villages and over 10 million workers. Nepal's handicraft industry thrives through state support despite industrial competition.

### Community and Cultural Significance
Craft production forms part of informal economies in cities like Istanbul, creating craft neighborhoods structured around artisan activity. Handicrafts often carry cultural, religious, or political significance (craftivism). Production requires specialized knowledge and verbal, hands-on training, fostering strong social bonds within communities. Craft economies are place-based, linking social relations through tangible goods and indicating sociopolitical complexity.

## Terms
- **Handicraft**: Traditional manual production of practical and aesthetic items using skilled handwork.
- **Arts and Crafts movement**: Late-19th-century design reform movement idealizing pre-industrial handicraft.
- **Guild system**: Medieval system where apprentices trained under master craftsmen.
- **Handmade effect**: Consumer preference for human-crafted over machine-produced goods.
- **Studio craft**: Use of traditional handicraft techniques by professional fine artists.
- **Slöjd**: Swedish term for compulsory handicraft education in Nordic schools.
- **Craftivism**: Use of craft for political or social activism.
- **Informal economy**: Economic activity not regulated by the state, often including craft production.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes ongoing scholarly debates about the distinction between handicraft and fine art, with boundaries often overlapping to create hybrid objects. Context matters—audiences may perceive handcrafted items as art when displayed in museums or prominent home positions. The term "arts and crafts" is easily confused with the Arts and Crafts movement, though the movement is as practical as it is aesthetic. Additionally, the political dimension of craftivism represents an evolving interpretation of handicraft's role beyond traditional cultural or religious significance.
