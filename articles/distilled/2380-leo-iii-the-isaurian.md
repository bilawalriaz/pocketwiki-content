# Leo III the Isaurian

## Overview
Leo III the Isaurian (r. 717–741) was the first Byzantine emperor of the Isaurian dynasty. He ended the Twenty Years' Anarchy, a period of rapid imperial turnover, and successfully defended the Empire against the Umayyad Caliphate. His reign is most famous for initiating the policy of iconoclasm, the prohibition of religious image veneration, which defined Byzantine religious and political conflict for over a century.

## Timeline
- **c. 685** — Born in Germanikeia (modern Kahramanmaraş, Turkey).
- **717** — Overthrows Emperor Theodosius III and is crowned Emperor Leo III.
- **717–718** — Successfully defends Constantinople against the second Arab siege.
- **726–729** — Issues edicts against the veneration of icons, beginning iconoclasm.
- **730** — Patriarch Germanus I resigns in protest; replaced by the iconoclast Anastasius.
- **732–733** — Transfers ecclesiastical jurisdictions in Southern Italy and Illyricum from Rome to Constantinople.
- **740** — Byzantine victory at the Battle of Akroinon halts Arab advances.
- **741** — Dies of dropsy; succeeded by his son Constantine V.

## Body

### Rise to Power and the Siege of Constantinople
Leo III, originally named Konon, rose through the military ranks as a *stratēgos* (theme commander). In 717, he conspired to overthrow the emperor Theodosius III and was crowned on March 25. His reign began with an immediate crisis: the second Arab siege of Constantinople (717–718). A massive Umayyad force besieged the capital for 12 months. Leo's defense, aided by the use of Greek fire and Bulgarian reinforcements, forced the Arabs to withdraw, saving the Empire from destruction.

### Military and Administrative Reforms
Leo consolidated imperial authority after the anarchy. He suppressed rebellions and defended the frontiers against continued Umayyad raids, culminating in the decisive victory at Akroinon in 740. To strengthen the state, he militarized administration, invited Slavic settlers to depopulated areas, and reformed the legal code. The *Ekloge ton nomon* (Selection of Laws), published in 741, revised Justinian's code, notably substituting mutilation for the death penalty in many cases and elevating the status of serfs.

### Iconoclasm and Conflict with the Papacy
Leo's most consequential policy was iconoclasm ("icon-breaking"). Citing a need to reject idolatry, he issued edicts (726–729) banning the veneration of icons. This provoked revolts, notably in Greece, and fierce opposition from the Church. Patriarch Germanus I resigned rather than comply. The policy created a major rift with the Papacy; Popes Gregory II and III condemned iconoclasm. In retaliation, Leo transferred papal jurisdictions in Southern Italy and Illyricum to the Patriarch of Constantinople, weakening papal authority and Byzantine control in Italy.

### Analysis of Iconoclasm
Scholars debate Leo's motivations. Some suggest the influence of aniconism from his Syrian homeland or the contemporary Umayyad Caliphate. Others, like historian Marie-France Auzépy, argue he was motivated by a desire to avert God's wrath, interpreted after a volcanic eruption in 726, by rejecting idolatry. A revisionist view by Brubaker and Haldon proposes Leo's policy was a moderate critique of public image use, not systematic destruction, and that the narrative of widespread persecution is a later "iconophile myth."

## Terms
- **Iconoclasm**: The religious and political movement opposing the veneration of icons (religious images), initiated by Leo III.
- **Ekloge ton nomon**: A legal code published in 741 that revised Justinian's laws, reflecting Leo's reforms.
- **Stratēgos**: The military governor of a Byzantine theme (province).
- **Greek fire**: An incendiary weapon used by the Byzantine navy, crucial in defending Constantinople.
- **Theme**: A military-administrative province of the Byzantine Empire.
- **Silention**: A restricted council transformed by Leo into a special assembly for imperial announcements.
- **Iconodule**: A defender of the veneration of icons (an "icon-worshipper").
- **Miliarēsion**: A silver coin introduced by Leo III, worth one-twelfth of a gold nomisma.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The "Isaurian" nickname**: Its origin is disputed—possibly a textual error, a deliberate delegitimization, or based on parental background.
- **Motivations for iconoclasm**: Scholars debate whether it was driven by Syrian aniconism, Islamic influence, a theological response to divine punishment (as per Auzépy), or was a more moderate policy than later sources claim.
- **Scale of persecution**: Brubaker and Haldon argue against the traditional view of systematic imperial destruction of images under Leo, suggesting the "iconophile myth" was constructed later.
- **Impact on Italy**: Leo's attempts to reassert imperial authority in Italy largely failed, and his policies weakened Byzantine control, contributing to the eventual separation of the Papacy from the Empire.
