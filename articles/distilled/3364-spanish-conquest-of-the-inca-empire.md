# Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire

## Overview
The Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire (1532–1572) was the pivotal campaign that dismantled the largest pre-Columbian civilization in the Americas, leading to Spanish colonization and the creation of the Viceroyalty of Peru. It was enabled by a devastating civil war within the Inca Empire, superior Spanish military technology, crucial alliances with indigenous groups, and the catastrophic impact of Old World diseases.

## Timeline
- **1528** — Emperor Huayna Capac dies, likely from smallpox, triggering a civil war between his sons Huáscar and Atahualpa.
- **1532** — Francisco Pizarro and 168 Spanish soldiers arrive in Peru.
- **Nov 16, 1532** — Battle of Cajamarca: Pizarro ambushes and captures the Inca emperor Atahualpa.
- **1533** — Atahualpa offers a ransom of gold and silver. He is later executed by the Spanish.
- **1533** — Pizarro captures the Inca capital of Cusco.
- **1535** — Manco Inca, initially a Spanish ally, rebels and besieges Cusco.
- **1538** — Battle of Las Salinas: Pizarro defeats his former ally Diego de Almagro.
- **1541** — Pizarro is assassinated by supporters of Almagro.
- **1572** — The last Inca emperor, Túpac Amaru, is captured and executed, ending the Neo-Inca State.

## Body

### The Inca Empire on the Eve of Conquest
By 1528, the Inca Empire (Tawantinsuyu) was the largest pre-Columbian civilization, spanning from modern Colombia to Chile. It had expanded rapidly from about 400,000 km² in 1448 to 1,800,000 km² in 1528, with an estimated population of 12–16 million. This vast, diverse realm was governed through a centralized administrative system where local leaders were monitored by Inca officials, all answering to the Sapa Inca (emperor). However, its size made it difficult to administer, and many subject peoples were only reluctantly part of the empire.

### Civil War and Spanish Arrival
The death of Emperor Huayna Capac and his heir from disease in 1528 sparked a civil war between his sons, Huáscar and Atahualpa. Atahualpa, with the loyalty of the northern army, eventually defeated Huáscar. This conflict fatally weakened the empire just as Pizarro arrived in 1532. The Spanish were aided by indigenous groups like the Huanca and Cañari, who sought revenge or independence from Inca rule. At Cajamarca, Pizarro's small force, using cavalry, firearms, and steel, ambushed Atahualpa's much larger but unarmed retinue, capturing the emperor in a battle that killed about 2,000 Incas.

### Capture, Ransom, and Execution of Atahualpa
During his captivity, Atahualpa offered a vast ransom of gold and silver to secure his release. The Spanish accepted, collecting the treasure by May 1533, but Pizarro had no intention of freeing him. Under pressure from other Spaniards and based on mistranslations, Atahualpa was tried on charges including polygamy and idolatry. He was forced to convert to Christianity and was garrotted on August 29, 1533.

### Rebellion and Final Conquest
After Atahualpa's death, the Spanish installed puppet rulers. Manco Inca, initially allied with the Spanish, rebelled in 1535 after being mistreated by Pizarro's brothers. He besieged Cusco in 1536 but was ultimately unsuccessful. Manco Inca retreated to Vilcabamba, establishing a Neo-Inca State that resisted for decades. The conquest was prolonged by internal Spanish conflicts, notably the civil war between Pizarro and Diego de Almagro, which ended with Almagro's execution in 1538 and Pizarro's assassination in 1541. The Neo-Inca State was finally crushed in 1572 with the execution of Túpac Amaru.

### Aftermath and Demographic Catastrophe
The Spanish consolidated control by founding Lima (1535) and establishing the Viceroyalty of Peru (1542). The indigenous population suffered a catastrophic decline, estimated from 10–15 million in 1520 to less than 3 million by 1570, primarily due to Old World diseases like smallpox and measles. Spanish colonization involved forced labor (mita), the imposition of Christianity, and the exploitation of resources, especially silver from Potosí, which caused significant environmental damage through mercury pollution.

## Terms
- **Sapa Inca**: The absolute ruler or emperor of the Inca Empire.
- **Tawantinsuyu**: The Quechua name for the Inca Empire, meaning "The Four Regions Together."
- **Conquistadors**: Spanish soldiers and explorers who conquered parts of the Americas in the 16th century.
- **Mita**: An Inca system of mandatory public service or labor tax, later adapted by the Spanish for mining.
- **Viceroyalty of Peru**: A Spanish colonial administrative division established in 1542 to govern the region.
- **Neo-Inca State**: The remnant Inca government based in Vilcabamba that resisted Spanish rule until 1572.
- **Chasqui**: An Inca messenger who ran along the empire's road system to relay information.
- **Requerimiento**: A Spanish legal document read to indigenous peoples, demanding their submission to the Spanish Crown and the Catholic Church.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Pre-existing Decline**: Some scholars argue the Inca Empire was already in decline before Spanish arrival due to internal strife and the administrative challenges of its vast size.
- **Disease Impact**: There is debate over whether the initial epidemic that struck the Inca in the 1520s was smallpox or another disease like Carrion's disease.
- **Role of Indigenous Allies**: The critical role of indigenous groups who allied with the Spanish is emphasized, challenging narratives of a simple Spanish vs. Inca conflict.
- **Long-term Population Decline**: The precise causes and scale of the demographic collapse are studied, with factors including disease, warfare, forced labor, and mercury poisoning from mining.
