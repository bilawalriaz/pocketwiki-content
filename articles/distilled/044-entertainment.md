# Entertainment

Entertainment is any activity that holds the attention and interest of an audience or gives pleasure and delight. It ranges from private, pre-recorded products to global performances, and includes storytelling, music, drama, dance, games, and sport. Though often associated with amusement, entertainment can also serve serious purposes such as ceremony, satire, or intellectual insight. A key feature is the audience, which transforms private recreation into entertainment; audiences may be passive (watching a play) or active (playing a game). Most forms have persisted for centuries, evolving with cultural, technological, and fashion changes, while some once-popular forms (e.g., public executions) are now illegal.

## Timeline

- **c. 1490** — William Caxton's usage of "entertain" recorded (Oxford English Dictionary).
- **1560** — Pieter Bruegel the Elder paints *Children's Games*, depicting typical children's games.
- **1595** — Queen Elizabeth I's Accession Day celebrations feature tournaments and jousting.
- **18th century** — Fireworks most popular; skilled Italian pyrotechnists summoned abroad.
- **1840** — Dickens and Thackeray write about a Newgate Prison hanging, calling executions "obscene entertainments."
- **1878** — Paris Exposition facilitates international cooperation on ideas and standards.
- **1935** — Ireland's Public Dance Hall Act bans certain popular dance forms.
- **1940s–1950s** — Radio gives way to television as the dominant family entertainment medium.
- **2000** — Fireworks displays mark the millennium worldwide at landmarks like Sydney Harbour Bridge and the Eiffel Tower.
- **2011** — Wii console ownership estimated at over 30% of US households, comparable to TV ownership in 1953.

## Body

### Etymology and psychology

The Oxford English Dictionary traces "entertain" to Latin *inter* (among) + *tenir* (to hold), meaning "to hold mutually" or "to engage, keep occupied." Psychologists say the function of media entertainment is "the attainment of gratification," with no other measurable benefit expected—unlike education (designed for understanding) or marketing (designed to encourage purchase). These distinctions blur in "edutainment" or "infotainment." Entertainment may also produce insight, exploring philosophical questions like "What does it mean to be human?" Examples include Shakespeare's *Hamlet*, the film *The Matrix*, and *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy*, which began as radio comedy and expanded into novels, film, games, and more.

### History and court entertainment

Storytelling is the ancient craft of communicating events using words, images, sounds, and gestures, passing on cultural values and history. Many entertainments evolved from serious activities: pole vaulting may have originated in the Netherlands for crossing canals or in warfare; gladiatorial combats combined sport, punishment, and entertainment. Social upheavals shaped entertainment—Revolutionary opera was sanctioned during China's Cultural Revolution, and World War I, the Great Depression, and the Russian Revolution all had effects. The Colosseum, dedicated AD 80, held 50,000 spectators for blood sport and stage shows.

Imperial and royal courts trained professional entertainers. Court entertainment often moved to commoners, as with Korean masked dance-dramas, which originated in shaman rituals. Court ceremonies demonstrated wealth and power, reinforcing the relationship between ruler and ruled. At Versailles, thousands of courtiers acted as both performers and spectators. Public punishment was once popular entertainment; a hanging was described as "a carnival," lasting until the 19th century when writers like Dickens and Thackeray condemned executions as "obscene entertainments."

### Children and forms of entertainment

Children's entertainment centres on play, mimicking adult activities and developing skills. Psychologists G. Stanley Hall and Jean Piaget established that children's capacities differ from adults, leading to content and rating systems designed for child audiences. In the 21st century, screen-based entertainment has drawn criticism for its effects on imagination and psychological well-being.

Banquets have been venues for amusement since ancient times, used to impress visitors and showcase music or dance. Music is a universal entertainment, divided into genres like classical, jazz, folk, rock, and pop; since the 20th century, pre-recorded music has been available cheaply. Games range from board games like Senet (played in Ancient Egypt) to video games, which expanded enormously in the late 20th century. Literature became a mass entertainment with printing and rising literacy; comics, including Japanese Manga, and caricature are graphic genres. Comedy, from Shakespeare's seventeen comedies to slapstick, provides laughter across cultures, though national nuances can make humour unintelligible elsewhere.

Performance includes theatre, opera, and masques (complex 16th–17th century court entertainments). Storytelling underlies film, drama, ballet, and opera; the Scheherazade tale inspired orchestral works by Rimsky-Korsakov, Ravel, and Szymanowski, a Pasolini film, and a video game. Theatre dates to Hellenistic times; Aristotle and Plato wrote on its purpose. Cinema became a global business from its start—the Lumière brothers sent cameramen worldwide—and CGI in the 21st century made spectacle cheaper. Dance combines music, movement, and storytelling; ballet moved from the French court to theatres under Louis XIV. Some dances were banned by political regimes: Nazis banned swing as "un-German," and the US banned dances associated with African Americans. Animals have entertained for millennia, from Roman lion and bear performances to horse and camel racing; blood sports like bear-baiting and cockfighting are now illegal. The circus, founded by Philip Astley in the 18th century, features acrobatics and juggling. Stage magic relies on deception and sleight of hand; Harry Potter books sold about 450 million copies by June 2011. Street performance (busking) has three forms: circle shows, walk-by acts, and café busking. Parades, from Roman triumphs to religious processions, entertain audiences, though pilgrimages like the Hajj are spiritual journeys, not entertainment. Fireworks, first used militarily in China, became a "crowning feature" of celebrations; Handel's *Music for the Royal Fireworks* remains popular. Sport entertains crowds; association football and cricket have global audiences via the FIFA World Cup and Cricket World Cup, while local sports like sumo wrestling in Japan and archery in Bhutan reflect culture.

### Industry, architecture, and electronic media

Entertainment is big business, especially in the US. The 20th-century entertainment industry packages and sells performances; components include Hollywood and Bollywood film industries and the sex industry. Amusement parks entertain with rides and themed "lands." New jobs emerged (gaffer, special effects supervisor), and prestigious awards recognise excellence.

Purpose-built venues include Greek theatres, Roman circuses (oval stadia), the Sydney Opera House (a World Heritage Site), and the Bayreuth Festspielhaus (designed for one composition). Key design concerns are speed of egress and safety—the Hillsborough disaster showed how poor design contributes to deaths. Some architecture is itself entertainment: the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao by Frank Gehry attracts tourists, while "follies" are decorative, non-functional buildings. Tourism can re-purpose buildings as attractions, blurring "historical authenticity and contemporary entertainment venues."

Electronic media enabled global delivery of entertainment by the second half of the 20th century. Radio (1940s) gave way to television (1950s), then electronic games (1970s), handheld devices, and networked play. Recording methods rapidly became obsolete; by 2011, Wii ownership matched 1953 TV ownership. Digital convergence challenges film industry practices—success is no longer determined by opening weekend but by exhibition "windows" like DVD and video-on-demand. User-generated content creates a "networked audience model" that makes programming obsolete. Despite technological change, the forms themselves—storytelling, music, theatre, dance, games—remain recognisably stable.

## Terms

- **Edutainment/infotainment**: Mixtures of education or information with entertainment.
- **Schadenfreude**: Pleasure derived from others' pain or unhappiness.
- **Masque**: Complex 16th–17th century court theatrical entertainment with dancing, singing, and acting.
- **Stagecraft**: The skill of managing, organising, and preparing a stage for performance.
- **CGI (computer-generated imagery)**: Digital technology for creating realistic images and spectacle in film.
- **Busking**: Street performance or entertainment for the public.
- **Circle show**: A street performance form that gathers a crowd with a distinct beginning and end.
- **Follies**: Buildings deliberately constructed for decoration, never intended for practical use.
- **Media convergence**: The merging of analogue and digital recording and delivery methods, with cultural as well as technological effects.

## Debates and open questions

- Whether entertainment can be distinguished from education and marketing, given that these fields use entertainment's appeal for their own goals.
- Whether some activities are entertainment or cruelty: hunting, fox hunting, bullfighting, and pigeon shooting are variously regarded as sport, entertainment, or cultural tradition, with animal advocacy groups on both sides of cultural claims.
- The negative effects of screen-based entertainment on children's imagination, adult cognition, and psychological well-being, including the "remarkable collapse of children's engagement with nature."
- Whether banning dances (e.g., Nazi Germany, Ireland, the US) reduces or increases their popularity—banning often made "the dance craze" greater.
- Whether online entertainment would completely replace television—expected by some by the mid-2010s, which did not happen.
