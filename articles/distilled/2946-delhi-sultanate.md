# Delhi Sultanate

## Overview
The Delhi Sultanate was a late medieval Islamic empire based in Delhi that ruled large parts of the Indian subcontinent for over three centuries (1206–1526). It was established by Turkic slave-generals of the Ghurid dynasty and comprised five successive dynasties. The Sultanate integrated the subcontinent into international Islamic networks, fostered the development of Indo-Islamic architecture and the Hindustani language, and was one of the few powers to repel Mongol invasions. Its rule ended with the Mughal conquest in 1526.

## Timeline
- **1192** — Muhammad of Ghor defeats the Rajput Confederacy at the Second Battle of Tarain.
- **1206** — Qutb ud-Din Aibak, a former slave of Muhammad of Ghor, becomes the first Sultan, founding the Mamluk dynasty.
- **1236–1240** — Razia Sultana rules, one of the few female rulers in Islamic history.
- **1290** — Jalal ud-Din Firuz Khalji seizes power, ending the Mamluk dynasty and founding the Khalji dynasty.
- **c. 1320** — Muhammad bin Tughluq's reign begins; the Sultanate reaches its greatest geographical extent.
- **1398** — Timur (Tamerlane) invades and sacks Delhi, causing massive destruction and leaving the Sultanate in anarchy.
- **1414** — Khizr Khan founds the Sayyid dynasty, ruling as a Timurid vassal.
- **1451** — Bahlul Khan Lodi founds the Lodi dynasty, restoring some of Delhi's influence.
- **1526** — Babur defeats Ibrahim Lodi at the Battle of Panipat, ending the Sultanate and establishing the Mughal Empire.

## Body

### Foundation and Early Dynasties
The Sultanate's foundation was laid by the Ghurid conqueror Muhammad of Ghor, who defeated the Rajput Confederacy in 1192. After his assassination in 1206, his Turkic slave-general Qutb ud-Din Aibak assumed power, establishing the Mamluk dynasty. The early sultans, particularly Iltutmish, consolidated control through conquest and by eliminating rival claimants to the Ghurid legacy. The Mamluk period saw the construction of iconic structures like the Qutb Minar complex. The dynasty ended when the Khalji general Jalal ud-Din Firuz Khalji assassinated the last Mamluk ruler in 1290.

### Khalji and Tughlaq Expansion
The Khalji dynasty, of Turko-Afghan heritage, pursued aggressive expansion. Ala ud-Din Khalji extended the Sultanate into Gujarat, Malwa, Rajputana, and South India, using slave generals like Malik Kafur. He implemented strict price controls and market regulations to fund his army. The subsequent Tughlaq dynasty, founded by Ghiyath al-Din Tughlaq, saw the Sultanate's peak under Muhammad bin Tughluq, who ruled most of the subcontinent. His reign was marked by ambitious but often disastrous policies, including a forced capital shift to Daulatabad and the minting of base-metal coins, which led to economic collapse, famine, and widespread rebellion. By the end of his reign, regional kingdoms like the Bahmani and Vijayanagara had asserted independence.

### Decline and Fall
The death of Firuz Shah Tughlaq in 1388 led to anarchy and civil war. This weakness invited the devastating invasion of Timur in 1398, which sacked Delhi and left the Sultanate in ruins. The subsequent Sayyid dynasty ruled as Timurid vassals over a shrunken territory. The Lodi dynasty, an Afghan line, temporarily restored some power but was undermined by internal strife. The last Sultan, Ibrahim Lodi, was defeated and killed by the Mughal invader Babur in 1526.

### Government, Economy, and Society
The Sultanate's political system was a Persianate model centralized to mobilize resources against Mongol and Hindu rivals. The sultans presented themselves as paramount sovereigns, often accepting Hindu officials and vassals while levying the discriminatory *jizya* tax on non-Muslims. Economically, rulers like Ala ud-Din Khalji involved the state heavily in the economy through price controls and market regulation. The period saw significant cultural synthesis, with the emergence of the Hindustani language and a distinctive Indo-Islamic architecture that blended Central Asian styles (domes, arches) with Indian craftsmanship. The Sultanate's military, initially based on Turkic Mamluk slaves, successfully repelled Mongol invasions.

### Architecture and Cultural Impact
Indo-Islamic architecture evolved from early structures like the Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque, which reused materials from demolished temples, to the development of true domes and arches. The Tughlaq period introduced a more austere, fortress-like style. The Sultanate facilitated the migration of soldiers, scholars, and artists from Central Asia, establishing Islamic culture in the subcontinent. This led to a new, mixed cultural identity, though it was accompanied by episodes of temple desecration and destruction of Hindu and Buddhist institutions, as documented in historical records.

## Terms
- **Mamluk**: A slave soldier, often of Turkic origin, who could rise to positions of great power. The first dynasty of the Sultanate was founded by such a slave-general.
- **Jizya**: A discriminatory tax levied on non-Muslim subjects in an Islamic state.
- **Indo-Islamic architecture**: A style blending Central Asian Islamic forms (domes, arches) with Indian craftsmanship and motifs, pioneered during the Sultanate.
- **Hindustani (Hindavi)**: The early form of the Hindustani language that emerged as a lingua franca during the Sultanate period.
- **Corps of Forty**: A council of powerful Turkic nobles who acted as kingmakers in the early Sultanate.
- **Sultan**: The title for an Islamic sovereign, used by the rulers of Delhi.
- **Wajih**: Elite household cavalry archers of Turkic slave background forming the core of the Sultanate's army.
- **Anwatan/Ghanima**: Taxes on war booty collected from conquered territories.

## Debates and open questions
- The extent and intentionality of the Sultanate's temple destruction campaigns are debated. Historian Richard Eaton documents 37 instances of desecration but notes this was not unique to Muslim rulers in medieval India, and that sultans also sometimes patronized or repaired temples.
- The economic impact of policies like Ala ud-Din Khalji's price controls is debated; while they strengthened the treasury, they also caused resentment and may have stifled trade.
- The claim of Sayyid descent from the Prophet Muhammad by the founders of the Sayyid dynasty is considered doubtful by modern historians.
