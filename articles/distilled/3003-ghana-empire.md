# Ghana Empire

## Overview
The Ghana Empire (c. 300–1200 CE) was a powerful West African state in the western Sahel, controlling trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt. Its wealth and influence made it a major political and economic force, and its legacy influenced later empires like Mali. The empire's origins, peak, and decline are subjects of ongoing scholarly debate, with evidence drawn from oral traditions, medieval Arab writers, and archaeology.

## Timeline
- **c. 300 BCE – 300 CE** — Proto-Mande agro-pastoralist chiefdoms flourish in the Tichitt region.
- **c. 300 CE** — A wet period in the Sahel and the introduction of the camel catalyze the formation of Wagadu (Ghana).
- **830** — First identifiable mention of the Ghana dynasty by al-Khwārizmī.
- **990** — Ghana conquers the trade city of Awdaghust.
- **1054** — Awdaghust falls to the Almoravids.
- **1063–1076** — Reign of Tunka Manin, described in detail by al-Bakri.
- **c. 1076** — Traditional (but debated) date for Almoravid conquest; Ghana converts to Islam.
- **1154** — Al-Idrisi describes a powerful, fully Muslim Ghana.
- **1203** — The Sosso people conquer Ghana.
- **1235** — Sundiata Keita of Mali defeats the Sosso; Ghana becomes a Mali vassal.
- **15th century** — Koumbi Saleh, the capital, is abandoned.

## Body

### Origins and Etymology
The empire's Soninke name was Wagadu, while "Ghana" was the title for its ruler, meaning "warrior" or "war chief." Oral traditions trace its founding to the legendary figure Dinga, who defeated a serpent deity named Bida. Modern scholarship, based on archaeology at sites like Dhar Tichitt, favors a local origin, suggesting the state emerged from ancient Mande agro-pastoralist societies around 300 BCE, aided by the camel's introduction and ironworking.

### First Apogee and Arab Records
By the 8th century, Arab writers described Ghana as a wealthy "Land of Gold." Its power rested on a monopoly over the trans-Saharan trade of gold (from the south) and salt (from the north), though it did not control the gold mines directly. The capital, likely Koumbi Saleh, was a major commercial hub. The king, or *Kaya Maghan* ("king of gold"), maintained a powerful court and a confederated state of vassal kingdoms.

### Almoravid Influence and Second Apogee
The Almoravid Berber movement from North Africa impacted Ghana in the 11th century. While a full military conquest in 1076–77 is now doubted by scholars, the Almoravids likely exerted political pressure. Ghana subsequently converted to Islam, which may have caused internal displacement. A 12th-century resurgence is described by al-Idrisi, who noted Ghana's extensive trade and fully Islamic judicial system.

### Decline and Fall
Ghana's decline was gradual, linked to the drying of the Sahel, which shifted trade routes south and west, strengthening its vassals. The Sosso people, a former vassal, conquered Ghana around 1203. Later, Sundiata Keita of the Mali Empire defeated the Sosso in 1235. Ghana then became a vassal state of Mali, retaining some prestige but losing its independence. Its capital was abandoned in the 15th century.

### Economy, Government, and Society
The economy was based on taxing trade, especially salt, while gold nuggets were royal property. The government was a confederacy with a central core and vassal states. Kingship was matrilineal, passing to the son of the king's sister. Society was structured around patrilineal clans. By the 11th century, Ghana had higher learning institutions, and Muslim officials played key roles in the court and commerce.

## Terms
- **Wagadu**: The Soninke name for the Ghana Empire, meaning "place of the Wague" (nobility) or "land of great herds."
- **Kaya Maghan**: Title for the ruler of Ghana, meaning "king of gold."
- **Soninke**: The dominant ethnic group of the Ghana Empire, part of the larger Mande peoples.
- **Trans-Saharan trade**: The network of trade routes across the Sahara Desert, crucial for the exchange of gold, salt, and other goods.
- **Vassal state**: A state that owes allegiance and tribute to a more powerful empire.
- **Matrilineal descent**: A system of kinship where lineage and inheritance are traced through the mother's line.
- **Almoravids**: A Berber-Muslim dynasty from North Africa that influenced the western Sahel in the 11th century.
- **Kafu**: A term for the territorial units or vassal kingdoms within the Ghana Empire.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origins**: Scholars debate whether Ghana's ruling dynasty was of local Soninke origin or foreign (e.g., Berber), with modern archaeology favoring a local development.
- **Almoravid Conquest**: The traditional narrative of a decisive Almoravid military conquest in 1076–77 is highly contested; evidence suggests political agitation rather than a full invasion.
- **Capital Location**: The identification of Koumbi Saleh as the capital described by al-Bakri is disputed, with some scholars suggesting al-Idrisi described a different, later capital near a river.
- **Decline**: The causes of Ghana's decline are multifaceted, involving environmental change (Sahel drying), economic shifts, and pressure from the Almoravids, Sosso, and finally Mali.
