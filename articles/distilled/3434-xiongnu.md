# Xiongnu

## Overview
The Xiongnu were a powerful nomadic confederation dominating the eastern Eurasian Steppe from the 3rd century BC to the late 1st century AD. Founded by Modu Chanyu, they were a major rival to China's Han dynasty, engaging in war, tribute, and diplomacy. Their legacy includes a split into Northern and Southern groups; the latter assimilated into China, with descendants founding states during the Sixteen Kingdoms period.

## Timeline
- **c. 209 BC** — Modu Chanyu unites the Xiongnu into a powerful confederation.
- **200 BC** — Modu besieges Han Emperor Gaozu, leading to the first heqin (marriage alliance) treaty.
- **133–119 BC** — Major Han-Xiongnu wars under Emperor Wu push the Xiongnu north of the Gobi.
- **60–53 BC** — Xiongnu civil war leads to a permanent split into Northern and Southern Xiongnu.
- **53 BC** — Southern Xiongnu chanyu Huhanye submits to Han tributary relations.
- **48 AD** — Southern Xiongnu establish a separate kingdom under Han protection.
- **89 AD** — Han general Dou Xian crushes the Northern Xiongnu at the Battle of the Altai Mountains.
- **216 AD** — Cao Cao reorganizes the Southern Xiongnu into Five Divisions near Taiyuan.
- **304 AD** — Liu Yuan, a Xiongnu noble, founds the Han-Zhao dynasty, initiating the Sixteen Kingdoms period.
- **319–351 AD** — The Later Zhao dynasty, founded by the Jie (a Xiongnu-related group), rules northern China.
- **407–431 AD** — The Helian Xia dynasty, founded by the Tiefu tribe, is established in the Ordos region.
- **439 AD** — Northern Liang, the last of the Sixteen Kingdoms (founded by the Lushuihu), falls to the Northern Wei.

## Body

### Origins and State Formation
The Xiongnu emerged in the Mongolian Plateau, with the Slab Grave Culture as their primary ancestor. Genetic research indicates complex mixture with West Eurasian populations. The first known leader, Touman, was defeated by the Qin dynasty in 215 BC. His son, Modu Chanyu, overthrew him in 209 BC and forged the Xiongnu into a formidable empire through military innovation, crushing rivals like the Donghu and Yuezhi. This new power threatened the Han dynasty, leading to the first major confrontation at the Battle of Baideng in 200 BC.

### Relations with the Han Dynasty
Relations were defined by a cycle of war and diplomacy. After initial defeats, the Han adopted heqin, sending "princesses" and gifts to secure peace. This treaty system lasted for decades but was often violated by Xiongnu raids. In the 2nd century BC, Emperor Wu launched major campaigns, pushing the Xiongnu north and securing the Hexi Corridor. Following a civil war (60–53 BC), the Southern Xiongnu chanyu Huhanye submitted to Han tributary status in 53 BC, receiving subsidies and resettling his people within Han borders. The Southern Xiongnu served as frontier auxiliaries but were later reorganized by the warlord Cao Cao.

### The Northern Xiongnu
The rump Northern Xiongnu state continued to raid the Han and control the Western Regions. They were severely weakened by famines and attacks from the Xianbei. A major Han expedition in 89 AD, followed by another in 91 AD, shattered their power. The Northern Chanyu fled northwest, and his people were largely absorbed by the Xianbei or surrendered to the Han. Remnants held out in the Tarim Basin until the mid-2nd century AD.

### Descendant States and Assimilation
The Southern Xiongnu, settled within China, eventually rebelled during the late Han turmoil. In 304 AD, their leader Liu Yuan founded the Han-Zhao dynasty, claiming descent from Han emperors through heqin marriages. This began the Sixteen Kingdoms period. Other Xiongnu-related states included the Later Zhao (founded by the Jie), the Helian Xia (founded by the Tiefu tribe), and the Northern Liang (founded by the Lushuihu). After the Northern Wei unified northern China in 439, the Xiongnu gradually assimilated, though some groups like the Jihu persisted into the Tang dynasty.

### Ethnolinguistic Identity and Genetics
The ethnic and linguistic identity of the Xiongnu elite is heavily debated, with proposed links to Turkic, Mongolic, Yeniseian, or Iranian languages. Genetic studies reveal a heterogeneous population: maternal lineages are predominantly East Asian, while paternal lineages show a significant mix of East Asian and West Eurasian haplogroups. High-status individuals tended to have more East Eurasian ancestry, while lower-status retainers showed greater West Eurasian admixture. This supports the view of the Xiongnu as a multi-ethnic confederation.

## Terms
- **Chanyu**: The supreme ruler of the Xiongnu, equivalent to an emperor.
- **Heqin**: A Chinese diplomatic policy of forming marriage alliances with nomadic groups, often involving sending princesses and gifts to secure peace.
- **Tributary relations**: A system where a state acknowledges the superiority of another (here, the Han) by sending tribute and hostages in exchange for gifts and political legitimacy.
- **Five Divisions**: The administrative reorganization of the Southern Xiongnu into five tribes by Cao Cao in 216 AD to control them.
- **Sixteen Kingdoms**: A period of political fragmentation in northern China (304–439 AD) during which several states, including those founded by Xiongnu descendants, were established.
- **Slab Grave Culture**: An archaeological culture in Mongolia considered the primary ancestor of the Xiongnu.
- **Yeniseian**: A language family spoken in Siberia; one proposed linguistic affiliation for the Xiongnu, particularly the Jie subgroup.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ethnolinguistic Origin**: There is no consensus on the language spoken by the Xiongnu elite. Theories propose Turkic, Mongolic, Yeniseian, or Iranian origins, with many scholars now viewing them as a multi-ethnic confederation.
- **Relationship to the Huns**: The hypothesis that the European Huns descended from the Xiongnu is widely discussed but not definitively proven. Sogdian documents refer to both by the same name (γwn), suggesting a connection.
- **Geographic Origin**: The exact homeland of the Xiongnu before their rise is disputed, with no archaeological mortuary practices providing a clear match.
- **Status and Ethnicity**: Genetic studies suggest a correlation between social status and ancestry within the Xiongnu, with high-status individuals showing more East Eurasian heritage, indicating a complex social structure.
