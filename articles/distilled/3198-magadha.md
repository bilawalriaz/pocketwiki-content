# Magadha

## Overview
Magadha was an ancient kingdom and region in the eastern Ganges Plain of India, corresponding to modern-day Bihar. It was one of the sixteen Mahajanapadas (great kingdoms) during the Second Urbanization period (c. 500 BCE). Magadha's political power grew to overshadow and conquer other kingdoms, forming the core of several major Indian empires. It was also the cultural and religious heartland where Buddhism and Jainism originated, making it central to the development of Indian history and philosophy.

## Timeline
- **c. 1200–900 BCE** — Magadhas listed as a non-Vedic tribe in the Atharvaveda.
- **c. 544–413 BCE** — Haryanka dynasty rules; kings Bimbisara and Ajatashatru are contemporaries of the Buddha and Mahavira.
- **c. 500 BCE onwards** — Second Urbanization and the rise of Śramaṇic religions (Buddhism, Jainism) in Greater Magadha.
- **c. 345–322 BCE** — Nanda dynasty conquers much of north India.
- **c. 322–185 BCE** — Chandragupta Maurya overthrows the Nandas, founding the Maurya Empire.
- **185–73 BCE** — Shunga Empire rules after the Mauryas.
- **73–28 BCE** — Kanva dynasty rules.
- **c. 240–c. 579 CE** — Gupta Empire, a period of classical Indian culture.
- **c. 6th–8th century CE** — Later Gupta dynasty rules Magadha after the imperial Guptas' decline.
- **11th–late 13th century** — Buddhist Pithipati kings rule parts of Magadha.

## Body

### Geography and Early History
The core Magadha kingdom was bounded by the Gaṅgā, Son, and Campā rivers, and the Vindhya mountains, corresponding to modern Patna and Gaya districts. "Greater Magadha" included a wider eastern Gangetic plain region with a distinct culture. In the Vedic period, the Magadhas were considered a non-Vedic tribe on the periphery of Brahmanical culture. The legendary Brihadratha dynasty is cited as the first ruling line.

### Rise to Power and Imperial Core
Magadha's power grew during the Mahajanapada period. The Haryanka dynasty (c. 544–413 BCE) saw expansion under Bimbisara and Ajatashatru, who conquered the Vajjika League and Anga. The kingdom's capitals were Rajagriha (Rajgir) and later Pataliputra (Patna). After internal strife, the Nanda dynasty (c. 345–322 BCE) unified much of north India. This was overthrown by Chandragupta Maurya, establishing the Maurya Empire (322–185 BCE), which made Magadha the center of a pan-Indian empire. Subsequent dynasties like the Shungas and later the Guptas (c. 240–c. 579 CE) also used Magadha as their power base.

### Religious and Cultural Significance
Magadha was the nerve center of the Śramaṇa movement, a wave of heterodox traditions rejecting Vedic authority. The culture of "Greater Magadha" placed Kshatriyas above Brahmins and emphasized asceticism and meditation for liberation from rebirth. Gautama Buddha lived and taught in Magadha, attaining enlightenment at Bodh Gaya and holding his first council at Rajgriha. Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara of Jainism, was also a contemporary. These religions received royal patronage from Magadhan kings like Bimbisara and the Nanda dynasty. Key Buddhist sites include the Mahabodhi Temple (a UNESCO site) and the Nalanda monastery.

### Language and Legacy
The Pali language, used in Theravada Buddhist texts, is identified with Magadhi Prakrit, the language of the region. Magadhi Prakrit later evolved into modern eastern Indo-Aryan languages like Magahi, Maithili, and Bhojpuri. Magadha's legacy is profound, having incubated major world religions and served as the political nucleus for successive empires that shaped the Indian subcontinent.

## Terms
- **Mahajanapada**: One of the sixteen "great realms" or kingdoms in ancient India during the 6th–4th centuries BCE.
- **Second Urbanization**: The process of large-scale urban development in the Gangetic plains from c. 500 BCE, leading to the rise of states and new religious movements.
- **Śramaṇa**: A tradition of wandering ascetics and philosophers in ancient India who rejected Vedic authority, emphasizing personal liberation through ascetic practices.
- **Prakrit**: A category of Middle Indo-Aryan languages, including Magadhi Prakrit, that evolved from Vedic Sanskrit and were used in everyday speech and literature.
- **Tirthankara**: A savior and spiritual teacher in Jainism who has conquered the cycle of death and rebirth.
- **Stupa**: A hemispherical structure used as a Buddhist shrine or reliquary mound.
- **Kshatriya**: The warrior and ruling varna (social class) in the traditional Hindu caste system.
- **Brahmin**: The priestly and scholarly varna in the traditional Hindu caste system.

## Debates and Open questions
- **Chronology**: There is significant uncertainty about the exact dates of Magadhan rulers. Scholars debate between a "Long Chronology" and a "Short Chronology," which is linked to the uncertain lifespans of the Buddha and Mahavira.
- **Kikata Identification**: Whether the Kikata tribe mentioned in the Rigveda are the forefathers of the Magadhas is debated. Some scholars identify them, while others argue the Rigvedic description places the Kikatas far to the west of Magadha.
- **Cultural Distinctiveness**: The extent and nature of the distinct "Greater Magadha" culture, as proposed by scholars like Johannes Bronkhorst, and its fundamental differences from Vedic culture, remains a subject of scholarly discussion.
