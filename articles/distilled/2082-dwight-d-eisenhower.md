# Dwight D. Eisenhower

## Overview
Dwight D. Eisenhower (1890–1969) was the 34th U.S. president (1953–1961) and a General of the Army who commanded Allied forces in Europe during World War II. His presidency is defined by Cold War containment, domestic prosperity, and the creation of the Interstate Highway System. Historians now rank him among the top-tier of U.S. presidents for his steady leadership and prescient warning about the "military-industrial complex."

## Body

### Early Life and Military Career
Born to a family facing financial hardship, Eisenhower developed an interest in military history from his mother's book collection. He attended West Point (class of 1915) and served in staff and training roles during World War I, never seeing combat. Between the wars, he served under generals like Fox Conner and Douglas MacArthur, mastering military theory and logistics. His 1935–1939 assignment in the Philippines, though marked by friction with MacArthur, honed his diplomatic skills for managing Allied coalition politics in WWII.

### World War II Leadership
Eisenhower's rapid rise began after Pearl Harbor, where he drafted war plans. He commanded Operation Torch in North Africa (1942) and the invasion of Sicily. As Supreme Allied Commander in Europe (1943–1945), he planned and executed the D-Day invasion of Normandy (June 6, 1944). He managed complex alliances with figures like Churchill and Montgomery, and made the controversial decision not to capture Berlin, leaving it to the Soviets. He documented Nazi concentration camps to prevent future denial of the Holocaust.

### Path to the Presidency
After the war, Eisenhower served as military governor of occupied Germany, Army Chief of Staff, and president of Columbia University (1948–1953). He became the first Supreme Commander of NATO (1951–1952). Entering the 1952 presidential race as a Republican, he defeated Senator Robert A. Taft for the nomination, partly to protect NATO and the New Deal. He won the presidency in a landslide, promising to contain communism and balance the budget.

### Domestic Policy
Eisenhower practiced "dynamic conservatism," preserving and expanding core New Deal programs like Social Security. He signed the Civil Rights Acts of 1957 and 1960 and sent federal troops to enforce school integration in Little Rock, Arkansas (1957). His most enduring domestic achievement was the 1956 Federal-Aid Highway Act, creating the Interstate Highway System. Following the Soviet launch of Sputnik (1957), he created NASA and the Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA), and signed the National Defense Education Act to bolster science education.

### Foreign Policy
Eisenhower's foreign policy focused on Cold War containment. He ended the Korean War with an armistice (1953), possibly using nuclear threats. His "New Look" policy relied on nuclear deterrence. He supported coups in Iran (1953) and Guatemala (1954). During the Suez Crisis (1956), he opposed the Anglo-French-Israeli invasion of Egypt, forcing their withdrawal. He condemned the Soviet invasion of Hungary (1956) but took no action. He intervened in Lebanon (1958) and developed the "Eisenhower Doctrine" to counter communism in the Middle East. In Asia, he supported South Vietnam after the French defeat, increasing U.S. military advisors.

### Health and Final Years
Eisenhower suffered a heart attack (1955), a stroke (1957), and surgery for Crohn's disease (1956) during his presidency. In his farewell address (1961), he famously warned against the undue influence of the "military-industrial complex." He retired to a farm in Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, and died in 1969.

## Terms
*   **Dynamic conservatism**: Eisenhower's political philosophy of preserving and moderately expanding popular New Deal programs while advocating fiscal restraint.
*   **New Look**: A national security policy emphasizing nuclear deterrence ("more bang for the buck") to reduce conventional military spending.
*   **Atoms for Peace**: A 1953 speech advocating the peaceful use of nuclear energy, leading to the Atomic Energy Act of 1954.
*   **Eisenhower Doctrine**: A 1957 policy pledging U.S. military and economic aid to Middle Eastern nations resisting communist influence.
*   **Military-industrial complex**: A term from Eisenhower's farewell address describing the relationship between a country's military establishment and the arms industry that supplies it.
*   **Executive privilege**: The principle, asserted by Eisenhower against Senator McCarthy, that the president can withhold information from Congress to protect national security or internal deliberations.
*   **Operation Overlord**: The code name for the Battle of Normandy, the Allied invasion of German-occupied France on June 6, 1944.
*   **SHAEF**: Supreme Headquarters Allied Expeditionary Force, the command staff for the Allied forces in Northwest Europe from 1944.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Nuclear Threats in Korea**: Historians debate whether Eisenhower explicitly threatened China with nuclear attack to secure the 1953 armistice.
*   **Civil Rights Commitment**: Scholars assess whether Eisenhower's enforcement of integration (e.g., Little Rock) reflected deep personal conviction or a reluctant defense of federal authority.
*   **Vietnam Involvement**: Analysts argue over whether Eisenhower's support for South Vietnam after 1954 inevitably drew the U.S. into the Vietnam War.
*   **Knowledge of U-2 Program**: Questions remain about how much Eisenhower knew about CIA overflights of the USSR before the 1960 U-2 incident, which scuttled a summit with Khrushchev.
*   **Presidential Effectiveness**: Early criticism labeled him a passive, golf-playing president, but access to his papers has led historians to re-evaluate him as a shrewd, behind-the-scenes manager who achieved his core goals.
