# Trưng sisters

## Overview
The Trưng sisters, Trưng Trắc and Trưng Nhị, were Lạc Việt military leaders who led a successful rebellion in AD 40 against the first Chinese domination of Vietnam, establishing a short-lived independent state. They are revered as national heroines in Vietnam, symbolizing resistance to foreign rule and female leadership. Their revolt, though brief, is a foundational event in Vietnamese history and identity.

## Timeline
- **112 BC** — Emperor Wu of Han annexes Nanyue, establishing Chinese commanderies in northern Vietnam.
- **c. 14–43** — Lifespans of Trưng Trắc and Trưng Nhị.
- **March 40 AD** — Trưng sisters launch rebellion from Red River Delta after Trưng Trắc's husband is killed by Chinese governor Su Ding.
- **40 AD** — Rebellion spreads; sisters capture 65 towns. Trưng Trắc is proclaimed queen regnant.
- **42 AD** — Han Emperor Guangwu sends General Ma Yuan with 20,000 troops to suppress the revolt.
- **43 AD** — Ma Yuan defeats the sisters. They are either executed or commit suicide; their heads are sent to Luoyang.
- **1158** — Emperor Lý Anh Tông constructs the first major Hai Bà Trưng Temple in Hanoi.
- **1962** — A statue of the sisters is erected in Saigon during the Vietnam War.
- **1963** — The Saigon statue is demolished after the overthrow of Ngô Đình Diệm.

## Body

### Historical Background and Rebellion
The Trưng sisters were born into a wealthy Lạc aristocratic family in Jiaozhi, a Chinese commandery. The rebellion was triggered by the killing of Trưng Trắc's husband, Thi Sách, by the Chinese governor Su Ding. In March 40 AD, the sisters led a Lạc Việt uprising that quickly expanded from the Red River Delta to commanderies including Hepu and Rinan. They captured approximately 65 settlements, and Trưng Trắc was proclaimed queen. The revolt represented the first major resistance against over two centuries of Chinese rule.

### Defeat and Aftermath
In 42 AD, the Han court dispatched General Ma Yuan with a punitive force of 20,000 troops. The sisters' army was defeated in 43 AD. Chinese sources state Ma Yuan beheaded the sisters and sent their heads to the Han court. Vietnamese chronicles suggest they committed suicide. Ma Yuan subsequently consolidated Chinese control, establishing new administrative divisions and infrastructure.

### Historiography and Sources
Primary accounts come from Chinese texts like the 5th-century *Book of the Later Han*, which provide brief, factual records of the rebellion and its suppression. The 1479 Vietnamese *Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư* (Complete Annals of Đại Việt) offers a more detailed narrative, framing the sisters as heroic figures resisting oppression. Key differences exist: Chinese sources omit details of Su Ding's tyranny and the sisters' suicide, while Vietnamese accounts emphasize their righteous cause and tragic end.

### Cultural Significance and Legacy
The Trưng sisters are central to Vietnamese national identity, symbolizing independence and resistance. Temples dedicated to them exist throughout Vietnam, with the most famous in Hanoi built in 1158. Their story is taught in schools and commemorated annually. Historically, their revolt has been cited to discuss pre-sinicization matriarchal social structures and women's leadership potential. Their image, often depicted riding war elephants, remains a powerful national symbol.

## Terms
- **Lạc Việt**: The ancient Vietnamese people who inhabited the Red River Delta region.
- **Commandery**: An administrative division of the Chinese Han dynasty, equivalent to a province.
- **Queen regnant**: A female monarch who reigns in her own right.
- **Jiaozhi**: The Chinese name for the commandery covering northern Vietnam during the Han dynasty.
- **Mê Linh**: The district and prefecture that was the sisters' home and initial power base.
- **Thi Sách**: The husband of Trưng Trắc, whose execution sparked the rebellion.
- **Ma Yuan**: The Han general who led the military campaign to crush the sisters' revolt.
- **Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư**: The 15th-century Vietnamese historical chronicle that is a primary source for the sisters' story.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause of Defeat**: Some historians, like Keith Taylor, suggest the rebellion may have faltered due to desertion because the rebels doubted a woman's ability to lead successfully.
- **Fate of the Sisters**: Chinese sources state they were beheaded, while Vietnamese tradition often holds they committed suicide to avoid capture.
- **Historical Accuracy**: The brevity of Chinese records versus the detailed Vietnamese chronicles leads to debate over the precise events, motivations, and the sisters' exact roles.
- **Social Implications**: Their leadership is interpreted by some as evidence of a pre-sinicization matriarchal society in Vietnam, though this interpretation is debated.
