# Maurice Ravel

## Overview
Joseph Maurice Ravel (1875–1937) was a French composer, pianist, and conductor whose meticulous craftsmanship and innovative orchestration made him France’s leading composer in the 1920s and 1930s. Though frequently grouped with Debussy under the label "Impressionism"—a term both rejected—Ravel distinguished himself through a classical concern for form, clarity, and structural balance, integrating diverse influences from Baroque and Spanish folk traditions to jazz and modernism. A slow, painstaking worker, he produced a relatively small but seminal catalogue (approximately sixty performable works), including piano pieces, chamber music, two operas, two piano concertos, and the ballet scores *Daphnis et Chloé* and *Boléro*. His 1922 orchestration of Mussorgsky’s *Pictures at an Exhibition* remains the definitive version. A neurological illness, possibly exacerbated by a 1932 taxi accident, silenced his creative output in his final years.

## Timeline
- **7 Mar 1875** — Born in Ciboure, Basque region, France; family moves to Paris three months later.
- **1889–1895** — Enters Paris Conservatoire (preparatory piano); wins first prize in piano (1891) but expelled in 1895 for lack of prizes in composition/harmony.
- **1897–1903** — Readmitted to Conservatoire; studies composition with Gabriel Fauré. Expelled again in 1900; attends Fauré’s classes as auditor until 1903.
- **1899** — Composes *Pavane pour une infante défunte*, his first widely known work.
- **1900–1905** — Five attempts at the Prix de Rome; 1905 elimination causes national scandal (*l’affaire Ravel*), forcing Director Dubois’s resignation and Fauré’s appointment.
- **1909–1912** — Founds Société Musicale Indépendante (1910); premieres *L’heure espagnole* (1911), *Ma mère l’Oye*, *Adélaïde*, and *Daphnis et Chloé* (1912).
- **1914–1918** — Serves as lorry driver at Verdun; mother dies (1917); composes *Le Tombeau de Couperin* (1914–17).
- **1920** — Completes *La valse*; declines Légion d’honneur; moves to Le Belvédère, Montfort-l’Amaury (1921).
- **1922** — Publishes orchestration of Mussorgsky’s *Pictures at an Exhibition*.
- **1926** — Premieres *L’enfant et les sortilèges* (libretto by Colette).
- **1928** — North American tour (25 cities); premieres *Boléro* (commissioned by Ida Rubinstein).
- **1930–1932** — Completes Piano Concerto in D major for Left Hand (Wittgenstein) and Piano Concerto in G major.
- **Oct 1932** — Taxi accident; subsequent aphasia and cognitive decline end composing career.
- **28 Dec 1937** — Dies in Paris at 62 after unsuccessful brain surgery; buried at Levallois-Perret.

## Body

### Early Life and Conservatoire Struggles
Ravel was born to a Swiss-engineer father and a Basque mother whose folk songs shaped his lifelong Hispanic affinity. Largely educated by his father, he entered the Paris Conservatoire in 1889. Despite winning a premier piano prize (1891), his independent mindset clashed with the conservative faculty. He was expelled in 1895, readmitted in 1897 to study with Gabriel Fauré—a pivotal mentor who valued his imagination—but expelled again in 1900 after winning no composition prizes. Director Théodore Dubois opposed Ravel’s progressive outlook, making him a "marked man." During this period, Ravel composed early works like *Sérénade grotesque* and the *Habanera* (later part of *Rapsodie espagnole*), and befriended pianist Ricardo Viñes and the iconoclastic Erik Satie, whose formal experiments Ravel deemed "of inestimable value."

### Les Apaches, Debussy, and the Prix de Rome Scandal
Around 1900, Ravel joined *Les Apaches*, a collective of artistic "outcasts" including Stravinsky and Manuel de Falla. They championed Debussy’s *Pelléas et Mélisande* (1902) against the establishment. While Ravel admired Debussy’s genius, he insisted his own aesthetic was opposed to Debussy’s symbolism, prioritizing craftsmanship over spontaneity. Their friendship cooled by 1905 amid factional disputes and Ravel’s support for Debussy’s deserted wife. Simultaneously, Ravel’s fifth failure at the Prix de Rome (1905)—eliminated in the first round while only students of jury member Charles Lenepveu advanced—ignited *l’affaire Ravel*. The scandal forced Dubois’s retirement and brought Fauré in to reform the Conservatoire.

### Pre-War Maturity and Ballets
Ravel established a pattern of composing for piano then orchestrating, yielding works like *Miroirs* (1905), *Rapsodie espagnole* (1907–08), and *Ma mère l’Oye* (1908–10). He co-founded the Société Musicale Indépendante (1910) to promote modern French music. His first opera, the Spanish comedy *L’heure espagnole* (1911), was delayed by concerns over its risqué plot. 1912 saw three ballet premieres: *Ma mère l’Oye*, *Adélaïde*, and the massive *Daphnis et Chloé* for Diaghilev’s Ballets Russes. The latter’s difficult gestation and under-rehearsed premiere caused a health collapse (neurasthenia). Ravel recognized the historic importance of Stravinsky’s *The Rite of Spring* (1913) immediately, predicting its impact would equal *Pelléas*.

### War Service and Post-War Shift
Rejected from the Air Force for age and a heart condition, Ravel enlisted at 40 as a lorry driver (1915), enduring bombardment and frostbite. He refused to join the nationalist league banning German music, arguing isolation would degenerate French art; the league retaliated by banning his works. His mother’s death (1917) plunged him into despair. Wartime output was sparse but included the *Piano Trio* and *Le Tombeau de Couperin*, a Baroque-styled suite memorializing friends killed in action.

Post-war, Ravel’s stamina was diminished, yet he was acknowledged as France’s leading composer after Debussy’s death (1918). He declined the Légion d’honneur (1920), alienating Satie but supporting the younger *Les Six*. His style shifted toward *dépouillement* (stripping away pre-war extravagance), evident in the sparer textures of *La valse* (1920)—rejected by Diaghilev as "the portrait of a ballet"—and the jazz-inflected *Chansons madécasses* (1926) and *L’enfant et les sortilèges* (1926). He moved to the countryside (Le Belvédère, 1921) and toured extensively, culminating in a triumphant 1928 North American tour. *Boléro* (1928), conceived as an "experiment" in orchestral tissue without thematic development, became a global sensation to Ravel’s bemusement: "I’ve written only one masterpiece – Boléro. Unfortunately there’s no music in it."

### Final Concertos and Illness
Ravel’s last major works were two piano concertos composed simultaneously. The Concerto in D for Left Hand (1930–31), commissioned by amputee Paul Wittgenstein, solves the texture problem of single-hand writing with dense, virtuosic writing. The Concerto in G (1931–32) blends Mozartian grace with jazz idioms. A 1932 taxi accident likely accelerated a pre-existing cerebral condition (diagnosed variously as frontotemporal dementia, Alzheimer’s, or Creutzfeldt–Jakob). Aphasia prevented further composition; a film score for *Don Quixote* (1933) was completed mostly by Ibert, though Ravel contributed three songs (*Don Quichotte à Dulcinée*). Brain surgery in 1937 brought only temporary improvement; he died in December 1937.

### Musical Language and Orchestration
Ravel viewed himself as a classicist, using traditional forms (ternary, rondo, sonata) to frame novel harmonies—modal melodies, ninth/eleventh chords, unresolved appoggiaturas—and rhythms. He studied each instrument’s timbre microscopically; critic Alexis Roland-Manuel ranked him with Stravinsky for knowing "the weight of a trombone-note." Only four works (*Rapsodie espagnole*, *La valse*, two concertos) were conceived for orchestra; others are transcriptions of piano or stage works. His 1922 *Pictures at an Exhibition* remains the benchmark for its "dazzling array of instrumental colour." In the 1930s, he simplified textures, as heard in the Mozartian slow movement of the G major Concerto.

### Piano, Chamber, and Vocal Music
Ravel’s piano output (<30 works) ranges from the simplicity of *Ma mère l’Oye* to the "transcendental virtuosity" of *Gaspard de la nuit* (1908), which demands a balance of "lightly worn virtuosity" and classical shape. He often honored predecessors (*Menuet sur le nom de Haydn*, *Le tombeau de Couperin*). Chamber works include the early String Quartet (1903, dedicated to Fauré), the *Introduction and Allegro* (1905), the *Piano Trio* (1914), and post-war sonatas for Violin/Cello (1922) and Violin/Piano (1927), the latter exploring the instruments’ "essential incompatibility." Vocal music is substantial: early cantatas show Debussy’s influence; *Histoires naturelles* (1906) pushes French word-setting toward the colloquial; *Shéhérazade* and *Chansons madécasses* explore exotic sensuality. He favored soprano/mezzo voices even for male narratives.

### Recordings and Legacy
Ravel was an early advocate for recording technology. His playing was captured on piano rolls (1914–28) and acoustic/electric discs, though he supervised rather than conducted the 1932 G major Concerto recording. He conducted *Boléro* (1930) and a sound film of the D major Concerto (1933). He declined all French state honors but accepted foreign ones (Royal Philharmonic Society, 1921; Ordre de Léopold, 1926; Oxford doctorate, 1928). His home became a museum; his estate’s disposition remains contested. Works by Satie, Stravinsky, and others were dedicated to his memory.

## Terms
- **Impressionism**: A term applied to Ravel and Debussy for their harmonic color and atmospheric suggestion; both composers rejected it as inaccurate.
- **Prix de Rome**: France’s prestigious composition prize; Ravel’s 1905 elimination caused a national scandal (*l’affaire Ravel*) that reformed the Conservatoire.
- **Les Apaches**: Informal group of avant-garde artists, poets, and musicians (c. 1900–1914) who supported Debussy and Ravel; name means "The Hooligans."
- **Dépouillement**: "Stripping away"; post-WWI aesthetic favoring lean textures and essentialism over late-Romantic extravagance.
- **Orchestration**: The art of scoring for orchestra; Ravel’s supreme technical mastery, treating instruments as distinct colors.
- **Boléro** (1928): One-movement ballet built on a single ostinato rhythm and two themes over a relentless crescendo; Ravel called it "orchestral tissue without music."
- **Concerto for the Left Hand** (1931): Written for Paul Wittgenstein (lost right arm in WWI); simulates two-hand texture through dense writing.
- **Gaspard de la nuit** (1908): Three-movement piano suite of extreme virtuosity, based on Aloysius Bertrand’s poems.
- **Le Tombeau de Couperin** (1914–17): Baroque-styled piano suite (orchestrated 1919); each movement memorializes a friend killed in WWI.
- **Société Musicale Indépendante**: Founded 1910 by Ravel and Fauré’s pupils to counter the conservative Société Nationale; premiered modern French and foreign works.

## Debates and open questions
- **Nature of final illness**: The exact neurological diagnosis remains unknown; experts have proposed frontotemporal dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, possibly triggered or accelerated by the 1932 taxi accident.
- **Ravel vs. Debussy influence**: Scholars dispute the chronology and direction of influence between the two composers; Ravel claimed independent evolution (citing *Jeux d’eau* preceding Debussy’s major piano works), while critics like Pierre Lalo accused him of borrowing Debussy’s sensitivity.
- **Sexuality and personal life**: Ravel was a lifelong bachelor; contemporaries speculated on homosexuality or romantic attachments to Misia Edwards or Hélène Jourdan-Morhange, but biographers (Nichols, Orenstein) conclude his sexuality remains a mystery.
- **Artistic merit of *Boléro***: Ravel dismissed it as having "no music," yet it became his most famous work; debate persists whether its popularity obscures his more structurally complex achievements.
- **Piano vs. orchestral versions**: Critics disagree on whether Ravel’s orchestrations improve the piano originals (e.g., *Alborada del gracioso*, *Le Tombeau de Couperin*); some find the piano versions sharper and more persuasive.
