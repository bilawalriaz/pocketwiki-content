# Mirror (1975 film)

## Overview
*Mirror* (1975) is a Soviet avant-garde film directed by Andrei Tarkovsky, structured as a dying poet's stream of consciousness. It is a deeply personal, non-linear work that blends memory, dream, and historical footage to explore the persistence of the past, guilt, and the nature of memory itself. Initially controversial for its unconventional narrative, it is now considered Tarkovsky's magnum opus and a landmark of world cinema.

## Timeline
- **1964** — Andrei Tarkovsky devises the original concept for a film about a man's memories, without showing the man himself.
- **1968** — Tarkovsky and Aleksandr Misharin write a script titled *Confession*, which is rejected by the Soviet film committee (Goskino).
- **1973** — Goskino head Filipp Ermash approves the script. Principal photography begins in July.
- **1974** — Filming concludes in March. Ermash initially rejects the finished film as "incomprehensible" but approves it without changes in the fall.
- **1975** — The film receives a limited, second-category release in the Soviet Union with only 73 copies.

## Body

### Structure and Narrative
The film portrays the thoughts and memories of Aleksei, a Soviet poet, across three timeframes: prewar (c. 1935), World War II, and the postwar present (1960s/70s). The adult Aleksei is never fully shown, as Tarkovsky believed a collection of memories could define a person without a direct portrait. The narrative is discontinuous and nonchronological, connecting scenes through motifs and individuals rather than plot. It incorporates dreams, newsreel footage, and the poetry of Tarkovsky's father, Arseny Tarkovsky, creating a flow compared to the stream of consciousness technique in modernist literature.

### Themes and Autobiography
The film is loosely autobiographical, drawing on Tarkovsky's childhood, including his family's evacuation during the war and his father's absence and return. It explores the "inescapable persistence of the past" and the protagonist's guilt and selfishness. Tarkovsky stated the film was therapeutic, allowing him to move on from his memories. While often seen as autobiographical, it is not hagiographic; the hero is described as a "weak, selfish man" tormented by his inability to repay his debt to life.

### Production and Reception
The script underwent numerous rewrites and titles. Filming was done without a clear structural plan, with much rewritten during the shoot. The editing process was extensive, with Tarkovsky believing scenes should come together spontaneously. The film initially polarized critics and audiences in the Soviet Union, with some condemning it as "elitist" and others praising it. It had a very limited release. Since then, it has been reappraised as a masterpiece, ranking highly in major polls like *Sight & Sound*'s greatest films lists.

## Terms
*   **Stream of consciousness:** A narrative technique that attempts to depict the multitudinous thoughts and feelings which pass through the mind.
*   **Nonlinear narrative:** A storytelling technique where events are portrayed out of chronological order.
*   **Oneiric:** Relating to dreams or dreaming.
*   **Goskino:** The Soviet State Committee for Cinematography, which controlled film production and distribution.
*   **Dacha:** A Russian seasonal or year-round second home, often located in the exurban areas.
*   **Great Patriotic War:** The Soviet term for the Eastern Front of World War II (1941–1945).

## Debates and Open questions
*   The film's narrative structure is intentionally enigmatic, with scholars comparing its organizational principle to music rather than conventional plot logic.
*   The extent to which the film is a direct autobiography versus a more universal exploration of memory and guilt remains a point of discussion.
*   The initial rejection by Soviet authorities and accusations of "cinephilic elitism" highlight the tension between Tarkovsky's artistic vision and state-sanctioned cinematic norms.
