# Chivalry

## Overview
Chivalry was an informal, evolving code of conduct for medieval knights, originating in France between 1170 and 1220. It combined warrior ethos, Christian piety, and courtly manners to define knightly honor and nobility. While popularized in literature, its historical reality is debated, and it influenced later concepts of gentlemanly behavior.

## Timeline
- **c. 930** — Odo of Cluny writes *Vita of St. Gerald of Aurillac*, an early support for a knightly class serving Christianity.
- **10th century** — The Peace and Truce of God movements place limits on knights to protect the weak.
- **1130s** — Geoffrey of Monmouth writes *Historia Regum Britanniae*, popularizing Arthurian legends.
- **c. 1170–1220** — The formal code of chivalry develops in France.
- **1183** — Death of Henry the Young King, whose lavish lifestyle influenced knightly culture.
- **1187** — Ralph Niger writes *De Re Militari*, an early work using the knight as a moral exemplar.
- **13th century** — Key chivalric texts appear: *Ordene de chevalerie*, Ramon Llull's *Libre del ordre de cavayleria*, and Geoffroi de Charny's *Livre de Chevalerie*.
- **1302** — English term "chivalry" first recorded.
- **14th century** — Jean Froissart's *Chronicles* document chivalric warfare in the Hundred Years' War.
- **15th century** — Sir Thomas Malory writes *Le Morte d'Arthur*.
- **1605–15** — Miguel de Cervantes publishes *Don Quixote*, satirizing chivalric ideals.
- **1884** — Léon Gautier publishes *La Chevalerie*, compiling his "Ten Commandments of Chivalry."

## Body

### Terminology and Definitions
The term "chivalry" derives from Old French *chevalerie*, meaning "horse soldiery." It originally denoted the body of heavy cavalry. The meaning evolved from a concrete military status to a broader ideal of the Christian warrior ethos and courtly love, propagated in 12th-century romance literature. Three key medieval works—by an anonymous author, Ramon Llull, and Geoffroi de Charny—independently summarize chivalric ideals, depicting a way of life combining military, noble, and religious elements.

### Pre-Chivalric Noble Conduct
Before codified chivalry, an uncodified "noble habitus" focused on the *preudomme* (a wise, honest man). This included virtues like loyalty, forbearance, hardiness, largesse (generosity), and honor. This "courtly" conduct was taught in aristocratic halls and was not exclusive to knights. Historian David Crouch argues that chivalry emerged as a more exclusive noble code in the late 12th century, partly as a response to the perceived moral failures of general courtliness.

### The Chivalric Code and Its Themes
The formal code of chivalry, as known in the Late Middle Ages, was a moral system combining warrior ethos, knightly piety, and courtly manners. In literature, chivalric duties were classified into three areas: duties to countrymen and fellow Christians (mercy, protection of the weak), duties to God (faith, championing good), and duties to women (courtly love, gentleness). Different emphases produced strands like warrior, religious, and courtly love chivalry.

### Chivalry and Christianity
Christianity significantly shaped chivalry. The Church promoted the concept of the "knight of Christ" (*miles Christi*) and used liturgies to bless swords. The Crusades were often seen as a chivalrous enterprise, and military orders like the Teutonic Knights venerated the Virgin Mary. However, chivalry was not synonymous with crusading; it encompassed a wider range of martial and aristocratic values.

### Military Reality and Decline
Chivalric ideals were based on the early medieval warrior class. Martial skills were central, extending to hunting and heraldry. However, the reality of warfare changed. In the 14th century, common infantry using longbows repeatedly defeated chivalric cavalry charges. The rise of professional soldiery and the decline of aristocratic warfare led to the demise of chivalry as a living institution by the end of the Middle Ages.

### Modern Legacy and Criticism
Chivalry's ideals persisted into modernity, influencing military codes, gentlemanly conduct, and Victorian social reformers. However, it faced criticism. Miguel de Cervantes satirized it as anachronistic. Historian Richard Kaeuper argues it was a complex, often contradictory code that did not necessarily create social order. Feminist and masculist critiques have also challenged its gendered assumptions.

## Terms
- **Chivalry**: An informal code of conduct for medieval knights, combining martial, religious, and courtly virtues.
- **Chevalerie**: Old French term for "horse soldiery," the origin of "chivalry."
- **Preudomme**: An ideal of a wise, honest, and sensible man in pre-chivalric noble conduct.
- **Noble Habitus**: The uncodified environment of behavioral and material expectations for the aristocracy.
- **Courtly Love**: A medieval concept of knightly devotion and service to a lady, often idealized in literature.
- **Miles Christi**: Latin for "knight of Christ," a concept of the knight as a defender of the faith.
- **Largesse**: The chivalric virtue of noble generosity.
- **Heraldry**: The system of coats of arms and rules for displaying them, associated with knightly identity.
- **Quixotism**: Behavior inspired by the impractical ideals of chivalry, from Cervantes' *Don Quixote*.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historical Reality vs. Literary Ideal**: Scholars debate whether chivalry was a lived reality or a poetic invention. Historian Jean de Sismondi argued it was "almost entirely poetical," always placed in a distant, idealized past.
- **Origins and Timing**: While the code is dated to 1170–1220, its roots in earlier "courtly" conduct and the precise factors leading to its emergence (e.g., the crisis of courtliness, the lifestyle of Henry the Young King) are analyzed by historians like David Crouch.
- **Social Impact**: Richard Kaeuper contends that chivalry was a problematic guide for knights, who played an ambivalent role in public order, and that its ideals were often contradictory and did not lead to a more peaceful society.
- **Modern Interpretation**: The term's meaning continues to evolve, with sociological uses noting gender biases in protective instincts, and historical analyses questioning the singular narrative of "the" chivalric code.
