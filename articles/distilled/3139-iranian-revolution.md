# Iranian Revolution

## Overview
The Iranian Revolution (1979) was a popular uprising that overthrew the Pahlavi monarchy and established an Islamic republic under Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini. It fundamentally reshaped Iran's government, society, and foreign policy, replacing a pro-Western, secular authoritarian regime with a theocratic system based on *velayat-e faqih* (guardianship of the Islamic jurist). The revolution is significant for its unique blend of religious, nationalist, and populist motivations, its massive popular mobilization, and its lasting impact on regional and global geopolitics.

## Timeline
- **1953** — CIA- and MI6-backed coup overthrows Prime Minister Mohammad Mossadegh, reinstating Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi as an absolute monarch.
- **1963** — Shah launches the White Revolution, a modernization program. Ayatollah Khomeini emerges as a vocal critic and is exiled in 1964.
- **1978 (Jan)** — Protests begin in Qom after a newspaper article denounces Khomeini.
- **1978 (Aug)** — Cinema Rex fire in Abadan kills around 400, fueling public outrage against the Shah.
- **1978 (Sep)** — Jaleh Square Massacre (Black Friday) occurs during martial law, killing dozens and destroying chances for reconciliation.
- **1978 (Oct-Nov)** — Nationwide general strike, especially in the oil industry, paralyzes the economy.
- **1978 (Dec)** — Millions march during Muharram protests, including on Tasu'a and Ashura.
- **1979 (Jan 16)** — Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi leaves Iran for exile.
- **1979 (Feb 1)** — Ayatollah Khomeini returns to Iran from exile.
- **1979 (Feb 11)** — Monarchy collapses; military declares neutrality. Khomeini assumes leadership.
- **1979 (Mar 30-31)** — Referendum approves the creation of an Islamic Republic with 98% support.
- **1979 (Nov 4)** — Iranian students seize the U.S. embassy, beginning the 444-day hostage crisis.

## Body

### Background and Causes
The revolution's roots lie in decades of discontent. The 1953 coup, which restored the Shah's absolute power and increased U.S. influence, created lasting resentment. The Shah's White Revolution (1963), while modernizing, alienated the clergy and traditional classes, and its economic benefits were unevenly distributed. The 1973 oil boom led to inflation, corruption, and a widening gap between rich and poor. By the late 1970s, the regime was widely seen as corrupt, repressive, and overly reliant on foreign powers, particularly the United States.

### The Revolutionary Movement (1977-1979)
Protests began in 1977, initially as a campaign of civil resistance by a broad coalition of Islamists, leftists, and liberals. Key events escalated the conflict: the mysterious death of Khomeini's son in 1977, the deadly Cinema Rex fire (1978), and the Jaleh Square Massacre (1978). Khomeini, exiled in Iraq and later France, became the revolution's unifying figurehead. Nationwide strikes, especially in the oil industry, crippled the economy. By December 1978, millions participated in Muharram protests. The Shah left Iran in January 1979, and Khomeini returned in February to massive crowds. Armed battles between rebels and loyalists led to the military's neutrality and the monarchy's collapse on February 11, 1979.

### Consolidation of the Islamic Republic
After the revolution, Khomeini and his supporters systematically consolidated power. A provisional government led by Mehdi Bazargan was sidelined, especially after the U.S. embassy hostage crisis (1979-1981). New institutions were created, including the Revolutionary Guards, Revolutionary Tribunals, and the Islamic Republican Party. Opposition groups—from secular liberals and leftists to moderate Islamists—were suppressed through arrests, executions, and bans. The 1979 constitution enshrined the principle of *velayat-e faqih*, making Khomeini the Supreme Leader with ultimate authority. A massive political massacre occurred between 1981 and 1982, targeting thousands of dissidents.

### Impact and Aftermath
The revolution had profound domestic and international effects. Domestically, it led to the Islamization of law and society, restrictions on women's rights and personal freedoms, and the persecution of religious minorities like the Baha'i. Economically, the state expanded its role, but growth was hampered by the Iran-Iraq War (1980-1988). Internationally, Iran adopted an anti-Western, anti-Israel stance and sought to export its revolutionary ideology, supporting groups like Hezbollah. The revolution inspired Islamist movements globally but also led to Iran's isolation from the West and conflicts with neighbors.

## Terms
*   **Velâyat-e Faqih (Guardianship of the Islamic Jurist):** The political theory, developed by Khomeini, that the leading Islamic jurist should hold supreme political authority in the absence of the Hidden Imam.
*   **SAVAK:** The Shah's secret police, established with CIA assistance, known for its repression of political dissent.
*   **White Revolution:** A series of top-down modernization and land reform programs launched by the Shah in 1963.
*   **Majlis:** The Iranian parliament or national consultative assembly.
*   **Revolutionary Guards (Pasdaran):** A military force established after the revolution to protect the Islamic Republic, becoming a powerful political and economic institution.
*   **Komitehs (Revolutionary Committees):** Local committees that served as the "eyes and ears" of the new regime, enforcing its will.
*   **Hezbollahi:** Paramilitary groups loyal to Khomeini, used to attack opposition demonstrations and offices.
*   **Gharbzadegi:** A concept coined by Jalal Al-e-Ahmad meaning "Westoxification," the idea that Western culture was a plague corrupting Iranian society.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Nature of the Revolution:** Scholars debate whether it was primarily a religious, social, or political revolution, or a unique blend of all three. Some argue it was a "classic" revolution against autocracy, while others emphasize its theocratic outcome.
*   **Khomeini's Intentions:** There is debate over whether Khomeini always intended to establish a theocracy or if he used the democratic rhetoric of the revolution to gain power, only revealing his full plan later.
*   **Role of the U.S.:** The extent of U.S. responsibility is debated. Some argue American support for the Shah created the conditions for revolution, while others focus on the Carter administration's confused and contradictory policies during the crisis, which may have inadvertently facilitated Khomeini's rise.
*   **Popular Support:** While the revolution had massive popular mobilization, the degree of genuine, widespread support for Khomeini's specific vision of an Islamic Republic versus opposition to the Shah is contested. The 98% referendum result is often viewed with skepticism.
