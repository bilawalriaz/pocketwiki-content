# Muhammad Ali of Egypt

## Overview
Muhammad Ali (1769–1849) was an Ottoman Albanian commander who became the de facto ruler of Egypt from 1805 to 1848. He founded the Muhammad Ali dynasty, which ruled Egypt and Sudan for over a century, and is widely regarded as the creator of the modern Egyptian state through his ambitious military, economic, and administrative reforms.

## Timeline
- **1769** — Born in Kavala, Ottoman Empire.
- **1801** — Arrives in Egypt as part of an Ottoman expeditionary force.
- **1805** — Appointed Wāli (governor) of Egypt.
- **1811** — Massacre of Mamluk leaders at the Cairo Citadel.
- **1811–1818** — Military campaign in Arabia, defeating the House of Saud.
- **1820–1821** — Conquest of Sudan begins.
- **1824–1827** — Sends forces to Greece to support the Ottoman Sultan; Egyptian fleet destroyed at Battle of Navarino (1827).
- **1831–1833** — First Egyptian–Ottoman War; son Ibrahim Pasha defeats Ottoman armies.
- **1839** — Second Egyptian–Ottoman War; Ottoman defeat at Battle of Nezib.
- **1840** — Convention of London: European powers force withdrawal from most conquests but grant hereditary rule in Egypt.
- **1848** — Retires due to senility; succeeded briefly by his son Ibrahim.
- **1849** — Dies in Alexandria.

## Body

### Rise to Power and Consolidation
Born in Kavala, Muhammad Ali's origins are debated; sources describe him as either Anatolian Turkish or Albanian. His career began in the Ottoman military. In 1801, he traveled to Egypt as part of an Ottoman force sent to expel French troops. Amid the ensuing power vacuum, he skillfully built alliances with local notables, soldiers, and Mamluk elements. In 1805, popular demand led to his appointment as Wāli. To eliminate the Mamluk threat, he invited their leaders to a celebration at the Cairo Citadel in 1811 and had them massacred, then dispatched his army to destroy the remaining Mamluk forces.

### Building the Egyptian State
Muhammad Ali aimed to create an autonomous, hereditary state. He reorganized Egypt's administration, centralizing all authority. He "nationalized" all *iltizam* (tax-farm) lands, creating a state monopoly on agricultural production, which proved highly profitable with new cash crops like long-staple cotton. He built an industrial base, focusing on weapons production and a navy, and established textile factories. To staff these, he used a *corvée* (forced labor) system, which was deeply unpopular. He also sent students to Europe, founded schools and hospitals, and established a professional bureaucracy, often placing his sons in key positions.

### Military Campaigns and Foreign Policy
Muhammad Ali first waged war on behalf of the Ottoman Sultan. He sent expeditions to Arabia (1811–1818) to recapture the Hejaz from the House of Saud and conquered Sudan (1820–1821) for its resources. He intervened in the Greek revolt (1824–1827), but his fleet was destroyed at Navarino. Seeking compensation, he later turned against the Ottoman Empire. In the First Egyptian–Ottoman War (1831–1833), his son Ibrahim Pasha defeated Ottoman armies and threatened Constantinople. A second war in 1839 again ended in Ottoman defeat. European intervention forced him to withdraw from most conquests, but the 1840 Convention of London recognized the hereditary rule of his dynasty in Egypt and Sudan.

### Social and Legal Reforms
He implemented legal reforms, passing the first penal legislation in 1829 and establishing a modern police force. In 1832, he allowed Antoine Clot to establish a School of Medicine for women, which trained *hakimas* (female doctors) to treat women and children, primarily to maintain a healthy population for his army. This initiative, while pioneering, was driven by military goals and increased state control over social life. His educational missions to Europe and the founding of the Bulaq Press (1819/21) contributed to the Arabic literary renaissance (*Nahda*).

## Terms
- **Wāli**: Governor of an Ottoman province; Muhammad Ali held this title in Egypt.
- **Mamluk**: A class of soldier-slaves who had dominated Egypt for centuries before Muhammad Ali's rise.
- **Iltizam**: A system of tax-farming where individuals collected taxes in exchange for a share; Muhammad Ali abolished it to centralize revenue.
- **Corvée**: A system of forced, unpaid labor, used by Muhammad Ali for public works and military conscription.
- **Hakima**: A female doctor, a title created for graduates of the women's medical school established under Muhammad Ali.
- **Nahda**: The Arabic literary and cultural renaissance of the 19th century, spurred by increased contact with Europe.
- **Bulaq Press**: The first indigenous printing press in the Arab world, founded in Cairo in 1819/21.
- **Convention of Kutahya (1833)**: Peace agreement ending the first Egyptian-Ottoman war, giving Muhammad Ali control of Crete and Syria.
- **Convention of London (1840)**: Agreement where European powers forced Muhammad Ali to withdraw from most territories but granted hereditary rule in Egypt.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origins**: His ancestry is debated, with sources describing him as either Anatolian Turkish or Albanian.
- **Legacy**: He is viewed by some as the "Father of Modern Egypt" for building a modern state, while others see him as a foreign conqueror who exploited Egyptian resources for personal and dynastic gain.
- **Reforms**: Historians debate whether his social reforms, like the women's medical school, were genuine modernization or primarily tools to strengthen his military and state control.
- **Health**: The cause of his late-life senility is debated, possibly due to natural aging or the effects of silver nitrate treatment for dysentery.
