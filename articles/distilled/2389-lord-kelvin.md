# Lord Kelvin

## Overview
William Thomson, 1st Baron Kelvin (1824–1907), was a Scottish mathematician, physicist, and engineer central to 19th-century physics unification. He formulated the first and second laws of thermodynamics, determined absolute zero, and led the engineering of the first successful transatlantic telegraph cable. His work established foundational concepts in thermodynamics, electrical measurement, and geophysics. The SI unit of absolute temperature, the kelvin, is named for him.

## Timeline
- **26 June 1824** — Born in Belfast, Ireland.
- **1833** — Family moves to Glasgow; father becomes a university professor.
- **1841** — Enters Peterhouse, Cambridge; graduates as second wrangler in 1845.
- **1846** — Appointed Professor of Natural Philosophy at the University of Glasgow, holding the post for 53 years.
- **1848** — Proposes an absolute temperature scale (later the Kelvin scale).
- **1851** — Publishes paper reconciling Carnot and Joule, key to the second law of thermodynamics.
- **1856** — Elected to the board of the Atlantic Telegraph Company.
- **1866** — Knighted by Queen Victoria for his role in the transatlantic cable project.
- **1892** — Created Baron Kelvin, of Largs, becoming the first scientist elevated to the House of Lords.
- **1897** — Publishes final estimate of Earth's age as 20–40 million years.
- **17 Dec 1907** — Dies at Netherhall, Largs, Scotland.

## Body

### Early Life and Academic Foundations
Born in Belfast, Thomson moved to Glasgow in 1833. He entered the University of Glasgow at age 10 and later studied at Cambridge, excelling in mathematics and sports. Influenced by Joseph Fourier's work on heat, he defended Fourier's mathematics against British critics. In 1845, he provided the first mathematical explanation of Michael Faraday's concept of electrical induction through a dielectric. In 1846, at age 22, he was appointed Professor of Natural Philosophy at Glasgow, a position he held until 1899.

### Thermodynamics and the Absolute Temperature Scale
Thomson's major theoretical work was in thermodynamics. After hearing James Prescott Joule challenge the caloric theory of heat, Thomson initially defended Sadi Carnot's theories. He proposed an absolute temperature scale in 1848, independent of any substance's properties, identifying absolute zero. He reconciled Carnot's ideas with Joule's principle of the mechanical equivalent of heat, leading to the second law of thermodynamics. This work, and collaboration with Joule, led to the discovery of the Joule–Thomson effect. Thomson also extrapolated the second law to the cosmos, originating the idea of "universal heat death."

### The Transatlantic Telegraph Cable
Thomson's work on the transatlantic cable made him famous and wealthy. Starting in 1854, he analyzed signal transmission physics, showing data rate was inversely proportional to the square of cable length. He argued for a larger cable design, contradicting the chief electrician. As scientific adviser, he developed key inventions, including the mirror galvanometer and siphon recorder, for receiving weak signals. Despite technical failures and conflicts, his practical engineering skills were crucial to the cable's eventual success in 1866, earning him a knighthood.

### Other Scientific Contributions
Thomson collaborated with Peter Guthrie Tait on the influential *Treatise on Natural Philosophy*, helping found the study of mechanics. He made significant contributions to atmospheric electricity, developing measurement instruments. He pioneered the vortex theory of the atom, stimulating knot theory in mathematics. In marine science, he invented a deep-sea sounding device and perfected the adjustable compass, correcting for magnetic deviation in iron ships. He also did foundational work in electrical standardization, inventing instruments like the quadrant electrometer and current balance.

### The Age of the Earth
Using heat conduction expertise, Thomson calculated the Earth's age based on its cooling rate. He argued against vast geological ages proposed by uniformitarians and Charles Darwin, initially estimating 20–400 million years. Over decades, he refined this to 20–40 million years, contending this was too short for Darwinian evolution. His estimate was ultimately overturned by the discovery of radioactive decay, which provided an internal heat source he had not considered. However, his calculations for the age of the Sun, based on gravitational contraction, were correct for the pre-fusion era.

### Later Life and Legacy
Kelvin remained active in science and industry until his death. He was the first president of the International Electrotechnical Commission. In 1900, he famously described two "clouds" over physics—the failure to detect the luminiferous aether and problems with the equipartition theorem—which pointed to the coming revolutions of relativity and quantum mechanics. He died in 1907 and was buried in Westminster Abbey near Isaac Newton.

## Terms
*   **Thermodynamics**: The branch of physics dealing with heat, work, and temperature, and their relation to energy, radiation, and physical properties of matter.
*   **Absolute Zero**: The lowest possible temperature (approximately −273.15°C), at which particle motion theoretically ceases.
*   **Kelvin Scale**: An absolute thermodynamic temperature scale where zero is absolute zero.
*   **Second Law of Thermodynamics**: A physical law stating that the total entropy of an isolated system can never decrease over time.
*   **Dielectric**: An electrical insulator that can be polarized by an applied electric field.
*   **Joule–Thomson Effect**: The temperature change of a real gas when forced through a valve or porous plug while insulated.
*   **Mirror Galvanometer**: A sensitive instrument for detecting electric current, using a mirror attached to a magnet to reflect a light beam.
*   **Vortex Theory of the Atom**: A 19th-century hypothesis that atoms were stable vortices in the luminiferous aether.
*   **Uniformitarianism**: The geological theory that changes in the Earth's crust have resulted from continuous and uniform processes.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Age of the Earth**: Kelvin's physics-based estimate of 20–40 million years conflicted with geological and biological evidence for a much older Earth. This debate was resolved after his death with the discovery of radioactivity.
*   **Limits of Classical Physics**: Kelvin's "two clouds" lecture highlighted unresolved problems in classical physics that ultimately led to relativity and quantum mechanics.
*   **Compass Design**: Biographical accounts differ on whether Kelvin's compass innovations were brilliant improvements or a commercial monopoly achieved by leveraging his reputation.
*   **False Attributions**: The statement "There is nothing new to be discovered in physics now" is widely but incorrectly attributed to Kelvin; it is a paraphrase of Albert A. Michelson.
