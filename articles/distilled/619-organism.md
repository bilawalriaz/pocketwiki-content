# Organism

## Overview
An organism is a living entity functioning as an individual, yet biology lacks a universally accepted definition. Standard criteria—autonomous reproduction, growth, and metabolism—exclude viruses, which evolve but lack metabolism, and struggle with colonial entities like eusocial insects or siphonophores, where labor is divided among specialized units. Evolutionary biologists Queller and Strassmann propose "organismality" as a spectrum defined by high cooperation and low conflict among component parts, a view that reclassifies mutualisms (lichens) and sexual partnerships (anglerfish) as organisms. This perspective frames individuality as an evolved social trait rather than a binary category, applicable across levels from RNA replicators to synthetic bio-engineered constructs.

## Timeline
- **1660s** — Term "organism" enters English (from Greek *organismos*, "instrument/tool") with obsolete meaning of organic structure.
- **1790** — Immanuel Kant defines an organism as "both an organized and a self-organizing being" in *Critique of Judgment*.
- **1944** — Erwin Schrödinger proposes "anti-entropy" (maintaining order) as a criterion for life.
- **1948** — Claude Shannon's information theory later applied to identify organisms as self-maintaining information systems.
- **2016** — Samuel Díaz‐Muñoz et al. formalize organismality as context-dependent degrees of cooperation and conflict.
- **Present** — Synthetic biology produces chimaeras, cyborgs, and hybrots, expanding boundary cases.

## Body

### Defining Criteria and Their Failures
Traditional definitions require **autonomous reproduction, growth, and metabolism**, but these exclude viruses and misclassify colonial organisms. **Noncompartmentability** (inability to divide without loss of function, per Richard Dawkins) fails because many organisms regenerate from fragments. **Individuality**—demanding simultaneous genetic uniqueness, homogeneity, and autonomy—collapses in clonal colonies. **Immune self/non-self discrimination** and **anti-entropy** (Schrödinger) or **information-theoretic self-maintenance** (Shannon) are proposed alternatives, yet no single criterion commands consensus. Some philosophers argue the category "organism" is dispensable; others suggest treating autonomy, genetic homogeneity, and uniqueness as independent dimensions, yielding multiple types of biological individuality.

### Organismality as Evolved Cooperation
Queller and Strassmann argue that **organismality**—the attributes making an entity an organism—arises when groups of simpler units (cells, insects, symbiotic partners) evolve high cooperation and low conflict. This social-evolution framework treats a lichen (fungus + alga/cyanobacteria), an anglerfish sexual partnership, and a eusocial ant colony as organisms because their parts collaborate adaptively with minimal internal competition. Díaz‐Muñoz et al. (2016) extend this: organismality is a continuous, context-dependent variable measured by cooperation/conflict ratios. Highly integrated life forms (e.g., vertebrates) may have passed through context-dependent stages toward complete unification.

### Levels of Biological Organization
- **Unicellular organisms** (protists, bacteria, archaea): single cells, often with organelles.  
- **Multicellular organisms** (animals, plants, fungi, algae): many specialized cells.  
- **Colonial organisms** (siphonophores): function as individuals but comprise communicating, organism-like **zooids**.  
- **Superorganisms** (ant colonies): many individuals acting as a single functional/social unit.  
- **Mutualisms** (lichens): multi-species partnerships where each provides needs the other cannot meet alone, flourishing in habitats inaccessible to either partner singly.

### Boundary Cases
**Viruses** lack autonomous metabolism, reproduction, and homeostasis; they rely on host machinery. Though they possess genes and evolve, most biologists classify them as non-living because their evolution and replication are host-dependent. Genes for energy metabolism found in some viruses likely originated via horizontal gene transfer from hosts.  
**RNA world** hypothesis: the first "organisms" were self-replicating RNA chains exhibiting heritability, variation, and differential reproductive output—the three mechanisms of Darwinian selection. Their fitness depended on replication fidelity, decay resistance, and resource acquisition, mediated by folded RNA configurations.  
**Organism-like colonies**: sponges, lichens, siphonophores, slime moulds, and eusocial colonies (ants, naked mole-rats) occupy a gray zone between colony and organism/superorganism.  
**Synthetic organisms**: chimaeras (multi-species cell composites), cyborgs (electromechanical integration), and hybrots (electronic-biological hybrids) display **teleonomic** (goal-seeking, error-correcting) behavior, blurring the line between evolved and designed systems.

## Terms
- **Organismality**: The suite of attributes (high cooperation, low conflict) that qualify an entity as an organism; proposed as an evolved, continuous trait rather than a binary category.  
- **Superorganism**: A colony (e.g., eusocial insects) functioning as a single adaptive unit through division of labor (germ-soma specialization).  
- **Zooid**: An individual, organism-like module within a colonial organism (e.g., siphonophore) that performs specialized functions.  
- **Mutualism**: An interspecies partnership where each partner supplies resources or services the other requires (e.g., lichen: fungus + photosynthetic alga/cyanobacteria).  
- **Teleonomic**: Exhibiting goal-directed, error-correcting behavior characteristic of living systems, whether evolved or engineered.  
- **Anti-entropy**: Schrödinger’s concept that living systems maintain internal order by exporting entropy to the environment.  
- **Noncompartmentability**: Dawkins’ criterion that an organism’s heterogeneous structure renders it non-functional if divided (though regeneration challenges this).  
- **RNA world**: Hypothetical early evolutionary stage where self-replicating RNA molecules performed both genetic and catalytic roles before DNA/protein systems.  
- **Horizontal gene transfer**: Movement of genetic material between organisms other than parent-to-offspring; likely source of metabolic genes in viruses.  
- **Chimaera**: An entity composed of cells from two or more distinct species, created naturally or via bioengineering.

## Debates and open questions
- **Is a single definition possible or necessary?** Some argue the concept is philosophically dispensable; others advocate multidimensional individuality (autonomy, genetic homogeneity, uniqueness as separate axes).  
- **Where does organismality begin?** Queller/Strassmann’s cooperation/conflict metric includes lichens and anglerfish; critics question whether multi-species partnerships dilute the concept.  
- **Are viruses organisms?** They evolve and carry genes but lack metabolism and autonomous replication; discovery of viral metabolic genes (cellular origin) fuels ongoing debate.  
- **Context-dependence of individuality:** Díaz‐Muñoz et al. posit organismality as context-dependent; it remains unclear how to measure cooperation/conflict quantitatively across diverse systems.  
- **Synthetic biology boundaries:** As chimaeras, cyborgs, and hybrots proliferate, the distinction between evolved teleonomy and designed goal-seeking behavior becomes a central philosophical and regulatory problem.
