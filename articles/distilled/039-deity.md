# Deity

## Overview

A deity is a supernatural being believed to have authority over some aspect of the universe or life, often considered sacred and worthy of worship. Belief in at least one deity who may interact with the world is called theism. Religions differ in how many deities they accept: monotheism (one), polytheism (many), henotheism (one supreme without denying others), and nontheism (no supreme creator, though lesser deities may exist). The concept varies widely across cultures, from an infinite transcendent creator to a finite entity with special significance.

## Timeline

- **c. 25,000 BCE** — Venus of Willendorf figurine created; possibly a prehistoric female deity
- **Early 3rd millennium BCE** — First written evidence of Egyptian deities appears
- **Later 3rd millennium BCE** — Egyptian deities systematized under Pharaohs as sacred kings
- **c. 2334 BCE** — Sargon of Akkad conquers Sumer; Sumerian deities syncretized with East Semitic ones
- **621 BCE** — King Josiah enforces monolatry in Judah
- **6th century BCE** — "Second Isaiah" affirms monotheism with certainty in Judaism
- **313 CE** — Edict of Toleration issued; Roman deities continued to be revered past this date
- **451 CE** — Council of Chalcedon declares Jesus Christ unites full deity and full humanity
- **12th century CE** — Germanic pagan worship rituals continue in Scandinavia
- **Modern period** — Germanic Heathenry revives worship of Germanic gods

## Body

### Etymology

The English word "deity" derives from Old French *deité*, Latin *deitatem* ("divine nature"), coined by Augustine of Hippo from *deus* ("god"). *Deus* traces to Proto-Indo-European (PIE) *deiwos*, yielding Sanskrit *Deva* ("shining one"), Greek *dios* and *Zeus*, and Latin *deus*. The feminine equivalent *devi* is cognate with Latin *dea* and Greek *thea*. In Old Persian, *daiva-* means "demon, evil god," while in Sanskrit it means "heavenly, divine." The term "god" derives from Proto-Germanic *guthan*, from PIE *ghut-* ("that which is invoked"), or alternatively from *ghu-to-* ("poured," from libation rituals). Originally neuter, "god" became masculine under Christian influence.

### Definitions

There is no universally accepted consensus on what constitutes a deity. Concepts range from "infinite transcendent being who created and lords over the universe" to a "finite entity or experience with special significance." Theism is belief in one or more deities; polytheism involves worship of multiple deities assembled into a pantheon. Henotheism accepts multiple deities as aspects of one divine principle; monolatry holds that many deities exist but only one may be worshipped. Monotheism holds that only one deity exists, usually described as omnipotent, omnipresent, omniscient, omnibenevolent, and eternal—though none of these qualities are essential to the definition. Deism posits a creator who does not intervene; pantheism equates the universe with God; pandeism proposes a creator who became a pantheistic universe; panentheism holds that divinity pervades but transcends the universe. Agnosticism holds that deity existence cannot be known; atheism is non-belief in any deity.

### Prehistoric evidence

Scholars infer probable prehistoric deities from inscriptions and cave drawings, though it is unclear what these depict. The once-common approach of interpreting virtually every prehistoric female figurine as a primordial goddess has been discredited. Modern archaeologists recognize that prehistoric figurines cannot be conclusively identified as deities, though case-by-case evaluation is possible. The Venus of Willendorf (c. 25,000 BCE) has been interpreted by some as a female deity. Probable deity representations have been found at 'Ain Ghazal and Çatalhöyük.

### Ancient Near Eastern religions

**Egyptian:** More than 1,400 deities are named in Egyptian texts (James P. Allen), with Christian Leitz estimating "thousands upon thousands." Terms were *nṯr* (god) and *nṯrt* (goddess), applying to any being outside everyday life, including spirits and deceased humans, but not demons. Most deities represented natural phenomena or social aspects as hidden immanent forces. Major deities included Min, Neith, Anubis, Atum, Bes, Horus, Isis, Ra, Meretseger, Nut, Osiris, Shu, Sia, and Thoth.

**Levantine:** Ancient Canaanites were polytheists; chief god El ruled with consort Asherah and their seventy sons. Baal was god of storm and fertility; Anat was goddess of war; Astarte was goddess of love. Israelites and Judahites originally believed in these deities alongside their national god Yahweh. El became syncretized with Yahweh. Monolatry was enforced during King Josiah's reforms (621 BCE); during the Babylonian captivity, some taught that other deities did not exist, and the "sons of El" became angels.

**Mesopotamian:** Deities (*dingir*) were almost exclusively anthropomorphic, generally immortal, though some (Dumuzid, Geshtinanna, Gugalanna) died or visited the underworld. The most important were the Anunnaki, including the "seven gods who decree": An, Enlil, Enki, Ninhursag, Nanna, Utu, and Inanna. After Sargon of Akkad's conquest, many were syncretized with East Semitic deities; Inanna became Ishtar. Marduk rose from obscurity to become creator of heaven, earth, and humankind, depicted zoomorphically as a "snake-dragon."

### Indo-European religions

**Germanic:** The terms cognate with "god" were originally neuter but became masculine after Christianisation. In Norse mythology, the Æsir were the principal gods (female: ásynjur), possibly rooted in an Indo-European root for "breath." The Vanir were associated with fertility; the Æsir–Vanir War ended in truce and reconciliation through exchanged hostages and intermarriage. Worship of Germanic gods continued in Scandinavia into the 12th century; Christian equivalents were substituted to suppress paganism. Heathenry has revived this worship.

**Greek:** Greek religion was polytheistic with no centralized church or sacred texts. Several deities trace to Indo-European traditions: Eos (dawn) is cognate to Indic Ushas and Roman Aurora; Zeus is cognate to Latin Iūpiter and Indic Dyaus. Aphrodite originated from the Near East. The Twelve Olympians were Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Aphrodite, Hermes, Demeter, Dionysus, Hephaestus, and Ares. Local deities included Pan, Nymphs, Naiads, Dryads, Nereids, satyrs, and the Erinyes. Greek deities were anthropomorphic "persons, not abstractions" (Walter Burkert), with unique expertise and flawed personalities; they were not omnipotent.

**Roman:** The Roman pantheon included Greek-syncretized deities (Venus, Apollo, Mars, Diana, Minerva, Ceres, Vulcan, Juno, Mercury, Vesta, Neptune, Jupiter) and non-Greek ones (Janus, Fortuna, Quirinus, Tellus). Varro distinguished three accounts of deities: mythical (poets), civil (people and city), and natural (philosophers). Cicero praised Varro's insights. Roman deities continued to be revered past the 313 CE Edict of Toleration.

### Native American religions

**Inca:** Viracocha was the abstract creator deity who existed before creating space and time. Inti (sun deity) ensured agricultural prosperity and was father of the first Inca king; Mama Qucha was goddess of the sea and waters. Other feminine deities included Mama Kuka, Mama Ch'aska, Mama Allpa, Mama Killa, and Mama Sara. Under Spanish colonialism, the Inca retained beliefs through syncretism: Inti became accepted as the Christian God while Andean rituals continued.

**Maya and Aztec:** Kukulkan was the Maya supreme creator deity, honored with step pyramid temples aligned to the spring equinox. Other Maya deities included Xib Chac (rain) and Ixchel (earth, weaving, pregnancy). The Maya calendar had 18 months of 20 days plus five unlucky days, each month with a presiding deity. The Aztec Quetzalcoatl resembled Kukulkan, but Aztec deity concepts remain poorly understood, as records were constructed by Christian missionaries. Aztec deities were usually zoomorphic or hybrid, not anthropomorphic.

### Polynesian

Polynesian theology centered on numerous deities, with island clusters using different names for the same ideas. The Māori called the supreme being Io, the original uncreated creator. Other deities included Tangaloa (creator of men), La'a Maomao (winds), Tu-Matauenga (war), Tu-Metua (mother goddess), Kane (procreation), and Rangi (sky father). Polynesian theology addressed creation, existence, guardianship, natural phenomena, and the journey of souls.

### Abrahamic religions

**Christianity:** Most mainstream congregations accept the Holy Trinity: three equal, cosubstantial persons—Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Origen first described them as *homooúsios* ("of the same substance"). Early Subordinationism (Father superior to Son) was condemned at the First Council of Nicaea (4th century). The Council of Chalcedon (451 CE) declared that in Jesus Christ, "fullness of deity and fullness of humanity are united."

**Islam:** *Ilah* (Arabic for "god") appears in *Allah* ("the god"). Islam is strictly monotheistic; the shahada declares "there is no ʾilāh (deity) but Allah." Persian *Khuda* and Turkic *Tengri* (Tanrı) also refer to God.

**Judaism:** Judaism affirms one God (Yahweh/YHWH) who revealed himself through the Exodus and Exile. Monotheism was affirmed with certainty in the sixth-century "Second Isaiah." Modern scholarship suggests idolatry resurfaced multiple times in Jewish religious life despite core monotheistic teachings. God is referred to as "He" because Hebrew has no neuter and God is "an active rather than a passive creative force."

**Mandaeism:** Hayyi Rabbi ("The Great Life") is the supreme God from whom all things emanate; Yushamin emanated as the "Second Life." Mandaeans believe God created all worlds, formed the soul, and placed it in the human body via angels.

### Asian religions

**Hinduism:** The concept of God spans henotheism, monotheism, polytheism, panentheism, pantheism, and monism. Deities are called *Deva* (god) or *Devi* (goddess). In earliest Vedic literature, all supernatural beings were *Asuras*; benevolent ones became *Sura*, *Deva*, or *Devi*. Humans can be reborn as devas through ethical living and saintly karma, enjoying heavenly bliss until merit runs out.

**Jainism:** Jainism rejects a creator God but incorporates *devas* (celestial beings) in its cosmology. Humans can be reborn as devas through ethical living (e.g., non-violence/ahimsa). Devas have sensory organs, reason, consciousness, compassion, and finite life. Perfected souls (Jina) and devas are worship-worthy.

**Buddhism:** Buddhists do not believe in a creator deity, but deities are essential to cosmology, rebirth, and saṃsāra. Devas reside in a heavenly realm (subdivided into twenty-six sub-realms), are mortal, and are reborn after death. Rebirth there results from ethical living and good karma. The realm's pleasures cause attachment, preventing nirvana; nonetheless, most Theravada lay people pursue rituals motivated by potential rebirth into the deva realm. Mahayana includes Buddhas, bodhisattvas, and fierce deities.

**Anitism:** Philippine indigenous religions have multiple pantheons across more than a hundred ethnic groups. Examples include Bathala (Tagalog), Mangechay (Kapampangan), Malayari (Sambal), Melu (Blaan), and Kaptan (Bisaya).

**Musok:** Korean folk polytheism venerates *sillyeong*—supernatural beings with divine power, generally invisible but occasionally appearing as humans or natural objects. They include nature spirits, heroes, and deified ancestors, summoned by *mudangs* during *Gut* rituals. Over 130 sillyeongs have been identified.

**Shinto:** Shinto venerates *kami* (no singular/plural distinction). The term has been translated as "god" or "spirit," but scholars deem these translations "quite unsatisfactory." There are said to be eight million kami (connoting infinity); they are not omnipotent, omniscient, or necessarily immortal.

**Taoism:** Taoist deities divide into "gods" (天神 heaven, 地祇 ground, 物灵 animism, 地府神灵 netherworld, 人体之神 human body, 人鬼之神 human ghost) and "xian" (immortals who cultivated the Tao, with vast supernatural powers and immortality).

**Zoroastrianism:** Ahura Mazda ("mighty lord" + "wisdom") is the creator and sole God. Zoroaster taught he is most powerful but not omnipotent, as his evil twin Angra Mainyu is nearly as powerful. The *daevas* are evil spirits created by Angra Mainyu; people must choose between good and evil. Ahura Mazda will eventually defeat Angra Mainyu. Originally represented anthropomorphically, Zoroastrianism became fully aniconic by the end of the Sasanian Empire.

### Local, regional, and universal deities

A deity may originate as local (e.g., Pan as an Arcadian deity whose influence spread). The Roman Empire adopted regional deities from Greece (Apollo), Asia (Cybele, Mithras), and Egypt (Isis, Serapis). Roman authorities eventually favored Yahweh, first associated with a mountain in northwestern Arabia, who became the supreme god of Christianity.

### Skeptical interpretations

Rational explanations of deity belief date to Ancient Greece. Democritus argued deities arose from observing natural phenomena. Euhemerus (3rd century BCE) argued gods were originally mortal kings, posthumously deified (Euhemerism). Sigmund Freud suggested God-concepts project one's father. Children naturally believe in supernatural entities without religious introduction. Humans have an overactive agency-detection system, likely evolved for survival—perceiving intelligent threats was adaptive. Humans also think teleologically and ascribe meaning, possibly a side-effect of social intelligence. Stories of supernatural encounters are retold and embellished because they describe standard categories with counterintuitive properties. Sociologists propose deities reflect a culture's self-esteem: lonely societies invent wrathful gods; secure societies invent loving ones. Émile Durkheim stated gods represent an extension of social life; Matt Rossano suggests God-concepts enforce morality and build cooperative communities.

## Terms

- **Theism**: Belief in the existence of at least one deity who may interact with the world
- **Monotheism**: Belief that only one deity exists
- **Polytheism**: Belief in and worship of multiple deities assembled into a pantheon
- **Henotheism**: Acceptance of one supreme deity without denying others, viewed as aspects of the same divine principle
- **Monolatry**: Belief that many deities exist but only one may be worshipped
- **Deva/Devi**: Sanskrit terms for god/goddess, from a root meaning "heavenly, divine, anything of excellence"
- **Kami**: Japanese Shinto term for deities or spirits, with no singular/plural distinction
- **Anthropomorphic**: Having human form or characteristics
- **Syncretism**: The merging of different religious beliefs or deities
- **Euhemerism**: The theory that gods were originally mortal kings, posthumously deified

## Debates and open questions

- There is no universally accepted consensus on what constitutes a deity; concepts vary considerably across cultures.
- Prehistoric figurines cannot be conclusively identified as deities; the former approach of interpreting all female figurines as a primordial goddess has been discredited.
- Aztec deity concepts remain poorly understood because historical records were constructed by Christian missionaries; the original concepts were likely more complex.
- Modern scholarship suggests idolatry was not absent in biblical faith and resurfaced multiple times in Jewish religious life, challenging the classical presentation of Judaism as purely monotheistic.
- The translation of the Japanese term *kami* as "god" or "spirit" is deemed "quite unsatisfactory and misleading" by scholars, who urge against translating it into English.
