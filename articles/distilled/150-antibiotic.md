# Antibiotic

## Overview

Antibiotics are antimicrobial substances active against bacteria, used to treat and prevent bacterial infections. They are the most important type of antibacterial agent, either killing bacteria (bactericidal) or inhibiting their growth (bacteriostatic). While a limited number also possess antiprotozoal activity, they are ineffective against viruses or fungi. The term literally means "opposing life" (from Greek *anti*, "against" and *bios*, "life"), and in medical usage traditionally refers to naturally produced substances (like penicillin), whereas fully synthetic antibacterials (like sulfonamides) are distinct but included in antimicrobial chemotherapy. Antibiotics revolutionized medicine in the 20th century, but their overuse has driven antimicrobial resistance (AMR), a global threat associated with nearly 5 million deaths annually.

- **350–550 CE** — Ancient Sudanese (Nubian) societies consumed tetracycline from fermented grain beverages.

- **1907** — Ehrlich discovered salvarsan (arsphenamine), the first synthetic antibacterial organoarsenic compound.
- **1910** — Ehrlich and Hata announced drug "606" (Salvarsan) for treating syphilis.

- **1932/1933** — Gerhard Domagk's team developed Prontosil, the first sulfonamide and systemically active antibacterial.

- **1942** — Chain, Florey, and Abraham purified penicillin G; Waksman first used the term "antibiotic" in journal articles.
- **1945** — Fleming, Chain, and Florey shared the Nobel Prize in Medicine for penicillin development.

- **2003** — European Union banned antibiotics as growth-promotional agents in livestock.

## Body

### Etymology and definitions

The term "antibiosis" ("against life") was introduced by French bacteriologist Jean Paul Vuillemin. Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch first described the phenomenon in 1877. Selman Waksman renamed these drugs "antibiotics" in 1947, having first used the term in 1942 to describe substances produced by microorganisms antagonistic to other microbes in high dilution. This original definition excluded synthetic compounds like sulfonamides, but current usage applies "antibiotic" to any medication that kills or inhibits bacteria, regardless of origin. "Antibacterial" derives from Greek *anti* ("against") + *baktērion* (diminutive of "staff, cane"), because the first bacteria discovered were rod-shaped.

### Medical uses and administration

Antibiotics treat or prevent bacterial infections and sometimes protozoan infections (e.g., metronidazole for parasitic diseases). When the pathogen is unknown, empiric therapy uses a broad-spectrum antibiotic based on symptoms pending lab results; once identified, definitive therapy uses a narrow-spectrum antibiotic, chosen partly on cost. Identification reduces cost, toxicity, and resistance emergence. Antibiotics may be given prophylactically to at-risk populations: immunocompromised patients (especially HIV cases to prevent pneumonia), those on immunosuppressive drugs, cancer patients, and surgical patients to prevent incision infections. They also prevent bacteremia and infective endocarditis in dental prophylaxis. Use for secondary prevention of coronary heart disease is unsupported and may increase mortality and stroke.

Routes include oral (usual), intravenous or injection (severe systemic infections), and topical (eye drops for conjunctivitis, ear drops, skin conditions like acne). Topical application achieves high local concentration, reduces systemic absorption and toxicity, and lowers total volume required, but risks include difficult dosing, local hypersensitivity, and contact dermatitis. Antibiotics should be administered as soon as possible, especially in life-threatening infections.

### Side effects and interactions

Antibiotics are screened for negative effects before approval and are usually safe, but adverse effects range from fever and nausea to photodermatitis and anaphylaxis. Common side effects of oral antibiotics include diarrhea from disruption of intestinal flora, potentially causing overgrowth of pathogens like *Clostridioides difficile*; probiotics can help prevent this. Vaginal flora disruption may cause yeast overgrowth (*Candida*). Quinolones with systemic corticosteroids risk tendon damage. Some antibiotics damage mitochondria (causing oxidative stress, suggested as a fluoroquinolone side-effect mechanism) and the endoplasmic reticulum, and can affect chloroplasts.

Most studies indicate antibiotics do not interfere with birth control pills (failure rate ~1%). Risk factors include non-compliance, vomiting, diarrhea, and menstrual irregularities; backup contraception is advised in such cases. Rifampicin may increase hepatic liver enzyme activity, breaking down pill ingredients faster. Alcohol with certain antibiotics (metronidazole, tinidazole, cephamandole, latamoxef, cefoperazone, cefmenoxime, furazolidone) causes a disulfiram-like reaction—vomiting, nausea, shortness of breath—by inhibiting acetaldehyde dehydrogenase. Alcohol reduces efficacy of doxycycline and erythromycin succinate.

### Pharmacodynamics and combination therapy

Successful antimicrobial therapy depends on host defenses, infection location, and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic properties. Bactericidal activity often depends on bacterial growth phase, requiring ongoing metabolic activity. In vitro characterization determines minimum inhibitory concentration and minimum bactericidal concentration. Combination therapy (two or more antibiotics) delays or prevents resistance emergence in diseases like tuberculosis and provides synergistic effects. Fosfomycin has the highest number of synergistic combinations. Combinations may also be antagonistic (e.g., chloramphenicol and tetracyclines antagonize penicillins); bacteriostatic plus bactericidal combinations are generally antagonistic. Antibiotics may be co-administered with resistance-modifying agents, such as β-lactam antibiotics with β-lactamase inhibitors (clavulanic acid, sulbactam) for β-lactamase-producing bacteria.

### Classes and production

Antibiotics are classified by mechanism of action, chemical structure, or spectrum. Cell wall-targeting drugs (penicillins, cephalosporins), cell membrane-targeting drugs (polymyxins), and enzyme-interfering drugs (rifamycins, lipiarmycins, quinolones, sulfonamides) are bactericidal. Protein synthesis inhibitors (macrolides, lincosamides, tetracyclines) are usually bacteriostatic, except bactericidal aminoglycosides. Narrow-spectrum antibiotics target specific bacteria (gram-negative or gram-positive); broad-spectrum affect a wide range. After a 40-year break, four new classes were introduced in the late 2000s/early 2010s: cyclic lipopeptides (daptomycin), glycylcyclines (tigecycline), oxazolidinones (linezolid), and lipiarmycins (fidaxomicin).

Most modern antibacterials are semisynthetic modifications of natural compounds (beta-lactams from *Penicillium* fungi). Aminoglycosides are still isolated from living organisms; sulfonamides, quinolones, and oxazolidinones are purely synthetic. Most are small molecules under 1000 daltons. Since Florey and Chain's 1939 efforts, large-scale production uses fermentation, usually in strongly aerobic conditions.

### Resistance and misuse

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a naturally occurring process driven largely by misuse and overuse, though many globally lack access to essential antimicrobials. Nearly 5 million deaths annually are associated with AMR; 1.27 million deaths were attributable to it in 2019. Resistance reflects evolutionary processes during therapy: antibiotics select for strains with enhanced survival capacity. The Luria–Delbrück experiment (1943) demonstrated antibacterial selection for strains with pre-existing resistance genes. Resistance arises through inheritable mutations, horizontal gene transfer (more likely where antibiotics are frequently used), and can impose a biological fitness cost, though compensatory mutations may aid survival. Mechanisms include intrinsic resistance (target absent from genome) and acquired resistance (mutation or extra-chromosomal DNA acquisition). Plasmids can carry multiple resistance genes, conferring cross-resistance. "Superbugs" like NDM-1 enzyme-producing bacteria resist broad-spectrum beta-lactams; an *E. coli* resistant to colistin ("last line of defence") was identified in the US in 2016. Anaerobic bacteria like *Bacteroides* now show penicillin resistance rates exceeding 90%.

Misuse includes self-prescribing, treating viral infections (like the common cold), incorrect dosages, and failure to complete courses. One study found physicians more likely to prescribe antibiotics to patients who appeared to expect them. The US Interagency Task Force on Antimicrobial Resistance coordinates CDC, FDA, and NIH efforts; France's "Antibiotics are not automatic" campaign (2002) reduced unnecessary prescriptions. The UK restricted use in 1970 (Swann report 1969); the EU banned growth-promotional use in 2003. US federal bills (S.742, H.R. 2562) to phase out nontherapeutic livestock use were proposed but not passed, despite FDA action ordered by a 2012 court ruling. Antibiotic purchase for farm animals has increased yearly despite industry pledges.

### History

Before the early 20th century, treatments relied on medicinal folklore; mixtures with antimicrobial properties were described over 2,000 years ago. Ancient Egyptians and Greeks used mold and plant materials. Nubian mummies studied in the 1990s contained significant tetracycline levels, conjectured to come from beer. Synthetic antibiotic chemotherapy began with Paul Ehrlich in the late 1880s, who proposed selective dyes that would kill bacteria without harming the host. In 1907, he discovered salvarsan (arsphenamine), the first synthetic antibacterial organoarsenic compound, developed with Alfred Bertheim. Ehrlich and Sahachiro Hata achieved success with the 606th compound tested; Hoechst marketed it as Salvarsan in 1910 for syphilis. Ehrlich received the 1908 Nobel Prize for immunology. Prontosil, developed by Gerhard Domagk's team (1932/1933), was the first sulfonamide; its active drug sulfanilamide was not patentable as it was already used in dyes. Domagk received the 1939 Nobel Prize.

Observations of antibiosis between microorganisms date to the late 19th century. In 1874, Sir William Roberts noted *Penicillium glaucum* cultures showed no bacterial contamination. Vincenzo Tiberio published on mold extract antibacterial power in 1895. Ernest Duchesne's 1897 dissertation was the first scholarly work on moulds' therapeutic capabilities; he observed *E. coli* eliminated by *Penicillium glaucum* and that inoculated animals did not contract typhoid. In 1928, Fleming noticed *Penicillium rubens* spores on a culture plate killed bacteria; he named the substance penicillin. Chain, Florey, and Abraham purified penicillin G in 1942; Norman Heatley developed the back extraction technique for bulk purification. Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin confirmed the chemical structure in 1945. Penicillin had potent activity, low toxicity, and was not inhibited by pus (unlike sulfonamides). Chain and Florey shared the 1945 Nobel Prize with Fleming. Florey credited René Dubos with pioneering systematic antibacterial searches; Dubos reported tyrothricin (20% gramicidin, 80% tyrocidine) from *Bacillus brevis* in 1939, effective in treating wounds but too toxic for systemic use. From 1935 to 1968, 12 new antibiotic classes launched; only two followed between 1969 and 2003.

### Antibiotic pipeline and new therapies

The WHO and Infectious Disease Society of America report the weak pipeline does not match bacteria's increasing resistance. Fifty-one new therapeutic entities were in phase 1–3 trials as of May 2017. Recently approved drugs include ceftaroline, oritavancin, telavancin, dalbavancin, tedizolid, fidaxomicin (first in a new narrow-spectrum macrocyclic class), and combinations ceftazidime-avibactam and ceftolozane-avibactam. The US ADAPT Act aims to fast-track antibiotic development, allowing FDA approval based on smaller clinical trials. New therapies under development include: natural product-based discovery (70–80% of current antibiotics derive from actinomycetes; some products inhibit efflux pumps or virulence factors); immunoglobulin therapy (monoclonal antibodies like bezlotoxumab for recurrent *C. difficile*); phage therapy (bacteriophages specific to certain bacteria, not disturbing intestinal microbiota, but with safety and regulatory hurdles); fecal microbiota transplants (~90% cure rate for *C. difficile*, not officially FDA-approved); antisense RNA treatments (silencing essential genes or resistance genes like *mecA*); and CRISPR-Cas9-based treatments (modifying the system to target resistance or virulence genes). Reducing selection pressure involves infection control, sanitation, vaccines, antibiotic stewardship, and prebiotics/probiotics; antibiotic cycling has proven ineffective.

## Terms

- **Antibiotic**: Substance produced by a microorganism (or, in current usage, any medication) that kills or inhibits bacteria.
- **Antibacterial**: Broader term including bactericides, bacteriostatics, antibacterial soaps, and disinfectants; antibiotics are a subset.
- **Bactericidal**: Agent that kills bacteria (e.g., penicillins, quinolones).
- **Bacteriostatic**: Agent that inhibits bacterial growth (e.g., macrolides, tetracyclines).
- **Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)**: Naturally occurring process where microbes evolve to survive antibiotics, driven by misuse and overuse.
- **Empiric therapy**: Broad-spectrum antibiotic given when the pathogen is unknown, pending lab results.
- **Minimum inhibitory concentration**: Lowest concentration of an antibacterial that prevents bacterial growth in vitro.
- **Horizontal gene transfer**: Exchange of resistance genes between bacteria via plasmids or other mechanisms.
- **Superbug**: Antibiotic-resistant bacterial strain that contributes to diseases previously well controlled.
- **Phage therapy**: Using bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria) to treat antibiotic-resistant infections.

## Debates and open questions

- Whether antibiotics interfere with oral contraceptives remains debated; most studies suggest low risk (~1% failure), but effects on intestinal flora and hepatic enzymes are inconclusive and controversial, requiring more studies.
- The effectiveness of antibiotic cycling (alternating antibiotics) has been proposed but recent studies show such strategies are ineffective against resistance.
- Regulatory and legislative delays in limiting livestock antibiotic use persist, partly due to industry resistance and time needed to test causal links between use and resistance.
- Fecal microbiota transplants are not officially FDA-approved but permitted under some conditions; work continues on standardization and oral delivery methods.
