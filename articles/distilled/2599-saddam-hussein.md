# Saddam Hussein

## Overview
Saddam Hussein was an Iraqi politician and revolutionary who dominated his country's politics for 35 years, serving as president from 1979 until his overthrow in 2003. A leading member of the Ba'ath Party, he championed an ideology of Arab nationalism and socialism known as Saddamism. His rule was marked by major wars, economic development, and severe human rights abuses, making him a deeply polarizing figure viewed as both a strong leader and a brutal tyrant.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Born to a Sunni Arab family, Saddam joined the Ba'ath Party in 1957. He gained early notoriety for his role in a failed 1959 assassination attempt on Prime Minister Abdul-Karim Qasim, after which he fled to Egypt. He returned to Iraq after the 1963 Ba'athist coup but was imprisoned from 1964 to 1966. As Vice President under Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr from 1968, he consolidated power by overseeing the nationalization of oil, modernizing infrastructure, and building a loyal security apparatus. He formally became president in 1979 after forcing al-Bakr's resignation.

### Domestic Rule and Repression
Saddam's regime was authoritarian, with power concentrated among Sunni Arabs, a minority of the population. He maintained control through a vast network of paramilitary and intelligence organizations, including the Mukhabarat (secret police), which used torture and assassination. His government was responsible for the murder or disappearance of an estimated 250,000 to 290,000 Iraqis. Conversely, he used oil wealth to fund extensive social programs, including free healthcare, education, and infrastructure projects. His government also promoted women's rights, achieving high female literacy and workforce participation rates.

### Major Conflicts
Saddam led Iraq into three major wars. The Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988) began with his invasion of Iran, ostensibly to prevent the spread of its Islamic Revolution. The conflict, which included Iraq's use of chemical weapons, ended in a stalemate with massive casualties. During this war, he also ordered the genocidal Anfal campaign against Kurdish rebels. In 1990, he invaded Kuwait over economic disputes and territorial claims, leading to the Gulf War (1990–1991), which ended in a decisive defeat by a US-led coalition. The war resulted in crippling UN sanctions and the suppression of internal uprisings.

### Final Years and Overthrow
In the 1990s, Saddam pursued an Islamist agenda through the Faith Campaign and faced continued US military pressure. In 2003, a US-led coalition invaded Iraq, citing (incorrect) claims of weapons of mass destruction and ties to terrorism. Saddam's regime collapsed quickly, and he was captured in December 2003. Tried by the Iraqi High Tribunal for the 1982 Dujail massacre, he was convicted and executed on 30 December 2006.

## Terms
- **Ba'athism**: An ideology combining Arab nationalism and Arab socialism, advocating for a unified Arab world.
- **Saddamism**: The specific policies and cult of personality developed by Saddam Hussein, blending Ba'athism with authoritarian rule.
- **Mukhabarat**: The Iraqi secret police, feared for its use of torture and assassination to eliminate opponents.
- **Anfal campaign**: A genocidal campaign (1986–1989) led by Ali Hassan al-Majid against Kurdish civilians and rebels in northern Iraq.
- **Faith Campaign**: A program initiated in 1993 to promote Islam in Iraqi society, including adding "Allahu Akbar" to the flag.
- **Oil-for-Food Programme**: A UN initiative (1996–2003) allowing Iraq to sell oil to buy humanitarian goods under sanctions.
- **Algiers Agreement**: A 1975 treaty with Iran that settled border disputes, including control of the Shatt al-Arab waterway.
- **Revolutionary Command Council (RCC)**: The Ba'athist governing body in Iraq, with Saddam as its chairman.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **International Support**: The extent of US and other foreign governments' support for Saddam during the Iran–Iraq War, including allegations of CIA involvement in early plots, remains debated due to classified documents.
- **Responsibility for Halabja**: While Iraq is now widely accepted as responsible for the 1988 chemical attack on Halabja, initial US and Iraqi government claims blamed Iran.
- **Nature of the Regime**: Scholars debate whether Saddam's government was totalitarian, authoritarian, or fascist, with some arguing key differences from classic totalitarian models like Hitler's or Stalin's.
- **Legacy in the Arab World**: Saddam remains a polarizing figure; some Arabs view him as a resolute leader against Western imperialism, while many Iraqis, especially Shias and Kurds, remember him as a brutal oppressor.
