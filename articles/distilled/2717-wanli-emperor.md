# Wanli Emperor

## Overview
The Wanli Emperor (r. 1572–1620), the Ming dynasty's 14th ruler, reigned for 48 years—the dynasty's longest. His rule began with effective governance under Grand Secretary Zhang Juzheng but devolved into imperial withdrawal, factional strife, and costly military campaigns. His reign is traditionally seen as a turning point toward Ming decline, though modern scholarship debates his personal responsibility versus systemic flaws in the Confucian bureaucratic system.

## Timeline
- **1572** — Wanli ascends the throne at age nine; Zhang Juzheng becomes dominant Grand Secretary.
- **1582** — Zhang Juzheng dies; Wanli dismantles his policies and purges his followers.
- **1592** — Japanese invasion of Korea (Imjin War) begins; Ming intervention follows.
- **1592** — Rebellion in Ningxia is quelled by Ming forces.
- **1596** — Wanli launches eunuch-led mining tax operations.
- **1597** — Second Japanese invasion of Korea is thwarted.
- **1599** — Yang Yinglong rebellion in the southwest is suppressed.
- **1601** — Wanli appoints his eldest son, Zhu Changluo, as heir after a 15-year dispute.
- **1606** — Mining tax operations are abolished due to corruption and opposition.
- **1618** — Nurhaci, leader of the Jurchens, raids Fushun, triggering war.
- **1619** — Ming armies are decisively defeated by the Jurchens at the Battle of Sarhu.
- **1620** — Wanli dies; succeeded by his son, the Taichang Emperor.

## Body

### Early Reign and Zhang Juzheng's Leadership (1572–1582)
Zhang Juzheng, with support from the Empress Dowager and eunuch Feng Bao, effectively ruled for the first decade. He implemented Legalist-inspired reforms to "enrich the country and strengthen the army," including the "single whip reform" (converting tax payments to silver) and administrative streamlining. These policies generated significant state surpluses, with granaries holding nine years' grain and treasuries amassing millions in silver by 1582. Zhang also pursued a defensive military strategy, appointing capable generals like Qi Jiguang. His centralized power and refusal to observe mourning for his father drew criticism, which intensified after his death.

### Later Reign and Imperial Withdrawal (1582–1620)
After Zhang's death, the Emperor grew disillusioned with bureaucratic attacks and factionalism. He increasingly withdrew from governance, canceling court audiences and leaving high offices vacant. By the early 1600s, nearly half of prefectural and over half of ministerial posts were unfilled. While basic administration continued, this paralysis weakened central control. The Emperor's authority was further challenged by the rise of collective debates and the growing influence of "public opinion" (gonglun) in official evaluations, which elevated moral character over administrative competence.

### Succession Dispute (1586–1614)
A prolonged conflict erupted over the succession between the Emperor's eldest son, Zhu Changluo, and his favored third son, Zhu Changxun. The Emperor delayed appointing an heir for over fifteen years, clashing with officials who upheld primogeniture. The dispute culminated in the 1615 "case of the attack with a stick," where a mentally unstable man was found in the heir's palace, sparking assassination rumors. The Emperor was forced to publicly affirm his relationship with his eldest son, who was finally named heir in 1601.

### Mine Tax Crisis (1596–1606)
To replenish the treasury, the Emperor dispatched eunuchs to oversee silver mining and tax collection, bypassing the traditional bureaucracy. This created a parallel eunuch administration that extorted funds from the gentry and populace. The operation was deeply unpopular with Confucian officials, who saw it as the state competing with the people for profit. After realizing the eunuchs were corrupt and the tax was harmful, the Emperor abolished the mining operations in 1606.

### Factional Struggles and the Donglin Movement (1604–1620)
In 1604, the Donglin Academy was established, becoming a hub for intellectuals advocating strict Confucian morality and criticizing the Emperor's policies. The movement opposed eunuch power and factionalism but often prioritized ideological purity over pragmatic governance. Factional disputes, frequently played out through official evaluations and purges, paralyzed the bureaucracy. The Donglin and their opponents used these evaluations to eliminate rivals, further destabilizing the government.

### Military Campaigns
The Wanli era saw major military efforts. The Ming successfully quelled the Ningxia rebellion (1592) and the Yang Yinglong rebellion in the southwest (1599–1600). The most significant conflict was the Imjin War (1592–1598), where Ming forces, alongside the Korean navy, repelled two Japanese invasions at great financial cost. However, the reign ended with a catastrophic defeat at the Battle of Sarhu (1619), where the rising Jurchen leader Nurhaci destroyed Ming armies, capturing part of Liaodong and signaling the dynasty's future threat.

## Terms
- **Grand Secretary (Dàxuéshì):** A high-ranking official in the Ming "Grand Secretariat," an advisory body that effectively ran the government in the emperor's name.
- **Single Whip Reform:** A fiscal policy consolidating various taxes and corvée labor obligations into a single payment in silver.
- **Corvée Labor:** A form of tax paid through unpaid work on public projects.
- **Censorate:** A government agency responsible for monitoring and impeaching officials.
- **Donglin Academy:** A scholarly and political movement advocating Confucian moral reform and criticizing the Wanli Emperor's policies.
- **Primogeniture:** The right of the firstborn son to inherit the throne.
- **Jurchens:** A semi-nomadic people from Manchuria who later founded the Qing dynasty.
- **Banners:** A social and military organization system used by the Jurchens/Manchus.
- **Gonglun (Public Opinion):** A concept in late Ming governance where collective official sentiment was used to evaluate and judge officials.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Imperial Responsibility vs. Systemic Failure:** Traditional historiography blames the Wanli Emperor's laziness and greed for the Ming decline. Modern scholars debate whether his withdrawal was a rational response to an unworkable system where Confucian moralism paralyzed effective governance.
- **The "Trapped" Emperor:** Historian Ray Huang famously portrayed Wanli as a ruler "trapped" by his bureaucracy, unable to enact his will. Others argue he retained significant power, as seen in his military funding and the mine tax episode.
- **Assessment of Military Policy:** While the reign ended with the defeat at Sarhu, the Ming military was active and largely successful in earlier campaigns (Ningxia, Korea, Bozhou). Scholars debate whether the later failures were due to imperial neglect or the overwhelming challenge posed by the unified Jurchen state.
