# Western New Guinea

## Overview
Western New Guinea, also known as Papua, is the western half of the island of New Guinea, which was formerly a Dutch colony and was transferred to Indonesia in 1962. It is a region of immense cultural and biological diversity, home to hundreds of indigenous groups and languages, but has also been marked by political conflict over self-determination since its integration into Indonesia.

## Timeline
- **c. 50,000 years ago** — Human habitation of the region begins.
- **16th century** — European traders, including Spanish and Portuguese, arrive; the name "Papua" is recorded.
- **1824** — The Netherlands and Britain agree the western half of New Guinea will be part of the Dutch East Indies.
- **1942-1944** — Japan occupies the region during World War II; Allied forces, led by the US, liberate it in 1944.
- **1961** — The Dutch recognize a Papuan national flag and anthem, signaling preparation for independence.
- **1962** — The New York Agreement transfers administration from the Netherlands to a UN temporary authority (UNTEA).
- **1963** — UNTEA transfers administration to Indonesia.
- **1969** — The controversial "Act of Free Choice" referendum results in the region's formal integration into Indonesia.
- **2001** — The region is granted "Special Autonomy" status by the Indonesian government.
- **2003** — The region is divided into two provinces: Papua and West Papua.
- **2022** — Four new provinces are created, bringing the total to six autonomous provinces within the region.

## Body

### Name and Identity
The name for the western half of New Guinea is politically charged. The official Indonesian and ISO name is "Papua." Independence activists use "West Papua," while the Indonesian government has also used "West Papua" for its westernmost province since 2007. Historically, it was known as Netherlands New Guinea (1895–1962), West New Guinea or West Irian (1962–1973), and Irian Jaya (1973–2002). The origin of "Papua" is debated, with theories linking it to the Tidore language meaning "not united" or a Malay word for "frizzled hair."

### Pre-Colonial and Colonial History
The region has been inhabited for over 50,000 years, with agriculture developing independently in the highlands. Austronesian peoples settled coastal areas around 3,000 years ago. From the 13th century, the island was part of the Majapahit Empire's sphere. By the 16th century, Muslim sultanates from the Moluccas, particularly Tidore, controlled coastal trade. The Dutch gradually established control, formally purchasing the territory from the Sultanate of Tidore in 1901 and incorporating it into the Dutch East Indies. Dutch control was limited, mostly to coastal areas, and they regarded the territory as a "wasteland."

### Integration into Indonesia
After World War II, the Netherlands prepared the territory for independence, fostering a Papuan national identity with a flag and anthem. However, Indonesia claimed the territory. Following military infiltrations by Indonesia and US mediation, the 1962 New York Agreement transferred administration to the UN, then to Indonesia in 1963. A 1969 referendum, the "Act of Free Choice," saw 1,026 hand-picked elders vote unanimously for integration under conditions described by critics as coerced. This integration is contested by the Free Papua Movement (OPM), which has waged a low-level insurgency since 1965.

### Demographics and Society
The 2022 population was approximately 5.6 million. The interior is predominantly indigenous Papuan, while coastal areas have mixed populations, including descendants of Papuan, Melanesian, and Austronesian intermarriage, as well as migrants from other Indonesian islands. The region is home to an estimated 200 to over 700 local languages. Christianity is the predominant religion, followed by Islam. The economy relies on agriculture, fishing, and natural resource extraction, notably the Grasberg Mine, one of the world's largest gold and copper mines.

### Geography and Ecology
The region is geologically part of the Australian continent, featuring the Maoke Mountains with peaks over 4,000 meters, including Puncak Jaya. It is covered in dense rainforest and has extraordinary biodiversity, with many endemic species of plants, marsupials, and birds-of-paradise. Key protected areas include Lorentz National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage site. Environmental threats include deforestation and pollution from mining.

## Terms
- **Act of Free Choice**: The 1969 UN-supervised referendum in which 1,026 Papuan representatives, selected by Indonesia, voted unanimously for integration with Indonesia. Its legitimacy is widely disputed.
- **Free Papua Movement (OPM)**: A separatist organization formed in 1965 that has waged an insurgency against Indonesian rule.
- **Special Autonomy**: A legal status granted to the Papua region in 2001, intended to give local government greater control and a larger share of resource revenues, though its implementation is debated.
- **Transmigration**: A Dutch and later Indonesian government program to resettle people from densely populated islands like Java to less populated areas, including Papua, which has altered the region's demographics.
- **Papuan Malay**: A Malay-based creole language that serves as the main lingua franca in the region.
- **Maoke Mountains**: The central mountain range of New Guinea, formed by tectonic collision, containing the island's highest peaks.
- **Grasberg Mine**: A massive gold and copper mine in the highlands, operated by PT Freeport Indonesia, which dominates the regional economy.
- **Noken**: A traditional system of decision-making in some Papuan communities where a tribal chief represents the will of the group.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The legitimacy of the 1969 "Act of Free Choice" remains a central point of contention, with critics calling it a "legitimised forced incorporation" and independence activists demanding a new, free referendum.
- The effectiveness and fairness of the "Special Autonomy" policy are debated, with some arguing it has not been fully implemented or has failed to address core grievances.
- The impact of transmigration and spontaneous migration on indigenous Papuan culture, political power, and land rights is a source of ongoing tension and conflict.
- The scale of human rights abuses and the death toll from the conflict since 1963 are disputed, with estimates ranging from tens of thousands to over 500,000.
