# Kingdom of Aksum

## Overview
The Kingdom of Aksum was a major trading empire in the Horn of Africa and South Arabia from the 1st century AD to around 960 AD. Based in modern Eritrea and northern Ethiopia, it became one of the four great powers of the ancient world alongside Rome, Persia, and China. Its strategic location on Red Sea trade routes and its adoption of Christianity made it a pivotal state connecting the Roman/Byzantine world with the Indian Ocean trade network.

## Timeline
- **c. 150 BCE–150 CE** — Aksumite polity emerges in the Horn of Africa.
- **1st century AD** — First historical mention in the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*; kingdom involved in Rome-India trade.
- **c. 200–230** — King Gedara expands Aksumite control into western Yemen.
- **270–310** — King Endybis begins minting coins; Aksum gains global recognition.
- **c. 320–c. 360** — King Ezana converts Aksum to Christianity, making it the state religion.
- **330** — Aksum conquers Meroë for a short period.
- **514–542** — King Kaleb invades Himyar (Yemen) at Byzantine request, reaching Aksum's greatest territorial extent.
- **570–575** — Aksum loses control of Yemen to the Sassanid Persian Empire.
- **Early 8th century** — Coinage ceases to be minted, signaling economic decline.
- **c. 960** — Kingdom collapses, possibly following an invasion by Queen Gudit.

## Body

### Rise and Early Power
Aksum emerged from the earlier Dʿmt civilization and developed into a significant power by the 1st century AD. Its capital, Axum, and port, Adulis, were central to a lucrative trade network exporting ivory, gold, and exotic goods to the Roman Empire. By the 3rd century, Aksum was recognized as a world power. Under King Gedara (c. 200–230), it expanded into South Arabia, controlling parts of modern Yemen until pushed out by a Himyarite-Sabaean alliance. Aksumite coins, first minted under King Endybis (270–310), have been found as far as India, demonstrating its wide trade connections.

### Christianization and Zenith
In the mid-4th century, King Ezana converted to Christianity, making Aksum one of the first states to adopt it officially. This created a strong cultural and political link with the Byzantine Empire. Aksum continued to expand, conquering Meroë and inheriting the name "Ethiopia." Its power peaked under King Kaleb (514–542), who invaded Himyar to protect Christians, annexing the region and reaching an estimated territorial extent of 2,500,000 km². However, Aksum's control in Yemen was challenged by local rulers like Abraha and eventually lost to the Sassanid Persians in the late 6th century.

### Decline and Fall
Aksum's decline began in the 7th century due to multiple factors. The rise of Islam shifted Red Sea trade routes away from Aksumite control. Persian and later Muslim dominance in the region, combined with the decline of its major trade partner, the Roman/Byzantine Empire, caused severe economic hardship. Internal pressures, including the expansion of Beja nomads who severed Aksum's link to its ports, forced the population inland. The capital was moved south, and the kingdom entered a "dark age." The final collapse around 960 is traditionally attributed to an invasion by Queen Gudit, though her historical existence is debated.

## Terms
- **Geʽez**: The ancient Semitic language of the Aksumites, which developed its own script (an abugida) and became the liturgical language of the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches.
- **Stelae (Obelisks)**: Tall, carved granite monuments erected to mark royal tombs, representing multi-story palaces and serving as major architectural achievements.
- **Adulis**: Aksum's primary port on the Red Sea, crucial for its trade with the Roman Empire and India.
- **Miaphysite**: A Christological position held by the Aksumite (Ethiopian) Church, which rejected the Council of Chalcedon (451) and maintained a distinct form of Oriental Orthodoxy.
- **Abugida**: A writing system where each character represents a consonant with an inherent vowel, which can be modified; the Geʽez script is an example.
- **First Hijrah**: The migration of early Muslims from Mecca to Aksum in 615 AD, where they were granted refuge by the Aksumite king.
- **Zagwe dynasty**: The dynasty that succeeded the Aksumite Empire in the 11th or 12th century, known for rock-hewn churches like those at Lalibela.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact cause and circumstances of the kingdom's final collapse around 960 are uncertain. The role and historical reality of the conquering queen Gudit (Yodit) are disputed, with her identity, origin, and religion debated by scholars.
- The extent of Jewish influence on Aksumite culture before its conversion to Christianity is debated, with some scholars suggesting a significant impact and others arguing it was minor.
- The precise location of the post-Aksumite capital, Kubar, remains unknown.
- The relative importance of different factors in Aksum's decline—such as Islamic trade shifts, environmental degradation, nomadic invasions, and internal strife—is a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion.
