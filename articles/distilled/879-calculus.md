# Calculus

## Overview
Calculus is the branch of mathematics that studies continuous change, comprising two major branches: differential calculus, which deals with instantaneous rates of change and slopes of curves, and integral calculus, which concerns the accumulation of quantities and areas under curves. These branches are connected by the fundamental theorem of calculus, which establishes that differentiation and integration are inverse operations. Developed independently by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in the late 17th century, calculus serves as the mathematical backbone for modeling dynamic systems across science, engineering, and economics.

## Body

### Limits and Infinitesimals
Calculus was originally developed using infinitesimals—objects treated like real numbers but infinitely small, greater than zero yet smaller than any positive real number. This approach fell out of favor in the 19th century due to difficulties in making the concept precise. The epsilon-delta approach to limits replaced infinitesimals, describing function behavior at inputs through values at nearby inputs using the real number system's intrinsic structure. Limits became the standard rigorous foundation during the 20th century, though infinitesimals were revived through non-standard analysis and smooth infinitesimal analysis.

### Differential Calculus
Differential calculus studies derivatives—the encoding of small-scale function behavior near a point. The derivative generalizes slope from straight lines to curves, representing the slope of the tangent line. For a function f at point a, the derivative is defined as the limit of the difference quotient: lim_{h→0} [f(a+h)−f(a)]/h. In Lagrange notation, the derivative of f is f′; in Leibniz notation, it is dy/dx. When input represents time, the derivative represents rate of change—for example, position's derivative gives velocity.

### Integral Calculus
Integral calculus studies indefinite integrals (antiderivatives) and definite integrals. The indefinite integral reverses differentiation, producing a family of functions differing by a constant (the constant of integration). The definite integral computes the algebraic sum of areas between a function's graph and the x-axis, defined as the limit of Riemann sums—sums of rectangle areas approximating the region. The integral symbol ∫ suggests summation, and dx indicates the variable of integration.

### Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
The fundamental theorem states that differentiation and integration are inverse operations. If f is continuous on [a,b] and F is its antiderivative, then ∫_a^b f(x)dx = F(b)−F(a). Additionally, d/dx ∫_a^x f(t)dt = f(x). This theorem, realized by both Newton and Leibniz, enables practical computation of definite integrals through antiderivatives rather than limit processes, and serves as a prototype for solving differential equations.

### Advanced Topics
Multivariable and vector calculus extend calculus to functions of several variables and vector fields in ℝ³, playing key roles in differential geometry and partial differential equations. Differential equations relate unknown functions to their derivatives, modeling physical quantities and their rates of change across disciplines. Real analysis provides rigorous foundations, while complex analysis studies functions of complex variables, where differentiability is more restrictive but yields infinitely differentiable holomorphic functions. The calculus of variations studies functionals—mappings from functions to real numbers—using the Euler-Lagrange equation to find functions that optimize functionals.

### History and Foundations
Modern calculus emerged in 17th-century Europe from Newton and Leibniz building on earlier work from Greece, China, India, the Middle East, and Japan. Early calculus faced criticism for its use of infinitesimals, notably Berkeley's "ghosts of departed quantities." Rigorous foundations were established through Cauchy and Weierstrass's work on limits, later incorporated into real analysis. Alternative rigorous approaches include non-standard analysis (hyperreal numbers) and smooth infinitesimal analysis (category theory-based).

## Terms
- **Infinitesimal**: An object treated like a real number but infinitely small—greater than zero yet smaller than any positive real number
- **Derivative**: A function encoding small-scale behavior of another function near a point; geometrically, the slope of the tangent line
- **Limit**: A value describing function behavior at an input based on values at nearby inputs, formalized through epsilon-delta definitions
- **Riemann sum**: A sum of rectangle areas approximating the area under a curve, whose limit defines the definite integral
- **Antiderivative**: A function whose derivative equals a given function; the indefinite integral
- **Holomorphic function**: A complex function differentiable in a neighborhood of each point in its domain
- **Functional**: A mapping from a set of functions to the real numbers
- **Constant of integration**: The unspecified constant C in an antiderivative, representing a family of functions differing by a constant

## Debates and Open Questions
The priority dispute between Newton and Leibniz over who first developed calculus divided English-speaking and continental European mathematicians for many years. The extent to which they were influenced by predecessors—particularly what Leibniz learned from Isaac Barrow—remains difficult to determine. Additionally, debates continue over foundational approaches: while limits became the standard 19th-century foundation, non-standard analysis and smooth infinitesimal analysis offer alternative rigorous frameworks that more closely align with the original Newton-Leibniz conception.
