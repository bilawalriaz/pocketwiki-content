# Civil engineering

## Overview
Civil engineering is the professional discipline concerned with designing, constructing, and maintaining the physical and naturally built environment, including public works such as roads, bridges, canals, dams, and sewage systems. It is traditionally considered the second-oldest engineering discipline after military engineering, from which it was explicitly distinguished in the 18th century to denote non-military infrastructure. The field applies principles of physics, mathematics, and materials science to solve societal problems, evolving from ancient artisanal craft guilds into a formalized profession with standardized education, licensure, and a strict code of ethics. Today, it encompasses numerous specialized sub-disciplines that coordinate to deliver complex infrastructure projects across public and private sectors.

## Timeline
- **c. 4000–2000 BC** — Earliest practice emerges in ancient Egypt, Indus Valley, and Mesopotamia with settlement construction and transport development (wheel, sailing).
- **c. 2700–2500 BC** — Construction of Egyptian pyramids; early large-scale structural engineering.
- **c. 3rd century BC** — Archimedes establishes early scientific principles (buoyancy, Archimedes' screw) applicable to civil engineering.
- **c. 312 BC** — Roman engineers construct the Appian Way; Romans develop extensive empire-wide infrastructure (aqueducts, roads, bridges).
- **7th century AD** — Brahmagupta uses Hindu-Arabic arithmetic for excavation volume computations in India.
- **1747** — École Nationale des Ponts et Chaussées founded in France; first institution for civil engineering education.
- **1771** — John Smeaton (first self-proclaimed civil engineer) forms the Smeatonian Society of Civil Engineers.
- **1818** — Institution of Civil Engineers (ICE) founded in London.
- **1819** — Norwich University founded in US; first private college to teach civil engineering.
- **1828** — ICE receives Royal Charter, formally recognizing civil engineering as a profession.
- **1835** — Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute awards first US civil engineering degree.
- **1905** — Cornell University awards first US civil engineering degree to a woman (Nora Stanton Blatch).

## Body

### History and Professionalization
Civil engineering’s history is intrinsically linked to advances in physics and mathematics. Throughout ancient and medieval periods, construction was executed by artisans (stonemasons, carpenters) organized in craft guilds, with knowledge passed empirically rather than scientifically; structures were repetitive and scale increases incremental. Early scientific exceptions include Archimedes (3rd century BC) and Brahmagupta (7th century AD). The Romans systematized infrastructure across their empire, building aqueducts, roads, and harbors. The term "civil engineering" was coined in the 18th century to differentiate civilian public works from military engineering. Professional formalization began with the founding of the École Nationale des Ponts et Chaussées (1747) in France. John Smeaton, builder of the Eddystone Lighthouse, became the first self-proclaimed civil engineer and formed the Smeatonian Society (1771). The Institution of Civil Engineers (ICE), founded in London (1818) and granted a Royal Charter (1828), codified the profession’s definition: directing natural power sources for human convenience, trade, and transportation. In the US, education began at Norwich University (1819), with the first degree awarded by Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute (1835); the first woman graduate was Nora Stanton Blatch at Cornell (1905).

### Education and Licensure
Civil engineers typically hold a bachelor’s degree (BEng/BSc or Bachelor of Technology), requiring three to five years of study covering physics, mathematics, project management, design, and broad sub-discipline surveying before specialization. Post-graduate degrees (MEng/MSc) allow further specialization. In most countries, an accredited degree is the first step toward professional certification. Candidates must subsequently satisfy work experience and examination requirements. Certified titles vary by jurisdiction: Professional Engineer (US, Canada, South Africa), Chartered Engineer (Commonwealth), Chartered Professional Engineer (Australia, New Zealand), or European Engineer (EU). International agreements facilitate cross-border practice. Licensure carries legal weight: in the US and Canada, only a licensed engineer may sign, seal, and submit plans to public authorities (enforced by laws like Quebec’s Engineers Act); the UK lacks such legislation, while Australia limits state licensing to Queensland. Engineers are bound by contract law, tort law (negligence), criminal law in extreme failures, building codes, environmental regulations, and professional codes of ethics.

### Sub-disciplines
The field comprises numerous specialized sub-disciplines, often overlapping on projects.

*   **General / Site Engineering:** Focuses on converting land usage (grading, drainage, pavement, utilities), coordinating with surveyors and specialists.
*   **Structural Engineering:** Designs and analyzes buildings, bridges, towers, tunnels, and offshore structures to withstand loads (dead, live, wind, seismic, thermal). Sub-fields include **Earthquake Engineering** (designing for seismic compliance) and **Wind Engineering**. Core considerations: strength, stiffness, stability, cost, safety, sustainability.
*   **Geotechnical Engineering:** Studies rock and soil mechanics to design foundations, retaining walls, and landfills. Challenges include soil variability, nonlinear stress-dependent behavior (stiffness, dilatancy), and limited investigation access. Closely linked to **Geo-environmental Engineering** (groundwater protection).
*   **Transportation Engineering:** Plans, designs, and operates infrastructure for moving people/goods (streets, highways, rail, airports, ports, mass transit). Involves traffic engineering, pavement design, queueing theory, and Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS).
*   **Environmental Engineering:** Contemporary term for sanitary engineering, expanded to include hazardous waste management and remediation. Covers water/air purification, wastewater treatment, solid waste/recycling, pollutant transport, and industrial ecology.
*   **Water Resources & Hydraulic Engineering:** Manages water as a natural resource (hydrology, meteorology, conservation). **Hydraulic Engineering** specifically addresses fluid flow/conveyance for pipelines, drainage, dams, canals, and levees using fluid statics/dynamics.
*   **Construction Engineering:** Manages planning, execution, material transport, and site development. Higher business risk drives focus on contracts, logistics, and supply pricing.
*   **Coastal Engineering:** Manages coastal zones against flooding (sea defense) and erosion (coastal protection), increasingly termed "coastal management."
*   **Materials Science & Engineering:** Studies fundamental properties of construction materials (concrete, asphalt, steel, aluminum, polymers, carbon fibers). Includes protection (paints), alloying, and nanotechnology research; critical for forensic analysis.
*   **Surveying:** Measures Earth-surface dimensions (angles, distances) using levels, theodolites, EDM, GPS, and laser scanning to produce maps for design and legal boundaries. Distinct profession (Land Surveying) with separate licensing (Cadastral surveying for boundaries/subdivisions); **Construction Surveying** (non-legal status) handles site layout and as-built verification.
*   **Municipal/Urban Engineering:** Coordinates municipal infrastructure networks (streets, sidewalks, water supply, sewers, lighting, waste management, parks) often built simultaneously under one authority.
*   **Forensic Engineering:** Investigates failures of materials, structures, or components causing injury/property damage to determine cause (product liability, civil/criminal cases) or improve component life.
*   **Civil Engineering Systems:** Applies systems thinking to manage complexity across the project life cycle (conception to decommissioning), integrating technical detail with holistic factor relationships.

## Terms
- ****Civil Engineering**** — Professional discipline designing, constructing, and maintaining the physical/natural built environment (public works), distinct from military engineering.
- ****Geotechnical Engineering**** — Sub-discipline applying soil/rock mechanics to design foundations, retaining walls, and earthworks; deals with nonlinear, variable material behavior.
- ****Hydraulic Engineering**** — Sub-discipline concerned with flow/conveyance of fluids (principally water) for pipelines, dams, drainage, and canals using fluid statics/dynamics.
- ****Professional Engineer (PE)**** — Licensure title (US, Canada, South Africa) permitting signing/sealing of engineering plans for public authority approval; requires accredited degree, experience, exams.
- ****Chartered Engineer (CEng)**** — Professional certification title in most Commonwealth countries, equivalent to PE, governed by institutions like ICE.
- ****Cadastral Surveying**** — Legally recognized land surveying to establish parcel boundaries (boundary surveys) or create new parcels (subdivision plans); performed by licensed land surveyors.
- ****Intelligent Transportation System (ITS)**** — Advanced applications in transportation engineering aiming to provide innovative services for traffic management and user information.
- ****Dilatancy**** — Volume change in soil associated with the application of shear stress; a nonlinear property complicating geotechnical prediction.
- ****Forensic Engineering**** — Investigation of structural/material/component failures to determine cause for liability, safety improvement, or legal proceedings.
- ****Site Engineering**** — Branch of general civil engineering focused on converting a tract of land from one usage to another (grading, drainage, utilities, pavement).

## Debates and open questions
*   **Terminology in Coastal Engineering:** The field debates terminology: "coastal defense" (traditional) vs. "coastal management" (modern), and whether "sea defense" (flooding) and "coastal protection" (erosion) should be strictly distinguished.
*   **Licensure Legislation Variance:** Significant international divergence exists on legal requirements for practice. The US/Canada enforce strict "sealing" laws; the UK has no such legislation; Australia limits state licensing to Queensland. The impact of this variance on public safety and professional mobility remains a point of discussion.
*   **Environmental vs. Sanitary Engineering Scope:** The transition from "sanitary engineering" to "environmental engineering" reflects an expanded scope (hazardous waste, remediation, industrial ecology), but the boundaries and overlap with public health engineering remain fluid.
*   **Surveying Professional Boundaries:** In the US/UK/Commonwealth, land surveying is a legally distinct profession from engineering (separate licensing, associations), yet civil engineers are trained in surveying basics. The delineation of responsibility for construction surveying vs. cadastral surveying is a persistent jurisdictional issue.
*   **Systems Thinking Integration:** Civil Engineering Systems advocates for holistic, life-cycle management, but practical integration of this approach across fragmented sub-disciplines (structural, geotechnical, transportation, etc.) remains a challenge.
