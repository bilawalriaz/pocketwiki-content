# Rashi

## Overview
Shlomo Yitzchaki (c. 22 February 1040 – 13 July 1105), known by the acronym **Rashi** (Rabbi Shlomo Yitzhaki), was a French rabbi born in Troyes, Champagne. He authored the first comprehensive, phrase-by-phrase commentaries on the Babylonian Talmud and the Tanakh (Hebrew Bible), which became the definitive lenses through which these texts are studied. By synthesizing Midrashic tradition, Hebrew grammar, and Old French glosses, he rendered the "simple meaning" (*peshat*) accessible to beginners while providing a foundation for advanced legal analysis. His Talmud commentary democratized scholarship by allowing study outside academies, and his Bible commentary became the first dated Hebrew printed book (1475). Over 300 "supercommentaries" analyze his work, and his responsa illuminate Jewish life during the First Crusade.

## Timeline
- **c. 22 Feb 1040** — Born in Troyes, Champagne (northern France).
- **c. 1055 (age 17)** — Married; began yeshiva studies in Worms under Yaakov ben Yakar.
- **1064** — Yaakov ben Yakar died; Rashi studied under Isaac ben Eliezer Halevi in Worms, then Isaac ben Judah in Mainz.
- **c. 1065 (age 25)** — Returned to Troyes; joined the *beth din* (rabbinical court).
- **c. 1070** — Founded a yeshiva in Troyes; assumed leadership of *beth din* after Zerach ben Abraham's death.
- **1096** — People's Crusade murdered Jews in Lorraine, including his teacher Isaac ben Eliezer Halevi's three sons; Rashi composed *Selichot* (penitential poems) mourning the destruction.
- **13 Jul 1105** — Died in Troyes at age 65; buried there.
- **18 Feb 1475** — First dated Hebrew printed book: Rashi's commentary on the Chumash (Reggio di Calabria).
- **1520s** — Daniel Bomberg printed the first complete Talmud with Rashi's commentary included; standard in every edition since.

## Body

### Name and Identity
Rashi is an acronym for **Rabbi Shlomo Yitzhaki** ("Shlomo son of Yitzhak"). Medieval Christian scholars erroneously expanded it to *Jarchi* or *Yarhi*, linking him to Lunel (Provence) via the Occitan *luna* (moon/Hebrew *yareach*). Leopold Zunz (1839) proved this was a Christian error propagated into Hebrew texts; the acronym solely denotes his patronymic. He is cited in sources as "Shlomo son of Rabbi Yitzhak" or "Shlomo Yitzhaki."

### Education and Intellectual Formation
An only child, Rashi lost his father young. At 17, he studied in Worms under **Yaakov ben Yakar** and **Isaac ben Eliezer Halevi**, then in Mainz under **Isaac ben Judah**. All were disciples of **Gershom ben Judah** ("Light of the Exile"), the foundational Ashkenazi authority. Rashi absorbed the oral Talmudic tradition, its logic, and argument forms, taking copious notes later incorporated into his commentaries. He was also influenced by the exegetical principles of **Menahem Kara**, a pioneer of *peshat* (plain meaning) interpretation.

### Career and Communal Leadership
Returning to Troyes at 25, Rashi joined the *beth din*, eventually heading it. He founded a yeshiva c. 1070 attracting many disciples. Jewish tradition calls him a vintner; while Troyes soil was poor for grapes, he shows deep knowledge of winemaking, and a responsum mentions a barrel sealed with his import mark. He likely never traveled beyond the Seine–Rhine corridor. The 1096 People's Crusade devastated Lorraine yeshivas and killed his teacher's sons; Rashi wrote seven surviving *Selichot* (e.g., *Adonai Elohei Hatz'vaot* for Rosh Hashanah eve, *Az Terem Nimtehu* for Fast of Gedalia) mourning the slaughter.

### Commentary on the Tanakh (Hebrew Bible)
Rashi's commentary on the **Chumash** (Five Books of Moses) is the essential companion for Orthodox Bible study. He clarifies the *peshat* so "a bright child of five could understand it," while embedding foundations for profound legal and mystical discourse. He draws on Midrash, Talmud, Aggadah, Hebrew grammar, and *halakhah* (Jewish law), often distinguishing *peshat* from *derash* (homiletical interpretation): "I am only concerned with the plain sense of Scripture... and such Agadoth that explain the words... in a manner that fits." He wrote commentaries on all Tanakh books except Chronicles I & II and Ezra–Nehemiah; Job is incomplete (ends at 40:25). The commentary evolved from his yeshiva lectures and was completed late in life. It was immediately accepted by Ashkenazi and Sephardi communities alike. The first dated Hebrew printed book (1475) was this commentary.

### Commentary on the Talmud
Rashi produced the first comprehensive commentary on the Babylonian Talmud, covering 30 of 39 tractates (his death halted completion). Unlike predecessors, he **elucidates phrase-by-phrase** without paraphrasing, supplying punctuation to the unpunctuated text (e.g., "This is a question," "He says this in surprise"). He uses analogies from contemporary crafts and professions and translates difficult Hebrew/Aramaic terms into **Old French**, preserving invaluable linguistic data. The commentary spread rapidly (reaching Yemen by mid-12th century) and has been printed in every Talmud edition since Bomberg (1520s), always placed on the inner margin facing the binding. Some tractate endings (Makkot, Bava Batra) were finished by his son-in-law Judah ben Nathan and grandson Rashbam; attributions for Nedarim, Nazir, Taanit, and Horayot are debated.

### Responsa and Halakhic Decisions
~300 responsa survive, copied by students and descendants (e.g., *Siddur Rashi*, *Mahzor Vitry*, *Sefer Hapardes*, *Sefer Haorah*). They address: linguistic analysis, prayer, food, Sabbath, non-Jewish wine, oaths, sales, loans/interest, communal affairs, and civil law. Post-1096 responsa guide conduct toward martyrs, forced converts, and widows' rights. They reveal a "kind, gentle, humble, and liberal" personality and meticulous textual focus.

### Poetry
Rashi composed *Selichot* (penitential prayers) still recited in Ashkenazi liturgy: *Nora va'Elyonim* (Rosh Hashanah eve), *Az Terem Nimtechu* (Fast of Gedalia), *Ophan Echad ba'Aretz*, *Akh Leilohim Nafshi Domi*, *Apekha Hashev*, *Torah haTemimah* (on Rhineland massacres), and *Tefillah l'Kademkha* (in 1613 Prague prayerbook). Other poems attributed to him lack evidence.

### Family and Descendants
Rashi had three daughters, no sons. He educated them in Torah and Talmud (unusual for the era); they assisted him when infirm. **Yocheved** married Meir ben Samuel; their sons—**Rashbam** (Shmuel, b. 1080), **Rivam** (Yitzchak, b. 1090), **Rabbeinu Tam** (Jacob, b. 1100), and Shlomo the Grammarian—became leading **Tosafists** (authors of *Tosafot*, critical glosses on Rashi/Talmud). **Miriam** married Judah ben Nathan (completed Makkot); their son Yom Tov headed a Paris yeshiva. **Rachel** married (and divorced) Eliezer ben Shemiah. Most Ashkenazi rabbinical dynasties trace lineage to Miriam or Yocheved. A late-20th-century claim that his daughters wore *tefillin* (phylacteries) lacks evidence. Moses Isserles (1530–1572) is reputedly a descendant.

### Legacy and Influence
**In Jewish circles:** Rashi democratized Talmud study—previously requiring academy attendance—enabling "anyone, regardless of means" to master tractates. His work solved the problem of rendering the Talmud's gnomic text coherent; subsequent scholars (Tosafists) emended and expanded him. In standard Talmud prints, **Tosafot** sits opposite Rashi. He established authoritative Talmudic texts by comparing manuscripts at Troyes fairs. Over 300 supercommentaries exist (e.g., *Gur Aryeh* by Maharal, *Sefer ha-Mizrachi* by Re'em, *Yeri'ot Shlomo* by Maharshal). *Chumash with Rashi* is standard in Orthodox editions; one may fulfill the *Shnayim mikra* obligation (reviewing the weekly portion) with Rashi instead of Targum Onkelos.

**In non-Jewish circles:** Rashi wrote in Old French (rare for the era), providing cultural/linguistic data. **Nicholas de Lyra** (14th c.), called the "ape of Rashi," relied heavily on him for *Postillae Perpetuate*, a source for **Luther's Bible translation**. Christian Hebraists viewed Rashi as the "authorized" Synagogue interpretation. Islamic author Harris Zafar cites Rashi's view that Song of Songs is God's allegory for Israel, not erotic poetry—relevant to Muslim claims that 5:16 mentions Muhammad.

### "Rashi Script"
The semi-cursive typeface used for Rashi's commentaries in printed Talmud and Tanakh is called **"Rashi script."** Rashi never used it; it derives from a 15th-century Sephardic hand. Early printers (Soncino, Bomberg) adopted it to distinguish rabbinic commentary (Rashi script) from the primary biblical/Talmudic text (square script).

## Terms
- ****Rashi**** — Acronym for *Rabbi Shlomo Yitzhaki*; the standard designation for Shlomo Yitzchaki (1040–1105).
- ****Peshat**** — The plain, literal, or contextual meaning of a biblical text; Rashi's primary exegetical goal.
- ****Derash / Aggadah**** — Homiletical, non-literal rabbinic interpretation (midrash); Rashi cites it only when it "fits" the words of Scripture.
- ****Talmud**** — The central rabbinic text comprising the Mishnah (c. 200 CE) and Gemara (c. 500 CE); Rashi commented on the Babylonian version.
- ****Tosafot**** — "Additions"; critical glosses on the Talmud (and Rashi) by 12th–13th-century scholars, many of them Rashi's descendants; printed opposite Rashi in standard editions.
- ****Beth din**** — Rabbinical court; Rashi joined and later headed the Troyes court.
- ****Responsa**** — Written answers to halakhic (legal) questions; ~300 of Rashi's survive.
- ****Selichot**** — Penitential poems/prayers; Rashi wrote several, some still in liturgy, mourning the 1096 Crusade massacres.
- ****Old French glosses**** — Rashi's translations of difficult Hebrew/Aramaic terms into his vernacular, preserving 11th-century French vocabulary/pronunciation.
- ****Rashi script**** — A semi-cursive Hebrew typeface (15th-century Sephardic origin) used in print to set off Rashi's commentary from the main text.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authorship of specific Talmud tractates:** Scholars (Akiva Eger, Zvi Hirsch Chajes) argue Rashi did not write the commentaries on **Nazir**, **Taanit**, **Nedarim**, or **Horayot**; some were completed by students (Judah ben Nathan, Rashbam) or derive from Gershom ben Judah's school.
- **Vintner tradition:** No direct evidence confirms Rashi was a winemaker, though his texts show technical knowledge and a responsum mentions his import seal; Troyes soil was largely unsuitable for viticulture.
- **Daughters wearing tefillin:** A modern legend claims Rashi's daughters wore phylacteries; while some medieval Ashkenazi women did, no contemporary source supports this for his daughters.
- **Ancestry claims:** Later sources claim 33rd-generation descent from Johanan HaSandlar/Gamaliel/Davidic line; Rashi himself never claimed this, and the early responsum of Solomon Luria omits it.
- **Jarchi/Yarhi error:** The evolution of this misnomer (Christian origin vs. Hebrew usage) is traced but illustrates medieval scholarly transmission errors.
- **Supercommentary proliferation:** Why Rashi's commentary—among many—became the singular "canonized" base for 300+ supercommentaries remains a subject of scholarly analysis (e.g., Eran Viezel).
