# Georgy Zhukov

## Overview
Georgy Zhukov was a Soviet military commander and one of the most celebrated figures of World War II. As a top general under Joseph Stalin, he played a decisive role in major Eastern Front victories, including the Battles of Moscow, Stalingrad, and Berlin. His post-war career was marked by political turbulence, reflecting the volatile relationship between military prestige and Soviet political power.

## Timeline
- **1 Dec 1896** — Born in Strelkovka, Russian Empire.

- **1939** — Commands Soviet forces to victory at Battle of Khalkhin Gol against Japan.
- **Feb 1941** — Appointed Chief of the General Staff of the Red Army.
- **22 Jun 1941** — Germany invades the USSR; Zhukov signs initial counter-offensive directive.

- **Aug 1942** — Becomes deputy commander-in-chief of the armed forces.
- **Jan 1943** — Promoted to Marshal of the Soviet Union.
- **Jun 1945** — First military governor of Soviet occupation zone in Germany.
- **1955** — Appointed Minister of Defence.
- **Oct 1957** — Forced into retirement after falling out of favor with Khrushchev.
- **18 Jun 1974** — Dies in Moscow.

## Body

### Early Life and Pre-War Career
Born to a poor peasant family, Zhukov was conscripted into the Imperial Army in 1915 and later joined the Red Army during the Russian Civil War. He rose through cavalry command positions in the 1920s and 1930s. His first major success came in 1939 at the Battle of Khalkhin Gol, a decisive victory over Japanese forces on the Mongolian border, earning him the Hero of the Soviet Union. In February 1941, Stalin appointed him Chief of the General Staff.

### World War II: The Eastern Front
Following the German invasion in June 1941, Zhukov's initial counter-offensive directive failed. He was removed as Chief of Staff in July after advocating for abandoning Kiev to avoid encirclement, a move Stalin rejected with catastrophic results. Zhukov was reassigned to command the Reserve Front, delivering the Red Army's first victory at Yelnya. He subsequently commanded the defense of Leningrad and played a key role in the Battle of Moscow.

From August 1942, as deputy commander-in-chief, Zhukov coordinated major operations, including the defense of Stalingrad and planning the subsequent counter-offensive. He was a key architect of the Soviet victory at the Battle of Kursk in 1943, though the extent of his role is debated by contemporaries like Marshal Rokossovsky. In 1944, he coordinated the fronts for Operation Bagration. In 1945, he commanded the 1st Belorussian Front in the Vistula–Oder Offensive and oversaw the final Battle of Berlin. He accepted the German surrender and inspected the Moscow Victory Parade.

### Post-War Political Career and Conflicts with Stalin
After the war, Zhukov's immense popularity made Stalin view him as a threat. He was stripped of key positions and relegated to minor commands in the Odessa and Urals districts, accused of looting and "Bonapartism." After Stalin's death in 1953, Zhukov supported Nikita Khrushchev's rise to power, playing a crucial role in the arrest of Lavrentiy Beria. He was appointed Defence Minister in 1955 and became a member of the Presidium.

Zhukov's political influence peaked when he defended Khrushchev against an "Anti-Party Group" in 1957, declaring the army would not move without his order. However, his demands for greater military autonomy and personal prestige soon threatened Khrushchev. In October 1957, Zhukov was abruptly removed from the Presidium and forced into retirement, accused of "non-party behaviour" and fostering a personality cult.

### Later Life and Legacy
Zhukov spent his retirement writing controversial memoirs, which faced heavy censorship. He was partially rehabilitated under Leonid Brezhnev, who invited him to review the 1965 Victory Parade. He suffered a debilitating stroke in 1967 and died in 1974. Zhukov is remembered as one of the greatest Russian and Soviet military commanders, alongside historical figures like Alexander Suvorov. His legacy is complex, celebrated for military genius but also scrutinized for political failures and the brutal methods of the Soviet advance.

## Terms
- **Marshal of the Soviet Union**: The highest military rank in the Soviet Union, awarded for exceptional service.
- **Stavka**: The supreme high command of the Soviet armed forces during World War II.
- **Hero of the Soviet Union**: The highest distinction in the USSR, awarded for heroic feats in service to the state.
- **Bonapartism**: A political accusation implying a military leader is using their prestige to seize state power, undermining the party.
- **Presidium of the Central Committee**: The top policy-making body of the Communist Party, later renamed the Politburo.
- **Operation Bagration**: A massive 1944 Soviet offensive that destroyed the German Army Group Centre.
- **Vistula–Oder Offensive**: A 1945 Red Army operation that advanced from the Vistula River in Poland to the Oder River in Germany.
- **Soviet occupation zone in Germany**: The area of Germany administered by the Soviet Union after World War II, which became East Germany.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Pre-war offensive plans**: Historians debate whether the Soviet Union had drawn up plans for a preemptive attack on Germany in 1941. Some researchers, like Victor Suvorov, argue Zhukov and Timoshenko presented such a plan to Stalin, but no approved plan has been found.
- **Role at Kursk**: While Zhukov claimed a central role in planning the Battle of Kursk, Marshal Rokossovsky, who commanded the Central Front, stated that planning was done without Zhukov and that he exaggerated his involvement.
- **Extent of Stalin's influence**: Zhukov's memoirs and later censored chapters portray Stalin as having little direct role in military strategy, a view contested by other sources that emphasize Stalin's hands-on command style.
- **Political acumen**: Zhukov's career highlights the tension between military heroism and political survival in the Soviet system. His repeated falls from power suggest a lack of political skill despite his battlefield prowess.
