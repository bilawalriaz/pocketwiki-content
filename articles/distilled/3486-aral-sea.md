# Aral Sea

## Overview
The Aral Sea, once the world's third-largest lake, was an endorheic (closed-basin) salt lake between Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan. It began shrinking catastrophically in the 1960s after Soviet irrigation projects diverted its feeder rivers, the Amu Darya and Syr Darya. This ecological disaster devastated local ecosystems, economies, and public health, though recent restoration efforts have partially revived its northern section.

## Timeline
- **1960s** — Soviet irrigation projects divert the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers, causing the Aral Sea to begin shrinking.
- **1987** — The lake splits into two separate bodies: the North Aral Sea and the South Aral Sea.
- **2003** — The South Aral Sea further divides into eastern and western basins.
- **2005** — The Dike Kokaral dam is completed to restore the North Aral Sea.
- **2007** — The Aral Sea has declined to 10% of its original size.
- **2009** — The southeastern basin of the South Aral Sea disappears.
- **2014** — NASA satellite images show the eastern basin of the Aral Sea has completely dried up, becoming the Aralkum Desert.

## Body

### Formation and History
The Aral Sea was fed by the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers. Historically, climate shifts and human diversions caused its water level to fluctuate. The modern crisis began in the 1960s when Soviet planners diverted the rivers to irrigate desert for cotton ("white gold") production. By 1960, 20 to 60 km³ of water annually went to farmland instead of the sea. The disappearance was anticipated by Soviet officials; as early as 1964, engineer Aleksandr Asarin noted the lake was doomed as part of state plans. The sea's surface area shrank by 60% and its volume by 80% between 1960 and 1998. Salinity increased from 10 g/L to approximately 30 g/L by 1990.

### Ecology
The Aral Sea had low indigenous biodiversity but hosted many endemic fish subspecies. The dramatic increase in salinity and loss of habitat led to the local extinction of most native fish, including all sturgeon species and the Aral trout. Introduced salt-tolerant species like herring and gobies were added in the 1960s, but their populations later collapsed. The North Aral Sea's recovery since the 1990s has allowed many native and introduced fish species to return. In contrast, the South Aral Sea remains too saline for most life, supporting only halotolerant organisms like brine shrimp. The sea's invertebrate community, once dominated by freshwater species, was also decimated, though some species are returning to the fresher North Aral.

### Environmental and Human Impact
The shrinking sea is considered a textbook ecosystem collapse. The receding shoreline left vast plains covered in salt and toxic chemicals from agricultural runoff and industrial pollution. Wind-borne toxic dust has caused severe public health problems, including high rates of cancer, respiratory illnesses, and child mortality. The once-thriving fishing industry, which employed 40,000 people, was destroyed; former fishing ports like Moynaq are now ghost towns far from the water. The regional climate has been altered, with a broader annual temperature range and more dust storms.

### Restoration Efforts
International and national efforts have focused on partial restoration. The International Fund for Saving the Aral Sea (IFAS) coordinates regional programs (ASBP-1, -2, -3) aimed at water management, environmental protection, and socio-economic development. The most successful project is the North Aral Sea restoration in Kazakhstan. The Dike Kokaral dam, completed in 2005, separated the North Aral from the South. This allowed the North Aral's water level to rise 12 m by 2008, salinity to drop, and fish populations to recover, reviving a small fishing industry. Plans exist to build a second dam to bring water closer to the port of Aralsk. The South Aral Sea, largely in Uzbekistan, has been abandoned to desiccation, with efforts focused on planting vegetation on the dried seabed to combat dust storms.

## Terms
- **Endorheic**: A closed drainage basin that retains water and allows no outflow to other external bodies of water.
- **Salinity**: The concentration of dissolved salts in water, measured in grams per liter (g/L).
- **Anadromous**: Fish that migrate from saltwater to freshwater to spawn (e.g., salmon).
- **Halotolerant**: Organisms that can tolerate high salinity levels.
- **Endemic**: Species or subspecies native and restricted to a certain place.
- **Irrigation canal**: Artificial waterways constructed to carry water from rivers or reservoirs to farmland.
- **Dike Kokaral**: A 13-kilometer-long dam built in 2005 to separate and restore the North Aral Sea.
- **Aral Sea Basin Programme (ASBP)**: A series of international programs aimed at addressing the environmental crisis in the Aral Sea region.

## Debates and Open questions
- The feasibility and cost of large-scale solutions, such as redirecting water from Siberian rivers (Ob, Irtysh) to refill the sea, remain highly debated.
- The future of the South Aral Sea is uncertain; political will for its restoration is lacking, and Uzbekistan is pursuing oil exploration on its dried bed.
- The long-term effectiveness of current restoration programs (ASBP-3) and the balance between upstream water use for agriculture versus downstream ecological needs are ongoing challenges.
