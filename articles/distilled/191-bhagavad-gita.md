# Bhagavad Gita

## Overview
The *Bhagavad Gita* ("God's Song") is a 700-verse Hindu scripture, likely composed between the second and first century BCE, embedded within the epic *Mahabharata*. It synthesizes Vedic *dharma* (duty), Sankhya *jnana* (knowledge), and *bhakti* (devotion), reconciling the tension between worldly action and spiritual liberation (*moksha*). Set as a dialogue between the warrior Arjuna and his charioteer Krishna (an avatar of Vishnu) on the eve of the Kurukshetra War, the text addresses Arjuna’s refusal to fight kin by teaching *nishkama karma*—selfless action performed without attachment to results. It holds unique pan-Hindu authority as a foundational text of Vedanta and Vaishnavism. The *Gita* is part of the *Prasthanatrayi*, the three foundational texts of Vedanta, alongside the Upanishads and the Brahma Sutras. The text identifies itself as an Upanishad: each chapter ends with the phrase *Gītāsu Upaniṣatsu*, and the feminine gender of *Gītā* reflects this classification.

## Timeline
- **c. 5th–3rd century BCE** — Historical context: Rise of Buddhism/Jainism; questioning of war ethics and rise of renunciation (per Basham).
- **c. 2nd–1st century BCE** — Likely composition of the *Bhagavad Gita* as part of the *Mahabharata* compilation.
- **c. 100 CE** — *Terminus ante quem*: Buddhist poet Asvaghosa quotes the *Mahabharata*, implying the Gita was established.
- **c. 800 CE** — Adi Shankara writes the oldest surviving commentary (*Bhashya*), establishing the Gita as part of the *Prasthanatrayi*.
- **c. 1000 CE** — Abhinavagupta writes *Gitartha-Samgraha* (Kashmir Shaivism).
- **c. 1100 CE** — Ramanuja writes *Gita Bhashya* (Vishishtadvaita/Qualified Non-dualism).
- **c. 1250 CE** — Madhvacharya writes *Gita Bhashya* (Dvaita/Dualism).
- **1479 CE** — Vallabhacharya writes *Tattva Dipika* (Shuddhadvaita/Pure Non-dualism).
- **1785** — Charles Wilkins publishes the first English translation.
- **1910–1911** — Bal Gangadhar Tilak writes *Gita Rahasya* in prison, emphasizing Karma Yoga for nationalism.
- **1929** — Mahatma Gandhi writes Gujarati commentary in Yeravada jail; calls Gita "spiritual dictionary."
- **1968** — A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada publishes *Bhagavad-Gita As It Is* (Gaudiya Vaishnavism); becomes most widely distributed English edition.

## Body

### Dating, Authorship, and Textual History
The *Gita* is generally dated to the second or first century BCE, though estimates range from 500 BCE to 100 CE. Its dating is entangled with the *Mahabharata*, a text compiled over centuries (c. 400 BCE–400 CE). Linguistically, it is classified as Epic-Puranic Sanskrit (post-Pāṇini, pre-classical compounds). Tradition attributes authorship to the sage Vyasa, but scholars regard Vyasa as a mythical compiler; the text shows evidence of multiple authors (three or more per Basham), indicated by discontinuous shifts between philosophical and theistic verses. Indologist van Buitenen argues the *Gita* was conceived by *Mahabharata* authors to resolve the epic’s central *dharmic* dilemma. The *Gita* manuscripts (found in *Bhishma-parvan* chapters 23–40) show minor variations compared to the rest of the epic. Adi Shankara, in his 8th-century commentary, explicitly states that the Gita has 700 verses, a declaration that established the standard benchmark for the critical edition. The text contains 700 verses in 18 chapters, structured primarily in *anushthubh* meter (8 syllables per quarter-verse), shifting to *tristubh* (11 syllables) at dramatic moments.

### Narrative Framework
On the Kurukshetra battlefield, Arjuna (Pandava prince) despairs upon seeing kin and teachers in the opposing army. He drops his bow, questioning the morality of war (*ahimsa* vs. *svadharma*). Krishna, as charioteer and guru, responds through 18 chapters of discourse. The dialogue moves from Arjuna’s dejection (Ch. 1) through Sankhya metaphysics (Ch. 2), Karma Yoga (Ch. 3), Jnana/Karma synthesis (Ch. 4–5), meditation (Ch. 6), and the revelation of Krishna’s divine nature (Ch. 7–11, including the *Vishvarupa* cosmic vision), culminating in Bhakti Yoga (Ch. 12), the Field/Knower distinction (Ch. 13), the three *gunas* (Ch. 14), the Supreme Person (Ch. 15), divine/demonic natures (Ch. 16), faith (Ch. 17), and a final summary on liberation through renunciation of fruits (Ch. 18).

### Philosophical Synthesis: Dharma, Yoga, and Moksha
The *Gita* synthesizes three paths to liberation (*moksha*), rejecting the *shramanic* ideal of non-action and extreme asceticism (*karsayanta*). It argues the body houses Krishna; torturing it tortures God.
1.  **Karma Yoga (Selfless Action):** One cannot avoid action; even inaction is action. One must perform *svadharma* (duty born of one's nature/station) without craving fruits (*nishkama karma* / *karma-phala-tyaga*). Right action, determined by knowledge, becomes worship.
2.  **Jnana Yoga (Knowledge):** Discernment of the eternal *Atman* (Self) from transient *Prakriti* (matter). The *Atman* is unborn, eternal, and indestructible (Ch. 2); the body is like worn-out garments. Knowledge burns away karma.
3.  **Bhakti Yoga (Devotion):** Loving surrender to a personal God (Krishna). Developed fully in Ch. 12, it is presented as the easiest and highest path, accessible to all regardless of caste or gender. It does not renounce action but dedicates it to Krishna.

The text reconciles the Brahmanical householder’s duty with the ascetic’s goal via "inner renunciation." It integrates Samkhya metaphysics (*Purusha/Prakriti*, three *gunas*: *sattva*, *rajas*, *tamas*) with Vedanta (*Brahman*, *Maya*). Krishna identifies as both the impersonal *Nirguna Brahman* (higher, unmanifest) and *Saguna Brahman* (personal Lord). The *Gita* blurs the distinction: the devotee realizes Krishna is the essential element in all beings, arriving at monism through theism.

### The *Svadharma* and Caste Debate
Krishna urges Arjuna to follow *svadharma*—the duty of his *varna* (Kshatriya/warrior).
*   **Traditional/Orthodox:** Supports *varna-dharma* based on birth; the Gita defends the caste hierarchy (Krishna: "The four-caste division has been created by Me").
*   **Neo-Hindu/Universalist (Vivekananda, Aurobindo, Gandhi, Dnyaneshwar):** Reinterpret *svadharma* as duty arising from one's innate nature (*svabhava*), profession, or life situation, rejecting birth-based caste. Vivekananda cites Ch. 13.27–28 (seeing the Lord equally in all beings) as egalitarian. Gandhi equates *svadharma* with *svadeshi* (service to immediate community).
*   **Critique (Ambedkar, Kosambi, Narla):** Ambedkar calls the Gita a "tool of Brahmanical Hinduism" defending Kshatriya violence and birth-based *varna*. Kosambi argues it supports upper-caste superiority while paying lip service to *ahimsa*.

### War, Violence, and Allegory
The battlefield setting generates divergent readings:
*   **Allegorical:** The war is internal. Arjuna = *Atman*/individual; Krishna = *Brahman*/Divine; chariot = body; enemies = vices (lust, anger, greed). Held by Vivekananda, Gandhi, Nikhilananda, Aurobindo (though Aurobindo insists it also governs outward action).
*   **Just War/Duty:** A religious defense of the Kshatriya duty to wage righteous war. Used by Tilak and Lajpat Rai to justify armed rebellion against colonial rule.
*   **Pacifist/Ethical Critique:** Gandhi derives *ahimsa* from the Gita (fighting injustice without hatred). Isherwood argues Arjuna *must* fight only because it is his specific *svadharma* in a righteous war; non-attachment sanctifies the act. Ambedkar condemns the "eternal soul" argument (Ch. 2:19–21) as a "defence of murder." Lal calls Krishna’s revelation a "confidence trick" bypassing Arjuna’s honest moral reasoning.

### Classical Commentaries (*Bhashyas*)
Many classical and modern Hindu intellectuals have written commentaries; some 227 commentaries have survived in the Sanskrit language alone. Key schools:
*   **Adi Shankara (c. 800, Advaita/Non-dualism):** Oldest extant. *Atman = Nirguna Brahman*. Krishna’s lordship is conventional reality (*vyavaharika*); distinction dissolves in absolute truth (*paramarthika*). *Bhakti* purifies mind (*citta-shuddhi*) for knowledge.
*   **Ramanuja (c. 1100, Vishishtadvaita/Qualified Non-dualism):** Brahman (Narayana/Vishnu) is a unity containing real souls and matter. Krishna’s body is spiritual (*aprakrita*), not illusory. *Bhakti* is the primary means to liberation.
*   **Madhvacharya (c. 1250, Dvaita/Dualism):** Five-fold difference: God (Vishnu), souls, and matter are eternally distinct. Hierarchy (*taratamya*) with Vishnu supreme. Liberation requires knowledge *plus* grace (*prasada*).
*   **Abhinavagupta (c. 1000, Kashmir Shaivism):** Shaiva Advaita; Self and matter both real. Uses *Atman, Brahman, Shiva, Krishna* interchangeably.
*   **Vallabhacharya (1479, Shuddhadvaita):** Universe is real manifestation of Krishna (Sat-Chit-Ananda). Path is *Pushtimarga* (Grace/*Seva* to living *svarups*).
*   **Chaitanya Mahaprabhu (1486–1533, Gaudiya Vaishnavism/Achintya-Bhedabheda):** Krishna is *Svayam Bhagavan* (source of Vishnu). Goal is *prema* (selfless love), not *moksha*. Method: *sankirtana* (congregational chanting).

### Modern Prominence and Global Reception
The Gita’s "extraordinary prominence is a feature of modernity" (Robinson), rising in the 1880s–1920s. The Theosophical Society (Blavatsky, Besant) promoted it as a universal "path of true spirituality." Neo-Hindus (Tilak, Aurobindo, Radhakrishnan, Vivekananda) used it for nationalist activism and universalist theology. Vivekananda systematized the "Four Yogas" (adding Raja Yoga). Gandhi centered his life on it (*Anasakti Yoga*). In the West, Wilkins’ 1785 translation sparked scholarly interest; over 1,800 translations exist in 75 languages. Prabhupada’s ISKCON edition (1968) is the most distributed English version (est. 23 million copies). Modern commentaries range from academic (Fowler, Zaehner) to psychotherapeutic (Keltner/Haidt: "awe-inspired transformation") and interfaith (Cornille’s Christian commentaries).

## Terms
- ****Dharma**** — Duty, righteousness, law; contextually *svadharma* (one's specific duty based on *varna* or nature).
- ****Karma Yoga**** — Path of selfless action (*nishkama karma*); performing duty without attachment to fruits/results.
- ****Jnana Yoga**** — Path of knowledge/wisdom; discernment of the eternal *Atman* from transient *Prakriti*.
- ****Bhakti Yoga**** — Path of loving devotion/surrender to a personal deity (Krishna); characterized as easiest and highest.
- ****Atman**** — The eternal, unchanging Self/Soul; distinct from body/mind; identical in all beings.
- ****Brahman**** — Ultimate Reality; *Nirguna* (without qualities, impersonal) and *Saguna* (with qualities, personal God/Ishvara).
- ****Prakriti**** — Material nature; the field of action composed of three *gunas*; transient and process-driven.
- ****Gunas**** — Three qualities binding beings to *Prakriti*: *Sattva* (purity/knowledge), *Rajas* (passion/activity), *Tamas* (ignorance/inertia).
- ****Moksha**** — Liberation from the cycle of rebirth (*samsara*); the ultimate goal of the yogas.
- ****Avatar**** — "Descent"; a deity (Vishnu) incarnating in the world (e.g., Krishna) to restore *dharma*.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Date and Composition:** Unresolved whether the *Gita* was an early *Mahabharata* component or a later insertion (2nd c. BCE vs. 1st c. CE vs. earlier).
*   **Unity vs. Composite:** Whether the text is a coherent philosophical work (van Buitenen) or a layered composite with interpolations (Basham, Basham notes Ch. 5 contradictions).
*   **Metaphysics:** Whether the *Gita* teaches strict Non-dualism (Advaita), Qualified Non-dualism (Vishishtadvaita), Dualism (Dvaita), or a unique synthesis (Bhedabheda/Achintya-Bhedabheda).
*   **Svadharma and Caste:** Whether *svadharma* sanctions birth-based *varna* hierarchy or refers to universal duty based on innate nature (*svabhava*).
*   **War and Violence:** Whether the text is an allegory for inner struggle, a justification for just war/righteous violence, or a pacifist manual for detached action.
*   **Primacy of Paths:** Whether *Bhakti*, *Jnana*, or *Karma* Yoga is supreme, or if they are equal/convergent (medieval commentators prioritized differently; moderns often claim synthesis).
*   **Krishna’s Ontology:** Whether Krishna is a partial *avatara* (Shankara), the full *Svayam Bhagavan* (Gaudiya), or an *avatara* of Narayana/Vishnu (Ramanuja, Madhva).
