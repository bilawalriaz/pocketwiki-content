# Choreography

## Overview
Choreography is the art of designing sequences of movement for physical bodies or their depictions, most commonly in dance. It involves specifying movement in terms of space, shape, time, and energy, and is a fundamental creative process in the performing arts and beyond.

## Timeline
- **16th century** — French court dances developed in an artistic pattern.
- **17th-18th centuries** — Social dance separates from theatrical performance; "choreography" applied to written dance records.
- **Late 18th century** — Jean-Georges Noverre is a well-known ballet master.
- **19th century** — Ballet develops its own vocabulary; romantic ballet choreographers include Marius Petipa.
- **1936** — George Balanchine first credited as "choreographer" for *On Your Toes*.
- **1950s** — The word "choreography" first appears in an American English dictionary.
- **c. 1982** — The International Choreographic Competition Hannover begins.
- **1 January 1978** — U.S. Copyright Act begins protecting "choreographic works" created after this date.
- **2008** — Tanja Liedtke Foundation begins collaborating with the Hannover competition.
- **2020** — The Hannover competition moves online due to the COVID-19 pandemic.
- **2020** — The International Online Dance Competition (IODC) is introduced.
- **2022** — The Hannover competition returns to the stage.

## Body

### Definition and Process
Choreography is the art of designing sequences of movements where motion, form, or both are specified. A choreographer creates these designs, a process called choreographing. The art involves specifying human movement and form in terms of space, shape, time, and energy, often within an emotional or non-literal context. Movement language is drawn from various dance techniques, including ballet, contemporary, jazz, hip hop, and folk dance, or combinations thereof.

### Etymology and History
The term "choreography" means "dance-writing," from the Greek *choreia* (circular dance) and *graphé* (writing). The word first appeared in an American dictionary in the 1950s, though the credit "choreographer" was first used for George Balanchine in 1936. Historically, Renaissance Italian dance masters created movements for social dances. In 17th and 18th century France, the term was applied to the written record of dances, which later became dance notation. The role of the ballet master evolved into the "arranger of dance as a theatrical art." Modern dance introduced a more naturalistic style, with influential choreographers like Michel Fokine and Isadora Duncan. Later figures such as Merce Cunningham, George Balanchine, and Alvin Ailey explored styles ranging from abstraction to representation.

### Choreographic Techniques
Dances are designed using two fundamental methods: **improvisation**, where a choreographer provides dancers with a score or general directives for movement, allowing personal interpretation; and **planned choreography**, where motion and form are dictated in detail. Common techniques for multiple dancers include mirroring (facing each other and doing the same), retrograde (performing a sequence in reverse), canon (performing the same move sequentially), levels (varying height), shadowing (standing one behind the other), and unison (moving together).

### Modern Applications and Competitions
Today, choreography imposes order on performance within space, time, and human capability. It is used not only in dance but also in opera, cheerleading, theatre, marching band, synchronized swimming, and video game production. The International Choreographic Competition Hannover, started c. 1982, is the world's longest-running choreography competition. It moved online during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020-2021 before returning to the stage in 2022. Other major competitions include the Beijing International Ballet and Choreography Competition and the Copenhagen International Choreography Competition.

### Copyright Protection
Under Section 102(a)(4) of the U.S. Copyright Act, "choreographic works" created after 1 January 1978 and fixed in a tangible medium are protected. Choreography is defined as "the composition and arrangement of a related series of dance movements and patterns organized into a coherent whole." However, ordinary motor activities, social dances, commonplace gestures, or athletic movements may lack sufficient authorship for protection. A notable case involved choreographer Kyle Hanagami, who sued Epic Games over a dance in *Fortnite*. The court dismissed the claims, finding the two-second, four-beat sequence was not protectable.

## Terms
- **Choreography**: The art of designing sequences of movement for bodies or their depictions.
- **Choreographing**: The process of creating choreography.
- **Dance Notation**: A written record of dances, historically linked to the term choreography.
- **Improvisation**: A choreographic method where dancers follow a score or general directives, allowing personal interpretation.
- **Planned Choreography**: A method where the choreographer dictates motion and form in detail.
- **Canon**: A technique where dancers perform the same move one after the other.
- **Retrograde**: Performing a sequence of moves in reverse order.
- **Mirroring**: A technique where dancers face each other and perform the same movements.
