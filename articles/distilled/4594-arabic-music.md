# Arabic music

## Overview
Arabic music is the diverse musical tradition of the Arab world, encompassing a wide range of styles and genres across many countries and dialects. It is characterized by its foundational maqam system, emphasis on melody and rhythm over harmony, and a long history of interaction with other regional styles, significantly influencing European music from the medieval period onward.

## Timeline
- **5th-7th century AD** — Pre-Islamic Arabian music flourishes, with distinct forms and instruments like the drum, lute, and rebab.
- **9th-10th century** — Scholars al-Kindi and al-Farabi publish major treatises on music theory; al-Farabi develops the pure Arabian tone system.
- **1252** — Safi al-Din develops a unique geometric form of musical notation for rhythms.
- **11th century** — Islamic Iberia becomes a center for instrument manufacture, spreading instruments like the lute and rebec to Europe.
- **1920s** — Cairo emerges as a center for musical innovation; the recording and film industry develops.
- **1932** — The first Congress of Arab Music is held in Cairo, documenting and analyzing Arabic music.
- **1940s-1960s** — Egyptian artists like Umm Kulthum and Abdel Halim Hafez pioneer the use of Western instruments in Arabic music.
- **1970s** — A strand of Arabic pop is born, blending Western styles with Arabic instruments and lyrics.
- **1990s-present** — A new generation of pop artists (e.g., Amr Diab, Nancy Ajram) rises to fame.

## Body

### History and Development
Pre-Islamic Arabian music, centered in Yemen, featured simple compositions where singers performed in a single maqam. The early Islamic period saw the codification of music theory by scholars like al-Kindi and al-Farabi, who identified twelve tones and developed the maqam system. By the 11th century, Islamic Iberia was a hub for instrument making, exporting instruments like the oud (lute) and rebab (rebec) to Europe. In the 20th century, Cairo became the epicenter of innovation, with female singers like Umm Kulthum and Fairuz adopting a secular approach. From the 1940s onward, Western instruments were integrated, leading to the birth of Arabic pop in the 1970s.

### Influence on European Music
Arabic music profoundly influenced European medieval and classical music, primarily through the adoption of instruments. The European lute, rebec, guitar, and naker are derived from the Arabic oud, rebab, qitara, and naqareh. The Arabic rabāb is the ancestor of all European bowed string instruments, including the violin. Some scholars debate whether the troubadour tradition and Guido of Arezzo's Solfège notation system also have Arabic origins, though documentary evidence is lacking for the latter.

### The Maqam System and Characteristics
The foundation of Arabic music is the maqam (mode), which determines the tonic, dominant, and ending notes. A maqam is built from ajnas (scale segments like trichords or tetrachords), with at least eight major ajnas in common use. Unlike Western music, Arabic music uses microtones, or quarter tones, which lie between the notes of the Western chromatic scale. The 1932 Cairo Congress concluded that the exact intonation of these notes varies by region (Egypt, Turkey, Syria, Iraq) and should not be tempered to a Western scale.

### Genres and Regional Styles
Arabic music includes sacred music (Islamic, Christian, Jewish) and secular art music genres like maqam al-iraqi and muwashshah. Modern genres include Franco-Arabic, Arabic R&B/hip-hop, electronica, jazz, and rock, often fusing traditional instruments with Western styles. Regional styles are diverse, from Egyptian Al Jeel and Shaabi to Moroccan Gnawa and Algerian Raï. The takht is the prototypical ensemble in Egypt and Syria, while Iraq uses the chalghi ensemble.

### Improvisation
Improvisation is a key element, with varying regional attitudes—some view it as intuitive artistry, others as a sign of lack of training. Taqsim is a respected improvisational tradition using a maqam to create a cathartic experience. Other forms include melismatic chant and the recitative style, often performed by women.

## Terms
- **Maqam**: A system of melodic modes used in traditional Arabic music, determining the tonic, dominant, and ending notes of a piece.
- **Ajnas**: Plural of jins; scale segments (trichords, tetrachords, or pentachords) that form the building blocks of a maqam.
- **Microtones**: Notes that lie between the semitones of the Western chromatic scale, such as quarter tones, essential to Arabic music.
- **Takht**: A traditional Arabic music ensemble, typically including instruments like the oud, qānūn, ney, and violin.
- **Taqsim**: An improvisational musical form based on a maqam, designed to evoke strong emotional responses.
- **Oud**: A short-necked, pear-shaped stringed instrument, a precursor to the European lute and guitar.
- **Rebab**: A bowed string instrument, the ancestor of the European rebec and violin.
- **Mawwal**: A traditional vocal improvisation, often performed before a main song.
- **Gnawa**: A musical style from Morocco and Algeria, blending sub-Saharan, Berber, and Arabic influences.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the extent of Arabic influence on European traditions like the troubadours and Solfège notation. While some argue for direct origins, others contend the evidence is inconclusive or that the traditions developed independently. The precise number and classification of ajnas and maqamat remain unsettled due to continuous innovation and regional variation.
