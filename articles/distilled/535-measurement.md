# Measurement

## Overview
Measurement is the quantification of attributes to compare objects or events against a standard reference quantity. It is foundational to trade, science, technology, and quantitative research. Historically, diverse local systems prevailed until the 18th century, when efforts to unify standards produced the International System of Units (SI), which reduces all physical measurements to combinations of seven base units defined by invariant physical constants. Metrology is the scientific discipline dedicated to measurement.

## Timeline
- **18th century** — Developments progress toward unifying widely accepted measurement standards.
- **1875** — Metre Convention establishes the General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM).
- **1960** — International System of Units (SI) developed from the metre–kilogram–second (MKS) system; international yard defined as exactly 0.9144 metres.
- **1983** — CGPM redefines the metre in terms of the speed of light.
- **2019** — Kilogram redefined in terms of the Planck constant.

## Body

### Methodology
Measurement is categorised by four criteria: **type** (the methodological character of comparison, such as ratio or ordinal scales), **magnitude** (the numerical value obtained via an instrument), **unit** (a mathematical weighting factor derived from a ratio to a standard artefact or natural constant), and **uncertainty** (the random and systematic errors indicating confidence level). Errors are evaluated by repeating measurements and assessing the **accuracy** (closeness to true value) and **precision** (repeatability) of the instrument.

### Standardisation of Measurement Units
The SI framework defines seven fundamental units (kilogram, metre, candela, second, ampere, kelvin, mole) without reference to physical artefacts. These **artefact-free definitions** fix units to exact values of physical constants, preventing drift from deterioration. The first proposal to tie a unit to an experimental standard was by Charles Sanders Peirce, who suggested defining the metre via a spectral wavelength, influencing the Michelson–Morley experiment. Standards are overseen internationally by the CGPM and nationally by bodies like NIST (US), NPL (UK), and the National Measurement Institute (Australia). Laws regulating measurement originated to prevent commercial fraud.

### Units and Systems
A **unit** is a standard quantity used to express other quantities.

**Imperial and US Customary Systems:** Derived from British English units, these **foot-pound-second systems** remain in use in the US and a few Caribbean countries. The UK officially uses SI but retains Imperial units for road distances (miles/yards), speed limits (mph), draught beer (imperial pint), and common body measurements (feet/inches, stone/pounds). Many metricated Commonwealth countries still use acres for land and gallons for fuel in commercial contexts.

**Metric System and SI:** A decimal system based on the metre and kilogram. The SI (1960) is the internationally recognised revision, built on the MKS system rather than the CGS system. It comprises seven **base units** (time, length, mass, temperature, amount of substance, electric current, light intensity) and **derived units** constructed from them (e.g., watt = m²·kg·s⁻³). **Compound units** combine base units for properties like density (kg·m⁻³). SI prefixes allow easy conversion by powers of ten (e.g., 1 metre = 100 centimetres).

**Length, Time, and Mass:**
*   **Length** is measured with rulers, tape measures, or laser interferometers.
*   **Time** is an abstract measurement of elemental changes over a non-spatial continuum, denoted by numbers or named periods.
*   **Mass** is the intrinsic property resisting momentum change; **weight** is the downward force in a gravitational field. In free fall, objects lack weight but retain mass. Spring scales measure force; balances compare weight; load cells provide high accuracy but require gravity.

### Economics and Survey Research
Economics employs **physical measures**, **nominal price value measures** (current prices), and **real price measures** (adjusted for inflation), differing by included/excluded variables. In **survey research**, questionnaires measure attitudes and behaviors. All measurements face **measurement error** (departure from true value), which can bias conclusions; results must be corrected for such errors.

### Exactness Designation
**Significant figures** convey exactness: all non-zero digits and zeros between them are significant (e.g., 12000 has two). Trailing zeros after a decimal separator denote greater exactness (e.g., 1.0 implies limits 0.95–1.05 vs. 1 implying 0.5–1.5).

### Difficulties
All measurements are approximations. Measuring a 1-metre fall illustrates error sources: the gravitational constant (9.8 m/s²) is precise only to two significant digits; gravity varies by altitude; mathematical operations (square roots) require rounding; and experimental errors include carelessness, timing inaccuracies, height measurement errors, air resistance, and human posture. Scientific rigor requires minimizing error and keeping estimates realistic.

### Definitions and Theories
**Classical Definition:** Standard in physical sciences; measurement determines ratios of quantities. Quantitative attributes are those measurable in principle. Traced to John Wallis, Isaac Newton, and foreshadowed in Euclid's *Elements*.

**Representational Theory:** Measurement is "the correlation of numbers with entities that are not numbers." **Additive conjoint measurement** assigns numbers based on structural similarities between number systems and qualitative systems. Weaker forms (e.g., Stanley Smith Stevens) require only assignment by a rule. Assignment alone (e.g., accounting "book value") is not measurement unless structural correlations are empirically established. Three components: **empirical relation** (observation-based correlation), **rule of mapping** (choices in mapping real world to mathematical range), and **representation condition**.

**Theory (Uncertainty View):** All data are inexact and statistical. Measurement is "a set of observations that reduce uncertainty where the result is expressed as a quantity." One begins with an initial guess and reduces uncertainty, assigning a range of values rather than a single point. This blurs the distinction between estimation and measurement.

**Quantum Mechanics:** Measurement determines a property (position, momentum, energy) of a quantum system. It yields statistical samples from a probability distribution (often discrete), alters the quantum state (wavefunction "collapse"), yet repeated measurements are reproducible. The unambiguous meaning remains an unresolved fundamental problem.

**Biology:** No well-established general theory exists, but theoretical context (especially evolution) is emphasized. Developed subfields include measurement of genetic diversity and species diversity.

## Terms
- ****Metrology**** — The science of measurement.
- ****International System of Units (SI)**** — The modern metric system reducing all physical measurements to seven base units defined by physical constants.
- ****Base Unit**** — A fundamental SI unit for a base quantity (e.g., metre, kilogram, second).
- ****Derived Unit**** — An SI unit constructed mathematically from base units (e.g., watt = m²·kg·s⁻³).
- ****Artefact-free Definition**** — A unit definition tied to an invariant physical constant rather than a physical object.
- ****Uncertainty**** — The estimated random and systematic errors of a measurement procedure, indicating confidence level.
- ****Accuracy**** — Closeness of a measurement to the true value.
- ****Precision**** — Repeatability or consistency of repeated measurements.
- ****Additive Conjoint Measurement**** — The technically elaborated form of representational theory where numbers are assigned based on structural similarities between number systems and qualitative systems.
- ****Significant Figures**** — Digits (non-zero and zeros between them) that convey the exactness of a measurement.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Quantum Measurement Problem:** The unambiguous meaning of quantum measurement is unresolved; the dominant interpretation involves wavefunction collapse, but alternatives exist.
*   **Representational Theory Validity:** Debate persists between strong (additive conjoint) and weak (Stevensian) forms regarding whether measurement requires empirical structural correlations or merely rule-based assignment.
*   **Biology Theory Gap:** There is no well-established general theory of measurement in biology, though the theoretical context of evolution is argued to be fundamental.
*   **Estimation vs. Measurement:** The uncertainty view implies no neat distinction exists between estimation and measurement, challenging classical positivist definitions.
