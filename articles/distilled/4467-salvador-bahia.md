# Salvador, Bahia

## Overview
Salvador is the capital of the Brazilian state of Bahia and one of the oldest cities in the Americas, founded in 1549. As Brazil's first colonial capital, it was a major port for the slave trade and sugar industry, shaping its profound African cultural influence. Today, it is a major economic, cultural, and tourist center, renowned for its cuisine, music, Carnival, and UNESCO-listed colonial architecture.

- **2014** — Hosts matches for the FIFA World Cup.

## Body

### History and Foundation
Salvador was established in 1549 as the fortress of São Salvador da Bahia de Todos os Santos by Tomé de Sousa, Brazil's first governor-general. It served as the capital of Colonial Brazil for 250 years, becoming a major port for the Atlantic slave trade and sugarcane industry. The city's layout was defined by its steep escarpment, creating an Upper Town (administrative) and Lower Town (commercial). It was a strategic prize, captured by the Dutch in 1624 and recaptured in 1625. Salvador remained the political heart of the region even after the national capital moved to Rio de Janeiro in 1763, and its elites initially resisted Brazilian independence until 1823.

### Demographics and Ethnicity
Salvador's population is predominantly of mixed ancestry, reflecting 500 years of intermarriage between African, European, and Native American peoples. The 2022 census recorded 2,417,678 residents, with 49.1% identifying as Pardo (multiracial), 34.1% as Black, and 16.5% as White. Genetic studies estimate the ancestral heritage of the population to be approximately 50% African, 40% European, and 10% Native American. This demographic makeup is a direct result of the city's central role in the colonial slave economy.

### Culture and Religion
Salvador is a global center of Afro-Brazilian culture. Its cuisine, featuring dishes like acarajé and moqueca, relies heavily on African ingredients like dendê oil. The city is the birthplace of modern capoeira, a martial art disguised as dance. Its Carnival is the world's largest street party. Religiously, it is a site of profound syncretism, where the Yorùbá religion of enslaved Africans merged with Roman Catholicism to form Candomblé. This is visible in festivals like the Feast of Bonfim, which honors both Jesus Christ and the Candomblé deity Oxalá.

### Economy and Infrastructure
Salvador is the economic core of the Northeast region, with the highest metropolitan GDP in the area. Its economy is driven by tourism, port activities, and industry, hosting headquarters for major companies. The city's infrastructure includes the Deputado Luís Eduardo Magalhães International Airport, a major port, and a metro system expanded for the 2014 FIFA World Cup. It is connected to the national highway network via BR-101 and BR-116.

### Tourism and Geography
The city's tourism is anchored by its 80 km of coastline along the Bay of All Saints and the Atlantic Ocean, and its historic center. The Pelourinho district, with its 17th-18th century colonial architecture, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The city's tropical rainforest climate features consistent temperatures and distinct wet and dry seasons. Major attractions include the Elevador Lacerda, connecting the Upper and Lower Towns, and numerous museums and parks.

## Terms
*   **Pelourinho:** The historic center of Salvador, named for the colonial pillories (*pelourinhos*) that once stood there; a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
*   **Candomblé:** A syncretic Afro-Brazilian religion that blends Yorùbá beliefs with Roman Catholicism.
*   **Axe (Axé):** A popular music genre originating in Salvador, blending Caribbean, African, and Brazilian rhythms, central to Carnival.
*   **Capoeira:** An Afro-Brazilian martial art that combines elements of dance, acrobatics, and music.
*   **Malê Revolt:** An 1835 slave revolt in Salvador led by Muslim enslaved Africans, one of the largest in Brazil.
*   **Elevador Lacerda:** A historic public elevator inaugurated in 1873, connecting Salvador's Upper and Lower Towns.
*   **Recôncavo:** The fertile, agricultural maritime region surrounding the Bay of All Saints, historically the economic heartland of Bahia.
*   **Pardo:** A Brazilian census category for multiracial people, often of mixed African, European, and Native American ancestry.

## Debates and Open Questions
The restoration of the Pelourinho neighborhood in the 1990s is a major point of debate. While it created a tourist attraction, the process involved the forced removal of thousands of working-class, predominantly Afro-descendant residents to the city's periphery, separating them from work and amenities. This has sparked significant political debate about the equitable distribution of economic benefits from heritage tourism. Additionally, despite its majority Black population, Salvador's political and economic elites have historically been dominated by a mostly European-descended upper class, leading to ongoing discussions about racial representation and inequality.
