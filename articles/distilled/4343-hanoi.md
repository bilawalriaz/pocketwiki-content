# Hanoi

## Overview
Hanoi is the capital and second-most populous municipality of Vietnam, serving as the nation's political, cultural, and historical heart. Its significance stems from over a millennium as a seat of power, from the Lý dynasty to the modern Socialist Republic, and its role as a major economic and educational hub.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Human habitation from the Palaeolithic and Mesolithic ages is evidenced in the area.
- **c. 3rd century BC** — The fortified citadel of Cổ Loa, the first political center of Vietnamese civilization in the region, is constructed.
- **1010** — Emperor Lý Thái Tổ establishes the capital of Đại Việt at Đại La, naming it Thăng Long ("ascending dragon").
- **1428** — King Lê Lợi renames the city Đông Kinh ("eastern capital") after overthrowing Ming Chinese rule.
- **1831** — The Nguyễn dynasty king Minh Mạng renames the city Hà Nội ("within the river").
- **1873** — Hanoi is conquered and occupied by the French military.
- **1902** — Hanoi becomes the capital of French Indochina.
- **2 September 1945** — Ho Chi Minh proclaims the independence of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam (DRV) with Hanoi as its capital.
- **1954** — Following the Geneva Conference, Hanoi becomes the capital of North Vietnam.
- **2 July 1976** — Hanoi becomes the capital of the unified Socialist Republic of Vietnam.
- **1 August 2008** — Hanoi's area is significantly expanded by merging with Hà Tây Province and other districts.

## Body

### Historical Evolution
The area has been continuously inhabited for approximately 4,000 years, following a period when it was submerged by rising sea levels. Early political centers included the Cổ Loa citadel (c. 3rd century BC) and the Chinese-founded settlement of Tống Bình (5th century AD). The city's modern history began in 1010 when the Lý dynasty established Thăng Long as the capital of Đại Việt. It remained the primary capital until 1802, when the Nguyễn dynasty moved the seat to Huế, and the city was renamed Hanoi in 1831. Hanoi served as the capital of French Indochina from 1902 to 1945. After the August Revolution, it became the capital of the DRV in 1945 and, after periods of conflict and division, the capital of the unified Socialist Republic of Vietnam in 1976.

### Environment and Climate
Hanoi faces significant environmental challenges, ranking among the world's most air-polluted cities, with fine dust concentrations far exceeding WHO limits. Major rivers flowing through the city are polluted, as 78% of wastewater is discharged without treatment. The city has a monsoon-influenced humid subtropical climate (Köppen Cwa) with four distinct seasons: hot, humid summers; mild autumns; cool, dry winters; and drizzly springs. Average annual rainfall is 1,612 mm, and the average annual temperature is 23.6 °C.

### Economy and Development
Hanoi is a major economic center, contributing significantly to Vietnam's GDP. Its economy is transitioning, with state-owned enterprises providing over three-quarters of jobs, but a growing private sector. The city is part of the historical Maritime Silk Road. Urban development has been guided by master plans since the 1990s, aiming to balance modernization with the protection of historic areas like the Old Quarter. The Đổi Mới economic reforms of 1986 spurred growth and attracted investment, though development has sometimes displaced residential areas.

### Demographics and Society
Hanoi's population has grown dramatically, especially after a major territorial expansion in 2008. As of 2019, it had over 8 million inhabitants, with the Viet (Kinh) ethnic group comprising 98.66% of the population. The city's civil society, shaped by Đổi Mới, consists of organizations that are often more policy-focused and tradition-bound compared to those in Ho Chi Minh City, reflecting different societal identities between North and South Vietnam.

### Landmarks and Culture
Hanoi's architecture is a blend of styles, including French colonial villas, brutalist apartments, and traditional tube houses. Key landmarks include the Old Quarter with its historic "36 streets," the Imperial Citadel of Thăng Long, the Temple of Literature (Vietnam's oldest university, founded 1010), and the Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum. The city was designated a UNESCO "City for Peace" in 1999.

### Infrastructure and Transportation
The city has an extensive road network and is the hub for Vietnam's railway system, including the Reunification Express line to Ho Chi Minh City. Traffic congestion is a major issue, with over 7.6 million registered vehicles as of 2022. A metro system is under development, with two lines operational as of 2024. A plan to ban motorbikes by 2030 has been proposed to reduce pollution and congestion.

## Terms
- **Đổi Mới**: Economic reforms initiated in 1986 to transition Vietnam towards a market-oriented economy.
- **Đại Việt**: The name of the Vietnamese feudal state from the 11th to the 18th century.
- **French Indochina**: The colonial union of French territories in Southeast Asia, including Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia, with Hanoi as its capital from 1902.
- **Köppen climate classification**: A system for classifying climates based on temperature and precipitation patterns.
- **Maritime Silk Road**: An ancient network of trade routes connecting East Asia to the Mediterranean via the Indian Ocean.
- **Old Quarter**: The historic commercial heart of Hanoi, known for its 36 traditional guild streets.
- **Reunification Express**: The primary railway line connecting Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City.
- **Thăng Long**: The historical name for Hanoi, meaning "ascending dragon," used from 1010 to the 19th century.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The optimal balance between modern urban development and the preservation of Hanoi's historic and cultural heritage remains a point of contention, as seen in public outcry over redevelopment plans.
- The effectiveness and social impact of policies to ban street vendors and motorbikes in pursuit of a "modern" city image are debated, with vendors seen by some as adding traditional character.
- The relationship between state and civil society organizations is often strained, influenced by a legacy of statism and differing approaches to social issues compared to southern Vietnam.
