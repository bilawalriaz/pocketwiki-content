# 2008 financial crisis

## Overview
The financial crisis of 2007–2008 was a severe worldwide economic crisis centered in the United States, triggered by the collapse of the U.S. housing bubble and the subsequent failure of major financial institutions. It led to the Great Recession, a global economic downturn, and prompted unprecedented government bailouts and regulatory reforms. The crisis exposed systemic risks from excessive leverage, complex financial products, and inadequate regulation, reshaping global finance and politics.

## Timeline
- **2004** — SEC relaxes net capital rule for major investment banks.
- **2006** — U.S. housing prices peak; mortgage delinquencies rise.
- **April 2, 2007** — New Century Financial, a major subprime lender, files for bankruptcy.
- **August 9, 2007** — BNP Paribas blocks fund withdrawals, signaling a global liquidity crisis.
- **March 17, 2008** — Bear Stearns collapses and is sold to JPMorgan Chase in a Fed-backed "fire sale."
- **September 7, 2008** — U.S. government seizes Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac.
- **September 15, 2008** — Lehman Brothers files for bankruptcy, the largest in U.S. history.
- **September 16, 2008** — Federal Reserve bails out American International Group (AIG).
- **October 3, 2008** — Congress passes the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act, creating the $700 billion TARP fund. |
- **March 6, 2009** — Dow Jones Industrial Average hits its crisis low of 6,469.95.
- **June 2009** — U.S. recession officially ends.
- **July 21, 2010** — Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act is enacted.

## Body

### Causes and Buildup
The crisis was fueled by a U.S. housing bubble, where home prices rose 124% between 1998 and 2006. This was enabled by a "global savings glut" of foreign capital seeking safe assets, which flowed into U.S. mortgage markets. Lending standards deteriorated, with a rise in subprime and no-documentation ("NINJA") loans. These mortgages were bundled into complex securities like mortgage-backed securities (MBS) and collateralized debt obligations (CDOs), which were often overrated by credit rating agencies. Deregulation, including the 1999 Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act repealing parts of Glass-Steagall, allowed commercial and investment banks to merge and take on greater risk. The Federal Reserve's low interest rates from 2000–2003 further fueled borrowing and speculation.

### The Crash and Global Contagion
As interest rates rose from 2004–2006, housing demand fell and prices declined, causing widespread mortgage defaults. The value of MBS and CDOs plummeted, triggering a liquidity crisis. The collapse of Lehman Brothers in September 2008 caused a global stock market crash and froze credit markets. The crisis spread internationally because toxic securities were owned by global investors, and interconnected derivatives like credit default swaps (CDS) linked institutions worldwide. The "shadow banking system"—unregulated entities like hedge funds and investment banks—experienced a run, further freezing lending.

### Government Response and Aftermath
Governments worldwide deployed massive bailouts and stimulus. In the U.S., the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) purchased toxic assets and injected capital into banks. The Federal Reserve implemented quantitative easing (QE), buying trillions in assets to inject liquidity. The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 provided fiscal stimulus. These actions, combined with similar global efforts, ended the worst of the recession by mid-2009. The crisis led to the Dodd-Frank Act in the U.S. and Basel III standards globally to increase bank capital and oversight. However, the economic impact was severe: U.S. unemployment peaked at 10%, household wealth fell by $11 trillion, and poverty rates increased.

## Terms
- **Mortgage-Backed Security (MBS):** A type of investment that is secured by a collection of mortgages.
- **Collateralized Debt Obligation (CDO):** A complex financial product that pools together various debt instruments, such as bonds or loans, and slices them into tranches with different risk levels.
- **Credit Default Swap (CDS):** A financial derivative that functions like insurance against the default of a borrower; the seller agrees to compensate the buyer if a specific debt instrument defaults.
- **Subprime Mortgage:** A loan made to a borrower with a poor credit history, typically carrying a higher interest rate to compensate for greater risk.
- **Shadow Banking System:** A network of financial intermediaries, such as hedge funds and investment banks, that facilitate credit creation but are not subject to the same regulatory oversight as traditional banks.
- **Quantitative Easing (QE):** An unconventional monetary policy where a central bank purchases long-term securities from the open market to increase the money supply and encourage lending.
- **Leverage:** The use of borrowed money (debt) to increase the potential return of an investment.
- **Securitization:** The process of pooling various types of contractual debt (like mortgages) and selling their related cash flows to third-party investors as securities.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars and official reports disagree on the primary cause of the crisis. The majority report of the U.S. Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission (FCIC) blamed "widespread failures in financial regulation and supervision," corporate governance, and risky behavior by financial institutions. A dissenting view, notably from FCIC member Peter Wallison, argued that government affordable housing policies and the actions of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac were the primary drivers. Other analyses point to the role of global capital flows, the "paradox of deleveraging," or systemic flaws in capitalism itself. The effectiveness of the post-crisis regulations, like Dodd-Frank (which was partially weakened in 2018), and whether the "too big to fail" problem has been solved remain open questions.
