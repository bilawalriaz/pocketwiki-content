# Nicéphore Niépce

## Overview
Joseph Nicéphore Niépce (1765–1833) was a French inventor and a pioneer of photography. He is credited with creating the world's oldest surviving photograph of a real-world scene and developing heliography, the first successful photographic process. His work laid the foundation for modern photography, though he died in obscurity, with his partner Louis Daguerre later receiving most of the initial credit.

## Timeline
- **7 March 1765** — Born in Chalon-sur-Saône, France.
- **1807** — Invented the Pyréolophore, one of the first internal combustion engines, with his brother Claude.
- **c. 1816** — Began early photographic experiments using silver chloride-coated paper.
- **1822** — Created the first permanent photographic image (a copy of an engraving), later destroyed.
- **1825** — Produced the earliest surviving photographic artifacts (photo-etchings of engravings).
- **c. 1826–27** — Created the oldest surviving camera photograph, a view from his estate window.
- **1829** — Entered a partnership with Louis Daguerre to improve photographic processes.
- **5 July 1833** — Died of a stroke, financially ruined.
- **1839** — The French government purchased Daguerre's improved process, granting a pension to Niépce's estate.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Service
Niépce was born into a wealthy family and was educated at an Oratorian college, where he learned science and the experimental method. He served as a staff officer in Napoleon's army but resigned due to ill health. After marrying, he became an administrator in Nice before resigning in 1795 to pursue scientific research with his older brother, Claude.

### Scientific Research and the Pyréolophore
In 1801, the brothers returned to the family estate to manage it as gentleman-farmers. Their collaborative research led to the invention of the Pyréolophore in 1807, an early internal combustion engine that ran on lycopodium powder dust explosions and was installed on a boat. They later pioneered a fuel injection system. The brothers also designed an improved hydraulic machine for the Marly waterworks, though their proposal was ultimately rejected in favor of a steam engine.

### Invention of Photography
Niépce's interest in lithography and the camera obscura led him to seek a way to permanently capture its images. After early experiments with silver chloride produced unstable negatives, he turned to bitumen of Judea, a light-sensitive asphalt. He dissolved bitumen in lavender oil, coated it on a surface, and exposed it to light through an engraving or in a camera. The light hardened the bitumen, which could then be used to create a permanent image. He called this process heliography ("sun drawing").

His first permanent image (1822) was a copy of an engraving, but it was later destroyed. The earliest surviving artifacts are photo-etchings from 1825. His greatest achievement was the oldest surviving camera photograph, created between 1822 and 1827. Exposure likely took several days. In 1829, he partnered with Louis Daguerre to develop the physautotype, an improved process. After Niépce's death, Daguerre perfected his own daguerreotype process, which the French government purchased in 1839, providing a pension to Niépce's son. While initially overshadowed, Niépce is now recognized as the inventor of the first successful photographic process.

### Other Inventions and Later Life
Niépce also built an early bicycle, the vélocipède, in 1818. His brother Claude died destitute in England after squandering family funds on failed Pyréolophore ventures. Nicéphore Niépce died in 1833, financially ruined.

## Terms
- **Heliography**: Niépce's photographic process, meaning "sun drawing," using light-sensitive bitumen to create permanent images.
- **Camera obscura**: A darkened box or room with a small hole that projects an inverted image of the outside scene onto an interior surface, used as a drawing aid.
- **Bitumen of Judea**: A naturally occurring asphalt that becomes less soluble when exposed to light, used by Niépce as the first practical photosensitive material.
- **Photo-etching**: A print made from a printing plate created photographically, rather than by hand-engraving.
- **Physautotype**: An improved photographic process developed by Niépce and Daguerre, using lavender oil distillate as the photosensitive substance.
- **Daguerreotype**: An early photographic process developed by Louis Daguerre after Niépce's death, which became the first commercially viable method.
- **Pyréolophore**: One of the world's first internal combustion engines, invented by the Niépce brothers in 1807.
- **Vélocipède**: An early form of bicycle, which Niépce built and improved in 1818.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact date of Niépce's first successful camera photograph is uncertain, with sources placing it sometime between 1822 and 1827.
- The exposure time for his oldest surviving photograph is debated. A common estimate of 8-9 hours is a later assumption; recreations using his methods suggest several days were needed.
- The extent of Louis Daguerre's reliance on Niépce's foundational work versus his own independent development remains a point of historical discussion, especially given the initial lack of credit for Niépce.
