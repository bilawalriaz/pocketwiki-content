# Natural History Museum, London

## Overview
The Natural History Museum in London is a world-leading public research institution and tourist attraction, housing approximately 80 million specimens across botany, entomology, mineralogy, palaeontology, and zoology. It is a centre for taxonomy, identification, and conservation, renowned for its iconic architecture and famous exhibits like dinosaur skeletons. Its collections, which include specimens collected by Charles Darwin, have immense historical and scientific value.

## Timeline
- **1756** — Sir Hans Sloane's collections, forming the foundation, are housed in Montagu House, Bloomsbury, as part of the British Museum.
- **1856** — Richard Owen is appointed Superintendent of the natural history departments, beginning reforms.
- **1864** — A competition is held to design a new building for the collections.
- **1866** — Alfred Waterhouse is hired to revise and execute the winning design.
- **1873** — Construction of the new museum building begins in South Kensington.
- **1881** — The new museum opens on 18 April, though the move is not complete until 1883.
- **1963** — The British Museum Act legally separates the museum from the British Museum, though it retains the name "British Museum (Natural History)".
- **1985** — The museum merges with the adjacent Geological Museum.
- **1992** — The Museums and Galleries Act formally changes the museum's title to the Natural History Museum.
- **2002** — Phase One of the Darwin Centre opens, housing spirit collections.
- **2009** — Phase Two of the Darwin Centre opens, housing dry collections in a "cocoon" structure.
- **2017** — The Diplodocus cast "Dippy" is replaced in Hintze Hall by the blue whale skeleton "Hope".

## Body

### Origins and Early History
The museum's collections originated from those of Sir Hans Sloane, purchased by the British Government in the 18th century. Housed within the British Museum, the natural history departments suffered from poor conservation, mismanagement, and a lack of public access. Significant reform began with the appointment of palaeontologist Richard Owen in 1856, who championed the creation of a dedicated, publicly accessible institution.

### The Waterhouse Building
Recognizing the need for more space, a new building was commissioned in South Kensington. After a design competition, architect Alfred Waterhouse substantially revised the plans, creating the landmark Romanesque-style building that opened in 1881. Its interiors and exteriors feature extensive architectural terracotta tiles depicting flora and fauna, with living species on the west wing and extinct species on the east—a separation requested by Owen.

### Institutional Independence
Despite moving to its own building, the museum legally remained a department of the British Museum, formally named "British Museum (Natural History)". A long campaign for independence, supported by figures like Darwin and Huxley, culminated in the British Museum Act 1963, which granted legal separation. The museum rebranded publicly in 1989 and its formal title was changed to the Natural History Museum by the Museums and Galleries Act 1992.

### Major Expansions and Collections
The museum merged with the Geological Museum in 1985, whose galleries were relaunched as the Earth Galleries in 1996. The most significant modern development is the Darwin Centre, built in two phases (2002 and 2009) to house tens of millions of preserved specimens and provide research facilities. Its most famous resident is "Archie," a giant squid preserved in a large tank. The museum's iconic exhibits include the Diplodocus cast "Dippy" (on tour since 2018) and the blue whale skeleton "Hope," which replaced it in Hintze Hall in 2017.

## Terms
- **Taxonomy**: The science of classification, naming, and describing organisms.
- **Spirit collections**: Specimens preserved in alcohol or formalin, primarily housed in the Darwin Centre.
- **Dry collections**: Preserved specimens not in liquid, such as pinned insects or dried plants, also in the Darwin Centre.
- **Architectural terracotta**: Fired clay used for building decoration, chosen by Waterhouse to resist London's soot.
- **Non-departmental public body**: A type of public body that is not a government department but is sponsored by one.
- **Exempt charity**: A charity that is exempt from registration with the Charity Commission but is regulated by its principal regulator.
- **Hintze Hall**: The museum's central hall, formerly known as the Central Hall, which houses the blue whale skeleton.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The museum's strategic direction has faced criticism, particularly regarding plans to move major parts of its collections to a new facility in Shinfield, Berkshire, with construction beginning in 2025 and an expected opening in 2031.
- The architectural terracotta separation of living and extinct species on the Waterhouse building's façade is interpreted as a statement of Owen's rebuttal of Darwin's theory of natural selection, though Waterhouse included some anomalies.
