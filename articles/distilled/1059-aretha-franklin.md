# Aretha Franklin

## Overview

Aretha Louise Franklin (March 25, 1942 – August 16, 2018) was an American singer, songwriter, and pianist widely regarded as the "Queen of Soul" and twice named by *Rolling Stone* as the greatest singer of all time. Born in Memphis, Tennessee, she moved to Buffalo, New York at age two and then to Detroit by age five, where she rose from gospel roots at New Bethel Baptist Church to become one of the best-selling music artists of all time, with over 75 million records sold and 112 Billboard-charting singles. Her career spanned five decades, marked by a powerful mezzo-soprano voice, deep gospel influence, and anthems like "Respect" that became rallying cries for civil rights and women’s empowerment.

## Body

### Early Life and Gospel Roots

Aretha Franklin was born to C. L. Franklin, a Baptist minister known for his "million-dollar voice," and Barbara Franklin, a pianist and vocalist. Her parents separated in 1948, and her mother died on March 7, 1952, before Aretha's 10th birthday. Raised primarily by her father and various caregivers, including gospel legend Mahalia Jackson, Franklin learned piano by ear and began performing at New Bethel Baptist Church. At age 12, she started touring with her father's gospel caravans and recorded her first album, *Songs of Faith*, at just 14.

### Rise to Fame: Columbia and Atlantic Years

At 18, Franklin signed with Columbia Records, where she explored jazz, blues, and pop but struggled commercially. In 1966, she moved to Atlantic Records, where producer Jerry Wexler encouraged her to embrace her gospel roots. This shift led to a string of hits beginning with "I Never Loved a Man (The Way I Love You)" and "Respect," which became her signature song and a feminist and civil rights anthem. Over the next decade, she dominated the charts with classics like "(You Make Me Feel Like) A Natural Woman," "Chain of Fools," and "Think."

### Later Career and Legacy

After leaving Atlantic in 1979, Franklin signed with Arista Records in 1980, where Clive Davis helped revive her career with albums like *Jump to It* and *Who's Zoomin' Who?*, featuring crossover hits such as "Freeway of Love" and "I Knew You Were Waiting (For Me)" with George Michael. Though her later output lacked consistent chart dominance, she remained a respected figure, performing at major events and earning tributes from peers. She won 18 Grammy Awards, was inducted into multiple halls of fame, and received the Presidential Medal of Freedom. In 2019, she was posthumously awarded a Pulitzer Prize Special Citation.

## Terms

- **Queen of Soul**: Title given to Franklin by radio personality Pervis Spann in the 1960s, reflecting her dominance in soul music.
- **Gospel**: A genre of Christian music rooted in African-American spirituals; central to Franklin's vocal style and early career.
- **Soul Music**: A genre blending R&B, gospel, and blues, characterized by emotional expression and strong vocals; Franklin was its most iconic exponent.
- **Muscle Shoals Rhythm Section**: The house band at FAME Studios in Alabama, known for backing legendary artists including Franklin on "I Never Loved a Man."
- **Mezzo-Soprano**: A vocal range between soprano and baritone; Franklin's voice was described as a powerful mezzo-soprano.
- **Civil Rights Anthem**: Songs associated with the struggle for racial equality; Franklin's "Respect" and "Natural Woman" became symbols of empowerment.
- **Grammy Living Legend Award**: Honor given by the Recording Academy to artists for lifetime achievement; Franklin received it in addition to 18 competitive Grammys.
- **Presidential Medal of Freedom**: The highest civilian honor in the U.S., awarded to Franklin in 2005 for her contributions to music and society.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Columbia Years Assessment**: Some critics argue that Columbia Records failed to capitalize on Franklin's gospel background, limiting her early potential. Others note her versatility across genres during this period.
- **Authenticity of "Respect"**: While Otis Redding originally wrote and recorded "Respect," Franklin's version transformed it into a feminist and civil rights anthem. The extent to which this reinterpretation overshadowed the original remains debated.
- **Posthumous Recognition**: Franklin's 2019 Pulitzer Prize Special Citation sparked discussion about whether other deserving artists have been overlooked by the Pulitzer committee.
- **Family Dynamics**: Franklin's relationship with her father, C. L. Franklin, has been scrutinized, particularly regarding his influence on her early career and personal life.
- **Eulogy Controversy**: Rev. Jasper Williams Jr.'s 2018 eulogy drew criticism for its political content and lack of focus on Franklin's life, raising questions about appropriate funeral oratory.
