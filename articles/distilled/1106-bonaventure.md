# Bonaventure

## Overview
Bonaventure (Giovanni di Fidanza, 1221–15 July 1274) was an Italian Franciscan friar who became the Order’s seventh Minister General, a Cardinal Bishop of Albano, and a leading scholastic theologian. He steered the Franciscans toward a moderate, intellectual path that made them the Church’s most prominent order until the rise of the Jesuits. His theology centered on integrating faith and reason through a Christocentric Neoplatonism: Christ is the "one true master" and the divine exemplar through whom creation emanates, is understood, and returns to God. Canonized in 1482 and declared a Doctor of the Church in 1588, he is known as the "Seraphic Doctor."

## Body

### Life and Governance
Bonaventure was born in Civita di Bagnoregio. He credited Francis of Assisi with miraculously saving his life in youth, motivating his later biography of the founder. After joining the Franciscans in 1243, he studied at Paris, receiving his master’s degree in 1257 alongside Thomas Aquinas after a dispute between secular masters and mendicant orders delayed his reception. Elected Minister General in 1257, he governed during a period of internal tension. The 1260 General Chapter of Narbonne prohibited publishing without permission—a decree prompted by Gerard of Borgo San Donnino’s unauthorized, heretical *Introductorius in Evangelium æternum* (1254), not initially aimed at Roger Bacon, though Bacon was later restricted by it. Bonaventure declined the Archbishopric of York (1265–66). He secured the election of Pope Gregory X, who made him Cardinal Bishop of Albano (1273) and compelled his attendance at the Second Council of Lyon (1274), where he died suddenly; the 1913 *Catholic Encyclopedia* suggested poisoning, omitted in the 2003 edition.

### Writings and Textual History
The critical Quaracchi edition organizes his works into a four-volume *Commentary on the Sentences* of Peter Lombard (written at age twenty-seven, his *magnum opus*) and eight volumes of other treatises. Key works include *Itinerarium mentis in Deum* (The Mind’s Road to God), *Breviloquium* (Brief Reading), *De reductione artium ad theologiam* (Reduction of the Arts to Theology), *Lignum vitae* (The Tree of Life), and *De Triplici via* (The Triple Way). *De reductione* was defended as authentic against Dieter Hattrup’s stylistic objections. *De septem itineribus aeternitatis* is now attributed to Rudolf von Biberach (c. 1270–1329). Many medieval works once ascribed to him are classified as *Pseudo-Bonaventure*.

### Philosophy: Augustinian Neoplatonism
Bonaventure exemplifies the Scholastic interpenetration of philosophy and theology; no work is purely philosophical. He is considered the premier medieval Augustinian (per De Wulf), integrating Aristotelian matter-form hylomorphism into an Augustinian framework. Against the rising Aristotelianism of Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas, and the empirical bent of Roger Bacon, Bonaventure championed a mystical, Platonizing speculation rooted in the "affections of the heart." He adopted the Christian Neoplatonic doctrine of **exemplarism**: eternal forms ("exemplars") exist as ideas in the mind of God, not as subsistent entities, serving as archetypes for creation. He vigorously opposed the Aristotelian eternity of the world, arguing reason can prove the world’s temporal beginning—a point of disagreement with Aquinas. Physicist Max Bernhard Weinstein characterized him as a "half-mystic" with "strong pandeistic inclinations."

### Theology: Emanation, Exemplarism, Consummation
Bonaventure structures theology on three Platonic principles rooted in Christ the Son: **emanationism** (creation flows from divine exemplars), **exemplarism** (the Word contains the rationes of all things), and **consummation** (creation returns to its source). In the *Collationes in Hexaemeron*, exemplarism grounds dogmas: divine love, foreknowledge, providence, human soul immortality, and creation’s goodness. He rejects **Avicennan necessitarianism** (associated with Arabic Aristotelianism), arguing that denying divine exemplars eliminates providence, reduces causality to blind necessity or chance, and obscures eschatological truths (hell, devil, beatitude). 

On faith and reason: all sciences serve theology; reason grasps some moral truths, but higher truths require **divine illumination** attained through prayer, virtue, and meditation culminating in ecstatic union. The supreme end—contemplative union in love—is only fully realized in the afterlife. Unlike Aquinas, Bonaventure held philosophy cannot be autonomous; without faith’s light, the philosopher falls into grave error. He offers multiple arguments for God’s existence (Anselm’s ontological argument, Augustine’s argument from eternal truths) and for the soul’s immortality (natural desire for perfect happiness, anticipating C. S. Lewis).

Three routes to God structure his thought: (1) **Vestiges** (*vestigia*)—non-intellectual creatures as "footprints" proving a temporal creation; (2) **Images** (*imagines*)—intellectual creatures (mind/will) reflecting God as illuminator and grace-giver; (3) **Being** (*esse*)—God as absolutely simple, perfect being whose essence entails existence, causing all composite beings (synthesizing Anselm with Aristotelian/Neoplatonic metaphysics). 

Pope Francis, in *Laudato si'*, cites Bonaventure’s view that pre-Fall humans could perceive the Trinitarian structure in every creature ("testifies that God is three"), a capacity darkened by sin. On disputed scholastic questions, he sided with Albert the Great in viewing theology as practical (moving the affections), held universals as divine ideas, matter as pure potentiality informed by God’s power, and denied a separate *intellectus agens* (agent intellect).

## Terms
- ****Exemplarism**** — The doctrine that eternal forms ("exemplars") exist as ideas in the mind of God, serving as the archetypes according to which all creatures are made.
- ****Emanationism**** — The Neoplatonic principle that creation flows forth from the divine source (the Word/Son) as an expression of the divine exemplars.
- ****Consummation**** — The teleological return of all creation to its divine origin and goal in God.
- ****Divine Illumination**** — The supernatural light granted by God necessary for the intellect to grasp higher theological truths beyond natural reason's reach.
- ****Vestigia (Vestiges)**** — "Footprints" of God found in non-intellectual material creatures, indicating God as ultimate cause.
- ****Imagines (Images)**** — The likeness of God found in intellectual creatures (human mind and will), reflecting God as illuminator and donor of grace.
- ****Intellectus Agens (Agent Intellect)**** — The faculty in Aristotelian psychology that abstracts universal forms from sensory particulars; Bonaventure denied it a separate subsistent existence.
- ****Avicennan Necessitarianism**** — The philosophical system (associated with Avicenna/Ibn Sina) positing necessary causal chains emanating from God, which Bonaventure rejected as denying divine freedom and providence.
- ****Pseudo-Bonaventure**** — The conventional name for medieval works once attributed to Bonaventure but later recognized as spurious.
- ****Seraphic Doctor**** — The title bestowed on Bonaventure upon his declaration as a Doctor of the Church in 1588, referencing the seraphic (burning/loving) character of his theology.

## Debates and open questions
- **Cause of death**: The 1913 *Catholic Encyclopedia* cites contemporary suggestions he was poisoned at the Council of Lyon; the 2003 *New Catholic Encyclopedia* omits this claim. The matter remains historically unresolved.
- **Authenticity of *De reductione artium ad theologiam***: Philosopher Dieter Hattrup denied authenticity based on style; the Quaracchi editors and recent research maintain it is "indubitably authentic."
- **Philosophy vs. Theology autonomy**: Bonaventure’s insistence that philosophy cannot be successfully pursued without faith’s light contrasts sharply with Aquinas’s view of philosophy as an autonomous discipline; this remains a defining fault line in interpreting 13th-century scholasticism.
- **Pandeism accusation**: Max Bernhard Weinstein’s characterization of "strong pandeistic inclinations" (identifying God with the universe’s formal structure via exemplarism) is a modern philosophical reading debated by scholars of medieval metaphysics.
