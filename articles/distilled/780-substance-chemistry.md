# Substance (chemistry)

## Overview
A chemical substance is matter with constant composition and characteristic properties, existing as elements or compounds. The concept was solidified by Joseph Proust’s law of constant composition in the late 18th century. Chemical substances can transition between phases without changing identity, and may react to form new substances; those that cannot are inert. Understanding chemical substances underpins chemistry, materials science, and industrial manufacturing.

## Timeline
- **Late 1700s** — Joseph Proust establishes law of constant composition
- **1865** — Kekulé describes benzene structure, resolving isomer debates
- **19th century** — Development of chemical synthesis and analytical methods
- **Feb 2021** — ~177 million organic/inorganic substances indexed in literature

## Body

### Definitions and scope
A chemical substance has fixed composition and properties, whether an element or compound. Mixtures of non-reacting substances are not chemical substances unless separated to desired purity. Special cases arise in inorganic chemistry: alloys (mixtures treated as substances by CAS), non-stoichiometric compounds (variable composition, e.g. palladium hydride), and broad legal definitions including mixtures with defined processes.

### Geology
In geology, inorganic solids of uniform composition are minerals; aggregates of minerals are rocks. Minerals often form solid solutions (e.g. feldspars like anorthoclase, where sodium and potassium substitute interchangeably).

### Law
Legal frameworks like EU REACH classify substances as monoconstituent, multiconstituent, or unknown/variable composition. Identification may rely on manufacturing process rather than exact chemical identity (e.g. charcoal).

### Polymer chemistry
Polymers are typically mixtures of varying molar masses, defined by precursor or reaction and molar mass distribution (e.g. polyethylene: LDPE, HDPE, UHMWPE).

### Historical development
Proust’s work on compounds like basic copper carbonate established that all samples of a compound share identical mass proportions of elements. Isomerism challenged early understanding—identical composition but different atomic arrangements (e.g. benzene, tartaric acid isomers). Stereochemistry and tautomerism further refined distinctions between substances.

### Chemical elements
Elements are substances of one atom type, unchangeable by chemical reactions (though nuclear transmutation is possible). As of 2025, 118 elements exist (~80 stable). Elements exhibit allotropes (e.g. O₂ vs O₃), and are classified as metals (lustrous, conductive, malleable), non-metals (high electronegativity, form negative ions), or metalloids (intermediate properties).

### Chemical compounds
Compounds combine elements via chemical bonds. Organic compounds center on carbon-hydrogen; others are inorganic. Bond types include covalent (shared electrons), ionic (oppositely charged ions), and coordination complexes (dative bonds to a metal or non-metal center, e.g. [Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄·H₂O; ligands bind centers, chelates use multiple atoms). Isomers share composition but differ structurally; tautomers interconvert spontaneously (e.g. glucose ring/open-chain forms).

### Substances versus mixtures
Mixtures contain multiple substances without fixed ratios (e.g. butter, soil). They separate mechanically (chromatography, magnets). Compounds form via chemical reactions with fixed ratios (e.g. Fe + S → FeS), exhibiting unique properties and resisting mechanical separation.

### Chemicals versus chemical substances
“Chemical” in general usage includes mixtures; in industry, chemicals are pure substances classified by volume: bulk (large-scale, low-cost), fine (small-scale, high-value), and research (lab-scale, high-cost). Production involves synthesis, purification, and impurity analysis. Grades vary (technical vs pharmaceutical/USP). Commercial “chemicals” may be variable mixtures defined by specs (e.g. gasoline).

### Naming and indexing
Substances have systematic IUPAC and CAS names, plus common names (e.g. glucose = 6-(hydroxymethyl)oxane-2,3,4,5-tetrol). CAS assigns registry numbers; databases track millions of compounds. Computer systems like SMILES and InChI aid identification.

## Terms
- **Chemical substance**: Matter with constant composition and properties.
- **Isomer**: Compounds with same composition but different atomic arrangement.
- **Allotrope**: Different structural forms of the same element.
- **Coordination complex**: Compound with dative bond to a central atom/ion.
- **Tautomer**: Isomers that interconvert spontaneously.
- **Non-stoichiometric compound**: Substance with variable elemental ratios.
- **Monoconstituent substance**: Single chemical entity (legal definition).
- **Molar mass distribution**: Range of molecular weights in a polymer sample.
- **CAS Registry Number**: Unique identifier for chemical substances.
- **Organometallic compound**: Compound with carbon-metal bonds.

## Debates and open questions
Debates persist over defining chemical substances due to indexing challenges from millions of reported compounds. Boundaries blur in non-stoichiometric materials, alloys, and polymers. Legal definitions expand “substance” to include variable-composition mixtures, raising questions about precision versus practicality in regulation and commerce.
