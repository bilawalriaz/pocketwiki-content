# Louis Riel

## Overview
Louis Riel (1844–1885) was a Canadian politician, founder of Manitoba, and Métis leader. He led two major resistances against the Canadian government to defend Métis rights during the incorporation of the Northwest Territories. His execution became a defining, polarizing event in Canadian history.

## Timeline
- **22 October 1844** — Born in St-Boniface, Red River Settlement.
- **1869–1870** — Leads the Red River Resistance; establishes a provisional government.
- **4 March 1870** — Orders the execution of Thomas Scott.
- **12 May 1870** — Manitoba Act passed, creating the province of Manitoba.
- **1870–1881** — Lives in exile in the United States; elected to Parliament but never takes his seat.
- **1881** — Marries Marguerite Monet dite Bellehumeur in Montana.
- **1884** — Called by Métis leaders in Saskatchewan to address grievances.
- **19 March 1885** — Declares the Provisional Government of Saskatchewan at Batoche.
- **16 May 1885** — Surrenders after the Battle of Batoche.
- **16 November 1885** — Executed for high treason in Regina.

## Body

### Early Life and Red River Resistance
Born into a respected Métis family, Riel was educated in Montreal for the priesthood but abandoned this after his father's death. He returned to Red River in 1868, a community facing uncertainty as Canada negotiated to acquire Rupert's Land. Lack of consultation and disruptive surveys sparked the Red River Resistance. Riel led the Métis National Committee, seizing Fort Garry in 1869.

He established a provisional government to negotiate Confederation terms. Opposing settlers formed a rival faction; after arresting them, Riel ordered the execution of Thomas Scott for insubordination. This act turned English-speaking Protestant Canada against him. Negotiations led to the Manitoba Act of 1870, creating Manitoba, but Riel fled to the U.S. as Canadian troops arrived.

### Exile and Mental Illness
Riel lived in exile for over a decade, elected three times to Parliament but never taking his seat. He received a conditional amnesty in 1875 requiring five years abroad. His mental health deteriorated, leading to institutionalization and intense religious beliefs, including seeing himself as a prophet. He settled in Montana, married, started a family, and became a U.S. citizen in 1883.

### North-West Rebellion and Execution
By the 1880s, Métis and First Nations in Saskatchewan faced starvation and land grievances. In 1884, leaders including Gabriel Dumont asked Riel to advocate for them. His peaceful petitions met government delay. His religious pronouncements grew unorthodox, alienating clergy and supporters. When responses were inadequate, a militant faction formed a provisional government, initiating armed conflict in March 1885.

The North-West Rebellion saw initial Métis successes at Duck Lake and Fish Creek. However, Riel insisted on defending Batoche conventionally; it fell in May 1885. He surrendered, was tried for high treason in Regina, and rejected an insanity defense, speaking on Métis rights. Found guilty, he was executed on 16 November 1885. Prime Minister John A. Macdonald refused clemency.

### Legacy and Historiography
Riel's execution caused a lasting rift: celebrated in Ontario but mourned in Quebec as a symbol of oppression. This polarization influenced Canadian politics for decades. Historiography shifted: early English-Canadian accounts called him a traitor; mid-20th-century historians emphasized political divisions; modern interpretations often view him as a defender of Métis rights, though some argue his violence was unnecessary. He is now recognized as a founder of Manitoba and a significant, controversial figure.

## Terms
- **Métis**: A distinct Indigenous people of mixed First Nations and European ancestry.
- **Red River Resistance (1869–1870)**: Uprising led by Riel against the transfer of Rupert's Land, resulting in Manitoba's creation.
- **Provisional Government**: Temporary government established by Riel to negotiate Confederation terms.
- **Manitoba Act (1870)**: Federal legislation creating Manitoba and its entry into Confederation.
- **North-West Rebellion (1885)**: Armed conflict by Métis and First Nations in present-day Saskatchewan.
- **High Treason**: Crime for which Riel was convicted, defined as levying war against the sovereign.
- **Exovedate**: Council formed by Riel during the rebellion, meaning "those who picked from the flock."
- **Amnesty**: Formal pardon; Riel's 1875 conditional amnesty required five years of exile.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Riel's Mental State**: Debate over whether his actions were visionary or evidence of mental illness, a defense he rejected.
- **Justification of Violence**: Arguments over whether armed rebellion was a necessary last resort or unnecessary escalation.
- **The Scott Execution**: Analysis of this political blunder and its catastrophic consequences for Riel's cause.
- **Historical Legacy**: Evolving interpretations from "traitor" to "folk hero" and "Father of Confederation," reflecting debates on Canadian identity and Indigenous rights.
