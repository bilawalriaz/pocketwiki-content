# Zhou dynasty

## Overview
The Zhou dynasty was the longest-ruling royal dynasty in Chinese history, lasting from c. 1046 BC to 256 BC. It is pivotal for establishing foundational Chinese political doctrines like the Mandate of Heaven, developing a feudal-like social system, and serving as the golden age for Chinese philosophy, including Confucianism, Taoism, and Legalism.

## Timeline
- **c. 1046 BC** — Zhou defeats Shang at Battle of Muye, establishing the dynasty.
- **c. 1046–771 BC** — Western Zhou period; royal power is strong, centered on the Wei River valley.
- **771 BC** — Sacking of Haojing by Quanrong ends Western Zhou; capital moves east.
- **c. 771–481 BC** — Spring and Autumn period; royal authority diminishes, power decentralizes.
- **403 BC** — Zhou court recognizes Han, Zhao, and Wei as independent states.
- **c. 475–221 BC** — Warring States period; large-scale warfare and state consolidation.
- **256 BC** — Qin captures Wangcheng, killing King Nan and formally ending the Zhou dynasty.
- **221 BC** — Qin conquers all rival states, founding the imperial Qin dynasty.

## Body

### Foundation and Western Zhou
The Zhou lineage traced its origins to a mythical culture hero, Houji, who improved agriculture. The dynasty was founded when King Wu, with allies like Jiang Ziya, defeated the Shang at the Battle of Muye around 1046 BC. To govern their expanded territory, the Zhou implemented the *fengjian* system, enfeoffing relatives and allies in hereditary fiefs. They legitimized their rule through the doctrine of the Mandate of Heaven, which held that Heaven granted the right to rule based on moral virtue and could withdraw it from corrupt rulers. The Western Zhou period saw strong royal control but ended when the capital Haojing was sacked in 771 BC by Quanrong tribes, prompting a move eastward.

### Eastern Zhou: Spring and Autumn and Warring States
The Eastern Zhou period was marked by the accelerating decline of royal authority. During the Spring and Autumn period (c. 771–481 BC), power became increasingly decentralized among regional lords. The subsequent Warring States period (c. 475–221 BC) was characterized by intense warfare as states fought for dominance. In 403 BC, the Zhou court formally recognized the independence of Han, Zhao, and Wei. By 344 BC, rulers like Duke Hui of Wei began claiming the title of "king" for themselves, abandoning any pretense of vassalage to the Zhou. This era of conflict ultimately led to the conquest of all rival states by Qin in 221 BC.

### Culture, Society, and Philosophy
The Zhou period is renowned for its bronze craftsmanship and the flourishing of the "Hundred Schools of Thought." Key philosophies that emerged include Confucianism, Taoism, and Legalism. The Zhou social system was based on patrilineal primogeniture, where the eldest son inherited the main lineage and authority. Agriculture was the economic base, with land tied to aristocratic lineages. The Zhou military initially fielded powerful armies but suffered a major defeat when King Zhao and his six armies were wiped out. The period also saw the development of Chinese writing into seal script and the beginnings of clerical script.

## Terms
- **Mandate of Heaven**: A Zhou political doctrine stating that Heaven grants the right to rule based on moral virtue and can revoke it from unjust rulers.
- **Fengjian**: The Zhou system of enfeoffment, where hereditary fiefs were granted to relatives and allies to govern territories.
- **Spring and Autumn period**: The first part of the Eastern Zhou (c. 771–481 BC), named after the Confucian chronicle, marked by decentralizing royal power.
- **Warring States period**: The later Eastern Zhou (c. 475–221 BC), characterized by large-scale warfare and consolidation among former Zhou client states.
- **Patrilineal primogeniture**: The inheritance system where the eldest son of each generation inherits the main lineage and political authority.
- **Ru**: The class of scholar-officials who mastered ritual, ceremony, and ancient canons, later associated with Confucianism.
- **Li**: The ritual system that encoded social hierarchy, ethics, and material regulations, later canonized in Confucian texts.
- **Houji**: The legendary culture hero and ancestor of the Zhou lineage, credited with improving agriculture.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact date of the Zhou dynasty's establishment is debated. The Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project supports c. 1046 BC, while scholars David Nivison and Edward L. Shaughnessy date it to 1045 BC.
- The nature of the *fengjian* system is debated; while often compared to European feudalism, key differences exist, such as the Zhou ruling from walled cities rather than castles and a distinct class system.
- The well-field system, an idealized model of agricultural land division described in classical texts, is generally viewed by modern historians as a later conceptual model rather than a documented administrative system of the Zhou period.
