# Joseph Pulitzer

## Overview
Joseph Pulitzer (1847–1911) was a Hungarian-born Jewish immigrant who became a dominant force in American journalism and politics. After serving in the Union Army during the Civil War, he built a newspaper empire in St. Louis and New York, pioneering "yellow journalism"—a sensationalist style emphasizing crime, scandal, and human-interest stories—to achieve mass circulation and advertising-based revenue models. His fierce circulation war with William Randolph Hearst in the 1890s defined the era. Pulitzer leveraged his papers for political influence, serving as a Missouri state representative and a U.S. Congressman, while championing populist causes like the Statue of Liberty pedestal fund. His enduring legacy is the Pulitzer Prizes, established by his 1911 bequest to Columbia University to recognize excellence in journalism and the arts.

## Body

### Early Life and Civil War Service
Born into a prominent Jewish merchant family in Makó, Hungary, Pulitzer enjoyed a privileged childhood until his father’s death in 1858 led to bankruptcy and poverty. Rejected by the Austrian, French, and British armies, he was recruited in Hamburg at age 17 to fight for the Union in the American Civil War. He was paid $200 to enlist in the 1st New York Cavalry Regiment, Company L (Lincoln Cavalry), joining the regiment in Virginia in November 1864 and fighting in the Appomattox Campaign. Because his regiment consisted largely of German immigrants, Pulitzer spoke little English until after the war, leaving the service in June 1865.

### St. Louis: Journalism and Political Ascent
Destitute, Pulitzer drifted to St. Louis, where the large German-speaking population allowed him to function. He worked menial jobs—mule hostler, waiter—while studying English and law at the Mercantile Library. He gained the mentorship of Carl Schurz, editor of the *Westliche Post*, who hired him as a reporter in 1868. Pulitzer entered politics as a Republican, winning a seat in the Missouri legislature in 1869 despite being three years under the constitutional age requirement. He championed the Fifteenth Amendment and fought corruption in the St. Louis County Court, a crusade that led to a shootout with a political rival, Captain Edward Augustine, in 1870.

### Break with Republicans and Party Switching
Pulitzer followed Schurz into the Liberal Republican revolt against President Grant in 1870, helping elect Benjamin Gratz Brown as Missouri governor. He served as a delegate to the 1872 Cincinnati convention nominating Horace Greeley. After the Liberal Republican collapse, Pulitzer briefly promoted a "People's Party" in 1874 but grew disillusioned and endorsed the Democratic ticket. By 1876, he was a committed Democrat, giving nearly seventy speeches nationwide for Samuel Tilden and denouncing his former mentor Schurz for returning to the Republican fold. While on his speaking tour, Pulitzer also wrote dispatches to the *New York Sun* on behalf of the Tilden campaign. This shift aligned with Pulitzer’s views on low tariffs and limited federal power, his earlier Republicanism driven primarily by opposition to slavery.

### The *St. Louis Post-Dispatch*
On his 31st birthday (1878), Pulitzer bought the failing *St. Louis Dispatch* and merged it with the *St. Louis Post*. He transformed the *Post-Dispatch* into a populist, sensationalist leader, raising circulation from under 4,000 to over 22,000 by 1882. He crushed a unionization attempt but paid the highest wages in the city. Politically, he defeated the "Bourbon Democrat" establishment, backing George Vest for Senate and blocking Tilden’s renomination in 1880. In 1880, Pulitzer made a second run for public office, this time for United States Representative from Missouri's second district. However, he was resoundingly defeated for the Democratic nomination (tantamount to victory in heavily Democratic St. Louis) by Bourbon Thomas Allen, 4,254 to 709. His tenure ended violently in 1882 when managing editor John Cockerill shot and killed Alonzo Slayback, a lawyer who had threatened him. Though Cockerill was not tried, the scandal destroyed Pulitzer’s local standing, prompting his move to New York.

### The *New York World* and National Influence
In 1883, Pulitzer purchased the money-losing *New York World* from Jay Gould for $346,000. Applying his St. Louis formula—sensational headlines, illustrations, crime coverage, and crusades—he grew circulation from 15,000 to 600,000, making it the nation’s largest paper. The *World* pioneered a "brief, breezy and briggity" style and introduced the first Sunday color comics supplement featuring *The Yellow Kid* (1895). Pulitzer used the paper for massive public campaigns, most famously the 1885 crowdfunding drive that raised over $100,000 (equivalent to $30M+ today) for the Statue of Liberty pedestal, publishing every donor’s name. He recruited investigative journalist Nellie Bly in 1887 and built the landmark New York World Building in 1890.

### Congressional Term and Political Rivalries
Elected to the U.S. House in 1884 (NY-9), Pulitzer served just over one year (March 1885 – April 1886), finding the *World* more powerful than Congress. He led the successful push to site the Statue of Liberty in New York. His paper was pivotal in Grover Cleveland’s 1884 victory, winning New York by 0.1%. This sparked a lifelong feud with Theodore Roosevelt, whom Pulitzer labeled a "reform fraud." In New York, Pulitzer battled rival editors: Charles Dana (*Sun*), who called him "Judas Pulitzer"; Whitelaw Reid (*Tribune*); and Leander Richardson, who used antisemitic slurs.

### The Hearst Rivalry and Yellow Journalism
In 1895, William Randolph Hearst bought the *New York Journal*, launching a circulation war that defined "yellow journalism." Both papers escalated sensationalism—sex, crime, graphic horrors—to push daily circulation toward one million copies. This competition drove the industry toward mass-circulation, advertising-dependent models. The rivalry precipitated the 1899 Newsboys' Strike, where child hawkers protested the publishers' refusal to lower wholesale prices after the Spanish-American War ended. The *World* also exposed a $40M payment to the French Panama Canal Company in 1909, leading to dismissed libel indictments against Pulitzer by Theodore Roosevelt and J.P. Morgan.

### Declining Health and Final Years
Pulitzer suffered from blindness, severe depression, and acute noise sensitivity from the 1890s onward. He managed the *World* remotely from his Manhattan mansion, Jekyll Island (GA), and Bar Harbor (ME). In 1904, he hired Frank I. Cobb as editor; after initial fierce clashes over editorial control, they developed a respectful partnership. When Pulitzer's son Ralph took over administrative responsibility in 1907, Pulitzer wrote a carefully worded resignation. Pulitzer died aboard his yacht in Charleston Harbor on October 29, 1911, his last words: "Leise, ganz leise" ("Softly, quite softly").

### Legacy: Journalism Education and the Pulitzer Prizes
Pulitzer first offered Columbia University $2M for a journalism school in 1892; President Seth Low refused. President Nicholas Murray Butler accepted the plan in 1902, but the Columbia School of Journalism opened only in 1912, after Pulitzer’s death. He also urged the founding of the Missouri School of Journalism (1908). His will established the Pulitzer Prizes, first awarded in 1917, recognizing excellence in journalism, literature, history, poetry, music, and drama. The family operated the *Post-Dispatch* and other assets until selling to Lee Enterprises in 2005; their television stations were later sold to Hearst Communications.

## Terms
- **Yellow Journalism**: A style of newspaper reporting emphasizing sensationalism, crime, sex, and graphic horror over factual accuracy, developed by Pulitzer and Hearst in the 1890s to drive mass circulation.
- **Liberal Republicans**: A 1870s reform faction opposing President Grant’s corruption and Reconstruction policies; Pulitzer bolted the GOP to join them, nominating Horace Greeley in 1872.
- **Bourbon Democrats**: Conservative, pro-business Democrats in the post-Reconstruction South and border states; Pulitzer’s primary political rivals in St. Louis and New York.
- **Crowdfunding (early form)**: Pulitzer’s 1885 campaign for the Statue of Liberty pedestal solicited small donations from the general public via the *World*, publishing every contributor’s name regardless of amount.
- **Newsboys' Strike (1899)**: A youth-led labor action against Pulitzer’s *World* and Hearst’s *Journal* protesting the publishers' refusal to reduce the wholesale bundle price after the Spanish-American War.
- **Lincoln Cavalry**: The 1st New York Cavalry Regiment, where Pulitzer served in Company L during the Appomattox Campaign (1864–65).
- **Missouri School of Journalism**: Founded at the University of Missouri in 1908 with Pulitzer’s urging; the world’s first formal journalism school, predating Columbia’s.
- **New York World Building**: Completed in 1890 (designed by George B. Post), housed Pulitzer’s paper; featured a triple-height entrance arch and dome dictated by Pulitzer.
- **Nellie Bly (Elizabeth Cochran Seaman)**: Pioneering investigative journalist recruited by Pulitzer in 1887; famous for undercover asylum exposé and round-the-world trip.
- **Alonzo Slayback Killing (1882)**: *Post-Dispatch* editor John Cockerill shot Slayback dead in the newsroom after Slayback threatened him; the scandal forced Pulitzer’s exit from St. Louis politics.
