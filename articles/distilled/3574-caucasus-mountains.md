# Caucasus Mountains

## Overview
The Caucasus Mountains are a major mountain range at the intersection of Europe and Asia, stretching between the Black and Caspian Seas. They form a significant geographical and cultural boundary, are home to Europe's highest peak, and were a key corridor on the historic Silk Road.

## Timeline
- **Late Triassic to Late Jurassic** — Evolution of the Caucasus begins during the Cimmerian orogeny at the margin of the Tethys Ocean.
- **Miocene** — The Greater Caucasus Mountains are uplifted during the Alpine orogeny.
- **Pliocene** — The Armenian highland is flooded by calc-alkaline basalts and andesites.
- **Pleistocene-Pliocene** — The highest summits, Elbrus and Kazbek, form as volcanoes.
- **Postglacial times** — Mount Elbrus erupts; fumarole activity is later registered near its summit.
- **December 1988** — The devastating Spitak earthquake destroys the Gyumri-Vanadzor region of Armenia.

## Body

### Geology and Formation
The Caucasus Mountains belong to the Alpide belt, a system extending from southeastern Europe into Asia. They formed primarily from a tectonic collision between the northward-moving Arabian plate and the Eurasian plate, which closed the Tethys Sea. This collision folded rocks deposited from the Jurassic to the Miocene, creating the Greater Caucasus. The Lesser Caucasus, largely of volcanic origin, experienced uplift and Cenozoic volcanic activity from the same event. The region remains seismically active, with frequent strong earthquakes.

### Physical Geography and Climate
The range is divided into the Greater Caucasus in the north and the Lesser Caucasus in the south, connected by the Likhi Range. Mount Elbrus (5,642 m) is the highest peak, though its classification as European is debated based on the continental boundary defined by the Greater Caucasus watershed. Climate varies greatly with elevation and location. Temperatures decrease with altitude, and precipitation increases from east to west and is generally higher on the southern slopes of the Greater Caucasus. The western slopes receive heavy snowfall, with some areas recording snow depths up to 7 meters.

### Landscape and Ecology
Landscapes range from subtropical lowlands to glaciers. The northern slopes of the Greater Caucasus feature oak and birch forests, transitioning to alpine meadows around 2,000 meters. The southwestern slopes have Colchian forests. The Lesser Caucasus has deciduous and coniferous forests on its northern and western slopes, while its southern slopes are dominated by grasslands and steppes. A large volcanic zone extends from southern Georgia into Armenia and Azerbaijan.

### History and Human Use
Crossing the Caucasus was a vital part of the northern Silk Route. The range contains several important passes, including the Jvari Pass and the Roki Tunnel. Transhumance, the seasonal herding of livestock to highland pastures, has been practiced by groups like the Tusheti shepherds for over 10,000 years.

## Terms
- **Alpide belt**: A major mountain-building system extending from southeastern Europe into Asia.
- **Cimmerian orogeny**: A mountain-building period from the Late Triassic to Late Jurassic.
- **Alpine orogeny**: A mountain-building period in the Cenozoic that uplifted the Greater Caucasus.
- **Transhumance**: The seasonal movement of livestock between fixed summer and winter pastures.
- **Ultras**: Mountains with a topographic prominence of more than 1,500 meters.
- **Colchian forests**: A type of mixed forest found on the southwestern slopes of the Greater Caucasus.
- **Fumarole**: A vent in the crust near a volcano that emits steam and gases.

## Debates and open questions
- There is a technical disagreement over whether Mount Elbrus, at 5,642 m, is in Europe. The continental boundary is usually defined by the crest of the Greater Caucasus, which would place Elbrus at the junction with Asia.
