# Spanish language

## Overview
Spanish (español), also called Castilian (castellano), is a Romance language of the Indo-European family that evolved from Vulgar Latin on the Iberian Peninsula. Originating in the medieval Kingdom of Castile, it is now the world's second-most spoken native language after Mandarin Chinese (519 million native speakers, 636 million total) and the most widely spoken Romance language. It ranks as the world's fourth-most spoken language overall after English, Mandarin Chinese, and Hindustani (Hindi-Urdu). Spanish is official in 20 countries and one territory in the Americas, Spain, and Equatorial Guinea, and is one of the six official languages of the United Nations, with global presence across Europe, the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Oceania. The country with the largest population of native speakers is Mexico.

## Body

### Origins and Historical Development
Spanish evolved from Vulgar Latin brought to Iberia by Romans after 210 BC. Pre-Roman Paleohispanic languages—including Basque, Iberian, Lusitanian, Celtiberian, and Gullaecian—were displaced. The oldest traces of Spanish appear in 9th-century texts from northern Iberia. According to Ramón Menéndez Pidal, the language developed from local Vulgar Latin sociolects centered in Burgos, later spreading to Toledo where the written standard emerged in the 13th century. The *reajuste de las sibilantes* (15th–16th centuries) transformed sibilant pronunciation, producing the modern ⟨j⟩ [x] and ⟨z⟩/[θ] distinctions.

### Names and Etymology
In Spain, the language is called both *español* and *castellano*, reflecting its origin in the Kingdom of Castile. The 1978 Constitution uses *castellano* for Spain's official language, distinguishing it from other co-official languages. The Royal Spanish Academy (RAE) prefers *español* but treats both terms as synonymous. *Castellano* derives from *Castilla* (castle), while *español* comes from the Occitan *espaignol*, from Vulgar Latin *hispaniolus* (of Hispania).

### Colonial Expansion and Global Spread
Spanish colonialism from the 16th century onward introduced the language to the Americas, the Spanish East Indies, and Equatorial Guinea. The *Gramática de la lengua castellana* (1492) by Elio Antonio de Nebrija was the first grammar of a modern European language. Colonial expansion made Spanish the dominant language of the Americas, where the majority of speakers now live. In the 20th century, Spanish expanded to the U.S. through immigration and to Equatorial Guinea and Western Sahara. Spanish is the third most used language on the Internet by number of users after English and Chinese, and the second most used language by number of websites after English.

### Grammar and Phonology
Spanish is a fusional, pro-drop (null-subject) language with subject–verb–object word order. Verbs have about fifty conjugated forms per verb, with 3 tenses: past, present, future; 2 aspects for past: perfective, imperfective; 4 moods: indicative, subjunctive, conditional, imperative; 3 persons: first, second, third; 2 numbers: singular, plural; 3 verboid forms: infinitive, gerund, and past participle. The language distinguishes formal (*usted*) and informal (*tú*/*vos*) address. Phonologically, Spanish has 5 vowels and 17–19 consonants. Key sound changes from Latin include lenition (vīta > vida), palatalization of geminates (annum > año), and the loss of initial /f/ (harina vs. farina). The *yeísmo* merger unites /ʎ/ and /ʝ/ in most dialects.

### Dialectal Variation
Major dialectal divisions include *distinción* (maintaining /θ/ in central/northern Spain) versus *seseo* (merging /θ/ and /s/ in southern Spain and the Americas). *Voseo* (using *vos* instead of *tú*) is common in Latin America, with regional variations in verb forms. *Ustedes* serves as both formal and informal second-person plural throughout Hispanic America. Mexican Spanish is the most widely spoken national variety.

### Writing System and Orthography
Spanish uses the Latin script with 27 letters including ⟨ñ⟩. The digraphs ⟨ch⟩, ⟨ll⟩, and ⟨rr⟩ were formerly considered single letters but are now treated as letter pairs. Stress typically falls on the penultimate syllable unless marked by an acute accent. Inverted question (¿) and exclamation (¡) marks introduce interrogative and exclamatory sentences.

### Organizations and Standardization
The Royal Spanish Academy (founded 1713) and the Association of Spanish Language Academies (1951, 23 national academies) standardize the language. The Instituto Cervantes (1991) promotes Spanish globally through 88 centers in 45 countries. Spanish is official in numerous international organizations including the UN, EU, and Organization of American States.

### Vocabulary and Influences
About 75% of Spanish vocabulary is Latin-derived, with significant Arabic influence (~8% of words) from the Al-Andalus period. Indigenous American languages (Quechua, Nahuatl) contributed vocabulary during colonization. French loanwords entered in the 18th century, while English influence grew in the 20th century, particularly in technology and computing. Regional vocabulary differences exist between Peninsular and American Spanish.

### Judaeo-Spanish (Ladino)
Ladino preserves medieval Spanish features and was spoken by Sephardi Jews expelled in the 15th century. It retains archaic forms lost in standard Spanish and incorporates Hebrew, French, Greek, and Turkish vocabulary. Ladino is endangered but experiencing minor revival in music. Haketia, a related variety, developed in northern Morocco.

## Terms
- **Vulgar Latin**: The colloquial form of Latin spoken by common people, ancestor of all Romance languages
- **Pro-drop**: A language feature allowing subject pronoun omission when contextually unnecessary
- **Yeísmo**: The merger of /ʎ/ and /ʝ/ phonemes in most Spanish dialects
- **Distinción**: Maintaining the phonemic distinction between /θ/ and /s/ (central/northern Spain)
- **Seseo**: Merging /θ/ and /s/ into a single /s/ sound (southern Spain and Americas)
- **Voseo**: Using *vos* instead of *tú* for informal second-person singular
- **Reajuste de las sibilantes**: 15th–16th century sound change affecting Spanish sibilants
- **Fusional language**: A language where words express multiple grammatical categories through inflection
- **Ladino**: Judaeo-Spanish, a variety preserving medieval Spanish features
- **Null-subject**: See pro-drop

## Debates and Open Questions
The etymology of *español* remains debated beyond the RAE's Occitan derivation theory, with alternatives proposed by scholars like Ramón Menéndez Pidal. The extent of Basque substrate influence on Spanish phonology, particularly the f-to-h mutation, is contested among linguists. The classification of *ch* and *ll* as letters versus digraphs has evolved, with the RAE's 2010 decision to treat them as letter pairs still discussed. Regional mutual intelligibility between Spanish and Portuguese varies, with written forms being more comprehensible than spoken. The future of Ladino faces extinction despite revival efforts, and the impact of English on Spanish vocabulary continues to evolve differently in Spain versus Latin America.
