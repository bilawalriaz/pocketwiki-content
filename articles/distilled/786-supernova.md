# Supernova

## Overview  
A supernova is a catastrophic stellar explosion marking the death of massive stars or white dwarfs in binary systems. These events briefly outshine entire galaxies and are crucial for producing heavy elements, triggering star formation, and generating cosmic rays. Understanding supernovae illuminates stellar evolution, galactic chemical enrichment, and cosmological distance measurement.

## Timeline  
- **4500±1000 BC** — Possible prehistoric record of supernova in Kashmir rock carving
- **AD 1006** — SN 1006, the first widely recorded supernova, observed in AD 1006 in the constellation of Lupus. This event was described by observers in China, Japan, Iraq, Egypt and Europe.
- **AD 1054** — SN 1054, which produced the Crab Nebula, was recorded by Chinese astronomers in AD 1054.
- **1572** — Tycho's Supernova (SN 1572) observed in Cassiopeia
- **1604** — Kepler's Supernova (SN 1604), last Milky Way supernova seen visually
- **1885** — SN 1885A, the first such observation was of SN 1885A in the Andromeda Galaxy.
- **1931** — The word supernova was coined by Walter Baade and Fritz Zwicky, who began using it in astrophysics lectures in 1931. Its first use in a journal article came the following year in a publication by Knut Lundmark, who may have coined it independently.
- **1987** — SN 1987A erupts in Large Magellanic Cloud
- **2016** — SN 2016gkg was detected by amateur astronomer Victor Buso from Rosario, Argentina, on 20 September 2016. It was the first time that the initial "shock breakout" from an optical supernova had been observed.

## Body  

### Mechanisms and Types  
Supernovae arise from two primary mechanisms: **thermal runaway** in white dwarfs and **core collapse** in massive stars. In thermal runaway, a white dwarf in a binary system accretes material until it reaches the Chandrasekhar limit (~1.44 solar masses), triggering runaway carbon fusion that completely disrupts the star (Type Ia). Alternatively, mergers of two white dwarfs can also cause this. Core collapse occurs when massive stars are unable to produce sufficient energy from fusion to counteract the star's own gravity, which must happen once the star begins fusing iron, but may happen during an earlier stage of metal fusion. This collapse rebounds, producing a shock wave that expels the outer layers. Depending on the progenitor's structure and mass loss history, different types emerge: Type II (with hydrogen), Type Ib (no hydrogen), Type Ic (no hydrogen or helium), and various subtypes like II-P (plateau light curve), II-L (linear decline), IIn (narrow emission lines), and IIb (transitional features).

### Classification and Light Curves  
Supernovae are classified by spectral features and light curve shapes. Type I lacks hydrogen lines; Type II shows them. Subtypes reflect specific characteristics: Type Ia showing a strong ionised silicon absorption line and reaching an absolute magnitude of −19.3 (or 5 billion times brighter than the Sun), making them valuable standard candles. Type Ib/c result from stripped massive stars. Light curves are powered by radioactive decay of 56Ni → 56Co → 56Fe, with Type Ia showing rapid decline and Type II displaying plateaus due to hydrogen recombination. Hypernovae, extremely luminous Type Ic events, may involve relativistic jets linked to gamma-ray bursts.

### Observation History and Discovery  
Early records include SN 1006 and SN 1054, noted by Chinese astronomers. The 16th-century supernovae (Tycho's and Kepler's) challenged Aristotelian cosmology. Telescopic discoveries began with SN 1885A in Andromeda. Modern surveys detect thousands annually using automated telescopes and CCDs. The Supernova Early Warning System (SNEWS) uses neutrino detectors to alert astronomers before visible light emerges. High-redshift searches employ light curves as standard candles for cosmology, while low-redshift studies anchor the Hubble diagram.

### Progenitors and Stellar Evolution  
Progenitor types determine supernova outcomes. Type Ia originates from carbon-oxygen white dwarfs in binaries. Core collapse supernovae stem from massive stars (>8 solar masses) that lose outer layers via winds or binary interactions. Red supergiants dominate Type II-P progenitors, though the "red supergiant problem" notes fewer high-mass detections than expected. Type Ib and Ic supernovae are hypothesised to have been produced by core collapse of massive stars that have lost their outer layer of hydrogen and helium, either via strong stellar winds or mass transfer to a companion. These stars are known as Wolf–Rayet stars. Electron-capture supernovae occur in stars in the transitional range (~8 to 10 solar masses) between white dwarf formation and iron core-collapse supernovae, with a degenerate O+Ne+Mg core. Failed supernovae may collapse directly into black holes without visible explosions if the initial core collapse cannot be reversed by the mechanism that produces an explosion.

### Energy Output and Effects  
Electromagnetic radiation constitutes a minor fraction of total energy released; core collapse supernovae emit most energy as neutrinos, with about 1046 joules, approximately 10% of the star's rest mass, converted into a ten-second burst of neutrinos. Type Ia energy comes from nuclear fusion, ejecting the entire mass of the original star at high kinetic energy, with around half a solar mass of that mass being 56Ni generated from silicon burning. Shock waves trigger star formation and distribute heavy elements (oxygen to rubidium) into the interstellar medium. Supernovae contribute significantly to cosmic ray acceleration and may have influenced early Solar System formation. Nearby events pose risks to Earth's biosphere, potentially depleting ozone layers.

### Current Models and Debates  
Despite progress, key questions persist. The dominant Type Ia progenitor mechanism (single-degenerate vs. double-degenerate) remains debated. Core collapse models struggle to fully explain explosion dynamics, particularly neutrino reheating efficiency. Asymmetric kicks imparted to neutron stars suggest complex hydrodynamics. Gravitational wave detection could reveal core processes, though none have been observed yet. The role of binary interactions versus single-star evolution in producing stripped-envelope supernovae is actively studied.

## Terms  
- **Chandrasekhar limit**: Maximum mass (~1.44 solar masses) supporting a white dwarf via electron degeneracy pressure  
- **Standard candle**: Astronomical object with known intrinsic brightness used to measure distances  
- **Light curve**: Graph of a supernova's luminosity over time  
- **Shock breakout**: Initial flash when a supernova's shock wave reaches the stellar surface  
- **Neutrino**: Subatomic particle emitted copiously during core collapse, escaping before visible light  
- **Red supergiant**: Cool, large star phase preceding core collapse for many Type II supernovae  
- **Wolf-Rayet star**: Massive, evolved star with strong stellar winds, stripped of hydrogen/helium envelopes  
- **Hypernova**: Extremely luminous Type Ic supernova associated with gamma-ray bursts  
- **Supernova remnant**: Expanding debris shell formed after a supernova explosion  
- **Metallicity**: Abundance of elements heavier than hydrogen and helium in a star or galaxy  

## Debates and Open Questions  
Several critical uncertainties remain: the precise mechanism powering core collapse supernovae explosions, particularly how stalled shock waves regain energy; whether Type Ia supernovae originate primarily from single-degenerate systems or white dwarf mergers; the true nature of ultra-stripped supernovae and their connection to double neutron star systems; and the origin of superluminous supernovae like ASASSN-15lh, whose extreme luminosity challenges conventional models. Additionally, the "red supergiant problem"—the discrepancy between predicted and observed high-mass progenitors—remains unresolved, as does the detection of Type Ia progenitor systems. Finally, the relationship between Type IIn supernovae and supernova impostors, and the role of binary interactions versus single-star evolution in producing stripped-envelope events, continue to generate active research.
