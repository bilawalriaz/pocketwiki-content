# Metaphysics (Aristotle)

## Overview
Aristotle's *Metaphysics* is a foundational philosophical work that investigates the nature of reality, being, and first principles. It is a compilation of treatises that together define the branch of philosophy later called metaphysics, exploring abstract subjects like substance, causation, and the existence of God. Its influence on Greek, Islamic, and medieval scholastic thought has been immense.

## Timeline
- **c. 350s BC** — Aristotle composes the treatises later compiled as the *Metaphysics*.
- **1st century BC** — Andronicus of Rhodes arranges Aristotle's works, placing these treatises "after the Physics," giving the collection its name.
- **3rd century AD** — Alexander of Aphrodisias writes a commentary on the first five books.
- **12th century** — Maimonides writes the *Guide to the Perplexed*, demonstrating compatibility of Aristotelian science with Biblical revelation.
- **1202–1204** — The Fourth Crusade facilitates the delivery of Greek manuscripts to Western Europe.
- **13th century** — William of Moerbeke translates the work into Latin, forming the basis for commentaries by Albert the Great, Thomas Aquinas, and Duns Scotus.
- **1924** — W. D. Ross publishes a major critical edition of the Greek text.
- **1957** — Werner Jaeger publishes another influential critical edition.

## Body

### Composition and Title
The *Metaphysics* is a compilation of Aristotle's lecture notes on abstract subjects. The title "Metaphysics" (Greek: *ta meta ta physika*, "those after the physics") originated from the arrangement of his works by Andronicus of Rhodes. Scholars debate whether "meta" means the work's subject goes "beyond" physics or simply refers to its physical placement in the collection. Aristotle himself called these writings "first philosophy."

### Core Content: Books I–VI
The work begins by defining wisdom (*sophia*) as knowledge of the first principles or causes of things. It surveys previous philosophers' theories on these causes, from the Ionian school to Plato. Key books establish foundational logic: Book IV defends the principle of contradiction and the principle of excluded middle. Book V provides definitions of key terms like "cause" and "nature." Book VI establishes a hierarchy of sciences, arguing that "first philosophy" (the study of being qua being) is superior because it concerns the ultimate causes of all reality.

### The Central Doctrine of Substance: Books VII–IX
These books form the core, focusing on "substance" (*ousia*), the primary kind of being. Aristotle considers four candidates for substance: essence, the universal, genus, and material substrate. He dismisses matter as substance, arguing that ultimate matter has no properties. He concludes that a thing's essence—what it is by its very nature, included in its definition—is the criterion of substantiality. Book IX defines potentiality (a principle of change) and actuality (a completed state), relating them to form and matter over time.

### Later Books and Legacy
Books X–XIV discuss unity, provide briefer versions of other sections, and address the philosophy of mathematics. Book XII famously describes the "unmoved mover" as "the thinking of thinking." The work's immense legacy includes commentaries by Alexander of Aphrodisias, Syrianus, and Asclepius. Its complexity is noted by Avicenna, who read it forty times before understanding it with al-Farabi's help. It profoundly influenced Islamic philosophers, Maimonides, and medieval scholastics like Aquinas.

### Textual Criticism
The work's varied topics and order led 19th-century critics to see it as a haphazard collection. 20th-century editors W. D. Ross and Werner Jaeger produced standard editions. Ross concluded that most books form a continuous work, with others inserted later. Jaeger argued the books derive from different periods of Aristotle's life. Editing remains an active field, with new critical editions proposing different arrangements and textual histories.

## Terms
- **Metaphysics**: The branch of philosophy Aristotle calls "first philosophy," studying being qua being and the ultimate causes of reality.
- **Substance (*ousia*)**: The primary kind of being; what exists independently and is the subject of properties. Aristotle identifies it with essence.
- **Essence**: The "what it is to be" of a thing; its defining nature, included in a *secundum se* (according to itself) account.
- **Potentiality**: A principle of change in a thing, either in itself or in another; the capacity to become something else.
- **Actuality**: The completed state of something that had the potential to be completed; the fulfillment of potential.
- **Unmoved Mover**: The ultimate cause of motion in the cosmos, described as "the thinking of thinking," which is eternal and divine.
- **Principle of Contradiction**: The logical principle that it is not possible for the same proposition to be both true and false at the same time and in the same respect.
- **First Principles (*arche*)**: The fundamental, primary causes or starting points of knowledge and being.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Interpretation of "Meta"**: Does the title imply the work's subject is "beyond" physics (metatheoretical) or merely refers to its physical placement in the canonical order of Aristotle's writings?
- **Composition and Unity**: Is the *Metaphysics* a unified work or a "farrago" (hotch-potch) of separate treatises? Scholars debate the original order and which books belong to a continuous core.
- **Dating**: It is notoriously difficult to specify when Aristotle wrote the individual treatises, as their arrangement is due to later editors.
- **Textual Criticism**: The stemma codicum (manuscript family tree) of the *Metaphysics* is contested, leading to different critical editions and proposed rearrangements of the books.
