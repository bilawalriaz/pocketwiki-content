# Season

## Overview  
A season is a division of the year marked by changes in weather, ecology, and daylight hours. On Earth, seasons arise primarily from the planet’s axial tilt of approximately 23.4 degrees relative to its orbital plane, combined with axial parallelism—the maintenance of the tilt direction toward Polaris throughout the year. These factors cause differential solar exposure between hemispheres, driving climatic and ecological cycles that various cultures define and name differently.

## Timeline  
- **c. 2000 BCE** — Ancient Egyptians previously defined seasons by the annual flooding of the Nile: flood, growth, low water
- **46 BCE** — Julius Caesar reforms calendar; Varro writes nine years later, assigning Julian Calendar dates (February 7, May 9, August 11, November 10) to the start of spring, summer, autumn, and winter
- **1780** — Societas Meteorologica Palatina defines meteorological seasons by whole months
- **AD 325** — Calendar equinox of 21 March is the same date as in the Easter tables current at the time of the Council of Nicaea; the Gregorian calendar is designed to keep the March equinox no later than 21 March
- **Present** — Astronomical seasons begin at solstices/equinoxes; meteorological at month starts

## Body  

### Axial Parallelism and Tilt  
Earth’s axis maintains a nearly constant orientation toward Polaris due to axial parallelism, varying only slightly over a 26,000-year cycle (axial precession). The 23.4-degree tilt (obliquity of the ecliptic) causes one hemisphere to receive more direct sunlight at any given time, producing opposite seasons. Solstices mark peak insolation—around June 21 in the Northern Hemisphere and December 21 in the Southern—while equinoxes occur when the Sun is directly overhead at the Equator, equalizing day and night globally.

### Orbital Eccentricity  
Earth’s elliptical orbit causes it to reach perihelion anywhere from 1 January to 5 January and aphelion anywhere from 3 July to 6 July, resulting in a 7% variation in solar energy received. However, this effect is minor compared to axial tilt and even slightly counteracts Northern Hemisphere seasonal trends. Landmass distribution amplifies warming in the Northern Hemisphere despite its winter occurring closer to the Sun.

### Regional and Cultural Variations  
Temperate regions typically recognize four seasons—spring, summer, autumn, winter—though ecologists often use a six-season model (prevernal, vernal, estival, serotinal, autumnal, hibernal) based on biological activity rather than calendar dates. Tropical areas commonly distinguish wet and dry seasons, driven by the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ICZ) migration. Indigenous groups like the Noongar of Australia recognise six seasons (maar-keyen bonar) tied to local flora, fauna, and weather patterns, while the Sami people of Scandinavia identify up to eight seasons.

### Calendar-Based Systems  
Meteorological seasons divide the year into three-month blocks aligned with temperature cycles: March–May (spring), June–August (summer), September–November (autumn), December–February (winter) in the Northern Hemisphere. The India Meteorological Department designates four climatological seasons: Winter (December–February, averaging 10–15°C in the northwest), Summer/pre-monsoon (March–May, 32–40°C in the interior), Monsoon/rainy (June–September, dominated by the southwest monsoon), and Post-monsoon/autumn (October–November). Astronomical seasons begin precisely at solstices and equinoxes, varying slightly each year due to orbital mechanics. Solar-based systems, such as the Chinese 24 solar terms, position solstices and equinoxes as seasonal midpoints, with the four seasons chūn (spring), xià (summer), qiū (autumn), and dōng (winter) beginning on Lichun (~4 February), Lixia (~6 May), Liqiu (~8 August), and Lidong (~8 November).

### Ecological and Thermal Lag Effects  
Seasonal lag delays temperature peaks by up to seven weeks after maximum insolation, influencing how cultures define season onset. Continental climates experience greater lag than maritime ones. In polar regions, extreme latitudes produce polar day and polar night, with twilight phases extending seasonal transitions. The astronomical seasons are not of equal length due to Earth’s elliptical orbit: 92.75 days from the March equinox to the June solstice, 93.65 days to the September equinox, 89.85 days to the December solstice, and 88.99 days back to the March equinox, making the March-to-September period 7.56 days longer than September-to-March.

### Multi-Season Reckoning Systems  
Some traditional calendars count a fifth season. In Europe, this relates to the English concept of Midsummer, High Summer, or the Dog days, and to Spanish distinctions between primavera, estío, and verano. In the Far East, the five-season cycle connects to Wuxing (Five Phases) cosmology, including the Chinese expression for 'late summer', chángxià (長夏), and the plum rain seasons méiyǔ (Mandarin), jangma (Korean), and tsuyu (Japanese). In South Asia, the Hindu calendar recognises six Ritu: Vasanta (spring), Grishma (summer), Varsha (monsoon), Sharada (autumn), Hemanta (early winter), and Shishira (prevernal/late winter), each spanning two months. The Bengali and Odia calendars follow similar patterns with differing start and end times, as does the Tamil calendar.

## Terms  
- **Axial Parallelism**: The Earth’s axis maintaining its orientation toward Polaris during orbit.  
- **Obliquity of the Ecliptic**: The 23.4-degree tilt of Earth’s axis relative to its orbital plane.  
- **Solstice**: The point of maximum solar insolation in a hemisphere (summer) or minimum (winter).  
- **Equinox**: When the Sun is directly overhead at the Equator, causing equal day and night worldwide.  
- **Seasonal Lag**: Delay between peak insolation and peak temperature due to thermal inertia of land and sea.  
- **Perihelion/Aphelion**: Points in Earth’s orbit closest to (January) and farthest from (July) the Sun.  
- **Intertropical Convergence Zone (ICZ)**: A low-pressure belt near the equator whose movement drives tropical rainfall patterns.  
- **Meteorological Seasons**: Calendar-based divisions aligned with temperature cycles, starting on the first day of March, June, September, and December.  
- **Cross-Quarter Days**: Midpoints between solstices and equinoxes, used in some traditional calendars.  
- **Thermal Seasons**: Temperature-defined seasons used in Sweden and Finland, beginning when mean daily temperatures cross thresholds (e.g., 0°C or 10°C).  

## Debates and Open Questions  
Scholars debate whether axial tilt alone sufficiently explains all seasonal variations, particularly in tropical zones where precipitation shifts dominate. The role of orbital eccentricity remains contested, with some arguing it contributes more significantly under different planetary configurations. Additionally, the alignment of calendar-based seasons with ecological realities raises questions about the adequacy of fixed-date systems in capturing true environmental transitions, especially under climate change scenarios.
