# Missouri River

## Overview
The Missouri River is the longest river in North America, stretching 2,341 miles (3,767 km) from the Rocky Mountains to the Mississippi River. It drains a vast, semi-arid watershed of over 500,000 square miles across ten U.S. states and two Canadian provinces. For over 12,000 years, it has been a vital corridor for human sustenance, transportation, and conflict, shaping the development of the American West. Its modern course is heavily engineered for navigation, irrigation, and flood control, which has significantly altered its natural ecology.

## Timeline
- **c. 10,000–12,000 years ago** — First human inhabitants arrive in the watershed at the end of the Pleistocene.
- **May 1673** — French explorers Louis Jolliet and Jacques Marquette become the first documented Europeans to see the Missouri River.
- **1714** — Étienne de Bourgmont leads the first formal European expedition up the Missouri, reaching at least the Platte River.
- **1803** — The United States acquires the Missouri River basin through the Louisiana Purchase.
- **1804–1806** — The Lewis and Clark Expedition travels the entire length of the Missouri to the Pacific Ocean.
- **1807** — Manuel Lisa's expedition sparks the explosive growth of the fur trade in the upper Missouri region.
- **1830s–1860s** — Over 500,000 pioneers depart from Missouri river towns for the American West via trails like the Oregon and California.
- **1844, 1881, 1926–27** — Catastrophic floods strike the Missouri basin, driving later flood-control efforts.
- **1940** — Fort Peck Dam, the first major dam on the Missouri's main stem, is completed.
- **1944** — The Flood Control Act authorizes the Pick-Sloan Plan for comprehensive river development.
- **1950s** — Construction begins on the five mainstem dams of the Pick-Sloan Plan (Garrison, Oahe, Big Bend, Fort Randall, Gavins Point).
- **1993, 2011** — Historic floods cause major damage and disrupt river navigation.

## Body

### Course and Watershed
The Missouri River officially begins at the confluence of the Jefferson and Madison Rivers in Montana. It flows east and south, passing through the Great Plains and major reservoirs like Fort Peck Lake, Lake Sakakawea, and Lake Oahe. Its largest tributary by volume is the Yellowstone River. The river's drainage basin is comparable in size to the province of Quebec and accounts for 45% of the Mississippi River's flow at St. Louis. The basin's climate is continental, with most precipitation occurring in summer.

### Geology and Formation
The river's path was shaped by the Laramide Orogeny (70–45 million years ago), which raised the Rockies and created the framework for the modern drainage system. During the Quaternary Ice Age, glaciation diverted the Missouri southeastward, integrating it into a single system. The river was historically nicknamed the "Big Muddy" for its enormous sediment load, which has been drastically reduced from 175–320 million short tons per year pre-development to 20–25 million short tons today due to dams.

### First Peoples and Early Exploration
Archaeological evidence suggests human presence in the watershed for 12,000 years. Major tribes along the river included the Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara, and Sioux. The introduction of the horse dramatically changed their nomadic lifestyle. French explorers first encountered the river in 1673, and it passed through French and Spanish control before the U.S. acquired it in 1803. The Lewis and Clark Expedition (1804–1806) provided the first comprehensive maps and ecological reports of the region.

### American Frontier and Conflict
The river was the primary route for 19th-century westward expansion. The fur trade, peaking in the 1830s, blazed trails later used by pioneers. Settlers moving into the Great Plains led to violent conflicts with Native Americans, including Red Cloud's War and the Great Sioux War of 1876–77, which resulted in the tribes being forced onto reservations.

### Dam-Building and Modern Development
In the 20th century, the river was extensively engineered. The Pick-Sloan Plan led to the construction of fifteen mainstem dams, creating the largest reservoir system in the U.S. These dams provide flood control, hydroelectric power, and irrigation but flooded vast areas of Native American reservation land. The river channel was also straightened and stabilized for navigation, reducing its length by nearly 200 miles.

### Navigation and Ecology
Steamboat navigation peaked in 1858 but was replaced by railroads. A modern barge channel exists from Sioux City to St. Louis, though traffic has declined due to drought and competition. Human development has severely degraded the river's ecology, destroying floodplain habitat and polluting waterways. Restoration efforts are now underway to remove some engineering structures and restore natural processes.

## Terms
- **Watershed**: The area of land where all water drains into a common outlet, such as a river.
- **Tributary**: A river or stream that flows into a larger river.
- **Mainstem**: The primary, continuous channel of a river.
- **Orogeny**: A mountain-building process, typically through tectonic uplift.
- **Channelization**: The straightening and deepening of a river's natural course to improve navigation or control flooding.
- **Riparian zone**: The interface between land and a river or stream.
- **Pick-Sloan Plan**: A 1944 U.S. federal plan for the comprehensive development of the Missouri River basin for flood control, irrigation, and power.
- **Sediment load**: The amount of solid material (silt, sand) carried by a river.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The construction of mainstem dams under the Pick-Sloan Plan flooded 150,000 acres of the Fort Berthold Indian Reservation, leading to a legal battle over treaty rights and compensation.
- Modern ecosystem restoration projects, which involve removing levees and dikes, raise concerns about re-mobilizing trapped sediments and exacerbating nutrient pollution downstream in the Gulf of Mexico.
- The future of navigation on the lower Missouri is uncertain, as the channel is underutilized and expensive to maintain, while drought and floods continue to disrupt traffic.
