# Sarmatians

## Overview
The Sarmatians were a large confederation of ancient Iranian equestrian nomads who dominated the Pontic steppe from the 5th century BC to the 4th century AD. Originating in the central Eurasian Steppe, they migrated westward, absorbed the related Scythians, and became a major power interacting with and influencing the Roman Empire, Germanic tribes, and the peoples of the Caucasus. Their legacy includes archaeological cultures, genetic contributions to later populations, and a mythologized descent claimed by Polish nobility.

## Timeline
- **c. 6th–4th centuries BC** — Ethnogenesis occurs as Central Asian nomads migrate into and conquer the Sauromatians in the southern Urals.
- **4th–3rd centuries BC** — Sarmatians begin migrating westward, taking advantage of Scythian decline.
- **c. 200 BC** — Sarmatians dominate the closely related Scythians in the Pontic Steppe.
- **1st century BC** — Major tribes (Iazyges, Roxolani, Alans) migrate further west, reaching the Danube and Pannonian Basin.
- **1st century AD** — Sarmatians begin encroaching upon the Roman Empire, often in alliance with Germanic tribes.
- **2nd century AD** — Alans create a powerful confederation in the North Caucasus and Pontic Steppe.
- **c. 200 AD** — Germanic Goths break Sarmatian dominance of the Pontic Steppe.
- **375 AD** — Huns conquer eastern Alans, massacring many and absorbing others into their polity.
- **4th century AD** — Hunnic invasions cause many Sarmatians to join Germanic tribes (Goths, Vandals) and settle in the Western Roman Empire.

## Body

### Origin and Migration
The Sarmatians emerged between the 6th and 4th centuries BC from migrations of Central Asian nomads into the territory of the earlier Sauromatians. This conquest increased eastern Asiatic physical features in the new population. Archaeologically, the transition is marked by the Filippovka kurgans (5th-4th centuries BC). Pressured by eastern groups like the Sakā and Dahā, and exploiting the decline of Scythian power after defeats by Macedonian kings, the Sarmatians began a major westward migration across the Don River into Scythia.

### Dominance in the Pontic Steppe and Europe
By the 2nd century BC, tribes like the Royal Sarmatians, Iazyges, and Roxolani had moved into the Pontic Steppe, absorbing the Scythians rather than displacing them. Their power expanded through the 1st centuries BC and AD, with tribes reaching the Danube and Pannonian Basin. They engaged in complex diplomacy and warfare with the Pontic Kingdom and the Roman Empire, sometimes as allies, sometimes as adversaries. The Alans, originating from a merger of tribes in Central Asia, became a dominant force by the 2nd century AD, creating a vast trade network.

### Decline and Assimilation
Sarmatian hegemony declined in the 2nd and 3rd centuries AD due to Hunnic and Gothic invasions. The Goths broke their power on the Pontic Steppe around 200 AD. The Hunnic conquest of 375 AD shattered the eastern Alans. Survivors assimilated in various ways: some joined Germanic tribes like the Vandals in migrating west; others were absorbed by early Slavs; and the Alans in the Caucasus contributed to the ethnogenesis of modern Ossetians. Those in the Bosporan Kingdom assimilated into Greek civilization.

### Culture and Archaeology
The Sarmatians spoke a heterogeneous Iranian language. Archaeologically, their culture is defined by four phases (Sauromatian to Late Sarmatian), characterized by kurgan burials, metalwork with Chinese and Persian influences, and specific weaponry like the long lance (*contus*). Their art styles influenced medieval Europe. Archaeological finds, such as graves of armed women, may have inspired Greek Amazon legends.

### Genetics and Physical Appearance
Genomic studies show Sarmatians were genetically similar to the Bronze Age Yamnaya culture, with some East Asian and ancient Iranian admixture. They contributed ancestry to later groups like the Huns and Hungarian conquerors. Physical descriptions from Roman authors note light hair among some tribes like the Alans, though modern historians debate the prevalence and significance of this trait.

## Terms
*   **Pontic Steppe:** The grassland region north of the Black Sea, a core territory of the Sarmatians.
*   **Kurgan:** An earthen mound covering a burial chamber, a characteristic Sarmatian grave type.
*   **Iranian language:** A branch of the Indo-European language family; Sarmatian languages are ancestral to modern Ossetian.
*   **Scythians:** A related group of Iranian nomads who dominated the Pontic Steppe before the Sarmatian rise.
*   **Alans:** A major Sarmatian tribe that formed a powerful confederation and survived into the Middle Ages.
*   **Iazyges:** A Sarmatian tribe that migrated to the Pannonian Basin and interacted with Rome.
*   **Roxolani:** A western Sarmatian tribe that raided Roman territories from the Danube region.
*   **Sauromatians:** The earlier culture in the southern Urals that was conquered and absorbed during Sarmatian ethnogenesis.
*   **Prokhorovka culture:** An archaeological term for the Early Sarmatian phase (4th–2nd centuries BC).

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The precise relationship and distinction between the archaeological "Sauromatian" and "Sarmatian" cultures, despite the similar ethnonyms.
*   The extent to which descriptions of Alans as tall and blond represent a general trait versus a selective or literary trope.
*   The degree of genetic and cultural continuity between the Sarmatians and later steppe peoples like the Huns and Alans.
