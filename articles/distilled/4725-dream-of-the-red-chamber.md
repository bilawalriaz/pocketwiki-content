# Dream of the Red Chamber

## Overview
*Dream of the Red Chamber* (also *The Story of the Stone*) is an 18th-century Chinese novel by Cao Xueqin, considered one of the Four Great Classic Novels. It is a detailed, psychological epic depicting the decline of the aristocratic Jia family, exploring themes of love, illusion, and the transience of worldly life. Its complex textual history and profound influence on Chinese culture and literature make it a central subject of the scholarly field known as Redology.

## Timeline
- **c. 1740s** — Cao Xueqin begins composing the novel.
- **1763/1764** — Cao Xueqin dies, leaving an unfinished manuscript of about 80 chapters.
- **Pre-1791** — Hand-copied manuscripts (the "rouge versions") of the first 80 chapters circulate.
- **1791** — Gao E and Cheng Weiyuan publish the first printed, 120-chapter edition, titled *Dream of the Red Chamber*.
- **1792** — A second, corrected edition by Cheng and Gao is published.
- **1921** — Hu Shih argues the last 40 chapters were written by Gao E, not Cao Xueqin.
- **1953** — Zhou Ruchang publishes a major study, boosting the novel's modern scholarly profile.
- **1973-1980** — David Hawkes and John Minford publish the acclaimed English translation, *The Story of the Stone*.
- **2014** — Researchers use data analysis to argue the first 80 and last 40 chapters have different authors.
- **2020** — Zhang Qingshan states it is unlikely Gao E wrote the last 40 chapters.

## Body

### Textual History and Authorship
Cao Xueqin began the novel in the 1740s, working until his death in 1763 or 1764. The first 80 chapters circulated in hand-copied "rouge versions," which contain annotations from commentators like Zhiyanzhai, offering clues to the original plot. In 1791, Gao E and Cheng Weiyuan published a 120-chapter printed edition, adding 40 chapters to complete the story. The authorship of these final chapters is the central debate in Redology. While Cheng and Gao claimed to have used Cao's drafts, many modern scholars, beginning with Hu Shih in 1921, believe they were written by Gao E. The discovery of a pre-1791 120-chapter manuscript complicates this, but stylistic analysis and the novel's internal foreshadowing continue to fuel the controversy.

### Plot and Characters
The novel follows the Jia family, whose wealth and prestige are symbolized by the construction of the Daguanyuan (Grand View Garden) for a daughter who becomes an Imperial Consort. The protagonist is Jia Baoyu, a sensitive young heir born with a jade, who rejects Confucian studies. The central story is the romantic rivalry between his true love, the melancholic poet Lin Daiyu, and his predestined bride, the pragmatic Xue Baochai. The narrative details the family's daily life and eventual fall from grace, featuring nearly 40 major and over 400 minor characters, with complex, realistic portraits of women.

### Themes and Interpretation
The novel explores the Buddhist and Taoist themes of illusion versus reality, and the transience of material life, symbolized by the family name Jia (a homophone for "false"). It is a profound memorial to the women of Cao's youth, emphasizing their spiritual qualities and talents, particularly in poetry. Feminist interpretations highlight its challenge to patriarchal norms through characters like the capable Wang Xifeng and Baoyu's own "feminine" traits. The work is also a rich depiction of Qing dynasty material culture, from medicine and cuisine to opera and architecture.

### Reception and Translation
Initially criticized by reformers like Liang Qichao, the novel was elevated by scholars such as Wang Guowei, who called it "the tragedy of tragedies." The New Culture Movement, led by Hu Shih, established Cao Xueqin's authorship and used the novel's Beijing dialect as a foundation for modern Mandarin. It has been translated into many languages, with major English versions by Yang Hsien-yi and Gladys Yang (*A Dream of Red Mansions*) and David Hawkes and John Minford (*The Story of the Stone*). The novel has inspired countless adaptations in film, television, opera, and other media.

## Terms
- **Redology (紅學)**: The field of academic study devoted to *Dream of the Red Chamber*.
- **Rouge versions (脂本)**: The early, hand-copied manuscripts of the first 80 chapters, named for the red and black ink annotations.
- **Zhiyanzhai (脂齋)**: The most prominent commentator on the rouge manuscripts, believed to have been close to Cao Xueqin.
- **Cheng-Gao version**: The 120-chapter printed edition published by Cheng Weiyuan and Gao E in 1791-1792.
- **Tanyi xue (探佚學)**: A branch of Redology focused on reconstructing the novel's lost original ending.
- **Daguanyuan (大觀園)**: The Grand View Garden, built for the Imperial Consort and the primary setting for the young characters' lives.
- **Baihua (白話)**: Written vernacular Chinese, the language style of the novel, as opposed to Classical Chinese.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authorship of the last 40 chapters**: The primary debate is whether Cao Xueqin wrote them and Gao E merely edited them, or if Gao E (or another author) composed them entirely. Evidence from foreshadowing, stylistic analysis, and the discovery of early manuscripts is used on all sides.
- **Cao Xueqin's original ending**: Scholars use annotations in the rouge versions and internal clues in the first 80 chapters to speculate on the fates of characters and the family's decline as Cao originally intended.
- **Interpretive frameworks**: The novel has been interpreted through Marxist critiques of feudal society, feminist analyses of gender roles, and philosophical readings of its Buddhist and Taoist themes, with ongoing scholarly discussion about which lens best captures its complexity.
