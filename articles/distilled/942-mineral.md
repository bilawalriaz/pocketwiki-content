# Mineral

## Overview

A mineral is a naturally occurring, solid substance with a well-defined chemical composition and an ordered atomic (crystalline) structure. Minerals are the fundamental building blocks of rocks and are essential to geology, economic resource extraction, and the study of Earth's history. The International Mineralogical Association (IMA) is the authoritative body defining and naming mineral species, recognizing 6,145 species as of May 2025. The concept is distinct from rocks (aggregates of minerals) and mineraloids (substances lacking a crystalline structure, like opal).

## Body

### Definitions and Standards

The IMA defines a mineral as a naturally occurring, solid substance with a well-defined chemical composition and an ordered atomic arrangement. This excludes anthropogenic or biogenic substances unless geological processes were involved in their formation. The substance must be solid in its natural occurrence (with native mercury as a historical exception) and possess a well-defined crystallographic structure. Hypothetical substances are excluded. The chemical composition may vary within limits, allowing for solid solutions (e.g., mackinawite, (Fe,Ni)₉S₈) and mineral groups (e.g., olivine group, (Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄).

### Biogenic Minerals

The IMA's exclusion of biogenic crystalline substances is contentious. Skinner (2005) argues for including biominerals—those formed by metabolic activities of organisms—within the mineral kingdom, defining them as "element or compound, amorphous or crystalline, formed through biogeochemical processes." Over 60 biominerals were discovered and named prior to IMA listing. These are not on the official IMA list but are recognized in the Dana classification. The IMA's "Working Group on Environmental Mineralogy and Geochemistry" studies minerals in the hydrosphere, atmosphere, and biosphere, including mineral-forming microorganisms found globally.

### Rocks, Ores, and Gems

A rock is an aggregate of one or more minerals or mineraloids. Rocks may be defined by relative abundances of key minerals (e.g., granite by quartz, alkali feldspar, plagioclase) or composed primarily of one mineral (e.g., limestone from calcite). Rock-forming minerals (e.g., quartz, feldspars, micas, amphiboles, pyroxenes, olivines, calcite) are the most abundant, with ~150 minerals being particularly important. Ores are minerals with high concentrations of valuable elements (e.g., cinnabar for mercury, sphalerite for zinc). Gems are minerals valued for beauty, durability, and rarity (~20 species qualify). Industrial minerals (e.g., muscovite) have commercial uses beyond gems, ores, or fuels.

### Chemistry and Classification

Mineral abundance and diversity are controlled by chemistry, dependent on elemental abundances in Earth's crust. Eight elements (oxygen, silicon, aluminium, iron, magnesium, calcium, sodium, potassium) make up over 98% of the crust. Minerals form based on stability at formation conditions. Chemical substitution occurs between ions of similar size and charge (e.g., Si⁴⁺ by Al³⁺). Coordination polyhedra describe cation-anion arrangements; the silica tetrahedron [SiO₄]⁴⁻ is the base unit of silicates. Changes in temperature, pressure, and composition alter mineralogy via processes like weathering, metasomatism, and the rock cycle.

### Physical Properties

Minerals are identified by physical properties: crystal structure/habit, hardness (Mohs scale), lustre, diaphaneity (transparency), colour, streak, cleavage, fracture, tenacity, and specific gravity. Crystal structure results from orderly atomic arrangement, with minerals restricted to 32 point groups across 6 crystal families. Twinning (intergrowth of crystals) and crystal habit (aggregate shape) are diagnostic. Hardness varies with crystallographic direction (e.g., kyanite). Lustre is metallic or non-metallic. Colour is often non-diagnostic due to trace elements (allochromatic) vs. essential elements (idiochromatic). Streak (powder colour) is more reliable for metallic minerals. Cleavage occurs along structural weakness planes; parting results from defects. Fracture describes breaks not along cleavage planes. Specific gravity correlates with atomic mass and lustre.

### Modern Classification

Minerals are classified by variety, species, series, and group. The two dominant systems are the Dana (four-part number based on composition and structure) and Strunz (combining chemical and structural criteria) classifications. Silicates comprise ~90% of the crust, based on [SiO₄]⁴⁻ tetrahedra polymerized into chains, sheets, and frameworks. Non-silicates (8% of crust) include native elements, sulfides, oxides, halides, carbonates, sulfates, phosphates, and organic compounds. Silicate subclasses are ordered by polymerization: tectosilicates (frameworks, e.g., quartz, feldspars), phyllosilicates (sheets, e.g., micas, clays), inosilicates (chains, e.g., pyroxenes, amphiboles), cyclosilicates (rings, e.g., tourmaline, beryl), sorosilicates (double tetrahedra, e.g., epidote), and orthosilicates (isolated tetrahedra, e.g., olivine, garnet).

## Terms

- **Mineral species**: A naturally occurring, solid substance with a well-defined chemical composition and ordered atomic structure.
- **Mineraloid**: A natural solid without a definite crystalline structure (e.g., opal, obsidian).
- **Polymorph**: Minerals with the same chemical formula but different crystal structures (e.g., quartz and stishovite, both SiO₂).
- **Solid solution**: A mineral with variable proportions of chemical elements occupying equivalent structural positions (e.g., (Fe,Ni)₉S₈).
- **Cleavage**: The tendency of a mineral to break along planes of structural weakness.
- **Streak**: The colour of a mineral in powdered form.
- **Mohs hardness scale**: An ordinal scale (1–10) measuring resistance to scratching.
- **Coordination polyhedron**: A geometric representation of how a cation is surrounded by anions (usually oxygen).
- **Biogenic mineral**: A mineral formed by biological processes (excluded from IMA list but included in some definitions).
- **Rock-forming mineral**: Abundant minerals that constitute the majority of rocks (e.g., quartz, feldspars, micas).

## Debates and Open Questions

The primary debate centers on the IMA's exclusion of biogenic minerals. Skinner (2005) argues for their inclusion, while the IMA maintains the exclusion unless geological processes are involved. Proposals to classify amorphous substances as minerals have not been accepted. The IMA is also hesitant to accept naturally occurring nanoparticles as minerals, though no minimum crystal size is defined. The definition of stability (room temperature vs. formation conditions) remains debated, with the IMA requiring only that structure and composition be well-determined (e.g., meridianiite, stable below 2°C). The classification of quasicrystals (e.g., icosahedrite) as minerals represents a recent expansion of the concept of "ordered atomic arrangement" beyond periodic crystals.
