# Michel de Montaigne

## Overview
Michel de Montaigne (1533–1592) was a French Renaissance philosopher and statesman who invented the essay as a literary genre, using it to explore the self with unprecedented frankness. His *Essais* merge autobiography, anecdote, and intellectual inquiry to argue that human nature is defined by variety and volatility, and that true certainty is unattainable—a position crystallized in his Pyrrhonian motto, "What do I know?" (*Que sais-je?*). Though admired in his lifetime primarily as a magistrate and mediator during the French Wars of Religion, his retrospective influence is vast: he pioneered a sceptical, anti-dogmatic mode of thought that shaped writers from Francis Bacon and Shakespeare to Descartes, Pascal, Emerson, and Nietzsche, and anticipated modern educational psychology by advocating experience and critical dialogue over rote memorization.

## Timeline

- **1568** — Publishes translation of Raymond Sebond’s *Theologia naturalis* at father’s request.
- **1570–1571** — Inherits estate; severely injured in riding accident; resigns magistracy; retires to château tower ("citadel") to write.
- **1580** — Publishes first two books of *Essais*; travels through France, Germany, Switzerland, Italy seeking cure for kidney stones.
- **1581** — Elected Mayor of Bordeaux (re-elected 1583); moderates between Catholics and Protestants during plague (1585).
- **1588** — Publishes expanded three-book edition of *Essais*; meets Marie de Gournay, later editor and "adopted daughter."
- **1589** — Supports Henry of Navarre (Henry IV) as *politique* prioritizing peace over religious allegiance.
- **13 Sep 1592** — Dies at Château de Montaigne, aged 59, from peritonsillar abscess causing tongue paralysis.
- **1595** — Posthumous "Bordeaux Copy" edition published with marginalia.
- **1774** — Travel journal discovered and published.
- **2019–2020** — Possible remains discovered at Musée d'Aquitaine; DNA analysis suggests high probability of identification.

## Body

### Family, Education, and the Making of a Free Mind
Montaigne’s upbringing was a deliberate pedagogical experiment designed by his father, Pierre Eyquem, to cultivate "liberty and delight." Born into a family ennobled by a herring-merchant fortune (1477) and marked by *Marrano* (Sephardic Jewish forced-convert) ancestry on both sides, Montaigne spent his first three years with a peasant family to "draw the boy close to the people." Returned to the château, he was immersed in Latin as a first language: a German tutor (Horstanus) and Latin-speaking servants created a total immersion environment, while Greek was taught through games and meditation rather than textbooks. This regime, devoid of severity, instilled a voluntary embrace of duty. At the College of Guienne (c. 1539–1546), under the famed Latinist George Buchanan, he completed the curriculum by age 13. The subsequent decade (1546–1557) is obscure, though he studied law and entered the local legal system.

### Public Career and the Friendship of a Lifetime
Montaigne served as councillor at the Court des Aides of Périgueux (1557) and the Parlement of Bordeaux (1557–1570), attending the court of Charles IX and the siege of Rouen (1562), receiving the Order of Saint Michael. At the Parlement he met Étienne de La Boétie (three years his senior), a humanist poet and author of the radical *Discourse on Voluntary Servitude*. Their friendship became the emotional and intellectual axis of Montaigne’s life: "Our souls mingled and blended... so completely that they effaced the seam that had joined them." Montaigne called it a bond occurring "once every three centuries." La Boétie’s death in 1563 (likely plague or tuberculosis) devastated Montaigne, who described the agony in a 1571 *Discourse*. Scholar Donald M. Frame argues this loss created an "imperious need to communicate" that birthed the *Essais*, with the reader replacing the dead friend.

### Retirement, the "Citadel," and the Birth of the *Essais*
In 1570, Montaigne inherited the château and became Lord of Montaigne. A near-fatal riding accident (a companion collided with him at full speed) prompted weeks of recovery and deep reflection on mortality. By 1571, at 38, he resigned his magistracy, retreated to the tower library (1,500 volumes), and inscribed a dedication to "freedom, tranquility, and leisure." There he wrote the *Essais* (first edition 1580, expanded 1588), a genre-defining collection of subjective, digressive explorations. The title (*essais* = "attempts" or "trials") signals their provisional nature. The longest chapter, "Apology for Raymond Sebond," adopts Pyrrhonism (radical scepticism suspending judgment) and coins his motto *Que sais-je?*

### Travels, Illness, and Vatican Scrutiny
From 1580–1581, Montaigne traveled France, Germany, Austria, Switzerland, and Italy, partly seeking relief for hereditary kidney stones (he refused doctors and drugs, recording expelled stones in his journal). The journey doubled as a pilgrimage to the Holy House of Loreto, where he donated a silver relief of his family kneeling before the Madonna. In Rome, the *Essais* were examined by Sisto Fabri, Master of the Sacred Palace under Gregory XIII. Montaigne apologized for references to pagan *fortuna*, praise for Julian the Apostate, and heretical poets; the text was returned on 20 March 1581 with permission to follow his conscience in revisions.

### Mayor, *Politique*, and Final Years
While in Lucca (1581), Montaigne learned he was elected Mayor of Bordeaux (like his father). He served 1581–1585, mediating Catholic-Protestant tensions during the Wars of Religion and managing the 1585 bubonic plague outbreak. The plague and war forced a two-year exile from his château (1586). In 1588 he published the definitive three-book *Essais* and met admirer Marie de Gournay. After Henry III’s assassination (1589), Montaigne, a Catholic, supported the Protestant Henry of Navarre (Henry IV), aligning with the *politiques*—those prioritizing national unity and royal authority over religious conformity. He died in 1592 during a Mass, paralyzed in tongue but lucid, having once declared conversation "the most fruitful and natural play of the mind."

### The *Essais*: Scepticism, Self-Portraiture, and the Art of Living
The *Essais* aim to describe humans—especially himself—with "complete frankness." Montaigne finds human nature defined by "great variety and volatility." He dissects his poor memory, his talent for mediation without emotional entanglement, his disdain for enduring fame, and his practice of detaching from worldly things to prepare for death. He attacks the religious conflicts of his era, arguing humans cannot attain true certainty (Pyrrhonism). On marriage, he views it as necessary for children but warns passionate love threatens freedom. On education (essay "On the Education of Children," dedicated to Diana of Foix), he rejects bookish rote learning and uncritical acceptance of authority (Aristotle, the Stoics). Instead, he demands a tutor who dialogues at the student’s pace, lets the student speak first, encourages debate, and teaches through experience, travel, and practice—so the student "imbibes their way of thinking, not learn their precepts" and makes knowledge their own, like bees turning flowers into honey. The goal: an active, socially interactive life, not passive obedience. Francis Bacon’s *Essays* (1597) show direct influence.

### Influence on Psychology and Education
Though not a scientist, Montaigne’s observations on thought, motivation, fear, happiness, and child education constitute a pre-history of psychology. His educational theory—individualized pacing, dialogic tutoring, critical questioning, experience over memorization, the tutor as facilitator of self-correction ("to change his mind and correct himself... are rare, strong, and philosophical qualities")—anticipates modern constructivist and student-centered pedagogy. He argued curiosity, not authority, should drive learning, and that book learning without practice produces "passive adults, uncritically obedient and unable to think independently."

### Literary and Philosophical Legacy
Montaigne’s Latin quotations draw heavily on Erasmus’s *Adagia*; Plutarch (500+ quotations) is his deepest stylistic and substantive influence. Precursors include Erasmus, Thomas More, John Fisher, and Guillaume Budé (c. 50 years earlier). Shakespeare likely read John Florio’s 1603 English translation; *The Tempest* closely echoes Florio’s "Of Cannibals." Pascal’s *Pensées* scepticism is traditionally linked to Montaigne; Pascal named him and Epictetus as his two most familiar philosophers. Later admirers span William Hazlitt (who imitated his familiar style), Ralph Waldo Emerson ("the frankest and honestest of all writers"), Friedrich Nietzsche ("augmented the joy of living"), Stefan Zweig, Eric Hoffer, John Cowper Powys, Judith Shklar, and Erich Auerbach, who called Montaigne "the first modern man" for his clear conception of "man's self-orientation... the task of making oneself at home in existence without fixed points of support."

## Terms
- ****Essais**** — Montaigne’s collection of short, subjective prose "attempts" (published 1580, 1588); the work that established the essay as a literary genre.
- ****Pyrrhonism**** — A school of radical scepticism (from Pyrrho) suspending judgment on all non-evident matters; adopted by Montaigne in "Apology for Raymond Sebond."
- ****Que sais-je?**** — "What do I know?" — Montaigne’s sceptical motto, expressing the impossibility of certain knowledge.
- ****Marrano**** — Spanish/Portuguese Jews forced to convert to Catholicism; Montaigne’s paternal great-grandfather and maternal grandfather were of such Sephardic origin.
- ****Politiques**** — French establishment faction (late 16th c.) prioritizing peace, national unity, and royal authority over religious allegiance; Montaigne aligned with them in supporting Henry IV.
- ****College of Guienne**** — Elite Bordeaux boarding school where Montaigne studied under George Buchanan (c. 1539–1546).
- ****Étienne de La Boétie**** — Humanist poet, author of *Discourse on Voluntary Servitude*; Montaigne’s closest friend (c. 1558–1563), whose death catalyzed the *Essais*.
- ****Marie de Gournay**** — Writer who admired Montaigne, met him in 1588; he called her his "adopted daughter"; she edited and published the posthumous *Essais*.
- ****Sisto Fabri**** — Master of the Sacred Palace under Pope Gregory XIII; examined the *Essais* in Rome (1581) and returned them with required apologies.
- ****John Florio**** — Translator of Montaigne’s *Essais* into English (1603), the version likely read by Shakespeare.

## Debates and open questions
- **Cause of La Boétie’s death**: Source cites "plague or tuberculosis" as likely but uncertain.
- **Alma mater**: "Little is certain" about Montaigne’s activities 1546–1557; his university (if any) for law studies remains unknown.
- **Shakespeare’s indebtedness**: While *The Tempest* closely follows Florio’s translation of "Of Cannibals," scholars debate whether other parallels reflect direct influence or shared classical sources (Seneca, Horace, Ovid, Virgil).
- **Identification of remains**: Human remains found at Musée d'Aquitaine (2019) show strong DNA and facial reconstruction matches, but cannot be 100% confirmed until descendant DNA is available.
- **Nature of Montaigne’s Catholicism**: He was a Roman Catholic who respected Protestant Henry of Navarre, apologized to the Vatican for pagan/heretical references, yet supported a *politique* peace—scholars debate the sincerity and consistency of his religious posture.
