# Muslim conquests in the Indian subcontinent

## Overview
The Muslim conquests of the Indian subcontinent (8th–18th centuries) established Islamic dynasties that profoundly shaped the region's political, cultural, and religious landscape. Beginning with early Arab raids and culminating in the Mughal Empire, these conquests introduced new administrative systems, facilitated cultural synthesis, and sparked enduring debates about religious tolerance and historical change.

## Timeline
- **c. 636–643 AD** — First recorded Arab naval raids on Thane, Bharuch, and Debal.
- **710–713 AD** — Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest of Sindh for the Umayyad Caliphate.
- **1001–1027 AD** — Mahmud of Ghazni's seventeen invasions into northwestern India.
- **1192** — Muhammad of Ghor defeats Prithviraj III at the Second Battle of Tarain, laying the foundation for Muslim rule in northern India.
- **1206** — Qutb ud-Din Aibak founds the Delhi Sultanate (Mamluk dynasty).
- **1398** — Timur sacks Delhi, causing massive destruction and weakening the Tughlaq dynasty.
- **1526** — Babur defeats the Lodhi Sultan at the First Battle of Panipat, founding the Mughal Empire.
- **1556–1605** — Reign of Akbar, marked by religious tolerance and administrative integration.
- **1658–1707** — Reign of Aurangzeb, characterized by orthodox Islamic policies and expansion into the Deccan.
- **1707** — Death of Aurangzeb, followed by the rapid decline of the Mughal Empire.
- **1757** — Battle of Plassey, marking the beginning of British East India Company dominance.

## Body

### Early Arab Incursions (8th–10th Centuries)
The first Muslim presence consisted of naval raids by the Rashidun and Umayyad Caliphates on coastal towns like Thane and Bharuch, likely for plunder or trade route security. The first major territorial conquest was Muhammad bin Qasim's invasion of Sindh (710–713), establishing a Muslim province. However, Arab control remained limited to parts of Sindh and Multan, facing resistance from local kingdoms. By the 10th century, Arab dominion was confined to two states, and their influence waned with the rise of local dynasties.

### The Ghaznavid and Ghurid Invasions (11th–12th Centuries)
The Turkic Ghaznavid Empire began the second phase. Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni launched seventeen raids, most famously sacking the Somnath temple. His campaigns evolved from targeting the Ismaili Fatimids of Multan to large-scale plunder of Hindu temples and monasteries. The Ghurid dynasty succeeded them. Muhammad of Ghor's victory at the Second Battle of Tarain (1192) was pivotal, leading to Muslim political control in the Indo-Gangetic plain and the eventual founding of the Delhi Sultanate.

### The Delhi Sultanate (13th–16th Centuries)
Following Muhammad of Ghor's assassination, his slave general Qutb ud-Din Aibak founded the Mamluk dynasty, initiating over three centuries of Turkic-Afghan rule. The Sultanate expanded under dynasties like the Khalji and Tughlaq, extending control into the Deccan. This period saw Persian-Indian cultural synthesis, the birth of Urdu, and Indo-Islamic architecture. It was also marked by periodic iconoclasm, the jizya tax on non-Muslims, and Timur's devastating sack of Delhi in 1398, which fragmented the Sultanate into regional kingdoms.

### The Mughal Empire (16th–18th Centuries)
Founded by Babur in 1526, the Mughal Empire became one of the world's largest and most sophisticated. Under Akbar, it pursued religious tolerance and administrative integration, abolishing the jizya and incorporating Hindu Rajputs. This contrasts with Aurangzeb's reign, which reimposed the jizya, destroyed temples, and pursued orthodox policies, leading to wars and administrative strain. After Aurangzeb's death in 1707, the empire rapidly declined due to weak successors, Maratha and Sikh resistance, and invasions by Nader Shah and Ahmad Shah Durrani, paving the way for British dominance.

### Impact and Legacy
The conquests introduced Islamic political frameworks, spurred trade, and fostered cultural exchange in art, architecture, and language. They also involved temple destruction, forced conversions, and social discrimination, as evidenced in texts like the *Chach Nama*. The narrative is contested: some scholars emphasize violence, while others highlight coexistence and cultural synthesis. The legacy remains a central, debated aspect of South Asian history.

## Terms
- **Jizya**: A poll tax on non-Muslim subjects (dhimmis) in exchange for protection and exemption from military service.
- **Dhimmi**: A non-Muslim subject under Islamic rule, granted protection and religious freedom in exchange for paying the jizya and accepting social restrictions.
- **Iconoclasm**: The deliberate destruction of religious icons and monuments, justified in some Islamic traditions as opposition to idolatry.
- **Mamluk**: Literally "slave," referring to Turkic slave-soldiers who formed a powerful military class and established dynasties in the Delhi Sultanate.
- **Sultanate**: An Islamic monarchy where the ruler holds the title of Sultan.
- **Mansabdari System**: The Mughal administrative system ranking officials (mansabdars) for military and civil service.
- **Zamindari System**: A land revenue system where zamindars (landlords) collected taxes from peasants for the state, becoming more prevalent as Mughal authority weakened.
- **Urdu**: A language developed during the Delhi Sultanate, blending Sanskritic Hindi with Persian, Arabic, and Turkish vocabulary, written in a modified Arabic script.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nature of Conquests**: Were they primarily driven by religious zeal (jihad) and iconoclasm, or by political expansion and economic plunder? Scholars debate the relative weight of religious versus political and economic factors.
- **Scale of Violence and Conversion**: Historians disagree on the extent of massacres, forced conversions, and population decline. Estimates like K.S. Lal's claim of a 30 million population decrease are contested by others who argue population grew.
- **Temple Destruction**: Was it a systematic policy of religious persecution, or primarily political, targeting symbols of sovereignty and wealth? Evidence shows both destruction and occasional patronage or repair by Muslim rulers.
- **Historical Sources**: The reliability of texts like the *Chach Nama* is questioned; some view it as a historical romance or political theory rather than a factual chronicle.
- **Legacy of Coexistence vs. Conflict**: To what extent did periods of harmony and cultural synthesis balance episodes of persecution and violence? This debate is central to understanding the long-term impact of Muslim rule on Indian society.
