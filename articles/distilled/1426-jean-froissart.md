# Jean Froissart

## Overview
Jean Froissart (c. 1337 – c. 1405) was a French-speaking medieval author and court historian from the County of Hainaut in the Low Countries. His *Chronicles* are the principal surviving expression of the 14th-century chivalric revival in England, France, and Scotland and serve as a vital primary source for the first half of the Hundred Years' War. Unlike modern historians, Froissart operated within a courtly patronage system, traveling extensively to gather first-hand accounts while dedicating works to aristocrats in exchange for benefices that funded his research. His legacy endures in over 100 illuminated manuscripts, including a lavish copy commissioned by Louis de Gruuthuse in the 1470s.

## Timeline
- **c. 1337** — Born in Valenciennes, County of Hainaut (Holy Roman Empire).
- **1361–1362** — Enters service of Philippa of Hainault, queen consort of Edward III of England, c. age 24.
- **1368** — Travels to Milan with Lionel, Duke of Clarence, to chronicle his wedding to Violante Visconti; Chaucer and Petrarch also present.
- **1369** — Queen Philippa dies; Froissart secures patronage of Joanna, Duchess of Brabant, and receives benefice of Estinnes.
- **c. 1368** — Mentions the verge and foliot (verge escapement) in European clockworks, an early technical reference.
- **1395** — Returns to England; expresses disappointment at perceived decline of chivalry.
- **c. 1405** — Dies (date and circumstances unknown); possibly buried at St. Monegunda of Chimay in Hainaut.
- **1470s** — Louis de Gruuthuse commissions a deluxe four-volume illuminated copy of the *Chronicles* (112 miniatures).

## Body

### Origins and Early Career
Froissart hailed from Valenciennes, on the western edge of the Holy Roman Empire bordering France. Details of his parentage and youth are obscure; earlier theories identifying his father as a painter of armorial bearings or Froissart himself as a former merchant turned cleric rely on poetic "I" personas now considered *pseudo-autobiographical* literary constructs rather than factual testimony. By roughly 1361–62, he left Hainaut for England to serve Philippa of Hainault, queen consort of Edward III. Archival research by scholar Godfried Croenen indicates this was not an official court appointment (such as historiographer or poet laureate) but a literary patronage relationship: Froissart dedicated poems to his "lady" and received occasional gifts in return.

### Travels and Historical Method
Froissart approached his chronicle work with serious empirical intent. He traveled widely across England, Scotland, Wales, France, Flanders, and Spain to gather material and eyewitness testimony. A key journey occurred in 1368 when he accompanied Lionel, Duke of Clarence, to Milan for the duke’s wedding to Violante Visconti, daughter of Galeazzo Visconti; notably, Geoffrey Chaucer and Petrarch were also in attendance. After Queen Philippa’s death in 1369, Froissart secured the patronage of Joanna, Duchess of Brabant, among others. The resulting rewards—including the benefice of Estinnes and a canonry of Chimay—provided the financial independence necessary for further travel and research. His 1395 return to England left him disillusioned by changes he interpreted as the end of the chivalric era.

### The *Chronicles* and Manuscript Tradition
The *Chronicles* constitute Froissart’s enduring claim to fame. The text survives in more than 100 illuminated manuscripts, testifying to its immediate and lasting prestige among the nobility. The most sumptuous copy was commissioned in the 1470s by the Flemish nobleman Louis de Gruuthuse; its four volumes contain 112 miniatures painted by leading Brugeois artists, including Loiset Lyédet. Beyond historical narrative, the *Chronicles* contain a notable early technical observation: Froissart is thought to be among the first to mention the verge and foliot (verge escapement) mechanism in European clockworks, dating the reference to 1368.

### Other Works
Besides the *Chronicles*, Froissart authored *Meliador*, a long Arthurian romance, and a substantial body of poetry encompassing both short lyrical forms and longer narrative poems. He also wrote *L'Horloge amoureux* (The Lover's Clock), a poetic work. While his poetry was once mined for biographical data, modern scholarship treats the first-person voice in these texts as a rhetorical device, not a transparent window onto the author’s life.

## Terms
- **County of Hainaut**: A feudal territory in the Low Countries, part of the Holy Roman Empire, bordering France; Froissart’s birthplace.
- **Philippa of Hainault**: Queen consort of Edward III of England (married 1328); Froissart’s first major patron (1361–1369).
- **Chivalric revival**: A 14th-century cultural movement idealizing knightly codes of conduct, tournaments, and courtly love; the *Chronicles* are its chief literary expression.
- **Hundred Years' War**: Series of conflicts (1337–1453) between England and France over the French throne; Froissart covers the first half.
- **Benefice**: An ecclesiastical office (e.g., canonry) providing income; Froissart held Estinnes and Chimay, funding his independence.
- **Pseudo-autobiographical**: Describes texts where a first-person "I" is a literary persona, not a reliable historical record of the author.
- **Verge and foliot (verge escapement)**: The earliest mechanical clock escapement mechanism; Froissart’s 1368 mention is a key early reference.
- **Louis de Gruuthuse**: Flemish nobleman and bibliophile who commissioned a deluxe four-volume illuminated *Chronicles* in the 1470s.
- **Loiset Lyédet**: Prominent Brugeois miniaturist attributed with paintings in the first two volumes of the Gruuthuse manuscript.
- **Lionel, Duke of Clarence**: Second surviving son of Edward III; Froissart traveled with him to Milan in 1368.

## Debates and open questions
- **Nature of court service**: Whether Froissart held an official salaried position (historiographer/poet) under Philippa of Hainault or operated as an independent poet relying on gift-exchange patronage remains contested; Croenen’s archival argument for the latter challenges older assumptions.
- **Reliability of poetic autobiography**: The extent to which poems like *L'Horloge amoureux* reflect Froissart’s actual life (e.g., merchant past, clerical status) versus literary convention is debated; the consensus leans toward "pseudo-autobiographical" fiction.
- **Date and place of death**: The exact year (c. 1405) and circumstances are unknown; burial at St. Monegunda of Chimay is a plausible but unverified hypothesis.
- **Chronicle composition history**: The *Chronicles* exist in multiple versions (A, B, C redactions) reflecting Froissart’s revisions over decades; the precise chronology and motivation for these rewrites remain subjects of scholarly investigation.
