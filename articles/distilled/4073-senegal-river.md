# Senegal River

## Overview
The Senegal River is a 1,086-kilometre-long river in West Africa that forms much of the border between Senegal and Mauritania. It has been a vital corridor for trade, migration, and cultural exchange for millennia, known to ancient Mediterranean civilizations and later as the legendary "River of Gold" that drew European exploration. Today, it is a critical resource for hydropower and agriculture, managed by an international organization of four riparian states.

## Body

### Geography and Hydrology
The Senegal River is formed by the confluence of the Semefé (Bakoye) and Bafing rivers in Mali, which originate in Guinea. It flows west and north, forming borders between Mali, Mauritania, and Senegal before emptying into the Atlantic Ocean near Saint-Louis. Its drainage basin covers 270,000 km². Major tributaries include the Falémé, Karakoro, and Gorgol rivers. Downstream of Kaédi, the river splits into two branches—the main channel and the Doué—which rejoin downstream of Podor, creating the Île à Morfil. Key infrastructure includes the Manantali Dam in Mali, the Maka-Diama Dam on the Mauritania-Senegal border, and the hydroelectric plants at Félou and Gouina.

### Historical Knowledge and Exploration
The river was known to ancient Mediterranean civilizations; Pliny the Elder called it "Bambotus" and Ptolemy "Nias." In the Early Middle Ages, Arab geographers like al-Masudi and al-Bakri described it, often believing it was connected to the Nile or Niger River. They called it the "River of Gold," a legend that fueled European exploration. Medieval European maps, such as the Catalan Atlas (1375), depicted it as a source of gold. Portuguese exploration in the 15th century, sponsored by Prince Henry the Navigator, culminated in Dinis Dias reaching its mouth in 1446. Regular trade with local Wolof states began shortly after.

### Etymology
The origin of the name "Senegal" is debated. One theory, from 16th-century chronicler João de Barros, claims it was named for a local Wolof chieftain. A popular folk etymology suggests it derives from the Wolof phrase *sunu gaal* ("our canoe"). More recent scholarship points to *Azenegue*, the Portuguese term for the Berber Zenaga people. Another strong theory links it to *Sanghana*, a city described by al-Bakri in 1068 at the river's mouth. The Serer people of the region propose a derivation from their religious term *Sene* (from the supreme deity Roog Seen) and *O Gal* ("body of water").

### Economic Importance
The river is a critical economic engine for Guinea, Mali, Mauritania, and Senegal. Its primary modern importance is in hydropower generation, with major dams and plants providing electricity for regional development. The OMVS coordinates its management. Navigation is currently limited, but there are feasibility studies for a 905 km navigable channel from Ambidédi in Mali to Saint-Louis, which would give landlocked Mali direct access to the Atlantic. The river's aquatic fauna is part of the Senegal-Gambia Catchments ecoregion, with low endemism.

## Terms
- **OMVS (Organisation pour la mise en valeur du fleuve Sénégal):** International organization founded in 1972 by Mali, Mauritania, and Senegal (Guinea joined in 2005) to manage the Senegal River basin.
- **Drainage basin:** The area of land where all water drains into a common outlet, here 270,000 km² for the Senegal River.
- **Trans-Saharan trade route:** Historic trade networks crossing the Sahara Desert, connecting North Africa with West African empires like Ghana and Mali.
- **Langue de Barbarie:** A thin sand strip separating the Senegal River's mouth from the Atlantic Ocean.
- **Île à Morfil:** The long strip of land between the two branches of the Senegal River downstream of Kaédi.
- **Endemic:** Species native and restricted to a specific geographic area; only three frog species and one fish are endemic to the Senegal-Gambia ecoregion.
- **Hydropower:** Electricity generated from the energy of falling or flowing water, a major use of the Senegal River via its dams.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary scholarly debate concerns the river's etymology, with competing theories linking it to a Wolof chieftain, a Wolof phrase, the Berber Zenaga people, the medieval city of Sanghana, or a Serer religious term. Another historical question is the precise location and identity of the legendary "Island of Gold" (Wangara/Palolus) depicted on medieval maps, which is conjectured to represent the Bambuk-Buré goldfield district surrounded by rivers.
