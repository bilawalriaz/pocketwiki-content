# Mekong

## Overview
The Mekong is the world's twelfth-longest river and Asia's third-longest, flowing approximately 4,909 km from the Tibetan Plateau through six countries to the South China Sea. It is a vital source of water, food, and transport for over 60 million people, but its ecosystem is now severely threatened by hydroelectric dam construction and climate change.

## Timeline
- **c. 210 BCE** — Earliest known settlements, like Ban Chiang, date to this period.
- **1st century** — The Indianised-Khmer culture of Funan flourishes in the Mekong delta.
- **5th century** — The Khmer culture Chenla state succeeds Funan.
- **1540** — Portuguese explorer António de Faria becomes the first European to encounter the Mekong.
- **1866-1868** — The French Mekong Expedition ascends the river, concluding it is unnavigable for trade.
- **1900** — The river's source on the Tibetan Plateau is found by Pyotr Kuzmich Kozlov.
- **1995** — Laos, Thailand, Cambodia, and Vietnam establish the Mekong River Commission (MRC).
- **2000** — China, Laos, Thailand, and Myanmar sign an agreement for commercial navigation on the upper Mekong.
- **2000s-2020s** — Construction of numerous hydroelectric dams along the river causes major ecological disruption.

## Body

### Course and Drainage Basin
The Mekong originates as the Za Qu on the Tibetan Plateau, becoming the Lancang River in China. It flows through deep gorges in Yunnan before forming borders between Myanmar, Laos, Thailand, and Cambodia. The river is divided into an upper basin (steep, contributing 15-20% of water) and a lower basin (flatter, with major tributaries). A unique feature is the flow reversal with Cambodia's Tonlé Sap lake: during floods, the Mekong pushes water into the lake; during low flow, the lake drains into the Mekong. The river ends in a complex delta in Vietnam.

### Geology and Natural History
The Mekong's drainage pattern is complex due to active underlying geology, unlike the simple tree-like patterns of rivers like the Amazon. The river's delta has advanced over 200 km in the last 6,000 years. The basin is a global biodiversity hotspot, second only to the Amazon, hosting an estimated 850 freshwater fish species and iconic large fish like the giant freshwater stingray and Mekong giant catfish. However, many species, including the Irrawaddy dolphin, are now critically endangered.

### Fisheries and Livelihoods
The Mekong supports one of the world's largest inland fisheries, yielding about 4.5 million tonnes of fish and aquatic products annually. This provides 47-80% of animal protein for rural diets in the lower basin. An estimated 40 million rural people depend on wild capture fisheries for food and income, making it a critical safety net for the poor.

### Dams and Navigation
As of 2024, the basin contains numerous dams, with 15 on the mainstream (13 in China, 2 in Laos). These dams disrupt sediment flow, fish migration, and natural flood pulses, exacerbating droughts. The river is a historic trade route, but navigation is difficult due to rapids and seasonal water level changes. International agreements aim to improve navigation, though plans to dredge rapids have faced local opposition and funding issues.

### Environmental Issues
Major threats include drought linked to climate change and dams, water pollution from rudimentary sewage treatment, and massive plastic pollution (the Mekong is one of the top 10 rivers flushing plastic to the ocean). Sand mining destabilizes riverbanks and reduces sediment flow to floodplains.

## Terms
- **Drainage basin**: The area of land where all water drains into a common river or lake.
- **Distributary**: A stream that branches off and flows away from a main river channel, common in deltas.
- **Flow reversal**: The seasonal phenomenon where the direction of water flow between the Mekong and Tonlé Sap lake changes.
- **Hydroelectric dam**: A structure built to generate electricity by using the force of flowing water.
- **Mekong River Commission (MRC)**: An intergovernmental organization for dialogue and cooperation on the sustainable management of the Mekong Basin.
- **Sediment**: Solid material, like sand and silt, carried and deposited by water, crucial for delta formation and soil fertility.
- **Tributary**: A river or stream that flows into a larger river.

## Debates and open questions
- The long-term sustainability of hydropower development versus the need for renewable energy is heavily debated, with calls for a moratorium on new dams.
- The precise impact of upstream dams on downstream flood pulse dynamics, drought severity, and fish stocks remains poorly understood and contested.
- The effectiveness of international agreements in balancing national development interests with the health of the shared river ecosystem is an ongoing challenge.
