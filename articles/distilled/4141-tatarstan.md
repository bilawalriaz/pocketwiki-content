# Tatarstan

## Overview
Tatarstan is a republic of Russia in Eastern Europe, part of the Volga Federal District, with its capital in Kazan. It is a significant region due to its strong cultural, linguistic, and ethnic ties to the Turkic and Islamic worlds, its role as a major industrial and oil-producing center, and its historically complex political relationship with the Russian federal government.

## Timeline
- **c. 700–1238** — Volga Bulgaria, the earliest known organized state in the region, adopts Islam in 922.
- **Late 1230s** — Volga Bulgaria falls to the Mongol armies of Batu Khan.
- **1430s** — The Khanate of Kazan is established as an independent successor state.
- **1552** — The Khanate of Kazan is conquered by Tsar Ivan the Terrible.
- **27 May 1920** — The Tatar Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic (ASSR) is established.
- **30 August 1990** — Tatarstan declares state sovereignty.
- **1992** — A referendum approves a new constitution defining Tatarstan as a "Sovereign State."
- **15 February 1994** — A power-sharing treaty is signed between Tatarstan and the Russian Federation.
- **2002** — Constitutional amendments redefine Tatarstan as part of the Russian Federation.
- **24 July 2017** — The 1994 autonomy agreement expires, ending Tatarstan's special status.
- **February 2024** — The head of the republic's title is changed from President to Rais.

## Body

### Geography and Economy
Tatarstan is located in the center of the East European Plain, between the Volga and Kama rivers. Its major natural resources are oil (with over one billion tons in estimated deposits) and natural gas. The economy is highly industrialized, with the petrochemical industry and machine building as key sectors. Major enterprises include the truck-maker KamAZ and the chemical company Kazanorgsintez. The republic also has a significant agricultural sector and a developed transport network.

### History and Political Status
The region's history includes the medieval states of Volga Bulgaria and the Khanate of Kazan, which was conquered by Russia in 1552. After a period of Soviet rule as the Tatar ASSR, Tatarstan declared sovereignty in 1990 and adopted a constitution in 1992 that was later deemed unconstitutional by Russia. A 1994 power-sharing treaty granted the republic significant autonomy, but this special status was formally ended in 2017. The head of the republic, formerly titled President, is now called the Rais.

### Demographics and Culture
The population is about 4 million, with ethnic Tatars (mostly Sunni Muslim) and ethnic Russians (mostly Orthodox Christian) as the largest groups. The official languages are Tatar and Russian. Tatar culture features unique geometric artistry, such as leather mosaic, and observes pre-Islamic holidays like Sabantuy. The republic has a strong sporting tradition and hosts major international events.

### Religion and International Relations
Islam, introduced in 922, is the most common faith. Tatarstan maintains active relations with the global Islamic world, particularly with Turkey and Iran, serving both federal foreign policy goals and local aspirations for international recognition. While radical Islam was historically rare, a rise in extremism was noted after 2012.

## Terms
- **Volga Bulgaria**: An early medieval Turkic state in the region that adopted Islam in 922.
- **Khanate of Kazan**: A successor state to the Golden Horde, independent from the 1430s until its conquest by Russia in 1552.
- **Tatar ASSR**: The Tatar Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic, established in 1920.
- **Rais**: The title for the head of the Republic of Tatarstan, changed from "President" in 2024.
- **Jadidism**: An Islamic reform movement that preached tolerance, influential in 19th-century Tatarstan.
- **Keräşen Tatars**: A minority group of Tatars who are Orthodox Christians.
- **Power-sharing treaty**: The 1994 agreement defining jurisdictional relations between Tatarstan and Russia, which expired in 2017.
- **Kazan Forum**: An annual platform for economic cooperation between Russia and the Islamic world.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The political status of Tatarstan remains a sensitive issue, balancing its historical pursuit of autonomy with Russia's centralizing reforms.
- The source of rising Islamic radicalism in the 2010s is debated, attributed to a combination of a desire for stronger religious identity and increased immigration.
- The inclusion and enumeration of the Keräşen Tatars as a distinct group has provoked controversy among intellectuals seeking to portray Tatars as a homogeneous people.
