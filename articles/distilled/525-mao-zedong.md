# Mao Zedong

## Overview

Mao Zedong (1893–1976) was the founding leader of the People's Republic of China (PRC), ruling from its establishment in 1949 until his death. A founding member of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) in 1921, he rose to power by developing a revolutionary strategy centered on the peasantry rather than the urban proletariat, culminating in victory in the Chinese Civil War. His rule transformed China from a fragmented, semi-colonial state into a unified nuclear power with improved literacy, life expectancy, and women's rights. However, his campaigns—particularly the Great Leap Forward (1958–1962) and the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)—caused tens of millions of deaths through famine, persecution, and violence. Mao's ideology, Mao Zedong Thought, adapted Marxism-Leninism to Chinese conditions and influenced revolutionary movements globally. Assessments of his legacy remain deeply polarized, viewing him as both a nation-builder and a tyrant responsible for catastrophic human suffering.

## Body

### Early Life and Radicalization (1893–1921)
Born in Hunan to a wealthy peasant father and Buddhist mother, Mao received a classical Confucian education but preferred popular novels. The 1911 Xinhai Revolution sparked his nationalism; he briefly served in the republican army. Exposure to Western liberalism (Smith, Mill, Rousseau) and the anarchism of Kropotkin shaped his early thought. Working as a librarian at Peking University (1918–1919) under Li Dazhao, he encountered Marxism and the Bolshevik Revolution. The May Fourth Movement (1919) radicalized him further; he organized students and workers in Hunan, publishing journals advocating mass mobilization and women's liberation. By 1920, he had married Yang Kaihui and fully embraced Marxism, meeting Chen Duxiu in Shanghai.

### Party Founding and United Front Strategy (1921–1927)
Mao attended the CCP's founding congress in 1921, initially adhering to orthodox Marxism's focus on the urban proletariat. However, the Comintern soon directed an alliance with Sun Yat-sen's Kuomintang (KMT). Mao became a KMT organizer, running the Peasant Movement Training Institute in Guangzhou (1926). His 1927 "Report on an Investigation of the Peasant Movement in Hunan" argued that the peasantry was the revolutionary vanguard—a view opposed by both the CCP center and the Comintern. When Chiang Kai-shek purged Communists in the 1927 White Terror, the urban party was decimated, validating Mao's rural focus.

### Rural Bases and the Long March (1927–1935)
Expelled by the CCP Central Committee for "military opportunism," Mao established the Jinggangshan base (1927–1928), merging bandit groups into a disciplined Red Army and implementing land reform. After a failed move to Hunan, he united with Zhu De's forces. In Jiangxi (1929–1934), he chaired the Soviet Republic of China but lost military control to Zhou Enlai and the Soviet-trained "28 Bolsheviks." The KMT's fifth encirclement campaign forced the Long March (Oct 1934–Oct 1935). At the Zunyi Conference (Jan 1935), backed by Stalin, Mao assumed de facto leadership. Only 7,000–8,000 survivors reached the Yan'an Soviet in Shaanxi, cementing Mao's authority.

### Yan'an, War, and Civil War Victory (1935–1949)
At Yan'an, Mao consolidated power through the Rectification Movement, developed his theories (New Democracy, Protracted War), and expanded the Red Army to 500,000 via the Second United Front (1937–1945). The Dixie Mission (1944) reported CCP governance as less corrupt and more effective than the KMT. Post-WWII, Soviet occupation of Manchuria aided the CCP. The People's Liberation Army (PLA) defeated KMT forces in 1948–49 (notably the Siege of Changchun), forcing Chiang to Taiwan. Mao proclaimed the PRC on 1 October 1949.

### Consolidation and Early Campaigns (1949–1957)
Mao traveled to Moscow (1949–50), securing a treaty reflecting Stalin's dominance. Domestically, land reform redistributed property via mass struggle sessions; the U.S. State Department later estimated 1–5 million deaths. The Campaign to Suppress Counter-revolutionaries targeted former KMT officials and merchants. The Three-anti (1951) and Five-anti (1952) campaigns purged corruption and capitalist elements in cities, causing hundreds of thousands of deaths, many by suicide. The First Five-Year Plan (1953–58) achieved rapid industrial growth with Soviet aid. The Hundred Flowers Campaign (1956–57) briefly invited criticism; the ensuing Anti-Rightist Movement persecuted ~550,000 intellectuals.

### Great Leap Forward and Famine (1958–1962)
To surpass the UK and US, Mao merged collectives into massive people's communes, diverting labor to backyard steel furnaces and infrastructure. Grain output fell ~15% (1959) and 10% (1960). Local cadres falsified harvest reports; the state requisitioned grain based on inflated figures, leaving peasants to starve. The Great Chinese Famine was a direct cause of the death of some 30 million Chinese peasants between 1959 and 1962. At the Lushan Conference in July and August 1959, Defence Minister Peng Dehuai criticized the policy; Mao purged him and his supporters, stifling criticism of the Great Leap policies. A campaign was launched and resulted in party members and ordinary peasants being sent to prison labour camps. Mao stepped down as President of China on 27 April 1959, but retained other top positions such as Chairman of the Communist Party and of the Central Military Commission. Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping reversed course in 1962, disbanding communes and importing grain.

### Sino-Soviet Split and Third Front (1960s)
Khrushchev's de-Stalinization (post-1953) and withdrawal of Soviet experts (1960) triggered a split over communist leadership. Mao accused the USSR of "revisionism" and built a rival global network (allying only with Albania). Fear of US invasion (Gulf of Tonkin, 1964) and later Soviet invasion (Zhenbao Island, 1969) drove the "Third Front" crash program: massive inland industrial, rail, and aerospace infrastructure (e.g., Chengdu-Kunming railway, Panzhihua Steel). This reduced regional disparities but diverted resources from coastal development.

### Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)
Fearing a new bureaucratic elite ("capitalist roaders"), Mao mobilized youth (Red Guards) to attack party leaders, intellectuals, and "Four Olds" (customs, culture, habits, ideas). Liu Shaoqi was purged and died in custody; Lin Biao, named successor, died fleeing in 1971. An estimate of around 400,000 deaths is a widely accepted minimum figure, according to Maurice Meisner. MacFarquhar and Schoenhals assert that in rural China alone some 36 million people were persecuted, of whom between 750,000 and 1.5 million were killed, with roughly the same number permanently injured. The Gang of Four (including Jiang Qing) rose to power. Mao declared it over in 1969; the Party officially condemned it as a "severe setback" in 1981.

### Foreign Policy and Military Modernization
Mao intervened in Korea (1950–53), losing ~180,000 troops but securing the North. US embargo followed until Nixon's 1972 visit. Mao launched the "Two Bombs, One Satellite" program, achieving atomic (1964) and hydrogen (1967) bombs and a satellite (1970). Project 523 (1967) developed artemisinin antimalarials for Vietnamese allies. The "Three Worlds Theory" (1970s) positioned China as leader of the "Third World" against both superpowers.

### Death and Succession
Mao's health declined (heart attacks, possible ALS/Parkinson's). He died 9 September 1976. Hua Guofeng briefly succeeded, arresting the Gang of Four. Deng Xiaoping emerged paramount leader by 1978, launching "Reform and Opening Up."

## Terms

- ****Mao Zedong Thought**** — The official ideology adapting Marxism-Leninism to Chinese conditions, emphasizing peasant-based revolution, continuous class struggle, and mass mobilization.
- ****Long March (1934–35)**** — The CCP's 6,000-mile strategic retreat from Jiangxi to Shaanxi; ~90% of 100,000 participants died; cemented Mao's supreme leadership.
- ****People's Communes**** — Large collective units (thousands of households) formed during the Great Leap Forward, merging agriculture, industry, and local governance; abolished private plots.
- ****Great Chinese Famine (1959–62)**** — Catastrophic famine caused by Great Leap policies (diverted labor, false reporting, excessive requisition) and natural disasters; ~30 million deaths.
- ****Cultural Revolution (1966–76)**** — Mao's mass mobilization of youth (Red Guards) to purge "capitalist roaders" and "Four Olds"; caused widespread chaos, persecution, and cultural destruction.
- ****Third Front**** — Crash industrialization program (1964–80) moving heavy industry, defense, and infrastructure to China's interior for survival against US/Soviet invasion.
- ****Sino-Soviet Split (1960s)**** — Ideological and geopolitical rupture between PRC and USSR over de-Stalinization, peaceful coexistence, and leadership of world communism.
- ****Two Bombs, One Satellite**** — Mao's crash program (1950s–70) for nuclear weapons (A-bomb 1964, H-bomb 1967) and satellite launch (1970), achieving strategic deterrence.
- ****Hundred Flowers Campaign (1956–57)**** — Brief period inviting intellectual criticism ("let a hundred flowers bloom"); followed by Anti-Rightist crackdown persecuting ~550,000 critics.
- ****Little Red Book**** — *Quotations from Chairman Mao Tse-tung* (1964), edited by Lin Biao; mandatory possession for Party members; symbol of Mao's personality cult.

## Debates and open questions

**Death tolls and intentionality:** Estimates for the Great Leap famine range from 15 to 55 million; for the Cultural Revolution, 400,000 to 20 million. Scholars debate whether Mao *wilfully* ignored evidence of starvation (Bernstein) or was misled by false reports, and whether Cultural Revolution violence was centrally directed or spiraled beyond control.

**Mao's theoretical originality:** Was Mao a creative adapter of Marxism to agrarian China (inventing "New Democracy," "People's War") or a nationalist using Marxist rhetoric? The CCP's 1981 Resolution on Certain Questions in the History of Our Party since the Founding of the People's Republic of China stated that Mao's contributions to the Chinese Communist Revolution far outweigh his mistakes, attributing his errors to individualist tendencies which arose when he departed from the collective view of the leadership.

**Economic legacy:** Did the First Five-Year Plan and Third Front lay the industrial foundation for post-1978 growth, or did Mao's policies (Great Leap, Cultural Revolution) waste decades of development? Fairbank and Meisner argue it was a "great modernizing epoch" despite failures; others emphasize the human cost and lost opportunities.

**Historical verdict:** The CCP's official "70% right, 30% wrong" formulation (Deng Xiaoping) contrasts with Western views ranging from "great modernizer" (Fairbank) to "great tyrant" (Short, Pantsov/Levine). The 1981 Resolution condemns the Cultural Revolution but affirms Mao's revolutionary contributions. In contemporary China, nostalgia for Mao's egalitarianism coexists with censorship of critical reassessment.

**International influence:** Maoism inspired global movements (Khmer Rouge, Shining Path, Naxalites), often with genocidal results. China itself has moved sharply away from Maoism since Mao's death, and most people outside of China who describe themselves as Maoist regard the Deng Xiaoping reforms to be a betrayal of Maoism. Nevertheless, the Chinese government has never officially repudiated the tactics of Mao. Mao's image remains on currency and in state symbolism, reflecting unresolved tension between revolutionary legitimacy and reform-era pragmatism.
