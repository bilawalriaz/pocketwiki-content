# Harmonica

## Overview
The harmonica is a free-reed wind instrument played worldwide in genres like blues, folk, and jazz. It produces sound via vibrating reeds activated by the player's breath, making it portable and versatile. Its design and techniques have evolved significantly since the 19th century, influencing and adapting to diverse musical traditions.

## Timeline
- **c. 1820** — Free-reed designs begin being created in Europe, inspired by Asian instruments like the sheng.
- **1821** — Christian Friedrich Ludwig Buschmann is often cited as the inventor of the harmonica.
- **1826** — Joseph Richter invents Richter tuning and the blow/draw mechanism.
- **1829** — Charles Wheatstone develops a mouth-organ called the "Aeolina."
- **1857** — Matthias Hohner begins mass-producing harmonicas in Trossingen, Germany.
- **1868** — Hohner begins supplying harmonicas to the United States.
- **1920s** — The diatonic harmonica reaches its modern form; first jazz and folk recordings are made.
- **1924** — Hohner produces the first chromatic harmonica.
- **1930s** — Harmonica gains attention from classical musicians like Larry Adler.
- **1950s** — Finn Magnus develops the all-plastic harmonica in the U.S.
- **1970s** — Howard Levy develops the overbending technique, enabling full chromatic play on diatonic models.

## Body

### Construction and Sound Production
The harmonica's basic parts are the comb (main body forming air chambers), reed plates (housing the reeds), and cover plates (projecting sound). Reeds are flat springs (typically brass) that vibrate when air flows past them, alternately blocking and unblocking an airway to produce sound. Pitch is determined by a reed's length, weight, and stiffness. Most modern harmonicas use "closing reeds," allowing a blow and draw reed to share a chamber without valves. Wind-savers (one-way valves) are used in chromatic and chord models to prevent air leakage.

### Types and Tuning
The most common type is the diatonic Richter-tuned harmonica (often called a "blues harp"), designed for a single key but capable of playing others via bending. Chromatic harmonicas use a button-activated slide to access all notes of the chromatic scale. Tremolo harmonicas have two slightly out-of-tune reeds per note for a wavering sound. Orchestral models include melody (e.g., horn harmonicas) and chord harmonicas for ensemble playing. The ChengGong harmonica features a sliding mouthpiece for varied chord voicings.

### Techniques and Performance
Key techniques include vibrato (creating a shaking sound via hand or glottal movements), bending (lowering pitch by adjusting embouchure, essential for blues), and overbending (overblowing/overdrawing to access notes outside the basic scale). Players use different "positions" to play in various keys and modes. Amplification with microphones and tube amplifiers, pioneered by artists like Little Walter, is central to blues harmonica, creating a distorted, powerful sound.

### History and Global Spread
Developed in early 19th-century Europe, the harmonica was mass-produced by Hohner from 1857 and became popular in the U.S. by the 1860s. It was used by soldiers in the American Civil War and became a fixture in American folk and blues music. In East Asia, the tremolo harmonica became dominant, with local tunings developed for folk music. Production centers are now global, with major companies in Germany, Japan, South Korea, and China.

### Medical and Related Uses
Playing the harmonica requires strong, resisted breathing, which can aid pulmonary rehabilitation for conditions like COPD. The harmonica is related to other free-reed instruments like the concertina and melodica, but is distinct from the unrelated glass harmonica.

## Terms
- **Free reed**: A vibrating reed that moves freely through a slot, as opposed to a reed that strikes an edge.
- **Comb**: The main body of the harmonica, which forms air chambers for the reeds.
- **Reed plate**: A metal plate to which reeds are attached, forming a unit within the harmonica.
- **Wind-saver**: A one-way valve used to prevent air leakage through an inactive reed.
- **Bending**: A technique to lower the pitch of a note by adjusting the embouchure.
- **Overbending**: Advanced techniques (overblow/overdraw) to raise a note's pitch, enabling chromatic play on a diatonic harmonica.
- **Richter tuning**: A standard tuning system for diatonic harmonicas, invented in 1826.
- **Chromatic scale**: A musical scale consisting of all twelve semitones within an octave.
- **Tremolo**: A tuning with two slightly out-of-tune reeds per note, creating a wavering effect.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Comb Material Effect**: There is dispute among players about whether the material of the comb (wood, plastic, metal) significantly affects the harmonica's tone, with some arguing it does not vibrate enough to matter.
- **Tuning Origins**: While Buschmann is often cited as the inventor in 1821, other inventors developed similar instruments concurrently, making definitive attribution difficult.
- **Plastic vs. Traditional Sound**: The development of plastic harmonicas during WWII made them more accessible but is often considered by players to produce an inferior sound compared to traditional metal and wood models.
