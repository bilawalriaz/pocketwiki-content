# Psychology

## Overview

Psychology is the scientific study of the mind and behavior, encompassing conscious and unconscious phenomena and mental processes such as thoughts, feelings, and motives. It is an academic discipline of broad scope, crossing boundaries between the natural and social sciences. Psychologists aim to understand mental functions, physiological and neurobiological processes, and the behavior of individuals and groups, ultimately seeking to benefit society through applications ranging from mental health treatment to solving problems in education, work, and social change.

## Timeline

- **c. 1510–1520** — Marko Marulić first employs the Latin form *psychiologia* in his book *Psichiologia de ratione animae humanae*.
- **387 BCE / 335 BCE** — Plato suggests the brain is where mental processes take place; Aristotle suggests the heart.
- **1830s** — Gustav Fechner begins psychophysics research in Leipzig; later articulates the Weber–Fechner law.
- **1879** — Wilhelm Wundt establishes the psychological laboratory at Leipzig University, bringing experimental psychology to the world.
- **1890** — William James defines psychology as "the science of mental life" in *The Principles of Psychology*.
- **1892** — The American Psychological Association (APA) is founded.
- **1913** — John B. Watson asserts the methodological behaviorist view of psychology as a purely objective experimental branch of natural science.
- **1917** — Robert Yerkes administers mental tests ("Army Alpha" and "Army Beta") to almost 1.8 million soldiers during World War I.
- **1950s** — The "cognitive revolution" begins, rejecting the anti-mentalist dogma of behaviorism; humanistic psychology emerges as a "third force."
- **1954** — Kenneth and Mamie Phipps Clark's doll tests research helps decide the landmark *Brown v. Board of Education* decision.
- **2010s** — The replication crisis emerges; systematic efforts find as many as two-thirds of highly publicized findings in psychology failed to be replicated.

## Body

### Etymology and Definitions

The word *psychology* derives from the Greek *psyche* (spirit or soul) and *-logia* (study or research). The word was first used in the Renaissance; in its Latin form *psychiologia*, it was first employed by Croatian humanist Marko Marulić in the decade 1510–1520. The earliest known reference in English was by Steven Blankaart in 1694. In 1890, William James defined psychology as "the science of mental life, both of its phenomena and their conditions." This definition was contested by John B. Watson in 1913, who asserted the methodological behaviorist view of psychology as a purely objective experimental branch of natural science, the theoretical goal of which "is the prediction and control of behavior." *Folk psychology* refers to the understanding of mental states and behaviors held by ordinary people, as contrasted with professionals' understanding.

### History

Ancient civilizations of Egypt, Greece, China, India, and Persia all engaged in philosophical study of psychology. In Ancient Egypt, the Ebers Papyrus mentioned depression and thought disorders. Greek philosophers including Thales, Plato, and Aristotle addressed the workings of the mind; Hippocrates theorized that mental disorders had physical rather than supernatural causes. In China, psychological thought emerged from the works of Laozi and Confucius and Buddhist teachings; *The Yellow Emperor's Classic of Internal Medicine* identified the brain as the nexus of wisdom and sensation. Indian philosophy, influenced by Hinduism, explored distinctions in types of awareness, with the Upanishads distinguishing a transient mundane self from an eternal, unchanging soul.

In Europe, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz argued that mental activity took place on an indivisible continuum. Christian Wolff identified psychology as its own science in the 1730s. Immanuel Kant advanced anthropology as a discipline but explicitly rejected experimental psychology. In 1783, Ferdinand Ueberwasser designated himself Professor of Empirical Psychology and Logic. In 1825, the Prussian state established psychology as a mandatory discipline in its educational system, though it did not yet embrace experimentation.

### Beginning of Experimental Psychology

Gustav Fechner's 1860 *Elements of Psychophysics* challenged Kant's negative view, showing mental processes could be measured by experimental methods. Wilhelm Wundt came to Leipzig University, where he established the psychological laboratory that brought experimental psychology to the world, focusing on breaking down mental processes into basic components. G. Stanley Hall, an American who studied with Wundt, founded an influential lab at Johns Hopkins University. Wundt's students spread the discipline globally: Hugo Münsterberg taught at Harvard; Yujiro Motora brought experimental psychology to the Imperial University of Tokyo; Narendra Nath Sen Gupta founded a psychology department at the University of Calcutta in 1905. Edward Titchener created the psychology program at Cornell and advanced "structuralist" psychology, analyzing the mind through introspection. William James, John Dewey, and Harvey Carr advanced functionalism, underlining the Darwinian idea of a behavior's usefulness. In Russia, Ivan Sechenov advanced the idea of brain reflexes, and Ivan Pavlov discovered classical conditioning in dogs.

### Consolidation and Funding

The first International Congress of Psychology took place in Paris in 1889; the APA was founded in 1892. American psychology gained status upon the U.S.'s entry into World War I, with Robert Yerkes administering mental tests to almost 1.8 million soldiers. The Rockefeller family began providing funding for behavioral research. During World War II and the Cold War, the U.S. military and intelligence agencies became leading funders of psychology. In Nazi Germany, the Göring Institute was created to align psychology with the Reich's goals, with psychologists providing *Seelenführung* (soul guidance). After the Russian Revolution, the Bolsheviks promoted psychology to engineer the "New Man" of socialism. Twentieth-century Chinese psychology originally modeled itself on U.S. psychology, but after the Chinese Communist Party gained control, the Stalinist Soviet Union became the major influence.

### Women in Psychology

In 1923, Anna Freud built on her father's work to psychoanalyze children, emphasizing tailoring sessions to the child's needs. Leta Stetter Hollingworth proved that functional periodicity (the belief women are impaired during menstruation) was incorrect, finding no evidence of decreased performance during the menstrual cycle. Mary Whiton Calkins invented the paired associates technique of studying memory; Karen Horney developed "womb envy" and neurotic needs; Melanie Klein impacted developmental psychology with play therapy. In the second half of the 20th century, Mary Ainsworth developed the Strange Situation Procedure and attachment theory. Mamie Phipps Clark's doll tests research helped establish the negative effects of racial discrimination on black children's self-image, contributing to the *Brown v. Board of Education* decision. In 1962, Martha Bernal became the first Latina to get a Ph.D. in psychology; in 1969, Marigold Linton became the first Native American woman to do so. In the 21st century, women make up about 78% of undergraduate and 71% of graduate students in psychology in the U.S., though gender disparities persist in pay and senior academic positions.

### Major Schools of Thought

**Neuropsychology** concerns relationships between brain function and behavior, examining how the nervous system influences cognitive processes. **Evolutionary psychology** approaches thought and behavior from a modern evolutionary perspective, suggesting psychological adaptations evolved to solve recurrent problems in ancestral environments. **Comparative psychology** studies behavior across species, tracing its origins to Charles Darwin. **Behaviorism** holds that a large part of behavior is learned; Ivan Pavlov demonstrated classical conditioning, and B.F. Skinner popularized operant conditioning. Noam Chomsky published an influential critique arguing behaviorist principles could not explain language acquisition. **Cognitive psychology** studies mental processes including perception, attention, memory, and problem solving, relying on an information processing model. The cognitive revolution starting in the 1950s rejected behaviorism's anti-mentalist dogma. **Social psychology** studies how behaviors, thoughts, and feelings are influenced by the social environment. **Psychoanalysis**, originating in the 1890s with Sigmund Freud, analyzes the unconscious mind; Karl Popper argued it was not falsifiable. **Humanistic psychology**, emerging in the 1950s, stresses free will and self-actualization; Abraham Maslow formulated a hierarchy of needs and Carl Rogers developed client-centered therapy. **Existential psychology** emphasizes understanding a client's total orientation toward the world; Viktor Frankl created logotherapy, focusing on a will to meaning.

### Themes

**Personality psychology** concerns enduring patterns of behavior, thought, and emotion. Trait theorists have developed taxonomies; since the 1980s, the Big Five (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism) emerged as an important theory. The study of the **unconscious mind** was a hallmark of early psychology; Freud popularized the concept, and cognitive psychologists have used a "filter" model of attention. **Motivation** studies why people or animals initiate, continue, or terminate behavior; Clark Hull formalized the drive reduction model. **Developmental psychology** studies how thought processes, emotions, and behaviors change over the life course; its main origins are found in the work of Jean Piaget. All researched **psychological traits** are influenced by both genes and environment; behavioral genetics researchers use twin studies and adoption studies to disentangle this confound.

### Applications

**Psychological testing** dates back to 2200 BCE in Chinese civil service examinations. Francis Galton is considered an inventor of modern mental testing; Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon developed a new intelligence test in 1905–1911, introducing the concept of mental age. Lewis Terman modified the Binet-Simon scale and introduced the intelligence quotient. Eugenicists used mental testing to justify compulsory sterilization, affirmed by the U.S. Supreme Court in *Buck v. Bell* (1927). **Clinical psychology** involves psychological assessment and psychotherapy; Lightner Witmer established the first psychology clinic in the U.S. in 1896. Cognitive-behavioral therapy emerged in the 1970s with the work of Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck. **Educational psychology** studies how humans learn in educational settings. **Industrial and organizational (I/O) psychology** applies psychological theories to the work lives of individuals; the Hawthorne effect describes how people's behavior can change when they think they are being observed. **Occupational health psychology (OHP)** emerged toward the end of the 20th century, concerned with worker health and safety.

### Research Methods

Quantitative psychological research lends itself to statistical hypothesis testing, using methods including the Pearson correlation coefficient, analysis of variance, and structural equation modeling. A true experiment with random assignment allows strong inferences about causal relationships; a quasi-experiment involves preexisting groups. Surveys measure attitudes and traits; observational studies include cross-sectional and longitudinal designs. The electroencephalogram (EEG) measures voltage changes across the brain; functional neuroimaging techniques track blood flow. Computational modeling simulates behavior. Animal experiments aid in investigating perception, emotion, learning, memory, and thought. Qualitative research involves interviews, first-hand observation, and participant observation.

### Contemporary Issues

Metascience has revealed problems in psychological research, including bias, problematic reproducibility, and misuse of statistics. A replication crisis has emerged; the Reproducibility Project found as many as two-thirds of highly publicized findings failed to be replicated. Critics view statistical hypothesis testing as misplaced; Jacob Cohen wrote in 1994 that psychologists routinely confuse statistical significance with practical importance. Researchers have documented a WEIRD bias ("Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic"), finding that 96% of psychological samples come from countries with only 12% of the world's population. Starting in 2020, researchers of non-human behavior have emphasized the STRANGE bias (Social background, Trappability, Rearing history, Acclimation, Natural changes, Genetic makeup, Experience).

### Ethics

Ethical standards have changed over time. The Nuremberg Code was established after World War II because of Nazi abuses; most countries adopted the Declaration of Helsinki. The APA's Ethical Principles consist of five General Principles: Beneficence and Nonmaleficence, Fidelity and Responsibility, Integrity, Justice, and Respect for People's Rights and Dignity. The Canadian Psychological Association developed its own code in 1986 drawing from four similar principles. University ethics committees evaluate proposed research to protect participants' rights and well-being. Research on nonhuman animals cannot proceed without ethics committee permission; Harry Harlow's isolation experiments on rhesus macaque monkeys drew moral condemnation.

## Terms

- **Psychophysics**: The study of the relationship between physical stimuli and psychological perception; Fechner articulated the principle that perception varies logarithmically with stimulus intensity.
- **Classical conditioning**: A learning process in which a neutral stimulus comes to elicit a response after being paired with a biologically potent stimulus; discovered by Ivan Pavlov.
- **Operant conditioning**: A learning method adding the concepts of reinforcement and punishment; popularized by B.F. Skinner.
- **Structuralism**: An approach analyzing and classifying aspects of the mind primarily through introspection; advanced by Edward Titchener.
- **Functionalism**: An approach underlining the Darwinian idea of a behavior's usefulness to the individual; advanced by William James, John Dewey, and Harvey Carr.
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy**: A psychotherapy synthesizing elements of behaviorism and cognitive psychology; modified from techniques developed by Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck.
- **WEIRD bias**: The bias in psychology studies using participants from "Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic" societies, limiting generalizability.
- **Replication crisis**: The ongoing problem in psychology where many notable findings have not been replicated in subsequent studies.
- **Psychometrics**: The field of mental testing and measurement; Francis Galton is considered an inventor of modern mental testing.
- **Informed consent**: The ethical principle that a patient or research subject must understand and freely choose to undergo a procedure.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether psychology is a "soft" science; Thomas Kuhn's 1962 critique implied psychology was in a pre-paradigm state, lacking agreement on overarching theory. Critics inside and outside the field argue mainstream psychology has become dominated by a "cult of empiricism" restricting research to methods derived from the physical sciences. Feminist critiques argue claims to scientific objectivity obscure the values and agenda of historically mostly male researchers. The effectiveness of positive psychological interventions is unclear—it is "far from clear" they are effective in making people happier. The influence of unconscious forces on choices bears on the philosophical question of free will; John Bargh, Daniel Wegner, and Ellen Langer describe free will as an illusion. Some observers perceive a gap between scientific theory and its application, citing unsupported clinical practices such as "facilitated communication for infantile autism" and rebirthing therapies, though these are outside mainstream clinical psychology doctoral programs.
