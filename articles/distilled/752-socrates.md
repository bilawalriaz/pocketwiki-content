# Socrates

## Overview
Socrates (c. 470–399 BC) was an Athenian philosopher who shifted Western philosophy from natural science to ethics and human affairs. He wrote nothing; our knowledge derives from dialogues by his students Plato and Xenophon, the comic playwright Aristophanes, and later Aristotle. These sources conflict, creating the "Socratic problem" of reconstructing his historical views. Socrates employed the *elenchus* (refutation), a dialectical method of questioning that exposes contradictions in interlocutors' beliefs, leading to *aporia* (impasse). He claimed ignorance of ethical definitions, arguing that virtue is knowledge and that no one errs willingly. His religious nonconformity—rejecting anthropomorphic gods, citing a private *daimonion* (divine sign), and redefining piety as philosophical self-examination—provoked his 399 BC trial for impiety and corrupting the youth. He refused exile or escape, accepting execution by hemlock. His legacy shaped the Academy, Stoicism, Cynicism, and later Christian, Islamic, and modern thought.

## Timeline
- **c. 470/469 BC** — Born in Alopece to Sophroniscus (stoneworker) and Phaenarete (midwife); Athenian citizen.
- **c. 431–404 BC** — Serves as hoplite in Peloponnesian War; distinguishes himself at Potidaea, Delium, Amphipolis.
- **406 BC** — As *epistates* (presiding officer), sole dissent against illegal collective trial of generals after Arginusae.
- **404 BC** — Refuses Thirty Tyrants' order to arrest Leon of Salamis; risks death rather than commit injustice.
- **399 BC** — Tried for *asebeia* (impiety) and corrupting youth; convicted by jury of 500+; proposes free meals as penalty; sentenced to death.
- **399 BC** — Drinks hemlock in prison after refusing escape; last words request sacrifice to Asclepius.
- **c. 380s BC** — Plato founds Academy; writes early "Socratic" dialogues (Apology, Crito, Euthyphro, etc.).
- **3rd c. AD** — Stoics, Skeptics, Cynics claim Socratic lineage; Epicureans and Pyrrhonists attack him.
- **8th–12th c.** — Arabic translations (Al-Kindi, Muʿtazila) integrate Socrates into Islamic philosophy.
- **15th c.** — Italian humanists (Bruni, Manetti, Ficino) revive Socrates as civic republican or holy sage.
- **19th–20th c.** — Hegel, Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Arendt, Strauss, Popper reinterpret Socrates for modernity.

## Body

### Sources and the Socratic Problem
Socrates authored no texts. The primary sources are Plato and Xenophon (both pupils), Aristophanes (contemporary comic poet), and Aristotle (Plato’s pupil, born after Socrates’ death). All write *logoi sokratikoi* (Socratic dialogues), a genre Aristotle identified. The accounts contradict: Plato’s Socrates is ironic, ignorant, and dialectical; Xenophon’s is conventional, self-controlled (*enkrateia*), and lacks *elenchus*; Aristophanes’ *Clouds* caricatures him as a sophistic naturalist. Aristotle treats Socrates doctrinally but focuses on Plato’s early dialogues. The "Socratic problem"—reconstructing the historical Socrates—arose because later authors may have used Socrates as a mouthpiece. Schleiermacher (1818) dismissed Xenophon as a soldier incapable of grasping philosophy. Vlastos argued Plato’s early dialogues reflect the historical Socrates; Kahn holds only the *Apology* is historical. Most scholars now study the *literary* Socrates rather than a recoverable historical figure.

### Biography
Born to affluent parents in Alopece, Socrates inherited a modest estate, freeing him from labor. He received standard elite education (gymnastics, music, poetry). Married twice (order unclear): to Xanthippe (three sons) and a daughter of Aristides. He served as hoplite at Potidaea, Delium, and Amphipolis, showing physical endurance. In 406 BC, as *epistates*, he alone opposed the illegal collective trial of generals who failed to rescue survivors at Arginusae. Under the Thirty Tyrants (404 BC), he refused to arrest Leon of Salamis, risking execution. He was notoriously ugly (snub nose, bulging eyes, potbelly), indifferent to hygiene and comfort, barefoot, wearing one cloak. He moderated but did not abstain from food, drink, and sex; Plato says he loved youths but channeled eros into soul-education. Politically, he criticized both democrats and oligarchs, obeyed laws, but avoided office. In 399 BC, Meletus, Anytus, and Lycon charged him with *asebeia* (not believing in city gods, introducing new *daimonia*) and corrupting youth. The trial lasted one day; he was convicted by a majority of 500+ jurors. He proposed free meals in the Prytaneum or a fine of one mina; jurors chose death. He refused escape, arguing that breaking the laws would harm the city and his own soul. He died after drinking hemlock, requesting a rooster be sacrificed to Asclepius.

### Trial of Socrates
The charges: (1) *asebeia*—denying anthropomorphic gods, believing in a private *daimonion*; (2) corrupting youth. The political context: Athens had just restored democracy after the Thirty Tyrants (critias, Charmides—Socrates’ associates) and an amnesty (403 BC) barred prosecution for pre-403 acts. Yet accusers used pre-amnesty associations. Socrates’ defense (Plato’s *Apology*): he is not a naturalist (contra *Clouds*) nor a sophist (he takes no fees). Corruption is involuntary—no one harms themselves willingly. The *daimonion* is a divine sign deterring him from errors since childhood; it is not a "new god." He claims to be "God’s gift" to Athens, a gadfly stirring the city to virtue. Convicted, he rejects exile, proposing maintenance or a fine. The jury votes death. He warns that his disciples will continue his questioning. Scholars debate motives: religious nonconformity, political threat (anti-democratic pupils), or synthesis (religion and state inseparable in Athens). The amnesty’s scope and the use of pre-403 evidence remain contested.

### Philosophy

#### Socratic Method (*Elenchus*)
The *elenchus* is a refutative dialectic: Socrates asks an "expert" for a definition (e.g., of courage, piety), then through short questions derives a contradiction between the definition and the expert’s other beliefs. The expert revises; the process repeats, usually ending in *aporia*. It tests consistency, not truth directly. Vlastos (1982) argued it cannot establish truth, only inconsistency. Constructivists hold it aims at positive knowledge; non-constructivists see it as purely negative. Socrates applies it to himself, claiming no doctrinal teaching.

#### Priority of Definition
Socrates insists on defining terms before discussing their properties. He seeks the common nature (*ousia*) of virtues (justice, courage) across examples. Guthrie links this to countering sophistic relativism. Critics (Geach) call it fallacious: one can know a proposition without defining its terms. Some attribute the principle to Plato, not Socrates.

#### Socratic Ignorance
Socrates famously claims "I know that I know nothing" (*Apology* 21d). He distinguishes *epistēmē* (systematic, certain knowledge) from lower cognition. In *Apology* 29b he says he *knows* injustice is evil. Vlastos resolves this via two senses: Knowledge-C (certain) and Knowledge-E (elenctic). Socrates has Knowledge-E of ethical truths but lacks Knowledge-C of their essences. Lesher objects that Socrates treats "knowledge" as univocal. Irwin takes the ignorance literally; Gulley sees pedagogical irony.

#### Socratic Irony
Socrates feigns ignorance or praises interlocutors mockingly (e.g., *Euthyphro*: "I must become your student"). Aristotle and Plato confirm his irony. Hellenistic view: playful attention-getter. Straussian view: esoteric concealment for select readers. Vlastos: complex double meaning—ironic in one sense, serious in another (e.g., denying knowledge of ethics seriously, but ironically denying all cognition).

#### Eudaimonism and Intellectualism
All action aims at *eudaimonia* (flourishing). Virtue is knowledge (*virtue intellectualism*); thus "no one errs willingly" (*Protagoras* 345c). If one knows the good, one does it (*motivational intellectualism*). *Akrasia* (weakness of will) is impossible—passions cannot override knowledge. Debate: are virtue and *eudaimonia* identical, or is virtue a means? Do agents desire the *actual* good or the *apparent* good?

#### Religion
Socrates rejects traditional piety (sacrifices for reward) and anthropomorphism. In *Euthyphro*, he poses the dilemma: is the pious loved by gods because it is pious, or pious because loved? Goodness is independent of divine will; gods must be pious. He redefines worship as philosophical self-examination. In *Apology*, he affirms belief in gods, oracles, and the soul’s immortality (*Phaedo*). Xenophon’s *Memorabilia* gives a teleological argument: cosmic "forethought" (eyelids) implies a wise, benevolent creator. He speaks of "god" (singular) and "gods" (plural)—possibly a supreme deity commanding subordinates. McPherran: Socrates filters divine signs through reason. Long: separating "religious" and "rational" is anachronistic.

#### *Daimonion*
An inner voice, present since childhood, that *only* inhibits intended actions, never commands. It deterred him from politics. Interpretations: rational intuition, subconscious impulse, dream, paranormal experience. Accusers cited it as "introducing new divinities."

#### Virtue and Knowledge
All virtues are one because all are forms of knowledge. Courage = knowledge of what is truly dangerous. Aristotle confirms Socrates sought definitions because he thought virtues were sciences (*epistēmai*).

#### Love
In *Lysis*, Socrates argues we love only what is useful to us (egoistic). In *Symposium*, he attributes a ladder of love (from bodies to Forms) to Diotima (possibly Aspasia). He claims dual love for Alcibiades and philosophy; Alcibiades admits failing to seduce him. Scholars debate whether Socratic love is egoistic, rational, or allows non-egoistic spousal love.

#### Political Philosophy
Socrates practiced "true politics": improving citizens’ souls, not gratifying voters (*Gorgias* 521d). He obeyed laws, served militarily, but avoided assemblies. He disobeyed the Thirty Tyrants (oligarchs) but respected democratic laws. Evidence for democratic leanings: refusal to arrest Leon, acceptance of death penalty. Evidence for oligarchic leanings: anti-democratic pupils, contempt for majority opinion, *Protagoras* passages. Liberal reading (*Crito*): citizens are autonomous, consent to laws by staying, but may disobey unjust orders after reflection. Popper: Socratic individualism supports "open society." Strauss: elitist, truths indigestible by masses. Arendt: questioning prevents "banality of evil."

### Legacy

#### Classical Antiquity
All post-Socratic schools (except Epicureans, Pyrrhonists) claimed him. Plato’s Academy, Aristotle’s Lyceum, Cynics (Antisthenes → Diogenes: virtue = only good), Cyrenaics (Aristippus: hedonism, skepticism of external knowledge), Megarians (Euclid: virtue + Parmenidean monism), Stoics (Socratic method for consistency, virtue = happiness, knowledge-based ethics), Academic Skeptics (Arcesilaus: Socratic ignorance as epochē). Stoics and Skeptics fought over Socrates’ heir; Stoics won by redefining ignorance as irony. Epicureans attacked his *daimonion*, irony, and character. Pyrrhonists called him a mock-humble prater.

#### Medieval World
Arabic translations (Al-Kindi, Jabir ibn Hayyan, Muʿtazila) brought Socrates to Islam. Muslim scholars admired his ethics-lifestyle unity (paralleling Muhammad), reinterpreted him as monotheist and otherworldly. In Byzantium, Christian scholars (Lactantius, Eusebius, Augustine) preserved him. Latin translations post-1453 revived him in West. Early Renaissance humanists (Bruni, Manetti) crafted a civic, republican Socrates, censoring pederasty and *daimonion* as god. Neoplatonists (Ficino) saw a holy Socrates, *daimonion* as divine wisdom, ignorance as God-given.

#### Modern Times
Montaigne: rationalist counter to fanaticism. Voltaire: reason-based theist. Hegel: Socrates introduces "free subjectivity" (self-determination), a world-historical turn; yet Athens was right to condemn him for undermining *Sittlichkeit* (ethical life). Kierkegaard: Socrates as pure ironist, teacher of humility; only *Apology* is authentic. Kierkegaard models his own task on Socrates: "audit the definition of a Christian." Nietzsche: *Birth of Tragedy* blames Socrates for killing Greek tragedy, replacing naturalism with rationalism, equating reason = virtue = happiness—a "Socratic culture" persisting to modernity. *Twilight of Idols* attacks the identity doctrine. Kaufmann argued Nietzsche admired Socrates; consensus: ambivalence. Post-WWII: Arendt (questioning prevents evil), Strauss (elitist critique of *polis*), Popper (Socratic individualism = open society).

## Terms
- ***Elenchus*** — Socrates’ method of refutation: questioning an interlocutor to expose contradictions in their beliefs, typically ending in *aporia* (impasse).
- ***Aporia*** — State of puzzlement or impasse reached when the *elenchus* shows one’s beliefs are inconsistent.
- ***Daimonion*** — Socrates’ "divine sign"—an inner inhibitory voice that warns him against errors but never gives positive commands.
- ***Asebeia*** — Impiety; the legal charge against Socrates for not believing in city gods and introducing new divinities.
- ***Eudaimonia*** — Human flourishing or happiness; the ultimate end of all action in Socratic ethics.
- ***Virtue intellectualism*** — Thesis that virtue is knowledge; therefore, knowing the good entails doing the good, and *akrasia* is impossible.
- ***Euthyphro dilemma*** — Socrates’ question: is the pious loved by gods because it is pious, or pious because loved? Challenges divine command theory.
- ***Logoi sokratikoi*** — "Socratic dialogues"; the literary genre (coined by Aristotle) depicting Socrates in conversation.
- ***Socratic problem*** — The scholarly difficulty of reconstructing the historical Socrates’ views from contradictory ancient sources.
- ***Sittlichkeit*** — Hegelian term for the substantive ethical life embodied in a state’s institutions and customs.

## Debates and open questions
1. **Historical Socrates vs. literary Socrates**: Can we recover the historical figure? Vlastos (early Plato = historical); Kahn (only *Apology*); most now study the literary character.
2. **Nature of the *elenchus***: Is it purely negative (exposing inconsistency) or constructive (yielding positive knowledge)?
3. **Socratic ignorance**: Literal (Irwin), pedagogical irony (Gulley), or semantic dualism (Vlastos: Knowledge-C vs. Knowledge-E)?
4. **Unity of virtue**: Are all virtues one (knowledge) or distinct? Does Socrates hold the "identity" or "sufficiency" thesis regarding virtue and *eudaimonia*?
5. **Motivation for trial**: Religious nonconformity, political threat (anti-democratic associates), or inseparable religious-political charges?
6. **Political stance**: Democrat (obeyed laws, refused oligarchs), oligarch (pupils, contempt for masses), liberal (consent, civil disobedience), or republican (active citizenship)?
7. **Nature of *daimonion***: Rational intuition, psychological phenomenon, divine voice, or literary device?
8. **Socratic love**: Egoistic (utility-based) or open to non-egoistic forms? Is Diotima historical (Aspasia) or fictional?
9. **Legacy ownership**: Stoics (knowledge-ethics) vs. Skeptics (ignorance) in antiquity; civic humanist vs. holy sage in Renaissance; rationalist hero vs. destroyer of tragedy in modernity.
