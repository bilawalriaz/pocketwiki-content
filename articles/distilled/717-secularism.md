# Secularism

## Overview  
Secularism is the principle of organizing human affairs according to naturalistic, material considerations rather than religious authority, most commonly expressed as the separation of religion from civil and state affairs. It is not inherently opposed to religion but seeks to minimize religion’s role in public life, and can range from hostility to neutrality to hospitality toward religious belief. Distinct national models—American, French, Turkish, and Indian—reflect different balances between state authority, religious freedom, and public policy, making secularism a contested yet central concept in modern democratic governance.

## Timeline  
- **c. 1851** — Term "secularism" coined by British agnostic writer George Holyoake
- **1636** — Roger Williams founded Providence Plantations with total religious freedom
- **1787–1791** — U.S. Constitution and Bill of Rights incorporate Lockean secular ideas
- **1790s** — Revolutionary France replaces Catholic influence with Cult of Reason
- **1905** — French law enshrines Laïcité, establishing strict state-religion separation
- **1923–1930s** — Mustafa Kemal Atatürk establishes Kemalist secularism (laiklik) in Turkey
- **1947** — India becomes a secular state after independence
- **1948** — Universal Declaration of Human Rights protects freedom of religion

## Body  

### Variations and Principles  
Secularism takes multiple forms depending on how strictly religion is separated from public life. The strictest form, associated with French laïcité, demands total state distance from all religions and non-religious convictions. A more humanistic variant emphasizes evidence-based policy and non-discrimination among religious and non-religious groups. A liberal or "pillarized" model allows state support for religions if applied equally, as seen in parts of Europe. Core principles include legal equality regardless of belief, separation of church and state, rule of law over religious law, freedom of thought, and freedom from religion. The term "pseudo-secularism" is used pejoratively in Indian discourse to criticize policies perceived as favoring one religion despite claims of neutrality.

### Historical Development  
Though rooted in ancient distinctions between sacred and secular authority, modern secularism emerged in the early modern period as functional differentiation reduced religion’s role as the primary lens for interpreting reality. Key thinkers—including Augustine, Ockham, Marsilius of Padua, Luther, Williams, Locke, and Talleyrand—shaped its philosophical foundations. In Europe, Enlightenment ideals promoted state supremacy and anti-clericalism, leading to revolutionary challenges to Catholic dominance. George Holyoake coined "secularism" in 1851 to advocate naturalistic living without necessarily rejecting religion, distinguishing it from atheism. Over time, especially in the 20th century, secularism became institutionalized through laws like France’s 1905 separation act and Atatürk’s reforms in Turkey, while India adopted a pluralist model after 1947.

### State Frameworks  
Political secularism manifests in four main frameworks. Separationist secularism, exemplified by the U.S., bars state support for any religion and enforces neutrality. Laïcité, used in France, asserts legal state supremacy over religion and restricts religious expression in public spaces. Kemalist secularism adapts laïcité to Turkey’s context. Accommodationism permits general religious support without favoring specific sects, as practiced in India and increasingly in the U.S. State atheism, associated with Marxist regimes, bans religious practice entirely and is debated as a form of secularism due to its suppression of belief. Each framework reflects different balances between limiting religious influence and protecting individual conscience.

### Contemporary Challenges  
Modern democracies are widely regarded as secular due to strong religious freedom protections and minimal clerical political influence. However, surveys indicate varying public comfort with religion’s role in public life—higher in the U.S., lower in Europe. Secularism correlates with social progressivism and urban, educated demographics, though it remains a minority identity in many communities. Critics argue secular ethics lack moral grounding, while scholars like John Rawls have shifted focus toward "overlapping consensus"—a framework allowing diverse life-stance groups to agree on basic principles without invoking secularism directly. Despite this, secularism persists as a foundational yet contested ideal in global political theory.

## Terms  
- **Laïcité**: French model of strict separation between state and religion, emphasizing state legal supremacy over religious institutions.  
- **Kemalist secularism (laiklik)**: Turkish adaptation of laïcité introduced by Mustafa Kemal Atatürk in the 1920s–30s to modernize society.  
- **Accommodationism**: System where the state supports religion generally without favoring specific sects; used in India and historically in the U.S.  
- **Separationist secularism**: Model requiring complete separation of church and state, with no state endorsement of religion; dominant in the U.S.  
- **State atheism**: Total ban on religious practice enforced by the state, typically linked to Marxist-Leninist regimes.  
- **Pseudo-secularism**: Pejorative term in Indian politics criticizing policies claimed to favor one religion despite secular rhetoric.  
- **Overlapping consensus**: Political philosophy concept, advanced by John Rawls, describing agreement among diverse groups on basic societal principles.  
- **Functional differentiation**: Process by which religion loses its role as the dominant framework for interpreting reality, becoming one institution among others.  

## Debates and Open Questions  
Scholars debate whether secularism inherently opposes religion or merely regulates its public role, with some viewing it as neutral and others as anti-religious. The extent to which secularism promotes genuine pluralism versus marginalizing religious voices remains contested, particularly in contexts like India where accommodationism raises questions about uniform civil codes. Additionally, the relevance of secularism in an era of resurgent religious influence has sparked discussions around "post-secularism." Finally, the shift in academic discourse from secularism to concepts like overlapping consensus raises questions about whether secularism retains analytical utility or has been superseded by alternative frameworks in political theory.
