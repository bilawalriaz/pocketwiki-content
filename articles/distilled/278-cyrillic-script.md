# Cyrillic script

## Overview
The Cyrillic script is a writing system developed in the 9th century AD at the Preslav Literary School in the First Bulgarian Empire. It was created by disciples of the Byzantine missionaries Cyril and Methodius—who had earlier devised the Glagolitic script—by adapting Greek uncial letters and adding Glagolitic characters for Slavic sounds absent in Greek. Named in honor of Saint Cyril, the script became the vehicle for Old Church Slavonic, spreading as the liturgical and literary lingua franca of the Orthodox Slavic world. Over the millennium, it was reformed for secular use (notably by Peter the Great in Russia, 1708–10) and adapted for over 50 Slavic and non-Slavic languages across Eurasia. As of 2019, roughly 250 million people use Cyrillic as an official national script; with Bulgaria’s 2007 EU accession, it became the EU’s third official script.

## Timeline
- **9th century AD** — Early Cyrillic alphabet developed at Preslav Literary School in First Bulgarian Empire under Tsar Simeon I.
- **890s** — Disciples of Cyril and Methodius (Clement of Ohrid, Naum of Preslav, et al.) finalize script based on Greek uncial plus Glagolitic letters.
- **12th century** — Cyrillic surpasses Glagolitic as dominant script for Church Slavonic.
- **14th–15th centuries** — Tarnovo Literary School (Evtimiy of Tarnovo, Gregory Tsamblak) exerts "second South-Slavic influence" on Russian, Serbian, Wallachian, Moldavian culture.
- **1708–1710** — Peter the Great introduces Civil script in Russia: westernized letterforms, upper/lower case distinction, archaic letters removed.
- **Early 19th century** — Vuk Stefanović Karadžić reforms Serbian Cyrillic: removes obsolete graphemes, adds Љ Њ Ђ Ћ Џ Ј for vernacular phonemes.
- **1860s** — Romanian officially switches from Cyrillic to Latin script.
- **1991** — Dissolution of USSR triggers script transitions in several successor states.
- **2007** — Bulgaria joins EU; Cyrillic becomes third official EU script after Latin and Greek.
- **4 April 2008** — Unicode 5.1 released, adding Cyrillic Extended-A/B blocks for early Cyrillic and minority languages.
- **2025** — Kazakhstan completes transition from Cyrillic to Latin script.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
The Early Cyrillic alphabet emerged in the First Bulgarian Empire during the reign of Tsar Simeon I the Great (893–927). Scholars at the Preslav Literary School—including Naum of Preslav, Constantine of Preslav, Joan Ekzarh, and Chernorizets Hrabar—adapted the Greek uncial script, augmenting it with ligatures and consonants from the older Glagolitic alphabet to represent Slavic sounds not found in Greek (e.g., *š, č, ž, jat’*). Unlike the Ohrid school, which retained Glagolitic, Preslav scholars favored Greek models. The script is named for Saint Cyril (Constantine the Philosopher) as homage, not authorship; Cyril and Methodius had created Glagolitic, while their Bulgarian disciples developed Cyrillic in the 890s. The earliest datable inscriptions appear at Preslav, Patleina Monastery, Ravna Monastery, and Varna Monastery (modern Shumen and Varna provinces).

### Spread and Liturgical Role
Cyrillic became the standard script for Church Slavonic (especially Old Church Slavonic), the liturgical language of the Orthodox Slavic world. Literature produced in Old Church Slavonic spread north from Bulgaria, becoming the lingua franca of the Balkans and Eastern Europe. The script was adopted for non-Slavic languages such as Romanian (until the 1860s) and used by Catholic and Muslim Slavs. A distinct Bosnian Cyrillic variant (poljičica) appeared by the 10th–11th century (Humac tablet) and persisted until the 18th century, with sporadic 20th-century use. In the 14th–15th centuries, the Tarnovo Literary School’s orthographic reforms—led by Patriarch Evtimiy of Tarnovo, Gregory Tsamblak, and Constantine of Kostenets—influenced Russian, Serbian, Wallachian, and Moldavian culture, a phenomenon known in Russia as the second South-Slavic influence.

### Major Reforms
**Peter the Great’s Civil Script (1708–10).** After his Grand Embassy to Western Europe, Peter mandated westernized letterforms closer to Latin type. Archaic letters were abolished; new letters were designed (some by Peter himself); upper- and lowercase distinction was introduced; and West European typography practices were adopted. The pre-reform *poluustav* forms were retained in Church Slavonic and persist for archaic effect.

**Vuk Karadžić’s Serbian Reform (early 19th c.).** Karadžić removed graphemes no longer pronounced in the vernacular and introduced five new letters—Љ (lj), Њ (nj), Ђ (đ), Ћ (ć), Џ (dž), Ј (j)—to match Serbian phonology, distancing the alphabet from Church Slavonic.

**Soviet and Post-Soviet Policies.** The USSR promoted Cyrillic for minority languages across the Caucasus, Central Asia, and Siberia. After 1991, Azerbaijan, Turkmenistan, and Moldova (except Transnistria) completed transitions to Latin; Uzbekistan uses both; Kazakhstan finalized its switch in 2025. Russia mandates Cyrillic for all federal public communications, a policy contested by some groups (e.g., Chechen separatists use Latin).

### Letterforms and Typography
Early Cyrillic manuscripts did not distinguish majuscule/minuscule. Letters served as numerals, inheriting values from Greek ancestors. Ligatures were common: *Yeri* (Ы) originated as Yer + I (Ъ+І); iotation used ligatures with І (Ꙗ, Ѥ, Ю, Ѩ, Ѭ). Letterforms evolved from medieval *vyaz’* (tall, narrow, shared strokes) directly to late Baroque, skipping a Renaissance phase. Peter’s Civil script imposed Latinate proportions; modern Cyrillic typefaces now share design principles with Latin families (serif placement, stroke endings, weight rules). Upright (*pryamoy shrift*) and italic (*kursiv*) forms follow German nomenclature. In italic, many lowercase glyphs differ sharply from upright (e.g., ⟨т⟩ resembles a small-capital T; ⟨д⟩ may resemble Latin ⟨g⟩). Serbian/Macedonian and Bulgarian standards permit distinct italic and upright shapes reflecting handwriting and Latin-like rounded arcs.

### Alphabets and Language Coverage
Cyrillic is the standard script for Slavic languages: Belarusian, Bulgarian, Macedonian, Pannonian Rusyn, Russian, Rusyn, Serbo-Croatian (Bosnian, Montenegrin, Serbian), Ukrainian. It also serves non-Slavic languages of Russia (e.g., Tatar, Chechen, Chuvash), the Caucasus (Abkhaz, Ossetian), Central Asia (Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Mongolian), and historically Alaska and Siberia. The first Cyrillic-derived alphabet was Abur (Komi); others include the Molodtsov alphabet (Komi) and numerous Caucasian alphabets.

### Romanization and Cyrillization
Multiple transliteration systems exist: Scientific (linguistics, based on Serbo-Croatian Latin), UN-recommended per language, ISO 9:1995, ALA-LC (North American libraries), BGN/PCGN (US/UK geographic names), GOST 7.79-2000 (replacing Soviet GOST 16876), and informal “translit”/“volapuk” for limited character sets. *Cyrillization* denotes representing non-Cyrillic writing systems with Cyrillic letters.

### Computer Encoding
Unicode (v.17.0) encodes Cyrillic across seven blocks: Cyrillic (U+0400–U+04FF), Supplement (U+0500–U+052F), Extended-A (U+2DE0–U+2DFF), Extended-B (U+A640–U+A69F), Extended-C (U+1C80–U+1C8F), Extended-D (U+1E030–U+1E08F), plus Phonetic Extensions and Combining Half Marks. Unicode avoids precomposed accented letters except where they constitute distinct alphabet letters (Й, Ў, Ё, Ї, Ѓ, Ќ, etc.) or distinguish homonyms (Ѐ, Ѝ in Bulgarian/Macedonian). Combining diacritics (e.g., U+0301 acute) mark stress/length. Legacy 8-bit encodings persist: Windows-1251, KOI8-R/KOI8-U, CP866, ISO/IEC 8859-5, MIK. Keyboard layouts follow national typewriter standards; phonetic/QWERTY layouts exist for non-native typists.

### Currency Signs Derived from Cyrillic
- Ukrainian hryvnia (₴) from cursive minuscule *he* (г).
- Russian ruble (₽) from majuscule *er* (Р).
- Kyrgyzstani som (С) from majuscule *es* (С).
- Kazakhstani tenge (₸) from *te* (Т).
- Mongolian tögrög (₮) from *te* (Т).

## Terms
- ****Glagolitic script**** — The earliest Slavic alphabet, created by Cyril and Methodius in the 860s; source of non-Greek letters in Cyrillic.
- ****Uncial**** — A majuscule script used in Greek and Latin manuscripts (3rd–8th c.), basis for Cyrillic letter shapes.
- ****Preslav Literary School**** — The primary cultural center of the First Bulgarian Empire where Early Cyrillic was developed (890s).
- ****Old Church Slavonic**** — The first literary Slavic language, written in Glagolitic and later Cyrillic; liturgical lingua franca of the Orthodox Slavs.
- ****Civil script (гражданский шрифт)**** — Peter the Great’s 1708–10 reformed Russian alphabet with Latinate forms, case distinction, and reduced letter set.
- ****Poluustav (полуустав)**** — Pre-reform Cyrillic bookhand (14th–17th c.) retained in Church Slavonic; tall, narrow, with ligatures.
- ****Vyaz’ (вязь)**** — Late medieval ornamental Cyrillic style: compressed, tall letters with shared strokes; used in icon inscriptions.
- ****Pryamoy shrift (прямой шрифт)**** — Native term for upright/roman type (cf. German *Normalschrift*).
- ****Kursiv (курсив)**** — Native term for italic type (from German *Kursive*), not cursive handwriting.
- ****Cyrillization**** — Rendering a non-Cyrillic writing system using Cyrillic letters.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authorship of Early Cyrillic:** Whether Cyril himself contributed to the script or it was entirely the work of his disciples in Preslav (Clement, Naum, Constantine, et al.) remains debated; the prevailing view attributes development to the Preslav school in the 890s.
- **Bosnian Cyrillic (Poljičica) origins and status:** Paleographers date its emergence to the 10th–11th century (Humac tablet), but its classification—as a distinct script, a regional variant, or a transitional form—is disputed.
- **Script politics in the post-Soviet space:** Transitions between Cyrillic and Latin (e.g., Kazakhstan’s 2025 switch, Chechnya’s competing mandates) reflect unresolved tensions between national identity, regional integration, and Russian federal policy.
- **Unicode support for historical and minority orthographies:** Despite Unicode 5.1/17.0 expansions, some Church Slavonic and early Cyrillic combinations remain incompletely supported; font fallback and OpenType *locl* feature implementation vary across platforms.
- **Typography standardization vs. regional variation:** The tension between unified Unicode code points and language-specific glyph shapes (Serbian/Macedonian vs. Russian vs. Bulgarian italics) creates rendering ambiguities when language tagging is absent.
