# Robert Fulton

## Overview
Robert Fulton (1765–1815) was an American engineer and inventor whose work fundamentally transformed transportation and warfare. He is best known for developing the first commercially successful steamboat, the *North River Steamboat* (or *Clermont*), which revolutionized river travel and trade in the United States. His career also included pioneering work on submarines and naval torpedoes.

## Body
### Early Life and European Career
Fulton was born into a farming family in Pennsylvania. After his father's death in 1785, he supported his family through painting before moving to Europe in 1786 for his health. In London, he lived with the artist Benjamin West and gained commissions as a painter. He soon became involved in "Canal Mania," patenting designs for inclined planes and dredging machines, though his practical canal work was unsuccessful.

### Submarine and Torpedo Development
While living in Paris from 1797, Fulton designed the *Nautilus*, the first practical muscle-powered submarine. After initial rejection, he secured French government support to build it; it was tested in 1800. Later, while working for the British government (1804–1806), he developed early naval "torpedoes" (modern mines) and tested them during the 1804 Raid on Boulogne with limited success.

### The Commercial Steamboat
Fulton's most significant achievement came after his return to the U.S. In partnership with Robert R. Livingston, he built the *North River Steamboat* (*Clermont*). On its maiden voyage in 1807, it traveled the 150 nautical miles from New York City to Albany in 32 hours, proving the commercial viability of steam-powered river travel. The success of the *Clermont* changed trade and transportation on major American rivers.

### Later Work and Legacy
Fulton continued his work on steam-powered vessels, helping to launch the *New Orleans* on the Mississippi River in 1812, a voyage that opened the heartland to steamboat commerce. He was also a member of the Erie Canal Commission and designed the *Demologos*, the first steam-driven warship, which was completed after his death. Fulton died in 1815 from pneumonia after rescuing a friend from the icy Hudson River.

## Terms
- **Commercially successful steamboat**: A steam-powered vessel used for profitable passenger or freight transport, as opposed to earlier experimental models.
- **Inclined plane**: A type of canal lift using a sloped plane to move boats between different water levels, an alternative to traditional locks.
- **Submarine (Nautilus)**: The first practical, human-powered submersible vessel, designed by Fulton and tested in 1800.
- **Naval torpedo**: In Fulton's time, this term referred to what are now called naval mines—underwater explosives designed to damage ships.
- **Paddle wheel**: A large wheel with paddles, turned by a steam engine, used to propel a boat through water.
- **Erie Canal Commission**: A body appointed to oversee the construction of the Erie Canal, a major waterway connecting the Hudson River to the Great Lakes.
- **Demologos**: The world's first steam-powered warship, designed by Fulton for the U.S. Navy during the War of 1812.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Priority of invention**: While Fulton is credited with the first *commercially successful* steamboat, earlier steam-powered vessels existed. The article notes that Claude de Jouffroy tested a paddle steamer in France in 1783, and John Fitch made a successful trial run in America in 1787.
- **Patent disputes**: Some historians have argued that Joseph-Philibert Desblanc's 1802 steamship patent had priority over Fulton's 1809 patent, though this claim was not upheld by authorities.
