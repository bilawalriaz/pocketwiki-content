# Balfour Declaration

## Overview
The Balfour Declaration was a 1917 statement by the British government supporting the establishment of a "national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine. Issued during World War I, it was the first public endorsement of Zionism by a major power. The declaration became a foundational document for the British Mandate for Palestine and is considered a principal cause of the ongoing Israeli–Palestinian conflict.

## Timeline
- **Nov 1914** — Britain declares war on the Ottoman Empire; Cabinet first discusses Palestine.
- **Jan 1915** — Herbert Samuel circulates a memorandum proposing British support for Zionist ambitions.
- **May 1916** — The secret Sykes-Picot Agreement is drafted, proposing international administration for Palestine.
- **Dec 1916** — David Lloyd George becomes Prime Minister, favoring partition of the Ottoman Empire.
- **Feb 1917** — First formal British-Zionist negotiations occur between Sir Mark Sykes and Zionist leaders.
- **Jun 1917** — Foreign Secretary Arthur Balfour asks Lord Rothschild and Chaim Weizmann to draft a declaration.
- **Oct 1917** — British War Cabinet approves the final text of the declaration.
- **Nov 2, 1917** — Balfour sends the declaration in a letter to Lord Rothschild.
- **Nov 9, 1917** — The text of the declaration is published in the press.
- **Dec 1917** — British forces capture Jerusalem.
- **Apr 1920** — The San Remo conference assigns the Palestine Mandate to Britain, incorporating the declaration.
- **Sep 1923** — The British Mandate for Palestine is formally ratified, legally enshrining the declaration.

## Body

### Background and Motivations
The declaration emerged from a confluence of factors during World War I. British officials, influenced by a mix of geopolitical calculation and "patrician anti-Semitism" that overestimated Jewish global power, believed support for Zionism would rally Jewish communities in the United States and Russia to the Allied cause and counter German propaganda. The policy was championed by a new government under Lloyd George, which favored partitioning the Ottoman Empire, unlike the previous Asquith administration. Zionist leaders, particularly Chaim Weizmann, skillfully lobbied British officials, while the British sought to pre-empt French claims to Palestine under the secret Sykes-Picot Agreement.

### Drafting and Content
The declaration was a short, carefully worded statement. The key phrase, "national home for the Jewish people," was intentionally vague and had no precedent in international law, avoiding the term "state" to appease internal opposition. The wording "in Palestine" (not "of Palestine") was a deliberate amendment to avoid committing to the entire territory. Two "safeguard clauses" were added to address concerns: one protecting the "civil and religious rights" of the existing non-Jewish communities (who were about 90% of the population), and another safeguarding the rights of Jews outside Palestine. Notably, the political rights of the Arab majority were not mentioned.

### Reaction and Implementation
The declaration galvanized the Zionist movement worldwide but was vehemently opposed by the Arab population of Palestine, who saw it as a betrayal by a European power ignoring the wishes of the native majority. It also created a "dual obligation" for Britain that proved impossible to fulfill. The declaration was incorporated into the League of Nations Mandate for Palestine, making it a legally binding international obligation. However, British policy oscillated, culminating in the 1939 White Paper that restricted Jewish immigration and was seen by Zionists as a repudiation of the declaration.

### Long-Term Impact
The declaration is widely regarded as a root cause of the Israeli–Palestinian conflict. It indirectly led to the emergence of the State of Israel in 1948 but also to decades of intercommunal violence and regional war. Historians debate whether it was a cynical imperial maneuver or a sincere, if flawed, attempt to address Jewish persecution. Its legacy remains deeply contested, with the British government officially acknowledging in 2017 that it should have called for the protection of Palestinian Arabs' political rights.

## Terms
- **Zionism**: A nationalist movement arising in the late 19th century for the re-establishment of a Jewish homeland in Palestine.
- **Mandate for Palestine**: A League of Nations mandate granted to Britain after WWI, with the explicit purpose of implementing the Balfour Declaration.
- **Sykes-Picot Agreement**: A 1916 secret treaty between Britain and France dividing proposed spheres of influence in the Middle East after the Ottoman Empire's defeat.
- **McMahon-Hussein Correspondence**: A series of 1915-16 letters in which Britain appeared to promise Arab independence in exchange for an Arab revolt against the Ottomans, with the status of Palestine later disputed.
- **National Home**: The deliberately ambiguous term used in the declaration, not equivalent to a sovereign state, to describe the Jewish aspiration in Palestine.
- **Aliyah**: A term for immigration to the Land of Israel; the First Aliyah (1882-1903) was the first modern wave of Jewish immigration to Palestine.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Primary Motivation**: Was the declaration driven primarily by sincere sympathy for Zionism, or by pragmatic imperial interests (e.g., winning the war, securing the route to India, countering France)?
- **Contradictory Promises**: Did the declaration contradict earlier British promises to the Arabs in the McMahon-Hussein Correspondence? Historians note the agreements were intentionally ambiguous and "intended to bear more than one interpretation."
- **Meaning of "National Home"**: Did British leaders intend for the declaration to eventually lead to a Jewish state? Private statements from figures like Lloyd George and Churchill suggest many did, while official policy remained ambiguous.
- **Authorship**: While Balfour's name is on it, scholars debate the primary author, with candidates including Lord Milner and Leo Amery for drafting key amendments.
- **Ongoing Legacy**: The declaration is considered by many a "principal cause" of the intractable Israeli-Palestinian conflict, while others argue it laid the foundation for the only Jewish state.
