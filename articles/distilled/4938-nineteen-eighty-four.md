# Nineteen Eighty-Four

## Overview
*Nineteen Eighty-Four* is a dystopian novel by George Orwell, published in 1949, that depicts a totalitarian superstate's use of mass surveillance, historical revisionism, and psychological torture to control its population. It is a seminal work of political fiction, exploring the manipulation of truth and the nature of power, and has profoundly influenced language and concepts related to authoritarianism.

## Timeline
- **1943-1944** — Orwell first conceives the novel, inspired by the Tehran Conference and the division of the world into "Zones of Influence."
- **1946** — Orwell begins writing on the remote Scottish island of Jura.
- **November 1947** — Completes the first draft while seriously ill with tuberculosis.
- **November 1948** — Finishes the second draft.
- **4 December 1948** — Sends the final manuscript to his publisher, Secker & Warburg.
- **8 June 1949** — Published in the UK; Orwell dies in September of that year.
- **13 June 1949** — Published in the US by Harcourt Brace.
- **1950s** — Banned in the Soviet Union; smuggled copies circulate among dissidents.
- **1984** — The film adaptation starring John Hurt is released.
- **1 January 2021** — Enters the public domain in most countries.

## Body

### Plot and Setting
The novel is set in a fictional future, believed to be 1984, in the superstate of Oceania, which includes the province of Airstrip One (formerly Great Britain). Society is ruled by the Party under the ideology of Ingsoc (English Socialism), led by the omnipresent figurehead Big Brother. The protagonist, Winston Smith, works at the Ministry of Truth, where he rewrites historical records to match the Party's current narrative. He secretly rebels by keeping a diary and begins a love affair with Julia, a fellow Party member. They believe they are contacted by O'Brien, an Inner Party member they think is part of a resistance group called the Brotherhood. However, O'Brien is a Thought Police agent; he and Julia are captured, separated, and tortured in the Ministry of Love. Winston is broken in Room 101, betrays Julia, and is ultimately released, having learned to love Big Brother.

### Conception and Writing
Orwell's ideas for the novel emerged between 1943 and 1944, influenced by the Tehran Conference and the emerging Cold War division of the world. He was also aware of earlier dystopian works like Yevgeny Zamyatin's *We* and Aldous Huxley's *Brave New World*. Orwell wrote much of the book on the remote island of Jura while suffering from severe tuberculosis. He completed the final manuscript in late 1948, retyping it himself due to his deteriorating health. The title was chosen over his earlier option, *The Last Man in Europe*.

### Themes and Inspirations
The novel's primary themes are totalitarianism, surveillance, and the manipulation of truth. Orwell drew direct inspiration from Stalinist Russia and wartime Britain. The Party's practices—such as the show trials, the cult of personality around Big Brother, and the constant rewriting of history—mirror Soviet tactics. The concept of "2 + 2 = 5" was a real communist slogan. The perpetual war between Oceania, Eurasia, and Eastasia reflects the shifting alliances of the Cold War. The novel also explores nationalism, censorship, and the stark inequality between the ruling Inner Party, the Outer Party, and the impoverished proles.

### Critical Reception and Legacy
Upon publication, *Nineteen Eighty-Four* received widespread critical acclaim for its originality and power, though some critics like C. S. Lewis and Isaac Deutscher found it flawed or politically simplistic. It was interpreted by some as a critique of both Stalinism and British socialism. The novel has had an immense cultural impact, popularizing terms like "Big Brother," "Thought Police," "doublethink," and "Newspeak." The adjective "Orwellian" entered the language to describe totalitarian practices. It remains a touchstone for discussions of privacy, propaganda, and government overreach, with sales surging during events like the 2013 NSA surveillance leaks.

## Terms
*   **Ingsoc**: The official ideology of Oceania, a Newspeak abbreviation for "English Socialism."
*   **Newspeak**: The Party's engineered language designed to limit the range of thought.
*   **Doublethink**: The ability to hold two contradictory beliefs simultaneously and accept both.
*   **Thought Police**: The secret police force responsible for uncovering and punishing thoughtcrime.
*   **Thoughtcrime**: The criminal act of holding unspoken beliefs or doubts that oppose the Party.
*   **Unperson**: A person who has been "vaporized" by the state, erased from all records and existence.
*   **Memory Hole**: A chute used to destroy inconvenient documents and historical evidence.
*   **Telescreen**: A two-way television that broadcasts propaganda and simultaneously surveils citizens.
*   **Room 101**: The final punishment chamber in the Ministry of Love, containing a prisoner's worst fear.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **The Epilogue's Meaning**: The appendix "Principles of Newspeak," written in the past tense, has led some critics to speculate it implies the eventual fall of the Party and the failure of Newspeak.
*   **The Reality of Big Brother and the Brotherhood**: The novel never confirms whether Big Brother exists as a person or if the Brotherhood resistance movement is real or a Party fabrication.
*   **Orwell's Political Intent**: While Orwell described the book as a satire on the "perversions to which a centralised economy is liable," debates persist about whether it is primarily an anti-communist, anti-socialist, or anti-totalitarian work.
*   **Comparison with *Brave New World***: A major scholarly debate contrasts Orwell's vision of control through pain and fear with Aldous Huxley's vision in *Brave New World* of control through pleasure and distraction.
