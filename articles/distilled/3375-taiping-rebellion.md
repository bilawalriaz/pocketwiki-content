# Taiping Rebellion

## Overview
The Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864) was a massive civil war in Qing China led by Hong Xiuquan, who established a Christian theocratic state in Nanjing. It was one of the deadliest conflicts in human history, with an estimated 20–30 million deaths, and severely weakened the Qing dynasty, accelerating the decentralization of power that contributed to the later Warlord Era.

## Timeline
- **1837–1843** — Hong Xiuquan fails imperial exams, has visions, interprets them via Christian pamphlets.
- **1844** — Feng Yunshan founds the God Worshipping Society.
- **January 1851** — Taiping forces rout Qing troops at Jintian; Hong declares himself Heavenly King.
- **March 1853** — Taiping capture Nanjing, renaming it Tianjing ("heavenly capital").
- **1853–1855** — Failed Northern Expedition against Beijing.
- **September 1856** — Tianjing Incident: internal purge kills Yang Xiuqing and thousands.
- **1860** — Taiping defeat Qing siege of Nanjing, seize wealthy Jiangsu/Zhejiang region.
- **1861–1862** — Zeng Guofan's Xiang Army captures Anqing, begins siege of Nanjing.
- **June 1864** — Hong Xiuquan dies; Nanjing falls to Qing forces in July.
- **August 1871** — Last Taiping army defeated in southern China.

## Body

### Origins and Ideology
The rebellion arose from late-Qing crises: famine, overpopulation, economic distress, and defeat in the First Opium War. Hong Xiuquan, a Hakka who failed civil service exams, had visions he later interpreted through Christian tracts as a divine mandate. He proclaimed himself the younger brother of Jesus Christ and sought to overthrow the Qing dynasty and establish a "Taiping Heavenly Kingdom" based on his syncretic "God Worshipping" faith, which blended Christianity with Chinese traditions. The movement initially grew by suppressing bandits but evolved into a full-scale war after Qing persecution.

### Course of the War
After capturing Nanjing in 1853, the Taiping state controlled significant parts of southern China. However, internal strife, notably the 1856 Tianjing Incident, weakened it. The Qing victory was achieved primarily through decentralized provincial armies, especially Zeng Guofan's Xiang Army, which employed loyal, locally-recruited troops. The conflict was a "total war," with both sides committing widespread massacres and scorched-earth tactics. The Taiping were ultimately besieged in Nanjing, which fell in 1864 after Hong's death.

### Policies and Society
The Taiping regime enacted radical social reforms: strict gender separation, abolition of foot binding, land socialization, and the suppression of opium and traditional Confucian/Buddhist practices. They destroyed temples and libraries. Their army, initially including many women, was disciplined and fanatical. They were well-equipped with modern weapons purchased from foreign suppliers.

### Relationship with Western Powers
Western powers remained officially neutral but were ambivalent. Some officials and missionaries were initially optimistic about the Christian Taiping, but after inspections revealed brutality and unorthodox practices, opinion turned negative. The British and French eventually provided limited military support to the Qing, fearing a strong, unified China under the Taiping.

### Aftermath and Legacy
The rebellion's suppression required the rise of Han-led provincial armies, decentralizing Qing power and paving the way for the Warlord Era. The death toll caused severe depopulation in the Yangtze delta. The Taiping example influenced later revolutionaries, including Sun Yat-sen. The conflict is also studied for its proto-communist social ideals and its role in weakening the Qing state alongside other 19th-century rebellions.

## Terms
- **Taiping Heavenly Kingdom**: The theocratic state established by Hong Xiuquan, with Nanjing (renamed Tianjing) as its capital.
- **God Worshipping Society**: The syncretic religious movement founded by Feng Yunshan that became the ideological basis for the rebellion.
- **Xiang Army**: The decentralized, gentry-led provincial army organized by Zeng Guofan that became the main Qing force against the Taiping.
- **Tianjing Incident**: The 1856 internal purge within the Taiping leadership that killed Yang Xiuqing and thousands of others, severely weakening the movement.
- **Ever Victorious Army**: A Qing military force composed of Chinese soldiers led by Western officers (e.g., Frederick Townsend Ward, Charles Gordon) that helped defend Shanghai.
- **Hakka**: A Han Chinese subgroup from which Hong Xiuquan and many early Taiping recruits originated.
- **Queue**: The braided hairstyle mandated for Han men under Qing rule; Taiping rebels wore their hair long in defiance.
- **Yong Ying**: The term for the regional, gentry-led armies (like the Xiang Army) that the Qing court increasingly relied upon.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Terminology**: Scholars debate whether to call the conflict the "Taiping Rebellion" (implying Qing legitimacy), "Taiping Civil War," or "Taiping Revolution" (emphasizing its radical transformational goals).
- **Death Toll**: Estimates vary widely from 20–30 million to as high as 73–100 million, with no reliable census data.
- **Nature of the Movement**: Historians disagree on whether it was primarily a religious movement, a nationalist uprising, or a proto-communist revolution.
- **Western Role**: The extent and motivation of limited Western intervention on the Qing side remain subjects of analysis.
