# Gran Colombia

## Overview
Gran Colombia (1819–1831) was a large republic in northern South America and parts of Central America, uniting the territories of the former Viceroyalty of New Granada. It was a pivotal experiment in post-colonial unity, but its existence was defined by a fundamental conflict between centralist and federalist visions of governance, which ultimately led to its dissolution into the modern nations of Colombia, Venezuela, and Ecuador.

## Timeline
- **1819** — Congress of Angostura proclaims the Republic of Colombia via the Fundamental Law.
- **1821** — Congress of Cúcuta promulgates the Constitution of Cúcuta, formally establishing the state.
- **1821** — Panama voluntarily joins the federation.
- **1822** — Provinces of Pasto, Guayaquil, and Quito are liberated and incorporated.
- **1824** — Gran Colombia's army helps consolidate Peru's independence.
- **1825** — Public political differences between President Bolívar and Vice President Santander emerge.
- **1826** — Venezuela nearly secedes under Commandant General José Antonio Páez; crisis is averted by compromise.
- **1828** — Convention of Ocaña fails to agree on a new constitution; Bolívar assumes dictatorial powers.
- **1828** — War with Peru begins over territorial claims.
- **1829** — Treaty of Guayaquil ends the war with Peru.
- **1830** — Simón Bolívar resigns as president; the federation begins to dissolve.
- **1831** — Gran Colombia is formally abolished, succeeded by Venezuela, Ecuador, and New Granada.

## Body

### Formation and Government
Gran Colombia was proclaimed in 1819 but formally constituted by the 1821 Constitution of Cúcuta. It was a unitary centralist state, divided into twelve departments, each governed by a centrally appointed intendant. The government consisted of a president, a bicameral congress, and a high court, with its capital in Bogotá. Simón Bolívar was elected president and Francisco de Paula Santander vice president. Due to the ongoing war for independence, Bolívar was often absent, leaving Santander to administer the government.

### Political Conflict and Dissolution
The state's history was dominated by a struggle between centralists, led by Bolívar, who favored a strong presidency, and federalists, led by Santander, who supported a decentralized government and adherence to the Constitution of Cúcuta. This conflict intensified after the war against Spain ended in the mid-1820s. Regional grievances resurfaced, particularly in Venezuela, where federalist sentiment was strong, and in Ecuador, which felt economically and politically marginalized. A failed constitutional convention in 1828 (Convention of Ocaña) led Bolívar to assume dictatorial powers, but he could not prevent the union's collapse. He resigned in 1830, and the federation dissolved into three separate republics.

### International Relations and Aftermath
Gran Colombia was the first Spanish American republic recognized by the United States (1822). Its dissolution created complex diplomatic questions regarding the succession of treaties and territorial claims. Peru, for instance, viewed the treaties with Gran Colombia as void after its collapse, while successor states like Colombia argued for continuity. Panama, which had joined voluntarily in 1821, remained part of the successor state of Colombia until it gained independence in 1903.

## Terms
- **Unitary centralist state:** A system of government where power is concentrated in a single central authority, with regional governments having limited autonomy.
- **Federalism:** A system of government where power is divided between a central authority and constituent political units (like states or provinces).
- **Uti possidetis:** A legal principle meaning "as you possess," used to argue that newly independent states should retain the borders of their preceding colonial administrative units.
- **Intendant:** A high-ranking official appointed by a central government to administer a department or province.
- **Viceroyalty of New Granada:** The Spanish colonial administrative division that governed much of northern South America from 1739 to 1777, whose territory Gran Colombia claimed.
- **Convention of Ocaña:** A 1828 constituent assembly that failed to produce a new constitution due to irreconcilable differences between Bolívar's supporters and federalists.
- **Unionistas:** Modern proponents of the political reunification of the former Gran Colombian territories.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Succession of Treaties:** A key diplomatic debate is whether the dissolution of Gran Colombia voided its international treaties (as Peru argued) or whether its successor states inherited those obligations.
- **Bolívar's Vision:** The failure of Gran Colombia is often cited as the collapse of Simón Bolívar's dream of a unified Spanish American state, raising questions about whether such unity was ever politically viable given regional identities and interests.
- **Reunification:** Periodic proposals for political reunification, such as one announced by Venezuelan President Hugo Chávez in 2008, continue to be a topic of discussion among "unionistas."
