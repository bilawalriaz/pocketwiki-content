# Joseph Priestley

## Overview
Joseph Priestley (1733–1804) was an English chemist, theologian, and political theorist whose work bridged Enlightenment science and radical religion. He is most famous for independently discovering oxygen (which he called "dephlogisticated air") and for his role in founding British Unitarianism. His life illustrates the conflict between scientific innovation and established authority, as his support for the French Revolution and heterodox theology led to his exile in the United States.

## Timeline
- **1733** — Born in Birstall, Yorkshire, to a Dissenting family.
- **1752** — Matriculates at Daventry Academy, a Dissenting academy.
- **1761** — Publishes *The Rudiments of English Grammar*; begins teaching at Warrington Academy.
- **1766** — Elected a Fellow of the Royal Society.
- **1767** — Publishes *The History and Present State of Electricity*; invents carbonated water.
- **1768** — Publishes *Essay on the First Principles of Government*, an early liberal political theory.
- **1774** — Isolates oxygen ("dephlogisticated air"); helps found British Unitarianism.
- **1774–86** — Publishes six volumes of *Experiments and Observations on Different Kinds of Air*.
- **1780** — Moves to Birmingham, joining the Lunar Society.
- **1791** — Fleeing the Priestley Riots, in which his home and church are burned, he moves to London.
- **1794** — Emigrates to the United States, settling in Northumberland County, Pennsylvania.
- **1804** — Dies in Northumberland, Pennsylvania.

## Body

### Scientific Work
Priestley's scientific reputation rested on his discovery of several gases ("airs") and his invention of carbonated water. His most famous discovery was oxygen in 1774, which he produced by focusing sunlight on mercuric oxide. He called it "dephlogisticated air" and found it supported respiration and combustion far better than common air. His work on gases, published in *Experiments and Observations on Different Kinds of Air*, also included discoveries of nitric oxide, ammonia, and sulphur dioxide. He used a "nitrous air test" to measure air quality, an early form of eudiometry. His earlier *History of Electricity* (1767) was a standard reference for over a century and included his proposal that electrical force follows an inverse-square law.

### Theology and Philosophy
Priestley's science was integral to his theology, which sought to fuse Enlightenment rationalism with Christian theism. He was a leading Rational Dissenter, emphasizing rational analysis of the Bible and nature. In works like *Institutes of Natural and Revealed Religion* (1772–74), he argued for a "primitive" Christianity free of later "corruptions," such as the Trinity, laying the groundwork for Unitarianism. His metaphysical works, including *Disquisitions Relating to Matter and Spirit* (1777), argued for materialism (the view that everything is made of matter) and necessitarianism (the belief that all events are determined by natural laws). He believed this deterministic universe, governed by a benevolent God, would eventually lead to a Christian millennium.

### Political Theory and Radicalism
Priestley was a staunch defender of religious Dissenters and an advocate for civil liberties. His *Essay on the First Principles of Government* (1768) distinguished political rights from civil rights, arguing the state should control only the public sphere, while education and religion are private matters. He supported the American Revolution and, initially, the French Revolution. His outspoken views and inflammatory language, such as a metaphor about laying "gunpowder" under the "old building of error," made him a target. The Birmingham Riots of 1791, where a mob burned his home and churches, were a direct result of this animosity. He later saw the French Revolution as a harbinger of the Second Coming.

### Later Life in America
After fleeing to the United States in 1794, Priestley continued his theological writings and supported the Unitarian cause, helping found the First Unitarian Church of Philadelphia. He became embroiled in American politics, criticizing President John Adams and advocating for free trade. His scientific output diminished due to isolation from European developments, but he stimulated American interest in chemistry. He died in Northumberland, Pennsylvania, in 1804.

## Terms
- **Dissenter**: A Protestant who does not conform to the established Church of England.
- **Rational Dissenter**: A Dissenter who emphasized rational analysis of the Bible and nature over dogma.
- **Phlogiston theory**: An 18th-century theory that combustion involved the release of a substance called phlogiston.
- **Dephlogisticated air**: Priestley's name for oxygen, which he believed was air with phlogiston removed.
- **Materialism**: The philosophical view that everything in the universe is made of matter.
- **Necessitarianism**: The belief that all events are determined by natural laws, leaving no free will.
- **Unitarianism**: A Christian denomination rejecting the Trinity, emphasizing the oneness of God.
- **Eudiometry**: The measurement of the purity or "goodness" of air, often using nitrous oxide.
- **Lunar Society**: An informal club of scientists, manufacturers, and philosophers in Birmingham.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Discovery of Oxygen**: Priestley isolated oxygen in 1774, but Carl Wilhelm Scheele may have discovered it earlier (though he published later), and Antoine Lavoisier was the first to explain it without phlogiston theory. The exact dating and credit for the discovery remain debated.
- **Rejection of Lavoisier's Chemistry**: Priestley's steadfast defense of phlogiston theory against Lavoisier's new chemistry has perplexed scholars. Explanations range from his identity as a "natural philosopher" rather than a modern chemist to his theological view of nature as infinite and coextensive with God.
- **Unity of His Work**: Scholars debate whether Priestley's scientific, theological, and political works form a coherent system or are contradictory. Recent scholarship challenges older views that his theology distracted from his science.
