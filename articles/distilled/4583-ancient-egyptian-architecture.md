# Ancient Egyptian architecture

## Overview
Ancient Egyptian architecture spans over three thousand years, evolving through distinct periods of change and upheaval. It is not a single style but a set of styles unified by conservative religious and funerary traditions. The most famous examples are the pyramids and Sphinx, but excavated temples, palaces, and tombs reveal a sophisticated tradition using locally sourced materials, astronomical alignment, and symbolic decoration that influenced later Greek and Roman architecture.

## Timeline
- **c. 2650 BC** — Imhotep designs the Step Pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara, the first monumental stone structure.
- **c. 2640–2532 BC** — Pharaoh Snefru initiates pyramid experiments, leading to the first true pyramids (Bent and Red Pyramids).
- **c. 2580–2490 BC** — The Giza pyramid complex is built, including the Great Pyramid and the Great Sphinx.
- **14th century BC** — Construction begins on Luxor Temple under Amenhotep III.
- **16th century BC onward** — Construction and expansion of the Karnak temple complex begins.
- **c. 1279–1213 BC** — Ramesses II builds the Ramesseum temple near Thebes.
- **New Kingdom (18th Dynasty)** — Pharaohs begin burial in hidden tombs in the Valley of the Kings.
- **14th century BC** — Amenhotep III constructs the massive temple complex of Malkata.
- **332 BC–395 AD** — Greco-Roman period sees hybrid architecture, e.g., the Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa.

## Body

### Materials and Construction
Due to wood scarcity, the primary materials were sun-baked mud brick and stone (mainly limestone, also sandstone and granite). From the Old Kingdom onward, stone was reserved for tombs and temples, while mud brick was used for palaces, fortresses, and town walls. Monumental buildings used the post and lintel method, with flat roofs supported by walls and columns. The core of pyramids was local stone or mud brick, with finer casing stones transported from distant quarries like Tura.

### Decoration and Motifs
Walls, columns, and piers were covered with painted hieroglyphs and pictorial frescoes. Motifs were symbolic, including the scarab, solar disk, vulture, and plants like papyrus, lotus, and palm. These decorations recorded events, spells, and daily life. Temples were often aligned with astronomical events like solstices.

### Columns and Gardens
Columns, carved from stone, often resembled bundled plants like papyrus or lotus. A key example is the Great Hypostyle Hall at Karnak (c. 1224 BC). Three garden types existed: temple gardens (e.g., with sacred Ished trees), private pleasure gardens with pools and flowers, and irrigated vegetable plots.

### Old Kingdom: Mastabas and Pyramids
Early royal tombs were mastabas—flat-roofed, rectangular structures with sloping sides. The Step Pyramid of Djoser (c. 2650 BC) evolved from this form. Pyramid development peaked at Giza, where the Great Pyramid (c. 2580 BC) showcased advanced engineering. The Great Sphinx, carved from bedrock, guarded the complex.

### New Kingdom: Temples and Hidden Tombs
The New Kingdom saw grand temple complexes like Luxor and Karnak, built and expanded by successive pharaohs. Luxor Temple features pylons, colonnades, and hypostyle halls. Karnak, developed over centuries, is vast, with the hypostyle hall containing 134 columns. Funerary architecture shifted to hidden rock-cut tombs in the Valley of the Kings to deter robbers.

### Greco-Roman and Fortifications
Under Ptolemaic and Roman rule, temples blended Egyptian and Greek styles (e.g., Composite capitals). Alexandria became a new capital with Greek urban planning. Fortresses, built mainly of mud brick with stone reinforcements, protected borders like the Nile Delta (Pelusium) and served as trade and military outposts (Jaffa).

### Transformation and Legacy
After Roman conquest, some temples were repurposed (e.g., Luxor Temple became a Roman camp, then a church and mosque). Ancient stones (spolia) were reused in medieval buildings. In the 19th–20th centuries, Egyptian motifs inspired modern revivalist architecture.

## Terms
- **Mastaba**: A flat-roofed, rectangular tomb with sloping sides, a precursor to pyramids.
- **Post and lintel**: A construction method using vertical posts supporting horizontal beams (lintels).
- **Hypostyle hall**: A roofed space supported by a dense grid of columns.
- **Pylon**: A monumental gateway flanking a temple entrance, typically sloping.
- **Obelisk**: A tall, four-sided, tapering monolith topped with a pyramidion.
- **Spolia**: Architectural elements reused from earlier monuments in new constructions.
- **Shaduf**: A hand-operated device for lifting water, used for irrigation from the late 18th Dynasty.
- **Barque shrine**: A chapel housing a sacred boat used in religious processions.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact purpose and symbolism of the Great Sphinx's face (possibly Khafre) are debated.
- The precise methods for aligning temples with astronomical events are studied through archaeoastronomy.
- The social organization and labor force for building pyramids (paid laborers vs. slaves) is clarified by archaeological evidence but remains a point of public misconception.
