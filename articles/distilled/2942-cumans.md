# Cumans

## Overview
The Cumans were a Turkic nomadic people who formed the western branch of the Cuman–Kipchak confederation, dominating the Eurasian Steppe from the 11th to 13th centuries. They were formidable warriors and traders whose migrations and military alliances profoundly influenced the politics and demographics of Eastern Europe, the Balkans, and the Middle East, particularly after their displacement by the Mongol invasions.

## Timeline
- **c. 1068–1078** — Cumans first enter the Bugeac (Bessarabia) region.
- **1068** — Battle of the Alta River: Cumans defeat the armies of three Rus' princes.
- **1091** — Cumans, allied with Byzantines, defeat the Pechenegs at the Battle of Levounion.
- **1185** — Cumans, allied with Bulgarians and Vlachs, help restore the Second Bulgarian Empire.
- **1223** — Rus'–Cuman alliance is defeated by Mongols at the Battle of the Kalka River.
- **1237–1241** — Mongol invasions destroy the Cuman–Kipchak confederation; mass westward migration begins.
- **1241** — Cuman leader Khan Köten is assassinated in Hungary, triggering a Cuman revolt and exodus.
- **1242** — After the Mongol invasion, King Béla IV of Hungary invites Cumans to settle permanently.
- **1278** — Cumans fight for Rudolf of Habsburg at the Battle on the Marchfeld.
- **1282** — Battle of Lake Hód: Hungarian King Ladislaus IV defeats Cuman rebels.
- **1304–1369** — The Codex Cumanicus, a linguistic manual, is compiled by Italian merchants and missionaries.

## Body

### Origins and Political Structure
The Cumans' original homeland is unknown, but they emerged from a conglomeration of Turkic and possibly Mongolic tribes, including the Qun. By the 11th century, they dominated a vast, loosely connected territory from the Danube to the Irtysh River, known as Cumania or Desht-i Qipchaq. They never formed a centralized state; instead, they were a confederation of tribes led by khans who acted independently. Their political organization was based on clans and tribes, with a social structure centered on family and kinship.

### Military Conflicts and Alliances
From the late 11th century, the Cumans were a major military force, engaging in constant raids and alliances. They fought and sometimes allied with Kievan Rus', the Byzantine Empire, the Kingdom of Hungary, and the Second Bulgarian Empire. They played a crucial role in the 1185 uprising that created the Second Bulgarian Empire and provided 14,000 light cavalry for Emperor Kaloyan's victory at the 1205 Battle of Adrianople. Their military tactics relied on light cavalry, composite bows, and feigned retreats.

### Mongol Invasions and Dispersal
The Mongol invasions of the 1230s shattered the Cuman–Kipchak confederation. After defeats at the Kalka River (1223) and during the 1237–1240 invasion, many Cumans fled west. Khan Köten led a large group to Hungary, where King Béla IV offered them asylum in 1238. However, Hungarian barons, suspicious of the Cumans, assassinated Köten in 1241, causing the Cumans to revolt and leave. After the Mongol devastation of Hungary, Béla IV invited them back, and they settled in the regions of Kunság (Greater and Little Cumania).

### Settlement and Integration
Cuman refugees also settled in the Second Bulgarian Empire, the Byzantine Empire, and the Latin Empire, where they became integrated into the elite. In Hungary, they were granted autonomy, their own jurisdiction, and privileges, serving as royal light cavalry. They maintained their identity for centuries, with their language and customs gradually fading by the 17th–18th centuries. Other Cumans were absorbed into the Golden Horde, while some became Mamluks in Egypt, with Sultan Baibars being a notable Cuman-origin ruler.

### Culture and Society
Cuman society was nomadic, centered on animal husbandry, especially horses. They practiced Tengrism, a shamanistic religion, and later adopted Christianity and Islam. Their language, a Kipchak Turkic dialect, was a lingua franca on the steppes and is documented in the Codex Cumanicus. They were known for their distinctive dress, including conical hats and sheepskin vests, and for burial practices involving mounds and grave goods.

## Terms
- **Cuman–Kipchak confederation:** A loose political and military alliance of Turkic nomadic tribes dominating the Eurasian Steppe from the 11th to 13th centuries.
- **Desht-i Qipchaq:** The Persian name for the "Steppe of the Kipchaks," referring to the Cuman–Kipchak territory.
- **Kipchak:** A Turkic people who formed the eastern half of the confederation; the term is often used interchangeably with Cuman in sources.
- **Mamluk:** A class of soldier-slaves, often of Turkic origin, who rose to power in the Islamic world, notably in Egypt.
- **Tengrism:** The shamanistic and animistic religion practiced by the Cumans, involving worship of the sky god Tengri.
- **Codex Cumanicus:** A 13th–14th century linguistic manual written to help Catholic missionaries communicate with the Cumans.
- **Kunság:** The historical region in Hungary settled by Cumans, divided into Greater and Little Cumania.
- **Stone babas:** Anthropomorphic stone statues found on the steppes, connected to Cuman religious cults.
- **Polovtsy:** The East Slavic name for the Cumans, derived from a root meaning "pale" or "blonde."

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact etymology of "Cuman" is debated, with theories linking it to hair color, horse color, a water vessel, or a word for "force."
- It is unclear whether the name "Kipchak" originally referred only to the eastern tribe or to the entire confederation, including the Cumans.
- The precise ethnic origins of the Cumans are uncertain; genetic studies of remains in Hungary show a heterogeneous population with both East Asian and West Eurasian mitochondrial DNA lineages, suggesting admixture during migrations.
- The relationship between the earlier Qun people mentioned in Chinese sources and the later Cumans is not definitively established.
