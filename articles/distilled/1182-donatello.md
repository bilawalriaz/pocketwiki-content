# Donatello

## Overview
Donatello (c. 1386–1466) was the defining sculptor of the Early Renaissance in Florence. He revolutionized the medium by reviving classical forms—most famously producing the first freestanding bronze nude since antiquity with his *David*—and inventing *stiacciato* (flattened-out) relief, a technique using minimal depth to suggest vast spatial recession through perspective. His work spans a dramatic stylistic evolution from classical monumentality and charm to a late, stark expressiveness bordering on brutality. He worked fluently in marble, bronze, wood, terracotta, and stucco, and pioneered the use of glass in sculpture. Though notoriously disorganized in business, he enjoyed lifelong Medici patronage, and his extended stays in Rome, Padua, and Siena disseminated his innovations across Italy, directly shaping the next generation of artists including Verrocchio and Michelangelo.

## Timeline
- **c. 1386** — Born in Florence to Niccolò di Betto Bardi, a wool-stretcher.
- **1401** — Accused of assault in Pistoia; likely meets Filippo Brunelleschi there.
- **1404–1407** — Apprentices in Lorenzo Ghiberti’s workshop on the Baptistery doors.
- **1408–1415** — Produces early marble statues for Florence Cathedral and Orsanmichele (*St. Mark*, *St. George*).
- **c. 1415–1417** — Creates *St. George Freeing the Princess*, the first milestone in *stiacciato* relief.
- **1423** — Begins documented work in bronze; receives commission for Siena Baptistery *Feast of Herod*.
- **c. 1425–1434** — Formal partnership with Michelozzo; produces Tomb of Antipope John XXIII and Brancacci Tomb.
- **c. 1430–1440** — Creates bronze *David* and *Amore-Attis* for the Medici; executes Cantoria reliefs for Florence Cathedral.
- **1443–1453** — Resides in Padua; casts Equestrian Monument of Gattamelata (first life-size equestrian since antiquity) and high altar complex for the Santo.
- **1457–1461** — Active in Siena; produces *Judith and Holofernes* (late stark style) and *John the Baptist*.
- **1460s** — Works on bronze pulpits for San Lorenzo, Florence (assembled posthumously).
- **13 Dec 1466** — Dies in Florence.

## Body

### Early Life and Formation
Donatello was born in Florence around 1386, the son of a wool-stretcher in the Arte della Lana. His first documented appearance is a 1401 assault charge in Pistoia, where he likely encountered Filippo Brunelleschi, ten years his senior. The pair traveled to Rome (c. 1402–1404) to systematically study and measure ancient ruins—an early archaeological pursuit that grounded Donatello’s lifelong classicism. Returning to Florence, he apprenticed with Lorenzo Ghiberti (1404–1407) on the Baptistery doors but quickly diverged from Ghiberti’s International Gothic elegance toward a more rugged, classically informed monumentality. He also worked on the Cathedral’s *Porta della Mandorla*, where Giovanni d’Ambrogio has been identified as a decisive mentor for the emergence of Renaissance sculpture.

### Early Statues for Florence: Monumentality and Perspective
By 1408 Donatello was receiving major public commissions. For the Cathedral, he carved a colossal seated *Saint John the Evangelist* (1409–11) and six prophets for Giotto’s Campanile (1414–1435), including the famous *Zuccone* (Habakkuk), reportedly his favorite. These figures, viewed from steep angles far below, required distorted proportions to appear correct—a practical lesson in optical correction. At Orsanmichele, his *St. Mark* (1411–13) introduced a powerful *contrapposto* with vertical drapery mimicking Doric flutes, while *St. George* (c. 1415–17) pushed forward into the viewer’s space, its face expressing *prontezza* (alert readiness). The base relief, *St. George Freeing the Princess*, marks the birth of *stiacciato*: figures project barely millimeters, yet overlapping planes and an arcaded hall create a measurable illusion of deep space.

### The *Stiacciato* Revolution and Bronze Mastery
Donatello’s *stiacciato* (literally "flattened-out") relief contrasted sharply with Ghiberti’s high/low relief mix in the "Gates of Paradise." In the bronze *Feast of Herod* (1423–27) for the Siena Baptistery font, he organized figures in three receding architectural planes viewed from a low vantage point. The composition uses two vanishing points, perhaps deliberately creating subliminal tension to match the grisly subject. Other *stiacciato* works—the *Madonna of the Clouds*, *Pazzi Madonna*, *Ascension with Christ Giving the Keys*—demonstrate a "tightly-stretched unified skin-plane" where minimal relief suggests infinite depth. Simultaneously, Donatello mastered bronze casting, though technical flaws (holes, patches) appear even in masterpieces like the *David*.

### Partnership with Michelozzo and the Wall Tomb
Around 1425 Donatello partnered with Michelozzo, an architect-sculptor skilled in workshop management and bronze technique. Their collaboration produced the Tomb of Antipope John XXIII (1425–28) in the Florence Baptistery, a vertically compressed, classicizing ensemble that became the *quattrocento* model for elaborate wall tombs. They also executed the Brancacci Tomb (1427–29) in Naples, where Donatello contributed the *Assumption* relief and bronze *spiritelli* (winged putti). The partnership dissolved in 1434, strained by Donatello’s chronic delays on the Prato Cathedral pulpit reliefs—ecstatic, bacchanalian dancing children delivered a decade late. During this period Donatello traveled to Rome (1430–33), studying antiquities and possibly meeting Leon Battista Alberti; the *David*’s proportions closely match Alberti’s theoretical canon.

### Children, Putti, and the *Cantoria*
Donatello gave unprecedented prominence to putti and *spiritelli*—naked, winged children drawn from classical Dionysiac imagery. The Siena font *spiritelli* (1429) are called "the first true free-standing figurines of the Renaissance." His *Cantoria* (singing gallery) frieze for Florence Cathedral (1433–39) develops the Prato pulpit style: half-nude putti in "Dionysiac abandon," their childish bodies transmuted into liberated animal life. The bronze *Amore-Attis* (early 1440s) bridges this playful genre and the sensuous eroticism of the *David*, wearing leggings that accentuate rather than conceal his nudity.

### The Bronze *David*: Nudity, Politics, and Eroticism
Commissioned by Cosimo de’ Medici c. 1434–40, the bronze *David* is the first freestanding male nude since antiquity. It transforms the biblical King of Israel into a "young Greek god" with a real boy’s narrow chest and a waist that becomes the "centre of plastic interest" radiating body planes—a norm for later Renaissance nudes. The figure stands in *contrapposto* on Goliath’s severed head; the giant’s helmet feathers brush David’s inner thigh, a deliberate sensuous touch. Contemporary gossip (recorded in the *Detti piacevoli* c. 1480) describes Donatello’s homosexuality as an open secret, tolerated and protected by the Medici; he allegedly hired beautiful boys and "stained" them with makeup to deter rivals. No sodomy denunciations survive in Florentine archives, though records are incomplete.

### Wood, Stucco, and the Old Sacristy
Donatello exploited wood’s lightness for processional crucifixes (Santa Croce, c. 1410) and the signed *St. John the Baptist* in Venice (1438). The *Penitent Magdalene* (late 1430s), long misdated to his late period, is a "formidably expressive" gaunt figure whose sunken eyes "have beheld the vision of God." In the Old Sacristy of San Lorenzo (c. 1435–43), designed by Brunelleschi, Donatello decorated the entire space: bronze doors with conservative stacked panels, and painted stucco roundels of Evangelists and Baptist scenes in the pendentives. The scheme drew early criticism—crude modeling, illegible detail from below, and a clash with Brunelleschi’s architecture. Donatello never used painted stucco again; the Della Robbia workshop soon perfected glazed terracotta as a superior alternative.

### Padua: The Equestrian Monument and the Santo
In 1443 Donatello moved to Padua for ten years. His Equestrian Monument of Gattamelata (cast 1447–50, installed 1453) was the first life-size bronze equestrian since antiquity, signed and placed in a public square rather than over a tomb. It draws on the Marcus Aurelius in Rome and the Horses of Saint Mark in Venice, becoming the prototype for centuries of European equestrian statues. For the Basilica of St. Anthony (the Santo), he created a vast high altar complex (1446–50): a bronze Crucifix, an enthroned Madonna and Child rising from her throne flanked by saints and sphinxes, four gold-highlighted reliefs of St. Anthony’s life, and twenty-one bronze reliefs plus a marble *Entombment*. The project trained a generation of Paduan bronze founders, making the city a center for small "table bronzes."

### Siena and the Late Stark Style
Aging and likely supplying designs rather than carving, Donatello worked in Siena (1457–61). The *Judith and Holofernes* (signed) epitomizes his late "stark, even brutal" manner: the killing is in progress, Judith raising her sword for a second blow, base reliefs showing the drunken feast preceding it. Real cloth impressed texture into the wax model; Holofernes’ legs may have been cast from life. The *John the Baptist* arrived missing a forearm. The *Madonna delle Grazie* (1459) incorporates blue glass inlays. When the Medici were expelled in 1494, *Judith* was moved to the Palazzo della Signoria, its meaning flipped: the Medici became the tyrannical victims, not the liberating underdogs.

### Final Years and Glass Innovations
Back in Florence, Donatello designed two pulpits for San Lorenzo (assembled posthumously), reliefs of "absolutely uncompromising use of every possible means to express emotion and suffering," cast from unfinished *modelli* with wild stylistic disparity. He experimented with glass throughout his career: stained glass designs for the Cathedral (1434), glass tesserae in relief backgrounds (Prato pulpit, Cantoria), a terracotta *Piot Madonna* with glass-covered wax and gold roundels, and the *Chellini Madonna* (c. 1450)—a bronze relief cast at even thickness so its reverse forms an intaglio mold for molten glass.

### Portraiture and Vasari’s Legacy
Aside from tomb effigies, Donatello produced no certain portraits. The Gattamelata has a generic "Caesar-like head," an "idealized portrait of brute power." The disputed terracotta bust of Niccolò da Uzzano (d. 1431) may be the earliest Florentine flat-bottomed bust, possibly made from a life/death mask. Giorgio Vasari’s *Life* (1540s) contains factual errors (birth dated 1403) but correctly identifies Donatello as the fount of Florentine sculptural dominance, linking him to Michelangelo via Bertoldo di Giovanni. Vasari’s Medici-centric narrative mirrored his own patronage.

### Legacy
Donatello’s influence permeates all fifteenth-century Florentine sculpture. His revival of classical forms was shared with Ghiberti, Jacopo della Quercia, and Luca della Robbia, but his *stiacciato* remained uniquely his—only Desiderio da Settignano approached it, and Michelangelo’s *Madonna of the Stairs* (c. 1490) is a rare later essay in the technique. His late expressive violence "overawed" contemporaries; only Verrocchio and Antonio del Pollaiuolo later explored similar effects. His Paduan and Sienese sojourns seeded local bronze industries. Michelangelo’s *David* (1501) stands as both "an ode and a challenge to Donatello."

## Terms
- ****Stiacciato**** — (Italian: "flattened-out") Donatello’s invention: extremely shallow relief where figures project mere millimeters, using overlapping planes and linear perspective to suggest deep spatial recession.
- ****Contrapposto**** — A pose where the figure’s weight rests on one leg, causing shoulders and hips to counterbalance, creating naturalistic S-curve tension. Revived from classical antiquity.
- ****Spiritelli**** — (Sing. *spiritello*) Winged, nude putti-like figures derived from classical Dionysiac attendants; Donatello gave them unprecedented prominence as freestanding bronzes and relief friezes.
- ****Condottiere**** — A mercenary captain commanding a private army hired by Italian city-states; Erasmo da Narni (Gattamelata) was one, commemorated by Donatello’s equestrian monument.
- ****Modello**** — (Pl. *modelli*) A preparatory model in clay, wax, or terracotta, finished to presentation standard, used to guide assistants or secure patron approval before final execution.
- ****Intaglio**** — An incised or concave image carved into a surface; the reverse of the *Chellini Madonna* was cast as an intaglio mold for glass.
- ****Pulpit**** — An elevated, enclosed platform in a church from which sermons are preached; Donatello’s Prato and San Lorenzo pulpits feature reliefs of dancing putti and Passion scenes.
- ****Cantoria**** — A singing gallery for choirs in a church; Donatello and Luca della Robbia each decorated one for Florence Cathedral (1430s).
- ****Arte della Lana**** — The Florentine wool-workers’ guild; Donatello’s father was a member, providing the family a solid artisanal income.
- ****Catasto**** — The 1427 Florentine tax census; Donatello’s declaration ("41 years old in July 1427") anchors his birth to c. 1386.

## Debates and open questions
- **Dating of the bronze *David***: No contemporary document records the commission; scholars argue for a range of c. 1430–1450 based on style, Medici politics, and the *Amore-Attis* relationship.
- **Extent of homosexuality**: The *Detti piacevoli* anecdotes (c. 1480) are the sole source; no legal denunciations survive, but archives are incomplete. Whether the *David*’s eroticism reflects Donatello’s life or a Neo-Platonic ideal remains contested.
- **Attribution of the San Lorenzo pulpits**: Reliefs vary wildly in quality; some appear cast from unfinished *modelli*. Debate continues over which passages are autograph, which by assistants (Bartolomeo Bellano, Bertoldo di Giovanni), and whether the ensemble was originally conceived as pulpits.
- **Authorship of the *Niccolò da Uzzano* bust**: The earliest Florentine portrait bust; attributed to Donatello, Desiderio da Settignano, or a later copy. The use of a life/death mask is hypothesized but unproven.
- **Workshop practice in Padua**: Donatello’s ten-year stay seeded Padua’s bronze industry, but the division of labor between him, Andrea del Caldiere (casting), and assistants on the Santo altar is unclear.
- **Vasari’s reliability**: Vasari wrote a century later, misdates the birth by 17 years, and conflates Donatello’s pupils with later artists. His anecdotes (money bucket, Brunelleschi crucifix rivalry) are literary constructions but shape the popular image.
