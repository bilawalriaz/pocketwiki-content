# Barents Sea

## Overview
The Barents Sea is a shallow marginal sea of the Arctic Ocean, bordered by northern Norway and Russia. It is a critical region for global climate, fisheries, and energy resources, and is undergoing rapid environmental change due to warming, a process termed "Atlantification."

## Timeline
- **c. 10,000 BP** — Early Holocene coastal deglaciation of Novaya Zemlya.
- **16th century** — Sea appears on maps as the "Murman Sea" (Russian for Norwegian).
- **1595** — Named "Barents Sea" by Europeans for Dutch navigator Willem Barentsz.
- **1920–1944** — Finland's territory reached the sea; Liinakhamari was its only ice-free winter harbour.
- **1933** — First full seabed map produced by Maria Klenova.
- **1943** — Battle of the Barents Sea occurs during World War II.
- **1969** — Norway begins hydrocarbon exploration.
- **1976** — Joint Norwegian–Russian Fisheries Commission established.
- **1980s** — Major gas fields discovered on both Norwegian and Russian sides.
- **2010** — Norway and Russia sign agreement resolving their long-standing boundary dispute.
- **2011** — Boundary agreement enters into force, opening the former "grey zone" for exploration.

## Body

### Geography and Climate
The Barents Sea is a relatively shallow shelf sea with an average depth of 230 metres. It is bordered by the Kola Peninsula, Svalbard, Franz Josef Land, and Novaya Zemlya, which separates it from the Kara Sea. The warm North Atlantic Drift keeps its southern half, including the ports of Murmansk and Vardø, ice-free year-round. The sea is characterized by three main water masses: warm, salty Atlantic water; cold Arctic water; and coastal water. The boundary between Atlantic and Arctic waters, the Polar Front, is sharp in the west but diffuse in the east. The sea is a global warming "hot spot," with reduced sea ice and water column stratification potentially altering Eurasian weather patterns.

### Ecology and Pollution
The North Atlantic Drift gives the Barents Sea high biological productivity. Its food web begins with phytoplankton blooms that feed zooplankton, which in turn support key fish stocks like cod, capelin, and haddock. The capelin is a crucial food source for top predators. The fisheries, especially cod, are of major economic importance to Norway and Russia. However, the sea is among the most polluted on Earth due to accumulated marine garbage, Soviet nuclear tests, radioactive waste dumping, and industrial pollution, with concerns about future increases from shipping and oil spills.

### History and Political Status
Historically known to Russians as the Murman Sea, it was named for Willem Barentsz in the 16th century. A significant World War II naval battle occurred there in 1943. During the Cold War, the Soviet Union used the sea as a bastion for ballistic missile submarines. For decades, Norway and Russia disputed their maritime boundary, with a 175,000 km² "grey zone" between their claims. This was resolved by a 2010 agreement that established an equidistant boundary, which came into force in 2011.

### Economy: Oil, Gas, and Fishing
Hydrocarbon exploration began in the 1960s, with major gas field discoveries in the 1980s, including the giant Shtokman field (discovered 1988). Development was hampered by high costs and remote conditions, though interest revived in the late 2000s. Fishing is managed jointly by the Norwegian-Russian Fisheries Commission, which sets Total Allowable Catches (TACs). However, underreporting of catches to avoid taxes means the true scale of extraction is underestimated, complicating ecosystem management.

### Bioprospecting
The meeting of temperate and Arctic waters creates a diverse ecosystem. Species adapted to these extreme conditions are targets for marine bioprospecting—the search for bioactive compounds with commercial applications in medicine, food, and industry. Norway strategically supports this field, with northern regions like Tromsø playing a central role.

## Terms
- **Marginal sea:** A sea that is part of an ocean but is partially enclosed by land.
- **Shelf sea:** A relatively shallow sea area over a continental shelf.
- **Atlantification:** The process by which the Barents Sea is becoming more like the Atlantic Ocean due to warming and reduced sea ice.
- **Polar Front:** The boundary between warm Atlantic water and cold Arctic water in the Barents Sea.
- **Total Allowable Catch (TAC):** The maximum quantity of a fish stock that can be caught in a given period.
- **Marine bioprospecting:** The search for useful biological compounds from marine organisms.
- **Bastion:** A defended area or stronghold; in this context, a protected area for strategic submarines.

## Debates and open questions
- The ecological and climatic consequences of the ongoing "Atlantification" and loss of permanent sea ice are not fully understood.
- The true impact of fishing on the Barents Sea ecosystem is obscured by inaccurate catch data and underreporting.
- The long-term environmental risks from accumulated pollution and increased future shipping and oil activity remain a major concern.
