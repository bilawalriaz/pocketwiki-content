# White Sea–Baltic Canal

## Overview
The White Sea–Baltic Canal (Belomorkanal) is a man-made ship canal in Russia connecting the White Sea (Arctic Ocean) with Lake Onega, and thus the Baltic Sea. Opened in 1933, it was the first major Soviet project built entirely with forced Gulag labor, serving as a propaganda showcase for the "reforging" of prisoners. Its shallow depth limited its economic utility, but it held significant military and symbolic importance.

## Timeline
- **1931–1933** — Canal constructed in 20 months by forced labor of Gulag inmates.
- **2 August 1933** — Canal opens for navigation.
- **1933** — Belbaltlag camp directorate reorganized into the White Sea-Baltic Combine (BBK) for operation.
- **1939–1940** — No action during the Winter War.
- **June 1941** — Finnish commandos fail to blow up locks; Finnish Air Force bombing raids begin.
- **December 1941** — Finnish troops capture Povenets; Soviet forces demolish the southern lock ladder.
- **July 1946** — Post-war repairs completed; navigation restored.
- **1964** — Importance increases after commissioning of the Volga–Baltic Waterway.
- **1970s** — Reconstruction increases guaranteed depth to 4 meters.
- **1985** — Cargo tonnage peaks at 7.3 million tonnes.

## Body

### Construction and Forced Labor
The canal was built between 1931 and 1933, almost entirely by manual labor, to meet the timeline of Stalin's first five-year plan. The workforce, managed by the Belbaltlag camp directorate, consisted of an estimated 100,000 Gulag convicts. Working conditions were brutal, with prisoners using primitive hand tools. Official records state 12,300 workers died (a mortality rate of about 8.7%), while historian Anne Applebaum estimates 25,000 deaths. The project was presented as a success of "reforging" (perekovka) class enemies through labor. A group of 120 Soviet writers, including Maxim Gorky, visited in 1933 and produced a book praising the project, though some were likely aware of the brutality.

### Physical Characteristics and Limitations
The total waterway is 227 km (141 mi) long, with 48 km (30 mi) being man-made. It features 19 locks, with the "Stairs of Povenets" (7 locks) forming the southern slope. The canal's minimal depth of 3.5 m (11.5 ft) was a critical limitation, far shallower than the originally proposed 5.4 m (17.7 ft). This depth accommodates only light river craft (up to 600 tonnes deadweight), not most seagoing vessels (which require 4.5–6 m drafts). The navigable channel is 36 m (118 ft) wide, with a speed limit of 8 km/h in artificial sections.

### World War II and Postwar History
During World War II, the canal route became a front line. Finnish forces captured the southern entrance in December 1941 after Soviet troops demolished the Povenets lock ladder, flooding the village. The canal was repaired and reopened in July 1946. Postwar upgrades included electrification of locks (completed by 1959) and integration into the Unified Deep Water System of European Russia in the 1970s.

### Usage and Economic Role
Traffic peaked in 1985 at 7.3 million tonnes but declined sharply after the Soviet Union's fall, with 0.3 million tonnes in 2002. The canal enables shipment of heavy/bulky items from industrial centers to Arctic ports. It has been used for military transfers of submarines between the Baltic and White Sea fleets and for shipping oil products, though an oil spill in 2003 halted those operations. The canal system includes five hydroelectric plants with a total capacity of 240 MW.

## Terms
- **Gulag**: The Soviet government agency that administered forced labor camps.
- **Belbaltlag**: The Directorate of the White Sea–Baltic Camps, which managed the canal's construction workforce.
- **Perekovka**: The Soviet concept of "reforging" or rehabilitating political prisoners through corrective labor.
- **Deadweight tonnage (dwt)**: A measure of how much weight a ship can safely carry.
- **Lock**: A device for raising and lowering boats between stretches of water of different levels on a waterway.
- **Summit pond**: The highest level of a canal, between the upper and lower sets of locks.
- **Zeka (zek)**: Russian slang for "inmate," derived from the term *kanaloarmeyets* ("canal-army-man") coined during the canal's construction.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact death toll during construction is debated, with official records stating 12,300 and historian Anne Applebaum estimating 25,000.
- The extent to which the visiting writers' brigade in 1933 was aware of the true working conditions remains a subject of historical analysis.
