# Origen

## Overview
Origen of Alexandria (c. 185 – c. 253) was a Christian philosopher, theologian, and textual scholar whose prolific output—roughly 2,000 treatises—systematized Christian theology for the first time. He founded the Christian School of Caesarea, produced the *Hexapla* (the first critical edition of the Hebrew Bible), and pioneered allegorical exegesis, the ransom theory of atonement, and Trinitarian terminology. Though revered as a genius by figures like Athanasius and the Cappadocian Fathers, his speculations on the pre-existence of souls, universal salvation (*apokatastasis*), and the subordination of the Son to the Father sparked centuries of controversy. Condemned as a heretic by Emperor Justinian I in 543 and possibly by the Second Council of Constantinople (553), Origen remains a pivotal, ambiguous figure: the architect of Eastern Christian theology who was never canonized.

## Timeline
- **c. 185–186** — Born in Alexandria to Leonides (martyred 202) and an unnamed mother; likely not a Roman citizen.
- **202** — Father executed under Septimius Severus; family property confiscated. Origen, eldest of nine, supports family.
- **c. 203** — Appointed catechist at Catechetical School of Alexandria; adopts ascetic lifestyle; sells library for study income.
- **c. 206** — Patronage of Ambrose begins; provided secretaries, enabling massive literary output.
- **212–214** — Travels to Rome (hears Hippolytus) and Arabia (invited by governor).
- **215** — Flees Caracalla’s sack of Alexandria to Caesarea Maritima; invited to preach by Palestinian bishops, angering Bishop Demetrius.
- **c. 220–230** — Composes *On the First Principles* (first systematic Christian theology) in Alexandria.
- **c. 231** — Ordained priest by bishops of Caesarea and Jerusalem during mission to Athens; Demetrius condemns ordination and alleges self-castration and heretical *apokatastasis*.
- **232** — Demetrius dies; Origen settles permanently in Caesarea, founds Christian School.
- **235** — Hides during Maximinus Thrax’s persecution; writes *Exhortation to Martyrdom*; Ambrose arrested.
- **c. 248** — Writes *Contra Celsum*, the definitive early Christian apologetic.
- **250–251** — Tortured under Decian persecution; refuses to apostatize.
- **c. 253** — Dies (age 69) from torture injuries, possibly at Tyre.
- **399–403** — First Origenist Crisis: Epiphanius, Jerome, and Theophilus of Alexandria condemn Origenist monks; Origen posthumously anathematized in Alexandria.
- **543** — Emperor Justinian I condemns Origen as heretic; orders writings burned.
- **553** — Second Council of Constantinople issues anathemas against "Origen" (likely targeting later Origenist teachings).

## Body

### Life and Career
Origen’s biography derives almost entirely from Eusebius’s *Ecclesiastical History* (written c. 340), which scholars treat cautiously due to its hagiographic tone and reliance on hearsay. Born to a Hellenized bourgeois father and a mother of lower status (possibly non-citizen), Origen was immersed in Scripture and Greek philosophy from childhood. After his father’s martyrdom in 202, the teenage Origen supported his family by teaching at the Catechetical School of Alexandria. He adopted the ascetic regimen of Greek Sophists—barefoot, one cloak, fasting—though Eusebius’s portrayal of him as a proto-monk is anachronistic.

A pivotal turn came when the wealthy Valentinian Gnostic Ambrose converted to orthodoxy under Origen’s influence and funded a professional scriptorium (seven stenographers, copyists, calligraphers). This patronage enabled Origen’s staggering productivity. He studied widely in Alexandria, including under Ammonius Saccas (founder of Neoplatonism), though Eusebius’s claim that he studied under Clement of Alexandria is likely a retrospective inference.

Conflict with Bishop Demetrius defined Origen’s middle career. Demetrius, elevating the episcopal office above the presbyterate, viewed Origen’s independent philosophical teaching as a threat. When Palestinian bishops invited Origen to preach (215) and later ordained him priest (c. 231), Demetrius retaliated with charges of insubordination, self-castration (allegedly literal obedience to Matt. 19:12), and heretical universalism. Modern scholars debate the castration: Trigg accepts it as notorious fact; McGuckin and Chadwick suspect malicious gossip, noting Origen’s own mature commentary condemns literal reading of the passage as idiotic.

Settling in Caesarea (232), Origen founded a Christian school structured on Middle Platonic curriculum: Socratic reasoning → cosmology/natural history → theology. He became the theological authority for Palestine and Arabia, engaged pagan intellectuals (Porphyry, Plotinus), and debated heretics (Beryllus on adoptionism, Heracleides on soul mortality). During the Decian persecution (250), he endured two years of torture (iron collar, stocks) but refused to renounce Christ; released after Decius’s death (251), he died shortly after.

### Major Works
**Hexapla** (c. 230s–240s): A six-column critical edition of the Old Testament—Hebrew, Hebrew in Greek letters, Aquila, Symmachus, Septuagint, Theodotion—with critical signs (obelus ÷, asterisk *) adapted from Alexandrian scholarship. For Psalms, an *Enneapla* (nine columns). The *Hexapla* anchored the Library of Caesarea and served as master copy for Constantine’s fifty Bibles. Fragments and a Syriac translation of the Septuagint column survive.

**On the First Principles** (*De Principiis*, c. 220–230): The first systematic Christian theology. Book 1: God, Trinity, angels. Book 2: Incarnation, soul, free will, eschatology. Book 3: Cosmology, sin, redemption. Book 4: Teleology, scriptural interpretation. Survives mainly in Rufinus’s abridged Latin translation (397); Greek fragments in *Philocalia* and Justinian’s *Letter to Mennas*.

**Contra Celsum** (c. 248): Point-by-point rebuttal of the pagan philosopher Celsus’s *On the True Word* (178). Origen argues Christianity fulfills Platonic philosophy, defends miracles as moral reform not magic, and elevates Christianity from “folk religion” to academic respectability. Eusebius deemed it a sufficient answer to all future critics.

**Exegetical corpus**: Hundreds of homilies (205–279 extant) on most biblical books, preached in a three-year cycle (238–244). Extensive commentaries (John—32 books, 9 survive; Matthew—25 books, 8 Greek + Latin; Romans—15 books, fragments; Song of Songs—most celebrated, survives in Rufinus’s Latin). Origen applied Alexandrian philological rigor (word concordances before they existed) and a threefold hermeneutic: *somatic* (literal/historical), *psychic* (moral), *pneumatic* (spiritual/allegorical). He viewed the spiritual sense as deepest, noting some passages have *only* allegorical meaning.

**Other works**: *On Prayer* (exegesis of Lord’s Prayer), *Exhortation to Martyrdom*, *Dialogue with Heracleides* (cordial Socratic debate on Logos theology), *On Pascha*. Over 100 letters (Eusebius); only three substantial ones survive.

### Theology and Views
**Christology**: Jesus = pre-existent soul closest to God, fused with Logos at incarnation. Fully human (human soul) and divine. First fully developed **ransom theory of atonement**: Christ’s death paid ransom to Satan, who was tricked because he could not hold the sinless Logos. Dominant in East; Anselm critiqued it in West (11th c.).

**Cosmology & Eschatology**: **Pre-existence of souls**—God created rational intelligences (*psychaí*); most “cooled” in love, fell into embodiment: least cooling → angels, moderate → humans, most → demons. One soul remained faithful, became Christ. **Apokatastasis** (universal restoration): Origen *speculated* all might be saved after purgatorial “divine fire” (inner anguish), since punishment is medicinal and “the end is like the beginning.” But he *denied* Satan’s salvation in *Letter to Friends*, and taught eternal punishment as necessary for the immature. Accused of teaching resurrection bodies vanish; Rufinus’s translation shows Origen affirmed some body necessary, preferring a stable corporeal form shaped by each soul’s unique *eidos*.

**Ethics**: Radical **free will**—even pre-existent souls chose their fall; Jacob/Esau disparity reflects pre-temporal choices. Divine foreknowledge ≠ predestination. **Pacifism**: Christians cannot fight; Christ forbade violence. OT violence allegorical; Ps. 7, Lam. 3 support nonviolence. If all were Christian, no war.

**Hermeneutics**: Scripture divinely inspired; OT and NT read by same rules. Three senses (body/soul/spirit) mapped to Proverbs/Ecclesiastes/Song of Songs. Contradictions in Gospels do not undermine spiritual truth. Twofold creation (Gen. 1–2) read allegorically.

**Theology Proper**: God the Father **apophatic**—invisible, incorporeal, simple unity, transcending space/time. **Logos** = rational creative principle, “essence of essences,” mediates creation. **Theosis**: Humans participate in divinity via the Logos, but only those in God’s image living virtuously. **Trinity**: Holy Spirit in Godhead; all three required for salvation. **Subordinationist**: Father > Son > Holy Spirit (Platonic proportions). Father invisible even to Son; Son invisible to Spirit. Origen wavers on whether Son/Spirit are *created*; at the time, subordinationism was standard orthodoxy. *Homoousios* attribution is textually dubious (Rufinus/Pamphilus); Williams argues Origen would have rejected it as heretical given then-synonymous *ousia/hypostasis*.

### Transmission and Influence
Origen cited Hermippus to claim Pythagoras borrowed from Judaism—early example of *interpretatio graeca* serving apologetics.

**Pre-Crisis Influence**: Defined Eastern theology. Dionysius of Alexandria emphasized subordinationism; Theognostus taught *homoousios*. Athanasius, Cappadocians, *and* Arius all claimed Origen. Methodius (early 4th c.) criticized speculations but largely agreed.

**First Origenist Crisis (late 4th c.)**: Epiphanius attacked Origen’s subordinationism, excessive allegory, speculative method. John of Jerusalem refused posthumous condemnation. Jerome flipped from admirer to accuser; Rufinus defended Origen, publishing sanitized Latin *First Principles* (397). In Egypt, Theophilus of Alexandria first supported Origenist monks (God incorporeal), then reversed under mob pressure (400), condemning Origen and expelling “Tall Brothers,” triggering John Chrysostom’s downfall.

**Second Origenist Crisis (6th c.)**: Targeted later Origenists (Evagrius Ponticus’s disciples). “Isochristoi” (souls equal to Christ) vs. “Protoktistoi” (Christ first-created). Justinian condemned Isochristoi and Origen (543), ordered writings burned. *Decretum Gelasianum* (519–553) banned Origen in West. Second Constantinople (553) anathemas likely target Evagrian ideas retroactively attributed to Origen; textual discrepancies suggest Origen’s name inserted later. Popes Vigilius through Gregory the Great unaware of Origen’s condemnation at the council.

**Aftermath**: Most speculative works lost; surviving corpus still massive. Revered throughout first millennium as father of exegesis. Erasmus (Renaissance) called him greatest Christian author, borrowed free-will arguments. Luther banned him (“not one word about Christ”); Zwingli and Jan Hus drew on him. Cambridge Platonist Henry More accepted most teachings. Pope Benedict XVI (2007) hailed him “crucial to Christian thought,” “true maestro.” Modern evangelicals admire scriptural devotion but reject allegory. Feast day April 27 (Evangelical Church in Germany); some Anglicans, Calvinists, Coptic Orthodox style him “St. Origen.”

## Terms
- ****Hexapla**** — Origen’s six-column critical edition of the Old Testament (Hebrew, Hebrew-in-Greek, Aquila, Symmachus, Septuagint, Theodotion), with critical signs (obelus, asterisk) marking textual variants.
- ****Apokatastasis**** — “Restoration of all things”; Origen’s speculative doctrine that all rational beings may eventually be reconciled to God after purgatorial purification, because divine punishment is medicinal and the end mirrors the beginning.
- ****Pre-existence of souls**** — Doctrine that God created rational intelligences (*psychaí*) before the material world; their “cooling” love determined their embodiment as angels, humans, or demons.
- ****Ransom theory of atonement**** — Christ’s death as ransom paid to Satan for humanity’s liberation; Satan deceived because he could not enslave the sinless Logos. First fully developed by Origen.
- ****Subordinationism**** — Trinitarian model ranking Father > Son > Holy Spirit in dignity and authority; standard among pre-Nicene theologians, later deemed heretical.
- ****Threefold hermeneutic**** — Origen’s interpretive schema: *somatic* (literal/historical), *psychic* (moral/ethical), *pneumatic* (spiritual/allegorical—the deepest sense).
- ****Theosis**** — Deification/divinization; participation in God’s nature via the Logos, limited to rational, virtuous beings created in God’s image.
- ****Logos theology**** — The Son/Logos as distinct rational principle mediating between transcendent Father and creation; “essence of essences,” “idea of ideas.”
- ****Encratite**** — Ascetic sect condemning marriage and sexual reproduction; Epiphanius accused Origen of Encratite tendencies.
- ****Isochristoi / Protoktistoi**** — Sixth-century Origenist factions: Isochristoi held all souls originally equal to Christ; Protoktistoi held Christ as unique first-created. Both condemned.

## Debates and open questions
- **Self-castration**: Did Origen literally castrate himself (Eusebius) or is the story malicious gossip (Chadwick, McGuckin)? Origen’s mature commentary condemns literal reading of Matt. 19:12.
- **Hebrew knowledge**: Did Origen know Hebrew well enough to compose the *Hexapla*, or did he rely on a “Hebrew master” consultant? Scholars range from “alphabet only” (Lietzmann) to “superficial reading ability” (Hanson, Bardy).
- **Universalism**: Did Origen teach *apokatastasis* as doctrine or only as hopeful speculation? *Letter to Friends* denies Satan’s salvation; *Contra Celsus* suggests esoteric teaching of universal restoration for the mature.
- **Homoousios**: Did Origen apply *homoousios* (“same substance”) to Father/Son? Surviving quote is in Rufinus’s translation of Pamphilus; Williams argues Origen would have rejected it given period terminology.
- **Second Council of Constantinople (553)**: Did the council anathematize Origen personally, or only later “Origenist” teachings (Evagrius)? Textual variants and papal silence suggest Origen’s name may be a later insertion.
- **Reincarnation**: Did Origen teach metempsychosis? He explicitly denies “transmigration of souls,” but MacGregor argues his eschatology implies it; Olson calls this a New Age misreading.
- **Lost works**: The *Hexapla*, most commentaries, and all speculative treatises are lost. The surviving corpus (filtered through Rufinus/Jerome) may not represent Origen’s most radical ideas.
