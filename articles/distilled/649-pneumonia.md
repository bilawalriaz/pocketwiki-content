# Pneumonia

## Overview
Pneumonia is an inflammatory condition of the lungs, primarily affecting the alveoli (small air sacs), causing symptoms like cough, fever, chest pain, and breathing difficulty. It is a leading cause of death globally, especially among the very young, elderly, and chronically ill.

## Timeline
- **c. 460–370 BC** — Hippocrates describes pneumonia symptoms
- **1875** — Edwin Klebs observes bacteria in pneumonia patients
- **1882–1884** — Friedländer and Fraenkel identify *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Klebsiella pneumoniae*
- **1884** — Christian Gram develops Gram stain
- **1918** — Osler calls pneumonia "captain of the men of death"
- **1938** — Viral pneumonia first described by Hobart Reimann
- **1988** — Hib vaccine introduced
- **1977/2000** — Pneumococcal vaccines introduced for adults and children
- **2009** — World Pneumonia Day established

## Body

### Signs and Symptoms
Symptoms include productive cough, fever, chills, shortness of breath, and chest pain. Elderly patients may show confusion. Children under five typically present with fever, cough, and rapid breathing. Severe cases can involve blue-tinged skin, seizures, or decreased consciousness.

### Causes
Pneumonia is caused by bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites. Over 100 pathogens have been identified, but only a few cause most cases. Mixed infections occur in 45% of children and 15% of adults. In a 2010–2012 Chicago/Nashville study, 62% of hospitalized CAP patients had no detectable pathogen; viruses were more common than bacteria (23% vs. 11%).

#### Bacteria
*Streptococcus pneumoniae* causes nearly 50% of community-acquired cases. Other bacteria include *Haemophilus influenzae* (20%), *Chlamydophila pneumoniae* (13%), and *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* (3%). Drug-resistant strains like DRSP and MRSA are increasing.

#### Viruses
Viruses cause about one-third of adult cases and 15% of pediatric cases. Common agents include influenza, RSV, rhinovirus, and SARS-CoV-2. Viral infections can predispose to secondary bacterial pneumonia.

#### Fungi and Parasites
Fungal pneumonia is rare but more common in immunocompromised individuals. *Pneumocystis jiroveci* is common in HIV/AIDS. Parasitic pneumonia occurs mainly in travelers or immunodeficient individuals.

#### Noninfectious
Noninfectious pneumonia includes idiopathic interstitial pneumonia and lipoid pneumonia from lipid aspiration.

### Mechanisms
Pneumonia often begins as an upper respiratory infection spreading to the lower airways. Pathogens invade alveoli, triggering immune responses involving macrophages, neutrophils, and cytokines. Severe cases can lead to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

### Diagnosis
Diagnosis relies on symptoms, physical exam, and chest imaging. WHO defines pediatric pneumonia clinically using respiratory rate and chest indrawing. Adults with normal vital signs and lung exams are unlikely to have pneumonia. CRP and procalcitonin help guide antibiotic use.

### Prevention
Vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal, Hib) reduce risk. Hand hygiene, smoking cessation, and treating underlying conditions are key. Breastfeeding lowers infant risk. Zinc supplementation reduces pneumonia rates in children.

### Management
Antibiotics are first-line for bacterial pneumonia. Hospitalization is needed for severe cases. CURB-65 score guides admission decisions. Corticosteroids improve outcomes in severe CAP. Viral pneumonia may require antivirals like oseltamivir. Aspiration pneumonia needs anaerobic coverage.

### Prognosis
Most bacterial pneumonia stabilizes within 3–6 days. Mortality is <1% in treated cases but rises to 10% in hospitalized patients and 30–50% in ICU. Complications include empyema, lung abscess, sepsis, and ARDS.

### Epidemiology
Pneumonia affects ~450 million people annually, causing 4 million deaths. It is the leading cause of death in children under five in low-income countries. In the U.S., it was the 8th leading cause of death in 2009.

### History
Ancient Greeks described pneumonia. The 19th century saw bacterial identification and Gram staining. Antibiotics and vaccines in the 20th century dramatically reduced mortality.

## Terms
- **Alveoli**: Small air sacs in the lungs where gas exchange occurs.
- **Consolidation**: Lung tissue filling with fluid or pus, visible on X-ray.
- **CURB-65**: A clinical score (Confusion, Urea, Respiratory rate, Blood pressure, Age ≥65) to assess pneumonia severity.
- **ARDS**: Acute respiratory distress syndrome; severe lung inflammation causing respiratory failure.
- **Aspiration pneumonia**: Lung infection resulting from inhaling food, saliva, or other contaminants.
- **Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)**: Pneumonia contracted outside hospitals or care facilities.
- **Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)**: Lung infection developing >48–72 hours after mechanical ventilation.
- **Procalcitonin**: A biomarker used to distinguish bacterial from viral infections.
- **Empyema**: Pus in the pleural cavity surrounding the lungs.
- **Opportunistic infection**: Infections caused by pathogens that typically don’t harm healthy individuals.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The distinction between "typical" and "atypical" pneumonia is no longer emphasized due to lack of supporting evidence.
- Optimal duration of antibiotic therapy remains debated; shorter courses (3–5 days) may be effective for some cases.
- Use of corticosteroids in hospital-acquired pneumonia lacks sufficient evidence.
- Vitamin C and D supplementation for pneumonia prevention/treatment remains under investigation.
- Whether to routinely follow up with chest X-rays after pneumonia resolution varies between guidelines.
