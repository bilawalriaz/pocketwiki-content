# Soil

## Overview

Soil is a living, three-phase mixture of minerals, organic matter, water, and air that supports plant growth, filters water, regulates atmospheric gases, and hosts most of Earth’s biodiversity. It forms through the interaction of climate, organisms, topography, parent material, and time (CLORPT), and is studied as both a physical medium (edaphology) and a dynamic ecosystem (pedology). Its functions—nutrient cycling, water retention, carbon sequestration, and habitat provision—make it central to agriculture, construction, climate regulation, and ecosystem stability.

## Body

### Formation and Classification

Soil forms through the interplay of five factors—climate, organisms, relief, parent material, and time (CLORPT)—acting on weathering parent material over time. Initial stages involve physical and chemical breakdown of rock, colonization by lichens and cyanobacteria, and accumulation of organic matter via plant roots and microbial activity. As development proceeds, distinct layers (horizons) form: O (organic), A (topsoil), E (eluviated), B (subsoil with accumulated minerals), and C (weathered parent material). The solum (A+B horizons) contains most biological activity. Soil classification began with Dokuchaev’s morphological approach around 1880, later refined into systems like USDA *Soil Taxonomy* (1999) and the World Reference Base for Soil Resources.

### Composition and Structure

A typical soil is ~50% solids (45% mineral, 5% organic matter) and ~50% pore space (half water, half air). Mineral particles are categorized by size: sand (>0.05 mm), silt (0.002–0.05 mm), and clay (<0.002 mm). These particles bind with organic compounds into aggregates (peds), creating structure. Texture determines water retention, drainage, and aeration. Bulk density ranges from 1.1–1.6 g/cm³; particle density is 2.6–2.7 g/cm³. Compaction reduces porosity, limiting root growth and gas exchange.

### Chemical Properties

Soil chemistry governs nutrient availability and pH buffering. Colloids—clay minerals and humus—carry negative charges that attract and hold cations (cation exchange capacity, CEC), measured in cmolc/kg. CEC is highest in clay-rich and organic soils. Base saturation (fraction of CEC occupied by Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺) correlates with pH. Acidic soils (pH < 6.5) release toxic Al³⁺ and Mn²⁺; alkaline soils (pH > 7.5) reduce micronutrient availability. Buffering capacity increases with CEC. Redox reactions influence nutrient cycling and gas fluxes (e.g., denitrification under anaerobic conditions).

### Biological Activity

Soil hosts immense biodiversity: a gram may contain billions of organisms across thousands of species. Biomass includes 70% microorganisms (bacteria, fungi), 22% macrofauna (earthworms, insects), and 8% roots. The soil food web processes organic matter through trophic levels—from bacteria/fungi feeding on detritus, to protozoa/nematodes consuming microbes, to earthworms and arthropods breaking down humus. Microbial respiration returns CO₂ to the atmosphere; stable humus sequesters carbon long-term. Soil organisms drive nutrient mineralization, decomposition, and aggregation.

### Water and Gas Dynamics

Soil water exists in stages: saturation, field capacity, wilting point, air-dry, and oven-dry. Available water capacity (difference between field capacity and wilting point) determines plant-accessible moisture. Capillary action moves groundwater upward. Soil gas differs markedly from atmospheric air: O₂ decreases due to root/microbial respiration, while CO₂ rises (often 10–100× atmospheric levels). Diffusion rates depend on porosity, texture, and moisture. Anaerobic conditions trigger denitrification, releasing N₂O and depleting soil nitrogen.

### Human Uses and Degradation

Soil supports 98.8% of human food production and underpins construction, mining, and landscaping. Degradation includes acidification (from fertilizers, acid rain), contamination (heavy metals, microplastics), desertification (overgrazing, deforestation), erosion (water, wind), and salination (irrigation in arid zones). Reclamation strategies include adding gypsum, organic amendments, biochar, and clay (e.g., bentonite trials in Thailand increased yields by 73%). Peatlands, though fertile when drained, emit greenhouse gases when disturbed.

## Terms

- **Pedosphere**: The global soil layer interfacing with lithosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, and biosphere.
- **CLORPT**: Acronym for Climate, Organisms, Relief, Parent material, Time—the five soil-forming factors.
- **Colloid**: Microscopic particles (1 nm–1 μm) of clay or organic matter with high surface area and charge, enabling ion exchange.
- **Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC)**: Measure of soil’s ability to retain and supply positively charged ions (cmolc/kg).
- **Horizon**: Distinct horizontal layer in a soil profile, labeled O, A, E, B, C based on composition and formation processes.
- **Humus**: Stable organic matter formed by decomposition of plant/animal residues; enhances fertility and water retention.
- **Bioturbation**: Mixing of soil by organisms (e.g., earthworms), influencing horizon development and nutrient distribution.
- **Denitrification**: Anaerobic microbial process converting soil nitrates to N₂, N₂O, and NO gases, reducing fertility.
- **Base Saturation**: Percentage of CEC occupied by base cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺); indicates soil fertility and pH buffering.
- **Leaching**: Downward movement of dissolved substances through soil via percolating water.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Soil age limits**: While most Earth soils are younger than the Pleistocene, fossilized soils exist from the Archean, raising questions about long-term pedogenic stability.
- **Carbon feedback loops**: Predictions that warming soils will increase CO₂ emissions remain debated due to uncertainties in soil carbon turnover dynamics.
- **Microbial contributions to soil gas**: Whether soil CO₂ levels self-regulate via root/microbial inhibition at extreme concentrations is contested.
- **Peatland climate impact**: Field-scale studies question whether rewetting peatlands consistently reduces greenhouse gas emissions or may enhance them under certain conditions.
- **Biochar safety**: Uncontrolled application of charred waste products risks harming soil biota and human health, despite benefits in nutrient-poor tropical soils.
- **Microplastic fate**: The extent and consequences of microplastic accumulation in agricultural soils from sewage sludge and irrigation remain poorly understood.
