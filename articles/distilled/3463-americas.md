# Americas

## Overview
The Americas are the combined landmass of North and South America, forming the second-largest continent by area and the third by population. They constitute the "New World," distinct from the "Old World" of Afro-Eurasia, and are defined by their post-Columbian history of European colonization, which reshaped their demographics, cultures, and economies. The region's vast geography, from Arctic tundra to tropical rainforests, supports immense ecological and cultural diversity.

## Timeline
- **c. 20,000–16,000 years ago** — First human migration from Asia into the Americas.
- **c. 3500 BCE** — Migration of the Inuit into the neoarctic completes initial Indigenous settlement.
- **982 CE** — Viking settlers arrive in Greenland.
- **1492–1504** — Christopher Columbus's voyages initiate permanent European contact.
- **1496** — Founding of Santo Domingo, the oldest European city in the Americas.
- **1519** — Founding of Panama City, base for Spanish conquest of South America.
- **1770s–1780s** — American Revolution begins the era of decolonization.
- **1811–1825** — Most Spanish and Portuguese colonies in South America gain independence.
- **1898** — Spanish–American War results in Cuban independence and U.S. control of Puerto Rico.
- **1982** — Patriation of the Canadian Constitution marks full legislative independence.

## Body

### Etymology and Terminology
The name "America" first appeared in 1507 on a world map by Martin Waldseemüller, derived from the Latin form of explorer Amerigo Vespucci's name. By 1538, Gerardus Mercator applied it to both North and South America. In English, "the Americas" or "the New World" typically refers to the combined landmass, while "America" alone often denotes the United States. This usage varies by language; for example, in Spanish and Portuguese, "América" is a single continent, and "estadounidense" (United Statesian) is preferred for U.S. citizens to avoid ambiguity.

### Pre-Columbian Era and Settlement
The pre-Columbian era covers all history before significant European influence. The first inhabitants migrated from Asia, with habitation sites in Alaska and Yukon from at least 20,000 years ago. A second migration of Na-Dene speakers followed, and the Inuit migration c. 3500 BCE completed the general settlement by Indigenous peoples. These civilizations, such as the Maya and Inca, developed permanent settlements, agriculture, and complex societies. Norse colonization in Greenland and Newfoundland (c. 982–1410 CE) was abandoned and had limited impact.

### European Colonization and Decolonization
Large-scale European colonization began with Columbus in 1492. Spain established the first settlements, using them as bases for conquest and the enslavement of Indigenous populations. Diseases from Europe and Africa caused a devastating population crash among Native Americans. Other European powers—France, England, the Netherlands—established colonies, often relying on enslaved Africans and European immigrants. Decolonization began with the American and Haitian Revolutions in the late 1700s, followed by Latin American wars of independence in the early 1800s. Peaceful decolonization continued into the 20th century, with Caribbean nations gaining independence after World War II.

### Geography and Ecology
The Americas span 14,000 km from north to south, with topography dominated by the American Cordillera mountain chain along the west and large river basins (Amazon, Mississippi) in the east. The continents were linked by the Isthmus of Panama about three million years ago, triggering the "Great American Interchange" of flora and fauna. Climate varies from arctic tundra to tropical rainforest. The region includes 35 sovereign states and numerous territories.

### Demographics and Culture
The population exceeds one billion, with two-thirds in the United States, Brazil, and Mexico. The population descends from Indigenous, European, African, and Asian groups, with significant mixed ancestries (Mestizo, Mulatto). Christianity is the predominant religion (86%). Spanish, English, and Portuguese are the most widely spoken languages. Culturally, the region is often divided into Latin America (Spanish/Portuguese-speaking) and Anglo-America (English-speaking). Football (soccer) and baseball are major sports.

### Economy
The Americas are a major global economic force. The United States is the world's second-largest exporter and largest importer. The continent dominates global production of soy, sugarcane, coffee, and maize, and is a major producer of beef, chicken, and milk. It holds significant reserves and production of oil, natural gas, and minerals like copper, lithium, and silver. Economic growth varies, with countries like the Dominican Republic and Panama showing rapid expansion, while Haiti has the lowest GDP per capita.

## Terms
- **Columbian Exchange**: The widespread transfer of plants, animals, culture, human populations, technology, and ideas between the Americas and the Old World following Columbus's 1492 voyage.
- **Pre-Columbian Era**: The period in the history of the Americas before the arrival of significant European influences.
- **Mestizo**: A person of mixed European and Indigenous ancestry.
- **Latin America**: The region of the Americas where Romance languages (Spanish, Portuguese, French) are predominantly spoken.
- **Anglo-America**: The region of the Americas where English is the predominant language, primarily the United States and Canada.
- **Great American Interchange**: The major westward and eastward migration of mammals between North and South America after the formation of the Isthmus of Panama.
- **Isthmus of Panama**: The narrow strip of land connecting North and South America, formed about three million years ago.
- **American Cordillera**: A series of mountain ranges running along the western coast of the Americas.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact dates, routes, and number of migrations during the initial peopling of the Americas remain subject to ongoing archaeological and genetic research.
- The linguistic and ethnic classification of Indigenous American language groups and populations is a complex and debated field.
- The appropriate terminology for referring to the United States and its citizens in relation to the wider Americas varies by language and cultural context, leading to ongoing discussion.
