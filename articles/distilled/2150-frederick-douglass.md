# Frederick Douglass

## Overview
Frederick Douglass (c. 1818–1895) was the most important leader of the 19th-century movement for African-American civil rights. Born into slavery, he escaped to become a renowned orator, writer, and statesman whose life and work fundamentally challenged the institution of slavery and shaped the fight for equality in the United States.

## Timeline
- **c. February 14, 1818** — Born into slavery in Talbot County, Maryland.
- **1838** — Escapes slavery and moves to New Bedford, Massachusetts.
- **1841** — Delivers first major anti-slavery speech at a convention in Nantucket.
- **1845** — Publishes first autobiography, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass*.
- **1845–1847** — Tours Ireland and Great Britain; British supporters purchase his freedom.
- **1847** — Founds the abolitionist newspaper *The North Star* in Rochester, New York.
- **1848** — Attends the Seneca Falls Convention, advocating for women's suffrage.
- **1852** — Delivers famous speech, "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?"
- **1863** — Recruits Black soldiers for the Union Army with "Men of Color to Arms!"
- **1872** — First African American nominated for U.S. Vice President (Equal Rights Party).
- **1877** — Appointed U.S. Marshal for the District of Columbia.
- **1889** — Appointed U.S. Minister to Haiti.
- **February 20, 1895** — Dies of a heart attack in Washington, D.C.

## Body

### Early Life and Escape
Frederick Augustus Washington Bailey was born into slavery on Maryland's Eastern Shore. Separated from his mother in infancy, he was later sent to Baltimore, where Sophia Auld began teaching him the alphabet. Her husband, Hugh Auld, forbade it, arguing literacy would make a slave discontented. Douglass later called this "the first decidedly antislavery lecture" he had ever heard, as it revealed that knowledge was "the pathway from slavery to freedom." He secretly continued learning to read and write. In 1833, he was sent to Edward Covey, a "slave-breaker," whose brutal whippings broke Douglass's spirit until he physically fought back, an act he saw as transforming him "from a slave" into "a man." With the help of Anna Murray, a free Black woman, Douglass escaped to New York City in September 1838. He and Murray married and settled in New Bedford, Massachusetts, adopting the surname Douglass.

### Abolitionist Career and Autobiographies
In Massachusetts, Douglass became a leading figure in the abolitionist movement. His powerful oratory and writings, including his first autobiography *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave* (1845), provided a living counterexample to pro-slavery claims that enslaved people lacked intellectual capacity. To avoid recapture after his book's fame, he toured Ireland and Great Britain (1845–1847), where he experienced freedom from racial discrimination and gained supporters who purchased his legal freedom. Upon returning, he founded *The North Star* newspaper. He later broke with mentor William Lloyd Garrison, rejecting Garrison's view that the Constitution was a pro-slavery document. Douglass came to believe the Constitution could be used as an instrument against slavery, a shift that created a major division in the abolitionist movement.

### Civil War, Reconstruction, and Later Life
Douglass argued that the Civil War's aim must be the complete abolition of slavery and that African Americans should fight for their freedom. He recruited Black soldiers, including his own sons, for the Union Army. After the war, he campaigned for the rights of freed slaves, supporting the 15th Amendment granting Black men the vote, though this caused a rift with some women's suffrage leaders like Elizabeth Cady Stanton. He held several political appointments, including U.S. Marshal and Recorder of Deeds for D.C., and served as U.S. Minister to Haiti. He continued to speak and write on civil rights, education, and women's suffrage until his death in 1895.

## Terms
- **Abolitionist**: A person who advocated for the immediate end of slavery.
- **Orator**: A skilled public speaker.
- **Slave-breaker**: A person hired to brutally punish and subdue enslaved people to ensure their obedience.
- **Underground Railroad**: A network of secret routes and safe houses used by enslaved people to escape to free states and Canada.
- **Reconstruction**: The period (1865–1877) after the Civil War during which the Southern states were reintegrated into the Union and laws were passed to protect the rights of formerly enslaved people.
- **Freedman's Savings Bank**: A private corporation established by Congress in 1865 to manage savings for freed slaves and Black soldiers.
- **Seneca Falls Convention**: The first women's rights convention, held in 1848, which issued a Declaration of Sentiments demanding equality for women.
- **15th Amendment**: Ratified in 1870, it prohibited the federal and state governments from denying a citizen the right to vote based on "race, color, or previous condition of servitude."

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Birth Date**: Douglass's exact birth date is unknown. He chose to celebrate February 14, but historian Dickson J. Preston determined he was likely born in February 1818.
- **Relationship with John Brown**: The nature of Douglass's involvement with John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry is debated. Douglass declined to join the raid, considering it suicidal, but the full extent of his prior knowledge and support remains a subject of historical analysis.
- **Relationship with Ottilie Assing**: Biographer David W. Blight concludes Douglass and journalist Ottilie Assing "were probably lovers," though surviving correspondence contains no definitive proof.
