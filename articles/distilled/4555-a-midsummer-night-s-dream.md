# A Midsummer Night's Dream

## Overview
*A Midsummer Night's Dream* is a comedy play by William Shakespeare, written between 1594 and 1596. It is one of his most popular and widely performed works, weaving together multiple plots centered on love, magic, and transformation. The play explores the chaotic and irrational nature of desire through the interactions of Athenian lovers, fairies, and amateur actors in a moonlit forest.

## Timeline
- **c. 1594–1596** — Shakespeare writes *A Midsummer Night's Dream*.
- **1598** — First known mention of the play in Francis Meres's *Palladis Tamia*.
- **8 October 1600** — Play entered into the Stationers' Register by Thomas Fisher.
- **1600** — First quarto (Q1) edition published.
- **1619** — Second quarto (Q2) printed by William Jaggard in the "False Folio".
- **1623** — Play appears in the First Folio.
- **1 January 1604** — First certain court performance at Hampton Court.
- **1642–1660** — Theatres closed during Puritan Interregnum; subplots performed as drolls.
- **1662** — Samuel Pepys sees the play and calls it "the most insipid ridiculous play".
- **1692** — Henry Purcell's musical adaptation *The Fairy-Queen* is performed.
- **1840** — Madame Vestris returns the full text to the stage at Covent Garden.
- **1935** — Major Hollywood film adaptation directed by Max Reinhardt.
- **1970** — Peter Brook's influential minimalist production for the Royal Shakespeare Company.

## Body

### Plot and Structure
The play's action is set in Athens and a nearby enchanted forest, revolving around the wedding of Duke Theseus and the Amazon queen Hippolyta. Four subplots interconnect: the conflict between Theseus and Hippolyta over an Indian changeling boy; the romantic entanglements of four young Athenians (Hermia, Lysander, Helena, and Demetrius); the rehearsal of a play-within-a-play by a group of amateur actors (the "Mechanicals"); and the interference of fairies. The Mechanicals, led by the carpenter Peter Quince and the over-enthusiastic weaver Nick Bottom, rehearse the tragic love story of "Pyramus and Thisbe" for the wedding.

The central action occurs in the forest, where the fairy king Oberon and queen Titania are estranged. To punish Titania, Oberon instructs his sprite Puck to use a magical flower ("love-in-idleness") to make her fall in love with the first creature she sees upon waking—which becomes the donkey-headed Bottom. Puck mistakenly applies the love juice to the Athenian Lysander instead of Demetrius, causing chaotic romantic confusion. By the end, Oberon reverses the spells, the lovers are reunited correctly, and the Mechanicals perform their comically bad play for the wedding celebration.

### Sources and Date
The play's exact composition date is debated but is usually placed in 1595 or early 1596, based on topical references and an allusion to Edmund Spenser's *Epithalamion*. It was likely written for an aristocratic wedding, possibly that of Thomas Berkeley and Elizabeth Carey. While not a direct adaptation, Shakespeare drew inspiration from sources like Ovid's *Metamorphoses* and Chaucer's "The Knight's Tale." The play was performed at The Theatre and later The Globe.

### Themes and Criticism
Scholars have analyzed the play through various lenses. A central theme is the nature of love, depicted as irrational, transformative, and sometimes dark. The "Carnivalesque" reading, endorsed by David Wiles and Harold Bloom, sees the play as part of an aristocratic festival that temporarily upends social order. Critics like David Bevington highlight the tension between sexual desire and rational restraint.

The play's treatment of time is ambiguous, with references to a new moon and the "rite of May" creating a confused, magical timeframe. Maurice Hunt and Victor Kiernan discuss the loss of individual identity in the forest, where characters' desires and roles blur. Gender roles and patriarchal authority are also key themes; Louis Montrose notes how female power is brought under male control, while Leonard Tennenhouse contrasts the rigid law of Athens with the chaotic freedom of the fairy world.

Critical history spans centuries. Early critics like Samuel Pepys (1662) dismissed it, while 19th-century critics like August Wilhelm Schlegel found unity in its plots. 20th-century interpretations include Jan Kott's focus on violence and sexuality, and feminist and psychoanalytic readings. The play's performance history shows a shift from heavily adapted spectacles in the 19th century to more textually faithful and conceptually innovative productions in the 20th and 21st centuries.

## Terms
- **Mechanicals**: The group of amateur actors, all working-class craftsmen, who rehearse and perform the play-within-a-play.
- **Love-in-idleness**: A magical flower whose juice, when applied to a sleeping person's eyelids, causes them to fall in love with the first living thing they see.
- **Changeling**: A child secretly exchanged for another; in the play, the Indian boy at the center of Oberon and Titania's dispute.
- **Carnivalesque**: A literary concept describing a temporary, festive overturning of social hierarchies and norms.
- **Decorum**: In literary criticism, the principle that characters should behave in ways appropriate to their social station.
- **Quarto (Q1, Q2)**: An early printed edition of a play, typically in a smaller format than a folio.
- **First Folio**: The first collected edition of Shakespeare's plays, published in 1623.
- **Incidental music**: Music composed to accompany a play, often including overtures and interludes.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Date of Composition**: While usually dated 1595–1596, the exact year and occasion (e.g., a specific wedding) remain debated.
- **Interpretation of Love**: Critics disagree on whether the play presents love as a positive, unifying force or as a dark, irrational, and potentially destructive passion.
- **Role of Theseus**: Scholars debate whether Theseus represents rational order and patriarchal authority or if his speech on "the lunatic, the lover, and the poet" validates imagination over reason.
- **The Changeling Boy**: The nature of Oberon's desire for Titania's Indian ward is ambiguous, leading to interpretations ranging from a political power struggle to psychosexual readings.
- **Time Scheme**: The play's internal chronology is inconsistent, leading to ongoing discussion about whether this is a flaw, a deliberate dream-like effect, or a reference to a specific "dark of the moon" period.
