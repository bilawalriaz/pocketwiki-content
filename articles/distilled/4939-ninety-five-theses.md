# Ninety-five Theses

## Overview
The Ninety-five Theses, written by Martin Luther in 1517, were a list of propositions challenging the Catholic Church's practice of selling indulgences. While framed as an invitation for academic debate, their rapid distribution and the controversy they sparked are retrospectively considered to have launched the Protestant Reformation, a major schism that reshaped European religion and politics.

## Timeline
- **1515** — Pope Leo X grants a plenary indulgence to finance St. Peter's Basilica.
- **1517** — Johann Tetzel preaches the indulgence near Wittenberg, alarming Luther.
- **31 Oct 1517** — Luther sends his Ninety-five Theses to Archbishop Albert of Brandenburg.
- **Nov 1517** — The Theses are printed and distributed across Germany.
- **Feb 1518** — Pope Leo X orders Luther's superior to silence him.
- **Aug 1518** — Luther is summoned to Rome to defend his views.
- **Oct 1518** — Luther defends his position before Cardinal Cajetan at Augsburg.
- **1521** — Luther is excommunicated after refusing to recant.

## Body

### Background and Cause
In the early 16th-century Catholic Church, indulgences were part of the "economy of salvation," reducing the temporal punishment for sins that remained after confession. This system was abused by clergy selling indulgences for profit. In 1517, Pope Leo X authorized a major indulgence to fund St. Peter's Basilica. Preacher Johann Tetzel's campaign near Wittenberg led Luther's parishioners to believe they no longer needed true repentance. Luther, a professor of moral theology, had long criticized indulgences for cheapening grace. He composed the Theses to challenge the theology and practice behind these sales.

### Content and Argument
The Theses were structured as propositions for academic debate. Luther argued that true repentance is an inner, lifelong struggle, not an external transaction. He challenged the pope's authority, stating the pope could only remit punishments he himself had administered, not the guilt of sin or punishments in purgatory. Luther attacked the "treasury of merit" doctrine underpinning indulgences, calling the true treasure of the church the gospel of Christ. He criticized indulgence preachers for promoting greed and false certainty, and he presented common laypeople's objections, such as why the pope didn't simply empty purgatory if he could.

### Intent and Distribution
Luther intended the Theses to initiate a scholarly disputation, a standard academic practice. On 31 October 1517, he sent them to Archbishop Albert of Brandenburg, expressing pastoral concern about misunderstandings fostered by indulgence preaching. While the traditional story of Luther posting the Theses on the Wittenberg church door is debated, the documents were quickly copied and printed in Basel, Leipzig, and Nuremberg. A German translation by Kaspar Nützel further accelerated their spread, turning a local academic exercise into a public controversy.

### Immediate Reaction and Escalation
The Theses provoked swift opposition. Tetzel called for Luther to be burned as a heresy. Theologian Johann Eck attacked Luther personally, leading to a public dispute. In 1518, Luther was summoned to Rome and later defended himself before Cardinal Cajetan, who identified two key heresies: Luther's denial of the papal treasury of merit and his assertion that a Christian could be certain of their forgiveness. Luther refused to recant, appealing to the pope, and was ultimately excommunicated in 1521.

### Legacy
The controversy ignited by the Ninety-five Theses became the catalyst for the Protestant Reformation. Although Luther later considered the indulgence issue minor compared to his later theological breakthroughs, the dispute propelled him into leadership of a movement that fractured the Western Church. The date of the Theses' circulation, 31 October, was established as Reformation Day, a public holiday in Germany commemorating the event's profound historical impact.

## Terms
*   **Indulgence:** A remission of the temporal punishment for sins, granted by the Catholic Church, which was believed to be drawn from the "treasury of merit."
*   **Purgatory:** In Catholic doctrine, a state of purification after death for those who die in God's grace but still need to be cleansed of venial sin or satisfy temporal punishment.
*   **Plenary Indulgence:** An indulgence that grants complete remission of all temporal punishment due to sin.
*   **Treasury of Merit:** The Catholic doctrine that the excess merits of Christ and the saints are stored in a "treasury" from which the pope can draw to grant indulgences.
*   **Temporal Punishment:** The suffering or penance required to atone for sin after the guilt has been forgiven through confession.
*   **Disputation:** A formal academic debate, a standard practice in medieval universities where a scholar would propose theses to be argued.
*   **Vicar of Christ:** A title for the Pope, signifying his role as the earthly representative of Jesus Christ.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **The Posting of the Theses:** The iconic story that Luther nailed the Theses to the door of All Saints' Church on 31 October 1517 is a later tradition. Historians debate whether he posted them at all, or if the act occurred later in November.
*   **Luther's Intent:** Scholars question whether Luther intended to spark a widespread controversy or was genuinely surprised by the explosive reaction. Some argue he used the academic format of a disputation as a protective cover for his criticisms.
*   **The Theses' Significance:** Luther himself later downplayed the importance of the indulgence controversy relative to his later theological works, suggesting the Theses were not initially intended as a break with Catholic doctrine.
