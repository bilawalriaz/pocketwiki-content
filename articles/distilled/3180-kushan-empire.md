# Kushan Empire

## Overview
The Kushan Empire (c. 30–c. 375 CE) was a major syncretic empire formed by the Yuezhi people in Central Asia. It became a pivotal hub for trade and cultural exchange between the Roman Empire, Han China, and India, playing a crucial role in the spread of Buddhism along the Silk Road.

## Timeline
- **c. 176–160 BC** — The Yuezhi are forced to migrate west from China after defeat by the Xiongnu.
- **c. 135 BC** — The Yuezhi reach and settle in Greco-Bactria.
- **c. 30–85 CE** — Kujula Kadphises unites the Yuezhi tribes and founds the Kushan dynasty.
- **c. 127 CE** — Kanishka the Great begins his reign, marking the empire's peak.
- **c. 127 CE** — The Kushan era, used as a calendar reference, begins.
- **2nd century CE** — The empire facilitates the spread of Mahayana Buddhism to China.
- **c. 230 CE** — Vasudeva I, the last "Great Kushan" emperor, rules until c. 230.
- **c. 240 CE** — The Sasanian Empire invades, establishing the Kushano-Sasanian Kingdom in the west.
- **c. 270–350 CE** — The "Little Kushans" rule in the Punjab region.
- **c. 350 CE** — The Kidarites invade, overthrowing the Kushano-Sasanians and ending major Kushan rule.

## Body

### Origins and Early History
The Kushans were one of five branches of the Yuezhi, an Indo-European nomadic people who migrated from northwestern China to Bactria (in modern Afghanistan and Uzbekistan) after 160 BC. The dynasty was founded by Kujula Kadphises, who united the tribes and conquered territories including Kabul, Paktiya, and Gandhara. Archaeological evidence from sites like Khalchayan shows early Kushan rulers with artificially deformed skulls, a Central Asian nomadic practice.

### Cultural Syncretism and Administration
The Kushans were highly syncretic, adopting and blending Hellenistic, Iranian, and Indian cultural elements. They used the Greek alphabet to write the Bactrian language and minted coins featuring a diverse pantheon of over thirty deities from Greek, Iranian, and Indian traditions. Administratively, they introduced a system of Kshatrapas (satraps) and Mahakshatrapas (great satraps). Their rule linked Indian Ocean seaborne trade with Silk Road commerce, fostering a period of relative peace and prosperity known as the "Pax Kushana."

### Territorial Expansion and Zenith
Under Kanishka the Great (r. c. 127–c. 150), the empire reached its maximum extent, covering parts of modern Afghanistan, Pakistan, India, Nepal, and Central Asia. The Rabatak inscription confirms his rule extended deep into the Gangetic plain, including cities like Saketa, Kausambi, and Pataliputra. The empire had two capitals: Purushapura (Peshawar) and Mathura. Kanishka is also renowned in Buddhist tradition for convening a great council and is considered one of Buddhism's greatest benefactors.

### Religion and the Spread of Buddhism
The Kushans were great patrons of Buddhism, which they helped spread to Central Asia and China. Their coinage depicts the Buddha and bodhisattvas like Maitreya. Emperor Huvishka (r. c. 150–c. 190) is associated with early epigraphic evidence of the Buddha Amitabha and may have been a Mahayana follower. Kushan Buddhist missionaries, such as Lokaksema, were active in Chinese capitals, translating scriptures and facilitating the Silk Road transmission of Buddhism.

### Art and Coinage
Kushan art, particularly the Greco-Buddhist art of Gandhara, fused Hellenistic, Persian, and Indian styles. Sculptures often show Western-style drapery and curly hair. The Kushans produced abundant coinage in gold, silver, and copper, which served as propaganda and was widely copied. Their gold coins, called Dinara, were once thought to be made from melted Roman coins, but modern analysis shows their gold has a distinct origin with high platinum and palladium content.

### Decline and Successors
After Vasudeva I (r. c. 190–c. 230), the empire fragmented. The western territories were conquered by the Sasanian Empire, which installed vassal rulers known as the Kushano-Sasanians. The eastern "Little Kushans" in Punjab were eventually subjugated by the Gupta Empire in the mid-4th century. The final remnants of Kushan rule in the northwest were destroyed by the Kidarites and later the Alchon Huns in the 5th century.

## Terms
- **Yuezhi**: The nomadic Indo-European people from whom the Kushans originated.
- **Syncretic**: Blending different beliefs, cultures, or schools of thought.
- **Pax Kushana**: A period of relative peace and stability during the height of the Kushan Empire.
- **Bactrian**: An Eastern Iranian language that became the official language of the Kushans, written in a modified Greek script.
- **Kshatrapa/Mahakshatrapa**: Titles for provincial governors (satraps) and great satraps in the Kushan administrative system.
- **Greco-Buddhist art**: An artistic style that fused Hellenistic Greek and Buddhist Indian elements, prominent in Gandhara under the Kushans.
- **Dinara**: A name for Kushan gold coins, derived from the Roman denarius.
- **Kushano-Sasanians**: Persian vassal rulers installed by the Sasanian Empire in former western Kushan territories.
- **Little Kushans**: The later, weakened Kushan rulers who held power in the Punjab region after the empire's fragmentation.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact relationship and distinction between the Yuezhi and the Kushans remains unclear in historical sources.
- The origin of the gold used in Kushan coinage is unknown; it is not of Roman provenance as once believed.
- The precise start date of Kanishka's era (and thus his reign) has been debated, though 127 CE is now generally accepted based on research by Harry Falk.
