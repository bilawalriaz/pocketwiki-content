# Chimor

## Overview
Chimor, also known as the Kingdom of Chimor or Chimú Empire, was the largest and most powerful kingdom on the Peruvian coast during the Late Intermediate Period (c. 900–1470 CE). Succeeding the Moche culture, it developed a complex, centralized state with a capital at Chan Chan, renowned for its monumental architecture, sophisticated irrigation, and elite metalworking. Its significance lies in being the last major Andean polity to resist the expansion of the Inca Empire.

## Timeline
- **c. 900 CE** — Chimú culture emerges on the north coast of Peru, succeeding the Moche.
- **c. 1370** — Nacen-pinco (Ñancempinco) rules, believed to have expanded the kingdom's frontiers.
- **c. 1320** — Chimú conquer the Jequetepeque Valley, establishing a subordinate administrative center at Farfán.
- **c. 1400–1450** — A mass child sacrifice event occurs at Huanchaco, possibly linked to El Niño.
- **1462–1470** — Inca emperor Topa Inca Yupanqui conquers Chimor, defeating Emperor Minchançaman.
- **1493** — Inca conquest of Chimor is nearly complete when Huayna Capac assumes the Inca throne.

## Body

### Origins and Expansion
The Chimú culture arose around 900 CE in the same territory as the earlier Moche civilization. According to Chimú oral history, the kingdom was founded by Taycanamo, who arrived by sea. His descendants, notably Guacricur and Ñancempinco, expanded the realm by conquering surrounding valleys. The first valleys may have joined willingly, but the Sican culture was acquired through conquest. By its peak, Chimor controlled over 1,000 km of coastline, from the Jequetepeque River in the north to as far south as Carabayllo, though its southern expansion was halted by the military power of the Lima Valley.

### Society and Economy
Chimú society was a rigid four-level hierarchy centered on the elite at Chan Chan. The capital, with an estimated population of 30,000, featured ten walled royal compounds (*ciudadelas*), workshops, and homes. The economy was based on intensive agriculture and fishing. The Chimú developed advanced hydraulic engineering, linking valleys with irrigation canals and using sunken farms (*huachaques*) and reservoirs. This system supported the cultivation of beans, sweet potatoes, and cotton. A complex bureaucracy controlled the import of raw materials, their processing into prestige goods by specialized artisans, and the state redistribution of products.

### Religion and Ritual
The Chimú worshipped the moon as their primary deity, believing it more powerful than the sun. They also venerated deities of Mars, Earth, the Sun, and the Sea. Offerings were central to religious rites, with Spondylus shells being highly valued. Archaeological evidence reveals large-scale ritual sacrifice, including the sacrifice of children (possibly to appease deities during El Niño events) and the killing of fishermen, whose remains were found at Punta Lobos.

### Art and Material Culture
The Chimú are renowned for their monochromatic black pottery, often shaped into figures or creatures, and their advanced metallurgy in copper, gold, silver, and tumbaga (a gold-copper alloy). They created intricate ornaments, tools, and ritual objects like the *tumi* knife. Textiles were made from cotton and camelid wool, often adorned with feathers from long-distance trade. Spondylus shell, imported from Ecuador, was a critical luxury good used for ornaments, offerings, and as a symbol of elite status and divine power.

### Downfall
Chimor was the last Andean kingdom capable of stopping the Inca Empire. The Inca conquest began in the 1470s under Topa Inca Yupanqui, who defeated the Chimú emperor Minchançaman. The emperor was taken to Cusco, and Chimú gold and silver were redirected to adorn the Inca temple Qurikancha. The conquest was nearly complete by 1493.

## Terms
- **Ciudadelas**: Large, walled royal compounds at Chan Chan, each associated with a Chimú king.
- **Huaca**: A sacred shrine or object of worship found in various districts.
- **Huachaques**: Sunken farms where the Chimú excavated to reach moist subsoil for agriculture.
- **Split inheritance**: A practice where a ruler's heir had to build a new palace, while the predecessor's wealth was distributed to more distant relatives.
- **Tumbaga**: An alloy of copper and gold used in Chimú metalworking.
- **Spondylus**: A genus of marine bivalve mollusk whose shell was a highly valued trade good and ritual object.
- **Audiencias**: U-shaped rooms in Chimú architecture, possibly used to control the flow of supplies.
- **Fonga Sigde**: An official who spread ground Spondylus shell powder to form a "red carpet" for the king.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact southern limit of Chimú expansion is contested by historians and archaeologists.
- Scholars debate the causes for Chimú territorial expansion, with theories ranging from deviations in inheritance patterns to state responses to El Niño events that destroyed irrigation canals.
- The means by which Spondylus was transported from Ecuador to Chimor—whether via independent merchants, state-administered trade, sea, or land routes—remains unclear.
