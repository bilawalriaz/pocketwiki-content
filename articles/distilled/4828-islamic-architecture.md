# Islamic architecture

## Overview
Islamic architecture encompasses the building styles of Islamic civilization from the 7th century to the present, spanning a vast geographic area from West Africa to East Asia. It is defined by the adaptation of local traditions to religious and cultural needs, resulting in diverse regional styles unified by common elements like mosques, courtyards, and geometric ornamentation. Its evolution reflects the spread of Islam, the rise and fall of empires, and the synthesis of Greco-Roman, Persian, and local artistic traditions.

## Timeline
- **622** — Prophet Muhammad builds the first mosque in Medina, establishing the hypostyle courtyard model.
- **661–750** — Umayyad Caliphate introduces the mihrab, horseshoe arch, and extensive mosaic decoration, synthesizing Byzantine and Sasanian styles.
- **750–1258** — Abbasid Caliphate shifts the center to Baghdad/Samarra, developing new stucco styles, the spiral minaret, and the four-centred arch.
- **785–786** — Great Mosque of Córdoba founded, marking the start of distinctive Moorish architecture in al-Andalus.
- **879** — Ibn Tulun Mosque completed in Cairo, a key example of early Abbasid-influenced architecture in Egypt.
- **970** — Fatimids found Cairo and build al-Azhar Mosque, developing their own architectural style.
- **11th–12th c.** — Seljuks popularize the four-iwan mosque plan and advance brick vaulting and tilework in Iran and Central Asia.
- **1250–1517** — Mamluks build elaborate multi-functional complexes in Cairo, characterized by stone carving and bulbous domes.
- **1453–17th c.** — Ottomans, inspired by Hagia Sophia, develop monumental central-dome mosques, perfected by architect Mimar Sinan.
- **1526–1857** — Mughals build iconic Indo-Islamic monuments like the Taj Mahal, blending Persian, Timurid, and Indian elements.
- **14th–16th c.** — Songhai and Hausa styles emerge in West Africa, using mud-brick and distinctive pyramidal minarets.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
Early Islamic architecture (7th–8th centuries) was shaped by the conquest of Byzantine and Sasanian territories. The first mosque in Medina, built by Muhammad, was a simple courtyard structure that evolved into the hypostyle model (a hall with a roof supported by columns). The Umayyad Caliphate (661–750) synthesized these traditions, introducing key features like the mihrab (a niche indicating the qibla, or direction of prayer) and the horseshoe arch. Major early monuments include the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem and the Great Mosque of Damascus, which used extensive mosaics and adapted basilica plans.

### Regional Diversification (10th–15th Centuries)
After the Abbasid Caliphate fragmented, distinct regional styles emerged. In al-Andalus and the Maghreb, Moorish architecture featured horseshoe arches, ribbed domes, and intricate stucco and tilework (zellij). In Iran and Central Asia, Seljuk and later Timurid architecture emphasized brick construction, glazed tiles, and the four-iwan mosque plan. The Mamluks in Egypt and Syria built complex, multi-functional urban complexes with elaborate stone carving. The Ottomans developed a style of grand, central-domed mosques, while the Mughals in India created a synthesis of Persian and local forms, exemplified by the Taj Mahal.

### Key Architectural Elements
Common elements include the courtyard (ṣaḥn), often with a fountain for ablutions, and the hypostyle prayer hall. Vaulting techniques evolved from Umayyad diaphragm arches to complex Persian muqarnas (honeycomb vaults). Domes varied regionally: Ottoman domes were centralized and spherical, Persian domes were often double-shelled and bulbous, and Maghrebi domes featured ribbed patterns. Ornamentation relied on geometric patterns, arabesques (stylized floral motifs), and calligraphy, as figural representation was generally avoided in religious contexts.

### Urban and Domestic Architecture
Islamic cities were organized around principles of privacy and hierarchy, with public spaces like mosques and markets giving way to private residential quarters. Domestic architecture centered on inward-facing courtyard houses. Distinctive features include the mashrabiya (a projecting wooden lattice screen for privacy) and, in regions like Yemen, multi-story tower houses. Gardens, especially the Persian chahar bagh (four-part paradise garden), were integral to palaces and tombs.

## Terms
- **Mihrab**: A concave niche in the qibla wall of a mosque that indicates the direction of Mecca.
- **Minaret**: A tower attached to a mosque from which the call to prayer is issued.
- **Qibla**: The direction of the Kaaba in Mecca, towards which Muslims face during prayer.
- **Hypostyle**: An architectural form for a hall or roof supported by a grid of columns.
- **Muqarnas**: A three-dimensional, honeycomb-like vaulting technique used for decoration and structural transition.
- **Iwan**: A rectangular hall or space, usually vaulted, walled on three sides, with one end entirely open.
- **Arabesque**: A form of artistic decoration consisting of rhythmic, interlacing patterns of vegetal or geometric motifs.
- **Chahar bagh**: A Persian-style garden divided into four quadrants by walkways or flowing water, symbolizing paradise.
- **Zellij**: A style of Islamic art using individually chiseled geometric tiles to form intricate mosaic patterns.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate whether "Islamic architecture" constitutes a unified style or simply architecture produced in Muslim-majority regions, given its vast geographic and cultural diversity. The origins of certain elements, like the minaret and muqarnas, are uncertain and may have developed independently in multiple regions. There is also discussion about the extent to which pre-Islamic Arabian architecture influenced early mosque design versus the dominant influence of conquered Byzantine and Sasanian traditions. Finally, the role of religious symbolism versus purely functional or aesthetic drivers in shaping architectural forms remains a topic of scholarly interpretation.
