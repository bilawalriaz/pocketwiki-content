# Investiture Controversy

## Overview
The Investiture Controversy was a major 11th-12th century conflict between the papacy and secular rulers, primarily the Holy Roman Emperor, over who had the authority to appoint (invest) bishops and abbots. This struggle reshaped the balance of power between church and state in medieval Europe, leading to nearly 50 years of open conflict and culminating in a compromise that limited imperial authority and strengthened the papacy.

## Timeline
- **1059** — Pope Nicholas II's bull *In nomine Domini* restricts papal elections to cardinals and bans lay investiture.
- **1075** — Pope Gregory VII issues the *Dictatus papae*, asserting exclusive papal power to appoint or depose churchmen.
- **1076** — Pope Gregory VII excommunicates and deposes Emperor Henry IV.
- **1077** — Henry IV performs penance at Canossa; excommunication is lifted.
- **1080** — Gregory VII excommunicates Henry IV again; Henry installs Antipope Clement III.
- **1084** — Henry IV captures Rome and crowns Clement III; Gregory VII is rescued by Normans.
- **1107** — Concordat of London resolves the English investiture dispute between Henry I and the papacy.
- **1111** — Henry V forces Pope Paschal II to grant him investiture rights in an abortive concordat.
- **1122** — Concordat of Worms ends the main conflict between Pope Callixtus II and Emperor Henry V.

## Body

### Background and Causes
After the fall of Rome, secular rulers (lay investiture) routinely appointed bishops and abbots, who often came from the nobility. This practice, combined with simony (the sale of church offices), allowed emperors like Henry III to control the church for administrative and military support. The reform movement, led by popes like Nicholas II and Gregory VII, sought to end this lay control and assert the church's independence and spiritual authority.

### The Conflict with Henry IV
The core struggle erupted between Pope Gregory VII and Emperor Henry IV. Gregory's reforms, including the ban on lay investiture, directly challenged Henry's authority to appoint bishops, who were crucial to his power. Henry's defiance led to his excommunication and deposition in 1076, which empowered rebellious German princes. The dramatic "Road to Canossa" in 1077 saw Henry do penance to regain absolution, but the conflict resumed. Henry eventually installed an antipope and seized Rome, forcing Gregory into exile, where he died in 1085. The papacy later regained strength under Urban II, who preached the First Crusade.

### The Concordat of Worms
Henry V continued the struggle but faced similar revolts and excommunication. After decades of conflict, the Concordat of Worms (1122) provided a compromise. It ended lay investiture: the emperor renounced investing bishops with spiritual symbols (ring and staff). In Germany, the emperor retained a role in elections (presiding over disputed cases) and received secular homage (symbolized by a scepter) from bishops. In Italy and Burgundy, elections were free from imperial interference. The emperor also renounced the right to choose the pope.

### The English Investiture Controversy
A parallel, earlier dispute in England between King Henry I and Archbishop Anselm was resolved by the Concordat of London (1107). Like the later German agreement, the king gave up investing bishops with spiritual symbols but retained the right to receive homage for their temporal lands. This model influenced the final settlement in the Empire.

## Terms
- **Investiture**: The ceremony of conferring the symbols of an office (e.g., ring and staff for spiritual authority, scepter for temporal authority).
- **Lay Investiture**: The appointment of church officials by secular (non-clergy) rulers.
- **Simony**: The buying or selling of church offices.
- **Concordat**: An agreement between the papacy and a civil power defining church-state relations.
- **Dictatus papae**: A list of 27 propositions by Gregory VII asserting supreme papal authority.
- **Antipope**: A claimant to the papacy in opposition to the legitimately elected pope.
- **Temporalities**: The landed properties and secular revenues associated with a church office.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The Concordat of Worms was ambiguous, leading scholars to debate whether it truly achieved the papacy's reform goals or was a pragmatic compromise that preserved significant imperial influence.
- The long-term impact is debated: some argue it permanently weakened imperial authority and delayed the formation of nation-states in Germany and Italy until the 19th century, while others see it as establishing a model for limiting secular power over the church.
- The political scientist Bruce Bueno de Mesquita argues the Concordat created incentives for northern European rulers to foster prosperity and liberty to gain leverage against the papacy, potentially enabling the later Protestant Reformation.
