# Ethiopian Empire

## Overview
The Ethiopian Empire, also known as Abyssinia, was a historic state in the Horn of Africa that lasted from the establishment of the Solomonic dynasty in 1270 until the 1974 coup d'état. It is notable for its ancient Christian heritage, its long resistance to European colonization, and its expansion to form the modern borders of Ethiopia and Eritrea.

## Timeline
- **c. 1270** — Yekuno Amlak founds the Solomonic dynasty, replacing the Zagwe kingdom.
- **1314–1344** — Emperor Amda Seyon I conducts major conquests, expanding the empire significantly.
- **1434–1468** — Reign of Emperor Zara Yaqob, a peak of centralized power and cultural flourishing.
- **1529–1543** — Jihad of Imam Ahmad Gran, supported by the Ottoman Empire, nearly conquers Ethiopia.
- **1543** — Ethiopian and Portuguese forces defeat Ahmad Gran at the Battle of Wayna Daga.
- **1636** — Emperor Fasilides founds Gondar as the new capital, beginning the Gondarine period.
- **1769–1855** — The *Zemene Mesafint* ("Era of the Princes"), a period of decentralization and warlord rule.
- **1855–1868** — Emperor Tewodros II reunifies the empire and begins its modernization.
- **1896** — Emperor Menelik II defeats Italy at the Battle of Adwa, securing Ethiopian independence.
- **1936–1941** — Italy occupies Ethiopia; Emperor Haile Selassie goes into exile.
- **1941** — Haile Selassie returns to the throne with British help.
- **1974** — The Derg military junta overthrows Emperor Haile Selassie, ending the monarchy.

## Body

### Founding and Early Expansion
The Solomonic dynasty was founded around 1270 by Yekuno Amlak, who claimed descent from the last Aksumite king and, through the legendary Queen of Sheba and King Solomon, from the biblical Menelik I. This lineage, codified in the 14th-century text *Kebra Nagast*, provided the dynasty's religious and political legitimacy. Initially a small state, the empire expanded dramatically under warrior-emperors like Amda Seyon I (r. 1314–1344), who doubled its size through campaigns against neighboring Muslim sultanates. This expansion shifted the regional balance of power in favor of the Christian empire for centuries.

### Golden Age and Conflict with Adal
The empire reached its zenith under Emperor Zara Yaqob (r. 1434–1468), who centralized administration, built churches, and extended hegemony. However, the neighboring Adal Sultanate, later supported by the Ottoman Empire, launched a major jihad in the 16th century under Imam Ahmad Gran. This invasion, beginning in 1529, devastated the empire and forced the emperor into hiding. The empire was saved only with the intervention of Portuguese musketeers, culminating in the decisive Ethiopian-Portuguese victory at the Battle of Wayna Daga in 1543.

### Early Modern Period and Decentralization
Following the Adalite wars, the empire faced new challenges: Ottoman occupation of coastal areas and large-scale Oromo migrations into southern territories. In the 1630s, Emperor Fasilides founded a new capital at Gondar, initiating a cultural golden age. However, after the deaths of Emperors Iyasu II and Iyoas I in the mid-18th century, the empire entered the *Zemene Mesafint*, a period where real power was held by regional warlords (*Ras*) and the emperor was a figurehead.

### Reunification and Modern Expansion
Emperor Tewodros II (r. 1855–1868) ended the *Zemene Mesafint* and reunified the empire. His successor, Yohannes IV, fought off Egyptian and Mahdist invasions. Under Emperor Menelik II (r. 1889–1913), the empire expanded to its modern boundaries through the conquest of southern and western kingdoms. Menelik also successfully defended Ethiopian independence by defeating Italy at the Battle of Adwa in 1896, using modern imported weaponry.

### Italian Occupation and the End of the Monarchy
After the Second Italo-Ethiopian War (1935–1936), Italy occupied Ethiopia and merged it with Eritrea and Italian Somaliland to form Italian East Africa. Emperor Haile Selassie went into exile. During World War II, British forces helped restore him to the throne in 1941. Post-war, Ethiopia became a UN member, and in 1952, Eritrea was federated under Selassie's rule. Despite modernization efforts, including constitutions in 1931 and 1955, the monarchy faced growing discontent due to the 1973 Wollo famine, economic issues, and student radicalism. A military coup by the Derg in 1974 deposed Selassie, formally abolishing the monarchy in 1975.

## Terms
- **Solomonic dynasty**: The imperial dynasty of Ethiopia, claiming descent from King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba, ruling from c. 1270 to 1974.
- **Zagwe dynasty**: The Agaw dynasty that ruled the Ethiopian Highlands before being overthrown by the Solomonic dynasty.
- **Kebra Nagast**: A 14th-century Ethiopian text meaning "Glory of the Kings," which establishes the Solomonic dynasty's biblical lineage.
- **Zemene Mesafint**: The "Era of the Princes" (c. 1769–1855), a period of political decentralization where regional warlords held power.
- **Chewa**: The regiments that formed the backbone of the Ethiopian imperial army, maintained through land grants (*Gult*).
- **Gondarine period**: A cultural golden age (17th–18th centuries) centered in the capital Gondar, marked by artistic and architectural achievements.
- **Adal Sultanate**: A medieval Muslim state in the Horn of Africa that waged major wars against the Ethiopian Empire.
- **Derg**: The military junta that overthrew Emperor Haile Selassie in 1974 and ruled Ethiopia until 1991.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The identity of the last Zagwe king overthrown by Yekuno Amlak is debated; scholars like Taddesse Tamrat argue it was Yetbarak, while Getatchew Mekonnen Hasen states it was Na'akueto La'ab.
- The precise nature and impact of the Oromo migrations on the empire's southern territories remain subjects of scholarly discussion.
- The causes of the *Zemene Mesafint* are complex, involving dynastic struggles, religious conflict, and the rise of regional military elites.
