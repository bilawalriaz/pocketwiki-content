# Lake Nicaragua

## Overview
Lake Nicaragua, also known as Cocibolca, is the largest freshwater lake in Central America. Its tectonic origin and strategic location between the Caribbean Sea and the Pacific Ocean have made it a focal point for historical trade routes, ecological study, and ambitious, though unrealized, canal projects.

## Timeline
- **Pre-1916** — The lake is part of a key trans-isthmian route, used by Cornelius Vanderbilt's Accessory Transit Company.
- **1916** — The U.S. and Nicaragua sign the Bryan–Chamorro Treaty, securing U.S. rights to a potential canal route.
- **1961** — The endemic Lake Nicaragua shark is reclassified as the widespread bull shark.
- **Late 1960s** — Scientists discover bull sharks can travel between the lake and the Caribbean Sea via the San Juan River.
- **1970** — The U.S. and Nicaragua mutually rescind the Bryan–Chamorro Treaty.
- **1981** — A government study finds severe pollution, with 32 tons of raw sewage entering the lake daily.
- **2014** — A 50-year concession is granted to HKND to build a $40 billion interoceanic canal; construction never begins. |

## Body

### Geography and Hydrology
Lake Nicaragua is a tectonic lake with an area of 8,264 km², making it the 19th largest lake in the world. It sits at an elevation of 32.7 meters above sea level and has a maximum depth of 26 meters. The intermittent Tipitapa River connects it to Lake Managua to the north. The lake drains eastward into the Caribbean Sea via the San Juan River, forming the largest international drainage basin in Central America. Despite its proximity to the Pacific Ocean, its outlet is on the Caribbean side.

### Historical and Economic Significance
The lake's connection to the Caribbean via the San Juan River historically made the city of Granada an Atlantic port. Before the Panama Canal, a primary route from the Atlantic to the Pacific involved traveling up the San Juan River, across the lake, and then overland via a stagecoach line. This route made the lake a target for Caribbean pirates. The potential for a canal across Nicaragua led to the Bryan–Chamorro Treaty of 1916, which the U.S. secured to prevent competition with the Panama Canal. The treaty was rescinded in 1970, but canal proposals, such as the 2014 HKND project, have periodically resurfaced. The 2014 project was abandoned due to ecological concerns, protests, and financing issues.

### Ecology and Environment
The lake is home to diverse species, including sawfish, tarpon, and bull sharks, which can navigate the San Juan River's rapids. It contains at least 16 endemic cichlid species in the broader region, though none are strictly endemic to the lake alone. Non-native tilapia are widely farmed but pose a threat to the ecosystem. The lake contains volcanic islands like Ometepe and Zapatera. Significant environmental concerns include pollution from sewage and industry, with a 1981 study finding 32 tons of raw sewage were released daily. The 2014 drought and potential contamination from any future canal construction are ongoing threats.

### Transportation
The lake was a vital component of passenger and freight shipping routes, especially during the California Gold Rush. Boat travel was historically the primary means of reaching islands like Ometepe and the Solentiname archipelago, and for accessing the San Juan River department before modern roads were built.

## Terms
- **Tectonic origin**: Formed by the movement of the Earth's crustal plates.
- **Interoceanic canal**: A navigable waterway connecting two oceans, designed to shorten maritime routes.
- **Endemic**: Native and restricted to a certain place; in ecology, a species found only in a specific geographic area.
- **Synonymized**: In taxonomy, when two different scientific names are found to refer to the same species, the older name typically takes precedence.
- **Effluent**: Liquid waste or sewage discharged into a river or the sea.
- **Aquaculture**: The farming of fish, shellfish, and aquatic plants.

## Debates and Open questions
- The ecological and social impacts of a potential Nicaragua Canal remain a major point of contention.
- The long-term effects of pollution, invasive species (like tilapia), and climate events (like droughts) on the lake's ecosystem are ongoing concerns.
- The feasibility and environmental cost of any future large-scale development projects on the lake are debated.
