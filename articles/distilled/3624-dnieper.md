# Dnieper

## Overview
The Dnieper (or Dnipro) is one of Europe's major transboundary rivers, flowing from Russia through Belarus and Ukraine to the Black Sea. It is the longest river in Ukraine and Belarus and the fourth-longest in Europe, serving as a critical waterway for transportation, hydroelectric power, and a historical and cultural symbol for the region.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — River is part of ancient Amber Road trade routes.
- **Late 8th–early 9th c.** — The "Varangians to the Greeks" trade route via the Dnieper rapids is established.
- **1927–1932** — Dnieper Hydroelectric Station (DniproHES) is built near Zaporizhzhia.
- **1932** — Dnieper rapids are inundated by the Dnieper Reservoir.
- **1944–1950** — DniproHES is rebuilt after WWII destruction.
- **1986** — Chernobyl disaster occurs on the Pripyat River, a major tributary.
- **6 June 2023** — Kakhovka dam is destroyed during the Russian invasion of Ukraine.

## Body

### Names and Etymology
The river has several historical and modern names. The common English name "Dnieper" (with a silent 'D') derives from the Russian *Dnepr*. The Ukrainian name is *Dnipro*. These are cognate with the Old East Slavic *Dŭněprŭ*, whose origin is debated, possibly from Sarmatian for "Farther River" or Scythian for "Deep River." The earlier Graeco-Roman name was *Borysthenes*, derived from a Scythian name possibly meaning "yellow place" or "place of beavers." The Huns called it *Var*, meaning "Broad."

### Geography and Hydrology
The Dnieper is approximately 2,200 km long, with a drainage basin of 504,000 km². It rises in the Valdai Hills of Russia, flows through Belarus and Ukraine, and empties into the Black Sea via the Dnieper–Bug estuary. Its basin contains about 32,000 tributaries, with the main ones being the Sozh, Berezina, Pripyat, and Inhulets. Historically, nine major rapids on its middle course obstructed navigation until they were flooded by reservoirs in the Soviet era.

### Navigation and Economy
About 2,000 km of the river is navigable. It is the primary waterway for Ukraine's river transport, though its overall contribution to total transport is less than 0.5%. A system of reservoirs and ship locks allows large vessels to reach Kyiv. The river is connected to other European waterways via the Dnieper–Bug Canal, though a weir near Brest, Belarus, currently interrupts this international link.

### Hydroelectric Power and Ecology
Six major dams and hydroelectric stations on the river produce about 10% of Ukraine's electricity. The first and most famous is the DniproHES. The river suffers from significant anthropogenic pollution, including radioactive waste from the Prydniprovsky Chemical Plant and the proximity to the Chernobyl Exclusion Zone. The 2023 destruction of the Kakhovka dam caused the reservoir to dry up, revealing the original river course and disrupting irrigation networks.

### Cultural Significance
The Dnieper is a national symbol of Ukraine, mentioned in its anthem. It has inspired literature by authors like Nikolai Gogol and Taras Shevchenko, and appears in films and music. Historical regions like "Dnieper Ukraine" and the Zaporozhian Cossacks ("beyond the rapids") are named for it.

## Terms
- **Liman:** An estuary or lagoon at the mouth of a river, such as the Dnieper–Bug estuary.
- **Tributary:** A river or stream that flows into a larger river.
- **Hydroelectric Station:** A facility that generates electricity using the flow of water, often via a dam.
- **Reservoir:** An artificial lake created by a dam to store water.
- **Rapids:** A swift section of a river where the channel is steep or obstructed.
- **Drainage Basin:** The area of land where all water drains into a common outlet, like a river.
- **Anthropogenic:** Resulting from human activity, often used to describe pollution.

## Debates and Open Questions
The etymology of the river's ancient name is disputed, with competing theories linking it to Sarmatian or Scythian languages. The exact number of historical rapids is also debated, with sources citing either nine major rapids or a smaller number. The future of the international waterway connection via the Dnieper–Bug Canal is uncertain due to political relations and infrastructure barriers.
