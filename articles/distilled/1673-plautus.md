# Plautus

## Overview
Titus Maccius Plautus (c. 254–184 BC) was a Roman playwright of the Old Latin period whose comedies are the earliest surviving complete works of Latin literature. He adapted Greek New Comedy—particularly the plays of Menander—into *Palliata comoedia*, reworking plots and characters to appeal to Roman audiences during the Middle Republic. Plautus transformed Greek models through *contaminatio* (blending multiple sources), expanding the role of the clever slave (*servus callidus*) into a primary plot-driver, and infusing the plays with vigorous wordplay, metrical variety, and distinctly Roman social commentary. His twenty surviving plays, written c. 205–184 BC, profoundly influenced later European comedy, notably Shakespeare’s *The Comedy of Errors* (based on *Menaechmi*) and Molière’s *The Miser* (based on *Aulularia*).

## Body

### Biography and Career
Little is certain about Plautus’s life. Tradition holds he was born in Sarsina (Umbria) c. 254 BC, worked as a stage-carpenter, and adopted the *nomen* "Maccius" (from the Atellan farce clown Maccus) and *agnomen* "Plautus" ("flat-footed"). He allegedly lost money in a nautical venture, then worked as a manual laborer while studying Greek New Comedy, especially Menander. His plays were produced between c. 205 and 184 BC at the *ludi* (religious festival games) on temporary wooden stages, as Rome had no permanent theater until 55 BC. His name became a guarantee of theatrical success.

### Greek Models and *Contaminatio*
Plautus wrote *Palliata comoedia* (comedy in Greek dress), a genre established by Livius Andronicus. His primary models were Greek New Comedy playwrights like Menander, whose works focused on domestic situations rather than the political satire of Old Comedy (Aristophanes). Plautus did not merely translate; he practiced *contaminatio*, blending elements from two or more Greek originals into a single Roman play (e.g., *Bacchides* adds a third deception to Menander’s *Dis Exapaton*). He "colonized" Greek theater: deconstructing tight plots, caricaturing elegant characters, and substituting vigorous, often farcical humor for Menander’s subtlety. Scholars debate his originality: some view him as a creative adapter imposing Roman vitality on Greek forms; others emphasize his dependence on Hellenistic models.

### Character Types and Social Inversion
Plautus relied heavily on stock characters, sacrificing psychological depth for humor. Key types include:
*   **The Clever Slave (*Servus Callidus*):** Derived from Greek comedy but greatly expanded. He drives the plot through deception, delivers exposition via monologue, and achieves a comic inversion of hierarchy—reducing masters to supplicants through the imperative mood and direct audience address.
*   **The Braggart Soldier (*Miles Gloriosus*):** The titular Pyrgopolynices, a vain, cowardly soldier duped by his slave Palaestrio.
*   **The Lustful Old Man (*Senex Amator*):** Figures like Lysidamus (*Casina*) or Demaenetus (*Asinaria*) pursue young women, inviting ridicule for their animalistic passion and childish regression.
*   **The Parasite:** A hanger-on (e.g., Artotrogus) who flatters for food, later evolving into Shakespeare’s Falstaff.
Female roles are less fixed than male ones; designations (*mulier, virgo, meretrix, ancilla*) shift by context. Approximately 18% of Plautus’s ~220 characters are unnamed.

### Language, Style, and Meter
Plautus wrote in colloquial, pre-classical Latin, preserving archaic forms (e.g., *mavolo* for *malo*, *-ier* infinitives, prodelision like *bonumst*) that reflect everyday speech rather than codified oratory. His style features:
*   **Wordplay:** Extensive puns, alliteration (*falsiloquom, falsicum, falsiiurium*), assonance, and riddle-jokes.
*   **Proverbs:** Hundreds woven into speeches, often concluding soliloquies for dramatic effect.
*   **Greek Vocabulary:** ~250 of 270 proper names are Greek; Greek terms for luxury goods (perfumes, foods) add flair, often spoken by slaves (many of Greek origin).
*   **Punic:** Two prayers in *Poenulus* preserve Carthaginian vowel pronunciation.
*   **Meter:** He favored *cantica* (lyric passages in varied meters) over Greek meters, primarily using iambic senarius (dialogue) and trochaic septenarius (livelier action), plus iambic septenarii for "cover monologues" marking time passage.

### Stagecraft and Performance
Plays were performed on shallow, long wooden stages with three scene-house openings, built and dismantled daily for specific *ludi* (especially the *Ludi Megalenses*). No permanent theater existed; the aristocracy viewed drama as demoralizing. Seating was hierarchical: elites sat, lower classes stood. The lack of orchestra/chorus forced closer actor-audience interaction; actors addressed spectators directly. Stage geography mirrored social status: characters exited/entered from directions befitting their rank (e.g., the *medicus* from the forum). Verbs of motion (*abeo, transeo, intus*) function as implicit stage directions.

### Historical Context: War and Society
Plautus wrote during Rome’s expansion. The Second Punic War (218–201 BC) left traces: *Miles Gloriosus* contains a patriotic passage ("the foe is near at hand") likely rallying support for Scipio Africanus’s strategy. *Stichus* (c. 200 BC) reflects post-war exhaustion and anxiety over the Second Macedonian War; the parasite Gelasimus embodies economic hardship, while characters obsess over *officium* (duty) and *pietas*. Plautus reflects, rather than invents, contemporary skepticism toward gods—characters compare mortals to deities or scorn the divine—but state oversight prevented overt impiety. He portrays Roman crude vitality as superior to Greek effeteness, aligning comedy with the Republic’s rising mastery.

### Manuscript Tradition
Survival depends on two main traditions:
1.  **Ambrosian Palimpsest (A):** 5th-century parchment overwritten with biblical texts; discovered 1815. Fragmentary but valuable for correcting errors; best for *Persa, Poenulus, Pseudolus, Stichus*.
2.  **Palatine Family (P):** Archetype (8th/9th c.) lost; reconstructed from 10th/11th-century descendants (B, C, D). Plays divided into two halves (*Amphitruo–Epidicus* and *Bacchides–Truculentus*). Both traditions likely derive from a 4th/5th-century ancestor. Scene headings (character names in red ink) were lost in both, leaving some minor characters unnamed.

### Reception and Influence
*   **Middle Ages/Renaissance:** *Amphitruo* most popular; first English translation. University productions of *Miles Gloriosus* (Oxford, 1522) and *Menaechmi* (Cardinal Wolsey’s house, 1527).
*   **Shakespeare:** *The Comedy of Errors* adapts *Menaechmi* (adding a second twin pair from *Amphitruo*) and Plautine stock characters (parasite→Falstaff, clever slave, opening monologue). Shakespeare blends Plautine farce with Elizabethan romance and Christian resolution.
*   **Molière:** *The Miser* closely follows *Aulularia* (Harpagon/Euclio, stolen money, "Where shall I run?" line). *The Blunderer* uses *Miles Gloriosus*’s Palaestrio as model for quick-witted servants.
*   **Modern:** *A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum* (1962 musical); TV’s *Up Pompeii!*; scholarly revival via Segal’s *Roman Laughter* (1968).

## Terms
- ****Palliata comoedia**** — Roman comedy adapted from Greek models, performed in Greek dress (*pallium*); established by Livius Andronicus.
- ****Contaminatio**** — The technique of blending plot elements from two or more Greek source plays into a single Roman adaptation.
- ****Servus Callidus**** — The "clever slave" stock character; drives plot through deception, delivers exposition, and inverts social hierarchy.
- ****Senex Amator**** — The "lustful old man" stock character; pursues a young woman, inviting ridicule for bestial passion and childishness.
- ****Parasite**** — A hanger-on (Greek *parasitos*) who flatters a patron for food; e.g., Artotrogus in *Miles Gloriosus*.
- ****Ludi**** — Public festival games with religious origins; the primary venue for dramatic performance in Republican Rome.
- ****Cantica**** — Lyric passages in varied meters (sung or chanted), favored by Plautus over spoken Greek meters.
- ****Prodelision**** — Elision where a form of *sum* (e.g., *est*) merges with the preceding word (e.g., *bonumst* for *bonum est*).
- ****Ambrosian Palimpsest (A)**** — Oldest manuscript of Plautus (5th c. AD), overwritten with biblical text; discovered 1815 in Milan.
- ****Palatine Family (P)**** — Manuscript tradition descending from an 8th/9th-century archetype; includes key witnesses B (Vatican), C, D.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Originality vs. Imitation:** To what extent are Plautus’s plays creative Roman transformations versus faithful (or diluted) copies of Greek New Comedy? Anderson argues for vigorous originality; others see competent adaptation.
*   **Greek Audience Competence:** Did Roman audiences understand the extensive Greek names and vocabulary? Seaman argues soldiers’ experience in Greece provided sufficient knowledge; others suggest the Greek flavor was exotic decoration.
*   **Political Engagement:** Are topical allusions (e.g., *Miles Gloriosus* passage) deliberate political intervention or safe reflection of public mood? The state controlled production, limiting overt subversion.
*   **Textual Reconstruction:** With the Ambrosian Palimpsest fragmentary and the Palatine archetype lost—and both possibly sharing a common ancestor—how closely do modern editions reflect Plautus’s actual wording, especially for scene headings and minor character names?
*   **Performance Details:** The exact nature of music, dance, and the *tibicen* (piper)’s role in *cantica* remains inferred from metrical patterns rather than explicit evidence.
