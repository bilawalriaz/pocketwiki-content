# Kwame Nkrumah

## Overview
Kwame Nkrumah (1909–1972) was a Ghanaian politician and revolutionary who became the first Prime Minister and President of Ghana, leading it to independence from the United Kingdom in 1957—the first sub-Saharan African colony to achieve majority-rule independence. A leading advocate of Pan-Africanism, his socialist and nationalist administration aimed to rapidly industrialize Ghana and unite Africa under a single continental leadership. His increasingly authoritarian rule culminated in a one-party state before he was deposed in a 1966 coup.

## Timeline
- **21 September 1909** — Born in Nkroful, Gold Coast (now Ghana).
- **October 1935** — Arrives in the United States to study at Lincoln University.
- **May 1945** — Returns to London; co-organizes the Fifth Pan-African Congress.
- **29 December 1947** — Begins work as General Secretary of the United Gold Coast Convention (UGCC).
- **12 June 1949** — Forms the Convention People's Party (CPP).
- **February 1951** — CPP wins landslide election; Nkrumah is released from prison to form a government.
- **6 March 1957** — Ghana gains independence; Nkrumah becomes Prime Minister.
- **1960** — Ghana becomes a republic; Nkrumah is elected President.
- **1964** — Constitutional amendment makes Ghana a one-party state with Nkrumah as President for life.
- **24 February 1966** — Nkrumah is deposed in a military coup while abroad.
- **27 April 1972** — Dies in exile in Bucharest, Romania.

## Body

### Early Life and Political Formation
Born in Nkroful, Nkrumah was educated at Catholic missions and the Government Training College (Achimota), where he was influenced by pan-Africanist ideas. He spent twelve years abroad, earning degrees in the United States (Lincoln University, University of Pennsylvania) and studying in London. In the U.S., he was active in student and pan-Africanist circles, meeting intellectuals like C. L. R. James. In London, he co-organized the Fifth Pan-African Congress in 1945, which strategized for African independence and a federal "United States of Africa."

### Rise to Power in the Gold Coast
Returning to the Gold Coast in 1947, Nkrumah became General Secretary of the UGCC, a party seeking self-government. His activist approach, including founding a youth wing and newspaper, created a rift with the party's conservative leadership. In 1949, he formed the CPP, which appealed directly to the common voter with the slogan "Self-Government Now." After organizing a "Positive Action" campaign of strikes and civil disobedience, he was imprisoned. The CPP won a landslide victory in the 1951 election, and Nkrumah was released to become Leader of Government Business, then Prime Minister in 1952. He negotiated with the British for independence, achieved on 6 March 1957.

### Governance and Domestic Policy
As Prime Minister and then President, Nkrumah pursued rapid industrialization and socialist policies. His administration funded major infrastructure projects, including the Akosombo Dam on the Volta River, and expanded education and healthcare. He sought to eliminate "tribalism" through legislation and reduce the power of traditional chiefs. To consolidate control, he passed the Preventive Detention Act (allowing imprisonment without trial) and in 1964 made Ghana a one-party state with himself as president for life. His government controlled the media and fostered a personality cult, with Nkrumah adopting the title "Osagyefo" (redeemer).

### Pan-Africanism and Foreign Policy
Nkrumah was a central figure in the Pan-African movement, hosting key conferences in Accra and helping found the Organisation of African Unity (OAU) in 1963. He advocated for a politically and economically integrated Africa, proposing a "Union of African States" and an African High Command. His foreign policy was non-aligned but leaned toward the Eastern Bloc, earning him the Lenin Peace Prize in 1962. Ghana provided military support to liberation movements across Africa, including in the Congo Crisis.

### Downfall and Legacy
Nkrumah's government faced economic difficulties, partly due to falling cocoa prices and debt from industrial projects. His authoritarian measures alienated many, including the military. He was overthrown in a 1966 coup while visiting China and Vietnam. The coup was led by the National Liberation Council, and allegations of CIA involvement have persisted but remain unverified. Nkrumah spent his final years in exile in Guinea, where he was named honorary co-president, and died in 1972. He is remembered as a foundational figure of African independence and Pan-Africanism, though his legacy is complicated by his turn to dictatorship.

## Terms
- **Pan-Africanism**: A political and cultural movement advocating for solidarity and unity among all people of African descent, and for the political and economic integration of the African continent.
- **Convention People's Party (CPP)**: The political party founded by Nkrumah in 1949, which led the Gold Coast to independence and dominated its politics until 1966.
- **Osagyefo**: An Akan title meaning "redeemer," adopted by Nkrumah as part of his personality cult.
- **Positive Action**: A campaign of strikes, boycotts, and civil disobedience organized by Nkrumah in 1950 to pressure the British for self-government.
- **Preventive Detention Act**: A 1958 law passed by Nkrumah's government that allowed for the imprisonment of political opponents without charge or trial for up to five years.
- **Organisation of African Unity (OAU)**: A continental organization founded in 1963 to promote African unity and solidarity, which Nkrumah helped establish.
- **Nkrumaism**: The political ideology of Kwame Nkrumah, a form of scientific socialism that combined Marxist principles with Pan-Africanism and anti-imperialism.
- **Volta River Project**: A major hydroelectric dam project (Akosombo Dam) initiated by Nkrumah to industrialize Ghana's economy, financed with help from the U.S., Israel, and the World Bank.

## Debates and open questions
- **CIA Involvement**: The extent of U.S. Central Intelligence Agency complicity in the 1966 coup that overthrew Nkrumah is debated. Former agents have claimed a pivotal role, but these accounts have never been fully verified.
- **Economic Legacy**: Scholars debate whether Nkrumah's rapid industrialization and socialist policies were a necessary foundation for development or whether they mismanaged the economy, leading to debt and the coup.
- **Authoritarian Turn**: There is discussion over whether Nkrumah's shift to a one-party state and repression was a pragmatic response to threats or an inevitable corruption of his initial democratic ideals.
