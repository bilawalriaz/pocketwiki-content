# Harpsichord

## Overview
The harpsichord is a keyboard instrument that produces sound by plucking strings with a plectrum, a mechanism that gives it a distinctive, bright tone. It was a dominant keyboard instrument in Western music from the Renaissance through the Baroque era, serving both as a solo instrument and as a core part of the basso continuo accompaniment. Its inability to vary volume by touch led to its replacement by the piano in the late 18th century, but it saw a major revival in the 20th century for historically informed performance and new compositions.

## Timeline
- **Late 14th century** — The harpsichord is most likely invented.
- **16th century** — Italian makers develop a lightweight design with thin cases and low-tension brass strings.
- **Late 16th century** — The Ruckers family in the Southern Netherlands begins a tradition of heavier, more powerful instruments, including the first two-keyboard models.
- **18th century** — French, English, and German builders create sophisticated, powerful instruments with multiple choirs and stops.
- **c. 1700** — Bartolomeo Cristofori builds the first piano, which strikes strings instead of plucking them.
- **Late 18th century** — The piano largely supplants the harpsichord, which becomes considered obsolete.
- **19th century** — The harpsichord almost disappears from use, except in opera for accompanying recitative.
- **20th century** — A revival begins, first with piano-technology instruments, then with a shift to historically informed building practices.

## Body

### Mechanism and Sound Production
A harpsichord's sound is generated by a plucking action. When a key is pressed, its back end rises, lifting a wooden jack. A small plectrum (historically bird quill, now often plastic) mounted on the jack plucks a string. The string's vibration is amplified by a soundboard, typically made of spruce or fir. When the key is released, the jack falls, and a felt damper stops the string's vibration. The plectrum is mounted on a pivoting tongue that allows it to swing back and pass the string silently on the return stroke.

### Strings, Tuning, and Soundboard
Each string is stretched between a tuning pin (adjusted with a wrench) and a hitchpin, passing over a nut and a bridge. The bridge sits on the soundboard, which is essential for projecting the sound. The standard historical tuning pitch is A4 = 415 Hz, roughly a semitone lower than the modern concert pitch of A4 = 440 Hz.

### Multiple Manuals and Choirs of Strings
Many harpsichords have multiple sets (choirs) of strings per note, allowing for varied volume and tone. Different choirs can be tuned to different pitches (e.g., 8' for normal pitch, 4' for an octave higher) or plucked at different points to alter timbre. The player selects which choirs sound using stops—mechanisms that move the jacks sideways so their plectra miss the strings. More complex instruments have two or more keyboards (manuals), each controlling a different choir, often with a coupler to link them.

### History and National Traditions
The instrument evolved into distinct national styles. Italian instruments were light and resonant. The Flemish Ruckers tradition used heavier construction for a more powerful tone. French builders refined the double manual for expressive control, while English and German makers created large, sonorous instruments with extensive pitch ranges. The piano, invented c. 1700, offered dynamic control (volume variation by touch) that the harpsichord lacked. As musical tastes changed, the piano supplanted the harpsichord by the late 18th century.

### Variants and Decoration
The harpsichord family includes smaller forms like the rectangular virginal and the angled spinet. The clavicytherium is a vertical, space-saving variant. Instruments were often elaborately decorated with paint, veneer, or carvings, and their keyboards sometimes featured reversed colors (white sharps, black naturals).

### Music and Revival
The core repertoire was composed during the Renaissance and Baroque eras by composers like William Byrd, François Couperin, Domenico Scarlatti, Handel, and J.S. Bach. After a 19th-century decline, the 20th-century revival, led by figures like Wanda Landowska and builders like Frank Hubbard, focused on historically accurate construction and performance, and the instrument also entered new musical styles.

## Terms
- **Plectrum**: A small wedge (quill or plastic) that plucks the string.
- **Jack**: A thin strip of wood holding the plectrum, lifted by the key.
- **Soundboard**: A thin wood panel that amplifies the string's vibrations.
- **Choir**: A set of strings tuned to the same pitch (e.g., an 8' choir).
- **Stop**: A mechanism to engage or disengage a choir of strings.
- **Manual**: A keyboard on a harpsichord.
- **Coupler**: A mechanism linking two manuals so one key activates both.
- **Basso continuo**: A Baroque accompaniment part, often played by a harpsichord.
- **Disposition**: The specification of a harpsichord's choirs and stops.
- **Short octave**: A keyboard layout where the lowest keys are tuned to pitches higher than their visual position suggests.

## Debates and Open Questions
The exact origin and inventor of the harpsichord are unknown, with the earliest instruments being "most likely" from the late 14th century. The precise reasons for its decline are tied to changing musical styles and the piano's rise, but the process was gradual. Modern historically informed performance practice involves ongoing research into historical building techniques, tuning systems, and performance conventions to recreate an authentic Baroque sound.
