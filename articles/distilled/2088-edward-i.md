# Edward I

## Overview
Edward I (r. 1272–1307), known as "Longshanks" and the "Hammer of the Scots," was a pivotal English king who restored royal authority, reformed law and administration, and expanded English dominion over Wales and parts of Scotland. His reign established Parliament as a permanent institution and implemented major legal statutes, but also involved costly wars, heavy taxation, and the expulsion of England's Jewish community. His legacy is debated, with historians praising his administrative achievements while criticizing his often ruthless policies toward conquered peoples and minorities.

## Body

### Early Life and Civil War
Edward was the eldest son of Henry III. In 1254, he married Eleanor of Castile and received Gascony, though his father retained control. Initially aligned with foreign favorites, he briefly supported baronial reform in 1259 before reconciling with his father. During the Second Barons' War (1264–1267), he fought for the royalist cause. After capture at Lewes (1264), he escaped and defeated Simon de Montfort at Evesham (1265). He later joined the Ninth Crusade (1270–1272), learning of his accession during the journey.

### Conquest of Wales
Edward's reign saw the complete conquest of Wales. After a first war in 1277, a second rebellion (1282–1283) ended with the death of Welsh prince Llywelyn ap Gruffudd. The Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) incorporated Wales into England, introducing English law and administration. Edward built a network of castles (e.g., Caernarfon, Conwy) and English towns, dispossessing the Welsh aristocracy. This colonial-style governance led to periodic revolts, such as in 1294, and fostered deep resentment.

### Law, Administration, and Parliament
Edward focused on restoring royal authority and reforming law. He conducted extensive inquests (the Hundred Rolls) to investigate abuses and lost royal rights. Major statutes—including Westminster I (1275), Gloucester (1278), and Westminster II (1285)—codified criminal and property law. He established that all liberties derived from the Crown through Quo warranto proceedings. Edward reformed the coinage and held regular Parliaments. The "Model Parliament" of 1295 included commons representatives (knights and burgesses) with full authority to consent to taxation, setting a key precedent.

### Wars with Scotland and France
Edward's claim of feudal overlordship over Scotland led to the "Great Cause" succession dispute (1290–1292), which he settled in favor of John Balliol. When Balliol allied with France, Edward invaded Scotland in 1296. Scottish resistance, led by William Wallace and later Robert the Bruce, continued throughout his reign. Simultaneously, a war with France (1294–1303) over Gascony drained resources and complicated Scottish campaigns. The Treaty of Paris (1303) restored Gascony to Edward.

### Finances, Opposition, and Expulsion of the Jews
Frequent wars required heavy taxation, leading to political opposition. In 1297, nobles led by Roger Bigod and Humphrey de Bohun protested military and fiscal demands, forcing Edward to confirm Magna Carta and the Charter of the Forest. Edward's financial policies also targeted England's Jews, who were the Crown's property and allowed to lend at interest. After exploiting them financially, he issued the Edict of Expulsion in 1290, permanently removing them from England—a move linked to securing a large parliamentary subsidy and rooted in contemporary antisemitism.

### Ireland and Later Years
Edward used Ireland primarily as a source of revenue and soldiers for his wars, leading to administrative corruption and a revival of Gaelic Irish power. In his final years, he campaigned against Robert the Bruce in Scotland but died en route in 1307, leaving an unfinished war and significant political and financial burdens to his son, Edward II.

## Terms
- **Feudal Suzerainty**: The overlordship or superior authority that a lord (like Edward over Scotland) claimed to hold over a vassal state.
- **Quo Warranto**: Legal proceedings ("by what warrant") used to investigate the origins of feudal liberties and determine if they were held by legitimate royal grant.
- **Statute of Rhuddlan (1284)**: The law that incorporated conquered Wales into England, establishing English administrative and legal systems.
- **Model Parliament (1295)**: A Parliament that included representatives from the commons (knights and burgesses) summoned with full authority to consent to taxation, setting a key precedent.
- **Lay Subsidy**: A tax levied on the movable property of the laity (non-clergy), requiring parliamentary consent.
- **Edict of Expulsion (1290)**: The royal decree that permanently expelled all Jews from England.
- **Confirmatio Cartarum (1297)**: Edward's confirmation of Magna Carta and the Charter of the Forest, signed under political pressure from rebellious nobles.
- **Hundred Rolls**: Census documents from an extensive inquest into royal administration and lost Crown rights, similar to the Domesday Book.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians debate Edward's character and legacy. Some, like Michael Prestwich, view him as a formidable and great king who met the standards of his time, while others, like G. W. S. Barrow, see him as a ruthless opportunist who created lasting antagonism, especially in Scotland. Welsh and Irish historians, such as R. R. Davies and James Lydon, emphasize the colonial and destructive nature of his policies in those countries. There is also significant debate over his antisemitism, with scholars like Robert Stacey arguing he pioneered state-sponsored antisemitism in England. Finally, his use of Parliament is contested: was he a collaborative constitutional reformer or an autocrat using Parliament as a tool to check baronial power?
