# Hundred Years' War

## Overview
The Hundred Years' War (1337–1453) was a series of intermittent conflicts between the kingdoms of England and France, rooted in feudal disputes over the Duchy of Aquitaine and a succession crisis for the French throne. It evolved into a broader struggle that reshaped European warfare, fostered national identities in both kingdoms, and ultimately led to French victory, ending English territorial ambitions on the continent.

## Timeline
- **1328** — Death of Charles IV of France; Philip VI of Valois crowned, rejecting Edward III's claim.
- **1337** — Philip VI confiscates Aquitaine; Edward III renews his claim to the French throne, beginning the war.
- **1346** — English victory at the Battle of Crécy.
- **1347** — English capture of Calais.
- **1356** — English victory at the Battle of Poitiers; King John II of France captured.
- **1360** — Treaty of Brétigny ends the first phase; Edward III gains territory but renounces his French claim.
- **1369** — War resumes; French under Charles V and Bertrand du Guesclin begin reconquering lost lands.
- **1415** — Henry V of England wins the Battle of Agincourt.
- **1420** — Treaty of Troyes names Henry V heir to the French throne.
- **1429** — Joan of Arc lifts the Siege of Orléans; French victory at Patay.
- **1453** — French victory at the Battle of Castillon ends the war; England loses all French territory except Calais.

## Body

### Origins and Causes
The conflict stemmed from long-standing tensions since the Norman Conquest of 1066, which made English kings vassals of the French crown for their French lands. The immediate trigger was the 1328 death of Charles IV, the last Capetian king. The French nobility rejected his nephew Edward III's claim, invoking a precedent that barred inheritance through the female line, and crowned Philip VI. Disputes over the sovereignty of Aquitaine (Gascony), where the English king owed homage to the French king, were a persistent source of friction. In 1337, Philip VI moved to confiscate Aquitaine, prompting Edward III to formally claim the French throne.

### The Edwardian War (1337–1360)
The early war saw major English victories at Crécy (1346) and Poitiers (1356), where the French king John II was captured. These successes, combined with the capture of Calais, gave England significant leverage. The phase concluded with the Treaty of Brétigny (1360), in which Edward III gained expanded lands in Aquitaine but renounced his claim to the French throne.

### The Caroline War (1369–1389)
Under Charles V and commander Bertrand du Guesclin, France adopted a strategy of avoiding major battles and using guerrilla tactics to reclaim territory. By 1380, the English had lost most of their gains, holding only Calais and a few ports. This period was also marked by the devastating Black Death (c. 1347–1351), which killed 30–60% of France's population and caused severe economic and social disruption.

### The Lancastrian War (1415–1453)
Henry V revived the war, exploiting a civil war between French factions (Armagnacs and Burgundians) and the mental illness of King Charles VI. His victory at Agincourt (1415) and the Treaty of Troyes (1420) seemed to secure an English triumph. However, the tide turned after Henry V's death in 1422. The emergence of Joan of Arc in 1429, who boosted French morale and led to the relief of Orléans, was a pivotal moment. French forces, now using professional armies and effective artillery, won decisive battles at Formigny (1450) and Castillon (1453), expelling the English from all French territory except Calais.

### Aftermath and Significance
The war ended English continental ambitions and accelerated the development of centralized nation-states in both France and England. Militarily, it marked the decline of feudal armies and chivalry, the rise of professional standing armies, and the decisive impact of artillery. The English defeat contributed to domestic instability, leading to the Wars of the Roses. In France, the victory solidified the Valois dynasty and fostered a stronger national identity.

## Terms
- **Aquitaine/Gascony/Guyenne**: The duchy in southwestern France held by English kings as vassals to the French crown, a central cause of the conflict.
- **Chevauchée**: A mounted raiding expedition designed to devastate enemy territory and undermine morale.
- **Compagnies d'ordonnance**: The first permanent, professional standing army in post-Roman Western Europe, established by Charles VII of France in 1445.
- **Hobelars**: Lightly armored mounted troops used by the English, who often fought on foot.
- **Salic Law**: A legal principle invoked to exclude women from inheriting the French throne, used to deny Edward III's claim.
- **Treaty of Brétigny (1360)**: Ended the first phase; Edward III gained territory but renounced his claim to the French throne.
- **Treaty of Troyes (1420)**: Named Henry V of England as heir to the French throne, disinheriting the Dauphin (future Charles VII).

## Debates and Open Questions
- The relative importance of the dynastic claim versus the sovereignty dispute over Aquitaine as the primary cause of the war is debated among historians.
- The role of emerging nationalism versus dynastic loyalty in motivating soldiers and populations is a subject of scholarly analysis.
- The long-term economic and demographic impact of the war, particularly in relation to the concurrent Black Death, remains a complex area of study.
