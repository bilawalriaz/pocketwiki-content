# Marcel Mauss

## Overview
Marcel Mauss (1872–1950) was a French sociologist and anthropologist, widely regarded as the "father of French ethnology." As the nephew and close collaborator of Émile Durkheim, he bridged the emerging disciplines of sociology and anthropology, shifting focus from abstract theory to comparative ethnography. His seminal work, *The Gift* (1925), established the study of reciprocity by arguing that gift exchange creates binding social bonds through obligations to give, receive, and reciprocate. Mauss founded key institutions (Institut Français de Sociologie, Institut d'Ethnologie) and trained the first generation of French fieldworkers, directly influencing structural anthropology (Claude Lévi-Strauss) and practice theory (Pierre Bourdieu).

- **1914–1919** — Serves as interpreter in French Army during WWI; Durkheim and many colleagues die.
- **1924** — Founds Institut Français de Sociologie.
- **1925** — Publishes *The Gift* (*Essai sur le don*), his most famous work.
- **1926** — Founds Institut d'Ethnologie to institutionalize Durkheim's legacy and fieldwork-based anthropology.
- **1931** — Elected first Chair of Sociology at Collège de France.
- **1934** — Publishes "Les techniques du corps" (Techniques of the Body).
- **1940** — Forced from Collège de France and Paris by German occupation and anti-Semitic laws.
- **10 Feb 1950** — Dies in Paris.

## Body

### Family, Education, and the Durkheim Connection
Mauss was born in Épinal, Vosges, to a Jewish family; his father was a merchant and his mother an embroidery shop owner. His maternal uncle, Émile Durkheim, became his primary intellectual mentor. Mauss obtained a religious education and was bar mitzvahed, yet by the age of eighteen he stopped practicing his religion. He studied philosophy at Bordeaux (1890) where Durkheim taught, beginning a rigorous epistolary tutelage. Durkheim treated Mauss as a "linchpin" for the *Année Sociologique* project, demanding strict discipline and assigning him the crucial religious sociology section. Mauss assisted Durkheim statistically for *Suicide* (1897) and helped recruit the journal's core team (Hubert, Fauconnet, Milhaud). Their collaboration produced *Primitive Classification* (1902), arguing that spatial and temporal categories originate in social organization. Mauss was also the first cousin of Claudette (née Raphael) Bloch, a marine biologist and mother of Maurice Bloch, who became a noted anthropologist. While Mauss identified as a Durkheimian, he diverged by engaging deeply in socialist politics (Dreyfus affair, Jaurès, left-wing press) and privileging empirical ethnography over pure theory.

### Academic Career and Institutional Building
Appointed to the EPHE in 1901, Mauss pioneered the "history of religions of non-civilized peoples," effectively founding French ethnology. WWI service (1914–19) provided a break from academia but brought trauma: the deaths of Durkheim and peers. Post-war, Mauss criticized Bolshevik violence and market destruction. He secured Durkheim's legacy by founding the Institut Français de Sociologie (1924) and Institut d'Ethnologie (1926), which launched the first generation of French fieldworkers (Griaule, Leiris, Dumont, Tillion, etc.). Elected to the Collège de France in 1931, he married his secretary in 1934. He was expelled in 1940 under Vichy anti-Semitic statutes, dying in isolation a decade later.

### Theoretical Core: The Gift and Total Social Facts
In *The Gift* (1925), Mauss analyzed exchange in "archaic" societies (Polynesia, Melanesia, Pacific Northwest) to reveal a universal mechanism. He argued gifts are never "free" but are **total prestations** (total social facts/*faits sociaux totaux*): transactions encompassing legal, economic, religious, and aesthetic dimensions. The object given remains spiritually tied to the giver (**inalienability**); it is "loaned rather than sold." This creates a triple obligation—**to give, to receive, and to reciprocate**—binding individuals across time. Failure to reciprocate results in loss of honour, status, or *mana* (spiritual authority). Mauss contrasted this with commodity exchange (alienable property) and argued modern capitalism rests on an unsustainable ideology of individual wants, urging a return to recognizing *faits sociaux totaux* as the basis of social cohesion.

### Sacrifice, Magic, and the Body
With Henri Hubert, Mauss developed a sociological theory of **sacrifice** (1898) as a dialectic of *sacralising* (directing the sacred toward a person/object) and *desacralising* (removing it). In *A General Theory of Magic* (1902), they defined magic as a social fact requiring collective belief: individual acts are not magic. They analyzed the **magician** (officer), **magical rites** (actions), and **magical representations** (beliefs). Mauss's 1934 essay "Techniques of the Body" (*Les techniques du corps*) argued the body is not a natural given but a cultural artifact shaped by **body techniques**—biological, sociological, and psychological habits (walking, swimming, sleeping) acquired through education. With Hubert, he defined the **person** as a "category of thought," the articulation of particular embodiment of law and morality. They believed that a person was constituted by *personages* (a set of roles) which were executed through the behaviors and exercise of specific body techniques and attributes.

### Legacy and Influence
Mauss linked Durkheimian sociology to modern French thought. He directly taught ethnographic method to the first French fieldworkers. His influence extends to **Claude Lévi-Strauss** (structural anthropology, alliance theory), **Pierre Bourdieu** (concept of *habitus* as embodied disposition), **Georges Bataille** (expenditure/*potlatch*), and the **Mouvement Anti-Utilitariste dans les Sciences Sociales** (MAUSS). *The Gift* inspired situationists (journal *Potlatch*) and debates on open-source/gift economies. Later anthropologists (Annette Weiner, Marilyn Strathern) refined his inalienability concept ("keeping while giving").

## Terms
- ****Total prestation / Total social fact (*fait social total*)**** — A transaction (like a gift) that simultaneously engages legal, economic, religious, political, and aesthetic institutions; the whole social system is mobilized.
- ****Inalienability**** — The quality of a gift object that remains spiritually/identifiably tied to the giver; it cannot be fully alienated (sold) but must circulate/return.
- ****Triple obligation**** — The three binding moments of gift exchange identified by Mauss: the obligation to *give*, to *receive* (refusal rejects the bond), and to *reciprocate*.
- ****Mana**** — A Polynesian/Melanesian concept (used by Mauss) denoting spiritual authority, power, or wealth; failure to reciprocate gifts causes its loss.
- ****Potlatch**** — A competitive feast/gift-giving ceremony among Pacific Northwest Indigenous groups, analyzed by Mauss as "total prestation" involving honour, rank, and destruction of property.
- ****Sacralisation / Desacralisation**** — The two poles of sacrifice (Mauss & Hubert): directing sacred power *onto* a victim/object, then removing it to return the profane world to order.
- ****Body techniques (*techniques du corps*)**** — Culturally specific, learned ways of using the body (walking, marching, sleeping) that are simultaneously biological, sociological, and psychological.
- ****Person / Personage**** — The "person" is a category of thought/law; "personages" are the masks/roles enacted through body techniques.
- ****Habitus**** — (Later developed by Bourdieu from Mauss) Durable, embodied dispositions and muscle memory generated by social structure; the "feel for the game."
- ****L'Année Sociologique**** — The journal founded by Durkheim (1898) with Mauss as key organizer/recruiter; the institutional hub of the French sociological school.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Universality of Reciprocity:** Critics (Alain Testart, Genevieve Vaughan, James Laidlaw) argue Mauss overgeneralized. Testart cites alms to beggars (anonymous, no expectation of return) as "free gifts." Vaughan argues the "three obligations" framework obscures unilateral maternal giving. Laidlaw describes Jain renouncers who receive food without reciprocity or desire, challenging the obligation model.
*   **Nature of the "Archaic":** Mauss synthesized disparate ethnographic sources (Polynesia, Melanesia, Northwest Coast, India) into a single evolutionary schema ("archaic societies"). Critics question the validity of treating these as a unified historical stage.
*   **Voluntary vs. Obligatory:** Mauss states gifts are "in theory voluntary, in fact obligatory." The tension between individual agency and structural coercion remains a central debate in economic anthropology.
*   **Magic as Social Fact:** Mauss and Hubert's definition of magic as *only* social/collective excludes individual ritual efficacy, a boundary debated in the anthropology of religion.
*   **Political Implications:** Mauss's socialist critique of market capitalism and advocacy for a society based on *faits sociaux totaux* raises questions about the feasibility of applying "archaic" reciprocity to modern complex societies.
