# Guadalajara

## Overview
Guadalajara is the capital and most populous city of the Mexican state of Jalisco, and the third-largest metropolitan area in Mexico. It is a major cultural, economic, and technological hub, known as the birthplace of mariachi and tequila, and as Mexico's "Silicon Valley" due to its electronics industry. Its history spans from pre-Hispanic times through Spanish colonization, independence, and rapid 20th-century growth.

## Timeline
- **1532** — First settlement founded by Cristóbal de Oñate at Nochistlán.
- **1542** — City officially founded in the Atemajac Valley and granted cityhood.
- **1539** — Holy Roman Emperor Charles V grants a coat of arms and title of city.
- **1560** — Guadalajara becomes the capital of the Kingdom of Nueva Galicia.
- **1810** — Independence leader Miguel Hidalgo establishes a revolutionary government and abolishes slavery in the city.
- **1823** — Guadalajara becomes the capital of the newly founded state of Jalisco.
- **1910** — Mexican Revolution begins; Guadalajara experiences relative calm.
- **1929** — Cristero War ends with President Calles's "Grito de Guadalajara."
- **1947** — First industrial park established, marking post-war growth.
- **1964** — City's population surpasses one million.
- **1992** — Guadalajara explosions destroy downtown streets, killing 206.
- **2011** — City hosts the Pan American Games.

## Body
### Foundation and Colonial Era
The city was founded multiple times before settling in the Atemajac Valley in 1542. It became the capital of Nueva Galicia, a kingdom within New Spain, and grew wealthy from agriculture, trade, and later silver mining. By the 18th century, it was Mexico's second-largest city. The University of Guadalajara was established by royal decree in 1791.

### Independence and 19th Century
During the Mexican War of Independence, Guadalajara was a key site where Miguel Hidalgo abolished slavery in 1810. The city remained under royalist control for most of the war. After independence, it became the capital of Jalisco in 1823. The 19th century brought economic growth, rail connections, and the establishment of electrical services.

### 20th Century and Modern Growth
The Mexican Revolution (1910–1920) had limited impact on Guadalajara, which then experienced rapid industrialization and population growth, especially from the 1940s to 1980s. The arrival of multinational electronics firms after NAFTA in the 1990s transformed its economy, earning it the "Silicon Valley of Mexico" nickname. The city hosted major events like the 2011 Pan American Games and will host matches for the 2026 FIFA World Cup.

### Economy and Technology
Guadalajara has Mexico's third-largest economy, based on commerce, services, and manufacturing. It is the country's main producer of software and electronic components, with facilities for companies like IBM, Intel, and HP. The city is a major distribution center and a hub for foreign trade, with exports primarily to the United States.

### Culture and Landmarks
The city is a core center of Mexican culture, associated with mariachi, tequila, and birria cuisine. Its historic center features colonial architecture, including the Guadalajara Cathedral and the UNESCO-listed Hospicio Cabañas, which houses murals by José Clemente Orozco. Key cultural events include the Guadalajara International Film Festival and the International Book Fair, the largest in the Americas.

## Terms
- **Nueva Galicia:** A kingdom within the Viceroyalty of New Spain, with Guadalajara as its capital.
- **Porfiriato:** The period (1876–1911) of Porfirio Díaz's presidency, marked by modernization and industrial growth.
- **Cristero War:** A 1920s conflict in Mexico between the government and Catholic rebels.
- **Tapatío:** A term for a person or thing from Guadalajara or the state of Jalisco.
- **SITEUR:** The Guadalajara light rail system (Sistema de Tren Eléctrico Urbano).
- **Macrobús:** Guadalajara's bus rapid transit system.
- **Birria:** A traditional Jalisco dish of stewed meat, often goat or beef.
- **Mariachi:** A genre of regional Mexican music strongly associated with Guadalajara.
- **Hospicio Cabañas:** An 18th-century building, now a cultural institute and UNESCO World Heritage site.

## Debates and Open questions
- The investigation into the 1992 Guadalajara explosions, which killed 206 people, lasted over 11 years and was closed without assigning clear responsibility, attributing the disaster to an accident.
- The city's economic shift toward high-tech manufacturing has been less favorable for the unskilled working class, highlighting ongoing socioeconomic inequality.
