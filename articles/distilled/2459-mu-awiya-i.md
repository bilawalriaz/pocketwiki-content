# Mu'awiya I

## Overview
Mu'awiya I (c. 597/603/605–680) was the founder and first caliph of the Umayyad Caliphate, ruling from 661 until his death. He transformed the caliphate from a communal elective office into a hereditary monarchy, establishing a Syrian-based dynasty that lasted until 750. His reign marked the end of the First Fitna (civil war) and the beginning of a more centralized, bureaucratic state, though his rule remains deeply contested in Islamic tradition.

- **680** — Died in Damascus; succeeded by his son Yazid I

## Body

### Early Life and Conversion
Mu'awiya was born into the powerful Banu Abd Shams clan of the Quraysh tribe in Mecca. His father, Abu Sufyan, was a leading opponent of the Islamic prophet Muhammad until the conquest of Mecca in 630, when the family converted. Mu'awiya became one of Muhammad's scribes, one of seventeen literate Qurayshites at the time.

### Rise in Syria
After Muhammad's death, Mu'awiya was appointed deputy commander in the conquest of Syria under Caliph Abu Bakr. He rose through the ranks under Caliphs Umar and Uthman, becoming governor of Syria. He consolidated power by allying with the powerful Banu Kalb tribe through marriage, developing coastal defenses, and initiating the first Muslim naval campaigns against the Byzantine Empire, including raids on Cyprus and the Battle of the Masts (655).

### The First Fitna and Accession
Following Caliph Uthman's assassination in 656, Mu'awiya refused to recognize Ali as caliph, demanding vengeance for Uthman. Their armies fought to a stalemate at the Battle of Siffin in 657, leading to an abortive arbitration. Mu'awiya then gained recognition as caliph in Syria and, with his ally Amr ibn al-As, conquered Egypt from Ali's governor. After Ali's assassination in 661, Mu'awiya compelled Ali's son Hasan to abdicate, securing his rule.

### Domestic Rule and Administration
Mu'awiya established his court in Damascus and relied on Syria's loyalist Arab tribes and a largely Christian bureaucracy. He is credited with creating government departments for correspondence, chancellery, and postal routes. He was the first caliph whose name appeared on coins and inscriptions. Domestically, he governed indirectly through alliances with provincial chieftains, preferring bribery and persuasion over force.

### External Conquests and Wars
Mu'awiya pursued near-annual raids against the Byzantine Empire in Anatolia. The historicity and dating of a major siege of Constantinople (c. 674–678) is debated by modern historians. He also launched the conquest of central North Africa (Ifriqiya) in 670 and secured Arab control over Armenia.

### Nomination of Yazid and Death
In an unprecedented move, Mu'awiya nominated his son Yazid I as his successor, introducing hereditary succession. This was opposed by prominent figures including Ali's son Husayn and Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr. Mu'awiya died in Damascus in April 680, and his succession led to the Second Fitna.

## Terms
- **Rashidun**: The four "rightly-guided" caliphs (Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, Ali) who succeeded Muhammad before the Umayyad dynasty.
- **First Fitna**: The first major civil war in Islam (656–661), following Uthman's assassination, primarily between Ali and Mu'awiya.
- **Second Fitna**: The civil war (680–692) following Mu'awiya's death, sparked by opposition to his son Yazid's succession.
- **Banu Kalb**: A powerful Arab tribe in the Syrian steppe that became Mu'awiya's key ally through marriage and military support.
- **Quda'a**: A tribal confederation in Syria, dominated by the Kalb, which formed the core of Mu'awiya's support base.
- **Diwan**: Government department; Mu'awiya established diwans for correspondence, chancellery, and postal routes.
- **Ashraf**: Tribal chieftains who served as intermediaries between the caliphal government and the tribesmen in the garrisons.
- **Ifriqiya**: The Islamic province in central North Africa (modern Tunisia), established during Mu'awiya's reign.
- **Shura**: Consultative council; a principle of Islamic governance that Mu'awiya's hereditary succession was seen to violate.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Siege of Constantinople**: The traditional view of a major Arab siege (c. 674–678) is challenged by some scholars who question its historicity or date it earlier.
- **Mu'awiya's Piety**: Some modern historians question his commitment to Islam based on inscriptions lacking explicit Islamic references, suggesting he may have adhered to a non-confessional monotheism.
- **Hereditary Succession**: The move to nominate Yazid is debated as either a pragmatic necessity for stability or a corrupting innovation that turned the caliphate into a monarchy.
- **Historical Sources**: The early Muslim histories, compiled in Abbasid Iraq, are largely anti-Umayyad, though they portray Mu'awiya in a relatively balanced manner compared to other Umayyads.
