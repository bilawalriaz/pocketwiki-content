# Hungarian Revolution of 1956

## Overview
The Hungarian Revolution of 1956 was a nationwide uprising against the Soviet-imposed communist government and its policies. It began as a student-led protest in Budapest and rapidly evolved into a popular revolution demanding political freedom, national sovereignty, and economic reform. The revolution was crushed by a massive Soviet military intervention, resulting in significant casualties and a refugee crisis, but it remains a defining moment of Cold War-era resistance and a symbol of the struggle for self-determination.

## Timeline
- **23 October 1956** — Student demonstrations in Budapest escalate into a nationwide uprising after police fire on protesters.
- **24 October 1956** — Soviet troops enter Budapest; Imre Nagy becomes Prime Minister.
- **28 October 1956** — A ceasefire is declared; the ÁVH secret police is disbanded.
- **1 November 1956** — Hungary declares neutrality and withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact.
- **3 November 1956** — Hungarian delegation negotiating Soviet withdrawal is arrested.
- **4 November 1956** — Massive Soviet invasion ("Operation Whirlwind") begins; Nagy broadcasts a final appeal.
- **10 November 1956** — Organized resistance in Budapest ends.
- **22 November 1956** — Imre Nagy and others are arrested after leaving the Yugoslav embassy.
- **June 1958** — Imre Nagy, Pál Maléter, and Miklós Gimes are executed after secret trials.

## Body

### Background and Causes
The revolution emerged from deep-seated discontent with the Stalinist regime of Mátyás Rákosi, which was a satellite of the USSR. The ÁVH secret police enforced political repression, while a Soviet-style economy led to declining living standards and heavy war reparations. The process of de-Stalinization, initiated by Khrushchev's 1956 speech, and the successful Polish October movement emboldened Hungarian reformers. Students re-established the banned MEFESZ union and issued demands for free elections and the withdrawal of Soviet troops.

### The Uprising and Revolutionary Government
The protest on 23 October turned violent when the ÁVH fired on demonstrators. Revolutionaries formed militias, attacked symbols of Soviet control, and local workers' councils (soviets) took power. The Rákosi government collapsed, and Imre Nagy became Prime Minister. His government disbanded the ÁVH, declared neutrality, and withdrew from the Warsaw Pact, hoping to negotiate a peaceful transition. Fighting largely ceased by late October as a new political order seemed to emerge.

### Soviet Intervention and Suppression
Despite initial negotiations, the USSR launched a full-scale invasion on 4 November. The operation, codenamed "Operation Whirlwind," involved 17 Soviet divisions and overwhelming firepower. Hungarian resistance, though fierce in pockets like the Corvin Passage and Csepel, was disorganized and crushed. The Nagy government fled, and a Soviet-backed government under János Kádár was proclaimed. The invasion killed approximately 2,500 Hungarians and 700 Soviet soldiers, and forced nearly 200,000 to flee the country.

### Aftermath and Legacy
The revolution was followed by mass arrests, executions, and the consolidation of Kádár's rule under continued Soviet military presence. Cardinal Mindszenty sought asylum in the U.S. embassy for 15 years. Internationally, the event exposed the limits of Western "rollback" rhetoric and caused major schisms in Western communist parties. The revolution was suppressed but not forgotten; it became a national symbol in Hungary, and 23 October is now a national holiday. In 1991, Russia officially apologized for the 1956 intervention.

## Terms
- **ÁVH (State Protection Authority):** The secret police of communist Hungary, responsible for political repression and violence against dissidents.
- **Warsaw Pact:** A 1955 mutual defense treaty among the USSR and its Eastern Bloc satellite states, including Hungary.
- **De-Stalinization:** The process of political liberalization in the USSR and its satellites following Stalin's death in 1953, notably marked by Khrushchev's 1956 speech.
- **Soviets (workers' councils):** Local councils of workers and citizens that assumed political and administrative power during the revolution, challenging the communist party's authority.
- **Operation Whirlwind:** The codename for the Soviet military operation to crush the Hungarian Revolution in November 1956.
- **COMECON (Council for Mutual Economic Assistance):** An economic organization under Soviet leadership that integrated the economies of the Eastern Bloc, limiting trade with the West.
- **MEFESZ:** The Union of Hungarian University and Academy Students, a democratic student union re-established in 1956 that helped organize the initial protests.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Western Responsibility:** A major debate concerns whether Radio Free Europe broadcasts, funded by the CIA, falsely implied Western military support, encouraging Hungarians to revolt and then abandoning them.
- **Soviet Motivations:** Scholars debate the internal Soviet decision-making process, particularly the influence of Mao Zedong's shifting position and the role of hardliners versus reformers like Khrushchev and Zhukov.
- **Nature of the Revolution:** Soviet historiography labeled it a "counter-revolution" instigated by Western fascists, while most Western and Hungarian historians view it as a popular national and democratic uprising.
- **Casualty Figures:** Precise numbers for deaths, especially during events like the Kossuth Square massacre on 25 October, remain disputed due to conflicting eyewitness accounts and propaganda.
