# Henry VIII

## Overview
Henry VIII (1491–1547) was King of England whose reign fundamentally reshaped the nation's religious, political, and social landscape. His desire for a male heir led to the English Reformation, the break from the Catholic Church, and the establishment of royal supremacy, making him one of the most consequential monarchs in English history.

## Timeline
- **28 June 1491** — Born in Greenwich, second son of Henry VII.
- **22 April 1509** — Accedes to the throne upon his father's death.
- **11 June 1509** — Marries Catherine of Aragon.
- **1513** — Invades France; English victory at Battle of Flodden.
- **1521** — Publishes *Assertio Septem Sacramentorum*, earning title "Defender of the Faith."
- **1533** — Archbishop Cranmer declares marriage to Catherine null; marries Anne Boleyn.
- **1534** — Acts of Supremacy passed, making Henry Supreme Head of the Church of England.
- **1536** — Execution of Anne Boleyn; marriage to Jane Seymour; Dissolution of the Monasteries begins.
- **1540** — Marriage to Anne of Cleves annulled; marriage to Catherine Howard.
- **1542** — Execution of Catherine Howard; all English monasteries dissolved.
- **1543** — Marries Catherine Parr; Third Succession Act restores Mary and Elizabeth to line of succession.
- **28 January 1547** — Dies at Whitehall Palace, succeeded by his son Edward VI.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Henry was born the second son and was not expected to become king. His elder brother Arthur's death in 1502 made him heir. He received a first-rate humanist education, becoming fluent in Latin and French. He ascended the throne in 1509 at age 17, quickly marrying his brother's widow, Catherine of Aragon, and executing his father's unpopular ministers.

### Foreign Policy and "The King's Great Matter"
Henry's early reign was marked by military campaigns in France, seeking to restore English territories. His primary domestic crisis, the "Great Matter," was his desire to annul his marriage to Catherine, who had failed to produce a surviving male heir. When Pope Clement VII refused, influenced by Catherine's nephew Emperor Charles V, Henry, advised by Thomas Cromwell, broke with Rome. Parliament passed the Acts of Supremacy (1534), declaring Henry the Supreme Head of the Church of England, severing England from papal authority.

### The English Reformation and Dissolution
The break with Rome initiated the English Reformation. Henry's chief minister, Thomas Cromwell, orchestrated the Dissolution of the Monasteries (1536–1540), transferring vast church wealth and lands to the Crown and a new gentry class. This act, along with the imposition of the Oath of Supremacy, faced resistance, most notably in the Pilgrimage of Grace (1536), which was brutally suppressed. While Henry rejected papal supremacy, he remained a religious conservative, persecuting both radical Protestants and Catholics who refused to acknowledge his supremacy.

### Marriages and Succession
Henry's six marriages were driven by his quest for a male heir and political alliances. After annulling his marriage to Catherine, he married Anne Boleyn, who bore him a daughter, Elizabeth. Anne was executed in 1536. His third wife, Jane Seymour, finally gave him a son, Edward, but died shortly after. Subsequent marriages to Anne of Cleves (annulled), Catherine Howard (executed for adultery), and Catherine Parr followed. The Third Succession Act (1543) placed Edward, then Mary, and then Elizabeth in the line of succession.

### Government, Finances, and Military
Henry's government was centralized by ministers like Cardinal Wolsey and Thomas Cromwell, who expanded the role of the Privy Council and used Parliament to enact reforms. Henry's lavish spending on wars, court, and building projects, combined with the costs of military campaigns in France and Scotland, drained the treasury. The dissolution of the monasteries provided temporary relief but did not solve long-term financial problems. He is credited with founding the Royal Navy and building a chain of coastal defences known as Device Forts.

### Physical Decline and Death
A severe jousting accident in 1536 caused a chronic leg wound that likely contributed to his later obesity, mood swings, and declining health. He became morbidly obese and immobile. He died in 1547 at age 55, succeeded by his nine-year-old son, Edward VI.

## Terms
*   **Act of Supremacy (1534):** Parliamentary statute declaring the monarch the Supreme Head of the Church of England, severing ties with Rome.
*   **Dissolution of the Monasteries:** The 1536–1540 process by which Henry VIII closed Catholic monasteries, seizing their wealth and lands for the Crown.
*   **Pilgrimage of Grace (1536):** A major uprising in northern England against Henry's religious reforms and the dissolution of monasteries.
*   **Praemunire:** A medieval law used to prosecute those who acknowledged papal authority over the king, employed by Henry to break with Rome.
*   **Reformation Parliament (1529–1536):** The series of parliaments that enacted the legislation separating the English church from Rome.
*   **Royal Supremacy:** The principle that the monarch is the head of state and, after 1534, the head of the Church of England.
*   **Tudor Rose:** The combined red and white rose emblem of the Tudor dynasty, symbolizing the union of the houses of Lancaster and York.
*   **Valor Ecclesiasticus:** A 1535 survey of church property compiled to assess its taxable value before dissolution.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Agency in the Reformation:** Historians debate whether Henry VIII was the driving force behind the English Reformation or if his chief minister, Thomas Cromwell, was the primary architect (the "Tudor Revolution in Government" thesis).
*   **Motivations for the Break with Rome:** Scholars question whether Henry's primary motivation was his desire for an annulment from Catherine of Aragon, a principled stand for national sovereignty, or a combination of political, financial, and religious factors.
*   **Henry's Personal Responsibility:** There is ongoing debate about the extent to which Henry's personal character—his tyranny, paranoia, and lust—directly caused the upheavals of his reign, versus the influence of court factions and structural forces.
*   **Religious Settlement:** The precise nature of Henry's own faith is debated; he broke with Rome but remained theologically conservative, creating an ambiguous religious settlement that satisfied neither ardent Catholics nor Protestants.
