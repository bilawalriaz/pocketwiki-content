# King Lear

## Overview
*King Lear* is a tragedy by William Shakespeare, written c. 1605–1606, depicting the catastrophic consequences of an aging king's decision to divide his kingdom based on his daughters' declarations of love. It is celebrated for its profound exploration of madness, blindness, and human nature, and is widely regarded as one of the greatest works in English literature.

## Timeline
- **c. 1605–1606** — Shakespeare writes *King Lear*.
- **26 Dec 1606** — Earliest known performance, before King James I at Whitehall.
- **1608** — First Quarto (Q1) published.
- **1619** — Second Quarto (Q2) published, unofficial and based on Q1.
- **1623** — First Folio (F1) published, containing a significantly different text.
- **1681** — Nahum Tate produces a revised version with a happy ending, which dominates the stage for over a century.
- **1823** — Edmund Kean reinstates Shakespeare's tragic ending in a major production.
- **1838** — William Macready performs Shakespeare's original text at Covent Garden, largely displacing Tate's version.

## Body

### Plot and Characters
The play begins with King Lear planning to divide his kingdom among his three daughters, Goneril, Regan, and Cordelia, based on their professions of love. When Cordelia refuses to flatter him, he disowns her and banishes the loyal Earl of Kent. Lear's division of power leads to his rejection by his elder daughters, descent into madness on a stormy heath, and eventual death from grief after Cordelia is executed. A parallel subplot involves the Earl of Gloucester, who is betrayed by his illegitimate son Edmund and blinded, while his legitimate son Edgar, disguised as the mad beggar "Poor Tom," remains loyal.

### Textual History and Sources
The modern text derives from three primary sources: the 1608 Quarto (Q1), the 1619 Quarto (Q2), and the 1623 First Folio (F1). The Quarto and Folio texts differ significantly, with hundreds of lines and words unique to each. Early editors conflated these versions, but modern scholarship debates whether the differences reflect Shakespeare's own revisions or errors in transmission. Shakespeare's main source was Holinshed's *Chronicles*, which itself drew from Geoffrey of Monmouth's *Historia Regum Britanniae*. The Gloucester subplot is adapted from Philip Sidney's *Arcadia*. Shakespeare's key innovation was the tragic ending, as earlier versions had Cordelia restore Lear to the throne.

### Performance History
For nearly 150 years after the Restoration (1606), Nahum Tate's adaptation with a happy ending was the standard stage version. The 19th century saw a gradual return to Shakespeare's text, with major productions by Edmund Kean and William Macready. The 20th century introduced influential modern interpretations, such as Peter Brook's stark 1962 production and Akira Kurosawa's 1985 film *Ran*, which transposed the story to feudal Japan. The play continues to be adapted globally into diverse theatrical and cinematic traditions.

### Interpretations
Scholars have analyzed *King Lear* through various lenses. Historicist readings see it as reflecting contemporary debates about "Nature," reason, and political authority, possibly alluding to King James I's policies. Psychoanalytic interpretations focus on Lear's narcissism, his regression, and the "maternal subtext" of his relationship with his daughters. The play's relationship to Christian doctrine is debated, with critics arguing over whether Lear achieves redemption or remains in a state of hopeless despair.

## Terms
- **Quarto (Q1, Q2):** Early printed editions of a play, often based on performance scripts or "foul papers."
- **First Folio (F1):** The first collected edition of Shakespeare's plays, published in 1623, which preserved many texts not available elsewhere.
- **Conflation:** The editorial practice of combining different source texts (like Q1 and F1) to create a single, composite version of the play.
- **Foul papers:** A playwright's rough draft or working manuscript, which may contain corrections and variations.
- **Promptbook:** A copy of a play used for a specific production, often marked with cues, cuts, and stage directions.
- **Restoration:** The period after 1606 when the English monarchy was restored, leading to major changes in theatre and the adaptation of Shakespeare's plays.
- **Pictorialism:** A 19th-century theatrical style emphasizing visual spectacle and elaborate sets over textual fidelity.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Textual Origin:** Do the significant differences between the Quarto and Folio texts indicate that Shakespeare revised the play, or are they the result of errors by scribes and printers?
- **Christian Meaning:** Does the play affirm a Christian vision of redemption through suffering, or does it present a bleak, godless universe?
- **Political Allegory:** To what extent is the play a commentary on the political tensions and royal policies of King James I's reign?
