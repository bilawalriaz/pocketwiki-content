# Masnavi

## Overview
The *Masnavi-ye-Ma'navi* (Spiritual Couplets) is a six-book Persian poem by the Sufi mystic Rumi, composed between c. 1258 and 1273. It is a foundational text of Sufism, using stories and anecdotes to teach the path of spiritual love for God. Often called the "Quran in Persian," it is considered one of the most important works in Islamic literature and a masterpiece of world mystical poetry.

## Timeline
- **c. 1258** — Rumi, aged about 54, begins dictating the *Masnavi* at the request of his disciple Husam al-Din Chalabi.
- **1258–1273** — Rumi composes the first five books and most of the sixth book.
- **1273** — Rumi dies; the sixth and final book remains incomplete.
- **1881** — James W. Redhouse publishes the first English translation (Book I only).
- **1924–40** — R. A. Nicholson publishes the "classic edition" with the first complete English translation.
- **2004–25** — Jawid Mojaddedi publishes the first complete translation into English verse (Oxford World's Classics).

## Body

### Creation and Structure
The *Masnavi* was begun at the request of Rumi's favorite disciple, Husam al-Din Chalabi, who noted that followers often read the works of earlier poets like Sana'i and 'Attar. Rumi therefore created a work in their didactic style to complement his other poetry. He dictated the verses in meetings where Chalabi would write them down. The poem consists of six books, each containing about 4,000 verses and its own prose introduction. The sixth book is unfinished, leading to speculation about a possible seventh volume.

### Themes and Narrative
The six books are thematically paired: Books 1 and 2 focus on the *nafs* (the lower, carnal self); Books 3 and 4 on Reason and Knowledge, personified by the Prophet Moses; and Books 5 and 6 on denying earthly existence to understand God. The poem uses no single plot but is a collection of stories from the Quran, hadith, and everyday life, all illustrating spiritual morals. Rumi employs seven principal narrative voices, including the authorial voice of a Sufi teacher, storytelling, analogy, character dialogue, moral reflection, spiritual discourse, and a "hiatus" where he questions his own verses. The general narrative pattern flows from Problem/Theme to Complication to Resolution.

### Editions and Translations
The first complete English translation was by R. A. Nicholson (1924–40), considered the classic scholarly edition. A major modern translation into English verse is by Jawid Mojaddedi (2004–25), the first of its kind. Other notable English translations include those by E. H. Whinfield (1887, abridged) and Alan Williams (2006, Book I). Paraphrases, such as those by Coleman Barks, are popular but not direct translations. The work has also been translated into Turkish, Urdu, and other languages.

## Terms
- **Masnavi (poetic form):** A Persian poetic form using rhyming couplets.
- **Sufism:** The mystical dimension of Islam, focused on inner spiritual experience and love of God.
- **Nafs:** The lower, carnal self or ego in Islamic psychology, which must be disciplined.
- **Hadith:** Accounts of the sayings, actions, and approvals of the Prophet Muhammad.
- **Didactic:** Intended to teach, particularly in imparting moral or intellectual instruction.
- **Couplet:** A pair of successive lines of verse, typically rhyming.

## Debates and Open Questions
The incomplete ending of the sixth book has led to scholarly debate about whether the work was finished at Rumi's death and to claims about the possible existence of a lost seventh volume.
