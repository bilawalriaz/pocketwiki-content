# Balkans

## Overview
The Balkans is a region in southeastern Europe with contested geographical and historical definitions, centered on the Balkan Peninsula. It is historically significant as a crossroads of cultures, empires, and religions, and its modern political landscape was shaped by the dissolution of Yugoslavia and the Ottoman Empire. The term itself is a 19th-century geopolitical construct that has acquired complex, often negative, connotations.

## Timeline
- **c. 5000–3500 BC** — Peak of the Danube (Old Europe) civilization.
- **1808** — German geographer August Zeune creates the concept of the "Balkan Peninsula."
- **1362–1496** — Ottoman conquest of most Balkan territories.
- **1821–1912** — Emergence of independent nation-states (Greece, Serbia, Montenegro, Romania, Bulgaria, Albania).
- **1912–1913** — First and Second Balkan Wars reshape the region.
- **1914** — Assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo triggers World War I.
- **1941–1944** — Axis occupation and partition of Yugoslavia and Greece during World War II.
- **1991–2001** — Dissolution of Yugoslavia and subsequent wars.
- **2008** — Kosovo declares independence from Serbia.
- **2019** — The former Republic of Macedonia is renamed North Macedonia.

## Body

### Definition and Etymology
The name "Balkan" is of obscure origin, possibly from Turkish for "wooded mountains." The concept of a "Balkan Peninsula" was coined in 1808 by August Zeune, who mistakenly believed the Balkan Mountains were the dominant range. The term was historically synonymous with Rumelia, the Ottoman provinces in Europe. Modern geographers often reject the "peninsula" label as it does not meet the technical definition (land border longer than sea borders), preferring "Southeast Europe." The region's borders are disputed; most definitions include Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Bulgaria, Croatia (south of the Sava/Kupa rivers), mainland Greece, Kosovo, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Romania (Northern Dobruja), Serbia (south of the Danube), and East Thrace (Turkey). Broader definitions may include Slovenia, Hungary, or Moldova.

### History
The Balkans were a Neolithic crossroads where farming spread from the Middle East to Europe. It was home to ancient peoples like the Thracians and Illyrians, later conquered by the Roman Empire. Slavic migrations in the 6th–7th centuries shaped the region's ethnic landscape. The Byzantine Empire was a dominant medieval power, spreading Eastern Orthodox Christianity. The Ottoman conquest began in the 14th century, creating a multi-religious empire. The 19th and early 20th centuries saw the rise of nationalist movements and independent states. The region was a flashpoint for World War I and was devastated in World War II. The Cold War saw most states under communist rule, with Yugoslavia and Albania breaking from the Soviet bloc. The 1990s were marked by the violent breakup of Yugoslavia.

### Nature and Geography
The landscape is dominated by mountain ranges like the Balkan, Dinaric Alps, and Pindus. The highest peak is Musala (2,925 m) in Bulgaria. Climates vary from Mediterranean on the coasts to humid continental inland. Soils are generally poor, except on plains. Resources include coal (notably in Kosovo), lignite, and some metal ores, but energy resources are scarce. Hydropower is widely used.

### Demographics and Culture
The region is ethnically diverse, including Albanians, Bosniaks, Bulgarians, Croats, Greeks, Macedonians, Romanians, Serbs, Slovenes, and Turks. It is a meeting point of Eastern Orthodoxy, Islam, and Roman Catholicism. Languages belong mainly to the Slavic, Romance, and Hellenic branches of the Indo-European family, plus Albanian, Turkish, and Hungarian. The Jewish communities, historically significant, were largely destroyed in the Holocaust.

### Economy and Politics
All states are republics with open-market economies. Income levels vary, with Slovenia, Croatia, and Greece having high-income economies. The Western Balkans (a political term for non-EU Balkan states) are transitioning from state-owned to market economies, with SMEs now dominant. The region is a focus of EU and NATO enlargement, with most countries being members or candidates.

## Terms
- **Balkanization**: The process of fragmentation or division of a region into smaller, often hostile, states or groups.
- **Rumelia**: The historical name for the Balkan provinces of the Ottoman Empire.
- **Peninsula**: A piece of land almost surrounded by water or projecting out into a body of water, with a land border shorter than its sea borders.
- **Jireček Line**: A hypothetical line dividing the Roman Empire's Latin-influenced north from its Greek-influenced south in the Balkans.
- **Western Balkans**: A political term for the Balkan countries not in the EU, primarily the former Yugoslav states except Slovenia.
- **Old Europe**: A term for the Neolithic Danube civilization that peaked c. 5000–3500 BC.
- **Polje**: A large, flat-bottomed depression in karst limestone regions, a common Balkan landscape feature.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Geographical Validity**: Modern geographers reject the term "Balkan Peninsula" because its land border is longer than its sea borders, failing the technical definition of a peninsula.
- **Definitional Dispute**: There is no universal agreement on which countries constitute the Balkans, with definitions varying from narrow (core states) to broad (including Hungary, Moldova).
- **Stigmatization**: The term "Balkans" and especially "Western Balkans" is seen by some, notably in Croatia and Slovenia, as pejorative, carrying negative connotations of conflict and backwardness, leading to a preference for "Southeast Europe."
- **Northern Border**: The exact northern geographical boundary of the Balkan region is a "delicate problem" that scholars often avoid, as rivers like the Danube and Sava do not form a clear, universally accepted line.
