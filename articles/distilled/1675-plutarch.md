# Plutarch

## Overview
Plutarch (c. 46–after 119 CE) was a Greek Middle Platonist philosopher, priest of Apollo at Delphi, and Roman citizen whose biographical and ethical writings shaped the Western conception of classical virtue. His *Parallel Lives* paired Greek and Roman statesmen to explore how character determines destiny, prioritizing moral portraiture over strict historiography. His *Moralia* essays applied Platonic philosophy to religion, ethics, and natural science, influencing thinkers from Montaigne and Shakespeare to the American Founders. As a Delphic priest and Amphictyonic League official, he helped revive the sanctuary of Apollo, embodying the fusion of Greek paideia and Roman imperial service that defined the Second Sophistic era.

## Timeline
- **c. 46 CE** — Born in Chaeronea, Boeotia, to a prominent local family.
- **AD 66–67** — Studied mathematics and philosophy in Athens under Ammonius.
- **c. AD 70** — Visited Rome with sponsor Lucius Mestrius Florus; gained Roman citizenship (name: Lucius Mestrius Plutarchus).
- **c. AD 95** — Appointed one of two priests of Apollo at Delphi; involved in Delphic revival.
- **AD 107–127** — Served five terms as *epimeletes* (manager) of the Amphictyonic League, organizing Pythian Games.
- **c. AD 119–125** — Death (estimates vary; latest sources say "after 119").

## Body

### Life and Career
Plutarch was born in Chaeronea, near Delphi, into a family long established in Boeotia. He studied in Athens under the Platonist Ammonius (AD 66–67) and attended the Delphic games where Nero competed, possibly meeting Vespasian. Through the patronage of Lucius Mestrius Florus, an associate of Vespasian, he obtained Roman citizenship and equestrian status, adopting the name Lucius Mestrius Plutarchus. He visited Rome c. AD 70, befriending consulars such as Quintus Sosius Senecio and Arulenus Rusticus. Despite these connections, he resided primarily in Chaeronea, serving as local magistrate (*archon*) and representing his town abroad. He was initiated into the mysteries of Apollo and likely the Eleusinian Mysteries. Around AD 95, he became a priest of the Delphic temple, aiding its revival during a construction boom funded by Greek patrons and imperial support. From 107 to 127, he managed the Amphictyonic League for five terms, organizing the Pythian Games. Late in life, Emperor Hadrian reportedly appointed him nominal procurator of Achaea, granting consular insignia. He married Timoxena; they had at least five children, two of whom died young. Surviving sons Autoboulos and Plutarch appear in his dialogues; a third, Soklaros, may be attested epigraphically.

### Parallel Lives
The *Parallel Lives* comprises 23 pairs of biographies (one Greek, one Roman) plus four unpaired lives, designed to illuminate shared moral virtues and vices rather than narrate history. Plutarch explicitly states in the *Life of Alexander* that his aim is the influence of character on destiny, often favoring revealing anecdotes over epoch-making events. He likens his method to portrait painting, drawing parallels between physical traits and moral character. Extant lives include Solon, Pericles, Alexander, Caesar, Cicero, Cato the Younger, and Brutus. Lost lives include those of Heracles, Philip II, Epaminondas, Scipio Africanus, and early emperors Augustus through Nero (only Galba and Otho survive, likely as a single moral-ethical diptych). The *Life of Alexander* preserves unique anecdotes and a detailed physical description from the sculptor Lysippos, tracing Alexander’s drive from youth to the moral decline marked by the murder of Cleitus. The *Life of Caesar* aligns closely with Suetonius and Caesar’s own commentaries, ending with the assassination and the phantom appearing to Brutus. The *Life of Pyrrhus* is the principal surviving source for Roman history 293–264 BCE, a period lost in Livy and Dionysius.

### Moralia
The *Moralia* collects 78 essays and speeches on philosophy, religion, science, and ethics. Key works include *On the Face in the Moon* (a source for Galileo), *On the Worship of Isis and Osiris* (a major source on Egyptian religion), *On the Decline of the Oracles*, and *On Peace of Mind*. Several works in the manuscript tradition are pseudepigrapha by "Pseudo-Plutarch" (3rd–4th century CE), including *Lives of the Ten Orators* and *On Fate*, though they retain historical value. The *Catalogue of Lamprias*, an ancient list attributed to a son, enumerates 227 works; only 78 survive. Lost *Moralia* treatises include *On Pyrrho’s Ten Modes* and *On the Difference between the Pyrrhonians and the Academics*.

### Philosophy
Plutarch identified as a Platonist but engaged Peripatetic and Stoic ideas while rejecting Epicureanism absolutely. He dismissed theoretical metaphysics as insoluble, focusing on moral and religious questions. Against Stoic materialism and Epicurean atheism, he upheld a transcendent God (Plato’s Form of the Good) and introduced a second principle, the Dyad—identified as an evil world-soul bound to matter, later ordered by reason into the divine world-soul yet remaining the source of evil. This dualism allowed him to affirm divine providence, daemon intermediaries, free will, and the soul’s immortality. His ethics fused philosophy with religion: myths contain allegorical truths; the gods of all nations are names for one divine Being. He justified divination as direct revelation accessible in "enthusiasm" (passive receptivity). He taught the philosopher Favorinus and practiced vegetarianism, defending it in two *Moralia* discourses.

### Influence
Plutarch’s impact on European literature is profound. Jacques Amyot’s French translations (Lives 1559, Moralia 1572) reached England via Thomas North’s English *Lives* (1579), which Shakespeare paraphrased and quoted verbatim in his Roman plays. Montaigne’s *Essays* contain over 400 references to Plutarch, modeling their discursive inquiry on the *Moralia*. Philemon Holland translated the complete *Moralia* into English (1603); John Dryden oversaw a Greek-based *Lives* (1683), later revised by Arthur Hugh Clough (1859). Rousseau cited Plutarch against meat-eating in *Emile* (1762); Boswell invoked him on biography in *Life of Johnson*. In America, Plutarch is the most-cited ancient historian in the *Federalist Papers*; Emerson called the *Lives* "a bible for heroes."

## Terms
- **Middle Platonism**: Philosophical school (1st c. BCE–3rd c. CE) synthesizing Plato’s doctrines with Aristotelian and Stoic elements; Plutarch’s primary affiliation.
- **Parallel Lives**: Plutarch’s paired biographies of Greek and Roman figures, structured to compare moral character rather than chronicle events.
- **Moralia**: "Customs and Mores"; Plutarch’s collected 78 essays and speeches on ethics, religion, and science.
- **Amphictyonic League**: Council of Greek tribes administering the sanctuaries of Delphi and Thermopylae; Plutarch served as its *epimeletes* (manager) for five terms.
- **Epimeletes**: Manager or overseer; Plutarch’s title for organizing the Pythian Games under the Amphictyonic League.
- **Pseudo-Plutarch**: Unknown author (3rd–4th c. CE) of works falsely attributed to Plutarch in the *Moralia* tradition (e.g., *Lives of the Ten Orators*).
- **Dyad**: In Plutarch’s metaphysics, the evil world-soul (second principle) that, when ordered by reason, becomes the divine world-soul but remains the source of evil.
- **Enthusiasm** (*enthousiasmos*): State of passive receptivity in which the soul receives divine revelation, justifying divination.
- **Procurator of Achaea**: Imperial financial/administrative post; Hadrian reportedly granted Plutarch this title honorifically, with consular insignia.
- **Second Sophistic**: Cultural movement (1st–2nd c. CE) reviving Greek rhetoric and paideia under Roman rule; Plutarch exemplifies its fusion of Hellenic identity and imperial service.

## Debates and open questions
- **Date of death**: Estimates range from c. 119 (older *Britannica*) to c. 125 (Gregory Crane); current *Britannica* states "after 119."
- **Roman name**: Citizenship name *Lucius Mestrius Plutarchus* is attested but the timing and sponsor (Florus) rely on inference from the *Life of Otho*.
- **Catalogue of Lamprias**: The list of 227 works attributed to a son, Lamprias, is considered by most modern scholars a later interpolation.
- **Galba and Otho**: Whether these surviving imperial lives form a single diptych separate from the *Parallel Lives* canon, and whether they were published under the Flavians or Nerva.
- **Vegetarianism**: Duration and strictness of Plutarch’s abstention from meat are unclear from the two *Moralia* discourses.
- **Lost works**: Twelve additional *Lives* and numerous *Moralia* treatises are known only by title or fragment; the original ending comparisons for all *Parallel Lives* pairs are missing (only 19 survive).
