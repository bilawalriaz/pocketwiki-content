# Tyre, Lebanon

## Overview
Tyre is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world, located on the coast of modern Lebanon. A major Phoenician metropolis, it was a legendary birthplace of figures like Europa and Dido, and a powerful maritime trading state. Its ancient archaeological sites, including a Roman hippodrome, led to its designation as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984, though it faces modern threats from urban development, conflict, and coastal erosion.

## Timeline
- **c. 2750 BC** — Traditional founding date, according to Herodotus.
- **9th–6th centuries BC** — Prominent period as a Phoenician city-state, establishing colonies like Carthage.
- **572 BC** — Conquered by Babylon.
- **332 BC** — Besieged and conquered by Alexander the Great; a causeway is built linking the island city to the mainland.
- **315 CE** — Cathedral of Paulinus inaugurated.
- **1860–61** — First archaeological excavations by Ernest Renan.
- **1946** — Large-scale excavations begin under Maurice Chéhab.
- **1984** — Inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **1998** — Tyre Coast Nature Reserve decreed.
- **2006** — Damage to archaeological sites during the Lebanon War; UNESCO issues a "Heritage Alert."
- **2018** — Istanbouli Theatre troupe establishes the Lebanese National Theater in the Rivoli Cinema.
- **2024** — UNESCO grants enhanced protection to Tyre's archaeological sites during the Israeli invasion of Lebanon.

## Body

### Geography and Territory
Tyre is located about 80 km south of Beirut. It originally consisted of an island city and a mainland settlement called Ushu (later Palaetyrus). The island was connected to the mainland by a causeway built by Alexander the Great in 332 BCE, which silted up over centuries to form a wide isthmus. The city has two ancient harbours: the northern "Sidonian port" and the southern "Egyptian port." The area is seismically active but at low risk, though tsunamis pose a threat. Vast natural gas reserves lie offshore, but exploitation is delayed by border disputes with Israel.

### History
The site has been occupied since the Bronze Age. Tyre became a powerful Phoenician city-state, settling colonies across the Mediterranean. It was conquered by Babylon in 572 BC and later by Alexander the Great in 332 BC after a brutal siege. The city flourished under Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, and Medieval rule. Archaeological excavations began in the 19th century and have uncovered significant remains, though work was interrupted by the Lebanese Civil War. UNESCO declared it a World Heritage Site in 1984 to protect it from conflict and urban development. Threats include development pressures, illegal antiquities trade, and severe coastal erosion.

### Nature Reserve and Environment
The Tyre Coast Nature Reserve, established in 1998, is a Ramsar Site protecting the last bio-geographic ecosystem in southern Lebanon. It includes a sandy beach, conservation zones for sea turtles and birds, and agricultural lands with springs. The reserve hosts diverse flora and fauna but is threatened by invasive species, pollution, and damage from the 2006 war. Rising sea levels due to global warming also threaten coastal erosion.

### Cultural Heritage and Archaeology
Major archaeological work has uncovered remains from the Hellenistic, Roman, and Byzantine periods, including the Al Bass/Hippodrome and Roman baths. Key figures include Maurice Chéhab and Honor Frost, a pioneer of underwater archaeology. Excavations have revealed ancient harbours, a Phoenician cremation cemetery, and an early church. The city's cultural life includes the Tyre International Festival held in the Roman hippodrome and the revival of cinemas as cultural spaces, such as the Lebanese National Theater. Threats to heritage include poor post-war construction and neglect of historical buildings.

### Demographics and Society
Tyre's population is predominantly Shia Muslim, with a smaller Christian community. The city hosts three major Palestinian refugee camps (Burj El Shimali, El Buss, Rashidieh) and a significant community of Syrian refugees. It is also known as "Little West Africa" due to a large diaspora community and many African migrant workers, primarily Ethiopian women. Poverty is a significant issue, with about 43% of Lebanese residents living in poverty as of 2016.

### Economy
The economy relies on tourism, construction, services, and remittances from the diaspora, especially in West Africa. UNIFIL contributes to local purchasing power. Agriculture includes olive and citrus farming, though with waste issues. The port handles used car imports, and traditional wooden boatbuilding continues. Real estate prices have risen due to diaspora investment. Offshore natural gas exploration is planned.

## Terms
- **Phoenician**: A Semitic-speaking civilization that originated in the Levant, known for maritime trade and establishing colonies like Carthage.
- **UNESCO World Heritage Site**: A location inscribed by the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization for its cultural, historical, or scientific significance.
- **Isthmus**: A narrow strip of land connecting two larger land areas, formed in Tyre by Alexander's causeway and subsequent siltation.
- **Ramsar Site**: A wetland site designated as internationally important under the Ramsar Convention.
- **Kafala system**: A sponsorship system in some Middle Eastern countries that ties migrant workers' legal status to their employer.
- **Neophyte**: A non-native species that has been introduced to a new region and established itself.
- **Tombolo**: A deposition landform in which an island is attached to the mainland by a narrow piece of land such as a spit or bar.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact location and extent of Tyre's ancient southern harbour remain debated, with various submerged structures identified but not fully confirmed.
- The origins of the city's founders are debated, with traditions suggesting they came from nearby Sidon.
- The precise date and nature of the northern harbour's construction and use throughout antiquity are subjects of ongoing archaeological investigation.
- The impact of climate change, particularly sea-level rise and coastal erosion, on Tyre's archaeological sites and modern city is a critical and evolving concern.
