# Siméon Denis Poisson

## Overview
Siméon Denis Poisson (1781–1840) was a prolific French mathematician and physicist whose work bridged pure mathematics and applied physics. While some of his physical interpretations were later disproven, his mathematical contributions—particularly in potential theory, statistics, and analytical mechanics—have had a lasting impact. He demonstrated how physical problems could inspire new mathematical ideas, influencing fields from electromagnetism to quantum mechanics.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Poisson was born to a retired soldier and showed exceptional mathematical talent early. He entered the École Polytechnique at 17, where professors allowed him independent study. His early memoirs on algebra and finite difference equations were so impressive that he graduated without final examinations, gaining immediate entry into scientific circles. He was mentored by giants like Lagrange and Laplace, who recognized his potential.

### Career and Teaching
Poisson spent his career at the École Polytechnique and other Parisian institutions, rising to full professor by 1806. He was an extraordinarily successful teacher and published over 300 works. His career progressed through numerous academic and administrative roles, culminating in his appointment as a peer of France in 1837. He remained focused on mathematics through political upheavals, including the Revolution and Bourbon Restoration.

### Contributions to Mathematics and Physics
Poisson's work spanned many fields. In potential theory, he generalized Laplace's equation to **Poisson's equation** (∇²ϕ = -4πρ), which relates a potential function ϕ to a source density ρ. This became fundamental in gravitation, electricity, and magnetism. In optics, he famously attempted to disprove the wave theory of light by predicting a bright spot (the **Poisson spot**) in the shadow of a circular object—an experiment that instead confirmed wave theory. In statistics, he developed the **Poisisson distribution** for modeling rare events, first appearing in his 1829 work on birth statistics.

### Analytical Mechanics and Continuum Mechanics
Poisson advanced analytical mechanics, building on Lagrange's work. He introduced the **Poisson bracket**, a mathematical operation on functions of canonical variables that became central to Hamiltonian mechanics and later quantum mechanics. In continuum mechanics, he independently derived equations for viscous fluid flow (now part of the Navier-Stokes equations) and contributed to elasticity theory, introducing **Poisson's ratio**. He also worked on wave propagation and heat conduction, adopting and extending Fourier's methods.

### Interaction with Évariste Galois
In 1831, Poisson reviewed a manuscript on group theory submitted by the young mathematician Évariste Galois. He found it "incomprehensible" but encouraged publication. The report reached Galois in prison, influencing his decision to compile his work, though he died shortly after his release.

## Terms
- **Poisson's equation**: A partial differential equation (∇²ϕ = -4πρ) generalizing Laplace's equation to include a source term ρ, used in potential theory.
- **Poisson spot**: A bright point at the center of the shadow of a circular object, predicted by Poisson to disprove wave theory but later observed, confirming it.
- **Poisson distribution**: A probability distribution expressing the probability of a given number of events occurring in a fixed interval, often for rare events.
- **Poisson bracket**: An anti-commutative operation [u, v] on functions of canonical variables in Hamiltonian mechanics, used to describe time evolution.
- **Poisson's ratio**: In elasticity, the ratio of transverse strain to axial strain, introduced in his work on elastic bodies.
- **Analytical mechanics**: A formulation of mechanics using generalized coordinates and calculus of variations, as opposed to Newtonian vectorial mechanics.
- **Potential theory**: The study of scalar potentials (like gravitational or electric potential) that satisfy Laplace's or Poisson's equations.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Poisson's interpretations of physical phenomena, such as his views on electricity as a fluid distinct from matter, were later disproven by researchers like Faraday.
- His attempts to prove the convergence of Fourier series were unsuccessful, though they led to the discovery of the Poisson kernel.
- The naming of the "Poisson spot" is debated, as similar observations were made earlier by Delisle and Maraldi.
