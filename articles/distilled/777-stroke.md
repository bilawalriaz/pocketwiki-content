# Stroke

## Overview  
A stroke is a sudden neurological deficit caused by disrupted cerebral blood flow, either from blockage (ischemic, ~87% of cases) or bleeding (hemorrhagic). It is a leading global cause of death and disability, with outcomes heavily dependent on rapid recognition, timely treatment, and structured rehabilitation.

## Timeline  
- **c. 460–370 BC**: Hippocrates describes sudden paralysis associated with apoplexy.  
- **1599**: Term "stroke" used synonymously with apoplectic seizure.  
- **1658**: Wepfer links hemorrhagic stroke to brain bleeding and identifies carotid/vertebral arteries.  
- **1927**: Term "cerebrovascular accident" introduced.  
- **1990s**: "Time is Brain" concept emphasizes urgency of treatment.  
- **2015**: Mechanical thrombectomy shown effective within 12–24 hours.  
- **2021**: WHO reports 55+ hour workweek increases stroke risk by 35%.  
- **2025**: Studies highlight pregnancy complications as stroke risk in women under 50.

## Body  

### Classification and Definition  
Stroke is classified as ischemic (87%) or hemorrhagic (13%). The WHO 1970s definition requires neurological deficit lasting >24 hours or resulting in death. Alternative terms like "brain attack" reflect urgency. Hemorrhagic transformation—bleeding into ischemic tissue—is common but its origin unclear.

### Types and Causes  
Ischemic strokes arise from thrombosis (atherosclerotic plaque), embolism (cardiac or arterial debris), hypoperfusion (e.g., shock), or venous thrombosis. Cryptogenic strokes account for 30–40%. Hemorrhagic strokes include intracerebral (within brain) and subarachnoid (between brain and meninges) types, often due to hypertension, aneurysms, or amyloid angiopathy.

### Signs and Symptoms  
Symptoms include facial droop, limb weakness, speech difficulty, vision loss, and severe headache (especially hemorrhagic). FAST mnemonic aids recognition; BE FAST adds balance and eye symptoms. Posterior circulation strokes are harder to detect. Sudden onset suggests embolic or subarachnoid causes.

### Pathophysiology  
Ischemia triggers the ischemic cascade: ATP depletion, glutamate release, calcium influx, free radical damage, and cell death. The ischemic penumbra—salvageable tissue around the core—is key to treatment windows. Collateral circulation via the circle of Willis can mitigate damage. Hemorrhagic strokes cause harm via mass effect, toxicity, and inflammation.

### Diagnosis  
Clinical exam (NIHSS) plus imaging confirms diagnosis. CT rules out hemorrhage (sensitivity 89%) but misses early ischemia (sensitivity 16%). MRI is superior for ischemia (sensitivity 83%). Additional tests identify causes: carotid ultrasound, ECG, echocardiogram, and blood work.

### Prevention  
Hypertension is the leading modifiable risk factor. Antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel) and anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs) prevent recurrence. Statins reduce risk by ~15%. Lifestyle changes (Mediterranean diet, exercise) and surgery (endarterectomy) help high-risk patients. Screening asymptomatic individuals is not recommended.

### Management  
Ischemic strokes within 3–4.5 hours may receive thrombolytics (rtPA); mechanical thrombectomy extends this window to 24 hours. Blood pressure is not routinely lowered acutely. Hemorrhagic strokes require supportive care and blood pressure control. Stroke units improve survival. Rehabilitation begins immediately, focusing on mobility, speech, and cognition.

### Rehabilitation and Recovery  
Multidisciplinary teams address motor, speech, and cognitive deficits. Most recovery occurs in first 6 months. Interventions include physical/occupational therapy, speech therapy, constraint-induced movement therapy, and emerging techniques like virtual reality and transcranial stimulation. Self-management programs enhance long-term outcomes.

### Prognosis and Epidemiology  
75% of survivors face functional limitations. Post-stroke depression affects 30–50%. Stroke is the third-leading cause of death globally (~10% of deaths). Incidence declined 10% in developed nations (1990–2010) but rose in developing regions. Two-thirds occur in those over 65. Men have higher incidence; women have higher mortality.

## Terms  
- **Ischemic stroke**: Brain tissue damage due to blocked blood supply.  
- **Hemorrhagic stroke**: Bleeding into or around the brain, causing compression and toxicity.  
- **Transient ischemic attack (TIA)**: Stroke-like symptoms resolving within 24 hours.  
- **Thrombolysis**: Drug treatment to dissolve blood clots.  
- **Thrombectomy**: Surgical removal of a clot from a brain artery.  
- **Ischemic penumbra**: Area of brain tissue at risk but potentially salvageable during stroke.  
- **Circle of Willis**: Arterial network at the brain’s base supplying collateral circulation.  
- **NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS)**: Tool assessing stroke severity via neurological exam.  
- **FAST**: Mnemonic for stroke symptoms: Face droop, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call emergency.  
- **Stroke unit**: Specialized hospital ward improving post-stroke survival and recovery.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- Optimal timing and candidates for thrombectomy beyond 24 hours remain debated.  
- Whether hemorrhagic transformation begins as ischemic stroke is unknown.  
- Efficacy of anticoagulants in primary prevention is uncertain due to bleeding risks.  
- Long-term benefits of newer rehabilitative technologies (VR, robotics) need more evidence.  
- Role of routine screening for asymptomatic carotid stenosis is contested.  
- Impact of workweek length (>55 hours) on stroke risk requires further study.  
- Effectiveness of cognitive rehabilitation for spatial neglect lacks strong evidence.
