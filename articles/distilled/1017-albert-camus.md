# Albert Camus

## Overview
Albert Camus (1913–1960) was a French philosopher, novelist, author, dramatist, journalist, world federalist, and political activist. He won the 1957 Nobel Prize in Literature at age 44, becoming the second-youngest recipient in history and the first laureate in literature born in Africa. His work centers on **absurdism**—the conflict between humanity’s search for meaning and the "unreasonable silence of the world"—arguing that one must embrace this tension without succumbing to suicide or nihilism. A moralist who leaned toward **anarcho-syndicalism** (a labor-focused anarchism advocating worker self-management), Camus fiercely opposed totalitarianism in both Soviet communism and fascism, leading to a famous rupture with Jean-Paul Sartre. During the Algerian War (1954–1962), he kept a neutral stance, advocating a multicultural and pluralistic Algeria, a position that was rejected by most parties. He died in a car crash at 46, leaving an unfinished autobiographical novel, *The First Man*.

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Born into poverty in French Algeria, Camus was a second-generation *pied-noir* who held French citizenship but witnessed the systemic disenfranchisement of Arab and Berber Algerians under the *indigénat* legal code. His father’s death in WWI and his mother’s illiteracy shaped his empathy for the marginalized. A scholarship secured by his mentor Louis Germain allowed him to study philosophy at the University of Algiers, where Jean Grenier introduced him to Greek philosophy, Nietzsche, and Schopenhauer. Tuberculosis, diagnosed at 17, forced him out of his home and ended his athletic ambitions, redirecting him toward writing. He completed his *licence de philosophie* in 1936 with a thesis on Plotinus, while absorbing novelist-philosophers like Dostoevsky and Kafka. In 1934, he began a relationship with Simone Hié, marrying her to help fight her morphine addiction; the marriage ended in divorce after he discovered her affair with her doctor.

### Political Awakening and the Resistance
Camus joined the French Communist Party (PCF) in 1935 not as a Marxist but as a "springboard" against colonial inequality. He soon clashed with Stalinist discipline: after the Algerian Communist Party (PCA) expelled him for collaborating with the nationalist Parti du Peuple Algérien, his distrust of bureaucracies prioritizing efficiency over justice solidified. He channeled this into theatre (*Théâtre de l'Equipe*) and journalism at *Alger républicain*, exposing colonial abuses in Kabylie. Fleeing the German advance in 1940, he moved to Lyon, where he married pianist and mathematician Francine Faure on 3 December 1940. He joined the Resistance in Paris, becoming editor-in-chief of the outlawed *Combat*. Using pseudonyms and false papers, he wrote daily editorials and *Letters to a German Friend*, framing resistance as a moral necessity against the "unreasonable silence of the world."

### The Cycles of Work: Absurdism and Revolt
Camus structured his oeuvre in three cycles, each comprising a novel, essay, and play. The **cycle of the absurd** (1942–44)—*The Stranger*, *The Myth of Sisyphus*, *Caligula*—posits that life lacks inherent meaning because human reason confronts an irrational universe. Rejecting suicide as a surrender of freedom, Camus argues we must live *with* the absurd, embracing lucidity and revolt. The **cycle of revolt** (1947–51)—*The Plague*, *The Rebel*, *The Just Assassins*—moves from metaphysical rebellion to political ethics. In *The Rebel*, he distinguishes **rebellion** (a "no" to oppression that affirms a shared human dignity, summarized as "I revolt, therefore we exist") from **revolution** (which historically sacrifices present lives for a hypothetical future, inevitably becoming tyranny). He uses Prometheus as a symbol of revolutionary humanism, insisting rebellion must impose moral limits on itself. The unfinished **cycle of love** centered on Nemesis, goddess of measured retribution.

### The Sartre Split and Cold War Politics
*The Rebel* (1951) triggered a definitive break with Sartre and the Marxist Left. Camus attacked "historical determinism"—the Marxist view that history justifies present terror—as a betrayal of human freedom. He advocated **libertarian socialism** and **anarcho-syndicalism**, writing for anarchist journals (*Le Libertaire*, *Solidaridad Obrera*) and founding the Groupes de Liaison Internationale to fuse surrealist creativity with revolutionary unionism. He condemned the Soviet invasion of Hungary (1956) and Franco’s Spain, while rejecting capital punishment globally (collaborating with Arthur Koestler on *Reflections on the Guillotine*). A sponsor of the Peoples' World Convention (1950–51), he pursued world federalism as the only path to peace.

### The Algerian Dilemma
Camus’s most fraught legacy concerns Algeria. He advocated a "new Mediterranean Culture" embracing multi-ethnicity against the fascist-leaning *Latinité* of other *pieds-noirs*. He supported the Blum-Viollette proposal for Arab citizenship (1936) and reported on the Sétif massacre (1945), demanding French reforms. When war erupted in 1954, he faced a moral collision: he identified with the *pied-noir* community but condemned FLN terrorism targeting civilians (famously stating, "If that is justice, then I prefer my mother"). He advocated a civilian truce and secretly aided condemned Algerian prisoners, but was distrusted by all sides. Critics like Edward Said later charged his fiction with colonial erasure; Camus confessed the conflict "affected him as others feel pain in their lungs."

### Personal Life
After the war, Camus lived in Paris with Francine Faure, who gave birth to twins, Catherine and Jean, in 1945. Camus had numerous affairs, particularly an irregular and eventually public affair with the Spanish-born actress María Casares, with whom he had extensive correspondence. Faure did not take this affair lightly; she had a mental breakdown and needed hospitalisation in the early 1950s. Camus, who felt guilty, withdrew from public life and was slightly depressed for some time.

### Final Years and Death
The 1957 Nobel Prize surprised Camus, who felt his work incomplete. He used the prize money to stage Dostoyevsky’s *The Possessed* (1959), a critical success. He worked on *The First Man*, an autobiographical novel exploring his "moral learning" in Algeria. On January 4, 1960, he died instantly in a Facel Vega crash driven by his publisher Michel Gallimard; the manuscript was recovered from the wreckage. A 2011 KGB assassination theory remains unsubstantiated. Sartre eulogized his "stubborn humanism"; Faulkner noted he had written "that which every artist... is hoping to do: I was here."

## Terms
- ****Absurdism**** — The philosophy that human existence is fundamentally meaningless because the search for reason clashes with an irrational, silent universe; Camus's response is lucid acceptance and revolt, not suicide or nihilism.
- ****Pied-noir**** — A person of French or European descent born in Algeria during French rule (1830–1962); Camus’s identity as a poor *pied-noir* shaped his dual perspective on colonial privilege and oppression.
- ****Indigénat**** — The arbitrary legal code in French Algeria denying Arab and Berber natives the rights of French citizens, creating the systemic inequality Camus opposed.
- ****Anarcho-syndicalism**** — A branch of anarchism viewing revolutionary labor unions (syndicates) as the vehicle for worker self-management and the abolition of the state/capitalism; Camus’s primary political affiliation.
- ****Metaphysical Rebellion**** — In *The Rebel*, the primordial human protest against the condition of existence (suffering, death, meaninglessness), which affirms a common human dignity ("I revolt, therefore we exist").
- ****Historical Rebellion**** — The attempt to translate metaphysical rebellion into political action; Camus warns it risks becoming tyranny when it claims "absolute truth" (History/Reason) to justify violence.
- ****Cycle of the Absurd**** — Camus’s first thematic trilogy (1942–44): *The Stranger* (novel), *The Myth of Sisyphus* (essay), *Caligula* (play), diagnosing the human condition.
- ****Cycle of Revolt**** — Camus’s second trilogy (1947–51): *The Plague*, *The Rebel*, *The Just Assassins*, exploring ethical limits of resistance.
- ****Latinité**** — A pro-fascist, antisemitic ideology among *pieds-noirs* asserting Latin/Mediterranean racial superiority; Camus countered it with a pluralist "Mediterranean Culture."
- ****The First Man**** — Camus’s unfinished autobiographical novel (published 1994), found in the car crash wreckage, re-examining his Algerian childhood and colonial complicity.

## Debates and open questions
- **Existentialist or Absurdist?** Camus rejected the "existentialist" label (associated with Sartre’s "existence precedes essence"), rooting his thought in Greek philosophy and Nietzsche. Scholars debate whether his focus on freedom, suicide, and absurdity places him inside or outside the tradition.
- **Colonial Complicity vs. Anti-Colonialism:** Critics (e.g., Edward Said) argue Camus’s fiction centers European experience, erasing Arab subjectivity. Defenders cite his early journalism exposing colonial crimes and his unique advocacy for a multi-ethnic Algeria. The 1994 publication of *The First Man* intensified this debate.
- **The "Prefer My Mother" Quote:** His 1957 Stockholm remark rejecting FLN terrorism is cited either as a defense of innocent life against revolutionary abstraction or as a reactionary prioritization of colonial kinship over justice.
- **Cause of Death:** The 2011 claim by Giovanni Catelli that the KGB engineered the 1960 car crash lacks documentary evidence and is rejected by most biographers.
- **Political Legacy:** Neoliberals (e.g., Nicolas Sarkozy) have attempted to claim Camus for anti-communist liberalism, angering the Left and his family, who emphasize his anarcho-syndicalism and anti-capital punishment stance.
