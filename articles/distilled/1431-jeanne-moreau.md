# Jeanne Moreau

## Overview
Jeanne Moreau (1928–2017) was a French actress, singer, screenwriter, and director whose career spanned seven decades, bridging classical theatre and the French New Wave. She established herself as a leading figure at the Comédie-Française before achieving international film stardom in the late 1950s through collaborations with directors like Louis Malle and François Truffaut. Her magnetic screen presence defined key New Wave works such as *Jules et Jim* (1962), while her versatility sustained a prolific career into the 2010s, earning major awards at Cannes, BAFTA, and the Césars. Orson Welles famously deemed her "the greatest actress in the world."

## Body

### Early Life and Theatrical Foundations
Moreau was born in Paris to Anatole-Désiré Moreau, a French restaurateur, and Katherine Buckley, an English dancer of part-Irish descent from Oldham, Lancashire. The family moved to Vichy during her childhood, spending summers at the ancestral village of Mazirat in Allier. During World War II, the family split; Moreau remained in Paris with her mother. At 16, a performance of Jean Anouilh’s *Antigone* inspired her to act. She studied at the Conservatoire de Paris, where her parents separated permanently; her mother subsequently returned to England with Moreau’s sister. In 1947, Moreau debuted at the Avignon Festival and quickly joined the Comédie-Française, becoming one of its leading actresses by her 20s. She performed in classics by Turgenev, Shaw (*Pygmalion*), Cocteau (*La Machine Infernale*), and Anna Bonacci (*The Dazzling Hour*), alongside stars like Gérard Philipe.

### Film Stardom and the French New Wave
After minor film roles starting in 1949, Moreau’s screen career accelerated in the late 1950s with the emerging New Wave generation. Louis Malle cast her in *Elevator to the Gallows* (1958) and *The Lovers* (1959), establishing her as a modern screen icon. She became a muse for major auteurs: François Truffaut centered *Jules et Jim* (1962) on her magnetic performance; Michelangelo Antonioni directed her in *La Notte* (1961); Luis Buñuel in *Diary of a Chambermaid* (1964); and Orson Welles in *The Trial* (1962), *Chimes at Midnight* (1965), and *The Immortal Story* (1968). Her 1960s filmography—including *La baie des anges* (1963), *Le journal d'une femme de chambre* (1964), and *Mademoiselle* (1966)—cemented her status. She won the Cannes Best Actress award for *Seven Days... Seven Nights* (1960) and the BAFTA for *Viva Maria!* (1965).

### Later Career, Directing, and Music
From the 1970s onward, Moreau expanded into writing, directing, and producing. Her directorial debut, *Lumière* (1976), was followed by *L'Adolescente* (1979) and the TV documentary *Lillian Gish* (1983). She continued acting for directors across generations, including Rainer Werner Fassbinder (*Querelle*, 1982), Wim Wenders (*Until the End of the World*, 1991), and Manoel de Oliveira (*Gebo et l'Ombre*, 2014). She won a late-career César for Best Actress in *The Old Lady Who Walked in the Sea* (1992). A vocalist since the 1960s, she released albums and performed with Frank Sinatra at Carnegie Hall in 1984. Her behind-the-camera work was profiled in the documentary *Calling the Shots* (1988).

### Personal Life and Public Engagement
Moreau married Jean-Louis Richard (1949–1964), with whom she had a son, Jérôme, and later American director William Friedkin (1977–1979). She had significant relationships with directors Louis Malle, François Truffaut, and Tony Richardson (who left Vanessa Redgrave for her in 1967), as well as designer Pierre Cardin and actor Theodoros Roubanis. She maintained close friendships with writers Jean Cocteau, Jean Genet, Henry Miller, and Marguerite Duras. In 1971, she signed the Manifesto of the 343, publicly declaring she had obtained an illegal abortion. In 2009, she signed a petition supporting Roman Polanski during his detention on 1977 sexual abuse charges, arguing it threatened film festival traditions. Shortly before her death in 2017, she expressed feeling "abandoned" by her inability to act.

## Terms
- **Comédie-Française**: France's premier state theatre troupe, founded in 1680; Moreau became a leading member in her 20s.
- **French New Wave (Nouvelle Vague)**: Late 1950s/60s film movement characterized by experimental style and auteur theory; Moreau was a central actress.
- **Auteur**: A filmmaker whose personal influence and artistic control over a movie are so great that they are regarded as the author; Moreau worked with many (Truffaut, Malle, Welles, Antonioni).
- **Cannes Film Festival**: Major international film festival; Moreau won Best Actress (1960) and an honorary Golden Palm (2003).
- **César Award**: France's national film award; Moreau won Best Actress (1992) and an honorary award (2008).
- **BAFTA Fellowship**: Lifetime achievement award from the British Academy; awarded to Moreau in 1996.
- **Manifesto of the 343**: 1971 petition signed by 343 French women admitting to illegal abortions to demand legalization.
- **Conservatoire de Paris**: Premier French performing arts college where Moreau trained.

## Debates and open questions
- **Polanski Petition (2009)**: Moreau’s support for Roman Polanski during his detention sparked controversy regarding the separation of artistic merit from personal conduct and the role of film festivals as "neutral" spaces.
- **Legacy Assessment**: While Welles called her "the greatest actress in the world," scholarly debate continues on the relative weight of her theatrical versus cinematic contributions and her agency as a director/writer compared to her iconic status as a muse.
