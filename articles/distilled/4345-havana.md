# Havana

## Overview
Havana is the capital and largest city of Cuba, serving as the nation's primary port, commercial center, and seat of government. Founded by the Spanish in 1519, its strategic harbor made it a critical hub for colonial trade and a heavily fortified city. Today, it is a major tourist destination known for its historic architecture, vibrant culture, and complex political and economic history, having transitioned from a Spanish colony to a U.S.-influenced resort city to the capital of a socialist state.

## Timeline

- **1592** — Granted the title of City by King Philip II of Spain.
- **1762** — Captured by the British during the Seven Years' War, then returned to Spain in 1763.
- **1837** — First railroad in Cuba (and Latin America) opens, connecting Havana to Bejucal.

- **1982** — Old Havana is declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2019** — A rare and destructive EF4 tornado strikes the eastern side of the city.

## Body

### History and Colonial Significance
Havana was founded in 1519 by Diego Velázquez de Cuéllar, though earlier settlements failed. Its deep-water harbor made it the essential stopover for Spanish galleons returning to Europe, leading to its designation as "Key to the New World." This strategic importance made it a frequent target for pirates and rival powers, resulting in extensive fortification. The British captured the city in 1762 during the Seven Years' War, briefly opening it to free trade before it was returned to Spain, which then made it the most heavily fortified city in the Americas. The city remained the colonial capital until the Spanish–American War in 1898.

### Post-Independence and Revolution
After independence in 1902, Havana flourished as a glamorous resort city, known as the "Paris of the Antilles," attracting U.S. tourists and investment. However, economic disparities, corruption, and political repression grew. The 1959 Revolution led by Fidel Castro overthrew the Batista government. The new regime nationalized industry and commerce, and the subsequent U.S. embargo and alignment with the Soviet Union transformed the city's economy. The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 caused a severe economic crisis, known as the "Special Period," from which the city has partially recovered through a renewed focus on tourism.

### Geography and Climate
Havana lies on Cuba's northern coast along the Straits of Florida. The city is built around a bay entered through a narrow inlet, which divides into three main harbors. The Almendares River flows through the city. It has a tropical savanna climate (Aw) with average temperatures ranging from 22°C (72°F) in winter to 28°C (82°F) in summer. Rainfall is heaviest from June to October, and the city is occasionally affected by hurricanes, though less often than southern Cuba.

### Administration and Demographics
Havana functions as both a city and a province, administered by a city-provincial council. The population was 2,142,939 in 2021 but had dropped to 1,749,964 by the end of 2024 due to migration. The city is ethnically diverse, with a 2012 census reporting 58.4% white, 26.4% mixed race, and 15.2% black. Roman Catholicism is the largest religion. The government controls internal migration to manage strain on urban infrastructure.

### Economy and Tourism
Havana's economy is diversified but state-dominated. It remains Cuba's primary port, handling 50% of national imports and exports. Key industries include manufacturing, pharmaceuticals, and tobacco (famous Habanos cigars). Tourism is now the primary economic driver, with over a million international visitors annually. The city is also a center for health tourism. Despite recent reforms allowing small private businesses, salaries remain low, and the economy faces ongoing challenges.

### Architecture and Culture
Contemporary Havana is described as three cities: the historic Old Havana (a UNESCO site), the commercial and nightlife hub of Vedado, and newer suburban districts. The architecture reflects its history, from Spanish colonial forts and Baroque churches to Neoclassical palaces, Art Deco landmarks like the Bacardi Building, and mid-20th-century modernist structures. The city is a cultural capital, home to the National Ballet of Cuba, the Havana Film Festival, and numerous museums and theaters.

## Terms
- **Captaincy General:** The Spanish administrative division governing Cuba from 1607 to 1898.
- **Casa de Contratación:** The Spanish crown-controlled trading house in Seville that held a monopoly on trade with the New World.
- **Contrabando:** Smuggling, which flourished in Havana due to strict Spanish trade restrictions.
- **Solares:** Large inner-city mansions or old hotels subdivided into rooms, often housing many families, considered a form of precarious housing.
- **Special Period:** The severe economic crisis in Cuba following the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991.
- **People's Councils (Consejos Populares):** Local administrative bodies in Havana responsible for neighborhoods and wards.
- **Betacism:** A linguistic phenomenon where the sounds for 'b' and 'v' merge, leading to the English spelling "Havana" from Spanish "Habana."

## Debates and open questions
- The origin of the name "Havana" is debated, with theories linking it to the Taíno chief Habaguanex or other local terms.
- The economic and social impact of the post-Soviet "Special Period" and the ongoing effects of the U.S. embargo on Havana's development remain subjects of analysis.
- The effectiveness of government policies to control population growth in Havana and manage internal migration is a persistent issue.
