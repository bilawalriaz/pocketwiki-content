# Ballet

## Overview
Ballet is a highly technical form of performance dance that originated in the Italian Renaissance courts of the 15th century. It developed into a concert dance form in France and Russia, establishing foundational techniques used in many other dance genres worldwide. Its evolution reflects broader artistic and cultural shifts, from courtly entertainment to a professionalized art with distinct national styles and modern innovations.

## Timeline
- **15th–16th c.** — Ballet originates in Italian Renaissance courts.
- **1618** — Introduction of the proscenium arch stages performers from the audience.
- **1661** — King Louis XIV founds the Académie Royale de Danse in France.
- **1672** — Louis XIV makes Jean-Baptiste Lully director of the Académie Royale de Musique, leading to the Paris Opera Ballet.
- **1681** — The first professional "ballerinas" perform at the Paris Opera.
- **Early–mid 19th c.** — The Romantic ballet era flourishes, emphasizing pointe work and supernatural themes.
- **1827** — *La Sylphide* is considered the first major Romantic ballet.
- **1870** — *Coppélia* is considered the last major Romantic ballet.
- **Early 20th c.** — The Ballets Russes, led by Sergei Diaghilev, revives interest in ballet and sparks the modern era.
- **1928** — George Balanchine's *Apollo* is considered the first neoclassical ballet.
- **1976** — Twyla Tharp's *Push Comes To Shove* innovates contemporary ballet.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
Ballet began as elaborate court entertainments in Renaissance Italy, where noble amateurs performed in ornate costumes that restricted movement. Under Catherine de' Medici, it spread to France. The adoption of the proscenium arch in 1618 shifted the focus from spectacle to technical skill, as the audience could now better view the dancers' feats. French ballet reached its peak under King Louis XIV, who established formal training institutions and the first professional company, the Paris Opera Ballet. The partnership of Jean-Baptiste Lully and Pierre Beauchamp codified the five major positions of the feet.

### 19th Century: Romantic and Classical Styles
After declining in France post-1830, ballet continued developing in Denmark, Italy, and Russia. The Romantic era (early to mid-19th century) was marked by the emergence of pointe work, the dominance of female dancers, and themes of intense emotion and supernatural beings. *La Sylphide* (1827) and *Giselle* are iconic works. This period laid the groundwork for classical ballet, which is based on traditional technique and vocabulary and includes national variations like Russian and French styles.

### 20th Century and Modern Eras
The Ballets Russes (early 20th century) revived ballet and initiated its modern era. The 20th century saw ballet influence other dance forms and split into new movements. Neoclassical ballet, pioneered by George Balanchine (e.g., *Apollo*, 1928), is often abstract and uses diverse music. Contemporary ballet, also influenced by modern dance, may be performed barefoot and incorporates floor work but still requires classical technique. Choreographers like Twyla Tharp and companies worldwide continue to evolve the form.

### Styles and Technical Methods
Major ballet styles include classical, romantic, neoclassical, and contemporary. Six internationally recognized teaching methods exist: the French School (the basis for all training), the Vaganova Method (Russian, emphasizing strength and harmony), the Cecchetti Method (Italian, anatomy-focused), the Bournonville Method (Danish, known for lightness), the Royal Academy of Dance method (English, slow progression), and the Balanchine Method (American, known for speed and unorthodox positions).

### Costumes, Career, and Health
Ballet costumes evolved from restrictive Renaissance court wear to lighter, more expressive designs, with the Romantic tutu and later the classical tutu becoming iconic. As a career, professional ballet is competitive and not highly paid, with dancers averaging about US$19 per hour as of 2020. Intensive training carries health risks, including stress fractures, ankle impingement, lower bone density in the arms, and a higher risk of eating disorders compared to the general population.

## Terms
- **Pointe work**: Dancing on the tips of the toes in specially reinforced shoes, a technique that emerged in the Romantic era.
- **Proscenium arch**: A stage design that frames the performance area, separating dancers from the audience.
- **Épaulement**: A stylistic use of the shoulders, head, and neck to create a three-dimensional effect in movement.
- **Port de bras**: The carriage and movement of the arms in ballet.
- **Ballon**: The illusion of lightness and effortless bouncing in jumps.
- **Plié**: A bending of the knees, a fundamental movement in ballet.
- **Neoclassical ballet**: A style that strips away elaborate plots and costumes to focus on abstract movement, often set to diverse music.
- **Vaganova Method**: A Russian training system that fuses French, Italian, and Russian styles, emphasizing strength and expressive harmony.
