# Equator

## Overview
The equator is the imaginary circle of latitude at 0° that divides Earth into the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. It is a fundamental reference line for geography, astronomy, and climate, defining the point of maximum rotational speed and influencing global weather patterns.

## Timeline
- **c. 48–12 million years ago** — Geological evidence shows the equator's position shifted significantly due to changes in Earth's axis.
- **1961** — Satellite measurements revealed the equatorial diameter is 300 meters greater than its diameter 90 degrees away.
- **1979** — The International Union of Geodesy and Geophysics (IUGG) adopted the GRS 80 ellipsoid with a defined equatorial radius.
- **1984** — The World Geodetic System 1984 (WGS 84) standard for GPS and cartography was established, using the same equatorial radius.
- **2009** — The International Astronomical Union (IAU) codified its current standard equatorial radius value.

## Body

### Definition and Geometry
The equator is the parallel of latitude defined as 0°. It is the only line of latitude that is also a great circle, meaning its plane passes through Earth's center. In three-dimensional geometry, it is the intersection of a rotating spheroid with the plane perpendicular to its axis of rotation and midway between its poles. The term can apply to any roughly spherical celestial body.

### Physical Characteristics and Measurement
Earth is not a perfect sphere; it bulges at the equator. Its average diameter is 12,742 km, but the equatorial diameter is about 43 km greater than the polar diameter. The precise length of the equator is calculated from standardized radii. The IAU 2009 equatorial radius is 6,378.1366 km, yielding a circumference of 40,075.0142 km. The WGS 84 and GRS 80 systems use a radius of 6,378.137 km, resulting in a circumference of 40,075.0167 km. The true equatorial plane is not fixed; it drifts about 9 meters annually due to shifts in Earth's rotation axis.

### Climate and Seasons
Near the equator, the Sun appears almost directly overhead year-round, resulting in stable, high temperatures and minimal seasonal variation. The terms "summer" and "winter" are not typically used. Maximum solar radiation occurs during the equinoxes (around March 20 and September 23), when the subsolar point crosses the equator. Day and night are nearly equal in length (about 12 hours each) throughout the year. Lowland equatorial regions generally have a tropical rainforest climate with high annual rainfall (2,500–3,500 mm) and temperatures averaging 31°C in the afternoon. Despite the heat, glaciers exist on high equatorial mountains like the Andes and Kilimanjaro.

### Geography and Human Use
The equator passes through 11 sovereign nations and the territorial seas of three others. Indonesia straddles the greatest length of the line. The equator's high rotational speed (460 m/sec) makes locations near it, such as the Guiana Space Centre, ideal for space launches, as rockets gain extra velocity from Earth's spin. A maritime tradition of "line-crossing ceremonies" marks a sailor's first passage over the equator.

## Terms
- **Great Circle:** A circle on a sphere whose plane passes through the sphere's center, representing the shortest distance between two points on the surface.
- **Celestial Equator:** The projection of Earth's equator onto the celestial sphere, used as a reference in astronomy.
- **Subsolar Point:** The point on Earth's surface where the Sun is directly overhead at noon.
- **Geoid:** The irregular, sea-level surface of Earth, shaped by gravity and rotation.
- **Ellipsoid:** A mathematical model of Earth's shape, flattened at the poles and bulging at the equator.
- **Atmospheric Refraction:** The bending of light as it passes through Earth's atmosphere, which makes the Sun visible before it rises and after it sets.

## Debates and Open Questions
The precise location of the equator is not fixed. Its plane drifts annually, and geological evidence indicates significant positional shifts over millions of years due to changes in Earth's axis, linked to tectonic events like the collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates. The exact length is also complex to determine due to Earth's irregular geoid shape.
