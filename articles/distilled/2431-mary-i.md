# Mary I

## Overview
Mary I was the first undisputed queen regnant of England, ruling from 1553 to 1558. Her reign is defined by her vigorous, and ultimately unsuccessful, attempt to reverse the English Reformation and restore Roman Catholicism, a policy that led to the execution of over 280 Protestant dissenters and earned her the posthumous nickname "Bloody Mary." Her marriage to the future King Philip II of Spain was deeply unpopular and entangled England in continental conflicts, culminating in the loss of Calais.

## Timeline
- **18 February 1516** — Born at Greenwich, the only surviving child of Henry VIII and Catherine of Aragon.
- **1533** — Declared illegitimate after her parents' marriage is annulled; removed from the line of succession.
- **1543** — Restored to the line of succession (after Edward) by the Third Succession Act.
- **6 July 1553** — Her half-brother, Edward VI, dies; she is excluded from his will in favour of Lady Jane Grey.
- **19 July 1553** — Deposes Lady Jane Grey and is proclaimed queen.
- **25 July 1554** — Marries Philip of Spain at Winchester Cathedral.
- **January 1555** — Heresy Acts revived by Parliament, leading to the Marian persecutions.
- **January 1556** — Philip becomes King of Spain; Mary is queen consort.
- **June 1557** — England declares war on France.
- **January 1558** — French forces capture Calais, England's last continental possession.
- **17 November 1558** — Mary dies at St James's Palace, succeeded by her half-sister Elizabeth.

## Body

### Early Life and Succession Crisis
Mary was a precocious child, educated in Latin, French, and music. Her status was shattered when her father, Henry VIII, annulled his marriage to her mother, Catherine of Aragon, in 1533. Mary was declared illegitimate, stripped of her title, and forced to acknowledge her father as Supreme Head of the Church of England. She was reconciled with Henry in 1543 and restored to the succession, but after his death, her Protestant half-brother Edward VI attempted to bypass her entirely. Upon Edward's death in 1553, Mary rallied support in East Anglia, deposed the nine-day queen Lady Jane Grey, and took the throne.

### Reign and the Spanish Marriage
As queen, Mary moved quickly to restore Catholicism. Her first Parliament declared her parents' marriage valid and abolished Edward's Protestant religious laws. A major political and religious shift came with her marriage to Philip of Spain in 1554. The union was deeply unpopular in England, sparking Wyatt's Rebellion, which Mary crushed. The marriage treaty carefully limited Philip's power in England, but the alliance drew the country into Spain's wars with France, resulting in the humiliating loss of Calais in 1558.

### Religious Policy and Persecutions
Mary's primary goal was to reunite England with the Roman Catholic Church. The Heresy Acts were revived in 1555, leading to the Marian persecutions, in which more than 280 Protestant dissenters, including prominent bishops like Thomas Cranmer, were burned at the stake. These executions, intended to enforce orthodoxy, instead created Protestant martyrs and generated lasting anti-Catholic sentiment. Mary also experienced a false pregnancy in 1554-55, which dashed hopes for a Catholic heir and secured the Protestant Elizabeth's position as successor.

### Final Years and Legacy
Mary's reign was plagued by poor harvests, economic difficulties, and her own declining health. She died childless in 1558, and her religious settlement was swiftly reversed by Elizabeth I. Historically, Mary has been condemned as a bloody tyrant, a view shaped by Protestant writers like John Foxe. Modern scholarship offers a more nuanced view, acknowledging her determination and the challenges she faced, but her reign remains defined by its religious intolerance and political failures.

## Terms
- **Queen Regnant**: A female monarch who reigns in her own right, as opposed to a queen consort who is the wife of a king.
- **English Reformation**: The 16th-century process by which the Church of England broke away from the authority of the Pope and the Roman Catholic Church.
- **Marian Persecutions**: The execution of over 280 Protestant dissenters by burning at the stake during Mary I's reign.
- **Heresy Acts**: Laws revived under Mary I that made it a capital offense to profess Protestant beliefs.
- **Jure uxoris**: A legal doctrine where a husband holds a title or property by right of his wife.
- **False Pregnancy**: A condition where a woman exhibits signs of pregnancy without actually being pregnant, often due to psychological factors.
- **Calais**: England's last remaining territory in mainland France, lost to the French in 1558.
- **Counter-Reformation**: The Catholic Church's response to the Protestant Reformation, aiming to reaffirm its doctrines and reform internal abuses.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Motivations for Persecution**: Was Mary's policy of burning heretics a sincere, if extreme, attempt to save souls and secure religious uniformity, or was it primarily a political tool to crush dissent?
- **Historical Reputation**: To what extent has Mary's negative legacy been shaped by Protestant propaganda and misogynistic biases in contemporary and later sources?
- **Effectiveness of Her Reign**: Did Mary's policies fail because they were fundamentally flawed, or because her short reign and external disasters (like poor harvests and the loss of Calais) prevented their success?
