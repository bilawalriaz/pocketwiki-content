# Nikita Khrushchev

## Overview
Nikita Khrushchev was First Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (1953–1964) and Chairman of the Council of Ministers (1958–1964). He denounced Stalin's cult of personality, initiated de-Stalinization, and presided over the tensest Cold War years, including the Cuban Missile Crisis. His tenure featured significant domestic reform and international confrontation, ending with his ouster.

## Timeline
- **15 April 1894** — Born in Kalinovka, Kursk Governorate, Russian Empire.
- **1918** — Joins the Bolshevik Party during the Russian Civil War.
- **1938** — Appointed First Secretary of the Communist Party of Ukraine.
- **5 March 1953** — Death of Joseph Stalin; power struggle ensues.
- **25 February 1956** — Delivers the "Secret Speech" denouncing Stalin at the 20th Party Congress.
- **1957** — Defeats the "Anti-Party Group" plot to oust him.
- **1958** — Becomes Chairman of the Council of Ministers, consolidating top leadership.
- **13 August 1961** — The Berlin Wall is constructed.
- **October 1962** — The Cuban Missile Crisis brings the world to the brink of nuclear war.
- **14 October 1964** — Ousted from power by Leonid Brezhnev and other Politburo members.
- **11 September 1971** — Dies in Moscow.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Born to poor peasants, Khrushchev worked as a metal fitter and herdsboy before becoming a Bolshevik political commissar in the Russian Civil War. He rose through party ranks under Lazar Kaganovich, overseeing Moscow Metro construction. During Stalin's Great Purge (1934–1938), Khrushchev actively supported repression, approving thousands of arrests in Moscow and Ukraine. In World War II, he served as a political commissar on several fronts, including Stalingrad, acting as an intermediary between Stalin and generals. After the war, he rebuilt Ukraine before being recalled to Moscow as one of Stalin's close advisers.

### Consolidation of Power and De-Stalinization
Following Stalin's death in 1953, Khrushchev outmaneuvered rivals like Lavrentiy Beria and Georgy Malenkov to become First Secretary. His defining act was the "Secret Speech" of February 1956, denouncing Stalin's purges and cult of personality, ushering in the "Khrushchev Thaw." This included releasing political prisoners and allowing modest artistic freedom. He survived a 1957 coup attempt by the "Anti-Party Group" (Molotov, Malenkov, Kaganovich) and later dismissed Marshal Zhukov, fully consolidating authority by 1958.

### Domestic Policies
Khrushchev's domestic agenda focused on improving living standards and reforming agriculture, often with mixed results. He launched the Virgin Lands Campaign to cultivate vast areas in Siberia and Kazakhstan and promoted corn cultivation, inspired by American farming. Policies included abolishing Machine-Tractor Stations and setting unrealistic production goals, contributing to agricultural failures and food shortages, notably after a 1963 drought. He initiated a massive housing program, constructing prefabricated "khrushchyovka" apartment blocks. Reforms also included restructuring the party into industrial and agricultural wings and launching an anti-religious campaign that closed thousands of churches.

### Foreign Policy and the Cold War
Khrushchev pursued "peaceful coexistence" while aggressively promoting Soviet prestige. He leveraged the 1957 launch of Sputnik to boast of missile superiority, creating a perceived "missile gap." His tenure saw major crises: the suppression of the 1956 Hungarian Revolution, the construction of the Berlin Wall in 1961, and the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis, resolved by withdrawing missiles from Cuba in exchange for a U.S. non-invasion pledge and a secret agreement on U.S. missiles in Turkey. Relations with China deteriorated sharply over de-Stalinization and ideological differences, leading to the Sino-Soviet split.

### Ouster and Legacy
By 1964, Khrushchev's erratic policy-making, agricultural failures, and the humiliation of the Cuban Missile Crisis had eroded his support. In October 1964, Leonid Brezhnev and other Politburo members forced his retirement, citing "advanced age and ill health." In retirement, he dictated memoirs smuggled to the West. His legacy is contradictory: he was both a Stalinist enforcer and the leader who dismantled Stalin's terror, a reformer who improved Soviet life and an autocrat who crushed dissent. His era provided inspiration and caution for later reformers like Mikhail Gorbachev.

## Terms
- **De-Stalinization**: The process of denouncing Stalin's cult of personality and reversing his most repressive policies, initiated by Khrushchev.
- **First Secretary**: The de facto leader of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union, a position Khrushchev held from 1953 to 1964.
- **Great Purge**: Stalin's campaign of political repression (1934–1938) involving mass arrests, executions, and imprisonment in the Gulag.
- **Virgin Lands Campaign**: Khrushchev's initiative to cultivate previously uncultivated land in Siberia and Kazakhstan to boost grain production.
- **Khrushchyovka**: A type of low-cost, prefabricated concrete apartment building constructed in large numbers under Khrushchev's housing program.
- **Secret Speech**: Khrushchev's 1956 address to the 20th Party Congress denouncing Stalin's crimes, delivered in a closed session.
- **Cuban Missile Crisis**: The 1962 confrontation between the U.S. and USSR over Soviet ballistic missiles deployed in Cuba, considered the closest the Cold War came to nuclear war.
- **Anti-Party Group**: A faction of senior Soviet leaders (Molotov, Malenkov, Kaganovich) who attempted to oust Khrushchev in 1957 but were defeated and expelled.
- **Nomenklatura**: The elite class of government and party officials in the Soviet Union whose careers were controlled by the party.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Responsibility for the Great Purge**: Historians debate Khrushchev's personal initiative versus compliance with Stalin's orders during the purges, where he approved thousands of arrests.
- **Motives for the Secret Speech**: Scholars question whether his denunciation was driven by genuine moral conviction, a desire to consolidate power, or a strategic need to rehabilitate the party's legitimacy.
- **Impact of Agricultural Policies**: The long-term effects of his agricultural reforms, like the Virgin Lands Campaign and corn promotion, are debated—some see them as well-intentioned but flawed experiments, others as reckless disruptions.
- **Role in the Cuban Missile Crisis**: Analysis continues on whether his decision to place missiles in Cuba was a desperate gamble to redress the strategic balance or a defensive move to protect a communist ally.
- **Reasons for His Ouster**: While official reasons cited age and health, historians point to policy failures, erratic leadership, and the resentment of the party elite he had alienated with his reforms.
