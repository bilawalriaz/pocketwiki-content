# Anna Akhmatova

## Overview
Anna Akhmatova (1889–1966), born Anna Gorenko, was a seminal Russian and Soviet poet whose career spanned the Silver Age of Russian poetry through the Stalinist terror and the post-Stalin thaw. A leading figure of the Acmeist movement, she rejected Symbolist mysticism for concrete imagery, emotional restraint, and formal discipline. Her early lyric fame gave way to decades of official censorship, the execution of her first husband Nikolai Gumilev, and the repeated imprisonment of her son Lev Gumilev and common-law husband Nikolai Punin. Choosing internal exile over emigration, she became a witness to Soviet repression, composing her masterpiece *Requiem* (1935–40) in secret. Though banned from publication for long periods, she survived to achieve late recognition, mentoring a new generation including Joseph Brodsky, and receiving international honours before her death.

- **1921** — Gumilev executed by Cheka for alleged monarchist conspiracy; Acmeist group effectively destroyed.
- **1925** — Work unofficially banned by party resolution; enters "Vegetarian Years" of suppressed publication.
- **1935–40** — Composes *Requiem* in secret, documenting Stalinist terror.
- **1939** — *From Six Books* briefly published then pulped; flat bugged, surveillance intensified (900-page file).
- **1941–44** — Witnesses Siege of Leningrad; evacuated to Tashkent; works on *Poem Without a Hero*.
- **1946** — Expelled from Union of Soviet Writers; Zhdanov denounces her as "half harlot, half nun."

- **1951** — Readmitted to Union of Writers following Stalin's death (1953).
- **1962** — Visited by Robert Frost; mentors Joseph Brodsky.
- **1964–65** — Receives Etna-Taormina prize (Sicily) and honorary doctorate from Oxford University.
- **5 March 1966** — Dies of heart failure in Moscow, age 76; buried at Komarovo Cemetery, Saint Petersburg.

## Body

### Early Life and the Acmeist Breakthrough
Born into a minor noble family with Tatar ancestry (adopting her grandmother’s surname as a pen name), Akhmatova moved to Tsarskoye Selo near Saint Petersburg as an infant. She began writing at 11, influenced by Pushkin, Baratynsky, and the Symbolists. In 1903 she met Nikolai Gumilev, marrying him in 1910 despite family opposition. That year she joined the Guild of Poets, co-founding **Acmeism**—a movement rejecting Symbolist vagueness for "craft," concrete themes, and formal rigor, parallel to Imagism in the West. Her debut *Evening* (1912, 500 copies) sold out instantly; *The Rosary* (1914) made her a cultural phenomenon, inspiring mass imitation. Critics dubbed her "Queen of the Neva" and "Soul of the Silver Age." Her early lyrics capture volatile emotional states—meeting, love, hatred, indifference—through fragmented, psychologically charged everyday images rather than logical narrative.

### Revolution, Repression, and the "Vegetarian Years"
Akhmatova stayed in Petrograd through the 1917 Revolution, Civil War, and famine, refusing emigration. She divorced Gumilev in 1918 and married Assyriologist Vladimir Shilejko, a union she later described as a punitive "cleansing." In 1921, Gumilev was executed by the Cheka in a reprisal for the Kronstadt rebellion, destroying the Acmeist circle and stigmatizing Akhmatova and her son Lev. Soviet criticism dismissed her work as "bourgeois aesthetic" and trivial "female" preoccupations. A 1925 party resolution unofficially banned her poetry; she survived through translation (Hugo, Tagore, Leopardi), Pushkin scholarship, and criticism. Friends fled, died, or were arrested: Mandelstam perished in the Gulag; Punin, her common-law husband from the 1920s, died there in 1953. Lev was repeatedly imprisoned from the 1930s onward. Akhmatova queued for hours at prisons, an experience distilled in *Requiem*’s famous encounter with a whispering woman: "Can you describe this? / And I said: 'I can.'"

### *Requiem*, War, and the Zhdanovshchina
Between 1935–40 Akhmatova composed *Requiem* orally, memorizing and burning drafts to evade surveillance (her flat was bugged; a 900-page NKVD file accumulated). The cycle—ten poems framing the Terror through biblical motifs (Mary at the Cross, Mary Magdalene)—rejects her earlier romantic persona for the public role of chronicler. During WWII she endured the Leningrad siege, was evacuated to Tashkent (falling ill with typhus), and began *Poem Without a Hero* (1940–65), a dense, allusive meditation on her epoch. Returning to a ruined Leningrad in 1944, she read to soldiers; some patriotic poems appeared in *Pravda*. In 1946, Stalin ordered a campaign against her and satirist Mikhail Zoshchenko. Andrei Zhdanov expelled her from the Writers’ Union, branding her "half harlot, half nun" and her work "eroticism, mysticism, and political indifference." Her 1945 visit from philosopher Isaiah Berlin was cited as evidence of ideological deviation.

### Late Recognition and Dual Legacy
To secure Lev’s release (he was re-arrested in 1949), Akhmatova published overtly propagandist verses ("In Praise of Peace")—works she never acknowledged in her canon. Lev was freed in 1956; she was readmitted to the Writers’ Union in 1951 and fully rehabilitated after Stalin’s death. Major collections appeared: *Poems* (1958), *Poems 1909–1960* (1961), *The Flight of Time* (1965)—though *Requiem* remained unpublished in the USSR until 1987. In her final years she mentored young poets (notably Brodsky), lived in Komarovo, and traveled abroad (Sicily, England) for the Taormina Prize and Oxford doctorate (1964–65). She died in 1966; thousands attended memorials in Moscow and Leningrad. Isaiah Berlin observed that her "unyielding passive resistance" made her a figure not only of literature but of Russian history.

### Work and Themes
Akhmatova’s oeuvre divides into early lyric miniatures (1912–25) and the later complex, philosophical cycles (1936–66). Acmeist principles—clarity, simplicity, disciplined form, concrete detail—persist throughout. Early poems (*Evening*, *Rosary*, *White Flock*) use short fragments of simple speech, emotional rather than logical links, charging ordinary objects with psychological weight. Later works confront time, memory, and historical trauma. *Requiem* (1935–40) is a lyrical cycle of lamentation and witness, not narrative, exploring suffering, despair, and devotion through the voices of women waiting at prison gates. *Poem Without a Hero* (1940–65), dedicated to friends who perished in the siege, is a 20-year composition blending personal history, cultural memory, and the 1945 Berlin encounter. Her translations span French, English, Italian, Armenian, and Korean. Essays on Pushkin and Dostoyevsky reveal her scholarly depth.

## Terms
- **Acmeism**: Early 20th-century Russian poetic school (Guild of Poets) rejecting Symbolist mysticism for craft, concrete imagery, and formal discipline; parallel to Imagism.
- **Silver Age**: Period (c. 1890–1917) of extraordinary flourishing in Russian poetry, ended by Revolution and Civil War.
- **Cheka**: Bolshevik secret police (1917–22); executed Gumilev in 1921.
- **Gulag**: Soviet forced-labor camp system; Lev Gumilev and Nikolai Punin imprisoned there.
- **Samizdat**: Clandestine copying and circulation of banned literature; Akhmatova’s late work spread this way.
- **Zhdanovshchina**: 1946–48 cultural purge led by Andrei Zhdanov; Akhmatova and Zoshchenko primary targets.
- **Requiem**: Akhmatova’s secret cycle (1935–40) on Stalinist terror; published in Munich 1963, USSR 1987.
- **Poem Without a Hero**: Long allusive poem (1940–65), considered her masterpiece alongside *Requiem*.
- **Isaiah Berlin**: British philosopher (1909–97); his 1945 visit to Akhmatova triggered state condemnation.
- **Joseph Brodsky**: Poet (1940–96), Akhmatova’s protégé; Nobel 1987, US Poet Laureate 1991.

## Debates and open questions
- **Propagandist poems (1950s)**: Scholars debate whether Akhmatova’s pro-Stalin verses were purely tactical to save her son or reflected genuine political compromise; she excluded them from her canon.
- **Lev Gumilev’s resentment**: Lev believed his mother prioritized poetry over his release; the extent of her influence on his 1956 freedom remains uncertain.
- **Lost works**: *Requiem* was memorized and burnt; *Enûma Elish* (play) and other pieces were destroyed or suppressed. The full extent of lost manuscripts is unknown.
- **Berlin visit significance**: The 1945 meeting’s content is reconstructed from memory; its role in triggering the 1946 purge is documented but the precise political calculus is debated.
- **Canon formation**: *Requiem*’s absence from Soviet editions until 1987 and its status as a dissident symbol complicate its reception history versus her "official" lyric legacy.
