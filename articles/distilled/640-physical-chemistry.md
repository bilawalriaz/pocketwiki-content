# Physical chemistry

## Overview  
Physical chemistry applies physics principles—thermodynamics, quantum mechanics, statistical mechanics, and kinetics—to understand chemical systems at both macroscopic and microscopic levels. It bridges atomic-scale behavior and bulk material properties, enabling prediction of reaction spontaneity, rates, phase behavior, and molecular structure. Unlike chemical physics, it emphasizes supra-molecular (bulk) phenomena such as chemical equilibrium and colloids.

## Timeline  
- **1752** — Mikhail Lomonosov coins "physical chemistry" and lectures at Petersburg University
- **1876** — J. Willard Gibbs publishes *On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances*, introducing Gibbs energy, chemical potentials, and phase rule
- **1887** — *Zeitschrift für Physikalische Chemie* founded by Ostwald and van 't Hoff
- **1896** — *Journal of Physical Chemistry* launched
- **1901–1909** — Ostwald, van 't Hoff, and Arrhenius receive Nobel Prizes in Chemistry
- **1930s** — Quantum mechanics develops into quantum chemistry, led by Linus Pauling
- **1940s–1950s** — Nuclear chemistry advances, including isotope separation during WWII
- **1999** — *Physical Chemistry Chemical Physics* begins (formerly *Faraday Transactions*)

## Body  

### Foundations and Scope  
Physical chemistry seeks to explain chemical behavior using physical laws. Core areas include thermodynamics (heat, work, energy transfer), quantum chemistry (electron behavior and bonding), chemical kinetics (reaction rates), and statistical mechanics (linking microscopic states to macroscopic observables). It addresses intermolecular forces, phase equilibria, electrochemistry, surface science, and colligative properties.

### Key Concepts  
Chemical compounds are modeled as bonded atoms; reactions involve bond breaking and forming. Accurate modeling requires knowledge of nuclear positions and electron distributions. Statistical mechanics shows that large ensembles (≈6×10²³ particles) can be described by few variables (pressure, temperature, concentration), enabling macroscopic predictions from molecular properties.

### Disciplines  
- **Quantum chemistry**: Applies quantum mechanics to determine bond strength, geometry, nuclear motion, and spectral transitions.  
- **Spectroscopy**: Studies electromagnetic radiation–matter interactions.  
- **Chemical thermodynamics**: Sets limits on reaction extent and energy conversion; assesses reactor feasibility.  
- **Chemical kinetics**: Examines reaction rates, transition states, and catalytic effects.  
- **Statistical mechanics**: Derives bulk properties from molecular-level data without empirical correlations.

### Historical Development  
Modern physical chemistry emerged in the 1860s–1880s through work on thermodynamics, electrolytes, and kinetics. Gibbs’ 1876 paper laid theoretical foundations. The field matured with journals like *Zeitschrift für Physikalische Chemie* (1887) and leaders such as Ostwald, van 't Hoff, and Arrhenius. Mid-20th-century advances included quantum chemistry (Pauling), spectroscopy, and nuclear chemistry. Contemporary developments involve computational methods and predictive algorithms for physicochemical properties from molecular structure.

## Terms  
- **Thermodynamics**: Study of energy transfer as heat and work in chemical systems.  
- **Quantum chemistry**: Application of quantum mechanics to chemical bonding and structure.  
- **Statistical mechanics**: Link between microscopic particle behavior and macroscopic system properties.  
- **Chemical kinetics**: Branch studying reaction rates and mechanisms.  
- **Phase rule**: Equation relating number of phases, components, and degrees of freedom in a system.  
- **Transition state**: High-energy intermediate state that reactants must pass through to form products.  
- **Colligative properties**: Solution properties depending on solute concentration, not identity.  
- **Spectroscopy**: Technique analyzing matter via interaction with electromagnetic radiation.  
- **Gibbs energy**: Thermodynamic potential indicating spontaneity of processes at constant temperature and pressure.  
- **Chemical potential**: Energy change when adding a mole of substance to a system.

## Debates and Open Questions  
The distinction between physical chemistry and chemical physics remains debated, particularly regarding molecular vs. supra-molecular focus. The extent to which macroscopic behavior can be fully predicted from molecular properties without empirical input is an ongoing question. Additionally, non-equilibrium and quasi-equilibrium thermodynamics seek to extend classical equilibrium-based theories to real-world dynamic systems.
