# Justice

## Overview
Justice is the principle of treating individuals fairly, classically defined as "the constant and perpetual will to render to each his due" (Institutes of Justinian, 6th century). It functions as a cardinal virtue and the "first virtue of social institutions" (John Rawls), governing the distribution of goods, the punishment of wrongdoing, and the structure of legal systems. Theories of justice address three core questions: what people are owed (distributive justice), how to respond to wrongdoing (retributive, restorative, utilitarian), and what gives justice its authority (divine command, natural law, social contract, or consequentialism). These frameworks often conflict—for example, utilitarianism may justify punishing the innocent to maximize welfare, while retributivism forbids it as a violation of desert.

## Timeline
- **c. 380 BC** — Plato publishes *The Republic*, defining justice as structural harmony in the soul and city; Thrasymachus counters that justice is "the interest of the strong."
- **c. 350 BC** — Aristotle writes *Nicomachean Ethics* and *Politics*, treating justice as both complete virtue and a particular virtue of proportional distribution.
- **6th century** — *Institutes of Justinian* codifies Roman law, defining justice as rendering to each their due.
- **354–430** — Augustine argues that without justice, "kingdoms are nothing but gangs of criminals on a large scale."
- **13th century** — Aquinas synthesizes natural law with Christian theology: reason is a "spark of the divine," grounding equal human dignity and inalienable rights.
- **17th century** — John Locke grounds justice in natural law, justifying property rights and a right to revolution.
- **1762** — Rousseau advances social contract theory: justice arises from mutual agreement to be governed.
- **1861** — John Stuart Mill publishes *Utilitarianism*, arguing justice is derived from welfare consequentialism, not a fundamental principle.
- **1971** — John Rawls publishes *A Theory of Justice*, proposing "justice as fairness" via the veil of ignorance and two principles (equal liberty; difference principle).
- **1974** — Robert Nozick publishes *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, defending historical entitlement theory and calling redistributive taxation theft.
- **2003/2008** — Neuroscience and primate studies (Emory, UCLA) suggest fairness perception is evolutionarily ancient and neurologically basic.

## Body

### Historical Foundations: Greek, Roman, and Confucian Thought
Plato treats justice as **harmony**—the right ordering of parts within the soul (reason, spirit, desire) and the city (rulers, auxiliaries, producers). Only philosophers, who grasp the Form of the Good, are fit to rule. Aristotle equates justice with lawful virtue and distinguishes **distributive justice** (geometric proportion: honors/goods to merit) from **corrective justice** (arithmetic proportion: restoring equality after harm). The *Institutes of Justinian* supply the enduring Roman definition: justice as a steady will to give each their due. Classical Confucian theory diverges sharply: justice is a personal and public virtue promoting social order, not a legal right, and lacks concepts of freedom or equality.

### Divine Command, Natural Law, and the Social Contract
**Divine command theory** makes justice dependent on God’s will. The *Euthyphro* dilemma (Plato) asks whether the good is commanded because it is good, or good because commanded—threatening either divine arbitrariness or moral independence from God. Kant and C. S. Lewis argue objective morality and God’s existence imply each other. Jewish, Christian, and Muslim traditions root justice in God’s nature (Genesis 18:19; Psalms 89:14), often paired with mercy. Buddhist ethics rejects desert-based justice entirely: karma is not reward/punishment but the causal continuation of intentions; justice means reducing suffering through compassion, interdependence (*pratītyasamutpāda*), and non-duality, aiming at collective liberation.

**Natural law** holds that universal moral principles are discoverable by reason. Aquinas grounds equal human dignity in reason as a divine spark. Locke uses natural law to justify property and revolution. This tradition opposes **legal positivism** (law as mere human command) and the divine right of kings.

**Social contract theory** (Rousseau, Rawls) derives justice from mutual agreement—actual or hypothetical—under conditions of equality and no bias. Rawls’s **veil of ignorance** models fairness: parties ignorant of their traits choose principles impartially, yielding (1) maximum equal basic liberties and (2) social/economic inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged (difference principle) and attach to offices open to all under fair equality of opportunity.

### Utilitarianism and Its Critics
**Utilitarian justice** (Mill) subordinates justice to **consequentialism**: right acts maximize total/average welfare. Punishment serves deterrence, rehabilitation, or incapacitation—whichever maximizes welfare. This can justify punishing the innocent or disproportionate penalties if consequences warrant. Mill explains the *feeling* of justice as a compound of retaliatory instinct and sympathy, not a fundamental moral fact.

**Retributive justice** insists the guilty *deserve* proportionate punishment, independent of consequences. It differs from revenge by being impartial and scaled. **Restorative justice** focuses on repairing harm, involving victims and offenders in dialogue; meta-analyses show high victim satisfaction but no recidivism reduction. **Mixed theories** (e.g., von Hirsch) keep desert as a constraint (punish worse crimes more) but allow utilitarian goals within that bound.

### Distributive Justice: Patterns, Entitlements, and Merit
Distributive theories answer three questions: *what* goods (wealth, power, opportunities), *among whom* (humans, sentient beings, nations), and *by what pattern* (equality, merit, need, status, property rights).

- **Rawls (Fairness)**: Two principles prioritize liberty, then permit inequality only if it helps the worst-off. Liberty rights are distributed equally; social/economic goods follow the difference principle.
- **Nozick (Property rights/Entitlement)**: Justice is historical, not patterned. A holding is just if acquired by **just acquisition** (labor on unowned resources) or **just transfer** (voluntary gift/sale). Redistributive taxation violates entitlement and is theft. Voluntary transactions are Pareto efficient, maximizing total wealth without coercion.
- **Meritocracy**: Goods match talent and effort.
- **Needs-based (Marxism)**: "From each according to ability, to each according to need."
- **Contribution-based**: Rewards proportional to social contribution (Homans).

Neuroscience (UCLA 2008) and primate research (Emory 2003) indicate fairness perception is hardwired and evolutionarily conserved.

### Ideals and Standards: Equality, Proportionality, and Relationality
**Legal egalitarianism** ("all equal before the law") is criticized by Anatole France (1894): formal equality ignores structural disadvantage (the law "forbids rich and poor alike to sleep under bridges"). **Equity** bridges law and justice, allowing context-sensitive application (e.g., community policing). **Proportionality**—rooted in Plato—requires rewards/punishments to match merit/gravity, now central to criminal law and human rights.

**Social justice** concerns the just relationship between individuals and society (privileges, opportunities, wealth, mobility). It differs from **cosmopolitanism** (global community) and **egalitarianism** (equal status/value); Hayek calls it meaningless, seeing justice as emergent from individual action and markets. **Relational justice** (Iris Marion Young, Young Kim) critiques distributive models for ignoring power relations, group differences, and institutional structures. Young emphasizes unjust power; Kim stresses individual moral agency within a liberalism of liberty, equality, diversity, and tolerance.

**Speed**: "Justice delayed is justice denied"; some jurisdictions guarantee speedy trial.

### Criminal Justice and Sentencing
In criminal law, a **sentence** is the judge’s final act (imprisonment, fine, etc.), guided by statutory ranges and guidelines. Purposes include retribution, deterrence, rehabilitation, incapacitation, denunciation, and restitution. Civil cases yield **verdicts/judgments** (damages, injunctions); punitive damages may add retributive/deterrent functions. **Disgorgement** strips gains even without victim loss.

### Evolutionary and Psychological Perspectives
**Evolutionary ethics** suggests justice intuitions evolved for ancestral small-group life, potentially mismatched to modern societies. **Psychology**: Victims value respectful treatment, information, voice, and fair procedure. Pemberton’s "Big 2" model identifies **agency** (control) and **communion** (social belonging) as core; crime shatters trust in a just world. Restoring justice may require rebuilding agency and communion more than retribution or restoration.

### Institutions
Institutions instantiate justice ideals in a world of disagreement and interconnection. They may approximate justice (courts, legislatures) or embody profound injustice (slavery). **Institutive justice** raises questions of legitimacy, procedure, codification, and interpretation. UN Sustainable Development Goal 16 calls for strong institutions to uphold justice.

## Terms
- ****Distributive justice**** — Theories addressing what goods are distributed, among whom, and by what pattern (equality, merit, need, entitlement).
- ****Retributive justice**** — Punishment justified because the guilty *deserve* it, proportionate to the crime, independent of future consequences.
- ****Restorative justice**** — Approach focusing on repairing harm through victim-offender dialogue, accountability, and meeting victims’ needs.
- ****Utilitarianism**** — Consequentialist theory: the right act maximizes total/average welfare; justice is derivative, not fundamental.
- ****Natural law**** — Universal moral/legal principles inherent in nature, discoverable by reason, grounding rights independent of enacted law.
- ****Divine command theory**** — Morality and justice are constituted by God’s authoritative commands.
- ****Social contract**** — Justice arises from mutual agreement (actual or hypothetical) to be governed under fair conditions.
- ****Veil of ignorance**** — Rawls’s thought experiment: choosers ignorant of their traits select principles impartially, modeling fairness.
- ****Entitlement theory**** — Nozick’s historical principle: a holding is just iff acquired by just acquisition or just transfer; patterned redistribution is theft.
- ****Proportionality**** — Principle that rewards/punishments must correspond appropriately to the merit or gravity of actions.
- ****Relational justice**** — Focuses on power relations, group differences, and institutional structures rather than only distribution of goods.
- ****Legal egalitarianism**** — Belief that all persons are equal before the law; criticized for ignoring disparate impact of neutral laws.

## Debates and open questions
- **Euthyphro dilemma**: Is justice grounded in God’s nature/commands, or does it exist independently, making God subject to it?
- **Fundamentality of justice**: Is justice a basic virtue (Rawls) or derivative from welfare (Mill)?
- **Desert vs. consequences**: Can punishing the innocent ever be justified (utilitarianism) or is it categorically forbidden (retributivism)?
- **Patterned vs. historical distribution**: Should justice assess current distribution against an ideal pattern (Rawls) or only the history of each holding (Nozick)?
- **Scope of equality**: Does justice require equality of outcome, opportunity, or only formal equality before the law?
- **Individual vs. relational**: Can justice be captured by distributing goods to individuals, or must it address structural power and group relations (Young)?
- **Evolutionary mismatch**: Are innate justice intuitions (fairness, retaliation) adapted to ancestral environments and maladaptive today?
- **Restorative efficacy**: Does restorative justice reduce recidivism (meta-analyses say no) or primarily satisfy victims?
- **Institutional legitimacy**: When do institutions instantiate justice vs. merely enforce power (e.g., slavery)?
