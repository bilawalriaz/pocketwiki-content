# Mental disorder

## Overview
A mental disorder is a clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotional regulation, or behavior that causes distress or functional impairment. It reflects a dysfunction in psychological, biological, or developmental processes. Causes are multifactorial—genetic, psychological, and environmental—with no single consistent cause established for most disorders. Diagnosis relies on standardized criteria (DSM-5-TR, ICD-11) applied through clinical interview, as objective biological tests are not yet validated for routine use. Treatment includes psychotherapy, psychiatric medication, lifestyle changes, and community support; involuntary treatment is a minority occurrence governed by mental health legislation. Mental disorders are highly prevalent globally (lifetime prevalence >33%), rank among the leading causes of disability (e.g., major depression is the third leading cause worldwide), and are associated with significant stigma, discrimination, and reduced access to care.

## Timeline
- **Ancient era** — Mesopotamian, Greek, Persian, and Islamic texts describe mental disorders; Greeks develop humorism theory (melancholy = excess black bile).
- **1808** — Term "psychiatry" coined; 19th-century industrialization drives massive asylum expansion in Western countries.
- **1952** — First *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders* (DSM) published by the APA; ICD adds mental disorders section.
- **1960s** — Challenges to psychiatric legitimacy emerge (Szasz, Goffman, Rosenhan study); deinstitutionalization begins in the West.
- **1980** — DSM-III introduces operational criteria; "nervous breakdown" vanishes from formal diagnosis.
- **1994** — DSM-IV defines mental disorder as a psychological syndrome associated with distress, disability, or risk, excluding normal grief.
- **2013** — DSM-5 redefines mental disorder as a syndrome reflecting dysfunction in psychological, biological, or developmental processes.
- **2019** — WHO reports global prevalence: depression (264M), dementia (50M), bipolar (45M), schizophrenia (20M); depression is 3rd leading cause of disability worldwide.
- **2022** — ICD-11 (effective Jan 2022) and DSM-5-TR released; classification codes deliberately converged but differences remain.

## Body

### Definition and Classification
A mental disorder is defined by clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotion regulation, or behavior reflecting underlying dysfunction (DSM-5, ICD-11). The DSM-IV acknowledged the definition "lacks a consistent operational definition that covers all situations," noting definitions may rely on pathology, symptomatology, deviance, or etiology depending on context. Two primary classification systems exist: the WHO’s ICD-11 (Chapter 06: Mental, behavioural or neurodevelopmental disorders) and the APA’s DSM-5-TR (2022). They have converged codes for broad comparability, but significant differences persist. Alternative systems include the Chinese Classification of Mental Disorders and the Psychodynamic Diagnostic Manual. A major scientific debate concerns categorical (distinct disorders) versus dimensional (continua of symptoms) models. High comorbidity in categorical systems supports dimensional approaches, such as the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology, which identifies a general psychopathology factor (p factor), internalizing (mood/anxiety), externalizing (behavioral/substance), and thought disorder dimensions. Critics argue categories medicalize normality and lack neurobiological validation; proponents note clinical utility.

### Major Disorder Categories
*   **Anxiety disorders:** Fear/anxiety interfering with function (specific phobias, generalized anxiety, social anxiety, panic disorder, agoraphobia, PTSD). OCD moved to "Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders" in DSM-5.
*   **Mood disorders:** Major depression (intense sustained sadness), dysthymia (milder prolonged depression), bipolar disorder (mania/hypomania alternating with depression). Debate exists on whether unipolar and bipolar are distinct categories or a spectrum.
*   **Psychotic disorders:** Dysregulation of belief, language, perception (delusions, hallucinations, thought disorder). Includes schizophrenia, delusional disorder, schizoaffective disorder (mixed psychotic/affective), and schizotypy (subthreshold traits).
*   **Personality disorders:** Enduring, inflexible maladaptive patterns of behavior and inner experience deviating from cultural expectations.
*   **Neurodevelopmental disorders:** Onset in developmental period; includes ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability.
*   **Eating disorders:** Disturbed eating behavior (e.g., anorexia, bulimia).
*   **Sleep disorders:** Disruption of sleep patterns (insomnia, narcolepsy, sleep apnea, REM behavior disorder, restless leg syndrome).
*   **Substance use disorders:** Persistent drug/alcohol use despite harm; includes tolerance and withdrawal.
*   **Dissociative disorders:** Severe disturbances of self-identity, memory, awareness (depersonalization-derealization, dissociative identity disorder).
*   **Neurocognitive disorders:** Cognitive decline (delirium, mild/major neurocognitive disorder/dementia).
*   **Other:** Impulse control (kleptomania, pyromania), somatic symptom disorders, factitious disorders, paraphilias, and rare named syndromes (e.g., Capgras, Cotard).

### Course and Disability
Onset typically spans childhood to early adulthood: impulse-control and some anxiety disorders in childhood; other anxiety, substance, and mood disorders in mid-teens; schizophrenia in late adolescence/early twenties. Course varies: some disorders are brief, others chronic or relapsing–remitting. Long-term schizophrenia studies show over half recover symptomatically, ~20–33% recover functionally, and "late" recovery occurs. For bipolar disorder, ~50% achieve symptomatic recovery within six weeks, nearly all within two years, though <50% have a new episode within two years. Disability ranges from minimal to substantial, varying over time and life domains (self-care, relationships, occupation). Psychiatric disabilities rank among the most disabling conditions globally (DALYs). Unipolar depression is the 3rd leading cause of disability worldwide (65.5M years lost). Among youth 10–24, nearly half of all disability is psychiatric. Suicide (often linked to mental disorder) is a leading cause of death under 35; 10–20M non-fatal attempts yearly.

### Risk Factors
The predominant view (2018) is a biopsychosocial interplay of genetic, psychological, and environmental factors.
*   **Genetics:** High heritability (especially autism, schizophrenia); family history links (depression, anxiety, narcissistic PD). No specific diagnostic biomarkers identified yet. Assortative mating: individuals with mental illness 2–3x more likely to partner with someone with mental illness (7x for schizophrenia/ADHD, 10x for autism).
*   **Environment:** Prenatal risks (unwanted pregnancy, substance use, maternal stress, birth complications, malnutrition). Social risks: abuse, neglect, bullying, trauma, socioeconomic inequality, migration, urbanicity. Specific risks: migration/discrimination/childhood trauma for psychosis; parenting factors (rejection, harsh discipline) for anxiety; childhood adversity exacerbates bipolar course.
*   **Drug use:** Cannabis, alcohol, caffeine linked to anxiety; cannabis, cocaine, amphetamines linked to psychosis/schizophrenia; cannabis debated for bipolar, linked to depression. Adolescent use risks brain development.
*   **Chronic disease:** HIV, diabetes, heart disease, stroke, cancer, arthritis increase mental disorder risk.
*   **Personality:** High neuroticism/emotional instability; pessimism for anxiety.
*   **Causal models:** Biopsychosocial model is the primary Western paradigm. Biological psychiatry uses a biomedical model (brain circuits, genetics, development). Diathesis–stress model posits genetic/developmental vulnerabilities exposed by life stress. Debate persists on "medical model" vs. "social model" of disability.

### Diagnosis
Diagnosis is primarily clinical: mental status examination (appearance, behavior, self-report, history, circumstances), sometimes supplemented by psychological testing (questionnaires, algorithms) or physical exam. Neuroimaging (MRI/fMRI) is research-only; a 2018 APA review concluded biomarkers lack the required sensitivity/specificity (≥80%) and large validation studies. Routine practice often uses unstructured interviews with limited evidence-based assessment training; inaccurate diagnosis and high comorbidity are common. HoNOS (Health of the Nation Outcome Scales) is widely used in English services (12 factors, 0–4 score) but criticized for limited subtlety. Critiques (Caplan, Frances, Szasz, Greenberg) highlight subjectivity, lack of biological tests, "medicalization of normality," disease mongering, and diagnostic labels obstructing recovery.

### Prevention and Management
WHO (2004) and EPA (2011) affirm prevention reduces burden; many interventions are cost-saving. Strategies: universal (school/media—limited power), selective (high-risk groups), indicated (early symptoms), parenting support. Management settings: psychiatric hospitals, outpatient clinics, community services; recovery-oriented approach growing.
*   **Lifestyle:** Diet, exercise, smoking cessation.
*   **Psychotherapy:** CBT (modify thought/behavior patterns), DBT, IPT, psychoanalysis, systemic/family therapy, humanistic approaches; eclectic/integrative practice common; therapeutic relationship crucial.
*   **Medication:** Antidepressants (depression, anxiety), anxiolytics/sedatives (anxiety, insomnia), mood stabilizers (bipolar), antipsychotics (psychosis, positive symptoms in schizophrenia), stimulants (ADHD).
*   **Other:** ECT (severe treatment-resistant depression, catatonia), psychosurgery (experimental/rare), psychoeducation, creative therapies, peer support, supported housing/employment, reasonable accommodations (e.g., psychiatric service dogs). Cannabis not recommended (2019). Compulsory treatment (involuntary commitment) used in minority; community vs. inpatient compulsion shows little difference in outcomes except possibly reduced victimization.

### Epidemiology
Lifetime prevalence >33% in most countries; 46% in US. Anxiety disorders most common globally, followed by mood disorders. US 12-month: anxiety 28.8%, mood 20.8%, impulse-control 24.8%, substance 14.6%. Europe: ~25% lifetime, ~10% 12-month (27% in 16-country review). Schizophrenia median lifetime prevalence 0.4% (lower in poorer countries). Personality disorders: ~13% 5-year prevalence (Norway), ~15% (US screen). Youth: ~7% preschool psychiatric diagnosis; ~10% 1–2 year olds with significant emotional/behavioral problems. Women have higher depression rates (73M women/year major depression; suicide 7th leading cause death women 20–59); depressive disorders account for 41.9% of psychiatric disability in women vs 29.3% in men.

### History
*   **Ancient:** Mesopotamia (deities' "hands"), Greece (humorism, terms: melancholy, hysteria, phobia), Persia/Arabia/Islamic world.
*   **Middle Ages/Early Modern Europe:** Divine/diabolical/humoral mix; witch-hunts; Galenic medicine (four humors); Catholic exorcism vs. Protestant prayer; rise of institutions; inconsistent diagnosis/treatment.
*   **18th Century:** Madness viewed as organic; harsh asylum care; moral treatment movement emerges late century.
*   **19th Century:** Massive asylum expansion; numerous classification schemes; "psychiatry" coined (1808); superintendents called "alienists."
*   **20th Century:** Psychoanalysis, Kraepelin classification; "inmates"→"patients," asylums→hospitals. US: mental hygiene movement, clinical psychology/social work professions. WWI: "shell shock." WWII: first DSM (1952), ICD mental health section. Mid-century: ECT, insulin shock, lobotomy, chlorpromazine. 1960s: Anti-psychiatry challenges (Szasz, Goffman, Rosenhan, gay rights activists); deinstitutionalization; consumer/survivor movement; lithium, benzodiazepines (1970s, later limited by dependence). 1990s: SSRIs, new antipsychotics widely prescribed; criteria-based DSM/ICD expansions; recovery approach.
*   **Africa/Nigeria:** Cultural views often spiritual (spells/bewitchment). WHO: <10% mentally ill Nigerians access psychiatrists; estimated 40–60M affected; reliance on traditional healers.

### Society and Culture
*   **Cultural variation:** Definitions of optimal vs. pathological functioning differ; medicalization/pathologization processes vary. DSM/ICD criticized for Euro-American bias (Kleinman: "culture-bound" label only for non-Western disorders). Spitzer argues cultural formulations lack scientific rationale.
*   **Latin America:** Stigma (embarrassment/shame) reduces treatment seeking. US Latin Americans slightly higher prevalence than immigrants. 2015–2018: serious mental illness in young adults rose 60% (4%→6.4%); major depressive episodes 8.4%→11.3%. Suicide rate half of non-Latin White Americans (2018), 2nd leading cause death ages 15–34; rates rose post-2020 (COVID). Familismo and religion serve as therapeutic resources but religion may also stigmatize.
*   **Religion:** Spiritual experiences meet criteria for delusional/psychotic disorders; link to schizophrenia (religious delusions common, religion may trigger).
*   **Movements:** Anti-psychiatry (Cooper 1967, Laing, Szasz, ex-patient "survivors") argues treatments damaging, history dangerous (ECT, lobotomy, over-drugging). Consumer/survivor movement campaigns for empowerment, rights, anti-stigma. Carers rights movement. Global mental health movement prioritizes equity worldwide.
*   **Stigma & Discrimination:** Pervasive (US Surgeon General 1999). Racial/ethnic minorities disproportionately affected. Misconceptions (Taiwan: weakness, lack of ambition). Employment discrimination major barrier (psychiatric disability > physical disability in Australia). Chinese society: mentally ill cannot legally marry. Media: predominantly negative (violence, incompetence); contributes to stigma. Public stereotype: dangerousness, desire for social distance. Professionals hold more negative stereotypes but less acceptance of restrictions.
*   **Violence:** Severe mental illness does not independently predict violence (4% US violence attributable to mental illness; 7.5% crimes by mentally ill directly symptom-related). Mentally ill are 11x more likely victims than perpetrators in inner-city US. Specific diagnoses (conduct disorder, antisocial PD, psychopathy) inherently linked to violence. Mediating factors: youth, male, low SES, substance use. Deinstitutionalization not linked to increased homicide. Violence typically in complex social/family/healthcare contexts.
*   **Laws:** 75% countries have mental health legislation. Involuntary commitment: grounds = imminent danger to self/others or need for treatment; requires independent review, advocacy access, capacity assessment. UN Principles (1991), CRPD (2006) protect rights. WHO reports many laws outdated/rights-violating. "Insanity" is a legal term (insanity defense).

### Mental Health Continuum
WHO: >1/3 people meet criteria for common mental disorders at some point. Keyes’ two-continua model: mental health and mental illness are related but distinct dimensions. Optimal mental health can coexist with mental illness; poor mental health can exist without mental illness.

### Other Animals
Non-human primate psychopathology studied since mid-20th century. Captive chimpanzees show >20 abnormal patterns (stereotypy, self-mutilation, disturbed emotions, learned helplessness) hypothesized as equivalents to human depression, anxiety, eating disorders, PTSD. Personality disorder concepts applied to great apes. Risk of anthropomorphism noted; assessment lacks linguistic evidence. Etiology: adverse rearing (maternal separation, sensory deprivation, isolation), integration stress, lack of space. Remediation: re-socialization (90% success in chimps), behavior therapy, enrichment, rare psychiatric drugs. Animal models induced via genetic/neurological/chemical manipulation; criticized empirically and ethically.

## Terms
- ****Mental disorder**** — A clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotion regulation, or behavior reflecting dysfunction in psychological, biological, or developmental processes, causing distress or impairment.
- ****DSM-5-TR**** — *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders*, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (2022); APA classification system using operational diagnostic criteria.
- ****ICD-11**** — *International Classification of Diseases*, 11th Revision (effective 2022); WHO broad medical classification; Chapter 06 covers mental, behavioural, neurodevelopmental disorders.
- ****Biopsychosocial model**** — Primary Western psychiatric paradigm incorporating biological, psychological, and social factors in etiology and treatment.
- ****Diathesis–stress model**** — Theory that genetic/developmental vulnerabilities (diathesis) interact with life stress to trigger disorder onset.
- ****Comorbidity**** — The co-occurrence of two or more disorders in the same individual; very common in psychiatric diagnosis.
- ****DALYs**** — Disability-adjusted life years; metric estimating years of life lost to premature death or lived with disability.
- ****Involuntary commitment**** — Compulsory admission to a mental health facility based on imminent danger to self/others or grave disability; governed by mental health legislation.
- ****Deinstitutionalization**** — Policy shift (mid-late 20th century) closing large psychiatric hospitals in favor of community-based mental health services.
- ****Anti-psychiatry**** — Movement (coined 1967) arguing psychiatric treatments are more damaging than helpful and diagnoses medicalize normality.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Categorical vs. dimensional classification:** Whether mental disorders are distinct categories or points on continuous dimensions (e.g., p factor, internalizing/externalizing spectra) remains unresolved; DSM/ICD remain categorical but acknowledge dimensional aspects.
*   **Validity of diagnostic constructs:** Critics argue categories lack neurobiological validation and medicalize normality; defenders cite clinical utility and reliability improvements in DSM-5/ICD-11.
*   **Biomarkers for diagnosis:** Despite research, no neuroimaging or genetic biomarkers meet APA criteria (sensitivity/specificity ≥80%, replication) for routine clinical diagnosis.
*   **Cultural universality vs. relativity:** Whether Western diagnostic categories (DSM/ICD) are universally valid or culturally biased; debate over "culture-bound syndromes" and the pathologization of non-Western experiences.
*   **Medical model vs. social model of disability:** Whether psychiatric disability is primarily an internal biological dysfunction requiring treatment or a mismatch between individual variation and social structures requiring accommodation.
*   **Involuntary treatment ethics:** Balancing personal liberty/autonomy against harm prevention and right to healthcare; expanding outpatient commitment laws raise rights concerns.
*   **Stigma reduction efficacy:** Methods and outcomes of anti-stigma campaigns are sometimes criticized; stigma remains a major barrier to care and social inclusion.
*   **Violence prediction:** The specific symptoms (e.g., psychosis subtypes) that confer violence risk independent of socio-demographic/substance factors are debated.
*   **Animal model validity:** Translational value of induced animal models for human mental disorders is contested on empirical and ethical grounds.
