# Cecil Rhodes

## Overview
Cecil John Rhodes (1853–1902) was a British mining magnate and politician who became a dominant figure in southern African imperial expansion. He founded the De Beers diamond monopoly, served as Prime Minister of the Cape Colony, and used his British South Africa Company to colonize the territories that became Rhodesia (now Zimbabwe and Zambia). His legacy is deeply contested, as he was both a key architect of British imperial policy and a figure whose actions and views are now central to debates about colonialism, racism, and historical memory.

## Timeline
- **5 July 1853** — Born in Bishop's Stortford, Hertfordshire, England.
- **1870** — Sent to South Africa for health reasons; enters the diamond trade in Kimberley.
- **1881** — Enters the Cape Parliament.
- **1888** — Founds De Beers Consolidated Mines with funding from Rothschild & Co.
- **1889** — Obtains a charter for the British South Africa Company (BSAC).
- **1890** — Becomes Prime Minister of the Cape Colony.
- **1895** — The BSAC territory is officially named "Rhodesia."
- **1896** — Forced to resign as Prime Minister after the failed Jameson Raid.
- **1902** — Dies of heart failure in Muizenberg, South Africa.

## Body

### Business and Diamond Monopoly
Rhodes arrived in South Africa in 1870 and entered the diamond trade at Kimberley. With funding from Rothschild & Co, he systematically bought out and consolidated diamond mines. In 1888, he and his partner Charles Rudd launched De Beers Consolidated Mines. By 1890, through a strategic partnership with the London Diamond Syndicate, Rhodes had secured a near-complete monopoly of the world's diamond supply. He later invested in fruit farming, helping to establish the modern Cape fruit industry.

### Political Career in the Cape Colony
Rhodes entered the Cape Parliament in 1881 and became Prime Minister in 1890. His government enacted laws that disenfranchised black Africans. The Franchise and Ballot Act (1892) raised the property requirement for voting from £25 to £75, disproportionately affecting black voters. The Glen Grey Act (1894) limited the amount of land black Africans could legally hold, further restricting their political rights. Rhodes was forced to resign in 1896 after supporting the disastrous Jameson Raid, an unauthorized attack on the Boer-controlled Transvaal.

### Imperial Expansion and Rhodesia
Rhodes used his wealth and the BSAC to expand British influence northward. He obtained mining concessions from indigenous leaders, such as the controversial Rudd Concession from Lobengula, King of the Ndebele. The BSAC administered territories north of the Limpopo River, which were collectively named "Rhodesia" in 1895. The company's rule led to conflict, including the Second Matabele War (1896), which Rhodes helped suppress. He envisioned a "Cape to Cairo Railway" to unify British territories in Africa.

### Political Views and Legacy
Rhodes was a fervent imperialist who believed in the supremacy of the "Anglo-Saxon race" and sought to expand the British Empire globally. He argued that imperialism was necessary to avoid civil war in Britain by providing new lands for settlement and markets. While he stipulated in his will that his Rhodes Scholarships should be open to all races, his policies in South Africa were explicitly designed to limit black political and economic power. His legacy is a subject of intense debate, with critics labeling him a white supremacist and "architect of apartheid," while some historians argue his views were more nuanced and typical of his era.

## Terms
- **De Beers**: The diamond mining company founded by Rhodes in 1888, which established a global monopoly.
- **British South Africa Company (BSAC)**: A chartered company, similar to the East India Company, that administered territories in southern and central Africa under Rhodes's control.
- **Jameson Raid (1895)**: A failed raid against the Boer republic of the Transvaal, supported by Rhodes, which led to his resignation as Prime Minister.
- **Cape Qualified Franchise**: The system in the Cape Colony that allowed voting based on property qualifications, which Rhodes's laws made harder for black Africans to meet.
- **Rudd Concession (1888)**: A mining concession obtained from Lobengula, which gave BSAC mining rights in Matabeleland and was later used to justify colonization.
- **Rhodes Scholarship**: An international postgraduate scholarship established by Rhodes's will for study at the University of Oxford.
- **Imperial Factor**: Rhodes's strategy of collaborating with the British government to secure legal and military backing for his private colonial ventures.

## Debates and open questions
- **Rhodes's Racism**: Scholars debate whether Rhodes was a biological racist or a "cultural racist" who believed in a hierarchy of civilization. His policies disenfranchised black Africans, but he also supported their right to vote under certain conditions and included non-white races in his scholarship's eligibility.
- **The Rhodes Must Fall Movement**: The campaign to remove Rhodes statues, particularly at the University of Cape Town and Oriel College, Oxford, has sparked global debate about how to handle symbols of colonialism and whether they can be separated from the institutions that benefit from them.
- **Historical Context vs. Modern Judgment**: Historians like Peter Godwin argue Rhodes should be judged as "a man of his time," while critics contend his actions and ideology have lasting harmful consequences that require condemnation.
