# Horatio Nelson, 1st Viscount Nelson

## Overview
Horatio Nelson was a British Royal Navy officer whose leadership and unconventional tactics secured multiple decisive victories during the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars. He is widely regarded as one of Britain's greatest naval heroes, with his death at the Battle of Trafalgar cementing his legendary status. His career exemplifies the age of sail warfare, marked by personal courage, strategic innovation, and complex political entanglements.

## Timeline
- **29 Sep 1758** — Born in Burnham Thorpe, Norfolk.
- **1 Jan 1771** — Begins naval career on HMS *Raisonnable* under his uncle Maurice Suckling.
- **11 Mar 1787** — Marries Frances "Fanny" Nisbet on Nevis.
- **1 Feb 1793** — France declares war; Nelson returns to service, commanding HMS *Agamemnon*.
- **14 Feb 1797** — Distinguishes himself at the Battle of Cape St Vincent, capturing two Spanish ships.
- **24 Jul 1797** — Loses his right arm at the Battle of Santa Cruz de Tenerife.
- **1 Aug 1798** — Wins a decisive victory over the French at the Battle of the Nile.
- **2 Apr 1801** — Commands the British fleet at the Battle of Copenhagen, famously "turning a blind eye" to a signal.
- **21 Oct 1805** — Killed by a French marksman during the British victory at the Battle of Trafalgar.
- **9 Jan 1806** — Given a state funeral and interred in St Paul's Cathedral.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Nelson joined the Royal Navy in 1771 through the influence of his uncle, a high-ranking officer. He gained early experience in the East and West Indies, participating in his first battle in 1775. After the American War of Independence, he faced periods of unemployment. The outbreak of the French Revolutionary Wars in 1793 allowed him to return to service, where he was active in the Mediterranean. He played a key role in the capture of Corsica, where he was wounded and partially lost sight in his right eye.

### Rise to Fame and Personal Loss
Nelson's reputation grew at the Battle of Cape St Vincent (1797), where he disobeyed orders to capture two Spanish ships. Shortly after, he lost his right arm at the failed attack on Santa Cruz de Tenerife. In 1798, he achieved a major strategic victory at the Battle of the Nile, destroying Napoleon's fleet and stranding his army in Egypt. This victory was followed by a controversial period supporting the Neapolitan monarchy, where his actions, including the execution of Admiral Caracciolo, remain debated. His affair with Emma Hamilton, wife of the British ambassador, began during this time.

### Baltic Campaign and Final Victory
In 1801, Nelson commanded the British fleet at the Battle of Copenhagen, securing a victory that helped dissolve the League of Armed Neutrality. After a period of leave in England, he was appointed commander-in-chief of the Mediterranean Fleet in 1803. He spent two years blockading the French fleet at Toulon. In 1805, he chased the Franco-Spanish fleet across the Atlantic and back. He devised a plan to attack in two columns, breaking the enemy line. At the Battle of Trafalgar on 21 October 1805, his fleet won a decisive victory, but Nelson was fatally wounded. His signal, "England expects that every man will do his duty," became famous.

### Legacy and Controversy
Nelson's death secured his status as a national hero. Monuments like Nelson's Column in Trafalgar Square commemorate him. However, his legacy is complex. His affair with Emma Hamilton and his actions in Naples were controversial. He also held pro-slavery views, influenced by his time in the Caribbean, and opposed the abolitionist movement. Despite this, he is celebrated for his naval genius and leadership, which was based on inspiring loyalty and courage.

## Terms
- **Post-captain**: A commissioned officer in the Royal Navy holding the rank of captain, eligible to command a ship of the line.
- **Line of battle**: The standard tactical formation where warships sailed in a single file to deliver broadsides.
- **Broadside**: An attack where a ship fires all its cannons on one side at an enemy vessel.
- **Blockade**: A naval operation to prevent ships or supplies from reaching or leaving an enemy port.
- **Signal code**: A system of flags used to communicate orders between ships in a fleet.
- **Prize money**: Financial reward awarded to a crew for capturing an enemy ship.
- **Commodore**: A naval officer commanding a squadron of ships, ranking above a captain but below a rear admiral.
- **Phantom limb**: The sensation that an amputated limb is still present.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Naples Campaign**: Nelson's role in the reoccupation of Naples and the subsequent reprisals against Jacobins is debated. Some view his actions, including the execution of Caracciolo, as a breach of honour, while others argue he was restoring order and acting under the authority of the Neapolitan monarchy.
- **Slavery**: Nelson's opposition to the abolitionist movement, influenced by his friendships with Caribbean slaveholders, is a controversial aspect of his legacy, contrasting with his heroic public image.
- **Tactical Innovation**: While celebrated for his unorthodox tactics at Trafalgar, some historians debate the extent to which his "pell-mell" battle plan was a radical departure from existing naval doctrine.
