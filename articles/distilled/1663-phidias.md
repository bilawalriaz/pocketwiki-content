# Phidias

## Overview
Phidias (c. 480 – c. 430 BC) was an Athenian sculptor, painter, and architect widely regarded by ancient critics and modern historians as the greatest exponent of Classical Greek sculpture. He defined the era’s "high style" through colossal chryselephantine (gold and ivory) cult statues—most famously the *Athena Parthenos* in the Parthenon and the *Statue of Zeus at Olympia*, the latter counted among the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. As artistic director of the Periclean building program on the Acropolis, he oversaw the sculptural decoration of the Parthenon, though the extent of his personal hand versus his workshop’s remains debated. His career ended in political scandal: accused of embezzling gold from the *Athena Parthenos* and impiety for portraying himself and Pericles on the statue’s shield, he died in prison (per Plutarch) or was executed by the Eleans (per Philochorus).

## Timeline
- **c. 480 BC** — Birth in Athens, son of Charmides.
- **c. 450–440 BC** — Creates bronze *Athena Lemnia* for Athenians on Lemnos; praised by Pausanias as his best work.
- **c. 447 BC** — Pericles commissions Phidias to create sculptures celebrating the victory at Marathon (490 BC); uses Delian League funds.
- **c. 447–438 BC** — Oversees Parthenon sculptural program; creates chryselephantine *Athena Parthenos* (dedicated c. 438 BC) and designs bronze *Athena Promachos* on Acropolis.
- **c. 438–432 BC** — Accused of embezzling gold from *Athena Parthenos*; acquitted by weighing the gold robe, then charged with impiety for self-portrait and Pericles portrait on shield.
- **c. 432 BC** — Completes chryselephantine *Statue of Zeus* at Olympia.
- **c. 430 BC** — Death: Plutarch says he died in prison; Philochorus says he was executed by Eleans after finishing Zeus.
- **1954–1958** — Excavation of Phidias’s workshop at Olympia reveals tools, terracotta molds, and a cup inscribed "I belong to Phidias" (authenticity disputed).
- **1878 onward** — Excavation of probable workshop on south slope of Acropolis (associated with *Athena Promachos*).
- **1910** — Mathematician Mark Barr adopts Greek letter φ (phi) for the golden ratio in Phidias’s honor; later doubts Phidias used it.

## Body

### Training and early works
Ancient sources name Hegias and Ageladas as Phidias’s masters. His earliest known commissions were Marathon victory dedications: a hero group centered on Miltiades at Athens, and a bronze group at Delphi featuring Apollo, Athena, Attic heroes, and Miltiades the Younger. At Pellene and Plataea he made further statues of Athena, and for Elis an ivory-and-gold Aphrodite. Plato (*Hippias Major*) notes Phidias rarely worked in marble, specializing instead in bronze and chryselephantine technique.

### The Periclean program and the Acropolis
In 447 BC Pericles launched a massive rebuilding of the Acropolis funded by the Delian League treasury, celebrating the Persian War victories. Phidias was appointed general superintendent (*episkopos*) of all artistic works. His two signature Acropolis statues were the *Athena Promachos* ("Athena who fights in the front line"), a colossal bronze visible from the sea, and the *Athena Parthenos*, a 12-meter chryselephantine cult image housed in the Parthenon. Inscriptions show the Parthenon’s pedimental marble blocks did not arrive until 433–434 BC, suggesting much of the architectural sculpture (metopes, frieze, pediments) was executed by Phidias’s workshop—pupils included Alcamenes and Agoracritus—rather than by his own hand.

### The *Athena Parthenos* and political downfall
The *Athena Parthenos* (c. 438 BC) stood roughly 12 m tall; its gold robe was removable and weighed 40–44 talents. Phidias’s enemies—political opponents of Pericles—accused him of stealing gold. He disproved the charge by weighing the robe, but was immediately indicted for *asebeia* (impiety) because the statue’s shield bore portraits of Phidias and Pericles among the Amazonomachy figures. Plutarch records he was imprisoned and died in jail; Philochorus (via a scholiast on Aristophanes) claims he fled to Elis and was executed there after completing the Zeus. Aristophanes’s *Peace* (421 BC) alludes obscurely to "an unfortunate incident involving Phidias."

### The *Statue of Zeus at Olympia*
Phidias’s other Seven Wonder, the seated *Zeus* (c. 432 BC), filled the cella of the Temple of Zeus at Olympia. The god’s flesh was ivory, his robe gold; the throne was entirely sculpted. Pausanias describes the head as "somewhat archaic in type." Late 5th-century BC Elean coins show small copies, confirming the pose. The Otricoli bust, once thought a copy, is stylistically a century later.

### Workshop archaeology
Excavations at Olympia (1954–58) uncovered the workshop where the Zeus was built, exactly where Pausanias located it. Finds include ivory-working tools, terracotta molds for drapery, and a black-glaze cup inscribed *Pheidíou eimí* ("I belong to Phidias")—though the inscription’s authenticity is disputed. A second workshop on the Acropolis south slope (excavated 1878, 1963–64, 2001+) is associated with the *Athena Promachos*. These sites allow reconstruction of chryselephantine construction: a wooden core, ivory plates for flesh, gold sheets for drapery, assembled on-site.

### Ancient critical reception
Ancient critics uniformly ranked Phidias supreme. Demetrius called his works "sublime and precise." Quintilian and Plutarch praised their *ethos*—permanent moral grandeur—contrasting them with the later "pathetic" school’s emotional excess. Pausanias (1.28.2) judged the *Athena Lemnia* "the best of all Pheidias’s works to see." Adolf Furtwängler (early 20th c.) identified a possible Roman copy: a head in Bologna and a torso in Dresden.

### Legacy and modern reception
No original work by Phidias survives; knowledge depends on Roman copies, literary descriptions, and workshop archaeology. In 1910 Mark Barr proposed φ for the golden ratio in Phidias’s honor, but later retracted the claim that Phidias consciously used it. His name designates a crater on Mercury and asteroid 4753 Phidias.

## Terms
- **Chryselephantine**: Sculpture technique using gold (*chrysos*) and ivory (*elephantos*) over a wooden core for flesh and drapery respectively.
- **Athena Parthenos**: "Athena the Virgin"; Phidias’s 12 m cult statue in the Parthenon, gold and ivory, dedicated c. 438 BC.
- **Athena Promachos**: "Athena who fights in the front line"; colossal bronze statue on the Acropolis, visible from sea.
- **Statue of Zeus at Olympia**: Seated chryselephantine cult image (c. 432 BC), one of the Seven Wonders.
- **Delian League**: Maritime alliance led by Athens; its treasury funded the Periclean building program.
- **Asebeia**: Impiety; capital charge in Athens, here brought for portraiture on a sacred shield.
- **Ethos**: In ancient art criticism, the permanent moral character or grandeur of a work, contrasted with *pathos* (emotional display).
- **Workshop practice**: Collaborative production under a master; Phidias’s pupils (Alcamenes, Agoracritus) likely executed much Parthenon architectural sculpture.
- **Roman copies**: Marble replicas of lost Greek bronzes; primary visual evidence for Phidias’s lost originals.
- **Golden ratio (φ)**: Mathematical proportion (1:1.618…); associated with Phidias by Mark Barr (1910) but not attested in antiquity.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authorship of Parthenon sculpture**: Did Phidias design the metopes, frieze, and pediments, or merely supervise? Inscriptions date marble arrival to 433–434 BC, after the *Athena Parthenos* dedication, complicating attribution.
- **Cause and place of death**: Plutarch (died in Athenian prison) vs. Philochorus (executed by Eleans at Olympia). Aristophanes’s vague reference prevents resolution.
- **Extent of chryselephantine innovation**: Was Phidias the inventor or the perfector of the technique? Earlier examples exist (e.g., Delphi), but his scale and fame set the standard.
- **Golden ratio use**: No ancient source links Phidias to φ; Mark Barr’s 1910 naming was honorific, not historical.
- **Workshop cup inscription**: The Olympia cup reading *Pheidíou eimí* is disputed—genuine ownership mark or later forgery?
- **Appearance of the *Athena Lemnia***: Furtwängler’s Bologna/Dresden reconstruction is influential but unproven; other 5th-c. Athena torsos exist.
