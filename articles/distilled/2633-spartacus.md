# Spartacus

## Overview
Spartacus was a Thracian gladiator who led a major slave uprising against the Roman Republic from 73 to 71 BC, known as the Third Servile War. His rebellion, which swelled to an army of tens of thousands, posed a significant challenge to Roman authority and has since become an enduring symbol of resistance against oppression.

## Timeline
- **c. 103 BC** — Spartacus is estimated to have been born in Thrace.
- **73 BC** — Spartacus and about 70 gladiators escape a school near Capua, beginning the revolt.
- **73 BC** — The rebels defeat initial Roman militia and establish a camp on Mount Vesuvius.
- **Winter 73–72 BC** — The rebel army trains, expands, and raids towns in southern Italy.
- **72 BC** — The Roman Senate dispatches consular legions, which are initially successful but then defeated by Spartacus.
- **72 BC** — Marcus Licinius Crassus is given command of eight legions to suppress the revolt.
- **Early 71 BC** — Spartacus's forces are pushed south by Crassus's legions.
- **71 BC** — Spartacus is betrayed by Cilician pirates after a deal for passage to Sicily.
- **71 BC** — Spartacus's army is besieged near Rhegium.
- **71 BC** — Spartacus breaks the siege and is defeated in a final battle; he is presumed killed.
- **71 BC** — 6,000 captured rebels are crucified along the Appian Way.

## Body

### Sources and Early Life
All historical accounts of Spartacus, primarily from Plutarch and Appian, were written over a century after his death and are not eyewitness reports. He is described as a Thracian, possibly from the Maedi tribe, who had served as a soldier with the Romans before being enslaved and trained as a gladiator.

### The Revolt and Military Success
In 73 BC, Spartacus and about 70 gladiators escaped a school near Capua. Despite their small start, they defeated Roman forces, recruited thousands of slaves and others, and grew into an army estimated at 70,000. Spartacus proved to be a capable tactician, using unconventional tactics and local resources to defeat several Roman expeditions.

### The Third Servile War
The Roman Senate eventually tasked Marcus Licinius Crassus with ending the rebellion. Crassus used harsh discipline and numerical superiority to push Spartacus's forces south. A planned escape to Sicily was thwarted by treacherous pirates. After being besieged, Spartacus's army broke out but was decisively defeated in a final battle in 71 BC. Spartacus was presumed killed, and 6,000 survivors were crucified.

### Objectives and Legacy
Spartacus's ultimate goals are debated; sources suggest he may have aimed to escape Italy or possibly march on Rome, but no account claims he sought to abolish slavery. His legacy as a revolutionary icon has inspired later figures and movements, from Toussaint Louverture to Karl Marx, and he remains a prominent figure in popular culture.

## Terms
- **Gladiator**: A enslaved or condemned person trained to fight in public spectacles in ancient Rome.
- **Ludus**: A gladiatorial training school.
- **Murmillo**: A class of heavyweight gladiator that carried a large shield (*scutum*) and a short sword (*gladius*).
- **Third Servile War**: The final and most successful slave uprising against the Roman Republic (73–71 BC).
- **Decimation**: A Roman military punishment where one in ten soldiers in a unit was executed.
- **Cisalpine Gaul**: A region of Roman-controlled territory in the Po Valley, north of the Apennines.
- **Appian Way**: One of the earliest and most important Roman roads, connecting Rome to Brindisi.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Spartacus's Motives**: Historians debate whether his primary goal was to escape Italy, to march on Rome, or to lead a broader social revolt. The sources are divided and contradictory on this point.
- **Leadership Structure**: Roman authors may have imposed a hierarchical view on the rebellion, potentially downplaying the roles of other leaders like the Gauls Crixus and Oenomaus.
- **Scale and Composition**: The exact size and diverse makeup of the rebel army (including Celts, Gauls, and legion veterans) are reconstructed from later sources and are subject to scholarly interpretation.
