# Tourism

## Overview  
Tourism is travel for pleasure or other purposes outside one’s usual environment for up to one year, encompassing both domestic and international movement. It is a major global economic sector, contributing roughly 8% of greenhouse-gas emissions and supporting millions of jobs, yet it faces challenges including environmental degradation, overtourism, and periodic crises such as the 2008 recession, the 2009 H1N1 outbreak, and the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic. The industry has evolved from elite Grand Tours to mass and niche markets, with growing emphasis on sustainability and local community impact.

## Timeline  
- **1772 / 1811**: Words “tourist” and “tourism” first recorded in English.  
- **1936**: League of Nations defines a foreign tourist as someone traveling abroad for at least 24 hours.  
- **1841**: Thomas Cook arranges first publicly advertised chartered excursion train in Britain.  
- **1930s–1940s**: Early academic definitions of tourism emerge (Ogilvie, Hunziker & Kraft).  
- **1960s–1970s**: Mass tourism expands; Mediterranean becomes key destination; socio-cultural impacts debated.  
- **1979**: Space tourism market begins with Russian orbital flights.  
- **1994**: UN classifies tourism into domestic, inbound, and outbound categories.  
- **2008–2009**: Global tourism declines due to Great Recession and H1N1 pandemic.  
- **2012**: International tourist arrivals surpass 1 billion globally for the first time.  
- **2017**: UN designates International Year for Sustainable Tourism for Development.  
- **2020**: COVID-19 pandemic causes 70% drop in international travel; U.S. loses $147B in tourism revenue.  

## Body  

### Definitions and Classifications  
Tourism involves temporary movement of people outside their usual environment for leisure, business, or other purposes lasting up to one year. Domestic tourism occurs within a traveler’s own country; international tourism includes inbound (non-residents entering) and outbound (residents leaving) flows. National tourism combines domestic and outbound; regional combines domestic and inbound. The term “tourist” is sometimes used pejoratively compared to “traveller,” reflecting cultural distinctions studied in the sociology of tourism.

### Types of Tourism  
Outdoor-oriented tourism includes nature, eco-, and adventure tourism (NEAT). Nature tourism covers general outdoor activities like hiking; ecotourism emphasizes education, conservation, and local benefit; adventure tourism involves high-skill, risky pursuits like mountaineering. Other forms include mass tourism (group-based, organized travel), niche tourism (specialized markets), space tourism (limited orbital flights), sports tourism, water/cruise tourism, winter tourism, and cultural/creative tourism. Emerging trends include DNA tourism, sleep tourism, dark tourism, and doom tourism.

### Tourism Products and Services  
A tourism product combines tangible and intangible elements—natural, cultural, and man-made resources—around a destination to create visitor experiences. Services span accommodation (homestays to five-star hotels), hospitality (food/beverage), transport (air, rail, car rental), travel agencies, cultural sites (museums, monuments), shopping, and ancillary services (insurance, parking). Tourism maps show functional zones of cities, guiding spatial planning.

### Historical Development  
Travel for pleasure existed in antiquity (e.g., Roman spa visits, Chinese mountain pilgrimages) but was limited to elites. The Grand Tour (17th–19th centuries) institutionalized educational travel across Europe. Industrialization enabled mass leisure travel, pioneered by Thomas Cook’s rail excursions from 1841. Colonialism intertwined with tourism, promoting “othering” through exoticized depictions of non-European cultures. Post-WWII democratization expanded access, though racial segregation in the U.S. restricted Black travelers until civil rights reforms.

### Economic and Social Impacts  
Tourism contributes significantly to GDP and employment, especially in service sectors. It drives infrastructure development and cultural exchange but can strain local environments and communities. Overtourism in cities like Barcelona has sparked resident protests over housing costs. Economic benefits depend on local integration—pro-poor tourism seeks to channel income to marginalized groups, though studies show limited reach. Events like the 1992 Winter Olympics caused irreversible environmental damage, illustrating tourism’s dual-edged impact.

### Sustainability and Challenges  
Tourism accounts for ~8% of global emissions, prompting shifts toward sustainable practices. UNESCO and UNWTO promote conservation of heritage sites threatened by overtourism (e.g., Lascaux Cave replica). Voluntourism and community-centric models aim to empower locals, though critiques highlight potential exploitation. Medical, educational, and event tourism reflect evolving motivations. Security concerns post-9/11 increased liability demands, while digital platforms enable dynamic packaging and peer-to-peer lodging (e.g., Airbnb). Climate change fuels “last chance tourism” to vanishing landscapes.

## Terms  
- **Inbound tourism**: Non-residents traveling into a country.  
- **Outbound tourism**: Residents traveling to another country.  
- **Ecotourism**: Responsible travel to fragile ecosystems supporting conservation and local communities.  
- **Dark tourism**: Visits to sites associated with death or tragedy.  
- **Overtourism**: Excessive visitor numbers harming destinations and residents.  
- **Pro-poor tourism**: Strategies ensuring tourism benefits low-income populations.  
- **Voluntourism**: Combining volunteering with tourism, often critiqued for power imbalances.  
- **Doom tourism**: Travel to endangered or disappearing locations.  
- **Creative tourism**: Active participation in host culture via workshops and learning.  
- **Sustainable tourism**: Balancing economic gains with environmental and social responsibility.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Scholars debate whether tourism inherently exploits or empowers local communities. Critics argue voluntourism undermines labor markets and imposes Western values, while proponents stress its potential for cultural exchange and development. Pro-poor tourism’s effectiveness remains contested, with research showing less than 25% of tourist spending reaching the poor. The role of technology in democratizing access versus enabling overtourism is under scrutiny. Additionally, the environmental cost of “last chance tourism” raises ethical questions about whether visiting fragile sites accelerates their decline. Security measures post-9/11 and pandemic-era restrictions also prompt ongoing discussion about balancing safety with openness in global mobility.
