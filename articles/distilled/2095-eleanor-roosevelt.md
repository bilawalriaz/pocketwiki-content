# Eleanor Roosevelt

## Overview
Eleanor Roosevelt (1884–1962) was an American political figure, diplomat, and activist who redefined the role of First Lady through her extensive public engagement and advocacy. As the longest-serving First Lady (1933–1945) and a key architect of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, she became one of the 20th century's most influential women, championing civil rights, human rights, and social justice on a global stage.

## Timeline
- **October 11, 1884** — Born in Manhattan, New York City.
- **1899–1902** — Attended Allenswood Academy in London, influenced by headmistress Marie Souvestre.
- **March 17, 1905** — Married Franklin Delano Roosevelt.
- **September 1918** — Discovered Franklin's affair with Lucy Mercer, leading to a marital crisis.
- **August 1921** — Franklin was stricken with a paralytic illness; Eleanor helped persuade him to stay in politics.
- **March 4, 1933** — Became First Lady upon Franklin's inauguration.
- **1933–1938** — Established and advocated for the Arthurdale, West Virginia, resettlement community.
- **December 10, 1948** — The UN General Assembly adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, which she helped draft.
- **November 7, 1962** — Died in New York City at age 78.

## Body

### Early Life and Marriage
Born into wealth and privilege, Roosevelt endured a traumatic childhood marked by the deaths of her parents and a brother. Her time at Allenswood Academy (1899–1902) under Marie Souvestre fostered her independent thinking and confidence. She married her fifth cousin, Franklin Delano Roosevelt, in 1905. Their marriage was complicated by Franklin's 1918 affair with Lucy Mercer; though they remained married, it became more of a political partnership. After Franklin's paralytic illness in 1921, Eleanor became his political stand-in, launching her own public career.

### Redefining the First Lady Role (1933–1945)
As First Lady, Roosevelt was unprecedentedly active and outspoken. She held regular press conferences (barring male reporters), wrote the daily syndicated column "My Day," hosted a weekly radio show, and spoke at a national party convention. She used her platform to advocate for civil rights, women's roles in the workplace, and refugees. Her major projects included the experimental community of Arthurdale, West Virginia (later deemed a failure), and work with the National Youth Administration. She was a vocal supporter of African-American rights, famously resigning from the Daughters of the American Revolution to help arrange Marian Anderson's 1939 concert at the Lincoln Memorial.

### Diplomacy and Human Rights (1945–1962)
After Franklin's death in 1945, Roosevelt became a U.S. delegate to the United Nations. As the first chair of the UN Commission on Human Rights, she was instrumental in drafting and gaining international support for the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), which she called "the international Magna Carta of all men everywhere." She continued her activism, chairing President Kennedy's Presidential Commission on the Status of Women until her death. She remained a globally admired figure, ranked ninth on Gallup's List of Most Widely Admired People of the 20th Century.

## Terms
- **First Lady of the United States:** The hostess of the White House and, traditionally, the wife of the President. Roosevelt transformed the role into one of active political and social advocacy.
- **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR):** A foundational document of international human rights law adopted by the UN General Assembly in 1948, which Roosevelt helped draft and champion.
- **New Deal:** The series of programs, public work projects, and financial reforms enacted by President Franklin D. Roosevelt in response to the Great Depression. Eleanor Roosevelt served as its "eyes and ears," advocating for its benefits to reach all citizens.
- **Arthurdale:** An experimental resettlement community for unemployed miners in West Virginia, established in 1933 with Eleanor Roosevelt's strong support. It was later widely regarded as a failure.
- **"My Day":** A daily syndicated newspaper column written by Eleanor Roosevelt from 1936 until her death in 1962, detailing her activities and views on social issues.
- **Sundown Town:** A town that was racially exclusive, typically forbidding Black people from living within its limits after dark. The New Deal town of Eleanor, West Virginia, was established as such a community.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nature of Relationships:** There is considerable scholarly debate about the nature of Eleanor Roosevelt's close relationships, particularly with journalist Lorena Hickok and bodyguard Earl Miller, with arguments ranging from deep friendship to romantic involvement.
- **Effectiveness of Advocacy:** Historians debate the effectiveness of her behind-the-scenes advocacy for civil rights and Jewish refugees during her husband's presidency, noting the tension between her public stance and the administration's political constraints.
- **Arthurdale's Legacy:** While often labeled a failure, some historians note that residents considered the community a "utopia" compared to their prior circumstances, complicating a simple verdict on the project.
