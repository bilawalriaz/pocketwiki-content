# Year

## Overview  
A year is a unit of time based on the Earth’s orbital period around the Sun, with the tropical year (≈365.2422 days) being the basis for the Gregorian calendar and the sidereal year (≈365.2564 days) measured against fixed stars. Beyond astronomy, “year” applies to lunar cycles, fiscal and academic periods, and even other planets’ orbits. Calendars must intercalate (add) days or months to reconcile astronomical years with integer day counts, leading to systems like the Julian, Gregorian, and lunisolar calendars.

## Timeline  
- **c. 46 BCE** — Julius Caesar introduces the Julian calendar with a 365.25-day year and leap years every four years
- **1582 CE** — Pope Gregory XIII reforms the Julian calendar into the Gregorian, refining leap-year rules to better match the tropical year
- **1923** — Revised Julian calendar proposed, used by some Eastern Orthodox Churches
- **1925** — Modern Solar Hijri (Jalali) calendar adopted in Persia, based on vernal equinox observation
- **2011** — IUPAC–IUGS recommends defining the annus as 31,556,925.445 seconds for geological use

## Body  

### Calendar Years and Leap Systems  
Calendars approximate the Earth’s orbital period using either integer days or intercalated months. The Gregorian calendar aims to ensure that the northward equinox falls on or shortly before March 21 and hence it follows the northward equinox year, or tropical year. Because 97 out of 400 years are leap years, the mean length of the Gregorian calendar year is 365.2425 days, with a relative error below one ppm (9·10−7) relative to the current length of the mean tropical year (365.242189 days) and even closer to the current March equinox year of 365.242374 days that it aims to match. The Julian calendar assumes a simpler 365.25-day year. Lunisolar calendars, like the Hebrew and traditional Chinese systems, add a 13th month periodically to align lunar months with the solar year. The Islamic lunar calendar ignores the solar year entirely, causing its months to drift relative to seasons.

### Astronomical Year Types  
Several precise definitions of the year exist depending on reference points:  
- **Tropical year**: Time for the Sun’s ecliptic longitude to increase by 360°, tied to seasonal cycles (~365.2422 days).  
- **Sidereal year**: Orbital period relative to fixed stars, with an average duration of 365.256363004 days (365 d 6 h 9 min 9.76 s) (at the epoch J2000.0).  
- **Anomalistic year**: Period between perihelion passages, with an average duration of 365.259636 days (365 d 6 h 13 min 52.6 s) (at the epoch J2011.0).  
- **Draconic year**: Sun’s return to the same lunar node (~346.62 days), governing eclipse patterns.  
- **Julian year**: A unit of time defined as 365.25 days, each of exactly 86400 seconds (SI base unit), totaling exactly 31,557,600 seconds.  
- **Besselian year**: A tropical year that starts when the (fictitious) mean Sun reaches an ecliptic longitude of 280°, computed as B = 1900.0 + (Julian dateTT − 2415020.31352) / 365.242198781.  
- **Gaussian year**: The sidereal year for a planet of negligible mass, governed by the Gaussian gravitational constant, with a length of 365.2568983 days.  
- **Heliacal year**: The interval between the heliacal risings of a star.  
- **Sothic year**: The heliacal year of Sirius, very close to the Julian year of 365.25 days.  
- **Full moon cycle**: The time for the Sun to complete one revolution with respect to the perigee of the Moon's orbit, lasting 411.78443029 days.  
These differ due to axial precession, orbital eccentricity, and gravitational perturbations.

### Year Numbering and Eras  
Year numbering assigns sequential numbers from a reference epoch. The Gregorian era (AD/CE) starts from a 6th-century estimate of Jesus’ birth, with no year zero. Other systems include AUC (Rome), AM (Hebrew), and AH (Islamic). Japanese history uses multiple imperial eras. Astronomical year numbering includes a year zero and negative values for BCE dates.

### Pragmatic Divisions  
Non-astronomical uses of “year” include:  
- **Fiscal year**: A 12-month accounting period varying by country (e.g., April 1 in Canada/India, October 1 in the U.S.).  
- **Academic year**: Student attendance cycle, often split into semesters or terms, typically starting in late summer in the Northern Hemisphere.  
- **Seasonal year**: Interval between recurring natural events (e.g., bird migrations, floods), which can vary by over a month annually.

### Symbols and Scientific Usage  
The symbol “a” (from Latin *annus*) is standard in science, supported by NIST, IAU, and IUPAC. Variants include “y” or “yr” in general use. In geology, prefixes like kyr (thousand), Myr (million), and Gyr (billion) denote deep time. The Unified Code for Units of Measure distinguishes tropical (*at*), Julian (*aj*), and Gregorian (*ag*) years. The IUPAC–IUGS proposal defines the annus as 31,556,925.445 seconds to avoid astronomical variability. As of April 2025, the IUPAC Green Book (4th edition) provides a definition of the year as a = 31556925.9747 seconds. The IUPAC–IUGS recommendation proved controversial as it conflicts with an earlier convention among geoscientists to use "a" specifically for absolute age before the present, and "y" or "yr" for time intervals. Use of "mya" and "bya" is deprecated in modern geophysics, the recommended usage being "Ma" and "Ga" for dates Before Present, but "m.y." for the durations of epochs.

## Terms  
- **Tropical year**: Solar year based on seasonal cycles; basis of the Gregorian calendar.  
- **Sidereal year**: Orbital period relative to fixed stars.  
- **Anomalistic year**: Time between Earth’s closest approaches to the Sun (perihelion).  
- **Draconic year**: Period for the Sun to return to the same lunar node; governs eclipses.  
- **Intercalation**: Insertion of extra days or months to align calendars with astronomical cycles.  
- **Leap year**: Year with an extra day added to synchronize the calendar with the solar year.  
- **Epoch**: Reference point from which year numbering begins (e.g., birth of Christ in the Gregorian system).  
- **Vague year**: Fixed 365-day year without leap corrections, used in ancient calendars.  
- **Julian year**: Exactly 365.25 days of 86,400 SI seconds; used in astronomy.  
- **Great Year**: Full cycle of axial precession (~25,700 years).

## Debates and Open Questions  
- **Calendar reform**: Proposals to replace the Gregorian calendar with more astronomically accurate systems remain unadopted.  
- **IUPAC–IUGS definition controversy**: Geoscientists debate whether “a” should denote absolute age or time intervals, conflicting with older conventions using “y” or “yr”.  
- **Long-term orbital changes**: Earth’s slowing rotation and shifting orbit make long-term predictions of year length uncertain.  
- **Use of year zero**: Some argue for including a year zero in civil calendars to simplify calculations, though this is not widely adopted.
