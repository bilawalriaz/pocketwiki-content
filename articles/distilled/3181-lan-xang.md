# Lan Xang

## Overview
Lan Xang was a major Lao kingdom that existed from 1353 to 1707, controlling the territory of modern-day Laos. For over three centuries, it was one of the largest kingdoms in Southeast Asia and forms the historical and cultural foundation of the modern Lao nation.

## Timeline
- **1353** — King Fa Ngum conquers Muang Sua (Luang Prabang) and founds Lan Xang.
- **1357** — Fa Ngum completes conquests, establishing a kingdom from China to the Khorat Plateau.
- **1373–1416** — Reign of King Samsenthai, who consolidates the kingdom and gains Ming recognition.
- **1428–1438** — Period of instability with seven kings, most killed by Queen Maha Devi.
- **1478–1479** — Đại Việt invades Lan Xang in the "White Elephant War"; Lan Xang repels them.
- **1520–1548** — King Photisarath makes Buddhism the state religion and moves the court to Vientiane.
- **1548–1571** — King Setthathirath defends Lan Xang from multiple Burmese invasions and moves the capital to Vientiane.
- **1574** — Burma briefly conquers Vientiane, leading to a period of vassalage and civil war.
- **1591** — Prince Nokeo Koumane returns from Burma and restores Lan Xang's independence.
- **1637–1694** — Reign of King Sourigna Vongsa, a golden age of peace, law, and cultural flourishing.
- **1707** — Succession dispute leads to the permanent split into the kingdoms of Luang Prabang and Vientiane.

## Body

### Origins and Founding
The region was originally settled by Austroasiatic and later Tai-Kadai speaking peoples. By the 14th century, the principal northern city-state was Muang Sua (Luang Prabang). In 1353, Fa Ngum, a prince raised in the Khmer Empire, returned with an army to claim Muang Sua. He conquered the surrounding territories, including Vientiane and areas on the Khorat Plateau, establishing the kingdom of Lan Xang Hom Khao ("The Land of a Million Elephants and the White Parasol"). His conquests created a large mandala state, a system of interdependent city-states bound by tribute to a central king.

### Consolidation and Cultural Flowering
Fa Ngum was deposed in favor of his son, Samsenthai, who consolidated the kingdom. After a period of instability under Queen Maha Devi, King Chakkaphat successfully defended Lan Xang from a massive Đại Việt invasion in 1479. The subsequent reigns of King Visoun and his successors marked a cultural golden age. Buddhism was solidified as the state religion, classical Lao literature was written, and major temples like Wat Visoun were built. The revered Phra Bang Buddha image was moved to Muang Sua, which was renamed Luang Prabang in its honor.

### Conflict with Burma and Ayutthaya
The 16th century brought intense conflict. King Photisarath moved the political capital to Vientiane for better administration. His son, Setthathirath, became king of both Lan Xang and Lanna but had to return to defend Lan Xang from the powerful Burmese king Bayinnaung. Setthathirath employed guerrilla warfare to successfully repel two major Burmese invasions (1565 and 1569-70), leaving Lan Xang as the only independent Tai kingdom. He later disappeared during a campaign against the Khmer.

### Restoration and Golden Age
After a period of Burmese vassalage and civil war, Prince Nokeo Koumane restored Lan Xang's independence in 1591. The kingdom reached its peak under King Sourigna Vongsa (1637–1694), who enacted legal reforms, maintained peace, and engaged in trade with the Dutch East India Company. His death without a clear heir led to a succession dispute that ultimately fractured the kingdom.

### Decline and Dissolution
The decline of Lan Xang was due to its landlocked geography, a feudal political structure prone to succession disputes, and limited agricultural surplus compared to neighbors like Ayutthaya. The kingdom's economy relied on high-value forest products that passed through intermediary states. After Sourigna Vongsa's death, a civil war resulted in the 1707 split into the rival kingdoms of Luang Prabang and Vientiane. A third kingdom, Champasak, emerged in 1713. These smaller states eventually fell under Siamese suzerainty.

## Terms
- **Mandala**: A political system where a central king's power radiates outward, with semi-autonomous city-states (muang) paying tribute rather than being directly administered.
- **Muang/Vieng**: A fortified city-state, the basic political unit in the Lan Xang mandala system.
- **Sangha**: The Buddhist monastic community, which held significant social, political, and educational power.
- **Palladium**: A sacred object (often a Buddha image) believed to protect a kingdom; the Phra Bang was Lan Xang's palladium.
- **Naga**: A mythical serpent spirit believed to inhabit the Mekong River and protect the kingdom.
- **Phi**: Spirits of the natural world, ancestors, or places in the Lao animist belief system (Satsana Phi).
- **Khorat Plateau**: The large, fertile plateau east of the Mekong in modern Thailand, a key region of Lan Xang.
- **Theravada Buddhism**: The school of Buddhism that became the state religion, emphasizing monastic discipline and the Pali scriptures.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact circumstances of King Setthathirath's death in 1571 are unclear; Lao and Burmese chronicles only presume he died in battle.
- The historical role and extent of power of Queen Maha Devi (Nang Keo Phimpha) in the 1430s is debated, including whether she formally ruled as queen.
- The primary causes of Lan Xang's decline are multifaceted, with scholars weighing the relative importance of geography, political instability, economic limitations, and external military pressure.
