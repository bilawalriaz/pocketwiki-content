# Demosthenes

## Overview
Demosthenes (384–322 BC) was an Athenian statesman and orator whose speeches are a primary source for 4th-century BC Greek politics. He is celebrated for his lifelong opposition to the expansion of Macedon under Philip II and Alexander the Great, embodying the struggle to preserve Athenian democracy and Greek independence. His rhetorical mastery set a standard for oratory studied for millennia.

## Timeline
- **384 BC** — Born in Athens to a wealthy sword-maker.
- **c. 366 BC** — Comes of age; sues his guardians for mismanaging his inheritance.
- **354 BC** — Delivers first political oration, *On the Navy*.
- **351 BC** — Delivers the *First Philippic*, calling for resistance to Philip II of Macedon.
- **348 BC** — Philip conquers Olynthus; Athens sues for peace.
- **346 BC** — Negotiates the Peace of Philocrates; later criticizes its terms.
- **338 BC** — Battle of Chaeronea: Philip defeats the Athenian-Theban alliance.
- **336 BC** — Philip is assassinated; Demosthenes leads an Athenian uprising.
- **330 BC** — Delivers *On the Crown*, defending his career against Aeschines.
- **324 BC** — Implicated in the Harpalus affair; fined and exiled.
- **322 BC** — Commits suicide to avoid capture by Macedonian forces.

## Body

### Early Life and Legal Career
Orphaned at seven, Demosthenes was cheated of his inheritance by his guardians. To seek legal redress, he studied rhetoric, overcoming a speech impediment through rigorous practice. He became a successful logographer (professional speechwriter) and advocate, gaining insight into Athenian law and politics. His early career was marked by a famous case, *Against Meidias*, which defended the principle that law must restrain the powerful.

### Political Rise and Opposition to Philip II
Demosthenes entered politics advocating for an active foreign policy against the rising power of Macedon. His *Philippics* and *Olynthiacs* are fiery calls to arms, warning that Philip II threatened all Greek liberty. He formed anti-Macedonian alliances, notably with Thebes, but his strategy failed at the Battle of Chaeronea (338 BC), which ended Athens's independence. He pragmatically accepted the subsequent peace but never ceased advocating for Greek autonomy.

### Later Years and Death
After Philip's assassination, Demosthenes encouraged a revolt against Alexander the Great, which was brutally suppressed. He was later tried and exiled in the Harpalus scandal, a corruption case involving Macedonian funds. Recalled after Alexander's death, he renewed his anti-Macedonian agitation during the Lamian War. Facing capture by Macedonian forces, he committed suicide in 322 BC.

### Oratory and Legacy
Ancient critics ranked Demosthenes among the greatest Attic orators for his powerful delivery, logical structure, and passionate patriotism. His style was characterized by simplicity, forceful rhythm, and a focus on practical political persuasion. He influenced Roman orators like Cicero and became a timeless symbol of democratic resistance and eloquent statesmanship.

## Terms
- **Logographer**: A professional speechwriter in ancient Athens who composed speeches for others to deliver in court.
- **Philippics**: A series of speeches by Demosthenes denouncing Philip II of Macedon; the term now means any bitter verbal attack.
- **Ekklesia**: The principal assembly of the democracy of ancient Athens, where citizens voted on policy.
- **Trierarch**: An Athenian citizen responsible for outfitting and commanding a trireme (warship) as a public service.
- **Choregos**: A wealthy citizen who financed and supervised the production of a theatrical or musical performance for a festival.
- **Graphē paranómōn**: A legal action in Athens for proposing an illegal decree; a common tool for political rivalry.
- **Hybris**: A legal and moral concept meaning violent outrage or aggravated assault, considered a crime against the community.
- **Areopagus**: A council of former archons in Athens with significant judicial and investigative powers.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Guilt in the Harpalus Affair**: Scholars debate whether Demosthenes was genuinely corrupt or was a victim of politically motivated charges after Athens was forced to scapegoat him.
- **Effectiveness of Policy**: Historians like Polybius and Paparrigopoulos criticize Demosthenes as short-sighted for opposing the inevitable unification of Greece under Macedon, while others like Grote praise his principled defense of liberty.
- **Authorship of Speeches**: The "Demosthenic corpus" includes speeches of disputed authorship, with scholars differing on which works are genuinely his.
- **Accuracy of Biographical Anecdotes**: Many stories about his speech impediment and training come from Plutarch, written centuries later, and their historicity is uncertain.
