# Alfred Hitchcock

## Overview
Alfred Hitchcock (1899–1980) was an English filmmaker widely regarded as one of cinema’s most influential figures. Over six decades he directed more than 50 feature films, pioneering the thriller genre and earning the sobriquet "Master of Suspense" for his ability to manipulate audience anxiety through visual storytelling, editing, and the "MacGuffin"—a plot device that motivates characters but lacks intrinsic narrative value. His career divides into a British period (1925–1939), where he directed the first British "talkie" (*Blackmail*) and established his brand, and a Hollywood period (1939–1976), where he produced classics such as *Vertigo*, *Psycho*, and *The Birds*. Though nominated five times, he never won a competitive Academy Award for Best Director; his film *Rebecca* won Best Picture. He became a cultural icon through cameo appearances, the television anthology *Alfred Hitchcock Presents* (1955–65), and a distinctive self-drawn silhouette logo.

- **1948** — Forms Transatlantic Pictures; releases first colour film, *Rope*, using long-take technique.
- **1954** — *Rear Window*, *Dial M for Murder*, and *To Catch a Thief* released; experiments with 3D and confined-space suspense.
- **1955–65** — Hosts and produces *Alfred Hitchcock Presents*; becomes household celebrity.
- **1955** — Becomes U.S. citizen.
- **1958** — *Vertigo* released; initially mixed reception, later voted greatest film ever (BFI 2012).
- **1960** — *Psycho* released; most profitable film, spawns franchise; shower scene redefines horror.
- **1963** — *The Birds* released; technically challenging mix of trained and mechanical birds.
- **1964** — *Marnie* released; controversial for depiction of sexual violence; Tippi Hedren alleges harassment.
- **1972** — *Frenzy* released; first Hitchcock film with nudity and explicit violence; return to UK production.
- **1976** — *Family Plot* released; final film.
- **3 Jan 1980** — Knighted (KBE) at Universal Studios; too ill to travel to London.
- **29 Apr 1980** — Dies of kidney failure at Bel Air home, aged 80.

## Body

### Early Life and British Apprenticeship (1899–1939)
Hitchcock was born above his parents’ greengrocer’s shop in Leytonstone. A strict Catholic upbringing and Jesuit education at St Ignatius College instilled a lifelong fear of authority and a fascination with guilt and punishment—evident in his "police station" childhood trauma. After his father’s death in 1914, he worked as a technical clerk at Henley’s Telegraph Company while taking night classes in engineering and art. In 1919 he entered the film industry as a title-card designer at Famous Players–Lasky, rapidly absorbing roles as co-writer, art director, and production manager.

When Paramount left London in 1922, Michael Balcon hired him at Gainsborough Pictures. A stint at Germany’s Babelsberg Studios (1924) exposed him to F. W. Murnau’s *The Last Laugh* and German Expressionist techniques, which shaped *The Lodger* (1927)—his first thriller and the film he called his true debut. *Blackmail* (1929) became Britain’s first full sound feature. Under a Gaumont-British contract (1933–38), he refined his style with *The 39 Steps* (1935) and *The Lady Vanishes* (1938), gaining international acclaim. By 1938 he had peaked in Britain; David O. Selznick lured him to Hollywood with a four-film deal.

### Hollywood: The Selznick Years and Wartime (1939–1945)
Hitchcock’s Selznick contract (1939–1945) was creatively fruitful but tense: Selznick exerted tight control, while Hitchcock resisted coverage shooting, preferring "jigsaw cutting" (editing in camera). *Rebecca* (1940) won Best Picture; *Foreign Correspondent* (1940) supported the British war effort. *Suspicion* (1941) began his four-film collaboration with Cary Grant. *Shadow of a Doubt* (1943), filmed on location in Santa Rosa, became his personal favourite. During the war he returned to Britain in late 1943 and early 1944 to make two Ministry of Information shorts (*Bon Voyage*, *Aventure Malgache*) and advised on the suppressed Holocaust documentary *German Concentration Camps Factual Survey* in June and July 1945.

### Independence and the Peak Decade (1945–1964)
Post-war, Hitchcock formed Transatlantic Pictures with Sidney Bernstein, producing *Rope* (1948)—an experiment in simulated continuous takes—and *Under Capricorn* (1949). After Transatlantic folded, he hit a creative peak at Paramount and Universal. Three Grace Kelly films (*Dial M for Murder*, *Rear Window*, *To Catch a Thief*, 1954–55) explored voyeurism and confined spaces. *Vertigo* (1958), starring James Stewart and Kim Novak, introduced the dolly-zoom ("Vertigo effect") and is now cited as his most personal work, examining Pygmalion-like obsession. *North by Northwest* (1959) perfected the "wrong man" chase thriller. *Psycho* (1960), shot cheaply in black-and-white with TV crew, shattered taboos with its shower scene and early heroine death, becoming his biggest financial success. *The Birds* (1963) and *Marnie* (1964) pushed technical and psychological boundaries; the latter’s depiction of rape and Tippi Hedren’s allegations of on-set harassment remain contentious.

### Television and Late Career (1955–1980)
*Alfred Hitchcock Presents* (1955–65) made him a global celebrity; his self-drawn nine-stroke silhouette became a logo. He directed 18 episodes and licensed his name to a mystery magazine. Health declined after the 1960s; Universal assigned *Torn Curtain* (1966) and *Topaz* (1969), both Cold War spy films marred by studio friction and the end of his collaboration with composer Bernard Herrmann. *Frenzy* (1972), shot in London, returned to visceral murder-thriller territory and included his first nudity. *Family Plot* (1976), a lighter caper, was his final film. He was knighted in January 1980 and died three months later.

### Filmmaking Style and Themes
Hitchcock’s "pure cinema" relied on visual storytelling over dialogue, using editing and camera movement to mimic a gaze, turning viewers into voyeurs (*Rear Window*, *Psycho*). Recurring motifs include the "wrong man" (eleven films), the "double" (order vs. chaos), blonde "ice queen" heroines, domineering mothers (*Psycho*, *The Birds*, *Frenzy*), and the MacGuffin. He pushed censorship limits—implying homosexuality, incest, and sexual violence—while charming censors. His "actors as cattle" remark was a provocation; in practice he demanded precise, economical performances integrated into pre-visualized compositions. He storyboarded extensively (though Bill Krohn’s research shows he often deviated on set) and shot chronologically to preserve emotional continuity, often over budget.

### Representation of Women and Actor Relations
Scholars debate his portrayal of women: Laura Mulvey’s "male gaze" thesis argues his camera adopts a heterosexual male perspective, fetishizing blonde, remote women who are ultimately humiliated. Bidisha catalogues archetypes—vamp, witch, "demon mommy"—all punished. Actresses (Kelly, Bergman, Hedren, Novak) delivered iconic performances under intense direction. Hitchcock valued actors who could "do nothing well"—react rather than emote—treating them as "animated props" within the frame. Despite his reputation, collaborators like Hume Cronyn and Walter Slezak praised his technical support.

### Writing, Storyboards, and Production
Hitchcock supervised scripts draft-by-draft, demanding visual narration. He claimed the film was "perfect" once scripted; shooting lost "40 per cent" of the conception. Storyboards served as a control mechanism against studio interference, though Krohn demonstrates many sequences (e.g., *North by Northwest* crop-duster) were improvised or reverse-engineered for publicity. He shot alternative takes for editing options, not coverage, retaining final cut authority in the cutting room.

## Terms
- ****MacGuffin**** — A plot device (object, goal, or secret) that motivates the characters and drives the narrative but has no intrinsic importance or explanatory value (coined by Angus MacPhail; exemplified by the stolen plans in *The 39 Steps*).
- ****Pure cinema**** — Hitchcock’s term for storytelling through visual means—editing, camera movement, composition—rather than dialogue or theatrical exposition.
- ****Dolly zoom (Vertigo effect)**** — A camera technique (developed by Irmin Roberts for *Vertigo*) where the camera dollies forward while zooming out (or vice versa), keeping the subject size constant while the background perspective distorts, conveying vertigo or psychological rupture.
- ****"Wrong man" plot**** — A narrative structure in which an innocent, ordinary protagonist is mistaken for a criminal or spy and pursued by authorities and villains alike; Hitchcock used it in eleven films.
- ****Hitchcock blonde**** — An archetypal leading lady: cool, elegant, blonde, often fetishized by costume and lighting, who becomes the object of male obsession or suspicion (template: Madeleine Carroll in *The 39 Steps*; epitome: Grace Kelly, Kim Novak, Tippi Hedren).
- ****Jigsaw cutting**** — Hitchcock’s practice of shooting only the exact angles and lengths needed for his pre-planned edit, denying producers alternative coverage to re-cut the film.
- ****Voyeurism (cinematic)**** — The positioning of the camera and editing to align the viewer’s gaze with a character’s surreptitious watching, implicating the audience in the act of looking (*Rear Window*, *Psycho*, *Marnie*).
- ****Double**** — A structural motif pairing two characters or objects to contrast order/chaos, good/evil, or sanity/madness (e.g., Bruno and Guy in *Strangers on a Train*; Uncle Charlie and young Charlie in *Shadow of a Doubt*).
- ****German Expressionism**** — A 1920s film style using distorted sets, chiaroscuro lighting, and exaggerated acting to externalize inner states; influenced Hitchcock at Babelsberg and in *The Lodger*.
- ****Motion Picture Production Code (Hays Code)**** — U.S. industry censorship guidelines (1934–1968) restricting crime, sex, and violence; Hitchcock consistently tested its boundaries through implication and dark humour.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authorship vs. collaboration**: How much of the "Hitchcock touch" belongs to Alma Reville (co-writer, editor, sounding board) versus Hitchcock’s sole vision? Charles Champlin argued "four hands"; Hitchcock’s 1979 AFI speech credited her for four distinct roles.
- **Misogyny vs. critique**: Do his films punish women for male fantasy (Mulvey, Bidisha), or expose the violence of the male gaze? *Marnie* and *Frenzy* intensify this debate with explicit sexual violence.
- **Tippi Hedren allegations**: Hedren and co-stars (Diane Baker) describe obsession, isolation, and harassment during *The Birds* and *Marnie*; Hitchcock’s defenders cite his professional rigor. No legal action was taken; the record rests on testimony.
- **Storyboard myth**: Bill Krohn’s *Hitchcock at Work* challenges the director’s self-mythologizing of total pre-visualization, showing extensive on-set improvisation and post-hoc storyboard creation for publicity.
- **Censorship strategy**: Did Hitchcock’s "inescapable inferences" (McGilligan) subvert the Production Code, or did they rely on the Code’s existence to generate tension through suggestion?
- **Vertigo’s canonization**: Its rise from box-office disappointment to BFI #1 (2012) reflects shifting critical paradigms (auteur theory, psychoanalytic criticism); whether it surpasses *Citizen Kane* remains a live poll question.
- **Late-period quality**: *Torn Curtain* and *Topaz* are widely dismissed; *Frenzy* divides critics between "return to form" and "gratuitous nastiness." The degree of studio coercion vs. directorial decline is unresolved.
