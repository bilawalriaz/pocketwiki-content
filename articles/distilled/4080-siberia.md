# Siberia

## Overview
Siberia is a vast geographical region in North Asia, forming the Asian part of Russia. It is defined by its immense size, sparse population, and extreme climate, and has been part of the Russian state since the 16th century. Its history is marked by indigenous cultures, Russian conquest, and Soviet-era industrialization and repression, while its economy is heavily based on natural resource extraction.

## Timeline
- **c. 40,000 years ago** — At least three human species (H. sapiens, H. neanderthalensis, Denisovans) live in southern Siberia.
- **13th century** — The Mongol Empire conquers a large part of Siberia.
- **Late 15th century** — The autonomous Khanate of Sibir is formed after the breakup of the Golden Horde.
- **1582** — The fall of the Khanate of Sibir begins the lengthy Russian conquest.
- **1590** — Tobolsk becomes the de facto capital of Russian Siberia.
- **1778** — Annexation of Chukotka concludes the Russian conquest of Siberia.
- **1891–1916** — The Trans-Siberian Railway is constructed, linking Siberia to European Russia.
- **30 June 1908** — The Tunguska event, a probable meteor air burst, occurs in central Siberia.
- **1929–1953** — The Gulag system of penal labor camps operates extensively in Siberia.
- **1991** — The Soviet Union collapses, leading to economic decline in Siberia.
- **2 December 2019** — The 'Power of Siberia' gas pipeline begins operation, supplying gas to China.

## Body

### Etymology and Definition
The origin of the name "Siberia" is uncertain. It was adopted into Russian through contact with the Khanate of Sibir in the 15th century. Hypotheses include derivation from a Mongolic word for 'dense forest', a Turkic phrase meaning 'water' and 'wild land', or a Proto-Slavic word for 'north'. There is no single precise definition of Siberia's borders. It is generally understood to stretch from the Ural Mountains to the Pacific Ocean and from the Arctic Circle to the northern borders of Kazakhstan, Mongolia, and China. The Russian government divides the area into three federal districts, with only the central one officially named "Siberian".

### Prehistory and Early History
Siberia has significant paleontological importance, with Pleistocene megafauna like mammoths preserved in permafrost. Genetic evidence shows that at least three human species coexisted there around 40,000 years ago. Ancient North Eurasian populations from Siberia contributed genetically to Native Americans, Europeans, and other groups. For millennia, various nomadic groups inhabited the region. The Mongol Empire conquered much of it in the 13th century, leading to the formation of the Khanate of Sibir.

### Russian Conquest and Imperial Era
Russian interest began with Novgorod's fur trade in the 11th century. The Tsardom of Russia started undermining the Khanate of Sibir in the 16th century, with Cossacks and traders pushing east. By the mid-17th century, Russia controlled territory to the Pacific. Siberia became a destination for internal exiles, with over 800,000 people exiled there in the 19th century. The Trans-Siberian Railway (1891–1916) spurred migration and industrialization.

### Soviet Era and Modern Period
The Soviet Union used Siberia extensively for the Gulag penal camp system, through which over 14 million people passed between 1929 and 1953. Major industrial cities like Norilsk developed from these camps. After the 1991 Soviet collapse, Siberia faced economic decline. The region remains a core source of Russia's oil, gas, and mineral wealth, exemplified by the 'Power of Siberia' pipeline.

### Geography and Climate
Siberia covers over 13.1 million km², about three-quarters of Russia's area. Its geography includes the West Siberian Plain and the Central Siberian Plateau. The climate is severe, with long, cold winters (January average of −25 °C) and short summers. Verkhoyansk and Oymyakon are among the coldest inhabited places on Earth. Permafrost is widespread. Global warming is causing thawing in Western Siberia, potentially releasing large amounts of methane.

### Demographics and Culture
The population is about 30 million, with a density of roughly three people per km². Over 85% are of European descent, primarily Russians. Significant indigenous minorities include Turkic groups (Yakuts, Tuvans) and Mongolic Buryats. The predominant religion is Russian Orthodox Christianity, but shamanism and other native beliefs persist. Culture includes traditional foods like stroganina (raw frozen fish) and pelmeni dumplings.

## Terms
- **Permafrost**: Ground that remains frozen for two or more consecutive years, widespread in Siberia.
- **Gulag**: The government agency that administered the Soviet system of forced-labor camps, many located in remote Siberia.
- **Trans-Siberian Railway**: A major rail line connecting Moscow with the Russian Far East, crucial for Siberian development and settlement.
- **Tunguska event**: A massive explosion in 1908 near the Podkamennaya Tunguska River, believed to be caused by the air burst of a meteor or comet.
- **Khanate of Sibir**: A Turkic-Mongol state that controlled western Siberia before its conquest by Russia.
- **Denisovans**: An extinct species or subspecies of archaic human identified from DNA in Siberian fossils.
- **Ancient North Eurasian**: A genetic population from Paleolithic Siberia that contributed to the ancestry of Native Americans and others.
- **Siberian High**: A large, semi-permanent system of high atmospheric pressure centered over northeastern Siberia in winter.

## Debates and open questions
- The precise etymology of "Siberia" remains uncertain, with multiple competing linguistic hypotheses.
- The exact territorial boundaries of Siberia are not fixed, varying between historical, geographic, and administrative definitions, which can be a politically sensitive topic.
- The scale, scope, and human cost of the Gulag system continue to be subjects of research and debate.
- The potential environmental impact of thawing permafrost in Siberia, particularly the release of methane, is a major concern in climate science.
