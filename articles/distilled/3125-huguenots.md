# Huguenots

## Overview
The Huguenots were French Protestants who adhered to the Reformed (Calvinist) tradition during the 16th and 17th centuries. Their struggle for religious freedom against Catholic persecution led to civil wars, a period of limited tolerance, and ultimately a mass exodus from France, creating a significant diaspora that influenced the development of several nations.

## Timeline
- **c. 1550** — Term "Huguenot" comes into common use for French Reformed Protestants.
- **1559** — First national synod of the Reformed Church of France is held in Paris.
- **1562** — Massacre of Vassy sparks the French Wars of Religion, which last intermittently until 1598.
- **1572** — St. Bartholomew's Day massacre results in the deaths of thousands of Huguenots.
- **1598** — Edict of Nantes grants Huguenots substantial religious and political autonomy.
- **1629** — Edict of Alès abolishes Huguenot political and military privileges.
- **1685** — Edict of Fontainebleau revokes the Edict of Nantes, making Protestantism illegal.
- **1688-1689** — Organized Huguenot migration to the Dutch Cape Colony (South Africa) begins.
- **1787** — Edict of Versailles officially ends persecution of Protestants in France.
- **1789** — Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen grants Protestants equal rights.
- **1985** — French President François Mitterrand issues a formal apology to Huguenot descendants.

## Body

### Origins and Growth
The Huguenot movement grew from pre-Protestant reformers and the spread of John Calvin's teachings. The term "Huguenot" likely originated as a derogatory pun, possibly linking the name of the Swiss politician Besançon Hugues to words for "housemates" or "oath-fellows." By the 1560s, Huguenots—concentrated in southern and western France—numbered about 1.8 million, or roughly 10% of the population. They included both "Huguenots of religion" (devout Calvinists) and "Huguenots of the state" (nobles opposing the Catholic Guise family's influence).

### Wars of Religion and the Edict of Nantes
Growing Catholic hostility led to the French Wars of Religion (1562–1598). The conflict included the devastating St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572, where thousands of Huguenots were killed. The wars concluded with the Edict of Nantes (1598), issued by King Henry IV (a former Huguenot who converted to Catholicism). This edict reaffirmed Catholicism as the state religion but granted Huguenots freedom of conscience, civil equality, and control over certain fortified cities.

### Persecution and the Revocation
Huguenot rebellions in the 1620s led to the loss of their political and military privileges. Under Louis XIV, persecution intensified, culminating in the Edict of Fontainebleau (1685), which revoked the Edict of Nantes. Protestantism was outlawed, churches were destroyed, and Huguenots were forced to convert or flee. The state-sponsored "dragonnades"—the quartering of soldiers in Protestant homes to force conversion—were particularly brutal. Louis XIV claimed the Huguenot population was reduced from 800,000–900,000 to just 1,000–1,500, an exaggeration, but the community was devastated.

### The Great Exodus
The revocation triggered a massive exodus. An estimated 200,000 to 1,000,000 Huguenots fled to Protestant regions, including the Dutch Republic, England, Prussia, Switzerland, and colonies in South Africa and North America. This "brain drain" deprived France of many skilled artisans, merchants, and intellectuals. The refugees often traveled by night with guides, facing arrest and robbery. Their flight damaged Louis XIV's reputation abroad and contributed to later conflicts with England and the Dutch Republic.

### Legacy and Modern Era
Persecution ended officially with the Edict of Versailles (1787), and full rights were granted in 1789. In 1985, France formally apologized for the persecution. Today, the United Protestant Church of France represents the main continuation of the Huguenot tradition. Descendants of the diaspora maintain their identity in countries like South Africa, the United States, and Australia, where they contributed to industries like winemaking, weaving, and glassmaking.

## Terms
- **Calvinism**: The Reformed Protestant tradition founded on the teachings of John Calvin, emphasizing God's sovereignty and predestination.
- **Edict of Nantes (1598)**: A decree by Henry IV that granted Huguenots religious toleration and political rights, ending the French Wars of Religion.
- **Edict of Fontainebleau (1685)**: The decree by Louis XIV that revoked the Edict of Nantes, outlawing Protestantism in France.
- **Dragonnades**: A policy of forcibly quartering soldiers in Huguenot homes to coerce conversion to Catholicism.
- **Camisards**: Huguenot peasants in the Cévennes region who revolted against persecution in the early 18th century.
- **Walloon churches**: French-speaking Reformed congregations established by Huguenot refugees in the Dutch Republic and elsewhere.
- **Huguenot cross**: The distinctive emblem of the Huguenots, now an official symbol of the United Protestant Church of France.

## Debates and Open Questions
The exact origin of the term "Huguenot" is debated, with hypotheses ranging from a reference to Swiss politician Besançon Hugues to a corruption of the name of King Hugues Capet or a derogatory term linking Protestants to ghosts. Historians also debate the precise demographic impact of the exodus, with estimates of the number of refugees varying widely. The long-term economic effect of the "brain drain" on France versus the technological and industrial benefits gained by receiving nations like Prussia and England remains a subject of study.
