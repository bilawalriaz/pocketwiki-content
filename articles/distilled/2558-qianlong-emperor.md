# Qianlong Emperor

## Overview
The Qianlong Emperor (r. 1735–1796) was the fourth Qing emperor to rule China proper and presided over the dynasty's territorial and cultural zenith, the "High Qing" era. His reign was marked by massive military expansion, prolific cultural patronage, and the suppression of dissent, but also by growing corruption, costly wars, and policies that sowed the seeds of the empire's later decline.

## Timeline
- **1711** — Born as Hongli, fourth son of the Yongzheng Emperor.
- **1735** — Ascends the throne after his father's death, using the era name Qianlong.
- **1739** — Foils a coup plot by Prince Hongxi.
- **c. 1755–1759** — Conquers the Dzungar Khanate, orders the Dzungar genocide, and annexes Xinjiang.
- **1750s** — Establishes firm Qing control over Tibet after the Lhasa riot of 1750.
- **1762** — Implements harsh anti-Muslim laws endorsed by official Chen Hongmou.
- **1765–1769** — Fights the Sino-Burmese War, which ends in a stalemate.
- **1773–1776** — Completes the *Siku Quanshu* (Complete Library of the Four Treasuries) while suppressing ~3,100 works.
- **1781** — Executes the Sufi leader Ma Mingxin, triggering Jahriyya rebellions.
- **1788** — Invades Vietnam to restore the Lê dynasty, but is defeated by the Tây Sơn.
- **1793** — Receives the Macartney Embassy from Britain, rejecting most trade requests.
- **1796** — Abdicates to honor his grandfather Kangxi's 61-year reign, but retains power as Emperor Emeritus.
- **1799** — Dies at age 87, with his son the Jiaqing Emperor having ruled in name only for three years.

## Body

### Military Expansion and Frontier Policy
Qianlong's reign saw the Qing Empire's greatest territorial expansion through the "Ten Great Campaigns." The most significant was the conquest of the Dzungar Khanate in Inner Asia, which led to the annexation of Xinjiang. This victory culminated in the Dzungar genocide, an explicit policy that destroyed the Dzungar people. He also established a protectorate over Tibet, fought inconclusive wars with Burma and Nepal, and failed to restore the Lê dynasty in Vietnam. These campaigns unified non-Han peoples like Uyghurs and Mongols but were financially draining and exposed military weaknesses, particularly against tropical diseases and guerrilla tactics.

### Cultural Patronage and Control
Qianlong was a major patron of Confucian culture and the arts. He amassed a vast imperial collection of art, jade, and bronzes, often pressuring officials to surrender treasures. His grandest project was the *Siku Quanshu*, the largest compilation of Chinese literature, which preserved texts but also served as a tool for political control. The accompanying literary inquisition suppressed works deemed anti-Qing or rebellious. He was a prolific writer himself, composing over 40,000 poems. To preserve Manchu identity, he promoted the Manchu language, commissioned dictionaries, and reinstituted the annual imperial hunt.

### Religion and Ethnic Policy
Qianlong personally practiced Tibetan Buddhism, presenting himself as the Bodhisattva Manjushri to legitimize rule over Mongols and Tibetans. He patronized the Gelug sect to "maintain peace among the Mongols." His policy toward Muslims shifted dramatically from his predecessors' tolerance. Endorsing harsh laws, he held Muslim leaders responsible for congregants' crimes, leading to severe tensions. This state involvement in disputes between Sufi orders (Jahriyya and Khafiyya) fueled major rebellions in the northwest. He also "Manchu-fied" the Eight Banner system, redefining identity by descent rather than culture and demobilizing many Han Bannermen.

### Foreign Relations and Decline
Qianlong's court viewed foreign states through a tributary lens. The 1793 Macartney Embassy from Britain, seeking expanded trade and an embassy, was politely rebuffed, with Qianlong's famous letter stating China had "no use for your country's manufactures." This is often cited as a symbol of Qing insularity. In his later years, he grew complacent and trusted the corrupt official Heshen, whose wealth later exceeded the treasury's. The combination of war costs, extravagant building projects, and corruption depleted the treasury surplus, ushering in the dynasty's gradual decline.

## Terms
- **Ten Great Campaigns:** A series of military expeditions under Qianlong that expanded Qing territory into Inner Asia, Burma, and Vietnam.
- **Dzungar genocide:** The deliberate, state-ordered destruction of the Dzungar Mongol people following their defeat, resulting in massive population loss.
- **Siku Quanshu (Complete Library of the Four Treasuries):** The largest collection of Chinese books ever compiled, used for both preservation and political censorship.
- **Literary Inquisition:** A campaign of censorship and persecution targeting authors and texts considered rebellious or anti-Qing.
- **Eight Banner system:** The basic military and social organization of the Qing dynasty, which Qianlong redefined along ethnic lines.
- **Canton System:** The trade policy limiting all Western maritime trade to the single port of Guangzhou (Canton), enforced from 1757.
- **Emperor Emeritus (Taishang Huang):** The title Qianlong held after his abdication, allowing him to retain ultimate power until his death.
- **Jahriyya and Khafiyya:** Two rival Sufi orders among Chinese Muslims whose conflict drew in the Qing state, leading to violent rebellions.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nature of Qing Expansion:** Scholars debate whether Qing expansion under Qianlong was colonial in nature, similar to European imperialism, or a continuation of traditional Inner Asian empire-building.
- **Macartney Embassy Interpretation:** Older interpretations saw the mission's failure as proof of Chinese arrogance and refusal to modernize. Recent scholarship, using more Qing documents, argues the court acted pragmatically, making calculated promises to avoid conflict with a rising British power.
- **Qianlong's Legacy:** Historians weigh his achievements in consolidating a multiethnic empire and cultural patronage against the costs of his militarism, censorship, and the corruption that accelerated the Qing's decline.
