# Graffiti

## Overview
Graffiti is writing or drawings made on surfaces without permission, ranging from simple tags to elaborate paintings. It has existed since ancient times but became a global phenomenon with modern hip-hop culture. Graffiti is controversial, often classified as vandalism but also recognized as a form of public art and political expression.

## Timeline
- **c. 35,000 years ago** — Cave paintings in Chauvet Cave, France, among the oldest known prehistoric graffiti.
- **c. 500 BC** — Oldest known written graffito found on the Greek island of Astypalaia.
- **1st century BC–4th century AD** — Safaitic graffiti inscriptions created in the deserts of southern Syria, eastern Jordan, and northern Saudi Arabia.
- **6th–18th centuries** — Over 1,800 individual graffiti are produced by visitors on the "mirror wall" at Sigiriya, Sri Lanka.
- **Late 1800s** — Oldest known graffiti monikers appear on traincars created by hobos and railworkers in the United States.
- **1967** — The phrase "Clapton is God" is spray-painted on a wall in London, an early example of modern celebrity graffiti.
- **Early 1970s** — Modern graffiti style begins with tags in New York City and Philadelphia, influenced by hip-hop culture.
- **1974** — The term "wildstyle" is popularized by the Wild Style graffiti crew in the Bronx, New York.
- **1983** — The documentary *Style Wars* is released, depicting New York's graffiti and hip-hop culture.
- **1993** — American student Michael P. Fay is sentenced to caning in Singapore for vandalism, sparking international debate.
- **2001** — IBM pays $120,000 in fines after a marketing campaign using unauthorized sidewalk graffiti in Chicago and San Francisco. |
- **2011** — Graffiti becomes a tool of political expression during the Arab Spring and other conflicts in the Middle East and North Africa.

## Body

### History and Evolution
Graffiti has ancient origins, with petroglyphs dating back tens of thousands of years. In ancient Greece and Rome, it was a common form of communication and not typically considered vandalism. The medieval period saw graffiti like the Safaitic inscriptions and tourist comments at Sigiriya. Modern graffiti emerged in the 1960s and 70s in New York City and Philadelphia, heavily influenced by hip-hop culture. Early forms included "tags" (stylized signatures) by artists like TAKI 183 and Cornbread, which evolved into more elaborate "throw-ups" and "pieces" on subway trains. The 1974 essay *The Faith of Graffiti* helped popularize the term "graffiti" for this modern movement.

### Types and Tools
Graffiti encompasses various styles and techniques. **Tags** are the simplest form, consisting of a writer's name or logo in a unique handstyle. **Throw-ups** are large, quickly executed bubble-letter pieces. **Pieces** are complex, multi-colored works with detailed fills and effects. **Wildstyle** is the most intricate form, often abstract and difficult for outsiders to read. **Stencil graffiti** uses pre-cut templates for quick application. Tools include spray paint, markers, and specialized art supplies. **Stickers** (or "slaps") are another common medium, allowing for discreet and rapid placement.

### Purpose and Expression
Graffiti serves multiple purposes. It is a form of **personal expression** and **public art**, valued by some for its accessibility and temporary nature. It can also be **territorial**, used by gangs to mark turf. A significant function is **radical and political expression**, seen in movements from the 1968 Paris protests to the Berlin Wall and the Arab Spring. Conversely, graffiti can be **offensive**, used for hate speech or genocide denial, as seen with murals of war criminals in the Balkans. The line between vandalism and art is frequently debated, exemplified by the work of Banksy, which is both celebrated and removed by authorities.

### Global Movements
Graffiti has become a worldwide phenomenon. In **Africa**, it emerged in the 1990s as a tool for social and political awareness, with festivals like FESTIGRAFF in Senegal. **South Africa** has a vibrant post-apartheid graffiti scene, particularly in Cape Town. In **Europe**, stencil art predated American influence, and modern graffiti reached Eastern Europe in the 1990s. The **Middle East** has seen graffiti used for political dissent, especially during the Arab Spring, with the Israeli West Bank barrier as a prominent canvas. **South America**, particularly Brazil, has an active culture with forms like *pichação* (tall, protest-oriented characters). **Southeast Asia** has been influenced by Western culture, with countries like Malaysia hosting street art festivals.

### Government and Legal Responses
Responses to graffiti vary globally. Many governments criminalize it, with penalties ranging from fines to imprisonment. **Singapore** famously caned American student Michael P. Fay in 1994 for vandalism. **Taiwan** has designated "Graffiti Zones" for legal expression. In the **United Kingdom**, the Anti-social Behaviour Act 2003 and campaigns like "Keep Britain Tidy" advocate zero tolerance. The **United States** uses tracker databases, gang injunctions, and hotlines to combat graffiti. Some cities, like Melbourne, Australia, and Stroud, UK, provide legal walls to channel artistic expression. In **China**, political graffiti is restricted, though general graffiti is often tolerated in Beijing.

### Commercialization and Media
Graffiti has been commercialized by corporations like IBM and Sony in advertising campaigns, sometimes leading to legal issues. It is also a subject in media, with influential documentaries like *Style Wars* (1983) and *Exit Through the Gift Shop* (2010), and films like *Wild Style* (1983). Graffiti artists often remain anonymous, as seen with Banksy, to avoid prosecution and maintain the subversive nature of the art form.

## Terms
- **Tag**: A writer's stylized signature, the most basic form of modern graffiti.
- **Throw-up**: A large, quickly executed piece of graffiti, often in bubble letters.
- **Piece**: A large, elaborate, and multi-colored graffiti work.
- **Wildstyle**: A complex, abstract, and interlocking style of graffiti lettering.
- **Stencil Graffiti**: An image created by spraying paint through a pre-cut template.
- **Pichação**: A form of Brazilian graffiti characterized by tall, cryptic characters, often used for protest.
- **Vandalism**: The action of defacing or destroying property without permission, a legal classification for unauthorized graffiti.
- **Subvertising**: The practice of making parodies of corporate and political advertisements.
- **Gang Injunction**: A civil court order designed to limit gang activity, often including restrictions on possessing graffiti tools.

## Debates and Open Questions
The central debate is whether graffiti is **art or vandalism**. This is reflected in the conflicting responses from authorities and the public, and in the career of artists like Banksy, whose work is both protected and removed. There is ongoing discussion about the effectiveness of **zero-tolerance policies versus designated legal walls** in reducing illegal graffiti. The **commercialization** of graffiti raises questions about its authenticity and subversive potential. Finally, the use of graffiti for **hate speech and genocide denial** presents a challenge for balancing free expression with community safety.
