# José Saramago

## Overview
José Saramago (1922–2010) was a Portuguese novelist, poet, and journalist who received the 1998 Nobel Prize in Literature for parables that blend imagination, compassion, and irony to apprehend an "elusory reality." Born into extreme rural poverty, he left school at age 12 to become a lathe operator and mechanic before turning to translation, journalism, and editing. He joined the Portuguese Communist Party in 1969 and served briefly as assistant director of the newspaper *Diário de Notícias* following the 1974 Carnation Revolution, but was dismissed after a right-wing counter-coup in November 1975. Saramago achieved international fame only in his sixties with *Memorial do Convento* (1982). His experimental style—characterized by page-long sentences, sparse punctuation, and the omission of quotation marks and proper nouns—serves themes of historical uncertainty, collective blindness, and the struggle for dignity against political and economic structures. A self-described libertarian communist and atheist, he clashed repeatedly with the Catholic Church and the Portuguese government, notably when Prime Minister Aníbal Cavaco Silva vetoed *The Gospel According to Jesus Christ* from a European literary prize in 1992, prompting Saramago’s exile to Lanzarote, Spain, where he lived with his wife, translator Pilar del Río, until his death.

## Body

### Early Life and Formation
Saramago was born in the Ribatejo village of Azinhaga to a family of landless peasants. The surname "Saramago" (Portuguese for wild radish) was an insulting nickname for his father, José de Sousa, mistakenly entered as the family name by a village clerk. In 1924, the family relocated to Lisbon, where his father became a police officer; months later, his four-year-old brother Francisco died. A pivotal childhood memory involved his grandfather, facing fatal illness, embracing the trees in his yard to say goodbye—a moment Saramago credited with marking him for life. Despite academic promise, poverty forced him into technical school at 12 to train as a lathe operator. He worked as a car mechanic for two years before discovering a public library, which ignited his literary vocation. He married Ilda Reis in 1944 and worked as a civil servant, then as an editor and translator at Estúdios Cor, and finally as a journalist.

### Political Engagement and the Revolution
Saramago joined the Portuguese Communist Party in 1969. After the 1974 Carnation Revolution, he was appointed assistant director of *Diário de Notícias* on April 9, 1975, during the leftist PREC period under Prime Minister Vasco Gonçalves. He shifted the paper’s editorial line toward communism, prompting half the editorial staff (30 journalists) to petition for a revision. At a heated plenary, Saramago intervened angrily, resulting in the expulsion of 24 journalists accused of being right-wingers. Following the November 25, 1975 counter-coup that ended the PREC, Saramago was himself fired. This dismissal catalyzed his full-time commitment to literature, producing a rapid succession of works: *Manual de Pintura e Caligrafia* (1977), *Objecto Quase* (1978), *Levantado do Chão* (1980), and *Viagem a Portugal* (1981).

### Literary Breakthrough and Major Works
Saramago’s international career began at age 60 with *Memorial do Convento* (1982), a baroque narrative set during the 18th-century Inquisition featuring a maimed soldier, a clairvoyant woman, and a priest dreaming of flight. Its translation as *Baltasar and Blimunda* (1988) brought global attention. Subsequent novels cemented his reputation: *The Year of the Death of Ricardo Reis* (1984), where poet Fernando Pessoa’s heteronym outlives him, won the British Independent Foreign Fiction Prize; *The History of the Siege of Lisbon* (1989) explored historical uncertainty through a proofreader who alters a text and falls in love; *The Stone Raft* (1986) imagined the Iberian Peninsula drifting into the Atlantic; *Blindness* (1995) depicted a country struck by an epidemic of "white blindness," abandoning proper nouns to emphasize identity loss; and *Death with Interruptions* (2005) posited a society where death ceases, examining spiritual and political collapse. His style features marathon sentences linked by commas, paragraphs spanning pages without dialogue markers (speaker changes signaled only by capitalization), and frequent intertextual references to his own oeuvre.

### Censorship, Exile, and Controversy
The 1991 publication of *The Gospel According to Jesus Christ* provoked a national crisis. The Catholic Church condemned its portrayal of God and Jesus as fallible, cruel human beings; Christ lives with Mary Magdalene and attempts to avoid crucifixion. Prime Minister Aníbal Cavaco Silva’s social-democratic government barred the novel from competing for the Aristeion Prize, citing offense to Catholic sensibilities. Saramago denounced this as political censorship and, with Pilar del Río, went into exile on the Canary island of Lanzarote. The Vatican later criticized his 1998 Nobel Prize as "ideologically slanted." Saramago responded that the Vatican should "focus on their prayers and leave people in peace," distinguishing respect for believers from contempt for the institution.

### Political Philosophy and Public Intellectual
Saramago defined his mature politics as libertarian communism. He ran symbolically for the European Parliament (1989–2009) and served as alderman in Lisbon (1989). He criticized the European Union, IMF, and global capital, notably during the 2008 financial crisis: "Marx was never so right as now." His blog collection *The Notebook* (2008–09) delivered "knock-down prose blows" against "organized lies." He supported Iberian Federalism and compared his engagement to George Orwell’s, insisting citizenship obliges intervention. His comments on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict sparked intense controversy: visiting Ramallah in 2002, he likened the "spirit of Auschwitz" to Israeli actions, drawing accusations of antisemitism. He later clarified he did not equate Ramallah with Auschwitz, but argued the *spirit* of the Holocaust was present. During the 2006 Lebanon War, he joined Noam Chomsky and others in condemning what they termed the "liquidation of the Palestinian nation."

### Final Years and Legacy
Saramago suffered from leukemia and pneumonia in his final years but maintained a rigid routine: "I write two pages. And then I read and read and read." He died in Lanzarote on June 18, 2010. Portugal declared two days of mourning; over 20,000 attended his Lisbon funeral, notably absent was President Cavaco Silva. His ashes were buried in 2011 beneath an olive tree at the José Saramago Foundation (Casa dos Bicos), which he founded in 2007 to defend the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, promote culture, and protect the environment. A "lost novel" from the 1950s, *Skylight* (*Claraboia*), was published posthumously in 2011. In 2024, his widow donated personal artifacts, including his glasses and a youthful manuscript, to the Nobel Prize Museum.

## Terms
- ****PREC** (Processo Revolucionário Em Curso)** — "Ongoing Revolutionary Process" (1974–75); a turbulent period in Portugal following the Carnation Revolution marked by radical leftist policies, nationalizations, and land occupations, ended by the November 25, 1975 coup.
- ****Heteronym**** — In Fernando Pessoa’s work, a distinct literary persona with its own biography, style, and philosophy, unlike a mere pseudonym; Saramago’s *The Year of the Death of Ricardo Reis* imagines one such heteronym surviving the poet.
- ****Libertarian Communism**** — A political philosophy advocating stateless, non-hierarchical communal ownership of production; Saramago adopted this label in later life, distinguishing it from authoritarian state communism.
- ****Aristeion Prize**** — A European literary award (1990–1999); Saramago’s *The Gospel According to Jesus Christ* was removed from its shortlist by the Portuguese government in 1992.
- ****Carnation Revolution**** — The April 25, 1974 military coup in Lisbon that overthrew the authoritarian Estado Novo regime, leading to democracy and decolonization.
- ****Iberian Federalism**** — Political concept advocating a federal union of Portugal and Spain; Saramago was a public supporter.
- ****Casa dos Bicos**** — "House of Spikes," a 16th-century Lisbon building with a distinctive diamond-stone façade; headquarters of the José Saramago Foundation since 2007.
- ****"White Blindness"**** — The fictional epidemic in *Blindness* (1995) where victims see only a milky whiteness, serving as an allegory for moral and social collapse.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Antisemitism allegations vs. political critique:** Critics charged Saramago’s 2002 Ramallah remarks—comparing the "spirit of Auschwitz" to Israeli actions and stating Jews "scratch their own wound to keep it bleeding"—were antisemitic. Saramago denied equating Ramallah with Auschwitz, arguing he targeted the *political instrumentalization* of the Holocaust. The debate centers on whether his rhetoric crossed from anti-Zionism into anti-Jewish tropes.
*   **Nobel Prize politicization:** The Vatican and Portuguese right-wing press criticized the 1998 award as ideologically motivated rather than literary. The Swedish Academy cited his "modern skepticism" and unique amalgam of irony and empathy; the controversy reflects broader tensions between secular literary institutions and religious/political establishments.
*   **Censorship vs. cultural sensitivity:** The 1992 removal of *The Gospel According to Jesus Christ* from the Aristeion Prize is debated as either state censorship violating artistic freedom or a legitimate democratic response to a work deemed blasphemous by a majority-Catholic electorate.
*   **Style as politics:** Scholars debate whether Saramago’s experimental syntax (absence of quotation marks, proper nouns, standard paragraphing) is primarily an aesthetic innovation or a political strategy to dismantle hierarchical language structures and authoritarian narratives.
