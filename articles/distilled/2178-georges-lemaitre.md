# Georges Lemaître

## Overview
Georges Lemaître (1894–1966) was a Belgian Catholic priest, theoretical physicist, and mathematician who is credited as the first to propose the modern Big Bang theory. His work connected the observed expansion of the universe to Einstein's general relativity, fundamentally shaping modern cosmology and demonstrating that scientific inquiry and religious faith can be separate domains.

## Timeline
- **17 July 1894** — Born in Charleroi, Belgium.
- **1914–1918** — Served in the Belgian Army during World War I.
- **1920** — Earned a doctorate in science from the Catholic University of Louvain.
- **22 September 1923** — Ordained as a priest.
- **1923–1925** — Studied in England with Arthur Eddington and in the US with Harlow Shapley.
- **1927** — Published paper deriving an expanding universe solution from general relativity and proposing a velocity-distance relation for galaxies.
- **1929** — Edwin Hubble published observational evidence for galaxy recession, confirming Lemaître's earlier theoretical prediction.
- **March 1931** — Proposed the "hypothesis of the primeval atom," the first formulation of the Big Bang theory.
- **1933** — Presented his theory at Caltech; Einstein reportedly praised it.
- **1953** — Received the first Eddington Medal for his work on the expansion of the universe.
- **20 June 1966** — Died in Louvain, shortly after learning of the discovery of the cosmic microwave background radiation.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Lemaître studied engineering, mathematics, physics, and philosophy at the Catholic University of Louvain. His studies were interrupted by World War I, during which he served in the Belgian army and was awarded the Belgian War Cross. After the war, he shifted focus to physics and mathematics, earning a doctorate in 1920. He was ordained a priest in 1923, encouraged by his mentor Cardinal Désiré-Joseph Mercier. Post-ordination, he traveled to England to work with astrophysicist Arthur Eddington and then to the United States to work with Harlow Shapley at Harvard and to complete a second doctorate at MIT.

### Cosmological Work and the Big Bang
In 1927, Lemaître published a paper arguing that Einstein's field equations of general relativity implied a non-static, expanding universe. He connected this theoretical prediction to a proportionality between the recessional velocity of galaxies and their distance, a relationship later confirmed by Edwin Hubble in 1929 and now called the Hubble–Lemaître law. In 1931, he proposed that the universe began from a single "primeval atom," an initial quantum that expanded and cooled—the first scientific formulation of the Big Bang theory. This idea was initially met with skepticism, including from Einstein, but gained acceptance after the 1965 discovery of the cosmic microwave background radiation, which Lemaître learned of shortly before his death.

### The Cosmological Constant
Lemaître consistently advocated for including a positive cosmological constant (Λ) in Einstein's equations. He argued it was necessary both for theoretical completeness and to reconcile the calculated age of the universe from the Hubble–Lemaître law with the observed ages of the oldest stars. His advocacy for an accelerating expansion driven by this constant was validated decades after his death by the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics, which credited him with the foundational idea.

### Other Scientific Contributions and Views on Faith
With Manuel Sandoval Vallarta, Lemaître demonstrated that cosmic rays are charged particles deflected by Earth's magnetic field, using early computers for calculations. He also contributed to numerical analysis, developing an early version of the fast Fourier transform. Regarding faith, Lemaître firmly opposed "concordism"—the attempt to merge scientific and theological explanations. He maintained that science and religion are separate domains, a view he upheld even when Pope Pius XII drew parallels between the Big Bang and the doctrine of creation.

## Terms
*   **Hubble–Lemaître law:** The observation that the recessional velocity of galaxies is proportional to their distance from Earth, indicating an expanding universe.
*   **Primeval atom:** Lemaître's term for the initial, singular quantum from which the universe expanded, now known as the Big Bang.
*   **Cosmological constant (Λ):** A term in Einstein's field equations representing a constant energy density filling space homogeneously, which Lemaître argued drives the universe's accelerating expansion.
*   **General theory of relativity:** Einstein's geometric theory of gravitation, which describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy.
*   **Cosmic microwave background (CMB):** The residual thermal radiation from the early universe, discovered in 1965, providing key evidence for the Big Bang theory.
*   **Concordism:** The attempt to reconcile scientific theories with religious scripture, a practice Lemaître eventually rejected.
*   **Friedmann equations:** A set of equations derived from general relativity that describe the expansion of a homogeneous and isotropic universe.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Interpretation of Einstein's praise:** There is disagreement over whether Einstein's reported 1933 comment—"This is the most beautiful and satisfactory explanation of creation"—referenced Lemaître's entire Big Bang theory or only his hypothesis that cosmic rays were "fossils" of the primeval decay.
*   **Omission in translation:** The reason Lemaître removed his original estimate of the "Hubble constant" from the 1931 English translation of his 1927 paper remained unclear for decades until clarified in 2011 as a choice to cite Hubble's stronger, more recent data.
*   **Role of the cosmological constant:** While Lemaître championed a positive Λ, Einstein initially rejected it after abandoning his static universe model. The constant's physical reality as "dark energy" was only confirmed long after both men's deaths.
