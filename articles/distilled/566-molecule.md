# Molecule

## Overview
A molecule is a stable assembly of two or more atoms held together by chemical bonds, typically covalent. It is the fundamental unit of discrete chemical substances, distinct from extended solids like salts, metals, or crystals, which lack identifiable molecular boundaries. The concept evolved from ancient atomic philosophy through 17th-century corpuscular theories to a rigorous quantum mechanical framework in the 20th century, enabling precise prediction of molecular geometry, bonding, and reactivity. Molecular science underpins chemistry, biology, and materials science, governing the structure of life (proteins, DNA) and the behavior of gases, liquids, and synthetic polymers.

## Timeline
- **c. 450 BC** — Greek philosophers (Leucippus, Democritus, Empedocles) propose atomic theory and elemental interactions via attraction/repulsion.
- **1661** — Robert Boyle publishes *The Sceptical Chymist*, hypothesizing matter consists of "corpuscles" that cluster and rearrange during chemical change.
- **1789** — William Higgins publishes views on combinations of "ultimate" particles, foreshadowing valency bond concepts.
- **1811** — Amedeo Avogadro coins "molecule" in *Essay on Determining the Relative Masses of the Elementary Molecules of Bodies*, distinguishing molecular particles from simple atoms in gases.
- **1833** — Marc Antoine Auguste Gaudin uses "volume diagrams" to depict molecular geometries (e.g., linear H₂O) and formulas based on Avogadro's hypothesis.
- **1917** — Linus Pauling encounters Dalton's hook-and-eye bonding; seeks quantum mechanical explanation.
- **1926** — Jean Perrin wins Nobel Prize in Physics for conclusively proving molecular existence via calculation of the Avogadro constant using three methods (emulsion, Brownian motion, Einstein's rotation theory).
- **1927** — Fritz London and Walter Heitler apply quantum mechanics to the hydrogen molecule, introducing valence bond theory and exchange forces.
- **1931** — Linus Pauling publishes "The Nature of the Chemical Bond," developing hybridization theory (e.g., sp³ in CH₄) to explain molecular structure using quantum mechanics.

## Body

### Definition and Classification
A molecule is defined as a group of two or more atoms held together by attractive chemical bonds. It may be **homonuclear** (composed of one element, e.g., O₂) or **heteronuclear** (a chemical compound of multiple elements, e.g., H₂O). In the kinetic theory of gases, the term extends to any gaseous particle, including monatomic noble gases. Crucially, assemblies held by non-covalent interactions (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds) are not considered single molecules. Polyatomic ions (e.g., NH₄⁺) are often treated as molecules in quantum physics and biochemistry, though strictly they are ions.

### Historical Evolution of the Concept
Early definitions viewed molecules as the smallest particles retaining a substance's chemical properties, but this failed for network solids (rocks, metals, salts) which lack discrete molecular units. The modern concept traces to Greek atomism (Leucippus, Democritus) and Empedocles' four elements with forces of attraction/repulsion. Robert Boyle (1661) provided a corpuscular foundation, arguing chemical change results from rearrangement of particle clusters. William Higgins (1789) anticipated valency by assigning force ratios between "ultimate particles." Amedeo Avogadro (1811) formally introduced the term "molecule," proposing gas particles are often atom clusters. Marc Antoine Auguste Gaudin (1833) visualized these concepts with volume diagrams showing molecular geometry and formulas.

### Quantum Mechanical Foundation
The 20th century shifted molecular theory from classical hooks to quantum mechanics. Jean Perrin (1926) empirically proved molecules exist by determining the Avogadro constant via Brownian motion, emulsions, and Einstein's rotation theory. Fritz London and Walter Heitler (1927) applied quantum mechanics to the hydrogen molecule, explaining saturable exchange forces via valence bond theory. Linus Pauling (1931) synthesized this with G.N. Lewis's electron-pair concepts, publishing *The Nature of the Chemical Bond*. He introduced **hybridization theory** (e.g., sp³ orbitals in methane forming four equivalent sigma bonds) to predict bond angles, lengths, and molecular shapes, unifying chemistry under quantum mechanics.

### Molecular Science and Prevalence
Molecular science comprises **molecular chemistry** (bond formation/breakage laws) and **molecular physics** (structure/property laws), though the distinction is vague. Molecules dominate the oceans, atmosphere, and organic matter (proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, lipids). However, most of Earth's solid crust—minerals, rocks, metals, salts, covalent crystals (diamond, quartz), and glasses—consists of extended networks (ionic, metallic, or covalent) without discrete molecules. These are described by repeating **unit cells** or disordered bonding, not molecular formulas.

### Bonding Types
**Covalent bonding** involves sharing electron pairs (bonding pairs) between atoms, creating a stable balance of attractive and repulsive forces; it is the primary force in discrete molecules. **Ionic bonding** involves electrostatic attraction between cations (electron loss) and anions (electron gain), termed electrovalence. It typically forms extended solid lattices (salts) at standard conditions, not discrete molecules, though vaporization can yield molecular ionic species. Metallic bonding in crystals is sometimes analogized to a "giant molecule," but metals behave distinctly from molecular substances.

### Size, Formulas, and Geometry
Molecules range from sub-nanometer (H₂, 0.74 Å bond length) to macroscopic (polymers like DNA). **Empirical formulas** give the simplest integer atom ratio (e.g., CH for acetylene C₂H₂); **molecular formulas** give exact atom counts; **structural formulas** depict 3D connectivity essential for complex stereochemistry. **Molecular geometry**—fixed equilibrium bond lengths and angles—determines properties and reactivity. **Isomers** (same formula, different structure) exhibit vastly different properties; **stereoisomers** may share physical properties but differ in biochemical activity.

### Spectroscopy and Theoretical Aspects
**Molecular spectroscopy** analyzes quantized energy levels via interaction with electromagnetic radiation: microwave (rotation), infrared (vibration), UV/Vis (electronic transitions/color), and nuclear magnetic resonance (nuclear environment). It identifies molecules in space and characterizes bonds and structure. Theoretically, the hydrogen molecular ion (H₂⁺) is the simplest system (one-electron bond), solvable exactly via the Schrödinger equation. Computational chemistry approximates solutions for complex molecules. IUPAC defines a molecule operationally as a depression on the potential energy surface deep enough to confine at least one vibrational state, including weakly bound species like the helium dimer (He₂). Philosophically, a molecule is not a fundamental particle but a chemist's concept describing the strength of atomic-scale interactions.

## Terms
- ****Homonuclear molecule**** — A molecule consisting of atoms of a single chemical element (e.g., O₂).
- ****Heteronuclear molecule**** — A chemical compound composed of atoms of two or more different elements (e.g., H₂O).
- ****Covalent bond**** — A chemical bond formed by the sharing of electron pairs (bonding pairs) between atoms.
- ****Ionic bond**** — A chemical bond resulting from electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions (cations and anions) formed by electron transfer (electrovalence).
- ****Hybridization theory**** — A model (developed by Pauling) where atomic orbitals mix to form hybrid orbitals (e.g., sp³) to explain molecular geometry and equivalent bond strengths.
- ****Empirical formula**** — The simplest integer ratio of elements in a compound (e.g., CH for C₂H₂).
- ****Molecular formula**** — A formula indicating the exact number of each type of atom in a molecule.
- ****Isomers**** — Molecules with the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements, resulting in different properties.
- ****Unit cell**** — The smallest repeating structural unit of a crystal lattice (e.g., in salts, metals, diamond), not a discrete molecule.
- ****Potential energy surface**** — A multidimensional surface representing the energy of a system as a function of atomic positions; a molecule corresponds to a depression deep enough to trap a vibrational state.

## Debates and open questions
- **Boundary of the definition:** Whether metallic crystals constitute "giant molecules" is contested; metals behave differently than discrete molecules.
- **Operational vs. fundamental nature:** IUPAC's energetic criterion (potential energy well depth) includes weakly bound species (e.g., He₂) not traditionally considered molecules, raising philosophical questions about whether "molecule" is a fundamental physical entity or a pragmatic chemical concept.
- **Inclusion of polyatomic ions:** Context-dependent usage persists; quantum physics and biochemistry often treat polyatomic ions as molecules, while strict chemical definitions may exclude them due to charge.
