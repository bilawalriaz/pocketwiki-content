# Atlanta

## Overview
Atlanta is the capital and most populous city of Georgia, serving as a major economic, cultural, and transportation hub of the Southeastern United States. Founded as a railroad terminus in the 1830s, it grew into a strategic Civil War target, was rebuilt as the "New South" capital, and became a pivotal center for the civil rights movement. Today, it is a Beta+ global city with a diverse economy and a population of 498,715 (2020 census).

## Body

### Founding and Early Growth
Atlanta was founded as "Terminus," the endpoint of the state-sponsored Western and Atlantic Railroad. The settlement, later renamed Marthasville and then Atlanta (a feminine form of "Atlantic"), was incorporated in 1847. Its identity was shaped by its role as a railroad nexus, which made it a strategic military hub during the Civil War. The city was captured and largely destroyed by Union forces under General William T. Sherman in 1864.

### Reconstruction and the "New South"
After the war, Atlanta rebounded quickly, aided by its rail network. The state capital relocated there in 1868. In the 1880s, newspaper editor Henry W. Grady promoted Atlanta as the capital of the "New South," an economy based on modern industry and commerce rather than agriculture. This vision was bolstered by the establishment of major educational institutions and the city's hosting of the 1895 Cotton States and International Exposition.

### 20th Century: Growth, Strife, and Civil Rights
The early 20th century saw rapid population growth and the rise of the Sweet Auburn district as a center of Black commerce. This period was also marked by severe racial violence, including the 1906 Atlanta Race Riot and the 1915 lynching of Leo Frank. After World War II, Atlanta became a manufacturing and technology hub. During the 1950s and 1960s, it was a major organizing center for the civil rights movement, led by figures like Martin Luther King Jr. and Ralph Abernathy. Desegregation occurred in stages, with public schools not fully desegregated until 1973. That same year, Maynard Jackson was elected the city's first Black mayor.

### Modern Era: Olympics and Transformation
Atlanta's global profile was elevated by hosting the 1996 Summer Olympics, which spurred significant infrastructure investment and a lasting economic impact. The 21st century has been defined by demographic shifts, with a rebounding white population and a declining Black majority due to suburbanization and gentrification. Major projects like the Beltline—a 22-mile loop converting old rail lines into trails and transit—have reshaped the city's geography and demographics. Atlanta's economy is now diverse, with dominant sectors in transportation, media, film production, and information technology. Hartsfield-Jackson International Airport has been the world's busiest most years since 1998.

### Geography and Cityscape
Situated in the Appalachian foothills at an elevation of 1,050 feet, Atlanta has the highest elevation of major cities east of the Mississippi. It straddles the Eastern Continental Divide. The city is known as the "City in a Forest" for having the densest urban tree canopy of any major U.S. city. Its urban core is defined by three high-rise districts—Downtown, Midtown, and Buckhead—along a north-south axis. The city's architecture is a mix of modern and postmodern skyscrapers, with a history of ambivalent preservation that led to the loss of many historic landmarks.

## Terms
- **Western and Atlantic Railroad**: The state-sponsored railroad whose terminus defined Atlanta's founding and name.
- **New South**: A post-Civil War vision for the region, promoted by Henry Grady, emphasizing industrialization and urbanization over an agrarian economy.
- **Blockbusting**: A discriminatory practice by realtors to manipulate housing markets, often by stoking racial fears, which Atlanta attempted to thwart in the 1960s.
- **Beltline**: A $2.8 billion urban redevelopment project converting a 22-mile freight rail loop into a multi-use trail and transit system.
- **Gentrification**: The process of wealthier people moving into a deteriorating neighborhood, renovating housing, and displacing lower-income residents, a major force in 21st-century Atlanta.
- **Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport**: The world's busiest airport by passenger traffic, a primary economic engine for the city.
- **MARTA (Metropolitan Atlanta Rapid Transit Authority)**: The public transit system serving the city and parts of its metro area via buses and heavy rail.
- **Sweet Auburn**: A historic African-American neighborhood and commercial district that emerged in the early 20th century.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The **Beltline project** has been criticized for its adverse effects on some Black communities, potentially accelerating displacement and inequality despite its benefits.
- Atlanta's **demographic shift** is ongoing, with the Black population declining from a high of 67% in 1990 to 47% in 2020, raising questions about the city's identity as a "Black mecca."
- The city's **transportation model** remains heavily car-dependent, leading to severe traffic congestion, though investments in transit, cycling, and the Beltline aim to provide alternatives.
- **Gentrification**, which began in the 1970s and accelerated after the Olympics, continues to reshape neighborhoods, politics, and culture, presenting both revitalization and displacement challenges.
