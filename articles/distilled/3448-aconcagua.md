# Aconcagua

## Overview
Aconcagua is the highest mountain in the Americas and the highest peak outside Asia, located in the Andes of Argentina. It is a major climbing destination, considered the highest non-technical mountain in the world, but its extreme altitude poses severe risks, including altitude sickness and hypothermia.

## Body

### Geology and Location
Aconcagua is in the Principal Cordillera of the Andes, in Mendoza Province, Argentina, about 112 km northwest of Mendoza and 15 km from the Chilean border. It was formed by the subduction of the Nazca Plate beneath the South American Plate. The mountain was once an active stratovolcano, but volcanic activity halted in the Miocene (c. 8-10 million years ago) when a decrease in subduction angle caused thrust faults to lift the mountain. Its flanks are composed of volcanic rocks like lavas and breccias, while nearby valleys contain colorful sedimentary deposits from an ancient shallow sea.

### Climbing and Routes
The normal route from the north is technically straightforward, requiring no ropes or specialized equipment, making Aconcagua the highest non-technical mountain. However, the extreme altitude (atmospheric pressure is ~40% of sea level at the summit) causes altitude sickness in most climbers. The mountain records about three deaths per year, earning it the nickname "Mountain of Death." Summit success rates are estimated at 30–40%. More demanding routes include the Polish Glacier Traverse and the difficult South Face. About 54% of climbers use the Normal Route, 43% the Polish Glacier Traverse, and 3% other routes.

### History and Records
The first recorded ascent was in 1897 by a British expedition led by Edward FitzGerald, though FitzGerald himself failed to summit on eight attempts. His Swiss guide, Matthias Zurbriggen, reached the top on 14 January. Subsequent first ascents include the east side via the Polish Glacier (1934), the Southwest Ridge (1953), and the South Face (1954). Notable records include the youngest summiteer (Tyler Armstrong, age 9, in 2013), the oldest (Scott Lewis, age 87, in 2007), and speed records for climbing and descending.

### Dangers and Logistics
Despite its non-technical nature, Aconcagua is dangerous due to altitude, cold, and weather. Hypothermia is common. Over 100 people have died since records began. Environmental hazards include improper human waste disposal, creating health risks. Water must be boiled or chemically treated. Waste management is a significant logistical challenge, with over 120 barrels (~22,500 kg) flown out by helicopter each season from base camps. Access to eco-friendly toilets often requires payment to organized services.

### Cultural Significance
For the Incas, Aconcagua was a sacred mountain where worship and sacrifices occurred. In 1985, sites at 5,167 m were discovered, among the highest Inca sites in the world. The remains of a high-status child, along with offerings like coca leaves, were found, indicating ritual activity.

## Terms
- **Stratovolcano:** A conical volcano built up by many layers of hardened lava and tephra.
- **Subduction:** A geological process where one tectonic plate sinks beneath another.
- **Normal Route:** The standard, least technically difficult path to a mountain's summit.
- **Technical Mountain:** A climb requiring specialized equipment like ropes, axes, and pins for safety.
- **Altitude Sickness:** A condition caused by ascending to high elevations too quickly, with symptoms like headache and nausea.
- **Acclimatization:** The process of the body adjusting to decreased oxygen levels at high altitude.
- **Bivouac:** A temporary, open-air shelter or campsite, often without a tent.
- **Polish Glacier:** A prominent glacier on the east face of Aconcagua, used as a climbing route.

## Debates and Open Questions
The origin of the name "Aconcagua" is uncertain, with multiple proposed etymologies from Mapudungun, Quechua, and Aymara languages. The exact summit success rate is an estimate, as park rangers do not maintain official records.
