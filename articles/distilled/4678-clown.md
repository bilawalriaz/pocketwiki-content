# Clown

## Overview
A clown is a performer who uses physical comedy, distinctive makeup, and costume to entertain, often by subverting social norms. The art form, known as clowning, has ancient roots and serves complex socio-religious and psychological functions, evolving from ritual figures to modern circus and children's entertainers. Clowns can evoke a wide range of emotions, from laughter to fear, reflecting their deep cultural significance.

## Body

### History and Origins
Clowning has ancient origins, with the earliest known clowns dating to Egypt around 2400 BC. Traditionally, clown roles often overlapped with priestly duties, serving a socio-religious function. The English word "clown" first appeared c. 1560, referring to a rustic fool. The modern clown character evolved from the *zanni* (rustic fool) characters of Italian commedia dell'arte, which themselves were based on ancient Greek and Roman theater. In 17th-century England, the harlequinade paired a clown with Harlequin. Joseph Grimaldi, in the early 1800s, transformed the clown into the central, mischievous figure of the harlequinade and created the iconic whiteface makeup.

### The Circus Clown
The circus clown tradition developed in the 19th century. Philip Astley added clowns to his equestrian shows in 1768 to amuse audiences between acts. Tom Belling Sr. created the Auguste (or red clown) character c. 1870 as a clumsy, lower-class foil to the sophisticated white clown. This Auguste template, with its red nose and oversized clothes, became the model for the modern circus and children's clown. In 20th-century North America, the tramp or hobo clown emerged, exemplified by performers like Emmett Kelly. The Bozo the Clown character, popular from the 1960s, further defined the standalone children's entertainer, leading to the custom of birthday clowns and hospital clowning.

### Types and Character
Clowns are categorized by makeup and role. The **whiteface clown** is sophisticated and intelligent, with full white makeup. The **Auguste clown** is a clumsy, anarchic fool with a red nose and exaggerated features. A **contra-auguste** often mediates between the two. The **character clown** adopts an eccentric persona, such as a hobo, butcher, or policeman, with makeup that is a comic slant on a human face. The **evil clown** is a horror subversion of the comic trope, popularized by characters like the Joker and Pennywise.

### Performance and Terminology
Clown performances are structured using **frameworks** (general outlines for acts) and a **menu** of act lengths: **entrées** (5-10 minutes), **side dishes** (1-3 minutes), and **clown stops** (brief interludes). Comedy is built from **gags** (short jokes or prop stunts), **bits** (sketches or routines), and **business** (character-expressing motions). Common prop stunts include the squirting flower and the tiny car gag.

## Terms
- **Clowning/Buffoonery:** The art of performing as a clown.
- **Ritual Clown:** A clown character involved in a religious or ritual capacity, common in some indigenous cultures.
- **Whiteface Clown:** A sophisticated clown type with full white makeup, derived from the Pierrot character.
- **Auguste Clown:** A clumsy, anarchic clown type with a red nose and exaggerated features, often a foil to the whiteface.
- **Contra-auguste:** A clown character who mediates between the whiteface and the Auguste.
- **Character Clown:** A clown who adopts an eccentric persona (e.g., hobo, policeman) with human-like makeup.
- **Coulrophobia:** The fear of clowns.
- **Gag:** A very short piece of clown comedy or a prop stunt.
- **Bit:** A clown's sketch or routine, composed of one or more gags.
- **Blow-off:** The comedic ending of a clown segment or routine.
