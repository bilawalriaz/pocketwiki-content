# Hymn

## Overview
A hymn is a song of praise or prayer, typically addressed to a deity or revered figure. The word derives from the Greek *hymnos*. While most familiar in Christianity, hymns are a global phenomenon, with ancient roots in cultures from Sumer and Egypt to India and China. They serve as a primary vehicle for communal worship, theological expression, and personal devotion across many world religions.

## Timeline
- **c. 2600 BC** — The Sumerian *Kesh temple hymn*, the oldest surviving literary text, is composed.
- **c. 1350 BC** — The Egyptian *Great Hymn to the Aten* is composed by Pharaoh Akhenaten.
- **c. 1200 BC** — The *Gathas*, hymns attributed to Zoroaster, are composed.
- **11th–7th c. BC** — Hymns from the Chinese *Classic of Poetry (Shijing)* are compiled.
- **7th c. BC** — The oldest *Homeric Hymns* are written in ancient Greece.
- **3rd c. BC** — The Alexandrian poet Callimachus writes a collection of six literary hymns.
- **4th c. AD** — The Christian hymn *Te Deum* is composed.
- **1532** — The first Protestant hymnal is published in Bohemia by the Unitas Fratrum.
- **18th c.** — Charles Wesley writes thousands of hymns, spreading Methodist theology.
- **19th c.** — The gospel song genre emerges in America with writers like Fanny Crosby.
- **1909** — The *Book of Life*, the first printed hymnal of the Russian Doukhobors, is published.

## Body

### Origins and Definition
The word "hymn" comes from the Greek *hymnos*, meaning "a song of praise." Ancient Eastern examples include the Sumerian *Kesh temple hymn*, the Egyptian *Great Hymn to the Aten*, the Indian *Rigveda*, and the Chinese *Classic of Poetry*. The Western tradition begins with the Greek *Homeric Hymns* (7th century BC). Early Christian writers adopted the term for songs of praise, often using it synonymously with "psalm." A hymn is defined as a reverent, devotional lyric poem designed to be sung, expressing worship and unifying a congregation.

### Christian Hymnody
Christian hymns, modeled on but distinct from biblical psalms, are directed to God and often reference Jesus Christ. The New Testament encourages singing "psalms and hymns and spiritual songs." Early non-scriptural hymns still in use include *Phos Hilaron* and *Te Deum*. The Protestant Reformation sparked two approaches: the **regulative principle**, which favored exclusive psalmody and banned non-biblical hymns and instruments, and the **normative principle**, which produced a burst of hymn-writing, led by figures like Martin Luther. Isaac Watts is called the "liberator of English hymnody" for writing the first hymns not direct paraphrases of Scripture. Charles Wesley's hymns spread Methodist theology and influenced the later gospel song genre, which typically includes a refrain and a faster tempo than traditional hymns.

### Music and Accompaniment
Early Christian hymns were likely sung a cappella. The Middle Ages saw the development of Gregorian chant, sung in unison. The Western church later adopted four-part harmony, organ accompaniment, and classical forms. Eastern Orthodox traditions generally use a cappella chant with a drone (*ison*), excluding instruments. Some Protestant groups, like the Churches of Christ, maintain a cappella worship, citing early church practice. The 19th century in America saw the rise of shape-note singing and tune-books like *Sacred Harp*, which fostered a participatory, congregational tradition that persists today.

### Hymn Meters
Hymn meter denotes the syllable count and stress pattern of each stanza, allowing texts to be paired with appropriate tunes. Common meters include **Long Meter (L.M.)** (88.88), **Short Meter (S.M.)** (66.86), and **Common Meter (C.M.)** (86.86). A tune's meter must match the text's meter and stress pattern for proper singing.

### Hymnody in Other Religions
Hymns are central to many faiths. In Hinduism, the *Rigveda* is a foundational collection of Vedic hymns, and the medieval **bhakti** movements produced devotional songs (*bhajans*, *kirtans*) in local languages. The Sikh holy scripture, the *Guru Granth Sahib*, is a collection of hymns (*Shabad*) set to musical *ragas*. In Judaism, *Zemirot* are Sabbath table songs, and *piyyutim* are liturgical hymns. In Islam, devotional music ranges from Gulf *nasheed* to South Asian *qawwali*. In Buddhism, sutra chanting is a daily practice, and *stotras* (hymns of praise) are used in Nepal. Zoroastrianism's sacred songs are the *Gathas* of Zoroaster.

## Terms
- **Hymnody**: The singing or composition of hymns; also refers to the body of hymns of a particular group or era.
- **Hymnal**: A collection of hymns, often including music, used for congregational singing.
- **Hymn tune**: The music to which a hymn text is set.
- **Meter**: The pattern of syllables and stresses in a hymn stanza (e.g., Common Meter).
- **Stotra**: A Sanskrit hymn or eulogy in praise of the divine, common in Hindu and Jain traditions.
- **Bhajan**: A Hindu devotional song, often simple and in a local language.
- **Gospel song**: A subgenre of Christian hymn with a refrain and often a faster tempo, associated with revivalism.
- **Shape notes**: A notation system using shaped noteheads to facilitate sight-singing, popular in 19th-century American tune-books.
- **A cappella**: Singing without instrumental accompaniment.

## Debates and Open Questions
A central historical debate was the **regulative vs. normative principle** of worship during the Reformation, determining whether non-biblical hymns and instruments were permissible. A modern debate concerns the classification of **contemporary worship music** and **gospel songs** as "hymns," with purists arguing for a distinction based on style and theological content. The "secularization" of hymns for use in non-religious settings is also noted.
