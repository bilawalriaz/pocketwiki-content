# 1556 Shaanxi earthquake

## Overview
The 1556 Shaanxi earthquake, also known as the Jiajing Great Earthquake, was one of the deadliest in recorded history. It struck the Weihe Basin in China's Ming dynasty, causing catastrophic destruction and a massive death toll, largely due to the collapse of yaodongs (artificial loess caves) where much of the population lived.

## Timeline
- **23 January 1556** — The earthquake occurs in the early morning, with its epicenter in the Wei River Valley near Huaxian, Shaanxi.
- **1556** — The scholar Qin Keda survives and records details of the earthquake.
- **1556** — Aftershocks continue several times a month for half a year.
- **1556** — The earthquake damages the Forest of Stone steles, breaking 40 of the 114 Kaicheng Stone Classics.
- **1556** — The Small Wild Goose Pagoda in Xi'an is reduced in height by three levels.
- **1569** — The Portuguese friar Gaspar da Cruz reports on the earthquake in his book *A Treatise of China*.

## Body

### Tectonic Setting and Geology
The earthquake occurred in the Weihe Basin, a rift basin forming the southern boundary of the Ordos Block. This basin, active since the Neogene, is bounded by large normal faults like the Huashan and North Qinling Faults, which are responsible for historical seismicity. Modern estimates place the earthquake's magnitude at approximately Mw 7.9–8.0, with a catastrophic intensity of XI on the Mercalli scale. The rupture likely occurred along the Huashan and Weinan faults, with geological studies identifying fault scarps potentially produced by the event, though their exact origin is debated.

### Impact and Destruction
The shaking was felt across a vast area, affecting over 97 counties in ten provinces. In the epicentral region, destruction was total; Huaxian lost more than half its population. The ground deformed dramatically, with 20-meter-deep crevices opening, new hills and valleys forming, and streams bursting forth. The primary cause of the extreme death toll was the collapse of yaodongs—artificial caves dug into loess cliffs—which buried inhabitants. Landslides triggered by the quake further contributed to the devastation.

### Death Toll and Aftermath
Modern estimates from the China Earthquake Administration (CEA) attribute roughly 100,000 direct deaths to the quake. An additional 730,000 people either died from subsequent famine and plagues or migrated, resulting in a total population reduction of 830,000 in imperial records. Some CEA scientists have offered higher estimates of 450,000 to 530,000 total deaths. The disaster prompted foreign accounts, such as that of Gaspar da Cruz, who interpreted it as divine punishment.

## Terms
- **Yaodongs**: Artificial caves dug into loess cliffs, serving as common dwellings in the Loess Plateau region.
- **Loess**: A wind-deposited, silty sediment that forms thick, easily eroded deposits, characteristic of the region.
- **Weihe Basin**: A rift basin in central China, part of the active Weihe-Shanxi Rift System.
- **Normal Fault**: A fracture in the Earth's crust where the hanging wall moves downward relative to the footwall, typical of extensional tectonics.
- **Moment Magnitude Scale (Mw)**: A measure of an earthquake's size based on the seismic moment, used for large events.
- **Mercalli Scale**: A scale that rates earthquake intensity based on observed effects and damage.
- **Hukou**: The system of household registration used in imperial China for census and tax purposes.

## Debates and Open Questions
The exact magnitude of the earthquake is debated. While older estimates placed it at Mw 8.0, more recent analysis of the 90 km rupture length along the Huashan and Weinan faults suggests a magnitude of Mw 7.0–7.5, indicating previous values may have been overestimated. Furthermore, it is not confirmed whether the observed fault scarps were produced solely by the 1556 event or formed by multiple seismic events over time.
