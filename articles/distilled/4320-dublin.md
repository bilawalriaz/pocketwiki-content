# Dublin

## Overview
Dublin is the capital and largest city of Ireland, situated on Dublin Bay at the mouth of the River Liffey. It is the country's primary economic, political, and cultural centre, with a history of settlement dating back millennia. Its development from a Viking trading post to a modern global city reflects Ireland's complex political and social evolution.

## Timeline
- **c. 140 AD** — Ptolemy's map references a settlement, *Eblana polis*, near Dublin.
- **c. 841** — Vikings establish a fortified base, which becomes a major commercial centre.
- **1169** — Norman invasion of Ireland begins, leading to English control.
- **1204** — Dublin Castle is founded on the orders of King John of England.
- **1592** — Trinity College is established by Queen Elizabeth I.
- **1759** — The Guinness brewery is founded at St. James's Gate.
- **1800** — Acts of Union transfer the Irish Parliament to Westminster, beginning a period of decline.
- **1916** — The Easter Rising causes significant destruction in central Dublin.
- **1922** — Dublin becomes the capital of the newly formed Irish Free State.
- **1988** — Dublin celebrates its official millennium, marking the year 988 as its founding.
- **2004** — The Luas light rail system is launched.
- **2010** — The Aviva Stadium opens on the site of the old Lansdowne Road stadium.

## Body

### Etymology and Early Settlement
The name Dublin derives from the Middle Irish *Duibhlinn*, meaning "black pool," referring to a tidal pool where the River Poddle entered the Liffey. The Viking settlement, *Dyflin*, took its name from this pool. A separate Gaelic ecclesiastical settlement, *Áth Cliath* ("ford of hurdles"), existed further upriver. The modern Irish name, *Baile Átha Cliath*, combines these elements. The Irish government recognises 988 as the city's founding year.

### Medieval and Early Modern History
Vikings established a fortified base in 841, which grew into a major commercial centre. The Norman invasion in 1169 brought English control, with Dublin Castle (founded 1204) becoming the centre of Anglo-Norman power. The city was the heart of the "Pale," the area of direct English control. The Tudor and Stuart periods saw Dublin become the administrative centre of English rule. The 18th century was a period of great prosperity and Georgian architectural expansion, making Dublin briefly the second-largest city in the British Empire.

### 19th Century to Independence
Following the Acts of Union in 1800, which dissolved the Irish Parliament, Dublin entered a period of political and economic decline. The city did not industrialise like Belfast. The Easter Rising of 1916, the War of Independence, and the Civil War caused major physical destruction. After the partition of Ireland in 1922, Dublin became the capital of the Irish Free State, later renamed Ireland in 1937.

### Modern Dublin
From the 1990s, Dublin was at the forefront of Ireland's "Celtic Tiger" economic boom, attracting major international technology and financial services companies. This led to significant redevelopment, particularly in the Docklands. The city faces contemporary challenges, notably a severe housing supply crisis. As of 2024, its GDP is €253.6 billion, making it one of the largest city economies in the EU.

### Government and Geography
Dublin is governed by the unicameral Dublin City Council. It is the seat of the Irish national parliament, the Oireachtas, which meets in Leinster House. The city is divided by the River Liffey and bordered by the Dublin Mountains to the south. Its climate is maritime, with mild temperatures and relatively low rainfall for Ireland. The city centre is encircled by the Royal and Grand Canals.

### Economy and Transport
The Dublin region is Ireland's economic centre, hosting the European headquarters of many global technology and finance firms. The city is a major transport hub. Dublin Airport is the busiest on the island. The road network is focused on the M50 motorway. Public transport includes the Dublin Bus network, the Luas light rail, and the DART commuter rail. Cycling has increased in popularity, supported by the Dublinbikes rental scheme.

### Culture and Demographics
Dublin has a rich literary heritage, producing Nobel laureates like Yeats, Shaw, and Beckett, and is a UNESCO City of Literature. The city's cultural institutions include the Abbey Theatre and the National Museum of Ireland. Demographically, the city is diverse, with significant immigrant populations from the EU and beyond. The 2022 census recorded a city council area population of 592,713 and a city and suburbs population of 1,263,219.

## Terms
*   **The Pale:** A region of direct English control in medieval Ireland, centred on Dublin.
*   **Celtic Tiger:** A period of rapid economic growth in Ireland from the mid-1990s to the late 2000s.
*   **Oireachtas:** The national parliament of Ireland, comprising the President and two houses: Dáil Éireann and Seanad Éireann.
*   **Luas:** Dublin's light rail (tram) system, launched in 2004.
*   **DART:** The Dublin Area Rapid Transit, an electrified commuter rail line running along the coast.
*   **Gaelscoil:** An Irish-language medium primary school.
*   **Gaelcholáiste:** An Irish-language medium secondary school.
*   **IFSC:** The International Financial Services Centre, a major hub for global financial firms established in 1987.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The precise location and nature of the earliest pre-Viking settlements in the Dublin area are subjects of ongoing archaeological investigation.
*   The extent and impact of the Viking slave trade on Dublin's early economic growth is noted by historians but remains a topic of scholarly discussion.
*   The historical north-south cultural and economic divide within the city, while less pronounced, continues to be a subject of social analysis.
*   The severity of the current housing crisis and its impact on the city's social fabric and economic competitiveness is a major ongoing public and political debate.
