# Lake Tanganyika

## Overview
Lake Tanganyika is an ancient African Great Lake, the world's second-largest freshwater lake by volume and second-deepest. It is a vital ecological and economic resource, hosting extraordinary biodiversity and providing a major source of protein for millions of people in the four countries that share it.

## Timeline
- **9–12 Mya** — Formation of the central basin of Lake Tanganyika begins.
- **7–8 Mya** — The northern basin forms.
- **2–4 Mya** — The southern basin forms.
- **c. 10,000 years ago** — The Ruzizi River, a major inflow, forms.
- **Mid-19th century** — Swahili Arab traders establish caravan routes to the lake.
- **1858** — British explorers Richard Burton and John Speke become the first known Westerners to find the lake.
- **1866–73** — David Livingstone explores the lake's shores.
- **1871** — Henry Morton Stanley meets David Livingstone at Ujiji on the lake's eastern shore.
- **Late 19th century** — Colonial powers (Britain, Germany, Belgium) establish territories around the lake.
- **1915–16** — The Battle for Lake Tanganyika occurs during World War I.
- **Mid-1950s** — Major commercial fishing begins on the lake.
- **1990s–present** — The lake's water level rises by about 2 metres, flooding shoreline areas.

## Body

### Geography and Hydrology
Lake Tanganyika is an ancient rift lake located in the Albertine Rift. It is the largest rift lake in Africa and holds about 16% of the world's available surface fresh water. The lake is long and narrow, with a mean depth of 572 m and a maximum depth of 1,471 m. Its water is alkaline (pH ~9 at the surface) and stratified, meaning layers do not mix easily. The deep water is anoxic (lacking oxygen) and contains hydrogen sulfide, limiting aerobic life to the upper ~100–250 m. The lake's outflow via the Lukuga River into the Congo system is intermittent, dependent on high water levels; it is often described as "practically endorheic" (having no outflow). Over 90% of its water comes from direct rainfall on its surface.

### Biology and Endemism
The lake is a hotspot for speciation and adaptive radiation, particularly among its fish. It is home to at least 250 endemic cichlid species, representing the most morphologically and genetically diverse cichlid assemblage in Africa due to the lake's great age. These cichlids occupy diverse ecological niches, from herbivores to piscivores. The pelagic zone is dominated by two endemic "Tanganyika sardine" species, which are prey for four endemic *Lates* (perch-like) species. The lake also hosts unique invertebrate communities, including 65 endemic snail species (many with thick, "marine-like" shells) and over 200 crustacean species, with high endemism in crabs and shrimp.

### Human Use and History
The lake supports a major fishery providing 25–60% of the animal protein for the regional population. Commercial fishing, which began in the mid-1950s, has contributed to declines in fish populations. Historically, the lake was a trade route for Swahili Arab traders. It was "discovered" by Western explorers in 1858 and later became a colonial territory. During World War I, it was the site of a notable naval campaign where British forces transported two motor boats overland to challenge German control.

## Terms
- **Endemic**: A species found only in a specific geographic location and nowhere else.
- **Adaptive Radiation**: The rapid diversification of a lineage into many new species with different adaptations.
- **Stratified**: A lake with distinct horizontal layers of water that do not mix, often due to temperature or density differences.
- **Anoxic**: Lacking dissolved oxygen.
- **Endorheic**: A drainage basin that retains water and allows no outflow to other external bodies of water.
- **Thalassoid**: Describing freshwater snails with thick, sculptured shells resembling those of marine snails.
- **Benthic**: Relating to the bottom of a body of water.
- **Pelagic**: Relating to the open water of a lake or ocean, away from the shore.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise historical patterns of the lake's inflows and outflows are debated, with proposals that it once had connections to Lake Rukwa, Lake Malawi, or the Nile.
- The taxonomic classification of Tanganyika's cichlids is debated, with proposals to split them into 12–16 tribes rather than the more commonly cited 5–10.
- The survival mechanisms of certain deep-water cichlid genera in virtually anoxic zones are unclear.
