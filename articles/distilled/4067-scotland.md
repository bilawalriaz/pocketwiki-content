# Scotland

## Overview
Scotland is a country that forms part of the United Kingdom, occupying the northern third of Great Britain and its adjacent islands. With a population of about 5.4 million, it has a distinct legal system, education system, and cultural identity. Its history as an independent kingdom, its 1707 union with England, and the modern devolution of powers to its own Parliament are central to understanding its contemporary political and social landscape.

## Body

### History and Government
The Kingdom of Scotland was established in the 9th century. Its independence was maintained through conflicts with England, notably the Wars of Scottish Independence in the early 14th century. A personal union of the crowns occurred in 1603, leading to the 1707 Treaty of Union, which merged the Scottish and English parliaments into the Parliament of Great Britain. This union faced significant opposition, including Jacobite risings. The 20th century saw a movement for devolution, culminating in the Scotland Act 1998, which re-established a devolved Scottish Parliament and Government with powers over most domestic policy. The UK Parliament remains sovereign over reserved matters like defence and foreign policy.

### Geography and Climate
Scotland's mainland is divided into the mountainous Highlands, the flatter Central Lowlands, and the hilly Southern Uplands. It has an estimated 900 islands, with major groups including the Hebrides, Orkney, and Shetland. The climate is temperate and oceanic, with milder winters and cooler summers than similar latitudes due to the Gulf Stream. The west is generally warmer and wetter than the east. Climate change is a significant concern, with Scotland aiming for carbon neutrality by 2045.

### Economy and Infrastructure
Scotland has a mixed economy with strong financial services, particularly in Edinburgh. Key industries include whisky production, tourism, and renewable energy. Historically an industrial powerhouse in shipbuilding and engineering, the economy has shifted towards services. Infrastructure includes five international airports, a rail network managed by the Scottish Government, and ferry services to the islands. Scotland generates a high proportion of its electricity from renewable sources.

### Demographics and Languages
The population is concentrated in the Central Belt. The main languages are English and Scots, with Scottish Gaelic spoken by less than 2% of the population, primarily in the Hebrides. Both Scots and Gaelic were given official status in 2025. The population has grown through a combination of natural increase and immigration, with significant historical emigration.

### Culture and Identity
Scottish culture is marked by distinct traditions in literature, music (including bagpipes), and sport. Golf originated in Scotland. National symbols include the saltire (St. Andrew's Cross), the thistle, and the unicorn. The national day is St. Andrew's Day (30 November). A distinct Scottish identity persists despite the political union, supported by institutions like the Scottish Parliament and legal system.

## Terms
- **Devolution**: The transfer of certain powers from the UK Parliament to a regional parliament, like the Scottish Parliament.
- **Auld Alliance**: A 1295 treaty between Scotland and France, primarily to deter English invasion.
- **Highland Clearances**: The 18th and 19th-century displacement of many Highland inhabitants to make way for sheep farming.
- **Jacobitism**: Political support for the exiled Catholic Stuart dynasty after 1688.
- **Scots Law**: Scotland's distinct legal system, combining civil and common law traditions.
- **Makar**: The national poet of Scotland, appointed to promote literacy and poetry.
- **Caledonia**: The Latin name used by Roman historians for the area north of the River Forth.
- **Shetland and Orkney**: Island groups acquired from Norway in 1472, with distinct cultural histories.

## Debates and Open Questions
The central ongoing debate is over Scottish independence. The 2014 referendum resulted in a "No" vote, but the issue remains prominent, especially following the UK's exit from the European Union, which Scotland largely voted against. The legality and timing of a potential second independence referendum are contested between the Scottish and UK governments.
