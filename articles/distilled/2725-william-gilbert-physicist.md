# William Gilbert (physicist)

## Overview
William Gilbert (1544?–1603) was an English physician and natural philosopher whose 1600 book *De Magnete* established the experimental study of magnetism and electricity. He argued that Earth itself is a giant magnet, explaining compass behavior, and pioneered the term "electricity." His work marked a shift from Aristotelian philosophy to empirical science, influencing later figures like Galileo and Kepler.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Gilbert was born to a borough recorder in Colchester and educated at St John's College, Cambridge. After earning his MD, he practiced medicine in London and traveled abroad. He became a Fellow of the Royal College of Physicians in 1573 and its President in 1600. From 1601 until her death, he served as physician to Elizabeth I, a role renewed by James VI and I.

### *De Magnete* and Magnetic Research
Gilbert’s major work, *De Magnete* (1600), synthesized earlier studies (like Robert Norman’s) and presented his own experiments. Using a spherical model Earth called a *terrella*, he demonstrated that Earth’s magnetism, not the pole star or a mythical magnetic island, causes compasses to point north. He argued Earth’s core is iron and that magnets can be divided into smaller magnets. He also described creating magnets by hammering heated iron aligned with Earth’s poles, though a 2011 Royal Society study found this method unreliable.

### Contributions to Astronomy and Electricity
In *De Magnete*, Gilbert supported Earth’s diurnal rotation, arguing it was more plausible than the daily rotation of immense celestial spheres. He suggested stars are at varying distances, not fixed on a sphere. He coined the term "electricity" from the Greek *elektron* (amber) to describe the attractive force from rubbed amber, inventing the first electroscope (a pivoted needle called a *versorium*). He believed electricity and magnetism were distinct, noting electrical attraction vanished with heat—a view later overturned by Ørsted and Maxwell.

### Later Work and Legacy
Gilbert’s posthumous *De Mundo* (1651) proposed a new natural philosophy, possibly edited by his brother or John Gruter. It suggested magnetic forces influenced lunar motion and tides, an idea Kepler later adapted. His experimental approach was praised by contemporaries like Thomas Thomson and William Whewell, though Francis Bacon criticized him for over-attributing phenomena to magnetism. Gilbert died in 1603 and was buried in Colchester.

## Terms
- **Terrella**: A spherical model of Earth used by Gilbert to study magnetism.
- **Electroscope**: An instrument Gilbert invented to detect static electricity, using a pivoted needle.
- **Electricus**: Neo-Latin term coined by Gilbert meaning "like amber," the root of "electricity."
- **Effluvium**: Gilbert’s term for an invisible substance emitted by electrically charged objects.
- **Diurnal rotation**: The daily spinning of Earth on its axis, which Gilbert argued caused the apparent motion of the sky.
- **Magnetomotive force**: A measure of magnetic field strength; the unit *gilbert* was named in his honor.
- **Aristotelian philosophy**: The dominant medieval worldview based on Aristotle’s ideas, which Gilbert rejected.
- **Scholastic method**: A medieval teaching style based on authority and logic, which Gilbert opposed.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Earth’s core composition**: Gilbert argued it was iron based on magnetism, but this remains debated.
- **Magnetism vs. electricity**: Gilbert saw them as separate; later science unified them as electromagnetism.
- **Gilbert’s influence on Galileo**: Some note Gilbert argued for Earth’s rotation 20 years before Galileo, though Copernicus stated it earlier.
- **Authorship of *De Mundo***: It is unclear whether Gilbert’s brother or John Gruter edited the posthumous work.
- **Cause of death**: Gilbert likely died of bubonic plague, but this is not certain.
