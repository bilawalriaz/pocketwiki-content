# I Ching

## Overview
The I Ching, or *Book of Changes*, is an ancient Chinese divination text and one of the oldest Chinese classics. Originally a Western Zhou dynasty manual for cleromancy (divination by casting lots), it evolved into a profound cosmological and philosophical work. Its 64 hexagrams, representing states of change, have been interpreted for millennia as a guide to moral decision-making and understanding the universe, influencing Confucianism, Taoism, and East Asian thought.

## Timeline
- **c. 1000–750 BC** — The core divination text, the *Zhouyi* ("Changes of Zhou"), is compiled during the Western Zhou period.
- **c. 827–782 BC** — Sinologist Edward Shaughnessy dates the compilation of the *Zhouyi* in its current form to the reign of King Xuan of Zhou.
- **c. 475–221 BC** — The text is used throughout Chinese society during the Warring States period, though with small variations.
- **c. 300 BC** — The *Great Commentary* (*Dazhuan*), the most important of the Ten Wings, is written, reinterpreting the text cosmologically.
- **136 BC** — Emperor Wu of Han canonizes the *Zhouyi* as the first of the Five Classics, dubbing it the *I Ching*.
- **3rd century AD** — Scholar Wang Bi integrates the philosophy of the Ten Wings into the central text, creating a dominant interpretive tradition.
- **11th century** — Neo-Confucian scholar Shao Yong rearranges hexagrams in a binary sequence, later inspiring Gottfried Leibniz.
- **12th century** — Zhu Xi synthesizes Han commentaries, reconstructs yarrow stalk divination, and establishes the standard for centuries.
- **1557** — Korean philosopher Yi Hwang produces an influential study, critiquing Neo-Confucian interpretations.
- **1703** — Gottfried Leibniz writes the first European commentary, arguing the hexagrams prove the universality of binary numbers.
- **1923** — Richard Wilhelm's influential German translation is published, later translated into English (1950).
- **1973** — An alternative hexagram order is discovered in the Mawangdui silk texts.

## Body

### The Divination Text: *Zhouyi*
The core of the I Ching is the *Zhouyi*, a divination manual from the Western Zhou dynasty. Its basic unit is the hexagram, a figure of six stacked broken (yin) or solid (yang) lines. The received text contains all 64 possible hexagrams, each with a name, a short hexagram statement, and six line statements. These statements, often cryptic, were used to interpret results from cleromancy, a method using yarrow stalks to produce numbers (6-9) that correspond to specific lines. The *Zhouyi* was traditionally attributed to cultural heroes like King Wen and the Duke of Zhou, but modern scholarship considers its authorship unknown.

### The Classic: I Ching and the Ten Wings
In 136 BC, the *Zhouyi* was canonized as the *I Ching*, part of the Five Classics. This version bound the original text to a set of ten philosophical commentaries called the Ten Wings, likely written around 300 BC. The most important, the *Great Commentary*, reinterpreted the I Ching as a microcosm of the universe, linking it to concepts like yin-yang and the Tao. Although traditionally attributed to Confucius, this association is no longer accepted. The Ten Wings transformed the text from a divination manual into a foundational work for Confucian and Taoist cosmology.

### Hexagrams and Interpretation
The hexagrams are arranged in the traditional King Wen sequence, which generally pairs hexagrams with their upside-down equivalents. The I Ching has been interpreted through various schools of thought. During the Eastern Han, New Text scholars focused on symbolic and numerological parallels, while Old Text scholars emphasized moral content. The most influential interpretation came from Wang Bi (3rd century), who integrated the Ten Wings' philosophy directly into the text. Later, Neo-Confucian Zhu Xi (12th century) synthesized these approaches, viewing the I Ching as a tool for moral self-cultivation.

### Influence Across East Asia and the West
The I Ching became a foundational text across East Asia. In Korea, philosopher Yi Hwang (1557) produced influential studies. In Japan, over 1,000 books on the I Ching were published during the Edo period, with scholars using it to interpret Western science. In Europe, Gottfried Leibniz (1703) saw the hexagrams as proof of binary numbers and theism. In the 20th century, the I Ching influenced Western counterculture, psychology (Carl Jung), and physics (Niels Bohr), while modern scholarship has applied new archaeological and philological rigor to its study.

## Terms
- **Cleromancy**: A form of divination using seemingly random numbers, as produced by manipulating yarrow stalks.
- **Hexagram**: A figure composed of six stacked horizontal lines, each either broken (yin) or solid (yang), representing a state of change.
- **Trigram**: A figure composed of three lines (broken or solid), of which there are eight. Hexagrams are formed by combining two trigrams.
- **Ten Wings**: A set of ten philosophical commentaries bound to the *Zhouyi* in the Han dynasty, transforming it into the *I Ching*.
- **Yin and Yang**: Complementary forces in Chinese cosmology, represented by broken (yin) and solid (yang) lines in the I Ching.
- **King Wen sequence**: The traditional order of the 64 hexagrams in the received *I Ching*.
- **New Text / Old Text**: Two rival schools of Han dynasty I Ching interpretation; New Text focused on symbolism, Old Text on moral meaning.
- **Neo-Confucianism**: A revival of Confucian philosophy during the Song dynasty, which integrated metaphysical concepts from the I Ching.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authorship and Dating**: The exact date and authorship of the *Zhouyi* are debated. Scholars like Shaughnessy propose a 9th-century BC date, while others suggest a range from the 10th to 4th centuries BC.
- **Function of Statements**: The reasons for having both hexagram statements and line statements, and how they were used in practice, are not known.
- **Hexagram Order**: The origin and significance of the King Wen sequence are uncertain. Alternative orders, like the Mawangdui arrangement, have been discovered.
- **Interpretation**: The meaning of the cryptic line statements has been debated for centuries, with modern "modernist" scholars proposing readings that often differ from traditional interpretations.
- **Role in Scientific Development**: There is debate over whether the I Ching's cosmological framework impeded or inspired scientific thought in China and the West.
