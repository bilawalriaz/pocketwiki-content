# Marlene Dietrich

## Overview
Marie Magdalene "Marlene" Dietrich (1901–1992) was a German-born actress and singer who became an American citizen and a defining icon of 20th-century cinema and cabaret. Her collaboration with director Josef von Sternberg at Paramount Pictures (1930–1935) established the "femme fatale" archetype through a unique fusion of lighting, costume, and androgynous glamour. During World War II, she renounced her German citizenship, sold more war bonds than any other star, and performed for Allied troops on front lines in Europe and North Africa, earning the U.S. Medal of Freedom and French Légion d'honneur. After her film career waned in the late 1930s, she reinvented herself as a highly paid, globally touring cabaret artist (1950s–1970s), refining a theatrical one-woman show with musical arranger Burt Bacharach. Her legacy endures as a symbol of gender-fluid fashion, political resistance to Nazism, and the constructed nature of stardom.

## Body

### Early Life and Career Beginnings
Dietrich was born into a bourgeois Berlin family; her father, a police lieutenant, died in 1916, and his friend Eduard von Losch married her mother but died in 1917 without adopting the children. A wrist injury ended her violin studies, leading to a brief stint in a cinema pit orchestra (1922) and chorus work in vaudeville and Max Reinhardt’s theatres. She debuted in film with *The Little Napoleon* (1923) and married Rudolf Sieber the same year; their daughter Maria was born in 1924. Throughout the 1920s, she balanced stage roles in Shakespeare, Wedekind, and Shaw with growing film parts in silents like *Café Elektric* (1927) and *The Ship of Lost Souls* (1929).

### The von Sternberg Collaboration (1930–1935)
*The Blue Angel* (1930), UFA’s first major sound film, made Dietrich an international star. Von Sternberg brought her to Paramount to counter MGM’s Greta Garbo. Over seven films, he sculpted her image through rigorous weight loss, acting coaching, and a signature visual style: lighting through veils and blinds, elaborate set design, and costumes that coded her as a glamorous, mysterious *femme fatale*. *Morocco* (1930) featured her iconic male drag performance and same-sex kiss, earning her only Oscar nomination. *Shanghai Express* (1932) grossed $1.5 million worldwide. Critics debate the authorship of this "Dietrich image"—von Sternberg claimed discovery; Dietrich’s later autonomy suggests partnership—but neither reached comparable heights after Paramount fired von Sternberg in 1935. The seven-film actress-director run remains nearly unique in cinema history.

### Hollywood Interregnum and Wartime Turn (1936–1945)
Post-von Sternberg, Dietrich made *Desire* (1936) with Frank Borzage, then endured the aborted *I Loved a Soldier*. High salaries from David O. Selznick (*The Garden of Allah*, 1936) and Alexander Korda (*Knight Without Armour*, 1937) made her one of the best-paid stars, but costly productions and declining popularity led exhibitors to label her "box office poison" (1938). In London (1937), she rejected Nazi recruitment offers, applied for U.S. citizenship, and placed her $450,000 Korda salary in escrow for refugees. After a failed Paramount return (*Angel*, 1937), Joe Pasternak cast her against type as saloon girl Frenchie in *Destry Rides Again* (1939), reviving her career with the hit "See What the Boys in the Back Room Will Have."

Following Pearl Harbor, Dietrich became a tireless war worker: she sold war bonds to 250,000 troops on a 1942–43 U.S. tour, then undertook two USO tours (1944–45) performing near front lines in Algeria, Italy, France, and Germany with Generals Gavin and Patton. Her revue included film songs, musical saw, and an Orson Welles–taught "mindreading" act. For the OSS "Musak" project, she recorded German-language songs (notably "Lili Marleen") to demoralize Wehrmacht soldiers; OSS chief William Donovan personally thanked her. She vouched for her sister Elisabeth—who ran a cinema for Nazi officers at Bergen-Belsen—sparing her prosecution, then disowned her entirely, later claiming to be an only child.

### Cabaret Reinvention and Global Touring (1950s–1975)
With film roles diminishing (notable exceptions: *A Foreign Affair* 1948, *Witness for the Prosecution* 1957, *Touch of Evil* 1958, *Judgment at Nuremberg* 1961), Dietrich pivoted to live performance. A 1953 Sahara Hotel (Las Vegas) engagement at $30,000/week, featuring Jean Louis’s sheer "nude dress," launched a two-decade cabaret career. Burt Bacharach became her arranger (mid-1950s–1964), expanding her repertoire beyond film songs to contemporary material and disguising her limited contralto range through dramatic arrangements; they recorded four albums. His departure devastated her: "I felt like giving everything up. I had lost my director, my support, my teacher, my maestro."

Her act split personas: body-hugging gowns and swansdown coat for "feminine" numbers; top hat and tails for male-associated songs ("One for My Baby"). She maintained her image via corsetry, tape facelifts, wigs, and precise lighting. The 1960 West Germany tour was an artistic triumph but financial failure; protesters chanted "Marlene Go Home!" while Mayor Willy Brandt welcomed her. In Israel, she sang Pete Seeger’s "Where Have All the Flowers Gone?" in German (1962), breaking a cultural taboo, and received the Medallion of Valor (1965). She earned a Special Tony Award (1968) for Broadway runs. Health declined in her 60s/70s: cervical cancer (1965), circulatory issues, painkiller/alcohol dependence, a 1973 stage fall requiring skin grafts, and a 1974 leg fracture. Her final performance was September 1975; a Sydney fall weeks later broke her thigh, ending her career.

### Paris Years and Death
Dietrich retreated to 12 Avenue Montaigne, Paris, bedridden for 13 years. She wrote prolifically (autobiography *Nehmt nur mein Leben*, 1979) and maintained phone contact with leaders (Reagan, Gorbachev, Thatcher). She participated in Maximilian Schell’s documentary *Marlene* (1984) via audio only. She died 6 May 1992 of kidney failure. Her Paris funeral at La Madeleine drew 1,500 mourners; her coffin bore the French flag, U.S. Medal of Freedom, and Légion d'honneur. Per her will, she was buried in Berlin’s Städtischer Friedhof III near her mother and birthplace. Her estate—3,000+ textiles, 15,000 photos, 300,000 document pages—was sold to Berlin for $5 million (1993), forming the Filmmuseum Berlin core collection.

### Personal Life and Sexuality
Dietrich was bisexual, frequenting 1920s Berlin gay bars (Mali und Igel) and boxing at Sabri Mahir’s studio. Married to Sieber until his 1976 death, she had numerous overlapping affairs (Gary Cooper, John Gilbert, Douglas Fairbanks Jr., James Stewart, Erich Maria Remarque, Jean Gabin—"probably the love of her life," Joseph P. Kennedy Sr., Yul Brynner, John F. Kennedy, John Wayne, Kirk Douglas, Frank Sinatra, and women including Mercedes de Acosta, Suzanne Baulé/Frede, and possibly Édith Piaf). She coined "Sewing Circle" for Hollywood’s closeted lesbian/bisexual actresses. Daughter Maria Riva’s 1992 biography depicts a cold, manipulative narcissist who treated her as an extension of herself; Riva nonetheless inherited and managed the estate sale to Berlin. Raised Lutheran, Dietrich lost faith during WWI ("If God created this world, he should review his plan") but received a Catholic Requiem Mass at her funeral.

### Legacy and Cultural Impact
Dietrich’s androgynous style—"I dress for the image... If I dressed for myself I wouldn't bother at all"—influenced fashion (Edith Head: "knew more about fashion than any other actress") and performance. Academic literature since 1975 analyzes her image through psychoanalysis and fetishism frameworks. Honors include: asteroid 1010 Marlene (1923), Marlene-Dietrich-Platz in Berlin (1997), honorary Berlin citizenship (2002), Google Doodle (2017), and references in songs from Rodgers & Hart (1935) to Madonna (1990) and Black Midi (2021). A 1997 West End/Broadway musical *Marlene* starred Siân Phillips. For some Germans, she remains controversial for siding with the Allies; a 1996 street-naming proposal in her birthplace failed, but the central plaza dedication reads: "Berlin world star of film and song. Dedication to freedom and democracy, to Berlin and Germany."

## Terms
- **Femme fatale**: A stock character of a mysterious, seductive woman whose charms ensnare lovers, often leading them into danger; Dietrich’s von Sternberg films codified this archetype.
- **Musak project**: OSS (Office of Strategic Services) musical propaganda broadcasts (1944) designed to demoralize German soldiers; Dietrich recorded German songs including "Lili Marleen" for it.
- **Contralto**: The lowest female singing voice; Dietrich’s limited range was masked by Bacharach’s arrangements.
- **Sewing Circle**: Dietrich’s term for the clandestine network of lesbian and bisexual actresses in Hollywood.
- **Box office poison**: 1938 exhibitor label applied to Dietrich, Garbo, Hepburn, et al., meaning their salaries exceeded their drawing power.
- **Nude dress**: Jean Louis–designed beaded silk soufflé gown (1953) creating an illusion of transparency; became a cabaret signature.
- **USO (United Service Organizations)**: Nonprofit providing entertainment to U.S. armed forces; Dietrich toured for it in 1944–45.
- **Medal of Freedom**: Highest U.S. civilian award (1947); Dietrich cited it as her proudest honor.
- **Légion d'honneur**: France’s highest order of merit; Dietrich received Chevalier (later Commandeur) for wartime work.
- **Babelsberg Studios**: Historic film studio near Berlin where *The Blue Angel* was shot; Dietrich appealed for its preservation in 1990.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authorship of the "Dietrich image"**: Scholars and contemporaries dispute whether von Sternberg "created" Dietrich or whether she was an equal partner; the source notes critics "vigorously debate" the credit split, with most agreeing neither replicated the success alone.
- **Sister Elisabeth and Bergen-Belsen**: Dietrich vouched for her sister (who ran a cinema for camp officials) but later erased her from her biography; the moral complexity of this protection versus disownment remains unresolved in the source.
- **Abortion claim**: Peter Bogdanovich reported Dietrich told him she became pregnant by James Stewart during *Destry Rides Again* and had a secret abortion; this is uncorroborated in the source.
- **Extent of OSS involvement**: Dietrich was the only performer told her recordings were for OSS use; the full scope of her intelligence work beyond "Musak" is not detailed.
- **Legacy in Germany**: The 1996 rejection vs. 1997 acceptance of public commemoration in Berlin-Schöneberg reflects ongoing national ambivalence toward her wartime allegiance.
