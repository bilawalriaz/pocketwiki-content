# Modern architecture

## Overview
Modern architecture, or the modern movement, was a dominant 20th-century architectural style that rejected historical ornament in favor of functionalism, minimalism, and new technologies using glass, steel, and concrete. It emerged from late 19th-century technological revolutions and became the principal style for institutional and corporate buildings after World War II until it was gradually replaced by postmodernism in the 1980s.

## Timeline
- **1848** — Cast plate glass process invented, enabling large windows.
- **1851** — Crystal Palace by Joseph Paxton demonstrates iron and plate glass construction.
- **1884** — First steel-framed skyscraper, the Home Insurance Building, built in Chicago.
- **1895** — Otto Wagner publishes *Moderne Architektur*, calling for a rationalist style.
- **1907** — Deutscher Werkbund founded in Munich to unite designers and industrialists.
- **1919** — Bauhaus school founded in Weimar by Walter Gropius.
- **1925** — International Exhibition of Modern Decorative and Industrial Arts in Paris.
- **1928** — Le Corbusier's Villa Savoye completed; first CIAM meeting held.
- **1932** — International Style named at a Museum of Modern Art exhibition in New York.
- **1937** — Paris International Exposition marks the end of pre-war architectural styles.
- **1945–1960** — Postwar reconstruction and housing shortages drive modernist building globally.
- **1979** — Philip Johnson's AT&T Building signals the start of postmodern architecture.

## Body

### Origins and Early Modernism (1890s–1914)
Modern architecture emerged from revolutions in technology, engineering, and materials like cast iron, reinforced concrete, and plate glass. Innovations such as the safety elevator (1854) and electric light made tall buildings practical. Theorist Eugène Viollet-le-Duc urged architects to use contemporary means without historical traditions. Early pioneers included Otto Wagner and Adolf Loos in Vienna, who advocated for functional, unornamented design. In Germany, the Deutscher Werkbund (1907) aimed to unite design and industry, exemplified by Peter Behrens' AEG turbine factory (1909). In the U.S., Frank Lloyd Wright developed his "Prairie Houses" with strong horizontal lines, while early skyscrapers like the Woolworth Building (1912) combined modern steel frames with historical decoration.

### Rise of Modernism and International Style (1918–1939)
After World War I, modernists like Le Corbusier, Walter Gropius, and Ludwig Mies van der Rohe championed pure forms and functionalism. Le Corbusier promoted slogans like "a house is a machine for living in" and designed iconic villas like the Villa Savoye (1928–31). The Bauhaus school (1919–33) fused arts and technology before being closed by the Nazis. Mies van der Rohe's Barcelona Pavilion (1929) became a landmark of the International Style. Parallel movements included Expressionism (e.g., Erich Mendelsohn's Einstein Tower) and Constructivism in the Soviet Union (e.g., Konstantin Melnikov's Rusakov Workers' Club). The CIAM (founded 1928) helped standardize modernist doctrine. Art Deco, while modern, retained lavish decoration, contrasting with modernist minimalism.

### Postwar Modernism and Global Spread (1945–1980s)
World War II accelerated innovation through material shortages and prefabrication, and massive reconstruction needs. Le Corbusier's Unité d'Habitation (1947–52) in Marseille became a prototype for social housing. In the U.S., European émigrés like Gropius, Breuer, and Mies van der Rohe taught at universities and shaped corporate modernism, with Mies' "less is more" philosophy defining glass-box skyscrapers like the Seagram Building (1958). American architects like Frank Lloyd Wright (Guggenheim Museum), Eero Saarinen (TWA Terminal), and Louis Kahn developed more expressive, sculptural forms. The style spread globally: Oscar Niemeyer designed Brasília (1956–61) in Brazil; Kenzo Tange worked in Japan; and Jørn Utzon created the Sydney Opera House (1957–73). By the 1970s, late modernism featured bold shapes, but growing criticism led to the postmodern shift, marked by Philip Johnson's AT&T Building (1979).

## Terms
- **Functionalism**: The principle that a building's form should be determined by its purpose, not historical styles.
- **International Style**: A phase of modern architecture emphasizing volume over mass, regularity, and lack of ornament, named in 1932.
- **Reinforced Concrete**: Concrete strengthened with iron or steel bars, allowing molded, large-span structures.
- **Bauhaus**: A German school (1919–1933) that integrated crafts and fine arts, influencing modernist design and education.
- **CIAM (Congrès Internationaux d'Architecture Moderne)**: An international organization (1928–1959) that promoted modernist urban planning principles.
- **Art Deco**: A decorative style of the 1920s–30s using geometric patterns and modern materials, distinct from modernist minimalism.
- **Brutalism**: A postwar style featuring raw concrete (*béton brut*) and massive, fortress-like forms.
- **Streamline Moderne**: A 1930s Art Deco variant with rounded corners and nautical features, evoking speed and modernity.
- **PWA Moderne**: A stripped-down classical style used for U.S. government buildings during the Great Depression.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The role of ornament: Adolf Loos equated ornament with crime, while Art Deco and some modernists used stylized decoration.
- Social goals vs. aesthetics: Modernism aimed to solve housing crises through standardization, but projects like Pruitt-Igoe (1974) faced failure and demolition.
- Cultural adaptation: Tropical Modernism adapted principles to local climates, but critics argue it sometimes served colonial agendas.
- Preservation: Many modernist buildings are now threatened, sparking debates over their historical value versus functional obsolescence.
