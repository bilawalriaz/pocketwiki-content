# Bread

## Overview
Bread is a staple food made from flour, water, and usually a leavening agent, baked into a solid foam. It is one of humanity's oldest prepared foods, with evidence of starch processing dating back 30,000 years and the oldest known bread remains from a 14,500-year-old Natufian site in Jordan. The dawn of agriculture (~10,000 BC) made grains the primary ingredient, and leavening—via wild yeast, retained dough (sourdough), beer barm, or later commercial yeast—became widespread by 6000 BC in Mesopotamia. The 1961 Chorleywood bread process industrialized production via high-energy mixing, drastically reducing fermentation time and enabling the use of lower-protein wheat, though critics cite reduced nutritional quality and digestibility. Bread remains culturally central in Europe, the Middle East, and derived cultures, serving as a religious symbol (e.g., the Christian Eucharist), a metaphor for sustenance ("bread-winner," "staff of life"), and a major source of dietary carbohydrates and protein.

## Timeline
- **30,000 years ago** — Starch residue on grinding rocks in Europe and Australia suggests primitive flatbread making from plant roots.
- **14,500 years ago** — Oldest direct evidence of bread-making found at a Natufian site in Jordan's northeastern desert.
- **c. 10,000 BC** — Neolithic Revolution spreads agriculture; grains become the mainstay of bread production.
- **c. 6000 BC** — Early leavened bread baked in southern Mesopotamia (Sumerian civilization).
- **c. 3000 BC** — Egyptians refine Sumerian methods, deliberately adding yeast to flour.
- **1961** — Chorleywood bread process developed in the UK, using intense mechanical working to slash fermentation time.
- **1862–1955** — Aerated Bread Company operates in the UK, leavening dough via forced carbon dioxide under pressure.

## Body

### History and Development
Bread originated from the processing of starchy plant roots (cattails, ferns) into flatbreads cooked on heated rocks. The Natufian culture in Jordan produced the earliest confirmed bread ~14,500 years ago, predating agriculture. With the Neolithic shift (~10,000 BC), cereal grains became dominant; yeast spores naturally present on grain surfaces caused dough left to rest to leaven spontaneously. Sumerians in Mesopotamia baked leavened bread by 6000 BC, possibly transmitting the technique to Egyptians by 3000 BC, who refined it by adding yeast directly. Ancient leavening sources included airborne yeast, beer barm (foam) used by Gauls and Iberians, wine-fermented grape juice paste, and retaining a piece of dough as a sourdough starter. The Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans viewed bakery sophistication as a marker of civilization. The 1961 Chorleywood process revolutionized industrial baking: high-energy mixing develops gluten rapidly, cutting fermentation to minutes, allowing lower-protein wheat, and lowering costs, though it faces criticism for nutritional impact.

### Types and Ingredients
Wheat (specifically bread wheat, high in gluten) is the primary grain, contributing the largest single share of global food supply. Gluten (formed by glutenin and gliadin proteins) provides the elastic-plastic foam structure trapping CO₂. Other wheat species (spelt, emmer, einkorn, kamut) and non-wheat cereals (rye, barley, maize, oats, sorghum, millet, rice) are used, though most lack sufficient gluten and require blending with wheat flour. Gluten-free breads use almond, rice, sorghum, corn, legume, or tuber flours (e.g., cassava) with additives like xanthan gum or HPMC to mimic gluten's structure. Standard ingredients include salt (flavor, yeast control, gluten strengthening), fats/shortenings (~3% by weight optimizes leavening by lubricating protein strands), liquids (water, milk, eggs), and commercial improvers (oxidizing/reducing agents, emulsifiers, enzymes). Calcium propanoate inhibits mold in commercial loaves.

### Leavening Methods
*   **Yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*):** Commercial baker's yeast (pure culture) offers speed and uniformity; artisan bakers often maintain wild yeast cultures. Methods include *straight dough* (all ingredients at once), *pre-ferments* (poolish, biga, pâte fermentée) for flavor, and *sourdough* (symbiotic wild yeast and lactobacilli fermentation, yielding lactic/acetic acid tang). Sourdough was the sole yeast-leavening method historically; starters were maintained weekly by peasant families.
*   **Chemical:** Baking powder (or baking soda + acid like buttermilk) produces gas quickly; used for "quick breads" (muffins, pancakes, banana bread).
*   **Steam:** Rapid vaporization of water inside dough bubbles drives oven spring; the primary rising mechanism once baking starts, as heat kills yeast early.
*   **Bacterial:** Salt-rising bread uses *Clostridium perfringens* (a food-borne pathogen) instead of yeast.
*   **Aeration/High-pressure:** CO₂ forced into dough under pressure (Aerated Bread Co., 1862–1955); later adapted in the Chorleywood Pressure-Vacuum mixer to control gas bubble size and composition.

### Physical-Chemical Properties and Nutrition
Bread is structurally an elastic-plastic foam (gas-in-solid). Glutenin provides elasticity (recovery from deformation); gliadin provides plasticity (permanent deformation). The crust forms via the Maillard reaction (sugars + amino acids + heat), yielding harder texture, complex flavor, higher dietary fiber, and antioxidants (e.g., pronyl-lysine), but also concentrates >99% of acrylamide (a neurotoxic, carcinogenic compound formed >120 °C). The crumb is the porous interior; it firms as bread stales. Nutritionally, bread supplies carbohydrates and protein. Refined flour breads are low in fiber/micronutrients but often fortified (UK law mandates calcium, iron, thiamine, niacin in non-wholemeal flour). Whole grain bread provides fiber, magnesium, iron, selenium, and B vitamins. Phenolic compounds (ferulic acid, lignans) occur in hulls and flaxseed-enriched breads. FODMAP carbohydrates (indigestible, cause flatulence/IBS symptoms) are high in industrial breads due to short (~1 hour) rising; a 4-hour rise breaks down ~90% of FODMAPs and phytates, improves flavor, and increases bioavailable trace elements—tolerance depends on process, not grain type.

### Preparation and Formulation
Professional recipes use *baker's percentage*: flour = 100%, other ingredients by weight. Hydration (water:flour) is critical: hard wheat absorbs ~62%, soft ~56%; artisan doughs range 60–75% water (higher = coarser crumb). Typical batch: 500 g flour yields one loaf or two baguettes. Flour protein (12–14% for bread flour) dictates dough quality; lower protein (9–11%) requires shorter mixing to avoid oxidation (whitens crumb). Mixing water dissolves soluble proteins (albumin, globulin, proteoses), leaving glutenin/gliadin to form gluten via kneading or long fermentation (autolyse). Doughs are usually baked, but some cuisines steam (mantou), fry (puri), or pan-bake (tortillas). Processing methods: straight dough, sourdough, sponge-and-dough, Chorleywood.

### Cultural Significance and Fraud
Bread symbolizes basic sustenance across cultures: "bread-winner," "putting bread on the table," Juvenal's "panem et circenses," Bolshevik "peace, land, and bread," "breadbasket" regions. Rituals include bread-and-salt welcomes (Europe), India's "roti, kapra aur makan" (bread, cloth, house), and Christianity's Eucharist. "Lord" derives from Anglo-Saxon *hlāfweard* ("bread keeper"). Slang uses "bread"/"dough" for money; "best thing since sliced bread" denotes innovation. Fraud includes medieval sand adulteration and modern concerns over wheat flour authenticity amid supply disruptions (e.g., Russo-Ukrainian war).

## Terms
- ****Gluten**** — Protein network (glutenin + gliadin) formed when wheat flour is hydrated and worked; gives dough elasticity and plasticity, trapping gas to create bread's foam structure.
- ****Leavening**** — Process of introducing gas (CO₂, steam, air) into dough before/during baking to lighten texture; achieved via yeast, chemicals, bacteria, steam, or mechanical aeration.
- ****Sourdough**** — Bread leavened by a symbiotic culture of wild yeast and lactobacilli in a flour-water starter; yields lactic/acetic acids for sour flavor and long fermentation.
- ****Chorleywood bread process**** — Industrial method (1961) using high-energy mixing to develop gluten rapidly, slashing fermentation time and allowing lower-protein wheat; standard for mass-produced bread.
- ****Baker's percentage**** — Formulation notation: flour = 100%; all other ingredients expressed as weight percentage of flour for precision and scalability.
- ****Crumb**** — Internal porous structure of bread (gas bubbles with elastic walls); distinct from the crust.
- ****Maillard reaction**** — Non-enzymatic browning of sugars and amino acids under heat; forms crust color, flavor, and antioxidants (and acrylamide).
- ****FODMAPs**** — Fermentable oligo-, di-, mono-saccharides and polyols; short-chain carbohydrates poorly absorbed in the small intestine, causing gas/IBS symptoms; reduced by long fermentation.
- ****Acrylamide**** — Neurotoxic, carcinogenic compound formed in starchy foods heated >120 °C; concentrated in bread crust.
- ****Autolyse**** — Rest period after mixing flour and water (before salt/yeast) allowing enzymes to hydrate proteins and begin gluten development, reducing required kneading.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Nutritional impact of Chorleywood process:** Critics argue high-speed mixing and short fermentation reduce nutritional value, digestibility, and increase FODMAPs/phytates compared to traditional long-fermentation methods; proponents cite efficiency, cost, and fortification.
*   **Acrylamide risk management:** Crust contains >99% of acrylamide; mitigation strategies (lower baking temps, additives, asparaginase) may affect crust quality/safety trade-offs.
*   **Gluten-free functional equivalence:** Replicating gluten's viscoelastic foam with hydrocolloids (xanthan, HPMC) and alternative proteins remains a technical challenge for texture and aeration.
*   **Fraud vulnerability:** Supply chain disruptions (e.g., war in Ukraine) heighten risk of wheat flour adulteration/substitution; detection and standardization are ongoing concerns.
*   **Sourdough microbial diversity:** Starter ecosystems vary geographically and historically; links between specific strains, flavor profiles, and health effects (e.g., FODMAP degradation, glycemic response) are under study.
