# Tax

## Overview  
Taxation is the mandatory levy imposed by governments on individuals or entities to fund public spending and regulate economic behavior. It transfers wealth from households and businesses to the state, influencing growth, welfare, and inequality through mechanisms like fiscal multipliers and deadweight losses. Tax systems vary widely across history and jurisdictions, shaped by legal frameworks, economic theory, and political philosophy. Understanding taxation is essential for grasping how societies organize resources, redistribute wealth, and respond to challenges like war, globalization, and environmental degradation.

## Timeline  
- **c. 3000–2800 BC** — First known tax system in Ancient Egypt (First Dynasty, Old Kingdom)
- **Roman Republic** — Property taxes (1–3%) collected via auctioned contracts to *publicani*
- **1798** — Britain introduces income tax during the Napoleonic Wars
- **1643** — England introduces excise duties under John Pym and the Long Parliament
- **17th century** — Taxation reaches 15–20% of final goods production in France, Netherlands, Scandinavia
- **1920** — Arthur Pigou publishes *The Economics of Welfare*, laying groundwork for Pigovian taxes
- **2010** — U.S. Internal Revenue Code contains 3.8 million words
- **2016** — Taxation averages 34.3% of GDP across OECD countries; Denmark at 45.9%

## Body  

### Historical Development  
The earliest recorded tax systems emerged in Ancient Egypt around 3000–2800 BC, relying on corvée (forced labor) and tithes (one-tenth of produce). The Rosetta Stone, a Ptolemaic decree, included tax concessions. Early taxation also appears in biblical texts, Vedic scriptures, the Hittite Empire, and the Persian Empire. In the Roman Republic, taxes were levied at 1–3% of assessed property value but collected through private contractors (*publicani*) who advanced revenue to the state. Augustus later replaced this with direct provincial taxes: 1% on wealth and flat rates per adult.

### Classification and Types  
Taxes are classified by the OECD into categories including income, corporate, capital gains, social-security contributions, payroll, property, inheritance, expatriation, remittance, transfer, and wealth taxes. Goods and services taxes include value-added tax (VAT), sales taxes, and excise duties—first introduced in England in 1643 on items like beer, tobacco, and paper. Other forms include poll taxes (fixed per person), seigniorage (tax on money creation), tariffs, and user fees. Some economic effects mimic taxation without formal levies, such as inflation tax and financial repression.

### Legal and Economic Frameworks  
Taxes can be **ad valorem** (percentage-based) or **per unit** (fixed amount). They are categorized as **direct** (borne by the taxpayer, e.g., income tax) or **indirect** (shifted to others, e.g., VAT). Progressivity varies: **progressive** taxes increase with income, **regressive** taxes decrease, and **proportional** taxes remain constant. **Lump-sum** taxes are fixed regardless of circumstance. Tax incidence—the actual burden—depends on market elasticities; the party with less elastic demand or supply bears more of the tax.

### Economic Effects  
Taxation affects economic welfare through both positive and negative channels. **Pigovian taxes** correct market failures by taxing negative externalities (e.g., carbon emissions), improving efficiency. **Progressive taxation** reduces inequality via redistribution. However, most taxes create **deadweight loss**—reducing transaction volume below efficient levels—and impose **compliance costs**. Taxes on inelastic bases (like land value) minimize distortion. The **Laffer curve** suggests that beyond a revenue-maximizing rate (~70%), higher taxes reduce total revenue.

### Policy and Governance  
Adam Smith outlined four criteria for good taxes: proportionality, certainty, convenience, and low administrative cost. Chartalist theory argues that monetarily sovereign states need not tax for revenue but use taxation to stabilize currency and express policy goals. A 2019 study found tax cuts for low-income groups boost employment more than those for the wealthy. Globally, tax-to-GDP ratios range from 0.8% (Kuwait) to 47% (Denmark). Public support hinges on perceived fairness and benefit delivery.

### International and Development Perspectives  
In developing countries, taxation correlates with state capacity and financial development. Wars historically drove tax innovations—Britain’s income tax funded the Napoleonic Wars; the U.S. adopted it during the Civil War. Resource-rich nations often achieve higher tax revenues. Trade taxes are declining globally, replaced by domestic consumption taxes. The World Bank’s Doing Business survey ranks tax compliance ease, with Gulf states leading and Sub-Saharan Africa lagging.

### Philosophical and Ideological Views  
Geoists advocate a single land-value tax, arguing it is efficient and moral since land is a common inheritance. Libertarians view most taxation as coercive theft, favoring minimal state funding. Socialists see taxation as transitional, aiming for its eventual obsolescence post-communism. Democratic theory holds that citizens consent to taxation through representation. Tax choice advocates propose letting individuals allocate portions of their taxes to preferred public services.

## Terms  
- **Corvée**: Forced labor provided to the state by peasants unable to pay other taxes.  
- **Tithe**: A tax-like payment of one-tenth of earnings or produce, historically paid to the Church.  
- **Publicani**: Private contractors in ancient Rome who advanced tax revenue to the state in exchange for collection rights.  
- **Excise Duty**: An indirect tax on goods during manufacture, production, or distribution, usually based on quantity or value.  
- **Seigniorage**: A tax on the creation of money, relevant in pre-fiat economies.  
- **Deadweight Loss**: The reduction in economic efficiency caused by a tax that discourages mutually beneficial transactions.  
- **Pigovian Tax**: A tax levied on goods with negative externalities to align private costs with social costs.  
- **Land Value Tax (LVT)**: A tax on the unimproved value of land, advocated for its lack of deadweight loss.  
- **Tax Incidence**: The division of a tax burden between buyers and sellers based on relative price elasticities.  
- **Laffer Curve**: A theoretical curve showing that tax revenue increases with rates up to a point, beyond which it declines.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Several contentious issues surround taxation:  
- Whether chartalist theory undermines the necessity of taxation for revenue in monetarily sovereign states.  
- The optimal level of progressivity in income taxation and its impact on growth versus equity.  
- The effectiveness of Pigovian taxes in addressing climate change and public health without regressive impacts.  
- The role of tax havens in enabling base erosion and profit shifting, and whether global coordination can curb these practices.  
- The legitimacy of taxation itself, with libertarians and Objectivists viewing it as coercive, while others see it as foundational to civil society.  
- The feasibility of implementing geoist policies, particularly the single land-value tax, at scale.  
- The tension between direct democracy and tax policy, with some evidence suggesting voter-controlled budgets lead to lower taxes.  
- The extent to which developing countries can build effective tax systems amid weak institutions and informal economies.
