# Florence Griffith Joyner

## Overview
Florence Griffith Joyner, known as "Flo-Jo," was an American track and field athlete who became a global icon in the late 1980s. She set world records in the 100m and 200m sprints that still stand, and her performances at the 1988 Olympics made her one of the most decorated female track athletes in history. Her career is also noted for its abrupt end and the enduring debates surrounding her record-breaking times and retirement.

- **July 16, 1988** — Set a world record of 10.49 in the 100m at the U.S. Olympic trials.
- **September 1988** — Won three gold medals (100m, 200m, 4x100m relay) and one silver (4x400m relay) at the Seoul Olympics.

- **September 21, 1998** — Died in her sleep at age 38 from a seizure caused by a congenital brain abnormality.

## Body

### Early Life and Career Beginnings
Born in Los Angeles, Griffith was athletic from a young age, winning national youth games as a teenager. She attended California State University, Northridge (CSUN) and later UCLA, where she was coached by Bob Kersee. She qualified for the 1980 Olympics but was unable to compete due to the U.S. boycott. She made her Olympic debut in 1984, winning a silver medal in the 200m.

### The 1988 Season and Olympic Dominance
After a period of reduced competition, Griffith Joyner returned to full-time athletics in 1987. In 1988, she achieved unprecedented success. At the U.S. Olympic trials, she set a world record of 10.49 seconds in the 100m, a massive improvement of 0.27 seconds over the previous mark. She also set an American record in the 200m. At the Seoul Olympics, she won gold in the 100m (10.54) and 200m (setting a world record of 21.34), and anchored the 4x100m relay team to gold. Her 4x400m relay team won silver.

### Retirement and Post-Athletic Career
In February 1989, just days before the introduction of mandatory random drug testing in the U.S., Griffith Joyner retired from competition, citing new business opportunities. She became a pop culture figure through endorsement deals, acting, and design work, including creating uniforms for the Indiana Pacers. In 1996, she attempted a comeback targeting the 400m but was forced to abandon it due to tendinitis.

### Style and Personal Life
Griffith Joyner was famous for her flamboyant style, including one-legged running suits, long decorated nails, and jewelry worn during races. She married 1984 Olympic triple jump champion Al Joyner in 1987, becoming sister-in-law to Jackie Joyner-Kersee. They had one daughter, Mary Ruth, born in 1990.

### Death
She died unexpectedly in her sleep on September 21, 1998, at age 38. The cause was suffocation during a severe epileptic seizure, linked to a congenital vascular brain abnormality (cavernous hemangioma). Toxicology found only common over-the-counter drugs.

## Terms
- **World Record:** The best performance ever officially ratified in an athletic event.
- **Electronic Timing:** Precision timing using electronic devices, standard for world records since the 1970s.
- **Wind-aided:** A performance achieved with a tailwind exceeding the legal limit of 2.0 m/s, which disqualifies it for record purposes.
- **Cavernous Hemangioma:** A congenital (present at birth) vascular abnormality in the brain that can cause seizures.
- **Tonic-clonic seizure:** A type of seizure formerly known as a grand mal seizure, involving loss of consciousness and convulsions.
- **IAAF:** The International Association of Athletics Federations, now known as World Athletics, the global governing body for track and field.
- **The Athletic Congress (TAC):** The former name of USA Track & Field, the national governing body for the sport in the United States.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The 10.49 World Record:** The legitimacy of her 100m world record is debated. Scientific studies commissioned after the race suggested an illegal tailwind of +5.0 to +7.0 m/s, but the IAAF never annulled it. The record is listed as "probably strongly wind assisted" by statisticians.
- **Performance-Enhancing Drugs:** Her dramatic improvement in 1988 and increased musculature led to persistent allegations of drug use from fellow athletes. She denied all allegations, passed all competitive tests, and an IOC official later stated exhaustive testing found no banned substances.
- **Retirement Timing:** Her retirement in February 1989, just before mandatory random drug testing was implemented, has fueled speculation, though she cited business opportunities as the reason.
