# Ibn Taymiyya

## Overview
Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328) was a highly influential and controversial Sunni Muslim scholar from the Hanbali school. His teachings on theology, law, and jihad have profoundly shaped modern Islamic revivalist movements, particularly Salafism and political Islamism. He is a pivotal figure whose ideas are both revered and contested, making him central to understanding contemporary Islamic thought.

## Timeline
- **1263** — Born in Harran, Mamluk Sultanate.
- **1269** — Family flees Mongol invasion, settles in Damascus.
- **1282** — Completes education; granted license to issue legal verdicts.
- **1293** — First imprisonment after protesting a governor's decision.
- **1298** — Writes *Al-`Aqidat al-Hamawiyat al-Kubra*, causing controversy with Ash'arites.
- **1300** — Participates in campaign against Alawites in Kisrawan.
- **1303** — Issues famous fatwa declaring jihad against Mongol Ilkhanids obligatory; fights at Battle of Marj al-Saffar.
- **1306** — Imprisoned in Cairo on charges of anthropomorphism.
- **1313** — Returns to Damascus; teaches notable students like Ibn Qayyim.
- **1318** — Issues controversial fatwa on divorce, leading to further conflict.
- **1326** — Final imprisonment for his treatise on tomb visitation.
- **1328** — Dies in prison in Damascus.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into a family of Hanbali scholars, Ibn Taymiyya's early education was in Harran before his family fled the Mongol invasion in 1269, settling in Damascus. He mastered the Quran, Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh), and hadith, studying under over 200 scholars. His education also included secular sciences like Arabic grammar, mathematics, and philosophy, which he later used to refute Aristotelianism and speculative theology (kalam). He completed his studies around 1282.

### Scholarly Career and Controversies
After his father's death in 1284, Ibn Taymiyya began teaching and writing. His life was marked by frequent conflict with religious and political authorities. He was imprisoned multiple times, with charges including anthropomorphism (attributing human physical characteristics to God), opposing popular Sufi practices like tomb visitation, and issuing controversial legal rulings. His relationship with the Mamluk authorities was complex, ranging from collaboration on military campaigns to open rebellion and incarceration.

### Involvement in the Mongol Invasions
Ibn Taymiyya's most famous political and theological intervention was during the Mongol invasions of Syria. He declared the Mongol Ilkhanid rulers, despite their nominal conversion to Islam, to be unbelievers (kuffar) because they ruled by their traditional Yassa code instead of Sharia. He issued fatwas making jihad against them obligatory, a groundbreaking legal opinion. He personally participated in the Battle of Marj al-Saffar in 1303, which helped end Mongol control of Syria.

### Theological and Legal Stance
Ibn Taymiyya was a polemicist against various schools, particularly Ash'arism and Maturidism in theology, and certain Sufi practices. He advocated for ijtihad (independent reasoning) over taqlid (blind adherence to a legal school). His creedal works, like *Al-Aqidah Al-Waasitiyyah*, insisted on a literal interpretation of God's attributes as described in the Quran and hadith, leading to accusations of anthropomorphism. While critical of some Sufi practices, he was affiliated with the Qadiriyya Sufi order and acknowledged Sufism as part of Islam.

### Legacy and Modern Influence
Ibn Taymiyya's influence was limited for centuries after his death but surged in the 19th and 20th centuries. Modern Salafi movements regard him as a key authority. His fatwas against the Mongol rulers, particularly the "Mardin fatwa," have been cited by modern Islamist and jihadist ideologues, including Sayyid Qutb and Osama bin Laden, to justify armed struggle against contemporary Muslim governments deemed un-Islamic. His thought is thus a foundational reference for both peaceful reformist and militant Islamist movements.

## Terms
- **Mujtahid**: A Muslim scholar qualified to exercise independent reasoning (ijtihad) in interpreting Islamic law.
- **Takfir**: The act of declaring a Muslim to be an unbeliever (kafir), effectively excommunicating them.
- **Salafism**: A reform movement within Sunni Islam advocating a return to the practices of the earliest generations of Muslims (the Salaf).
- **Kalam**: Islamic speculative theology, often using Greek philosophical methods, which Ibn Taymiyya criticized.
- **Sharia**: Islamic law derived from the Quran and the Sunnah (traditions of the Prophet Muhammad).
- **Yassa**: The traditional Mongol legal code, which Ibn Taymiyya opposed as man-made law.
- **Dar al-Islam / Dar al-Harb**: Classical Islamic concepts dividing the world into the "domain of Islam" and the "domain of war."
- **Tawassul**: The practice of seeking intercession or help through a prophet or saint, which Ibn Taymiyya restricted.
- **Bid'ah**: Religious innovation, considered blameworthy in Islam, which Ibn Taymiyya vigorously opposed.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Interpretation of the Mardin Fatwa**: Scholars debate whether Ibn Taymiyya's fatwa was a call for defensive jihad against an external Mongol enemy or a revolutionary doctrine justifying violence against Muslim rulers who do not fully implement Sharia. A 2010 conference argued the key text was misprinted.
- **Relationship with Sufism**: While often portrayed as anti-Sufi, evidence shows he was part of a Sufi order and praised many Sufi masters. Scholars debate the extent of his rejection versus his aim to reform Sufi practices he saw as innovative.
- **Historical Influence**: There is scholarly disagreement on whether Ibn Taymiyya had a significant direct influence on later movements before the 19th century or if his modern prominence is largely a product of contemporary revivalist reinterpretation.
- **Philosophical Stance**: His position is complex; he is seen as both a strict literalist who rejected philosophy and a materialist who engaged with philosophical concepts like the nature of time and God's relationship to the universe.
