# Tocharians

## Overview
The Tocharians were an ancient Indo-European people who inhabited the oasis cities along the northern edge of the Tarim Basin (modern Xinjiang, China) from roughly the 2nd century BC to the 9th century AD. They are known primarily through thousands of Buddhist and administrative manuscripts written in two related languages, Tocharian A and B. Their significance lies in their position as the easternmost known speakers of a centum Indo-European language, their role as key intermediaries on the Silk Road, and their unique blend of Western and Eastern cultural influences.

## Timeline
- **c. 2000 BC** — Agricultural communities first appear in the oases of the northern Tarim Basin.
- **2nd century BC** — Oasis settlements develop into city-states; the Xiongnu nomads begin to dominate the region.
- **60 BC** — Han dynasty establishes the Protectorate of the Western Regions, bringing the Tarim Basin under Chinese influence.
- **2nd century AD** — Kushan Empire expands into the Tarim, spreading Buddhism, Sanskrit, and Brahmi script.
- **3rd–8th centuries AD** — Tocharian manuscripts are produced; Kucha becomes a major center of Buddhist learning.
- **c. 480–550 AD** — Hephthalites conquer the western Tarim Basin, influencing local art and culture.
- **560 AD** — Göktürks take control of the Turfan and Kucha areas.
- **630 AD** — Chinese monk Xuanzang visits Kucha, providing a detailed account of its society and Buddhism.
- **648 AD** — Tang dynasty conquers Kucha after a brutal campaign, ending its political independence.
- **803 AD** — Uyghur Khaganate takes control of the northern Tarim.
- **866 AD** — Uyghurs establish the Kingdom of Qocho, leading to cultural and linguistic assimilation.
- **9th century AD** — Tocharian languages are believed to have become extinct.

## Body

### Origins and Precursors
The origins of the Tocharians are uncertain. A leading theory links them to the Afanasievo culture (c. 3500–2500 BC), an eastern offshoot of the Yamnaya culture from the Pontic steppe. This migration could explain the early isolation of Proto-Tocharian from Indo-Iranian linguistic innovations. Genetic studies, however, show the famous Tarim mummies (from 2000 BC) were an isolated local population unrelated to Afanasievo, though contemporaneous populations in Dzungaria to the north did have Afanasievo ancestry. Another proposed precursor is the Chemurchek culture (2750–1900 BC), which shares stylistic similarities with Western European cultures.

### Languages and Society
The Tocharians are known from about 7,600 documents (c. 400–1200 AD) written in two distinct but related Indo-European languages: Tocharian A (Agnean) and Tocharian B (Kuchean). They were mutually unintelligible and likely diverged in the 1st millennium BC. Tocharian A appears to have become a literary and liturgical language, while Tocharian B was used in daily life. The languages are classified as centum languages, lacking the satemization seen in eastern Indo-European branches. The people lived in oasis city-states like Kucha and Agni, which served as vital trade hubs on the Silk Road. They were ruled by local kings, practiced intensive agriculture, and were described by Chinese sources as having European physical features.

### Religion and Culture
The Tocharians were predominantly Buddhists, specifically followers of the Sarvastivada school. Their monastic texts and the stunning murals of the Kizil Caves, depicting Jataka tales and Buddha legends, are primary evidence of their culture. Pre-Buddhist beliefs likely included a sun goddess and other deities, with some linguistic traces in their vocabulary. Their art shows influences from Gandhara, India, and later from their Hephthalite and Turkic rulers, blending Eastern and Western styles.

### Political History and Decline
The Tarim oasis states were perpetually caught between nomadic empires to the north and Chinese dynasties to the east. They were ruled successively by the Xiongnu, Han, Kushans, Hephthalites, and Göktürks. The Tang dynasty's conquest in the mid-7th century, marked by the brutal subjugation of Kucha in 648, was a major blow. From the 8th century, Turkic-speaking Uyghurs settled in the region. Over centuries of intermarriage and cultural exchange, the Tocharian population assimilated with the Uyghurs, and their languages were gradually replaced by Old Uyghur, leading to extinction by the 9th century.

## Terms
- **Centum language**: A branch of the Indo-European languages (including Tocharian, Celtic, and Germanic) characterized by the merging of certain consonant sounds, as opposed to satem languages.
- **Satemization**: A linguistic innovation in some Indo-European branches (like Indo-Iranian and Slavic) where certain consonants shifted, which Tocharian lacks.
- **Silk Road**: Ancient network of trade routes connecting East Asia with the Mediterranean, crucial for the exchange of goods, ideas, and religions like Buddhism.
- **Sarvastivada**: A major school of Early Buddhism whose texts were central to Tocharian religious life.
- **Afanasievo culture**: A Bronze Age archaeological culture (c. 3500–2500 BC) in the Altai region, considered a possible precursor to the Tocharians.
- **Hephthalites**: A nomadic empire (5th–6th centuries AD) that controlled parts of Central Asia and the Tarim Basin, influencing local art and politics.
- **Kucha**: The largest and most prominent Tocharian city-state, a major center of Buddhist scholarship and trade.
- **Old Uyghur**: The Turkic language that replaced Tocharian in the Tarim Basin after the 8th century.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ethnic and Linguistic Origins**: The exact route by which Indo-European speakers reached the Tarim Basin is debated. While the Afanasievo culture is a leading candidate, genetic evidence complicates a direct link to the earliest Tarim inhabitants.
- **The "Tocharian" Name**: The modern name is a misnomer. It derives from ancient Greek references to the Tókharoi people of Bactria, who likely spoke an Iranian language. The name was incorrectly applied to the Tarim Basin languages in the early 20th century but persists by convention.
- **Tocharian C (Kroränian)**: The existence of a third Tocharian language is inferred from loanwords in Prakrit documents from southeastern Tarim sites, but evidence is meager and the theory is not universally accepted.
- **Yuezhi Connection**: A minority view suggests the Yuezhi (founders of the Kushan Empire) were originally Tocharian speakers who later adopted Bactrian, an Iranian language.
