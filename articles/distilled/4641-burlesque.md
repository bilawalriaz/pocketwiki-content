# Burlesque

## Overview
Burlesque is a form of entertainment that uses ludicrous imitation and exaggeration to parody serious works or subjects, deriving its name from the Italian *burla* (joke). It originated as a literary and theatrical genre in the 16th-17th centuries, later evolving into a popular American variety show format featuring bawdy comedy and striptease, which has seen a modern revival.

## Timeline
- **Early 16th c.** — Term first appears in title of Francesco Berni's *Opere burlesche*.
- **Late 17th c.** — "Burlesque" used in English for literary/theatrical imitation.
- **1830s-1890s** — Victorian theatrical burlesque popular in London.
- **1868** — Lydia Thompson's "British Blondes" popularize burlesque in New York.
- **Early 20th c.** — American burlesque develops distinct circuits and incorporates striptease.
- **1930s-1940s** — Hollywood films attempt to recreate burlesque spirit.
- **1940s** — American burlesque declines due to Prohibition and crackdowns.
- **1990s-present** — Neo-Burlesque revival begins in the U.S. and U.K.

## Body

### Literary Origins and Development
Burlesque as a literary term, meaning grotesque imitation of the dignified, became widespread in 17th-century Italy and France before spreading to England. It relied on the audience's knowledge of the original subject for its effect. The genre was divided into **high burlesque**, which applied an elevated style to a commonplace subject (e.g., Alexander Pope's *The Rape of the Lock*), and **low burlesque**, which used an irreverent style on a serious subject (e.g., Samuel Butler's *Hudibras*). The term has been retrospectively applied to earlier works by Chaucer and Shakespeare.

### In Music
From the early 18th century, "burlesque" described musical works juxtaposing serious and comic elements for a grotesque effect. It later indicated a bright, high-spirited mood. Examples include Richard Strauss's *Burleske* (1890) and movements in works by Bach, Mahler, and Shostakovich. The term also applied to operettas by composers like Johann Strauss II and to Igor Stravinsky's *Petrushka* (1911).

### Victorian Theatrical Burlesque
Popular in London from the 1830s to 1890s, Victorian burlesque was a musical theatre parody adapting well-known operas, plays, or ballets into broad, often risqué comic plays. It mocked original conventions, frequently featured women in travesty roles, and used rhyming couplets with puns. It evolved from one-act pieces to full-evening entertainments before being supplanted by Edwardian musical comedy.

### American Burlesque
American burlesque, an offshoot of the Victorian genre, incorporated elements of minstrel shows. It typically had three parts: comic sketches, variety acts, and a concluding chorus or burlesque sketch. By the early 20th century, striptease gradually supplanted singing and dancing, with stars like Gypsy Rose Lee and Sally Rand. The genre declined after Prohibition and crackdowns in the 1930s-40s but has experienced a Neo-Burlesque revival since the 1990s, featuring performers like Dita Von Teese.

## Terms
- **Burlesque**: A work intended to cause laughter by ludicrous imitation of serious subjects.
- **High burlesque**: Imitation applying an elevated style to a commonplace subject.
- **Low burlesque**: Imitation applying an irreverent style to a serious subject.
- **Travesty**: A form of burlesque involving grotesque or debasing representation.
- **Extravaganza**: A theatrical form related to Victorian burlesque, often more spectacular.
- **Neo-Burlesque**: A modern revival of burlesque performance, often with a nostalgic or artistic focus.
- **Soubrette**: In burlesque, a female performer who sang, danced, and showed off her figure.
