# Hydropower

## Overview

Hydropower is the conversion of the gravitational potential or kinetic energy of falling or flowing water into electricity or mechanical work. It is the world's largest renewable electricity source, supplying roughly 15% of global generation and over half the electricity in more than 35 countries, while also serving as half of pumped-storage hydroelectricity, the dominant grid-scale energy-storage technology. Its appeal lies in low direct carbon emissions and relatively steady output, but large dams and reservoirs carry economic, social, and environmental costs that have sparked sustained opposition since the late 20th century.

## Timeline

- **c. 4th century BC** — Water wheels and watermills appear in the ancient Near East; Sumerians and Babylonians use hydropower for irrigation.
- **1st century BC** — Vitruvius describes Roman water-powered mills; the Barbegal mill (France) processes 28 tons of grain daily.
- **202 BC–220 AD** — Han-dynasty China employs waterwheel-powered trip hammers and bellows; Du Shi applies waterwheel power to piston bellows.
- **1848** — James B. Francis designs the 90%-efficient reaction turbine; the Francis turbine remains in widespread use.
- **1880s–1895** — Benoît Fourneyron's turbine installed commercially; Niagara Falls plant (1895) demonstrates long-distance AC transmission.
- **1900s–1930s** — Global dam-building boom; Hoover Dam (1931) and Grand Coulee Dam symbolize large-scale electrification.
- **1970s–1990s** — Small hydropower rebounds; international anti-dam movement gains strength; dam construction costs rise 4% annually.
- **2011** — Ethiopia begins construction of the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Nile.

## Body

### Principles and Power Calculation

Available hydropower depends on hydraulic head (energy per unit weight of water) and volumetric flow rate. Head comprises static head (proportional to vertical drop) and dynamic head (related to flow velocity). The useful power output is:

$$\dot{W}_{\text{out}} = -\eta \rho \dot{V} g \Delta h$$

where η is turbine efficiency, ρ water density, $\dot{V}$ volumetric flow rate, g gravitational acceleration, and Δh the height difference. For example, an 85%-efficient turbine with 80 m³/s flow and 145 m head yields about 97 MW. Operators compare actual electrical output to theoretical potential energy to determine efficiency, following test codes such as ASME PTC 18 and IEC 60041. Efficiency calculations must account for friction losses in penstocks, tailwater rise, local gravity variation, air temperature, barometric pressure, water density, and forebay/tailbay altitudes.

### Mechanical Applications

Before electricity, hydropower drove watermills for grinding grain (gristmills), sawing timber and stone, textile production, ore crushing, dock cranes, and domestic lifts. A trompe uses falling water to compress air for distant machinery without moving parts. In 1910, a compressed-air plant on the Montreal River at Ragged Shutes supplied 5,000 horsepower to nearby mines. Run-of-river systems and water wheels can extract kinetic energy without significant head change.

### Electricity Generation

Hydroelectricity dominates hydropower use. Two main plant types exist: reservoir-based plants (dam plus storage) offering on-demand power, and run-of-river plants (barrage without reservoir) dependent on continuous flow. Plant sizes range from micro-hydro to country-scale installations; as of 2019, the five largest power stations globally were conventional hydroelectric dams. Pumped-storage hydroelectricity stores energy by pumping water uphill during low demand and releasing it during peaks. Tidal stream generators represent an emerging marine application. Global installed capacity reached nearly 1400 GW in 2021, the highest among renewables.

### Disadvantages and Limitations

Dam failures can cause catastrophic loss of life and pollution. Reservoirs disrupt ecosystems by blocking fish migration, cooling and deoxygenating downstream water, trapping sediment (starving deltas), and destroying habitat. Large, deep reservoirs emit methane from decomposing submerged vegetation—equivalent to nearly a billion tonnes of CO₂ annually. Construction displaces communities and may flood cultural or religious sites. Run-of-river projects face seasonal flow variability, reducing dry-season output.

### History

Ancient waterwheels emerged independently in the Mediterranean and China by the 4th century BC. The Roman era perfected water-powered mills, including the Barbegal complex and sawmills using crank-and-connecting-rod mechanisms. Medieval and early modern hushing techniques used water torrents to expose mineral veins, evolving into 19th-century hydraulic mining. The Islamic Golden Age (8th–13th centuries) spread watermills, tide mills, and water turbines across three continents. The 19th century saw the transition from open water wheels to enclosed turbines, culminating in the Francis (1848) and Pelton (1870s) designs. The 20th century prioritized large dams for mass electrification, driven by industrial competition and Cold War geopolitics, but faced growing environmental opposition after the 1970s as costs rose and ecological damage became evident.

### Regional Development

**United States:** Early projects emphasized "wasted potential" over local need; Niagara's success relied on Tesla's AC transmission. Federal dams (Hoover, Shasta, Grand Coulee) linked hydropower to New Deal job creation and WWII production, but failed to deliver promised rural prosperity and harmed salmon runs. Post-war dam building faced environmental opposition, leading to cancellations in Dinosaur National Monument and the Grand Canyon.

**Africa:** Foreign powers used dams as development tools—the World Bank (Kariba, Akosombo), USSR (Aswan High Dam). The Aswan Dam (1970s) generated one-third of Egypt's electricity but sparked Nile-water disputes with Sudan and Ethiopia, whose Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam began in 2011. Small hydropower electrified mines and railways in the 1890s but declined with grid expansion; 21st-century revival targets rural electrification.

**Europe:** Northern Europe exploited abundant rainfall and mountainous terrain; southern Europe turned to hydropower amid coal shortages. Italy achieved near-nationwide electrification from hydropower by WWI. Germany's pre-war dams prioritized flood control and irrigation, with hydropower as a bonus. Post-WWII dam building intensified but triggered conflicts over water rights, irrigation, and displacement.

## Terms

- **Hydraulic head:** Energy per unit weight of water, comprising static head (vertical drop) and dynamic head (flow velocity).
- **Turbine efficiency (η):** Ratio of useful power output to theoretical power available in the water, accounting for mechanical and hydraulic losses.
- **Run-of-river plant:** Hydroelectric facility using a barrage to divert river flow without a large reservoir, dependent on continuous water availability.
- **Pumped-storage hydroelectricity:** Energy storage system pumping water uphill during low demand and releasing it through turbines during peak demand.
- **Francis turbine:** Reaction turbine (1848) combining radial and axial flow, achieving ~90% efficiency, still widely used today.
- **Pelton wheel:** Impulse turbine (1870s) using high-velocity water jets for high-head applications.
- **Probable maximum flood:** Worst-case flood scenario used in dam design, routed via spillways to prevent overtopping.
- **Trompe:** Device using falling water and air bubbles to generate compressed air for distant machinery without moving parts.

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars debate whether ancient Indian cakkavattaka references describe water-powered noria or hand/tread-operated devices, with some arguing the textual evidence is ambiguous. The environmental trade-offs of large dams remain contested: while they provide low-carbon baseload power and flood control, their methane emissions, ecosystem disruption, and social displacement challenge their sustainability credentials. The role of small hydropower in rural electrification versus its impact on local stream ecosystems is actively studied. Additionally, the geopolitical implications of transboundary dams—particularly on the Nile—continue to generate diplomatic tension, with no agreed framework for equitable water sharing.
