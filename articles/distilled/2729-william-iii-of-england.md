# William III of England

## Overview
William III of Orange (1650–1702) was a pivotal European ruler who, as Stadtholder of the Dutch Republic and later King of England, Scotland, and Ireland, dedicated his life to opposing the expansionist policies of France's Louis XIV. His 1688 invasion of England, known as the Glorious Revolution, established a constitutional monarchy and Protestant succession, fundamentally shaping the political development of the British Isles and the European balance of power.

## Timeline
- **4 Nov 1650** — Born Prince of Orange; father dies before his birth.
- **1672** — Becomes Stadtholder during the Dutch "Disaster Year" (Rampjaar).
- **4 Nov 1677** — Marries his cousin Mary, daughter of the Duke of York.
- **5 Nov 1688** — Lands at Brixham, England, with a Dutch invasion force.
- **13 Feb 1689** — Declared joint sovereign of England with Mary II by Parliament.
- **1 Jul 1690** — Defeats James II at the Battle of the Boyne in Ireland.
- **28 Dec 1694** — Mary II dies; William rules alone.
- **20 Sep 1697** — Treaty of Rijswijk ends the Nine Years' War.
- **8 Mar 1702** — Dies at Kensington Palace; succeeded by Anne.

## Body

### Early Life and Rise to Power
William was the only child of William II, Prince of Orange, and Mary, Princess Royal. His father's death made him sovereign Prince of Orange from birth. His education was overseen by the Dutch republican government, led by Grand Pensionary Johan de Witt, who sought to limit the power of the House of Orange. William became Stadtholder in 1672 during the Rampjaar, a crisis year when the Dutch Republic was invaded by France and its allies. This crisis allowed him to overcome republican opposition, reorganize the Dutch army, and establish himself as the primary military and political opponent of Louis XIV.

### Glorious Revolution and Joint Rule
In 1688, William invaded England at the invitation of Protestant leaders who opposed the Catholic rule of his father-in-law, King James II. James fled, and in 1689, Parliament offered the crown to William and his wife Mary as joint sovereigns, passing the Bill of Rights to limit royal power. Mary governed during William's frequent absences for the Nine Years' War against France. Her death in 1694 left William to rule alone, facing Jacobite opposition in Britain and continuing the European war.

### Military Campaigns and European Policy
William's career was defined by his opposition to French hegemony. He reformed the Dutch army into a professional force and led coalitions against Louis XIV in the Franco-Dutch War and the Nine Years' War. Though often outnumbered, he employed strategies of attrition to limit French gains. His military reforms, including modernizing logistics and enforcing strict discipline, made the Dutch army one of Europe's finest. He died in 1702, just as the War of the Spanish Succession began, a conflict he had worked to prevent through partition treaties.

### Constitutional Legacy in Britain
William's reign settled the long-standing conflict between the Crown and Parliament in Parliament's favor. The Bill of Rights (1689) established key constitutional principles, including parliamentary control over taxation and the army. The Act of Settlement (1701) secured a Protestant succession by passing the crown to the House of Hanover after his sister-in-law Anne, ensuring the exclusion of Catholics. These acts formed the foundation of the modern British constitutional monarchy.

## Terms
- **Stadtholder**: The chief magistrate and military commander of the Dutch Republic, a position held by the House of Orange.
- **Rampjaar**: The "Disaster Year" of 1672, when the Dutch Republic was simultaneously invaded by France, England, and other states.
- **Glorious Revolution**: The 1688 invasion and subsequent transfer of power from James II to William III and Mary II, largely without bloodshed in England.
- **Bill of Rights (1689)**: A constitutional document that established parliamentary sovereignty and limited the powers of the monarch.
- **Jacobites**: Supporters of the deposed King James II and his Stuart heirs, who sought to restore them to the throne.
- **Act of Settlement (1701)**: An act that settled the succession on the Protestant House of Hanover, excluding Catholics.
- **League of Augsburg**: An anti-French coalition formed in 1686, which later became the Grand Alliance during the Nine Years' War.
- **Nonjurors**: Individuals, including over 400 clergy, who refused to swear oaths of allegiance to William and Mary.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Complicity in De Witt's Murder**: William's role in the 1672 lynching of Johan de Witt and his brother is debated; he was never proven complicit but thwarted prosecutions and rewarded some perpetrators.
- **Military Command**: Historians are divided on his qualities as a field commander, with some praising his strategic insight and others criticizing his recklessness and high casualty rates.
- **Sexuality**: Rumors of homosexual inclinations, fueled by his close relationships with male courtiers like Bentinck and Keppel, are disputed by biographers, with some seeing them as Jacobite propaganda.
- **Succession and Legacy**: His failure to produce an heir directly led to the Act of Settlement, which secured the Protestant succession but also sowed seeds for future conflict by excluding closer Catholic relatives.
