# Ghazal

## Overview
The ghazal is a classical poetic form originating in 7th-century Arabia, characterized by its strict structure of independent, rhyming couplets. It is a profound expression of both romantic and spiritual love, exploring themes of longing, loss, and the beauty found within pain. Its influence spread across the Islamic world and beyond, becoming a central lyric genre in Persian, Urdu, and other languages, and later adapting into a popular musical form in South Asia.

## Timeline
- **7th century** — Ghazal originates in Arabia, evolving from the romantic prelude (*nasīb*) of the longer *qasida* form.
- **661–750** — Ghazal blossoms as an independent genre during the Umayyad era.
- **10th century** — Ghazal introduced into Persian literature, adopting Arabic structural and thematic frameworks.
- **12th–13th c.** — Persian ghazal is formalized; the poet's pen name (*takhallus*) becomes a mandatory element.
- **12th century** — Ghazal introduced to the Indian subcontinent with the establishment of the Delhi Sultanate.
- **13th century** — Hasan Sijzi and Amir Khusrow are pivotal in developing the Indo-Persian ghazal.
- **18th–19th c.** — Ghazal transitions from Persian to Urdu, entering its "Golden Age" with poets like Mir Taqi Mir and Mirza Ghalib.
- **19th century** — Ghazal influences Western literature, notably Goethe's *West–Eastern Diwan*.
- **20th century** — Ghazal becomes a popular music genre in South Asia; Agha Shahid Ali champions the form in English.

## Body

### Poetic Form and Structure
A ghazal is a short poem of five to fifteen couplets (*sher* or *bayt*). Its uniqueness lies in strict rules: all couplets share the same meter (*bahr*) and rhyme scheme (*qafiya*), and all second lines end with a repeated refrain (*radif*). The first couplet (*matla'*) must contain both the rhyme and refrain in both lines. The final couplet (*maqta'*) traditionally includes the poet's pen name (*takhallus*). While each couplet is a self-contained semantic unit, they are linked by a unified theme or tone, often allusively.

### Origins and Spread
The form evolved from the *nasīb*, the romantic opening of the pre-Islamic Arabic *qasida*. It developed two early schools: the Udhri (Platonic, suffering love) and the Hijazi (erotic, lighthearted). With the spread of Islam, the ghazal moved into Persia, where it was formalized, and then into the Indian subcontinent via Persian-speaking courts. It was further disseminated by Sufi mystics, who used its imagery to express divine love. By the 18th century, it had become the preeminent lyric genre in Urdu.

### Themes and Interpretation
The core theme is *ishq* (love), often unrequited, portraying the pain of separation from a beloved. This earthly love (*ishq-e-majazi*) is frequently paralleled with divine love (*ishq-e-haqiqi*) in Sufi interpretations, where the "Beloved" is God and "wine" symbolizes spiritual ecstasy. The form's ambiguity allows couplets to be read on both secular and sacred levels. Later masters like Ghalib expanded its scope to include philosophical and existential inquiries.

### Musical Adaptation
Intrinsically linked to music since its inception, the ghazal was performed by professional singers in early Islamic centers. It developed distinct musical traditions across regions, such as the *Shashmakom* in Central Asia and *Ghazal Gayaki* in South Asia. In the 20th century, it became a mainstream popular music genre in India, Pakistan, and Afghanistan, modernized by artists like Mehdi Hassan and Jagjit Singh, who incorporated Western instruments while retaining classical frameworks.

## Terms
- **Bayt/Sher**: A couplet; the basic unit of a ghazal.
- **Qafiya**: The end rhyme pattern that all couplets share.
- **Radif**: A repeated word or phrase that follows the *qafiya* at the end of each second line.
- **Matla'**: The opening couplet, where both lines contain the *qafiya* and *radif*.
- **Maqta'**: The final couplet, traditionally containing the poet's pen name (*takhallus*).
- **Takhallus**: The poet's nom de plume, incorporated into the *maqta'*.
- **Bahr**: The metrical pattern and syllabic count governing each line.
- **Ishq**: The central theme of love, encompassing both earthly and divine longing.
- **Qasida**: The longer, pre-Islamic Arabic ode from which the ghazal evolved.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes scholarly debate on the precise interpretation of ghazal couplets, which are designed for ambiguity and can support multiple, simultaneous meanings (e.g., secular vs. sacred). The adaptation of the ghazal into English has also been contentious, with debates over whether strict adherence to classical constraints (like the *qafiya* and *radif*) is necessary for a poem to be considered a "real ghazal."
