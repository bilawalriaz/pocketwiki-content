# Pope Gregory VII

## Overview
Pope Gregory VII (c. 1015–1085), born Hildebrand of Sovana, was a pivotal reforming pope whose reign (1073–1085) was defined by the Investiture Controversy, a conflict with Holy Roman Emperor Henry IV over the right to appoint church officials. He championed papal supremacy, enforced clerical celibacy, and attacked simony (the buying of church offices), fundamentally reshaping the relationship between secular and spiritual authority in medieval Europe.

## Timeline
- **c. 1015** — Born Hildebrand in Sovana, Tuscany.
- **1073** — Elected pope by acclamation; takes the name Gregory VII.
- **1074** — Issues decrees against simony and for clerical celibacy.
- **1075** — Synod reaffirms only the pope can appoint bishops.
- **1076** — Excommunicates Emperor Henry IV; Henry is deposed by a German synod.
- **1077** — Henry IV performs penance at Canossa; Gregory lifts excommunication.
- **1080** — Gregory again excommunicates and deposes Henry IV.
- **1081–84** — Henry IV invades Italy, captures Rome (1084), and installs Antipope Clement III.
- **1085** — Dies in exile in Salerno.

## Body

### Early Life and Rise to Power
Hildebrand was educated in Rome and followed his mentor, Pope Gregory VI, into exile. He later served as a key advisor and administrator to several popes, including Leo IX and Nicholas II. He was instrumental in enacting the 1059 papal election decree that gave cardinals exclusive rights to elect the pope. By 1059, he was archdeacon of Rome, the most powerful figure in the papal administration.

### Election and Reform Program
Gregory was elected pope by popular acclamation in 1073, a process later criticized as irregular. His pontificate launched the Gregorian Reform, which aimed to free the Church from secular control. In 1074 and 1075, he condemned simony and mandated clerical celibacy, threatening excommunication for violators. His core principle was that the pope, as God's vice-regent, held supreme authority over all secular rulers.

### The Investiture Controversy with Henry IV
The central conflict erupted when Gregory decreed that only the pope could appoint bishops, directly challenging the Holy Roman Emperor's traditional right of investiture (appointing officials). After Henry IV continued to appoint bishops and support excommunicated advisors, Gregory excommunicated him in 1076. This act weakened Henry's political standing, forcing him to seek reconciliation. In the famous Walk to Canossa (1077), Henry performed public penance to have the excommunication lifted, though the underlying dispute remained unresolved.

The conflict reignited. In 1080, Gregory again excommunicated and deposed Henry, who responded by convening a council that elected an antipope, Clement III. Henry later captured Rome in 1084, forcing Gregory into exile. He died in Salerno in 1085, having withdrawn most excommunications except those against Henry and Clement III.

### Papal Policy in Europe
Gregory asserted papal authority across Christendom. He clashed with King William I of England over church appointments and the division of English ecclesiastical provinces, though he ultimately accommodated William's power. His relationship with the Normans in southern Italy was fraught; they failed to protect him when Henry IV invaded. He claimed sovereignty over regions like Corsica and Sardinia and attempted to organize a crusade to aid the Byzantine Empire against Muslim attacks, foreshadowing the First Crusade.

### Internal Reforms and Legacy
Gregory's reforms sought to curb lay control of the Church and centralize authority in Rome. His 1079 decree regulating cathedral schools is seen as a key step in the birth of modern universities. He also enforced the doctrine of the Eucharist's real presence. His assertion of papal supremacy made him an enduring symbol for both supporters and opponents of the Catholic Church. He was canonized in 1728.

## Terms
- **Investiture Controversy**: The 11th-12th century conflict between popes and secular rulers over the appointment of church officials.
- **Simony**: The act of buying or selling ecclesiastical privileges, such as church offices.
- **Clerical Celibacy**: The rule requiring Catholic clergy to abstain from marriage and sexual relations, enforced by Gregory.
- **Papal Supremacy**: The doctrine that the pope holds supreme spiritual and temporal authority over all Christians and rulers.
- **Antipope**: A claimant to the papacy in opposition to the legitimately elected pope.
- **Acclamation**: A method of election where a candidate is chosen by the spontaneous, public outcry of a crowd.
- **Excommunication**: A censure excluding a person from the sacraments and communion of the Church.
- **Dictatus Papae**: A list of propositions (c. 1075–87) asserting the pope's absolute authority.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The spontaneity of Gregory's election by acclamation in 1073 is debated; some sources suggest it was orchestrated.
- The exact nature and extent of Gregory's "autocratic" exercise of power were criticized even by contemporaries.
- Historians assess his flexibility and political skill versus his rigid ideological stance, with modern views often highlighting his adaptability.
