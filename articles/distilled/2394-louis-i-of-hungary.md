# Louis I of Hungary

## Overview
Louis I of Hungary (1326–1382), also known as Louis the Great, was a powerful Angevin monarch who ruled Hungary and Croatia from 1342 and Poland from 1370. His reign is noted for military expansion, legal reforms, and the establishment of a personal union between Hungary and Poland, though his attempts to create a lasting empire were ultimately unsuccessful.

## Timeline
- **5 March 1326** — Louis is born in Visegrád, Hungary.
- **16 July 1342** — Louis succeeds his father, Charles I, as King of Hungary.
- **18 September 1345** — His brother Andrew is murdered in Naples, prompting Louis's campaigns there.
- **1347–1350** — Louis launches two military campaigns in the Kingdom of Naples.
- **1351** — Louis holds a Diet, confirming noble liberties and introducing entail and peasant rent laws.
- **18 February 1358** — The Treaty of Zadar forces Venice to renounce Dalmatian towns.
- **5 November 1370** — Louis inherits the Kingdom of Poland after the death of his uncle, Casimir III.
- **17 September 1374** — The Privilege of Koszyce secures Polish nobility's support for his daughters' succession.
- **10/11 September 1382** — Louis dies in Nagyszombat (Trnava), succeeded by his daughter Mary in Hungary and Jadwiga in Poland.

## Body

### Early Reign and Neapolitan Wars
Louis inherited a centralized kingdom and a rich treasury. His early reign was dominated by the goal of avenging his brother Andrew's murder in Naples. He launched two campaigns (1347–1350), occupying large territories and adopting Neapolitan royal titles, but the Holy See never recognized his claim. His rule in Southern Italy was unpopular due to atrocities by his mercenaries, and he withdrew all troops in 1351.

### Domestic Policy and Balkan Expansion
Like his father, Louis ruled with absolute power. At the Diet of 1351, he confirmed the equal liberties of all Hungarian nobles, introduced an entail system for estates, and set a uniform peasant rent ("ninth") while confirming peasants' right to free movement. He waged wars against Lithuania, Serbia, and the Golden Horde in the 1350s, restoring Hungarian authority along frontiers. He forced Venice to renounce Dalmatian towns in 1358 and extended nominal suzerainty over rulers in Bosnia, Moldavia, Wallachia, and parts of Bulgaria and Serbia, though his attempts to convert Orthodox or pagan subjects to Catholicism made him unpopular in the Balkans.

### Union with Poland and Succession
After inheriting Poland in 1370, Louis sought to secure the succession for his daughters. He issued the Privilege of Koszyce in 1374, drastically reducing taxes for Polish nobles in exchange for their recognition of his daughters' rights. His rule in Poland remained unpopular. In Hungary, he reformed the high court system, allowing royal free cities to delegate jurors. In his final years, he supported Pope Urban VI during the Western Schism and helped his relative Charles of Durazzo seize the Neapolitan throne.

### Legacy and Assessment
Louis was the only Hungarian monarch called "the Great." His reign saw significant military success and the expansion of Hungarian influence, though the idea of an empire "washed by three seas" is a later romantic exaggeration. Historians debate whether his wars were realistic or driven by personal pleasure. He was a patron of the arts, commissioning the Illuminated Chronicle and building palaces, but his university foundation in Pécs failed due to lack of funding.

## Terms
- **Angevin:** A European royal house from which Louis descended through his father.
- **Diet:** A formal assembly of nobles and clergy convened to advise the king and consent to laws.
- **Entail:** A legal system introduced by Louis where noble estates passed to male kinsmen, or the Crown, if the owner died without sons.
- **Golden Bull:** A charter of 1222 that defined the liberties of the Hungarian nobility; Louis confirmed most of its provisions.
- **Ninth:** A uniform rent of one-tenth of agricultural produce that landowners were authorized to collect from peasants.
- **Suzerainty:** A relationship where a ruler has nominal authority over another ruler, who retains internal autonomy.
- **Western Schism:** A period (1378–1417) when rival popes in Rome and Avignon were recognized by different European powers; Louis supported the Roman pope, Urban VI.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Motivations for War:** Historians debate whether Louis's frequent wars were driven by realistic political goals or by his personal enjoyment of conflict.
- **Succession in Poland:** The "pessimistic" historical tradition, from Jan of Czarnków, paints his rule in Poland as unstable and oppressive, while the "optimistic" view sees him as preserving Polish unity.
- **Extent of Power:** The claim that his realm was an empire "washed by three seas" is contested, as Poland did not border the Baltic and his authority over Black Sea territories was uncertain.
