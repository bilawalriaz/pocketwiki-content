# Yalta Conference

## Overview
The Yalta Conference (4–11 February 1945) was a pivotal World War II meeting where the "Big Three" Allied leaders—U.S. President Franklin D. Roosevelt, British Prime Minister Winston Churchill, and Soviet General Secretary Joseph Stalin—negotiated the postwar reorganization of Germany and Europe. Held in Crimea, it aimed to establish a collective security order and grant self-determination to liberated peoples. However, the agreements reached, particularly regarding Eastern Europe, became intensely controversial and are seen as a key precursor to the Cold War division of the continent.

## Timeline
- **Nov 1943** — Tehran Conference: First major wartime meeting of the Big Three.
- **Oct 1944** — Moscow Conference: Churchill and Stalin reach an informal spheres-of-influence agreement.
- **4–11 Feb 1945** — Yalta Conference held in Crimea, Soviet Union.
- **1 Mar 1945** — Roosevelt assures U.S. Congress of a "start on the road to a world of peace."
- **11 Mar 1945** — Roosevelt agrees with Churchill that the Yalta agreement on Poland must be upheld.
- **21 Mar 1945** — U.S. Ambassador Harriman cables that the Soviet program is "totalitarianism."
- **27 Mar 1945** — Soviet NKVD arrests 16 Polish opposition leaders invited to provisional government talks.
- **12 Apr 1945** — Death of President Roosevelt.
- **Jul–Aug 1945** — Potsdam Conference: Attlee and Truman meet with Stalin; issues from Yalta are addressed.
- **16 Jan 1947** — Polish elections result in a communist state by 1949.

## Body

### Context and Participants
By February 1945, Allied victory was assured. Soviet forces were 65 km from Berlin, while Western Allies fought on Germany's border. The conference's goal was to shape postwar Europe. France's Charles de Gaulle was not invited, a diplomatic slight he attributed to Roosevelt's personal antagonism, though the Soviets also objected to his inclusion. The meeting was initiated by Roosevelt but held at Stalin's preferred location of Yalta, partly due to his fear of flying.

### Key Negotiations and Agreements
Each leader had a distinct agenda: Roosevelt sought Soviet entry into the Pacific War against Japan and UN participation; Churchill pushed for free elections in Eastern Europe, especially Poland; Stalin demanded a Soviet sphere of influence for security. Poland was the first and most contentious issue. Stalin insisted on retaining Soviet-occupied eastern Polish territory (the Curzon Line) and compensating Poland with German lands in the west. The "Declaration of Liberated Europe" pledged the "earliest possible establishment through free elections governments responsive to the will of the people," but its language was deliberately vague.

Germany was to be divided into four occupation zones (including one for France), undergo demilitarization and denazification, and pay reparations. Stalin agreed to join the UN after securing a veto power for permanent Security Council members and extra votes for Ukraine and Byrussia. In a secret deal, Stalin pledged to enter the Pacific War three months after Germany's defeat in exchange for territorial concessions, including Southern Sakhalin and the Kuril Islands. The Big Three also signed reciprocal repatriation agreements for displaced persons and prisoners of war.

### Aftermath and Legacy
The conference's promises were quickly broken. Stalin installed a communist government in Poland, ignoring the pledge for free elections. By March 1945, U.S. officials realized the Soviet aim was "totalitarianism" in Eastern Europe. The Yalta agreements were seen as a "betrayal" by many Poles and later in American politics, where they were criticized for being too generous to Stalin. The subsequent Cold War division of Europe led to the creation of the Soviet-dominated Eastern Bloc, including satellite states like Poland, Hungary, and East Germany. The Potsdam Conference (July 1945) addressed some unresolved issues but confirmed the new geopolitical reality.

## Terms
- **Big Three**: The three principal Allied powers of World War II: the United States, the United Kingdom, and the Soviet Union.
- **Declaration of Liberated Europe**: A Yalta pledge to allow European peoples to create democratic institutions of their own choice through free elections.
- **Curzon Line**: A proposed demarcation line for Poland's eastern border, based on ethnic demographics, which became the basis for Soviet territorial gains.
- **Denazification**: The Allied policy of removing Nazi ideology and members from positions of influence in postwar Germany.
- **Spheres of influence**: Informal agreements between powers to exercise dominant control within a specific geographic area.
- **Repatriation agreements**: Yalta accords mandating the return of displaced persons and prisoners of war to their countries of origin, regardless of personal consent.
- **Satellite state**: A country that is formally independent but under heavy political, economic, and military control by another power (e.g., Soviet satellite states in Eastern Europe).

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Was Yalta a "betrayal"?** The conference is central to the "Western betrayal" narrative, which argues that the U.S. and UK abandoned Eastern Europe to Soviet domination. Critics claim the vague language on elections allowed Stalin to impose communist regimes.
- **Could the West have acted differently?** Historians debate whether Roosevelt and Churchill had the leverage to enforce the Declaration of Liberated Europe, given the Soviet military occupation of the East. Some argue the agreements were pragmatic concessions to existing power realities.
- **Impact on the Cold War:** Yalta is often cited as a foundational event of the Cold War, but scholars disagree on whether it caused the conflict or merely reflected pre-existing tensions and power imbalances that made conflict likely.
