# Puritans

## Overview
Puritans were English Protestants in the 16th and 17th centuries who sought to purify the Church of England of remaining Roman Catholic practices, believing the Reformation was incomplete. They became a major political force in England and founded influential settlements in New England, shaping early American religious, educational, and political culture. Their legacy includes the development of Congregationalism, ideas on church-state relations, and a lasting impact on Protestant theology and practice.

## Timeline
- **1559** — Elizabethan Religious Settlement establishes the Church of England as Protestant.
- **1564** — First recorded use of the term "Puritan" as a pejorative.
- **1603** — Millenary Petition presents Puritan reform demands to James I.
- **1642–1646** — First English Civil War; Puritans become a major political force.
- **1643** — Westminster Assembly convenes to reform church doctrine and polity.
- **1649** — Execution of Charles I; Puritan-led Commonwealth established.
- **1660** — Restoration of the monarchy; Puritans lose political power.
- **1662** — Act of Uniformity leads to the "Great Ejection" of ~2,400 Puritan clergy.
- **1689** — Toleration Act grants limited freedom to Nonconformist worship.
- **1692** — Salem witch trials occur in Massachusetts.

## Body

### Origins and Terminology
Puritanism emerged from dissatisfaction with the Elizabethan Religious Settlement, which retained Catholic elements like bishops, vestments, and the Book of Common Prayer. The term "Puritan" was initially a pejorative label for Protestants deemed extremist, first recorded in 1564. Puritans themselves used terms like "the godly." The movement included both "non-separating" Puritans who sought reform within the Church of England and "separating" Puritans who left it entirely.

### Theological Foundations
Puritans were Calvinists who embraced covenant theology, which posits a "covenant of works" with Adam and a "covenant of grace" for salvation through Christ's sacrifice. They believed in double predestination (the elect are saved, the damned are not) and that salvation required a personal conversion experience. This experiential piety, involving introspection and a "born again" moment, emphasized sanctification (progressive holiness) and influenced later evangelical Protestantism.

### Church Polity and Worship
Puritans were divided on church governance: some favored episcopal polity (bishops), others presbyterian polity (rule by elders), and a growing number, especially Independents, advocated congregational polity (autonomous local churches). They criticized the Church of England's liturgy, advocating for simpler worship centered on the sermon. They rejected Catholic remnants like the sign of the cross in baptism and kneeling for communion, and eliminated choirs and organs, favoring exclusive psalmody.

### Political Power and Fragmentation
By the 1630s, Puritans allied with parliamentary opposition and Scottish Presbyterians. They gained power after the First English Civil War but failed to establish a unified theocracy. The Westminster Assembly (1643) produced the Westminster Confession of Faith but could not resolve polity disputes. Puritan rule during the Interregnum (1649–1660) saw limited religious freedom but no national Presbyterian church. The movement fragmented over emigration and theological differences.

### The Great Ejection and Nonconformity
The 1660 Restoration and 1662 Act of Uniformity expelled Puritan clergy who refused to conform, leading to the "Great Ejection." These Dissenters formed separate congregations, laying the groundwork for Congregationalist, Presbyterian, and Baptist churches. The 1689 Toleration Act granted them limited legal recognition, and the term "Nonconformist" gradually replaced "Dissenter."

### New England Settlement and Society
Between 1629 and 1640, about 21,000 Puritans migrated to New England, establishing the Massachusetts Bay Colony and others. They created a society focused on biblical literacy, founding schools like the Boston Latin School (1635) and Harvard College (1636). Puritan hegemony in New England lasted about a century, characterized by close church-state ties, strict moral codes, and intolerance toward dissenters like Quakers, though Roger Williams advocated separation of church and state.

### Beliefs, Family, and Social Control
Puritans believed in strict gender roles: husbands were spiritual heads, wives were submissive but influential in the home. They regulated behavior through public censure for sins ranging from fornication to economic fraud. They believed in the active presence of the devil and witchcraft, leading to witch hunts, most famously the Salem trials of 1692. They also held millennialist beliefs, interpreting current events through biblical prophecy.

### Cultural and Political Legacy
Puritans banned Christmas and many forms of recreation, seeing them as impious. They opposed theatre and regulated alcohol. Their emphasis on education and individual conscience influenced the American Revolution and British liberal tradition. Puritanism contributed to the development of modern capitalism (per Max Weber's thesis) and experimental science (the Merton Thesis).

## Terms
- **Covenant Theology**: A Calvinist framework positing God's covenants with humanity: a "covenant of works" with Adam and a "covenant of grace" for salvation through Christ.
- **Double Predestination**: The belief that God has predestined some for salvation (the elect) and others for damnation.
- **Conversion**: The Puritan experience of spiritual regeneration, often involving a personal "born again" moment.
- **Sanctification**: The progressive growth in holiness following conversion.
- **Episcopal Polity**: Church governance by bishops.
- **Presbyterian Polity**: Church governance by elected elders (presbyters).
- **Congregational Polity**: Church governance by the autonomous local congregation.
- **Nonconformist**: A Protestant who does not conform to the doctrines or practices of the established Church of England.
- **Great Ejection**: The 1662 expulsion of Puritan clergy who refused to conform to the Act of Uniformity.
- **Millennialism**: The belief in a future thousand-year reign of Christ on Earth before the Last Judgment.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Historians debate a precise definition of Puritanism, with Patrick Collinson arguing it was defined only in opposition to anti-Puritanism.
- The Merton Thesis linking Puritanism to the rise of experimental science remains debated.
- The extent to which Puritan political thought influenced the American Revolution versus classical republicanism is contested.
- The nature of Puritan intolerance in New England versus their advocacy for religious liberty in England presents a historical paradox.
