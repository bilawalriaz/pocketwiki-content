# Bering Strait

## Overview
The Bering Strait is a narrow, shallow waterway separating the Chukchi Peninsula of Russia from the Seward Peninsula of Alaska. It is a critical geographic and historical chokepoint, famous as the likely migration route for early humans into the Americas and as a symbolic and physical border between continents and, during the Cold War, between superpowers.

## Timeline
- **c. 4.8–7.4 million years ago** — The Bering Strait is thought to have opened.
- **c. 900,000 years ago** — The strait narrows, possibly contributing to the ice age.
- **1648** — Semyon Dezhnyov probably passes through the strait.
- **1728** — Vitus Bering, a Danish-born Russian navigator, enters the strait.
- **1732** — Mikhail Gvozdev becomes the first European to cross from Asia to America.
- **1864** — A Russian-American telegraph company begins preparations for an overland line; later abandoned.
- **March 1913** — Captain Max Gottschalk makes the first documented modern crossing on foot via dogsled.
- **1956** — The Soviet Union proposes a joint dam project to warm the Arctic Ocean.
- **1987** — Lynne Cox swims between the Diomede Islands, a Cold War-era symbolic gesture.
- **August 2008** — First crossing using an amphibious vehicle.
- **Since 2012** — The Russian coast of the Bering Strait is a closed military zone.

## Body

### Geography and Human Migration
The strait is about 82 km wide at its narrowest and only 90 m deep. It connects the Chukchi Sea (Arctic Ocean) to the north and the Bering Sea to the south. The area is sparsely populated by Yupik, Inuit, and Chukchi peoples. The dominant scientific theory for human migration to the Americas posits that during periods of lower sea levels caused by glaciation, a land bridge known as Beringia was exposed, allowing migration from Asia to North America without boats.

### Exploration and Crossings
European knowledge of the strait evolved from the mythical "Strait of Anián" to confirmed passages by Russian explorers like Dezhnyov and Bering in the 17th and 18th centuries. The 20th and 21st centuries have seen numerous adventurous crossings by foot, dogsled, swim, kayak, and vehicle. These crossings often highlight the strait's role as a border, with unauthorized arrivals frequently leading to arrest and deportation, especially since the Russian coast became a closed military zone in 2012.

### Proposed Engineering Projects
The strait has been the subject of ambitious engineering proposals. A telegraph line was planned in 1864 but abandoned. In 1906, a railroad project was authorized but never built. Modern proposals include a 103 km tunnel (the TKM-World Link) and a 200 km underwater railroad. Geopolitical engineering ideas have included a 90 km dam (proposed by the USSR in 1956 to warm the Arctic) and a 300 km dam (proposed in the 21st century to preserve Arctic ice). Earlier, Charles P. Steinmetz proposed widening the strait to melt the Arctic.

### The "Ice Curtain" Border
During the Cold War, the strait was the heavily fortified "Ice Curtain" border between the US and USSR. The Diomede Islands, only 3.8 km apart, became a stark symbol of division, preventing traditional indigenous crossings. This border remains sensitive; the Russian side is now a closed military zone, requiring special permits for foreign visitors.

## Terms
- **Beringia**: The land bridge, now submerged, that connected Asia and North America during periods of lower sea levels.
- **Strait**: A narrow passage of water connecting two larger bodies of water.
- **Paleo-Indians**: The earliest known inhabitants of the Americas.
- **Ice Curtain**: The Cold War-era border between the US and USSR across the Bering Strait.
- **Closed military zone**: A restricted area, like the Russian coast of the Bering Strait since 2012, where civilian access is prohibited without special authorization.
- **Diomede Islands**: Two islands (Big Diomede, Russia; Little Diomede, US) in the Bering Strait that are very close to each other.

## Debates and Open questions
The source presents the Beringia migration theory as the "most accepted" but notes it is a "scientific theory." The proposed engineering projects, such as dams to alter the Arctic climate, are presented as proposals with significant political, financial, and environmental objections, indicating ongoing debate about their feasibility and consequences.
