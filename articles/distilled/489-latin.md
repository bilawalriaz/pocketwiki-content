# Latin

## Overview
Latin is a classical Italic language of the Indo-European family, originally spoken by the Latins in Latium (modern Lazio) around Rome. Through the expansion of the Roman Republic and Empire, it became the dominant language of the Italian Peninsula and the Mediterranean world. Though it ceased to be a native spoken language between the 6th and 9th centuries—evolving into the Romance languages (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, Romanian, Catalan)—Latin survived as the international language of science, scholarship, diplomacy, and the Catholic Church in Europe until the early 19th century. Its highly fusional grammar (inflecting for case, number, gender, tense, mood, voice, and aspect) and vocabulary form the root of much technical terminology in law, medicine, theology, and the sciences, and constitute a major layer of the English lexicon.

## Timeline
- **c. 753 BC** — Traditional founding of Rome; Old Latin spoken in Latium.
- **c. 75 BC** — Transition from Old Latin to standardized Classical Latin (Golden Age literature).
- **1st century BC – 3rd century AD** — Classical Latin flourishes; Vulgar Latin (colloquial registers) diverges in speech.
- **3rd – 6th century AD** — Late Latin written period; accelerated divergence from Classical norms.
- **476 AD** — Fall of Western Roman Empire; Germanic kingdoms adopt Latin for law/administration.
- **6th – 9th century** — Vernacular Latin evolves into distinct Romance languages.
- **c. 700 – 1500** — Medieval Latin: written lingua franca across Western/Catholic Europe, including non-Romance lands.
- **c. 1300 – 1500** — Renaissance Latin: humanist revival of Classical standards; basis for Neo-Latin.
- **1500 – c. 1700** — Neo-Latin peak: vast output in science (e.g., Newton), philosophy, diplomacy; spoken Latin taught in schools.
- **1700 onward** — Decline of spoken Latin skills; shift to French/native languages in diplomacy; education shifts to reading-only.
- **1962–1965** — Second Vatican Council permits vernacular in Mass; Ecclesiastical Latin remains official language of Holy See.
- **Present** — Contemporary Latin used in Church, academia, mottos, media (Radio Bremen, Vatican Radio), and Living Latin movement.

## Body

### Historical Phases
Latin is traditionally divided into phases distinguished by vocabulary, spelling, and syntax, though scholars debate exact boundaries. **Old Latin** (Archaic/Early Latin) was spoken from the Roman Kingdom (trad. 753 BC) to c. 75 BC, attested in inscriptions and early literature (Plautus, Terence). Its alphabet derived from Etruscan, initially written right-to-left or boustrophedon before standardizing left-to-right. **Classical Latin** (c. 75 BC – AD 200) was a conscious literary creation by orators and poets (Cicero, Virgil, Caesar), standardized by grammar schools acting as informal academies. **Vulgar Latin** (*sermo vulgi*) refers to the informal, colloquial registers attested in graffiti, curse tablets, and comic fragments (Plautus, Petronius). It was not a separate language but the spoken counterpart that, free from written standards, developed regional dialects. **Late Latin** (3rd–6th centuries) shows faster divergence: increased preposition use, word order approaching Romance languages, and a growing gap between written and spoken forms. After the Western Empire fell (476), Germanic kingdoms adopted Latin for law; the Byzantine Empire used it for legislation (e.g., Justinian’s *Corpus Iuris Civilis*) until Greek replaced it by the 11th century.

### From Vernacular to Romance Languages
While written Latin standardized, spoken forms diverged. The Muslim conquest of Iberia (711) severed communication between major Romance regions, accelerating differentiation. By the 9th century, distinct Romance languages appear in writing (earliest extant texts), though Medieval Latin remained the written standard. The six most spoken today are Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, Romanian, and Catalan. Romanian diverged earlier due to isolation from western unifying influences (Christian culture). Remarkably, for many Italians (e.g., Petrarch), no sharp separation existed between Latin and Italian into the Renaissance.

### Medieval, Renaissance, and Neo-Latin
**Medieval Latin** (c. 700–1500) functioned as a written lingua franca without a native spoken base, spreading into Germanic and Slavic lands for international communication (Holy Roman Empire). Lacking Roman institutional support, it was linguistically liberal: e.g., using *fui/fueram* instead of *sum/eram* for perfect passive auxiliaries, and shifting word meanings under vernacular influence. **Renaissance Latin** (c. 1300–1500) saw humanists (Petrarch, Erasmus, More) purge Medieval "corruptions" to imitate Classical models. Textual criticism produced accurate editions (Casaubon, Scaliger), though early printing also spread errors. **Neo-Latin** (Renaissance onward) comprises the vast majority of Latin literature—science (Newton’s *Principia*), law, philosophy, theology, and translations (Descartes). Latin remained the language of schooling, diplomacy, and most books until c. 1700, when French replaced it in diplomacy and education shifted from spoken/written to reading-only.

### Contemporary Latin
Latin has no native speakers but persists in specific domains. **Religious:** The Catholic Church retains it as the official language of the Holy See, the Roman Rite, and canon law; the Tridentine Mass uses Latin; the Novus Ordo may use it. Vatican City has a Latin ATM. **Mottos:** Widely used by states (Canada: *A mari usque ad mare*; Spain: *Plus ultra*; USA: *E pluribus unum*), militaries (USMC: *Semper Fidelis*; RAF: *Per ardua ad astra*), and universities (Harvard: *Veritas*). **Other uses:** Switzerland uses *Helvetia/CH* for neutrality; films (*The Passion of the Christ*), opera (*Oedipus rex*), and radio (Vatican Radio, formerly YLE/Radio Bremen) broadcast in Latin. The **Living Latin movement** teaches it as a spoken language (University of Kentucky, Oxford, Princeton). The Latin Wikipedia exceeds 140,000 articles.

### Legacy and Influence
**Romance descendants:** Italian, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Romanian, Catalan, Romansh, Sardinian. Logudorese Sardinian and Standard Italian are phonologically closest to Latin. **English vocabulary:** Latin influence occurred via 6th-century ecclesiastical borrowing (Augustine of Canterbury), the Norman Conquest (Anglo-Norman), and 16th–18th-century "inkhorn terms" (e.g., *imbibe, extrapolate*). Romance words constitute ~59% of English, 20% of German, 14% of Dutch vocabularies. **Technical terminology:** Roman law, medicine (Galen), engineering, and taxonomy (Linnaeus, influenced by Pliny’s *Historia Naturalis*) established Latin/Greek as the source for modern scientific and legal terms. **Education:** Classics were central to European and early US elite education (Harvard entrance required Latin prose/verse). Wheelock’s *Latin* (1956) remains a standard US textbook. Study varies: ~500,000 students/year in Germany (down from 800k in 2008); 2.3% of UK state primaries offer it. **Official status:** Kingdom of Hungary (11th–1844), Poland/Lithuania (10th–18th c.), Croatian Parliament (13th–1847; coins until 2023).

### Phonology and Orthography
**Consonants:** Reconstructed via ancient grammarians, misspellings, puns, loanwords, and Romance outcomes. /z/ appears only in Greek loans (1st c. BC). ⟨q⟩ always followed by ⟨u⟩ = [kʷ]. Double consonants = long (geminate) sounds (e.g., *annus* = /annus/). Classical Latin lacked ⟨J, U, W⟩; ⟨I, V⟩ served as both vowels/consonants. **Vowels:** Distinct long/short quantity (marked by apex/macron in modern texts). Quality differs: long /aː/ vs short /a/, etc. (Allen’s *Vox Latina* vs. Calabrese’s Sardinian counter-evidence). Nasal vowels before final ⟨m⟩ or ⟨n⟩+⟨s/f⟩. **Diphthongs:** ⟨ae⟩ (like *mine*), ⟨au⟩ (like *power*); ⟨oe⟩ rare. Old Latin ⟨ai, oi, ou, ei⟩ monophthongized to Classical ⟨ae, ū, ī⟩. By late Empire, ⟨ae, oe⟩ merged with ⟨e⟩ in speech. **Syllables:** Count = vowel sounds. Stress: penult if long, else antepenult (disyllables stress first). **Orthography:** Alphabet derived Etruscan → Greek → Phoenician; now world’s most used script. Classical texts lacked punctuation, case, and word spacing (interpuncts sometimes used). **Alternative scripts:** Praeneste fibula (7th c. BC, Etruscan script); Franks Casket (8th c., mixed runes/Latin).

### Grammar (Morphology and Syntax)
Latin is **synthetic** and **fusional**: semantic roots combine with suffixes marking grammatical categories. Word order is free (inflections disambiguate), but nuances shift with order. **Nouns/Adjectives/Pronouns** decline into **7 cases**: Nominative (subject), Genitive (possession/partitive), Dative (indirect object/agent), Accusative (direct object/extent), Ablative (separation/instrument/prepositional), Vocative (address), Locative (place—cities/small islands/domus). **5 declensions** grouped by stem vowel and genitive singular ending. No articles (*puer currit* = "the/a boy runs"). **Adjectives:** 1st/2nd declension (gendered like nouns) and 3rd declension (mostly like 3rd declension nouns). **Verbs:** 4 conjugations by present stem vowel (ā, ē, e, ī). **6 tenses** (present, imperfect, future, perfect, pluperfect, future perfect) in 2 systems (present/perfect). **3 moods** (indicative, imperative, subjunctive) + non-finite forms. **2 voices** (active, passive; deponent verbs = passive form, active meaning). **4 principal parts** (1st sg. pres. act. ind., pres. act. inf., 1st sg. perf. act. ind., supine/perf. pass. part.). **Prepositions** govern Accusative or Ablative (rarely Genitive). **Numbers:** 1–3 and hundreds declined; 4–100 indeclinable.

### Vocabulary and Numbers
Core vocabulary is Italic (Proto-Indo-European). Borrowings: Etruscan (*persona, histrio*), Greek (post-272 BC Hellenization: *camera, symbolum, balineum*; added Y, Z to alphabet), Germanic (*beber*), Celtic (*bracae*), Hebrew/Greek (Christian terms), Germanic/Old English (Middle Ages). Word formation via compounding/affixing (e.g., *omnipotens* from *omnis* + *potens*). Numbers written with letters (Roman numerals) or Arabic digits; 1–3 and hundreds decline for gender.

## Terms
- ****Fusional language**** — A synthetic language where a single affix encodes multiple grammatical categories (e.g., Latin *-t* = 3rd person + singular + present + active + indicative).
- ****Declension**** — A class of nouns/adjectives/pronouns sharing inflectional patterns for case, number, and gender (Latin has five).
- ****Conjugation**** — A class of verbs sharing inflectional patterns for person, number, tense, voice, mood, and aspect (Latin has four main conjugations).
- ****Vulgar Latin**** — The colloquial, spoken registers of Latin (*sermo vulgi*), distinct from the literary standard, which diverged regionally into Romance languages.
- ****Neo-Latin**** — Latin composed from the Renaissance (c. 1300) onward, encompassing scientific, literary, and diplomatic works (e.g., Newton, Erasmus).
- ****Ecclesiastical Latin**** — The form used by the Roman Catholic Church for liturgy, canon law, and official documents; retains living pronunciation (Italianate).
- ****Living Latin**** — A pedagogical movement teaching Latin as a spoken, communicative language rather than solely a written/read one.
- ****Inkhorn terms**** — 16th–18th century English neologisms coined from Latin/Greek roots, often criticized as pedantic (e.g., *imbibe, extrapolate*).
- ****Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum (CIL)**** — The monumental multi-volume collection of ~270,000 known Latin inscriptions, standard for epigraphy.
- ****Deponent verb**** — A verb with passive morphology but active meaning (e.g., *hortor, hortārī, hortātus sum* "to urge").

## Debates and open questions
*   **Periodization:** Scholars disagree on exact boundaries and defining features between Old, Classical, Late, and Medieval Latin; alternative schemes exist.
*   **Vulgar Latin definition:** The term ambiguously covers both informal speech at any period and the specific post-Imperial spoken variety ancestral to Romance; 19th-century view of it as a separate parallel language is rejected.
*   **Vowel quality:** W. Sidney Allen (*Vox Latina*) argues long/short vowels differed in quality (e.g., /iː/ vs /ɪ/); Andrea Calabrese disputes this, citing Sardinian/Lucanian mergers of quantity pairs without quality distinction.
*   **⟨ui⟩ as diphthong:** Debated due to rarity, absence in Roman grammarians, and etymological alternations (e.g., *huic* vs *quoi* → *cui*).
*   **Pronunciation reconstruction:** Relies on indirect evidence (ancient testimony, misspellings, Romance outcomes); the "Classical" pronunciation taught today is a scholarly reconstruction, not a continuous tradition.
*   **Extent of Neo-Latin corpus:** The vast majority of all Latin text belongs to the Neo-Latin period, but its full scope remains uncatalogued and understudied.
