# Arab–Byzantine wars

## Overview
The Arab–Byzantine wars were a series of conflicts from the 7th to 11th centuries between the Islamic caliphates and the Byzantine Empire. They began with the rapid Muslim conquests that stripped Byzantium of its wealthiest provinces, leading to a centuries-long struggle for survival and later resurgence. The wars fundamentally reshaped the Mediterranean world, halted Arab expansion into Europe, and contributed to the political and religious divergence between Western and Eastern Christendom.

## Timeline
- **634–638** — Arab conquest of Roman Syria, culminating in the decisive Battle of the Yarmuk (636).
- **639–642** — Arab conquest of Egypt under 'Amr ibn al-'As.
- **647–698** — Gradual Arab conquest of the Byzantine Exarchate of Africa, ending with the fall of Carthage.
- **655** — Byzantine naval defeat at the Battle of the Masts opens the Mediterranean to Arab expansion.
- **674–678** — First Arab Siege of Constantinople, repelled by the use of "Greek fire."
- **717–718** — Second Arab Siege of Constantinople, a major Byzantine defensive victory.
- **c. 720–863** — Stabilization of the frontier along the Taurus Mountains; era of ritualized raids and counter-raids.
- **827–878** — Aghlabid conquest of Sicily from the Byzantines.
- **863** — Byzantine victory at the Battle of Lalakaon marks the start of a military resurgence.
- **961** — Byzantine reconquest of Crete.
- **969** — Byzantine capture of Antioch and parts of northern Syria.
- **1025** — Byzantine Empire under Basil II reaches its greatest territorial extent in centuries.

## Body

### Background and Initial Conquests (629–718)
The wars began in the aftermath of the exhausting Byzantine–Sasanian wars and the Plague of Justinian, which left both empires vulnerable. Following the unification of Arabia under Islam, Rashidun forces launched a rapid offensive. Syria was conquered after the Battle of the Yarmuk (636), and Egypt fell by 642. The loss of these provinces deprived Byzantium of crucial grain supplies and manpower. Arab naval power, established in the 650s, challenged Byzantine control of the Mediterranean. The Umayyad Caliphate, which succeeded the Rashidun in 661, continued expansion, capturing North Africa and launching two major sieges of Constantinople (674–678 and 717–718). Both sieges failed, the second decisively, stabilizing the frontier along the Taurus Mountains.

### Frontier Warfare and the Abbasid Period (718–863)
After 718, outright conquest gave way to a fortified frontier zone. The Arab side developed the *thughur* (fortified border districts), while the Byzantines organized their remaining Anatolian territory into *themata* (military-administrative districts). Warfare became ritualized, with annual Arab summer and winter raids (*ṣawā'if* and *shawātī*) aimed at plunder rather than conquest. The Abbasid Caliphate, which overthrew the Umayyads in 750, was less expansionist but continued these raids, which were important for caliphal prestige. This period also saw the Byzantine adoption of Iconoclasm (the banning of religious images), partly in response to military setbacks and natural disasters, which caused internal religious strife and weakened ties with the Papacy. Meanwhile, Arab forces conquered Crete (c. 827) and gradually took Sicily (831–878).

### Byzantine Resurgence and Peak (863–11th Century)
A turning point came with the Byzantine victory at the Battle of Lalakaon (863). Under the Macedonian dynasty (from 867), a resurgent Byzantium exploited the fragmentation of the Abbasid Caliphate. Generals like Nikephoros Phokas and John Tzimiskes conquered the Emirate of Melitene, pushed into Syria, and recovered Crete (961). By the reign of Basil II (r. 976–1025), the Byzantine Empire had reconquered Antioch and much of northern Syria, reaching its greatest extent in five centuries. This resurgence was enabled by the *theme* system, which provided a large, locally-based army.

### Effects and Historiography
The long wars had profound effects. Byzantium lost its western provinces but survived as a defensive power focused on its eastern frontier. The Empire's inability to protect the Papacy from Arab raids and its internal religious conflicts (like Iconoclasm) contributed to the Great Schism and the rise of Frankish power under Charlemagne. Historians note that both Arabic and Byzantine sources are problematic and biased, requiring critical scrutiny. The conflict's legacy includes the lasting political and cultural division between the Islamic world and Christian Europe.

## Terms
- **Themata**: Large military-administrative districts in Anatolia, formed from old field armies, where soldiers were granted land in exchange for service. They formed the backbone of Byzantine defense.
- **Thughur**: The fortified Arab frontier zone along the Taurus Mountains, comprising refortified cities like Tarsus and Melitene.
- **Iconoclasm**: A Byzantine policy (c. 726–787 and 814–843) of banning the veneration of religious images, adopted partly in response to military crises and perceived divine disfavor.
- **Greek fire**: A devastating incendiary weapon used by the Byzantine navy, crucial in repelling the Arab sieges of Constantinople.
- **Jihad**: In this context, the Islamic concept of struggle or warfare in the path of God, used to justify the annual raids against the Byzantine "House of War" (*Dār al-Ḥarb*).
- **Ṣawā'if**: The plural of *ṣā'ifa*, referring to the large-scale Arab summer raids into Byzantine Anatolia.
- **Hudna**: A temporary truce in Islamic law, contrasting with permanent peace (*ṣulḥ*), which could only be achieved through the enemy's conversion or submission.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise numbers and composition of forces in major battles (e.g., the sieges of Constantinople) are debated, as sources are often late and contradictory.
- The extent to which local populations (like Monophysites in Syria or Copts in Egypt) welcomed or resisted the Arab conquest is a subject of scholarly discussion.
- The primary causes and motivations behind the Byzantine adoption of Iconoclasm—whether it was a direct response to military defeats, a theological movement, or a political tool—remain debated.
- The relative impact of the Arab wars versus internal decay in weakening the Byzantine Empire before the Seljuk victory at Manzikert (1071) is a key historiographical question.
