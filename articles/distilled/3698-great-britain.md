# Great Britain

## Overview
Great Britain is the largest island in the British Isles and the ninth-largest in the world, comprising the countries of England, Scotland, and Wales. It is a major European landmass with a rich history of human settlement, geological formation, and political union, forming the core of the United Kingdom.

## Timeline
- **c. 950,000–850,000 years ago** — Oldest evidence of archaic humans (Happisburgh footprints) found in Britain.
- **c. 40,000 years ago** — Modern humans arrive in Britain.
- **c. 6500 BC** — Britain becomes an island as rising sea levels flood the Doggerland land bridge.
- **c. 4000 BC** — Neolithic farmers from Anatolia arrive, replacing hunter-gatherers.
- **c. 2000 BC** — Bronze Age Bell Beaker Culture arrives, associated with major population replacement.
- **AD 43–c. 410** — Roman conquest and occupation of most of the island, creating the province of Britannia.
- **5th–6th centuries** — Anglo-Saxon settlement in the east and south; Gaelic expansion in the north-west.
- **1066** — Norman conquest of England.
- **1603** — Union of the Crowns: James VI of Scotland becomes James I of England, creating a personal union.
- **1707** — Acts of Union merge the English and Scottish parliaments, creating the Kingdom of Great Britain.

## Body

### Terminology and Name
The archipelago has been called the "British Isles" for over 2,000 years. The earliest name for the main island was **Albion**, possibly from Latin for "white," referring to the cliffs of Dover. Greek explorer Pytheas (c. 320 BC) used a term derived from a Celtic word possibly meaning "the painted ones." The Latin **Britannia** was used by the Romans for the island. The prefix "Great" was first used officially in 1474 to distinguish the larger island from "Lesser Britain" (Brittany). Politically, "Great Britain" refers to England, Scotland, and Wales, but not Northern Ireland.

### Prehistory and Geology
Britain was a peninsula of Europe until catastrophic floods created the English Channel between 450,000 and 180,000 years ago. It became a true island around 6500 BC when the **Doggerland** land bridge was submerged. The island's geology is complex, with its oldest rocks (Lewisian gneisses) dating to at least 2,700 million years ago. The landscape varies from low rolling countryside in the east and south to hills and mountains in the west and north.

### History and Political Union
After the Roman withdrawal, Germanic tribes (Angles, Saxons, Jutes) settled the south and east, while Gaelic tribes from Ireland influenced the north-west, leading to the formation of the Kingdom of Scotland by the 9th century. The Norman Conquest of 1066 reshaped English society. A personal union of the English and Scottish crowns began in 1603. This was formalized into a single political state, the Kingdom of Great Britain, by the **Acts of Union** in 1707.

### Demographics and Culture
The population was about 61 million in 2011. London is the capital of England and the UK. The dominant language is British English, which developed from Old English. Celtic languages survive: Welsh (c. 700,000 speakers), Scottish Gaelic, and Cornish. Christianity has been the largest religion since the Early Middle Ages, with Anglicanism as the most popular denomination. The island's fauna and flora are less diverse than continental Europe's due to its size, isolation, and history of habitat loss, though it hosts significant birdlife.

## Terms
- **Doggerland**: A now-submerged land bridge that connected Great Britain to mainland Europe until about 6500 BC.
- **Albion**: The earliest known name for the island of Great Britain.
- **Britannia**: The Latin name used by the Romans for the island and later the Roman province.
- **Acts of Union (1707)**: Legislation that merged the parliaments of England and Scotland, creating the Kingdom of Great Britain.
- **Union of the Crowns (1603)**: The personal union of the English and Scottish crowns under James VI and I.
- **Brythonic languages**: A branch of Celtic languages including Welsh, Cornish, and Breton.
- **Anglicanism**: A major Christian tradition originating from the English Reformation, with the British monarch as its Supreme Governor in England.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise meaning of "Great Britain" in common and political usage is fluid; it is often used loosely to refer to the entire United Kingdom, though this is technically incorrect.
- The origin of the name "Britain" from Pytheas's term is debated, with theories ranging from "painted ones" to other Celtic roots.
- The extent and nature of population replacements during the Bronze Age (Bell Beaker Culture) and Anglo-Saxon periods are subjects of ongoing genetic and archaeological research.
