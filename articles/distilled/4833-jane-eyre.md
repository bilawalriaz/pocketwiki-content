# Jane Eyre

## Overview
*Jane Eyre* is a novel by Charlotte Brontë, published in 1847 under the pen name "Currer Bell." It is a bildungsroman (a coming-of-age story) that follows the moral and spiritual development of its eponymous heroine through an intimate first-person narrative. The novel is celebrated for its revolutionary psychological intensity and its exploration of themes like class, religion, and feminism, making it a foundational work in English literature.

## Timeline
- **1847** — *Jane Eyre* is published in London by Smith, Elder & Co.
- **1848** — The first American edition is published by Harper & Brothers.
- **19th C.** — The novel is originally published in three volumes.
- **1857** — Elizabeth Gaskell's biography reveals the autobiographical elements of the novel.
- **1966** — Jean Rhys publishes *Wide Sargasso Sea*, a prequel reimagining Bertha Mason's story.
- **1979** — S. Gilbert and S. Gubar publish *The Madwoman in the Attic*, a key feminist analysis.
- **2003** — The novel is ranked 10th in the BBC's "The Big Read" poll for Britain's best-loved book.
- **2011** — A major film adaptation starring Mia Wasikowska is released.

## Body

### Plot and Structure
The novel is a first-person narrative divided into 38 chapters, originally published in three volumes. It follows Jane Eyre through five distinct stages of her life in the north of England during the late reign of George III. The story begins with her abusive childhood at Gateshead Hall, followed by her education at the harsh Lowood Institution. She then becomes a governess at Thornfield Hall, where she falls in love with her employer, Edward Rochester. After fleeing Thornfield, she finds refuge with the Rivers family at Moor House before ultimately reuniting with and marrying Rochester. The narrative is notable for its psychological depth and its direct address to the reader, famously concluding with the line, "Reader, I married him."

### Context and Autobiographical Elements
The early sequences at Lowood are derived from Charlotte Brontë's own experiences. The death of Helen Burns from consumption (tuberculosis) recalls the deaths of Brontë's sisters Elizabeth and Maria at the Clergy Daughters School. The tyrannical Mr. Brocklehurst is based on the school's minister, Rev. William Carus Wilson. The Gothic manor of Thornfield Hall was likely inspired by North Lees Hall, which had a history of confining a lunatic. The novel's themes of social class and the role of a governess also reflect Brontë's own life.

### Genres and Literary Significance
*Jane Eyre* is a hybrid of several genres. As a **bildungsroman**, it charts Jane's growth to maturity and self-knowledge. It incorporates **Gothic** elements, such as the mysterious laughter at Thornfield and the "madwoman in the attic," Bertha Mason, who serves as Jane's dark double. The novel is also a **romance**, but one that subverts conventions by focusing on the heroine's moral and spiritual integrity. Literary critic Daniel S. Burt has called Brontë "the first historian of the private consciousness," positioning her as an ancestor to modernist writers.

### Major Themes
The novel engages with several key social issues. **Feminism** is central, as Jane asserts her equality and spiritual independence, famously declaring to Rochester, "I am not talking to you now through the medium of custom... it is my spirit that addresses your spirit; just as if both had passed through the grave, and we stood at God's feet, equal,—as we are." **Social class** is explored through Jane's transitions from dependent orphan to independent woman. **Race** is a critical theme, embodied by Bertha Mason, a Creole woman from the West Indies whose perceived "impurity" and madness are contrasted with Jane's European rationality. This racial dynamic has been extensively analyzed, notably in Jean Rhys's prequel *Wide Sargasso Sea*.

## Terms
- **Bildungsroman**: A novel dealing with one person's formative years or spiritual education.
- **Gothic**: A genre using supernatural elements, intense emotion, and a dark, mysterious atmosphere.
- **Homodiegetic narrator**: A narrator who is also a character in the story.
- **Byronic hero**: A brooding, rebellious, and mysterious male character, exemplified by Mr. Rochester.
- **Coverture**: A legal doctrine where a woman's legal rights were subsumed by those of her husband.
- **Consumption**: An archaic term for tuberculosis.
- **Creole**: A person of mixed European and African descent, born in a European colony.
- **Pen name**: A pseudonym, especially one used by an author (e.g., "Currer Bell").

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Bertha Mason's Race and Madness**: Scholars debate whether Bertha's madness is a result of her racial "impurity," as suggested by 19th-century beliefs, or a response to her oppression and confinement.
- **Feminist Interpretation**: While seen as a pioneering feminist text, some critics note that Jane's ultimate fulfillment comes through marriage and domesticity, complicating a straightforward feminist reading.
- **Narrative Reliability**: The close first-person perspective has been criticized for making the reader "too uncritically accepting of her worldview," raising questions about Jane's reliability as a narrator.
- **The "Gothic Double"**: The relationship between Jane and Bertha is a central scholarly debate, with Bertha often interpreted as Jane's repressed, passionate, and "savage" double.
