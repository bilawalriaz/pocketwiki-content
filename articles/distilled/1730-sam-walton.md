# Sam Walton

## Overview
Samuel Moore Walton (1918–1992) was an American business magnate who co-founded Walmart and Sam’s Club, building the world’s largest corporation by revenue and biggest private employer. After learning retail at JCPenney and operating Ben Franklin variety stores, he launched the first Walmart in 1962 in Rogers, Arkansas, pioneering a model of discount stores in small towns served by a centralized logistics network. His strategy—everyday low prices, volume buying, and company-owned trucking—drove explosive growth from 190 stores in 1977 to 1,960 by his death. Walton was the richest person in the U.S. from 1982 to 1988; his family remained the wealthiest in the nation for decades. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1992, weeks before dying of multiple myeloma at age 74.

## Body

### Early life and education
Walton was born in Kingfisher, Oklahoma, and moved frequently as his father shifted from farming to farm mortgaging during the Great Depression. The family settled in Columbia, Missouri, where Walton worked paper routes, milk delivery, and magazine sales to help support the household. He graduated from David H. Hickman High School, voted “Most Versatile Boy,” then attended the University of Missouri as an ROTC cadet. He earned a B.A. in economics in 1940, serving as president of Burall Bible Class and joining Beta Theta Pi, the secret society QEBH, and Scabbard and Blade. He credited his upbringing with teaching him to be a “giver rather than taker” and decided during Army service that he wanted to go into retailing for himself.

### Military service
Three days after college graduation, Walton joined JCPenney as a management trainee in Des Moines. He resigned after 18 months in anticipation of the draft, briefly working at a DuPont munitions plant near Tulsa. He then entered the U.S. Army Intelligence Corps, supervising security at aircraft plants at Fort Douglas in Salt Lake City, Utah, and attained the rank of captain before leaving in 1945.

### The first stores
With a $20,000 loan from father-in-law Leland Robson and $5,000 of his own savings, Walton bought a Ben Franklin franchise in Newport, Arkansas, in 1945. He pioneered concepts that defined his later success: pricing as good as or better than distant city stores to keep shoppers local, and keeping shelves consistently stocked. Sales grew from $80,000 to $225,000 in three years. The landlord, P. K. Holmes, declined to renew the lease—lacking a renewal option and charging 5% of sales as rent—to give the store to his son. Holmes bought the inventory for $50,000, which Walton called “a fair price.” The episode taught Walton the critical importance of lease terms.

With a year left on the Newport lease, Walton negotiated a new location on the Bentonville, Arkansas square. He secured a 99-year lease on the adjacent shop only after his father-in-law paid the reluctant owner $20,000 without Walton’s knowledge. The store opened May 9, 1950, with a one-day remodeling sale. Sales rose from the prior $72,000 to $105,000 in year one, then $140,000 and $175,000.

### A chain of Ben Franklin stores
Managing stores 220 miles apart forced Walton to delegate. He and his brother Bud, a former pilot, bought a small airplane to scout locations. In 1954 they opened a store in Ruskin Heights, Missouri. Walton encouraged managers to invest up to $1,000 in their store or the next outlet, aligning incentives and sharpening managerial focus. By 1962, the brothers owned 16 stores across Arkansas, Missouri, and Kansas (fifteen Ben Franklins and one independent in Fayetteville).

### First Walmart
The first Wal-Mart Discount City opened July 2, 1962, at 719 West Walnut Street in Rogers, Arkansas. Walton acknowledged that the one-stop-shopping format was based on Meijer’s innovative concept. Contrary to industry practice, he placed stores in small towns rather than large cities, where competition was limited and a dominant store could deter new entrants. To make the model work, he built a logistics backbone: stores located within a day’s drive of regional warehouses, supplied by Walmart’s own trucking fleet. Volume buying and efficient delivery enabled discounted name-brand merchandise. Growth accelerated from 190 stores in 1977 to 800 in 1985. The company’s regional impact—both positive and negative—became known as the “Walmart Effect.”

### Personal life
Walton married Helen Robson on Valentine’s Day 1943; they had four children: Rob (1944), John (1946–2005), Jim (1948), and Alice (1949). He was a long-term supporter of country music; Dolly Parton’s debut album *Hello, I’m Dolly* was the first widely stocked at Walmart in 1967. Active in the 1st Presbyterian Church in Bentonville, Walton served as Elder and Sunday School teacher. He integrated the Christian concept of “servant leadership”—Christ as a servant leader—into Walmart’s corporate structure, emphasizing service to others.

### Health issues and death
Diagnosed with hairy cell leukemia in 1982 and bone cancer in 1990, Walton underwent radiation and chemotherapy at MD Anderson Cancer Center in Houston. He died April 5, 1992, of multiple myeloma in Little Rock, a week after his 74th birthday. Days earlier he was still reviewing sales data from his hospital bed. News of his death was relayed by satellite to all 1,960 stores. At that time the company employed 400,000 people and generated nearly $50 billion in annual sales from 1,735 Walmarts, 212 Sam’s Clubs, and 13 Supercenters. Ownership passed to his wife and children; Rob succeeded him as Chairman, John served as a director until his 2005 plane crash, and Jim chairs Arvest Bank. The Walton family held five of the top ten spots among richest Americans until 2005.

### Honors and awards
*Time* named Walton one of the 100 most influential people of the 20th Century (1998). He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom from President George H. W. Bush in March 1992. Forbes ranked him richest in the U.S. from 1982 to 1988; the top spot shifted to John Kluge in 1989 when editors began crediting the fortune jointly to Walton and his four children. Bill Gates first headed the list in 1992, the year Walton died. The University of Missouri awarded him an honorary LL.D. in 1984; the Sam M. Walton College of Business at the University of Arkansas bears his name; he was inducted into the Junior Achievement U.S. Business Hall of Fame in 1992.

## Terms
- **Ben Franklin variety store**: A franchised five-and-dime chain (Butler Brothers) where Walton learned retail operations before founding Walmart.
- **Everyday low prices (EDLP)**: A pricing strategy promising consistently low prices without temporary sales or promotions; central to Walmart’s model.
- **Walmart Effect**: The significant economic and social impact—both positive and negative—that a Walmart store has on a region where it locates.
- **Servant leadership**: A leadership philosophy, rooted in Christian theology, where the leader’s primary role is to serve others; Walton embedded this in Walmart’s culture.
- **Regional warehouse / distribution center**: A logistics hub supplying stores within a one-day drive; enabled Walmart’s efficient, company-owned trucking network.
- **Hairy cell leukemia**: A rare, slow-growing blood cancer diagnosed in Walton in 1982.
- **Multiple myeloma**: A cancer of plasma cells (a type of blood cancer) that caused Walton’s death in 1992.
- **Sam’s Club**: A membership-only warehouse retail chain founded by Walton in 1983 in Midwest City, Oklahoma.
- **Meijer**: A Midwest superstore chain whose one-stop-shopping concept Walton acknowledged as the basis for Walmart’s format.
- **Presidential Medal of Freedom**: The highest civilian award in the United States, presented to Walton in March 1992.

## Debates and open questions
- **Small-town impact**: The “Walmart Effect” remains debated—proponents cite lower consumer prices and job creation; critics argue it displaces local retailers, depresses wages, and hollows out downtowns.
- **Labor practices**: Walton’s anti-union stance and Walmart’s wage and benefit policies have drawn sustained criticism and legal challenges.
- **Sourcing and “Buy American”**: The 1962 launch emphasized American-made products, but the company’s massive scale later drove heavy reliance on global supply chains, raising questions about the durability of the original commitment.
- **Succession and family control**: The transition to the next generation (Rob Walton as Chairman, family ownership via Walton Enterprises) illustrates the governance challenges of a founder-led public company with concentrated family control.
