# Woman

## Overview

A woman is an adult female human, distinguished biologically by two X chromosomes and female reproductive anatomy, and socially by gender roles that vary across cultures and history. The concept of "woman" encompasses both biological sex and gender identity, including cisgender women (whose sex assigned at birth aligns with their gender identity) and transgender women (who were assigned male at birth). Women have been central to human societies throughout history, yet have often faced systemic limitations due to patriarchal structures. Understanding women involves examining biological, psychological, social, and historical dimensions, all of which continue to evolve.

## Body

### Biology and Anatomy

Women typically have two X chromosomes, while men have an XY combination. During fetal development, all embryos initially develop female-typical genitalia until around week 6 or 7, when the SRY gene on the Y chromosome triggers male differentiation in males. Female sex differentiation occurs independently of gonadal hormones. Key reproductive organs include the ovaries (which produce ova), fallopian tubes, uterus, vagina, and vulva. Secondary sex characteristics such as breast development, wider hips, and higher body fat composition emerge during puberty due to estrogen. Menarche, the onset of menstruation, typically occurs between ages 12 and 13. Menopause, marking the end of reproductive capability, usually occurs between ages 49 and 52. Women generally live longer than men, with a global average advantage of six to eight years.

### Gender and Identity

Gender refers to the social and cultural roles associated with being male or female, distinct from biological sex. Most cultures recognize a binary system of gender, though some acknowledge third genders. Femininity—attributes and behaviors associated with women—is shaped by both biological influences and social construction. Most women are cisgender, meaning their gender identity matches their sex assigned at birth. Some women are transgender, having been assigned male at birth but identifying as female. Transgender women may experience gender dysphoria and seek gender-affirming care, including hormone therapy and surgery. Intersex individuals have atypical sex characteristics involving chromosomes, genitalia, or gonads, and may identify as women.

### Health

Women face unique health risks and conditions. They have higher rates of osteoporosis, autoimmune diseases, Alzheimer's disease, and breast cancer, while having lower rates of lung cancer, Parkinson's disease, and hypertension. Female-specific conditions include endometriosis, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), and gynecologic cancers. Maternal mortality remains a critical issue, with approximately 260,000 deaths globally in 2023, predominantly in low-income countries. Reproductive rights—including access to contraception, safe abortion, and prenatal care—are crucial determinants of women's health. Drug responses and diagnostic thresholds can differ between sexes, underscoring the importance of sex-based medical research.

### History and Society

Women have played pivotal roles throughout history, though often underrepresented in formal records. Known historical figures include Enheduanna (c. 2285 BCE), the first named author, and various queens and rulers of ancient civilizations. In recent centuries, women's participation in education and the workforce has expanded dramatically. In the U.S., married women's labor force participation rose from 5.6% in 1900 to 23.8% in 1923. By the 21st century, women in many OECD countries surpass men in educational attainment, though they remain underrepresented in STEM fields and leadership positions. Women's suffrage movements achieved voting rights in most countries by the mid-20th century, though some nations granted this right only in the 21st century.

### Violence and Rights

Violence against women is a pervasive global problem, defined by the UN as any act of gender-based violence resulting in harm. Forms include domestic abuse, sexual harassment, trafficking, forced marriage, female genital mutilation, and honor killings. Patriarchal norms, inadequate legal protections, and poor enforcement perpetuate this violence. Sexual violence escalates during armed conflict, as seen in genocides and wars throughout history. Legal recognition of crimes such as marital rape has progressed slowly in many regions. Feminist movements continue advocating for gender equality, reproductive autonomy, and protection from violence.

### Culture and Roles

Gender roles have shifted significantly, especially in the 20th and 21st centuries. Traditional expectations often confined women to domestic spheres, but economic necessity and social change have expanded opportunities. The gender pay gap persists across most societies, and women remain underrepresented in executive and political leadership. Educational disparities have largely reversed in developed nations, with women earning the majority of university degrees. However, barriers in career advancement, particularly in science and technology, remain. Cultural attitudes toward marriage, motherhood, and family structure continue evolving, with increasing acceptance of diverse arrangements.

## Terms

- **Cisgender**: A person whose gender identity corresponds with the sex they were assigned at birth.
- **Transgender**: A person whose gender identity differs from the sex they were assigned at birth.
- **Intersex**: A person born with reproductive or sexual anatomy that doesn't fit typical definitions of male or female.
- **Menarche**: The first occurrence of menstruation in a female during puberty.
- **Menopause**: The time when menstrual periods stop permanently, typically occurring between ages 49 and 52.
- **Estrogen**: Primary female sex hormone responsible for development of secondary sexual characteristics.
- **SRY gene**: A gene on the Y chromosome that triggers male sex differentiation; its absence leads to female development.
- **Gender dysphoria**: Psychological distress resulting from the incongruence between one's gender identity and sex assigned at birth.
- **Gender-affirming care**: Medical and social interventions that help align a person's physical or social expression with their gender identity.
- **Femininity**: Attributes, behaviors, and roles culturally associated with women and girls.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several contentious issues surround the definition and treatment of women. The inclusion of transgender women in spaces designated for women, such as sports and shelters, generates ongoing debate about fairness and safety. Medical interventions for intersex individuals, particularly non-consensual procedures in infancy, are increasingly criticized by human rights advocates. Disagreements persist over the balance between religious freedom and women's rights, especially regarding reproductive autonomy and dress codes. The extent to which gender roles are biologically determined versus socially constructed remains a subject of scholarly discussion. Additionally, the global variation in women's rights—from suffrage to reproductive freedom—reflects ongoing tensions between tradition, law, and modernity.
