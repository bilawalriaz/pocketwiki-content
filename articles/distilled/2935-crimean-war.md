# Crimean War

## Overview
The Crimean War (1853–1856) was a conflict between Russia and an alliance of the Ottoman Empire, France, the United Kingdom, and Sardinia. It was fought primarily over control of territories within the declining Ottoman Empire and the balance of power in Europe. The war is significant for its early use of modern technologies like railways and telegraphs, its extensive media coverage, and its role in catalyzing major social and political reforms in Russia, including the abolition of serfdom.

## Timeline
- **1853 (July)** — Russian troops occupy the Danubian Principalities (Moldavia and Wallachia).
- **1853 (Oct)** — Ottoman Empire declares war on Russia.
- **1853 (Nov)** — Battle of Sinop: Russian fleet destroys an Ottoman squadron.
- **1854 (March)** — United Kingdom and France declare war on Russia.
- **1854 (Sept)** — Allied forces land in Crimea and win the Battle of the Alma.
- **1854 (Oct)** — Battle of Balaclava, including the Charge of the Light Brigade.
- **1854 (Nov)** — Battle of Inkerman; the Siege of Sevastopol begins in earnest.
- **1855 (March)** — Tsar Nicholas I dies; succeeded by Alexander II.
- **1855 (Sept)** — French capture the Malakoff redoubt; Sevastopol falls.
- **1856 (March)** — Treaty of Paris signed, ending the war.

## Body

### Causes and Background
The war's deep causes lay in the "Eastern Question"—the geopolitical problem posed by the steady decline of the Ottoman Empire. Russia sought to expand its influence southward and claimed the right to protect Orthodox Christians within Ottoman territories. This threatened the balance of power desired by Britain and France, who wanted to preserve the Ottoman Empire as a buffer. The immediate trigger was a dispute over the control of Christian holy places in Palestine, which escalated into a Russian demand for a protectorate over all Ottoman Orthodox subjects. After Ottoman rejection, Russia occupied the Danubian Principalities, leading to an Ottoman declaration of war. Britain and France joined after the Russian destruction of the Ottoman fleet at Sinop, fearing Russian dominance in the Black Sea.

### The War's Course
The main theater was the Crimean Peninsula, where allied forces landed in September 1854 to capture the Russian naval base at Sevastopol. After early victories at the Alma and Balaclava, the campaign devolved into a brutal 11-month siege of Sevastopol, marked by horrific conditions and massive casualties from disease and combat. The city fell in September 1855 after a French assault on the Malakoff redoubt. Fighting also occurred in the Caucasus, the Baltic Sea, the White Sea, and the Pacific. The Kingdom of Sardinia joined the allies in 1855. Diplomatically isolated and facing potential invasion, Russia sued for peace.

### Outcome and Consequences
The Treaty of Paris (1856) ended the war. It demilitarized the Black Sea, forbidding Russia and the Ottoman Empire from maintaining warships there. The Danubian Principalities gained greater autonomy, and the Ottoman Empire was admitted to the Concert of Europe. For Russia, the defeat was a profound humiliation that exposed its military, technological, and administrative backwardness. This crisis spurred the "Great Reforms" under Tsar Alexander II, including the emancipation of the serfs in 1861. The war also marked the first major conflict documented by war correspondents and photographers, and it led to reforms in military medicine, pioneered by figures like Florence Nightingale.

## Terms
- **Eastern Question:** The 19th-century geopolitical dilemma over how to handle the declining Ottoman Empire and the competing interests of European powers.
- **Concert of Europe:** The system of great-power diplomacy established after the Napoleonic Wars to maintain a balance of power and prevent continental war.
- **Danubian Principalities:** The Ottoman vassal states of Moldavia and Wallachia (later united as Romania), a key area of Russian-Ottoman conflict.
- **Siege of Sevastopol:** The prolonged (1854–55) allied siege of Russia's main Black Sea naval base, the war's central and most costly campaign.
- **Charge of the Light Brigade:** A disastrous British cavalry charge during the Battle of Balaclava, symbolizing the war's tactical mismanagement.
- **Treaty of Paris (1856):** The peace agreement that ended the war, notably demilitarizing the Black Sea.
- **Emancipation Reform of 1861:** The Russian decree that abolished serfdom, a major social reform catalyzed by the shock of defeat in the Crimean War.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians debate whether the war was an inevitable result of long-term geopolitical tensions or the product of "diplomatic drift" and miscalculation by leaders like Tsar Nicholas I and Napoleon III. Some argue Britain followed a calculated strategy to destroy the emerging Russian navy, while others emphasize the role of public opinion and press sensationalism in pushing governments toward conflict. The long-term impact is also debated: while the treaty punished Russia, some historians argue Austria lost the most by alienating Russia, which contributed to its later isolation and defeats.
