# Free will

## Overview

Free will is the capacity to choose between alternative courses of action, exercise control necessary for moral responsibility, or act as the ultimate source of one's actions. It is central to concepts of moral desert, praise, blame, and deliberation. The core philosophical problem is whether free will can coexist with determinism—the view that every event is necessitated by prior causes and natural laws. **Incompatibilists** argue free will and determinism are mutually exclusive; this camp includes **metaphysical libertarians** (free will exists, so determinism is false) and **hard determinists** (determinism is true, so free will is impossible). **Hard incompatibilists** argue free will is impossible regardless of whether determinism or indeterminism (randomness) is true. **Compatibilists** maintain free will is compatible with determinism, often redefining "free will" as the absence of external coercion, the ability to act on one's desires, or a psychological capacity for reasons-responsiveness. The debate spans metaphysics, theology, neuroscience, and psychology, with no consensus after millennia of discussion.

## Timeline

- **4th century BCE** — Aristotle and Stoics (e.g., Chrysippus) articulate early compatibilist ideas: freedom as absence of external hindrance.
- **3rd century CE** — Alexander of Aphrodisias identifies incompatibilist free will: control requires causal undetermination in decision-making.
- **4th century CE** — Christian philosophy introduces the term *liberum arbitrium* (free will), traditionally meaning lack of necessity in the will.
- **13th century** — Thomas Aquinas synthesizes Aristotelian teleology with theology: humans seek fixed ends but choose means; free will as "appetitive power."
- **1739** — David Hume analyzes causality as constant conjunction, weakening the notion of necessity; argues free will may be a "verbal" dispute.
- **1780s** — Immanuel Kant places moral decision-making outside empirical causality, in the noumenal realm.
- **1960s** — Carl Ginet formulates the **Consequence Argument**: if determinism is true, we have no control over the past or laws of nature, hence no control over present actions.
- **1965** — Kornhuber & Deecke discover the **readiness potential** (Bereitschaftspotential), a pre-movement neural buildup.
- **1980s** — Benjamin Libet experiments show unconscious brain activity (~550ms) precedes conscious intention to act (~200ms before movement), sparking debate on conscious veto.
- **2007** — Survey finds 79% of evolutionary biologists believe in free will.
- **2020** — Survey shows 59.2% of specialist philosophers accept compatibilism, 18.8% libertarianism, 11.2% no free will.

## Body

### The Core Dilemma: Determinism vs. Free Will
The conflict arises from two strong intuitions: humans experience robust agency and self-determination, yet the physical world appears governed by causal closure (no non-physical causes) and nomological determinism (future fixed by past + laws). If determinism is true, all choices are consequences of events before one's birth. This creates a moral dilemma: how can agents be responsible for actions caused entirely by the remote past? **Incompatibilists** insist free will requires being the *ultimate* cause (*causa sui*) of one's actions—an origination that determinism precludes. **Compatibilists** reject this "metaphysically free will" as incoherent or unnecessary, arguing freedom is a practical, non-metaphysical status (e.g., absence of coercion, alignment with desires).

### Incompatibilism: Hard Determinism, Libertarianism, and Hard Incompatibilism
**Hard determinists** (e.g., d'Holbach) accept determinism and deny free will. Determinism takes several forms: *causal* (nomological/scientific, per Laplace's Demon), *logical* (future propositions have present truth values), and *theological* (divine foreknowledge or decree). **Metaphysical libertarians** (e.g., Reid, van Inwagen, Kane) deny determinism, requiring indeterminism for free will. Sub-types include:
*   **Non-causal theories:** Actions are spontaneous, self-generating, not caused by prior mental states (risk: randomness, not control).
*   **Event-causal theories:** Physical indeterminism (e.g., quantum) provides "elbow room." Variants: *Deliberative indeterminism* (indeterminism only in option generation, selection deterministic), *Centred accounts* (probabilistic choice weighted by reasons), *Efforts of will theory* (Kane: indeterminism as resistance overcome by effort, securing "ultimate responsibility").
*   **Agent/substance-causal theories:** Substance dualism; agent (soul) intervenes in physical world undetermined by prior events (problem: interactionism, randomness of choice).

**Hard incompatibilists** (e.g., Derk Pereboom, Galen Strawson) argue free will is impossible *whether* determinism is true or false. Strawson's "Basic Argument": to be truly responsible, one must be responsible for one's mental constitution, requiring an infinite regress of self-creation (*causa sui*), which is impossible. Pereboom maps positions where free will is absent regardless of determinism's truth value.

### Compatibilism: Redefining Freedom
Compatibilists deny determinism threatens free will. **Classical compatibilists** (Hobbes, Hume, Voltaire) define free will as *freedom of action*: doing what one wills without physical impediment. "Could have done otherwise" means: *if* one had wanted to, one *could* have. **Modern compatibilists** distinguish *freedom of will* (psychological) from *freedom of action*.
*   **Hierarchical mesh (Frankfurt):** Free will requires effective *second-order desires* (desires about which first-order desires to act on). "Willing addicts" act freely; "unwilling addicts" have conflicting second-order desires but are overborne; "wanton addicts" lack second-order desires and thus personhood.
*   **Reasons-responsiveness (Fischer, Dennett):** Free will is the capacity to recognize and react to reasons. Dennett adds *unpredictability*: due to chaos and epistemic limits, the future is ill-defined for finite beings; free will is the ability to act contrary to expectations.
*   **Neocompatibilism (Costa):** Restrictions on free will occur at three levels: physical (body), psychological (decision), rational (formation of reasons/desires). Manipulation (e.g., Jim Jones cult) operates at the rational level via insufficiently conscious external reasons.
*   **Physicalism & Neuroscience:** Most compatibilists are physicalists. Cognitive naturalism studies mind as complex feedback systems (neural networks). Brain health, dopamine, disorders affect volition. The "will" may disconnect from freedom to act (addiction). Some models treat conscious will as the first-person perspective of neural correlates of choosing.

### Theological Determinism
**Strong theological determinism:** God decrees all events; incompatible with libertarian free will (hard theological determinism/fatalism). **Weak theological determinism:** God infallibly foreknows future actions. The classic argument: foreknowledge → fixed future → no alternate possibilities → no libertarian free will. **Compatibilist responses:** (1) Deny truth-value to future contingents (Aristotle, open theism); (2) Assert God's knowledge is non-temporal/atemporal (Boethius, Aquinas, C.S. Lewis); (3) Deny the Principle of Alternate Possibilities (Frankfurt cases: an agent can be responsible even if they couldn't have done otherwise, provided the counterfactual intervener didn't actually act).

### Eastern Perspectives
**Buddhism:** Rejects both absolute free will and hard determinism. Teaches *pratītyasamutpāda* (dependent origination/conditioned genesis): every volition is conditioned by ignorance and prior causes. Karma is cause-effect of moral actions *in this life*, less deterministic than Hindu karma. Denies a substantial *agent* (anatta); "free action, retribution, but no agent passing between moments." Freedom is compatible with conditionality; denying freedom undermines moral effort.
**Hinduism:** Six orthodox schools differ. *Samkhya*: matter unfolds deterministically; freedom (*kaivalya*) is realizing separateness of self (purusha) from matter (prakriti). *Yoga*: Only Ishvara (God) is truly free. *Nyaya/Vaisheshika*: Strong determinism implied. *Vedanta* (Vivekananda): Will is bound by cause-effect, but "something behind the will is free"; freedom requires transcending the universe. Madhvacharya: Souls have no free will; Vishnu determines all actions.
**Islam:** Central issue is reconciling human freedom with God's *jabr* (commanding power). *Ash'arism* (dominant Sunni): "Acquisition" (*kasb*)/dual-agency compatibilism—God creates the act, human "acquires" it; both divine power and human will asserted. *Mu'tazila* (rationalists): Humans create their own actions via free will (brain/nervous system) to preserve divine justice (*'Adl*). Shia theology often leans toward stronger predestination. Free will is basis for accountability on Judgment Day.

### Scientific Challenges
**Quantum Physics:** Indeterministic interpretations (e.g., Copenhagen) introduce fundamental randomness. However, randomness ≠ control. If actions stem from quantum randomness, mental processes lack influence. Relevance to macroscopic brain function is contested (decoherence, thermal noise). Bohr argued no connection between quantum indeterminism and freedom of will.
**Genetics:** "Nature vs. nurture" debate. Behavioral genetics suggests high heritability for many traits. Pinker argues explanation ≠ exculpation; responsibility requires only responsiveness to praise/blame, not uncaused behavior. Environmental determinism threatens free will as much as genetic.
**Neuroscience (Libet & Beyond):** Libet (1980s): Readiness potential (RP) begins ~550ms before movement; conscious intention (W-time) ~200ms before. Suggests brain "decides" unconsciously before conscious will. **Debates:** (1) RP may reflect non-specific preparation, not specific decision (Schurger et al.). (2) "Conscious veto" (free won't) may exist in the ~150ms window before movement (Libet). (3) Veto itself may be unconscious. (4) Free will is a longer-term, higher-level phenomenon, not captured in 150–350ms motor tasks. (5) Pathologies illuminate agency: Tourette's (semi-voluntary tics, suppressible but explosive release), Alien Hand Syndrome (limb moves without will; isolated primary motor cortex activation vs. normal premotor→motor sequence; often corpus callosum damage), Schizophrenia (delusion of control; hypothesized comparator mechanism failure for proprioception).
**Experimental Psychology (Wegner, Pronin):** Wegner: Conscious will is an *inference* (illusion) based on priority (thought before action), consistency (thought matches action), and exclusivity (no other apparent cause). Experiments manipulate these cues to create false feelings of authorship or non-authorship. Pronin: *Introspection illusion*—people trust their own introspections, attributing more free will to self than others. Belief in free will correlates with cultural universals, personality (extroverts dissociate determinism from responsibility), and situational factors (emotional crimes elicit incompatibilist intuitions).

### Belief in Free Will: Demographics and Effects
*   **Philosophers (2020):** 59.2% compatibilism, 18.8% libertarianism, 11.2% no free will.
*   **Evolutionary Biologists (2007):** 79% believe in free will.
*   **General Public:** Tend toward "naive compatibilism"; believe acts are freer when opposing forces, planning, or random (though true randomness is hard to produce).
*   **Effects of Disbelief:** Early studies (Baumeister, Vohs) linked induced disbelief to cheating, aggression, reduced helpfulness, impaired counterfactual thinking. **Recent large-scale replications** often find *no effect* on moral behavior. Critique: "Disproving free will" may induce *fatalism* (helplessness), not determinism + compatibilism. Fatalism, not determinism, may drive negative outcomes. Belief in free will predicts better job performance (Stillman).

## Terms

- ****Compatibilism**** — The view that free will is logically compatible with determinism; free will is typically defined as absence of coercion, alignment with desires, or reasons-responsiveness.
- ****Incompatibilism**** — The view that free will and determinism are logically mutually exclusive.
- ****Libertarianism (Metaphysical)**** — Incompatibilist view that determinism is false and free will exists, requiring indeterminism (often agent-causal or event-causal).
- ****Hard Determinism**** — Incompatibilist view that determinism is true, therefore free will does not exist.
- ****Hard Incompatibilism**** — View that free will is impossible whether determinism is true or false (e.g., Pereboom, Strawson).
- ****Determinism (Nomological/Causal)**** — The thesis that the past and laws of nature necessitate exactly one future (Laplace's Demon).
- ****Principle of Alternate Possibilities (PAP)**** — "A person is morally responsible only if they could have done otherwise." Target of Frankfurt-style counterexamples.
- ****Readiness Potential (Bereitschaftspotential)**** — A gradual buildup of neural electrical activity preceding voluntary movement, discovered 1965; central to Libet's timing experiments.
- ****Causa Sui**** — "Cause of itself"; the traditional requirement for ultimate responsibility (being the uncaused cause of one's actions).
- ****Dependent Origination (Pratītyasamutpāda)**** — Buddhist doctrine: all phenomena arise conditioned by prior causes; no independent agent, but volition and moral responsibility operate within conditionality.
- ****Acquisition (Kasb)**** — Ash'arite Islamic compatibilism: God creates the act, the human "acquires" it, making both divine power and human will effective.
- ****Frankfurt Cases**** — Thought experiments where an agent acts on their own desires but a counterfactual intervener would have forced that action if they hadn't; used to argue PAP is false.
- ****Epiphenomenalism**** — View that consciousness is a causally inert byproduct of physical processes (like a steam whistle on a locomotive).

## Debates and Open Questions

1.  **Consequence Argument Validity:** Does the inability to change the past/laws entail inability to do otherwise? Compatibilists (Lewis) argue "could have done otherwise" is conditional on different past circumstances; incompatibilists (van Inwagen) insist this concedes "incredible abilities" (changing past/laws).
2.  **Sourcehood vs. Alternate Possibilities:** Is free will fundamentally about *origination* (being the ultimate source) or *alternative possibilities* (could have done otherwise)? Frankfurt cases target the latter; sourcehood theorists (Pereboom, Kane) argue the former is essential and threatened by determinism *and* indeterminism.
3.  **Quantum Indeterminism & Agency:** Does quantum randomness provide the "elbow room" for libertarian free will, or merely inject noise? Can mental states causally influence quantum probabilities in the brain (Eccles, Kane)? Or is the brain too warm/wet for quantum coherence (Tegmark)?
4.  **Libet's Legacy:** Does the RP represent a *decision* or non-specific *preparation*? Is the ~150ms window sufficient for a "conscious veto" (free won't), or is the veto itself neurally predetermined? Do motor-timing experiments generalize to deliberative, long-term decisions?
5.  **Neuroscience of Agency:** How do pathologies (Tourette's, Alien Hand, Schizophrenia) map onto the architecture of volition? Is the sense of agency a post-hoc inference (Wegner) or a functional component of control?
6.  **Fatalism vs. Determinism in Psychology:** Do experiments "disproving free will" actually induce fatalism (inevitability + helplessness) rather than deterministic compatibilism? If so, negative behavioral effects may not generalize to informed determinism.
7.  **Theological Foreknowledge:** Can God's atemporal knowledge (Boethius/Aquinas) preserve libertarian free will without making God's knowledge dependent on future events? Does Molinism (middle knowledge of counterfactuals of freedom) resolve the tension?
8.  **Definition of "Free Will":** Is the debate merely verbal (Hume, pseudo-problem view)? Do compatibilists and incompatibilists talk past each other using different definitions, or is there a substantive metaphysical disagreement about the *nature* of control required for moral responsibility?
