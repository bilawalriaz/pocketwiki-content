# History of South Africa

## Overview
South Africa's history is a complex narrative of indigenous settlement, colonial conquest, racial segregation, and a hard-won transition to democracy. It matters as a case study of how mineral wealth, imperial competition, and ideological systems like apartheid can shape a nation's trajectory, and how a society can negotiate a path from oppression to a constitutional democracy, albeit one still grappling with profound social and economic challenges.

## Timeline
- **c. 125,000 years ago** — Modern human settlement occurs in the Middle Stone Age.
- **c. 4th century AD** — Bantu-speaking people begin migrating into southern Africa.
- **1488** — Portuguese explorer Bartolomeu Dias rounds the Cape of Good Hope.
- **1652** — The Dutch East India Company establishes a settlement at the Cape under Jan van Riebeeck.
- **1830s–1840s** — The Great Trek sees Boer (Afrikaner) pioneers migrate inland, establishing republics.
- **1867–1886** — Discovery of diamonds (1867) and gold (1886) triggers economic transformation and conflict.
- **1899–1902** — The Second Anglo-Boer War ends with British victory and Boer republic annexation.
- **1910** — The Union of South Africa is created as a self-governing dominion of the British Empire.
- **1948** — The National Party introduces the formal system of apartheid.
- **1960** — The Sharpeville massacre leads to international condemnation and armed resistance.
- **1990** — President F. W. de Klerk unbans liberation parties and releases Nelson Mandela.
- **1994** — First democratic elections are held; Nelson Mandela becomes president.

## Body

### Early History and Colonization
The territory of modern South Africa was a major centre of human evolution, inhabited by Australopithecines since at least 2.5 million years ago. Modern human settlement is associated with the Khoisan (Khoi and San) people. From the 4th century AD, Bantu-speaking groups migrated south, establishing kingdoms like Mapungubwe (c. AD 900–1300). European contact began with Portuguese explorers in the late 15th century. In 1652, the Dutch East India Company (VOC) established a refreshment station at the Cape, which evolved into a settler colony. The VOC imported slaves from the East, and the descendants of unions between Dutch settlers, Khoisan, and slaves formed the basis of the Cape Coloured and Cape Malay communities.

### British Rule, Boer Republics, and Mineral Revolution
Britain seized the Cape Colony in 1795 and again in 1806. British policies, including the abolition of slavery and the imposition of English, prompted many Dutch-speaking Boers to undertake the Great Trek (1830s–1840s) inland, where they clashed with indigenous groups like the Zulu and established independent republics (e.g., the South African Republic and Orange Free State). The discovery of diamonds (1867) and gold (1886) transformed the region's economy, attracting foreign capital and labour, but also intensifying imperial rivalry. This culminated in the Second Anglo-Boer War (1899–1902), a brutal conflict that ended with British victory and the annexation of the Boer republics.

### Union, Segregation, and Apartheid
In 1910, Britain amalgamated its colonies and the former Boer republics into the Union of South Africa, a self-governing dominion. The new state quickly enacted segregationist laws like the 1913 Natives' Land Act. In 1948, the National Party formalized racial segregation into the system of apartheid, which denied political and economic rights to the non-white majority. Apartheid was enforced through legislation like the Homeland Citizens Act (1970), which created "Bantustans," and was maintained by state violence and international isolation. Resistance grew from passive protest to armed struggle, led by groups like the African National Congress (ANC).

### Transition and Democratic Era
Mounting internal resistance, international sanctions, and the end of the Cold War pressured the apartheid government to negotiate. In 1990, President F. W. de Klerk unbanned liberation parties and released Nelson Mandela. After negotiations, the first multiracial democratic elections were held in 1994, won by the ANC. The new government faced immense challenges: repaying apartheid-era debt, combating the HIV/AIDS epidemic, and addressing deep-seated poverty and inequality. Despite political gains, the post-apartheid era has been marked by ongoing struggles with corruption, crime, an energy crisis, and social unrest, though the democratic order has remained intact.

## Terms
*   **Khoisan:** Collective term for the indigenous San (hunter-gatherers) and Khoikhoi (pastoralists) peoples of southern Africa.
*   **Bantu Expansion:** The major prehistoric migration of Bantu-speaking peoples from West-Central Africa across much of the continent, reaching southern Africa by the 4th century AD.
*   **Voortrekkers:** Dutch-speaking Boer pioneers who migrated inland from the Cape Colony during the 1830s and 1840s.
*   **Apartheid:** The system of institutionalized racial segregation and white minority rule enforced in South Africa from 1948 to the early 1990s.
*   **Umkhonto weSizwe (MK):** The armed wing of the African National Congress, formed in 1961 to carry out sabotage against apartheid targets.
*   **Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC):** A post-apartheid restorative justice body established in 1996 to investigate human rights violations committed during the apartheid era.
*   **State Capture:** A form of systemic political corruption in which private interests significantly influence a state's decision-making processes, a major scandal during the Zuma presidency.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The causes and scale of the **Mfecane** (the period of widespread chaos and warfare in the early 19th century) are debated, with theories ranging from Zulu militarism to ecological factors and the slave trade.
*   The **economic legacy of apartheid** remains contested, particularly regarding the extent to which post-1994 neoliberal policies have addressed or exacerbated historical inequalities.
*   The **effectiveness of the TRC** is debated, with criticism that it prioritized reconciliation over justice and failed to secure meaningful reparations for victims.
*   The **causes of contemporary challenges** like poverty, corruption, and service delivery failures are attributed variously to the enduring structural legacies of apartheid, post-1994 policy choices, or governance failures.
