# Andes

## Overview

The Andes are the longest continental mountain range in the world, forming a continuous highland along the western edge of South America. Stretching roughly 8,900 km (5,500 mi) and up to 700 km (430 mi) wide, they extend through seven countries—Argentina, Chile, Bolivia, Peru, Ecuador, Colombia, and Venezuela—and host the highest peaks outside Asia, including Aconcagua at about 6,961 m (22,838 ft). The range is a product of ongoing tectonic subduction, shapes regional climate through rain shadows and aridity, and has been the cradle of major civilizations from the Inca Empire to modern highland cities.

## Timeline

- **Late Proterozoic–early Paleozoic** — Pre-Andean orogenies occur as terranes collide with eastern South America's cratons
- **Triassic–Jurassic** — Pangaea breaks up, developing rifts that set the stage for the Andes
- **Cretaceous** — Andes begin taking present form via uplifting, faulting, and folding of ancient rocks
- **c. 15th century** — Inca Empire expands in the central Andes
- **1532** — Incas defeated by Spanish-led alliance; Spanish colonization and mining begin
- **1826–1827** — Woodbine Parish and Joseph Barclay Pentland survey the Bolivian Andes
- **19th century** — Independence wars overthrow Spanish rule; five independent Andean states emerge
- **1960** — Valdivia earthquake (M9.5), strongest ever recorded on seismometers (as of 2024)
- **2010** — Maule earthquake (M8.8) strikes the region
- **2015** — Illapel earthquake (M8.2) occurs

## Body

### Geography and divisions

The Andes are split into several parallel ranges (cordilleras, from the Spanish *cordel* "rope") separated by intermediate depressions, and include high plateaus such as the Altiplano—the world's second-highest plateau after Tibet. The range is grouped into three major divisions: the Southern Andes (Argentina and Chile, south of Llullaillaco), the Central Andes (Bolivia and Peru), and the Northern Andes (Colombia, Ecuador, Venezuela), the latter split into three branches. The Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta is often, but not always, treated as part of the Northern Andes. The Leeward Antilles (Aruba, Bonaire, Curaçao) were formerly thought to be submerged Andean peaks, but geological studies show the tectonic boundary is more complex.

### Geology and orogeny

The Andes are an orogenic belt along the Pacific Ring of Fire, formed by subduction of oceanic crust (Nazca and Antarctic Plates) beneath the South American Plate, a process active since the Mesozoic and Tertiary. Climate accelerated uplift: as the range rose, a rain shadow dried Chile's coast, starving the subduction zone of sediment and reducing lubrication, which increased contractional coastal uplift. The range is bounded east by sedimentary basins (Orinoco, Amazon, Madre de Dios, Gran Chaco) and contains bends called oroclines—notably the Bolivian Orocline at about 18° S, where the Andes turn from northwest to south, overlapping the Altiplano's maximum width. The Arica Elbow marks the coastline bend; the Maipo and Patagonian Oroclines lie further south.

### Seismic activity and volcanism

Subduction along the entire west coast continues to produce earthquakes and eruptions. Major events include the 1960 Valdivia earthquake (M9.5), the 2010 Maule (M8.8), and the 2015 Illapel (M8.2). Seismic activity varies with plate friction, subduction angle, buoyancy, rate, and mantle hydration; the highest activity is between 33°S and 35°S, where subduction is nearly horizontal. Hydrated zones correlate with frequent low-magnitude quakes, while dehydrated zones may produce larger future events. The largest shallow intraplate earthquake struck Peru in 1947 (Ms 7.5). Volcanoes are distributed in four zones separated by gaps, producing diverse activity styles and products. The Andes also host major ore deposits: porphyry copper in Chile and Peru (making them the first- and second-largest copper exporters), saltpeter deposits, lithium-rich salars at Atacama and Uyuni, and Bolivia's tin belt and silver deposits at Cerro Rico de Potosí.

### Climate and hydrology

Climate varies with latitude, altitude, and proximity to the sea. The southern section is rainy and cool, the central section dry, and the northern Andes rainy and warm (average 18 °C in Colombia). The snow line rises from 4,500–4,800 m in the tropics to 4,800–5,200 m in drier southern Peru and northern Chile, then descends to 2,000 m at 40°S, 500 m at 50°S, and 300 m in Tierra del Fuego. The Dry Andes (Atacama to Maule River) have sporadic precipitation and strong temperature oscillations; rock glaciers are larger and more common than glaciers in Central Chile and Mendoza due to high solar radiation. At glacial times (~20,000 years ago), glaciers were over ten times longer, with ice streams up to 112.5 km long and over 1,250 m thick.

### Flora and fauna

The Andes host about 30,000 species of vascular plants, roughly half endemic. Notable plants include *Cinchona pubescens* (source of quinine), tobacco, potatoes, and high-altitude Polylepis forests (found up to 4,500 m), which are now highly endangered—possibly as little as 10% of original woodland remains. The region is the world's most important for amphibians (almost 1,000 species, ~2/3 endemic) and rich in mammals (~600 species), birds (>1,700), reptiles (>600), and fish (~400). Iconic fauna include the vicuña, guanaco, domesticated llama and alpaca, chinchillas, Andean condor, huemul, cougar, flamingos, and the Titicaca flightless grebe and Titicaca water frog. Hummingbirds, including hillstars, occur above 4,000 m, but diversity peaks in humid cloud forests, home to the mountain tapir, spectacled bear, and yellow-tailed woolly monkey.

### Human activity

The Andes form a north–south axis of cultural influence, culminating in the Inca Empire in the 15th century, built through militarism and meticulous governance, including aqueducts and roads. The Incas were defeated in 1532 by a small Spanish army (180 men) allied with tens of thousands of subjugated peoples; Machu Picchu remained hidden from the Spanish. Quechua and Aymara are the main surviving languages. Major modern cities include Bogotá (over ten million metropolitan population), Santiago, Medellín, Cali, and Quito; La Paz is the world's highest capital at ~3,650 m, with parts of its conurbation up to 4,200 m. Transportation is challenging: highways and railroads are costly to build, many passengers travel by air, and llamas and alpacas still serve as pack animals. Agriculture relies on irrigation (practiced for over 6,000 years) and terracing, with potato, maize, tobacco, cotton, quinoa, and coffee as key crops. Mining has been central since the Spanish conquest—Potosí and Cerro de Pasco were principal Spanish mines—and today Chile and Peru lead in copper, while Argentina, Bolivia, and Chile hold the three largest lithium reserves.

## Terms

- **Cordillera**: A chain of mountain ranges; from Spanish *cordel* "rope."
- **Subduction**: The process where one tectonic plate slides beneath another, driving Andean uplift and volcanism.
- **Orogeny**: Mountain-building events; the Andean orogeny is ongoing.
- **Orocline**: A bend in a mountain chain, such as the Bolivian Orocline at ~18° S.
- **Altiplano**: The world's second-highest plateau, located in the central Andes.
- **Porphyry copper**: Copper mineralization from hydrothermal fluids cooling in plutons or volcanic systems.
- **Salar**: A salt flat; Atacama and Uyuni are major lithium sources.
- **Snow line**: The elevation above which snow persists year-round; varies with latitude and aridity.
- **Polylepis**: High-altitude Andean trees, now endangered, found up to 4,500 m.
- **Rain shadow**: A dry area on the leeward side of mountains, created by the Andes and linked to the Atacama Desert.

## Debates and open questions

- The etymology of "Andes" is debated: most agree it derives from Quechua *anti* ("east," as in Antisuyu), but some suggest Aymara *anta* ("copper").
- Whether the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta is part of the Northern Andes is debated.
- Whether the Leeward Antilles represent submerged Andean peaks is now questioned by geological studies.
- The patchy distribution of Polylepis forests is unclear—natural or the result of clearing beginning in the Incan period.
- The Venezuelan Coastal Range's status as an Andean extension is debatable.
