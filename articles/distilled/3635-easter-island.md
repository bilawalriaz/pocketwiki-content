# Easter Island

## Overview
Easter Island, or Rapa Nui, is a remote Chilean territory in the southeastern Pacific Ocean, renowned for its nearly 1,000 monumental stone statues (moai) created by the early Rapa Nui people. It is one of the world's most isolated inhabited islands and a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The island's history is a case study in Polynesian navigation, cultural achievement, and societal transformation, with ongoing scholarly debate about the causes of its pre-European ecological and social changes.

## Timeline
- **c. 1200 CE** — Current best estimate for initial Polynesian settlement.
- **1722** — First recorded European contact by Dutch explorer Jacob Roggeveen on Easter Sunday.
- **1770** — Spanish expedition claims the island for the Spanish Crown.
- **1862-63** — Peruvian slave raids abduct about 1,500 Rapa Nui, introducing smallpox.
- **1864** — First Christian missionary, Eugène Eyraud, arrives; reports existence of rongorongo script.
- **1877** — Population reaches an all-time low of 111 native inhabitants.
- **1888** — Chile annexes Easter Island via the Agreement of Wills.
- **1966** — Rapa Nui people are granted Chilean citizenship.
- **1995** — UNESCO designates Easter Island a World Heritage Site.
- **2007** — Island gains constitutional status of "special territory" of Chile.

## Body

### Settlement and Early Culture
Oral tradition states the island was settled by a two-canoe expedition led by the chief Hotu Matu'a from a homeland in eastern Polynesia. Radiocarbon dating places the colonization around 1200 CE. The settlers, who most likely came from the Gambier or Marquesas Islands, developed a thriving culture centered on ancestor worship. The most visible element was the production of massive moai statues, which were carved from tuff at the Rano Raraku quarry and transported to stone platforms (ahu) along the coast. These statues likely represented deified ancestors, embodying the concept of mana (spiritual power). By the 15th century, society was organized into two confederations, the Tu'u and 'Otu 'Itu.

### Ecocide Hypothesis and Its Critique
A major scholarly debate concerns the island's pre-European environmental and social collapse. The "ecocide hypothesis," popularized by Jared Diamond, argues that the Rapa Nui people caused their own downfall through extreme deforestation (ecocide) to move statues and clear land, leading to resource depletion, warfare, and cannibalism. This version suggests a high population (perhaps 15,000) that crashed before European arrival.

This hypothesis is highly contested. Critics, including archaeologists Robert DiNapoli and Carl Lipo, argue that the island never had a large population and experienced steady growth until European contact. They note a lack of archaeological evidence for widespread pre-contact warfare, fortifications, or cannibalism. Studies suggest deforestation began immediately but was exacerbated by the introduced Polynesian rat and a drier climate. The primary cause of the population collapse is now attributed to post-contact events: European diseases, slave raids, and exploitation.

### European Contact and Population Collapse
European contact began in 1722. The most devastating period was the 1860s, when Peruvian slave raids abducted about half the population. Survivors returned with smallpox, which decimated the remaining inhabitants. By 1877, only 111 Rapa Nui remained. The island was annexed by Chile in 1888. For much of the 20th century, the Rapa Nui were confined to the village of Hanga Roa while the rest of the island was leased as a sheep ranch. They gained Chilean citizenship in 1966, and the island's administration was reopened.

### Modern Status and Culture
Today, Easter Island is a special territory of Chile with a population of several thousand, of which about 45% identify as Rapa Nui. The economy relies on cultural tourism. The Rapa Nui language is threatened by Spanish, but cultural revitalization efforts are ongoing, highlighted by the annual Tapati festival. The island faces modern challenges, including illegal fishing, environmental pressures from tourism, and ongoing indigenous rights movements concerning land ownership.

## Terms
- **Moai**: Monolithic stone statues carved by the Rapa Nui people, most representing deified ancestors.
- **Ahu**: Stone platforms upon which moai were erected, serving as ceremonial and burial sites.
- **Mana**: A Polynesian concept of spiritual power or authority, believed to be embodied in chiefs and ancestors.
- **Rongorongo**: An undeciphered system of glyphs incised on wooden tablets, the only Polynesian script known to have existed.
- **Tangata manu (Birdman cult)**: A religious competition that replaced the ancestor cult, where a champion was chosen through a dangerous annual race.
- **Huri mo'ai**: The period of statue toppling, which began after European contact and was likely an act of socio-political conflict.
- **Hanau**: Confederations or social groupings based on lineage that existed on the island by the 15th century.
- **Tumu ivi 'atua**: Bearers of the island's culture, history, and genealogy, whose knowledge was lost during the 19th-century population collapse.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause of Pre-European Collapse**: The central debate is whether the Rapa Nui society collapsed due to self-inflicted ecological disaster (ecocide) before European contact, or if the population decline was primarily caused by post-contact diseases, slavery, and exploitation.
- **Population Size**: Estimates of the pre-European population vary widely, from a few thousand to as high as 15,000, with recent research favoring lower numbers.
- **Statue Transportation**: The exact methods used to transport the massive moai from the quarry to the ahu are still debated, with theories involving wooden sledges, log rollers, or "walking" the statues with ropes.
- **Decipherment of Rongorongo**: The meaning and nature of the rongorongo script remain unknown, as it has not been deciphered and few texts survive.
