# Planet

## Overview
A planet is a large, rounded astronomical body orbiting a star, stellar remnant, or brown dwarf. The Solar System has eight planets—four terrestrial (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) and four giant (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune)—with dwarf planets like Pluto and Ceres classified separately since 2006. Over 6,400 exoplanets have been confirmed beyond the Solar System, revealing diverse worlds including hot Jupiters and super-Earths.

- **2005** — Eris discovered, 27% more massive than Pluto
- **2006** — IAU defines planet; Pluto reclassified as dwarf planet

## Body

### Formation
Planets form through accretion within protoplanetary disks surrounding young stars. Dust particles collide and stick together, forming planetesimals that grow via gravity into protoplanets. Once massive enough, bodies achieve hydrostatic equilibrium (round shape). Giant planets form beyond the frost line where ices and gases accumulate; terrestrial planets form closer to stars from rocky material. Photoevaporation and solar wind clear remaining disk material. Internal heating from impacts and radioactive decay causes differentiation into cores, mantles, and crusts.

### Solar System Planets
The eight planets are divided into terrestrials (rocky: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) and giants (Jupiter, Saturn as gas giants; Uranus, Neptune as ice giants). Jupiter is the most massive at 318 Earth masses; Mercury the smallest at 0.055 Earth masses. Dwarf planets like Ceres (asteroid belt) and Pluto, Eris (Kuiper belt) are rounded but haven't cleared their orbits. At least 19 planetary-mass moons exist, including Ganymede (largest moon) and Titan (only moon with thick atmosphere).

### Exoplanets
Over 6,400 exoplanets confirmed as of 2026, ranging from gas giants to Moon-sized bodies. Notable discoveries include hot Jupiters (close-orbiting giants like 51 Pegasi b), super-Earths (1–10 Earth masses), and mini-Neptunes. About one in five Sun-like stars hosts an Earth-sized planet in its habitable zone. Free-floating rogue planets and planets orbiting brown dwarfs challenge traditional definitions.

### Attributes
Planetary orbits are elliptical, described by eccentricity, semi-major axis, and inclination. Axial tilt creates seasons; rotation periods vary widely (Venus: 243 days; Jupiter: 10 hours). Most hot Jupiters are tidally locked. Planets achieve hydrostatic equilibrium above certain masses. Internal differentiation produces cores and mantles; magnetic fields arise from fluid motion in cores. Atmospheres depend on gravity and temperature—Titan has Earth-like pressure, while Mars lost most of its atmosphere.

### History and Definitions
The term "planet" derives from Greek *planētai* ("wanderers"). Ancient civilizations identified seven classical planets. The 2006 IAU definition requires: (1) orbit a star, (2) be rounded by gravity, (3) clear orbital neighborhood. Dwarf planets meet first two but not third. Critics propose geophysical definitions based solely on roundness. Alternative criteria like Jean-Luc Margot's π parameter quantify orbital clearing ability.

### Naming and Symbols
Planetary names derive from Babylonian, Greek, and Roman mythology. Earth's name comes from Old English *erda* ("ground"). Modern systems use mythological figures for trans-Neptunian objects (underworld/creation myths). Planetary symbols originated as Greek monograms; crosses were later added for Christian influence.

## Terms
- **Accretion**: Gradual accumulation of material by gravity during planet formation
- **Hydrostatic equilibrium**: State where gravity pulls an object into a spherical shape
- **Protoplanetary disk**: Rotating disk of gas and dust surrounding a young star
- **Planetesimal**: Kilometer-sized body formed during early accretion stages
- **Dwarf planet**: Rounded body that hasn't cleared its orbital neighborhood
- **Hot Jupiter**: Gas giant orbiting very close to its parent star
- **Habitable zone**: Region around a star where liquid water can exist on a planet's surface
- **Tidally locked**: Body with rotation period equal to orbital period (one face always visible)
- **Metallicity**: Abundance of elements heavier than helium in astronomical objects
- **Orbital clearing**: Process by which a planet accumulates or ejects nearby objects

## Debates and Open Questions
The IAU's 2006 planet definition remains controversial. Planetary geologists favor geophysical definitions based on roundness rather than orbital dynamics, arguing location shouldn't determine planethood. The boundary between planets and brown dwarfs (13 Jupiter masses for deuterium fusion) is disputed, with some catalogs including objects up to 60 Jupiter masses. The minimum size for dwarf planet classification varies—Grundy et al. (2019) suggest 900–1000 km diameter for trans-Neptunian objects, larger than the ~400 km threshold for icy moons. Free-floating planetary-mass objects' classification remains unresolved. The IAU's 2018 exoplanet working definition uses 13 Jupiter masses as the deuterium-burning limit, but many catalogs exceed this threshold.
