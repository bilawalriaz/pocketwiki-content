# Natural selection

## Overview

Natural selection is the differential survival and reproduction of individuals due to differences in heritable phenotypic traits, serving as the primary mechanism of adaptive evolution. First formalized by Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace in 1858, it explains how populations become adapted to their environments without intentional design, contrasting with artificial selection by human breeders. The theory unified biology by providing a naturalistic explanation for the diversity of life, speciation, and the appearance of purpose in nature, later integrating with genetics in the "modern synthesis" (1930s–1940s) and expanding in the 21st century through extended evolutionary syntheses that restore organismal agency and developmental biology (evo-devo) to the core of evolutionary theory.

## Body

### Historical Development

**Pre-Darwinian Theories**  
Ancient philosophers like Empedocles and Lucretius suggested nature randomly produces varied forms, with only self-sustaining ones persisting. Aristotle rejected this chance-based view in *Physics*, arguing for teleology (purpose-driven form), though he acknowledged rare "monstrosities" in *Generation of Animals*. In the 9th century, Al-Jahiz described a struggle for existence regarding population regulation, but not individual variation. Leonardo da Vinci (c. 1500) argued animal shapes are not fixed by a higher power but generated in diverse forms and selected by environmental compatibility. In the 18th century, Pierre Louis Maupertuis and Erasmus Darwin revived such ideas. The prevailing Western view held that individual differences were mere deviations from Platonic "created kinds" until geology's uniformitarianism revealed vast time scales, making gradual transmutation plausible. Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (early 19th century) proposed inheritance of acquired characteristics (Lamarckism), influencing later Soviet biology under Trofim Lysenko. Edward Blyth (1835–1837) worked on variation and artificial selection in nature, acknowledged by Darwin.

**Darwin's Theory**  
Inspired by Beagle voyage observations and Malthus's principle of population (exponential growth vs. arithmetic resources), Darwin conceived natural selection in 1838: favourable variations are preserved, unfavourable ones destroyed, leading to new species. He defined it as the "principle by which each slight variation, if useful, is preserved." For Darwin, natural selection resulted from three processes: inheritance (transmission and ontogeny), variation (partly from organismal agency), and the struggle for existence (competition and mutual aid). He delayed publication to gather consilience; in 1858, Alfred Russel Wallace independently sent him the same principle. Charles Lyell and Joseph Dalton Hooker arranged a joint Linnean Society reading. *On the Origin of Species* (1859) provided exhaustive evidence. Darwin acknowledged earlier anticipations by Wells and Matthew but noted they lacked development and evidence. He viewed selection as the "main but not exclusive means of modification," also citing sexual selection, use/disuse (Baldwin effect), direct action of external conditions, and spontaneous variation (mutation). He later regretted the term "Natural Selection," preferring "Natural Preservation." While evolution gained acceptance, natural selection remained controversial for perceived weakness and its "unguided," non-progressive nature. Herbert Spencer coined "survival of the fittest" (1864), adopted by Darwin in the 5th edition (1869), though modern biologists avoid it as tautological.

**The Modern Synthesis and After**  
Darwin lacked a theory of heredity; Gregor Mendel's work (contemporary but obscure) was rediscovered in 1900. The modern synthesis (1930s–1940s) integrated Mendelian genetics with selection: Fisher provided mathematics (*The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection*, 1930), Haldane the "cost" of selection, Wright the nature of adaptation, and Dobzhansky showed mutation supplies raw material for selection (*Genetics and the Origin of Species*, 1937). Ernst Mayr (1942) emphasized reproductive isolation for speciation. W.D. Hamilton (1964) introduced kin selection. A "second synthesis" (late 20th century) arose from molecular genetics, creating evo-devo, which explains form evolution via genetic regulatory programs controlling embryonic development. In the 21st century, evidence has prompted extended evolutionary syntheses challenging the gene-centred view, returning organismal agency—inheritance, phenotypic variation, and struggle for existence—to the heart of natural selection theory.

### Terminology and Mechanism

**Definitions**  
Natural selection is most often defined as the differential survival and reproduction of phenotypic variants supported by heritable traits. Traits increasing reproductive success are "selected for"; those decreasing it are "selected against." It is crucial to distinguish the mechanisms causing selection from its effects.

**Heritable Variation and Differential Reproduction**  
Phenotypic variation exists in all populations. Variants improving survival and reproduction leave more offspring. If these advantages are heritable, the trait frequency increases over generations. The environment "selects for" advantageous traits without intentional choice (unlike artificial selection). Classic examples include the peppered moth (*Biston betularia*) in Industrial Revolution Britain: soot-blackened trees favored dark morphs via camouflage; the Clean Air Act 1956 reversed the trend. Modern genetics identifies a transposable element insertion in the *cortex* gene as the cause. A polygenic example is ash trees (*Fraxinus excelsior*) in Britain evolving resistance to the invasive ash dieback fungus (*Hymenoscyphus fraxineus*); genomic prediction shows the new generation is genetically more resistant due to selection on standing variation.

**Fitness**  
Fitness is central but subtle: defined not by longevity but by reproductive success (genes contributed to the next generation). An organism living half as long but producing twice as many surviving offspring has higher fitness. Fitness is an average over a population due to chance effects. "Survival of the fittest" (fixation/substitution) removes less fit variants but does not inherently improve fitness. "Improvement in fitness" depends on absolute survival and replication of a variant for beneficial mutations to arise (e.g., Kishony Mega-plate experiment). Richard Lenski's *E. coli* long-term experiment demonstrates both processes simultaneously: adaptation in a competitive environment where competition slows beneficial mutations by limiting replications.

**Competition**  
Competition lowers the fitness of one organism due to another's presence, typically over limited resources (food, water, territory). It can be intra- or interspecific, direct or indirect. r/K selection theory (MacArthur & Wilson) models this: r-selected species exploit empty niches with many low-survival offspring; K-selected species compete in crowded niches with few high-investment offspring. The "room to roam" theory suggests expansion may outweigh competition in larger clades.

**Social Species**  
Darwin argued natural selection operates differently in social species. Social plants (grasses, thistles) aid each other passively via cross-fertilization and reduced predation. Social animals (e.g., wolves, pelicans, some birds/mammals) gain active benefits: division of labor (insects), sentinel warnings, mutual grooming, cooperative hunting, and group defense. Darwin concluded selection adapts individuals for the benefit of the whole community. The 20th-century gene-centred view denied group selection; 21st-century evolutionists are less dismissive.

### Classification of Selection

Selection acts on any heritable phenotypic trait; pressure can be altered by any environmental aspect. It is not always directional; it often maintains the status quo by eliminating less fit variants.

*   **By effect on a trait:** Stabilizing selection holds a trait at a stable optimum (disadvantaging deviations). Directional selection favors extreme values. Disruptive selection favors extremes over intermediates (can precede speciation).
*   **By effect on genetic diversity:** Purifying (negative) selection removes variation (opposed by *de novo* mutation). Balancing selection maintains variation, e.g., via heterozygote advantage (e.g., human ABO blood groups) or negative frequency-dependent selection.
*   **By life cycle stage:** Viability (survival) selection increases survival probability; fecundity (fertility) selection increases reproductive rate given survival. Subdivisions include pre/post-reproductive viability, sexual selection, gametic selection (gamete survival), and compatibility selection (zygote formation).
*   **By unit of selection:** Individual selection (benefit of individual), gene selection (directly at gene level, apt for kin selection/intragenomic conflict), group selection (groups as replicators—debated).
*   **By resource competed for:** Sexual selection (competition for mates—intrasexual or intersexual; can cause Fisherian runaway or sexy son hypothesis; often linked to sexual dimorphism). Ecological selection (all other means: kin selection, competition, infanticide). Darwin sometimes defined natural selection as ecological selection, treating sexual selection as separate.

### Arms Races

Natural selection drives evolutionary arms races. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria (e.g., MRSA) escalates as misuse selects for resistance, prompting new drugs, selecting for further resistance. Pesticide resistance in plants/insects follows similar dynamics. Natural arms races also occur: a gene suppressing male-killing by *Wolbachia* parasites spread in the butterfly *Hypolimnas bolina* on Samoa in just five years.

### Evolution by Means of Natural Selection

**Prerequisites**  
Heritable genetic variation affecting phenotypic fitness is required. Sources: mutations, genetic recombination, karyotype alterations. Most mutations are neutral or slightly deleterious (in humans, majority slightly deleterious). Mutations in regulatory "toolkit" genes (e.g., *HOX*) have large phenotypic effects; most are lethal, but nonlethal ones (e.g., cervical rib, polydactyly) can spread if fitness increases. Traits are not immutable; without selection, high-fitness traits become variable and deteriorate (vestigialization/evolutionary baggage), though they may retain limited function or be co-opted (preadaptation), e.g., blind mole-rat eye retaining photoperiod perception.

**Speciation**  
Requires reproductive isolation (reduced gene flow). Hybrids are selected against, opposing isolation evolution—except in allopatric speciation (geographic separation). E.B. Poulton (1903) realized isolation could evolve via divergence: incompatible alleles at the same gene in different lineages cause selection against heterozygotes (Bateson–Dobzhansky–Muller model). Reinforcement can favor increased pre-zygotic isolation, directly driving speciation.

### Genetic Basis

**Genotype and Phenotype**  
Selection acts on phenotypes (observable characteristics), which are plastic—shaped by development and behavior in the environment, not solely by genotype. Different gene versions (alleles) affect fitness-relevant traits. Some traits are single-gene; most are polygenic, with each gene contributing a small effect, creating a phenotypic continuum.

**Directionality of Selection**  
When a phenotypic component is heritable, selection alters allele frequencies: directional (one allele increases to fixation), stabilizing (deleterious alleles eliminated, conserving function), disruptive (extremes favored over intermediates, can cause sympatric speciation via niche partitioning). Balancing selection maintains alleles at intermediate frequencies: heterozygote advantage (e.g., sickle-cell heterozygote malaria resistance), disruptive selection (bimodal trait distribution), or frequency-dependent selection (fitness depends on phenotype distribution; game theory applies to kin selection/reciprocal altruism).

**Selection, Variation, and Drift**  
Neutral variation (no fitness effect) accounts for much molecular diversity (Kimura's neutral theory). Genetic drift eliminates neutral variation over time; bottlenecks cause founder effects. Selection reduces variation by eliminating maladapted individuals; mutation introduces it, creating a mutation–selection balance. Genetic linkage: alleles close on a chromosome recombine less. Selective sweeps: a favored allele rises, dragging linked neutral/deleterious alleles via "genetic hitchhiking," creating blocks of linkage disequilibrium. Background selection: persistent purifying selection at a site weeds out linked variation, reducing genomic variability without clear disequilibrium blocks.

### Impact

**Intellectual Influence**  
Darwin's ideas, alongside Smith and Marx, profoundly shaped 19th-century thought. Stephen Jay Gould argued natural selection "dethroned" traditional comforts (e.g., human specialness). Daniel Dennett called it a "universal acid" leaking into wider domains: evolutionary computation, quantum Darwinism, evolutionary economics, epistemology, psychology, and cosmological natural selection ("universal Darwinism").

**Origin of Life**  
A leading hypothesis: life began as self-replicating RNA polymers. When RNA chains met Darwin's conditions (heritability, variation, competition for limited resources), natural selection began. Fitness depended on intrinsic adaptive capacities (replication fidelity, decay avoidance, resource acquisition) determined by folded nucleotide sequences (ribozymes).

**Cell and Molecular Biology**  
Wilhelm Roux (1881) proposed embryonic development results from Darwinian competition among parts (molecules to organs). Modern "cellular Darwinism" (Jean-Jacques Kupiec) posits random molecular variation generates cell diversity; cell interactions impose order.

**Social and Psychological Theory**  
Engels (1872) saw Darwin satirizing human "free competition." Spencer and Francis Galton interpreted selection as progressive, justifying colonialism, eugenics, and social Darwinism. Konrad Lorenz (1940) misused it for Nazi policies (later disowned). Modern sociobiology and evolutionary psychology explain human psychology as adaptation to ancestral environments (e.g., Chomsky/Pinker on innate language acquisition; incest avoidance, gender roles). Richard Dawkins (1976) introduced "memes" as cultural units undergoing selection/recombination, expanded by Dennett for consciousness and culture.

**Information and Systems Theory**  
Alfred J. Lotka (1922) proposed selection as a physical principle of energy use; Howard T. Odum developed the maximum power principle (thermodynamics). Principles inspire computational techniques: genetic algorithms (John Holland, 1970s; David Goldberg) simulate reproduction/mutation to optimize solutions in rough energy landscapes.

**In Fiction**  
Darwinian themes pervade literature: optimistic (humanity evolving toward perfection) or pessimistic (dire consequences of struggle). Samuel Butler's *Erewhon* (1872); H.G. Wells's "The Man of the Year Million" (1893) depicting huge-headed, shrunken-bodied future humans.

## Terms

- ****Natural Selection**** — Differential survival and reproduction of individuals due to differences in heritable phenotypic traits; primary mechanism of adaptive evolution.
- ****Artificial Selection**** — Intentional breeding by humans for desirable traits; contrasted with natural selection's lack of intentional choice.
- ****Fitness**** — Reproductive success (genetic contribution to next generation), not longevity; defined as an average over a population.
- ****Modern Synthesis**** — Mid-20th-century integration of Darwinian selection with Mendelian genetics and population genetics.
- ****Evo-devo**** — Evolutionary developmental biology; compares developmental processes across organisms to infer how development evolved, linking molecular genetics to morphology.
- ****Extended Evolutionary Synthesis**** — 21st-century frameworks challenging the gene-centred view, restoring organismal agency, development, and extra-genetic inheritance to evolutionary theory.
- ****Stabilizing Selection**** — Selection favoring intermediate phenotypes, eliminating extremes, maintaining a trait at a stable optimum.
- ****Directional Selection**** — Selection favoring one extreme phenotype, shifting allele frequencies toward fixation of the favored variant.
- ****Balancing Selection**** — Selection maintaining genetic variation in a population (e.g., heterozygote advantage, frequency-dependent selection).
- ****Selective Sweep**** — Rapid increase of a favored allele's frequency, dragging linked neutral/deleterious alleles to high frequency via genetic hitchhiking.

## Debates and Open Questions

*   **Unit of Selection:** Ongoing debate over the degree to which group selection (vs. individual/gene selection) occurs in nature; 21st-century views are more open to multi-level selection than the gene-centred modern synthesis.
*   **Gene-Centred vs. Organismal Agency:** The 20th-century "selfish gene" / environment-as-agent paradigm is challenged by extended syntheses arguing organisms are active agents in their own evolution (niche construction, developmental bias, extra-genetic inheritance).
*   **Role of Genetic Drift vs. Selection:** Neutral theory (Kimura) posits drift explains much molecular diversity; the balance between drift, mutation, and selection strength remains a quantitative research frontier.
*   **Mechanisms of Speciation:** While allopatric speciation is well-supported, the frequency and mechanisms of sympatric speciation (e.g., via disruptive selection) and the precise role of reinforcement are debated.
*   **Origin of Life:** How inorganic matter transitioned to self-replicating RNA (or other polymers) meeting Darwin's conditions (heritability, variation, competition) remains unresolved.
*   **Evolutionary Psychology:** Hypotheses linking specific modern behaviors (gender roles, incest avoidance) to ancestral adaptations are difficult to test and remain controversial regarding methodology and cultural confounding.
*   **Memetics:** The utility of "memes" as a rigorous scientific framework for cultural evolution (vs. metaphor) is debated.
