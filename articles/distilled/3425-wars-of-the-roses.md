# Wars of the Roses

## Overview
The Wars of the Roses (1455–1487) were a series of dynastic civil wars fought for control of the English throne between the House of York (white rose) and the House of Lancaster (red rose). Rooted in socio-economic troubles and the weak rule of Henry VI, the conflict saw power shift between the houses before concluding with the victory of Henry Tudor, who united the claims through marriage and founded the Tudor dynasty, ending the medieval period in England.

## Timeline
- **1455** — First Battle of St Albans; York captures Henry VI.
- **1460** — Battle of Northampton; Yorkists capture Henry VI again.
- **1460** — Act of Accord disinherits Henry VI's son in favor of Richard of York.
- **1461** — Battle of Wakefield; Richard of York is killed.
- **1461** — Edward IV crowned after Battle of Towton.
- **1470** — Henry VI restored to throne (Readeption).
- **1471** — Edward IV defeats and kills Warwick at Barnet; Henry VI's heir killed at Tewkesbury; Henry VI dies.
- **1483** — Richard III deposes Edward V; Princes in the Tower disappear.
- **1485** — Henry Tudor defeats Richard III at Bosworth Field.
- **1487** — Henry VII defeats Yorkist rebels at Stoke Field, ending the wars.

## Body
### Origins and Causes
The conflict stemmed from the socio-economic aftermath of the Hundred Years' War and the system of "bastard feudalism," where powerful nobles maintained private armies (affinities). The weak and mentally unstable rule of Henry VI revived the claim of his cousin, Richard of York. The rival houses descended from sons of Edward III: Lancaster from John of Gaunt (third son) and York from Edmund of Langley (fourth son), with York also claiming descent through the female line from the second son, Lionel of Antwerp.

### Early Conflict and Yorkist Victory (1455–1461)
Warfare began in 1455 with York's capture of Henry at St Albans. After a period of uneasy peace, fighting resumed. Richard of York was killed at the Battle of Wakefield in 1460, but his son Edward inherited the claim. Edward defeated the Lancastrians at Towton in 1461 and was crowned Edward IV, securing the throne.

### Reign of Edward IV (1461–1483)
Edward IV's rule was marked by internal strife. His secret marriage to Elizabeth Woodville alienated his chief ally, Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick ("the Kingmaker"). Warwick rebelled, briefly restoring Henry VI to the throne in 1470. Edward returned from exile, defeated and killed Warwick at Barnet in 1471, and defeated the Lancastrians at Tewkesbury, killing Henry VI's son. Henry VI died shortly after, likely murdered. Edward ruled unopposed until his death in 1483.

### Richard III and the Tudor Victory (1483–1487)
Edward IV was succeeded by his young son, Edward V, who was deposed within months by his uncle, Richard III. The disappearance of Edward V and his brother fueled opposition. Henry Tudor, a Lancastrian claimant through the female line, invaded from France. With the support of disaffected Yorkists, he defeated and killed Richard III at Bosworth Field in 1485. Henry VII married Elizabeth of York, uniting the houses. The final Yorkist rebellion was crushed at Stoke Field in 1487.

### Impact and Legacy
The wars resulted in the extinction of the direct male Plantagenet line and the rise of the Tudor dynasty, which strengthened the monarchy. While the social devastation may have been exaggerated, the wars claimed an estimated 105,000 lives. The Tudors used the conflict's memory to justify their rule and promote a narrative of restored peace and order.

## Terms
- **Bastard Feudalism**: A late medieval system where nobles maintained private armies (affinities) through financial payments rather than traditional feudal land grants.
- **Act of Accord (1460)**: A compromise that disinherited Henry VI's son and named Richard of York as heir to the throne.
- **Lord Protector**: A regent appointed to govern during a monarch's minority or incapacity.
- **Readeption of Henry VI (1470)**: The brief restoration of Henry VI to the throne by Warwick the Kingmaker.
- **Bill of Attainder**: A legislative act declaring a person guilty of treason without trial, resulting in loss of property and titles.
- **Affinity**: The network of retainers, servants, and supporters bound to a noble lord.
- **Tudor Rose**: The combined red and white rose emblem adopted by Henry VII to symbolize the union of Lancaster and York.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Historians disagree on the primary causes, weighing factors like bastard feudalism, the weak rule of Henry VI, and the succession crisis from Edward III's reign.
- The fate of the "Princes in the Tower" (Edward V and his brother) remains unknown, with the most accepted theory being their murder on Richard III's orders.
- The scale of social disruption is debated; revisionists argue the wars' impact on the general populace was limited, as fighting was largely confined to nobles and their retinues.
