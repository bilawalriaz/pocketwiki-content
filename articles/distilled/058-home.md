# Home

## Overview

A home, or domicile, is a space used as a permanent or semi-permanent residence for one or more human occupants, sometimes including companion animals. It provides sheltered spaces for domestic activities like sleeping, preparing food, eating, and hygiene, as well as for work and leisure. The concept of "home" extends beyond physical structures—it can be static (house, apartment), mobile (houseboat, trailer, yurt), or digital (virtual space)—and is researched across disciplines including psychology, sociology, and politics. The home as a concept has evolved significantly over time, from prehistoric caves to modern smart homes, and encompasses experiences like exile, belonging, homesickness, and homelessness.

## Body

### Prehistoric and Ancient Homes

The earliest human homes were likely natural features such as caves. Evidence shows Homo erectus in China at Zhoukoudian, Homo rhodesiensis in South Africa, Homo neanderthalensis and Homo heidelbergensis in Europe, Homo floresiensis in Indonesia, and Denisovans in Siberia all inhabited caves from at least one million years ago. In southern Africa, early modern humans regularly used sea caves starting about 180,000 years ago, when they learned to exploit the sea—the oldest known site is PP13B at Pinnacle Point. This may have enabled rapid expansion out of Africa and colonization of Australia by 60–50,000 years ago. Caves served as shelters, burial sites (rock-cut tombs), and religious sites (Buddhist caves, Cave of a Thousand Buddhas, sacred caves of Crete). As technology progressed, humans began constructing dwellings like huts and longhouses from the late Neolithic.

By the Bronze Age (c. 3500–1200 BC), Mesopotamian communities at Uruk and Ubaid built permanent mudbrick dwellings with uniform bricks and bitumen mortar, organized around courtyards along straight streets with shared wells and ovens. Ancient Egyptian towns at Amarna and Deir el-Medina (Old Kingdom, c. 2686–2181 BC onwards) featured mudbrick houses with flat roofs, reception rooms, private chambers, and small courtyards. The Indus Valley Civilisation (c. 2600–1900 BC) at Mohenjo-daro and Harappa had standardized fired-bricks, two-story houses with private wells, indoor bathrooms with drainage, and south-facing courtyards for ventilation. On Bronze Age Crete, Minoan palaces at Knossos incorporated light wells and lustral basins. By the 1st century BC, affluent Romans lived in domus—multiroom houses around an atrium and peristyle garden—while the majority lived in cramped, fire-prone insulae (multi-story apartment blocks).

### Post-Classical and Renaissance Homes

After the Western Roman Empire's fall in the 5th century, European domestic architecture reverted to timber-framed or wattle-and-daub huts, while elites inhabited stone manor houses with great halls. By the 12th century, manor houses featured a central hall, private solar chambers, and service wings. Medieval towns had multi-storey timber-framed "hall houses" with jettied upper floors lining narrow streets. In the Islamic world from the 8th century, inward-facing courtyard houses predominated, with shaded central courts, water features, mashrabiya screens (latticework for ventilation and privacy), and decorated plasterwork. In East Asia, the Chinese siheyuan compound—standardized during the Yuan and Ming dynasties—offered multigenerational living around a north–south axis courtyard.

The Renaissance (14th–17th centuries) brought classical ideals to domestic design. The Palazzo Medici Riccardi in Florence (begun 1444) introduced rusticated façades and symmetrical floor plans; Palladio's Venetian villas emphasized proportion and garden integration. Advances in glassmaking allowed larger windows, and masonry chimneys replaced central hearths, improving light and air quality. From the 14th to 16th centuries, homelessness was perceived as a "vagrancy problem" threatening the state.

### Modern Era and the Home Concept

According to Kirsten Gram-Hanssen, the strong relation between home and physical building is rooted in the 17th-century Enlightenment; before this, home was more public than private. Traits like privacy and intimacy gained prominence, aligning home with the bourgeoisie. Edward Coke's legal declaration—"The house of everyman is to him as his castle"—popularized as "The Englishman's home is his castle," reinforced the home-house connection. In 18th-century England, upper-class women were scorned for activities outside the home due to the longstanding home-women association. The smart home concept arose in the 19th century with limited electricity introduction. The home-work distinction formulated in the 20th century, with home acting as sanctuary—a site of supreme comfort and familial intimacy buffering the greater world.

### Common Types of Homes

A house is a single-unit residential building, ranging from rudimentary huts to complex structures with plumbing, electrical, and HVAC systems. The social unit living in a house is a household—typically a family, but possibly roommates or unconnected individuals. Townhouses or row houses contain numerous family dwellings. Joseph Rykwert distinguished home from house: a house requires a building, a home does not; house is "emotionally neutral" while home evokes "personal, cognitive aspects." By the mid-18th century, home's definition extended beyond a house.

Mobile homes (house trailers, park homes) are prefabricated structures built on permanently attached chassis, used permanently or temporarily. Houseboats are boats designed primarily as homes, often moored and tethered to land for utilities; float houses are a Canadian and American term for houses on rafts. In Western countries, houseboats are privately owned or rented to holiday-goers; in European canals (Amsterdam, London, Paris), people dwell in them year-round. Traditional yurts (gers) are portable round tents covered with skins or felt, used by nomadic groups in Central Asian steppes, with latticework walls, door frames, ribs, and a crown wheel; modern yurts may use steam-bent wood, metal framing, canvas, or radiant insulation.

### Homelessness and Home Loss

Homelessness can result from natural disasters, fraud, theft, arson, war, voluntary sale, relationship breakdown, government expropriation, repossession, eviction, or lease expiration. Jurisdiction-dependent causes include adverse possession, unpaid property taxes, and corruption in failed states. Personal insolvency, mental illness, or severe physical incapacity without affordable care commonly lead to home loss. Refugees have fled their homes due to violence or persecution, seeking shelter or asylum. Scholars argue the concept of home is dependent on homelessness: "in a sense, without homelessness, we would not be concerned with what home means."

### Anthropogenic Significance

Gaston Bachelard and Martin Heidegger consider the home-dwelling connection an "essential characteristic" of humanity. Psychologically, "The strongest sense of home commonly coincides geographically with a dwelling," attenuating with distance but not in a fixed way. Homes satisfy identity-based desires and function as a "symbol of the self." Emmanuel Levinas wrote of home as where one regains a sense of self upon seclusion from the world. Home has been described as an "essentially contested concept" with connotations of security, identity, ritual, and socialisation. Dysfunctional sociality may negate a residence's sense of being a home; alienated, one may feel "metaphorically homeless." In modern America, owned houses have greater cachet as homes; some scholars contend the house-home conflation results from popular media and capitalist interest. Shelley Mallett proposed home as abstractions: space, feeling, praxis, or "a way of being in the world"—reflected in the proverb "A house is not a home." A person's home state physiologically influences behavior and mental health; Marianne Gullestad wrote of home as the center of everyday life reflecting cultural values like gender roles. Zygmunt Bauman said to be homesick is to desire belonging. Places like homes trigger self-reflection; collective historical sites like Gettysburg or Ground Zero do similarly. The connection between home and family is so pertinent that some scholars consider the terms synonymous.

## Terms

- **Domicile**: A legal term for a permanent or semi-permanent residence
- **Insulae**: Ancient Roman multi-story apartment blocks, often cramped and fire-prone
- **Domus**: Ancient Roman affluent multiroom urban houses around an atrium and peristyle garden
- **Mashrabiya**: Islamic latticework screens providing ventilation and privacy
- **Siheyuan**: Chinese compound with multigenerational living around a north–south axis courtyard
- **Yurt (ger)**: Portable round tent used by Central Asian nomads, with latticework walls and felt covering
- **Household**: The social unit living in a house, typically a family
- **Vernacular**: Dwellings constructed in accordance with residents' needs, per Paul Oliver
- **Liminal space**: A transitional or in-between space, relevant to home studies

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether the strong home-house connection is universal or rooted in Enlightenment thinking, as Gram-Hanssen argues. The concept of home is considered an "essentially contested concept," with varied definitions across cultures—some ascribing less value to residential privacy. Debate exists over whether a rooming house can provide a home, and whether the house-home conflation results from popular media and capitalist interests. The home-homelessness dichotomy is debated: some scholars claim home's concept depends on homelessness for its meaning. Gender differences in home experience—men conditioned to experience control and little labour, women the reverse—remain a subject of scholarly analysis.
