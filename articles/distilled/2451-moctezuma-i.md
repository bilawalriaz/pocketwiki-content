# Moctezuma I

## Overview
Moctezuma I (c. 1398–1469) was the second Aztec emperor and fifth king of Tenochtitlan. His reign consolidated the Aztec Empire, expanded its territory significantly, and established Tenochtitlan as the dominant partner in the Triple Alliance. He is often confused with his later descendant, Moctezuma II, who encountered the Spanish.

## Timeline
- **c. 1398** — Born to Emperor Huitzilihuitl and Queen Miahuaxihuitl.
- **1426** — After his brother Chimalpopoca's assassination, Moctezuma and Tlacaelel support their half-uncle Itzcoatl as ruler.
- **1440** — Elected tlatoani ("Great Speaker") by nobles at age 42.
- **1440–1453** — Period of internal peace, allowing for major reforms.
- **1445** — Conquers the state of Oaxaca.
- **1446** — Faces a locust swarm that destroys crops.
- **1449** — Lake Texcoco floods Tenochtitlan.
- **1450–1453** — Severe drought and frost cause famine, ending the period of peace.
- **c. 1458** — Leads a successful expedition against the Mixtec city-state of Coixtlahuaca.
- **1469** — Dies; succeeded by his daughter Atotoztli or her son Axayacatl.

## Body
### Accession and Consolidation of Power
Moctezuma was the son of Emperor Huitzilihuitl. After his brother Chimalpopoca was assassinated, Moctezuma and his brother Tlacaelel led a militant noble faction that installed their half-uncle Itzcoatl as ruler. Under Itzcoatl, Moctezuma served as a general. Upon Itzcoatl's death in 1440, Moctezuma was elected tlatoani by this noble group. His coronation was a major ceremony involving human sacrifice.

### Expansion and the Triple Alliance
As ruler, Moctezuma solidified the Aztec Triple Alliance with the city-states of Texcoco and Tlacopan. This alliance structured the empire's spoils: Tenochtitlan and Texcoco each received two-fifths, with Tlacopan receiving one-fifth. He expanded the empire beyond the Valley of Mexico to the Gulf Coast, subjugating the Huastec and Totonac peoples and gaining access to exotic goods. He also led major conquests, including the Mixtec city of Coixtlahuaca in about 1458, from which he extracted significant tribute.

### Infrastructure and Reform
Moctezuma's reign saw major infrastructure projects, most notably the construction of a double aqueduct system with Nezahualcoyotl of Texcoco, which supplied Tenochtitlan with fresh water. During a period of internal peace (1440–1453), he enacted strict social, political, and economic reforms. These included sumptuary laws dictating housing and dress based on social class, and legal policies that emphasized class separation and centralized judicial authority, with only Moctezuma authorized to issue death sentences.

### Natural Disasters and the Flower War
His rule was marked by severe natural disasters: a locust swarm (1446), a flood (1449), and a multi-year drought and frost (beginning 1450) that caused famine and population loss. Believing the disasters were divine punishment, Moctezuma increased human sacrifice. This led to the institution of the "Flower War," a series of ritualized conflicts with city-states like Tlaxcala and Cholula, designed to capture prisoners for sacrifice.

## Terms
*   **Tlatoani**: The title for the ruler of an Aztec city-state, meaning "Great Speaker" or "He Who Speaks."
*   **Triple Alliance**: The political and military alliance between Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan that formed the core of the Aztec Empire.
*   **Flower War**: A series of pre-arranged, ritualized battles between the Aztec Triple Alliance and certain enemy city-states, primarily to capture victims for human sacrifice.
*   **Sumptuary Laws**: Laws that regulate consumption, such as what clothing, food, or housing a person of a given social class is allowed to use.
*   **Tlacaelel**: Moctezuma's brother, a powerful advisor and general who was influential in Aztec politics and religion.
*   **Nezahualcoyotl**: The ruler (tlatoani) of Texcoco and a key ally of Moctezuma I.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact nature of Moctezuma's succession is debated. Sources indicate he was succeeded by his daughter Atotoztli, who may have ruled as tlatoani or as regent for her son, Axayacatl.
*   The role of his brother Tlacaelel is complex; some sources suggest he opposed Moctezuma's early leadership, while others state he was offered the throne and refused it.
