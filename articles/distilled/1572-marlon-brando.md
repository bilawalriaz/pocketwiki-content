# Marlon Brando

## Overview
Marlon Brando (1924–2004) was an American actor widely considered the most influential screen performer of the 20th century. He popularized the Stanislavski system—an approach emphasizing psychological realism and emotional memory—bringing a raw, naturalistic style that displaced classical theatricality in Hollywood. His early roles, particularly Stanley Kowalski in *A Streetcar Named Desire* (1951) and Terry Malloy in *On the Waterfront* (1954), established a new paradigm for male vulnerability and rebellion. After a fallow period in the 1960s marked by commercial failures and a reputation for difficulty, he staged a historic comeback as Vito Corleone in *The Godfather* (1972), winning his second Oscar. Brando’s career was defined by a tension between his immense talent and his disdain for the industry, manifesting in erratic behavior, weight fluctuations, and a pioneering use of cue cards to preserve spontaneity. Beyond film, he was a visible activist for civil rights, Native American sovereignty, and anti-apartheid causes, famously declining his 1973 Oscar to protest Hollywood’s depiction of Indigenous peoples.

## Body

### Early Life and Acting Foundations
Brando’s childhood was marked by parental instability: his father traveled frequently as a salesman, and his mother, a stage actress, was often absent, leaving Brando with abandonment issues. After expulsion from Shattuck Military Academy (1943) and a failed attempt to enlist (rejected for a "trick knee" from a football injury), he followed his sisters to New York. There, he studied at the Dramatic Workshop of the New School under German director Erwin Piscator and, crucially, Stella Adler. Adler taught the Stanislavski system, which encouraged actors to explore internal motivations and emotional memory to fully realize a character. Brando distinguished himself immediately; Adler recalled an exercise where the class was told to act like chickens facing a nuclear bomb. While others panicked, Brando calmly pretended to lay an egg, reasoning, "I'm a chicken—what do I know about bombs?" He rejected the label "Method actor" associated with Lee Strasberg, insisting Strasberg never taught him and exploited students, crediting only Adler and Kazan for his craft.

### Broadway Breakthrough and *Streetcar*
Brando’s Broadway debut came in *I Remember Mama* (1944). He gained critical attention in *Truckline Cafe* and *Candida* (1946), but his career pivoted when Tallulah Bankhead fired him from *The Eagle Has Two Heads* (1946). Bankhead’s acidic recommendation letter to Tennessee Williams—calling Brando "a total pig of a man... perfect as Stanley"—secured him the role of Stanley Kowalski in *A Streetcar Named Desire* (1947), directed by Elia Kazan. Williams initially wanted John Garfield, but Kazan cast the younger Brando, noting his reading "humanizes the character... brutality and callousness of youth rather than a vicious old man." Brando based the portrayal on boxer Rocky Graziano, studying him at a gym. The role made him a sensation; the 1951 film adaptation earned his first Oscar nomination and cemented his status as a symbol of the "generation gap."

### Early Film Stardom and *On the Waterfront*
Brando’s film debut, *The Men* (1950), saw him live in a veterans' hospital for a month to portray a paraplegic. He followed with *Viva Zapata!* (1952), researching in Mexico, and *Julius Caesar* (1953), where his Mark Antony impressed John Gielgud. *The Wild One* (1953) made him a global style icon, though he was puzzled by the teen rebellion it inspired. For *On the Waterfront* (1954), Brando initially declined due to Kazan’s testimony before the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC), but producer Sam Spiegel persuaded him. Brando improvised the famous "I coulda been a contender" scene, gently pushing away his brother’s gun rather than playing it at gunpoint, arguing Terry Malloy would never believe his brother would shoot. Kazan called the result perhaps the best performance in American film history. Brando won his first Oscar, though he left the premiere depressed by his own work.

### The 1960s: Commercial Decline and Artistic Drift
Brando’s only directorial effort, *One-Eyed Jacks* (1961), ballooned from three to six months and double the budget; Paramount seized control in post-production. The disastrous *Mutiny on the Bounty* (1962) in Tahiti cemented his "difficult" reputation, nearly bankrupting MGM. He signed a five-picture deal with Universal that yielded mostly flops (*The Ugly American*, *Bedtime Story*, *The Appaloosa*, *A Countess from Hong Kong*). He clashed with Charlie Chaplin on *Countess*, finding Chaplin’s didactic direction authoritarian. Critics like Pauline Kael savaged him as a "buffoon" mocking his own talent. Bright spots included *The Chase* (1966) and *Burn!* (1969), the latter Brando’s personal favorite, directed by Gillo Pontecorvo, though they "nearly killed each other" on set.

### The Godfather and Career Resurgence
By 1970, Brando was deemed "unbankable." Francis Ford Coppola fought Paramount to cast him as Vito Corleone in *The Godfather* (1972). Studio president Stanley Jaffe demanded a screen test, a reduced fee ($50,000), and financial liability for delays. Brando’s test—using cotton balls to puff his cheeks—convinced Gulf+Western chief Charles Bluhdorn. On set, Brando supported the young cast (Pacino, Duvall, Caan) and defended Coppola from studio interference. He won his second Oscar but declined it, sending Sacheen Littlefeather to protest the film industry’s treatment of Native Americans and the ongoing Wounded Knee occupation. The role revived his box-office standing for the last time.

### Late Career: *Last Tango*, *Apocalypse Now*, and Final Roles
*Last Tango in Paris* (1972), directed by Bernardo Bertolucci, featured graphic sexual content. Brando refused to memorize lines, using cue cards. The infamous "butter" rape scene was conceived by Brando and Bertolucci without informing co-star Maria Schneider until moments before filming; Schneider later described feeling "violated" and "a little raped" by both men. Brando refused to speak to Bertolucci for 15 years. In *Apocalypse Now* (1979), Brando arrived overweight and unprepared (having not read *Heart of Darkness*), forcing Coppola to reconceive Colonel Kurtz as a man who "indulged every aspect of himself." Brando was paid $1 million a week; the film earned him ~$9 million. Later work included the anti-apartheid *A Dry White Season* (1989, Oscar nomination), the self-parody *The Freshman* (1990), and the well-received *The Score* (2001). His final years saw critically panned roles in *The Island of Dr. Moreau* (1996) and *Free Money* (1998).

### Personal Life and Relationships
Brando’s personal life was tumultuous. He fathered at least 11 children (three adopted) with multiple partners. He described himself as bisexual in 1976. Key relationships included: a youthful affair with *Streetcar* castmate Sandy Campbell; a long affair with Mexican actress Katy Jurado; a volatile romance with Rita Moreno, who attempted suicide after a botched abortion Brando arranged; marriages to Anna Kashfi (1957–59, son Christian) and Movita Castaneda (1960–68, annulled, two children); a long partnership with Tarita Teriipaia (met on *Mutiny*, two children, adopted two more); and a long-term relationship with housekeeper Maria Cristina Ruiz (three children). He maintained a profound, likely platonic bond with childhood friend Wally Cox; after Cox’s death (1973), Brando kept his ashes for 20 years, eventually scattering both their ashes together in Death Valley and Tahiti.

### Activism
Brando translated screen rebellion into real-world activism. He performed in the Zionist play *A Flag Is Born* (1946), supported JFK and LBJ, and donated to the SCLC and Medgar Evers’ children’s fund. He joined the 1963 March on Washington and Freedom Rides. In 1964, he was arrested at a "fish-in" in Washington state protesting broken treaty rights for Native Americans (the Puyallup tribe named the spot "Brando’s Landing"). His 1973 Oscar refusal via Sacheen Littlefeather remains the most famous protest in Academy history. He advocated against apartheid, favoring a boycott of his films in South Africa (1964) and starring in *A Dry White Season* (1989). His views on Israel shifted from early support to condemnation of Palestinian displacement. His 1979 *Playboy* and 1996 *Larry King Live* comments—that Hollywood was "run by Jews" who prevented negative Jewish stereotypes on screen while allowing others—drew accusations of antisemitism, though Jewish friends like agent Jay Kanter defended him as a supporter of Israel and Jewish causes.

### Later Life, Health, and Death
Brando gained significant weight in the 1970s, exceeding 300 lbs by the 1990s, suffering type 2 diabetes and liver cancer. He patented a drum-tuning mechanism (2002–04) and operated a ham radio from his Tetiaroa atoll. A close friend of Michael Jackson, he stayed at Neverland Ranch frequently; Jackson provided a golf cart with oxygen for Brando’s mobility. In April 2001, he was hospitalized for pneumonia. He recorded one line for *The Godfather* video game (2006) shortly before death. He died July 1, 2004, at UCLA Medical Center of respiratory failure from pulmonary fibrosis with congestive heart failure. He was cremated; ashes scattered in Tahiti and Death Valley alongside Wally Cox’s.

## Terms
- ****Stanislavski system**** — An acting technique developed by Konstantin Stanislavski emphasizing psychological realism, emotional memory, and the actor's internal motivation to "live the part" rather than represent it mechanically.
- ****Method acting**** — An American offshoot of the Stanislavski system (associated with Lee Strasberg and the Actors Studio) focusing on affective memory—recalling personal emotions to fuel a character. Brando rejected this label, crediting Stella Adler instead.
- ****Cue cards**** — Large cards displaying dialogue, placed off-camera. Brando used them extensively from the 1950s onward to avoid memorization, believing reading them simulated the natural hesitation of spontaneous speech.
- ****HUAC**** — House Un-American Activities Committee; a congressional body investigating alleged communist influence. Elia Kazan’s 1952 testimony naming colleagues caused a rift with Brando, who later worked with Kazan on *On the Waterfront*.
- ****Wounded Knee Occupation**** — A 1973 standoff at Wounded Knee, South Dakota, between American Indian Movement activists and federal agents. Brando cited it in his 1973 Oscar refusal.
- ****Tetiaroa**** — A twelve-island atoll in French Polynesia purchased by Brando in 1967 after filming *Mutiny on the Bounty*. He built an eco-resort and research station there.
- ****Sacheen Littlefeather**** — Actress and activist (born Marie Louise Cruz) who appeared at the 1973 Oscars in Apache attire to decline Brando’s Best Actor award for *The Godfather*, protesting Native American treatment.
- ****"I coulda been a contender"**** — Improvised line from *On the Waterfront* (1954), spoken by Terry Malloy to his brother Charlie. Often cited as the most famous line in American cinema.
- ****Pennebaker Productions**** — Brando’s production company, formed in 1957 and named for his mother Dorothy Pennebaker, intended to develop socially relevant films.
- ****Butter scene**** — The controversial anal rape sequence in *Last Tango in Paris* (1972) using butter as lubricant. Conceived by Brando and Bertolucci without co-star Maria Schneider’s prior knowledge.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authorship of the "Method" legacy**: Brando insisted he was not a "Method actor" (Strasberg’s version) but an Adler/Stanislavski disciple. Scholars debate whether his naturalism stems from the System, his innate instinct, or a synthesis, and whether his later cue-card reliance was technique or laziness.
- ***Last Tango in Paris* ethics**: The "butter scene" remains a flashpoint. Bertolucci admitted (2013) to withholding details from Schneider to elicit genuine rage; Schneider felt violated. Brando felt exploited by Bertolucci. The debate centers on the line between directorial manipulation and assault in pursuit of realism.
- **Antisemitism vs. critique of power**: Brando’s statements that Hollywood Jews suppressed negative Jewish stereotypes while permitting others are cited as antisemitic tropes (dual loyalty, control). Defenders note his lifelong support for Jewish causes, Israel (early on), and civil rights, framing his remarks as clumsy criticism of Hollywood’s hypocrisy rather than hatred.
- **Talent vs. professionalism**: Critics and biographers dispute whether Brando’s 1960s–70s decline resulted from studio mistreatment, personal trauma (mother’s death, Cox’s death), boredom, or self-sabotage. His late flashes of brilliance (*The Godfather*, *A Dry White Season*, *The Freshman*) complicate a simple "decline" narrative.
- **Sexuality and identity**: Brando described himself as bisexual and had documented relationships with men (Wally Cox, Sandy Campbell, per Capote) and many women. The extent to which his sexuality informed his portrayals of masculinity, vulnerability, and rebellion—and the industry’s suppression of it—remains an open interpretive field.
