# Cymbal

## Overview
Cymbals are common percussion instruments made of thin, round plates of copper alloys, often used in pairs. They are fundamental to a wide range of musical ensembles, from orchestras and marching bands to jazz and rock groups, providing dynamic accents, color, and rhythmic drive.

## Timeline
- **7th century BC** — Representations of cymbals appear in reliefs from the Armenian Highlands.
- **Ancient times** — Cymbals are used in ancient Egypt, Greece, Rome, and throughout the Bible.
- **3rd or 4th century AD** — Cymbals may have been introduced to China from Central Asia.
- **Circa 977-1010 CE** — The Persian epic *Shahnameh* mentions cymbals (sanj) at least 14 times, often in a war context.
- **14th century or earlier** — Cymbals are employed by Turkish janissaries.
- **By the 17th century** — Turkish janissary cymbals are adopted into European music.
- **Mid-18th century** — Cymbals become commonly played in European military bands and orchestras.
- **19th century** — Composers begin calling for larger roles for cymbals, spurring development of new shapes and techniques.
- **Early 1900s** — Early jazz drummers borrow the suspended cymbal mounting style, leading to modern drum kit cymbals.

## Body

### History and Cultural Use
Cymbals have existed since ancient times, with evidence from the Armenian Highlands, Mesopotamia, Egypt, Greece, and Rome. In India, they have been used since ancient times and remain integral to temple rituals. The Persian epic *Shahnameh* (c. 977-1010 CE) mentions cymbals (sanj) frequently, often describing their use in war to frighten enemies or celebrate victory. The text sometimes calls them "Indian cymbals." In Persian culture, cymbals also feature in the Ashura mourning ceremony. Cymbals were used by Turkish janissaries by the 14th century, and their adoption into European music by the 17th century led to their common use in orchestras and military bands by the mid-18th century.

### Anatomy and Sound Production
A cymbal's sound is determined by its anatomy. The central hole is for mounting or holding. The raised **bell** or **cup** around the hole produces a high "pinging" pitch. The **bow** is the main surface, often divided into a thicker **ride area** (for stick articulation) and a thinner **crash area** near the **edge** or **rim**. Cymbals are measured by diameter (larger = louder, longer sustain) and weight (heavier = louder, more cut; thinner = fuller sound, lower pitch). The **profile** (height of the bow) affects pitch: a higher profile yields a higher pitch.

### Orchestral and Ensemble Types
Orchestral cymbals offer a wide range of color and effect, capable of projecting through a full orchestra. **Clash cymbals** (or crash/hand cymbals) are traditionally used in pairs, held by straps. They can be crashed, tapped, scraped, or choked. They are often paired with the bass drum for powerful accents, a practice seen in older scores and still used in drum kits. The **suspended cymbal** is mounted to vibrate freely and can be played with mallets for effects ranging from soft, eerie sounds to loud crashes and building tremolos. **Hi-hat** cymbals evolved from clash cymbals and are a core part of the modern drum kit, played with the foot. **Ancient or tuned cymbals** (like **crotales**) are small, thick, and produce definite pitches, resembling hand-bells.

### Modern Drum Kit Cymbals
The modern drum kit typically includes a **crash** cymbal for accents, a **ride** cymbal for steady rhythms, and a pair of **hi-hat** cymbals. The **crash/ride** is a versatile hybrid. Other specialized types include **splash**, **china**, **sizzle**, and **bell** cymbals, each offering distinct timbres for different musical effects.

## Terms
- **Cymbalist**: A player of cymbals.
- **Bell/Cup**: The raised central section of a cymbal, producing a high "pinging" pitch.
- **Bow**: The main surface of the cymbal surrounding the bell.
- **Ride area**: The thicker section of the bow closer to the bell, suited for stick articulation.
- **Crash area**: The thinner, tapering section of the bow near the edge, suited for crashing.
- **Profile**: The vertical height of the cymbal's bow; a higher profile generally means a higher pitch.
- **Suspended cymbal**: A cymbal mounted to vibrate freely, often played with mallets for rolls and swells.
- **Hi-hat**: A pair of cymbals mounted on a stand and operated by a foot pedal, a core component of a drum kit.
- **Crotales**: Small, thick, tuned cymbals that produce definite pitches, descended from ancient designs.

## Debates and Open questions
The exact origin and initial spread of cymbals are not definitively known, with evidence pointing to multiple ancient civilizations. The *Shahnameh* refers to cymbals as "Indian," but does not claim they are Persian in origin, leaving their precise path of cultural transmission open to scholarly interpretation.
