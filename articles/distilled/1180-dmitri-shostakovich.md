# Dmitri Shostakovich

## Overview
Dmitri Dmitriyevich Shostakovich (1906–1975) was a Soviet-era Russian composer and pianist whose 15 symphonies and 15 string quartets form the backbone of a vast output spanning orchestral, chamber, operatic, and film music. He achieved early international fame with his First Symphony (1926) but spent his career navigating the lethal politics of the Stalinist state. His opera *Lady Macbeth of Mtsensk* was officially condemned in 1936 ("Muddle Instead of Music"), triggering a first denunciation that threatened his life; a second, broader crackdown followed in 1948 under the Zhdanov Doctrine, banning his works and dismissing him from teaching posts. Shostakovich survived by writing "desk drawer" works for private preservation, composing film scores for income, and producing public works that satisfied authorities—most famously the Fifth Symphony (1937), subtitled "A Soviet artist's creative response to just criticism." His music fuses Mahlerian scale, neoclassical clarity, grotesque satire, and a personal system of musical cryptograms (notably the DSCH motif). Since the 1979 publication of the disputed memoir *Testimony*, scholars debate whether his works contain coded anti-government dissent or represent forced compliance, a controversy that defines his modern reception.

## Body

### Youth and Conservatory Training
Shostakovich was born into a family of Polish-Lithuanian descent exiled to Siberia after the 1863 January Uprising; his father was an engineer under Dmitri Mendeleev. He began piano at nine with his mother, displaying prodigious memory. In 1919, aged 13, he entered the Petrograd Conservatory under Alexander Glazunov. He studied piano with Leonid Nikolayev, composition with Maximilian Steinberg, and counterpoint with Nikolay Sokolov. His graduation piece, the **First Symphony** (1925), premiered May 12, 1926, conducted by Nikolai Malko with the Leningrad Philharmonic. The work’s fusion of academic rigor and modernist wit launched his international career; Bruno Walter and Leopold Stokowski soon performed it abroad.

### Early Career and First Denunciation (1926–1937)
Shostakovich pursued a dual career as pianist and composer. His idiosyncratic, "anti-sentimental" playing earned only a diploma at the 1927 Chopin Competition in Warsaw (appendicitis struck opening day). He befriended musicologist Ivan Sollertinsky, who introduced him to Mahler and Bruckner. Early works included the Second Symphony (*To October*, 1927) and the satirical opera *The Nose* (1930), savaged by the Russian Association of Proletarian Musicians (RAPM). His opera *Lady Macbeth of Mtsensk* (1934) was initially hailed as a model Soviet work. On January 26, 1936, Stalin attended a performance and left early. Two days later, *Pravda* published "Muddle Instead of Music," calling the opera "dissonant, muddled... quacks, hoots, pants and gasps." A second *Pravda* attack targeted his ballet *The Limpid Stream*. Amid the Great Purge, friends and relatives (Marshal Tukhachevsky, Nikolai Zhilyayev, Boris Kornilov) were executed or imprisoned. Shostakovich’s income plummeted from ~12,000 to ~2,000 rubles monthly.

### The Fourth and Fifth Symphonies: Withdrawal and Rehabilitation
The *Pravda* attacks coincided with the composition of the **Fourth Symphony**, a Mahlerian, modernist work. Rehearsals began December 1936, but the Philharmonic manager persuaded Shostakovich to withdraw it; it premiered only in 1961. His response was the **Fifth Symphony** (1937), musically more conservative. Its November 21, 1937 premiere in Leningrad provoked tears and official acclaim as "A Soviet artist's creative response to just criticism." Critics who had recanted now praised his "return." He began teaching at the Leningrad Conservatory and composed his First String Quartet.

### World War II: The "War Trilogy" (Symphonies 7–9)
After the 1941 German invasion, Shostakovich remained in besieged Leningrad, serving as a fire warden and broadcasting radio addresses. He completed the **Seventh Symphony** in evacuation at Kuybyshev (Dec 1941). Premiered March 1942, it became a global symbol of resistance; the August 1942 Leningrad performance by a depleted orchestra (14 players reinforced by amateurs) is legendary. The **Eighth Symphony** (1943), tragic in tone, puzzled authorities expecting a victory celebration; Western critics dubbed it "Stalingrad Symphony." The **Ninth Symphony** (1945) was short and light—Gavriil Popov praised its *joie de vivre*, but official critics (Israel Nestyev) and *The New York World-Telegram* condemned it as a "childish" trivialization of victory. During the war he also wrote the Second Piano Trio (1944), with a Jewish-inspired finale dedicated to Sollertinsky.

### Second Denunciation and the "Desk Drawer" Period (1948–1953)
In 1948, the **Zhdanov Decree** denounced Shostakovich, Prokofiev, and Khachaturian for "formalism"—Western influence and "non-Russian" output. The Central Committee demanded "proletarian" music. Shostakovich was dismissed from the Conservatory, works banned, privileges withdrawn; he feared nightly arrest. He composed in three streams: film scores for income (e.g., *The Fall of Berlin*), official cantatas for rehabilitation (*Song of the Forests*, 1949, praising Stalin as "great gardener"), and serious "desk drawer" works: **Violin Concerto No. 1** and the song cycle **From Jewish Folk Poetry** (1948). The latter, written during a state anti-Semitic campaign, masked Jewish suffering with three "happy" songs; it remained unperformed until after Stalin’s death. In 1949, Stalin sent him to the Cultural and Scientific Congress for World Peace in New York, where he was forced to read a prepared speech and endorse the denunciation of Stravinsky—a humiliation he never forgave Nicolas Nabokov for exposing.

### Post-Stalin Rehabilitation and Party Membership (1953–1960)
Stalin’s death (1953) enabled the **Tenth Symphony**, widely heard as a musical portrait of Stalin (savage second movement) and incorporating the **DSCH motif** (D–E♭–C–B) and the Elmira motif (for pupil Elmira Nazirova). A flood of "desk drawer" premieres followed. Shostakovich had close, complex relationships with pupils Galina Ustvolskaya (proposed marriage after Nina’s 1954 death; she refused) and Nazirova. He married Margarita Kainova (1956–1961). In **1960**, under pressure to chair the RSFSR Union of Composers (a post requiring Party membership), he joined the Communist Party. His son Maxim recalled he wept and later said he was blackmailed; Lev Lebedinsky claimed he was suicidal. The **Eighth String Quartet**, composed in three days, quotes DSCH and past works; Shostakovich told Isaac Glikman: "if some day I die, nobody is likely to write a work in memory of me, so I had better write one myself."

### Late Period: Public Service and Private Mortality (1962–1975)
As a Supreme Soviet delegate (from 1962) and Composers’ Union chairman (1960–1968), Shostakovich signed official statements (including a 1973 denunciation of Andrei Sakharov, which his widow said was unauthorized) but privately aided constituents. His **Thirteenth Symphony** (*Babi Yar*, 1962), setting Yevgeny Yevtushenko’s poems on the Nazi massacre of Jews, provoked controversy; Yevtushenko was forced to add a stanza noting Russian/Ukrainian victims. In 1965, he co-signed protests for poet Joseph Brodsky, helping commute his sentence. Chronic illness dominated his final decade: a debilitating right-hand condition (diagnosed as poliomyelitis, possibly motor neurone disease) ended his piano playing (1958); heart attacks (1966, 1970, 1971) and broken legs (1967) followed. He quit smoking after 1966; lung cancer was diagnosed in 1973. Late works obsess over death: the **Fourteenth Symphony** (1969, song cycle for Britten), the **Fifteenth Symphony** (1971, quoting Wagner, Rossini, his own Fourth), and the final **Viola Sonata** (1975). He died August 9, 1975, in Moscow.

### Musical Language and Influences
Shostakovich’s style is broadly tonal but integrates atonality, chromaticism, and (in late quartets) tone rows used thematically, not serially. Hallmarks: sharp contrasts, grotesquerie, ambivalent tonality, neoclassical clarity, and Mahlerian orchestral scale. Key influences: **Bach** (fugues, passacaglias), **Beethoven** (late quartets), **Mahler** (symphonic architecture, emotional extremes), **Berg** (musical codes/quotations), **Mussorgsky** (Russian realism, satire—reorchestrated *Boris Godunov*, *Khovanshchina*), **Prokofiev** (early piano works), and **Stravinsky** (ambivalent: "composer I worship, thinker I despise"). The 1936 denunciation marked a caesura: early works (First Symphony, *The Nose*, *Lady Macbeth*, Fourth Symphony) are experimental; post-1936 works are more conservative, with chamber music (15 quartets) dominating late output. He developed **self-quotation** into a structural principle: the aria "Seryozha, khoroshiy moy" from *Lady Macbeth* reappears in the Eighth and Fourteenth Quartets; the DSCH motif permeates the Tenth Symphony, Eighth Quartet, and later works.

### Jewish Themes and Covert Resistance
From the 1940s, Shostakovich was drawn to Jewish music’s "ability to build a jolly melody on sad intonations." Works incorporating Jewish idioms: Fourth Quartet (1949), First Violin Concerto (1948), Piano Trio No. 2 (1944), *From Jewish Folk Poetry* (1948). After examining Moisei Beregovski’s 1944 thesis on Jewish folk music, he acquired a song collection; the cycle initially depicted Jewish suffering, then added three "Soviet" songs to disguise intent. The Union of Composers blocked it in 1949; it premiered only post-Stalin (1953). The **Thirteenth Symphony** (*Babi Yar*, 1962) set Yevtushenko’s poems on the 1941 Kiev massacre; though the poem was published, the symphony remained politically risky. The satirical cantata **"Rayok"** (1960s), mocking the anti-formalism campaign, was kept hidden until after his death.

### Posthumous Reputation and the *Testimony* Debate
Opinion on Shostakovich’s music remains polarized. William Walton called him "the greatest composer of the 20th century"; David Fanning (Grove) cites a "musical language of colossal emotional power" forged under conflicting pressures. Critics: Pierre Boulez dismissed it as "second... pressing of Mahler"; Philip Gershkovich called him "a hack in a trance"; Stravinsky found *Lady Macbeth* "brutally hammering... monotonous"; Robin Holloway described "battleship-grey melody... factory-functional structure." Gerard McBurney argues his eclecticism—borrowing from "low" popular music and avant-garde circles—was a deliberate technique for large-scale contrast and structure.

The central scholarly dispute concerns **dissidence vs. compliance**. The **revisionist** view (Solomon Volkov, *Testimony*, 1979) claims the memoir is Shostakovich’s dictated memoirs, revealing coded anti-government messages in the music (DSCH, quotations, *yurodivy*/holy fool role). Supported by his children Maxim and Galina (though Maxim initially said Volkov’s book was not his father’s work), and scholars Ian MacDonald, Elizabeth Wilson. **Orthodox** scholars (Laurel Fay, Richard Taruskin) contest *Testimony*’s authenticity: Fay showed the only signed pages reproduce earlier, non-controversial interviews; Ho and Feofanov counter that two signed pages contain controversial claims (e.g., on Meyerhold’s wife’s murder). The debate remains unresolved.

## Terms
- ****DSCH motif**** — Shostakovich’s musical monogram: D–E♭–C–B (in German notation: D, Es, C, H), used as a structural and autobiographical signature in works from the Tenth Symphony onward.
- ****Zhdanov Doctrine / Decree (1948)**** — Central Committee resolution condemning "formalism" in music (Western influence, "non-Russian" output), demanding "proletarian" art; led to bans, dismissals, and forced public apologies for Shostakovich, Prokofiev, Khachaturian, et al.
- ****Formalism**** — Soviet ideological term for music deemed excessively complex, abstract, or Western-influenced, prioritizing structure over accessible socialist content; the primary charge in both 1936 and 1948 denunciations.
- ****"Desk drawer" works**** — Compositions written during the 1948–53 suppression with no expectation of performance, kept private (e.g., Violin Concerto No. 1, *From Jewish Folk Poetry*, Fourth–Sixth String Quartets).
- ****Yurodivy (Holy Fool)**** — Russian tradition of a truth-teller who feigns madness to speak dangerous truths to power; Volkov argues Shostakovich adopted this role vis-à-vis the Soviet state.
- ****Testimony**** — 1979 book by Solomon Volkov presented as Shostakovich’s memoirs; central to the revisionist claim that his music encodes dissent. Authenticity fiercely debated.
- ****Babi Yar**** — Ravine near Kiev where Nazis massacred ~33,000 Jews in September 1941; subject of Yevtushenko’s 1961 poem and Shostakovich’s Thirteenth Symphony (1962).
- ****From Jewish Folk Poetry**** — 1948 song cycle setting texts from a Beregovski-edited collection; initially eight songs on Jewish suffering, three added to praise Soviet life; banned until 1953.
- ****Rayok**** — Satirical cantata (1960s) mocking the anti-formalism campaign and Soviet cultural bureaucracy; kept hidden until after Shostakovich’s death.
- ****Pravda editorial "Muddle Instead of Music" (28 Jan 1936)**** — Anonymous (Stalin-directed) attack on *Lady Macbeth of Mtsensk*, launching the first state denunciation and a nationwide campaign forcing critics to recant praise.

## Debates and open questions
1. **Authenticity of *Testimony***: Is Volkov’s book a genuine memoir dictated by Shostakovich, a compilation of recycled articles and gossip, or a fabrication? The signed pages reproduce earlier interviews, but two may contain new, controversial material.
2. **Coded dissent vs. survival strategy**: Do the DSCH motif, self-quotations, and Jewish themes constitute deliberate anti-government encoding (revisionist view), or are they personal/artistic signatures shaped by the necessity of outward conformity (orthodox view)?
3. **The Fifth Symphony’s meaning**: Is the finale’s "rejoicing" forced and hollow (as *Testimony* claims), or a genuine triumph of the Soviet artist over formalist errors (official 1937 interpretation)?
4. **Shostakovich’s Party membership (1960)**: Was it a pragmatic acceptance of reduced repression under Khrushchev, coercion/blackmail (per Maxim Shostakovich and Lebedinsky), or a genuine political commitment?
5. **The Thirteenth Symphony’s risk**: Since Yevtushenko’s *Babi Yar* poem was legally published, was setting it an act of courage or a calculatedly safe gesture? The forced addition of a stanza on Russian/Ukrainian victims suggests official unease.
6. **Late style and tone rows**: In the final quartets and Fourteenth Symphony, Shostakovich uses 12-tone rows thematically, not serially. Does this reflect a late modernist resurgence, a private language for "desk drawer" expression, or a continuation of Bergian quotation technique?
7. **Role in Sakharov denunciation (1973)**: Did Shostakovich willingly sign, was his name added without consent (per his widow), or did he sign under duress to retain ability to help others?
