# Upton Sinclair

## Overview

Upton Beall Sinclair Jr. (1878–1968) was an American author, muckraker journalist, and political activist, best known for his 1906 novel *The Jungle*, which exposed labor and sanitary conditions in the U.S. meatpacking industry and helped spur the 1906 Pure Food and Drug Act and Meat Inspection Act. A prolific writer of nearly 100 books, he won the 1943 Pulitzer Prize for Fiction and ran as the Democratic nominee for governor of California in 1934. His work documented the industrialized United States from both working-class and industrialist perspectives, and he was a lifelong socialist advocate.

## Body

### Early life and education

Sinclair was born to a liquor salesman father whose alcoholism shadowed his childhood, and a strict Episcopalian mother who disliked alcohol, tea, and coffee. His parents were of British ancestry, with Scottish paternal grandparents. His mother's family was affluent, and Sinclair often stayed with wealthy maternal grandparents, giving him insight into how both rich and poor lived in the late 19th century. His father's family was financially ruined by the Civil War, the end of slavery, and an extended agricultural depression.

Sinclair developed a love for reading at age five and read every book his mother owned. He did not start school until age 10 and was deficient in math, working hard to catch up. He wrote jokes, dime novels, and magazine articles to pay tuition at City College of New York. At Columbia University, he learned Spanish, German, and French, and wrote up to 8,000 words of pulp fiction per day using stenographers. He left without a degree and wrote four commercially unsuccessful but critically well-received books between 1901 and 1904. He became estranged from his mother around age 16, staying away from her for 35 years.

### The Jungle and early career

Sinclair considered himself a poet and dedicated time to writing poetry. In 1904, he spent seven weeks undercover in Chicago's meatpacking plants to research *The Jungle*, published in 1906. The novel featured Jurgis Rudkus, a Lithuanian immigrant worker, and his family, portraying their mistreatment by employers. Jack London called it "the Uncle Tom's Cabin of wage slavery." Domestic and foreign purchases of American meat fell by half. Sinclair wrote: "I aimed at the public's heart, and by accident I hit it in the stomach." President Theodore Roosevelt called Sinclair a "crackpot" but agreed with some conclusions, saying "Radical action must be taken to do away with the efforts of arrogant and selfish greed on the part of the capitalist," though he opposed legislation he considered "socialist."

With the income from *The Jungle*, Sinclair founded the utopian—but non-Jewish white only—Helicon Home Colony in Englewood, New Jersey, which burned down under suspicious circumstances within a year. In 1913–1914, he made three trips to Colorado coal fields, leading to *King Coal*, which confronted John D. Rockefeller Jr. and his role in the 1914 Ludlow Massacre. He helped organize demonstrations against Rockefeller in New York City.

### Political career

Sinclair's first campaign for office was in 1906 for Congress, polling 3% of the vote. He broke with the Socialist Party in 1917 to support World War I but returned by the 1920s. He founded the Southern California chapter of the American Civil Liberties Union in 1923, the same year he was arrested for speaking at a rally supporting free speech rights of Industrial Workers of the World during the San Pedro Maritime Strike; the arresting officer proclaimed: "We'll have none of that Constitution stuff."

In 1934, Sinclair ran for California governor as a Democrat under the End Poverty in California (EPIC) platform. He gained 879,000 votes but lost to incumbent Frank Merriam, who gained 1,138,000 votes. Hollywood studio bosses unanimously opposed him, pressuring employees and making false propaganda films. Sinclair stated there was a "campaign of lying" against him "ordered by the biggest businessmen in California and paid for with millions of dollars." The Socialist Party expelled him for joining the Democratic Party, which destroyed the Socialist Party in California. Communists also disassociated from him, considering him a capitalist. After his loss, Sinclair abandoned EPIC and returned to writing, publishing *I, Candidate for Governor: And How I Got Licked* in 1935. He remarked in 1951: "The American People will take Socialism, but they won't take the label."

### Personal life

Sinclair married Meta Fuller in 1900; their son David was born in 1901. Despite his opposition to sex outside marriage and view of sex as necessary only for reproduction, Sinclair had a love affair with Anna Noyes. Meta later had a love affair with John Armistead Collier. In 1911, Meta left Sinclair for poet Harry Kemp. An Amsterdam court annulled the marriage in 1912 on grounds of Meta's adultery. Sinclair married Mary Craig Kimbrough in 1913; they remained married until her death in 1961. He married Mary Elizabeth Willis that same year and died in 1968. His cousin was Wallis Simpson, whose romance with King Edward VIII led to his abdication in 1936; Sinclair wrote in support of their marriage.

### Major writings

*The Brass Check* (1919) was a systematic critique of the "free press" in the United States, covering yellow journalism techniques created by William Randolph Hearst. Sinclair called it "the most important and most dangerous book I have ever written." Four years after its publication, the first code of ethics for journalists was created.

The Sylvia novels (1913, 1914) were collaborations with Mary Craig, published under Upton's name. The Lanny Budd series (1940–1953) comprised 11 novels featuring a central character who mingles with world leaders; the third book, *Dragon's Teeth*, won the 1943 Pulitzer Prize. The series was published in translation in 21 countries.

Sinclair was also interested in health and nutrition, experimenting with diets and fasting, which he wrote about in *The Fasting Cure* (1911). He favored a raw food diet and was for long periods a complete vegetarian.

## Terms

- **Muckraker**: A journalist who exposes corruption and misconduct, especially in business and politics
- **The Jungle**: Sinclair's 1906 novel exposing conditions in Chicago's meatpacking industry
- **EPIC**: End Poverty in California, Sinclair's 1934 gubernatorial platform
- **Intercollegiate Socialist Society**: Organization Sinclair called for in 1905
- **Ludlow Massacre**: 1914 event in Colorado coal fields that Sinclair confronted in *King Coal*
- **Yellow journalism**: Sensationalist reporting techniques, associated with William Randolph Hearst
- **Helicon Home Colony**: Utopian colony Sinclair founded in New Jersey, which burned down
- **The Brass Check**: Sinclair's 1919 exposé of American journalism malpractice
- **Lanny Budd**: Central character of Sinclair's 11-novel series (1940–1953)

## Debates and open questions

Sinclair's legacy includes scholarly debate about his political identity: he is considered an early American democratic socialist in the 21st century, though he was expelled from the Socialist Party in 1934 for joining the Democrats. His 1934 campaign remains controversial for the propaganda tactics used against him, which he described as a "campaign of lying" ordered by businessmen. President Theodore Roosevelt's characterization of Sinclair as a "crackpot" who was "hysterical, unbalanced, and untruthful" contrasts with Roosevelt's agreement with some of Sinclair's conclusions about capitalist greed. The suspicious burning of Helicon Home Colony remains unexplained.
