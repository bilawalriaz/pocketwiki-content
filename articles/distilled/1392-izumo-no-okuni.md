# Izumo no Okuni

## Overview
Izumo no Okuni (c. 1578 – c. 1613) was a Japanese shrine maiden (*miko*) and entertainer credited with founding *kabuki*, a major theatrical art form whose name literally means "the art of singing and dancing." Around 1603, she began performing innovative, often sexually suggestive dances on the dry riverbed of the Kamo River in Kyoto, recruiting lower-class women and outcasts to form an all-female troupe. Her performances blended sacred *nembutsu odori* (a Buddhist invocation dance) with secular skits depicting lovers and prostitutes, creating a sensation that spawned widespread imitation. Okuni retired circa 1610 and disappeared; in 1629, the Tokugawa shogunate banned women from the stage citing moral corruption, transforming kabuki into the all-male tradition that persists today.

- **1603** — Begins performing on the dry riverbed of the Kamo River (Shijōgawara) and at Kitano Shrine; forms all-female troupe from female outcasts and former prostitutes. Performances dubbed "kabuki" for their eccentricity.

- **c. 1610** — Retires and disappears from historical record.
- **1629** — Shōgun Tokugawa Iemitsu bans women from kabuki (*onnagata* ban) due to moral outcry and audience fights; young men replace women, later banned in turn, leading to the all-male *yarō-kabuki* tradition.
- **c. 1613 / 1640** — Estimated death date (sources conflict).

## Body

### Early Life and Shrine Service
Okuni was born circa 1578 in the vicinity of the Izumo Grand Shrine, where her father, Nakamura Sanemon, worked as a blacksmith and other family members served. She became a *miko* (shrine maiden), a role involving ritual dance and spiritual service, and gained renown for her skill in dancing and acting as well as her beauty. As was customary for shrines seeking contributions, she was dispatched to Kyoto to perform sacred dances and songs. In Kyoto, she also performed *nembutsu odori* (nembutsu dance), a folk dance originating with the 10th-century Pure Land Buddhist evangelist Kūya. By Okuni’s time, the dance had become largely secular; her adaptation was noted for its sultriness and sexual innuendo. She further attracted crowds with humorous skits depicting lovers' trysts and encounters between men and prostitutes. Defying an order to return to the shrine, she remained in Kyoto, though she continued sending money back.

### Founding of Kabuki
Around 1603, Okuni began performing on the Shijōgawara (Fourth Street Dry Riverbed) of the Kamo River and at Kitano Shrine, including performances for the Imperial court. She gathered female outcasts—particularly those involved in prostitution—teaching them acting, dancing, and singing to form an exclusively female troupe. The term *kabuki* derives from *kabuku* ("to lean" or "deviate from the norm") and *mono* ("people"), originally describing oddly dressed, swaggering individuals called *kabukimono*; an alternative etymology suggests *katamuki* ("slanted" or "strongly inclined"). Observers applied the label to Okuni’s troupe due to their social daring and eccentricity. Early kabuki consisted of dance and song without significant plot, criticized as overly sexual and cacophonous but praised as colorful and beautiful. Because the troupe was all-female, actresses played both male and female roles. The style—also called *onnagabuki* (women's kabuki) or *shibai*—was quickly imitated by brothels seeking to entertain wealthy clients and market prostitutes with performance skills.

### Later Years and the Ban on Women
One of Okuni’s signature pieces featured the romantic character Nagoya Sansaburō, a real samurai who died in 1603; in the drama, her voice lures his spirit back to dance with her. Historical speculation regarding a real-life romantic link remains unresolved. Okuni retired around 1610 and vanished; her death date is debated (c. 1610, 1613, or 1640). In 1629, citing moral reform and violent disputes among male spectators vying for actresses' attention, Shōgun Tokugawa Iemitsu forbade women from performing kabuki. They were replaced by young men (*wakashū*), but this too was banned due to similar associations with sex work and "corruption of morals," eventually restricting the stage to adult men (*yarō-kabuki*), the standard in official theatres today.

### Cultural Impact and Legacy
Beyond founding kabuki, Okuni introduced the forerunner of the *hanamichi* ("path of flowers"), a runway extending from the rear of the theatre through the audience to the stage, a feature adopted by multiple Japanese theatre arts. Her influence extends to modern musical theatre. A fictionalized biography by Ariyoshi Sawako was serialized in *Fujin Kōron* (1967–1969). A commemorative statue stands in Kyoto near the Kamo River, close to the Minami-za kabuki theatre.

## Terms
- ****Miko**** — Shrine maiden; a female attendant at a Shinto shrine who performs ritual dances and assists with ceremonies.
- ****Kabuki**** — Classical Japanese dance-drama; the name derives from *kabuku* ("to lean/deviate"), implying "avant-garde" or "eccentric" performance.
- ****Nembutsu odori**** — "Nembutsu dance"; a folk dance invoking the name of Amitābha Buddha, originated by 10th-century monk Kūya, later secularized.
- ****Onnagabuki**** — "Women's kabuki"; the early form of kabuki performed by all-female troupes (also called *shibai*).
- ****Kabukimono**** — "Eccentric people"; flamboyantly dressed, swaggering urban youths of the late 16th/early 17th century who gave the art form its name.
- ****Hanamichi**** — "Path of flowers"; a raised runway passing through the audience to the stage, used for entrances, exits, and dramatic moments.
- ****Yarō-kabuki**** — "Men's kabuki"; the all-male form established after bans on women (1629) and young men (1652), standard in modern kabuki.
- ****Shōgun Tokugawa Iemitsu**** — Third Tokugawa shōgun (r. 1623–1651) who issued the 1629 edict banning women from the kabuki stage.
- ****Izumo Grand Shrine**** — One of Japan's oldest and most important Shinto shrines, located in Izumo Province; Okuni's childhood home and workplace.
- ****Nagoya Sansaburō**** — Samurai (d. 1603) dramatized in Okuni's popular "spirit summoning" dance; historical relationship to Okuni uncertain.

## Debates and open questions
- **Exact birth and death dates:** Sources cite birth c. 1578; death variously c. 1610, 1613, or 1640.
- **Relationship with Nagoya Sansaburō:** Uncertain whether they were lovers or if he was solely a theatrical device.
- **Etymology of "kabuki":** Competing theories derive it from *kabukimono* (eccentric persons) or *katamuki* (slanted/inclined).
- **Reasons for retirement/disappearance:** No historical record explains why she vanished c. 1610.
