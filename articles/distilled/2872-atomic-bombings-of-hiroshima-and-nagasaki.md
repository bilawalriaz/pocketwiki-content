# Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki

## Overview
The atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945 were the first and only use of nuclear weapons in warfare. Conducted by the United States during the final days of World War II, the bombings killed an estimated 150,000 to 246,000 people, mostly civilians. They remain a pivotal event, marking the dawn of the nuclear age and sparking enduring debates about military necessity, ethics, and geopolitics.

## Timeline
- **1938** — Discovery of nuclear fission makes an atomic bomb theoretically possible.
- **1939** — Einstein–Szilard letter prompts preliminary U.S. research.
- **Late 1941** — British MAUD Report indicates a bomb is feasible with less uranium than thought, accelerating U.S. work.
- **1942** — Manhattan Project established under U.S. Army Corps of Engineers.
- **1945** — U.S. firebombing campaign devastates 64 Japanese cities, including Tokyo.
- **16 July 1945** — Trinity Test successfully detonates the first atomic bomb.
- **26 July 1945** — Potsdam Declaration demands Japan's unconditional surrender.
- **6 August 1945** — "Little Boy" (uranium bomb) is dropped on Hiroshima.
- **9 August 1945** — "Fat Man" (plutonium bomb) is dropped on Nagasaki.
- **15 August 1945** — Japan announces its surrender.
- **2 September 1945** — Japan signs the instrument of surrender, ending World War II.

## Body

### Background and Development
The Manhattan Project, a secret U.S. program, was initiated by fears of a German atomic bomb. It cost over $2 billion and employed 125,000 people. Two bomb types were developed: the gun-type "Little Boy" (uranium-235) and the implosion-type "Fat Man" (plutonium-239). By mid-1945, Japan's military and civilian economy were collapsing under a relentless conventional bombing campaign and naval blockade.

### Preparations and Target Selection
A Target Committee selected cities largely untouched by previous raids to assess the bomb's effects. Criteria included military significance and a size that would demonstrate destructive power. Kyoto was removed from the list by Secretary of War Henry Stimson due to its cultural importance. The 509th Composite Group was trained and equipped with specialized B-29 bombers for the mission. A proposed non-combat demonstration was rejected by officials who feared it would fail to compel surrender and would waste a scarce weapon.

### The Bombings and Immediate Aftermath
On 6 August 1945, the B-29 *Enola Gay* dropped "Little Boy" on Hiroshima, detonating at 580 meters with a yield of 15–16 kilotons. The blast and resulting firestorm destroyed 12 km² of the city. On 9 August, "Fat Man" was dropped on Nagasaki after the primary target, Kokura, was obscured by clouds. Its 21-kiloton yield was confined by hills to the Urakami Valley. In both cities, survivors faced immediate blast and fire injuries, followed by acute radiation sickness. Over 90% of Hiroshima's doctors and nurses were killed or injured.

### Post-Attack and Surrender
Initial Japanese reports were confused. The government's scientific advisors confirmed the use of a nuclear weapon. On 9 August, the Soviet Union invaded Manchuria. After intense debate, Emperor Hirohito intervened to accept the Allied terms of surrender, citing the "new and most cruel bomb" as a primary reason. Japan announced its surrender on 15 August.

### Long-Term Effects and Legacy
The bombings caused long-term health effects, including increased rates of leukemia and other cancers among survivors (*hibakusha*). Studies found no significant increase in birth defects among children of survivors, but a higher risk of severe mental retardation for those exposed in utero between 8–26 weeks gestation. The bombings ushered in the nuclear age, leading to a Cold War arms race. They sparked enduring debates over their military necessity, ethical justification, and role in ending the war versus intimidating the Soviet Union.

## Terms
*   **Hibakusha**: Japanese term for "explosion-affected people," referring to survivors of the atomic bombings.
*   **Manhattan Project**: The secret U.S. research and development project during WWII that produced the first atomic bombs.
*   **Little Boy**: The gun-type fission weapon using uranium-235, dropped on Hiroshima.
*   **Fat Man**: The implosion-type nuclear weapon using plutonium-239, dropped on Nagasaki.
*   **Kiloton**: A unit of explosive energy equivalent to 1,000 tons of TNT.
*   **Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS)**: Illness caused by exposure to high levels of ionizing radiation over a short period.
*   **Potsdam Declaration**: The July 1945 ultimatum demanding Japan's unconditional surrender.
*   **Operation Downfall**: The planned Allied invasion of Japan, which was canceled after Japan's surrender.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Necessity**: Supporters argue the bombings saved more lives than a costly invasion would have. Critics contend Japan was near surrender and that the bombings were unnecessary.
*   **Ethics and Legality**: Some view the bombings as a war crime or state terrorism due to the massive civilian casualties. A 1963 Japanese court ruling called them illegal under international law of the time.
*   **Geopolitical Motives**: A prominent theory suggests the bombings were intended to intimidate the Soviet Union at the start of the Cold War.
*   **Role of Soviet Entry**: Historian Tsuyoshi Hasegawa argues the Soviet declaration of war on 9 August was a more decisive factor in Japan's surrender than the atomic bombs.
