# Peter Kropotkin

## Overview
Pyotr Kropotkin (1842–1921) was a Russian prince turned anarchist theorist and geographer who became the leading proponent of **anarchist communism**—a stateless, decentralized society based on voluntary cooperation and mutual aid rather than central authority or wage labor. His scientific work on Siberian geography and evolutionary biology provided empirical grounding for his political theory, arguing that cooperation, not competition, is the primary driver of evolutionary success. Kropotkin’s accessible synthesis of anarchist thought influenced revolutionaries globally, though his support for the Allies in World War I fractured the movement and his return to revolutionary Russia ended in disillusionment with Bolshevik authoritarianism.

## Timeline
- **9 Dec 1842** — Born in Moscow to aristocratic family; mother dies when he is three.
- **1857–1862** — Attends elite Page Corps in St. Petersburg; serves as Page de Chambre to Tsar Alexander II; develops revolutionary views.
- **1862–1867** — Serves as officer in Siberia; leads geological expeditions (Manchuria, East Siberian Mountains); discovers Patom/Vitim Plateaus; introduced to anarchism via Proudhon by exiled prisoner Mikhailov.
- **1871–1872** — Inspired by Paris Commune; travels to Switzerland, joins Jura Federation (Bakuninist), converts to anarchism.
- **1873–1874** — Joins Chaikovsky Circle in St. Petersburg; writes first political memo on stateless reconstruction; arrested by Third Section (March 1874).
- **June 1876** — Escapes from St. Petersburg military hospital; flees to Western Europe via Scandinavia/England.
- **1877–1881** — Edits *Le Révolté* in Geneva; marries Sofia Ananieva-Rabinovich (1878); participates in London Congress (1881), birth of "Black International"; expelled from Switzerland (Aug 1881).
- **1882–1886** — Arrested in France (Dec 1882); tried in **Trial of the 66** (Jan 1883); sentenced to 5 years under Dufaure Law; imprisoned at Clairvaux; released 1886 due to ill health.
- **1886–1917** — Settles in London; co-founds *Freedom* newspaper; publishes major works: *The Conquest of Bread* (1892), *Fields, Factories, and Workshops* (1899), *Mutual Aid* (1902); supports Allies in WWI (1914), splitting anarchist movement.
- **June 1917** — Returns to Russia after February Revolution; refuses Provisional Government cabinet post; meets Lenin; criticizes Bolshevik centralization and terror.
- **8 Feb 1921** — Dies of pneumonia in Dmitrov; Moscow funeral is last major legal anarchist demonstration in Soviet Russia.

## Body

### Early Life and Scientific Career
Born into the Rurikid nobility, Kropotkin rejected his class privilege early. At the Page Corps, he developed a "populist position" on serf emancipation and began underground writings. Seeking independence and scientific purpose, he chose a difficult posting with the Amur Cossacks in Siberia (1862–1867). There, he conducted major geographical expeditions, proving the Siberian interior was a **plateau system** (Patom and Vitim Plateaus) rather than a plain—a discovery that won a Russian Geographical Society gold medal and enabled the Lena gold fields' development. He also developed theories on the **Ice Age glaciation of Europe**. Simultaneously, his administrative work under Governor-General Kukel (prison reform, city self-governance) and witnessing the brutal suppression of Polish exiles convinced him that **state reform was ineffectual** and that peasant communal organization was superior. Exiled poet Mikhail Larionovitch Mikhailov introduced him to Pierre-Joseph Proudhon’s mutualist anarchism during this period.

### Turn to Anarchism and Imprisonment
The 1871 Paris Commune radicalized Kropotkin. A 1872 trip to Switzerland brought him into the **Jura Federation**, the Bakuninist anti-authoritarian wing of the First International. Their "egalitarianism and independence of expression" instantly converted him. Returning to Russia, he joined the **Chaikovsky Circle**, advocating a revolutionary program centered on **federated agrarian communes** and urban workers, rejecting the conspiratorial model of Sergey Nechayev. His 1873 memo outlined a stateless society: common property, worker control, shared labor, and labor vouchers. Arrested in March 1874 as a former Page de Chambre, his case was a scandal. Held in the Peter and Paul Fortress, he was allowed to finish his glaciation report. With his sister's help, he escaped from a military hospital in June 1876, fleeing to England and then Switzerland.

### Exile: Switzerland, France, and the "Black International"
In Geneva (1877–1881), Kropotkin edited the Jura Federation’s paper and launched *Le Révolté* (1879), becoming the foremost voice for **anarchist communism**—distribution based on need, not labor. He married Ukrainian student Sofia Ananieva-Rabinovich (1878). At the 1881 **London Congress**, he played a central role in reconstituting the International Workingmen's Association (later termed the "Black International" by historians), though he clashed with Errico Malatesta over "propaganda of the deed" and the International's secrecy. Days later, Switzerland expelled him for an article in *Le Révolté* praising propaganda of the deed (authorship debated by historians).

In France (1881–1886), rising anarchist militancy (boss shootings, the Montceau-les-Mines riots, the Assommoir bombing) triggered state repression using the **Dufaure Law** (banning the International). Kropotkin was arrested in December 1882 and tried in the **Trial of the 66** (January 1883). Despite a vigorous defense, he received the maximum 5-year sentence. At Clairvaux prison, he continued scientific work; Élisée Reclus published *Words of a Rebel* (1885), a collection of his *Le Révolté* writings. A public campaign by intellectuals and legislators secured his early release in 1886.

### London Exile and Mature Works
Settling in Harrow, London (1886–1917), Kropotkin co-founded *Freedom* (1886), the first English anarchist periodical. He entered British scientific circles (British Association for the Advancement of Science, *Nature*, *Geographical Journal*) and intellectual salons (William Morris, W.B. Yeats). His revolutionary zeal softened into scholarly synthesis post-1890. Major works emerged from journal articles:
*   **The Conquest of Bread (1892):** Blueprint for a revolutionary transition to anarchist communism; immediate abolition of money/wages; free distribution from social product.
*   **Fields, Factories, and Workshops (1899):** Argument for **decentralized production** integrating industry and agriculture; advocacy for national **self-sufficiency** via irrigation/greenhouses.
*   **Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution (1902):** His principal scientific work. Countering **social Darwinism** (e.g., Francis Galton), he documented cooperation as the dominant survival strategy across species, including humans. Biologist Stephen Jay Gould later validated his observations as consistent with modern biology.
*   **The Great French Revolution (1909), Modern Science and Anarchism (1913):** Historical and philosophical syntheses.
He toured Canada (1897) and the US (1897, 1901), aiding Doukhobor emigration and meeting Emma Goldman, Johann Most, and Benjamin Tucker.

### World War I and Return to Russia
Kropotkin’s 1914 **support for the Allies** (Britain/France) against Germany shattered the anti-war anarchist consensus, damaging his standing. He urged Russians to support the war effort. Returning after the February 1917 Revolution, he refused a cabinet seat but advocated defending the revolution. Lenin personally approved his Moscow residence (1918). In Dmitrov, he met Lenin, corresponded on policy, and advocated for **workers' cooperatives** and against **Bolshevik centralization, hostage-taking, and terror**. He simultaneously urged Western anarchists to oppose Allied intervention. His influence on the Revolution was minimal, but his prison advocacy restored some goodwill lost over the war. He died in 1921; his Moscow funeral was the last legal mass anarchist gathering in Soviet Russia.

### Philosophy: Core Pillars

#### Critique of Capitalism and State Socialism
Kropotkin rejected both **capitalism** and **state socialism (Marxism/Blanquism)**. Against capitalism, he attacked the **power of employers** enabled by state protection of private property, not just surplus value extraction. He dismissed the **labor theory of value**, seeing no necessary link between work and commodity value. Against state socialism, he argued the **state is structurally rooted in class domination** and cannot be used for emancipation. A "revolutionary government" is a contradiction: it lacks local knowledge, suppresses popular initiative, recreates classes, and requires violence to maintain order. **Private property and the state must be abolished simultaneously.** Post-revolution, order arises from the "free initiative of the people" and "boldness of thought," not guillotines.

#### Mutual Aid and Cooperation
In *Mutual Aid* (1902), Kropotkin argued **cooperation (mutual aid)** is the primary evolutionary factor for species success, countering the "social Darwinist" emphasis on competition. He did not deny competition ("mutual struggle") but showed sociable species are "most numerous, prosperous, and open to progress." In humans, mutual aid is instinctual; the state and capitalism suppress it. **Anarchist communism** restores this: a **gift economy** where a developed society produces enough for all to take freely according to need. He criticized Mikhail Bakunin’s **collectivism** (labor notes/wages) as a wage system by another name that recreates inequality and authority, since measuring individual contribution is impossible and empowers the measurer.

#### Ethics and Religion
Kropotkin grounded anarchism in a naturalistic ethics of **solidarity and forgiveness**. He traced anarchist roots to Christian heretics (Anabaptists, Hussites) and admired Jesus and Buddha as ethical teachers. Christianity’s innovation was **forgiveness/rejection of revenge**, replacing vengeful pagan gods with a God of universal love. His unfinished *Ethics* argued morality evolves from social instincts.

## Terms
- ****Anarchist Communism**** — Kropotkin’s vision: a stateless, classless society where production is distributed freely according to need ("from each according to ability, to each according to need"), organized via voluntary federation of communes and worker associations.
- ****Mutual Aid**** — The evolutionary and social principle that cooperation within a species is the primary driver of survival and progress, countering the "social Darwinist" focus on competition.
- ****Propaganda of the Deed**** — Insurrectionary acts (assassinations, bombings) intended to ignite mass revolt; Kropotkin was ambiguously linked to this via a controversial 1881 article.
- ****Collectivism (Bakuninist)**** — Anarchist economic model retaining labor remuneration (labor notes); Kropotkin rejected it as a disguised wage system enabling inequality and authority.
- ****Dufaure Law**** — French law (1872) banning the International Workingmen's Association; used to prosecute Kropotkin and 65 others in the 1883 Trial of the 66.
- ****Black International**** — Historians' term for the revolutionary International reconstituted at the 1881 London Congress, oriented toward clandestine organization and propaganda of the deed.
- ****Social Darwinism**** — Late 19th-century application of "survival of the fittest" to human society, justifying hierarchy and competition; Kropotkin's *Mutual Aid* was a direct scientific rebuttal.
- ****Chaikovsky Circle**** — St. Petersburg populist revolutionary group (early 1870s) focused on propaganda among workers/students; Kropotkin joined but clashed over strategy.
- ****Fields, Factories, and Workshops**** — Kropotkin’s 1899 work arguing for decentralized, localized production integrating industry and agriculture to achieve regional self-sufficiency.
- ****The Conquest of Bread**** — Kropotkin’s 1892 seminal text detailing the practical organization of an anarchist communist revolution: expropriation, free distribution, abolition of money.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Authorship of "The Action" (1881):** Historians dispute whether Kropotkin wrote the *Le Révolté* article praising propaganda of the deed that caused his Swiss expulsion. Caroline Cahm argues he did not; Martin A. Miller, Jean Maitron, and Max Nettlau’s edition attribute it to him.
*   **WWI Stance:** His "Manifesto of the Sixteen" support for the Allies remains the sharpest controversy of his life, viewed by contemporaries (Malatesta, Goldman, Berkman) as a betrayal of anti-militarist principle, though Kropotkin framed it as defense against Prussian militarism.
*   **Scientific Validity of *Mutual Aid*:** While Stephen Jay Gould endorsed Kropotkin’s observations as consistent with modern biology (cooperation enhances fitness), the extent to which mutual aid *dominates* over competition in evolution remains a topic of discussion in evolutionary biology.
*   **Bolshevik Engagement:** Debate persists on whether Kropotkin’s 1917–1921 critiques of Lenin (letters on terror, centralization, bureaucracy) represented a consistent anarchist application or a tragic failure to break publicly with the Revolution he had awaited.
*   **Role in London Congress (1881):** The degree of Kropotkin’s opposition vs. complicity in the turn toward a secretive, insurrectionary "Black International" is interpreted differently by historians (Cahm vs. Miller).
