# Baal Shem Tov

## Overview  
Israel ben Eliezer (c. 1700–1760), known as the Baal Shem Tov ("Master of the Good Name"), was a Jewish mystic and healer regarded as the founder of Hasidic Judaism. His central teaching emphasized *devekut*—a direct, continuous connection with the divine infused in all human activity—alongside the mystical significance of Hebrew letters and the supreme importance of prayer. Though he left no written works, his sayings were preserved by disciples and became foundational to Hasidic thought.

## Timeline  
- **c. 1700** — Birth of Israel ben Eliezer; birthplace uncertain—possibly in the Principality of Moldavia (modern Bukovina), though some legends cite Okopy
- **1711** — Joins the *ḥaburat Machane Yisroel*, a mystical group led by Adam Baal Shem (at age 13/14, per legendary accounts)
- **1716** — Marries; his first wife soon dies, and he travels throughout Eastern Galicia
- **~1740** — Settles in Medzhybizh and begins publicly teaching his doctrines
- **1740–1760** — Resides in Medzhybizh; recorded in Polish censuses as a "baal shem" and healer
- **1760** — Last known census appearance; dies around this time in Medzhybizh

## Body  

### Biography and Early Life  
Born to elderly parents, Israel ben Eliezer was raised in poverty. Orphaned at age three, he was adopted by the Jewish community of Tluste. As a youth, he encountered Kabbalah through the secretive *ḥaburat Machane Yisroel*. His early career included roles as a teacher, ritual slaughterer, and tavern keeper before emerging as a *baal shem*—a practitioner believed to perform miracles using divine names.

### Teachings and Doctrine  
The Baal Shem Tov taught that true worship lies in cleaving to God (*devekut*) through every action and moment. He emphasized personal spiritual intuition over scholastic study alone, asserting that the ideal human being reveals divine unity. His doctrine positioned individuals as intermediaries between the Creator and creation, capable of channeling divine mercy through faith. This worldview aligned with panentheistic interpretations of Judaism, where God is both transcendent and immanent.

### Legacy and Influence  
Leaving no books, the Baal Shem Tov’s teachings survive primarily through the records of his disciples, especially Jacob Joseph of Polonne. After his death, Hasidism fragmented into competing factions, complicating efforts to authenticate his exact words. Nonetheless, his emphasis on joy, prayer, and direct divine experience shaped Hasidic spirituality. His students founded major Hasidic dynasties, including those of Dov Ber of Mezeritch and Nachman of Chernobyl.

### Legends and Historical Record  
Contemporary sources are sparse; most accounts come from later hagiographical collections like *Shivḥei haBesht*. These include fantastical tales of miracles, visions, and divine interventions. While scholars debate their historicity, they reflect the evolving mythos surrounding the movement’s founder. Some letters attributed to him, including one describing prophetic visions, remain disputed in authenticity.

### Later Activities and Historical Stance  
The Baal Shem Tov took sides with Talmudists against Frankism, a Sabbataeanist movement. After the mass conversion of Frankists to Christianity, he allegedly said that as long as a diseased limb remains connected to the body, there is hope for its recovery; but once severed, it is lost. It is alleged that he died out of grief over the Frankists leaving Judaism.

## Terms  
- **Baal Shem Tov**: Hebrew title meaning "Master of the Good Name"; refers to a mystical practitioner believed to wield divine power.  
- **Devekut**: A Hebrew term denoting clinging or cleaving to God, central to Hasidic spirituality.  
- **Hasidic Judaism**: A mystically inclined branch of Orthodox Judaism emphasizing joy, prayer, and devekut, founded by the Baal Shem Tov.  
- **Kabbalah**: Jewish mystical tradition exploring the nature of God, the universe, and divine emanations.  
- **Panentheism**: Belief that God is both immanent within the world and transcendent beyond it.  
- **Tzadik**: A righteous person, often a spiritual leader in Hasidic communities believed to mediate divine blessings.  
- **Ḥaburat Machane Yisroel**: A clandestine group of mystics led by Adam Baal Shem, which initiated the young Israel ben Eliezer into Kabbalah.  
- **Shivḥei haBesht**: A collection of legends about the Baal Shem Tov, compiled in 1815 and considered a primary source for his biography.  
- **Shedim**: Evil spirits or demons in Jewish folklore, sometimes said to be exorcised by baalei shem.  
- **Melamed**: A teacher, typically of young children in traditional Jewish education (*ḥeder*).

## Debates and Open Questions  
Scholars debate the authenticity of posthumously published letters attributed to the Baal Shem Tov, particularly those claiming prophetic abilities or heavenly ascents. The precise location of his birth remains contested—some sources cite Okopy in Ukraine, while historian Moshe Idel argues for Bukovina based on linguistic and geographical analysis. The reliability of *Shivḥei haBesht* as a historical source is questioned due to its legendary content, though it likely preserves a small kernel of factual memory. Additionally, the extent to which his teachings were systematized by later followers versus accurately transmitted remains unclear, given the absence of original texts.
