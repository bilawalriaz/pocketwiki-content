# Pyu city-states

## Overview
The Pyu city-states were Tibeto-Burman-speaking urban centers in Upper Myanmar from the 2nd century BCE to the mid-11th century CE. They represent Burma's earliest recorded civilization, establishing a millennium-long urban tradition linking the Bronze Age to the Pagan Kingdom. Their culture, heavily influenced by Indian trade and Buddhism, laid foundations for later Burmese script, calendar, and architecture.

## Timeline
- **c. 2nd century BCE** — Pyu people migrate into the Irrawaddy River Valley from Yunnan.
- **c. 200 BCE–100 CE** — Beikthano, the oldest excavated Pyu city, is inhabited.
- **1st century CE** — Halin is founded, becoming the largest Pyu city until the 7th/8th century.
- **80 CE** — The Sakra Era calendar is adopted in the Pyu realm.
- **3rd–6th centuries CE** — Earliest evidence of Pyu writing appears.
- **5th–7th centuries CE** — Sri Ksetra is founded; it later supersedes Halin as the premier center.
- **22 March 638 CE** — The Vikrama Dynasty at Sri Ksetra launches the calendar that becomes the Burmese calendar.
- **754/760 CE** — The Nanzhao Kingdom begins raids on Upper Burma.
- **832 CE** — Nanzhao warriors sack Halin, taking 3,000 prisoners.
- **Late 10th century** — Burmans overtake leadership of the Pyu realm.
- **Mid-11th century** — The Pagan Kingdom unifies the Irrawaddy valley, incorporating Pyu territories and cultural legacies.
- **2014 CE** — Halin, Beikthano, and Sri Ksetra are designated UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

## Body

### Origins and Urban System
The Pyu, Tibeto-Burman speakers, migrated from the Qinghai Lake region into the Irrawaddy valley. They established walled cities in three key irrigated regions: the Mu River Valley, Kyaukse plains, and Minbu region. Twelve walled cities have been excavated, with five major ones: Beikthano, Maingmaw, Binnaka, Halin, and Sri Ksetra. These cities were contemporaries of other early Southeast Asian statelets like Funan and Dvaravati. The Pyu realm was part of an overland and maritime trade route between China and India.

### Major City-States
**Beikthano** (named for the Hindu god Vishnu) is the oldest scientifically excavated site (200 BCE–100 CE), a large rectangular fortified settlement. **Halin**, founded in the 1st century CE, was the largest city until the 7th/8th century, known for salt production and its early Pyu script. **Sri Ksetra** ("Field of Fortune"), founded later, became the largest and most influential Pyu center by the 7th/8th century. It was a major trade entrepôt and is the most extensively excavated site, revealing a circular city plan, Buddhist remains, and the calendar later adopted by Burma. **Maingmaw** and **Binnaka** in the Kyaukse region are contemporaries of Beikthano, with similar artifacts.

### Economy and Trade
The economy was based on irrigated agriculture, particularly rice cultivation, and long-distance trade. The Pyu realm connected overland routes from China with Indian Ocean maritime trade. Artifacts found at Pyu sites originate from as far as northwest India, Java, and the Philippines. The Pyu minted silver coinage from the 5th century, which influenced mainland Southeast Asian coinage until the end of the Pyu period.

### Culture and Religion
Pyu culture was deeply influenced by Indian trade, adopting Brahmic scripts, the Buddhist calendar, and architectural concepts. By the 4th century, they were predominantly Theravada Buddhist, though with significant Mahayana, Tantric, and Hindu influences. Their religion was syncretic, blending Buddhist practices with pre-Buddhist traditions like elaborate cremation burials in urns. The Pyu language, a Tibeto-Burman tongue, co-existed with Sanskrit and Pali at court. Their script may be the source of the later Burmese script.

### Architecture and City Planning
Pyu architecture, especially temple designs, was influenced by Indian styles from the Andhra region. They built Buddhist stupas and monasteries, with designs that later influenced Pagan-era temples. City planning combined indigenous circular patterns with Indian rectangular layouts and 12 gates, a model that influenced later Burmese capitals. Their irrigation techniques for dams and canals were foundational for Upper Burma's agricultural base.

### Decline and Legacy
The Pyu civilization was weakened by repeated raids from the Nanzhao Kingdom in the 9th century, notably the 832 sack of Halin. This enabled the gradual migration and settlement of Burmans, who founded Pagan. By the late 10th century, Burmans had overtaken Pyu leadership. The Pyu were gradually assimilated into the expanding Pagan Kingdom, their language fading by the late 12th century. However, their cultural legacy endured: Pagan's rulers claimed descent from Pyu kings, and Pyu influences in script, calendar, city planning, and temple architecture became integral to Burmese civilization.

## Terms
- **Pyu millennium**: The thousand-year period (c. 2nd century BCE to mid-11th century CE) of Pyu city-state dominance in Upper Burma.
- **Tibeto-Burman**: A language family to which the Pyu language and Old Burmese belong.
- **Brahmi script**: An ancient Indian writing system from which the Pyu script, and possibly the Burmese script, derived.
- **Theravada Buddhism**: The school of Buddhism that became predominant among the Pyu, emphasizing the Pali Canon.
- **Sakra Era**: A calendar system adopted by the Pyu in 80 CE, based on the Buddhist calendar.
- **Mandala**: A geometric design symbolizing the universe; Pyu city plans incorporated mandala concepts linking the city center to the heavens.
- **Entrepôt**: A central port or trading center; Sri Ksetra served as a key entrepôt between Indian Ocean and overland China trade.
- **Nanzhao Kingdom**: A Yunnan-based kingdom whose 9th-century raids severely weakened the Pyu city-states.
- **Pagan Kingdom**: The successor state that unified the Irrawaddy valley in the mid-11th century, absorbing Pyu territories and cultural elements.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact origins and migration routes of the Pyu from the Qinghai Lake region are reconstructed from limited evidence.
- The political relationship between the major Pyu city-states (e.g., overlord-vassal ties) is unclear; they may have been largely independent.
- The pace and scale of Burman migration into the Pyu realm are debated; radiocarbon dating suggests a gradual process extending into the 10th century.
- The existence and location of the legendary "Pinle Pyu" (Sea Pyu) city remains speculative, with possible sites under investigation.
- The full extent of Pyu administrative control over distant coastal trading towns is not confirmed.
