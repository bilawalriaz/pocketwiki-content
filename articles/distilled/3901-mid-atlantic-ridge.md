# Mid-Atlantic Ridge

## Overview
The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is a massive underwater mountain range and divergent plate boundary running the length of the Atlantic Ocean. It is the most prominent feature of the global mid-ocean ridge system, marking where tectonic plates pull apart and new oceanic crust is formed. Its discovery and mapping were pivotal in confirming the theories of seafloor spreading and plate tectonics.

## Timeline
- **c. 200–160 million years ago** — The ridge's formation contributed to the breakup of the supercontinent Pangaea.
- **1853** — Matthew Fontaine Maury first infers a ridge in the North Atlantic.
- **1872** — The HMS *Challenger* expedition confirms the ridge's existence and extension into the South Atlantic.
- **1925** — Sonar confirms the ridge and finds it extends into the Indian Ocean.
- **1950s** — Mapping by Tharp, Heezen, and Ewing reveals the ridge's central rift valley and its place in a global system.

## Body
### Discovery and Confirmation
The existence of a submarine ridge was first proposed in 1853 by Matthew Fontaine Maury. It was confirmed during the 1872 HMS *Challenger* expedition, which discovered a large rise while surveying for a telegraph cable route. Sonar verification in 1925 showed the feature was continuous, extending around Africa into the Indian Ocean. In the 1950s, detailed mapping by Marie Tharp, Bruce Heezen, and Maurice Ewing revealed the ridge's distinctive central valley and seismic activity. Their work showed it was part of a 40,000 km global mid-ocean ridge system, a discovery that provided key evidence for the theories of seafloor spreading and plate tectonics.

### Geology and Structure
The ridge sits atop a broader geological feature called the Mid-Atlantic Rise, a progressive bulge in the ocean floor caused by upward convective forces in the asthenosphere (the hot, ductile layer of the upper mantle). A deep rift valley runs along the ridge's axis, marking the actual divergent boundary where magma erupts to create new crust. The ridge first formed in the Triassic period from a series of three-armed grabens (down-dropped blocks of crust) on Pangaea. Two arms typically become active plate boundaries; the failed third arms, called aulacogens, became the basins for major rivers like the Mississippi, Amazon, and Niger.

### Notable Features and Islands
The ridge is divided near the equator by the Romanche Trench, one of the Atlantic's deepest points. While mostly submerged, portions of the ridge rise above sea level to form islands. In the North Atlantic, these include Jan Mayen, Iceland (where the ridge is exposed as the Neovolcanic Zone), and the Azores. In the South Atlantic, they include Ascension Island, Saint Helena, Tristan da Cunha, Gough Island, and Bouvet Island. The average spreading rate along the ridge is about 2.5 cm per year.

## Terms
*   **Mid-ocean ridge:** An underwater mountain system formed by plate tectonics at divergent boundaries.
*   **Divergent plate boundary:** A boundary where tectonic plates move apart, allowing magma to rise and form new crust.
*   **Rift valley:** A lowland area where Earth's crust is spreading apart, often filled with volcanic activity.
*   **Asthenosphere:** The hot, flowing layer of the upper mantle beneath the lithosphere (crust and rigid upper mantle).
*   **Aulacogen:** A failed arm of a three-armed rift system that becomes a sedimentary basin, often occupied by a river.
*   **Seafloor spreading:** The process where new oceanic crust forms at a mid-ocean ridge and moves away from the ridge.
*   **Plate tectonics:** The theory that Earth's outer shell is divided into several plates that glide over the mantle.

## Debates and open questions
The source notes that the ridge's contribution to the breakup of Pangaea is considered in modern tectonic models, where subduction and mantle plumes are hypothesized to be primary mechanisms. This indicates a historical and ongoing scholarly discussion about the relative importance of different forces in continental rifting.
