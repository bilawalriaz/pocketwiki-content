# Singapore

## Overview
Singapore is a city-state and island country in Southeast Asia, strategically located off the southern tip of the Malay Peninsula. From its origins as the 14th-century trading port of Temasek, it evolved through successive empires and British colonial rule into a modern global hub. Its rapid post-independence development, driven by strategic governance and economic openness, transformed it into one of the world's wealthiest nations, despite lacking natural resources.

## Timeline
- **c. 1299**: Kingdom of Singapura founded by Sang Nila Utama (per Malay Annals)
- **1365**: Name "Temasek" referenced in Javanese Nagarakretagama
- **1819**: Stamford Raffles establishes British trading post; Treaty of Singapore signed
- **1867**: Singapore comes under direct British control as part of Straits Settlements
- **1942**: Japan occupies Singapore following British surrender
- **1959**: Self-governance granted; PAP wins first general election
- **1963**: Joins Federation of Malaysia
- **1965**: Expelled from Malaysia; becomes independent Republic of Singapore
- **1967**: Co-founds ASEAN
- **1990**: Goh Chok Tong becomes second prime minister
- **2004**: Lee Hsien Loong becomes third prime minister
- **2024**: Lawrence Wong becomes fourth prime minister

## Body

### Early History and Colonial Foundations
Singapore's early history centered on Temasek, a maritime emporium mentioned in 14th-century sources. The name Singapura (Sanskrit for "lion city") was adopted after the establishment of the Kingdom of Singapura by a Sumatran prince. Under British rule from 1819, Singapore grew from a small settlement of ~1,000 to over 80,000 residents by 1860, driven by Chinese immigration for pepper and gambier cultivation. The 1890s rubber boom solidified its role as a global trading hub.

### Japanese Occupation and Post-War Transition
World War II marked a turning point when Japan captured Singapore in 1942, calling it Syonan-to ("light of the south"). The fall was Britain's worst military defeat, with 85,000 troops captured. The Sook Ching massacre killed between 5,000 and 25,000 ethnic Chinese. Post-liberation, Singapore faced severe infrastructure damage, food shortages, and social unrest. The British Military Administration ended in 1946, making Singapore a separate crown colony.

### Independence and Nation-Building
Singapore briefly joined Malaysia in 1963 but was expelled in 1965 due to ideological differences. Under Lee Kuan Yew's leadership, the newly independent nation pursued rapid industrialization, shifting from labor-intensive industries to high-tech manufacturing and financial services. This transformation made Singapore one of the Four Asian Tigers, achieving one of the world's highest GDP per capita levels.

### Political System and Governance
Singapore operates as a parliamentary republic under the Westminster system, dominated by the People's Action Party (PAP) since 1959. While constitutionally a multi-party democracy with regular elections, it functions as a de facto one-party state. The PAP's "Singapore model" combines free-market economics with strong state intervention, meritocracy, and strict law enforcement. Political reforms like Group Representation Constituencies (1988) and elected presidency (1993) aimed to ensure minority representation and fiscal accountability.

### Economic Development
Singapore's economy evolved from entrepôt trade to a diversified hub for finance, manufacturing, and logistics. It ranks among the world's most competitive economies with AAA credit ratings. Key sectors include financial services, electronics, pharmaceuticals, and maritime logistics. The Port of Singapore remains one of the world's busiest, while Changi Airport consistently ranks among the best globally. Despite being labeled a tax haven, Singapore maintains strict anti-corruption measures.

### Social and Cultural Framework
Singapore's multicultural society recognizes four official languages: English, Mandarin, Malay, and Tamil. English serves as the lingua franca in government and education. The bilingual education policy preserves ethnic identities while promoting national unity. Cultural policies emphasize racial harmony and social cohesion, with initiatives like the "Garden City" vision creating extensive green spaces. Traditional practices coexist with modern urban planning.

### Contemporary Challenges
Modern Singapore grapples with an aging population, low fertility rates (TFR 1.1), and high immigration dependency. Climate change poses existential threats, with plans to spend $100 billion on coastal protection. The government has implemented carbon taxes and green energy initiatives. Socially, debates continue over civil liberties, with restrictions on public assembly and press freedom. Recent reforms include repealing Section 377A (2023) decriminalizing homosexuality while maintaining traditional marriage definitions.

## Terms
- **Entrepôt**: A trading hub where goods are imported, stored, and re-exported without significant processing
- **Thalassocracy**: A state with dominion over the sea, typically exercising power through naval supremacy
- **Crown Colony**: A territory governed directly by the British Crown rather than through local legislative assemblies
- **Group Representation Constituency (GRC)**: Electoral divisions where teams of candidates must include members from minority communities
- **Non-Constituency Member of Parliament (NCMP)**: Opposition politicians appointed to Parliament to ensure representation despite losing elections
- **Singlish**: Colloquial Singaporean English incorporating vocabulary and grammatical structures from local languages
- **Garden City**: Singapore's urban planning vision emphasizing parks, tree-lined streets, and green spaces
- **Four Asian Tigers**: High-income economies (Singapore, Hong Kong, South Korea, Taiwan) known for rapid industrialization
- **Meritocracy**: System where advancement is based on individual ability and achievement rather than birth or wealth
- **Asian Values**: Political philosophy emphasizing economic development, social harmony, and collective responsibility over individual rights

## Debates and Open Questions
Several historical and contemporary issues remain debated. The historicity of the Malay Annals' account of Singapura's founding is questioned by scholars, though archaeological evidence confirms 14th-century settlement. The precise timing and reasons for the name change from Temasek to Singapura remain unclear. Politically, Singapore's classification as an "illiberal democracy" or "soft-authoritarian state" reflects ongoing debates about the balance between economic development and civil liberties. The effectiveness of the "Singapore model" as an exportable governance framework is contested, particularly regarding its applicability to larger, more diverse societies. Environmental challenges, including sea-level rise and sand scarcity for land reclamation, pose unresolved questions about long-term sustainability. The tension between maintaining social cohesion and expanding individual freedoms continues to evolve, particularly regarding LGBTQ+ rights and political pluralism.
