# Indian classical music

## Overview
Indian classical music is the classical music tradition of the Indian subcontinent, encompassing two major, historically intertwined systems: the North Indian Hindustani tradition and the South Indian Carnatic tradition. It is a sophisticated art form built on the foundational concepts of raga (melodic framework) and tala (rhythmic cycle), emphasizing improvisation and the creation of a specific mood or essence (rasa). Its roots lie in ancient Vedic literature, and it has evolved over millennia, absorbing regional and external influences while maintaining a core theoretical structure.

## Timeline
- **c. 1000 BCE** — The Samaveda, structured with melodic themes, is composed.
- **c. 500 BCE** — Yāska's nirukta studies reference distinct musical terms.
- **c. 200 BCE – 200 CE** — Bharata Muni's Natyashastra, a foundational text on performing arts, is finalized.
- **13th century** — Sarangadeva's Sangeeta-Ratnakara becomes a definitive text for both major traditions.
- **14th–15th centuries** — Socio-political turmoil begins to isolate North and South Indian music traditions.
- **16th century** — Purandara Dasa systematizes Carnatic music theory. Tansen innovates under Mughal patronage, influencing Hindustani music.
- **16th century onwards** — North Indian (Hindustani) and South Indian (Carnatic) traditions acquire distinct forms.
- **1958–1970s** — Ravi Shankar popularizes Indian classical music in the West.
- **2000s–present** — Rapid global growth of Indian classical music, especially in North America.

## Body

### Historical Foundations
The roots of Indian classical music are found in the Vedic literature of Hinduism, particularly the Samaveda (c. 1000 BCE), which is set to melodic themes. Ancient Indian thought combined recitation, melody, and dance. The Natyashastra, a classic Sanskrit text by Bharata Muni, is the foundational treatise for music and dance, classifying instruments and establishing theoretical frameworks. The 13th-century text Sangeeta-Ratnakara by Sarangadeva is regarded as the definitive work by both the Hindustani and Carnatic traditions.

### Major Traditions: Hindustani and Carnatic
Until the 14th century, Indian classical music was a generally integrated system. The socio-political turmoil of the Delhi Sultanate era isolated the north from the south, leading to distinct traditions by the 16th century.

**Hindustani music**, found in North India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh, emphasizes improvisation and exploration of a raga. It developed under the patronage of Muslim courts, which discouraged Sanskrit and encouraged technical music, leading to assimilation of Persian and Arabian influences. Its core forms include the ancient Dhrupad and the modern Khyal (meaning "imagination"). Improvisation methods like Alap, Jor, and Jhala are central.

**Carnatic music**, from South India, is considered a more native development after the divergence. It is generally more rhythmically intensive and composition-based than Hindustani music. Purandara Dasa (1484–1564) is revered as its "great father" for systematizing its theory. Its concert structure, formalized by Ariyakudi Ramanuja Iyengar, features fixed compositions and a larger role for accompanists.

### Foundational Elements: Raga and Tala
**Raga** is the central melodic concept. It is not a simple tune or scale, but a framework of melodic features—including note sequence, emphasis, and ornamentation—designed to evoke a specific rasa (mood or essence). A raga is based on a scale of swaras (notes), but the artist has freedom to improvise within its rules.

**Tala** is the metrical framework that measures musical time in a repeating cycle. It is more flexible than Western meters, often lacking a fixed "strong-weak" beat pattern, with accents determined by the musical phrase. The first beat of a tala cycle is called sam.

### Instruments and Note System
Instruments include the sitar, sarod, and tabla (Hindustani); and the veena, mridangam, and nadaswaram (Carnatic). The tanpura provides a continuous drone. Both systems divide the octave into 12 semitones, with seven basic swaras (Sa, Re/Ri, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni). Indian music uses just-intonation tuning, unlike the equal temperament of modern Western music.

## Terms
- **Raga**: A melodic framework for improvisation, designed to evoke a specific mood (rasa).
- **Tala**: A repeating rhythmic cycle that provides the metrical structure for a performance.
- **Swaras**: The basic notes of the scale (Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni).
- **Rasa**: The aesthetic essence or mood evoked by a musical performance.
- **Matra**: A single beat or unit of time within a tala.
- **Gharana**: A school or tradition of Hindustani music, often tracing lineage to a master musician.
- **Bandish**: A fixed, composed piece in Hindustani music.
- **Alap**: An improvised, unmetered introduction to a raga in Hindustani music.
- **Melakarta**: A system for classifying ragas in Carnatic music.
- **Thaat**: A system for classifying ragas in Hindustani music.

## Debates and Open Questions
The nature and extent of Persian and Arabian musical influence on Hindustani music is debated. Some scholars, like the 16th-century Pundarika Vittala, argued that Persian maqams derived from older Indian ragas. Others, like Haidar Rizvi (1941), suggested the influence was in the opposite direction. Modern ethnomusicology suggests the traffic of musical ideas was likely bidirectional.
