# Miles Davis

## Overview
Miles Davis (1926–1991) was an American trumpeter, bandleader, and composer whose career traced the evolution of jazz from bebop through fusion. Over nearly five decades, he spearheaded or defined major stylistic shifts—including cool jazz, hard bop, modal jazz, post-bop, and jazz fusion—by constantly restructuring his bands and rejecting stylistic stasis. His 1959 album *Kind of Blue* remains the best-selling jazz record in history, while his 1970 release *Bitches Brew* catalyzed the jazz-rock fusion movement. Davis’s influence extended beyond jazz into rock, funk, classical, and hip-hop, driven by a philosophy of individual expression and creative response to shifting musical contexts. His personal life was marked by substance abuse, domestic violence, and a late-career turn to visual art.

## Timeline
- **May 26, 1926** — Born in Alton, Illinois, to an affluent African-American family.
- **1944** — Moves to New York City; attends Juilliard; joins Charlie Parker’s bebop quintet.
- **1949–1950** — Records *Birth of the Cool* sessions with nonet arranged by Gil Evans, John Lewis, and Gerry Mulligan; defines cool jazz.
- **1954–1955** — Overcomes heroin addiction; records early hard bop sessions for Prestige (e.g., *Walkin'*).
- **July 1955** — Breakthrough performance at Newport Jazz Festival; signs with Columbia Records.
- **1955–1958** — Forms first quintet (initially Sonny Rollins, then Coltrane; Garland, Chambers, Jones); records *'Round About Midnight* and *Milestones*.
- **1957–1962** — Orchestral collaborations with Gil Evans: *Miles Ahead*, *Porgy and Bess*, *Sketches of Spain*.
- **Aug 1959** — Releases *Kind of Blue* (modal jazz landmark); sells over 5 million copies in US.
- **1964–1968** — Second quintet (Shorter, Hancock, Carter, Williams); pioneers post-bop ("time no changes") on *E.S.P.*, *Miles Smiles*, *Nefertiti*.
- **1969–1975** — Electric period: *In a Silent Way*, *Bitches Brew*, *On the Corner*; integrates rock, funk, electronics; alienates jazz purists.
- **1975–1980** — Hiatus due to health issues, addiction, and legal troubles.
- **1981–1991** — Comeback: pop-inflected albums (*The Man with the Horn*, *Tutu*, *Amandla*); extensive touring; visual art career.
- **Sep 28, 1991** — Dies in Santa Monica, CA, from stroke, pneumonia, and respiratory failure, aged 65.
- **2006** — Inducted into Rock and Roll Hall of Fame.

## Body

### Early Life and Musical Formation
Born into an affluent African-American family in Alton, Illinois, Davis was raised in East St. Louis. His father, a dentist, and mother, a music teacher, provided a privileged upbringing including a 200-acre Arkansas estate. Davis received his first trumpet at 13 from a family friend and studied with Elwood Buchanan, a local teacher who emphasized a clear, vibrato-less tone—a concept that defined Davis’s signature sound. By 15, he was playing professionally in local bands and studying theory after failing to execute a passage at a competition. He graduated high school in 1944, briefly joining Billy Eckstine’s band (featuring Parker, Gillespie, Blakey) when their trumpeter fell ill, confirming his decision to pursue music in New York.

### Bebop Apprenticeship and the Birth of Cool (1944–1950)
In New York, Davis enrolled at Juilliard but spent nights seeking Charlie Parker at Harlem jam sessions (Minton’s, Monroe’s). He dropped out in 1945 to join Parker’s quintet, recording with the "Reboppers" and displaying an early cool jazz sensibility on "Now's the Time." After touring with Benny Carter and Eckstine, Davis grew frustrated with Parker’s erratic behavior and drug addiction. In August 1948, Davis declined an offer to join Duke Ellington's orchestra as he had entered rehearsals with a nine-piece band featuring baritone saxophonist Gerry Mulligan and arrangements by Gil Evans. The group used atypical instrumentation (French horn, tuba) to create an orchestral, vocal-like texture. Their 1949–50 Capitol sessions, compiled as *Birth of the Cool* (1957), established the "West Coast" cool jazz aesthetic. Arrangements were by Gil Evans, John Lewis and Gerry Mulligan. A 1949 Paris festival appearance exposed Davis to a racially integrated cultural environment he found transformative.

### Hard Bop, Addiction, and the First Quintet (1951–1958)
Returning from Paris, Davis descended into heroin addiction, losing work and pawning his trumpet. He supported his habit through hustling and sporadic recording for Prestige (1951–54). In 1954, he detoxed at his father’s St. Louis home, returning to New York physically stronger. Influenced by pianist Ahmad Jamal’s use of space, Davis adopted the Harmon mute and relaxed phrasing, becoming a central figure in hard bop—a bluesier, harder-driving alternative to cool jazz. His 1954 *Walkin'* session is often cited as the genre’s birth. A 1955 larynx operation left him with a permanent rasp after he argued against medical orders to stay silent.

His career turned decisively at the 1955 Newport Jazz Festival, where his solo on "'Round Midnight" won critical acclaim and a Columbia Records contract (George Avakian). To fulfill his Prestige obligation, he formed a quintet with Sonny Rollins on tenor saxophone, Red Garland on piano, Paul Chambers on double bass and Philly Joe Jones on drums. Rollins was replaced by John Coltrane, completing the membership of the first quintet. Their marathon 1956 sessions yielded four acclaimed albums (*Cookin'*, *Relaxin'*, *Workin'*, *Steamin'*). Davis also began orchestral collaborations with Gil Evans: *Miles Ahead* (1957, flugelhorn), *Porgy and Bess* (1959), and *Sketches of Spain* (1960), blending jazz with classical and Spanish forms.

### Modal Jazz and *Kind of Blue* (1958–1963)
Seeking to escape chord-change complexity, Davis turned to modal jazz—improvising on static scales rather than shifting harmonies—inspired by George Russell’s theories and a Les Ballets Africains performance. By May 1958, he had replaced Jones with drummer Jimmy Cobb, and Garland left the group. He wanted someone who could play modal jazz, so he hired Bill Evans, a young pianist with a background in classical music. In March–April 1959, the sextet (adding Cannonball Adderley, alto sax) recorded *Kind of Blue* with minimal rehearsal; three of five tracks were first takes. Released August 1959, it became the best-selling jazz album ever (5M+ US, 6.4M worldwide), honored by the US House in 2009 as a national treasure. A 1959 police beating outside Birdland (acquitted 1960) deepened Davis’s cynicism. Coltrane left in 1960; the quintet recorded *Someday My Prince Will Come* (1961) and *Seven Steps to Heaven* (1963), introducing Herbie Hancock, Ron Carter, and Tony Williams.

### The Second Quintet and Post-Bop (1964–1968)
Wayne Shorter’s 1964 arrival completed the "second great quintet" (Shorter, Hancock, Carter, Williams). Shorter became principal composer; the group’s studio output (*E.S.P.* 1965, *Miles Smiles* 1967, *Nefertiti* 1967) pioneered "time no changes"—abandoning fixed chord sequences for open, interactive improvisation where the rhythm section responded fluidly to soloists. From 1967, they performed continuous sets, tunes flowing without pause. *Miles in the Sky* and *Filles de Kilimanjaro* (1968) introduced electric instruments (bass, piano, guitar) and rock rhythms, foreshadowing fusion. By the time the second half of *Filles de Kilimanjaro* was recorded, bassist Dave Holland and pianist Chick Corea had replaced Carter and Hancock. Davis soon took over the compositional duties of his sidemen.

### The Electric Period: Fusion and Funk (1969–1975)
*In a Silent Way* (Feb 1969), edited by producer Teo Macero from a single session with two keyboardists (Corea, Zawinul) and guitarist John McLaughlin, launched jazz fusion. *Bitches Brew* (1970), a double album of long, spliced compositions, sold 1M+ copies by 2003 and peaked at No. 35 Billboard. Davis opened for rock acts (Fillmore East, Isle of Wight 600,000), facing "sellout" accusations from jazz critics and black press. The band evolved toward funk: *Live-Evil* (1971) featured bassist Michael Henderson; *On the Corner* (1972), influenced by Karlheinz Stockhausen’s avant-garde "space music," emphasized rhythmic density over solos. Davis aimed for young Black audiences but felt Columbia marketed it to jazz radio. Health declined: 1972 car crash (broken ankles), painkillers, cocaine. Final 1975 Osaka concerts (*Agharta*, *Pangaea*) were played with his back to the audience, reliant on alcohol, codeine, morphine. He retired after Newport and Schaefer festivals in July/September 1975.

### Hiatus and Comeback (1975–1991)
Davis spent five years in seclusion, battling addiction (cocaine, alcohol), hip failures, and legal issues (child support, shooting incident 1969). A 1975 hip replacement failed; Columbia nearly dropped him until United Artists interest forced a fund arrangement. In 1980, he attempted a return, struggling to regain embouchure. *The Man with the Horn* (1981) sold well but drew poor reviews. A 1982 stroke (alcohol-induced) paralyzed his right hand; recovery via acupuncture, exercise, and Cicely Tyson’s support (married 1981, divorced 1989) restored his playing. Tyson encouraged painting, which became a lifelong parallel pursuit. The 1980s bands (Marcus Miller, Bill Evans, John Scofield, Mino Cinelu) used programmed synths, sampling, and drum loops (*Tutu* 1986, *Amandla* 1989), shifting to a melodic, pop-accessible style. Davis collaborated with Prince, Toto, Scritti Politti, and Zane Giles. He acted in *Scrooged* (1988) and *Dingo* (1991). His final performance was Hollywood Bowl, August 25, 1991; he died September 28 from stroke, pneumonia, respiratory failure.

### Personal Life and Controversies
Davis married three times: Frances Taylor (1959–68, dancer; abuse documented in autobiography, linked to sickle cell anemia pain and cocaine), Betty Mabry (1968–69, model/songwriter; introduced him to rock/funk; accused of affair with Hendrix), Cicely Tyson (1981–89, actress; pivotal in recovery). He had four children: Cheryl, Gregory, Miles IV, Erin. A 1969 shooting (grazed hip) stemmed from promoter disputes. Late-life relationship with sculptor Jo Gelbard (painting mentor) ended with violence attributed to diabetic medication aggression. Estate valued >$1M (1991); will excluded sons Gregory and Miles IV. Davis took AZT (antiretroviral) during final hospitalization; manager denied AIDS rumors (1989).

### Views on Earlier Work and Critical Reception
From the electric period onward, Davis refused to play early repertoire (*Birth of the Cool*, *Kind of Blue*), calling it "warmed-over turkey" and stating "Jazz is dead... finito!" He viewed stylistic repetition as artistic death. Critics polarized: Stanley Crouch labeled him "the most brilliant sellout"; Wynton Marsalis dismissed post-1975 work as "not jazz"; Robert Walser cited "missing more notes than any other major trumpet player"; James Lincoln Collier called him an "adequate instrumentalist." Conversely, *The Guardian* called him a "pioneer of 20th-century music"; AllMusic argued "jazz stopped evolving when Davis wasn't there to push it"; Francis Davis framed his career as "an ongoing critique of bebop." The 2013 *A.V. Club* article highlighted the tension between musical genius and domestic abuse.

### Legacy and Honors
Davis is widely cited as the most influential jazz musician post-1945. *The Pudding* (2016) found 2,452 Wikipedia pages mention him, 286+ citing influence. Eight Grammy wins, Lifetime Achievement Award, three Hall of Fame Grammys. 2009 House resolution honored *Kind of Blue*’s 50th anniversary. Rock and Roll Hall of Fame (2006), honorary doctorate New England Conservatory (1986), Léonie Sonning Prize (1984), Chevalier Legion of Honour (1991). Trumpet from *Kind of Blue* at UNC Greensboro (Miles Davis Jazz Studies Program). Documentaries: *The Miles Davis Story* (2001, Emmy), *Birth of the Cool* (2019). Films: *Miles Ahead* (2015), upcoming *Miles & Juliette*. Statue in Alton, IL (2015). BBC Radio 4 series *Legend* (2026). Publishing rights acquired by Reservoir Media (2025).

## Terms
- ****Bebop**** — A complex, fast-tempo jazz style (1940s) characterized by improvisation on chord changes; Davis learned it in Parker’s quintet.
- ****Cool Jazz**** — A lighter, arranged style (late 1940s) emphasizing melodic flow and relaxed tempos; codified by Davis’s *Birth of the Cool* nonet.
- ****Hard Bop**** — A 1950s style blending bebop with blues/gospel intensity; Davis’s *Walkin'* (1954) is a seminal recording.
- ****Modal Jazz**** — Improvisation based on static scales (modes) rather than rapid chord changes; realized on *Kind of Blue* (1959) via George Russell’s theories.
- ****Post-Bop**** — A 1960s approach (Davis’s second quintet) using original compositions, flexible rhythm ("time no changes"), and interactive group improvisation.
- ****Jazz Fusion**** — A late 1960s/70s hybrid combining jazz improvisation with rock rhythms, electric instruments, and studio editing; launched by *In a Silent Way* and *Bitches Brew*.
- ****Time No Changes**** — Davis’s term for his 1960s quintet’s method: abandoning fixed chord sequences for open forms where rhythm section follows soloist’s melody.
- ****Harmon Mute**** — A trumpet mute (wa-wa) placed close to the microphone; Davis used it from 1954 for a intimate, whisper-like timbre.
- ****Sickle Cell Anemia**** — Genetic blood disorder causing joint pain; Davis suffered from it (diagnosed 1961), contributing to substance use and volatile behavior.
- ****Teo Macero**** — Columbia producer (1958–1980s) who edited Davis’s electric albums via splicing, tape loops, and multi-track assembly (e.g., *Bitches Brew*).

## Debates and open questions
- **Artistic merit of electric/fusion period (1969–1975):** Critics remain split; some (Crouch, Marsalis) view it as a commercial sellout or "not jazz," while others (AllMusic, *The Guardian*) see it as a vital, innovative expansion of the genre.
- **Technical proficiency vs. conceptual innovation:** Detractors (Walser, Collier) argue Davis was an "adequate" trumpeter who missed notes; supporters contend his use of space, timbre, and bandleading constituted a higher musical intelligence.
- **Separation of art and biography:** The documented domestic violence (Taylor, Gelbard) and misogyny raise ethical questions about celebrating Davis’s legacy, highlighted in recent criticism (*A.V. Club*, Francis Davis).
- **Commercial motivation vs. artistic integrity:** Davis claimed his 1970s funk turn aimed to reach young Black audiences (R&B/rock radio), but Columbia marketed it to jazz stations; debate persists on whether he compromised or was compromised.
- **AZT/HIV status:** Davis received AZT during final hospitalization (per Quincy Troupe); manager denied AIDS (1989). Source does not confirm diagnosis; remains an open biographical question.
