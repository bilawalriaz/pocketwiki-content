# Muhammad Ahmad

## Overview
Muhammad Ahmad (1843–1885) was a Sudanese religious leader who proclaimed himself the Mahdi (an eschatological redeemer in Islamic tradition) in 1881. He led a successful revolution against Egyptian and Ottoman rule, establishing a vast Islamic state in Sudan. His movement, the Mahdist State, fundamentally reshaped Sudan's political and religious landscape and remains a significant historical force.

## Timeline
- **12 August 1843** — Muhammad Ahmad is born in Labab Island, Dongola, Sudan.
- **1861** — Begins training under Sufi Sheikh Muhammad Sharif of the Samaniyya Order.
- **1870** — Moves to Aba Island, builds a mosque, and gains a reputation as a teacher and mystic.
- **25 July 1878** — Becomes leader of the Samaniyya Order after the death of Sheikh al-Qurashi wad al-Zayn.
- **29 June 1881** — Proclaims himself the Mahdi, launching the Mahdist revolt.
- **11 August 1881** — First clash with Egyptian forces on Aba Island.
- **Late 1883** — Ansar forces defeat an Egyptian army and capture El Obeid.
- **1884** — British officer General Charles George Gordon arrives in Khartoum to oversee an evacuation.
- **April 1884** — The Siege of Khartoum begins.
- **26 January 1885** — Khartoum falls to the Mahdist forces; General Gordon is killed.
- **22 June 1885** — Muhammad Ahmad dies of typhus; succeeded by his deputy, Abdullahi ibn Muhammad.
- **1898** — Anglo-Egyptian forces under Kitchener reconquer Sudan, defeating the Mahdist state.

## Body

### Early Life and Religious Authority
Born into a notable Arabized Nubian family, Muhammad Ahmad showed an early devotion to religious study over his family's boat-building trade. He trained within the Samaniyya Sufi order, eventually becoming its leader in 1878 after a dispute with his mentor, Sheikh Muhammad Sharif. He established a following on Aba Island through his reputation for piety and asceticism, teaching a reformist Islam focused on a return to the practices of early Muslim communities.

### The Mahdist Proclamation and Rebellion
On 29 June 1881, Muhammad Ahmad declared himself the Mahdi, a claim rooted in Samaniyya beliefs that the redeemer would come from their order. He framed his mission as a return to the unified Ummah (Muslim community) of the Prophet Muhammad's time. His followers were called the Ansar. The rebellion gained widespread support across Sudan, uniting various tribes—including non-Muslims—and regions against Turco-Egyptian rule. The Ansar achieved major military victories, capturing El Obeid in 1883 and defeating a British-led Egyptian force at the Battle of Shaykan.

### The Siege of Khartoum and the Fall of the City
In 1884, the British government, seeking to evacuate from Sudan, sent General Charles Gordon to Khartoum. Gordon's mission shifted to defense as the Ansar besieged the city in April 1884. A British relief expedition under Lord Wolseley advanced too slowly. Khartoum fell on 26 January 1885, two days before the relief force arrived. Gordon was killed in the fighting. The victory cemented Mahdist control over most of Sudan.

### Death, Succession, and Legacy
Muhammad Ahmad died of typhus on 22 June 1885, just five months after capturing Khartoum. His successor, Abdullahi ibn Muhammad (the Khalifa), maintained the state until its defeat by Anglo-Egyptian forces in 1898. Muhammad Ahmad's tomb in Omdurman was destroyed after the reconquest. Despite the state's collapse, his legacy endured. His descendants, notably his great-great-grandson Sadiq al-Mahdi, continued to lead the Ansar community and were influential in modern Sudanese politics, with Sadiq al-Mahdi serving as Prime Minister twice.

## Terms
- **Mahdi**: In Islamic eschatology, a messianic figure who will appear at the end of times to restore justice and true faith.
- **Ansar**: The name given to Muhammad Ahmad's followers, meaning "the helpers," echoing the early supporters of the Prophet Muhammad in Medina.
- **Samaniyya**: A Sufi order in Sudan from which Muhammad Ahmad emerged and which provided the theological basis for his Mahdi claim.
- **Sufi**: A mystical branch of Islam focused on inner spiritual experience and devotion.
- **Ulema / Ulama**: Islamic scholars and religious authorities.
- **Ummah**: The global Muslim community.
- **Khalifa**: The successor or deputy to the Mahdi; the title used by Abdullahi ibn Muhammad.
- **Hadra**: In Sufi tradition, a spiritual gathering of prophets and holy men; Muhammad Ahmad claimed to have been appointed Mahdi by such an assembly.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Legitimacy of the Mahdi Claim**: Orthodox Sudanese Ulema loyal to the Turco-Egyptian government rejected Muhammad Ahmad's claim, arguing it did not conform to prophecies in Hadith literature, particularly regarding the "time of troubles" required for the Mahdi's appearance.
- **Nature of the Revolution**: While rooted in religious prophecy, the revolt drew support from diverse tribes and regions, including non-Muslims, leading to its characterization as a national rather than purely religious revolution.
- **Political Legacy**: Muhammad Ahmad is sometimes considered a precursor of Sudanese nationalism. His political and religious movement, through the Umma Party and the Ansar community, remained a significant force in Sudanese politics long after the Mahdist State's fall.
