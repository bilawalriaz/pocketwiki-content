# Bhutan

## Overview
Bhutan is a landlocked Himalayan kingdom that transitioned from a theocratic state to a democratic constitutional monarchy. It is globally recognized for its unique development philosophy of Gross National Happiness (GNH), which prioritizes cultural preservation and environmental conservation alongside economic growth. Its history is marked by successful resistance to colonization, 17th-century unification, and recent managed political liberalization.

## Timeline
- **c. 2000 BCE** — Evidence of human habitation in Bhutan.
- **7th century CE** — Buddhism introduced; Tibetan king Songtsen Gampo orders construction of first temples.
- **1616–1651** — Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal unifies Bhutan, defeats Tibetan invasions, and establishes the Tsa Yig legal code.
- **1774** — British East India Company attacks Bhutan; a peace treaty is signed.
- **1864–65** — Duar War; Bhutan cedes the Bengal Duars to British India.
- **1907** — Ugyen Wangchuck unanimously elected as the first hereditary King.
- **1910** — Treaty of Punakha gives Britain control over Bhutan's foreign policy.
- **1949** — A similar treaty is signed with newly independent India.
- **1971** — Bhutan joins the United Nations.
- **2008** — A new constitution establishes a parliamentary democracy; first national elections held.
- **2022** — Gross National Happiness commission dissolved; framework abandoned.
- **2023** — Bhutan is officially delisted as a least developed country.

## Body

### Etymology and Early History
The name "Bhutan" likely derives from the Tibetan word "Böd," meaning Tibet, and was historically used by outsiders. The country's official name is *Druk yul* ("Land of the Thunder Dragon"). Archaeological evidence suggests habitation from around 2000 BCE. The state of Lhomon or Monyul may have existed between 500 BCE and 600 CE. Buddhism was introduced in the 7th century CE, with significant propagation beginning in 746 CE.

### Unification and Theocratic Rule
Until the early 17th century, Bhutan was a patchwork of warring fiefdoms. Unification was achieved by the Tibetan lama Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal, who fled religious persecution. He built a network of *dzongs* (fortresses), codified the *Tsa Yig* legal system, and established a dual system of government with both spiritual and civil administrators. After his death in 1651, his was kept secret for 54 years, and Bhutan later experienced internal conflict and wars with Tibet and the British.

### Modernization and Democracy
The Wangchuck dynasty was established in 1907. Bhutan maintained a de facto protectorate relationship with Britain, later continued with India, until a revised treaty in 2011 granted it full control over foreign relations. King Jigme Singye Wangchuck initiated democratic reforms, transferring powers to elected bodies. He abdicated in 2008, and his son was crowned as the country adopted a parliamentary democracy. In recent years, Bhutan has faced economic challenges, including a tourism decline during the COVID-19 pandemic and a mass emigration of young people.

### Geography and Environment
Bhutan is the most mountainous country in the world, with 98.8% of its land covered by mountains. Its highest peak, Gangkhar Puensum (7,570 m), is the world's highest unclimbed mountain. The country's climate varies from subtropical in the south to polar in the north. Bhutan is a global biodiversity hotspot, with over 71% forest cover. It maintains a policy of keeping at least 60% of its land under forest cover and has designated over 40% as protected areas. The country has net negative greenhouse gas emissions due to its forests acting as a carbon sink.

### Government and Politics
Bhutan is a constitutional monarchy with a parliamentary government. The head of state is the King (*Druk Gyalpo*), and the head of government is the Prime Minister. The legislature consists of the National Council (upper house) and the National Assembly (lower house). The legal system is based on the traditional *Tsa Yig* code, influenced by English common law. Bhutan's political culture has evolved from absolute monarchy to a multi-party democracy since 2008.

### Economy
Bhutan's economy is small but has experienced rapid growth, primarily driven by hydropower exports to India. Agriculture provides the main livelihood for over half the population. The country has significant untapped mineral resources but prioritizes environmental conservation. Tourism is a regulated industry, with a daily sustainable development fee for most foreign visitors. Bhutan has also entered the cryptocurrency sector, using its hydropower for Bitcoin mining.

### Demographics and Culture
Bhutan's population is primarily composed of the Ngalop and Sharchop ethnic groups. The national language is Dzongkha. Vajrayana Buddhism is the state religion, followed by a majority of the population, with Hinduism as the second-largest religion. Bhutanese culture is deeply Buddhist, with traditional dress (*gho* for men, *kira* for women) mandated in official settings. The country's unique architecture features *dzongs* and traditional buildings without nails or iron bars.

## Terms
- **Dzong**: A distinctive type of fortress architecture in Bhutan, serving as both a religious and administrative center.
- **Gross National Happiness (GNH)**: Bhutan's development philosophy that prioritizes cultural preservation, environmental conservation, good governance, and sustainable economic development over GDP.
- **Tsa Yig**: The traditional legal code of Bhutan, established by Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal in the 17th century.
- **Druk Gyalpo**: The title of the King of Bhutan, meaning "Dragon King."
- **Lhotshampa**: A term for Bhutanese people of Nepali ancestry, meaning "southerners."
- **Driglam Namzhag**: A code of etiquette and dress introduced in the 1980s to promote national identity.
- **Vajrayana Buddhism**: The school of Buddhism that is the state religion of Bhutan, also known as Tantric or Tibetan Buddhism.
- **Dzongkhag**: The administrative districts of Bhutan.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The 1990s expulsion of Lhotshampa refugees remains a sensitive human rights issue, with debates over the number of deportees (estimated between 80,000 and 107,000) and the government's actions.
- The abandonment of the Gross National Happiness framework in 2022 in favor of GDP has sparked debate about Bhutan's development path and the balance between economic growth and its traditional values.
- The ongoing border dispute with China over approximately 269 square kilometers of territory is a key foreign policy challenge.
- The mass emigration of over 50,000 young people since the pandemic raises questions about Bhutan's economic future and social stability.
