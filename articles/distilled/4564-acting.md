# Acting

## Overview
Acting is the art of telling a story through the enactment of a character by an actor across various media. It is a fundamental human activity rooted in mimesis (imitation), requiring a broad range of skills developed through extensive training. The practice has evolved from ancient Greek origins to modern techniques, involving complex psychological and physiological processes for the performer.

## Timeline
- **c. 335 BCE** — Aristotle writes *Poetics*, distinguishing *mimesis* (enactment) from *diegesis* (narration).
- **Ancient Greece** — Thespis of Icaria is identified as one of the first known actors, stepping out of the chorus.
- **1910s onwards** — Konstantin Stanislavski develops his 'system' of actor training in Russia.
- **1924-1927** — Viola Spolin studies with Neva Boyd, later pioneering improvisation in the US.
- **1938** — Antonin Artaud publishes *The Theatre and its Double*, proposing non-signifying communication.
- **1930s onwards** — Joan Littlewood pioneers improvisation in the UK.
- **1970** — Bertolt Brecht's epic theatre emphasizes the actor's demonstration of socio-historical significance.
- **1996-2014** — Mike Leigh develops feature films substantially through improvisation.
- **2017** — A study on American university actors confirms elevated heart rates during performance.

## Body

### Origins and Training
The Western art of acting traces its origins to ancient Greece, where Thespis (c. 6th century BCE) is credited as the first actor to step out of the dithyrambic chorus and speak as a character. This shift from narration to enactment is central to the concept of *mimesis*. Modern professional actors typically undergo extensive training at conservatories or universities, which can last two to four years. Training focuses on skills like textual interpretation, voice, and movement. In North America, the most popular method derives from Stanislavski's 'system', later developed as method acting. Other approaches include physically based techniques from practitioners like Jacques Lecoq or Jerzy Grotowski.

### Improvisation and Process
Improvisation, the creation of drama spontaneously without a fixed text, is a key element in many acting traditions. It was notably used by the Italian *commedia dell'arte* troupes. Stanislavski incorporated improvisation into his training system, a practice extended by his students. In the US, Viola Spolin promoted improvisational games as a training tool, arguing they help actors find expressive freedom. The process of preparing a role involves rehearsal, where actors practice with directors and technical staff, and auditioning, where they perform monologues or sides for casting directors.

### Semiotics and Audience
The semiotics of acting studies how performance elements function as signs to create meaning for an audience. While audiences understand actors are not their characters, they accept the fictional reality. Theatrical theorist Antonin Artaud proposed communication could occur "beneath" conscious meaning through "a-signifying signs" or "affects." Different practitioners engage with semiotics differently: Stanislavski focused on shaping performance for overall dramatic significance, while Bertolt Brecht's epic theatre used acting to demonstrate socio-historical meaning. Theatre semiotician Patrice Pavis argues all acting is based on codified systems of behavior, even those claiming to be "natural."

### Physiological Effects
Acting in front of an audience commonly induces "stage fright," a form of stress with symptoms like increased heart rate and sweating. Studies, including a 2017 one on university students, show actors' heart rates elevate before and during performances. More experienced actors display less heart rate variability, indicating more stable stress management. While the stress is real, it can improve performance, and the practice of acting has been shown to be an effective tool for reducing social anxiety.

## Terms
- **Mimesis**: The mode of storytelling through enactment by an actor, as opposed to narration.
- **Diegesis**: The mode of storytelling via narration, where events are described rather than enacted.
- **Hypokrisis**: The ancient Greek term for the art of acting, often discussed as part of rhetoric.
- **Thespian**: A term for an actor, derived from the name of the first known actor, Thespis.
- **Commedia dell'arte**: A form of Italian masked comedy that used substantial improvisation.
- **Method acting**: An approach to acting, developed in America from Stanislavski's system, emphasizing emotional and psychological realism.
- **Semiotics**: The study of signs and symbols and their use in performance to create meaning.
- **Gestus**: In Brecht's epic theatre, a performed physical attitude or gesture that reveals social attitudes.
- **Stage fright**: A form of performance anxiety or stress experienced by actors in front of an audience.

## Debates and Open Questions
A central debate concerns the actor's psychological state: should they sincerely believe in and be moved by their role (sincerity), or maintain a detached, demonstrative distance (hypocrisy)? This question, framed by Denis Diderot, relates to the desired effect on the audience and the social function of theatre. Furthermore, the extent to which acting conventions are "natural" versus ideologically coded is contested. While some approaches like Stanislavski's aim for self-evident realism, semioticians like Pavis argue all acting is governed by historical codes of what is considered believable.
