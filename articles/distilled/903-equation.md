# Equation

## Overview
An equation is a mathematical statement asserting the equality of two expressions, connected by the equals sign (=). Equations are central to mathematics and its applications because they encode relationships between quantities, allowing unknowns to be determined from known ones. They range from simple linear equations to complex differential and functional equations, and their study spans algebra, geometry, number theory, and analysis.

## Timeline
- **1557**: Robert Recorde invents the "=" symbol, choosing parallel lines as the most equal mark.
- **17th century**: René Descartes develops Cartesian coordinates, linking algebra and Euclidean geometry.
- **1670s**: Differential equations emerge with calculus, modeling rates of change in physics and engineering.
- **1800s**: Niels Henrik Abel and Paolo Ruffini prove the Abel–Ruffini theorem: general quintic equations are not solvable by radicals.
- **19th–20th centuries**: Algebraic geometry and number theory formalize the study of polynomial and Diophantine equations.

## Body

### Description and Basic Properties
An equation consists of a left-hand side (LHS) and right-hand side (RHS) separated by "=". Solving an equation means finding values of the unknowns (variables) that make the equality true. Equations are either **identities** (true for all variable values) or **conditional equations** (true only for specific values). The balance analogy—where equal operations on both sides preserve equality—underlies all algebraic manipulation. Equivalent transformations include adding/subtracting equal quantities, multiplying/dividing by non-zero constants, and applying identities. Applying functions to both sides may introduce **extraneous solutions**, requiring verification.

### Algebra and Polynomial Equations
Polynomial equations take the form P(x) = 0 or P = Q, where P and Q are polynomials. They are classified by degree: linear (degree 1), quadratic (degree 2), cubic (degree 3), and so on. Univariate equations involve one variable; multivariate involve several. While degrees one through four admit general algebraic solutions, the Abel–Ruffini theorem proves that degree five and higher cannot always be solved by radicals. Systems of linear equations—collections of linear equations solved simultaneously—are foundational in linear algebra and widely applied in science and engineering.

### Geometry and Analytic Methods
Analytic geometry uses equations to describe geometric figures. A plane in 3D space is given by ax + by + cz + d = 0; a circle by x² + y² = r². Cartesian coordinates, introduced by Descartes, transform geometric problems into algebraic ones. **Parametric equations** express coordinates as functions of a parameter (e.g., x = cos t, y = sin t for the unit circle). These tools enable the study of curves, surfaces, and higher-dimensional objects through algebraic and analytical techniques.

### Number Theory and Diophantine Equations
A **Diophantine equation** requires integer solutions. Named after Diophantus of Alexandria (3rd century), these equations often have fewer equations than unknowns and seek integer lattice points on algebraic curves or surfaces. Linear Diophantine equations (e.g., ax + by = c) and exponential variants are studied in Diophantine analysis. An **algebraic number** is a root of a non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients; numbers like π that are not algebraic are **transcendental**.

### Differential and Functional Equations
A **differential equation** relates a function to its derivatives, modeling processes involving rates of change in physics, chemistry, biology, and economics. **Ordinary differential equations (ODEs)** involve functions of one variable; **partial differential equations (PDEs)** involve multivariable functions and their partial derivatives, describing phenomena like heat, sound, and fluid flow. Solutions may be exact (closed-form) or approximated numerically. Other types include integral equations, difference equations (discrete analogs), and stochastic differential equations (involving random processes).

## Terms
- **Identity**: An equation true for all values of its variables.
- **Conditional equation**: An equation true only for specific values of its variables.
- **Extraneous solution**: A solution introduced by a transformation that does not satisfy the original equation.
- **Parameter**: A known quantity in an equation, often denoted by letters at the start of the alphabet (a, b, c).
- **Univariate/Multivariate**: Describing equations with one or multiple variables, respectively.
- **Algebraic number**: A number that is a root of a non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients.
- **Transcendental number**: A number that is not algebraic (e.g., π).
- **ODE/PDE**: Ordinary/partial differential equation, depending on the number of independent variables.
- **Diophantine equation**: A polynomial equation seeking integer solutions.
- **Parametric equation**: An equation expressing variables as functions of one or more parameters.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Whether a given Diophantine equation has integer solutions is, in general, undecidable (Hilbert's tenth problem, proven by Matiyasevich in 1970).
- The solvability of polynomial equations by radicals is fully characterized by Galois theory, but efficient root-finding for high-degree polynomials remains computationally challenging.
- The distinction between algebraic and transcendental numbers is well-defined, but proving specific numbers (beyond π and e) to be transcendental is often difficult.
- For differential equations, the existence and uniqueness of solutions depend on specific conditions, and many PDEs lack general solution methods, relying instead on numerical approximation.
