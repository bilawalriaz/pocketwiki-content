# Paper

## Overview
Paper is a thin sheet of matted cellulose fibers derived from lignocellulose, manufactured by dissolving pulp into a slurry that is drained and dried. Invented in China by the 2nd century BCE and attributed to Han court eunuch Cai Lun (105 CE), it spread via the Islamic world to medieval Europe, where water-powered mills mechanized production. The 19th-century invention of wood pulping (1843/1844) and the Second Industrial Revolution replaced rag paper with wood pulp as the dominant feedstock. Today, paper serves primarily as packaging and sanitary products rather than information storage, with Asia leading global production.

- **2021** — Global graphic paper production <100 million tonnes; other paper/paperboard >320 million tonnes.

## Body

### Precursors and Etymology
Paper was preceded by papyrus, parchment, vellum, barkcloth, palm leaves, and bamboo/wooden slips. The word "paper" derives from Latin *papyrus* (Greek *pápūros*), the Cyperus plant used in ancient Egypt. However, papyrus is a lamination of natural plant pith, while paper is manufactured from fibers altered by maceration. Papyrus was geographically limited to Egypt, more expensive, laborious, fragile, and moisture-sensitive, leading to its replacement by paper.

### Invention and Global Diffusion
Archaeological evidence places paper-like material in China by the 2nd century BCE. The pulp process is ascribed to Cai Lun (105 CE). The traditional narrative cites the 751 CE Battle of Talas—where captured Tang papermakers allegedly transmitted the craft to the Abbasid Caliphate—as the diffusion point. However, a 722 CE archive from Panjikent (Sogdian, Arabic, Chinese papers) and 313 CE Sogdian paper in Dunhuang prove paper existed in Central Asia centuries earlier. By the late 8th century, paper was made in Baghdad, entering Europe as *bagdatikos*. In the 13th century, Europe adopted the craft, building the first water-powered paper mills.

### Raw Material Transition: Rag to Wood
Before industrialization, the dominant paper was **rag paper**, made from discarded natural-fiber textiles collected by ragpickers. In 1844, Charles Fenerty and Friedrich Gottlob Keller independently developed wood pulping, decoupling production from textile waste. Coupled with the Second Industrial Revolution, this made **pulpwood paper** the dominant variety.

### Pulping Processes
**Pulp** is a lignocellulosic mixture of isolated fibers.
*   **Chemical pulping** separates lignin from cellulose using a cooking liquor, preserving fiber length. The product is **wood-free paper** (lignin-free, not tree-free). Three main processes exist: the **sulfite process** (1840s, dominant pre-WWII); the **kraft process** (1870s, dominant today), which generates heat for electricity and recovers inorganic reagents; and **soda pulping** for high-silicate materials like straw.
*   **Mechanical pulping** (thermomechanical/TMP and groundwood/GW) grinds or refines wood without removing lignin. Yield is high (>95%), but residual lignin causes yellowing and brittleness; short fibers produce weaker paper. It requires high electrical energy but costs less than chemical pulping.
*   **Recycling (deinked pulp)**: Invented by Justus Claproth (1774). Hydrogen bonds are broken mechanically in water. Most recycled paper includes virgin fiber for quality. Three classifications: **Mill broke** (internal waste, often not counted as reclaimed); **Preconsumer waste** (offcuts, unsold printed matter); **Postconsumer waste** (used office paper, newsprint, magazines). Recycled paper is generally weaker and less bright than virgin pulp.

### Paper Machine Production
Pulp is formed into a **paper web** on a machine (e.g., Fourdrinier). Water is removed by **pressing** (felt collects water) and **drying** (steam-heated can dryers >93°C / 200°F, reducing moisture to <6%). Machine-made paper is **wove paper** (uniform density, no laidlines). **Laidlines** (close, regular lines) and **chainlines** (wider spacing) appear on handmade paper from wire/bamboo moulds; handmade sheets also show **deckle edges** (feathery borders). **Calendering** polishes surfaces. **Sizing** applies a layer (e.g., calcium carbonate, kaolin) to alter feel and prevent ink spread. Sheets are cut **long-grain** (grain parallel to longer dimension).

### Types, Thickness, and Weight
Thickness (caliper): 0.07–0.18 mm. Weight metrics differ regionally:
*   **US (basis weight)**: Weight of a ream (500 sheets) at "basic size" before cutting. Printing paper: 20–32 lb; Cover stock: 68 lb; Card stock: ≥110 lb.
*   **ISO 216 (grammage)**: Grams per square metre (g/m² or gsm). Printing: 60–120 gsm; Card: >160 gsm. ISO sizes (A0 = 1 m²; A1 = half A0, etc.) based on surface area, adopted Germany 1922.
Density ranges from 250 kg/m³ (tissue) to 1500 kg/m³ (specialty); printing paper ~800 kg/m³. Heavier grammage correlates with higher perceived quality in sensory marketing.

**Seven categories**: Printing; Wrapping (wax, kraft); Writing (ledger, bond); Blotting (little/no size); Drawing (rough, cartridge); Handmade (decorative, Japanese, no grain direction); Specialty (cigarette, toilet tissue, industrial).

### Applications
Information storage share fell from 0.33% (1986) to 0.007% (2007) despite absolute capacity rising 8.7 to 19.4 petabytes. Postal letters were <0.05% of telecom capacity (1986), declining sharply with digital tech. Paper remains vital in visual arts (2D/3D shapes, collage, watercolor) and has evolved into a structural material for furniture.

### Environmental Impact
Consumption rose 400% in 40 years; 35% of harvested trees used for paper. Old-growth logging is <10% of pulp but highly controversial. Paper is ~40% of US waste (71.6 million tons/year); average US office worker prints 31 pages/day; ~16 billion paper cups used annually in US.
**Bleaching**: Elemental chlorine produces chlorinated dioxins (persistent organic pollutants under Stockholm Convention). Dioxins are carcinogenic, bioaccumulate in fatty tissues, and cause reproductive, developmental, immune, and hormonal harm; >90% human exposure via food chain. Industry emitted ~1% of global greenhouse gases (2010), ~0.9% (2012).

### Current Production Trends
FAO 2021 data: Asia surpassed North America as top producing continent. Graphic paper (printing/writing) production continues post-2000s decline (<100 million tonnes). **Paperboard** (cardboard, sanitary products) soared >320 million tonnes, driven by e-commerce since 2010s and boosted further by COVID-19 lockdowns.

## Terms
- ****Lignocellulose**** — The primary raw material of paper, composed of lignin and cellulose fibers.
- ****Pulp**** — A lignocellulosic mixture of isolated fibers suspended in water (slurry) before sheet formation.
- ****Lignin**** — An organic polymer in wood that binds cellulose fibers; causes yellowing/brittleness if retained.
- ****Wood-free paper**** — Paper from chemical pulp where lignin is removed; does not imply tree-free.
- ****Kraft process**** — Dominant chemical pulping method (1870s) using heat-generating reaction to produce energy and recover chemicals.
- ****Mechanical pulp**** — Pulp made by grinding/refining wood without lignin removal; high yield but weak, non-archival paper.
- ****Deinking**** — Process (invented 1774) to remove printing inks from recycled paper fibers.
- ****Laidlines / Chainlines**** — Texture marks on handmade paper: close lines (laidlines) perpendicular to wider lines (chainlines) from the mould wires.
- ****Wove paper**** — Machine-made paper with uniform density and no laidlines, formed on wire mesh.
- ****Grammage (gsm)**** — Paper weight in grams per square metre (ISO 216 standard).
- ****Paperboard**** — Heavy paper-based material (>160 gsm) including cardboard and sanitary products.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Transmission to Islamic world**: The Battle of Talas (751 CE) narrative conflicts with archaeological evidence (Panjikent 722 CE, Dunhuang 313 CE) showing paper established in Central Asia earlier.
*   **Old-growth logging**: Represents <10% of pulp but remains a focal point of environmental controversy regarding biodiversity and carbon storage.
*   **Bleaching chemistry**: Elemental chlorine produces regulated dioxins; industry shift to elemental chlorine-free (ECF) or totally chlorine-free (TCF) processes is an ongoing mitigation effort not detailed in source.
*   **Recycling limits**: Fibers degrade each cycle; deinked pulp is generally equal or lower quality than source, requiring virgin fiber input for strength.
