# Byzantine Empire

## Overview
The Byzantine Empire was the medieval continuation of the Roman Empire centred on Constantinople, surviving the 5th-century fall of the West to endure until the Ottoman conquest of 1453. Its citizens called themselves "Romans" (*Romaioi*); the term "Byzantine" was coined by later scholars. The empire preserved Roman law and administration while adopting Greek as its primary language and Christianity as its state religion. It acted as a cultural and military shield for Europe against Persian, Arab, and Turkish expansion, transmitting classical knowledge to the Islamic world and Renaissance Italy. Its history is defined by cycles of territorial loss—particularly the 7th-century Arab conquests and the 1204 Fourth Crusade—and recovery under dynasties like the Macedonians and Komnenoi, before a final two-century decline into a rump state.

## Timeline
- **324–337** — Constantine I moves capital to Constantinople; legalises Christianity.
- **379–395** — Theodosius I makes Christianity the state religion; last emperor to rule both halves.
- **476** — Fall of Western Roman Empire; Eastern Empire continues.
- **527–565** — Reign of Justinian I: *Corpus Juris Civilis* codified; Hagia Sophia built; western Mediterranean reconquered; plague begins (541).
- **630s–640s** — Arab conquests permanently remove Syria, Egypt, and North Africa; loss of ~75% of revenue.
- **717–718** — Leo III repels Arab siege of Constantinople; Iconoclasm begins.
- **867–1056** — Macedonian dynasty: territorial expansion, cultural renaissance, Basil II's victory over Bulgaria (1014).
- **1071** — Battle of Manzikert: Seljuks capture Anatolia; Norman capture of Bari ends Italian presence.
- **1081–1180** — Komnenian restoration: Alexios I, John II, Manuel I recover regional authority; First Crusade (1095).
- **1204** — Fourth Crusade sacks Constantinople; empire fragments into Latin and Greek successor states.
- **1261** — Michael VIII Palaiologos recaptures Constantinople; empire reduced to regional power.
- **1453** — Ottoman Sultan Mehmed II captures Constantinople; Constantine XI dies; empire ends.

## Body

### Nomenclature and Identity
The empire’s inhabitants identified as *Romaioi* (Romans) and their state as *Rhomanía* (Romanland). The label "Byzantine" derives from *Byzantion*, the Greek settlement preceding Constantinople, and was applied only to the city’s residents in antiquity. Western Europeans, asserting their own Roman legitimacy via the Papacy and the Holy Roman Empire (after 800), called the empire "Greece" and its people "Greeks." The term "Byzantine Empire" gained currency only after the 15th-century fall, popularised by scholars like Hieronymus Wolf; some modern historians reject it as anachronistic and prejudicial.

### Political History: Dynastic Cycles
**Foundations (324–518).** Constantine I refounded Byzantium as Constantinople (330), a strategically superior capital near wealthy eastern provinces. He introduced the gold *solidus*, stabilising currency for centuries. Theodosius I (379–395) established Nicene Christianity as the state religion and was the last ruler of a united empire. The East survived the 5th-century crises that destroyed the West—avoiding barbarian warlord rule through diplomacy (e.g., Zeno redirecting Theodoric the Ostrogoth to Italy) and Anastasius I’s fiscal reforms (abolishing the *chrysargyron* tax).

**Apogee and Crisis (518–717).** Justinian I (527–565) briefly restored Mediterranean unity, codifying law in the *Corpus Juris Civilis* and building the Hagia Sophia. However, the Plague of Justinian (541), war with Persia, and the Lombard invasion of Italy overextended resources. The 7th-century Arab conquests shattered the empire, permanently removing the wealthy Levant and Egypt and cutting revenue by roughly three-quarters. The empire survived by retreating into Anatolia, reorganising provinces into military districts (*themata*), and repelling Arab sieges of Constantinople (674–678, 717–718) using Greek fire.

**Recovery and Fragmentation (718–1204).** The Isaurian dynasty stabilised the frontiers but introduced Iconoclasm (banning religious icons), causing internal strife. The Macedonian dynasty (867–1056) oversaw a two-century renaissance: Basil II (976–1025) annexed Bulgaria and expanded to the Euphrates, but neglected administrative reform. Post-1025, civil wars and new enemies (Seljuks, Pechenegs, Normans) eroded the position. The disaster at Manzikert (1071) opened Anatolia to Turkish settlement. The Komnenoi (1081–1180) restored regional power through diplomacy, the *pronoia* system (land grants for military service), and Crusader cooperation, but Manuel I’s defeat at Myriokephalon (1176) ended offensive capability. Dynastic infighting invited the Fourth Crusade, which sacked Constantinople in 1204.

**Final Centuries (1204–1453).** The empire fragmented into the Empire of Nicaea, Empire of Trebizond, and Despotate of Epirus. Michael VIII Palaiologos recovered Constantinople (1261) but faced insurmountable problems: civil wars (1320–1328, 1341–1354), the rise of the Ottomans, reliance on Venetian/Genoese naval power, and population collapse. The Ottomans took Adrianople (1369), Thessalonica (1387), and besieged Constantinople (1394–1402, lifted only by Timur’s intervention). By 1453, the city’s population had fallen to ~50,000; Mehmed II’s final siege ended the empire on 29 May. Constantinople remained the largest and wealthiest city in Europe until the 13th century, when it was overtaken by Paris.

### Structures of the State
**Governance.** Diocletian and Constantine separated civil and military administration. After the 7th-century crises, the *theme system* replaced older provinces: *strategos* (generals) governed districts, supported by soldier-farmers. Central power concentrated in the emperor and court; the Senate became ceremonial. Cities lost autonomy as warfare centralised authority.

**Law.** Roman law was codified in the *Codex Theodosianus* (438) and definitively in Justinian’s *Corpus Juris Civilis* (529–534). The shift to Greek legislation (*Novellae*) marked the transition to Byzantine law. Leo III’s *Ekloge* (741) emphasised humanity and inspired practical provincial codes (Farmers’, Sailors’, Soldiers’ Law). Leo VI’s *Basilika* (60 books, c. 900) became the standard Byzantine code; Constantine Harmenopoulos’ *Hexabiblos* (1345) summarised it for late-era use.

**Christianity and the Church.** Constantine’s patronage integrated Christianity into the state. Theological disputes (Arianism, Nestorianism, Monophysitism) split eastern churches; Chalcedonian Orthodoxy dominated imperial territory, while Miaphysite and Nestorian communities largely fell under Muslim rule after the 7th century. The East–West Schism (1054) formalised centuries of divergence over papal authority, the *Filioque* clause, and ritual differences. The Orthodox Church became a pillar of Byzantine identity and a key diplomatic tool.

### Warfare
**Military Evolution.** The late 6th-century army comprised ~150,000 professional *comitatenses* (field armies) and ~195,000 *limitanei* (frontier garrisons). The 7th-century collapse forced a shift to *thematic* militias: part-time soldier-farmers defending specific districts. By the 11th century, professional *tagmata* regiments (including the Varangian Guard) replaced thematic forces for offensive operations. The late 10th-century army (~140,000) was perhaps the highest-quality force the empire produced, but its defensive capacities were neglected during the 11th-century civil wars, leading to the loss of Anatolia to the Seljuks. Post-1081, the *pronoia* system funded a mercenary-heavy army. The Palaiologan army never exceeded 5,000 field troops; fewer than 8,000 defended Constantinople in 1453.

**Naval Power.** The navy reorganised into provincial fleets (*themata*) using *dromons* armed with Greek fire (an incendiary weapon burning on water). It dominated the eastern Mediterranean until the 11th century, when reliance on Venice and Genoa eroded indigenous capability. The fleet was disbanded in 1284; 14th-century rebuilding attempts were blocked by Genoa.

**Diplomacy.** Resource scarcity made diplomacy primary. Tools included embassies, client management, political marriage, bribery, espionage, and assassination. The *limitrophe* system (buffer client states) protected frontiers until its 11th-century collapse. After 1204, diplomacy and the Orthodox Church were vital for the rump state’s survival.

### Society
**Demography.** Population peaked at ~27 million (540), fell to ~12 million (800) after plague and Arab conquests, recovered to ~18 million (1025), then collapsed to 3–5 million (1282) and 2 million (1312) after 1204. Constantinople shrank from ~500,000+ to 50,000 by 1453. Slavery declined legally and economically; *coloni* (tenant farmers) represented an intermediate dependency.

**Language.** Latin persisted in law and military until ~400; Greek replaced it in administration by Justinian’s time. The empire lost its linguistic diversity in the wars of the 7th and 8th centuries, becoming overwhelmingly Greek-speaking. *Diglossia* emerged: a wide gap between literary Attic Greek (written) and vernacular *Koine* (spoken). Late Palaiologan writers began using vernacular in verse (e.g., *Chronicle of the Morea*), foreshadowing modern Greek.

**Culture.** Feasting defined elite culture; the fork and salad dressing (oil/vinegar) spread west. Foods included *garos* (fish sauce), *baklava*, *paston* (cured meat), Feta, caviar, *tiropita*, *dolmades*, and *trachanas* soup. Sweet wines (Malvasia, Commandaria) and *retsina* were staples. Chariot racing (until 1204) was the longest continuous sport; *tzykanion* (polo) and jousting arrived via Crusaders/West; board games (*tavli*) grew popular.

### Economy
Geography and maritime dominance lowered transport costs, driving growth. Mature centres (Asia Minor, Egypt, Aegean) thrived despite insecurity. The 6th-century plagues and wars collapsed the ancient economy; cities became fortified, rural areas became hamlets. 9th-century revival brought agricultural growth and urban expansion. Italian merchants (Venice, Genoa, Pisa) captured international trade by the 11th–12th centuries. The fall of Constantinople during the Fourth Crusade in 1204 destroyed centuries of its wealth. Large landholdings were confiscated, and the empire fragmented into smaller rump states ruled by competing factions, making governance inefficient and increasing the costs of doing business. Italian merchants further dominated trade as the events of 1204 opened the Black Sea to Western merchants, permanently altering the empire's fortunes. The mixed economy (state intervention, public works, market liberalisation) remained adaptable but deteriorated under Ottoman pressure.

### Arts and Sciences
**Art and Architecture.** Subjects were Christian, non-naturalistic. Gold-ground mosaics (Hagia Sophia, San Vitale) were hallmarks. Justinian’s Hagia Sophia (532–537), by Isidore of Miletus and Anthemius of Tralles, established the pendentive dome model copied from Novgorod to Kiev. Iconoclasm (726–843) destroyed vast figurative art; Iconophiles restored veneration. Macedonian art (867–1056) standardised cross-in-square churches and symmetrical frontality (Hosios Loukas, Daphni). Komnenian art added emotional expression. Palaiologan works, looted in 1204, influenced the Italo-Byzantine style (Cimabue, Giotto).

**Literature.** Written in medieval Greek (Attic literary vs. Koine vernacular diglossia). Early period (330–650): synthesis of Hellenism, Christianity, Paganism (John Chrysostom, Procopius). "Byzantine Dark Ages" (650–800): minimal output. Macedonian Renaissance (800–1000): encyclopedism, hagiography reorganisation. 1000–1250: individualism, mysticism, romance (*Digenes Akritas*), Psellos, Prodromos. Late period: Western influence, Greek-to-Latin translations (Plethon, Bessarion), feeding Italian Renaissance.

**Music.** Eclectic roots: Christian plainsong, Jewish, ancient Greek. Sacred chant (monodic, unaccompanied, Greek) remains central to Eastern Orthodoxy. *Oktōēchos* (8 modes) governed composition from the 8th century. Notation evolved: *ekphonetic* (9th), *palaeo-Byzantine* (10th), "Round Notation" (12th, first fully diastematic). Major forms: *kontakion* (Romanos the Melodist), *kanōn* (Andrew of Crete), *sticheron* (Kassia). Palaiologan "kalophonic" style (John Koukouzeles) influenced post-Byzantine music. Secular music (organ, *aulos*, *lyra*, *tambouras*) was oral/improvised; no instrumental scores survive.

**Science and Technology.** Scholars transmitted classical knowledge to Islam and Italy, commenting on Greek, Islamic, Latin, and Hebrew texts. Key figures: Isidore of Miletus (Archimedes), Leo the Mathematician, John Philoponus (critique of Aristotle, influenced Galileo), Pedanius Dioscorides (pharmacology), Ptolemy (geography/astronomy, influenced Copernicus). Innovations: riding stirrup (mounted archery), specialised horseshoe, lateen sail, Greek fire (c. 674–678). Healthcare: pioneered the hospital as a curative institution, not just a place to die.

## Terms
- ****Romaioi**** — The self-designation of Byzantine citizens ("Romans"), reflecting their legal and cultural continuity with the Roman Empire.
- ****Theme (Thema)**** — A military-administrative district (7th–11th c.) where soldier-farmers held land in exchange for hereditary military service under a *strategos*.
- ****Corpus Juris Civilis**** — Justinian I’s 6th-century codification of Roman law (Codex, Digest, Institutes, Novellae), the foundation of Byzantine and later European civil law.
- ****Greek Fire**** — A petroleum-based incendiary weapon (c. 670s) projected by siphons; burned on water, decisive in naval defence of Constantinople.
- ****Iconoclasm**** — Imperial policy (726–843, with intervals) banning religious icons as idolatry; caused deep social division and massive art destruction.
- ****Pronoia**** — A grant of state revenue (often land taxes) to an individual in exchange for military service; feudal-like system dominant 11th–15th c.
- ****Diglossia**** — The coexistence of two language registers: high literary Attic Greek (written/formal) and vernacular Koine Greek (spoken/daily life).
- ****Varangian Guard**** — Elite imperial bodyguard regiment, initially Scandinavian/Rus, later Anglo-Saxon; loyal directly to the emperor.
- ****Limitrophe System**** — A defensive diplomatic network of client states on the frontiers acting as buffers against major powers (collapsed 11th c.).
- ****Third Rome**** — Russian doctrine (post-1453) claiming Moscow as the successor to Byzantine Orthodoxy and imperial legitimacy.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Foundation Date:** No consensus exists. Traditional dates centre on Constantine I (324/330) or Theodosius I (395); "Late Antiquity" scholars argue for a 7th/8th-century break; Geoffrey Greatrex believes that it is impossible to precisely date the foundation of the Byzantine Empire.
*   **Terminology:** Historians debate whether "Byzantine Empire" is a useful convention or a prejudicial anachronism obscuring the state’s Roman self-identity.
*   **Economic Trajectory:** The extent of the 6th-century plague’s demographic impact vs. the 7th-century Arab conquests as drivers of the "ancient economy’s" collapse is contested.
*   **Military Decline:** Whether the 11th-century collapse resulted from systemic fiscal failure, the *theme* system’s obsolescence, civil war, or the structural shift to mercenary *tagmata* is debated.
*   **1204 as Turning Point:** Scholars dispute whether the Fourth Crusade’s sack was a recoverable catastrophe or the definitive structural break making 1453 inevitable.
*   **Scientific Originality:** The empire’s role is framed either as passive preservation/transmission or as active innovation (e.g., Philoponus’ physics, hospital medicine), complicated by applying modern "science" definitions.
