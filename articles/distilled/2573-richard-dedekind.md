# Richard Dedekind

## Overview
Richard Dedekind (1831–1916) was a German mathematician whose work fundamentally shaped modern number theory, abstract algebra, and the foundations of mathematics. He is best known for defining real numbers via "Dedekind cuts" and for pioneering concepts in set theory and the axiomatic foundation of arithmetic, influencing later mathematicians like Cantor, Frege, and Peano.

## Timeline
- **1831** — Born in Braunschweig, Germany.
- **1850** — Entered the University of Göttingen, studying under Gauss and Stern.
- **1852** — Received doctorate; studied in Berlin with Riemann.
- **1858** — Began teaching at the Polytechnic school in Zürich.
- **1862** — Returned to Braunschweig to teach at the newly upgraded Technische Hochschule.
- **1872** — Published *Stetigkeit und irrationale Zahlen* ("Continuity and irrational numbers"), introducing the Dedekind cut.
- **1879–1894** — Published editions of Dirichlet's lectures, introducing the concept of an ideal.
- **1888** — Published *Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen?* ("What are numbers and what are they good for?"), proposing axioms for natural numbers.
- **1916** — Died in Braunschweig.

## Body

### Dedekind Cut and Real Numbers
While teaching calculus, Dedekind developed the **Dedekind cut** to rigorously define irrational numbers. A cut partitions the rational numbers into two classes: a "lower" class (all numbers less than a given point) and an "upper" class (all numbers greater). This construction showed that every point on the number line corresponds to a number (rational or irrational), eliminating gaps and establishing the **completeness** of the real number continuum. He published this idea in 1872.

### Set Theory and Foundations of Arithmetic
Dedekind provided the first precise definition of an **infinite set**: a set is infinite if it is "similar" (equinumerous) to a proper subset of itself. This work anticipated Georg Cantor's development of set theory. In 1888, he proposed an axiomatic foundation for the natural numbers based on the primitive notions of "1" and the "successor function." Giuseppe Peano later formulated a simpler, equivalent set of axioms that became standard.

### Algebraic Number Theory and Ideals
While editing Dirichlet's lectures on number theory, Dedekind studied algebraic number fields. He introduced the concept of an **ideal**—a subset of algebraic integers closed under addition and multiplication by any integer in the set—to generalize Kummer's "ideal numbers" and solve problems unique factorization. This work, published in supplements to Dirichlet's lectures (1879, 1894), became foundational for **ring theory**. He also applied ideals to Riemann surfaces with H. M. Weber, providing an algebraic proof of the Riemann–Roch theorem.

## Terms
- **Dedekind cut**: A partition of the rational numbers into two sets, used to define real numbers, including irrationals.
- **Completeness**: The property of the real number line having no "gaps"; every bounded set has a least upper bound.
- **Similar (sets)**: Two sets are similar if there is a one-to-one correspondence between them (equinumerous).
- **Ideal**: In ring theory, a subset of a ring closed under addition and under multiplication by any element of the ring.
- **Ring**: A set equipped with addition and multiplication operations satisfying certain axioms (term later coined by Hilbert).
- **Axiomatic foundation**: A system of mathematics built from a small set of assumed truths (axioms) and logical rules.
- **Successor function**: A function that maps a number to the next one in a sequence (e.g., n → n+1).

## Debates and Open Questions
- Dedekind's work on infinite sets and the foundations of arithmetic anticipated and influenced the logicism of Frege and Russell, though the exact nature of his philosophical commitments is debated.
- A fractured friendship with Georg Cantor arose from allegations that Cantor plagiarized Dedekind's proof on infinity, a claim supported by recent findings of their correspondence.
