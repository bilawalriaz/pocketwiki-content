# Soul

## Overview
The soul is the immaterial essence of a living being, widely believed across cultures to be immortal and distinct from the material body. Religions conceptualize it differently: Christianity and Islam affirm an eternal soul facing divine judgment; Hinduism and Sikhism view the *Ātman* as a divine spark seeking liberation (*moksha*) through cycles of rebirth governed by karma; Buddhism uniquely denies a permanent self (*anattā*), teaching that the "soul" is a transient aggregate of impermanent processes; Jainism holds that every living entity possesses an eternal *jīva* bound by karma until liberation. Philosophy has debated the soul’s nature since antiquity: Plato and Socrates argued for an immortal, tripartite soul; Aristotle defined it as the "first actuality" of an organized body, inseparable from it; medieval thinkers like Avicenna and Aquinas synthesized these views with theology, arguing for the soul’s subsistence after death; modern philosophy (Kant) limits knowledge of the soul to a transcendental "I," while contemporary debates center on mind-body dualism (interactionism, parallelism, epiphenomenalism) versus physicalism. Psychology and anthropology confirm the belief in a soul is a cross-cultural cognitive universal.

## Timeline
- **8th century** — Earliest attestations of Old English *sāwl* (Vespasian Psalter), meaning 'self', 'life', or 'animate existence'.
- **c. 5th–4th century BCE** — Socrates and Plato establish the soul (*psyche*) as the immortal, rational essence of a person; Aristotle defines it as the "first actuality" of a natural body in *De Anima*.
- **c. 5th century BCE** — Buddhism emerges, teaching *anattā* (non-self) and rejecting eternalism and annihilationism.
- **c. 1st century CE** — Early Christian texts (Paul, Hebrews) distinguish *psyche* (soul) and *pneuma* (spirit); belief in immortal soul and resurrection solidifies.
- **c. 11th century** — Avicenna (Ibn Sina) distinguishes soul and spirit; argues soul's immortality follows from its nature; "Floating Man" thought experiment.
- **c. 13th century** — Thomas Aquinas synthesizes Aristotelian hylomorphism with Christian doctrine: soul as subsistent form, immortal, *tota in toto corpore*.
- **11th–13th centuries** — Islamic *falsafa* (al-Farabi, Ibn Rushd) and *kalam* (al-Ghazali, al-Baydawi) debate rational vs. non-rational soul, immortality, and hierarchy of intellects.
- **18th century** — Immanuel Kant identifies the soul as the transcendental "I"; critiques rational psychology, arguing the soul's nature is unknowable.
- **1901** — Duncan MacDougall conducts "21 grams experiment" attempting to measure soul's weight; later dismissed as unscientific.
- **20th century** — Contemporary philosophy of mind formalizes dualist theories (interactionism, parallelism, epiphenomenalism) vs. physicalism/idealism.

## Body

### Etymology and Cross-Cultural Universality
The English *soul* derives from Old English *sāwl* (8th century), cognate with Germanic terms (Gothic *saiwala*, Old Norse *sála*). Early usages denoted 'self', 'life', or the immaterial thinking aspect contrasted with the body. Anthropologists and psychologists (Rank, Bering, Humphrey, Goetz) find that belief in a soul-body distinction is a cross-cultural universal; humans are intuitive "soul-body substance dualists" from infancy.

### Buddhism: The Doctrine of Non-Self (*Anattā*)
Buddhism fundamentally rejects a permanent soul (*ātman*). The Buddha refuted both **eternalism** (soul survives forever) and **annihilationism** (soul dies with body), identifying both as craving-driven "soul-theories." The five aggregates (*skandhas*)—form, sensation, perception, mental formations, consciousness—are impermanent (*anicca*) and empty (*śūnyatā*); no enduring self exists within them. Rebirth occurs via a causal stream of consciousness (*cetanā*), not a transmigrating entity. The *Milinda's Questions* (chariot analogy) illustrates the **Two Truths**: conventionally, a "person" exists as a label for aggregates; ultimately, no essence is found. The Buddha left the soul's existence as an "unanswerable question," debated as either irrelevant to enlightenment or logically invalid (presupposing a self).

### Christianity: Immortality, Resurrection, and Origin
Christianity teaches an eternal soul facing immediate particular judgment at death (heaven/hell), followed by a future bodily resurrection and reunification in a renewed creation (Revelation 21–22). Paul distinguishes *psychē* (soul/*nephesh*) and *pneuma* (spirit/*ruah*), fueling debate between **dichotomy** (body/soul) and **trichotomy** (body/soul/spirit). The **origin of the soul** is disputed: *creationism* (God creates each soul at conception), *traducianism* (soul generated by parents), or *pre-existence*. **Corruptionism** (human ceases at death, soul persists) vs. **Survivalism** (human and soul both persist) divides Thomists; a middle view posits the separated soul as an "incomplete person."

### Hinduism and Jainism: *Ātman*, *Jīva*, and Liberation
In Hinduism (especially Vedanta), *Ātman* is the true self, identical with **Brahman** (ultimate reality) in Advaita; liberation (*moksha*) requires self-knowledge (*ātma jñāna*). *Jīva* denotes the individual embodied soul. Jainism posits that *every* living being (plants, bacteria, humans) has an eternal *jīva* distinct from *ajīva* (non-soul: matter, time, space). The *jīva* cycles through *saṃsāra* bound by karma; liberation (*mukta*) manifests the soul's innate qualities fully. *Arihants* are souls victorious over passions while still embodied.

### Islam: *Rūḥ*, *Nafs*, and Philosophical Schools
Islam distinguishes **rūḥ** (divine spirit/breath of life) and **nafs** (self/psyche/disposition). The Quran describes three *nafs* types: *ammāra* (commanding evil/appetite), *lawwāma* (accusing/conscience), *muṭmaʾinna* (tranquil/paradise-bound).
*   **Falsafa (Philosophy):** Following Aristotle, philosophers (al-Farabi, Ibn Sina, Ibn Rushd) divided the soul into non-rational (vegetative, animal—mortal) and rational (practical/theoretical intellect). They debated the rational soul's immortality: Ibn Sina argued it is uncreated and immortal; Ibn Rushd held the entire soul perishes.
*   **Kalam (Theology):** Al-Ghazali defined the human as a spiritual substance (*jawhar rūḥānī*), identifying the tranquil soul with the Quranic *nafs muṭmaʾinna*. He viewed *nafs* as a vehicle for the soul, influenced by angels (*ilhām*) or devils (*waswās*). Al-Baydawi constructed a cosmological hierarchy of Intellects and souls (heavenly, earthly, jinn).
*   **Ismailism:** Neo-Platonic cosmology; the soul is immaterial, trapped in matter. Salvation requires discarding sensual pleasure for intellectual ascent. Nasir Khusraw viewed the rational soul as a potential angel or demon, actualized by obedience or sin.

### Judaism: *Nefesh*, *Ruach*, *Neshamah*, and Kabbalah
Hebrew terms (*nefesh*, *ruach*, *neshamah*, *chayah*, *yechidah*) denote aspects of life/breath, not a Greek-style dualistic "soul." Early Judaism lacked an immortal separable soul; this view emerged by the Common Era. The Talmud maintains psychophysical unity while allowing simultaneous spiritual existence. **Kabbalah** systematizes five soul levels corresponding to worlds: *Nefesh* (instinct), *Ruach* (emotion), *Neshamah* (intellect), *Chayah* (divine transcendence), *Yechidah* (essence bound to God). *Gilgul* (reincarnation) involves the "animal soul" (*nefesh habehamit*). Some traditions locate the soul in the *luz* bone (atlas or sacrum).

### Shamanism: Soul Dualism and Soul Flight
**Soul dualism** (body soul / free soul) is central to shamanistic traditions globally (Austronesian, Chinese, Tibetan, African, Native American, Ancient Egyptian). The **body soul** (*nawa* in Proto-Austronesian) governs vital functions, located in the abdomen/liver/heart. The **free soul** (*qaNiCu*) resides in the head, wanders during sleep/trance/death, and can be stolen or lost ("soul loss"), causing illness/insanity. Shamans heal by retrieving the free soul in the spirit world. Some groups (Tagbanwa, Inuit) posit more than two souls with specialized functions.

### Sikhism: *Ātman*, Karma, and Grace
The soul (*Ātman*) is pure consciousness, eternal, and connected to the Divine (*Paramatman*). It transmigrates under **karma** and **hukam** (divine order), obscured by ego (*haumai*) and attachment. Liberation (*mukti*) ends the cycle via meditation on God's Name (*Nam Simran*) and remembrance of *Waheguru*, merging into *Sach Khand* (Realm of Truth). Ethical living (*Kirat Karo*, *Vand Chhako*, *seva*) aligns the soul with divine will, though grace (*nadar*) ultimately transcends karmic limits.

### Taoism: *Hun*, *Po*, and the Tao
The soul is a dynamic energy balance, not a static entity. **Hun** (ethereal soul, *yang*) governs spirit/mind, departs at death. **Po** (corporeal soul, *yin*) governs instincts/body, dissolves with the corpse. Death is *shijie* ("release from the corpse"); post-mortem fate varies (transformation, immortality, ascension). Texts (*Zhuangzi*, *Daodejing*) emphasize the self's unity with the **Tao** (universal principle), dissolving boundaries: "Heaven and earth were born at the same time I was, and the ten thousand things are one with me."

### Ancient Greek Philosophy: Socrates, Plato, Aristotle
*   **Socrates/Plato:** The soul (*psyche*) is the immortal essence, source of life (self-motion) and mind. **Tripartite soul**: *Logos* (reason, head), *Thymos* (spirit/anger, chest), *Eros* (appetite, stomach)—mirroring the ideal state's classes. *Phaedo* argues for immortality; *Republic* unifies life-giving, moral, and cognitive functions.
*   **Aristotle:** Soul is the **"first actuality" of a naturally organized body** (form to body's matter). It is not separable (except perhaps the *active intellect*). Three hierarchical grades: *Bios* (nutritive—plants), *Zoë* (sensitive/motive—animals), *Psuchē* (rational—humans). Faculties exercised = "second actuality." *De Anima* III.5 controversially suggests the *active intellect* may be immortal and separable.

### Medieval Philosophy: Avicenna, Aquinas
*   **Avicenna (Ibn Sina):** Distinguished soul and spirit. Soul's immortality is a consequence of its nature, not a purpose. **"Floating Man"**: suspended without sensation, one still grasps "I exist," proving the soul is a primary substance, not dependent on body. Viewed human soul as the tenth intellect.
*   **Thomas Aquinas:** Synthesized Aristotle and Avicenna. Soul = first actuality of living body; *tota in toto corpore* (whole in whole, whole in every part). Rational soul is a **subsistent form** (not matter/form composite), thus incorruptible and immortal. Operations independent of organs (intellection) prove it can exist without body. Affirmed divine infusion of soul, particular judgment, and bodily resurrection.

### Modern Philosophy: Kant and Contemporary Debates
*   **Immanuel Kant:** The soul is the transcendental **"I"** (unity of apperception). We cannot *know* the soul as substance (requires intuition, which we lack), only *think* it. Rational psychology's paralogisms (substance, simplicity, identity, distinction from world) are errors conflating conceptual subject with cognized object. Soul's immateriality is a necessary practical postulate, not theoretical knowledge.
*   **Contemporary Philosophy of Mind:** Three dualist theories address mind-body relation: **Interactionism** (two-way causation, intuitive but faces "interaction problem"); **Parallelism** (mental/physical run in sync, no causation); **Epiphenomenalism** (physical causes mental, mental is causally inert "shadow"). **Non-dualist** alternatives: **Physicalism** (all is physical) and **Idealism** (mind grounds reality).

### Psychology and Parapsychology
*   **Psychology:** Otto Rank ("Seelenglaube") traced soul-belief through animistic (creating soul), religious (representing soul), and scientific (knowing soul) eras. Ernest Becker linked "soul" to Kierkegaard's "self" (total personality/ground of being). Cognitive science (Bering, Humphrey) posits innate dualism.
*   **Parapsychology:** Duncan MacDougall's 1901 "21 grams experiment" weighed dying patients; one lost 21.3g at death. Dismissed by physicists (Park) and psychologists (Hood) as unreliable, non-replicable, and lacking scientific merit.

## Terms
- ****Ātman**** — Sanskrit for "inner self" or "soul"; in Hinduism, the true self identical with Brahman (Advaita) or the eternal essence seeking *moksha*.
- ****Anattā (Anātman)**** — Buddhist doctrine of "non-self"; denial of a permanent, unchanging soul or essence within the five aggregates.
- ****First Actuality**** — Aristotelian definition of the soul: the structural organization (form) of a natural body that enables its characteristic life functions (second actuality).
- ****Hun / Po**** — Taoist dual souls: *Hun* (ethereal, *yang*, spiritual consciousness, departs at death); *Po* (corporeal, *yin*, bodily instincts, dissolves with corpse).
- ****Jīva**** — In Jainism and Hinduism, the individual living soul/entity; in Jainism, eternal but bound by karma until liberation (*mukta*).
- ****Nafs / Rūḥ**** — Islamic terms: *Nafs* = self, psyche, disposition (three stages: commanding, accusing, tranquil); *Rūḥ* = divine spirit, breath of life.
- ****Subsistent Form**** — Aquinas's term for the human rational soul: a form that exists in its own right (not merely inhering in matter), making it immortal.
- ****Transcendental "I"**** — Kant's designation for the soul as the necessary unity of consciousness ("I think"), which cannot be known as an object but is a condition of knowledge.
- ****Tota in toto corpore**** — Latin: "whole in the whole body"; Aquinas's doctrine that the soul is fully present in the entire body and in every part simultaneously.
- ****Two Truths (Buddhism)**** — Conventional truth (*samvṛti-satya*): persons exist as designations for aggregates; Ultimate truth (*paramārtha-satya*): no inherent essence or self exists.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Buddhism:** Did the Buddha deny *all* self-concepts or only the *ātman* defined by aggregates? Is the "unanswerable question" silence due to irrelevance, logical invalidity, or an inexpressible transcendent self (Mahayana *tathāgatagarbha* debate)?
*   **Christianity:** When does the soul enter the embryo (conception, animation, birth)? Is the intermediate state corruptionist (human ceases), survivalist (human persists), or an "incomplete person"?
*   **Islamic Philosophy:** Is the rational soul created in time (al-Ghazali) or eternal/uncreated (Ibn Sina)? Does the *active intellect* survive individually (Ibn Sina) or only universally (Ibn Rushd/Averroism)?
*   **Aristotle:** Does *De Anima* III.5 teach the immortality of the *active intellect* (and personal survival) or only its divine, impersonal eternity?
*   **Mind-Body Problem:** Can interactionism explain *how* non-physical mind moves physical body? Does epiphenomenalism render mental causation illusory? Is physicalism compatible with qualia/subjectivity (the "hard problem")?
*   **Empirical Verification:** Can the soul's existence or weight be scientifically measured (MacDougall), or is it categorically outside empirical investigation (Kant, modern consensus)?
