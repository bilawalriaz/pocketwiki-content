# Uruguay River

## Overview
The Uruguay River is a major South American river that flows north to south, forming international boundaries between Brazil, Argentina, and Uruguay. It is economically vital for hydroelectric power and was the site of a significant international environmental and diplomatic conflict over industrial pollution.

## Timeline
- **c. 1975** — A treaty between Argentina and Uruguay established a framework for joint decisions on matters related to the Uruguay River.
- **1947** — Turvo State Park was created in Brazil to protect the area around the Moconá Falls.
- **April 2005** — Residents of Gualeguaychú, Argentina, began protesting proposed pulp mills on the river.
- **December 2005** — Argentine protesters began intermittent blockades of international bridges linking Argentina and Uruguay.
- **2006** — Argentina brought its dispute with Uruguay over the pulp mills to the International Court of Justice (ICJ).
- **October 2, 2009** — The ICJ completed hearings on the pulp mill dispute.
- **2010** — The ICJ ruled that Uruguay failed to inform Argentina about the mills but that they did not pollute the river.
- **2010** — Argentina and Uruguay created a joint commission to coordinate activities on the river.

## Body

### Course and Geography
The Uruguay River is approximately 1,838 km long, originating in the Serra do Mar of Brazil at about 200 meters above sea level. Its upper course through Rio Grande do Sul is uneven and not navigable. A distinctive feature is a submerged canyon formed during the Ice Age, up to 100 meters deep, which is visible at the Moconá Falls. These falls are unusual for being parallel to the river, are 10-12 meters high, and are protected on the Brazilian side by Turvo State Park. The river becomes navigable from around Salto Chico and, together with the Paraná River, forms the Río de la Plata estuary. Its main tributary is the Río Negro. The river's drainage basin covers 365,000 km².

### Economic Use and Infrastructure
The river's primary economic use is hydroelectric generation, with major dams including the Salto Grande Dam and the Itá Dam. It is crossed by five international bridges connecting Argentina with Brazil and Uruguay. The river's average discharge is measured at Salto Grande.

### The Cellulose Plant Conflict
A major dispute arose between Argentina and Uruguay over the construction of cellulose processing plants by European companies at Fray Bentos, Uruguay, opposite the Argentine town of Gualeguaychú. Argentina alleged Uruguay violated their 1975 treaty by not obtaining joint approval and argued the plants would pollute the shared river. Protests began in 2005, leading to bridge blockades and a diplomatic crisis. The case was taken to the International Court of Justice. In 2010, the ICJ ruled that while Uruguay failed in its duty to inform Argentina, the mills did not pollute the river, so ordering their closure was unjustified. The two nations subsequently formed a joint commission for river coordination.

## Terms
- **Drainage basin**: The area of land where all water drains into a common outlet, here the Uruguay River.
- **Estuary**: A partially enclosed coastal body of water where freshwater from a river mixes with saltwater from the ocean, like the Río de la Plata.
- **Hydroelectricity**: Electricity generated from the energy of falling or flowing water, typically using dams.
- **International Court of Justice (ICJ)**: The principal judicial organ of the United Nations, which settles legal disputes between states.
- **Mesopotamia**: In Argentina, the region between the Paraná and Uruguay rivers.
- **Pulp mill**: A factory that produces wood pulp for paper manufacturing.
- **Submerged canyon**: A deep, narrow valley carved into the riverbed, often by ancient geological processes.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source highlights a key scholarly and diplomatic debate: the environmental impact of the pulp mills. Argentina and local protesters claimed the plants would pollute the river, while Uruguay and the subsequent ICJ ruling concluded the mills did not deposit a significant amount of toxins. This disagreement over scientific evidence and treaty obligations was central to the conflict.
