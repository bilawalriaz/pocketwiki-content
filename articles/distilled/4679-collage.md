# Collage

## Overview
Collage is a visual art technique where a new whole is created by assembling different forms, such as paper, photographs, or found objects, onto a flat surface. It became a defining method of modern art in the early 20th century, fundamentally challenging traditional notions of painting and representation by incorporating fragments of everyday life and culture.

## Timeline
- **10th century** — Paper collage first known in Japan, used by calligraphers on poems.
- **15th-16th c.** — Gold leaf and gemstones applied to religious images in Gothic cathedrals.
- **17th-18th c.** — Decoupage becomes popular in Europe, with advanced techniques developed.
- **1860s** — Early Victorian photocollage practices documented.
- **1912** — Georges Braque and Pablo Picasso pioneer modern collage (*papier collé*).
- **1920s** — Kurt Schwitters experiments with wood collages.
- **1950s-60s** — Robert Rauschenberg creates "Combines," blending painting and sculpture.
- **1956** — Richard Hamilton creates the iconic pop art photomontage *Just what is it...*
- **1960s** — Romare Bearden uses photomontage to explore Black identity and history.
- **1978** — *Collage City* by Rowe and Koetter applies collage theory to architecture.
- **1990s-2000s** — Digital collage and sampling become prevalent in music and visual arts.

## Body

### Origins and Modernist Breakthrough
While collage-like techniques existed for centuries, such as 10th-century Japanese paper collage and 18th-century botanical "flower Mosaicks" by Mary Delany, the form is most associated with early 20th-century modernism. In 1912, Cubist painters Georges Braque and Pablo Picasso, using the term *papier collé*, began incorporating cut paper and other materials into their works. This was not mere decoration; the glued patches "collided with the surface plane of the painting," reexamining the relationship between painting and sculpture. Picasso's *Still Life with Chair Caning* (1912), which pasted oilcloth onto canvas, is a seminal example.

### Evolution and Major Movements
Collage techniques were adopted and transformed by subsequent art movements. Surrealists like Joseph Cornell used collage to create dream-like scenes, employing methods like cubomania (reassembling cut image squares). Pop Art artists, such as Tom Wesselmann and Andy Warhol, used collage and photomontage to incorporate imagery from mass media and popular culture. The technique also evolved into three-dimensional assemblage, pioneered by artists like Picasso and Marcel Duchamp with found objects, and later defined by Robert Rauschenberg's "Combines" in the 1950s.

### Photomontage and Digital Collage
Photomontage, collage made from photographs, became a distinct practice. A key early example is Richard Hamilton's 1956 pop art work. The technique was later used powerfully by artists like Romare Bearden. The advent of digital technology has transformed collage creation. Digital collage uses computer software like Photoshop to combine and manipulate images, enabling new forms of visual storytelling and becoming a standard tool in contemporary art and media.

### Collage in Other Contexts
The collage concept has influenced many fields. In architecture, Colin Rowe and Fred Koetter's 1978 book *Collage City* used it as a metaphor to challenge modernist uniformity. In music, sampling and sound collage became foundational to genres like hip-hop and electronic music, with albums like DJ Shadow's *Endtroducing.....* (1996) made entirely from preexisting recordings. Collage is also a common technique in children's book illustration, as seen in the work of Eric Carle.

## Terms
- **Collage**: A technique of art creation where a new whole is assembled from different forms, typically on a flat surface.
- **Assemblage**: The three-dimensional equivalent of collage, using combined objects to create a new form.
- **Papier collé**: A term coined by Braque and Picasso for collage using cut paper.
- **Photomontage**: A collage made specifically from photographs or parts of photographs.
- **Decoupage**: A technique of decorating objects by gluing cut-out paper images and applying multiple coats of varnish.
- **Cubomania**: A surrealist collage method where an image is cut into squares and reassembled randomly.
- **Combines**: A term coined by Robert Rauschenberg for works that blend painting and sculpture, often using assemblage.
- **Digital collage**: The use of computer tools to create collages by combining and transforming visual elements.
- **Derivative work**: A copyright term for a new work based on one or more preexisting works, which a collage may be considered.

## Debates and Open questions
There is scholarly debate about collage's true origins. While many institutions attribute its start to Picasso and Braque in 1912, evidence of early Victorian photocollage from the 1860s suggests earlier practice. Furthermore, the definition of collage is contested, particularly regarding works using natural, unaltered materials like driftwood. Some argue these lack the "semiotic collision" of culturally significant fragments central to the original Cubist concept. Copyright law also presents ongoing challenges, as the use of existing works in collage can conflict with derivative work protections, leading to legal disputes in fields like music sampling.
