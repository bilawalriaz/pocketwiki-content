# Biodiversity

## Overview
Biodiversity is the variability of life on Earth, measured across genetic, species, ecosystem, and phylogenetic levels. It is distributed unevenly, peaking in tropical forests—which cover under 20% of land but hold ~50% of species—and declining toward the poles. Since the Cambrian explosion (~540 million years ago), biodiversity has grown rapidly but suffered six major mass extinctions; the Permian–Triassic event (251 Ma) was the most severe, requiring 30 million years for vertebrate recovery. Human activity has driven an ongoing "sixth mass extinction" (Holocene extinction) since the late Pleistocene, accelerating loss rates to 100–10,000 times background levels. Habitat destruction for agriculture is the primary driver, compounded by climate change and overexploitation. Biodiversity underpins ecosystem services (provisioning, regulating, cultural) valued at ~$150 trillion annually, supports human health and agriculture, and holds intrinsic cultural value. Conservation efforts focus on protected areas (targeting 30% of land/sea by 2030), restoration, and transformative societal change.

## Timeline
- **4.54 Ga** — Earth forms; earliest undisputed life evidence by 3.7 Ga.
- **~540 Ma** — Cambrian explosion: rapid appearance of most multicellular phyla.
- **~251 Ma** — Permian–Triassic extinction: worst mass extinction; vertebrate recovery takes 30 Myr.
- **66 Ma** — Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction: non-avian dinosaurs and ammonites vanish.
- **Late Pleistocene** — Anthropogenic megafaunal extinctions begin (overhunting hypothesis).
- **1986** — Term "biodiversity" coined by W. G. Rosen at National Forum on BioDiversity.
- **1992** — UN Convention on Biological Diversity adopted.
- **2007** — Estimate: up to 30% of species extinct by 2050.
- **2016** — Living Planet Report: vertebrate populations declined ~50% since 1970.
- **2019** — IPBES Global Assessment: nature deteriorating at unprecedented, accelerating rate.
- **2020** — WWF report: 68% of examined vertebrate populations destroyed (1970–2016).
- **2022** — UN Biodiversity Conference (COP15): 30×30 target adopted (protect 30% land/sea by 2030); all countries except US and Holy See sign, including 22 targets (restore 30% degraded ecosystems, increase funding).
- **2023** — UN High Seas Treaty (BBNJ) adopted for marine biodiversity beyond national jurisdiction.

## Body

### Definitions and Scope
Biologists define biodiversity as the "totality of genes, species and ecosystems of a region," unifying taxonomic, ecological, morphological, and functional diversity. No single definition is fixed; the FAO (2019) describes it as variability among living organisms and their ecosystems, while the WHO calls it variability from all sources. It commonly replaces "species diversity" and "species richness" but remains a reimagined concept.

### Number of Species
Mora et al. (2011) estimate ~8.7 million terrestrial and ~2.2 million oceanic species (strongest for eukaryotes; likely lower bound for prokaryotes). Other estimates: 220,000 vascular plants; 0.7–1 million marine species; 10–30 million insects (0.9M known); 5–10 million bacteria; 1.5–3 million fungi (75,000 documented by 2001); 1 million mites. Microbial diversity is poorly known; the Global Ocean Sampling Expedition (2004–2006) vastly increased gene counts from plankton, potentially reshaping taxonomic definitions. Many species may go extinct before description; birds and mammals are best studied, fishes and arthropods least.

### Current Biodiversity Loss
Decline rates exceed any in human history—hundreds of times background rates. Key figures: up to 30% of species projected extinct by 2050 (2007 estimate); ~140,000 species/year lost (species-area theory); 25% of mammals could vanish in 20 years (2012 studies). WWF 2016 Living Planet Report: monitored vertebrate populations down ~50% since 1970; 39% terrestrial, 39% marine, 76% freshwater declines; Latin America down 83%. High-income nations show 10% gains offset by low-income losses, reflecting resource outsourcing. WWF 2020 report: 68% of examined vertebrate populations destroyed (1970–2016). Germany: insect biomass down 75% in 25 years (2017). Of 70,000 species (2023), around 48% experiencing population declines from human activity, 3% increasing. IUCN Red List: 40% of 40,177 assessed species threatened (16,119); 9,251 critically endangered (late 2022). Drivers: habitat destruction (farming), overexploitation, climate change, invasive species, pollution. IPBES cites human population growth and overconsumption; others emphasize export-commodity habitat loss. A 2025 study attributes loss across all ecosystems to human activities. Climate change and biodiversity are coupled: biodiversity aids mitigation/adaptation, but habitat loss and overexploitation may be larger immediate drivers.

### Distribution Patterns
Biodiversity varies with temperature, precipitation, altitude, soils, geography, and species interactions (biogeography). Highest in tropics (e.g., Yasuní National Park, Ecuador) and localized regions (Cape Floristic Region); lowest in polar zones. Terrestrial biodiversity ~25× marine. Forests harbor most terrestrial life: 80% of amphibians, 75% of birds, 68% of mammals, 60% of vascular plants in tropical forests. Mangroves nursery fish/shellfish, protect seagrass/coral reefs. Forests cover ~4 billion acres (~1/3 land), hold ~80% of world’s biodiversity; 1 billion ha primary forest; 700+ million ha protected. Temperate forests: fewer species, wide distributions. Montane/lowland tropics: many small-range species. Europe, parts of Asia, N. America: low intactness from dense populations/agriculture. N. Africa, S. Australia, coastal Brazil, Madagascar, S. Africa: striking losses. European forests: >1/3 land (227 Mha), area up ~10% since 1990.

#### Latitudinal Gradients
Species richness increases from poles to equator (latitudinal gradient). Ultimate driver: higher mean temperature at equator. Exceptions: aquatic/marine gradients unverified; parasites don’t follow rule; soil bacterial diversity peaks in temperate zones (carbon inputs, habitat connectivity). 2016 "fractal biodiversity" hypothesis combines species pool size and ecosystem fractal nature, using temperature, moisture, and net primary production as the main variables of an ecosystem niche and as the axis of the ecological hypervolume to build fractal hypervolumes whose fractal dimension rises to three moving towards the equator.

#### Biodiversity Hotspots
Regions with high endemism and severe habitat loss (term by Norman Myers, 1988). Most are tropical forests. Examples: Brazil’s Atlantic Forest (20,000 plants, 1,350 vertebrates, millions of insects, half endemic); Madagascar (independent evolution since 66 Ma separation); India; Colombia (highest species/area, most endemics: 10% Earth’s species, 1,900+ birds, 10% mammals, 14% amphibians, 18% birds); Indonesia (17,000 islands, 10% flowering plants, 12% mammals, 17% reptiles/amphibians/birds). Specialized habitats (alpine, peat bogs) drive endemism. Measurement biased by research effort (Gilbert White, 1768: "most variety which is the most examined").

### Evolution Over Geologic Time
Result of 3.5 Gyr evolution. Life possibly established few hundred Myr post-Earth formation. Until ~2.5 Ga: only microorganisms (archaea, bacteria, protists). Phanerozoic (last 540 Myr): rapid growth via Cambrian explosion (most multicellular phyla appear); recent studies suggest earlier start (Ediacaran) and continuation (Ordovician). Next 400 Myr: invertebrate diversity flat, vertebrate diversity exponential, punctuated by mass extinctions. Carboniferous rainforest collapse hit anamniotic limbed vertebrates; amniotes unaffected until early Permian (~293 Ma). Permian–Triassic (251 Ma) worst: 30 Myr vertebrate recovery. Cretaceous–Paleogene (66 Ma): non-avian dinosaurs, ammonites extinct. Paleobiodiversity: fossil record suggests last few Myr had greatest diversity, but sampling bias (better preservation of recent strata) debated. Some argue corrected diversity similar to 300 Ma; others trust fossil record. Current macroscopic diversity estimates: 2M–100M, best ~9M (mostly arthropods). Diversity increases continually absent selection.

#### Diversification
Global carrying capacity debated. Marine life: logistic growth; terrestrial life: exponential rise. Tetrapods occupy only 36% of habitable modes; without humans, diversity might rise exponentially. Phanerozoic diversity fits hyperbolic model (second-order positive feedback: diversity ↔ community complexity) better than exponential/logistic. Similarity to human population curves: both from hyperbolic trend + cyclical/stochastic dynamics. Post-mass-extinction rediversification faster for ammonoids than bivalves due to stronger second-order feedback. Holocene extinction (since human emergence) may eliminate most species within 100 years at current rates. New species discovered: 5,000–10,000/year (mostly insects); ~90% of arthropods unclassified. Most terrestrial diversity in tropical forests; land > ocean (~8.7M total, ~2.1M marine).

#### Species Diversity in Geologic Time
5–50 billion species ever existed; if 50M alive today, >99% extinct pre-humans. Current estimates: 10M–14M (1.2M documented, 86% undescribed); 2016 study suggests 1 trillion species (0.001% described). Total DNA base pairs: 5.0×10³⁷ (50 billion tonnes); biosphere mass: up to 4 trillion tons carbon. 2016: 355 genes identified from LUCA (last universal common ancestor). Earth age: 4.54 Ga. Earliest undisputed life: 3.7 Ga (Eoarchean). Microbial mats: 3.48 Ga sandstone (W. Australia). Graphite in 3.7 Ga metasediments (W. Greenland). 2015: biotic remains in 4.1 Ga rocks (W. Australia)—if life arose quickly, it may be common in universe.

### Role and Benefits of Biodiversity

#### Ecosystem Services
Grouped as provisioning (food, wood, water), regulating (climate, pest/disease control), cultural (aesthetics, recreation, spiritual). Humans cannot easily replicate ecosystems (e.g., insect pollination: $2.1–14.6B in 2003; global ecosystem services ~$33T/yr, 1997).

**Provisioning**: Plant species diversity ↑ fodder yield (271 studies); crop genetic diversity ↑ yield (575 studies, though 100-study review mixed); tree diversity ↑ wood production (53 studies; trait diversity effect unclear).

**Regulating**: Biodiversity stabilizes ecosystems. Plant richness ↑ functional diversity; niche complementarity (nutrient cycling, water regulation) buffers climate stress (e.g., varied leaf traits/root depths improve water use, reduce drought/heat impact). Higher biodiversity ↑ resilience to pests/diseases (host trait diversity interrupts spread). Coastal genetic diversity ↑ adaptive capacity to temperature/precipitation shifts. Terrestrial diversity ↑ mutualisms (pollination, mycorrhizae) → nutrient uptake, productivity. German grassland experiment: species-rich communities resist dry/wet stress, maintain productivity, but richness reduced resilience under dry extremes, no effect under wet extremes. Fish diversity ↑ fisheries yield stability (8 studies). Plant diversity ↑ carbon sequestration (uptake, not long-term storage; 479 studies), soil nutrient remineralization (103), soil organic matter (85), ↓ plant disease (107). Natural enemy diversity ↓ herbivorous pests (266 studies; 18 observational), but intraguild predation can make single predators more effective (38 studies).

#### Agriculture
Intraspecific diversity: genetic variation within a species (e.g., potato varieties). Interspecific diversity: number/types of species. Planned diversity (crops, livestock) vs. associated diversity (weeds, herbivores, pathogens—uninvited). Beneficial associated biodiversity: wild pollinators (bees, syrphid flies), natural pest enemies → pest control, nutrient cycling, pollination. ~80% human food from 20 plant species, but ≥40,000 species used. Extinction shrinks future options.

#### Human Health
Biodiversity loss = global health issue, linked to climate change. Species that buffer disease transmission disappear; survivors (e.g., West Nile, Lyme, Hantavirus vectors) increase transmission (Keesing & Harvell). Impacts: nutrition, infectious disease, medicinal resources, mental health, disaster risk reduction (wetlands buffer storms/sea-level rise). Drug discovery: ≥50% US pharmaceuticals from biological sources; ~80% world population relies on nature-derived medicines (modern/traditional). Tiny fraction of wild species screened. Marine biodiversity critical for cancer, antiviral, AIDS treatments. Bioprospecting can drive loss and violate local laws (biopiracy).

#### Business and Industry
Boston Consulting Group (2021): biodiversity’s economic value = regulation, culture, habitat, provisioning. Worth ~$150T/yr (~2× global GDP). Loss costs ~$5T/yr. Supply chains depend on intact ecosystems; disruption costs exceed gains.

#### Cultural and Aesthetic Value
Intrinsic spiritual/aesthetic value, countering purely utilitarian conservation. Non-material benefits: knowledge systems, education.

### Measuring Biodiversity
Analytical limits: <1% of described species studied beyond existence. Most species microbial; science fixated on macroscopic world. Microbial life metabolically/environmentally more diverse (extremophiles). On rRNA tree, visible life = tiny twigs. Inverse size-population relationship: most multicellular species are insects. High insect extinction rates support Holocene extinction hypothesis.

### Biodiversity Changes (Non-Loss)

#### Natural Seasonal Variations
Spring ↑ biodiversity (breeding/feeding); winter ↓ (insect death, migration). Plant/invertebrate population fluctuations drive seasonal shifts.

#### Introduced and Invasive Species
Barriers (rivers, mountains, oceans) enable allopatric speciation. Invasive species breach barriers, often supplanting natives via niche occupation or resource competition. Humans move species intentionally/accidentally. Diverse ecosystems may resist invaders. Invasives ↑ local (alpha) diversity but ↓ turnover (beta diversity); gamma diversity may fall from other extinctions. Few invaders cause host extinction (Dutch elm disease, emerald ash borer, chestnut blight); extirpation, decline, homogenization more common. Human introductions occur on far shorter timescales than natural range expansion. Some countries: exotic species outnumber indigenous (e.g., kudzu in N. America; pines invading southern hemisphere).

#### Hybridization and Genetic Pollution
Endemics threatened by genetic pollution: uncontrolled hybridization, introgression, genetic swamping from introduced species’ numerical/fitness advantage. Hybridization/introgression side-effects of invasion. Abundant species swamps rare species’ gene pool; not always morphologically apparent. Some gene flow is normal adaptation; not all genotypes preservable. But hybridization can threaten rare species’ existence.

### Conservation
Conservation biology matured mid-20th century. Ethic: manage resources to sustain biodiversity (species, ecosystems, evolution, human culture). Strategic plans engage policy at all scales; action plans link human well-being, natural capital, macroeconomics, ecosystem services. EU Directive 1999/22/EC: zoos preserve biodiversity via research/breeding.

#### Protection and Restoration Techniques
Exotic species removal allows native niche recovery; identification via DNA barcoding (DAISY). Practical only for large populations (cost). Reintroduction candidates identified via Encyclopedia of Life, GBIF. Biodiversity banking (e.g., Australian Native Vegetation Management Framework) monetizes value. Gene banks store specimens/genetic material; some aim for reintroduction (tree nurseries). Reduced/targeted pesticides ↑ survival in ag/urban areas. Migratory species need wildlife corridors; national boundaries complicate creation.

#### Protected Areas
Forest reserves, biosphere reserves protect wildlife/habitat. Scientists urge 30% planet protected by 2030, 50% by 2050. COP15 (2022): 30×30 adopted by ~200 countries. At adoption: 17% land, 10% ocean protected. 2020 study mapped regions for conservation/climate goals. >238,563 protected areas = 14.9% land surface (IUCN 2018), varying in size, protection level, management. Benefits: conserve nature, secure long-term ecosystem services (genetic resources, medicine, water, recreation, disaster buffers), socioeconomic values.

##### National Parks and Wildlife Sanctuaries
National parks (IUCN Category II): large natural areas protecting ecological processes, enabling education/recreation. Wildlife sanctuaries: shelter for non-releasable animals or temporary rehabilitation. Both preserve biodiversity. NPS: parks maintain animal/habitat integrity via conservation, public education.

##### Forest Protected Areas
18% world forest area (700+ Mha) in legally protected areas (national parks, conservation areas, game reserves). Est. 726 Mha forest protected. South America highest share (31%). Forests harbor 45,000+ floral, 81,000+ faunal species (5,150 floral, 1,837 faunal endemic); 60,065 tree species globally. Forest reserves sometimes grant hunting/grazing rights to fringe communities. Europe: 50 Mha (24%) protected for biodiversity/landscape; 72 Mha (32%) for soil/water/ecosystem services.

### Role of Society

#### Transformative Change
IPBES Global Assessment (2019): nature deteriorating at unprecedented, accelerating rate. Requires transformative change: sustainable agriculture, reduced consumption/waste, fishing quotas, collaborative water management. "Nature-positive" goal: halt/reverse biodiversity loss from 2020 baseline, achieve full "nature recovery" by 2050. Mainstreaming embeds biodiversity into public/private practice globally/locally.

#### Citizen Science
Public participation in research widely used in biodiversity. Volunteers contribute massive on-ground data; tech improvements ↑ flow/quality. 2016 study: nearly half of GBIF occurrence records from volunteer-contributed datasets. Platforms: iNaturalist, eBird.

### Legal Status

#### International
UN Convention on Biological Diversity (1992), Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety; UN BBNJ/High Seas Treaty (2023) for marine biodiversity beyond jurisdiction; CITES; Ramsar (Wetlands); Bonn (Migratory Species); UNESCO World Heritage (indirect); UNESCO Global Geoparks; regional (Apia Convention); bilateral (Japan-Australia Migratory Bird Agreement). CBD gives sovereign national rights over biological resources (not property); commits to conserve, develop sustainably, share benefits. Bioprospecting expects benefit-sharing; without it, becomes biopiracy. Access and Benefit Sharing Agreements (ABAs) operationalize sovereignty; CBD implies informed consent. COP15 (Dec 2022): all countries except US and Holy See signed 30×30 + 22 targets (restore 30% degraded ecosystems, increase funding).

#### European Union
EU Biodiversity Strategy 2030 (May 2020): core of climate mitigation. Targets: protect 30% sea/land (esp. old-growth forests); plant 3B trees by 2030; restore 25,000 km free-flowing rivers; cut pesticides 50%; 25% organic farming (Farm to Fork); increase agrobiodiversity; €20B/yr funding; mainstream into business. ~50% global GDP nature-dependent; Natura 2000 benefits €200–300B/yr in Europe.

#### National Level Laws
Law-ecosystem links ancient (property rights, threatened ecosystem protection, hunting/fishing rights). Species protection laws recent (e.g., US Endangered Species Act). Gene pool laws ~century old; genetic engineering → tighter GMO/gene patent laws.
