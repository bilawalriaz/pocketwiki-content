# Late modern period

## Overview
The Late Modern period is a historical era that began with the French Revolution in 1789 and is defined by the rise of industrial capitalism, nation-states, and global empires. It matters because it established the political, economic, and technological foundations of the contemporary world, culminating in two world wars and the dawn of the Information Age. Its endpoint is debated, with scholars placing it at 1945, the late 20th century, or considering it ongoing.

## Timeline
- **c. 1760** — Industrial Revolution begins in Great Britain.
- **1789** — French Revolution starts, marking the conventional beginning of the Late Modern period.
- **1815** — Congress of Vienna establishes post-Napoleonic European order.
- **1884–85** — Berlin Conference formalizes the European "Scramble for Africa."
- **1914–18** — World War I causes the collapse of four empires.
- **1917** — Russian Revolution leads to the creation of the Soviet Union.
- **1929** — Wall Street Crash triggers the Great Depression.
- **1939–45** — World War II results in an estimated 62 million deaths.
- **1947–91** — Cold War period of geopolitical tension between the US and USSR.
- **Mid-20th c.** — Information Age begins, shifting the economy toward information technology.

## Body

### Definition and Periodization
The Late Modern period is generally considered to have begun in 1789 with the French Revolution. Its endpoint is contested. Some historians end it in 1945 with the conclusion of World War II, while others extend it to the present day. Proposed subsequent eras include the Information Age (mid-20th century onward), the Postmodern era (late 20th century onward), and the Post-industrial society (service-based economy). The period itself is often subdivided, for example, into the Cold War era (c. 1947–1991) and the Digital Revolution (late 20th century onward).

### Industrial Revolutions and 19th-Century Transformations
The Industrial Revolution, which began in Great Britain around 1760, was driven by technologies like the steam engine. Historians debate its exact dates, with Eric Hobsbawm placing it between 1789 and 1848. The 19th century saw the decline of older empires (Spanish, Ottoman, Mughal) and the rise of new powers. The British Empire, after defeating Napoleon, became the world's dominant power, controlling a quarter of the world's population and land by 1914. This era also witnessed significant political changes, including Italian unification (c. 1815–1871) and the abolition of slavery in major powers like Britain (1833), Russia (1861), and the United States (1865).

### Imperialism and the Scramble for Africa
European imperialism intensified in the 19th century. The "Scramble for Africa" was formally initiated at the Berlin Conference (1884–85), where European powers partitioned the continent without consulting Africans. By 1914, nearly the entire continent was under European control, with only Liberia and Ethiopia remaining independent. This colonial expansion was driven by economic exploitation and geopolitical competition.

### Rise of New Powers and Global Conflict
The late 19th and early 20th centuries saw the modernization of Japan during the Meiji Restoration and the decline of China's Qing Dynasty. The United States expanded westward under the ideology of "Manifest Destiny" and fought a Civil War (1861–65) over slavery. Tensions between imperial powers and nationalist movements contributed to World War I (1914–18), which destroyed four empires and reshaped global borders. The subsequent Russian Revolution (1917) created the Soviet Union, leading to a new ideological conflict.

### The Interwar Period and World War II
The period between the world wars was marked by economic turmoil, from the "Roaring Twenties" to the Great Depression (beginning 1929). The League of Nations failed to prevent aggression by powers like Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan. World War II (1939–45) was the deadliest conflict in history, involving the Holocaust and ending with the atomic bombings of Japan. The war led to the decline of European empires and the beginning of the Cold War between the United States and the Soviet Union.

### Late 20th Century and the Information Age
The post-1945 era saw decolonization, the Cold War, and the rise of the United States and USSR as superpowers. The latter half of the century was defined by the Information Age, a shift from industrial to information-based economies driven by digital technology. This period also saw political polarization in regions like Latin America and the expansion of global communication networks.

## Terms
- **Industrial Revolution**: The transition to new manufacturing processes in Great Britain, c. 1760, which spread globally and fundamentally changed economic and social structures.
- **Nation-State**: A sovereign state whose territory corresponds to a population identified as a nation, a concept that solidified during the 19th century.
- **Imperialism**: A policy of extending a country's power and influence through diplomacy or military force, particularly through the establishment of colonies.
- **Scramble for Africa**: The invasion, occupation, and division of African territory by European powers during the New Imperialism period, c. 1881–1914.
- **Cold War**: A state of geopolitical tension after World War II between powers in the Western Bloc (United States) and the Eastern Bloc (Soviet Union).
- **Information Age**: A historical period beginning in the mid-20th century, characterized by a shift from traditional industry to an economy based on information technology.
- **Postmodernism**: An intellectual stance skeptical of the grand narratives and ideologies of modernism, emerging in the late 20th century.
- **Manifest Destiny**: A 19th-century American belief that the United States was destined to expand across the North American continent.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Periodization**: There is no consensus on the exact end date of the Late Modern period. Some end it in 1945, others in the late 20th century, and some consider it ongoing.
- **Industrial Revolution Timing**: Historians disagree on the precise dates, with estimates ranging from c. 1760 to 1830 or 1789 to 1848.
- **Causes of World War I**: The war's origins are debated, with factors including militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism, and specific crises like the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand.
- **Nature of Colonialism**: Scholars debate the extent to which colonial rule was a "mandate" for welfare versus a system of exploitation that caused severe deterioration in material and moral conditions for native populations.
