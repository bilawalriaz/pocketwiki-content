# Glasgow

## Overview
Glasgow is Scotland's largest city and the third-most populous in the United Kingdom. It grew from a small medieval religious settlement into a major industrial powerhouse of the British Empire, renowned for shipbuilding and engineering. After a period of post-industrial decline in the 20th century, it has regenerated into a leading centre for culture, education, and finance.

## Body

### Origins and Early Growth
The area has been settled for millennia, with the River Clyde providing a natural location for fishing. The city itself was reputedly founded by the Christian missionary Saint Mungo in the 6th century. It grew as a religious centre around Glasgow Cathedral and the University of Glasgow (founded 1451). After the Acts of Union in 1707, Glasgow became a major hub for international trade, particularly in sugar, tobacco, and cotton, prospering from its involvement in the Atlantic slave trade.

### Industrial Revolution and Imperial Peak
From the late 18th century, textile manufacturing and engineering dominated the economy. The discovery of local ironstone in 1801 and the invention of hot-blast furnaces in 1828 spurred iron and steel production. Extensive dredging of the River Clyde made Glasgow the world centre of shipbuilding. The city also became the largest locomotive builder in Europe. This industrial might led to rapid population growth, with Glasgow becoming one of the first European cities to reach a population of one million and earning the title "Second City of the British Empire."

### 20th-Century Decline and Regeneration
After World War I, Glasgow suffered from post-war recession and the Great Depression, which contributed to the rise of the "Red Clydeside" movement. While it recovered before World War II, the post-war era saw a lengthy period of de-industrialisation as global competition weakened its traditional industries. This led to high unemployment and urban decay. A radical programme of urban renewal in the 1960s and 70s involved demolishing slums and relocating populations to new towns like Cumbernauld and East Kilbride, causing the city's population to decline sharply. Since the 1980s, a concerted regeneration effort, highlighted by the 1990 European Capital of Culture status, has diversified the economy into services, finance, and tourism.

### Culture and Architecture
Glasgow's cultural institutions, including the Kelvingrove Art Gallery and Museum and the Scottish Opera, have international reputations. The city is notable for its Victorian architecture and the work of Charles Rennie Mackintosh, a leading figure of the Glasgow School and Art Nouveau movement. Much of the historic city centre was built with red or blond sandstone, which became blackened by industrial soot until the Clean Air Act of 1956. The city is also known for its distinctive sandstone tenement buildings.

### Sport
Glasgow has a profound association football heritage; the world's first international match was held there in 1872. The city is home to the Old Firm rivalry between Celtic and Rangers. It hosted the 2014 Commonwealth Games and will host the 2026 edition. Other major sports include rugby union, represented by the Glasgow Warriors, and ice hockey by the Glasgow Clan.

## Terms
*   **Tenement:** A type of apartment building, common in Glasgow, typically built from red or blond sandstone with large rooms and high ceilings.
*   **Red Clydeside:** A term for the radical socialist movement and period of intense industrial and political unrest in Glasgow and the Clydebank area around the time of World War I.
*   **Old Firm:** The collective name for the rivalry between Glasgow's two major football clubs, Celtic and Rangers.
*   **Royal Burgh:** A type of Scottish town that was granted a royal charter, giving it special privileges and a degree of self-government.
*   **Scottish Enlightenment:** A period of intellectual and scientific accomplishment in Scotland during the 18th century, of which Glasgow was a major centre.
*   **Unitary Council Area:** A type of local government district in Scotland with full responsibility for all aspects of local administration, established in 1996.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes accusations that the Scottish Office deliberately undermined Glasgow's economy in the post-war period by diverting new industries to other regions and creating new towns to disperse its population. The policy of mass tenement demolition in the mid-20th century is now widely considered short-sighted and wasteful, having destroyed many architecturally significant buildings.
