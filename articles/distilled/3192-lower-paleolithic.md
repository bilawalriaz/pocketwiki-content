# Lower Paleolithic

## Overview
The Lower Paleolithic is the earliest and longest subdivision of the Old Stone Age, spanning from approximately 3.3 million to 300,000 years ago. It marks the dawn of human technology, beginning with the first stone tools and ending as more complex behaviors and advanced tool-making techniques emerged. This period is fundamental to understanding the origins of human cognition, social structure, and global dispersal.

## Timeline
- **c. 3.3 million years ago** — Oldest known stone tools (Lomekwian) appear at Lomekwi 3, Kenya.
- **c. 2.6 million years ago** — Oldowan (Mode 1) tool industry begins, possibly used by australopithecines.
- **c. 1.8 million years ago** — *Homo erectus* appears; Acheulean (Mode 2) hand-axe technology develops.
- **c. 1.83 million years ago** — Evidence of early hominin presence in Asia (stone tools in Malaysia).
- **c. 800,000–600,000 years ago** — *Homo heidelbergensis* appears, a candidate for early symbolic language.
- **c. 400,000 years ago** — *Homo rhodesiensis* and *Homo cepranensis* appear.
- **c. 300,000 years ago** — Period ends; *Homo sapiens* first appears; prepared-core technologies emerge.

## Body

### Origins and Early Tools
The Lower Paleolithic began with the first evidence of stone tool production. The oldest known tools, dated to 3.3 million years ago, are from Lomekwi 3 and predate the genus *Homo*. The Oldowan industry, long considered the oldest, developed around 2.6 million years ago and was likely used by early *Homo* species like *Homo habilis* for scavenging. This technological shift is linked to climate changes in East Africa that expanded savannahs, prompting a dietary move toward carnivorous scavenging.

### The Rise of *Homo erectus* and Global Spread
The appearance of *Homo erectus* by about 1.8 million years ago marked a major transition from scavenging to hunting, establishing a hunting-gathering lifestyle. This species migrated out of Africa, dispersing stone tools across Eurasia. In Europe, the Oldowan tradition evolved into parallel flake (Clactonian) and hand-axe (Acheulean) traditions. The development of hunting and complex communication drove significant brain expansion over the next two million years.

### Later Developments and Regional Variations
By about 600,000 years ago, *Homo heidelbergensis* appeared, potentially developing early symbolic language. In Europe, populations intermediate between *H. erectus* and *H. sapiens* emerged. The Acheulean hand-axe tradition spread to Arabia and India but not to Southeast Asia. In India, the Madrasian culture (Acheulian) is known from sites like Attirampakkam, with tools dated to 1.5 million years ago. The period concluded around 300,000 years ago with the appearance of *Homo sapiens* and more complex technologies.

## Terms
- **Lower Paleolithic**: The earliest subdivision of the Old Stone Age, from ~3.3 million to 300,000 years ago.
- **Lomekwian**: The oldest known stone tool technology, dated to 3.3 million years ago.
- **Oldowan (Mode 1)**: A simple stone tool industry characterized by choppers and flakes, beginning ~2.6 million years ago.
- **Acheulean (Mode 2)**: A more advanced tool industry featuring bifacial hand-axes, associated with *Homo erectus*.
- **Levallois technique**: A prepared-core knapping method for producing flakes, developed during this period.
- **Homo habilis**: An early *Homo* species, traditionally associated with the first Oldowan tools.
- **Homo erectus**: A hominin species that migrated out of Africa and was associated with hunting and Acheulean tools.
- **Homo heidelbergensis**: A later hominin species, a candidate for early symbolic language and a possible ancestor of Neanderthals and *Homo sapiens*.

## Debates and open questions
- Whether the earliest control of fire by hominins dates to the Lower or Middle Paleolithic remains an open question.
- The exact hominin species responsible for the Lomekwian tools (3.3 million years ago) is not definitively known.
- The relationship between *Homo heidelbergensis*, Neanderthals, and *Homo sapiens* is debated, with some evidence suggesting Neanderthals evolved from *H. erectus* or *H. heidelbergensis*.
