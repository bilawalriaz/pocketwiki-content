# Phoenix Islands Protected Area

## Overview
The Phoenix Islands Protected Area (PIPA) is a vast marine protected area in the central Pacific, within the Republic of Kiribati. It is one of the largest and deepest protected areas on Earth, conserving a pristine coral archipelago ecosystem. Its significance is underscored by its designation as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, though its protection status has been subject to political and economic pressures.

## Timeline
- **2000** — Comprehensive fauna surveys of the Phoenix Islands last conducted before PIPA declaration.
- **2006** — Survey conducted to assess non-native pest species and plan restoration programs.
- **2008** — Rat and rabbit eradication programs begin on McKean and Rawaki islands.
- **2009** — Kiribati submits PIPA for UNESCO World Heritage consideration.
- **2010** — PIPA inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2015** — Full ban on all commercial extractive activities, including fishing, takes effect.
- **2021** — Kiribati government announces termination of the protected area to boost tuna fishing.
- **2023** — PIPA officially reopened to commercial fishing.

## Body

### Establishment and Management
PIPA was established through a partnership between the Republic of Kiribati and conservation organizations. It is managed by the PIPA Conservation Trust. A total ban on commercial fishing was implemented in 2015, with only a small sustainable-use zone around Canton Island for its resident population. However, in 2021, the Kiribati government announced the termination of the protected area to boost tuna fishing, and it was officially reopened to commercial fishing in 2023.

### Natural Heritage and Ecology
The PIPA encompasses a large, intact oceanic coral archipelago ecosystem. It includes three atolls, five low islands, two submerged reefs, and at least 14 seamounts (presumed extinct volcanoes). Surveys from 2000 found the reefs in excellent health, free from bleaching and disease. The ecosystem supports approximately 800 known species, including around 200 corals, 500 fish, 18 marine mammals, and 44 bird species. The deep-sea habitats are rich in biodiversity, particularly octocorals.

### Threats and Restoration
A key threat is the introduction of iron from shipwrecks and anchor gear into this naturally iron-poor region. This iron leaching is linked to the proliferation of turf algae and the formation of degraded "black reefs," with no documented recovery at affected sites. Another major threat has been invasive species. Successful eradication programs for rats and rabbits on several islands (McKean, Rawaki, Enderbury, Birnie) have led to spectacular recovery of native vegetation and seabird populations, including the endangered Phoenix petrel.

## Terms
- **Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ):** A sea zone prescribed by the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, over which a state has special rights regarding exploration and use of marine resources.
- **Marine Protected Area (MPA):** A clearly defined geographical space, recognized and managed through legal or other effective means, to achieve the long-term conservation of nature.
- **Atoll:** A ring-shaped coral reef, island, or series of islets, enclosing a lagoon.
- **Seamount:** An underwater mountain formed by volcanic activity.
- **Black Reef:** A degraded reef ecosystem characterized by a proliferation of turf algae and bacterial mats, often linked to iron pollution from shipwrecks.
- **Live Coral Cover (LCC):** The percentage of a reef's surface covered by living coral tissue.
- **Endemic:** A species native and restricted to a certain place.
- **Invasive Species:** A non-native organism that causes ecological or economic harm in its new environment.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate centers on the conflict between conservation and economic development. The 2015 fishing ban was criticized by some for allowing too much fishing prior to full closure. The 2021 government decision to terminate the protected area to boost tuna fishing directly pits short-term economic gains against long-term ecological preservation. The effectiveness of the PIPA Trust and the future of its conservation goals under the new fishing regime remain open questions.
