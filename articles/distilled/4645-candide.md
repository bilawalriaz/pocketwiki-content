# Candide

## Overview
*Candide, ou l'Optimisme* is a French satire by Voltaire, published in 1759. It follows the naive protagonist Candide on a global journey of suffering and disillusionment, directly attacking the philosophy of Leibnizian optimism—the idea that we live in the "best of all possible worlds." The novella is a cornerstone of Western literature, renowned for its sharp wit, fast-paced plot, and enduring critique of philosophical, religious, and political institutions.

## Body

### Historical and Literary Background
Voltaire wrote *Candide* in response to the philosophical problem of evil, catalyzed by the 1755 Lisbon earthquake and the Seven Years' War. These events directly contradicted the Leibnizian doctrine of optimism, which insisted on God's benevolence. Voltaire's work is a *conte philosophique* (philosophical tale) that parodies the adventure novel and the *bildungsroman* (coming-of-age story). Its closest literary relative is Jonathan Swift's *Gulliver's Travels* (1726), another satire about a naive traveler hardened by misfortune.

### Creation and Publication
The exact composition period is uncertain, but scholars estimate it was primarily written in late 1758. The publication was extremely secretive, likely the "most clandestine work of the century," due to its illicit content. It was published simultaneously in Geneva, Amsterdam, London, and Paris. The novel underwent one major revision in 1761, which included an addition to Chapter 22.

### Plot and Characters
The story begins in the Edenic castle of Baron Thunder-ten-Tronckh in Westphalia, where the naive Candide is tutored in optimism by Professor Pangloss. After being expelled for kissing Cunégonde, the Baron's daughter, Candide is thrust into a world of relentless hardship. He is conscripted into war, survives the Lisbon earthquake, is flogged at an *auto-da-fé* (a public penance), and travels through Europe and the Americas. Key characters include the optimistic Pangloss, the practical valet Cacambo, the pessimistic philosopher Martin, and Cunégonde, who endures her own series of brutal misfortunes. The plot is episodic and fantastical, with characters repeatedly facing death and improbable escapes.

### Style and Satire
Voltaire's style is characterized by a bitter, matter-of-fact tone that contrasts horrific events with dry, humorous narration. The satire works by presenting real-world evils—war, religious persecution, natural disasters, and human cruelty—in stark detail to ridicule the philosophy that justifies them. A famous example is the execution of an admiral "to encourage the others," which satirizes military justice. The novel's fast pace and improbable plot allow for compounding tragedies, creating a darkly comic effect.

### Philosophy and Conclusion
The central philosophical target is Leibnizian optimism, embodied by Pangloss, who absurdly justifies every catastrophe as necessary for the "best of all possible worlds." After his journey, Candide rejects this dogma. The novel's conclusion is enigmatic: Candide, Pangloss, Martin, and their companions settle on a small farm. They meet a Turk whose philosophy is to work only to avoid "boredom, vice, and poverty." Candide's final, famous precept is "we must cultivate our garden" (*il faut cultiver notre jardin*), advocating for practical, industrious work over abstract philosophical debate.

## Terms
- **Leibnizian Optimism**: The philosophical doctrine, founded by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, that God created the best of all possible worlds, where all evil is necessary for a greater good.
- **Conte Philosophique**: A genre of philosophical tale or novella, popular in the 18th century, used to convey philosophical ideas through narrative.
- **Bildungsroman**: A novel dealing with a protagonist's formative years or spiritual education, often a coming-of-age story.
- **Auto-da-fé**: The ritual of public penance of condemned heretics and apostates of the Catholic Inquisition, often followed by execution.
- **Picaresque Novel**: A genre of prose fiction that depicts the adventures of a roguish hero of low social class who lives by his wits in a corrupt society.
- **Theodicy**: A theological attempt to reconcile the existence of a benevolent, omnipotent God with the presence of evil in the world.
- **Manichaean**: A follower of a dualistic religion positing a struggle between a good, spiritual world and an evil, material world; used to describe a pessimistic worldview.
- **Meliorism**: The belief that the world can be made better by human effort, a middle ground between optimism and pessimism.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The Meaning of "Cultivate Our Garden"**: Scholars debate whether this conclusion advocates for passive retreat from society, active industrious contribution to it, or simply a practical focus on one's immediate affairs.
- **Voltaire's Voice**: The "inside/outside" controversy questions whether the novel's message should be read as directly expressing Voltaire's personal philosophy ("outside") or as an independent narrative whose meaning is contained within the text itself ("inside").
- **Philosophical Prescription**: Critics argue over whether Voltaire is advocating for a specific philosophy (like Martin's pessimism) or simply rejecting optimism without offering a systematic alternative.
