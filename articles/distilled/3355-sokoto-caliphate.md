# Sokoto Caliphate

## Overview
The Sokoto Caliphate was a vast Sunni Muslim state in West Africa, founded in 1804 by the scholar Usman dan Fodio following a successful jihad against the Hausa Kingdoms. At its peak, it was the largest and most populous empire in West Africa, linking over 30 emirates and 10–20 million people. Its establishment marked a major shift in the region's religious, political, and social landscape, promoting Islamic law and scholarship. The caliphate was dissolved in 1903 when its territories were conquered and partitioned by British, French, and German colonial forces.

## Timeline
- **c. 1802** — Sultan Yunfa of Gobir attempts to assassinate Usman dan Fodio, forcing him into exile.
- **February 1804** — Usman dan Fodio and his followers migrate to Gudu; he is elected "Commander of the Faithful."
- **1804–1808** — Fulani War: Usman's forces conquer the Hausa Kingdoms, including Kano (1807) and Gobir (1809).
- **1809** — Muhammad Bello, son of Usman, founds the city of Sokoto, the new capital.
- **1815** — Usman dan Fodio retires, dividing administration between his brother (Gwandu) and son (Sokoto).
- **c. 1837** — The caliphate reaches a population of 10–20 million, the most populous in West Africa.
- **1846** — Fulani jihadist movements contribute to the fall of the Sayfawa dynasty of the Kanem-Bornu Empire.
- **1891** — French explorer Parfait-Louis Monteil visits Sokoto, noting internal conflict and weakening power.
- **1903** — British forces defeat Sultan Muhammadu Attahiru I; the caliphate is officially dissolved and partitioned.
- **1906** — Armed resistance to British colonial rule in the region ends.

## Body

### Origins and Jihad
The Sokoto Caliphate emerged from a religious and political crisis in the Hausa states. In the late 18th century, increased Islamic preaching critiqued the perceived un-Islamic practices and high taxes of the Hausa aristocracies. Usman dan Fodio, a respected Fulani Islamic scholar, was initially supported by the leadership of Gobir. However, when his former student Yunfa became sultan, he restricted dan Fodio's activities, leading to dan Fodio's exile in 1804. His followers elected him "Commander of the Faithful," and he declared a jihad. Despite early defeats, his forces, using guerrilla tactics and popular support against despotic rule, conquered the major Hausa states by 1809, establishing a new political order.

### Expansion and Administration
The caliphate expanded rapidly from 1808 to the 1830s, becoming one of Africa's largest states. It was organized as a loose confederation of over 30 emirates, each led by an emir appointed by the Sultan of Sokoto. The sultan held spiritual and suzerainty authority, while emirs governed with wide autonomy. A major administrative division existed between the Sokoto Sultanate in the east and the Gwandu Emirate in the west. The state's structure was based on Islamic principles, with leaders chosen for piety and scholarship rather than hereditary succession. Expansion was facilitated by a system of *ribats* (fortified settlements) that supported agriculture, settled pastoral Fulani, and spread Islam and Arabic literacy.

### Economy and Slavery
The caliphate's economy was based on agriculture, trans-Saharan trade, and a massive slave system. All land was declared *waqf* (community property), though allocated by rulers. Major crops included cotton, grain, and kola nuts. Slavery was integral to the economy, with slaves acquired through raiding and war. By 1900, the caliphate had an estimated 1 to 2.5 million slaves, making it one of the world's largest slave societies. Slaves worked on plantations and in domestic settings. While Islamic law allowed for manumission and conversion, the system was exploitative, and harsh punishments for disobedient slaves were documented.

### Scholarship and Legacy
The Sokoto era was a period of intense Islamic scholarship. Founding leaders Usman dan Fodio, Abdullahi dan Fodio, and Muhammad Bello authored hundreds of works on theology, law, and science. Women, including Usman's daughter Nana Asma'u, were also educated and contributed to scholarship. The caliphate's legacy is profound: it spread Islam across the Sudanian Savanna, inspired later jihads in West Africa, and its administrative and religious structures influenced the region long after its political dissolution. The title of Sultan of Sokoto remains a significant religious position in Nigeria today.

## Terms
- **Caliphate**: An Islamic state led by a caliph, a political and religious successor to the Prophet Muhammad.
- **Jihad**: In this context, a holy war or struggle to defend or spread Islam.
- **Emirate**: A territory or state ruled by an emir, a high-ranking Muslim noble or governor.
- **Sultan**: A title for a Muslim sovereign, especially in Ottoman and some other Islamic states.
- **Waqf**: An inalienable religious endowment in Islamic law, typically property dedicated for charitable purposes.
- **Ribat**: A fortified Islamic religious house or frontier post, often combining military and educational functions.
- **Tariqa**: A Sufi order or brotherhood, a school of Islamic mysticism.
- **Sharia**: Islamic law derived from the Quran and the traditions of the Prophet Muhammad.
- **Manumission**: The act of an owner freeing their slaves.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nature of the State**: Scholars debate whether the Sokoto Caliphate was primarily a religious movement or an ethnic Fulani empire. The prevailing view is that it was a religiously motivated state, though all but one of its early emirs were Fulani.
- **Scale of Slavery**: Estimates of the slave population vary widely, from 1 million to 2.5 million, with some scholars suggesting slaves constituted between a quarter and a half of the total population.
- **Colonial Impact**: The British colonial policy of retaining the Sultan as a symbolic figurehead while abolishing political authority created a complex legacy, blending traditional Islamic authority with colonial administration.
- **Modern Ideological Use**: The caliphate's legacy is invoked by contemporary groups like Boko Haram, though historians note fundamental differences between the original jihad's conservative Maliki-Sufi ideology and modern Salafi-jihadist thought.
