# Guelphs and Ghibellines

## Overview
The Guelphs and Ghibellines were rival political factions in medieval Italian city-states, supporting the Pope and the Holy Roman Emperor, respectively. Their conflict, rooted in the 11th-century Investiture Controversy, dominated Italian politics for centuries, shaping alliances, wars, and the balance of power between secular and ecclesiastical authority long after the original imperial-papal dispute had faded.

## Timeline
- **1075–1122** — Investiture Controversy between the Papacy and Holy Roman Empire begins and ends with the Concordat of Worms.
- **1140** — Siege of Weinsberg; the Welf and Hohenstaufen battle cries "Welf" and "Waiblingen" evolve into the names Guelph and Ghibelline.
- **1176** — Guelph Lombard League defeats Emperor Frederick Barbarossa at the Battle of Legnano, securing city autonomy.
- **1227–1250** — Emperor Frederick II clashes repeatedly with Popes Gregory IX and Innocent IV, intensifying the factional divide.
- **1260** — Ghibelline Siena defeats Guelph Florence at the Battle of Montaperti.
- **1268** — Execution of Conradin ends the Hohenstaufen dynasty; faction names become tied to local families.
- **1289** — Tuscan Guelphs defeat Ghibellines at the Battle of Campaldino.
- **c. 1300** — Florentine Guelphs split into Black (pro-papal) and White (anti-papal) factions; Dante is exiled as a White Guelph.
- **1325** — War of the Bucket between Guelph Bologna and Ghibelline Modena.
- **1529** — Charles V consolidates imperial power in Italy, rendering the factional division largely irrelevant.

## Body

### Origins and Imperial-Papal Conflict
The factions emerged from the Investiture Controversy, a dispute over whether secular rulers or the pope had authority to appoint bishops. After Emperor Henry V's death, the Welf family (ancestors of the Guelphs) and the Hohenstaufen dynasty (ancestors of the Ghibellines) vied for the imperial throne. The Welfs allied with the papacy, while the Hohenstaufens defended imperial rights. In Italy, cities threatened by the expanding Papal States often became Ghibelline, while those seeking autonomy from the Empire aligned with the Guelphs. The Guelph Lombard League's victory at Legnano in 1176 forced Emperor Frederick Barbarossa to recognize the autonomy of northern Italian cities.

### 13th-Century Struggles and Frederick II
The conflict intensified under Emperor Frederick II (r. 1220–1250), who clashed with Popes Gregory IX and Innocent IV. Frederick's campaigns to subdue rebellious cities and annex papal territories led to multiple excommunications. Despite setbacks like the Battle of Parma (1248), his forces often regained ground. Frederick's death in 1250 left the Hohenstaufen empire still powerful, but the struggle continued under his heirs, Conrad IV and Manfred of Sicily, against papal-backed forces like Charles I of Naples. The Ghibelline victory at Montaperti (1260) was a high point, but the dynasty's end in 1268 shifted the factions' focus from imperial-papal rivalry to local power struggles.

### Local Rivalries and Later Centuries
After the Hohenstaufen fell, the terms "Guelph" and "Ghibelline" became markers of allegiance to specific families or cities, not just the empire or papacy. In Florence, the Guelphs (often merchants) split into Black and White factions by 1300, leading to Dante's exile. In Genoa, rival families like the Guelph Fieschi and Grimaldi fought the Ghibelline Doria and Spinola. The conflict persisted into the 15th and 16th centuries, with factions aligning with foreign powers like France (Guelph) or the Emperor (Ghibelline) during the Italian Wars. By the mid-16th century, the division had lost political relevance.

### Heraldry and Cultural Legacy
Factions displayed their allegiance through heraldry: Guelphs used a "chief of Anjou" (blue with yellow fleurs-de-lis), while Ghibellines used a "chief of the empire" (golden with a black eagle). Architecture also differed, with Ghibelline crenellations being swallow-tailed and Guelphs square. The conflict inspired works like Dante's *Inferno* and Boccaccio's *Decameron*, and influenced later literature and art.

## Terms
- **Investiture Controversy**: The 11th–12th century conflict over whether secular rulers or the pope could appoint bishops.
- **Lombard League**: An alliance of northern Italian cities that resisted imperial authority, primarily Guelph-aligned.
- **Hohenstaufen**: A German royal dynasty that supported imperial rights against papal claims, giving rise to the Ghibellines.
- **Welf**: A German noble family whose name evolved into "Guelph," allied with the papacy.
- **Commune**: A self-governing Italian city-state, often the battleground for factional conflicts.
- **Capo d'Angiò**: A heraldic "chief of Anjou" used by Guelphs in coats of arms.
- **Capo dell'impero**: A heraldic "chief of the empire" used by Ghibellines in coats of arms.
- **Black and White Guelphs**: Florentine sub-factions; Blacks were pro-papal, Whites were anti-papal.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact origins of the names "Guelph" and "Ghibelline" from the battle cries at Weinsberg are debated, with some scholars questioning the direct linguistic evolution.
- The degree to which the factions were driven by ideological loyalty to the empire or papacy versus local political and economic interests remains a subject of historical analysis.
- The role of the conflict in shaping later Italian political identity and its long-term impact on the region's development is still studied.
