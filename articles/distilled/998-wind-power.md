# Wind power

## Overview  
Wind power is the conversion of wind’s kinetic energy into useful work, primarily electricity generation via wind turbines grouped into wind farms and connected to the electrical grid. It is a renewable, low-cost, and low-environmental-impact energy source, but its variability requires complementary dispatchable generation, storage, or demand management to ensure reliable supply. Rapid expansion is needed to meet Paris Agreement climate goals, though growth faces challenges from fossil fuel subsidies, permitting delays, and grid integration constraints.

## Timeline  
- **c. 9th century** – Windmills and windpumps developed in present-day Iran, Afghanistan, and Pakistan.  
- **1887** – First electricity-generating wind turbine built by James Blyth in Scotland.  
- **1888** – Charles F. Brush constructs a 12 kW wind turbine in Ohio, USA.  
- **1973** – Oil crisis spurs development of utility-scale wind generators in Denmark and the US.  
- **2008** – US installed wind capacity reaches 25.4 GW.  
- **2015** – Global wind share of electricity generation was 3.5%, up from earlier levels.  
- **2021** – With about 100 GW added during 2021, mostly in China and the United States, global installed wind power capacity exceeded 800 GW.  
- **2023** – 116.6 GW of new capacity added globally, a 50% increase over 2022, bringing total to 1,021 GW, marking 13% growth compared to the previous year.  
- **2024** – Wind supplies over 2,494 TWh, or 8.1% of world electricity; 30 countries generate over 10% of their electricity from wind.  
- **2025** – Wind supplies about 2,700 TWh, over 8% of world electricity.

## Body  

### Wind Energy Resources  
Wind power is proportional to the cube of wind speed, meaning doubling wind speed increases available power eightfold. The global wind kinetic energy averaged approximately 1.50 MJ/m² from 1979 to 2010, with the atmosphere acting as a thermal engine sustaining wind at 2.46 W/m². Wind resource assessment uses tools like the Global Wind Atlas and time-varying models such as Renewables.ninja. The Weibull distribution commonly models wind speed variability, with the Rayleigh distribution as a simpler approximation when the Weibull factor is near 2.

### Wind Farms  
A wind farm is a group of turbines in one location, often including hundreds of units across large areas. Most modern turbines are horizontal-axis machines with three blades and upwind rotors mounted on tall tubular towers. Turbines are spaced 7 rotor diameters apart and connected via medium-voltage collection systems. Offshore wind farms, such as the UK’s Hornsea Wind Farm (1,218 MW in November 2021), benefit from stronger, more consistent winds but face higher construction and maintenance costs.

### Generator Characteristics and Grid Integration  
Modern turbines typically use variable-speed generators with partial or full-scale power converters, offering better grid interconnection and low-voltage ride-through capabilities. Grid codes specify requirements for power factor, frequency constancy, and fault behavior. Black start capability is being developed in regions like Iowa where wind dominates generation.

### Capacity, Production, and Growth  
Wind supplied over 2,494 TWh in 2024, accounting for 8.1% of global electricity. Capacity factors range from 35% to 44%, depending on equipment and location. To meet Paris Agreement targets, analysts recommend annual expansion of over 1% of global electricity generation. Growth is hindered by fossil fuel subsidies and lengthy permitting processes.

### Variability and Predictability  
Wind output fluctuates hourly, daily, and seasonally, posing challenges for grid stability. There is an 80% chance output changes less than 10% in an hour, but a 40% chance of a 10% change within five hours. Weather forecasting helps prepare for predictable variations. Wind and solar are often complementary—solar peaks in summer while wind is stronger in winter.

### Energy Storage and Economics  
Conventional hydropower complements wind well by storing excess energy and releasing it during low-wind periods. Pumped-storage, compressed air, and thermal storage also support grid balance. Wind power has reached grid parity in many regions, with levelized costs falling to $26–$50/MWh in 2021. However, the merit order effect reduces market value during high-wind periods.

### Turbine Design  
Wind turbines convert kinetic energy into electricity through aerodynamic blade design. The Betz limit (59%) defines the maximum theoretical efficiency, with modern turbines achieving 70–80% of this. Designs must account for wake effects, blade strength, and control systems.

### History  
Wind power originated with sails and evolved into windmills for grinding grain and pumping water by the 9th century. The first electricity-generating turbine was built by James Blyth in 1887. The 1973 oil crisis catalyzed utility-scale development. By 2023, global capacity reached 1,021 GW.

### Politics and Public Opinion  
Public support for wind power remains strong in Europe and the US, though it declined from 75% in 2020 to 62% in 2021 in the US, with the Democratic Party supporting the use of wind energy twice as much as the Republican Party. Onshore projects face opposition over landscape impact, noise, and tourism concerns. Community ownership and benefit-sharing schemes help mitigate resistance. Governments are streamlining permitting to accelerate deployment.

## Terms  
- **Capacity Factor**: Ratio of actual annual energy output to theoretical maximum if operating at nameplate capacity continuously.  
- **Grid Parity**: Point at which wind power cost matches that of conventional energy sources.  
- **Betz Limit**: Maximum theoretical efficiency (59%) for converting wind kinetic energy to mechanical energy.  
- **Curtailment**: Reducing wind farm output below potential due to insufficient transmission capacity or grid constraints.  
- **Merit Order Effect**: Reduction in electricity market prices during periods of high renewable generation due to low marginal costs.  
- **Wake Effect**: Reduction in wind speed downstream of turbines, affecting subsequent turbines.  
- **Nameplate Capacity**: Maximum output rating of a turbine or power plant under ideal conditions.  
- **Levelized Cost of Electricity (LCOE)**: Average cost per unit of electricity over a plant’s lifetime, including capital, fuel, and operational expenses.  
- **Black Start**: Ability of a power plant to restart and supply electricity without external grid support.  
- **HVDC (High Voltage Direct Current)**: Efficient long-distance transmission technology used for connecting remote wind farms.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- **Maximum Penetration Levels**: No universally accepted upper limit for wind integration; depends on grid flexibility, storage, and interconnection.  
- **Role of Storage vs. Dispatchable Backup**: Whether battery storage or traditional backup plants are more cost-effective for balancing variability.  
- **Offshore vs. Onshore Expansion**: Trade-offs between higher offshore costs and greater wind consistency.  
- **Impact of Fossil Fuel Subsidies**: Extent to which subsidies slow renewable adoption globally.  
- **Community Acceptance**: Balancing local opposition with climate goals, particularly regarding landscape and noise impacts.  
- **Future of Floating Wind**: Potential for deeper-water installations versus high development costs.
