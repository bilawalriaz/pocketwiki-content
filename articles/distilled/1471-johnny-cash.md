# Johnny Cash

## Overview
John R. Cash (1932–2003) was an American singer-songwriter whose deep bass-baritone voice and genre-spanning catalog—encompassing country, rockabilly, folk, gospel, and blues—made him one of the best-selling music artists in history, with over 90 million records sold. Born to poor cotton farmers in Arkansas, he rose from the Sun Records rockabilly scene of the 1950s to become a cultural icon known as the "Man in Black," a persona adopted in solidarity with the poor, prisoners, and the marginalized. His career was defined by a tension between outlaw rebellion and devout Christian faith, marked by severe amphetamine and barbiturate addiction, historic prison concerts, and advocacy for Native American rights. A late-career collaboration with producer Rick Rubin on the *American Recordings* series reintroduced him to younger audiences through stark covers of contemporary rock songs, cementing his status as the only artist besides Elvis Presley inducted into both the Country Music and Rock and Roll Halls of Fame (as well as the Gospel Music Hall of Fame).

## Body

### Early Life and Musical Foundations
Cash was born in Kingsland, Arkansas, during the Great Depression. In 1935, his family joined the Dyess Colony, a New Deal resettlement project where they farmed cotton; Cash worked the fields from age five, singing gospel with his family. This poverty and a devastating 1937 flood (chronicled in "Five Feet High and Rising") instilled a lifelong empathy for the working class. A pivotal trauma occurred in 1944 when his beloved older brother Jack was fatally injured by an unguarded table saw; Cash carried guilt over the incident for decades. His early musical diet consisted of gospel radio, the Carter Family, Irish folk via Dennis Day on the Jack Benny program, and mentorship from African American guitarist Gus Cannon in Memphis. He learned guitar at 12 and sang on local radio in high school, his voice deepening from high tenor to bass-baritone.

### Military Service and the Sun Records Breakthrough
Cash enlisted in the Air Force in 1950, serving as a Morse code intercept operator in West Germany. He claimed to be among the first Americans to learn of Stalin’s death via intercepts. He formed his first band, the Landsberg Barbarians, and bought his first guitar. After discharge in 1954, he moved to Memphis, selling appliances while auditioning for Sam Phillips at Sun Records. Phillips initially rejected Cash’s gospel material, urging a commercial sound. Cash returned with a rockabilly style backed by Luther Perkins (guitar) and Marshall Grant (bass)—the Tennessee Two—scoring hits with "Cry! Cry! Cry!" and "Folsom Prison Blues." His 1956 "I Walk the Line" crossed over to the pop top 20. Frustrated by Phillips’s refusal to record gospel and a 3% royalty rate, Cash jumped to Columbia Records in 1958.

### The Outlaw Years: Addiction, Arrests, and Activism
As fame grew in the late 1950s, Cash developed severe dependencies on amphetamines (to endure touring) and barbiturates. His behavior became erratic; he was arrested seven times for misdemeanors (never serving more than one night). Notable incidents include a 1965 forest fire in California sparked by his camper (destroying 508 acres and endangered condor habitat, resulting in an $82,001 settlement) and a 1965 El Paso arrest for smuggling 688 Dexedrine and 475 Equanil tablets in his guitar case. Despite the chaos, he produced hits like "Ring of Fire" (1963) and concept albums *Bitter Tears* (1964), a protest record for Native Americans written largely by Peter La Farge, and *Sings the Ballads of the True West* (1965). *Bitter Tears* faced radio blacklisting; Cash famously took out a *Billboard* ad shaming DJs for cowardice, pushing "The Ballad of Ira Hayes" to #3 country. He was adopted by the Seneca Nation’s Turtle Clan in 1966 and performed benefit concerts on reservations.

### Redemption, Marriage, and the "Man in Black"
Cash hit bottom in 1967 after a Georgia arrest for pills following a car crash. Sheriff Ralph Jones’s stern warning catalyzed a turnaround. June Carter and the Carter Family moved in to help him detox; he proposed onstage in February 1968 and married her a week later. He rediscovered his faith via an altar call at Evangel Temple (pastored by Jimmie Rodgers Snow), though bassist Marshall Grant notes Cash did not fully quit amphetamines until ~1970, achieving seven clean years before relapses in 1977 and 1983. The 1968 *At Folsom Prison* and 1969 *At San Quentin* live albums were massive commercial triumphs, the latter yielding the #1 crossover hit "A Boy Named Sue." By the early 1970s, Cash codified his "Man in Black" image—wearing black for the poor, prisoners, and war dead—hosting *The Johnny Cash Show* (1969–71) on ABC, which showcased eclectic guests from Bob Dylan to Louis Armstrong and gave early exposure to Kris Kristofferson.

### The Highwaymen, Health Crises, and Columbia Departure
Inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame in 1980 (youngest living member at 48), Cash saw his chart relevance wane in the 1980s despite steady touring. He joined Waylon Jennings, Willie Nelson, and Kris Kristofferson as The Highwaymen (1985–95), releasing three successful albums. A 1983 ostrich attack on his farm caused abdominal injuries requiring painkillers, triggering a relapse. In 1988, while visiting a hospitalized Jennings, Cash was diagnosed with a heart condition and underwent double bypass surgery, claiming a near-death experience. His 1984 novelty single "The Chicken in Black"—often mythologized as a protest against Columbia—was actually a label suggestion Cash initially embraced, then withdrew after Jennings mocked the video. Columbia shelved sessions with Billy Sherrill (released 2014 as *Out Among the Stars*) and dropped Cash after 1985.

### American Recordings and Late-Career Renaissance
After a failed stint at Mercury (1987–91), Cash was signed by Rick Rubin to American Recordings (formerly Def American). Rubin stripped production to Cash alone with his Martin Dreadnought guitar in his living room. The 1994 *American Recordings* won a Grammy for Best Contemporary Folk Album and drew acclaim at Glastonbury. Subsequent volumes—*Unchained* (1996, with Tom Petty & the Heartbreakers, Grammy for Best Country Album), *American III: Solitary Man* (2000), *American IV: The Man Comes Around* (2002)—featured covers of Nine Inch Nails ("Hurt"), Depeche Mode ("Personal Jesus"), Soundgarden ("Rusty Cage"), and U2 ("The Wanderer," written by Bono). The "Hurt" video became a cultural touchstone. Cash also acted in *Dr. Quinn, Medicine Woman*, *The Simpsons* (as "Space Coyote"), and *North and South*.

### Personal Life: Vivian, June, and Faith
Cash married Vivian Liberto in 1954 during Air Force training; they had four daughters (Rosanne, Kathy, Cindy, Tara) and divorced in 1966 due to his addiction, touring, and infidelity. He met June Carter backstage at the Grand Ole Opry in 1955; their 13-year courtship culminated in marriage in 1968 and one son, John Carter Cash (1970). June was his anchor through multiple rehabs, famously flushing his pills. Cash’s faith was central yet unconventional: baptized Southern Baptist in 1944, he later identified simply as "a Christian," ordained as a minister in the 1970s, pilgrimaged to Israel (1978), narrated the New Testament, and co-wrote the film *Gospel Road* (1973). He befriended Billy Graham and performed at his crusades, viewing his work as testimony rather than proselytizing.

### Illness and Death
In 1997, Cash was diagnosed with Shy–Drager syndrome (multiple system atrophy), initially misdiagnosed as Parkinson’s. The prognosis was 18 months; the diagnosis later shifted to autonomic neuropathy from diabetes. He curtailed touring, hospitalized for pneumonia in 1998. June died May 15, 2003; Cash recorded 60 songs in his final four months, performing his last concert July 5, 2003, where he spoke of June visiting him "from Heaven." He died September 12, 2003, at Baptist Hospital in Nashville, age 71, buried beside June at Hendersonville Memory Gardens.

### Legacy and Posthumous Honors
Cash wrote over 1,000 songs and influenced artists across genres; Bob Dylan credited Cash’s 1964 *Broadside* letter ("shut up and let me sing") and support for his electric turn. Posthumous releases include *American V: A Hundred Highways* (2006, debuted #1), *American VI: Ain't No Grave* (2010), and *Songwriter* (2024, 1993 demos). The 2005 biopic *Walk the Line* (Phoenix/Witherspoon) won two Oscars. Honors: Grammy Lifetime Achievement (1999), National Medal of Arts (2001), *Rolling Stone* #31 Greatest Artists (2004). His boyhood home in Dyess is a National Historic Site; a tarantula (*Aphonopelma johnnycashi*), a Nashville Sounds mascot race, and the Johnny Cash Trail in Folsom commemorate him.

## Terms
- **Rockabilly**: Early 1950s fusion of country and rhythm & blues characterized by uptempo rhythms, slap bass, and twangy guitar; Cash’s first commercial style at Sun Records.
- **Tennessee Three**: Cash’s long-time backing band (originally Tennessee Two: Luther Perkins, guitar; Marshall Grant, bass; later adding W.S. "Fluke" Holland, drums), known for "train-like chugging" boom-chicka-boom rhythm.
- **Million Dollar Quartet**: Impromptu Dec 4, 1956 jam session at Sun Studios among Elvis Presley, Jerry Lee Lewis, Carl Perkins, and Cash; recordings released decades later.
- **Bitter Tears (1964)**: Concept album of protest songs for Native Americans, written largely by Peter La Farge; resisted by radio and Columbia, championed by Cash via *Billboard* ad.
- **Man in Black**: Cash’s signature all-black stage attire, adopted early for practicality, later explained as mourning for the poor, prisoners, addicts, and war dead.
- **Folsom Prison Blues / At Folsom Prison**: Signature song (1955) and 1968 live album recorded at Folsom State Prison; the album hit #1 country and crossed to pop, reviving Cash’s career.
- **The Highwaymen**: 1985–95 country supergroup of Cash, Waylon Jennings, Willie Nelson, Kris Kristofferson; three albums, toured extensively.
- **American Recordings**: Rick Rubin’s label (rebranded from Def American) and the 1994–2003 series of sparse, cover-heavy albums that revitalized Cash’s late career.
- **Shy–Drager syndrome**: Neurodegenerative disease (multiple system atrophy) diagnosed in 1997; later reclassified as autonomic neuropathy from diabetes.
- **Carter Family**: Pioneering country group (A.P., Sara, Maybelle Carter); Cash toured with Maybelle and her daughters (Anita, June, Helen) in early 1960s; married June in 1968.

## Debates and open questions
- **Origin of "Ring of Fire"**: June Carter Cash claimed the song’s mariachi horn arrangement came to Johnny in a dream; his first wife Vivian Liberto asserted in her 2007 memoir that Johnny gave June co-writing credit for monetary reasons.
- **Cause of 1965 forest fire**: Cash blamed a defective camper exhaust; his nephew Damon Fielder believed Cash started a warming fire and, impaired by drugs, let it escape.
- **"The Chicken in Black" motive**: Biographer Robert Hilburn (2013) disputes the legend that Cash recorded this 1984 novelty single to sabotage his Columbia contract, arguing Cash enthusiastically accepted the label’s suggestion until Waylon Jennings mocked the video.
- **Ancestry claims**: Family lore claimed Cherokee ancestry via paternal grandmother; a 2021 DNA test on daughter Rosanne (via *Finding Your Roots*) found no Native American markers but identified sub-Saharan African DNA on the paternal line.
- **Extent of late-life sobriety**: Marshall Grant’s memoir states Cash did not fully quit amphetamines until ~1970 (not 1968), relapsed in 1977 and 1983, and entered multiple rehabs (Betty Ford, Cumberland Heights, Loma Linda) through the 1990s.
