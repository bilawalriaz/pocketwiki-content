# Alexis de Tocqueville

## Overview
Alexis de Tocqueville (1805–1859) was a French aristocrat, diplomat, and political philosopher whose analyses of democracy, revolution, and empire remain foundational to sociology and political science. His masterwork, *Democracy in America* (1835/1840), written after a nine-month U.S. tour, diagnosed the novel social state of equality reshaping Western civilization, warning that democracy’s drive toward equality could foster individualism, majority tyranny, and a soft despotism that infantilizes citizens. In *The Old Regime and the Revolution* (1856), he argued the French Revolution continued the centralizing modernization of the state begun under Louis XIV, failing only because inexperienced deputies clung to abstract Enlightenment ideals. A classical liberal active in the July Monarchy and Second Republic, Tocqueville defended parliamentary government and abolitionism yet supported the violent colonization of Algeria, advocating racial segregation there while opposing the invasion of Kabylia. His restless liberalism—shifting from centre-left to centre-right—has invited claimed by diverse political traditions.

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Tocqueville was born into an old Norman aristocratic family; his great-grandfather, the statesman Malesherbes, was guillotined in 1793, and his parents narrowly escaped the same fate during the Terror. Under the Bourbon Restoration, his father became a peer and prefect. Tocqueville attended the Lycée Fabert in Metz. This background—aristocratic by birth, liberal by conviction, haunted by revolutionary violence—shaped his lifelong preoccupation with the transition from aristocracy to democracy and the conditions of stable liberty.

### Travels and *Democracy in America*
**North America (1831–1832).** Ostensibly sent by the July Monarchy to study U.S. prisons, Tocqueville and Gustave de Beaumont traveled from eastern cities to the Michigan frontier, down the Ohio and Mississippi to New Orleans, and across the South. They also visited Lower Canada. The result was a joint prison report (1833) and, more famously, *Democracy in America* (Vol. I, 1835; Vol. II, 1840). Tocqueville wrote as a detached social scientist observing a society in the grip of the Market Revolution, Western expansion, and Jacksonian democracy. He aimed to help the French understand their own position between a fading aristocratic order and an emerging democratic one.

**England and Ireland (1833, 1835).** Tocqueville visited England to court Mary Mottley, marrying her in 1836. He observed the 1832 Reform Act, concluding England could democratize without violent convulsions because its nobility was open to new recruits—unlike France, where *gentilhomme* denoted birth alone. In Ireland (1835), he found “all the evils of an aristocracy and none of its advantages”: no moral tie between Protestant landlords and Catholic tenants, producing an “unbelievable unity” between the Irish clergy and the people. He noted English centralization was legislative, not administrative, coexisting with a vibrant civic association that limited state intervention.

**Algeria (1841, 1846).** After France invaded Algeria in 1830, Tocqueville became parliament’s foremost expert. Initially hoping for Franco-Arab amalgamation, his 1841 visit reversed his view. He criticized the “coarseness and violence” of military rule toward colonists but concluded Algerian society lacked political life, justifying violent subjugation and rejecting assimilation of Muslims into French civil life. He advocated a dual legal system—separate legislation for European settlers and Arab populations—a structure later realized in the 1870 Crémieux Decree and *Code de l’indigénat*. Yet he opposed the 1847 invasion of Kabylia, likening the Kabyles to Rousseau’s “noble savage” and denouncing General Bugeaud’s plan as seditious. His 1847 *Report on Algeria* warned that unchanged methods would end in a bloodbath, urging domination only of commercially strategic points (e.g., Port of Algiers, Strait of Gibraltar) rather than total political control.

### Political Career: July Monarchy to Second Republic
Tocqueville despised the July Monarchy (1830–1848) but entered the Chamber of Deputies in 1839, sitting centre-left. He defended abolitionism and free trade while supporting Algerian colonization. In 1847 he tried to found a “Young Left” party advocating wage increases and progressive taxation to undercut socialist appeal.

After the February 1848 Revolution, he was elected to the Constituent Assembly, serving on the constitutional commission. He defended bicameralism and presidential election by universal suffrage, viewing the conservative countryside as a check on revolutionary Paris. During the June Days Uprising (1848), he backed General Cavaignac’s suppression, advocating “regularization” of the state of siege and suspension of constitutional order. As Foreign Minister (June–October 1849), he supported laws restricting clubs and the press, arguing order was the *sine qua non* for serious politics and the precondition for steady liberty. This contrasted with his defence of freedoms in *Democracy in America*.

He supported Cavaignac against Louis Napoléon Bonaparte in the 1848 presidential election. After Bonaparte’s 2 December 1851 coup, Tocqueville joined deputies resisting at the 10th arrondissement, was detained at Vincennes, and retired to his château, refusing to serve a “usurper and despot.” Biographer Joseph Epstein notes he gave thirteen years to political liberty and spent his final years fighting the same battle from his desk.

### *Democracy in America*: Core Arguments
**Equality, Liberty, and the Social State.** Tocqueville saw democracy as an enterprise balancing liberty and equality. He warned of a “depraved taste for equality” that impels the weak to pull the strong down, preferring “equality in servitude to inequality in freedom.” Centralized government, he wrote, “excels in preventing, not doing.” Inheritance laws dividing estates among heirs (unlike French primogeniture) created a constant churn of wealth, making hereditary fortunes insecure and forcing each generation to struggle anew. This fostered a society devoted to “middling” values and money-making, unlike Europe where the lower classes had no hope of wealth and the upper classes found money-making vulgar.

**Civil Society, Individualism, and Association.** For Tocqueville, civil society was a sphere of private entrepreneurship regulated by civil code (a view shared by Hegel and Marx). He redefined *individualism* not as selfishness but as a “calm and considered feeling” disposing citizens to withdraw into family and friends, leaving the greater society to itself. It could yield positive “self-interest properly understood”—prompting association for common purposes—or negative isolation. Association, he argued, counters the tyranny of the majority by enabling citizens to “take control over their own lives” without government aid. Critics debate whether individualism is a corrective to egoism (Kaplan) or merely a delayed form of it (Schwindt).

**Tyranny of the Majority and Soft Despotism.** Tocqueville warned that radical equality could birth new tyrannies: a materialist bourgeoisie and atomized individualism might make citizens “enamored with a relaxed love of present enjoyments,” meekly accepting a despotic power “all the more powerful because it does not resemble one.” Unlike Roman emperors who affected only small groups, democratic despotism would extend an “immense protective power” over a “multitude of men, uniformly alike, equal, constantly circling for petty pleasures,” keeping them as “perpetual children” like a shepherd over a “flock of timid animals.”

**Majority Rule and Mediocrity.** Ordinary Americans refused to defer to intellectual superiors, producing equality but also mediocrity. Men of talent faced two choices: join limited intellectual circles or amass fortunes in the private sector. Tocqueville knew “no country where there is less independence of mind, and true freedom of discussion, than in America.” The majority encloses thought within a “formidable fence”; stepping outside brings not inquisition but daily persecution and political exclusion. Kaplan interprets this as Tocqueville identifying the problem not that people are too strong, but that they feel “too weak” and “swept up in something they could not control.”

**Race, Slavery, and Indigenous Peoples.** Tocqueville perceived the evils inflicted on Black and Indigenous peoples. He contrasted Virginia’s gold-seeking adventurers with New England’s religious Puritans, identifying slavery as the “capital fact” shaping the South: it “dishonors labor,” introduces idleness, ignorance, pride, luxury, and distress. He observed racial prejudice was *stronger* in states that had abolished slavery than where it persisted. Colonization (Liberia) could not keep pace with Black population growth; the “Negro race will never leave those shores.” As a “persevering enemy of despotism,” he was “pained and astonished” that the freest people maintained personal servitude. On Indigenous peoples, he predicted assimilation was impossible due to their pride, so they would become extinct; displacement was policy. He opposed Arthur de Gobineau’s racial hierarchy theories. Historian Olivier Le Cour Grandmaison argues Tocqueville was ahead of his time in using “extermination” to describe U.S. colonization and Indian removal.

**Jury Service.** Tocqueville considered the civil jury the most effective showcase of democracy: it communicates the “spirit of the judges” to citizens, invests each with a “kind of magistracy,” and makes them feel duties toward society. Jury service educates citizens in self-government and rule of law, benefiting both society and the juror’s civic qualities.

**United States and Russia.** He forecast the preeminence of the United States and Russia as the two main global powers, each “called by some secret design of Providence one day to hold in its hands the destinies of half the world.”

### *The Old Regime and the Revolution* (1856)
Published after his political retirement, this work analyzes French society before 1789. Tocqueville argued the Revolution’s importance was to continue the modernization and centralization of the French state begun under Louis XIV. Its failure stemmed from the inexperience of deputies “too wedded to abstract Enlightenment ideals.” The book investigates the structural forces—administrative centralization, fiscal privilege, literary politics—that made the Revolution probable and shaped its course.

### Religion and Personal Life
A lifelong tuberculosis sufferer, Tocqueville died in 1859, survived by his wife Mary Mottley (married 1836, no children). She converted to Catholicism before marriage; Tocqueville’s own religiosity was “utilitarian,” viewing religion as a “social cement” and safety valve for passions that might otherwise fuel revolutionary torrents dangerous to liberty. Provided it was separated from state power, he did not believe the church was bound to be anti-democratic.

## Terms
- ****Individualism**** — A “calm and considered feeling” disposing citizens to isolate themselves from the mass and withdraw into a narrow circle of family and friends, distinct from egoism (blind instinct).
- ****Self-interest properly understood**** — The positive form of individualism where citizens associate for mutual purpose, recognizing that their own long-term interest aligns with the common good.
- ****Tyranny of the majority**** — The omnipotence of majority opinion that encloses thought within a “formidable fence,” persecuting dissenters socially and politically rather than legally.
- ****Soft despotism**** — A democratic despotism that does not break wills but guides them, keeping citizens as “perpetual children” under an “immense protective power” that provides petty pleasures.
- ****Social state**** — The fundamental condition of a society (e.g., equality of conditions) that determines its laws, mores, and political possibilities.
- ****Centralization**** — The concentration of administrative or legislative power; Tocqueville distinguished French administrative centralization from English legislative centralization.
- ****Code de l’indigénat**** — The legal code governing Muslim Algerians as subjects rather than citizens, contrasted with French civil law for European settlers (later realized after Tocqueville’s advocacy of dual legislation).
- ****Primogeniture**** — Inheritance by the eldest child (usually male), keeping estates intact; contrasted with U.S. partible inheritance that fragmented wealth across generations.
- ****Market Revolution**** — The early 19th-century transformation of the U.S. economy through commercialization, transportation improvements, and wage labor, observed by Tocqueville.
- ****July Monarchy**** — The constitutional monarchy of Louis-Philippe I (1830–1848), which Tocqueville despised but served as a deputy.

## Debates and open questions
- **Individualism vs. egoism:** Kaplan argues Tocqueville saw individualism as a correctable way of thinking that can foster association; Schwindt contends it is merely a delayed form of egoism that ultimately merges with it.
- **Liberalism’s consistency:** Tocqueville’s support for repressive laws in 1849 (press, clubs, state of siege) contrasts with his *Democracy in America* defence of liberties. Scholars debate whether this reflects a pragmatic “order first” liberalism or a contradiction.
- **Algeria: moderate or architect of apartheid?** Jean-Louis Benoît calls Tocqueville a “moderate supporter” of colonization who opposed Kabylia’s invasion; Olivier Le Cour Grandmaison emphasizes his justification of “extermination” warfare, advocacy of racial segregation, and rejection of Muslim assimilation.
- **Race and democracy:** Tocqueville opposed Gobineau’s racial hierarchy yet deemed Black and Indigenous peoples “undemocratic” and predicted Indigenous extinction. The tension between his anti-slavery moralism and his colonial policies remains contested.
- **Religion’s role:** Was Tocqueville’s “utilitarian” view of religion (social cement, separated from state) a genuine theological stance or a political instrument for stabilizing democracy?
- **Unfinished second volume of *The Old Regime and the Revolution*:** The incomplete work leaves open how Tocqueville would have traced the Revolution’s legacy into the Second Empire.
