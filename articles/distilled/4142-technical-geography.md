# Technical geography

## Overview
Technical geography is the branch of geography focused on developing and applying tools, methods, and theories to obtain, analyze, interpret, and communicate spatial information. It is distinct from human and physical geography in its primary concern with the techniques and technologies themselves, rather than the specific content of the data. This branch provides the foundational methods—such as cartography, Geographic Information Systems (GIS), and remote sensing—that are essential for research across all geographical disciplines.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Origins of cartography and surveying, early forms of technical geography.
- **9th century** — Islamic geographers adopt technical elements from Ptolemy's *Geographia*.
- **1739** — Term "technical geography" first appears in English in *Geography Reform'd*.
- **1950s-1960s** — Quantitative revolution leads to clearer differentiation of technical geography.
- **1959** — Waldo Tobler publishes first paper on computer cartography.
- **1960** — Roger Tomlinson creates the first Geographic Information System (GIS).
- **1970** — Waldo Tobler proposes his first law of geography.
- **1978** — First GPS satellites launched by the U.S. military.
- **1990s** — Key sub-disciplines like GIScience, geomatics, and geoinformatics emerge.
- **2000** — Full GPS capability made available to the public.
- **2006** — *Geographia Technica* journal established; UCGIS publishes GISTBoK.
- **2009** — UNESCO EOLSS adopts a three-branch model including technical geography.

## Body

### Core Concepts and Laws
Technical geography is grounded in theoretical concepts for handling spatial-temporal data. Central among these are **autocorrelation** (the statistical measure of how a variable correlates with itself across space or time, foundational to Tobler's first law of geography) and **frequency** (the count of events across space or time). **Cartographic generalization**—the process of simplifying map data to reveal patterns—is a fundamental technique. The discipline also engages with proposed "laws of geography," such as Tobler's first law, which emerged from the quantitative revolution's shift toward empirical, law-making geography.

### History and Evolution
The term "technical geography" was used as early as 1739 to describe the theory and methods of map and globe construction. While techniques like surveying are ancient, the field differentiated clearly during the 20th-century quantitative revolution, spurred by WWII advances in cartography and aerial photography. The later Information Age, with computers, satellites, and software, pushed the focus toward information management. This led to the creation of overlapping sub-disciplines like geographic information science (GIScience), geomatics, and geoinformatics, whose definitions are still debated.

### Techniques and Tools
Technical geography researches and employs a suite of technologies. **Remote sensing** (acquiring data about Earth from aircraft or satellites) advanced rapidly through military applications in the World Wars and Cold War. **Computer cartography and GIS** (systems for storing, analyzing, and visualizing spatial data) revolutionized the field after the 1960s, becoming a primary tool. The **Global Positioning System (GPS)**, fully available to the public in 2000, enabled rapid, low-cost acquisition of spatial coordinates. The discipline also studies the relationship between these tools and their users.

### Sub-branches and Criticism
Major sub-branches include quantitative geography (focusing on statistical methods), geomatics, geoinformatics, and GIScience. There is ongoing ontological debate about whether "technical geography" is the best term to unify these fields, with alternatives like "geographic information technology" also in use. Some critics argue geography must always be applied to a human or physical subset, denying the need for a separate branch. Additionally, allegations of gender bias exist, as the field is disproportionately male, possibly linked to perceptions of science as a masculine domain.

## Terms
*   **Autocorrelation:** A statistical measure of how similar data points are to each other based on their spatial or temporal distance.
*   **Cartographic Generalization:** The process of simplifying geographic information on a map to ensure clarity and communication at a given scale.
*   **Geographic Information System (GIS):** A framework for gathering, managing, and analyzing spatial and geographic data.
*   **Geomatics:** The discipline of gathering, storing, processing, and delivering geographic information or spatially referenced data.
*   **Geoinformatics:** The science and technology dealing with the structure and character of spatial information, its capture, storage, and processing.
*   **Geographic Information Science (GIScience):** The academic discipline which studies data structures and computational techniques to capture, represent, process, and analyze geographic information.
*   **Remote Sensing:** The acquisition of information about an object or phenomenon without making physical contact, typically from aircraft or satellites.
*   **Quantitative Geography:** A subfield emphasizing the use of mathematical and statistical methods to analyze spatial data.
*   **Tobler's First Law of Geography:** "Everything is related to everything else, but near things are more related than distant things."

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Terminology and Ontology:** There is no consensus on the best term ("technical geography," "GIScience," "geomatics," etc.) to describe the study of spatial information methods as a distinct branch of geography.
*   **Status as a Branch:** Some scholars argue that geography is inherently applied and cannot have a separate "technical" branch focused solely on methods, contending there is insufficient peer-reviewed literature to support it.
*   **Gender Bias:** The field faces criticism for a culture that may introduce gender bias, as it is disproportionately practiced by men, which some link to broader perceptions of science and technology.
*   **Relationship with Critical Geography:** A historical tension exists between the positivist, quantitative methods of technical geography and the ideological critiques of critical geography.
