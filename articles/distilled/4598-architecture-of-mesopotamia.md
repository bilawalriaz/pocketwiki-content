# Architecture of Mesopotamia

## Overview
Mesopotamian architecture, spanning from the 10th millennium BC to the 6th century BC, encompasses the built environment of cultures like the Sumerians, Akkadians, Babylonians, and Assyrians in the Tigris–Euphrates river system. It is foundational to understanding early urbanization, as it pioneered concepts like city planning, the courtyard house, and monumental religious structures such as ziggurats. The study relies on archaeological evidence, texts, and pictorial representations, revealing a society where architecture was deeply tied to religion, social order, and the practical challenges of a riverine environment.

## Timeline
- **10th millennium BC** — First permanent structures built in the region.
- **4th millennium BC (Uruk period)** — Development of urban planning and monumental temple complexes at Uruk.
- **3rd millennium BC (Early Dynastic Period)** — Emergence of palaces; explosion of temple design diversity; construction of early ziggurats.
- **c. 3000 BC** — Introduction of courtyards into residential architecture.
- **2nd millennium BC (Old Babylonian Period)** — Continued development of palaces, as seen at Mari.
- **1st millennium BC (Iron Age)** — Assyrian palaces with elaborate stone relief sculptures and lamassu figures built at Nimrud, Khorsabad, and Nineveh.
- **6th century BC** — Latest known ziggurats constructed.

## Body

### Building Materials and Decoration
The primary building material was sun-baked mudbrick, which was less durable than fired brick and led to the periodic rebuilding of structures on the same spot, creating elevated mounds known as **tells**. Brick styles evolved over time, with plano-convex bricks common in the Early Dynastic Period. Decoration evolved from painted plaster and colored cones in earlier periods to sculptured stone slabs (orthostats) lining Assyrian palace walls. Babylonian architecture favored brilliant colors, glazed tiles, and three-dimensional figures, while imported materials like cedar and lapis lazuli were prized.

### Urban Planning
The Sumerians were the first to conceive of the city as a planned construct, with Uruk as a template. Cities were typically divided into residential, commercial, and civic spaces, with a high temple complex at the core. A belt of irrigated agricultural land surrounded the city, connected by a three-tiered network of roads and, more importantly, canals. Urban growth was a mix of planned elements (walls, main streets) and organic development within those limits.

### Houses
The predominant house type was the inward-facing **courtyard house** (*bītu*), which organized rooms around an open central space for cooling and privacy. Houses were built from mudbrick, mud plaster, and reeds, with wooden doors and roofs. They often had multiple stories, with the ground floor used for shops or storage and upper floors for living and sleeping. The design emphasized a strict division between public and private space, with a single, indirect entrance from the street.

### Palaces
Palaces (*ekallu*, "big house") emerged in the Early Dynastic I period and grew in complexity as political power centralized. They were large socio-economic institutions containing residential quarters, workshops, storehouses, and shrines. Assyrian palaces of the Iron Age are especially famous for their extensive narrative stone reliefs and colossal protective figures like **lamassu**. Their architecture was organized around large ceremonial courtyards.

### Temples and Ziggurats
Temples often predated the cities they were in and evolved from small shrines to massive complexes. They were conceived as the meeting point between gods and humans, symbolically representing the cosmic **axis mundi**. Early temples used a "bent-axis" approach, while later ones adopted a straight axis. The **ziggurat**, a stepped pyramidal tower, became a defining feature. Built of a mudbrick core with a baked brick facing, it supported a shrine at its summit. The Ziggurat of Ur is a classic example, designed with three receding stages and monumental staircases.

### Landscape Architecture
Open space planning was integral to Mesopotamian cities, with areas set aside for orchards and gardens. Irrigation agriculture created some of the first garden forms, such as the enclosed quadrangular *sar*, a precursor to later Persian paradise gardens. Fountains and water features, like a carved basin from Girsu dating to c. 3000 BC, were also present.

## Terms
- **Tell**: An artificial mound formed by the accumulated debris of successive settlements built on the same site.
- **Ziggurat**: A massive stepped pyramidal temple tower, built of sun-baked brick with a baked brick facing, topped by a shrine.
- **Courtyard house (bītu)**: The typical Mesopotamian house design, with rooms arranged around an open central courtyard.
- **Lamassu**: A protective mythological figure with a human head, body of a bull or lion, and wings, often flanking palace gates.
- **Orthostat**: A stone slab set upright along the lower part of a wall, often carved with reliefs in Assyrian palaces.
- **Axis mundi**: A symbolic center or connection point between the heavens, earth, and underworld; the temple served this function.
- **Bent-axis approach**: A temple design where the entrance requires a 90-degree turn to face the cult statue, contrasting with a straight-axis approach.
- **Apotropaic**: Having the power to avert evil influences or bad luck; a function of figures like the lamassu.

## Debates and Open questions
- The precise function and symbolic meaning of ziggurats are debated; one hypothesis suggests they were built to resemble mountains, but textual and archaeological evidence for this is considered limited.
- The reconstruction of the third stage of the Ziggurat of Ur by excavator Leonard Woolley is an interpretation, as only two stages survive.
- The social organization and construction methods of residential architecture, particularly for the poorest inhabitants who may have used perishable materials, are not well-documented archaeologically.
