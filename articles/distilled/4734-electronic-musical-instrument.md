# Electronic musical instrument

## Overview
An electronic musical instrument (electrophone) produces sound using electronic circuitry, outputting an audio signal that is amplified and played through a loudspeaker. They are a fundamental category in modern music, especially in popular and electronic genres, and represent an active, interdisciplinary field of research in new interfaces and sound generation.

## Body

### Classification and Definition
In musicology, electronic instruments are classified as electrophones, the fifth category in the Hornbostel-Sachs system, added by Sachs in 1940. The category is debated. Sachs included electrically actuated or amplified acoustic instruments (e.g., electric guitar) and instruments where sound is primarily generated by electrically driven oscillators (e.g., synthesizers). Modern ethnomusicologists argue that, to align with the system's core principle of classifying by initial sound source, only the oscillator-based instruments (subcategory 53) should be considered true electrophones.

### Early Development (18th Century–1940s)
The first electrified instruments, like the Denis d'or (1753) and clavecin électrique (1761), used electricity to enhance acoustic mechanisms, not to generate sound. The first true electric synthesizer was Elisha Gray's Musical Telegraph (1876). The invention of the vacuum tube (audion) in 1906 enabled signal generation and amplification, leading to instruments like the Telharmonium (1897), Theremin (1919), and ondes Martenot (1928). The Hammond organ (1929), based on tonewheels, became widely popular, while the Novachord (1938) was the first commercial synthesizer, featuring envelope control—a key feature of later synths.

### Analogue Synthesis and the Studio Era (1950s–1970s)
Early synthesis was analogue, using voltage-controlled oscillators, filters, and amplifiers. Large, modular systems like the RCA Mark II (1957) were studio-bound. Robert Moog's modular synthesizer (1964) was more musician-friendly and established the 1-volt-per-octave pitch control standard. The Minimoog (1970) was the first portable, integrated synthesizer, making analogue synthesis accessible for live performance. Early synths were mostly monophonic (one note at a time). Polyphony (chords) was first achieved in organs, then in complex synths like the Sequential Circuits Prophet-5 (1977), which used digital memory to store and recall sounds.

### The Digital Revolution and MIDI (1980s–2000s)
Digital synthesis, using computers to generate sound, became practical with Yamaha's DX7 (1983), which used FM synthesis and was the first mass-market digital synth. Sampling—playing back recorded sounds—was pioneered by the Fairlight CMI (1978). A crucial standard, MIDI (1983), allowed electronic instruments, computers, and controllers to communicate, enabling complex studio setups and separating the user interface (controller) from the sound generator (synthesizer).

### Modern Instruments and Culture
Today, electronic instruments are ubiquitous. The separation of controller and synthesizer is common, with interfaces ranging from traditional keyboards to novel designs like the touchpad-based Kaossilator or the tangible-block Reactable. A vibrant DIY culture exists, including "circuit bending" (creatively short-circuiting devices) and building modular synthesizer kits. Chiptune music uses the sound chips of vintage computers and game consoles.

## Terms
- **Electrophone**: A musical instrument that produces sound primarily through electronic circuitry.
- **Synthesizer**: An electronic instrument that artificially generates sound using techniques like analogue circuitry or digital processing.
- **MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface)**: A standard protocol that allows electronic instruments, computers, and other devices to communicate and control each other.
- **Polyphony**: The ability of an instrument to play multiple notes or chords simultaneously.
- **FM Synthesis**: A digital sound synthesis method that uses frequency modulation to generate complex timbres.
- **Sampling**: The technique of recording and playing back snippets of sound (samples) as musical notes.
- **Modular Synthesizer**: A synthesizer composed of separate, interchangeable modules (e.g., oscillators, filters) connected by patch cables.
- **Controller**: A user interface device (e.g., a keyboard, touchpad) that sends performance data to a separate sound-generating synthesizer.
- **Circuit Bending**: The creative practice of modifying the circuits of battery-powered electronic devices to create new, often unpredictable, musical instruments.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary scholarly debate concerns the classification of electrophones within the Hornbostel-Sachs system. The original 1940 addition by Sachs included electrically controlled acoustic instruments (like the electric guitar). However, modern ethnomusicologists argue this violates the system's core principle of classifying by the initial sound source. They propose that only instruments where sound is *primarily* generated by electronic oscillators (subcategory 53) should be classified as electrophones, placing instruments like the electric guitar in their acoustic categories (chordophones).
