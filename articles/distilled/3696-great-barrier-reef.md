# Great Barrier Reef

## Overview
The Great Barrier Reef is the world's largest coral reef system, a vast structure of over 2,900 individual reefs and 900 islands stretching over 2,300 km off the coast of Queensland, Australia. Built by billions of tiny coral polyps, it is the planet's biggest single structure made by living organisms and supports immense biodiversity. It is a critical natural wonder facing severe threats, primarily from climate change-induced coral bleaching, making its preservation a global concern.

## Timeline
- **c. 25 million years ago** — Northern Queensland drifts into tropical waters, allowing initial coral growth.
- **c. 9,000 years ago** — The current, living reef structure begins growing on an older platform after the Last Glacial Maximum.
- **1770** — James Cook's ship, HMS Endeavour, runs aground on the reef.
- **1802** — Matthew Flinders maps and names the "Great Barrier Reef."
- **1975** — The Great Barrier Reef Marine Park is established by the Australian Government.
- **1981** — The reef is selected as a World Heritage Site.
- **2004** — A new zoning plan increases highly protected zones from 4.5% to over 33.3%.
- **2016-2017** — Severe mass coral bleaching events cause widespread coral death, especially in the north.
- **2019** — The latest five-year Outlook Report on the reef's health is published.
- **2022** — Another mass bleaching event is confirmed; annual surveys show greatest coral cover recovery in 36 years in some areas.

## Body

### Geology and Formation
The reef's development is complex, influenced by tectonic movement, sea level changes, and sedimentation. The substrate is a coastal plain formed from eroded sediments of the Great Dividing Range. Coral skeletons date back 500,000 years, but the current living reef is estimated to be 6,000 to 9,000 years old, having grown on older platforms as sea levels rose after the last ice age. The reef system is divided into 70 bioregions, featuring diverse structures like ribbon reefs in the north and fringing reefs in the south.

### Ecology and Biodiversity
The reef supports extraordinary life, including 30 cetacean species, 1,500 fish species, six sea turtle species, and 400 coral species. Many corals reproduce via mass spawning events triggered by water temperature and lunar cycles. The ecosystem also includes dugongs, sharks, molluscs, and vast seagrass beds. The islands host over 2,000 plant species and are critical nesting sites for over a million birds.

### Environmental Threats
The primary threats are climate change, pollution, crown-of-thorns starfish outbreaks, and overfishing. Climate change causes ocean warming and acidification, leading to mass coral bleaching events (notably in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017, and 2020) where corals expel symbiotic algae and often die. Over half the reef's coral cover was lost between 1995 and 2017. Pollution from agricultural runoff (sediments, nutrients, pesticides) degrades water quality, exacerbating bleaching and fueling crown-of-thorns starfish outbreaks, which prey on coral.

### Human Use and Management
The reef has been used by Aboriginal Australian and Torres Strait Islander peoples for at least 40,000 years. Today, tourism is a major economic activity, generating billions of AUD annually and attracting about two million visitors per year. Management is coordinated through the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority under the "Reef 2050 Plan," which aims to improve water quality and build resilience. Key measures include zoning (with over 33% highly protected), regulations on fishing and tourism, and efforts to reduce land-based pollution. However, critics note the plan does not sufficiently address the root cause of climate change.

## Terms
- **Coral Polyp:** The tiny, soft-bodied organism that is the building block of coral reefs, secreting a calcium carbonate skeleton.
- **Coral Bleaching:** A stress response where corals expel the symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae) living in their tissues, causing them to turn white and often die if prolonged.
- **Crown-of-Thorns Starfish:** A large, coral-eating starfish whose population outbreaks can devastate reef areas.
- **Eutrophication:** Excessive enrichment of water with nutrients (like nitrogen and phosphorus), leading to algal blooms that deplete oxygen and harm other life.
- **Ocean Acidification:** The ongoing decrease in ocean pH caused by the absorption of atmospheric carbon dioxide, which reduces the availability of aragonite, a mineral essential for coral growth.
- **Mass Spawning:** The synchronized release of eggs and sperm by many coral species, typically once a year, for reproduction.
- **Bioregion:** A geographically distinct area with a characteristic assemblage of natural communities and species.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Effectiveness of the Reef 2050 Plan:** Experts debate whether the plan's measures are sufficient, particularly given its lack of direct action on greenhouse gas emissions, the primary driver of climate change.
- **Resilience and Recovery:** While some areas show recovery (e.g., fast-growing *Acropora* coral in 2022), scientists question whether the reef can keep pace with increasing ocean temperatures and the frequency of bleaching events.
- **Future of Coral Composition:** The mass die-offs of mature breeding corals have led to drastically lower birth rates and a shift in the types of corals reproducing, potentially causing a long-term reorganization of the reef ecosystem.
- **Impact of Deep-Water Bleaching:** Recent research indicates deep-water (benthic) coral communities are also vulnerable to thermal stress, but these events are less documented than shallow-water bleaching.
