# Vandals

## Overview
The Vandals were a Germanic people who migrated from Central Europe to North Africa, where they established a powerful kingdom that lasted for over a century. Their conquest of the wealthy Roman province of Africa and their famous sack of Rome in 455 AD made them a major force in the late Western Roman Empire. Their kingdom's eventual destruction by the Byzantine Empire marked a key event in the transition from Late Antiquity to the Early Middle Ages.

## Body

### Origins and Early History
The Vandals were first recorded by Roman writers in the 1st and 2nd centuries AD as a broad grouping of Germanic tribes, including the Burgundians, Varini, and Gutones, living between the Elbe and Vistula rivers. Archaeologists associate them with the Przeworsk culture. By the 2nd century, distinct Vandal groups like the Hasdingi and Silingi appear in records. During the Marcomannic Wars (166–180 AD), the Hasdingi entered Dacia as Roman allies. In the 3rd and 4th centuries, they faced pressure from both Romans and the expanding Goths. By the late 4th century, the region was overwhelmed by Goths, Alans, and Huns, prompting a major Vandal migration.

### Migration to Iberia
In the early 5th century, Hasdingi and Silingi Vandals, along with other peoples, began a major armed migration. They crossed the Rhine into Gaul in 406 and moved into the Iberian Peninsula by 409. There, they received land from the Romans as *foederati* (allied barbarian groups). The Visigoths, invading Iberia on Roman orders, crushed the Silingi Vandals and the Alans in 417–418. The remnants of these groups united under the Hasdingi king Gunderic. After further conflicts with Romans and Suebi, Gunderic's half-brother Genseric became king in 428.

### The Vandal Kingdom in North Africa
In 429, Genseric led the Vandals into North Africa. After defeating Roman forces, they besieged Hippo Regius, where St. Augustine died in 430. A treaty in 435 gave them territory, but Genseric broke it in 439 by seizing Carthage without a fight. He made it his capital and ruled a kingdom that included North Africa, Sicily, Sardinia, Corsica, and the Balearic Islands. The Vandals, who were Arian Christians, often persecuted their Trinitarian Roman subjects. In 455, Genseric sacked Rome for 14 days. The kingdom successfully defended itself against major Roman expeditions in 460 and 468.

### Decline and Fall
After Genseric's death in 477, the kingdom weakened due to internal religious strife and external pressure from Berbers and Ostrogoths. In 530, the tolerant King Hilderic was overthrown by his cousin Gelimer. In 533, Byzantine Emperor Justinian I sent General Belisarius to restore Hilderic. Belisarius defeated the Vandals at the battles of Ad Decimum and Tricamarum. Gelimer surrendered in 534, ending the Vandal kingdom. North Africa was reabsorbed into the Roman Empire, and the Vandals as a distinct group disappeared, with many absorbed into the Byzantine army or integrating with local populations.

## Terms
- **Foederati**: Barbarian groups granted land within the Roman Empire in exchange for military service.
- **Arianism**: A Christian belief, considered heretical by the Roman Church, that Christ was not co-equal with God the Father. The Vandals were Arians.
- **Trinitarian**: Christians who adhere to the orthodox doctrine of the Trinity (Father, Son, Holy Spirit as one God), which was the official faith of the Roman Empire.
- **Przeworsk culture**: An archaeological culture in Central Europe (c. 2nd century BC to 5th century AD) associated with the early Vandals.
- **Marcomannic Wars**: A series of conflicts (166–180 AD) between the Roman Empire and Germanic tribes north of the Danube.
- **Hasdingi**: One of the two main Vandal tribal groups that led the migration to North Africa.
- **Silingi**: The other main Vandal tribal group, associated with the region of Silesia, which was largely destroyed in Iberia.
- **Vandalic War**: The 533–534 conflict in which the Byzantine Empire conquered the Vandal Kingdom.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact etymology of the name "Vandal" is unclear, with theories linking it to words for "twisted" or "water."
- The relationship between the Vandals and the Lugii, another Germanic group in the same region, is debated; they may have been the same people or closely related.
- The precise location of the Silingi Vandals before their migration is uncertain, though they are linked to Silesia.
- The scale of destruction during the Vandal sack of Rome in 455 is questioned by modern historians, who note the Vandals may have been more systematic than purely wanton.
- The degree to which Vandal rule disrupted or continued Roman economic and cultural life in North Africa is debated, with archaeological evidence suggesting some continuity.
