# Nok culture

## Overview
The Nok culture was an early Iron Age civilization in central Nigeria, renowned for its distinctive terracotta sculptures. Flourishing from approximately 1500 BCE to 1 BCE, it represents one of the earliest known societies in West Africa to produce large-scale figurative art and to develop iron smelting. Its artistic traditions may have influenced later West African cultures, and its archaeological study has been significantly impacted by widespread looting.

## Timeline
- **c. 1500 BCE** — Possible emergence of Nok culture in central Nigeria.
- **c. 900 BCE** — Earliest known Nok terracotta sculptures developed.
- **c. 750–550 BCE** — Iron metallurgy may have independently developed in the Nok culture.
- **1928** — First Nok terracotta discovered by Colonel Dent Young near the village of Nok, Kaduna State.
- **1943** — New series of clay figurines discovered near Nok; Bernard Fagg identifies their connection to the 1928 find.
- **1961** — Preliminary excavations at Taruga site reveal iron slag, pottery, and figurine fragments, establishing Nok as an Iron Age culture.
- **1970s–present** — Large-scale looting of Nok terracotta sites begins and intensifies.
- **1979** — Nigeria establishes the National Commission for Museums and Monuments (NCMM) to manage cultural heritage.
- **2005–2021** — Joint German-Nigerian Frankfurt Nok Project conducts systematic archaeological fieldwork.

## Body

### Origin and Migration
The origin of the Nok people is debated. Scholars suggest they may have migrated from the Sahel region of West Africa or the Central Sahara, possibly bringing knowledge of pearl millet cultivation and pottery. This migration may have occurred before 1500 BCE, with the Nok and related Gajiganna people diverging before settling in their respective regions.

### Archaeology and Discovery
Nok terracottas were first found accidentally during tin mining. Systematic archaeology began with the 1961 Taruga excavation, which uncovered iron slag, pottery, and figurines, confirming the culture's Iron Age context. Later projects, like the Frankfurt Nok Project (2005-2021), aimed to study sites systematically, though many had already been looted. The sculptures are typically found as fragments due to erosion and deposition.

### Terracotta Sculptures
Nok terracottas are hollow, coil-built, nearly life-sized human heads and bodies with stylized features and detailed hairstyles. Their original function is unknown but may include ancestor portrayal, grave markers, or ritual objects. Evidence from burial pits suggests they were part of a complex funerary culture. The sculptures are the earliest known large-scale figurative art in sub-Saharan Africa (excluding ancient Egypt) and may have influenced later West African artistic traditions like those of Ife and Benin.

### Settlements and Society
Nok settlement sites are found on hilltops and plains, often with evidence of domestic pottery and iron working. The houses were built from organic materials that have decomposed, leaving no architectural traces. Archaeological evidence indicates small-scale, locally autonomous communities with no signs of social stratification, urbanism, or monumental architecture. Complexity appears to have been primarily ritualistic.

### Economy and Subsistence
The Nok people practiced mixed farming, cultivating pearl millet and cowpeas, and likely used an agroforestry system. They also gathered wild fruits and honey. Hunting is suggested by terracotta depictions of figures with weapons. Pottery was used for food preparation and storage, and grinding stones were common. No animal bones have survived due to acidic soil.

### Iron Metallurgy and Stone Tools
Iron smelting may have been independently invented by the Nok people between 750 and 550 BCE. Their stone tool assemblage is characterized by grinding tools and ground stone axes, but a notable absence of cutting tools and projectile points.

### Looting and Repatriation
Since the 1970s, Nok sites have been heavily looted for the international art market, with over 90% of known sites in research areas illegally excavated. This has destroyed invaluable archaeological context. Nigerian authorities have implemented laws and awareness campaigns to combat looting, but resources for enforcement are limited, and artifacts continue to be smuggled abroad.

## Terms
- **Terracotta**: A type of fired clay used for sculpture and pottery.
- **Iron Age**: A period in human history characterized by the widespread use of iron for tools and weapons.
- **Alluvial**: Soil, sand, or gravel deposited by flowing water.
- **Metallurgy**: The science or technology of extracting metals from their ores and fabricating them.
- **Agroforestry**: A land use management system that integrates trees and shrubs with crops and/or livestock.
- **Pearl millet**: One of Africa's oldest grain crops, known for its drought resistance.
- **NCMM**: National Commission for Museums and Monuments, the Nigerian agency responsible for cultural heritage.
- **Stratigraphy**: The study of rock layers (strata) and layering, used in archaeology to date sites.
- **Therianthropic**: Depicting a being with both human and animal characteristics.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origin**: The precise homeland and migration routes of the Nok people remain unknown, with theories pointing to the Sahel or Central Sahara.
- **Function of Sculptures**: The primary purpose of the terracotta figures—whether ritual, funerary, or otherwise—is still debated.
- **Social Complexity**: While the art is sophisticated, the archaeological record shows no evidence of social hierarchy or urban development, leading to questions about the nature of their society.
- **Descendants**: The link between the Nok culture and later peoples like the Yoruba or Jukun is suggested by stylistic similarities but is not definitively proven.
