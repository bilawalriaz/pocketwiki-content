# Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth

## Overview
The Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth (1569–1795) was a large, multi-ethnic federation between the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania. It was a major European power known for its unique political system of elective monarchy and noble democracy, as well as its exceptional religious tolerance. Its eventual decline and partition by neighboring powers ended independent Polish statehood for over a century.

## Timeline
- **1385** — Union of Krewo establishes a personal union between Poland and Lithuania.
- **1 July 1569** — Union of Lublin creates the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth as a single federative state.
- **1572** — Death of Sigismund II Augustus ends the Jagiellonian dynasty; the monarchy becomes elective.
- **1573** — Warsaw Confederation Act guarantees religious freedom for all citizens.
- **1596** — Union of Brest creates the Greek Catholic Church in the Commonwealth, heightening religious tensions.
- **1648** — Khmelnytsky Uprising begins a major Cossack rebellion against Polish rule.
- **1655–1660** — The "Deluge," a devastating Swedish invasion, severely weakens the Commonwealth.
- **1683** — King John III Sobieski leads a relief force that defeats the Ottomans at the Battle of Vienna.
- **1772** — First Partition of Poland: Russia, Prussia, and Austria annex portions of Commonwealth territory.
- **1791** — Constitution of 3 May is enacted, the first modern codified constitution in Europe.
- **1795** — Third Partition: The Commonwealth is completely erased from the map of Europe.

## Body

### Formation and Political System
The Commonwealth was formally established by the Union of Lublin in 1569, merging the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania into a single state with a common monarch, parliament (Sejm), and foreign policy. Its political system, known as "Golden Liberty," was an early form of constitutional monarchy. The king was elected by the nobility (szlachta) and his power was limited by the Henrician Articles and the pacta conventa (agreements made at his election). The Sejm held legislative power, and any deputy could use the *liberum veto* to block legislation. This system, while democratic for its time, eventually led to political paralysis and magnate oligarchy.

### Golden Age and Decline
The late 16th and early 17th centuries were a period of expansion and cultural flourishing, marked by military victories like the annexation of Livonia and the temporary occupation of Moscow. However, the mid-17th century brought catastrophic wars, including the Khmelnytsky Uprising and the Swedish Deluge, which caused massive population loss and territorial gains by neighbors like Russia and Prussia. The weakened state became increasingly subject to foreign interference, especially from Russia, which manipulated royal elections and politics.

### Economy and Society
The economy was predominantly agrarian, based on the export of grain, cattle, and timber via the Baltic port of Gdańsk. This system relied on a feudal plantation economy worked by serfs. The Commonwealth was highly multicultural, with Poles, Lithuanians, Ruthenians (ancestors of Belarusians and Ukrainians), Germans, and a large Jewish population. While the Warsaw Confederation of 1573 guaranteed religious tolerance, tensions grew between Catholics, Protestants, and Orthodox Christians, particularly in the eastern territories.

### Late Reforms and Partitions
In its final decades, reformers attempted to modernize the state. The Constitution of 3 May 1791 abolished the liberum veto, established popular sovereignty, and strengthened central government. Fearing a resurgent Poland, neighboring powers—Russia, Prussia, and Austria—intervened. The constitution was overturned, and the Commonwealth was partitioned in three stages (1772, 1793, 1795), completely disappearing from the map.

## Terms
- **Szlachta**: The noble class of the Commonwealth, which held political rights and privileges, comprising about 10% of the population.
- **Golden Liberty (Złota Wolność)**: The political doctrine of the Commonwealth, emphasizing the rights of the nobility and the elective monarchy.
- **Sejm**: The bicameral parliament of the Commonwealth, with legislative power.
- **Liberum veto**: A parliamentary procedure allowing any single deputy to halt the proceedings of the Sejm and nullify all legislation passed in that session.
- **Pacta conventa**: Latin for "agreed-upon agreements," a contract between the elected king and the nobility, outlining the king's obligations.
- **Henrician Articles**: A constitutional statute from 1573 that bound the king and guaranteed certain rights to the nobility.
- **Sarmatism**: The dominant ideology of the Polish nobility, which emphasized their alleged descent from ancient Sarmatians and promoted specific cultural and social values.
- **Voivodeship (Województwo)**: The main administrative division of the Commonwealth, governed by a voivode.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The degree to which the Commonwealth's political system was a true democracy versus an oligarchy of the nobility is debated, given that only the szlachta had political rights.
- Historians debate the primary causes of the Commonwealth's decline, weighing factors like the liberum veto's political paralysis, magnate selfishness, foreign interference, and social tensions.
- The legacy of the Commonwealth is viewed differently in modern Poland and Lithuania; for Poland, it is often a source of national pride, while in Lithuania, its historical perception has been more ambivalent, though this is changing.
