# Lake Mweru

## Overview
Lake Mweru is a large freshwater lake on the Congo River system, forming part of the border between Zambia and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC). It is a significant geographical feature in the region, historically important as a trade route and currently vital for its fishery and local economies. Its development has been shaped by colonial boundaries, regional conflicts, and its natural characteristics as a rift valley lake.

- **1890–92** — British and Belgian expeditions pass by, leading to colonial outposts and the end of the slave trade.

- **1987** — Main road on the Zambian side is tarred to Nchelenge, spurring population growth.

- **2010** — Dikulushi Copper Mine is sold to Australian company Mawson West.

## Body

### Physical Geography
Lake Mweru is a rift valley lake in the Lake Mweru-Luapula graben, a branch of the East African Rift. It is mainly fed by the Luapula River from the south and the Kalungwishi River from the east, and drained by the Luvua River at its north end, which flows to the Congo. The lake is 118 km long and 45 km wide on average, with a stable water level that fluctuates only 1.7 metres annually due to regulation by the upstream Bangweulu Swamps and the swift outflow of the Luvua. It is shallow in the south and deeper in the north, with maximum depths of 20 and 27 metres. A smaller, marshy lake, Mweru Wantipa, lies to its east.

### Exploration and Colonial History
The lake was known to regional traders before European contact. David Livingstone is credited with its discovery by Europeans in 1867–68. His accounts helped rally opposition to the slave trade, which persisted in the area until the 1890s. Following the Stairs Expedition's seizure of Katanga for Belgium in 1892, the lake's western shore became part of the Belgian Congo and the eastern shore part of Northern Rhodesia (now Zambia). This division allocated 58% of the lake's waters to Zambia and 42% to the DRC. Colonial administration and the suppression of the slave trade were established through outposts like the British boma at Chiengi and Belgian posts at Lukonzolwa and Pweto.

### Human Geography and Economy
The lake's shores are dotted with fishing villages and main towns like Nchelenge (Zambia) and Kilwa (DRC). Its economy is centered on a significant fishery, historically supplying copper mines with species like longfin tilapia ("pale"). Commercial fishing was pioneered by Greek settlers in the early 20th century. In recent decades, the catch has declined due to overfishing, and methods like "chisense" (light-fishing for small pelagic fish) have increased. Transport relies on water routes and roads, with the Congolese side's infrastructure suffering from neglect and conflict. The Dikulushi Copper Mine north of Kilwa uses a pontoon ferry across the lake to transport concentrate. Tourism is undeveloped due to past conflicts and reduced wildlife populations.

## Terms
- **Rift valley lake**: A lake formed within a geological graben, a depressed block of the Earth's crust bordered by parallel faults, as part of a continental rift system.
- **Endorheic**: A drainage basin that retains water and allows no outflow to other external bodies of water, such as rivers or oceans.
- **Grab**: A valley with a distinct escarpment on each side caused by the displacement of a block of land downward.
- **Boma**: A fortified enclosure or administrative outpost, a term used in colonial Africa.
- **Gill net**: A fishing net designed to catch fish by their gills as they try to pass through the mesh.
- **Chisense**: A fishing method using lights at night to attract small pelagic fish, which are then scooped up with fine nets.
- **Longfin tilapia (Oreochromis macrochir)**: A species of cichlid fish native to the region, locally called "pale," which is a key commercial catch.
- **Kapenta**: Small pelagic (open-water) fish, similar to sardines, caught using light-fishing techniques.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes a historical border dispute where about 33 square kilometres of Zambian territory near Pweto was ceded to the DRC (then Zaire) following the relocation of a British boma. The long-term ecological and economic impacts of overfishing and the shift to "chisense" fishing methods on Lake Mweru's ecosystem and communities remain open questions. The future development potential of the lake's tourism and fisheries is contingent on regional stability and conservation efforts.
