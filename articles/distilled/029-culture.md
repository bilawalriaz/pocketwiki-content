# Culture

## Overview

Culture is the encompassing concept of social behavior, institutions, and norms found in human societies, including knowledge, beliefs, arts, laws, customs, and habits. Humans acquire culture through learning processes like enculturation and socialization, which explains the diversity of cultures across societies. Culture is a central concept in anthropology, transmitted through social learning, and includes both material expressions (technology, architecture, art) and immaterial aspects (social organization, mythology, philosophy, science). The term has evolved from ancient Roman agricultural metaphors to a modern understanding shaped by German Romanticism, anthropology, sociology, and cultural studies, with ongoing debates about its definition, evaluation, and protection.

## Timeline

- **c. 50,000 years ago** — Evolution of behavioral modernity; ability to categorize and represent experiences with symbols
- **45 BC** — Cicero writes *Tusculanae Disputationes*, using "cultura animi" (cultivation of the soul)
- **1795** — Wilhelm von Humboldt calls for an anthropology synthesizing Kant's and Herder's interests
- **1860** — Adolf Bastian argues for "the psychic unity of mankind" and elementary ideas
- **1870** — Edward Tylor proposes theory of religion evolution, redefining culture as diverse activities of all human societies
- **1918–1933** — Cultural sociology emerges in Weimar Germany with Alfred Weber's *Kultursoziologie*
- **1964** — Richard Hoggart founds the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies (CCCS), coining "cultural studies"
- **1970s onward** — Stuart Hall and colleagues create an international intellectual movement in cultural studies
- **1990s** — Psychological research on culture influence begins to grow, challenging assumed universality
- **21st century** — Protection of culture becomes focus of increasing national and international activity

## Body

### Etymology and Early Meanings

The modern term "culture" derives from Cicero's *Tusculanae Disputationes*, where he wrote of *cultura animi* ("cultivation of the soul"), using an agricultural metaphor for developing a philosophical soul. Samuel von Pufendorf later took over this metaphor in a modern context, no longer assuming philosophy was humanity's natural perfection. Edward S. Casey noted the word meant "place tilled" in Middle English, tracing back to Latin *colere* ("to inhabit, care for, till, worship"). Richard Velkley observed that culture "originally meant the cultivation of the soul or mind," acquiring its later modern meaning in 18th-century German thinkers developing Rousseau's criticism of modern liberalism and Enlightenment. Anthropologist E. B. Tylor defined culture as "that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society." Terror management theory posits culture provides humans with a basis for perceiving themselves as persons of worth, denying animal insignificance and death.

### German and English Romanticism

Immanuel Kant formulated an individualist definition of enlightenment as "man's emergence from his self-incurred immaturity," urging "Sapere Aude" ("Dare to be wise!"). In reaction, Johann Gottfried Herder argued human creativity is as important as rationality and proposed a collective form of *Bildung*—the totality of experiences providing coherent identity and common destiny to a people. During the Romantic era, German scholars developed a more inclusive notion of culture as "worldview" (*Weltanschauung*), holding each ethnic group has a distinct worldview incommensurable with others. Adolf Bastian argued for "the psychic unity of mankind," proposing all societies share "elementary ideas" (*Elementargedanken*), with different cultures as local modifications. Franz Boas, trained in this tradition, brought it to the United States.

In 19th-century England, Matthew Arnold used "culture" to refer to "the best that has been thought and said in the world," comparable to the German *bildung*. In practice, culture referred to an elite ideal associated with art, classical music, and haute cuisine, identified with "civilization." The Romantic movement's interest in folklore led to identifying culture among non-elites, creating the distinction between high culture (ruling class) and low culture. Following Hobbes and Rousseau, Europeans contrasted "culture" with "the state of nature," classifying some nations as more civilized than others—leading to Herbert Spencer's Social Darwinism and Lewis Henry Morgan's theory of cultural evolution. Other critics saw high culture as corrupting, viewing folk music as honestly expressing natural life and indigenous peoples as "noble savages." In 1870, Tylor applied these ideas to propose a theory of religion evolving from polytheistic to monotheistic forms.

### Anthropology and Sociology

In the 20th century, "culture" emerged as the central concept of American anthropology, referring to the universal human capacity to classify and encode experiences symbolically. American anthropology is organized into four fields: biological, linguistic, and cultural anthropology, and archaeology. Franz Boas coined *Kulturbrille* ("culture glasses"), referring to the lenses through which a person sees their own culture, which can blind us to things outsiders notice.

For sociologist Georg Simmel, culture referred to "the cultivation of individuals through the agency of external forms which have been objectified in the course of history." Culture can be non-material (values, beliefs, norms, language, institutions) or material (physical evidence in objects and architecture). Cultural sociology first emerged in Weimar Germany, then was reinvented in the English-speaking world through the cultural turn of the 1960s, incorporating structuralist and postmodern approaches. Cultural sociologists tend to reject scientific methods, focusing hermeneutically on words, artifacts, and symbols, though quantitative sociologists have recently entered the field.

### Cultural Studies

In the UK, scholars influenced by Marxism, including Stuart Hall and Raymond Williams, developed cultural studies, identifying culture with consumption goods and leisure activities, seeing patterns of consumption as determined by relations of production. Richard Hoggart coined the term in 1964 when founding the CCCS. The field combines political economy, communication, sociology, literary theory, media theory, and other disciplines to study cultural phenomena. Cultural studies concerns meanings and practices of everyday life, where a "text" includes films, photographs, fashion, and hairstyles—all meaningful artifacts of culture. The British version took overtly political, left-wing views, criticizing popular culture as "capitalist" mass culture, absorbing ideas from the Frankfurt School. The American version, grounded in a pragmatic, liberal-pluralist tradition, concerned itself more with audience reactions and the liberatory aspects of fandom. Marxist approaches assume mass production of culture with power residing among producers, while non-Marxist approaches suggest different ways of consuming artifacts affect meaning.

### Psychology and Protection

Starting in the 1990s, psychological research on culture challenged the universality assumed in general psychology. People from collectivistic cultures, such as the Japanese, suppress positive emotions more than Americans. Culture affects how people experience emotions, personality, and reactions to culture shock. Cognitive tools may function differently across cultures—for example, people raised with an abacus develop distinctive reasoning styles. Westerners are more motivated by successes, while East Asians are better motivated by avoiding failure. The notion of the anxious, rebellious adolescent has been criticized; Robert Epstein noted "American-style teen turmoil is absent in more than 100 cultures around the world, suggesting that such mayhem is not biologically inevitable."

UNESCO and Blue Shield International coordinate international protection of cultural heritage. The Hague Convention for the Protection of Cultural Property in the Event of Armed Conflict and the UNESCO Convention on the Protection and Promotion of the Diversity of Cultural Expressions deal with culture protection. Article 27 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights gives people the right to participate in cultural life and protection of their contributions. According to Karl von Habsburg, destruction of cultural assets is part of psychological warfare, targeting the identity of opponents and cultural memory. Tourism increasingly impacts culture through physical damage and socio-cultural effects.

## Terms

- **Enculturation/socialization**: Learning processes through which humans acquire culture
- **Material culture**: Physical expressions of culture, such as technology, architecture, and art
- **Intangible cultural heritage**: Immaterial aspects including mythology, philosophy, literature, and science
- **Cultural relativism**: Stance holding cultures cannot be objectively ranked because evaluation is situated within a given culture's value system
- **Weltanschauung**: "Worldview"; the distinct perspective each ethnic group supposedly possesses
- **Bildung**: German concept of formation/education; totality of experiences providing coherent identity to a people
- **Kulturbrille**: "Culture glasses"; the lenses through which a person sees their own culture
- **Acculturation**: Replacement of traits of one culture with another, as happened to indigenous peoples during colonization
- **Diffusion**: Transfer of cultural ideas between societies; form moves but not necessarily meaning
- **High culture/low culture**: Distinction between elite culture and popular or folk culture of lower classes

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether culture can be objectively ranked or evaluated, with cultural relativism arguing evaluation is necessarily situated within a given culture's value system. Marxist and critical theory perspectives argue culture is used politically as a tool of elites to manipulate the proletariat and create false consciousness. The distinction between "civilized" and "uncivilized" peoples has been criticized as expressing conflicts between European colonial powers and their subjects. Researchers debate whether adolescent turmoil is biologically inevitable or a relatively recent phenomenon created by modern society, with brain research "crucial for a full picture, but it does not provide an ultimate explanation." The relationship between culture and the human mind—whether the mind is independent from culture—remains an open question in psychology.
