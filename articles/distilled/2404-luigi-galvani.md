# Luigi Galvani

## Overview
Luigi Galvani (1737–1798) was an Italian physician and scientist whose experiments with frog legs in the 1780s demonstrated the link between electricity and muscle movement, founding the field of bioelectricity. His work sparked a major scientific debate with Alessandro Volta and laid essential groundwork for modern electrophysiology and neuroscience.

## Timeline
- **1737** — Born in Bologna, Papal States.
- **1754** — Enrolled at the University of Bologna to study medicine.
- **1759** — Graduated with a degree in medicine and philosophy.
- **1762** — Published first work on bone anatomy; married Lucia Galeazzi.
- **1766** — Appointed curator of the anatomical museum in Bologna.
- **1780** — Discovered that frog leg muscles twitched when touched by an electrical spark.
- **1782** — Appointed Professor of Obstetric Arts at the University of Bologna.
- **1791** — Published his main work, *De Viribus Electricitatis in Motu Musculari Commentarius*.
- **1798** — Refused a loyalty oath to the Cisalpine Republic, was stripped of his posts, and died in poverty.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Born to a goldsmith in Bologna, Galvani studied medicine at the University of Bologna under notable mentors. After graduating in 1759, he practiced medicine and began lecturing on anatomy. His academic career progressed with appointments as curator of the anatomical museum (1766) and later as Professor of Obstetric Arts (1782), a position he held for 16 years.

### Discovery of Animal Electricity
Galvani became interested in "medical electricity," a field emerging from 18th-century research on electricity's effects on the body. In 1780, he observed that a frog's leg muscles twitched when struck by an electrical spark. His subsequent decade of research culminated in the 1791 publication of his major work, which detailed the effects of electricity on animal preparations and ignited intense scientific controversy.

### The Galvani-Volta Controversy
Alessandro Volta initially repeated and supported Galvani's experiments but later disagreed with the conclusion that an intrinsic "animal electric fluid" caused the muscle contractions. Volta argued the effect was due to the different metals used in the apparatus. This respectful but fundamental disagreement led Volta to coin the term "Galvanism" for electricity produced by chemical action, and it spurred the development of the electric battery. Galvani, reluctant to debate publicly, had his nephew Giovanni Aldini defend his theory.

### Personal Life and Death
Galvani was described as a modest, charitable, and deeply religious man who viewed his work as a spiritual mission. After his wife's death in 1790, he lived with his brother. In 1798, he refused to swear a loyalty oath to the new French-backed Cisalpine Republic, resulting in his dismissal and poverty. He died later that year, shortly after his reinstatement was announced.

## Terms
- **Animal electricity**: The theory proposed by Galvani that a vital electrical force exists within animal tissues, responsible for nerve and muscle function.
- **Bioelectricity**: The study of electrical potentials and currents in living organisms.
- **Galvanism**: A direct electric current produced by chemical action, a term coined by Alessandro Volta.
- **Electrochemical cell**: A device that generates electrical energy from chemical reactions, the principle behind a battery.
- **Cisalpine Republic**: A French client state in northern Italy (1797–1802) that required loyalty oaths from public officials.
- **Galvanize**: To coat a metal with a protective layer of zinc; also, to shock or excite someone into action, named after Galvani's experiments.

## Debates and Open Questions
The central debate was between Galvani and Volta over the source of electricity in muscle contraction. Galvani maintained it was an intrinsic "animal electricity," while Volta argued it was generated by the contact of dissimilar metals in the experimental apparatus. Modern science shows both were partially correct: biological electricity has chemical underpinnings similar to an electrochemical cell, which can be replicated outside the body.
