# Perth

## Overview
Perth (Nyungar: Boorloo) is the capital city of Western Australia and the fourth-most-populous city in Australia, with over 2.3 million residents. Founded in 1829 as the administrative centre of the Swan River Colony, it has grown from a remote British settlement into a major global city, serving as the economic hub for Western Australia's vast mining and resource industries. Its development is marked by waves of immigration, economic booms, and a unique identity shaped by its geographical isolation and Mediterranean climate.

- **1962** — Perth is nicknamed the "City of Light" when residents light up for astronaut John Glenn.

- **2021** — The South West Native Title Settlement, including the Whadjuk agreement, is finalised.

## Body

### History and Foundation
Perth is situated on the traditional lands of the Whadjuk Noongar people, who have inhabited the area for at least 48,000 years. The British established the Swan River Colony in 1829, with Captain James Stirling founding Perth as its administrative centre. The colony, initially a free settlement, began accepting convicts from 1850 to supply labour. Confrontations with the Indigenous population escalated due to conflicting land-use systems, leading to violent events such as the Pinjarra massacre in 1834. The city experienced significant growth during the late 19th-century gold rushes and again after World War II, driven by high net migration from the British Isles, Southern Europe, and more recently, Asia.

### Geography and Climate
Perth is located on the Swan Coastal Plain between the Indian Ocean and the Darling Scarp. The Swan River (Derbarl Yerrigan) flows through the city, which has a hot-summer Mediterranean climate (Köppen Csa). Summers are hot and dry, while winters are mild and wet. Perth is Australia's sunniest capital city, averaging 8.8 hours of sunshine daily. The city is geographically isolated; its nearest major city, Adelaide, is over 2,100 km away. The metropolitan area covers 6,418 square kilometres and is defined by 30 local government areas.

### Demographics and Culture
Perth's population is diverse, with high proportions of British- and Irish-born residents. Post-WWII immigration brought significant communities from Italy, Greece, and other European countries. Since the end of the White Australia policy in 1973, migration from Asia, particularly China and India, has increased. The city has a distinct arts scene, hosting the annual Perth Festival and Fringe World Festival. Its relative isolation has fostered a tight-knit music community, producing notable acts like Tame Impala and AC/DC's Bon Scott. Key cultural institutions are concentrated in the Perth Cultural Centre.

### Economy and Infrastructure
Perth's economy is dominated by service industries, supporting the state's mining, petroleum, and agricultural export sectors. Major industrial areas include Kwinana and Welshpool. The city is a growing hub for technology startups. Infrastructure includes Perth Airport, the port of Fremantle, and the Transperth public transport network. The city's water supply, historically reliant on rainfall and groundwater, now includes desalination plants due to reduced rainfall. Perth is served by several universities, including the University of Western Australia and Curtin University.

## Terms
- **Swan River Colony**: The original British settlement established in 1829, later renamed Western Australia.
- **Whadjuk Noongar**: The Aboriginal clan group whose traditional territory includes the Perth metropolitan area.
- **Darling Scarp**: A low escarpment forming the eastern boundary of the Perth metropolitan area.
- **Fremantle Doctor**: A cooling sea breeze that blows from the south-west on summer afternoons.
- **Transperth**: The public transport system serving the Perth metropolitan area, including trains, buses, and ferries.
- **Kwinana Industrial Area**: A major heavy industrial zone south of Perth, established post-WWII.
- **South West Native Title Settlement**: A legal agreement finalised in 2021 recognising Noongar traditional ownership of south-west Western Australia.

## Debates and Open Questions
The history of European settlement involves ongoing scholarly debate regarding the nature and scale of conflict with the Indigenous Whadjuk Noongar people, including events like the Pinjarra massacre. The city's extreme geographical isolation presents continuous challenges and opportunities for economic development and cultural connectivity. Furthermore, the long-term impact of reduced winter rainfall on Perth's water security and urban planning remains a critical issue for the city's future.
