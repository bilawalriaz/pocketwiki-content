# Indo-Scythian Kingdom

## Overview
The Indo-Scythians (or Indo-Sakas) were Iranian-speaking nomadic Scythian tribes from Central Asia who migrated south into Afghanistan, eastern Iran, and northwestern India from the 2nd century BCE to the 4th century CE. Their invasions, triggered by conflicts with the Xiongnu, displaced other peoples and led to the establishment of kingdoms that conquered the Indo-Greeks and later became vassals or satraps under the Kushan and Gupta empires. They played a significant role in the history of the Indian subcontinent, contributing to cultural and religious developments, including the spread of Buddhism.

## Timeline
- **c. 175 BCE** — Yuezhi are defeated by Xiongnu, triggering a domino effect of migrations that displaces Saka tribes southward.
- **c. 85–60 BCE** — Maues, the first Indo-Scythian king, establishes Saka power in Gandhara and the Indus Valley.
- **c. 80 BCE** — Maues conquers Gandhara and Taxila from the Indo-Greeks.
- **58 BCE** — Indian king Vikrama reclaims Ujjain, establishing the Vikrama era.
- **c. 55 BCE** — Azes I defeats the Indo-Greek king Hippostratos, securing Indo-Scythian control of northwestern India.
- **c. 60 BCE** — Indo-Scythians conquer the Mathura region in northern India.
- **c. 10 CE** — Rajuvula, an Indo-Scythian satrap, eliminates the last Indo-Greek king, Strato II.
- **c. 75 CE** — Kushans begin expanding into India, eventually subjugating Indo-Scythian territories.
- **2nd century CE** — Indo-Scythian power declines after defeat by the Satavahana emperor Gautamiputra Satakarni.
- **395 CE** — Gupta emperor Chandragupta II defeats the last Western Satrap, Rudrasimha III, ending Indo-Scythian rule.

## Body

### Origins and Migration
The Indo-Scythians were part of the broader Saka (Scythian) cultural continuum of Iranian-speaking horse nomads from the Central Eurasian steppe. Their southward migration was triggered in the 2nd century BCE when the Yuezhi tribe, defeated by the Xiongnu, fled west, displacing Saka tribes in a domino effect. This pushed groups like the Sacaraucae and Massagetae into conflict with the Parthian Empire. Some Saka groups settled in the region of Drangiana (later called Sakastan or Sistan) in southwestern Afghanistan and southeastern Iran.

### Kingdoms and Satraps
The first Indo-Scythian kingdom was established by Maues in Gandhara. After a period of fragmentation, Azes I consolidated control over northwestern India. The Indo-Scythians ruled through a system of satraps (provincial governors). Two main groups emerged: the Northern Satraps in the Mathura region and the Western Satraps in Gujarat and Malwa. After initial conquests, many Indo-Scythian rulers became vassals of the expanding Kushan Empire. The Western Satraps continued to rule in western India until their final defeat by the Gupta Empire in 395 CE.

### Coinage and Art
Indo-Scythian coinage is generally of high quality, blending Indo-Greek and local traditions. Coins typically use Greek script on the obverse and Kharoshthi on the reverse, depicting the king on horseback rather than in portrait. They often feature Greek gods and Buddhist symbols like the triratana. In art, Indo-Scythians are depicted in Gandharan sculptures and reliefs (e.g., the Buner reliefs) wearing characteristic soft tunics, trousers, and pointed hoods. Stone palettes from this period show a mix of Greek, Parthian, and Scythian influences.

### Buddhism and Legacy
The Indo-Scythians were patrons of Buddhism, continuing the tradition of the Indo-Greeks. Several kings made royal dedications to Buddhist monuments, such as the Bimaran casket and the Mathura lion capital. Their support helped disseminate Buddhism beyond India. In Indian literature, they are referred to as "Shaka" and are described as part of the "mleccha" (barbarian) hordes from the northwest. Their presence left a linguistic and cultural legacy in northern India.

## Terms
- **Saka/Scythian**: Iranian-speaking nomadic peoples from the Central Asian steppe; "Saka" is the Persian term, equivalent to the Greek "Scythian."
- **Satrap**: A provincial governor or viceroy in the Persian and Hellenistic empires, adopted by the Indo-Scythians.
- **Kharoshthi**: An ancient script used in Gandhara and the northwest Indian subcontinent, often on the reverse of Indo-Scythian coins.
- **Triratana**: A Buddhist symbol representing the Three Jewels (Buddha, Dharma, Sangha), found on Indo-Scythian coins.
- **Mleccha**: A Sanskrit term for foreigners or "barbarians," used in Indian texts to describe invaders like the Shakas.
- **Gandhara**: An ancient region in present-day Pakistan and eastern Afghanistan, a major center of Indo-Scythian and later Kushan power.
- **Kushan Empire**: A syncretic empire that succeeded the Indo-Scythians in northwestern India, founded by the Yuezhi tribe.
- **Vikrama era**: A historical calendar system traditionally believed to have been established by King Vikrama in 58 BCE after a victory over the Shakas.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The theory that the Shakya clan of Gautama Buddha was of Scythian origin is debated; scholars like Michael Witzel support it, while others like Johannes Bronkhorst criticize it for lacking evidence.
- The exact route taken by Maues to capture Taxila is inferred from petroglyphs, but the precise military campaigns remain a subject of archaeological study.
- The identification of ancient places like "Chipin" (Kipin) in accounts of the Sai-Wang hordes is disputed, with scholars proposing locations such as Kashmir or Kafiristan.
