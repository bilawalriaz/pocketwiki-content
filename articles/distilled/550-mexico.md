# Mexico

## Overview
Mexico is a federal republic in North America, the world's 13th largest country by area and 10th by population (over 134 million as of 2026). It is the most populous Spanish-speaking nation and a cradle of Mesoamerican civilization, hosting advanced societies like the Olmecs, Maya, and Aztecs before Spanish conquest in 1521 established the colony of New Spain. Three centuries of colonial rule—fueled by silver mining and a stratified, multiracial society—ended with the 1810–1821 War of Independence. The 19th century was defined by territorial loss to the U.S. (1848), liberal-conservative civil wars, French intervention, and the 35-year Porfiriato dictatorship (1876–1911). The 1910–1920 Mexican Revolution produced the 1917 Constitution, which remains the governing document, and led to seven decades of one-party rule by the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI). Since the 2000 democratic transition, Mexico has emerged as a newly industrialized economy (13th largest by GDP) and a megadiverse cultural power, though it contends with persistent poverty, corruption, and cartel violence.

## Body

### Indigenous Civilizations and Spanish Conquest
Human presence dates to at least 11,500 BC. Mesoamerica emerged as one of six independent cradles of civilization, developing maize domestication, a vigesimal (base-20) numeric system, writing, and monumental architecture. The Olmecs (c. 1500 BC) diffused cultural traits across the region. Subsequent powers included the Maya and Zapotecs (writing systems), Teotihuacan (classic-period empire, pop. >150,000), the Toltecs, and finally the Aztecs (Mexica), who built a tribute empire centered on Tenochtitlan. Spanish conquest began in 1519; Cortés exploited indigenous alliances and smallpox to capture Tenochtitlan in 1521. The colony of New Spain was built on two pillars: the *encomienda* system leveraging existing indigenous labor hierarchies, and vast silver deposits in the north. Society was stratified by a complex *casta* system favoring European-born *peninsulares* over American-born *criollos*, mestizos, indigenous peoples, and enslaved Africans. The crown restricted trade to Veracruz and Acapulco (Manila Galleon link to Asia), creating a transoceanic economic hub.

### Independence, Instability, and Territorial Loss
Napoleon’s 1808 invasion of Spain triggered the 1810 *Grito de Dolores* by Miguel Hidalgo. Independence was achieved in 1821 under Agustín de Iturbide’s Plan of Iguala. The First Empire (1821–23) collapsed into the First Republic (1824). The 1824–1855 period saw chronic instability: military coups, the "Age of Santa Anna" (dominating politics 1829–1855), Spanish reconquest attempts, the Pastry War with France (1838–39), and the Texas Revolution. To populate Texas, Mexico invited Anglo-American settlers, who eventually seceded. The 1846–1848 Mexican–American War resulted in the loss of roughly half the national territory (present-day U.S. Southwest) via the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo.

### Liberal Reform, French Intervention, and the Porfiriato
Liberals overthrew Santa Anna in 1855 (*La Reforma*). The 1857 Constitution separated church and state, stripped corporate privileges (*fueros*), and mandated sale of church and communal indigenous lands. Conservatives revolted (Reform War, 1858–61), then invited French intervention. Napoleon III installed Maximilian I as Emperor (1864–67). Liberal President Benito Juárez led a government-in-exile; with U.S. pressure post-Civil War, France withdrew, and Juárez executed Maximilian. The "Restored Republic" (1867–76) saw Juárez and Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada presidencies. Porfirio Díaz seized power in 1876, ruling 35 years (*Porfiriato*). His *científicos* advisors applied positivist "order and progress": railroads, telegraphs, foreign investment, and industrial growth. However, land concentration, labor repression (e.g., Yaqui deportation), and electoral fraud fueled the 1910 Revolution.

### Mexican Revolution and Constitutionalism
Fraud in the 1910 election sparked uprisings led by Francisco I. Madero. Díaz resigned 1911; Madero elected but overthrown and murdered in the 1913 "Ten Tragic Days" coup by Victoriano Huerta (with U.S. Ambassador support). A Constitutionalist coalition (Venustiano Carranza, Álvaro Obregón, Pancho Villa) and southern Zapatistas defeated Huerta (1914). The victors splintered: Obregón defeated Villa (Battle of Celaya, 1915); Carranza became president. The 1917 Constitution (still in force) empowered land expropriation (Article 27), codified labor rights (Article 123), and strengthened anticlericalism. Carranza was overthrown by Sonoran generals (Plan of Agua Prieta, 1920); Obregón elected president.

### One-Party Rule (1920–2000)
Post-revolutionary presidents were revolutionary generals: Obregón, Plutarco Elías Calles, Lázaro Cárdenas, Manuel Ávila Camacho. Calles founded the National Revolutionary Party (PNR, later PRI) in 1929 to manage succession. The *Maximato* (1929–34) saw Calles rule behind puppet presidents until Cárdenas expelled him. Cárdenas (1934–40) implemented radical land reform and the 1938 oil expropriation (creating Pemex). The "Mexican Miracle" (1940s–70s) brought industrialization, urbanization, and growth under import-substitution, but rising inequality and authoritarianism. The 1968 Tlatelolco Massacre (300–800 dead) exposed state repression. The 1980s brought debt crisis, the 1985 earthquake, and the first major electoral challenge (Cuauhtémoc Cárdenas vs. Carlos Salinas, 1988, marred by fraud). Salinas (1988–94) implemented neoliberal reforms, privatized state firms, and signed NAFTA (effective 1 Jan 1994). The same day, the Zapatista Army of National Liberation (EZLN) rose in Chiapas. Ernesto Zedillo (1994–2000) managed the peso crisis (IMF bailout) and electoral reforms enabling the 2000 transition.

### Contemporary Mexico (2000–Present)
Vicente Fox (PAN, 2000–06) ended PRI hegemony. Felipe Calderón (PAN, 2006–12) launched a militarized "War on Drugs," escalating violence (~127,000+ deaths since 2006). Enrique Peña Nieto (PRI, 2012–18) passed structural reforms but faced corruption scandals. Andrés Manuel López Obrador (MORENA, 2018–24) won a landslide on an anti-corruption, pro-poor platform ("Fourth Transformation"), expanding social programs and increasing the minimum wage rapidly. Claudia Sheinbaum (MORENA) won the 2024 election in a landslide, becoming the first female president (inaugurated 1 Oct 2024). Persistent challenges include cartel fragmentation and violence, forced disappearances (100,000+ missing since 2007), femicide, journalist killings, judicial independence concerns (V-Dem notes democratic backsliding since 2019), and healthcare access gaps.

### Geography, Climate, and Biodiversity
Mexico spans 1,972,550 km² between latitudes 14°–33°N. The Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt (Pico de Orizaba, 5,700 m) crosses east-west; the Sierra Madre Oriental and Occidental (Rocky Mountain extensions) run north-south. The Tropic of Cancer divides temperate north from tropical south. Climate varies from Sonoran Desert (50°C+) to southern tropical lowlands (>2,000 mm rain). Mexico is one of 17 megadiverse countries (4th in biodiversity, 1st in reptiles, 2nd in mammals). It hosts 10–12% of world species (200,000+). Deforestation remains high (2nd fastest globally in 2002); 170,000 km² are Protected Natural Areas (34 biosphere reserves, 67 national parks). Native crops (maize, tomato, beans, cacao, vanilla, chiles) underpin global food systems.

### Government and Politics
The 1917 Constitution establishes a federal presidential republic with three branches. The bicameral Congress of the Union: Chamber of Deputies (500: 300 plurality, 200 proportional) and Senate (128: 64 plurality pairs, 32 first-minority, 32 proportional). The President (single 6-year term, *sexenio*) is head of state, government, and military. The Supreme Court of Justice (9 justices, 12-year terms, elected by popular vote since 2025) heads the judiciary. Three parties dominated the 20th/early 21st century: PRI (catch-all, hegemonic 1929–2000), PAN (conservative, 1939), PRD (left, 1989). MORENA (left-populist, founded 2014) has ruled since 2018. The federation comprises 31 states and Mexico City (autonomous entity since 2016). States have governors (6-year terms) and unicameral legislatures; municipalities are the base unit.

### Economy
Mexico is a newly industrialized, upper-middle-income economy (13th largest by nominal GDP and PPP; ~US$3.5T PPP, 2026). Key sectors: automotive (largest producer in North America; "Big Three" + VW, Nissan, Kia, Audi, BMW), electronics (6th globally, 2nd largest exporter to US), oil (Pemex, 6th largest producer, but exports fell from 62% to 7% of total 1980–2000), and remittances (top foreign income source, >US$50B/yr). Trade is heavily US/Canada-dependent (NAFTA/USMCA). Inequality remains high (OECD 2nd highest after Chile; top 10% hold 36% of income). Poverty rates fell 2018–24 (41.9% to 29.6% per INEGI), but healthcare access worsened (16.2% to 34.2% lacking). Minimum wage rose sharply (88 pesos 2018 → 315 pesos/US$17.20 2026). Energy: state-dominated (CFE, Pemex); renewables growing (Villanueva solar park, 828 MW; Oaxaca wind), but fossil fuels dominate; top 15 GHG emitter (2023); targets 35–40% cuts by 2030, net-zero 2050.

### Demographics and Society
Population: 134.4M (2026). Life expectancy: 75 (2020). 76.8% urban; 53% in 48 metro areas (Greater Mexico City: 21.8M). Ethnicity: 19.4% self-identify indigenous (2020); 6.1% speak an indigenous language (68 groups, 364 variants; Nahuatl 1.7M, Yucatec Maya 850K). 2.04% Afro-Mexican. Spanish is de facto national language; indigenous languages are *de jure* "national languages" (2003 Law). Religion: 77.8% Catholic (2nd largest absolute population globally); 11.2% Protestant/Evangelical; 8.1% none. Syncretism common (Day of the Dead, Santa Muerte). Education: 95.25% literacy (2020); UNAM top public university. Health: transitional epidemiology (mortality patterns near developed nations); dual public (IMSS, ISSSTE) / private system; medical tourism significant on northern border.

### Culture
Mexican culture synthesizes indigenous and Spanish roots, forged through conquest, syncretism, and *mestizaje* ideology (promoted post-Revolution by José Vasconcelos, *La Raza Cósmica*). **Art**: 7,500-year cave paintings; pre-Columbian murals (Bonampak, Teotihuacan); colonial religious art; 19th-century romanticism; "Big Three" muralists (Rivera, Siqueiros, Orozco) defined revolutionary narrative; Frida Kahlo (personal portraiture); 21st-century Mexico City = highest concentration of art museums globally. **Architecture**: Mesoamerican pyramids (Teotihuacan, Maya cities); colonial *tequitqui*, Baroque, Neoclassical (Hospicio Cabañas); 20th-century modernism (Ciudad Universitaria, UNESCO site); Luis Barragán (Pritzker 1980, vernacular minimalism). **Literature**: Pre-Columbian poetry (Nezahualcoyotl); colonial chronicles (Bernal Díaz); Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz; 19th-century liberal novel (*El Periquillo Sarniento*); Revolution novel (*Los de abajo*); Nobel laureate Octavio Paz (*Labyrinth of Solitude*); Rulfo, Fuentes, Poniatowska. **Cinema**: Golden Age (1940s–50s, rival to Hollywood; María Candelaria, Buñuel); New Wave (1990s–2000s: *Amores Perros*, *Roma*); "Three Amigos" directors (Iñárritu, Cuarón, del Toro). **Music**: Indigenous, colonial religious, 19th-century nationalist opera; Carlos Chávez (indigenous symphonies); traditional genres (mariachi, banda, norteño, corridos/narcocorridos); 1960s rock/La Onda counterculture; global stars (Santana, Maná, Los Lobos). **Cuisine**: UNESCO Intangible Heritage (2010); fusion of Mesoamerican staples (corn, chile, beans, tomato, cacao, nixtamalization) and Spanish imports (pork, beef, wheat, dairy); iconic dishes (mole, pozole, tamales, tacos); convent sweets; tequila/mezcal.

## Terms
- ****Mesoamerica**** — A historical region and cultural area in North America (central Mexico to Costa Rica) where complex pre-Columbian societies flourished, sharing traits like maize cultivation, a 260-day ritual calendar, and pyramid building.
- ****Nixtamalization**** — An ancient Mesoamerican process of soaking and cooking maize in an alkaline solution (lime water), which improves nutritional value (releasing niacin), flavor, and dough-forming properties for tortillas.
- ****Casta system**** — A colonial-era hierarchical racial classification system in New Spain ranking people by ancestry (e.g., *peninsulares*, *criollos*, *mestizos*, *mulatos*, *indios*, *negros*), determining legal rights, tribute obligations, and social status.
- ****Porfiriato**** — The 35-year dictatorship of General Porfirio Díaz (1876–1911), characterized by "order and progress," massive foreign investment, railroad expansion, and political repression, ending with the Mexican Revolution.
- ****Constitution of 1917**** — The current supreme legal document, drafted by the victorious Constitutionalists; notable for Article 27 (state ownership of subsoil resources, land reform) and Article 123 (labor rights), plus strong anticlerical provisions.
- ****PRI (Institutional Revolutionary Party)**** — The hegemonic political party founded in 1929 (as PNR) to institutionalize the Revolution; held the presidency uninterrupted 1929–2000 via corporatist control of labor, peasant, and popular sectors.
- ****NAFTA / USMCA**** — The North American Free Trade Agreement (1994–2020), replaced by the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement; created a trilateral free trade zone, deeply integrating Mexican manufacturing (especially auto/electronics) with U.S. supply chains.
- ****EZLN (Zapatista Army of National Liberation)**** — A predominantly indigenous armed group that rose in Chiapas on 1 Jan 1994 (NAFTA's first day) opposing neoliberalism and demanding autonomy; transitioned to a non-violent social movement after 12 days of combat.
- ****Mestizaje**** — The ideology, promoted heavily post-1920, that Mexican national identity is founded on the biological and cultural mixing (*mestizaje*) of indigenous and European peoples, marginalizing Afro-Mexican and pure indigenous identities.
- ****Pemex (Petróleos Mexicanos)**** — The state-owned petroleum company created by the 1938 oil expropriation; long the fiscal backbone of the state, now being partially privatized via exploration licenses.

## Debates and open questions
- **Democratic trajectory**: Since 2019, V-Dem and other indices register "democratic backsliding" (weakening checks on executive, judicial independence concerns, electoral reform disputes). Whether the 2024 supermajority consolidates or reverses this is contested.
- **Security strategy**: The militarized "War on Drugs" (since 2006) correlates with soaring homicides and disappearances (127,000+ deaths, 100,000+ missing). Debates persist on whether to continue military-led enforcement, pivot to police reform, or pursue negotiated pacts.
- **Judicial reform (2024)**: The shift to popular election of all federal judges (including Supreme Court) aims to combat corruption but raises fears of politicization, loss of expertise, and erosion of judicial independence.
