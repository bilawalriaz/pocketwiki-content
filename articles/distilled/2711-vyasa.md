# Vyasa

## Overview
Vyasa is a central sage (rishi) in Hindu tradition, revered as the compiler of the Vedas and the author of the epic Mahābhārata. He is considered a partial incarnation of Vishnu and one of the immortals (Chiranjivis). His work shaped the core scriptures of Hinduism, and his narrative role in the Mahābhārata makes him a pivotal figure in its theological and political events.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000–1500 BCE (traditional)** — Vyasa is born as Krishna Dvaipayana to Satyavati and sage Parashara on an island.
- **Dvapara Yuga (traditional)** — Vyasa divides the single, eternal Veda into four parts: Rigveda, Samaveda, Yajurveda, and Atharvaveda.
- **Dvapara Yuga (traditional)** — Under Niyoga practice, Vyasa fathers Dhritarashtra, Pandu, and Vidura to continue the Kuru dynasty.
- **Dvapara Yuga (traditional)** — Vyasa authors the Mahābhārata, the eighteen Purāṇas, and the Brahma Sutras.
- **Dvapara Yuga (traditional)** — Vyasa grants divine vision to Sanjaya to narrate the Kurukshetra War to Dhritarashtra.
- **Post-Kurukshetra War** — Vyasa facilitates post-war reconciliation, including bringing forth the spirits of the fallen.
- **Guru Purnima (annual)** — Festival commemorating Vyasa's birth and his division of the Vedas.

## Body

### Divider of the Vedas and Author
Vyasa's primary epithet, Veda Vyasa ("Splitter of the Vedas"), comes from his act of subcategorizing the primordial Veda into four canonical texts to make divine knowledge accessible to humans with limited capacity. According to the Vishnu Purana, this division occurs in every third Dvapara Yuga by an incarnation of Vishnu. Vyasa is also credited as the author of the Mahābhārata, the eighteen major Purāṇas (encyclopedic works), and the Brahma Sutras (a foundational text of Vedanta philosophy).

### Role in the Mahābhārata
Vyasa is not just the author but a key character in the Mahābhārata. He is the biological father of the central figures of the Kuru dynasty conflict: the blind king Dhritarashtra, the pale king Pandu (fathers of the Kauravas and Pandavas respectively), and the wise Vidura. He sired them through the practice of Niyoga (levirate) at his mother Satyavati's request to continue the royal line after her sons died childless.

As a spiritual guide, Vyasa attempts to prevent the war between the cousins. He counsels both sides, grants Sanjaya the divine sight to narrate the battle, and provides philosophical teachings to Yudhishthira and Arjuna during and after the conflict. He plays a crucial role in post-war political and moral reconstruction, including reconciling the grieving Gandhari and guiding the widows.

### Personal Life and Legacy
Vyasa's son, Shuka, is his spiritual successor. His four chief disciples—Paila, Jaimini, Vaishampayana, and Sumantu—were each entrusted with spreading one of the four Vedas. He is believed to have lived on the banks of the Ganges in modern Uttarakhand. In later tradition, he is said to have debated and approved the commentary on the Brahma Sutras by the philosopher Adi Shankara.

## Terms
- **Rishi**: A sage or seer in Hinduism, especially one who is a composer of Vedic hymns.
- **Vedas**: The oldest scriptures of Hinduism, considered eternal and revealed knowledge.
- **Purāṇas**: A genre of ancient Indian texts containing encyclopedic information on mythology, cosmology, and theology.
- **Brahma Sutras**: A foundational text of Vedanta philosophy, summarizing the philosophical ideas of the Upanishads.
- **Niyoga**: An ancient practice where a man is appointed to father children with a widow to continue a lineage.
- **Chiranjivi**: One of the seven or eight immortals in Hindu tradition who are believed to still be alive.
- **Dvapara Yuga**: The third of the four ages (yugas) in Hindu cosmology, characterized by declining virtue.
- **Kalpa**: A cyclic period of creation and dissolution in Hindu cosmology, lasting 4.32 billion years.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The historicity of Vyasa as a single individual is debated; he may represent a tradition or a lineage of compilers.
- The claim that Vyasa dictated the Mahābhārata to Ganesha is considered by scholars to be a later interpolation to the epic.
- The exact dating of the Mahābhārata and the Bhagavad Gita (contained within it) is uncertain, with scholarly estimates ranging from the 5th century BCE to the 2nd century BCE for the Gita's core.
