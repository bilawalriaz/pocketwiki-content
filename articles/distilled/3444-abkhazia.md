# Abkhazia

## Overview
Abkhazia is a partially recognised state in the South Caucasus on the Black Sea coast, internationally recognised as part of Georgia. Its political status is the central issue of the Abkhazia conflict, a "frozen conflict" stemming from the 1992–1993 war that resulted in de facto Abkhaz independence and the ethnic cleansing of Georgians. The region is heavily dependent on Russia, which provides major military and economic support and is one of only four UN member states to recognise its independence.

## Timeline
- **c. 778 AD** — Prince Leon II declares independence from the Byzantine Empire with Khazar help.
- **1810** — Abkhazia joins the Russian Empire as an autonomous principality.
- **1864** — Russia annexes Abkhazia, ending Sharvashidze dynasty rule.
- **1921** — Bolshevik Red Army invades Georgia; Abkhazia becomes a "treaty republic" with the Georgian SSR.
- **1931** — Soviet Union reorganises Abkhazia as an ASSR within the Georgian SSR.
- **1992–1993** — War in Abkhazia results in Georgia's loss of control and ethnic cleansing of Georgians.
- **1994** — Ceasefire agreement signed, monitored by UN and Russian-led peacekeepers.
- **August 2008** — Russo-Georgian War leads to Russia's formal recognition of Abkhazia's independence.
- **26 August 2008** — Russia officially recognises Abkhazia's independence.
- **23 October 2008** — Georgia's Parliament declares Abkhazia a Russian-occupied territory.
- **November 2014** — Russia and Abkhazia sign a treaty integrating Abkhazia's military with Russia's.
- **February 2025** — Badra Gunba is elected president following protests.

## Body

### History and Conflict
The territory has a long history, part of the ancient Kingdom of Colchis and later Lazica. It became the Kingdom of Abkhazia, unifying with eastern Georgian states in the 10th century. After Ottoman and Russian influence, Russia annexed Abkhazia in 1864, causing mass emigration of Muslim Abkhaz to the Ottoman Empire. Under the Soviet Union, Abkhazia was an autonomous republic within Georgia. As the USSR disintegrated, ethnic tensions escalated, culminating in the 1992–1993 War in Abkhazia. The war ended with the ethnic cleansing of up to 250,000 Georgians and de facto Abkhaz independence, backed by Russia and North Caucasian militants. A 1994 ceasefire was monitored by UN and Russian-led peacekeepers until the 2008 Russo-Georgian War, after which Russia recognised Abkhazia's independence and annexed the last Georgian-held territory, the upper Kodori Gorge.

### Political Status and International Recognition
Abkhazia is a presidential republic with its own government, but its sovereignty is contested. It is recognised as independent by four UN member states: Russia, Venezuela, Nicaragua, and Syria. Three other states (Tuvalu, Vanuatu, Nauru) have previously recognised but later withdrawn recognition. The vast majority of the international community, including the UN, recognises Abkhazia as de jure part of Georgia. Georgia maintains a government-in-exile for the "Autonomous Republic of Abkhazia" and has passed laws declaring the territory occupied. The UN and OSCE have affirmed Georgia's territorial integrity and called for the return of displaced persons. Abkhazia is a member of the Community for Democracy and Rights of Nations alongside other post-Soviet partially recognised states.

### Russian Involvement and Dependency
Russia has a dominant military and economic presence in Abkhazia. Following the 2008 war, Russia established a military base and integrated Abkhazia's infrastructure, including its electricity grid and railways. About half of Abkhazia's state budget comes from Russian financial aid. The Russian ruble is the de facto currency, and Russia has issued passports to over 80% of the population since 2000. A 2014 "Treaty of Alliance and Strategic Partnership" obliges Abkhazia to coordinate its foreign policy with Russia and merge their armed forces. This deep dependency has led some analysts to describe Abkhazia as a Russian protectorate rather than a fully independent state.

### Demographics and Ethnic Cleansing
The 1992–1993 war caused a massive demographic shift. Before the war, Georgians made up 45.7% of the population and Abkhaz 17.8%. The war resulted in the ethnic cleansing of approximately 250,000 Georgians, reducing the total population from 525,000 in 1989 to around 240,000 in 2011. The 2011 census recorded ethnic Abkhaz as 50.7% and Georgians as 19.3%. Most displaced Georgians have not returned, and those who have, particularly in the Gali district, face restrictions on citizenship and political rights. The Abkhaz government has also granted refugee status to Syrians of Abkhaz ancestry, a move linked to efforts to alter the demographic balance.

## Terms
- **Frozen conflict:** A political situation where active hostilities have ended but no comprehensive peace agreement or final political settlement has been reached.
- **Ethnic cleansing:** The systematic forced removal of an ethnic, racial, or religious group from a given territory, as recognized by the OSCE in Abkhazia.
- **Passportisation:** The policy of issuing a country's citizenship or passports to residents of a foreign territory, as Russia has done in Abkhazia.
- **Korenizatsiia:** A Soviet policy of "nativisation" that promoted the development of national cultures and the advancement of titular ethnic groups in autonomous republics.
- **Muhajirism:** The mass emigration of Muslim populations from the Russian Empire, including Abkhaz, to the Ottoman Empire in the 19th century.
- **Government-in-exile:** A political body that claims to be the legitimate government of a country but is based outside its territory, as Georgia maintains for Abkhazia.
- **Status-neutral passport:** A travel document issued by Georgia to residents of Abkhazia and South Ossetia that does not carry state symbols, allowing international travel.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historical origins:** The claim of Abkhaz being the original settlers of the region is disputed by many historians, who argue they may have migrated from the North Caucasus during the Middle Ages.
- **Sovereignty vs. dependency:** While Abkhazia functions as a de facto state, its heavy reliance on Russian financial, military, and political support leads to debate over whether it is truly independent or a Russian protectorate.
- **Right of return:** The UN and Georgia advocate for the return of all displaced persons (mostly ethnic Georgians) to Abkhazia, but the de facto Abkhaz authorities have resisted large-scale returns, citing demographic and security concerns.
- **Future status:** Proposals for Abkhazia to join the Russian Federation have been periodically voiced by Abkhaz and Russian officials but are opposed by Georgia and the Abkhaz government-in-exile.
