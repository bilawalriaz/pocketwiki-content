# Sixteen Kingdoms

## Overview
The Sixteen Kingdoms (304–439) was a period of political fragmentation in northern China following the collapse of the Western Jin dynasty. It was defined by the rise of short-lived states founded by non-Han peoples, collectively known as the "Five Barbarians," who competed for supremacy while the Jin dynasty continued in the south. This era was the first time non-Han rulers governed the Chinese heartland as emperors, forcing a redefinition of Chinese identity through experiments in multi-ethnic governance that laid the groundwork for the later Sui and Tang dynasties.

## Timeline
- **304** — Li Xiong founds Cheng-Han in Sichuan; Liu Yuan rebels to restore Han dynasty, founding Han-Zhao.
- **311** — Han-Zhao captures Luoyang (Disaster of Yongjia).
- **316** — Han-Zhao captures Chang'an, ending Western Jin. Eastern Jin established in Jiankang.
- **319** — Shi Le founds Later Zhao after splitting from Han-Zhao.
- **351** — Fu Jiàn founds Former Qin.
- **370** — Former Qin conquers Former Yan.
- **376** — Former Qin unifies northern China.
- **383** — Former Qin defeated at Battle of Fei River by Eastern Jin.
- **384** — Former Qin collapses; Later Yan, Later Qin, and Western Yan are founded.
- **398** — Northern Wei (Tuoba Xianbei) captures Central Plains from Later Yan.
- **417** — Eastern Jin destroys Later Qin.
- **439** — Northern Wei conquers Northern Liang, reunifying northern China.

## Body

### Background and Fall of Western Jin
Since the Han dynasty, non-Han peoples like the Xiongnu, Xianbei, Di, Qiang, and Jie had been resettling in northern China, often as frontier auxiliaries or laborers. The War of the Eight Princes (291–306) severely weakened the Jin court, allowing these groups to exploit the chaos. In 304, Liu Yuan (Xiongnu) and Li Xiong (Di) launched rebellions. Han-Zhao forces captured the Jin capitals in 311 and 316, ending the Western Jin. The Jin court reconstituted itself in the south as the Eastern Jin.

### Fragmentation and the Rise of Major States
The period was characterized by constant warfare and shifting alliances. The Later Zhao (319–351), founded by the Jie leader Shi Le, unified much of the north but collapsed after his death into civil war and ethnic cleansing. In the northwest, the Former Liang (301–376) preserved Han literati culture. The Former Yan (337–370), founded by the Xianbei Murong clan, rose in the northeast. The Former Qin (351–394), led by the Di Fu clan, achieved a brief unification of the north under Fu Jiān, who adopted Confucian governance. His defeat at the Battle of Fei River (383) led to a new wave of fragmentation.

### Post-Fei River Fragmentation and Reunification
After 383, the north splintered again. The Later Yan (384–409), Later Qin (384–417), and Western Qin (385–431) emerged. The Later Qin, under Yao Xing, became a center for Buddhist scholarship, sponsoring the monk Kumārajīva. In the northeast, the Northern Wei (386–535), founded by the Xianbei Tuoba clan, gradually expanded. Under Emperor Taiwu (r. 423–452), the Northern Wei systematically conquered its rivals, including the Helian Xia, Northern Yan, and finally the Northern Liang in 439, reunifying northern China.

### Governance and Society
Northern kingdoms adopted Han-style imperial titles and bureaucracy, often using a "dual-system" to govern Han and non-Han populations separately via institutions like the Chanyu Office. Society was shaped by fortified manors (*wubao*) run by local magnates, who held significant autonomy. These magnates were often recognized as officials by non-Han rulers in exchange for resources, creating a class of "hidden households" (*yinhu*) outside state control.

### Relations with the Eastern Jin
The Eastern Jin claimed legitimacy as the true continuation of the Jin dynasty and refused to treat northern states as equals. It launched several northern expeditions, with mixed success. General Huan Wen destroyed Cheng-Han (347) and briefly recovered Luoyang. General Liu Yu later destroyed the Southern Yan (410) and Later Qin (417), but could not hold the territory. These campaigns were often hampered by court politics and internal rebellions.

### Religion and Culture
Buddhism received widespread state patronage for the first time, flourishing under rulers like Shi Hu (Later Zhao) and Yao Xing (Later Qin). Monks like Fotu Cheng and Kumārajīva held high positions, and translation of sutras accelerated. This period also saw the carving of early Buddhist cave art in the Hexi Corridor (e.g., Mogao Caves). Taoism, particularly the Way of the Celestial Masters, remained influential, especially in the Cheng-Han state.

## Terms
- **Five Barbarians**: Collective term for the Xiongnu, Xianbei, Di, Jie, and Qiang peoples who founded most of the Sixteen Kingdoms.
- **Sinicization**: The process of adopting or being influenced by Chinese culture, language, and customs.
- **Mandate of Heaven**: Chinese political doctrine that a ruler's authority to govern is granted by a divine cosmic force.
- **Dual-system**: A governance model separating administrative systems for Han Chinese and non-Han populations.
- **Chanyu Office**: An administrative body established by non-Han rulers to manage the affairs of their own tribal peoples.
- **Wubao**: Fortified manors or estates controlled by local magnates, serving as economic and military units.
- **Yinhu**: "Hidden households"—populations concealed from state tax and labor registers by private magnates.
- **Battle of Fei River (383)**: Decisive defeat of the Former Qin by the Eastern Jin, leading to the re-fragmentation of northern China.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact ethnic origins of the Jie people, particularly Shi Le's group, remain disputed among historians.
- The historiographical definition of the "Sixteen Kingdoms" is based on the 6th-century work of Cui Hong, which excludes several contemporary regimes like the Western Yan and Dai/Northern Wei, leading to debates about periodization and inclusion.
- The degree to which non-Han rulers were genuinely sinicized versus pragmatically adopting Chinese institutions for control is a subject of ongoing scholarly analysis.
