# John Milton

## Overview
John Milton (1608–1674) was an English poet, polemicist, and civil servant whose life paralleled the political upheavals of Stuart England. He is best known for *Paradise Lost* (1667), a blank-verse epic on the Fall of Man composed after he became totally blind, which cemented his reputation as one of the greatest English poets. A Puritan and republican, Milton served as Secretary for Foreign Tongues under the Commonwealth and Oliver Cromwell, defending the regicide of Charles I in Latin tracts that earned him a European reputation. His *Areopagitica* (1644), a passionate attack on pre-publication censorship, remains a foundational text for freedom of speech. Milton’s political commitments led to imprisonment and the burning of his books after the Restoration of 1660, but he spent his final years completing his major poetic works. His innovative blank verse, Latinate vocabulary, and synthesis of Christian theology with classical learning profoundly influenced English literature, though his republicanism made him a polarizing figure for centuries.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Milton was born into a prosperous London family; his father, a scrivener and composer disinherited for embracing Protestantism, provided a private tutor, Thomas Young, a Scottish Presbyterian who introduced Milton to religious radicalism. At St Paul’s School and Christ’s College, Cambridge, Milton mastered Latin, Greek, and Hebrew, ranking fourth in his BA class (1629). He clashed with the curriculum’s formal Latin debates and may have been rusticated (suspended) in his first year after quarrelling with his tutor, Bishop William Chappell, though plague is also a possible cause. Despite alienation from peers, he wrote significant early English poems, including *L'Allegro* and *Il Penseroso*, and his first published poem, an epitaph on Shakespeare (1630).

### Private Study, Poetry, and the Grand Tour
After his MA (1632), Milton undertook six years of intensive self-directed study at his father’s homes in Hammersmith and Horton, reading theology, philosophy, history, and science in multiple languages. He is considered among the most learned English poets. During this period he wrote the masque *Comus* (1634), arguing for temperance and chastity, and the pastoral elegy *Lycidas* (1637). In 1638–39, he toured France and Italy for 15 months. In Florence, he met the astronomer Galileo under house arrest and attended academies; in Rome, he accessed intellectual circles and the Vatican Library; in Geneva, he encountered a model of republicanism that later informed his political thought. He cut the tour short due to news of impending civil war in England and the death of his friend Charles Diodati.

### Civil War, Polemics, and Marriage
Returning to England in 1639, Milton wrote prose tracts against episcopacy (church government by bishops) for the Puritan/Parliamentary cause, attacking Archbishop William Laud. He became a schoolmaster, leading to his tract *Of Education* (1644). In 1642, aged 34, he married 17-year-old Mary Powell; the marriage quickly failed—she left him, finding his lifestyle austere and holding Royalist views. Her desertion prompted Milton to publish a series of pamphlets (1643–45) arguing for divorce on grounds of incompatibility rather than solely adultery, drawing fierce criticism from Presbyterian divines like Herbert Palmer. The hostile reception to these tracts spurred *Areopagitica* (1644), his seminal speech against pre-publication licensing, synthesizing neo-Roman liberty with Christian conscience. Mary returned in 1645; they had four children before her death in 1652.

### Secretary for Foreign Tongues and the Commonwealth
Following the Parliamentary victory and Charles I’s execution (1649), Milton published *The Tenure of Kings and Magistrates*, justifying regicide and popular sovereignty. He was appointed Secretary for Foreign Tongues (March 1649), composing diplomatic correspondence in Latin and producing propaganda. He answered the Royalist bestseller *Eikon Basilike* with *Eikonoklastes* (1649) and replied to Claudius Salmasius’s *Defensio Regia* with *Defensio pro Populo Anglicano* (1651), earning European fame for his Ciceronian Latin. A second defence, *Defensio Secunda* (1654), praised Cromwell but urged fidelity to revolutionary principles. By 1652, Milton was totally blind (likely bilateral retinal detachment or glaucoma), dictating to aides including Andrew Marvell. He held the secretaryship until 1660, though deputies performed most duties after his blindness.

### The Restoration and Final Years
Cromwell’s death (1658) triggered the Republic’s collapse. Milton stubbornly opposed the return of monarchy, publishing *The Ready and Easy Way to Establish a Free Commonwealth* (1660), advocating an oligarchic council with perpetual membership—a position at odds with popular sentiment. Upon the Restoration (May 1660), he hid, was arrested, and briefly imprisoned; his books were burnt. Friends like Marvell secured his release. He married Elizabeth Minshull (1663) and lived quietly in London, retiring to Chalfont St. Giles during the Great Plague. He published minor prose works, including *History of Britain* (1670) and *Of True Religion* (1672), arguing for toleration (except Catholics), which fed into the Exclusion Crisis debates.

### Poetry: *Paradise Lost* and Late Works
Milton’s poetry appeared slowly: a 1645 collection was his only verse in print until *Paradise Lost*. Composed 1658–1664 while blind and impoverished, the epic was dictated to amanuenses. It sold to publisher Samuel Simmons for £5 (plus royalties); the first quarto edition (1667) sold out in 18 months. The poem addresses the Fall of Man—Satan’s temptation of Adam and Eve and their expulsion from Eden—reflecting Milton’s despair at the Revolution’s failure yet affirming human potential. He followed with *Paradise Regained* and the tragedy *Samson Agonistes* (1671), both reflecting his post-Restoration situation. The 1674 second edition of *Paradise Lost* expanded to 12 books and included a note on "why the poem rhymes not," defending blank verse as recovered "ancient liberty."

### Philosophical and Theological Views
Milton’s theology was heterodox and idiosyncratic, rooted in Puritan emphasis on conscience but extending beyond orthodoxy. Scholar Stephen Fallon argues Milton leaned toward **monism** (or animist materialism)—the view that a single animate, self-active material substance composes all reality, from stones to God—rejecting Cartesian dualism and Hobbesian mechanism. This appears in *Paradise Lost* where angels eat and engage in sexuality, and in *De Doctrina Christiana* (unpublished until 1823), which denies the dual natures of man and argues for Creation *ex Deo* (out of God). Milton was likely **Arian** (viewing the Son as subordinate to the Father), licensing the anti-Trinitarian Racovian Catechism in 1650, though he later called the Trinity a "plain doctrine in Scripture." He rejected Catholicism and episcopacy as "Babylon," viewed England as an elect nation akin to Israel during the Interregnum, and in later works treated the Restoration as a Fall from Grace. He died alienated from all sects, finding Quakers most congenial, attending no services.

### Political Thought
Milton’s politics combined Reformed faith with Humanist zeal. He opposed the divine right of kings, fought for the Puritan cause, but turned on the Presbyterian Parliament when it imposed censorship (*Areopagitica*). His thought evolved: 1641–42 focused on church polity; 1649–54 justified the regicide and Commonwealth; 1659–60 attempted to forestall the Restoration. He praised Cromwell initially but grew critical, aligning with republicans like Sir Henry Vane. In *The Ready and Easy Way*, he proposed an unelected perpetual council, citing Dutch and Venetian models, rejecting popular democracy. Scholars (e.g., John Rogers) argue this shift toward elitism—government by a competent minority—reflects lost faith in the populace, mirrored in *Paradise Lost* where God uses force against disobedient subjects. His ideas influenced the Radical Whigs and, through them, the American Revolution.

### Religious Toleration and Divorce
In *Areopagitica*, Milton argued for "the liberty to know, to utter, and to argue freely according to conscience," linking liberty to religious conscience and truth-seeking, not unrestricted license. He advocated disestablishment as the only path to broad toleration. On divorce, he argued in *The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce* (1643) that it was a private matter, not legal/ecclesiastical, citing "mutual solace" as marriage’s goal. The Westminster Assembly did not condemn him; the *Westminster Confession* (1646) later allowed divorce for abandonment or adultery. Yet Puritan reaction was mixed: Herbert Palmer demanded his book be burnt. Milton abandoned the campaign after 1645 but supported polygamy in *De Doctrina*.

### Literary Legacy and Influence
Milton’s blank verse—unrhymed iambic pentameter—was a radical innovation. He treated rhyme as "modern bondage," pursuing "ancient liberty" through freedom, breadth, and imaginative suggestiveness. He modified rigid metrical norms: using hypermetrical syllables, stress inversions, shifting caesuras, and enjambment to create a "transcendental union of order and freedom." Though 18th-century poets (Pope, Gray) initially found his rhythm "displeasing," by the late 18th century his "harmony" was recognized as a new mode of image-making. His Latinate neologisms and archaic diction ("Miltonian dialect") influenced Pope, Thomson, and the Romantics. *Paradise Lost* was immediately recognized as sublime; Dryden, Addison, Johnson, and Voltaire revered it. Blake claimed Milton as a spiritual father; Wordsworth modelled *The Prelude* on it; Keats struggled against "Miltonic inversions." *Frankenstein* is read as a Romantic rewriting of *Paradise Lost*. Victorian writers (Carlyle, Eliot, Hardy) revered him; T.S. Eliot and Ezra Pound attacked him, but F.R. Leavis and Harold Bloom affirmed his inescapable centrality. *Areopagitica* is cited in First Amendment jurisprudence; titles from *Paradise Lost* inspire works from *Darkness at Noon* to *His Dark Materials*. Milton’s verse also inspired major musical settings by Handel, Haydn, and Parry.

## Terms
- ****Blank verse**** — Unrhymed poetry, typically in iambic pentameter; Milton pioneered its use for non-dramatic epic poetry, treating it as "ancient liberty" recovered from the "bondage of Rimeing."
- ****Areopagitica**** — Milton’s 1644 prose speech to Parliament opposing the Licensing Order of 1643; a foundational argument for freedom of speech and press grounded in conscience and the pursuit of truth.
- ****Secretary for Foreign Tongues**** — Milton’s government post (1649–1660) under the Commonwealth Council of State; responsible for composing diplomatic correspondence in Latin and other languages and producing propaganda.
- ****Regicide**** — The killing of a king; Milton defended the 1649 execution of Charles I in *The Tenure of Kings and Magistrates* and *Eikonoklastes*, arguing for the people’s right to hold rulers accountable.
- ****Monism (Animist Materialism)**** — The philosophical view, attributed to Milton by Stephen Fallon, that a single animate, self-active material substance constitutes all reality (matter, mind, angels, God), rejecting mind-body dualism.
- ****Arianism**** — The theological doctrine that the Son is subordinate to the Father, not co-eternal; Milton is widely suspected of holding this view, though debated.
- ****Erastianism**** — The doctrine that the state is supreme over the church in ecclesiastical matters; Milton attacked this in *A Treatise of Civil Power* (1659).
- ****Good Old Cause**** — The term for the Parliamentarian/republican cause during the Civil War and Interregnum; Milton remained "unrepentant" for his commitment to it.
- ****Caesura**** — A pause within a line of verse; Milton shifted caesuras from the traditional middle/end positions to all parts of the line, contributing to his rhythmic freedom.
- ****De Doctrina Christiana**** — An unfinished Latin theological treatise (published 1823/1825, authorship probable) outlining Milton’s heterodox views, including anti-Trinitarianism and arguments for divorce and polygamy.

## Debates and open questions
- **Cause of blindness**: Debated between bilateral retinal detachment and glaucoma; the source notes no consensus.
- **Rustication at Cambridge**: Whether Milton was suspended for quarrelling with tutor William Chappell, whipped (per Aubrey), or sent home due to the 1625 plague remains disputed.
- **Arianism vs. Orthodoxy**: Scholars dispute whether Milton was a consistent Arian (subordinationist); he licensed the Racovian Catechism (1650) but later affirmed the Trinity as a "plain doctrine."
- **Monism vs. Dualism in *Paradise Lost***: Fallon argues for a consistent animist monism (angels/humans share material substance); John Rogers counters that the Creation scene’s "dead matter" signals a hierarchical dualism and elitist break from monism.
- **Elitism in later politics**: Whether Milton’s *Ready and Easy Way* proposal for a perpetual oligarchic council represents a pragmatic defense of liberty or an elitist abandonment of popular sovereignty is debated (Rogers vs. Woolrych/Worden).
- **Authorship of *De Doctrina Christiana***: The treatise was undiscovered until 1823; its attribution to Milton is probable but disputed.
- **Private life vs. public polemic**: Biographers debate whether Mary Powell’s desertion *caused* Milton’s divorce tracts or merely coincided with pre-existing intellectual convictions (Anna Beer warns against cynicism).
