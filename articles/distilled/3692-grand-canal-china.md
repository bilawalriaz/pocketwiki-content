# Grand Canal (China)

## Overview
The Grand Canal is the world's longest artificial waterway, a system of interconnected canals in North and East China that served as the primary north-south transport artery for imperial China. Its construction and maintenance were crucial for transporting grain from the fertile south to northern capitals, enabling political integration, economic prosperity, and cultural exchange across the empire for over a millennium.

## Timeline
- **486 BC** — King Fuchai of Wu orders construction of the Han Canal, connecting the Yangtze and Huai rivers.
- **605–609** — Emperor Yang of Sui completes the Grand Canal, linking the capital Luoyang to the south and northeast.
- **984** — Qiao Weiyue invents the pound lock to improve canal navigation.
- **1128** — Yellow River dikes are deliberately breached, diverting the river and damaging the canal.
- **1280s** — Yuan dynasty engineers dig a new summit section across Shandong, creating a direct Beijing-Hangzhou route.
- **1411–1415** — Ming dynasty undertakes a major renovation, dredging and building new channels and locks.
- **1855** — Yellow River floods and changes course, severing the canal in Shandong.
- **1949–present** — People's Republic of China reconstructs and improves the canal for modern transport and water transfer.
- **2014** — UNESCO designates the Grand Canal as a World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Origins and Sui Dynasty Unification
The oldest sections date to the Spring and Autumn period (c. 5th century BC), built for military and trade purposes by states like Wu and Yue. The canal was first unified into a national system under Emperor Yang of Sui (r. 604–618). To supply his capital at Luoyang and armies fighting the Goguryeo–Sui War, he conscripted millions of laborers to connect existing waterways. This created a continuous route from the Yangtze Delta to the north, though it relied on a mix of artificial and natural channels. The massive human cost contributed to rebellions and the Sui's rapid fall, but the canal proved vital for the subsequent Tang dynasty.

### Economic Lifeline and Dynastic Maintenance
During the Tang (618–907) and Song (960–1279) dynasties, the canal was the empire's economic backbone, transporting tax grain (*caoyun*) and goods. Cities like Yangzhou and Kaifeng flourished as major hubs. The canal's importance grew with each dynasty that maintained it, including the Mongol Yuan, Ming, and Qing. The Yuan dynasty (1271–1368) rerouted the canal directly to their new capital at Beijing, shortening its length. The Ming dynasty (1368–1644) performed a major renovation (1411–1415) to improve efficiency. The canal's fate was often tied to the Yellow River; deliberate flooding during wars (e.g., 1128) and natural course changes (e.g., 1855) repeatedly damaged it, requiring constant repair.

### Modern Era and Current Use
After the 1855 Yellow River flood and the rise of railways, the canal declined. The People's Republic of China has since reconstructed it. It now serves as the eastern route of the South-North Water Transfer Project, pumping Yangtze water to the arid north. While the southern section (south of the Yellow River) remains heavily used for bulk cargo like coal and construction materials, the northern section is largely non-navigable due to silt and water shortages. Pollution from industrialization has been a major concern, prompting recent cleanup and restoration projects.

## Terms
- **Grand Canal (Dà Yùnhé):** The world's longest artificial waterway, a system linking major rivers in North and East China.
- **Jing–Hang Grand Canal:** The current main stem of the canal, extending 1,776 km from Beijing to Hangzhou.
- **Caoyun:** The system of transporting tax grain via the Grand Canal from the south to northern capitals.
- **Pound lock:** A canal lock with gates at both ends, invented in 984 to allow ships to navigate elevation changes.
- **Jiangnan Canal:** The southernmost section of the modern canal, from Hangzhou to Zhenjiang, heavily used for barge traffic.
- **South-North Water Transfer Project:** A modern Chinese infrastructure project using the Grand Canal's eastern route to divert Yangtze water northward.
- **Mandate of Heaven:** The traditional Chinese belief that a dynasty's right to rule is granted by heaven and can be lost due to poor governance or natural disasters.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact date and origin of the earliest canal sections, like the Hong Canal, are uncertain and debated by historians.
- The long-term environmental impact of the South-North Water Transfer Project on the canal's ecosystem and local fisheries remains a concern.
- The feasibility and cost of fully restoring navigation on the northern section of the canal to Beijing are ongoing subjects of planning and debate.
