# Social equality

## Overview
Social equality is a state in which all individuals possess equal rights, liberties, and status, requiring the absence of legally enforced class or caste boundaries and discrimination based on inalienable identity traits. It encompasses formal equality (equal opportunity based on merit) and substantive equality (equality of outcomes for groups). The concept has evolved from ancient philosophical roots through Enlightenment secularism to modern political theory, driving major historical movements such as abolitionism, suffrage, civil rights, and decolonization. Advancement correlates with economic development, education, healthcare access, and democratic institutions, though scholars debate whether justice requires equal starting conditions, fair procedures, or equal results.

## Timeline
- **Ancient Greece** — Stoic philosophers assert universal human reason; Plato and Aristotle analyze equality within hierarchical frameworks.
- **16th–17th century** — Reformation challenges traditional religious hierarchies, enabling secular foundations for equality.
- **17th–18th century** — Enlightenment thinkers (Locke, Rousseau, Montesquieu, Voltaire, Kant, Wollstonecraft) establish equal rights, governance, and gender equality as political principles.
- **1776** — U.S. Declaration of Independence asserts "all men are created equal," becoming a foundational reference for later movements.
- **1789** — French Revolution abolishes feudal privileges, establishing legal equality.
- **1848** — Seneca Falls Convention launches organized women's suffrage movement.
- **1868–1912** — Meiji Reforms in Japan abolish feudal hierarchies and introduce legal equality.
- **1945** — United Nations founded to promote international cooperation and human rights.
- **1948–1994** — Anti-Apartheid Movement in South Africa dismantles racial segregation.
- **1950s–1960s** — U.S. Civil Rights Movement, led by figures like Martin Luther King Jr., dismantles segregation laws.
- **1970s–present** — Disability Rights Movement achieves legislation like the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990); LGBTQ+ rights movements expand legal and social recognition.
- **1971** — John Rawls publishes *A Theory of Justice*, defining equality via primary goods; later debated by Dworkin, Sen, and Nozick.

## Body

### Definition and Scope
Social equality is defined variously as equality of power, rights, goods, opportunities, capabilities, or combinations thereof. It is distinct from mere alleviation of suffering; rather, it expresses the ideal that any two individuals deserve equal respect and an equal right to participate in society regardless of hierarchy. Societies promoting social equality avoid rank distinctions, basing interpersonal relations on mutual respect. The concept applies both within societies—covering civil rights, expression, autonomy, and access to public goods—and between societies, where globalization reveals power disparities between states and their citizens. As progress occurs, the scope expands to address newly recognized inequalities.

### Philosophical History
Early Greek philosophy introduced the theme: Stoics posited universal reason; Plato explored monastic equality and "equality in depravity"; Aristotle developed a citizenship-based equality but rejected total social equality in favor of hierarchy. The European Reformation challenged religious hierarchies, allowing post-Reformation political philosophy to provide a secular foundation. The Enlightenment (17th–18th centuries) cemented the modern intellectual basis: Locke and Rousseau grounded legitimate authority in equal citizen rights; Montesquieu and Voltaire stressed equality in governance and thought; Kant established universal moral dignity; Wollstonecraft extended these principles to women. In the 20th century, John Rawls defined justice through primary goods (liberty, opportunity, respect, wealth). Ronald Dworkin added responsibility for voluntary choices versus natural endowments. Amartya Sen shifted focus from resources to capabilities. Robert Nozick rejected distributive equality, arguing producers are entitled to their resources regardless of resulting inequality.

### Types of Social Equality
**Gender equality** addresses disparities between men, women, and intersex people; the WHO estimates one in three women globally face violence or abuse linked to inequality. **Racial and ethnic equality** targets hierarchies based on origin. **Belief and ideology equality** demands equal status for all political and religious convictions. **Disability rights** require both non-discrimination and accommodation (e.g., the U.S. ADA) to overcome environmental barriers and stigma. **Political equality**—formalized by the 14th, 19th Amendments and the Voting Rights Act of 1965—guarantees participation, yet **economic equality** remains elusive; systems produce unequal access to education and resources that legislation alone cannot close, leaving the promise of equal participation incomplete for many.

### Means of Advancement
Empirical correlations link social equality to several structural factors. Economic development and industrialization correlate with rising equality, as does equitable resource distribution. Education access promotes equality among individuals. Healthcare access and social protection systems reduce health and living-standard disparities. Democratic participation and civil rights protections correlate with political and legal equality. Welfare policies and redistributive taxation moderate economic inequality. These factors are interconnected: development enables education and healthcare, which support democratic engagement, which in turn sustains redistributive policies.

### Standards of Equality
**Ontological equality** holds that everyone is created equal at birth, possessing inherent natural rights (reflected in the U.S. Declaration of Independence and Locke’s philosophy). **Equality of opportunity** asserts that competitive procedures for allocating scarce resources should use only relevant criteria (merit), ignoring irrelevant factors like race or gender; Lesley Jacobs identifies this as core to egalitarian justice and characteristic of bourgeois capitalist societies. **Equality of condition** argues for equal starting points, requiring structural intervention ("social engineering") to compensate for initial disadvantages; Sharon Kahn emphasizes that true freedom requires eliminating systemic barriers, not just legal ones. **Equality of outcome** demands identical end results regardless of effort; it is often conflated with communism, though Lenin described socialism as equal *opportunity* to use collective means of production, not equal outcomes. In education, this standard seeks comparable attainment medians across demographic groups.

## Terms
- ****Formal equality**** — Equal opportunity for individuals based on merit; procedures are identical for all.
- ****Substantive equality**** — Equality of outcomes for groups; also called social equity.
- ****Ontological equality**** — The principle that all humans are created equal at birth with inherent natural rights.
- ****Equality of opportunity**** — A standard where competitive allocation uses only relevant criteria, ignoring irrelevant identity factors.
- ****Equality of condition**** — A standard requiring equal starting points, often via structural interventions to offset initial disadvantages.
- ****Equality of outcome**** — A standard where all individuals or groups end with the same results regardless of input or effort.
- ****Primary goods (Rawls)**** — The metrics of justice: basic liberties, opportunities, respect, and wealth/income.
- ****Capability approach (Sen)**** — Evaluation of equality based on the substantive freedom to achieve valued functionings, not just resource holdings.
- ****Egalitarianism**** — The advocacy of social equality.
- ****Intersectionality (implied)**** — The overlapping of identity categories (race, gender, class, disability, etc.) that compound discrimination.

## Debates and open questions
- **Metric of justice**: Rawls (primary goods) vs. Dworkin (responsibility-sensitive resources) vs. Sen (capabilities) vs. Nozick (entitlement/historical acquisition).
- **Scope of responsibility**: Whether individuals should bear costs of voluntary choices but not unchosen circumstances (Dworkin), and how to distinguish them in practice.
- **Outcome vs. opportunity**: Whether equality of condition or outcome requires illiberal "social engineering" that violates liberty or meritocratic norms.
- **Economic vs. political equality**: Whether formal political rights are meaningful without material economic equality to exercise them.
- **Universalism vs. cultural particularism**: Whether standards derived from Western Enlightenment (ontological equality, human rights) apply globally or conflict with local traditions.
- **Expanding categories**: How to address emerging inequalities (e.g., digital access, algorithmic bias, genetic enhancement) within existing frameworks.
