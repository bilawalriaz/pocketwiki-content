# Migration Period

## Overview
The Migration Period (c. 300–600 AD) was a transformative era in European history marked by large-scale movements of peoples, primarily Germanic tribes, into and within the territories of the Western Roman Empire. These migrations contributed to the empire's collapse and the establishment of new post-Roman kingdoms, fundamentally reshaping the political, cultural, and ethnic landscape of Europe. The period's causes, scale, and consequences remain subjects of significant scholarly debate.

## Timeline
- **c. 375** — Huns invade Europe from Asia, prompting Gothic migrations.
- **376** — Tervingi (Visigoths) cross the Danube into Roman territory.
- **406** — Vandals, Alans, and Suebi cross the Rhine into Gaul.
- **410** — Visigoths sack Rome.
- **476** — Odoacer deposes the last Western Roman Emperor, Romulus Augustulus.
- **486** — Clovis I of the Franks defeats Syagrius, consolidating Frankish power in Gaul.
- **489** — Ostrogoths under Theodoric invade Italy.
- **568** — Lombards conquer much of Italy, often marking the period's traditional end.
- **711** — Moors invade the Iberian Peninsula.
- **c. 895** — Hungarian conquest of the Carpathian Basin.

## Body

### Causes and Scale of Migration
The period was driven by various factors, including pressure from the Huns, internal Roman instability, and economic incentives. Migrations involved groups of varying sizes, from small war bands to entire tribes. Modern estimates suggest the Gothic crossing of the Danube (376) involved 90,000–200,000 people, the Rhine crossing (406) up to 200,000, and the Lombard invasion of Italy (568) around 150,000. Total migration into Roman territory may have ranged from 500,000 to over 1,000,000 people.

### First Wave: Germanic Settlements (c. 300–500)
Germanic tribes, including Goths, Vandals, and Franks, moved into Roman provinces. The Visigoths settled in Gaul and later Iberia. The Franks gradually entered Gaul, establishing a kingdom under Clovis I that became the nucleus of France and Germany. Anglo-Saxons settled in Britain after Roman control ended, while Burgundians settled in parts of modern France and Switzerland.

### Second Wave: Slavic and Other Expansions (c. 500–700)
Slavic tribes expanded into central, southern, and eastern Europe, making the eastern half predominantly Slavic-speaking. Turkic Avars and later Magyars (Hungarians) also migrated. The Lombards settled in Italy. Bulgars moved into the Balkans, altering its demographic landscape permanently. Arab armies attempted invasions of southeastern Europe but were repelled, while Moors conquered most of Iberia from the Visigoths.

### Barbarian Identity and Ethnogenesis
Scholars debate how "barbarian" identities were formed. The 19th-century "primordialist" view saw tribes as coherent biological entities. Modern "constructionist" theories, like the Austrian school's concept of *ethnogenesis*, argue that tribal identities were social constructs forged by elite "tradition bearers" (*Traditionskern*) using metaphors of kinship. This process was dynamic, not based on fixed racial or ethnic lines.

### Viewpoints on the Period's Nature
Terminology reflects differing perspectives: German and Slavic scholars often use "migration" (*Völkerwanderung*), while French and Italian historiography uses "barbarian invasions." Guy Halsall argues the migrations were a *result* of the Roman Empire's fall, not its cause, as tribes were drawn into its internal collapse. The takeover varied regionally, from administrative handovers in Aquitaine to power vacuums in Gaul. The Eastern Roman Empire adapted and survived, while the West fragmented into new kingdoms.

## Terms
- **Völkerwanderung**: German term for the Migration Period, literally "wandering of peoples."
- **Ethnogenesis**: The process by which a group of people comes to be regarded as a distinct ethnic group or tribe.
- **Traditionskern** ("kernel of tradition"): A core group of military or aristocratic elites who provided the stimulus for forming tribal polities.
- **Foederati**: Barbarian tribes allied with and settled within the Roman Empire under treaty terms.
- **Hospitalitas**: The Roman practice of quartering soldiers among the civilian population, later adapted to settle allied barbarian armies.
- **Limes**: The fortified frontier of the Roman Empire.
- **Barbaricum**: The lands beyond the Roman Empire's frontiers, inhabited by non-Roman peoples.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause vs. Effect**: Did increased migrations cause the fall of the Western Roman Empire, or were they a consequence of its internal breakdown?
- **Scale of Migration**: Estimates for the size of migrating groups vary widely, and the total demographic impact is debated.
- **Nature of Identity**: Were "barbarian" tribes coherent ethnic groups with a shared identity, or fluid political constructs?
- **Historiographical Perspective**: Is the period best understood as a destructive "invasion" or a transformative "migration" and settlement process?
