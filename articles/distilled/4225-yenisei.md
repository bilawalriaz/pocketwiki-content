# Yenisei

## Overview
The Yenisei is the fifth-longest river system in the world and the largest river draining into the Arctic Ocean. It flows north through central Siberia, dividing the Western Siberian Plain from the Central Siberian Plateau, and is historically significant for its role in trade, transportation, and as a homeland for indigenous peoples.

## Timeline
- **c. 7th century** — First written mention of the river as "Kem" in Chinese sources during contact with the Yenisei Kyrgyz.
- **1605** — Russians first reach the upper Yenisey, traveling from the Ob River.
- **1600s** — Russian Cossacks build Fort Mangazeya near the Taz River, leading to contact with Samoyed peoples and adoption of the name "Yenisei."
- **1864** — The first river steamers arrive on the Yenisei, brought from the Netherlands and the UK.
- **1876-1881** — Early steamship exploration and supply runs are established, including the wreck of the *Thames*.
- **1893** — Captain Joseph Wiggins sails the *Orestes* with rail for the Trans-Siberian Railway construction.
- **1913** — Explorer Fridtjof Nansen visits Nosonovskij Ostrov in the Yenisey estuary.
- **1985** — A canal inclined plane is built at the Krasnoyarsk Dam.
- **2001** — The first recreation team completes a navigation of the Yenisey's entire length.
- **2025** — A ship accident causes a major diesel fuel spill along the river in Krasnoyarsk Krai.

## Body

### Etymology and Names
The river's earliest recorded name is "Kem," appearing in 7th-century Chinese texts. This term is preserved in modern Sayan Turkic languages like Tuvan (*xem*). The name "Yenisei" was adopted by Russians in the 17th century, likely from Samoyed-speaking peoples. Its exact etymology is unclear, with various speculative proposals from linguists like Max Vasmer and V. K. Nikonov remaining unconfirmed.

### Geography and Hydrology
The Yenisei proper, from the confluence of the Great and Little Yenisey at Kyzyl to its mouth, is 3,487 km long. Its total length from the source of the Selenga tributary is 5,075 km. It drains a basin of 2,580,000 km². A significant feature is Lake Baikal, the world's deepest and oldest lake, on the Upper Yenisei. The river's delta lies between the Gyda and Taymyr Peninsulas.

### Ecology and Wildlife
The Yenisey basin hosts 55 native fish species, including two endemics: *Gobio sibiricus* and the grayling *Thymallus nigrescens*. Notable species include the Siberian sturgeon. The valley's flora features Siberian pine and larch. The Taimyr reindeer herd, the world's largest, migrates along the Yenisey for winter grazing.

### Navigation and History
Nomadic tribes like the Ket and Yugh have lived along the river since ancient times. The Yeniseian language family originated here. Early Turkic stelae from the 7th-9th centuries AD are found in the valley. Russian exploration began in 1605. Steam navigation started in 1864 but proved difficult due to the river's long, shallow, ice-bound estuaries. Traffic declined after the Trans-Siberian Railway's completion. During WWII, the Yenisey was part of a proposed Nazi-Japanese division line for Asia.

### Pollution
The river suffers from radioactive contamination from the former plutonium production facility at Krasnoyarsk-26 (now Zheleznogorsk). A significant diesel fuel spill of about 30 metric tons occurred in June 2025.

## Terms
- **Yeniseian language family**: A language family historically spoken by indigenous peoples along the Yenisei River.
- **Drainage basin**: The area of land where all water drains into a common outlet, like a river.
- **Endemic**: A species native and restricted to a certain place.
- **Trans-Siberian Railway**: A network of railways connecting Moscow with the Russian Far East.
- **Samoyed**: A group of Uralic languages spoken in Siberia.
- **Tundra**: A biome where tree growth is hindered by low temperatures and short growing seasons.

## Debates and Open Questions
The precise etymology of "Yenisei" remains unclear and debated. While several linguistic proposals exist, such as derivation from Samoyed or Turkic words, researchers note that many are speculative and not supported by reliable contemporary linguistic sources.
