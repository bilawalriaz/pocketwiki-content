# Great Purge

## Overview
The Great Purge (1936–1938) was a campaign of political repression in the Soviet Union orchestrated by Joseph Stalin. It involved mass arrests, show trials, executions, and imprisonment in Gulag labor camps, targeting perceived enemies of the state, including Communist Party officials, military leaders, ethnic minorities, and ordinary citizens. The purge was a pivotal event in Stalin's consolidation of absolute power, with devastating consequences for Soviet society and military readiness on the eve of World War II.

## Timeline
- **1924** — Death of Vladimir Lenin creates a power vacuum in the Communist Party.
- **1928** — Joseph Stalin gains full control of the party after defeating opponents.
- **1934** — Sergei Kirov is assassinated, providing a pretext for the purges.
- **Aug 1936** — First Moscow Trial begins, targeting Zinoviev and Kamenev.
- **Jan 1937** — Second Moscow Trial targets lesser figures like Radek and Piatakov.
- **Jun 1937** — Secret military tribunal purges Red Army commanders, including Tukhachevsky.
- **Jul 1937** — NKVD Order No. 00447 launches mass operations against "ex-kulaks" and "anti-Soviet elements."
- **Mar 1938** — Third Moscow Trial (Trial of the Twenty-One) includes Bukharin and Yagoda.
- **Sep 1936–Aug 1938** — Peak of the purge under NKVD chief Nikolai Yezhov (the "Yezhovshchina").
- **Nov 1938** — Stalin halts mass operations; Yezhov is replaced by Lavrentiy Beria.
- **Feb 1956** — Nikita Khrushchev delivers his "Secret Speech," denouncing Stalin's purges.

## Body

### Background and Causes
After Lenin's death, Stalin consolidated power by 1928, but his leadership faced criticism in the early 1930s due to the human cost of forced collectivization and industrialization. Fearing internal "wreckers" and a potential "fifth column" amid threats from Nazi Germany and Japan, Stalin sought to eliminate all opposition. The assassination of popular official Sergei Kirov in 1934 served as the immediate catalyst, with Stalin using it to launch investigations that uncovered alleged conspiracies among his rivals.

### The Moscow Trials and Military Purge
The purges were publicly showcased through three Moscow Trials (1936–1938), where former Bolshevik leaders like Zinoviev, Kamenev, and Bukharin were accused of treason and conspiracy. Confessions, obtained through torture and threats to families, were the primary evidence. Simultaneously, the Red Army high command was decimated; three of five marshals and most senior officers were purged, severely weakening Soviet military capability. The trials also consumed their own architects, as former NKVD chief Yagoda was tried and executed in 1938.

### Mass Operations and Nationality Campaigns
Beyond the elite, the NKVD conducted mass repressions under orders like NKVD Order No. 00447, targeting "ex-kulaks," former officials, and "socially harmful elements." These operations used a quota system and extrajudicial NKVD troikas. Ethnic minorities, particularly Poles, Germans, and Ukrainians, were singled out in "national operations," accounting for 36% of purge victims despite being 1.6% of the population. The Polish Operation alone resulted in over 111,000 executions.

### End and Aftermath
Stalin reversed course in late 1938, criticizing NKVD excesses and executing Yezhov. However, surveillance and mistrust persisted. The purges resulted in an estimated 700,000 to 1.2 million deaths. After Stalin's death, Khrushchev's 1956 speech exposed the falsity of the trials, leading to the gradual rehabilitation of many victims. The legacy includes mass graves discovered across the former USSR and ongoing historical debate about Stalin's role and the purge's motivations.

## Terms
- **NKVD (People's Commissariat for Internal Affairs):** The Soviet interior ministry and secret police responsible for carrying out the purges.
- **Show Trials:** Public, staged judicial proceedings where defendants were forced to confess to fabricated crimes, often under torture.
- **Gulag:** The Soviet system of forced-labor camps where millions of prisoners, including purge victims, were sent.
- **Troikas:** Three-person NKVD commissions that issued extrajudicial sentences, often death or Gulag terms, during mass operations.
- **Wreckers (or "diversionists"):** A term for alleged saboteurs and counter-revolutionaries, used to justify arrests.
- **Kulaks:** Wealthy peasants, a primary target during collectivization and later the purges.
- **Article 58:** A section of the Soviet penal code defining "counter-revolutionary crimes," used to prosecute purge victims.
- **Rehabilitation:** The post-Stalin process of officially exonerating those unjustly convicted during the purges.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Stalin's Role:** Historians debate whether Stalin was a master planner of the terror or if he reacted to developments and was misled by subordinates like Yezhov. Archival evidence confirms his direct involvement in signing execution lists.
- **Motivations:** Interpretations range from Stalin's paranoia and desire to eliminate all opposition, to an exaggerated fear of counter-revolution and foreign invasion, to the purge being a tool for social engineering.
- **Scope and Scale:** While official figures document over 680,000 executions in 1937–38, total deaths including Gulag mortality range from 700,000 to 1.2 million. The targeting of ethnic minorities has led some scholars to label parts of the purge as genocidal.
- **Historiographical Schools:** The "Totalitarian School" views the purge as a tool of absolute state control, while "Revisionists" argue it was driven by elite factionalism and that state control was not omnipresent.
