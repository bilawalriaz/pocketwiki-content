# Global North and Global South

## Overview
The terms "Global North" and "Global South" are a socioeconomic classification grouping countries based on development, wealth, and political power, rather than strict geography. The Global North generally refers to developed, industrialized nations, while the Global South encompasses developing or least developed countries. This framework is used to analyze global inequality, historical colonial relationships, and contemporary geopolitical dynamics, though its boundaries and utility are subjects of ongoing scholarly debate.

## Body

### Definition and Characteristics
The Global North and South are defined by socioeconomic factors like wealth, industrialization, and political freedom, not latitude. According to UNCTAD, the Global North includes North America, Europe, Israel, Japan, South Korea, Australia, and New Zealand. The Global South includes Africa, Latin America, the Caribbean, Asia (excluding the aforementioned), and Oceania (excluding Australia and New Zealand). Countries in the Global South are typically characterized by lower incomes, higher poverty, inadequate infrastructure, and economies reliant on primary sectors like agriculture. The Global North is wealthier, with diversified economies exporting advanced manufactured goods.

### Historical Development of the Terms
The classification emerged as a successor to Cold War-era terms like "Third World," which originated in 1952. The term "Global South" gained traction in the late 20th century as a less hierarchical alternative. Its use accelerated in the 21st century, appearing in hundreds of publications by 2013. The Brandt Line, proposed in the 1980s, visually depicted this divide at approximately 30° North latitude. The concept is linked to "South–South cooperation" (SSC), where Global South countries collaborate politically and economically to challenge Northern dominance, a trend boosted by the migration of manufacturing from North to South.

### Theories Explaining the Divide
Several theories explain the persistent inequality. **Dependency theory** argues that colonial relationships created a core-periphery structure where the South remains economically dependent on the North, exporting raw materials and importing manufactured goods. **Modernization theory** posited that developing societies could achieve growth by adopting Western technology and institutions. In contrast, **post-development theory** critiques this model, advocating for locally-defined development paths independent of Western paradigms. **New Economic Geography** attributes the divide to the clustering of industries in the North, which benefits from economies of scale.

### Challenges and Critiques
The accuracy of the North-South binary is challenged by globalization and the rise of major Southern economies like China and India. Some scholars, such as Jason Hickel, argue that global inequality has actually increased since globalization, with the North extracting trillions in value from the South. The term itself is criticized as a vague blanket that obscures significant wealth differences within countries and may primarily benefit Southern elites. An alternative, geographically fluid definition sees the "Global South" as any space or people negatively impacted by capitalist globalization, regardless of location.

## Terms
- **Global North:** A socioeconomic grouping of generally wealthier, industrialized, and democratic countries, including North America, Europe, and parts of Asia and Oceania.
- **Global South:** A socioeconomic grouping of generally developing or least developed countries, often with histories of colonialism, located in Africa, Latin America, Asia, and Oceania.
- **South–South cooperation (SSC):** Political and economic collaboration among Global South countries to address development challenges and reduce dependence on the Global North.
- **Brandt Line:** A proposed boundary at ~30°N latitude that visually separates the "rich North" from the "poor South," used to illustrate global inequality.
- **Dependency theory:** A theory explaining underdevelopment as a result of exploitative historical and economic relationships between colonizing "core" nations and colonized "peripheral" nations.
- **Structural adjustment:** Economic policies promoted by the IMF and World Bank in the 1980s-90s, requiring fiscal austerity and liberalization in exchange for loans, often criticized for harming social services.
- **Post-development theory:** A perspective arguing that Western models of development are inappropriate and that societies should pursue their own culturally specific paths to well-being.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Is the divide narrowing or widening?** Some argue globalization has allowed Southern economies to catch up, while others contend that the North extracts more value than ever, increasing inequality.
- **Is the term "Global South" useful or problematic?** Critics say it is a vague, monolithic label that obscures internal inequalities and may benefit elites. Proponents see it as a valuable tool for political solidarity and resisting Northern hegemony.
- **How should the boundaries be defined?** The classification is debated, with some arguing Australia and New Zealand are marginalized like Southern nations, and others proposing a fluid definition based on impact from global capitalism rather than nationality.
- **What is the future of the divide?** Debates continue on whether free trade will reduce the gap, whether middle-income countries are trapped, and how institutions like the UN should be reformed to better represent the Global South.
