# Aria

## Overview
An aria is a self-contained piece for a single voice, typically with orchestral accompaniment, that serves as a vehicle for emotional expression and vocal virtuosity within larger works like opera, oratorio, and cantata. It evolved from simple melodies into highly structured forms that became the central dramatic and musical element of opera for centuries, reflecting changing aesthetic ideals about the balance between music, drama, and text.

## Timeline
- **14th century** — The Italian term "aria" first appears in music, meaning a style of singing or playing.
- **Late 16th century** — The term begins to refer to an instrumental form.
- **Late 17th century** — Operatic arias solidify into binary (A–B) and ternary (da capo, A–B–A) forms.
- **Early 18th century** — Composers like Alessandro Scarlatti establish the da capo aria with ritornelli as the key element of *opera seria*.
- **c. 1720–1760** — The standard aria form in *opera seria* becomes highly formalized in structure and allocation to characters.
- **Mid-18th century** — Christoph Willibald Gluck initiates reforms to return opera to dramatic focus, reducing the aria's dominance.
- **Early 19th century** — Bravura arias remain central in Italian opera; the cabaletta form becomes popular.
- **After 1850** — Giuseppe Verdi's operas feature more extended, dramatically integrated arias.
- **Post-1850** — Wagner's through-composed operas contain fewer identifiable self-contained arias.

## Body

### Etymology and Early Use
The term "aria" derives from the Greek and Latin words for "air." By the early 16th century, it commonly meant a simple, strophic song setting, as seen in *madrigale arioso*. Initially, it referred broadly to any expressive melody, instrumental or vocal.

### Aria Form in Opera
In staged works, arias evolved from simple melodies into structured forms distinct from speech-like recitative, which carried the plot. By the late 17th century, two main forms dominated: binary (A–B) and ternary da capo (A–B–A). In Italian opera, the da capo aria incorporated a ritornello—a recurring instrumental episode that framed the vocal sections and became a defining feature of 18th-century opera. French Baroque opera developed its own forms, including extended binary arias and the rondeau (ABACA). The French *ariette*, influenced by Italian *opera seria*, was a fully developed da capo aria used in divertissements, while shorter *airs* were embedded in recitative.

### The 18th-Century *Opera Seria* Aria
By the early 1700s, the da capo aria with ritornelli was the cornerstone of *opera seria*. Its regular structure—opening ritornello, vocal section modulating to the dominant, a second ritornello, a more elaborate vocal repeat, and a final ritornello—provided balance and contrast. Aria types were categorized by character (e.g., *aria parlante* for narrative, *aria di bravura* for heroines, *aria buffa* for comic roles). The allocation of arias to characters was highly formalized, with principal roles receiving five arias each, distributed to avoid consecutive similar moods. This convention, however, led to fossilization, prompting Gluck's reforms to prioritize dramatic truth over vocal display.

### 19th-Century Developments and Reform
Despite Gluck's ideals, bravura arias remained focal in early 19th-century Italian opera (e.g., Rossini, Donizetti). The cabaletta—a two-part aria with a lyrical section followed by an animated one—became a favored form. Later, composers like Verdi wrote more extended, dramatically integrated arias that advanced characterization. Wagner's through-composed operas largely dissolved the traditional aria into a continuous musical texture, seeking to unify music and drama completely.

### Concert and Instrumental Aria
Concert arias are standalone pieces written for vocal display, not part of a larger work (e.g., Beethoven's *Ah! perfido*). The term was also used in the 17th and 18th centuries for instrumental music modeled on vocal forms, such as Bach's "Goldberg Variations," subtitled "Aria with diverse variations."

## Terms
- **Aria**: A self-contained piece for one voice, usually with accompaniment, expressing emotion or character.
- **Recitative**: Speech-like vocal style in opera that advances the plot, contrasting with the melodic aria.
- **Da capo aria**: A ternary (A–B–A) aria form where the opening section is repeated, often with ornamentation.
- **Ritornello**: A recurring instrumental episode in an aria that frames vocal sections.
- **Opera seria**: A serious, heroic genre of opera dominant in the 18th century, heavily reliant on formal arias.
- **Opera buffa**: Comic opera featuring arias that reflect character types (e.g., servants, elderly suitors).
- **Cabaletta**: A two-part aria form with a lyrical *cantabile* section followed by a more animated section.
- **Ariette**: In French Baroque opera, a fully developed da capo aria used in divertissements.
- **Through-composed**: Music that is continuously varied without repeated sections, as in Wagner's later operas.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the da capo aria with ritornelli is "thought by some writers to be a direct antecedent of sonata form," indicating a scholarly debate about its structural legacy. The extent to which Gluck's reforms successfully rebalanced music and drama, and the precise nature of the transition from aria-dominated to through-composed opera, are also implied areas of historical interpretation.
