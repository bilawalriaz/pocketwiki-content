# Ludwig Wittgenstein

## Overview
Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951) was an Austro-British philosopher whose work in logic, philosophy of language, and philosophy of mind fundamentally shaped 20th-century analytic philosophy. Born into one of Europe’s wealthiest families in Vienna, he studied engineering before turning to philosophy under Bertrand Russell at Cambridge. His early masterpiece, the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921), argued that language mirrors the logical structure of the world and that philosophical problems arise from misunderstanding this logic; he famously concluded that "whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." After a decade of schoolteaching and architectural work, he returned to Cambridge and developed a radically different later philosophy, published posthumously as *Philosophical Investigations* (1953). This later work rejected the *Tractatus*’s picture theory of meaning, arguing instead that meaning is determined by use within diverse "language games" embedded in forms of life. Wittgenstein’s life was marked by intense personal suffering, including the suicides of three brothers, his own depression, wartime service, and a controversial tenure as a schoolteacher involving corporal punishment. He is widely regarded as one of the most important and influential philosophers of the modern era.

## Body

### Family Background and Early Life
Wittgenstein was born into the Wittgenstein family, one of the wealthiest in the Austro-Hungarian Empire. His paternal great-great-grandfather, Moses Meier, adopted the surname Wittgenstein in 1808. His father, Karl Otto Clemens Wittgenstein, dominated Austria’s steel cartel and was a major patron of the arts (commissioning Rodin, financing the Secession Building). The family palace hosted Brahms, Mahler, and Klimt. Wittgenstein was the youngest of nine children; three of his four brothers committed suicide (Hans in 1902, Rudi in 1904, Kurt in 1918). The family atmosphere was described as intense, perfectionist, and culturally saturated but emotionally fraught. Wittgenstein was educated at home until 1903, when he entered the Realschule in Linz. There he stood out for his pure High German, stutter, elegance, and sensitivity. He lost his religious faith around this time but retained a lifelong, non-dogmatic engagement with Christianity, influenced by Schopenhauer (later rejected), Kierkegaard, Dostoevsky, and Tolstoy. He was also influenced by Otto Weininger’s *Sex and Character* (1903), attending Weininger’s funeral; Wittgenstein later called Weininger’s errors "great" because negating them yielded important truths.

### Engineering, Mathematics, and Turn to Philosophy
Wittgenstein studied mechanical engineering in Berlin (1906–1908) and aeronautics at Manchester (1908–1911), patenting a tip-jet propeller design. Frustrated by practical engineering, he turned to the foundations of mathematics after reading Russell’s *Principles of Mathematics* and Frege’s *Basic Laws of Arithmetic*. He visited Frege at Jena in 1911; Frege "wiped the floor" with him but invited him back. Frege directed him to Russell at Cambridge. Wittgenstein arrived unannounced at Trinity College in October 1911 and quickly dominated Russell’s lectures. Russell soon recognized his genius, writing that Wittgenstein would solve problems he was too old to solve. Wittgenstein joined the Moral Sciences Club and the Cambridge Apostles but found both discussion styles intolerable. He suffered severe depression, telling Russell he felt the "curse of those who have half a talent" and that logic drove him mad. He formed a close intellectual and romantic relationship with David Pinsent (1912–1913), with whom he conducted psychology experiments and travelled to Iceland and Norway.

### World War I and the *Tractatus*
In 1913, after inheriting his fortune, Wittgenstein retreated to Skjolden, Norway, where he worked on *Notes on Logic*, seeking a general decision procedure for logical truth. He built a remote hut ("Østerrike") and lived there intermittently until the 1930s. At the outbreak of WWI, he volunteered for the Austro-Hungarian Army despite eligibility for exemption. He served on a ship, in an artillery workshop, and on the Russian front (Brusilov Offensive), directing fire from an observation post in no-man’s-land. He was decorated repeatedly for "exceptionally courageous behaviour." In 1917–1918 he served on the Italian front. Throughout the war he kept philosophical notebooks, experiencing a religious awakening: "To believe in God means to see that life has a meaning." He carried Tolstoy’s *The Gospel in Brief* constantly, influencing the *Tractatus*’s numbered structure. He completed the *Tractatus* in August 1918 while on leave. Shortly after, his uncle died, his brother Kurt suicided, and he learned of David Pinsent’s death in a plane crash. He returned to the front, was captured in November 1918, and spent nine months in an Italian POW camp.

### Schoolteaching, Publication, and the Vienna Circle
Physically and mentally exhausted, Wittgenstein gave away his fortune to his siblings (insisting it not be held in trust) and trained as an elementary teacher (1919). He taught in remote Austrian villages (Trattenbach, Hassbach, Puchberg, Otterthal) under his real name. He was an enthusiastic but severe teacher: he caned boys and boxed girls’ ears for mathematical errors, pulled girls’ hair, and shouted at the headmaster and priest. In April 1926 (the Haidbauer incident), he struck an 11-year-old slow learner, Josef Haidbauer, causing collapse; he fled the school, resigned, and faced psychiatric evaluation. Ten years later he returned to apologize to former pupils as a "confession" to dismantle his pride.

The *Tractatus* was published in German in 1921 (in Ostwald’s *Annalen*) and in English in 1922 (Kegan Paul, with Russell’s introduction and Ramsey–Ogden translation). Its seven propositions argue that the world is the totality of facts, propositions are pictures of facts, the general form of a proposition is a truth-function of elementary propositions, and the limits of language are the limits of philosophy. Ethics, aesthetics, and the mystical cannot be said but only shown. The Vienna Circle (Schlick, Waismann, Carnap, Feigl) studied the *Tractatus* intensively (1927–1932), but Wittgenstein was never a member and his influence was limited to Schlick and Waismann; Carnap noted Wittgenstein’s attitude was more like a "creative artist" or "religious prophet" than a scientist. In 1926–1928 Wittgenstein designed Haus Wittgenstein for his sister Margaret, obsessing over windows, doors, radiators, and proportions.

### Return to Cambridge and Later Philosophy
Urged by Ramsey and Keynes, Wittgenstein returned to Cambridge in 1929. He submitted the *Tractatus* as a PhD thesis (examined by Russell and Moore; Moore called it "a work of genius") and became a Fellow of Trinity. He lectured from 1930, developing the ideas that became *Philosophical Investigations*. He resumed attending the Moral Sciences Club but often dominated it. In 1936–1937 he worked in Norway on the *Investigations* and delivered "confessions" to friends. The 1938 Anschluss classified the Wittgenstein siblings as Jews (three Jewish grandparents); they negotiated Mischling status from Hitler using family foreign assets (1,700 kg gold), causing a permanent split with brother Paul. Wittgenstein became Professor of Philosophy in 1939 (naturalised British April 1939). His lectures were intense, original research sessions with long silences and emphatic gestures. He discussed mathematics with Alan Turing and taught Helle Lambridis. He denied mathematical facts exist, reversing his early view.

### War Work and Final Cambridge Years
Intolerant of teaching philosophy during WWII, Wittgenstein worked as a dispensary porter at Guy’s Hospital, London (1941–1942), then as a lab assistant at the Royal Victoria Infirmary, Newcastle (1943–1944), studying wound shock. He befriended Keith Kirk and met Erasmus Barlow. He returned to Cambridge in 1944; relations with Russell were cold (Russell dismissed his later work; Wittgenstein said Russell’s ethical/political works should be bound in blue and not read). He resigned his chair in 1947. In 1946 he clashed with Karl Popper at the Moral Sciences Club, reportedly waving a poker and demanding an example of a moral rule; Popper offered "Not to threaten visiting speakers with pokers."

### Final Years and Death
Wittgenstein spent 1947–1949 in Ireland (Ross’s Hotel, Redcross, Rosroe) and 1949 in the US (Ithaca, with Norman Malcolm), where he began the epistemological work published as *On Certainty*. Diagnosed with inoperable prostate cancer in 1949, he moved between Vienna, Cambridge, London, Oxford, and Norway. His sister Hermine died in 1950. In early 1951 he wrote a new will (executor Rush Rhees; literary administrators Anscombe and von Wright). In April 1951 he experienced a sudden return of philosophical capacity ("the curtain in my brain has gone up"). He worked on MS 177 until 27 April, fell ill, and died 29 April 1951. His last words: "Tell them I've had a wonderful life." He received a Catholic burial, though he did not consider himself Catholic; he viewed Catholicism as a way of life.

### Posthumous Work and Legacy
*Philosophical Investigations* (1953) presents language as a multiplicity of "language games"; meaning is use. Philosophical problems arise when language "goes on holiday" into a metaphysical "frictionless ice" where it cannot work; philosophers must return to the "rough ground" of ordinary use to dissolve problems. The *Blue Book* (1933–34 dictations) marks the transition. The Nachlass comprises ~20,000 pages; editorial work has published *Philosophical Remarks*, *Philosophical Grammar*, *Remarks on Colour*, *On Certainty*, and others.

Wittgenstein’s legacy is immense but contested. Russell called him the "most perfect example of genius." The *Investigations* was ranked the most important 20th-century philosophy book by US philosophers (1999). Interpretations divide: "New Wittgenstein" (Diamond, Crary, Conant) reads the *Tractatus* therapeutically as "plain nonsense" aiming to cure philosophical confusion; critics (Glock) say this ignores Wittgenstein’s own statements about ineffable insight. Kripke’s *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982) posits a rule-following paradox; defenders (Kusch) and critics (McDowell, Hacker, Baker) dispute its fidelity. Dennett noted the *Tractatus* stays on reading lists for its "tantalizing perplexity."

## Terms
- **Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus**: Wittgenstein’s 1921 early work arguing that propositions picture facts via shared logical form; the limits of language mark the limits of philosophy; ethics and the mystical are shown, not said.
- **Philosophical Investigations**: Posthumous 1953 work rejecting the *Tractatus*; argues meaning is use within "language games" embedded in forms of life; philosophy dissolves problems by describing ordinary language.
- **Language game**: A rule-governed, context-dependent use of language within a specific form of life; meaning derives from participation in the game, not from reference to objects.
- **Picture theory of meaning**: Early view (in *Tractatus*) that a proposition is a logical picture of a state of affairs; its structure mirrors the world’s structure.
- **Form of life**: The shared human practices, customs, and institutions that give language games their context and intelligibility.
- **Language gone on holiday**: Wittgenstein’s metaphor for philosophical language detached from its ordinary use, spinning idle on "frictionless ice."
- **Rule-following paradox**: Kripke’s interpretation of *Investigations* §201: no course of action can be determined by a rule, because any action can be made to accord with the rule.
- **Mischling status**: Nazi racial classification for "mixed blood" (1–2 Jewish grandparents); the Wittgenstein siblings (3 Jewish grandparents) obtained this rare exemption from Hitler in 1939 using family assets.
- **Haidbauer incident**: 1926 episode where Wittgenstein struck a slow-learning pupil, Josef Haidbauer, causing collapse; led to resignation and later public apologies.
- **Nachlass**: Wittgenstein’s unpublished manuscripts, typescripts, and dictations (~20,000 pages), edited posthumously by Anscombe, von Wright, and Rhees.

## Debates and open questions
- **Continuity vs. rupture**: Scholars dispute whether the later philosophy radically breaks with the *Tractatus* or develops it gradually (via the *Blue Book* and intermediate manuscripts).
- **New Wittgenstein / therapeutic reading**: Does the *Tractatus* advance a metaphysical theory (ineffable insights) or is it "plain nonsense" intended to therapeutically dissolve the urge to philosophize? Critics argue the therapeutic reading contradicts Wittgenstein’s own letters and conversations.
- **Kripke’s rule-following scepticism**: Is the paradox in *Investigations* a genuine sceptical problem about meaning (Kripke) or a pseudo-problem from misreading (McDowell, Hacker, Baker)?
- **Religious stance**: Wittgenstein expressed sincere religious sympathy, read Christian texts devotionally, and desired a Catholic burial, yet rejected dogma and institutional religion. Was he a believer, an agnostic, or a "cultural" religious thinker?
- **Influence on Vienna Circle**: Did the *Tractatus* inspire logical positivism, or did the Circle appropriate it for pre-existing empiricist views? Carnap and Schlick were deeply impressed; Grayling argues influence was limited.
- **Ethics of his teaching**: The Haidbauer incident and routine corporal punishment of girls remain a stain; his 1936 apologies were framed as philosophical "confessions" to dismantle pride—does this mitigate or compound the moral issue?
