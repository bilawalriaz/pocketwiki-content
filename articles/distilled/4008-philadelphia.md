# Philadelphia

## Overview
Philadelphia is Pennsylvania's largest city and the sixth-most populous in the U.S., with a 2020 population of 1.60 million. Founded in 1682 by William Penn, it served as the nation's capital and a central stage for the American Revolution, hosting the First and Second Continental Congresses and the Constitutional Convention. Today, it is a major economic, cultural, and educational hub known for its historical landmarks, diverse neighborhoods, and influential role in American music and sports.

## Timeline
- **1682** — William Penn founds Philadelphia as the capital of the Province of Pennsylvania.
- **1774** — Hosts the First Continental Congress at Carpenters' Hall.
- **1776** — The Second Continental Congress adopts the Declaration of Independence in Independence Hall.
- **1777-1778** — British Army occupies Philadelphia for nine months during the Revolutionary War.
- **1787** — The U.S. Constitution is ratified at the Philadelphia Convention.
- **1790** — Philadelphia is superseded as the nation's largest city by New York City.
- **1854** — The Act of Consolidation expands the city limits to encompass all of Philadelphia County.
- **1950** — City population peaks at over 2 million residents.
- **1985** — Philadelphia Police Department bombing of the MOVE compound destroys 61 homes.
- **2007** — City population begins to grow again after decades of decline.
- **2018** — Comcast Technology Center is completed, becoming the tallest building in the U.S. outside Manhattan and Chicago.

## Body

### Founding and Colonial Era
Before European settlement, the Lenape (Delaware Indians) inhabited the region. William Penn received a charter from Charles II in 1681 and founded Philadelphia in 1682 as a "Greene Countrie Towne" and haven for religious freedom, naming it from the Greek for "brotherly love." Despite Penn's plan for a spread-out town, residents crowded the Delaware River port. The city grew into a major trading center, with Benjamin Franklin instrumental in establishing early civic institutions like a library, hospital, and fire company.

### American Revolution and Early Nationhood
Philadelphia's central location and economic importance made it the natural seat of the American Revolution. It hosted the First Continental Congress in 1774 and the Second Continental Congress, which adopted the Declaration of Independence in 1776. During the Revolutionary War, the city was occupied by the British from September 1777 to June 1778. Following the war, the U.S. Constitution was ratified there in 1787. Philadelphia served as the nation's capital on five occasions between 1776 and 1800, when the federal government moved to Washington, D.C.

### 19th and 20th Centuries
The 19th century saw Philadelphia become the nation's first major industrial city, with textiles and railroads leading its economy. Immigrants from Ireland, Germany, and later Eastern Europe and Italy fueled population growth, leading to the 1854 consolidation of the city and county. The African-American population grew significantly during the Great Migration. The city's population peaked in 1950 but then declined due to suburbanization and industrial restructuring, leading to economic struggles and a near-bankruptcy in the 1980s. Revitalization and gentrification began in the late 20th century, reversing population decline by the 2000s.

### Economy and Transportation
Philadelphia's economy is driven by financial services, healthcare, biotechnology, and education. It is a top U.S. venture capital hub, aided by proximity to New York City and Washington, D.C. The Philadelphia Stock Exchange (owned by Nasdaq) is the nation's oldest. The city is a major transportation node: 30th Street Station is the third-busiest Amtrak hub, and Philadelphia International Airport is a primary transatlantic gateway for American Airlines. The Port of Philadelphia (PhilaPort) and Interstate 95 are critical logistics infrastructure.

### Culture and Demographics
Philadelphia is a city of "firsts," including the first U.S. library (1731), hospital (1751), and zoo (1874). It has 67 National Historic Landmarks, with Independence National Historical Park as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The city is known for its cuisine (cheesesteaks, hoagies), the Mummers Parade, and its influential music scene, particularly in soul, R&B, and rock. Demographically, it is diverse, with large Black, White, Hispanic, and Asian populations. The city has a significant foreign-born population and is home to the nation's fifth-largest Muslim population.

### Government and Politics
Philadelphia operates under a strong-mayor form of government. Since 1952, every mayor has been a Democrat. The city council consists of district and at-large members. Philadelphia is a Democratic stronghold in presidential elections, consistently giving over 80% of its vote to the party's candidates since 2008. The city's political structure includes a system of wards and divisions.

## Terms
- **Lenape (Delaware Indians):** The Native American tribe that originally inhabited the Delaware Valley region before European colonization.
- **Consolidation Act of 1854:** Legislation that merged the City of Philadelphia with the surrounding Philadelphia County, greatly expanding the city's boundaries.
- **Gentrification:** The process of renovating and improving a neighborhood so that it conforms to middle-class taste, often displacing lower-income residents.
- **Strong-mayor government:** A system where the mayor has significant executive authority, including appointing department heads without city council approval.
- **Percent for Art ordinance:** A 1959 Philadelphia policy mandating that one percent of the funds for the construction or renovation of city-owned buildings be spent on public art.
- **Mural Arts Program:** A 1984 city initiative that commissions public murals to beautify neighborhoods and provide an outlet for graffiti artists.
- **SEPTA:** The Southeastern Pennsylvania Transportation Authority, which operates public transit (buses, trains, subways, trolleys) in the Philadelphia region.
- **PhilaPort:** The port authority and operator of the Port of Philadelphia, a major shipping facility on the Delaware River.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The origin of the city's name is derived from Greek, but other historical cities named Philadelphia leave the precise inspiration for Penn's choice as a matter of historical interpretation.
- The city's long population decline from 1950 to 2006 and its subsequent reversal are documented, but the long-term sustainability of this growth amid challenges like affordable housing shortages remains an open question.
- The effectiveness of the "Green Cities, Clean Water" environmental policy is noted as promising but insufficient, with researchers stating the city must expand plans to adequately mitigate climate change impacts like increased runoff and temperatures.
