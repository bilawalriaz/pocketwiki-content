# Xerxes I

## Overview
Xerxes I (c. 518–465 BC) was the fourth King of Kings of the Achaemenid Persian Empire, succeeding his father Darius the Great. He is most famous in Western history for leading a massive invasion of Greece in 480 BC, which ended in a decisive Persian defeat. His reign marked the height of Achaemenid power but also saw significant revolts and the consolidation of imperial control through military and administrative action.

## Timeline
- **c. 518 BC** — Xerxes is born to Darius the Great and Atossa, daughter of Cyrus the Great.
- **486 BC** — Darius dies; Xerxes is crowned king after defeating a succession challenge from his elder brother Artobazan.
- **484 BC** — Xerxes suppresses a revolt in Egypt and appoints his brother Achaemenes as satrap.
- **484–482 BC** — Babylon revolts twice against Persian rule.
- **483–480 BC** — Xerxes prepares for the invasion of Greece, including digging the Xerxes Canal and building pontoon bridges.
- **480 BC** — Persian forces win at Thermopylae and Artemisium, capture and burn Athens, but are defeated by the Greek fleet at Salamis.
- **479 BC** — The Persian land army under Mardonius is defeated at Plataea, ending the invasion.
- **465 BC** — Xerxes is assassinated by the commander of his bodyguard, Artabanus.

## Body

### Accession and Consolidation of Power
Xerxes was chosen by Darius as successor over his elder brother Artobazan, partly due to his mother Atossa's royal lineage as Cyrus the Great's daughter. Upon Darius's death in 486 BC, Xerxes faced immediate challenges. He personally led a campaign to crush a revolt in Egypt in 484 BC. Babylon also revolted twice, in 484 BC and 482 BC, led by rebels Bel-shimanni and Shamash-eriba. After suppressing these uprisings, Xerxes divided the large Babylonian satrapy and dropped the title "King of Babylon" from his titulature. Ancient sources claim he destroyed Babylon's temples and removed the statue of the god Marduk, though modern scholars debate the extent of this vengeance.

### The Greek Campaign
Xerxes inherited his father's plan to punish Athens and its allies for their role in the Ionian Revolt. After four years of preparation, he led a vast multi-ethnic army and navy into Europe in 480 BC. The Persians won the Battle of Thermopylae after a Greek betrayal and fought the Greeks to a stalemate at Artemisium. They then captured and burned Athens. However, the Greek fleet won a pivotal victory at the Battle of Salamis. Fearing entrapment in Europe, Xerxes retreated to Asia with most of his army, leaving General Mardonius to continue the campaign. Mardonius was defeated and killed at the Battle of Plataea in 479 BC, ending the Persian invasion.

### Construction and Death
After returning from Greece, Xerxes focused on completing major building projects initiated by his father. At Persepolis, he oversaw the construction of the Gate of All Nations, the Hall of a Hundred Columns, and the completion of the Apadana and Treasury. He also built his own palace, twice the size of his father's, and maintained the Royal Road. In 465 BC, Xerxes was assassinated by Artabanus, commander of the royal bodyguard. Following his death, a succession struggle ensued, ultimately resulting in his son Artaxerxes I taking the throne.

## Terms
- **Achaemenid Empire**: The first Persian Empire, founded by Cyrus the Great, which at its height stretched from the Balkans to the Indus Valley.
- **Satrap**: A provincial governor in the Achaemenid Empire, responsible for governing a region called a satrapy.
- **Hellespont**: The narrow strait (now the Dardanelles) connecting the Aegean Sea to the Sea of Marmara, which Xerxes bridged for his invasion.
- **Immortals**: The elite 10,000-man unit of the Persian army, so named because their number was immediately replenished if a soldier died.
- **Phalanx**: A military formation of infantry standing shoulder-to-shoulder in close ranks, used by Greek hoplites.
- **Perserschutt**: The archaeological term for the destruction layer of debris left by the Persian burning of Athens in 480 BC.
- **Ahura Mazda**: The supreme deity in Zoroastrianism, whom Xerxes worshipped and credited for his victories.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Size of the Persian Army**: Ancient sources like Herodotus claim Xerxes's force was around one million strong, but modern estimates place the combatant number closer to 60,000.
- **Treatment of Babylon**: Scholars debate whether Xerxes systematically destroyed Babylonian temples and the statue of Marduk, or if these stories stem from later anti-Persian bias.
- **Role of Carthage**: Ancient sources suggest Xerxes coordinated a Carthaginian invasion of Sicily to distract Greek allies, but modern scholarship is skeptical of this alliance.
- **Historical Basis of Esther**: Xerxes is often identified with King Ahasuerus in the biblical Book of Esther, but there is no consensus on what historical event, if any, inspired the story.
