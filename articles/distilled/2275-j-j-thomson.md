# J. J. Thomson

## Overview
Sir J.J. Thomson (1856–1940) was a British physicist whose work fundamentally reshaped atomic theory. He is best known for discovering the electron in 1897, the first subatomic particle, proving atoms were divisible. He also pioneered mass spectrometry by identifying the first stable isotopes. As a long-time Cavendish Professor at Cambridge, he mentored a generation of physicists, including seven Nobel laureates.

## Timeline
- **18 Dec 1856** — Born in Cheetham Hill, Manchester.
- **1876** — Enters Trinity College, Cambridge.
- **22 Dec 1884** — Appointed Cavendish Professor of Physics at Cambridge.
- **1897** — Announces discovery of the "corpuscle" (electron) via cathode ray experiments.
- **1904** — Proposes the "plum pudding model" of the atom.
- **1906** — Awarded Nobel Prize in Physics for work on electricity conduction in gases.
- **1912** — With F.W. Aston, provides first evidence for stable isotopes using neon.
- **1918** — Becomes Master of Trinity College, Cambridge.
- **30 Aug 1940** — Dies in Cambridge; ashes interred in Westminster Abbey.

## Body

### Discovery of the Electron
Thomson's landmark work involved cathode rays, which were known to be deflected by magnetic fields. He conducted experiments in a high-vacuum tube, demonstrating the rays were also deflected by electric fields, proving they were composed of charged particles. By comparing the magnetic and electric deflections, he calculated the mass-to-charge ratio of these particles, which he called "corpuscles." He found this ratio was over 1,000 times smaller than that of a hydrogen ion, indicating the particles were far lighter than atoms. He concluded these negatively charged corpuscles were universal building blocks of atoms, which he named "electrons" (a term coined earlier by George Johnstone Stoney). This discovery proved atoms were divisible.

### The Plum Pioneering Model and Atomic Theory
To explain atomic neutrality, Thomson proposed a model in 1904: a sphere of uniform positive charge with negatively charged electrons embedded within it, like plums in a pudding. This "plum pudding model" was the first to incorporate subatomic particles into atomic structure. His work also included early studies on the electromagnetic theory of light and the concept of electromagnetic mass. He demonstrated that hydrogen atoms contained only a single electron, constraining atomic models.

### Isotopes and Mass Spectrometry
In 1912, Thomson and his assistant F.W. Aston investigated "canal rays" (positive ions). They passed neon ions through electric and magnetic fields, observing two distinct parabolic traces on a photographic plate. This indicated neon atoms had two different masses, providing the first evidence for isotopes of a stable element. This experiment was the first use of mass spectrometry, a technique later refined by Aston.

### Teaching and Legacy
Thomson was an influential teacher at Cambridge. Seven of his students and research assistants won Nobel Prizes, including Ernest Rutherford (Chemistry) and his own son, George Paget Thomson (Physics, 1937), who proved the wave-like properties of electrons. He served as Master of Trinity College from 1918 until his death.

## Terms
- **Cathode Rays:** Streams of particles emitted from the negative electrode (cathode) in a vacuum tube, later identified as electrons.
- **Electron:** The first-discovered subatomic particle, a negatively charged "corpuscle" with a very small mass and a large charge-to-mass ratio.
- **Charge-to-Mass Ratio (e/m):** The ratio of a particle's electric charge to its mass, a key property Thomson measured for cathode rays.
- **Plum Pudding Model:** Thomson's 1904 atomic model depicting electrons embedded in a uniform sphere of positive charge.
- **Isotopes:** Atoms of the same element with different atomic masses (different numbers of neutrons).
- **Mass Spectrometry:** An analytical technique that separates ions based on their mass-to-charge ratio, pioneered by Thomson and Aston.
- **Canal Rays:** Streams of positively charged ions produced in gas-discharge tubes, the subject of Thomson's isotope research.
- **Corpuscle:** The term Thomson used for the electron; he preferred it to emphasize the particle's negative charge.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nature of Cathode Rays:** Before Thomson, physicists debated whether cathode rays were waves in the "aether" or material particles. Thomson's experiments definitively showed they were negatively charged particles.
- **Atomic Structure:** Thomson's plum pudding model was the leading atomic model until it was disproven by Ernest Rutherford's gold foil experiment (1911), which revealed a dense, positively charged nucleus.
- **Terminology:** Thomson resisted the term "electron" for years, preferring "corpuscle" to avoid confusion with a hypothetical "positive electron." The name was popularized by other physicists like George FitzGerald and Hendrik Lorentz.
