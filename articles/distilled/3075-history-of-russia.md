# History of Russia

## Overview
The history of Russia spans from early human settlements to the modern Russian Federation, tracing the development of a major world power through cycles of unification, fragmentation, and expansion. It is defined by the synthesis of Slavic, Byzantine, and Scandinavian cultures, the impact of Mongol rule, the rise of autocracy, revolutionary upheaval, and the Soviet superpower era, culminating in the post-Soviet state's assertive foreign policy.

## Timeline
- **862** — Traditional start of Russian history with the establishment of the Rus' state, ruled by Varangians.
- **988** — Kievan Rus' adopts Christianity from the Byzantine Empire, shaping Russian culture for a millennium.
- **1237–1240** — Mongol invasions destroy Kievan Rus', leading to its disintegration.
- **1547** — Ivan IV (the Terrible) transforms the Grand Duchy into the Tsardom of Russia.
- **1613** — Michael Romanov is crowned, beginning the Romanov dynasty's rule until 1917.
- **1721** — Peter the Great renames the state the Russian Empire after the Great Northern War.
- **1861** — Alexander II abolishes Russian serfdom.
- **1917** — The February Revolution ends the monarchy; the October Revolution brings the Bolsheviks to power.
- **1922** — The Soviet Union is officially formed from the Russian SFSR, Ukrainian SSR, Byelorussian SSR, and Transcaucasian SFSR.
- **1941–1945** — The Soviet Union defeats Nazi Germany in World War II, suffering massive casualties.
- **1991** — The Soviet Union dissolves; the Russian Federation emerges as its primary successor state.
- **2014** — Russia annexes the Crimean Peninsula, leading to international sanctions.

## Body

### Early History and Kievan Rus'
The first East Slavic state, Rus', emerged in the 9th century along the Dnieper River, controlling a vital trade route between Scandinavia and the Byzantine Empire. In 988, Prince Vladimir I adopted Eastern Orthodox Christianity, creating a cultural synthesis of Byzantine and Slavic influences. Kievan Rus' disintegrated due to internal princely conflicts and was ultimately destroyed by the Mongol invasions of 1237–1240.

### The Rise of Moscow and the Tsardom
After the Mongol period, the Grand Duchy of Moscow emerged as the center for gathering Russian territories. Ivan III (the Great) tripled Moscow's territory, ended Mongol dominance, and laid the foundations of a centralized Russian state. His grandson, Ivan IV (the Terrible), became the first Tsar in 1547, expanding the state into a multiethnic empire but also plunging it into a reign of terror. His death without an heir led to the Time of Troubles, a period of civil war and foreign intervention, which ended with the establishment of the Romanov dynasty in 1613.

### The Russian Empire
Peter the Great (r. 1721–1725) modernized Russia, created a navy, and established St. Petersburg as a new capital, firmly placing Russia in the European state system. Catherine the Great (r. 1762–1796) continued westernization and expanded the empire southward to the Black Sea and westward through the partitions of Poland. The 19th century saw the abolition of serfdom in 1861 under Alexander II, but also growing social unrest and revolutionary movements. Russia's defeat in the Crimean War (1853–1856) exposed its military and administrative backwardness, prompting reforms.

### Revolution, Civil War, and the Soviet Union
Economic breakdown, military failures in World War I, and discontent with autocracy triggered the Russian Revolution of 1917. The Bolsheviks, led by Lenin, seized power in the October Revolution. After a brutal civil war, the Soviet Union was formed in 1922. Under Joseph Stalin, the USSR underwent rapid, forced industrialization and collectivization, which caused a devastating famine. Stalin's Great Purges led to mass repression. The Soviet Union emerged from World War II as a superpower, having defeated Nazi Germany at a cost of around 26–27 million lives. The Cold War (c. 1947–1991) saw the USSR and the United States compete for global influence.

### The Russian Federation
Economic stagnation and political reforms under Mikhail Gorbachev led to the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991. The Russian Federation, under Boris Yeltsin, experienced a turbulent transition to a market economy, marked by hyperinflation and the rise of oligarchs. Vladimir Putin's presidency since 2000 has been characterized by the reassertion of state control, economic growth fueled by energy exports, and an assertive foreign policy, including the 2014 annexation of Crimea and the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, which have resulted in significant international sanctions.

## Terms
- **Varangians**: Scandinavian Norsemen (Vikings) who were traders and mercenaries, and whose rulers founded the first East Slavic state.
- **Kievan Rus'**: The first East Slavic state, centered in Kiev, which controlled trade routes and adopted Orthodox Christianity.
- **Mongol Yoke**: The period of Mongol domination over the Russian principalities (c. 1240–1480), during which they paid tribute to the Golden Horde.
- **Tsar**: The title adopted by Russian monarchs from Ivan IV onward, signifying an autocratic ruler.
- **Serfdom**: A system of bound labor where peasants were tied to the land and owned by the landowner, abolished in Russia in 1861.
- **Soviet**: A council of workers' or soldiers' deputies that became the basic unit of government in the Soviet system.
- **Perestroika**: The policy of restructuring the Soviet political and economic system initiated by Mikhail Gorbachev in the mid-1980s.
- **Glasnost**: The policy of increased openness and transparency in government institutions and activities in the Soviet Union under Gorbachev.
- **Oligarchs**: A small group of business leaders who gained immense wealth and political influence in Russia during the privatization of the 1990s.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact origins of the Rus' state and the role of the Varangians versus the native Slavs remain subject to scholarly interpretation.
- The long-term impact of Mongol rule on Russian political culture and institutions is debated, with arguments ranging from significant disruption to administrative and military influence.
- The causes of the Soviet Union's collapse are contested, with emphasis variously placed on economic failure, Gorbachev's reforms, nationalist movements, or the Cold War arms race.
- The nature of Vladimir Putin's political system—whether it is a form of managed democracy, authoritarianism, or a unique hybrid—and the long-term consequences of its assertive foreign policy are ongoing subjects of analysis.
