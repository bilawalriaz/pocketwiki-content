# Clement of Rome

## Overview

Clement I, also known as Pope Clement of Rome, was the Bishop of Rome in the late 1st century (died c. 100) and is regarded as the first of the Apostolic Fathers. His sole authentic work, the First Epistle to the Corinthians (1 Clement), is one of the oldest Christian documents outside the New Testament and affirms apostolic succession by defending the authority of bishops and presbyters. His life and death are shrouded in conflicting traditions, and his writings became foundational for early church structure and theology.

## Timeline

- **c. 60** — Paul arrives in Rome; large Christian congregation exists
- **c. 96** — Clement writes 1 Clement to the Corinthian church
- **c. 100** — Clement dies, possibly in exile or martyrdom
- **c. 170** — 1 Clement is read publicly in the church at Corinth
- **4th century** — Apocryphal legends of Clement’s martyrdom emerge
- **869** — Cyril brings alleged relics of Clement from Crimea to Rome

## Body

### Life and Historical Context

Little is definitively known about Clement’s life. Early church lists vary, naming him as the second, third, or fourth bishop of Rome. Tertullian claimed he was ordained directly by Peter, while Eusebius called him the third bishop and a co-laborer of Paul. Irenaeus described him as the successor of Anacletus and a personal acquaintance of the Apostles. The *Liber Pontificalis* states that Peter appointed Clement as his successor. Modern scholars generally agree that the exact order of early Roman bishops remains uncertain, and the concept of a monarchical episcopacy in Rome at this early date is debated.

Clement likely lived during a period of significant persecution. Nero punished Christians after the Great Fire of Rome in 64, and Domitian (81–96) may have continued such efforts. The *Shepherd of Hermas*, a 2nd-century text, refers to a Clement active in communicating with other churches, possibly identifying him with Clement I. A later tradition identified him with Paul’s “fellow laborer” mentioned in Philipps 4:3, though modern scholars like Ralph P. Martin and F. F. Bruce argue this identification lacks evidence.

### Death and Legends

The earliest sources—Eusebius and Jerome—record no details of Clement’s death. However, 4th-century apocryphal accounts describe his exile under Emperor Trajan to the Chersonesus (Crimea), where he worked in a stone quarry. There, he allegedly prayed for water, struck the ground with his pickaxe, and caused a spring to flow, converting many locals. As punishment, he was martyred by being tied to an anchor and thrown into the sea. The *Liber Pontificalis* claims he died in Greece in 100, during Trajan’s reign. These stories lack historical corroboration but became central to his hagiography.

Relics attributed to Clement were later venerated in Rome and Kyiv. Cyril allegedly brought bones found in Crimea to Rome around 867, where they were enshrined in the Basilica di San Clemente. Other traditions claim his head is housed in the Kyiv Monastery of the Caves.

### Writings

#### First Epistle to the Corinthians (1 Clement)

Clement’s only authentic writing, 1 Clement, was composed around 96 AD in response to a dispute in the Corinthian church where certain presbyters had been deposed. He urged repentance and reinstatement, emphasizing obedience to church authority rooted in apostolic appointment. The letter refers to “bishops and deacons” as established by the Apostles, and uses the terms *episkopoi* (overseers) and *presbyteroi* (elders) interchangeably for the clergy above deacons. It is considered the earliest affirmation of apostolic succession and provides insight into early church governance.

The letter also contains theological reflections on justification, resurrection, and Christ as high priest. Some scholars interpret Clement as holding a faith-alone view of salvation, citing 1 Clement 32:4, while others see synergistic elements. The text was highly regarded and read alongside Scripture in Corinth by c. 170.

#### Other Attributed Works

The *Second Epistle of Clement* (2 Clement) was long attributed to Clement but is now widely considered a 2nd-century homily by another author. Similarly, the *Epistles on Virginity* and the *False Decretals* (a 9th-century collection of forged papal letters) were once ascribed to Clement but are rejected by modern scholarship. The *Clementine literature*, a pseudo-Clementine romance, portrays Clement as a descendant of Emperor Domitian and a mediator of apostolic teaching, but these works are literary constructs rather than historical sources.

## Terms

- **Apostolic Fathers**: Early Christian writers and bishops of the late 1st and early 2nd centuries, considered successors to the Apostles.
- **Apostolic Succession**: The doctrine that church authority is transmitted through an unbroken line of bishops from the Apostles.
- **Monarchical Episcopacy**: The system of church government in which a single bishop holds supreme authority in a local church.
- **Presbyter-Bishop**: A clergy member who combined pastoral and administrative roles; common in early Christianity before the full development of the episcopal office.
- **Homily**: A sermon or religious discourse, often based on scriptural texts.
- **Pseudo-Clementine Literature**: Early Christian novels and theological works falsely attributed to Clement of Rome.
- **False Decretals**: A 9th-century collection of forged papal decretals, falsely attributed to early popes including Clement.
- **Trinitarian Theology**: Belief in the three persons of the Trinity—Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—as one God.
- **Sola Fide**: Latin for "by faith alone," a Protestant theological principle emphasizing salvation through faith apart from works.
- **Synergism**: Theological view that salvation involves cooperation between divine grace and human effort.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several key questions remain unresolved regarding Clement’s life and legacy:

1. **Chronological Placement**: Was Clement the second, third, or fourth bishop of Rome? Early sources differ, and the lack of consistent records makes definitive placement impossible.

2. **Martyrdom**: Did Clement die naturally or as a martyr? The earliest sources (Eusebius, Jerome) do not mention martyrdom, while later legends describe execution under Trajan. The discrepancy raises questions about the historicity of his death.

3. **Authorship of 2 Clement**: While traditionally attributed to Clement, most scholars now consider it a 2nd-century homily by an unknown author. The reasons for its misattribution remain unclear.

4. **Theological Interpretation**: Did Clement support sola fide (justification by faith alone) or synergism (cooperation with grace)? His statement that “we are not justified by ourselves but by faith” supports the former, but his emphasis on judgment and moral conduct suggests the latter. Scholars continue to debate his soteriological stance.

5. **Church Governance**: Did Clement advocate for a single bishop ruling each church, or did he envision a collective presbyterate? His interchangeable use of *episkopos* and *presbyter* complicates modern interpretations of early church structure.

6. **Identity with Paul’s Companion**: Is the Clement mentioned in Philippians 4:3 the same as Clement of Rome? Modern scholars reject the traditional identification due to lack of evidence.

These debates reflect broader uncertainties about the transition from apostolic to institutional Christianity and the development of early church hierarchy and theology.
