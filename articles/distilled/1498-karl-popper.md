# Karl Popper

## Overview
Sir Karl Popper (1902–1994) was an Austrian–British philosopher of science and political thinker whose work defined the 20th-century debate on scientific methodology and liberal democracy. He rejected the classical inductivist view that science progresses by verifying theories through repeated observation, arguing instead that scientific theories can never be proven true but can be decisively falsified by a single counterexample. This **falsifiability criterion** became his demarcation line between science (e.g., Einstein’s relativity) and non-science (e.g., psychoanalysis, historicism). In politics, Popper defended the **open society**—a liberal democracy permitting peaceful reform—against totalitarianism, historicism (the belief in inevitable historical laws), and unlimited tolerance (the **paradox of tolerance**). His epistemology, **critical rationalism**, holds that all knowledge is conjectural and grows through the evolutionary cycle of problem-solving: conjectures (tentative theories) subjected to rigorous error elimination.

## Timeline
- **28 Jul 1902** — Born in Vienna, Austria-Hungary, to assimilated Jewish parents who converted to Lutheranism.
- **1919** — Briefly joins Communist youth groups; witnesses police shoot unarmed protesters, prompting rejection of Marxism and historicism.
- **1928** — Earns doctorate in psychology under Karl Bühler at University of Vienna.
- **1934** — Publishes *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (German: *Logik der Forschung*), introducing falsifiability as the criterion of demarcation.
- **1937** — Emigrates to New Zealand; lectures at Canterbury University College.
- **1944–45** — Publishes *The Poverty of Historicism* and *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, critiquing totalitarianism and historicism.
- **1946** — Moves to London School of Economics (LSE) as reader; founds Department of Philosophy, Logic and Scientific Method (1946).
- **1949** — Appointed Professor of Logic and Scientific Method at LSE.
- **1965** — Knighted by Queen Elizabeth II.
- **1969** — Retires from academic life; remains intellectually active.
- **1982** — Invested as Member of the Order of the Companions of Honour.
- **17 Sep 1994** — Dies in Kenley, Surrey, UK, aged 92.

## Body

### Philosophy of Science: Falsifiability and Critical Rationalism
Popper’s central contribution is the **criterion of falsifiability** as the solution to the **problem of demarcation** (distinguishing science from metaphysics). He argued that the classical **inductivist** method—deriving universal laws from singular observations—is logically invalid: no number of confirming instances (e.g., "white swans") can verify a universal theory ("all swans are white"), but a single counterinstance ("black swan") falsifies it. This **logical asymmetry** makes falsification the engine of science. A theory is scientific only if it makes risky, testable predictions that *could* conflict with observation. Einstein’s general relativity (predicting light deflection) met this standard; Freudian psychoanalysis and Adlerian psychology did not, as they could explain any behaviour post-hoc and were thus **unfalsifiable**.

Popper termed his epistemology **critical rationalism**. It rejects **justificationism** (the search for certain foundations or "good positive reasons" for beliefs). Instead, knowledge grows through an evolutionary algorithm: **Problem Situation 1 (PS₁) → Tentative Theories (TT) → Error Elimination (EE) → Problem Situation 2 (PS₂)**. Theories are conjectures generated by creative imagination to solve specific problems. They are subjected to severe tests (attempted falsifications); those surviving are not "more true" but more "fit"—better adapted to the current problem situation. This mirrors biological natural selection. Consequently, all scientific knowledge remains **conjectural** and **fallible**; progress means tackling ever deeper problems, not approaching final truth.

### The Problem of Induction and Rationality
Popper claimed to have dissolved the **problem of induction** (Hume’s challenge that past regularities do not logically guarantee future ones). He argued science does not use induction. Instead, scientists propose bold, universal conjectures and deductively test their predictions. We rationally prefer the theory that is **most falsifiable** (simplest, least ad hoc, most informative) because it exposes itself most severely to refutation. There is no inductive justification for expecting the sun to rise tomorrow; there is only a corroborated theory that has survived tests so far. Rationality, for Popper, extends beyond science: it is the general method of **criticism**—finding and eliminating contradictions without *ad hoc* immunizing stratagems—applicable to metaphysics, morals, and politics.

### Philosophy of Arithmetic and Metaphysics
Popper addressed the status of mathematics (e.g., "2+2=4"), which seems logically necessary and unfalsifiable, yet applicable to reality. His solution: distinguish **pure** mathematics (tautological, non-empirical) from **applied** mathematics (empirical, falsifiable). "2 apples + 2 apples = 4 apples" describes physical behaviour; if placing apples in a container yields 5, the applied theory is falsified, though the pure calculus remains valid.

In metaphysics, Popper adopted Alfred Tarski’s **semantic theory of truth** (1933) to rehabilitate **truth as correspondence**. A sentence is true iff it corresponds to the facts. This supported **metaphysical realism** and the regulative ideal of **verisimilitude** (truthlikeness): theories can be objectively compared by their quantitative balance of truth-content minus falsity-content, though Popper’s formal measure proved technically flawed. He also proposed **World 3** (objective knowledge: theories, books, art) as an autonomous evolutionary realm distinct from World 1 (physical) and World 2 (subjective mind). World 3 products (e.g., scientific theories) evolve independently and exert downward causation on individual minds (World 2), a form of **pluralistic realism** distinct from physicalism or dualism.

### Evolution, Darwinism, and Free Will
Popper famously called Darwinism a **"metaphysical research programme"**—not a testable universal law, but a fruitful framework generating testable hypotheses (e.g., antibiotic resistance). He later recanted the "untestable" claim, arguing natural selection *can* be formulated as a testable, non-tautological theory (with exceptions), though it remains a "most successful metaphysical research programme." He developed the **spearhead model** ("active Darwinism"): organisms pursue goals via a control organ (brain); mutations altering goals/behaviour precede phenotypic adaptation ("hopeful behavioural monsters"), making evolution goal-directed from within, not passive selection from without.

On **free will**, Popper (with John Eccles) proposed a **two-stage model** combining quantum indeterminacy and selective control. The brain generates a probabilistic range of possibilities (like genetic mutations); the mind applies a selective procedure (like natural selection) to eliminate unacceptable options. Freedom is not chance but the interplay of randomness and control.

### Political Philosophy: Open Society and Historicism
Popper’s politics stem from his epistemology. **Historicism**—the claim that history follows inevitable laws toward a predetermined end (found in Plato, Hegel, Marx)—is pseudoscientific because the growth of knowledge (a causal factor in history) cannot be predicted. Since we cannot know future knowledge, we cannot predict future society. Historicism underpins **totalitarianism** (closed societies) by justifying violent revolution for an "inevitable" utopia.

The **open society** is the alternative: a liberal democracy where institutions allow peaceful **piecemeal engineering** (trial-and-error reform) rather than violent **utopian engineering**. It protects individual freedom and critical discussion. Popper formulated the **paradox of tolerance**: unlimited tolerance destroys tolerance; a tolerant society must claim the right to suppress intolerant movements that refuse rational argument and resort to force. He also attacked the **"conspiracy theory of society"** (ills result from powerful groups' intentional designs), arguing social outcomes are largely unintended consequences of intentional actions.

Popper co-founded the Mont Pelerin Society (1947) but dissented from its laissez-faire focus, prioritizing humanitarian values over market advocacy. He viewed the Cold War as democracy vs. totalitarianism, not capitalism vs. socialism.

### Religion and Ethics
Popper was agnostic: "I don't know whether God exists." He rejected organized religion (fanaticism, myths) and arrogant atheism, valuing honest belief and the comfort religion provides. He saw ethics as central, affiliating with humanist societies; the open society concept became the "ideological backbone" of humanist campaigning. He believed religious convictions cannot be rationally justified but are not meaningless.

### Criticism and Responses
Major critiques include:
*   **Quine–Duhem thesis**: Hypotheses are tested in bundles; a failed prediction falsifies the whole package, not a single theory (e.g., Neptune’s discovery saved Newton’s laws by rejecting "seven planets"). Popper acknowledged this in *Logic of Scientific Discovery*, treating falsification as a methodological decision by the scientific community.
*   **Thomas Kuhn**: *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) argued science operates in "normal science" paradigms, not constant falsification; anomalies are puzzles, not refutations. Popper criticized Kuhn’s **relativism**; his student Imre Lakatos synthesized them via **research programmes** (falsifying progressive/degenerating programmes, not isolated statements).
*   **Statistical/Logical objections**: Carl Hempel noted universal statements like "all metals melt at some temperature" are unfalsifiable in practice but scientific. Popper replied such statements are consequences of accepted theories (atomic theory), inheriting scientific status.
*   **Psychoanalysis**: Adolf Grünbaum and Roger Scruton argued Freudian theory *does* have empirical content and testable consequences (e.g., repression), though Scruton agreed it remains non-scientific.
*   **Sources**: Michel ter Hark argued Popper borrowed key ideas (evolutionary epistemology, problem-solving) from his tutor Otto Selz, whose work was suppressed by Nazism.

## Terms
- ****Falsifiability**** — The criterion that a theory is scientific only if it makes predictions that could, in principle, be contradicted by observation (i.e., it is refutable).
- ****Critical Rationalism**** — Popper’s epistemology: knowledge grows not by justification/induction but by conjectures subjected to severe criticism and error elimination.
- ****Problem of Demarcation**** — The philosophical task of distinguishing science from non-science (metaphysics, pseudoscience); Popper’s answer is falsifiability.
- ****Inductivism**** — The traditional view that scientific laws are derived by generalizing from repeated observations; Popper rejected this as logically invalid.
- ****Verisimilitude (Truthlikeness)**** — A quantitative measure of a theory’s truth-content minus its falsity-content; allows comparing false theories as closer/further from truth.
- ****Historicism**** — The doctrine that history follows inevitable, knowable laws toward a determinate end; Popper deemed it pseudoscientific and the root of totalitarianism.
- ****Open Society**** — A liberal democracy allowing peaceful reform via critical discussion and piecemeal engineering; contrasted with closed, totalitarian "utopian engineering."
- ****Paradox of Tolerance**** — Unlimited tolerance leads to tolerance’s extinction; a tolerant society must suppress movements that reject rational argument and use force.
- ****World 3**** — The objective, autonomous realm of human knowledge products (theories, books, art), distinct from the physical world (World 1) and subjective mind (World 2).
- ****Spearhead Model / Active Darwinism**** — Evolution driven by organisms’ goal-directed behaviour (mutations in control organs precede phenotypic change), not passive environmental selection.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Demarcation vs. Practice**: Does falsifiability describe actual scientific practice? Kuhn and Lakatos argued scientists do not abandon theories at the first anomaly; Popper countered his method is *normative* (how science *should* work to ensure progress), not sociological.
*   **Quine–Duhem Holism**: Can a single hypothesis ever be falsified in isolation? Popper accepted holistic testing but maintained the *methodological rule* to falsify the least central assumption first is a rational convention.
*   **Verisimilitude Formalization**: Popper’s specific mathematical definition (truth-content minus falsity-content) was proven inadequate (Pavel Tichý, David Miller). Whether a viable formal measure exists remains open.
*   **Darwinism’s Status**: Popper’s shifting views (metaphysical framework → testable theory with exceptions) fuel ongoing debate on whether natural selection is a tautology, a metaphysical research programme, or a fully empirical theory.
*   **Source of Ideas**: The extent of Otto Selz’s influence on Popper’s evolutionary epistemology and problem-solving model is debated; Selz’s unpublished work complicates attribution.
*   **Political Application**: The "paradox of tolerance" is widely cited in law/policy (e.g., banning extremist parties), but the threshold for "intolerance" justifying suppression remains contested.
