# Rainer Maria Rilke

## Overview
Rainer Maria Rilke (1875–1926) was an Austrian poet and novelist widely regarded as a master of German literature. His work bridges late Romanticism and Modernism, characterized by a shift from subjective lyricism to the objective "thing-poems" influenced by Auguste Rodin, and culminating in the mystical, existential *Duino Elegies* and *Sonnets to Orpheus*. Rilke’s peripatetic life—spanning Prague, Munich, Russia, Paris, and finally Switzerland—fed a poetry that interrogated the limits of human consciousness, the terror of angels as symbols of a higher indifference, and the artist’s task of transforming death and alienation into praise. His *Letters to a Young Poet* cemented his posthumous reputation as a spiritual guide for artists.

## Timeline

- **1886–1891** — Attends military academy in Sankt Pölten; leaves due to illness.
- **1897** — Meets Lou Andreas-Salomé in Munich; changes first name to "Rainer" at her urging.
- **1899–1900** — Travels to Russia with Andreas-Salomé; meets Leo Tolstoy and the Pasternak family.
- **1901** — Marries sculptor Clara Westhoff; daughter Ruth born.
- **1902–1910** — Lives primarily in Paris; serves as secretary to Auguste Rodin (1905–06); publishes *New Poems* (1907–08) and *The Notebooks of Malte Laurids Brigge* (1910).
- **1912** — Begins *Duino Elegies* at Duino Castle; visits Toledo to study El Greco.
- **1914–1916** — Stranded in Germany by WWI; undergoes brief military service (discharged June 1916); poetic output nearly silenced.
- **1919** — Moves to Switzerland to escape post-war chaos; meets Baladine Klossowska.
- **Feb 1922** — Completes *Duino Elegies* and writes *Sonnets to Orpheus* in a "savage creative storm" at Château de Muzot.
- **1923–1926** — Increasingly debilitated by leukemia; publishes French poems *Vergers* (1926).
- **29 Dec 1926** — Dies in Valmont Sanatorium, Switzerland; buried in Raron cemetery.

## Body

### Early Life and Formation (1875–1896)
Rilke was born into a German-speaking family in Prague. His childhood was marked by his mother Phia’s grief over a deceased infant daughter, leading her to treat young René as a replacement girl, dressing him in "fine clothes" like a "big doll." His parents separated in 1884. Pressured into a military academy (1886–1891), he suffered physical and psychological distress, leaving due to illness. After a brief stint at a trade school in Linz (expelled 1892), he received private tutoring in Prague, passing the university entrance exam in 1895. He studied literature, art history, and philosophy in Prague until 1896, when he left for Munich to pursue a literary life.

### Lou Andreas-Salomé and the Russian Journeys (1897–1901)
In Munich, Rilke met Lou Andreas-Salomé, a married intellectual 15 years his senior who became his mentor, lover, and lifelong confidante. She urged him to change his name to "Rainer" for a more Germanic sound. Between 1899 and 1900, he made two pivotal trips to Russia with her, meeting Leo Tolstoy and the peasant poet Spiridon Drozhzhin, and encountering the family of Boris Pasternak. Rilke later cited the cultures of Bohemia and Russia as the key influences on his consciousness. In 1900, at the Worpswede artists' colony, he met sculptor Clara Westhoff, marrying her in 1901; their daughter Ruth was born that December. The marriage effectively dissolved shortly after, though they never divorced due to Rilke’s nominal Catholic status.

### Paris and the Rodin Apprenticeship (1902–1910)
Rilke moved to Paris in 1902 to write a monograph on Auguste Rodin, becoming the sculptor’s secretary (1905–06). Rodin taught him *objective observation*—looking at things until they reveal their essence—catalyzing a radical stylistic break. Rilke abandoned the incantatory subjectivity of his early verse for the *New Poems* (1907, 1908), famous for "thing-poems" (*Dinggedichte*) that treat objects (a panther, a carousel, a rose) as autonomous presences. Simultaneously, he absorbed the structural rigor of Paul Cézanne. Paris also provided the material for his only novel, *The Notebooks of Malte Laurids Brigge* (1910), a semi-autobiographical, expressionistic fragment exploring alienation, the falsity of social masks, and the proximity of death in a modern, industrialized city.

### Duino, War, and Silence (1911–1919)
In 1911–12, Rilke stayed at Duino Castle near Trieste, hosted by Princess Marie von Thurn und Taxis. There he began the *Duino Elegies*, opening with the invocation: "Who, if I cried out, would hear me among the hierarchies of angels?" A visit to Toledo to study El Greco’s angels likely shaped the work’s central symbol: angels as terrifying, indifferent embodiments of a higher reality that does not need us. WWI trapped Rilke in Germany; his Paris property was confiscated. Conscripted in 1916, the trauma of basic training—echoing his military academy horror—silenced him as a poet. He was discharged through influential friends but remained largely unproductive until the war’s end.

### The Swiss Breakthrough: Muzot (1919–1926)
Rilke fled to Switzerland in June 1919, eventually settling at Château de Muzot (Valais) in 1921, secured rent-free by patron Werner Reinhart. In February 1922, a "savage creative storm" yielded the completion of the *Duino Elegies* and, immediately after, the 55 *Sonnets to Orpheus* (inspired by the death of Wera Knoop, his daughter’s friend). These twin cycles constitute his supreme achievement: the *Elegies* transmuting ontological torment into an affirmation of earthly existence despite the "terrifying" angels; the *Sonnets* exploring the Orphic power of song to reconcile life and death. His final years were plagued by leukemia. He produced significant late poems (*Gong*, *Mausoleum*) and French lyrics (*Vergers*, 1926), and conducted his only sustained poetic correspondence with Erika Mitterer (1924–25). He died December 29, 1926.

### Political Engagements
Rilke initially supported the 1917 Russian Revolution and the 1919 Bavarian Soviet Republic, mourning the murders of Rosa Luxemburg, Kurt Eisner, and Karl Liebknecht. He read far-left newspapers daily. After the Freikorps crushed the Bavarian Republic, he grew reticent for safety. In early 1926, shortly before death, he wrote three letters praising Benito Mussolini and fascism as a "healing agent," a stance scholars view as a complex, late aberration possibly driven by a longing for order amid chaos.

### Major Works Analysis

**The Book of Hours (1905)**
Three cycles (*The Book of Monastic Life*, *The Book of Pilgrimage*, *The Book of Poverty and Death*) exploring the search for God through symbols of Saint Francis and Orthodox Christianity (observed in Ukraine). It marks the transition from conventional piety to a poetry where God is not a given but a "neighbor" to be built by human hands.

**The Notebooks of Malte Laurids Brigge (1910)**
Written in Paris, this expressionistic novel uses impressionistic techniques (learned from Cézanne/Rodin) to fragment narrative. The protagonist Malte records the prodigal son, death by illness, childhood freedom, and the "false faces" of urban anonymity. It probes the crisis of individuality in a reified world, drawing on Nietzsche (via Andreas-Salomé) and the Danish atheist novel *Niels Lyhne*.

**Duino Elegies (1912–1922)**
Ten elegies weighing beauty against existential suffering. They reject Christian consolation: angels are "terrifying" (*schrecklich*) because they embody a perfection that excludes human fragility. The poems move from despair ("every angel is terrifying") to a demanding affirmation of the "open" world—lovers, the dead, the anonymous—tasking the poet with making the invisible visible through praise.

**Sonnets to Orpheus (1922)**
Two parts (26 + 29 sonnets) written in weeks after the *Elegies*. Orpheus ("god with the lyre") figures the poet who sings transformation. Wera Knoop appears directly once but "governs the whole course." Themes include metamorphosis, the animal world, cultural difference, and the conquest of time through the "eternal" moment of the poem.

**Letters to a Young Poet (1902–1908, pub. 1929)**
Ten letters to Franz Xaver Kappus, a military cadet at the academy Rilke had fled. Rilke refuses to critique Kappus’s verses, instead advising on the inner disposition required: solitude, patience, living the questions, and embracing difficulty as the crucible of art. They articulate the ethics of his early Paris period.

### Style and Themes
Rilke’s mature style relies on metaphor, metonymy, and contradiction to enact a "crisis of faith" in language itself. Greek myths (Orpheus, Apollo, Daphne) are not allusions but lived analogies for the artist’s ordeal. The *Duino* angels—terrifying because they "do not know" us—replace the Christian God. Roses, windows, and mirrors function as "things" that hold the tension between interior and exterior. His French poems (late) extend this project into a second language, stripping German’s compound density for a sparer clarity.

### Legacy
Rilke is a best-selling poet in the US, frequently cited in self-help contexts and popular media (film, TV, music) for his aphorisms on love, solitude, and angels. W. H. Auden is called his "most influential English disciple." He influenced poets (Gass, Kinnell, Merwin, Ashbery, Bly), novelists (Pynchon), and philosophers (Gadamer). The rock band Rainer Maria bears his name. His work remains a touchstone for the idea that poetry is a way of *inhabiting* the world more deeply, not merely describing it.

## Terms
- **Thing-poem (*Dinggedicht*)**: A poem that presents an object (animal, statue, landscape) with objective, non-symbolic intensity, allowing the thing its own reality; pioneered in *New Poems* under Rodin’s influence.
- **Objective observation**: Rodin’s method of sustained, patient looking until the subject reveals its essential form; adopted by Rilke as a poetic discipline.
- **Angels (Rilkean)**: In the *Duino Elegies*, not Christian messengers but terrifying personifications of a higher, indifferent perfection that recognizes neither human prayer nor suffering.
- **Expressionism**: Early 20th-century movement distorting external reality to express inner states; *Malte Laurids Brigge* uses its fragmented, subjective narrative technique.
- **Orpheus**: Mythic singer who charmed stones and descended to Hades; for Rilke, the archetype of the poet who transforms trauma into song, reconciling life and death.
- **Wera Knoop (1900–1919)**: Friend of Rilke’s daughter Ruth; her death catalyzed the *Sonnets to Orpheus*, where she appears as "dancer" and Eurydice figure.
- **Lou Andreas-Salomé (1861–1937)**: Psychoanalyst, writer, and Rilke’s mentor/lover; introduced him to Nietzsche, Russia, and the name "Rainer"; remained his key intellectual interlocutor.
- **Château de Muzot**: 13th-century tower in Veyras, Valais, where Rilke completed his masterworks in 1922; bought for him by patron Werner Reinhart.
- **Franz Xaver Kappus (1883–1966)**: Recipient of *Letters to a Young Poet*; cadet at the military academy Rilke attended, seeking vocational counsel.
- **Baladine Klossowska (1886–1969)**: Polish-German painter; Rilke’s companion from 1919 until his death; mother of artist Balthus.

## Debates and open questions
- **The 1926 Mussolini letters**: Whether Rilke’s praise of fascism as a "healing agent" reflects genuine political conviction, a desperate longing for order amid terminal illness, or a misunderstanding of the regime’s nature remains contested.
- **Autobiography vs. persona in *Malte Laurids Brigge***: Scholars debate how far the novel’s protagonist is a transparent mask for Rilke versus an constructed artistic figure enabling existential inquiry.
- **The "angel" symbol**: Whether the *Duino* angels represent a secularized transcendence, a psychological projection of the poet’s own ideal, or a genuine mystical encounter is unresolved.
- **Late French poems**: The status of *Vergers* and *Quatrains valaisans*—minor exercises or a distinct, stripped-down late style—divides critics.
- **Influence of psychoanalysis**: Andreas-Salomé’s Freudian training (1912–13) informed her readings of Rilke; the extent to which Rilke internalized psychoanalytic concepts versus resisting them is debated.
- **Translation fidelity**: The density of Rilke’s German (compound nouns, syntactic ambiguity) makes English translation notoriously contested; no version captures all registers.
