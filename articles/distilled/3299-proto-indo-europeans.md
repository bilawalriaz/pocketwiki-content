# Proto-Indo-Europeans

## Overview
The Proto-Indo-Europeans (PIE) are a postulated prehistoric ethnolinguistic group who spoke the reconstructed common ancestor of the Indo-European language family. Knowledge of them is derived from linguistic reconstruction, archaeology, and archaeogenetics. They are significant as the likely source of the languages spoken by billions of people today, and their proposed migrations shaped the genetic and cultural landscape of Eurasia.

## Timeline
- **c. 6400–3500 BC** — Mainstream scholarly placement of PIE speakers in the Pontic–Caspian steppe during the Late Neolithic to Chalcolithic.
- **c. 3500–3000 BC** — Proposed period of major migrations from the steppe, coinciding with the taming of the horse and the use of solid wheels.
- **c. 3000 BC** — Spread of steppe-related ancestry (Yamnaya culture) into Northern and Central Europe, associated with the Corded Ware culture.
- **c. 2500 BC** — Yamnaya-related ancestry appears in Bronze Age Mycenaean Greece.
- **19th Century** — Early scholarly debate on Indo-European homeland (Urheimat) begins, divided between European and Asian hypotheses.
- **Early 20th Century** — The term "Aryan" becomes associated with a discredited racial theory.
- **1956 onwards** — Marija Gimbutas systematizes the Kurgan (Steppe) hypothesis.
- **1980s onwards** — Colin Renfrew advocates the Anatolian hypothesis (spread with farming).
- **2015** — Major ancient DNA studies provide strong evidence for a massive migration from the Pontic-Caspian steppe into Europe.

## Body

### Definition and Culture
The Proto-Indo-Europeans are defined as a linguistic community, not a nation or race. They were likely a loose network of clans and tribes. Linguistic reconstruction suggests they were pastoralists who domesticated cattle, horses, and dogs, and practiced agriculture with technology like the plow. They used solid-wheeled wagons, worshipped a sky father (*Dyḗus Ph2tḗr), and had a patrilineal kinship system. A reconstructed folktale, "The Smith and the Devil," implies they possessed metallurgy.

### History of Research and Urheimat Hypotheses
Research began with linguistic evidence, leading to early European vs. Asian origin debates. The field advanced significantly in the 1970s with improved dating techniques and greater access to Eastern European archaeology. The most widely accepted theory is the **Kurgan (or Steppe) hypothesis**, which places PIE origins in the Pontic-Caspian steppe during the Chalcolithic, with expansions in the 3rd millennium BCE. A minority view is the **Anatolian hypothesis**, which proposes a peaceful spread from Anatolia with the Neolithic farming expansion around 7000 BC. Other theories, like the Armenian hypothesis, have marginal support. The term "Aryan" was historically used for PIE speakers but is now largely abandoned due to its racist misuse.

### Genetics and Archaeogenetics
Genetic studies strongly support a steppe origin. The Yamnaya culture of the Pontic-Caspian steppe is genetically modeled as a mix of Eastern European Hunter-Gatherers (EHG) and Caucasus Hunter-Gatherers (CHG). The Y-chromosome haplogroups R1a and R1b, common in Europe and South Asia, expanded from the steppe. Around 4500 years ago, a major genetic influx from the Yamnaya into Europe is documented, with Corded Ware people tracing ~75% of their ancestry to them. Bronze Age Mycenaeans also show 13–18% steppe ancestry. While some genetic evidence supports a Near Eastern component in steppe populations, critics argue this reflects ancient admixture, not a recent migration from the south. The Anatolian hypothesis is challenged by the lack of evidence for a large-scale steppe intrusion into ancient Anatolia and the presence of non-Indo-European peoples there in the 2nd millennium BC.

## Terms
- **Proto-Indo-European (PIE):** The reconstructed common ancestor of the Indo-European language family.
- **Urheimat:** The original homeland of a proto-language and its speakers.
- **Kurgan hypothesis:** The theory that PIE speakers originated in the Pontic-Caspian steppe and expanded via migrations.
- **Anatolian hypothesis:** The theory that Indo-European languages spread peacefully from Anatolia with the Neolithic farming expansion.
- **Yamnaya culture:** A Late Chalcolithic/Early Bronze Age culture of the Pontic-Caspian steppe, genetically linked to PIE speakers.
- **Corded Ware culture:** A widespread archaeological culture in Northern and Central Europe, genetically linked to steppe migrations.
- **Haplogroup:** A genetic population group sharing a common ancestor, used to trace paternal (Y-DNA) or maternal (mtDNA) lineages.
- **Patrilineal:** Descent traced through male lines.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the PIE homeland and timing. While the Steppe hypothesis is mainstream, the Anatolian and Armenian hypotheses persist. A key open question is the exact process of language spread: was it primarily through elite dominance and cultural diffusion (as in some steppe models) or through massive population replacement (as genetics suggests for Europe)? The role of the Caucasus as a corridor or potential homeland is also actively debated. Furthermore, the precise relationship between the spread of genes, languages, and specific archaeological cultures remains a complex, interdisciplinary puzzle.
