# Saul Bellow

## Overview
Saul Bellow (1915–2005) was a Canadian-American novelist whose work fused high philosophical inquiry with street-level vernacular, earning him the Nobel Prize in Literature (1976), the Pulitzer Prize (1976), and three National Book Awards. Born to Lithuanian-Jewish immigrants in Quebec and raised in Chicago, Bellow transformed the immigrant experience into a universal exploration of consciousness, alienation, and the struggle for transcendence amid "the big-scale insanities of the 20th century." His protagonists—intellectual outsiders wrestling with madness, materialism, and the demand for nobility—mirrored his own trajectory from Trotskyist youth to cultural conservative. Bellow’s prose, celebrated for its "Melvillean cadences jostling the jivey Yiddish rhythms," established a backbone of 20th-century American letters alongside Faulkner, influencing generations of writers despite critiques of digressiveness and gendered caricature.

- **1993** — Moves to Brookline, Massachusetts to teach at Boston University.
- **April 5, 2005** — Dies in Brookline at age 89; buried in Brattleboro, Vermont.

## Body

### Early Life and Formation
Bellow was born in Lachine, Quebec, two years after his parents fled Saint Petersburg, Russia. The family’s descent from "prosperous cosmopolitans" to poverty—his father working as an onion importer, bakery worker, coal deliverer, and bootlegger—instilled a sense of falling that Bellow later transmuted into "embittered irony." A respiratory illness at age eight confined him to bed, sparking voracious reading; he decided to become a writer after reading *Uncle Tom’s Cabin*. In 1924, the family moved to Chicago’s Humboldt Park, the neighborhood that became the geographic soul of his fiction. His mother’s death when he was 17 freed him from her wish that he become a rabbi or violinist, though his early Hebrew training rooted a lifelong engagement with the Torah. At Tuley High School he befriended Isaac Rosenfeld, later the model for King Dahfu in *Henderson the Rain King*.

### Education, Anthropology, and Early Politics
Bellow left the University of Chicago for Northwestern, avoiding an English department he perceived as anti-Jewish. He graduated with honors in anthropology and sociology—a discipline that seeded his novels with structural and cultural references. In the 1930s, he joined the Chicago Federal Writers' Project, a cohort including Richard Wright and Nelson Algren. Most colleagues were Communist Party members or sympathizers; Bellow, a Trotskyist, endured their taunts. This period solidified his view of literature as a "ferocious assimilation of learning" against ideological conformity.

### Breakthrough: *The Adventures of Augie March* and the Picaresque
A 1948 Guggenheim Fellowship funded a move to Paris, where Bellow wrote *The Adventures of Augie March* (1953). The novel’s famous opening—"I am an American, Chicago born"—launches a picaresque journey (a genre of episodic adventure featuring a roguish but appealing hero) through Depression-era Chicago and Mexico. Critics likened its structure to *Don Quixote*. Written in a colloquial-philosophical style, it established Bellow’s signature: a democracy of registers mixing high culture (Proust, Henry James) with Yiddish-inflected street slang. The book won the 1954 National Book Award and announced a major new voice.

### Chicago Return, *Herzog*, and the Intellectual Protagonist
After years in New York, Bellow returned to Chicago in 1962 to join the Committee on Social Thought at the University of Chicago, a multidisciplinary graduate program where he taught for over three decades alongside philosopher Allan Bloom (later fictionalized in *Ravelstein*). He chose Chicago for its vitality and representativeness, living in Hyde Park despite its high crime. *Herzog* (1964) became a surprise bestseller: a cerebral novel about a professor writing unsent letters to the living and dead. Its commercial success proved that a "troubled college professor" could anchor mass-market fiction. *Humboldt’s Gift* (1975) returned to the theme of mental instability versus genius, modeling the title character on the self-destructive poet Delmore Schwartz and incorporating anthroposophy (Rudolf Steiner’s spiritual science), which Bellow had studied in Chicago.

### Nobel Prize and Late Career
The success of *Humboldt’s Gift* propelled Bellow to the 1976 Nobel Prize. In his Stockholm address, he urged writers to be "beacons for civilization" against intellectual torpor. The same year he delivered the Jefferson Lecture, the U.S. government’s highest humanities honor. He continued teaching widely—Yale, Princeton, NYU, Bard, Boston University—moving to Brookline, MA in 1993 for the latter post. His final novel, *Ravelstein* (2000), a fictionalized portrait of Allan Bloom, was hailed by some as his most integrated work. Bellow died in 2005 at 89, leaving a bibliography of 14 novels/novellas, four story collections, a play, and non-fiction including the memoir *To Jerusalem and Back* (1976).

### Themes: Transcendence, Jewish Identity, and the American Experiment
Bellow’s central theme is the individual’s struggle to achieve awareness and nobility amid societal "madness, materialism and misleading knowledge." His protagonists—often Jewish outsiders like Augie March, Moses Herzog, and Artur Sammler—possess "heroic potential" but wrestle with alienation. Bellow resisted the label "Jewish writer," yet Jewish life and the immigrant hunger for transcendence permeate his work. He celebrated America’s "uniqueness and vibrancy" while diagnosing its cultural decay. Autobiographical elements are pervasive; Bellow stated Eugene Henderson was the character most like himself.

### Style: High-Low Synthesis and the Weight of Sentences
Bellow’s prose synthesizes "Melvillean cadences" with "jivey Yiddish rhythms," interspersing arcane philosophical speculation (anthroposophy, theology, anthropology) with jokes and street argot. His sentences are noted for their density and musicality—"they weigh more than anyone else’s"—creating a "great teeming democracy" of crooks, intellectuals, and frauds. Critics like James Wood emphasize the "beauty... high lyricism... firm but luxurious pleasure in language itself." V.S. Pritchett judged his shorter works, particularly the novella *Seize the Day* (1956), as his best—a "small gray masterpiece."

### Assessment and Criticism
Martin Amis called Bellow "the greatest American author ever"; Philip Roth placed him with Faulkner as the "backbone of 20th-century American literature." Yet detractors exist. Vladimir Nabokov privately dismissed him as a "miserable mediocrity." Kingsley Amis preferred crime writer John D. MacDonald. Ron Rosenbaum argued Bellow strained to yoke "Windy City wiseguy" energy with "undigested chunks of arcane... philosophic thought." Sam Tanenhaus catalogued "longueurs and digressions," "punitively caricatured ex-wives," and static characters—but concluded these shortcomings are irrelevant for an author who "is a world... systems blazing at random out of the darkness."

### Political Views and Controversies
Bellow drifted from Trotskyism to cultural conservatism. He opposed political correctness, comparing its "thought-police" tactics to Stalinism. A 1980s remark—"Who is the Tolstoy of the Zulus? The Proust of the Papuans?"—sparked accusations of racism; Bellow called it "off the cuff... pedantic" but stood by his anti-Stalinist critique of enforced rage as "a luxury... instrument of censorship." In Chicago, a street-naming in Hyde Park was blocked by Alderman Toni Preckwinkle over remarks deemed racist; a Humboldt Park block was named Saul Bellow Way instead. He briefly supported U.S. English (advocating English as official language) but left the group in 1988.

## Terms
- **Picaresque**: A genre of episodic fiction featuring a roguish, low-born protagonist who lives by wit in a corrupt society; Bellow adapts this for *The Adventures of Augie March*.
- **Anthroposophy**: Rudolf Steiner’s "spiritual science" blending mysticism and empirical study; Bellow attended Chicago study groups and wove it into *Humboldt’s Gift*.
- **Committee on Social Thought**: University of Chicago graduate program (founded 1941) fostering multidisciplinary inquiry; Bellow taught there 1962–1993 alongside Allan Bloom.
- **Trotskyist**: Follower of Leon Trotsky’s Marxist theory opposing Stalinism; Bellow’s 1930s affiliation isolated him among Communist-leaning Writers' Project peers.
- **National Book Award for Fiction**: Annual U.S. literary prize; Bellow is the only writer to win it three times (1954, 1965, 1971).
- **Jefferson Lecture**: Highest U.S. federal honor for humanities achievement; Bellow delivered it in 1977 ("The Writer and His Country Look Each Other Over").
- **Federal Writers' Project**: New Deal program employing writers during the Great Depression; Bellow’s cohort included Richard Wright and Nelson Algren.
- **Delmore Schwartz (1913–1966)**: Brilliant, self-destructive poet; model for Von Humboldt Fleisher in *Humboldt’s Gift*.
- **Allan Bloom (1930–1992)**: Philosopher, Bellow’s close friend and colleague; fictionalized as Abe Ravelstein in *Ravelstein* (2000).
- **Shtetl**: Small Jewish town in Eastern Europe; Bellow’s father was born in Druya, in the Pale of Settlement.

## Debates and open questions
- **Literary canon status**: Is Bellow the supreme 20th-century American novelist (Amis, Roth), or a "miserable mediocrity" (Nabokov) whose philosophical digressions fracture narrative unity (Rosenbaum)?
- **Gender and caricature**: Do Bellow’s female characters—especially ex-wives—reflect misogynistic "punitively caricatured" portraits drawn from marital discord (Tanenhaus), or are they part of his universal satirical gaze?
- **Political legacy**: Was Bellow’s anti-political-correctness stance a principled defense of intellectual freedom against Stalinist "thought-police," or a reactionary defense of privilege that produced racist rhetoric (the "Zulus/Papuans" remark)?
- **Jewish writer label**: Bellow rejected the category, yet his work centers Jewish alienation and transcendence; does the label illuminate or reductively ghettoize his universal ambition?
- **Late work valuation**: Is *Ravelstein* (2000) a crowning synthesis of wisdom and wit (Rosenbaum), or does it share the "longueurs" of his later period?
