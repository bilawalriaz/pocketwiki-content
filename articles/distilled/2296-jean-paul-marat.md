# Jean-Paul Marat

## Overview
Jean-Paul Marat (1743–1793) was a French physician, scientist, and radical journalist. His fiery writings made him a central figure of the French Revolution's early, violent phase. He championed the sans-culottes (urban working poor) and became a revolutionary martyr after his assassination, his image later used to justify the Reign of Terror.

## Timeline
- **24 May 1743** — Born in Boudry, Prussian Principality of Neuchâtel.
- **1774** — Publishes *Chains of Slavery*, criticizing English corruption.
- **1775** — Receives an MD from the University of St Andrews.
- **1777** — Appointed physician to the bodyguard of the Comte d'Artois in Paris.
- **1780** — Publishes *Recherches Physiques sur le Feu*, beginning conflict with the Academy of Sciences.
- **12 Sep 1789** — Begins publishing his radical newspaper, *L'Ami du peuple*.
- **10 Aug 1792** — The Tuileries Palace is stormed; Marat emerges from hiding to support the insurrection.
- **2 Sep 1792** — The September Massacres begin; Marat's Committee on Surveillance circulates a provincial call to action.
- **Sep 1792** — Elected to the National Convention as a Paris deputy.
- **24 Apr 1793** — Tried and acquitted by the Revolutionary Tribunal after Girondin accusations.
- **13 Jul 1793** — Assassinated in his bathtub by Charlotte Corday.
- **17 Jul 1793** — Corday is executed for Marat's murder.

## Body

### Early Life and Pre-Revolutionary Career
Born to a Sardinian father and Genevan mother, Marat left home at 16 to seek education in France. He studied medicine without formal qualifications, practiced in London, and published his first political work, *Chains of Slavery* (1774), arguing for popular sovereignty inspired by Rousseau. After securing an MD, he moved to Paris in 1776, becoming a successful court physician. He then devoted himself to scientific research on fire, light, and electricity. His experiments often put him at odds with the official Academy of Sciences, notably Antoine Lavoisier, after the Academy praised his methods but refused to endorse his conclusions.

### Revolutionary Journalist and Politician
Marat abandoned science for politics upon the calling of the Estates-General in 1788. His newspaper, *L'Ami du peuple* (from September 1789), became his platform for fierce attacks on revolutionary leaders he deemed corrupt or counter-revolutionary. He repeatedly called for violent measures, famously suggesting "five or six hundred heads cut off" to secure liberty. Forced into hiding for years, he emerged publicly only during the 10 August 1792 insurrection that overthrew the monarchy.

### The September Massacres and the Committee on Surveillance
Following the insurrection, Marat became an influential member of the Paris Commune's Committee on Surveillance. In early September 1792, as Prussian forces invaded France and captured Verdun, panic gripped Paris. The Committee, with Marat's involvement, oversaw the September Massacres, in which over 1,000 prisoners were summarily executed. Marat authored and signed a circular on 3 September hailing the killings as a model for the provinces. While his direct responsibility is debated, his rhetoric and position made him a focal point for the violence, driven by popular fear of internal betrayal during a military crisis.

### National Convention and Assassination
Elected to the National Convention, Marat was a persistent critic of the moderate Girondins. After his acquittal from their charges in April 1793, his faction helped engineer their overthrow on 2 June. Suffering from a debilitating skin disease, he worked from his bathtub. On 13 July, Charlotte Corday, a Girondin sympathizer, gained an audience and stabbed him. Her stated goal was to "avenge the fate of her friend" and stop the violence Marat represented. She was executed four days later.

### Martyrdom and Legacy
Marat's death transformed him into a revolutionary martyr. Organized by Jacques-Louis David, his funeral was a grand spectacle, and David's painting *The Death of Marat* immortalized him. He was briefly interred in the Panthéon, and his bust replaced crucifixes in churches during the dechristianization campaign. After the Thermidorian Reaction, his reputation was officially tarnished, his remains removed from the Panthéon, and his busts destroyed. He remained a revered figure in the Soviet Union, with places and a battleship named after him.

## Terms
*   **Sans-culottes:** The radical militant partisans of the lower classes in the French Revolution, literally "without breeches," referring to their rejection of aristocratic knee-breeches.
*   **Girondins:** A political faction during the French Revolution that favored a constitutional monarchy and later a republic, but opposed the radical Montagnards and the violence of the Terror.
*   **Montagnards (The Mountain):** The radical, left-wing political faction in the National Convention, seated on the higher benches, who dominated the Committee of Public Safety during the Reign of Terror.
*   **September Massacres:** A wave of mob violence in Paris (2–7 September 1792) in which mobs stormed prisons and killed over 1,000 prisoners, amid fears of counter-revolutionary plots during the Prussian invasion.
*   **Reign of Terror:** The period of the French Revolution (1793–1794) marked by mass executions of perceived enemies of the revolution, overseen by the Committee of Public Safety.
*   **Paris Commune:** The revolutionary government of Paris, which played a key role in the insurrections of 1789 and 1792 and often clashed with the national legislative assemblies.
*   **Committee of General Security:** A powerful committee of the National Convention responsible for police and internal security, which oversaw the arrests during the Terror.
*   **Thermidorian Reaction:** The coup d'état of 9 Thermidor Year II (27 July 1794) that overthrew Robespierre and ended the most radical phase of the Revolution.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Responsibility for the September Massacres:** Scholars debate Marat's role. Some, citing his inflammatory rhetoric and his Committee's circular, attribute significant responsibility to him. Others, like Georges Lefebvre, argue the massacres were a spontaneous "ebullition of popular fury" driven by the military crisis and collective mentality, not the will of any individual.
*   **The Nature of His Skin Disease:** Historically attributed to syphilis, modern medical analysis, based on descriptions of intense itching, blistering, and location, suggests a diagnosis of dermatitis herpetiformis.
*   **Historical Reputation:** Marat's legacy is deeply polarized. He is alternately remembered as a "Friend of the People" who defended the poor and a bloodthirsty demagogue whose rhetoric fueled the Terror. His apotheosis and subsequent vilification reflect the shifting political tides of the Revolution itself.
