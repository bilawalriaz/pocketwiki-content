# Paul von Hindenburg

## Overview
Paul von Hindenburg was a German field marshal and statesman, a national hero of World War I who later served as President of the Weimar Republic. His legacy is defined by his military leadership on the Eastern Front and his pivotal, controversial role in the political crisis of 1933, when he appointed Adolf Hitler as Chancellor, enabling the Nazi seizure of power.

## Timeline
- **2 October 1847** — Born in Posen, Kingdom of Prussia.
- **1866** — Fights in the Austro-Prussian War; wounded at the Battle of Königgrätz.
- **1870–71** — Serves in the Franco-Prussian War; present at the proclamation of the German Empire.
- **1 November 1914** — Promoted to field marshal after the Battle of Tannenberg.
- **29 August 1916** — Appointed Chief of the Great General Staff, becoming de facto military dictator.
- **25 June 1919** — Retires from the army after World War I.
- **12 May 1925** — Inaugurated as the second President of the Weimar Republic.
- **30 March 1930** — Appoints Heinrich Brüning as chancellor, beginning the era of presidential government.
- **30 January 1933** — Appoints Adolf Hitler as Chancellor of Germany.
- **2 August 1934** — Dies at his estate in Neudeck, East Prussia.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Career
Born to Prussian nobility, Hindenburg entered the Prussian Army as a cadet. He saw combat in the Austro-Prussian (1866) and Franco-Prussian (1870–71) wars. After graduating from the Prussian War College, he served on the General Staff, where he studied under Count Alfred von Schlieffen. He rose through the ranks, commanding corps and divisions, before retiring in 1911.

### World War I: Eastern Front and Supreme Command
Recalled at the war's outbreak, Hindenburg was given command of the German Eighth Army in East Prussia. With his chief of staff, Erich Ludendorff, he orchestrated the decisive Battle of Tannenberg (1914), destroying the Russian Second Army. This victory, along with subsequent successes, made him a national hero. In 1916, he replaced Erich von Falkenhayn as Chief of the Great General Staff. Together with Ludendorff, he established a de facto military dictatorship, directing the war effort. They oversaw Germany's victory on the Eastern Front and implemented new defensive tactics and the Hindenburg Line in the West. However, their leadership also led to Germany's defeat in 1918. They informed Kaiser Wilhelm II the war was lost and advised an armistice.

### The Weimar Republic and Rise to the Presidency
After the war, Hindenburg retired but remained a potent symbol. In 1925, he was elected President of the Weimar Republic, a role he accepted reluctantly to preserve national unity. He supported the Locarno Treaties (1925) but denied Germany's sole responsibility for WWI. As the Reichstag became increasingly fragmented, Hindenburg and his advisors, notably Kurt von Schleicher, increasingly used Article 48 of the constitution to govern by presidential decree, undermining parliamentary democracy.

### Appointment of Hitler and the End of the Republic
Facing political deadlock and the rising Nazi Party, Hindenburg initially refused Hitler's demand for the chancellorship. However, in January 1933, under pressure from advisors, he appointed Hitler as chancellor in a coalition government. After the Reichstag fire, Hindenburg signed the Reichstag Fire Decree, suspending civil liberties. He also signed the Enabling Act of 1933, which gave Hitler's government dictatorial powers. Though he privately distrusted Hitler, Hindenburg approved of the Nazi regime's nationalist goals and its suppression of the Social Democrats. Upon Hindenburg's death in 1934, Hitler merged the offices of president and chancellor, becoming the undisputed dictator of Germany.

## Terms
*   **Ober Ost:** The name for the combined German military command on the Eastern Front, which Hindenburg led from late 1915. It also referred to the vast occupied territory under its administration.
*   **Schwerpunkt:** A German military doctrine meaning "focal point," involving concentrating overwhelming force at a specific point to achieve a breakthrough, as used at Tannenberg.
*   **Article 48:** A provision in the Weimar Constitution that allowed the president to rule by emergency decree, bypassing the Reichstag. Its frequent use under Hindenburg weakened democracy.
*   **Enabling Act (1933):** A law passed by the Reichstag that gave Hitler's cabinet the power to enact laws without parliamentary consent, effectively ending the Weimar Republic.
*   **Volksgemeinschaft:** A Nazi concept of a "people's community" based on racial purity and national unity, which Hindenburg supported as a goal for Germany.
*   **Stab-in-the-back myth (Dolchstoßlegende):** The false theory, promoted by Hindenburg and others, that the German army was not defeated militarily in 1918 but was betrayed by civilians, socialists, and Jews on the home front.
*   **Freikorps:** Right-wing paramilitary units composed largely of demobilized soldiers, which the army supported to suppress revolutionary uprisings after WWI.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Hindenburg vs. Ludendorff:** Historians debate the extent of Hindenburg's command authority during WWI. While Ludendorff's memoirs portray him as the dominant strategist, contemporary accounts and postwar testimonies suggest Hindenburg set overall objectives and managed his subordinates, though Ludendorff handled day-to-day operations.
*   **Responsibility for Hitler's Appointment:** Scholars analyze whether Hindenburg was a senile figurehead manipulated by advisors like Schleicher, or an active, if reluctant, participant who believed he could control Hitler and use the Nazis to achieve conservative and nationalist goals.
*   **The Stab-in-the-Back Myth:** Hindenburg's 1919 testimony before the Reichstag commission, which blamed the defeat on home-front betrayal, was crucial in legitimizing this myth, which became a cornerstone of right-wing and Nazi propaganda against the Weimar Republic.
