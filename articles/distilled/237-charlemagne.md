# Charlemagne

## Overview
Charlemagne (2 April 748 – 28 January 814), a member of the Carolingian dynasty, united most of Western and Central Europe through conquest and diplomacy, becoming King of the Franks (768), King of the Lombards (774), and the first Emperor in the West since 476 (800). His reign fused Germanic kingship, Roman imperial tradition, and Christian universalism, establishing administrative, legal, and educational reforms—collectively termed the Carolingian Renaissance—that structured medieval European governance for centuries. The coronation by Pope Leo III created a rival imperial claim to Byzantium, initiating the "problem of two emperors." Though his empire fragmented rapidly after his death under the Treaty of Verdun (843), the political structures and the ideal of a unified Christendom endured, making him a foundational figure for both French and German monarchies and a later symbol of European integration.

## Body

### Political Background and Rise to Power
The Franks, Christianised under Clovis I, established the Merovingian kingdom in Gaul. By the 7th century, Merovingian power waned due to partible inheritance and aristocratic feuding, elevating the Mayors of the Palace. Pepin of Herstal (victor at Tertry, 687) and his son Charles Martel consolidated authority; Martel left the throne vacant (737–743) and divided the realm between his sons Carloman and Pepin the Short. Pepin deposed the last Merovingian, Childeric III, in 751, securing papal legitimation via Stephen II’s anointing (754). Charlemagne, born c. 748 to Pepin and Bertrada of Laon, received military training but his literacy remains debated (Einhard notes he attempted writing late in life). He first issued charters in 760 and campaigned in Aquitaine. Upon Pepin’s death (768), Charlemagne and Carloman inherited separate spheres (Austrasia vs. Burgundy/Provence/Aquitaine). Tensions flared over Aquitaine and Lombard policy; Carloman’s sudden death (771) left Charlemagne sole ruler, prompting him to repudiate his Lombard wife and marry Hildegard, securing the support of Count Gerold in Carloman’s former lands.

### Conquest of Lombardy and the Saxon Wars
Pope Adrian I (772) appealed for aid against Lombard King Desiderius, who held papal cities and sheltered Carloman’s heirs. Charlemagne besieged Pavia (773–774), captured Verona, and absorbed the Lombard kingdom—an "extraordinary" peaceful annexation facilitated by Lombard elective custom and elite acquiescence. He took the title King of the Lombards. Simultaneously, he launched over 30 years of war against the Saxons (beginning 772 with the destruction of the Irminsul). The conflict intensified after Widukind’s rebellion (782), culminating in the Massacre of Verden (4,500 beheaded) and the *Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae*, a legal code imposing death for paganism to erase Saxon identity. Widukind submitted in 785 (baptised with Charlemagne as godfather), but annual campaigns of devastation and forced deportations continued until 799, prompting Charlemagne to establish his court at Aachen for frontier access.

### Spanish March and Eastern Frontiers
In 777, Iberian dissidents (Sulayman al-Arabi, allies of Yusuf al-Fihri) sought Frankish aid against the Emirate of Córdoba. Charlemagne crossed the Pyrenees but suffered a Basque ambush at Roncevaux Pass (778) during withdrawal. His son Louis later captured Barcelona (801), securing the Spanish March. In Italy, Charlemagne subdued Benevento (787) and repelled a Byzantine-Avar alliance. He deposed his cousin, Bavarian Duke Tassilo III (788), annexing Bavaria after accusations of conspiring with the Avars and Widukind. From Bavaria, he crushed the Avar Khaganate (791–796), expanding Frankish rule eastward. Diplomatically, he exchanged envoys with Abbasid Caliph Harun al-Rashid (receiving the elephant Abul-Abbas, 802) and protected exiled Anglo-Saxon rulers, treating their kingdoms as "satellite states."

### Imperial Coronation and Byzantine Relations
Pope Leo III, attacked by Roman rivals (799), fled to Charlemagne. At Christmas 800 mass in St. Peter’s, Leo crowned Charlemagne *Imperator Romanorum*. Historians debate Charlemagne’s foreknowledge (Einhard claims surprise; Fried argues planning by 798) and the title’s meaning: a vacancy created by Empress Irene’s usurpation (Annals of Lorsch), a bid for Italian legitimacy (Collins), or a tool to integrate Saxons (Mayr-Harting). Charlemagne avoided the style "Emperor of the Romans," preferring "Emperor governing the Roman Empire" to placate Byzantium. Nikephoros I (802) refused recognition, leading to naval conflict over Venice/Istria. Peace came in 811–812 under Michael I, who recognised Charlemagne as emperor (though not "of the Romans").

### Governance, Law, and Succession
Charlemagne ruled via itinerant courts until c. 800, then sedentary from Aachen. He expanded the *missi dominici* (royal envoys) into paired cleric-lay teams (*Capitulare missorum generale*, 802) to oversee justice and loyalty oaths. He ordered revisions of Frankish and Lombard law codes and issued capitularies on administration, coinage, and grain prices. The *Divisio Regnorum* (806) partitioned the realm: Charles the Younger received Francia/Saxony; Pepin, Italy/Bavaria; Louis, Aquitaine/Provence. The imperial title was unaddressed. After the deaths of Charles and Pepin (810–811), Charlemagne designated Louis as sole heir and crowned him co-emperor (813) without papal involvement.

### Carolingian Renaissance
Stability, Mediterranean contacts, and foreign scholars (Alcuin, Theodulf, Peter of Pisa) drove a cultural revival. Charlemagne mandated clerical education (*Admonitio generalis*, *Epistola de litteris colendis*), standardised Latin (creating a trans-regional literary language), and promoted the *Carolingian minuscule* script (basis for modern type). Scriptoria produced ~90,000 9th-century manuscripts. Palace schools taught liberal arts; rural priests were expected to provide basic literacy and catechesis. The *Libri Carolini* (794) opposed Byzantine iconoclasm and the *filioque* clause was defended at Aachen (809), deepening the East–West theological rift.

### Religious Policy
Charlemagne held 23 synods, imposing the *Dionysio-Hadriana* canon law, the Rule of St. Benedict, and a Roman-Frankish liturgy. He enforced vernacular preaching, the Lord’s Prayer, and the Creed. Noble argues the "Roman Catholic Church as a historical phenomenon... is a Carolingian construction," creating a unified *Christendom* over local traditions. His *filioque* advocacy and rejection of Nicaea II (787) accelerated schism with Constantinople.

### Death and Dynastic Collapse
Charlemagne died of pleurisy (28 Jan 814) after a year of omens (eclipse, palace collapse). Louis the Pious succeeded but faced civil wars with his sons. The Treaty of Verdun (843) split the empire into West, Middle, and East Francia. Carolingians ruled West Francia until 987 and East Francia until 911. Otto I revived the imperial title (962), founding the Holy Roman Empire (dissolved 1806).

## Terms
- ****Carolingian Dynasty**** — Frankish noble family founded by Charles Martel; ruled Francia 751–987 (West) / 911 (East).
- ****Mayor of the Palace**** — Chief steward of the Merovingian court; became effective ruler as royal power declined.
- ****Partible Inheritance**** — Frankish custom dividing realm among all sons; caused fragmentation under Merovingians.
- ****Missi Dominici**** — Royal envoys (paired cleric and lay lord) sent to enforce justice and loyalty in local districts.
- ****Capitulary**** — Legislative act issued by Carolingian rulers, often in chapters (*capitula*).
- ****Carolingian Minuscule**** — Standardised, legible script developed c. 780; basis for Renaissance humanist and modern type.
- ****Spanish March**** — Frankish military frontier zone south of Pyrenees (incl. Barcelona), buffer against al-Andalus.
- ****Filioque**** — Latin clause ("and the Son") added to Nicene Creed in West; rejected by East; theological flashpoint.
- ****Divisio Regnorum**** — 806 charter dividing empire among Charlemagne’s three legitimate sons.
- ****Holy Roman Empire**** — Entity founded/revived by Otto I (962) claiming succession to Charlemagne; lasted until 1806.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Birth Date**: Sources conflict (742 vs. 747/748); most scholars now accept 2 April 748 based on Easter-style annal reckoning.
- **Literacy**: Einhard says Charlemagne tried to learn writing late; Fried argues he could read; Dutton calls evidence "circumstantial and inferential at best."
- **Coronation Intent**: Was Charlemagne surprised (Einhard) or the planner (Fried, Collins)? Did Leo act to fill a "vacancy" (Irene’s gender) or to secure a protector?
- **Imperial Title Meaning**: Universal Christian monarchy (Pirenne) vs. Italian legitimacy (Collins) vs. Saxon integration tool (Mayr-Harting).
- **Massacre of Verden Scale**: Annals cite 4,500; Fried accepts "basic truth" but notes possible exaggeration; Barbero calls it "greatest stain."
- **Pepin the Hunchback’s Legitimacy**: Born to Himiltrude; Paul the Deacon says "before legal marriage"; Pope Stephen called it legitimate; later court treated him as illegitimate.
- **Nationality/legacy**: 19th-century German vs. French historiography (butcher of Saxons vs. Father of Europe); Nazi appropriation vs. post-WWII European integration symbol.
