# Knossos

## Overview
Knossos is an archaeological site on the island of Crete, Greece, and the largest centre of the Minoan civilisation, widely regarded as Europe's earliest advanced civilisation. Dominated by the monumental Palace of Minos, it was a major administrative, economic, and religious hub from the Neolithic period through the Bronze Age. Its long occupation and association with Greek myths like the Labyrinth and the Minotaur make it a cornerstone for understanding Aegean prehistory and the foundations of European urban life.

## Timeline
- **c. 7000 BC** — Earliest known Neolithic settlement founded on Kephala hill.
- **c. 1900 BC** — First palace constructed, marking the start of the First Palace period.
- **c. 1700 BC** — First palace destroyed, likely by an earthquake.
- **c. 1700–1450 BC** — Second Palace period; the palace is rebuilt on a grander scale, the height of Minoan power.
- **c. 1450 BC** — Palace partially destroyed during a wider period of disruption; Mycenaean Greek influence increases.
- **c. 1350 BC** — Final destruction of the palace by fire, ending the palatial phase.
- **1878–1879** — First modern excavation by Minos Kalokairinos.
- **1900** — Arthur Evans begins large-scale, systematic excavations.
- **2025** — Inscribed as part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site "Minoan Palatial Centres."

## Body

### Neolithic to Prepalatial Foundations
The settlement at Knossos began around 7000 BC, making it one of the oldest long-lived settlements in the Aegean. For millennia, it was a farming community that grew in size and complexity. By the Early Bronze Age (c. 3000–1900 BC), known as the Prepalatial period, Knossos showed increased craft specialisation and social differentiation, setting the stage for palatial organisation.

### The Palace of Minos
The first palace was built c. 1900 BC and functioned as a centre for administration, storage, and ritual. After its destruction c. 1700 BC, a larger, more elaborate palace was constructed. This Second Palace period (c. 1700–1450 BC) was the zenith of Minoan Knossos. The complex, covering about 14,000 m², was arranged around a central court and featured advanced engineering, including drainage systems, light wells, and multi-storey construction. Its west wing contained extensive storage magazines and the Throne Room complex, whose function (royal or ritual) is debated. The palace's intricate plan of corridors and rooms likely inspired the later myth of the Labyrinth.

### Administration, Writing, and Mycenaean Rule
Knossos was a bureaucratic centre using writing systems like Cretan hieroglyphs and Linear A (undeciphered). After c. 1450 BC, Mycenaean Greeks took control, and the palace produced an archive of Linear B tablets. These tablets, written in an early form of Greek, record administrative details about livestock, textiles, and personnel, providing crucial evidence for Late Bronze Age governance. The palace was finally destroyed by fire c. 1350 BC.

### Post-Palatial and Historical Periods
After the palace's fall, Knossos remained an important settlement. In the Early Iron Age, it was a significant community with prosperous cemeteries. During the Archaic and Classical periods, it was a major Cretan city-state, issuing coinage and linked to myths of King Minos. It continued as a Roman city, known as Colonia Iulia Nobilis Cnossus, before declining as Heraklion rose to prominence.

### Archaeology and Mythology
Modern archaeology began with Minos Kalokairinos in 1878, but Arthur Evans's excavations from 1900 onward defined the site. Evans uncovered the palace, introduced the term "Minoan," and undertook extensive reconstructions that are now part of the site's modern history but are sometimes criticised for going beyond secure evidence. Knossos is inextricably linked to Greek myths, including King Minos, the Minotaur, and Daedalus. The palace's famous frescoes, like the Bull-Leaping Fresco, and artefacts like the snake goddess figurines are iconic, though their interpretations are often debated due to their fragmentary state.

## Terms
- **Minoan civilisation**: The Bronze Age civilisation of Crete, centred on palaces like Knossos, considered the first advanced civilisation in Europe.
- **Palace**: A monumental complex interpreted as an administrative, economic, and religious centre, not necessarily a royal residence.
- **Linear B**: An undeciphered script used for administrative records in the Minoan and Mycenaean periods.
- **Linear B**: A syllabic script used for administrative records in the Mycenaean period, deciphered as an early form of Greek.
- **Pithoi**: Large ceramic storage jars used in palace magazines.
- **Lustral basin**: A sunken area within a building, often accessed by stairs, thought to have ritual significance.
- **Labrys**: A double axe, a common Minoan symbol whose possible linguistic link to "labyrinth" is debated.
- **Prepalatial period**: The Early Bronze Age phase (c. 3000–1900 BC) before the construction of the first palaces.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The primary function of the palace is debated: was it primarily a royal residence, a ceremonial/religious centre, or an administrative hub?
- The purpose of the Throne Room complex is uncertain, with interpretations ranging from a king's seat to a priestly ritual space.
- The connection between the word "labyrinth," the labrys (double axe), and the palace's complex plan is a subject of scholarly debate.
- The extent and accuracy of Arthur Evans's early 20th-century reconstructions, which define the visitor experience today, remain a point of discussion in archaeology.
