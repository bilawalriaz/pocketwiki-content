# Supply and demand

## Overview  
Supply and demand is the foundational economic model explaining how prices and quantities are determined in perfectly competitive markets. It holds that, all else equal, the price of a good adjusts until the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied, reaching market-clearing equilibrium. While central to microeconomics, the model faces theoretical limits at the macro level, as shown by the Sonnenschein-Mantel-Debreu theorem, which demonstrates that aggregate demand may not behave like individual demand.

## Timeline  
- **c. 2000 BCE** — Tirukkural contains early articulation of supply-demand logic
- **1691** — John Locke discusses price determination via buyer-seller ratios
- **1755** — Francis Hutcheson links price to demand and acquisition difficulty
- **1767** — James Denham-Steuart first uses phrase "supply and demand"
- **1776** — Adam Smith publishes *The Wealth of Nations*
- **1803** — Thomas Robert Malthus uses "supply and demand" extensively
- **1817** — David Ricardo titles chapter "On the Influence of Demand and Supply on Price"
- **1838** — Augustin Cournot develops mathematical supply-demand model, including diagrams
- **1870** — Fleeming Jenkin publishes first drawing of supply and demand curves in English, including comparative statics from shifts and application to labor market
- **1890** — Alfred Marshall further develops and popularizes supply-demand model in *Principles of Economics*

## Body  

### Graphical Representations  
A **supply schedule** (or supply curve) shows how much producers are willing to sell at each price, derived under perfect competition where firms are price-takers. Supply depends on marginal cost: firms produce more when price exceeds cost. Input prices, technology, expectations, and firm count shift the curve. Two common functional forms are linear (e.g., Q = 3P − 6) and constant-elasticity (e.g., Q = 5P^0.5). The **demand schedule** (or demand curve) shows how much consumers buy at each price, driven by marginal utility. It typically slopes downward per the **law of demand**, though exceptions exist: **Veblen goods** (luxury items more desirable at higher prices) and **Giffen goods** (inferior staples whose demand rises with price due to income effects). Linear (e.g., Q = 32 − 2P) and constant-elasticity (e.g., Q = 3P⁻²) are standard forms.

### Equilibrium  
Equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, represented by the intersection of supply and demand curves. **Market equilibrium** clears the market—no surplus or shortage. **Comparative statics** analyzes how shifts in either curve alter equilibrium price and quantity. Demand increases shift the curve rightward, raising both price and quantity; supply increases (e.g., from tech progress) lower price and raise quantity. Movements along curves reflect price changes; shifts reflect non-price determinants.

### Partial Equilibrium  
**Partial equilibrium** focuses on one market in isolation, holding other prices and incomes constant. This simplifies analysis but ignores cross-market effects. It contrasts with **general equilibrium**, which models entire economies simultaneously. Partial equilibrium is useful for small markets or policy analysis where spillovers are negligible.

### Other Markets  
The model extends beyond goods to labor markets (wages as price), money markets (interest rates), and biological systems. In labor markets, supply and demand determine wages, though empirical support is mixed. In money markets, central banks may fix supply (vertical) or interest rates (horizontal). Applications also include physician, nursing, and teacher shortages.

### Empirical Estimation  
Demand and supply can be statistically estimated using **simultaneous-equation methods** in econometrics. **Structural estimation** identifies causal relationships but faces the **parameter identification problem**, requiring exogenous variables. **Reduced-form estimation** regresses endogenous variables on exogenous ones, offering simpler but less theoretically grounded results.

### Macroeconomic Uses  
At the macro level, **aggregate demand-aggregate supply (AD-AS)** models apply supply-demand logic to total output and price levels. However, the **Sonnenschein-Mantel-Debreu theorem** shows aggregate demand need not slope downward, undermining assumptions of unique, stable equilibrium. This challenges the use of simple supply-demand frameworks for macroeconomic prediction.

### Criticism  
Critics like **Piero Sraffa** questioned partial equilibrium’s assumptions, especially the upward-sloping supply curve. **Post-Keynesians** argue the model fails to explain **administered prices**, where firms set prices via markups rather than market forces. The SMD theorem further undermines general equilibrium theory by showing aggregation breaks key microeconomic properties.

## Terms  
- **Market-clearing price**: Price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.  
- **Perfect competition**: Market structure with many buyers/sellers, no market power, and full information.  
- **Marginal cost**: Cost of producing one additional unit.  
- **Law of demand**: Inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded, ceteris paribus.  
- **Elasticity**: Responsiveness of quantity to price changes; constant-elasticity functions assume fixed responsiveness.  
- **Comparative statics**: Method comparing equilibria before and after a change in determinants.  
- **Partial equilibrium**: Analysis of one market assuming others remain unchanged.  
- **General equilibrium**: Simultaneous analysis of all markets in an economy.  
- **Parameter identification problem**: Challenge in distinguishing structural parameters from reduced-form estimates.  
- **Sonnenschein-Mantel-Debreu theorem**: Result showing aggregate demand may not inherit individual rationality properties.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- Can aggregate demand reliably slope downward, or does the SMD theorem invalidate this assumption?  
- Is partial equilibrium analysis valid when markets are interdependent?  
- Do real-world prices reflect competitive equilibrium or firm-administered markups?  
- Are supply and demand curves empirically observable, particularly in labor markets?  
- Should macroeconomic models rely on microfoundations given aggregation failures?
