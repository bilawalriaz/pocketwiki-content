# Fiji

## Overview
Fiji is an archipelagic nation in the South Pacific, part of Melanesia, comprising over 330 islands. Its history is marked by indigenous settlement, European contact, British colonial rule, and a complex post-independence political landscape characterized by ethnic tensions and military coups. The country's economy is one of the most developed in the Pacific, heavily reliant on tourism and sugar exports.

## Timeline
- **c. 3500–1000 BC** — Settlement by Austronesian peoples begins.
- **1643** — Dutch explorer Abel Tasman becomes the first known European to sight Fiji.
- **1874** — Fiji is ceded to the United Kingdom by Seru Epenisa Cakobau and other chiefs, becoming a Crown colony.
- **1879** — The first Indian indentured labourers arrive to work on sugar plantations.
- **1970** — Fiji gains independence from the UK as the Dominion of Fiji.
- **1987** — Two military coups lead to the declaration of a republic and the dominance of ethnic Fijians in politics.
- **2000** — A coup led by George Speight topples the first Indo-Fijian-led government.
- **2006** — Commodore Frank Bainimarama stages a military coup.
- **2009** — The constitution is abrogated after a court rules the 2006 coup government unlawful; Bainimarama is reappointed.
- **2014** — A democratic election is held, won by Bainimarama's FijiFirst party.
- **2022** — Sitiveni Rabuka becomes Prime Minister after elections, ending Bainimarama's rule.

## Body

### Early History and European Contact
Fiji was settled by Austronesian peoples by at least 3500–1000 BC, followed by Melanesians. By the 10th century, it was within the sphere of the Tu'i Tonga Empire. The first European sighting was by Abel Tasman in 1643. Sustained contact began in the late 18th century with sandalwood traders, whalers, and missionaries. In the 1840s, the warlord Seru Epenisa Cakobau rose to prominence, eventually converting to Christianity in 1854 after pressure from foreign powers and Tongan allies.

### Colonial Era and Conflict
Cakobau's government, established in 1871, faced internal rebellions from the Kai Colo (highland clans) and pressure from white settlers seeking land. To subdue the Kai Colo, Cakobau's forces, later backed by the British, waged campaigns in the 1870s. Amidst economic problems and land disputes, Cakobau ceded Fiji to Britain in 1874. A devastating measles epidemic in 1875–76 killed about one-third of the Fijian population. Governor Sir Arthur Gordon suppressed the "Little War" insurgency (1875–76), established a system of indirect rule through chiefs, and in 1878 began importing indentured labourers from India to work on sugar plantations. Resistance movements like the Tuka emerged but were ruthlessly suppressed.

### Independence and Political Instability
Fiji gained independence in 1970 with a constitution balancing ethnic Fijian and Indo-Fijian interests. Democratic rule was interrupted by military coups in 1987, which institutionalized ethnic Fijian political dominance and led to significant Indo-Fijian emigration. A 1997 constitution restored multi-party democracy, but a 2000 coup by George Speight overthrew the first Indo-Fijian-led government. Commodore Frank Bainimarama seized power in a 2006 coup, citing corruption. After a court ruled his government unlawful in 2009, the constitution was abrogated. Bainimarama's FijiFirst party won elections in 2014 and 2018, but lost its majority in 2022, leading to a change in government.

### Economy and Society
Fiji has a mixed economy with a large subsistence sector. Key industries are tourism, sugar processing, gold mining, and garment manufacturing. The population is predominantly native Fijian (iTaukei, 54.3%) and Indo-Fijian (38.1%), with the latter's percentage declining due to emigration. English, Fijian, and Fiji Hindi are official languages. Christianity is the dominant religion (69.2%), with Hinduism (24.0%) and Islam (5.8%) also significant. Rugby union is the most popular sport.

## Terms
*   **iTaukei:** The Fijian-language endonym for indigenous Fijians.
*   **Indo-Fijians:** Citizens of Fiji descended from Indian indentured labourers brought by the British in the 19th century.
*   **Kai Colo:** A general term for the various Fijian clans resident in the inland, mountainous districts of Viti Levu who resisted coastal and colonial control.
*   **Lotu:** The process of enforced cultural and religious change to Christianity under colonial influence.
*   **Tuka:** A series of indigenous Fijian resistance movements against colonial control, preaching a return to traditional ways.
*   **Blackbirding:** The coercion or kidnapping of people from the Pacific Islands to work as labourers, often under deceptive or violent conditions.
*   **Vunivalu:** A chiefly title meaning warlord or paramount chief, held by Seru Epenisa Cakobau.
*   **Great Council of Chiefs:** A body established by Governor Gordon in 1876, composed of appointed chiefs to enact colonial orders and advise the governor; it was abolished in 2012.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The accuracy and extent of 19th-century accounts of Fijian cannibalism are disputed by scholars, with some archaeological evidence supporting the practice but its interpretation remaining debated.
*   The failure to quarantine the ship carrying Cakobau's sons back from Sydney in 1875, which led to a measles epidemic killing ~40,000 Fijians, is alleged by some to have been a deliberate act, though historians have found no evidence for this.
*   The political and social implications of defining "Fijian" as a civic term for all citizens versus an ethnic term for indigenous Fijians remain a source of tension.
