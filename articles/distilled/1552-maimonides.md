# Maimonides

## Overview
Moses ben Maimon (1135/1138–1204), known as Maimonides or by the Hebrew acronym Rambam, was a Sephardic Jewish rabbi, philosopher, physician, and legal codifier whose work defined medieval Jewish thought. Born in Córdoba during the twilight of the Jewish Golden Age in Spain, his family fled Almohad persecution in 1148, eventually settling in Fustat, Egypt. There he served as *nagid* (head) of the Jewish community and court physician to the Ayyubid Sultan Saladin. His magnum opus, the fourteen-volume *Mishneh Torah*, systematized *halakha* (Jewish law) and remains a canonical legal authority. In *The Guide for the Perplexed*, he reconciled Aristotelian philosophy with Torah, arguing that reason and revelation cannot contradict. His Thirteen Principles of Faith became the cardinal creed of Orthodox Judaism. Maimonides’ synthesis of Greek science, Islamic philosophy, and Jewish tradition made him a pivotal figure in both the Islamic Golden Age and Western Scholasticism, influencing Thomas Aquinas and modern bioethics alike.

## Body

### Early Life and Exile
Maimonides was born into a distinguished rabbinic family in Córdoba at the end of the "Golden Age" of Jewish culture in Muslim Spain. His father, Maimon ben Joseph, a *dayyan* (rabbinic judge), educated him in Torah and the teachings of Isaac Alfasi. The 1148 Almohad conquest abolished *dhimmi* status (protected non-Muslim standing secured by *jizya* tax), forcing Jews to choose conversion, death, or exile. The family chose exile, wandering through southern Spain and North Africa for a decade. During this instability, Maimonides mastered Greek philosophy via Arabic translations and Islamic sciences, laying the groundwork for his later rationalism.

### Settlement in Egypt and Communal Leadership
By 1168, the family settled in Fustat under the Fatimid Caliphate. Maimonides distinguished himself by organizing the ransom of Jews captured during King Amalric of Jerusalem’s siege of Bilbeis, coordinating funds across communities and negotiating with Crusaders. This leadership led to his appointment as *nagid* (head of Egyptian Jewry) c. 1171. He was deposed in 1173 in favor of Sar Shalom ben Moses, whom he opposed for alleged tax farming. After years of excommunications and polemics—recorded in Abraham bar Hillel’s *Megillat Zutta*—Maimonides regained the post in 1195. His son Abraham succeeded him, establishing a four-generation Maimonidean dynasty in the office.

### Medical Career and Court Service
The loss of family capital in his brother David’s failed India trade venture compelled Maimonides to practice medicine professionally. Trained in Spain and Fez, he followed Galenic humorism but emphasized empirical observation over blind authority. He became physician to Qadi al-Fadil, vizier to Sultan Saladin, and subsequently to Saladin and the Ayyubid dynasty. His famous letter describes an exhausting daily routine: mornings at the palace, afternoons treating throngs of patients (Jews and gentiles) until nightfall, even on Shabbat. His treatises on asthma, diabetes, hepatitis, poisons, and regimen (*Regimen Sanitatis*) stressed preventive care, hygiene, and the mind-body connection, influencing physicians for centuries.

### Philosophical Project: Reason and Revelation
Maimonides’ philosophy centers on the Scholastic axiom that divine revelation and human reason cannot conflict. Educated primarily through Arab Muslim philosophers—al-Farabi, ibn Sina (Avicenna), and ibn Rushd (Averroes)—he sought to harmonize Aristotle with Torah.
*   **Negative Theology:** In *The Guide for the Perplexed*, he adopts apophatic theology: God can only be described by what He is *not* (e.g., not ignorant rather than wise; not non-existent rather than existing). Anthropomorphic biblical language (God’s "anger," "hand") is metaphorical, designed to wean Israelites from idolatry.
*   **Creation and Miracles:** God is the Necessary Being; the universe operates via fixed natural laws (identified with angels/Platonic forms). Miracles are not violations of nature but rare, pre-ordained events within the natural order.
*   **Prophecy and Intellect:** Prophecy is the perfection of the rational faculty combined with imagination, attainable through study—though God may withhold it. The "Active Intellect" (Aristotle’s *noûs poietikós*) emanates to the perfected soul, granting immortality.

### Theology and Ethics
*   **Thirteen Principles of Faith:** Formulated in his *Commentary on the Mishnah* (Sanhedrin 10), these include God’s existence, unity, incorporeality, eternity; exclusive worship; prophecy; Mosaic supremacy; Torah’s divine origin and immutability; divine omniscience; reward/punishment; Messiah; and bodily resurrection. Initially controversial (critiqued by Crescas and Albo), they were later canonized in liturgy (*Ani Ma’amin*, *Yigdal*).
*   **Theodicy:** Evil is not a positive creation but the *absence of good* (privation). Most evil is self-inflicted through unchecked physical desires; natural evil (death) is rare and necessary for cosmic balance. Human perception of evil’s dominance stems from anthropocentric myopia.
*   **Tzedakah Hierarchy:** An eight-rung ladder of charity, ascending from grudging giving to enabling self-sufficiency (gift/loan/partnership). The highest form preserves the recipient’s dignity and independence.
*   **True vs. Necessary Beliefs:** "True beliefs" perfect the intellect (e.g., God’s incorporeality). "Necessary beliefs" maintain social order (e.g., God’s "anger" deters sin), even if philosophically false.

### Eschatology: Resurrection and the World to Come
Maimonides affirmed bodily resurrection as a fundamental dogma (Principle 13) but interpreted it narrowly: it is temporary, not universal, and the resurrected eventually die again. The ultimate *Olam Ha-Ba* (World to Come) is purely spiritual—disembodied souls enjoying the "radiance of the Divine Presence" without physical needs. Immortality is achieved by perfecting the intellect through knowledge of God, a path of *halakhic* duty rather than philosophical intuition alone (contra Spinoza). This nuanced view sparked the "Resurrection Controversy," prompting his *Treatise on Resurrection* to refute claims he denied it allegorically.

### Relationship to Kabbalah
Maimonides was not a Kabbalist. *The Guide* explicitly conceals *Sod* (esoteric Torah secrets) from the uninitiated. Mainstream scholarship views his Aristotelian rationalism as a metaphysics mutually exclusive with Kabbalah’s theosophy. However, his rationalism inadvertently aided Kabbalists by purging corporeal anthropomorphisms from earlier mystical texts (*Hekhalot* literature), even as Kabbalists insisted their theosophy alone accessed Divine mysteries.

### Major Works
**Jewish Law and Theology:**
*   *Mishneh Torah* (c. 1170–1180): 14-book Hebrew code of *halakha*, synthesizing Talmud and Geonic rulings. Initially opposed for lacking citations and appearing to supplant Talmud study, it became the benchmark for later codes (*Arba'ah Turim*, *Shulchan Aruch*). Joseph Karo ruled communities following the Rambam need not follow later decisors.
*   *Commentary on the Mishnah* (1166–1168, Arabic): First full commentary; includes three philosophical introductions (on Oral Law, Eschatology/13 Principles, Ethics/Eight Chapters).
*   *Sefer Hamitzvot*: Enumeration of 613 commandments with 14 methodological principles (*shorashim*).
*   *The Guide for the Perplexed* (1186–1190, Judeo-Arabic): Philosophical masterpiece reconciling Aristotle and Judaism; banned/burned by French rabbis in Montpellier.
*   *Teshuvot* (Responsa): Includes *Epistle to Yemen*, *Letter on Martyrdom*, *Treatise on Resurrection*.

**Medicine (10 Arabic works):**
*   *Regimen Sanitatis* (Guide to Good Health): Written for Sultan al-Afdal; first medical book printed in Florence (1477).
*   *Medical Aphorisms* (1,500 entries), *Treatise on Asthma*, *Treatise on Poisons*, *Glossary of Drug Names* (405 entries in 6 languages), and treatises on hemorrhoids, cohabitation, and Hippocrates/Galen commentaries.
*   *Oath of Maimonides* (likely later, 1793 print): Ethical medical pledge distinct from the Hippocratic Oath.

**Philosophy:**
*   *Treatise on Logic* (14 chapters): Lexicon of Aristotelian logical terms per al-Farabi. Authorship debated (Herbert Davidson questions it; Yosef Qafih affirms it).

## Terms
- ****Halakha**** — The collective body of Jewish religious laws derived from the Written and Oral Torah.
- ****Mishneh Torah**** — Maimonides’ 14-volume comprehensive code of Jewish law (Hebrew), also called *Yad ha-Chazaka* ("The Strong Hand," numerical value 14).
- ****Nagid**** — The official head of the Jewish community in Egypt, recognized by Islamic authorities.
- ****Dhimmi**** — Protected non-Muslim subject in Islamic law, granted communal autonomy in exchange for the *jizya* (poll tax).
- ****Apophatic Theology**** — "Negative theology": describing God only by what He is *not* (e.g., "not ignorant" vs. "wise"), since positive attributes imply composition/limitation.
- ****Active Intellect**** — (Aristotelian *noûs poietikós*) The universal, immaterial intellect emanating from God; conjunction with it perfects the human soul and grants immortality.
- ****Tzedakah**** — "Righteousness/Justice": the religious obligation of charity; Maimonides ranked eight levels, highest being enabling self-sufficiency.
- ****Olam Ha-Ba**** — "The World to Come": the final, purely spiritual state of immortal souls after the Messianic era and temporary resurrection.
- ****Sod**** — "Secret": the esoteric, mystical dimension of Torah interpretation (Kabbalah), which Maimonides concealed in *The Guide*.
- ****Geonim**** — Heads of Babylonian academies (c. 6th–11th centuries); post-Talmudic authorities whose rulings Maimonides incorporated.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Birth Date:** 1135 vs. 1138 (Passover Eve); sources disagree.
*   **Authorship of *Treatise on Logic*:** Herbert Davidson argues Maimonides did not write it based on Arabic manuscripts; Yosef Qafih and tradition affirm it.
*   **Resurrection vs. World to Come:** Whether Maimonides’ affirmation of temporary bodily resurrection contradicts his philosophical view of eternal, disembodied intellectual immortality. He claimed consistency via "miracles within nature," but critics (then and now) see tension.
*   **Thirteen Principles:** Did Maimonides view them as binding dogma (prescriptive) or a summary of existing belief (descriptive)? Their omission from *Mishneh Torah* and *Guide* fuels debate; Crescas and Albo challenged specific principles (e.g., resurrection, Messiah).
*   **Kabbalah Stance:** Was Maimonides strictly anti-mystical, or does *The Guide* contain a rationalist mysticism (intellectual conjunction with Active Intellect) functionally similar to Kabbalistic *devekut* (cleaving)?
*   **Medical Oath/Prayer:** The *Oath of Maimonides* (1793) and *Prayer of Maimonides* are likely later compositions (attributed to Markus Herz), not authentic.
*   **Burial:** Medieval sources claim reburial in Tiberias; no contemporary evidence confirms removal from Fustat. The Tiberias tomb inscription ("From Moses to Moses arose none like Moses") reflects the medieval epitaph.
