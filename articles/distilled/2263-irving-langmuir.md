# Irving Langmuir

## Overview
Irving Langmuir (1881–1957) was an American chemist, physicist, and engineer whose work at General Electric revolutionized surface chemistry, plasma physics, and materials science. He won the 1932 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his investigations of surface chemistry, which laid the foundation for modern thin-film technology and catalysis. His career bridged fundamental research and practical invention, from improved light bulbs to cloud seeding.

## Biography and Research

### Early Life and Education
Langmuir was born in Brooklyn and encouraged by his parents to observe nature carefully. His older brother Arthur, a research chemist, fostered his scientific curiosity. After attending schools in America and Paris, he graduated from Chestnut Hill Academy (1898) and earned a metallurgical engineering degree from Columbia (1903). He completed his Ph.D. at Göttingen in 1906 under Friedrich Dolezalek, studying gas recombination during cooling. He taught at Stevens Institute of Technology before joining General Electric in 1909.

### Work at General Electric
Langmuir’s early research focused on improving incandescent light bulbs. He enhanced the diffusion pump, leading to high-vacuum tubes. With colleague Lewi Tonks, he discovered that filling bulbs with inert gas (like argon) greatly extended tungsten filament life, provided extreme cleanliness was maintained. He also found that coiling filaments improved efficiency. His surface chemistry work began when he observed hydrogen dissociating into atoms and forming a single-atom-thick layer on tungsten.

### Plasma Physics and Welding
Langmuir pioneered plasma research, coining the term "plasma" for ionized gases. He and Tonks discovered electron density waves (Langmuir waves). In 1924, he invented the Langmuir probe to measure plasma temperature and density. He also developed atomic hydrogen welding, the first plasma-based welding process, now evolved into gas tungsten arc welding.

### Surface Chemistry and Nobel Prize
In 1917, Langmuir theorized that oil molecules form oriented monolayers (single-molecule-thick films) on water, with hydrophilic heads in the water and hydrophobic tails exposed. This work, later expanded with Katharine Blodgett on thin films and adsorption, earned him the 1932 Nobel Prize "for his discoveries and investigations in surface chemistry."

### Later Work and Pathological Science
After WWI, Langmuir contributed to atomic theory, defining valence shells and isotopes. In the 1930s, he shifted to atmospheric science, discovering Langmuir circulation (wind-driven sea surface patterns). During WWII, he worked on sonar and de-icing, leading to cloud seeding experiments with ice nuclei like dry ice and silver iodide—a technique whose efficiency remains debated. In 1953, he coined "pathological science" to describe research flawed by unconscious bias, citing ESP and flying saucers as examples.

### Personal Life
Langmuir married Marion Mersereau in 1912; they adopted two children. He was an agnostic and enjoyed mountaineering, skiing, and classical music. He died in 1957 from a heart attack.

## Terms
- **Monolayer**: A layer of material exactly one molecule thick, as in Langmuir’s oil films on water.
- **Plasma**: An ionized gas of free electrons and ions, a state of matter Langmuir studied and named.
- **Langmuir probe**: A diagnostic tool measuring plasma temperature and density via a biased electrode.
- **Thermionic emission**: The release of charged particles from a hot filament, studied by Langmuir.
- **Pathological science**: Research following scientific method but distorted by unconscious bias or subjective effects.
- **Cloud seeding**: Introducing nuclei (e.g., silver iodide) into clouds to induce precipitation.
- **Langmuir circulation**: Wind-driven, helical surface currents in oceans or lakes.
- **Valence shell**: The outermost electron shell of an atom, involved in chemical bonding.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Priority dispute**: Langmuir’s 1919 "concentric theory of atomic structure" built on Lewis’s cubical atom theory; credit for the theory itself is mostly attributed to Lewis, though Langmuir popularized it.
- **Cloud seeding efficiency**: The technique’s effectiveness in practice, especially in Australia and China, remains controversial.
- **Pathological science**: The concept is debated in terms of its boundaries and application to modern cases like cold fusion.
