# Washington, D.C.

## Overview
Washington, D.C., is the federal capital of the United States and its only federal district, established by the Constitution to be under exclusive congressional jurisdiction. It serves as the seat of the U.S. federal government and is a major world political capital, hosting federal headquarters, international organizations, and embassies. The city's unique status—neither a state nor part of one—has led to ongoing debates about its residents' voting rights and representation.

## Timeline
- **July 16, 1790** — Residence Act signed, approving creation of a national capital on the Potomac River.
- **1800** — Federal government moves from Philadelphia to the new, unfinished capital.
- **1801** — District of Columbia officially organized under exclusive federal control.
- **1814** — British forces burn federal buildings during the War of 1812.
- **1846** — Congress returns Virginia's ceded land (including Alexandria) to Virginia.
- **1862** — Slavery abolished in the district via the Compensated Emancipation Act.
- **1871** — Organic Act creates a single municipal government for the entire district.
- **1961** — 23rd Amendment grants district residents votes in the Electoral College.
- **1973** — Home Rule Act provides for an elected mayor and council.
- **2021** — House passes a statehood bill; it does not advance in the Senate.
- **August 11, 2025** — President Trump invokes federal control over the Metropolitan Police Department.

## Body

### Founding and Early History
The district was created by the Constitution (Article I, Section 8) to be a federal seat under congressional control, ensuring the national government's security and independence from any state. The Residence Act of 1790, part of the Compromise of 1790, approved its location on the Potomac River. President George Washington selected the exact site, and the city was designed by Pierre Charles L'Enfant. Land was ceded by Maryland and Virginia, forming a 100-square-mile square. The district was officially organized in 1801, removing its residents from state representation. Slavery existed from its founding, and the city became a major slave market.

### Growth, Civil War, and Redevelopment
The district's population and government expanded during the Civil War, and slavery was abolished there in 1862. In 1846, Congress returned Virginia's ceded land, reducing the district's size. The Organic Act of 1871 consolidated the district into a single municipality. In the early 20th century, the McMillan Plan aimed to beautify the capital and preserve L'Enfant's design. The New Deal and World War II spurred further federal construction and population growth, peaking at over 800,000 in 1950.

### Home Rule and Statehood Movement
The 23rd Amendment (1961) granted the district presidential electoral votes. The Home Rule Act of 1973 established an elected local government, though Congress retains oversight. Since the 1980s, a statehood movement has sought full congressional representation for district residents. A 2021 statehood bill passed the House but failed in the Senate. In 2025, President Trump invoked federal authority to take control of the city's police department, citing public safety concerns.

### Geography and Climate
The district is bordered by Maryland and Virginia, with the Potomac River forming its southwestern boundary. It has a humid subtropical climate with hot, humid summers and chilly winters. The city is known for its extensive park system, including Rock Creek Park, and the National Mall. Its low-rise skyline is regulated by the Height of Buildings Act of 1910.

### Demographics and Economy
As of the 2020 census, the population was 689,545, with a large African American community that has declined due to suburbanization and gentrification. The economy is anchored by the federal government, which employs 25% of the district's workforce. Tourism is the second-largest industry. The metropolitan area is a major economic hub with a high per capita income.

### Culture and Landmarks
Washington, D.C., is a center for the arts, home to the Kennedy Center and numerous theaters. It hosts the Smithsonian Institution's museums, most of which are free. The National Mall and Tidal Basin feature iconic monuments and memorials. The city has a diverse culinary scene and is known for its historic neighborhoods like Georgetown.

### Government and Politics
The district has a mayor-council government but is subject to congressional oversight. Residents lack voting representation in Congress, a status they pay federal taxes for. The city is overwhelmingly Democratic in presidential elections. Its budget faces structural deficits due to tax-exempt properties and a lack of commuter taxes.

## Terms
- **Federal District**: A unique political entity under the exclusive jurisdiction of the U.S. Congress, not part of any state.
- **Home Rule Act (1973)**: Legislation granting the district an elected mayor and council, while Congress retains final authority over its laws.
- **Retrocession**: The 1846 return of land ceded by Virginia to the district back to the state.
- **Height of Buildings Act (1910)**: A federal law limiting building heights in the district, creating its characteristic low-rise skyline.
- **L'Enfant Plan**: The original 1791 design for the city, featuring broad avenues and open spaces.
- **McMillan Plan**: A 1901 urban design plan to restore and expand upon L'Enfant's vision.
- **23rd Amendment**: Ratified in 1961, it grants the district three electoral votes for president and vice president.
- **Embassy Row**: A section of Massachusetts Avenue where many foreign embassies are located.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Statehood vs. Status Quo**: Proponents argue district residents deserve full voting rights and self-governance. Opponents contend statehood would unfairly grant Senate representation to a single city and undermine the concept of a separate federal district.
- **Federal Control**: The use of presidential authority to take over local police, as in 2025, raises questions about the balance of power between local and federal authorities in the district.
- **Budgetary Autonomy**: The district's structural budget deficit and congressional control over its finances remain contentious issues.
