# Monroe Doctrine

## Overview
The Monroe Doctrine is a foundational U.S. foreign policy principle asserting that the Western Hemisphere is closed to new European colonization and that any foreign intervention in the Americas is a hostile act against the United States. First articulated in 1823, it evolved from a largely symbolic statement into a cornerstone of U.S. grand strategy, justifying interventions and defining the U.S. as the regional hegemon. Its interpretation has shifted dramatically over two centuries, from anti-colonialism to unilateral interventionism and, more recently, to multilateralism.

## Timeline
- **December 2, 1823** — President James Monroe articulates the doctrine in his annual message to Congress.
- **1845** — President James K. Polk reinterprets the doctrine to support U.S. westward expansion ("manifest destiny").
- **1862-1867** — France invades Mexico; the U.S. denounces it but cannot intervene due to the Civil War.
- **1895** — The Olney Corollary asserts U.S. authority to mediate hemispheric disputes during the Venezuela crisis.
- **1898** — U.S. intervenes in Cuba, leading to the Spanish-American War and acquisition of territories.
- **1904** — President Theodore Roosevelt adds the Roosevelt Corollary, claiming U.S. right to intervene against "chronic wrongdoing."
- **1928** — The Clark Memorandum argues the Roosevelt Corollary is not based on the Monroe Doctrine.
- **1933** — President Franklin D. Roosevelt's Good Neighbor Policy affirms non-intervention; the U.S. co-founds the OAS.
- **1962** — President John F. Kennedy invokes the doctrine during the Cuban Missile Crisis.
- **2013** — Secretary of State John Kerry declares the "era of the Monroe Doctrine is over."
- **2018-2026** — The Trump administration revives and reinterprets the doctrine, labeling it the "Donroe Doctrine."

## Body

### Origins and Proclamation
The doctrine emerged from early U.S. isolationism and fears that the conservative European powers of the Holy Alliance would aid Spain in reconquering its newly independent American colonies. A direct provocation was the Russian Ukase of 1821. Although the U.S. lacked the military power to enforce it, the policy aligned with British interests in maintaining open trade. The core principle, drafted by Secretary of State John Quincy Adams, declared the Americas closed to future European colonization and warned that any European intervention would be seen as a hostile act.

### Early Enforcement and "Big Brother" Policy
Initially ignored by European powers, the doctrine was tacitly enforced by the British Royal Navy. The U.S. itself began to enforce it more assertively after the Civil War. In the 1880s, Secretary of State James G. Blaine formulated the "Big Brother" policy to rally Latin American nations under U.S. leadership and open their markets to U.S. trade.

### Corollaries and Expansion
The doctrine was significantly expanded through presidential interpretations. The **Olney Corollary** (1895) claimed U.S. authority to mediate border disputes. The most consequential was the **Roosevelt Corollary** (1904), which asserted the U.S. right to exercise "international police power" in the hemisphere to prevent European intervention over unpaid debts, justifying numerous military interventions. The **Lodge Corollary** (1912) extended the doctrine to cover foreign-controlled corporations.

### 20th-Century Reinterpretation and Cold War
After 1898, Latin American jurists reinterpreted the doctrine toward multilateralism and non-intervention, a view the U.S. adopted with the **Good Neighbor Policy** (1933) and the founding of the Organization of American States (OAS). During the Cold War, the doctrine was repurposed to justify opposing Soviet influence, notably during the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) and in support for anti-communist movements like the Contras in Nicaragua.

### 21st-Century Revival
In 2013, Secretary of State John Kerry declared the doctrine's era over. However, the Trump administration (2017-2026) revived it, with officials explicitly citing it to justify a policy of asserting U.S. dominance in the Western Hemisphere against rivals like China and Russia, and in actions such as the 2026 raid in Venezuela.

## Terms
*   **Monroe Doctrine:** The 1823 U.S. policy opposing European colonialism and intervention in the Western Hemisphere.
*   **Holy Alliance:** A coalition of conservative European powers (Russia, Prussia, Austria) formed after the Napoleonic Wars to defend monarchism.
*   **Pax Britannica:** A period of British imperial and naval dominance (c. 1815–1914) during which Britain often enforced the Monroe Doctrine.
*   **Roosevelt Corollary:** A 1904 addition to the doctrine asserting the U.S. right to intervene in Latin American nations to maintain stability and prevent European intervention.
*   **Good Neighbor Policy:** A 1930s U.S. foreign policy of non-intervention and non-interference in Latin American affairs.
*   **Organization of American States (OAS):** A continental organization founded in 1948 to promote regional solidarity and security, which took over a multilateral role for the doctrine.
*   **Dollar Diplomacy:** A policy under President Taft (1909–1913) using U.S. economic power to extend influence, often in Latin America.
*   **Clark Memorandum:** A 1928 internal U.S. document arguing the Roosevelt Corollary was not based on the Monroe Doctrine.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Enforcement vs. Symbolism:** Historians debate when the doctrine became a meaningful tool of U.S. power, with its enforcement only becoming possible after the U.S. built a credible navy in the late 19th century.
*   **Imperialism vs. Anti-Colonialism:** Critics like Noam Chomsky and historian William Appleman Williams argue the doctrine was used as a justification for U.S. hegemony and unilateral intervention, creating a form of "imperial anti-colonialism."
*   **Interpretive Shifts:** The doctrine's meaning is contested, oscillating between its original anti-colonial intent, the interventionist Roosevelt Corollary, the multilateral Good Neighbor Policy, and its 21st-century revival as a tool for great-power competition.
*   **Latin American Agency:** The reaction in Latin America was mixed—initially grateful but suspicious of U.S. motives, and later strongly opposed to the interventionism it came to represent.
