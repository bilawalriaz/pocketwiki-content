# Abiogenesis

## Overview

Abiogenesis is the natural process by which life arises from non-living matter, such as simple organic compounds. The prevailing scientific hypothesis is that the transition from non-living to living entities on Earth was not a single event, but a process of increasing complexity involving the formation of a habitable planet, the prebiotic synthesis of organic molecules, molecular self-replication, self-assembly, autocatalysis, and the emergence of cell membranes. The transition from non-life to life has not been observed experimentally, but many proposals have been made for different stages of the process.

The study of abiogenesis aims to determine how pre-life chemical reactions gave rise to life under conditions strikingly different from those on Earth today. It uses tools from biology and chemistry, attempting a synthesis of many sciences. Life functions through the chemistry of carbon and water, and builds on four chemical families: lipids for cell membranes, carbohydrates such as sugars, amino acids for protein metabolism, and the nucleic acids DNA and RNA for heredity. A theory of abiogenesis must explain the origins and interactions of these classes of molecules.

## Timeline

- **~14 Gya** — Big Bang; only hydrogen, helium, and lithium present; first stars form heavier elements via nucleosynthesis
- **4.6 Gya** — Solar System begins forming from gravitational collapse of a giant molecular cloud
- **4.54 Gya** — Earth forms, dated by radiometric dating of calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions in meteorites
- **~4.48 Gya** — Collision with Theia creates the Moon; Earth's primary atmosphere removed
- **4.4–4.3 Gya** — Likely predominantly a water world; oceans condense as early as the Moon-forming impact
- **4.1–3.8 Gya** — Late Heavy Bombardment; possibly a period of raised impact rate rather than one cataclysmic event
- **3.8 Gya** — Earliest evidence of life on Earth dates from Western Australia
- **4.32–3.48 Gya** — Life most likely emerged on Earth, based on evidence from the geologic record
- **3.77–4.32 Gya** — Earliest physical evidence of life reported: microbialites in banded iron formation rocks in Nuvvuagittuq Greenstone Belt, Quebec (disputed)
- **3.7 Gya** — Biogenic graphite found in metasedimentary rocks from southwestern Greenland
- **3.49 Gya** — Microbial mat fossils in cherts from Pilbara, Western Australia; Dresser Formation stromatolites at 3.48 Gya
- **1952** — Miller–Urey experiment demonstrates amino acid synthesis from inorganic precursors
- **2024** — LUCA's age inferred as ~4.2 Gya (4.09–4.33 Gya) via pre-LUCA gene duplicates

## Body

### Conceptual history

One ancient view, from Aristotle until the 19th century, was spontaneous generation—that "lower" animals arose from decaying organic substances. This was questioned from the 17th century; in 1668 Francesco Redi showed no maggots appeared in meat when flies were prevented from laying eggs, and by the mid-19th century the theory was considered disproven. Panspermia, dating to Anaxagoras in the 5th century BC, holds that life originated elsewhere and came to Earth via meteoroids, asteroids, comets, or planetoids; some interest exists in a Martian origin with later transfer to Earth.

In 1871 Charles Darwin speculated that life's original spark may have been in a "warm little pond" with ammonia and phosphoric salts. Alexander Oparin in 1924 and J. B. S. Haldane in 1929 proposed the Oparin–Haldane hypothesis: earliest cells slowly self-organized from a primordial soup. In 1952, the Miller–Urey experiment passed an electric current through a highly reducing mixture of methane, ammonia, hydrogen, and water vapor, forming organic monomers such as amino acids. Current consensus describes the primitive atmosphere as weakly reducing or neutral, diminishing amino acid production; adding iron and carbonate minerals produces a diverse array of amino acids.

### Producing a habitable Earth

Soon after the Big Bang, only hydrogen, helium, and lithium existed. Early massive stars produced heavier elements by stellar nucleosynthesis, proceeding to the most stable element, iron-56; heavier elements formed during supernovas. Carbon was formed mainly in white dwarf stars, enabling rocky planet formation. The Hadean eon (until 4.031 Gya) was at first inhospitable to life. The Moon-forming impact left an atmosphere of water vapor, nitrogen, and carbon dioxide; dissolved CO2 made seas slightly acidic (pH ~5.5). The Late Heavy Bombardment hypothesis originally posited a single cataclysmic impact at 3.9 Gya that could sterilize Earth, but more recent research suggests multiple small-scale bombardment periods with less destructive power.

The Earth was formed at 4.54 Gya, and the earliest evidence of life on Earth dates from 3.8 Gya from Western Australia. The earliest physical evidence of life (2017) was microbialites in banded iron formation rocks in the Nuvvuagittuq Greenstone Belt of Northern Quebec, at least 3.77 and possibly 4.32 Gya, though later research disputed this interpretation, suggesting abiotic processes. Biogenic graphite dates to 3.7 Gya in Greenland; carbon isotope ratios in Jack Hills zircons suggest life could have existed from 4.1 Gya. A 2024 study inferred LUCA's age as ~4.2 Gya, much sooner after the origin of life than previously thought.

### Prebiotic synthesis

Organic molecules on early Earth could have had terrestrial origins (driven by impact shocks, ultraviolet light, redox coupling, or electrical discharges) or extraterrestrial origins (pseudo-panspermia). Purine and pyrimidine nucleobases—including guanine, adenine, cytosine, uracil, and thymine—as well as sugars, have been found in meteorites. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are the most common and abundant polyatomic molecules in the observable universe and a likely constituent of Earth's primordial sea. The RNA component uracil in the Murchison meteorite was likely formed extraterrestrially, based on 12C/13C isotopic ratios.

Laboratory synthesis has demonstrated that biologically relevant molecules can form from simple carbon sources. The 1861 formose reaction created sugars including tetroses, pentoses, and hexoses when formaldehyde is heated under basic conditions with divalent metal ions like calcium; R. Breslow proposed it was autocatalytic in 1959. Nucleobases can be synthesized from hydrogen cyanide (HCN) and ammonia; freezing temperatures assist purine synthesis, while cytosine and uracil may require boiling temperatures. Seven amino acids and eleven types of nucleobases formed in ice when ammonia and cyanide were left in a freezer for 25 years. Prebiotic peptide synthesis could have occurred via high-temperature concentration conditions or plausible condensing agents; mineral surface catalysis alone is insufficient, as surfaces also catalyze hydrolysis.

### Producing protocells

The largest unanswered question is how simple protocells first arose and differed in reproductive contribution, thus initiating evolution. The lipid world theory postulates that the first self-replicating object was lipid-like. Phospholipids form lipid bilayers in water; these bodies may expand by inserting additional lipids and spontaneously split into two offspring. A protocell is a self-organized, spherical collection of lipids proposed as a stepping-stone to life, capable of evolving. A functional protocell exhibiting Darwinian evolution has not yet been achieved in a laboratory setting. Irene Chen and Jack W. Szostak suggest elementary protocells can give rise to primitive forms of differential reproduction, competition, and energy storage. Vesicles can undergo evolutionary processes under pressure cycling conditions simulating tectonic fault zones.

### Producing biology

Life requires a loss of entropy locally, but this does not contradict the second law of thermodynamics, since a living organism creates order at the expense of increased entropy elsewhere. Multiple energy sources were available: geothermal heat, sunlight, lightning, and micro-meteorite entries. In 1961, Peter Mitchell proposed chemiosmosis as a cell's primary energy conversion system; in the first organisms, the proton gradient could have been provided by differences between hydrothermal vent flow and surrounding seawater. The RNA world hypothesis describes early Earth with self-replicating and catalytic RNA but no DNA or proteins; it was proposed in 1962 by Alexander Rich, with the term coined by Walter Gilbert in 1986. RNA replicase can both code and catalyse further RNA replication—it is autocatalytic.

Starting with Carl Woese's work from 1977, genomics placed LUCA between Bacteria and a clade of Archaea and Eukaryota. In 2016, a set of 355 genes likely present in LUCA was identified from 6.1 million prokaryotic genes sequenced, identifying 355 protein clusters from among 286,514 protein clusters. Results suggest LUCA was anaerobic with a Wood–Ljungdahl pathway, nitrogen- and carbon-fixing, thermophilic, with DNA, the genetic code, and ribosomes. It likely inhabited an anaerobic hydrothermal vent setting and was evidently already complex—not the first living thing.

### Suitable geological environments

**Deep sea hydrothermal vents**: The alkaline hydrothermal vent theory posits life began at submarine vents, where hydrogen-rich fluids emerge from serpentinization of ultra-mafic olivine with seawater. Russell demonstrated alkaline vents create an abiogenic proton motive force chemiosmotic gradient. Mineral particles inside vents have enzyme-like catalytic properties. Arguments against include: vents do not concentrate prebiotic materials due to dilution; key compounds like methane are in far lower concentrations than in Miller–Urey conditions; and no studies have experimentally demonstrated synthesis of sugars, amino acids, or nucleotides under plausible vent conditions.

**Surface bodies of water**: Wet-dry cycles concentrate prebiotic compounds and enable condensation reactions. Hot springs provide the ionic balance universally found in cells—high K+/Na+ ratio, Mn2+, Zn2+, phosphate—which marine environments lack. A phylogenomic analysis shows LUCA's intracellular fluid ionic composition is identical to that of hot springs. Temperate freshwater bodies could have accumulated prebiotic materials; atmospheric reconstructions suggest surface temperatures of 0–40 °C. Cold-start theories note the Sun's luminosity was ~25% weaker; icy environments trade faster reaction rates for increased stability of larger polymers.

**Inside the continental crust**: Tectonic fault zones could present a stable, protected environment for long-term prebiotic evolution. At ~1000 m depth, CO2 provides conditions near the supercritical-gaseous phase transition, allowing lipophilic organic molecules to accumulate. Periodic pressure variations from geysers or tides keep the environment in constant non-equilibrium, forming vesicles selected for stability.

### Homochirality

Living organisms use molecules with the same chirality: amino acids are left-handed while nucleotides and sugars are right-handed. Without a chiral source, molecules form in 50/50 racemic mixtures. Non-racemic mixtures can arise from asymmetric physical laws or circularly polarized light; the solar nebula may have been such an environment. This is consistent with some meteorite-delivered amino acids exhibiting chiral asymmetry of the same sign as terrestrial life. A small bias can be amplified by asymmetric autocatalysis, as in the Soai reaction.

## Terms

- **LUCA**: Last universal common ancestor; the single-celled organism ancestral to all modern life, living ~4 billion years ago with hundreds of genes.
- **RNA world**: Hypothesized early Earth with self-replicating and catalytic RNA but no DNA or proteins.
- **Miller–Urey experiment**: 1952 experiment demonstrating amino acid synthesis from inorganic precursors under reducing conditions.
- **Protocell**: Self-organized, spherical collection of lipids proposed as a stepping-stone to life, capable of evolving.
- **Chemiosmosis**: Cell's primary energy conversion system (proposed 1961 by Peter Mitchell), powered by proton gradients across membranes.
- **Wood–Ljungdahl pathway**: Reductive acetyl-CoA metabolic pathway; inferred in LUCA, indicating anaerobic metabolism.
- **Homochirality**: Uniformity of chiral (non-mirror-symmetric) units; life uses left-handed amino acids and right-handed sugars.
- **Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)**: Most common polyatomic molecules in the universe; proposed to have mediated RNA synthesis (PAH world hypothesis).
- **Spontaneous generation**: Ancient view that "lower" animals arose from decaying organic substances; disproven by the mid-19th century.
- **Panspermia**: Idea that life originated elsewhere and came to Earth via meteoroids, asteroids, comets, or planetoids.

## Debates and open questions

- Whether the earliest physical evidence of life (Nuvvuagittuq microbialites) is biogenic or explainable by abiotic processes such as "chemical gardens" or volcanic ejecta.
- Whether the Late Heavy Bombardment was a single sterilizing cataclysmic event at 3.9 Gya or a period of raised impact rate with less destructive power.
- Whether life originated in deep-sea hydrothermal vents, hot springs, temperate surface waters, icy environments, or inside the continental crust; each setting has supporting evidence and counterarguments.
- Whether LUCA was thermophilic or mesophilic; hyperthermophily could be convergent evolution, and reverse gyrase (found only in thermophiles) likely originated in archaea and transferred to bacteria, implying it was not in LUCA.
- Whether the RNA world was the first self-replicating system or whether other self-replicating and self-catalyzing molecules preceded RNA.
- The PAH world hypothesis remains untested.
- The transition from non-life to life has not been observed experimentally; a functional protocell exhibiting Darwinian evolution has not yet been achieved in a laboratory setting.
