# Iran–Iraq War

## Overview
The Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988) was a devastating conventional conflict between two neighboring states, sparked by Iraq's invasion of Iran. It became the deadliest war between regular armies of developing countries, causing around 500,000 deaths and ending in a stalemate with no permanent border changes. The war reshaped regional power dynamics, entrenched sectarian tensions, and left both economies shattered.

## Timeline
- **22 Sep 1980** — Iraq launches full-scale invasion of Iran.
- **24 Oct 1980** — Iraq captures the city of Khorramshahr after a prolonged battle.
- **Jun 1982** — Iran recaptures all its territory, pushing Iraqi forces back to pre-war borders.
- **20 Jun 1982** — Saddam Hussein proposes a ceasefire; Iran rejects it, demanding regime change in Iraq.
- **1984** — The "Tanker War" escalates as both sides attack oil shipping in the Persian Gulf.
- **Feb 1986** — Iran captures the al-Faw peninsula in southern Iraq.
- **18 Jul 1988** — Iran accepts UN Security Council Resolution 598, agreeing to a ceasefire.
- **8 Aug 1988** — Ceasefire becomes effective, ending major combat operations.
- **1991** — UN Security Council identifies Iraq as the aggressor of the war.

## Background and Causes
The war's origins lie in long-running disputes over the Shatt al-Arab river and regional power ambitions. Iraq, led by Saddam Hussein, sought to prevent Iran's new Islamic Revolution from exporting its ideology and to rally Iraq's Shia majority against its Sunni-controlled Ba'athist government. Iraq also aimed to replace Iran as the dominant Persian Gulf power and to retake the eastern bank of the Shatt al-Arab, ceded to Iran in the 1975 Algiers Agreement.

Following Iran's 1979 revolution, tensions escalated. Iran's leader, Ruhollah Khomeini, called for the overthrow of Iraq's Ba'athist government. In April 1980, an Iran-backed assassination attempt on Iraqi Deputy Prime Minister Tariq Aziz provided a *casus belli*. Iraq, having heavily invested in its military since 1975, saw an opportunity to attack a weakened Iran, whose military had been crippled by post-revolutionary purges and equipment shortages.

## Course of the War
**1980–1982: Invasion and Iranian Counterattack**
Iraq's initial invasion stalled by December 1980. Iran, despite its weakened state, launched successful counteroffensives in 1982, recapturing all lost territory. The war then entered a new phase as Iran invaded Iraq, aiming to overthrow Saddam Hussein.

**1982–1986: Stalemate and Attrition**
The conflict bogged down into trench warfare. Iraq adopted a defensive strategy, using fortified lines and chemical weapons to bleed Iranian forces. Iran launched numerous offensives, often using "human wave" tactics with poorly equipped Basij volunteers, followed by Revolutionary Guard and regular army units. Key battles included the fighting in the marshes and the First Battle of al-Faw (1986), where Iran captured the strategic peninsula.

**1987–1988: Iraqi Resurgence and Ceasefire**
By 1988, Iraq, massively re-armed and supported by foreign powers, launched successful counter-offensives using combined arms and chemical weapons. It recaptured al-Faw and pushed Iranian forces back. Concurrently, the "War of the Cities" saw ballistic missile exchanges, and the "Tanker War" drew in international naval forces. Facing military defeats, economic collapse, and the threat of further chemical attacks, Iran accepted a UN-brokered ceasefire in August 1988.

## Foreign Involvement
Iraq received extensive financial and military support from the United States, Soviet Union, France, and Gulf Arab states, who viewed it as a counterbalance to revolutionary Iran. Iran was aided by Syria, Libya, China, North Korea, and Israel, though its support was more limited. The U.S. provided Iraq with intelligence, economic aid, and dual-use technology, while also engaging in direct naval clashes with Iran (e.g., Operation Praying Mantis).

## Aftermath and Legacy
The war ended with no territorial changes and no reparations. The UN identified Iraq as the aggressor. Both nations suffered immense economic damage, with Iraq accruing over $130 billion in debt. The conflict entrenched the power of Iran's Revolutionary Guard, contributed to Iraq's later invasion of Kuwait, and left a legacy of sectarian tension and unresolved grievances. It was the first modern war to feature widespread use of chemical weapons and ballistic missiles against cities.

## Terms
- **Ba'athist government**: The secular, pan-Arab nationalist ruling party in Iraq, led by Saddam Hussein.
- **Basij**: A paramilitary volunteer militia in Iran, often used in human wave attacks.
- **Human wave attacks**: Iranian infantry tactics involving massed frontal assaults, often by poorly trained volunteers, to overwhelm enemy lines.
- **Shatt al-Arab**: A river forming part of the Iran–Iraq border, a central dispute in the conflict.
- **Anfal campaign**: A genocidal Iraqi military campaign against Kurdish civilians in northern Iraq during the war.
- **Tanker War**: The phase of the conflict involving attacks on commercial shipping in the Persian Gulf.
- **Operation Earnest Will**: The U.S. military operation to escort reflagged Kuwaiti tankers during the Tanker War.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Who won?** Most analysts consider the war a stalemate, though some argue Iraq emerged as the victor due to its late-war successes, while Iran claims victory for thwarting Iraq's territorial aims and later securing the Shatt al-Arab.
- **Chemical weapons use**: While Iraq's extensive use is well-documented, allegations of Iranian chemical attacks are disputed and lack conclusive evidence. The international community's response, particularly U.S. support for Iraq despite knowledge of its chemical use, remains controversial.
- **U.S. role**: The extent and motivation of U.S. support for Iraq, including during its use of chemical weapons, and the causes of incidents like the downing of Iran Air Flight 655, are subjects of ongoing historical analysis.
