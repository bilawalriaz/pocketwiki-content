# Roald Amundsen

## Overview
Roald Amundsen (1872–1928) was a Norwegian polar explorer who became the first person to reach the South Pole (1911) and the first verified explorer to reach the North Pole (1926). A central figure of the Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration, he succeeded through meticulous preparation, the adoption of Inuit survival techniques—specifically dog sleds and fur clothing—and a willingness to innovate, transitioning from sea-based to aerial exploration. His rivalry with Robert Falcon Scott defined the race to the South Pole, while his later disappearance during a rescue mission cemented his legacy as a martyr of polar science.

## Timeline
- **16 Jul 1872** — Born in Borge, Østfold, Norway, to a family of shipowners.
- **1897–1899** — Serves as first mate on the Belgian Antarctic Expedition (RV *Belgica*), the first to overwinter in Antarctica.
- **1903–1906** — Leads the first successful traversal of the Northwest Passage on the *Gjøa*, learning Arctic survival from Netsilik Inuit.
- **3 Jun 1910** — Departs Norway on the *Fram* for the South Pole, secretly changing destination from the North Pole.
- **14 Dec 1911** — Reaches the South Pole with a party of five, a month before Robert Scott.
- **1918–1925** — Attempts the Northeast Passage on the *Maud*; fails to drift over the North Pole but yields valuable scientific data.
- **1925** — Reaches 87°44′ N by flying boat (Dornier Do J), the highest latitude by air at the time.
- **12 May 1926** — Flies over the North Pole in the airship *Norge*, the first verified attainment of the pole.
- **18 Jun 1928** — Disappears in the Barents Sea while flying a rescue mission for the crashed airship *Italia*.

## Body

### Early Life and Formative Influences
Born in Borge, Norway, Amundsen was the fourth son of shipowner Jens Amundsen. His mother, Hanna Sahlqvist, pressured him to study medicine; he complied until her death when he was 21, then abandoned university for the sea. At fifteen, reading Sir John Franklin’s narratives of overland Arctic expeditions "shaped the whole course of my life." He was also a childhood playmate of Carsten Borchgrevink, a pioneering Antarctic explorer.

### The Belgian Antarctic Expedition (1897–1899)
At 25, Amundsen joined Adrien de Gerlache’s expedition as first mate aboard the *Belgica*. The ship became trapped in sea ice at 70°30′S, forcing the first overwintering in Antarctica. The crew, poorly prepared, faced scurvy and madness. American doctor Frederick Cook likely saved them by hunting fresh meat—specifically offal—which provided sufficient vitamin C to prevent scurvy in the absence of citrus. This experience taught Amundsen the critical importance of diet and preparation.

### The Northwest Passage (1903–1906)
Amundsen led a six-man crew on the 45-ton *Gjøa*, choosing a small, shallow-draft vessel to hug the coast. They traversed Baffin Bay, Parry Channel, and the straits of the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, spending two winters at King William Island (Gjoa Haven). There, the Netsilik Inuit taught him essential survival skills: using sled dogs for haulage and wearing animal skins instead of wool, which fails when wet. These lessons directly enabled his later Antarctic success. After clearing the archipelago in August 1905, he sledged 500 miles to Eagle, Alaska, to telegraph success, returning to Oslo in November 1906.

### South Pole Expedition (1910–1912)
Originally planning a North Pole drift, Amundsen pivoted to Antarctica in 1909 after hearing Frederick Cook and Robert Peary claimed the North Pole. He departed on the *Fram* in June 1910, informing Robert Scott of the change only at Madeira. Arriving at the Bay of Whales (Ross Ice Shelf) in January 1911, he established Framheim base. Adopting Inuit methods—skis, dog sleds, fur clothing—he laid depots at 80°, 81°, and 82° South. A premature September start failed in extreme cold, causing a quarrel; Amundsen sent dissenters to explore King Edward VII Land. The final party of five (Amundsen, Olav Bjaaland, Helmer Hanssen, Sverre Hassel, Oscar Wisting) departed 19 October, pioneering a route up the unknown Axel Heiberg Glacier to the Polar Plateau. They reached the pole on 14 December 1911, naming the camp Polheim and the plateau King Haakon VII's Plateau. They returned to Framheim on 25 January 1912 with 11 surviving dogs, having eaten the rest. Amundsen attributed his "smooth and uneventful" trek to careful preparation, dog handling, and ski use, contrasting sharply with Scott’s fatal struggle.

### North Polar Expeditions and the Northeast Passage (1918–1925)
Amundsen launched a new ship, *Maud*, in 1918 to drift across the Arctic Ocean via the Northeast Passage, inspired by Fridtjof Nansen’s *Fram* drift. The goal was scientific exploration of the Arctic Basin, with geophysicist Harald Sverdrup aboard. The *Maud* froze in off Cape Chelyuskin but the ice proved too thick to drift freely; it froze and thawed repeatedly near the New Siberian Islands and Wrangel Island. Amundsen suffered a broken arm and a polar bear attack, limiting his field role. A dog-sled dash to Nome failed due to open water in the Bering Strait. After two winters without reaching the pole, Amundsen sailed to Seattle for repairs (1921), eventually shifting strategy to aerial attempts. The *Maud* drifted for three more years under Wisting, never reaching the pole, and was eventually seized by creditors. Despite the navigational failure, Sverdrup’s scientific data—partly lost and later recovered by Russian scientist Nikolay Urvantsev—proved valuable.

### Aerial Expeditions and the *Norge* Flight (1923–1926)
A 1923 flight attempt from Alaska failed due to aircraft damage. In 1925, Amundsen and Lincoln Ellsworth flew two Dornier Do J flying boats (N-24, N-25) to 87°44′ N, a record latitude. Forced down on the ice, the crew shoveled 600 tons of ice to create a runway on starvation rations, barely escaping in the overloaded N-25. In 1926, Amundsen, Ellsworth, and 14 others (including Italian aeronautical engineer Umberto Nobile and Oscar Wisting) crossed the Arctic in the semi-rigid airship *Norge*, designed by Nobile. They left Spitsbergen 11 May, flew over the North Pole on 12 May, and landed in Alaska the next day. Because prior claims by Cook (1908), Peary (1909), and Richard E. Byrd (1926, days earlier) are disputed as dubious or fraudulent, the *Norge* crew are recognized as the first *verified* explorers to reach the North Pole.

### Disappearance and Death
On 18 June 1928, Amundsen vanished while flying a French Latham 47 flying boat on a rescue mission for Nobile, whose new airship *Italia* had crashed returning from the pole. Amundsen’s crew included Norwegian pilot Leif Dietrichson and French pilot René Guilbaud. A wing-float and fuel tank washed up near Tromsø, suggesting a crash in the Barents Sea. The Norwegian government called off the search in September 1928; bodies were never found. Modern searches (2004, 2009) by the Royal Norwegian Navy using the *Hugin 1000* submarine found no wreckage.

### Personal Life and Legacy
A lifelong bachelor, Amundsen had a long-term secret relationship with Kristine Elisabeth "Kiss" Bennett (met 1907) and later became engaged to American divorcée Bess Magids. Biographers note other possible romances, but Amundsen reportedly "found little use in activities that didn't help him fulfill his polar ambitions." His legacy is vast: the Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station, the lunar Amundsen crater (considered for NASA's Artemis III landing), numerous geographic features, schools, and cultural depictions (films *The Red Tent*, *Amundsen*; TV serial *The Last Place on Earth*). Inuit oral history in Gjoa Haven claims Amundsen fathered a son, Luke Ikuallaq, during the 1903–1905 stay; however, 2012 Y-DNA testing excluded a match to Amundsen’s direct male line, though not to other crew members.

## Terms
- **Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration**: The period (c. 1897–1922) characterized by major, often state-sponsored expeditions to the Antarctic continent, marked by extreme hardship and scientific discovery.
- **Northwest Passage**: A sea route through the Arctic Ocean, along the northern coast of North America via waterways through the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans.
- **Northeast Passage**: A shipping lane along the Russian and Norwegian coasts across the Arctic, connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans.
- **Ross Ice Shelf (Barrier)**: The largest ice shelf in Antarctica (formerly "Great Ice Barrier"), a floating sheet of ice fed by glaciers, where Amundsen established his base.
- **Axel Heiberg Glacier**: A major glacier in the Transantarctic Mountains, discovered and climbed by Amundsen’s party as their route to the Polar Plateau.
- **Polar Plateau**: The high-altitude, flat ice cap covering the interior of East Antarctica, where the South Pole is situated.
- **Fram**: A specially designed Norwegian ship (built for Fridtjof Nansen) with a rounded hull to withstand ice pressure by rising up rather than being crushed; used by Amundsen for his South Pole expedition.
- **Maud**: Amundsen’s purpose-built ship for the 1918–1925 Northeast Passage/drift expedition, named for Queen Maud of Norway.
- **Norge**: A semi-rigid airship designed by Umberto Nobile, used for the 1926 verified flight over the North Pole.
- **Scurvy**: A disease caused by vitamin C deficiency; prevented on the *Belgica* by eating fresh raw meat/offal, a lesson Amundsen applied later.

## Debates and open questions
- **North Pole Priority**: The *Norge* flight (1926) is the first *verified* attainment, but claims by Frederick Cook (1908), Robert Peary (1909), and Richard E. Byrd (1926) remain subjects of intense historical debate regarding their accuracy or possible fraud.
- **Inuit Descendancy Claims**: Oral history from Gjoa Haven identifies Amundsen as the father of Luke Ikuallaq (born c. 1903–1905). While 2012 Y-DNA testing ruled out Amundsen’s direct paternal line, not all claimants have been tested, and a match to other European crew members has not been investigated.
- **Cause of 1928 Disappearance**: The exact crash site and mechanism of the Latham 47 flying boat remain unknown; wreckage has never been located despite modern sonar searches.
- **Scott vs. Amundsen Ethics**: Historians debate Amundsen’s secrecy in switching from the North to South Pole (misleading Scott and backers) and his consumption of sled dogs, versus Scott’s reliance on man-hauling and ponies.
