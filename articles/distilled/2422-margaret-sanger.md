# Margaret Sanger

## Overview
Margaret Sanger (1879–1966) was a pivotal American birth control activist, nurse, and founder of Planned Parenthood. She is regarded as a founder and leader of the U.S. birth control movement, whose decades-long campaign to legalize contraception fundamentally reshaped reproductive health and women's rights in the 20th century.

## Timeline
- **1879** — Born in Corning, New York.
- **1914** — Published *The Woman Rebel* newsletter, popularizing the term "birth control."
- **1916** — Opened the first birth control clinic in the U.S. (Brooklyn, NY).
- **1918** — New York Court of Appeals allowed physicians to dispense contraceptives.
- **1921** — Founded the American Birth Control League (ABCL).
- **1923** — Established the Clinical Research Bureau (CRB), a physician-staffed clinic.
- **1936** — Court victory in *United States v. One Package* permitted nationwide physician dispensing of contraceptives.
- **1939** — ABCL and BCCRB merged into the Birth Control Federation of America.
- **1942** — Organization renamed Planned Parenthood Federation of America.
- **1952** — Co-founded the International Planned Parenthood Federation (IPPF).
- **1950s** — Persuaded philanthropists to fund development of the first birth control pill.
- **1965** — *Griswold v. Connecticut* legalized contraception for couples nationwide.
- **1966** — Died in Tucson, Arizona.

## Body

### Early Activism and Legal Battles
Sanger's activism began while working as a nurse in New York City's slums, where she witnessed women suffering from frequent pregnancies and dangerous self-induced abortions. The Comstock Act (1873) prohibited distributing information about contraception. Sanger deliberately flouted this law, publishing *The Woman Rebel* (1914) and the pamphlet *Family Limitation* (1914), which led to her arrest. She fled to England in 1914, where exposure to Malthusian ideas about overpopulation and the work of sexologist Havelock Ellis shaped her views. Upon returning in 1915, she faced trial but charges were dropped. In 1916, she opened the first U.S. birth control clinic in Brooklyn; it was shut down by police after nine days. Her subsequent trial and appeal resulted in a landmark 1918 New York court ruling allowing physicians to dispense contraceptives.

### Building an Organized Movement
Sanger founded the American Birth Control League (ABCL) in 1921 and the Clinical Research Bureau (CRB) in 1923, a physician-staffed clinic that became a model for others. She published the *Birth Control Review* (1917–1940) and wrote influential books like *Woman and the New Race* (1920). By the late 1920s, a network of affiliated clinics operated nationwide. A schism between the ABCL and Sanger's CRB was mended in 1939 with their merger into the Birth Control Federation of America, which was renamed Planned Parenthood in 1942. Sanger later helped found the International Planned Parenthood Federation (1952) and was instrumental in securing funding for the development of the first birth control pill in the 1950s.

### Engagement with Eugenics and Race
Sanger aligned with the eugenics movement, a popular Progressive Era reform effort, to gain scientific legitimacy for birth control. She argued contraception would reduce the number of "unfit" people (e.g., the "feeble-minded," "paupers") and improve the human race. However, her approach was distinct: she emphasized voluntary contraception for individuals over government-mandated sterilization and did not target specific ethnicities. Her work served women of all races; by 1929, about 12% of clinic patients were from Harlem. In 1930, she opened a Harlem clinic with an all-African American staff and advisory board, supported by W.E.B. Du Bois. In 1939, she co-created the "Negro Project" to deliver birth control information to impoverished African Americans, advising the use of African American ministers to prevent rumors of racist intent. While some scholars, like Angela Davis, have interpreted this as evidence of a racist population control agenda, most historians find no evidence Sanger was racist and view her eugenic advocacy as a strategic, if problematic, alliance.

### Views on Abortion and Free Speech
Sanger staunchly opposed abortion, viewing it as a dangerous societal ill that contraception could eliminate. Her clinics never offered abortions during her lifetime, and she publicly condemned the practice except to save a mother's life. She framed her activism as a free speech battle, repeatedly violating anti-obscenity laws to provoke legal challenges. This strategy, combined with public speaking tours (even to groups like the KKK), generated publicity and momentum for the movement, despite frequent arrests and opposition from the Catholic Church.

## Terms
- **Comstock Act (1873):** Federal law that prohibited the distribution of contraceptives or information about them via mail.
- **Birth Control:** A term Sanger popularized in 1914 as a more candid alternative to euphemisms like "family limitation."
- **Diaphragm:** A barrier contraceptive method Sanger encountered in Europe and imported illegally into the U.S., which became a key tool for early clinics.
- **Eugenics:** A social reform movement aiming to improve the genetic composition of the human race, which Sanger allied with to promote birth control.
- **Malthusianism:** The theory, based on Thomas Malthus's writings, that overpopulation leads to poverty and social ills, which influenced Sanger's views.
- **Clinical Research Bureau (CRB):** The first physician-staffed birth control clinic in the U.S., founded by Sanger in 1923.
- **American Birth Control League (ABCL):** Organization founded by Sanger in 1921 to promote birth control; it later merged to form Planned Parenthood.
- **Negro Project (1939):** A program co-founded by Sanger to deliver birth control information to impoverished African American communities.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Sanger's Eugenics:** Scholars debate whether Sanger's support for eugenics was a sincere belief or a strategic alliance to gain legitimacy for her birth control activism.
- **Racial Intentions:** A persistent controversy, amplified by anti-abortion activists, claims Sanger's work was a racist population control program. Most historians find no evidence she was racist, interpreting her eugenic language as targeting "unfit" individuals, not ethnicities, and her actions as serving diverse communities.
- **Abortion Stance:** While publicly anti-abortion, some historians suggest Sanger may have privately viewed it as an ethical last resort, creating a tension with her public advocacy.
