# California

## Overview
California is the most populous U.S. state and a global economic powerhouse, with a diverse geography ranging from coastal cities to deserts and mountains. Its history is marked by Indigenous civilizations, Spanish colonization, Mexican rule, and rapid American expansion fueled by the Gold Rush, shaping its unique cultural and economic identity.

## Timeline
- **Pre-1542** — Indigenous peoples, with over 70 distinct groups, inhabit California.
- **1542** — Spanish explorer Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo leads the first European expedition to the coast.
- **1769–1770** — Portolá expedition establishes first Spanish missions and presidios, beginning colonization.
- **1821** — Mexico gains independence from Spain, including Alta California.
- **1846** — Bear Flag Revolt by American settlers; U.S. military invasion begins during Mexican-American War.
- **1848** — Gold discovered at Sutter's Mill; Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo cedes California to the U.S.
- **1850** — California admitted as the 31st state (a free state) under the Compromise of 1850.
- **1869** — First transcontinental railroad completed, connecting California to the eastern U.S.
- **1906** — San Francisco earthquake and fire devastate the city.
- **1941–1945** — World War II boosts California's population and economy through military and industrial growth.
- **1960s** — Hollywood and Silicon Valley emerge as global centers for entertainment and technology.
- **2020** — First confirmed U.S. COVID-19 case in California; state of emergency declared.

## Body

### Indigenous and Colonial Foundations
Prior to European contact, California was one of the most culturally and linguistically diverse regions in pre-Columbian North America, with at least 300,000 people from over 70 ethnic groups. They developed complex ecosystem management, including controlled burning. Spanish exploration began in 1542, but sustained colonization started with the 1769 Portolá expedition, which established missions, presidios, and pueblos. The mission system, led by figures like Junípero Serra, controlled much land and reshaped Indigenous life. By 1812, Russian traders also established a short-lived fort at Fort Ross.

### Mexican Rule and U.S. Conquest
Mexico gained independence from Spain in 1821, taking control of Alta California. The mission system was secularized by 1834, leading to large ranchos owned by Californios. Political instability and increasing American settlement set the stage for conflict. The 1846 Bear Flag Revolt and the U.S. invasion during the Mexican-American War led to the 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, which ceded California to the United States.

### The Gold Rush and Statehood
Gold was discovered at Sutter's Mill in 1848, just before the treaty, triggering the California Gold Rush. This caused a massive population boom, transforming the territory's demographics and economy. The California genocide occurred during this period, with state-sanctioned policies and settler violence leading to the deaths of thousands of Indigenous people. California was admitted as a free state in 1850. The first transcontinental railroad, completed in 1869, further integrated California into the nation.

### 20th Century to Present
The 20th century saw massive population growth, driven by opportunities in agriculture, aerospace, and defense. Hollywood became a global entertainment hub, and Silicon Valley emerged as the center of the technology industry. The state faced challenges including the 1906 earthquake, water conflicts, and social unrest. In the 21st century, California grapples with climate change impacts like droughts and wildfires, housing affordability crises, and economic inequality, while remaining the world's largest sub-national economy.

## Terms
- **Californios**: Hispanic residents native to California, with roots in the Spanish and Mexican eras.
- **Bear Flag Revolt**: 1846 uprising by American settlers in Sonoma against Mexican rule, a prelude to the U.S. conquest.
- **Compromise of 1850**: U.S. legislative package that admitted California as a free state.
- **California genocide**: The systematic killing and displacement of Indigenous peoples by settlers and state policies in the mid-19th century.
- **Silicon Valley**: The southern part of the San Francisco Bay Area, a global center for high-tech innovation and enterprise.
- **Mediterranean climate**: Characterized by warm, dry summers and mild, wet winters, typical of coastal California.
- **San Andreas Fault**: A major geological fault line running through California, responsible for significant seismic activity.
- **Central Valley**: A large, fertile agricultural region in the state's interior, crucial for U.S. food production.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent and intentionality of the California genocide is a subject of scholarly debate, with some historians and officials, like Governor Gavin Newsom, formally acknowledging it.
- The causes and solutions for California's chronic water shortages and management, balancing agricultural, urban, and environmental needs, remain politically contentious.
- The impact of high costs of living and recent population declines on California's future economic and political influence is actively discussed.
