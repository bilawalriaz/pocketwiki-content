# Catalonia

## Overview
Catalonia is an autonomous community of Spain, designated as a nationality, located in the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula. It has a distinct history, language (Catalan), and culture, and has been a major economic and political force within Spain. Its modern political identity is shaped by a long history of self-governance, periods of suppression, and a contemporary independence movement.

## Timeline
- **1137** — Dynastic union of the County of Barcelona and the Kingdom of Aragon forms the Crown of Aragon.
- **1640–1652** — Revolt of the Principality of Catalonia (Reapers' War) against the Spanish Crown.
- **1659** — Treaty of the Pyrenees cedes northern Catalonia (Roussillon) to France.
- **1714** — Siege and capitulation of Barcelona ends Catalan resistance in the War of the Spanish Succession.
- **1716** — Philip V's Nueva Planta decrees suppress Catalonia's institutions and legal system.
- **1932** — The Second Spanish Republic grants Catalonia its first Statute of Autonomy.
- **1939–1975** — Francoist dictatorship abolishes Catalan self-government and bans the official use of Catalan.
- **1979** — A new Statute of Autonomy is approved, restoring self-government and defining Catalonia as a "nationality."
- **2006** — A new Statute of Autonomy is approved by referendum, later partially struck down by Spain's Constitutional Court in 2010.
- **2017** — Catalan Parliament unilaterally declares independence following a disputed referendum; Spain imposes direct rule.

## Body

### Historical Development
Catalonia's history begins with pre-Roman Iberian tribes and Greek colonies. It was later part of the Roman province of Hispania Tarraconensis. After the fall of Rome, it was ruled by Visigoths and then Muslims before being incorporated into the Frankish Empire as a series of counties. The County of Barcelona became the dominant power. In 1137, a dynastic union with Aragon created the Crown of Aragon, within which the Principality of Catalonia developed a distinct legal and institutional system, including the Courts and the Generalitat. Catalonia became a major Mediterranean maritime and commercial power.

Tensions with the Spanish monarchy led to revolts, notably the Reapers' War (1640–1652). Following the War of the Spanish Succession, Catalonia's defeat in 1714 led to the abolition of its institutions via the Nueva Planta decrees. The 19th century saw industrialization, a cultural renaissance (Renaixença), and the rise of nationalism. The Second Republic (1931–1939) granted autonomy, which was abolished under Franco's dictatorship (1939–1975). Democracy restored self-government with the 1979 Statute of Autonomy.

### Politics and Government
Catalonia is governed by the Generalitat de Catalunya, which holds exclusive jurisdiction in areas like education, health, and culture. Its fundamental law is the Statute of Autonomy. The government consists of the Parliament, the President of the Generalitat, and the Executive Council. The 2006 Statute, which defined Catalonia as a nation, was challenged and partially annulled by Spain's Constitutional Court in 2010, a ruling that significantly fueled the independence movement.

Support for independence has fluctuated, peaking in the 2010s. A unilateral independence referendum was held on 1 October 2017, deemed illegal by Spanish authorities. The subsequent declaration of independence by the Catalan Parliament led to the imposition of direct rule by Madrid, the dissolution of the Catalan government, and legal proceedings against its leaders. Several were imprisoned and later pardoned in 2021. In the May 2024 elections, the left-wing unionist Socialists' Party of Catalonia (PSC-PSOE) returned to office.

### Language and Culture
Catalan is the "own language" of Catalonia and is co-official with Spanish and Aranese (Occitan). Language policy since the 1980s has aimed at "linguistic normalisation" to promote Catalan use in public life and education. Spanish remains widely spoken, especially in urban areas.

Catalan culture is rich and distinct, with notable contributions in art (Dalí, Miró, Gaudí), literature, and traditions like *castells* (human towers) and the *sardana* dance. Barcelona is a major international cultural and tourist hub. The region has its own national symbols, including the *Senyera* flag and the National Day on 11 September (Diada), commemorating the 1714 defeat.

### Economy
Catalonia is one of Spain's most industrialized and economically dynamic regions, with a GDP comparable to that of entire European countries. Its economy is diversified, with strong secondary (37%) and tertiary (60%) sectors. Key industries include tourism, manufacturing, and services. Barcelona is a major port and a leading European hub for startups. The region faced significant debt after the 2008 financial crisis and the 2017 political turmoil, which also prompted some major companies to relocate their headquarters from Catalonia.

## Terms
- **Generalitat de Catalunya:** The institution of self-government of Catalonia, comprising the Parliament, the President, and the Executive Council.
- **Statute of Autonomy:** The fundamental organic law of Catalonia, second only to the Spanish Constitution, which defines its powers and institutions.
- **Corts Catalanes:** The historical parliamentary body of the Principality of Catalonia, with legislative power dating back to the 13th century.
- **Nueva Planta decrees:** A set of laws issued by Philip V after 1714 that abolished the separate institutions and laws of the Crown of Aragon, including Catalonia.
- **Renaixença:** A 19th-century cultural and political renaissance movement that revived the Catalan language and culture.
- **Senyera:** The flag of Catalonia, featuring four red bars on a golden background, based on the coat of arms of the Crown of Aragon.
- **Diada:** The National Day of Catalonia, celebrated on 11 September, commemorating the fall of Barcelona in 1714.
- **Mossos d'Esquadra:** The autonomous police force of Catalonia, under the command of the Generalitat.

## Debates and open questions
- The core political debate centers on Catalonia's relationship with Spain, ranging from demands for greater autonomy or federalism to full independence.
- The 2010 Constitutional Court ruling on the 2006 Statute of Autonomy is seen by many Catalans as a breach of trust and a key catalyst for the surge in pro-independence sentiment.
- The legitimacy and consequences of the 2017 independence referendum and unilateral declaration remain deeply contested between pro-independence and unionist factions, and between Catalan and Spanish institutions.
- The economic impact of the political instability, including corporate relocations and debt levels, is a subject of ongoing analysis and debate.
