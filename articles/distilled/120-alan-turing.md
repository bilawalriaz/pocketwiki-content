# Alan Turing

## Overview

Alan Mathison Turing (1912–1954) was an English mathematician, computer scientist, logician, cryptanalyst, philosopher, and theoretical biologist. He is widely considered the father of theoretical computer science for formalizing the concepts of algorithm and computation through the Turing machine, a model of a general-purpose computer. During World War II, he led critical codebreaking efforts at Bletchley Park that helped the Allies defeat the Axis powers, and his later work laid foundations for artificial intelligence and mathematical biology. His life ended tragically following prosecution for homosexual acts, then criminal in the UK, leading to a posthumous government apology and royal pardon decades later.

## Body

### Early life and education

Turing was born in London while his father, Julius, an Indian Civil Service officer, was on leave from British India. His parents wanted their children raised in Britain, so Turing and his elder brother John stayed with a retired Army couple while their parents travelled between England and India. At age ten, Turing discovered *Natural Wonders Every Child Should Know*, which he credited with opening his eyes to science. He attended St Michael's primary school, where the headmistress called him a genius, then Hazelhurst Preparatory School, and from age 13, Sherborne School. His first day coincided with the 1926 General Strike; determined to attend, he rode his bicycle 60 miles from Southampton unaccompanied. His teachers emphasized classics over science, and his headmaster warned his parents he risked "falling between two stools," but Turing showed remarkable mathematical ability, solving advanced problems in 1927 without having studied calculus.

At Sherborne, Turing formed a deep friendship with Christopher Morcom, described as his first love. Morcom died in February 1930 from complications of bovine tuberculosis. Turing's grief was profound; he corresponded with Morcom's mother for years and wrote on spiritual matters, speculating about the connection between spirit and body. It has been speculated that Morcom's death caused Turing's atheism and his conviction that all phenomena must have materialistic explanations.

### Computability and the Turing machine

Turing studied the Mathematical Tripos at King's College, Cambridge from 1931 to 1934, earning first-class honours. His dissertation proved a version of the central limit theorem (unknown to him, already proven by Jarl Waldemar Lindeberg in 1922, but the committee found Turing's methods original). Between 1935 and 1936, working concurrently with Alonzo Church, Turing tackled the decidability of problems, starting from Gödel's incompleteness theorems. His 1936 paper "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem" reformulated Gödel's results using hypothetical devices now known as Turing machines. The Entscheidungsproblem (decision problem), posed by David Hilbert in 1928, asked whether there exists an algorithm to decide the truth of any mathematical statement. Turing proved no such solution exists by showing the halting problem—whether a Turing machine will ever stop—is undecidable. He also introduced the "Universal Machine" (now the universal Turing machine), capable of performing any computation representable as an algorithm. According to the Church–Turing thesis, Turing machines and Church's lambda calculus can compute anything computable. Turing's approach was more accessible than Church's, and John von Neumann acknowledged the central concept of the modern computer was due to Turing's paper.

### Cryptanalysis at Bletchley Park

From September 1938, Turing worked part-time with the Government Code and Cypher School (GC&CS). After the Polish Cipher Bureau shared details of the Enigma machine's wiring in July 1939, Turing and Dilly Knox developed a broader solution. The Polish method relied on an insecure indicator procedure the Germans later changed; Turing's approach used crib-based decryption (fragments of probable plaintext), for which he specified the bombe, an electromechanical machine improving on the Polish bomba. He reported to Bletchley Park on 4 September 1939, the day after Britain declared war on Germany, and signed the Official Secrets Act.

Turing made five major cryptanalytical advances: specifying the bombe; deducing the German navy's indicator procedure; developing Banburismus, a statistical technique using a weight-of-evidence measure called the "ban" to rule out rotor sequences; developing Turingery for working out cam settings of the Lorenz SZ 40/42 cipher machine; and developing Delilah, a portable secure voice scrambler. His statistical papers on probability in cryptography were kept classified by GCHQ until April 2012. Official war historian Harry Hinsley estimated this work shortened the war in Europe by more than two years.

In late 1941, frustrated by limited resources, Turing and colleagues wrote directly to Winston Churchill, who responded "ACTION THIS DAY," ensuring priority support. More than two hundred bombes were in operation by war's end. Turing tackled naval Enigma "because no one else was doing anything about it." He travelled to the US in November 1942 to work with US Navy cryptanalysts. Hugh Alexander, who succeeded Turing as head of Hut 8, wrote that Turing's work was "the biggest factor in Hut 8's success" and that if anyone was indispensable, it was Turing.

### Early computers and artificial intelligence

After the war, Turing designed the Automatic Computing Engine (ACE) at the National Physical Laboratory, presenting the first detailed design of a stored-program computer in February 1946. The Official Secrets Act prevented him from explaining his analysis, causing delays and disillusionment. The Pilot ACE ran its first program on 10 May 1950. In 1948, Turing joined the University of Manchester, becoming deputy director of the Computing Machine Laboratory, where he wrote the first Programmer's Manual for the Manchester Mark 1 and consulted for Ferranti. In "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950), Turing proposed the Turing test: a computer could be said to "think" if a human interrogator could not distinguish it from a human through conversation. He suggested building a program to simulate a child's mind and subjecting it to education. A reversed form, CAPTCHA, is widely used on the Internet today. Turing also wrote a chess program, Turochamp, which he "ran" by hand since computers lacked power.

### Mathematical biology

In 1951, Turing turned to mathematical biology, publishing "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis" in January 1952. He proposed that a reaction–diffusion system—chemicals reacting and diffusing across space—could account for pattern formation in organisms. He modelled catalytic reactions with partial differential equations, showing that patterns emerge when a catalyst A and inhibitor B diffuse at different rates. Lacking powerful computers, he used linear approximations, producing correct qualitative results. His work predicted oscillating chemical reactions such as the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction, first observed in the 1960s. Published before DNA's structure was understood, it remains seminal in mathematical biology, explaining patterns including cat fur spots, feather growth, and left-right asymmetry. A 2023 study with chia seeds experimentally confirmed his mathematical model.

### Prosecution and death

In January 1952, Turing's house was burgled; during the police investigation, he acknowledged a sexual relationship with 19-year-old Arnold Murray. Homosexual acts were criminal offences, and both were charged with gross indecency under the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885. Turing pleaded guilty and was sentenced to 12 months' probation conditional on undergoing organotherapy ("chemical castration")—injections of stilboestrol (diethylstilbestrol), a synthetic oestrogen that rendered him impotent and caused breast tissue growth. His conviction removed his security clearance and barred him from GCHQ consultancy. He was denied entry to the US but visited Norway in summer 1952.

On 8 June 1954, his housekeeper found him dead; the post mortem determined he died the previous day from cyanide poisoning, aged 41. An apple lay half-eaten beside his bed, though it was not tested for cyanide. The inquest determined suicide. His mother never accepted this verdict. Biographers Andrew Hodges and David Leavitt speculated he was re-enacting the poisoned apple scene from *Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs*, his favourite fairy tale. Doubts persist: his nephew Dermot Turing and philosopher Jack Copeland suggested accidental inhalation of cyanide fumes from an electroplating apparatus in his spare room; Copeland noted autopsy findings were more consistent with inhalation than ingestion, and Turing had shown no despondency, even listing tasks for after the holiday weekend.

### Apology and pardon

In August 2009, a petition urging government apology gathered over 30,000 signatures. Prime Minister Gordon Brown issued an official apology on 10 September 2009, calling Turing's treatment "appalling." A 2012 e-petition for a pardon gathered over 37,000 signatures; MP John Leech led a campaign through Parliament. On 24 December 2013, Queen Elizabeth II signed a pardon—only the fourth royal pardon since World War II, and unusual because pardons normally require technical innocence and a family request. In 2016, the government announced an "Alan Turing law," enacted in the Policing and Crime Act 2017, retroactively pardoning men cautioned or convicted under historical legislation outlawing homosexual acts in England and Wales, securing pardons for 75,000 others.

## Terms

- **Turing machine**: A hypothetical device that formalizes computation; reads and writes symbols on an infinite tape according to rules, capable of any algorithmically computable function.
- **Universal Turing machine**: A Turing machine that can simulate any other Turing machine—the conceptual basis of the stored-program computer.
- **Entscheidungsproblem**: The "decision problem" posed by David Hilbert in 1928: whether an algorithm can decide the truth of any mathematical statement. Turing proved it unsolvable.
- **Halting problem**: The question of whether a Turing machine will eventually stop; Turing proved it undecidable, establishing limits of computation.
- **Bombe**: An electromechanical machine specified by Turing that searched Enigma settings using cribs and logical contradiction detection.
- **Banburismus**: Turing's sequential statistical technique for ruling out Enigma rotor sequences, using a weight-of-evidence unit called the "ban."
- **Turingery**: Turing's method for working out cam settings of the Lorenz cipher machine's wheels.
- **Turing test**: A proposed standard for machine intelligence: a computer "thinks" if a human interrogator cannot distinguish it from a human through conversation.
- **Reaction–diffusion system**: Turing's mathematical model of chemicals reacting and diffusing to create biological patterns (morphogenesis).
- **Chemical castration**: Hormone treatment (stilboestrol injections) Turing accepted as an alternative to prison after his 1952 conviction.

## Debates and open questions

The cause of Turing's death remains debated. The inquest ruled suicide, but the evidence is also consistent with accidental poisoning. Jack Copeland argued the autopsy findings better fit inhalation of cyanide fumes from an electroplating apparatus than ingestion, noting Turing habitually ate an apple before bed and had shown no despondency. Turing's nephew Dermot Turing rejected the suicide theory, pointing out the conviction and treatment had ended years earlier and no physiological evidence linked them to his mental health. Turing's mother believed the death was accidental, resulting from careless storage of laboratory chemicals. Biographer Andrew Hodges theorized Turing deliberately made his death look accidental to shield his mother from the knowledge of suicide. John W. Dawson Jr. questioned the suicide verdict, noting Turing was found "lying neatly in his bed"—hardly expected of a man fighting cyanide-induced suffocation—and had made no effort to put his affairs in order.
