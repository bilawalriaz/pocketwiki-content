# Gothic art

## Overview
Gothic art was a major medieval artistic style that emerged in 12th-century France and spread across Europe, defined by its close relationship with Gothic architecture. It marked a shift from the more abstract Romanesque style towards greater naturalism, human emotion, and the use of new media like stained glass and panel painting. The style evolved through phases, including the International Gothic court style, and eventually merged into the Renaissance, though it persisted in some regions into the 16th century.

## Timeline
- **Early 12th century** — Gothic art originates in Île-de-France, France, at the Abbey Church of St Denis.
- **c. 1145** — Sculptures on the Western Portal at Chartres Cathedral show an early, elongated Gothic style.
- **c. 1200** — Gothic painting begins to develop in England and France, over 50 years after Gothic architecture.
- **1215–1220** — More naturalistic sculpture appears on the south transept portal of Chartres Cathedral.
- **Mid-13th century** — The earliest full manuscripts with French Gothic illustrations are created.
- **1253–1270** — The Parisian Psalter of Saint Louis is illuminated with 78 full-page paintings.
- **Late 14th century** — The sophisticated "International Gothic" court style develops.
- **1420s–1430s** — Early Netherlandish painters like Robert Campin and Jan van Eyck innovate with oil painting and realism.
- **Late 15th century** — International Gothic continues to evolve; printed books with illustrations become accessible.
- **16th century** — Late Gothic art in regions like Germany is gradually absorbed into the Renaissance.

## Body

### Origins and Spread
Gothic art emerged in the early 12th century in the Île-de-France region, concurrent with the development of Gothic architecture. Its earliest form was monumental sculpture on cathedral walls. The style rapidly spread across Western and Central Europe, disseminated by monastic orders like the Cistercians. While it never fully replaced more classical styles in Italy, it evolved into a coherent "International Gothic" style by the late 14th century.

### Religious Themes and Marian Devotion
Gothic art was predominantly religious, often typological—showing Old and New Testament stories side-by-side to highlight their connection. A major theme was the resurgence of Marian devotion, which transformed depictions of the Virgin Mary from a formal Byzantine icon into a more human, affectionate mother. This shift reflected broader changes in theology and devotional practices, which also led to new, emotionally charged images of Christ's suffering, such as the Pietà.

### Secular Art and Patronage
The rise of cities, trade, and a wealthy bourgeois class during the Gothic period led to a proliferation of secular art. Increased literacy and vernacular literature encouraged themes from romances and daily life. Artists were often members of guilds, leading to better record-keeping and more known artists by name. Secular works included illuminated manuscripts for private devotion (like Books of Hours), ivory carvings, and metalwork, though fewer have survived than religious art.

### Painting in Various Media
Gothic painting developed later than sculpture and architecture, with a gradual transition from Romanesque styles. It was practiced in four primary media:
*   **Frescoes** remained the main narrative craft on church walls in southern Europe, with well-preserved examples in Scandinavian churches.
*   **Stained glass** became a prestigious art form in northern Europe, enabled by Gothic architecture's large windows. Techniques evolved to allow more color variation.
*   **Illuminated manuscripts** provide the most complete record of Gothic painting styles. The period saw the creation of royal bibles, psalters, and later, Books of Hours for the laity.
*   **Panel painting** became innovative in the 15th century, especially with Early Netherlandish artists who used oil paint to achieve minute detail and complex symbolism.

### Sculpture: Monumental and Portable
Sculpture evolved from the elongated, wall-attached figures of early Gothic portals (like Chartres) to more naturalistic, freestanding forms. In Italy, sculptors like Nicola Pisano developed a Proto-Renaissance style influenced by Roman art. Later Gothic sculpture featured large, expressive wooden altarpieces in Germany. Portable sculpture, such as small ivory devotional figures and mirror-cases, became a major industry, particularly in Paris, catering to a lay and often female market.

## Terms
*   **Gothic Art:** A medieval artistic style originating in 12th-century France, characterized by its association with Gothic architecture and a move towards naturalism.
*   **Typological:** An artistic approach that pairs Old Testament events with New Testament events to show them as prefiguring each other.
*   **International Gothic:** A refined, courtly style of art that evolved in the late 14th century and spread across Europe.
*   **Fresco:** A technique of mural painting executed on freshly laid lime plaster, using water-based pigments.
*   **Illuminated Manuscript:** A manuscript in which the text is supplemented with decoration such as gold or silver, miniature paintings, and elaborate borders.
*   **Panel Painting:** A painting on a flat panel made of wood, a common format for altarpieces and devotional images before the widespread use of canvas.
*   **Tympanum:** The semi-circular decorative wall surface over an entrance, bounded by a lintel and arch, often filled with sculpture in Gothic churches.
*   **Devotio Moderna:** A movement for religious reform in the late medieval period, emphasizing personal piety and the imitation of Christ's humanity.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The precise origins of the Gothic style are debated, with scholars considering possible influences from Lombard, Frankish, and Norse traditions, as well as deeper psychological roots.
*   The transition from Romanesque to Gothic painting is described as "very imprecise and not at all a clear break," making it difficult to define a sharp starting point for the style in painting.
*   The term "Gothic" was initially used pejoratively by Renaissance critics like Giorgio Vasari to mean "barbaric," and its eventual acceptance as a neutral art-historical term was a gradual process.
