# Science

Science is a systematic discipline that builds and organizes knowledge in the form of testable explanations about nature and society. It is driven by the scientific method: an empirical cycle involving making observations, producing hypotheses, testing them with evidence, and drawing conclusions. The scientific community continuously challenges, validates, and organizes this body of knowledge. Modern science is typically divided into natural sciences (studying the physical world), social sciences (studying individuals and societies), and formal sciences (logic, mathematics, theoretical computer science), which rely on deductive reasoning rather than the empirical method. Applied sciences, such as engineering and medicine, use scientific knowledge for practical purposes.

## Timeline

- **c. 3000–1200 BCE** — Earliest written records of science appear in Bronze Age Egypt and Mesopotamia (mathematics, astronomy, medicine)
- **4th century BCE** — Aristotle creates a systematic programme of teleological philosophy

- **6th century CE** — John Philoponus questions Aristotle's physics, introducing the theory of impetus
- **1088** — University of Bologna emerges as the first university in Europe
- **c. 1440** — Movable type printing press spreads from Mainz, making scholarly books widely accessible

- **1858–1859** — Darwin and Wallace propose evolution by natural selection; Darwin publishes *On the Origin of Species*

- **2003–2019** — Human Genome Project completed (2003); Higgs boson confirmed (2013); gravitational waves observed (2015); first black hole image (2019)

## Body

### Etymology and Definition

The word *science* has been used in English since the 14th century, meaning "the state of knowing," borrowed from the Latin *scientia* ('knowledge, awareness, understanding'). In the past, science was a synonym for "knowledge" or "study." In 1834, William Whewell introduced the term *scientist* in a review of Mary Somerville's book, crediting it to "some ingenious gentleman" (possibly himself). Previously, researchers were called "natural philosophers" or "men of science."

### Early History and Classical Antiquity

Science has no single origin; scientific thinking emerged gradually over tens of thousands of years. Some scholars use "protoscience" to label past activities resembling modern science, though this label is criticised as denigrating or presentist. Direct evidence becomes clearer with writing systems in Ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia (c. 3000–1200 BCE). Egyptians developed a non-positional decimal numbering system, geometry, and a calendar; Mesopotamians studied natural chemicals for manufacturing and had intense interest in medicine.

In classical antiquity, there was no real analogue of a modern scientist; well-educated, usually upper-class, almost universally male individuals investigated nature. The pre-Socratic philosophers first distinguished "nature" from "convention." The Milesian school, founded by Thales, first attempted to explain natural phenomena without the supernatural. The Pythagoreans developed number philosophy; Leucippus and Democritus developed atomic theory; Hippocrates established systematic medical science. Socrates applied philosophy to human matters, developing the Socratic method of hypothesis elimination. Aristotle created a systematic teleological philosophy in the 4th century BCE. Aristarchus proposed heliocentrism, but Ptolemy's geocentric *Almagest* was accepted instead. Positional notation for numbers emerged between the 3rd and 5th centuries CE along Indian trade routes.

### Middle Ages and Renaissance

After the Western Roman Empire's collapse, knowledge deteriorated in Western Europe, though Latin encyclopaedists like Isidore of Seville preserved ancient knowledge. The Byzantine Empire preserved and improved prior learning; John Philoponus questioned Aristotle's physics, introducing the theory of impetus, which inspired Galileo. Under the Abbasids, Arabic translations of Greek texts were improved by Arabic scientists. Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen) used controlled experiments in optics; Avicenna's *Canon of Medicine* was used until the 18th century. The University of Bologna emerged in 1088, and the Renaissance of the 12th century saw experiments by observing, describing, and classifying nature.

The movable type printing press (c. 1440) ended manuscript culture, allowing researchers to consult ancient texts and compare observations. In the 16th century, Copernicus formulated a heliocentric model challenging Ptolemy's geocentric system, though it only fell out of favour 70 years later after Galileo's telescopic observations of 1610.

### Scientific Revolution to 19th Century

Tycho Brahe's accurate observations and Galileo's telescopic observations turned astronomy into the first modern science. Kepler used Brahe's data to discover elliptical orbits and laws of planetary motion. The "New Science" of the 17th century was more mechanistic, integrated with mathematics, and focused on new evidence. At the start of the Enlightenment, Newton formed classical mechanics; Leibniz incorporated Aristotelian terms in a new non-teleological way. Science's declared purpose became producing wealth and inventions. Scientific societies and academies replaced universities as research centres. The 18th century saw advances in medicine, physics, biological taxonomy (Linnaeus), magnetism, electricity, and chemistry; Adam Smith published *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), often considered the first work on modern economics.

The 19th century saw professionalisation, precision instruments, and terms like "biologist" and "physicist." Darwin and Wallace independently proposed evolution by natural selection (1858); Mendel outlined principles of inheritance (1865). Dalton suggested modern atomic theory; the laws of thermodynamics developed from understanding that not all energy forms have equal quality. Electromagnetic theory was established by Ørsted, Ampère, Faraday, Maxwell, Heaviside, and Hertz. X-rays inspired the discovery of radioactivity by Becquerel and Curie (1896); the electron was discovered in 1897.

### 20th and 21st Centuries

Antibiotics and artificial fertilisers improved living standards; environmental issues like ozone depletion and climate change came to public attention. World Wars and the Cold War led to competitions like the Space Race. The cosmic microwave background discovery (1964) led to rejection of the steady-state model in favour of the Big Bang theory. The modern synthesis reconciled Darwinian evolution with genetics; relativity and quantum mechanics complemented classical mechanics. Integrated circuits and communications satellites led to the information technology revolution. The Human Genome Project was completed in 2003; induced pluripotent stem cells were made in 2006; the Higgs boson was confirmed in 2013; gravitational waves were first observed in 2015; the first black hole image was presented in 2019.

### Branches of Science

Natural science studies the physical world, divided into life science and physical science (physics, chemistry, astronomy, earth science). Social science studies human behaviour and societies, including anthropology, economics, history, political science, psychology, and sociology; it often uses historical methods, case studies, and statistical approaches due to limitations on controlled experiments. Formal science generates knowledge using formal systems (abstract structures for inferring theorems from axioms), relying exclusively on deductive reasoning; there is disagreement on whether it constitutes a science. Applied science uses scientific knowledge for practical goals (engineering, medicine); basic science focuses on advancing theories and laws. Computational science applies computer simulations and machine learning; interdisciplinary science combines disciplines like bioinformatics.

### Research and Scientific Method

Scientific research is labelled basic (search for knowledge) or applied (search for solutions). The scientific method assumes objective reality governed by natural laws discoverable through systematic observation and experimentation. Hypotheses are put forward using parsimony principles, seeking consilience with accepted facts, and are used to make falsifiable predictions tested by experimentation. Surviving hypotheses may become scientific theories—validly reasoned, self-consistent frameworks describing broader sets of observations. Eliminating bias requires transparency, careful design, and peer review. Intersubjective verifiability—the ability to reach consensus and reproduce results—is fundamental. The first scientific journals appeared in 1665 (*Journal des sçavans* and *Philosophical Transactions*); by 1981, an estimated 11,500 scientific and technical journals were in publication.

### Philosophy of Science

The most popular position is empiricism, holding that knowledge is created by observation; it contrasts with rationalism (associated with Descartes), which holds that knowledge is created by the human intellect. Karl Popper's critical rationalism rejected empiricism's description of theory-observation connection, proposing falsifiability instead of verifiability as the landmark of scientific theories. Instrumentalism emphasises theories as instruments for explaining and predicting, viewing them as black boxes. Thomas Kuhn argued that observation and evaluation occur within a paradigm; normal science is "puzzle solving" within a paradigm, while revolutionary science occurs in paradigm shifts when anomalies accumulate. Methodological naturalism maintains science should be restricted to natural explanations. The is-ought problem highlights that facts alone cannot tell us what we should do, connecting to value-ladenness.

### Community and Society

The scientific community is a network of interacting scientists maintaining quality through peer review. Science has historically been male-dominated, with women facing discrimination and denial of credit. Learned societies have existed since the Renaissance; national academies include the Accademia dei Lincei (1603), Royal Society (1660), and French Academy of Sciences (1666). The Nobel Prize is awarded annually for advances in medicine, physics, and chemistry. Research funding is competitive; most developed countries spend 1.5–3% of GDP on research, with about two-thirds of OECD research carried out by industry. Science policy seeks to consider how science and technology can best serve the public. Anti-science attitudes can stem from perceptions of authorities as biased, past exploitation of marginalised groups, contradictions with deeply held beliefs, or fear of social rejection. The replication crisis (coined early 2010s) affects parts of science, with low replication rates found in social and behavioural sciences. Scientific misconduct involves intentionally misrepresented data; pseudoscience masquerades as science to claim legitimacy. Academic freedom can be reduced by censorship, and political bias can exist in science.

## Terms

- **Scientific method**: Empirical cycle of making observations, producing hypotheses, testing them with evidence, and drawing conclusions
- **Hypothesis**: A tentative explanation put forward to explain an observation, used to make falsifiable predictions
- **Theory**: A validly reasoned, self-consistent model or framework describing the behaviour of certain natural events, typically covering broader sets of observations than a hypothesis
- **Empiricism**: The position that knowledge is created by a process involving observation; scientific theories generalise observations
- **Falsifiability**: Popper's proposed landmark of scientific theories—the capacity for a theory to be proven false by observation
- **Paradigm**: A logically consistent "portrait" of the world consistent with observations made from its framing (Kuhn)
- **Protoscience**: A label for past activities resembling modern science in some but not all features
- **Teleological philosophy**: Aristotle's systematic programme explaining phenomena in terms of final causes or purposes
- **Replication crisis**: An ongoing systemic crisis where results of a fraction of scientific studies prove unreproducible
- **Pseudoscience**: An area of study or speculation that masquerades as science to claim illegitimate legitimacy

## Debates and open questions

- Whether formal sciences (mathematics, logic, theoretical computer science) constitute a science, given their reliance on deductive reasoning rather than empirical evidence
- Whether the label "protoscience" for past activities is denigrating or too suggestive of presentism
- The replication crisis: a fraction of scientific studies have proven unreproducible, with low replication rates in social and behavioural sciences
- Whether empiricism or rationalism correctly describes how scientific knowledge is created; Popper's falsificationism versus verification
- Kuhn's claim that paradigm acceptance or rejection is a social process as much as a logical process, though not relativism
- The is-ought problem: how scientific evidence and theories can lead to decisions, given that facts alone cannot tell us what we should do
