# Wrocław

## Overview
Wrocław is a major city in southwestern Poland and the historical capital of the Silesia region. Its strategic location on the Oder River made it a crossroads of trade and a prize for successive empires, shaping a complex, multicultural history. Today, it is a vibrant academic, economic, and cultural hub, known for its reconstructed heritage, large student population, and modern innovation.

## Timeline
- **c. 940** — City founded at the intersection of the Via Regia and Amber Road trade routes.
- **1000** — Diocese of Wrocław established at the Congress of Gniezno; city first mentioned in Thietmar's Chronicle.
- **1241** — City abandoned and burned during the first Mongol invasion of Poland.
- **1335** — Falls to John of Luxembourg; later incorporated into the Holy Roman Empire (1348).
- **1518** — Protestant Reformation reaches the city.
- **1742** — Most of Silesia, including Breslau, is annexed by Prussia after the Treaty of Breslau.
- **1811** — Protestant Viadrina University merges with local Jesuit University to form the Silesian Frederick-William University (now University of Wrocław).
- **1945** — After a brutal siege, the city becomes part of Poland following the Potsdam Conference; German population expelled, Polish settlers arrive.
- **1997** — The "Millennium Flood" devastates large parts of the city.
- **2016** — Wrocław is named European Capital of Culture and UNESCO World Book Capital.
- **2017** — Hosts the World Games.

## Body

### Historical Identity and Name
Wrocław's history spans over a millennium, with control shifting between Polish, Bohemian, Austrian, Prussian, and German rule. The origin of its name is debated; it likely derives from the Old Polish given name *Wrocisław*, not Duke Vratislav I of Bohemia. The German name *Breslau* evolved from the original Slavic form. The city was a major Polish center in the early Middle Ages, listed as one of three capitals by Gallus Anonymus. After the Mongol invasion (1241), German settlers gradually became the dominant population, though the city retained a multi-ethnic character. It passed to the Bohemian Crown (1335), then to the Habsburgs, and was annexed by Prussia in 1742, becoming a major German city. The 1910 census recorded 95.7% German-speakers, with a significant Jewish community.

### 20th Century Upheaval and Transformation
Under Nazi rule, Breslau became a stronghold of the party, persecuting Poles, Jews, and political opponents. During World War II, it was declared a fortress (*Festung*) and endured a devastating siege by the Soviet Army in 1945, which destroyed half the city. Following the Yalta and Potsdam Conferences, the city was transferred to Poland. The pre-war German population was expelled, and Wrocław was repopulated by Poles, many resettled from former eastern territories annexed by the Soviet Union (like Lviv). This dramatic demographic shift reversed the city's ethnic composition. Post-war reconstruction focused on restoring historical landmarks, though traces of the German past were systematically removed.

### Modern City and Economy
Since 1945, Wrocław has rebuilt itself as a Polish city with a mixed architectural heritage. It is now Poland's third-largest city and a major economic and academic center. The city is home to over 130,000 students and numerous universities, including the historic University of Wrocław. Its economy is diversified, with strong sectors in high-tech, R&D, manufacturing (buses, electronics), and services. Wrocław is a significant transport hub with an international airport, major railway junction, and highway connections. It hosted matches for UEFA Euro 2012 and the 2017 World Games. The city is known for its cultural landmarks, such as the UNESCO-listed Centennial Hall, the Main Market Square, and the symbolic "Wrocław dwarfs" figurines.

## Terms
*   **Gord:** An early Slavic fortified settlement or stronghold.
*   **Magdeburg rights:** A set of town laws originating from the German city of Magdeburg, granting self-governance and commercial privileges, widely adopted in medieval Central Europe.
*   **Festung (Fortress):** A city declared by Hitler to be defended to the last man, resulting in its near-total destruction during siege warfare.
*   **Kresy:** The former eastern borderlands of Poland, annexed by the Soviet Union after WWII; many Poles from these regions were resettled in Wrocław.
*   **Voivodeship:** The largest administrative division of Poland, equivalent to a province or state.
*   **Orange Alternative:** An anti-communist underground movement in 1980s Poland, known for absurd street protests; its legacy is commemorated by Wrocław's dwarfs.
*   **Recovered Territories:** The Polish term for the former German eastern territories assigned to Poland after WWII, including Silesia.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Name Origin:** The traditional theory that the city was named after Duke Vratislav I of Bohemia is contradicted by the timeline of Czech control; the name likely derives from the Old Polish personal name *Wrocisław*.
*   **Ethnic Composition:** The size of the Polish minority in early 20th-century Breslau is debated; official German censuses recorded about 3% Polish-speakers in 1910, while some Polish scholars estimate up to 10% of Polish extraction.
*   **Post-War Demographics:** While the city was repopulated by Poles from the lost eastern territories (*Kresy*), by 1949 only 20% of the new population were actual refugees from those areas; the rest came from other Polish regions.
