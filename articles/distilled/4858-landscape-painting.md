# Landscape painting

## Overview
Landscape painting is the depiction of natural scenery—such as mountains, valleys, rivers, and trees—where the primary subject is a wide view. It is a major genre in both Western and East Asian art, with distinct traditions and historical developments. In the West, it was historically considered inferior to history painting but rose to prominence in the 19th century, while in East Asia, particularly China, it has been the most prestigious art form for centuries, deeply connected to philosophical and spiritual traditions.

## Timeline
- **c. 1500 BCE** — Earliest known "pure landscapes" (no human figures) appear as frescos in Minoan art.
- **1st century BCE** — Roman landscape frescos survive from sites like Pompeii and Herculaneum.
- **8th century** — Wang Wei (699–759) is credited with the decisive shift to monochrome Chinese landscape painting.
- **14th century** — Giotto and followers begin reintroducing landscape elements as backgrounds in Western art.
- **Early 16th century** — Albrecht Altdorfer and the German Danube School produce early pure landscape paintings.
- **1559–1561** — Publication of the *Small Landscapes* prints signals a shift toward close-up, realistic views in the Low Countries.
- **17th century** — Dutch Golden Age sees dramatic growth and specialization in landscape painting.
- **18th century** — Watercolour landscape painting becomes an English specialty; topographical prints are popular.
- **19th century** — Romanticism intensifies interest in wild landscapes; landscape becomes the dominant art form in Europe.
- **Mid-19th century** — The Hudson River School emerges in the U.S., creating monumental landscapes.
- **Late 19th century** — Japanese ukiyo-e landscape prints by Hokusai and Hiroshige gain wide popularity.

## Body

### Origins and Early Traditions
Detailed landscapes as a distinct subject are not universal in art and develop only with sophisticated traditions for other subjects. The earliest "pure landscapes" are Minoan frescos (c. 1500 BCE). Ancient Roman landscapes survive as frescos, while Chinese landscape painting, or *shan shui* ("mountain-water"), became a classic, highly prestigious tradition. A major contrast is that in the West, landscape painting occupied a low position in the genre hierarchy until the 19th century, whereas in East Asia, the imaginary landscape was the most prestigious form of visual art.

### The Western Tradition
In medieval Western art, interest in landscape largely disappeared, reviving in the 14th century with artists like Giotto. Landscape painting was established as a genre in the 15th century, often as a setting for religious scenes. The Renaissance saw the development of perspective and the "world landscape"—a panoramic style with a high viewpoint. The 17th century, especially the Dutch Golden Age, brought dramatic growth and realist techniques. Landscape painting's status rose significantly in the 19th century with Romanticism and movements like the Barbizon School and Impressionism, which made it a primary source of stylistic innovation.

### The East Asian Tradition
Chinese landscape painting, deeply influenced by Daoism, is considered China's greatest artistic contribution. The monochrome ink tradition, emphasizing brushwork and spiritual contemplation, was perfected during the Song dynasty. Japanese art initially adapted Chinese styles but developed its own schools, such as the professional Kanō school and the later ukiyo-e woodblock prints, which made landscape art widely accessible. In both cultures, the ideal was often an imaginary, contemplative view rather than a topographical record.

### Techniques and Related Genres
Techniques evolved to simulate natural forms, from copying ragged rocks to using random ink blots. While early landscapes were mostly imaginary, artists increasingly sketched from nature, though widespread outdoor painting (*en plein air*) only became common with the invention of portable paints in the 1870s. Related genres include seascapes, cityscapes, and moonscapes. The term "landscape" itself entered English from the Dutch *landschap* in the early 17th century, initially meaning only a painting of a landscape.

## Terms
- **Landscape painting**: The depiction of natural scenery as the primary subject.
- **Shan shui**: The Chinese ink painting tradition meaning "mountain-water," focused on imaginary, spiritual landscapes.
- **Topographical view**: A picture whose primary purpose is to depict an actual, specific place accurately.
- **World landscape**: A panoramic style of landscape painting with a high aerial viewpoint, popular in the 16th century.
- **En plein air**: The practice of painting outdoors, directly from the subject.
- **Genre hierarchy**: The traditional ranking of artistic subjects, with history painting at the top and landscape often lower in the West.
- **Literati painting**: In China, works by amateur scholar-gentlemen, often poets, which were highly valued over professional work.
- **Ukiyo-e**: A Japanese art genre of paintings and woodblock prints, which included famous landscape series.
