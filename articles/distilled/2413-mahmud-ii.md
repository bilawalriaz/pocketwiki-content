# Mahmud II

## Overview
Mahmud II (r. 1808–1839) was an Ottoman sultan who initiated sweeping reforms to modernize the empire's administration, military, and legal systems. Often called the "Peter the Great of Turkey," his reign was pivotal in laying the foundations for the subsequent Tanzimat era of modernization, though it also saw significant territorial losses and military defeats.

- **1 July 1839** — Dies of tuberculosis; succeeded by his son Abdülmecid I.

## Body

### Accession and Early Reign
Mahmud II came to power in 1808 following a coup against his half-brother, Mustafa IV, who had ordered his execution. He was saved by his mother and a slave, Cevri Kalfa, and placed on the throne by the rebel leader Alemdar Mustafa Pasha, who became his vizier. Early in his reign, the empire faced external pressures, including the Russo-Turkish War of 1806–12, which concluded with the Treaty of Bucharest in 1812. The treaty ceded Bessarabia to Russia but allowed the Ottomans to regain territories in Transcaucasia.

### Military Crises and Territorial Losses
Mahmud's reign was marked by significant military setbacks. The Greek War of Independence, beginning in 1821, culminated in the Ottoman defeat at the Battle of Navarino (1827) and the forced recognition of Greek independence in 1832. Concurrently, the Russo-Turkish War of 1828–29 resulted in further territorial concessions to Russia. The French conquest of Ottoman Algeria in 1830 further signaled the empire's decline. These losses underscored the urgent need for internal reform.

### The Auspicious Incident and Military Reform
A cornerstone of Mahmud's reforms was the abolition of the Janissary Corps in 1826, an event known as the Auspicious Incident. When the Janissaries protested his proposed military modernization, Mahmud used his newly reformed troops to crush them, burning their barracks and executing many. This decisive action removed a major conservative obstacle and paved the way for the creation of a modern, European-style conscript army, the Asakir-i Mansure-i Muhammediye. He also rebuilt the Ottoman navy, acquiring its first steamships in 1828.

### Administrative and Legal Reforms
Mahmud II implemented extensive bureaucratic and legal changes to reassert central authority. He issued edicts (firmans) that curtailed the arbitrary power of provincial governors (Pashas) and established a formal judicial appeal system. He simplified the administration, abolished sinecure offices, and placed the revenues of religious endowments (awqaf) under state control. In 1838, he established the Supreme Council of Judicial Ordinances, and in 1839, he introduced a Council of Ministers. He also founded the official gazette *Takvim-i Vekayi* in 1831 and created the positions of Foreign Minister and Undersecretary in 1836, establishing permanent embassies in Europe.

### Social and Cultural Reforms
Mahmud sought to modernize Ottoman society through standardization and cultural shifts. He mandated the adoption of the fez for military and civilian officials to create a homogeneous appearance, replacing traditional dress. He also relaxed restrictions on alcoholic beverages. To strengthen the state's finances, he reformed the tax system, abolishing corrupt collection practices and establishing commissions to levy the capitation tax (haraç). He attached military fiefs (tımars) to public domains to increase state resources and suppressed the power of hereditary local lords (derebeys).

## Terms
- **Janissaries**: Elite infantry corps of the Ottoman Empire, abolished by Mahmud II in 1826.
- **Tanzimat**: The era of sweeping reforms in the Ottoman Empire that followed Mahmud II's reign.
- **Auspicious Incident**: The 1826 event in which Mahmud II forcibly disbanded the Janissary Corps.
- **Derebeys**: Hereditary local chiefs ("valley lords") whose power Mahmud II sought to suppress.
- **Firman**: An edict or decree issued by the Sultan.
- **Haraç**: A capitation tax levied on non-Muslim subjects.
- **Awqaf**: Inalienable charitable endowments, whose revenues Mahmud placed under state administration.
- **Takvim-i Vekayi**: The first official newspaper in Ottoman Turkish, established in 1831.
- **Asakir-i Mansure-i Muhammediye**: The modern Ottoman army established after the abolition of the Janissaries.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source material does not present explicit scholarly debates, but it frames Mahmud II's legacy within the broader narrative of Ottoman decline and modernization. His reign is pivotal in discussions about whether internal reforms could have reversed the empire's territorial disintegration or merely delayed it. The effectiveness and social impact of his top-down reforms, particularly the forced adoption of the fez and the suppression of traditional institutions like the Janissaries, remain points of historical analysis regarding their acceptance and long-term consequences.
