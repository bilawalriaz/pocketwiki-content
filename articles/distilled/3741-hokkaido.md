# Hokkaido

## Overview
Hokkaido is Japan's second-largest and northernmost main island, forming its own prefecture and region. Historically inhabited by the indigenous Ainu people, it was colonized and renamed by the Meiji government in 1869, a process that dispossessed the Ainu and established the island as a strategic agricultural and industrial frontier for Japan. Its colder climate and vast arable land make it a critical agricultural hub for the nation.

## Timeline
- **c. 15,000 years ago** — Jōmon period culture flourishes in Hokkaido.
- **1457** — Takeda Nobuhiro defeats Ainu leader Koshamain, leading to Matsumae clan dominance.
- **1669–1672** — Shakushain's revolt, the last large-scale Ainu resistance, is crushed.
- **1855–1858** — Tokugawa shogunate takes control of most of Ezochi to counter Russian threats.
- **1869** — Meiji government annexes Ezochi, renames it Hokkaido, and establishes the Development Commission.
- **1871–1873** — American Horace Capron advises on Western agriculture and mining techniques.
- **1899** — Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act further marginalizes the Ainu.
- **1945** — U.S. Navy attacks Hokkaido ports; Soviet invasion plans are halted by the U.S.
- **1947** — Hokkaido becomes a full-fledged prefecture under the revised Local Autonomy Act.
- **1972** — Sapporo hosts the Winter Olympics.
- **2018** — A magnitude 6.6 earthquake causes a island-wide blackout.

## Body

### Names and Identity
The island was historically called Ezo or Ezochi ("land of the barbarians"). The Meiji government adopted the name Hokkaido ("northern sea route") in 1869, a compromise between proposed names. The suffix "-dō" ambiguously means "prefecture." Modern Ainu activists propose alternative names like *aynu mosir* ("land of the humans"), though these terms originally referred to different geographical scopes.

### Early History and Feudal Rule
During the Jōmon period, a hunter-gatherer culture thrived without conflict. Yayoi colonization introduced agriculture. From the Nara period, trade occurred with mainland Japan. During the Muromachi period, Japanese settlements in the south led to conflicts, culminating in the Matsumae clan's victory in 1457. The Matsumae held exclusive trading rights with the Ainu until the Edo period, though Ainu revolts continued until the 18th century.

### Meiji Colonization
Following the Boshin War, the Meiji government annexed Ezochi in 1869 to secure it against Russia and exploit its resources. The Development Commission, led by Kuroda Kiyotaka, hired American experts like Horace Capron to introduce Western agricultural and mining techniques. This state-sponsored colonization involved migrating Japanese settlers, providing them with land and aid, and systematically dispossessing the Ainu through land seizures and assimilation policies, such as the 1899 Protection Act. Prison labor was also used to accelerate development.

### Geography and Climate
Hokkaido is mountainous with volcanic plateaus and major plains like the Ishikari and Tokachi. It has a humid continental climate with cool summers and cold, snowy winters driven by the Siberian High and Aleutian Low. The island is seismically active with several active volcanoes. Its northern portion is in the taiga biome, and winter drift ice affects the Sea of Okhotsk coast.

### Economy and Society
The service sector dominates the economy, but agriculture is vital; Hokkaido has nearly one-fourth of Japan's arable land and leads in producing wheat, potatoes, dairy, and marine products. Tourism is significant, especially for summer and winter sports. The population is 4,985,419 (2025), with the lowest density in Japan. Sapporo is the largest city and fifth-largest in Japan.

## Terms
- **Ainu**: The indigenous people of Hokkaido, historically hunter-gatherers, who were dispossessed and assimilated during Meiji colonization.
- **Ezo/Ezochi**: Historical names for Hokkaido, meaning "land of the barbarians."
- **Hokkaido**: The current name, meaning "northern sea route," adopted in 1869.
- **Development Commission (Kaitakushi)**: The Meiji government agency established in 1869 to colonize and develop Hokkaido.
- **Matsumae clan**: A Japanese feudal family that held exclusive trading rights with the Ainu in southern Ezochi from the 15th to 19th centuries.
- **Seikan Tunnel**: The undersea railway tunnel connecting Hokkaido to Honshu.
- **Subprefecture**: Administrative divisions in Hokkaido that handle local governance due to the island's large size.
- **Yokozuna**: The highest rank in professional sumo; Hokkaido has produced the most yokozuna in Japan.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The origin of the name "Hokkaido" is debated; it may derive from the Ainu word *Kai* or be influenced by the Sino-Japanese reading of *Emishi*.
- Theories differ on the ethnic origins of the Ainu and the people of northern Honshu (Emishi), with the "transformation" theory suggesting gradual change from Jomon peoples and the "replacement" theory positing their displacement by Yayoi immigrants.
- The classification of Hokkaido's colonization as "settler colonialism" is a subject of academic analysis, focusing on the state-sponsored dispossession of the Ainu and importation of Japanese settlers.
