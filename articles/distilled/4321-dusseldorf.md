# Düsseldorf

## Overview
Düsseldorf is the capital of North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany's most populous state, and a major European business, finance, and cultural center. It grew from a small riverside settlement into a regional capital, industrial hub, and modern metropolis known for its trade fairs, fashion, and international community, particularly a large Japanese population.

## Body

### Early History and Duchy of Berg
Düsseldorf originated as a fishing and farming settlement at the mouth of the Düssel River. It came under the rule of the County of Berg in 1186. The pivotal date of 14 August 1288, when it received town privileges following the Battle of Worringen, cemented its status and sparked a historic rivalry with Cologne. Significant growth began under Duke William II of Berg (r. from 1380), who expanded the town and built fortifications. After the ducal line died out in 1609, the Wittelsbachs made Düsseldorf their capital, fostering a period of cultural flourishing under Elector Johann Wilhelm II (r. 1690–1716).

### Prussian and Industrial Era
Following the Napoleonic Wars, Düsseldorf became part of Prussia in 1815, losing its capital status but gaining a role as a regional administrative seat. The 19th century brought rapid industrialization due to its Rhine location and proximity to the Ruhr, with growth in iron, steel, and chemical industries. The city's population soared from 40,000 in 1850 to 100,000 in 1882. Culturally, it was known as the "City of Art and Gardens," home to prominent painters and composers.

### 20th Century to Present
Düsseldorf suffered damage in both World Wars, including strategic bombing and forced labor camps. It was captured by U.S. forces in April 1945. In 1946, it became the capital of North Rhine-Westphalia, cementing its political importance. The post-war "economic miracle" saw it emerge as a key hub for trade fairs, finance, and business. From the 1960s, a significant Japanese community established itself, creating a "Little Tokyo" district. The city transitioned from heavy industry to services, telecommunications, and fashion, with urban renewal projects like the Medienhafen.

### Economy and International Business
Düsseldorf is a leading German economic center with a 2023 GDP of approximately €62.7 billion. It is a major financial hub with over 30,000 employees in the sector, hosting stock exchanges and headquarters for companies like HSBC Germany and NRW.BANK. The city is a global trade fair capital; Messe Düsseldorf organized 27 shows in 2023, attracting over 1.1 million visitors. It hosts numerous international corporate headquarters, including two Fortune Global 500 companies (Uniper, Metro) and three DAX companies (Rheinmetall, Henkel, GEA). The Japanese community is the largest in Germany, with over 6,000 nationals in the city proper.

### Culture and Society
Düsseldorf is a major arts hub, anchored by the Kunstakademie Düsseldorf, which influenced the Düsseldorf School of Painting and Photography. The city has a rich musical legacy, notably as the birthplace of electronic pioneers Kraftwerk and bands like Neu!. Its cultural calendar is highlighted by the Rhenish Carnival, one of Germany's largest. The city is also known for its fashion industry and culinary scene, especially its Altbier beer and traditional Rhenish cuisine. Demographically, as of 2019, 42.6% of residents had a migration background. The largest foreign national groups are Turkish, Ukrainian, and Greek.

## Terms
- **GaWC Alpha- world city**: A classification indicating a high level of international financial and business integration.
- **Messe**: A German term for a trade fair or exhibition center.
- **Stadtbahn**: A light rail system that often runs in tunnels in city centers.
- **Altbier**: A top-fermented, amber-colored beer traditional to Düsseldorf.
- **Karneval**: The Rhenish Carnival, a pre-Lenten festival with parades and celebrations.
- **Wirtschaftswunder**: The "economic miracle" of rapid West German growth in the 1950s and 1960s.
- **DAX**: A German stock market index of the 30 major German companies trading on the Frankfurt Stock Exchange.
- **Radschläger**: A traditional Düsseldorf figure of a boy doing a cartwheel, a city symbol.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes the enduring, often humorous, rivalry with Cologne, which stems from commercial competition after the 1288 Battle of Worringen and extends to football, beer preferences (Altbier vs. Kölsch), and carnival. The origin of the city's "cartwheeler" tradition is also noted as having different theories, indicating a point of local historical debate.
