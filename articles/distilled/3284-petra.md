# Petra

## Overview
Petra is an ancient city in southern Jordan, famous for its rock-cut architecture and advanced water management systems. Carved from rose-red sandstone, it was the capital of the Nabataean Kingdom and a major trading hub on incense routes. Its strategic location and engineering allowed it to flourish in the desert before its decline under Roman rule and eventual abandonment. Today, it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the New 7 Wonders of the World.

## Timeline
- **c. 7000 BC** — Early farmers settle at Beidha, north of Petra.
- **4th century BC** — Nabataeans, a nomadic Arab tribe, settle in the area.
- **2nd century BC** — Petra becomes the capital of the Nabataean Kingdom.
- **1st century AD** — Peak prosperity; Al-Khazneh (Treasury) is constructed.
- **106 AD** — Nabataea is annexed by the Roman Empire as Arabia Petraea.
- **363 AD** — A major earthquake destroys many structures and water systems.
- **4th–7th centuries** — Byzantine period; churches are built, but the city declines.
- **12th century** — Crusaders build fortresses in the area.
- **1812** — Swiss traveler Johann Ludwig Burckhardt rediscovers Petra for the West.
- **1985** — Petra is designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2007** — Named one of the New 7 Wonders of the World.

## Body

### Early History and Nabataean Rise
The area around Petra was inhabited from at least 7000 BC. By the Iron Age (1200–600 BC), it was occupied by the Edomites, who used the mountainous terrain for water storage and trade. The Nabataeans, originally nomadic, settled in the 4th century BC. Archaeological and linguistic evidence confirms they were a northern Arabian tribe. Their name for the city was likely *Raqēmō*. They transformed Petra into a wealthy trading hub by controlling incense routes and developing sophisticated rainwater collection and desert farming techniques.

### Roman and Byzantine Periods
In 106 AD, the Roman Empire annexed Nabataea, renaming the province Arabia Petraea. Petra continued to flourish initially, with Roman roads built and Emperor Hadrian visiting in 130 AD. However, its importance waned as sea trade routes grew and the rival city of Palmyra rose. A devastating earthquake in 363 AD crippled the city's vital water infrastructure. Under Byzantine rule, several churches were built, and papyri from the 6th century indicate the city was still active. By the early Islamic era, Petra was largely abandoned.

### Rediscovery and Modern Era
Petra was lost to the Western world until 1812, when Swiss explorer Johann Ludwig Burckhardt, disguised as an Arab, found it while seeking Aaron's Tomb. Systematic archaeology began in the late 19th century. In the 1980s, the Jordanian government relocated the local Bedul Bedouin community to a nearby settlement to promote tourism and conservation. Petra is now Jordan's most-visited tourist attraction, facing challenges from erosion, flooding, and the impact of mass tourism.

### Architecture and Layout
Petra is renowned for its Hellenistic-style rock-cut architecture, reflecting Nabataean trade connections. The most famous structure is **Al-Khazneh (the Treasury)**, a 37-meter-tall mausoleum possibly for King Aretas IV. The **Monastery**, even larger, was dedicated to the deified king Obodas I. The city center includes a massive theatre (capacity ~8,500), a colonnaded street, and a garden complex with a pool. The **High Place of Sacrifice** atop Jebel Madbah was used for rituals like libation and animal sacrifice. The **Royal Tombs** showcase Nabataean adaptations of Hellenistic design.

### Geostrategic and Religious Importance
Petra's location controlled key caravan routes connecting Gaza, Damascus, the Red Sea, and the Persian Gulf. The Nabataeans worshipped Arab deities like Dushara and al-Uzza. Later, under Byzantine rule, Christianity took hold, with churches built and the Urn Tomb converted in 447 AD. Petra is also linked to Abrahamic traditions; it is identified with the burial place of Aaron (Jabal Haroun) and the site where Moses struck a rock for water (Wadi Musa).

## Terms
- **Nabataeans**: A nomadic Arab people who settled in Petra and built a wealthy kingdom based on trade.
- **Hellenistic architecture**: A style blending Greek and local elements, seen in Petra's tomb facades.
- **Al-Khazneh (The Treasury)**: Petra's most iconic rock-cut building, likely a royal mausoleum.
- **Siq**: The narrow, natural gorge that serves as the main entrance to Petra.
- **High Place of Sacrifice**: A ceremonial site on a mountain peak used for Nabataean rituals.
- **Arabia Petraea**: The name of the Roman province created after annexing the Nabataean Kingdom.
- **Bedul**: A Bedouin tribe historically living in Petra's caves, relocated in the 1980s.
- **UNESCO World Heritage Site**: A designation for sites of outstanding universal cultural or natural value.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact original name of Petra is debated; *Raqēmō* is supported by inscriptions, but biblical texts suggest alternatives like *Sela* or *Rekem*.
- The function of the large, monumental platform discovered in 2016 outside the city center remains unknown.
- The precise causes for Petra's final decline are debated, with factors including earthquakes, shifting trade routes, and political changes.
- The identity and purpose of the female figure on the Treasury's tholos (possibly Isis-Tyche) are not definitively confirmed.
