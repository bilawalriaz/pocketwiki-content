# July Revolution

## Overview
The July Revolution of 1830, also known as the French Revolution of 1830 or *les Trois Glorieuses* ("the Three Glorious Days"), was a second French Revolution that overthrew the Bourbon King Charles X. It established a new constitutional monarchy under his cousin Louis Philippe, Duke of Orléans, marking a shift from hereditary right to popular sovereignty and the final fall of the senior Bourbon line in favor of the Orléanist cadet branch.

## Timeline
- **16 September 1824** — Louis XVIII dies; his brother Charles X inherits the throne.
- **17 March 1830** — The Chamber of Deputies passes a motion of no confidence (the Address of the 221) against Charles X's ministry.
- **25 July 1830** — Charles X issues the July Ordinances, dissolving the newly elected Chamber, restricting press freedom, and altering electoral laws.
- **27 July 1830** — The first day of the revolution begins in Paris with protests and clashes between civilians and troops.
- **28 July 1830** — Fighting intensifies; Marshal Marmont, commanding royal forces, reports to the king that it is "no longer a riot, it is a revolution."
- **29 July 1830** — Revolutionaries capture key Parisian buildings, including the Tuileries Palace and the Hôtel de Ville.
- **2 August 1830** — Charles X and his son abdicate; they depart for Great Britain.
- **9 August 1830** — Louis Philippe is proclaimed King of the French, beginning the July Monarchy.

## Background
After Napoleon's defeat in 1814, the Congress of Vienna restored the Bourbon monarchy under Louis XVIII, who was forced to grant the Charter of 1814. His successor, the reactionary Charles X, quickly alienated the public and the liberal Chamber of Deputies. His policies included the Anti-Sacrilege Act (imposing the death penalty for profaning the Eucharist) and financial indemnities for properties confiscated during the Revolution, which critics saw as attacks on the Charter. In March 1830, the Chamber passed a motion of no confidence. Charles dissolved parliament and, after liberal victories in the subsequent elections, issued the July Ordinances on 25 July 1830, directly challenging the constitutional order.

## The Revolution
The publication of the July Ordinances on 26 July sparked immediate protest. Journalists signed a collective declaration of resistance, and business owners shuttered factories, creating unemployment that fueled public anger. On 27 July, crowds attacked soldiers in the streets of Paris, marking the first of the "Three Glorious Days." Fighting intensified on 28 July as royal forces, under the reluctant command of Marshal Marmont, struggled to contain the uprising. A committee of liberal opposition leaders petitioned Marmont to mediate, but King Charles X, advised by his minister Polignac, refused to withdraw the ordinances. By 29 July, revolutionaries had erected over 4,000 barricades and captured key buildings, including the Tuileries Palace and the Hôtel de Ville. The royal army, facing desertions and lacking provisions, was unable to suppress the revolt.

## Result
The revolution led to the abdication of Charles X on 2 August 1830. Although he intended his grandson to succeed him, the provisional government instead installed Louis Philippe as a constitutional monarch, creating the July Monarchy. This new regime was based on the principle of popular sovereignty rather than hereditary right. The event inspired uprisings elsewhere, including the Belgian Revolution, but also led to internal discontent, such as the June Rebellion of 1832 by disillusioned republicans. The July Monarchy lasted until the Revolution of 1848.

## Terms
- **July Ordinances**: The decrees issued by Charles X on 25 July 1830 that dissolved the Chamber of Deputies, restricted press freedom, and altered electoral laws, directly triggering the revolution.
- **July Monarchy**: The constitutional monarchy established after the 1830 revolution, with Louis Philippe as king, lasting until 1848.
- **Legitimists**: Supporters of the exiled senior Bourbon line after 1830.
- **Orléanists**: Supporters of Louis Philippe and the Orléanist branch of the Bourbon dynasty.
- **Address of the 221**: A motion of no confidence passed by the Chamber of Deputies against Charles X's ministry in March 1830.
- **Popular Sovereignty**: The principle that the authority of a state and its government are created and sustained by the consent of its people, through which the people exercise power.
- **Charter of 1814**: The constitution granted to Louis XVIII upon the Bourbon Restoration, establishing a constitutional monarchy.

## Debates and Open Questions
The article notes that the revolution's underlying motives were complex, leading to disillusionment among Parisian republicans who later revolted in 1832. The July Monarchy itself remained "doubtfully popular, disliked for different reasons by both Right and Left." The precise role of different social groups, such as the commercial middle class excluded by the ordinances versus the unemployed workers, in driving the uprising is a point of historical analysis.
