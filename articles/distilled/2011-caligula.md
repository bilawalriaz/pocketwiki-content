# Caligula

## Overview
Gaius Julius Caesar Augustus Germanicus, known as Caligula, was the third Roman emperor, ruling from AD 37 until his assassination in AD 41. His brief reign is famously depicted as a descent from a popular, promising start into tyranny, extravagance, and madness, though modern scholarship often reinterprets these events as political conflict and the exercise of imperial power. His death marked the end of the direct male line of the Julii Caesares.

## Timeline
- **31 August AD 12** — Born in Antium to Germanicus and Agrippina the Elder.
- **AD 19** — Father Germanicus dies; family returns to Rome.
- **AD 31** — Joins Emperor Tiberius on Capri after family's destruction.
- **16 March AD 37** — Tiberius dies; Caligula becomes emperor.
- **Mid-October AD 37** — Falls seriously ill; recovers by late October.
- **19 June AD 38** — His sister and heir, Drusilla, dies; she is later deified.
- **AD 39** — Relationship with Senate deteriorates; treason trials begin.
- **Late AD 39/Early AD 40** — Military campaign on the Rhine frontier; execution of Lepidus.
- **Spring AD 40** — Aborted invasion of Britannia.
- **AD 40** — Annexation of Mauretania; ordered statue in Jerusalem Temple.
- **24 January AD 41** — Assassinated by Praetorian officers; succeeded by Claudius.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Caligula was born into the imperial family, the son of the popular general Germanicus and Agrippina the Elder. After his father's death in AD 19, his mother's feud with Emperor Tiberius led to the destruction of his family, leaving Caligula as the sole male survivor. He was sent to live with Tiberius on Capri in AD 31, where he learned to dissimulate and survive under the emperor's suspicion. Upon Tiberius's death in AD 37, the 24-year-old Caligula was hailed emperor, receiving all powers at once—a first for the principate.

### Early Reign and Illness
Caligula's first seven months were widely praised as a "Golden Age." He granted generous gifts to the public and military, burned Tiberius's treason trial records, and honored his family. In late AD 37, he fell seriously ill. After his recovery, he began eliminating potential rivals, including his co-heir Gemellus and his former protector, the Praetorian prefect Macro. The death of his beloved sister Drusilla in AD 38, whom he deified, is often cited as a turning point in his behavior.

### Public Profile and Conflict with the Senate
Caligula cultivated popularity with the masses through lavish games and by sharing their enthusiasms, like chariot racing. He openly despised the Senate, humiliating its members and subjecting them to treason trials. He proposed making his horse, Incitatus, a consul—a likely insult to the senatorial class. His extravagant building projects, like a temporary bridge at Baiae, and new taxes funded by the Praetorian Guard, strained the treasury and public goodwill.

### Provincial Policy and Religion
Caligula's reign saw the annexation of Mauretania and a failed invasion of Britannia. His conflict with Jewish communities in Alexandria and Judaea escalated when he ordered a statue of himself in the Temple of Jerusalem, a plan abandoned only after his death. He took his role as pontifex maximus seriously but also permitted a cult of his living person, impersonating gods like Jupiter. While some eastern provinces hailed him as a "New Sun-god," his actions in Rome were seen as blasphemous or insane by contemporaries like Philo.

### Assassination and Aftermath
On 24 January AD 41, Caligula was assassinated by officers of the Praetorian Guard, including Cassius Chaerea, whom Caligula had routinely insulted. The Senate briefly considered restoring the Republic, but the Praetorian Guard instead found and proclaimed his uncle Claudius as emperor. Claudius executed the assassins but refused to damn Caligula's memory, though many of his inscriptions and statues were later altered.

## Terms
- **Principate:** The system of government established by Augustus, where the emperor ruled as *princeps* ("first among equals") while maintaining a facade of republican institutions.
- **Praetorian Guard:** The emperor's elite bodyguard unit, stationed in Rome, which played a key role in imperial politics and assassinations.
- **Maiestas:** The Roman legal charge of treason, often used politically to eliminate enemies of the emperor.
- **Genius cult:** A form of veneration for the guiding spirit or divine essence of a person, including living emperors, which was distinct from full godhood.
- **Damnatio memoriae:** A senatorial decree condemning an emperor's memory, involving the erasure of their name and images from public monuments.
- **Sesterces:** A Roman silver coin, a standard unit of account for prices, wages, and taxes.
- **Comitia:** The popular assembly of Roman citizens, whose electoral rights Caligula briefly restored.
- **Pontifex maximus:** The chief priest of the Roman state religion, a title held by the emperor.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Mental State:** Ancient sources portray Caligula as insane, but modern historians debate whether his actions were politically calculated, a result of illness, or simply the exercise of absolute power without restraint.
- **Incitatus:** Was Caligula's proposal to make his horse a consul a serious act of madness, a dark joke, or a deliberate insult to the Senate?
- **Bridge at Baiae:** Was the pointless bridge a display of absolute power, a military feint related to Britain, or pure extravagance?
- **Temple Statue:** Was Caligula's order to place his statue in the Jerusalem Temple a political act of imperial cult enforcement or evidence of personal megalomania?
- **Sources:** How reliable are the hostile accounts of Suetonius and Dio, written decades after his death, compared to the contemporary but biased account of Philo?
