# Uyghur Khaganate

## Overview
The Uyghur Khaganate was a Turkic tribal confederation that dominated the Mongolian steppe from 744 to 840 CE. It was a pivotal ally of the Tang dynasty, helping to suppress rebellions, and controlled key Silk Road trade. Its adoption of Manichaeism as a state religion and its eventual collapse due to internal strife and Kyrgyz invasion mark it as a significant, if short-lived, empire in Central Asian history.

## Timeline
- **744** — Uyghurs, with Karluk allies, defeat the Basmyls and establish the Khaganate, ending the Second Turkic Khaganate.
- **747** — Bayanchur Khan succeeds his father, initiating a golden age of expansion and trade with the Tang.
- **757** — Uyghur cavalry assists Tang forces in retaking Chang'an and Luoyang during the An Lushan Rebellion.
- **762** — Khagan Bögü converts to Manichaeism after encountering Sogdian priests, making it the state religion.
- **779** — Khagan Bögü is killed by his uncle, Tun Bagha Tarkhan, who suppresses Manichaeism and purges Sogdian influence.
- **795** — The Yaghlakar dynasty ends; a general, Qutluq II, founds the new Ädiz dynasty.
- **840** — The Yenisei Kyrgyz, invited by a rival Uyghur minister, sack the capital Ordu-Baliq, destroying the empire.
- **840s** — Uyghurs flee south, establishing successor kingdoms in Ganzhou and Qocho (near Turpan).

## Body

### Rise and Golden Age
The Uyghurs were originally a tribe within the Tiele confederation under the Turkic Khaganate. After the Western Turkic Khaganate fell to the Tang in 657, the Uyghurs allied with China. In 742, they rebelled against the Second Turkic Khaganate with the Karluks and Basmyls. After defeating the Basmyls in 744, the Uyghurs established their own empire under Khagan Kutlug I Bilge Khagan, with a capital at Ordu-Baliq. The empire expanded rapidly under Bayanchur Khan (r. 747–759), bringing many steppe peoples under its rule. The Uyghurs became the dominant power in Central Asia after the Tang's retreat following the 751 Battle of Talas.

### Alliance with the Tang and Manichaeism
The Uyghur Khaganate's most critical relationship was with the Tang dynasty. In 756, Emperor Suzong sought Uyghur military aid against the An Lushan Rebellion. In exchange for cavalry assistance in 757 and 762, the Tang paid vast quantities of silk and granted favorable trade terms, including a fixed price for horses. This alliance was cemented by exchange marriages. In 762, Khagan Bögü converted to Manichaeism, likely influenced by Sogdian merchants who controlled Silk Road trade. This made Manichaeism the official state religion, a move that may have also asserted independence from Tang cultural influence.

### Decline and Fall
Internal strife and external pressure led to the Khaganate's decline. In 779, Khagan Bögü was killed by his uncle, Tun Bagha Tarkhan, who opposed a planned invasion of the Tang and purged Sogdian influence. While later khagans restored Manichaeism, the empire weakened. In 839, a famine and epidemic devastated the livestock-based economy. A civil war between rival khagans allowed the Yenisei Kyrgyz to invade in 840. Invited by a defecting Uyghur minister, a Kyrgyz force of ~80,000 horsemen sacked and destroyed Ordu-Baliq. The Uyghur state collapsed, and its people fled south.

### Successors and Legacy
The Kyrgyz showed little interest in governing the former empire. The Uyghurs migrated south, establishing two successor states: the Ganzhou Uyghur Kingdom in modern Gansu and the Kingdom of Qocho near Turpan. The Qocho Uyghurs converted to Buddhism and later became vassals of the Qara Khitai and then the Mongol Empire, serving as important administrators. The Old Uyghur alphabet was adapted as the official script of the Mongol Empire. Another group of Uyghurs joined the Karluks, who later helped found the Kara-Khanid Khanate.

### Relationship with the Sogdians
The Uyghurs formed a crucial economic and political alliance with Sogdian merchants, who dominated Silk Road trade. The Uyghurs provided protection, while the Sogdians facilitated trade between the steppe and China. The adoption of Manichaeism was a key aspect of this relationship. While conversion was driven by the elite for political and economic reasons, it sometimes faced resistance from the common people who held shamanistic beliefs. This alliance benefited both groups but tied the Khaganate's fate to the volatile politics of the Silk Road.

## Terms
- **Khagan**: The supreme ruler of a Turkic or Mongol nomadic empire.
- **Toquz Oghuz**: Meaning "Nine Tribes," the name for the tribal confederation that formed the Uyghur Khaganate.
- **Manichaeism**: A syncretic religion founded by the prophet Mani, combining elements of Zoroastrianism, Christianity, and Buddhism. It became the Uyghur state religion in 762.
- **Sogdians**: An Iranian people from Central Asia who were the dominant merchants along the Silk Road during this period.
- **Ordu-Baliq**: The fortified capital city of the Uyghur Khaganate, built on the site of the former Göktürk capital.
- **Yaghlakar dynasty**: The founding ruling dynasty of the Uyghur Khaganate, which lasted until 795.
- **Ädiz dynasty**: The second ruling dynasty of the Uyghur Khaganate, established by the general Qutluq II in 795.
- **An Lushan Rebellion**: A devastating civil war (755–763) against the Tang dynasty, which the Uyghurs helped suppress.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact motivation for the Uyghur adoption of Manichaeism is debated. While the source links it to Sogdian influence and trade, it may also have been a deliberate political choice to differentiate themselves from the Buddhist Tang.
- The genetic studies of Uyghur remains show significant variation in ancestry (West Eurasian vs. East Eurasian), suggesting the Khaganate was genetically diverse and that admixture patterns were complex and not uniform across the population.
- The nature and extent of the Kyrgyz "control" over the former Uyghur territory after 840 is unclear. The source argues against the idea of a significant Kyrgyz empire, suggesting their presence on the Mongolian Plateau was brief.
