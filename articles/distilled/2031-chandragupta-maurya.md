# Chandragupta Maurya

## Overview
Chandragupta Maurya (reigned c. 320–298 BCE) was the founder of the Maurya Empire, the first pan-Indian imperial dynasty. His rise from humble origins to overthrowing the Nanda dynasty and defeating Greek governors established a centralized state that laid the groundwork for economic prosperity and religious synthesis in ancient India. His reign marks the beginning of a historically verifiable chronology for the Indian subcontinent.

## Timeline
- **c. 326–325 BCE** — Alexander the Great campaigns in India and leaves Greek governors in the northwest.
- **c. 323 BCE** — Alexander dies; unrest and local warfare erupt in the Indian subcontinent.
- **c. 322–319 BCE** — Chandragupta defeats the Nanda dynasty and seizes Pataliputra, founding the Maurya Empire.
- **c. 317–312 BCE** — Chandragupta expands into the Punjab, defeating Greek governors.
- **c. 305–303 BCE** — Chandragupta forms a dynastic marriage-alliance with Seleucus I Nicator, gaining eastern Indus territories.
- **c. 298 BCE** — Chandragupta possibly abdicates; he dies between 297–293 BCE.

## Body

### Rise to Power and Empire Building
Chandragupta's rise began in the power vacuum after Alexander the Great's death in 323 BCE. According to the Roman historian Justin, Chandragupta led a revolt against Greek governors in the northwest, then turned against the unpopular Nanda dynasty in Magadha. With his mentor Chanakya, he recruited an army and conquered the Nanda capital, Pataliputra, establishing the Maurya Empire. The chronology is debated: his conquest of Magadha is generally dated to c. 322–319 BCE, with his expansion into Punjab following at c. 317–312 BCE. An alternative chronology places these events later, between c. 311–305 BCE.

His empire expanded further through a dynastic marriage-alliance with the Seleucid emperor Seleucus I Nicator around 305–303 BCE. In exchange for 500 war elephants, Chandragupta gained control over territories in the eastern Indus Valley, possibly including parts of eastern Afghanistan. His rule also extended to Gujarat, as evidenced by later inscriptions.

### Administration and Infrastructure
Chandragupta established a decentralized administration with provinces, local governments, and a council of ministers (mantriparishad). His chief minister was Chanakya. The empire was organized into territories (janapadas) protected by forts (durga) and funded by a treasury (kosa). According to the Greek ambassador Megasthenes, administration was structured into three parallel bodies: one for villages (managing irrigation, land, and forests), one for cities (overseeing trade, markets, and foreigners), and one for the military.

The Maurya state invested heavily in infrastructure. Evidence from the Junagadh rock inscription shows Chandragupta commissioned irrigation reservoirs and conduits. He is credited with building a thousand-mile highway from Pataliputra to Taxila and expanding road networks across the subcontinent, which boosted trade and military mobility. The state maintained a monopoly on weapon manufacturing but encouraged private mining.

### Religion and Legacy
Chandragupta himself sponsored Vedic sacrifices and Brahmanical rituals. However, his reign and the subsequent Maurya Empire saw the rise of Buddhism, Jainism, and Ājīvika alongside Vedic traditions. This period initiated a synthesis of Brahmanic and non-Brahmanic traditions that later converged into Hinduism. His grandson Ashoka would later patronize Buddhism's expansion.

His historical legacy is reconstructed from fragmentary Greek, Roman, and Indian sources written centuries after his life. The identification of the Greek "Sandracottus" with the Sanskrit "Chandragupta" by William Jones in 1793 was crucial for synchronizing Indian and Greco-Roman history.

## Terms
*   **Maurya Empire:** The first pan-Indian imperial dynasty, founded by Chandragupta Maurya and later ruled by his son Bindusara and grandson Ashoka.
*   **Nanda Dynasty:** The ruling dynasty of Magadha that Chandragupta overthrew to establish the Maurya Empire.
*   **Chanakya (Kautilya):** Chandragupta's chief minister and mentor, traditionally credited as the author of the *Arthashastra*.
*   **Pataliputra:** The capital city of the Maurya Empire, located in present-day Bihar.
*   **Seleucus I Nicator:** A Macedonian general who founded the Seleucid Empire; he formed a marriage-alliance with Chandragupta.
*   **Megasthenes:** A Greek ambassador sent by Seleucus to Chandragupta's court; his writings are a key primary source.
*   **Mantriparishad:** The council of ministers that advised the Maurya king.
*   **Sallekhana:** A Jain ritual of peacefully fasting to death, which later Jain legends claim Chandragupta performed.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Chronology:** The exact dates of Chandragupta's rise and reign are debated. The conventional date for his conquest of Magadha is c. 322–319 BCE, but an alternative chronology places his accession between c. 311–305 BCE.
*   **Origins and Early Life:** There are no contemporary historical facts about his origins; accounts vary from humble birth to a connection with the Nanda dynasty. His epithet "Vrishala" is interpreted differently as either "son of a shudra" or "best of kings."
*   **Religion in Later Life:** Later Jain legends (from after 600 CE) claim Chandragupta converted to Jainism, retired to Karnataka, and performed *sallekhana*. However, contemporary Greek evidence describes him performing Brahminical rituals and hunting, contradicting the Jain practice of *ahimsa* (nonviolence). Scholars debate whether these legends confuse him with his later descendant, Samprati Chandragupta.
*   **Extent of Empire:** The exact boundaries of his empire, particularly in the Indus Valley and southern India, are uncertain. Inscriptions from his grandson Ashoka's reign suggest control extended to Karnataka, but whether this was achieved by Chandragupta or his son Bindusara is unclear.
