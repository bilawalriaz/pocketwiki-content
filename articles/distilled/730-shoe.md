# Shoe

## Overview
A shoe is a paired item of footwear designed to protect and comfort the human foot, traditionally shaped for left and right feet. Over time, shoes evolved from simple functional items into complex artifacts of fashion, status, and cultural identity. The global shoe industry is a $200 billion annual market, yet faces sustainability challenges, with 90% of shoes ending in landfills due to material complexity.

## Body

### Early Evidence and Origins
The earliest known shoes are sagebrush bark sandals from Fort Rock Cave, Oregon, dating to approximately 10,500–9200 BP. The oldest leather shoe, found in Armenia’s Areni-1 cave, dates to 3500 BC and was crafted from a single piece of cowhide. Ötzi the Iceman (3300 BC) wore shoes with brown bearskin bases and deerskin side panels. Footprints in South Africa, dated between 73,000 and 136,000 BP, suggest earlier footwear use, supported by bone awls from the same period. Archaeological analysis of toe bones shows reduced thickness around 40,000–26,000 years ago, indicating widespread shoe use.

### Regional Developments
In the Americas, Native Americans crafted moccasins from leather or bison hides, often decorated with beads. In Africa and the Middle East, thong sandals (precursors to flip-flops) appeared in Egyptian murals by 4000 BC. The "Cleopatra," a soleless sandal, reflected ornamental rather than practical use. In Asia and Europe, Greeks largely avoided footwear, viewing it as unnecessary, while Romans adopted shoes as symbols of civilization. Roman soldiers wore chiral boots with riveted insoles, and rank was indicated by design complexity. In China and Japan, rice straw was commonly used for footwear.

### Medieval and Early Modern Period
During the Middle Ages, the espadrille emerged in Spain, featuring braided jute soles. Foot binding in China began among nobility during the Shang dynasty and peaked by c. AD 960, banned by Mongols in 1279 and Manchus in 1644, but persisted until Mao’s 1949 prohibition. Medieval shoes used the turnshoe method, later replaced by the welted rand technique around 1500. Pattens and chopines became status symbols in 15th-century Europe, evolving into high heels worn by both genders. By 1580, men wore heels, and in 17th-century France, only aristocrats were permitted to wear red heels.

### Industrial Era and Mechanization
Shoemaking commercialized in the mid-18th century as a cottage industry. Mechanization began during the Napoleonic Wars with Marc Brunel’s mass-production machinery in 1812, though it failed post-war. The sewing machine (1846) and Lyman Blake’s stitching machine (1856) revolutionized production. By the 1890s, mechanization was largely complete. The 20th century saw the rise of athletic shoes, with Nike’s founding in 1964 introducing innovations like waffle soles and midsole cushioning. The global market reached $107.4 billion in 2007, with China dominating production.

### Modern Industry and Globalization
The AGO system (1910) introduced stitchless, glued shoes. Synthetic materials like rubber and PVC reduced biodegradability, with most shoes taking 1000 years to decompose. Globalization shifted US shoe imports from 4% (1960) to 89% (1995). The Philippines’ Marikina region declined after WTO entry in 1995 due to Chinese competition. Today, China accounts for 63% of global production and 55% of industry revenue.

### Construction and Anatomy
Modern shoes consist of a sole (outsole, midsole, insole), upper, heel, and vamp. Soles are typically made from rubber, polyurethane, or PVC. Insoles attach to the upper’s lasting margin, while midsoles provide shock absorption. Heels vary in height for fashion or function, with features like the "gentleman’s corner" to prevent trouser entanglement. Uppers use laces, zippers, or Velcro, with aglets protecting lace ends. Specialized designs include cleats for traction and stiff soles for cycling.

### Types and Cultural Significance
Shoes serve diverse purposes: athletic (running, basketball), boots (cowboy, hiking), dress (Oxfords, high heels), and casual (sneakers, sandals). Cultural symbolism is profound—shoe removal signifies reverence in Semitic traditions, while showing soles is an insult in Arab culture. Empty shoes symbolize death in Greek tradition. Fairy tales like Cinderella and films like *The Wizard of Oz* feature iconic footwear. Sneaker culture emerged in the 1980s, with "sneakerheads" collecting limited editions.

## Terms
- **Chiral**: Shoes designed specifically for left or right foot.
- **Turnshoe**: Medieval construction method where the upper is turned inside-out during assembly.
- **Welted rand**: Technique where uppers are sewn to a stiff sole without turning.
- **Strights**: Shoes made without left/right distinction.
- **Midsole**: Layer between insole and outsole for shock absorption.
- **Vamp**: Front part of the shoe covering the foot’s upper surface.
- **Mondopoint**: Sizing system measuring both foot length and width.
- **Sneaker**: Rubber-soled shoe named for its quiet tread.

## Debates and Open Questions
The exact timeline of shoe adoption remains debated, as perishable materials limit archaeological evidence. Some scholars argue for earlier use based on footprint analysis, while others caution against over-interpreting ambiguous traces. The cultural shift from barefoot to shod societies in ancient Greece and Rome is also contested, with some historians emphasizing symbolic over practical motivations. The sustainability of the modern shoe industry, particularly recycling efforts, remains unresolved, with 90% of shoes still ending in landfills despite innovations like the Nike Considered line.
