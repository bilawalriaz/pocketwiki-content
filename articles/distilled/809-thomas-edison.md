# Thomas Edison

## Overview
Thomas Alva Edison (February 11, 1847 – October 18, 1931) was an American inventor and businessman whose work on the incandescent light bulb, phonograph, electric power distribution, and early motion pictures fundamentally shaped modern life. He registered 1,093 U.S. patents and pioneered the concept of the industrial research laboratory, transforming invention from individual tinkering into organized, team-based innovation. His influence extended beyond technology into business strategy, public relations, and the very structure of how companies develop new products.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Thomas Edison was born on February 11, 1847 in Milan, Ohio, but grew up in Port Huron, Michigan after his family moved there in 1854. His mother, a former teacher, provided his only formal education. Developing hearing problems at age 12—likely from scarlet fever and untreated ear infections—Edison became completely deaf in one ear and barely hearing in the other. He later claimed this isolation helped him concentrate. Working as a news butcher on trains, he earned $50 weekly by age 13, investing profits in electrical and chemical experiments. He founded the *Grand Trunk Herald* newspaper, which ran 24 issues with 500 subscribers.

### Telegraphy and Early Career
From 1863 to 1869, Edison worked night shift telegraphy jobs across multiple states and Canada. His tinkering often interfered with work, including a near-train collision that led to resignation. In Boston, he invented a stock ticker but lost money expanding to New York. His first patent (U.S. 90,646 for an electric vote recorder) was granted June 1, 1869. Moving to New York City that year, he partnered with Franklin Leonard Pope and attracted wealthy investors. By 1874, selling his quadruplex telegraph to Western Union for $30,000 enabled him to expand operations and invest in his brother's street railway.

### Menlo Park Laboratory
Built in 1876 with quadruplex telegraph proceeds, Menlo Park became the first industrial research laboratory. Edison averaged one patent every 10–12 days throughout his career, though many inventions were collaborative efforts by his staff. He drove employees to "the limits of human exhaustion," working 18-hour days himself. The lab expanded to two city blocks, stocked with "almost every conceivable material." Edison cultivated publicity carefully, controlling access while tailoring his public image through interviews.

### Carbon Microphone and Telephone
In 1876, Edison began improving telephone microphones, developing a carbon microphone using granules between metal plates that changed resistance with sound pressure. Testing 150 materials, he found parchment and tinfooil optimal for diaphragms. David Edward Hughes independently published similar work in 1878, causing controversy since Hughes acknowledged advice from Edison's colleagues.

### Phonograph Development and Commercialization
Edison's phonograph (1877) emerged from his desire to record telegraph messages for error-free transcription. Using tinfoil cylinders, the device amazed the public. Demonstrated before the National Academy of Sciences in April 1878, Edison obtained a patent but delayed commercial development until Bell's competitors introduced wax-coated cylinders. The Edison Phonograph Company (1887) faced financial troubles and litigation, with Edison ultimately gaining control after five years. Technical challenges included making recording materials durable enough for repeated use. Edison's 1900 model sold for $10, with 3,000 musical records produced by 1,000 employees. Radio adoption severely impacted sales by 1921, with 90% fewer records sold compared to 1920.

### Electric Light System
Beginning in 1878, Edison developed a complete electrical illumination system to compete with gas lighting. Testing various filaments including cardboard, grasses, hemp, and palmetto, he settled on carbonized bamboo, which lasted over 1,200 hours. His January 27, 1880 patent (U.S. 223,898) covered carbon filament strips coiled and connected to platina contact wires. The high-resistance filament led to the 110V power standard still used in the U.S. Edison's "Village of Light" demonstration at Menlo Park attracted visitors by February 1880.

### Electric Power Distribution
Edison founded the Edison Illuminating Company in December 1880, patenting a complete electricity distribution system. The massive copper wire requirements led to his three-prong wire system innovation. Before commercializing, he invented a zinc-solution cell meter to measure customer power consumption. The 93 kW Holborn Viaduct station in London (January 1882) preceded Pearl Street Station in Manhattan (September 4, 1882), which supplied 946 customers with 110V DC. Subscriptions quickly grew to 508 customers with 10,164 lamps.

### Competition and the War of Currents
As Edison expanded DC systems, Westinghouse promoted AC using European-developed transformers (1885–1886), enabling long-distance transmission over thinner wires. Edison's DC plants served only one-mile radius, limiting customer reach. Publicly declaring AC "unworkable," Edison partnered with Harold P. Brown in a propaganda campaign electrocuting animals with AC and supporting electric chair development to smear AC. Despite his own AC experiments, Edison was outcompeted. The 1892 merger of Edison General Electric with Thomson-Houston created General Electric under J.P. Morgan's control, marginalizing Edison from his own industry.

### Mining Ventures
Starting in the late 1870s, Edison pursued low-grade iron ore and beach sand mining through the Edison Ore Milling Company (1880). Promising hundreds of tons monthly, the operation failed after three years with only one customer receiving ore. His Ogdensburg, New Jersey facility featured automated systems including electrically-powered 70-ton rollers and 480 electromagnets for magnetic separation. Though unprofitable, the venture led to licensing technology to successful producers.

### Cement Industry
Using mining waste sand, Edison established the Edison Portland Cement Company (1899). He designed 150-foot horizontal kilns that baked cement longer at lower temperatures, reducing coal consumption. His 1901 housing initiative cast entire three-story houses in single molds, but insufficient demand ended the project. The cement business remained unprofitable until 1922 despite becoming a significant domestic industry entity by 1907.

### West Orange Laboratory
By 1887, Edison outgrew Menlo Park, building a larger West Orange facility. A 1914 fire destroyed 13 buildings ($1.5 million damage) but Edison rebuilt with newest technology. During the 1921 recession, he fired thousands of employees, nearly facing bankruptcy before economic recovery.

### Fluoroscopy and Battery Research
Edison abandoned X-ray fluoroscopy after nearly losing his eyesight and seriously injuring assistants Clarence Dally and Charles Dally. Beginning in 1899, Edison developed nickel-iron alkaline batteries for electric cars, testing 10,000 electrode combinations. Despite Henry Ford's $1.1 million investment, the battery never achieved commercial success as electric cars disappeared.

### Motion Pictures
Edison and Dickson developed the Kinetograph camera (patented 1897) and Kinetoscope viewer (1891), producing nearly 1,200 films (1894–1911). The 1903 Motion Picture Patents Company (Edison Trust) consolidated nine major studios. Edison fired Edwin S. Porter in 1914, ending his film business decline. He disliked talkies, stating they "spoiled everything" by concentrating on voice over acting.

### National Security Work
Heading the Naval Consulting Board (1915), Edison proposed submarine detection methods that failed adoption. His nickel-iron batteries caused a hydrogen explosion on USS E-2 (January 1916) killing five men; Edison blamed operator error despite naval criticism.

### Rubber Research
Concerned about U.S. rubber dependency (75% of world consumption in 1915), Edison tested 17,000 plant samples, finding Goldenrod suitable for 12% latex yield. He collaborated with Ford and Firestone on botanical research.

### Chemical Production
During WWI chemical shortages, Edison produced phenol at Silver Lake and benzene at Johnstown and Bessemer facilities. Aniline dye production ended German monopoly, making these ventures profitable.

## Terms
- **Industrial research laboratory**: Organized facility for systematic technological development, pioneered by Edison at Menlo Park
- **Carbon microphone**: Transmitter using carbon granules whose resistance varies with sound pressure
- **Direct current (DC)**: Electrical flow in one direction, Edison's power distribution standard
- **Alternating current (AC)**: Electrical flow that periodically reverses direction, Westinghouse's competing system
- **War of Currents**: Late 1880s–1890s competition between Edison's DC and Westinghouse's AC systems
- **Kinetograph**: Edison's motion picture camera, patented 1897
- **Kinetoscope**: Individual-viewing motion picture device introduced 1891
- **Edison effect**: Thermionic emission phenomenon used in his 1883 voltage-regulating patent, foundational to vacuum tubes
- **Tasimeter**: Highly sensitive infrared radiation measuring device invented for 1878 solar eclipse observation
- **Edison Trust**: Motion Picture Patents Company (1908) consolidating nine major film studios

## Debates and Open Questions
Several aspects of Edison's legacy remain debated among historians. The extent of his personal contribution versus his employees' work is contested; while he held 1,093 patents, many inventions were collaborative efforts, particularly those of William Kennedy Laurie Dickson (motion pictures) and John Ott (light bulb improvements). Edison's role in the "War of Currents" raises questions about whether his anti-AC campaign was genuinely safety-motivated or primarily competitive. His deafness's impact on his work—whether it enhanced concentration or merely coexisted with his achievements—remains speculative. The circumstances of Mary Stilwell Edison's death (age 29) are officially listed as unknown causes. Edison's relationship with Nikola Tesla, who worked briefly for him before their famous falling-out, continues to generate scholarly discussion about innovation ethics and business practices.
