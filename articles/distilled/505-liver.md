# Liver

## Overview
The liver is a major metabolic organ found exclusively in vertebrates, performing an estimated 500 essential functions including detoxification, protein synthesis, nutrient storage, and bile production for digestion. Weighing approximately 1.5 kg in adults, it is the heaviest internal organ and largest gland, located in the right upper abdomen beneath the diaphragm. It receives a dual blood supply—oxygenated blood via the hepatic artery and nutrient-rich blood via the portal vein—and is structurally organized into functional units called hepatic lobules. The liver possesses a unique capacity for compensatory regeneration, yet irreversible failure requires transplantation, first performed in 1963.

## Timeline
- **3rd–8th week embryonic** — Organogenesis occurs; hepatic diverticulum forms from foregut endoderm and septum transversum mesenchyme.
- **Birth** — Liver comprises ~4% of body weight (~120 g); umbilical vein and ductus venosus obliterate within days.
- **1963** — Thomas Starzl performs first human liver transplant in the United States.
- **1967** — Roy Calne performs first human liver transplant in Cambridge, England.
- **1989** — First living donor liver transplantation performed for pediatric recipient.
- **2018** — Liver transplantation remains the only option for complete liver failure.

## Body

### Structure and Gross Anatomy
The liver is a dark reddish-brown, wedge-shaped organ with two lobes of unequal size. It is connected to the hepatic artery (oxygen-rich blood) and portal vein (nutrient-rich blood from GI tract, spleen, pancreas), which subdivide into liver sinusoids leading to hepatic lobules—the functional units. Lobules consist of hepatocyte plates radiating from a central vein toward portal triads (hepatic artery, portal vein, bile duct) at the corners. The organ is covered by Glisson's capsule (fibroelastic connective tissue) and, except for a bare area, a peritoneal serous coat. The Couinaud classification divides the liver into eight functionally independent segments (I–VIII) based on vascular supply, each with its own inflow, outflow, and biliary drainage; segment I (caudate lobe) receives supply from both portal vein branches.

### Development
Organogenesis spans the third to eighth embryonic weeks. The liver originates from the ventral foregut endoderm (hepatic diverticulum) induced by adjacent septum transversum mesenchyme, with contributions from cardiac fibroblast growth factor and retinoic acid from lateral plate mesoderm. Hepatoblasts migrate, differentiate into biliary epithelial cells (near portal veins) and hepatocytes (away from veins), and establish sinusoids and bile canaliculi. The left umbilical vein becomes the ductus venosus; the right vitelline vein becomes the portal vein. In the fetus, the umbilical vein supplies nutrient-rich blood, bypassing the liver via the ductus venosus; the fetal liver produces blood stem cells migrating to the thymus. At birth, the liver weighs ~120 g (4% body weight), growing to 1.4–1.6 kg (2.5–3.5% body weight) in adulthood.

### Functions

#### Metabolism
The liver regulates carbohydrate, protein, and lipid metabolism. It stores ~100 g glycogen (glycogenesis), releases glucose via glycogenolysis, and performs gluconeogenesis (glucose from amino acids, lactate, glycerol). It synthesizes all plasma proteins except gamma-globulins, including coagulation factors (I, II, V, VII–XIII), albumin (maintains oncotic pressure), and angiotensinogen (blood pressure regulation). In lipid metabolism, it synthesizes cholesterol, triglycerides, and lipoproteins, and produces bile for fat emulsification and vitamin K absorption. It also produces insulin-like growth factor 1 (childhood growth, adult anabolism).

#### Detoxification and Breakdown
The liver breaks down insulin, hormones, and bilirubin (via glucuronidation for bile excretion). It converts toxic ammonia to urea (urea cycle) for urinary excretion. It metabolizes drugs and toxins (Phase I/II reactions), sometimes causing toxication (metabolite more toxic than precursor). Kupffer cells (phagocytic macrophages lining sinusoids) remove dead red blood cells and bacteria.

#### Storage, Blood Reservoir, and Lymph
The liver stores vitamins A (1–2 years), D (1–4 months), B12 (3–5 years), K, E, iron, copper, and trace metals. As an expandable venous organ, it holds ~450 mL blood normally (10% total volume) and can expand to store 0.5–1 L extra during cardiac failure/peripheral congestion. Due to highly permeable sinusoids, the liver produces ~50% of the body's resting lymph (protein concentration ~6 g/dL).

#### Immunological and Hematological Roles
The mononuclear phagocyte system acts as a sieve for portal-borne antigens. In the first trimester, the liver is the primary site of erythropoiesis; bone marrow takes over by week 32. Hepatic stellate cells (in perisinusoidal space) store vitamin A and participate in fibrosis response.

### Clinical Significance

#### Disease
Common diseases include nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (~1/3 global prevalence), viral hepatitis (A–E; chronic B/C infect ~248M and ~142M globally, main causes of liver cancer), alcoholic liver disease (hepatitis, fatty liver, cirrhosis), drug-induced injury (e.g., paracetamol), autoimmune conditions (primary biliary cholangitis), and pediatric disorders (biliary atresia, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency). Cirrhosis (fibrosis from any cause) increases vascular resistance, causing portal hypertension and portosystemic anastomoses. Hepatic encephalopathy results from toxin accumulation; Budd–Chiari syndrome from hepatic vein occlusion. Jaundice (bilirubin deposition) signals impaired excretion. Caffeine/coffee consumption correlates with reduced cirrhosis progression, fibrosis, and cancer risk.

#### Diagnosis and Regeneration
Diagnosis relies on liver function tests (blood), serology, imaging (ultrasound/CT), and biopsy (percutaneous, ultrasound-guided). The liver uniquely regenerates lost mass: 25% remnant can restore full function via hepatocyte proliferation (G0→G1→mitosis, p75 receptor-mediated), though this is compensatory growth (functional restoration, not original form). Bipotential hepatic oval cells (in canals of Hering) may differentiate into hepatocytes or cholangiocytes (bile duct epithelium).

#### Transplantation
Indicated for irreversible failure (chronic cirrhosis from hepatitis C, alcohol, autoimmune hepatitis; rarely fulminant failure). Allografts typically from deceased donors (brain injury); living donor transplants use segments 2/3 (20% adult liver) for children or right lobe (60%) for adults. Both donor/recipient livers regenerate. Donor mortality risk is higher for right-lobe hepatectomy; preoperative MDCT (vascular anatomy/volumetry) and MRI (biliary tree) screen anatomy feasibility.

### Society, Culture, and Comparative Biology
Culturally, the liver symbolizes the soul (Greek Prometheus myth), courage (Zulu *isibindi*, English "lily-livered"), anger (Talmud), or desire (Persian *jigar*). Culinary uses include mammalian/fowl/fish livers (rich in iron, vitamins A/B12); Romance language terms (French *foie*, Spanish *hígado*) derive from *ficatum* (fig-fattened goose liver). The Humr tribe (Sudan) consumes giraffe liver/bone marrow drink (*umm nyolokh*), reported as psychoactive (possibly DMT). Tungusic peoples historically used rotting liver as arrow/bullet poison. In vertebrates, the liver is the largest internal organ, typically bilobed (except snakes: cigar-shaped). Neonatal marsupials retain hepatic hematopoiesis post-birth. The amphioxus hepatic caecum is a homolog producing liver-specific proteins (vitellogenin, antithrombin) but is not a true liver.

## Terms
- ****Hepatocyte**** — The primary functional cell of the liver (parenchymal cell), constituting 70–85% of liver volume; performs metabolic, synthetic, and detoxification functions.
- ****Hepatic lobule**** — The microscopic functional unit of the liver, roughly hexagonal, with hepatocyte plates radiating from a central vein to peripheral portal triads.
- ****Portal triad**** — A structural component at lobule corners containing a branch of the hepatic artery, portal vein, and common bile duct; visible on ultrasound as "Mickey Mouse sign."
- ****Glisson's capsule**** — A dense fibroelastic connective tissue layer covering the liver, extending into the parenchyma alongside vessels and ducts; named for Francis Glisson.
- ****Couinaud classification**** — A system dividing the liver into eight functionally independent segments (I–VIII) based on vascular inflow, outflow, and biliary drainage.
- ****Kupffer cell**** — Specialized phagocytic macrophages lining the liver sinusoids; remove bacteria, dead red blood cells, and debris from portal blood.
- ****Cholangiocyte**** — Epithelial lining cell of the bile ducts; cuboidal in small ducts, columnar and mucus-secreting in larger ducts near the porta hepatis.
- ****Ductus venosus**** — Fetal shunt carrying oxygenated blood from the left portal vein to the left hepatic vein/inferior vena cava, bypassing the liver; becomes ligamentum venosum after birth.
- ****Hepatic encephalopathy**** — Neuropsychiatric syndrome caused by accumulation of toxins (e.g., ammonia) normally cleared by the liver; can progress to coma/death.
- ****Living donor liver transplantation**** — Surgical removal of a liver segment (e.g., Couinaud segments 2/3 or right lobe) from a living donor for transplant; both livers regenerate.

## Debates and open questions
- **Psychoactive giraffe liver**: The Humr tribe's *umm nyolokh* drink is reported to cause hallucinations; anthropologists debate whether this is pharmacological (e.g., dimethyltryptamine/DMT) or psychosomatic/conventional. No detailed chemical analysis has confirmed or refuted an intoxicating substance.
- **Mechanism of liver regeneration**: Whether regeneration is driven solely by mature hepatocyte proliferation or significantly involves bipotential hepatic oval cells (stem/progenitor cells in canals of Hering) remains under investigation.
- **Artificial liver development**: As of 2018, no device replicates all liver functions; liver dialysis supports short-term failure only. Research on stem cell-generated artificial livers continues.
- **Donor mortality in right-lobe living donation**: Early series reported at least 14 donor deaths; the risk-benefit ratio for adult-to-adult right-lobe transplantation remains controversial compared to left-lobe donation.
