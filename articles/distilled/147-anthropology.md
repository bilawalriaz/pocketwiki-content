# Anthropology

## Overview

Anthropology is the scientific study of humanity that crosses biology and sociology, concerned with human behavior, human biology, cultures, societies, and linguistics, in both the present and past, including archaic humans. Social anthropology studies patterns of behaviour, while cultural anthropology studies cultural meaning, including norms and values; the term sociocultural anthropology is commonly used today. Linguistic anthropology studies how language influences social life, and biological (or physical) anthropology studies the biology and evolution of humans and their close primate relatives. Archaeology, often referred to as the "anthropology of the past," explores human activity by examining physical remains; in North America and Asia it is generally regarded as a branch of anthropology, while in Europe it is considered either an independent discipline or a subfield of related disciplines such as history and palaeontology.

## Timeline

- **1647** — The Bartholins at the University of Copenhagen define l'anthropologie as the science treating of man, divided into Anatomy and Psychology.
- **1839** — Étienne Serres uses the term to describe the natural history, or paleontology, of man; the Société Ethnologique de Paris is founded.
- **1842–1843** — The Ethnological Society of New York and the Ethnological Society of London are founded on the Paris model.
- **1850** — A chair in anthropology and ethnography is created at the French National Museum of Natural History.
- **1859** — The Société d'Anthropologie de Paris meets for the first time; Darwin and Wallace unveil the theory of evolution.
- **1859–1864** — Theodor Waitz publishes Die Anthropologie der Naturvölker, defining anthropology as "the science of the nature of man."
- **1863** — The Anthropological Society of London breaks away from the Ethnological Society, following the "new anthropology."
- **1865–1902** — Similar organizations follow: Madrid (1865), Vienna (1870), Italy (1871), and the American Anthropological Association (1902).
- **1898** — 48 educational institutions in 13 countries offer some anthropology courses; none of the 75 faculty are under a department named anthropology.
- **1989** — The European Association of Social Anthropologists (EASA) is established.

## Body

### Etymology and origin of the term

The abstract noun anthropology is first attested in reference to history. Its present use first appeared in Renaissance Germany in the works of Magnus Hundt and Otto Casmann, whose Neo-Latin anthropologia derived from the combining forms of the Greek words ánthrōpos ("human") and lógos ("study"). Its adjectival form appeared in the works of Aristotle, and it began to be used in English, possibly via French Anthropologie, by the early 18th century.

### 19th-century development

Anthropology and many other current fields are the intellectual results of the comparative methods developed in the early 19th century. Theorists in anatomy, linguistics, and ethnology began making feature-by-feature comparisons, suspecting that similarities between animals, languages, and folkways resulted from unknown processes or laws. For them, the publication of Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species was the epiphany of everything they had begun to suspect. Paul Broca, who discovered the speech center of the human brain (Broca's area), became an immediate convert to Transformisme (evolutionism) and defined anthropology as "the study of the human group, considered as a whole, in its details, and in relation to the rest of nature." Theodor Waitz defined anthropology as "the science of the nature of man," stressing that data of comparison must be empirical and that "the same laws of thought are applicable to all men." In 1863, Richard Francis Burton and James Hunt formed the Anthropological Society of London, adopting Waitz's definitions; among the first associates was Edward Burnett Tylor, inventor of cultural anthropology. Most of the new societies were evolutionist, with one notable exception: the Berlin Society for Anthropology, Ethnology, and Prehistory (1869), founded by Rudolph Virchow, who insisted Darwin's conclusions lacked empirical foundation. Some consider anthropology to have become a tool for colonisers, who study their subjects to gain a better understanding and control.

### 20th and 21st centuries

Since the work of Franz Boas and Bronisław Malinowski in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, social anthropology in Great Britain and cultural anthropology in the US have been distinguished by their emphasis on cross-cultural comparisons, long-term in-depth examination of context, and participant-observation (experiential immersion in the research area). Cultural anthropology has emphasized cultural relativism, holism, and the use of findings to frame cultural critiques. In the US, anthropology has traditionally been divided into the four-field approach developed by Boas: biological or physical anthropology; social, cultural, or sociocultural anthropology; archaeological anthropology; and linguistic anthropology. European countries with overseas colonies tended to practice more ethnology (a term coined by Adam F. Kollár in 1783). During the 1970s and 1990s, sociocultural anthropology underwent an epistemological shift away from positivist traditions, while archaeology and biological anthropology remained largely positivist.

### Fields

Sociocultural anthropology draws together cultural anthropology (the comparative study of how people make sense of the world) and social anthropology (the study of relationships among individuals and groups). Inquiry is guided in part by cultural relativism, the attempt to understand other societies in terms of their own cultural symbols and values. Ethnography is based upon long-term fieldwork, with participant observation as a foundational method; ethnology involves the systematic comparison of different cultures. The study of kinship and social organization is a central focus, as kinship is a human universal. The Standard Cross-Cultural Sample (SCCS) includes 186 cultures.

Biological anthropology examines the biological and social factors affecting the evolution of humans and other primates; subfields include forensic anthropology, paleoanthropology, dental anthropology, primatology, and human biology. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century racism played a significant role in motivating certain avenues of research, including the early development of bioarchaeology; a major driver was the effort to "establish the intellectual superiority of the white race," and remains of Indigenous, enslaved, or marginalized people were often treated as scientific specimens.

Archaeology studies the human past through material remains, including artifacts, faunal remains, and human-altered landscapes. Ethnoarchaeology studies living human groups to better understand evidence left by past groups. Linguistic anthropology seeks to understand human communications, verbal and non-verbal, variation in language across time and space, and the relationship between language and culture. Ethnography is a method of analysing social or cultural interaction; Tim Ingold distinguishes it from anthropology, arguing anthropology seeks general theories while ethnography concerns itself with fidelity.

### Key topics in sociocultural anthropology

The anthropology of art concerns the universality of 'art' as a cultural phenomenon; Western categories like 'painting' or 'sculpture' do not exist in most non-Western contexts, so anthropologists focus on formal features with evident 'aesthetic' qualities. Media anthropology emphasizes ethnographic studies of producers, audiences, and cultural aspects of mass media. Ethnomusicology, formally termed by Dutch scholar Jaap Kunst c. 1950, studies music in its cultural, social, and other contexts. Visual anthropology studies and produces ethnographic photography, film, and new media.

Economic anthropology seeks to explain human economic behavior across the widest historical, geographic, and cultural scope; its origins begin with Bronisław Malinowski and Marcel Mauss on gift-giving exchange as an alternative to market exchange. Political economy applies historical materialism to anthropology, introducing questions of history and colonialism. Applied anthropology refers to the application of method and theory to solve practical problems. The anthropology of development views development from a critical perspective, asking why poverty is increasing despite development goals.

Kinship refers to patterns of social relationships, including descent and marriage; people have biological families (consanguinity, or "blood ties") and can also have chosen families. Feminist anthropology is a four-field approach seeking to reduce male bias in research findings and scholarly production; it is inclusive of the anthropology of birth. Medical anthropology studies "human health and disease, health care systems, and biocultural adaptation," focusing on six basic fields including medical knowledge systems, the patient-physician relationship, and the impact of biomedicine in non-Western settings. Nutritional anthropology examines the interplay among economic systems, nutritional status, and food security. Psychological anthropology studies the interaction of cultural and mental processes. Cognitive anthropology uses cognitive science methods to explain patterns of shared knowledge. Transpersonal anthropology studies the relationship between altered states of consciousness and culture.

Political anthropology concerns political systems in light of social structures; it developed primarily with politics in stateless societies and, from the 1960s, turned toward more complex settings with states and markets. Legal anthropology specializes in "the cross-cultural study of social ordering." Public anthropology, created by Robert Borofsky, aims to address problems beyond the discipline and foster social change. Cyborg anthropology, originating in 1993, studies humankind's relations with technological systems; Donna Haraway's 1985 Cyborg Manifesto is considered its founding document. Digital anthropology studies the relationship between humans and digital-era technology. Ecological anthropology studies "cultural adaptations to environments." Environmental anthropology offers interdisciplinary approaches to environmental issues, with anthropology's distinguishing feature being its interest in non-industrial indigenous and traditional societies. Ethnohistory studies ethnographic cultures and indigenous customs through historical records. The anthropology of religion assumes complete continuity between magical thinking and religion, and that every religion is a cultural product. Urban anthropology is concerned with urbanization, poverty, and neoliberalism.

### Key topics in archaeological and biological anthropology

Anthrozoology (or "human–animal studies") studies the interaction between living things. Biocultural anthropology explores relationships between human biology and culture; after World War II, emphasis shifted from a racial perspective toward exploring the role culture plays in shaping human biology. Evolutionary anthropology studies the evolution of human physiology and behaviour, combining natural and social science. Forensic anthropology applies physical anthropology and human osteology in legal settings, most often in criminal cases with decomposed remains. Paleoanthropology combines paleontology and physical anthropology to study ancient humans through fossil hominid remains.

### Organizations and ethics

The largest organization of anthropologists is the American Anthropological Association (AAA), founded in 1903. The European Association of Social Anthropologists (EASA), established in 1989, seeks to advance the status of anthropology in Europe and increase the visibility of marginalized traditions. Anthropologists have over time assisted state policies and projects, especially colonialism. Some commentators contend the discipline grew out of colonialism, and that ethnographic work is often ahistorical, writing about people as if "out of time" in an "ethnographic present." Present-day anthropologists typically advocate cultural relativism, the notion that cultures should not be judged by another's values. Ethical commitments include noticing and documenting genocide, infanticide, racism, sexism, mutilation, and torture. Anthropologists' involvement with the U.S. government has caused controversy: Franz Boas publicly objected to US participation in World War I, while by the 1940s many of his contemporaries were active in the Allied war effort. Professional bodies often object to state use of anthropology; the AAA's "Principles of Professional Responsibility" once stated that "no secret research, no secret reports or debriefings of any kind should be agreed to or given." In 2009, the AAA's Commission on the Engagement of Anthropology with the US Security and Intelligence Communities concluded that the Human Terrain System (HTS), working with the US military in Afghanistan and Iraq, could no longer be considered a legitimate professional exercise of anthropology.

### Post-World War II developments

Before WWII, British 'social anthropology' and American 'cultural anthropology' were distinct traditions; after the war, borrowing led some to speak of 'sociocultural' anthropology collectively. Anthropology tends to provide a holistic, highly empirical account of phenomena. Biological anthropologists are interested in human variation and human universals. Anthropologists divide the world into time periods (e.g., Paleolithic and Neolithic) and cultural regions, developing comparative methods. Since the 1980s, it has become common to set ethnographic research in the North Atlantic region, examining connections between locations rather than limiting research to a single locale, and broadening focus beyond daily life to settings such as scientific laboratories, social movements, and businesses.

## Terms

- **Cultural relativism**: The notion that cultures should not be judged by another's values or viewpoints, but examined dispassionately on their own terms.
- **Ethnography**: A research method based on long-term fieldwork and participant observation; also the text generated from such fieldwork.
- **Participant observation**: A foundational method of social and cultural anthropology involving experiential immersion in the research area.
- **Ethnology**: The systematic comparison of different cultures; a term coined by Adam F. Kollár in 1783.
- **Kinship**: The study of patterns of social relationships, including descent and marriage; a human universal.
- **Four-field approach**: The traditional US division of anthropology into biological, sociocultural, archaeological, and linguistic anthropology, developed by Franz Boas.
- **Positivism**: The epistemological tradition emphasizing empirical, scientific methods; sociocultural anthropology shifted away from it in the 1970s–1990s, while archaeology and biological anthropology remained largely positivist.
- **Human Terrain System (HTS)**: A US military program embedding anthropologists in Afghanistan and Iraq; deemed incompatible with disciplinary ethics by the AAA in 2009.
- **Ethnomusicology**: The study of music in its cultural, social, and other contexts; formally termed by Jaap Kunst c. 1950.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether anthropology grew out of colonialism and remains in league with it, or whether it can be separated from that legacy. Ethnographic work is criticized as ahistorical, writing about people as if "out of time" in an "ethnographic present." Herbert S. Lewis critiqued older works that presented other cultures as strange, arguing the field should learn from its mistakes rather than discard findings. The ethics of military involvement remain contested: the AAA concluded HTS is incompatible with disciplinary ethics, while stressing that constructive engagement between anthropology and the military is possible. The field also debates whether 'art' is a universal cultural phenomenon, given that Western categories do not exist in most non-Western contexts.
