# Sugar

## Overview

Sugar refers to a class of sweet-tasting, soluble carbohydrates, primarily monosaccharides (simple sugars like glucose, fructose, galactose) and disaccharides (compound sugars like sucrose, lactose, maltose). Sucrose, the dominant commercial sugar, is extracted mainly from sugarcane and sugar beet. Sugars are ubiquitous in plant tissues and are among the most widely consumed food components globally, with average annual per capita consumption reaching about 24 kg, though this varies significantly by region. Their widespread use has driven major historical economic and social transformations, including colonization and slavery, while contemporary concerns focus on health impacts of excessive intake, particularly from added sugars.

## Body

### Chemistry and Classification

Sugars are chemically defined as carbohydrates with the general formula (CH₂O)ₙ. Monosaccharides, such as glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), fructose, and galactose, are the simplest form, typically conforming to CₙH₂ₙOₙ with n between 3 and 7. These molecules contain hydroxyl (−OH) and carbonyl (C=O) groups, exist cyclically in water, and have isomeric forms that rotate plane-polarized light. Disaccharides—sucrose, lactose, and maltose—are formed via condensation reactions between two monosaccharides, expelling a water molecule. Longer chains form oligosaccharides and polysaccharides like starch and cellulose, which are not classified as sugars.

### Historical Development

Sugar production began in the Indian subcontinent from sugarcane, with species originating in India and New Guinea. Arab traders introduced sugar to Europe via Sicily and Spain, and the word "sugar" traces through Sanskrit, Persian, and Arabic into medieval Latin and Old French. European colonization expanded sugar cultivation to the New World, establishing plantation economies in Cuba, Jamaica, and Brazil. The 19th-century invention of beet sugar processing in Germany diversified production. By the 19th century, sugar became a household staple, driving economic expansion and social change, including the transatlantic slave trade.

### Production and Processing

Global sugar production relies on two primary crops: sugarcane (contributing ~21% of crop production from 2000–2021) and sugar beet. In 2022, sugarcane production reached 1.9 billion tonnes, led by Brazil (38%) and India (23%). Sugarcane thrives in tropical climates and is processed by extracting juice, clarifying with lime, evaporating water, and crystallizing. Sugar beet, a biennial root crop, is cultivated in temperate regions and processed similarly. Refining raw sugar involves affination, carbonatation or phosphatation purification, color removal via activated carbon or ion-exchange resins, and recrystallization. ICUMSA numbers measure purity, with refined sugar scoring below 300 and raw sugar above 1,500.

### Forms and Uses

Commercial sugars vary by crystal size, shape, and processing. Granulated (table) sugar, with ~0.6 mm crystals, is the most common form for domestic and industrial use. Brown sugars retain molasses, affecting flavor and moisture. Liquid forms include glucose syrup, corn syrup, and invert sugar, produced by enzymatic hydrolysis or heating. Burnt sugars and caramels result from heating near 200°C. Sugar serves multiple functions: sweetening, preservation, texture modification, and as a precursor in fermentation for alcoholic beverages.

### Consumption and Health Effects

Globally, sugar provides approximately 10% of daily calories, with regional variation—North and South Americans consume up to 50 kg annually, while Africans consume under 20 kg. The WHO recommends limiting free sugars to less than 10% of total energy intake, with further reduction to below 5% providing additional benefits. Excessive consumption is linked to obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, dental caries, and nutritional displacement. While sugar does not directly cause cancer, obesity-related cancers show indirect associations. The "sugar rush" phenomenon in children is considered a myth, though energy drinks containing sugar correlate with unhealthy behaviors.

## Terms

- **Monosaccharide**: Simple sugar (e.g., glucose, fructose) with formula CₙH₂ₙOₙ, the basic building block of carbohydrates.
- **Disaccharide**: Compound sugar formed by two bonded monosaccharides (e.g., sucrose, lactose, maltose).
- **Free sugars**: Monosaccharides and disaccharides added to foods plus those in honey, syrups, and fruit juices.
- **ICUMSA number**: International Commission for Uniform Methods of Sugar Analysis standard measuring sugar purity; lower numbers indicate higher purity.
- **Affination**: First stage of sugar refining, softening raw sugar crystals by immersing them in concentrated syrup.
- **Carbonatation**: Refining process using precipitation to remove impurities from sugar syrup.
- **Chaptalisation**: Adding sugar to fermenting wine must to increase alcohol content.
- **Invert sugar**: Mixture of glucose and fructose created by heating sucrose in water.
- **Molasses**: Viscous by-product remaining after sugar crystallization from cane or beet juice.
- **Glycosidic bond**: Covalent bond linking monosaccharides in disaccharides and polysaccharides, formed with water expulsion.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several contentious issues surround sugar consumption and policy. The Sugar Research Foundation faced criticism for allegedly influencing research in the 1960s–70s to downplay sucrose risks while emphasizing saturated fat. Whether sugar alone causes obesity remains debated; WHO evidence suggests it contributes through caloric excess rather than direct causation. The "sugar rush" myth persists despite low-quality evidence showing no mood-enhancing effects. Nutritional displacement thresholds vary, with some studies indicating effects below 25% energy intake. Interstellar sugar origins remain speculative, with hypotheses including asteroid delivery versus primordial Solar System presence. The detection of erythrulose in 2026 raised questions about prebiotic chemistry's role in life's origins. Policy effectiveness of sugar taxation and labeling requirements continues to generate mixed research outcomes.
