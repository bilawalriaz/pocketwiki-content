# Northeast India

## Overview
Northeast India, officially the North Eastern Region (NER), is a geographically and administratively distinct area comprising eight states. It is one of the world's most linguistically diverse regions and holds significant strategic and ecological importance for India, bordering five countries and containing vast biodiversity and mineral resources.

## Timeline
- **c. 100 BCE** — Chinese explorer Zhang Qian's writings indicate an early trade route via Northeast India.
- **7th century CE** — Chinese Buddhist monk Xuanzang visits the kingdom of Kamarupa.
- **1826** — The First Anglo-Burmese War brings the entire region under British control.
- **1865** — India's first oil well is dug in Digboi, Assam.
- **1944** — The Battle of Imphal and Kohima, a decisive WWII Allied victory, is fought in the region.
- **1947** — Indian independence and partition landlocks the Northeast.
- **1962** — The Sino-Indian War occurs; China captures parts of the North-East Frontier Agency (NEFA).
- **1971** — The North Eastern Council (NEC) is constituted as the development agency for the region.
- **1972** — Meghalaya, Manipur, and Tripura become full-fledged states.
- **1987** — Arunachal Pradesh and Mizoram become full-fledged states.
- **2002** — Sikkim is integrated as the eighth member of the North Eastern Council.

## Body

### History and Formation
The earliest settlers were likely Austroasiatic speakers from Southeast Asia, followed by Tibeto-Burman and Indo-Aryan groups. The region was home to the ancient kingdom of Kamarupa. British control was established after the First Anglo-Burmese War (1826). The modern states were formed after Indian independence, with most achieving statehood between 1956 and 1987. Sikkim was added to the region in 2002.

### Strategic Importance and Conflicts
The region's strategic location has made it a site of major conflicts. The 1944 Battle of Imphal and Kohima was a turning point in the Burma Campaign of World War II. The 1962 Sino-Indian War saw Chinese forces capture territory in what is now Arunachal Pradesh, which China still claims. Post-independence, the region's isolation and ethnic diversity have contributed to separatist unrest, with various militant groups active in several states.

### Geography and Climate
Physiographically, the region includes the Eastern Himalaya, Patkai hills, and the Brahmaputra and Barak valleys. It has a predominantly humid sub-tropical climate with heavy monsoon rainfall; Cherrapunji in Meghalaya is one of the rainiest places on Earth. The region is a high-risk seismic zone due to the convergence of three tectonic plates. It is covered by the Brahmaputra-Barak river system and contains India's last remaining rainforests.

### Biodiversity and Ecology
Northeast India is a global biodiversity hotspot, part of the Indo-Burma and Eastern Himalaya hotspots. It contains 51 of India's 9 major vegetation types and 8,000 flowering plant species. The region is a center of origin for citrus fruits and rich in wild crop relatives. Its fauna includes endangered species like the one-horned rhinoceros, hoolock gibbon, and Gangetic dolphin. The International Council for Bird Preservation identified the Assam plains and Eastern Himalaya as an Endemic Bird Area.

### Demographics and Culture
The population is about 46 million, with Assam holding 68%. The region is home to over 200 ethnic groups and approximately 220 languages from multiple families, including Sino-Tibetan, Indo-Aryan, and Austroasiatic. Assamese serves as a lingua franca. Hinduism and Christianity are the major religions. The culture is rich in folk traditions, with notable classical dances like Manipuri Raas Leela and Sattriya. Traditional handicrafts involve bamboo, cane, and handloom weaving.

### Administration and Economy
The North Eastern Council (NEC), under the Ministry of Development of North Eastern Region (DoNER), coordinates development. The economy is largely agrarian, with jhum (slash-and-burn) cultivation practiced by indigenous groups. Infrastructure is developing but lags behind national averages, with road and rail networks being limited. The "Act East" policy aims to integrate the region economically with East and Southeast Asia through connectivity projects.

## Terms
- **North Eastern Council (NEC):** The statutory body constituted in 1971 to act as the regional planning and development agency for the eight northeastern states.
- **Siliguri Corridor:** A narrow strip of land in West Bengal that connects the northeastern states to the rest of India.
- **Jhum cultivation:** A traditional slash-and-burn agricultural system practiced by indigenous communities in the region.
- **Ophiolite:** A section of the Earth's oceanic crust and upper mantle that has been uplifted and exposed above sea level; the region houses a notable example.
- **Biodiversity hotspot:** A biogeographic region with significant levels of biodiversity that is threatened by human activity; Northeast India is part of two such hotspots.
- **Endemic Bird Area (EBA):** A geographical area that supports two or more species of restricted-range birds.
- **Act East Policy:** India's foreign policy that focuses on enhancing economic, strategic, and cultural relations with the Asia-Pacific region, with the Northeast as a gateway.
- **McMahon Line:** The demarcation line between the northeastern Indian state of Arunachal Pradesh and Tibet, which China does not recognize.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact cause of the escalation into the 1962 Sino-Indian War is still disputed by both Chinese and Indian sources.
- The impact of jhum cultivation on forest cover is debated; while often cited as a reason for loss, it is also a traditional system supporting diverse crop cultivation.
- The effectiveness of the "Act East" policy and various connectivity projects in overcoming the region's geographical and infrastructural isolation for economic development remains a key question.
