# Heraclius

## Overview
Heraclius (c. 575–641) was a Byzantine emperor whose reign was defined by a dramatic reversal of fortunes. He first led a near-catastrophic war against the Sasanian Persian Empire, losing vast territories, before staging a remarkable comeback to defeat them. However, his exhausted empire was then immediately overwhelmed by the rapid Arab conquests, which permanently stripped away its wealthiest eastern provinces. His reign also saw major cultural and religious shifts, including the official adoption of Greek over Latin and a failed attempt to unify Christian doctrine.

## Timeline
- **608** — Heraclius and his father revolt against Emperor Phocas.
- **5 October 610** — Heraclius is crowned emperor after capturing Constantinople.
- **613** — Heraclius is defeated by Persians at the Battle of Antioch.
- **614–622** — Persians conquer Syria, Palestine, and Egypt.
- **4 April 622** — Heraclius launches a major counter-offensive against Persia.
- **626** — Avar-Slav-Persian siege of Constantinople fails.
- **627** — Heraclius defeats the Persians at the Battle of Nineveh.
- **629** — Heraclius restores the True Cross to Jerusalem.
- **636** — Arab forces defeat Byzantines at the Battle of Yarmouk, taking Syria.
- **11 February 641** — Heraclius dies; Egypt is largely lost to the Arabs.

## Body

### Revolt and Accession
Heraclius, son of the Exarch of Africa, rose against the unpopular usurper Phocas in 608. After his cousin secured Egypt, Heraclius sailed to Constantinople. The imperial guard deserted Phocas, allowing Heraclius to seize power and execute him in 610. He married twice, first to Eudokia and later to his niece Martina, a union that caused scandal and produced children with birth defects.

### Byzantine–Sasanian War (602–628)
Heraclius inherited a disastrous war. After his defeat at Antioch in 613, the Persians conquered Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, reducing the empire to Asia Minor. Facing total defeat, Heraclius rebuilt the military using church treasures and launched a brilliant counter-offensive in 622. After years of campaigning, he won a decisive victory at the Battle of Nineveh in 627. The Persian Shah Khosrow II was overthrown, and his successor sued for peace, returning all conquered territory. The war left both empires severely weakened.

### Byzantine–Arab Wars and Final Years
The unified Arab Caliphate quickly exploited the post-war weakness. After initial skirmishes, a major Byzantine army was crushed at the Battle of Yarmouk in 636, leading to the loss of Syria. Heraclius died in 641 as the Arabs were conquering Egypt. His later reign was marked by the failed religious compromise of Monothelitism and the official shift from Latin to Greek as the state language.

### Legacy and Administration
Heraclius is remembered for his victory over Persia and the recovery of the True Cross. He adopted the title *Basileus* (Greek for "sovereign") and may have initiated early administrative reforms, though the full Thematic system is now attributed to later emperors. He also established diplomatic ties with Slavic tribes in the Balkans. His personal life was marred by scandal and family strife, which complicated his succession.

## Terms
*   **Exarch:** A governor of a Byzantine province with extensive military and civil authority.
*   **Sasanian Empire:** The last Persian imperial dynasty before the Muslim conquest, a major rival to Byzantium.
*   **Rashidun Caliphate:** The first caliphate after Muhammad, which led the early Muslim conquests.
*   **Monothelitism:** A theological compromise proposed by Heraclius, stating Christ had two natures but only one will. It was rejected by both Chalcedonian and non-Chalcedonian Christians.
*   **Miaphysite:** A Christian doctrine holding that Christ's divine and human natures are united in one composite nature, prevalent in Egypt and Syria.
*   **Basileus:** The Greek term for "king" or "emperor," which Heraclius adopted as the official imperial title.
*   **Thematic system:** A Byzantine military-administrative province system, now dated to the mid-7th century, not Heraclius's reign.
*   **True Cross:** A Christian relic believed to be from the crucifixion of Jesus, captured by Persians in 614 and recovered by Heraclius.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Heraclius's Origins:** Scholars debate his ethnic background. Some, like Walter Kaegi, consider an Armenian origin "probable," while Anthony Kaldellis argues the primary sources do not support this claim.
*   **The True Cross Narrative:** The story of Heraclius recovering and returning the True Cross is a famous legend, but some scholars, like Constantin Zuckerman, suggest the relic returned in 629 may have been a fake for political purposes.
*   **Islamic Traditions:** Islamic sources describe Heraclius receiving a letter from the Prophet Muhammad and considering Islam. Most Western historians view these accounts as part of religious tradition rather than verifiable history, noting a lack of contemporary Byzantine evidence.
