# Amelia Earhart

## Overview
Amelia Earhart (1897–1937) was an American aviator who became the first woman to fly solo nonstop across the Atlantic Ocean (1932) and the first person to fly solo from Hawaii to California (1935). Her 1928 crossing of the Atlantic as a passenger made her an international celebrity, which she leveraged to promote commercial aviation and women’s rights, co-founding the Ninety-Nines organization for female pilots. In 1937, while attempting an equatorial circumnavigation of the globe in a Lockheed Electra 10E, Earhart and navigator Fred Noonan disappeared over the Pacific Ocean near Howland Island. The U.S. government concluded they ran out of fuel and crashed at sea, though numerous alternative theories persist. Earhart was declared legally dead in 1939.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Amelia Mary Earhart was born July 24, 1897, in Atchison, Kansas, in the home of her maternal grandfather Alfred Gideon Otis, a prominent banker and former judge. Her parents, Samuel "Edwin" Stanton Earhart and Amelia "Amy" Otis Earhart, had a strained marriage complicated by Edwin’s alcoholism and career instability. The family moved frequently—Des Moines, St. Paul, Chicago—while Amelia and her sister Grace Muriel ("Pidge") spent formative years with their grandparents in Atchison. Amy Earhart rejected conventional gender expectations, allowing the girls to wear bloomers and explore freely; Amelia’s childhood adventures included building a backyard roller-coaster ramp (1904) that gave her a "sensation of exhilaration" she later equated with flying. She saw her first aircraft at the 1908 Iowa State Fair but dismissed it as "a thing of rusty wire and wood." After her parents’ 1914 separation and her grandmother’s death (which placed Amy’s inheritance in a trust to protect it from Edwin), Earhart attended Hyde Park High School in Chicago, graduating in 1916. She kept a scrapbook of women in male-dominated careers and briefly attended Ogontz School in Pennsylvania.

### Nursing, Illness, and the Turn to Aviation
During a 1917 Christmas visit to Toronto, Earhart encountered wounded WWI soldiers and trained as a nurse’s aide with the Red Cross, serving at Spadina Military Hospital. There, conversations with military pilots sparked her interest in flying. The 1918 Spanish flu pandemic infected her; she was hospitalized for pneumonia and maxillary sinusitis, enduring painful, unsuccessful sinus washouts in the pre-antibiotic era. Chronic sinusitis plagued her for life, sometimes requiring a cheek bandage over a drainage tube. Convalescing nearly a year in Northampton, Massachusetts, she read poetry, learned banjo, and studied mechanics. She enrolled at Columbia University in 1919 but left in 1920 to join her reunited parents in California.

### Learning to Fly and Early Records
On December 28, 1920, a 10-minute passenger flight with Frank Hawks at Daugherty Field convinced Earhart: "I knew I had to fly." She hired Neta Snook as instructor (12 hours for $500), working multiple jobs—photographer, truck driver, stenographer—to save $1,000 for lessons. Her first lesson was January 3, 1921, at Kinner Field; she cropped her hair short per the custom of female flyers. Six months later, against Snook’s advice, she bought a used chromium-yellow Kinner Airster biplane, "The Canary." On October 22, 1922, she flew it to 14,000 ft, setting a women’s world altitude record. She received FAI license #6017 on May 16, 1923. Financial losses from a failed gypsum-mine investment exhausted her inheritance; she sold the Canary, bought a Kissel "Speedster" automobile ("Yellow Peril"), and after a 1924 sinus operation and her parents’ divorce, drove her mother cross-country to Boston. There she worked as a teacher and social worker at Denison House, joined the American Aeronautical Society’s Boston chapter (becoming vice president), flew out of Dennison Airport, and sold Kinner aircraft while writing newspaper columns promoting aviation.

### 1928 Transatlantic Crossing as Passenger
Inspired by Lindbergh’s 1927 solo flight, heiress Amy Phipps Guest planned to be the first woman to cross the Atlantic by air but withdrew under family pressure, instead sponsoring a "right sort of girl." Publicist George Palmer Putnam and Captain Hilton Railey selected Earhart after Rear Admiral Belknap recommended her from Denison House. Earhart negotiated the title of "commander" aboard the Fokker F.VIIb *Friendship* (pilot Wilmer Stultz, mechanic Louis Gordon), with final authority until London. After weather delays at Trepassey, Newfoundland, they departed June 17, 1928. Stultz flew entirely on instruments through fog; Earhart kept the log but could not take controls due to inexperience with instrument flying. The radio failed early. After 20 hours 49 minutes, low on fuel, they landed in the Burry Estuary, Wales. Earhart insisted the pilots deserved credit: "I was just baggage, like a sack of potatoes ... maybe someday I'll try it alone." A ticker-tape parade in New York and a White House reception with President Coolidge followed. Putnam managed her lecture tours and endorsements (including Lucky Strike, with proceeds to Byrd’s Antarctic expedition), cementing her "Lady Lindy" celebrity.

### Promoting Aviation and Competitive Flying
Earhart became associate editor at *Cosmopolitan*, advocating for commercial air travel and women in aviation. In 1929 she and Margaret Bartlett Thornton promoted Transcontinental Air Transport (TAT) and helped launch Ludington Airline (New York–Washington, D.C. shuttle). She became VP of National Airways (later Northeast Airlines). In 1934 she secured NYU admission for Isabel Ebel as the first woman aeronautical engineering student. She flew the 1929 Women’s Air Derby ("Powder Puff Derby"), placing third in the heavy division after friend Ruth Nichols crashed. In 1930, as a National Aeronautic Association official, she persuaded the FAI to establish separate women’s records. On April 8, 1931, she set a world autogyro altitude record (18,415 ft) in a Pitcairn PCA-2. She co-founded the Ninety-Nines (1929), serving as first president (1930), and protested the 1934 Bendix Trophy Race’s ban on women by refusing to fly Mary Pickford to the event.

### Marriage to George Putnam
Earhart married Putnam February 7, 1931, in Noank, Connecticut, after he proposed six times. She had broken an earlier engagement to Samuel Chapman. Their prenuptial letter declared a "partnership" with "dual control": "I shall not hold you to any medieval code of faithfulness ... I may have to keep some place where I can go to be by myself." She kept her name; Putnam was called "Mr. Earhart." No honeymoon—she began a nine-day autogyro tour for Beech-Nut. Putnam’s sons from his first marriage, David Binney Putnam and George Palmer Putnam Jr., became her stepsons.

### 1932 Solo Transatlantic Flight
On May 20, 1932, Earhart departed Harbour Grace, Newfoundland, in a Lockheed Vega 5B, advised by Bernt Balchen. She battled strong winds, icing, and mechanical issues (including a cracked exhaust manifold and altimeter failure). After 14 hours 56 minutes, she landed in a pasture near Culmore, Northern Ireland. A farmhand asked, "Have you flown far?" She replied, "From America." She received the Distinguished Flying Cross (first woman), the French Legion of Honor, and the National Geographic Society Gold Medal from President Hoover. She befriended Eleanor Roosevelt (who earned a student pilot permit) and confided in rival Jacqueline Cochran.

### Additional Solo Records (1935)
- January 11, 1935: First solo Honolulu → Oakland (Lockheed 5C Vega), routine flight with radio entertainment.
- April 19, 1935: First solo Los Angeles → Mexico City ("old Bessie, the fire horse").
- May 8, 1935: First solo nonstop Mexico City → Newark (large crowds at landing a hazard).
- 1935 Bendix Trophy Race: Fifth place; her stock Vega (195 mph) outclassed by 300+ mph purpose-built racers; marred by a competitor’s fatal crash and severe weather.
Between 1930–1935 she set seven women’s speed/distance records in Kinner Airster, Lockheed Vega, and Pitcairn Autogiro. Recognizing the Vega’s limits for transoceanic flight, she planned a global circumnavigation "as near its waistline as could be."

### West Coast Move and Purdue Affiliation
A November 1934 fire destroyed the Putnams’ Rye, New York home. Putnam sold his publishing interest and became head of Paramount Pictures’ editorial board in North Hollywood. In June 1935 they bought a home in Toluca Lake. In September 1935, Earhart and Paul Mantz formed Earhart-Mantz Flying School at Burbank Airport (Mantz operated via United Air Services; Putnam handled publicity), teaching instrument flying on Link Trainers. Also in 1935, she joined Purdue University as visiting faculty counseling women on careers and technical advisor to the Department of Aeronautics.

### 1937 World Flight Attempt

#### Planning and Aircraft
Purdue established the Amelia Earhart Fund for Aeronautical Research, providing $50,000 for a Lockheed Electra 10E ("flying laboratory") with extra fuel tanks, built July 1936 at Burbank. The equatorial route totaled ~29,000 miles. Original crew: Earhart (pilot), Harry Manning (navigator/radio operator, captain of the *President Roosevelt* that brought her home in 1928). Manning’s navigation during test flights showed errors (20 miles off at night; Putnam and Mantz deemed this unacceptable). Fred Noonan, ex-Pan Am navigator who established China Clipper routes, was added as second navigator. Plan: Noonan navigates Hawaii → Howland Island (difficult leg); Manning continues to Australia; Earhart proceeds solo thereafter.

#### First Attempt (Westward) – Abandoned
March 17, 1937: Departed Oakland → Honolulu with Earhart, Noonan, Manning, Mantz. Propeller hub issues required service at Luke Field, Pearl Harbor. On takeoff for Howland, an uncontrolled ground-loop collapsed the landing gear; both propellers struck ground. Cause disputed: tire blow (witnesses), pilot error (Mantz), or gear collapse (Earhart). Electra severely damaged; shipped to Lockheed for repairs.

#### Second Attempt (Eastward)
Repaired Electra; Manning left crew (only skilled radio operator). Putnam pressed to remove navigation/communication/safety gear to save weight. Crew: Earhart and Noonan. Departed Miami June 1, 1937. Stops in South America, Africa, India, Southeast Asia. Arrived Lae, New Guinea, June 29 (~22,000 miles done). Remaining: ~7,000 miles over Pacific.

#### Lae → Howland Island (July 2, 1937)
Departed Lae 10:00 am local (12:00 am GMT) with ~1,100 US gallons fuel. Destination: Howland Island (6,500 ft × 1,600 ft, 10 ft high), 2,556 miles away. Expected arrival next morning (July 2) after ~20 hours. USCGC *Itasca* stationed at Howland for radio comms, homing signal, RDF, and smoke plume. All navigation aids failed.
- 3 pm Lae time: Earhart at 10,000 ft, descending for clouds.
- 5 pm: 7,000 ft, 150 knots.
- *Itasca* received strong voice transmissions (KHAQQ) but Earhart could not hear *Itasca*.
- 6:14 am: "Within 200 miles," requests RDF bearing; whistles for continuous signal. *Itasca*’s RDF could not tune to 3105 kHz.
- 6:45 am: "100 miles out," requests bearing.
- 7:30–7:40 am: *Itasca* logs "half hour fuel remaining."
- 7:58 am: Earhart cannot hear *Itasca*, asks for voice signals to take radio bearing. *Itasca* sends Morse on 7500 kHz; Earhart hears it but cannot determine direction (no null in loop antenna).
- Last transmission: Flying on line of position 157–337 degrees (calculated by Noonan through Howland). Contact lost.

#### Disappearance and Search
U.S. government concluded fuel exhaustion and ocean crash. Navy/Coast Guard search until July 19 cost $4M (costliest to date); no physical evidence found. Post-search, Putnam funded private searches of nearby islands. Probate court waived seven-year waiting period; Earhart declared dead January 5, 1939. Modern deep-sea searches (Jourdan 2003/2006, Ballard 2019, Romeo 2024) found no wreckage.

#### Speculation
Mainstream view: crashed and sank near Howland. Alternatives: Gardner Island (Nikumaroro) hypothesis—landed on reef, survived briefly; Japanese capture theory—taken in South Seas Mandate; New Britain theory—turned back toward Rabaul (1990 veteran report of jungle wreck unconfirmed); survival/identity change (Irene Bolam claim, 1970 book, settled lawsuit). In September 2025, President Trump ordered declassification of government records.

### Legacy
Earhart’s celebrity, independence, and disappearance created enduring cultural impact. Feminist role model; inspired >1,000 WASP pilots in WWII. Birthplace home in Atchison is Amelia Earhart Birthplace Museum (maintained by Ninety-Nines). Annual Amelia Earhart Festival since 1996. Tributes: commemorative flights (Pellegreno 1967, Finch 1997, Mendieta 2001); Purdue’s Earhart Hall (1964), bronze statue (2009), Amelia Earhart Terminal (2025); Earhart Light on Howland (non-operational); Amelia Earhart Airport (Atchison); Amelia Earhart Dam (Massachusetts); "Earhart Tree" in Hilo (planted 1935). Zonta International Amelia Earhart Fellowship (1938) awards $10,000 to women in aerospace PhDs (1,764 fellowships, $11.9M+ to 1,335 women from 79 countries). 1963 US 8¢ airmail stamp. Motorsports Hall of Fame (1992). *Time* Woman of the Year 1935 (2019). Pop culture: novels (Turtledove, Mendelsohn), films (*Flight for Freedom* 1943, *Amelia* 2009, *Night at the Museum 2* 2009), TV (*Star Trek: Voyager*), music (Joni Mitchell, Public Service Broadcasting, Laurie Anderson), Lego set, Barbie doll, minor planet/lunar crater.

### Records and Achievements (Selected)
**Women’s Firsts:** Atlantic crossing (1928, passenger); autogyro flight (1931); US transcontinental in autogyro (1931); solo Atlantic (1932); nonstop US coast-to-coast (1932). **Overall Firsts:** Atlantic twice (1932); solo Honolulu–Oakland (1935); solo LA–Mexico City (1935); solo nonstop Mexico City–Newark (1935); solo Red Sea–Karachi (1937). **Records:** Women’s altitude 14,000 ft (1922); women’s transcontinental speed (1933); 100 km speed (1931); autogyro altitude 18,415 ft (1931); Oakland–Honolulu east-west speed (1937). **Honors:** First woman Distinguished Flying Cross (1932).

### Books by Earhart
- *20 Hrs. 40 Min.* (1928): Journal of 1928 transatlantic flight (actual log: 20 hr 49 min).
- *The Fun of It* (1932): Memoir and essay on women in aviation.
- *Last Flight* (1937): Compiled by Putnam from journal entries sent during world flight; considered partially Earhart’s original work.

## Terms
- **Fédération Aéronautique Internationale (FAI)**: International body certifying aviation records and licenses.
- **Autogyro**: Rotary-wing aircraft with unpowered rotor in free autorotation; forward thrust from engine-driven propeller.
- **Line of position (LOP)**: Navigational line (here 157–337°) calculated from celestial observation, indicating position relative to a point.
- **Radio direction finding (RDF)**: Technique using a directional loop antenna to locate a radio transmitter’s bearing.
- **Greenwich Civil Time (GCT)**: Time standard based on Greenwich noon; used by Earhart; *Itasca* used a Naval zone system offset by 30 minutes.
- **Ninety-Nines**: International organization of women pilots founded 1929; named for 99 charter members; Earhart first president.
- **Lockheed Electra 10E**: Twin-engine, all-metal monoplane modified with extra fuel tanks for 1937 world flight
