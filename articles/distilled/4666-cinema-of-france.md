# Cinema of France

## Overview
The cinema of France is the oldest national film industry in Europe, originating in 1895. It has been a global leader in cinematic innovation, from the invention of the medium to influential movements like the New Wave. Despite a recent decline in export market share, it remains a major cultural force, supported by government protection and a robust domestic market.

## Timeline
- **1895** — The Lumière brothers hold the first public film screening, launching cinematography.
- **1902** — Georges Méliès releases *A Trip to the Moon*, the first science fiction film.
- **c. 1904-1911** — Pathé Frères leads the world in film production and distribution.
- **Post-WWI** — French film production declines due to lack of capital; U.S. films flood Europe.
- **1930s** — Poetic Realism emerges, with key films by Jean Renoir and Marcel Carné.
- **1951** — The film magazine *Cahiers du cinéma* is founded, laying groundwork for the New Wave.
- **1958-1960s** — The French New Wave movement begins with films by Truffaut, Godard, and others.
- **1966** — The comedy *La Grande Vadrouille* becomes a massive box office hit.
- **1980s** — The *Cinéma du look* style emerges, with films like *Diva* and *Betty Blue*.
- **2001** — *Amélie* becomes the highest-grossing French-language film in the United States.
- **2011** — *The Intouchables* becomes one of the most-watched films in French history.
- **2013** — France is the world's second-largest film exporter.

## Body

### Early Dominance and the Silent Era
The French film industry began with the Lumière brothers' 1895 invention of the cinématographe. By the early 1900s, it was the world's most important, dominated by firms like Pathé, Gaumont, and Méliès. Pioneers like Alice Guy-Blaché directed hundreds of films, and by 1914, cinema was a major economic challenger to theater.

### Interwar Poetic Realism and Post-War Decline
After World War I, a lack of capital crippled French production, allowing cheaper American films to dominate. France responded with import quotas. Between the wars, directors like Jean Renoir and Jean Vigo developed Poetic Realism, creating iconic films such as Renoir's *La Règle du Jeu* (1939) and Carné's *Les Enfants du Paradis* (1945), which was filmed during the Nazi occupation.

### The New Wave and Commercial Success
From the late 1950s, critics from *Cahiers du cinéma* became directors, creating the French New Wave. Led by Jean-Luc Godard and François Truffaut, this movement produced groundbreaking films like *Breathless* (1960) and inspired global filmmakers. Simultaneously, commercial comedies starring Louis de Funès, like *La Grande Vadrouille* (1966), were immensely popular. The movement faded by the late 1960s, but its influence persisted.

### Modern Era and Global Standing
From the 1980s onward, French cinema saw stylistic waves like *Cinéma du look* and major international successes, including *La Femme Nikita* (1990) and *Amélie* (2001). In 2013, France was the world's second-largest film exporter. However, its global box office share has since declined significantly, falling to 15th by 2023. The industry is supported by government measures, including broadcast license requirements and taxes that subsidize production. France consistently produces more films than any other European country, and its domestic market often favors non-American films.

## Terms
- **Poetic Realism**: A film movement of the 1930s that blended realistic settings with poetic, often fatalistic, storytelling.
- **French New Wave (Nouvelle Vague)**: A 1950s-60s movement where critics-turned-directors used innovative techniques like jump cuts and location shooting.
- **Cinématographe**: The portable camera-projector developed by the Lumière brothers.
- **Import quota**: A government restriction on the number of foreign films that can be shown, used to protect domestic production.
- **Cinéma du look**: An 1980s French film style emphasizing slick, commercial visuals and themes of alienation.
- **Palme d'Or**: The highest prize awarded at the Cannes Film Festival.
- **César Award**: The national film award of France, similar to the Oscars.
