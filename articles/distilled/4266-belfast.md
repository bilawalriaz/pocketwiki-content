# Belfast

## Overview
Belfast is the capital and largest city of Northern Ireland, situated on the River Lagan. Its history is marked by rapid industrial growth, deep sectarian division, and a recent peace-driven transformation. The city's identity and politics remain shaped by the legacy of the Troubles and the ongoing constitutional question of its place within the United Kingdom and Ireland.

## Timeline
- **1613** — Chartered as an English settlement by Sir Arthur Chichester.
- **1798** — Society of United Irishmen organises a republican insurrection, defeated locally.
- **1801** — Irish Parliament abolished; Ireland incorporated into the United Kingdom.
- **1888** — Granted city status by Queen Victoria.
- **1912** — Ulster Covenant signed at City Hall, opposing Home Rule.
- **1920–22** — "Savage and unprecedented" communal violence as Belfast becomes capital of Northern Ireland.
- **1941** — Belfast Blitz devastates the city centre and housing stock.
- **1969–1998** — The Troubles: sustained violence, British Army deployment, and paramilitary conflict.
- **1998** — Good Friday Agreement returns power-sharing government to Stormont.
- **2021** — Belfast designated a UNESCO City of Music.
- **2023** — Windsor Framework establishes Belfast as a dual-access trading point for EU and UK goods.

## Body

### Historical Development
Belfast's site has been occupied since the Bronze Age. A Norman settlement existed by the 13th century, but the modern city began with a 1613 plantation charter. Scottish Presbyterians, arriving in the 17th century, drove early growth and linen production. Their political disaffection contributed to the 1798 rebellion and the 1801 union with Great Britain. Access to British capital fuelled an industrial boom from the 1820s, making Belfast the world's leading linen producer ("Linenopolis") and a major shipbuilder. By the 1900s, its shipyards built up to a quarter of all UK tonnage.

### Sectarian Division and Conflict
Industrialisation drew large numbers of Catholics from rural poverty, creating a demographic shift in a largely Protestant town. Tensions, heightened by the "constitutional question" over Home Rule, erupted into violence. Key periods include the 1920–22 communal violence, which killed 500 and displaced 10,000, and the Troubles (late 1960s–1998). The latter involved paramilitaries, security forces, and the British Army's longest continuous deployment. The 1998 Good Friday Agreement ended major violence but left a legacy of physical and social division, with "peace walls" separating communities.

### 21st-Century Transformation
Since the 1990s, Belfast has seen a service-sector boom in financial technology, tourism, and film production. The city centre has been redeveloped, notably the Titanic Quarter. Demographically, the Catholic population has grown, and in 2011, nationalists outnumbered unionists on the City Council for the first time. The 2016 Brexit referendum saw a majority in Belfast vote to remain in the EU. The subsequent Windsor Framework (2023) allows Northern Ireland to effectively remain in the EU Single Market for goods while retaining access to the UK market, a status marketed as a unique economic advantage.

## Terms
*   **Plantation:** The 17th-century colonisation of Ulster with Protestant settlers from Britain.
*   **Home Rule:** The proposed devolution of government to Ireland within the United Kingdom, fiercely opposed by Ulster unionists.
*   **The Troubles:** The three-decade conflict (c. 1968–1998) involving republican and loyalist paramilitaries, state forces, and civil rights protests.
*   **Peace Walls:** Physical barriers separating Protestant and Catholic neighbourhoods, erected during the Troubles.
*   **Good Friday Agreement (1998):** The peace deal that established a power-sharing government in Northern Ireland.
*   **Unionist:** A political tradition favouring Northern Ireland's continued membership of the United Kingdom.
*   **Nationalist:** A political tradition favouring a united Ireland.
*   **Windsor Framework (2023):** The post-Brexit agreement allowing Northern Ireland dual access to EU and UK goods markets.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The source notes that the reasons for the outbreak of the Troubles in the late 1960s are "disputed" by nationalists and unionists.
*   The target date of 2023 for removing all peace walls passed with only a small number dismantled, indicating the enduring nature of physical division.
*   The electoral balance in Belfast has shifted, with no single community holding an overall majority, raising questions about future governance and identity.
*   The unique post-Brexit trading status of Belfast under the Windsor Framework is presented as both a critical economic opportunity and a point of political contention.
