# Classical antiquity

## Overview
Classical antiquity is the period of cultural history in Europe, roughly from the 8th century BC to the 5th century AD, centered on the interwoven civilizations of ancient Greece and Rome. It is foundational to Western culture, as the Greco-Roman world shaped the language, politics, law, philosophy, art, and science of the Mediterranean and, through later revivals, the modern world.

## Timeline
- **c. 8th–7th centuries BC** — Legendary poet Homer lives; earliest Greek alphabetic inscriptions appear.
- **776 BC** — Traditional date for the first Ancient Olympic Games.
- **509 BC** — Roman monarchy is overthrown; the Roman Republic is established.
- **499–449 BC** — Greco-Persian Wars conclude with Greek victory and Athenian dominance.
- **431–404 BC** — Peloponnesian War ends with a Spartan victory over Athens.
- **323 BC** — Death of Alexander the Great; his empire fragments, beginning the Hellenistic period.
- **146 BC** — Roman conquest of Greece marks the end of the Hellenistic period.
- **27 BC** — Augustus becomes first Roman Emperor, beginning the Imperial period.
- **AD 117** — Roman Empire reaches its maximum territorial extent under Emperor Trajan.
- **393 AD** — Theodosian decrees end the Roman imperial cult, establishing Christianity.
- **476 AD** — Deposition of the last Western Roman Emperor, conventionally marking antiquity's end.
- **529 AD** — Closure of the Platonic Academy in Athens by Emperor Justinian I.

## Body

### Archaic Period (c. 8th to c. 6th centuries BC)
This era saw a gradual resurgence after the Late Bronze Age collapse. In Greece, it followed the Dark Ages and featured advancements in political theory, democracy, philosophy, and theatre. The Orientalizing pottery style replaced geometric designs, influenced by Egypt and the Near East. In Italy, the Etruscans established political control by the late 7th century BC, while the Roman Kingdom, traditionally founded in 753 BC, was overthrown in 510 BC, leading to the Republic in 509 BC.

### Classical Greece (5th to 4th centuries BC)
This period, from the end of Athenian tyranny in 510 BC to Alexander the Great's death in 323 BC, was marked by conflict and cultural flourishing. The Greco-Persian Wars (499–449 BC) secured Greek independence and led to Athenian dominance in the Delian League. This rivalry with Sparta culminated in the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC). The 4th century saw shifting hegemonies from Sparta to Thebes, until the rise of Macedon under Philip II and his son Alexander the Great, whose conquests spread Greek culture across the Persian Empire.

### Hellenistic Period (323–146 BC)
Following Alexander's death, his empire was divided among his generals (the Diadochi). Greek became the lingua franca, and Hellenistic culture interacted with Persian, Egyptian, and Near Eastern traditions. Significant advances occurred in science and philosophy, particularly Aristotelianism. The period ended with the Roman Republic's expansion, culminating in the conquest of Greece in 146 BC.

### Roman Republic and Empire (5th century BC to 5th century AD)
The Roman Republic (c. 509–27 BC) grew from a regional power to a super-regional force through wars in Italy, the Punic Wars, and Macedonian Wars. Internal strife and civil wars led to its transformation into the Roman Empire. The Empire, at its height under Trajan (AD 117), controlled the entire Mediterranean. Culturally, it was Hellenized but incorporated eastern traditions like Christianity. Roman society was patriarchal, with the *familia* under a male head's authority.

### Late Antiquity (4th to 6th centuries AD)
The Western Roman Empire weakened due to the crisis of the third century, Germanic invasions, and the rise of Christianity, which became the state religion by 393 AD. The traditional end date is 476 AD, with the deposition of the last Western emperor. The Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire persisted, preserving classical culture and scholarship. The period's end is debated, with other proposed dates being the closure of the Platonic Academy (529) or the Muslim conquests of the 7th–8th centuries, which severed Mediterranean unity.

## Terms
- **Greco-Roman world**: The interwoven civilizations of ancient Greece and Rome, forming the cultural basis of classical antiquity.
- **Hellenistic period**: The era following Alexander the Great's death (323 BC), characterized by the spread of Greek culture and its interaction with other civilizations.
- **Res publica**: Latin term meaning "public matter," from which "republic" derives; used by Romans to refer to their state even under emperors.
- **Lingua franca**: A common language used for communication between people of different native languages; Greek served this role in the Hellenistic East.
- **Diadochi**: The rival generals, successors of Alexander the Great, who fought for control of his empire after his death.
- **Late antiquity**: The period of transition from classical antiquity to the Middle Ages, roughly the 4th to 6th centuries AD.
- **Pirenne Thesis**: A historical theory that the true end of antiquity was caused by the 7th–8th century Muslim conquests, which disrupted Mediterranean unity.

## Debates and Open Questions
The precise end of classical antiquity is debated. While 476 AD (fall of the Western Roman Empire) is conventional, scholars also cite 529 AD (closure of the Platonic Academy) or the Muslim conquests of the 7th–8th centuries as more definitive breaks. The transition was a slow, complex socio-economic change, not a single event. The political legacy of the Roman Empire as a universal state continued to influence medieval and early modern Europe, from Charlemagne's coronation as "Roman Emperor" in 800 to the Russian Czars' claim as heirs to Byzantium.
