# Busan

## Overview
Busan is South Korea's second-largest city and its busiest port, serving as the economic, cultural, and educational hub of the southeastern region. Its strategic coastal location has shaped its history from ancient fishing settlements to a modern global logistics and convention center, making it a key player in Northeast Asian trade and a major cultural destination known for film and festivals.

## Body

### History and Development
The area has been inhabited since the Neolithic period, with fishing as a primary food source. Around the 1st century, the chiefdom of Koch'ilsan-guk was absorbed by the Silla kingdom. During the Goryeo period, the region faced intensified raids from Japanese pirates (wokou), leading to the construction of defensive fortresses. The modern port was established in 1423 alongside a Japanese concession. After the devastating Japanese invasions of 1592–1598, diplomatic relations were restored, and the port was rebuilt. Busan became Korea's first international port in 1876 and developed rapidly as a trade hub with Japan during the colonial period. Its strategic importance was highlighted during the Korean War, when it served as a refuge and de facto capital, defended by the Pusan Perimeter. In 1963, it became a Directly Governed City.

### Geography and Climate
Busan is located on the southeastern tip of the Korean Peninsula, bordered by mountains and the Korea Strait. The city is built in narrow valleys between the Nakdong and Suyeong Rivers. Its climate is a humid subtropical (Köppen: Cfa) influenced by the East Asian monsoon, with mild winters and hot, humid summers prone to typhoons. It is the most typhoon-prone city in South Korea. Notable past typhoons include Sarah (1959), Maemi (2003), and Hinnamnor (2022).

### Economy and Port
Busan's economy is dominated by the service industry (70.3%) and manufacturing (19.8%). As the world's sixth-largest container port, it processed 21.81 million TEU in 2020 and is a key node on the 21st Century Maritime Silk Road. The city is a center for marine science, finance (hosting the Korea Exchange), and the MICE (Meetings, Incentives, Conferences, and Exhibitions) industry. Major commercial districts include Seomyeon and Gwangbok-dong/Nampo-dong.

### Culture and Attractions
Busan is a major cultural hub, hosting the Busan International Film Festival (BIFF), often called the "Cannes of Asia." It is known for its beaches, such as Haeundae and Gwangalli, and cultural villages like Gamcheon. The city's cuisine reflects its history as a military and refugee center, with specialties like *Dongnae pajeon* (scallion pancake) and *milmyeon* (cold wheat noodles), a adaptation of northern *naengmyeon* created by Korean War refugees. It also features numerous temples, museums, and hot spring resorts.

### Sports
Busan is known as the "capital of baseball" and is home to the Lotte Giants. The city hosted major events like the 2002 FIFA World Cup and Asian Games. Local sports teams have historically faced a "curse," with long title droughts, though the Busan KCC Egis (men's basketball) won a championship in 2024.

## Terms
- **Waegwan**: A Japanese concession community or trading post established in Korean port cities.
- **Wokou**: Pirates, often Japanese, who raided coastal areas of Korea and China.
- **Pusan Perimeter**: The defensive line established by UN and South Korean forces around Busan during the Korean War.
- **Directly Governed City**: A major South Korean city with a status equivalent to a province, administered directly by the central government.
- **TEU**: Twenty-foot Equivalent Unit, a standard measure for container cargo volume.
- **MICE**: An acronym for Meetings, Incentives, Conferences, and Exhibitions, a sector of the tourism industry.
- **Milmyeon**: A Busan cold noodle dish made with wheat flour, adapted from the northern dish *naengmyeon* by Korean War refugees.
- **Dongnae pajeon**: A savory pancake from Busan made with whole scallions, chili peppers, and seafood.
