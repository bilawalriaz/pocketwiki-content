# Garden

## Overview
A garden is a planned, controlled space—typically outdoors—for cultivating, displaying, and enjoying plants and nature. Gardens blend natural and artificial materials and serve aesthetic, functional, and recreational purposes, ranging from ornamental displays to food production. The concept of control distinguishes even the wildest garden from uncultivated land. Gardens have evolved across cultures, reflecting social values, artistic ideals, and environmental relationships.

## Timeline
- **c. 1600–1046 BC** — Earliest recorded Chinese gardens in Yellow River valley during Shang dynasty
- **720 CE** — First recorded Japanese gardens mentioned in *Nihon Shoki*
- **57 BC – 668 AD** — Oldest Korean garden records from Three Kingdoms period
- **1495** — King Charles VIII introduces Italian garden style to France after Italian campaign
- **1528** — King Francis I creates gardens at Château de Fontainebleau with fountains and parterres
- **Mid-16th century** — Tuileries gardens originally designed in Paris under Henry II
- **1624** — Jacobean gardens described as "a delightful confusion" by Henry Wotton
- **18th century** — English landscape gardens developed, emphasizing naturalistic design

## Body

### Etymology and Definition
The word "garden" derives from Middle English *gardin*, from Anglo-French *gardin*/*jardin*, of Germanic origin, akin to Old High German *gard* meaning enclosure. Cognates include "yard," "court," and Latin *hortus* (garden), all denoting defined enclosed spaces. In British English, "garden" refers to a small enclosed area adjoining a building; in American English, this is called a "yard."

### Uses and Functions
Gardens serve multiple purposes: aesthetic enjoyment, food production, recreation, and spiritual reflection. They support plant cultivation, garden-based learning, bird and insect observation, seasonal reflection, relaxation, family gatherings, and children's play. Food-producing gardens differ from farms by their smaller scale, labor-intensive methods, and purpose—personal enjoyment or self-sustenance rather than commercial sale.

### History

#### Asia
Chinese gardens originated in the Shang dynasty (1600–1046 BC) as large enclosed royal parks for hunting and cultivation. Three characters described garden types: *yòu* (royal animal enclosures), *pǔ* (plant gardens), and *yuán* (general gardens, adopted during the Qin dynasty). Notable examples include the Terrace, Pond and Park of the Spirit and Shaqui, featuring the infamous Wine Pool and Meat Forest. Japanese gardens began as imperial pleasure gardens, documented in the *Nihon Shoki* (720 CE). Korean gardens, dating over two millennia, emphasize natural simplicity and harmony with the landscape.

#### Europe
European gardening became an art form in the mid-16th century, symbolizing the "ideal republic" and evoking Garden of Eden imagery. French formal gardens emerged under Italian influence, with King Charles VIII importing designers after 1495. The French formal style emphasized geometric symmetry, parterres, and controlled topiary, exemplified by the Tuileries gardens redesigned for Louis XIV. In contrast, the English landscape garden (18th century) favored naturalistic designs. British domestic gardening traditions were practical until the Grand Manner era, with Tudor gardens emphasizing color contrast and knot designs.

### Design
Garden design involves planning layouts and plantings for both private and professional projects. Landscape architects require advanced training and often licensing. Design elements include hard landscapes (paths, walls, water features) and plant selections considering horticultural needs, seasonal appearance, growth habits, and maintenance requirements. Gardens combine natural elements (flora, fauna, soil, water, light) with constructed features (sheds, pergolas, ponds). Design is broadly divided into formal and naturalistic styles, with utilization, style, integration with structures, and budget as primary considerations.

### Environmental Impact
Gardening can harm or benefit the environment. Damage includes habitat destruction, peat extraction, water consumption, pesticide effects on wildlife, and climate change from greenhouse gas emissions. Gardeners produce carbon dioxide through soil disturbance, burning waste, power tool use, and peat application; methane through soil compaction and anaerobic composting; and nitrous oxide through excess fertilizer use. Conversely, gardeners can mitigate climate change by using perennials, composting organic waste, avoiding peat, using hand tools, and planting nitrogen-fixing species. Rain gardens reduce stormwater runoff by absorbing rainfall.

## Terms
- **Parterre**: Ornamental garden design with symmetrical patterns, often using clipped hedges or colored plants
- **Topiary**: Horticultural practice of training perennial plants into ornamental shapes
- **Hard landscape**: Constructed garden elements including paths, walls, patios, and water features
- **Naturalistic garden**: Design style mimicking wild, unstructured plant growth
- **Formal garden**: Design characterized by geometric layouts and controlled plant arrangements
- **Pergola**: Freestanding garden structure of posts and beams supporting climbing plants
- **Folly**: Ornamental garden structure with no practical purpose, built for decoration
- **Horticulture**: Science and practice of garden cultivation and management
- **Landscape architecture**: Professional discipline designing outdoor spaces at various scales
- **Rain garden**: Shallow garden designed to absorb rainfall from hard surfaces

## Debates and Open Questions
The environmental impact of gardening remains contested, with ongoing debate over the balance between aesthetic preferences and ecological responsibility. Climate change effects on gardens are projected to be predominantly negative, though specific regional impacts vary. The distinction between gardens and farms continues to evolve as urban agriculture and market gardening blur traditional boundaries. Eastern garden traditions, particularly Korean gardens, remain understudied in Western scholarship despite their historical significance.
