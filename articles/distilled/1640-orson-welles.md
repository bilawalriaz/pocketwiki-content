# Orson Welles

## Overview
George Orson Welles (1915–1985) was an American actor, director, writer, and producer whose innovations in theatre, radio, and film established him as one of history’s most influential filmmakers. A child prodigy mentored by Roger Hill, he leveraged the New Deal’s Federal Theatre Project to stage groundbreaking, politically charged productions like the all-Black "Voodoo Macbeth" (1936) and the labor opera *The Cradle Will Rock* (1937). His 1938 radio broadcast *The War of the Worlds* caused a reported panic and secured him an unprecedented RKO contract granting final cut—a rarity in the studio system. His debut film, *Citizen Kane* (1941), co-written with Herman J. Mankiewicz and shot by Gregg Toland using deep focus and long takes, is consistently ranked among the greatest films ever made. However, studio interference on *The Magnificent Ambersons* (1942) and the cancellation of *It’s All True* began a lifelong pattern of Welles struggling to maintain creative control and financing. He spent subsequent decades as an independent filmmaker in Europe and Hollywood, self-financing masterpieces like *Othello* (1951), *Touch of Evil* (1958), *The Trial* (1962), and *Chimes at Midnight* (1966) while acting in others’ films to fund his own. Welles’s distinct style—nonlinear narrative, dramatic lighting, deep focus, and radio-derived sound design—pioneered the auteur theory. Though many projects remained unfinished at his death, his work redefined cinematic language.

## Body

### Early Life and Theatrical Foundations
Welles was born into affluence in Kenosha, Wisconsin, but his childhood fractured after his parents separated in 1919. His father, an inventor, became an alcoholic; his mother, a concert pianist, died of hepatitis in 1924 when Welles was nine. He attended the Todd Seminary for Boys in Woodstock, Illinois, where teacher Roger Hill created an ad hoc curriculum allowing Welles to stage theatrical experiments and use the school’s radio station—his first broadcast experience. After his father’s death in 1930, Welles traveled Europe, talking his way onto the stage at Dublin’s Gate Theatre (1931) and later joining Katharine Cornell’s touring company (1933–34). He married Virginia Nicolson in 1934 and, through Cornell’s director Guthrie McClintic, met producer John Houseman, who cast him in Archibald MacLeish’s verse play *Panic* (1935). By 1935, Welles was a high-earning radio actor in Manhattan, working on *The March of Time* and *Columbia Workshop* with a repertory company that would become the Mercury Theatre ensemble.

### Federal Theatre Project and Mercury Theatre (1936–1938)
Houseman, directing the Negro Theatre Unit of the Works Progress Administration’s Federal Theatre Project (FTP), invited Welles to join in 1935. Welles invested his own radio earnings ($1,500/week) to bypass bureaucracy. His first FTP production, *Macbeth* (April 1936), set in a mythical Haitian court with an all-Black cast of 150, became the "Voodoo Macbeth," touring 4,000 miles including a segregated Texas Centennial Exposition. Simultaneously, Welles directed *Horse Eats Hat* and *Dr. Faustus* (using light as scenic element on a near-black stage). In 1937, FTP cutbacks locked the premiere of Marc Blitzstein’s labor opera *The Cradle Will Rock*; Welles led cast and audience 20 blocks to the Venice Theatre, performing without union musicians or actors in a legendary impromptu staging. Breaking with the FTP, Welles and Houseman founded the Mercury Theatre (November 1937), opening with a modern-dress *Caesar* staged as an anti-fascist "cathedral of light" evoking Nuremberg rallies. The company’s radio arm, *The Mercury Theatre on the Air*, debuted July 1938 on CBS.

### *The War of the Worlds* and the RKO Contract
On October 30, 1938, the Mercury broadcast a news-bulletin adaptation of H.G. Wells’s *The War of the Worlds*. The format, combined with listeners dial-spinning during breaks, reportedly caused widespread panic (though the extent is debated). The resulting notoriety drew Hollywood offers. Welles resisted until RKO president George Schaefer offered a 63-page contract (August 21, 1939) granting Welles final cut on two films—subordinating studio financial interests to his creative control—widely considered the greatest contract ever given an untested filmmaker.

### *Citizen Kane* (1941)
RKO rejected Welles’s first two proposals but approved *Citizen Kane*. Co-written with Herman J. Mankiewicz (based on William Randolph Hearst), the script was condensed from 300 pages of Welles’s notes and Mankiewicz’s draft. Welles cast Mercury actors (Joseph Cotten, Agnes Moorehead, Everett Sloane) and hired cinematographer Gregg Toland. They pioneered **deep focus photography** (keeping foreground, middle, and background simultaneously sharp) and **long takes** to mimic human vision, avoiding cuts. Bernard Herrmann composed the score. Hearst’s newspapers blacklisted the film; MGM’s Louis B. Mayer offered to buy and destroy the negative. *Kane* received nine Oscar nominations but won only Best Original Screenplay (shared). Limited release and uneven distribution yielded mediocre box office; the film was vaulted in 1942, only to be revived in France (1946) and US television (1956), eventually topping *Sight & Sound* polls for decades.

### *The Magnificent Ambersons* and *It’s All True* (1942)
Welles’s second RKO film, adapted from Booth Tarkington’s novel, suffered from Stanley Cortez’s slow cinematography and budget overruns. Welles had conceded final cut in contract renegotiations. A 131-minute cut previewed poorly in Pomona (75% negative cards); RKO seized control, cutting 50 minutes, reshooting the ending, and replacing much of Herrmann’s score. The released 88-minute version remains acclaimed but is considered a "great tragedy" of film history. Simultaneously, Welles was producing *It’s All True*, a Good Neighbor policy omnibus film for Nelson Rockefeller’s Office of the Coordinator of Inter-American Affairs (OCIAA). Sent to Brazil for Carnival (February 1942), Welles lost his RKO sponsors (Schaefer resigned, Rockefeller left the board). RKO fired him, edited *Ambersons* without him, and cancelled *It’s All True*, seizing the footage. Welles never recovered from the "Showmanship In Place of Genius" smear campaign.

### Wartime Radio and Stage (1942–1946)
Returning from Brazil, Welles produced the *I Pledge America* War Bond drive (raising $10M+), hosted *Hello Americans* and *Ceiling Unlimited* for CBS/OCIAA, and created *The Mercury Wonder Show* (1943), a big-top magic/circus spectacle for 48,000 service members. He hosted *The Orson Welles Almanac* (1944) on military bases, reviving traditional jazz, and led the Fifth War Loan Drive (raising $21B in bonds). He campaigned extensively for Roosevelt in 1944, speaking at Fenway Park and filling in for FDR at a forum. After FDR’s death (April 1945), Welles delivered a noted tribute and covered the San Francisco UN Conference for Blue-ABC radio. His radio series *Orson Welles Commentaries* (1946) championed the case of Isaac Woodard, a Black veteran blinded by police, prompting Justice Department action.

### Hollywood Struggles: *The Stranger*, *The Lady from Shanghai*, *Macbeth* (1946–1948)
Welles directed *The Stranger* (1946) for International Pictures under a restrictive contract to prove he could deliver on time/budget. It was the first commercial film to use concentration camp footage (seen at the UN Conference). Completed early and under budget, it became Welles’s only bona fide box-office hit, but International reneged on a promised four-picture deal. For Columbia’s Harry Cohn, Welles directed *The Lady from Shanghai* (1947) to repay financing for his failed Broadway musical *Around the World* (1946). Cohn ordered heavy cuts (removing ~1 hour, including a funhouse climax) and rescored it; the film flopped in the US but became a European noir classic. Welles then directed a low-budget *Macbeth* (1948) for Republic, using stylized sets, pre-recorded soundtrack, and a collage script recasting the play as pagan vs. Christian conflict. Republic disliked the Scottish accents, delayed release a year, and forced Welles to cut 20 minutes and add narration. Critics panned it; Jean Cocteau hailed its "crude, irreverent power."

### European Exile and *Othello* (1948–1956)
Welles acted in Europe to fund *Othello* (1949–51), filming in Italy and Morocco with Micheál Mac Liammóir (Iago) and Hilton Edwards (Brabantio). Financing gaps halted production repeatedly; Welles acted in *The Third Man* (1949, as Harry Lime), *Prince of Foxes* (1949), and *The Black Rose* (1950). *Othello* won the Grand Prix at Cannes (1952) despite a flawed soundtrack. Welles worked in UK radio (*The Black Museum*, *Adventures of Sherlock Holmes*) and TV (*King Lear*, 1953). He directed *Mr. Arkadin* (1955) for mentor Louis Dolivet, who removed Welles from editing; five versions exist. Welles directed two BBC TV series (1955): *Orson Welles’ Sketch Book* (including an episode on police brutality/Isaac Woodard) and *Around the World with Orson Welles*.

### *Touch of Evil* and *Don Quixote* (1956–1959)
Returning to Hollywood, Welles directed a *Fountain of Youth* pilot (Peabody winner) and acted in *Man in the Shadow* (1957). At Universal, Charlton Heston insisted Welles direct *Touch of Evil* (1958), reuniting Welles with Russell Metty, Maurice Seiderman, Cotten, and Akim Tamiroff. Welles finished on schedule/budget, but the studio re-edited, reshot, and added exposition. Welles’s 58-page memo was ignored. The film won the Brussels World’s Fair grand prize and influenced the French New Wave (Truffaut, Godard). Walter Murch re-edited per Welles’s memo in 1998. Welles began *Don Quixote* in Mexico (1957), starring Mischa Auer and Tamiroff, but endlessly reworked it for decades, never completing a satisfactory version.

### *The Trial*, *Chimes at Midnight*, and Unfinished Projects (1960–1970)
In Europe, Welles directed *The Trial* (1962) for the Salkinds, shooting interiors in Paris’s abandoned Gare d’Orsay after funds ran out for sets. He called it his best film; it failed commercially. He met Oja Kodar, his partner for 20 years. *Chimes at Midnight* (1965–66), drawn from six Shakespeare plays (*Five Kings*), starred Welles as Falstaff with narration by Ralph Richardson. Welles considered it his masterpiece. He directed *The Immortal Story* (1968) for French TV. *The Deep* (1967, from *Dead Calm*) was abandoned after Laurence Harvey’s death. *Orson’s Bag* (1969) for CBS was halted when the IRS seized funding. Welles moved back to the US in 1970.

### Final Years: *The Other Side of the Wind* and Legacy Projects (1970–1985)
Welles self-financed *The Other Side of the Wind* (1970–76), co-written with Kodar, starring John Huston as an aging director. Iranian backing collapsed after the Shah’s fall, trapping the film in legal limbo until 2018. Welles acted in *Waterloo* (1970), *Treasure Island* (1972), and *Casino Royale* (1967). He completed *F for Fake* (1973), an essay film on forger Elmyr de Hory, and hosted *Orson Welles Great Mysteries* (1973–74). He narrated *Future Shock* (1972), *Bugs Bunny: Superstar* (1975), and commercials (Paul Masson, Carlsberg). He worked on *The Dreamers*, *Filming the Trial*, and *Orson Welles’ Magic Show*—none completed. His final roles: voice of Unicron in *The Transformers: The Movie* (1986) and Henry Jaglom’s *Someone to Love* (1987, released posthumously). He died October 10, 1985, hours after taping a *Merv Griffin Show* interview.

### Activism and Politics
Welles was a lifelong progressive Democrat. As OCIAA goodwill ambassador (1941–42), he promoted hemispheric solidarity in Latin America, likely gathering intelligence. He crusaded for Isaac Woodard (1946), campaigned for FDR (1944), and considered a 1946 Senate run (seat won by Joseph McCarthy). Listed in *Red Channels* (1950), he was ostracized during the Red Scare. He narrated *The Begatting of the President* (1970, satirizing Nixon), praised Tito (1970s), and spoke to 700,000 at a 1982 nuclear disarmament rally attacking Reagan.

### Personal Life
Welles married three times: Virginia Nicolson (1934–40, daughter Christopher), Rita Hayworth (1943–47, daughter Rebecca), Paola Mori (1955–death, daughter Beatrice). Longtime partner Oja Kodar (from 1966) collaborated professionally. Rumored son Michael Lindsay-Hogg (with Geraldine Fitzgerald) was inconclusive via DNA; McGilligan (2015) cites timeline impossibility. Rebecca’s son Marc McKerrow (revealed 2004) died 2010. Welles suffered lifelong asthma, spinal anomalies, and obesity (over 275 lbs by 1953). Raised Episcopalian, he identified as atheist late in life ("I don't want to bore God"). Ashes buried in Spain (1987) on Antonio Ordóñez’s Ronda estate.

## Terms
- **Deep focus photography**: Cinematographic technique keeping near and far planes simultaneously sharp, pioneered by Welles and Gregg Toland in *Citizen Kane* to mimic human vision and avoid editing cuts.
- **Long take**: An extended, unedited shot lasting significantly longer than conventional editing pace; Welles used these to create spatial continuity and real-time tension.
- **Final cut privilege**: Contractual right of a director to approve the final edited version of a film; Welles secured this unprecedented right in his 1939 RKO contract.
- **Federal Theatre Project (FTP)**: New Deal program (1935–39) funding live theatre to employ artists; Welles used it for *Voodoo Macbeth* and *The Cradle Will Rock*.
- **Mercury Theatre**: Independent repertory company founded by Welles and John Houseman (1937–41) producing stage and radio work (*Caesar*, *War of the Worlds*).
- **Auteur theory**: Critical framework positing the director as the primary "author" of a film; Welles is cited as the "ultimate auteur" for his control over writing, directing, acting, and editing.
- **Good Neighbor policy**: FDR’s Latin American foreign policy (non-intervention, cultural exchange); Welles’s *It’s All True* and OCIAA work supported it.
- **Voodoo Macbeth**: Welles’s 1936 FTP *Macbeth* set in a mythical Haitian court with an all-Black cast, replacing Scottish witchcraft with vodou.
- **The War of the Worlds broadcast**: October 30, 1938, Mercury Theatre radio adaptation using news-bulletin format; caused reported listener panic.
- **Unfinished films**: Welles left numerous projects incomplete (*Don Quixote*, *The Other Side of the Wind*, *The Deep*, *The Dreamers*) due to financing, legal disputes, or perfectionism.

## Debates and open questions
- **Extent of *War of the Worlds* panic**: Contemporary reports claimed mass hysteria; later scholarship suggests the panic was exaggerated by newspapers hostile to radio competition.
- **Mankiewicz vs. Welles authorship of *Citizen Kane***: Industry debate over screenwriting credit; Welles claimed final authorship, Mankiewicz won the Oscar (shared). Pauline Kael’s "Raising Kane" (1971) argued Mankiewicz was primary author; later scholars contest this.
- **Authentic version of *The Magnificent Ambersons***: The 50 minutes cut by RKO (including the original ending) are lost; scholars debate whether Welles’s 131-minute cut was a masterpiece or needed trimming.
- **Paternity of Michael Lindsay-Hogg**: Fitzgerald’s alleged affair with Welles (c. 1939–40); DNA inconclusive; McGilligan (2015) argues Welles was in US during conception window.
- **Completion of *Don Quixote***: Welles worked on it 1957–1980s, constantly re-editing; Jesús Franco’s 1992 assembly from decaying footage is disputed as unrepresentative.
- **Legal status of *The Other Side of the Wind***: Iranian funding, Welles’s estate, and multiple claimants locked the film for decades; released only in 2018 after Netflix settlement.
