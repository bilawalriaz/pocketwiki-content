# Nutrition

## Overview  
Nutrition is the biochemical and physiological process by which organisms use food and water to obtain energy and build cellular structures. It is central to survival, growth, and reproduction across all life forms, and its scientific study—nutritional science—has evolved from early chemical experimentation to modern understandings of macronutrients, micronutrients, and dietary health. The field emphasizes human nutrition due to its direct impact on public health, agriculture, and disease prevention.

## Timeline  
- **Late 18th century** — Scientific analysis of food begins during the Chemical Revolution
- **1910s** — Modern nutrition science emerges with identification of individual micronutrients
- **1926** — Thiamine becomes the first vitamin chemically identified
- **1932** — Vitamin C linked to protection against scurvy
- **1930s–1940s** — First Recommended Dietary Allowances developed amid concerns over wartime deficiencies
- **1860** — First specialized pet food manufactured
- **Beginning of Holocene (~10,000 years ago)** — Neolithic Revolution introduces agriculture, shifting human diet from foraging to farming

## Body  

### Nutrients and Their Roles  
Nutrients are substances that provide energy and structural components for organisms. They include elements like carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus, as well as macromolecules such as carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins. Macronutrients are needed in large amounts; micronutrients—in smaller quantities—are further divided into vitamins (organic) and minerals (inorganic). Essential nutrients cannot be synthesized by the body and must be obtained through diet. Malnutrition arises from insufficient or excessive intake of essential nutrients, contributing to conditions like obesity, type 2 diabetes, and deficiency diseases.

### Nutritional Groups and Metabolism  
Organisms are classified based on how they acquire carbon and energy. Autotrophs produce their own nutrients from inorganic sources, while heterotrophs consume other organisms. Mixotrophs combine both strategies. Phototrophs derive energy from light; chemotrophs from chemical reactions. Organotrophs obtain electrons from organic matter; lithotrophs from inorganic substances. Prototrophs can synthesize essential nutrients; auxotrophs cannot. These classifications reflect evolutionary adaptations to diverse environments and resource availability.

### Diet and Foraging  
An organism’s diet encompasses all food it consumes. A balanced diet supports physical and mental health and requires adequate intake of vitamins, minerals, essential amino acids, and fatty acids. Foraging—the search for nutrients—is guided by sensory detection and regulated by mechanisms like optimal foraging theory, which models behavior as a cost-benefit trade-off. Specialists target specific foods; generalists exploit varied sources. Cultural and religious practices also shape dietary choices in humans.

### Nutrient Cycles  
Nutrient cycles describe the movement of inorganic matter through soil, air, water, and living organisms. Unlike energy flow, which is unidirectional, mineral cycling is continuous. Key cycles include those of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and water. These processes sustain ecosystem function and influence global phenomena such as climate and oxygen levels. Disruptions to nutrient cycling pose risks to environmental stability.

### Nutrient Deficiency and Malnutrition  
Malnutrition results from inadequate or excessive nutrient intake and differs from mere inadequacy. Deficiencies may stem from reduced consumption or impaired absorption, often exacerbated by illness. Symptoms can be hidden or severe, leading to diseases such as scurvy, anemia, or cretinism. The body responds by lowering energy expenditure and drawing on stored reserves. Daily Reference Values help prevent deficiencies in populations, though individual needs vary by age, sex, activity level, and health status.

### Nutrition Across Organisms  

#### Animals  
Animals are heterotrophs requiring carbohydrates, amino acids, and fatty acids. Carbohydrates yield 4 kcal/g, fats 9 kcal/g, and proteins 4 kcal/g. Herbivores rely on microbial fermentation to digest cellulose; obligate carnivores depend on meat for certain nutrients. Animal behavior, including migration and breeding, is closely tied to food availability. Conditioned food aversions and nutrient-specific hunger regulate intake.

#### Humans  
Humans are omnivores whose nutrition shifted dramatically with the advent of agriculture. The Chemical Revolution enabled systematic study of nutrients, while industrialization allowed mass production and fortification. Human diets include cereals, meats, dairy, fruits, and vegetables. Processed foods enhance safety and shelf-life but may lack essential nutrients. Poor nutrition contributes to both deficiency disorders and chronic diseases like obesity and metabolic syndrome. Social determinants strongly influence dietary patterns and health outcomes.

#### Domesticated Animals  
Managed by humans, domesticated animals receive formulated feeds tailored to their needs. Pet foods, developed since 1860, aim to deliver complete nutrition. Cats require taurine and other meat-derived nutrients; large-breed puppies risk overnutrition from energy-dense diets. Proper feeding ensures health and longevity in captive and working animals.

#### Plants  
Plants absorb nutrients from soil and atmosphere via roots and leaves. Essential elements include carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, and potassium. Nitrogen fixation by bacteria converts atmospheric nitrogen into usable forms. Energy comes from photosynthesis, not nutrient uptake. Root hairs facilitate ion absorption through cation exchange, while stomata mediate gas exchange.

#### Fungi  
Fungi are chemoheterotrophs that secrete enzymes to break down organic matter externally before absorbing nutrients through their mycelium. They exist as saprophytes, parasites, or symbionts. Their absorptive nutrition supports decomposition and mutualistic relationships critical to ecosystem health.

#### Protists  
Protists exhibit wide nutritional diversity. Algae perform photosynthesis; protozoa vary from free-living hunters to parasitic invaders. Many are mixotrophic, switching between autotrophy and heterotrophy depending on conditions. Some use mycelium-like structures for nutrient absorption.

#### Prokaryotes  
Bacteria and archaea employ varied nutritional strategies. Phototrophs harness sunlight; lithotrophs metabolize inorganic compounds. Some are predatory, using chemotaxis or enzyme secretion to capture prey. Extremophiles thrive in harsh, nutrient-poor settings by exploiting alternative energy sources.

## Terms  
- **Autotroph**: Organism producing its own nutrients from inorganic substances.  
- **Heterotroph**: Organism obtaining nutrients by consuming other organisms.  
- **Macronutrient**: Nutrient required in large amounts (e.g., carbohydrates, proteins, fats).  
- **Micronutrient**: Nutrient needed in small amounts (e.g., vitamins, minerals).  
- **Essential nutrient**: Nutrient the body cannot synthesize and must obtain from diet.  
- **Malnutrition**: Condition resulting from insufficient or excessive nutrient intake.  
- **Photosynthesis**: Process by which plants convert light energy into chemical energy.  
- **Cation exchange**: Soil process where root-released hydrogen ions displace nutrient cations for uptake.  
- **Optimal foraging theory**: Model predicting animal foraging behavior as maximizing energy gain while minimizing effort.  
- **Mixotroph**: Organism capable of both autotrophic and heterotrophic nutrition.

## Debates and Open Questions  
The extent to which social determinants versus biological factors drive nutrition-related health disparities remains debated. Additionally, the long-term effects of processed foods and additives on human health continue to be studied. In ecology, questions persist about how climate change will alter nutrient cycling and its downstream impacts on food security and ecosystem resilience.
