# History of chemistry

## Overview
The history of chemistry traces humanity's evolving understanding of matter and its transformations, from ancient practical arts like metallurgy and fermentation to the modern science of atomic and molecular interactions. It matters because it underpins technological progress, from the Bronze Age to pharmaceuticals, and reveals how scientific methods replaced mystical explanations through systematic experimentation and theory.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Egyptians use meteoric iron for weapons; gold metallurgy known in Egypt.
- **c. 3500 BC** — Bronze Age begins with smelting of copper and tin alloys.
- **c. 1200 BC** — Iron Age begins with Hittite ironworking.
- **1661** — Robert Boyle publishes *The Sceptical Chymist*, advocating experimental rigor.
- **1789** — Antoine Lavoisier publishes *Traité Élémentaire de Chimie*, establishing conservation of mass and modern nomenclature.
- **1803** — John Dalton proposes atomic theory and law of multiple proportions.
- **1869** — Dmitri Mendeleev publishes his periodic table of elements.
- **1897** — J.J. Thomson discovers the electron.
- **1905** — Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch develop the Haber process for ammonia synthesis.
- **1913** — Niels Bohr proposes quantized atomic model.
- **1926** — Erwin Schrödinger formulates wave mechanics.
- **1953** — James Watson and Francis Crick deduce DNA's double helix structure.

## Body

### Ancient Foundations
Early humans harnessed chemical reactions like fire for cooking, pottery, and metal purification. By 1000 BC, civilizations practiced metallurgy, extracting metals from ores via smelting. The Bronze Age (c. 3500 BC) marked a shift with copper-tin alloys, while the Iron Age (c. 1200 BC) began with harder, more abundant iron. Philosophical theories emerged, such as Empedocles' four elements (earth, fire, air, water) and Greek atomism (Democritus' "atomos"), though these were speculative.

### Medieval Alchemy
Alchemy, a protoscience blending mysticism and practical experimentation, dominated from Greco-Roman Egypt through the Islamic Golden Age. Alchemists like Jābir ibn Hayyān classified substances and developed apparatuses (e.g., alembic). Paracelsus introduced the tria prima (sulfur, mercury, salt). Despite goals like the philosopher's stone, alchemy lacked systematic naming and reproducible methods, leading to skepticism and fraud. Its experimental foundations, however, paved the way for modern chemistry.

### Early Modern Chemistry (17th–18th Centuries)
Robert Boyle's *The Sceptical Chymist* (1661) challenged classical elements and emphasized experimentation. Georg Stahl's phlogiston theory (1702) explained combustion but was later disproven. Key discoveries included Joseph Black's isolation of carbon dioxide (1754) and Henry Cavendish's hydrogen (1766). Antoine Lavoisier overturned phlogiston with oxygen's role in combustion, established conservation of mass (1789), and created modern chemical nomenclature. Alessandro Volta's voltaic pile (1800) founded electrochemistry.

### 19th-Century Advances
John Dalton's atomic theory (1803) and Jöns Jacob Berzelius's atomic weights and chemical symbols systematized chemistry. Humphry Davy used electrolysis to discover alkali metals. Friedrich Wöhler's synthesis of urea (1828) disproved vitalism, spurring organic chemistry. Dmitri Mendeleev's periodic table (1869) organized elements by atomic weight, predicting undiscovered ones. Thermodynamics advanced via Josiah Willard Gibbs's free energy concept (1876). William Ramsay discovered noble gases (1894–1898), and Marie and Pierre Curie identified radioactivity and elements polonium and radium.

### 20th-Century Transformations
Ernest Rutherford's nuclear model (1911) and Niels Bohr's quantized atom (1913) revolutionized atomic theory. Quantum mechanics, with Schrödinger's wave equation (1926) and Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (1927), explained chemical bonding via Gilbert Lewis's electron-pair theory (1916). Otto Hahn's nuclear fission discovery (1938) enabled nuclear energy. Linus Pauling applied quantum principles to molecular structure. The late century saw computational chemistry, polymer science (e.g., fullerenes, 1985), and biotechnological advances like PCR (1983).

## Terms
- **Alchemy**: Protoscience blending mysticism and experimentation, seeking transformations like the philosopher's stone.
- **Phlogiston theory**: 18th-century theory that combustible materials release phlogiston during burning, disproven by Lavoisier.
- **Atomic theory**: Dalton's model stating matter consists of indivisible atoms with unique weights.
- **Periodic table**: Mendeleev's classification of elements by atomic weight and properties.
- **Electrolysis**: Process using electric current to drive chemical reactions, isolating elements.
- **Vitalism**: Belief that organic compounds require a "vital force," disproven by Wöhler's urea synthesis.
- **Radioactivity**: Spontaneous emission of radiation by elements, discovered by the Curies.
- **Quantum mechanics**: Theory describing particle behavior at atomic scales, explaining chemical bonding.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The transition from alchemy to chemistry involved debates over reproducibility and esoteric language.
- Atomic theory faced skepticism from energeticists like Wilhelm Ostwald until Jean Perrin's experiments (early 1900s).
- The structure of benzene was contested after Kekulé's proposal (1865).
- Quantum mechanics' interpretation (e.g., wave-particle duality) sparked philosophical debates among physicists like Schrödinger.
