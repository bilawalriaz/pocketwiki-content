# Kingdom of Hungary

## Overview
The Kingdom of Hungary was a monarchy in Central Europe that existed for nearly a millennium, from 1000 to 1946. It was a major European power for much of its history, evolving from a Christian state founded by Saint Stephen I into a multi-ethnic kingdom that endured Mongol invasions, Ottoman occupation, Habsburg rule, and significant territorial losses in the 20th century. Its history is central to understanding the political and ethnic development of modern Central Europe.

## Timeline
- **c. 895** — Hungarians, led by Árpád, settle the Carpathian Basin, establishing the Principality of Hungary.
- **c. 1000** — Coronation of Stephen I as the first king, founding the Christian Kingdom of Hungary.
- **1102** — Union of the Kingdom of Hungary and the Kingdom of Croatia under King Coloman.
- **1241–42** — Mongol invasion devastates Hungary, killing 15–25% of the population.
- **1526** — Battle of Mohács; Ottoman victory leads to the collapse of central authority and the kingdom's partition.
- **1541** — Fall of Buda solidifies the three-way partition: Habsburg Royal Hungary, Ottoman Hungary, and the Principality of Transylvania.
- **1703–11** — Rákóczi's War for Independence against Habsburg rule.
- **1867** — Austro-Hungarian Compromise creates the dual monarchy of Austria-Hungary.
- **1918** — Monarchy ends with the deposition of King Charles IV after World War I.
- **1920** — Treaty of Trianon cedes 72% of Hungary's territory; the kingdom is nominally restored under Regent Miklós Horthy.
- **1946** — The monarchy is formally abolished, and Hungary becomes a republic under Soviet influence.

## Body

### Origins and High Middle Ages
The Kingdom of Hungary was established around 1000 with the coronation of Stephen I, who Christianized the state and established the Árpád dynasty. The kingdom became a European power, successfully defending against the Holy Roman Empire and expanding. A pivotal moment was the 1102 personal union with Croatia under King Coloman. The 13th century was marked by the devastating Mongol invasion of 1241, which killed a significant portion of the population, followed by a period of recovery and fortification under King Béla IV.

### Late Middle Ages and Golden Age
After the Árpád dynasty ended in 1301, Hungary was ruled by the Angevin and then other dynasties. The kingdom reached a golden age under Matthias Corvinus (1458–1490), known for his modern mercenary army, economic reforms, and diplomatic skill. This period also saw successful resistance to the expanding Ottoman Empire, notably at the Siege of Belgrade in 1456. The era ended with the catastrophic defeat at the Battle of Mohács in 1526, which led to the kingdom's partition.

### Partition and Ottoman-Habsburg Struggle
Following Mohács, Hungary was divided into three parts: Habsburg Royal Hungary in the north and west, Ottoman-controlled central and southern territories, and the semi-independent Principality of Transylvania in the east. The Habsburgs, who held the Hungarian throne, fought prolonged wars against the Ottomans. The Long War (1593–1606) and other conflicts continued until the Ottoman defeat at the Battle of Vienna in 183 and the subsequent reconquest of Hungary by the end of the 17th century.

### Habsburg Rule and National Revival
After the Ottoman expulsion, the Habsburgs consolidated control, leading to Hungarian uprisings for independence, most notably Rákóczi's War (1703–1711) and the Hungarian Revolution of 1848. The revolution was crushed with Russian help. A lasting compromise was reached in 1867 with the Austro-Hungarian Compromise, creating the dual monarchy of Austria-Hungary. The Lands of the Crown of Saint Stephen (Hungary and Croatia-Slavonia) gained internal autonomy and experienced economic growth.

### 20th Century and Dissolution
After World War I, the monarchy collapsed in 1918. Following a brief communist republic, the kingdom was restored in 1920 under Regent Miklós Horthy. The Treaty of Trianon (1920) stripped Hungary of 72% of its territory and left millions of ethnic Hungarians in neighboring states. In the interwar period, Hungary allied with Nazi Germany, regaining some territories via the Vienna Awards. It joined the Axis in World War II, suffering heavy losses. The country was occupied by the Soviet Union in 1944, leading to a communist takeover and the formal abolition of the monarchy in 1946.

## Terms
*   **Árpád dynasty:** The ruling dynasty of Hungary from the settlement of the Carpathian Basin (c. 895) until the death of Andrew III in 1301.
*   **Personal union:** A relationship where two or more states are united by having the same monarch, but their governments and laws remain separate. The union of Hungary and Croatia from 1102 is an example.
*   **Principality of Transylvania:** A semi-independent state that emerged in eastern Hungary after the Ottoman conquest, often under Ottoman suzerainty.
*   **Habsburg Monarchy:** The collection of territories ruled by the Habsburg family, which held the Hungarian throne continuously from 1526 to 1918.
*   **Treaty of Trianon (1920):** The peace treaty that formally ended World War I for Hungary, resulting in the loss of 72% of its territory and population.
*   **Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867:** The agreement that created the dual monarchy of Austria-Hungary, granting the Kingdom of Hungary internal autonomy.
*   **Regency (1920–1946):** The period after the monarchy's nominal restoration when Hungary was governed by a regent, Miklós Horthy, in the absence of a king.
*   **Lands of the Crown of Saint Stephen:** The official name for the territories of the Kingdom of Hungary within Austria-Hungary (1867–1918).

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The precise nature of the relationship between the Kingdom of Hungary and the Kingdom of Croatia after 1102 is debated, with interpretations ranging from a personal union to a more integrated state.
*   The historical justification and ethnic composition of the territories assigned by the Treaty of Trianon remain highly contested in Hungarian and neighboring historiographies.
*   The degree of autonomy and the political character of the Principality of Transylvania during Ottoman suzerainty is a subject of ongoing scholarly analysis.
