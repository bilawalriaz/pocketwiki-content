# Black hole

## Overview
A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so intense that nothing, including light, can escape. Predicted by Einstein’s general relativity, black holes form when massive stars collapse or through other high-density processes. They are defined by an event horizon—a boundary beyond which causal influences cannot reach outside observers—and a central singularity where spacetime curvature becomes infinite. The "no-hair theorem" states that a stable black hole is fully described by just three properties: mass, electric charge, and angular momentum. While long considered mathematical curiosities, observational breakthroughs since the 1960s—including X-ray binaries, gravitational waves, and direct imaging of shadows—have confirmed their existence as stellar-mass and supermassive objects central to galaxy evolution.

## Timeline

- **1796** — Pierre-Simon Laplace independently suggests massive invisible stars.

- **1916** — Karl Schwarzschild finds the first exact solution to Einstein's equations for a spherical mass; identifies a singular radius (Schwarzschild radius).
- **1939** — Oppenheimer and Snyder publish the Oppenheimer–Snyder model of gravitational collapse, describing a star freezing at its Schwarzschild radius from an external view.
- **1958** — David Finkelstein identifies the Schwarzschild surface as an event horizon—a unidirectional membrane.
- **1963** — Roy Kerr discovers the exact solution for a rotating black hole (Kerr metric).
- **1965** — Roger Penrose proves singularities are generic in gravitational collapse (singularity theorem).
- **1967** — Pulsars discovered, confirming neutron stars exist and spurring interest in compact objects; term "black hole" popularized by John Wheeler.
- **1971–1974** — Cygnus X-1 identified as the first widely accepted stellar-mass black hole candidate via X-ray and optical observations.
- **1974** — Stephen Hawking predicts Hawking radiation, linking black hole mechanics to thermodynamics.
- **1995** — VLBA observations of water masers in NGC 4258 rule out dense star clusters, confirming a supermassive black hole.
- **2015** — LIGO detects gravitational waves (GW150914) from a binary black hole merger, the first direct detection.
- **2019** — Event Horizon Telescope releases the first direct image of a black hole shadow (M87*).
- **2022** — Event Horizon Telescope releases image of Sagittarius A*, the Milky Way's central supermassive black hole.

## Body

### Theoretical Foundations and Early History
The concept of a body trapping light originated in the late 18th century with John Michell and Pierre-Simon Laplace, who envisioned massive "dark stars" using Newtonian gravity. Michell correctly hypothesized such objects could be detected by their gravitational pull on visible companions. The modern framework arrived with Einstein’s 1915 general relativity, which redefined gravity as spacetime curvature. Months later, Karl Schwarzschild solved the field equations for a static, spherical mass, finding a singularity at a specific radius (the Schwarzschild radius). For decades, physicists including Arthur Eddington and Einstein himself doubted the physical reality of this solution, believing pressure or rotation would halt collapse. In 1939, Oppenheimer and Snyder modeled the collapse of a pressureless dust sphere, showing that from an outside perspective the star appears to freeze at the Schwarzschild radius due to gravitational time dilation, while an infalling observer crosses it in finite time. David Finkelstein’s 1958 work redefined this boundary as an event horizon—a causal boundary—and introduced coordinates allowing description of the infalling perspective, convincing many skeptics.

### The Golden Age and Mathematical Structure
The 1960s–1970s saw a "golden age" where black holes became mainstream. Roy Kerr (1963) and Ezra Newman (1965) found solutions for rotating and charged rotating black holes (Kerr–Newman metric). Work by Werner Israel, Brandon Carter, and others culminated in the **no-hair theorem**: a stationary black hole is completely characterized by mass, angular momentum, and electric charge; all other details ("hair") are radiated away as gravitational waves or swallowed. Roger Penrose’s 1965 singularity theorem proved singularities are inevitable in generic collapse, not artifacts of symmetry. Simultaneously, the discovery of pulsars (1967) validated neutron stars, making black holes plausible endpoints for heavier stars. Jacob Bekenstein and Stephen Hawking developed **black hole thermodynamics**, identifying surface area with entropy and surface gravity with temperature. Hawking’s 1974 prediction of **Hawking radiation**—thermal emission due to quantum effects at the horizon—implied black holes evaporate, creating the information loss paradox.

### Observational Confirmation
The first strong candidate, **Cygnus X-1**, was identified 1971–1974 as an X-ray binary where a massive star feeds a compact companion exceeding the neutron star mass limit (Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit, ~2–3 M☉). Supermassive black holes (millions to billions M☉) were confirmed in galactic centers via stellar dynamics (e.g., stars orbiting Sagittarius A* tracked by Ghez and Genzel groups, yielding ~4.3 million M☉), maser kinematics (NGC 4258, 1995), and the **M–sigma relation** linking black hole mass to host galaxy bulge velocity dispersion. The **Event Horizon Telescope** (EHT) produced the first horizon-scale images of M87* (2019) and Sgr A* (2022), resolving the black hole shadow. **Gravitational wave astronomy** began with LIGO’s 2015 detection of GW150914, a merger of ~30 and 35 M☉ black holes, providing direct tests of strong-field gravity and population statistics.

### Formation and Evolution
**Stellar-mass black holes** (typically 5–100 M☉) form via core-collapse supernovae or direct collapse of massive stars (>20–25 M☉), sometimes through fallback or neutron star mergers. The minimum mass is set by the maximum stable neutron star mass (~2–3 M☉). **Intermediate-mass black holes** (10²–10⁴ M☉) are rare candidates possibly formed in dense clusters or hierarchical mergers. **Supermassive black holes** (>10⁶ M☉) reside in most galactic centers; their rapid appearance at high redshift (z ≥ 7) challenges standard accretion models, motivating "direct collapse" of massive gas clouds or super-Eddington accretion as seed mechanisms. **Primordial black holes** may have formed from early-universe density fluctuations. Post-formation, black holes grow via accretion (powering quasars and X-ray binaries) and mergers. Accretion disks convert gravitational potential to radiation with high efficiency (5.7–42% of rest mass), often launching **relativistic jets** via magnetic processes (Blandford–Znajek) or rotational energy extraction (Penrose process). The **final parsec problem** describes the difficulty of supermassive binaries merging due to stellar depletion. Hawking radiation causes evaporation, but stellar-mass and larger holes currently gain mass from the cosmic microwave background (2.7 K) faster than they radiate (Hawking temperature ~62 nK for 1 M☉).

### Structure and Geometry
A non-rotating (Schwarzschild) black hole has a spherical event horizon at radius $r_s = 2GM/c^2$. Rotating (Kerr) holes have an oblate horizon, an inner **Cauchy horizon**, and an **ergosphere** where frame-dragging forces co-rotation. The **innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO)** marks the inner edge of thin accretion disks (3 $r_s$ for Schwarzschild; closer for prograde orbits around Kerr holes). The **photon sphere** (1.5 $r_s$ for Schwarzschild) defines the black hole **shadow** imaged by EHT. Inside the horizon, all timelike paths terminate at the **singularity** (a point for Schwarzschild, a ring for Kerr), where curvature diverges and general relativity breaks down. Quantum gravity proposals (string theory fuzzballs, loop quantum gravity) suggest singularities may be resolved.

### Classification
Black holes are categorized by mass/origin:
*   **Primordial / Micro:** Hypothetical, formed early universe; could be tiny (10⁻⁵ g), evaporating quickly via Hawking radiation.
*   **Stellar:** 2–100 M☉ from stellar collapse; observed in X-ray binaries and gravitational waves.
*   **Intermediate:** 10²–10⁴ M☉; rare, possible cluster merger products.
*   **Supermassive:** >10⁶ M☉; galactic nuclei, power AGN/quasars.
*   **Ultramassive:** >10⁹–10¹⁰ M☉; theoretical upper limit ~5–10×10¹⁰ M☉ set by disk instability.

## Terms
*   **Event Horizon:** The causal boundary of a black hole; events inside cannot affect outside observers. Defined globally, not locally detectable by an infalling observer.
*   **Singularity:** A region where spacetime curvature becomes infinite and geodesics terminate. Point-like (Schwarzschild) or ring-like (Kerr).
*   **Schwarzschild Radius ($r_s$):** The radius of the event horizon for a non-rotating, uncharged black hole: $r_s = 2GM/c^2$.
*   **No-Hair Theorem:** A stationary black hole is fully described by mass, charge, and angular momentum; all other multipole moments are determined by these three.
*   **Hawking Radiation:** Thermal radiation predicted by quantum field theory in curved spacetime; causes black hole evaporation with temperature inversely proportional to mass.
*   **Accretion Disk:** A rotating disk of plasma formed by infalling matter with angular momentum; heats via viscous dissipation, emitting X-rays and powering jets.
*   **ISCO (Innermost Stable Circular Orbit):** The smallest radius at which a massive particle can orbit stably; inside it, matter plunges rapidly.
*   **Ergosphere:** Region outside a rotating black hole’s horizon where frame-dragging forces all observers to co-rotate; energy can be extracted via the Penrose process.
*   **M–Sigma Relation:** Empirical correlation between supermassive black hole mass and the stellar velocity dispersion of its host galaxy bulge.
*   **Gravitational Waves:** Ripples in spacetime from accelerating masses; detected from merging black holes by LIGO/Virgo.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Information Loss Paradox:** Hawking radiation appears thermal and information-free; if the black hole evaporates completely, unitarity (quantum mechanics) is violated. Proposed resolutions include remnants, firewalls, holography (AdS/CFT), or soft hair.
*   **Singularity Resolution:** Does quantum gravity (string theory, loop quantum gravity) replace the singularity with a regular core (fuzzball, Planck star)?
*   **Supermassive Black Hole Seeds:** How did billion-solar-mass quasars form by z ~ 7? Direct collapse, super-Eddington accretion, Population III remnants, or primordial seeds?
*   **Final Parsec Problem:** How do supermassive binaries overcome stellar depletion to merge within a Hubble time? Gas dynamics, triple interactions, or gravitational wave hardening?
*   **Cosmic Censorship:** Does the weak cosmic censorship hypothesis hold—i.e., are naked singularities (extremal or over-extremal Kerr) forbidden in realistic collapse?
*   **Intermediate-Mass Black Holes:** Do they exist as a distinct population? What are their formation channels (runaway collisions, hierarchical mergers)?
*   **Black Hole Thermodynamics Microscopics:** What are the quantum microstates accounting for Bekenstein–Hawking entropy? String theory counts D-brane states; loop quantum gravity counts horizon punctures.
