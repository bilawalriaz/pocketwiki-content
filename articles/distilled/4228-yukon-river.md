# Yukon River

## Overview
The Yukon River is a major watercourse of northwestern North America, flowing from British Columbia through Canada's Yukon territory and Alaska to the Bering Sea. It is the longest river in Alaska and Yukon, historically vital for transportation during the Klondike Gold Rush and for subsistence fisheries. The river faces modern challenges from pollution and declining salmon runs, with ongoing management efforts by tribal and governmental organizations.

## Timeline
- **1867** — United States purchases Alaska; Alaska Commercial Company acquires Russian-American Company assets and builds posts on the Yukon.
- **1896–1903** — Klondike Gold Rush; the river is a principal transportation route.
- **1950s** — Klondike Highway completed, ending regular paddle-wheel riverboat service.
- **1990** — Yukon River Drainage Fisheries Association formed by fishers.
- **March 2001** — U.S. and Canada pass the Yukon River Salmon Agreement.
- **2008–2009** — Federal Disaster Declaration issued for Commercial Chinook fisheries; complete closure of commercial fishing.
- **2010** — Alaska Department of Fish & Game issues first-ever net mesh size restriction (7.5 inches).
- **2021** — Alaska Department of Fish & Game bans all fishing of Chinook and chum salmon, including for subsistence.
- **Summer 2023** — U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service counts 58,500 Chinook salmon, the second lowest season on record.

## Course and Geography
The river's exact source is debated, but the generally accepted source is the Llewellyn Glacier at Atlin Lake in British Columbia. The river proper begins at the northern end of Marsh Lake, south of Whitehorse. It flows through major lakes including Laberge and passes through communities like Whitehorse, Dawson City (Yukon), and Eagle, Circle, and Fort Yukon (Alaska). The river empties into the Bering Sea via the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta. Its total drainage area is 854,700 km², with 323,800 km² in Canada. The river's name, "Yukon," is a contraction of the Gwichʼin phrase *chųų gąįį han*, meaning "white water river," referring to glacial runoff.

## Transportation and Infrastructure
The Yukon was a key transportation route during the Gold Rush. Paddle-wheel riverboats operated until the 1950s. Today, only four vehicle bridges cross the river, including the Lewes Bridge and the Yukon River Bridge on the Dalton Highway. A car ferry operates at Dawson City in summer, replaced by an ice bridge in winter. Barge services transport heavy goods to communities along the river and its tributaries.

## Ecology and Fisheries
The river supports one of the world's longest salmon runs, with Chinook, coho, and chum salmon migrating thousands of miles. Chinook salmon, arriving in early June, have the longest journey (up to 2,000 miles) and are prized for their fatty meat. Villages rely on salmon for subsistence, culture, and commerce, using methods like gillnets and fish wheels. Salmon recruitment has declined sharply since the late 1980s, with causes debated (climate change, ocean food supply, bycatch). Management includes the Yukon River Panel (established by the 2001 Agreement) and tribal organizations like the Association of Village Council Presidents.

## Pollution and Water Quality
The river has a recent history of pollution from military installations and other sources. However, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency does not list it as impaired, and U.S. Geological Survey data shows relatively good turbidity, metals, and dissolved oxygen levels. The Yukon River Inter-Tribal Watershed Council, a cooperative of 70 First Nations and tribes, works to make the river safe to drink again.

## Terms
- **Drainage area**: The total land area from which water drains into a river system.
- **Subsistence fishing**: Fishing for direct consumption by local communities, not for commercial sale.
- **Chinook salmon**: The largest Pacific salmon species, known for long migrations and fatty meat.
- **Gillnet**: A fishing net designed to catch fish by their gills as they swim through.
- **Turbidity**: A measure of water clarity; high turbidity indicates cloudy water from suspended particles.
- **Yukon River Panel**: An international body advising on salmon management, created by the 2001 U.S.-Canada agreement.
- **Klondike Gold Rush**: A migration of prospectors to the Yukon region (1896–1903) seeking gold.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact source of the Yukon River is debated, with candidates including Llewellyn Glacier, Lake Lindeman, or Teslin Lake.
- The root cause of declining salmon runs is debated, with questions about climate change effects, fishing methods, and impacts from the Bering Sea Pollock trawl fleet.
- The literal meaning of the name "Yukon" was historically debated among Dene nations, with evidence suggesting a Gwich'in origin meaning "white water river."
