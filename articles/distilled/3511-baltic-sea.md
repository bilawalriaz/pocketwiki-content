# Baltic Sea

## Overview
The Baltic Sea is a large, shallow, brackish (low-salinity) arm of the Atlantic Ocean, enclosed by nine countries and the North and Central European Plain. It is the world's largest brackish water basin, formed by glacial erosion, and its unique ecology and strategic location have made it a vital trade route and a historical arena for conflict. Today, it is a critical maritime space for energy, infrastructure, and security, surrounded almost entirely by NATO and EU members.

## Timeline
- **c. 130,000–115,000 BP** — The Eemian Sea, a precursor to the modern Baltic, exists.
- **16,000–11,700 years BP** — The Baltic Ice Lake forms after the last glaciation.
- **11th century** — Adam of Bremen first names it the "Baltic Sea" (Mare Balticum).
- **13th–16th centuries** — The Hanseatic League dominates Baltic trade.
- **16th–17th centuries** — Wars for *Dominium maris baltici* ("Lordship over the Baltic Sea") are fought between Poland, Denmark, and Sweden.
- **17th century** — Sweden achieves near-total control of the sea.
- **1720** — Russia becomes the dominant power after the Great Northern War.
- **1974** — The first Helsinki Convention on protecting the Baltic marine environment is signed.
- **1987** — The Baltic Sea freezes over entirely for the last recorded time.
- **1992** — A new, comprehensive Helsinki Convention is signed by all coastal states and the EU.
- **2022** — The Nord Stream pipelines are sabotaged.
- **2023–2024** — Finland and Sweden join NATO, making the sea almost entirely surrounded by alliance members.

## Body

### Geography and Hydrography
The Baltic Sea is a shelf sea and inland sea, about 1,600 km long with an average depth of 55 m and a maximum depth of 459 m. It drains into the North Sea via the Danish Straits (Øresund, Great Belt, Little Belt). Its hydrology is defined by a strong vertical stratification (halocline): a low-salinity surface layer (0.3–0.9%) flows out, while a denser, saltier bottom layer flows in. This stratification limits oxygen exchange, creating large anoxic (oxygen-depleted) dead zones. The sea's salinity decreases from the southwest to the northeast, with the Gulf of Bothnia being nearly fresh.

### Etymology and History
The name's origin is debated, possibly from the Germanic word *belt* (strait) or a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "white." Historically known as the Suebic or Sarmatian Sea, it became a major trade route during the Viking Age and later the Hanseatic League. Control was contested for centuries, culminating in Swedish dominance in the 17th century, followed by Russian and Prussian influence. The 20th century saw it as a Cold War frontier; today, its geopolitical landscape has shifted dramatically with the NATO accession of Finland and Sweden.

### Ecology and Environment
The Baltic's brackish water supports a mix of marine and freshwater species, with biodiversity decreasing from the saltier southwest to the fresher northeast. It is home to glacial relict species and critically endangered populations like the Baltic ringed seal. Major environmental threats include eutrophication (nutrient pollution causing algal blooms), anoxic dead zones, and contamination from dumped chemical weapons and ammunition from World War II. Climate change poses future risks, including potential shifts from a carbon sink to a source.

### Modern Infrastructure and Security
The Baltic Sea is a vital corridor for energy (oil, gas, wind power) and data via submarine cables and pipelines. Since 2022, a series of sabotage incidents (Nord Stream, Balticconnector, data cables) have highlighted the vulnerability of this critical maritime infrastructure (CMI). In response, NATO has increased its presence with operations like Baltic Sentry, and the EU has enhanced maritime security strategies. The region is now a focal point for countering hybrid warfare threats that operate below the threshold of open conflict.

## Terms
- **Brackish water**: Water with a salinity level between fresh water and seawater, typical of the Baltic Sea.
- **Halocline**: A vertical gradient in salinity; in the Baltic, it separates the fresher surface water from saltier deep water.
- **Anoxic zone**: An area of water or sediment depleted of dissolved oxygen, often caused by stratification and organic pollution.
- **Eutrophication**: Excessive nutrient enrichment of water, often from agricultural runoff, leading to algal blooms and oxygen depletion.
- **Major Baltic Inflow (MBI)**: An infrequent, large-scale inflow of dense, salty North Sea water that ventilates the Baltic's deep basins.
- **Post-glacial rebound**: The rise of land masses after the removal of the weight of ice sheets, still occurring in the northern Baltic at up to 8 mm per year.
- **Critical Maritime Infrastructure (CMI)**: Essential underwater assets like pipelines, cables, and wind farms vital for economic and security functions.
- **Hybrid warfare**: Covert or ambiguous actions (e.g., sabotage, disinformation) that fall below the threshold of conventional war.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise geographical and administrative boundary of the "Baltic Sea" is debated, with different definitions used by hydrographers, legal conventions, and biologists, affecting assessments of its ecological health.
- The origin of the name "Baltic" is uncertain, with multiple competing etymological theories.
- The future impact of climate change on the Baltic's unique brackish ecosystem, ice cover, and stratification is a subject of ongoing scientific modeling and concern.
- The effectiveness and legal frameworks for protecting critical undersea infrastructure against hybrid threats remain a developing challenge for NATO and the EU.
