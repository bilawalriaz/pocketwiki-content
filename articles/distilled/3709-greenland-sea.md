# Greenland Sea

## Overview
The Greenland Sea is a marginal sea of the Atlantic Ocean, bordered by Greenland, Svalbard, and Iceland. It is a critical component of the global climate system, as its cold, dense waters drive the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), a major ocean current system that regulates global climate. The sea is characterized by its harsh Arctic environment, significant ice formation, and a history of resource exploitation, from whaling to potential hydrocarbon reserves.

## Timeline
- **c. 15th century** — Inuit begin non-industrial whale hunting in the area.
- **1600s–1911** — The Greenland Sea is a major whaling ground, leading to severe depletion of whale populations.
- **1765–1766** — First scientific explorations conducted by Russian expeditions under Admiral V. Chichagov.
- **1876–1878** — Scientific investigations carried out as part of the Norwegian North-Atlantic Expedition.
- **1909** — Fridtjof Nansen describes the complex water current system.
- **1921** — Norway opens the first meteorological station on Jan Mayen island.
- **1952** — A major storm results in the loss of ships carrying 79 Norwegian seal hunters in the West Ice.
- **Late 1990s** — Scientists report an increase in the local bowhead whale population.
- **2013** — Three consortia obtain hydrocarbon extraction rights to four areas of the Greenland Sea.
- **2017** — First complete man-powered crossing of the Greenland Sea achieved by the Polar Row expedition.
- **2021** — The Greenland government stops administering licenses for oil exploration.

## Body

### Geography and Extent
The Greenland Sea is bounded by Greenland to the west, the Svalbard archipelago to the east, and Iceland to the south. Its official limits, defined by the International Hydrographic Organization, are marked by lines connecting specific coastal points. The sea floor is a deep depression, with the Molloy Deep in the connecting Fram Strait reaching 5,570 m (18,270 ft). The southern part, south of the Jan Mayen Fracture Zone, is sometimes called the Iceland Sea.

### Hydrology, Climate, and Ice
The sea has an Arctic climate with temperatures rarely above 0 °C (32 °F). Its hydrology is dominated by two major currents: the cold East Greenland Current flowing south along the west, and the warm Spitsbergen Current flowing north along the east. This creates a counter-clockwise water flow. The sea is a key site for deep water formation; cold, salty water sinks here, driving the global thermohaline circulation. Historically, the Odden ice tongue was a major winter ice formation area that facilitated this deep convection, but it has rarely developed since the 1990s. The West Ice, a large winter ice area north of Iceland, is a critical breeding ground for seals.

### Fauna and Human Use
The Greenland Sea supports a food chain from small invertebrates to large mammals like seals and whales. Commercial whaling from the 1600s until 1911 decimated whale populations, including the once-abundant bowhead whale. Signs of recovery for bowhead whales have been observed since the late 1990s. The West Ice has been used for seal hunting for over 200 years. The sea's potential hydrocarbon resources have attracted exploration interest, with several oil companies obtaining concessions in 2013, though Greenland halted new oil exploration licenses in 2021.

### Global Climate Interactions
Water from the Atlantic flows through the Greenland Sea into the Arctic Ocean via the AMOC. Eddies in the sea redistribute heat between the Atlantic and Arctic. Changes in the Greenland Sea, such as reduced sea ice due to warming, have been linked to broader climate impacts, including altered precipitation patterns in India, Korea, and Russia.

## Terms
- **Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC):** A large system of ocean currents that transports warm water from the tropics northward in the Atlantic, playing a key role in global climate regulation.
- **Thermohaline circulation:** The global density-driven circulation of the oceans, often called the "global conveyor belt," which is driven by differences in water temperature (thermo) and salinity (haline).
- **Cabbeling:** The process where two water masses of similar density but different temperature and salinity mix to form a new, denser water mass that sinks.
- **Frazil ice:** A collection of loose, randomly oriented needle-shaped ice crystals in water.
- **Pancake ice:** Circular pieces of ice with raised rims, formed by the collision of small pieces of ice in rough seas.
- **Odden ice tongue:** A former large, tongue-shaped area of winter ice formation in the Greenland Sea that was crucial for deep water formation.
- **West Ice:** A large, continuous area of winter sea ice north of Iceland between Greenland and Jan Mayen, important as a seal breeding ground.
- **East Greenland Current:** A cold ocean current that flows southward along the eastern coast of Greenland, transporting sea ice and cold water from the Arctic Ocean.
- **Spitsbergen Current:** A warm ocean current that flows northward along the western side of the Svalbard archipelago, an extension of the North Atlantic Current.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise definition and classification of the Greenland Sea (as part of the Arctic or Atlantic Ocean) is noted as imprecise or arbitrary.
- The future impact of changes in water flow through the Greenland Sea on global climate patterns is unclear.
- The potential for recovery of whale populations, particularly the bowhead whale, after historical depletion is an ongoing area of study.
