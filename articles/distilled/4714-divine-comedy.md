# Divine Comedy

## Overview
The *Divine Comedy* is an Italian narrative poem by Dante Alighieri, written c. 1308–1321. It is a foundational work of Italian and Western literature, depicting the author's allegorical journey through Hell, Purgatory, and Heaven. The poem synthesizes medieval Catholic theology, classical philosophy, and contemporary politics, and was instrumental in establishing the Tuscan dialect as the standard Italian language.

## Timeline
- **c. 1308** — Dante begins writing the *Comedy*.
- **1302** — Dante is exiled from Florence as a White Guelph.
- **c. 1321** — Dante completes the poem shortly before his death.
- **1351–1355** — Giovanni Boccaccio's work first uses the adjective "Divina" for the poem.
- **1416** — First complete translation (into Latin prose) is made by Giovanni da Serravalle.
- **1428** — First translation into another vernacular (Castilian) is completed.
- **1472** — First printed edition is published in Foligno, Italy.
- **1555** — Lodovico Dolce's edition is the first to use the title *Divina Comedia*.

## Body

### Structure and Story
The poem consists of 14,233 lines divided into three *cantiche* (singular *cantica*): *Inferno*, *Purgatorio*, and *Paradiso*. Each *cantica* has 33 cantos, with an introductory canto bringing the total to 100. It is written in *terza rima*, a hendecasyllabic (eleven-syllable) verse scheme with an interlocking ABA BCB CDC rhyme pattern. The narrative follows Dante's journey through the afterlife from Good Friday to the Wednesday after Easter in 1300, guided by Virgil (human reason), Beatrice (divine revelation), and finally Saint Bernard of Clairvaux (contemplative mysticism). The structure of each realm follows a numerical pattern of nine divisions plus one, totaling ten.

### Inferno
The journey begins in a dark wood, symbolizing sin. Virgil guides Dante through Hell, which is structured into nine circles based on Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*. Sins are punished by *contrapasso*, a symbolic poetic justice. The circles are divided into three main categories: sins of incontinence (Upper Hell), violence (Circle 7), and fraud and treachery (Circles 8 and 9). Two special categories are Limbo (for virtuous pagans) and the heretics. Allegorically, this section represents the soul's recognition and rejection of sin.

### Purgatorio
Dante and Virgil ascend the Mountain of Purgatory, created by Satan's fall. It has seven terraces for cleansing the seven deadly sins, classified by motive (love perverted, deficient, or malicious) rather than action. Below these terraces is Ante-Purgatory for the late repentant and excommunicated. The summit is the Garden of Eden. This section demonstrates medieval knowledge of a spherical Earth, discussing different stars, time zones, and the sun's position. Allegorically, it represents the penitent Christian life.

### Paradiso
Guided by Beatrice, Dante ascends through nine concentric celestial spheres based on Ptolemaic cosmology. The seven lowest spheres correspond to the cardinal virtues, with the first three showing deficiencies and the last four positive examples. The eighth sphere contains the Church Triumphant (those with the theological virtues), and the ninth, the Primum Mobile, contains angels. The final realm is the Empyrean, the essence of God. The poem concludes with Dante's vision of the Triune God. This section is more theological, drawing heavily on Scholastic philosophy.

### Influences and Themes
The poem is a synthesis of classical, Christian, and Islamic thought. Dante's primary classical models are Virgil, Ovid, Lucan, and Statius, with Aristotle as the philosophical foundation. The language and theology are deeply indebted to the Vulgate Bible and Scholasticism, especially the work of Thomas Aquinas. The poem also shows possible influences from Islamic philosophy, particularly Averroes, and debated parallels to works like the *Kitab al Miraj*. Thematically, it is a complex allegory with literal, moral, allegorical, and anagogical (spiritual) levels of meaning. It incorporates contemporary science, discussing astronomy, optics, and mechanics.

## Terms
- **Cantica/Canti**: The three main books (*Inferno*, *Purgatorio*, *Paradiso*) and their subdivisions.
- **Terza Rima**: The interlocking three-line rhyme scheme (ABA BCB...) used throughout the poem.
- **Contrapasso**: The symbolic punishment in Hell that mirrors or inverts the sin committed.
- **Allegory**: A narrative with a hidden moral or spiritual meaning; the poem operates on literal, moral, allegorical, and anagogical levels.
- **Scholasticism**: A medieval philosophical system that sought to reconcile Christian theology with Aristotelian reason, exemplified by Thomas Aquinas.
- **Katabasis**: A descent into the underworld, a literary genre to which the *Inferno* belongs.
- **Empyrean**: The highest heaven, the dwelling place of God, beyond the physical spheres.
- **Primum Mobile**: The outermost moving sphere in Ptolemaic cosmology, which imparts motion to the other spheres.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Islamic Influence**: Scholar Miguel Asín Palacios argued Dante derived features of the afterlife from Islamic texts like the *Kitab al Miraj* and the works of Ibn Arabi. This theory is debated; some scholars see parallels but question transmission, while others, like Francesco Gabrieli, express skepticism.
- **Letter to Cangrande**: The authenticity of this letter, in which Dante allegedly explains how to read the poem, is disputed.
- **Original Manuscript**: No original manuscript written by Dante has survived, though hundreds of 14th and 15th-century copies exist.
- **Title's Adjective**: The adjective "Divina" was not part of Dante's original title (*Comedìa*) and was added later, likely by Boccaccio, to denote the poem's profound subject.
