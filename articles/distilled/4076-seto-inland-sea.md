# Seto Inland Sea

## Overview
The Seto Inland Sea is a marginal sea separating Japan's main islands of Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu. Historically, it served as a vital transportation and trade route connecting Japan's regions and linking the country to Korea and China. Today, it is a major industrial, tourist, and transportation corridor, now linked by a series of monumental bridges.

## Timeline
- **c. 10,000 BC** — Sea forms after the last ice age as water fills a basin between mountain ranges.
- **12th century** — Taira no Kiyomori attempts to move the capital to Fukuhara (Kobe) to promote trade.
- **Edo period (1603-1868)** — Sea becomes one of Japan's busiest transport routes, central to the sankin-kōtai system.
- **1868** — Kobe port is founded, opening the sea to international shipping.
- **1934** — Setonaikai National Park is established as one of Japan's first three national parks.
- **1975-1999** — Construction of the Honshu-Shikoku Bridge Project connects the islands by road and rail.

## Body

### Geography and Formation
The Seto Inland Sea is approximately 450 km long and 15-55 km wide, with an average depth of 38 meters. It is not a true inland sea but a marginal sea of the Pacific Ocean, partially enclosed by the Japanese archipelago. It contains nearly 3,000 islands, with Awaji-shima being the largest. The sea connects to the Pacific via the Naruto Strait and Kii Channel, and to the Sea of Japan via the Kanmon Straits.

### Historical Significance
From ancient times, the sea was a primary transport line between the Kansai region, Kyushu, and foreign countries. Its importance fostered powerful private navies, or *suigun*, such as the Kono and Kobayakawa clans. During the Edo period, it was a key part of the coastal shipping route, facilitating the sankin-kōtai system and making Osaka the economic center. After the Meiji Restoration, coastal cities were industrialized, and a naval headquarters was established at Kure.

### Modern Industry and Transport
Major coastal cities include Osaka, Kobe, and Hiroshima. Key industries are steel, shipbuilding, vehicle manufacture, and oil refining. The sea remains crucial for international and domestic cargo transport. The development of land transport, particularly the San'yō and Yosan railway lines, reduced its primacy for east-west travel. The Honshu-Shikoku Bridge Project, completed in the late 20th century, now connects the islands by highway and rail.

### Tourism and Culture
The Seto Inland Sea is a major tourist destination, praised for its beauty since the 19th century. The Setonaikai National Park covers most of its coastline. Key sites include the UNESCO World Heritage Itsukushima Shrine, the Naruto whirlpools, and the art islands of the Setouchi Triennale festival. The region's landscape and history have inspired Japanese literature from the *Man'yōshū* to *The Tale of the Heike*.

## Terms
- **Marginal sea**: A division of a wider ocean partially enclosed by islands or peninsulas, adjacent to the open ocean.
- **Suigun**: Private navies or "water armies" that controlled sea lanes and coastal areas during Japan's feudal period.
- **Sankin-kōtai**: The Edo-period system requiring feudal lords (*daimyō*) to alternate residence in the shogunal capital, Edo.
- **Amphidromous**: A fish that migrates between salt and fresh water, but not necessarily to breed (e.g., the ayu).
- **Phytoplankton**: Microscopic marine plants whose dense blooms can cause red tides, depleting oxygen and killing fish.
- **Koku**: A historical Japanese unit of volume for rice, used to measure a domain's wealth and a samurai's stipend.
