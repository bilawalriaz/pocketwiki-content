# Camera

## Overview
A camera is an instrument that captures and stores images and videos, either digitally via an electronic image sensor or chemically via light-sensitive material such as photographic film. As a pivotal technology in photography and videography, cameras have driven progress in visual arts, media, entertainment, surveillance, and scientific research. The camera's evolution—from 19th-century chemical processes to 21st-century computational photography—reflects broader technological and societal shifts.

## Timeline

- **1888** — Original Kodak camera introduced, promoting smaller, cheaper cameras
- **1952** — Ricohflex III TLR launched, advancing Japanese camera development
- **1954** — Asahiflex II becomes the world's first SLR camera with an instant return mirror
- **1960** — Olympus AutoEye introduced as a 35mm SLR with automatic exposure
- **1970s** — Electronics integrated into camera design (e.g., Polaroid SX-70, Canon AE-1)

- **1986** — Nikon introduces the SVC, an analog-recording electronic single-lens reflex camera
- **2003** — Digital camera sales surpass film cameras in the United States, culminating the late 20th century transition to digital photography
- **2000** — Sharp introduces the first digital camera phone (J-SH04 J-Phone)

## Body

### History
The 19th century began with the camera obscura and chemical experiments by pioneers like Thomas Wedgwood, Nicéphore Niépce, and Henry Fox Talbot, who created dedicated cameras for chemical photography. The 1839 daguerreotype process enabled commercial manufacturing, and by the 1850s, designs were standardized. Dry plates and roll-film in the latter half of the century led to smaller, cheaper cameras like the 1888 Kodak. In the 20th century, Germany led post-WWI development with precision cameras like the Leica and Contax. Japanese manufacturers dominated the second half, introducing electronics in the 1970s. The first digital camera was developed by Kodak engineer Steven Sasson in 1975, using a 0.01-megapixel CCD from Fairchild Semiconductor to capture black-and-white images onto cassette tape. In 1986, Nikon introduced the SVC, an analog-recording electronic SLR. Digital photography emerged in the late 20th century, culminating in digital camera sales surpassing film cameras in the United States by 2003. The 21st century saw mass adoption of digital cameras and smartphones, along with computational photography using AI and algorithms.

### Mechanics
All cameras use a basic design: light enters through a converging lens, is controlled by a shutter, and is recorded on a light-sensitive medium. Exposure control involves aperture and shutter speed. The aperture, adjusted via f-stops (typically f/1.4 to f/32), regulates light entry; smaller f-stops allow more light and create shallow depth of field. The shutter controls exposure duration, with mechanical types including leaf and focal-plane shutters. Light meters measure scene brightness, often averaging to 18% middle gray, with advanced metering modes like center-weighted and matrix metering.

### Lens
A camera lens focuses light onto the sensor or film using multiple optical elements to reduce aberrations like chromatic aberration and vignetting. Focal length (in mm) determines field of view: wide-angle lenses capture broad scenes, telephoto lenses magnify narrow views. Zoom lenses offer variable focal lengths, while prime lenses have fixed focal lengths and often superior quality. Focus is adjusted manually or via autofocus systems using contrast or phase detection. Image stabilization compensates for camera shake.

### Viewfinder
The viewfinder shows the scene to be captured, aiding composition and focus. Optical viewfinders (in SLRs) use mirrors or prisms, while electronic viewfinders (in mirrorless cameras) display digital previews. Parallax error occurs when the viewfinder and lens axes misalign, more noticeable at close distances.

### Film and Sensor
Image capture occurs when light strikes film or a digital sensor. Film cameras require manual loading and advancing to prevent double exposure. Digital cameras use CCD or CMOS sensors to convert light into electrical signals. CCDs offer high image quality but consume more power; CMOS sensors are more efficient with faster frame rates. Digital images are stored on media like SD cards and can be reviewed immediately on built-in monitors.

### Camera Accessories
Accessories include flashes (using gas-filled tubes for brief, bright light), lens hoods (blocking stray light), filters (altering light density or color), tripods (stabilizing the camera), and lens caps (protecting the lens). Specialized tools like cable releases prevent camera shake during long exposures.

### Primary Types
Single-lens reflex (SLR) cameras use a mirror to direct light to the viewfinder, avoiding parallax error. The Asahiflex II (1954) was the world's first SLR with an instant return mirror. Large-format cameras use sheet film for high-resolution, technical photography. Medium-format cameras use 120/220 roll film for higher resolution than 35mm. Twin-lens reflex (TLR) cameras use paired lenses for viewing and taking. Compact cameras prioritize portability. Rangefinder cameras use separate viewing systems for precise focusing. Motion picture cameras capture sequential frames at rates like 24 fps to create the illusion of motion.

## Terms
- **Aperture**: Opening controlling light entry, measured in f-stops
- **Shutter speed**: Duration the shutter remains open, controlling exposure time
- **F-stop**: Increment system for aperture size; smaller numbers mean larger openings
- **Depth of field**: Range of distance within which objects appear acceptably sharp
- **Parallax error**: Misalignment between viewfinder and lens axes causing inaccurate framing
- **Focal length**: Lens measurement (mm) determining field of view and magnification
- **CCD/CMOS**: Sensor types converting light into electrical signals
- **SLR**: Single-lens reflex camera using mirrors for through-the-lens viewing
- **Exposure value (EV)**: Combined measure of aperture and shutter speed determining total light exposure
- **Computational photography**: Use of algorithms and AI to enhance digital image quality
