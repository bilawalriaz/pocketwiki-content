# Philipp Melanchthon

## Overview
Philipp Melanchthon (1497–1560) was a German Lutheran reformer and the first systematic theologian of the Protestant Reformation. As Martin Luther's primary collaborator, he systematized Lutheran doctrine, authored the foundational Augsburg Confession, and shaped Protestant educational systems, making him an intellectual leader who, alongside Luther and Calvin, defined early Protestantism.

## Timeline
- **1497** — Born Philipp Schwartzerdt in Bretten; surname later changed to Melanchthon.
- **1508** — Father and grandfather die; he moves to Pforzheim with his grandmother.
- **1518** — Becomes professor of Greek at the University of Wittenberg at age 21.
- **1521** — Publishes *Loci communes*, the first systematic Lutheran theology.
- **1520** — Marries Katharina Krapp; they have four children.
- **1530** — Authors the Augsburg Confession, presented at the Diet of Augsburg.
- **1532** — Publishes *Commentarii in Epistolam Pauli ad Romanos*, refining justification doctrine.
- **1535** — Second edition of *Loci* introduces Synergism, rejecting strict determinism.
- **1540** — Produces the *Variata* edition of the Augsburg Confession, later signed by Calvin.
- **1547** — Controversies arise over the Interim and the doctrine of Adiaphora.
- **1557** — Attends the Colloquy of Worms, facing accusations of heresy.
- **1560** — Dies in Wittenberg; buried beside Luther.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born in 1497, Melanchthon was introduced to classical learning at the Latin school in Pforzheim. His great-uncle, the humanist Johann Reuchlin, suggested he change his surname from the German "Schwartzerdt" (black earth) to the Greek equivalent "Melanchthon." After his father's death in 1508, he studied at the universities of Heidelberg and Tübingen, earning degrees in philosophy and theology. Influenced by Reuchlin and Erasmus, he rejected scholastic theology. In 1518, he accepted a call to teach Greek at Wittenberg, where he began collaborating with Martin Luther.

### Professor at Wittenberg and Early Reformation Work
At Wittenberg, Melanchthon studied Scripture, particularly Paul's epistles, and became a key reformer. He defended Luther after the 1519 Leipzig disputation and, in 1521, published *Loci communes*, which systematized Lutheran doctrine by emphasizing faith over good deeds. This work became foundational for Lutheran scholasticism. He also helped draft church and school regulations in 1528 and accompanied the elector to the Diet of Speyer in 1529, though hopes for imperial recognition of the Reformation were unmet.

### The Augsburg Confession and Later Theology
Melanchthon was the primary author of the 1530 Augsburg Confession, a defining Protestant document presented at the Diet of Augsburg. Though based on Luther's articles, its irenic tone sometimes displeased Luther. Melanchthon later wrote the *Apology* in response to Catholic rebuttal. His 1532 commentary on Romans introduced the forensic view of justification as "being accounted just." In 1540, he revised the Confession into the *Variata*, which included a different treatment of the Lord's Supper and was signed by John Calvin.

### Controversies and Later Years
Melanchthon's later career was marked by theological disputes. He initially sought compromise on the Lord's Supper with Martin Bucer but upheld the real presence against Zwinglian views. He faced criticism for teaching that works are necessary for salvation and for his doctrine of Synergism, which ascribed a role to human will in conversion. In the 1540s, he controversially accepted some Catholic practices as *adiaphora* (matters indifferent to salvation) in the Leipzig Interim, a concession he later regretted. After Luther's death in 1546, he became the theological leader of the Reformation but was attacked by Gnesio-Lutherans as a heretic. He died in 1560, embittered by ongoing disputes.

### Scholar, Educator, and Moral Philosopher
Melanchthon was a central figure in Christian humanism. He reformed educational curricula, advocating for classical learning and expanding the traditional liberal arts to include history, geography, and natural sciences. His philosophical works, based on Aristotle and Cicero, influenced German schools for over a century. In ethics, he argued that moral principles are knowable through reason but that revealed law is necessary due to sin. His hermeneutics emphasized the literal sense of Scripture, and his commentaries were widely used.

### Relationship with Luther and Personal Character
Melanchthon and Luther complemented each other: Luther was the fiery pioneer, while Melanchthon was the scholarly systematizer. Though their relationship had strains, especially over Melanchthon's conciliatory approach, Luther praised his work. Melanchthon was described as physically weak but intellectually brilliant, with a peaceful, scholarly temperament. He valued family life, hospitality, and prayer, and maintained extensive correspondence.

## Terms
- **Augsburg Confession (1530):** The primary doctrinal statement of Lutheranism, presented at the Diet of Augsburg, largely authored by Melanchthon.
- **Loci communes (1521):** Melanchthon's first systematic theology, outlining core Lutheran doctrines from Paul's epistles.
- **Synergism:** The doctrine, taught by Melanchthon, that human free will cooperates with divine grace in conversion.
- **Adiaphora:** Matters considered theologically indifferent, which Melanchthon controversially allowed in the Leipzig Interim.
- **Variata (1540):** A revised edition of the Augsburg Confession with a different phrasing on the Lord's Supper, later signed by Calvin.
- **Forensic justification:** The view that justification is God's declaration of righteousness, not an internal change, emphasized by Melanchthon.
- **Christian humanism:** A movement blending classical learning with Christian faith, which Melanchthon championed in education.
- **Gnesio-Lutherans:** "Genuine Lutherans" who opposed Melanchthon's compromises and accused him of heresy.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Melanchthon's concessions:** Scholars debate whether his acceptance of *adiaphora* in the Interim was a pragmatic compromise or a betrayal of principle.
- **Synergism vs. Luther's determinism:** His shift to teaching human cooperation in salvation created lasting tension within Lutheranism.
- **Theological legacy:** Some view him as a faithful systematizer of Luther, while others see his humanistic and irenic tendencies as a departure from core Lutheran thought.
- **Authorship of the Augsburg Confession:** While primarily his work, the extent of Luther's influence and approval remains discussed.
