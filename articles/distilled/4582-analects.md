# Analects

## Overview
The *Analects* (*Lunyu*) is a foundational Chinese philosophical text compiling the sayings and ideas of Confucius (551–479 BC) and his contemporaries. It is the most reliable source for Confucius's thought and has been central to East Asian intellectual and cultural life for over two millennia, shaping ethics, politics, and education.

## Timeline
- **c. 479 BC** — Death of Confucius; disciples begin collecting his sayings.
- **475–221 BC** — Warring States period; large portions of the *Analects* are composed.
- **206 BC–220 AD** — Han dynasty; the text achieves its final form.
- **c. 153–128 BC** — "Old Text" version of the *Analects* discovered in a wall.
- **c. 50 BC** — Oldest extant manuscripts (Dingzhou and Pyongyang Analects) are written.
- **c. 175 AD** — "Stone Classics of the Xinping Era" inscribed, including the *Analects*.
- **c. 248 AD** — He Yan's influential commentary, *Collected Explanations*, is published.
- **960–1279 AD** — Song dynasty; the *Analects* is elevated above the Five Classics as one of the "Four Books."
- **1130–1200** — Zhu Xi writes his definitive commentary, *Collected Commentaries*.
- **2011** — Tomb of the Marquis of Haihun yields the oldest extant manuscript (pre-50 BC).
- **2010s** — Warring States manuscripts (c. 300 BC) confirm early preservation of Confucius's sayings.

## Body

### Creation and Textual History
The traditional account, recorded by Ban Gu, holds that the *Analects* was compiled by Confucius's disciples from their records of his conversations. Modern scholarship, based on linguistic analysis and manuscript discoveries, indicates the text was compiled over about 200 years during the Warring States period. Manuscripts from c. 300 BC confirm the tradition existed by the mid-Warring States period. By the early Han dynasty, the text was widely known in a mostly complete form.

During the Han, three versions existed: the "Lu" (20 chapters), "Qi" (22 chapters), and an "Old Text" version (21 chapters). The received text is primarily based on the Lu version, synthesized with the Qi version by the Han scholar Zhang Yu. The oldest complete commentary is He Yan's *Collected Explanations* (c. 248 AD), which blended Confucian and Daoist interpretations. The most influential commentary is Zhu Xi's *Collected Commentaries* (12th century), which established the *Analects* as one of the "Four Books" and became the standard for imperial examinations until 1905.

### Core Philosophy: Ren and Li
Confucius's social philosophy centers on cultivating *ren* (仁), an all-encompassing virtue often translated as "benevolence" or "humaneness." *Ren* is achieved through modesty, courage, and the practice of the inverted Golden Rule: "Do not do to others what you would not like done to yourself." The foundation for cultivating *ren* is filial piety—devotion to parents and siblings.

*Ren* is practiced broadly, while *li* (禮), meaning rituals and propriety, governs specific social relationships. *Li* is not about suppressing desires but reconciling them with social responsibility. The five key relationships (ruler-subject, father-son, husband-wife, elder-younger brother, friend-friend) structure society. A person of *ren* and *li* understands their role and acts with integrity.

### Political and Educational Thought
Confucius believed a state's welfare depends on the moral cultivation of its people, starting with its leaders. A ruler must govern by virtuous example (*de*, 德) and education, not punishment. The "rectification of names" is crucial: officials must behave in accordance with their titles. A ruler who lacks virtue forfeits the right to rule.

Education aims to produce ethically cultivated individuals who are grave, articulate, and sincere. Confucius taught through questioning and analogy, emphasizing balance between study and self-reflection. He accepted students of any social class who were sincere and eager to learn.

## Terms
- **Analects (Lunyu)**: "Edited conversations" or "selected speeches"; the collection of Confucius's sayings.
- **Ren (仁)**: All-encompassing virtue; "benevolence" or "humaneness."
- **Li (禮)**: Rituals, propriety, and social norms that structure relationships.
- **De (德)**: Moral virtue or force that enables legitimate rule.
- **Filial Piety**: Devotion and respect for one's parents and older siblings.
- **Rectification of Names**: Ensuring social roles and titles correspond to actual behavior.
- **Five Classics**: The canonical texts Confucius was believed to have transmitted.
- **Four Books**: The *Analects*, *Mencius*, *Great Learning*, and *Doctrine of the Mean*, promoted by Zhu Xi.
- **Ziyue**: "The Master said"; formulaic introduction to many passages.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authorship and Compilation**: Scholars debate the timeline and layers of composition, with theories ranging from early disciples to later Warring States editors. The "four stratum theory" and linguistic analysis suggest multiple stages.
- **Textual Integrity**: The relationship between the received text and the lost Lu, Qi, and Old Text versions is uncertain. The fragmentary final chapter (Yao Yue) may be an accretion or contain transmission errors.
- **Authenticity of Sayings**: Some scholars question the authenticity of certain sayings, noting that 75% of Confucius's quotes in the *Mencius* are absent from the *Analects*.
- **Interpretation**: The meaning of key terms like *ren* is debated. He Yan's commentary fused Confucianism with Daoism, while Zhu Xi's aimed for a unified Confucian orthodoxy, showing how interpretation has shifted with intellectual trends.
