# Middle East

## Overview
The Middle East is a geopolitical region spanning West Asia, Egypt, and European Turkey, defined by political borders rather than continental boundaries. It is the cradle of civilization, where writing, agriculture, and the Abrahamic religions (Judaism, Christianity, Islam) originated. Historically a crossroads of empires—from Assyria and Persia to the Ottomans—it was reshaped after World War I into modern nation-states under European mandates. Today, its vast petroleum reserves (concentrated in Gulf Arab states) grant it immense strategic and economic importance, though the region faces acute vulnerability to climate change, water scarcity, and high youth unemployment.

## Timeline
- **c. 10,000 BC** — Neolithic Revolution; independent discovery of agriculture in the Fertile Crescent and Nile Valley.
- **c. 3500–3000 BC** — Rise of earliest civilizations: Sumer (Mesopotamia), ancient Egypt, and Kish (Levant); development of writing systems.
- **7th century AD** — Islamic conquest unifies the region under the Rashidun Caliphate, establishing dominant Arab-Islamic identity.
- **1516–1517** — Ottoman Empire conquers the Levant, Egypt, and Hejaz; begins centuries of Ottoman–Safavid rivalry.
- **1918** — Collapse of the Ottoman Empire after WWI; region partitioned into British and French Mandates.
- **1945** — Mass oil production begins; Saudi Arabia, Iran, Kuwait, Iraq, and UAE emerge as major producers.
- **1948** — Establishment of Israel; departure of European powers accelerates through the 1960s.
- **1957** — First official U.S. government use of "Middle East" in the Eisenhower Doctrine.
- **1979** — Iranian Revolution triggers migration of religious minorities; shifts regional geopolitics.
- **2000s** — "Greater Middle East" concept coined by Bush administration; region hosts major global sporting events to diversify economies.

## Body

### Terminology and Definitions
The term "Middle East" likely originated in the 1850s with the British India Office but was popularized by U.S. naval strategist Alfred Thayer Mahan in 1902 to designate the area between Arabia and India, centered on the Persian Gulf. British diplomat Valentine Chirol expanded it to include "regions of Asia which extend to the borders of India." Before WWI, "Near East" referred to the Balkans and Ottoman lands, while "Middle East" meant the Caucasus, Persia, and Arabia. The Ottoman collapse in 1918 retired "Near East" from general English usage (retained only in archaeology), transferring the label to the emerging Islamic world. The U.S. government first used it officially in the 1957 Eisenhower Doctrine, defining it from Libya to Pakistan. Critics (e.g., Louay Khraish, Hassan Hanafi) argue the term is Eurocentric and colonialist. Translations vary: German uses *Naher Osten* (Near East); Slavic languages use "Near East" equivalents; Romance languages use "Middle East" equivalents (French *Moyen-Orient*, Spanish *Oriente Medio*). The Arabic *ash-Sharq al-Awsaṭ* is now standard in Arab press.

### Countries and Territories
The common definition includes 17 UN-recognized countries and one British Overseas Territory (Akrotiri and Dhekelia), covering Arabia, Asia Minor, East Thrace, Egypt, Iran, the Levant, Mesopotamia, and the Socotra Archipelago. Thirteen of 18 countries are part of the Arab world. The three most populous are Egypt, Iran, and Turkey; Saudi Arabia is largest by area. "Greater Middle East" (Bush administration, 2000s) extends the concept to the Muslim world, adding Afghanistan, Pakistan, and Central Asian states. The Maghreb (North Africa) is sometimes included due to Arabic language.

### Historical Trajectory
**Cradles of Civilization:** The region was the first settled by modern humans outside Africa. Agriculture arose independently in the Fertile Crescent and Nile Valley (Neolithic), spreading to Europe, the Indus Valley, and Eastern Africa. The earliest civilizations—Sumer, Akkad, Assyria, Babylonia, Egypt, and Kish—emerged here, followed by Hittite, Elamite, Persian, and Levantine (Phoenicia, Israel) cultures.
**Imperial Unification:** The Neo-Assyrian Empire first unified the Near East, succeeded by the Achaemenid, Macedonian, Parthian, Sasanian, Roman, and Byzantine Empires. The region was the Roman Empire’s intellectual/economic center, garrisoned with 5–6 legions to defend against Sasanian and Bedouin raids.
**Islamic Golden Age (7th century onward):** The Islamic conquest unified the entire Middle East as a distinct region for the first time, creating the dominant Arab-Islamic identity. Four caliphates (Rashidun, Umayyad, Abbasid, Fatimid) ruled for 600+ years. Population of "Islamic territory" estimated at 12.5 million in 1000 CE (Anatolia 8M, Syria 2M, Egypt 1.5M). Later powers included Mongols, Seljuks (spreading Turko-Persian culture), Crusader states (c. 200 years), and from the 16th century, the Ottoman Empire and Safavid Iran.
**Modern Era:** Post-WWI Ottoman partition created modern states under British/French mandates. Israel’s establishment (1948) and European withdrawal (1960s) defined the contemporary map, with U.S. influence rising from the 1970s. Oil discovery (mass production c. 1945) granted new strategic centrality; the region holds ~2/3 of world reserves. The Cold War made it a theater of US/NATO vs. Soviet/Warsaw Pact competition for oil access and ideological alignment. Sectarian tension (Sunni/Shia) persists alongside periods of tolerance.

### Geography and Climate
Core sub-regions: Arabian Peninsula, Anatolia, Levant, Mesopotamia (together the Fertile Crescent), and Iranian Plateau. Climate is predominantly hot/arid (Arabian/Egyptian deserts). Key rivers enable agriculture: Nile (Egypt), Tigris/Euphrates (Mesopotamia), Jordan (Levant). The Levantine coast and most of Turkey enjoy a temperate Mediterranean climate (dry summers, cool/wet winters). Gulf Arab states hold vast petroleum reserves. Aridity and fossil-fuel dependence make the region among the most vulnerable to climate change and water scarcity.

### Economy
Economies range from very poor (Gaza, Yemen) to extremely wealthy (Qatar, UAE). **2023 Nominal GDP leaders:** Saudi Arabia ($1.06T), Turkey ($1.03T), Israel ($0.54T). **Nominal GDP per capita leaders:** Qatar ($83,891), Israel ($55,535), UAE ($49,451), Cyprus ($33,807). **GDP PPP leaders:** Turkey ($3.6T), Saudi Arabia ($2.3T), Iran ($1.7T). **GDP PPP per capita leaders:** Qatar ($124,834), UAE ($88,221), Saudi Arabia ($64,836), Bahrain ($60,596), Israel ($54,997). Yemen is lowest nominal per capita ($573).
**Structure:** Some states rely heavily on oil exports (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Kuwait); others are diversified (Cyprus, Israel, Turkey, Egypt). Industries: oil, agriculture (cotton, cattle, dairy), textiles, leather, surgical instruments, defense equipment. Banking is key for UAE and Bahrain. Tourism is underdeveloped (except Cyprus, Turkey, Egypt, Lebanon, Israel) due to conservatism and turmoil, but UAE, Bahrain, and Jordan are expanding post-COVID. **Unemployment:** Regional rate 10.8% (2025); youth (15–29, 30% of population) unemployment ~28%.

### Demographics
**Ethnic Groups:** Arabs (largest), then Iranian peoples (Persians, Kurds, Lurs, Baloch), then Turkic peoples (Turks, Azeris, Turkmen). Native minorities: Arameans, Assyrians, Copts, Druze, Jews, Samaritans, Mandaeans, Yazidis, Zazas, Greek Cypriots. European diasporas: Albanians, Bosniaks, Circassians, Greeks, Franco/Italo-Levantines. Migrant communities: South Asians (Indians, Pakistanis, Bangladeshis), Filipinos, Chinese, Indonesians, Pashtuns, Romani, Afro-Arabs.
**Migration:** Gulf states absorbed Egyptian, Yemeni, and Levantine labor (1970s–1990s); Europe drew North African workers. 13M first-generation Arab migrants globally (5.8M in other Arab states). 2009 remittances to Arab countries: $35.1B; flows to Jordan, Egypt, Lebanon exceed trade revenues by 40–190%. Minorities (Kurds, Jews, Assyrians, Greeks, Armenians, Mandaeans) fled persecution (e.g., Iran post-1979 Revolution).
**Languages:** Top six by speakers: Arabic, Persian, Turkish, Kurdish, Modern Hebrew, Greek. ~20 minority languages spoken. Arabic (Semitic, Afro-Asiatic) is official in North Africa and most West Asian states; dialects vary. Modern South Arabian languages (Mehri, Soqotri) in Yemen/Oman. Aramaic dialects spoken by Assyrians/Mandaeans; Western Aramaic survives in two Syrian villages. Persian (Indo-European) dominates Iran. Turkish (Turkic, East Asian origin) confined mostly to Turkey. Kurdish (Iran, Iraq, Syria, Turkey); Sorani is Iraq’s second official language. Hebrew revived late 19th century (Eliezer Ben-Yehuda); first native speaker 1882; official in Israel (Arabic special status post-2018). Greek official in Cyprus; formerly lingua franca of western Middle East until Muslim expansion and Turkification of Anatolia. English official in Akrotiri/Dhekelia; widely taught as L2. French used in Lebanon, taught in Egypt/Syria. Russian widely spoken in Israel (1990s aliyah). South Asian languages (Bengali, Hindi, Urdu) prevalent in Gulf migrant hubs (20–55% of populations).
**Religion:** Islam is largest; Judaism and Christianity originated here and remain represented. Christians: 78% of Cyprus, 40.5% of Lebanon (president, half cabinet/parliament Christian). Minority faiths: Baháʼí, Yarsanism, Yazidism, Zoroastrianism, Mandaeism, Druze, Shabakism. Ancient religions: Mesopotamian, Canaanite, Manichaeism, Mithraism, gnostic sects.

### Culture
**Sport:** Region increasingly hosts global events (World Cup, Formula 1, etc.) to diversify economies. South Asian diaspora drives cricket popularity.

## Terms
- ****Fertile Crescent**** — Crescent-shaped region spanning the Levant and Mesopotamia where agriculture and earliest civilizations arose.
- ****Levant**** — Eastern Mediterranean littoral (modern Syria, Lebanon, Israel, Palestine, Jordan, Cyprus).
- ****Mesopotamia**** — Land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers (modern Iraq, parts of Syria/Turkey/Iran); site of Sumer, Akkad, Babylon.
- ****Caliphate**** — Islamic polity led by a caliph (successor to Muhammad); four major caliphates ruled the Middle East 7th–13th centuries.
- ****Mandate**** — League of Nations authorization post-WWI for Britain/France to administer former Ottoman territories.
- ****OPEC**** — Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries; dominated by Middle Eastern members controlling ~2/3 world oil reserves.
- ****Gulf Arab states**** — Countries on the Persian Gulf (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, Oman) with vast petroleum reserves.
- ****Mashriq**** — Arabic term for the eastern Arab world (Levant, Iraq), contrasted with *Maghreb* (western Arab world/North Africa).
- ****Turko-Persian culture**** — Synthesis of Turkic and Persian traditions spread by Seljuks and later dynasties across the region.
- ****Eurocentric**** — Perspective centering Europe; critics argue "Middle East" reflects Western geopolitical framing rather than indigenous geography.

## Debates and open questions
- **Definition boundaries:** No consensus on exact country inclusion; terms "Near East," "Middle East," "Greater Middle East," and "MENA" overlap inconsistently across governments, academia, and media.
- **Terminology legitimacy:** Scholars from the region (Khraish, Hanafi) contest "Middle East" as a colonial/Eurocentric imposition; indigenous alternatives (e.g., *Mashriq*, *Khāvar-e miyāneh*) coexist with translated Western terms.
- **Ethnic vs. civic identity:** Tension between dominant Arab-Islamic identity and minority nationalisms (Kurdish, Assyrian, Amazigh, etc.) drives conflict and migration.
- **Resource curse vs. diversification:** Oil wealth creates rentier states with high youth unemployment; post-COVID tourism and sport investments test economic transition away from fossil fuels.
- **Climate adaptation:** Extreme aridity, water scarcity, and heat threaten habitability; governance capacity for transboundary water management (Nile, Tigris/Euphrates, Jordan) is untested.
