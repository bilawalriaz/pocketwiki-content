# Lake Baikal

## Overview
Lake Baikal is a rift lake in Siberia, Russia, and is the world's deepest, oldest, and most voluminous freshwater lake. It holds approximately 22–23% of the world's unfrozen fresh surface water and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site renowned for its exceptional biodiversity and unique endemic species.

## Timeline
- **c. 24,000 BP** — Evidence of human habitation by the Mal'ta–Buret' culture near the lake.
- **6th century** — The Kurykans tribe inhabits the area and gives the lake a name meaning "much water."
- **1628–1658** — Russian expansion into the Buryat area around Lake Baikal occurs.
- **1643** — Kurbat Ivanov becomes the first European to see Lake Baikal and Olkhon Island.
- **1896–1902** — The Trans-Siberian Railway is built around the southwestern end of the lake.
- **1918** — A minor military engagement occurs between the Czechoslovak Legion and the Red Army at the lake.
- **Winter 1920** — The retreating White Russian Army crosses the frozen lake in the Great Siberian Ice March.
- **1956** — The impounding of the Irkutsk Dam raises the lake's level by 1.4 m.
- **1990** — Anatoly Sagalevich sets the record for the deepest freshwater dive (1,637 m) in Lake Baikal.
- **1993** — Neutrino research begins at the Baikal Deep Underwater Neutrino Telescope (BDUNT).
- **1996** — UNESCO declares Lake Baikal a World Heritage Site.
- **2008** — The Baykalsk Pulp and Paper Mill is closed due to unprofitability.

## Body

### Geography and Hydrography
Lake Baikal is a rift lake formed by the Baikal Rift Zone, where the Earth's crust is pulling apart. It is 636 km long and 79 km wide, with a maximum depth of 1,642 m. The rift floor lies 8–11 km below the surface, making it the deepest continental rift on Earth. The lake is divided into three basins (North, Central, South) by underwater ridges. It is fed by over 330 rivers, with the Selenga being the main tributary, and drains via a single outlet, the Angara River. The lake's age is estimated at 25–30 million years, making it the most ancient lake in geological history. Its sediments provide a detailed record of climatic variation over the past 6.7 million years.

### Water Characteristics
Baikal is one of the clearest lakes, with winter transparency reaching 30–40 m. The surface freezes for 4–5 months annually. Water temperature varies significantly with depth and season, with deep-water temperatures remaining stable at 3.1–3.4 °C. The average surface temperature has risen by almost 1.5 °C in the last 50 years, shortening the ice-cover period. The lake is rich in oxygen even at great depths.

### Ecology
Lake Baikal hosts over 1,000 plant and 2,500 animal species, with many being endemic. Its aquatic flora is dominated by green algae and diatoms. The Baikal seal (nerpa) is the lake's only mammal species and is endemic. The fish community includes the endemic omul whitefish and the unique, translucent golomyanka, which are the primary prey for the seal. The lake's invertebrate fauna is exceptionally diverse, particularly amphipod crustaceans, with over 350 endemic species. Freshwater sponges from the endemic family Lubomirskiidae make up about 44% of the benthic animal biomass.

### Human History and Economy
The Baikal region has a long history of human habitation. Russian expansion into the area occurred in the 17th century. The Trans-Siberian Railway, completed in 1902, significantly increased access. Today, the lake is a major tourist destination, with popular activities including ice-walking in winter and hiking in summer. The economy includes tourism, fishing, and research. A special economic zone was declared in 2007.

### Environmental Concerns
The lake faces pollution from industrial and tourist sources. The Baykalsk Pulp and Paper Mill, which discharged waste directly into the lake, operated from 1966 until its final closure in 2013. A planned oil pipeline was rerouted away from the lake after environmental protests. Other threats include a proposed uranium enrichment center, a Chinese-owned bottled water plant (halted after protests), and waste from tourist sites and ships.

## Terms
- **Rift lake**: A lake formed in a rift valley, a lowland area where the Earth's crust is pulling apart.
- **Endemic**: A species found only in a specific geographic location.
- **Homothermic**: A state where water is the same temperature throughout a certain depth.
- **Benthic**: Relating to the bottom of a body of water.
- **Amphipod**: A type of small crustacean, a major group of invertebrates in Lake Baikal.
- **Omul**: An endemic whitefish species of Lake Baikal, important for local fisheries.
- **Golomyanka**: A unique, translucent, deep-water fish endemic to Lake Baikal.
- **Nerpa**: The Baikal seal, the world's only exclusively freshwater seal species.
- **Cryptodepression**: A lake floor that lies below sea level (e.g., Lake Baikal's bottom is 1,186.5 m below sea level).

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact total number of plant and animal species in the lake is believed to be significantly higher than current counts.
- Significant taxonomic uncertainties remain for the lake's diatom species.
- The true diversity of ostracod crustaceans is likely heavily underestimated.
- The long-term ecological impact of the closed Baykalsk Pulp and Paper Mill's lignin sludge reservoirs remains a hazard.
- The environmental consequences of the constructed uranium enrichment center near Angarsk are a concern for critics.
