# Black Death

## Overview
The Black Death (1346–1353) was a catastrophic plague pandemic caused by the bacterium *Yersinia pestis* that killed an estimated 30–60% of Europe’s population (up to 50 million people) and roughly 33% of the Middle Eastern population. It was the second great natural disaster to strike Europe during the Late Middle Ages (the first one being the Great Famine of 1315–1317) and is considered the second plague pandemic, following the Plague of Justinian (541–549). The disease spread rapidly via trade routes, likely transmitted by fleas on black rats and through human-to-human pneumonic transmission. Its demographic collapse triggered profound economic shifts—labor shortages raised wages but inflation hurt real incomes—and destabilized feudalism. It also sparked violent persecutions of Jews and other minorities and influenced cultural movements such as the Renaissance. Plague recurred in Europe until the late 17th century and globally until the early 19th century.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BCE** — *Yersinia pestis* DNA found in Swedish tomb; possibly linked to Neolithic population decline.
- **541–549 CE** — Plague of Justinian (first plague pandemic); confirmed as *Y. pestis* in 2013.
- **1338–1339** — Spike in "pestilence" deaths recorded on Syriac tombstones near Lake Issyk-Kul, Kyrgyzstan; *Y. pestis* DNA confirmed in 2022 as direct ancestor of Black Death strain.
- **1345–1346** — Mongol Golden Horde under Jani Beg besieges Genoese port of Caffa (Crimea); plague reported in besieging army.
- **1347** — Plague enters Europe via Genoese ships fleeing Caffa to Constantinople (summer); reaches Sicily (October), then Genoa, Venice, Pisa, Marseilles (January 1348).
- **1348–1350** — Sweeps through France, Spain, England, Germany, Scandinavia; reaches Norway (1349), Moscow (1353).
- **1347–1349** — Devastates Middle East and North Africa: Alexandria, Cairo (over 1/3 dead), Gaza, Damascus, Aleppo, Mecca, Tunis, Morocco.
- **1377** — Ragusa (Dubrovnik) institutes 30-day isolation (*trentino*) for arrivals from plague-affected areas, later extended to 40 days (*quarantino*), origin of "quarantine".
- **1360–1671** — Second plague pandemic: recurrent outbreaks across Europe and Mediterranean nearly every year.
- **1855–1960** — Third plague pandemic originates in China; spreads globally; kills 10 million in India alone.
- **1894** — Alexandre Yersin identifies *Yersinia pestis* in Hong Kong; Paul-Louis Simond establishes flea-vector mechanism (1898).
- **2010–2011** — Ancient DNA (aDNA) from European mass graves definitively confirms *Y. pestis* as cause of Black Death.
- **2022** — aDNA from Kyrgyzstan graves (1338–1339) confirms origin of Black Death strain in Central Asia.
- **2026** — *Nature* study identifies oldest known plague outbreaks in human remains: *Y. pestis* DNA in teeth of 18 hunter-gatherers near Lake Baikal, Siberia, ~5,500 years ago.

## Body

### Names and Nomenclature
Contemporary Latin terms included *pestis*, *pestilentia*, and *mortalitas*. In English before the 18th century, it was "the pestilence," "great death," or "first murrain." The term "Black Death" was not used in 1347; it appears in Homer’s *Odyssey* and Seneca’s *mors atra* as a literary metaphor for death. The phrase *mors nigra* was used in 1350 by Simon de Covino regarding astrological conjunction, not unambiguously the pandemic. Swedish and Danish chroniclers popularized "black death" (*den sorte død*) in the 15th–16th centuries; it entered English in the 1750s. Previously, most European languages used calques of *magna mortalitas* (Great Death).

### Previous Plague Epidemics
*Y. pestis* infected humans in Eurasia by the Late Neolithic/Early Bronze Age (c. 3000 BCE). The Plague of Justinian (541–549, recurrences to 750) was the first pandemic, confirmed as *Y. pestis* in 2013. Chinese physicians Chao Yuanfang (610) and Sun Simiao (d. 652) described "malignant bubo" in Lingnan (Guangzhou), suggesting the first pandemic spread eastward by c. 600.

### Origin and "Patient Zero"
The immediate geographic origin is disputed: Central Asia, China, the Middle East, and the Caspian Sea region are all candidates. Genetic analysis places *Y. pestis* evolution ~7,000 years ago; flea-mediated strains emerged ~3,800 years ago. A 2026 *Nature* study identified the oldest known evidence of plague outbreaks in human remains, finding traces of *Yersinia pestis* DNA in the teeth of 18 ancient hunter-gatherers from four cemeteries near Lake Baikal in Siberia. Carbon dating revealed two outbreaks, with the earliest cases dating to approximately 5,500 years ago — some 200 years earlier than previously established. Researchers concluded the disease likely spread initially from marmots through contact with raw organs or infected hides, and also transmitted between people through respiratory means. Many victims were young children, with three young girls buried side by side among the dead. The 2022 discovery of *Y. pestis* DNA in teeth from Kara-Djigach and Burana (Kyrgyzstan), dated 1338–1339, identifies the direct ancestral strain of the Black Death. The siege of Caffa (1347) by Jani Beg’s Golden Horde introduced it to Europe; catapulted corpses and/or rats spread it to defenders. Genoese galleys carried it to Constantinople and the Mediterranean.

### Causes and Transmission Theories
**Early theory:** Paris Medical Faculty blamed planetary conjunction (miasma); some Muslim doctors saw divine punishment.  
**Modern consensus:** Climate change in Asia drove rodents into populated areas. *Y. pestis* is enzootic in flea-rodent cycles (marmots, ground squirrels) in Central Asia, Kurdistan, West Asia, etc. Transmission typically: resistant rodent hosts maintain the bacterium; susceptible rodents die, fleas seek new hosts (humans). Flea midgut blockage by replicating *Y. pestis* causes regurgitation of bacteria into bite site.  
**DNA evidence (2010–2011):** PCR and genomic sequencing from mass graves (East Smithfield, London; France; Germany) confirmed *Y. pestis* as the causative agent. The Black Death strain is ancestral to most modern strains and descendant of the Justinianic strain.  
**Alternative models:** Human ectoparasites (fleas, body lice) may better fit mortality curves than rat-flea-human, especially in cooler Northern Europe where rat fleas survive poorly. Archaeological lack of mass rat die-offs and speed of spread support person-to-person (pneumonic/louse-borne) transmission.  
**Summary:** No single alternative has wide acceptance. Many scholars argue for a combination of bubonic, septicemic, and pneumonic plague, possibly with co-circulating diseases (typhus, smallpox), to explain seasonality and high mortality.

### Territorial Origins Debate
According to a team of medical geneticists led by Mark Achtman, *Yersinia pestis* "evolved in or near China" over 2,600 years ago. Later research by a team led by Galina Eroshenko placed its origins more specifically in the Tian Shan mountains on the border between Kyrgyzstan and China. However more recent research notes that the previous sampling contained East Asian bias and that sampling since then has discovered strains of *Y. pestis* in the Caucasus region previously thought to be restricted to China. There is also no physical or specific textual evidence of the Black Death in 14th century China. As a result, China's place in the sequence of the plague's spread is still debated to this day. According to Charles Creighton, records of epidemics in 14th-century China suggest nothing more than typhus and major Chinese outbreaks of epidemic disease post-date the European epidemic by several years. The earliest Chinese descriptions of the bubonic plague do not appear until the 1640s. Nestorian graves near Issyk-Kul (1338–1339) with "pestilence" inscriptions and *Y. pestis* DNA support a Central Asian epicenter. The Silk Road was disrupted by 1325–1340, making it an unlikely vector. Some argue the plague originated near the Caspian Sea or in Kurdistan and never reached China; others propose four distinct *Y. pestis* strains predominated in different Eurasian regions. China’s population fell 15–33% (1340–1370), but war/famine, not pandemic plague, are cited as primary causes.

### European Outbreak (1347–1353)
From Caffa, Genoese ships brought plague to Constantinople (summer 1347), killing Emperor John VI Kantakouzenos’s son. Twelve galleys reached Sicily (Oct 1347); spread to Genoa, Venice, Pisa (Jan 1348), Marseilles (late Jan 1348). Moved northwest: France, Spain, Portugal, England by June 1348; east/north through Germany, Scotland, Scandinavia 1348–1350. Norway infected 1349 via ship to Askøy/Bergen. Reached Moscow 1353. Isolated areas (Basque Country, parts of Belgium/Netherlands, Alpine villages) were less affected. Unfavorable weather decimated rodents, forcing fleas to humans; summer peaks in Mediterranean, autumn peaks in southern Baltic. Pre-existing malnutrition weakened immunity.

### West Asian and North African Outbreak
By autumn 1347: Alexandria (via slave ship from Constantinople). Summer 1348: Cairo (Mamluk capital, pop. ~600,000); >1/3 died, Nile choked with corpses; recurred >50 times in 150 years. Spring 1347: Gaza (April), Damascus (July), Aleppo (Oct); Ascalon, Acre, Jerusalem, Sidon, Homs infected. 1348–1349: Antioch (residents fled, died en route). April 1348: Tunis (from Sicily); Moroccan army dispersed, carried plague to Morocco. 1348: Mecca via Hajj pilgrims. 1351/1352: Yemen via released Rasulid sultan. 1349: Mosul massive epidemic; Baghdad second wave.

### Signs and Symptoms
**Bubonic:** Fever 38–41°C, headaches, joint pain, nausea, malaise. Buboes (gavocciolos) in groin, neck, armpits—apple/egg-sized, oozing pus/blood. Black/livid spots on limbs. Death in 2–7 days; untreated mortality ~80%. Boccaccio and others describe rapid spread from buboes to skin spots.  
**Pneumonic:** Lung infection; fever, cough, blood-tinged sputum becoming free-flowing bright red. Mortality 90–95%. Noted by Lodewijk Heyligen (1348).  
**Septicemic:** Rarest; high fever, purple skin patches (purpura from disseminated intravascular coagulation). Mortality near 100%. Progress so rapid buboes often do not develop.

### Consequences
**Deaths:** No exact figures; estimates 75–200 million in Eurasia. Europe: 25–60% (Benedictow argues 60%; Daileader 45–50%; Pope Clement VI agents calculated 23.84 million dead of 75 million = 31%). High variability: 2022 pollen study shows high mortality in Scandinavia, France, W. Germany, Greece, C. Italy; uninterrupted agriculture in C./E. Europe, Iberia, Ireland. Urban centers hit hardest: Paris lost 50%; Florence 110–120k to 50k (80% in four months 1348); Hamburg/Bremen 60%; London ~62,000 dead. Germany lost ~40,000 settlements by 1450. Clergy mortality high. Russia similar to West. Successive outbreaks (1347, 1362, 1371, 1382) showed declining mortality (Chalmel de Vinario); by 1380s predominantly affected children. Egypt ~40% dead; Cairo halved by 18th century.

**Economic:** Poor disproportionately affected. Labor shortage → wages soared; rents collapsed (e.g., £5 to £1). But inflation reduced real incomes of wage-laborers. Landowners commuted labor services to money rents. Tax collection failed (empty lands, collectors refused plague zones). Trade disruptions contributed to Mongol Empire collapse.

**Environmental:** Thomas Van Hoof (Utrecht) suggests reforestation from abandoned farmland cooled climate, possibly triggering the Little Ice Age.

**Persecutions:** Religious fervor/fanaticism rose. Jews, friars, foreigners, beggars, pilgrims, lepers, Romani blamed. Lepers and those with skin diseases killed. Strasbourg massacre (Feb 1349): ~2,000 Jews murdered; Mainz and Cologne annihilated Aug 1349; 60 major + 150 minor Jewish communities destroyed by 1351. Many Jews fled to Poland (welcomed by Casimir the Great). Astrology, earthquakes, well-poisoning accusations; many saw divine punishment for sins.

**Social:** Florence’s devastation (1348–1350) may have shifted worldview toward earthly life, contributing to Renaissance (alongside Greek scholars post-Byzantine fall). New piety: religious art sponsorship, guilds, devotion to Blessed Sacrament, Virgin, Five Wounds, Holy Name. Church in England "wholly disorganised" by 1351; inadequately prepared clergy accepted; "religious paralysis" then revival with devotional, self-reflective tone. Feudalism destabilized: labor value rose, commoners gained freedom, workers migrated for better wages, food prices fell, land abundant, inheritance from dead relatives. Quarantine originated: Ragusa 30-day isolation (1377), extended to 40 days (*quarantino*).

### Recurrences
**Second plague pandemic (14th–17th c.):** Present in Europe nearly every year 1346–1671 (Biraben). Major waves: 1360–63, 1374, 1400, 1438–39, 1456–57, 1464–66, 1481–85, 1500–03, 1518–31, 1544–48, 1563–66, 1573–88, 1596–99, 1602–11, 1623–40, 1644–54, 1664–67. Retreated from Europe (18th c.) and North Africa (19th c.). France: ≥2 million dead 1600–1670 (750k in 1628–32). Italy: 1.7 million in early 17th c. Spain: >1.25 million in 17th c. Islamic world: plague nearly yearly 1500–1850. Algiers: 30–50k dead in multiple waves. Cairo: >50 epidemics in 150 years; last 1840s. Ottoman Constantinople: 37 epidemics 1701–50, 31 more 1751–1800. Baghdad: severe, sometimes 2/3 population died. Sub-Saharan Africa: debated (Sussman says 20th c.; others say second pandemic reached it).

**Third plague pandemic (1855–1960):** Began in China mid-19th c.; global spread; 10 million dead in India. 1894 Hong Kong: Yersin identifies *Y. pestis*; Simond proves flea vector (1898). Australia: 12 outbreaks 1900–1925 (>1,000 deaths, mostly Sydney) → Public Health Dept. established. North America: San Francisco 1900–04, 1907–08.

**Recent decades:** Treatment: insecticides, antibiotics, vaccine. Drug resistance feared; resistant strain found Madagascar 1995; outbreaks 2014, 2017 (170 dead, thousands infected). Modern case fatality ~11% with antibiotics; higher in underdeveloped regions.

## Terms
- **Yersinia pestis**: Gram-negative bacterium causing plague; discovered 1894 by Alexandre Yersin.
- **Bubonic plague**: Most common form; infection of lymphatic system via flea bite, causing swollen lymph nodes (buboes).
- **Pneumonic plague**: Highly lethal respiratory form; spread via infectious droplets; mortality 90–95%.
- **Septicemic plague**: Rarest form; bloodstream infection causing disseminated intravascular coagulation and purpura; mortality near 100%.
- **Enzootic**: Disease constantly present in an animal population (e.g., *Y. pestis* in rodent-flea cycles).
- **Quarantine**: From Italian *quarantino* (forty days); isolation period for ships/people to prevent disease spread; first imposed in Ragusa (1377) as 30-day *trentino*.
- **Miasma theory**: Pre-germ theory belief that disease spread through "bad air" or corrupted atmosphere.
- **Patient zero**: Index case; earliest identified victim of an outbreak (here, 1338–1339 Kyrgyzstan graves).
- **Ancient DNA (aDNA)**: Genetic material recovered from archaeological remains; used to confirm *Y. pestis* in Black Death victims (2010 onward).
- **Second plague pandemic**: Series of plague recurrences in Europe/Mediterranean from 14th to 17th centuries, beginning with the Black Death.

## Debates and open questions
- **Geographic origin**: Whether the Black Death originated in Central Asia (Tian Shan/Issyk-Kul), China, the Caspian region, or Kurdistan; conflicting genetic, textual, and archaeological evidence.
- **Role of the Silk Road**: Whether it facilitated spread (Mongol armies/traders) or was too disrupted by 1340 to be a major vector.
- **Transmission mechanism**: Rat-flea-human vs. human ectoparasite (fleas/lice) vs. pneumonic person-to-person; which best explains speed, seasonality, and mortality curves, especially in Northern Europe.
- **Presence in 14th-century China**: Whether the Black Death reached China (population fell 15–33% but evidence points to war/famine) or if distinct *Y. pestis* strains circulated regionally.
- **Co-circulating pathogens**: Whether typhus, smallpox, or other diseases contributed significantly to mortality alongside *Y. pestis*.
- **Demographic uniformity**: 2022 pollen study challenges assumption of uniformly devastating impact across Europe; high regional variability requires explanation.
- **Climate link**: Whether plague-driven reforestation contributed to the Little Ice Age (Van Hoof hypothesis).
