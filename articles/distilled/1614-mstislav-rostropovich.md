# Mstislav Rostropovich

## Overview
Mstislav Rostropovich (1927–2007) was a Soviet and Russian cellist and conductor who expanded the cello repertoire more than any predecessor by inspiring, commissioning, and premiering over 100 new works. He formed deep artistic partnerships with leading composers including Shostakovich, Prokofiev, Britten, Dutilleux, Lutosławski, Penderecki, and Schnittke. Beyond music, he was a prominent human rights advocate; his sheltering of Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn and defense of artistic freedom led to Soviet harassment, loss of citizenship in 1978, and exile to the United States in 1974. He later served as music director of the National Symphony Orchestra in Washington, D.C. (1977–1994), supported democratic movements in Russia during the 1991 coup attempt and 1993 constitutional crisis, and founded the Kronberg Academy and the Rostropovich-Vishnevskaya Foundation for children's health.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Rostropovich was born into a musical family in Baku. His father, Leopold, was a cellist and student of Pablo Casals; his mother, Sofiya, was a conservatory-trained pianist. The family moved to Orenburg during World War II and then to Moscow in 1943. Rostropovich began piano at four and cello at eight under his father. At 16, he entered the Moscow Conservatory, studying cello with his uncle Semyon Kozolupov, piano, and composition with Vissarion Shebalin and Dmitri Shostakovich. He won the inaugural Soviet young musicians competition in 1945, graduated in 1948, and became a professor of cello there in 1956.

### Soviet Career and Composer Partnerships
Rostropovich gave his first cello concert in 1942 and won top prizes in Prague and Budapest (1947, 1949, 1950). He formed lifelong creative bonds with Soviet composers. Nikolai Myaskovsky dedicated his Cello Sonata No. 2 to him (premiered 1949). Inspired by that performance, Sergei Prokofiev wrote his Cello Sonata Op. 119 (premiered 1950 with Richter) and Symphony-Concerto (premiered 1952); Rostropovich later completed Prokofiev's Cello Concertino with Dmitry Kabalevsky. Dmitri Shostakovich wrote both his cello concertos for Rostropovich, who premiered them. In 1948, when Shostakovich was dismissed from his professorships under the anti-formalism decree, the 21-year-old Rostropovich quit the conservatory in protest. He also smuggled the manuscript of Shostakovich's Symphony No. 13 ("Babi Yar") to the West.

### International Recognition and the 1968 Proms
Rostropovich toured Western Europe, meeting Benjamin Britten, who dedicated his Cello Sonata, three Solo Suites, and Cello Symphony to him; the two recorded Schubert's Arpeggione Sonata together. He also partnered with Henri Dutilleux (*Tout un monde lointain...*), Witold Lutosławski (Cello Concerto), Krzysztof Penderecki (Cello Concerto No. 2), Luciano Berio, and Olivier Messiaen. He studied conducting privately with Leo Ginzburg and debuted publicly in 1962. On 21 August 1968, he performed Dvořák's Cello Concerto at The Proms with the USSR State Symphony Orchestra on the very day the Warsaw Pact invaded Czechoslovakia. After a performance met with heckling and then cheers, he held the conductor's score aloft as a gesture of solidarity with Prague.

### Exile and Human Rights Advocacy
Rostropovich's advocacy for "art without borders" and democratic values drew Soviet retaliation. In 1970, he sheltered Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, leading to severe restrictions: bans on foreign touring for him and his wife, and curtailed domestic performances. The family left the USSR in 1974. In 1977, Soviet authorities instructed bloc musicians to boycott an international competition he organized. He was stripped of Soviet citizenship in 1978. He did not return until 1990, after citizenship was restored.

### Western Career and Return to Russia
From 1977 to 1994, Rostropovich directed the National Symphony Orchestra in Washington, D.C., while maintaining an active solo career with artists like Martha Argerich, Sviatoslav Richter, and Vladimir Horowitz. He founded the Mstislav Rostropovich Baku International Festival and performed regularly at the Aldeburgh Festival. During the fall of the Berlin Wall, he gave an impromptu performance. In August 1991, he flew to Moscow to stand with Boris Yeltsin during the coup attempt. He supported Yeltsin during the 1993 constitutional crisis, conducting the National Symphony in Red Square. In 1993, he helped found the Kronberg Academy. He commissioned Rodion Shchedrin's opera *Lolita* and conducted its 1994 premiere. With Galina Vishnevskaya, he established the Rostropovich-Vishnevskaya Foundation (1991) to improve children's health in the former Soviet Union. Their art collection was purchased intact by Alisher Usmanov in 2007 to keep it in Russia.

### Final Years
Rostropovich's health declined in 2006. President Vladimir Putin visited him ahead of his 80th birthday celebration in March 2007. He entered the Blokhin Russian Cancer Research Centre on 7 April 2007 and died on 27 April. His funeral on 29 April drew thousands, including Putin, Queen Sofía of Spain, and President Ilham Aliyev of Azerbaijan. He was buried in Novodevichy Cemetery.

## Terms
- **Cello Sonata Op. 119 (Prokofiev)**: Major work premiered by Rostropovich and Sviatoslav Richter in 1950, written specifically for the cellist.
- **Symphony-Concerto (Prokofiev)**: Large-scale work for cello and orchestra dedicated to and premiered by Rostropovich in 1952.
- **Cello Concertos No. 1 & 2 (Shostakovich)**: Both concertos were written for and premiered by Rostropovich, becoming cornerstones of the repertoire.
- **Babi Yar / Symphony No. 13 (Shostakovich)**: Symphony setting Yevgeny Yevtushenko's poems about the 1941 massacre of Jews in Kyiv; Rostropovich smuggled the manuscript out of the USSR.
- **Prague Spring (1968)**: Period of political liberalization in Czechoslovakia ended by Warsaw Pact invasion on 21 August 1968, coinciding with Rostropovich's Proms concert.
- **Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn**: Nobel laureate writer and dissident sheltered by Rostropovich in 1970, triggering the cellist's official disgrace.
- **National Symphony Orchestra (Washington, D.C.)**: Major U.S. orchestra Rostropovich led as music director from 1977 to 1994.
- **Kronberg Academy**: International academy for string players founded in 1993 with Rostropovich's instrumental support; he remained a patron until death.
- **Rostropovich-Vishnevskaya Foundation**: Nonprofit founded in 1991 in Washington, D.C., to improve children's health in the former Soviet Union.
- **BACH.Bow**: Curved bow designed by cellist Michael Bach; Rostropovich participated in its development and testing between 1997 and 2001.

## Debates and open questions
- **Artistic vs. political legacy**: Sources emphasize both equally; the tension between his status as a Soviet cultural icon and his persecution by the state remains a central interpretive framework.
- **Extent of repertoire expansion**: The article claims he premiered 117 works and enlarged the repertoire "more than any cellist before or since"—a quantitative claim that invites musicological verification.
- **Role in 1991 and 1993 Russian crises**: His direct participation (joining Yeltsin, conducting in Red Square) is documented, but the precise impact of his celebrity on political outcomes is not assessed in the source.
- **Citizenship restoration timing**: Citizenship was restored in 1990, yet he did not return until later; the interplay of personal choice and bureaucratic process is not detailed.
