# Character (arts)

## Overview
A character is a person or being in a narrative, such as a novel, play, or film. The term, derived from Ancient Greek, evolved to mean a part played by an actor and is central to storytelling, guiding readers through plots and themes. Characters can be fictional, based on real people, or archetypes, and their study involves analyzing their relationships and functions within a work.

## Timeline
- **c. 335 BCE** — Aristotle's *Poetics* defines character (*ethos*) as a qualitative part of tragedy, emphasizing its role in revealing decision.
- **1749** — Henry Fielding's *Tom Jones* popularizes the modern English use of the word "character."
- **1888** — August Strindberg's *Miss Julie* features characters representing specific social positions.
- **1891** — Henrik Ibsen's *Hedda Gabler* uses characters to reveal ideological conflicts.
- **20th Century** — E. M. Forster defines "flat" and "round" characters in *Aspects of the Novel*.

## Body

### Definition and Origin
The English word "character" dates from the Restoration period but became widely used after appearing in Henry Fielding's *Tom Jones* (1749). It evolved from the sense of "a part played by an actor," replacing the earlier term *dramatis personae* ("masks of the drama"). A character, especially when enacted, creates "the illusion of being a human person." In literature, characters help readers understand plots and themes. Since the 19th century, the art of creating characters is called characterization.

### Creation and Types
Authors create characters from imagination or by amplifying traits of real people, historical figures, or themselves. Characters can be archetypes (e.g., hero, father figure) or stock characters (generic, flat types). A charactonym is a name that implies a character's traits, like Shakespeare's Mercutio. A character representing a class or group is a type, as seen in Ibsen's and Strindberg's plays.

### Character Analysis and Classification
Characters are analyzed through their relationships and oppositions with others. E. M. Forster distinguished flat characters (two-dimensional, uncomplicated) from round characters (complex, developing). Dynamic characters change during a story (e.g., Ebenezer Scrooge), while static characters do not. In television, characters are classified as regular (appear in most episodes), recurring (appear frequently), or guest (appear briefly).

### Classical Analysis
Aristotle's *Poetics* (c. 335 BCE) defines character (*ethos*) as the quality that reveals a person's decisions. He argues for the primacy of plot (*mythos*) over character, stating that characters exist for the sake of the action. Ancient Greek comedy, as defined in the *Tractatus coislinianus*, involves three character types: the buffoon, the ironist, and the boaster. By the Roman era, character types were used to define dramatic genres.

## Terms
- **Character**: A person or being in a narrative.
- **Characterization**: The art of creating characters, practiced by actors or writers.
- **Type**: A character representing a particular class or group.
- **Stock character**: A generic, flat character often used in supporting roles.
- **Charactonym**: A name that implies a character's psychological makeup or appearance.
- **Flat character**: A two-dimensional, uncomplicated character.
- **Round character**: A complex, developing character.
- **Dynamic character**: A character who changes over the course of a story.
- **Static character**: A character who remains the same throughout a story.
- **Ethos**: Aristotle's term for character, meaning the quality that reveals decision.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the relation between characters and story action shifts historically, often mirroring societal changes in ideas about individuality and social order. Aristotle's primacy of plot over character remains a foundational point of analysis in dramatic theory.
