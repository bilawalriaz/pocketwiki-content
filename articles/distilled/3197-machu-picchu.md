# Machu Picchu

## Overview
Machu Picchu is a 15th-century Inca citadel in Peru, renowned as a masterpiece of engineering and architecture. Built as a royal estate for the Inca emperor Pachacuti, it was abandoned around the time of the Spanish conquest and remained largely unknown to the outside world until its international "discovery" in 1911. Its significance lies in its exceptional preservation, providing a unique window into Inca civilization, and its status as a global cultural icon and major tourist destination.

## Timeline
- **c. 1420–1530** — Period of occupation, based on radiocarbon dating.
- **c. 1450** — Traditional date for construction under Emperor Pachacuti.
- **Mid-16th Century** — Site abandoned, likely due to the Spanish conquest and disease.
- **1902** — Peruvian explorer Agustín Lizárraga reaches the ruins.
- **1911** — Hiram Bingham's expedition brings the site to international attention.
- **1912–1915** — Bingham's Yale-sponsored excavations; artifacts removed to Yale University.
- **1981** — Peru designates the surrounding area a National Historic Sanctuary.
- **1983** — UNESCO designates Machu Picchu a World Heritage Site.
- **2007** — Named one of the New Seven Wonders of the World.
- **2012** — Yale University returns the last batch of artifacts to Peru.
- **2020** — Site closed for eight months due to the COVID-19 pandemic.
- **2024** — Daily visitor limit increased to 4,500.

## Body

### Construction and Purpose
Machu Picchu was constructed in the classical Inca style of precisely fitted dry-stone walls. It is believed to have been built around 1450 as a seasonal royal estate for the Inca emperor Pachacuti, following his conquest of the Urubamba region. The site was not passed down through succession but was used for about 80 years before being abandoned, likely due to the Spanish conquest and the spread of smallpox. The population consisted of about 750 people, mostly support staff (*yanaconas*) of diverse genetic origins, along with religious specialists and temporary workers.

### Daily Life and Agriculture
The inhabitants were immigrants from various regions, as evidenced by skeletal remains showing different diets and parasites. The site's agriculture relied on *andenes* (artificial terraces) engineered for drainage and soil fertility, primarily growing maize and potatoes. However, the terrace area was insufficient to feed the population, so most food was imported from surrounding valleys. Animals like llamas, alpacas, guinea pigs, and dogs were present, likely brought from higher altitudes for meat, ritual, and companionship.

### Rituals and Architecture
The site features key structures like the Temple of the Sun, the Temple of the Three Windows, and the Intihuatana ritual stone. While human sacrifice was practiced in the Inca Empire, evidence at Machu Picchu points mainly to animal offerings, particularly camelids. The site is divided into an agricultural sector and an urban sector, the latter split into an upper town (*Hanan*) with sacred plazas and a lower town (*Hurin*) with residences and workshops. The architecture includes trapezoidal doorways and sophisticated water channels.

### Rediscovery and Controversy
Although known locally, the site was brought to international attention by American historian Hiram Bingham in 1911, who was searching for the lost Inca capital of Vilcabamba. Bingham initially acknowledged the prior visit by Agustín Lizárraga but later claimed the discovery for himself. His 1912–1915 expeditions, sponsored by Yale and National Geographic, removed thousands of artifacts to Yale University. This led to a long dispute, with Peru accusing Yale of cultural theft. An agreement was reached in 2007, and all artifacts were returned to Peru by 2012, now housed in the Machu Picchu Museum–Casa Concha.

### Preservation and Tourism
Peru declared the area a National Historic Sanctuary in 1981, and UNESCO named it a World Heritage Site in 1983. It is Peru's most visited tourist destination, with over 1.5 million visitors annually as of 2025. Tourism management has included visitor limits (currently 4,500 per day), a no-fly zone, and the cancellation of proposed cable cars and hotels due to environmental concerns. The site was closed during the COVID-19 pandemic and again briefly in 2023 due to national protests.

## Terms
*   **Andenes:** Artificial agricultural terraces built by the Inca to create flat farmland on steep slopes, engineered for drainage and soil fertility.
*   **Yanacona:** A class of permanent support staff or retainers in the Inca Empire, often of diverse ethnic origins, who served in royal estates like Machu Picchu.
*   **Dry-stone walls:** A construction technique where stones are precisely cut and fitted together without the use of mortar.
*   **Intihuatana:** A ritual stone at Machu Picchu whose name means "place where the sun is tied" in Quechua; its exact function is debated but it is aligned with solar events.
*   **Kancha:** A basic Inca architectural unit consisting of a rectangular enclosure with buildings around a central courtyard.
*   **Hanan/Hurin:** The division of an Inca settlement into an upper town (*Hanan*) for religious and elite functions and a lower town (*Hurin*) for residential and workshop areas.
*   **Capacocha:** An Inca ritual involving the sacrifice of carefully selected children to secure supernatural favor.
*   **Sapa Inca:** The sovereign ruler of the Inca Empire.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Chronology:** While traditional models based on historical texts placed construction around 1450, recent radiocarbon dating of human remains suggests an earlier occupation period from c. 1420 to 1530.
*   **Original Name:** The site's original Inca name is not definitively known. A 2021 study suggests it may have been called "Huayna Picchu" or simply "Picchu," with the name "Machu Picchu" becoming associated with the ruins through Hiram Bingham's publications.
*   **Function of the Intihuatana:** Scholars generally reject the popular idea that it served as a precise solar calendar gnomon. Its purpose may have been for horizontal solar observations or symbolic alignment with sacred mountains.
*   **Discovery Credit:** The narrative of discovery is contested. While Hiram Bingham brought the site to global fame, Peruvian explorer Agustín Lizárraga reached it in 1902, and local populations were always aware of its existence.
