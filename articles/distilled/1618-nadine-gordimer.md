# Nadine Gordimer

## Overview
Nadine Gordimer (1923–2014) was a South African novelist, short-story writer, and anti-apartheid activist who won the 1991 Nobel Prize in Literature. Her fiction—spanning fifteen novels and over two dozen story collections—dissected the moral and psychological tensions of apartheid, portraying ordinary people forced into impossible choices by racial laws. She joined the banned African National Congress (ANC), hid activists, and helped Nelson Mandela edit his 1964 "I Am Prepared to Die" speech. After apartheid ended, she campaigned against HIV/AIDS and state secrecy laws, refusing to leave South Africa despite crime and political disillusionment.

## Timeline
- **20 Nov 1923** — Born near Springs, Transvaal, to Jewish immigrants Isidore (Lithuanian) and Hannah (British).
- **1937** — First published stories ("The Quest for Seen Gold", "Come Again Tomorrow") at age 13.
- **1948** — Moved to Johannesburg; first story collection *Face to Face* published 1949.
- **1951** — *The New Yorker* accepts "A Watcher of the Dead", launching international career.
- **1953** — First novel, *The Lying Days*, published.
- **1960** — Arrest of friend Bettie du Toit and Sharpeville Massacre spur full-time activism.
- **1962–64** — Helps Mandela edit defence speech; hides ANC leaders; joins ANC while banned.
- **1974** — Wins Booker Prize (shared) for *The Conservationist*.
- **1976–81** — *The Late Bourgeois World* (banned 10 yrs), *A World of Strangers* (banned 12 yrs), *Burger's Daughter* (banned 1 month, then unbanned), *July's People* published.
- **3 Oct 1991** — Awarded Nobel Prize in Literature.
- **1990s–2000s** — Active in HIV/AIDS fundraising (*Telling Tales*, 2004); critic of Mbeki’s AIDS stance and ANC secrecy bill.
- **13 Jul 2014** — Dies in Johannesburg, aged 90.

## Body

### Early Life and Formative Influences
Gordimer was raised in a secular household in the mining town of Springs. Her father, a Lithuanian Jewish refugee from Tsarist Russia, was politically passive regarding Black South Africans; her mother, a British Jewish immigrant, founded a crèche for Black children, modelling activism. A police raid on the family home—targeting a servant’s letters—gave Gordimer early exposure to state repression. Largely home-schooled due to her mother’s fear of a weak heart, she began writing young, publishing adult fiction by 16. A year at the University of the Witwatersrand (1945–46) introduced her to professionals across the colour bar and the Sophiatown cultural renaissance.

### Literary Career: Apartheid-Era Novels
Gordimer’s work traces the evolution of white South African consciousness under apartheid. *The Lying Days* (1953), a *Bildungsroman* set in Springs, follows Helen’s political awakening. *Occasion for Loving* (1963) criminalises interracial love under the Immorality Act. *The Conservationist* (1974, Booker Prize) uses the antihero Mehring, a wealthy industrialist preserving nature to preserve apartheid, to expose the system’s foundation on unacknowledged Black corpses. *Burger's Daughter* (1979), a "coded homage" to lawyer Bram Fischer, portrays Rosa Burger inheriting her parents’ revolutionary burden after the 1976 Soweto Uprising. *July's People* (1981) imagines a violent revolution where a white liberal couple flees to their servant July’s village, reversing power dynamics to test the limits of liberalism.

### Activism and Censorship
The 1960 arrest of her closest friend, Bettie du Toit, and the Sharpeville Massacre radicalised Gordimer. She joined the illegal ANC, hid leaders, and testified at the 1986 Delmas Treason Trial for 22 activists—calling it her proudest day. The regime banned *The Late Bourgeois World* (10 years), *A World of Strangers* (12 years), and briefly *Burger's Daughter*; Gordimer refused SABC airtime and co-founded the Anti-Censorship Action Group and the Congress of South African Writers. She served as Vice President of International PEN. When the Publications Appeal Board unbanned *Burger's Daughter* but upheld bans on Black authors, she denounced the double standard in *Essential Gesture* (1988).

### Post-Apartheid Engagement
After 1990, Gordimer turned to new crises. She organised *Telling Tales* (2004), a story anthology funding the Treatment Action Campaign’s fight for state HIV/AIDS treatment, sharply criticising President Thabo Mbeki’s denialism. She opposed the ANC’s Protection of State Information Bill (2012) as a threat to free speech. Internationally, she joined Nobel laureates warning the U.S. against destabilising Cuba (2005) and declined the Orange Prize shortlist (1998) because it honoured only women. She taught at U.S. universities and Massey College, Toronto, but remained in her Johannesburg home of five decades, refusing gated communities.

### Jewish Identity in Life and Fiction
Though Jewish by heritage and married twice to Jewish men, Gordimer identified as an atheist with a "religious temperament." Critics note Jewish characters appear sporadically—Joel Aaron in *The Lying Days* (a Zionist who makes *aliyah*), the *Shemira* ritual in "A Watcher of the Dead" (1951), the protagonist Hillela in *A Sport of Nature* (1987)—but often through non-Jewish eyes. Scholar Michael Wade argues Jewishness functions as a "repressed theme," mirroring Gordimer’s own ambivalence: she could not make the "leap of faith" but felt her moral values stemmed from the Judeo-Christian tradition. She rejected the notion that Jewish persecution drove her activism, insisting "all decent people" share the duty to fight evil.

### Later Novels and Themes
Post-apartheid novels explore new fault lines. *The House Gun* (1998) examines white suburban fear, ubiquitous firearms, and a Black lawyer defending a white murderer. *The Pickup* (2001, Commonwealth Writers’ Prize) follows a white South African woman and an undocumented Arab immigrant, reversing the alien/insider dynamic when they move to his homeland. *Get a Life* (2005), written after her husband’s death, parallels an ecologist’s radiation therapy (making him a nuclear hazard at home) with his fight against a nuclear plant, merging bodily and political vulnerability. *No Time Like the Present* (2012) centres on a half-Jewish man married to a Zulu woman, meditating on identity in the new South Africa.

## Terms
- **Apartheid**: South Africa’s legal system (1948–1994) enforcing racial segregation and white minority rule.
- **African National Congress (ANC)**: Black-led liberation movement, banned 1960–1990; Gordimer joined while illegal.
- **Sharpeville Massacre (1960)**: Police killed 69 Black protesters; catalyst for Gordimer’s activism.
- **Bildungsroman**: Novel of formation/education; *The Lying Days* is a key example.
- **Immorality Act**: Apartheid law criminalising interracial sex; central to *Occasion for Loving*.
- **Shemira**: Jewish ritual of guarding a body from death to burial; depicted in "A Watcher of the Dead".
- **Aliyah**: Jewish immigration to Israel; Joel Aaron’s choice in *The Lying Days*.
- **Treatment Action Campaign (TAC)**: South African NGO demanding state HIV/AIDS treatment; Gordimer fundraised for it.
- **Protection of State Information Bill**: 2010s ANC bill criticised by Gordimer as censorship.
- **Sophiatown Renaissance**: 1940s–50s Black cultural flowering in Johannesburg; Gordimer encountered it at university.

## Debates and open questions
- **Jewish identity as repressed theme**: Scholars (Wade, Bonert, Ivry) dispute whether Gordimer’s sparse, often anthropological portrayal of Jewish characters reflects personal ambivalence, assimilationist pressure, or a deliberate universalism.
- **Post-apartheid relevance**: *July's People* was removed from a school list in 2001 as "deeply racist, superior and patronising"—a charge Gordimer called a grave insult. Critics still debate whether her liberal white gaze illuminates or distorts Black experience.
- **Activism vs. artistry**: Gordimer insisted fiction should not be propaganda, yet her novels are inseparable from her activism. The Nobel citation praised "magnificent epic writing" of "very great benefit to humanity"—implying the two are fused.
- **Unauthorised biography (*No Cold Kitchen*, 2006)**: Roberts’ account of her husband’s death, a 1950s affair, and her Israel views led Gordimer to disown the book, raising questions about authorial control over life narrative.
