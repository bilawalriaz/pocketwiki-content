# Albert Ellis

## Overview
Albert Ellis (1913–2007) was an American psychologist who founded Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT), the first form of cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT). He argued that emotional disturbances stem not from external events but from irrational beliefs about those events, and he developed an active-directive method to dispute and replace those beliefs. A 1982 survey ranked him the second most influential psychotherapist in history, behind Carl Rogers and ahead of Sigmund Freud. His work catalyzed the "cognitive revolution" in psychotherapy, shifting the field from psychoanalysis toward evidence-based, structured interventions integrating cognition, emotion, and behavior.

## Timeline
- **1913** — Born in Pittsburgh, PA; raised in The Bronx, NY.
- **1934** — Earned BA in business from City College of New York.
- **1943** — Completed MA in clinical psychology at Teachers College, Columbia University.
- **1947** — Awarded PhD in Clinical Psychology from Columbia; practiced classical psychoanalysis.
- **1953** — Formally broke with psychoanalysis; began calling himself a rational therapist.
- **1955** — Presented Rational Therapy (RT), the precursor to REBT.
- **1957** — Formally proposed the first cognitive behavioral therapy framework.
- **1959** — Founded the Institute for Rational Living (later Albert Ellis Institute).
- **1962** — Published *Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy*, his first major REBT text.
- **1971** — Named Humanist of the Year by the American Humanist Association.
- **1982** — Ranked second most influential psychotherapist in survey of US/Canadian psychologists.
- **2003** — Signed Humanist Manifesto; received congratulations from major public figures on 90th birthday.
- **2005–2006** — Removed from and reinstated to the board of his institute after legal dispute.
- **2007** — Died July 24 at age 93; posthumously awarded APA Outstanding Lifetime Contributions to Psychology (2013).

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Ellis was born in Pittsburgh and raised in the Bronx by Jewish immigrant grandparents. His father was largely absent; his mother was described as emotionally distant and self-absorbed. Ellis endured severe childhood illnesses, including eight hospitalizations between ages five and seven, with minimal parental support. He reported developing "indifference" to this neglect. During adolescence, he committed numerous sexual assaults via frotteurism (nonconsensual rubbing against women in crowded spaces), which he later acknowledged with remorse but also described as "cheapest and easiest sex." He also suffered extreme shyness and public speaking anxiety; at 19, he systematically desensitized himself by forcing conversations with 100 women in a month. He earned a BA in business (1934), failed in business and fiction writing during the Great Depression, then turned to non-fiction on human sexuality, which led him to clinical psychology. He entered Teachers College, Columbia University in 1942, earning an MA in 1943 and a PhD in 1947. The program was psychoanalytic; Ellis initially embraced Freud but grew disillusioned.

### Development of Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT)
Influenced by Karen Horney, Alfred Adler, Erich Fromm, Harry Stack Sullivan, Alfred Korzybski’s general semantics, and Stoic philosophy, Ellis broke completely with psychoanalysis by January 1953. He presented Rational Therapy (RT) in 1955, positing that clients’ emotional pain stems from irrational, self-defeating beliefs within their personal philosophy. The therapist actively demonstrates the irrationality, rigidity, and self-defeatism of these beliefs to foster cognitive reconstruction. In 1957, he formally proposed the first cognitive behavioral therapy: adjusting thinking and behavior as treatment for emotional problems. His confrontational, directive style provoked hostility from the psychoanalytic and behaviorist establishments. He founded the Institute for Rational Living in 1959, chartered as a training institute in 1968. In the mid-1990s, he renamed the system Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT) to emphasize the interdependence of cognition, emotion, and behavior. REBT is recognized as the first form of CBT. Ellis later acknowledged REBT’s limitations, stating it is not "an unmitigated cure for everyone."

### Sexology and Sexual Revolution
Ellis was a prominent sexologist and early advocate of the American sexual revolution. He collaborated with Alfred Kinsey and published extensively on sexuality. *Sex Without Guilt* (1958) advocated liberal sexual attitudes. *Homosexuality: Its Causes and Cure* (1965) initially viewed homosexuality as a pathology. After the American Psychiatric Association declassified homosexuality as a disorder in 1973, Ellis revised his view in *Sex and the Liberated Man* (1976), arguing homosexuality is not inherently good or evil and that treatment should not be attempted for most. He updated *Sex Without Guilt* in 2001 to reflect evolved humanistic ethics, dedicating a chapter to enhancing homosexual love lives.

### Unconditional Self-Acceptance
Ellis advocated accepting oneself simply for being alive, human, and unique, rejecting global self-ratings (e.g., "I am good/bad") and dependence on others’ approval. This concept distinguishes self-acceptance from self-esteem, which he viewed as conditional and destabilizing.

### Integrity Assessment Research
From 1979 onward, Ellis explored behavioral integrity—reliability, honesty, loyalty—through applied experimental psychology at his Institute. With Mike Abrams and Lidia Dengelegi Abrams, he developed *Personality Theories*, evaluating integrity as a cognitive norm and identifying reliability as a common factor across personality samples.

### Religion and Humanism
Ellis was a "probabilistic atheist" (near-certainty no god exists) and secular humanist, named Humanist of the Year (1971) and a signer of the Humanist Manifesto (2003). Early on, he argued devout religious beliefs harmed mental health, defining "religiosity" as any dogmatic, demanding belief system—including orthodox psychotherapy and aggressive atheism. He debated religious psychologists (e.g., Orval Hobart Mowrer). Later, he softened his stance, acknowledging survey evidence that belief in a loving God can be psychologically healthy. He co-authored *Counseling and Psychotherapy with Religious Persons* (2001) to integrate religious material into REBT, insisting REBT itself is independent of his atheism.

### Political Views
Ellis was a lifelong peace advocate and opponent of militarism. He praised libertarian economist Walter Block’s *Defending the Undefendable*.

### Later Professional Recognition
Despite early hostility, Ellis’s prominence grew as CBT gained empirical support (notably via Aaron T. Beck’s cognitive therapy). By the 1980s, he was the most-cited author in US psychology journals after 1957. He received the APA Distinguished Professional Contributions Award (1985) and numerous other honors. His institute launched a professional journal (late 1960s) and "The Living School" for children (early 1970s), incorporating REBT principles. He led Friday Night Workshops (public therapy demonstrations) from 1965 until his death and created "rational humorous songs" for self-help.

### Final Years
Ellis worked 16-hour days until age 92. After serious intestinal surgery (2004), a board dispute led to his removal (2005) and reinstatement via lawsuit (2006). He filed a further suit against the Institute in June 2007. With his wife, Australian psychologist Debbie Joffe Ellis, he continued working through pneumonia and rehabilitation. He died July 24, 2007, at 93, having authored over 80 books and 1,200 articles. His autobiography *All Out!* was published posthumously (2010). Debbie Joffe Ellis continues his legacy.

### Personal Life
First marriage to Karyl Corper (1938) ended in annulment; three children born after her remarriage. Second marriage to Rhoda Winter (1956) ended in divorce. Open relationship with Janet L. Wolfe (psychologist, Institute director) from 1965–2002. Married Debbie Joffe in 2004, calling her "the greatest love of his life."

### Criticism
Critics accused Ellis of misinterpreting Freud and lacking rigorous empirical testing compared to Aaron T. Beck. His confrontational language and aggressive debate style (e.g., with Nathaniel Branden) drew frequent criticism.

## Terms
- **Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT)**: An active-directive, philosophically and empirically based psychotherapy positing that emotional disturbances are caused by irrational beliefs about events, not the events themselves; the first form of CBT.
- **Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT)**: A family of psychotherapies integrating cognitive, emotive, and behavioral methods to change maladaptive thinking and behavior; REBT is its earliest form.
- **Irrational Belief**: A rigid, self-defeating, absolutist belief (e.g., "I must be perfect") that REBT targets for dispute and replacement with flexible, rational alternatives.
- **Unconditional Self-Acceptance (USA)**: Accepting oneself wholly because one is alive and human, without global self-rating or dependence on external validation.
- **Probabilistic Atheism**: Ellis’s term for the view that while absolute certainty of no god is impossible, the probability is so low it warrants no attention.
- **Religiosity**: Ellis’s idiosyncratic definition: any devout, dogmatic, demanding belief system, applicable to religious, political, or psychotherapeutic orthodoxies.
- **Frotteurism**: A paraphilic disorder involving nonconsensual rubbing against others for sexual arousal; Ellis disclosed a history of this behavior in adolescence/early adulthood.
- **Systematic Desensitization**: A behavioral technique (self-applied by Ellis at 19) involving gradual exposure to a feared stimulus (talking to women) to reduce anxiety.
- **Cognitive Revolution**: The paradigm shift in psychotherapy from psychoanalysis/behaviorism to cognitive-behavioral approaches, catalyzed by Ellis and Aaron T. Beck.
- **Friday Night Workshops**: Public therapy demonstrations Ellis led weekly from 1965 until his death, treating audience volunteers.

## Debates and open questions
- **Empirical rigor**: Critics (including Aaron T. Beck) argued Ellis relied more on philosophical argument and clinical demonstration than controlled outcome research; the relative efficacy of REBT vs. other CBT variants remains studied.
- **Therapeutic style vs. model**: Ellis’s confrontational, abrasive personal delivery contrasted with the varied, client-tailored styles of REBT practitioners; the extent to which his persona helped or hindered dissemination is debated.
- **Homosexuality classification**: Ellis’s initial pathologizing of homosexuality (1965) and later reversal (1976) reflect the field’s broader shift; the role of therapist values in diagnosing "disturbance" remains an ethical question.
- **Religion and mental health**: Ellis’s shift from viewing religiosity as inherently harmful to acknowledging adaptive value of theistic belief illustrates the tension between therapist worldview and client autonomy in value-laden therapy.
- **Legacy disputes**: The 2005–2007 legal battles over control of the Albert Ellis Institute and its property raise questions about governance of founder-led professional organizations.
