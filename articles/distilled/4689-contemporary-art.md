# Contemporary art

## Overview
Contemporary art is the art of today, generally defined as work created from the 1970s onward. It is characterized by its global, multicultural, and technologically influenced nature, continuing the 20th-century trend of challenging artistic boundaries. Its defining feature is a lack of a single unifying ideology or style, making it diverse and open-ended, and it engages with broad cultural dialogues about identity, community, and society.

## Body

### Definition and Scope
The term "contemporary art" as a specific category emerged with Modernism in the early 20th century. Its start date is fluid and moves forward with the present; works from 1910 are no longer considered contemporary. While some definitions include art from the 1960s, a common benchmark is art from around 1970 to the present. The boundary with "modern art" is often blurred, with some institutions like the Musée National d'Art Moderne not differentiating between them. Sociologist Nathalie Heinich distinguishes the two, arguing that modern art challenges representation, while contemporary art challenges the very notion of an artwork itself.

### Themes and Characteristics
Contemporary art is defined by its diversity in materials, forms, and subjects, lacking a uniform organizing principle. Common themes include identity politics, the body, globalization, technology, and critiques of institutions and politics. Since the late 20th century, it has increasingly reflected issues of migration, cultural identity, and globalization, with works addressing immigration and displacement.

### Institutions and the Art World
The art world relies on a network of museums, galleries, schools, and commercial and non-profit organizations. The line between for-profit and non-profit sectors has blurred. Corporations also participate by exhibiting art, sponsoring awards, and building collections, often using art's prestige for marketing. Institutions are criticized for gatekeeping, often excluding work like "outsider art" (art by self-taught artists) or craft, unless it meets certain conceptual criteria.

### Public Reception and Concerns
Contemporary art can be at odds with public values, leading to skepticism or rejection from some critics and audiences. A persistent concern since the early 20th century is the question of what constitutes art. The concept of the "avant-garde" influences which works gain notice from galleries and museums.

## Terms
- **Contemporary Art:** Art created from the 1970s onward, characterized by diversity and a lack of a single unifying style.
- **Modern Art:** Art from the late 19th to mid-20th century, often seen as a historical movement distinct from contemporary art.
- **Avant-garde:** Innovative and experimental art that pushes the boundaries of what is accepted as the norm.
- **Outsider Art:** Art created by self-taught artists who are assumed to work outside the mainstream art historical context.
- **Institutional Critique:** An artistic practice that examines and questions the role and structures of art institutions.
- **Coolhunting:** The practice of seeking out emerging trends, often used in marketing and linked to the use of contemporary art's prestige.

## Debates and Open questions
- **Definitional Boundaries:** There is ongoing debate about the precise start date for contemporary art and where it ends, with definitions varying from the 1960s to the present.
- **Inclusion and Exclusion:** Institutions are criticized for regulating what counts as contemporary art, often excluding outsider art and craft unless they align with specific conceptual values.
- **Public Legitimacy:** Skeptics argue that public rejection of much contemporary art is a reasonable response, questioning its reliance on theoretical discourse over visual experience.
