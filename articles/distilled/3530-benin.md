# Benin

## Overview
Benin is a West African nation with a coastline on the Bight of Benin. Its history is marked by the powerful pre-colonial Kingdom of Dahomey, French colonial rule, and a post-independence journey through military rule, Marxism-Leninism, and a transition to a multi-party democracy. The country's economy is heavily dependent on agriculture, particularly cotton, and it faces challenges including poverty and climate vulnerability.

- **1892** — France takes over the territory.
- **1899** — French Dahomey is incorporated into French West Africa.
- **1 August 1960** — Dahomey gains full independence from France.
- **26 October 1972** — Lt. Col. Mathieu Kérékou overthrows the government in a coup.
- **30 November 1975** — The country is renamed the People's Republic of Benin.
- **1990** — Marxism-Leninism is abolished; the country is renamed the Republic of Benin.
- **1991** — Nicéphore Soglo defeats Kérékou in an election, marking a democratic transition.
- **2016** — Patrice Talon is elected president.
- **7 December 2025** — A coup attempt against President Talon is foiled.
- **12 April 2026** — Romuald Wadagni wins a presidential election criticized as neither free nor fair.

## Body

### Pre-colonial and Colonial History
Before 1600, the region comprised diverse city-states and tribal areas. The Kingdom of Dahomey, founded on the Abomey plateau, expanded in the 17th and 18th centuries, conquering coastal cities like Allada and Whydah by 1727. The kingdom became a major player in the transatlantic slave trade, earning the region the name "Slave Coast." The decline of the slave trade, partly due to British abolition in 1807, weakened Dahomey. France took control in 1892, incorporating the area into French West Africa as French Dahomey. Colonial rule outlawed slavery, leading to internal struggles over land and labor redistribution.

### Post-Independence and Political Evolution
Dahomey gained independence in 1960 under President Hubert Maga. The early post-colonial period was marked by political instability and coups, with power rotating among figures representing different ethnic regions. In 1972, Mathieu Kérékou seized power. In 1975, he renamed the country the People's Republic of Benin and established a Marxist-Leninist state, nationalizing key industries. The regime evolved through nationalist, socialist, and later, more liberal phases. Economic crisis in the late 1980s led Kérékou to renounce Marxism. A 1990 constitution established the multi-party Republic of Benin. Kérékou lost power in 1991 but returned to win elections in 1996 and 2001. Subsequent presidents include Thomas Boni Yayi (2006–2016) and Patrice Talon (2016–2026). Talon's tenure saw constitutional reforms, including the creation of a Senate in 2025, and a 2026 election widely criticized for lacking credible opposition.

### Geography and Climate
Benin is a narrow, north-south strip of land bordered by Togo, Nigeria, Burkina Faso, and Niger. Its geography ranges from a low-lying, marshy coastal plain to forest-savanna plateaus and the Atacora Mountains in the northwest, with Mont Sokbaro (658 m) as the highest point. The climate is tropical with two rainy and two dry seasons. The country is vulnerable to climate change, particularly sea-level rise in the populous south and desertification in the north. Forest cover has declined significantly, from 4.8 million hectares in 1990 to 3.1 million in 2020.

### Society and Culture
Benin is ethnically diverse, with major groups including the Fon, Yoruba, Bariba, and Dendi. French is the official language, used in administration and secondary education, while indigenous languages like Fon and Yoruba are widely spoken. Christianity (53% in 2020) and Islam (31%) are the main religions, with a significant minority practicing Vodun and other traditional faiths, especially in the south. The adult literacy rate was 51% in 2022. Health challenges include malaria and a high maternal mortality rate, though HIV/AIDS incidence has fallen sharply.

### Economy
Benin's economy is dependent on subsistence agriculture and cotton, which accounts for 40% of GDP and about 80% of official exports. The Port of Cotonou is a key revenue source, facilitating regional trade. Despite consistent GDP growth of 4–5% over two decades, poverty has increased, affecting 40.1% of the population in 2015. The country faces challenges including an insufficient electrical supply and a large informal economy with issues of child and forced labor.

## Terms
*   **Kingdom of Dahomey:** A powerful pre-colonial state in present-day Benin, known for its military, including an elite female corps, and its role in the transatlantic slave trade.
*   **Bight of Benin:** A large bay on the Gulf of Guinea in the Atlantic Ocean, which gives the modern country its name.
*   **Vodun:** A traditional religion practiced widely in Benin, involving the veneration of spirits and deities; often referred to as Voodoo.
*   **Marxism-Leninism:** A communist ideology adopted by Benin's government from 1974 to 1990, involving state control of the economy and a single-party system.
*   **Bamako Initiative:** A community-based healthcare reform model that improved service provision in Benin after the 1980s.
*   **Harmattan:** A dry, dusty wind from the Sahara that blows over Benin from December to March.
*   **OHADA:** The Organization for the Harmonization of Business Law in Africa, a body of which Benin is a member.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The 2026 presidential election was characterized by international observers as "neither free nor fair," raising questions about the health of Benin's democracy following the Talon era.
*   The long-term impact of the 2025 constitutional reform, which created a Senate and was passed under President Talon, on political stability and the balance of power is uncertain.
*   The effectiveness of government policies in reducing poverty, which has increased despite economic growth, remains a critical issue.
