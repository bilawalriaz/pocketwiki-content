# Guam

## Overview
Guam is an organized, unincorporated territory of the United States in the western Pacific Ocean. It is the largest and southernmost of the Mariana Islands and the westernmost point of the U.S. The island's strategic location has made it a focal point of colonial and military history, from Spanish colonization to its current role as a major U.S. military hub. Its culture is a blend of indigenous Chamorro traditions and Spanish, American, and other Asian influences.

## Timeline
- **c. 1500–1400 BC** — First settled by Austronesian migrants from the Philippines.
- **March 6, 1521** — First European contact by Ferdinand Magellan.
- **June 15, 1668** — Spanish colonization begins with the arrival of a Catholic mission.
- **June 21, 1898** — U.S. captures Guam during the Spanish–American War.
- **April 11, 1899** — Spain formally cedes Guam to the U.S. via the Treaty of Paris.
- **December 8, 1941** — Japan invades and occupies Guam.
- **July 21, 1944** — U.S. forces recapture Guam (Liberation Day).
- **1950** — The Guam Organic Act establishes its civilian government and grants U.S. citizenship to residents.
- **1968** — The Guam Elective Governor Act allows for popular election of the governor.
- **1997** — A referendum on unification with the Northern Mariana Islands is rejected.

## Body

### Pre-Contact and Spanish Era
The Chamorro people, related to Austronesian peoples, settled Guam and the Mariana Islands around 3,500 years ago. Their ancient society was matrilineal and had a class system, with distinctive megalithic "latte stone" house foundations. Portuguese explorer Ferdinand Magellan was the first European to visit in 1521, but Spain did not officially claim the island until 1565. Full Spanish colonization began in 1668, leading to Spanish-Chamorro Wars and a devastating population decline from disease and conflict. Guam served as a key stop for Spanish Manila galleons.

### American Era and World War II
The U.S. captured Guam in 1898 during the Spanish–American War. It became a naval station and a hub for trans-Pacific communications. During World War I, the German raider SMS *Cormoran* was interned at Apra Harbor; its scuttling on April 7, 1917, marked the first U.S. combat action against Germany. In World War II, Japan invaded Guam on December 8, 1941, occupying it for about 31 months. The occupation involved severe atrocities, killing an estimated 10% of the population. U.S. forces recaptured the island in the 1944 Battle of Guam.

### Post-War Development and Politics
The 1950 Guam Organic Act created the territory's modern government and granted U.S. citizenship. The governor became an elected office in 1968. Guam's economy, once reliant on military spending, expanded with tourism after security restrictions were lifted in 1963. The island's political status is a major ongoing debate. While a 1982 plebiscite showed interest in commonwealth status, the federal government rejected the proposed model. A Commission on Decolonization was established in 1997 to educate the public on options including statehood, free association, and independence. Public opinion polls indicate a strong preference for U.S. statehood.

### Geography, Climate, and Ecology
Guam is a volcanic and coralline limestone island, 30.17 miles long and 4 to 12 miles wide. Its highest point is Mount Lamlam (1,334 ft). The climate is tropical rainforest, with a wet season from July to November and a dry season from January to May. The island is vulnerable to typhoons. Guam's ecology is diverse but severely threatened by invasive species, notably the brown tree snake, which has caused the extinction or endangerment of most native bird species. Conservation efforts include marine preserves and the Guam National Wildlife Refuge.

### Demographics and Culture
As of 2020, the largest ethnic groups are Asians (35.5%) and Chamorros (32.8%). The official languages are English and Chamorro, with Filipino also commonly spoken. The predominant religion is Christianity, mostly Catholicism. Chamorro culture is a blend of indigenous, Spanish, American, and Asian influences, visible in cuisine, language, and customs. There has been a recent resurgence in preserving pre-Hispanic traditions.

### Economy and Military
Guam's economy depends on tourism, primarily from Japan and South Korea, and U.S. military installations. Joint Region Marianas controls about 29% of the island's land, including Naval Base Guam and Andersen Air Force Base. A planned relocation of Marines from Okinawa has been scaled back and delayed. The Compacts of Free Association (COFA) allow citizens of freely associated states to reside in Guam, impacting public assistance programs.

## Terms
- **Unincorporated organized territory**: A U.S. jurisdiction with a locally elected government and a congressional charter (the Organic Act), but not part of any state.
- **Chamorro**: The indigenous people of the Mariana Islands, including Guam.
- **Latte stone**: A megalithic pillar consisting of a limestone base (*haligi*) and a capstone (*tåsa*), used as a foundation for traditional houses.
- **Manila galleon**: Spanish trading ships that sailed between Manila and Acapulco from 1565 to 1815, with Guam as a key stop.
- **Guam Organic Act of 1950**: The U.S. federal law that established Guam's civilian government and granted its residents U.S. citizenship.
- **Compacts of Free Association (COFA)**: Agreements between the U.S. and freely associated states (Federated States of Micronesia, Marshall Islands, Palau) that allow their citizens to live and work in the U.S.
- **Joint Region Marianas**: The unified U.S. Department of Defense command overseeing military installations on Guam.
- **Decolonization**: The process of a non-self-governing territory achieving self-determination, with options typically including independence, free association, or integration (e.g., statehood).

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns Guam's future political status. Options include becoming a U.S. state, entering into a compact of free association, or pursuing independence. A Commission on Decolonization was established in 1997 to educate the public, but progress has been slow. Public opinion polls consistently show a preference for statehood. The United Nations supports greater self-determination for Guam, which remains on its list of non-self-governing territories.
