# Adolescence

## Overview

Adolescence (from Latin *adolescere*, 'to mature') is the transitional stage of human physical and psychological development from puberty to adulthood. While typically associated with the teenage years, its expressions can begin earlier or end later; the World Health Organization defines it as ages 10–19, though some definitions range from 10 to 30. It is marked by biological changes (puberty, brain development), cognitive growth (abstract thinking, metacognition), and psychosocial development (identity formation, peer relationships), all shaped by cultural context.

## Body

### Biological development

Puberty is a multi-year period of rapid physical growth and psychological change culminating in sexual maturity, typically beginning at ages 10–11 for girls and 11–12 for boys. Onset is influenced primarily by heredity, with environmental factors (diet, exercise) also playing a role. The pituitary gland triggers a hormonal chain reaction: the testes release testosterone, the ovaries release estrogen. Key landmarks are spermarche (first ejaculation, average age 13) for males and menarche (first menstruation, typically around age 13 ± 2–3 years) for females. Menarche requires a certain body fat proportion, so girls with high-fat diets and less physical activity menstruate earlier.

Timing of puberty has psychological consequences. Early-maturing boys tend to be taller, stronger, more confident, but face increased social pressure and are more likely to engage in risky behaviors. Early-maturing girls may develop eating disorders, face sexual advances before emotional maturity, and are more exposed to alcohol and drug abuse. Girls reach full physical development around ages 15–17; boys complete puberty around 16–17.

The adolescent growth spurt results from simultaneous release of growth hormones, thyroid hormones, and androgens. Males experience it about two years later than females, with peak height velocity of 8.8–11.8 cm/year for males (ages 13–15) and 8.0–10.0 cm/year for females (ages 11–13). Growth begins in extremities (head, hands, feet), then arms and legs, then torso—explaining adolescents' sometimes disproportionate appearance. Weight gained during adolescence constitutes nearly half of adult body weight. During puberty, bones become harder; the epiphysis (ends of long bones) closes at puberty's conclusion. Boys develop muscle much faster than girls; girls gain more body fat. Post-pubertal muscle-to-fat ratio is about 3:1 for boys, 5:4 for girls.

Primary sex characteristics are those directly related to sex organs. In males, puberty involves growth of testes, scrotum, then penis, with seminal vesicles, prostate, and bulbourethral gland enlarging. In females, changes involve growth of uterus, vagina, and reproductive system; regular ovulation follows menarche by about two years, so girls appear physically mature before fully fertile. Secondary sex characteristics (not directly related to reproduction) include pubic/facial/body hair, voice deepening in males; breast elevation, hip widening in females. These are categorized in five Tanner stages, named after the British pediatrician who devised the system.

The brain reaches 90% of adult size by age six, so it grows little during adolescence. White matter increases linearly; grey matter follows an inverted-U pattern due to synaptic pruning (elimination of unnecessary neural connections), which makes the brain more efficient through increased myelination (insulation of axons). The prefrontal cortex—involved in decision-making and cognitive control—undergoes significant myelination and pruning, improving information processing and impulse control. The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is important for impulse control and planning; the ventromedial prefrontal cortex for decision-making; the orbitofrontal cortex for evaluating rewards and risks. Three key neurotransmitters: glutamate (excitatory; most pruned connections contain its receptors), dopamine (associated with pleasure; increased limbic activity may explain risk-taking), and serotonin (regulates mood; receptor gene expression changes dramatically in frontal and prefrontal cortex).

### Cognitive development

Adolescence is a time of rapid cognitive development. Piaget describes it as the stage when thoughts become more abstract and egocentric thoughts decrease. The dual systems model proposes a maturational imbalance between the socioemotional system and cognitive control systems, contributing to adolescent impulsivity. Two major theoretical approaches exist: the constructivist view (based on Piaget; cognitive improvement is sudden and drastic) and the information-processing perspective (growth of specific thinking components).

By ages 12–14, critical thinking and decision-making competency are comparable to adults. Improvements occur in five areas: attention (selective and divided), memory (working and long-term), processing speed (improves sharply until age 14/15, then levels off), organization (mnemonic devices and strategies), and metacognition (knowledge of one's own thinking patterns). Prefrontal cortex pruning levels off by age 14–15 but can continue into the sixth decade; white matter increases until around age 45.

Adolescents develop hypothetical and abstract thinking: deductive reasoning, planning ahead, understanding probability, and comprehending puns, proverbs, metaphors, and sarcasm (children under nine often cannot). Metacognition—thinking about thinking—leads to better self-control and increased introspection, but can produce two forms of egocentrism: the imaginary audience and the personal fable, which peak around age 15. Perspective-taking involves understanding how one person's thoughts influence another's.

Adolescents question absolute truths and learn that some rules are culturally relative rather than common-sense based, which can lead to questioning authority. Regarding risk-taking, the behavioral decision-making theory proposes that adolescents and adults both weigh rewards and consequences, but adolescents give more weight to rewards, particularly social rewards. Some argue there are evolutionary benefits to risk-taking: leaving the family of origin, impressing potential mates, and counterbalancing older generations' conservatism. Adolescents show impaired behavioral inhibition, including deficits in extinction learning (the process by which conditioned responses diminish), which has implications for risky behavior and behavioral treatments for anxiety or addiction.

### Psychological development

The formal study of adolescent psychology began with G. Stanley Hall's *Adolescence* (1904). Hall, first president of the American Psychological Association, defined adolescence as ages 14–24 and viewed it as a time of internal turmoil (*sturm und drang*), based on Darwin's evolutionary theory and Freud's psychodynamic theory. His assertions stood until the 1950s, when Erik Erikson (identity formation vs. role fulfillment) and Anna Freud (biologically based, culturally universal disturbances) formulated their theories. All three agreed adolescence was inherently disturbing; less turbulent aspects were ignored until the 1980s.

Jean Macfarlane founded UC Berkeley's Institute of Human Development in 1927, initiating studies of healthy development. The Oakland Growth Study (1931) collected longitudinal data from 1932 to 1981. These studies led Glen Elder in the 1960s to propose a life course perspective with four principles: historical time and place, timing in one's life, linked lives, and human agency. In 1984, the Society for Research on Adolescence became the first official organization dedicated to the field.

### Social development

Identity development is a central task of adolescence. The two main aspects are self-clarity and self-esteem. Researchers use three approaches: self-concept, sense of identity, and self-esteem. Self-concept—opinions and beliefs defined confidently and consistently—shifts from simple physical self-descriptions to values, thoughts, and opinions. Adolescents conceptualize "possible selves" (ideal self they wish to become, feared self they avoid). Differentiation—recognizing contextual influences on behavior—appears fully developed by mid-adolescence, peaking in 7th–9th grades.

Erik Erikson argued that not everyone fully achieves identity; his theory includes the identity crisis, where adolescents explore possibilities before committing to beliefs. James Marcia developed a method testing progress along these stages, classifying individuals into: identity diffusion (where all children begin), identity foreclosure (commitments without exploration), moratorium (process of exploration), or identity achievement (moratorium resulting in commitments). Identity achievement rarely occurs before age 18. Environment plays a huge role: studies show adolescents with more privileged upbringings develop their identity more successfully.

Sexual orientation development: Troiden's 1989 four-stage model for homosexual identity includes sensitization (childhood awareness of same-sex attractions), identity confusion (inner turmoil), identity assumption (coming out to family and friends), and commitment (adopting sexual identity as lifestyle). This model has been contested. A study of 194 LGBT youths found awareness of orientation occurred around age 10, with coming out to peers around 16 and adults around 17. The suicide rate among LGBT adolescents is up to four times higher than heterosexual peers due to bullying and rejection.

Self-esteem: contrary to popular belief, there is no empirical evidence for a significant drop in baseline self-esteem during adolescence, though "barometric self-esteem" fluctuates rapidly. Girls enjoy high self-esteem when engaged in supportive friendships; boys derive it from successfully influencing friends and defining their relation to authority. After romantic relationship endings, women are twice as likely to experience depression; men are three to four times more likely to commit suicide.

Relationships: Peer groups become essential, with communication increasing significantly. Susceptibility to peer pressure increases during early adolescence, peaks around age 14, and declines. Adolescents associate in "cliques" (exclusive, single-sex groups) during early adolescence, which merge into mixed-sex groups later, then break down as socialization becomes couple-oriented. On a larger scale, "crowds" are groups sharing common interests, often the basis for stereotyping (jocks, nerds). Family relationships change: parent–child conflict increases during puberty over minor control issues (curfew, clothing, privacy), though most adolescents still share their parents' attitudes on important life issues. Divorce (rate up to about 50%) usually negatively affects adolescents, with effects continuing into their twenties and thirties.

Romantic relationships increase throughout adolescence: by age 15, 53% of adolescents have had a relationship lasting at least one month. A 2002 American study found average age of first sexual intercourse was 17.0 for males and 17.3 for females. Dating violence occurs: 12–25% of adolescents reported physical violence in relationships; a quarter to a third reported psychological aggression. One in four sexually active teenagers will contract an STI. Girls 15–19 have higher gonorrhea rates than any other age group; one-quarter of all new HIV cases occur in those under 21.

### Culture

Culture shapes adolescent development in social responsibilities, sexual expression, and belief systems. Mark Abrams coined the term "teenager" in 1959 for consumers aged 13–25. In Britain, teenagers first came to public attention during World War II amid fears of juvenile delinquency; by the 1950s, media presented them in terms of generational rebellion, though this was typically belied by intergenerational cooperation.

Three types of autonomy: emotional independence, behavioral autonomy (regulating one's own behavior), and cognitive autonomy (independent reasoning). The "teen timetable" questionnaire measures cultural differences in expected autonomy ages—White parents and adolescents expect autonomy earlier than those of Asian descent. In sub-Saharan Africa, notions of individuality and freedom may not be useful; development is relational and interdependent.

Social roles vary by culture. In many developing countries, children attend fewer years of schooling so they can begin working at adolescence. Half of all 16-year-olds in China were employed in 1980, versus less than one-fourth in 1990. American teenagers spend more time on leisure activities than adolescents in many other countries.

Belief system development involves transformation of understanding, rational direction toward a life course, and active seeking of new ideas rather than unquestioning acceptance of adult authority. Cultures define the transition to adultlike sexuality by milestones like menarche or semenarche. Restrictive cultures discourage premarital sexual activity through separation of sexes, public shaming, or physical punishment; permissive cultures tolerate or encourage it. The U.S. is relatively restrictive, with little normalization of teen sex and ongoing debate between abstinence-only and comprehensive sex education.

Legal issues: since the 1989 Convention on the Rights of the Child, almost every country (except the U.S. and South Sudan) has committed to protecting children (under 18) against child labor, military enrollment, prostitution, and pornography. The age of majority is often 18. Legal working age in Western countries is usually 14–16; minimum school leaving age spans from 10 to 18. Voting age is typically 18 (16 in Brazil; was 25 in Uzbekistan). Age of consent ranges from 12 to 20.

Illicit drug use has roughly plateaued in the U.S. Marijuana is the most commonly used illicit drug: past-year usage among 12th graders rose from 34.9% to 35.9% (2015–2018). More teens now smoke marijuana (23.8%) than cigarettes (43.6%—this figure appears to be a source error; the text states more teens smoke marijuana than cigarettes). Prescription drug abuse is prevalent: 9.9% of high school seniors report abusing prescription drugs within the past year. Teenage alcohol use is stable: 30.2% of 12th graders consumed alcohol within the previous month. Adolescents who drink may be up to sixteen times more likely than non-drinkers to use illicit drugs. Peer perception of drug use strongly shapes individual habits. Boys tend to view drinking as more social; girls more frequently use alcohol as a coping mechanism.

Media: Modern adolescents are exposed to more media than any prior generation. Almost all American households have a television; more than 90% of adolescents use the Internet. As of 2012, 73% of 12–17 year olds had at least one social networking profile. A 2015 review reported adolescents lack awareness of strategies to cope with cyberbullying, associated with increased likelihood of depression. In 2020, 32% of adolescent girls using Instagram reported feeling worse about their body image.

Transitions into adulthood include markers such as leaving school, starting full-time work, leaving home, getting married, and becoming a parent. Transition ceremonies include Confirmation, Bar and Bat Mitzvahs, Quinceañeras, sweet sixteens, cotillions, and débutante balls. In other cultures, initiation ceremonies may involve tattoos or scarification. The time frame varies drastically—from nearly a decade in the U.S. to a few days elsewhere.

### Supporting adolescent development

Jacquelynne Eccles proposed stage-environment fit theory: declines in motivation and wellbeing in early adolescence result from a mismatch between adolescents' developing needs (autonomy, meaningful work, supportive relationships) and school environments. The transition to middle school often increases teacher control and weakens student–teacher relationships precisely when autonomy desires increase. Maria Montessori proposed the Erdkinder ("children of the earth")—a residential farm-school for adolescents aged roughly 12–15 integrating academic study with practical work—though few schools implement it.

## Terms

- **Puberty**: Multi-year period of rapid physical growth and psychological change culminating in sexual maturity
- **Menarche**: Onset of menstruation, typically around age 13
- **Spermarche**: First ejaculation, on average at age 13
- **Synaptic pruning**: Elimination of unnecessary neural connections, making the brain more efficient
- **Myelination**: Insulation of axons that increases neural processing efficiency
- **Metacognition**: Thinking about thinking itself; monitoring one's own cognitive activity
- **Imaginary audience**: Adolescent egocentrism form where one believes others are constantly watching and judging them
- **Personal fable**: Adolescent egocentrism form involving belief in one's uniqueness and invulnerability
- **Identity achievement**: Marcia's stage where exploration (moratorium) has resulted in commitments
- **Stage-environment fit theory**: Eccles's theory that adolescent declines stem from mismatch between needs and school environments

## Debates and open questions

The concept of adolescence itself has been criticized. Robert Epstein states an undeveloped brain is not the main cause of teenagers' turmoils, noting "American-style teen turmoil is absent in more than 100 cultures around the world, suggesting that such mayhem is not biologically inevitable." He questions whether adolescent brain characteristics cause teen tumult or result from lifestyle and experiences. David Moshman says brain research "is crucial for a full picture, but it does not provide an ultimate explanation." Critics point to individual differences in brain growth, arguing "the adult in every adolescent" is too generalizing, and advocate non-chronological emancipation rather than culturally arbitrary measures of "maturity." Some compare adolescent behavior to KZ syndrome (inmate behavior in adult prison camps), arguing aggression may be explained by oppression and "immature" risk behavior by a desire for escape. Neuroscientists cannot agree on when brain development ends; there is no empirical study showing a causal relationship between prefrontal cortex development and irrational behaviors. Scholars also debate whether adolescence is a universal biological stage or a recent social construction.
