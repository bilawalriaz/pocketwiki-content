# Zhiyi

## Overview
Zhiyi (538–597 CE) was a Chinese Buddhist monk and philosopher who founded the Tiantai school, one of the most influential Buddhist traditions in East Asia. He is renowned for creating the first comprehensive, systematic Chinese Buddhist framework, synthesizing various Mahayana teachings into a coherent whole centered on the Lotus Sutra. His work profoundly shaped Buddhist thought and practice in China, Japan (as Tendai), Korea (as Cheontae), and Vietnam, influencing traditions like Chan (Zen) and Pure Land.

## Timeline
- **538 CE** — Born in Huarong District, Jing Prefecture (now Hubei), China.
- **c. 555 CE** — Becomes a Buddhist monk at age eighteen after losing his parents and hometown to war.
- **560–567 CE** — Studies under the meditation master Nanyue Huisi, his most important teacher.
- **567 CE** — Moves to the capital Jinling (Jiangsu) to teach the Lotus Sutra and other texts for eight years.
- **575 CE** — Relocates to Tiantai Mountain (Zhejiang) for eleven years of study and practice, building a monastery there.
- **585 CE** — Returns to Jinling at the request of the King of Chen to give lectures on the Lotus Sutra.
- **c. 585–590s CE** — Delivers lectures that are later edited into his three great works.
- **597 CE** — Dies; his disciple Guanding preserves and edits his teachings.

## Body

### Biography and Legacy
Zhiyi lost his family to war as a teenager and became a monk at eighteen. After studying under various teachers, he met his key mentor, Nanyue Huisi, in 560. Following Huisi's approval, Zhiyi taught in the capital for eight years before retreating to Tiantai Mountain in 575 for intensive practice. He later returned to the capital, where he taught the Lotus Sutra and was honored by the future Sui Emperor Yang. His disciple Guanding (561–632) was instrumental in recording, editing, and preserving his teachings into the foundational texts of the Tiantai school.

### Core Doctrinal System
Zhiyi's system is built on the **Threefold Truth**, which unifies emptiness (all phenomena lack inherent existence), provisional existence (phenomena appear and function conventionally), and the Middle (the non-dual unity of the first two truths). This framework, adapted from Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka, posits that these three truths are perfectly interpenetrating and inseparable.

He interpreted the Lotus Sutra as the supreme "Complete" or "Round" teaching that reveals the Buddha's ultimate intent. He divided the sutra into the Trace Gate (revealing the One Vehicle, where all Buddhist paths lead to Buddhahood) and the Original Gate (revealing the eternal, primordial Buddha). This interpretation allowed him to classify all other Buddhist teachings as provisional skillful means that ultimately point to the same truth.

### Meditation and Practice (Zhiguan)
Zhiyi's *Mohe Zhiguan* (Great Cessation-Contemplation) is a comprehensive guide to meditation. He defined **zhiguan** (cessation-and-contemplation) as encompassing all Buddhist practice: cessation (śamatha) refers to calming the mind and ethical conduct, while contemplation (vipaśyanā) refers to wisdom and insight. He outlined three approaches: gradual, variable, and perfect-and-sudden. The perfect-and-sudden method directly contemplates the non-dual ultimate reality, where "ignorance is dharma-nature" and samsara is nirvana.

### The Path and Buddha-Nature
Zhiyi taught that all beings possess **Buddha-nature**, which he described as an active, threefold process involving practice, wisdom, and true nature. He emphasized the doctrine of **inherent evil**, arguing that ultimate reality encompasses both good and evil; the difference between Buddhas and ordinary beings lies in cultivation, not inherent nature. He outlined the **Six Degrees of Identity**, a path schema showing that all beings are inherently identical with Buddhahood (Identity in Principle) but progress through stages of understanding and practice to its full realization.

## Terms
- **Threefold Truth**: The core doctrine unifying emptiness, provisional existence, and the Middle Way as a single, interpenetrating reality.
- **Zhiguan (Cessation-Contemplation)**: The integrated practice of calming the mind (śamatha) and developing insight (vipaśyanā), which Zhiyi saw as encompassing all Buddhist cultivation.
- **Lotus Sutra**: The Mahayana scripture that serves as the primary basis for Zhiyi's system, which he interpreted as the ultimate "Complete" teaching.
- **One Vehicle (Ekayāna)**: The teaching that all Buddhist paths (Śrāvaka, Pratyekabuddha, Bodhisattva) are provisional means leading to the single goal of Buddhahood.
- **Panjiao (Classification of Teachings)**: A hermeneutical system to organize the Buddha's various teachings into a coherent hierarchy, with the Lotus Sutra at the apex.
- **Buddha-nature (Foxing)**: The innate potential for Buddhahood in all beings, which Zhiyi saw as a dynamic, threefold reality.
- **Inherent Evil (Xing E)**: The Tiantai doctrine that ultimate reality inherently includes both good and evil; liberation involves transforming, not eliminating, evil.
- **Six Degrees of Identity**: A schema of the Buddhist path, from the innate but unrecognized identity with Buddhahood to its complete realization.
- **Mohe Zhiguan**: Zhiyi's major treatise on meditation and doctrine, outlining the practice of perfect-and-sudden contemplation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authenticity of Works**: Scholars debate the provenance of many texts attributed to Zhiyi, such as those on Pure Land and Guanyin, which may be later compositions by disciples.
- **Relationship to Madhyamaka**: Some scholars (like Paul Swanson) argue Zhiyi's Threefold Truth is consistent with classical Madhyamaka, while others (like Ng Yu-Kwan) see it as a distinct "Middle Way–Buddha Nature" thought that moves beyond Madhyamaka's apophatic negation.
- **Systematization**: The more developed "Five Periods and Eight Teachings" schema is attributed to Zhiyi by tradition but was likely systematized by his later disciple Zhanran.
- **Inherent Evil**: The doctrine that Buddha-nature includes evil is a distinctive and sometimes controversial Tiantai teaching, differing from traditions that view Buddha-nature as purely pure.
