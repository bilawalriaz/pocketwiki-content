# Fish

## Overview
Fish are aquatic, gill-bearing vertebrates lacking limbs with digits, encompassing over 33,000 extant species—more than all other vertebrate classes combined. Modern phylogenetics classifies fish as a paraphyletic group (all vertebrates except tetrapods), replacing the traditional single class *Pisces*. They are a critical natural resource for humans, providing a major source of dietary protein through wild capture and aquaculture, while also serving cultural, recreational, and scientific roles. However, overfishing and habitat degradation threaten many populations, with 2,367 species listed as endangered or critically endangered as of 2025.

## Timeline
- **c. 530 Mya (Cambrian)** — Earliest fishlike animals (e.g., *Haikouichthys*) with notochords and frontal eyes appear in fossil record.
- **Late Cambrian** — Jawless forms such as conodonts appear.
- **Silurian** — First jawed vertebrates appear: armoured placoderms (e.g., *Dunkleosteus*), cartilaginous Chondrichthyes, and bony Osteichthyes.
- **Devonian ("Age of Fishes")** — Great diversification of placoderms, lobe-finned fishes, and early sharks.
- **End-Devonian extinction** — Placoderms wiped out; bony fish become dominant.
- **Carboniferous** — Tetrapods evolve from lobe-finned fish, developing lungs homologous to swim bladders.
- **c. 3,500 BCE** — Fish farming (aquaculture) begins in ancient China.
- **1869** — Suez Canal opens, enabling Lessepsian migration of Indo-Pacific species into the Mediterranean.
- **1960s** — Nile perch introduced to Lake Victoria, causing collapse of endemic cichlid populations.
- **2017** — FAO reports 34% of global marine fish stocks classified as overfished.
- **2020** — Global capture: 65 Mt marine, 10 Mt freshwater; aquaculture: ~50 Mt (mainly freshwater).
- **2025** — IUCN Red List names 2,367 fish species as endangered or critically endangered.

## Body

### Evolution and Diversity
Fish originated during the Cambrian explosion (~530 Mya) as small filter feeders with notochords. The Silurian saw the emergence of jaws (placoderms, Chondrichthyes, Osteichthyes), and the Devonian ("Age of Fishes") brought massive diversification. The end-Devonian extinction eliminated placoderms, allowing bony fish (Osteichthyes)—divided into lobe-finned (Sarcopterygii) and ray-finned (Actinopterygii)—to dominate. Teleosts, a crown group of ray-finned fish capable of protruding their jaws, now comprise ~96% of living fish species. Tetrapods evolved from lobe-finned fish during the Carboniferous; their limbs and lungs are homologous to fish fins and swim bladders. Because any clade containing all bony fish also contains tetrapods, "fish" is a paraphyletic group, rendering the traditional class *Pisces* obsolete in formal taxonomy.

Extant fish are unevenly distributed: over 32,000 bony fish species, ~1,100 cartilaginous fish, and ~100 jawless hagfish and lampreys (2016 data). The nine largest families (e.g., Cyprinidae, Gobiidae, Cichlidae) contain a third of all species. Size ranges from the 16 m whale shark to 8 mm teleosts (*Paedocypris progenetica*). Habitats split roughly equally between freshwater (~15,200 species) and marine (~14,800 species), with diversity hotspots in Indo-Pacific coral reefs and tropical river basins (Amazon, Congo, Mekong). Fish occupy nearly all aquatic environments, from high mountain streams to hadal depths (record: *Abyssobrotula galatheae* at 8,370 m), though none inhabit the deepest 25% of the ocean.

### Anatomy and Physiology
**Locomotion & Buoyancy:** Typical fish swim by alternating muscle contractions forming S-curves that propagate to the caudal fin. Paired and median fins act as control surfaces. Because body tissue is denser than water, many bony fish use a **swim bladder** (gas-filled organ) to adjust buoyancy; cartilaginous fish lack this and rely on large, oil-filled livers and dynamic lift. Scales provide protection and, via silvering, camouflage in open water.

**Circulation:** Fish possess a **single-loop circulatory system**: heart → gills (oxygenation) → body tissues → heart. This differs from the mammalian double loop. Oxygen-rich blood flows to tissues without additional pumping.

**Respiration:** Most fish use **gills** (filaments with capillary networks) for countercurrent gas exchange: water flows one way, blood the opposite, maximizing oxygen uptake. Cartilaginous fish have multiple exposed gill slits (usually 5 pairs) and often must swim to ventilate (ram ventilation). Bony fish have a single gill opening per side covered by a bony **operculum** and use buccal pumping (head muscles) to ventilate. ~400 species in 50 families can breathe air (e.g., lungfish, bichirs, mudskippers), using modified swim bladders, lungs, or digestive tracts to survive hypoxic water or emerge onto land.

**Digestion & Excretion:** The gut runs mouth → esophagus → stomach → intestine → anus. Pyloric caeca (finger-like pouches) of uncertain function often branch near the stomach. The liver produces bile for fat emulsification. Most fish excrete nitrogenous waste as ammonia, primarily across gills. Kidneys manage osmoregulation: saltwater fish lose water osmotically and produce concentrated urine; freshwater fish gain water and produce dilute urine.

**Nervous System & Senses:** Fish brains are typically small (~1/15th of similar-sized bird/mammal), but mormyrids and sharks have relatively large brains. Key structures: olfactory lobes (smell; huge in hagfish/sharks), telencephalon (olfaction processing), optic lobes (vision; large in visual hunters), cerebellum (balance/swimming; huge in mormyrids for electrosense), and brain stem (respiration/osmoregulation). Sensory systems include: **lateral line** (detects water currents/vibrations; navigation in blind cave fish), **ampullae of Lorenzini** (electroreception in sharks/catfish), **vision** (spherical lens; many have color/UV/polarized light vision), **hearing** (otoliths + lateral line/swim bladder), and **magnetoreception** (e.g., salmon; mechanism unknown).

**Cognition & Special Abilities:** Fish demonstrate self-awareness (mirror tests in manta rays, wrasses), tool use (choerodon wrasse, archerfish), spatial memory (maze learning), and sentience (pain perception). **Electrogenesis**: some fish (elephantfishes, electric eels) generate electric fields for navigation/communication in turbid water; strongly electric fish (electric eel) produce shocks to stun prey. **Endothermy**: most fish are ectothermic, but Scombroidei (tunas, billfishes), opah (whole-body), and some sharks (Lamnidae, Alopiidae) are endothermic, maintaining elevated core temperatures (up to 20°C above ambient) via countercurrent heat exchangers.

**Reproduction:** Over 97% are **oviparous** (external fertilization, eggs shed into water). Marine species release many small eggs yielding planktonic larvae with yolk sacs; larvae metamorphose to juveniles in weeks. Some are **oviparous with internal fertilization** (skates, using claspers). **Viviparous** species (surf-perches, lemon sharks) retain embryos and nourish them via placental analogs. **DNA repair** pathways in embryos mitigate environmental genotoxicity (UV, pollutants); zebrafish are a model for genotoxicity testing.

**Immunity:** Fish have non-specific defenses (skin, scales, antimicrobial mucus) and specific adaptive immunity. Jawless fish use **Variable Lymphocyte Receptors (VLRs)**; jawed fish use **V(D)J recombination** (B/T cells with immunoglobulins/T-cell receptors), a system basal to all jawed vertebrates. Immune organs vary: jawless fish (anterior kidney, gut); cartilaginous fish (epigonal organs, Leydig's organ, spiral valve); teleosts (thymus, spleen, kidney).

### Behavior
**Shoaling & Schooling:** A **shoal** is a loose social group; a **school** is a polarized, synchronized group. Schooling enhances predator vigilance and foraging efficiency. Collective antipredator tactics include scattering/reassembling. Fish also aggregate to spawn (e.g., capelin migrations).

**Communication:** Fish produce **acoustic signals** for feeding, aggression, and courtship. **Stridulatory sounds**: grinding bones/teeth (e.g., French grunt *Haemulon flavolineatum* at ~700 Hz; seahorse *Hippocampus reidi* clicks/growls via coronet-neurocranium friction). **Non-stridulatory sounds**: swim bladder vibration via sonic muscles (e.g., oyster toadfish grunts/boat whistles 140–260 Hz; red drum *Sciaenops ocellatus* drumming 100–>200 Hz). Sound production is often sex-specific (e.g., only male toadfish produce boat whistles; female red drum lack sonic muscles).

### Conservation
**Overfishing:** FAO (2017) classified 34% of marine stocks as overfished. Collapses occur when survivors cannot replace removals (e.g., Pacific sardine: 800,000 t in 1937 → 24,000 t in 1968; NW Atlantic cod: 1% of historical biomass by 1992). Commercial extinction ≠ biological extinction. Management conflicts exist between industry employment and scientific warnings of stock destruction within 50 years.

**Other Threats:** Habitat degradation (pollution, dams, water extraction, invasive species) disproportionately affects freshwater endemics (e.g., 3 of 10 Spanish endemic freshwater fishes threatened). Dams (Kariba, Aswan) reduced catches. Bottom trawling damages seabeds (Georges Bank). Invasive species alter ecosystems: comb jelly collapsed Black Sea anchovy fishery; Suez Canal (1869) enabled Lessepsian migration, transforming Mediterranean biodiversity; Nile perch introduction to Lake Victoria (1960s) collapsed ~500 endemic cichlid species, simplifying the fishery to three species.

### Importance to Humans
**Economic:** Fish provide ~1/6 of global animal protein (2007 estimate). Wild capture dominates historically, but aquaculture (since ~3,500 BCE in China) is rising. 2020 production: 65 Mt marine capture, 10 Mt freshwater capture, ~50 Mt aquaculture (mostly freshwater). Top marine captures: anchoveta (4.9 Mt), Alaska pollock (3.5 Mt), skipjack tuna (2.8 Mt).

**Recreation:** Recreational fishing (pleasure/competition) contrasts with commercial (profit) and artisanal (food) fishing. Popular in North America/Europe; involves rod/reel/bait; governments manage target species.

**Culture:** Fish symbolize deities (Enki, Atargatis, Matsya, Kāmohoaliʻi), religious icons (Christian *ichthys*, Jonah), and constellations (Pisces). They feature in art/literature (*The Old Man and the Sea*) and film (*Finding Nemo*, *Jaws*). Healers in ancient Mesopotamia wore fish-garb; Oannes was said to wear fish skin.

## Terms
- ****Paraphyletic group**** — A taxonomic group that includes an ancestor but not all its descendants (e.g., fish = all vertebrates except tetrapods).
- ****Teleost**** — A crown group of ray-finned fish (Actinopterygii) characterized by protrusible jaws; comprises ~96% of living fish species.
- ****Swim bladder**** — A gas-filled internal organ in bony fish used to control buoyancy; homologous to tetrapod lungs.
- ****Operculum**** — A bony flap covering and protecting the gills in bony fish; enables buccal pumping for respiration without swimming.
- ****Countercurrent exchange**** — A mechanism where fluid flows in opposite directions across a membrane (e.g., water vs. blood in gills), maximizing diffusion efficiency.
- ****Oviparous**** — Reproductive mode where eggs are fertilized and develop outside the mother's body (over 97% of fish).
- ****Viviparous**** — Reproductive mode where embryos develop inside the mother and receive nutrition via a placental analog (e.g., some sharks, surf-perches).
- ****Lateral line**** — A sensory system of neuromasts in the skin detecting water currents, vibrations, and nearby movement; functions in schooling, predation, and navigation.
- ****Ampullae of Lorenzini**** — Electroreceptors (in sharks, rays, catfish) detecting weak electric fields (millivolt range) for prey detection and navigation.
- ****Endothermy**** — The ability to maintain body temperature above ambient via metabolic heat production; found in tunas, billfishes, opah, and some sharks.

## Debates and open questions
- **Phylogenetic classification**: Whether to treat Osteichthyes as a clade including tetrapods (making "fish" a grade) or maintain traditional paraphyletic groupings; *Fishes of the World* notes increasing acceptance of the former.
- **Magnetoreception mechanism**: The physical basis (e.g., quantum radical pair vs. magnetite) remains unknown despite behavioral evidence in salmon and other species.
- **Fisheries management**: Sharp disagreement between fisheries scientists (advocating stringent protection) and the fishing industry (emphasizing resiliency and employment) on sustainable harvest levels.
- **Conservation data gaps**: Fish are harder to study than terrestrial taxa; population data is often lacking, especially for freshwater and deep-sea species, hindering Red List assessments.
- **Pyloric caeca function**: The role of these intestinal pouches in digestion remains "doubtful" per the source text.
