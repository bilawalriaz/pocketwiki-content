# Caral–Supe civilization

## Overview
Caral–Supe (also called Norte Chico) was a complex pre-Columbian society in north-central coastal Peru, flourishing between the 4th and 2nd millennia BCE. It is recognized as the oldest-known civilization in the Americas and one of the six sites where civilization independently originated in the ancient world. Its development, marked by monumental architecture and a lack of ceramics, challenges traditional models of how complex societies arise.

## Timeline
- **c. 3500 BCE** — Formation of the first city at Huaricanga in the Fortaleza area.
- **3200 BCE onward** — Large-scale human settlement and communal construction become clearly apparent.
- **2627–2020 BCE** — Period of major habitation at the central site of Caral.
- **c. 2500–2000 BCE** — Greatest expansion; population and development shift decisively toward inland sites.
- **c. 2200 BCE** — Influence spreads along the coast; linked to abandonment of Caral due to a prolonged drought (4.2-kiloyear climate event).
- **c. 1800 BCE** — Civilization begins to decline as more powerful centers emerge elsewhere.

## Body

### Geography and Environment
The civilization flourished along three river valleys—Fortaleza, Pativilca, and Supe—with associated sites along the Huaura River. This coastal region is extremely arid, but development was made possible by irrigation from over 50 rivers carrying Andean snowmelt. Monumental architecture at all sites is found close to irrigation channels.

### Diet and Subsistence
The diet included domesticated plants like squash, beans, and sweet potato, alongside a significant seafood component (anchovies, sardines, clams). Maize was also widely grown and consumed from 3000 to 1800 BCE. Cotton (*Gossypium barbadense*) was a vital non-food crop, essential for making fishing nets and textiles, creating a mutual dependency between coastal fishing and inland agricultural communities.

### Architecture and Society
Caral–Supe is known for its dense concentration of large sites with monumental architecture, including rectangular platform mounds and sunken circular plazas. The largest pyramid at Caral measures 160 by 150 meters and rises 18 meters high. Construction used quarried stone and river cobbles packed in reed "shicra-bags." The scale of these projects suggests centralized leadership capable of mobilizing large labor forces. Evidence points to economic power based on controlling cotton and trade, and ideological power possibly linked to common deity symbols like the Staff God. Notably, there is no evidence of warfare, defensive structures, or physical coercion.

### Absent Technologies and Record-Keeping
The civilization completely lacked ceramics and visual arts like sculpture. However, it had sophisticated textile technology and may have used quipu (knotted string devices) for record-keeping, suggesting a proto-writing system. Musical instruments, such as flutes made from pelican bone, have also been found.

## Terms
- **Pre-ceramic**: A culture that does not produce or use pottery.
- **Platform mound**: A large, flat-topped earthen or stone structure built for ceremonial or elite residential purposes.
- **Sunken circular plaza**: A depressed, circular ceremonial space often found adjacent to platform mounds.
- **Quipu (khipu)**: A recording device made of knotted strings, used for storing information, possibly including numeric and logographic data.
- **Staff God**: A major deity in later Andean cultures, depicted as a figure holding staffs; its earliest known depiction is from Caral–Supe.
- **Maritime Foundation of Andean Civilization (MFAC)**: A theory that the civilization's rise was based primarily on a maritime (seafood) subsistence economy.
- **4.2-kiloyear climate event**: A severe, global drought event around 2200 BCE linked to the abandonment of Caral.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Maritime vs. Agricultural Foundation**: A central debate is whether the civilization was founded on maritime resources (the MFAC theory) or on agriculture. While seafood was significant, dietary analysis shows plant foods dominated the diet at both coastal and inland sites.
- **Coastal vs. Inland Primacy**: Scholars dispute whether coastal sites like Aspero developed first and influenced inland sites, or if inland centers like Caral were dominant from the start. Radiocarbon dating suggests contemporaneous development, but the relationship remains contested.
- **Political Organization**: The exact nature of its government is unclear. While economic and ideological power bases are suggested, the absence of evidence for warfare or physical coercion challenges standard models of state formation.
- **Credit and Attribution**: The discovery and naming of the civilization have been the subject of a significant academic dispute, particularly between Peruvian archaeologist Ruth Shady and American researchers Jonathan Haas and Winifred Creamer, involving allegations of plagiarism and insufficient credit.
