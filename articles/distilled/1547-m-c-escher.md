# M. C. Escher

## Overview
Maurits Cornelis Escher (1898–1972) was a Dutch graphic artist whose woodcuts, lithographs, and mezzotints explore mathematical concepts—impossible objects, infinity, symmetry, tessellation, and hyperbolic geometry—despite his lack of formal mathematical training. Long neglected by the art establishment, which found his work too intellectual and insufficiently lyrical, he was embraced by mathematicians and scientists, notably after Martin Gardner featured him in *Scientific American* (1966). His visual intuition anticipated research in crystallography, topology, and the representation of infinity on a plane, and his imagery permeated popular culture through album covers, book illustrations, and Douglas Hofstadter’s *Gödel, Escher, Bach* (1979).

## Timeline
- **17 June 1898** — Born in Leeuwarden, Friesland, Netherlands.
- **1919–1922** — Attends Haarlem School of Architecture and Decorative Arts; studies woodcut under Samuel Jessurun de Mesquita; switches from architecture to decorative arts.
- **1922** — Travels through Italy and Spain; visits the Alhambra in Granada, triggering lifelong fascination with tessellation.
- **1923–1935** — Lives in Rome; marries Jetta Umiker (1924); three sons born; extensive sketching trips across Italy, Corsica, Sicily.
- **May–June 1936** — Returns to Alhambra and Mezquita of Córdoba; makes detailed drawings of mosaic patterns; becomes "addicted" to tessellation.
- **1935** — Leaves Italy due to Mussolini’s political climate; moves to Château-d’Œx, Switzerland.
- **1937** — Moves to Uccle, Belgium; art shifts from observational to geometric imagination; begins woodcuts based on 17 wallpaper groups.
- **Jan 1941** — Flees WWII to Baarn, Netherlands; most famous works created during this period.
- **1954** — International Congress of Mathematicians in Amsterdam; Escher’s work displayed; meets Roger Penrose and H.S.M. Coxeter.
- **1957–1960** — Corresponds with Coxeter on hyperbolic geometry; creates *Circle Limit I–IV* wood engravings.
- **1955 / 1967** — Awarded Knighthood, then Officer, of the Order of Orange-Nassau.
- **July 1969** — Completes final work, *Snakes* (woodcut, threefold rotational symmetry, nine print operations).
- **1970** — Moves to Rosa Spier Huis, Laren (artists’ retirement home).
- **27 March 1972** — Dies in Hilversum, aged 73; buried in Baarn.

## Body

### Early life and education
Escher was the youngest son of civil engineer George Arnold Escher. A sickly child who failed second grade, he excelled only at drawing. After a brief stint at the Technical College of Delft (1918), he enrolled at the Haarlem School of Architecture and Decorative Arts (1919–1922). He failed architecture courses, partly due to a persistent skin infection, and switched to decorative arts under graphic artist Samuel Jessurun de Mesquita, who taught him woodcut technique.

### Formative travels and the Alhambra revelation
In 1922 Escher toured Italy and Spain. The Moorish architecture of the 14th-century Alhambra in Granada, with its interlocking geometric tile patterns based on symmetries, "triggered his interest in the mathematics of tessellation." He settled in Rome (1923–1935), marrying Jetta Umiker in 1924, and traveled extensively through Italy, Corsica, and Sicily, sketching townscapes and landscapes that fed his early observational work. A pivotal return to the Alhambra and the Mezquita of Córdoba in 1936 produced detailed studies of mosaic patterns; he described tessellation as "a real mania to which I have become addicted." After 1937, he worked exclusively in his studio, shifting from observation to geometric analysis and visual imagination.

### Political displacement and mature period
Unwilling to accept the fascist climate in Italy—his son was forced to wear a Balilla uniform—Escher moved the family to Switzerland (1935), then Belgium (1937), and finally Baarn, Netherlands (January 1941), where he remained until 1970. The Dutch climate allowed intense focus. He designed Dutch postage stamps (1935, 1949) and lectured widely after 1953; a planned 1962 North American tour was cancelled by illness, but the material became the book *Escher on Escher*.

### Tessellation: "Regular Division of the Plane"
Escher called tessellation "Regular Division of the Plane," titling his 1958 book thus. After 1936, he used geometric grids to create interlocking designs of birds, fish, and reptiles (e.g., *Study of Regular Division of the Plane with Reptiles*, 1939; *Reptiles* lithograph, 1943). His brother Berend, a geologist, sent him papers by mathematician George Pólya and crystallographer Friedrich Haag on plane symmetry groups. Escher mastered the 17 canonical wallpaper groups, producing 43 drawings of distinct symmetry types and developing his own notation. Mathematician Doris Schattschneider characterized his 1941–42 sketchbook as "mathematical research" addressing: (1) possible tile shapes for regular plane division, and (2) how tile edges relate by isometries. *Metamorphosis I* (1937) began a narrative series transforming polygons into figurative motifs; *Metamorphosis III* extends nearly seven metres.

### Impossible geometries and perspective play
Escher’s first "impossible reality" print was *Still Life and Street* (1937). *Relativity* (1953) and *House of Stairs* (1951) feature multiple gravitational perspectives. *House of Stairs* attracted Roger and Lionel Penrose, who published "Impossible Objects: A Special Type of Visual Illusion" (1956), introducing the Penrose triangle (tribar). Escher incorporated the tribar into *Waterfall* (1961), a perpetual-motion machine, and created *Ascending and Descending* (1960) with endless staircases. He admired Piranesi’s *Carceri* prints for their complex architectural perspectives and acknowledged forerunners Parmigianino (spherical reflection) and Hogarth (false perspective). Though contemporaneous with Surrealism and Op art, he remained independent of movements.

### Platonic solids and dimensionality
Escher frequently embedded Platonic solids (tetrahedrons, cubes), stellated polyhedra, cylinders, and spheres. In *Reptiles* (1943), two-dimensional lizards crawl off the page into three dimensions, then return. He wrote: "The flat shape irritates me… I make them come out of the plane." *Gravitation* shows animals climbing a stellated dodecahedron. *Waterfall*’s towers are topped with a compound of three cubes and a stellated rhombic dodecahedron—now called "Escher’s solid"—first used in *Stars* (1948), which contains all five Platonic solids animated by chameleons. An amateur astronomer with a 6 cm refracting telescope, he recorded binary-star observations.

### Levels of reality
Works such as *Drawing Hands* (1948)—two hands drawing each other—embody Escher’s fascination with the coexistence of the flat plane and illusory volume. Critic Steven Poole noted the "black magic of the artistic illusion made creepily manifest." Escher described himself as a "reality enthusiast," combining "formal astonishment with a vivid and idiosyncratic vision."

### Infinity and hyperbolic geometry
At the 1954 International Congress of Mathematicians in Amsterdam, Roger Penrose and H.S.M. Coxeter were struck by Escher’s intuitive grasp. Inspired by *Relativity*, the Penroses devised the tribar and endless staircase; Escher closed the cycle with *Waterfall* and *Ascending and Descending*. In 1957 Coxeter sent Escher a figure of a hyperbolic tessellation—tiles shrinking toward a circle’s edge—which "gave me quite a shock" as the precise means to represent infinity on a plane. Escher analyzed the construction, sent Coxeter a diagram (confirmed correct), and persisted in "Coxetering," producing the *Circle Limit I–IV* wood engravings. Coxeter later declared them "absolutely right to the millimeter."

### Printmaking technique
Escher worked primarily in lithography and woodcut; his few mezzotints are considered masterpieces. *Snakes* (1969), his final work, required three blocks rotated three times each (nine print operations per impression) to achieve perfect alignment of shrinking rings toward both center and edge, encapsulating his mastery of symmetry and infinity.

## Terms
- **Tessellation (Regular Division of the Plane)**: Covering a plane with congruent shapes (tiles) leaving no gaps or overlaps; Escher’s term for his systematic interlocking patterns.
- **Wallpaper groups**: The 17 mathematically distinct symmetry groups for periodic tilings of the plane; Escher mastered all 17.
- **Isometry**: A distance-preserving transformation (translation, rotation, reflection, glide reflection) relating tile edges in a tessellation.
- **Impossible object**: A two-dimensional figure perceived as a three-dimensional object that cannot exist in Euclidean space (e.g., Penrose triangle, endless staircase).
- **Hyperbolic geometry**: Non-Euclidean geometry with constant negative curvature; allows infinite regular tilings within a finite circle (Poincaré disk model).
- **Platonic solids**: The five convex regular polyhedra (tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, icosahedron).
- **Stellated polyhedron**: A polyhedron extended by lengthening faces or edges to form a star-like shape (e.g., stellated dodecahedron).
- **Möbius strip**: A surface with only one side and one boundary component; featured in *Möbius Strip II* (1963).
- **Curvilinear perspective**: A method of representing 3D space on a 2D plane using curved lines rather than straight vanishing lines; explored with art historian Albert Flocon.
- **Metamorphosis (in Escher’s work)**: A visual narrative where one tessellated motif gradually transforms into another across a single composition.

## Debates and open questions
- **Art-world reception**: Critics long dismissed Escher as "too intellectual and insufficiently lyrical"; conceptual art’s rise did not fully rehabilitate him because traditional critics still disliked his narrative themes and perspective games.
- **Mathematical status**: Escher insisted he had "no mathematical ability," yet Schattschneider and Coxeter judged his investigations as genuine mathematical research. The tension between intuitive visual reasoning and formal proof remains a point of discussion.
- **Priority and influence**: The Penrose triangle and endless staircase were devised by the Penroses after seeing *Relativity*; Escher then used them in *Waterfall* and *Ascending and Descending*. The cycle of mutual inspiration raises questions about the boundary between artistic intuition and mathematical discovery.
- **Completeness of *Circle Limit* series**: Escher produced four *Circle Limit* woodcuts (I–IV); whether he considered the series finished or would have continued "Coxetering" is unrecorded.
- **Legacy in science**: Schattschneider identified eleven research strands anticipated by Escher (e.g., color symmetry, local vs. global regularity, Escher’s algorithm for pattern generation); the extent to which he directly inspired versus anticipated these remains a scholarly topic.
