# Mikhail Lomonosov

## Overview
Mikhail Lomonosov (1711–1765) was a Russian polymath whose work spanned science, literature, and education. He is considered a founder of modern geology and a key figure in the development of the Russian literary language. His scientific contributions, including early formulations of the law of conservation of mass and the theoretical prediction of Antarctica, were foundational to Russian science, though many were not widely recognized internationally until after his death.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Lomonosov was born to a prosperous peasant fisherman in northern Russia. Self-taught from religious texts, he walked to Moscow in 1730 to pursue education, gaining admission to the Slavic Greek Latin Academy by falsely claiming noble birth. His academic talent earned him a scholarship to the St. Petersburg Academy and a four-year grant to study in Germany. At the University of Marburg, he studied philosophy under Christian Wolff, a key figure of the German Enlightenment, which profoundly influenced his scientific and administrative thinking. He later studied mineralogy in Freiberg but left without permission after disputes with his instructor. He married in Marburg and returned to Russia in 1741.

### Scientific Contributions
Lomonosov made significant contributions across multiple sciences. In physics, he conducted experiments in 1756 that challenged Robert Boyle's work and anticipated the law of conservation of mass, stating that matter is neither created nor destroyed in chemical reactions. He proposed a corpuscular theory of matter, viewing heat as motion and suggesting a wave theory of light. In astronomy, he claimed to discover the atmosphere of Venus during its 1761 transit, though modern analysis suggests he likely observed a solar flash, not the atmospheric arc. He also designed an improved reflecting telescope. In geology, his 1763 work *On The Strata of the Earth* argued for explaining the planet's past through present processes, placing him as a pioneer before James Hutton. He demonstrated the organic origin of coal and petroleum and theoretically predicted the existence of Antarctica based on iceberg formation.

### Literature and Legacy
Beyond science, Lomonosov shaped the modern Russian literary language. He developed a system of Russian versification based on accentual-syllabic meter, wrote influential odes, and authored the first Russian grammar and rhetoric textbooks. He established a three-style theory (high, middle, low) for literary composition. He co-founded Moscow University in 1755, which was later named after him. His legacy includes numerous institutions, geographic features, and awards bearing his name, cementing his status as the "Father of Russian Science."

## Terms
*   **Polymath:** A person of wide-ranging learning or expertise.
*   **Phlogiston theory:** An obsolete scientific theory that a fire-like element called phlogiston is released during combustion.
*   **Law of conservation of mass:** A fundamental principle stating that mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
*   **Corpuscle:** In Lomonosov's theory, a "collection" of elements forming a small mass, later termed a molecule.
*   **Transit of Venus:** The passage of Venus across the face of the Sun, used historically to measure astronomical distances.
*   **Accentual-syllabic verse:** A poetic meter based on both the number of syllables and the pattern of stressed syllables.
*   **Table of Ranks:** A formal list of positions and honors in the Russian Empire, establishing a hierarchy of civil and military service.

## Debates and Open questions
The primary scholarly debate concerns Lomonosov's 1761 observation of Venus. While long credited with discovering its atmosphere, modern research suggests he likely saw a bright flash of the solar photosphere, not the thin arc caused by atmospheric refraction. His original sketches are lost, and his written descriptions do not clearly match the atmospheric phenomenon. The first confirmed observations of the Venusian atmospheric arc were made by other astronomers during the 1769 transit.
