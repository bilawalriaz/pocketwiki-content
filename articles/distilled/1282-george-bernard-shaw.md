# George Bernard Shaw

## Overview
George Bernard Shaw (1856–1950) was an Irish-born British playwright, critic, and political activist whose works reshaped English-language theatre. Insisting on being called "Bernard Shaw," he wrote over sixty plays—including *Man and Superman*, *Pygmalion*, and *Saint Joan*—and won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1925. His plays fused satire with social commentary, promoting Fabian socialism and challenging Victorian norms. Shaw’s influence extended beyond drama into politics, music criticism, and public intellectual life, leaving a legacy encapsulated in the term "Shavian."

## Body

### Early Life and Self-Education
Shaw was born into a declining Protestant family in Dublin. His father, George Carr Shaw, was an alcoholic civil servant turned corn merchant. Shaw’s mother, Bessie, had a close relationship with George John Lee, a musician, leading to lifelong speculation about Shaw’s paternity. Raised in a musically rich environment, Shaw developed a deep appreciation for opera and literature. He despised formal schooling, attending four schools between 1865 and 1871, all of which he found stifling. After leaving school, he worked as a junior clerk in a Dublin land agency before moving to London in 1876 following his sister’s death.

In London, Shaw struggled financially while pursuing writing. He gained access to the British Museum Reading Room, immersing himself in self-directed learning. He wrote two novels—*Immaturity* and *The Irrational Knot*—neither of which found immediate publishers. During this time, he also ghostwrote for Lee and worked briefly at the Edison Telephone Company. His political awakening began in 1882 after hearing Henry George speak, leading him to explore Marxist economics and join the Fabian Society in 1884.

### Political Awakening and Fabianism
Shaw joined the Fabian Society in 1884, quickly becoming its most influential pamphleteer. He rejected Marxism in favor of gradualist socialism, advocating for change through permeation—infiltrating existing institutions rather than revolutionary upheaval. This approach was solidified during debates within the Fabians over anarchism and reinforced after the violent suppression of a Trafalgar Square rally in 1887. Shaw helped shape the society’s intellectual direction, contributing essays to *Fabian Essays in Socialism* (1889) and supporting the founding of the London School of Economics in 1895.

He remained active in Fabian politics through the early 1900s, opposing the Boer War and criticizing both Liberal and Labour leaders. However, by 1911, he had grown disillusioned with the Fabians’ limited impact and stepped down from the executive committee. Despite this, he continued to contribute financially and ideologically, investing in *The New Statesman* in 1912.

### Literary Career and Critical Work
Shaw’s early attempts at fiction were unsuccessful, but his career as a critic flourished. From 1884 to 1894, he wrote music and drama reviews for *The World* and *The Star*, earning a reputation for incisive analysis and advocacy for Wagner over Brahms. His theatrical criticism championed Ibsen and condemned Victorian melodrama, laying the groundwork for his own dramatic innovations.

His first play, *Widowers’ Houses* (1892), introduced his “Plays Unpleasant” trilogy, followed by the “Plays Pleasant” series, including *Arms and the Man* (1894). These works used humor and irony to critique class, gender, and morality. Shaw’s breakthrough came with *Man and Superman* (1902), a philosophical comedy exploring evolution and human nature. The Royal Court Theatre’s productions in the 1900s cemented his status, with *Major Barbara* (1905), *The Doctor’s Dilemma* (1906), and *Caesar and Cleopatra* (1907) achieving both critical acclaim and popular success.

### Political Stances and Controversies
Shaw’s public statements often sparked controversy. During World War I, he published *Common Sense About the War* (1914), blaming all warring nations equally—a stance that alienated many contemporaries. He supported Irish Home Rule but opposed full independence, proposing a federal solution in *How to Settle the Irish Question* (1917). His admiration for figures like Mussolini and Stalin in the 1920s and 1930s reflected his growing belief in authoritarianism as a means to achieve social progress.

He also promoted eugenics, alphabet reform, and opposed vaccination and organized religion. These views, while reflective of his era, have since drawn sharp criticism. Nonetheless, Shaw remained productive, writing plays like *The Apple Cart* (1929) and *Too True to Be Good* (1931), and adapting *Pygmalion* into a successful film in 1938.

### Later Years and Death
In his final decades, Shaw continued writing plays and political treatises, including *Everybody’s Political What’s What* (1944). He declined a knighthood and the Order of Merit, believing artistic merit should be judged posthumously. He died in 1950 at age 94, having refused state honors. His ashes were scattered in the garden at Shaw’s Corner alongside those of his wife, Charlotte.

## Terms
- **Fabian Society**: A British socialist organization advocating gradual reform rather than revolution.
- **Gradualism**: The policy of achieving socialism through incremental changes within existing systems.
- **Permeation**: A Fabian strategy of influencing society by introducing socialist ideas into mainstream institutions.
- **Shavian**: Term describing ideas or styles characteristic of Bernard Shaw.
- **Plays Unpleasant**: Shaw’s first trilogy of plays (*Widowers’ Houses*, *The Philanderer*, *Mrs Warren’s Profession*) designed to challenge societal norms.
- **Discussion Drama**: A theatrical form emphasizing debate and ideological exploration over plot.
- **Eugenics**: The study of improving human hereditary traits through selective breeding; Shaw was a proponent.
- **New Woman**: A feminist archetype in late Victorian literature representing independence and rebellion against traditional roles.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars continue to debate the extent of Shaw’s paternal uncertainty, particularly regarding George John Lee. His political legacy remains contentious—while praised for advancing socialist thought, his admiration for dictators and support for eugenics have tarnished his reputation. Critics also question whether his plays are timeless classics or products of their era. The interpretation of his religious views—from atheism to mysticism—remains fluid, as does the significance of his unconsummated marriage to Charlotte Payne-Townshend. Finally, the enduring relevance of his theatrical innovations versus accusations of didacticism fuel ongoing scholarly discourse.
