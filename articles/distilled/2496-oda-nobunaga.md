# Oda Nobunaga

## Overview
Oda Nobunaga (1534–1582) was a Japanese daimyō and the first of the three "Great Unifiers" of Japan. He rose from a minor lord in Owari Province to become the most powerful figure in the country by dismantling the Ashikaga Shogunate and conquering most of Honshu. His innovative military tactics, economic policies, and ruthless suppression of opposition paved the way for the eventual unification of Japan under his successors, Toyotomi Hideyoshi and Tokugawa Ieyasu.

## Timeline
- **1534** — Born in Nagoya, Owari Province.
- **1551** — Succeeds his father as head of the Oda clan after a succession crisis.
- **1560** — Defeats Imagawa Yoshimoto at the Battle of Okehazama, a pivotal victory.
- **1567** — Conquers Mino Province, renames Inabayama Castle to Gifu, and adopts the seal "Tenka Fubu."
- **1568** — Enters Kyoto and installs Ashikaga Yoshiaki as shogun.
- **1573** — Drives Ashikaga Yoshiaki from Kyoto, ending the Ashikaga Shogunate.
- **1571** — Burns the Enryaku-ji monastery on Mount Hiei.
- **1575** — Defeats the Takeda clan at the Battle of Nagashino.
- **1576–1579** — Builds Azuchi Castle on Lake Biwa.
- **1580** — The Ikkō-ikki surrender their stronghold at Ishiyama Hongan-ji.
- **1582** — Killed by his retainer Akechi Mitsuhide in the Honnō-ji Incident.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Born the heir of a powerful Owari clan, Nobunaga was known in his youth as the "Fool of Owari" for his eccentric behavior. After securing his clan's leadership following his father's death in 1551, he consolidated power in Owari. His breakthrough came in 1560 at the Battle of Okehazama, where he used a surprise attack to defeat the much larger army of Imagawa Yoshimoto. This victory established his reputation and allowed him to form key alliances, including one with Matsudaira Motoyasu (the future Tokugawa Ieyasu). He then conquered Mino Province in 1567, moving his base to Gifu and declaring his ambition to unify Japan under the banner of "Tenka Fubu" (All the world by force of arms).

### Unification Campaigns and the End of the Shogunate
In 1568, Nobunaga marched to Kyoto to install Ashikaga Yoshiaki as shogun, but their alliance soon soured. Nobunaga refused to be subordinate, leading Yoshiaki to form an anti-Nobunaga coalition. Nobunaga systematically defeated his enemies, destroying the Asakura and Azai clans by 1573. That same year, he drove Yoshiaki from Kyoto, effectively ending the Ashikaga Shogunate. He then focused on crushing the Ikkō-ikki, a Buddhist militia. After a brutal campaign that included the razing of Mount Hiei (1571) and the decade-long Siege of Ishiyama Hongan-ji (1570–1580), he crippled their power. He also defeated the Takeda clan at the Battle of Nagashino (1575) and repelled challenges from the Mōri and Uesugi clans.

### Policies and Innovations
Nobunaga's rule was marked by revolutionary policies. Militarily, he was an early and massive adopter of firearms (tanegashima), using massed volley fire to devastating effect. He also pioneered large-scale civil engineering on battlefields, building roads and siege works. Economically, he abolished internal tariffs and promoted *rakuichi rakuza* (free markets), breaking the power of traditional guilds to stimulate commerce. He implemented land surveys (*Oda Kenchi*) and monetary reforms to stabilize the economy. Politically, he promoted a merit-based system for retainers, elevating men like Hideyoshi and Mitsuhide regardless of birth. He built the magnificent Azuchi Castle as a symbol of his power and cultural patronage.

### Death and Succession
At the height of his power in 1582, Nobunaga was betrayed by his general Akechi Mitsuhide. While staying at Honnō-ji temple in Kyoto with a minimal guard, Nobunaga was ambushed. After a brief resistance, he committed seppuku. His heir, Nobutada, was also killed. Mitsuhide's rebellion was short-lived; he was soon defeated by Toyotomi Hideyoshi, who then took control of Nobunaga's domain and completed the unification of Japan.

## Terms
*   **Daimyō:** A powerful feudal lord in pre-modern Japan who ruled over a large domain.
*   **Sengoku Period:** The "Warring States" period (c. 1467–1615) of constant civil war and social upheaval.
*   **Tenka Fubu:** Nobunaga's motto, meaning "All the world by force of arms," signifying his goal of unifying Japan through military power.
*   **Ikkō-ikki:** A major armed resistance movement composed of followers of the Jōdo Shinshū Buddhist sect.
*   **Tanegashima:** The Japanese matchlock gun, introduced by the Portuguese in 1543.
*   **Rakuichi Rakuza:** An economic policy abolishing guild monopolies and establishing free markets.
*   **Seppuku:** Ritual suicide by disembowelment, traditionally performed by samurai to preserve honor.
*   **Azuchi-Momoyama Period:** The historical era (c. 1573–1600) named for the castles of Nobunaga and Hideyoshi, marking the end of the Sengoku period.
*   **Ashikaga Shogunate:** The feudal military government (1336–1573) that Nobunaga effectively ended.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Relationship with the Shogunate:** Scholars debate whether Nobunaga initially intended to rule through the Ashikaga Shogunate as a puppet government or always planned to overthrow it. Recent theories suggest the breakdown was due to Yoshiaki's betrayal rather than Nobunaga's premeditated plan.
*   **Attitude toward the Imperial Court:** While conventionally seen as pressuring the court, newer research indicates Nobunaga may have consistently valued and financially supported the Emperor and court nobles to enhance his own legitimacy.
*   **Motives for Self-Deification:** According to the Jesuit Luís Fróis, Nobunaga attempted to deify himself during his lifetime. The authenticity of this claim is doubted by many researchers due to a lack of Japanese sources, though the existence of a related stone deity is mentioned in some chronicles.
*   **Nature of His Reforms:** Historiographical views have shifted. Post-WWII scholarship often portrayed Nobunaga as a revolutionary who destroyed the old order. However, more recent research suggests he may have been a conservative who worked within existing systems and promoted gradual reform.
