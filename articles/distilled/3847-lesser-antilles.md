# Lesser Antilles

## Overview
The Lesser Antilles is a volcanic island arc in the Caribbean Sea, forming the eastern boundary of the Caribbean Plate. It is distinguished from the larger Greater Antilles to the west and is geographically and culturally significant as a region of diverse political entities and a complex history of indigenous settlement and European colonization.

## Timeline
- **c. 7700–6100 B.P.** — Earliest Archaic Age habitation evidenced at sites like Banwari Trace in Trinidad.
- **c. 400–200 BC** — Initial sphere of interaction and migration from coastal South America begins, marked by Saladoid pottery.
- **1493** — Christopher Columbus reaches the Lesser Antilles on his second voyage.
- **1625** — French merchant Pierre Belain d'Esnambuc sails to the Caribbean to establish a settlement.
- **1635** — Spain cedes Martinique to France; Belain establishes the first permanent French colony.
- **1797** — British forces begin occupying Trinidad.
- **1802** — Spain recognizes British sovereignty over Trinidad.
- **1917** — The United States purchases the Danish West Indies.
- **1938** — Venezuelan islands are divided into the State of Nueva Esparta and the Federal Dependencies.
- **1986** — Aruba secedes from the Netherlands Antilles.
- **1995** — The Soufrière Hills volcano on Montserrat becomes active, destroying the capital.
- **2007** — Saint Martin and Saint Barthélemy become separate overseas entities of France.
- **2010** — The remainder of the Netherlands Antilles is dissolved.

## Body

### Pre-Columbian History
The earliest inhabitants were from the Archaic Age (c. 3300 BC–AD 100), who relied on stone tools for hunting and fishing. Sites are concentrated in the northern islands and Trinidad, with evidence of early plant management. The Neolithic period (Early Ceramic Age) began around 400–200 BC with the arrival of Saladoid pottery from South America, indicating migration and cultural exchange. The dominant indigenous people at the time of European contact were the Kalinago.

### European Colonization and Political Evolution
The Spanish were the first Europeans to arrive, with Columbus claiming islands in 1493. Over the following centuries, Spanish, French, Dutch, and English powers fought for control. France established permanent colonies in the 17th century, such as on Martinique. Trinidad was created as a Spanish province in the 16th century and was ceded to Britain in 1802. The 20th century saw major political shifts, including the U.S. purchase of the Danish West Indies in 1917 and the gradual independence of British colonies. The region remains politically fragmented, with eight sovereign states and numerous dependencies of France, the Netherlands, the UK, the US, and Venezuela.

### Geography and Geology
The islands form a long arc between the Greater Antilles and South America. They are divided into the Leeward Islands (north), Windward Islands (south), and Leeward Antilles (west, off Venezuela). Geologically, most islands result from the subduction of the South American Plate under the Caribbean Plate, creating the Lesser Antilles subduction zone and associated volcanic and seismic activity. The climate is tropical maritime, with warm temperatures, high humidity, and distinct wet and dry seasons. Precipitation varies greatly due to orographic effects from trade winds.

## Terms
- **Antilles**: The collective name for the Greater Antilles and Lesser Antilles.
- **Archaic Age**: A pre-ceramic period characterized by stone and bone tools, with a subsistence economy based on hunting, fishing, and gathering.
- **Kalinago**: The dominant indigenous people of the Lesser Antilles at the time of European contact.
- **Leeward Islands**: The northern group of the Lesser Antilles, so named because they were downwind for sailing ships.
- **Windward Islands**: The southern group of the Lesser Antilles, upwind for sailing ships arriving from Europe.
- **Subduction zone**: A geological area where one tectonic plate sinks beneath another, causing volcanic and earthquake activity.
- **Orographic rainfall**: Precipitation caused when moist air is forced upward by mountains, cooling and condensing.
- **Saladoid**: A ceramic-age culture originating in South America, identified by its distinctive pottery.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact migration patterns into the southern Lesser Antilles during the Archaic Age are debated, with scenarios ranging from direct colonization from South America to bypassing the region entirely.
- There are controversies over conflicting stratigraphic data and radiocarbon dates regarding the timing and nature of the transition to the Early Ceramic Age and the interaction between Saladoid migrants and existing Archaic populations.
