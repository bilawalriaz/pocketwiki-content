# John Ford

## Overview
John Ford (1894–1973), born John Martin Feeney in Maine to Irish immigrants, was a defining American film director of Hollywood’s Golden Age. Over a 50-year career directing more than 130 films, he won a record four Academy Awards for Best Director (*The Informer*, *The Grapes of Wrath*, *How Green Was My Valley*, *The Quiet Man*). He is renowned for Westerns—*Stagecoach*, *The Searchers*, *The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance*—that used Monument Valley’s vast landscapes to frame characters against a harsh terrain, elevating the genre from B-movie status to artistic prestige. Ford cultivated a repertory "Stock Company" of actors (John Wayne, Henry Fonda, Ward Bond) and crew, forging a visual style marked by static long shots, deep focus, and expressive compositions influenced by F.W. Murnau. He served in the OSS during WWII, filming the Battle of Midway and D-Day, rising to Rear Admiral. Though his later years saw declining health and commercial struggles, his influence on global cinema—from Kurosawa and Welles to Spielberg and Scorsese—remains foundational.

## Body

### Early Life and Entry into Film
Ford was born into a large Irish-Catholic family in Maine. His father, John Augustine Feeney, arrived from Galway in 1872; his mother, Barbara Curran, from the Aran Islands. After Portland High School (where he earned the nickname "Bull" playing football), he followed his older brother Francis—a successful actor-writer-director at Universal—to California in 1914. He worked as assistant, stuntman, and actor (including an uncredited Klansman in Griffith’s *The Birth of a Nation*), adopting "Jack Ford." Universal chief Carl Laemmle gave him his first directing job in 1917, reportedly saying, "Give Jack Ford the job—he yells good."

### Silent Era: Apprenticeship and *The Iron Horse*
Between 1917 and 1928 Ford directed 62 shorts and features for Universal, though only ten survive complete. He formed a crucial four-year, 25-film partnership with actor Harry Carey, whose son Harry "Dobe" Carey Jr. became a lifelong Ford player. His first surviving feature, *Straight Shooting* (1917), was saved from studio-ordered cuts by Laemmle. In 1920 he moved to William Fox studio. His breakthrough came with *The Iron Horse* (1924), an epic of the transcontinental railroad shot on location in the Sierra Nevada with massive logistics (5,000 extras, 800 Native Americans, 1,300 bison). Despite weather delays and studio pressure, Fox backed him; the film grossed $2 million on a $280,000 budget. Ford’s last silent Western was *3 Bad Men* (1926); the genre then fell from major-studio favor for 13 years.

### Transition to Sound and 1930s Mastery
Ford embraced sound early, directing Fox’s first sung song (*Mother Machree*, 1928) and first all-talking feature (*The Black Watch*, 1929). German director F.W. Murnau’s *Sunrise* (1927) profoundly influenced Ford’s visual style—evident in *Four Sons* (1928), shot on Murnau’s leftover sets. Ford’s 1930s output was steady (2–3 films/year) and commercially potent; by 1938 he earned $220,068, more than double the U.S. president’s salary. Key films: *The Lost Patrol* (1934), a critical hit; *The Informer* (1935), Murnau-influenced IRA drama winning his first Oscar; *The Hurricane* and *Wee Willie Winkie* (1937), each grossing over $1 million. He developed a stable "Stock Company" of actors (Victor McLaglen, Ward Bond, John Carradine) and crew (cinematographers Joseph August, Gregg Toland; editor Jack Murray).

### 1939–1941: Annus Mirabilis
*Stagecoach* (1939) revived the A-picture Western. After a year of rejections from major studios, independent Walter Wanger produced it. Ford cast unknown John Wayne as Ringo over studio objections; the film grossed $1 million on a $400,000 budget, won two Oscars, and made Wayne a star. Orson Welles studied it 40 times for *Citizen Kane*. *Young Mr. Lincoln* (1939) and *Drums Along the Mohawk* (1939, first color, uncredited Faulkner script) followed. *The Grapes of Wrath* (1940), adapting Steinbeck with script by Nunnally Johnson and photography by Gregg Toland, won Ford his second Best Director Oscar and transformed him, per Andrew Sarris, into "America's cinematic poet laureate." *The Long Voyage Home* (1940, from O’Neill plays) and *Tobacco Road* (1941) preceded *How Green Was My Valley* (1941), a Welsh mining saga moved to California due to the Blitz. It won five Oscars, including Best Picture and Ford’s third Best Director award.

### War Years: OSS and Combat Photography
Commissioned in the Navy Reserve, Ford headed the OSS photographic unit. He filmed the Battle of Midway from Sand Island’s power plant, taking a machine-gun bullet in the arm; *The Battle of Midway* (1942) won a Documentary Oscar. He directed the propaganda film *December 7* (1943), also an Oscar winner. On D-Day he crossed on the USS *Plunkett*, filmed Omaha Beach from the ship and later on the beach with Coast Guard cameramen; the footage was suppressed for showing heavy casualties (color reels reportedly survive in archives). He rose to top adviser to OSS chief William Donovan, cited for bravery under fire. His last wartime feature, *They Were Expendable* (1945), a PT-boat drama starring Wayne and Robert Montgomery, was a top-20 hit despite Ford’s professed dislike for the project.

### Post-War Independence: Argosy Pictures
Refusing a $600,000/year Fox contract, Ford co-founded Argosy Pictures with Merian C. Cooper. *The Fugitive* (1947, from Greene’s *The Power and the Glory*, shot in Mexico with Gabriel Figueroa) Ford considered his best, but it flopped and ended his collaboration with writer Dudley Nichols. The "Cavalry Trilogy"—*Fort Apache* (1948), *She Wore a Yellow Ribbon* (1949), *Rio Grande* (1950)—starred Wayne, established writer Frank S. Nugent, and grossed strongly. *Wagon Master* (1950), the only Ford film he scripted since 1930, became his personal favorite and spawned TV’s *Wagon Train*. Republic Pictures financed *Rio Grande* only if Ford made *The Quiet Man* (1952), his Irish passion project; it became his highest-grossing film ($4M domestic) and won his fourth Best Director Oscar.

### 1950s: Peaks and Crises
*Mogambo* (1953, Africa, with Gable, Gardner, Kelly) was a $5.2M hit. *Mister Roberts* (1955) saw Ford punch Henry Fonda during a dispute, causing a lasting rift; Ford suffered a ruptured gallbladder and was replaced by Mervyn LeRoy. *The Searchers* (1956), shot in Monument Valley, is now hailed as his masterpiece (AFI’s #1 Western, #12 all-time); its influence echoes in *Lawrence of Arabia*, *Star Wars*, and Buddy Holly’s "That’ll Be the Day." *The Wings of Eagles* (1957) and the Navy documentary *The Growler Story* followed. *The Rising of the Moon* (1957, Ireland, produced by Lord Killanin) lost money. *Gideon’s Day* (1958, London, only police film) and *The Last Hurrah* (1958, with Tracy) were modest. *The Horse Soldiers* (1959, Civil War, Wayne/Holden) grossed $3.6M but was marred by cast tensions, cost overruns, and the fatal stunt fall of Fred Kennedy, after which Ford lost interest and cut the planned ending.

### Final Decade: Decline and Legacy
Health declined from decades of drinking, smoking, and Midway wounds; vision and memory faltered. *Sergeant Rutledge* (1960, Black cavalryman) was mismarketed and failed. *Two Rode Together* (1961, Stewart/Widmark) did fair business. *The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance* (1962, Wayne/Stewart), shot on Paramount soundstages as an artistic choice (per biographer Joseph McBride), grossed $3M and is often called his last great film. He directed the Civil War segment of Cinerama epic *How the West Was Won* (1962) and TV’s *Flashing Spikes*. *Donovan’s Reef* (1963, Hawaii) was his last Wayne film and last hit. *Cheyenne Autumn* (1964), a $4.2M Native American elegy, lost $1M. Illness forced him off *Young Cassidy* (1965). *7 Women* (1966, China mission) flopped. His final work, *Chesty* (1970, released 1976), honored Marine Lewis Puller. In 1973, the AFI honored him with its first Life Achievement Award; Nixon promoted him to Admiral and awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom. He died August 31, 1973.

### Personality and Directing Style
Ford cultivated a "tough, two-fisted, hard-drinking Irish sonofabitch" persona to shield a sensitive, erudite private self. He wore dark glasses (light-sensitive left eye), chewed handkerchiefs on set, mandated Earl Grey tea breaks, banned swearing, and drank in solitary binges after wrap. He was generous anonymously (once secretly funding a destitute actor’s wife’s surgery and pensioning the couple). On set he was tyrannical: mocking actors ("big idiot" to Wayne), punching Fonda, hazing crew (e.g., sadistic treatment of Lord Wallscourt due to ancestral Irish grievance). Yet actors like Wayne and Harry Carey Jr. credited him with eliciting their best work through minimal explicit direction—demanding they mimic his casual walk-throughs. He composed entirely in his head (no storyboards), "cut in the camera" (shooting minimal footage to prevent studio re-editing), favored static long shots and tracking shots over close-ups, and used recurring motifs: doorways, trains, "death hand" poker (aces and eights), the hymn "Shall We Gather at the River," and character names (Quincannon, Kirby Yorke). Monument Valley, though geographically inaccurate for many plots, became his signature canvas, defining the cinematic West so powerfully Welles claimed others avoided it fearing plagiarism.

### Politics
Early politics were progressive (admired FDR, JFK, Lincoln), but associations with Wayne, Stewart, and Ward Bond suggested Republican leanings. In 1950, as Directors Guild president, he defended Joseph Mankiewicz against Cecil B. DeMille’s loyalty-oath faction, demanding unity and an end to political purges—though transcripts later showed a more nuanced exchange where Ford also defended DeMille. He declared himself a "Maine Republican" in 1947, voted Goldwater (1964), supported Nixon (1968, 1972) and the Vietnam War, backed Reagan for California governor, and privately funded the IRA from the 1920s onward. Scholar Tag Gallagher calls him "essentially apolitical."

### Influence and Honors
Ford is widely ranked among cinema’s most influential directors. Admirers include Kurosawa ("I pay close attention to his productions"), Welles ("John Ford, John Ford, and John Ford"), Bergman ("best director in the world"), Lean, Leone, Lucas, Scorsese, Spielberg (dramatized meeting Ford in *The Fabelmans*), Tarkovsky, and Godard. He holds the record for Best Director Oscars (4). Ten actors earned Oscar nominations in his films (five wins). Eleven films are in the National Film Registry (tied with Hawks). He received the AFI Life Achievement Award (1973), Presidential Medal of Freedom (1973), and military honors including the Legion of Merit with Combat "V" and Purple Heart.

## Terms
- **Auteur**: A filmmaker whose personal creative vision dominates a film, recognized as its "author"; Ford was among the first American directors so labeled.
- **Stock Company**: An informal repertory troupe of actors (e.g., Ward Bond, Harry Carey Jr., Mae Marsh, Ben Johnson) and crew who worked repeatedly with Ford across decades.
- **Cutting in the camera**: Ford’s practice of shooting only the footage needed, in sequence, minimizing coverage to retain editorial control and prevent studio interference.
- **Monument Valley**: Utah-Arizona border landscape Ford used in seven Westerns (*Stagecoach* through *Cheyenne Autumn*); its iconic buttes defined the cinematic West despite geographic inaccuracy.
- **Cavalry Trilogy**: *Fort Apache* (1948), *She Wore a Yellow Ribbon* (1949), *Rio Grande* (1950)—linked by James Warner Bellah stories, John Wayne, and themes of duty and ritual.
- **OSS (Office of Strategic Services)**: WWII U.S. intelligence agency; Ford headed its photographic unit, filming covert ops and major battles.
- **"Death hand"**: Poker hand (two aces, two eights, ace of spades) Ford used as a visual motif for doomed characters, referencing Wild Bill Hickok’s legendary final hand.
- **Argosy Pictures**: Independent production company Ford co-founded with Merian C. Cooper (1946) after refusing a studio contract; produced *The Fugitive*, Cavalry Trilogy, *The Quiet Man*, *Mogambo*.
- **Expressionistic lighting**: High-contrast, stylized interior lighting (chiaroscuro) influenced by F.W. Murnau, used by Ford to convey mood and psychology.
- **Earl Grey break**: Ford’s daily mid-afternoon tea ritual on set, part of his regimented, idiosyncratic working routine.

## Debates and open questions
- **D-Day footage**: Ford claimed he landed on Omaha Beach on D-Day and filmed in color; historians (Stephen Ambrose, National Archives) have failed to locate the footage, and some researchers argue he arrived days later, calling the account a "tall tale."
- **Directors Guild showdown (1950)**: Joseph Mankiewicz’s memoir credits Ford with single-handedly defeating Cecil B. DeMille’s loyalty-oath push; a 2016 court transcript reveals a more collective revolt and Ford defending DeMille against expulsion, complicating the heroic narrative.
- **Catholic faith and marriage**: Ford was Catholic; wife Mary McBride Smith was a non-Catholic divorcée. The source notes the "level of Ford's commitment to the Catholic faith is disputed" and the difficulty this caused is "unclear."
- **Sexuality rumors**: Maureen O’Hara’s 2004 autobiography claims she saw Ford kissing a famous male actor at Columbia Studios; the actor is unnamed and the claim is uncorroborated.
- **Political consistency**: Scholars debate whether Ford was "essentially apolitical" (Gallagher) or a committed conservative/Republican in later life, given his Goldwater/Nixon votes, Vietnam support, and IRA funding.
- **Lost silent films**: Only 10 of 62+ silent features survive complete; rediscoveries (e.g., *Upstream* in New Zealand, 2009; *Bucking Broadway* in France, 2002) continue to reshape understanding of his early style.
