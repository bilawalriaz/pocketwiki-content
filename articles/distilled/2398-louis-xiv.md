# Louis XIV

## Overview
Louis XIV (1638–1715), known as the "Sun King," was King of France for 72 years, the longest reign of any sovereign monarch in history. He is the archetypal absolute monarch, centralizing power, patronizing the arts, and waging costly wars that made France Europe's leading power but left it deeply indebted. His reign defined the Age of Absolutism and the cultural "Grand Siècle" of France.

## Timeline
- **5 September 1638** — Born in Château de Saint-Germain-en-Laye to Louis XIII and Anne of Austria.
- **14 May 1643** — Becomes King of France at age four; mother Anne of Austria becomes regent.
- **1648–1653** — The Fronde, a series of civil wars, deeply influences his distrust of the nobility and Paris.
- **1661** — After Cardinal Mazarin's death, begins personal rule, declaring he will govern without a chief minister.
- **1682** — The royal court officially moves to the Palace of Versailles.
- **1685** — Issues the Edict of Fontainebleau, revoking the Edict of Nantes and persecuting Protestants.
- **1688–1697** — Fights the Nine Years' War against the Grand Alliance.
- **1700** — Accepts the Spanish throne for his grandson Philip V, triggering the War of the Spanish Succession.
- **1713** — Signs the Peace of Utrecht, ending the War of the Spanish Succession.
- **1 September 1715** — Dies at Versailles, succeeded by his five-year-old great-grandson, Louis XV.

## Body

### Early Life and the Fronde
Born a "miracle" child after his parents' 23-year marriage, Louis XIV's belief in the divine right of kings was instilled by his mother. His childhood was marked by the Fronde (1648–1653), a civil war where aristocrats and the Parlement of Paris rebelled against royal authority. The experience of being driven from Paris and placed under virtual arrest bred in him a lifelong hatred of the capital and a determination to control the nobility.

### Personal Rule and Centralization
Upon Mazarin's death in 1661, Louis took personal control of the government. He appointed Jean-Baptiste Colbert to reform finances, reducing the deficit through efficient taxation and mercantilist policies that boosted industry and trade. To neutralize the aristocracy, he compelled them to live at his lavish Palace of Versailles, transforming them from feudal rivals into ceremonial courtiers. He also reformed the military under Louvois, creating a professional, disciplined force loyal to the crown.

### Wars and Foreign Policy
Louis's reign was defined by warfare, driven by ambition and a desire for glory. He fought three major continental wars: the Franco-Dutch War (1672–1678), the Nine Years' War (1688–1697), and the War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714). While French armies were often victorious, these conflicts strained the nation's resources and united much of Europe against France. His aggressive expansionism, including the use of "Chambers of Reunion" to legally annex territories, ultimately led to his diplomatic isolation.

### Religion and the Edict of Fontainebleau
A devout Catholic, Louis sought religious uniformity. In 1685, he revoked the Edict of Nantes, which had granted rights to Huguenots (French Protestants). The resulting Edict of Fontainebleau banned Protestant worship, forced conversions through "dragonnades" (quartering soldiers in homes), and led to the emigration of about 200,000 skilled Huguenots, damaging the French economy and severely harming Louis's international reputation.

### Arts, Culture, and Versailles
Louis was a prolific patron of the arts, supporting figures like Molière, Racine, and Lully, and fostering French classicism. He transformed Versailles into a dazzling political tool and the cultural center of Europe. His image as the "Sun King" was propagated through art, ballet (which he danced in), and grand architecture, cementing his absolute authority in the public imagination.

### Death and Succession
Louis died of gangrene in 1715, having outlived his son and two grandsons. His heir was his five-year-old great-grandson, Louis XV. On his deathbed, he reportedly regretted his wars and advised his successor to be a peaceful prince and alleviate his subjects' burdens.

## Terms
*   **Absolutism:** A system of government where the ruler holds total, unchecked power, often justified by the divine right of kings.
*   **Fronde:** A series of civil wars in France (1648–1653) during Louis XIV's minority, involving nobles and the Parlement of Paris against royal authority.
*   **Divine Right of Kings:** The doctrine that a monarch's authority comes directly from God, not from the people or any earthly institution.
*   **Edict of Nantes:** A 1598 decree granting religious and political freedoms to Huguenots in France.
*   **Dragonnades:** A policy of forcibly quartering dragoons (soldiers) in Protestant homes to coerce conversion to Catholicism.
*   **Mercantilism:** An economic theory promoting state regulation of a nation's economy to increase state power, often through exports and accumulating bullion.
*   **Gallicanism:** A tradition in the Catholic Church in France that limited the authority of the Pope in French affairs.
*   **Grand Siècle:** The "Great Century," referring to the cultural, artistic, and political dominance of France during Louis XIV's reign.
*   **Chambers of Reunion:** Legal bodies established by Louis XIV to interpret old treaties and justify the annexation of territories.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Motives for Revoking the Edict of Nantes:** Historians debate whether Louis acted primarily to please the Pope, to upstage Emperor Leopold I, or to fulfill his coronation oath to eradicate heresy and unify French society.
*   **Economic Impact of Huguenot Emigration:** While some historians argue the exodus of skilled workers severely weakened the French economy, others contend it was exaggerated, as many prominent Protestants converted and remained.
*   **Legacy of His Wars:** Scholars disagree on whether his military campaigns were a glorious expansion that secured defensible borders for France or a reckless pursuit of vanity that bankrupted the state and sowed the seeds of the French Revolution.
*   **The "I am the state" Quote:** The famous attribution "L'État, c'est moi" ("I am the state") is widely considered apocryphal by historians, though it encapsulates the perception of his absolute rule.
