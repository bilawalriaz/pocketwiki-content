# Midwestern United States

## Overview
The Midwestern United States is one of the four U.S. Census Bureau regions, comprising 12 states in the north-central part of the country. Known as the "Heartland," it is a major agricultural and industrial hub whose central location makes it a critical transportation crossroads. Its political landscape, often featuring swing states, frequently decides national elections.

## Timeline
- **c. 12,000 BCE – 8,000 BCE** — Paleo-American cultures present in the Great Plains and Great Lakes areas.
- **1534** — Jacques Cartier explores the Saint Lawrence River, beginning the French period.
- **1673** — Jacques Marquette and Louis Jolliet map the northern Mississippi River.
- **1763** — Treaty of Paris ends French control; Britain gains lands east of the Mississippi.
- **1787** — Northwest Ordinance creates the Northwest Territory.
- **1803** — United States acquires the Louisiana Purchase from France.
- **1825** — Erie Canal opens, creating an all-water route to New York City.
- **1837** — John Deere invents the steel plow, enabling prairie farming.
- **1854** — Kansas–Nebraska Act leads to "Bleeding Kansas" conflicts.
- **1860s** — Republican Party, founded in the Midwest, rises to national power.
- **1903** — Henry Ford founds the Ford Motor Company in Detroit.
- **1959** — Saint Lawrence Seaway opens, connecting the Great Lakes to the Atlantic.

## Body

### Early History and Native Americans
The region was home to successive Native American cultures, including the Mississippian mound-builders (c. 900–1500 CE) and numerous Great Lakes and Plains tribes. The "Middle Ground" theory describes a period of mutual accommodation and trade between Native peoples and French, then British, empires in the Great Lakes region from the 17th to early 19th centuries.

### European Exploration and Settlement
French exploration began in the 16th century, establishing New France. After the 1763 Treaty of Paris, British and then American control expanded. The 1803 Louisiana Purchase doubled the nation's size. American settlement accelerated after the 1794 Battle of Fallen Timbers, with "squatters" or "pioneers" moving into the region, often displacing Native Americans through a series of wars and treaties.

### Transportation and Industry
The Ohio River, Great Lakes, and Missouri River were vital early waterways. The Erie Canal (1825) and later railroads, with Chicago as a central hub, spurred massive growth. In the 20th century, the auto industry, centered in Detroit, made the Midwest a global manufacturing leader. The region's economy is a mix of agriculture (the "Corn Belt"), heavy industry, and finance.

### Politics and Society
The Midwest is a key political battleground. The Republican Party was founded here in the 1850s. The region was a center of the abolitionist movement and the Underground Railroad. It later saw strong Progressive, Populist, and labor movements. Demographically, it was shaped by waves of European immigration (especially German and Scandinavian) and the Great Migration of African Americans from the South in the 20th century.

## Terms
- **Corn Belt**: A region of the Midwest where corn has been the predominant crop since the 1850s.
- **Great Migration**: The movement of six million African Americans out of the rural Southern United States to the urban Northeast, Midwest, and West from 1916 to 1970.
- **Middle Ground**: A historical concept describing a period of mutual accommodation and trade between Native Americans and European empires in the Great Lakes region.
- **Northwest Ordinance**: The 1787 law that created the Northwest Territory, establishing a path for new states to join the Union and prohibiting slavery in the region.
- **Populism**: A political movement of the late 19th century, representing farmers and laborers against big business and banks.
- **Progressivism**: A political movement of the early 20th century aimed at using government regulation to address social and economic problems caused by industrialization.
- **Squatter**: A settler without a legal claim to the land, a common figure in early Midwestern settlement.
- **Underground Railroad**: A network of secret routes and safe houses used by enslaved African Americans to escape to free states and Canada.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Defining the Midwest**: The exact composition of the region is debated. While the Census Bureau defines 12 states, other organizations include or exclude states like Missouri, Kentucky, or Oklahoma based on cultural or geographic criteria.
- **Cultural Identity**: There is debate over whether the industrial Great Lakes cities or the agricultural Great Plains better represent the "core" Midwestern identity.
- **Political Shifts**: The region's political landscape is in flux, with former swing states like Iowa and Ohio trending Republican, while others like Michigan and Wisconsin remain highly contested.
