# Pittsburgh

## Overview
Pittsburgh is a major city in southwestern Pennsylvania, historically known as "the Steel City" for its central role in the U.S. steel industry. Its strategic location at the confluence of the Allegheny, Monongahela, and Ohio Rivers made it a vital industrial and transportation hub. After severe deindustrialization in the late 20th century, Pittsburgh successfully transitioned its economy to focus on healthcare, education, and technology, becoming a model for post-industrial urban renewal.

## Timeline
- **1758** — Named "Pittsburgh" by General John Forbes in honor of William Pitt.
- **1794** — Incorporated as a borough.
- **1816** — Incorporated as a city.
- **1875** — Andrew Carnegie begins steel production at Edgar Thomson Steel Works.
- **1901** — Carnegie Steel merges into U.S. Steel.
- **1936** — Worst flood in city history; 62 die.
- **1948** — "Renaissance I" clean air and revitalization project begins.
- **1980s** — Major deindustrialization; massive layoffs in steel and electronics.
- **2009** — Hosts the G20 summit.
- **2026** — Hosts the NFL draft for the first time.

## Body

### Etymology and Early History
The area was long inhabited by Native Americans, including the Shawnee and Seneca. The first European to enter the region was French explorer Robert de La Salle in 1669. The site became a flashpoint for imperial conflict during the French and Indian War, with the British building Fort Pitt in 1758. The settlement grew as a frontier outpost, with its first civilian government created in 1771. It became a possession of Pennsylvania in 1785.

### Industrial Rise and Peak
Pittsburgh's growth accelerated with the advent of the iron and steel industries. The War of 1812 stimulated local manufacturing. By the 1840s, it was a major industrial center, though the Great Fire of 1845 destroyed over a thousand buildings. The Civil War boosted the economy with demand for armaments. Andrew Carnegie's adoption of the Bessemer process in 1875 revolutionized steel production. By 1910, Pittsburgh was the nation's eighth-largest city, producing up to half of the nation's steel. The city's population peaked at over 680,000 in 1950.

### Deindustrialization and Renaissance
Following World War II, the city launched "Renaissance I" to combat severe air and water pollution. However, beginning in the early 1980s, the steel and electronics industries collapsed during national restructuring, causing massive layoffs. The city pivoted its economic base to healthcare, education, and high technology. While the metropolitan area population has grown again, the city proper's population has not rebounded to its industrial-era highs. Pittsburgh has been called "the poster child for managing industrial transition."

### Geography and Climate
Pittsburgh is located on the Allegheny Plateau. Its downtown, the "Golden Triangle," sits at the river confluence. The city is famously hilly, with 446 bridges and 800 sets of public stairs. It has a humid continental or subtropical climate with four distinct seasons, hot summers, and cold, snowy winters. Annual precipitation averages 39.61 inches, and snowfall averages 44.1 inches.

### Demographics and Society
The 2020 census recorded a population of 302,971. The racial makeup was 64.7% non-Hispanic white, 23.0% Black or African American, and 5.8% Asian. The city has large Italian-American, Irish-American, and German-American communities. Demographic shifts since the 1950s, including urban renewal projects that displaced residents, have led to gentrification in neighborhoods like East Liberty and Lawrenceville. The city is predominantly Christian, with a significant Catholic population.

### Economy
The economy is now driven by healthcare (led by the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center), education, technology, and finance. The region is home to ten Fortune 500 companies, including U.S. Steel, PNC Financial Services, and Kraft Heinz. Former industrial sites have been repurposed, such as the Google office in a former Nabisco factory. The nonprofit arts industry generates significant economic activity.

### Arts, Culture, and Sports
Pittsburgh has a rich cultural scene, including the Carnegie Museums, Andy Warhol Museum, and Phipps Conservatory. It is the birthplace of the modern zombie film genre and Mister Rogers' Neighborhood. The city's major professional sports teams—the Steelers (NFL), Penguins (NHL), and Pirates (MLB)—all share black and gold colors. The city is also known for its distinctive "Pittsburghese" dialect.

### Government and Politics
The government consists of a mayor and a nine-member city council. The city shifted from a Republican stronghold to a Democratic one after the Great Depression. In 2021, it elected its first African-American mayor, Ed Gainey. Pittsburgh is considered the birthplace of the national Republican Party, which held its first convention there in 1856.

### Transportation
Pittsburgh is a city of bridges, with 446 within city limits. It is served by Pittsburgh Regional Transit, which operates buses, a light rail system ("the T"), and historic inclines. The Pittsburgh International Airport provides commercial service. The Port of Pittsburgh is the 20th-largest in the U.S. by tonnage.

## Terms
- **Deindustrialization:** The decline of industrial activity in a region, leading to job losses and economic restructuring.
- **Renaissance:** A series of urban renewal and environmental cleanup projects in Pittsburgh, beginning in the late 1940s.
- **Bessemer process:** A 19th-century method for mass-producing steel from molten pig iron, which revolutionized the industry.
- **Pittsburghese:** The distinctive local dialect of English, influenced by Scots-Irish, German, and Eastern European immigrants.
- **Golden Triangle:** The downtown area of Pittsburgh, bounded by the confluence of the Allegheny and Monongahela Rivers.
- **Incline:** A funicular railway used to transport passengers up and down the city's steep hillsides.
- **Combined Statistical Area (CSA):** A geographic region defined by the U.S. Census Bureau, consisting of adjacent metropolitan areas with strong economic ties.

## Debates and open questions
- **Environmental Racism:** Studies indicate that minority communities in Pittsburgh are disproportionately affected by air and water pollution, leading to concerns about environmental injustice.
- **Gentrification:** Urban renewal and redevelopment projects, particularly from the 1950s onward, are criticized for displacing low-income and Black residents, contributing to ongoing gentrification debates.
- **Economic Transition:** While Pittsburgh is hailed as a successful model for post-industrial transition, questions remain about the sustainability of its new economy and the equitable distribution of its benefits.
