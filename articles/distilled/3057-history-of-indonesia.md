# History of Indonesia

## Overview
Indonesia is the world's largest archipelagic state, comprising over 17,000 islands. Its history is defined by its strategic maritime location, which fostered trade and cultural exchange, leading to a diverse population shaped by migrations, Hindu-Buddhist kingdoms, Islamic sultanates, and European colonialism. The modern nation emerged from a struggle for independence after World War II and has since navigated challenges of democracy, economic development, and national unity.

## Timeline
- **c. 1.5 million years ago** — Earliest evidence of human presence (Homo erectus fossils).
- **c. 2000 BCE** — Austronesian people, ancestors of most modern Indonesians, arrive from Taiwan.
- **7th century CE** — Srivijaya, a powerful Hindu-Buddhist naval kingdom, flourishes on Sumatra.
- **Late 13th century** — The Hindu Majapahit kingdom is founded in Java, reaching its peak in the 14th century.
- **13th century** — Earliest evidence of Islamized populations in northern Sumatra.
- **1602** — The Dutch East India Company (VOC) is established, becoming the dominant European power.
- **1942–1945** — Japanese occupation during WWII ends Dutch rule.
- **17 August 1945** — Sukarno and Hatta proclaim Indonesian independence.
- **December 1949** — The Netherlands formally recognizes Indonesian sovereignty.
- **1965–1966** — Anti-communist purge following a failed coup; over 500,000 killed.
- **1968** — General Suharto becomes president, beginning the "New Order" era.
- **21 May 1998** — Suharto resigns amid economic crisis and protests, beginning the "Reformasi" era.

## Body

### Prehistory and Early Kingdoms
The Indonesian archipelago was inhabited by Homo erectus at least 1.5 million years ago. Modern humans arrived by around 45,000 years ago, developing advanced maritime skills. Austronesian migrations from Taiwan around 2000 BCE brought new cultures and the practice of wet-field rice cultivation, which allowed villages and small kingdoms to flourish by the 1st century CE. Indian cultural influence, including Hinduism and Buddhism, spread from the 2nd century, leading to early kingdoms like Tarumanagara and Kutai.

### Hindu-Buddhist Civilizations
From the 7th century, the thalassocracy (maritime empire) of Srivijaya dominated trade through the Strait of Malacca and became a center of Mahayana Buddhism. On Java, the Mataram Kingdom flourished, constructing major temples like Borobudur and Prambanan. The most powerful pre-Islamic state was the Hindu Majapahit kingdom, founded in the late 13th century. Under Prime Minister Gajah Mada, its influence extended across much of the archipelago in the 14th century, but it declined by the early 16th century.

### Islamic Civilizations and European Arrival
Islam spread through trade links, with the earliest evidence in northern Sumatra in the 13th century. It became the dominant religion in Java and Sumatra by the end of the 16th century, often blending with existing traditions. The Portuguese were the first Europeans to arrive in the early 16th century, seeking to control the spice trade. They were followed by the Dutch, who established the VOC in 1602. The VOC became the dominant European power, founding Batavia (Jakarta) in 1619 and exploiting local rivalries to build a colonial empire.

### Colonial Rule and Independence
After the VOC went bankrupt in 1800, the Dutch state took direct control as the Dutch East Indies. A brief British interlude under Thomas Stamford Raffles (1811–1816) preceded the return of Dutch rule. The Dutch implemented exploitative systems like the Cultivation System and later an "Ethical Policy." Nationalist movements emerged in the early 20th century. The Japanese occupation (1942–1945) weakened Dutch authority and inspired the independence movement. Following Japan's surrender, independence was proclaimed on 17 August 1945. After a four-year struggle, the Netherlands recognized Indonesian sovereignty in December 1949.

### Sukarno's Era and the New Order
President Sukarno guided the new nation through a parliamentary democracy, then an authoritarian "Guided Democracy" system. His rule ended after a failed 1965 coup, which triggered a violent anti-communist purge. General Suharto took power in 1968, establishing the "New Order" regime characterized by political repression, anti-communism, and economic growth driven by foreign investment, but also widespread corruption. His rule collapsed in 1998 amid the Asian financial crisis and mass protests.

### Reformasi and Contemporary Indonesia
The post-Suharto "Reformasi" era has seen a transition to democracy, including direct presidential elections. Key events include the peaceful independence of East Timor in 2002 and the 2004 Aceh peace deal following a devastating tsunami. Challenges include terrorism, corruption, and managing economic growth. In 2024, Prabowo Subianto was inaugurated as president. The government is pursuing the relocation of the capital to Nusantara in East Kalimantan.

## Terms
- **Thalassocracy**: A state with primary power based on the sea and naval dominance.
- **Wet-field rice cultivation**: An agricultural method requiring flooded fields (paddies) and complex social organization for irrigation.
- **Cultivation System (Cultuurstelsel)**: A Dutch colonial policy forcing peasants to use part of their land for cash crops for export.
- **Reformasi**: The period of political and economic reforms following the end of Suharto's authoritarian rule in 1998.
- **Guided Democracy**: Sukarno's political system (1959–1966) that replaced parliamentary democracy with a guided, authoritarian form of government.
- **New Order**: The regime of President Suharto (1968–1998), characterized by anti-communism, economic development, and political repression.
- **Transmigration**: A government program to relocate people from densely populated islands like Java to less populated outer islands.
- **Konfrontasi**: Indonesia's policy of opposition (1963–1966) to the formation of the Federation of Malaysia.

## Debates and open questions
- The identity and ultimate aims of the organizers of the 30 September 1965 coup attempt remain debated, with the official account blaming the PKI.
- The "Act of Free Choice" in West Papua (1969), where 1,025 representatives unanimously voted for integration with Indonesia, is widely criticized as not reflecting the will of the Papuan people.
- The causes and responsibility for the violence during the May 1998 riots, including allegations of systematic sexual violence, are subjects of ongoing calls for investigation.
- The long-term social and economic impacts of the Transmigration program on indigenous populations and regional conflicts are contested.
