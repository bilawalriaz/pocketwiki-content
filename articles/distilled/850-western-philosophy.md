# Western philosophy

## Overview

Western philosophy encompasses the philosophical traditions of the Western world, beginning with ancient Greek thought and evolving through medieval, modern, and contemporary periods. It is distinguished by its emphasis on reason, systematic inquiry, and the integration of philosophical questions with broader cultural and intellectual developments. The term "philosophy" itself derives from the Greek *philosophia*, meaning "love of wisdom."

## Timeline

- **c. 625 BC** — Thales of Miletus begins pre-Socratic philosophy
- **570 BC** — Pythagoras establishes numerical metaphysics
- **399 BC** — Socrates executed in Athens
- **387 BC** — Plato founds the Academy
- **384 BC** — Aristotle born; later founds the Peripatetic school
- **1781** — Kant publishes *Critique of Pure Reason*
- **1807** — Hegel publishes *Phenomenology of Spirit*
- **1900–1901** — Husserl's *Logical Investigations* and Russell's *Principles of Mathematics* mark the start of 20th-century philosophy

## Body

### Ancient Philosophy

Ancient Western philosophy originated with the pre-Socratics, who sought the *arche* (first principle) of the cosmos through reason rather than myth. Thales proposed water as the fundamental substance; Anaximander posited the boundless (*apeiron*); Anaximenes identified air. Pythagoras introduced mathematical harmony, while Heraclitus emphasized constant flux. Parmenides argued for a singular, unchanging reality, countered by Zeno's paradoxes. Empedocles synthesized the four elements with Love and Strife, and Anaxagoras introduced *nous* (mind) as the ordering principle. Atomism, developed by Leucippus and Democritus, offered a materialist explanation of reality.

The Sophists, including Protagoras ("man is the measure of all things"), promoted relativism and rhetoric, challenging objective truth. Socrates shifted focus to ethics and human affairs, employing the Socratic method of questioning. His trial and execution in 399 BC marked a turning point. Plato, his student, founded the Academy and developed theories of Forms, virtue as knowledge, and epistemology. Aristotle, Plato’s student, established the Peripatetic school, contributing to logic, metaphysics, ethics, and natural science. His four causes and concept of the unmoved mover became foundational.

Hellenistic schools—Stoicism, Epicureanism, Skepticism, and Cynicism—focused on achieving *eudaimonia* (flourishing) through inner tranquility (*ataraxia*) and virtue. Pyrrhonism, influenced by Buddhist thought encountered during Alexander’s campaigns, advocated suspension of judgment to attain peace.

### Medieval Philosophy

Medieval philosophy integrated classical Greek thought with Christian theology. Augustine Christianized Platonism, addressing evil, time, and free will. Scholasticism emerged as a method emphasizing dialectical reasoning and conceptual analysis. Anselm formulated the ontological argument for God’s existence; Aquinas synthesized Aristotelian philosophy with Christian doctrine, developing the five ways to prove God’s existence and defining the soul as the essence of human beings. The period also saw contributions from Islamic philosophers like Avicenna and Averroes, and Jewish thinkers like Maimonides.

Renaissance humanism shifted focus from metaphysics to ethics, literature, and classical texts, emphasizing human dignity and secular inquiry. The period bridged medieval and modern thought, with figures like Petrarch promoting the study of antiquity.

### Modern Philosophy

Modern philosophy arose with increased independence from religious authority and the rise of scientific inquiry. Descartes initiated modern epistemology with Cartesian doubt and *cogito ergo sum*, while Hobbes and Locke developed political theories rooted in the state of nature. Spinoza and Leibniz offered alternative metaphysical systems. British empiricists—Locke, Berkeley, and Hume—argued that knowledge derives from sensory experience. Kant revolutionized philosophy by reconciling rationalism and empiricism, asserting that the mind structures experience through a priori categories.

German idealism, led by Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel, viewed reality as mentally constituted. Hegel’s dialectical method aimed to resolve contradictions through historical progression toward absolute knowledge. Marx transformed Hegelian idealism into historical materialism, analyzing class struggle and economic structures.

Late modern philosophy saw the rise of utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill), pragmatism (Peirce, James, Dewey), and existentialism (Kierkegaard, Nietzsche). Logic advanced significantly through Boole and Frege, laying groundwork for analytic philosophy.

### Contemporary Philosophy

Contemporary philosophy splits into analytic and continental traditions. Analytic philosophy emphasizes logical rigor, language analysis, and scientific methodology. Key developments include logical positivism (Vienna Circle), ordinary language philosophy (Wittgenstein, Austin), and later critiques of meaning and reference (Quine, Kripke, Davidson). Philosophy of mind grappled with consciousness, identity, and the mind-body problem, while ethics revisited virtue ethics (Anscombe, MacIntyre) and applied moral theory.

Continental philosophy, rooted in German idealism and phenomenology, includes existentialism (Heidegger, Sartre), hermeneutics (Gadamer), critical theory (Frankfurt School), and post-structuralism (Foucault, Derrida). These traditions critique scientific rationalism and explore subjectivity, power, and meaning.

Process philosophy, initiated by Whitehead, redefines reality as fundamentally dynamic and relational.

## Terms

- **Arche**: The first principle or fundamental substance of the universe, sought by pre-Socratic philosophers.
- **Eudaimonia**: A state of flourishing or well-being, central to Aristotelian ethics.
- **Ataraxia**: Tranquility or freedom from disturbance, a goal in Hellenistic philosophy.
- **Apatheia**: Freedom from passion or emotional disturbance, especially in Stoicism.
- **Dialectical method**: A form of argument involving contradiction and resolution, used in scholasticism and Hegelian philosophy.
- **Cartesian doubt**: The method of systematic skepticism pioneered by Descartes to establish certain knowledge.
- **Phenomenology**: The study of structures of consciousness and experience, founded by Husserl.
- **Logical positivism**: A 20th-century movement asserting that only empirically verifiable or logically necessary statements are meaningful.
- **Ordinary language philosophy**: A mid-20th-century approach analyzing how language functions in everyday contexts.
- **Existential attitude**: A sense of disorientation and confrontation with meaninglessness, central to existentialism.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several enduring debates shape Western philosophy. The mind-body problem remains unresolved, with physicalist, dualist, and panpsychist approaches competing. The analytic-continental divide persists despite efforts to bridge it. Questions about moral objectivity, free will versus determinism, and the role of science in understanding reality continue to generate controversy. In epistemology, internalism versus externalism debates how justification and knowledge operate. Political philosophy grapples with justice, rights, and the role of the state. Additionally, the influence of Eastern thought on Western philosophy, particularly in areas like personal identity and consciousness studies, raises questions about cross-cultural philosophical dialogue and synthesis.
