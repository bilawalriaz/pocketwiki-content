# Joke

## Overview
A joke is a short, structured narrative in oral literature designed to provoke laughter, culminating in a punchline that reveals a humorous element through wordplay, irony, or incongruity. Jokes are a fundamental form of social communication and cultural expression, studied across disciplines like linguistics, psychology, and anthropology to understand their structure, transmission, and role in human interaction.

## Timeline
- **c. 1900 BC** — Oldest identified joke, a Sumerian proverb, is recorded.
- **c. 1600 BC** — Second oldest joke, from Ancient Egypt, is found on the Westcar Papyrus.
- **4th-5th century AD** — The *Philogelos*, the earliest extant joke book, is compiled in ancient Greek.
- **1470** — Poggio Bracciolini's *Facetiae*, an early joke anthology, is published in Europe.
- **1905** — Sigmund Freud publishes *Jokes and their Relation to the Unconscious*.
- **1910** — The Aarne–Thompson classification system for folktales, including jokes, is first published.
- **1985** — Victor Raskin publishes the Script-based Semantic Theory of Humour (SSTH).
- **1989** — The International Society for Humor Studies is founded.
- **1991** — Raskin and Attardo develop the General Theory of Verbal Humour (GTVH).

## Body

### Definition and Structure
A joke is a brief oral narrative, often with dialogue, that builds to a punchline. The punchline is the pivot where the audience's understanding shifts from a primary interpretation to a secondary, incompatible one, creating humour. This process involves two stages: perceiving an incongruity and then resolving it. While jokes benefit from brevity, subverting this structure (e.g., the anti-joke "shaggy dog story") can also be humorous. Jokes are distinct from other forms of humour like slapstick or situational comedy.

### History and Transmission
Jokes are ancient, with the oldest known examples dating to c. 1900 BC. They were historically considered ephemeral, lowbrow entertainment. The printing press in the 15th century led to the spread of jestbooks. Jokes are passed anonymously in both private and public settings, and their text is often modified by tellers to suit the audience. The internet has created new, rapid cycles of topical joke dissemination.

### Telling and Context
Telling a joke is a cooperative act requiring mutual agreement between teller and audience. It follows a sequence: framing (e.g., "Have you heard the one..."), telling, and response. The context—including the relationship between teller and audience and the social setting—critically shapes the joke's text and reception. Jokes can function to gauge attitudes, mark group identity, or simply entertain.

### Joke Cycles and Classification
A joke cycle is a collection of jokes about a single target or situation, such as ethnic groups, professions, or catastrophes (e.g., the Challenger disaster). Folklorists study these cycles to understand cultural attitudes. Classification systems like the Aarne–Thompson index and the Thompson Motif Index organize jokes by tale type or story element. The more modern General Theory of Verbal Humour (GTVH) classifies jokes along six independent "Knowledge Resources": Script Opposition, Logical Mechanism, Situation, Target, Narrative Strategy, and Language.

### Academic Study
Jokes are studied from multiple perspectives. Psychologists explore the sense of humour and laughter as a response. Linguists, like Raskin and Attardo, analyze the semantic mechanisms that make a text funny. Folklorists and anthropologists examine jokes as cultural artifacts that reflect social norms and relationships, such as "joking relationships" of permitted disrespect. New fields like computational humour attempt to model joke generation and recognition, though this remains challenging due to the need for complex, real-world knowledge.

## Terms
- **Punchline**: The final part of a joke that signals a shift in interpretation and is intended to provoke laughter.
- **Script Opposition**: In humour theory, the clash between two opposing interpretations or "scripts" (e.g., real/unreal) triggered by the punchline.
- **Joke Cycle**: A collection of jokes sharing a common target, situation, or narrative structure.
- **Joking Relationship**: An anthropological term for a social bond characterized by institutionalised banter and permitted disrespect.
- **Ephemera**: Documents (like early joke broadsides) created for a specific, short-term purpose and not intended for preservation.
- **GTVH (General Theory of Verbal Humour)**: A linguistic classification system that describes jokes using six independent parameters or "Knowledge Resources."
- **Gelotology**: The scientific study of laughter and its effects on the body.
- **Folklore**: The traditional beliefs, customs, and stories of a community, passed through the generations by word of mouth.

## Debates and Open questions
- **Meaning and Function**: While some seek deeper meaning in jokes, others argue their primary value is entertainment. Scholars debate the specific social and psychological functions of different joke cycles.
- **Classification Challenges**: Existing systems like the Aarne–Thompson index are criticized for being hierarchical, ignoring obscene content, and being ill-suited for modern jokes. The GTVH attempts a multi-dimensional solution but is complex.
- **Computational Humour**: A major open question is whether computers can ever truly understand and generate humour, given that it requires encyclopedic, real-world knowledge and contextual understanding that machines currently lack.
- **Interdisciplinary Integration**: Researchers note that a complete understanding of jokes requires integrating insights from linguistics, psychology, folklore, and other fields, as each discipline only captures part of the phenomenon.
