# Nadia Boulanger

## Overview
Juliette Nadia Boulanger (1887–1979) was a French music teacher, conductor, and composer whose pedagogical influence shaped the trajectory of twentieth-century Western classical music. Born into a distinguished musical family, she abandoned a promising compositional career—believing she lacked the requisite talent—to focus on teaching, ultimately mentoring over 250 significant musicians, including Aaron Copland, Philip Glass, Quincy Jones, and Astor Piazzolla. Boulanger broke gender barriers as the first woman to conduct major orchestras in Europe and America, premiering works by Stravinsky and Copland. Her teaching philosophy emphasized rigorous technical mastery—harmony, counterpoint, and score analysis—as the foundation for individual artistic voice, encapsulated in her concept of *la grande ligne* (the long line), the architectural continuity of a musical work.

## Body

### Early Life and Musical Formation
Nadia Boulanger was born on her father Ernest’s 72nd birthday. Ernest, a Prix de Rome winner (1835) and Conservatoire professor, and Raissa, a Russian princess, provided an intensely musical household. Initially repelled by music, Nadia’s attitude shifted at age five during her mother’s pregnancy with Lili; she began reproducing sounds at the piano and absorbed her father’s singing lessons. At seven, she began Conservatoire preparation; at nine, she entered officially, studying solfège, harmony, organ, and composition with Gabriel Fauré. She won first prizes in harmony (1903), organ, *accompagnement au piano*, and fugue (1904). At her *accompagnement* exam, she met Raoul Pugno, a renowned pianist-organist-composer who became a mentor and collaborator. From 1904, she taught privately at the family apartment, establishing the Wednesday group class in analysis and sight-singing and the famed "at homes"—salons frequented by Stravinsky, Fauré, Paul Valéry, and others—that continued for seven decades.

### Compositional Ambitions and the Prix de Rome
Encouraged by Pugno and Fauré, Boulanger pursued the First Grand Prix de Rome, her father’s laurels. Her music, described by Caroline Potter as highly chromatic yet tonally based with Debussian influence, reached the final round in 1907 but did not win. In 1908, she submitted an instrumental fugue instead of the required vocal fugue, causing a press furor resolved only by ministerial decree to judge the work on musical merit; she won the Second Grand Prix for *La Sirène*. She and Pugno collaborated on the song cycle *Les Heures claires* (1908) and the opera *La ville morte* (1910–13). In 1909, Lili, then sixteen, declared her own intention to win the Prix. Nadia failed to reach the 1909 final; Lili won in 1913, the first woman to do so. During WWI, the sisters organized the *Comité Franco-Américain* to aid musician-soldiers. Lili’s health collapsed under the strain; she died in March 1918.

### Post-War Pivot: Teaching as Vocation
Lili’s death devastated Nadia. Jeanice Brooks notes a dichotomy of private grief (a home shrine, influenced by spiritism and Catholicism) and public fortitude; Nadia framed survival as a duty to "resemble" Lili. She performed over twenty concerts in 1919 programming her and Lili’s music, joined the new *École normale de musique* (founded by Cortot and Mangeot) teaching harmony, counterpoint, analysis, organ, and composition, and wrote music criticism for *Le Monde Musical*. A brief return to composition in 1920 (songs on Camille Mauclair texts) and two women’s-rights concerts featuring Lili’s music ended with her later disavowal of feminism and opposition to women’s suffrage. In 1921, she joined the inaugural faculty of the American Conservatory at Fontainebleau, where Aaron Copland was a student. Overwhelmed by her schedule, she suffered migraines and toothaches, ceased criticism, and told Fauré, "I wrote useless music," committing fully to teaching.

### Transatlantic Career and Conducting Breakthroughs
Boulanger’s 1924–25 US tour, arranged by Walter Damrosch and the New York Symphony, included the premiere of Copland’s *Symphony for Organ and Orchestra* (written for her). In 1927, George Gershwin sought lessons; after half an hour, she declared, "I can teach you nothing"—a verdict Gershwin treasured. Amid the 1934 Stavisky riots and general strike in Paris, she resumed conducting, debuting with the *École normale* orchestra (Mozart, Bach, Françaix) and soon leading the *Orchestre Philharmonique de Paris*. Her mother’s death in 1935 freed her for extended US work. In 1936, she substituted for Cortot in piano masterclasses and broadcast lecture-recitals for the BBC in London, becoming the first woman to conduct the London Philharmonic. Her 1937 HMV recordings of Monteverdi madrigals (on modern instruments, controversially) brought early music to a wider audience. That November, she was the first woman to conduct a full Royal Philharmonic Society concert (Fauré’s *Requiem*, Monteverdi’s *Amor*). Critics noted her restrained dynamics—never above *mezzo-forte*—yielding veiled sonorities of great expressive power. Her 1938 US tour brought premieres of Stravinsky’s *Dumbarton Oaks Concerto* and first-woman conductorships of the Boston Symphony, New York Philharmonic (Carnegie Hall), Philadelphia Orchestra, and Washington National Symphony—102 lectures in 118 days. HMV released her conducting Françaix’s Piano Concerto and playing Brahms with Dinu Lipatti.

### Wartime Exile and Post-War Consolidation
As war loomed in 1940, Boulanger helped students flee, departing at the last moment with Stravinsky via Madrid and Lisbon, arriving in New York 6 November 1940. She taught at the Longy School (Cambridge) and Peabody Conservatory (Baltimore), covering music history, harmony, counterpoint, fugue, orchestration, and composition. Returning to Paris in January 1946, she became professor of *accompagnement au piano* at the Conservatoire. In 1953, she assumed directorship of the Fontainebleau School. High-profile engagements followed: organizing music for the 1956 Monaco royal wedding, a 1958 US tour with television films, induction into Sigma Alpha Iota, a 1962 Turkey tour with protégée İdil Biret, a White House invitation from the Kennedys, and jury duty at the 1966 Moscow Tchaikovsky Competition. She taught at the Yehudi Menuhin School, Royal College of Music, and Royal Academy of Music (BBC-broadcast) into her eighties. Failing eyesight and hearing slowed but did not halt her work; a 90th-birthday celebration at Fontainebleau in 1977 featured a cake with 90 floating candles and a piece by student Emile Naoumoff. She died in Paris in 1979.

### Pedagogy: Craft, Attention, and *La Grande Ligne*
Boulanger distinguished the well-made work from the masterpiece: technical conditions for the latter are knowable, but its defining essence is not. She judged music solely on aesthetic content—"a good waltz has just as much value as a good fugue" (Lennox Berkeley). An admirer of Debussy, disciple of Ravel, champion of Stravinsky, she bore little sympathy for Schoenberg’s dodecaphony. Her core demand was total attention: "life is denied by lack of attention, whether it be to cleaning windows or trying to write a masterpiece." She viewed teaching as pleasure, privilege, and duty, accepting any student who wanted to learn. Treatment varied by ability: talented students faced rigorous interrogation and stress; less career-oriented pupils received leniency. Michel Legrand claimed disliked students were graduated quickly with first prizes, while serious students stayed years without reward. Her methods combined traditional harmony, score reading at piano, species counterpoint, analysis, and fixed-*Do* solfège sight-singing. She referenced repertoire constantly—e.g., noting a student’s harmonic progressions mirrored Bach and Chopin—and demanded *la grande ligne*, the long-line architectural continuity. She opposed innovation for its own sake: "never strain to avoid the obvious." Quincy Jones recalled her dictum: "Your music can never be more or less than you are as a human being." She insisted she could not bestow creativity, only the liberty to "read, to listen, to see, to understand," citing late bloomers (Rameau, Roussel, Wojtowicz) against the myth of childhood prodigy necessity. Her memory was prodigious: by twelve she knew Bach’s *Well-Tempered Clavier* by heart; Copland attested she knew "everything there was to know about music," from pre-Bach to post-Stravinsky, with all technical crafts at her fingertips. Students described her Bach playing as transformative—Murray Perahia "awed by the rhythm and character," Janet Craxton calling her chorales "the single greatest musical experience of my life."

### Honours and Key Works
Boulanger received the *Chevalier* (1932) and *Grand officier* (1977) of the Légion d’honneur, Order of Polonia Restituta (1934), Foreign Honorary Member of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences (1962), Howland Memorial Prize (1962), Médaille d’Or of the Académie des Beaux-Arts (1975), Order of the British Empire (1977), Order of St. Charles of Monaco (1977), Order of the Crown of Belgium (1977), and posthumous induction into the American Classical Music Hall of Fame (2012). Her compositions include over 30 songs (1901–1922), chamber works (3 *pièces* for organ/piano, 1911–14), organ pieces on Flemish tunes (1917), orchestral *Allegro* (1905) and *Fantaisie variée* (1912), and collaborations with Pugno: *Les Heures claires* (1909) and the opera *La ville morte* (1910–13).

## Terms
- **Prix de Rome**: Prestigious French government scholarship for composers (and other artists), awarded after a multi-round competition; winner received a multi-year residency at the Villa Medici in Rome. Ernest Boulanger won in 1835; Nadia won Second Grand Prix in 1908; Lili won First Grand Prix in 1913.
- **Conservatoire de Paris**: France’s principal state-funded music academy; Nadia entered at nine, studied with Fauré, Vierne, Guilmant, and later taught there (1946 onward).
- **Accompagnement au piano**: A Conservatoire discipline training pianists to reduce orchestral scores at sight, transpose, and accompany singers/instrumentalists; a core professional skill Nadia taught and examined.
- **Solfège / Fixed-Do**: Ear-training system where syllables (do, re, mi…) correspond to absolute pitches (C, D, E…), not scale degrees; Nadia used it for sight-singing and dictation.
- **Species Counterpoint**: Pedagogical method (Fux, *Gradus ad Parnassum*) teaching strict voice-leading through five graded "species" of note-against-note to florid counterpoint; a pillar of Nadia’s curriculum.
- **La grande ligne**: Nadia’s term for the long-range structural continuity and directional momentum that gives a work architectural coherence; her "one all-embracing principle" (Copland).
- **École normale de musique de Paris**: Private music school founded 1919 by Alfred Cortot and Auguste Mangeot; Nadia taught harmony, counterpoint, analysis, organ, composition there from 1919.
- **American Conservatory at Fontainebleau**: Summer school for American musicians founded 1921 at the Château de Fontainebleau; Nadia taught harmony from its first session, became director in 1953.
- **Monteverdi Madrigals**: Secular vocal works by Claudio Monteverdi (1567–1643); Nadia’s 1937 HMV recordings on modern instruments were landmark early-music advocacy.
- **Dodecaphony / Twelve-tone technique**: Compositional method by Arnold Schoenberg using all twelve chromatic pitches in a fixed order (tone row); Nadia "bore little sympathy" for it.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cessation of Composition**: Scholars debate whether Nadia stopped composing due to genuine self-assessment ("useless music"), grief and guilt over Lili’s death, internalized gender bias, or the overwhelming demands of teaching and family support. Caroline Potter notes her music was "highly chromatic… Debussy's influence apparent," suggesting a viable voice.
- **Gender Attitudes**: Despite her eminence and Lili’s historic Prix de Rome win, Nadia opposed women’s suffrage and held that a woman’s duty was wife/mother. Ned Rorem observed she "always give the benefit of the doubt to her male students while overtaxing the females." The contradiction between her practice and professed beliefs remains a focus of feminist musicology.
- **Pedagogical Uniformity vs. Individuality**: Virgil Thomson criticized her for applying "routine recipes and bromides from standard repertory," while Copland praised her singular focus on *la grande ligne*. The tension between her repertoire-based reference method and her insistence on "be yourself" is unresolved in student testimonies.
- **Early-Music Performance Practice**: Her 1937 Monteverdi recordings used modern instruments and forces, drawing criticism from purists. The debate anticipates the later Historical Informed Performance (HIP) movement; her role as popularizer vs. authenticity advocate is contested.
- **Canon Formation**: By championing Stravinsky and early music while marginalizing the Second Viennese School, Boulanger significantly shaped the mid-century Anglo-American compositional canon. The extent to which her personal tastes determined institutional curricula through her vast student network is an open historiographical question.
