# Gregorian chant

## Overview
Gregorian chant is the central tradition of Western plainchant, a form of monophonic, unaccompanied sacred song in Latin used in the Roman Catholic Church. It developed mainly in western and central Europe during the 9th and 10th centuries, becoming the official music of the Christian liturgy and a foundational element of Western musical notation and polyphony.

## Timeline
- **c. 375** — Antiphonal psalmody introduced to the West by St. Ambrose.
- **c. 520** — Benedict of Nursia establishes the Rule of St. Benedict, laying down the protocol for the Divine Office.
- **c. 872** — John the Deacon writes of Pope Gregory I's compilation of chants.
- **752–753** — Pope Stephen II celebrates Mass in Gaul using Roman chant.
- **785–786** — Pope Adrian I sends a papal sacramentary with Roman chants to Charlemagne's court.
- **c. 930** — First extant notated chant manuscripts (e.g., Graduale Laon) appear.
- **1025** — Guido d'Arezzo develops the gamut and hexachord system.
- **1562–63** — Council of Trent bans most sequences.
- **1614** — The *Editio medicea* drastically revises chant to contemporary standards.
- **1903** — Pope Pius X accepts the Solesmes restoration as authoritative.
- **1963** — Second Vatican Council reaffirms Gregorian chant's "pride of place" in liturgy.
- **2011** — First volume of the *Graduale Novum Editio Magis Critica* is published.

## Body

### Origins and Development
Gregorian chant evolved from earlier Christian plainchant, which was heavily influenced by Jewish psalmody. Distinct regional traditions (e.g., Gallican, Old Roman, Ambrosian) emerged after the fall of the Western Roman Empire. The chant later attributed to Pope Gregory I (c. 540–604) was likely a Carolingian synthesis of Roman and Gallican chants, commissioned by rulers like Charlemagne to unify liturgy. This synthesis was systematized, fitted into a modal theory, and notated with neumes, creating a uniform repertory that spread rapidly across Europe.

### Musical Form and Modality
Gregorian chant is monophonic and organized into eight modes, each defined by a final (ending note), dominant (reciting tone), and ambitus (range). Melodies are primarily stepwise, using a diatonic scale. Chants are categorized as syllabic (one note per syllable), neumatic (a few notes per syllable), or melismatic (many notes per syllable). They function liturgically as recitatives, antiphonal chants (with refrains), or responsorial chants (alternating solo and choir). The system of hexachords, developed by Guido d'Arezzo, organized pitches for singing.

### Notation and Rhythm
Early notation used neumes to indicate melodic contour but not exact pitch. By the 13th century, square notation on a four-line staff became standard. The rhythm of chant is debated; medieval notation suggests a free rhythm guided by text accent, not a fixed meter. Modern performance practice, influenced by the Solesmes school, uses equal note values with slight lengthenings for emphasis, though musicologists continue to study early manuscripts for more authentic rhythmic interpretation.

### Liturgical Function and Performance
Gregorian chant is performed unaccompanied, traditionally by male choirs in monasteries and churches. It serves two main liturgical cycles: the Mass (with Proper chants like the Introit and Alleluia, and Ordinary chants like the Kyrie and Gloria) and the Divine Office (canonical hours). While once obligatory, the Second Vatican Council allowed other sacred music, though chant retains a privileged status.

### Influence and Legacy
Gregorian chant is the direct ancestor of Western musical notation. Its melodies provided the foundation (*cantus firmus*) for medieval and Renaissance polyphony. The development of organum (early harmony) and the compositional form of the Mass were built upon chant. Though its liturgical use has diminished, it remains a vital subject of musicological study and a living tradition in some monastic communities.

## Terms
- **Plainchant**: A general term for monophonic, unaccompanied sacred song.
- **Monophonic**: Music with a single melodic line, without harmony.
- **Neume**: An early form of musical notation indicating melodic shape.
- **Mode**: A system of organizing pitches around a central note (final) and a reciting tone (dominant).
- **Ambitus**: The range of pitches in a melody.
- **Centonization**: The practice of composing a chant by assembling stock melodic phrases.
- **Hexachord**: A six-note scale pattern (C, F, or G) used in medieval music theory.
- **Gamut**: The complete system of pitches and hexachords used in chant.
- **Cantus firmus**: A pre-existing melody (often a chant) used as the basis for a polyphonic composition.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authorship**: The role of Pope Gregory I is legendary; scholars agree the mature chant is a later Carolingian synthesis.
- **Rhythm**: There is no consensus on medieval performance practice. Theories range from free rhythm (Solesmes) to proportional mensuralism, with ongoing research into neume interpretation.
- **Melodic Authenticity**: Modern critical editions (e.g., *Graduale Novum*) aim to correct errors in later sources by comparing early manuscripts, but decisions on melodic variants remain scholarly challenges.
- **Liturgical Role**: The precise balance between preserving chant's traditional "pride of place" and integrating other sacred music in modern worship is an ongoing discussion.
