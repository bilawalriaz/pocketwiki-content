# Provence

## Overview
Provence is a historical and cultural region in southeastern France, stretching from the Rhône River to the Italian border and the Mediterranean Sea. It was the first Roman province beyond the Alps, giving it its name, and has maintained a distinct cultural and linguistic identity despite being part of France for over 500 years. Its history of successive rulers, from Romans to Counts of Provence, and its role as the seat of the Avignon Papacy, have shaped its unique character.

## Timeline
- **c. 600 BC** — Greek colonists from Phocaea found Massalia (Marseille).
- **125 BC** — Romans establish permanent settlements after defeating a Celtic confederation.
- **49 BC** — Massalia loses territories after siding with Pompey against Julius Caesar.
- **1309–1377** — The Avignon Papacy: seven popes reign in Avignon.
- **1481** — Provence is legally incorporated into the French royal domain.
- **1720–1722** — The Great Plague of Marseille kills about 40,000 people.
- **1793** — Napoleon Bonaparte helps recapture Toulon from British forces.
- **1860** — The County of Nice is annexed by France.
- **1944** — Allied Operation Dragoon lands on the Var coast, liberating Provence.

## Body

### Ancient and Roman Provence
The coast shows evidence of human habitation dating back over a million years, with significant Paleolithic sites like the Cosquer Cave (27,000–19,000 BC). By the Neolithic period, settled farming communities emerged. From the 10th to 4th centuries BC, the region was inhabited by Ligurian tribes, later joined and partly displaced by Celtic migrants. The Greeks established the major trading port of Massalia (Marseille) around 600 BC, which flourished and founded numerous colonies. In the 2nd century BC, Rome intervened to protect Massalia from local tribes, leading to the conquest of the region. It became the Roman province *Provincia Romana* (later *Gallia Narbonensis*), heavily Romanized with infrastructure like roads, aqueducts, and cities such as Arles and Nîmes.

### Medieval Rule and the Avignon Papacy
After Roman power waned, Provence saw invasions by Germanic tribes (Visigoths, Ostrogoths, Burgundians, Franks) and Saracens. It was ruled by various dynasties, including the Bosonids and later the House of Aragon, before passing to the Angevin dynasty in 1246. The 14th century was marked by the Avignon Papacy (1309–1377), when the papal court resided in Avignon, and by the devastation of the Black Death. The last independent ruler was "Good King René" (d. 1480), after which Provence was legally united with France in 1486.

### Integration into France and Modern History
Provence experienced the Wars of Religion in the 16th century and later revolts against Bourbon centralization, notably in Marseille. The 18th century brought economic growth but also the Great Plague of Marseille (1720). During the French Revolution, the region was largely royalist and saw significant violence. Under Napoleon, it suffered from British blockades. The 19th century brought prosperity via trade with North Africa and the arrival of the railroad. The 20th century saw World War II occupation and liberation (1944), followed by post-war reconstruction, mass tourism, and immigration from North Africa.

### Geography and Climate
Provence is characterized by the Rhône and Durance rivers, the Camargue delta (western Europe's largest), and varied mountain ranges like the Maritime Alps, Mont Ventoux, and the Calanques. The landscape includes the *garrigue*, a scrubland of aromatic shrubs. The climate is predominantly Mediterranean, with hot, dry summers and mild winters, influenced by the cold, dry *mistral* wind. Micro-climates exist, from Alpine conditions in the highlands to the sunniest coast around Toulon.

### Culture and Language
The historical language is Provençal, a dialect of Occitan, which saw a literary golden age with the troubadours (11th–12th centuries) and a 19th-century revival led by Frédéric Mistral. The region's art was shaped by its light, attracting painters like Cézanne, Van Gogh, and Matisse. Cuisine is Mediterranean, based on olive oil, garlic, seafood, and herbs, with iconic dishes like *bouillabaisse* and *ratatouille*. Wine production, especially rosé, is significant, with key appellations like Côtes de Provence and Bandol. The traditional liqueur is *pastis*.

## Terms
- **Provincia Romana**: The name given by Romans to the region, meaning "the Roman province," which evolved into "Provence."
- **Oppidum**: A large fortified Celtic or Gallo-Roman settlement, often on a hilltop.
- **Garrigue**: A type of low, aromatic scrubland found on limestone soils in the Mediterranean region.
- **Mistral**: A strong, cold, dry wind that blows down the Rhône Valley towards the coast.
- **Calanques**: Narrow, steep-walled inlets along the Mediterranean coast, formed in limestone cliffs.
- **Félibrige**: A 19th-century literary movement to revive the Provençal (Occitan) language and culture.
- **Appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC)**: A French certification granted to certain French geographical indications for wines, cheeses, and other agricultural products.
- **Pastis**: An anise-flavored liqueur and the traditional drink of Provence.
