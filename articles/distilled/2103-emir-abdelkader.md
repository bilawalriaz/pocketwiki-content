# Emir Abdelkader

## Overview
Abd al-Qadir ibn Muhyi al-Din (c. 1806–1883) was an Algerian religious scholar and military leader who organized a 17-year resistance against the French colonial invasion of Algeria (1831–1847). As an Islamic scholar and Sufi unexpectedly thrust into military command, he united Algerian tribes and is noted for his humanitarian conduct in warfare. After surrender and imprisonment in France, he later settled in Damascus, where his intervention to save Christians during a massacre earned him international acclaim.

- **14 Aug 1844** — Morocco, his ally, is defeated by France at the Battle of Isly.

- **22 Dec 1847** — Surrenders to French General Lamoricière.
- **16 Oct 1852** — Released from imprisonment in France by President Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte.
- **Dec 1855** — Settles in Damascus, Syria.
- **July 1860** — Saves thousands of Christians during a massacre in Damascus.
- **26 May 1883** — Dies in Damascus; buried in the mausoleum of Ibn Arabi.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into the religious elite of the Ottoman Regency of Algiers, Abd al-Qadir's family claimed descent from the Prophet Muhammad. He received a rigorous traditional Islamic education at his father's zawiya (religious school), mastering the Quran, jurisprudence, and theology. A pilgrimage to Mecca and travels to Damascus and Baghdad (1825–1827) deepened his religious commitment and exposed him to political reforms in the region.

### Resistance Leadership (1832–1847)
Following the French invasion of Algiers in 1830, tribal leaders elected the young scholar Abd al-Qadir as Emir to lead a jihad (struggle) against the occupiers in 1832. He quickly united the tribes of western Algeria. His early military success and diplomatic skill led to the Desmichels Treaty (1834) and later the more favorable Treaty of Tafna (1837), which recognized his authority over a large interior state. During the ensuing peace, he consolidated a theocratic administration, minted currency, built schools, and organized a standing army. The peace collapsed in 1839 when France violated the treaty. The subsequent French campaign, led by General Bugeaud, employed a devastating scorched-earth policy, destroying Abd al-Qadir's fortifications and economic base. After the capture of his smala in 1843 and French military pressure, he withdrew to Morocco. Following defeats by both French and Moroccan forces, he surrendered to the French in December 1847.

### Imprisonment and Exile
Despite promises of safe passage to the Ottoman Empire, Abd al-Qadir was imprisoned in France for nearly five years (1848–1852). His cause became a cause célèbre, with figures like Victor Hugo advocating for his release. President Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte freed him in 1852, and Abd al-Qadir swore an oath not to incite further unrest in Algeria. He first settled in Bursa, Turkey, before moving to Damascus in 1855.

### Later Life in Damascus
In Damascus, Abd al-Qadir lived as a revered scholar and Sufi mystic. In July 1860, during a sectarian massacre of Christians, he personally intervened to save thousands of lives, sheltering refugees in his home and the city citadel. This act earned him international honors, including the Grand Cross of the Legion of Honour from France and gifts from Abraham Lincoln and Great Britain. He authored the *Kitab al-Mawaqif* ("Book of Halts"), a major work on Sufi spirituality. He died in Damascus in 1883 and was buried in the mausoleum of the mystic Ibn Arabi.

## Terms
- **Emir (Amir al-Mu'minin):** "Commander of the Faithful," a title denoting supreme political and religious authority.
- **Jihad:** An Islamic term meaning "struggle," here referring to the armed resistance against French colonial invasion.
- **Zawiya:** A religious school or lodge, often associated with Sufi orders.
- **Sufi:** A Muslim mystic or adherent of Sufism, the branch of Islam focused on inner spiritual experience.
- **Smala (Deira):** The mobile capital or encampment of Abd al-Qadir's itinerant state, housing his court, administration, and followers.
- **Khalifalik:** A province within Abd al-Qadir's state, governed by a caliph (khalifa) appointed by him.
- **Scorched-earth policy:** A military strategy of destroying resources (crops, villages, livestock) to deny them to the enemy and break civilian support.
- **Fatwa:** A ruling on a point of Islamic law given by a qualified authority.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Responsibility for the 1846 prisoner massacre:** The execution of approximately 300 French prisoners was ordered by a lieutenant in Abd al-Qadir's absence. While Abd al-Qadir later took formal responsibility to protect his men, contemporary French authorities who cross-checked evidence concluded he was innocent of direct involvement.
- **Nature of his state:** Scholars debate whether the state Abd al-Qadir built was a proto-national entity or primarily a religiously-defined polity. His consistent refusal of the title "sultan" and emphasis on spiritual authority over political power complicate a purely nationalist interpretation.
- **Legacy in Algeria:** His legacy is complex within Algeria. While revered as a hero of resistance, his oath to Napoleon III not to stir unrest and his later life in exile have been subjects of discussion regarding his role in the longer struggle for independence.
