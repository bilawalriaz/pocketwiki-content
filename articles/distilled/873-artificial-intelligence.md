# Artificial intelligence

## Overview
Artificial intelligence (AI) is the study and engineering of computational systems capable of performing tasks typically associated with human intelligence—learning, reasoning, perception, and decision-making. It matters because it underpins transformative technologies across science, industry, and society, while raising profound questions about ethics, labor, and existential risk.

## Timeline
- **c. 1943** – McCulloch and Pitts introduce artificial neurons.
- **1950** – Turing proposes the Turing test in *Computing Machinery and Intelligence*.
- **1956** – AI founded at the Dartmouth workshop; Logic Theorist presented.
- **1974** – First AI winter begins after Lighthill Report criticism.
- **1980s** – Expert systems revive funding; second AI winter follows by late 1980s.
- **1997** – IBM’s Deep Blue defeats chess champion Garry Kasparov.
- **2012** – Deep learning with GPUs dominates benchmarks, sparking AI boom.
- **2016** – AlphaGo defeats Go champion Lee Sedol.
- **2020** – GPT-3 released; 2022 sees ChatGPT launch, accelerating public adoption.

## Body

### Goals and Subfields
AI research breaks intelligence into subproblems: reasoning, knowledge representation, planning, natural language processing (NLP), perception, and robotics. Reasoning evolved from symbolic logic to probabilistic models handling uncertainty. Knowledge representation uses symbols, ontologies, and knowledge bases, though large language models (LLMs) now learn implicitly from text. Planning involves agents acting rationally via utility maximization and expected utility calculations. Learning includes supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement, transfer, and deep learning. NLP enables reading, writing, translation, and dialogue. Perception uses sensors for vision, speech, and robotics. Social intelligence explores affective computing and human-like interaction. General intelligence (AGI) aims for human-level versatility.

### Techniques
Search and optimization include state space search (e.g., means-ends analysis) and local search (e.g., gradient descent, evolutionary algorithms). Logic encompasses propositional and predicate logic, fuzzy logic, and non-monotonic reasoning. Probabilistic methods like Bayesian networks and Markov decision processes handle uncertainty. Statistical learning includes classifiers (decision trees, SVMs, naive Bayes) and neural networks. Deep learning uses multi-layered networks (CNNs, RNNs, LSTMs) for pattern recognition. Transformers and attention mechanisms revolutionized NLP. Hardware advances—especially GPUs and TPUs—enabled scaling.

### Applications
AI powers search engines, recommendation systems, autonomous vehicles, medical diagnostics (e.g., AlphaFold for protein folding), gaming (Deep Blue, AlphaGo, AlphaStar), finance, military systems, and generative content creation. Ethical concerns span bias, privacy, environmental impact, misinformation, job displacement, and existential risk.

### Ethics and Risks
Bias arises from skewed training data (e.g., Google Photos labeling Black users as “gorillas”). Transparency issues plague deep networks; explainability tools like SHAP and LIME attempt mitigation. Privacy risks stem from mass data collection. Environmental costs grow with compute demands. Misinformation spreads via recommendation algorithms optimizing engagement. Autonomous weapons and surveillance raise safety and governance concerns. Debates continue over AGI timelines, regulation, and whether AI poses existential threats.

### History and Philosophy
AI originated in mid-20th century cybernetics and computation theory. Cycles of optimism and funding cuts shaped its trajectory. Philosophical debates center on defining intelligence, machine consciousness, and the symbolic vs. connectionist divide. Frameworks like the EU AI Act and global summits address governance. Open questions include AGI feasibility, alignment, and long-term societal impact.

## Terms
- **Agent**: An entity that perceives its environment and acts to achieve goals.
- **Backpropagation**: Algorithm for training neural networks by propagating errors backward to adjust weights.
- **Transformer**: Neural architecture using attention mechanisms, foundational to modern LLMs.
- **Utility**: Numerical measure of an agent’s preference for a given state.
- **Ontology**: Formal specification of concepts, relations, and properties in a domain.
- **Hallucination**: When an AI generates false or nonsensical outputs confidently.
- **Reinforcement Learning**: Training agents via rewards/punishments for actions.
- **Markov Decision Process**: Mathematical framework for modeling decision-making under uncertainty.
- **Fairness**: Ensuring AI systems avoid discriminatory outcomes across groups.
- **Explainable AI (XAI)**: Methods making AI decisions interpretable to humans.

## Debates and Open Questions
Key debates include whether AGI is achievable or desirable, how to align superintelligent systems with human values, the validity of existential risk warnings versus techno-optimism, and the adequacy of current regulatory frameworks. Disagreements persist on job displacement severity, the role of symbolic versus neural approaches, and balancing innovation with safety. The timeline for transformative AI remains hotly contested among researchers and policymakers.
