# Czechoslovakia

## Overview
Czechoslovakia was a Central European state that existed from 1918 to 1992. It was founded as a democratic republic after World War I, endured Nazi occupation and division during World War II, was re-established as a communist state within the Eastern Bloc, and peacefully dissolved into the Czech Republic and Slovakia in 1992. Its history reflects the major conflicts and ideological struggles of 20th-century Europe.

## Timeline
- **1918** — Czechoslovakia declares independence from Austria-Hungary.
- **1938** — Munich Agreement cedes the Sudetenland to Nazi Germany.
- **1939** — State dismembered; Slovak Republic proclaimed, German Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia established.
- **1945** — State re-established after WWII, minus Carpathian Ruthenia.
- **1948** — Communist Party seizes power in a coup.
- **1955** — Czechoslovakia joins the Warsaw Pact.
- **1968** — Prague Spring liberalization period ends with Soviet-led invasion.
- **1989** — Velvet Revolution peacefully ends communist rule.
- **1992** — Czechoslovakia dissolves into the Czech Republic and Slovakia.

## Body

### Formation and First Republic (1918–1938)
Czechoslovakia was founded in 1918 from the lands of the dissolved Austro-Hungarian Empire, including Bohemia, Moravia, parts of Silesia, Slovakia, and Carpathian Ruthenia. Led by its first president, Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk, it was a democratic republic. The state was multi-ethnic, with Czechs (51%) and Germans (22%) as the largest groups. Its official ideology of "Czechoslovakism" defined the population as a single nation, which was resisted by Slovak nationalists and other minorities. During the interwar period, it was a stable democracy and formed the "Little Entente" alliance with Romania and Yugoslavia.

### World War II and Occupation (1938–1945)
In 1938, the Munich Agreement allowed Nazi Germany to annex the Sudetenland, crippling Czechoslovak defenses. In 1939, the state was dismembered: Slovakia became a Nazi satellite state, Carpathian Ruthenia was annexed by Hungary, and the remainder became the German Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia. The Nazi regime pursued plans for the Germanization and eradication of the Czech intelligentsia. The Jewish population was virtually annihilated. A government-in-exile under Edvard Beneš continued to fight with the Allies.

### Communist Era (1948–1989)
After WWII, the state was re-established without Carpathian Ruthenia (ceded to the USSR). In 1948, the Communist Party seized power, establishing a Marxist-Leninist state with a command economy. It became a satellite of the Soviet Union, joining Comecon (1949) and the Warsaw Pact (1955). A period of political liberalization in 1968, the Prague Spring, was crushed by a Soviet-led invasion. In 1969, the country was formally federalized into Czech and Slovak republics. Dissent grew in the 1970s and 1980s, led by figures like Václav Havel.

### Dissolution (1989–1992)
The peaceful Velvet Revolution began on 17 November 1989, leading to the end of communist rule by 28 November. The country was renamed the Czech and Slovak Federative Republic. Growing nationalist tensions led parliament to vote for a peaceful dissolution. On 31 December 1992, Czechoslovakia split into two independent states: the Czech Republic and Slovakia.

## Terms
- **Sudetenland**: The border region of Czechoslovakia with a majority ethnic German population, annexed by Nazi Germany in 1938.
- **Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia**: The rump Czech lands under direct German administration during WWII.
- **Prague Spring**: A 1968 period of political liberalization in Czechoslovakia, ended by Soviet invasion.
- **Velvet Revolution**: The non-violent 1989 transition from communist rule to democracy.
- **Comecon**: The Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, the economic organization of the Eastern Bloc.
- **Warsaw Pact**: The military alliance of the Soviet Union and its Eastern European satellite states.
- **Czechoslovakism**: The official state ideology promoting a unified Czechoslovak nation.
- **Beneš Decrees**: Post-WWII presidential decrees concerning the status and property of ethnic Germans and Hungarians.

## Debates and open questions
- The legacy and validity of the Beneš Decrees, which abrogated citizenship and confiscated property from ethnic Germans and Hungarians, remain controversial among nationalist groups in the Czech Republic, Germany, Austria, and Hungary.
- The policy of "Czechoslovakism" and the political subordination of Slovaks within the state are cited as central issues that weakened the country and contributed to its eventual dissolution.
