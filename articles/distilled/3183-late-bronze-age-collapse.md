# Late Bronze Age collapse

## Overview
The Late Bronze Age collapse was a widespread societal breakdown in the Eastern Mediterranean and Near East during the late 13th to early 12th century BC. It marked the sudden end of interconnected palace economies and literate states, leading to a sharp material decline, the loss of writing systems, and a transition into regional "Dark Ages." Understanding this collapse is crucial for studying the fragility of complex societies and the transition from the Bronze Age to the Iron Age.

## Timeline
- **c. 1550–1200 BC** — Late Bronze Age: Era of advanced, interconnected palace economies (Egyptians, Hittites, Mycenaeans).
- **c. 1200 BC** — Traditional date for the start of the collapse; widespread destruction and abandonment of sites.
- **c. 1190 BC** — Destruction of Ugarit; fall of the Mycenaean palace at Mycenae.
- **c. 1186–1155 BC** — Reign of Ramesses III; Egypt defeats invasions by the "Sea Peoples."
- **c. 1180 BC** — Destruction of Pylos in Greece.
- **c. 1178 BC** — Ugarit is confirmed destroyed by this date.
- **c. 1200–800 BC** — Greek Dark Ages: Period of small, isolated village cultures following the collapse.
- **c. 1100 BC** — Phoenician city-states gain autonomy as Egyptian and Assyrian power wanes.
- **c. 1056 BC** — Death of Ashur-bel-kala; the Middle Assyrian Empire begins to withdraw and weaken.

## Body

### Background and Interconnected Systems
The Late Bronze Age was characterized by advanced civilizations with complex institutions, monumental architecture, and extensive trade networks. These societies relied on a "palace economy," a system where wealth was concentrated in a centralized bureaucracy and redistributed by the sovereign. This intricate web of political and economic interdependence made the entire region vulnerable to cascading failures if a key node was disrupted.

### Evidence of Collapse by Region
The collapse was not uniform. In **Anatolia**, the Hittite Empire fragmented and its capital, Hattusa, was burned. In **Greece**, all major Mycenaean palaces were destroyed, leading to a 90% abandonment of small sites in the Peloponnese and a long "Dark Age." **Egypt** survived but was severely weakened, withdrawing from the Levant and suffering economic strain from constant warfare. In the **Levant**, cities like Ugarit were violently destroyed, while others like Lachish were rebuilt. **Syria** saw the rise of Aramean populations and the destruction of key trade centers. **Cyprus** experienced less destruction, with some sites like Kition showing cultural continuity.

### Scholarly Debate on Scale
Initial scholarship, notably by Robert Drews, argued for near-universal violent destruction of cities between Pylos and Gaza around 1200 BC. However, recent archaeological reassessment, particularly by Jesse Millek, challenges this view. Millek's research indicates that over 60% of the 153 destruction events traditionally ascribed to this period were either misdated, assumed with little evidence, or never occurred. This suggests the collapse may have been more limited in scale than previously thought.

### Proposed Causes
No single cause is accepted; a combination of factors is likely. **Climate change** is a major theory, with evidence of severe drought in the Eastern Mediterranean from c. 1198–1196 BC. **Migratory invasions** by groups known as the "Sea Peoples" are documented in Egyptian records, though their exact role is debated. **Technological shifts**, such as the spread of ironworking and new infantry tactics, may have destabilized chariot-based states. Other proposed factors include **earthquakes**, **pandemics** (possibly an early bubonic plague), and **systemic failure** due to over-complexity and economic disruption.

## Terms
- **Palace Economy**: A redistributive economic system where a central authority (the palace) controls production and distribution of goods.
- **Sea Peoples**: A modern term for a confederacy of naval raiders who attacked the Eastern Mediterranean civilizations at the end of the Bronze Age.
- **Dark Ages**: A period of societal regression, reduced literacy, and simpler material culture following the collapse, notably in Greece.
- **Chariot Warfare**: A dominant military tactic of the Bronze Age elite, potentially rendered obsolete by new infantry weapons.
- **Ironworking**: The production of iron tools and weapons, which spread during and after the collapse, marking the transition to the Iron Age.
- **Arameans**: Northwest Semitic-speaking peoples who migrated into Syria and the Levant during the collapse, eventually dominating the region linguistically.
- **General Systems Collapse**: A theory proposing that societal collapse results from increasing complexity beyond a sustainable level.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Scale of Destruction**: Was the collapse a universal cataclysm or a more limited series of regional crises? Recent archaeology favors the latter.
- **Causality**: What was the primary driver—climate change, invasion, internal revolt, or systemic failure? Most scholars now argue for a "perfect storm" of multiple, interacting factors.
- **Role of the Sea Peoples**: Were they a primary cause of collapse, or merely opportunistic raiders exploiting already-weakened states?
- **Continuity vs. Rupture**: To what extent did cultural and economic practices continue uninterrupted in certain regions (e.g., Cyprus, parts of the Levant) despite political collapse?
