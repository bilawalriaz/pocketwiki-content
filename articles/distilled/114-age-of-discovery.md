# Age of Discovery

## Overview

The Age of Discovery (c. 1418 – c. 1620), also called the Age of Exploration, was an early modern period during which European seafarers explored, colonized, and conquered regions across the globe. It connected previously isolated parts of the world into a single world-system, laying the groundwork for globalization and the interconnected global economy of the 21st century. The era saw the opening of maritime routes to the East Indies, the European colonization of the Americas, and the creation of European colonial empires, reshaping global power dynamics and initiating the Columbian exchange between the Old and New Worlds.

## Body

### Concept and Background

The term "age of discovery" has been scrutinized; J. H. Parry called it the "Age of Reconnaissance," arguing it produced the expansion of geographical knowledge and empirical science. Edmundo O'Gorman argued the physical encounter with new territories was less important than Europeans' effort to integrate this new knowledge into their worldview—what he called "the invention of America." The "discovery doctrine," expounded by the US Supreme Court in 1823, drew on assertions of European powers' right to claim land during explorations; this concept has been challenged by Indigenous peoples as forced and negating indigenous presence.

After the fall of the Western Roman Empire, Christian Europe was largely a backwater compared to the Muslim world. The Crusades brought Europe into contact with the Middle East and its valuable goods. Muslim control of territory and the trade monopolies of Venice and Genoa long obstructed trade east of the Strait of Gibraltar. Key technological advances were the adoption of the magnetic compass (invented during the Chinese Han dynasty, spread to Europe by the late 12th or early 13th century) and the fusion of northern European and Mediterranean shipbuilding traditions, producing the full-rigged ship and the caravel. The rediscovery of Ptolemy's Geographia in 1400 reinforced the idea that the Indian Ocean was landlocked. Medieval overland travelers—including Marco Polo (1271–1295) and Ibn Battuta (1325–1354)—had little immediate effect, as the Mongol Empire collapsed and the Black Death blocked travel.

### Chinese Missions (1405–1433)

Between 1405 and 1421, the Ming emperor Yongle sponsored large tributary missions in the Indian Ocean under admiral Zheng He. The fleets visited Arabia, East Africa, India, the Malay Archipelago, and Thailand, exchanging goods and presenting gifts. The voyages had a significant effect on maritime networks, promoting ports like Malacca, Cochin, and Malindi, and generating revenue for China. However, after the emperor's death, the Ming retreated into the haijin policy of isolationism, and successor emperors felt the expeditions were harmful to the Chinese state.

### Portuguese and Spanish Exploration

The Portuguese began systematically exploring the Atlantic coast of Africa in 1418 under Prince Henry the Navigator. In 1488, Bartolomeu Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope, proving the Indian Ocean accessible from the Atlantic. In 1492, Spain funded Columbus's plan to sail west to reach the Indies; he encountered a continent uncharted by Europeans (though explored by Norse 500 years earlier). The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) divided the world between Portugal and Spain. In 1498, Vasco da Gama reached India by sailing around Africa. In 1500, Cabral claimed Brazil for Portugal. In 1513, Balboa reached the Pacific from the New World. The Magellan–Elcano expedition (1519–1522) completed the first circumnavigation.

### Indian Ocean and Pacific

Portuguese expansion continued eastward: Malacca was conquered in 1511, the Spice Islands (Banda) reached in 1512, and China in 1513. The Portuguese were defeated by the Chinese in 1521 and 1522 but were allowed to occupy Macau in 1557. In the Pacific, Magellan's voyage gave Spain valuable knowledge; the Treaty of Zaragoza (1529) allocated the Spice Islands to Portugal and the Philippines to Spain. Inland Spanish expeditions conquered the Aztec Empire (1519–1521) and the Inca Empire (1532–1533), driven by conquistadors—soldiers of fortune who supplied their own equipment in exchange for profit shares. European diseases like smallpox decimated indigenous populations.

### Northern European Involvement

France, the Netherlands, and England did not recognize the Treaty of Tordesillas. After the Dutch rebelled against Spain in 1568 (Eighty Years' War) and Philip II became King of Portugal in 1580, England and the Netherlands challenged the resulting Catholic superpower. The fall of Antwerp in 1585 drove skilled Protestant merchants to Amsterdam, transforming it into a major commercial center. Dutch and British interest led to the foundation of English (1600) and Dutch (1602) chartered companies. Northern European explorers searched for northern passages: Willoughby and Chancellor opened trade with Russia (1553); Frobisher sought a northwest passage (1576); Barentsz explored the Arctic (1594–1597); Hudson explored the Hudson River and Hudson Bay (1609–1611). Willem Janszoon made the first recorded European landfall on Australia in 1606; Abel Tasman circumnavigated New Holland (1642–1644), proving Australia was not part of the mythical southern continent.

### Russian Exploration of Siberia

After conquering the Tatar khanates of Kazan and Astrakhan, Russia opened the way to the Urals. In 1581, Yermak began his conquest of the Khanate of Sibir, capturing its capital in 1582. Russian exploration resumed in the early 17th century, led by Cossacks hunting furs. Pyanda explored the Lena River (1620–1624); Beketov founded Yakutsk (1632); Ivanov discovered Lake Baikal (1643); Moskvitin reached the Pacific (1639); Poyarkov reached the Amur River (1644); Dezhnyov passed through the Bering Strait (1648). By the mid-17th century, Russians had explored almost all of Siberia.

### Global Impact

European expansion produced the Columbian exchange, transferring plants, animals, populations, diseases, and culture between hemispheres. Europeans brought cattle, horses, and sheep to the New World; they received tobacco, potatoes, tomatoes, and maize. Eurasian diseases wiped out 50–90% of the American Indian population. The global silver trade saw China receive 100 tons of silver annually between 1600 and 1800; Mexico and Peru produced about 80% of the world's silver from 1500 to 1800. The European economic center shifted from the Mediterranean to Western Europe, with Antwerp and then Amsterdam becoming central. Spain's influx of gold and silver caused a price revolution and inflation, leading to Spain's first bankruptcy in 1557.

## Terms

- **Caravel**: An Iberian ship type with a sternpost-mounted rudder and shallow draft, used extensively for exploring the African coast from 1440.
- **Columbian exchange**: The transfer of plants, animals, human populations, diseases, and culture between the Old World and New World.
- **Conquistadors**: Spanish members of inland expeditions; soldiers of fortune who supplied their own equipment in exchange for a share of profits.
- **Encomienda**: A system of tribute and forced labor that formed the initial economy of Spanish colonies.
- **Haijin**: The Ming dynasty's policy of isolationism that halted Zheng He's voyages.
- **Mare clausum**: The policy declaring the Atlantic closed to other states, initiated by papal bulls granting Portugal exclusive rights.
- **Nanban trade**: The trade period initiated by the Portuguese arrival in Japan in 1543, during which the Japanese adopted technologies like the arquebus.
- **Price revolution**: The inflationary cycle in Spain and Europe caused by the influx of gold and silver from the New World.
- **Volta do mar**: A maneuver of sailing westward in the Atlantic to avoid becalming, which led Cabral to encounter Brazil.

## Debates and open questions

The concept of "discovery" itself is debated: scholars like Edmundo O'Gorman argue the encounter was less important than the European effort to integrate new knowledge into their worldview ("the invention of America"), while Indigenous peoples and researchers have challenged the colonial claiming of "discovery" as forced and negating indigenous presence. The term "Age of Exploration" has been discussed as framed for colonial ventures, discrimination, and exploitation; alternatives like "Age of Contact" or "Contact Period" have been proposed. Some historians suggest the Portuguese may have encountered the South American bulge earlier than Cabral's 1500 landing, explaining John II's insistence on moving the Tordesillas line west. The theory of Portuguese discovery of Australia, supported by historian Kenneth McIntyre, is one among several competing theories. Claims that João Vaz Corte-Real discovered Newfoundland in 1473 are based on sources considered unreliable.
