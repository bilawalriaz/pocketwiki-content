# Hans Christian Andersen

## Overview
Hans Christian Andersen (1805–1875) was a Danish writer whose 156 fairy tales, translated into over 125 languages, became foundational to Western children's literature. Though he also wrote novels, plays, and travelogues, he is remembered primarily for stories like "The Little Mermaid," "The Ugly Duckling," and "The Emperor's New Clothes," which blend childlike wonder with moral and existential depth.

## Timeline
- **1805**: Born in Odense, Denmark, on 2 April.
- **1816**: Father dies; mother remarries; Andersen moves to Copenhagen to pursue acting.
- **1822**: Publishes first story, "The Ghost at Palnatoke's Grave."
- **1829**: Gains recognition with "A Journey on Foot from Holmen's Canal to the East Point of Amager."
- **1833**: Receives travel grant from King Frederick VI; begins European travels.
- **1835–1837**: Fairy Tales Told for Children published in three installments by C. A. Reitzel.
- **1835**: "The Little Mermaid" and "The Emperor's New Clothes" appear in final installment.
- **1847**: First visit to England; meets Charles Dickens.
- **1851**: Publishes travelogue *In Sweden*.
- **1872**: Falls ill after injury; develops liver cancer.
- **1875**: Dies on 4 August at age 70 in Rolighed, near Copenhagen.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into modest circumstances in Odense, Andersen was the son of a cobbler who falsely believed the family had noble origins. His mother, an illiterate washerwoman, remarried after his father’s death in 1816. Sent to a school for poor children, Andersen worked as an apprentice weaver and tailor before moving to Copenhagen at 14 to pursue acting. Though his voice changed and ended his theatrical ambitions, a colleague recognized his poetic talent. With support from Royal Danish Theatre director Jonas Collin, Andersen attended grammar school in Slagelse and later Elsinore, where he endured harsh discipline and discouragement from faculty.

### Literary Career and Fairy Tales
Andersen’s early works included the short story "A Journey on Foot" (1829) and the novel *The Improvisatore* (1835), which brought him international acclaim. His first fairy tale collection, *Fairy Tales Told for Children*, was published in installments from 1835 to 1837. These stories, including "The Princess and the Pea," "Thumbelina," and "The Little Mermaid," departed from traditional folktales by emphasizing individual emotion and psychological complexity. Danish critics initially rejected the informal tone and perceived immorality of his tales, prompting Andersen to pause publication for a year. He continued writing fairy tales in subsequent series beginning in 1838 and 1845, eventually gaining European fame despite lingering skepticism in Denmark.

### Travelogues and Later Works
An avid traveler, Andersen published numerous travelogues such as *In Sweden* (1851), *A Poet's Bazaar*, *In Spain*, and *A Visit to Portugal* (1866). These works combined descriptive narrative with philosophical reflection on authorship and human nature. In the 1840s, he returned to drama with limited success but found renewed popularity through his fairy tales. His later years were marked by royal patronage, including recognition from King Christian IX and connections to the Russian Romanov dynasty through marriage alliances.

### Personal Life and Relationships
Andersen’s personal life was marked by unrequited love and complex emotional bonds. He formed deep friendships with men, including Edvard Collin, whose rejection deeply affected him. His romantic infatuations included Jenny Lind, whom he idealized and immortalized in "The Nightingale." Modern biographers debate the nature of his sexuality, with some interpreting his relationships and diaries as evidence of homoerotic desire, while others caution against labeling him definitively. His journals reveal a lifelong struggle with loneliness and a yearning for both love and artistic recognition.

## Terms
- **Fairy Tale**: A short narrative, often involving magical elements, traditionally intended for children but frequently carrying deeper moral or psychological themes.
- **Travelogue**: A descriptive account of travel experiences, often blending personal observation with cultural commentary.
- **Rigsdaler**: The former currency of Denmark, used during Andersen’s time.
- **Shilling**: A former British coin worth one-twentieth of a pound sterling.
- **Illuminated Manuscript**: A handwritten book decorated with illustrations and elaborate lettering, referenced in Andersen’s early education context.
- **Narrative Voice**: The persona assumed by the teller of a story, particularly relevant in Andersen’s conversational fairy tale style.
- **Moral Didacticism**: The practice of teaching ethical lessons through literature, central to 19th-century children’s writing.
- **Bicentenary**: The 200th anniversary of a person’s birth, celebrated globally in 2005 for Andersen.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars continue to debate the extent and nature of Andersen’s sexual identity. Biographer Jackie Wullschlager interprets his relationships with men like Harald Scharff as evidence of physical liaisons, while researchers from the H.C. Andersen Centre argue that such claims contradict his moral and religious values. The authenticity and interpretation of his private journals remain contentious, reflecting broader tensions in biographical scholarship about how to assess historical figures’ identities within evolving social frameworks.
