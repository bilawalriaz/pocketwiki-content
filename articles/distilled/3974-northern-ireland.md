# Northern Ireland

## Overview
Northern Ireland is a part of the United Kingdom in the north-east of the island of Ireland. Its creation in 1921 through the partition of Ireland established a region with a unionist (predominantly Protestant) majority wishing to remain in the UK, and a nationalist (predominantly Catholic) minority seeking a united Ireland. This division has been the source of significant political conflict, most notably the Troubles, but a peace process culminating in the 1998 Good Friday Agreement established a devolved power-sharing government.

## Body

### Creation and Early History
Northern Ireland was created in 1921 by the Government of Ireland Act 1920, which partitioned Ireland into two devolved territories. The six northeastern counties were chosen to ensure a decisive Protestant unionist majority, who wished to remain in the United Kingdom. The creation was marred by severe communal violence between Protestant unionists and Catholic nationalists, particularly in Belfast, resulting in over 500 deaths and 10,000 refugees. For its first fifty years, Northern Ireland had an unbroken series of Unionist Party governments, which were accused of systemic discrimination against the Catholic/nationalist minority in areas like housing, employment, and electoral boundaries.

### The Troubles and Peace Process
In the late 1960s, a civil rights campaign against discrimination was met with loyalist opposition, sparking the Troubles—a thirty-year conflict involving republican and loyalist paramilitaries and state forces. The conflict claimed over 3,500 lives. The 1998 Good Friday Agreement provided a framework for peace, establishing a devolved power-sharing government (the Northern Ireland Assembly) and affirming that Northern Ireland will remain in the UK until a majority in both Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland vote otherwise. The agreement required paramilitary disarmament and police reform. While the major armed campaigns have ended, sectarianism and segregation remain social problems.

### Governance and Politics
Since 1998, Northern Ireland has had a devolved government within the UK. The Northern Ireland Assembly and Executive are responsible for most domestic matters, while the UK Parliament retains control over reserved and excepted matters like taxation and foreign policy. The main political divide is between unionists (who wish to remain in the UK) and nationalists (who seek a united Ireland). The Good Friday Agreement mandates a cross-community government, with the roles of First Minister and deputy First Minister shared between the largest unionist and nationalist parties. In 2024, Sinn Féin's Michelle O'Neill became the first nationalist to hold the post of First Minister.

### Economy and Demographics
Northern Ireland's economy was historically industrial but declined during the Troubles; it has grown significantly since the late 1990s. Key sectors include services, public administration, and agriculture. The population was 1.9 million at the 2021 census. Identity is complex: 42.8% identified as British, 33.3% as Irish, and 31.5% as Northern Irish. The 2021 census also found that for the first time, the proportion of the population from a Catholic background (45.7%) exceeded that from a Protestant background (43.5%).

## Terms
*   **Unionist/Loyalist:** Predominantly Protestant political identity supporting Northern Ireland's continued membership in the United Kingdom.
*   **Nationalist/Republican:** Predominantly Catholic political identity supporting the unification of Ireland into a single independent state.
*   **The Troubles:** The period of intense ethno-nationalist conflict in Northern Ireland from the late 1960s until 1998.
*   **Good Friday Agreement (1998):** The peace accord that ended the Troubles, establishing power-sharing devolution and affirming the principle of consent for Northern Ireland's constitutional status.
*   **Devolution:** The transfer of certain powers from the UK Parliament to a local legislature, in this case the Northern Ireland Assembly.
*   **Power-sharing:** A form of government requiring the executive to include representatives from both major communities (unionist and nationalist).
*   **Gerrymandering:** The manipulation of electoral district boundaries to give an unfair political advantage to a particular party or group.
*   **Ulster:** A historical province of Ireland comprising nine counties; six of these form Northern Ireland. The term is often used by unionists as a synonym for Northern Ireland.
*   **Plantation of Ulster:** The 17th-century process of colonizing the province with English and Scottish Protestant settlers.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Status and Terminology:** There is no universally accepted term for Northern Ireland; it is variously described as a country, province, or region, with the choice often revealing political allegiance.
*   **Legacy of the Past:** The extent of collusion between state forces and loyalist paramilitaries during the Troubles remains disputed, as does the best approach to dealing with the legacy of the conflict.
*   **Constitutional Future:** While the Good Friday Agreement affirms the current status, opinion polls show a persistent, though minority, preference for a united Ireland, and demographic shifts may influence future debates.
