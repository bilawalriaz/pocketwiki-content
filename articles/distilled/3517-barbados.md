# Barbados

## Overview
Barbados is a small eastern Caribbean island nation in the Lesser Antilles. A former British colony, its society and economy were fundamentally shaped by the sugar plantation system and the transatlantic slave trade. It gained independence in 1966 and transitioned from a constitutional monarchy to a parliamentary republic in 2021.

## Timeline
- **c. 1600 BC** — Archaeological evidence suggests first human settlement.
- **13th century** — Inhabited by Kalinago people; originally named Ichirouganaim.
- **Late 15th century** — Claimed for the Crown of Castile by Spanish navigators, who named it "Barbudos".
- **1625** — The English ship *Olive Blossom* arrives; possession is taken for King James I.
- **1627** — First permanent English settlers arrive, establishing a colony.
- **1640** — Sugar cane cultivation is introduced, transforming the economy and society.
- **1652** — The Charter of Barbados (Treaty of Oistins) is signed, ending a conflict with the Commonwealth of England.
- **1816** — Bussa's rebellion, the largest slave revolt, is suppressed.
- **1833** — Slavery is abolished in the British Empire by the Slavery Abolition Act.
- **1966** — Barbados gains full independence as a Commonwealth realm with Elizabeth II as Queen.
- **2021** — Barbados transitions to a parliamentary republic, replacing the monarchy with a ceremonial president.

## Body

### Pre-colonial and European Arrival
Human presence dates to c. 1600 BC. Amerindian settlement occurred from the 4th to 7th centuries AD by the Saladoid-Barrancoid group, followed by Arawaks. The Kalinago visited regularly but left no permanent settlement. Spanish and Portuguese claims occurred in the 16th century, with Spanish slave raiding likely reducing the native population before English settlement.

### English Settlement and the Sugar Revolution
The first English ship arrived in 1625, with permanent settlement beginning in 1627. The introduction of sugar cane from Dutch Brazil in 1640 completely transformed Barbados. Sugar cultivation required large investments and heavy labour, displacing small white landowners and massively increasing the importation of enslaved Africans. By 1724, enslaved Africans vastly outnumbered free whites, a shift codified in harsh slave codes.

### Slavery, Rebellion, and Abolition
Brutal slavery conditions led to several planned rebellions, the largest being Bussa's rebellion in 1816. While suppressed, the repression strengthened the abolitionist movement. Slavery was abolished throughout the British Empire in 1833, though the planter class retained power and most workers lived in poverty.

### Path to Independence and Republic
Economic hardship and limited political rights in the 1930s led to unrest and the formation of parties like the Barbados Labour Party (BLP). Universal suffrage was introduced in 1950. After a period in the British West Indies Federation, Barbados gained full internal self-government in 1961 and independence on 30 November 1966. It remained a Commonwealth realm until 30 November 2021, when it transitioned to a republic with a ceremonial president, while remaining in the Commonwealth of Nations.

### Geography and Climate
Barbados is the easternmost Caribbean island, lying on the boundary of the South American and Caribbean plates. It is a coral-limestone island, rising gently to the central Scotland District, with Mount Hillaby as its highest point (340 m). The climate is tropical with a wet season (June–December) and a dry season (December–May). It is susceptible to hurricanes but often spared the worst effects.

### Government and Politics
Barbados is a parliamentary republic. The President is the head of state, elected by Parliament, and the Prime Minister is the head of government. It has a bicameral Parliament with a House of Assembly and a Senate. The political system is dominated by the BLP and the Democratic Labour Party (DLP). Barbados is a member of CARICOM and other international organizations.

### Economy
Historically dependent on sugar, Barbados has diversified into manufacturing, tourism, and offshore finance. It is classified as a high-income economy but faces challenges like poverty and unemployment. The economy has experienced periods of recession and recovery, notably after the 2008 global financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic. In 2018, the government defaulted on its sovereign debt, leading to a restructuring completed in 2019.

### Demographics and Culture
The population is predominantly of Afro-Caribbean ancestry (close to 90%). English is the official language, but Bajan Creole is widely spoken. Christianity is the largest religion. Barbadian culture is a blend of African, British, and other influences. The Crop Over festival, originally a sugar harvest celebration, is a major cultural event. Cricket is the most popular sport.

## Terms
- **Commonwealth realm:** A sovereign state within the Commonwealth of Nations that has the British monarch as its head of state.
- **Plantation economy:** An agricultural system reliant on large-scale cultivation of cash crops (like sugar) for export, historically using enslaved or indentured labour.
- **Indentured servant:** A person bound by contract to work for another for a specified number of years, often used as a labour system before the full shift to slavery.
- **Sugar revolution:** The rapid economic and social transformation of Caribbean islands following the introduction of sugar cane cultivation and processing.
- **Slave code:** A set of laws designed to control enslaved populations and define their status as property.
- **Commonwealth of Nations:** A voluntary association of 56 independent countries, most of which are former territories of the British Empire.
- **CARICOM:** The Caribbean Community, an organization of Caribbean nations promoting economic integration and cooperation.
- **Bajan Creole:** An English-based Creole language spoken by most Barbadians in everyday life.
- **Diapir:** A geological formation where a mass of softer rock rises from the mantle through overlying strata, which is how Barbados was formed.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact origin of the name "Barbados" is debated, with theories pointing to the bearded fig tree, the bearded appearance of the Kalinago people, or sea foam on reefs.
- The total number of Irish people forcibly transported to Barbados during the Cromwellian era is unknown and "highly contentious," with estimates ranging widely.
- The transition to a republic in 2021 did not have a clear majority in public opinion polls conducted at the time, with a significant portion of the population indifferent or opposed.
