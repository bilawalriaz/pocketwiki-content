# Kingdom of France

## Overview
The Kingdom of France was a major European political entity that evolved from the Carolingian Empire's western fragment into a powerful, centralized state. It dominated European politics and colonial expansion from the High Middle Ages until its abolition during the French Revolution, with subsequent restorations ending in 1848. Its history is defined by the consolidation of royal power, religious conflict, and the rise and fall of absolute monarchy.

## Timeline
- **843** — Treaty of Verdun divides the Carolingian Empire; Charles the Bald receives West Francia.
- **987** — Hugh Capet is elected king, founding the Capetian dynasty.
- **1190** — Philip II becomes the first king to style himself "King of France."
- **1337–1453** — Hundred Years' War with England over the French throne and territories.
- **1494–1559** — Italian Wars begin France's early modern period, ending in defeat by Spain and the Habsburgs.
- **1562–1598** — French Wars of Religion between Catholics and Huguenots.
- **1598** — Edict of Nantes grants toleration to Huguenots.
- **1643–1715** — Reign of Louis XIV, who establishes absolute monarchy and makes France Europe's dominant power.
- **1756–1763** — Seven Years' War results in the loss of most French North American colonies.
- **1789** — French Revolution begins.
- **1792** — Monarchy is abolished and the First French Republic is proclaimed.
- **1814** — Bourbon monarchy is restored after Napoleon's defeat.
- **1848** — July Monarchy falls; monarchy is abolished for the final time.

## Body

### Origins and Early Capetians
The Kingdom of France originated from West Francia, the territory ceded to Charles the Bald by the Treaty of Verdun in 843. After the Carolingian line ended in 987, Hugh Capet was elected king, establishing the Capetian dynasty which ruled France, through its Valois and Bourbon cadet branches, until 1792. Early Capetian kings controlled little beyond the Île-de-France, while powerful vassals like the counts of Blois and the Plantagenet kings of England held vast territories. Philip II (r. 1180–1223) significantly expanded royal authority, recovering lands from the Plantagenets and becoming the first to use the title "King of France."

### Late Middle Ages and Centralization
The death of Charles IV in 1328 without a male heir led to the succession of Philip VI of Valois, sparking the Hundred Years' War (1337–1453) with England. The war, alongside the Black Death, devastated the population but ultimately resulted in French victory and the expulsion of English forces. Subsequent Valois kings fought the Italian Wars (1494–1559), which marked the beginning of the early modern era but ended in French defeat by the Habsburgs. This period saw the gradual centralization of royal power and the emergence of a strong fiscal system.

### Religious Conflict and the Ancien Régime
The Protestant Reformation divided France between a Catholic majority and a Huguenot minority, leading to the brutal Wars of Religion (1562–1598). The conflict ended with the accession of the Protestant Henry IV (first Bourbon king), who converted to Catholicism and issued the Edict of Nantes in 1598, granting Huguenots religious toleration. The early modern period, known as the Ancien Régime, was characterized by increasing royal absolutism. Louis XIV (r. 1643–1715) epitomized this system, centralizing power, revoking the Edict of Nantes in 1685, and making France the continent's leading power, though at the cost of major wars and debt.

### Decline and Revolution
The 18th century saw growing discontent with the monarchy. Louis XV lost most of France's North American colonies in the Seven Years' War (1756–1763). Louis XVI's reign was marked by financial crisis, exacerbated by French intervention in the American Revolutionary War. A severe winter in 1788 and widespread hunger led to the French Revolution in 1789. The absolute monarchy was limited in 1791, then abolished in 1792, leading to the execution of Louis XVI in 1793.

### Restoration and Final Abolition
The monarchy was restored in 1814 under Louis XVIII of the Bourbon dynasty, who attempted a constitutional compromise. His successor, Charles X, attempted to reassert absolutism, triggering the July Revolution of 1830. He was replaced by the "citizen king" Louis Philippe of the House of Orléans. His July Monarchy faced corruption and social unrest, and was overthrown in the February Revolution of 1848, which established the Second Republic and ended the monarchy permanently.

## Terms
- **Capetian Dynasty**: The royal dynasty founded by Hugh Capet in 987 that ruled France continuously until 1792, including its Valois and Bourbon branches.
- **Ancien Régime**: The aristocratic, social, and political system of early modern France under the late Valois and Bourbon dynasties, characterized by absolute monarchy and regional administrative patchworks.
- **Huguenots**: French Protestants of the Reformed tradition who faced persecution, particularly after the revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685.
- **Edict of Nantes**: A 1598 decree by Henry IV that granted civil rights and religious toleration to Huguenots, ending the French Wars of Religion.
- **Gallicanism**: A policy asserting the autonomy of the French Catholic Church from papal authority, allowing the king to select bishops.
- **Salic Law**: A traditional law cited to justify excluding women from inheriting the French throne, which led to the succession of Philip VI in 1328.
- **Divine Right of Kings**: A political doctrine asserting that monarchs derive their authority directly from God and are not accountable to earthly powers.
- **Fronde**: A series of civil wars (1648–1653) during Louis XIV's minority, involving nobles and parliaments against royal authority.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which the early Capetian kings' authority was truly limited versus their strategic use of feudal mechanisms is a subject of scholarly analysis.
- The precise demographic impact of the Black Death on 14th-century France is debated, with estimates of population loss ranging from 50% or more.
- Historians continue to analyze the relative importance of religious, social, and political factors in causing the French Wars of Religion.
- The degree to which Louis XIV's policies of centralization and religious uniformity ultimately weakened the monarchy by creating lasting resentment is a key historiographical question.
