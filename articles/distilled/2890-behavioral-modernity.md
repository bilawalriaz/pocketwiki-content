# Behavioral modernity

## Overview
Behavioral modernity refers to the suite of cognitive and behavioral traits—such as symbolic thought, planning, and cumulative culture—that define contemporary human societies. It is a key concept for understanding the evolutionary and cultural divergence of *Homo sapiens* from other hominins. The debate centers on whether these traits appeared suddenly in a "revolution" or accumulated gradually over hundreds of thousands of years.

## Timeline
- **c. 350,000–260,000 years ago** — *Homo sapiens* originate in Africa.
- **c. 320,000 years ago** — Evidence of long-distance trade and pigment use at Olorgesailie, Kenya.
- **c. 164,000 years ago** — Systematic heat-treating of stone for tools begins at Pinnacle Point, South Africa.
- **c. 100,000–75,000 years ago** — Sporadic archaeological signals of symbolism and advanced tools appear in Africa.
- **c. 77,000–100,000 years ago** — Engraved ochre slabs and shell beads found at Blombos Cave, South Africa.
- **c. 50,000 years ago** — Widespread, continuous manifestations of modern behaviors become visible globally.
- **c. 48,000 years ago** — First Cro-Magnon expansion into Europe.
- **c. 40,000–50,000 years ago** — Proposed "Upper Paleolithic Revolution" or "cognitive revolution" in Europe.
- **c. 34,000–20,000 years ago** — Evidence of burials and personal ornaments at Zhoukoudian, China.

## Body

### Evolution and Theoretical Models
Anatomically modern humans possessed the necessary neural architecture by at least ~300,000 years ago, but early small, fragmented populations limited the transmission of complex behaviors. Archaeological evidence for symbolism and advanced technology appears sporadically in Africa between ~150,000–75,000 years ago, suggesting intermittent expression. Widespread, continuous manifestations only became visible after populations grew denser and social networks expanded.

Two main hypotheses explain this pattern. The **Late Upper Paleolithic model** posits a sudden "cognitive revolution" around 40,000–50,000 years ago, possibly driven by a genetic change enabling complex language, which prompted migration out of Africa. In contrast, **gradualist models** argue modern behavior arose through incremental steps, with archaeological signatures appearing earlier in the African Middle Stone Age due to demographic or subsistence changes. A middle view sees a relatively accelerated but still gradual "revolution" in Africa spanning tens of thousands of years.

### Definition and Archaeological Indicators
Behavioral modernity is defined by universal human traits like abstract thought, planning, trade, cooperative labor, and body decoration. Archaeologically, it is inferred from evidence such as burials, figurative art, use of pigments and jewelry, bone tools, long-distance resource transport, blade technology, and composite tools. However, the concept is critiqued for potential taphonomic bias (where site preservation skews evidence) and for being a simplistic trait list. Some scholars argue for "behavioral variability" instead, noting that population pressure or cultural change may better explain shifts in tool types than a leap from "archaic" to "modern."

### Evidence from Africa
Africa provides the earliest evidence for modern behaviors. Key sites include Blombos Cave (engraved ochre, shell beads), Diepkloof (engraved ostrich eggshells), and Pinnacle Point (heat-treated stone tools, marine resource exploitation). Evidence for specialized projectile weapons, ochre processing workshops, and trade networks exists from sites like Sibudu Cave, Katanda, and Olorgesailie, dating from ~320,000 to ~60,000 years ago. This record challenges Europe-centered models by situating the gradual assembly of modern behaviors in Africa long before 50,000 years ago.

### Evidence from Europe and Asia
European archaeology complicates the "Upper Paleolithic Revolution" model. While the Aurignacian tool complex is often seen as a modern human signature, "transitional" complexes suggest innovation occurred in steps. Furthermore, Neanderthals produced symbolic artifacts (e.g., grooved teeth, ochre) and possibly performed ritual burials, indicating they may have had their own cultural traditions comparable to behavioral modernity. In East Asia, early human presence (~100,000 years ago) and evidence of symbolic behavior (e.g., beads at Zhoukoudian) before 50,000 years ago also argue against behavioral modernity as a sudden impetus for migration.

## Terms
- **Behavioral modernity:** A suite of cognitive and behavioral traits, including symbolic thought and cumulative culture, associated with *Homo sapiens*.
- **Cumulative culture:** The "ratchet effect" where cultural knowledge is built upon and refined across generations through high-fidelity social learning.
- **Upper Paleolithic Revolution:** A hypothesized sudden leap in cognitive and behavioral modernity in Europe around 40,000–50,000 years ago.
- **Gradualist model:** The theory that modern human behavior emerged through a slow accumulation of traits over a long period.
- **Taphonomic bias:** Distortion in the archaeological record caused by differential preservation of sites and artifacts.
- **Levallois technology:** A prepared-core stone tool technique (Mode 3) associated with Neanderthals and early modern humans.
- **Microlithic technology:** Small stone tools, often hafted onto handles or shafts, characteristic of later Stone Age periods.
- **FOXP2 gene:** A gene associated with speech and language; its role as a driver of a sudden cognitive revolution is debated.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Sudden vs. Gradual Emergence:** Did modern behavior appear in a rapid "revolution" around 50,000 years ago, or did it accumulate gradually in Africa over hundreds of thousands of years?
- **Species-Specific Trait:** Is behavioral modernity unique to *Homo sapiens*, or did other hominins like Neanderthals possess similar capacities, as suggested by their symbolic artifacts?
- **Drivers of Change:** Was the global spread of modern behavior triggered by a specific genetic mutation (e.g., for language), or by demographic factors like increased population density and social network size?
- **Eurocentrism:** To what extent has the traditional model been biased by focusing on the European record, and how does the African archaeological record reshape the timeline?
