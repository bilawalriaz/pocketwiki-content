# Claude Lévi-Strauss

## Overview

Claude Lévi-Strauss (1908–2009) was a Belgian-born French anthropologist and ethnologist whose work was key in the development of the theories of structuralism and structural anthropology. He held the chair of Social Anthropology at the Collège de France between 1959 and 1982, was elected a member of the Académie française in 1973 and was a member of the School for Advanced Studies in the Social Sciences in Paris. He received numerous honors from universities and institutions throughout the world. His work applied structural linguistics—particularly Ferdinand de Saussure's models—to anthropology, seeking universal patterns in kinship, myth, and culture. By treating culture as a system of symbolic communication governed by deep structural rules, he reshaped the humanities and social sciences, influencing fields from philosophy to literary theory.

## Body

### Early Life and Fieldwork

Born in 1908 to French-Jewish (turned agnostic) parents in Brussels, where his father was working as a portrait painter, Lévi-Strauss grew up in Paris and showed early interest in philosophy and politics. After passing the agrégation in philosophy in 1931, he accepted a position in São Paulo in 1935, where he conducted his only sustained ethnographic fieldwork among indigenous groups in the Amazon. Though criticized for limited immersion and language barriers, this experience shaped his focus on kinship and symbolic systems.

### Structural Anthropology and Kinship Theory

Returning to France after WWII, Lévi-Strauss developed structural anthropology by applying Saussurean linguistics to cultural phenomena. In *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949), he argued that kinship is not based on descent but on alliance—the exchange of women between groups. He proposed that underlying structures of thought, like phonemes in language, generate observable cultural forms. His model emphasized binary oppositions (e.g., nature/culture) and sought to reduce complex data into comprehensible relational systems.

### Myth and Universal Thought

In *The Savage Mind* (1962) and *Mythologiques* (1964–1971), Lévi-Strauss analyzed myths as languages composed of mythemes—basic units linked by function rather than narrative sequence. He contended that myths resolve contradictions through mediation, exemplified by trickster figures like the coyote or raven, which bridge life/death or agriculture/hunting. These analyses supported his broader claim that human thought follows universal structural laws, even in seemingly arbitrary domains like mythology.

### Intellectual Legacy and Controversies

Lévi-Strauss's structuralism sparked debates with existentialists like Sartre and drew criticism for its abstract formalism and alleged Eurocentrism. Critics questioned whether his models adequately accounted for historical change or individual agency. Nonetheless, his interdisciplinary influence extended into philosophy, literary criticism, and art history, cementing his status as a central figure in 20th-century intellectual life. He won the 1986 International Nonino Prize in Italy and received numerous honors including membership in the Académie française (1973), the American Academy of Arts and Letters, and honorary doctorates from Oxford, Harvard, Yale, and Columbia.

## Terms

- **Structuralism**: A theoretical approach seeking underlying patterns of thought in human culture and behavior.
- **Mytheme**: The basic unit of myth, analogous to the phoneme in language.
- **Bricoleur**: A thinker who constructs meaning using pre-existing materials and tools, contrasted with the engineer who designs from scratch.
- **Alliance theory**: The idea that kinship is structured around inter-group marriages and exchanges rather than blood ties.
- **Binary opposition**: A pair of contrasting concepts (e.g., raw/cooked) that structure human thought and cultural expression.
- **Totemism**: A system of belief linking societies or individuals to spiritual beings or natural objects.
- **École Libre des Hautes Études**: A wartime academic institution in New York for displaced French scholars.
- **House society**: Societies organized around the domestic household rather than lineage or descent groups.

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars continue to debate the validity and relevance of Lévi-Strauss's structural methods. Critics argue that his emphasis on abstract structures overlooks historical specificity, individual agency, and power dynamics within societies. Postcolonial theorists question whether his universalist claims mask ethnocentric assumptions about "primitive" versus "modern" thought. Additionally, debates persist over whether structuralism remains a viable framework in anthropology or has been superseded by practice-based and materialist approaches. The extent to which his theories apply beyond Western academic contexts also remains contested.
