# City Lights

## Overview
*City Lights* (1931) is a silent romantic comedy-drama film written, produced, and directed by and starring Charlie Chaplin. It is a landmark work that defied the rise of "talkies" (films with recorded dialogue) by using only a synchronized musical score and sound effects, not spoken dialogue. The film is widely considered Chaplin's masterpiece and one of the greatest films ever made, celebrated for its emotional depth and the iconic performance of its final scene.

## Timeline
- **1928** — Chaplin begins developing the script for *City Lights* as the silent film era ends.
- **Dec 27, 1928** — Principal photography begins.
- **Nov 1929** — Chaplin briefly fires star Virginia Cherrill, then re-hires her.
- **Sep 1930** — Filming concludes after a lengthy production.
- **Jan 30, 1931** — Gala premiere in Los Angeles with Albert Einstein as guest of honor.
- **Mar 7, 1931** — General release in the United States.
- **1949** — Critic James Agee calls the final scene "the greatest single piece of acting ever committed to celluloid."
- **1991** — Selected for preservation in the U.S. National Film Registry.
- **2007** — Ranked 11th on the American Film Institute's list of the best American films.

## Body

### Production and Sound
Chaplin conceived *City Lights* as a silent film even as sound films became dominant. As an independent producer, he retained creative control to continue without dialogue, using only a synchronized score and sound effects. The film's plot evolved from an earlier idea about a clown concealing his blindness from his daughter, which inspired the blind flower girl character. The drunken millionaire subplot was based on a previous concept. Filming was exceptionally long and meticulous, with Chaplin shooting over 314,000 feet of film for a final cut of about 8,000 feet, a ratio of nearly 39:1. The production was marked by tensions, including the temporary firing of Virginia Cherrill.

### Music and Score
*City Lights* was the first film for which Chaplin composed the score, working with Arthur Johnston. The score was intended to convey the characters' emotions through melody. The main theme for the blind flower girl is based on the Spanish song "La Violetera" by José Padilla. Chaplin did not credit Padilla and lost a subsequent lawsuit over the use of the song.

### Release and Reception
The film premiered successfully in January 1931, with Albert Einstein in attendance. Despite initial nervousness from Chaplin about its reception in the sound era, *City Lights* was a major financial and critical success, earning over $4 million in worldwide rentals. Reviews were largely positive, praising its artistry and humor, though some critics, like Alexander Bakshy, disliked its silent format and sentimentality. The film's popularity endured through re-releases.

### Legacy and Acclaim
*City Lights* is consistently ranked among the greatest films in history. It was voted the #2 best film of all time in the first *Sight & Sound* poll in 1952. Directors like Orson Welles, Stanley Kubrick, and Andrei Tarkovsky have cited it as a favorite. The final scene, where the cured blind girl recognizes the Tramp, is particularly celebrated as a pinnacle of cinematic acting. The film is preserved in the National Film Registry and appears on numerous "best of" lists from the AFI, BBC, and others.

## Terms
- **Synchronized score**: A musical soundtrack edited to match the timing of the film's action, without spoken dialogue.
- **Talkies**: A colloquial term for films with recorded dialogue, which became the industry standard in the late 1920s.
- **Leitmotif**: A recurring musical theme associated with a particular character, idea, or situation.
- **Intertitles**: Text cards inserted into silent films to convey dialogue or narrative information.
- **Shooting ratio**: The amount of film shot compared to the length of the final film; a high ratio indicates extensive filming and editing.
- **National Film Registry**: A U.S. program that selects films for preservation as being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant."

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Autobiographical elements**: Psychologist Stephen Weissman has hypothesized that the film is highly autobiographical, with the blind girl representing Chaplin's mother and the millionaire representing his father.
- **Artistic defiance**: The film is seen as an act of defiance against the sound film era, with Chaplin believing his Tramp character could not be adapted to talkies and still work.
- **Critical reception**: While overwhelmingly praised, some contemporary critics, like Alexander Bakshy of *The Nation*, were highly critical, objecting to its silent format and sentimentality.
