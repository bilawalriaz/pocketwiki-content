# Empress Dowager Cixi

## Overview
Empress Dowager Cixi (1835–1908) was a Manchu noblewoman who became the de facto ruler of the late Qing dynasty for nearly half a century. As regent for two emperors, she navigated immense internal rebellions and foreign imperialist pressures, making pragmatic decisions that prolonged the dynasty's life but also contributed to its instability. Her legacy is fiercely debated, oscillating between that of a ruthless reactionary and a capable, modernizing administrator.

## Timeline
- **29 Nov 1835** — Born Xingzhen of the Yehe Nara clan in Beijing.
- **1851** — Selected as a concubine for the Xianfeng Emperor.

- **22 Aug 1861** — Xianfeng Emperor dies; Cixi becomes Empress Dowager.
- **Nov 1861** — Executes the Xinyou Coup, ousting the Eight Regent Ministers.
- **1875** — Installs her nephew Zaitian as the Guangxu Emperor after Tongzhi's death.
- **1881** — Co-regent Empress Dowager Ci'an dies, leaving Cixi as sole regent.
- **21 Sep 1898** — Suppresses the Hundred Days' Reform; places Guangxu under house arrest.
- **1900** — Supports the Boxer Rebellion; flees Beijing after the Eight-Nation Alliance invades.
- **1902** — Returns to Beijing and initiates the New Policies reform program.
- **15 Nov 1908** — Dies in Beijing, one day after the Guangxu Emperor.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Cixi entered the Forbidden City as a concubine in 1852 and rose to become Noble Consort Yi after giving birth to the Xianfeng Emperor's only surviving son in 1856. Her literacy in Chinese allowed her to assist the ailing emperor with state documents, giving her early political experience. After Xianfeng's death in 1861, she conspired with court officials and Prince Gong to overthrow the Eight Regent Ministers in the Xinyou Coup. This established her as co-regent alongside Empress Dowager Ci'an, ruling "behind the curtain" for the child Tongzhi Emperor.

### The Tongzhi Restoration
During the Tongzhi era (1861–1875), Cixi oversaw the suppression of the Taiping Rebellion and initiated the Tongzhi Restoration. This period focused on technological and military modernization—such as purchasing foreign warships and establishing the Tongwen Guan language school—while rejecting Western political institutions. She consolidated power by demoting her ally Prince Gong in 1865 after he became too influential. Her rule was marked by pragmatic reforms, including appointing Han Chinese officials like Zeng Guofan to key military positions, a break from Manchu tradition.

### The Guangxu Era and Reform
After Tongzhi's death in 1875, Cixi installed her young nephew as the Guangxu Emperor, maintaining her regency. She continued to wield significant influence even after his formal accession in 1889. In 1898, the Guangxu Emperor launched the Hundred Days' Reform, a radical modernization program. Fearing a loss of power and potential disorder, Cixi staged a coup, placing the emperor under house arrest and executing leading reformers. Her support for the anti-foreign Boxer Rebellion in 1900 led to the invasion of Beijing by the Eight-Nation Alliance and her flight to Xi'an. After the humiliating Boxer Protocol, she returned to Beijing in 1902 and implemented the New Policies, a reform program more sweeping than the one she had suppressed.

### Death and Aftermath
Cixi died on 15 November 1908, one day after the Guangxu Emperor. Forensic tests in 2008 revealed the Guangxu Emperor was poisoned with arsenic, with Cixi widely suspected as the perpetrator to prevent him from reversing her policies. She was succeeded by the child Puyi. In 1928, her tomb was looted by the warlord Sun Dianying, who desecrated her remains and stole its legendary treasures, an event that shocked the nation and contributed to the last emperor Puyi's later sympathies with Japan.

## Terms
- **Empress Dowager**: The title for the widow of an emperor, or the mother of a reigning emperor, who may act as regent.
- **Regent**: A person appointed to administer a state because the monarch is a minor, absent, or incapacitated.
- **Xinyou Coup**: The 1861 palace coup in which Cixi and Prince Gong ousted the Eight Regent Ministers to seize power.
- **Tongzhi Restoration**: The period of political and military reforms in the 1860s aimed at preserving the Qing dynasty.
- **Hundred Days' Reform**: A failed 1898 program of radical modernization initiated by the Guangxu Emperor.
- **Boxer Rebellion**: An anti-foreign uprising in China (1899–1901) supported by Cixi, which ended in a foreign invasion.
- **Boxer Protocol**: The 1901 treaty that ended the Boxer Rebellion, imposing heavy indemnities and foreign military presence in Beijing.
- **New Policies**: The sweeping administrative, educational, and military reforms implemented by Cixi after 1901.
- **Beiyang Fleet**: The modernized Chinese navy established in the 1880s, which was largely destroyed in the First Sino-Japanese War.

## Debates and open questions
- **Legacy**: Historians debate whether Cixi was a conservative despot who doomed the dynasty or a pragmatic ruler who skillfully managed crises and initiated necessary modernization. Revisionists like Jung Chang argue she was a capable reformer, while critics like Pamela Kyle Crossley contend this overcorrects her historical image.
- **Guangxu Emperor's death**: While 2008 forensic tests confirmed arsenic poisoning, definitive proof of who administered it is lacking. Cixi remains the prime suspect, but the role of Yuan Shikai is also theorized.
- **Boxer Rebellion support**: Scholars analyze whether Cixi's support for the Boxers was a genuine anti-imperialist stance, a miscalculation, or a desperate attempt to harness popular sentiment against foreign encroachment.
- **Impact of reforms**: There is debate over whether her post-1901 New Policies were a sincere effort to save the dynasty through constitutional monarchy or too little, too late to prevent revolution.
