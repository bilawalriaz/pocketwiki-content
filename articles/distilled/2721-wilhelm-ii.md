# Wilhelm II

## Overview
Wilhelm II was the last German Emperor, reigning from 1888 to 1918. His erratic personal rule and aggressive foreign policy, particularly the naval arms race with Britain, are widely seen as key factors in the deterioration of Germany's international position and the outbreak of World War I. His abdication in 1918 ended both the German Empire and the Hohenzollern dynasty's centuries-long rule over Prussia.

## Timeline
- **27 January 1859** — Born in Berlin to Prince Frederick William and Victoria, Princess Royal.
- **15 June 1888** — Succeeds his father, Frederick III, as German Emperor and King of Prussia.
- **March 1890** — Dismisses Chancellor Otto von Bismarck and begins the "New Course."
- **1898** — First political visit to the Ottoman Empire, securing promises for the Berlin–Baghdad railway.
- **31 March 1905** — Provokes the First Moroccan Crisis with a visit to Tangier.
- **1908** — The *Daily Telegraph* Affair damages his international reputation.
- **28 June 1914** — Assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand triggers the July Crisis.
- **4 August 1914** — Germany enters World War I.
- **9 November 1918** — Abdication is announced, ending the German monarchy.
- **10 November 1918** — Flees to exile in the Netherlands.
- **4 June 1941** — Dies in exile at Huis Doorn, Netherlands.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Born with a withered left arm due to a traumatic breech birth, Wilhelm's childhood was marked by his mother's obsession with his disability and his own developing "Prussianized" identity, which alienated him from his liberal-leaning parents. He became emperor in 1888 during the "Year of Three Emperors," following the brief reign of his terminally ill father, Frederick III.

### The New Course and Domestic Rule
Upon dismissing the long-serving Chancellor Otto von Bismarck in 1890, Wilhelm embarked on the "New Course," seeking to exert direct personal control over policy. He replaced seasoned statesmen with more pliable chancellors and promoted social welfare legislation, such as the 1891 Workers Protection Acts. He also enthusiastically sponsored scientific research and public education, though his erratic personality often undermined his administration.

### Foreign Policy and the Path to War
Wilhelm's foreign policy was characterized by impulsiveness and a desire for Germany to claim its "place in the sun." His "Hun speech" of 1900, inflammatory statements in the *Daily Telegraph* Affair (1908), and the Moroccan Crisis (1905) alienated other powers. His most consequential project was a massive naval build-up, championed by Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz, which directly challenged British naval supremacy and led to a costly arms race. By 1914, Germany's only reliable allies were the weaker Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire.

### World War I and Abdication
During the July Crisis of 1914, Wilhelm issued a "blank cheque" of support to Austria-Hungary, a key step toward war. However, once conflict began, he was largely sidelined as a "shadow Kaiser," with the military high command, eventually a de facto dictatorship under Hindenburg and Ludendorff, controlling strategy. As Germany faced defeat in 1918, Wilhelm lost the support of the army and the public. He abdicated on 9 November 1918 and fled to the Netherlands, where he lived in exile until his death.

### Exile and Later Views
In exile at Huis Doorn, Wilhelm wrote his memoirs, hunted extensively, and followed European politics. He initially hoped the Nazi Party might restore the monarchy but grew to despise Adolf Hitler, condemning the Night of the Long Knives and Kristallnacht. Despite this, he sent Hitler congratulatory telegrams on early German victories in World War II, hoping for a monarchical restoration. He died in 1941.

## Terms
*   **Hohenzollern dynasty:** The royal house that ruled Prussia and later the German Empire.
*   **New Course:** The policy of personal rule and assertive expansion adopted by Wilhelm II after dismissing Bismarck.
*   **Risk Theory (Tirpitz Plan):** The naval strategy that a powerful German fleet could threaten Britain, forcing it to accommodate German demands.
*   **Blank Cheque:** Germany's unconditional promise of support to Austria-Hungary during the July Crisis of 1914.
*   **Schlieffen Plan:** Germany's pre-war military strategy for a two-front war, involving a rapid invasion of France before turning to fight Russia.
*   **Shadow Kaiser:** A term describing Wilhelm II's diminished, ceremonial role during World War I.
*   **Weimar Republic:** The democratic government established in Germany after the abdication of Wilhelm II and the end of World War I.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Degree of Personal Rule:** Historians debate the extent of Wilhelm's "personal rule" versus the influence of his chancellors and the military bureaucracy.
*   **Responsibility for World War I:** The "Bismarck myth" argues that dismissing Bismarck destroyed the balance of power and made war inevitable. Others, like historian Modris Eksteins, contend Bismarck's dismissal was overdue and that his policies had already left Germany divided and isolated.
*   **Impact of Personality:** Scholars emphasize how Wilhelm's unstable personality—marked by insecurity, impulsiveness, and a love-hate relationship with Britain—directly shaped his erratic and damaging foreign policy.
