# Mirabai

## Overview
Mirabai (c. 1498–1547) was a 16th-century Rajput princess and Hindu mystic poet whose devotional songs (bhajans) to Krishna made her a central figure in the North Indian Bhakti movement. Born into the Rathore royal family of Merta, she married the crown prince of Mewar in 1516 but was widowed by 1521. Defying widowhood conventions and aristocratic norms, she pursued an ecstatic, personalized devotion to Krishna, identifying him as her true husband (Girdhar Nagar). Legends recount persecution by her in-laws—including poison and a snake basket—from which she escaped through divine intervention. While millions of verses are attributed to her, scholars authenticate only a few hundred, with the earliest manuscripts dating to the 18th century. Her poetry, composed in Rajasthani Braj Bhasha using mātric meter and set to rāgas, expresses *madhurya bhava* (conjugal love for the divine) and *viraha* (pain of separation). She remains a cultural icon of spiritual resistance, celebrated across caste lines and adapted in Sikh texts, film, and modern literature.

## Timeline
- **c. 1498** — Born in Kudki (modern Beawar district, Rajasthan) to Ratan Singh Rathore; childhood in Merta.
- **1516** — Married Bhoj Raj, crown prince of Mewar.
- **1518** — Bhoj Raj wounded in wars against the Delhi Sultanate.
- **1521** — Bhoj Raj dies of battle wounds; father Ratan Singh and father-in-law Rana Sanga die shortly after Battle of Khanwa against Babur.
- **c. 1521–1527** — Persecution by in-laws under new ruler Vikram Singh begins (legendary: poison, snake basket, drowning order).
- **c. 1527–1547** — Leaves Mewar; pilgrimages to Vrindavan and Dwarka (per Mewari tradition) or resides in Udaipur (per Punjabi *Prem Ambodh Pothi*).
- **1547** — Death in Dwarka or Vrindavan; legend states she merged into a Krishna idol after being poisoned by brother-in-law.
- **c. 1600** — Nabhadas’s *Bhaktamal* mentions her, confirming wide recognition in Bhakti circles.
- **1604** — Compositions attributed to her appear in Bhai Banno’s copy of the Adi Granth (excluded from standardized Sikh scripture).
- **1693** — *Prem Ambodh Pothi* (Gurmukhi text linked to Guru Gobind Singh) includes her as one of sixteen historic bhakti saints.
- **18th century** — Earliest written records of her poems appear (except two earlier hymns); critical editions compiled later.

## Body

### Biography and Historical Context
Primary records of Mirabai’s life are absent; scholars reconstruct her biography from 17th-century hagiographies written within 150 years of her death. She was born into the Rathore Rajput clan, daughter of Ratan Singh and granddaughter of Rao Dudaji of Merta, making her a great-granddaughter of Rao Jodha (founder of Jodhpur) and cousin of Jaimal Rathore. Her 1516 marriage to Bhoj Raj, heir to the Mewar throne, allied two major Rajput houses. The political landscape shifted violently after 1518: Bhoj Raj was wounded fighting the Delhi Sultanate, dying in 1521. Days later, her father and father-in-law Rana Sanga died following their defeat by Babur at the Battle of Khanwa (1527). Vikram Singh succeeded Rana Sanga, and legend holds that he and the Mewar court persecuted Mirabai for her public devotion—singing, dancing, and mixing across castes—which they deemed insulting to Rajput honor. Nancy Martin-Kershaw argues the conflict was less religious than political, driven by instability during the Mughal-Rajput wars. Mirabai eventually left Mewar, spending her final decades in Dwarka or Vrindavan, where she died in 1547.

### Hagiographical Variations
Mirabai’s life story diverges sharply across regions. The dominant Mewari tradition depicts her fleeing Chittor after in-law persecution, traveling to Braj (Vrindavan) and Dwarka. In contrast, the early 18th-century Punjabi *Prem Ambodh Pothi* (c. 1693, associated with Guru Gobind Singh’s court) portrays her as sedentary in Udaipur, married to a man named Giridhar, and persecuted by her *own father*—who attempts poison and a sword, thwarted when Krishna manifests wielding the *sudarshan chakra* (divine discus). Other legends claim Emperor Akbar visited her with Tansen (chronologically impossible, as Tansen joined Akbar’s court in 1562, 15 years after her death) or that the leather-worker saint Ravidas was her guru (a chhatri at her Chittorgarh temple bears Ravidas’s footprint, but no historical evidence corroborates the link). These variations reflect how communities reshaped her narrative to serve local theological and social agendas.

### Poetry: Language, Form, and Themes
Mirabai’s poems are lyrical *padas* (metric verses) in Rajasthani Braj Bhasha, predominantly using *mātric* (syllabic) meter and set to *rāgas* for singing as *bhajans*. Her signature epithet for Krishna is *Girdhar Nagar* (“Mountain Bearer”), whom she addresses as lover, husband, yogi, and God. Core themes include *madhurya bhava* (bridal mysticism), total surrender (*prapatti*), and *viraha*—the agony of separation mirrored in nature imagery (monsoon clouds, peacock cries). She characterizes Krishna as a detached yogi who seduces and abandons, heightening the emotional intensity. Her style fuses defiance, longing, ecstasy, and joy, transcending caste: high-caste audiences identify with her royal suffering; low-caste groups revere her renunciation of status and acceptance of Ravidas as guru. The *Bhaktamal* (c. 1600) praises her unconditional love as remarkable for a “degenerate age” (*Kali Yuga*).

### Textual Authenticity and Manuscript Record
The manuscript trail for Mirabai’s poetry is exceptionally thin. Only two poems appear in pre-18th-century manuscripts; a search of Rajasthani libraries found none before the late 1700s, with only a handful predating 1800. The standard critical edition by Parasuram Caturvedi cites no source manuscripts. John Stratton Hawley warns of an “element of enigma”: no verifiable link exists between the historical Mira and the thousands of verses attributed to her. Scholars identify a distinct “Mirabai style”—intimate, surrender-centered, inseparable from her legend—but remain divided on authorship. Most *bhajans* sung today were likely composed later by admirers, projecting her voice onto new devotional needs.

### Sikh Literature and Ravidas Connection
Mirabai appears in Sikh textual history in two ways. In 1604, Bhai Banno’s unauthorized copy of the Adi Granth included her compositions; the Sikh Gurus rejected these for the standardized scripture. The *Prem Ambodh Pothi* (1693), attributed to Guru Gobind Singh, lists her among sixteen historic bhakti saints significant to Sikhism. Legends link her to Ravidas (Raidas), the untouchable leather-worker poet-saint: a pavilion at her Chittorgarh temple bears his engraved footprint, and she composed a song declaring, “I got a guru in the form of Sant Ravidas, there by obtaining life’s fulfillment.” While historically unverified, this association underscores her cross-caste appeal and the Bhakti movement’s challenge to hierarchy.

### Influence and Legacy
Scholars view Mirabai as both a historical poet-saint and a “canonical projection of social imagination” (Parita Mukta). Her legends embody “anticipatory radical democracy,” breaking feudal bonds through sheer conviction. Edwin Bryant emphasizes her enduring power: she “stands up for what is right and suffers bitterly… with a language of love” spanning human and divine emotion. From the 18th century onward, communities actively retold her story to inspire devotion rather than preserve history. She has inspired films (1945 Tamil *Meera* with M.S. Subbulakshmi; 1979 Hindi *Meera* by Gulzar), a 1997 TV series, novels (Kiran Nagarkar’s *Cuckold*), documentaries, music albums, and the Meera Mahal museum in Merta. English translations by Alston, Subramanian, Bly/Hirshfield, Schelling, Landes-Levi, Snell, and Sethi have globalized her voice.

## Terms
- **Bhakti movement**: Medieval Indian devotional trend (7th–17th c.) emphasizing personal, emotional devotion to a deity over ritual orthodoxy and caste barriers.
- **Madhurya bhava**: “Sweet mood”—a devotional attitude modeling the soul’s relationship to God as conjugal love (exemplified by the *gopis* of Braj).
- **Viraha**: The poetic theme of separation from the divine, expressing agonizing longing that paradoxically deepens devotion.
- **Padas**: Short metric verses, often set to music; the primary form of Mirabai’s compositions.
- **Mātric meter**: Syllabic (non-quantitative) poetic meter common in Rajasthani and Braj poetry, counting syllables rather than morae.
- **Rāga**: Melodic framework for Indian classical music; Mirabai’s *padas* are assigned specific rāgas for performance as *bhajans*.
- **Girdhar Nagar**: “Lifter of the Mountain” (referencing Krishna holding Govardhan Hill); Mirabai’s favored epithet for Krishna as her divine husband.
- **Hagiography**: Idealized biography of a saint; Mirabai’s life is known almost exclusively through conflicting hagiographical traditions.
- **Prem Ambodh Pothi**: “Ocean of Love” (c. 1693), a Gurmukhi text linked to Guru Gobind Singh, portraying Mirabai as a Sikh-revered bhakti saint with a divergent life story.
- **Bhaktamal**: “Garland of Devotees” (c. 1600) by Nabhadas; earliest datable reference to Mirabai, confirming her prominence in Bhakti circles by 1600.

## Debates and open questions
- **Historicity vs. legend**: No contemporary records exist; scholars debate which biographical elements (persecution, travel, death) reflect history versus hagiographic embellishment.
- **Authorship of poems**: With only two pre-18th-century manuscript attestations, the authenticity of the vast attributed corpus is contested; scholars disagree on criteria for a “Mirabai style.”
- **Ravidas as guru**: The claim rests on a temple footprint and a single song; no corroborating evidence exists, yet it shapes her cross-caste symbolism.
- **Cause of persecution**: Was it religious nonconformity (music, caste mixing) or political instability during Mughal-Rajput wars (Martin-Kershaw)?
- **Regional narratives**: Why do Mewari and Punjabi traditions differ so radically (flight vs. sedentary; in-laws vs. father as persecutor)? What does this reveal about community self-fashioning?
- **Sikh scripture exclusion**: Why were her compositions in Bhai Banno’s Adi Granth copy rejected by the Gurus, yet she appears in *Prem Ambodh Pothi*?
