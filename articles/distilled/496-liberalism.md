# Liberalism

## Overview

Liberalism is a political and moral philosophy centered on individual rights, liberty, consent of the governed, political equality, private property, and equality before the law. Emerging as a distinct movement during the Age of Enlightenment, it sought to replace hereditary privilege, absolute monarchy, and state religion with representative democracy, rule of law, and free markets. John Locke is widely regarded as the father of modern liberalism for systematizing the social contract theory, arguing that governments derive legitimacy from protecting natural rights to life, liberty, and property. Liberalism powered the Glorious, American, and French Revolutions and became the dominant ideology of modern history, though its definition diverged: in Europe and Latin America it often denotes a commitment to limited government and laissez-faire economics (classical liberalism), while in North America it refers to social liberalism, which endorses a regulated market and an expanded welfare state to ensure equal opportunity. Throughout the 20th century, liberal democracies prevailed in two world wars and the Cold War, spreading constitutional government, civil rights, and market economies globally, though they now face challenges from authoritarian capitalism and populist movements.

## Timeline

- **1688** — Glorious Revolution in Britain establishes parliamentary sovereignty and right of revolution; later called the "first modern liberal revolution."
- **1689–1690** — John Locke publishes *Two Treatises of Government* and *Letters Concerning Toleration*, founding liberalism as a distinct tradition based on social contract and natural rights.
- **1776** — American Revolution; Declaration of Independence; Adam Smith publishes *The Wealth of Nations*, founding classical economics.
- **1789** — French Revolution; abolition of feudalism (Aug 4) and passage of Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen.

- **1819** — Benjamin Constant distinguishes "Liberty of the Ancients" (participatory) from "Liberty of the Moderns" (civil liberties, representative government) at the Coppet Group.
- **1859** — John Stuart Mill publishes *On Liberty*, defining liberty as absence of interference unless harm to others; defends free speech and warns of "tyranny of the majority."
- **1861–1865** — American Civil War; Union victory boosts liberalism globally (Don H. Doyle).
- **1864** — Pope Pius IX issues *Syllabus of Errors* condemning liberalism; liberal forces expel Jesuit order in several countries.
- **Late 19th C.** — T. H. Green develops "positive liberty" (freedom *to* act), shifting British Liberal Party toward social liberalism (New Liberalism) and state intervention for welfare.
- **1917** — Russian Revolution overthrows Tsar; liberalism appears triumphant in Europe after WWI, but faces rising fascism and communism.
- **1933–1936** — John Maynard Keynes publishes *The Means to Prosperity* and *The General Theory*, founding Keynesian economics; justifies state intervention to manage demand.
- **1930s–1960s** — Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal and Lyndon B. Johnson's Great Society institutionalize social liberalism in the U.S. (Social Security, Civil Rights Act, Voting Rights Act, Medicare/Medicaid).
- **1970s–1980s** — Economic crises prompt shift to neoliberalism (Thatcher, Reagan), rolling back Keynesian consensus in favor of deregulation and marketization.
- **1989–1991** — Collapse of communist states in Eastern Europe leaves liberal democracy as the dominant Western model.
- **21st C.** — Liberal parties wield power globally; ideology faces challenges from authoritarian capitalism (e.g., China) and right-wing populism.

## Body

### Origins and Core Philosophy
Liberalism traces its etymology to the Latin *liber* ("free"). While precursors exist in antiquity (Marcus Aurelius, Pericles) and Imperial China, liberalism as a systematic ideology emerged in 17th-century England. The English Civil War saw the Levellers demand religious freedom, frequent parliaments, and equality before the law. The 1688 Glorious Revolution entrenched parliamentary sovereignty. John Locke provided the philosophical foundation: adopting Thomas Hobbes’s "state of nature" and social contract, Locke argued individuals possess natural rights to life, liberty, and property that precede government. Unlike Hobbes, who favored an absolute sovereign (Leviathan) for security, Locke insisted government legitimacy requires the ongoing consent of the governed and that tyranny justifies revolution. His *Two Treatises* (1690) rejected the divine right of kings (defended by Robert Filmer) and established the separation of church and state, arguing conscience cannot be ceded to government authority. In the *First Treatise*, Locke refuted Filmer’s patriarchal biblical interpretation, arguing the grant of dominion in Genesis was to humans over animals, not men over women, and that "conjugal society is made up by a voluntary compact between men and women." The source notes Locke "was not a feminist by modern standards" but accomplished a major step toward pluralism by integrating women into social theory.

### The Evolution of Liberty: Negative vs. Positive
Early liberals (Adam Smith, John Stuart Mill) defined liberty negatively—as the absence of interference from state or society. Mill’s *On Liberty* (1859) articulated the "harm principle": power over individuals is justified only to prevent harm to others. He warned of the "tyranny of the majority" suppressing minority rights, a concept also explored by Alexis de Tocqueville in *Democracy in America* (1835). In the late 19th century, British philosopher T. H. Green introduced "positive liberty": the freedom *to* achieve one’s potential, requiring the state to create enabling conditions (education, health, economic security). Green viewed society as an organic whole where individuals have a duty to promote the common good. This shifted liberalism from minimal state (classical) to interventionist welfare state (social/new liberalism). L. T. Hobhouse and John A. Hobson argued poverty and ignorance made formal liberty meaningless without material means to exercise it.

### Classical Liberalism and Political Economy
Classical liberalism advocates *laissez-faire* economics, free trade, limited government, and the rule of law. Adam Smith’s *Wealth of Nations* (1776) argued that unregulated competition, guided by an "invisible hand," maximizes social wealth through individual self-interest. He opposed monopolies, trade barriers, and guilds, limiting state roles to defence, public works, and the administration of justice. Jean-Baptiste Say emphasized the entrepreneur’s coordinating role as an intermediary combining land, capital, and labour, distinguishing entrepreneurial profit from the remuneration of capital. Say's Law, summarized as "products are paid for with products" or "a glut occurs only when too much resource is applied to making one product and not enough to another," was later characterized by John Maynard Keynes as "supply creates its own demand." Thomas Malthus (population geometry vs. arithmetic food growth) and David Ricardo (iron law of wages) provided pessimistic demographic and wage theories that reinforced non-intervention. Utilitarianism (Jeremy Bentham: "greatest happiness of the greatest number") became the policy justification for *laissez-faire*, arguing net social benefit was higher without state redistribution, though it also prompted reforms like the Metropolitan Police and prison reforms. Classical liberals (Richard Cobden) also linked free trade to peace, arguing commerce made war too costly for industrial nations.

### Social Liberalism and the Welfare State
By the late 19th century, industrial capitalism’s inequalities—poverty, unemployment, urban squalor—challenged classical assumptions. Critics included Victorian writers (Dickens, Carlyle, Matthew Arnold) and Jean Charles Léonard de Sismondi, whose *New Principles of Political Economy* (1819) offered the first comprehensive liberal critique of early capitalism and influenced both Mill and Marx. John Stuart Mill himself shifted toward socialism, advocating cooperative wage systems. T. H. Green tasked the state with fostering environments for moral self-realization. The "New Liberalism" (c. 1900) in Britain, led by Hobhouse and Hobson, argued liberty required a strong, welfare-oriented state to equalize opportunity. This culminated in the U.S. with FDR’s New Deal (1930s: Social Security, WPA) and LBJ’s Great Society (1960s: Civil Rights Act, Voting Rights Act, Medicare, Medicaid, War on Poverty). Modern U.S. liberalism now encompasses reproductive rights, LGBTQ+ rights, environmental justice, and universal healthcare/education access.

### Keynesian Economics and the Post-War Consensus
The Great Depression discredited *laissez-faire*. John Maynard Keynes, a former classical liberal turned social liberal, provided the theoretical alternative. In *The General Theory* (1936), he challenged Say’s Law, introducing "price stickiness" (wages don't fall easily) to explain persistent unemployment equilibria. He advocated counter-cyclical public spending ("prime the pump") to stimulate aggregate demand when private investment failed. Keynesianism dominated Western economic policy (1945–1980), underpinning the post-war welfare state and full employment. The 1970s stagflation crisis prompted a neoliberal turn (Thatcher, Reagan), emphasizing deregulation, privatization, and monetary control over fiscal stimulus, marking a paradigm shift away from the Keynesian consensus.

### Liberalism in Global Context: Ottoman Empire, Middle East, and Latin America
In the 19th/early 20th century Ottoman Empire and Middle East, liberalism drove the *Tanzimat* reforms and *Al-Nahda* (Arab Renaissance), promoting constitutionalism, secularism, and nationalism (Young Ottomans, Islamic Modernism). Prominent intellectuals included Rifa'a al-Tahtawi, Namık Kemal, and İbrahim Şinasi. However, reformist ideas remained confined to elites and segments of the emerging middle class; many Muslims viewed them as foreign impositions, complicating state efforts and contributing to a crisis in Islam and subsequent Islamic revivalism. In Latin America, 18th-century liberal agitation drove independence from Spain/Portugal; new regimes often adopted positivism (faith in science) to legitimize authority. In the Middle East, liberalism led to constitutional periods (Ottoman First and Second Constitutional Era, Persian constitutional period), but declined in the late 1930s due to Islamism and pan-Arab nationalism. Later intellectuals advocating liberal values included Taha Hussein, Ahmed Lutfi el-Sayed, Tawfiq al-Hakim, Abd El-Razzak El-Sanhuri, and Muhammad Mandur. In Iran, liberal nationalist Mohammad Mosaddegh was elected Prime Minister (1951) but ousted in a 1953 coup backed by Western interests, ending liberal dominance there.

### 19th Century: Nationalism, Unification, and Religious Opposition
The American Civil War (1861–1865) ensured national integrity and abolished slavery; historian Don H. Doyle argues the Union victory greatly boosted liberalism's course. In Europe, a mixture of liberal and nationalist sentiments unified Italy and Germany in the late 19th century. A liberal regime in Italy ended the secular power of the Popes, prompting the Vatican to launch a counter-crusade: Pope Pius IX issued the *Syllabus of Errors* (1864) condemning liberalism in all its forms. In many countries, liberal forces responded by expelling the Jesuit order.

### Anti-State Liberalism and Anarcho-Capitalism
A radical strand, identified by Ralph Raico as the "anti-state liberal tradition," rejects the state entirely. French philosopher Jakob Mauvillon (18th C.) and later Gustave de Molinari (1840s) argued security and law could be provided by private competition rather than a state monopoly. Molinari’s *The Production of Security* argued consumers should choose protection agencies. This tradition, distinct from Locke’s view of the state evolving from society, posits a fundamental conflict between voluntary society and coercive state (society of contract vs. society of authority). In the mid-20th century, Murray Rothbard synthesized Austrian economics and 19th-century individualist anarchism (Lysander Spooner, Benjamin Tucker) into "anarcho-capitalism," advocating a stateless society regulated by private dispute resolution, tort/contract law, and the non-aggression principle (NAP).

### Liberal Feminism
Liberal feminism seeks gender equality through political and legal reform within the liberal framework. Mary Wollstonecraft’s *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) argued women’s apparent inferiority resulted from lack of education ("cages"), not nature, demanding personhood and citizenship. John Stuart Mill’s *The Subjection of Women* (1869) compared marriage to slavery, arguing equality was necessary to discover true natural differences. Equity feminism (1980s onward), a classically liberal/libertarian variant (Steven Pinker), focuses strictly on equal treatment under law without commitments on psychological or biological differences.

### 20th Century: Wars, Cold War, and Civil Rights
WWI and the collapse of four empires initially seemed a liberal triumph (League of Nations, self-determination, cultural pluralism). However, the Great Depression fueled fascism (Nazi Germany, Italy) as a reaction against liberalism and communism. WWII Allied victory set up the Cold War: liberal Western Bloc vs. Communist Eastern Bloc. The U.S. civil rights movement (1960s) highlighted liberalism’s internal drive to expand "consent of the governed" to racial minorities. The 1989–1991 collapse of Eastern European communism appeared to confirm liberal democracy as the final form of government, but the 21st century brought challenges: China’s authoritarian capitalism, right-wing populism, and democratic backsliding. Larry Diamond notes democracy retreated after 1945, rebounded by 1995, but now faces a "recession."

### Criticism and Support
Liberalism has drawn criticism from diverse ideological groups. **Anarchists** criticise the social contract as creating an oppressive state. **Catholicism** engaged in lengthy power struggles with liberal governments; Christian democracy emerged as a reaction against laissez-faire industrialisation. **Conservatives** (Edmund Burke) criticised the reckless pursuit of progress and rationality (French Revolution), though liberal conservatism shares classical liberal principles. Patrick Deneen (*Why Liberalism Failed*, 2018) argues liberalism causes inequality, cultural decline, and centralized bureaucracy; Vladimir Putin claims liberalism is "obsolete." **Fascists** oppose liberalism's materialism, rationalism, and individualism but agree on private property. **Feminists** argue the public/private distinction allows bigotry in the private sphere. **Islam** contains both support (Quran 2:256: "no compulsion in religion") and opposition (Islamic supremacism, criminalizing apostasy). **Marxists** (Marx, Lenin) reject liberal theory's foundations, viewing wage slavery as inherent to liberal capitalism, though some liberals (Paine, Gesell) opposed wage slavery. **Social democracy** emerged in the 20th century to reform capitalism progressively, sharing strong similarities with social liberalism; one political scientist called American liberalism "bootleg social democracy" due to the absence of a significant social democratic tradition in the U.S.

## Terms

- ****Social Contract**** — A theoretical agreement where individuals cede some natural rights to a sovereign authority in exchange for protection of life, liberty, and property (Hobbes, Locke).
- ****Natural Rights**** — Rights (life, liberty, property) inherent to humans by nature or reason, preceding and limiting government authority (Locke).
- ****Negative Liberty**** — Freedom *from* external interference or coercion by the state or other individuals; the classic liberal definition (Mill, Berlin).
- ****Positive Liberty**** — Freedom *to* act autonomously and realize one's potential, requiring state provision of enabling conditions (education, welfare) (T. H. Green).
- ****Laissez-faire**** — Economic doctrine opposing government intervention in markets beyond protecting property and enforcing contracts; "let do" (Smith, classical liberals).
- ****Say's Law**** — Classical economic proposition that "products are paid for with products"; production generates the income necessary to purchase other goods (Jean-Baptiste Say). The phrase "supply creates its own demand" was coined by John Maynard Keynes.
- ****Keynesian Economics**** — Theory advocating government fiscal intervention (deficit spending) to manage aggregate demand and stabilize employment during recessions (J.M. Keynes).
- ****Neoliberalism**** — Late 20th-century policy paradigm emphasizing deregulation, privatization, free trade, and monetary restraint, rolling back the Keynesian welfare state (Thatcher, Reagan).
- ****Tyranny of the Majority**** — Danger in democracies where the majority uses its power to suppress the rights of minorities (Tocqueville, Mill).
- ****Anarcho-Capitalism**** — Anti-state liberal theory advocating elimination of the state in favor of private property, free markets, and competing private security/arbitration agencies (Molinari, Rothbard).
- ****Coppet Group**** — Circle of European thinkers (1804–1815) at Coppet Castle under Madame de Staël, including Benjamin Constant, Wilhelm von Humboldt, and Lord Byron, pivotal for modern liberalism and romanticism.

## Debates and open questions

*   **Definition of Liberty:** The tension between negative liberty (non-interference) and positive liberty (capacity to act) remains the central theoretical fault line, dividing classical liberals/libertarians from social liberals/progressives.
*   **Role of the State:** Whether the state should be a minimal "night-watchman" (classical), a welfare provider ensuring equal opportunity (social), or abolished entirely (anarcho-capitalist) is unresolved.
*   **Economic Justice vs. Efficiency:** The Keynesian/neoliberal debate persists: does state intervention correct market failures and ensure stability, or does it distort incentives and reduce growth?
*   **Universalism vs. Cultural Relativism:** Liberalism’s universalist claim (human rights apply everywhere) clashes with charges of Western imperialism, particularly in the Middle East and China, where liberal reforms are often viewed as foreign impositions.
*   **Democracy's Future:** With democratic backsliding, the rise of illiberal democracies (elections without rights), and the competitive model of authoritarian capitalism (China), the 20th-century assumption that liberal democracy is the inevitable endpoint of history is contested.
*   **Scope of Rights:** Ongoing expansion of rights claims (reproductive, transgender, environmental, digital privacy) tests the limits of liberal individualism and the state's capacity to adjudicate conflicting liberties.
