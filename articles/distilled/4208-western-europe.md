# Western Europe

## Overview
Western Europe is a geographic, cultural, and political region whose definition has evolved over time. Its modern identity was most sharply defined during the Cold War (1945–1991) as the democratic, capitalist bloc opposed to the Soviet-led Eastern Bloc. Today, it is a region of immense economic wealth and political influence, though its exact borders vary by classification system.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — La Tène culture develops in Western Europe.
- **3rd–5th centuries** — Roman Empire divides into Latin-speaking West and Greek-speaking East.
- **1054** — East–West Schism splits Christianity into Catholicism (West) and Eastern Orthodoxy.
- **15th century** — Renaissance and Age of Discovery begin; "the West" concept emerges.
- **1945** — Yalta Conference post-WWII division of Europe.
- **1946** — Winston Churchill popularizes the term "Iron Curtain."
- **1948** — Treaty of Brussels signed.
- **1954** — Western European Union established.
- **1989** — Fall of the Iron Curtain.
- **1991** — Dissolution of the Soviet Union and the Warsaw Pact.
- **2011** — Western European Union declared defunct.

## Body

### Historical and Cultural Foundations
The concept of "the West" originated in Europe as a counterpart to "the East," initially referring to the Latin-speaking, Roman Catholic half of the ancient Mediterranean world. A key historical division was reinforced by the split of the Roman Empire into Western and Eastern halves. The collapse of the Western Roman Empire and the survival of the Eastern (Byzantine) Empire, along with the Great Schism of 1054, deepened cultural and religious differences. The Reformation in the 16th century further divided Western Christianity into Catholicism and Protestantism.

### The Cold War Division
The modern perception of Western Europe was solidified during the Cold War. Following World War II, Europe was divided by the "Iron Curtain" into two spheres: the Western Bloc, aligned with the United States and NATO, and the Eastern Bloc, aligned with the Soviet Union and the Warsaw Pact. This division, based on opposing political and economic systems, defined the region's borders and identity for 46 years until the Iron Curtain fell in 1989 and the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991.

### Modern Classifications and Economy
Today, "Western Europe" has no single official definition. The UN geoscheme includes countries like Germany, France, and Switzerland. The CIA classification includes the UK and Ireland but excludes Germany and Switzerland. EuroVoc's list is broader. Economically, the region is one of the world's richest. Germany has Europe's highest GDP, Luxembourg has the world's highest GDP per capita, and Switzerland and Luxembourg have the world's highest average wages.

### Languages and Climate
Languages in Western Europe primarily belong to two Indo-European families: the Romance languages (e.g., French, Spanish) descended from Latin, and the Germanic languages (e.g., English, German). The climate varies from Mediterranean in the south to alpine in mountain ranges like the Alps, with a mild, humid climate in the west and northwest influenced by the North Atlantic Current. The region is identified as a hotspot for accelerating heatwaves.

## Terms
- **Iron Curtain**: The political, military, and ideological barrier erected by the Soviet Union after WWII to seal off itself and its dependent eastern and central European allies from open contact with the West.
- **East–West Schism**: The 1054 break in communion between what are now the Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic Churches.
- **La Tène culture**: A late Iron Age archaeological culture in Western and Central Europe, c. 450 BC to the Roman conquest.
- **Western Bloc**: The group of nations aligned with the United States and NATO during the Cold War.
- **Eastern Bloc**: The group of communist nations of Central and Eastern Europe, aligned with the Soviet Union during the Cold War.
- **UN geoscheme**: A system devised by the UN Statistics Division for statistical convenience, dividing countries into regional and subregional groups.
- **Romance languages**: A group of languages descended from Vulgar Latin, including French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, and Romanian.
- **Germanic languages**: A branch of the Indo-European language family, including English, German, Dutch, and the Scandinavian languages.

## Debates and Open Questions
The article notes that many historians and social scientists view the Cold War definition of Western and Eastern Europe as outdated. The precise borders of Western Europe remain contested, as evidenced by the differing lists from the UN, CIA, and EuroVoc. The region's relationship with the broader "Western world" concept is also a point of contextual variation.
