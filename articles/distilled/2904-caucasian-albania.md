# Caucasian Albania

## Overview
Caucasian Albania was an ancient kingdom in the Caucasus, primarily in the area of modern-day Azerbaijan. It was a multi-ethnic, multilingual state that played a significant role as a buffer and battleground between the Roman/Byzantine and Persian (Parthian/Sassanid) empires. Its history is central to understanding the complex ethnic, cultural, and political transformations of the region, and its legacy is a subject of modern scholarly and political debate.

## Timeline
- **1st century BC** — First mentioned by Greek historians; emerges as a vassal state of Armenia.
- **66-65 BC** — Roman general Pompey campaigns against and defeats Albanian forces.
- **252 AD** — Caucasian Albania becomes a vassal of the Sassanid Empire.
- **c. 330 AD** — Christianization begins; King Urnayr is baptized by the Armenian Gregory the Illuminator.
- **387 AD** — After the partition of Armenia, Albania expands to include the Armenian provinces of Artsakh and Utik.
- **5th century** — Sassanid King Yazdegerd II's Zoroastrian edict leads to a rebellion by Christian Albanians, Armenians, and Georgians.
- **552 AD** — The seat of the Albanian Catholicos (religious leader) is transferred to Partav.
- **Mid-7th century** — Falls to the Arab conquest and is incorporated into the Rashidun Caliphate.
- **10th century** — The capital, Barda, is a major city but is later sacked by Rus' raiders.
- **13th century** — The region is overrun by the Mongol Empire.

## Body

### Geonyms and Geography
The modern name "Caucasian Albania" is an exonym derived from Greek and Latin sources. Its original endonym is unknown, though the Armenian name *Aghuank* (possibly from *ału*, "sweet") was used. The Parthian name was *Ardhan* (Arran). In pre-Islamic times, the kingdom's territory was wider, covering eastern Transcaucasia. Its boundaries shifted, but it was generally bounded by the Greater Caucasus mountains, the Caspian Sea, and the Kura River. The capital was initially Qabala, later moved to Partav in the 5th century.

### Ethnogenesis and Languages
The population was highly diverse, with Strabo reporting 26 tribes and languages. The ancestors of modern Lezgic-speaking peoples (like the Udi, Lezgins, and Tsakhurs) likely lived there. After Christianization, the population was gradually assimilated: some became Armenian or Georgian, while eastern groups were Islamized and later Turkified, contributing to the ethnogenesis of modern Azerbaijanis. The Caucasian Albanian language, written with an alphabet created by Mesrob Mashtots in the 5th century, is the likely ancestor of the modern Udi language. Iranian languages, particularly Parthian and later Middle Persian, were also influential in administration and culture.

### Religion and the Church
The original population practiced polytheism, later influenced by Zoroastrianism under Persian rule. Christianity was introduced in the early 4th century, closely tied to the Armenian Apostolic Church. The Church of Caucasian Albania maintained its own Catholicos but was often under Armenian ecclesiastical influence. After the Arab conquest and internal schisms, the church declined; by 705, its leadership was re-subordinated to the Armenian Patriarchate, effectively ending its independent status. Islam spread from the 8th century onward.

### History and Political Alliances
Caucasian Albania's history was defined by its position between great powers. It was part of the Achaemenid and Parthian empires before becoming a vassal of the Sassanids in 252 AD. It maintained complex relations with Rome, which briefly established a protectorate. A key event was the 387 AD partition of Armenia, after which Albania, with Sassanid backing, annexed the Armenian provinces of Artsakh and Utik, significantly expanding its territory and Armenian cultural influence. The kingdom was ruled by various dynasties, including the Arsacids and later the Mihranids, before being conquered by the Arabs in the 7th century.

### Impact of Armenian Culture
After 387 AD, Armenian politics, culture, and language had a profound impact on Caucasian Albania. The annexed provinces of Artsakh and Utik were demographically and culturally Armenian. Armenian became the language of the church and administration, and Armenian feudal terms, toponyms, and historical narratives (like those of Movses Kaghankatvatsi) dominate the medieval sources for the region's history.

## Terms
*   **Exonym/Endonym:** An exonym is a name for a group used by outsiders; an endonym is the name used by the group itself.
*   **Suzerainty:** A relationship in which one state controls the foreign affairs of another, which retains internal autonomy.
*   **Marzpan:** A Sassanid provincial governor.
*   **Catholicos:** A high-ranking bishop or patriarch in certain Eastern Christian churches.
*   **Lezgic languages:** A group of Northeast Caucasian languages spoken in Dagestan and Azerbaijan.
*   **Mihranid dynasty:** A Persian or Parthian royal family that ruled Caucasian Albania from the 6th century until the Arab conquest.
*   **Khachkar:** A carved, cross-bearing stone stele, a distinctive Armenian art form.

## Debates and Open Questions
The history of Caucasian Albania is a major topic of debate, particularly in the context of the Nagorno-Karabakh conflict. Azerbaijani historiography often presents Caucasian Albanians as the primary ancestors of modern Azerbaijanis and claims Armenian cultural monuments in the region as originally Albanian. This revisionist approach, criticized by many Western and Russian scholars, involves re-attributing Armenian historical figures and sites to an "Albanian" identity. Scholars like Thomas de Waal and Robert Hewsen caution that Soviet and post-Soviet Azerbaijani editions of historical sources have been edited to remove references to Armenians. The debate extends to the ownership of historical sites, such as the David Gareja monastery complex, with Azerbaijan claiming it as Caucasian Albanian and Georgia asserting its Georgian origin.
