# William Hogarth

## Overview
William Hogarth (1697–1764) was a pioneering English artist who revolutionized visual storytelling by creating sequential, satirical prints that critiqued the social and moral vices of 18th-century life. His "modern moral subjects" were widely popularized through engravings, making him the most significant English artist of his generation and establishing a lasting tradition of narrative satire in art.

- **1753** — Published his aesthetic treatise, *The Analysis of Beauty*.
- **1757** — Appointed Serjeant Painter to King George II.
- **26 Oct 1764** — Died in London; buried in Chiswick.

## Body

### Early Life and Training
Hogarth was born into a family that faced financial hardship; his father was imprisoned for debt, an experience that may have informed the "hard edge" of his later work. He trained as an engraver but did not complete his apprenticeship. He later studied at drawing academies in London, where he was influenced by French and Italian art. His early career involved engraving trade cards and book illustrations, and he began creating satirical prints on contemporary events like the South Sea Bubble crash.

### The "Modern Moral Subjects"
Hogarth's fame rests on his sequential narrative series, which he called "modern moral subjects." These were often first painted and then widely disseminated as engravings. Key series include *A Harlot's Progress* (1731), depicting a country girl's downfall into prostitution; *A Rake's Progress* (1733–35), following a young heir's dissipation into madness; and *Marriage A-la-Mode* (1743–45), a tragic satire on a marriage of convenience between aristocracy and new wealth. These works combined realistic detail with moralizing satire, often parodying religious art to critique contemporary society. The success of these series led to rampant piracy, prompting Hogarth to lobby for and secure the first copyright law for visual artists, "Hogarth's Act" (1735).

### Social and Political Satire
Beyond his major series, Hogarth produced powerful standalone prints addressing social issues. *Beer Street and Gin Lane* (1751) contrasted the prosperity of beer-drinking with the squalor of gin addiction, supporting temperance legislation. *The Four Stages of Cruelty* (1751) traced the progression from animal abuse to murder, advocating for humane treatment. His work often engaged with political themes, such as the *Election* series (1755–58) satirizing parliamentary corruption. He also attempted history painting, with works like *The Gate of Calais* (1748) using national stereotypes to assert British superiority.

### Portraits and Later Career
Hogarth was a successful portrait painter, known for capturing character and movement, as in his portrait of actor David Garrick as Richard III (1745). He painted his friend Captain Coram (1740) for the Foundling Hospital, of which he was a founding governor. In 1753, he published *The Analysis of Beauty*, defining his aesthetic theory around the "Line of Beauty," a serpentine line he believed embodied grace. He was appointed Serjeant Painter to the King in 1757. His later works include the pessimistic *Tailpiece, or The Bathos* (1764).

## Terms
*   **Modern Moral Subjects:** Hogarth's term for his narrative series of paintings and prints that told cautionary tales about contemporary social vices.
*   **Engraving:** A printmaking technique where an image is incised into a metal plate, allowing for mass reproduction.
*   **Conversation Piece:** An informal group portrait, often of family or friends in a domestic setting.
*   **Hogarth's Act (1735):** The Engravers' Copyright Act, the first law to protect the copyright of visual artists against unauthorized reproductions.
*   **Line of Beauty:** A serpentine, S-shaped curve that Hogarth, in *The Analysis of Beauty*, argued was the fundamental principle of grace and liveliness in art.
*   **Serjeant Painter:** A court appointment responsible for painting and decorating royal residences and properties.
*   **Deism:** A philosophical belief in a creator God who does not intervene in the universe, a view attributed to Hogarth.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Attribution:** Some prints, like *Burlington Gate* (1731), once attributed to Hogarth are now disputed by modern authorities such as Ronald Paulson.
*   **Artistic Ambition vs. Critical Reception:** Hogarth aspired to be a history painter (depicting grand historical or biblical scenes), but this work was critically undervalued by contemporaries like Joshua Reynolds, who favored a more classical "grand style."
*   **Interpretation of Satire:** The precise targets and meanings of details within his densely symbolic works continue to be analyzed and debated by scholars.
