# Metallurgy

## Overview
Metallurgy is the scientific and technological study of metallic elements, their alloys, and related processes, encompassing both the extraction of metals from ores and the engineering of metal components for consumer and industrial products. It provides the scientific foundation for metalworking, distinguishing itself from craft-based practices through systematic understanding of physical and chemical behavior. The field is divided into chemical metallurgy (focused on extraction and corrosion) and physical metallurgy (concerned with mechanical properties and microstructure), and has evolved from ancient gold-working to modern applications in nanotechnology, superconductors, and biomedical materials.

## Timeline
- **40,000 BCE** — Native gold used in Spanish caves
- **7th–6th millennia BCE** — Cold metallurgy of native copper in Anatolia and Iraq
- **6200–5000 BCE** — First smelting in Balkans and Carpathians
- **5500 BCE** — Copper smelting at Pločnik, Serbia (Vinča culture)
- **4600–4200 BCE** — Varna Necropolis gold treasure, Bulgaria
- **c. 3500 BCE** — Bronze Age begins with copper-tin alloy
- **c. 1200 BCE** — Iron smelting invented by Hittites, beginning Iron Age
- **300 BCE** — Wootz steel production in Indian subcontinent
- **1556** — Georg Agricola publishes *De re metallica*

## Body

### Etymology and Definition
The term "metallurgy" derives from the Ancient Greek *metallourgós* ("worker in metal"), combining *métallon* ("mine, metal") and *érgon* ("work"). Originally an alchemical term for extracting metals from minerals, it was redefined in the late 19th century as the scientific study of metals, alloys, and processes. A specialist in the field is called a metallurgist.

### Historical Development
The earliest metals used by humans were those found in native form—gold, silver, copper, tin, and meteoric iron—enabling cold-working without smelting. The first evidence of hot metallurgy (smelting) appears in the Balkans and Carpathian Mountains around 6200–5000 BCE, with the Vinča culture producing smelted copper artifacts by 5500 BCE. The Carpatho-Balkan region became the earliest major metallurgical province in Eurasia, overshadowing contemporary centers. Lead smelting may have preceded copper smelting in the Near East by the 6th millennium BCE. The Bronze Age began around 3500 BCE when the Near East discovered that combining copper and tin produced superior bronze. Iron smelting, far more technically demanding, was developed by the Hittites around 1200 BCE, initiating the Iron Age. The wootz steel process emerged in southern India by 300 BCE, producing high-quality steel exported as "Seric Iron" to Rome and later known as Damascus steel in Europe. Georg Agricola's 16th-century *De re metallica* documented the complex mining and metallurgical practices of the era.

### Branches of Metallurgy
Chemical metallurgy focuses on metal reduction and oxidation, studying mineral processing, extraction, thermodynamics, electrochemistry, and corrosion. Physical metallurgy examines mechanical and physical properties, covering crystallography, material characterization, mechanical metallurgy, phase transformations, and failure mechanisms. Production is further divided into ferrous metallurgy (iron-based, accounting for 95% of global metal production) and non-ferrous metallurgy (other metals).

### Extractive Metallurgy
Extractive metallurgy involves removing valuable metals from ores and refining them to purer forms. The process begins with mining, followed by crushing or grinding to separate valuable particles from waste. Concentration techniques isolate the desired metal, which is then reduced physically, chemically, or electrolytically. Leaching can dissolve minerals directly from ore bodies when conditions permit. Tailings from one process may serve as feed for extracting secondary metals, and concentrates containing multiple valuable metals require further separation.

### Metal Alloys and Applications
The iron-carbon system, including steels and cast irons, has received extensive study. Plain carbon steels serve low-cost, high-strength applications, while cast irons (including ductile iron) offer different properties. Engineering metals include aluminum, chromium, copper, magnesium, nickel, titanium, zinc, and silicon (a non-metal). Corrosion-resistant applications use stainless steels, nickel alloys, titanium alloys, and copper alloys. Lightweight requirements drive use of aluminum and magnesium alloys in automotive and aerospace sectors. Nickel-based superalloys like Inconel serve high-temperature applications, while single crystal alloys minimize creep at extreme temperatures. High-purity single crystal silicon is essential for semiconductor devices.

### Production Engineering
Production metallurgy focuses on creating metallic components for consumer and engineering products through alloy production, shaping, heat treatment, and surface treatment. Metallurgists balance properties such as cost, weight, strength, toughness, hardness, corrosion resistance, fatigue resistance, and performance across temperature extremes. Hardness testing (Rockwell, Vickers, Brinell scales) characterizes elasticity and plasticity. Environmental factors critically affect performance: ferrous metals corrode in saltwater, metals become brittle under cryogenic conditions, cyclic loading causes fatigue, and constant stress at high temperatures leads to creep.

### Metalworking Processes
Casting involves pouring molten metal into shaped molds, with variants including sand casting, investment casting (lost wax), die casting, and continuous casting. Forging hammers red-hot billets into shape. Rolling passes billets through progressively narrower rollers to create sheets. Extrusion forces hot metal through dies. Machining uses lathes and mills to cut cold metal. Sintering heats compressed metal powders in non-oxidizing environments. Fabrication cuts and bends sheets into structural shapes. Laser cladding builds 3D structures using laser-melted metallic powder. 3D printing sinters or melts powder metal in three-dimensional space. Cold-working processes increase strength through work hardening, creating microscopic defects that resist further deformation.

### Heat Treatment
Heat treatment modifies metal properties including strength, ductility, toughness, hardness, and corrosion resistance. Annealing heats metal then cools slowly, softening it and relieving internal stresses. Quenching cools metal rapidly after heating, freezing molecules in the hard martensite form. Tempering relieves stresses from hardening, reducing hardness while improving impact resistance. Thermo-mechanical treatments combine mechanical and thermal processes for enhanced properties, commonly applied to high-alloy steels, superalloys, and titanium alloys.

### Surface Treatments
Electroplating bonds thin metal layers (gold, silver, chromium, zinc) to surfaces using electrical charging of electrodes in electrolyte solutions, reducing corrosion and improving appearance. Shot peening blasts small spherical shot against surfaces, creating compression stresses that prolong part life and prevent fatigue, stress corrosion, and cracking failures. Thermal spraying (HVOF, plasma spray, flame spray, arc spray, metalizing) applies molten coating materials at high velocity, offering superior high-temperature performance compared to electroplating. Electroless deposition autocatalytically plates metals onto nonconductive surfaces like plastics and ceramics through chemical reduction of metal cations in liquid baths.

### Characterization
Metallography, invented by Henry Clifton Sorby, examines microscopic and macroscopic metal structures. Samples are ground, polished to mirror finish, and etched to reveal microstructure, then examined under optical or electron microscopes to determine composition, mechanical properties, and processing history. Crystallography uses X-ray or electron diffraction to identify materials and crystal structures, with quantitative analysis calculating phase amounts and strain levels. Advanced techniques include scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), and atom-probe tomography (APT).

## Terms
- **Alloy**: A mixture of two or more metallic elements, sometimes including non-metallic elements to achieve specific properties.
- **Smelting**: The process of extracting metal from ore by heating and melting, often using a blast furnace.
- **Martensite**: A hard, crystalline structure formed in steel during rapid cooling (quenching).
- **Work hardening**: The strengthening of metal through plastic deformation at cold-working temperatures, creating microscopic defects.
- **Creep**: The gradual deformation of metal under constant stress at elevated temperatures.
- **Crystallography**: The study of crystal structures in materials, often using X-ray or electron diffraction.
- **Thermo-mechanical treatment**: Combined mechanical and thermal processing to optimize material properties.
- **Electroplating**: A chemical surface treatment bonding thin metal layers using electrical current in an electrolyte solution.
- **Wootz steel**: High-quality ultra-high carbon steel produced in ancient India, characterized by carbide inclusions and nanomaterial microstructure.
- **Extractive metallurgy**: The practice of removing valuable metals from ores and refining them into purer forms.

## Debates and Open Questions
The precise beginnings of metallurgy remain unclear, with new archaeological discoveries continuously emerging. The relationship between lead and copper smelting chronologies is debated, as lead artifacts from the 6th millennium BCE in the Near East may predate copper smelting. The extent of technological exchange between early metallurgical centers, such as between the Balkans and the Near East, is not fully understood. The mechanisms behind pattern formation in wootz/Damascus steel continue to be studied, with recent reproduction research highlighting the roles of ore impurities and thermal cycling. The transition from cold to hot metallurgy and its cultural drivers remain topics of ongoing investigation.
