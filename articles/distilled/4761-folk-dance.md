# Folk dance

## Overview
Folk dances are traditional dances that reflect the life of a people from a specific country or region, typically performed at social gatherings by non-professionals. They are distinguished from ritual or religious dances and are primarily learned informally through observation and imitation, preserving local identity across generations. Understanding folk dance provides insight into cultural heritage, social cohesion, and the historical movement of traditions across borders.

## Timeline
- **Pre-modern era** — Folk dances develop organically within communities, long before modern national boundaries.
- **Late 19th century** — Partner dances like the polka, mazurka, and waltz become fashionable across Europe.
- **1970s** — The Balfolk movement begins in Western and Central Europe as part of a folk revival.
- **c. 2000** — Balfolk events grow in popularity, featuring live folk-inspired music.

## Body

### Characteristics and Definition
Folk dances are participatory social activities, not staged performances, though some are later adapted for theater. They are performed by people with little or no professional training, guided by inherited traditions. New dancers learn informally by observing and imitating experienced participants. The term is generally reserved for dances significantly bound by tradition from periods when a clear distinction existed between the dances of the "common folk" and the social elite. Modern social dances like hip hop are typically called street or vernacular dances, not folk dances.

### European Traditions
European folk dances include sword dances and forms like contra dance and Scottish country dance, though the latter are sometimes inaccurately labeled folk dances in a strict ethnological sense. Many country and ballroom dances evolved from earlier folk traditions. Dance forms often predate modern national borders, leading to shared traditions across countries; for example, several Serbian, Bulgarian, and Croatian dances share steps, names, and melodies. International folk dance groups exist worldwide, and the Balfolk movement promotes social dance events with live music, featuring dances like the schottische and polka.

### Asian Traditions
The Middle East, Central Asia, and South Asia have diverse folk dances reflecting cultural exchange along trade routes like the Silk Road. Examples include the Dabke (Middle East), Attan (Afghanistan), and Lezginka (Caucasus/Central Asia). India's dances, such as Bhangra (Punjab) and Garba (Gujarat), express agricultural and social themes. Sri Lanka has dances like Leekeli (Stick Dance) and Kalagedi (Pot Dance). East and Southeast Asian dances, such as Japan's Bon Odori, China's Yangge, and the Philippines' Tinikling, often combine music, costume, and symbolism during festivals.

### Americas and Africa
In South America, dances like the Cueca (Chile, Bolivia), Cumbia (Colombia), and Samba (Brazil) are prominent. North American traditions include Clogging and Square Dance in the U.S., and Baile Folklorico in Mexico. African folk dances include Adowa (Ghana) and Bikutsi (Cameroon). Oceania features Hula (Hawaii) and Haka (New Zealand).

## Terms
- **Folk dance**: A dance reflecting the life of a people from a specific region, typically performed socially by non-professionals and learned informally.
- **Ritual dance**: A dance of religious or ceremonial purpose, not considered a folk dance.
- **Balfolk**: A social dance event featuring live folk-inspired music, popular in Western and Central Europe since the 1970s folk revival.
- **Vernacular dance**: A social dance that evolves spontaneously within a community, like hip hop, distinct from tradition-bound folk dance.
- **Ethnic dance**: A term emphasizing the cultural roots of a dance; nearly all folk dances are ethnic.
- **Sword dance**: A traditional European folk dance form involving swords or sword-like objects.
- **International folk dance**: A recreational activity where participants learn and perform traditional dances from various cultures.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that some choreographed social dances, such as contra dance and modern Western square dance, are sometimes described as folk dances, though this usage is not accurate in the strictest ethnological sense. The boundary between folk dance and other social or ballroom dances is often blurred, as many country and ballroom dances trace their origins to earlier folk traditions that underwent refinement and formalization.
