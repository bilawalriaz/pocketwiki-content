# Śāriputra

## Overview
Śāriputra (Pali: Sāriputta) was one of the Buddha's two chief male disciples, renowned for his wisdom and leadership in the early Buddhist community (Sangha). He played a key role in systematizing the Buddha's teachings and is traditionally credited with helping to develop the Abhidharma, a technical philosophical analysis of doctrine. His legacy is central to Theravada Buddhism, while in Mahayana texts he often serves as a counterpoint to illustrate more advanced doctrines.

## Timeline
- **c. 6th–5th century BCE** — Born as Upatiṣya in Magadha, near Rājagaha.
- **Youth** — Becomes childhood friends with Kolita (Maudgalyāyana); both become spiritual wanderers.
- **After searching other teachers** — Encounters the monk Aśvajit, hears the "Ye Dharma Hetu" stanza, and attains the first stage of enlightenment (sotapanna).
- **Shortly after** — Ordains with Maudgalyāyana under the Buddha; the Buddha declares them his chief disciples.
- **Two weeks after ordination** — Attains arhatship (full enlightenment) while fanning the Buddha.
- **During Buddha's ministry** — Acts as "General of the Dharma," teaching, clarifying doctrine, and managing monastic affairs.
- **Shortly before the Buddha's death** — Attains parinirvana (final death) in his hometown after converting his mother.
- **After death** — Relics are cremated and initially enshrined at Jetavana Monastery.
- **1851** — Archaeologists Cunningham and Maisey discover relic caskets inscribed with his name at Sanchi and Satdhāra.
- **1947–1952** — Discovered relics are returned from Britain and distributed to Sri Lanka, Burma, and India.

## Body

### Early Life and Meeting the Buddha
Born Upatiṣya to a wealthy family, he was the son of Śārī (hence his later name). He and his friend Kolita mastered the Vedas before becoming disillusioned with worldly life. After studying under the sceptic teacher Sañjaya, they parted with a pact to share any path to liberation they found. Upatiṣya soon met the monk Aśvajit, who taught him the core Buddhist principle of dependent origination. Upon hearing this, Upatiṣya attained the first stage of enlightenment. He immediately shared the teaching with Kolita, who also attained the same stage. Together with many of Sañjaya's followers, they ordained under the Buddha. The Buddha then designated Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana as his two chief disciples, a role foretold from a past life.

### Role as Chief Disciple
As the first chief disciple, Śāriputra was foremost in wisdom. His primary role was the systematization and analysis of the Buddha's teachings. He frequently asked the Buddha questions to elicit profound teachings and was authorized to teach in the Buddha's absence. He held a leadership role in the Sangha, overseeing monastic affairs, assigning meditation objects, and was the first disciple allowed to ordain others. He was known for his strict adherence to monastic rules and his meticulous nature. While Maudgalyāyana was foremost in psychic powers, Śāriputra also demonstrated such abilities, though his wisdom was considered his defining trait.

### Abhidharma and Legacy
Śāriputra is traditionally credited as the primary compiler of the Abhidharma, an "abstract and highly technical systemization of the doctrine." According to Theravada tradition, he received summaries of the Buddha's teachings to devas and reorganized them into the Abhidharma Pitaka. Different Buddhist schools attribute various foundational Abhidharma texts to him. Historians debate this attribution, suggesting the Abhidharma developed over centuries, but a simpler precursor may have existed early on. In Theravada Buddhism, he is venerated as a saintly figure close to a second Buddha. In Mahayana sutras, his role varies: he is a principal interlocutor in texts like the Heart Sutra and Lotus Sutra, but in others (e.g., the Vimalakīrti Sūtra), he is depicted as unable to grasp advanced Mahayana concepts like emptiness, serving as a representative of the Śrāvaka (Hinayana) path.

### Death and Relics
Śāriputra realized his death was imminent and returned to his hometown to convert his mother. He died peacefully shortly before the Buddha. His relics were cremated and enshrined. Archaeological excavations in the 19th century uncovered relic caskets with his name at Sanchi and Satdhāra, suggesting they were redistributed by later kings like Asoka. These relics were eventually returned from Britain in the 20th century and are now enshrined in India, Sri Lanka, and Burma.

## Terms
- **Arhat**: A "worthy one" who has attained nirvana and is freed from the cycle of rebirth.
- **Sangha**: The Buddhist monastic community.
- **Abhidharma**: "Higher Dharma"; a systematic and philosophical analysis of Buddhist teachings.
- **Sotapanna**: "Stream-enterer"; the first of four stages of enlightenment in Theravada Buddhism.
- **Parinirvana**: The final death of an arhat, after which they are no longer reborn.
- **Dharmasenāpati**: "General of the Dharma"; a title for Śāriputra denoting his teaching authority.
- **Prajñāpāramitā**: "Perfection of wisdom"; a central Mahayana concept regarding the understanding of emptiness.
- **Śrāvaka**: "Hearer"; a disciple who follows the path to arhatship, often contrasted with the Mahayana bodhisattva path.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historicity of Abhidharma Attribution**: Scholars debate whether Śāriputra actually compiled the Abhidharma. Some argue the text developed centuries later, while others suggest a simpler, oral precursor existed in his time.
- **Dual Traditions**: There is scholarly discussion about whether the historical Śāriputra was primarily a forest ascetic or a settled scholar. Some texts portray him as a forest saint, while the dominant tradition emphasizes his scholastic and administrative role.
- **Mahayana Portrayal**: The depiction of Śāriputra as unable to understand Mahayana doctrines in some sutras is interpreted by scholars as a literary device to highlight the profundity of those teachings, rather than a historical critique.
