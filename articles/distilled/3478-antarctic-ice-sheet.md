# Antarctic ice sheet

## Overview
The Antarctic ice sheet is the largest glacier on Earth, covering 98% of the Antarctic continent. It holds about 61% of the planet's fresh water, and its stability is critical for global sea levels and ocean circulation. Its two main parts, the East and West Antarctic Ice Sheets, respond very differently to climate change, with the smaller West Antarctic Ice Sheet being far more vulnerable.

## Timeline
- **c. 60–45.5 million years ago** — Icing of Antarctica begins in the Late Palaeocene or middle Eocene.
- **c. 34 million years ago** — Glaciation escalates during the Eocene–Oligocene extinction event.
- **c. 5–3 million years ago** — The West Antarctic Ice Sheet declines during the warm early Pliocene.
- **1950s–2000** — The West Antarctic Ice Sheet warms by over 0.1 °C per decade.
- **Since 1957** — The whole continent shows an average warming trend of >0.05 °C per decade.
- **1970s–early 2020s** — The upper cell of Southern Ocean circulation strengthens by 50–60%; the lower cell weakens by 10–20%.
- **Early 2020s** — The East Antarctic Ice Sheet shows net mass gain from precipitation, while the West Antarctic Ice Sheet loses significant ice.
- **By 2100** — Antarctic ice loss alone is projected to add ~11 cm to global sea level rise.
- **By 2050** — One study suggests the Southern Ocean overturning circulation could lose half its strength under a worst-case scenario.
- **By 2300** — A potential collapse of the Southern Ocean overturning circulation would likely be complete.

## Body

### Geography and Structure
The Antarctic ice sheet covers 14 million km² and contains 26.5 million km³ of ice, with an average thickness of over 2 km. It is divided by the Transantarctic Mountains into the large, land-based East Antarctic Ice Sheet (EAIS) and the smaller West Antarctic Ice Sheet (WAIS). The WAIS is a "marine-based" ice sheet, meaning its bed lies more than 2,500 meters below sea level in places, making it vulnerable to ocean-driven melting. Its major outlet glaciers, like Thwaites and Pine Island, drain into floating ice shelves.

### Climate Response and Sea Level Rise
The EAIS is over 10 times larger and higher in elevation than the WAIS, making it less vulnerable to climate change. While the EAIS has recently gained mass from increased snowfall, the WAIS is losing ice rapidly. By 2100, Antarctic melt alone could raise sea levels by about 11 cm. The WAIS is susceptible to "marine ice sheet instability," a process where warm ocean water melts the ice from below, potentially leading to rapid, irreversible collapse. If triggered, this could add tens of centimeters more to sea level rise. A full collapse of the WAIS would eventually cause 3.3–4.3 meters of sea level rise, while the more stable EAIS could contribute 0.5–0.9 meters from current warming levels.

### Impact on Ocean Circulation
Antarctic ice loss generates 1100–1500 billion tons of fresh meltwater per year. This freshens the Southern Ocean, increasing its stratification and weakening the dense, saline "Antarctic bottom water" that drives the lower cell of the Southern Ocean overturning circulation. Since the 1970s, this lower cell has weakened by 10–20%. A complete collapse of this circulation is possible if global warming reaches 1.7–3 °C, though it would unfold over centuries, potentially causing major shifts in Southern Hemisphere climate and marine ecosystems.

### Geological History
Antarctic glaciation began between 60 and 45.5 million years ago, driven primarily by a decrease in atmospheric CO₂ levels. A major escalation occurred about 34 million years ago when CO₂ fell to around 760 ppm. The West Antarctic Ice Sheet declined during the warm early Pliocene (5–3 million years ago), but the East Antarctic Ice Sheet remained stable.

## Terms
- **Marine-based ice sheet**: An ice sheet whose bed lies below sea level, making it vulnerable to melting from warm ocean water.
- **Marine ice sheet instability**: A positive feedback loop where warm water melts the grounding line of a marine-based glacier, causing it to retreat and accelerate ice loss.
- **Southern Ocean overturning circulation**: A global ocean circulation pattern driven by the sinking of cold, dense, saline Antarctic bottom water.
- **Isostatic rebound**: The rise of land masses after the removal of the heavy weight of ice sheets, which can add to global sea level rise.
- **Transantarctic Mountains**: The mountain range that divides the East and West Antarctic Ice Sheets.
- **Sverdrup (Sv)**: A unit of ocean current flow equal to 1 million cubic meters per second.
- **Eocene–Oligocene extinction event**: A major climate transition about 34 million years ago when Antarctic glaciation escalated.

## Debates and Open questions
- The exact timeline and likelihood of a collapse of the Southern Ocean overturning circulation remain highly uncertain, with limited confidence in climate model projections.
- The potential for marine ice sheet instability to be triggered in the near future, and its precise consequences for the rate of sea level rise, is a major area of research.
- The long-term fate of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet is debated; while very likely to disappear even with no further warming, the process could take between 500 and 13,000 years depending on future emissions.
