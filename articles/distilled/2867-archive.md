# Archive

## Overview
An archive is a collection of historical records or materials, or the facility that houses them, preserved for their enduring cultural, historical, or evidentiary value. Archives are distinct from libraries because they typically hold unique, unpublished primary source documents that are the natural byproduct of an individual or organization's activities. The study and practice of managing these records is called archival science.

## Body

### Definition and Etymology
An archive contains primary source documents that have accumulated naturally over an individual or organization's lifetime, showing their history and function. This is contrasted with documents consciously created to communicate a message to posterity. The English word "archive" derives from the Greek *arkheion*, originally the home of a ruler where state documents were kept. The terms "archive" and "archivist" entered English in the 17th and 18th centuries but were not widely used domestically until the late 19th century.

### History and Theory
The practice of keeping official records is ancient, with well-developed archives in China, Greece, and Rome. Modern archival theory has roots in the French Revolution, which led to the creation of the French National Archives. The principle of *respect des fonds* (respecting the original order and provenance of records) was systematized in the 19th century. Archivists select records for permanent preservation based on their enduring value.

### Types of Archives
Archives are categorized by the institutions that maintain them. **Academic archives** preserve institutional history and serve scholars. **Business archives** maintain corporate records to control brand history and are typically not public. **Government archives** (e.g., the U.S. National Archives and Records Administration, The National Archives in the UK) are often open to the public. **Church archives**, like the Vatican Apostolic Archive, hold religious records. **Film archives** preserve audiovisual heritage. **Non-profit archives** are maintained by historical societies or foundations. **Web archiving** uses crawlers to collect parts of the internet. **Dark archives** are collections kept in cold storage, not openly accessible.

### Standardization and Protection
The International Council on Archives (ICA) develops standards like ISAD(G) for archival description. Archives face threats from natural disasters and conflict; organizations like UNESCO and Blue Shield International work to protect cultural property in archives, including creating "no-strike lists" during wars.

### Limitations and Alternatives
Physical archives are increasingly digitizing materials for greater accessibility, though gaps may exist. Conventional archives can prioritize tangible items over ephemeral experiences, potentially marginalizing certain narratives. In response, practices like **anarchiving** and **counter-archiving** aim to decolonize archives and prioritize intersectional accessibility, often using social media to disseminate history directly to communities.

## Terms
- **Archivist**: A person who works in archives.
- **Archival science**: The study and practice of organizing, preserving, and providing access to archival materials.
- **Primary source**: A document or record created at the time under study, serving as firsthand evidence.
- **Respect des fonds**: An archival principle stating that records from the same creator (fonds) should be kept together and their original order respected.
- **Repository**: A physical place of storage for archives.
- **Fonds**: The entire body of records of an organization, family, or individual that have been created and accumulated as the result of an organic process.
- **Provenance**: The origin and chain of custody of archival records.
- **Anarchiving**: A practice that disseminates history directly to communities, often via social media, to bypass traditional institutions.
- **Dark archive**: An archive that is collected and preserved but not made openly accessible.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source highlights a debate about what is considered "archivable." Conventional archives may prioritize tangible items over ephemeral experiences, actions, and bodies, which can limit the content available and alienate marginalized populations. This has led to counter-archiving efforts that challenge traditional conceptions of history and aim to preserve narratives often absent from institutional archives. The destruction of archives, as seen in the lack of a Palestinian state archive, also forces historians to rely on alternative sources and methods.
