# John Donne

## Overview
John Donne (1571/72–1631) was an English poet, cleric, and lawyer whose work defines the metaphysical poets—a group later so named by Samuel Johnson for their use of intellectual complexity, extended metaphors (conceits), and colloquial rhythms. Born into a recusant Catholic family during a period of persecution, Donne was barred from university degrees for refusing the Oath of Supremacy. His early secular poetry, circulated in manuscript, combined eroticism, satire, and social critique. A secret marriage to Anne More in 1601 destroyed his diplomatic prospects, plunging the couple into years of poverty and prolific childbearing. Under pressure from King James I, Donne took Anglican orders in 1615, eventually becoming Dean of St Paul’s Cathedral (1621–31). His later sermons and divine poems, such as *Devotions upon Emergent Occasions* (1624) and *Death’s Duel* (1631), grapple with mortality and salvation using the same analytical wit that characterized his love lyrics. Posthumous editors constructed a narrative of conversion from "Jack Donne" the rake to "Dr. Donne" the divine, though modern scholars emphasize the continuity of his intellect across both phases.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Donne was born into a prominent recusant network: his mother was a great-niece of Thomas More, and his uncle Jasper Heywood was a Jesuit leader. After private education, he attended Oxford (1583) and Cambridge (1586) but could not graduate because as a Catholic he refused the Oath of Supremacy acknowledging the monarch as head of the church. He then studied law at Thavies Inn and Lincoln’s Inn (1591–92), preparing for a diplomatic career. The 1593 arrest and death of his brother Henry for harbouring a priest precipitated a crisis of faith. Donne subsequently travelled in Europe, fought in the Earl of Essex’s raids on Cádiz (1596) and the Azores (1597), and mastered Italian and Spanish. By 1598, he secured the prestigious post of secretary to Sir Thomas Egerton, placing him at the centre of legal and political power.

### Marriage, Poverty, and Patronage
In 1601, Donne secretly married Anne More, niece of his employer Egerton. The marriage, conducted without the consent of Anne’s father, George More (Lieutenant of the Tower), cost Donne his position and led to brief imprisonment in Fleet Prison. Released once the marriage was validated, Donne retreated to rural Surrey (Pyrford, then Mitcham), living in severe financial strain while Anne bore twelve children in sixteen years (five died young). He scraped a living as a lawyer and wrote anti-Catholic pamphlets for Thomas Morton. During this period, he composed *Biathanatos*, a treatise defending suicide, though he did not publish it. The 1617 death of Anne, shortly after a stillbirth, devastated him, inspiring the 17th Holy Sonnet. His fortunes turned when he gained the patronage of Sir Robert Drury, for whom he wrote the *Anniversaries* (1611–12), and the favour of King James I, who refused to restore him to secular service but pressed him toward ordination.

### Clerical Career and Major Prose
Ordained in 1615, Donne rapidly accumulated preferments: Royal Chaplain, Reader in Divinity at Lincoln’s Inn (1616–22), and chaplain to Viscount Doncaster on an embassy to Germany (1618–20). In 1621, he became Dean of St Paul’s, a powerful and lucrative position. A near-fatal illness in 1623 (likely typhus) produced *Devotions upon Emergent Occasions* (1624), a series of meditations on sickness and human interconnectedness famous for Meditation XVII ("No man is an island... for whom the bell tolls"). He served as vicar of St Dunstan-in-the-West (1624) and prolocutor to Charles I (1625). His preaching drew large crowds; 160 sermons survive, culminating in *Death’s Duel* (1631), delivered weeks before his death, which frames life as a descent toward the grave redeemed only by resurrection.

### Poetry: Secular and Divine
Donne’s verse circulated privately in manuscript during his life; the first collected *Poems* appeared in 1633. His early work—elegies, songs, satires, and erotic poems like "The Flea" and "Elegy XIX: To His Mistress Going to Bed"—uses startling, often scientific or legal imagery to dissect love and social hypocrisy. The *Anniversaries* (1611–12) mark a turn toward the cosmic and funereal, mourning Elizabeth Drury as a symbol of a decaying world. The *Holy Sonnets* (c. 1609–10, 1617), including "Batter my heart" and "Death Be Not Proud," apply the same violent, paradoxical logic to the soul’s struggle for grace. "A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning" exemplifies the metaphysical conceit, likening separated lovers to the fixed and moving legs of a drafting compass—distinct yet inseparably joined.

### Style and the Metaphysical Label
Donne’s style rejects Elizabethan smoothness for "strong lines" (Ben Jonson’s phrase): abrupt openings, jagged rhythms mimicking speech, tense syntax, and dense paradox. Samuel Johnson coined "metaphysical poets" in 1781, citing Donne’s tendency to "perplex the minds of the fair sex with nice speculations of philosophy" where "nature only should reign." The defining device is the *metaphysical conceit*—an extended metaphor yoking radically unlike things (lovers and a compass; a flea and a marriage bed)—to force intellectual surprise into emotional truth. While Neoclassical critics condemned this as metaphysical "abuse of metaphor," Romantics (Coleridge, Browning) and modernists (T. S. Eliot, F. R. Leavis) recovered him as a poet of unified sensibility, where thought and feeling are simultaneous.

### Legacy and Reception History
Donne’s posthumous reputation was shaped by editors and biographers who imposed a binary narrative: the libertine "Jack Donne" transformed into the holy "Dr. Donne." The 1633 *Poems* mixed erotic and divine verse indiscriminately, but post-1635 editions segregated them into "Songs and Sonnets" and "Divine Poems," cementing the conversion story. Izaak Walton’s 1640 biography reinforced this by paralleling Donne with St. Paul. Evelyn Simpson (1948) challenged the dichotomy, arguing for an "essential unity" beneath the contradictions. Donne is commemorated on 31 March in Anglican, Episcopal, and Lutheran calendars. His monument in St Paul’s—depicting him shrouded, standing on an urn—survived the Great Fire of 1666 and sparked a fashion for "resurrection monuments." Musical settings span from contemporaries (Dowland, Lawes) to Britten (*The Holy Sonnets of John Donne*, 1945) and John Adams (*Doctor Atomic*, 2005).

## Terms
- ****Recusant**** — A person who refused to attend Anglican services in early modern England, remaining loyal to Roman Catholicism despite legal penalties.
- ****Oath of Supremacy**** — An oath required for university degrees and public office, acknowledging the English monarch as Supreme Governor of the Church of England.
- ****Inns of Court / Inns of Chancery**** — Professional associations for barristers and law students in London (e.g., Lincoln’s Inn, Thavies Inn) providing legal education and dining rights.
- ****Metaphysical poets**** — A term coined by Samuel Johnson (1781) for 17th-century poets (Donne, Herbert, Marvell, etc.) characterized by intellectual conceits, argumentative structure, and colloquial rhythm.
- ****Metaphysical conceit**** — An extended, often elaborate metaphor linking two vastly dissimilar objects or ideas (e.g., lovers and a drafting compass) to reveal a hidden similarity.
- ****Unified sensibility**** — T. S. Eliot’s term for a poet’s ability to amalgamate disparate experiences—thought and feeling, the cerebral and the sensual—into a single poetic whole.
- ****Coterie poetry**** — Verse circulated privately in manuscript among a select circle of friends and patrons, rather than published for the general market.
- ****Popish recusants**** — Legal term defined by the 1593 Act for Catholics who refused Anglican worship; "Popish" derives from *Papa* (Pope), used pejoratively.
- ****Dean of St Paul’s**** — The senior cleric of St Paul’s Cathedral, London, with administrative authority over the cathedral chapter and significant income.
- ****Prolocutor**** — The chairman or spokesperson of the Convocation of Canterbury, the clerical assembly of the Church of England.

## Debates and open questions
- **Dating and chronology**: Most poems were unpublished in Donne’s lifetime; the 1633 posthumous edition provides the first texts. Scholars debate whether the "Jack Donne / Dr. Donne" binary reflects a genuine biographical rupture or an editorial construction, with Helen Gardner and Evelyn Simpson arguing for stylistic and thematic continuity across the corpus.
- **Religious affiliation**: The timing and sincerity of Donne’s move from Catholicism to Anglicanism remain contested—was it coerced by James I (who blocked his secular advancement), a genuine theological resolution, or pragmatic careerism? His anti-Catholic polemics (*Pseudo-Martyr*, *Ignatius His Conclave*) were written for patrons before ordination.
- **Authorship and canon**: Manuscript discoveries (e.g., the 2018 Melford Hall find) continue to alter the canon. Variants between manuscript circulation and print editions complicate establishing authoritative texts.
- **Gender and eroticism**: Feminist and queer readings interrogate the power dynamics in the erotic elegies (e.g., "The Flea," "To His Mistress Going to Bed"), asking whether the speaker’s intellectual dominance enacts or subverts patriarchal norms.
- **Sermons vs. poems**: The 160 surviving sermons reveal a public, oral, doctrinal voice distinct from the private, manuscript lyric. The relationship between these modes—whether they represent separate faculties or a single rhetorical project—remains a critical frontier.
