# Comedy

## Overview
Comedy is a dramatic genre intended to induce laughter and amusement. It originated in ancient Greece as a form of political satire and has evolved into a vast array of subgenres and performance styles across global cultures, serving as a tool for social critique, entertainment, and the exploration of human nature.

## Timeline
- **c. 425 BCE** — Aristophanes begins writing Old Comedy in Athens, developing it from earlier satyr plays.
- **c. 335 BCE** — Aristotle defines comedy in *Poetics* as an imitation of people "worse than the average" in a ridiculous way.
- **16th century** — Italian *commedia dell'arte* develops, influencing later forms like Punch and Judy.
- **1590s-1610s** — William Shakespeare writes his comedies, defined by happy endings and light-hearted tone.
- **1850s** — British music hall theatre becomes popular, featuring slapstick and comedy routines.
- **1880s-1930s** — American vaudeville emerges, featuring comedians like W.C. Fields and the Marx Brothers.
- **1917** — Marcel Duchamp's *Fountain* exemplifies surreal humour in art.
- **Post-WWII** — The British radio show *The Goon Show* popularizes surreal humour.
- **1960s-1970s** — Television sitcoms like *M*A*S*H* and *The Simpsons* gain global influence.
- **1990s-present** — Stand-up comedy and alternative comedy gain prominence; digital media expands access.

## Body

### Origins and Classical Theory
Comedy originated in ancient Greece, where political satire performed by comic poets influenced public opinion in Athenian democracy. The genre is fundamentally based on an *agon*, or conflict, often between youth and societal conventions. Aristotle defined comedy as an imitation of people "worse than the average," specifically in the "Ridiculous"—a deformity not causing pain. He taught that comedy was positive for society as it brings happiness, while Plato warned it could undermine rational self-control.

### Historical Development in the West
From Aristophanes' Old Comedy, the genre evolved through Roman forms, the *commedia dell'arte*, and Shakespearean comedy, which is characterized by happy endings, often marriages. The 19th century saw the rise of music hall and vaudeville, which honed the skills of performers like Charlie Chaplin. The 20th century brought new mediums; silent film amplified slapstick, while radio and television, with shows like *The Goon Show* and *Seinfeld*, brought comedy to mass audiences. Surreal humour, rooted in illogical and absurd juxtapositions, emerged as a significant avant-garde form.

### Global Traditions and Modern Forms
Comedy has distinct traditions worldwide. Indian classical drama included satire, with humour (*hāsyam*) defined as one of the nine essential emotional responses (*rasas*). Chinese *xiangsheng* (crosstalk) emphasizes rapid, witty dialogue, while Japanese *Rakugo* is a solo comic storytelling art. Modern comedy is categorized into numerous overlapping subgenres, including satire, parody, farce, romantic comedy, and black comedy, each using different sources of humour, from social taboo violation to bizarre situations.

## Terms
*   **Agon**: The central conflict or struggle in a comedy, often between opposing groups or ideas.
*   **Satire**: The use of humour, irony, or exaggeration to expose and criticize people's stupidity or vices, particularly in the context of contemporary politics.
*   **Parody**: An imitation of the style of a particular writer, artist, or genre with deliberate exaggeration for comic effect.
*   **Slapstick**: A style of humour involving exaggerated physical activity which exceeds the boundaries of normal physical comedy.
*   **Commedia dell'arte**: A form of theatre characterized by masked "types" and improvised dialogue based on sketches or scenarios.
*   **Ridiculous**: In Aristotle's theory, a species of the ugly that is a mistake or deformity not productive of pain or harm.
*   **Rasa**: In Indian aesthetics, the essential emotional response (like humour or pathos) evoked in an audience by a performance.
*   **Surreal Humour**: Humour based on deliberate violations of causal reasoning, creating illogical and absurd situations.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the precise origins of comedy, with Aristotle noting they were obscure because comedy was not treated seriously from its start. There is also a historical tension between views of comedy as a positive, civilizing force (Aristotle) versus a potentially destructive one (Plato). The definition and boundaries of comedy remain fluid; in the late 20th century, some scholars preferred the term "laughter" to avoid ambiguous genre labels like "grotesque" or "irony."
