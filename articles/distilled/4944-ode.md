# Ode

## Overview
An ode is a formal, elaborate lyric poem that praises or glorifies a subject, such as a person, event, or nature, with intellectual and emotional depth. Originating in Ancient Greece as a musical performance, it evolved into a major poetic form in English literature, characterized by its dignified language and structured stanzas.

## Timeline
- **Ancient Greece** — Odes originate as poetic pieces performed with musical accompaniment (aulos, lyre).
- **c. 5th century BC** — Pindar establishes the tripartite structure (strophe, antistrophe, epode) for victory odes.
- **1st century BC** — Horace writes odes in Latin, using regular stanzas for intimate themes.
- **1656** — Abraham Cowley publishes *Pindarique Odes*, introducing an irregular "Cowleyan" form to England.
- **1757** — Thomas Gray writes *The Progress of Poesy* and *The Bard*, notable Pindaric odes.
- **1807** — William Wordsworth's *Ode on Intimations of Immortality* revives the Cowleyan/Pindaric form.
- **1819** — John Keats composes his "Five Great Odes," including *Ode to a Nightingale* and *Ode on a Grecian Urn*.
- **19th century** — Percy Bysshe Shelley writes *Ode to the West Wind* in terza rima stanzas.
- **20th century** — W.H. Auden writes *Ode*, engaging with the theme of war.

## Body

### Origins and Classical Form
The ode began in Ancient Greece as a lyric poem performed with music. The classic Pindaric form, named after the poet Pindar, is tripartite: the **strophe** (theme introduced), **antistrophe** (contrasting perspective), and **epode** (summary). This structure reflected a chorus's movement on stage. Pindaric odes are often irregular in meter but use identical structures for the strophe and antistrophe.

### Horatian and Irregular Variations
The Roman poet Horace developed a more intimate, regular form. **Horatian odes** (or homostrophic odes) consist of stanzas (often quatrains) with the same rhyme scheme and meter, tackling personal themes like love. Later, the **irregular ode** (or Cowleyan ode) broke formal conventions. Abraham Cowley's 17th-century "Pindariques" misunderstood Pindar's meter, using iambic lines with irregular length and rhyme, a style widely imitated.

### Development in English
Early English odes include Edmund Spenser's *Epithalamium*. Cowley's irregular form dominated the 17th century. In the 18th century, with better understanding of Pindar, poets like Gray wrote true Pindaric odes. The Romantic era saw a revival: Wordsworth used Cowley's form, while Keats and Shelley perfected regular stanza patterns. Keats's 1819 odes are among the most celebrated. The form declined after the 19th century, with notable exceptions like Laurence Binyon's *For the Fallen* and W.H. Auden's *Ode*.

## Terms
- **Ode**: A formal, elaborate lyric poem praising a subject with dignity and sincerity.
- **Pindaric ode**: A tripartite ode (strophe, antistrophe, epode) following the style of Pindar.
- **Horatian ode**: An ode with regular stanzas (often quatrains) and a consistent rhyme scheme, after Horace.
- **Irregular ode**: An ode that abandons strict formal structures, often called a Cowleyan ode.
- **Strophe**: The first part of a Pindaric ode, introducing a theme.
- **Antistrophe**: The second part of a Pindaric ode, offering a contrasting perspective.
- **Epode**: The final part of a Pindaric ode, summarizing the theme.
- **Lyric poetry**: Poetry expressing personal emotions or thoughts, typically in a songlike form.
- **Ekphrasis**: A vivid literary description of a visual work of art, as in Keats's *Ode on a Grecian Urn*.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes a scholarly misunderstanding: Abraham Cowley's "Pindariques" were based on a misinterpretation of Pindar's metrical practice, yet they established a popular English form. John Milton later corrected this in his preface to *Samson Agonistes*, describing his chorus as "monostrophic" or "apolelymenon" (without regard to the tripartite structure), which he considered non-essential to the poem itself.
