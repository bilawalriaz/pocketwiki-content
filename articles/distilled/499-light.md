# Light

## Overview
Light is electromagnetic radiation, specifically the visible spectrum (approximately 400–700 nm) perceivable by the human eye, though in physics "light" encompasses all wavelengths. It exhibits wave-particle duality: propagating as waves (explaining interference and diffraction) and interacting as discrete, massless particles called photons. Its vacuum speed, exactly 299,792,458 m/s, is a fundamental constant. The primary natural source is the Sun; historically fire dominated artificial light until electric lighting replaced it. Understanding light—via geometrical optics, physical optics, and quantum optics—underpins technologies from lenses and fiber optics to lasers and quantum computing.

## Timeline
- **c. 5th century BC** — Empedocles proposes emission theory of vision; Euclid (c. 300 BC) later formalizes rectilinear propagation and reflection laws in *Optica*.
- **c. 55 BC** — Lucretius describes light as minute atoms traveling through space, anticipating particle theory.
- **1637** — Descartes publishes wave-based refraction theory (incorrectly assuming higher speed in denser media), founding modern physical optics.
- **1675** — Newton publishes *Hypothesis of Light*, advocating corpuscular (particle) theory; *Opticks* (1704) cements its dominance for a century.
- **1678/1690** — Huygens develops mathematical wave theory in *Treatise on Light*, proposing luminiferous aether and transverse waves.
- **1801–1802** — Young demonstrates interference, establishing wave nature; proposes wavelength determines color and three-receptor color vision.
- **1816–1818** — Fresnel and Arago confirm transverse wave nature via polarization and Poisson's spot, refuting longitudinal-only models.
- **1845** — Faraday discovers Faraday rotation, linking light to electromagnetism; speculates light is electromagnetic vibration (1846–47).
- **1862/1873** — Maxwell derives electromagnetic wave speed matching light speed, identifying light as EM radiation; Hertz confirms experimentally (1887).
- **1850** — Foucault measures light speed in water/air, confirming wave prediction (slower in denser media) and ending classical particle theory.
- **1900** — Planck introduces energy quanta to explain black-body radiation.
- **1905** — Einstein applies light quanta to photoelectric effect, asserting their physical reality.
- **1923** — Compton scattering confirms particle-like momentum transfer for X-rays.
- **1926** — Lewis coins "photon"; Dirac quantizes EM field (1927), leading to quantum electrodynamics (QED) by 1940s.
- **1950s–60s** — Glauber, Sudarshan, Mandel develop quantum optics (coherent states, photon statistics).
- **1977** — Kimble et al. demonstrate single-atom single-photon emission.

## Body

### Electromagnetic spectrum and visible light
Electromagnetic radiation (EMR) is classified by wavelength: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma ray. Visible light (roughly 400–700 nm, though definitions vary 380–800 nm) consists of photons energetic enough to induce electronic excitation in retinal molecules, triggering vision. Infrared photons lack this energy; they are sensed by some animals (e.g., snakes) via thermal imaging (molecular vibration heating). Ultraviolet is blocked by the cornea (<360 nm) and lens (<400 nm) and damages retinal cells, though lens-less animals (insects, shrimp) detect it via quantum absorption. Under ideal conditions, humans can perceive up to 1,050 nm (infrared) and down to ~310 nm (ultraviolet). Light spectrum also drives plant development (photomorphogenesis).

### Speed of light
The vacuum speed of light (*c*) is defined as exactly 299,792,458 m/s; the meter is now defined from this constant. All EMR travels at *c* in vacuum. Historical measurements: Rømer (1676, Jupiter's moon Io) inferred ~22 min for Earth-orbit diameter; Fizeau (1849, toothed wheel) found 313,000,000 m/s; Foucault (1862, rotating mirrors) found 298,000,000 m/s and crucially showed light slows in water; Michelson (1926, Mt. Wilson to Mt. San Antonio) reached 299,796,000 m/s. In media, speed reduces (e.g., ~¾*c* in water). Experiments with Bose-Einstein condensates (rubidium) "stop" light by storing it in atomic excitations, later re-emitting it—not halting photons in flight.

### Optics
Optics studies light-matter interaction. **Geometrical optics** (ray approximation) applies when wavelength ≪ object size; governs lenses, mirrors, cameras, eyes, fiber optics. **Physical optics** adds wave properties (diffraction, interference). **Quantum optics** treats individual photon-matter interactions.

**Surface scattering**: Transparent objects transmit light; opaque objects reflect/absorb. Most surfaces scatter (glossiness) due to roughness (surface scattering) or internal refractive-index variations (internal scattering). Translucent objects transmit but scatter specific wavelengths internally.

**Refraction**: Bending at a boundary between transparent media, described by Snell's Law: *n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂*, where *n* is refractive index (*n*=1 in vacuum, >1 in matter). Frequency stays constant; wavelength changes, causing direction change if incident obliquely. Lenses exploit this to magnify (spectacles, microscopes, telescopes).

### Light sources
Sources are thermal or non-thermal. **Thermal (black-body)**: Spectrum depends on temperature. Sun (~6,000 K chromosphere) peaks in visible; ~44% of ground-reaching radiation is visible. Incandescent bulbs emit ~10% visible, rest infrared. Flames emit mostly infrared; glowing particles yield visible fraction. As temperature rises, peak shifts: red → white → blue-white (Wien's displacement law). Blue-white thermal emission is rare on Earth (stars); blue in gas flames/welding is molecular (CH radicals ~425 nm), not thermal.

**Atomic/molecular emission**: Atoms emit/absorb at characteristic energies (emission lines). Spontaneous: LEDs, gas discharge lamps (neon, mercury-vapor), flames (sodium yellow). Stimulated: lasers, masers.

**High-energy particle processes**: Decelerating free charges (cyclotron, synchrotron, bremsstrahlung radiation); Cherenkov radiation (particles exceeding light speed in medium).

**Luminescence**: Chemoluminescence (chemical reaction); bioluminescence (living organisms, e.g., fireflies, plankton). Fluorescence (immediate re-emission after energetic excitation); phosphorescence (delayed re-emission); cathodoluminescence (subatomic particle excitation, used in CRT displays). Others: electroluminescence, scintillation, sonoluminescence, triboluminescence.

**Very high energy**: Particle-antiparticle annihilation; radioactive decay (gamma rays).

### Measurement
**Radiometry** measures total optical power (all wavelengths). **Photometry** weights power by standardized human brightness perception (luminous efficacy). Cone cells (three types) peak sensitivity at ~555 nm; equal radiometric intensity at different wavelengths appears differently bright. Photometric units (lumen, lux, candela) quantify perceived brightness for lighting design. Photocells/CCDs respond to IR/UV without costly filters, diverging from human perception.

### Light pressure
Light exerts pressure via photon momentum transfer: *P = Power / c*. Effect is negligible macroscopically (1 mW laser ≈ 3.3 pN; lifting a penny requires ~30 billion such pointers). Significant at nanoscale (NEMS: driving switches, actuators). At astronomical scales, spins up asteroids (YORP effect) and enables solar sail propulsion. **Crookes radiometer** rotation is *not* light pressure (it's thermal transpiration in partial vacuum); **Nichols radiometer** confirms light pressure via tiny torque. Einstein (1909) predicted "radiation friction": moving objects reflect more photons on the leading face, creating a velocity-dependent drag. Momentum is usually parallel to propagation, but transverse in evanescent waves.

### Historical theories
**Classical Greece/India**: Empedocles (emission vision), Euclid (rectilinear rays, reflection), Lucretius (atomic particles). Indian Samkhya/Vaisheshika: light as subtle element (*tanmatra*); Buddhists (Dignāga, Dharmakirti): light as momentary energy atoms. Ptolemy (refraction).

**Descartes (1637)**: Light as mechanical wave in plenum; correctly used wave analogy for refraction but wrongly assumed faster speed in denser media. Foundation of modern physical optics.

**Particle theory (Newton, 1675/1704)**: Corpuscles emitted in straight lines. Explained reflection; explained refraction by *incorrectly* assuming acceleration in denser media (gravitational pull). Explained diffraction via corpuscle-induced aether waves. Polarization qualitatively explained by Newton; Malus (1810) and Biot (1812) mathematized particle polarization. Laplace predicted black holes from corpuscular gravity. Dominated 18th century.

**Wave theory (Hooke, Huygens, Euler, Young, Fresnel)**: Hooke (1665) pulse/water analogy; Huygens (1678/1690) mathematical waves in luminiferous aether, slowing in denser media. Young (1801–02) interference experiments, wavelength-color link, three-receptor vision. Fresnel/Arago (1816–18): transverse waves explain polarization; Poisson's spot (Arago experiment) confirms wave diffraction. Weakness: required aether, later disproven by Michelson-Morley (1887). Foucault (1850) measured slower speed in water, confirming wave theory and ending classical particle theory.

**Electromagnetic theory (Faraday, Maxwell, Hertz)**: Faraday (1845) Faraday rotation (magneto-optic effect); speculated light as EM vibration (1846–47). Maxwell (1862/1873) derived EM wave speed = *c*, unifying light and electromagnetism (Maxwell's equations). Hertz (1887) generated/detected radio waves, confirming reflection, refraction, diffraction, interference—enabling radio, radar, TV, wireless.

**Quantum theory (Planck, Einstein, Compton, Lewis, Dirac, QED)**: Planck (1900) energy quanta for black-body. Einstein (1905) photoelectric effect → light quanta real. Compton (1923) X-ray scattering → particle momentum. Lewis (1926) "photon". Dirac (1927) quantized EM field; Jordan/Fock → quantum field theory (second quantization). 1940s: QED (Schwinger, Feynman, Dyson, Tomonaga). Wave-particle duality: neither classical particle nor wave; low-frequency EMR behaves more wave-like, high-frequency more particle-like; visible light shows both. Bose-Einstein statistics (1924–25): photons are massless spin-1 bosons.

**Quantum optics (1950s–present)**: Glauber, Sudarshan, Klauder, Mandel applied quantum theory to EM field → photon statistics, coherent states (laser vs. thermal vs. squeezed light). Kimble et al. (1977) single-atom single-photon emission. Squeezed states discovered. Ultrashort pulses (Q-switching, mode-locking) → ultrafast spectroscopy, optical tweezers (levitating atoms/cells), Doppler/Sisyphus cooling → Bose-Einstein condensation. Quantum entanglement, teleportation, logic gates → quantum information theory.

### Use for light on Earth
Sunlight powers photosynthesis (green plants → sugars/starches → virtually all biological energy). Some animals generate light via bioluminescence: fireflies (mate location), vampire squid (counter-illumination camouflage).

## Terms
- ****Photon**** — Massless elementary particle (quantum) of light, carrying energy proportional to frequency (*E = hν*) and momentum; spin-1 boson.
- ****Wave-particle duality**** — Light exhibits both wave properties (interference, diffraction) and particle properties (discrete energy/momentum transfer); neither classical model fully describes it.
- ****Refractive index (*n*)**** — Ratio of vacuum light speed to speed in a medium (*n = c/v*); determines refraction angle via Snell's Law.
- ****Black-body radiation**** — Thermal electromagnetic spectrum emitted by an ideal absorber; peak wavelength shifts inversely with temperature (Wien's law).
- ****Photometry**** — Measurement of light weighted by human spectral sensitivity (peak ~555 nm); units: lumen, lux, candela.
- ****Radiometry**** — Measurement of absolute optical power across all wavelengths; units: watt, W/m².
- ****Luminiferous aether**** — Hypothetical medium for light wave propagation (Huygens, 1678); disproven by Michelson-Morley experiment (1887).
- ****Faraday rotation**** — Rotation of linear polarization plane when light travels parallel to a magnetic field in a dielectric; first evidence linking light and electromagnetism (1845).
- ****Quantum electrodynamics (QED)**** — Relativistic quantum field theory describing light-matter interaction; developed 1940s (Feynman, Schwinger, Tomonaga, Dyson).
- ****Squeezed light**** — Non-classical quantum state with reduced uncertainty in one quadrature (amplitude or phase) below vacuum noise, at expense of increased uncertainty in the other.

## Debates and open questions
- **Nature of light**: Historical oscillation between particle (Newton) and wave (Huygens/Young/Fresnel) theories resolved into quantum duality, but interpretational debates persist (e.g., wavefunction realism vs. instrumentalism).
- **Aether vs. relativity**: Wave theory required a medium; Michelson-Morley null result led to special relativity (1905), eliminating aether but reframing spacetime as the "medium."
- **Quantum measurement**: Quantum optics reveals light cannot be fully described by classical fields; photon detection involves irreducible probabilistic collapse (degree of coherence, photon statistics).
- **Gravity and light**: Laplace (1796) used particle theory to predict light-trapping bodies (black holes); general relativity (1915) re-derived this via spacetime curvature, confirmed by Eddington (1919).
- **Ultrafast/quantum control**: Current frontiers—attosecond pulses, quantum entanglement distribution, optical tweezers for macroscopic superpositions—test quantum-classical boundary and enable quantum technologies.
