# Bing Crosby

## Overview
Harry Lillis "Bing" Crosby Jr. (1903–1977) was an American singer and actor who became one of the first multimedia stars and the most popular musical artist of the 20th century. He leveraged the advent of the electrical microphone to develop an intimate, conversational "crooning" style that replaced the theatrical belting of the vaudeville era, influencing generations of singers from Frank Sinatra to Elvis Presley. Beyond entertainment, Crosby was a pivotal technological entrepreneur: his insistence on pre-recording radio shows drove the U.S. adoption of magnetic tape recording (via Ampex), pioneered editing techniques like the laugh track, and funded early videotape development. He dominated record sales, radio ratings, and box office receipts from the 1930s through the 1950s, winning an Academy Award for *Going My Way* (1944) and selling an estimated 300 million to 1 billion records worldwide, with "White Christmas" remaining the best-selling single of all time. He is one of only 33 people to have three stars on the Hollywood Walk of Fame, in the categories of motion pictures, radio, and audio recording.

## Body

### Early Life and Musical Foundations
Born in Tacoma and raised in Spokane, Washington, Crosby was the fourth of seven children in a musical household; his father played records on an Edison phonograph and his mother sang. He attended Gonzaga High School and University, playing baseball but leaving without a degree (honorary doctorate awarded 1937). A pivotal 1917 summer job at Spokane's Auditorium exposed him to Al Jolson, whose "electric" ad-libbing style left a lasting impression. His nickname "Bing" originated circa 1910 from a neighbor, Valentine Hobart, referencing a local newspaper comic feature, *The Bingville Bugle*; the childhood moniker "Bingo from Bingville" was later shortened.

### The Rhythm Boys and the Microphone Revolution
Crosby’s professional start came in 1923 with the Musicaladers in Spokane. In 1925, he and Al Rinker headed to Los Angeles, aided by Rinker’s sister, singer Mildred Bailey. Hired by Paul Whiteman in 1926, they debuted at Chicago’s Tivoli Theatre. The addition of pianist Harry Barris created The Rhythm Boys, allowing them to be heard in large New York theaters without microphones. Touring with Whiteman, Crosby recorded alongside jazz legends Bix Beiderbecke, Jack Teagarden, and the Dorsey brothers, absorbing jazz phrasing and improvisation. His 1928 rendition of "Ol' Man River" became his first number-one hit. Dissatisfied with Whiteman, the trio joined the Gus Arnheim Orchestra at the Ambassador Hotel’s Coconut Grove, where Crosby’s solos began to overshadow the group. A solo film contract with Mack Sennett in 1931 dissolved the act.

### Solo Stardom: Radio, Records, and Film
Crosby’s solo radio debut on September 2, 1931, made him an instant national sensation. By year-end, he signed with Brunswick Records and CBS Radio; ten of 1931’s top 50 songs featured him. He replaced the "belting" style of Jolson with a conversational intimacy enabled by the microphone—critic Henry Pleasants termed it "singing in American." This defined the *crooner* epithet. In 1934, he moved to Jack Kapp’s new Decca Records. Kapp persuaded Crosby to drop jazz mannerisms for a clear, versatile style, diversifying his repertoire into country, Hawaiian, Irish, and rhythm & blues, yielding number-one hits across genres. Crosby’s support for Kapp’s 35-cent singles (down from $1) and royalty-based contracts is credited with saving the record industry during the Depression. From 1936 to 1946, he hosted NBC’s *Kraft Music Hall*, cementing his status as the nation’s top entertainer. His film career launched with *The Big Broadcast* (1932), leading to 55 top-billed features, including the *Road to...* series with Bob Hope and Dorothy Lamour (1940–1962).

### World War II and "White Christmas"
During WWII, Crosby toured the European Theater, broadcast propaganda to German forces (earning the nickname "Der Bingle"), and topped a GI poll as the individual who did the most for morale, ahead of Roosevelt and Eisenhower. His recording of Irving Berlin’s "White Christmas," debuted on radio Christmas 1941, became a cultural phenomenon. It hit No. 1 in October 1942 for 11 weeks, re-charted 16 times, and has sold over 50 million copies worldwide—the best-selling single ever. The original 1942 master wore out from pressing demand, forcing a 1947 re-recording with the same musicians. Crosby dismissed his role: "a jackdaw with a cleft palate could have sung it successfully."

### Peak Film Years and Television Transition
Crosby was the No. 1 box-office draw for five straight years (1944–1948). In 1946, he starred in three of the year’s five highest-grossing films: *The Bells of St. Mary's*, *Blue Skies*, and *Road to Utopia*. He won the Best Actor Oscar for *Going My Way* (1944) and was nominated for the sequel *The Bells of St. Mary's* (1945), becoming the first of six actors to be nominated twice for playing the same character (Father O'Malley). He received a third nomination for *The Country Girl* (1954). He narrated *The Legend of Sleepy Hollow* for Disney (1949). As film dominance waned, he moved to television, producing *The Fireside Theater* (1950) as filmed "telefilms" rather than live broadcasts. He became a staple variety show guest and hosted ABC’s *The Hollywood Palace*. His final TV appearance, the 1977 Christmas special *Merrie Olde Christmas*, featured a duet with David Bowie ("Peace on Earth/Little Drummer Boy") that became a holiday standard and his final hit single.

### Singing Style and Vocal Characteristics
Crosby was a self-defined "phraser," prioritizing lyric delivery over vocal acrobatics. His style fused jazz rhythm and bent notes (learned from Bix Beiderbecke and Louis Armstrong) with a conversational baritone. Critic Henry Pleasants praised his 1930s octave (B-flat to B-flat) as "one of the loveliest," though it dropped to a bass range (G to G, eventually low E-flat) by the 1950s. Jack Kapp steered him from jazz affectations toward a clear, genre-spanning technique. Tommy Dorsey advised Frank Sinatra: "there's only one singer you ought to listen to and his name is Crosby. All that matters to him is the words."

### Entrepreneurship and Recording Technology
Frustrated by NBC’s refusal to allow pre-recorded radio shows (requiring live East/West Coast broadcasts), Crosby sat out seven months in 1945–46. He then moved to the fledgling ABC network, which agreed to air taped shows. In June 1947, Crosby Enterprises witnessed a demonstration of the German Magnetophon tape recorder brought back by Army Signal Corps engineer Jack Mullin. Crosby hired Mullin and invested $50,000 in Ampex to manufacture American versions. Magnetic tape allowed editing (retakes, splicing, time-shifting), transforming radio production into a process resembling film post-production. This innovation birthed the laugh track (salvaging audience laughter from a censored Bob Burns joke) and enabled Crosby to give an early Ampex Model 300 to Les Paul, catalyzing multitrack recording. Crosby Enterprises demonstrated the first videotape recording on November 11, 1951 (blurred images on modified Ampex 200 at 360 ips), though *The Fireside Theater* had to use film. Crosby sold his tape operation to 3M in 1956 and co-founded United Western Recorders studio with Frank Sinatra.

### Business Ventures and Sports
Crosby became one of show business’s wealthiest figures through diversified investments: real estate, mines, oil, cattle, music publishing, Minute Maid Orange Juice (principal stockholder), television stations (KCOP-TV Los Angeles, KPTV Portland, Spokane CBS affiliate), and the Pittsburgh Pirates (25% owner, 1946–1977; World Series wins 1960, 1971). He co-founded the Del Mar Thoroughbred Club (1937), raced horses via Binglin Stable (California and Argentina), and hosted the "Crosby Clambake" pro-am golf tournament (now AT&T Pebble Beach Pro-Am). An accomplished golfer (two handicap, 1981 U.S. Amateur winner Nathaniel Crosby is his son), he was inducted into the World Golf Hall of Fame (1978).

### Personal Life and Family
Crosby married Dixie Lee in 1930; they had four sons (Gary, twins Dennis and Phillip, Lindsay) before her death from ovarian cancer in 1952. Their marriage was strained by her alcoholism; Crosby nearly divorced her in 1941 and had a confirmed affair with actress Joan Caulfield (1945–late 1940s), which ended after Cardinal Francis Spellman and his mother intervened. He married actress Kathryn Grant in 1957; they had three children (Harry III, Mary Frances, Nathaniel). After Crosby’s death, eldest son Gary published *Going My Own Way* (1983), alleging physical and psychological abuse. Siblings Phillip, Lindsay, and Dennis largely disputed the severity, characterizing their father as a strict disciplinarian typical of the era. Biographer Gary Giddins questions the memoir’s reliability. Lindsay (1989) and Dennis (1991) died by suicide; Gary (1995) of lung cancer; Phillip (2004) of a heart attack. Legal disputes between the two families over community property and publicity rights persisted into the 2010s.

### Health and Death
Crosby survived a life-threatening fungal lung infection in January 1974, returning to active touring. On March 20, 1977, he ruptured a disc falling offstage during a CBS taping. His final U.S. concert was August 16, 1977 (Concord, CA); he sang without amplification during a power failure. After a UK tour and final album *Seasons*, he flew to Spain on October 13. On October 14, after playing 18 holes at La Moraleja Golf Course near Madrid (winning by one stroke), he collapsed 20 yards from the clubhouse, dying instantly of a heart attack at age 74. His last words: "That was a great game of golf, fellas. Let's go have a Coca-Cola." He was buried at Holy Cross Cemetery, Culver City, California.

## Terms
- ****Crooner**** — A singer of popular ballads using an intimate, conversational style enabled by the electrical microphone, distinct from the projecting "belting" required in pre-microphone vaudeville.
- ****Phrasing**** — The art of shaping a song's lyric and melody through timing, breath, and emphasis to convey meaning; Crosby self-identified as a "phraser" who prioritized words over vocal display.
- ****Magnetophon**** — A German-engineered reel-to-reel tape recorder (developed by BASF/AEG, 1930s) using 6.5mm ferric oxide tape, offering near-professional broadcast fidelity; brought to the U.S. by Jack Mullin in 1947.
- ****Ampex**** — California electronics company (founded by Alexander M. Poniatoff) backed by Crosby’s $50,000 investment to commercialize the Magnetophon; their Model 200A became the U.S. broadcast standard. |
- ****Laugh Track**** — Pre-recorded audience laughter inserted into broadcasts; accidentally invented on Crosby’s *Philco Radio Time* when writer Bill Morrow salvaged laughter from a censored Bob Burns routine for a later show.
- ****Telefilm**** — A television program recorded on film (rather than broadcast live) for syndication to individual stations; used for Crosby’s *The Fireside Theater* (1950) before videotape was viable.
- ****Kraft Music Hall**** — NBC radio variety show (1936–1946) hosted by Crosby; cemented his national dominance and featured his theme song, "Where the Blue of the Night."
- ****Road to... Films**** — Series of seven musical comedies (1940–1962) starring Crosby, Bob Hope, and Dorothy Lamour (replaced by Joan Collins in the final film), defining a comic "on-and-off" duo dynamic.
- ****Der Bingle**** — Nickname coined in 1944 by London-based journalist Bob Musel for Crosby’s German-language propaganda broadcasts; adopted by English-speaking fans.
- ****Blind Trust**** — Estate mechanism in Crosby’s will delaying sons' inheritance until age 65 to "keep them out of trouble"; they received monthly income from a separate 1952 trust established by Dixie Lee.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Record Sales Totals:** Sources vary wildly—Guinness cited 400 million (1973) and 500 million (1977); other estimates range from 300 million to 1 billion. No official auditing body validates a definitive figure.
*   **Gary Crosby’s Abuse Allegations:** Gary’s 1983 memoir *Going My Own Way* depicts Crosby as cruel and physically abusive. Siblings Phillip, Lindsay, and Dennis publicly disputed the severity, describing strict but typical period discipline. Biographer Gary Giddins considers the memoir unreliable on abuse claims.
*   **Mafia Connections:** A 1999 *New York Post* article claimed recently released FBI files revealed Mob ties. However, the FBI files (public since 1992) show only one reference to organized crime (a 1959 memo regarding an invitation to Moe Dalitz at a ranch Crosby had already sold) and numerous threats *against* Crosby.
*   **Right of Publicity as Community Property:** Post-1999 litigation between Dixie Lee’s trust and Crosby’s widow Kathryn centered on whether Crosby’s right of publicity constituted community property under California law. The Court of Appeals ruled the 1999 settlement barred the claim, leaving the underlying property question unresolved.
