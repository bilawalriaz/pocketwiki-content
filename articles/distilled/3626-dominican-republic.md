# Dominican Republic

## Overview
The Dominican Republic is a Caribbean nation occupying the eastern five-eighths of the island of Hispaniola. It shares the island with Haiti, making it one of only two Caribbean islands divided between two sovereign states. The country has a complex history marked by indigenous settlement, European colonization, periods of independence and foreign occupation, and a long dictatorship, before transitioning to a representative democracy. It is the largest economy in the Caribbean and a major tourist destination.

## Timeline
- **c. 600 CE** — Taíno people, ancestors of the indigenous population, reach Hispaniola.
- **December 5, 1492** — Christopher Columbus lands on Hispaniola, claiming it for Castile.
- **1496** — Bartholomew Columbus founds Santo Domingo, the first permanent European settlement in the Americas.
- **1697** — Treaty of Ryswick cedes the western third of Hispaniola to France.
- **January 1, 1804** — Haiti declares independence from France, controlling the western part of the island.
- **November 30, 1821** — Santo Domingo declares independence from Spain (Ephemeral Independence).
- **February 27, 1844** — Dominican independence from Haiti is declared, beginning the First Republic.
- **1861–1865** — Spain re-annexes the Dominican Republic, followed by the Dominican Restoration War.
- **1916–1924** — The United States occupies the Dominican Republic.
- **1930–1961** — Rafael Trujillo rules as dictator.
- **May 30, 1961** — Trujillo is assassinated.
- **April–September 1965** — Dominican Civil War leads to a second U.S. occupation.
- **1996–present** — The country moves towards stable representative democracy.

## Body

### Early History and Colonization
The island of Hispaniola was first settled around 6,000 years ago. By around 600 CE, the Arawakan-speaking Taíno people had arrived, dividing the island into five chiefdoms. Christopher Columbus claimed the island for Castile in 1492, and his brother founded Santo Domingo in 1496, creating the first permanent European settlement in the Americas. The Taíno population declined drastically due to European diseases and conflict, leading to the importation of African slaves. By 1697, Spain recognized French control over the western third of the island.

### Independence and Political Instability
In 1821, Santo Domingo declared independence from Spain, but was annexed by Haiti in 1822. After 22 years of Haitian rule, Dominican nationalists led by Juan Pablo Duarte and others declared independence in 1844, winning the Dominican War of Independence. The following decades were marked by civil wars, factionalism, and failed Haitian invasions. In 1861, the country was re-annexed by Spain, but a subsequent Restoration War (1863–1865) re-established independence. The late 19th century saw continued political strife and economic debt, with a brief period of stability under dictator Ulises Heureaux.

### 20th Century: Occupation and Dictatorship
Political instability and foreign debt led to a U.S. occupation from 1916 to 1924, which built infrastructure and established a professional National Guard. In 1930, Rafael Trujillo, a National Guard graduate, seized power. His 31-year dictatorship was characterized by economic growth, infrastructure development, and absolute repression, including the 1937 massacre of Haitians in the border region. Trujillo was assassinated in 1961. A brief democratic period under Juan Bosch was ended by a 1963 coup, leading to a civil war and a second U.S. occupation in 1965. Joaquín Balaguer, a former Trujillo associate, then ruled for most of the next 30 years.

### Modern Era and Economy
Since 1996, the Dominican Republic has held regular elections, transitioning to a stable representative democracy. The economy has shifted from agriculture to a diversified mix of services, manufacturing, and tourism. It is the most visited destination in the Caribbean and has experienced rapid GDP growth, though it faces challenges like income inequality, corruption, and a chronic energy shortage. The country's population is predominantly mixed-race, with significant Haitian immigration being a major social and political issue.

## Terms
*   **Taíno:** The indigenous Arawakan-speaking people who inhabited Hispaniola before European contact.
*   **Caudillo:** A military or political leader with great power, especially in a Latin American country.
*   **Merengue:** A lively, fast-paced style of music and dance originating in the Dominican Republic.
*   **Bachata:** A genre of music and dance that originated in the Dominican countryside, often with romantic themes.
*   **La Trinitaria:** The secret society founded in 1838 by Juan Pablo Duarte to fight for Dominican independence from Haiti.
*   **Distrito Nacional (D.N.):** The national district of Santo Domingo, the capital city, which functions as a province.
*   **Jus sanguinis:** A principle of nationality law in which citizenship is determined by parental citizenship, not place of birth.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact population of Hispaniola at the time of European contact in 1492 is debated, with estimates ranging from tens of thousands to 2 million.
*   The integration of the large Haitian immigrant population and the status of Dominicans of Haitian descent remain major social and political issues.
*   The causes and legacy of the 1937 massacre of Haitians by the Trujillo regime continue to be a point of historical and diplomatic sensitivity.
