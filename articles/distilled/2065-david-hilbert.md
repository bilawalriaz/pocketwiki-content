# David Hilbert

## Overview
David Hilbert (1862–1943) was a German mathematician who made foundational contributions across numerous fields, including invariant theory, geometry, and the foundations of mathematics. He is best known for his 1900 list of 23 unsolved problems, which shaped 20th-century research, and for his formalist program, which sought to establish mathematics on a complete, consistent axiomatic foundation—a goal later shown to be impossible by Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems.

- **1909** — Began work on integral equations, leading to the concept of Hilbert space.
- **1915** — Published an axiomatic derivation of the field equations of general relativity.
- **1920s** — Proposed Hilbert's program for the formalist foundations of mathematics.
- **1930** — Delivered retirement address with the epitaph "Wir müssen wissen. Wir werden wissen."
- **1931** — Gödel's incompleteness theorems demonstrated the impossibility of Hilbert's program.
- **1943** — Died in Göttingen; funeral attended by fewer than a dozen people.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Hilbert was born in 1862 to a Prussian judge and a mother interested in philosophy and astronomy. After an unhappy period at the Friedrichskolleg Gymnasium, he transferred to the more science-oriented Wilhelm Gymnasium. He enrolled at the University of Königsberg in 1880, where he formed a lifelong friendship with Hermann Minkowski. In 1884, Adolf Hurwitz joined the university, and the three began an intense scientific exchange. Hilbert received his doctorate in 1885 and remained at Königsberg as a Privatdozent until 1895, when he moved to the University of Göttingen, which he helped turn into the world's leading mathematical center.

### Mathematical Contributions
Hilbert's first major work solved "Gordan's Problem" with an abstract existence proof for the finiteness of generators for invariants, a method initially criticized for its non-constructive nature. He later unified algebraic number theory in his 1897 treatise *Zahlbericht*. In geometry, his 1899 *Grundlagen der Geometrie* replaced Euclid's axioms with a modern axiomatic system, emphasizing that undefined concepts like "point" and "line" could be any objects satisfying the specified relationships. He also constructed the Hilbert curve, a space-filling curve, in response to Giuseppe Peano's earlier example.

### The 23 Problems and Formalism
In 1900, Hilbert presented 23 unsolved problems, including the Riemann hypothesis and the consistency of arithmetic, which guided mathematical research for the century. His approach led to the formalist school, which views mathematics as symbol manipulation according to formal rules. Hilbert's program aimed to prove mathematics complete and consistent using finitary methods. This was undermined in 1931 by Gödel's incompleteness theorems, which showed that any consistent formal system powerful enough for arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency.

### Physics and Functional Analysis
Around 1909, Hilbert began studying integral equations, introducing infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, which became fundamental to functional analysis and quantum mechanics. In 1915, he independently derived the field equations of general relativity, though he fully credited Einstein as the originator. His work provided a rigorous mathematical framework for physics, later compiled in the influential *Methods of Mathematical Physics* with Richard Courant.

### Later Years and Legacy
Hilbert developed pernicious anemia around 1925, which severely limited his work. He witnessed the Nazi purge of Göttingen's faculty in 1933, including colleagues like Emmy Noether and Hermann Weyl. His famous epitaph, "Wir müssen wissen. Wir werden wissen" ("We must know. We will know"), reflects his lifelong optimism about mathematical knowledge, despite Gödel's results.

## Terms
- **Invariant theory**: A branch of algebra studying properties unchanged under transformations.
- **Axiomatic method**: A system where all statements are derived from a set of assumed axioms.
- **Hilbert space**: An infinite-dimensional generalization of Euclidean space, essential for quantum mechanics.
- **Formalism**: A philosophy of mathematics that treats mathematics as manipulation of symbols according to formal rules.
- **Incompleteness theorem**: Gödel's result showing that any consistent formal system for arithmetic contains true statements unprovable within it.
- **Nullstellensatz**: Hilbert's theorem giving a criterion for when a set of polynomials has a common root.
- **Space-filling curve**: A continuous curve that passes through every point in a higher-dimensional space.
- **Law of excluded middle**: A logical principle stating that any proposition is either true or false.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Constructivism vs. Formalism**: Hilbert's non-constructive existence proofs were criticized by intuitionists like L.E.J. Brouwer, who rejected the law of excluded middle over infinite sets.
- **Hilbert's Program**: Gödel's theorems showed the program's original goals were unattainable, but it spurred the development of proof theory and recursion theory.
- **Einstein–Hilbert priority**: Both derived the field equations of general relativity nearly simultaneously in 1915; Hilbert acknowledged Einstein's priority, and no public dispute arose.
- **Solvability of Hilbert's problems**: Some problems remain open (e.g., Riemann hypothesis), while others are considered too vaguely stated for definitive resolution.
