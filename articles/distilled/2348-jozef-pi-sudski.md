# Józef Piłsudski

## Overview
Józef Piłsudski (1867–1935) was a Polish statesman and military leader instrumental in re-establishing Polish independence in 1918. As Chief of State (1918–1922) and later the de facto authoritarian leader (1926–1935), he shaped the Second Polish Republic's foreign policy and military.

## Timeline
- **5 December 1867** — Born in Zułów, Russian Empire (now Lithuania).
- **1887** — Arrested and exiled to Siberia for alleged plot against Tsar Alexander III.
- **1893** — Joined the Polish Socialist Party (PPS).
- **1914** — Formed the Polish Legions to fight alongside Austria-Hungary against Russia.
- **1917** — Imprisoned in Magdeburg by Germans after the "Oath Crisis."
- **11 November 1918** — Became Chief of State of newly independent Poland.
- **1920** — Led Polish forces to victory in the Battle of Warsaw during the Polish–Soviet War.
- **1923** — Retired from active politics after a government dominated by opponents took power.
- **12–14 May 1926** — Returned to power in the May Coup, establishing the Sanation government.
- **12 May 1935** — Died of liver cancer in Warsaw.

## Body

### Early Life and Revolutionary Activity
Born into a Polish noble family in the Russian-controlled Grand Duchy of Lithuania, Piłsudski was raised with suppressed Polish patriotic traditions. After brief medical studies, he was arrested in 1887 for an alleged plot against the Tsar and exiled to Siberia for five years. Upon return, he joined the Polish Socialist Party (PPS) in 1893, merging socialism with Polish nationalism. He edited the underground newspaper *Robotnik* and, after imprisonment, fled to Galicia. In the early 1900s, he formed paramilitary units for armed resistance against Russian rule, funding his cause through assassinations and robberies. During the 1905 Russian Revolution, he led strikes and uprisings in Congress Poland.

### World War I and the Polish Legions
Anticipating war, Piłsudski trained a military force under "sporting clubs." In 1914, he formed the Polish Legions within the Austro-Hungarian Army to fight Russia, predicting Poland's rebirth required Russia's defeat by the Central Powers, who would then fall to the Entente. In 1917, after the Russian Revolution, he refused loyalty to the German Kaiser, leading to imprisonment in Magdeburg. This act enhanced his reputation as uncompromised by collaboration. He was released in November 1918 as German authority collapsed.

### Chief of State and the Polish–Soviet War
On 11 November 1918, Piłsudski became Commander-in-Chief and formed a government for independent Poland. He organized a national army from veterans of partitioning powers and introduced socialist reforms like the eight-hour day. His major foreign policy initiative was the "Intermarium" federation, a modern Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth from the Baltic to the Black Sea. This vision led to conflict with Soviet Russia. In 1920, Piłsudski allied with Ukrainian leader Symon Petliura and captured Kiev. The Soviet counter-offensive threatened Warsaw, but Piłsudski's plan secured a decisive Polish victory at the Battle of Warsaw in August 1920, halting the Red Army. The war ended with the Treaty of Riga (1921), partitioning Belarus and Ukraine and ending his Intermarium project.

### The May Coup and Sanation Government
After the 1921 constitution limited presidential powers, Piłsudski retired in 1923, criticizing parliamentary corruption and inefficiency. He returned in the May Coup of 1926, overthrowing the elected government. Refusing the presidency, he became Minister of Military Affairs, ruling as a strongman through puppet presidents. His Sanation regime ("moral purification") curtailed parliamentary democracy, imprisoned opponents at Bereza Kartuska, and pursued "state-assimilation" for ethnic minorities, which initially improved conditions for Jews but deteriorated in the 1930s. He focused on military and foreign affairs, signing non-aggression pacts with the Soviet Union (1932) and Germany (1934) to buy time, while maintaining alliances with France and Romania.

### Death and Legacy
Piłsudski died in 1935, leaving a power vacuum. His cult of personality endured. He is celebrated as the father of modern Polish independence and savior of Europe from Bolshevism in 1920. His legacy is complex, admired for military genius and state-building but criticized for authoritarian methods and subsequent political instability.

## Terms
- **Chief of State (Naczelnik Państwa):** Head of state of the Second Polish Republic (1918–1922), held by Piłsudski.
- **Polish Legions:** Military units formed by Piłsudski in 1914 within the Austro-Hungarian Army for Polish independence.
- **Intermarium (Międzymorze):** Piłsudski's concept for a federation of Central and Eastern European states from the Baltic to the Black Sea.
- **Sanation (Sanacja):** The authoritarian faction and government led by Piłsudski after 1926, aimed at "moral purification."
- **Battle of Warsaw (1920):** Decisive Polish victory in the Polish–Soviet War, the "Miracle on the Vistula," halting Soviet advance.
- **May Coup (1926):** Piłsudski's military takeover on 12–14 May 1926.
- **Bereza Kartuska Prison:** Detention camp established in 1934 for imprisoning political opponents without trial.
- **State-assimilation:** Policy judging citizens by loyalty to the Polish state rather than ethnicity.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authoritarianism vs. Necessity:** Scholars debate if Piłsudski's post-1926 rule was necessary to stabilize democracy or damaged parliamentary traditions.
- **Military Strategy:** His Battle of Warsaw plan is praised as genius or criticized as lucky. Credit relative to other commanders and advisors is debated.
- **Foreign Policy Foresight:** Non-aggression pacts with Germany and the USSR are seen as pragmatic diplomacy or a miscalculation underestimating Hitler.
- **Legacy of the Coup:** The May Coup's long-term impact on Poland's political development and WWII preparedness remains analyzed.
