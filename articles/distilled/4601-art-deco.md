# Art Deco

## Overview
Art Deco is a visual arts, architecture, and design style that first appeared in Paris in the 1910s and flourished internationally from the 1920s to the early 1930s. It represents a synthesis of modernist aesthetics with luxury and craftsmanship, symbolizing the era's faith in social and technological progress before being supplanted by the functionalism of the International Style after World War II.

## Timeline
- **1901** — Société des artistes décorateurs (SAD) founded in France, elevating decorative artists' status.
- **1903** — Wiener Werkstätte founded, a key precursor in geometric design.
- **1910-1913** — Théâtre des Champs-Élysées built, the first landmark Art Deco building.
- **1912** — La Maison Cubiste exhibited at the Salon d'Automne, showcasing Cubist influence on interior design.
- **1925** — International Exhibition of Modern Decorative and Industrial Arts held in Paris, giving the style its name.
- **1929** — French Union of Modern Artists founded, representing the modernist faction within Art Deco.
- **1930s** — Streamline Moderne emerges as a sleeker, more aerodynamic variant of Art Deco.
- **1937** — Last major Parisian Art Deco buildings completed for the International Exposition.
- **1966** — Term "Art Déco" first appears in print for a major exhibition in Paris.
- **1968** — Bevis Hillier's book popularizes the term as a broad stylistic label.

## Body

### Origins and Influences
Art Deco emerged from a confluence of influences. Its geometric forms drew from the Vienna Secession and Cubism, while its vibrant colors were inspired by Fauvism and the Ballets Russes. The style also incorporated motifs from ancient and non-European civilizations, including Egypt, China, and Mesoamerica, fueled by archaeological discoveries. New technologies, particularly reinforced concrete and mass-produced plate glass, enabled its architectural forms. The 1925 Paris Exposition, which required all exhibits to be modern, was the defining event that crystallized and named the movement.

### The 1925 Exposition and Core Aesthetics
The 1925 International Exhibition of Modern Decorative and Industrial Arts was the style's zenith, attracting 16 million visitors. It showcased the work of traditionalist designers like Émile-Jacques Ruhlmann, who used rare materials like ebony and ivory for luxury objects. The exhibition's rules banning historical styles promoted a modern aesthetic characterized by symmetry, geometric forms, and fine craftsmanship. It also featured early modernist pavilions, such as Le Corbusier's, highlighting the internal tension between ornate luxury and functional simplicity that would define the style's later years.

### Evolution and Decline
By the late 1920s, a split emerged between traditionalists and modernists. The modernists, including Le Corbusier, advocated for mass production, inexpensive materials, and form following function. The Great Depression reduced the market for luxury goods, forcing even traditional firms to adapt. This led to the rise of Streamline Moderne in the 1930s, a sleeker variant with curved forms inspired by aerodynamics. After World War II, Art Deco was largely replaced by the International Style, though it experienced a revival in the 1960s and continues to influence contemporary design.

### Art Deco in Practice
The style permeated all visual arts. In architecture, it adorned skyscrapers like the Chrysler Building and ocean liners like the SS *Normandie*. Furniture designers like Ruhlmann created exquisite pieces, while modernists like Donald Deskey used new materials like aluminum and Bakelite. Graphic arts featured bold posters by designers like Cassandre. Jewelry by Cartier and Lalique used geometric cuts and colorful gemstones. Sculpture was integral to architecture, with works by Paul Manship (e.g., *Prometheus*) and Antoine Bourdelle. The style also defined fashion, with designers like Paul Poiret and Coco Chanel popularizing simpler, more practical silhouettes.

## Terms
- **Arts Décoratifs**: French for "decorative arts," the term from which "Art Deco" is derived.
- **Streamline Moderne**: A later, sleeker variant of Art Deco from the 1930s, featuring curved forms and smooth surfaces inspired by aerodynamics.
- **Wiener Werkstätte**: A Viennese association of craftsmen and designers (1903-1932) that influenced Art Deco's geometric forms.
- **Reinforced Concrete**: A key construction material that allowed Art Deco buildings greater freedom of form.
- **Bakelite**: An early form of plastic used in 1930s Art Deco furniture and product design.
- **Chryselephantine**: A style of small sculpture using materials like bronze, ivory, and gold leaf, popular in Art Deco salons.
- **Pochoir**: A stencil-based printing system that allowed for crisp lines and vivid colors in Art Deco textiles and graphics.
- **International Style**: The functional, unadorned architectural style that replaced Art Deco after World War II.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source highlights a central tension within Art Deco between **traditionalism and modernism**. Traditionalists, like furniture designer Paul Follot, defended ornament and luxury, arguing the "superfluous is indispensable." Modernists, led by Le Corbusier, attacked this as elitist and promoted affordable, mass-produced design for all. This debate over whether decorative arts should serve the wealthy few or the general public was a defining conflict of the era. Additionally, the term "Art Deco" itself is a retrospective label; during its heyday, the style was known by many names (e.g., Style Moderne, Zigzag Moderne) and was not recognized as a single, homogeneous movement.
