# Malay language

## Overview
Malay is an Austronesian macrolanguage native to Maritime Southeast Asia and the Malay Peninsula, spoken by roughly 80 million first-language speakers and nearly 300 million total speakers. It is pluricentric: standardized as Bahasa Melayu in Malaysia, Singapore, and Brunei, and as Bahasa Indonesia (Indonesian) in Indonesia and Timor-Leste. Historically, it spread as a trade lingua franca through the Srivijaya and Malacca Sultanates, absorbing heavy Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian, Tamil, and later European loanwords. Today it functions as a national or working language across seven countries, with a deep structural divergence between the Indonesian and Malaysian standards that reflects different colonial legacies (Dutch vs. British) and lexical preferences (Sanskrit vs. Arabic for neologisms).

## Timeline
- **c. 2000 BCE** — Proto-Malayo-Polynesian breaks up as Austronesian peoples expand southward from Taiwan into Maritime Southeast Asia.
- **c. 1000 BCE** — Proto-Malayic spoken in western Borneo, the likely Malayic homeland.
- **1 May 683 CE** — Kedukan Bukit inscription (Sumatra), earliest known Old Malay stone inscription, written in Pallava script.
- **7th–11th c.** — Srivijaya empire spreads Old Malay as a trade lingua franca across the Malay Peninsula and Archipelago.
- **1303 CE** — Terengganu Inscription Stone, earliest evidence of Classical Malay in Jawi (Arabic) script and Islam as state religion.
- **1345–1377** — Tanjung Tanah Law produced in the Melayu Kingdom (Sumatra), a pre-Islamic legal text in Malay.
- **1402–1511** — Malacca Sultanate era: Classical Malay becomes the region’s lingua franca, heavily infused with Arabic vocabulary via Islamic literature.
- **1511** — Portuguese capture Malacca; royal court relocates to Johor Sultanate, continuing Classical Malay literary tradition.
- **1521–1522** — Letters from Sultan Abu Hayat of Ternate, among oldest surviving Malay correspondence, show Malay as a non-native lingua franca.
- **1824** — Anglo-Dutch Treaty divides the region; British colonize the Malay Peninsula/Borneo, Dutch colonize the East Indies, both standardizing Malay for administration.
- **1968** — Malay becomes sole official language in Peninsular Malaysia (West Malaysia).
- **1974** — Malay becomes sole official language in East Malaysia.
- **2023** — Indonesian recognized as an official language of the UNESCO General Conference.

## Body

### Origin and Classification
Linguists locate the Malayic homeland in western Borneo, where Proto-Malayic was spoken by at least 1000 BCE. Its ancestor, Proto-Malayo-Polynesian, diverged from Proto-Austronesian by 2000 BCE during the southward Austronesian expansion from Taiwan. Malay belongs to the Malayic subgroup of Austronesian, a dialect continuum spread by Sumatran traders. The boundary between dialect and language is unclear: Brunei Malay and Kedah Malay, for example, are not readily intelligible with the standard, yet are considered close relatives. Malayic varieties are traditionally grouped as Malay proper, Para-Malay (Sumatran languages like Minangkabau, Musi, Duano’), and Aboriginal Malay (Orang Asli languages like Jakun, Temuan), though this reflects geography and ethnicity more than strict linguistics.

### Historical Periods
**Old Malay (7th–14th c.)** was influenced by Sanskrit and served as Srivijaya’s trade lingua franca. The Kedukan Bukit inscription (683 CE) in Pallava script is the earliest attestation. **Transitional Period (14th–early 15th c.)** includes pre-Islamic texts like the Tanjung Tanah Law (1345–1377) and the Terengganu Stone (1303 CE), the first Jawi-script evidence and marker of Islam’s adoption. **Classical Malay (1402–1511)** flourished under the Malacca Sultanate, absorbing massive Arabic vocabulary (plus Sanskrit and Tamil) through Islamic literature, evolving into a form recognizable to modern speakers. After Malacca’s fall to the Portuguese (1511), the court moved to Johor, dispersing literary centers to Aceh, Java, Makassar, and the Moluccas. **Late Modern Malay (19th c.)** saw British and Dutch colonial powers standardize the language for administration, using each other’s scholarship. **Modern Malay** comprises the national standards of Malaysia, Singapore, Brunei, and Indonesia (Indonesian), plus Timor-Leste’s working language.

### Writing System
Malay is officially written in the Latin script (Rumi in Malaysia, Singapore, Brunei; Latin in Indonesia). The Arabic-derived Jawi script is co-official in Brunei and Malaysia, used in religious schools (sekolah agama) and required for institutional names. Historically, Old Malay used Pallava, Kawi, and Rencong scripts; Jawi replaced them from the Pasai kingdom through the Malacca Sultanate. From the 17th century, Dutch and British influence gradually displaced Jawi with Rumi. Hindu-Arabic numerals are used.

### Phonology
Standard Malay has 19 native consonants and 6 vowels (/i, e, a, u, o, ə/), expanded from an original four via splits of /i/ → /i, e/ and /u/ → /u, o/. Non-native consonants (/f, v, z, ʃ, x, q/) appear only in loans (Arabic, Dutch, English). Orthographic conventions: /ɲ/ = *ny*, /ŋ/ = *ng*, /tʃ/ = *c*, /dʒ/ = *j/, /ʃ/ = *sy*, /x/ = *kh*, /j/ = *y*, glottal stop = final *k* or *’*. Arabic loans historically introduced /ð/ (written *z*) and /θ/ (written *s*, formerly *th*), which many speakers replace with native sounds. Vowel harmony requires non-open vowels in bisyllabic words to agree in height (e.g., *hidung* “nose” allowed, *hedung* not). Final-syllable *a* mutation is an areal feature of Western Austronesia.

### Grammar
Malay is agglutinative, forming words via affixation (prefixes, suffixes, circumfixes), composition (compounding), and reduplication. No grammatical gender: *dia* = “he/she,” *dia punya* = “his/her.” No grammatical plural: *orang* = “person/people.” Verbs are not inflected for person, number, or tense; tense is marked by adverbs (*sudah* “already,” *belum* “not yet”) or time words. A complex verb-affix system encodes voice, intentionality, and mood. Basic word order is OVA (often called “passive”), with agent and object separated by the verb; AVO is the alternative. There is no grammatical subject in the English sense.

### Vocabulary
Heavy borrowing reflects trade and colonial history: Arabic (religious terms), Sanskrit, Tamil, Persian, Sinitic languages, then Portuguese, Dutch, and English (scientific/technical terms). Indonesian favors Sanskrit for neologisms (influence of Javanese/Balinese); Malaysian/Brunei Malay favors Arabic (Islamic practice). Colonial administration terms derive from Dutch (Indonesian) vs. English (Malaysian/Brunei). The divergence between Indonesian and Malaysian standards is systemic, creating a “discernible cognitive gap” beyond mere dialect difference (e.g., *pasal* vs. *perkara* for “article,” *pernyataan* vs. *perisytiharan* for “declaration”).

### Varieties and Sociolinguistic Status
**Malaysia:** National language (Art. 152), sole official language in West Malaysia (1968) and East Malaysia (1974); English remains strong in professions and courts. **Brunei:** Similar to Malaysia; distinct vernacular Brunei Malay coexists with Standard Malay. **Singapore:** National language; anthem (*Majulah Singapura*) and military/police parade commands are in Malay, though English is the main lingua franca. **Indonesia:** Indonesian (from Riau Malay) is the constitutional national language (Art. 36) and unifying lingua franca across diverse islands; “Malay” (*bahasa Melayu*) refers to vernacular varieties in Sumatra/West Kalimantan. **Timor-Leste:** Indonesian is a constitutional working language alongside English, with official languages Tetum and Portuguese. **Southern Thailand:** Pattani Malay (Yawi) spoken in five provinces, no official status. **Philippines:** Malay loanwords integrated into Tagalog (e.g., *dalam hati*, *luwalhati*); Indonesian taught to armed forces and students. **Australia:** Indonesian taught since 1950s, one of three Asian target languages. **South Africa:** Cape Malay community contributed Classical Malay words to Afrikaans.

Dialects split into western (Sumatra, Borneo, e.g., Riau, Palembang, Jambi, Betawi) and eastern (creoles: Makassar, Manado, Ambon, Kupang, Dili, Papuan). Key differences: *kita* = “we” (western) vs. “I” (Manado); eastern varieties lack possessive pronouns, using *pe* (Manado) or *pu* (Ambon, from *punya* “to have”) for possession. Pronunciation of final *a* varies regionally (/ə/, /ɔ/, /a/, /o/, /ɛ/, /ɑ/).

## Terms
- ****Austronesian**** — A language family spanning Southeast Asia, the Pacific, and Madagascar; includes Malay, Tagalog, Javanese, Malagasy, and others.
- ****Macrolanguage**** — A group of mutually intelligible speech varieties (dialect continuum) with no common traditional name, often considered distinct languages by speakers (ISO 639-3 classification).
- ****Pluricentric**** — A language with multiple interacting standard varieties, each codified for a different nation (e.g., Malay in Malaysia, Indonesia, Brunei, Singapore).
- ****Lingua franca**** — A common language used for communication between speakers of different native languages.
- ****Classical Malay**** — The literary standard of the Malacca and Johor Sultanates (15th–17th c.), heavily influenced by Arabic and Islamic literature; also called Court Malay.
- ****Jawi**** — An Arabic-derived script for Malay, co-official in Brunei and Malaysia, used in religious education and official names.
- ****Rumi**** — The Latin script for Malay, official in Malaysia, Singapore, and Indonesia.
- ****Agglutinative**** — A morphological type where words are built by stringing affixes onto roots, each affix expressing a single grammatical meaning.
- ****Reduplication**** — Repetition of a word or part of a word to indicate plurality, intensity, or other grammatical nuances.
- ****OVA/AVO**** — Basic word orders in Malay: Object–Verb–Agent (OVA, often called “passive”) and Agent–Verb–Object (AVO), distinguished by verb voice.

## Debates and open questions
- **Dialect vs. language boundaries:** Ethnologue lists several Malayic varieties as separate languages (e.g., Orang Asli lects, Brunei Malay, Kedah Malay), but mutual intelligibility is high and the distinction remains unresolved.
- **Malayic homeland:** Western Borneo is the leading hypothesis, but the exact location and migration routes are still debated.
- **Creole status:** Eastern varieties (Makassar, Manado, Ambon, etc.) are sometimes classified as creoles because speakers are not ethnically Malay; the line between dialect and creole is unclear.
- **Cognitive divergence:** The systemic split between Indonesian and Malaysian standards may reflect deeper cognitive differences, not just lexical choice; the extent and implications are under study.
- **Jawi preservation:** Efforts to maintain Jawi in Malaysia face competition from the dominance of Rumi; the script’s long-term role is uncertain.
