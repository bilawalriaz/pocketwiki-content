# Hero

## Overview
A hero is a person, real or fictional, admired for courageous or noble acts, especially when facing adversity. The concept has evolved from classical figures motivated by personal honor and glory to modern ones who act selflessly for the common good. The study of heroes spans literature, history, psychology, and philosophy, exploring their role in culture, storytelling, and human identity.

## Timeline
- **c. 2100 BC** — Epic of Gilgamesh, one of the earliest literary heroes, emerges.
- **c. 8th century BC** — Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey* define the classical hero archetype.
- **1587** — First recorded use of the English word "heroine."
- **1841** — Thomas Carlyle publishes *On Heroes, Hero-Worship, & the Heroic in History*.
- **1936** — Lord Raglan publishes *The Hero*, outlining 22 common mythic traits.
- **1943** — Sidney Hook publishes *The Hero in History*.
- **1949** — Joseph Campbell publishes *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, detailing the monomyth.
- **1977** — Feminist critique of male-focused hero theory begins with Judith Fetterley's work.

## Body

### Etymology and Classical Antiquity
The word "hero" derives from the Greek *hērōs*, originally denoting a figure with divine ancestry or one later given divine honors. The female form "heroine" entered English in 1587. In classical literature, a hero is a semi-divine warrior whose life centers on fighting for honor and glory, often with a flawed personal code. Figures like Achilles and Hector from the Trojan War exemplify this: Achilles is defined by superhuman strength and uncontrollable rage (*menis*), while Hector is portrayed as a noble defender of Troy. Classical heroes often had conflicted relationships with deities and were bound by an inescapable fate, as illustrated in the story of Oedipus. Despite their moral flaws, they were venerated in ancient Greek hero cults.

### Mythic Archetypes and Folklore
Scholars have identified universal patterns in hero stories. Lord Raglan developed a 22-trait scale to identify mythical heroes. Joseph Campbell's monomyth, or "Hero's Journey," describes a common three-stage narrative (Departure, Initiation, Return) found across cultures, featuring archetypes like the call to adventure and supernatural aid. In folklore analysis, Vladimir Propp identified the hero as one of eight character types in Russian fairy tales, distinguishing between "seeker" heroes who embark on a quest and "victim" heroes who are acted upon.

### Historical and Philosophical Perspectives
The role of the "great man" in history has been debated. Philosophers like Hegel and Thomas Carlyle argued that history is shaped by heroic individuals. This view was contested by thinkers like Karl Marx, who emphasized social forces and class struggle, and the Annales school, which prioritized long-term geographical and economic trends over individual actions. Modern examples of transformative leaders include Martin Luther King Jr. and Nelson Mandela. Feminist scholars have also critiqued the traditionally male focus of hero theory.

### Modern Fiction and Psychology
In modern literature, the hero is often a more complex or problematic figure, as seen in Thackeray's *Vanity Fair*, subtitled "A Novel without a Hero." The superhero genre, featuring larger-than-life figures like Superman and Batman, remains a dominant cultural force. Psychologically, heroes are associated with traits like bravery, moral integrity, and self-sacrifice. Ernest Becker argued in *The Denial of Death* that heroism is a symbolic defense against mortality, allowing individuals to feel part of something eternal through "immortality projects." Research also explores the evolutionary and social functions of heroic behavior.

## Terms
- **Monomyth**: A universal narrative pattern, detailed by Joseph Campbell, describing a hero's journey through stages of departure, initiation, and return.
- **Hero's Journey**: The common story structure of a hero's adventure, involving a call to adventure, trials, and a return transformed.
- **Demi-god**: A figure with one divine and one mortal parent, like Achilles, common in classical mythology.
- **Menis**: The Greek term for "wrath," a defining trait of heroes like Achilles.
- **Hero Cult**: Ancient Greek religious practices venerating deified heroes for blessings and protection.
- **Great Man Theory**: A historical philosophy asserting that history is primarily shaped by the influence of exceptional individuals.
- **Immortality Project**: Ernest Becker's concept of a symbolic belief-system that allows individuals to transcend the fear of death through heroic or meaningful action.
- **Pankration**: An ancient Greek martial art combining boxing and wrestling, associated with heroic prowess.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Universality of the Hero's Journey**: The pervasiveness of Joseph Campbell's monomyth across all cultures is debated by scholars.
- **Role of the Individual vs. Social Forces**: A central debate in history concerns whether transformative change is driven by heroic individuals or broader social, economic, and geographical forces.
- **Gender and Heroism**: Feminist critiques challenge the traditionally male-centric definition and study of heroes, questioning how heroism is gendered in literature and society.
- **Psychological Basis**: The evolutionary and psychological motivations for heroic risk-taking, such as whether it is a costly signal or a form of altruism, remain areas of study.
