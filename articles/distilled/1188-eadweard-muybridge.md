# Eadweard Muybridge

## Overview
Eadweard Muybridge (1830–1904) was an English-born photographer who became a pivotal figure in the transition from still photography to motion pictures. After emigrating to the American West and surviving a catastrophic 1860 stagecoach accident that altered his personality, he mastered the wet-plate collodion process and gained fame for dramatic landscapes of Yosemite and government surveys of Alaska and the Modoc War. His defining achievement came between 1872 and 1879 when, funded by railroad magnate Leland Stanford, he developed multi-camera systems triggered by tripwires to settle debates about animal locomotion—proving horses lift all four hooves off the ground simultaneously during a gallop. At the University of Pennsylvania (1883–1886), he produced over 100,000 sequential images of humans and animals, published as the landmark *Animal Locomotion* (1887). He invented the zoopraxiscope to project these sequences as moving pictures, establishing the conceptual and technical foundation for cinema. His work profoundly influenced visual art, biomechanics, and early filmmakers, bridging 19th-century scientific inquiry and 20th-century media.

## Body

### Early Life, Emigration, and the 1860 Accident
Born into a grain-merchant family in Kingston upon Thames, Muybridge emigrated at age 20 to the United States, working as a bookseller in New York, New Orleans, and San Francisco. In 1860, a runaway stagecoach crash in Texas inflicted a severe head injury—killing the driver and a passenger, leaving Muybridge unconscious for days and with lasting double vision, deafness, and personality changes. Modern neurologists speculate the trauma damaged his orbitofrontal cortex and anterior temporal lobes, potentially disinhibiting his creativity and contributing to later erratic behavior. During convalescence in England (1860–1866), he learned the wet-plate collodion process (a photographic technique requiring glass plates to be coated, exposed, and developed while wet), secured British patents for printing and washing machinery, and briefly directed failed mining ventures.

### Photographer of the American West (1867–1873)
Returning to San Francisco in 1867 as "Helios," Muybridge operated a mobile darkroom ("Helios' Flying Studio") and produced over 400 stereograph cards (paired images viewed through a lens to create 3D depth). His Yosemite views—taken with a heavy view camera on precarious cliffs—emphasized romantic sublimity over Carleton Watkins' classical serenity. Government commissions followed: documenting the Tlingit people and landscapes of Alaska (1868), West Coast lighthouses from the tender *Shubrick* (1871), and the Modoc War (1873), where he staged scenes for dramatic effect despite slow emulsions. His time-lapse documentation of the San Francisco Mint construction (1870–1872) likely attracted Leland Stanford's attention.

### The Stanford Horse Studies and Chronophotography (1872–1879)
Stanford, a race-horse owner, hired Muybridge to analyze equine gaits invisible to the naked eye. Artists conventionally depicted trotting horses with one foot grounded and galloping horses with legs fully extended ("flying gallop"). After years of developing faster shutters, more sensitive emulsions, and electrically triggered mechanisms with Central Pacific Railroad engineers, Muybridge succeeded on 15 June 1878 at Stanford's Palo Alto Stock Farm. A battery of 12 cameras, tripped by wires connected to an electromagnetic circuit, captured a horse in sequential phases. The images revealed the "unsupported transit": all four hooves leave the ground not when extended, but when collected beneath the body during the switch from front-leg pulling to hind-leg pushing. The results, published as *The Horse in Motion* cabinet cards and widely reproduced as woodcut engravings, revolutionized artistic anatomy and biomechanics. Muybridge adapted these sequences for his zoopraxiscope (a projector rotating painted glass discs to animate images), a direct precursor to cinematography.

### Personal Crisis: Marriage, Homicide, and Acquittal (1871–1875)
Muybridge married Flora Shallcross Stone in 1871; their son Florado was born in 1874. Discovering Flora's affair with Harry Larkyns and evidence she believed Larkyns fathered the child, Muybridge tracked Larkyns to Calistoga and shot him dead on 17 October 1874. At trial, his attorney pleaded insanity from the 1860 head injury; acquaintances testified to his post-accident personality shift. Muybridge undermined the defense by acknowledging premeditation, yet the jury acquitted him on grounds of justifiable homicide—effectively endorsing a "law of human nature" over statute. Flora divorced him; she died in 1875. Muybridge placed Florado in orphanages, maintaining minimal contact. The trial transcripts remain a key resource for forensic neurology.

### University of Pennsylvania Motion Studies (1883–1886)
Following a rupture with Stanford over credit for *The Horse in Motion* (1882)—where Stanford's friend Dr. J.B.D. Stillman used Muybridge's photos without attribution—Muybridge secured backing from Philadelphia luminaries including artist Thomas Eakins and Provost William Pepper. At a dedicated outdoor studio at 36th and Pine Streets, he deployed banks of 12 cameras with improved lenses to produce over 100,000 images. Subjects—nude or lightly clothed against measured grids—included professors, athletes, students, disabled patients from Blockley Almshouse, and zoo animals. Sequences captured walking, stair-climbing, hammering, athletic feats, and domestic labor. Muybridge himself posed nude swinging a pickaxe. He substituted failed exposures with representative images to illustrate idealized motion, prioritizing artistic clarity over strict scientific recording—a divergence from French physiologist Étienne-Jules Marey, who used single-camera multiple exposures (chronophotography) for precise physiological analysis. The two men met in 1881 and mutually influenced each other's work.

### Publication, Projection, and Late Career
The collotype portfolio *Animal Locomotion* (1887) comprised 11 volumes, 781 plates, and 20,000 images, becoming a foundational reference for artists, animators, and biomechanists. At the 1893 Chicago World's Columbian Exposition, Muybridge operated the Zoopraxographical Hall—the first commercial movie theater—projecting moving sequences to paying audiences. He retired to England in 1894, publishing the enduring *Animals in Motion* (1899) and *The Human Figure in Motion* (1901). He died in 1904; his gravestone misspells his surname "Maybridge." Kingston Museum holds his bequeathed equipment, including the zoopraxiscope and 2,000 magic lantern slides.

### Legacy and Cultural Impact
Muybridge's sequential vision permeates modern culture: Marcel Duchamp's *Nude Descending a Staircase* (1912), Francis Bacon's overlapping figures, the "bullet time" effect in *The Matrix* (1999), and Philip Glass's opera *The Photographer* (1982). His grids and seriality influenced Sol LeWitt, Steven Pippin, and Wayne McGregor. In 2017, five frames of his horse "Annie G" were encoded in bacterial DNA via CRISPR. His work remains a touchstone for debates on photographic truth, scientific imaging, and the birth of cinema.

## Terms
- **Wet-plate collodion process**: A 1850s photographic technique requiring a glass plate to be coated with light-sensitive collodion, exposed in the camera, and developed immediately while still wet; dominant until dry plates replaced it in the 1880s.
- **Stereograph**: A pair of nearly identical photographs mounted side-by-side; when viewed through a stereoscope, they merge into a single three-dimensional image. Immensely popular in the Victorian era.
- **Chronophotography**: The photographic capture of sequential phases of motion for scientific or artistic analysis. Muybridge used multiple cameras; Marey later used single-camera multiple exposures.
- **Zoopraxiscope**: Muybridge's projection device (c. 1879) that rotated glass discs painted with sequential silhouettes or photographs to animate motion pictures, predating flexible film strip cinematography.
- **Unsupported transit**: The phase in a horse's gallop when all four hooves are off the ground simultaneously. Muybridge proved this occurs when legs are collected under the body, not extended fore and aft.
- **Electromagnetic shutter trigger**: A system where a subject trips a wire, closing an electrical circuit that releases a camera shutter, enabling precise synchronization across multiple cameras.
- **Collotype**: A photomechanical printing process using a gelatin-coated plate to produce high-fidelity continuous-tone reproductions; used for *Animal Locomotion* plates.
- **Phenakistiscope**: An early animation device (pre-dating Muybridge) using a spinning disc with sequential images viewed through slots; Muybridge sold souvenir discs derived from his photos.
- **Bullet time**: A modern visual effect using arrays of timed still cameras to orbit a frozen or slow-motion subject—inverse of Muybridge's multiple cameras capturing a moving subject.
- **Orbitofrontal cortex**: A brain region involved in decision-making and emotional regulation; hypothesized damaged in Muybridge's 1860 accident, possibly explaining his personality change.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authorship of Yosemite images**: 21st-century scholars have claimed many landscapes attributed to Muybridge were actually made by or under Carleton Watkins; the extent of collaboration or misattribution remains disputed.
- **Scientific vs. artistic intent**: Muybridge substituted failed exposures with "representative" images in *Animal Locomotion*, prioritizing illustrative clarity over strict data integrity. Scholars debate whether this compromises the work's scientific validity or reflects a legitimate hybrid practice.
- **Stanford's $25,000 wager**: Popular lore claims Stanford bet $25,000 on the "unsupported transit" hypothesis; no documentary evidence supports the bet, though Stanford spent an estimated $50,000 funding the research.
- **Neurological basis of creativity**: The hypothesis that Muybridge's orbitofrontal cortex injury disinhibited his creativity (Shimamura, UC Berkeley) is plausible but unprovable post-mortem; it remains a compelling case study in neuroaesthetics.
- **Paternity of Florado**: Flora's inscription "Harry" on a photo of their son suggested she believed Larkyns was the father; adult photos show strong resemblance to Muybridge. The biological paternity is unresolved.
- **Priority in motion-picture invention**: Muybridge's zoopraxiscope projected painted discs, not photographic film. His role as a "father of cinema" is debated relative to Marey, Edison, the Lumière brothers, and Louis Le Prince, each contributing distinct technical steps.
