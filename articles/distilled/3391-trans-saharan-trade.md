# Trans-Saharan trade

## Overview
Trans-Saharan trade refers to the exchange of goods, people, and ideas between North Africa and sub-Saharan Africa via caravan routes across the Sahara Desert. It peaked from the 8th to the early 17th centuries, facilitating the rise of powerful West African empires, the spread of Islam, and a triangular trade in gold, salt, and slaves. Its decline was driven by European coastal trade and political disruptions.

## Timeline
- **c. 7000 BCE** — Pastoralism, settlements, and pottery present in the central Sahara.
- **c. 800 BCE** — Founding of Carthage, which became a terminus for West African gold, ivory, and slaves.
- **3rd century CE** — Earliest evidence for domesticated camels in the region, enabling regular trans-Saharan contact.
- **7th-8th centuries** — Regular trade routes develop with the beginnings of West Africa's Islamic conversion.
- **8th century** — Muslims from North Africa begin traveling to the Ghana Empire.
- **c. 1050** — Ghana loses Aoudaghost to the Almoravids; trade shifts to benefit the Malinke.
- **11th-12th centuries** — Sijilmasa peaks in importance as a gold-trading and minting center.
- **1591-92** — Battle of Tondibi: Moroccan invasion destroys Timbuktu and Gao, disrupting trade.
- **16th century** — Portuguese coastal trade becomes prime importance to West Africa.
- **1890s** — French invasion of the Sahel and railway construction further undermine trans-Saharan routes.

## Body

### Early History and Routes
Trade across the Sahara began in prehistoric times, with ancient routes like the Forty Days Road (Darb El Arba'īn) and the Ghadames Road connecting oases. The Garamantes of Libya controlled eastern routes from at least 1500 BCE. The introduction of the camel in the 3rd century CE was transformative, enabling more regular contact. However, regular trade routes only developed in the 7th and 8th centuries, coinciding with the Islamic conversion of West Africa. Two main routes emerged: one from Morocco to the Niger bend, and another from Tunisia to Lake Chad.

### Medieval Trade and Empires
The medieval period saw the peak of trans-Saharan trade, dominated by the gold-salt exchange. Northern economies controlled salt mines like Taghaza, while West African states like Ghana, Mali, and Songhai had abundant gold. Caravans, guided by Berbers and sometimes numbering 12,000 camels, relied on careful coordination and oasis networks. The trade enriched cities like Sijilmasa, Timbuktu, and Gao. The eastern route was crucial for the Kanem–Bornu Empire.

### The Slave Trade
The trans-Saharan slave trade was a constant feature. The Garamantes used slaves for irrigation in antiquity, and the trade continued through the medieval period. Slaves were used as domestic servants, concubines, and soldiers in North Africa and West Africa. Historian John Wright estimates an average of 5,000 people were transported annually over 1,250 years, totaling 6-7 million, with the majority moved after the 1600s.

### Spread of Islam
Islam spread along trade routes, facilitated by Muslim merchants. It provided a common framework of values, a trusted network of believers, and a common language (Arabic) for commerce. Conversion increased trade and was often adopted by West African ruling elites. Islam spread into the Western Sudan by the 10th century and into Hausa lands by the 12th-13th centuries.

### Decline
The trans-Saharan trade declined due to several factors. Portuguese exploration opened European coastal trade routes in the 16th century. The major blow was the 1591-92 Moroccan invasion, which destroyed key trading cities. Later, French colonization and railway construction in the 1890s, followed by post-independence national boundaries and conflicts like the Tuareg rebellions, further severed traditional routes.

## Terms
- **Caravan**: A large group of people and animals (typically camels) traveling together across the desert for trade.
- **Pastoralism**: An economy based on herding livestock, such as sheep, goats, and cattle.
- **Foggara**: Underground irrigation systems used by the Garamantes, maintained with slave labor.
- **Sahel**: The semi-arid transitional zone south of the Sahara Desert.
- **Gold-Salt Trade**: The primary economic exchange, where gold from the south was traded for salt from the north.
- **Middlemen**: Traders who acted as intermediaries between producers and consumers, often controlling access to trade routes.
- **Azalai**: Traditional, lightly-used camel caravan routes still in operation, such as from Agadez to Bilma.

## Debates and Open questions
- The scale of the slave trade in the Roman era is debated; some historians argue it may have been higher than in medieval times due to high Roman demand.
- The theory that the Garamantes used chariot routes for trade is contested, as no horse skeletons from the period have been found and chariots had low trade capacity.
- The exact number of people enslaved via trans-Saharan routes is challenging to estimate, with modern figures relying on historical averages and projections.
