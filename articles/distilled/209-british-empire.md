# British Empire

## Overview
The British Empire comprised the dominions, colonies, protectorates, mandates, and other territories ruled or administered by the United Kingdom and its predecessor states. Originating in late 16th-century English and Scottish overseas ventures, it became the largest empire in history by the early 20th century, governing 412 million people (23% of the world population) and covering 35.5 million km² (24% of Earth's land area) by 1920. It was described as "the empire on which the sun never sets", as the sun was always shining on at least one of its territories. Its dominance was underpinned by the Royal Navy, industrial supremacy, and the "informal empire" of trade and finance. The empire peaked territorially after the First World War but was fatally weakened by the economic and military strains of the two world wars. A rapid decolonisation followed 1945, culminating in the 1997 handover of Hong Kong. Fourteen overseas territories remain under British sovereignty, and most former possessions belong to the Commonwealth of Nations, with fifteen realms sharing King Charles III as monarch.

## Body

### Origins and Early Expansion (1497–1707)
English exploration began under Henry VII with John Cabot’s 1497 voyage to Newfoundland. Throughout the 16th century, England lagged behind Portugal and Spain but engaged in privateering against Spanish treasure fleets under Elizabeth I, while writers like Richard Hakluyt and John Dee (who coined "British Empire") advocated for colonisation. England’s first systematic colonisation occurred in Ireland via "plantations" of Protestant settlers, providing a template and personnel for later American ventures. The 1603 Union of Crowns under James VI/I brought peace with Spain, shifting focus from raiding to settlement. The Virginia Company founded Jamestown (1607), while Puritans established Plymouth (1620). In the Caribbean, a "Sugar Revolution" (mid-17th century) transformed islands like Barbados into highly profitable slave economies. The Navigation Acts (1651) restricted colonial trade to English ships, sparking Anglo-Dutch Wars that ultimately strengthened England’s American position. The Hudson’s Bay Company (1670) and Royal African Company (1672) monopolised the fur and slave trades respectively; British ships carried a third of all slaves shipped across the Atlantic—approximately 3.5 million Africans—until the abolition of the trade by Parliament in 1807. Scotland attempted its own colonies (Nova Scotia, 1629; Darien, Panama, 1698), but the Darien scheme’s financial disaster helped drive the 1707 Acts of Union.

### Imperial Consolidation and Conflict (1707–1783)
The new Kingdom of Great Britain emerged from the War of Spanish Succession (1701–14) with key gains: Newfoundland, Acadia, Gibraltar, Menorca, and the *asiento* (slave-trade rights to Spanish America). Rivalry with France defined the mid-century. In India, the East India Company (EIC) defeated the Nawab of Bengal and French allies at Plassey (1757), securing Bengal and becoming a territorial power. The global Seven Years' War (1756–63) ended with the Treaty of Paris (1763): France ceded New France (Canada) and Louisiana (to Spain); Spain ceded Florida; Britain became the dominant maritime power. However, attempts to tax the Thirteen Colonies without representation triggered the American Revolution (1775–83). French and Spanish intervention proved decisive; Britain recognised US independence in 1783. This loss marked the transition from the "First" to the "Second Empire," shifting focus to Asia and the Pacific. Loyalist migration northward reshaped Canada, prompting the creation of New Brunswick (1784) and the division of Upper/Lower Canada (1791).

### Global Dominance and the *Pax Britannica* (1783–1914)
After losing America, Britain established a penal colony in Australia (1788) and expanded in the Pacific (Nootka Convention, 1790; Vancouver Island exploration). In India, the EIC defeated Mysore (1799) and the Maratha Confederacy (1818), controlling most of the subcontinent. The Napoleonic Wars (1803–15) confirmed British naval supremacy (Trafalgar, 1805); the 1815 settlements added Cape Colony, Ceylon, Malta, Mauritius, and others. The Industrial Revolution reduced the economic reliance on slavery; the Slave Trade Act (1807) and Slavery Abolition Act (1833) ended the trade and the institution (with exceptions until 1844). Steamships and telegraphs (the "All Red Line" cable network by 1902) enabled control over a vastly expanded empire—adding 10 million sq mi and 400 million people between 1815–1914. Britain exercised "informal empire" over economies like China, Argentina, and Siam through trade dominance.

**India and the Raj:** The EIC’s opium trade with China reversed silver drains from tea imports; Chinese seizure of opium triggered the First Opium War (1839–42), ceding Hong Kong. The 1857 Indian Rebellion (sepoy mutiny) ended Company rule; the Government of India Act (1858) established the British Raj, where an appointed governor-general administered India and Queen Victoria was crowned the Empress of India in 1876. India became the "Jewel in the Crown." Late-19th-century famines killed over 15 million; British famine policy evolved slowly.

**Settler Colonies and Dominion Status:** The Durham Report (1839) recommended responsible government for Canada, granted to Nova Scotia (1848) and extended elsewhere. The British North America Act (1867) confederated Canada. Australia federated (1901); New Zealand gained self-government. The 1907 Imperial Conference coined "Dominion status." The 1926 Balfour Declaration and 1931 Statute of Westminster confirmed Dominions as autonomous, equal communities within the British Commonwealth of Nations.

**Africa and the Great Game:** Britain acquired the Cape Colony (1806), sparking the Boer Great Trek (1830s–40s) and eventual Boer republics (Transvaal, Orange Free State). The Suez Canal opening (1869) became the "jugular vein of Empire"; Britain bought shares (1875) and occupied Egypt (1882). The Berlin Conference (1884–85) regulated the Scramble for Africa. Cecil Rhodes pursued a "Cape to Cairo" railway, annexing Rhodesia. The Second Boer War (1899–1902) brought the republics under British control. In Central Asia, the "Great Game" with Russia saw the disastrous First Anglo-Afghan War (1839–42), the Crimean War (1854–56), and eventual agreement on spheres of influence (1907).

### World Wars and the Imperial System (1914–1945)
Pre-war alliances (Japan 1902, France 1904, Russia 1907) countered Germany. In WWI, Britain seized German colonies in Africa and the Pacific; the Sykes-Picot Agreement (1916) secretly partitioned the Ottoman Empire. Over 2.5 million Dominion troops served; Gallipoli and Vimy Ridge forged distinct national identities. Under the terms of the concluding Treaty of Versailles signed in 1919, the empire reached its greatest extent with the addition of 1.8 million sq mi (4.7 million km²) and 13 million new subjects. The colonies of Germany and the Ottoman Empire were distributed to the Allied powers as League of Nations mandates. Britain gained control of Palestine, Transjordan, Iraq, parts of Cameroon and Togoland, and Tanganyika. The interwar period saw the Anglo-Japanese Alliance lapse (1922), naval parity with the US, and rising nationalism. The 1919–21 Irish War of Independence created the Irish Free State (Dominion). In India, the 1919 Amritsar Massacre galvanised resistance. The 1926 Balfour Declaration and 1931 Statute of Westminster formalised Dominion legislative independence.

In WWII, Britain declared war (1939); Dominions followed independently (Ireland remained neutral). The Fall of France (1940) left Britain alone until US entry (1941). The Atlantic Charter’s self-determination clause was ambiguously interpreted; Churchill rejected its application to India. Japanese conquests in Southeast Asia (1941–42), especially the Fall of Singapore, shattered imperial prestige and pushed Australia/NZ toward the US (ANZUS, 1951). The war bankrupted Britain, accelerated Indian nationalism, and elevated the US and USSR as superpowers.

### Decolonisation and Decline (1945–1997)
Post-war bankruptcy (US loan 1946) and Cold War dynamics forced reassessment. The US opposed colonialism but backed Britain to contain communism. Labour PM Attlee initiated rapid disengagement.

**Asia:** Indian independence (1947) partitioned the subcontinent into India and Pakistan amid mass violence and displacement (~hundreds of thousands dead). Burma and Ceylon followed (1948). The Malayan Emergency (1948–60) against Chinese communists ended with Malayan independence (1957) and later Malaysia (1963; Singapore expelled 1965).

**Middle East:** Britain withdrew from Palestine (1948) as Israel declared independence. The 1956 Suez Crisis—Eden’s collusion with France/Israel to retake the nationalised canal—failed under US financial pressure and Soviet threats. It exposed Britain’s dependence on US acquiescence, ending its first-rate power status. Britain withdrew "East of Suez" by 1971 (Aden 1967; Gulf states 1971).

**Africa:** Macmillan’s "Wind of Change" (1960) accelerated independence: Sudan (1956), Gold Coast/Ghana (1957), Nigeria (1960), Kenya (1963, after the brutal Mau Mau rebellion), etc. Southern Rhodesia’s Unilateral Declaration of Independence (1965) led to civil war and the Lancaster House Agreement creating Zimbabwe (1980).

**Caribbean and Pacific:** The West Indies Federation collapsed (1962); Jamaica, Trinidad, Barbados, and others gained independence through the 1960s–80s. Pacific territories (Fiji 1970, Vanuatu 1980) followed.

**Final Constitutional Cuts:** The Canada Act (1982), Australia Act (1986), and New Zealand Constitution Act (1986) severed remaining British legislative authority. The 1982 Falklands War reversed post-Suez decline. The 1997 Hong Kong handover (Sino-British Joint Declaration, 1984) symbolised the empire’s end for many, including Prince Charles.

## Terms
- ****Dominion**** — A self-governing territory within the British Empire (later Commonwealth) recognised as autonomous and equal in status to Britain, formalised by the 1926 Balfour Declaration and 1931 Statute of Westminster (e.g., Canada, Australia).
- ****East India Company (EIC)**** — A joint-stock company chartered in 1600 to trade in Asia; it acquired territorial control in India, ruling via its own army until the 1857 Rebellion prompted the Crown to assume direct rule (the Raj).
- ****Pax Britannica**** — The period (1815–1914) of British global hegemony and relative peace, underpinned by unchallenged naval power and dominance of world trade.
- ****Scramble for Africa**** — The rapid, competitive colonisation of Africa by European powers (1880s–1900s), regulated by the 1884–85 Berlin Conference which defined "effective occupation" as the basis for claims.
- ****Great Game**** — The 19th-century strategic rivalry between the British and Russian Empires for influence in Central Asia, driven by British fears of a Russian land invasion of India.
- ****Informal Empire**** — British economic and political dominance over nominally independent states (e.g., China, Argentina, Siam) through trade, investment, and naval power, without formal colonial administration.
- ****Indentured Labour**** — A contract labour system replacing slavery after 1834; over 500,000 Indians migrated to Caribbean and other colonies under fixed-term contracts, often in coercive conditions.
- ****Mandate**** — A League of Nations authorisation (post-WWI) for Allied powers to administer former German/Ottoman territories as a "sacred trust of civilisation" until self-government; Britain held mandates in Palestine, Iraq, Tanganyika, etc.
- ****Suez Crisis (1956)**** — The failed Anglo-French-Israeli invasion of Egypt after Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal; US opposition forced withdrawal, publicly confirming Britain's reduced global status.
- ****Statute of Westminster (1931)**** — British legislation giving legal effect to the Balfour Declaration, establishing legislative independence for the Dominions (Canada, Australia, NZ, South Africa, Irish Free State, Newfoundland).

## Debates and open questions
*   **Economic relationship with industrialisation:** Scholars debate whether the Industrial Revolution was facilitated by or dependent on imperial resources, markets, and capital (e.g., profits from slavery, Indian cotton).
*   **Famine mortality in India:** Historians such as Amartya Sen argue British policies (laissez-faire grain export, inadequate relief) worsened late-19th-century famines killing over 15 million; others cite environmental factors and pre-existing structural vulnerabilities.
*   **Net legacy assessment:** Niall Ferguson contends the empire provided net economic/institutional benefits (rule of law, infrastructure, English language); Caroline Elkins and post-colonial scholars emphasise systemic violence, emergency laws, detention camps, and underdevelopment.
*   **Democratic correlation:** A noted correlation exists between former British colonies and post-independence democracy, though it weakens significantly after 30 years; causality versus selection effects remain contested.
*   **Public memory:** 21st-century British public opinion on the empire remains broadly positive, contrasting with critical academic consensus and apathy toward the modern Commonwealth.
