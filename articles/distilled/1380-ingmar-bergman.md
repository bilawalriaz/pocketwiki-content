# Ingmar Bergman

## Overview

Ernst Ingmar Bergman (1918–2007) was a Swedish film and theatre director and screenwriter, widely considered one of the greatest and most important filmmakers in cinema history. A prominent figure of both European film and Swedish cinema, his works are described as "profoundly personal meditations into the myriad struggles facing the psyche and the soul." His films typically explore existential questions of mortality, loneliness, religious faith, and sexual desire, and he forged a lasting creative partnership with cinematographers Gunnar Fischer and Sven Nykvist.

## Timeline

- **1918** — Born in Uppsala, Sweden, to a Lutheran minister father and nurse mother
- **1934** — Aged 16, attends a Nazi rally in Weimar and sees Adolf Hitler
- **1937** — Enrolls at Stockholm University College to study art and literature
- **1944** — Writes screenplay for *Torment* (directed by Alf Sjöberg), his first major film accomplishment
- **1955** — Achieves worldwide success with *Smiles of a Summer Night*
- **1957** — Releases *The Seventh Seal* and *Wild Strawberries* ten months apart
- **1961–1963** — Directs the "faith trilogy": *Through a Glass Darkly*, *Winter Light*, *The Silence*
- **1966** — Releases *Persona*, which he considered one of his most important works
- **1976** — Arrested and charged with income tax evasion; charges dropped but he goes into self-imposed exile in Munich
- **1982** — Directs *Fanny and Alexander*, which he stated would be his last film
- **2003** — Retires from filmmaking; directs final stage production, Ibsen's *Ghosts*
- **2007** — Dies in his sleep on Fårö, aged 89, the same day as Michelangelo Antonioni

## Body

### Early life and education

Bergman was born in Uppsala on 14 July 1918, son of nurse Karin (née Åkerblom) and Lutheran minister Erik Bergman, later chaplain to the King of Sweden. His family had complex noble and merchant ancestry of Finnish, German, Dutch, and Swedish origin. Raised in a devout Lutheran household surrounded by religious imagery, Bergman later stated he lost his faith at age eight, coming to terms with this while making *Winter Light* in 1962. His father was a strict parent who locked young Ingmar in dark closets for infractions. At age nine, Bergman traded tin soldiers for a magic lantern, creating a private puppet-theatre world with Strindberg plays. His school years were unhappy; his principal later called him a "problem child," and Bergman wrote that he "hated school as a principle, as a system and as an institution."

In 1934, aged 16, he attended a Nazi rally in Weimar and saw Hitler, later writing that "for many years, I was on Hitler's side, delighted by his success and saddened by his defeats," though the revelation of the concentration camps was "a hideous shock." He enrolled at Stockholm University College in 1937, became involved in student theatre, and had a physical confrontation with his father over a romantic involvement that caused a long estrangement. In 1942 he directed his own script *Caspar's Death*, which led Svensk Filmindustri to offer him a scriptwriting position.

### Film career

Bergman's film career began in 1941 with script rewriting, but his first major accomplishment was the 1944 screenplay for *Torment*, directed by Alf Sjöberg, which sparked national debate on Swedish education. He first achieved worldwide success with *Smiles of a Summer Night* (1955), followed by *The Seventh Seal* and *Wild Strawberries* (both 1957). From the early 1960s he spent much of his life on the island of Fårö. His early-1960s films *Through a Glass Darkly*, *Winter Light*, and *The Silence* explored faith and doubt in God; critics called them a trilogy, a notion Bergman initially rejected but later adopted with equivocation. *Persona* (1966), starring Bibi Andersson and Liv Ullmann, won few awards but is considered his masterpiece. Other notable films include *The Virgin Spring* (1960), *Shame* (1968), *Cries and Whispers* (1972), *Scenes from a Marriage* (1973), and *The Magic Flute* (1975).

### Tax evasion charges and exile

On 30 January 1976, Bergman was arrested and charged with income tax evasion concerning an alleged 1970 transaction of 500,000 Swedish kronor between his company Cinematograf and its Swiss subsidiary Persona. He suffered a nervous breakdown and was hospitalised. The charges were dropped on 23 March 1976, the prosecutor saying the alleged crime "had no legal basis." Despite this, Bergman vowed never to work in Sweden again, closed his Fårö studio, and went into self-imposed exile in Munich. His exile films included *The Serpent's Egg* (1977), *Autumn Sonata* (1978), and *From the Life of the Marionettes* (1980). He temporarily returned to direct *Fanny and Alexander* (1982), and by mid-1978 had partly resumed work at the Royal Dramatic Theatre. He remained in Munich until 1984, later saying he had effectively lost eight years of his professional life.

### Style of working

Bergman developed a personal repertory company of Swedish actors, including Max von Sydow, Bibi Andersson, Harriet Andersson, Erland Josephson, Ingrid Thulin, Gunnel Lindblom, and Gunnar Björnstrand. Liv Ullmann, who appeared in nine of his films, became most closely associated with him both artistically and personally; they had a daughter, Linn Ullmann (born 1966). With cinematographer Sven Nykvist, Bergman developed such rapport that he would only briefly describe the desired mood and composition before leaving Nykvist to work. Bergman never had funding problems, citing that he avoided US-style box-office obsession and that his films were low-budget (*Cries and Whispers* cost about $450,000). He usually wrote his own screenplays, thinking about them for months or years beforehand, and increasingly let actors improvise dialogue in later works. He directed more than 60 films and documentaries and more than 170 plays.

### Theatrical work

Bergman was also an active stage director throughout his life. At twenty-six he became the youngest theatrical manager in Europe at Helsingborg City Theatre, later directing at Gothenburg (1946–1949) and Malmö (from 1953, seven years). He directed the Royal Dramatic Theatre in Stockholm (1960–1966) and the Residenz Theatre in Munich (1977–1984). His final stage production was Ibsen's *Ghosts* at the Brooklyn Academy of Music in 2003.

### Personal life and death

Bergman was married five times: to Else Fisher (1943–1945), Ellen Lundström (1945–1950), Gun Grut (1951–1959), Käbi Laretei (1959–1969), and Ingrid von Rosen (1971 until her death in 1995). He had nine children, one of whom predeceased him, and romantic relationships with actresses Harriet Andersson, Bibi Andersson, and Liv Ullmann. He suffered from insomnia and severe stomach problems since childhood. He retired from filmmaking in December 2003, had hip surgery in October 2006, and died in his sleep on 30 July 2007, aged 89. He was buried privately at Fårö Church on 18 August 2007.

## Terms

- **Repertory company**: A group of actors regularly cast together across multiple productions; Bergman's included von Sydow, Ullmann, and the Anderssons.
- **Magic lantern**: An early type of image projector; Bergman's childhood toy that sparked his interest in visual storytelling.
- **Cinematographer**: The person responsible for the camera work and lighting of a film; Bergman's key collaborators were Gunnar Fischer and Sven Nykvist.
- **Palme d'Or**: The highest prize awarded at the Cannes Film Festival; Bergman's films were nominated for it.
- **Irving G. Thalberg Memorial Award**: An honorary Academy Award for creative producers; Bergman received it in 1971.
- **Best Foreign Language Film**: An Academy Award category; three of Bergman's films won it (*The Virgin Spring*, *Through a Glass Darkly*, *Fanny and Alexander*).
- **Existentialism**: A philosophical approach focusing on individual existence, freedom, and meaning; central to Bergman's subject matter.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether Bergman's three early-1960s films (*Through a Glass Darkly*, *Winter Light*, *The Silence*) constitute a planned trilogy; Bergman initially denied common motifs but later seemed to adopt the notion with equivocation. Bergman's legacy is also contested: critic Jonathan Rosenbaum published "Scenes From an Overrated Career" in 2007, while others, including Stanley Kubrick, Martin Scorsese, and Woody Allen, have praised him as among the greatest film artists. Bergman's religious views remain ambiguous: he said he lost his faith at eight and described himself as not believing in an afterlife, yet in 2000 stated a conversation with Erland Josephson helped him believe he would see his wife Ingrid again, and Max von Sydow claimed in 2012 that Bergman contacted him after death to prove an afterlife. An early draft of Bergman's autobiography contained an admission of rape of girlfriend Karin Lannby, which was edited out of the final version.
