# History of opera

## Overview
Opera is a multidisciplinary art form combining music, drama, dance, and visual spectacle. Originating in late 16th-century Florence, it evolved from attempts to revive ancient Greek theater and has since developed parallel to major musical currents, reflecting cultural, philosophical, and political ideas. Its history is marked by ongoing debates about the balance between music and text, the role of vocal virtuosity, and the integration of various artistic elements.

## Timeline
- **1597** — Jacopo Peri's *Dafne*, the first opera, is created.
- **1600** — Peri's *Euridice*, the first surviving complete opera, is premiered.
- **1607** — Claudio Monteverdi's *L'Orfeo* helps define the genre with structured arias and instrumental sinfonias.
- **1637** — The Teatro San Cassiano opens in Venice, the first public opera house.
- **1669** — The Académie Royale de Musique is founded in Paris.
- **1762** — Christoph Willibald Gluck's *Orfeo ed Euridice* begins his operatic reform.
- **1786** — Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart's *Le Nozze di Figaro* premieres.
- **1821** — Carl Maria von Weber's *Der Freischütz* creates German national opera.
- **1853** — Giuseppe Verdi's *La Traviata* premieres.
- **1876** — Richard Wagner's complete *Der Ring des Nibelungen* cycle is premiered at Bayreuth.
- **1890** — Pietro Mascagni's *Cavalleria rusticana* marks the start of verismo.
- **1925** — Alban Berg's *Wozzeck* premieres, a landmark of atonal opera.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
Opera emerged in late 16th-century Florence from the Florentine Camerata, a group of humanists seeking to revive ancient Greek theatrical music. They championed monody (a single vocal line) over polyphony. Jacopo Peri's *Dafne* (1597) and *Euridice* (1600) were the first examples. Claudio Monteverdi's *L'Orfeo* (1607) was pivotal, introducing structured arias, instrumental sinfonias, and differentiating vocal roles. Early opera was secular, a novelty in a religious-dominated artistic landscape, and spread from Florence to other Italian cities like Mantua and Rome.

### Baroque and National Styles
During the Baroque era (17th–mid-18th century), opera became more ornate and public. Venice led with the first public opera house (1637), making opera a commercial enterprise dependent on public taste. This fostered the bel canto vocal style and the prominence of castrati. In France, Jean-Baptiste Lully adapted opera to French taste (*tragédie en musique*), emphasizing ballet and chorus. The Neapolitan school later introduced a more classicist style, distinguishing between recitativo secco and accompagnato. The late Baroque saw the formal split into opera seria (serious, mythological themes) and opera buffa (comic, popular themes).

### Classical and Gluckian Reform
The Classical period emphasized clarity and balance. Christoph Willibald Gluck initiated a reform to prioritize dramatic truth over vocal display, integrating music, text, and staging more organically. His works, like *Orfeo ed Euridice* (1762), aimed for simplicity and emotional directness. Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart then synthesized these reforms with profound psychological depth and musical brilliance in masterpieces like *Le Nozze di Figaro* (1786) and *Don Giovanni* (1787). Ludwig van Beethoven's sole opera, *Fidelio* (1805), infused the genre with political and moral idealism.

### 19th-Century Romanticism and Nationalism
Romanticism elevated the status of composers and vocalists, reflecting bourgeois tastes. In Italy, the bel canto era peaked with Rossini, Bellini, and Donizetti, focusing on vocal brilliance. Giuseppe Verdi later infused Italian opera with nationalist sentiment and dramatic realism. In Germany, Richard Wagner sought a "total work of art" (*Gesamtkunstwerk*), using leitmotifs and continuous musical drama. The second half of the century saw the rise of national schools in Russia (Mussorgsky, Tchaikovsky), Czech lands (Smetana, Dvořák), and elsewhere, using vernacular languages and folk themes.

### Late 19th to Early 20th Century: Verismo and Beyond
Italian verismo (c. 1890) reacted against romantic idealism, depicting gritty, contemporary life with intense emotion. Mascagni's *Cavalleria rusticana* (1890) and Leoncavallo's *Pagliacci* (1892) were seminal, followed by Giacomo Puccini's melodically rich works like *La Bohème* (1896) and *Tosca* (1900). Simultaneously, French impressionism (Debussy's *Pelléas et Mélisande*, 1902) and German post-Romanticism (Richard Strauss's *Salome*, 1905) expanded harmonic and dramatic language.

### 20th Century: Avant-Garde and Tradition
The 20th century saw radical experimentation alongside traditionalism. Expressionism explored psychological extremes (Berg's *Wozzeck*, 1925). Dodecaphony (Schönberg) and neoclassicism (Stravinsky) offered new structural models. After WWII, composers like Britten revitalized opera in English, while others explored minimalism (Philip Glass) and electronic music. Opera also adapted to new media like radio, film, and television, and the "Three Tenors" phenomenon increased its popular appeal.

## Terms
- **Libretto**: The written text of an opera, including dialogue and lyrics.
- **Recitative**: A style of delivery in operas and oratorios that mimics the rhythms of speech, used to advance the plot.
- **Aria**: A self-contained piece for one voice, with or without orchestral accompaniment, expressing a character's emotions.
- **Bel canto**: A vocal style emphasizing beauty of tone, legato phrasing, and technical agility, prominent in early 19th-century Italian opera.
- **Leitmotif**: A recurring musical theme associated with a particular character, idea, or object, famously used by Richard Wagner.
- **Verismo**: An operatic genre originating in late 19th-century Italy that depicts realistic, often violent stories of ordinary people.
- **Gesamtkunstwerk**: A "total work of art," a concept championed by Wagner that unifies all artistic elements (music, drama, visual arts) into a single, cohesive whole.
- **Basso continuo**: A form of musical accompaniment used in the Baroque era, typically featuring a keyboard instrument (harpsichord) and a bass instrument (cello).
- **Castrato**: A male singer castrated before puberty to retain a high vocal range, prominent in Baroque opera.

## Debates and Open Questions
A central historical debate concerns the relative importance of music versus text in opera. Early theorists like Monteverdi argued "the word must be decisive," while later composers like Mozart stated "poetry must be the obedient servant of music." Wagner sought to fuse all arts into a single entity. Another ongoing discussion involves the balance between vocal virtuosity (as in bel canto) and dramatic expression. The 20th century also sparked debates about the viability of opera as a genre, with avant-garde "anti-opera" challenging traditional forms, while others sought to revive narrative and melody.
