# Sumer

## Overview

Sumer was the earliest known civilization, emerging in southern Mesopotamia (modern Iraq) between the 5th and 4th millennia BC. It gave rise to foundational innovations including cuneiform writing, urban planning, legal codes, and complex irrigation systems, establishing it as one of the world’s six cradles of civilization. Sumerian culture profoundly influenced later Mesopotamian societies and left enduring legacies in language, law, mathematics, and governance.

## Timeline

- **c. 5500–3300 BC** — Initial settlement by West Asian speakers of the Sumerian language
- **c. 4000–2500 BC** — Proto-writing develops; earliest texts appear at Uruk and Jemdet Nasr
- **c. 3500 BC** — Cuneiform writing system emerges
- **c. 2900 BC** — Early Dynastic period begins; first historical king Enmebaragesi
- **c. 2334 BC** — Akkadian Empire founded by Sargon of Akkad
- **c. 2112 BC** — Third Dynasty of Ur established under Ur-Nammu
- **c. 2004 BC** — Fall of Ur III; Amorite rule begins
- **c. 1800 BC** — Rise of Babylon under Hammurabi

## Body

### Origins and Identity

The term "Sumer" derives from the Akkadian name for its non-Semitic, non-Indo-European inhabitants, whose language was a linguistic isolate. In their own tongue, they called their land *Kengir* ("Country of the Noble Lords") and their language *Emegir*. Most scholars believe the Sumerians settled between c. 5500 and 3300 BC, though some propose origins in the Green Sahara or Dilmun (Bahrain). The pre-Sumerian population, known as the Ubaidians or Proto-Euphrateans, developed agriculture, trade, and early urbanism before being absorbed into Sumerian society.

### Archaeological Discovery

Modern understanding of Sumer began in the late 19th century. Jules Oppert first used the term "Sumer" in 1869. Excavations at Girsu (1877), Nippur (1889–1900), and Shuruppak (1902–1903) uncovered vast archives of cuneiform tablets, leading to major scholarly works like *Les Inscriptions de Sumer et d’Akkad* (1905) and *Grundzüge der sumerischen Grammatik* (1923).

### Political Organization and City-States

By the late 4th millennium BC, Sumer was divided into independent city-states such as Ur, Uruk, Lagash, and Kish. Each was centered around a temple dedicated to a patron deity and governed by an *ensi* (priest-governor) or *lugal* (king). These rulers were closely tied to religious rituals and often claimed divine sanction. The Sumerian King List records dynasties and legendary pre-flood rulers, blending myth and history.

### Historical Periods

#### Ubaid and Uruk Periods (c. 6500–3100 BC)

The Ubaid period saw the spread of painted pottery and the rise of temple-centered settlements. The Uruk period marked a transition to mass-produced pottery, large urban centers, and the development of writing. Uruk became the world’s first city with over 50,000 inhabitants. Society was likely theocratic, led by priest-kings (*ensi*) and councils of elders.

#### Early Dynastic Period (c. 2900–2334 BC)

This era witnessed the rise of secular kingship (*lugal*) and increased militarization. Legendary figures like Gilgamesh and Enmerkar appear in texts and inscriptions. The 1st Dynasty of Lagash (c. 2500–2270 BC) produced Eannatum, who created one of history’s first empires and commissioned the Stele of the Vultures depicting warfare.

#### Akkadian Empire (c. 2334–2154 BC)

Founded by Sargon of Akkad, this was the first empire to unify much of Mesopotamia. Akkadian became the administrative language, while Sumerian persisted in scholarly and religious contexts. The empire declined due to Gutian invasions and internal instability.

#### Ur III Period (c. 2112–2004 BC)

The Third Dynasty of Ur briefly restored Sumerian rule under Ur-Nammu and Shulgi. However, Amorite incursions weakened central authority, paving the way for Babylonian ascendancy.

### Culture and Society

Sumerian society was highly stratified, with a rigid hierarchy beneath the king. The Code of Ur-Nammu reflects legal and social norms. Women held significant roles as priestesses and property owners, though their status declined after 2300 BC. Marriage customs emphasized arranged unions and bridal gifts. Sexual attitudes were pragmatic, focusing on ritual purity rather than moral judgment.

### Language and Writing

Sumerian is classified as a language isolate, unrelated to any known family. Its writing system evolved from pictographic symbols to wedge-shaped cuneiform impressed on clay tablets. Thousands of texts survive, covering literature, law, commerce, and science. The Epic of Gilgamesh, composed in cuneiform, stands as the earliest surviving work of literature.

### Religion and Cosmology

Sumerians practiced anthropomorphic polytheism, with each city-state having its own patron deity. Major gods included An (sky), Enlil (storms), Enki (wisdom), and Inanna (love and war). Creation myths emphasized sacred marriages (*hieros gamos*) between divine pairs. The afterlife was envisioned as a dark underworld ruled by Ereshkigal.

### Technology and Innovation

Sumerians pioneered numerous technologies: the wheel, plow, sailboat, and bronze tools. They developed advanced mathematics using a sexagesimal (base-60) system, which influences time measurement today. Their legal codes and bureaucratic systems laid groundwork for future civilizations.

### Economy and Trade

Agriculture relied heavily on irrigation canals, shadufs, and organized labor. Barley and silver served as currency. Long-distance trade connected Sumer to the Indus Valley, Anatolia, and the Persian Gulf. The Sumerians brewed beer, wove textiles, and crafted intricate jewelry and sculptures.

### Decline and Legacy

Soil salinity from irrigation degraded agricultural productivity, contributing to population decline between 2100 and 1700 BC. Political fragmentation followed the fall of Ur III, culminating in Babylonian dominance under Hammurabi. Sumerian survived as a liturgical language until the 1st century AD. Its innovations in writing, law, and urbanism influenced Egypt, Assyria, and beyond.

## Terms

- **Cuneiform**: Wedge-shaped script impressed on clay tablets, developed by the Sumerians.
- **Ensi**: A priestly governor or ruler of a Sumerian city-state.
- **Lugal**: A king or ruler, literally meaning "great man."
- **Ziggurat**: A stepped temple platform common in Sumerian architecture.
- **Me**: Sacred decrees or divine laws governing civilization, attributed to the gods.
- **Hieros Gamos**: A sacred marriage ritual symbolizing the union of male and female deities.
- **Sexagesimal**: A numeral system based on the number 60, used by the Sumerians.
- **Ubaidian**: Pre-Sumerian inhabitants of southern Mesopotamia, associated with the Ubaid culture.
- **Corvée**: Forced labor imposed by the state, often for public works like irrigation.
- **Dilmun**: A trading state identified with modern Bahrain, referenced in Sumerian mythology.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Origins of the Sumerians**: Scholars debate whether they were indigenous to southern Mesopotamia, migrated from the Caucasus, or originated in the Green Sahara or Dilmun.
- **Proto-Euphratean Language**: Some linguists argue for a substrate language beneath Sumerian, while others reject this as speculative.
- **Sacred Prostitution**: Whether temple-based ritual sex existed remains contested among historians.
- **Population Genetics**: While modern Marsh Arabs show genetic affinity, the exact demographic continuity between ancient and contemporary populations is unclear.
- **Chronology**: Disputes persist between short, middle, and long chronologies for dating events like the Akkadian Empire and Ur III period.
