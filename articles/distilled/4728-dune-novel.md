# Dune (novel)

## Overview
*Dune* is a 1965 epic science fiction novel by Frank Herbert, widely regarded as one of the genre's most influential works. It explores complex themes of politics, religion, ecology, and human potential within a feudal interstellar society, centered on the struggle for control of a desert planet and its life-extending spice. Its rich world-building and prescient environmental themes have made it a cultural landmark, inspiring numerous adaptations and sequels.

## Timeline
- **1957** — Frank Herbert researches sand dunes in Oregon, sparking his interest in ecology.
- **1963-64** — The first part of the novel, *Dune World*, is serialized in *Analog* magazine.
- **1965** — The second part, *The Prophet of Dune*, is serialized; the complete novel *Dune* is published by Chilton Books.
- **1966** — *Dune* wins the inaugural Nebula Award and ties for the Hugo Award.
- **1969-85** — Herbert writes five sequels, from *Dune Messiah* to *Chapterhouse: Dune*.
- **1984** — A film adaptation directed by David Lynch is released to negative reviews.
- **1986** — Frank Herbert dies.
- **1999** — Brian Herbert and Kevin J. Anderson begin publishing prequel novels.
- **2000** — A miniseries adaptation airs on the Sci-Fi Channel.
- **2021** — A new film adaptation directed by Denis Villeneuve is released to critical acclaim.
- **2024** — *Dune: Part Two*, the sequel film, is released.

## Body

### Origins and Publication
Herbert's inspiration began in 1957 with research on Oregon's sand dunes and their ecology. He combined this with interests in desert-born religions, messianic figures (influenced by T. E. Lawrence), and feudalism, which he saw as a natural human condition. He also drew from sources like Lesley Blanch's *The Sabres of Paradise* for terminology and themes. After five years of writing, the novel was first published in 1965 by Chilton Books, an auto manual publisher. Initial sales were poor, but it gained popularity through word-of-mouth.

### Plot Summary
The story follows Paul Atreides, whose noble family is given stewardship of the desert planet Arrakis, the sole source of the valuable spice "melange." The spice extends life, enhances mental abilities, and is essential for interstellar travel. The Atreides are betrayed and destroyed in a conspiracy by their rivals, House Harkonnen and the Emperor. Paul escapes into the desert with his mother, Lady Jessica, a member of the Bene Gesserit sisterhood. He is adopted by the native Fremen people, embraces his prophesied messianic role, and ultimately leads a rebellion to seize control of Arrakis and the Empire, but realizes he cannot prevent the ensuing holy war.

### Themes and Influences
Herbert deliberately suppressed advanced technology to focus on human politics and institutions. The novel is a complex blend of "hard" science fiction (ecology, technology) and "soft" science fiction (religion, psychology). Its ecology is pioneering, depicting Arrakis as a complex, interconnected system. The narrative serves as a cautionary tale about charismatic leaders and the dangers of messianic figures, with Herbert stating, "beware of heroes." The Fremen culture and language are heavily influenced by Arabic and Bedouin traditions, and the plot parallels the story of T. E. Lawrence.

### Critical Reception and Legacy
*Dune* is considered a landmark of science fiction, praised by authors like Arthur C. Clarke and Robert A. Heinlein for its depth and originality. It has sold nearly 20 million copies and is regularly cited as one of the best-selling sci-fi novels. Its influence extends to music, games (notably *Dune II*, which helped define the real-time strategy genre), and other media. The names of its planets are used for real features on Saturn's moon Titan.

## Terms
*   **Melange (Spice):** An enormously valuable drug found only on Arrakis that extends life, enhances mental abilities, and is essential for space navigation.
*   **Bene Gesserit:** An exclusively female group with advanced physical and mental abilities who pursue a secret, multi-generational breeding program.
*   **Kwisatz Haderach:** The ultimate goal of the Bene Gesserit breeding program: a male who can access both male and female genetic memories and see the future.
*   **Mentat:** A human trained to perform complex mental calculations and data processing, serving as a "human computer" in a universe where such technology is forbidden.
*   **Fremen:** The fierce, desert-adapted native people of Arrakis who ride giant sandworms and have a culture centered on water conservation.
*   **Spacing Guild:** An organization whose Navigators use melange to gain limited prescience, enabling safe interstellar travel.
*   **Sardaukar:** The Emperor's elite, ferocious soldiers, trained on a harsh prison planet.
*   **Landsraad:** The council of the Great Houses of the feudal interstellar empire.
*   **Shai-hulud:** The Fremen name for the giant sandworms of Arrakis, which are central to their ecology and religion.
*   **Gom jabbar:** A poisoned needle used by the Bene Gesserit in a test of human self-control.

## Debates and Open questions
*   **Adaptation Difficulty:** *Dune* has been called "unfilmable" due to its complex plot, internal monologues, and vast world-building, leading to a history of troubled film adaptations.
*   **Cultural and Religious Influence:** Scholars debate the extent and nature of the novel's influences, particularly its use of Arabic language and Islamic concepts, and whether it constitutes a critique or an appropriation.
*   **Heroism and Leadership:** A central debate concerns the novel's message about charismatic leaders. While Herbert intended it as a warning ("beware of heroes"), some readers interpret Paul Atreides's rise as a straightforward heroic fantasy.
*   **Ecological Message:** The novel is celebrated as a pioneering work of ecological fiction, but discussions continue about whether its primary focus is environmentalism or a broader political and philosophical allegory.
