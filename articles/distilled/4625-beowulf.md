# Beowulf

## Overview
*Beowulf* is an Old English epic poem of 3,182 alliterative lines, preserved in a single manuscript (the Nowell Codex) from c. 975–1025 AD. It is the most important work of Old English literature, telling the story of the hero Beowulf in pagan Scandinavia. Its composition date is fiercely debated, and its blend of heroic legend, Christian elements, and historical figures makes it a foundational text for understanding early medieval culture and literary transmission.

## Timeline
- **c. 521 AD** — Raid by King Hygelac into Frisia, a historical event mentioned in the poem and by Gregory of Tours.
- **5th–6th centuries** — The poem's narrative is set during this period, featuring historical Scandinavian figures.
- **c. 975–1025 AD** — The Nowell Codex manuscript, containing *Beowulf*, is produced.
- **1731** — The manuscript is damaged in a fire at Ashburnham House, London.
- **1786** — Grímur Jónsson Thorkelin makes the first transcriptions of the manuscript.
- **1805** — Sharon Turner publishes the first modern English translation of selected verses.
- **1922** — Frederick Klaeber publishes his influential scholarly edition of the poem.
- **1936** — J.R.R. Tolkien delivers his landmark lecture "Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics."
- **1999** — Kevin Kiernan's *Electronic Beowulf* (digital edition) is first published.
- **2014** — Tolkien's prose translation of *Beowulf* is published posthumously.

## Body

### Narrative and Structure
The poem is set in pagan Scandinavia. The hero Beowulf travels to Denmark to aid King Hrothgar, whose mead hall Heorot is terrorized by the monster Grendel. Beowulf kills Grendel and then Grendel's mother. He later becomes king of his own people, the Geats. Fifty years later, he slays a dragon but is mortally wounded. The poem begins and ends with funerals and is tightly structured, with digressions that provide historical depth and parallel the main action. It begins *in medias res* (in the middle of things).

### Manuscript and Dialect
The sole surviving manuscript is the Nowell Codex (British Library, Cotton Vitellius A.XV). It was written by two scribes; the first regularized spelling into West Saxon, while the second preserved more archaic dialect forms. The poem is predominantly in the Late West Saxon dialect but contains many other dialectal forms, suggesting a long and complex transmission history across England.

### Date, Authorship, and Oral Tradition
The manuscript dates to c. 975–1025, but the poem's composition is debated. J.R.R. Tolkien argued for an 8th-century date, supported by linguistic evidence like Kaluza's law (a metrical rule about vowel length). Others propose a 9th-century date linked to King Alfred's court. The question of whether *Beowulf* was transmitted orally before being written down is central to its interpretation. Scholar Albert Lord argued it was composed orally, but later scholars like Thomas Gardner and John Miles Foley contend the text is too varied to be purely formulaic, suggesting a blend of oral and literate traditions.

### Sources and Analogues
No definitive sources exist. Proposed parallels include Scandinavian works like *Grettis saga* and the story of Hrolf Kraki, and international folktales like the "Bear's Son Tale" (ATU type 301) and the Irish "The Hand and the Child." Biblical influences are clear, with allusions to Genesis, Exodus, and Daniel. Classical influence from Homer or Virgil is debated but considered less direct.

### Interpretation and Criticism
Modern criticism began with Tolkien's 1936 lecture, which refocused attention on the poem's literary value over historical speculation. A central debate is the relationship between the poem's pagan setting and its Christian Anglo-Saxon scribes. Critics like Fred C. Robinson see a Christian poet commenting on paganism, while others, like Ursula Schaefer, argue the poem exists within both horizons simultaneously. The poem is also analyzed as a study of heroic society, feuding, and dynastic politics.

## Terms
- **Alliterative verse**: A poetic form where the first half of a line is linked to the second by similarity in initial sound.
- **Nowell Codex**: The single 11th-century manuscript that contains *Beowulf*.
- **Kaluza's law**: A metrical principle concerning vowel length in unstressed syllables, used to argue for an early composition date.
- **Scop**: An Anglo-Saxon court poet or singer.
- **Thegn**: A lord's retainer or servant in Anglo-Saxon society.
- **Kenning**: A compound metaphorical phrase (e.g., "whale-road" for sea) used in Old English poetry.
- **In medias res**: A narrative technique that begins the story in the middle of the action.
- **Elegy**: A poem of serious reflection, typically a lament for the dead; Tolkien argued *Beowulf* is closer to this than an epic.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Date of Composition**: Is the poem an 8th-century work, nearly contemporary with its 11th-century manuscript, or based on an earlier oral version?
- **Oral vs. Literate Composition**: Was the poem created through oral-formulaic performance or by a literate, Christian author?
- **Paganism vs. Christianity**: Is the poem a pagan work with Christian elements added, or a Christian composition set in a pagan past? How should its religious perspective be interpreted?
- **Sources**: Are there direct literary sources (Scandinavian, classical, or biblical), or is the poem a unique synthesis of traditional material?
