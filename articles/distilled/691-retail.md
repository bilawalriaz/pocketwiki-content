# Retail

## Overview
Retail is the sale of goods and services directly to end-users (consumers), distinct from wholesaling which targets business customers. It serves as the final link in the supply chain, purchasing in bulk and selling in small quantities for profit. The sector is a major employer and economic driver, evolving from ancient itinerant peddlers and marketplaces to modern omnichannel operations integrating physical stores, e-commerce, and digital technologies like AI and big data. Retail strategy balances procurement, the "6 Ps" marketing mix (product, place, promotion, price, personnel, presentation), and pricing tactics to build long-term customer relationships and optimize the shopping experience.

## Timeline
- **>10,000 years ago** — Archaeological evidence of trade, likely barter systems.
- **7th-millennium BCE** — Retail trade with coinage emerges in Asia Minor (modern Turkey).
- **200 BCE** — Chinese packaging and branding signal family, place, and product quality.
- **600–900 CE** — Government-imposed product branding used in China.
- **960–1127 (Song Dynasty)** — China develops consumerist culture accessible to ordinary consumers.
- **13th century** — Permanent shops emerge in populous European cities.
- **1455** — Construction begins on the Grand Bazaar in Istanbul (oldest continuously operating market).
- **15th century** — Mexica (Aztec) market of Tlatelolco is the largest in the Americas.
- **17th century** — Permanent shops with regular hours supplant markets/fairs as main retail outlets.
- **Late 18th century** — Grand shopping arcades (covered, multi-vendor spaces) emerge in Europe.
- **Mid-19th century** — Department stores emerge, reshaping habits and redefining service/luxury; mail order retail expands.
- **18th century** — Josiah Wedgwood devises money-back guarantee and "buy one get one free" tactics.
- **1954** — First modern shopping mall (Northland Mall) opens near Detroit, designed by Victor Gruen.
- **1991–2000** — Average U.S. supermarket size grows from 31,000 to 44,000 sq ft.
- **2012** — ~70% of U.S. retail workers are part-time (cited estimate).
- **2016** — China becomes the world's largest retail market.
- **2018** — Global shopping tourism market valued at ~$1.2 trillion (projected 6.7% CAGR through 2023). |
- **21st century** — "Retail apocalypse": large stores reduce footprint or close due to online competition and debt; omnichannel/automation (e.g., cashierless) expands.

## Body

### Etymology and Legal Definition
The word "retail" derives from Old French *retaillier* ("to shape by cutting," c. 1365), first recorded as a noun in 1433 meaning "a sale in small quantities." Legally, retail is selling directly to consumers; some jurisdictions require ≥80% of sales to end-users. Sales to businesses are "non-retail activity." A store selling mostly to consumers is a retailer regardless of "wholesale outlet" branding. The core distinction: wholesale is business-to-business; retail is business-to-consumer. The term also applies to service providers (banking, tourism, healthcare, education) selling standardized services to many small customers.

### Retail Strategy and Procurement
Strategy distinguishes **strategic planning** (long-term competitive positioning, reviewed every 3–5 years by the CEO) from **managerial decision-making** (implementation). Strategic planning requires an **environmental scan** of competitive, market, economic, and statutory-political trends. Analysis covers market size/attractiveness, customer segmentation (demographics, psychographics, habits), internal capabilities (HR, tech, financial, scale economies), competition (substitutes, perceptual mapping), product mix (sales/sq ft, stock-turn, profitability), distribution channels (lead times, cost efficiency), and cost-benefit economics. Research links store positioning to customer socio-economic status and strategy/service quality to customer loyalty. Procurement—obtaining goods in needed quantities and placing them for purchase—is central to strategy; profit margins depend on achieving market-competitive transaction costs.

### Retail Marketing Mix (The 6 Ps)
Day-to-day tactics use the **retail marketing mix**, expanding the traditional 4 Ps with **Personnel** and **Presentation** (physical evidence) as key differentiators.
*   **Product:** Assortment breadth/depth, brands, service level (self-service to full-service), and support services (credit, delivery, after-sales).
*   **Place:** Location, space utilization, operating hours. Macro factors (demographics, infrastructure) and micro factors (parking, delivery access) guide site selection. A major trend is **multichannel/omnichannel retailing** (e.g., buy online, pick up in-store) to counter online disruption. Online shoppers initially prefer familiar retailers but become less loyal with experience.
*   **Promotion:** Communication tactics to drive traffic and sales.
*   **Price:** Strategy (operations-, revenue-, customer-, value-, relationship-, or socially-oriented) sets the broad approach; tactics (discounts, everyday low pricing, high-low, loss leaders, bundling, psychological pricing) achieve short-term goals. Payment modes (cash, credit, EFTPOS, lay-by) carry handling costs. Price is *not* the top factor for most consumers.
*   **Personnel:** Labor needs vary by season/time; flexible scheduling is common (70% U.S. part-time in 2012). Staff contribute to service quality and differentiation.
*   **Presentation (Physical Evidence/Retail Servicescape):** Store exterior/interior, layout, lighting, music, signage, uniforms, website. The **decompression zone** (store entrance) is critical. Design aims to increase dwell time balanced against convenience. Ambient conditions (music, temperature) target the store's market.

### Shopper Profiles and Motivations
Research identifies two core motives: **utilitarian** (task-related, rational, efficiency-seeking) and **hedonic** (pleasure, escapism, experiential involvement). Sproles and Kendall’s (mid-1980s) cross-culturally stable typology segments consumers by decision-making style:
*   **Quality conscious/Perfectionist:** Systematic comparison for best quality.
*   **Brand conscious:** Prefers expensive/designer labels; equates price with quality.
*   **Recreation conscious/Hedonistic:** Enjoys shopping as entertainment.
*   **Price conscious:** Seeks value, sales, discounts.
*   **Novelty/Fashion conscious:** Seeks excitement, trends, variety.
*   **Impulsive:** Spur-of-the-moment, unconcerned with value/expenditure.
*   **Confused (by overchoice):** Information overload from too many options.
*   **Habitual/Brand loyal:** Routine purchases, minimal evaluation.
These stable styles aid market segmentation.

### Pricing Tactics and Sales Techniques
Broad strategy is set centrally (e.g., head office for chains). Common tactics: discount pricing, everyday low prices, high-low pricing, loss leaders, bundling, promotional pricing, psychological pricing. Josiah Wedgwood (18th century) pioneered money-back guarantees and "buy one get one free." Retailers also use add-on/upsell/cross-sell, value-based selling, and timing closes. Real estate costs and theft increase prices; zoning can reduce competition.

### Retail Formats and Classification
**Retail formats** (formulas) shape consumer choice and expectations.
*   **By product:** *Softline* (consumables <3 years: clothing, cosmetics, stationery); *Grocery* (supermarkets, hypermarkets, convenience stores: food + household consumables); *Hardline* (durables: appliances, furniture, autos); *Specialist* (niche: bookstores, galleries, gift shops).
*   **By ownership:** Small family-run stores persist globally, but large chains dominate via buying power and private labels, shifting power from wholesalers to retailers.
*   **By structure:** Shopping arcades (covered, multi-vendor, 18th c.), department stores (mid-19th c., leisure + retail), shopping malls (planned, self-contained, 1954 onward), "mega-stores"/warehouse stores (late 20th c.). U.S. retail space per capita is nearly double Europe's.
*   **Home shopping:** Catalog/mail order, TV/radio response, online shopping.

### Impact of Technology
Beyond e-commerce, **big data, AI, computer vision, and IoT** transform the entire journey (browsing to checkout). Retailers use data to: (1) personalize marketing/customer experience, (2) optimize supply chains, (3) dynamically adjust prices for profit. **Shopping tourism** is significant ($1.2T in 2018, 6.7% CAGR projected). Post-COVID evolution analyzed in *Leading Travel and Tourism Retail* (2023).

### Historical Evolution
*   **Ancient:** Barter > coinage (Asia Minor, 7th-millennium BCE). Greece: *agora* (open space, mats/stalls). Rome: *forum* (earliest permanent shop-fronts). China: Early branding/packaging (200 BCE), state branding (600–900 CE), Song Dynasty consumerism (960–1127).
*   **Medieval Europe:** Few permanent shops; customers visited tradesmen's workshops. Markets/fairs dominated outside cities. Grand Bazaar (Istanbul, 1455). Aztec Tlatelolco (15th c., largest in Americas).
*   **Early Modern (17th–18th c.):** Permanent shops with regular hours replace markets. Store interiors evolve (counters, display cases, browsing). Shopping arcades emerge (glass roofs, bourgeoisie). Co-operative stores serve working poor.
*   **Modern (Industrial Revolution–21st c.):** Department stores (mid-19th c.) redefine service/luxury/leisure. Mail order expands with transport/postal improvements. Victor Gruen’s mall concept (1954) creates comfortable, time-extending environments. Late 20th c.: Footprint growth (supermarkets +42% 1991–2000; mega-stores).
*   **21st Century:** Pressure from online sales shrinks store sizes. **Retail apocalypse** (esp. North America): sharp store reductions/closures due to digital competition and debt. Rise of omnichannel (BOPIS) and automation (cashierless).

## Terms
- ****Retail**** — Sale of goods/services in small quantities directly to end-users (consumers), distinct from wholesaling (B2B).
- ****Wholesaling**** — Sale of goods in large quantities to businesses/institutions for resale or operational use.
- ****Omnichannel Retailing**** — Integrated model combining physical stores, online, mobile, and other channels (e.g., buy online, pick up in-store).
- ****Retail Marketing Mix (6 Ps)**** — Tactical framework: Product, Place, Promotion, Price, Personnel, Presentation (Physical Evidence).
- ****Retail Servicescape**** — The physical environment of the retail encounter (layout, lighting, music, signage, aromas, signage).
- ****Decompression Zone**** — The store entrance area where customers transition from outside; critical for first impressions and slowing pace.
- ****Utilitarian Motivation**** — Shopping as a rational, task-oriented errand to be completed efficiently.
- ****Hedonic Motivation**** — Shopping for pleasure, escapism, and experiential enjoyment.
- ****Private Labels**** — Brands owned by the retailer (not manufacturer), competing alongside national brands.
- ****Retail Apocalypse**** — Early 21st-century wave of store closures/bankruptcies driven by e-commerce competition and debt.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Format Definition:** Scholars debate whether **Retail Format** (retail formula) should be a 7th "P" in the marketing mix, alongside Product, Place, Promotion, Price, Personnel, and Presentation.
*   **Store Footprint Future:** The long-term trajectory of physical store size is contested; U.S. space per capita is ~2x Europe's, but 21st-century data shows reductions ("retail apocalypse") while omnichannel models require physical footprints for fulfillment.
*   **Online Loyalty Dynamics:** Research indicates online shoppers start loyal to known retailers but switch more readily as they gain experience; the long-term equilibrium for digital loyalty remains unresolved.
*   **Technology Integration:** The extent to which AI, computer vision, and IoT will automate core functions (checkout, inventory, personalization) versus augment human labor is an open strategic question.
*   **Legal Thresholds:** Jurisdictions vary on the % of consumer sales required to legally define "retail" (commonly 80%), creating classification ambiguity for hybrid B2B/B2C models.
