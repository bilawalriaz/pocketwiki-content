# Mary, mother of Jesus

## Overview
Mary of Nazareth was a first-century Jewish woman, wife of Joseph and mother of Jesus, who holds a uniquely exalted position across Christianity, Islam, and the Baháʼí and Druze faiths. In Christianity, she is venerated as the Theotokos ("God-bearer" or Mother of God), a title dogmatically defined at the Council of Ephesus (431) to affirm Christ's unified divine and human natures. While all major Christian traditions honor her as "blessed among women," they diverge sharply on Marian dogmas: Catholics and some Orthodox define her Immaculate Conception (conception without original sin) and bodily Assumption into heaven, while most Protestants reject these as unbiblical. In Islam, Maryam is the only woman named in the Quran, honored as the greatest woman in history and mother of the prophet Isa (Jesus), conceived miraculously by God's command. Her veneration has shaped liturgy, art, and pilgrimage for two millennia, making her the most prominent female figure in Abrahamic religions.

## Timeline
- **c. 66–90 AD** — Synoptic Gospels and Acts of the Apostles written; primary historical sources for Mary's life.
- **c. 150–160 AD** — Justin Martyr articulates Mary as the "New Eve," establishing a foundational theological typology.
- **c. 182 AD** — Irenaeus of Lyon develops the Eve-Mary parallel in *Against Heresies*, linking Mary's obedience to salvation.
- **3rd century** — Earliest known Marian prayer, *Sub tuum praesidium* ("Beneath Thy Protection"), composed (c. 270).
- **431 AD** — Council of Ephesus defines Mary as **Theotokos** (God-bearer), rejecting Nestorianism.
- **553 AD** — Second Council of Constantinople confirms Mary as **Aeiparthenos** (Ever-Virgin).
- **787 AD** — Second Council of Nicaea authorizes veneration of icons, establishing the theology of *hyperdulia* (veneration due to Mary) distinct from *latria* (worship due to God).
- **1854 AD** — Pope Pius IX defines the **Immaculate Conception** as Catholic dogma (*Ineffabilis Deus*).
- **1950 AD** — Pope Pius XII defines the **Assumption** of Mary as Catholic dogma (*Munificentissimus Deus*).
- **1964 AD** — Second Vatican Council issues *Lumen Gentium*, Chapter 8, integrating Marian doctrine into ecclesiology.

## Body

### Names and Titles
Mary's Hebrew name, *Miryam* (Maryam/Mariam), appears in the New Testament Greek as *María* and *Mariám*. In Christianity, her primary titles reflect theological doctrines: **Theotokos** ("God-bearer," defined at Ephesus 431), **Aeiparthenos** ("Ever-Virgin," confirmed 553), and **Panagia** ("All-Holy"). Catholics use a vast array of titles (e.g., *Immaculate Conception*, *Queen of Heaven*, *Our Lady*), many derived from the *Litany of Loreto*. The title "Queen of Heaven" (*Regina caeli*) had pre-Christian usage for goddesses like Isis and Ishtar. In Islam, she is **Maryam**, honored as *Sayyidatuna* ("Our Lady"), *Siddiqah* ("Truthful One"), *Qānitah* ("Devoutly Obedient"), and *Tahira* ("Purified One"—one of only two humans, with Jesus, never touched by Satan). The Quran identifies her as the "daughter of Imran" and "sister of Aaron," the latter understood metaphorically as a link to the biblical Miriam.

### Life in Ancient Sources
**New Testament:** The earliest reference is Galatians 4:4 ("born of a woman," c. 50s AD). The infancy narratives in Matthew and Luke (c. 80–90 AD) describe the **Annunciation** (Gabriel announces virginal conception by the Holy Spirit), the **Nativity** in Bethlehem (c. 6–4 BC), the Presentation at the Temple (40 days post-birth), the Flight to Egypt, and the finding of Jesus in the Temple at age 12. Mary appears at the Wedding at Cana (John 2, where Jesus addresses her as "Woman"—a respectful term in Koine Greek), at the Crucifixion (entrusted to the Beloved Disciple), and in the upper room at Pentecost (Acts 1:14). Her death is unrecorded in scripture.

**Genealogy:** Matthew traces Jesus' legal descent through Joseph (Davidic line). Luke 1:36 identifies Mary as a relative of Elizabeth (Levitical/Aaronic line). Some scholars propose Luke's genealogy (through Nathan) is Mary's, making her Davidic by blood.

**Brothers of Jesus:** The Gospels name James, Joses, Judas, and Simon as Jesus' "brothers" (*adelphoi*). Three historic interpretations exist: (1) **Epiphanian view** (Eastern Orthodox): Joseph's sons from a prior marriage; (2) **Hieronymian view** (Roman Catholic): Cousins, children of Mary's sister (Mary of Clopas); (3) **Helvidian view** (Protestant majority): Full biological siblings born after Jesus.

**Apocryphal Accounts:** The *Protoevangelium of James* (c. 150 AD) names her parents Joachim and Anne, describes her Temple consecration at age three, betrothal to Joseph at 12–14, and emphasizes her perpetual virginity. *Life of the Virgin* (attributed to Maximus the Confessor, 7th c.) portrays her as a leader in the early Church. Hyppolitus of Thebes (7th/8th c.) claims she died in 41 AD, 11 years post-Crucifixion.

### Religious Perspectives

#### Catholic
The Catholic Church defines four Marian dogmas: **Mother of God** (Theotokos), **Perpetual Virginity**, **Immaculate Conception** (preserved from original sin from conception, defined 1854), and **Assumption** (bodily taken to heaven, defined 1950). Mary is *hyperdulia* (highest veneration), distinct from *latria* (worship). She is Mediatrix of all graces and Co-Redemptrix (participating in salvation), though the latter is not dogma. Devotions include the Rosary, Scapular, Miraculous Medal, and Marian months (May, October). Popes John Paul II and Benedict XVI emphasized a "Marian Christology": "To return to Christ, go through Mary."

#### Eastern Orthodox
Orthodoxy venerates Mary as **Theotokos**, "more honorable than the Cherubim." Her role is **doxological** (expressed in hymnography, icons, liturgy) rather than academic dogma. Five of twelve Great Feasts are Marian. The *Protoevangelium of James* informs traditions of her Temple upbringing. Orthodoxy **rejects the Immaculate Conception** because its theology of *ancestral sin* differs from the Western Augustinian *original sin*; Mary needed redemption but was purified at the Annunciation. They celebrate the **Dormition** ("falling asleep") and bodily Assumption, but without a dogmatic definition on whether death preceded it. Jesus' "brothers" are Joseph's children by a prior marriage.

#### Protestant
**Lutheran:** The *Formula of Concord* teaches Virgin Birth, Theotokos, and Perpetual Virginity. Luther initially held the Immaculate Conception and Assumption but later questioned the latter's biblical basis; he rejected intercessory invocation as idolatry. **Anglican:** Views range from Anglo-Catholic (Rosary, Assumption, Immaculate Conception) to Reformed (rejecting Marian feasts). The 2005 "Seattle Statement" (*Mary: Grace and Hope in Christ*) marked ecumenical convergence. **Methodist:** Accepts Virgin Birth and Perpetual Virginity (per Wesley); rejects Immaculate Conception; Assumption is a pious opinion. **General:** Most Protestants honor Mary as a model disciple but reject veneration, intercession, and defined dogmas beyond scripture.

#### Nontrinitarian
Unitarians, Christadelphians, Jehovah's Witnesses, and Latter-day Saints acknowledge Mary as Jesus' biological mother but reject "Mother of God" and Immaculate Conception. LDS scripture calls her "most beautiful and fair" and a "chosen vessel," affirming virgin birth but viewing Christ as a separate divine being from the Father.

#### Jewish
The Talmud does not name Mary; the *Toledot Yeshu* (medieval, post-4th c.) contains polemical tales (e.g., Jesus as son of a soldier *Panthera*—likely a distortion of *parthenos*, "virgin"). Scholars consider it historically worthless but influential in shaping negative medieval Jewish perceptions.

#### Islamic
Maryam holds the highest female status in Islam. Quran 3:42: "Allah has selected you, purified you, and chosen you over all women." She is the only woman named in the Quran (50 references); Surah 19 (*Maryam*) details the Annunciation and virgin birth. Islamic tradition holds Mary and Jesus were the only humans never touched by Satan. The Quranic Annunciation narrative closely parallels Luke 1. Shabbir Akhtar notes compatibility between Islamic and Catholic views on Mary's purity.

#### Druze and Baháʼí
Druze revere *Sayyida Maryam* as holy and pure, sharing pilgrimage sites with Christians (e.g., Our Lady of Lebanon, Saidnaya). Baháʼí writings (*Kitáb-i-Íqán*) venerate her as "that most beauteous countenance" and affirm the "mystery of the Immaculacy."

### Christian Devotions

#### History
Marian piety begins in the 2nd century (Justin Martyr, Irenaeus: Mary as New Eve). The *Protoevangelium of James* (c. 150) marks the emergence of Mary as a figure of devotion. The earliest prayer, *Sub tuum praesidium* (c. 270), seeks her protection. The 4th century sees liturgical feasts and church dedications (Santa Maria Maggiore, Rome). The **Collyridian** heresy (Arabia, 300s) worshipped Mary with bread offerings—condemned by Epiphanius. In Byzantium, Mary replaced Artemis at Ephesus; Queen Theodora (6th c.) promoted her cult. The Middle Ages (12th c. onward) saw explosive growth in legends, feasts, and the title *Mediatrix*.

#### Feasts
Originating from the Nativity cycle: **Purification/Presentation** (Feb 2, 40 days post-birth, 5th c.), **Annunciation** (Mar 25), **Assumption/Dormition** (Aug 15/28), **Nativity of Mary** (Sep 8). Eastern Orthodoxy has four additional 7th/8th c. feasts. Catholics have the most numerous feasts, including *Our Lady of Victory* (Lepanto, 1571). Protestants generally do not observe Marian feasts.

#### Relics
Since the Assumption/Dormition implies no bodily remains, relics are limited to hair, milk, and clothing (Cincture at Vatopedi, Girdle at Homs, veil/robe at Blachernae). Calvin (1543) mocked the volume of "Holy Milk" as physically impossible. The *tilma* of Juan Diego (Our Lady of Guadalupe, 1531) is a unique contact relic. Pilgrimage sites include the *Santa Casa* (Loreto), the House of the Virgin Mary (Ephesus, based on Anne Catherine Emmerich's 19th c. visions), and the Tomb of the Virgin (Jerusalem).

### In Arts
**Iconography:** Blue robes originate in Byzantium (c. 500 AD; blue = imperial color) and medieval Europe (lapis lazuli > gold in value, signaling patronage devotion). Key motifs: *Hodegetria* (She who shows the Way), *Eleusa* (Tenderness), *Orans* (Praying), *Stabat Mater* (at the Cross), *Pietà* (cradling dead Christ). Ethiopian tradition (1600s+) produced thousands of miracle paintings (healing, feeding, rescuing). **Music:** Major settings include Monteverdi's *Vespro della Beata Vergine* (1610), Bach's *Magnificat* (1723), Schubert's and Gounod's *Ave Maria*, Tavener's *Mother and Child* (2002).

## Terms
- ****Theotokos**** — Greek: "God-bearer" or "Mother of God"; title defined at Council of Ephesus (431) affirming Christ's single divine-human personhood.
- ****Aeiparthenos**** — Greek: "Ever-Virgin"; title confirmed at Second Council of Constantinople (553) asserting Mary's virginity before, during, and after birth.
- ****Immaculate Conception**** — Catholic dogma (1854): Mary was preserved from original sin from the moment of her conception. Distinct from the Virgin Birth of Jesus.
- ****Assumption / Dormition**** — Assumption (Catholic dogma, 1950): Mary's body taken to heaven. Dormition (Eastern): her "falling asleep" (death) followed by bodily translation.
- ****Hyperdulia**** — The highest degree of veneration (*dulia*) reserved for Mary; distinct from *latria* (worship of God). Defined at Second Nicaea (787).
- ****Protoevangelium of James**** — 2nd-century apocryphal gospel; source for Mary's parents (Joachim/Anne), Temple upbringing, and perpetual virginity; foundational for Orthodox Marian tradition.
- ****Magnificat**** — Mary's canticle of praise (Luke 1:46–55), spoken during the Visitation; central to Christian liturgy (Vespers).
- ****Sub tuum praesidium**** — Earliest known Marian prayer (c. 270 AD): "Beneath Thy Protection"; found on Egyptian papyrus.
- ****Mediatrix**** — Title/role: Mary as mediator of graces from Christ to humanity; Catholic theological concept, not dogmatically defined as *Co-Redemptrix*.
- ****Panagia**** — Greek: "All-Holy"; primary Orthodox title for Mary, used in liturgy and icon inscriptions (often abbreviated ΜΡ ΘΥ).

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historicity of the Infancy Narratives:** Scholars debate the historical reliability of Matthew 1–2 and Luke 1–2, noting theological shaping (e.g., Bethlehem birth to fulfill Micah 5:2) and legendary accretion.
- **Perpetual Virginity vs. "Brothers of Jesus":** The meaning of *adelphoi* (brothers/cousins/step-brothers) and Matthew 1:25 ("until she bore a son") remains contested; the Helvidian (siblings), Epiphanian (step-siblings), and Hieronymian (cousins) views reflect deeper ecclesiological divides.
- **Immaculate Conception:** Rejected by Orthodoxy and Protestants due to differing hamartiology (doctrine of sin); Catholics define it as a privilege of grace *through* Christ's merits, anticipating the Cross.
- **Assumption/Dormition Details:** Whether Mary died (Dormition) or was assumed alive is not dogmatically settled in Catholicism (*Munificentissimus Deus* leaves it open); Orthodoxy generally affirms death.
- **Mary as Co-Redemptrix / Mediatrix of All Graces:** A proposed fifth Catholic dogma; debated for its implications on Christ's sole mediatorship (1 Tim 2:5).
- **Quranic "Sister of Aaron":** Whether this denotes literal kinship, tribal affiliation, or typological identification with Miriam (Moses' sister) is debated; a Hadith clarifies it as metaphorical naming.
- **House of Mary in Ephesus:** Based on 19th-century visions of Anne Catherine Emmerich; archaeological support is lacking, but it is a major Catholic pilgrimage site.
- **Collyridianism:** The 4th-century Arabian sect offering *bread* to Mary raises questions about early boundaries between veneration and worship.
