# Wilhelm I

## Overview
Wilhelm I (1797–1888) was the first head of state of a united Germany, serving as King of Prussia from 1861 and German Emperor from 1871. His reign, dominated by his partnership with Chancellor Otto von Bismarck, saw the military unification of Germany through wars against Denmark, Austria, and France, establishing the German Empire and Prussia's dominance in European politics.

## Timeline
- **22 March 1797** — Born in Berlin as the second son of Prince Frederick William and Louise of Mecklenburg-Strelitz.
- **1814–15** — Fought with distinction in the Napoleonic Wars (War of the Sixth Coalition).
- **7 June 1840** — Became heir presumptive when his childless elder brother, Frederick William IV, became King of Prussia.
- **1848** — Crushed the Revolutions of 1848 in Berlin; earned the nickname "Prince of Grapeshot" and was briefly exiled to England.
- **1858** — Formally named Prince Regent for his incapacitated brother, Frederick William IV.
- **2 January 1861** — Ascended to the Prussian throne upon his brother's death.
- **1864** — Commanded Prussian forces in the Second Schleswig War against Denmark.
- **1866** — Commanded Prussian forces in the Austro-Prussian War, culminating in the decisive victory at Königgrätz.
- **18 January 1871** — Proclaimed German Emperor in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles.
- **11 May 1878** — Survived the first assassination attempt by anarchist Max Hödel.
- **2 June 1878** — Severely wounded in a second assassination attempt by Karl Nobiling.
- **9 March 1888** — Died in Berlin at age 90, succeeded by his son Frederick III.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Career
Born the second son, Wilhelm was not expected to rule. He was educated by Johann Friedrich Gottlieb Delbrück and entered the Prussian army at age twelve. He fought bravely against Napoleon in the War of the Sixth Coalition, earning the Iron Cross, and later commanded troops at Waterloo. A lifelong antipathy towards France resulted from these experiences. He became a prominent army spokesman, advocating for a strong, well-trained military. In 1829, he married Princess Augusta of Saxe-Weimar-Eisenach in a marriage that was outwardly stable but not happy.

### Regency and Accession
In 1840, Wilhelm became heir presumptive to his childless brother, King Frederick William IV. During the Revolutions of 1848, he brutally suppressed a revolt in Berlin, forcing him into temporary exile in England. He later commanded the army to crush an uprising in Baden. In 1857, his brother suffered a stroke, and Wilhelm became Prince Regent in 1858, initiating a "New Era" by appointing a liberal Minister President. He ascended to the throne in 1861, immediately clashing with the liberal Landtag (parliament) over military budgets.

### King of Prussia and Unification of Germany
The conflict over military funding led Wilhelm, on the advice of his Minister of War Albrecht von Roon, to appoint Otto von Bismarck as Minister President in 1862. Bismarck, responsible solely to the king, forced through the military reforms. Under Wilhelm's nominal command, Prussia won the Second Schleswig War (1864) and the Austro-Prussian War (1866), establishing Prussia as the leading German power. After the Austro-Prussian War, Bismarck dissuaded Wilhelm from marching on Vienna, preferring a quick peace that allowed for a future alliance with Austria. In 1867, Wilhelm became president of the newly formed North German Confederation.

### German Emperor
Following Prussia's victory in the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71), the German states unified. Wilhelm reluctantly accepted the title "German Emperor" (Deutscher Kaiser) rather than "Emperor of Germany," fearing it would overshadow his Prussian kingship. He was proclaimed emperor on 18 January 1871 at Versailles. Despite his considerable constitutional power, Wilhelm largely delegated governance to Bismarck, focusing on ceremonial duties. His reign saw the passage of the Anti-Socialist Laws in 1878, justified by two failed assassination attempts against him, and the formalization of the Scramble for Africa at the Berlin Conference (1884–85).

### Later Years and Death
Wilhelm remained popular in his later years as a symbol of "old Prussia." He died in 1888, the "Year of the Three Emperors," after a short illness. He was succeeded by his son Frederick III, who died just 99 days later, passing the throne to Wilhelm's grandson, Wilhelm II.

## Terms
- **Landtag**: The parliament of Prussia, with which Wilhelm clashed over military budgets.
- **Minister President**: The head of government in Prussia; Bismarck used this post to direct policy, answerable only to the king.
- **Prince Regent**: A person who rules in place of a monarch who is incapacitated or a minor; Wilhelm served as regent for his brother from 1858.
- **North German Confederation**: A federation of German states under Prussian leadership created in 1867, a precursor to the German Empire.
- **Bundespräsidium**: The hereditary presidency of the North German Confederation, held by the King of Prussia.
- **Anti-Socialist Laws**: Laws passed in 1878 that banned socialist and working-class organizations, used to suppress political opposition.
- **Reichstag**: The parliament of the German Empire.
- **Dual Alliance (Zweibund)**: A secret treaty of 1879 between Germany and Austria-Hungary, directed against Russia.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent of Wilhelm's personal power versus his reliance on Bismarck is debated; while he had constitutional authority, he often acquiesced to his chancellor, who sometimes threatened resignation to get his way.
- The personal relationship between Wilhelm and Bismarck is characterized as that of a "vassal to his feudal superior" by Bismarck, but it was Bismarck who effectively directed state affairs.
- The significance of Wilhelm's coronation date (18 January) is noted as symbolically linking the new empire to the first Prussian coronation in 1701, though it had no constitutional importance.
