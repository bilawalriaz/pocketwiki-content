# Carthage

## Overview
Carthage was a major Phoenician colony and later independent city-state in North Africa, located in modern Tunisia. It grew into a powerful maritime empire that dominated trade across the Western Mediterranean for centuries. Its rivalry with Rome culminated in the Punic Wars, ending with its total destruction in 146 BC. The site was later rebuilt as Roman Carthage and remained a significant urban center through the Byzantine and early Islamic periods, making it a key location for studying ancient Mediterranean history, trade, and cultural exchange.

## Timeline
- **c. 814 BC** — Traditional founding of Carthage by Phoenicians from Tyre.
- **264–146 BC** — The three Punic Wars fought between Carthage and the Roman Republic.
- **146 BC** — Destruction of Punic Carthage by Rome after the Third Punic War.
- **44 BC** — Julius Caesar refounds the city as a Roman colony.
- **429** — Vandals under Gaiseric capture Carthage.
- **533–534** — Byzantine Empire reconquers Carthage from the Vandals.
- **698** — Umayyad forces capture and destroy Roman Carthage.
- **1159** — Hafsid dynasty destroys Carthage's remaining defenses.
- **1830** — First archaeological survey of the site by Christian Tuxen Falbe.
- **1884** — Metropolitan see of Carthage restored; Saint Louis Cathedral built.
- **1919** — Carthage becomes a commune.
- **1979** — Archaeological site designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Founding and Punic Empire
Carthage was founded as a Phoenician colony, traditionally by the legendary queen Dido (Elissa) from Tyre. The name derives from Punic *qrt-ḥdšt*, meaning "new city." It developed from a trading post into the capital of a Punic empire that controlled parts of the Southwest Mediterranean. The city-state sent out its own magistrates to govern colonies, distinguishing it from other Phoenician settlements. Its strategic location on a promontory with artificial harbors made it a master of maritime trade.

### Society, Economy, and Government
Punic society was built on trade and agriculture. The surrounding countryside (*chora*) was intensively farmed, with agricultural knowledge adapted from the eastern Mediterranean. A 28-volume agricultural treatise by Mago (c. 300 BC) was later translated into Latin and Greek. The state was a "mixed constitution" with elected *suffets* (chief magistrates), a council of elders, and popular assemblies. Aristotle praised its stability but noted its oligarchic tendencies, where wealth often determined office. Carthage fielded mercenary armies and was renowned for its manufacturing, especially during the Third Punic War when it rapidly produced arms and ships.

### Destruction and Roman Rebuilding
After the Third Punic War, Rome besieged and destroyed Carthage in 146 BC, selling 50,000 survivors into slavery. The site was later refounded by Julius Caesar as a Roman colony. It grew into one of the largest cities in the Roman Empire, becoming the capital of the province of Africa and a major center of early Christianity. The city was captured by the Vandals in 429 and later by the Byzantine Empire in 533–534.

### Islamic Period and Modern Era
In 698, Umayyad forces captured Carthage and deliberately destroyed its infrastructure to prevent Byzantine reconquest, marking the end of Byzantine influence in the region. The regional capital shifted to Tunis. Carthage remained occupied and was used as a fort until its defenses were razed by the Hafsids in the 12th century. In the modern era, it became a suburb of Tunis and a major archaeological site. Excavations have revealed much about Punic life, though the debate over child sacrifice at the *tophet* (a sacred precinct) continues.

## Terms
- **Suffet**: The chief magistrate of Carthage, elected annually, similar to a Roman consul.
- **Punic**: Relating to the Phoenician-derived culture of Carthage and its colonies; from Latin *pūnicus*.
- **Tophet**: A sacred precinct in Carthage containing urns with the remains of infants and animals, central to the child sacrifice debate.
- **Byrsa**: The central citadel and acropolis of ancient Carthage.
- **Cothon**: The artificial, inner harbors of Carthage, one for commerce and one for the navy.
- **Chora**: The agricultural hinterland or farmlands surrounding the city-state.
- **Libyphoenicians**: The primary citizen class of Carthage, of Phoenician descent born in Africa.
- **104 Judges**: A powerful judicial body in Carthage that evaluated the conduct of generals and other officials after their terms.

## Debates and open questions
- **Child Sacrifice**: Archaeological evidence from the *tophet* shows urns with infant and animal bones. Scholars debate whether this represents ritual sacrifice (as claimed by ancient Greek and Roman sources) or simply a cemetery for children who died of natural causes.
- **Constitutional Nature**: While Aristotle called Carthage a "mixed constitution," the precise balance of power between the *suffets*, council, assembly, and military commanders is not fully understood.
- **Population and Scale**: Estimates of Carthage's population at its peak vary widely, with ancient sources like Strabo suggesting 700,000, while modern scholars consider this likely exaggerated.
- **Genetic Heritage**: Craniometric and genetic studies of Punic remains show a heterogeneous population with affinities to both Levantine (Phoenician) and local North African (Berber) groups, indicating significant admixture.
