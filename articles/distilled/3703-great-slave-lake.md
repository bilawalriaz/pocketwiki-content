# Great Slave Lake

## Overview
Great Slave Lake is the largest lake in the Northwest Territories, Canada, and the deepest lake in North America. It is a major geographical feature of the Canadian subarctic, significant for its ecological richness, historical role in the fur trade, and as the site of modern settlements and resource development.

- **c. 2,500 years ago** — Taltheilei Shale tradition culture present.
- **Mid-18th century** — Lake mapped by European fur traders; named "Grand lac des Esclaves."
- **1930s** — Gold discovered on the North Arm, leading to the founding of Yellowknife.
- **1960** — Yellowknife Highway (Highway 3) built around the lake's west side.

## Body

### Indigenous History and Naming
Indigenous peoples settled the lake's shores after glacial retreat, with archaeological evidence showing distinct cultural periods defined by lithic tool types. The lake's English name derives from the Cree term for the Dene people who lived on its southern shores, which was translated by French explorers as "Grand lac des Esclaves." In the late 2010s, calls to restore an Indigenous name, such as Tu Nedhé (Dene Soline) or Tucho (Dehcho Dene), gained prominence due to the original name's association with slavery.

### Geography and Ecology
The lake is a remnant of glacial Lake McConnell, with an irregular shoreline and a very deep eastern arm (Christie Bay, 614 m). Its chief tributaries are the Hay, Slave, Lockhart, and Taltson Rivers, and it drains via the Mackenzie River. The lake is frozen for an average of eight months per year. The Slave River provides high nutrients, supporting a rich aquatic ecosystem with species like lake whitefish, lake trout, and northern pike, though climate change is affecting their populations.

### Modern Settlement and Infrastructure
Towns around the lake include Yellowknife (the NWT capital), Hay River, and Łutselk'e. The Dettah ice road provides a seasonal connection between Yellowknife and the community of Dettah. The region's economy was historically driven by the fur trade and later by gold mining, exemplified by the now-abandoned Pine Point Mine.

## Terms
- **Dene**: A group of First Nations peoples; the lake's name refers to a group called Slavey by their enemies, the Cree.
- **Canadian Shield**: A large area of exposed Precambrian rock forming the ancient geological core of North America, bordering the lake's south and east.
- **Lithic tools**: Stone tools, whose changing types and sizes mark different archaeological cultural periods.
- **Climax polygonal bog**: A stable, final-stage wetland ecosystem found on plains surrounding the lake.
- **Whooping Crane Summer Range**: A critical nesting site for a remnant flock of endangered whooping cranes, located south of the lake in Wood Buffalo National Park.
- **Ice road**: A seasonal road built on a frozen body of water, like the Dettah ice road connecting Yellowknife and Dettah.

## Debates and open questions
The primary debate concerns the lake's name. Educator Dëneze Nakehk'o and others argue the name "Great Slave Lake" is offensive and unfitting, advocating for its restoration to an Indigenous name like Tu Nedhé or Tucho. This is part of a broader movement in the late 2010s to rename places in the Northwest Territories to their original Indigenous names.
