# C. V. Raman

## Overview
Chandrasekhara Venkata Raman (1888–1970) was an Indian physicist whose work on light scattering led to the discovery of the Raman effect, a fundamental phenomenon in quantum physics. He was the first Asian and first non-white person to win the Nobel Prize in Physics (1930), and his research established the field of Raman spectroscopy, which has wide applications in chemistry, materials science, and biology.

## Timeline
- **7 November 1888** — Born in Tiruchirappalli, India.
- **1904** — Earned B.A. from University of Madras, topping exams in physics and English.
- **1907** — Published first scientific paper; joined Indian Finance Service in Calcutta.
- **1917** — Appointed first Palit Professor of Physics at University of Calcutta.
- **1921** — Proposed molecular scattering as the cause of the sea's blue color, contradicting Rayleigh's theory.
- **1924** — Elected Fellow of the Royal Society.
- **1926** — Founded the *Indian Journal of Physics*.
- **28 February 1928** — Discovered the Raman effect with student K. S. Krishnan.
- **1930** — Awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on light scattering.
- **1933** — Became first Indian director of the Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore.
- **1934** — Founded the Indian Academy of Sciences.
- **1948** — Established the Raman Research Institute in Bangalore.
- **21 November 1970** — Died in Bangalore at age 82.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Raman was a precocious student, passing matriculation at age 11 and earning a B.A. from the University of Madras at 16. His first research paper, on light diffraction, was published in 1906. Health concerns prevented him from studying in England, so he joined the Indian Finance Service in Calcutta in 1907. There, he began independent research at the Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science (IACS), India's first research institute.

### Career and Scientific Contributions
Raman's early work focused on acoustics, studying the science of musical sounds in instruments like violins and Indian drums. In 1921, while traveling by ship, he used a spectroscope and Nicol prism to observe that the Mediterranean Sea's blue color was not merely reflected sky light (as Lord Rayleigh had theorized) but involved molecular scattering by water itself.

His most famous discovery, the Raman effect, occurred on 28 February 1928 with his student K. S. Krishnan. Using a spectrograph he developed, they found that when light passes through a transparent material, some scattered light changes wavelength—a phenomenon they called "modified scattering." This was a new type of light scattering, distinct from the Compton effect for X-rays. The discovery provided early proof of the quantum nature of light and led to Raman spectroscopy, a key analytical tool. He received the 1930 Nobel Prize for this work.

Later, Raman studied crystal dynamics, the structure of diamonds, and the optical properties of various materials. He also co-developed the Raman–Nath theory of acousto-optic effects with Nagendra Nath.

### Institutional Leadership and Controversies
Raman founded several scientific institutions, including the Indian Journal of Physics (1926), the Indian Academy of Sciences (1934), and the Raman Research Institute (1948). He was the first Indian director of the Indian Institute of Science (IISc) from 1933.

His career was marked by several disputes. He had a long-running theoretical conflict with physicist Max Born over lattice dynamics. He was openly critical of government-controlled science in India, clashing with Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and institutions like the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research. At IISc, he faced criticism for favoring physics over other fields and for his recruitment of Born. He also initially refused admission to Kamala Sohonie, the first woman applicant to IISc, on gender grounds, though she later gained entry after a protest.

### Personal Life and Legacy
Raman married Lokasundari Ammal in 1907. He was an agnostic who wore traditional Indian attire. He was obsessed with winning the Nobel Prize, which he did in 1930. He resigned from the Royal Society in 1968 for unrecorded reasons. India celebrates National Science Day on 28 February to commemorate his discovery of the Raman effect.

## Terms
- **Raman effect**: The inelastic scattering of light by molecules, resulting in scattered light with a different wavelength (color) than the incident light. It is the basis of Raman spectroscopy.
- **Raman scattering**: Another term for the Raman effect, referring to the specific type of light scattering discovered by C. V. Raman.
- **Spectrograph**: An instrument that disperses light into its component wavelengths (spectrum) for measurement and analysis.
- **Rayleigh scattering**: The elastic scattering of light by particles much smaller than the wavelength of light, which explains why the sky is blue.
- **Compton effect**: The scattering of X-rays or gamma rays by charged particles, demonstrating the particle nature of electromagnetic radiation.
- **Acousto-optic effect**: The interaction between sound waves and light waves, where sound waves modulate light.
- **Lattice dynamics**: The study of vibrations of atoms in a crystalline solid.
- **Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science (IACS)**: India's first research institute, founded in 1876, where Raman conducted his early work.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Independent discovery of the Raman effect**: Soviet physicists Grigory Landsberg and Leonid Mandelstam independently observed the effect in crystals in February 1928, possibly a week before Raman. The Nobel Committee awarded the prize solely to Raman, citing his observation in solids, liquids, and gases, which proved the effect's universal nature.
- **Role of K. S. Krishnan**: Krishnan was the primary researcher in the experiments leading to the Raman effect's discovery and co-authored most related papers, but he was not included in the Nobel Prize. Raman later acknowledged Krishnan as a co-discoverer but publicly disparaged him.
- **Raman–Born controversy**: A decades-long dispute over the correct theory of lattice dynamics. Most physicists supported Born's theory, which was later validated, but Raman refused to concede.
- **Views on women in science**: Raman's initial refusal to admit Kamala Sohonie to IISc because of her gender reflects the broader historical bias against women in scientific research during that era.
