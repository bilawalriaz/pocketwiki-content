# Amerigo Vespucci

## Overview
Amerigo Vespucci (1454–1512) was a Florentine merchant, navigator, and explorer whose published letters—whether authentic or fabricated—convinced early 16th-century European cartographers that the lands reached by Columbus were not Asia but a "New World" (Mundus Novus). In 1507, Martin Waldseemüller applied the Latinized name "America" to this fourth continent in Vespucci's honor, a convention that became permanent. Vespucci participated in at least two well-documented voyages (1499–1500 for Spain, 1501–1502 for Portugal) and possibly two others alleged only in disputed letters. He ended his career as *piloto mayor* (master navigator) for Spain's Casa de Contratación, responsible for pilot licensing and the official *Padrón Real* map.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Vespucci was born into a politically connected but not wealthy Florentine notary family. His grandfather served 36 years as chancellor of the Signoria. Unlike his university-educated brothers, Amerigo was tutored by his uncle, Dominican friar Giorgio Antonio Vespucci, a leading humanist scholar. This education provided fluency in Latin, rhetoric, philosophy, and—crucially—geography and astronomy, including the works of Ptolemy, Strabo, and Florentine astronomer Paolo dal Pozzo Toscanelli.

### Early Career and Move to Seville
In 1478, Vespucci joined a Florentine diplomatic mission to Paris led by another uncle, Guido Antonio. After returning, he worked briefly with his father, then entered the service of Lorenzo di Pierfrancesco de' Medici in 1482. He managed household and business affairs, demonstrating commercial aptitude. In 1488, Lorenzo dispatched him to Seville to audit agent Tomasso Capponi and evaluate replacement Gianotto Berardi. Berardi took over the Medici account and ran a parallel business in African slavery and ship chandlery. By 1492, Vespucci had settled permanently in Seville, marrying María Cerezo (possibly connected to Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba). He became deeply involved in Berardi's support of Columbus's voyages, investing in provisions for the second fleet.

### The Voyages: Evidence and Disputes
Knowledge of Vespucci's explorations rests almost entirely on letters attributed to him. Historians sharply dispute authorship, veracity, and the number of voyages (two, three, or four).

**Alleged First Voyage (1497–1498):** Recorded only in the 1505 *Letter to Soderini*. Vespucci claims departure 10 May 1497, return 15 October 1498, sailing northwest from Honduras for 870 leagues—a course crossing Mexico to the Pacific. Most historians consider this a forgery or a conflation of later voyages, possibly fabricated to claim priority over Columbus for reaching the mainland.

**Second Voyage / Voyage of 1499–1500 (Spain):** Well-documented. Vespucci joined Alonso de Ojeda's fleet (four ships, two financed by Vespucci) licensed by Spain to explore Columbus's pearl-rich discoveries. Role unclear: possibly commercial representative or pilot (Ojeda later recalled "Morigo Vespuche" as a pilot). Fleet split near Suriname; Vespucci sailed south, seeking Ptolemy's "Cape of Cattigara" to reach the Indian Ocean. They encountered the Amazon and Para river outflows (freshwater 25 miles offshore), reached a strong adverse current at ~40 leagues south, turned back, then coasted north to Gulf of Paria and Venezuela. Returned via Hispaniola and a slave raid in the Bahamas (232 Lucayans captured).

**Third Voyage / Voyage of 1501–1502 (Portugal):** Well-documented. Manuel I hired Vespucci as pilot under Gonçalo Coelho to investigate Cabral's Brazilian landfall and its relation to the Treaty of Tordesillas line. Departed Lisbon May 1501, resupplied at Cape Verde (meeting Cabral). Reached Brazil at 6° S 17 Aug 1501; hostile natives killed a crewman. Sailed south, traded at 23° S (named Rio de Janeiro 1 Jan 1502). Turned home 13 Feb 1502; Vespucci estimated 32° S, modern estimates ~25° S. Vespucci wrote to Lorenzo de' Medici that this land was a "New World" unknown to ancients.

**Alleged Fourth Voyage (1503–1504):** Described only in the *Soderini letter*. Coelho led a Portuguese expedition ~1503, but no independent evidence places Vespucci aboard. Dates and details are problematic.

### Return to Seville and Official Career
By early 1505, Vespucci was back in Seville. His Portuguese service did not harm his standing with Ferdinand II; he was summoned for navigation consultations, paid for services, and naturalized as a Castilian subject (April 1505). In March 1508, he was appointed *piloto mayor* of the Casa de Contratación. Duties: examining and licensing pilots for Indies voyages, compiling the *Padrón Real* (master map) from returning pilots' data. Salary: 50,000 maravedis + 25,000 expenses. He held the post until death, also continuing as a ship chandler. A planned voyage as captain to the "spice islands" never materialized.

### Naming of America
Two letters published during Vespucci's lifetime—*Mundus Novus* (1503) and *Letter to Soderini* (1505)—circulated widely in Europe. At Saint-Dié, scholars Walter Lud, Matthias Ringmann, and Martin Waldseemüller read a French translation of the *Soderini letter* and studied a Portuguese map of the new coast. In April 1507, they published *Introduction to Cosmography* with a world map. Ringmann's preface proposed "Amerige" (Land of Amerigo) or "America" (feminine to match Europa/Asia). The map applied "America" to the New World (South America); 1,000 copies printed, four editions in first year. Gerardus Mercator extended the name to both continents in 1538. Waldseemüller later (1513) retracted the name, labeling the land "Terra Incognita" and crediting Columbus, but the convention held. Most historians believe Vespucci died unaware of the honor; many assert he did not write the *Soderini letter* that inspired it.

### The Vespucci Letters
Two published letters (lifetime) and three manuscript letters (published posthumously) form the evidentiary base.

**Published (lifetime):**
- *Mundus Novus* (1503): Latin letter to Lorenzo di Pierfrancesco de' Medici describing 1501–1502 voyage. Extremely popular: 12 editions in a year, 50+ by 1550.
- *Letter to Soderini* (1505): Italian letter to Piero Soderini, gonfaloniere of Florence. Sensational tone; only source claiming four voyages. Authorship and veracity widely questioned.

**Manuscript (posthumous discovery, authenticated by Alberto Magnaghi 1924):**
- *Letter from Seville* (1500): Describes 1499–1500 voyage (Spain). Published 1745.
- *Letter from Cape Verde* (1501): Written at voyage start for Portugal; details Cabral encounter. Published 1807.
- *Letter from Lisbon* (1502): Continuation of above; describes remainder of 1501–1502 voyage. Published 1789.
- *Ridolfi Fragment* (1502): Partial letter referencing three voyages (two Spain, one Portugal). Published 1937; assertions controversial.

Most historians accept the three manuscript letters as authentic; the two published letters are suspected fabrications by Florentine publishers based on genuine material.

### Historiography: The "Vespucci Question"
Debate over Vespucci's voyages and honesty spans centuries.
- **Early critics (1515–1856):** Sebastian Cabot (1515) doubted the 1497 voyage. Bartolomé de las Casas called Vespucci a liar stealing Columbus's credit. By 1600, consensus: impostor. Alexander von Humboldt (1839) deemed 1497 impossible but accepted the two Portuguese voyages; argued Vespucci died believing he reached Asia. Ralph Waldo Emerson (1856) called him a "thief" and "pickle dealer."
- **Rehabilitation (1857 onward):** Francisco Adolfo de Varnhagen (1857) defended the *Soderini letter* entirely. John Fiske, Henry Harrisse supported Vespucci.
- **Magnaghi thesis (1924):** Alberto Magnaghi denied Vespucci's authorship of both published letters. Proposed they were publisher fabrications combining genuine Vespucci letters with other sources. Concluded only the second (1499–1500) and third (1501–1502) voyages were real; first and fourth (from *Soderini letter*) were fictions. Two-voyage thesis popularized by Frederick J. Pohl (1944).
- **Four-voyage defenders:** Germán Arciniegas (1955) argued all four true. Luciano Formisiano (1992) accepted four voyages but differed on first voyage details.
- **Modern nuance:** Samuel Morison (1974) rejected first voyage, noncommittal on published letters. Felipe Fernández-Armesto (2007) calls authenticity "inconclusive"; hypothesizes first voyage = variant of second; third unassailable; fourth probably true.

### Legacy
Vespucci's historical weight lies more in his letters—authentic or not—than his physical discoveries. They transformed European geographical consciousness: within years, the public knew of a "New World" (Mundus Novus), a continental landmass south of the equator, densely populated and habitable, contradicting ancient authorities who held only ocean existed there. Vespucci himself used *novus mundus* or *una nuova terra* ("new land"), describing it as continuous mainland (*terra ferma*) but never the noun "continent" (a term introduced by later translators). The naming of "America" exemplifies the power of Italian humanist literature to shape historical memory.

## Terms
- ****Casa de Contratación**** — House of Trade (House of Commerce), founded 1503 in Seville; central Spanish agency licensing pilots, collecting navigation data, and maintaining the *Padrón Real* for Indies voyages.
- ****Padrón Real**** — Official Spanish master map (model map) compiled from returning pilots' reports; kept at Casa de Contratación; updated continuously.
- ****Piloto mayor**** — Master navigator / chief pilot; senior official at Casa de Contratación responsible for examining/licensing pilots and overseeing the *Padrón Real*.
- ****Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)**** — Papal-mediated treaty dividing non-European world between Spain (west) and Portugal (east) along a meridian 370 leagues west of Cape Verde; motivated Portugal's 1501 expedition to locate Brazil relative to the line.
- ****Mundus Novus**** — "New World"; title of Vespucci's 1503 published Latin letter to Lorenzo de' Medici; became the European label for the newly discovered continental landmass.
- ****Soderini Letter**** — 1505 Italian letter to Piero Soderini, gonfaloniere of Florence; only source claiming four Vespucci voyages (including disputed 1497–1498); inspired Waldseemüller's "America."
- ****Cape of Cattigara**** — Ptolemaic geographical concept: a southeastern cape of Asia (Malay Peninsula) believed to border the Indian Ocean; Vespucci sought it in 1499–1500 to reach Asia by sailing south.
- ****Signoria**** — The executive government of the Republic of Florence; Vespucci's grandfather served 36 years as its chancellor.
- ****Maravedí**** — Spanish copper coin; unit of account for salaries and contracts (e.g., Vespucci's 50,000 maravedis/year + 25,000 expenses; Berardi's 500,000 maravedis investment in Columbus).
- ****Lucayans**** — Indigenous Taíno inhabitants of the Bahamas; 232 captured in a slave raid during Vespucci's 1499–1500 return voyage.

## Debates and open questions
1. **Authorship of published letters:** Did Vespucci write *Mundus Novus* (1503) and *Letter to Soderini* (1505), or were they publisher fabrications based on his genuine manuscript letters? Magnaghi (1924) argued for fabrication; most modern scholars suspect heavy editing or fabrication, but consensus is not absolute.
2. **Number and reality of voyages:** Two voyages (1499–1500, 1501–1502) are well-supported. The 1497–1498 voyage (only in *Soderini letter*) is widely considered fictitious or a duplicate of 1499–1500. The 1503–1504 voyage (only in *Soderini letter*) lacks independent confirmation; scholars split on its reality.
3. **Priority of "New World" recognition:** Did Vespucci genuinely realize in 1501–1502 that Brazil was a continent distinct from Asia, or did he (like Columbus) die believing he had reached Asia's edge? Humboldt (1839) argued the latter; the *Mundus Novus* text claims the former.
4. **Role on 1499–1500 voyage:** Was Vespucci a pilot/navigator (as Ojeda later recalled), a commercial agent for investors, or a passenger? His own letters imply leadership; his inexperience suggests otherwise.
5. **Awareness of "America" naming:** Was Vespucci aware of Waldseemüller's 1507 map naming the continent after him? Most historians believe he died unaware (1512); Waldseemüller himself retracted the name in 1513.
