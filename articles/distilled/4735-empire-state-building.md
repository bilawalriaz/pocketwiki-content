# Empire State Building

## Overview
The Empire State Building is a 102-story Art Deco skyscraper in Midtown Manhattan, New York City. Completed in 1931, it was the world's tallest building for nearly 40 years and remains a global cultural icon, symbolizing American ambition and engineering prowess. Its design, rapid construction during the Great Depression, and enduring popularity as a tourist attraction make it a defining structure of the 20th century.

## Timeline
- **1893** — The Waldorf-Astoria Hotel opens on the future building's site.
- **1929** — Empire State Inc. acquires the site; design competition with other "tallest" contenders begins.
- **Mar 17, 1930** — Construction begins.
- **Sep 19, 1930** — Steel structure topped out at 1,048 ft, 12 days ahead of schedule.
- **May 1, 1931** — Building officially opens.
- **1933** — Featured in the film *King Kong*, cementing its pop culture status.
- **1950** — A radio/TV antenna is erected, beginning its role as a broadcast hub.
- **Oct 19, 1970** — Surpassed by the North Tower of the World Trade Center as world's tallest.
- **1981** — Designated a New York City Landmark.
- **1986** — Added to the National Register of Historic Places as a National Historic Landmark.
- **2001** — Becomes NYC's tallest again after the September 11 attacks destroy the World Trade Center.
- **2012** — Surpassed by One World Trade Center as NYC's tallest.

## Body

### Design and Construction
The building was designed by Shreve, Lamb & Harmon in the Art Deco style. Its form, with a symmetrical massing and a series of setbacks, was dictated by the 1916 Zoning Resolution to allow sunlight to reach the streets. The design went through fifteen versions to ensure it would be the world's tallest, ultimately reaching a roof height of 1,250 feet (380 m). Construction was a feat of speed and coordination, beginning on March 17, 1930, and opening just 13.5 months later on May 1, 1931. At its peak, 3,439 workers were on site in a single day. The project cost $40.9 million, under its $60 million budget.

### Architecture and Features
The facade is clad in Indiana limestone, giving it a signature blonde color, and features geometric Art Deco ornamentation. The building's interior includes a famous lobby with narrative bronze motifs and marble finishes. It originally housed 64 elevators, now replaced, and its structural steel frame was designed to be exceptionally stiff. The spire, originally intended as a mooring mast for airships (a plan abandoned due to high winds), now serves as a base for broadcast antennas. Since 1977, its iconic lighting system has been used to celebrate holidays, events, and causes, with a 2012 upgrade to LED fixtures allowing for over 16 million colors.

### Tourism and Cultural Impact
The building's observation decks on the 86th and 102nd floors (with an added 80th-floor observatory in 2019) attract about four million visitors annually. It has been featured in over 250 films and TV shows since *King Kong* (1933). The American Society of Civil Engineers named it one of the Seven Wonders of the Modern World, and it was ranked first on the American Institute of Architects' List of America's Favorite Architecture in 2007. Its height has long been used as a standard reference for other structures.

### Ownership and Economic History
The building struggled financially during the Great Depression, earning the nickname "Empty State Building." It did not turn a profit until the early 1950s. Ownership has changed hands several times, with notable figures like Harry Helmsley and Donald Trump involved in complex legal battles over its lease and land in the 1990s. In 2013, the Empire State Realty Trust (ESRT), a public company, was formed, consolidating ownership. A major $550 million renovation began in 2009, improving energy efficiency and visitor amenities.

## Terms
- **Art Deco**: A decorative style of the 1920s and 30s characterized by geometric shapes and bold colors.
- **Setback**: A step-like reduction in a building's mass as it rises, often required by zoning laws.
- **Zoning Resolution (1916)**: NYC law requiring buildings to step back from the street to allow light and air to reach ground level.
- **Spire**: The tapering structure on top of a building; here, it houses broadcast equipment.
- **Mooring Mast**: A tower designed for tethering airships or dirigibles.
- **LEED Gold**: A certification for buildings meeting high environmental and energy-efficiency standards.
- **Observatory**: An enclosed or open-air platform for public viewing from a high point in a building.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Airship Mast Feasibility**: The original plan to use the spire as an airship terminal was abandoned after a failed 1931 docking attempt proved it too dangerous due to high winds and turbulence.
- **"Tallest" Title Rivalry**: The building's status as "world's tallest" was the result of a fierce 1920s competition with the Chrysler Building and 40 Wall Street, involving last-minute design changes.
- **Broadcast Signal Quality**: A congressional report noted that signal transmissions from the Empire State Building are "problematic" due to interference from nearby buildings, unlike the former Twin Towers' superior location.
