# Money

## Overview
Money is any item or verifiable record generally accepted as payment for goods, services, and debts (such as taxes) within a specific socio-economic context. It performs four primary functions: a **medium of exchange** (avoiding the "coincidence of wants" problem inherent in barter), a **unit of account** (a common measure of value), a **store of value** (reliably saved and retrieved), and a **standard of deferred payment** (a unit for denominating debts). Historically, money emerged as commodity money possessing intrinsic value (e.g., grain, shells, precious metals). Modern systems rely on **fiat money**—currency without intrinsic or backing value, declared legal tender by government decree. The money supply comprises physical currency (M0) and **bank money** (commercial bank deposits created via lending), with the latter forming the vast majority of broad money in developed economies.

## Timeline
- **c. 100,000 years ago** — Possible use of barter-like methods; no evidence of barter-based societies.
- **c. 3000 BC** — First usage of the term "shekel" (unit of weight) in Mesopotamia.
- **c. 650–600 BC** — First stamped gold/silver coins minted in Lydia (modern scholars' estimate).
- **7th century** — Promissory notes used in China (precursor to paper money).
- **960–1279 (Song dynasty)** — First government-issued paper banknotes (*jiaozi*) in China.
- **13th century** — Knowledge of paper money reaches Europe via travellers (Marco Polo, William of Rubruck).
- **1661** — First European banknotes issued by Stockholms Banco (Sweden).
- **17th–19th centuries** — Gold standard replaces gold coins as currency in Europe; notes made legal tender.
- **1900** — Most industrializing nations on a gold standard.
- **1944** — Bretton Woods Conference fixes currencies to the U.S. dollar (fixed to gold).
- **1971** — U.S. suspends dollar convertibility to gold; global shift to floating fiat currencies.
- **1990** — All U.S. central bank–commercial bank transfers become electronic.
- **2008** — Bitcoin introduces decentralised, borderless digital currency.

## Body

### Etymology and Early Forms
The word "money" derives from Latin *moneta* ("coin"), linked to the temple of Juno Moneta on Rome’s Capitoline Hill where the mint was located. The term *specie* (from Latin *in specie*, "in kind") historically denoted coin-money. Anthropological evidence suggests societies never relied primarily on barter; instead, non-monetary economies operated on **gift economy** and **debt** principles. Barter occurred mainly between strangers or enemies. The first formal commodity money appeared in Mesopotamia c. 3000 BC (the shekel, based on barley weight). Cowry shells served as currency across the Americas, Asia, Africa, and Australia. Lydia introduced the first stamped gold and silver coins c. 650–600 BC.

### Evolution of Representative and Paper Money
**Representative money** emerged when gold/silver merchants issued receipts redeemable for deposited commodity money; these receipts eventually circulated as money. Paper money originated in China during the Tang (618–907) and Song (960–1279) dynasties to avoid transporting heavy copper coins. *Jiaozi* evolved from merchant promissory notes into state-issued currency by the early 12th century, becoming a uniform nationwide currency by the mid-13th century, enabled by woodblock and movable-type printing. Simultaneously, the medieval Islamic world (7th–12th centuries) developed a sophisticated monetary economy based on the stable *dinar*, pioneering cheques, savings accounts, and banking institutions. Europe adopted paper money in 1661 (Stockholms Banco, Sweden), driven by the impracticality of large copper coins. Paper currency reduced transport risks and facilitated lending and joint-stock companies but introduced inflationary risks (noted by David Hume) and was often associated with war financing.

### The Gold Standard and the Fiat Transition
The **gold standard**—where paper notes are convertible into fixed quantities of gold—replaced gold coins in Europe (17th–19th centuries). By 1900, most industrial nations adopted it. Gresham’s Law ("bad money drives out good") saw gold hoarded while notes circulated. Post-WWII, the Bretton Woods system pegged currencies to the U.S. dollar, which was fixed to gold. In 1971, the U.S. suspended dollar-gold convertibility, leading most currencies to become unbacked **fiat money**, valued solely by government decree (legal tender laws) and, per Modern Monetary Theory, by the demand created through taxation.

### Functions and Properties
William Stanley Jevons (1875) defined four functions: medium of exchange, measure of value (unit of account), standard of deferred payment, and store of value. Modern texts often subsume "standard of deferred payment" into the other three. A tension exists between the **medium of exchange** (requires circulation) and **store of value** (requires holding). To fulfill its functions, money must be: **fungible** (interchangeable units), **durable**, **divisible**, **portable**, **acceptable**, and **scarce** (limited supply).

### Money Supply and Creation
The money supply is the total stock of financial instruments fulfilling money’s functions, measured via **monetary aggregates** based on liquidity:
*   **M0 (Base money):** Central bank currency + bank reserves at the central bank.
*   **M1 (Narrow money):** Currency + demand deposits (checking accounts).
*   **M2 (Broad money):** M1 + savings accounts + small time deposits (<$100k).
*   **M3:** M2 + large time deposits + institutional accounts.
In modern **fractional-reserve banking**, commercial banks create the majority of broad money (M1/M2) by recording loans as deposits. They do not merely intermediate existing deposits nor require central bank reserves (M0) to lend. Central banks influence but do not fully control this creation via interest rates and reserve requirements. **Market liquidity** describes ease of trade; money is the most liquid asset.

### Types of Money
*   **Commodity money:** Value derives from the material itself (gold, silver, barley, shells, cigarettes). The commodity *is* the money.
*   **Representative money:** Tokens (notes, certificates) exchangeable for a fixed quantity of commodity (e.g., gold standard notes).
*   **Fiat money:** No intrinsic/backing value; value by government order (legal tender). Advantage: legal framework allows replacement if damaged/destroyed.
*   **Commercial bank money:** Non-physical demand deposits; claims on banks created via lending. Carries insolvency risk.
*   **Digital/Electronic money:** By 1990, all U.S. interbank transfers were electronic; by 2000s, most money existed as database entries. **Bitcoin (2008)** introduced decentralised, trustless digital currency via distributed consensus.

### Monetary Policy
Under commodity standards, money supply depended on mining rates (gold rushes caused inflation; slow mining caused deflation). Modern **monetary policy** (conducted by central banks like the Fed, ECB, BoJ, PBOC, BoE) manages fiat supply to target low/stable inflation, maximum employment, and moderate interest rates. Tools: policy interest rates, open market operations, forward guidance, reserve requirements. **Monetarism** (1970s–80s) targeted money supply growth but failed due to unstable money demand; since the 1990s, central banks target inflation directly using interest rates. Policy failure risks hyperinflation, stagflation, recession, or monetary collapse (e.g., post-Soviet Russia).

### Locality and Currency Substitution
Money is valid only in a "particular country or socio-economic context." Governments enforce a single currency via tax requirements and fraud laws. **Currency substitution** occurs when communities switch money: intentionally (government reform, e.g., Brazil’s cruzeiro to real) or spontaneously (rejection during hyperinflation). **Gresham’s Law** drives "bad" (debased) money into circulation while "good" money is hoarded. Non-governmental money can emerge (e.g., cigarettes in POW camps).

### Financial Crimes
*   **Counterfeiting:** Imitation currency without state sanction. Ancient (plated Lydian *Fourrées*); historically combated by using hard-to-copy items (shells, rare metals). Modern "Superdollars" are high-quality fakes; Euro counterfeiting significant since 2002 but lower than USD.
*   **Money laundering:** Transforming crime proceeds into ostensibly legitimate assets. Term often conflated with broader financial crime (terrorism financing, tax evasion, sanctions evasion) involving securities, digital currencies, and traditional cash.

## Terms
- ****Fiat money**** — Currency without intrinsic value or commodity backing, declared legal tender by government decree.
- ****Commodity money**** — Money whose value comes from the commodity of which it is made (e.g., gold, barley, shells).
- ****Representative money**** — Tokens (notes, certificates) redeemable for a fixed quantity of a backing commodity (e.g., gold).
- ****Legal tender**** — Money legally valid for discharging debts; creditors must accept it for "all debts, public and private."
- ****Money supply**** — Total stock of financial instruments (currency + deposits) available in an economy for transactions.
- ****Monetary aggregates (M0, M1, M2, M3)**** — Measures of money supply categorized by liquidity; M0 is base money, M1/M2 are narrow/broad money.
- ****Fractional-reserve banking**** — System where banks hold reserves equal to only a fraction of deposits, creating money via lending.
- ****Bank money (Broad money)**** — Non-physical demand deposits created by commercial banks when issuing loans; largest component of money supply in developed nations.
- ****Gresham’s Law**** — Principle that "bad money drives out good": undervalued/overvalued money circulates while higher-value money is hoarded.
- ****Seigniorage**** — (Implicit from text: profit made by government by issuing currency, especially the difference between face value and production cost.)

## Debates and open questions
*   **Function separation:** Whether money’s roles (medium of exchange vs. store of value) are compatible or require distinct instruments; Jevons’s four functions vs. modern three-function consensus.
*   **Money creation mechanism:** The extent to which central banks control broad money creation vs. endogenous creation by commercial banks via lending (Money Multiplier theory vs. endogenous money view).
*   **Monetarism vs. Inflation Targeting:** The failure of money-supply targeting (1980s) due to unstable velocity/demand, leading to the current consensus on interest-rate-based inflation targeting.
*   **Fiat backing:** Whether fiat money is backed solely by social convention/legal tender laws or fundamentally by the state’s power to impose taxes (Modern Monetary Theory view).
*   **Digital currency future:** The role of decentralised cryptocurrencies (Bitcoin) vs. Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs) in the monetary system; regulatory treatment of anonymous digital currencies.
*   **Optimal inflation/deflation:** The trade-offs between gold-standard-era deflationary pressures and fiat-era inflation targeting; costs of "failed monetary policy" (hyperinflation, collapse).
