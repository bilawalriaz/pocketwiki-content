# Henry Moore

## Overview
Henry Moore (1898–1986) was an English sculptor whose semi-abstract bronze works, often depicting reclining figures and mother-and-child motifs, became iconic public art worldwide. His transition from direct carving to large-scale bronze casting, combined with his wartime drawings of Londoners during the Blitz, established him as a leading figure in 20th-century British modernism. Moore's wealth from commissions funded the Henry Moore Foundation, which continues to promote the visual arts.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Moore was born in Castleford, Yorkshire, to a coal mine worker who valued education as a path away from mining. Despite his father's initial opposition to sculpture as manual labor, Moore pursued art, winning scholarships to Leeds School of Art and the Royal College of Art. His exposure to primitive art at the British Museum and modernist influences like Brâncuși shaped his shift from academic traditions to direct carving, where tool marks became integral to the work.

### Hampstead Years and Artistic Development
After teaching at the Royal College of Art, Moore moved to Hampstead in 1929, joining a small colony of avant-garde artists who were taking root there, including Barbara Hepworth, Ben Nicholson, and Naum Gabo. His friendship and professional rivalry with Hepworth fostered mutual artistic growth. Moore flirted with Surrealism and joined Paul Nash's modern art movement "Unit One" in 1933. A pivotal 1924 trip to Northern Italy exposed him to Michelangelo and the Toltec-Mayan Chac Mool, inspiring his signature reclining figure motif.

### Wartime Experience and Drawings
During WWII, Moore resigned his Chelsea teaching post and produced powerful drawings of Londoners sheltering in the Underground during the Blitz. Commissioned by the War Artists' Advisory Committee, these works, along with drawings of miners at his father's former colliery, enhanced his international reputation. The family relocated to Hoglands farmhouse in Hertfordshire, which became his lifelong home and studio.

### Post-War Commissions and Style
After the war, Moore embraced large-scale bronze casting, producing increasingly monumental works suited for public spaces. His transition from direct carving to modelling maquettes in clay or plaster, then casting in bronze via lost-wax technique, allowed him to scale up. Works like *Reclining Figure: Festival* (1951) and *Nuclear Energy* (1967) exemplified his exploration of solid form contrasted with penetrating voids. He employed assistants including Anthony Caro and maintained a collection of natural objects for inspiration. Moore's sense of England emerging undefeated from siege led to his focus on pieces characterised by endurance and continuity.

### Legacy and Foundation
Moore's global fame made him the world's most successful living artist at auction by the 1980s. Concerned with preserving his legacy, he set up the Henry Moore Trust in 1972, and to mitigate his tax burden, he established the Henry Moore Foundation as a registered charity in 1977. The foundation maintains his Perry Green home as a museum. His influence extended to generations of sculptors, though some, like the Geometry of Fear group, reacted against his "establishment" status.

## Terms
- **Direct carving**: A sculptural technique where the form evolves by cutting directly into the material, leaving tool marks visible.
- **Lost-wax casting**: A bronze casting method where a wax model is encased in clay, melted out, and replaced with molten metal.
- **Maquette**: A small-scale preliminary model or sculpture used to plan a larger work.
- **Unit One**: A modern art movement Moore joined in 1933, founded by Paul Nash.
- **Geometry of Fear**: Works produced by eight new British sculptors at the 1952 Venice Biennale, contrasting with Moore's ideals.
- **War Artists' Advisory Committee (WAAC)**: A WWII British committee that commissioned artists to document the war.
- **Reclining Figure**: Moore's signature sculptural motif, often abstracted and pierced, inspired by pre-Columbian art.
- **Henry Moore Foundation**: A registered charity established in 1977 to promote public appreciation of visual arts and preserve Moore's legacy.
- **Endurance and Continuity**: Themes in Moore's post-war work reflecting England's resilience during WWII.

## Debates and Open Questions
Moore's relationship with the art establishment was complex. He turned down a knighthood in 1951 because he felt that the bestowal would lead to a perception of him as an establishment figure and that "such a title might tend to cut me off from fellow artists whose work has aims similar to mine". He was, however, appointed a Member of the Order of the Companions of Honour in 1955 and a Member of the Order of Merit in 1963. Critics debated whether his commercial success and public monuments compromised his artistic integrity. The "Geometry of Fear" sculptors explicitly rejected his ideals of endurance, favoring more angular, existential forms. Additionally, in December 2005, the two ton Reclining Figure (1969–70) was lifted by crane from the grounds of the Henry Moore Foundation and has not been recovered. Two men were jailed for a year in 2012 for stealing a sculpture called Sundial (1965), and the work was recovered. The 2012 controversy over selling *Draped Seated Woman* highlighted tensions between public access and private ownership of public art.
