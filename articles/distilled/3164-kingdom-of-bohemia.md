# Kingdom of Bohemia

## Overview
The Kingdom of Bohemia was a medieval and early modern monarchy in Central Europe, predecessor to the modern Czech Republic. As an Imperial State within the Holy Roman Empire, its kings were prince-electors. The kingdom at various times ruled over a vast Crown of Bohemia including Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia. Its history is defined by dynastic shifts, a powerful "Golden Age," and the religious-national conflict of the Hussite movement, which shaped its identity for centuries.

## Timeline
- **1198** — Přemysl Ottokar I formally establishes the Kingdom of Bohemia.
- **1212** — Golden Bull of Sicily confirms Bohemia's royal status and independence from most imperial obligations.
- **1278** — Přemysl Ottokar II dies at the Battle on the Marchfeld, losing most non-Bohemian lands.
- **1346** — Charles IV of the House of Luxembourg becomes King of Bohemia.
- **1348** — Charles IV founds Charles University in Prague and creates the Crown of Bohemia.
- **1356** — Golden Bull of 1356 codifies the Holy Roman Emperor election, naming the Bohemian king as one of seven electors.
- **1409** — Decree of Kutná Hora grants Czechs a majority vote at Charles University, leading to a German exodus.
- **1415** — Jan Hus is burned at the stake, sparking the Hussite movement.
- **1458** — George of Poděbrady, a Hussite, is elected King of Bohemia.
- **1526** — Louis II dies at the Battle of Mohács; the Bohemian Crown passes to the Habsburg dynasty.
- **1620** — Defeat at the Battle of White Mountain ends Bohemian autonomy and the Hussite political movement.
- **1918** — The Kingdom of Bohemia is dissolved, forming the core of the new Czechoslovak Republic.

## Body

### Establishment and the Přemyslid Dynasty
The Kingdom of Bohemia was formally established in 1198 when Přemysl Ottokar I had his royal status acknowledged by Philip of Swabia. This was confirmed by the Golden Bull of Sicily in 1212, making the Bohemian king exempt from most imperial obligations except council participation. The 13th century saw expansion under Přemysl Ottokar II, who acquired Austria and Alpine territories but lost them after his defeat and death in 1278. His son, Wenceslaus II, briefly united the Bohemian and Polish crowns.

### The Golden Age under Charles IV
The 14th century, particularly the reign of Charles IV (1342–1378), is considered the Golden Age of Czech history. Charles strengthened Bohemia's power, created the Crown of Bohemia (incorporating Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia), and made Prague an imperial capital. He founded Charles University in 1348 and issued the Golden Bull of 1356, which defined the imperial election process and secured the Bohemian king's role as an elector.

### The Hussite Movement and Wars
The Hussite movement (c. 1402–1485) began as a religious reform challenge to papal authority, led by Jan Hus, who was executed in 1415. It evolved into a national and anti-imperial struggle, with Hussite forces defeating four papal crusades. The conflict pitted Czech Hussites against Catholic Germans and the Holy Roman Emperor. The movement led to the Compacts of Basel (1433), which temporarily reconciled moderate Hussites (Utraquists) with the Catholic Church. The era concluded with the election of the Hussite king George of Poděbrady in 1458.

### Jagiellonian and Habsburg Rule
After George's death, the Jagiellonian dynasty ruled Bohemia, often as absentee monarchs focused on Hungary. In 1526, the death of King Louis II at the Battle of Mohács brought the Habsburg dynasty to the throne. Habsburg rule sparked resistance, culminating in the 1618 Defenestration of Prague and the Thirty Years' War. The Bohemian defeat at the Battle of White Mountain in 1620 ended local autonomy and led to forced re-Catholicization.

### Decline and Dissolution
Bohemia lost most of Silesia to Prussia in the 18th-century Silesian Wars. After the Holy Roman Empire's dissolution in 1806, the kingdom became a crown land of the Austrian Empire, and later the Austro-Hungarian Empire. It retained its name and formal status until 1918, when it was dissolved and became the core of the newly formed Czechoslovak Republic.

## Terms
- **Crown of Bohemia:** A political entity created by Charles IV in 1348, uniting the Kingdom of Bohemia with the Margraviate of Moravia, Duchies of Silesia, and Lusatia under a single monarch.
- **Golden Bull of 1356:** An imperial decree issued by Charles IV that codified the election process for Holy Roman Emperor, naming the King of Bohemia as one of the seven prince-electors.
- **Hussites:** Followers of the religious reformer Jan Hus, who challenged papal authority and advocated for reforms like communion in both kinds (bread and wine).
- **Utraquists:** The moderate wing of the Hussite movement, named from the Latin *sub utraque specie* ("under each kind"), who accepted the Compacts of Basel.
- **Taborites:** A radical, anti-hierarchical sect of Hussites who took their name from the town of Tábor and upheld the Bible as the sole authority.
- **Compacts of Basel:** A 1433 agreement that temporarily reconciled the Utraquist Hussites with the Catholic Church, accepting key Hussite tenets.
- **Defenestration of Prague:** The 1618 act of throwing imperial governors from a window in Prague, which triggered the Bohemian Revolt and the Thirty Years' War.
- **Battle of White Mountain (1620):** The decisive defeat of the Bohemian estates by Habsburg forces, ending Bohemian autonomy and the Hussite political movement.
- **Ostsiedlung:** The medieval migration and settlement of German-speaking peoples into Central and Eastern Europe, which significantly influenced Bohemia's demographics and economy.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The Hussite movement is interpreted by some historians as a precursor to the Protestant Reformation, while others emphasize its role as a Czech national movement against German and imperial domination.
- The long-term ethnic Czech–German conflict is sometimes seen as having roots in the Hussite Wars and the subsequent re-Catholicization and Germanization policies after 1620.
- The precise dating and nature of the incorporation of Moravia into the Bohemian state (c. 1019/1029) remains uncertain.
