# Lee de Forest

## Overview
Lee de Forest (1873–1961) was a pivotal American inventor whose creation of the three-element "Audion" triode vacuum tube in 1908 provided the first practical electronic amplifier. This invention was foundational to the Electronic Age, enabling radio broadcasting, long-distance telephony, and talking motion pictures. His career was marked by brilliant innovation, financial turmoil, and contentious patent disputes.

## Timeline
- **1873** — Born in Council Bluffs, Iowa.
- **1899** — Earns Ph.D. from Yale for work on Hertzian waves.
- **1906** — Files patent for the diode vacuum tube detector.
- **1907** — Files patent for the grid Audion (triode).
- **1910** — Conducts early experimental radio broadcasts from the Metropolitan Opera.
- **1912** — AT&T purchases rights to Audion patents for telephone line amplification.
- **1913** — Begins legal battle with Edwin Howard Armstrong over regeneration circuit priority.
- **1916** — Resumes radio broadcasting, including first audio election report broadcast.
- **1921** — Shifts focus to developing the Phonofilm sound-on-film process.
- **1923** — Phonofilm short films premiere in New York.
- **1926** — Phonofilm Company files for bankruptcy; Hollywood adopts competing sound systems.
- **1961** — Dies in Hollywood, California.

## Body

### Early Life and Radio Work
Born to a minister, de Forest spent his youth in Alabama, isolated from the white community. After a doctorate from Yale, he pursued radio ("wireless telegraphy"), seeking to improve on Marconi's coherer receiver. His early efforts included developing "responder" receivers and competing with Marconi in yacht race transmissions, which ended in failure due to technical breakdowns and signal interference.

### The Audion and Early Companies
De Forest's career was financed by promoters like Abraham White, whose American De Forest Wireless Telegraph Company was built on stock fraud. While the company built Navy stations, de Forest struggled to develop a non-infringing receiver. He resigned in 1906. His most significant invention, the grid Audion triode, evolved from experiments with ionized gases in tubes. The addition of a control grid (patented 1908) created the first electronic amplifier, though its initial operation was unreliable and its physics poorly understood.

### Amplification and Patent Battles
Working at Federal Telegraph, de Forest developed the "cascade amplifier" by chaining Audions. AT&T recognized its potential for telephone repeaters and, after its engineers improved the tube's vacuum, purchased the rights in 1913. This led to the first transcontinental telephone calls in 1915. A bitter priority dispute with Edwin Howard Armstrong over the regeneration (feedback) circuit ensued. While courts ultimately awarded de Forest legal priority, many in the engineering community believed Armstrong was the true inventor.

### Phonofilm and Later Years
De Forest later developed Phonofilm, an optical sound-on-film process. Despite early demonstrations, Hollywood studios rejected it, favoring competing systems like Fox Movietone (based on Theodore Case's work) and Vitaphone. Phonofilm's company went bankrupt in 1926. De Forest remained a vocal critic of radio entertainment and died in 1961 with limited funds, having been recognized with major awards like the IEEE Medal of Honor and an honorary Oscar.

## Terms
- **Audion/Triode:** The first three-element vacuum tube (filament, grid, plate), invented by de Forest, capable of amplifying electrical signals.
- **Regeneration:** A feedback circuit that greatly amplifies radio signals; the subject of a major priority dispute between de Forest and Armstrong.
- **Phonofilm:** De Forest's optical sound-on-film process for adding audio to movies.
- **Coherer:** An early, unreliable radio receiver component used by Marconi that required physical tapping to reset.
- **Cascade Amplifier:** A circuit that chains multiple amplifying tubes together for greater signal strength.
- **Variable Density:** The method used in Phonofilm to record sound as parallel lines of varying shades of gray on film.
- **High Vacuum Tube:** An improved Audion design, developed by AT&T engineers, that could handle higher voltages for telephone use.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Priority of Invention:** The most contentious debate concerns the regeneration circuit. U.S. courts legally recognized de Forest's priority based on a 1912 notebook entry, but the engineering community largely credited Edwin Howard Armstrong's more comprehensive development and understanding.
- **Credit for Sound Film:** De Forest's Phonofilm was a pioneering sound-on-film system, but its commercial failure and his contentious relationships with collaborators like Theodore Case complicate his legacy as the "father of sound movies."
- **Understanding of His Own Invention:** Scholars note de Forest initially did not fully grasp the physics of his Audion or the significance of the feedback principle he observed, which fueled the regeneration controversy.
