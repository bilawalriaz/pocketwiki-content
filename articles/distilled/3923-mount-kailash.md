# Mount Kailash

## Overview
Mount Kailash is a 6,638-meter peak in Tibet's Transhimalaya, sacred to four major religions and revered as the spiritual center of the world. Its unique status as an unclimbed mountain, protected by religious prohibition, and its role as the source of major Asian rivers make it a profound geographical and cultural landmark.

## Body

### Geography and Geology
Mount Kailash is located in the Gangdise Shan range of the Transhimalaya, near the trijunction of China, India, and Nepal. Its peak reaches 6,638 m (21,778 ft). The mountain is situated between the sacred lakes Manasarovar (the world's highest freshwater lake) and Rakshastal. The sources of four major rivers—the Indus, Sutlej, Brahmaputra, and Ghaghara—originate within a 60 km (37 mi) stretch in the region. Geologically, the mountain is a large metasedimentary roof pendant on a granite base, formed by ancient marine sediments deposited before the collision of the Indian and Asian continental plates. The region shows evidence of past glaciation and is experiencing accelerated warming and permafrost thaw.

### Mountaineering and Prohibition
Despite its allure, Mount Kailash has never been successfully climbed. Early surveys by Hugh Ruttledge (1926) and attempts by Herbert Tichy (1936) and Colonel R. C. Wilson all concluded the mountain was unclimbable or faced impassable conditions. The Chinese government has strictly prohibited any climbing activities due to the mountain's profound religious significance. This ban was reaffirmed when a Spanish team was denied permission in 2001. Notably, famed mountaineer Reinhold Messner declined an invitation to climb in the 1980s, stating that conquering it would "conquer something in people's souls."

### Religious Significance
Mount Kailash is sacred in four religions. In **Buddhism**, it is identified as the mythological Mount Meru, the center of the universe. A legend tells of the saint Milarepa defeating a Bon priest in a race to the summit. For **Bon**, the mountain is the abode of the sky goddess Sipaimen and the center of their ancient empire. In **Hinduism**, it is the abode of the god Shiva and his family, later identified with Mount Meru. Epics describe the demon-king Ravana shaking the mountain, only to be trapped by Shiva's toe. In **Jainism**, the first Tirthankara, Rishabhadeva, is believed to have attained moksha (liberation) on the peak.

### Pilgrimage
The pilgrimage, or *yatra*, is one of the world's oldest. It involves trekking to Lake Manasarovar and performing a 53 km (33 mi) circumambulation (*kora*) of the mountain. Hindus, Buddhists, and Jains walk clockwise, while Bonpos walk counterclockwise. The route typically takes three days on foot, with the highest point at the Drölma pass (5,650 m). The most devout perform the entire circuit via full-body prostrations, a process taking about three weeks. Access has been politically sensitive, with pilgrimages from India resuming in 1981 after a two-decade closure following the 1962 war. The route was suspended from 2020 to 2023 due to the pandemic.

## Terms
- **Circumambulation (Kora):** The ritual act of walking around a sacred site; at Kailash, it is a 53 km trek.
- **Transhimalaya:** A mountain range north of the main Himalayan range, where Kailash is located.
- **Metasedimentary:** Rock formed from sedimentary rock that has been subjected to heat and pressure.
- **Moksha:** In Jainism, liberation from the cycle of rebirth.
- **Tirthankara:** A savior and spiritual teacher in Jainism.
- **Flysch zone:** A geological formation of layered sedimentary rocks, marking the boundary between the Himalayas and Transhimalayas.
- **Yatra:** A pilgrimage or journey to a sacred place.
- **Garpön:** A local Tibetan leader or official.

## Debates and Open Questions
The exact geological formation of the mountain is described with terms like "might be," indicating some scholarly uncertainty. The precise start date of pilgrimages is unknown. The impact of accelerated climate change on the region's water resources and fragile ecosystem is a major ongoing concern, prompting conservation initiatives like the Kailash Sacred Landscape Conservation and Development Initiative.
