# Pompeii

## Overview
Pompeii was a prosperous Roman city in the Campania region of Italy, buried under volcanic ash and pumice during the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD. Its exceptional preservation provides an unparalleled snapshot of Roman urban life, architecture, and society, making it a cornerstone of archaeological study and a major global tourist attraction.

## Timeline
- **8th century BC** — First stable settlements founded by the Oscans.
- **c. 740 BC** — Greek influence begins; Doric Temple built.
- **524 BC** — Etruscans settle in the area; city joins Etruscan League.
- **474 BC** — Greeks defeat Etruscans at Battle of Cumae, gaining control.
- **c. 424 BC** — Samnites conquer the region, including Pompeii.
- **89 BC** — Siege by Sulla; Pompeii becomes a Roman colony.
- **70 BC** — Amphitheatre built.
- **AD 59** — Riot in the amphitheatre leads to a 10-year ban on events.
- **AD 62** — Severe earthquake causes major damage.
- **AD 79** — Mount Vesuvius erupts, burying the city.
- **1592** — First known rediscovery by Domenico Fontana.
- **1748** — Systematic excavations begin under Spanish engineer Alcubierre.
- **1863** — Giuseppe Fiorelli introduces scientific methods and plaster casts.
- **1997** — Designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2018** — 'Great Pompeii Project' begins major conservation and new excavations.

## Body

### Early History and Development
Pompeii's site was first settled by Oscans in the 8th century BC. It entered the Greek sphere around 740 BC, with the construction of the Doric Temple. The city was later controlled by Etruscans (from 524 BC) and then Samnites (from c. 424 BC), who expanded it. After the Samnite Wars, Pompeii entered the Roman orbit but maintained autonomy until 89 BC, when it was besieged by Sulla during the Social Wars. It became a Roman colony, *Colonia Cornelia Veneria Pompeianorum*, and its elite assimilated by Latinizing their names.

### The Roman City and its Economy
As a Roman colony, Pompeii prospered from agriculture (especially wine and oil production) and trade. Major public buildings were constructed, including the Amphitheatre (70 BC), theatres, baths, and the Forum. The city was supplied with water by a branch of the Serino Aqueduct from c. 20 BC. Its economy was diverse, featuring at least 31 bakeries, nearly 100 *thermopolia* (snack-bars), and numerous workshops for wool processing, dyeing, and garum (fish sauce) production. The fertile volcanic soil supported crops like barley, wheat, grapes, and olives.

### The Eruption and Burial
On 24–25 October AD 79, Mount Vesuvius erupted catastrophically. The first phase was a rain of pumice (*lapilli*) lasting about 18 hours, allowing most inhabitants to escape. The second phase involved deadly pyroclastic flows—superheated ash clouds—that killed remaining residents instantly via heat exposure (at least 250°C). The city was buried under up to 6 metres of volcanic material. A 2023 study indicates some building collapses during the eruption also caused fatalities.

### Rediscovery and Excavation
The city's location was forgotten until the 16th century. Systematic excavation began in 1748, marking the birth of modern archaeology. Early methods were haphazard and looting was common. Giuseppe Fiorelli (from 1863) introduced scientific documentation and the plaster-casting technique to preserve voids left by decomposed bodies. Excavations have uncovered about two-thirds of the city. Since 2018, the 'Great Pompeii Project' has focused on conservation and targeted new excavations in previously unexplored areas, leading to discoveries like a ceremonial chariot (2021) and a dining hall with rare frescoes (2024).

### Conservation and Tourism
Preservation is a major challenge due to weathering, erosion, tourism, and past poor restoration. The site was placed on the World Monuments Watch list multiple times. A major EU-funded project began in 2012 to stabilize structures. Pompeii is a top Italian tourist site, attracting about 2.5 million visitors annually. To manage crowds, daily ticket sales are now limited to 20,000, and timed entry is used in peak season.

## Terms
- **Pumice/Lapilli**: Lightweight, porous volcanic rock ejected during an eruption.
- **Pyroclastic Flow**: A fast-moving current of hot gas and volcanic matter that destroys all in its path.
- **Thermopolium**: An ancient Roman snack bar or inn that sold hot food and drinks.
- **Fullonica**: A workshop for cleaning and processing woolen cloth.
- **Garum**: A fermented fish sauce used as a condiment in Roman cuisine.
- **Plaster Casting**: A technique invented by Fiorelli to fill voids in the ash with plaster, creating models of victims and organic objects.
- **Insulae**: City blocks in Roman urban planning.
- **Dolia**: Large terracotta pots used for storing food or drink.
- **Socii**: Allied states in ancient Rome that maintained internal autonomy.
- **Vulgar Latin**: The colloquial, everyday form of Latin spoken by the common people, as opposed to Classical Latin.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Date of Eruption**: Traditionally thought to be in August, but evidence (clothing, food, coins, a 2018 charcoal inscription) strongly supports an October or November date.
- **Cause of Death**: Long believed to be ash suffocation, but a 2010 study concluded that exposure to extreme heat from pyroclastic flows was the primary cause.
- **Population**: Estimates vary widely from 10,000–20,000 based on historical accounts, to a more recent 11,000–11,500 based on household counts.
- **Post-Eruption Activity**: Evidence suggests the site was reoccupied by a small informal settlement until the 5th century, and that systematic looting of valuables like marble statues occurred soon after the burial.
