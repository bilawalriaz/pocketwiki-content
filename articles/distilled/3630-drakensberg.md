# Drakensberg

## Overview
The Drakensberg is the eastern portion of the Great Escarpment, a massive geological formation enclosing the central Southern African plateau. It stretches over 1,000 km along the borders of South Africa and Lesotho, reaching its highest elevation of 3,482 m. The range is a vital water source, a biodiversity hotspot, and holds immense cultural significance due to its vast collection of San rock art.

## Timeline
- **c. 2000 million years ago** — Formation of the ancient quartzite rocks of the Transvaal Supergroup in the north.
- **c. 300 million years ago** — Deposition of the Ecca shales of the Karoo Supergroup.
- **c. 250 million years ago** — Deposition of the younger Beaufort rocks of the Karoo Supergroup.
- **c. 200 million years ago** — Laying down of Clarens sandstone under desert conditions.
- **c. 180 million years ago** — Eruption of basalt lava, forming the thick cap of the Lesotho and Eastern Cape Drakensberg.
- **c. 150 million years ago** — Breakup of Gondwana begins; a failed rift valley forms the gap north of Tzaneen.
- **Past 20 million years** — Massive uplifting of the southern African plateau, especially in the east.
- **At least 40,000 years ago** — San people inhabit the Drakensberg, creating rock art.
- **2000** — uKhahlamba Drakensberg Park is listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Geology and Formation
The Drakensberg is the eastern wall of the Great Escarpment, formed from the breakup of the supercontinent Gondwana. Its composition varies along its length. The northern sections (Limpopo, Mpumalanga) are capped by hard, erosion-resistant quartzite over 2 billion years old. South of the 26°S parallel, the escarpment is composed of softer sedimentary rocks (Ecca and Beaufort groups) from the Karoo Supergroup, making it less rugged. The highest portion, forming the Lesotho-KwaZulu-Natal border, consists of a thick layer of basalt lava (180 million years old) resting on Clarens sandstone. The plateau has been uplifted over the past 20 million years, creating the current high elevation.

### Appearance and Landscape
The escarpment appears as a mountain range from below. Its appearance is dictated by geology: the hard quartzite and basalt sections (Mpumalanga, Lesotho) are rugged with steep pinnacles, inspiring the Zulu name *uKhahlamba* ("Barrier of up-pointed spears"). The softer rock sections (KwaZulu-Natal-Free State border) are more rounded. The top of the escarpment is generally flat. Lightning strikes are a significant force in shaping the summit landscapes, creating angular debris previously attributed solely to periglacial (freeze-thaw) processes.

### Ecology and Hydrology
The Drakensberg is a critical water source, feeding the Orange River (southern Africa's longest) and the Tugela River. It hosts the world's highest waterfall, Tugela Falls (947 m). The ecosystem is divided by elevation: the high alti-montane grasslands (above 2,500 m) feature tussock grasses and endemic plants like the Spiral Aloe. The lower montane grasslands (1,800-2,500 m) are home to rare African conifers (*Podocarpus*). The area supports 299 bird species, including the endemic mountain pipit, and mammals like eland and the southern white rhinoceros, which was saved from extinction here.

### Human Habitation and Cultural Significance
The region includes towns in South Africa and the entire nation of Lesotho. Its most significant cultural feature is the vast collection of San (Bushman) rock art, estimated at 35,000-40,000 paintings in caves formed in the Clarens sandstone. This is the largest concentration of such art in Africa south of the Sahara. Anthropological evidence suggests San presence for at least 40,000 years, with the oldest paintings dating to about 2,400 years ago.

### Conservation
The high, remote peaks are relatively undamaged, and much is protected in reserves and parks, including the UNESCO World Heritage Site uKhahlamba Drakensberg Park. The lower grasslands have been heavily impacted by agriculture and overgrazing. Conservation efforts include the Maloti-Drakensberg Transfrontier Conservation Area, which aims to protect the high mountain ecosystem across national borders.

## Terms
- **Great Escarpment**: A major geological formation of steep cliffs enclosing the central Southern African plateau.
- **Periglacial**: An environment subject to intense freeze-thaw cycles, shaping the landscape.
- **Basalt**: A hard, dark volcanic rock formed from cooled lava.
- **Sandstone**: A sedimentary rock formed from sand-sized grains.
- **Quartzite**: A hard, metamorphic rock formed from sandstone.
- **Alti-montane**: Relating to high mountain zones, typically above the tree line.
- **Endemic**: A species native to and found only in a specific, limited geographic area.
- **San (Bushman)**: The indigenous hunter-gatherer people of Southern Africa, known for their rock art.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact age of the San rock art is difficult to determine due to the materials used; while the oldest paintings are about 2,400 years old, paint chips suggest human presence over a thousand years earlier.
- The origin of the Afrikaans name "Drakensberg" ("Dragons' Mountains") is unknown, as is the reason for its selection by early Dutch settlers.
- The precise status of Tugela Falls as the world's highest waterfall is debated, with Venezuela's Angel Falls also being a candidate.
