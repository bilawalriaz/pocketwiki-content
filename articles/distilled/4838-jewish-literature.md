# Jewish literature

## Overview
Jewish literature encompasses works written by Jews on Jewish themes, in Jewish languages, or by Jewish authors on any theme. It spans from ancient Biblical and rabbinic texts to modern secular works, reflecting the evolution of Jewish culture, language, and identity across millennia. This literature is significant as a record of Jewish thought, history, and creative expression, and it has contributed to the national literatures of many countries.

## Timeline
- **7th–8th centuries** — Liturgical poetry (Piyyut) flourishes in Byzantine Palestine with poets like Yose ben Yose and Eleazar Kalir.
- **12th century** — Joseph Ibn Zabara writes *Sefer Sha'ashu'im*, a story combining folktales and philosophy.
- **13th century** — Isaac ibn Sahula writes *Meshal ha-Kadmoni*, combining aggadah with original stories.
- **1707–1746** — Moses Hayyim Luzzatto writes *La-Yesharim Tehillah*, considered the first product of modern Hebrew literature.
- **1864** — Sholem Yankev Abramovitsh publishes *Dos kleyne mentshele*, marking the start of modern Yiddish literature.
- **Early 20th century** — Hayim Nahman Bialik emerges as a pioneer of modern Hebrew poetry and Israel's national poet.
- **1966** — Shmuel Yosef Agnon wins the Nobel Prize for Literature for his Hebrew novels and stories.
- **1978** — Isaac Bashevis Singer wins the Nobel Prize for Literature for his Yiddish works.
- **1987** — Joseph Brodsky, a Russian-language poet of Jewish descent, wins the Nobel Prize in Literature.

## Body

### Medieval Jewish Literature
Medieval Jewish literature includes fiction, poetry, and other forms. Fiction featured works like *Sefer ha-Ma'asiyyot* (fables based on legends) and *Ben ha-Melekh ve-ha-Nazir* (based on an Indian tale). Poetry flourished, with liturgical forms in Palestine and later religious and secular poems from Spanish, Provençal, and Italian poets like Solomon ibn Gabirol and Yehuda Halevi. Most Hebrew poetry used mono-rhymed, quantitatively metered forms influenced by Arabic style. Other medieval literature comprised philosophical, mystical (Kabbalistic), ethical (musar), and halakhic (legal) works, as well as Bible commentaries.

### Modern Hebrew Literature
Modern Hebrew literature began with the Haskalah (Jewish Enlightenment), breaking from religious traditions. Moses Hayyim Luzzatto's 1743 allegorical drama is a foundational work. The 19th century saw regional centers of writers in Galicia, Amsterdam, Prague, Hungary, Italy, and the Russian Empire, including satirists, lyric poets, and the creator of the Hebrew novel, Abraham Mapu. The early 20th century was dominated by Hayim Nahman Bialik, who revived the Hebrew language, and Shaul Tchernichovsky. Israeli literature later gained international recognition with Nobel laureate Shmuel Yosef Agnon and authors like Amos Oz and A.B. Yehoshua.

### Yiddish, American, and Other Modern Literatures
Modern Yiddish literature began in 1864 and was advanced by Sholem Aleichem and I.L. Peretz, with later Nobel laureate Isaac Bashevis Singer. American Jewish literature in English includes works by Saul Bellow, Philip Roth, and Allen Ginsberg. German-Jewish literature features major figures like Heinrich Heine and Franz Kafka. Russian-language Jewish literature includes Isaac Babel and Nobel laureate Joseph Brodsky. Ladino literature primarily consists of fables and folktales, often featuring the trickster "Ejoha." Judeo-Tat literature includes 20th-century poets like Sergey Izgiyayev and Zoya Semenduyeva.

## Terms
- **Piyyut**: Liturgical Jewish poetry, which flourished in Byzantine Palestine.
- **Aggadah**: Non-legalistic lore and legends in rabbinic literature, used as source material for fiction.
- **Haskalah**: The Jewish Enlightenment movement of the 18th–19th centuries, which promoted secular education and culture.
- **Musar literature**: Ethical literature dealing with virtues and vices.
- **Halakhic literature**: Literature concerning Jewish law.
- **Kabbalistic literature**: Mystical literature within Judaism.
- **Quantitative metre**: A poetic meter based on syllable length, influenced by Arabic poetic style.
- **Yiddish**: A High German-derived language historically spoken by Ashkenazi Jews.
- **Ladino**: Judeo-Spanish, the language of Sephardic Jews.
- **Judeo-Tat**: A Jewish language spoken by the Mountain Jews of the Caucasus.
