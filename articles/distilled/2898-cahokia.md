# Cahokia

## Overview
Cahokia was the largest and most influential urban settlement of the Mississippian culture, a pre-Columbian civilization that flourished in the central and southeastern United States. Located near present-day St. Louis, it was a major political and religious center from c. 1050–1350 AD, with a population that may have exceeded contemporary London and Paris. Its complex society, monumental earthen mounds, and extensive trade networks make it the most significant archaeological site north of Mexico.

## Timeline
- **c. 600 CE** — Cahokia is settled during the Late Woodland period.
- **c. 900 CE** — Mound building begins; maize agriculture is successfully introduced.
- **c. 1050 CE** — The "Big Bang": rapid urbanization, construction of major precincts, and population surge.
- **1100–1200 CE** — The Stirling phase: peak of political centralization and monumental construction.
- **c. 1160–1170 CE** — A large residential compound in East St. Louis is burned, possibly indicating unrest.
- **c. 1175 CE** — The first large central palisade is constructed around the city core.
- **1200–1300 CE** — The Moorehead phase: cultural shifts, weakening political structures, and population decline.
- **c. 1250 CE** — Population decreases by half or more; Cahokian artifacts appear in distant polities like Moundville.
- **c. 1350 CE** — Cahokia is abandoned.
- **1982 CE** — UNESCO designates Cahokia Mounds a World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Rise and Urban Planning
Cahokia's rapid growth around 1050 CE was fueled by large-scale immigration from across the mid-continent. The city was meticulously planned on a quadripartite worldview, oriented to the cardinal directions. Its core featured Monks Mound, the Grand Plaza (a leveled 50-acre ceremonial space), and a network of causeways and smaller mounds. The urban landscape included residential zones, temples, and specialized structures like the Woodhenge timber circles, which were used for astronomical observations.

### Society and Religion
Cahokian society was complex and stratified. Political power was consolidated through religion, evidenced by intense public rituals. Mound 72, a ridge-top mortuary, contained high-status burials, including the "Birdman" burial on a bed of shell beads, and evidence of mass human sacrifice. The city's economy relied on intensive agriculture (maize, beans, squash) and long-distance trade in exotic goods like copper, marine shells, and chert tools. A copper workshop near Mound 34 is the only known Mississippian example, producing religious items like long-nosed god maskettes.

### Decline and Abandonment
Cahokia's decline began in the late 12th century. Scholars propose multiple, interrelated causes: environmental stress (droughts, floods, deforestation), social unrest (evidenced by the burned compound and defensive palisade), and political fragmentation. The large immigrant population may have hindered cultural cohesion. Health issues from a maize-heavy diet (pellagra) and pollution are also suggested. By 1350 CE, the city was abandoned, contributing to a regional depopulation known as the "Vacant Quarter."

## Terms
- **Mississippian culture**: A Native American agricultural society that flourished in the central and southeastern U.S. from c. 1000–1550 CE, characterized by platform mounds and maize agriculture.
- **Platform mound**: A flat-topped earthen mound used as a base for a building, such as a temple or elite residence.
- **Palisade**: A defensive wall of wooden stakes or logs enclosing a settlement.
- **Woodhenge**: A series of large timber circles at Cahokia, likely used as a solar calendar for astronomical observations.
- **Eastern Agricultural Complex**: A suite of native North American crops (e.g., goosefoot, sumpweed) cultivated before the widespread adoption of maize.
- **Nixtamalization**: A process of treating maize with alkali to make it more nutritious and prevent diseases like pellagra.
- **Ridge-top mortuary**: A burial mound constructed on a natural ridge, often for high-status individuals.
- **Greater Cahokia**: The densely populated region of interconnected sites surrounding Cahokia proper, including East St. Louis and St. Louis Mounds.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause of Collapse**: The primary reason for Cahokia's abandonment is debated. Theories include environmental degradation, social unrest, political fragmentation, disease, or a combination of factors. Recent research challenges earlier models of soil depletion and catastrophic flooding.
- **Social Integration**: Scholars debate how well the large immigrant population was integrated. Evidence of separate burials suggests possible ethnic segregation and weak social cohesion.
- **Function of the Palisade**: Whether the large palisade built c. 1175 CE was primarily for defense against warfare or for ritual/formal separation of sacred space is contested.
- **Population Estimates**: The peak population is highly variable, with estimates ranging from 6,000 to 40,000, significantly impacting interpretations of the city's scale and influence.
