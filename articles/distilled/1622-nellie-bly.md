# Nellie Bly

## Overview
Elizabeth Cochrane Seaman (1864–1922), writing as Nellie Bly, was a pioneering American journalist who invented immersion reporting. She first gained fame by feigning insanity to expose abuses at the Women’s Lunatic Asylum on Blackwell’s Island (1887), a stunt that forced institutional reforms and launched the era of "stunt girl" journalism. In 1889–90, she circled the globe in 72 days, beating Jules Verne’s fictional benchmark and becoming a global celebrity. Later, she ran her late husband’s steel-container factory, patented inventions (including a milk can and stacking garbage can), and returned to reporting on suffrage and World War I. Her career demonstrated that women could perform high-stakes investigative work, reshaping the profession.

## Body

### Early Life and Entry into Journalism
Elizabeth Jane Cochran was born in 1864 to Michael Cochran, a mill owner and local official, and his second wife, Mary Jane Kennedy. After her father’s death in 1870, the family lost financial stability. She attended Indiana Normal School for one term in 1879 but left for lack of funds. In 1885, a *Pittsburgh Dispatch* column arguing women belonged only in the home provoked her to write a fiery rebuttal under the name “Lonely Orphan Girl.” Editor George Madden hired her, assigning the pen name “Nellie Bly” (from a Stephen Foster song; the editor misspelled “Nelly”). Her early investigative series on female factory workers—conducted undercover—drew praise from laborers but complaints from owners, leading to her reassignment to the women’s pages. Frustrated, she spent six months in Mexico as a foreign correspondent, publishing *Six Months in Mexico* (1888), until authorities threatened arrest for her criticism of the Díaz dictatorship.

### Asylum Exposé and the Birth of Stunt Journalism
Penniless in New York after four months of rejection, Bly convinced Joseph Pulitzer’s *New York World* to let her feign insanity to investigate the Women’s Lunatic Asylum on Blackwell’s Island. She practiced disturbed behavior in a boarding house until police committed her. Inside, she documented freezing baths, spoiled food, physical abuse, and the warehousing of immigrant women who could not defend themselves in English. After ten days, *World* attorneys secured her release. Her two-part series (Oct. 9, 1887) and book *Ten Days in a Mad-House* caused a sensation, prompted a grand-jury investigation and reforms, and established “stunt girl” reporting—a genre that, while dismissed by some as sensationalism, gave women their first foothold in hard-news journalism.

### Around-the-World Record
In 1888 Bly proposed to her editor a real-life version of Verne’s *Around the World in Eighty Days*. On November 14, 1889, with two days’ notice, she departed on the *Augusta Victoria*, traveling 24,898 miles via England, France (meeting Verne in Amiens), the Suez Canal, Ceylon, Singapore, Hong Kong, and Japan. The *World* ran a “Nellie Bly Guessing Match” to sustain public interest. She returned January 25, 1890, in 72 days, 6 hours, 11 minutes—a world record briefly held until George Francis Train completed the trip in 67 days. Her account, *Nellie Bly’s Book: Around the World in Seventy-two Days* (1890), cemented her fame.

### Industrialist and Inventor
In 1895, at 31, Bly married 73-year-old manufacturer Robert Seaman. When his health failed, she took over Iron Clad Manufacturing Co., which produced steel milk cans and boilers. After Seaman’s death in 1904, the company introduced the 55-gallon steel drum (patented by employee Henry Wehrhahn). Bly herself held U.S. patents 697,553 (milk can) and 703,711 (stacking garbage can). She ran the firm as a welfare model with health benefits and recreation, but poor financial oversight and embezzlement by a manager drove it into bankruptcy.

### Later Reporting and Death
Returning to journalism, Bly covered the 1913 Woman Suffrage Procession for the *New York Evening Journal*, headlining “Suffragists Are Men’s Superiors” and correctly forecasting the 19th Amendment (1920). During World War I she reported from the Serbian–Austrian front, becoming the first woman and one of the first foreigners in that zone; she was briefly arrested as a British spy. She died of pneumonia on January 27, 1922, at St. Mark’s Hospital, New York, and was buried in Woodlawn Cemetery, The Bronx.

### Legacy and Cultural Memory
Bly was inducted into the National Women’s Hall of Fame (1998) and honored on a U.S. postage stamp (2002). The *New York Press Club* awards an annual Nellie Bly Cub Reporter Award; the Museum of Political Corruption gives a Nellie Bly Award for investigative reporting. A memorial sculpture, *The Girl Puzzle*, opened on Roosevelt Island (2021). Her life has inspired a Broadway musical (1946), operas, films (*10 Days in a Madhouse*, 2015), a *Doctor Who* audio drama, and numerous novels. The 1890 board game *Round the World with Nellie Bly*, a Brooklyn amusement park, a tarantula species (*Pamphobeteus nellieblyae*), a Toronto fireboat, and a Pennsylvania Railroad express train all bear her name.

## Terms
- **Stunt girl journalism**: Late-19th-century practice where female reporters went undercover or undertook sensational feats to generate circulation; Bly’s asylum exposé is the archetype.
- **Immersion journalism**: Reporting method in which the journalist enters the environment being investigated (e.g., asylum, factory) to experience conditions firsthand.
- **Blackwell’s Island**: Island in New York’s East River (renamed Roosevelt Island in 1971) housing the Women’s Lunatic Asylum Bly infiltrated.
- **Pulitzer’s *New York World***: Newspaper owned by Joseph Pulitzer that pioneered “New Journalism” and sponsored Bly’s asylum and world-trip assignments.
- **Around the World in Eighty Days**: 1873 Jules Verne novel whose fictional journey Bly replicated and beat in 1889–90.
- **Iron Clad Manufacturing Co.**: Steel-container firm Bly led after her husband’s death; produced early 55-gallon drum prototypes.
- **Elizabeth Bisland Wetmore**: *Cosmopolitan* reporter who raced Bly around the world in the opposite direction (1889–90).
- **Ten Days in a Mad-House**: Bly’s 1887 exposé (articles and book) that triggered asylum reforms and launched her fame.
- **Nellie Bly Guessing Match**: *World* circulation contest inviting readers to predict Bly’s exact return time during her world trip.
- **U.S. Patent 697,553 / 703,711**: Bly’s patents for a novel milk can and a stacking garbage can, filed under her married name Elizabeth Cochrane Seaman.

## Debates and open questions
- **Stunt journalism’s legacy**: Scholars debate whether “stunt girl” reporting was merely a circulation gimmick of the sensationalist press or a crucial entry point that proved women could do high-level investigative work (Brooke Kroeger argues the latter).
- **Authorship of the 55-gallon drum**: Popular accounts often credit Bly with inventing the standard oil drum, but the patents (808,327 and 808,413) belong to Henry Wehrhahn; Bly’s own patents cover a milk can and a stacking garbage can.
- **Lost novels**: Between 1889 and 1895 Bly wrote eleven serial novels for *New York Family Story Paper*; thought lost, they were rediscovered in a British archive in 2021, raising new questions about her literary output and its relationship to her journalism.
- **Financial competence vs. exploitation**: Biographers differ on whether Bly’s business failure stemmed from her own “hopeless” financial naivety (Kroeger) or primarily from systematic embezzlement by trusted employees during protracted bankruptcy litigation.
