# Amman

## Overview
Amman is the capital and largest city of Jordan, serving as the nation's economic, political, and cultural center. Its history spans from a prehistoric Neolithic settlement to the capital of the ancient Ammonite Kingdom, followed by Hellenistic, Roman, and Islamic periods. Modern Amman was revitalized in the late 19th century and has grown exponentially since becoming the capital of Transjordan in 1921, shaped by successive waves of immigration and refugees.

## Timeline
- **c. 7000 BC** — Neolithic settlement at 'Ain Ghazal, known for its ancient plaster statues.
- **13th century BC** — Amman is the capital of the Ammonite Kingdom, known as Rabat Amman.
- **3rd century BC** — Conquered by Ptolemy II Philadelphus and renamed Philadelphia.
- **63 BC** — Conquered by Rome; becomes part of the Decapolis league.
- **7th century AD** — Conquered by the Rashidun Caliphate; renamed Amman.
- **1878** — Resettled by Circassians under the Ottoman Empire.
- **1903** — Connected to the Hejaz Railway, spurring commercial growth.
- **1921** — Designated the capital of the Emirate of Transjordan.
- **1948** — Major influx of Palestinian refugees after the Arab–Israeli War.
- **2005** — Al-Qaeda hotel bombings kill 60 people.
- **2011** — Onset of significant refugee influx from the Syrian Civil War.

## Body

### Ancient and Medieval History
The area of Amman has been inhabited since the Neolithic period, with the site of 'Ain Ghazal yielding some of the world's oldest human statues. In the Iron Age, it was the Ammonite capital of Rabat Amman. After Alexander the Great's conquest, the Ptolemaic ruler Ptolemy II renamed it Philadelphia. The city later flourished as a member of the Roman Decapolis, featuring a theater, temples, and colonnaded streets. Following the Islamic conquest in the 7th century, its original Semitic name, Amman, was restored. Its importance waxed and waned through the Umayyad, Abbasid, Crusader, and Mamluk periods, eventually being largely abandoned by the Ottoman era.

### Modern Revival and Growth
Amman was resettled in 1878 by Circassian refugees. Its transformation into a major hub began with the arrival of the Hejaz Railway in 1903. Designated the capital of Transjordan in 1921, it attracted immigrants from across the Levant. The city's population surged with successive waves of refugees: Palestinians in 1948 and 1967, Iraqis in 1990 and 2003, and Syrians from 2011 onward. Originally built on seven hills, it now spans over 19 hills, with East Amman containing historic sites and West Amman serving as the modern economic center.

### Economy and Infrastructure
Amman has a fast-growing economy and is ranked as a Beta− global city. It is a major hub for multinational corporations in the Middle East, particularly in banking, pharmaceuticals, and logistics. Tourism is significant, with the city being a center for medical tourism. The city is served by Queen Alia International Airport and has a developing public transport system, including a Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) network. Rapid urban expansion has strained infrastructure, notably water supply and road capacity.

### Demographics and Culture
Amman's population reached over 4 million by 2015, comprising about 42% of Jordan's total. The city lacks a single distinct dialect due to its diverse origins, with the main demographic groups being of Palestinian and Jordanian descent. It is a predominantly Sunni Muslim city with a significant Christian minority. Culturally, Amman is considered liberal, with a vibrant culinary scene, nightlife, and numerous museums, including The Jordan Museum. The city hosts various cultural events and has a growing independent music scene.

## Terms
- **Primate city**: A city that is disproportionately larger and more influential than other cities in the country.
- **Decapolis**: A league of ten Greek cities in the eastern Roman Empire, including Philadelphia (Amman).
- **Hejaz Railway**: A historic railway line connecting Damascus to Medina, crucial for Amman's early 20th-century growth.
- **Transjordan**: The former name for the territory east of the Jordan River, which became the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan.
- **Circassians**: A people from the North Caucasus region, many of whom were resettled in Ottoman Amman in the late 19th century.
- **Greater Amman Municipality (GAM)**: The administrative body governing the city of Amman.
- **Bus Rapid Transit (BRT)**: A high-capacity bus-based public transport system.
- **Beta− global city**: A city classification indicating it is an important world city that links regional economies to the world economy.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact location and nature of the Crusader castle in Amman, referenced in historical texts, remain uncertain, with archaeological evidence pointing more strongly to an Ayyubid-period structure.
- The proportion of Amman's population with Palestinian versus Jordanian (from other parts of Jordan) descent is not officially recorded, making precise demographic analysis difficult.
- The rapid, large-scale urban expansion of Amman has created significant challenges for municipal planning and resource management, such as water scarcity, which are ongoing concerns.
