# Hong Xiuquan

## Overview
Hong Xiuquan (1814–1864) was a Chinese revolutionary and religious leader who, after failing imperial examinations and experiencing visions, came to believe he was the younger brother of Jesus Christ. He led the Taiping Rebellion, a massive uprising against the Qing dynasty that established the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom in southern China. His movement blended a unique form of Christianity with Chinese traditions and millenarian ideas, resulting in one of the deadliest conflicts in history.

## Body

### Early Life and Visions
Born into a Hakka family, Hong Xiuquan repeatedly failed the civil service examinations, which had a pass rate of less than one percent. After his third failure in 1837, he suffered a nervous breakdown and had visions of a heavenly family, including a celestial father and elder brother. In 1843, after a fourth failure, he interpreted these visions through Christian pamphlets he had received years earlier. He concluded his celestial father was God the Father, his elder brother was Jesus Christ, and he was divinely tasked to rid the world of "demon worship," which he identified with Confucianism and Buddhism.

### The God Worshipping Society
Hong began destroying idols and preaching his syncretic faith, which fused Christianity, Daoism, and millenarianism. He presented it as a restoration of the ancient Chinese faith in Shangdi. His associate Feng Yunshan founded the God Worshipping Society to spread these teachings. By 1850, the sect had over 10,000 followers, many of them Hakka, and came into increasing conflict with Qing authorities and local groups in Guangxi.

### Rebellion and the Heavenly Kingdom
In January 1851, Hong's forces routed Qing troops at Jintian, formally starting the Taiping Rebellion. After a mobile campaign, they captured Nanjing in 1853, making it the capital, Tianjing. Hong implemented radical reforms, including a communal treasury, gender equality in theory (though men and women were segregated), and the suppression of opium and polygamy. He ruled from his palace through proclamations. The movement's internal politics turned violent in 1856 with the Tianjing incident, where Hong purged the powerful leader Yang Xiuqing and others, severely weakening the rebellion.

### Decline and Death
The Taiping Kingdom gradually lost ground after a failed attack on Shanghai in 1860. By 1864, Tianjing was besieged and starving. Hong ordered his subjects to eat "manna," which he gathered as weeds. He fell ill in April 1864 and died on June 1st. He was succeeded by his teenage son, Hong Tianguifu. Qing forces captured the city a month later, exhumed Hong's body, and cremated it to ensure he had no resting place.

## Terms
- **Taiping Rebellion**: A massive civil war in China (1850–1864) led by Hong Xiuquan against the Qing dynasty.
- **God Worshipping Society**: The religious sect founded by Feng Yunshan to spread Hong Xiuquan's teachings.
- **Heavenly Kingdom (Taiping Tianguo)**: The theocratic state established by Hong Xiuquan in southern China.
- **Tianjing (Heavenly Capital)**: The name given to Nanjing, the Taiping capital.
- **Hakka**: A Chinese ethnic group; Hong and many early followers were Hakka.
- **Millenarianism**: A belief in a coming fundamental transformation of society, often linked to a messianic figure.
- **Shangdi**: A supreme deity in ancient Chinese religion, whom Hong identified with the Christian God.
- **Tianjing incident**: The 1856 internal purge within the Taiping leadership initiated by Hong Xiuquan.
- **Green Standard Army**: The regular army of the Qing dynasty that fought the Taiping rebels.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact cause of Hong Xiuquan's death is debated; while likely from illness related to eating weeds, suicide by poison has also been suggested.
- The nature of Hong's visions and his sincerity in believing his divine mission are subjects of scholarly analysis.
- The legacy of Hong and the Taiping Rebellion is contested, with views ranging from admiration by Chinese revolutionaries like Sun Yat-sen and Mao Zedong to condemnation as a destructive cult.
