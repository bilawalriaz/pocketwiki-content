# Ibn al-Haytham

## Overview
Ibn al-Haytham (c. 965–c. 1040), known in the West as Alhazen, was a polymath of the Islamic Golden Age whose work in optics fundamentally changed the scientific understanding of vision and light. He is considered the father of modern optics and a pioneer of the scientific method, emphasizing experimentation and mathematical proof centuries before the Renaissance. His most influential work, the *Book of Optics*, corrected ancient theories and laid the groundwork for later scientists like Kepler and Newton.

## Timeline
- **c. 965** — Born in Basra, Iraq.
- **Early career** — Held a vizier position in Basra; attempted to regulate Nile flooding.
- **c. 1011–1021** — Wrote his seminal work, *Kitāb al-Manāẓir* (*Book of Optics*).
- **After 1021** — Lived in Cairo, writing and tutoring after the death of Caliph Al-Hakim.
- **c. 1025–1028** — Published *Doubts Concerning Ptolemy*, criticizing Ptolemaic astronomy.
- **c. 1038** — Wrote *The Model of the Motions of Each of the Seven Planets*.
- **c. 1040** — Died in Cairo.
- **Late 12th/early 13th c.** — *Book of Optics* translated into Latin, influencing European science.
- **1572** — Latin edition of *Optics* published by Friedrich Risner.

## Body

### Theory of Vision and the *Book of Optics*
Ibn al-Haytham's *Book of Optics* (1011–1021) synthesized earlier Greek and Islamic theories to create a new, comprehensive model of vision. He definitively argued for the **intromission theory**, proving that vision occurs when light reflects from an object and enters the eye, rather than the eye emitting rays (**extramission theory**). He correctly identified that vision is processed in the brain and is subjective, affected by personal experience. His theory explained how a coherent image forms on the eye by asserting that only perpendicular rays from each point on an object are perceived, resolving the problem of multiple rays. While this explanation had weaknesses, it was the most comprehensive of its time and enormously influential in Europe, inspiring later work by Kepler.

### Contributions to Optics and Physics
Ibn al-Haytham conducted systematic experiments with lenses, mirrors, and light, establishing that light travels in straight lines. He was the first to give a complete statement of the **law of reflection**, specifying that the incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in the same plane. He studied **refraction**, noting that light's speed changes in different media and that it follows the path of "least time," an early form of Fermat's principle. His work in **catoptrics** (mirror study) included analyzing spherical and parabolic mirrors and describing **spherical aberration**. He also provided the first clear description and analysis of the **camera obscura**, using it to observe solar eclipses.

### Scientific Method and Mathematical Work
Ibn al-Haytham was a pioneer of the **scientific method**, insisting that hypotheses must be tested through repeatable experiments and mathematical proof. He systematically varied experimental conditions to study phenomena like light intensity. In mathematics, he worked on **conic sections** and **number theory**, deriving formulas for sums of powers (including the fourth power) that enabled him to calculate the volume of a paraboloid—a method akin to integration. He also explored the **Euclidean parallel postulate** and formulated the **Lambert quadrilateral**.

### Astronomy and Other Fields
He wrote over 200 works, critiquing Ptolemaic astronomy in *Doubts Concerning Ptolemy* for its physical contradictions. He proposed a new geometric planetary model that eliminated the equant while maintaining a geocentric universe. He also wrote on theology, philosophy, and mechanics, and his *Book of Optics* contained early discussions on the psychology of perception, including an explanation of the **Moon illusion** based on perceived distance.

## Terms
- **Intromission theory**: The theory that vision occurs when light rays enter the eye from an object.
- **Extramission theory**: The theory that vision occurs when the eye emits rays that interact with objects.
- **Catoptrics**: The branch of optics dealing with reflection, especially by mirrors.
- **Dioptrics**: The branch of optics dealing with refraction, especially through lenses.
- **Law of reflection**: The principle that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, with all rays lying in the same plane.
- **Spherical aberration**: The blurring of an image caused by light rays striking different parts of a spherical mirror or lens not converging at a single point.
- **Camera obscura**: A darkened room or box with a small hole through which an outside scene is projected as an inverted image on the opposite wall.
- **Scientific method**: A systematic approach to research involving observation, hypothesis, experimentation, and verification.
- **Conic sections**: Curves (ellipse, parabola, hyperbola) formed by intersecting a cone with a plane.

## Debates and Open questions
Scholars debate the extent to which Ibn al-Haytham's experimental techniques were truly novel or built upon earlier Islamic and ancient traditions. His claim to have discovered the principle of least time is noted as an early form of Fermat's principle, but its direct influence is unclear. Some, like psychologist Omar Khaleifa, argue he founded experimental psychology and psychophysics, but this is contested due to a lack of evidence for quantitative methods. His explanation of vision using perpendicular rays, while influential, was recognized even then as incomplete.
