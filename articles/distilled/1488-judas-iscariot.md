# Judas Iscariot

## Overview
Judas Iscariot (c. 3 AD – c. 30–33 AD) was one of the original Twelve Apostles of Jesus Christ according to the four canonical gospels. He is infamous for betraying Jesus to the Sanhedrin in the Garden of Gethsemane for thirty pieces of silver, identifying him with a kiss. This act precipitated Jesus’s crucifixion, which Christian theology holds brought salvation to humanity, creating a paradox where Judas’s treachery was necessary for divine fulfillment yet earned him eternal condemnation. The gospels offer conflicting motives—greed, demonic possession (Satan entering him), or political disillusionment—and contradictory accounts of his death (suicide by hanging in Matthew; a gruesome fall in Acts). Non-canonical texts like the *Gospel of Judas* (2nd century AD) reframe him as Jesus’s truest disciple acting on secret instructions. Historically, Judas’s name became a byword for betrayal and was weaponized in medieval Christian antisemitism to personify the Jewish people.

## Timeline
- **c. 30–33 AD** — Judas betrays Jesus in Gethsemane; Jesus crucified; Judas dies (accounts vary).
- **c. 55 AD** — Paul’s First Corinthians 11:23–24 uses *paradidōmi* ("handed over/betrayed"), earliest textual allusion.
- **c. 70 AD** — Gospel of Mark (earliest gospel) names Judas Iscariot as one of the Twelve and the betrayer.
- **c. 80–90 AD** — Gospels of Matthew and Luke written; Matthew details 30 pieces of silver and suicide; Luke adds Satan entering Judas.
- **c. 90–100 AD** — Gospel of John written; identifies Judas as treasurer, thief, and possessed by Satan; omits 30 pieces of silver.
- **c. 100 AD** — Papias of Hierapolis records a grotesque, non-canonical account of Judas’s death by divine swelling.
- **c. 180 AD** — Irenaeus denounces the *Gospel of Judas* as heretical fiction in *Against Heresies*.
- **2nd Century AD** — *Gospel of Judas* composed (Coptic copy found c. 1970s, published 2006); portrays Judas as sole recipient of secret gnosis.
- **4th Century AD** — *Gospel of Nicodemus* adds legend of Judas’s wife and a miraculous chicken.
- **Medieval Period** — Judas becomes archetype of traitor in art/literature; associated with red hair and Jewish stereotypes, fueling antisemitism.
- **1320s** — Dante’s *Divine Comedy* places Judas in the ninth circle of Hell (Judecca), eaten by Lucifer.
- **1965** — Hugh J. Schonfield’s *The Passover Plot* argues Judas acted with Jesus’s consent to fulfill prophecy.
- **2006** — National Geographic publishes translation of the Coptic *Gospel of Judas* codex.

## Body

### Historicity and Name
Most secular historians accept Judas’s historical existence, though a minority (e.g., Hyam Maccoby, John Shelby Spong) argue the figure was constructed as an attack on Judaeans. The name "Judas" (Greek for Hebrew *Judah*, "praised") was extremely common; the New Testament lists several positive figures named Judas (Jesus’s brother Jude, apostle Judas son of James, prophet Judas Barsabbas). The epithet "Iscariot" likely derives from Hebrew *Ish-Kerioth* ("man from Kerioth"), supported by John 6:71 naming his father "Simon Iscariot." Alternative theories—Latin *sicarius* ("dagger-man," linking him to the Sicarii rebels), Hebrew for "liar," or Aramaic for "to deliver"—are debated but lack gospel support or anachronistically place the Sicarii in the 30s AD.

### Role as Apostle and Betrayal
All four gospels list Judas among the Twelve. The Synoptics state he participated in the apostolic ministry of preaching, healing, and exorcism. John differentiates him as the treasurer (*glōssokomon*) who stole funds and was identified by Jesus as "a devil" (John 6:70). The betrayal is multiply attested (Mark, John, Acts), leading scholars like Bart Ehrman to deem it historically certain; early Christians would not invent a story reflecting poorly on Jesus’s judgment. Ehrman argues Judas betrayed not Jesus’s location (known publicly) but his secret teaching that he was the Messiah. John P. Meier summarizes the consensus: Jesus chose him, and he handed Jesus to Jerusalem authorities, precipitating execution.

Motives vary by gospel: Mark implies money was offered but not necessarily the primary motive; Matthew has Judas ask for payment (30 pieces of silver); Luke and John state Satan entered him. John adds Judas objected to perfume "wasted" on Jesus. Historical hypotheses suggest Judas was a nationalist Zealot disillusioned by Jesus’s passivity, or that authorities wanted Jesus restrained until after Passover. Jesus foresaw the betrayal (Mark 14:18; John 13:21), and at the Last Supper identified the betrayer by dipping bread (John 13:26).

### Death Accounts
**Matthew 27:3–10:** Remorseful, Judas returns the silver to priests, who refuse it ("blood money"). He hangs himself (per Deut 21:22–23). Priests buy a potter’s field (Akeldama, "Field of Blood") for burying strangers.
**Acts 1:18 (Peter speaking):** Judas bought the field himself with the "wages of wickedness"; falling headlong, his body burst open.
**Papias (c. 100 AD):** God’s wrath caused grotesque swelling; Judas died on his land, emitting a stench lasting a century.
**Gospel of Nicodemus (4th c.):** Judas’s wife mocks his fear of Jesus’s resurrection; a roasting chicken crows, proving resurrection possible; Judas hangs himself.
**Gospel of Judas:** Judas visions the disciples stoning him.

The Matthew/Acts discrepancy challenged biblical inerrancy (e.g., C.S. Lewis). Harmonizations (Augustine: rope snapped, body fell and burst) are rejected by modern scholars; ancient histories often vary (cf. Tacitus/Suetonius on Otho). David Reed argues Matthew’s account is a *midrash* fulfilling Zechariah 11:12–13 (30 pieces, potter) and Jeremiah 19/32 (potter’s field, blood, burial). Matthew’s citation of "Jeremiah" for a Zechariah passage is noted as an error by Jerome and Calvin; James White suggests "Jeremiah" headed the prophetic scroll group.

### Theological Debates: Free Will and Damnation
The tension between divine foreknowledge and human agency dominates Judas theology. If Jesus foresaw the betrayal (John 13:18, 17:12), was Judas free? Bruce Reichenbach argues foreknowledge negates free will and thus culpability; others counter that foreknowledge ≠ causation. The gospels bind Judas to "fulfillment of Scripture" (Psalm 41:9; Zechariah 11) yet pronounce "woe" (Mark 14:21). Early churches saw "as it is written" fulfilled in Isaiah 53/Psalm 22 (suffering servant), not requiring Judas’s specific betrayal.

**Protestant views:** Erasmus defended free will; Luther denied it (immutable will). Calvin held Judas predestined to damnation yet guilty, as God’s will to deliver the Son does not transfer guilt to God.
**Catholic doctrine:** No official "Canon of the Damned." Vatican II speaks generally of hell; Catechism §597 states Judas’s personal sin is known only to God (context: rejecting collective Jewish guilt). The Council of Trent (Canon 6) anathematizes the view that God *properly* produces evil (Judas’s betrayal), affirming human free will. The Tridentine Mass Collect for Holy Thursday contrasts Judas’s punishment with the good thief’s reward. Ludwig Ott cites Judas as an example of particular judgment to eternal perdition.
**Literary/Dante:** *Divine Comedy* places Judas in Judecca (9th circle), eternally chewed by Lucifer alongside Brutus and Cassius (traitors to benefactors).
**Balthasar (1969):** Re-reads *paradidōmi* as Jesus "handing himself over"; speculates on "eternal super-kenosis" where God endures godlessness. Critics (Léon Cristiani) label this Origenism (denying eternal hell), condemned in 548 AD.

### Apocryphal and Gnostic Roles
**Cainites (per Irenaeus):** Gnostic sect venerating Judas as instrument of Sophia (Wisdom); his betrayal defeated the Demiurge (material creator), a victory over matter.
**Syriac Infancy Gospel:** Boy Judas possessed by Satan bites Jesus; contact exorcises Satan. The bitten side matches the Crucifixion spear wound.
**Gospel of Judas (2nd c., Coptic codex 1970s):** Jesus reveals cosmic mysteries to Judas alone; Judas sacrifices Jesus’s body to liberate the divine spark. National Geographic’s 2006 translation ("truest disciple") was contested by April DeConick (translating *daimon* as "demon," not "spirit") and Joan Acocella (questioning motives to counter antisemitism). Consensus: no historical information.
**Gospel of Barnabas (medieval, Islamic-aligned):** Judas transformed into Jesus’s likeness and crucified in his place; Jesus ascends, later returns to reveal truth. Rejected by Muslim scholars (contradicts Quran, uses Andalusi Arabic).

### Cultural Representations
Judas is the Western archetype of the traitor. In Eastern Orthodox Holy Wednesday hymns, he contrasts with the repentant sinful woman; Wednesday fasting commemorates his betrayal. Eucharistic prayers reference "not betraying you with a kiss like Judas." Artistic conventions: red hair (Spanish culture, Shakespeare), linking him to negative Jewish stereotypes; dark or absent halo at the Last Supper. Literature: 13th-century English ballad blames Judas’s sister; Caravaggio’s *Taking of Christ* (1602); Elgar’s *The Apostles* (Judas forces Jesus’s hand); Borges’s "Three Versions of Judas" (1944); *Jesus Christ Superstar* (1970, Judas as protagonist); *The Last Days of Judas Iscariot* (2005, Purgatory trial); *The Book of Longings* (2020, Judas as Zealot revolutionary).

## Terms
- ****Sanhedrin**** — The supreme Jewish legislative and judicial council in Jerusalem, to whom Judas betrayed Jesus.
- ****Paradidōmi (παραδίδωμι)**** — Greek verb meaning "to hand over," "deliver up," or "betray"; used in Paul and gospels for Judas’s act and God’s delivery of Jesus.
- ****Sicarii**** — 1st-century Jewish radicals ("dagger-men") who assassinated opponents in crowds; hypothesized (disputedly) as the root of "Iscariot."
- ****Demiurge**** — In Gnosticism, the inferior creator god of the material world (often identified with the Old Testament God), distinct from the supreme Monad/One.
- ****Pleroma**** — In Gnosticism, the fullness of the divine realm/aeons; the *Gospel of Judas* claims Jesus and Judas reveal truths about it.
- ****Akeldama (Ḥaqel Dama)**** — Aramaic "Field of Blood"; purchased with Judas’s returned silver (Matthew) or by Judas himself (Acts) for burying strangers.
- ****Midrash**** — Jewish interpretive method expanding scripture; scholars argue Matthew’s death narrative is a midrash on Zechariah/Jeremiah.
- ****Super-kenosis**** — Hans Urs von Balthasar’s term: God’s eternal "self-emptying" enduring abandonment/death within the Trinity (from *kenosis*, Phil 2:7).
- ****Origenism**** — Theological system of Origen (condemned 548 AD) including apokatastasis (universal salvation) and denial of eternal hell.
- ****Pseudepigrapha**** — Falsely attributed writings; e.g., *Gospel of Barnabas* (not by 1st-c. Barnabas), *Gospel of Nicodemus* (4th c.).

## Debates and open questions
1. **Historicity of the betrayal details:** While Judas’s existence and role as betrayer are widely accepted, scholars debate whether the specific motives (greed vs. nationalism vs. demonic influence) and the 30 pieces of silver are historical or theological constructions fulfilling Zechariah 11.
2. **Translation of *paradidōmi*:** Whether Paul’s use implies a human betrayer (Judas) or God’s sovereign "handing over" of Jesus (Rom 8:32) affects the earliest evidence for Judas’s act.
3. **Origin of "Iscariot":** "Man from Kerioth" is the leading etymology, but the *sicarius* (Zealot) theory persists despite chronological issues; the name’s true origin remains uncertain.
4. **Harmonization of death accounts:** Whether Matthew (suicide) and Acts (accidental fall) describe the same event (rope snapped) or reflect independent traditions (Papias offers a third) is unresolved; modern scholarship generally views them as irreconcilable legendary developments.
5. **Theological status of Judas:** Catholic teaching avoids declaring Judas damned (no "Canon of the Damned"), yet Trent’s Catechism and liturgy strongly imply eternal perdition. The tension between Judas as necessary instrument of salvation and morally culpable agent remains a core paradox in atonement theology.
6. **Value of the *Gospel of Judas*:** Whether the 2006 Coptic text preserves independent early traditions or is a late 2nd-century Gnostic fabrication with zero historical value is settled toward the latter, but its interpretation (*daimon* = spirit vs. demon) remains contested.
