# Banff National Park

## Overview
Banff National Park, established in 1885 as Canada's first national park, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site in the Alberta Rocky Mountains. It is a globally significant protected area whose history reflects an ongoing tension between conservation and development, driven by tourism, resource extraction, and evolving environmental policies.

## Timeline
- **1885** — Banff Hot Springs Reserve established by Prime Minister John A. Macdonald.
- **1887** — Rocky Mountains Park Act expands the reserve, creating Canada's first national park.
- **1902** — Park expanded to 11,400 km², later reduced in 1911 due to grazing/logging pressure.
- **1917** — First Banff Winter Carnival held; elk introduced from Yellowstone.
- **1930** — National Parks Act renames it Banff National Park and fixes its area at 6,697 km².
- **1940** — Alberta's first known mountain pine beetle outbreak occurs in Banff.
- **1968** — Banff Springs Hotel winterized, making Banff a year-round destination.
- **1984** — Declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of the Canadian Rocky Mountain Parks.
- **1988** — National Parks Act amended, making ecological integrity the first priority.
- **1990s** — Banff–Bow Valley Study initiated to address environmental concerns from development.
- **2009** — Last known caribou in the park die in an avalanche.
- **2017** — Plains bison reintroduced to the park.

## Body

### History and Establishment
The park was created from a dispute over hot springs discovered during Canadian Pacific Railway construction. Conservationists prevailed, setting aside the springs as a protected reserve. The railway was instrumental in early development, building grand hotels and advertising to attract wealthy tourists. The park's boundaries fluctuated for decades, expanding and contracting due to political and economic pressures before being fixed in 1930.

### Human Impact and Internment
The park's history includes the removal of Stoney Nakoda First Nations people between 1890 and 1920. During World War I, immigrants from Austria-Hungary and Germany were interned in camps at Castle Mountain and Cave and Basin, where they built early infrastructure. During the Great Depression, public works projects constructed much of the park's current infrastructure, including roads and facilities.

### Conservation and Management
Management policies have shifted from viewing wildlife as game to prioritizing ecological integrity. The 1988 amendment to the National Parks Act made this the top priority. The Banff–Bow Valley Study (1994-1996) resulted in over 500 recommendations to limit development, including capping the town of Banff's population and curtailing resort expansions. Parks Canada now uses an ecosystem-based management approach.

### Geology and Glaciers
The park's mountains are composed of sedimentary rock pushed east during the Laramide orogeny (80-55 million years ago) and carved by glaciers. The Columbia Icefield is the largest uninterrupted glacial mass in the Rockies. Glaciers are retreating rapidly; between 1985 and 2005, 29 glaciers disappeared from the park, and the total glaciated area dropped from 625 to 500 km².

### Ecology and Wildlife
The park spans montane, subalpine, and alpine ecoregions. It is home to 56 mammal species, including grizzly bears, wolves (which recolonized in the 1980s), elk, and bighorn sheep. Human activity has caused significant imbalance, including the extirpation of native species like the Banff longnose dace and the decline of caribou. Wildlife crossings and fencing along the Trans-Canada Highway have reduced animal-vehicle collisions by over 80%.

### Tourism and Development
Banff is one of North America's most visited national parks, with over three million tourists annually. Tourism contributes an estimated CA$6 billion to the economy. Development pressures, such as the expansion of Sunshine Village ski resort, have been contentious and often challenged by environmental groups like CPAWS. The Icefields Parkway, a 230-km scenic road, connects Banff to Jasper National Park.

## Terms
- **Ecological Integrity**: The condition of an ecosystem where native species and processes are intact, made the first priority for park management in 1988.
- **Laramide Orogeny**: A period of mountain-building (80-55 million years ago) that formed the Rocky Mountains by pushing sedimentary rock layers eastward.
- **Montane/Subalpine/Alpine**: The three ecoregions in the park, defined by elevation and vegetation, from lower forests to treeless peaks.
- **Prescribed Burns**: Controlled fires set by park management to mimic natural fire cycles and restore ecosystem health.
- **Wildlife Crossings**: Structures like underpasses and overpasses built along highways to allow animals to migrate safely and reduce vehicle collisions.
- **UNESCO World Heritage Site**: A place listed for its cultural or natural significance, with an obligation to preserve it for future generations.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Development vs. Conservation**: Ongoing conflict between expanding tourism infrastructure (e.g., ski resorts, highways) and preserving ecological integrity, as seen in the Sunshine Village and Three Sisters developments.
- **Wildlife Management Efficacy**: Questions persist about the success of strategies like elk population control and the reintroduction of bison, and whether they adequately restore natural predator-prey dynamics.
- **Climate Change Impact**: The accelerating retreat of glaciers threatens future water supplies and alters the park's landscape and ecosystems.
- **Indigenous Rights and History**: The park's history includes the displacement of First Nations, and there is a growing movement to reassert their cultural presence and rights within the park.
