# Tenochtitlan

## Overview
Tenochtitlan was the capital city of the Aztec Empire, built on an island in Lake Texcoco in the Valley of Mexico. Founded c. 1325, it grew to become the largest city in the pre-Columbian Americas, renowned for its engineering and urban planning. Its capture by Spanish conquistadors and their Tlaxcaltec allies in 1521 marked the end of the Aztec Empire and led to the founding of Mexico City on its ruins.

## Timeline
- **c. 1325** — Mexica people found Tenochtitlan on a swampy island in Lake Texcoco, following a prophecy of an eagle on a cactus.
- **1453** — Completion of the "levee of Nezahualcoyotl," separating fresh and brackish waters around the city.
- **1473** — Tenochtitlan wages war against its neighbor Tlatelolco.
- **1486–1502** — Reign of Ahuitzotl; the city is rebuilt and expanded after a major flood.
- **8 November 1519** — Hernán Cortés and his men arrive in Tenochtitlan.
- **1520** — During the Toxcatl festival, Spanish forces massacre Aztec nobles and warriors.
- **13 August 1521** — After a 90-day siege, Tenochtitlan falls to Spanish and Tlaxcaltec forces.
- **Post-1521** — Cortés founds the Spanish capital of Mexico City on the city's ruins.
- **1978–1982** — Major excavations of the Templo Mayor ruins begin after the discovery of the Coyolxauhqui stone disc.

## Body

### Geography and Engineering
Tenochtitlan covered an estimated 8 to 13.5 km² on the western side of Lake Texcoco. The city was connected to the mainland by three causeways with removable bridges and was interlaced with canals for canoe traffic. To manage the brackish lake, a 12–16 km levee was built c. 1453 to keep fresh spring water around the city. Two terracotta aqueducts, each over 4 km long, supplied fresh water from Chapultepec springs for cleaning and washing.

### City Planning and Society
The city was divided into four zones, each split into 20 districts called *calpullis* ("big houses"). A central walled ceremonial precinct contained about 45 public buildings, including the Templo Mayor (dedicated to Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc), the *tlachtli* (ball game court), and the palace of Moctezuma II, which featured 100 rooms, zoos, a botanical garden, and an aquarium. The main marketplace in neighboring Tlatelolco was estimated by Cortés to host 60,000 traders daily. Society was highly stratified, with classes including the *macehualtin* (commoners), *pipiltin* (nobles), and *pochteca* (merchants). Status was displayed through housing materials, from reed-and-mud for commoners to stone masonry for the wealthy.

### Conquest and Colonial Legacy
Cortés was welcomed by Moctezuma II in 1519. Tensions escalated after Spanish officers were killed in a provincial incident. In 1520, during the Toxcatl festival, Spanish forces under Pedro de Alvarado massacred Aztec nobles, triggering a revolt. The Spanish were besieged in the city and eventually expelled. Cortés returned to besiege Tenochtitlan for over 90 days, causing famine and spreading diseases to which the population had no immunity. The city fell on 13 August 1521. Cortés then systematically destroyed and rebuilt it as the Spanish capital, Mexico City. A European-only *traza* was built in the center, while the indigenous population was settled in two main areas, San Juan Tenochtitlan and Santiago Tlatelolco.

### Ruins and Archaeology
The Templo Mayor was dismantled to build a cathedral. Its location was rediscovered in the 20th century, with major excavations beginning in 1978 after the discovery of a 3.25-meter stone disc depicting the moon goddess Coyolxauhqui. The ruins, built in seven periods, have sunk at an angle due to the lake sediment. Mexico City's Zócalo stands on the original central plaza, and many modern streets follow the ancient causeways. The Aztec calendar stone, a 4-meter, 18.1-metric-ton sculpture carved c. 1470, was also found in the ruins.

## Terms
- **Altepetl**: A Nahuatl term for a city-state or polity; Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco were two such states on the island.
- **Calpulli**: A district or neighborhood within the city, meaning "big house," which served as a basic unit of social and land organization.
- **Chinampa**: An agricultural system of "floating gardens" used to expand the island and grow food.
- **Macehualtin**: The commoner class in Aztec society.
- **Pipiltin**: The nobility class, consisting of relatives of leaders.
- **Pochteca**: A hereditary class of long-distance merchants who traded across Mesoamerica.
- **Tlachtli**: A ball game court with religious and ritual significance.
- **Trazas**: The European-only central district established by the Spanish in colonial Mexico City.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact founding date of Tenochtitlan is unclear; 13 March 1325 was a symbolic choice made in 1925.
- The etymology of "Tenochtitlan" is uncertain. It is traditionally thought to mean "Among the prickly pears [growing among] rocks," but a late 16th-century manuscript suggests the vowel sound was different, leaving the true meaning debated.
- Population estimates for the city at the time of Spanish contact vary widely, from over 200,000 to as high as 400,000 inhabitants.
