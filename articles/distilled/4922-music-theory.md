# Music theory

## Overview
Music theory is the study of the frameworks that explain how music is created, performed, and understood. It encompasses both practical rudiments (like notation) and scholarly analysis of musical principles across cultures and history. As a discipline, it seeks to define the fundamental materials and processes of music, distinguishing it from the analysis of individual works.

## Timeline
- **c. 1200–1000 BCE** — The Samaveda and Yajurveda provide early Indian musical testimony.
- **Before 1500 BCE** — Earliest known Mesopotamian clay tablets with musical theory (intervals, tunings).
- **c. 238 BCE** — The Chinese *Lüshi chunqiu* recalls the legend of Ling Lun creating twelve pitch pipes.
- **200 BCE–200 CE** — The Indian *Natya Shastra* discusses intervals, scales, and melodic structures.
- **c. 500 CE** — Boethius writes *De institutione musica*, a key medieval European treatise.
- **1028** — Guido d'Arezzo introduces solmization syllables for describing notes and intervals.
- **c. 1280** — Franco of Cologne codifies mensural notation for rhythmic duration.
- **1859** — France standardizes concert A at 435 Hz (later superseded by 440 Hz).
- **1977** — The Society for Music Theory is founded in the United States.

## Body

### Definition and Scope
Music theory has three interrelated uses: the study of rudiments (notation, key signatures); the history of scholarly views on music; and a sub-field of musicology defining general musical processes. It is concerned with how musicians make music, including tuning systems and composition. Because the conception of music expands, a modern definition could include any sonic phenomena, including silence. Historically, medieval European study of music in the Quadrivium was an abstract system of proportions, distant from practice, which later influenced tuning systems.

### Historical Foundations
The earliest theoretical texts are Mesopotamian clay tablets (before 1500 BCE) listing intervals and tunings. Ancient Chinese theory began with numbers (twelve pitches, five-note scales) and legends like Ling Lun's creation of pitch pipes. The Indian *Natya Shastra* (200 BCE–200 CE) systematically discussed intervals and scales. Greek writings included technical manuals on notation and scales, and philosophical treatises on music's universal order. Medieval European theory was dominated by Boethius's *De institutione musica* (c. 500), which focused on mathematical proportions. Later, practical treatises emerged, such as Guido d'Arezzo's work on solmization (1028) and Franco of Cologne's codification of mensural notation (c. 1280), which evolved into modern rhythmic notation.

### Fundamentals of Music
Music theory analyzes aural phenomena. **Pitch** is the perceived lowness or highness of a tone; its frequency is measurable, but perception is subjective. **Scales and modes** are arrangements of pitches; Western theory commonly uses a twelve-tone chromatic scale, while other cultures use different systems (e.g., quarter tones). **Consonance and dissonance** are subjective qualities of stability or tension in intervals, varying by culture and era. **Rhythm** is the arrangement of sounds and silences in time, measured by meter. **Melody** is a succession of tones, while **harmony** involves simultaneous pitches (chords). **Timbre** (tone color) distinguishes instruments playing the same pitch. **Dynamics** refer to volume variations, and **articulation** describes how notes are performed (e.g., staccato, legato). **Texture** is how melodic, rhythmic, and harmonic materials combine (e.g., monophonic, polyphonic). **Form** is the overall structure of a piece.

### Academic Discipline and Analysis
As a modern academic subfield, music theory uses various analytical methods. **Musical analysis** asks "how does this music work?" and includes approaches like **Schenkerian analysis**, which reveals hierarchical pitch structures in tonal music. **Transformational theory** focuses on relations between musical objects rather than the objects themselves. **Music perception and cognition** studies how music is processed by the mind. The field also examines **genre** (conventional categories of music) and **technique** (performer control). **Mathematics** is applied through **musical set theory** (using set theory to organize pitches) and **serialism** (a compositional method using ordered series for musical elements). **Musical semiotics** studies signs and meaning in music.

### Education and Careers
Music theory has been taught in conservatories for centuries, but its status as a distinct academic discipline is recent. Professional societies (e.g., Society for Music Theory, 1977) were founded to promote scholarship. Most theorists work as professors, requiring a PhD. The job market is competitive, with more graduates than tenure-track positions. Theoretical training typically includes a B.Mus. or B.A., followed by graduate study.

## Terms
- **Musicology**: The wider study of musical cultures and history, of which music theory is a subfield.
- **Tuning systems**: Methods for determining the precise size of intervals between pitches (e.g., equal temperament).
- **Chromatic scale**: A Western scale dividing the octave into twelve semitones.
- **Consonance**: A subjective feeling of stability or harmony in an interval or chord.
- **Dissonance**: A subjective feeling of tension or incompleteness in an interval or chord.
- **Meter**: The regular pulse grouping of music into measures or bars.
- **Texture**: The combination of melodic, rhythmic, and harmonic materials in a composition (e.g., monophonic, homophonic).
- **Schenkerian analysis**: A method for analyzing tonal music by revealing its hierarchical pitch structure.
- **Serialism**: A compositional technique that uses ordered series to manipulate musical elements like pitch.
- **Musical set theory**: The application of mathematical set theory to organize and describe musical objects.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The definition of music itself is ever-expanding, leading to debates about what sonic phenomena should be included in its theory.
- The subjective nature of concepts like consonance/dissonance and musical expression means their theoretical frameworks are not absolute and vary across cultures and historical periods.
- The role and validity of analysis are debated; some composers, like Edgard Varèse, have claimed analysis "decomposes" and "mutilates" a work's spirit.
- The components of musical expression remain a subject of extensive and unresolved dispute among scholars.
