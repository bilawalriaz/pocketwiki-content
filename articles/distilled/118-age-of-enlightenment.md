# Age of Enlightenment

# Overview

The Age of Enlightenment (also called the Age of Reason) was a period of intellectual and cultural flourishing in Europe and Western civilization, emerging in the late 17th century in Western Europe and peaking in the 18th century. It emphasized reason, empirical evidence, and the scientific method, promoting ideals of individual liberty, religious tolerance, progress, and natural rights. Its thinkers advocated constitutional government, separation of church and state, and rational social and political reform, ultimately undermining traditional monarchy and religious authority and paving the way for the political revolutions of the 18th and 19th centuries.

## Timeline

- **1637** — Descartes publishes *Discourse on the Method*; some date the Enlightenment's start here
- **1651** — Hobbes publishes *Leviathan*, ushering in social contract debate
- **1687** — Newton publishes *Principia Mathematica*; others date the Enlightenment's start here
- **1689** — Locke publishes *A Letter Concerning Toleration* and *Two Treatises of Government*
- **1715** — Death of Louis XIV; French historians traditionally date the Enlightenment's beginning here
- **1751–1772** — Diderot and d'Alembert publish the *Encyclopédie* in 35 volumes
- **1764** — Beccaria publishes *On Crimes and Punishments*, condemning torture and the death penalty
- **1776** — Adam Smith publishes *The Wealth of Nations*; American Revolution begins
- **1784** — Kant publishes *Answering the Question: What Is Enlightenment?*
- **1789** — French Revolution begins; traditional end date of the Enlightenment
- **1804** — Death of Kant; latest proposed end date of the Enlightenment

## Body

### Philosophical foundations

The Enlightenment emerged from and built upon the Scientific Revolution of the 16th and 17th centuries, which had established new methods of empirical inquiry through the work of figures such as Galileo Galilei, Johannes Kepler, Francis Bacon, Pierre Gassendi, Christiaan Huygens, and Isaac Newton. Philosophical foundations were laid by thinkers including Descartes, Hobbes, Spinoza, and Locke. Bacon's empiricism and Descartes' rationalism laid the groundwork; Descartes' method of doubt led to a dualistic doctrine of mind and matter, refined by Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690) and Hume's writings in the 1740s. Spinoza challenged Cartesian dualism with his assertion of the unity of matter.

According to Jonathan Israel, two distinct lines of Enlightenment thought emerged: a moderate variety (following Descartes, Locke, and Christian Wolff) seeking accommodation between reform and traditional power structures, and a Radical Enlightenment (inspired by Spinoza) advocating democracy, individual liberty, freedom of expression, and eradication of religious authority. The moderate tendency was generally deistic; the radical tendency separated morality entirely from theology. Both were opposed by a conservative Counter-Enlightenment seeking a return to faith.

In mid-18th-century Paris, the philosophical movement was led by Voltaire and Rousseau, who argued for a society based on reason rather than faith, a civil order based on natural law, and science based on experiment and observation. Montesquieu introduced the idea of separation of powers, adopted by the authors of the U.S. Constitution. Kant tried to reconcile rationalism and religious belief, individual freedom and political authority. Mary Wollstonecraft argued that women as well as men should be treated as rational beings.

### Science and institutions

Science played a central role in Enlightenment thought. Lavoisier's experiments created the first modern chemical plants in Paris; the Montgolfier brothers launched the first manned hot-air balloon flight in 1783. Science was dominated by scientific societies and academies, which had largely replaced universities as centres of research and development; by 1789 there were over 70 official scientific societies in Europe. Fontenelle coined "the Age of Academies" for the 18th century. The popularization of science proceeded through the *Encyclopédie* and Voltaire's and Émilie du Châtelet's popularizations of Newtonianism. Rousseau criticized the sciences for distancing man from nature and not operating to make people happier.

### Sociology, economics, and law

Hume and other Scottish Enlightenment thinkers developed a "science of man," merging scientific study of human behavior in ancient and primitive cultures with awareness of modernity's determining forces; modern sociology largely originated from this movement. Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), often considered the first work on modern economics, was immediately preceded and influenced by Turgot's *Reflections on the Formation and Distribution of Wealth* (1766). Beccaria's *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764), translated into 22 languages, condemned torture and the death penalty and was a founding work in the field of penology and the classical school of criminology.

### Politics and government

Locke based his governance philosophy on social contract theory, a subject that permeated Enlightenment political thought. English philosopher Thomas Hobbes ushered in this new debate with his work *Leviathan* in 1651. Locke, Hobbes, and Rousseau agreed that government authority lies in the consent of the governed. Locke held that individuals enter civil society to protect natural rights (life, liberty, property) via an "unbiased judge." Rousseau argued that people join civil society via the social contract to achieve unity while preserving individual freedom, embodied in the sovereignty of the general will. Hume and Ferguson criticized social contract theory, arguing governments derive from habitual authority and social development rather than consent.

European rulers including Catherine II of Russia, Joseph II of Austria, and Frederick II of Prussia applied Enlightenment thought to religious and political tolerance, becoming known as "enlightened despots." Voltaire despised democracy, arguing the absolute monarch must be an enlightened "philosopher-king." The Enlightenment is frequently linked to the American Revolution (1776) and French Revolution (1789). Tocqueville proposed the French Revolution as the inevitable result of opposition between the monarchy and Enlightenment men of letters, whose "literary politics" promoted a discourse of equality.

### Religion

Enlightenment religious commentary responded to the preceding century of religious conflict, especially the Thirty Years' War. Locke abandoned theological commentary for an "unprejudiced examination" of Scripture, determining Christianity's essence to be belief in Christ the redeemer. Deism—simple belief in God the Creator without reference to the Bible or any other miraculous source—was agreeable to many thinkers. Few enlightened intellectuals were true atheists; most were critics of orthodox belief, wedded to skepticism, deism, vitalism, or pantheism. The "Radical Enlightenment" promoted separating church and state, an idea credited to Locke, who argued government lacked authority over individual conscience. Jefferson called for a "wall of separation between church and state."

### National variations

The Enlightenment took hold across Europe with local emphases. In France it was associated with anti-government and anti-Church radicalism; in Germany it reached deep into the middle classes with spiritualistic and nationalistic tone. The English Enlightenment's existence is debated; Porter argues it came early and succeeded, so intellectuals like Gibbon, Burke, and Johnson were conservative supporters of the standing order. The Scottish Enlightenment disseminated principles of sociability, equality, and utility through schools and universities. The Jewish Enlightenment (Haskalah) arose in the 1770s, advocating against Jewish reclusiveness and diminishing traditional community authority. Other national movements included the Dutch, French, German (Aufklärung), Austrian, Greek, Hungarian, Romanian, Swiss, Italian, Spanish, Haitian, Portuguese, Swedish, Russian, Polish, and various non-European enlightenments (China, Japan, Korea, India, Egypt, Ottoman Empire, Arab world).

## Terms

- **Empiricism**: The theory that knowledge comes primarily from sensory experience and evidence
- **Social contract**: An agreement in which government authority lies in the consent of the governed
- **Natural rights**: Universal, inalienable rights all humans possess in pre-political societies (life, liberty, property)
- **Deism**: Belief in God the Creator without reference to the Bible or miraculous sources, relying solely on personal reason
- **Enlightened absolutism**: Rule by absolute monarchs who applied Enlightenment thought to religious and political tolerance
- **Counter-Enlightenment**: Contemporary criticism defending traditional religious and political authorities against rationalist critique
- **Republic of Letters**: An egalitarian intellectual realm governed by knowledge, acting across political boundaries
- **Public sphere**: A realm of communication with new arenas of debate, open urban public space, and an explosion of print culture
- **Salonnières**: Women who hosted and governed Parisian salons, the principal social institutions of the Republic of Letters
- **Haskalah**: The Jewish Enlightenment, an intellectual movement among Central and Eastern European Jews

## Debates and open questions

Scholars disagree on the Enlightenment's definition, chronology, and geographical extent. Dating of its beginning ranges from Descartes' 1637 *Discourse on the Method* to Newton's 1687 *Principia Mathematica*; French historians traditionally place it between 1715 and 1789, while many now date its end to the start of the 19th century, with the latest proposed year being Kant's death in 1804. The existence of an English Enlightenment has been hotly debated, as has a Swedish Enlightenment. The rights of women and non-White people were generally overlooked in Enlightenment philosophy, which is often explicitly Eurocentric; scientific racism first emerged at this time. Horkheimer and Adorno critiqued the Enlightenment's rational basis in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1947), arguing the "wholly enlightened earth radiates under the sign of disaster triumphant." Jason Josephson Storm argues the Enlightenment as a clearly defined period separate from Renaissance and Romanticism constitutes a myth, noting vastly different periodizations and that the term was applied after the fact. Historians debate whether the public sphere was truly egalitarian, whether Freemasonry was a main factor in the Enlightenment, and whether ideas themselves (Israel) or social/economic transformations caused the era's revolutionary changes.
