# Sundarbans

## Overview
The Sundarbans is the world's largest contiguous mangrove forest, spanning approximately 10,277 km² across the Ganges Delta in Bangladesh and India. It is a critical ecosystem providing habitat for the endangered Bengal tiger and acting as a natural barrier against cyclones and tsunamis for millions of coastal residents. The forest faces severe threats from climate change, human activity, and development projects.

## Timeline
- **4th-2nd century BCE** — Human settlement traced to the Mauryan era.
- **1757** — British East India Company obtains proprietary rights from the Mughal Emperor.
- **1869** — First Forest Management Division for the Sundarbans is established.
- **1875-1876** — Large portions declared reserved forests under the Indian Forest Act.
- **1973** — Sundarbans Tiger Reserve core area declared in India.
- **1977** — Wildlife sanctuaries established in Bangladesh.
- **1984** — Sundarbans National Park declared in India.
- **1987** — Sundarbans inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2001** — Sundarbans National Park designated a Biosphere Reserve.
- **2007** — Cyclone Sidr damages ~40% of the Sundarbans.
- **2009** — Cyclone Aila devastates the region, affecting at least 100,000 people.
- **2020** — Indian Sundarbans assessed as endangered under IUCN Red List of Ecosystems.

## Body

### Geography and Formation
The Sundarbans is a vast delta formed by the confluence of the Ganges, Brahmaputra, and Meghna rivers in the Bay of Bengal. It is a complex network of tidal waterways, mudflats, and islands of salt-tolerant mangrove forests. The forest floor varies from 0.9 to 2.11 metres above sea level. The ecosystem has evolved through millennia of sediment deposition and is characterized by a transition between freshwater rivers and saline bay water. Approximately one-third of the land disappears and reappears daily with the tides.

### Ecology and Biodiversity
The forest is dominated by the sundri (*Heritiera fomes*) and gewa (*Excoecaria agallocha*) trees. It supports 453 wildlife species, including 290 birds, 120 fish, 42 mammals, 35 reptiles, and 8 amphibians. The Sundarbans is a vital habitat for the Bengal tiger, with an estimated 180 individuals (106 in Bangladesh, 74 in India). Despite a total ban on wildlife killing or capture (except fish), there has been a consistent pattern of depleted biodiversity and species loss in the 20th century, with the ecological quality of the forest declining.

### Threats and Climate Change
The Sundarbans faces threats from natural events and human activities. Cyclones Sidr (2007) and Aila (2009) caused massive damage. Climate change is a primary threat, causing sea-level rise, increased salinity, and coastal erosion. Studies estimate the coast is retreating up to 200 metres per year. The rising salinity threatens the salinity-sensitive sundri trees. Human-made hazards include the coal-fired Rampal Power Station, which a 2016 UNESCO report anticipated would further damage the forest, and oil spills like the 2014 Southern Star VII incident.

### Human Use and Administration
The forest has been used for centuries for agriculture, aquaculture, and timber. It constitutes 51% of Bangladesh's total reserve forest and contributes significantly to its forest revenue. The area has a population of over 4 million. Protected areas cover 15% of the mangroves and include four UNESCO World Heritage Sites: Sundarbans National Park (India), and Sundarbans West, South, and East Wildlife Sanctuaries (Bangladesh).

## Terms
- **Mangrove:** Salt-tolerant trees and shrubs growing in coastal saline or brackish water.
- **Delta:** A landform created by deposition of sediment that is carried by a river as the flow leaves its mouth and enters slower-moving or stagnant water.
- **Pneumatophores:** Specialized aerial roots that grow upward from anaerobic mud to obtain oxygen.
- **Ecoregion:** An ecologically and geographically defined area that is a distinct unit of nature.
- **Salinity:** The concentration of salt in water or soil.
- **Managed retreat:** The purposeful relocation of people and infrastructure away from areas prone to hazards like flooding.
- **Biosphere Reserve:** An international conservation designation for areas of terrestrial and coastal ecosystems.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact origin of the name "Sundarbans" is debated, with theories ranging from "beautiful forest" to a corruption of "Samudraban" (Sea Forest).
- The impact of the Rampal Power Station on the forest's ecology is a point of contention between environmental activists and the government of Bangladesh.
- The precise rate and future impact of sea-level rise on the Sundarbans ecosystem remain subjects of ongoing study and concern.
