# Natural rubber

## Overview  
Natural rubber is a polymer of isoprene (cis-1,4-polyisoprene) harvested primarily from the latex of the Pará rubber tree (*Hevea brasiliensis*). Valued for its elasticity, resilience, and impermeability, it underpins industries from tires to medical devices. Its production history is deeply tied to colonial exploitation and global trade shifts, and it remains a critical yet volatile commodity influenced by geopolitics, disease, and climate.

## Timeline  
- **c. 1600 BC** — Mesoamerican use of rubber for balls and objects
- **1736** — La Condamine introduces rubber to French Academy
- **1839** — Charles Goodyear develops vulcanization
- **1876** — Henry Wickham smuggles rubber seeds from Brazil to Kew
- **1909** — Synthetic rubber synthesized by chemical means
- **1941–1942** — Japanese forces seize Malaya and Dutch East Indies, disrupting rubber supply
- **2020–2021** — Pandemic drives 30% spike in rubber prices

## Body  

### Sources and Varieties  
The primary commercial source is the Amazonian rubber tree (*Hevea brasiliensis*), native to Brazil but now cultivated pan-tropically. Other plants like *Landolphia* species (Congo rubber), dandelions (*Taraxacum kok-saghyz*), and guayule (*Parthenium argentatum*) have been explored for rubber content. Guayule is notable for hypoallergenic properties. Non-*Hevea* sources often require more complex processing.

### Historical Development  
Indigenous Mesoamerican cultures first used rubber around 1600 BC for balls and waterproofing. European awareness began with La Condamine’s 1736 introduction to France. Vulcanization by Goodyear in 1839 stabilized rubber chemically. Colonial expansion followed: Henry Wickham’s smuggling of seeds enabled Asian cultivation, shifting dominance from South America to Southeast Asia. Exploitation in the Congo Free State and Amazon led to severe human rights abuses during the rubber boom.

### Production and Cultivation  
More than 29 million metric tonnes of rubber were produced in 2022, of which over 50% was natural (15.1 million tonnes). Asia was the main source of natural rubber, accounting for about 90% of output in 2021. The three largest producers, Thailand, Indonesia, and Vietnam, together accounted for around 61% of all natural rubber production in 2022. Trees are tapped by scoring bark to collect latex, usually on alternate or third days. Economic lifespan is around 32 years. Cultivation requires specific climates: rainfall of around 250 centimetres, temperature range of about 20 to 34 °C, and atmospheric humidity of around 80%. Deforestation concerns have placed rubber under the 2023 EU Regulation on Deforestation-free products (EUDR).

### Processing and Chemistry  
Latex is processed into block rubber (e.g., SVR grades) or concentrated for dipped goods. Vulcanization—heating with sulfur or peroxides—cross-links polyisoprene chains, enhancing durability and elasticity. Additives like carbon black improve strength. Natural rubber is an elastomer and a thermoplastic. Once the rubber is vulcanized, it is a thermoset. Rubber is the polymer cis-1,4-polyisoprene—with a molecular weight of 100,000 to 1,000,000 daltons.

### Physical Properties  
Rubber exhibits hyperelastic behavior, showing the Mullins and Payne effects. It strain-crystallizes when stretched, contributing to resilience. Susceptible to ozone cracking and oxidation due to allylic C–H bonds. Glass transition temperature affects elasticity—below it, rubber stiffens (as seen in the Challenger disaster). Rubber begins to melt at approximately 180 °C.

### Uses  
Natural rubber is vital in tires, hoses, gloves, footwear, and medical devices. Uncured forms serve as adhesives and insulating materials. Synthetic alternatives offer better chemical resistance but lack natural rubber’s elasticity. Around 25 million tonnes of rubber are produced each year, of which 30 percent is natural.

### Economic and Environmental Concerns  
Price volatility stems from supply shocks, geopolitical events, and competition from crops like palm oil. Climate change and plant diseases threaten yields. Smallholder farmers dominate production, facing economic instability. During the 2020 and 2021 international COVID-19 pandemic, demand for rubber gloves surged, leading to a spike in rubber prices of about 30%. Microbial degradation is limited; rubber persists in most composting environments.

## Terms  
- **Isoprene**: Organic compound forming rubber polymers.  
- **Elastomer**: Material with high elasticity.  
- **Vulcanization**: Cross-linking process improving rubber durability.  
- **Latex**: Milky colloid containing rubber particles.  
- **Tapping**: Bark incision to extract latex.  
- **Mullins Effect**: Stress softening under cyclic loading.  
- **Payne Effect**: Stiffening at low strain amplitudes.  
- **Thermoset**: Material that hardens irreversibly upon heating.  
- **Carbon Black**: Filler enhancing rubber strength.  
- **Hypoallergenic**: Less likely to cause allergic reactions.

- Is the EU deforestation regulation sufficient to curb environmental harm?
