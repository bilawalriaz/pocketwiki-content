# Puyi

## Overview
Puyi (1906–1967) was the last emperor of China, reigning as the Xuantong Emperor of the Qing dynasty from 1908 to 1912. His life spanned the collapse of imperial China, the rise of Japanese militarism, and the establishment of the People's Republic, making him a unique figure in 20th-century history. He later served as the puppet Kangde Emperor of the Japanese-controlled state of Manchukuo during World War II, after which he was imprisoned as a war criminal before being reformed and released by the Chinese Communist Party.

## Timeline
- **7 February 1906** — Born in Beijing to Prince Chun Zaifeng.
- **December 1908** — Ascends the Qing throne at age two as the Xuantong Emperor.
- **12 February 1912** — Abdicates following the Xinhai Revolution, ending imperial rule.
- **1–12 July 1917** — Briefly restored to the throne by loyalist general Zhang Xun.
- **5 November 1924** — Expelled from the Forbidden City by warlord Feng Yuxiang.
- **1 March 1932** — Installed as Chief Executive of the Japanese puppet state of Manchukuo.
- **1 March 1934** — Crowned Emperor of Manchukuo under the era name Kangde.
- **15 August 1945** — Abdicates as Emperor of Manchukuo after Japan's surrender.
- **17 August 1945** — Captured by Soviet forces at Mukden (Shenyang).
- **1950** — Repatriated to the People's Republic of China and imprisoned.
- **9 December 1959** — Released from prison after being declared "reformed."
- **17 October 1967** — Dies in Beijing of kidney cancer and heart disease.

## Body

### Early Reign and Abdication
Puyi was chosen by Empress Dowager Cixi to succeed the childless Guangxu Emperor. He ascended the throne at age two, with his father Prince Chun as regent. His childhood in the Forbidden City was isolated and rigidly ceremonial, marked by cruelty towards eunuchs and a lack of normal human relationships. The Xinhai Revolution of 1911 led to his abdication in 1912 under the Articles of Favorable Treatment, which allowed him to retain his title and remain in the Forbidden City. A brief, failed restoration attempt by Zhang Xun occurred in 1917.

### Life in the Forbidden City and Tianjin
After his 1924 expulsion from the Forbidden City by warlord Feng Yuxiang, Puyi took refuge in the Japanese legation and later moved to the Japanese concession in Tianjin. There, he lived a lavish but aimless life, surrounded by advisers with conflicting agendas. He was deeply affected by the 1928 looting of the Qing imperial tombs, which solidified his resentment toward the Kuomintang and his turn toward Japanese protection.

### Manchukuo
Following the Japanese invasion of Manchuria in 1931, Puyi was installed as the puppet ruler of Manchukuo, first as Chief Executive and then as Emperor Kangde from 1934. He was a virtual prisoner under Japanese control, signing decrees for a regime that used forced labor and committed atrocities. His personal life was unhappy; his wife Wanrong descended into opium addiction, and his own behavior became increasingly erratic and cruel. He was forced to declare war on the Allies in 1941 and witnessed the collapse of Manchukuo during the Soviet invasion in August 1945.

### Imprisonment and "Reeducation"
Puyi was captured by the Soviet Red Army in 1945 and held in Siberia until 1950, when he was repatriated to China. He spent nearly a decade in Fushun War Criminals Prison, where he underwent a process of political "reeducation." Confronted with evidence of Japanese atrocities committed in his name, he expressed remorse for his past actions. In 1959, he was granted amnesty and presented as a model of successful reform.

### Later Life
After his release, Puyi worked as a gardener and later as an editor. He published a government-approved autobiography, *From Emperor to Citizen*, in the 1960s. He married nurse Li Shuxian in 1962. During the Cultural Revolution, he was protected from the worst excesses by Premier Zhou Enlai. He died in 1967.

## Terms
*   **Xuantong Emperor:** Puyi's regnal name during his reign as the last emperor of the Qing dynasty (1908–1912, 1917).
*   **Articles of Favorable Treatment:** The 1912 agreement between the Qing court and the Republic of China that allowed Puyi to retain his imperial title and remain in the Forbidden City after abdication.
*   **Manchukuo:** A puppet state of the Empire of Japan in Manchuria (1932–1945), headed by Puyi as its nominal ruler.
*   **Kangde Emperor:** Puyi's regnal name during his reign as the Emperor of Manchukuo (1934–1945).
*   **Kwantung Army:** The Japanese army stationed in Manchuria, which effectively controlled Manchukuo and Puyi.
*   **Salt Tax Palace:** The former administrative building in Changchun (Xinjing) that served as Puyi's residence and prison during his time as Emperor of Manchukuo.
*   **Fushun War Criminals Prison:** The facility in Liaoning where Puyi and other high-ranking prisoners were held and subjected to political reeducation by the People's Republic of China.
*   **From Emperor to Citizen:** Puyi's government-supervised autobiography, published in the 1960s, which detailed his life and political "reformation."

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Agency in Manchukuo:** Scholars debate the extent of Puyi's agency versus his role as a helpless puppet of the Japanese Kwantung Army. While he was a prisoner in many respects, he also signed decrees and made public statements supporting the regime.
*   **Sincerity of Reform:** The authenticity of Puyi's political transformation and remorse during his imprisonment and later life is questioned. Some view it as a genuine change, while others see it as a survival strategy under a new authoritarian power.
*   **Personal Life and Sexuality:** Historical accounts, including from his brother Pujie and journalist Edward Behr, suggest Puyi was bisexual and possibly impotent, with a troubled personal life that included cruelty to staff and the death of his wife Wanrong's child. The full truth remains obscured by limited primary sources.
