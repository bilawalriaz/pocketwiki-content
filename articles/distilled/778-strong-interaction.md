# Strong interaction

## Overview  
The strong interaction is one of the four fundamental forces of nature, responsible for binding quarks into protons, neutrons, and other hadrons, and for holding atomic nuclei together via the residual nuclear force. It is the strongest of the fundamental forces—about 100 times stronger than electromagnetism at nuclear distances—and its energy accounts for nearly all the mass of visible matter. Understanding the strong interaction is essential to nuclear physics, particle physics, and cosmology, as it governs both stellar fusion and the stability of matter itself.

## Timeline  
- **Pre-1971** — Physicists uncertain how atomic nuclei are bound despite proton repulsion
- **1964** — Gell-Mann and Zweig propose the quark model
- **1970s** — Development of quantum chromodynamics (QCD) as the theory of the strong force
- **Later 20th century** — Experimental confirmation of color confinement and asymptotic freedom

## Body  

### History and Conceptual Development  
Before 1971, physicists could not explain why atomic nuclei remain stable despite the mutual electromagnetic repulsion between positively charged protons. A stronger, attractive force was postulated—the strong force—acting on protons and neutrons. In 1964, Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig independently proposed that baryons (like protons and neutrons) and mesons are composed of elementary particles called quarks. Gell-Mann named them "quarks," while Zweig called them "aces." This quark model revealed that the nucleon-nucleon attraction was a side effect of a deeper force binding quarks within hadrons. The modern theory, quantum chromodynamics (QCD), describes this force as mediated by gluons and based on a color charge analogous to—but more complex than—electric charge.

### Behavior Within Hadrons  
At distances smaller than about 0.8 femtometers (fm), the strong interaction is carried by gluons and binds quarks into hadrons such as protons and neutrons. This regime is often called the color force. The strong force is described mathematically by QCD, a non-abelian gauge theory based on the symmetry group SU(3). The force carrier is the gluon, a massless boson that itself carries color charge—unlike the neutral photon in electromagnetism. Quarks and gluons are the only particles with non-zero color charge, so they interact only with each other. The strength of the interaction is governed by the strong coupling constant, modified by the gauge color charge of the particle. A unique feature is that the strong force does not weaken with distance; instead, it remains roughly constant at about 10,000 newtons beyond a limiting separation. This leads to **color confinement**: pulling quarks apart adds enough energy to create new quark-antiquark pairs, making it impossible to isolate individual quarks. Only hadrons are observed. High-energy collisions produce jets of hadrons rather than free quarks or gluons, though quark-gluon plasmas have been observed under extreme conditions.

### Behavior Between Hadrons  
At distances approaching or exceeding the proton radius (up to ~3 fm), a residual force remains between color-neutral hadrons. This is the **residual strong force**, also known as the nuclear force, and it binds protons and neutrons into atomic nuclei. It is mediated indirectly by virtual mesons—particularly pions (π) and rho mesons (ρ)—which transmit gluon-based interactions between nucleons. The residual force is much weaker than the color force within hadrons, analogous to how van der Waals forces are weaker than the electromagnetic forces within atoms. Unlike the color force, the residual strong force diminishes rapidly with distance, approximately following a negative exponential decay (Yukawa potential). The slower decline of the repulsive electromagnetic force between protons compared to the residual attraction causes instability in heavy nuclei (those with atomic number greater than 82, such as lead). Despite being weaker, the nuclear force is still energetic: transitions release gamma rays, and the mass defect—the difference between the mass of a nucleus and the sum of its nucleons—reflects the potential energy of the nuclear force. These mass differences power nuclear fusion (in stars) and nuclear fission (in reactors and weapons).

### Unification and Open Frontiers  
Grand Unified Theories (GUTs) seek to unify the strong interaction with the electroweak interaction, similar to how electromagnetism and the weak force were unified. A key property of the strong force relevant to unification is **asymptotic freedom**: the strength of the strong force decreases at higher energies or temperatures. The theoretical energy at which the strong force equals the electroweak force is called the grand unification energy. However, no GUT has yet been successfully formulated to describe this unification, and it remains an unsolved problem in physics.

## Terms  
- **Color charge**: A property of quarks and gluons analogous to electric charge, but with three types (red, green, blue) and their anti-colors; governs participation in the strong interaction.  
- **Color confinement**: The phenomenon where quarks and gluons cannot be isolated because the energy required to separate them creates new particles instead.  
- **Asymptotic freedom**: The property of the strong force where its strength decreases at high energies or short distances.  
- **Quantum chromodynamics (QCD)**: The quantum field theory describing the strong interaction, based on SU(3) gauge symmetry and mediated by gluons.  
- **Gluon**: The massless gauge boson that mediates the strong force between quarks and other gluons.  
- **Residual strong force**: The leftover strong interaction between color-neutral hadrons, responsible for binding protons and neutrons in atomic nuclei.  
- **Nuclear force**: Another name for the residual strong force acting between nucleons in a nucleus.  
- **Yukawa potential**: A potential that decreases exponentially with distance, used to describe the residual strong force between nucleons.  
- **Mass defect**: The difference between the mass of a nucleus and the sum of the masses of its individual nucleons, due to nuclear binding energy.  
- **Grand Unified Theory (GUT)**: A hypothetical framework aiming to unify the strong, weak, and electromagnetic forces into a single force at high energies.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- Whether a complete Grand Unified Theory can successfully describe the unification of the strong and electroweak forces remains unresolved.  
- The exact form of the distance-dependent behavior of the residual strong force lacks a simple analytical expression, despite the Yukawa potential approximation.  
- The conditions under which quark-gluon plasmas form and their precise properties are still active areas of experimental and theoretical research.
