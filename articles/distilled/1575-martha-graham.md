# Martha Graham

## Overview
Martha Graham (1894–1991) was an American modern dancer, teacher, and choreographer who created the Graham technique, a codified movement system built on the principle of "contraction and release" derived from stylized breathing. Over a seventy-year career, she choreographed 181 works, founded the oldest dance company in America, and transformed dance into a vehicle for psychological and national expression, earning comparisons to Picasso and Stravinsky for her revolutionary impact. She was the first dancer to perform at the White House (1938), serve as a cultural ambassador abroad, and receive the Presidential Medal of Freedom with Distinction (1976).

- **1928** — Joins faculty of Neighborhood Playhouse School of the Theatre.
- **1936** — Refuses invitation to represent U.S. at Berlin Olympic Art Competitions, protesting Nazi persecution of artists.

- **1990** — *Maple Leaf Rag* completed as final ballet.
- **April 1, 1991** — Dies in New York City from pneumonia, aged 96.

## Body

### Early Life and Denishawn Training
Born to a Presbyterian family in Allegheny City, Pennsylvania, Graham moved to Santa Barbara, California, at age fourteen. Her father, George Graham, was an "alienist" (early psychiatrist). In 1911, seeing Ruth St. Denis perform sparked her interest in dance. She enrolled at the Denishawn School of Dancing and Related Arts, founded by St. Denis and Ted Shawn, remaining until 1923. During this period, she performed an Egyptian dance by Shawn in a 1922 short film by Hugo Riesenfeld attempting to synchronize dance with a live orchestra.

### Establishing a Technique and Company
Leaving Denishawn in 1923, Graham danced in the *Greenwich Village Follies* for two years. A brief tenure at the Eastman School of Music (1925) under Rouben Mamoulian produced the two-color film *The Flute of Krishna*. In 1926, she established the Martha Graham Center of Contemporary Dance on New York's Upper East Side and debuted her first independent concert of 18 solos and trios, acknowledging Denishawn's pervasive influence. She began a long photographic collaboration with Soichi Sunami and joined the Neighborhood Playhouse faculty in 1928. Her technique crystallized around "contraction and release"—a stylized conception of breathing that became the foundation of modern dance vocabulary.

### Political Engagement and *American Document*
In 1936, the Nazi regime invited Graham to represent the U.S. at the Berlin Olympic Art Competitions. Despite assurances from Joseph Goebbels that her Jewish dancers would receive immunity, she refused, citing the persecution of artists. This refusal and the rise of fascism stimulated *American Document* (1938), a patriotic work expressing democratic ideals through excerpts from the Declaration of Independence, Lincoln's Gettysburg Address, and the Emancipation Proclamation. Graham believed dance must "reveal certain national characteristics" to have validity and roots. That same year, she became the first dancer to perform at the White House, invited by the Roosevelt family.

### Major Works and Collaborations
Graham collaborated with leading composers including Aaron Copland (*Appalachian Spring*), Louis Horst (her longtime musical director), Samuel Barber, William Schuman, and Gian Carlo Menotti. Her only full-length ballet, *Clytemnestra* (1958), featured a score by Egyptian-born Halim El-Dabh; Graham danced the title role, remaining onstage nearly the entire duration. The work dramatized the Greek myth of Agamemnon's sacrifice of Iphigenia, Clytemnestra's revenge, and her subsequent murder by Orestes. Erick Hawkins, the first man in her company (1938), became her husband (1948–1954) and frequent lead dancer.

### Media, Preservation, and Later Career
Graham initially resisted recording her dances, believing they must be experienced live. Exceptions included collaborations with photographers Imogen Cunningham (1930s), Barbara Morgan (1940s), and Philippe Halsman (1940s), whose *Dark Meadow* images she deemed the most complete photographic record. In 1952, she allowed filming of her exchange with Helen Keller, whose tactile experience of vibration inspired Graham. After her final performance (dated variously 1968–1970), Graham suffered severe depression and alcoholism, hospitalized in a coma. She recovered in 1972, quit drinking, reorganized her company, and choreographed ten new ballets, concluding with *Maple Leaf Rag* (1990). She choreographed until her death from pneumonia in 1991, completing her autobiography *Blood Memory* shortly prior.

### Company Legacy and Notable Alumni
The Martha Graham Dance Company (founded 1926) is the oldest in America. It incubated generations of major artists: early female dancers included Anna Sokolow, Pearl Lang, Yuriko, and Sophie Maslow; male dancers included Erick Hawkins, Merce Cunningham, Paul Taylor, Glen Tetley, and Donald McKayle. In 1978, a pre-fame Madonna danced with the company, nicknamed "Madame X" by Graham. Assistants Linda Hodes, Pearl Lang, Diane Gray, and Yuriko preserved Graham's technique through taped interviews and performance videos.

## Terms
- **Contraction and release**: The core principle of Graham technique, derived from a stylized conception of breathing; the torso curves inward (contraction) and lengthens outward (release) to generate movement.
- **Denishawn School**: The pioneering dance school founded by Ruth St. Denis and Ted Shawn where Graham trained (mid-1910s–1923); source of her early technical and theatrical foundation.
- **Alienist**: An archaic term for a psychiatrist or physician specializing in mental illness; the profession of Graham's father, George.
- **Cultural ambassador**: A representative of a nation's arts sent abroad; Graham was the first dancer to serve in this role for the U.S.
- **Presidential Medal of Freedom with Distinction**: The highest U.S. civilian honor; awarded to Graham in 1976 by President Gerald Ford.
- **American Document (1938)**: A choreographic response to fascism using U.S. founding texts to assert democratic ideals and national identity.
- **Clytemnestra (1958)**: Graham's only full-length ballet, set to Halim El-Dabh's score, dramatizing the Oresteia myth with Graham in the title role.
- **Louis Horst**: Composer, pianist, and Graham's primary musical collaborator (1926–1964); she credited his faith with giving her "a landscape to move in."
- **Martha Graham Dance Company**: Founded 1926; oldest U.S. dance company; incubator for Cunningham, Taylor, Tetley, Sokolow, and others.
- **Blood Memory (1991)**: Graham's autobiography, completed days before her death and published posthumously.

## Debates and open questions
- **Date of final performance**: Sources conflict—Agnes de Mille cites May 25, 1968 (*Time of Snow*); Russell Freedman cites 1969; Graham's own *Blood Memory* cites 1970 (*Cortege of Eagles* at age 76).
- **Scope of "contraction and release"**: While universally recognized as Graham's innovation, the extent to which it was a codified system versus an evolving philosophical approach remains a subject of pedagogical debate among her successors.
- **Attribution of influence**: Glen Tetley noted many artists "stole Martha's unique personal vocabulary, consciously or unconsciously"; the precise lineage of specific movements into broader modern dance and postmodern dance (e.g., Cunningham, Taylor) is often difficult to disentangle.
