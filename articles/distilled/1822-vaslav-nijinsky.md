# Vaslav Nijinsky

## Overview

Vaslav Nijinsky (1889/1890–1950) was a Russian ballet dancer and choreographer of Polish ancestry, regarded as the greatest male dancer of the early 20th century. Associated with Sergei Diaghilev's Ballets Russes, he was celebrated for virtuosity, gravity-defying leaps, and intense characterizations, and he could dance *en pointe*—a rare skill for male dancers. His boundary-pushing choreography for ballets like *L'après-midi d'un faune* and *Le Sacre du Printemps* introduced modern dance and sparked scandals, while his later schizophrenia and institutionalization shaped a tragic legacy.

## Body

### Early life and training

Nijinsky was born in Kiev to Polish touring dancers. His parents separated in 1897 after his father fell in love with another dancer; his mother moved with the children to St Petersburg. A family friend arranged for Enrico Cecchetti to sponsor Nijinsky's application to the Imperial Ballet School, which he joined in 1900. He studied under Sergei Legat, Nikolai Legat, and Pavel Gerdt. Despite poor academic results—he was nearly expelled in 1902 and briefly expelled in 1903 for a catapult prank—his dancing was exceptional. In 1904, choreographer Marius Petipa selected him for a principal role, but the ballet was never performed due to the Russo-Japanese War.

### Imperial Ballet and early career

In 1905, Nijinsky was caught in the Bloody Sunday massacre in St Petersburg, where imperial cavalry charged the crowd, leaving him with a head wound. His 1905 student performance drew spontaneous mid-performance applause. He graduated in 1907 and joined the Imperial Ballet as coryphée (mid-rank), bypassing the corps de ballet. His performances, notably the Bluebird pas de deux from *The Sleeping Beauty*, made audiences feel he was flying. In 1910, Mathilde Kschessinska selected him for a revival of *Le Talisman*, where he created a sensation as the Wind God Vayou.

### Ballets Russes

Nijinsky met Diaghilev, who became his lover and manager. The 1909 Paris season of the Ballets Russes was an artistic and social sensation. Nijinsky's expressive execution in Fokine's pieces, including *Le Pavillon d'Armide* and *Cléopâtre*, established his fame. His partnership with Tamara Karsavina was legendary. In January 1911, he danced at the Mariinsky in tights without trousers, causing a scandal; he refused to apologize and was dismissed, freeing him to focus on the Ballets Russes.

### Choreography and controversy

Nijinsky's choreography pushed boundaries. *L'après-midi d'un faune* (1912) ended with a mimed masturbation, defended by artists including Auguste Rodin and Marcel Proust. *Le Sacre du Printemps* (1913), with music by Igor Stravinsky, used radically angular movements expressing pagan sacrifice; violence broke out in the audience at its premiere. *Jeux* (1913) was conceived as a flirtation among three males, but Diaghilev insisted on one male and two females.

### Marriage and dismissal

In 1913, Diaghilev replaced Nijinsky with Fokine for new ballets, and Nijinsky sailed on a South American tour without Diaghilev. Romola de Pulszky, a Hungarian admirer, pursued him; they married on 10 September 1913 in Buenos Aires. Diaghilev, devastated, dismissed Nijinsky by telegram. Nijinsky had no contract or wages, only expenses. He formed his own London company in 1914, but it failed due to poor management, inadequate rehearsal, and a nervous breakdown.

### Later life and illness

During World War I, Nijinsky was confined to house arrest in Budapest as an enemy Russian citizen. In 1916, after complex negotiations involving a prisoner exchange and intercession by King Alfonso XIII and President Woodrow Wilson, he rejoined the Ballets Russes for a US tour, managing the company and choreographing *Till Eulenspiegel*. His last professional performance was in Montevideo in 1917. Signs of schizophrenia had appeared. In 1919, he gave a final improvised solo performance in St Moritz, then was diagnosed with schizophrenia and committed to an asylum. For 30 years he was in and out of psychiatric hospitals, often silent. He died in 1950.

### Legacy

Nijinsky's diary, written in 1919, was published in a bowdlerized version by his wife in 1936; an unexpurgated edition appeared in 1995. No film exists of him dancing; Diaghilev forbade filming, believing contemporary film could not capture the artistry. Nijinsky is immortalized in photographs, many by E. O. Hoppé, and has inspired numerous ballets, plays, films, poems, novels, and artworks.

## Terms

- **Ballets Russes**: Diaghilev's ballet company, which revolutionized Western art, dance, and music from 1909.
- **Coryphée**: A mid-rank position in ballet, above corps de ballet, below soloist.
- **En pointe**: Dancing on the tips of the toes, traditionally reserved for female dancers.
- **Imperial Ballet School**: The Russian state ballet school in St Petersburg where Nijinsky trained.
- **Schizophrenia**: A mental disorder characterized by delusions, disorganized language, and disorganized behavior; Nijinsky was diagnosed in 1919.
- **Sergei Diaghilev**: Impresario and producer who founded the Ballets Russes and managed Nijinsky's career.
- **Michel Fokine**: Choreographer who created many Ballets Russes works and collaborated with Nijinsky.

## Debates and open questions

Nijinsky's birth year is uncertain (1889 or 1890). His diagnosis of schizophrenia, made by Eugen Bleuler in 1919, is accepted but the onset is debated. The cause of his brother Stanislav's death in 1917 is disputed. The bowdlerization of Nijinsky's diary by his wife Romola—who deleted about 40 percent, including references to homosexuality—raised questions about his sexuality and the authenticity of his voice; the 1995 unexpurgated edition restored the original text. Whether Nijinsky's dismissal from the Ballets Russes was due to his marriage, his missed performance, or Diaghilev's jealousy remains a matter of interpretation.
