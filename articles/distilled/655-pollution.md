# Pollution

## Overview  
Pollution is the introduction of contaminants—substances or energy—into the natural environment that cause harm. While natural events like volcanic eruptions and wildfires contribute, the term typically refers to human-driven contamination from industry, transport, agriculture, and poor waste management. It manifests in forms such as air, water, soil, noise, light, thermal, and chemical pollution, with severe consequences for human health and ecosystems. In 2019, pollution was responsible for approximately nine million deaths globally, making it a critical challenge of the Anthropocene era.

## Timeline  
- **Prehistoric** — Soot in caves indicates early indoor air pollution from fires
- **c. 1306** — King Edward I bans coal burning in London due to smoke
- **1858** — Great Stink on the Thames prompts London’s sewer system
- **1881** — Chicago and Cincinnati enact first U.S. clean air laws
- **1952** — Great Smog of London kills at least 4,000; leads to Clean Air Act
- **1963–1970** — U.S. passes Clean Air Act, Clean Water Act, NEPA
- **1974** — EPA bans fish consumption from Hudson River due to PCB dumping
- **1980** — Superfund legislation passed after Love Canal and Valley of the Drums scandals
- **2007** — IPCC report confirms human role in global warming since 1950
- **2019** — Lancet study finds pollution kills 9 million people annually
- **2022** — Study finds chemical pollution exceeds planetary boundaries
- **2023** — Global Framework on Chemicals agreed in Bonn with 28 targets

## Body  

### Definitions and Types  
Pollution encompasses any substance or energy—solid, liquid, gas, or radiant—that degrades environmental quality or poses health risks. The U.S. EPA defines it as materials that impair natural resources or offend senses, while the UN emphasizes undesirable effects based on nature, location, or quantity. Major types include air pollution (chemicals, particulates), water pollution (industrial discharge, sewage), soil contamination (heavy metals, hydrocarbons), and emerging concerns like plastic pollution, electromagnetic radiation, and light pollution. Chemical pollution involves novel entities such as PFAS ("forever chemicals"), recognized as a planetary boundary threat in 2022.

### Natural Causes  
Volcanic eruptions release gases like CO₂, SO₂, and hydrogen halides, contributing to acid rain and climate change. They also emit fine particles containing toxins like arsenic and mercury. Wildfires, often ignited by lightning, produce CO₂, CO, and fine particulates harmful to animals and humans.

### Human Generation  
Human activities dominate pollution sources. Motor vehicles and coal-fired power plants are top contributors to air pollution. Industrial sectors—including chemical plants, refineries, and mining—emit hazardous wastes, heavy metals, and persistent compounds. Agriculture contributes via pesticide spraying, livestock emissions, and runoff. Plastic production has generated massive waste, with most plastics persisting for centuries. Greenhouse gas emissions drive climate change, linking air and water pollution through ocean acidification and disrupted ecosystems.

### Effects  
Pollution impacts human health broadly. Air pollution alone caused 1.67 million deaths in India in 2019 and over 50,000 annually in the U.S., primarily from PM2.5 and fossil fuel combustion. Water pollution leads to 1.4 million premature deaths yearly, especially in regions lacking sanitation. Exposure increases risks of respiratory disease, cancer, neurological damage, and developmental disorders. Vulnerable groups—children, elderly, and marginalized communities—are disproportionately affected. Pollution also fuels socioeconomic issues: studies link it to increased crime, reduced educational performance, and lower worker productivity.

Environmentally, pollution disrupts ecosystems. Biomagnification concentrates toxins in food webs; ocean acidification threatens marine life; smog reduces plant photosynthesis; and nitrogen deposition alters soil chemistry. Invasive species spread via plastic debris, forming garbage patches like the Great Pacific Garbage Patch.

### Regulation and Monitoring  
Pollution control aims to reduce emissions and effluents into air, water, and soil. Prevention and waste minimization are preferred over end-of-pipe solutions. Policies include life-cycle assessments, transparency measures, and economic instruments like Pigouvian taxes and cap-and-trade systems. International coordination occurs through UNEP and treaty bodies. In 2023, a Global Framework on Chemicals was adopted with 28 targets, including phasing out hazardous pesticides by 2035.

### Cost  
Pollution imposes significant societal costs, acting as a negative externality where firms do not bear full environmental damage. Abatement costs reached nearly $27 billion in the U.S. in 2005. Free markets often overproduce polluting goods because external costs are unpriced. Economists advocate for internalizing these costs via taxation or regulation to achieve socially optimal pollution levels, balancing utility gains against environmental harm.

### History  
Pollution has paralleled civilization since prehistoric fire use. The Industrial Revolution intensified contamination, with coal-powered cities facing severe air and water quality issues. Early regulations emerged in medieval Europe, but modern frameworks developed mid-20th century. Key milestones include the Clean Air Acts, Superfund laws, and global climate agreements. Nuclear accidents and large-scale disasters highlighted transboundary risks, fostering international cooperation.

## Terms  
- **Negative externality**: A cost imposed on third parties not involved in a market transaction, such as pollution affecting public health.  
- **Biomagnification**: Increase in toxin concentration as it moves up the food chain.  
- **Planetary boundaries**: Thresholds defining safe operating limits for Earth system processes; chemical pollution is one.  
- **PFAS (Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances)**: Synthetic "forever chemicals" resistant to degradation, linked to health issues.  
- **Smog**: Air pollution mixture of smoke and fog, often photochemical, formed by reactions involving sunlight.  
- **Pigouvian tax**: A tax levied on goods generating negative externalities to align private and social costs.  
- **Legacy pollution**: Persistent contamination remaining at sites long after original sources ceased.  
- **Nonpoint source pollution**: Diffuse pollution from multiple dispersed origins, e.g., agricultural runoff.  
- **Point source pollution**: Pollution originating from a single identifiable location, e.g., a factory pipe.  
- **Life-cycle assessment (LCA)**: Evaluation of environmental impacts throughout a product’s existence.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Debate persists over whether certain phenomena qualify as pollution. Some definitions exclude greenhouse gases or noise, while others incorporate broader energy forms like light or electromagnetic radiation. The classification of novel entities—especially synthetic chemicals like PFAS—as planetary boundaries remains evolving. Questions surround the feasibility of achieving zero pollution versus managing acceptable thresholds. Additionally, the balance between economic growth and environmental protection continues to shape policy discourse, particularly regarding developing nations’ right to industrialize sustainably.
