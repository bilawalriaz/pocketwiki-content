# King Arthur

## Overview
King Arthur is a legendary British ruler and folk hero, central to the medieval literary tradition known as the Matter of Britain. While early sources portray him as a post-Roman war leader fighting Anglo-Saxons, his historicity is debated, and most modern historians consider him a fictional figure. His legend evolved from Welsh mythology into a pan-European phenomenon through medieval literature, shaping enduring cultural ideals of chivalry and kingship.

## Timeline
- **c. 500** — Traditional period for Arthur's supposed battles against Anglo-Saxons.
- **9th century** — First datable mention in *Historia Brittonum*, listing 12 battles.
- **10th century** — *Annales Cambriae* links Arthur to Badon (516–518) and Camlann (537–539).
- **c. 1138** — Geoffrey of Monmouth's *Historia Regum Britanniae* creates the first full narrative.
- **c. 1170–1190** — Chrétien de Troyes writes influential French romances, adding Lancelot and the Grail.
- **1191** — Alleged tomb of Arthur discovered at Glastonbury Abbey.
- **c. 1225–1240** — French prose cycles (Vulgate, Post-Vulgate) further develop the legend.
- **Late 15th century** — Thomas Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur* compiles and popularizes the romance tradition.
- **1859** — Alfred Tennyson's *Idylls of the King* sparks a major Victorian revival.
- **20th–21st century** — Legend thrives in modern novels, films, and other media.

## Body

### Historicity
Arthur is first mentioned in 9th- and 10th-century texts (*Historia Brittonum*, *Annales Cambriae*) as a war leader (*dux bellorum*) who fought at Badon Hill. However, these sources date to centuries after his supposed time. Key problems include the absence of Arthur in contemporary sources like Gildas's 6th-century *De Excidio* and Bede's *Ecclesiastical History*, which mention Badon but not Arthur. Most modern historians therefore exclude him from accounts of sub-Roman Britain, though some scholars like Andrew Breeze argue for a historical basis. The name "Arthur" likely derives from the Roman *Artorius*, though alternative etymologies exist.

### Early Welsh and Pre-Galfridian Traditions
Before Geoffrey of Monmouth, Arthur appears in Welsh poetry and prose as a peerless warrior and monster-slayer. He is a protector of Britain from both human and supernatural threats, a leader of a band of superhuman heroes, and a figure connected to the Welsh Otherworld, Annwn. Key early texts include *Y Gododdin*, poems attributed to Taliesin, and the prose tale *Culhwch and Olwen*. In these, Arthur is an active, heroic figure, sometimes called "Chief of the Lords of this Island."

### Geoffrey of Monmouth and the Romance Tradition
Geoffrey of Monmouth's *Historia Regum Britanniae* (c. 1138) created the foundational narrative, introducing Uther Pendragon, Merlin, Guinevere, Excalibur, and Arthur's final battle at Camlann. This work was immensely popular and provided the historical framework for later romances. French writers, especially Chrétien de Troyes, shifted focus to Arthur's knights (Lancelot, Gawain, Percival) and introduced the Holy Grail. In these romances, Arthur often becomes a passive "do-nothing king." The tradition culminated in Thomas Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur* (15th century), which became the primary source for later English-language works.

### Decline, Revival, and Modern Legend
Interest in Arthur waned after the Middle Ages, with scholars like Polydore Vergil rejecting his historicity. A major revival began in the 19th century, led by Alfred Tennyson's *Idylls of the King*, which reworked the legend for Victorian values. The 20th and 21st centuries have seen countless adaptations in literature (e.g., T.H. White, Mary Stewart), film, and television, often reinterpreting the legend to address contemporary themes like feminism, democracy, and historical realism.

## Terms
- **Matter of Britain**: The body of medieval literature and legend concerning King Arthur and his knights.
- **Historicity**: The quality of being historically real or based on fact.
- **Pre-Galfridian**: Referring to Arthurian texts written before Geoffrey of Monmouth's *Historia*.
- **Galfridian**: Referring to Arthurian texts influenced by or derived from Geoffrey of Monmouth's work.
- **Dux bellorum**: Latin for "leader of wars," the title given to Arthur in the *Historia Brittonum*.
- **Annwn**: The Welsh Otherworld, a realm of magic and the supernatural.
- **Vulgate Cycle**: A 13th-century French prose cycle of Arthurian romances, also called the Lancelot-Grail Cycle.
- **Le Morte d'Arthur**: Thomas Malory's 15th-century English compilation of Arthurian legends.
- **Nine Worthies**: A medieval list of nine exemplary historical and legendary figures, including Arthur.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Was Arthur a historical person?** Most historians doubt it, citing lack of contemporary evidence, but a minority argue for a historical Romano-British leader.
- **What is the origin of the name "Arthur"?** The most accepted etymology is from the Roman *Artorius*, but alternatives like a Celtic "bear-man" or the star Arcturus are debated.
- **How did the legend evolve?** Scholars analyze the shift from the active, heroic Welsh Arthur to the passive king of French romance, and the influence of Geoffrey of Monmouth's inventions.
- **What motivated the 1191 Glastonbury discovery?** Theories include monastic fundraising, political suppression of Celtic opposition, or addressing problematic beliefs in Arthur's return.
