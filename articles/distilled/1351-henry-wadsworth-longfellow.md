# Henry Wadsworth Longfellow

## Overview  
Henry Wadsworth Longfellow (1807–1882) was an American poet and educator whose lyrical works and translations made him the most popular poet of his era. His major works include *Evangeline*, *The Song of Hiawatha*, and *Paul Revere’s Ride*. A central figure among the Fireside Poets, he helped shape American literary identity while also facing criticism for his European influences and sentimental style.

## Body  

### Early Life and Education  
Longfellow was born into a prominent New England family; his grandfather, General Peleg Wadsworth, was a Revolutionary War veteran and Congressman. His ancestry included Mayflower passengers like Richard Warren and William Brewster. He showed early literary promise, publishing his first poem at age 13. At Bowdoin College, he befriended Nathaniel Hawthorne and graduated in 1825 with high honors.

### European Tours and Professorships  
After graduation, Longfellow embarked on a three-year European tour (1826–1829), learning French, Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, and German largely through self-study. He met Washington Irving in Madrid, who encouraged his writing. Returning to the U.S., he taught at Bowdoin before accepting the Smith Professorship at Harvard in 1836. During this period, he translated works from European languages and published travel writings and early poetry.

### Courtship and Marriage to Frances Appleton  
Longfellow pursued Frances "Fanny" Appleton persistently despite her initial reluctance. Their courtship inspired his novel *Hyperion* (1839). They married in 1843, and their marriage brought both personal happiness and financial stability. The couple had six children, though two died young. Their daughter Fanny’s birth in 1847 coincided with the publication of *Evangeline*, marking a peak in his literary career.

### Death of Frances and Later Years  
In 1861, Frances died tragically when her dress caught fire while sealing her children’s hair. Longfellow suffered severe burns saving her and wore a beard thereafter. Her death deeply affected him emotionally and creatively, leading him to focus more on translation. He completed his landmark English translation of Dante’s *Divine Comedy* in 1867, which became widely respected. He continued writing and translating until his death in 1882.

### Writing Style and Themes  
Longfellow wrote primarily lyric poetry but experimented with various forms including hexameter, blank verse, ballads, and sonnets. His work emphasized musicality and storytelling, often drawing from mythology and legend. Though private personally, he occasionally infused his poetry with grief over family losses. He advocated for a distinctively American literature and supported abolitionism, though his stance was moderate. His translations played a significant role in introducing European literature to American audiences.

### Critical Reception and Legacy  
Longfellow achieved unprecedented popularity during his lifetime, with his works translated into multiple languages. However, critics like Edgar Allan Poe accused him of imitation and plagiarism, particularly of Alfred Lord Tennyson. Later writers such as Walt Whitman and Lewis Mumford dismissed him as derivative. Despite declining academic esteem in the 20th century, he remains culturally significant—his 70th birthday was celebrated nationally, and he was the first non-British poet honored with a bust in Westminster Abbey’s Poet’s Corner.

## Terms  
- **Fireside Poets**: A group of 19th-century New England poets known for domestic themes and accessible verse.  
- **Lyric Poetry**: Short poems expressing personal emotion or meditation.  
- **Hexameter**: A metrical line of six feet, used in classical epics.  
- **Anapestic/Trochaic Meter**: Metrical patterns based on stressed and unstressed syllables.  
- **Didacticism**: The practice of teaching morals or lessons through literature.  
- **Peritonitis**: Inflammation of the abdominal lining, cause of Longfellow’s death.  
- **Laudanum**: An opium-based tonic used medicinally in the 19th century.  
- **Ether (anesthetic)**: Used for pain relief during surgery and childbirth.  
- **Fireside Poets**: See above.  
- **Poet’s Corner**: A famous area in Westminster Abbey honoring British and international poets.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Scholars debate whether Longfellow’s popularity reflected genuine artistic merit or merely public taste for sentimental verse. His critics argue he lacked innovation compared to contemporaries like Whitman, while defenders highlight his influence on American culture and his mastery of form. The extent to which his personal tragedies shaped his poetry remains contested, especially regarding the emotional depth of his elegiac works. Additionally, the motivations behind Poe’s attacks—whether personal rivalry or calculated publicity—are still discussed among literary historians.
