# St. Louis

## Overview
St. Louis is an independent city in Missouri, located near the confluence of the Mississippi and Missouri Rivers. Founded by French fur traders in 1764, it became a major 19th-century port and a gateway to the American West. Today, it is a significant economic, cultural, and transportation hub, though its history is marked by periods of rapid growth, racial segregation, and population decline.

## Body

### Founding and Early History
The area was a center of the Mississippian culture, known for its earthwork mounds, before European contact. French explorers arrived in the 17th century, and the region was ceded to Spain in 1763 after the Seven Years' War. In 1764, French fur traders Pierre Laclède and Auguste Chouteau founded St. Louis on a high bluff overlooking the Mississippi River, naming it for King Louis IX of France. The settlement's economy was built on the fur trade. The U.S. acquired the territory in 1803 via the Louisiana Purchase, and St. Louis became the departure point for the Lewis and Clark Expedition.

### 19th-Century Growth and Division
Steamboat traffic and immigration, particularly from Ireland and Germany, fueled rapid growth in the 19th century. The city was a slave state hub with complex racial dynamics, including freedom suits filed by enslaved people. After the Civil War, the completion of the Eads Bridge in 1874 linked St. Louis to industrial development in Illinois. In 1877, the city voted to secede from St. Louis County, becoming an independent city with fixed boundaries—a decision that later limited its ability to annex growing suburbs.

### 20th-Century Peak, Decline, and Social Struggle
St. Louis was the nation's fourth-largest city by 1900 and hosted the 1904 World's Fair and Olympics. The 20th century saw significant industrialization and the Great Migration of African Americans to the city. However, it was also marked by severe racial segregation, including a 1916 residential segregation ordinance and widespread racial covenants. The city peaked in population in 1950 but then experienced dramatic decline due to suburbanization, industrial restructuring, and its limited geographic size. Urban renewal projects, like the Pruitt-Igoe housing complex, often failed.

### Modern Economy and Culture
Today, St. Louis has a diverse economy with strengths in services, manufacturing, and biotechnology, anchored by institutions like Washington University and numerous Fortune 500 companies. The city is a major transportation hub with extensive rail, river, and air connections. Its cultural landscape includes the Gateway Arch, major sports franchises, and a rich musical heritage in blues and jazz. Revitalization efforts since the 1980s have focused on downtown and specific neighborhoods, though the city continues to address challenges like crime and population loss.

## Terms
- **Independent City:** A city not legally part of any county, governing itself. St. Louis has been one since 1877.
- **Mississippian Culture:** A Native American agricultural society (c. 900–1500) known for building large earthen mounds, centered at nearby Cahokia.
- **Louisiana Purchase:** The 1803 acquisition by the U.S. of the Louisiana Territory from France, which included St. Louis.
- **Gateway Arch:** A 630-foot stainless steel monument on the St. Louis riverfront, symbolizing the city's role as the "Gateway to the West."
- **Pruitt-Igoe:** A large public housing complex built in the 1950s that became a symbol of urban decline and was demolished in the 1970s.
- **MetroLink:** The light rail system serving the St. Louis metropolitan area.
- **Port of St. Louis:** A major inland port on the Mississippi River, one of the largest in the U.S. by tonnage.
- **Great Migration:** The movement of six million African Americans from the rural South to cities in the North, Midwest, and West between 1916 and 1970.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Impact of Independence:** Scholars debate the long-term consequences of St. Louis's 1877 separation from St. Louis County, particularly how it limited the city's tax base and ability to compete with suburbs.
- **Causes of Population Decline:** The relative importance of factors like "white flight," industrial job loss, and the city's fixed geographic boundaries in its post-1950 population loss is a subject of ongoing analysis.
- **Racial Segregation and Inequality:** The legacy and ongoing effects of historical policies like racial covenants and discriminatory housing practices on the city's current social and economic geography are widely studied.
- **Urban Revitalization:** The effectiveness and equity of downtown-focused revitalization efforts versus neighborhood-based investment remain topics of local policy debate.
