# Martial arts

## Overview
Martial arts are codified systems of combat practiced for self-defense, military and law enforcement applications, competition, physical fitness, spiritual development, and cultural preservation. While the term derives from the Latin "arts of Mars" (Roman god of war) and was used for European combat systems as early as the 1550s, it was popularized globally in the 1960s–1970s through Hong Kong cinema and figures like Bruce Lee. These arts vary by technical focus (armed/unarmed, striking/grappling), intent (combat sport, self-defense, spirituality), and cultural origin, ranging from ancient folk wrestling to modern hybrid systems like Mixed Martial Arts (MMA).

## Timeline
- **10,000–6,000 BCE** — Cave paintings in eastern Spain depict organized groups fighting with bows and arrows.
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Depictions of belt wrestling appear in Mesopotamian and Ancient Egyptian art.
- **3rd millennium BCE** — Earliest known depiction of boxing found on a Sumerian relief in Mesopotamia.
- **c. 350 BCE** — Sun Tzu writes *The Art of War* during China's Warring States period.
- **12th century** — Establishment of samurai nobility in Japan formalizes Japanese martial arts traditions.
- **1598** — *Muyejebo* published, documenting Korean martial arts during the Joseon era.
- **1882** — Kano Jigoro founds the Kodokan School, establishing modern Judo.
- **1913** — Fédération Internationale d'Escrime founded, standardizing international fencing rules.
- **1950s–1960s** — Jujutsu, judo, and karate gain mainstream Western popularity; Taekwondo developed in Korea.
- **1993** — First Pancrase event held in Japan; emergence of modern MMA competition.
- **2011** — UNESCO inscribes Taekkyon on the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity List.

## Body

### Etymology and Terminology
The term "martial arts" originates from a Latin phrase meaning "arts of Mars," referencing the Roman god of war, and described European combat systems by the 1550s. It gained global currency during the 1960s–1970s "chopsocky" film wave led by Bruce Lee. Prior to the 1970s, "martial science" and "Chinese boxing" were common terms for Asian arts. Some scholars argue "fighting arts" is more accurate, as many systems were not created by or for professional warriors ("martial" in the strict sense).

### Classification by Technical Focus
**Unarmed arts** are grouped into striking (punches, kicks, elbows, knees), grappling (throwing, joint locks/chokes, pinning, clinch fighting), and hybrid systems covering both. Examples include Boxing and Karate (striking), Judo and Wrestling (grappling), and Muay Thai or Wing Chun (hybrid/clinch). **Armed arts** employ melee weapons (blades, polearms, sticks) and include traditions like Eskrima, Silat, Kalaripayattu, Kobudo, and Historical European Martial Arts (HEMA). Specific Japanese weapon arts include Kenjutsu/Kendo (sword), Bojutsu (staff), and Kyudo (archery). Modern sports derivatives include fencing and competitive archery.

### Classification by Intent
**Combat-oriented** systems prioritize practical fighting ability. **Spirituality-oriented** arts emphasize inner development. Korean arts (Taekkyon, Taekwondo, Hapkido) focus on "inner peace" through meditation, justifying force only for self-defense. Japanese *Budo* (martial way) uses the suffix *-do* ("path") to denote a spiritual journey. The Persian *Varzesh-e Pahlavani* (Pahlevani and Zourkhaneh rituals), recognized by UNESCO, originated as warrior training during the Safavid era.

### Historical Development
**Pre-modern origins:** Warfare evidence dates to the Epipalaeolithic/Neolithic. Wrestling (c. 3000 BCE) and boxing (3rd millennium BCE) are the oldest documented combat sports. Chinese and Indian arts likely influenced each other early on; the Sangam literature (2nd century BCE–2nd century CE) records precursors to Kalaripayattu. In Europe, Greek Olympic events (boxing, wrestling, pankration) and Roman gladiatorial combat were early formalizations. Medieval European fight manuals (e.g., Liechtenauer’s longsword poem, late 14th century) and Asian treatises (Ming-era *Ji Xiao Xin Shu*, Japanese samurai texts, Indian *Agni Purana*, Korean *Muyejebo*) codified techniques.

**Modern sports emergence (19th–early 20th century):** The mid-to-late 19th century saw the codification of boxing (Marquess of Queensberry Rules, 1867), wrestling, fencing, and Japanese arts (Judo founded 1882; Karate, Kendo developed from Edo-period schools suppressed during the Meiji Restoration). Western interest in Asian arts grew via trade; Edward William Barton-Wright introduced Jujutsu to Europe and founded Bartitsu (1890s). Fencing and Greco-Roman wrestling entered the 1896 Olympics.

**Mid-to-late 20th century:** WWII and the Korean War exposed Western military personnel to Asian arts, boosting Jujutsu, Judo, and Karate popularity (1950s–60s). Osamu Noguchi created Kickboxing (1950s); American Kickboxing emerged in the 1970s. Bruce Lee’s films and open teaching sparked a global "kung fu wave" (1970s). Military systems developed included KAPAP (1930s), Krav Maga (1950s), Systema (USSR), and Sanshou (China). The US military revived hand-to-hand training with LINE (1989).

**Contemporary era (1990s–present):** The first Pancrase event (1993) and K-1 rules catalyzed modern MMA. Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu proved dominant in early UFC/PRIDE events. HEMA revived via rediscovered medieval/Renaissance manuals. Taekkyon gained UNESCO recognition (2011).

### Revival and Suppression
British colonial authorities banned many Indian martial arts, forcing Kalaripayattu and others underground or disguising them as dance (e.g., Silambam, Mardhani Khel). Some arts survived in the diaspora (Silambam in Malaysia/Indonesia). Gatka and Kalaripayattu are currently resurging.

### Testing, Competition, and Sport
Testing (forms, sparring) determines progression (e.g., belt ranks). **Sparring types:** *Light/medium-contact* restricts force, uses point scoring, and protective gear; critics argue it builds bad habits. *Full-contact* aims for knockout/submission; used in UFC, boxing, K-1, Kyokushin Karate (bare-knuckle, no head punches), and Olympic Taekwondo (padded gear, knockout valid). Grappling arts (BJJ, Judo) are full-contact via grappling force. The "100-man kumite" in Kyokushin is an extreme endurance test.

**Martial sports** dissociate from combative origins (e.g., Olympic fencing). Olympic events include Judo, Taekwondo, Boxing, Wrestling, Fencing, Archery. The quest for "best art" spawned minimal-rules tournaments (Vale Tudo, Shooto, Pancrase, UFC 1), evolving into MMA. Non-sparring competitions include breaking, forms (kata/poomsae), and tricking. Governments have regulated arts into sports for political control (e.g., PRC’s Wushu to suppress subversive lineage traditions).

### Health Benefits
Training improves physical fitness (strength, stamina, flexibility, coordination) and mental health (self-esteem, self-control, emotional/spiritual well-being). Some schools focus purely on therapy. Bruce Lee viewed martial arts as emotional/mental expression through fluidity and spontaneous reaction.

### Military and Law Enforcement Applications
Traditional concepts inform modern training: *point shooting* (firearms via muscle memory, akin to Iaido). WWII SOE agents Fairbairn and Sykes taught *Defendu* (boxing/Jujutsu hybrid) to Allied special forces; Rex Applegate’s *Kill or Get Killed* became a classic manual. Modern composite systems: US Army Combatives (Matt Larsen), Israeli KAPAP/Krav Maga, USMC MCMAP, European Unifight. Historical dagger defenses (Fiore dei Liberi, Codex Wallerstein) integrated into US manuals (1942) and persist alongside Eskrima/Silat. Bayonet (spear derivative) used in Iraq War. Police use arts like Aikido (Tokyo Riot Police).

### Industry and Fraud
A major industry since the 1970s. Estimates: 50M Karate practitioners (Web Japan), 70M Taekwondo practitioners (South Korea, 2009), 6.9M US participants (2007), US equipment wholesale $314M (2007), US industry revenue $40B (2003, R.A. Court). Equipment includes conditioning tools (makiwara, wooden dummy, breaking boards) and protective gear (gloves, headgear).

The 1970s popularity surge spawned fraudulent schools ("McDojos," "belt factories"), the 1980s "ninja craze," and comic-book scams targeting adolescents. Colored belts (Kyū ranks) became commercial incentives. Red flags: cult-like mentality, discouraging cross-training, teaching ineffective/dangerous techniques (e.g., "no-touch KOs"), outlandish lineage claims, self-promotion to 10th Dan, or claiming invented "deadliest" styles.

## Terms
- ****Budo**** — Japanese term for "martial way"; the suffix *-do* ("path") indicates a discipline aimed at spiritual development, not just combat technique.
- ****Kata / Poomsae / Aka**** — Choreographed patterns of movements practiced solo or in pairs to preserve technique, demonstrate skill, or compete; used in Karate, Taekwondo, and other arts.
- ****Kumite**** — Sparring or "meeting of hands" in Japanese arts; in Kyokushin, the *100-man kumite* is an extreme endurance test of 100 consecutive rounds.
- ****McDojo / Belt Factory**** — Pejorative terms for commercial schools prioritizing profit over quality, characterized by rapid belt promotions, high fees, and questionable technique.
- ****Mixed Martial Arts (MMA)**** — A full-contact combat sport allowing striking and grappling from various arts, evolved from minimal-rules tournaments (Vale Tudo, UFC 1, Pancrase).
- ****Historical European Martial Arts (HEMA)**** — Modern practice reconstructing European combat systems from surviving medieval/Renaissance fight manuals (e.g., Liechtenauer, Fiore dei Liberi).
- ****Varzesh-e Pahlavani**** — Persian "heroic sport" (Pahlevani and Zourkhaneh rituals), a UNESCO-listed system originating in the Safavid era for warrior training.
- ****Defendu**** — A WWII-era close-quarters combat system blending Western boxing and Jujutsu, taught to Allied special forces by Fairbairn and Sykes.
- ****Krav Maga**** — Israeli military self-defense system developed in the 1950s, emphasizing instinctive movements and aggressive counter-attacks.
- ****Sangam Literature**** — Ancient Tamil texts (2nd century BCE–2nd century CE) documenting early South Indian combat techniques, precursors to Kalaripayattu.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Terminology accuracy:** Whether "martial arts" is a misnomer for systems never used by professional warriors, with "fighting arts" proposed as a better descriptor.
*   **Sport vs. combat effectiveness:** Whether competition rules (point sparring, protective gear, banned techniques) diminish real-world applicability or encourage "trophy hunting" over moral cultivation.
*   **Pedagogical value of contact levels:** Whether light/medium-contact sparring instills bad habits (pulling punches) or serves as necessary scaffolding for full-contact readiness.
*   **Cultural preservation vs. modernization:** Tension between maintaining traditional lineage/family systems (often suppressed by states, e.g., PRC’s Wushu regulation) and adapting to global sport/industry standards.
*   **Fraud detection:** Difficulty for consumers in distinguishing legitimate instruction from "McDojos," cult-like groups, or instructors making false claims (no-touch knockouts, invented styles, self-awarded high ranks).
