# Encyclopédie

## Overview
The *Encyclopédie* (1751–1772) was a monumental French encyclopedia edited by Denis Diderot and Jean le Rond d'Alembert. It is the defining intellectual project of the Enlightenment, aiming to systematically gather and disseminate all human knowledge to "change the way people think." Its publication was a major cultural and political event, facing censorship and controversy for its progressive ideas on religion, politics, and society.

## Timeline
- **1728** — Ephraim Chambers publishes *Cyclopaedia* in English, the initial model for the project.
- **1745** — A prospectus for a French translation, titled *Encyclopédie*, is published.
- **1747** — Jean Paul de Gua de Malves resigns as editor; Diderot and d'Alembert are hired.
- **1751** — The first volume of the *Encyclopédie* is published.
- **1752** — A royal edict briefly suspends publication for its "irreligious" content.
- **1758** — D'Alembert resigns as co-editor; Diderot continues alone.
- **1759** — The French government suspends the project's official privilege.
- **1765** — The final text volumes (8–17) are published, with a falsified Neuchâtel imprint.
- **1772** — The final volumes of engravings are completed.
- **1776–1780** — Charles Joseph Panckoucke publishes supplementary volumes and an index.

## Body

### Origins and Publication
The *Encyclopédie* began as a planned French translation of Ephraim Chambers's *Cyclopaedia*. After disputes with the initial translator, bookseller André Le Breton hired d'Alembert and Diderot to lead the project. Publication began in 1751 but was soon suspended by royal edict for its radical content. Work continued unofficially, aided by powerful supporters like minister Malesherbes. To avoid censorship, later volumes were published with a falsified Swiss imprint (Neuchâtel) while likely being printed in Paris. The first edition comprised 17 text volumes and 11 volumes of engravings, totaling about 71,818 articles.

### Contributors and Compilation
Over 140 named contributors, known as the *Encyclopédistes*, participated, including Voltaire, Rousseau, and Montesquieu. The most prolific was Louis de Jaucourt, who wrote 25% of the articles. The work was largely compiled from existing sources, most notably Chambers's *Cyclopaedia* and the Jesuit *Dictionnaire de Trévoux*, often with little alteration. Diderot later regretted some of this copying. The *Encyclopédie* pioneered the systematic attribution of articles to contributors using symbols.

### Structure and Scope
D'Alembert's "Preliminary Discourse" presented a "tree of knowledge" classifying human understanding into Memory (History), Reason (Philosophy), and Imagination (Poetry), placing human reason at its center. The encyclopedia used extensive cross-references, though their use was inconsistent. Its scope was a dictionary of the "sciences, arts, and crafts," emphasizing the mechanical arts (e.g., detailed articles on glass-making, silk-making) over history. Biographies were initially excluded but later included, often within geographical entries.

### Contents and Controversies
The *Encyclopédie* disseminated Enlightenment political theories, such as the social contract and natural rights, challenging divine right monarchy. Its treatment of religion was ambivalent, ranging from orthodox to openly critical of the Catholic Church. Economically, it advocated for laissez-faire principles and criticized monopolies. The work's detailed coverage of technology and crafts aimed to elevate the status of artisans and democratize practical knowledge.

## Terms
- **Encyclopédistes**: The collective name for the contributors to the *Encyclopédie*.
- **Preliminary Discourse**: The introduction by d'Alembert that outlines the encyclopedia's philosophy and presents a taxonomy of human knowledge.
- **Tree of Knowledge**: The classification system in the "Preliminary Discourse" that organizes all human knowledge into three main branches.
- **Rubrics**: Parenthetical labels at the start of articles indicating their place in the "tree of knowledge."
- **Cross-references**: Links between articles, intended to connect knowledge fragmented by alphabetical order.
- **Philosophes**: The intellectuals of the French Enlightenment who contributed to and championed the *Encyclopédie*.
- **Laissez-faire**: An economic theory favoring free markets and minimal government interference, advocated in the *Encyclopédie*.
- **Social contract**: A political theory where government authority derives from the consent of the governed, discussed in articles like "Political Authority."

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Subversive Cross-References**: A long-standing claim that the *Encyclopédie* contained a "system" of ironic cross-references to secretly attack authority. Scholar Marie Leca-Tsiomis argues there is no evidence for this, noting similar references existed in its conservative source, the *Cyclopaedia*.
- **Editorial Control vs. Contributor Independence**: While Diderot aimed for a unified project, contributors often followed their own interests, resulting in a work of "contradictions" rather than a cohesive party statement.
- **Impact on the French Revolution**: The *Encyclopédie* is strongly associated with the intellectual causes of the Revolution, but the extent of its direct influence on the general populace versus the elite is debated.
