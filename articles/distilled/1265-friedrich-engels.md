# Friedrich Engels

## Overview
Friedrich Engels (1820–1895) was a German philosopher, social theorist, and revolutionary socialist whose lifelong collaboration with Karl Marx produced the foundational texts of Marxism, including *The Communist Manifesto* (1848). Born into a wealthy Prussian textile family, Engels rejected his Pietist upbringing to embrace radical Hegelian philosophy and, crucially, gained firsthand experience of industrial capitalism while managing his family's cotton mill in Manchester. This empirical grounding allowed him to author *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845), a seminal empirical study that framed the proletariat as the revolutionary subject of history. For twenty years, Engels led a "double life" as a successful businessman in Manchester, financially sustaining the impoverished Marx family in London while maintaining a clandestine revolutionary partnership. After retiring in 1869, he moved to London, edited the posthumous volumes of *Das Kapital*, and became the leading authority of the Second International, codifying "scientific socialism" in works like *Anti-Dühring* (1878) and *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific* (1880). His application of dialectics to nature in *Dialectics of Nature* later became a cornerstone of Soviet state ideology, sparking enduring scholarly debate over whether Engels systematized Marx's thought into a rigid "dialectical materialism" distinct from Marx's own historical method.

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation (1820–1844)
Engels was born in Barmen, a centre of early German industrialisation ("the German Manchester"), into a family whose wealth derived from cotton milling and bleaching. His parents were devout Pietists, a Lutheran movement stressing personal devotion and a Calvinist work ethic. Engels rebelled early: removed from the *Gymnasium* in 1837 to enter commerce, he pursued literature and philosophy secretly. In Bremen (1838–1841), he shed his faith under the influence of David Strauss’s *The Life of Jesus* (1835), declaring himself a "Straussian," and adopted a Hegelian pantheism. His *Letters from Wuppertal* (1839), published pseudonymously, attacked the social devastation of industrialisation—child labour, pollution, alcoholism—and linked it to the hypocrisy of Pietist factory owners, causing a rupture with his family.

During his 1841–1842 military service in Berlin, Engels attended university lectures and joined *Die Freien* (The Free), a radical Young Hegelian circle including Bruno Bauer and Max Stirner. They inverted Hegel’s conservative system, using his dialectic to attack religion and the Prussian state. Ludwig Feuerbach’s *The Essence of Christianity* (1841) provided the decisive break: religion was the alienation of human essence onto a divine object. Engels briefly co-authored a satire depicting Marx as a "swarthy chap from Trier," but their first meeting in Cologne (1842) was "chilly," as Marx distrusted the Berlin radicals' abstract propagandism. Moses Hess then claimed to have converted Engels to communism in late 1842.

### The Manchester Experience and Early Theory (1842–1844)
Engels’s father sent him to the family firm in Manchester to quell his radicalism; the move backfired. Manchester, the "shock-city" of the Industrial Revolution, provided the empirical basis for his theory. Guided by his partner Mary Burns, an Irish factory worker, Engels investigated the slums ("Little Ireland"), Chartist meetings, and Owenite halls. He concluded that "economic factors... play a decisive role in the development of the modern world."

This research produced two foundational texts. *Outlines of a Critique of Political Economy* (1844) applied Feuerbach’s concept of alienation to economics, arguing private property turned humans into commodities. Marx called it a "brilliant sketch" that shifted his own focus from philosophy to political economy. *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845) combined personal observation with parliamentary reports to document the "social murder" of the working class by the bourgeoisie. Though later scholarship shows Engels relied heavily on written sources rather than pure observation, the work established the proletariat as the historical agent of its own liberation and provided the first application of historical materialism to a concrete national history.

### Collaboration with Marx and the 1848 Revolutions (1844–1849)
Their second meeting in Paris (August 1844) revealed "complete agreement in all theoretical fields." They co-authored *The Holy Family* (1845), critiquing the Bauer brothers' idealism, and *The German Ideology* (1846), which formulated historical materialism: "It is not consciousness that determines life, but life that determines consciousness." They argued the economic "base" (mode of production) determines the legal, political, and ideological "superstructure." The manuscript was abandoned "to the gnawing criticism of the mice."

In Brussels, they organized the Communist Correspondence Committee, which merged with the League of the Just to form the Communist League. Engels drafted two catechism programmes (*Draft of a Communist Confession of Faith*, *Principles of Communism*) before suggesting the title *The Communist Manifesto*. Published February 1848, it declared history as the history of class struggles.

During the 1848 Revolutions, they edited the *Neue Rheinische Zeitung* in Cologne, supporting a bourgeois-democratic revolution as a prerequisite for a proletarian one. Engels specialized in military commentary. After the counter-revolution triumphed, he fought in the Baden-Palatinate uprising (May–July 1849) as aide-de-camp to August Willich, participating in four engagements including Rastatt Fortress. The experience of revolutionary military incompetence instilled a lifelong distrust of amateurism in warfare.

### The Manchester Decades: The "Double Life" (1850–1870)
Reunited in London (1849), Engels and Marx faced poverty. In November 1850, Engels returned to Manchester, reconciling with his family to enter the firm as a clerk, eventually becoming a partner (1864). For twenty years, he lived a "nervous, sapping sacrifice": a respectable cotton merchant by day (member of the Royal Exchange, rider with the Cheshire Hounds), a communist revolutionary by night. He sent £3,000–£4,000 to Marx, funding *Das Kapital*.

Despite business demands, Engels maintained intense intellectual collaboration. He ghostwrote hundreds of articles for Marx’s *New-York Daily Tribune* correspondence. He wrote *The Peasant War in Germany* (1850), using historical materialism to argue the 1848 failure repeated the 16th-century Peasants' War: the German bourgeoisie was too timid to lead. He became a respected military analyst ("The General"), writing on the Crimean War, the American Civil War, and naval technology, partly to counter the "military-putschist" theories of Willich.

Engels provided Marx with crucial industrial data for *Das Kapital*—machinery costs, bookkeeping, cotton market structures, and insider details on financial crises like the 1857 "kite-flying" (speculative bill-jobbing). He wrote anonymous reviews generating a "journalistic firestorm" for Volume I (1867). He retired in 1869 with a £12,500 settlement.

### London: The "Grand Lama" and Guardian of Marxism (1870–1895)
Engels moved to 122 Regent's Park Road, London, becoming the intellectual hub of international socialism. He led the General Council of the First International, orchestrating the expulsion of Mikhail Bakunin’s anarchists at the Hague Congress (1872). His *On Authority* (1872) argued that large-scale industry necessitated authority and subordination even post-revolution.

Prompted by Eugen Dühring’s rising influence in the German Social Democratic Party (SPD), Engels wrote *Anti-Dühring* (1878), a comprehensive exposition of "scientific socialism" covering philosophy, political economy, and socialism. It popularized three "dialectical laws" (transformation of quantity into quality, interpenetration of opposites, negation of the negation) derived from Hegel but applied to nature and history. The extracted pamphlet *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific* (1880) became the most translated Marxist text of the era (20,000 copies in ten languages by 1892).

Simultaneously, Engels worked on *Dialectics of Nature* (1873–1886), an unfinished attempt to prove dialectical laws were "real laws of development of nature," engaging with contemporary science (electricity, thermodynamics, Darwin). Marx’s responses were "brief and non-committal." Published posthumously in the USSR (1925), it became the basis for Soviet "dialectical materialism."

After Marx’s death (1883), Engels became the "first fiddle." He edited *Das Kapital* Volumes II (1885) and III (1894) from Marx’s "hotchpotch" of illegible drafts. His editorial choices were controversial: he downplayed the autonomous role of credit in crises (favouring overproduction), presented the falling rate of profit as predicting inevitable collapse, and argued in a supplement that the law of value operated in pre-capitalist societies for millennia—contradicting Marx’s own analysis.

Using Marx’s notes on Lewis H. Morgan, Engels wrote *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884), arguing the patriarchal monogamous family arose with private property, marking the "world historic defeat of the female sex." It became a foundational socialist feminist text, though criticized for flawed anthropology and idealist explanations of patriarchy.

In his *Letters on Historical Materialism* (1890s), Engels clarified their method against "vulgar economic determinism," using the metaphor of a "parallelogram of forces": history is the resultant of innumerable individual wills shaped by material conditions, where the superstructure reacts upon the base. He admitted they had "overemphasised the economic factor" in polemics.

Engels advised the Second International (founded 1889), criticizing British sectarianism (SDF) and guiding Italian socialists. In his final years, he developed a strategy of "revolutionary electoralism": universal suffrage allowed the working class to win power via the ballot, as modern armies made insurrection "madness." His "theory of the vanishing army" predicted socialist conscripts would eventually refuse to repress the people. His 1895 introduction to *The Class Struggles in France* emphasized this electoral path, though the SPD press (*Vorwärts*) censored revolutionary passages, portraying him as purely parliamentary—a distortion he protested. He died of oesophageal cancer in August 1895.

## Terms
- ****Historical Materialism**** — The theory, developed jointly by Marx and Engels, that the material "base" of society (the mode of production and class relations) determines the legal, political, and ideological "superstructure"; summed up as "It is not consciousness that determines life, but life that determines consciousness."
- ****Alienation (Entfremdung)**** — A concept adapted from Feuerbach’s religious critique by Engels (1844) and Marx: under capitalism, private property separates workers from the products of their labour, their creative activity, their species-being, and other humans, turning human essence into an alien, commodity form.
- ****Scientific Socialism**** — Engels’s term (contrasted with "Utopian Socialism") for the Marx-Engels doctrine, claiming a scientific basis in the materialist conception of history and the analysis of capitalist political economy, rather than moral ideals or blueprints for future societies.
- ****Dialectics of Nature**** — Engels’s unfinished project (1873–1886) arguing that dialectical laws (e.g., transformation of quantity into quality) are objective laws governing the natural world, not just human thought or history; published 1925, it became the philosophical core of Soviet "dialectical materialism."
- ****Double Life**** — The period 1850–1869 when Engels worked as a managing partner in the family firm Ermen & Engels in Manchester (publicly a respectable bourgeois, fox-hunting merchant) while secretly cohabiting with Mary Burns in working-class districts and funnelling profits to fund Marx’s work in London.
- ****The General**** — Nickname given to Engels by the Marx household for his expertise in military theory; he wrote extensively on the Crimean War, American Civil War, and naval technology, viewing military science as essential for revolutionary strategy.
- ****Anti-Dühring**** — Engels’s 1878 polemic against Eugen Dühring, written with Marx’s approval, which systematized their shared philosophy into "scientific socialism" and formulated the three "dialectical laws" later canonized in Marxism–Leninism.
- ****Socialism: Utopian and Scientific**** — A popular pamphlet (1880) extracted from *Anti-Dühring* contrasting Marxism with earlier socialists (Owen, Fourier, Saint-Simon); by 1892, the most widely translated Marxist text, serving as the primary introduction to the theory for the Second International.
- ****Theory of the Vanishing Army**** — Engels’s late strategic concept (1890s): universal conscription fills armies with socialist workers; as socialist electoral strength grows, the army becomes unreliable and "vanishes" as a pillar of the capitalist state, making peaceful transition via suffrage possible.
- ****Parallelogram of Forces**** — Engels’s metaphor (1890s) for historical causation: the historical event is the resultant vector of innumerable intersecting individual wills, each determined by specific material conditions; the economic base is the "ultimately determining factor," but the superstructure interacts and reacts.

## Debates and open questions
**The "Divergence Thesis" (Engels vs. Marx).** Since the 1920s (György Lukács, Karl Korsch), Western Marxists (Leszek Kołakowski, Norman Levine, Terrell Carver) have argued Engels distorted Marx by creating a rigid, scientistic "dialectical materialism" applying natural-scientific laws to society. They claim Engels’s *Dialectics of Nature* posits a "naturalistic evolutionism" alien to Marx’s "anthropocentrism" (nature as human "inorganic body") and "epistemology of praxis." Critics argue this "Engelsian inversion" laid the groundwork for Stalinist dogmatism.

**Defense of Continuity.** Biographers (Tristram Hunt) and scholars (Kaan Kangal) counter that Marx approved *Anti-Dühring*, shared Engels’s interest in natural science, and that Engels explicitly distinguished the dialectic of nature (gradual merging of opposites) from the dialectic of society (sharp class contradictions). They argue the divergence thesis is a political "scapegoating" of Engels for later Marxism’s failures, noting an "unconscionable philosophical chasm" between Engels’s open, critical method and Stalinist dogma.

**Editing *Das Kapital* Volumes II and III.** Engels’s editorial interventions are contested. He structured Marx’s chaotic drafts but modified crisis theory (emphasizing overproduction over credit), presented the falling rate of profit as a confident prediction of inevitable collapse, and argued the law of value operated in pre-capitalist societies—contradicting Marx’s manuscripts. Scholars debate whether he preserved the core critique or imposed his own earlier theoretical preferences.

**The Origin of the Family and Feminist Theory.** Engels’s *The Origin of the Family* (1884) is foundational for socialist feminism (Clara Zetkin, Rosa Luxemburg), linking women’s oppression to private property and the "world historic defeat of the female sex." Critics (Simone de Beauvoir, Kate Millett, feminist anthropologists) attack its unilinear determinism, flawed reliance on Lewis H. Morgan’s anthropology, idealist explanation of patriarchy (male desire to bequeath property), and contrast with Marx’s more multilinear *Ethnological Notebooks* granting women more agency.

**Revolutionary Strategy: Insurrection vs. Electoralism.** Engels’s 1895 advocacy of "revolutionary electoralism" and the "vanishing army" theory sparked the "revisionist controversy." Eduard Bernstein cited it to justify evolutionary socialism; Lenin and others rejected it as opportunism. The failure of the "vanishing army" prediction (socialist soldiers did not universally mutiny in 1914) created a "tactical vacuum" filled by vanguardism (Lenin) or reformism (Bernstein). Scholars debate whether Engels’s late tactical shift was a realistic adaptation to mass democracy or a theoretical capitulation.

**Authorship of *The Communist Manifesto*.** While the final text was written by Marx, it drew heavily on Engels’s *Principles of Communism* and *Draft of a Communist Confession of Faith*. Scholars debate the extent of doctrinal divergence between the catechism drafts and the Manifesto’s rhetorical final form, though consensus holds the fundamental arguments were shared.
