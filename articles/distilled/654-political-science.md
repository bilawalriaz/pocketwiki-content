# Political science

## Overview
Political science is a social science that studies the allocation and transfer of power, systems of governance, political behavior, and public policy, aiming to describe and explain political phenomena through empirical, descriptive, and explanatory means rather than normative judgment. It emerged as a distinct academic discipline in the late 19th century, separating from political philosophy, history, and law, and was shaped by empiricism, positivism, and university professionalization. The field emphasizes methodological pluralism, employing quantitative and qualitative tools to analyze political processes, institutions, and behavior across countries and contexts, and it continues to evolve through scholarly debates over scientific rigor, relevance, and theoretical integration.

## Body

### Origin and Institutional Formation
Political science began to take shape as a distinct university discipline in the late 19th century, separating from history, philosophy, and law. The term "political science" was not always clearly distinguished from political philosophy, but antecedents included moral philosophy, political economy, political theology, and history. University departments and chairs titled "political science" emerged during this period, and the designation "political scientist" came to denote those with advanced degrees in the field. The American Political Science Association (APSA), founded in 1903, and its journal *American Political Science Review* (1906), were created to distinguish the study of politics from economics and other social phenomena. APSA membership grew from 204 in 1904 to 1,462 in 1915, and members played a key role in establishing distinct political science departments. Internationally, the International Political Science Association was founded in 1949 as part of a UNESCO initiative, followed by national associations in France (1949), Britain (1950), and West Germany (1951).

### Behavioral Revolution and Formal Modeling
In the 1950s and 1960s, a behavioral revolution shifted emphasis toward the systematic, scientific study of individual and group behavior, moving away from institutional analysis and legal interpretation. Scholars such as Robert Dahl, Philip Converse, Paul Lazarsfeld, and Bernard Berelson exemplified this approach. The late 1960s and early 1970s saw increased use of deductive, game-theoretic formal modeling, borrowing theory and methods from economics to study institutions and behavior. William H. Riker and colleagues at the University of Rochester were central proponents. Despite progress, scholars noted that systematic theoretical development remained modest and uneven.

### 21st Century Developments
The Perestroika Movement, introduced in 2000, reacted against what supporters called the "mathematicization" of political science, advocating for methodological pluralism and greater relevance to external audiences. Some evolutionary psychology theories suggest humans evolved psychological mechanisms suited to small-group politics, which may explain cognitive biases in modern political behavior. Crisis anticipation remains an important area, with statistical indicators such as increased variance and correlations used to predict political transitions, as demonstrated in analyses of the 2014 Ukrainian crisis.

### Research Methods and Methodological Diversity
Political science is methodologically diverse, drawing from psychology, social research, and political philosophy. Because it studies conscious human actors, controlled experimentation is often difficult, leading to reliance on observation, surveys, case studies, process tracing, historical analysis, ethnography, and interviews. Theoretical tools include game theory, agent-based models, equation-based models, and opinion dynamics. Political theorists employ feminist theory, the Cambridge School's historical analysis, and Straussian approaches. The field overlaps with sociology, anthropology, and public health, sometimes inheriting or developing contrasting methodologies. The research paper remains the most common form of academic writing in generalist political science.

### Education and Professional Structure
Political science occupies a position between the sciences and humanities in the academy, typically housed in arts and sciences colleges or separate departments. Most U.S. institutions offer BA programs, with MA, PhD, and professional degrees in public policy and administration also common. The field is known as "government" in some older or non-North American institutions, while "political studies" implies a broader approach. Pi Sigma Alpha is the main national honor society for undergraduate and graduate students in the United States.

## Terms
- **Behavioral revolution**: A 1950s–1960s movement emphasizing scientific study of political behavior over institutional or textual analysis.
- **Game theory**: A theoretical framework using mathematical models of strategic interaction among rational actors.
- **Methodological pluralism**: The use of diverse research methods and theoretical approaches within a single discipline.
- **Normative political science**: Scholarship focused on value judgments about how politics ought to be.
- **Positive political science**: Scholarship aimed at describing and explaining political phenomena as they are.
- **Process tracing**: A qualitative method for identifying causal mechanisms in political processes.
- **Scientific method**: Systematic observation, measurement, and experimentation used to develop and test knowledge.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the extent to which political science can achieve the same level of predictive and explanatory rigor as the natural sciences, given the complexity and consciousness of human actors. The Perestroika Movement highlighted tensions between mathematical formalization and methodological diversity, with ongoing disagreement over the proper balance between quantitative and qualitative approaches. The integration of normative and positive political science remains contested, as does the field's relevance to external audiences. Additionally, the applicability of evolutionary psychology to political behavior raises questions about the universality and adaptability of ancestral psychological mechanisms in modern political systems.
