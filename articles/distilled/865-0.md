# 0

## Overview

Zero (0) is a number representing an empty quantity and serves as the additive identity across integers, rationals, reals, and complex numbers. As a digit, it functions as a placeholder in positional notation systems, enabling compact representation of large numbers. Its development—from ancient placeholder symbols to a full-fledged number in Indian mathematics and its subsequent transmission to the Islamic world and Europe—fundamentally shaped mathematics, science, and computing.

## Body

### Etymology and Names

The English word "zero" derives from Italian zero, itself from Arabic ṣafira or ṣifr, which originally meant "empty" in pre-Islamic Arabic. The Arabic term was used to translate the Sanskrit śūnya ("void, empty"). Fibonacci used zephyrum, which evolved through Venetian zevero to "zero." Alternative English names include nought, naught, nil, and cipher. In contexts with adjacent digits, zero is often pronounced "oh." Sports use specialized terms: "love" in tennis (possibly from French l'œuf, "egg") and "duck" in cricket (from "duck's egg").

### Mathematical Roles

As a digit, zero serves as a placeholder in positional number systems, indicating that a particular power of the base contributes nothing to the total. For example, in 205, the zero signifies no tens. This principle applies across all bases, including binary and hexadecimal. As a number, zero is the smallest nonnegative integer, the additive identity (x + 0 = x), and an absorbing element for multiplication (x · 0 = 0). It is even, neither prime nor composite, and neither positive nor negative. Division by zero is undefined because zero has no multiplicative inverse. The expression 0/0 is an indeterminate form requiring alternative methods like l'Hôpital's rule. The empty sum equals 0, and the empty product equals 1, making 0! = 1.

### Historical Development

In the Ancient Near East, Egyptians used the nfr hieroglyph as a placeholder in accounting records, while Babylonians developed a sophisticated base-60 positional system using spaces and later two slanted wedges as placeholders. However, these were never treated as numerical values. In the Pre-Columbian Americas, the Maya Long Count calendar required zero as a placeholder in its vigesimal system, with the earliest known use dating to 36 BC. The Maya used a shell-shaped glyph for zero, likely independently developed, possibly by the Olmecs. In classical antiquity, Greek astronomers adopted the Babylonian placeholder, with Ptolemy using a zero symbol (—°) in the Syntaxis Mathematica, also known as the Almagest, by 150 CE for continuous astronomical functions.

In India, zero evolved from a placeholder to a full number. The Lokavibhāga (458 AD) used a decimal place-value system with zero, and Brahmagupta's Brahmasputha Siddhanta (628) formulated explicit arithmetic rules, though his division-by-zero description was incorrect. Bhāskara II's 12th century treatise Līlāvatī instead proposed that division by zero results in an infinite quantity. The first known use of a zero symbol (a small circle) appeared on a stone inscription at the Chaturbhuj Temple, Gwalior, in 876 AD.

### Transmission and Adoption

The concept reached the Islamic world through translations commissioned by Caliph Al-Mansur in 773, with Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī using Hindu numerals in astronomical tables by 813 and explaining zero's use around 825. The Arabic term ṣifr became the basis for the word "algorithm." The Hindu-Arabic system entered Europe via Al-Andalus in the 11th century, with Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202) being pivotal. European adoption was gradual, with zero-based indexing in programming emerging in the late 1950s through LISP and ALGOL.

### Modern Applications

In physics, zero marks natural reference points like absolute zero temperature (lowest possible value) or arbitrary ones like the Celsius freezing point. In computer science, binary systems use 0 and 1 to represent electrical states, with 0 often denoting false in Boolean logic. Zero-based array indexing (as in C) optimizes memory addressing. Databases distinguish null values from zero. Signed zero representations exist in some floating-point formats (IEEE 754), though mathematically −0 and +0 are identical. Unix systems use zero as the epoch reference point (January 1, 1970).

## Terms

- **Additive identity**: A number that, when added to any number, leaves it unchanged (0 + x = x)
- **Placeholder**: A digit indicating that a particular positional value contributes nothing to the total
- **Positional notation**: A number system where a digit's value depends on its position relative to others
- **Indeterminate form**: An expression like 0/0 whose value cannot be determined without additional context
- **Empty sum**: The sum of zero numbers, defined as 0
- **Empty product**: The product of zero numbers, defined as 1
- **Signed zero**: A representation where zero has distinct positive and negative forms in some computer systems
- **Epoch**: A reference date from which time measurements begin (e.g., Unix epoch starts at 00:00 Jan 1, 1970)
- **Vigesimal**: A base-20 number system used by the Maya
- **Sexagesimal**: A base-60 number system used by the Babylonians

## Debates and Open Questions

Several historical questions remain debated: whether the Egyptian nfr hieroglyph truly represented zero or merely indicated balance; whether the Olmecs invented zero in the Americas before the Maya; the exact origin of the Indian circular zero symbol; and whether zero should be classified as a natural number (mathematicians remain divided on this). The philosophical resistance of ancient Greeks to zero as a number—rooted in their belief that "there is no void"—also reflects enduring tensions about the nature of nothingness versus numerical representation.
