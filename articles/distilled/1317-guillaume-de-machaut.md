# Guillaume de Machaut

## Overview
Guillaume de Machaut (c. 1300–1377) was a French composer and poet who dominated the *ars nova* ("new art") style of late medieval music. He is regarded as the most significant French composer and poet of the 14th century and the leading European composer of his era. Machaut synthesized the poet-composer tradition of the troubadours and trouvères, perfecting secular *formes fixes* (rondeau, virelai, ballade) and the motet while producing the earliest known complete polyphonic Mass Ordinary by a single composer, the *Messe de Nostre Dame*. His unprecedented survival of manuscripts—curated by himself—provides rare biographical depth and a comprehensive view of his output, influencing later poets such as Geoffrey Chaucer and Eustache Deschamps.

## Timeline
- **c. 1300** — Born near Reims, one of seven children; surname likely derives from the town of Machault, 30 km northeast of Reims.
- **1323–1346** — Employed as secretary to John I, Count of Luxembourg and King of Bohemia; accompanies him on military expeditions across Europe.
- **1330–1337** — Appointed canon of Verdun (1330), Arras (1332), and Reims (1337), providing ecclesiastical income.
- **1346** — King John killed at the Battle of Crécy; Machaut enters service of various aristocrats, including Bonne of Luxembourg, Jean de Berry, Charles V (Duke of Normandy), and Charles II of Navarre.
- **1349** — Survives the Black Death; patron Bonne dies of the plague.
- **Early 1360s** — Composes *Messe de Nostre Dame*, likely for Reims Cathedral.
- **1361–1365** — Writes *Le voir dit*, a meta-fictional narrative poem recounting a purported love affair with Péronne d'Armentières.
- **c. 1372** — Writes *Prologue*, a poetic treatise on his craft, serving as preface to his collected works.
- **1377** — Dies in Reims, possibly in April; elegies composed by contemporaries such as F. Andrieu.

## Body

### Life and Career
Machaut was educated in the Reims region and entered the service of John of Luxembourg in 1323, a position that required extensive travel on military campaigns. He accumulated multiple canonries (Verdun, Arras, Reims), securing financial stability while retaining his role as a royal secretary. After King John’s death at Crécy (1346), Machaut transitioned to the patronage of the French high nobility, notably the future Charles V and Charles II of Navarre. He survived the Black Death (1348–49), which killed his patron Bonne, and spent his final decades in Reims supervising the compilation of his complete-works manuscripts. His poem *Le voir dit* (c. 1361–65) claims to document a late love affair with the young Péronne d'Armentières, though scholars debate its autobiographical veracity.

### Secular Music
Machaut composed predominantly secular music, setting courtly love lyrics in five main genres: the *lai* (a long, non-strophic sequence), the *virelai*, the *ballade*, the *rondeau* (the three *formes fixes*—fixed forms with recurring refrains), and the *motet*. He retained the structural *formes fixes* but innovated through creative text-setting and cadences; for instance, his rondeaux often feature a long melisma (many notes on one syllable) on the penultimate syllable, though exceptions like R18 "Puis qu'en oubli" are largely syllabic (one note per syllable). His motets are typically three-voice compositions where the *tenor* (lowest voice, often borrowed from chant) sings a sacred Latin text while the upper two voices sing secular French texts, creating a deliberate concordance between sacred and secular realms. Musicologist Gilbert Reaney notes Machaut’s unique mastery of both secular and sacred idioms was unprecedented since Adam de la Halle.

### Sacred Music
Machaut’s *Messe de Nostre Dame* (early 1360s) is the earliest complete polyphonic setting of the Mass Ordinary (Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei, Ite missa est) attributable to a single composer. While the earlier Tournai Mass is a cyclic compilation by multiple hands, Machaut’s work was conceived as a unified cycle, though musicologists remain deeply divided on its cyclicity. Arguments against unity include: (1) the mass uses two distinct modes (one for Kyrie/Gloria/Credo, another for Sanctus/Agnus/Ite), lacking a single tonal centre; (2) no extended melodic theme or parody technique (reworking a pre-existing polyphonic work) unifies all movements; (3) evidence suggests the movements were not composed in a single creative act. However, stylistic consistency, the Marian (Mary-centered) focus of the chosen chants, and the composer’s explicit manuscript ordering support its classification as a forerunner to 15th-century cyclic masses by composers like Josquin des Prez. The once-popular theory that it was written for Charles V’s coronation is now considered unlikely.

### Poetry
Machaut’s lyric corpus comprises roughly 400 poems (235 ballades, 76 rondeaux, 39 virelais, 24 lais, 10 complaintes, 7 chansons royales), codifying the *formes fixes*. Most lyrics appear in a separate manuscript section, *Les loanges des dames*, unaccompanied by music, suggesting he typically wrote text before setting select poems to melody. His narrative poems, or *dits* (first-person, octosyllabic rhymed couplets), employ conventions from the *Roman de la rose*: allegorical dreams, personified characters (Hope, Love), and a lover-narrator seeking his lady. Key works include *Le remède de fortune* (c. 1340s), which embeds one of each lyric genre; *Jugement du roy de Behaigne* (before 1346) and *Jugement du roy de Navarre* (1349), poetic debates judged by kings; *Confort d'ami* (1357), a consolation for the imprisoned Charles II of Navarre using biblical and classical *exempla*; and *Prise d'Alexandrie* (after 1369), a verse chronicle of the Crusader exploits of Peter I of Cyprus. His *Prologue* (c. 1372) articulates his principles of genre, rhyme, and manuscript organization, reflecting a career-long obsession with ordering his oeuvre.

### Legacy
Machaut’s death prompted elegies from composers like F. Andrieu. His poetry directly shaped Eustache Deschamps, Jean Froissart, Christine de Pizan, René d'Anjou, and Geoffrey Chaucer. A hypothetical meeting between Chaucer and Machaut is possible during Chaucer’s captivity near Reims (1359) or diplomatic missions at Calais (1360), both involving Jean de Berry. At the Lusignan court in Cyprus, Machaut’s *Prise d'Alexandrie* was read aloud at banquets, reinforcing the courtiers’ self-image as claimants to Jerusalem.

## Terms
- **Ars nova** ("new art"): 14th-century French musical style characterized by increased rhythmic complexity and notation precision, superseding the *ars antiqua*.
- **Ars subtilior** ("more subtle art"): Late 14th-century style following *ars nova*, noted for extreme rhythmic intricacy; Machaut’s death (1377) marks the conventional boundary.
- **Formes fixes** ("fixed forms"): Three standard French secular song structures—rondeau (ABaAabAB), ballade (aabC), and virelai (A bba A)—defined by recurring refrain patterns.
- **Motet**: Polyphonic vocal composition; in Machaut’s works, typically three voices with a sacred Latin *tenor* and two secular French upper voices.
- **Tenor** (from Latin *tenere*, "to hold"): The lowest voice in early polyphony, often borrowing a plainchant melody sung in long notes.
- **Melisma**: A group of notes sung to a single syllable of text; Machaut frequently uses long melismas on the penultimate syllable of rondeau phrases.
- **Syllabic**: Text setting with one note per syllable.
- **Cyclic Mass**: A setting of the Mass Ordinary movements (Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei) unified by a common musical theme, mode, or cantus firmus.
- **Parody technique**: A Renaissance compositional method where a new mass is based on all voices of a pre-existing polyphonic work (e.g., a motet); absent in Machaut’s Mass.
- **Dit** (French "spoken"): A first-person narrative poem in octosyllabic rhymed couplets, not meant to be sung, often using allegorical dreams and characters.
- **Exempla** (singular *exemplum*): Moralizing anecdotes from biblical or classical sources used for instruction, as in *Confort d'ami*.

## Debates and open questions
- **Cyclicity of the *Messe de Nostre Dame***: Musicologists remain deeply divided. The mass lacks a single tonal centre (using two modes), a unifying melodic theme, or parody technique, and movements may not have been composed simultaneously. However, stylistic consistency, Marian thematic unity, and Machaut’s manuscript ordering argue for intentional unity.
- **Autobiographical truth of *Le voir dit***: The poem presents itself as a true story (*voir dit*) of a love affair with Péronne d'Armentières, but includes satirical inconsistencies (e.g., the narrator is old, ill, and impotent) that undermine its factual reliability.
- **Occasion for the Mass**: The traditional claim that the Mass was composed for the coronation of Charles V (1364) is now thought unlikely; the specific liturgical occasion, if any, remains unknown.
- **Chaucer–Machaut contact**: While both were in the retinue of Jean de Berry during the 1360 Treaty of Brétigny negotiations at Calais, and Chaucer was captured near Reims in 1359, no direct evidence confirms they met.
