# Nursing

## Overview

Nursing is a health care profession that integrates the art and science of caring, focusing on the protection, promotion, and optimization of health; prevention of illness and injury; facilitation of healing; and alleviation of suffering through compassionate presence. As the largest component of most healthcare environments, nurses develop care plans collaboratively with physicians, therapists, patients, and families. The profession has evolved from ancient religious caregiving to a modern, evidence-based discipline with diverse specialties, advanced practice roles, and ongoing challenges related to workforce supply and occupational hazards.

## Body

### Premodern Foundations

Nursing historians debate whether ancient care qualifies as "nursing" in the modern sense. The fifth-century BC Hippocratic Collection describes skilled male attendants who may have provided care akin to modern nursing. Around 600 BC, the *Sushruta Samhita* emphasized the importance of anatomical knowledge for nurses. During the Middle Ages, religious orders across Christian, Islamic, and Buddhist traditions provided nursing-like care, with the biblical figure Phoebe often cited as "the first visiting nurse." These traditions established the ethos of compassionate care that remains central to nursing today. The use of the title "sister" for senior nurses in the UK reflects these religious roots.

During the Reformation, Protestant reformers shut down monasteries and convents, allowing a few hundred municipal hospices to remain in operation in northern Europe. Nuns who had been serving as nurses were given pensions or told to marry and stay home. Nursing care went to the inexperienced as traditional caretakers, rooted in the Roman Catholic Church, were removed from their positions. The nursing profession in Europe was extinguished for approximately 200 years.

### 19th Century Professionalization

The Crimean War catalyzed modern nursing when Grand Duchess Elena Pavlovna organized the Order of Exaltation of the Cross, sending twenty-eight "sisters" under Aleksandra Stakhovich to military hospitals in 1854. Florence Nightingale laid the foundations of professional nursing after the Crimean War, in light of a comprehensive statistical study she made of sanitation in India, leading her to emphasize the importance of sanitation. "After 10 years of sanitary reform, in 1873, Nightingale reported that mortality among the soldiers in India had declined from 69 to 18 per 1,000." Nightingale's *Notes on Nursing* (1859) was a popular call to action. Her five environmental factors (fresh air, clean water, drainage, cleanliness, light) became foundational principles.

Nightingale's influence was complemented by Jamaican "doctresses" like Mary Seacole, who served in Crimea and practiced herbal healing. Other pioneers included Agnes Hunt (first orthopedic nurse), Valérie de Gasparin (opened the world's first nursing school at La Source), Agnes Jones (training regime at Liverpool), Linda Richards (first professionally trained US nurse, also established schools in Japan), Clara Barton (founder of American Red Cross), and Saint Marianne Cope (hospital cleanliness standards). The deaconess movement began in Germany in 1836, spreading over 5,000 deaconesses across Europe within fifty years.

### 20th Century Transformation

In the 19th and early 20th century, nursing was considered a woman's profession, just as doctoring was a profession for men. With increasing expectations of workplace equality during the late 20th century, nursing became an officially gender-neutral profession, though in practice the percentage of male nurses remained well below that of female physicians in the 21st century. Hospital-based training became standard in the US, replacing Nightingale-style schools, but this led to exploitation as hospitals viewed nurses as inexpensive labor. Both World Wars transformed the profession: British nurses served in every overseas campaign during WWII, more US nurses volunteered than any other occupation, and the Nazis had their own Brown Nurses, numbering 40,000.

Post-war developments included undergraduate and graduate nursing degrees, the establishment of nursing research as an academic discipline, and the formation of professional organizations and journals. The first issue of *Nursing Research* appeared in 1952, marking nursing's emergence as a scientific field.

### Modern Challenges and Developments

Global nursing shortages affect South East Asia and Africa most severely. A 2022 McKinsey survey found 28-38% of nurses in the US, UK, Singapore, Japan, and France likely to leave direct patient care, citing safe working environments, work-life balance, meaningful work, and flexible schedules as retention factors. Pay ranked eighth among retention factors. A 2023 American survey found that around 30% were considering leaving patient care.

Nursing education now offers multiple pathways leading to professional credentials, with roles and responsibilities varying by education level. Licensed Practical Nurses (LPNs) have less education and narrower scope than Registered Nurses (RNs). In the United Kingdom and the United States, clinical nurse specialists and nurse practitioners diagnose health problems and prescribe medications and other therapies, depending on regulations that vary by state. In the U.S., nurse practitioners are nurses with a graduate degree in advanced practice nursing, and are permitted to prescribe medications. They practice independently in a variety of settings in more than half of the United States.

Digital health platforms like ConnectRN and Nomad Health connect nurses with job opportunities, while evidence-based practice integrates research findings with clinical expertise and patient preferences. The nursing process as defined by the American Nurses Association comprises five steps: 1) evaluate, 2) implement, 3) plan, 4) diagnose, and 5) assess.

### Occupational Hazards

Nurses face significant occupational risks including high stress levels (40% burnout, 43.2% emotional exhaustion), workplace violence (57% threatened, 17% assaulted in 2011), and musculoskeletal injuries (30-70% related to patient handling). The back is the most frequently injured body part, with up to 72% of nurses reporting non-specific low back pain. Interventions include stress management programs, organizational improvements, and in some Japanese hospitals, powered exoskeletons.

## Terms

- **Nursing process**: A five-step method (evaluate, implement, plan, diagnose, assess) used to provide systematic nursing care
- **Evidence-based practice (EBP)**: Integration of research findings with clinical expertise and patient preferences in care decisions
- **Clinical nurse specialist**: Advanced practice nurse with graduate education who can diagnose and prescribe medications
- **Nurse practitioner**: Nurse with graduate degree in advanced practice nursing, permitted to prescribe medications
- **Activities of daily living (ADL)**: Basic self-care activities like hygiene and toileting that nurses help patients manage
- **Research utilization (RU)**: Earlier movement focusing on applying research findings to practice, superseded by EBP
- **Deaconess**: Member of a religious order providing nursing care, part of a movement beginning in Germany in 1836
- **Doctress**: Term used in Jamaican tradition for skilled healers and caregivers, including figures like Mary Seacole

## Debates and Open Questions

Nursing historians debate whether ancient care qualifies as "nursing" in the modern sense, given definitional challenges. The profession continues to grapple with defining nursing science, as evidenced by repeated scholarly attempts to answer "What is nursing science?" The tension between nursing as care-focused versus cure-focused practice remains, with the line between nursing and medicine often blurred. Gender dynamics persist despite official gender neutrality, with male nurses receiving higher pay than female counterparts. The effectiveness of traditional manual patient handling training versus evidence-based alternatives remains contested, as does the optimal balance between individual-focused and organization-focused interventions for occupational stress.
