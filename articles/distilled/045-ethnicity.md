# Ethnicity

## Overview

An ethnicity or ethnic group is a group of humans who identify with each other based on perceived shared attributes—such as language, culture, ancestry, traditions, religion, history, or social treatment—that distinguish them from other groups. Ethnicities are maintained through long-term endogamy (marriage within the group) and may have a narrow or broad spectrum of genetic ancestry. The concept is central to understanding social identity, nationalism, race relations, and conflict, and has been theorized through competing frameworks—primordialism (ethnicity as ancient and natural) versus constructivism (ethnicity as a social product)—that shape how scholars interpret its origins and persistence.

## Timeline

- **c. 480 BC** — Herodotus defines Greek ethnic identity by shared descent, language, sanctuaries, and customs.
- **Classical antiquity** — Ethnography begins; early authors like Anaximander and Hecataeus of Miletus precede Herodotus.
- **Early Modern English–mid-19th century** — "Ethnic" used to mean heathen or pagan, derived from Greek *ethnos* via Latin *ethnicus*.
- **19th century** — Term returns to meaning "peculiar to a tribe, race, people or nation"; ethnic nationalism develops, tied to race by German theorists including Johann Gottfried Herder.
- **1920s** — Sociologist Robert E. Park puts forward ethnicity theory in the US, based on the assimilation model.
- **1930s–1940s** — "Ethnic" gains sense of "different cultural groups" and "minority group," replacing the deprecated term "race."
- **1950** — UNESCO's "The Race Question" statement recommends dropping "race" in favor of "ethnic groups."
- **1953** — "Ethnicity" first recorded expressing "ethnic character."
- **1969** — Fredrik Barth publishes "Ethnic Groups and Boundaries," instrumental in spreading the term in social studies.
- **1972** — "Ethnic group" enters the Oxford English Dictionary.
- **1982** — Anthropologist David Craig Griffith summarizes research arguing racial and ethnic categories are symbolic markers of incorporation into the global economy.

## Body

### Terminology and conceptual history

The term *ethnic* derives from Greek *ethnos* through its adjectival form *ethnikos*, loaned into Latin as *ethnicus*. In Early Modern English until the mid-19th century, it meant heathen or pagan, as the Septuagint used *ta ethne* ("the nations") to translate Hebrew *goyim* (foreign, non-Hebrew nations). In Homeric Greek, the term could refer to any large group, a host of men, or a swarm of animals; in Classical Greek it took on a meaning comparable to "ethnic group," mostly translated as "nation, tribe, a unique people group"; only in Hellenistic Greek did it narrow to "foreign" or "barbarous" nations. The term *ethnic group* was first recorded in 1935 and entered the Oxford English Dictionary in 1972. The abstract *ethnicity* had been used for "paganism" in the 18th century but came to express "ethnic character" (first recorded 1953). The process of ethnic identity formation is called *ethnogenesis*, in use since about 1950.

### Definitions and major theories

Herodotus (8.144.2) defined Greek ethnic identity by shared descent, language, sanctuaries and sacrifices, and customs. However, Isocrates argued the name "Hellenes" suggested not a race but an intelligence, applied to those sharing culture rather than blood. Max Weber maintained that ethnic groups were *künstlich* (artificial, a social construct), based on subjective belief in shared *Gemeinschaft* (community); group formation resulted from the drive to monopolize power and status. Barth went further, arguing ethnicity was perpetually negotiated by external ascription and internal self-identification, with categorical distinctions maintained despite mobility and contact. Ronald Cohen (1978) claimed that identification of "ethnic groups" by outsiders often reflected inaccurate labels more than indigenous realities, and that claims concerning ethnic identity are often colonialist practices.

Two debates dominated the study of ethnicity: primordialism versus instrumentalism (whether ethnicity is an externally given social bond or an ad hoc political resource), and constructivism versus essentialism (whether identities are products of historical forces or ontological categories). World War II was a turning point: the consequences of Nazi racism discouraged essentialist interpretations, and ethnic groups came to be defined as social rather than biological entities. Approaches include primordialism (ethnicity has historical continuity into the far past), perennialism (nations and ethnic communities are basically the same phenomenon, either continuous or recurrent), constructivism (ethnic groups are products of social interaction), and modernism (ethnicity correlates with the rise of nation-states in the early modern period). Instrumentalist perennialism sees ethnicity as a mechanism of social stratification; Donald Noel theorized that ethnic stratification emerges only when groups contact one another with high ethnocentrism, competition, and differential power.

### Ethnicity theory in the United States

Robert E. Park's ethnicity theory (1920s) argued race is a social category and only one of several factors in determining ethnicity, alongside religion, language, customs, nationality, and political identification. It was based on the assimilation model, outlining four steps: contact, conflict, accommodation, and assimilation. Park attributed the marginalized status of people of colour to failure to assimilate into American culture rather than biological inferiority. Michael Omi and Howard Winant's theory of racial formation critiqued ethnicity theory for being based exclusively on White immigration patterns and neglecting how race complicates communities' interactions with social and political structures. They argued that looking to cultural defect as the source of inequality ignores the structural components of racism.

### Ethnicity and nationality

Anthropologists and historians following Ernest Gellner and Benedict Anderson see nations and nationalism as developing with the rise of the modern state-system in the 17th century, culminating in nation-states. In the 19th century, modern states sought legitimacy through claiming to represent "nations," but invariably included excluded populations who demanded inclusion or autonomy. Multi-ethnic states result either from recent state borders at variance with traditional tribal territories (as throughout Africa) or from recent immigration of ethnic minorities into formerly homogenous nation-states (as in the Netherlands). Feminist scholars, notably Nira Yuval-Davis, have shown that women participate in creating and reproducing ethnic and national categories as "cultural carriers" within the private, family sphere.

### Ethnicity and race

Before Weber, race and ethnicity were seen as two aspects of the same thing; after his introduction of ethnicity as a social construct, they became more divided. The 1950 UNESCO statement "The Race Question" said cultural traits have no demonstrated genetic connection with racial traits and recommended dropping "race" altogether in favor of "ethnic groups." David Craig Griffith (1982) argued that racial and ethnic distinctions allocate workers to different rungs on labor markets, with capitalism imparting effective values to these distinctions. According to Eric Wolf, racial categories were constructed during European mercantile expansion, and ethnic groupings during capitalist expansion. In the US, the Office of Management and Budget states the definition of race for the US Census is not "scientific or anthropological" and takes into account "social and cultural characteristics as well as ancestry."

### Ethno-national conflict

In the 20th century, scholars argued conflicts among ethnic groups can be resolved in two ways: some (Jürgen Habermas, Bruce Barry) argue the state should enforce political and legal equality without acknowledging ethnic identity; others (Charles Taylor, Will Kymlicka) argue states must recognize ethnic identity and accommodate particular needs. The 19th century saw the development of ethnic nationalism, when race was tied to nationalism by German theorists including Herder. Periods cited as examples include the 19th-century consolidation of the German Empire and 20th-century Nazi Germany, which promoted the pan-ethnic idea of acquiring lands inhabited by ethnic Germans. Conflicts in the Near East, south-east Europe, and former Soviet territories are often misleadingly labelled civil wars when they are inter-ethnic conflicts within multi-ethnic states.

### Ethnic groups by continent

Africa is the most ethnically and linguistically diverse continent, with over 3,000 ethnic groups and more than 2,000 languages across 54 countries. Asia has abundant ethnic groups adapted to varied climates; India has more than 2,000 distinct ethnic groups, Indonesia more than 600 across 17,000 islands, and Russia more than 185 recognized groups besides the 80% ethnic Russian majority. Europe has 87 distinct "peoples of Europe" (Pan and Pfeil, 2004), with 33 forming majorities in at least one sovereign state; national minority populations total about 105 million (14% of 770 million Europeans). In North America, the largest pan-ethnic group in the US is White Americans; enslaved Africans were brought from the 16th to 19th centuries. In 2021, 13.5 million Americans identified as non-Hispanic and more than one race, and 20.3 million Hispanic Americans identified as multiracial. South America's populations are commonly of mixed-race (mestizo), indigenous, European, African, and to a lesser extent Asian ancestry. Nearly all states in Oceania have majority indigenous populations, except Australia, New Zealand, and Norfolk Island. Australia's first evident ethnic group was Aboriginal peoples, occupying the continent for more than 50,000 years; Europeans first arrived in 1770 when Captain James Cook charted the east coast.

## Terms

- **Endogamy**: Marriage within a specific group, maintaining ethnic boundaries over generations.
- **Ethnogenesis**: The process by which a separate ethnic identity is formed, whether through division or amalgamation.
- **Primordialism**: View that ethnicity has existed throughout human history with continuity into the far past.
- **Constructivism**: View that ethnic groups are products of human social interaction, not basic human conditions.
- **Ethnocentrism**: The tendency to view the world primarily from one's own culture's perspective and downgrade other groups.
- **Ethnic stratification**: A system where relatively fixed group membership (race, religion, nationality) is used as a major criterion for assigning social positions.
- **Assimilation**: Shedding the particular qualities of a native culture to blend in with a host culture.
- **Panethnicity**: The merging of formerly separate ethnicities into a broader ethnic category.
- **Perennialism**: Approach seeing nations and ethnic communities as basically the same phenomenon, either continuous or recurrent through history.
- **Mestizo**: A person of mixed Spanish and Native American ancestry, common in Mexico and South America.

## Debates and open questions

Whether ethnicity qualifies as a cultural universal is debated: many social scientists, such as Fredrik Barth and Eric Wolf, do not consider ethnic identity universal, regarding it as a product of specific inter-group interactions rather than an essential quality. The primordialism–instrumentalism and constructivism–essentialism debates remain important, though Eriksen argues they have been superseded by attempts to respond to politicized forms of self-representation. Scholars also debate whether descent is always a marker of ethnicity—Kanchan Chandra defines ethnic identity narrowly as a subset of identity categories determined by belief in common descent, while others include culture, language, and history. The question of whether states should acknowledge ethnic identity or enforce purely political and legal equality remains unresolved.
