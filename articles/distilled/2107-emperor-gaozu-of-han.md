# Emperor Gaozu of Han

## Overview
Emperor Gaozu of Han (Liu Bang) was the founder and first emperor of the Han dynasty, ruling from 202 BC until his death in 195 BC. Rising from a peasant background, he overthrew the Qin dynasty and defeated his rival Xiang Yu in a civil war, establishing a dynasty that would endure for over four centuries. His reign established key policies that stabilized the empire after decades of war, including reduced taxation, a shift toward Confucianism, and a pragmatic foreign policy toward the Xiongnu.

## Timeline
- **c. 256 or 247 BC** — Liu Bang is born in Pei County, state of Chu.
- **209 BC** — Liu Bang becomes an outlaw after freeing penal laborers; joins the rebellion against the Qin dynasty.
- **207 BC** — Liu Bang captures the Qin capital Xianyang; the last Qin ruler, Ziying, surrenders to him.
- **206 BC** — Xiang Yu divides the former Qin Empire into Eighteen Kingdoms; Liu Bang is made King of Han, assigned the remote Bashu region.
- **206–202 BC** — The Chu–Han Contention civil war is fought between Liu Bang and Xiang Yu.
- **202 BC** — Liu Bang defeats Xiang Yu at the Battle of Gaixia and establishes the Han dynasty.
- **200 BC** — Liu Bang is besieged by the Xiongnu at the Battle of Baideng; initiates the *heqin* policy for peace.
- **195 BC** — Liu Bang dies in Changle Palace, Chang'an, and is succeeded by his son Liu Ying (Emperor Hui).

## Body

### Early Life and Qin Rebellion
Liu Bang was born into a peasant family in Pei County. He served as a minor local sheriff (*tingzhang*) under the Qin dynasty. After freeing penal laborers who had escaped—facing execution under Qin law—he became an outlaw on Mount Mangdang. In 209 BC, following the Dazexiang uprising against the Qin, Liu Bang rallied the people of Pei County to kill their magistrate and join the rebellion. He became known as the Duke of Pei. Joining the larger rebel forces under King Huai II of Chu, Liu Bang was tasked with attacking the Qin heartland. In 207 BC, he outpaced his rival Xiang Yu, captured the Qin capital Xianyang, and accepted the surrender of the last Qin ruler, Ziying. His forces were ordered to maintain discipline, and Qin laws were abolished, winning local support.

### Chu–Han Contention and Rise to Power
After the Qin fell, the rebel leader Xiang Yu divided the empire, forcing Liu Bang to accept the remote kingdom of Han in Bashu. At the Feast at Swan Goose Gate, Xiang Yu planned to assassinate Liu Bang but was persuaded not to. Liu Bang later escaped and, on the advice of his strategist Han Xin, secretly attacked and conquered the Three Qins, reclaiming the Guanzhong region by 205 BC. This sparked the Chu–Han Contention. Liu Bang suffered a catastrophic defeat at Pengcheng in 205 BC, losing over 200,000 men. He regrouped, secured alliances, and fought a series of battles, including the Battle of Jingsuo (205 BC) and the Battle of Chenggao (204 BC). A temporary peace, the Treaty of Hong Canal, was signed in 202 BC. Liu Bang soon broke the treaty, and with the help of his generals Han Xin and Peng Yue, trapped and defeated Xiang Yu at the Battle of Gaixia. Xiang Yu committed suicide, and Liu Bang became the undisputed ruler.

### Founding and Reign of the Han Dynasty
In 202 BC, Liu Bang established the Han dynasty, initially with its capital at Luoyang, later moved to Chang'an for better defense. He reduced taxes and corvée labor to help the population recover from war. Influenced by the scholar Lu Jia, he promoted Confucianism as a governing philosophy, replacing the harsh Legalism of the Qin. He also reformed the legal system by relaxing some laws. To secure the empire, he gradually eliminated the seven non-clan vassal kings, executing Han Xin and Peng Yue and suppressing rebellions by Ying Bu and Zang Tu. Facing the Xiongnu threat, after a defeat at Baideng in 200 BC, he adopted the *heqin* policy, using arranged marriages and tribute to maintain peace. In his final years, he successfully resisted pressure to change his heir apparent, Liu Ying, from his empress Lü Zhi.

## Terms
- **Corvée labor**: Unpaid, compulsory labor owed by subjects to the state, often for public works projects.
- **Heqin**: A Han dynasty foreign policy of appeasement involving arranged marriages and tribute payments to the Xiongnu to maintain peace.
- **Legalism**: A Chinese philosophy of government that emphasized strict laws and harsh punishments, dominant during the Qin dynasty.
- **Confucianism**: A Chinese philosophical system emphasizing moral virtue, social harmony, and benevolent governance, which became the state ideology under the Han.
- **Vassal kings**: Regional rulers granted territories by the emperor in exchange for allegiance and tribute; many were not from the Liu imperial clan.
- **Chu–Han Contention**: The civil war (206–202 BC) between Liu Bang (Han) and Xiang Yu (Chu) for control of the former Qin Empire.
- **Eighteen Kingdoms**: The division of the former Qin Empire into regional states by Xiang Yu after the dynasty's fall.
- **Three Qins**: The three kingdoms (Qin, Yong, and Sai) into which the Guanzhong region was divided by Xiang Yu, later conquered by Liu Bang.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact birth year of Liu Bang is debated, with sources giving either 256 BC or 247 BC.
- The historicity of legendary events, such as the slaying of the white serpent and the prophecy of the "son of the Red Emperor," is a matter of scholarly debate, often seen as imperial mythology created to legitimize his rule.
