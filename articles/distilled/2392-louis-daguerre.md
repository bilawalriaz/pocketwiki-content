# Louis Daguerre

## Overview
Louis-Jacques-Mandé Daguerre (1787–1851) was a French artist and scientist who, with Nicéphore Niépce, developed the daguerreotype process. This was the first publicly available and commercially successful photographic process, making him one of the fathers of photography. His work revolutionized image-making, though he was also a celebrated painter and inventor of the diorama theatre.

## Timeline
- **18 November 1787** — Born in Cormeilles-en-Parisis, France.
- **c. 1822** — Opens the first diorama theatre in Paris.
- **1829** — Partners with Nicéphore Niépce to improve photographic processes.
- **1833** — Niépce dies; Daguerre continues experiments alone.
- **7 January 1839** — Daguerreotype process announced to the French Academy of Sciences.
- **19 August 1839** — French government acquires the rights and publishes the process, declaring it "free to the world."
- **1839** — Elected Honorary Academician to the U.S. National Academy of Design.
- **8 March 1839** — Fire destroys his Paris diorama theatre; he saves daguerreotype specimens.
- **10 July 1851** — Dies in Bry-sur-Marne, France.

## Body

### Early Career and the Diorama
Daguerre was trained in architecture and panoramic painting. He became a celebrated theatre designer and, in 1822, opened a diorama theatre in Paris. This was a large-scale theatrical illusion using massive translucent paintings (about 70 ft wide and 45 ft tall) lit from different angles. The auditorium revolved to show different scenes, creating awe-inspiring effects of light and movement. The dioramas were highly profitable, earning 200,000 francs a year, and new theatres opened in London and Berlin. Interest waned in the 1840s with the rise of motion photography and cinema.

### Development of the Daguerreotype
In 1829, Daguerre partnered with Nicéphore Niépce, who had created the first permanent camera photograph using bitumen. After Niépce's death in 1833, Daguerre focused on silver salts. His process involved coating a silver-plated copper sheet with light-sensitive silver iodide. The crucial breakthrough was discovering that a faint, invisible "latent" image from a short exposure could be chemically developed into a visible one using mercury vapor. The image was then "fixed" with salt water, later replaced by sodium thiosulfate ("hypo"). The resulting image was laterally reversed and had to be viewed at a specific angle. It was unique and could only be duplicated by re-photographing it.

### Public Release and Competition
After failing to find private investors, Daguerre went public in 1839. The French government acquired the rights and gave the process to the world for free, except in the UK, where a patent was filed, inhibiting its spread. Simultaneously, William Henry Fox Talbot in England had developed a different process using paper negatives (the calotype, 1841), which allowed for multiple prints but lacked the daguerreotype's fine detail. The daguerreotype dominated until the early 1850s, when the cheaper, easier-to-view wet collodion process (producing ambrotypes and tintypes) largely replaced it by 1860.

## Terms
- **Daguerreotype**: A photographic process producing a unique image on a silver-plated copper sheet. The image is laterally reversed and must be viewed at an angle.
- **Latent image**: An invisibly faint image on a photographic plate that can be chemically developed into a visible one.
- **Fixing**: The chemical process of making a photographic image insensitive to further light exposure, typically by removing unexposed silver salts.
- **Heliograph**: An early term for a photograph made by sunlight.
- **Diorama theatre**: A large-scale theatrical illusion using massive, translucent paintings lit from different angles to create realistic scenes.
- **Calotype**: A paper-based photographic process (1841) that produced a negative from which multiple positive prints could be made.
- **Wet collodion process**: A photographic method (early 1850s) using a glass plate coated with collodion, which had to be exposed while still wet. It led to cheaper processes like ambrotypes and tintypes.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Priority of invention**: The announcement of Daguerre's process in 1839 prompted William Henry Fox Talbot to claim priority, as he had independently developed photographic methods. The two processes were fundamentally different, but the competition spurred further development.
- **The "free to the world" gift**: The French government's declaration made the daguerreotype a global standard, but the simultaneous patent filing in the UK created a unique commercial barrier there, affecting its adoption compared to competing processes.
