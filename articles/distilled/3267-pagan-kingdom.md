# Pagan Kingdom

## Overview
The Pagan Kingdom (c. 849–1287) was the first Burmese kingdom to unify the regions of modern-day Myanmar. Its 250-year rule established the foundation for Burmese language, culture, and Theravada Buddhism in the region. The kingdom's decline, driven by internal economic strain and external Mongol invasions, led to centuries of political fragmentation.

## Timeline
- **c. 849** — King Pyinbya fortifies the settlement at Pagan.
- **1044** — Anawrahta seizes power, beginning the empire's formation.
- **1057** — Traditional date for Anawrahta's conquest of Thaton, aiding Theravada Buddhism's spread.
- **1084–1112** — Reign of King Kyansittha, a period of cultural synthesis.
- **1112–1167** — Reign of Alaungsithu, who standardizes administration and expands irrigation.
- **1174–1211** — Reign of Narapatisithu (Sithu II), during which the empire reaches its greatest extent.
- **1211–1235** — Reign of Htilominlo, the last great temple builder.
- **1277–1301** — Series of Mongol invasions begin.
- **1287** — King Narathihapate is assassinated; the empire collapses.
- **1297** — The Myinsaing brothers overthrow the last Pagan king, ending the dynasty's rule.

## Body

### Origins and Early Pagan
The Pagan dynasty was founded by the Mranma (Bamar) people from the Nanzhao kingdom in the mid-to-late 9th century, settling in the Irrawaddy valley among the earlier Pyu city-states. The Mranma gradually absorbed Pyu culture and territory. By the mid-10th century, Pagan had grown from a small settlement into a regional principality. King Anawrahta's accession in 1044 marks the start of verifiable history; he transformed Pagan into a regional power by developing the Kyaukse irrigation basin into a rice granary, which became the kingdom's economic base.

### The Pagan Empire
Anawrahta and his successors unified the Irrawaddy valley and its periphery, creating the first Burmese empire. The kingdom's core was the irrigated Dry Zone of Upper Myanmar, while peripheral regions were administered as tributary states. The empire's economy was primarily agricultural, driven by the expansion of irrigated lands. A key practice was the donation of tax-free land to the Buddhist clergy (sangha), which initially stimulated economic growth but later severely eroded the crown's tax base. By the 13th century, between one-third and two-thirds of Upper Myanmar's cultivatable land was held by religious institutions.

### Culture, Religion, and Architecture
Pagan was an "ethnic mosaic" where Burman, Pyu, and Mon cultures fused, leading to the gradual dominance of the Burmese language and ethnicity. The kingdom was a major center for Theravada Buddhism, which received royal patronage from Anawrahta onward. However, other traditions like Mahayana Buddhism, Hinduism, and animist nat worship remained influential. Pagan's rulers built over 10,000 Buddhist temples, of which over 2,000 remain. Architectural styles evolved from Pyu and Indian models, with innovations in vaulting and the development of distinctively Burmese stupa and hollow temple (gu) designs.

### Decline and Fall
The kingdom declined in the mid-13th century due to a vicious circle: the continuous growth of tax-free religious land reduced royal revenue, weakening the crown's ability to maintain loyalty and control. This led to internal disorders and external challenges. The Mongol invasions (1277–1301) exploited this weakness. After King Narathihapate fled and was assassinated in 1287, vassal states revolted and the empire disintegrated. The Mongols did not occupy Pagan but created a power vacuum, leading to 250 years of political fragmentation.

## Terms
- **Mandala system**: A political model where a sovereign directly rules a core region and administers surrounding areas as tributary vassal states with varying autonomy.
- **Kyundaw system**: The conscription system requiring local chiefs to supply a quota of men for military service based on population.
- **Sangha**: The Buddhist monastic community.
- **Nat spirits**: Indigenous animist deities worshipped alongside Buddhism.
- **Ari monks**: A group of Forest Dweller monks with Tantric and animist practices who held influence at the Pagan court.
- **Gu-style temple**: A hollow Buddhist temple used for meditation and worship, as opposed to a solid stupa.
- **Stupa (pagoda)**: A solid Buddhist structure, often bell-shaped, typically containing a relic chamber.
- **Hluttaw**: The king's privy council, which evolved to manage day-to-day and military affairs.
- **Kyundaw**: Royal bondsmen who owed hereditary service to the crown in exchange for land grants and tax exemptions.
- **Athi**: Commoners who lived on communally-held land and paid head taxes but owed no regular royal service.

## Debates and open questions
- **Origin of the Burmese alphabet**: Mainstream scholarship holds it was derived from the Mon script in 1058, but some evidence suggests it may have evolved from the Pyu script in the 10th century.
- **Anawrahta's conquest of Thaton**: Traditional chronicles state this 1057 conquest brought Theravada Buddhism to Pagan. However, scholar Michael Aung-Thwin argues this is a post-Pagan legend and that Theravada Buddhism was likely borrowed directly from the Pyus or India.
- **Extent of Pagan's control**: While chronicles claim a vast empire, archaeological evidence confirms a smaller core. Scholars debate the degree of effective authority Pagan had over peripheral regions like Arakan and the Shan Hills.
- **Cause of decline**: The primary cause is debated between internal economic collapse due to tax-free land donations and external pressure from Mongol invasions, which are seen as interconnected factors.
