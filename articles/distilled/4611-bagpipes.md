# Bagpipes

## Overview
The bagpipe is a woodwind instrument classified as an aerophone, using enclosed reeds fed from a constant air reservoir in the form of a bag. While the Scottish Great Highland bagpipe is globally famous, bagpipes have been played for centuries across Europe, North Africa, West Asia, and parts of South Asia. The instrument's construction allows for continuous sound, and its history reflects a complex evolution from possible ancient origins to a modern global presence.

## Timeline
- **c. 1000 BCE** — Possible depiction of bagpipes on a Hittite slab at Euyuk, Anatolia (debated).
- **1st-2nd c. CE** — Ancient writers describe instruments using a bladder as an alternative blowing aid.
- **Mid-13th c.** — Bagpipes depicted in the *Cantigas de Santa Maria* (Castile).
- **c. 1380** — Bagpipes explicitly mentioned in Chaucer's *The Canterbury Tales*.
- **Late 14th/early 15th c.** — Earliest known bagpipe artifact: a chanter found in Rostock, Germany.
- **1547** — First clear reference to the Scottish Highland bagpipe at the Battle of Pinkie.
- **1760** — Joseph MacDonald's *Compleat Theory*: first serious study of Highland bagpipe music.
- **19th-20th c.** — Scottish regiments spread global awareness of the Great Highland bagpipe.
- **2010** — Pakistan is the world's largest bagpipe producer, with an industry worth $6.8 million. |
- **2015** — Astronaut Kjell N. Lindgren plays bagpipes in outer space on the ISS.

## Body

### Construction
A set of bagpipes minimally consists of an air supply, a bag, a chanter, and usually at least one drone. The **air supply** is most commonly a blowpipe, but bellows-driven pipes (a 16th/17th-century innovation) use a bellows, creating "cauld wind pipes" that allow for more delicate reeds. The **bag** is an airtight reservoir made from animal skin or synthetic materials like Gore-Tex, regulated by arm pressure to maintain continuous sound. The **chanter** is the melody pipe, played with two hands. Most chanters are open-ended, creating a constant legato sound; technical ornaments are used to create articulation. A few, like the uilleann pipes, have closed ends allowing for rests. The **drone** is a pipe that produces a constant harmonizing note, typically the tonic, and is usually cylindrically bored with a single reed.

### History and Spread
Evidence for bagpipes before the 13th century is uncertain, with ancient descriptions likely referring to mouth-blown instruments using a bag as an alternative blowing aid. Modern scholarship suggests bagpipes became a distinct class in the European Medieval era with the addition of drones. Representations increased in Western European art from the early second millennium. The instrument spread widely, with regional variations developing. In Scotland, the Great Highland bagpipe became a martial instrument, with its "great music" (*ceòl mór*) reflecting this role. Concurrently, many traditional bagpipe forms in Europe declined due to the rise of classical music and later, recorded music.

### Modern Usage and Revival
The 19th and 20th centuries saw the Great Highland bagpipe become globally known through British military regiments, leading to its adoption by militaries and police/fire services worldwide. In recent decades, many traditional bagpipe types have experienced a resurgence, driven by folk music revivals. Examples include the Irish uilleann pipes, Galician gaita, and Balkan gaida. Bagpipes have also entered non-traditional music genres like rock and film scores. The world's largest producer is now Pakistan. Innovations include electronic bagpipes and the first custom-built MIDI bagpipes.

## Terms
- **Aerophone**: A musical instrument that produces sound primarily by causing a body of air to vibrate.
- **Chanter**: The melody pipe of a bagpipe, played with two hands.
- **Drone**: A pipe that produces a constant, harmonizing note throughout play.
- **Stock**: A socket that fastens the various pipes (chanter, drones) to the bag.
- **Blowpipe/Bellows**: The component used to supply air to the bag.
- **Cauld wind pipes**: Bagpipes supplied by a bellows, so air is not heated or moistened by the player's breath.
- **Ceòl mór**: The "great music" of the Scottish Highland bagpipe, reflecting its martial origins.
- **Gaida**: A term for bagpipes common in Southeastern and Eastern Europe.
- **Practice chanter**: A chanter without a bag or drones, used for quiet practice.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The interpretation of ancient artifacts and texts (e.g., the Hittite slab, the Greek *askaulos*, descriptions of Nero's *tibia utricularis*) as evidence for true bagpipes is debated. Modern scholarship suggests these were likely mouth-blown instruments using a bag as an aid, not a distinct class until drones were added in the Medieval era.
- The precise origins and early development of bagpipes in the British Isles prior to the 14th century are contested.
