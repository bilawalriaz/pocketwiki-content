# William Rowan Hamilton

## Overview
Sir William Rowan Hamilton (1805–1865) was a preeminent Irish mathematician, physicist, and astronomer whose work fundamentally shaped modern theoretical physics. He is best known for reformulating classical mechanics (Hamiltonian mechanics) and for inventing quaternions, a foundational system in algebra and linear algebra. His contributions bridged optics, dynamics, and mathematics, providing tools still essential in physics today.

## Timeline
- **4 August 1805** — Born in Dublin, Ireland.
- **1822** — Began systematic study of Laplace's *Mécanique Céleste*; identified a potential error, leading to introduction to Royal Astronomer John Brinkley.
- **1823** — Completed first original mathematical papers; entered Trinity College Dublin.
- **1827** — Appointed Andrews Professor of Astronomy and Royal Astronomer of Ireland; moved to Dunsink Observatory. Published *Theory of Systems of Rays*.
- **1832** — Predicted conical refraction in biaxial crystals.
- **1834** — Presented principle of "Varying Action" (Hamilton's principle) to the Royal Society. Awarded first Cunningham Medal.
- **1835** — Knighted; awarded Royal Medal.
- **1843** — Discovered the algebra of quaternions.
- **1856** — Introduced the icosian game (Hamiltonian path in graph theory).
- **2 September 1865** — Died in Dublin; buried in Mount Jerome Cemetery.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Hamilton displayed prodigious talent from a young age, particularly in languages and mental calculation. A defeat in a mental arithmetic contest at age 10 shifted his focus from languages to mathematics. He studied Euclid, Newton, and advanced works on his own. In 1822, he found a possible error in Laplace's *Mécanique Céleste*, which led to his introduction to John Brinkley. He entered Trinity College Dublin in 1823, where he excelled, earning "optimes" (top grades) in classics and physics. His undergraduate career was cut short in 1827 when, at age 22, he was appointed to succeed Brinkley as Royal Astronomer, a post he held for life.

### Dunsink Observatory and Personal Life
Hamilton lived at Dunsink Observatory from 1827 until his death. He was a celebrated lecturer who attracted diverse audiences. His personal life included early romantic disappointments and a difficult marriage to Helen Bayly in 1833. He was deeply influenced by literary figures like William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge, with whom he corresponded and visited. He was a devout Christian and a "devoted Coleridgean" philosopher.

### Physics: Optics and Mechanics
Hamilton's first major work was on geometrical optics, culminating in *Theory of Systems of Rays* (1827). He developed a single function (Hamilton's principal function) that unified mechanics and optics, helping establish the wave theory of light. From this work, he predicted the phenomenon of **conical refraction** in certain crystals, which was later experimentally confirmed. He then applied his principle of "Varying Action" to dynamics, creating a powerful reformulation of classical mechanics known as **Hamiltonian mechanics**. This approach revealed a deep symmetry between momentum and position and proved adaptable to later theories like quantum mechanics.

### Mathematics: Quaternions
Seeking to extend complex numbers to higher dimensions, Hamilton discovered **quaternions** in 1843. This algebraic system, involving four dimensions, required abandoning the commutative law of multiplication. He famously carved the fundamental equation \(i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1\) on Broom Bridge. Quaternions introduced the cross and dot products and the modern use of the term "vector." Though initially seen as a key tool for physics, their practical use was later supplanted by vector analysis, though they remain important in mathematics and computer graphics.

### Other Mathematical Work
Hamilton also contributed to the theory of equations (examining the quintic), Fourier analysis, and the invention of the **hodograph**. In graph theory, he introduced the **icosian game**, based on what is now called a Hamiltonian path.

## Terms
- **Hamiltonian mechanics**: A reformulation of classical mechanics using a function (the Hamiltonian) that often represents total energy, emphasizing momentum and position coordinates.
- **Quaternions**: A number system extending complex numbers to four dimensions, where multiplication is non-commutative.
- **Conical refraction**: The optical phenomenon where a ray of light entering a biaxial crystal at a specific angle emerges as a hollow cone of rays.
- **Hamilton's principle**: The principle of "Varying Action," stating that the path taken by a physical system is one for which the action integral is stationary.
- **Hodograph**: A diagram representing the path traced by the tip of a velocity vector.
- **Icosian game**: A puzzle based on finding a Hamiltonian path (a path visiting each vertex exactly once) on a dodecahedron.
- **Optime**: An exceptionally high examination grade at Trinity College Dublin.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent of Hamilton's childhood linguistic abilities is disputed by some historians, who claim he had only a basic understanding of the many languages he studied.
- The literary theory that Lewis Carroll satirized quaternions in *Alice in Wonderland* has been challenged by evidence presented in 2022, which counters the suggestion based on misunderstandings of quaternions and their history.
