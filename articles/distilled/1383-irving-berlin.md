# Irving Berlin

## Overview
Irving Berlin (1888–1989), born Israel Beilin in the Russian Empire, immigrated to the United States at age five to escape anti-Jewish pogroms. Rising from poverty on New York’s Lower East Side, he became the defining figure of the Great American Songbook, writing an estimated 1,500 songs over a 60-year career. Uniquely, Berlin could not read sheet music and played piano only in F-sharp, using a transposing keyboard to change keys. His work—spanning ragtime, romantic ballads, Broadway scores, and Hollywood film musicals—was characterized by simple, vernacular lyrics aimed at the "heart of the average American." He composed enduring anthems including "God Bless America," "White Christmas," and "There's No Business Like Show Business." George Gershwin called him "the greatest songwriter that has ever lived"; Jerome Kern concluded, "Irving Berlin has no place in American music—he is American music."

## Body

### Early Life and Immigration
Berlin was born in Tyumen, Siberia, where his father, Moses, an itinerant cantor, had taken the family. The Beilins fled the shtetl of Tolochin (present-day Belarus) to escape brutal pogroms. Berlin’s only childhood memory of Russia was watching his house burn down. In 1893, the family sailed from Antwerp on the SS *Rhynland*, arriving at Ellis Island on September 14. After naturalization, the surname became "Baline." They settled in a Cherry Street tenement on the Lower East Side. Moses died when Berlin was 13, forcing the children to work: Berlin sold newspapers, his sisters wrapped cigars, and his mother worked as a midwife. Exposed to saloon music while hawking papers, Berlin began singing for coins, eventually becoming a singing waiter and song plugger in Chinatown cafes.

### Tin Pan Alley Breakthrough
At 18, Berlin worked as a singing waiter at the Pelham Cafe, teaching himself piano after hours. He published "Marie from Sunny Italy" (1907) with pianist Mike Nicholson. In 1909, he joined the Ted Snyder Company as a staff lyricist, quickly becoming a top Tin Pan Alley writer. A publisher’s error—accepting a lyric as a complete song—forced Berlin to compose a melody on the spot, launching his dual career as composer-lyricist. His 1911 breakthrough, "Alexander's Ragtime Band," revived the ragtime craze initiated by Scott Joplin. Though initially dropped from a Broadway revue as an instrumental, adding lyrics made it "the musical sensation of the decade" per *Variety*. George Gershwin hailed it as "the first real American musical work." The song sparked an international dance craze, epitomized by Vernon and Irene Castle, and drove Berlin’s first complete stage score, *Watch Your Step* (1914), called the "first syncopated musical."

### Transition to Ballads and Wartime Service
Personal tragedy shaped Berlin’s shift from ragtime to romantic ballads. His first wife, Dorothy Goetz, died of typhoid fever in 1912, six months after their marriage. His grief produced his first ballad, "When I Lost You," a million-seller. He followed with hits like "I Love a Piano" (1915) and "A Pretty Girl is Like a Melody" (1919), which became the theme for Ziegfeld’s Follies. During World War I, Berlin wrote patriotic songs ("For Your Country and My Country") and was drafted in 1917. Stationed at Camp Upton, he wrote the all-soldier revue *Yip Yip Yaphank*, raising $150,000 for a camp service center. He wrote "God Bless America" for the show but cut it, filing it away for 20 years.

### Broadway and the Music Box Theatre
Post-war, Berlin partnered with Sam Harris to build the Music Box Theatre (1921), the only Broadway house constructed for a songwriter’s works. He produced annual *Music Box Revues* (1921–1925) and *As Thousands Cheer* (1933), a newspaper-themed revue yielding "Easter Parade," "Heat Wave," and "Supper Time"—a lynching protest song sung by Ethel Waters. He wrote book shows including *The Cocoanuts* (1929, starring the Marx Brothers), *Face the Music* (1932), and *Louisiana Purchase* (1940, satirizing Huey Long). His 1920s–30s catalog defined the era: "What'll I Do" (1924), "Always" (1925, written for Ellin Mackay), "Blue Skies" (1926), "Puttin' On the Ritz" (1928), and "I've Got My Love to Keep Me Warm" (1937).

### Hollywood and "White Christmas"
Berlin scored 15 original Hollywood films, often starring Fred Astaire, Ginger Rogers, and Bing Crosby. *Top Hat* (1935) began a series of distinctive film musicals with new scores (*Follow the Fleet*, *Carefree*). Later films blended new songs with catalog hits: *Alexander's Ragtime Band* (1938), *Holiday Inn* (1942), *Blue Skies* (1946), *Easter Parade* (1948). *Holiday Inn* introduced "White Christmas" (1942), sung by Bing Crosby. Released shortly after Pearl Harbor, it voiced GIs’ homesickness and became the best-selling single ever (50+ million copies). Berlin won the Academy Award for Best Original Song; the awkwardness of presenting the Oscar to himself prompted a rule change. "White Christmas" remained the last Berlin song to hit No. 1 on release.

### World War II: *This Is the Army* and *Annie Get Your Gun*
After Pearl Harbor, Berlin wrote bond-drive songs ("Any Bonds Today?") and donated all royalties to government agencies. His major contribution was *This Is the Army* (1942), a 300-man soldier revue touring global battlefronts for three and a half years. Berlin took no salary, donating profits ($10+ million) to Army Emergency Relief. The 1943 film adaptation co-starred Ronald Reagan. Exhausted by the tour, Berlin initially declined *Annie Get Your Gun* (1946) after Jerome Kern’s sudden death, claiming ignorance of "hillbilly music." Persuaded by Rodgers and Hammerstein, he wrote the score in a creative burst, producing his biggest stage hit (1,147 performances). "There's No Business Like Show Business" became Ethel Merman’s trademark; "Anything You Can Do" was written overnight. Alec Wilder called the score’s perfection "a profound shock" compared to earlier works.

### Later Career and Retirement
*Miss Liberty* (1949) disappointed, but *Call Me Madam* (1950), starring Merman as a Perle Mesta-inspired hostess, won the Tony for Best Score. Two attempts at a musical about Addison Mizner—*The Last Resorts* (1952, incomplete) and *Wise Guy* (1956, unproduced)—failed. A brief retirement ended with *Mr. President* (1962), attended by JFK but unsuccessful. Berlin officially retired at 74, writing only "An Old-Fashioned Wedding" for the 1966 *Annie Get Your Gun* revival. He managed his publishing company until death, rarely appearing publicly after the late 1960s.

### Songwriting Methods and Musical Style
Berlin wrote a complete song daily, usually after dinner until 4–5 AM, believing craft outweighed inspiration. He never learned to read music, playing solely in F-sharp on transposing pianos and dictating songs to a "musical secretary." Victor Herbert advised him against formal theory, fearing it would "cramp your style." Berlin joined ASCAP as a charter member in 1914. He prioritized lyrics for hits but melody for longevity. Critics note his songs flow from "everyday speech" (Stephen Holden), avoiding the "hothouse" artifice of operetta-influenced peers. He helped birth jazz idioms; Gershwin credited Berlin with proving ragtime/jazz was "the only musical idiom... that could aptly express America." Unlike Yale-educated Cole Porter, Berlin’s vernacular simplicity masked sophisticated craft.

### Personal Life
Berlin married Dorothy Goetz in 1912; her death months later devastated him. In the 1920s, he courted Ellin Mackay, a Catholic heiress whose father, Clarence Mackay, opposed the match. They eloped in 1925 after she returned from Europe; the wedding made the *New York Times* front page. Mackay disowned her for nearly three years, reconciling after the 1928 death of their infant son, Irving Jr. Berlin gave Ellin the rights to "Always" as a wedding gift, ensuring her income. Their 63-year marriage produced three daughters: Mary Ellin Barrett, Linda Louise Emmet, and Elizabeth Irving Peters. Berlin remained connected to his Lower East Side roots, visiting old neighborhoods on foot. He was a Freemason, Elk, civil rights advocate (honored by National Conference of Christians and Jews, 1944), and agnostic. *This Is the Army* featured the first integrated U.S. Army unit. J. Edgar Hoover investigated him for years. He voted for both parties, supported Eisenhower ("I Like Ike"), and refused tax shelters: "I want to pay taxes. I love this country."

### Legacy and Legal Influence
Berlin died at 101 on September 22, 1989. Broadway dimmed its lights; President George H.W. Bush called him "a legendary man whose words and music will help define the history of our nation." His catalog includes 25 chart-toppers, 8 Oscar nominations, and holiday perennials ("White Christmas," "Easter Parade," "Happy Holiday"). "God Bless America" became a post-9/11 anthem; its royalties ($10+ million) fund the Boy/Girl Scouts. The 1964 *Irving Berlin et al. v. E.C. Publications* (Mad Magazine) case set precedent protecting parody rights; Judge Irving Kaufman ruled Berlin could not "claim a property interest in iambic pentameter." Douglas Moore ranked him with Stephen Foster and Walt Whitman as a "great American minstrel" who "caught and immortalized... what we say, what we think about, and what we believe." Jerome Kern’s epitaph: "Irving Berlin has no place in American music—he is American music."

## Terms
- **Great American Songbook**: The canon of the most important and influential American popular songs and jazz standards from the early 20th century, largely shaped by Berlin’s output.
- **Tin Pan Alley**: The collection of New York City music publishers and songwriters (c. 1885–1930s) who dominated American popular music; Berlin was its leading figure.
- **Transposing piano**: A keyboard with a lever mechanism that mechanically shifts the action to play in a different key while the pianist fingers the same notes; Berlin owned three to compensate for playing only in F-sharp.
- **Song plugger**: A pianist/singer employed by music publishers to perform new songs in shops, cafes, and theaters to boost sheet music sales; Berlin’s early profession.
- **Ragtime**: A syncopated musical style (peaking 1895–1919) that preceded jazz; Berlin’s "Alexander's Ragtime Band" revived its popularity, though the song is technically a march.
- **Music Box Theatre**: Broadway house at 239 West 45th St., built 1921 by Berlin and Sam Harris specifically to showcase Berlin’s revues; he retained an ownership interest for life.
- **All-soldier revue**: A theatrical production written, performed, and staged entirely by military personnel; *Yip Yip Yaphank* (WWI) and *This Is the Army* (WWII) were Berlin’s models.
- **Great American Minstrel**: Term used by composer Douglas Moore to classify Berlin with Stephen Foster and Walt Whitman as a chronicler of the national vernacular and psyche.
- **ASCAP (American Society of Composers, Authors and Publishers)**: Performance-rights organization founded 1914; Berlin was a charter member, advocated by Victor Herbert.
- **Vernacular (in songwriting)**: Everyday, colloquial language; Berlin’s stated aim was to write in the "American vernacular" to reach the "heart of the average American."

## Debates and open questions
- **Birthplace uncertainty**: Berlin learned as an adult he was likely born in Tyumen, Siberia, not Tolochin, Belarus, where his family originated; records are inconclusive.
- **"Alexander's Ragtime Band" authorship disputes**: Contemporary claims suggested Berlin plagiarized the melody from a Black composer or Scott Joplin; biographers generally dismiss this, noting the song’s structure as a march quoting "Swanee River" and a bugle call.
- **Musical literacy vs. genius**: Scholars debate whether Berlin’s inability to read music or play in keys other than F-sharp limited his harmonic sophistication or forced a melodic directness that became his strength.
- **"God Bless America" as anthem vs. prayer**: Critics have debated whether the song functions as a secular national hymn or a religious invocation; Berlin called it a personal expression of immigrant gratitude.
- **Cultural appropriation in early "coon songs" and ragtime**: Berlin’s early catalog includes dialect songs and ragtime derived from African American traditions; historians assess his role in popularizing Black musical forms for white audiences versus exploitation.
- **Mad Magazine parody ruling scope**: The 1964 appeals decision protecting parody of meter/structure remains a cited precedent in copyright fair use law; its boundaries for musical parody are still litigated.
- **Political conservatism in later years**: Berlin’s shift toward conservative views (supporting Eisenhower, opposing tax shelters, FBI scrutiny) contrasts with his civil rights advocacy; biographers differ on whether this reflects genuine patriotism or ideological rigidity.
