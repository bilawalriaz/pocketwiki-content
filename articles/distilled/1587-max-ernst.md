# Max Ernst

## Overview
Max Ernst (1891–1976) was a German-born painter, sculptor, printmaker, and poet who became a primary pioneer of the Dada movement and Surrealism in Europe. Lacking formal artistic training, he developed revolutionary automatic techniques—most notably **frottage** (pencil rubbings of textured surfaces) and **grattage** (scraping paint over objects placed under canvas)—to bypass conscious control and access the unconscious. His service in World War I shattered his worldview, prompting a lifelong rebellion against rationalism and aesthetic convention. Forced to flee Nazi-occupied France with the aid of Peggy Guggenheim, he later influenced the emergence of Abstract Expressionism in New York before returning to France, where he achieved major institutional recognition.

## Body

### Early Life and Expressionist Roots
Ernst was born the third of nine children in Brühl. His father, Philipp, a teacher of the deaf and amateur painter, instilled both a respect for nature and a rebellious attitude toward authority. In 1909, while studying philosophy, psychiatry, and art history at the University of Bonn, Ernst visited psychiatric asylums and became fascinated by the art of mentally ill patients—a formative encounter with "outsider" creativity. He began painting that year, producing garden sketches and portraits. By 1911, he had joined August Macke’s *Die Rheinischen Expressionisten* group. The 1912 *Sonderbund* exhibition in Cologne exposed him to Picasso, Van Gogh, and Gauguin, prompting an ironic style that juxtaposed grotesque elements with Cubist and Expressionist motifs. His artistic trajectory was violently interrupted by World War I; drafted in 1914, he served on both fronts. The trauma defined his mythology: he later wrote that "Max Ernst died" on August 1, 1914, and was "resurrected" on November 11, 1918. The war killed many of his Expressionist peers, including Macke and Franz Marc.

### Dada, Surrealism, and Technical Innovation
Demobilized in 1918, Ernst married Luise Straus and founded the Cologne Dada group in 1919 with Johannes Theodor Baargeld, publishing magazines and organizing exhibitions. Inspired by Giorgio de Chirico’s metaphysical paintings and mundane print sources (catalogues, teaching manuals), he pioneered **collage** as a major artistic language, creating irrational juxtapositions. In 1921, he met Paul Éluard, who became a lifelong advocate, and André Breton, the leader of Surrealism. Unable to obtain papers, Ernst entered France illegally in 1922, joining the Surrealist nucleus in a complex domestic arrangement with Éluard and his wife Gala in Saint-Brice.

In 1925, seeking to automate the creative process, Ernst invented **frottage**: placing paper over textured surfaces (wood grain, leaves) and rubbing with pencil to generate chance images. He quickly adapted this to oil painting as **grattage**, scraping wet pigment over objects (wire, glass, string) placed beneath the canvas to reveal imprints, used notably in *Forest and Dove*. He also explored **decalcomania** (pressing paint between two surfaces). These techniques allowed him to "hallucinate" landscapes and creatures rather than illustrate them. His personal iconography centered on birds; his alter ego **Loplop** (a bird-superior) appeared frequently, which Ernst traced to a childhood trauma where his pet bird died moments before his sister’s birth, fusing the two events. His 1926 painting *The Virgin Chastises the Infant Jesus before Three Witnesses* caused a scandal. He married Marie-Berthe Aurenche in 1927 and appeared in Luis Buñuel’s film *L'Âge d'Or* (1930). By 1934 he had taken up sculpture, working alongside Alberto Giacometti.

### War, Exile, and American Influence
With the outbreak of WWII in 1939, Ernst—still a German citizen—was interned at Camp des Milles as an "undesirable foreigner." Released through the intercession of Paul Éluard and journalist Varian Fry, he was arrested again by the Gestapo after the German occupation of France but escaped to the United States in 1941 with Fry and Peggy Guggenheim. In New York, alongside émigrés like Marcel Duchamp and Marc Chagall, Ernst’s experimental methods and automatic techniques directly influenced the nascent **Abstract Expressionism** movement. He married Guggenheim in 1942 (divorced 1946). In 1946, he married American Surrealist painter Dorothea Tanning in a double ceremony with Man Ray. They moved to Sedona, Arizona, where the high desert landscape resonated with his earlier imagery. There, he built a cottage by hand, hosted European intellectuals, wrote *Beyond Painting*, and cast the sculpture *Capricorn*. The book’s publicity brought him financial success from the 1950s onward.

### Late Career and Institutional Recognition
From the 1950s, Ernst lived primarily in France. In 1954, he won the Grand Prize for Painting at the Venice Biennale, cementing his international stature. Major retrospectives followed at the Museum of Modern Art (New York, 1961), Tate Gallery (London, 1962), and Moderna Museet (Stockholm, 1969). A massive touring retrospective (1970–72) drew from the Menil Collection in Houston, which holds over 100 works. He continued producing graphic novels, illustrated books (including editions of Lewis Carroll), and sculptures until his death in Paris in 1976 at age 84.

## Terms
- ****Frottage**** — A surrealist automatic technique invented by Ernst in 1925: placing paper over a textured surface (wood, leaf, fabric) and rubbing with pencil or crayon to transfer the texture, generating chance imagery for further development.
- ****Grattage**** — An oil-painting analogue of frottage, invented c. 1925: objects are placed under a primed canvas; wet paint is scraped (trowelled/raked) across the surface to reveal the objects' imprints, creating unexpected forms.
- ****Decalcomania**** — A transfer technique explored by Ernst (and other Surrealists): wet paint is pressed between two surfaces (canvas/glass, paper/paper); when separated, the suction creates organic, branching, or geological patterns.
- ****Collage**** — The assembly of disparate printed or found materials (engravings, catalogues, maps) into new compositions; Ernst used it from 1919 to create irrational, dream-like narratives, notably in his "collage novels."
- ****Loplop**** — Ernst’s recurring avian alter ego ("Superior of the Birds"), appearing in paintings and collages (e.g., *Loplop Presents André Breton*); Ernst linked it to a childhood fusion of his pet bird’s death and his sister’s birth.
- ****Dada**** — An anti-art, anti-war movement (c. 1916–1924) embracing nonsense, chance, and rejection of bourgeois aesthetics; Ernst founded the Cologne branch in 1919.
- ****Surrealism**** — A literary and artistic movement (launched 1924 by André Breton) seeking to resolve dream and reality into a "surreality" via automatism, chance, and the unconscious; Ernst was a core visual practitioner.
- ****Abstract Expressionism**** — Post-WWII American painting movement (New York School) emphasizing gesture, scale, and unconscious expression; Ernst’s exile presence and automatic techniques were a catalyst for its development.
- ****Camp des Milles**** — Internment camp near Aix-en-Provence where Ernst was held in 1939 as an "undesirable foreigner" (enemy alien) before his release and eventual escape from Vichy France.
- ****Collage Novel**** — A book-length sequence of collages functioning as a visual narrative without text; Ernst’s major examples include *La femme 100 têtes* (1929), *Rêve d'une petite fille...* (1930), and *Une Semaine de Bonté* (1934).

## Debates and open questions
- **Origin of Loplop**: Ernst offered a specific autobiographical explanation (bird’s death/sister’s birth), but scholars debate whether this is a genuine primal scene or a constructed myth consistent with Surrealist self-fashioning.
- **Political engagement**: Most of Ernst’s work is non-political; *Fireside Angel* (1937), subtitled *The Triumph of Surrealism*, is a rare explicit response to fascism. Critics debate whether his focus on the unconscious constituted a retreat from or a deeper form of political resistance.
- **Authorship in automatic techniques**: Since frottage and grattage rely on chance and material agency, critics question the balance of authorship: does the artist "find" the image or "make" it, and how does this redefine artistic intention?
- **Influence on Abstract Expressionism**: While Ernst’s presence in New York (1941–1953) is documented as influential, the precise channels of transmission to artists like Jackson Pollock (who saw Ernst’s work at Peggy Guggenheim’s *Art of This Century* gallery) remain a subject of art-historical tracing.
- **Market and legacy**: The 1954 Venice Biennale Grand Prize sparked debate about whether institutional acceptance domesticated a radically anti-institutional artist, or validated Surrealism’s entry into the canon.
