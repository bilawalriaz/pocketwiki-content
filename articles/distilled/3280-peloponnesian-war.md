# Peloponnesian War

## Overview
The Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) was a devastating conflict between the Athenian-led Delian League and the Spartan-led Peloponnesian League for control of Ancient Greece. It ended Athenian imperial dominance and established a brief period of Spartan hegemony, fundamentally altering Greek society and politics. The war marked a shift from limited, formalized warfare to total war between competing political ideologies, contributing to the end of Greece's golden age.

## Timeline
- **431 BC** — War begins with a Theban attack on Plataea; Spartan invasion of Attica.
- **425 BC** — Athenian victory at Sphacteria, capturing Spartan hoplites.
- **424 BC** — Athenian defeat by Thebes at Delium.
- **421 BC** — Peace of Nicias signed, ending the first phase.
- **418 BC** — Spartan victory at Mantinea restores control over the Peloponnese.
- **415–413 BC** — Sicilian Expedition ends in catastrophic Athenian defeat.
- **413 BC** — Sparta, allied with Persia, breaks the peace and fortifies Decelea.
- **411 BC** — Athenian oligarchic coup; democracy later restored.
- **406 BC** — Athenian victory at Arginusae, followed by execution of their generals.
- **405 BC** — Decisive Spartan victory at Aegospotami destroys the Athenian fleet.
- **404 BC** — Athens surrenders; its walls are torn down and the Delian League dissolved.

## Body

### Prelude and Causes
The war's roots lay in the growth of Athenian power after the Greco-Persian Wars. Athens transformed the Delian League into an empire, using tribute to fund its navy and public works, which alarmed Sparta. Tensions escalated through a series of conflicts in the mid-5th century BC, culminating in the Thirty Years' Peace (446/5 BC). The peace broke down due to disputes involving Corinth, Corcyra, and Potidaea, and Athens's Megarian decree. A final debate in Sparta led to a declaration that Athens had broken the peace.

### The Archidamian War (431–421 BC)
The first phase was named for Spartan king Archidamus II. Sparta's strategy was to invade Attica, while Athens, under Pericles, relied on its navy and the protection of the Long Walls. A devastating plague in Athens (430 BC) killed over 30,000 people, including Pericles. After his death, Athens adopted a more aggressive strategy. Key events included the Athenian victory at Sphacteria (425 BC) and the capture of Amphipolis by Sparta (417 BC). The deaths of the war hawks Cleon and Brasidas allowed for the Peace of Nicias in 421 BC.

### The Peace of Nicias and the Sicilian Expedition
The peace was unstable, with constant skirmishes. Argos, supported by Athens, formed a democratic coalition in the Peloponnese, which Sparta defeated at Mantinea (418 BC). In 415 BC, Athens launched the Sicilian Expedition to aid an ally against Syracuse. The expedition, led initially by Alcibiades and then Nicias, was a disaster. Syracuse, with Spartan help, destroyed the Athenian force by 413 BC, crippling Athens's military and financial power.

### The Decelean War and Athenian Defeat
After Sicily, Sparta, now financed by the Persian Empire, fortified Decelea near Athens, disrupting its silver mines and forcing a costly sea-based supply line. Athens recovered temporarily under Alcibiades's leadership, winning battles like Cyzicus (410 BC). However, Persian support shifted decisively to Sparta under Cyrus the Younger. The Spartan general Lysander destroyed the Athenian fleet at Aegospotami (405 BC). Starving and besieged, Athens surrendered in 404 BC.

### Aftermath and Significance
Sparta dismantled the Athenian Empire and installed the oligarchic Thirty Tyrants in Athens, who were overthrown in 403 BC. Spartan hegemony was short-lived, challenged by the Corinthian War (395–387 BC) and ended by Thebes at the Battle of Leuctra (371 BC). The war's destruction and divisions weakened the Greek city-states, paving the way for Macedonian domination under Philip II and Alexander the Great.

## Terms
- **Delian League:** An alliance of Greek city-states led by Athens, originally formed to fight Persia, which evolved into an Athenian empire.
- **Peloponnesian League:** An alliance of Greek city-states led by Sparta.
- **Hoplite:** A heavily armed foot soldier, the core of ancient Greek armies.
- **Trireme:** A fast warship with three banks of oars, dominant in the Mediterranean.
- **Long Walls:** Fortified walls connecting Athens to its port, Piraeus, ensuring sea access during sieges.
- **Helots:** A subjugated population in Sparta who worked the land and were a constant security concern.
- **Strategos:** An Athenian general or military commander.
- **Ecclesia:** The principal assembly of citizens in an Athenian democracy.
- **Thirty Tyrants:** A pro-Spartan oligarchy installed in Athens after its defeat in 404 BC.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Responsibility for the War:** Ancient sources like Thucydides present a complex causation, but modern historians debate whether Athens's imperial aggression or Sparta's fear of its rising power was the primary cause.
- **Thucydides' Account:** While considered reliable, his narrative ends in 411 BC, leaving the war's conclusion dependent on later, sometimes biased sources like Xenophon.
- **The Role of Persia:** The extent and timing of Persian intervention, and whether it was decisive or merely accelerated an inevitable Spartan victory, is a subject of analysis.
