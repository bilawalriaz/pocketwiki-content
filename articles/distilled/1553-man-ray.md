# Man Ray

## Overview
Man Ray (1890–1976), born Emmanuel Radnitzky in Philadelphia to Russian Jewish immigrants, was an American-born, French-naturalized visual artist who spent most of his career in Paris. A significant contributor to Dada and Surrealism—though his ties remained informal—he worked across painting, photography, film, and assemblage. He considered himself a painter above all, yet revolutionized photography through the cameraless photogram (which he named the "rayograph"), the rediscovery of solarization with Lee Miller, and pioneering fashion and portrait photography. His work frequently referenced his family's tailoring background through motifs like mannequins, irons, and fabric. Forced to Hollywood by World War II, he returned to Paris in 1951, where he spent his final decades recreating earlier works and managing his legacy.

## Body

### Background and Early Life
Man Ray was the eldest child of Melach "Max" Radnitzky, a tailor, and Manya "Minnie" Radnitzky. The family moved to Williamsburg, Brooklyn, in 1897. In 1912, reacting to prevalent antisemitism, the family changed their surname to Ray; Emmanuel adopted "Man Ray" as his single name. His father ran a tailoring business from the home, enlisting the children to help, while his mother designed clothes and invented patchwork items. Though Man Ray wished to distance himself from this background, tailoring left an enduring mark: mannequins, flat irons, sewing machines, needles, and fabric swatches appear throughout his oeuvre, and art historians note similarities between his collage/painting techniques and tailoring methods. Curator Mason Klein has suggested he may have been "the first Jewish avant-garde artist."

### First Artistic Endeavors (New York, 1904–1921)
After graduating Boys' High School (1904–1909), where he learned drafting, Man Ray declined an architecture scholarship to pursue art. He worked as a commercial artist and technical illustrator while painting in 19th-century styles, though he avidly followed European modernists at Alfred Stieglitz's 291 gallery and the Ashcan School. Sporadic classes at the National Academy of Design and Art Students League proved unhelpful. Enrollment at the anarchist-run Ferrer Centre (autumn 1912) sparked intense development; he exhibited in their 1912–13 show and published his first poem, *Travail*, in *The Modern School* (1913). He also contributed cover art to Emma Goldman's *Mother Earth* (1914).

The 1913 Armory Show introduced him to European avant-garde practices; his paintings soon displayed Cubist facets. Befriending Marcel Duchamp, who sought to depict movement in static images, led to works like *The Rope Dancer Accompanies Herself with Her Shadows* (1916), showing repetitive positions of a dancer's skirts. In 1915, at the Grantwood art colony, he held his first solo show. His first proto-Dada object, *Self-Portrait* (assemblage), appeared in 1916. He produced his first significant photographs in 1918, initially to document his artwork. Abandoning conventional painting for Dada, he published two one-issue periodicals (*The Ridgefield Gazook*, 1915; *TNT*, 1919) and developed mechanical/photographic image-making methods, including spray-gun drawings, airbrush on glass (*Aerograph*, 1919), and readymades like *The Gift* (1921, a flatiron with tacks) and *Enigma of Isidore Ducasse* (a sewing machine wrapped in cloth). In 1920, he helped Duchamp build *Rotary Glass Plates* (kinetic art) and co-founded the Société Anonyme, the first U.S. museum of modern art (collection donated to Yale, 1941). Concluding "Dada cannot live in New York. All New York is dada," he departed for Paris.

### Paris (1921–1940)
Arriving in July 1921, Man Ray settled in Montparnasse. His accidental rediscovery of the cameraless photogram—dubbed "rayographs"—was hailed by Tristan Tzara as "pure Dada creations." He soon met Alice Prin ("Kiki de Montparnasse"), his companion for most of the 1920s and subject of iconic images like *Violon d'Ingres* (1924), where violin f-holes are superimposed on her back, referencing Ingres. She starred in his avant-garde films *Le Retour à la raison* (1923) and *L'Étoile de mer* (1928). In 1929, he began an affair with Surrealist photographer Lee Miller, who became his assistant; together they reinvented solarization. Miller left in 1932. From late 1934 to August 1940, he was with French model Ady Fidelin, who appeared in many photographs but was largely written out of his history until 2022.

Man Ray became the preeminent portrait photographer of the Paris avant-garde. Figures including Tzara, Picasso, Joyce, Stein, Cocteau, Dalí, Guggenheim, Oppenheim, and Artaud posed for him. His 1927 portraits of Maharaja Yashwant Rao Holkar II and his wife reflect his international fame. His photography of African art in the collections of Paul Guillaume and Charles Ratton produced iconic images like *Noire et blanche*; scholar Wendy A. Grossman argues "no one was more influential in translating the vogue for African art into a Modernist photographic aesthetic than Man Ray."

He was represented in the first Surrealist exhibition (Galerie Pierre, 1925) alongside Arp, de Chirico, Ernst, Masson, Miró, and Picasso. Key works included *Object to Be Destroyed* (a metronome with an eye) and *Violon d'Ingres*. He directed influential *Cinéma Pur* shorts: *Le Retour à la Raison* (1923, first "cine-rayograph"), *Emak-Bakia* (1926), *L'Étoile de Mer* (1928), and *Les Mystères du Château de Dé* (1929). He assisted Duchamp on *Anemic Cinema* (1926), operated camera for Léger's *Ballet Mécanique* (1924), and appeared playing chess with Duchamp in René Clair's *Entr'acte* (1924). Duchamp, Man Ray, and Francis Picabia formed a core trio of experimental collaborators.

### Hollywood (1940–1951)
World War II forced his return to the U.S. In Los Angeles, he focused on painting, residing at the Chateau des Fleurs and Villa Elaine Apartments. Days after arriving, he met Juliet Browner, a dancer trained with Martha Graham and experienced artists' model of Romanian-Jewish lineage. They married in 1946 in a double wedding with Max Ernst and Dorothea Tanning. They befriended George Hodel (later a Black Dahlia suspect) and his wife Dorothy Harvey. In 1948, a solo show at Copley Galleries, Beverly Hills, featured his new *Shakespearean Equations* canvases.

### Later Life (1951–1976)
Man Ray returned to Paris in 1951, settling with Juliet at 2 bis rue Férou near the Jardin du Luxembourg. In his final quarter-century, he recreated iconic earlier works in new forms and directed limited-edition replicas of objects, working with Marcel Zerbib and later Arturo Schwarz. He published *Self-Portrait* in 1963. He continued producing paintings, photographs, collages, and objects until his death from a lung infection on November 18, 1976. His epitaph reads "Unconcerned, but not indifferent." Juliet, interred beside him in 1991, bears the epitaph "Together again." She organized a trust for his work, donating extensively to museums; plans to restore his studio as a public museum proved too costly, and most contents were stored at the Centre Pompidou.

### Innovations
Man Ray pioneered several technical innovations: the "rayograph" (cameraless photogram) for Surrealist imagery; solarization (rediscovered with Lee Miller, creating partial tonal reversal); the first "cine-rayograph" (*Le Retour à la raison*, 1923), a motion picture made without a camera; and the first light painting (*Space Writing (Self-Portrait)*, 1935), predating Picasso's 1949 light paintings by fourteen years.

### Accolades and Art Market
In 1974, he received the Royal Photographic Society's Progress Medal and Honorary Fellowship. *ARTnews* (1999) named him one of the 25 most influential 20th-century artists, citing his groundbreaking photography and explorations across film, painting, sculpture, collage, assemblage, and prototypes of performance and conceptual art. His *Le Violon d'Ingres* (1924) sold for $12.4 million at Christie's New York (May 14, 2022), setting the world auction record for a photograph. *Noire et Blanche* (1926) sold for €2,688,750 at Christie's Paris (November 9, 2017). Only two other works have commanded more: *Promenade* (1916, $5,877,000, Sotheby's 2013) and *Catherine Barometer* (1920, $3,252,500, Christie's 2017).

### Legacy
*Self-Portrait* was republished in 1999. His photographs have appeared on album covers for the band Cigarettes After Sex (*Anatomies*, 2012; *Plume*, 2015) and on a U.S. Postal Service "Modern Art in America" stamp (*Noire et Blanche*, 2013). He was portrayed by Frank Bourke in the 2025 Belgian series *This Is Not a Murder Mystery*.

## Terms
- **Rayograph**: Man Ray's term for a photogram—a photographic image made without a camera by placing objects directly on photosensitive paper and exposing it to light.
- **Solarization**: A photographic phenomenon where the image recorded on a negative or print is wholly or partially reversed in tone (dark areas appear light); Man Ray and Lee Miller reinvented this as a controlled artistic technique.
- **Readymade**: A term associated with Marcel Duchamp describing ordinary manufactured objects selected and modified by the artist to become art (e.g., Man Ray's *The Gift*, a flatiron with tacks).
- **Cinéma Pur**: An avant-garde film movement emphasizing visual rhythm, abstraction, and the medium's pure plastic qualities over narrative; Man Ray's short films are key examples.
- **Cine-rayograph**: Man Ray's term for a motion picture made without a camera, achieved by placing objects directly on film stock (first realized in *Le Retour à la raison*, 1923).
- **Light painting**: A photographic technique where a light source is moved during a long exposure to "draw" in space; Man Ray's *Space Writing (Self-Portrait)* (1935) is the first known example.
- **Société Anonyme**: An itinerant collection founded in 1920 by Man Ray, Duchamp, and Katherine Dreier; it functioned as the first museum of modern art in the United States.
- **Violon d'Ingres**: Man Ray's 1924 photograph of Kiki de Montparnasse with violin f-holes painted on her back, referencing the painter Ingres's habit of playing violin as a hobby; a seminal Surrealist juxtaposition.
- **Object to Be Destroyed**: A metronome with a photograph of an eye attached to its pendulum (1923), later remade as *Indestructible Object*; epitomizes Dada/Surrealist use of found objects and threat of destruction.
- **Shakespearean Equations**: A series of paintings (late 1940s) inspired by mathematical models Man Ray photographed in the 1930s, linking mathematics, Shakespeare, and visual art.

## Debates and open questions
- **Extent of Jewish identity in his work**: Man Ray suppressed his birth name and background; scholars debate whether his work encodes Jewish identity (e.g., tailoring motifs as coded reference) or represents a deliberate erasure. Mason Klein's "first Jewish avant-garde artist" claim remains discussed.
- **Ady Fidelin's erasure**: Fidelin, Man Ray's partner from 1934–1940 who stayed in Nazi-occupied France, was excluded from his autobiography and most scholarship until recent recovery efforts (notably 2022). The reasons—his silence, her choice, or historical bias—are debated.
- **Primacy of painting vs. photography**: Man Ray insisted he was a painter, yet his fame and market value rest overwhelmingly on photography. The tension between his self-identification and historical reception shapes assessments of his legacy.
- **Dada/Surrealist affiliation**: His ties were informal; he participated in key exhibitions and publications but resisted formal membership. Scholars debate whether this independence strengthened his innovation or reflects an inability to commit to collective programs.
- **George Hodel connection**: Steve Hodel's claim that the Black Dahlia murder staging was an homage to Man Ray's Surrealism is speculative and not accepted by mainstream art history or criminology.
