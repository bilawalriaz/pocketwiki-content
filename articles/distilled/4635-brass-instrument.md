# Brass instrument

## Overview
A brass instrument is a musical instrument that produces sound by the sympathetic vibration of air in a tubular resonator, driven by the player's vibrating lips. This method of sound production, called a "lip reed," defines the family, not the material, meaning instruments can be made of brass, wood, or other materials. Brass instruments are fundamental to orchestras, concert bands, and jazz ensembles, with their pitch controlled by changing the length of the tubing via valves, slides, or other mechanisms.

## Timeline
- **c. 1795** — The trumpet transitions from a natural brass instrument to one with valves.
- **c. 1820** — The horn transitions from a natural brass instrument to one with valves.
- **1814** — Heinrich Stölzel invents an early piston valve (the Stölzel valve).
- **Mid-19th C.** — The Vienna valve is developed as an improved piston design.
- **By 1864** — A standard three-valve layout becomes almost universal.
- **Late 19th C.** — Mass manufacturing improves piston valves, making them predominant.
- **1910s-1920s** — E. A. Couturier company builds instruments with a continuous conical bore.
- **2010s** — Plastic brass instruments emerge as a cheaper, durable alternative.

## Body

### Families and Sound Production
Modern brass instruments are primarily divided into two families based on how they change pitch. **Valved brass instruments** use valves (typically three or four) to add lengths of tubing, lowering the pitch. This family includes the trumpet, horn, euphonium, and tuba. **Slide brass instruments**, like the trombone, use a slide to change the tubing length. Two other families are now largely obsolete for practical use: **natural brass instruments** (like the bugle) play only the notes of their harmonic series, and **keyed brass instruments** (like the cornett) use finger holes or keys.

The sound is produced by the player's lips vibrating against a mouthpiece, creating a "lip reed." The player's embouchure (lip tension and air flow) selects a specific harmonic from the series available for a given tubing length. Brass instruments are highly directional, projecting sound from the bell, unlike non-directional woodwinds.

### Bore Geometry
Instruments are characterized by their **bore**, the tubing between mouthpiece and bell. A **cylindrical bore** (constant diameter, e.g., trumpet, trombone) produces a brighter tone. A **conical bore** (increasing diameter, e.g., horn, tuba) produces a more mellow tone. Instruments are also classified by bore diameter relative to length: **whole-tube instruments** (e.g., tuba) can easily play the fundamental tone, while **half-tube instruments** (e.g., trumpet) find the second partial (first overtone) the lowest practical note.

### Valves and Tuning
Valves lower the pitch by adding tubing. A standard three-valve layout was established by 1864. However, the physics of adding fixed lengths of tubing creates inherent intonation problems; certain valve combinations (notably 1st+3rd and 1st+2nd+3rd) are naturally sharp. Players compensate using **triggers** or **throws** (manual slide extensions), or instruments use mechanical **compensation systems** where extra tubing is automatically engaged with certain valve combinations. Some instruments use additional valves (e.g., five or six on tubas) instead of complex compensation.

### Materials and Manufacture
Instruments are traditionally made of brass, often lacquered or plated with gold or silver to prevent corrosion. Alternatives include other copper or silver alloys, which are biostatic. Plastic instruments, emerging in the 2010s, offer a cheaper, robust alternative with a somewhat different sound. The bell is shaped through a process called **metal beating**.

### Ensembles
Brass instruments are core components of various ensembles. Orchestras typically use trumpets, horns, trombones, and a tuba. Concert bands have a larger brass section, often including euphoniums. British brass bands consist entirely of brass, mostly conical bore instruments. Jazz big bands feature cylindrical bore trumpets and trombones. Quintets (two trumpets, horn, trombone, tuba) are a common small ensemble format.

## Terms
- **Embouchure**: The use of the lips, facial muscles, tongue, and teeth to control the vibration and air flow for playing a wind instrument.
- **Harmonic series**: The sequence of notes that can be produced on a brass instrument by varying lip tension for a given length of tubing.
- **Bore**: The tubing between the mouthpiece and the bell of the instrument.
- **Cylindrical bore**: A bore with a constant diameter, producing a brighter tone.
- **Conical bore**: A bore with a constantly increasing diameter, producing a mellow tone.
- **Whole-tube instrument**: An instrument with a larger bore relative to length, allowing easy play of the fundamental tone.
- **Half-tube instrument**: An instrument with a smaller bore relative to length, where the second partial is the lowest practical note.
- **Compensation system**: A valve mechanism that automatically adds extra tubing when certain valve combinations are used to correct intonation.
- **Trigger/Throw**: A manual mechanism to extend a slide for tuning adjustment while playing.
