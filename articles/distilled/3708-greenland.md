# Greenland

## Overview
Greenland is an autonomous territory within the Kingdom of Denmark, and the world's largest island. It is a vast Arctic landmass, over 80% covered by an ice sheet, with a small, predominantly Inuit population concentrated along the ice-free coasts. Its strategic location, mineral wealth, and the accelerating melt of its ice sheet due to climate change have made it a subject of significant geopolitical and environmental interest.

## Timeline
- **c. 2500 BC** — Earliest Palaeo-Inuit cultures (Saqqaq) enter Greenland.
- **c. 986** — Norse settlers led by Erik the Red establish colonies in the southwest.
- **c. 1300** — Thule people, ancestors of modern Inuit, arrive in Greenland.
- **1500-1501** — Portuguese expeditions under Gaspar Corte-Real explore the coastline.
- **1721** — Dano-Norwegian missionary Hans Egede establishes a colony at Godthåb (Nuuk).
- **1814** — Treaty of Kiel transfers Greenland from Norwegian to Danish crown.
- **1953** — Danish constitution ends Greenland's colonial status, integrating it as a county.
- **1979** — Greenland Home Rule Act grants limited autonomy.
- **2009** — Greenland Self-Government Act is enacted, expanding self-rule and recognizing Greenlanders as a separate people.
- **2022** — Denmark and Canada resolve the Hans Island border dispute by splitting the island.
- **2025** — United States pursues a campaign to annex Greenland, triggering a crisis.

## Body

### History and Settlement
Greenland has been inhabited for at least 4,500 years by successive Palaeo-Inuit cultures. Norse settlers from Iceland and Norway colonized the southwest from 986, establishing communities that lasted about 400 years before disappearing in the 15th century, possibly due to the Little Ice Age, economic decline, and conflict. The Thule culture, ancestors of the modern Inuit, arrived around 1300. European interest resumed in the 16th century, leading to Dano-Norwegian recolonization in the 18th century. Greenland became a Danish colony, with its trade monopolized and society isolated until World War II.

### Political Status and Self-Governance
Greenland's political relationship with Denmark has evolved from colony to integrated county to autonomous territory. The 1953 constitution ended colonial status. A 1979 referendum established Home Rule, and a 2008 referendum led to the Self-Government Act of 2009. This act recognized the Greenlandic people's right to self-determination and transferred responsibility for most domestic affairs, including natural resources, to the local government, Naalakkersuisut. Denmark retains control of citizenship, monetary policy, security, and foreign affairs. Greenland is not part of the EU but has an associated relationship.

### Geography and Climate
Greenland is the world's largest non-continental island, with a total area of 2,166,086 km². The Greenland ice sheet covers 81% of its land. The population of 56,583 (2022) is the least dense in the world, concentrated in coastal towns, especially along the southwest. The climate is Arctic, but the ice sheet is warming rapidly. Since the 1980s, annual ice loss has exceeded gains from snowfall. The melting contributes significantly to global sea level rise and could potentially disrupt Atlantic ocean circulation. If the entire ice sheet melted, global sea levels would rise by about 7.5 meters.

### Economy and Resources
Greenland's economy is heavily dependent on Danish financial aid, which averaged over 20% of GDP from 2019-2023. The fishing industry, particularly shrimp, accounts for over 90% of exports. Despite its mineral wealth, mining has been limited due to high costs, environmental concerns, and lack of infrastructure. In 2021, Greenland banned new oil and gas exploration. Tourism is a growing sector. The public sector plays a dominant role, and the territory has a modern welfare system with free education and healthcare.

### Society and Culture
The population is 89.5% Greenlandic Inuit. Greenlandic (Kalaallisut) is the sole official language, though Danish is widely used. The culture is a blend of Inuit and Scandinavian traditions. Social issues include a very high suicide rate and historical challenges related to postwar urbanization and Danish assimilation policies, such as the compulsory contraception of Inuit women in the 1960s-70s. Greenland has extensive LGBTQ rights. Traditional arts like tupilak carving and drum dancing (qilaatersorneq) are important cultural expressions.

## Terms
*   **Naalakkersuisut:** The executive government of Greenland, headed by a premier.
*   **Inatsisartut:** The parliament of Greenland, consisting of 31 members.
*   **Bloktilskud:** The annual block grant from Denmark to Greenland, a major component of public revenue.
*   **Thule culture:** The ancestral culture of the modern Inuit, which migrated to Greenland around 1300 AD.
*   **Palaeo-Inuit:** Early pre-Inuit peoples who inhabited the Arctic, including the Saqqaq and Dorset cultures in Greenland.
*   **Kalaallisut:** The West Greenlandic dialect, which is the basis for the standard Greenlandic language.
*   **Tupilak:** A traditional Inuit carved figure, often representing a mythical or spiritual being.
*   **Qilaatersorneq:** The traditional Inuit drum dance and song, recognized by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Norse Disappearance:** The exact cause of the 15th-century Norse settlement collapse is debated, with theories ranging from climate change (Little Ice Age) and economic decline to conflict with Inuit and environmental degradation.
*   **Path to Independence:** While the Self-Government Act provides a legal framework for independence, the economic viability of an independent Greenland, given its reliance on Danish aid and fishing, is a central political question.
*   **Climate Change Impacts:** The rate and consequences of the Greenland ice sheet's melt are subjects of intense scientific study, with projections for sea level rise and potential tipping points being actively researched.
*   **Geopolitical Future:** Greenland's strategic importance has led to renewed interest from major powers, notably the United States, raising questions about its future sovereignty and international alignment.
