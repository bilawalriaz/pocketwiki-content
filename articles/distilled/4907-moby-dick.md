# Moby-Dick

## Overview
*Moby-Dick; or, The Whale* (1851) is Herman Melville's epic novel, widely considered a masterpiece of American literature. Narrated by sailor Ishmael, it chronicles the whaling ship *Pequod*'s voyage, which becomes Captain Ahab's monomaniacal quest for vengeance against the great white sperm whale, Moby Dick. Blending realistic whaling adventure with profound philosophical exploration, its initial commercial failure contrasts with its later recognition as a foundational work of the American Renaissance.

## Timeline
- **1820** — The whaleship *Essex* is sunk by a sperm whale, inspiring the novel's ending.
- **c. 1839** — The albino sperm whale Mocha Dick, a model for Moby Dick, is featured in an article by J. N. Reynolds.
- **1841-44** — Herman Melville serves as a common sailor, including on the whaler *Acushnet*, providing firsthand experience.
- **Feb 1850** — Melville begins writing *Moby-Dick*.
- **Aug 1850** — Melville meets Nathaniel Hawthorne, an encounter that deeply influences the novel's development.
- **Oct 18, 1851** — The British edition, *The Whale*, is published in London by Richard Bentley.
- **Nov 14, 1851** — The American edition, *Moby-Dick; or, The Whale*, is published in New York by Harper & Brothers.
- **1891** — Melville dies; *Moby-Dick* is out of print and largely forgotten.
- **1920s** — The "Melville Revival" begins, establishing the novel's reputation as a Great American Novel.

## Body

### Plot and Narrative
Ishmael travels to New Bedford to join a whaling voyage, sharing a room with the Polynesian harpooner Queequeg. They sign aboard the *Pequod* under the mysterious Captain Ahab, who reveals his sole purpose: to hunt Moby Dick, the whale that took his leg. The voyage follows the *Pequod* across the globe, interspersing detailed whaling accounts with philosophical digressions. The crew encounters other ships with stories related to the white whale. The climax is a three-day chase where Moby Dick destroys the *Pequod*, killing Ahab and nearly all the crew. Ishmael alone survives, rescued by the ship *Rachel*, clinging to Queequeg's coffin, crafted as a seachest.

### Structure and Style
Melville employs a complex structure, blending narrative with cetological essays, sermons, stage directions, and soliloquies. Ishmael acts as both character and reflective narrator. Chapters are organized into sequences, with a key device being nine meetings ("gams") with other ships, charting Ahab's growing obsession. The style is linguistically rich, drawing from the Bible, Shakespeare, and Milton, using a vast, inventive vocabulary to convey epic scale.

### Themes and Interpretation
The novel explores the epistemological quest for meaning in a deceptive world, symbolized by the hunt for the whale. It examines perception versus reality, famously in "The Doubloon" chapter where crew members interpret the same coin differently. Race and social harmony are central, embodied in the diverse crew and Ishmael and Queequeg's friendship, later shattered. Pip, a traumatized Black cabin boy, highlights the brutalizing effects of commodification. The novel is also read as a critique of Transcendentalist self-reliance, with Ahab's destructive monomania representing its dangerous extreme.

### Composition and Sources
Melville drew on his own whaling experience and extensive research. Key sources include the 1820 sinking of the *Essex* and the legend of Mocha Dick. He relied on cetological works, especially Thomas Beale's *Natural History of the Sperm Whale*. Composition likely involved a two-stage process: a straightforward whaling narrative transformed into a philosophical epic after Melville's encounter with Hawthorne and deep immersion in Shakespeare, evidenced by deepening symbolism and tragic structure.

### Publication and Initial Reception
The novel was first published in Britain as *The Whale* in October 1851, and in the U.S. as *Moby-Dick* in November. The British edition, censored and missing the epilogue, received mixed reviews. American reviews were more hostile, influenced by the British reception and the novel's perceived irreverence and stylistic excess. It was a commercial failure, selling about 3,200 copies in Melville's lifetime and going out of print.

### Legacy and Adaptation
*Moby-Dick* was rediscovered during the "Melville Revival" in the 1920s, cementing its classic status. Its iconic imagery and themes have inspired countless adaptations in film, art, and literature, from John Huston's 1956 film to Ralph Ellison's *Invisible Man*. It remains a central text in American literature, celebrated for its ambition, linguistic power, and enduring exploration of obsession, nature, and the human condition.

## Terms
- **Cetology**: The study of whales, dolphins, and porpoises.
- **Gam**: A social meeting between two ships at sea.
- **Monomania**: Mental illness characterized by obsession with one idea.
- **Spermaceti**: Waxy substance from sperm whale heads, used for candles and lubricants.
- **Transcendentalism**: 19th-century American movement emphasizing intuition, nature, and inherent goodness.
- **American Renaissance**: Period (c. 1830-1860) of flourishing American literary and intellectual activity.
- **Epilogue**: A concluding section commenting on the narrative (here, explaining Ishmael's survival).
- **Fast-Fish and Loose-Fish**: Whaling legal concept where ownership is determined by who has a hold of the whale.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Composition Theory**: Scholars debate whether Melville radically transformed a straightforward adventure into a philosophical epic after meeting Hawthorne, or if complexity was present from inception.
- **Interpretive Multiplicity**: The novel's meaning is elusive. E. M. Forster noted it is "full of meanings," with interpretations ranging from metaphysical parable to critique of American expansionism.
- **The Missing Epilogue**: The omission from the British edition, making Ishmael's survival impossible, is a point of scholarly interest regarding its impact on early reception.
- **Ahab's Heroism**: Critics debate whether Ahab is a tragic hero, a villainous monomaniac, or a symbol of destructive human will.
