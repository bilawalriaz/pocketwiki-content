# Belize

## Overview
Belize is a small, diverse country on the northeastern coast of Central America, bordering Mexico, Guatemala, and the Caribbean Sea. It is the only Central American nation with English as its official language and is a member of the Caribbean Community (CARICOM). Its history is marked by ancient Maya civilization, British colonial rule, and a modern struggle over territorial integrity with Guatemala, while its geography features the world's second-largest barrier reef and rich biodiversity.

## Timeline
- **c. 1500 BC – 900 AD** — Maya civilization spreads across and flourishes in the area of present-day Belize.
- **1502–1504** — Christopher Columbus sails along the Gulf of Honduras, making initial European contact.
- **1638** — English settlers begin European exploration and establish the first permanent settlement.
- **1798** — British victory over Spain in the Battle of St. George's Caye secures British control.
- **1840** — The territory becomes a British colony.
- **1862** — It is formally declared a British Crown Colony, named British Honduras.
- **1964** — British Honduras is granted self-government under a new constitution.
- **1973** — The colony is officially renamed Belize.
- **21 September 1981** — Belize achieves full independence from the United Kingdom.
- **2019** — Belizeans vote in a referendum to send the long-standing territorial dispute with Guatemala to the International Court of Justice (ICJ).

## Body

### History and Colonial Legacy
The Maya civilization was present in the region for millennia, with major political centers like Caracol and Lamanai. Spanish explorers claimed the area but failed to settle it. English pirates and loggers (the "Baymen") established settlements, leading to conflict with Spain. The British victory at the Battle of St. George's Caye (1798) was pivotal. The colony, initially reliant on logwood and mahogany extraction using enslaved African labor, became a formal British Crown Colony in 1862. Slavery was abolished in 1833, but economic and social structures kept the formerly enslaved population in a subordinate position. The 20th century saw economic crises, the growth of nationalist movements led by the People's United Party (PUP), and the achievement of self-government in 1964. Independence came in 1981, though Guatemala's claim to Belizean territory delayed full international recognition.

### Geography and Environment
Belize has a tropical climate with distinct wet and dry seasons and is highly vulnerable to hurricanes, which have historically shaped its development (e.g., the 1961 Hurricane Hattie led to the capital's relocation). Its most significant geographic feature is the Belize Barrier Reef, a UNESCO World Heritage Site and part of the larger Mesoamerican Barrier Reef System. The country has high biodiversity and extensive forest cover, though deforestation is an ongoing concern. Approximately 37% of its land territory is under some form of official protection, one of the highest rates in the Americas. The economy is small and private-enterprise based, with agriculture (sugar, bananas), tourism, and recently, crude oil production as key sectors.

### Government and Politics
Belize is a parliamentary constitutional monarchy with King Charles III as head of state, represented by a governor-general. The government is modeled on the British system, with a bicameral National Assembly. Politics have been dominated since the 1970s by two parties: the centre-left People's United Party (PUP) and the centre-right United Democratic Party (UDP). A major ongoing issue is the Guatemalan territorial claim, which involves about 53% of Belize's mainland. Both nations have agreed to submit the dispute to the International Court of Justice (ICJ) for a final ruling.

### Society and Demographics
Belize's population (397,483 in 2022) is highly diverse. The largest ethnic group is the Mestizo/Hispanic community (about 52%), followed by the Creole (Afro-Belizean) population. Other significant groups include the Maya (Yucatec, Mopan, Q'eqchi'), Garifuna, East Indians, and Mennonites. English is the official language, but Belizean Creole, Spanish, and Mayan languages are widely spoken, making the country highly multilingual. The religious landscape is predominantly Christian, with Catholicism as the largest denomination, though Protestantism is growing. Emigration, particularly to the United States, has significantly influenced demographic shifts.

## Terms
- **CARICOM**: The Caribbean Community, an international organization promoting economic integration and cooperation among its member states, of which Belize is a member.
- **Mesoamerican Biological Corridor**: A vast network of protected areas stretching from southern Mexico to Panama, crucial for biodiversity conservation, in which Belize plays a key part.
- **Belize Barrier Reef**: A 300-kilometer-long section of coral reef system, the second-largest in the world, vital for tourism and marine biodiversity.
- **People's United Party (PUP)**: The centre-left political party that dominated Belizean politics from the 1950s until 1984 and again in later periods.
- **United Democratic Party (UDP)**: The centre-right political party that has been the main rival to the PUP since the 1970s.
- **Garifuna**: An Afro-Indigenous ethnic group descended from West/Central Africans, Arawak, and Island Carib peoples, with a distinct language and culture.
- **Belizean Creole (Kriol)**: An English-based creole language widely spoken as a lingua franca, developed during the era of slavery.
- **Baymen**: The name given to the English and Scottish settlers and loggers in 18th-century Belize who fought against Spanish control.
- **International Court of Justice (ICJ)**: The principal judicial organ of the United Nations, to which Belize and Guatemala have agreed to submit their territorial dispute.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The Guatemalan Territorial Dispute**: The core unresolved issue is Guatemala's claim to approximately 53% of Belize's territory, based on an 1859 treaty. The case is now before the ICJ, with both countries submitting written arguments. The outcome will have profound implications for Belize's sovereignty and borders.
- **Indigenous Land Rights**: Despite court rulings (e.g., a 2015 Caribbean Court of Justice order) affirming customary land titles for Maya communities, progress in legally recognizing and demarcating these lands has been slow, leading to ongoing tension between the state and indigenous groups.
- **Demographic and Cultural Shifts**: The country has experienced significant demographic change since the 1980s, with the Hispanic/Mestizo population becoming the largest group due to migration from Central America and emigration of Creoles. This shift impacts the nation's cultural and linguistic landscape.
