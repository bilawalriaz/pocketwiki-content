# Goryeo

## Overview
Goryeo (918–1392) was a Korean dynasty that unified the peninsula after the Later Three Kingdoms period, merging the identities of Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla into the foundation of modern Korean identity. Its name is the origin of the English word "Korea." The dynasty is renowned for its cultural achievements, including celadon pottery and the Tripitaka Koreana, and for its resilience against foreign invasions, particularly from the Khitan Liao and Mongol Yuan empires.

## Timeline
- **918** — Wang Kŏn (Taejo) founds Goryeo, establishing the capital at Kaesong.
- **935–936** — Goryeo peacefully annexes Later Silla and conquers Later Baekje, unifying the peninsula.
- **942** — "Manbu Bridge Incident": Taejo exiles Khitan envoys, signaling hostility.
- **993–1022** — Goryeo–Khitan Wars: Goryeo resists Liao invasions, eventually establishing a defensive border.
- **1055** — Ch'oe Ch'ung establishes the School of Nine Studies, promoting private academies.
- **1107–1108** — General Yun Kwon campaigns against Jurchens, building the Nine Fortresses.
- **1170** — Military coup begins de facto rule by warrior families, notably the Ch'oe clan.
- **1231–1259** — Mongol invasions; Goryeo resists for decades before becoming a Yuan vassal.
- **1251** — Second Tripitaka Koreana woodblocks are completed.
- **1356** — King Gongmin purges pro-Mongol factions and reclaims northern territories.
- **1388** — General Yi Sŏnggye rebels, leading to the dynasty's end.
- **1392** — Yi Sŏnggye establishes the Joseon dynasty.

## Body

### Founding and Unification
Goryeo was founded in 918 by General Wang Kŏn, who overthrew the unstable ruler Kung Ye of Later Goguryeo. Unlike his predecessor, Wang Kŏn pursued a policy of magnanimity, peacefully annexing the weakened Later Silla in 935 and conquering Later Baekje in 936 with the help of its deposed founder, Kyŏn Hwŏn. This ended the Later Three Kingdoms period. Goryeo then incorporated the ruling class of the northern kingdom of Balhae, whose people shared Goguryeo origins. This influx of Balhae refugees, welcomed by Taejo, is considered by Korean historians to have achieved a "true national unification," merging the identities of the Three Kingdoms.

### Political Consolidation and Cultural Flourishing
Early Goryeo rulers worked to centralize power against the regional *hojok* (豪族) lords. King Gwangjong (r. 949–975) freed slaves held by nobles and introduced the *kwagŏ* civil service examinations to build a loyal bureaucracy. His successors, Gyeongjong and Seongjong, further centralized administration and promoted Confucianism, founding the national university Kukchagam in 992. The 11th and early 12th centuries are considered Goryeo's golden age. The capital Kaesong became a major trade hub, with merchants from Song China and the Middle East. This era saw great achievements in art, such as Goryeo celadon, and scholarship, including the first Tripitaka Koreana (completed 1087) and the development of metal movable type printing in the 13th century.

### Foreign Relations and Military Conflicts
Goryeo maintained a complex diplomatic stance, positioning itself as the center of its own "Haedong" (East of the Sea) world while engaging in nominal tributary relationships with Chinese dynasties for trade and diplomacy. It successfully repelled major invasions by the Khitan Liao dynasty (993–1022), establishing the defensive Cheolli Jangseong wall. Later, it faced conflicts with rising Jurchen tribes, leading to campaigns like the construction of the Nine Fortresses (1107). The most devastating challenge came from the Mongol invasions (1231–1259). After decades of resistance, Goryeo became a semi-autonomous vassal of the Yuan dynasty, with its kings marrying Mongol princesses. This period ended when King Gongmin (r. 1341–1374) purged Mongol influence and reclaimed northern territories.

### Late Period and Fall
The final century of Goryeo was marked by internal strife and external threats. After Gongmin's assassination in 1374, the court was unstable. Goryeo successfully defended against Red Turban rebels and Japanese pirates (*Wokou*) in the 1350s–1380s. In 1388, King U planned a campaign against the Ming dynasty. General Yi Sŏnggye, ordered to lead the army, instead turned back and seized power in a coup. He deposed the last Goryeo king and founded the Joseon dynasty in 1392.

## Terms
- **Hojok (豪族):** Regional lords who held quasi-independent power during the Later Three Kingdoms and early Goryeo periods.
- **Kwagŏ (科擧):** The civil service examination system, based on Chinese models, introduced to create a centralized bureaucracy loyal to the monarch.
- **Tripitaka Koreana:** A complete collection of Buddhist scriptures carved onto approximately 81,258 woodblocks in the 13th century, preserved at Haeinsa temple.
- **Goryeo Celadon:** High-quality pottery with distinctive jade-green glazes and intricate inlay techniques (*sanggam*), celebrated across East Asia.
- **Haedong (海東):** Meaning "East of the Sea," a term Goryeo used to refer to its own cultural and political sphere.
- **Cheolli Jangseong (千里長城):** A defensive wall built from 1033 to 1044 along the Yalu River to protect against Khitan invasions.
- **Sambyeolcho:** A rebel military unit that resisted Mongol domination after Goryeo's surrender in 1270.
- **Sanggam:** An inlay technique for celadon pottery involving black and white designs set into the clay before glazing.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The nature of Goryeo's "tributary" relationships with Chinese dynasties is debated. Historian Hans Bielenstein argues these were largely diplomatic fictions, with Goryeo maintaining effective autonomy until the Mongol conquest.
- The extent of Goryeo's control over the Nine Fortresses built against the Jurchens is uncertain; they were handed back in 1108, possibly due to the high cost of maintaining them against superior Jurchen cavalry.
- The role of the Balhae refugees in shaping Goryeo's population and identity is a subject of scholarly interest, with speculation that they may have become demographically dominant in certain regions.
