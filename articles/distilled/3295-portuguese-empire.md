# Portuguese Empire

## Overview
The Portuguese Empire (c. 1415–1999) was the first and longest-lasting European colonial empire, originating in the Age of Discovery. It established a global network of trading posts and colonies across Africa, Asia, and the Americas, profoundly shaping global trade, culture, and demographics. Its decline was a gradual process spanning centuries, culminating in the decolonization of its African territories and the return of Macau to China.

## Timeline
- **1415** — Conquest of Ceuta, marking the start of overseas expansion.
- **1488** — Bartolomeu Dias rounds the Cape of Good Hope.
- **1494** — Treaty of Tordesillas divides the non-European world with Spain.
- **1498** — Vasco da Gama reaches India, establishing the sea route to Asia.
- **1500** — Pedro Álvares Cabral reaches Brazil.
- **1580** — Iberian Union begins; Portuguese crown united with Spain.
- **1663** — End of the Iberian Union; start of the "Second Empire" focused on Brazil.
- **1822** — Brazil declares independence.
- **1890** — British Ultimatum forces Portugal to abandon the "Pink Map" plan in Africa.
- **1974** — Carnation Revolution in Portugal leads to rapid decolonization in Africa.
- **1999** — Handover of Macau to China, effectively ending the empire.

## Body

### Origins and First Empire (1415–1663)
Following the Reconquista, Portugal turned overseas, motivated by crusading ideals, glory, and trade. Sponsored by figures like Prince Henry the Navigator, explorers used the caravel to push down the African coast, establishing trading posts (*feitorias*) for gold, ivory, and slaves. The 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas with Spain divided the globe between them. Vasco da Gama's 1498 voyage to India opened the sea route to Asia, and Portugal rapidly built a network of forts from East Africa to Japan, dominating the spice trade. In the Americas, Cabral claimed Brazil in 1500, but initial efforts focused on Asia. The 1580 union with Spain dragged Portugal into conflicts with rivals like the Dutch and English, leading to the gradual loss of many Asian and African possessions.

### Second Empire and Brazilian Gold (1663–1822)
After restoring independence from Spain in 1640, Portugal's empire became increasingly centered on Brazil. The 1693 discovery of gold in Minas Gerais triggered a massive gold rush, making Brazil the colony's economic engine and funding the Portuguese state. The Marquis of Pombal implemented Enlightenment-inspired reforms in the 18th century, centralizing control and suppressing the Jesuits. Despite revolts, Brazilian loyalty held until Napoleon's invasion of Portugal in 1808 forced the royal court to flee to Rio de Janeiro. Brazil was elevated to a kingdom in 1815, but growing local desire for autonomy led to its peaceful independence in 1822 under Pedro I.

### Third Empire and Decolonization (1822–1999)
After losing Brazil, Portugal focused on expanding its African colonies into territories like Angola and Mozambique. The 1890 British Ultimatum ended its ambitions for a trans-African colony. Under the Salazar dictatorship (1932–1968), Portugal clung to its empire, renaming colonies "overseas provinces" and using forced labor. The 1961–1974 Portuguese Colonial War in Africa, coupled with the costly defense of territories like Goa (annexed by India in 1961), destabilized the regime. The 1974 Carnation Revolution overthrew the dictatorship, leading to a hasty withdrawal from Africa and the 1975 Indonesian invasion of East Timor. The final act was the 1999 handover of Macau to China.

## Terms
- **Caravel:** A small, highly maneuverable sailing ship developed by the Portuguese in the 15th century, crucial for early oceanic exploration.
- **Feitoria:** A fortified trading post or factory established by the Portuguese in foreign territories to conduct and monopolize trade.
- **Iberian Union (1580–1663):** The period when the Portuguese crown was united with the Spanish crown under the Spanish Habsburgs, making Portuguese colonies vulnerable to Spain's enemies.
- **Estado Novo:** The authoritarian, corporatist dictatorship in Portugal (1933–1974) under António de Oliveira Salazar that resisted decolonization.
- **Pluricontinentalism:** The ideology promoted by the Estado Novo that Portugal was not a colonial power but a transcontinental state with "overseas provinces."
- **Carnation Revolution:** The largely bloodless military coup in Portugal on April 25, 1974, that overthrew the Estado Novo regime and initiated decolonization.
- **Retornados:** The mass exodus of Portuguese settlers and mixed-race people from former African colonies after 1974.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact discovery of Brazil in 1500 is debated; some historians speculate the Portuguese may have known of its existence earlier, given the Treaty of Tordesillas.
- The economic impact of the empire on Portugal is analyzed as substantial, with colonial trade accounting for roughly a fifth of per-capita income during its heyday (1500–1800), though it did not prevent Portugal's relative decline within Europe.
- The motivations and effectiveness of Portugal's late-colonial policy under Salazar are debated, particularly the use of the "pluricontinentalism" ideology to justify holding onto colonies against the global trend of decolonization.
