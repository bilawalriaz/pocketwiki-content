# Firearm

## Overview
A firearm is any weapon that propels projectiles via high-pressure gas generated by chemical propellant combustion, historically black powder and, since the 1860s, smokeless powder. Small arms—firearms portable enough for one person to carry—include handguns, rifles, shotguns, and automatic weapons. Originating in 10th-century Song China as fire lances, firearms evolved through hand cannons, matchlocks, and breech-loading cartridges into modern precision instruments, fundamentally reshaping warfare, law enforcement, and civilian life worldwide.

## Timeline
- **10th century** — Fire lance invented in Song China, combining gunpowder with spear-mounted bamboo tubes
- **1288** — Oldest surviving firearm, the Heilongjiang hand cannon, dated in China
- **13th century** — Metal-barreled hand cannons replace fire lances in China
- **14th century** — Firearm technology spreads across Eurasia; Europeans adopt firearms
- **1845** — Louis-Nicolas Flobert invents first rimfire metallic cartridge
- **1860s** — Smokeless powder gradually replaces black powder
- **1908** — German "Spandau" and British Lewis gun introduce modern automatic fire in WWI
- **1934** — U.S. National Firearms Act regulates automatic weapons and SBRs
- **1968** — U.S. Gun Control Act defines "machine gun" and expands regulation
- **1986** — Hughes Act bans civilian transfer of machine guns registered after 1986

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
The first firearms emerged in 10th-century Song China as fire lances—bamboo or metal tubes mounted on spears that expelled ignited gunpowder and shrapnel. These evolved into metal-barreled hand cannons by the 13th century, featuring fitted projectiles and higher-nitrate gunpowder. The Heilongjiang hand cannon (1288) is the oldest known surviving firearm, used by Jurchen commander Li Ting to suppress a rebellion. Knowledge spread slowly: Arabs and Mamluks acquired firearms by the late 13th century, Europeans by the 14th, Koreans from China in the 14th, and the Japanese from the Portuguese in the 16th century. In Southeast Asia, true firearms arrived no earlier than 1460 via Islamic traders, with local production accelerating after the Portuguese captured Malacca in 1511.

### Propellants and Cartridges
Early firearms used black powder, a mixture of saltpeter, charcoal, and sulfur. Beginning in the 1860s, smokeless powder—primarily nitrocellulose—replaced black powder due to its cleaner combustion and higher velocity. The metallic cartridge, invented by Louis-Nicolas Flobert in 1845, unified bullet, powder, and primer in a single weatherproof brass case. Centerfire cartridges (primer at the base center) became standard for high-powered firearms, while rimfire cartridges (primer in the rim) remain common for small-caliber rounds. Full metal jacket (FMJ) bullets, with a copper alloy jacket over a lead core, were adopted militarily under the 1899 Hague Convention for reduced tissue damage and reliable feeding.

### Firing Mechanisms
Matchlock (15th century) used a burning cord to ignite priming powder. Wheellock (16th century) employed a spinning steel wheel to generate sparks, offering better weather resistance but at high cost. Flintlock (17th century) used a flint striking a frizzen to create sparks, becoming dominant for over two centuries. Percussion cap (caplock, early 19th century) replaced exposed priming powder with a self-contained explosive cap struck by a hammer, greatly improving reliability and weatherproofing. Modern firearms use primer-ignited centerfire or rimfire mechanisms, with electronic firing as an experimental alternative.

### Actions and Operation
Manual actions require user manipulation: single-shot (one shot per load), lever-action (Henry rifle, Winchester Model 1894), bolt-action (Remington Model 700), pump-action (Remington Model 870), and break-action (double-barreled shotguns). Automatic actions use cartridge energy to cycle: blowback (Walther PPK), gas-operated (M16 direct impingement, AK-47 piston), and recoil-operated (Glock). Semi-automatic firearms fire one round per trigger pull with automatic cycling; fully automatic fire continuously while the trigger is depressed. Burst mode fires a preset number of rounds (typically three) per trigger pull. Selective fire allows switching between modes.

### Classification by Type
Handguns are fired with one hand and include pistols (fixed chamber, magazine-fed), revolvers (rotating cylinder with multiple chambers), and derringers (small, often double-barreled). Long guns have barrels 10–30 inches and include rifles (rifled barrels for accuracy), shotguns (smoothbore, firing shot or slugs), and carbines (barrels under 20 inches). Military classifications distinguish light firearms (individually portable) from heavy weapons (requiring platforms). Small arms encompass all individually-served light firearms. Assault rifles (selective-fire, intermediate cartridges, detachable magazines) originated with the German StG 44 in WWII. Battle rifles use full-power cartridges. Sniper rifles prioritize precision beyond 300 meters. Submachine guns fire pistol cartridges. Personal defense weapons (PDWs) bridge handguns and carbines for close-quarters. Machine guns provide sustained automatic fire, categorized as light, medium, or heavy based on portability and crew requirements.

### Ammunition and Magazines
Early firearms were muzzle-loaded with loose powder and ball. Self-contained cartridges enabled breech-loading. Internal magazines (fixed, non-removable) were common in early 20th-century military rifles (M1 Garand, Lee-Enfield). Detachable box magazines allow rapid reloading. Belt-fed systems (canvas, then metal links) power most machine guns. Clips assist loading internal magazines but are distinct from magazines. Modern firearms typically use detachable box magazines holding 5–30 rounds.

### Legal Definitions
No universal definition exists. U.S. federal law defines firearms as weapons expelling projectiles via explosive action, excluding antique firearms (pre-1898) and air guns (mechanical compression). The National Firearms Act (1934) and Gun Control Act (1968) regulate machine guns, short-barreled rifles/shotguns, suppressors, and destructive devices. The Hughes Act (1986) banned civilian transfer of post-1986 machine guns. Other nations define firearms differently: the EU includes any portable barrelled weapon expelling projectiles via combustible propellant; the UK includes air guns; Australia excludes industrial tools and signal flares. International treaties (UN, Inter-American Convention) define firearms as portable barrelled weapons expelling projectiles via explosive action, excluding pre-1900 antiques.

### Health Hazards
Firearms cause significant mortality and morbidity. In 2017, the U.S. recorded 39,773 gun-related deaths (over 60% suicides). Global firearm homicides rose from 128,000 (1990) to 173,000 (2015), though rates declined due to population growth. Nonfatal injuries occur at roughly 3:1 ratio to deaths, with assaults accounting for over half. Noise-induced hearing loss affects frequent shooters, with gunshots reaching 140–175 dB. Proper storage and handling training reduce accidental discharges. Economic costs are substantial: estimated at $4.7 billion annually in Canada (2001) and $100 billion in the U.S. (2001).

## Terms
- **Deflagration**: Subsonic combustion of propellant producing high-pressure gas to propel projectiles
- **Caliber**: Bore diameter of a firearm, measured in millimeters or inches
- **Action**: Mechanism describing how a firearm loads, fires, and ejects cartridges
- **Rifling**: Spiral grooves in a barrel that spin-stabilize bullets for accuracy
- **Centerfire**: Cartridge with primer compound in the base center, used in high-powered firearms
- **Rimfire**: Cartridge with primer in the rim, typically for low-pressure small-caliber rounds
- **Full metal jacket (FMJ)**: Bullet with a soft core enclosed in a harder metal jacket
- **Selective fire**: Capability to switch between semi-automatic, burst, and fully automatic modes
- **Suppressor**: Device attached to a barrel that reduces noise by channeling and cooling propellant gases
- **Short-barreled rifle (SBR)**: Rifle with barrel under 16 inches, regulated under U.S. NFA

## Debates and Open Questions
The definition of "assault rifle" remains politically contested, with some restricting it to selective-fire military weapons and others applying it broadly to civilian semi-automatic rifles with cosmetic similarities. The effectiveness of gun control laws in reducing violence is debated, with studies showing mixed results across jurisdictions. The classification of certain firearms as "dangerous and unusual" under U.S. constitutional law continues to evolve through court decisions. The global proliferation of 3D-printed firearms raises new regulatory challenges. Whether civilian ownership of semi-automatic firearms enhances or reduces public safety remains a subject of ongoing research and political division. The distinction between "assault weapons" bans and their actual impact on crime rates is frequently debated in policy circles.
