# Sergei Prokofiev

## Overview

Sergei Prokofiev (1891–1953) was a Russian composer, pianist, and conductor, regarded as one of the major composers of the 20th century. He created acknowledged masterpieces across numerous genres, including seven completed operas, seven symphonies, eight ballets, five piano concertos, two violin concertos, a cello concerto, a symphony-concerto for cello and orchestra, and nine completed piano sonatas. His best-known works include the March from *The Love for Three Oranges*, *Lieutenant Kijé*, the ballet *Romeo and Juliet*, and *Peter and the Wolf*. After leaving Russia following the 1917 Revolution, he spent years in the US and Europe before returning to the Soviet Union in 1936, where he produced his greatest Soviet successes but also faced official condemnation for "formalism" in 1948.

## Body

### Childhood and education

Prokofiev was born at a rural estate in Sontsovka, then in the Yekaterinoslav Governorate of the Russian Empire. His father was an agronomist; his mother, who had devoted her life to music after losing two daughters, inspired him by practicing Chopin and Beethoven in the evenings. He wrote his first piano composition at age five, an "Indian Gallop" in the F Lydian mode (a major scale with a raised 4th scale degree), because he felt "reluctance to tackle the black notes." By seven he learned chess, a lifelong passion. At nine he composed his first opera, *The Giant*; opera remained his favorite genre.

In 1902, composer Reinhold Glière began teaching him, providing the theoretical tools that let Prokofiev experiment with dissonant harmonies and unusual time signatures. In 1904, Alexander Glazunov, impressed by the young composer's works, urged his mother to enroll him at the Saint Petersburg Conservatory. There he studied with Anatoly Lyadov, Nikolai Tcherepnin, and Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov (though only "after a fashion"), and formed a lifelong friendship with fellow student Nikolai Myaskovsky. He developed a reputation as a musical rebel, performing his own adventurous works. His father's death in 1910 ended his financial support, but he had begun making a name in the St Petersburg Evenings of Contemporary Music.

### Early career and first ballets

Prokofiev's harmonic experimentation continued with *Sarcasms* (1912), which uses polytonality (multiple keys sounding simultaneously). His Piano Concerto No. 2 caused a scandal at its 1913 premiere, with audiences exclaiming "To hell with this futuristic music!" In 1914, Diaghilev commissioned his first ballet, *Ala and Lolli*; but when Prokofiev brought the work in progress to him in Italy in 1915, Diaghilev rejected it as "non-Russian," urging Prokofiev to write music "national in character." Diaghilev then commissioned *Chout* ("The Buffoon"), based on a folk tale from Alexander Afanasyev's collection. Its 1921 Paris premiere was a huge success; Stravinsky called it "the single piece of modern music he could listen to with pleasure."

During World War I, Prokofiev studied organ to avoid conscription. He composed *The Gambler* from Dostoyevsky's novel, but its 1917 premiere was cancelled due to the February Revolution. That summer he composed his First Symphony, the *Classical*, in a style he imagined Joseph Haydn would have used if alive then, but incorporating modern elements (neoclassicism). Believing Russia "had no use for music at the moment," he obtained permission from Lunacharsky—who called him "a revolutionary in music"—and left for America in May 1918.

### Life abroad and return

In the US, Prokofiev's opera *The Love for Three Oranges* was delayed by the illness and death of Chicago Opera's music director, costing him his American solo career. He moved to Paris in 1920, where he completed his Third Piano Concerto. The opera finally premiered in Chicago in December 1921. In 1922 he moved to Ettal, Bavaria, to work on *The Fiery Angel*, based on Valery Bryusov's novel. He married Spanish singer Carolina (Lina) Codina in 1923. Around 1924 he embraced Christian Science, which he believed beneficial to his health and temperament.

In Paris, Diaghilev commissioned *Le pas d'acier* (The Steel Step), a "modernist" ballet portraying Soviet industrialisation, which Parisian audiences received enthusiastically. Prokofiev made his first Soviet tour in 1927. His Third Symphony (1928), based on *The Fiery Angel*, was characterized by Serge Koussevitzky as "the greatest symphony since Tchaikovsky's Sixth." His last Diaghilev ballet, *The Prodigal Son* (1929), impressed audiences with its final scene. By the early 1930s, the Great Depression inhibited new opera and ballet productions in the West, and Prokofiev—who saw himself as a composer first—resented time lost to touring. He increasingly turned to Soviet commissions, including the film score *Lieutenant Kijé* and the ballet *Romeo and Juliet* for the Kirov Theatre. The ballet's production was postponed indefinitely when the staff of the Bolshoi was overhauled at the behest of the chairman of the Committee on Arts Affairs, Platon Kerzhentsev.

### Soviet years and war

In 1936, Prokofiev settled permanently in Moscow. He composed *Peter and the Wolf* for Natalya Sats' Central Children's Theatre. His *Cantata for the 20th Anniversary of the October Revolution* was blocked by official Platon Kerzhentsev and not performed until 1966. He collaborated with filmmaker Sergei Eisenstein on *Alexander Nevsky* (1938), adapting the score into a large-scale cantata. His first Soviet opera, *Semyon Kotko*, was postponed when director Vsevolod Meyerhold was arrested in 1939. Later in 1939, Prokofiev composed his Piano Sonatas Nos. 6, 7, and 8, Opp. 82–84, widely known today as the "War Sonatas." Biographer Daniel Jaffé argued that Prokofiev, "having forced himself to compose a cheerful evocation of the nirvana Stalin wanted everyone to believe he had created" (i.e., in *Zdravitsa*, composed to celebrate Stalin's 60th birthday), then expressed his "true feelings" in the three sonatas. *Romeo and Juliet* finally premiered at the Kirov in 1940 and became an instant success.

The German invasion of 1941 made Tolstoy's *War and Peace* seem timely; Prokofiev began composing an opera version, co-writing the libretto with Mira Mendelson, his longtime companion. Evacuated to the Georgian SSR, he composed his Second String Quartet and Piano Sonata No. 7. During the war, restrictions on style slackened. He joined Eisenstein in Alma-Ata to compose music for *Ivan the Terrible* and the ballet *Cinderella*. He conducted the first performance of his Fifth Symphony on 13 January 1945, appearing to reach the peak of his celebrity. On 20 January 1945, he suffered a concussion from untreated chronic hypertension and never fully recovered.

### Postwar and death

The 1948 Zhdanov decree denounced Prokofiev and five other composers for "formalism"—a "renunciation of the basic principles of classical music" in favor of "muddled, nerve-racking" sounds. Eight of his works were banned. His divorce from Lina was ruled to have no legal basis since the marriage occurred in Germany; he wed Mira on 13 January 1948. Lina was arrested for espionage and sentenced to 20 years of hard labor; she was released in 1956. Prokofiev's opera *The Story of a Real Man* was cancelled by the Kirov, and he progressively withdrew from public life. After a stroke in 1949, doctors limited his composing to an hour a day. He wrote his Cello Sonata for Mstislav Rostropovich and recomposed his Cello Concerto into the Symphony-Concerto. His last completed work, the Seventh Symphony, premiered on 11 October 1952. He died of hypertensive crisis on 5 March 1953, the same day as Stalin; his funeral was attended by about 30 people, including Shostakovich. He is buried in Moscow's Novodevichy Cemetery, where Mira later joined him per their joint request.

## Terms

- **Formalism**: The official Soviet charge against Prokofiev and others in 1948, described as a "renunciation of the basic principles of classical music" in favor of "muddled, nerve-racking" sounds.
- **Lydian mode**: A major scale with a raised 4th scale degree, used in Prokofiev's first composition.
- **Neoclassicism**: A style incorporating Classical-era forms with modern musical elements, as in Prokofiev's First Symphony.
- **Polytonality**: The use of multiple keys simultaneously, as in *Sarcasms*.
- **Drambalet**: "Dramatised ballet," officially promoted at the Kirov to replace works based primarily on choreographic display; the precepts guiding *Romeo and Juliet*'s scenario.
- **Ballets Russes**: Sergei Diaghilev's ballet company, which commissioned several of Prokofiev's ballets.
- **Zhdanov Doctrine**: The 1948 cultural crackdown, named after Andrei Zhdanov, that denounced Prokofiev and others for formalism.
- **People's Artist of the RSFSR**: An honorary title conferred on Prokofiev in 1947, the day before the Zhdanov decree was published.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether Prokofiev's Soviet-era "mass songs" and works like *Zdravitsa* represented genuine conviction or forced compliance. Biographer Daniel Jaffé argued that Prokofiev, "having forced himself to compose a cheerful evocation of the nirvana Stalin wanted everyone to believe he had created," then expressed his "true feelings" in the War Sonatas, citing the central movement of Sonata No. 7, which opens with a theme based on Schumann's lied "Wehmut" ("Sadness"). The story of Prokofiev snubbing a senior official during the 1948 conference is described as "possibly apocryphal," though corroborated by the head of the composers' union. Prokofiev's reputation in the West suffered from Cold War antipathies, and his music has never won the same esteem from Western academics as Stravinsky's or Schoenberg's, despite his "gift, virtually unparalleled among 20th-century composers, for writing distinctively original diatonic melodies" (Richard Taruskin).
