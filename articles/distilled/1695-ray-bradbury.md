# Ray Bradbury

## Overview
Ray Bradbury (1920–2012) was an American author and screenwriter celebrated for bringing science fiction and fantasy into the literary mainstream through poetic prose and a focus on human character over technological speculation. His most famous works—*Fahrenheit 451* (1953), *The Martian Chronicles* (1950), *The Illustrated Man* (1951), and *Something Wicked This Way Comes* (1962)—explore censorship, nostalgia, and the dark side of imagination. A self-educated writer who rejected the "science fiction" label in favor of "fantasy" or "Midwest surrealist," Bradbury authored more than 27 novels and story collections, which included many of his 600 short stories. More than eight million copies of his works, published in over 36 languages, have been sold around the world. He viewed writing as a daily discipline sparked by two childhood incidents: his mother taking him to see Lon Chaney in *The Hunchback of Notre Dame* (1923) at age three, and a 1932 carnival encounter with a performer named Mr. Electrico, who knighted him with an electrified sword and commanded, "Live forever!"

## Body

### Early Life and Formative Influences
Bradbury was born in Waukegan, Illinois—a town he later fictionalized as "Green Town"—and spent early years surrounded by extended family. The family moved to Tucson, Arizona twice (1926–27, 1932–33) for his father's work before settling in Los Angeles in 1934 with only $40. Bradbury attributed his lifelong habit of writing every day to two incidents. The first, when he was three years old, was his mother's taking him to see Lon Chaney in the 1923 silent film *The Hunchback of Notre Dame*. The second occurred in 1932, when a carnival entertainer, one Mr. Electrico, knighted the young man with an electrified sword and intoned: "Live forever!" Bradbury remarked that he felt changed and began writing full-time within days. In Los Angeles, he roller-skated Hollywood seeking autographs, met special-effects pioneer Ray Harryhausen and radio star George Burns (selling him a joke at age 14), and attended Los Angeles High School, where he joined drama and poetry clubs. He graduated in 1938 but did not attend college due to the Depression, instead educating himself at the public library "three days a week for 10 years."

### Literary Influences and Genre Definition
Bradbury read voraciously in youth: H.G. Wells, Jules Verne, Edgar Allan Poe, Edgar Rice Burroughs (writing a *John Carter of Mars* sequel at 12), and later Katherine Anne Porter, Edith Wharton, and Jessamyn West. He joined the Los Angeles Science Fiction Society at 16 and met Robert A. Heinlein, who influenced him toward "humanistic science fiction." Bradbury resisted the "science fiction" label, defining it as "the art of the possible" and insisting *Fahrenheit 451* was his only true science fiction novel because it was "based on reality." He classified *The Martian Chronicles* as fantasy—"a Greek myth"—arguing myths have staying power because "it couldn't happen." He stopped reading science fiction in his 20s, turning to poets Alexander Pope and John Donne, and studied Eudora Welty for atmospheric density and John Steinbeck for objective insight.

### Career Milestones and Major Works
Rejected from WWII military service due to bad eyesight, Bradbury published in fanzines starting in 1938. His first published story was "Hollerbochen's Dilemma", in the January 1938 number of Ackerman's fanzine *Imagination!*. His first paid piece, "Pendulum", written with Henry Hasse, was published in the pulp magazine *Super Science Stories* in November 1941, for which he earned $15. His first solo story sale, "The Lake" ($13.75), came at age 22; he was a full-time writer by 24. *Dark Carnival* (1947) was his first collection. A crucial break came when Truman Capote, then an editorial assistant at *Mademoiselle*, pulled "Homecoming" from the slush pile; it won an O. Henry Award (1947). *The Martian Chronicles* originated when Bradbury recounted to a Doubleday editor that everyone wanted a novel and he did not have one. The editor, coincidentally named Walter Bradbury, asked if the short stories might be tied together into a book-length collection. The title was the editor's idea; he suggested: "You could call it *The Martian Chronicles*." Bradbury typed the outline overnight at a New York YMCA and received a $750 advance. *Fahrenheit 451* expanded from a 25,000-word novella, *The Fireman*, after a publisher requested a novel; the title came from the LA fire chief's statement that book paper burns at 451°F. Bradbury wrote the novel in UCLA's Powell Library on dime-per-half-hour typewriters. Later works include *Dandelion Wine* (1957, semi-autobiographical), *Something Wicked This Way Comes* (1962, dark fantasy), and *Green Shadows, White Whale* (1992, fictionalized memoir of filming *Moby Dick* in Ireland).

### Writing Philosophy and "Green Town"
Bradbury attributed his lyrical prose to daily poetry reading: "If you're reluctant to weep, you won't live a full and complete life." He described inspiration as stories that "run up and bite me in the leg—I respond by writing them down." He viewed science as "incidental," using it for social commentary and allegory. "Green Town" (Waukegan) serves as a symbolic landscape of safety and home that paradoxically hosts the macabre; it anchors *Dandelion Wine*, *Something Wicked This Way Comes*, *Farewell Summer*, and the story cycle *Summer Morning, Summer Night*. Bradbury saw Green Town as a look back at the vanishing American heartland that formed his roots.

### Cultural Contributions and Public Life
Bradbury used fiction to critique media alienation and political correctness. He noted *Fahrenheit 451* predicted earbud-style radios ("seashells") and wall-sized screens decades early. He consulted on the 1964 New York World's Fair (US Pavilion narration) and Walt Disney World's Spaceship Earth. A staunch library advocate ("libraries raised me"), he raised funds to save California branches and negotiated a unique clause allowing *Fahrenheit 451* e-book library lending. He hosted *The Ray Bradbury Theater* (1985–92), headed the Pandemonium Theatre Company, and spoke at the Santa Barbara Writers Conference for 30+ years. He resisted e-books initially ("We have too many machines") but relented for library access. Politically independent, he shifted from Democrat to Republican after 1968 (except 1976), praised Reagan, criticized Clinton and Obama (for ending crewed spaceflight), and opposed affirmative action and political correctness on campuses.

### Adaptations and Media Legacy
Bradbury's works spawned extensive adaptations: 31 stories for EC Comics (1950–54), early TV anthologies (*Tales of Tomorrow*, *Alfred Hitchcock Presents*), radio (*Dimension X*, *X Minus One*), and film (*It Came from Outer Space*, 1953; *The Beast from 20,000 Fathoms*, 1953, from "The Fog Horn"; Truffaut's *Fahrenheit 451*, 1966; *The Illustrated Man*, 1969; *Something Wicked This Way Comes*, 1983). He wrote the *Moby Dick* screenplay (1956) and adapted 65 stories for his TV series. Soviet TV adapted six stories; Uzbek and Lithuanian directors produced animated versions. *Bradbury 13* (1984, NPR radio dramas) won a Peabody Award. *The Halloween Tree* (1993) was an animated feature he narrated. A 2005 dispute with Michael Moore over *Fahrenheit 9/11* title usage ended with Moore's apology but no title change.

### Personal Life, Politics, and Death
Bradbury lived with his parents until marrying Marguerite McClure in 1947 (age 27); she was the only woman he dated. They had four daughters. He never drove, using bicycles and public transit. Raised Baptist, he called himself a "delicatessen religionist" drawing from Eastern and Western faiths, viewing his career as "At play in the fields of the Lord." Close friends included Ray Harryhausen (best man, 70+ year friendship), Charles Addams (illustrated "Homecoming," planned unrealized collaboration), and Gene Roddenberry (declined *Star Trek* writing). A 1999 stroke left him partially wheelchair-dependent; he retired from conventions in 2009 but wrote until days before his death. He died June 5, 2012, in Los Angeles. His personal library went to Waukegan Public Library; his home office was recreated at the Ray Bradbury Center in Indianapolis.

## Terms
- ****Fix-up novel**** — A novel created by linking previously published short stories into a cohesive narrative (e.g., *The Martian Chronicles*).
- ****Green Town**** — Bradbury's fictionalized version of Waukegan, Illinois, serving as a symbolic heartland setting for his semi-autobiographical and dark fantasy works.
- ****Mr. Electrico**** — A carnival performer who, in 1932, touched young Bradbury with an electrified sword and commanded "Live forever!", catalyzing his writing vocation.
- ****Pulp magazine**** — Inexpensive fiction magazines printed on wood-pulp paper (e.g., *Super Science Stories*, *Weird Tales*) where Bradbury published early work.
- ****Fanzine**** — Amateur-produced magazine for fans of a genre (e.g., *Imagination!*, *Futuria Fantasia*) that published Bradbury's first stories.
- ****Slush pile**** — Unsolicited manuscripts submitted to publishers; Capote discovered Bradbury's "Homecoming" in *Mademoiselle*'s slush pile.
- ****Midwest surrealist**** — A critical label applied to Bradbury, reflecting his blending of Midwestern small-town realism with fantastical, dreamlike elements.
- ****Delicatessen religionist**** — Bradbury's self-description for his eclectic, non-dogmatic spirituality drawing from multiple faith traditions.
- ****Seashells**** — The in-ear radio devices in *Fahrenheit 451* that anticipate modern earbuds/Bluetooth headsets.
- ****Retro Hugo Award**** — A Hugo Award given retroactively for years when no Hugos were awarded; Bradbury won for *Fahrenheit 451* (1953) in 2004.

## Debates and open questions
- **Genre classification**: Bradbury insisted he wrote fantasy, not science fiction (except *Fahrenheit 451*), defining SF as "the art of the possible" and fantasy as "the art of the impossible." Critics and scholars continue to debate whether this distinction holds for his Mars and dystopian works.
- **Literary merit vs. genre ghetto**: *The New York Times* credited him with bringing SF into the literary mainstream, yet some academic circles historically marginalized his work as "mere" genre fiction despite his Pulitzer citation (2007) and National Medal of Arts.
- **Technology stance**: Bradbury embraced computers early (1985: "simply books") but later resisted e-books and decried "too many machines" (2010), creating a tension between his futuristic visions and personal technoskepticism.
- **Political evolution**: His shift from New Deal Democrat to Reagan Republican (with a Carter interlude) and late-life anti-government rhetoric ("destroy part of our government") complicates readings of his anti-authoritarian fiction.
- **Adaptation fidelity**: Bradbury praised some adaptations (Truffaut's *Fahrenheit 451*) but called *The Martian Chronicles* miniseries "just boring," raising questions about the translatability of his prose style to visual media.
- **Mr. Electrico's historicity**: The Mr. Electrico anecdote is central to Bradbury's self-mythology, but no independent verification of the performer or event exists beyond Bradbury's own retellings.
