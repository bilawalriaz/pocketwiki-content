# Gulf Stream

## Overview
The Gulf Stream is a powerful, warm Atlantic ocean current originating in the Gulf of Mexico, flowing northward along the U.S. East Coast before veering toward Europe. It is a key component of the North Atlantic Gyre and significantly influences regional climates, storm formation, and marine ecosystems. Its strength and path are driven by wind patterns and Earth's rotation, and it plays a critical role in the global climate system by transporting heat northward.

## Timeline
- **1512** — Juan Ponce de León's expedition provides the first European discovery of the Gulf Stream.
- **1768** — Benjamin Franklin, prompted by a question about mail ship delays, learns of the current from whaler Timothy Folger.
- **1769** — Franklin's chart of the Gulf Stream is printed in London.
- **1950s-60s** — Direct measurements of the Gulf Stream's velocity and temperature begin via research vessels.
- **Late 1970s** — Satellite remote sensing begins to map the current's position and variability in near-real time.
- **1992** — NASA's TOPEX/Poseidon satellite mission launches, providing continuous data on sea-surface height related to circulation.
- **2018** — Studies published in *Nature* find the Gulf Stream to be at its weakest in at least 1,600 years.

## Body

### Formation and Driving Forces
The Gulf Stream originates from the Atlantic North Equatorial Current, which splits and rejoins north of the Straits of Florida. It is primarily a wind-driven, western-intensified current. The trade winds and westerlies create a wind stress pattern that, combined with the conservation of potential vorticity (a measure of spin in a fluid), results in a narrow, fast-moving poleward flow along the ocean's western boundary. This process, known as western intensification, makes the Gulf Stream stronger than currents on the eastern side of the Atlantic basin.

### Physical Properties and Transport
The Gulf Stream is typically 100 km wide and 800 to 1,200 m deep, with a maximum surface speed of about 2.5 m/s. It transports an immense volume of water—30 million cubic metres per second (30 sverdrups) through the Florida Straits, increasing to 150 sverdrups south of Newfoundland. This dwarfs the combined flow of all rivers entering the Atlantic. As the current moves north, evaporative cooling and brine exclusion (salt expulsion during sea ice formation) increase the water's density, causing it to sink and contribute to the North Atlantic Deep Water, a deep southward-flowing current.

### Climate and Ecological Influence
The Gulf Stream moderates the climate of the U.S. East Coast from Florida to Virginia and, via its extension the North Atlantic Current, makes Northwest Europe significantly warmer than other regions at similar latitudes. It allows ice-free ports in northern Norway and supports diverse marine life. The current transports tropical fish and organisms northward, creating biodiversity hotspots like Nantucket, which marks a transition zone between southern and northern species. Its warm waters also fuel cyclone development, providing the thermal energy needed for tropical storms and intensifying extratropical cyclones.

### Future and Scientific Understanding
While the Gulf Stream itself is not projected to collapse even if the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) weakens, it is expected to slow down. This would cause significant effects, including sea-level rise along the North American coast and altered precipitation and storm patterns. Scientific understanding has evolved from early navigation charts to modern satellite and float networks, which provide a three-dimensional view of its structure and role in global circulation.

## Terms
- **Western intensification**: The process by which ocean currents on the western boundaries of ocean basins (like the Gulf Stream) become narrower, faster, and deeper than their eastern counterparts due to Earth's rotation.
- **Sverdrup (Sv)**: A unit of ocean current transport equal to one million cubic metres per second.
- **Potential vorticity**: A conserved quantity in fluid dynamics that combines the spin of a fluid parcel and its thickness; changes in latitude or depth alter this spin, driving currents.
- **Thermohaline circulation**: Large-scale ocean circulation driven by differences in water density, which is controlled by temperature (thermo) and salinity (haline).
- **Brine exclusion**: The process where salt is expelled from forming sea ice, increasing the salinity and density of the surrounding water.
- **North Atlantic Deep Water**: A deep, cold, and salty water mass formed in the North Atlantic that flows southward as part of the global ocean conveyor belt.
- **Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC)**: The system of ocean currents in the Atlantic that includes the Gulf Stream and the southward flow of deep water, crucial for heat transport.
- **Cold-core ring**: A clockwise-rotating (in the Northern Hemisphere) ocean eddy that breaks off from the Gulf Stream, trapping colder water from the north.
- **Warm-core ring**: A counter-clockwise-rotating (in the Northern Hemisphere) ocean eddy that breaks off from the Gulf Stream, trapping warmer water from the south.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The primary debate concerns the future stability of the Gulf Stream and its parent circulation, the AMOC. While the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report concludes the Gulf Stream will not collapse, its projected slowdown and the associated climatic impacts are subjects of intense study.
- The extent to which the Gulf Stream's recent weakening (as of 2018) is due to natural variability versus anthropogenic climate change is an active area of research.
- The precise mechanisms and feedbacks linking the Gulf Stream's behavior to regional sea-level rise and extreme weather events in Europe and North America are still being refined.
