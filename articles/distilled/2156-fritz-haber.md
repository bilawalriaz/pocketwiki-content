# Fritz Haber

## Overview
Fritz Haber (1868–1934) was a German chemist whose work embodies a profound moral paradox. He is celebrated for developing the Haber-Bosch process, a method for synthesizing ammonia that revolutionized agriculture and is credited with supporting roughly half the world's population. Conversely, he is condemned as the "father of chemical warfare" for pioneering the weaponization of poison gases during World War I. His life and legacy are central to discussions on the ethical responsibilities of scientists.

## Timeline
- **9 Dec 1868** — Born in Breslau, Kingdom of Prussia (now Wrocław, Poland).
- **1891** — Receives doctorate in chemistry from Friedrich Wilhelm University, Berlin.
- **1906** — Appointed full professor of physical chemistry at the University of Karlsruhe.
- **1909** — First chemist to successfully synthesize ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen in a lab.
- **1913** — Carl Bosch scales the Haber process to industrial production at BASF.
- **22 Apr 1915** — First large-scale use of chlorine gas as a weapon at the Second Battle of Ypres, overseen by Haber.
- **2 May 1915** — His wife, Clara Immerwahr, dies by suicide.
- **1918** — Awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the synthesis of ammonia (received 1919).
- **1919–1923** — Continues secret work on chemical weapons development for Germany.
- **1920s** — His institute develops Zyklon A, a cyanide-based insecticide.
- **7 Apr 1933** — Nazi "Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service" targets Jewish scientists.
- **30 Apr 1933** — Haber resigns from his directorship in protest of the law.
- **29 Jan 1934** — Dies of heart failure in Basel, Switzerland, while en route to a new position in Mandatory Palestine.

## Body

### Early Life and Academic Career
Born into a Jewish family in Breslau, Haber identified strongly as German. After a difficult relationship with his father and a brief stint in the family business, he pursued academia. He converted from Judaism to Lutheranism, possibly to improve his career prospects. At the University of Karlsruhe, he made significant contributions to electrochemistry and chemical thermodynamics, publishing influential works before being appointed a full professor in 1906.

### The Haber-Bosch Process
In 1909, Haber achieved the long-sought goal of synthesizing ammonia from atmospheric nitrogen and hydrogen gas under high temperature and pressure, using a catalyst. This was a direct application of Le Châtelier's principle. The process was scaled to industrial production by Carl Bosch at BASF, becoming the Haber-Bosch process. It enabled the mass production of nitrogen-based fertilizers, drastically increasing agricultural yields and supporting global population growth. It also ended Chile's near-monopoly on natural nitrate deposits used for fertilizers and explosives. Haber received the 1918 Nobel Prize for this work.

### World War I and Chemical Warfare
A fervent German nationalist, Haber enthusiastically supported Germany in World War I. He was promoted to captain and made head of the Chemistry Section in the Ministry of War. He led the development and deployment of chlorine gas and other poison gases, personally overseeing its first use at the Second Battle of Ypres in 1915. He formulated "Haber's rule," which relates gas concentration to exposure time for lethal effects. He defended chemical warfare as a legitimate tool to break the stalemate of trench warfare, arguing it was no more inhumane than other weapons. His work led to the development of gas masks for defense.

### Later Years and Exile
Between the wars, Haber continued weapons research and led a failed project to extract gold from seawater. His institute developed the insecticide Zyklon A. Following the Nazi rise to power in 1933, Haber was targeted by antisemitic laws despite his conversion and service. He resigned in protest and, in poor health, left Germany. He briefly worked in England before accepting a position at the Sieff Research Institute in Mandatory Palestine. He died of heart failure in Basel, Switzerland, in January 1934 while en route. His work on cyanide gas was later adapted into Zyklon B, used in the Holocaust.

## Terms
*   **Haber-Bosch Process:** An industrial method for synthesizing ammonia (NH₃) from nitrogen (N₂) and hydrogen (H₂) gases under high temperature and pressure, using a catalyst.
*   **Le Châtelier's Principle:** A principle stating that if a dynamic equilibrium is disturbed by changing the conditions, the position of equilibrium moves to counteract the change.
*   **Haber's Rule:** A mathematical relationship Haber formulated stating that the toxicity of an inhaled substance is a function of its concentration and the duration of exposure.
*   **Zyklon A:** A cyanide-based pesticide developed at Haber's institute in the 1920s. A later formulation, Zyklon B, was infamously used in the Holocaust.
*   **Privatdozent:** A qualification in German academia that allows a scholar to teach at a university, often without a salary, after completing a habilitation.
*   **Manifesto of the Ninety-Three:** A 1914 proclamation signed by 93 leading German intellectuals, including Haber, defending Germany's actions at the start of World War I.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Moral Responsibility:** Haber's legacy is fiercely debated. Is he a savior who fed billions or a war criminal whose work enabled mass killing? His defense of chemical warfare and the subsequent use of his institute's research for the Holocaust are central to this dilemma.
*   **Clara Immerwahr's Suicide:** The exact reasons for his wife's suicide are speculated upon. Some accounts suggest she was a pacifist who opposed his chemical weapons work, with her death occurring shortly after the first chlorine gas attack.
*   **Scientific Ethics:** Haber's life is a case study in the ethical dilemmas of scientists during wartime, famously captured in his statement: "during peace time a scientist belongs to the world, but during war time he belongs to his country."
