# Sigismund III Vasa

## Overview
Sigismund III Vasa (1566–1632) was King of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania from 1587 to 1632 and King of Sweden from 1592 to 1599. A devout Catholic, his reign was defined by efforts to unify his realms under Catholicism, leading to significant territorial expansion but also internal conflict and the eventual loss of the Swedish crown. His rule coincided with the end of the Polish Golden Age and the beginning of its decline.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Born to a Swedish father and a Polish mother, Sigismund was raised Catholic in a Protestant-leaning Sweden. Elected King of Poland in 1587, he inherited the Swedish throne in 1592, creating a personal union. His attempts to impose Catholicism in Sweden sparked a civil war with his Protestant uncle, Charles IX, leading to his deposition from the Swedish throne in 1599.

### Domestic Rule and Opposition
Sigismund sought to strengthen royal authority and promote the Counter-Reformation, employing Jesuits as advisors. This clashed with the Polish nobility (*szlachta*) and Chancellor Jan Zamoyski, who defended the elective monarchy and religious freedoms guaranteed by the 1573 Warsaw Confederation. Tensions culminated in the Zebrzydowski rebellion (1606), which, though defeated, reinforced the nobility's political privileges.

### Foreign Wars and Expansion
Sigismund's reign saw Poland's greatest territorial expansion. Exploiting Russia's Time of Troubles, Polish forces captured Smolensk and occupied Moscow (1610–1612). The subsequent Truce of Deulino (1618) secured these gains. He also fought the Ottomans, with a decisive Polish victory at the Battle of Khotyn (1621) halting their advance. However, prolonged conflict with Sweden ended in defeat; the Truce of Altmark (1629) forced Poland to cede Livonia to Sweden.

### Culture and Legacy
Sigismund moved the capital from Kraków to Warsaw in 1596. His reign fostered the Baroque style in architecture and arts, and saw the Polish language gain prominence over Latin. He is commemorated by Sigismund's Column in Warsaw, erected by his son. His legacy is controversial: he was a capable ruler who expanded the state, but his religious intolerance and foreign wars planted seeds of future decline.

## Terms
- **Counter-Reformation**: The Catholic Church's campaign to re-establish its authority and combat Protestantism.
- **Sejm**: The Polish parliament, which held significant power in the elective monarchy.
- **Szlachta**: The Polish nobility, a powerful political class with extensive privileges.
- **Warsaw Confederation (1573)**: A charter guaranteeing religious freedom for nobles in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth.
- **Time of Troubles**: A period of political crisis and civil war in Russia (1598–1613).
- **Winged Hussars**: Elite Polish heavy cavalry renowned for their decisive charges in battle.
- **Henrician Articles**: A set of privileges for the Polish nobility, limiting royal power, established in 1573.
- **Riksdag**: The Swedish parliament.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate whether Sigismund's reign marked the definitive end of the Polish Golden Age or its final, brilliant phase. His personal ambition to create a Catholic super-state is seen by some as visionary, while others view it as a catastrophic miscalculation that drained the Commonwealth's resources and weakened its political system. The exact nature of his relationship with courtier Urszula Meyerin remains a subject of historical speculation.
