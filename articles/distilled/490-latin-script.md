# Latin script

## Overview
The Latin script (or Roman script) is a writing system derived from the Greek alphabet via the Etruscans and Ancient Romans. It is the basis for the International Phonetic Alphabet and the ISO basic Latin alphabet (the 26 letters used in English). It is the world’s most widely adopted writing system, serving as the standard for Western and Central Europe, most of sub-Saharan Africa, the Americas, Oceania, and many languages elsewhere. Its global dominance stems from the expansion of the Roman Empire, the spread of Western Christianity, and European colonization.

## Timeline
- **7th century** — Letter **W** originates as doubled **V** (VV) in Old English for the /w/ sound.
- **16th century** — Rounded capital **U** differentiated from pointed **V**; **J** emerges as a consonantal swash form of **I**.
- **17th century** — **J** introduced into English for the consonant sound.
- **19th century** — **J** universally recognized as a distinct letter in alphabetic order; Romanians return to Latin alphabet, dropping Cyrillic.
- **1928** — Republic of Türkiye adopts Latin alphabet for Turkish, replacing modified Arabic script.
- **1930s** — USSR peoples adopt Unified Turkic Alphabet (Latin-based); reversed to Cyrillic in 1940s.
- **1957** — People's Republic of China introduces Latin script for Zhuang language, replacing Sawndip.
- **1960s** — ISO/IEC 646 standardizes Latin alphabet based on ASCII (26 × 2 letters).
- **1991** — Post-Soviet collapse: Azerbaijan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Moldova adopt Latin alphabets; Ethiopia ethnic groups switch from Geʽez to Latin.
- **1999** — Tatarstan passes law for Latin co-official status; overruled by Russian government in 2000.
- **2015** — Kazakhstan announces transition to Latin for Kazakh by 2025.
- **2020** — ~2.6 billion people (36% of world population) use the Latin alphabet.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
The Latin script descends from a Greek alphabet used in Cumae (Magna Graecia), modified by the Etruscans and then the Ancient Romans. The classical Latin alphabet used **I** and **V** for both vowels and consonants, which proved inconvenient for later Germanic and Romance languages. **W** originated as a doubled **V** (VV) in 7th-century Old English, replacing the Runic letter wynn (Ƿ) by the 11th century. In the 16th century, a rounded minuscule **u** yielded capital **U** for the vowel, while a pointed **v** was derived for the consonant. A word-final swash form **j** split from **I** for the consonant; **J** entered English in the 17th century but was not universally ordered as a distinct letter until the 19th century.

### Medieval Spread and Religious Correlation
With the expansion of the Roman Empire, the Latin alphabet spread across the western Mediterranean, while the eastern half retained Greek. During the Middle Ages, Western Christianity drove adoption among Northern European peoples speaking Celtic (displacing Ogham), Germanic (displacing Runic), Baltic, and Uralic languages (Hungarian, Finnish, Estonian). West and South Slavic speakers adopted it with Roman Catholicism; East Slavic speakers generally adopted Cyrillic with Orthodox Christianity. Serbian uses both scripts, with Cyrillic official and Latin common elsewhere.

### Global Expansion Since 1500
In 1500, Latin script was largely confined to Western, Northern, and Central Europe. European colonization (Spanish, Portuguese, English, French, German, Dutch) spread it to the Americas, Oceania, parts of Asia, Africa, and the Pacific. It replaced Arabic and Brahmic scripts for many Austronesian languages (Philippines, Malaysian, Indonesian). Portuguese missionaries devised a Latin alphabet for Vietnamese (replacing Chinese characters) and introduced Roman script for Konkani in Goa. In the 19th century, French rule replaced Chinese characters with Latin script in Vietnamese administration.

### 20th-Century Script Reforms
Major state-driven transitions reshaped the script’s map:
- **1928**: Türkiye adopted Latin for Turkish under Atatürk.
- **1930s USSR**: Turkic peoples (Azeri, Yakuts, Tatars, Bashkirs, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, etc.) and Moldovans adopted the Unified Turkic Alphabet; all were replaced by Cyrillic in the 1940s (except Latvians, Lithuanians, Estonians, Germans, Poles, Finns, Georgians, Armenians).
- **1930s–40s**: Most Kurds replaced Arabic script with two Latin alphabets; Latin remains widely used despite official Arabic use.
- **1957/1982**: China reformed Zhuang from Sawndip (Chinese-based) to a Latin/Cyrillic/IPA mix, later standardized to Latin-only.
- **1991 Ethiopia**: After the Derg’s collapse, Kafa, Oromo, Sidama, Somali, and Wolaitta switched from Geʽez to Latin; debate continues for Hadiyya and Kambaata.

### Post-Soviet and 21st-Century Transitions
After 1991, Azerbaijan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, and Moldova officially adopted Latin alphabets; Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and Transnistria retained Cyrillic due to Russian ties.
- **1999 Tatarstan**: Law for Latin co-official status by 2011; overruled by Russian federal ban in 2000.
- **2015 Kazakhstan**: Announced Kazakh Latin alphabet to replace Cyrillic by 2025.
- **2019 Canada**: Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami announced unified Latin-based writing system for Inuit languages, modeled on Greenlandic.
- **2021 Uzbekistan**: Finalized transition from Cyrillic to Latin for Uzbek by 2023 (plans stalled since 1993).
- **Crimean Tatar**: Uses both scripts; Ukraine approved Latin switch by 2025 (2021), but Russia’s 2014 annexation dropped Latin in Crimea.

### International and National Standards
By the 1960s, computing and telecommunications required a non-proprietary encoding. ISO/IEC 646 encapsulated the Latin alphabet based on US ASCII (26 × 2 letters). Later ISO/IEC 10646 (Unicode Latin) retained this basic set with extensions for other languages. DIN 91379 specifies a Unicode subset supporting all EU/EFTA official languages (including Greek and Cyrillic) plus German minority languages, enabling transliteration per ISO standards; efforts aim to make it a European CEN standard.

### Adaptation Mechanisms for New Languages
Languages adapt the Latin alphabet via four extension methods: **diacritics** (e.g., German ä, ö, ü; Romanian ă, â, î, ș, ț), **ligatures** (fusion of letters: Æ/æ from AE, Œ/œ from OE, & from *et*, ẞ/ß from ſʒ or ſs), **multigraphs** (digraphs like English *ch*, *sh*, *th*; Dutch *ij* capitalized as *IJ* or *Ĳ*; trigraphs like German *sch*, Breton *c'h*), and **new letter forms** (e.g., Runic wynn, thorn, eth in Old English; African reference alphabet letters Ɛ, Ɔ, Ŋ, Ɓ, Ɗ, Ƙ; Turkish/Azerbaijani/Kazakh dotted İ/i and dotless I/ı; Azerbaijani Ə). **Collation** (alphabetical ordering) varies: Swedish treats å, ä, ö as separate letters; German sorts ä, ö, ü with base letters; Spanish sorts ñ between n and o but accents with unaccented vowels. **Capitalization** rules differ historically and by language (Old English rarely capitalized; 18th-century English capitalized all nouns like modern German).

### Romanization
Words from non-Latin scripts (Arabic, Chinese, etc.) are transliterated into Latin script for embedding in Latin text or international communication, termed **romanization**. It was prominent in early computing limited to 7-bit ASCII; Unicode reduced its necessity, though keyboards may still restrict input to romanized forms.

## Terms
- **Diacritic**: A small symbol added to a letter (above, below, or through) to change its phonetic value, indicate tone, mark syllable boundaries, or distinguish homographs (e.g., umlaut ¨, acute ´, cedilla ¸).
- **Digraph**: A pair of letters representing a single sound or sound sequence not predictable from the individual letters (e.g., English *sh*, Dutch *ij*).
- **Trigraph**: Three letters functioning as a single unit for one sound (e.g., German *sch*, Breton *c'h*).
- **Ligature**: A fused glyph of two or more letters (e.g., Æ from AE, Œ from OE, & from Latin *et*, ẞ from ſs).
- **Collation**: The defined sorting order of characters in an alphabet, which may treat modified letters or multigraphs as distinct letters or as variants of base letters.
- **Romanization**: Transliteration or transcription of a language natively written in another script into the Latin alphabet.
- **ISO basic Latin alphabet**: The 26 uppercase and 26 lowercase letters (A–Z, a–z) standardized in ISO/IEC 646 and Unicode, derived from the English alphabet via ASCII.
- **African reference alphabet**: A standardized set of extended Latin letters (e.g., Ɛ, Ɔ, Ŋ, Ɓ, Ɗ, Ƙ) for African languages, with sound values close to IPA.
- **Unified Turkic Alphabet**: A Latin-based script adopted by Turkic peoples in the USSR in the 1930s, replaced by Cyrillic in the 1940s.
- **Sawndip**: A Chinese-character-based writing system formerly used for the Zhuang language in China, replaced by Latin script in 1957.

## Debates and open questions
- **Script choice in post-Soviet states**: Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Azerbaijan, Turkmenistan, and Moldova have moved to Latin; Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and Transnistria retain Cyrillic due to Russian ties. Ukraine, Kyrgyzstan, and Mongolia have discussed switching; Mongolia opted to revive its traditional script instead.
- **Tatarstan’s Latinization**: Regional law (1999) for co-official Latin was overruled by Russian federal ban (2000); status remains contested.
- **Crimean Tatar script**: Ukraine approved Latin transition by 2025 (2021), but Russia’s 2014 annexation imposed Cyrillic; Crimean Tatars outside Crimea continue using Latin.
- **Ethiopia’s script transitions**: Kafa, Oromo, Sidama, Somali, and Wolaitta adopted Latin after 1991; debate continues for Hadiyya and Kambaata on whether to follow suit.
- **Kurdish script divide**: Official Kurdish government uses Arabic script; majority of Kurdish speakers use Latin alphabets introduced in the 1930s–40s.
