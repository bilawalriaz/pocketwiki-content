# Multan

## Overview
Multan is one of Asia's oldest continuously inhabited cities, located in Pakistan's Punjab province. Its strategic position on ancient trade routes made it a major cultural, religious, and economic hub, earning it the title "City of Saints" for its numerous Sufi shrines. Its history reflects a pattern of conquest and resilience, from the Achaemenids and Alexander the Great to the Mughals, Sikhs, and British.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000–2800 BCE** — Early Harappan period sites in the region.
- **326 BCE** — Conquered by Alexander the Great during the Mallian campaign.
- **712 CE** — Captured by Muhammad bin Qasim after the conquest of Sindh.
- **855 CE** — Becomes capital of the Emirate of Multan under the Banu Munabbih.
- **Late 10th century** — Qarmatian Isma'ilis destroy the Multan Sun Temple.
- **1005–1010 CE** — Mahmud of Ghazni conquers Multan and suppresses Isma'ilism.
- **1175 CE** — Conquered by Muhammad Ghori of the Ghurid dynasty.
- **1557 CE** — Captured by Mughal Emperor Akbar's forces.
- **1818 CE** — Conquered by the Sikh Empire under Ranjit Singh.
- **1848–1849 CE** — Revolt and siege lead to British conquest after the Second Anglo-Sikh War.

## Body

### Ancient and Medieval History
The region shows settlement from the early Indus Valley Civilization. The ancient town, possibly named for the Malli tribe, was besieged by Alexander the Great in 326 BCE. It was later conquered by the Umayyad commander Muhammad bin Qasim in 712 CE. In the 9th century, it became the capital of the Emirate of Multan. The city was a major trading center with ties to the Persianate world. In the 11th and 12th centuries, it attracted Sufi mystics, becoming known as the "City of Saints."

### Rule of Successive Empires
After the Ghaznavids, Multan was ruled by the Ghurids and then became part of the Delhi Sultanate's Mamluk, Khalji, and Tughluq dynasties. It was sacked by Timur in 1398. The Langah Sultanate made it their capital in 1445. The Mughals, under Akbar, established Multan Subah in 1580, one of their largest provinces. The city enjoyed two centuries of peace as "Dar al-Aman" (Abode of Peace) under Mughal rule.

### Decline and Modern Era
Following Mughal decline, Multan was invaded by Nader Shah (1739) and Ahmad Shah Durrani (1752). The Sikh Empire captured it in 1818. The 1848 Multan Revolt triggered the Second Anglo-Sikh War, leading to British conquest in 1849. Under the British, the city's political importance waned, though canal colonies were developed. After Pakistan's independence in 1947, the city's population grew, and it remains a major urban center.

## Terms
- **Emirate:** A state ruled by an emir, a title for a commander or ruler.
- **Subah:** An administrative province of the Mughal Empire.
- **Sufi:** A mystic who seeks direct experience of God; Sufism is the mystical dimension of Islam.
- **Qarmatian Isma'ili:** A radical Isma'ili Shia sect known for its anti-establishment actions.
- **Satpanth:** A syncretic tradition blending Hindu and Isma'ili practices.
- **Dar al-Aman:** Arabic for "Abode of Peace," referring to a period of stability.
- **Canal Colony:** An area developed for agriculture through the construction of irrigation canals.
- **Sajjada nashin:** A custodian of a Sufi shrine.

## Debates and open questions
The exact origin of the name "Multan" is debated, with theories linking it to the Malli tribe, a sun deity, or Persian/Sanskrit words for "frontier land." The city's precise location during Alexander's siege is uncertain, as it was then on an island in the Ravi River, which has since shifted course.
