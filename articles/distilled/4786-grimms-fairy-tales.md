# Grimms' Fairy Tales

## Overview
Grimms' Fairy Tales (*Kinder- und Hausmärchen*) is a foundational German collection of folk and fairy tales compiled by brothers Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm. First published in 1812, it grew through seven editions to 210 tales by 1857. The collection is a seminal work of Western children's literature, listed in UNESCO's Memory of the World Register, and has profoundly influenced folklore studies, literature, and the arts.

## Timeline
- **1802** — Jacob Grimm begins studies at the University of Marburg.
- **1806** — Jacob returns from Paris and shifts focus from law to German literature.
- **1812** — First edition, Vol. 1, published with 86 tales.
- **1815** — First edition, Vol. 2, published with 70 more tales.
- **1819** — Second edition published; tales revised and expanded.
- **1825** — *Kleine Ausgabe* ("small edition") of 50 tales for children is published.
- **1857** — Seventh and final edition published, containing 210 tales and 10 "Children's Legends".

## Body

### Origin and Motivation
Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm studied law at the University of Marburg, where Professor Friedrich Carl von Savigny influenced their interest in German history and folklore. Friends Achim von Arnim and Clemens Brentano, who wished to publish folk tales, prompted the brothers to begin collecting oral stories and tales from books and acquaintances in Kassel. Their initial goal was to write a history of old German literature and preserve German cultural heritage.

### Composition and Evolution
The first volume was published in 1812, followed by a second in 1815. The collection underwent significant revisions through seven editions. Stories were added and removed; by the final 1857 edition, the corpus held 210 tales plus 10 "Children's Legends." The early editions were criticized for being unsuitable for children due to their scholarly notes and violent or mature content. Subsequent editions softened this material—for example, changing biological mothers to stepmothers in tales like *Snow White* and *Hansel and Gretel*—and removed sexual references, though violence against villains often increased.

### Popularity and Influence
The brothers' initial aim was to establish their scholarly reputation. Their international success, however, was cemented by Jacob's *German Grammar* (1819). The collection inspired folklore collectors worldwide, including Russia's Alexander Afanasyev and Norway's Asbjørnsen and Moe, who similarly sought to preserve national tales. The tales have been widely adapted in art, music, and literature, and their cultural impact is vast, having been both praised as foundational Western culture and controversially interpreted by figures like Adolf Hitler.

## Terms
- **Kinder- und Hausmärchen (KHM):** The original German title, meaning "Children's and Household Tales."
- **Folklore:** The traditional beliefs, customs, and stories of a community, passed through generations.
- **Romantic nationalism:** A movement emphasizing the unique cultural spirit of a nation, often expressed through language and folklore.
- **Kleine Ausgabe:** A "small edition" of 50 tales published in 1825, designed specifically for child readers.
- **UNESCO Memory of the World Register:** A program safeguarding documentary heritage of global significance.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Suitability for children:** The collection's title and evolution reflect an ongoing debate about what constitutes appropriate material for children, with the Grimms progressively editing tales to be more "child-friendly."
- **Authorial intent vs. oral tradition:** Scholars debate the extent to which the Grimms' edits shaped the tales versus preserving authentic oral tradition.
- **Cultural interpretation:** The tales have been subject to varied and sometimes conflicting interpretations, from Freudian psychology to nationalist propaganda, as seen in Hitler's racial reading of *Cinderella*.
