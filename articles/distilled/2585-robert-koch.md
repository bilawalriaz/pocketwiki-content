# Robert Koch

## Overview
Robert Koch (1843–1910) was a German physician and microbiologist who discovered the specific causative agents of anthrax, tuberculosis, and cholera. His work established the germ theory of disease, provided the scientific basis for public health, and earned him the 1905 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. He is considered a founder of modern bacteriology and medical bacteriology.

## Timeline
- **11 December 1843** — Born in Clausthal, Germany.
- **1876** — Discovers *Bacillus anthracis* as the cause of anthrax.
- **1880** — Appointed government advisor at the Imperial Health Office in Berlin.
- **24 March 1882** — Announces discovery of *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*.
- **1884** — Discovers the cholera bacterium in Calcutta, India.
- **1885** — Becomes Director of Hygienic Institute and Professor at Berlin University.
- **1890** — Announces tuberculin as a tuberculosis treatment; it proves ineffective.
- **1891** — Becomes director of the Royal Prussian Institute for Infectious Diseases.
- **1905** — Awarded Nobel Prize for his work on tuberculosis.
- **27 May 1910** — Dies in Baden-Baden, Germany.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Koch studied medicine at the University of Göttingen, graduating with highest honors in 1866. After various medical posts and service as a surgeon in the Franco-Prussian War, he was appointed district physician in Wollstein (now Wolsztyn, Poland) in 1872. Using a microscope gifted by his wife, he set up a private laboratory where he began his groundbreaking microbiological research.

### Discovery of Anthrax and Bacteriological Methods
In 1876, Koch discovered that anthrax was caused by a specific bacterium, *Bacillus anthracis*, and identified its dormant spore stage. This was the first proof that a microscopic organism could cause a specific disease, supporting the germ theory. He developed crucial techniques, including the first use of an oil immersion lens, condenser, and microphotography in microscopy. To grow bacteria in pure culture, he pioneered the use of agar (suggested by Fanny Hesse) as a solid medium, which led to the development of the Petri dish by his assistant Julius Richard Petri.

### Discovery of Tuberculosis and Tuberculin
In 1882, Koch identified *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* as the cause of tuberculosis, a disease then widely believed to be hereditary. This discovery fulfilled his own principles for proving a pathogen causes a disease, later formalized as Koch's postulates. He later developed tuberculin, an extract from the bacterium, intending it as a cure. Its public announcement in 1890 led to a major scandal when clinical trials proved it ineffective and even harmful, damaging his reputation. Tuberculin was later found useful for diagnosing tuberculosis.

### Discovery of Cholera
In 1883, Koch led a team to Egypt and then India to study cholera. In Calcutta, he identified the causative bacterium, describing it as "a little bent, like a comma." He linked the disease to contaminated water sources. Although he could not fully satisfy his own postulates (as animals were immune), his discovery was correct. The bacterium was later named *Vibrio cholerae*.

### Koch's Postulates
Koch's methods for linking a specific microbe to a specific disease were summarized into four postulates, formulated by his assistant Friedrich Loeffler in 1883. They state: 1) The organism must be present in every case of the disease; 2) It must be isolated and grown in pure culture; 3) The pure culture must cause the disease when inoculated into a healthy, susceptible individual; 4) The same organism must be re-isolated from the experimentally infected host. These postulates became the gold standard for identifying infectious disease agents.

### Later Work and Controversies
Koch's career included disputes with Louis Pasteur over proof of causality for anthrax. A major controversy arose from his 1901 claim that human and bovine tuberculosis were the same, later proven incorrect, and his dismissal of the risk of cattle tuberculosis to humans. He also served as a neutral arbitrator for the 1902 Nobel Prize in Medicine, influencing the award to Ronald Ross over Giovanni Battista Grassi for malaria research.

## Terms
- **Germ theory of disease**: The theory that many diseases are caused by the presence and action of specific microorganisms.
- **Pure culture**: A laboratory culture containing only a single species or strain of microorganism.
- **Koch's postulates**: A set of four criteria designed to establish a causative relationship between a microbe and a disease.
- **Tuberculin**: A protein extract from *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, originally tested as a treatment but now used as a diagnostic tool.
- **Agar**: A polysaccharide derived from algae used as a solidifying agent in culture media for growing bacteria.
- **Petri dish**: A shallow, lidded dish used to culture microorganisms, developed by Julius Richard Petri based on Koch's methods.
- **Bacillus anthracis**: The bacterium that causes anthrax.
- **Mycobacterium tuberculosis**: The bacterium that causes tuberculosis.
- **Vibrio cholerae**: The bacterium that causes cholera.

## Debates and open questions
- **Tuberculin's efficacy**: Koch's initial claim that tuberculin was a cure for tuberculosis was a major scientific failure and scandal, though it later found use in diagnosis.
- **Human vs. bovine tuberculosis**: Koch's assertion that human and cattle tuberculosis bacilli were identical and that cattle posed no health risk was later proven incorrect, leading to significant public health implications.
- **Nobel Prize arbitration**: Koch's role as a "neutral arbitrator" in the 1902 Nobel Prize dispute between Ronald Ross and Giovanni Battista Grassi was criticized as biased due to his personal conflict with Grassi.
