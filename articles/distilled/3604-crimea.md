# Crimea

## Overview
Crimea is a peninsula in Eastern Europe on the northern coast of the Black Sea, historically a crossroads between the classical world and the Eurasian steppe. Its strategic location has made it a contested territory for empires from antiquity to the present. The region is internationally recognized as part of Ukraine but has been under Russian occupation since 2014.

## Timeline
- **c. 5th century BCE** — Greek colonies established on the southern coast.
- **13th century** — The interior falls to the Mongol Golden Horde.
- **1443–1783** — The Crimean Khanate exists, later as an Ottoman dependency.
- **1783** — The Russian Empire annexes Crimea after the Russo-Turkish War.
- **1854–1856** — The Crimean War is fought on the peninsula.
- **1921** — The Crimean Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic is created.
- **1944** — Soviet authorities deport the Crimean Tatars and other groups.
- **1954** — The USSR transfers Crimea from the Russian SFSR to the Ukrainian SSR.
- **1991** — Following Ukrainian independence, Crimea becomes the Autonomous Republic of Crimea.
- **2014** — Russian forces occupy and annex Crimea, a move not recognized by most countries.

## Body

### Historical Geography and Name
The peninsula, historically called the Tauric Peninsula, has been at the boundary between settled and nomadic civilizations. Its recorded history begins with Greek colonies on its south coast in the 5th century BCE, which were later absorbed by Roman and Byzantine Empires. The interior was controlled by various steppe nomads, culminating in the Mongol Golden Horde in the 13th century. The Crimean Khanate emerged from the Horde in the 15th century and became an Ottoman dependency. The name "Crimea" derives from the Turkic term *Qırım*, while the classical name "Tauric" comes from the peninsula's ancient Tauri inhabitants.

### Imperial and Soviet Eras
After defeating the Ottoman Empire, the Russian Empire annexed Crimea in 1783, significantly increasing its Black Sea power. The peninsula's strategic value led to the Crimean War (1854–1856). Following the 1917 Russian Revolution, Crimea became an autonomous Soviet republic. During World War II, it was occupied by Germany. After Soviet reconquest in 1944, Stalin ordered the ethnic cleansing and deportation of Crimean Tatars and other nationalities, an act described as cultural genocide. Crimea was downgraded to an oblast in 1945. In 1954, the USSR transferred the oblast to the Ukrainian SSR on the 300th anniversary of the Pereyaslav Treaty.

### Modern Status and Occupation
After Ukrainian independence in 1991, most of the peninsula was reorganized as the Autonomous Republic of Crimea. A 1997 treaty allowed Russia to base its Black Sea Fleet in Sevastopol. In 2014, following political upheaval in Ukraine, Russian forces occupied the peninsula. A referendum, deemed illegal and unrecognized internationally, was held, and Russia annexed Crimea. Most countries continue to recognize Crimea as Ukrainian territory. The annexation led to international sanctions targeting Crimea's economy, particularly its tourism industry.

### Geography and Climate
Covering 27,000 km², Crimea is almost an island, connected to the mainland by the Isthmus of Perekop. It is geographically divided into three zones: the northern steppe, the Crimean Mountains, and the fertile Southern Coast. The climate varies from moderate continental in the north to subtropical Mediterranean on the south coast, making it a historic resort destination. The peninsula's hydrography includes over 250 rivers, the largest being the Salhyr, and numerous salt lakes.

### Economy and Infrastructure
The economy relies on agriculture (cereals, wine-making), fishing, industry, and tourism. Following the 2014 annexation and sanctions, tourism initially declined but later rebounded. Key infrastructure includes the Crimean Bridge, opened in 2018, which links the peninsula to Russia's Krasnodar Krai. The peninsula has its own electricity generation but has relied on an underwater power cable from Russia since 2014. Transport networks include railways, highways, and the world's longest trolleybus route (96 km) between Simferopol and Yalta.

### Demographics and Culture
The population is approximately 2.2 million. According to the 2014 Russian census, 84% named Russian as their native language. Crimean Tatars, a Muslim minority, were forcibly deported in 1944 but have been returning since the Soviet collapse. The culture reflects its layered history, with influences from Greek, Tatar, Ottoman, and Russian traditions. Notable cultural figures include the poet Adam Mickiewicz and the painter Ivan Aivazovsky. Crimean Tatar singer Jamala won Eurovision 2016 with a song about the 1944 deportations.

## Terms
- **Isthmus of Perekop**: A narrow strip of land connecting Crimea to mainland Ukraine.
- **Crimean Khanate**: A Turkic state that emerged from the Golden Horde and was an Ottoman dependency from the 15th to 18th centuries.
- **Crimean Tatars**: A Turkic ethnic group indigenous to Crimea, subjected to mass deportation by the Soviet Union in 1944.
- **Autonomous Republic of Crimea**: The political entity Crimea was organized into after Ukrainian independence in 1991.
- **Crimean Bridge**: A 19 km road-rail bridge opened in 2018, connecting Crimea to Russia's Krasnodar Krai.
- **Syvash**: A system of shallow lagoons separating the Crimean Peninsula from the Ukrainian mainland.
- **Tauric Peninsula**: The classical name for Crimea, derived from the ancient Tauri people.
- **Golden Horde**: A Mongol and later Turkicized khanate that controlled the Crimean interior in the 13th–15th centuries.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The legality of the 2014 referendum and subsequent Russian annexation is not recognized by most of the international community, with Ukraine and over 100 UN member states affirming Crimea as Ukrainian territory.
- The status and rights of the Crimean Tatar population, particularly regarding cultural and educational autonomy, remain a significant issue following their historical deportations and ongoing return.
- The long-term geopolitical implications of the annexation and the resulting sanctions on Crimea's economy and infrastructure are subjects of ongoing analysis.
