# Mercury (planet)

## Overview
Mercury is the smallest and innermost planet in the Solar System, a rocky terrestrial body with a massive iron core comprising roughly 57% of its volume. It possesses a tenuous exosphere rather than a true atmosphere, resulting in extreme surface temperatures ranging from −170 °C to 420 °C. Mercury rotates in a 3:2 spin–orbit resonance, meaning it spins three times for every two orbits (88 Earth days), producing a solar day of 176 Earth days. Its highly eccentric orbit and near-zero axial tilt create permanently shadowed polar craters where water ice persists. Its mantle is highly homogeneous, which suggests that Mercury had a magma ocean early in its history, like the Moon. Mercury is expected to be destroyed, along with Venus, and possibly the Earth and the Moon, when the Sun becomes a red giant in approximately seven or eight billion years. Only three missions—Mariner 10, MESSENGER, and BepiColombo—have visited Mercury due to the extreme delta-v required to reach the Sun's deep gravity well.

## Timeline
- **c. 14th century BC** — Earliest recorded observations on Assyrian MUL.APIN tablets ("the jumping planet").
- **350 BC** — Ancient Greeks recognize morning and evening "stars" as a single planet (Hermes/Stilbon).
- **1610** — Thomas Harriot and Galileo make first telescopic observations.
- **1631** — Pierre Gassendi observes first predicted transit of Mercury across the Sun.
- **1859** — Urbain Le Verrier identifies anomalous perihelion precession unexplained by Newtonian mechanics.
- **1965** — Radar observations (Pettengill & Dyce) determine 59-day rotation period; Giuseppe Colombo proposes 3:2 spin–orbit resonance.
- **1974–1975** — Mariner 10 conducts three flybys, mapping <45% of surface and discovering magnetic field.
- **1991** — Radar observations reveal high-reflectivity polar deposits consistent with water ice.
- **2004–2015** — MESSENGER mission launches, orbits Mercury (2011–2015), maps entire surface, confirms polar ice and volcanic history.
- **2018–present** — BepiColombo (ESA/JAXA) launches; completes six flybys (2021–2025); orbital insertion planned for 2026.

## Body

### Physical Characteristics and Internal Structure
Mercury is a terrestrial planet with an equatorial radius of 2,439.7 km, smaller than the moons Ganymede and Titan but significantly denser (5.427 g/cm³). Its high uncompressed density (5.3 g/cm³ vs. Earth’s 4.4 g/cm³) implies a massive iron-rich core. The core radius is estimated at 2,020 ± 30 km, occupying ~57% of the planet's volume (vs. 17% for Earth). The mantle-crust layer is ~420 km thick, with a crust estimated at 35 km (probe data) or 26 ± 11 km (isostasy model). According to current models, Mercury may have a solid silicate crust and mantle overlaying a solid outer core, a deeper liquid core layer, and a solid inner core. The mantle is highly homogeneous, which suggests that Mercury had a magma ocean early in its history, like the Moon. The planet's mantle is chemically heterogeneous, suggesting the planet went through a magma ocean phase early in its history. Crystallization of minerals and convective overturn resulted in a layered, chemically heterogeneous crust with large-scale variations in chemical composition observed on the surface.

Three hypotheses explain the large core: (1) a giant impact stripped the primordial mantle; (2) extreme protosun temperatures vaporized surface rock ("rock vapor" atmosphere lost to solar wind); (3) solar nebula drag preferentially removed lighter particles during accretion. MESSENGER found high surface potassium and sulfur—volatiles that would have been driven off by the heat of hypotheses 1 or 2—favoring hypothesis 3, though BepiColombo (arriving 2026) will test further.

### Surface Geology
Mercury’s surface resembles the Moon: heavily cratered, with inter-crater plains (oldest surfaces) and younger smooth plains (likely volcanic, filling basins). It is more heterogeneous than the Moon or Mars. Key features include:
*   **Impact basins:** 46 identified. Caloris Planitia (1,550 km diameter) is the largest; its antipode hosts "Weird Terrain" (hilly, fractured ground) likely formed by converging seismic waves or ejecta. Tolstoj and Beethoven are other major multi-ring basins.
*   **Compressional features (rupes):** Lobate scarps up to 1,000 km long and 3 km high, formed by global contraction as the core cooled. Mapping suggests a radius reduction of 1–7 km. Major thrust faulting ended ~3.6–3.7 Ga, but small scarps (<50 Myr old) indicate ongoing contraction.
*   **Volcanism:** 51 pyroclastic deposits identified (90% in craters), indicating prolonged explosive activity. A compound volcano (9+ overlapping vents) exists in Caloris Basin. Smooth plains post-date Caloris ejecta.

### Surface Conditions and Exosphere
Surface temperatures range 100–700 K (−173 to 427 °C). At perihelion, the subsolar point reaches 700 K; at aphelion, 550 K. Nightside averages 110 K. Despite proximity to the Sun, Mercury is second-hottest after Venus (which traps heat via atmosphere). Near-zero axial tilt (0.027°) creates permanent polar shadows. Radar-bright deposits in polar craters confirmed by MESSENGER as water ice (~10¹⁴–10¹⁵ kg), likely sourced from cometary impacts or interior outgassing, possibly insulated by regolith.

The exosphere (surface pressure <0.5 nPa) contains H, He, O, Na, Ca, K, Mg, Si, OH. It is unstable—atoms continuously lost and replenished. Sources: solar wind (H, He), radioactive decay (He, Na, K), micrometeorite vaporization (Na, K, Ca; currently enhanced by Comet Encke), and ice sublimation (H₂O). High abundances of O⁺, OH⁻, H₃O⁺ suggest solar wind sputtering releases water-related ions from the surface/exosphere.

### Magnetic Field and Magnetosphere
Mercury possesses a global, dipolar magnetic field ~1.1% of Earth’s strength (300 nT at equator), aligned near the spin axis (10° tilt). Generated by a dynamo in the liquid outer core, sustained by tidal heating from high orbital eccentricity. The magnetosphere deflects solar wind but is "leaky": MESSENGER discovered flux transfer events ("magnetic tornadoes" up to 800 km wide) allowing solar wind direct surface access via magnetic reconnection at a rate 10× higher than Earth’s.

### Orbit, Rotation, and Longitude
Mercury has the most eccentric planetary orbit (e=0.21), ranging 46–70 million km from the Sun. Orbital period: 87.969 Earth days. The 3:2 spin–orbit resonance (sidereal day 58.65 days; solar day 176 days) is stabilized by eccentricity: at perihelion, tidal torque peaks, locking the long axis toward the Sun. This resonance was confirmed by radar in 1965, overturning the synchronous rotation hypothesis.

Orbital inclination is 7° to the ecliptic (highest of planets), allowing transits only in May/November (~every 7 years). The 3:2 resonance causes unique solar apparent motion: at two "hot poles" (0°W, 180°W), the Sun reverses direction near perihelion, passing overhead three times over ~16 Earth days, creating the hottest surface points. At two "cold poles" (90°W, 270°W), the Sun passes overhead only at aphelion.

Longitude zero is defined at a hot pole (Hun Kal crater center = 20°W). Perihelion precession is 5,600″/century (Earth-relative); Newtonian mechanics predicts 5,557″. The 43″ discrepancy was explained by Einstein’s general relativity (1915). Simulations show Mercury’s eccentricity varies chaotically (0–0.45); a future Jupiter resonance gives a 1% chance of orbit destabilization in 5 Gyr (collision with Sun/Venus, ejection, or inner system disruption).

### Observation History
Ancient records: Assyrian (14th c. BC), Babylonian (Nabu), Greek (Hermes/Stilbon), Chinese (Chen-xing/Water Star), Hindu (Budha), Maya (owl messenger). Medieval Islamic astronomers (al-Zarqālī, 11th c.) noted oval geocentric orbit; Ibn Bajjah (12th c.) observed possible transits. Nilakantha Somayaji (15th c. India) developed a partially heliocentric model.

Telescopic era: Harriot/Galileo (1610); Marius (1612) noted phases; Gassendi (1631) observed Kepler-predicted transit; Zupi (1639) confirmed solar orbit. Schröter (1800) claimed 20 km mountains; Bessel erroneously derived 24-hr rotation/70° tilt. Schiaparelli (1880s) mapped albedo features, proposed 88-day synchronous rotation. Antoniadi (1934) published definitive maps (source of many feature names).

Radar breakthrough: Kotelnikov (USSR, 1962) first radar bounce; Pettengill/Dyce (Arecibo, 1965) proved 59-day rotation. Colombo immediately interpreted as 3:2 resonance. Radar later mapped polar ice deposits. Optical ground-based imaging improved with lucky imaging (Mt. Wilson, 2000).

### Space Probe Exploration
Reaching Mercury requires high delta-v (decelerating into Sun’s gravity well); aerobraking impossible (no atmosphere). Only three missions:
*   **Mariner 10 (1974–75):** First gravity assist (Venus); three flybys, closest 327 km. Mapped <45% surface (same face lit each pass). Discovered magnetic field and compressional scarps.
*   **MESSENGER (2004–15):** Multiple gravity assists (Earth, Venus×2, Mercury×3); orbital insertion March 2011. Mapped 100% surface, confirmed polar ice, characterized exosphere/magnetosphere, found high K/S (refuting giant impact/vaporization), measured core structure via gravity. Crashed April 30, 2015.
*   **BepiColombo (2018–present):** ESA/JAXA dual orbiter (mapper + magnetosphere probe). Six flybys completed (2021–2025); orbital insertion 2026. Will test formation hypotheses via surface composition (IR, UV, X-ray, gamma-ray spectrometry).

## Terms
- ****Spin–orbit resonance**** — A gravitational coupling where a body's rotation period and orbital period are locked in a ratio of small integers (Mercury: 3 rotations per 2 orbits).
- ****Exosphere**** — A tenuous, collisionless atmosphere where particles follow ballistic trajectories; surface-bounded on Mercury (pressure <0.5 nPa).
- ****Rupes (pl. rupēs)**** — Escarpment or cliff; on Mercury, lobate scarps formed by thrust faults from global contraction.
- ****Caloris Planitia**** — Largest impact basin on Mercury (1,550 km diameter); surrounded by mountainous ring and filled with smooth plains.
- ****Weird Terrain**** — Hilly, fractured region at the antipode of Caloris Basin, likely formed by converging seismic waves or ejecta from the impact.
- ****Flux transfer event**** — Twisted magnetic flux tube connecting planetary field to interplanetary space ("magnetic tornado"), allowing solar wind entry via reconnection.
- ****Perihelion precession**** — Gradual rotation of the orbit's closest approach point; Mercury's anomalous 43″/century excess confirmed general relativity.
- ****Delta-v**** — Change in velocity required for spacecraft maneuvers; exceptionally high for Mercury due to deep solar gravity well.
- ****Airy isostasy**** — Model where crustal thickness varies to maintain gravitational equilibrium (like icebergs); used to estimate Mercury's crustal thickness (26 ± 11 km).
- ****Late Heavy Bombardment**** — Hypothesized period (~4.1–3.8 Ga) of intense impact cratering in the inner Solar System; Mercury shows evidence of this epoch.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Formation mechanism:** Giant impact, protosun vaporization, or nebular drag? MESSENGER's high volatile (K, S) data challenges the first two; BepiColombo will provide decisive compositional data.
*   **Core state and composition:** Exact radii of solid inner core, liquid outer core, and solid FeS layer; light element budget (Si, S, C) in the core.
*   **Volcanic timeline:** Duration and cessation of effusive vs. explosive volcanism; age of the Caloris compound volcano (~1 Ga?).
*   **Polar ice origin:** Cometary delivery vs. interior outgassing; total inventory and stratigraphy (regolith cover thickness).
*   **Magnetic field generation:** Detailed dynamo operation in a thin liquid shell; cause of high reconnection rate and "leaky" magnetosphere.
*   **Long-term orbital stability:** 1% chance of chaotic destabilization via Jupiter resonance over 5 Gyr; potential fates (Sun impact, Venus collision, ejection).
