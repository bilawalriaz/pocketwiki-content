# Peace of Westphalia

## Overview
The Peace of Westphalia (1648) refers to two treaties signed in Osnabrück and Münster that ended the Thirty Years' War in the Holy Roman Empire and the Eighty Years' War between Spain and the Dutch Republic. It reshaped the political and religious landscape of Europe, establishing new territorial boundaries and principles of religious coexistence. The peace is often cited as a foundational moment in modern international relations, though its direct role in creating the concept of state sovereignty is debated by historians.

## Body

### Background and Negotiations
Europe was exhausted by decades of warfare, primarily the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) and the overlapping Eighty Years' War. The Thirty Years' War, initially a conflict between Catholic and Protestant states within the Holy Roman Empire, evolved into a broader struggle for European dominance, pitting the Catholic Habsburgs (ruling Austria and Spain) against a coalition led by Protestant Sweden and Catholic France. The immense death toll, estimated between 4.5 and 8 million, created an urgent need for peace. Negotiations began in Cologne in 1636 but were complex and protracted. They eventually moved to the Westphalian cities of Münster and Osnabrück, chosen as neutral, demilitarized zones. Münster hosted talks between the Empire and France, while Osnabrück hosted talks between the Empire and Sweden. A total of 109 delegations participated between 1643 and 1649, though they never met in a single plenary session.

### Treaties and Religious Settlements
The peace was formalized in two separate treaties: the Treaty of Münster (between the Emperor and France and their allies) and the Treaty of Osnabrück (between the Emperor and Sweden and their allies). A central achievement was the reorganization of the Holy Roman Empire. The treaties guaranteed the right to practice Catholicism, Lutheranism, and Calvinism, finally granting legal recognition to Calvinism. Crucially, they reinterpreted the earlier Peace of Augsburg (1555). Instead of the principle of *cuius regio, eius religio* (the ruler determines the state religion), Westphalia established that subjects were no longer forced to follow their ruler's religion. The normative date for determining a state's official religion was set to 1 January 1624. The Holy See, under Pope Innocent X, vehemently condemned the settlement.

### Territorial and Political Adjustments
The treaties made significant territorial changes. France gained bishoprics near Lorraine and cities in Alsace. Sweden received Western Pomerania, Wismar, and the bishoprics of Bremen and Verden, giving it a seat in the Imperial Diet. Brandenburg-Prussia received Farther Pomerania and other bishoprics. The Palatinate was divided, with Bavaria retaining the Electoral vote it had gained in 1623 and a new, eighth vote created for the re-established Elector Palatine. The Swiss Confederacy gained formal legal independence from the Empire. The treaties also reformed the Imperial Chamber Court, mandating that half its judges be Protestant. France and Sweden were recognized as guarantors of the imperial constitution with a right to intercede.

## Terms
*   **Thirty Years' War (1618–1648):** A devastating series of conflicts in Central Europe, initially fought between Catholic and Protestant states within the Holy Roman Empire, which evolved into a wider European power struggle.
*   **Eighty Years' War (c. 1568–1648):** The Dutch revolt against Spanish rule, which concluded with the recognition of the Dutch Republic's independence.
*   **Holy Roman Empire:** A multi-ethnic complex of territories in Western and Central Europe that existed from the Early Middle Ages until 1806.
*   **Habsburgs:** A European royal dynasty that ruled the Holy Roman Empire, Spain, and other territories.
*   ***Cuius regio, eius religio***: A Latin phrase meaning "whose realm, his religion," the principle from the Peace of Augsburg (1555) that allowed a ruler to determine the official religion of their state.
*   ***Ius reformandi***: The right of a ruler to determine the religion of their state, a principle modified by the Peace of Westphalia.
*   **Imperial Diet:** The deliberative body of the Holy Roman Empire, representing its various states.
*   **Westphalian sovereignty:** A concept in international relations holding that each state has exclusive sovereignty over its territory and domestic affairs, often (but controversially) attributed to the Peace of Westphalia.

## Debates and Open Questions
A major scholarly debate concerns the concept of "Westphalian sovereignty." Some international relations scholars identify the treaties as the origin of modern principles like state sovereignty and non-interference. However, many historians challenge this as the "Westphalian myth," arguing that the treaties' text does not establish these as new international law principles. They contend that the sovereignty discussed was internal to the Holy Roman Empire's constitutional arrangements and that the modern state system emerged later. Regardless of its direct legal impact, the narrative of the Peace of Westphalia has profoundly shaped international political and legal thought for centuries.
