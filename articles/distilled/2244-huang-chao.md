# Huang Chao

## Overview
Huang Chao (835–884) was a wealthy salt trader who led the Huang Chao Rebellion (874–884), a massive uprising that fatally weakened the Tang dynasty. His capture of the capital Chang'an and brief reign as emperor of the Qi dynasty marked the dynasty's point of no return, after which real power shifted decisively to regional military governors (*jiedushi*).

## Timeline
- **835** — Huang Chao is born into a family of wealthy salt smugglers.
- **Mid-870s** — Joins Wang Xianzhi's rebellion against the Tang dynasty.
- **881** — Captures Tang capital Chang'an; Emperor Xizong flees.
- **881** — Proclaims himself emperor of the new Qi dynasty.
- **Spring 881** — His forces are defeated at Fengxiang Circuit by Tang loyalist Zheng Tian.
- **Winter 882** — Shatuo general Li Keyong arrives to aid Tang forces.
- **Spring 883** — Li Keyong and Tang allies defeat a major Qi army (150,000 men).
- **Summer 883** — Li Keyong enters Chang'an; Huang Chao flees east.
- **884** — Defeated by former subordinates Zhu Wen and Shang Rang, who had surrendered to Tang.
- **July 13, 884** — Killed (or commits suicide) in Langhu Valley; his Qi dynasty ends.

## Body

### Background and Rebellion
The Tang dynasty, though still capable of military campaigns after the An Lushan Rebellion (755–763), suffered from increased power of provincial military governors (*jiedushi*) and widespread public grievance. Impoverished farmers, tax-burdened landowners, and merchants, including large salt smuggling networks, fueled mid-9th century rebellions. Huang Chao, from a family that grew wealthy from illegal salt trade, was skilled in arms and writing but failed the imperial examinations multiple times, which hardened his resolve to rebel. He joined Wang Xianzhi's uprising in the mid-870s.

### Emperor of Qi and Fall
After splitting from Wang, Huang's army moved south, conquering Guangzhou. In 881, his forces captured Chang'an, forcing Emperor Xizong to flee. Huang declared himself emperor of the Qi dynasty, appointing his wife Empress Cao and officials like Shang Rang as chancellors. He attempted to co-opt the Tang administrative system, keeping lower-ranking officials but executing those who refused submission. He also tried to win over Tang regional generals, with mixed success. His regime faced persistent Tang resistance, notably from Zheng Tian at Fengxiang. The turning point came with the arrival of the Shatuo general Li Keyong. In 883, Li Keyong's forces defeated a large Qi army and entered Chang'an, forcing Huang to abandon the city. After successive defeats, including by former followers Zhu Wen and Shang Rang, Huang was cornered. He died in July 884 in Langhu Valley, reportedly killed by his nephew Lin Yan, though alternative accounts suggest suicide.

### Legacy and Legend
Huang Chao's rebellion permanently destroyed Tang central authority, accelerating the rise of autonomous military governors and leading to the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period. Legends emerged after his death, possibly as decoys, suggesting he escaped and became a Buddhist monk. He was also a poet; one of his famous lines, "The capital's full of golden armored soldiers," expressed his rebellious anger and was later referenced in Chinese culture.

## Terms
- **Jiedushi**: Provincial military governors who gained significant autonomous power during the late Tang dynasty.
- **Salt monopoly**: The state-controlled salt trade, which generated revenue but also fueled smuggling and rebellion when enforced harshly.
- **Shatuo**: A Turkic people whose military leaders, like Li Keyong, played a crucial role in late Tang and subsequent dynastic conflicts.
- **Qi dynasty**: The short-lived state (881–884) proclaimed by Huang Chao after capturing Chang'an.
- **Nine-rank system**: The Tang civil service classification system used to rank officials, which Huang Chao attempted to manipulate.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause of death**: Historical accounts conflict on whether Huang Chao was killed by his nephew Lin Yan or committed suicide to enable his army's surrender.
- **Escape theory**: Some speculate that the heads presented were decoys, allowing Huang Chao to escape and live as a monk, based on the distance to Shi Pu's camp and the resemblance of his brothers.
- **Impact on Tang collapse**: Scholars debate the precise degree to which Huang Chao's rebellion, versus the prior rise of *jiedushi*, was the primary cause of the Tang dynasty's fall.
