# Chaldea

## Overview
Chaldea was a region in the marshy southeastern corner of Mesopotamia, home to a group of West Semitic migrants who arrived between the 11th and 9th centuries BC. While the Chaldeans were not native to Mesopotamia, they eventually rose to dominate Babylonia, founding the Neo-Babylonian Empire (626–539 BC) that destroyed the Assyrian Empire. The term "Chaldean" later lost its ethnic meaning, becoming a classical designation for astrologers and astronomers.

## Timeline
- **c. 1100–900 BC** — West Semitic tribes, including the Chaldeans (Kaldu), migrate into southeastern Babylonia from the Levant.
- **852 BC** — First historical attestation: Assyrian king Shalmaneser III subjugates Chaldean tribes.
- **780 BC** — Chaldean Marduk-apla-usur usurps the Babylonian throne, setting a precedent for future bids.
- **748 BC** — Native Babylonian king Nabonassar overthrows Chaldean usurpers.
- **721–710 BC** — Chaldean Marduk-apla-iddina II (Merodach-Baladan) briefly seizes Babylon with Elamite support.
- **626 BC** — Chaldean Nabopolassar seizes Babylon, beginning the Neo-Babylonian Empire.
- **612 BC** — Alliance of Babylonians, Medes, and others sacks the Assyrian capital Nineveh.
- **605 BC** — Nebuchadnezzar II (Nabopolassar's son) defeats Egypt at Carchemish, securing the empire.
- **587 BC** — Nebuchadnezzar II captures Jerusalem, destroys Solomon's Temple, and deports Jews to Babylon.
- **539 BC** — Persian Cyrus the Great conquers Babylon, ending the Neo-Babylonian Empire.

## Body

### Origins and Settlement
The Chaldeans were latecomers to Mesopotamia, distinct from the native East Semitic Akkadian-speaking Babylonians and Assyrians. They were West Semitic-speaking migrants from the Levant who arrived during a period of Babylonian weakness, settling in the marshy, alluvial plains of the far southeast. Their territory, often called *mat Kaldi* or *Bit Yâkin* (the name of their largest tribe), was centered on the lower Euphrates and Tigris. They were semi-nomadic and initially had little political impact, living under the domination of native Babylonian and later Assyrian kings.

### Rise and the Neo-Babylonian Empire
Chaldean leaders repeatedly attempted to seize the Babylonian throne, typically by exploiting periods of Assyrian internal strife and allying with stronger powers like Elam. Their greatest success came after the death of the powerful Assyrian king Ashurbanipal in 627 BC, which triggered a devastating civil war. The Chaldean chieftain Nabopolassar seized Babylon in 626 BC and, after years of warfare, allied with the Medes to destroy the Assyrian Empire by 612 BC. His son, Nebuchadnezzar II, became the empire's most famous ruler, rebuilding Babylon into a legendary city and conducting extensive military campaigns, including the destruction of Jerusalem.

### Language and Cultural Assimilation
The ancient Chaldeans likely spoke a West Semitic language similar to Old Aramaic. However, they rapidly assimilated into the dominant Mesopotamian culture. By the time of their empire, they had adopted Akkadian names, religion, and customs. The use of Imperial Aramaic as a lingua franca across Mesopotamia further integrated them. By the fall of Babylon in 539 BC, the Chaldeans had ceased to exist as a distinct ethnic group, fully blending into the native population.

### Legacy and the Term "Chaldean"
After the Persian conquest, "Chaldean" lost its meaning as an ethnic or geographic term. It survived as a classical label for a societal class of astrologers and astronomers in southern Mesopotamia, a usage found in the Book of Daniel and by Greek and Roman writers. In late antiquity, the term was misapplied by Jerome to the Aramaic dialect used in parts of the Bible. Later, it was adopted by Rome for a 15th-century church union, leading to the modern Chaldean Catholic Church. Historians generally consider its adherents to be ethnic Assyrians, not descendants of the ancient Chaldean tribes.

## Terms
*   **Chaldea (Kaldu):** A region in southeastern Mesopotamia and the name of the migrant tribes who settled there.
*   **Bit Yâkin:** The largest and most powerful of the five main Chaldean tribes.
*   **Neo-Babylonian Empire:** The empire (626–539 BC) founded by the Chaldean dynasty after the fall of Assyria.
*   **Akkadian:** The East Semitic language family native to Mesopotamia, including Babylonian and Assyrian dialects.
*   **Imperial Aramaic:** The lingua franca of the Neo-Assyrian Empire, which became widespread across the region.
*   **Akkadianized:** The process by which the Chaldeans adopted the language, religion, and customs of the native Mesopotamians.
*   **Ur of the Chaldees (Ur Kaśdim):** A city cited in the Hebrew Bible as the starting point of Abraham's journey.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The precise location and original extent of the Chaldean homeland, particularly the territory of Bit Yâkin, is not known precisely.
*   The ethnic identity of later rulers like Neriglissar and Labashi-Marduk is unclear; they may have been native Babylonians rather than ethnic Chaldeans.
*   The modern use of "Chaldean" to denote a separate ethnicity from Assyrians is a point of contention. Historians and international organizations generally treat Chaldeans as ethnic Assyrians, based on genetic, linguistic, and historical factors, while some activists advocate for a distinct identity.
