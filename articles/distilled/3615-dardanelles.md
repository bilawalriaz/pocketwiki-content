# Dardanelles

## Overview
The Dardanelles is a narrow, natural strait in northwestern Turkey that forms part of the continental boundary between Asia and Europe. It connects the Aegean Sea to the Sea of Marmara, and by extension via the Bosporus, to the Black Sea, making it a crucial international waterway for maritime trade and a site of major historical and military significance.

## Timeline
- **c. 480 BC** — Persian King Xerxes I builds pontoon bridges across the strait to invade Greece.
- **334 BC** — Alexander the Great crosses the strait from Europe to Asia.
- **1354** — The Ottoman Empire conquers Gallipoli, gaining control of the strait.
- **1841** — The London Straits Convention limits passage to Turkish warships in peacetime.
- **1915** — Allied forces launch the Gallipoli campaign to force the straits; they are repulsed.
- **1920** — The Treaty of Sèvres demilitarizes the strait and places it under League of Nations control.
- **1923** — The Treaty of Lausanne restores the straits to Turkish territory but requires demilitarization.
- **1936** — The Montreux Convention, still in force, allows Turkey to regulate naval traffic.
- **2022** — The 1915 Çanakkale Bridge, the first fixed crossing, opens; the strait is closed to foreign warships due to the Russian invasion of Ukraine.

## Body
### Geography and Strategic Importance
The Dardanelles is 61 km long and 1.2 to 6 km wide, with a maximum depth of 103 meters. Its narrow, winding shape and strong currents make navigation hazardous. The strait is the sole maritime connection between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean, giving it immense geostrategic value for controlling access to and from the Black Sea nations. Its shores have been fought over for millennia, from the Trojan War to the Gallipoli Campaign.

### Ancient and Byzantine Eras
The strait, known to the ancient Greeks as the Hellespont ("Sea of Helle"), was central to myths like the Golden Fleece and Hero and Leander. The ancient city of Troy controlled its western entrance. It was crossed by Persian and Macedonian armies for invasions. During the Byzantine period, the strait was vital for the defense of Constantinople and a regulated source of customs revenue.

### Ottoman and Modern Eras
Ottoman control from 1354 was challenged in the 19th century as Russia sought access. The 1841 London Straits Convention established international rules, limiting passage to Turkish warships in peacetime. The failed 1915 Allied Gallipoli campaign was a pivotal moment in World War I. Following the war, the strait's status was internationalized by the Treaty of Sèvres (1920), then returned to Turkey with demilitarization conditions by the Treaty of Lausanne (1923). The current legal regime is the Montreux Convention (1936), which grants Turkey control over naval transit. The strait remains a critical route for oil exports and was closed to foreign warships in 2022.

### Crossings
For most of history, the strait was crossed only by ferry. The first fixed land crossing, the 1915 Çanakkale Bridge, opened in March 2022. The strait is also crossed by submarine power and communication cables.

## Terms
- **Strait:** A narrow passage of water connecting two larger bodies of water.
- **Pontoon bridge:** A floating bridge made of boats or barges.
- **Demilitarized zone:** An area where military forces and installations are prohibited.
- **Montreux Convention:** A 1936 international agreement governing the Turkish Straits, giving Turkey control over naval traffic.
- **Geostrategic:** Relating to the strategic importance of a geographic location.
- **Pre-dreadnought battleship:** An early type of battleship, obsolete by World War I.
- **Belligerent:** A state or person engaged in a war or conflict.
