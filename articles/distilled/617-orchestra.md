# Orchestra

## Overview
An orchestra is a large instrumental ensemble typical of classical music or jazz, combining instruments from four main families: strings, woodwinds, brass, and percussion. The term derives from the Greek *orchēstra*, the area in front of an ancient Greek theatre stage reserved for the chorus. Evolving from non-standardized Baroque groups to the massive forces required by Wagner and Mahler, the modern symphony orchestra typically employs 70–100 musicians led by a conductor. It serves as the primary vehicle for symphonies, concertos, opera, ballet, and film scores. The term "orchestra" can also be applied to a jazz ensemble, for example in the performance of big-band music. Historically male-dominated, major orchestras adopted screened auditions in the late 20th century to address gender imbalance, while financial pressures have recently forced several U.S. ensembles into bankruptcy.

## Timeline
- **1600–1750** — Baroque era: orchestras non-standardized; led by concertmaster or harpsichordist playing basso continuo.
- **c. 1750–1820** — Classical era: instrumentation standardizes around paired winds and strings (Haydn, Mozart).
- **Early 1800s** — Beethoven expands the "standard complement" (paired winds/brass), adding piccolo, trombones, and contrabassoon for specific symphonies.
- **1815** — Heinrich Stölzel and Friedrich Blühmel invent piston and rotary valves, enabling fully chromatic brass.
- **Mid–Late 1800s** — Richard Wagner founds Bayreuth orchestra; expands forces (e.g., six harps, Wagner tubas) and redefines conductor's role.
- **Early 1900s** — Gustav Mahler pushes orchestral size to extremes (Symphony No. 8); recording technology raises performance standards.
- **1922** — Persimfans formed in Soviet Union as a conductorless, committee-led orchestra; disbanded after ten years.
- **1997** — Vienna Philharmonic admits first female permanent member (harpist Anna Lelkes) after protests.
- **2010–2011** — Multiple U.S. orchestras (Philadelphia, Louisville, Honolulu, New Mexico, Syracuse) file for bankruptcy.

## Body

### Definition and Core Instrumentation
An orchestra combines instruments from four sections. The **string section** (violins divided into first and second, violas, cellos, double basses) forms the core. The **woodwind section** typically uses pairs of flutes, oboes, clarinets, and bassoons, with auxiliary instruments (piccolo, cor anglais, bass clarinet, contrabassoon, saxophone) added as needed. The **brass section** comprises horns, trumpets, trombones, and tuba, sometimes with cornet and euphonium. The **percussion section** centers on timpani plus auxiliary instruments (snare, cymbals, triangle, tambourine, tam-tam, mallet instruments). Harp and keyboard instruments (piano, harpsichord, pipe organ, celesta) appear regularly; electric/electronic instruments and guitars are used in modern repertoire.

### Historical Evolution of Size and Standardization
**Baroque (1600–1750):** No standard size or instrumentation. Bach’s Köthen ensemble had ~18 players; Corelli’s Rome orchestra ranged 35–80, expanding to 150 for festivals. Leadership fell to the concertmaster or a **basso continuo** (harmonic bass line) player on harpsichord/organ.
**Classical (c. 1750–1820):** Standardization around a small string section and paired winds (oboes, flutes, bassoons, horns), occasionally supplemented by clarinets, trumpets, and timpani.
**Beethoven’s Expansion:** Beethoven solidified the doubled-wind model and calculated expansions: third horn in *Eroica* (No. 3) for harmonic flexibility; piccolo, contrabassoon, and trombones for the triumphal finale of No. 5; trombones for the storm in No. 6 (*Pastoral*); four horns, chorus, and expanded percussion in No. 9. This model dominated for decades.
**Romantic Expansion:** Valved brass (1815) and Boehm-system woodwinds enabled chromatic playing. Berlioz wrote the first systematic treatise on instrumentation. Wagner’s Bayreuth orchestra demanded unprecedented forces (six harps in *Das Rheingold*, Wagner tubas) and elevated the conductor to an interpretive authority. Mahler later required massive ensembles (Symphony No. 8).
**20th/21st Century:** Recording technology exposed minor errors, raising execution standards. Instrumentation became flexible; composers hand-pick forces, adding synthesizers, *ondes martenot*, non-Western instruments, or electric guitars.

### Organization and Leadership
**Hierarchy:** Each section has a **principal** (leader/soloist). The **concertmaster** (principal first violin) is second-in-command to the conductor, leads tuning, determines string bowings, and manages musical personnel issues. Principal oboe traditionally gives the tuning A. String sections play *tutti* (unison) or *divisi* (divided parts); wind/brass principals play unique parts.
**Conductor:** Stands on a podium using a baton to set tempo, cue entries, and shape phrasing. Role emerged mid-18th century; prior to this, ensembles were led by concertmaster or keyboardist. Conductors select repertoire, study scores, run rehearsals, and handle administrative duties.
**Conductorless Ensembles:** Revived in 1922 by Soviet *Persimfans* (committee-led; failed due to tempo-change difficulties). Modern examples include Orpheus Chamber Orchestra (deferring to principal players) and early-music groups using Baroque leadership models (concertmaster/harpsichordist).
**Multiple Conductors:** Used for offstage instruments (sub-conductor relays principal’s cues) or **polytempo** works (e.g., Stockhausen’s *Gruppen* for three orchestras, premiered 1958).

### Auditions, Employment, and Gender History
**Auditions:** Tenured positions require multi-round auditions before a panel (conductor, concertmaster, section principal). Candidates play solo repertoire and difficult orchestral excerpts; finalists may undergo a trial week. The Vienna Philharmonic uses completely screened blind auditions.
**Contracts:** Range from tenured permanent positions to single-concert substitutes ("subs") or seasonal contracts for large works or leave coverage.
**Gender Integration:** Historically male-only; first women hired as harpists. Vienna Philharmonic resisted until 1997 (protests, ridicule), admitting Anna Lelkes. In 2012, women made up 6% of the orchestra's membership; violinist Albena Danailova became concertmaster in 2008. Brass, percussion, and double bass sections remain predominantly male in many top ensembles.

### Amateur Ensembles
**School/University:** Student ensembles led by teachers/professors; may be string-only or full orchestra. Conservatories often tier orchestras by skill.
**Youth Orchestras:** Regional or national (e.g., National Youth Orchestra of Canada) for teens/young adults.
**Community Orchestras:** Adult amateurs, ranging from beginner (no public concerts) to advanced (professional repertoire). May hire professionals as section leaders.

### Repertoire and Performance Contexts
**Core Repertoire:** Symphonies (multi-movement works from score), overtures (originally opera introductions; later independent programmatic works), concertos (soloist with orchestral accompaniment), opera/ballet/musical theatre pit work, choral works.
**Venue History:** Baroque: aristocratic palaces, opera houses, churches. Classical: rise of public concert halls alongside aristocratic patronage. 20th/21st century: government funding (Europe/N. America) supplements tickets/donations; recordings, radio, TV, and streaming provide new revenue.
**Faking:** Musicians occasionally simulate difficult passages (high/fast) rather than play every note. Attributed to insufficient rehearsal or poorly written parts; considered unacceptable if due to lack of practice.

### Financial Crises and Counter-Movements (U.S. Focus)
**Late 20th Century Crisis:** High fixed costs vs. shrinking subscriber base; endowment losses; donor fatigue; government cuts; recording revenue collapse. Bankruptcies: Philadelphia (Ch. 11, 2011), Louisville (Ch. 11, 2010), Northwest Chamber, Honolulu, New Mexico, Syracuse (Ch. 7, 2006–2011).
**Responses:** Conductor pay cuts; advocacy for new music/community engagement (Tilson Thomas, Salonen); personnel reductions (usually string section); experimentation with presentation.

## Terms
- ****Basso continuo**** — A continuous bass line with improvised harmonies, played by chordal instruments (harpsichord, organ) and bass instruments in Baroque music.
- ****Concertmaster**** — Principal first violinist; leader of string section and orchestra's second-in-command; sets bowings and leads tuning.
- ****Divisi (div.)**** — Instruction for a string section to divide into multiple parts (e.g., upper/lower) rather than play in unison.
- ****Philharmonic orchestra**** — Synonym for symphony orchestra (Greek *phil-* "loving" + *harmony*); often used to distinguish co-resident ensembles (e.g., London Philharmonic vs. LSO).
- ****Chamber orchestra**** — Smaller ensemble (~15–50 players), often performing Baroque/Classical repertoire or modern works for reduced forces.
- ****Tutti**** — Passage where the full section or orchestra plays together in unison (as opposed to solo or divisi).
- ****Baton**** — Short wooden rod used by conductors to make gestures more visible to musicians.
- ****Blind audition**** — Audition where a screen hides the candidate from the panel to prevent gender/racial bias.
- ****Faking**** — Orchestral musician's practice of simulating a difficult passage without playing all written notes.
- ****Polytempo / Polyconductor**** — Music requiring multiple simultaneous tempos, necessitating sub-conductors for each ensemble group led by a principal conductor.

## Debates and open questions
- **Financial sustainability:** Can the traditional large symphony orchestra model survive in the U.S. without radical changes to funding, repertoire, or community engagement?
- **Gender equity:** Despite screened auditions, why do brass, percussion, and low-string sections remain predominantly male in top-tier orchestras?
- **Authenticity vs. modernity:** To what extent should modern orchestras adopt period instruments, smaller forces, and conductorless leadership for pre-19th-century music?
- **Artistic relevance:** How should orchestras balance canonical repertoire with new music, technology (electronics, streaming), and popular culture to attract 21st-century audiences?
- **Labor vs. artistry:** Is "faking" an acceptable pragmatic solution to impossible writing and limited rehearsal, or does it undermine artistic integrity?
