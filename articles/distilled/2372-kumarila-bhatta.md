# Kumārila Bhaṭṭa

## Overview
Kumārila Bhatta was a 7th-century CE Hindu philosopher and champion of the Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā school. He is renowned for his defense of Vedic authority and ritualism against Buddhist idealism, and his work profoundly influenced later Indian philosophy. His arguments for the Vedas' unauthored nature and his theories on language and epistemology remain central to classical Indian thought.

## Body

### Philosophy and Works
Kumārila Bhatta was a staunch ritualist and a key figure in the Mīmāṃsā school, which upholds the supreme validity of the Vedas. His philosophy is sometimes classified as existential realism. He authored three subcommentaries on Sabara's commentary on Jaimini's *Purva Mimamsa Sutras*: the *Ślokavārttika*, *Tantravarttika*, and *Tuptika*. His work is noted for its extensive quotations and influence on other Indian philosophical schools.

### Philosophy of Language
Kumārila and his followers (the Bhāṭṭa school) advocated a compositional view of semantics called *abhihitānvaya* ("designation of what has been denoted"). This theory holds that sentence meaning is understood only after grasping the meanings of individual words, which are independent, complete objects. This view was debated for centuries by the rival Prabhākara school, which argued for *anvitābhidhāna*—that sentence meanings are grasped directly from contextual cues, skipping the stage of individual word comprehension.

### Epistemology
Kumārila championed the doctrine of intrinsic validity (*svatah pramanya*). This epistemological principle states that all cognitions are valid at the moment of apprehension unless proven otherwise. He argued against the need for second-order justification before accepting a cognition as valid, asserting that the capacity for validity is inherent.

### Theism and Vedic Authority
As a Mīmāṃsā philosopher, Kumārila rejected the notion of a supreme creator God. He critiqued cosmological arguments for a divine creator, presenting a dilemma: if a creator's body is created, it leads to infinite regress; if it is uncreated, the same could be said for the world itself. His central project was to establish the Vedas as *apauruṣeyā* (unauthored), safeguarding their authority from reproach.

### Engagement with Buddhism
Kumārila mounted a multi-pronged critique of Buddhism. He argued that Buddhist scriptures, often composed in vernacular languages rather than Sanskrit, lacked the authority of the eternally valid Veda. He attacked the Buddhist doctrine of momentariness (*kshanika*), arguing it leads to the absurd conclusion that the universe is non-existent between moments. His portrayal of Buddhist arguments suggests he engaged with a school more sophisticated than Dignāga but less so than Dharmakīrti.

## Terms
- **Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā**: A school of Hindu philosophy focused on the interpretation of the Vedas, particularly the earlier (pūrva) portions concerning ritual action.
- **Apauruṣeyā**: The doctrine that the Vedas are unauthored, eternal, and not of human origin.
- **Svatah pramanya**: The epistemological doctrine of intrinsic validity, where cognitions are presumed valid until disproven.
- **Abhihitānvaya**: The Bhāṭṭa theory of sentence meaning, where meaning is derived compositionally from individual word meanings.
- **Anvitābhidhāna**: The rival Prabhākara theory of sentence meaning, where words are understood as already connected in a sentence.
- **Kshanika**: The Buddhist doctrine of momentariness, which holds that all phenomena exist only for an infinitesimal moment.
- **Āstika**: An orthodox school of Indian philosophy that accepts the authority of the Vedas.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historical Engagement**: Scholar John Taber argues Kumārila's portrayal of Buddhist arguments is less sophisticated than Dharmakīrti's, challenging the traditional account that they were direct contemporaries and debaters.
- **Birthplace**: His origin is uncertain, with competing theories placing him in South India, North India, or eastern India (Kamarupa/Assam).
- **Relationship with Dharmakīrti**: A legend states Kumārila spoke abusively to his nephew Dharmakīrti, driving him to become a Buddhist monk, but this is contested by textual analysis.
