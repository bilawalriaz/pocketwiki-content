# Milky Way

## Overview
The Milky Way is the barred spiral galaxy containing the Solar System, appearing from Earth as a hazy band of unresolved starlight. It spans roughly 87,400 light-years in diameter (D25 isophote) and hosts 100–400 billion stars, with a supermassive black hole (Sagittarius A*, ~4.1 million solar masses) at its center. The Sun orbits ~27,000 light-years from the center on the inner edge of the Orion Arm, completing a circuit every ~240 million years. For centuries, astronomers debated whether the Milky Way constituted the entire universe; the 1920 Great Debate and Edwin Hubble’s 1923 observations of Cepheid variables in Andromeda proved it is merely one of billions of galaxies. Modern surveys (notably Gaia) map its structure, revealing a warped disk, a central bar, a dark matter halo, and a history of mergers—most notably a simulated merger with the "Kraken" galaxy ~11 billion years ago. The Milky Way and Andromeda are approaching each other and may merge in ~4.3 billion years.

## Timeline
- **13.61 Gya** — Milky Way begins forming from overdensities shortly after the Big Bang; oldest globular clusters form.
- **~11 Gya** — Cosmological simulations indicate a major merger with a large galaxy dubbed the "Kraken."
- **8.8 ± 1.7 Gya** — Formation of the galactic thin disk; star formation rate drops sharply.
- **4.6 Gya** — Sun forms in the Orion Arm.
- **384–322 BC** — Aristotle argues the Milky Way is an atmospheric phenomenon; Democritus/Anagoras propose it is distant starlight.
- **973–1048** — Al-Biruni describes it as "countless fragments of nebulous stars."
- **1610** — Galileo Galilei resolves the band into individual stars with a telescope.
- **1755** — Immanuel Kant speculates the Milky Way is a rotating disk of stars ("island universe") and that nebulae are external galaxies.
- **1785** — William Herschel maps the Milky Way via star counts, placing the Sun near the center.
- **1904** — Jacobus Kapteyn detects two opposing stellar streams, the first evidence of galactic rotation.
- **1920** — Great Debate between Harlow Shapley and Heber Curtis on the scale of the universe and nature of spiral nebulae.
- **1923** — Edwin Hubble identifies Cepheid variables in Andromeda, proving it is an external galaxy ~275,000 parsecs away.
- **2005** — Spitzer Space Telescope confirms the Milky Way is a barred spiral with a larger central bar than thought.
- **2013–2024** — Gaia spacecraft provides distance estimates by determining the parallax of a billion stars, expanding observed stars from ~2 million to ~2 billion; reveals disk wobble, stellar streams, and magnetic field structure.
- **~4.3 Gya (future)** — Predicted collision/merger with the Andromeda Galaxy.

## Body

### Mythology and Etymology
The name "Milky Way" derives from the Greek *galaxías kýklos* ("milky circle"). In one Greek myth, Zeus placed infant Heracles at Hera’s breast; when she awoke and pushed him away, spilled divine milk formed the band. In another, Athena gave Heracles to Hera, but his forcefulness caused her to rip him away in pain. The Latin *via lactea* translates this concept. In the Babylonian epic *Enūma Eliš*, the Milky Way is created from the severed tail of the primeval salt water dragon Tiamat, set in the sky by Marduk after slaying her; this was once thought to derive from a Sumerian version with Enlil, but is now considered a Babylonian invention to elevate Marduk. Cultures worldwide named it for its appearance: "Birds' Path" (Uralic/Turkic/Baltic) for migratory routes; "Silver River" (East Asia); "River of Heaven" (Japan/Vietnam); "Ganga of the Sky" (Sanskrit); "Pathway of Straw" (West Asia/Arabs); "Winter Street" (Scandinavia, visible best in winter); and "Road to Santiago" (pilgrims' guide). Indigenous Australians saw the dark dust lanes (Great Rift, Coalsack) as an "Emu in the Sky" or "house river."

### Appearance and Visibility
The Milky Way appears as a ~30° wide band of white light arching across the celestial sphere, brightest toward Sagittarius (the Galactic Center). It comprises unresolved stars in the galactic plane; dark regions like the Great Rift are interstellar dust blocking background light. The band obscures the "Zone of Avoidance." Visibility requires a sky darker than ~20.2 mag/arcsec² (Bortle scale 4 or better), no moonlight, and dark-adapted eyes. Light pollution prevents over one-third of humanity from seeing it. The galactic plane is inclined ~60° to the ecliptic and ~63° to the celestial equator, causing seasonal visibility shifts (best May–August in the Northern Hemisphere).

### Astronomical History
**Ancient views:** Aristotle (384–322 BC) deemed it an atmospheric exhalation; Olympiodorus (c. 495–570 AD) countered it must be celestial due to lack of parallax. Medieval Islamic astronomers (Al-Biruni, Avempace, al-Tusi, Ibn Qayyim) progressively argued it was a dense collection of faint stars.
**Telescopic era:** Galileo (1610) resolved stars. Kant (1755) hypothesized a rotating stellar disk and "island universes." Herschel (1785) mapped a flattened system with the Sun near the center. Kapteyn (1904) found stellar streaming, implying rotation. Curtis (1917) used novae in Andromeda to argue for external galaxies. Hubble (1923) settled the debate using Cepheid variables.
**Modern surveys:** The ESA Gaia mission (launched 2013) revolutionized astrometry, providing distance estimates by determining the parallax of a billion stars and expanding precise stellar distances from ~2 million to ~2 billion stars. Gaia data revealed a wobbling disk (2020), mapped magnetic fields (2024), and traced merger debris (e.g., Sagittarius Dwarf stream).

### Astrography: Sun’s Location and Motion
The Sun sits ~27,000 ly (8.3 kpc) from the Galactic Center, near the inner rim of the Orion Arm (Local Arm), 5–30 pc above the midplane. It resides in the Local Bubble/Fluff, between the Radcliffe Wave and Split linear structures. The Solar System orbits at ~220 km/s, completing a galactic year in ~240 Myr (18–20 orbits so far). The solar apex points toward Deneb (Cygnus), ~90° from the Galactic Center. The Sun oscillates through the plane ~2.7 times per orbit; earlier hypotheses linking this to mass extinctions are unsupported by recent CO data.

### General Characteristics: Size and Mass
**Size:** The stellar disk D25 diameter is 26.8 ± 1.1 kpc (87,400 ± 3,600 ly), thickness ~1.35 kpc (4,000 ly). Recent simulations suggest a dark matter area may extend up to a diameter of almost 2 million light-years (613 kpc). A 2020 study predicted the edge of the dark matter halo around 292 ± 61 kpc (952,000 ± 199,000 ly), a diameter of 584 ± 122 kpc (1.905 ± 0.3979 Mly). Debate persists on the stellar disk edge: some studies find stars to 31.5 kpc, others identify the Monoceros/TriAnd rings as warped disk flares rather than distinct structures.
**Mass:** Estimates vary widely: 5.8×10¹¹ to 1.54×10¹² M☉ (virial mass). A 2023 Gaia-based study suggests a low virial mass of 2.06×10¹¹ M☉. Dark matter dominates (perhaps 90% per 2019 data). Baryonic mass: stars ~4.6–6.4×10¹⁰ M☉; interstellar gas (90% H, 10% He) ~10–15% of stellar mass; dust ~1% of gas mass. A hot gaseous halo (1–2.5 MK) detected by Chandra/XMM-Newton may contain the "missing baryons," with mass comparable to the stellar disk.

### Rotation and Peculiar Velocity
The rotation curve is flat (~200–220 km/s) well beyond the bulge, implying unseen dark matter (or modified gravity). The Milky Way moves at ~630 km/s relative to the Hubble flow (toward the Great Attractor/Shapley Supercluster) and 552 ± 6 km/s relative to the CMB dipole (toward Hydra). The Local Group falls toward the Virgo Cluster at 967 km/s, slowed by mutual gravity.

### Contents
100–400 billion stars; at least as many planets (Kepler data: ~1 per star average, 17+ billion Earth-sized, up to 40 billion in habitable zones). Stellar remnants: ~10 billion white dwarfs, 1 billion neutron stars, 100 million stellar black holes. Interstellar medium (gas/dust) fills the disk. The halo contains old stars, ~150 globular clusters, and most dark matter. The galaxy is a "neutrino desert" (below-average neutrino luminosity).

### Structure
**Type:** Barred spiral (Hubble type Sbc), confirmed by Spitzer (2005).
**Center:** A boxy/peanut bulge (possibly a pseudobulge from bar instabilities) within ~10,000 ly. Sagittarius A* (SMBH, 4.100 ± 0.034 million M☉) anchors the center. The bar’s half-length (1–5 kpc) and angle (10–50°) are debated; a "5 kpc ring" of molecular gas and star formation may encircle it. Fermi bubbles (25,000 ly gamma-ray lobes) indicate past central activity.
**Spiral Arms:** Four main arms (Norma, Scutum–Centaurus, Sagittarius, Perseus) plus the Near/Far 3 kpc Arms. Old stars trace two major arms (Perseus, Scutum–Centaurus); gas/young stars trace four. The Sun sits in a spur (Orion Arm). Arms may result from collisions with Sagittarius Dwarf (simulation, 2011). The disk is warped in an "S" curve.
**Halo:** Spheroidal, triaxial, extending to ~180,000 ly (limited by Magellanic Clouds). Contains old stars, globular clusters (40% retrograde), and dark matter. Substructures: Sagittarius Stream, Virgo Stellar Stream (merging dwarf), possible Canis Major debris (now attributed to warp).

### Formation and Evolution
Formed from primordial overdensities ~13.61 Gya. Halo stars/clusters formed first. Rapid spin collapsed gas into a disk (~8.8 Gya thin disk formation), with a ~5 Gyr hiatus (gas too hot). Grew via mergers (cosmological simulations indicate a "Kraken" merger ~11 Gya) and gas accretion (Magellanic Stream, Smith Cloud). Unusually quiet merger history for last 10 Gyr. Currently in the "green valley" (transitioning from star-forming "blue cloud" to passive "red sequence"); star formation may cease in ~5 Gyr, accelerated by the future Andromeda merger. Oldest stars (e.g., HE 1523-0901 ~13.2 Gya, 2MASS J1808-5104 ~13.5 Gya) approach the Universe’s age (13.80 Gya).

### Intergalactic Neighborhood
Member of the Local Group (~50 galaxies, binary with Andromeda). Satellites: Large/Small Magellanic Clouds (LMC/SMC), Sagittarius Dwarf Elliptical Galaxy, and numerous dwarf galaxies including Canis Major Dwarf (the closest), Ursa Minor Dwarf, Sculptor Dwarf, Sextans Dwarf, Fornax Dwarf, Leo I Dwarf, Carina Dwarf, Draco Dwarf, and Leo II Dwarf. The smallest dwarf galaxies are only ~500 light-years in diameter. Surprisingly, most satellites orbit in a coherent plane (disk of satellites), contradicting ΛCDM predictions of isotropic distribution. The LMC/SMC induce the disk warp via a dark matter wake. Andromeda approaches at 100–140 km/s; merger likely in ~4.3 Gyr, forming a giant elliptical/disk galaxy. The Local Group resides in the Virgo Supercluster, part of Laniakea, flowing toward the Great Attractor.

## Terms
- ****Barred spiral galaxy**** — A spiral galaxy with a central bar-shaped structure of stars; the Milky Way is type Sbc (loosely wound arms).
- ****D25 isophote**** — Standard galaxy size measure: the contour where B-band (blue) surface brightness reaches 25 mag/arcsec².
- ****Kiloparsec (kpc)**** — 3,262 light-years; standard unit for galactic distances.
- ****Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*)**** — Bright radio source at the Galactic Center; a supermassive black hole of ~4.1 million solar masses.
- ****Dark matter halo**** — Inferred massive, invisible component extending far beyond the stellar disk, dominating gravitational potential.
- ****Gaia spacecraft**** — ESA astrometry mission (2013–present) measuring parallax and proper motions for ~1 billion stars, expanding observed stars to ~2 billion.
- ****Rotation curve**** — Plot of orbital speed vs. distance from center; flatness implies dark matter.
- ****Green valley**** — Region in galaxy color–magnitude space between star-forming "blue cloud" and passive "red sequence."
- ****Zone of Avoidance**** — Sky region obscured by the Milky Way’s dust plane, hiding extragalactic objects.
- ****Fermi bubbles**** — Giant gamma-ray lobes (25,000 ly) above/below the Galactic Center, likely from past starburst/AGN activity.

## Debates and open questions
- **Mass discrepancy:** Virial mass estimates range from 2×10¹¹ to 1.5×10¹² M☉; the 2023 Gaia-based low value (2.06×10¹¹ M☉) challenges standard halo models.
- **Disk extent:** Does the stellar disk truncate at ~13–20 kpc, or extend smoothly to 30+ kpc? Are Monoceros/TriAnd rings distinct structures or warped disk flares?
- **Bar structure:** Single vs. double bar; half-length (1–5 kpc) and orientation (10–50°) remain uncertain; RR Lyrae stars do not trace the bar.
- **Bulge nature:** Classical bulge vs. pseudobulge (bar-driven); peanut-shaped morphology complicates classification.
- **Satellite plane:** Most satellites orbit in a thin, rotating plane—unexpected in ΛCDM cosmology where halos should be isotropic.
- **Spiral arm pattern:** Two-armed (old stars) vs. four-armed (gas/young stars); pitch angle (7–25°); transient vs. long-lived density waves; role of Sagittarius Dwarf impacts.
- **Missing baryons:** Does the hot gaseous halo (detected in X-rays) account for the ~50% of expected baryons not seen in stars/cool gas?
- **Formation timeline:** Exact timing of the thin disk hiatus, the Kraken merger (simulation-based), and the transition to green valley status.
