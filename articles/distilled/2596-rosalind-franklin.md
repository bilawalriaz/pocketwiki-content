# Rosalind Franklin

## Overview
Rosalind Franklin (1920–1958) was a British chemist and X-ray crystallographer whose work was central to understanding the molecular structures of DNA, RNA, viruses, coal, and graphite. Her experimental data, particularly the X-ray diffraction image Photo 51, were critical to the discovery of DNA's double helix structure, but her contributions were largely unrecognized during her lifetime, leading to her being described as the "wronged heroine" or "dark lady of DNA."

## Timeline
- **25 July 1920** — Born in London into an affluent British Jewish family.
- **1941** — Graduated with a degree in natural sciences from Newnham College, Cambridge.
- **1942–1945** — Worked at the British Coal Utilisation Research Association (BCURA), studying coal porosity.
- **1945** — Awarded a PhD from Cambridge for her coal research.
- **1947–1950** — Worked in Paris with Jacques Mering, becoming an expert in X-ray crystallography of amorphous substances.
- **1951** — Joined King's College London to work on DNA X-ray diffraction.
- **May 1952** — Produced the landmark Photo 51, an X-ray diffraction image of B-DNA.
- **March 1953** — Moved to Birkbeck College to work on virus structures.
- **25 April 1953** — Watson and Crick published their DNA double helix model in *Nature*, with Franklin and Wilkins' supporting papers in the same issue.
- **1955–1958** — Led pioneering research on the structure of tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) and other RNA viruses at Birkbeck.
- **16 April 1958** — Died of ovarian cancer at age 37.
- **1962** — Francis Crick, James Watson, and Maurice Wilkins were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery of the DNA structure.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Franklin showed exceptional scholastic abilities from childhood. She attended St Paul's Girls' School, excelling in science, and then Newnham College, Cambridge, graduating in 1941. After a disappointing PhD start under Ronald Norrish, she took a research position at BCURA in 1942. Her work on coal porosity formed the basis of her Cambridge PhD, awarded in 1945.

### Coal and Carbon Research in Paris
In 1947, Franklin moved to Paris to work with Jacques Mering at the Laboratoire Central des Services Chimiques de l'État. Mering taught her to apply X-ray diffraction to amorphous substances. She studied atomic changes in coal and carbonaceous materials converting to graphite, coining the terms "graphitising" and "non-graphitising carbon." Her work became part of mainstream coal physics and chemistry.

### DNA Research at King's College London
In 1951, Franklin began work at King's College London on DNA X-ray diffraction. She developed a camera chamber with precise humidity control, producing superior diffraction images. She discovered DNA existed in two forms: a long, thin "B" form (high humidity) and a short, fat "crystalline" "A" form (lower humidity). Her analysis of the A-form concluded phosphate groups were on the molecule's outside. In November 1951, she presented data suggesting a helical structure with 2, 3, or 4 chains.

A conflict arose due to poor communication from director John Randall about her role versus Maurice Wilkins'. This friction was worsened by Franklin's direct manner and Wilkins' shyness. In January 1953, James Watson visited King's and, after a tense encounter, was shown her Photo 51 by Wilkins without her permission. In February 1953, Francis Crick was shown a Medical Research Council (MRC) report containing Franklin's crystallographic calculations, confirming key parameters like the 34 Ångström repeat distance and C2 symmetry.

By March 1953, Franklin had concluded both DNA forms were double helices. She and Raymond Gosling submitted a manuscript on B-DNA on 17 March, one day before Watson and Crick completed their model. Watson and Crick published their model on 25 April 1953, acknowledging Franklin and Wilkins only in a footnote. Franklin's accompanying paper provided experimental evidence supporting their model.

### Virus Research at Birkbeck College
Franklin left King's in March 1953 for Birkbeck College, leading virus research under John Desmond Bernal. She used X-ray crystallography to study tobacco mosaic virus (TMV), proving all virus particles were the same length and their protein coat was helical. Her team, including Aaron Klug, published seminal work on TMV and other plant viruses, showing RNA was wound along the inner surface of the hollow virus. She also began work on the polio virus before her death.

### Personal Life and Death
Franklin was described as an agnostic but remained consciously Jewish. She loved traveling and trekking. Diagnosed with ovarian cancer in September 1956, she continued working through her illness. She died on 16 April 1958 at age 37. Exposure to X-ray radiation is sometimes considered a possible factor.

## Terms
- **X-ray crystallography**: A technique to determine atomic and molecular structure by analyzing diffraction patterns from X-rays striking a crystal.
- **Amorphous substance**: A solid material lacking long-range crystalline order, like glass or coal.
- **Photo 51**: The famous X-ray diffraction image of B-DNA taken by Raymond Gosling under Franklin's supervision in May 1952, providing key evidence for helical structure.
- **B-DNA and A-DNA**: Two DNA forms discovered by Franklin. B-DNA is long and thin (high humidity); A-DNA is short and fat (lower humidity).
- **Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV)**: A well-studied RNA virus infecting tobacco plants, used by Franklin's team to pioneer virus structural study.
- **Graphitising and non-graphitising carbon**: Terms Franklin coined to classify coals by their ability to convert to graphite upon heating.
- **Patterson function**: A mathematical method in X-ray crystallography to analyze diffraction patterns and deduce atomic arrangements.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Sexism at King's College**: Biographer Anne Sayre argued Franklin faced institutional sexism, including exclusion from common rooms. Others, including her sister and biographer Brenda Maddox, dispute this, noting other women in the department and her father's support.
- **Data Sharing and Acknowledgment**: The extent to which Watson and Crick used Franklin's data without her knowledge is debated. While Photo 51 was shown by Wilkins and an MRC report was shared by Max Perutz, some argue this was collaborative, as suggested by a 2023 *Nature* article based on newly found documents.
- **Franklin's Contribution to the Model**: Franklin had independently concluded DNA was a double helix by March 1953, before Watson and Crick's model was known. The debate centers on whether she would have published the complete structure herself and how her cautious, data-first approach differed from their model-building strategy.
- **Nobel Prize Omission**: Franklin was not awarded the 1962 Nobel Prize, partly because she died in 1958 and the prize is not awarded posthumously. However, rules at the time would have allowed it had she been nominated while alive. The discovery was not fully proven until after her death, and the Nobel was awarded for the body of work on nucleic acids.
