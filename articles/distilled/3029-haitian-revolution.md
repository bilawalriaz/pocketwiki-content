# Haitian Revolution

## Overview
The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) was a successful insurrection by enslaved Africans against French colonial rule in Saint-Domingue, leading to the founding of Haiti. It was the only known slave rebellion in history that resulted in the creation of a free state ruled by former captives. The revolution challenged European beliefs about racial inferiority and had a profound impact on the institution of slavery throughout the Americas.

## Body

### Background and Social Structure
Saint-Domingue was France's wealthiest colony, producing 60% of the world's coffee and 40% of its sugar. Its economy depended on a brutal slave system with a high mortality rate, requiring constant importation of enslaved Africans. Society was rigidly stratified into three groups: white colonists (*blancs*), free people of color (*gens de couleur libres*), and enslaved Blacks, who outnumbered whites nearly 11 to 1. Tensions were high among all groups, with frequent violence and a large population of runaway slaves (*Maroons*).

### Outbreak and Early Revolt (1791–1793)
The revolution was triggered by the French Revolution's ideals and a 1791 decree granting citizenship to wealthy free people of color, which white planters refused to honor. On August 21, 1791, a massive slave uprising began in the north after a Vodou ceremony. The rebels, initially seeking freedom from slavery rather than independence from France, quickly controlled a third of the colony. The violence was extreme on both sides, with rebels killing thousands of whites and destroying hundreds of plantations.

### Foreign Intervention and Abolition (1793–1798)
France declared war on Britain and Spain in 1793, leading both to intervene in Saint-Domingue. To secure the colony and recruit Black soldiers, French commissioners abolished slavery. The French National Convention formally abolished slavery in all colonies in February 1794. Toussaint Louverture, a brilliant former slave, emerged as a key leader, first fighting for Spain, then switching to France. He and other Haitian generals repelled British and Spanish forces, who suffered catastrophic losses to yellow fever.

### Louverture's Leadership and Napoleon's Invasion (1798–1802)
After the British withdrawal in 1798, Louverture consolidated power, defeating internal rivals and invading Spanish Santo Domingo in 1800. In 1801, he issued a constitution making himself governor-for-life and calling for Black autonomy. In response, Napoleon sent a massive expedition in 1802 under his brother-in-law Charles Leclerc to restore French rule and, secretly, slavery. The ensuing war was brutal, with the French employing scorched-earth tactics and mass executions. Louverture was captured through deception in 1802 and died in a French prison.

### War of Independence and Victory (1802–1804)
After Louverture's capture, resistance continued under Jean-Jacques Dessalines. When it became clear the French intended to reimpose slavery, the rebellion intensified. The French army was devastated by yellow fever and fierce Haitian guerrilla warfare. A British naval blockade prevented French reinforcements. The decisive Battle of Vertières on November 18, 1803, led to the surrender of the remaining French forces. On January 1, 1804, Dessalines declared independence, naming the new nation Haiti.

### Aftermath and Legacy
The new state was economically ruined and depopulated. Dessalines ruled as dictator, ordering the 1804 massacre of the remaining French population and implementing a forced labor system. Haiti was the first independent Black republic and the second independent nation in the Western Hemisphere. Its independence was a landmark event that inspired abolitionist and anti-colonial movements but also led to its long-term isolation and economic hardship, including a crippling "independence debt" paid to France.

## Terms
- **Saint-Domingue**: The French colony on the island of Hispaniola, now Haiti, which was the world's leading sugar producer.
- **Gens de couleur libres**: Free people of color in the French colonies, often of mixed race, who had some rights but faced discrimination.
- **Maroons**: Enslaved people who escaped and formed independent communities in remote areas.
- **Vodou**: A syncretic religion blending West African beliefs with Catholicism, which played a unifying role in the rebellion.
- **Code Noir**: A 1685 French decree intended to regulate the treatment of slaves, which was largely ignored by planters.
- **Grands blancs**: Wealthy white plantation owners in the colony.
- **Petits blancs**: Poorer white colonists, often overseers or laborers.
- **Yellow fever**: A tropical disease that killed a massive proportion of European troops sent to the Caribbean.
- **Scorched-earth tactics**: A military strategy of destroying anything useful to the enemy, employed by both sides.
- **Independence debt**: The 150 million franc reparations Haiti was forced to pay France in 1825 for recognition of its independence.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Historians debate the extent to which the Haitian Revolution was a unified movement versus a series of coincidental conflicts that ended in a fragile truce.
- The role and agency of women in the revolution have historically been overlooked but are now receiving more scholarly attention.
- The long-term impact of the revolution on the abolition of slavery in the Americas is debated; some argue it was a major economic pressure, while others contend slavery continued to flourish for decades.
- The historiography of the revolution is considered "silenced" by that of the French Revolution, receiving relatively less public attention.
