# Paul Klee

## Overview
Paul Klee (1879–1940) was a Swiss-born German artist whose highly individual style synthesized expressionism, cubism, and surrealism. A natural draftsman who mastered color theory through long experimentation, he taught at the Bauhaus school alongside Wassily Kandinsky and produced roughly 9,000 works. His art reflects his musicality, dry humor, and poetic sensibility, often combining geometric abstraction with childlike figuration. Persecuted by the Nazis as a "degenerate" artist, he fled to Switzerland in 1933, where he continued working despite the debilitating autoimmune disease scleroderma until his death in 1940.

## Timeline
- **18 Dec 1879** — Born in Münchenbuchsee, Switzerland, to German music teacher Hans Wilhelm Klee and Swiss singer Ida Marie Frick.
- **1898–1901** — Studies at the Academy of Fine Arts in Munich under Heinrich Knirr and Franz von Stuck; excels at drawing but struggles with color.
- **1901–1902** — Travels to Italy with Hermann Haller; studies Old Masters but feels overwhelmed by Humanist tradition.
- **1906** — Marries pianist Lily Stumpf; son Felix Paul born 1907. First solo exhibition in Bern (1910).
- **1911** — Joins *Der Blaue Reiter* editorial team; meets Kandinsky, August Macke, and Franz Marc.
- **1912** — Visits Paris; encounters Cubism and Robert Delaunay’s color theory.
- **Apr 1914** — Breakthrough trip to Tunisia with Macke and Louis Moilliet: "Color and I are one." Paints first pure abstract, *In the Style of Kairouan*.
- **1916–1918** — Conscripted as *Landsturmsoldat*; serves behind front lines and in aircraft maintenance/clerk roles; continues painting.
- **1921–1931** — Teaches at the Bauhaus (Weimar, then Dessau) as "Form" master; publishes *Pedagogical Sketchbook* (1924).
- **1923** — Founds *Die Blaue Vier* (The Blue Four) with Kandinsky, Lyonel Feininger, Alexej von Jawlensky; exhibits in US.
- **1928** — Travels to Egypt; influences later pointillist works like *Ad Parnassum*.
- **1931–1933** — Teaches at Düsseldorf Academy; targeted by Nazi press, home searched by Gestapo, dismissed.
- **Late 1933** — Emigrates with family to Switzerland.
- **1935** — Diagnosed with scleroderma (posthumous diagnosis); output drops then surges to 1,254 works in 1939.
- **29 Jun 1940** — Dies in Muralto, Locarno, Switzerland; Swiss citizenship granted six days postmortem.

## Body

### Early Life and Musical Background
Klee grew up in Bern in a musical household; his father was a conservatory-trained music teacher, his mother a singer. He was a violin prodigy, invited at age 11 to join the Bern Music Association. Though his parents pushed music, he rebelled in his teens, finding modern music meaningless and craving the radical freedom of visual art. He kept a diary from 1897 to 1918, revealing early literary interests and a dry wit. At the Munich Academy (1898–1901), he mastered drawing but despaired of color: "I probably would never learn to paint." A 1901–02 Italian tour exposed him to Renaissance masters, intensifying his color struggle. Back in Bern, he developed experimental techniques—needle-scratching on blackened glass (*verre églomisé*)—and produced the *Inventions* etching cycle (1903–05), his first exhibited works.

### Marriage, *Der Blaue Reiter*, and the Tunisian Breakthrough
Klee married Lily Stumpf in 1906; she supported the family with piano lessons while he kept house and painted. In 1911, Alfred Kubin introduced him to Munich’s avant-garde. He joined the *Der Blaue Reiter* almanac (founded by Kandinsky and Marc) and exhibited in their 1912 *Schwarz-Weiß* show. A 1912 Paris trip introduced him to Cubism and Delaunay’s "pure painting," prompting pale watercolor experiments. The decisive turning point came in April 1914: a twelve-day trip to Tunisia with Macke and Moilliet. Overwhelmed by North African light, he wrote, "Color has taken possession of me... I am a painter." Faithfulness to nature receded; he embraced "cool romanticism of abstraction," producing his first pure abstract, *In the Style of Kairouan* (1914), built from colored rectangles—his basic building block, often likened to musical notes.

### World War I and Early Recognition
Conscripted in March 1916, Klee served in aircraft maintenance and as a clerk at a flying school, roles that allowed him to continue painting. The deaths of friends Macke and Marc in battle distressed him, yielding war-themed lithographs like *Death for the Idea* (1915). By 1917 critics acclaimed him the best new German artist; works like *Ab ovo* (watercolor on gauze with chalk ground) showed sophisticated texture. He secured a contract with dealer Hans Goltz in 1919, and a 300-work retrospective followed in 1920.

### Bauhaus Years (1921–1931)
Walter Gropius appointed Klee to the Bauhaus in Weimar (later Dessau) as a "Form" master in bookbinding, stained glass, and mural painting. He welcomed the school’s theoretical conflicts: "I also approve of these forces competing one with the other if the result is achievement." Kandinsky rejoined the staff in 1922, renewing their friendship. Klee published *Pedagogical Sketchbook* (1924) and lectured *On Modern Art* (1924). In 1923, with Galka Scheyer, he co-founded *Die Blaue Vier* (The Blue Four), which exhibited in the United States. He visited Egypt in 1928, a less intense experience than Tunisia but one that informed the pyramidal structure and stamped-dot technique of *Ad Parnassum* (1932). The first major monograph on his work appeared in 1929 (Will Grohmann).

### Nazi Persecution, Exile, and Late Work
In 1931 Klee left the Bauhaus for the Düsseldorf Academy. A Nazi newspaper attacked him in 1933 as a "Galician Jew" (he was of German paternal lineage); the Gestapo searched his home and he was fired. His self-portrait *Struck from the List* (1933) depicts closed eyes, compressed lips, and a large X on the back of his head. The family emigrated to Switzerland in late 1933. Seventeen of his works appeared in the 1937 "Degenerate Art" exhibition; 102 were seized from German museums. In Switzerland, scleroderma (hardening of connective tissue) progressed, making swallowing difficult. Output plummeted to 25 works in 1936 but rebounded to a career-high 1,254 in 1939. Late works feature heavier lines, larger geometric blocks, hieroglyphic symbols, and ambivalent themes—personal fate, political satire (*Revolution of the Viadukt*, 1937), and mortality (*Death and Fire*, 1940). He died in Muralto on 29 June 1940; Swiss citizenship was granted six days later.

### Style, Methods, and Theory
Klee worked in isolation, synthesizing Expressionism, Cubism, Futurism, Surrealism, and Abstraction into an unclassifiable personal idiom. He combined media inventively—oil, watercolor, ink, pastel, etching—on diverse grounds (canvas, burlap, gauze, newsprint, metal foil). Techniques included spray paint, knife application, stamping, glazing, and impasto. A master draftsman, he developed color mastery through decades of experiment, often linking color harmony to musical structure: rectangles as notes, palettes as keys, complementary pairs as consonance, "dissonant" colors as tension. Works are typically small, fragile, childlike, mixing geometric grids with playful figures, letters, numbers, and musical notation. Titles reflect humor, mood, and political conviction. Rainer Maria Rilke noted his drawings were "transcriptions of music." Pamela Kort emphasizes his lifelong parody and wit as political dimensions. He also made 50 hand puppets (1916–25) for his son Felix, not counted in his official oeuvre.

### Poetry and Pictorial Writing
Klee wrote poetry throughout his life, published posthumously (German 1960, English translations 1962, 1974). His poems and paintings cross-fertilize: "The practised hand / often knows it better than the head." He incorporated poems into pictures as *Bilderschrift* (pictorial writing), e.g., *Once emerged from the grey of night* (1918), where colored letter-squares follow a handwritten verse. *Tree nursery* (1929) uses incised primitive script; meaning resides in structural rhythms the viewer reconciles. Scholar K. Porter Aichele links this to Concrete and Visual Poetry movements.

### Graphic Works Evolution
**Early (Bern/Munich, 1903–11):** Nineteen etchings, including the satirical *Inventions* cycle (1903–05). *Two Men, Supposing the Other to be in a Higher Position* (1903) depicts Kaiser Wilhelm II and Franz Joseph I bowing uncertainly without insignia. Needle-scratching on blackened glass yielded 57 *verre églomisé* works (1905–06).  
**Mystical-Abstract (1914–19):** Tunisian watercolors translate light into Cézanne/Delaunay-inspired grids (*Street Café*). *Foehn in Marc's Garden* shows abstraction advancing. War service birthed *Velvetbells* (1917), merging graphic signs, vegetal forms, and phantasy; birds appear, echoing planes seen at Gersthofen. *Once emerged from the grey of night* (1918) integrates letters in colored squares. Oil paint adopted from 1919 (*Villa R*).  
**Bauhaus/Düsseldorf (1921–33):** *Twittering Machine* (1922)—crank-driven open-beaked birds, critiquing technization’s theft of self-determination; seized as "degenerate," now at MoMA. *The Goldfish* (1925), *Cat and Bird* (1928), *Main Road and Byways* (1929). *Ad Parnassum* (1932, 100×126 cm), a pointillist pyramid of stamped dots and lines with a sun above, synthesizes Egyptian numerology and musical structure. 1933 output: 482 works; self-portrait *Struck from the List* marks dismissal.  
**Late Switzerland (1933–40):** Large formats dominate. Hieroglyphic elements intensify from 1938 (*Insula dulcamara*, 88×176 cm, white face with black-circled eyes = death). *Revolution of the Viadukt* (1937): bridge arches riot against chains. Final unsigned still life *Angel of Death* (1940) depicts flowers, pot, sculpture, angel, and moon on black ground.

### Reception and Legacy
Contemporaries recognized his uniqueness: Oskar Schlemmer (1916) praised his wisdom in a single line; Wilhelm Hausenstein linked his violin playing (Bach, Mozart) to his art. Surrealists were divided: Max Ernst and René Crevel admired him; André Breton despised him. Walter Benjamin bought *Angelus Novus* (1921), interpreting its angel as the "angel of history" in his *Theses on the Philosophy of History*. Marcel Duchamp (1949) noted the deceptive naivety masking mature technique and inexhaustible invention. Klee inspired over 50 musical compositions (1943–2019), from Gunther Schuller’s *Seven Studies* to Sakanaction’s song "Klee." The Zentrum Paul Klee (Renzo Piano, 2005, Bern) rotates 150 of 4,000 works due to photosensitivity. Archives exist at the University of Jena (since 1995). Nazi-looted works from Jewish collectors remain subject to restitution claims (e.g., *Swamp Legend*). The open-access journal *Die Zwitscher-Maschine* publishes Klee research.

## Terms
- **Bauhaus**: German school of art, design, and architecture (1919–1933) where Klee taught as a "Form" master.
- **Der Blaue Reiter (The Blue Rider)**: Munich-based avant-garde group (1911–14) founded by Kandinsky and Marc; Klee joined its almanac editorial team.
- **Die Blaue Vier (The Blue Four)**: Exhibition society (1923) formed by Klee, Kandinsky, Feininger, Jawlensky, promoted in the US by Galka Scheyer.
- **Landsturmsoldat**: Reserve soldier in Imperial Germany; Klee’s conscription status (1916–18).
- **Pedagogical Sketchbook (1924)**: Klee’s Bauhaus teaching manual, distilling his form theory.
- **Pointillism**: Technique of applying distinct dots of color; Klee adapted it in *Ad Parnassum* using stamped dots.
- **Scleroderma**: Autoimmune disease hardening connective tissue; diagnosed posthumously, afflicted Klee from 1935.
- **Verre églomisé**: Technique of reverse painting or scratching on glass; Klee used needle on blackened pane (1905–06).
- **Bilderschrift (Pictorial writing)**: Klee’s term for works integrating poetic text and visual signs (e.g., *Once emerged from the grey of night*).
- **Degenerate Art (Entartete Kunst)**: Nazi label for modern art; 17 Klee works shown in 1937 Munich exhibition, 102 seized from museums.

## Debates and open questions
- **Citizenship and identity**: Born in Switzerland to a German father, Klee held German citizenship (paternal line) and served in the German army; his Swiss naturalization was granted only days after his death. Scholars debate how this liminal status shaped his reception in both countries.
- **Classification of style**: Klee resists single-movement labels (Expressionism, Cubism, Surrealism, Abstraction). The extent to which his synthesis constitutes a unique category versus an eclectic amalgam remains open.
- **Music–painting analogy**: Klee explicitly mapped color rectangles to musical notes and palettes to keys. Musicologists and art historians debate whether this is a structural isomorphism or a metaphorical framework.
- **Political engagement**: Long viewed as apolitical or whimsical, recent scholarship (Kort, Aichele) argues his parody, wit, and grotesque satire encode anti-fascist critique. The degree and intentionality of political content are contested.
- **Late work and illness**: The correlation between scleroderma’s progression and stylistic shifts (larger forms, heavier lines, hieroglyphs, output surge) is documented but interpreted variously as adaptation, defiance, or physiological determinism.
- **Nazi-era provenance**: Numerous works passed through Nazi seizures (museums) and forced sales (Jewish collectors). Restitution claims (e.g., *Swamp Legend*) involve complex legal and ethical questions still unresolved.
