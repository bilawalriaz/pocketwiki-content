# Helen Keller

## Overview
Helen Adams Keller (1880–1968) was an American author, disability rights advocate, and political activist who became the first deafblind person in the United States to earn a college degree. Her life story, particularly her education with teacher Anne Sullivan, became a global symbol of perseverance, while her later career as a prolific writer and socialist activist made her a significant, if sometimes controversial, public figure.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Keller was born into a family that had lost its pre-Civil War status. At 19 months, an illness (possibly meningitis) left her blind and deaf. She developed a home sign system with her family. In 1887, inspired by the story of Laura Bridgman, her mother sought help from Alexander Graham Bell, who directed them to the Perkins Institute for the Blind. The institute sent Anne Sullivan, a visually impaired alumna, to teach Keller. Sullivan's breakthrough came when she spelled "water" into Keller's hand under a water pump, allowing Keller to connect the symbol with the object. This began a nearly 50-year companionship.

Keller's formal education included the Perkins Institute, the Wright-Humason School for the Deaf, and the Cambridge School for Young Ladies. In 1900, she entered Radcliffe College, funded by Henry Huttleston Rogers after an introduction by Mark Twain. She graduated in 1904, becoming the first deafblind person to earn a Bachelor of Arts degree. She learned to speak using the Tadoma method (feeling lips and throat) and was proficient in Braille and fingerspelling.

### Career, Writing, and Activism
Keller became a world-renowned lecturer and author, writing 12 books and hundreds of essays. Her 1903 autobiography, *The Story of My Life*, publicized her education. She was a lifelong advocate for people with disabilities, helping found Helen Keller International in 1915 to address vision and health issues.

Politically, Keller was a radical socialist. She joined the Socialist Party of America in 1909 and the Industrial Workers of the World (IWW) in 1912. She campaigned for women's suffrage, labor rights, and world peace, and was a founding member of the ACLU in 1920. Her activism led to public criticism; when the *Brooklyn Eagle* editor dismissed her socialist views as products of her disabilities, she famously retorted that the paper was "socially blind and deaf." She was placed on the FBI's watchlist for her political activities. Keller also supported eugenics in 1915, advocating for the denial of life-saving procedures to infants with severe impairments.

### Companions and Later Life
After Sullivan's marriage in 1905 and declining health, Polly Thomson, a Scottish woman, joined Keller's household, eventually becoming her constant companion. Keller and Thomson traveled globally, raising funds for the blind. Sullivan died in 1936, and Thomson in 1960. Keller's final companion was her nurse, Winnie Corbally. Keller suffered a series of strokes in 1961 and died in 1968. She was awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964 and inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame in 1973.

### Portrayals and Legacy
Keller's life has been widely portrayed, most famously in William Gibson's play *The Miracle Worker* (1959) and its 1962 film adaptation. Her birthplace is a National Historic Landmark. She has been honored on U.S. currency (the Alabama state quarter), stamps, and with statues and streets worldwide. Archival material was lost in the September 11, 2001 attacks.

## Terms
- **Deafblind:** A condition of having both severe hearing and vision loss.
- **Tadoma method:** A tactile method of learning to speak by feeling the speaker's lips and throat vibrations.
- **Fingerspelling:** A system of representing letters of the alphabet using hand shapes, used in manual alphabets.
- **Braille:** A system of writing and reading for the blind, using raised dots.
- **Socialist Party of America (SPA):** A political party advocating for socialism, active in the early 20th century.
- **Industrial Workers of the World (IWW):** A radical international labor union known as the "Wobblies."
- **Eugenics:** A discredited pseudoscientific belief in improving the genetic quality of a human population, often through selective breeding or sterilization.
- **Cryptomnesia:** A memory error where a forgotten memory is mistaken for a new creation or original thought.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Plagiarism Allegation:** Her 1891 story "The Frost King" was found to be substantially similar to "The Frost Fairies" by Margaret Canby. The Perkins Institute investigation concluded it was likely a case of cryptomnesia (unconscious memory), not deliberate plagiarism.
- **Political Radicalism vs. Public Image:** Keller's socialist and IWW activism often conflicted with the inspirational, apolitical image of her as a "miracle" over disability, leading to public criticism and media dismissal of her political views.
- **Support for Eugenics:** Her 1915 writings in favor of denying medical care to infants with severe disabilities stand in stark contrast to her lifelong advocacy for people with disabilities, presenting a complex and controversial aspect of her legacy.
