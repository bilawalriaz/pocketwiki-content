# Mitanni

## Overview
Mitanni (c. 1550–1260 BC) was a Hurrian-speaking kingdom in northern Syria and southeast Anatolia with an Indo-Aryan ruling elite. It was a major power in the Late Bronze Age, rivaling Egypt, the Hittites, and Assyria, but its history is poorly documented due to a lack of native texts, relying instead on records from its neighbors.

## Timeline
- **c. 1600 BC** — First attestation of the name "Ḫabigalbat" for the region in Old Babylonian texts.
- **c. 1550–1260 BC** — Period of the Mitanni kingdom's existence.
- **c. 1500–1480 BC** — Reign of King Barattarna, who expanded Mitanni west to Aleppo and made Alalakh a vassal.
- **c. 1480–1460 BC** — Reign of King Shaushtatar, who sacked the Assyrian capital Assur.
- **Early 14th century BC** — Reign of Shuttarna II; Mitanni-Egypt alliance peaks with the marriage of his daughter Gilu-Hepa to Pharaoh Amenhotep III.
- **c. 1360–1335 BC** — Reign of Tushratta; Mitanni declines due to Hittite and Assyrian pressure.
- **c. 1345 BC** — Hittite king Suppiluliuma I launches the "Great Syrian War," sacking the Mitanni capital Washukanni.
- **Late 14th century BC** — Hittites install Shattiwaza as a vassal king of a reduced Mitanni.
- **c. 1350–1260 BC** — Mitanni exists as a province of the Middle Assyrian Empire.
- **c. 1250 BC** — King Shattuara II rebels against Assyria with Hittite help but is defeated by Shalmaneser I.

## Body

### Origins and Names
The Hurrian people were present in the region from the late 3rd millennium BC. The Mitanni kingdom was known by several names: Ḫabigalbat/Ḫanigalbat in Babylonian and Assyrian texts, Naharin in Egyptian records, and Ḫurri to the Hittites. The name "Mitanni" may derive from an Indo-Aryan root meaning "united," while "Ḫanigalbat" has been interpreted as "the Great Hani" or connected to a topographic term for the Upper Euphrates region.

### Rise and Peak
Mitanni emerged as a powerful kingdom by the late 17th or early 16th century BC. Under kings like Barattarna and Shaushtatar, it expanded to control a sphere from Kizzuwatna in the west to Arrapha in the east. Its power rested on a warrior elite, the *maryannu* (chariot drivers), and it maintained a confederation of directly administered provinces and vassal states. Mitanni's peak came in the early 14th century BC under Shuttarna II, when it formed a strong alliance with Egypt against the rising Hittite threat.

### Decline and Fall
Mitanni's decline began with internal succession wars and the growing power of its neighbors. The Hittite king Suppiluliuma I invaded Mitanni's Syrian vassals and sacked its capital, Washukanni, around 1345 BC. Simultaneously, the Assyrian king Ashur-uballit I attacked and annexed Mitanni territory. By the late 14th century, Mitanni was reduced to a Hittite vassal state in the Khabur Valley. In the 13th century, the Assyrian king Shalmaneser I finally annexed the remaining territory, ending Mitanni as an independent entity.

### Society and Administration
Mitanni was a hierarchical confederation. The king, a "Great King," ruled over directly administered provinces governed by officials and over vassal kingdoms. Society was stratified, with the *maryannu* chariot-warrior elite at the top, followed by commoners (*ḫupšu*), occupational specialists, and impoverished subjects. The administration used cuneiform Akkadian, often mixed with Hurrian. The kingdom's military relied heavily on chariotry, with troops organized into units and equipped with bronze-plated armor.

### Indo-Aryan Influences
The Mitanni ruling class bore Indo-Aryan throne names (e.g., Shuttarna, Tushratta). Indo-Aryan deities (Mitra, Varuna, Indra) were invoked in treaties, and technical terms, especially in horse-training texts like that of Kikkuli, are of Indo-Aryan origin. This indicates an Indo-Aryan elite that adopted the Hurrian language of the majority population, a key feature of the kingdom's cultural identity.

### Archaeology
Archaeological evidence for Mitanni is found across Upper Mesopotamia. Key sites include Tell Brak, which saw major development c. 1600 BC, and the capital Washukanni, likely at Tell Fekheriye. A characteristic artifact is Painted Nuzi Ware pottery. Excavations at Kemune in Iraqi Kurdistan revealed a Mitanni palace and cuneiform tablets, providing rare direct evidence of the kingdom's administration.

## Terms
- **Hurrians**: An ancient people who formed the main population of Mitanni and spoke a non-Indo-European, non-Semitic language.
- **Indo-Aryan**: A branch of the Indo-European language family; the Mitanni elite used Indo-Aryan names and religious terms.
- **Maryannu**: The chariot-warrior elite class that formed the military and social foundation of Mitanni power.
- **Vassal state**: A kingdom or city that owed allegiance and tribute to a more powerful overlord, like Mitanni.
- **Cuneiform**: A system of writing using wedge-shaped marks on clay tablets, used for Akkadian and Hurrian texts.
- **Ḫanigalbat/Naharin**: Alternative names for the Mitanni kingdom used by Assyrian and Egyptian sources, respectively.
- **Washukanni**: The capital city of the Mitanni kingdom, located on the Khabur River.
- **Painted Nuzi Ware**: A distinctive style of decorated pottery characteristic of the Mitanni period.

## Debates and open questions
- The precise location of the capital, Washukanni, is debated, though Tell Fekheriye is a leading candidate.
- The extent and nature of Indo-Aryan influence are disputed; some scholars argue it was limited to the elite, while others see it as foundational to the kingdom's establishment.
- The origins of Painted Nuzi Ware are unsolved, with possible links to Aegean Kamares Ware.
- The internal political dynamics and the exact sequence of events during the succession crises of the 14th century BC are unclear due to the lack of native Mitanni records.
