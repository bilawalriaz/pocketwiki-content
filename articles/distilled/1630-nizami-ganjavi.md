# Nizami Ganjavi

## Overview
Nizami Ganjavi (c. 1141–1209) was a 12th-century Persian poet widely regarded as the greatest romantic epic poet in Persian literature. Born in Ganja (in the Seljuq empire, now Azerbaijan), he spent his life in the South Caucasus, synthesizing pre-Islamic Iranian legends, Islamic philosophy, and diverse scientific knowledge into a new literary standard. Unlike court poets, he lived a relatively secluded life under the patronage of rival local dynasties, producing the *Khamsa* (Quintet)—five long narrative poems (*masnavi*) that redefined the genre by blending colloquial realism with profound philosophical and Sufi undertones. His work became the foundational model for Persianate literature across Iran, Central Asia, the Ottoman Empire, and Mughal India, influencing centuries of poetry, miniature painting, and music.

## Timeline
- **c. 1141** — Born in Ganja (Seljuq Empire) to a Persian family; father Yusuf, grandfather Zakki; mother Ra'isa of Kurdish origin.
- **Early life** — Orphaned; raised and educated by maternal uncle Khwaja Umar; mastered Arabic, Persian literature, mathematics, astronomy, medicine, law, philosophy, and arts.
- **1163 (or 1176)** — Completes *Makhzan al-Asrar* (Treasury of Mysteries), first poem of the *Khamsa*, dedicated to Fakhr al-Din Bahramshah.
- **1177–1180** — Composes *Khosrow o Shirin*, dedicated to Seljuk Sultan Toghril II and Eldiguzid rulers; first wife (Kipchak slave) dies upon completion; son Mohammad born.
- **1192** — Completes *Leyli o Majnun*, dedicated to Shirvanshah Akhsatan; persuaded by his son to undertake the Arabic legend.
- **1194 or 1196–1202** — Composes *Eskandar-Nameh* (Book of Alexander), dedicated to ruler of Ahar; depicts Alexander as Iranian knight/prophet.
- **1197** — Completes *Haft Peykar* (Seven Beauties), dedicated to Ala' al-Din Korp Arslan of Maragha; considered his masterpiece.
- **1209** — Dies in Ganja; buried in a mausoleum that becomes a cultural landmark.

## Body

### Life and Social Context
Nizami was born into an urban Persian family in Ganja, a city with a predominantly Iranian population in the South Caucasus. Orphaned early, he was raised by his maternal uncle Khwaja Umar, receiving an exceptionally broad education covering Islamic law, theology, philosophy, mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and the arts. He was not a court poet and does not appear in dynastic annals; biographical details derive mainly from his own poems and later *tazkerehs* (literary memoirs), which mix fact with legend. He married three times; his first wife, a Kipchak slave girl gifted by the ruler of Darband, was his "most beloved" and mother of his only son, Mohammad. She died upon the completion of *Khosrow o Shirin*; his subsequent two wives also died prematurely, each death coinciding with the finish of an epic. The region’s cultural diversity—evidenced by the anthology *Nozhat al-Majales* (c. 1250)—featured poets from working-class backgrounds using colloquial expressions and northwestern Pahlavi dialect, a milieu that shaped Nizami’s realistic style.

### Education and Intellectual Synthesis
Nizami earned the honorific *Hakim* (Sage) for his encyclopedic learning. He was not a systematic philosopher like Avicenna or a theoretical Sufi like Ibn 'Arabi, but a "philosopher and gnostic" who synthesized diverse Islamic thought traditions. His poems demonstrate mastery of *Koranic* exegesis, Iranian myths, history, ethics, esoteric thought, music, and visual arts. This synthesis allowed him to bridge pre-Islamic and Islamic Iran, creating a "new standard of literary achievement" that unified the ancient world with contemporary Persian culture. His favorite pastime was reading Ferdowsi’s *Shahnameh*, which he used as a primary source for three epics, while adopting the rhetorical sophistication and meter of Gorgani’s *Vis o Ramin* for *Khosrow o Shirin*.

### The Khamsa (Five Treasures)
Nizami’s magnum opus is the *Khamsa* (or *Panj Ganj*), five *masnavi* poems totaling roughly 30,000 distichs. The first, *Makhzan al-Asrar* (c. 1163/1176), is an ethico-philosophical work of 2,250 distichs influenced by Sanai’s *Hadiqat al-Haqiqa*; it uses twenty discourses to preach kingly justice, spiritual awareness, and preparation for the afterlife. The remaining four are medieval romances reworking earlier sources with structural unity and psychological depth.

*Khosrow o Shirin* (1177–1180, ~6,500 distichs) adapts a *Shahnameh* episode on Sasanian King Khosrow II and Armenian Princess Shirin. It introduces the selfless lover Farhad as a foil to the king, imitates a key scene and the *hazaj* meter from *Vis o Ramin*, and integrates astrology. *Leyli o Majnun* (1192, ~4,600 distichs) transforms an Arabic Bedouin legend into a Persian epic: the poet Qays (Majnun) loves Layla, is thwarted by her father, wanders the desert composing poetry, and dies at her grave. Nizami added humanitarian touches (Majnun saving animals during war) and Persian epic techniques. *Eskandar-Nameh* (1194/1196–1202, ~10,500 distichs) draws on the *Alexander Romance* and Quranic *Dhu'l-Qarnayn*. It splits into *Sharaf-Nama* (Alexander as world conqueror) and *Iqbal-Nama* (Alexander as philosopher-king debating Greek/Indian sages, modeled on Plato’s *Republic*), portraying Alexander as an Iranian knight. *Haft Peykar* (1197), dedicated to the ruler of Maragha, romanticizes the life of Sasanian King Bahram V Gur. Bahram marries seven princesses from the seven climes, each housed in a color-coded dome aligned with a planet; each tells a sensuous story. The frame narrative shifts to justice when Bahram discovers corruption, executes his minister, converts the domes to fire temples, and mysteriously disappears while hunting—a pun on *gūr* (wild ass/tomb).

### Poetic Style and Innovation
Nizami mastered the *masnavi* (double-rhymed verse), bringing a "colloquial and realistic style to the Persian epic." He moved beyond courtly panegyric to explore psychological realism, complex narrative structures (letters, dialogues, frame tales), and the interplay of sacred and profane love. His rhetoric, heavily influenced by Gorgani, is noted for "richness and fineness of metaphor, accuracy, and profundity of psychological observation." He intertwined poetry with science—botany, astronomy, mineralogy—reflecting a worldview where knowledge and art were inseparable. While his *Khamsa* appears romantic on the surface, later poets like Jami argued they are "a mask for the essential truths and for the explanation of divine knowledge."

### Legacy and Influence
The *Khamsa* became the supreme model for Persianate narrative poetry. It was imitated by major poets including Amir Khusro, Jami, Hatefi, Fuzuli, Ali-Shir Nava'i, and Ahmad Khani across Iran, Central Asia, Ottoman Turkey, and Mughal India. The *Khamsa* was the most frequently illustrated text after the *Shahnameh* in Persian and Mughal miniature painting (e.g., the 1590s Mughal manuscript for Emperor Akbar). In the Soviet era, Azerbaijani composers Uzeyir Hajibeyov and Gara Garayev created ballets and songs from *Haft Peykar* and *Leyli o Majnun*; a minor planet (3770 Nizami) was named in 1974. In Azerbaijan, his mausoleum in Ganja (rebuilt 1991) and the Nizami Museum of Literature in Baku honor him; 2021 was declared "Year of Nizami." Western reception includes Goethe’s praise for his portrayal of "deepest love" and Eric Clapton’s 1970 hit "Layla," inspired by the poem. A 2004 Cambridge conference affirmed his status as a "Key to the Treasure of Hakim."

## Terms
- ****Masnavi**** — A poetic form of rhyming couplets (double-rhymed verses) used for long narrative poems in Persian, Arabic, Turkish, and Urdu literature.
- ****Khamsa / Panj Ganj**** — "Quintet" or "Five Treasures"; Nizami’s set of five long narrative poems: *Makhzan al-Asrar*, *Khosrow o Shirin*, *Leyli o Majnun*, *Eskandar-Nameh*, *Haft Peykar*.
- ****Hakim**** — Honorific meaning "the Sage" or "Wise One," denoting a scholar-poet master of philosophy, science, and esoteric knowledge.
- ****Tazkereh**** — A genre of biographical anthologies compiling lives, sayings, and critical assessments of poets; primary (though legendary) source for Nizami’s biography.
- ****Shahnameh**** — Ferdowsi’s 11th-century "Book of Kings," the Persian national epic; Nizami’s primary source for pre-Islamic history and three of his romances.
- ****Vis o Ramin**** — 11th-century Persian romance by Gorgani; provided the meter (*hazaj*), rhetorical style, and specific scenes (e.g., lovers arguing in snow) for *Khosrow o Shirin*.
- ****Dhu'l-Qarnayn**** — "Two-Horned One," a Quranic figure identified with Alexander the Great; basis for the Islamic Alexander legend in *Eskandar-Nameh*.
- ****Iqbal-Nama / Sharaf-Nama**** — The two books of *Eskandar-Nameh*: *Sharaf-Nama* (Glory Book) covers Alexander’s conquests; *Iqbal-Nama* (Fortune Book) covers his philosophical maturation.
- ****Nozhat al-Majales**** — c. 1250 anthology of Persian quatrains from 116 poets of northwestern Iran (including 24 from Ganja), revealing the region’s colloquial, diverse literary scene.
- ****Gūr**** — Persian pun meaning both "wild ass" (onager) and "tomb"; central to the ending of *Haft Peykar* where Bahram Gur hunts the ass but finds his grave.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Ethnic and national attribution:** Soviet and post-Soviet scholarship (especially Azerbaijani) has emphasized Nizami’s "Azerbaijani" or "Turkic" identity based on his birthplace, while Iranian and Western scholars (e.g., De Blois, Encyclopaedia Iranica) stress his Persian language, self-identification as Iranian, and the Iranian cultural milieu of Ganja. The article notes his mother was Kurdish and father possibly from Qom; the debate remains politically charged.
*   **Dating of works:** Precise composition dates are uncertain; *Makhzan al-Asrar* is dated 1163 or 1176; *Eskandar-Nameh* 1194 or 1196–1202. The sequence of the last two epics (*Eskandar-Nameh* vs. *Haft Peykar*) is also debated.
*   **Identity of first wife:** Modern scholars (Vahid Dastgerdi) proposed her name was Afaq based on a verse; others (Said Nafisi) argue *afaq* means "horizon" in context, not a proper name.
*   **Nature of *Khalwat* (spiritual vigils):** In *Makhzan al-Asrar*, Nizami describes solitary vigils; scholars debate whether these reflect actual Sufi practice or a "literary fantasy" of the spiritually inclined poet.
*   **Sufi influence:** While Nizami uses Sufi terminology and themes (especially in *Makhzan al-Asrar* and *Haft Peykar*), he is not a systematic Sufi theorist; the extent and orthodoxy of his mysticism remain interpretive questions.
