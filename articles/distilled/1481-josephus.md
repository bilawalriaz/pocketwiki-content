# Josephus

## Overview
Flavius Josephus (c. AD 37 – c. 100), born Yosef ben Mattityahu in Jerusalem, was a priestly aristocrat who became the principal historian of the First Jewish–Roman War (AD 66–70). After commanding Jewish forces in Galilee, he surrendered at Yodfat (AD 67), predicting his captor Vespasian would become emperor. When this occurred, Vespasian freed him; Josephus adopted the Flavian name, gained Roman citizenship, and accompanied Titus during the siege of Jerusalem (AD 70). Writing under Flavian patronage in Rome, he produced *The Jewish War* (c. 75) and *Antiquities of the Jews* (c. 94). These works are the primary extra-biblical sources for Second Temple Judaism, the Hasmonean and Herodian dynasties, and figures such as Pontius Pilate, John the Baptist, and Jesus. His legacy is contested: traditional scholarship viewed him as a Pharisee and traitor; modern scholars debate whether he was a Pharisee by conviction or an aristocratic priest accommodating Roman power, while his historiography shifts from critical to rhetorical modes across his corpus.

## Timeline
- **c. AD 37** — Born in Jerusalem to a priestly father (Matthias) and a mother of Hasmonean royal descent.
- **c. AD 63** — Travels to Rome (age ~26) to negotiate release of imprisoned priests; succeeds with aid of Poppaea.
- **AD 66** — Appointed military governor of Galilee at outbreak of First Jewish–Roman War; fortifies towns, trains 65,000 troops.
- **AD 67 (July)** — Besieged at Yodfat (Jotapata) for six weeks; survives suicide pact, surrenders to Vespasian and Titus.
- **AD 69** — Released after Vespasian becomes emperor; predicts Vespasian’s rise, attributing it to divine revelation.
- **AD 70** — Serves as Titus’s translator during siege of Jerusalem; parents held hostage by Simon bar Giora.
- **AD 71** — Moves to Rome; granted citizenship, pension, and Flavian patronage; marries Alexandrian Jewish woman (third wife).
- **c. AD 75** — Completes *The Jewish War* (7 volumes, Greek), blaming "fanatics" and corrupt Roman governors for the revolt.
- **c. AD 94** — Completes *Antiquities of the Jews* (21 volumes) and *Life* (autobiographical defense against Justus of Tiberias).
- **c. AD 97** — Publishes *Against Apion* (2 volumes), defending Judaism’s antiquity against Greek critics.
- **c. AD 100** — Death (approximate).

## Body

### Biography and the War
Josephus was born into Jerusalem’s elite: his father Matthias belonged to the Jehoiarib priestly order (first of 24), and his mother claimed Hasmonean royalty. Educated alongside his brother Matthias, he traveled to Rome c. AD 63 to free priests imprisoned by Nero, succeeding with help from Nero’s wife Poppaea. At the war’s outbreak (AD 66), the Sanhedrin appointed him governor of Galilee. He faced immediate internal division: Sepphoris and Tiberias preferred Roman peace, while John of Gischala, backed by the Sanhedrin, challenged Josephus’s authority. Josephus fortified over a dozen sites in Upper and Lower Galilee and trained 65,000 men. After Roman forces under Placidus and Vespasian arrived, Josephus was besieged at Yodfat for six weeks (lunar month Tammuz, 13th year of Nero). When the city fell, he hid in a cave with 40 companions; they drew lots to kill each other rather than surrender. Josephus and one other survived the lots and surrendered to the Romans (July AD 67).

### Defection, Prophecy, and Roman Patronage
While captive, Josephus claimed a divine revelation: God had decided to "punish" the Jews, given "fortune" to the Romans, and chosen Josephus to "announce the things that are to come." He predicted Vespasian would become emperor. When Vespasian acceded in AD 69, he freed Josephus, who adopted the family name Flavius. Josephus interpreted the Temple’s destruction (AD 70) as divine punishment for Jewish sins, urging submission to Rome while maintaining the covenant remained intact pending repentance. He accompanied Titus to Rome (AD 71), received citizenship, a pension, and an estate in conquered Judaea. Vespasian arranged a marriage to a captured Jewish woman (later divorced); Josephus married three more times (an Alexandrian Jewess c. 71, a Cretan Greek Jewess c. 75), fathering five sons, two surviving childhood.

### Scholarship: The "Classical" vs. "Modern" Concept
Traditional ("classical") scholarship identified Josephus as a Pharisee and traitor to the Jewish nation. Mid-20th-century scholars revised this ("modern concept"), viewing him as a Pharisee-patriot and credible historian. Steve Mason (1991) challenged both, arguing Josephus was an orthodox aristocratic priest who associated with the Pharisees’ philosophical school out of deference, not conviction. Rabbinic sources marginalized him for a millennium; the 10th-century Hebrew *Yosippon* (based on Latin *Pseudo-Hegesippus*) reintroduced him to Jews. Jewish attitudes softened during the 19th-century Haskalah (Enlightenment), when neutral translations appeared, and the Masada narrative was reinterpreted from fanaticism to heroic resistance.

### Historical Value and Archaeology
Josephus provides the primary extra-biblical account of the post-Exilic period, Maccabees, Hasmoneans, Herod the Great, the high-priestly succession, and groups (Sadducees, Pharisees, Essenes, Zealots). He notes 240 Galilean towns/villages, customs (linen curtain at door, Sabbath meal at noon, polygamy), and figures (Pilate, Agrippa I/II, John the Baptist, James, Jesus). Carl Ritter (1840s) warned Josephus was "controlled by political motives" to honor Jews before Greeks/Romans, filling his history with "distortions and exaggerations." Modern historians use him with caution. His description of Herod’s tomb at Herodium guided Ehud Netzer’s 35-year search (discovered 2007), though Patrich and Arubas (2013) challenged the identification as too modest; Roi Porat maintains it is Herod’s.

### Audience and Motives for *Antiquities*
Scholars debate Josephus’s audience. *Antiquities* explains basic Judean laws/customs, assuming a Gentile readership, yet some argue he wrote primarily for Jews to demonstrate loyalty after the "traitorous War" (repentance motive). Other motives: dispel misrepresentation of Jewish origins, apologize to Greek cities of the Diaspora to protect Jews, or petition Roman authorities against persecution. Isaac Abarbanel (15th c.) differentiated a Hebrew version for Jews from Greek/Latin versions for Gentiles.

### Historiography: Critical vs. Rhetorical
In *War*’s preface, Josephus criticizes historians who exaggerate Roman greatness and diminish Jewish actions, pledging accuracy for both sides while admitting he cannot suppress his sadness. In *Antiquities*’ preface, he asserts all events succeed well by God’s reward, yet promises to follow records "in the order of time" without addition or subtraction, distinguishing history from philosophy. Louis H. Feldman argues *War* commits to critical historiography, while *Antiquities* shifts to rhetorical historiography (the Hellenistic norm). The title *Antiquities* (archaeology) implies a systematic, topical Jewish history from origins—covering institutions, religion, and private life—rather than strict political chronology.

### Works
1.  **The Jewish War** (c. 75, 7 vols., Greek): Originally addressed to "upper barbarians" (Mesopotamian Jews) in "paternal tongue" (Western Aramaic). Covers Maccabees to fall of Masada and Roman victory celebrations. Blames "unrepresentative, over-zealous fanatics" who displaced aristocratic leaders, and corrupt Roman governors.
2.  **Antiquities of the Jews** (c. 94, 21 vols.): Completed under Domitian. Jewish history from creation for Greek/Roman audience; apologetic for Jewish antiquity/universal significance. Sources: Hebrew Scriptures, eyewitness to war. Presents Tanakh figures as philosopher-leaders; includes autobiographical appendix defending his conduct.
3.  **Life** (c. 94–99): Autobiographical defense against Justus of Tiberias’s allegations, revisiting his Galilee command.
4.  **Against Apion** (c. 97, 2 vols.): Defense of Judaism as classical religion/philosophy, stressing antiquity vs. Greek novelty; refutes Apion and Manetho.
5.  **Spurious**: *Discourse to the Greeks concerning Hades* (adaptation of Hippolytus).

### Literary Transmission
Popular with 4th-century+ Christians as independent witness to Jesus; accessible in Greek East. Latin translations often abbreviated (e.g., 4th-c. *Pseudo-Hegesippus*). Gutenberg Press spurred vernacular translations (mostly from Latin). First standard Greek text in French (1544, Arnoldus Arlenius); first English (Thomas Lodge, 1602). William Whiston’s 1732 English translation (from 1544 Greek) became the post-Bible staple for English Christians. Benedictus Niese’s *editio maior* (1885–95) is the standard Greek edition; Loeb Classical Library uses Niese/Thackeray. Old Slavonic translations exist but contain Christian interpolations.

## Terms
- ****Hasmonean dynasty**** — Jewish ruling family (c. 140–37 BC) descended from the Maccabees; Josephus’s mother claimed royal descent from them.
- ****Jehoiarib**** — First of the 24 priestly orders serving in the Jerusalem Temple; Josephus’s paternal lineage.
- ****Sanhedrin**** — Supreme Jewish council in Jerusalem; initially supported John of Gischala against Josephus in Galilee.
- ****Yodfat (Jotapata)**** — Galilean fortress where Josephus commanded resistance; fell to Vespasian after six-week siege (AD 67).
- ****Flavian dynasty**** — Roman imperial house (Vespasian, Titus, Domitian); Josephus became their client, adopting the nomen *Flavius*.
- ****Pharisees**** — Jewish sect emphasizing oral law and resurrection; Josephus’s affiliation debated (classical: member; modern: patriot; Mason: aristocrat deferring to their philosophy).
- ****Pseudo-Hegesippus**** — 4th-century Latin abbreviation/translation of *The Jewish War*; primary vector for Josephus in medieval West.
- ****Yosippon**** — 10th-century Hebrew chronicle paraphrasing *Pseudo-Hegesippus* and Latin *Antiquities*; main Jewish access to Josephus until Haskalah.
- ****Haskalah**** — Jewish Enlightenment (19th century); produced neutral vernacular translations, rehabilitating Josephus’s reputation among Jews.
- ****Rhetorical historiography**** — Hellenistic norm prioritizing literary artistry and moral philosophy over critical source analysis; Feldman identifies this mode in *Antiquities*.

## Debates and open questions
- **Pharisaic affiliation**: Was Josephus a convinced Pharisee (classical view), a Pharisee-patriot (modern view), or an aristocratic priest deferring to Pharisaic philosophy without membership (Mason)?
- **Traitor vs. pragmatist**: Does his surrender at Yodfat (surviving a suicide pact) and Flavian patronage constitute betrayal, or pragmatic survival to preserve Jewish history?
- **Audience of *Antiquities***: Written primarily for Gentiles (explains basics) or Jews (repentance/apologia)? Unresolved.
- **Historical reliability**: To what extent do political motives (honoring Jews before Romans, blaming "fanatics") distort his accounts of the war, geography, and customs?
- **Herod’s tomb**: Does the Herodium structure Netzer identified match Josephus’s description, or is it too modest (Patrich/Arubas)?
- **Testimonium Flavianum**: The *Antiquities* passage on Jesus (not detailed in source text) is widely debated for Christian interpolation vs. authentic core.
- **Historiographical shift**: Does the move from critical (*War*) to rhetorical (*Antiquities*) historiography reflect genre change, patronage pressure, or intellectual evolution?
