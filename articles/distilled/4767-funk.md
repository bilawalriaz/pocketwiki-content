# Funk

## Overview
Funk is a music genre that originated in African-American communities in the mid-1960s. It is defined by a strong rhythmic groove, often at slower tempos, that prioritizes rhythm over melody and harmony. Funk emerged from a mixture of earlier African-American genres and became a major influence on subsequent styles like hip-hop and electronic dance music.

## Timeline
- **Late 1940s** — New Orleans musicians like Professor Longhair incorporate Afro-Cuban clave patterns into R&B, creating a rhythmic template for funk.
- **Mid-1960s** — James Brown develops his signature "on the one" groove, emphasizing the downbeat and syncopated, interlocking parts.
- **1967** — Dyke and the Blazers release "Funky Broadway," one of the first soul-era records with "funky" in the title.
- **Late 1960s** — Sly and the Family Stone and Parliament-Funkadelic begin fusing funk with psychedelic rock and jazz influences.
- **1970s** — Funk achieves peak mainstream visibility; artists like Kool & the Gang, Earth, Wind & Fire, and Bootsy Collins popularize the genre.
- **1975** — Parliament releases "Chocolate City," using metaphor to address Black political power.
- **1982** — Afrika Bambaataa's "Planet Rock" pioneers electro-funk, using synthesizers and drum machines.
- **1980s** — Synthesizers and drum machines increasingly replace live instrumentation in funk production.
- **1990s** — Funk samples become foundational to hip-hop, especially West Coast G-funk.
- **2000s** — A "nu-funk" revival scene emerges, influenced by rare funk records.

## Body

### Origins and Etymology
Funk's roots are in African-American musical traditions, blending gospel, soul, jazz, R&B, and blues. The word "funk" originally meant a strong odor, derived from Latin *fumigare* ("to smoke"). In early 20th-century jazz slang, it came to mean something "deeply or strongly felt." In African-American communities, it carried a positive connotation of a musician's hard-working, sweaty, and "superlative" performance. The term was first used in music titles around 1907, and by the 1950s, it described a slow, sexy, riff-oriented, and danceable rhythm.

### Musical Characteristics
Funk is rhythmically driven, with a strong emphasis on the "groove." It often uses a slower tempo than other popular music, allowing for complex subdivisions of the beat, particularly sixteenth-note patterns. The rhythm is built on interlocking parts from bass, drums, and guitar, creating a "hypnotic" feel. Harmonically, funk uses extended chords from jazz (like minor elevenths) but often stays on a single-chord vamp. Vocals are percussive and energetic, and lyrics frequently address social issues facing the African-American community, using metaphorical language and double entendres.

### Instrumentation
The **bass guitar** is central, playing repetitive, syncopated lines that form the song's "hook." Techniques like **slap and popping** (thumb-slapping low notes and finger-popping high notes) are distinctive. **Drumming** focuses on feel and groove, with economical fills and techniques like open hi-hat accents. **Electric guitar** plays percussive, muted "chicken scratch" rhythms, often using two interlocking parts. **Horn sections** (saxophone, trumpet, trombone) play short, rhythmic stabs. **Keyboards** range from acoustic piano and Hammond organ to synthesizers like the Minimoog, which could replace the bass guitar.

### History and Development
James Brown is credited as the seminal funk innovator. In the mid-1960s, he shifted the rhythmic emphasis from the backbeat to the downbeat ("the one"), creating a new, harder-driving sound with hits like "Papa's Got a Brand New Bag" (1965). His arranger, Pee Wee Ellis, and drummer, Clyde Stubblefield, incorporated New Orleans rhythmic techniques. In the late 1960s and 1970s, groups like Sly and the Family Stone and Parliament-Funkadelic expanded funk by fusing it with psychedelic rock and jazz. The 1970s were funk's peak mainstream era, with numerous hit artists. In the 1980s, electronic instruments like synthesizers and drum machines began to dominate production, leading to subgenres like electro-funk. Since the 1990s, funk's primary influence has been through sampling in hip-hop and electronic music.

### Derivatives and Influence
Funk has spawned numerous subgenres, including **funk rock** (fusing funk with rock), **go-go** (a Washington, D.C.-based blend of funk, R&B, and hip-hop), **boogie** (electronic post-disco funk), **electro funk** (using electronic instruments), **funk metal**, and **G-funk** (gangsta rap mixed with psychedelic funk). Funk samples and breakbeats are extensively used in hip-hop and electronic dance music.

## Terms
- **Groove**: The rhythmic feel of a piece of music, created by the interplay of instruments, that makes it danceable.
- **The One**: The heavy emphasis on the first beat of every measure, a signature innovation of James Brown.
- **Slap and Pop**: A bass guitar technique using thumb-slapped low notes and finger-popped high notes for a percussive effect.
- **Chicken Scratch**: A percussive electric guitar technique involving muted strumming and quick releases.
- **Vamp**: A repeated musical phrase or chord progression, often a single chord in funk.
- **Extended Chords**: Complex chords with added notes beyond the basic triad, such as sevenths, ninths, or elevenths.
- **Call and Response**: A musical pattern where a lead vocalist or instrument is answered by another voice or instrument.
- **Double Entendre**: A phrase with two meanings, often used in funk lyrics to imply sexual themes while evading censorship.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise origins of funk are debated, with scholars noting the importance of New Orleans rhythmic traditions and the innovations of James Brown.
- The role and recognition of women in funk's history is a subject of scholarly critique, noting that the genre's narrative has often been male-centered, overlooking pioneers like Betty Davis and Labelle.
- The extent to which funk's lyrical messages were understood by mainstream white audiences in the 1970s is discussed as a factor in its chart performance.
