# Louis Armstrong

## Overview
Louis Armstrong (1901–1971) was an American trumpeter and vocalist whose innovations fundamentally reshaped jazz from a collective ensemble art into a soloist’s medium. Rising from poverty in New Orleans, he mastered the cornet in a juvenile detention home band before joining King Oliver’s Creole Jazz Band in Chicago and Fletcher Henderson’s orchestra in New York, where his rhythmic drive and harmonic sophistication influenced a generation of musicians. His 1920s Hot Five and Hot Seven recordings established the vocabulary of jazz improvisation and popularized scat singing, while his later All-Stars ensemble and global tours made him a beloved entertainer and cultural ambassador. Armstrong’s gravelly voice and charismatic stage presence crossed racial barriers, earning him mainstream popularity rare for a Black artist of his era, though his public silence on civil rights drew criticism until his forceful 1957 condemnation of the Little Rock school crisis. He received the Grammy Award for Best Male Vocal Performance for "Hello, Dolly!" in 1965 and a posthumous Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award in 1972.

## Timeline
- **Aug 4, 1901** — Born in New Orleans (date debated; Armstrong claimed July 4, 1900).
- **Dec 31, 1912** — Arrested for firing a blank; sentenced to Colored Waif’s Home, where he received formal cornet instruction from Peter Davis.
- **1918–1921** — Played on Mississippi riverboats with Fate Marable’s band, learning sight-reading and expanding his musicianship.
- **1922** — Joined King Oliver’s Creole Jazz Band in Chicago; made first recordings with Oliver for Gennett Records (1923).
- **1924–1925** — Played with Fletcher Henderson Orchestra in New York; switched to trumpet; recorded with Bessie Smith and Sidney Bechet.
- **Nov 1925–1928** — Led Hot Five and Hot Seven sessions for Okeh Records, producing seminal solos ("West End Blues," "Potato Head Blues") and the first hit scat recording ("Heebie Jeebies," 1926).
- **1929** — Returned to New York; featured in *Hot Chocolates*; recorded "Ain't Misbehavin'," his biggest-selling record to date.
- **1937** — Became first African American to host a nationally sponsored radio broadcast (CBS).
- **Aug 13, 1947** — Manager Joe Glaser dissolved the big band; formed Louis Armstrong and His All-Stars, a small traditional jazz group.
- **Feb 21, 1949** — First jazz musician on the cover of *Time* magazine.
- **1956–1959** — Collaborated with Ella Fitzgerald on three albums: *Ella and Louis* (1956), *Ella and Louis Again* (1957), *Porgy and Bess* (1959).
- **1957** — Publicly denounced Governor Orval Faubus and President Eisenhower over Little Rock desegregation crisis; stated he would cancel a planned State Department tour of USSR.
- **1964** — "Hello, Dolly!" hit No. 1 on *Billboard* Hot 100, making Armstrong (age 62 years, nine months, five days) the oldest person to top the chart; dislodged The Beatles.
- **1968** — "What a Wonderful World" topped UK charts for a month.
- **Jul 6, 1971** — Died of a heart attack in Corona, Queens, New York.

## Body

### Early Life and Musical Foundations
Armstrong grew up in New Orleans’ "Battlefield" district in extreme poverty. After his father abandoned the family, he was raised by his grandmother until age five, then returned to his mother. A pivotal relationship formed with the Karnoffskys, a Lithuanian Jewish family for whom he worked; they advanced him money for his first cornet and treated him with kindness amid the discrimination they also faced. Armstrong wore a Star of David for the rest of his life in their honor. At age 11, he dropped out of school and sang in a street quartet. On New Year’s Eve 1912, he fired a borrowed gun and was committed to the Colored Waif’s Home. There, Peter Davis taught him cornet and made him bandleader, providing his first structured musical education. Upon release in 1914, he apprenticed in local honky-tonks and brass bands, absorbing the nascent jazz idiom from pioneers like King Oliver.

### Riverboats, Chicago, and the Henderson Orchestra
From 1918 to 1921, Armstrong played on Streckfus Steamers riverboats with Fate Marable’s band. Marable insisted on sight-reading and written arrangements, an experience Armstrong called "going to the University." In 1922, Oliver summoned him to Chicago to play second cornet at Lincoln Gardens. The band’s 1923 Gennett recordings—acoustic sessions requiring Armstrong to stand 15 feet from Oliver to balance volume—capture his early power. Lil Hardin, the band’s pianist and Armstrong’s future second wife, urged him to leave Oliver and develop an independent voice. In 1924, Armstrong joined the Fletcher Henderson Orchestra in New York, the premier Black dance band. He switched to trumpet to blend with the section, and his swinging, blues-inflected phrasing transformed the orchestra’s feel, directly influencing tenor saxophonist Coleman Hawkins. During this period, he recorded extensively with blues singers Bessie Smith, Ma Rainey, and Alberta Hunter.

### The Hot Five and Hot Seven: Defining Jazz Soloing
Returning to Chicago in 1925, Armstrong recorded under his own name for Okeh. The Hot Five (Kid Ory, Johnny Dodds, Johnny St. Cyr, Lil Armstrong, no drummer) and later Hot Seven (adding drums and tuba) produced 24 records in 12 months. These sessions shifted jazz from collective polyphony to featured improvisation. Armstrong’s solos on "Cornet Chop Suey," "Potato Head Blues," and the 1928 "West End Blues"—with its iconic opening cadenza—demonstrated unprecedented rhythmic sophistication, harmonic awareness, and melodic invention. He also recorded the first hit scat vocal, "Heebie Jeebies" (1926), allegedly after dropping sheet music. His 1928 duet "Weather Bird" with pianist Earl Hines remains a landmark of collaborative improvisation. Young musicians nationwide memorized these records, establishing Armstrong as the primary architect of the jazz solo.

### Vocal Innovation and Mainstream Stardom
Armstrong’s singing paralleled his trumpet in influence. His 1929 recording of "Ain't Misbehavin'" from the show *Hot Chocolates* became a massive hit. He exploited the new RCA ribbon microphone (1931) to develop an intimate, "crooning" timbre that shaped Bing Crosby and subsequent pop vocalists. His radical re-phrasing of "Lazy River" (1931)—ignoring the written melody, syncopating phrases, and launching into scat—codified jazz vocal interpretation. The gravelly timbre, syncopated phrasing, and trumpet-like improvisational approach became the template for jazz singing. Despite the Great Depression, Armstrong maintained visibility through film (*Pennies from Heaven*, 1936), radio (first Black host of a sponsored national show, 1937), and relentless touring.

### The All-Stars and Global Ambassadorship
Big-band economics collapsed in the 1940s. In 1947, manager Joe Glaser replaced Armstrong’s orchestra with a six-piece "All-Stars" group (initially Jack Teagarden, Earl Hines, Barney Bigard, Arvell Shaw, Cozy Cole), reviving traditional New Orleans jazz for a new audience. The All-Stars toured globally for over 20 years, performing 300+ nights annually. Armstrong became "Ambassador Satch," sponsored by the State Department to tour Africa, Europe, and Asia. His 1964 "Hello, Dolly!" topped the *Billboard* chart, displacing The Beatles and making him the oldest person (62 years, nine months, five days) with a No. 1 hit. He continued recording standards like "What a Wonderful World" (1967) until health forced his retirement.

### Civil Rights and Public Persona
Armstrong generally avoided political statements, drawing criticism from younger Black activists who viewed his grinning, handkerchief-waving stage persona as "Uncle Tomism." However, in 1957, during the Little Rock Central High School crisis, he erupted in a televised interview: Governor Faubus was "uneducated," President Eisenhower had "no guts" and was "two-faced," and he would not represent the U.S. in the Soviet Union while "they're treating my people in the South." The FBI opened a file; a Mississippi radio station banned his records; but Jackie Robinson and Lena Horne publicly supported him. Armstrong insisted on speaking for himself, firing a road manager who tried to walk back his comments.

### Health, Personal Habits, and Death
Armstrong’s aggressive trumpet technique and narrow mouthpieces caused chronic lip ulceration; a 1930s European tour forced a year’s layoff. He self-treated with salves and razor-blade excision of scar tissue, only seeking medical care in his final years. He was a lifelong marijuana user (arrested in 1930), advocating it over alcohol, and a devotee of the laxative Swiss Kriss, which he distributed to friends and royalty alike. He married four times (Daisy Parker, Lil Hardin, Alpha Smith, Lucille Wilson), adopted a disabled cousin’s son (Clarence), and acknowledged a probable daughter (Sharon Preston-Folta) in a 1955 letter to Glaser. After a 1959 heart attack in Italy and recurring heart/kidney failure in 1968–69, he played a final engagement at the Waldorf-Astoria in March 1971 against medical advice, suffered another heart attack, and died in his sleep on July 6, 1971, in Corona, Queens.

## Terms
- **Collective improvisation**: Early jazz style where multiple front-line instruments (cornet, clarinet, trombone) improvise simultaneously over a rhythm section; Armstrong shifted focus to **solo improvisation**.
- **Scat singing**: Improvised vocal jazz using nonsense syllables; Armstrong’s 1926 "Heebie Jeebies" was the first hit recording of the form.
- **Hot Five / Hot Seven**: Armstrong’s Okeh recording groups (1925–1928) that established the jazz soloist’s vocabulary; personnel included Kid Ory, Johnny Dodds, Earl Hines, and others.
- **All-Stars**: Armstrong’s small traditional jazz ensemble (1947–1971), formed after big bands became economically unviable; featured rotating all-star alumni like Jack Teagarden and Earl Hines.
- **RCA ribbon microphone**: Velocity microphone introduced in 1931 that captured vocal warmth and nuance, enabling the intimate "crooning" style Armstrong and Bing Crosby popularized.
- **Satchelmouth / Satchmo**: Nickname derived from "satchel mouth," referencing either his large mouth or a childhood habit of storing coins in his cheeks.
- **Little Rock Crisis (1957)**: Arkansas Governor Orval Faubus blocked school desegregation; Armstrong’s public condemnation of Faubus and Eisenhower made national headlines.
- **Voyager Golden Record**: NASA time capsule launched in 1977; includes Armstrong’s "Melancholy Blues" as a representative of Earth’s sounds.
- **Cornet vs. Trumpet**: Armstrong began on cornet (conical bore, mellower); switched to trumpet (cylindrical bore, brighter) in 1924 to blend with Henderson’s section.
- **Cutting contest**: Informal competitive jam session where musicians alternate solos to demonstrate superiority; Armstrong earned his early reputation in these.

## Debates and open questions
- **Birth date**: Armstrong claimed July 4, 1900; baptismal records suggest August 4, 1901; scholars debate the accuracy of both.
- **"Heebie Jeebies" scat origin**: Armstrong told the story of dropping sheet music and improvising nonsense syllables; some historians consider it apocryphal, though Armstrong confirmed it in memoirs.
- **"Russian Lullaby" memoir anachronism**: In a 1969 memoir, Armstrong quoted lyrics to Irving Berlin’s 1927 "Russian Lullaby" as a childhood memory from 1907; likely a conflation caused by his doctor sharing the sheet music during recovery.
- **Civil rights legacy**: Critics then and now debate whether Armstrong’s entertainer persona undermined the movement or whether his 1957 outburst and quiet integration of white venues (e.g., integrated bands, hotel suites) constituted effective activism.
- **Paternity of Sharon Preston-Folta**: DNA testing has not been conducted; Armstrong’s 1955 letter to Glaser affirms belief in paternity and orders support, but the claim remains unproven.
- **Freemasonry claim**: Often cited as a Prince Hall Mason; the Louis Armstrong House Museum states no such lodge existed and Armstrong identified as a Knight of Pythias.
