# Jin dynasty (1115–1234)

## Overview
The Jin dynasty (1115–1234) was a Jurchen-led empire that conquered northern China from the Liao and Song dynasties, ruling a multicultural state for over a century before its destruction by the Mongols. It represents a key period of non-Han rule in Chinese history, marked by the tension between preserving Jurchen identity and adopting Chinese administrative and cultural practices.

## Timeline
- **1115** — Wanyan Aguda declares the Jin dynasty after rebelling against the Liao.
- **1125** — The Jin conquer the Liao dynasty, capturing Emperor Tianzuo.
- **1127** — The Jingkang Incident: Jin forces capture the Song capital Kaifeng and the Song emperors.
- **1142** — Treaty of Shaoxing establishes the Huai River as the border with the Southern Song.
- **1161** — Emperor Wanyan Liang launches a failed invasion of the Song and is assassinated.
- **1164** — Treaty of Longxing ends the war with the Song, ushering in 40 years of peace.
- **1211** — Mongols under Genghis Khan begin their invasion of the Jin Empire.
- **1215** — The Mongols capture the Jin Central Capital (modern Beijing).
- **1232–33** — The Siege of Kaifeng (Bianjing) features advanced gunpowder warfare.
- **1234** — The Jin dynasty falls to the Mongols and their Song allies at Caizhou.

## Body

### Origins and Rise
The Jin dynasty emerged from the Jurchen people, descendants of the Tungusic-speaking Mohe who lived in Northeast China. The ruling Wanyan clan consolidated power under leaders like Wugunai (r. 1021–1074) and his grandson Aguda. Resentment against the Khitan Liao dynasty, which extorted tribute and abused Jurchen customs, fueled Aguda's rebellion. In 1115, he declared himself emperor, adopting Chinese imperial traditions. The Jin quickly conquered the Liao by 1125, aided by defections from the Liao's Balhae population, who shared common Mohe ancestry with the Jurchens.

### Conquest of the North and Conflict with Song
After defeating the Liao, the Jin turned south against the Song dynasty. In 1127, they captured the Song capital Kaifeng in the Jingkang Incident, taking emperors Huizong and Qinzong hostage. The Song court fled south, establishing the Southern Song. After decades of intermittent warfare, the Treaty of Shaoxing (1142) formalized a border along the Huai River, with the Song paying annual tribute. The Jin state administered a multicultural empire, distinguishing between former Liao subjects (hanren/yanren) and former Song subjects (nanren).

### Internal Development and Sinicization
The Jin government merged Jurchen tribal customs with institutions from the Liao and Song, maintaining five capitals. Emperors like Shizong (r. 1161–1189) promoted Jurchen language and culture to counteract sinicization, while others, like Wanyan Liang, fully embraced Chinese traditions. The dynasty saw cultural advances, including the founding of the Quanzhen Taoist school and the production of major Buddhist and Taoist canons. However, internal strife, floods, and the rising Mongol threat in the north began to erode its stability.

### Mongol Invasion and Fall
The Mongol Empire, under Genghis Khan, invaded the Jin in 1211, exploiting internal divisions and military weaknesses. After capturing the northern capitals, the Mongols besieged the final Jin stronghold at Kaifeng (1232–33), where both sides used advanced gunpowder weapons like "heaven-shaking-thunder bombs." The city fell after a devastating siege. The last Jin emperor, Aizong, committed suicide in 1234 at Caizhou, marking the dynasty's end.

## Terms
- **Jurchen**: A Tungusic people from Northeast China who founded the Jin dynasty.
- **Wanyan**: The imperial clan of the Jin dynasty.
- **Sinicization**: The process of adopting Han Chinese culture and institutions.
- **Meng-an**: A Jurchen military-administrative unit, a "battalion" of 7–10 *mouke*.
- **Mouke**: A basic Jurchen social unit of 300 households, also a military company.
- **Treaty of Shaoxing (1142)**: Agreement that set the Huai River as the Jin-Song border and required Song tribute payments.
- **Heaven-shaking-thunder bomb**: An early gunpowder weapon used in sieges, described as an iron vessel filled with gunpowder.
- **Quanzhen School**: A Taoist sect founded during the Jin dynasty, emphasizing monasticism and inner alchemy.

## Debates and open questions
- The ethnic composition of the Balhae kingdom, a predecessor state in the region, is debated, with sources differing on the roles of Mohe and Goguryeo peoples.
- The extent to which the Jin dynasty was a "Chinese" dynasty versus a Jurchen empire is a matter of historical interpretation, highlighted by their adoption of the dynastic element "earth" to claim succession from the Song.
- The precise reasons for the Mongol invasion of the Jin are uncertain, with theories ranging from revenge for past killings to strategic expansion.
