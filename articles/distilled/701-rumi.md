# Rumi

## Overview

Jalāl al-Dīn Muḥammad Rūmī (1207–1273) was a Persianate Sufi mystic, poet, and founder of the Mevlevi Order, whose works—especially the *Masnavi*—rank among the greatest in Persian literature and Islamic mysticism. His life was shaped by the Mongol invasions that displaced his family westward, and a transformative encounter with the wandering dervish Shams Tabrīzī, whose disappearance triggered Rumi’s intense creative and spiritual flowering. Today, Rumi is celebrated globally as one of the most widely read poets, with his teachings on divine love influencing both classical and contemporary spiritual and literary traditions.

## Body

### Early Life and Migration

Rumi was born in Wakhsh, near Balkh (modern-day Tajikistan), into a family of Islamic scholars and mystics. His father, Bahā’ ud-Dīn Walad, was a prominent jurist and Sufi affiliated with the liberal Hanafi Maturidi school. Around 1215–1220, the Mongol invasions of Central Asia forced the family to migrate westward through Nishapur, Baghdad, Mecca, Damascus, and other cities before settling in Konya in 1228. The region had recently been conquered by the Seljuk Sultanate of Rûm, giving Rumi the nisba “Rumi,” meaning “from Rûm” or “the Roman,” referencing the former Byzantine territories.

### Education and Spiritual Formation

After his father’s death in 1231, Rumi inherited his position as a molvi (Islamic teacher) and head of a madrasa. He continued his training under Burhan ud-Din Muhaqqiq Termazi in both Sharia (Islamic law) and Tariqa (Sufi path) for nine years. During this period, he also traveled to Damascus. His early exposure to Sufism, combined with the influence of classical Persian poets like Attar and Sanai, laid the groundwork for his later mystical poetry.

### Encounter with Shams-e Tabrīzī

In 1244, Rumi met Shams-e Tabrīzī, a wandering dervish whose intense spiritual presence profoundly transformed Rumi’s life. Their relationship—often interpreted as one of deep spiritual love—led Rumi to abandon his scholarly duties. Shams disappeared mysteriously in 1248, likely killed by Rumi’s son or due to charges of blasphemy. Rumi’s grief over Shams’s loss inspired the *Divan-e Shams-e Tabrīzī*, a collection of lyric poems expressing longing and divine love.

### Composition of the Masnavi

After Shams’s disappearance, Rumi found companionship in Salaḥ ud-Dīn-e Zarkūb and later Hussam-e Chalabi, his scribe. In 1258, while walking in the vineyards outside Konya, Hussam suggested composing a work akin to the *Ilāhīnāma* of Sanai or the *Mantiq ut-Tayr* of Attar. Inspired, Rumi began dictating the *Masnavi*, a six-volume mystical poem of approximately 27,000 couplets. Completed over twelve years, it weaves together Qur’anic exegesis, Sufi metaphysics, fables, and everyday scenes. Often called the “Qur’an in Persian,” it is regarded as one of the greatest works of Islamic literature.

### Later Life and Death

Rumi spent his final years in Konya, continuing to teach, compose, and lead a growing community of followers. He fell ill in December 1273 and died on 17 December, predicting his own death in a final ghazal. His funeral was attended by Muslims, Christians, and Jews, reflecting his broad appeal. He was buried beside his father, and a grand shrine—the “Green Tomb”—was erected over his grave. His son Sultan Walad and followers established the Mevlevi Order, known for the whirling dervishes and the Sama ceremony.

### Teachings and Religious Outlook

Rumi’s teachings center on divine love (*ishq*) as the path to union with God. He emphasized *tawhid* (the oneness of God) through love, interpreting the Qur’anic verse “Know that ‘There is no god but He,’ and ask forgiveness for your sin” as a call to both divine knowledge and self-negation (*fana*). Music, poetry, and dance were central to his practice, leading to the development of the Sama ritual. While firmly rooted in Islam, Rumi’s work exhibits religious pluralism, famously declaring, “The lamps are different, but the Light is the same.” He praised the Quran as the ultimate guide and Muhammad as the perfect exemplar, yet his vision extended beyond sectarian boundaries.

### Major Works

Rumi’s poetic works include the *Masnavi*, the *Divan-e Shams-e Tabrīzī*, and numerous ghazals and rubaiyat. His prose works—*Fihi Ma Fihi* (Discourses), *Majāles-e Sab'a* (Seven Sermons), and *Makatib* (Letters)—offer insights into his teachings and daily interactions. Written primarily in Persian, his works occasionally incorporate Arabic, Turkish, and Greek.

### Legacy

The Mevlevi Order, founded in 1273, became a major Sufi brotherhood in the Ottoman Empire, producing notable poets and musicians. In 1925, the Turkish Republic banned Sufi orders, closing all tekkes. However, the Mevlâna Museum in Konya reopened in 1927, and annual festivals celebrating Rumi’s Urs continue today. UNESCO declared 2007 the “International Rumi Year,” marking his 800th birthday with global events. His poetry has been translated into dozens of languages, and he is considered one of the most widely read poets in the United States.

## Terms

- **Sufism**: A mystical branch of Islam emphasizing direct personal experience of God.
- **Tariqa**: The Sufi spiritual path or way, focusing on inner purification and divine love.
- **Fana**: The Sufi concept of spiritual annihilation of the ego to achieve union with God.
- **Sharia**: Islamic law derived from the Quran and Hadith.
- **Nisba**: A surname or identifier indicating origin or affiliation (e.g., Rumi = from Rûm).
- **Masnavi**: A poem written in rhyming couplets, often of a mystical or didactic nature.
- **Divan**: A collection of lyric poems, typically ghazals and qasidas.
- **Sama**: A Sufi ritual involving music, chanting, and movement to achieve spiritual ecstasy.
- **Mevlevi Order**: The Sufi order founded by Rumi’s followers, known for the whirling dervishes.
- **Tawhid**: The Islamic doctrine of the oneness of God.

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars debate the nature of Rumi’s relationship with Shams-e Tabrīzī, ranging from spiritual mentorship to romantic or homoerotic love. The circumstances of Shams’s disappearance remain uncertain, with theories including murder by Rumi’s son, blasphemy charges, or voluntary departure. The authenticity of Rumi’s claimed descent from Abu Bakr is disputed by modern scholars. Additionally, while Rumi is often portrayed as a universal mystic in Western popular culture, debates persist about the extent to which his Islamic identity and the political context of his time should inform interpretations of his work. The hagiographical accounts in Aflāki’s *Manāqib al-Ārifīn* are also treated critically, as they blend legend with historical fact.
