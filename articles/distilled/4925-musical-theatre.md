# Musical theatre

## Overview
Musical theatre combines songs, spoken dialogue, acting, and dance to tell a story. Emerging as a distinct modern form in the 19th century from earlier light opera and variety shows, its significance lies in the unique integration of these elements, where music and movement are equally important to dialogue in advancing plot and emotion, making it a major global art form.

## Timeline
- **1850s-1880s** — Offenbach's operettas and Gilbert & Sullivan's comic operas establish key structural elements.
- **1890s-1900s** — Edwardian musical comedies, led by George Edwardes, become popular.
- **1915-1918** — Princess Theatre musicals (Kern, Bolton, Wodehouse) advance story-song integration.
- **1927** — *Show Boat* premieres, integrating book and score with serious themes.
- **1943** — Rodgers & Hammerstein's *Oklahoma!* completes the integrated musical, launching the "Golden Age."
- **1957** — *West Side Story* innovates in music and choreography.
- **1960** — *The Fantasticks* opens off-Broadway, becoming the longest-running musical.
- **1975** — *A Chorus Line* becomes Broadway's longest-running show, winning the Pulitzer.
- **1981** — Andrew Lloyd Webber's *Cats* begins the British "megamusical" era.
- **1986** — *The Phantom of the Opera* opens, later becoming Broadway's longest-running show.
- **1996** — *Rent* brings rock music and contemporary themes to Broadway.
- **2015** — *Hamilton* debuts, using hip-hop to retell American history.

## Body

### Definitions and Core Concepts
A "book musical" integrates songs and dances into a well-made story with serious dramatic goals. Its three components are music, lyrics, and book (script). The creative team (director, musical director, choreographer) interprets the work, shaped by technical design. Musicals are typically two acts: the first introduces characters and conflict, the second resolves it. A key principle is that emotion too strong for speech becomes song; too strong for song becomes dance.

### Comparison with Opera
Musical theatre is related to opera but distinguished by greater focus on spoken dialogue, essential dancing, and often popular music styles. Performances use the audience's language. While an opera singer is primarily a singer, a musical theatre performer is often an actor first who must also sing and dance—a "triple threat." Modern musicals typically use microphones and amplification.

### Historical Development
Antecedents include ancient Greek drama, medieval plays, and Renaissance forms like commedia dell'arte. In the 18th-19th centuries, popular forms included ballad opera and comic opera. Modern musical theatre emerged in the 19th century with Offenbach, Gilbert and Sullivan, and American musical comedies by Harrigan and Hart. The early 20th century saw Edwardian musical comedy and American works by George M. Cohan.

The "Golden Age" (1940s-1960s) was defined by Rodgers and Hammerstein's integrated musicals, starting with *Oklahoma!* (1943). This era emphasized cohesive plots where songs and dances advanced the story, with dance innovations by Jerome Robbins and Bob Fosse, and darker works by Stephen Sondheim.

From the 1970s, the musical diversified. Rock musicals like *Hair* (1967) and *Jesus Christ Superstar* (1971) emerged. The 1980s were dominated by British "megamusicals" like *Cats* and *The Phantom of the Opera*, characterized by pop scores and spectacular effects. The 1990s and 21st century have seen jukebox musicals, film adaptations, and original works like *Rent* and *Hamilton* exploring contemporary themes and styles.

### Global and Amateur Performance
Musicals are performed worldwide, from Broadway and London's West End to off-Broadway, regional, community, and school productions. Vibrant scenes exist in Europe, Asia, Australasia, and Latin America. Amateur and school productions have a long history, supported by organizations like the UK's National Operatic and Dramatic Association (founded 1899).

## Terms
- **Book Musical**: A musical where songs and dances are fully integrated into a well-made story.
- **Book/Libretto**: The script, containing story, dialogue, and stage directions.
- **Score**: The complete set of songs and musical scenes.
- **Triple Threat**: A performer equally skilled as actor, singer, and dancer.
- **Megamusical**: A large-scale, big-budget musical with pop scores and spectacular effects.
- **Jukebox Musical**: A musical built around pre-existing popular songs.
- **Pit Orchestra**: The instrumental ensemble accompanying performers.
- **Golden Age**: The 1940s-1960s period of integrated musicals by Rodgers and Hammerstein.

## Debates and Open Questions
A key tension exists between original innovation and the commercial safety of revivals, adaptations, and jukebox musicals. Critics like Stephen Sondheim argue spectacular, tourist-oriented productions risk turning Broadway into a "tourist attraction." Historian John Kenrick counters that change signifies a living genre. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated discussions about theatre's financial model and digital streaming's role, with its long-term impact on live performance still unfolding.
