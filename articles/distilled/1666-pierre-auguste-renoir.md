# Pierre-Auguste Renoir

## Overview
Pierre-Auguste Renoir (1841–1919) was a leading French Impressionist painter celebrated for his vibrant light, saturated color, and intimate depictions of feminine beauty and modern leisure. Initially a porcelain painter, he became a central figure in the first Impressionist exhibition (1874) before breaking with the movement in the 1880s to pursue a more disciplined, classical line inspired by Renaissance masters. Despite severe rheumatoid arthritis in his final decades, he continued painting—adapting his technique with assistants and a moving canvas—and produced a vast oeuvre of several thousand works, now held in major collections worldwide.

## Body

### Youth and artistic formation
Born to a tailor in Limoges, Renoir moved to Paris in 1844, living near the Louvre. He showed early talent for singing under Charles Gounod but left school at thirteen for a porcelain factory apprenticeship. When mechanical reproduction ended that trade in 1858, he painted missionary hangings and fan decorations while preparing for the École des Beaux-Arts. In 1862 he joined Charles Gleyre’s studio, where he met Alfred Sisley, Frédéric Bazille, and Claude Monet. Poverty often prevented him from buying paint. His first Salon acceptance came in 1864, but recognition arrived slowly; a breakthrough occurred at the 1868 Salon with *Lise with a Parasol*, depicting his lover Lise Tréhot. During the 1871 Paris Commune he was nearly executed as a spy until Commune leader Raoul Rigault recognized him. The 1874 loss of his friend Jules Le Cœur and access to the Fontainebleau forest shifted his painting subjects.

### Impressionist exhibitions and early maturity
Inspired by Camille Pissarro and Édouard Manet, Renoir joined Monet, Sisley, Pissarro, and others to mount the First Impressionist Exhibition in April 1874, showing six paintings to mixed reviews but personal acclaim. At the 1876 Second Exhibition he focused on portraits to attract commissions; the 1877 Third Exhibition featured *Dance at Le Moulin de la Galette* and *The Swing*, now iconic Impressionist scenes of outdoor leisure. He skipped the fourth and fifth Impressionist exhibitions, returning to the Salon, where *Mme Charpentier and her Children* (1878) triumphed in 1879, establishing him as a fashionable painter. That year he met his main patron, Paul Bérard, who hosted him at the Château de Wargemont in Normandy.

### Travels, stylistic crisis, and the "Ingres period"
In 1881 Renoir traveled to Algeria (following Eugène Delacroix), Madrid (to study Diego Velázquez), and Italy (to see Titian in Florence and Raphael in Rome). In Palermo he painted Richard Wagner’s portrait in thirty-five minutes. Pneumonia in 1882 permanently damaged his lungs. The Italian Renaissance masters convinced him he had reached a dead end with Impressionism: "I could neither paint nor draw." From the mid-1880s he adopted a severe, linear style emphasizing outline and drawing—termed his "Ingres period"—producing *Blonde Bather* (1881–82) and *The Large Bathers* (1884–87). A summer in Guernsey (1883) yielded fifteen landscapes. In Montmartre he employed Suzanne Valadon as a model; she later became a noted painter herself.

### Late work, disability, and family life
After 1890 Renoir softened his outlines again, returning to thinly brushed color for monumental nudes and domestic scenes such as *Girls at the Piano* (1892) and *Grandes Baigneuses* (1887). He married Aline Charigot in 1890; she had modeled for *Luncheon of the Boating Party* (1881) and bore their sons Pierre (1885), Jean (1894), and Claude (1901). Around 1892 rheumatoid arthritis began to deform his hands and ankylose his right shoulder. In 1907 he moved to "Les Collettes" in Cagnes-sur-Mer for the Mediterranean climate. Despite progressive disability, he painted for twenty more years, using an assistant to place the brush in his hand (not strapped to paralyzed fingers, as often claimed) and a moving canvas for large works. He also created sculptures with Richard Guino. In 1919 he visited the Louvre to see his works beside the old masters and died later that year.

### Artistic characteristics and legacy
Renoir’s paintings are defined by vibrant light, saturated color, and candid, intimate compositions, often featuring the female nude. Early works reflect the colorism of Delacroix, the luminosity of Camille Corot, and the realism of Gustave Courbet and Manet (including the use of black as a color). He admired Edgar Degas’s sense of movement and the 18th-century masters François Boucher and Jean-Honoré Fragonard. Painting *en plein air* with Monet at La Grenouillère (1869), he discovered that shadows reflect surrounding colors (diffuse reflection), not brown or black. By the mid-1880s he broke with Impressionism for a classical formal technique, then synthesized both approaches in his late, abundantly fleshed nudes. A prolific artist of several thousand paintings, his largest collection (181 works) resides at the Barnes Foundation in Philadelphia. The catalogue raisonné by Bernheim-Jeune (1983–2014) and a pending Wildenstein Institute catalogue document his oeuvre. A version of *Bal du moulin de la Galette* sold for $78.1 million in 1990.

## Terms
- **Impressionism**: A 19th-century art movement characterized by visible brushstrokes, emphasis on light and color, and painting *en plein air* to capture fleeting moments.
- **Salon**: The official annual art exhibition of the Académie des Beaux-Arts in Paris; the primary venue for artistic recognition in 19th-century France.
- **En plein air**: French for "in the open air"; the practice of painting outdoors to observe natural light directly.
- **Diffuse reflection**: The optical phenomenon, discovered by Renoir and Monet, whereby shadows take on the reflected colors of surrounding objects rather than appearing brown or black.
- **Ingres period**: Renoir’s mid-1880s phase (named after Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres) emphasizing precise drawing, clear outlines, and classical form over Impressionist brushwork.
- **Rheumatoid arthritis**: A chronic autoimmune disease causing joint inflammation, deformity, and ankylosis (stiffening); severely limited Renoir’s mobility from c. 1892 onward.
- **Ankylosis**: Abnormal stiffening and immobility of a joint due to fusion of the bones; affected Renoir’s right shoulder.
- **Catalogue raisonné**: A comprehensive, annotated listing of all known works by an artist; Renoir’s was published by Bernheim-Jeune (1983–2014).
- **Barnes Foundation**: A Philadelphia institution holding the largest single collection of Renoir paintings (181 works).
- **Communards**: Supporters of the 1871 Paris Commune, a radical socialist government that briefly ruled Paris.

## Debates and open questions
- **Attribution disputes**: The Bernheim-Jeune catalogue raisonné and the Wildenstein Institute’s pending critical catalogue disagree on certain works, notably an unsigned painting at Picton Castle (featured in the TV series *Fake or Fortune*).
- **Arthritis painting method**: Popular accounts claim Renoir painted with brushes strapped to paralyzed fingers; the source clarifies he could still grasp a brush but needed an assistant to place it in his hand.
- **Stolen works**: *Paysage Bords de Seine* was withdrawn from a 2012 auction after being identified as stolen from the Baltimore Museum of Art in 1951; provenance research remains active.
