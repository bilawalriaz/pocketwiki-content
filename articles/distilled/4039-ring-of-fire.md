# Ring of Fire

## Overview
The Ring of Fire is a 40,000 km (25,000 mi) long tectonic belt of earthquakes and volcanoes surrounding most of the Pacific Ocean. It is the most seismically and volcanically active region on Earth, hosting about 90% of the world's earthquakes and 750-915 active or dormant volcanoes. Its existence is a direct result of the subduction of oceanic tectonic plates beneath other plates around the Pacific basin.

## Timeline
- **c. 190 million years ago** — The Pacific Plate comes into existence in the Early Jurassic.
- **c. 115 million years ago** — Subduction zones forming the Ring of Fire's initial configuration develop in South America, North America, and Asia.
- **c. 70 million years ago** — Subduction zones in Indonesia and New Guinea are created.
- **c. 35 million years ago** — The New Zealand subduction zone forms; the Ring of Fire's modern outline is established.
- **1825** — G. P. Scrope describes the chains of volcanoes around the Pacific rim.
- **1912** — Geologist Patrick Marshall introduces the term "Andesite Line."
- **1960** — The 1960 Valdivia earthquake (Mw 9.4–9.6) in Chile, the largest earthquake ever recorded.
- **1980** — The eruption of Mount St. Helens in the United States.
- **1991** — The eruption of Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines, the world's second-largest eruption of the 20th century.
- **2011** — The Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami (Mw 9.0–9.1) in Japan.

## Body

### Formation and Tectonic Setting
The Ring of Fire is not a single structure but a chain of geological features created by subduction, where one tectonic plate sinks beneath another at convergent boundaries. The current configuration was established over the last 115 million years as the Pacific Plate grew and older plates like the Farallon and Kula were consumed. The process involves various plates, including the Antarctic, Nazca, Cocos, Pacific, Juan de Fuca, Philippine, and Australian plates, interacting in a complex manner. This subduction creates oceanic trenches, volcanic arcs, and back-arc basins.

### Volcanic Activity and Types
The belt contains two-thirds of the world's volcanoes. Most are stratovolcanoes (e.g., Mount St. Helens), formed by explosive eruptions of tephra and lava flows. Their lavas are primarily andesite and basaltic andesite. Other types include shield volcanoes and submarine seamounts. The four largest volcanic eruptions in the Holocene epoch (last 11,700 years) occurred within the Ring of Fire, including at Fisher Caldera (Alaska, 8700 BC) and Mount Mazama (Oregon, 5677 BC).

### Earthquakes and Seismicity
Approximately 90% of the world's earthquakes occur in the Ring of Fire, including most of the largest. These are often megathrust earthquakes at subduction zones. Notable examples include the 1960 Valdivia earthquake (Chile), the 1964 Alaska earthquake, and the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake (Japan). The next most seismically active region is the Alpide belt, which accounts for 5–6% of earthquakes.

### Geographic Boundaries and Gaps
While most boundaries are agreed upon, the inclusion of western Indonesia (often placed in the Alpide belt) and the Antarctic Peninsula is debated. The Ring is not a complete circle; gaps exist where subduction zones are absent. For example, a gap in North America is caused by the transform San Andreas Fault. Volcanic activity in some gaps, like in northern British Columbia, is due to continental rifting, not subduction.

## Terms
- **Subduction:** A geological process where one tectonic plate sinks beneath another at a convergent boundary.
- **Convergent Boundary:** A boundary where two tectonic plates move toward each other.
- **Stratovolcano:** A steep, conical volcano built up by many layers of hardened lava and tephra, often associated with explosive eruptions.
- **Andesite:** An extrusive igneous rock of intermediate composition, common in subduction zone volcanoes.
- **Megathrust earthquake:** An earthquake occurring at a subduction zone, capable of producing the largest magnitudes on Earth.
- **Oceanic trench:** A long, narrow, deep depression in the ocean floor, typically one running parallel to a plate boundary and marking a subduction zone.
- **Holocene Epoch:** The current geological epoch, spanning the last 11,700 years.
- **Alpide belt:** The Earth's other major earthquake and volcanic zone, running east-west through southern Asia and southern Europe.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise geographic limits of the Ring of Fire are debated, particularly regarding the inclusion of western Indonesia and the Antarctic Peninsula.
- The exact number of volcanoes within the Ring (estimated between 750 and 915) depends on which regions are included in its definition.
