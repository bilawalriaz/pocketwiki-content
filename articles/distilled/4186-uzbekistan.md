# Uzbekistan

## Overview
Uzbekistan is a sovereign, doubly landlocked country in Central Asia, surrounded entirely by other landlocked nations. It is the region's most populous republic, with over 39 million people. Its history spans ancient Silk Road civilizations, Soviet rule, and independence in 1991. Its strategic location and rich cultural heritage, including historic cities like Samarkand and Bukhara, make it a significant regional power.

## Timeline
- **8th–6th centuries BC** — Eastern Iranian nomads (Scythians) found kingdoms in Khwarazm, Sogdiana, and Bactria.
- **7th century** — The Muslim conquest of Persia leads to the spread of Islam in the region.
- **13th century** — The Mongol invasion destroys the Khwarazmian Empire.
- **14th century** — Timur (Tamerlane) establishes the Timurid Empire, with Samarkand as its capital.
- **16th century** — The Timurid territories are conquered by the Shaybanids, marking the start of the Uzbek period.
- **19th century** — Most of Central Asia is gradually incorporated into the Russian Empire.
- **27 October 1924** — The Uzbek Soviet Socialist Republic is created within the Soviet Union.
- **31 August 1991** — Uzbekistan declares independence following the failed coup in Moscow.
- **2 September 2016** — President Islam Karimov dies; Shavkat Mirziyoyev becomes his successor.
- **September 2017** — The national currency becomes fully convertible at market rates.

## Body

### History and Etymology
Recorded history begins with nomadic Scythian tribes. The region was later part of the Achaemenid, Greco-Bactrian, and Sasanian empires before Arab conquests introduced Islam in the 8th century. The Samanid Empire and subsequent Turkic dynasties fostered a golden age, with cities like Bukhara and Samarkand thriving on the Silk Road. The Mongol invasion in the 13th century caused massive destruction. In the 14th century, Timur (Tamerlane) built a vast empire, initiating a cultural renaissance. After the Timurid state fragmented, Uzbek nomadic tribes conquered the area in the 16th century. The region was later divided between the Emirate of Bukhara and the khanates of Khiva and Kokand before Russian conquest in the 19th century. The name "Uzbekistan" first appears in 16th-century literature, and the origin of "Uzbek" is debated, with theories linking it to "free/independent," the Oghuz tribe, or Özbeg Khan of the Golden Horde.

### Government and Politics
Uzbekistan is a secular state with a semi-presidential government. It is divided into 12 regions, one autonomous republic (Karakalpakstan), and Tashkent City. After independence, Islam Karimov ruled as an authoritarian president until his death in 2016. His successor, Shavkat Mirziyoyev, has initiated reforms, improving relations with neighbors and attracting foreign investment. However, international organizations still characterize the country as an authoritarian state with limited civil rights, citing issues like torture and restrictions on freedoms. The government has been accused of forced labor, particularly in the cotton industry, though recent UN reports indicate major progress in stamping out forced labor in the cotton harvest.

### Economy
Uzbekistan's economy is transitioning to a market system, with a focus on import substitution. It is a major global producer and exporter of cotton, gold, natural gas, and uranium. Agriculture employs 27% of the labor force. The country has significant untapped oil and gas reserves. After high inflation in the early 1990s, economic stabilization efforts brought inflation down to single digits. The IT sector is a fast-growing part of the economy. Challenges include corruption, state dominance, and historically low foreign investment, though reforms aim to improve the business climate.

### Geography and Environment
Uzbekistan is a dry, doubly landlocked country with a continental climate. Less than 10% of its land is intensively cultivated. The Aral Sea, once the world's fourth-largest inland sea, has catastrophically shrunk due to Soviet-era irrigation for cotton, causing severe environmental damage, including soil salinity and dust storms. Air pollution is a major issue, with Tashkent often ranking among the world's most polluted cities. The country is vulnerable to climate change, particularly water stress, and seismic activity.

### Demographics and Culture
Uzbeks form over 89% of the population. The official language is Uzbek, written in both Latin and Cyrillic scripts. Russian is widely used as a lingua franca. The population is 96% Muslim, mostly Sunni. The country has a young population and a 100% adult literacy rate. Uzbek cuisine is "noodle-rich," with signature dishes like palov (plov). Football is the most popular sport, and the country has achieved notable success in boxing, wrestling, and chess. Uzbekistan has five UNESCO World Heritage cultural sites, including the historic centers of Bukhara and Samarkand.

## Terms
- **Doubly landlocked**: A country surrounded entirely by other landlocked countries. Uzbekistan and Liechtenstein are the only two in the world.
- **Silk Road**: An ancient network of trade routes connecting the East and West, crucial for the exchange of goods, culture, and ideas.
- **Timurid Empire**: A 14th-15th century empire founded by Timur (Tamerlane), known for its cultural and scientific achievements, especially in Samarkand.
- **Shaybanids**: A Turko-Mongol dynasty that conquered the Timurid territories in the 16th century, marking the start of the Uzbek period.
- **Oliy Majlis**: The bicameral parliament of Uzbekistan, consisting of a lower house (Legislative Chamber) and an upper house (Senate).
- **Import substitution**: An economic policy aimed at reducing foreign dependency by replacing imported goods with domestic production.
- **Aral Sea disaster**: The catastrophic shrinking of the Aral Sea since the 1960s due to Soviet irrigation projects, causing major ecological and health problems.
- **Shashmaqam**: A form of Central Asian classical music that originated in Bukhara, characterized by six musical modes.
- **Palov (Plov)**: A traditional Uzbek rice dish with meat, carrots, and onions, considered the national dish.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The origin of the name "Uzbek" is disputed, with multiple theories involving linguistic, tribal, and historical figures.
- The percentage of the ethnic Tajik population in Uzbekistan is controversial, with official figures around 5% and some Western academics estimating 25–30%.
- The extent of political reform under President Mirziyoyev is debated, with some viewing it as a potential "Uzbek Spring" while international indices still classify the country as a closed autocracy.
- The long-term environmental and socioeconomic impacts of the Aral Sea's desiccation and ongoing climate change remain critical challenges.
