# Kara Sea

## Overview
The Kara Sea is a marginal sea of the Arctic Ocean located north of Siberia, Russia. It is a significant, though ice-bound, fishing ground and contains major undeveloped petroleum and natural gas reserves. Its history is marked by early exploration attempts, tragic expeditions, and later Soviet development, alongside ongoing concerns about nuclear waste dumping.

## Timeline
- **1556** — Stephen Borough's expedition is stopped by ice at the Kara Sea entrance.
- **1736–1737** — Admiral Stepan Malygin's voyage creates the first accurate map of the Arctic coast between the Pechora and Ob rivers.
- **1878–1879** — Adolf Erik Nordenskiöld becomes the first to navigate the Northeast Passage via the Kara Sea.
- **1912** — Three Russian expeditions (Sedov, Brusilov, Rusanov) become trapped and fail in the Kara Sea ice.
- **1917** — After the Russian Revolution, exploration and development of the Northern Sea Route increase greatly.
- **1930–1932** — Georgy Ushakov completely maps the Severnaya Zemlya archipelago.
- **1935–1936** — Icebreaker *Sadko* cruises discover Ushakov Island.
- **1942** — German "Operation Wunderland" naval campaign has limited success due to ice and weather.
- **1965–1988** — The Soviet Union dumps nuclear reactors and waste into the Kara Sea.
- **1993** — The Great Arctic State Nature Reserve is established, including a section of Kara Sea islands.
- **2010** — Russia licenses Rosneft to develop the East-Prinovozemelsky oil and gas field.
- **2014** — US sanctions force Exxon to halt operations in the Kara Sea.

## Body

### Geography and Environment
The Kara Sea is separated from the Barents Sea by the Kara Strait and Novaya Zemlya, and from the Laptev Sea by Severnaya Zemlya. It has an area of about 880,000 km² and a mean depth of 110 meters. The sea is ice-bound for most of the year, with water circulation heavily influenced by freshwater runoff from major Siberian rivers (like the Ob and Yenisei) and inflow from the Barents Sea. Unlike other Arctic marginal seas, many of its islands are located in the open central sea, not just along the coasts.

### Exploration and History
Early attempts to navigate the sea, such as those by Stephen Borough (1556) and Arthur Pet (1580), failed due to ice and fog. The first successful mapping was by Stepan Malygin in 1736–1737. The first complete navigation of the Northeast Passage was achieved by Nordenskiöld in 1878–1879. The year 1912 was tragic, with three expeditions becoming trapped in the ice. After 1917, Soviet exploration intensified, leading to the mapping of Severnaya Zemlya and the discovery of Ushakov Island. During World War II, the German "Operation Wunderland" (1942) was a limited naval campaign in the area.

### Resources and Modern Issues
The Kara Sea is an important fishing ground and contains the East-Prinovozemelsky field, a significant undeveloped petroleum and natural gas resource. In 2010, Russia licensed Rosneft to develop this field, but US sanctions in 2014 halted Exxon's operations there. A major environmental concern is radioactive contamination from Soviet nuclear dumping between 1965 and 1988, which included reactors, spent fuel, and other waste. The scuttled submarine *K-27*, with reactors that could potentially re-achieve criticality, is a specific hazard. In 1993, the Great Arctic State Nature Reserve was established to protect parts of the sea's island ecosystems.

## Terms
- **Marginal sea:** A sea partially enclosed by islands, archipelagos, or peninsulas, and connected to the open ocean.
- **Northeast Passage:** A shipping route along the northern coast of Russia, connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans.
- **Polynya:** An area of open water surrounded by sea ice.
- **Sv (Sverdrup):** A unit of ocean current transport equal to 1,000,000 cubic meters per second.
- **Criticality:** The state of a nuclear reactor where a self-sustaining chain reaction is achieved.
- **Scuttled:** Deliberately sunk by its own crew.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise environmental risk posed by the dumped nuclear reactors, especially the *K-27*, is a subject of ongoing concern and study.
- The balance between developing the sea's significant hydrocarbon resources and protecting its fragile Arctic ecosystem remains a contentious issue.
