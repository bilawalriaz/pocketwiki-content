# Country

## Overview
The term "country" lacks a single, universally binding definition in international law or common usage. It functions as a flexible label applied to sovereign states, entities with limited recognition (e.g., Taiwan, Kosovo), constituent countries within sovereign unions (e.g., the UK, Denmark), and dependent territories with varying autonomy. The word also denotes non-political regions defined by geography, culture, or economy (e.g., "coal country," "the West Country"). This ambiguity stems from competing theories of statehood—declarative (statehood exists via objective criteria) versus constitutive (statehood requires recognition by others)—and the absence of a binding UN criteria for membership. Consequently, counts of "countries" vary: the ISO 3166 standard lists 249 codes, while the UN recognizes 193 member states.

## Body

### Definition and Semantic Range
In English, "country" has become strongly associated with political divisions, often serving as a synonym for "state" or "nation," though the relationships between these terms vary globally. Anthropologist Clifford Geertz (1997) argued they form a "promiscuous fusion" impossible to separate into a clean dichotomy. Beyond polity, the term designates sub-national regions: cultural areas (West Country), geographic descriptors ("big sky country"), economic zones ("coal country"), and rural areas ("the countryside"). Philosopher Simon Keller notes the metaphysical ambiguity: countries are treated variously as collections of citizens, projects, ideals, organic entities, or autonomous agents. Indigenous perspectives, such as that of Melissa Lucashenko (2005), further complicate the definition, framing "Country" as a relational concept encompassing Economy, Geography, Society, Myth, and History—often portable and residing in people rather than fixed places.

### Statehood and Recognition
When denoting a specific polity, "country" covers four categories: sovereign states, states with limited recognition, constituent countries, and dependent territories. Sovereign states possess supreme legitimate authority over a territory, yet no universal agreement exists on the total count due to disputed sovereignty. Entities like Taiwan, the Sahrawi Republic, and Kosovo have limited recognition; breakaway states like Transnistria have none. Constituent countries exist within sovereign unions: the Danish Realm (Denmark, Faroe Islands, Greenland), the Kingdom of the Netherlands (Netherlands, Aruba, Curaçao, Sint Maarten), and the United Kingdom (England, Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland). Dependent territories (e.g., British Overseas Territories, Overseas France, Åland, US territories, China's special administrative regions) are often called "countries" due to administrative autonomy; a few (Hong Kong, Greenland, Macau) are treated as separate "countries of origin" in international trade.

### International Law: Declarative vs. Constitutive Theories
International law defines sovereignty negatively ("not under another") but provides no binding criteria for statehood for UN members. Practice falls between two theories. The **declarative theory**, codified in the 1933 Montevideo Convention (Article 1), defines a state as a person of international law possessing: (1) a permanent population, (2) a defined territory, (3) a government, and (4) capacity to enter relations with other states. Article 3 implies statehood exists independent of recognition; the Convention codified customary law applicable to all subjects of international law. This view is supported by the EU's Badinter Committee and legal scholar James Crawford. The **constitutive theory** holds that a state becomes an international person *only* through recognition by at least one other country. Oppenheim (1912) argued international law "takes no notice" of an unrecognized state. The Organisation of African Unity (1976) treated recognition as an individual act of sovereignty by each member state.

### Identification: Names, Flags, and Symbols
**Names:** Most countries have a long name (formal, describing government form) and a short name (common identifier). Long names change with government shifts (e.g., Upper Volta → Burkina Faso; Burma → Myanmar; Swaziland → Eswatini; Turkey → Türkiye). Names carry diplomatic weight (e.g., the North Macedonia/Greece dispute). ISO 3166 assigns codes to 249 entities, 193 being UN member states.
**Flags:** Early flags were rulers' personal banners. Maritime republics (12th century) used flags for naval ID. The Age of Revolution (late 18th century) shifted flags to represent collective citizenry. 19th-century nationalism standardized national flags in Europe; colonialism exported the concept globally. Post-WWII, vexillology (flag study) professionalized.
**Anthems:** Patriotic musical compositions symbolizing history/traditions. Official adoption became common in the 19th century, though some predate it (e.g., UK's "God Save the King," Sweden's "Du gamla, Du fria" remain de facto). Constituent countries within sovereign states often have their own anthems (e.g., Wales' "Hen Wlad Fy Nhadau").
**Other Symbols:** Coats of arms/emblems, seals, mottos, and national colors.

### Patriotism
Patriotism is a positive emotional connection—love, devotion, attachment—to one's country. It combines ethnic, cultural, political, and historical dimensions and relates closely to nationalism, primarily civic nationalism and sometimes cultural nationalism.

### Economy: Classification Systems
Organizations classify countries to identify economic trends.
**UN (World Economic Situation and Prospects):** Uses per capita Gross National Income (GNI). Categories: developed countries, economies in transition, developing countries. Subgroups based on geography/ad hoc criteria. Developing regions: Africa, East Asia, South Asia, Western Asia, Latin America/Caribbean. Transition economies: former Soviet Union, South-Eastern Europe. Developed regions: Northern America, Europe, Asia/Pacific.
**World Bank (Atlas Method):** Classifies by GNI per capita (2018 data for 2020 fiscal year):
*   Low-income: ≤ $1,025
*   Lower-middle-income: $1,026 – $3,995
*   Upper-middle-income: $3,996 – $12,375
*   High-income: ≥ $12,376
Regions: East Asia/Pacific, Europe/Central Asia, Latin America/Caribbean, Middle East/North Africa, North America, South Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa.
**Operational Policies (World Bank):** IDA countries (concessional lending), IBRD countries (market-rate lending), Blend countries (eligible for both).

## Terms
- ****Sovereign state**** — A political entity with supreme legitimate authority over a defined territory and permanent population.
- ****Constituent country**** — A distinct polity within a larger sovereign state (e.g., Scotland in the UK; Greenland in the Danish Realm).
- ****Dependent territory**** — A territory politically dependent on a sovereign state but often possessing autonomous administration.
- ****Limited recognition**** — Status of entities (e.g., Kosovo, Taiwan) recognized by some UN members but not universally or by the UN system.
- ****Declarative theory**** — Legal view (Montevideo Convention) that statehood exists objectively via four criteria, independent of recognition.
- ****Constitutive theory**** — Legal view that statehood is created exclusively through recognition by other states.
- ****Montevideo Convention (1933)**** — Treaty codifying declarative criteria for statehood: population, territory, government, capacity for relations.
- ****ISO 3166**** — International standard assigning codes to 249 country/territory entities; 193 are UN member states.
- ****GNI per capita**** — Gross National Income divided by population; primary metric for UN and World Bank economic classifications.
- ****Vexillology**** — The scholarly study of flags, professionalized after WWII.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Criteria for statehood:** No binding UN definition exists; tension persists between declarative (objective criteria) and constitutive (recognition-based) theories.
*   **Count of countries:** Counts differ by institution (UN: 193 members; ISO: 249 codes; Olympic Committee: 206; FIFA: 211) due to varying recognition policies for disputed/dependent territories.
*   **Semantic instability:** "Country" conflates sovereign state, nation, constituent unit, dependent territory, geographic region, and rural area, resisting a "dichotomous opposition" (Geertz) or clear definition (*The Economist*).
*   **Indigenous vs. Western ontology:** Concepts like "Country" (capitalized) denote a relational, portable, multi-dimensional entity (Lucashenko) incompatible with the Western legal fiction of a bounded territorial state.
*   **Recognition as political act:** The OAU (1976) framed recognition as an individual sovereign choice, not a legal obligation, leaving new states in limbo.
