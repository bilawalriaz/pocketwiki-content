# Crusader states

## Overview
The Crusader states, or Outremer (French for "overseas"), were four Catholic feudal polities established in the Levant and southeastern Anatolia from 1098 to 1291. Founded by the First Crusade, they represented a significant, though ultimately transient, Western European presence in the Middle East, existing as a minority society amidst a diverse indigenous population until their destruction by the Mamluk Sultanate.

## Timeline
- **1095** — Pope Urban II calls the First Crusade at the Council of Clermont.
- **1098** — County of Edessa and Principality of Antioch are established.
- **1099** — Crusaders capture Jerusalem, establishing the Kingdom of Jerusalem.
- **1102** — County of Tripoli is founded.
- **1144** — Zengid Turks capture the County of Edessa.
- **1187** — Saladin defeats the Crusaders at the Battle of Hattin and captures Jerusalem.
- **1189-1192** — The Third Crusade recaptures coastal cities but not Jerusalem.
- **1244** — Jerusalem is permanently lost to the Khwarazmians and Ayyubids.
- **1268** — Mamluks capture Antioch.
- **1289** — Mamluks destroy the County of Tripoli.
- **1291** — Mamluks capture Acre, ending the Kingdom of Jerusalem.

## Body

### Foundation and Consolidation (1098–1130)
The Crusader states emerged from the First Crusade, an armed pilgrimage proclaimed to reclaim the Holy Land. The Franks, as Western Europeans were known, established four states: the County of Edessa, the Principality of Antioch, the County of Tripoli, and the Kingdom of Jerusalem. Their foundation was facilitated by the fragmented political state of the Levant, where Seljuk Turkish and Fatimid Egyptian powers were in conflict. The Franks, though a minority, replaced local warlords in cities and formed treaties with neighboring Muslim rulers. The period saw the establishment of feudal institutions and the first military orders, the Knights Templar and Hospitallers, to protect pilgrims and defend the new states.

### Muslim Revival and the Fall of Edessa (1131–1174)
The unification of Muslim Syria under Zengid and then Ayyubid rulers, particularly Nur ad-Din and Saladin, shifted the balance of power. The fall of Edessa in 1144 shocked Christendom and prompted the failed Second Crusade (1148). The Crusader states, especially the Kingdom of Jerusalem, became increasingly dependent on diplomacy and alliances, including with the Byzantine Empire. Internal Frankish divisions and the strategic genius of Saladin, who united Egypt and Syria, culminated in the catastrophic Crusader defeat at the Battle of Hattin in 1187 and the loss of Jerusalem.

### Decline and the Mamluk Conquest (1174–1291)
After Hattin, the Crusader states were reduced to a coastal strip. The Third Crusade (1189–1192) recovered cities like Acre but not Jerusalem. The subsequent period was marked by internal civil wars, such as the War of the Lombards, and the rise of the Mamluk Sultanate in Egypt. The Mamluks, under sultans like Baibars and Qalawun, systematically dismantled the remaining Crusader strongholds, capturing Antioch (1268), Tripoli (1289), and finally Acre (1291), ending the Crusader presence.

### Government, Society, and Economy
The Kingdom of Jerusalem developed a sophisticated feudal monarchy with a High Court (Haute Cour) that gained power over the crown, especially after the loss of Jerusalem in 1187. Society was stratified, with the Frankish minority ruling over a diverse indigenous population of Greek Orthodox, Armenian, Syrian, and Maronite Christians, as well as Muslims and Jews. While some integration occurred, particularly in urban areas, communities largely remained separate with their own legal and religious systems. The economy was initially based on agriculture but shifted heavily to commerce after 1187, with Italian maritime republics like Venice and Genoa dominating trade through privileged colonial communes in port cities.

### Military and Legacy
The Crusader states' military power relied on feudal levies, mercenaries, and the professional standing armies of the military orders. Frankish knights, with their heavy cavalry and crossbowmen, were formidable but could not sustain heavy casualties. Their military architecture, blending European, Byzantine, and Muslim traditions, was a significant achievement. The legacy of the Crusader states includes the military orders, which survived for centuries, and the stimulation of trade and cultural exchange between Europe and the Near East, though the extent of this exchange is debated.

## Terms
- **Outremer**: French for "overseas," a medieval term for the Crusader states.
- **Franks**: The term used in contemporary sources for the Western European, primarily French-speaking, Catholic settlers.
- **Feudalism**: The social and legal system where land was granted in fief in return for military service and loyalty.
- **High Court (Haute Cour)**: The supreme judicial and political assembly of the Kingdom of Jerusalem, composed of the king and his tenants-in-chief.
- **Military Orders**: Catholic religious orders whose members combined monastic life with military service, such as the Knights Templar and Hospitallers.
- **Dhimmi**: In Islamic law, the status of protected non-Muslim communities (Christians and Jews) who paid a poll tax (jizya).
- **Mamluk**: Originally slave soldiers in the Islamic world, they later formed a powerful military caste and ruling dynasty in Egypt and Syria.
- **Assizes**: The customary laws of the Crusader states, particularly the Kingdom of Jerusalem, which were recorded after the loss of the capital.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nature of Society**: Historians debate whether Frankish society was a segregated colonial elite (as argued by Prawer and Riley-Smith) or showed more integration and cultural exchange with indigenous populations, a view supported by some archaeological evidence (Ellenblum).
- **Motivations**: The primary motivations for the Crusades and settlement are debated, ranging from religious zeal and pilgrimage to economic opportunity and feudal ambition.
- **Historiographical Approach**: There is ongoing discussion about whether the Crusader states should be studied as an extension of Western European expansion or as distinct polities within the context of Near Eastern political history.
