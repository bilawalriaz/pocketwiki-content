# Mobile phone

## Overview  
A mobile phone is a portable wireless telephone that connects to a cellular network via radio frequency, enabling voice calls, text messaging, internet access, and multimedia services. It evolved from 0G analog car phones to today’s smartphones, driven by successive generations (1G–5G) of cellular standards. Mobile phones are among the most widely adopted consumer technologies, with over 9.1 billion subscriptions globally by 2024.

## Timeline  
- **1917**: Eric Tigerstedt patents a "pocket-size folding telephone"  
- **1973**: Martin Cooper (Motorola) demonstrates first handheld mobile phone  
- **1979**: NTT launches world’s first commercial cellular network in Japan  
- **1983**: DynaTAC 8000x becomes first commercially available handheld phone  
- **1991**: 2G digital GSM network launched in Finland by Radiolinja  
- **2001**: 3G launched in Japan by NTT DoCoMo on WCDMA standard  
- **2009**: First public LTE (4G) service launched in Scandinavia  
- **2019**: Global rollout of 5G networks begins  

## Body  

### Technology Generations  
Mobile telephony advanced through generations: 0G systems were analog, car-mounted, and supported few calls. 1G introduced automated analog cellular networks. 2G brought digital voice and data (SMS, MMS), led by GSM in Europe. 3G enabled mobile broadband and video calling. 4G (LTE) delivered high-speed data for streaming and IoT. 5G offers ultra-low latency and gigabit speeds for AR/VR, autonomous vehicles, and critical communications.

### Hardware Components  
All mobile phones include a CPU (microprocessor on MOS IC), battery (typically lithium-ion), input mechanism (keypad or touchscreen), display (LCD/OLED), speakers, and SIM card slot. Smartphones use NAND flash memory; feature phones often use NOR flash. Displays range from under 90mm (feature phones) to over 130mm ("phablets"). Battery life spans 2–3 years, with Li-ion enduring 500–2,500 charge cycles.

### Software and Platforms  
Feature phones run basic software; smartphones use full operating systems—primarily Android (71.9% market share as of March 2025) and iOS (27.7%). Apps are distributed via app stores like Apple’s App Store (launched 2008) and Google Play Store. SMS messaging began in 1992; MMS followed in 2002.

### Network Infrastructure  
Cellular networks divide service areas into small "cells," each served by a base station using distinct frequencies. Frequency reuse allows thousands of simultaneous calls. Handoffs between cells maintain call continuity. Digital networks increased capacity by hosting multiple calls per frequency. Wi-Fi offloading helps reduce cellular traffic.

### Social and Economic Impact  
Mobile phones transformed communication, commerce, and culture. In developing countries, they provide access to banking (e.g., Kenya’s M-PESA), information, and emergency services. They enabled new industries like app development and micro-enterprises. However, concerns include distracted driving, cyber tracking, electronic waste, and conflict minerals funding wars in the DRC.

## Terms  
- **Cellular Network**: A system dividing geographic areas into cells, each with its own base station, enabling frequency reuse and large-scale mobile communication  
- **SIM Card**: A microchip storing subscriber identity (IMSI) and authentication keys, allowing users to switch devices while retaining service  
- **2G/3G/4G/5G**: Successive generations of mobile standards offering increasing data speeds and capabilities  
- **GSM**: Global System for Mobile Communications, a 2G digital standard originating in Europe  
- **LTE**: Long-Term Evolution, a 4G standard offering high-speed wireless broadband  
- **IMEI**: International Mobile Equipment Identity, a unique identifier for mobile devices  
- **Feature Phone**: A mobile phone with limited computing power compared to smartphones, supporting basic voice, text, and media functions  
- **Smartphone**: A mobile device with advanced computing capabilities, internet connectivity, and support for third-party applications  

## Debates and Open Questions  
- **Health Effects**: IARC suggests possible links between heavy mobile phone use and brain tumors, but evidence remains inconclusive after 15+ years of research  
- **Driving Safety**: Texting while driving significantly increases crash risk, yet enforcement is complicated by difficulty distinguishing legal from illegal phone use  
- **Environmental Impact**: Manufacturing accounts for 40–50% of phones’ environmental footprint; recycling efforts like Apple’s Liam robot exist but face scalability challenges  
- **Conflict Minerals**: Demand for rare earth metals fuels conflicts in the DRC; initiatives like Fairphone aim for ethical sourcing but remain niche  
- **Digital Divide**: While mobile adoption is near-universal in many regions, disparities persist in access to smartphones and reliable networks in rural and low-income areas
