# Rosa Luxemburg

## Overview
Rosa Luxemburg (1871–1919) was a Polish-born Marxist theorist and revolutionary, a leading figure in the German socialist movement. She is remembered for critiques of reformism and imperialism, defense of revolutionary democracy, and her murder during the failed Spartacist uprising. Her legacy remains central to debates on mass action, party leadership, and democratic freedom within socialism.

## Timeline
- **5 March 1871** — Born in Zamość, Russian-controlled Congress Poland.
- **1889** — Fled Poland to avoid arrest; settled in Zurich, Switzerland.
- **1893** — Co-founded the Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland (SDKP), rejecting Polish independence for international class struggle.
- **1898** — Moved to Berlin after a marriage of convenience for German citizenship; joined the SPD.
- **1899** — Published *Social Reform or Revolution?*, defending revolution against Eduard Bernstein's reformism.
- **1905** — Traveled to Warsaw during the Russian Revolution; arrested and later developed her mass strike theory.
- **1906** — Published *The Mass Strike, the Political Party and the Trade Unions*.
- **1913** — Published *The Accumulation of Capital*, arguing capitalism's need for expansion drives imperialism.
- **4 August 1914** — SPD voted for war credits; Luxemburg began organizing anti-war opposition, forming the Spartacus League.
- **1916** — Wrote the *Junius Pamphlet* from prison, popularizing "socialism or barbarism."
- **1918** — Wrote *The Russian Revolution*, critiquing Bolshevik authoritarianism while in prison.
- **1 January 1919** — Co-founded the Communist Party of Germany (KPD).
- **15 January 1919** — Murdered by Freikorps paramilitaries after the Spartacist uprising was crushed.

## Body

### Early Life and Political Formation
Born to a secular Jewish family in Russian-ruled Poland, Luxemburg was influenced by the Haskalah and Polish Romantic poetry. A childhood hip disease left her with a permanent limp. She joined revolutionary socialist circles as a teenager, fleeing to Switzerland in 1889. In Zurich, she studied law and economics, met Leo Jogiches, and co-founded the SDKP, opposing the Polish Socialist Party's nationalism. Her 1897 doctoral thesis argued Poland's industrial development was tied to the Russian market, making independence economically counterproductive.

### The Revisionist Debate and Mass Strike Theory
Moving to Germany in 1898, Luxemburg became a leading voice of the SPD's revolutionary wing. In *Social Reform or Revolution?* (1899), she argued that reforms were the *means* to develop class consciousness, while revolution was the ultimate *aim*; abandoning this goal reduced socialism to reformism. Inspired by the 1905 Russian Revolution, she developed her mass strike theory in 1906. She saw it not as a single, controlled event but as a spontaneous, continuous process where economic and political struggles merged, led by the party but driven by the creative energy of the masses. This clashed with the SPD and trade union leadership's cautious "strategy of attrition."

### Theorist of Imperialism and Anti-War Activism
Luxemburg's major work, *The Accumulation of Capital* (1913), posited that capitalism could not realize its surplus value internally and was therefore driven to expand into and destroy non-capitalist societies, making imperialism an inherent, violent feature of the system. This analysis underpinned her fierce anti-militarism. After the SPD supported World War I in 1914, she was imprisoned for most of the conflict. From prison, she wrote the *Junius Pamphlet* (1916), condemning the war as a betrayal of the working class and framing the choice as "socialism or barbarism."

### Critique of the Russian Revolution and Final Years
While celebrating the 1917 Bolshevik Revolution, Luxemburg offered a sharp critique in a prison manuscript (published posthumously in 1922). She condemned the suppression of democratic freedoms, arguing that "freedom is always and exclusively freedom for the one who thinks differently." She feared that without free political life, a new bureaucracy would rule in the name of the proletariat. Released during the German Revolution of 1918–1919, she co-founded the KPD. She initially doubted the timing of the January 1919 Spartacist uprising but supported the workers once they were in the streets. After its suppression, she was captured and murdered by right-wing Freikorps paramilitaries.

## Terms
*   **Revisionism:** A late 19th-century socialist theory, associated with Eduard Bernstein, that argued capitalism was adapting and that socialism should be achieved through gradual reforms rather than revolution.
*   **Mass Strike:** A concept developed by Luxemburg describing a period of intensified class struggle where economic and political strikes, demonstrations, and uprisings merge into a revolutionary process driven by spontaneous mass action.
*   **Imperialism:** In Luxemburg's theory, the necessary and violent expansion of capitalist economies into non-capitalist regions to realize surplus value and ensure survival.
*   **Spartacus League:** An anti-war, revolutionary socialist group in Germany during WWI, co-founded by Luxemburg, which later became the Communist Party of Germany (KPD).
*   **Freikorps:** Right-wing paramilitary units composed of demobilized soldiers, used by the German government to suppress left-wing uprisings in 1918–1919.
*   **Dictatorship of the proletariat:** A Marxist concept for a transitional state where the working class holds political power to suppress counter-revolution and build socialism. Luxemburg insisted it must be democratic in form.
*   **Spontaneity:** For Luxemburg, the self-directed, creative, and revolutionary energy of the masses in action, which the party must lead but cannot artificially create.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Luxemburgism vs. Leninism:** A central debate concerns the correct relationship between the revolutionary party and the masses. Critics from the Stalinist tradition accused Luxemburg of underestimating the need for a disciplined vanguard party, while her supporters see her emphasis on spontaneity and democracy as a vital corrective to authoritarian models.
*   **Critique of the Russian Revolution:** Scholars debate whether Luxemburg's posthumously published critique was a final, considered judgment or if she might have modified her views had she lived. Her comrades, like Clara Zetkin, claimed she had revised her position.
*   **Economic Theory:** Her thesis in *The Accumulation of Capital* that capitalism requires non-capitalist markets to survive was rejected by many leading Marxists, including Lenin and Kautsky, but has been revisited by later theorists.
*   **Legacy:** Her legacy is contested. She is a heroine for democratic socialists, council communists, and the New Left, but was denounced as a heretic by the Stalinist tradition. Her opposition to Polish independence remains controversial in Poland.
