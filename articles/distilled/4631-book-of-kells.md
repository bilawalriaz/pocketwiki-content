# Book of Kells

## Overview
The Book of Kells is an illuminated Latin Gospel book created c. 800 AD by Columban monks, likely beginning at Iona and completed at Kells. It is a masterpiece of Insular art, renowned for its extravagant decoration that combines Christian iconography with intricate Celtic motifs. The manuscript is a primary artifact of early medieval Irish and Scottish monastic culture, demonstrating sophisticated artistic and textual traditions.

## Timeline
- **c. 800 AD** — Manuscript created, likely begun at Iona and completed at Kells.
- **807–814** — Abbey of Kells founded/refounded from Iona.
- **1007** — Stolen from Kells Abbey; recovered months later without its jewelled cover.
- **12th century** — Land charters copied onto blank pages; abbey dissolved.
- **1654** — Sent to Dublin for safekeeping from Cromwell's forces.
- **1661** — Presented to Trinity College Dublin by Bishop Henry Jones.
- **19th century** — Pages cropped during rebinding; rise to worldwide fame.
- **1953** — Rebound in four volumes by Roger Powell.
- **2006** — Digital version produced by Trinity College.
- **2011** — Added to UNESCO's Memory of the World Register.

## Body
### Origin and History
The Book of Kells is a late example of Insular manuscripts produced in British and Irish monasteries from the 6th to 9th centuries. Its creation date (c. 800) is long after St. Columba's death (597), discrediting earlier traditions. The most accepted theory is that it was begun at Iona and finished at Kells, possibly due to Viking raids (c. 793–794). The manuscript was at Kells by 1007, when it was stolen and recovered. It remained there until 1654, when it was sent to Dublin for safety, eventually entering Trinity College in 1661.

### Physical Description and Contents
The manuscript consists of 340 vellum folios (680 pages), now bound in four volumes. It contains the four Gospels, largely from the Vulgate but with Old Latin variants, preceded by prefatory texts like canon tables and Gospel summaries. The text is written in Insular majuscule script by at least three scribes. The book was never fully completed; some pages lack final decoration, and key chapter numbers for the canon tables were never inserted, rendering them unusable.

### Decoration and Artistry
The illumination is the most lavish of any Insular Gospel book, featuring ten full-page miniatures, including the famous Chi Rho page, and extensive decorated text pages. The art combines Christian symbols (e.g., evangelist symbols, Virgin and Child) with intricate Celtic knotwork, interlace, and zoomorphic designs in vibrant colors. Pigments like woad and lichen suggest a Scottish origin. The decoration prioritizes aesthetic splendor over textual utility, consistent with its sacramental purpose as an altar book.

### Purpose and Significance
The book was a sacramental object for display on the high altar, not for daily study. Its grandeur reflects the wealth and artistic achievement of Columban monasteries. It demonstrates Ireland's cultural sophistication post-Roman Empire, with trade links evidenced by imported pigments. Its influence extended to the Celtic Revival, and it remains a globally significant cultural artifact.

## Terms
- **Insular art**: Artistic style developed in the British Isles and Ireland (6th–9th centuries), characterized by intricate interlace, knotwork, and stylized figures.
- **Vulgate**: The Latin translation of the Bible by St. Jerome, completed c. 405 AD, which became the standard text in the Western Church.
- **Vetus Latina**: Older Latin translations of the Bible used before the Vulgate.
- **Vellum**: High-quality calfskin used as a writing surface for manuscripts.
- **Insular majuscule**: A rounded, decorative script developed in Insular monasteries.
- **Chi Rho monogram**: A symbol formed from the Greek letters Χ (chi) and Ρ (rho), abbreviating "Christ."
- **Canon tables**: Tables created by Eusebius of Caesarea to cross-reference parallel passages in the Gospels.
- **Carpet page**: A fully decorated page with intricate geometric or interlace patterns, resembling a carpet.
- **Scriptorium**: A room in a monastery dedicated to the copying and illumination of manuscripts.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Place of origin**: Competing theories include Iona alone, Kells alone, a combination of both, or a Pictish monastery like Dunkeld. The Iona-Kells theory is most widely accepted.
- **Purpose of creation**: It may have been made for the 200th anniversary of St. Columba's death or to mark the translation of his relics.
- **Unfinished state**: The reason for the incomplete decoration and missing chapter numbers is unknown; it may have been interrupted by Viking raids or other events.
- **Gerald of Wales' account**: The 12th-century description of a great Gospel book in Kildare may refer to the Book of Kells or a now-lost manuscript.
