# History of Islam

## Overview
The history of Islam traces the development of the Islamic faith and its civilizations from the 7th-century mission of Prophet Muhammad to the modern era. It encompasses rapid early conquests, the rise and fall of caliphates and empires, the spread of the faith across three continents, and its profound impact on global culture, science, and politics. This history is central to understanding the modern Middle East, North Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and interactions between the Islamic world and other global powers.

## Timeline
- **c. 570 CE** — Birth of Muhammad in Mecca.
- **610 CE** — Muhammad begins receiving divine revelations.
- **622 CE** — The Hijra to Yathrib (Medina), starting the Islamic calendar.
- **632 CE** — Death of Muhammad; beginning of the Rashidun Caliphate.
- **661–750 CE** — Umayyad Caliphate rules from Damascus, expanding to al-Andalus and the Indus.
- **750–1258 CE** — Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad presides over the Islamic Golden Age.
- **909–1171 CE** — Fatimid Caliphate, a Shi'a Ismaili dynasty, rules from Egypt.
- **1095–1291 CE** — The Crusades between Christian Europe and Muslim powers.
- **1258 CE** — Mongol sack of Baghdad ends the Abbasid Caliphate in the East.
- **1299–1922 CE** — Ottoman Empire dominates the Middle East, Balkans, and North Africa.
- **1501–1736 CE** — Safavid Empire establishes Twelver Shi'ism in Iran.
- **1526–1857 CE** — Mughal Empire rules most of the Indian subcontinent.

## Body

### Inception and Early Expansion
Islam originated in the Arabian Peninsula during late antiquity. Prophet Muhammad, born in Mecca c. 570 CE, began receiving revelations in 610 CE forming the Quran. After persecution, he migrated to Medina in 622 CE (the Hijra), establishing the first Muslim community. By his death in 632 CE, most Arabian tribes had converted. The Rashidun Caliphate (632–661) launched rapid conquests, taking Syria, Egypt, and Persia from the weakened Byzantine and Sasanian empires. Expansion was facilitated by imperial collapse and support from local non-Muslim populations, who often aided Muslims for religious autonomy and lower taxes.

### The Caliphates and Sectarian Split
The Rashidun period ended in civil war (the First Fitna), leading to the Umayyad Caliphate (661–750) based in Damascus. The Umayyads expanded the empire from Spain to India but faced discontent from non-Arab Muslims (mawali) and Shi'a sympathizers. They were overthrown by the Abbasid Revolution in 750. The Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258) in Baghdad presided over the Islamic Golden Age, a flourishing of science, philosophy, and arts. It gradually fragmented as regional dynasties gained autonomy. The Sunni-Shi'a split solidified during this era, rooted in succession disputes.

### Regional Empires and the Mongol Invasions
By the 13th century, the Islamic world comprised powerful states like the Delhi Sultanate and Seljuk Turks. The Mongol invasions of the 13th–14th centuries destroyed the Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad (1258) and devastated Persia and the Levant. Mongol successor states eventually converted to Islam. The Mamluk Sultanate halted the Mongols at Ain Jalut (1260), and the Timurid Renaissance flourished in Central Asia. In West Africa, Islamic empires like Mali and Songhai thrived, with Timbuktu as a learning center.

### Gunpowder Empires and Colonialism
The early modern period (15th–18th centuries) was dominated by three "gunpowder empires": the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal. The Ottomans conquered Constantinople (1453) and expanded into Europe and the Middle East. The Safavids made Twelver Shi'ism Iran's state religion, creating a sectarian divide with the Sunni Ottomans. The Mughals built a syncretic empire in South Asia. From the 19th century, European colonial powers dismantled these empires. The Ottoman Empire collapsed after World War I, leading to the modern Middle East's state system. The Mughal Empire was replaced by the British Raj. This colonialism and subsequent struggles for independence, including the Arab-Israeli conflict, continue to shape the Muslim world.

## Terms
- **Caliph (Khalīfa):** Successor to Muhammad as political and religious leader of the Muslim community.
- **Hijra:** Muhammad's migration from Mecca to Medina in 622 CE, starting the Islamic calendar.
- **Sunni:** Largest Islam branch, holding the caliph should be elected and following Muhammad's traditions.
- **Shi'a:** Branch believing leadership should have passed to Muhammad's son-in-law Ali and his descendants.
- **Jizya:** Poll tax on non-Muslim subjects for protection and military exemption.
- **Mawali:** Non-Arab Muslim clients of Arab tribes; their status was contested in early empires.
- **Sufism:** Mystical dimension of Islam focused on inner spiritual search for God.
- **Mamluk:** Soldier of slave origin serving in Islamic armies, sometimes establishing dynasties.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historiography of Early Islam:** Scholars debate the reliability of early sources compiled decades after events. Methodologies range from descriptive to skeptical, with revisionists suggesting more complex origins.
- **Muhammad's Intentions:** Debate over whether Muhammad intended a universal religion or primarily Arab monotheistic reform, citing Quran's Arabic focus and interfaith accommodations.
- **Nature of Early Conquests:** Historians debate if early conquests were driven by religious zeal, economic motives, or secular expansionism, with religious narratives possibly constructed retrospectively.
- **Sectarian Origins:** The timing and causes of the Sunni-Shi'a split are debated, from immediate political dispute to gradual theological differentiation over centuries.
