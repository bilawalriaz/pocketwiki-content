# Baltimore

## Overview
Baltimore is Maryland's most populous city and a major U.S. port on the Patapsco River, near the Chesapeake Bay. Designated an independent city in 1851, it has been a pivotal site in American history, from the War of 1812 to the Civil War, and evolved from a leading industrial and transportation hub to a modern service-based economy. Its history, architecture, and cultural institutions make it a significant urban center, though it faces ongoing challenges with population decline, poverty, and crime.

## Timeline
- **1706** — Port of Baltimore established for the tobacco trade.
- **1729** — Town of Baltimore founded.
- **1776-1777** — Second Continental Congress meets in Baltimore, making it the temporary U.S. capital.
- **1814** — British bombardment of Fort McHenry inspires "The Star-Spangled Banner."
- **1830** — Baltimore and Ohio Railroad (B&O) built, reinforcing the city as a transportation hub.
- **1844** — First telegraph line (Baltimore-Washington) shared.
- **1851** — Baltimore becomes an independent city by the Maryland Constitution.
- **1861** — Pratt Street Riot occurs, one of the first violent incidents of the Civil War.
- **1904** — Great Baltimore Fire destroys over 1,500 buildings.
- **1910** — City enacts the first residential segregation ordinance in the U.S.
- **1980** — Harborplace opens, beginning the redevelopment of the Inner Harbor.
- **2024** — Francis Scott Key Bridge collapses after a ship strike.

## Body

### Early History and Founding
The Baltimore area was inhabited by Native Americans for millennia, with Paleo-Indian and Woodland period sites identified. In the early 1600s, the Susquehannock used the area as hunting grounds. European colonization began in earnest with the 1634 arrival of colonists at St. Clement's Island. The colonial legislature created the Port of Baltimore in 1706, and the Town of Baltimore was founded in 1729. The three settlements of Baltimore, Jonestown, and Fells Point were incorporated as the City of Baltimore in 1796–1797.

### 19th Century: War, Industry, and Growth
Baltimore played a key role in the American Revolution, briefly serving as the nation's capital in 1776-1777. The War of 1812 was pivotal; the British bombardment of Fort McHenry in 1814 inspired the national anthem. The city pioneered gas lighting in 1816 and grew rapidly as a manufacturing and shipping center, aided by the B&O Railroad (1830) and the National Road. It became a major port of entry for immigrants, second only to New York. During the Civil War, Baltimore was a strategic Union stronghold, though it experienced early violence like the 1861 Pratt Street Riot. The city became an independent entity from Baltimore County in 1851.

### 20th Century to Present: Transition and Challenges
The Great Baltimore Fire of 1904 caused massive destruction but led to improved building standards. In 1910, the city enacted a pioneering residential segregation ordinance, later ruled unconstitutional. Post-WWII demographic shifts saw the Black population rise significantly. The Inner Harbor was redeveloped from abandoned warehouses into a tourist and commercial hub starting in the 1980s. Economically, Baltimore shifted from heavy industry to a service economy, with Johns Hopkins Hospital and University becoming top employers. The city has struggled with population loss since its 1950 peak of 949,708, high poverty rates, and persistent violent crime, though homicides have declined in recent years. In 2024, the Francis Scott Key Bridge collapsed.

## Terms
- **Independent city**: A city not part of any county, treated as a county-equivalent for governmental purposes.
- **Fall line**: The geological boundary between the Piedmont Plateau and the Atlantic coastal plain, dividing the city into "lower" and "upper" sections.
- **Formstone**: A patented, cement-based veneer applied over brick, giving many Baltimore rowhouses their distinct look.
- **Gentrification**: The process where higher-income residents move into a lower-income urban area, often leading to increased property values and demographic change.
- **Port of Baltimore**: A major shipping and cruise port on the Patapsco River, a key economic engine for the region.
- **Rowhouse**: A terraced house sharing side walls with neighbors, a dominant residential form in Baltimore.
- **Sister city**: A form of legal or social agreement between two geographically and politically distinct localities for the purpose of promoting cultural and commercial ties.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The long-term impact of Baltimore's status as an independent city on its political representation and economic fortunes is debated, with some arguing it limits state assistance.
- The effectiveness of urban redevelopment projects like the Inner Harbor in addressing city-wide issues of poverty and inequality remains a topic of discussion.
- The causes and potential solutions for Baltimore's high violent crime rate, despite recent declines, are subjects of ongoing scholarly and public policy debate.
