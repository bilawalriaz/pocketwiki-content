# Arabic literature

## Overview
Arabic literature encompasses all writing in the Arabic language, from the pre-Islamic oral poetry of the 6th century to contemporary global works. Its development is deeply intertwined with the spread of Islam, the unifying influence of the Qur'an, and the cultural exchanges of the Islamic Golden Age. It remains a vibrant and influential body of work, with scholars like Jan Just Witkam suggesting it may be the largest literary corpus in the world.

## Timeline
- **Pre-Islamic** — Oral poetry (al-shiʿr al-Jāhilī) flourished, with poets as tribal spokesmen.
- **7th Century** — The Qur'an is revealed, becoming the foundational text of Islamic literature and standardizing Arabic.
- **Rashidun Caliphate** — Literary centers develop in Mecca, Medina, Damascus, Kufa, and Basra; poetry serves the spread of Islam.
- **Umayyad Caliphate** — Literature becomes fractured and serves political factions; poetry markets like Mirbad are prominent.
- **Abbasid Caliphate** — The Islamic Golden Age begins; the House of Wisdom in Baghdad fosters major literary production and translation.
- **8th-15th Centuries** — Andalusi literature flourishes in Islamic Iberia, producing influential works and forms like the muwashshah.
- **19th Century** — The Nahda (Renaissance) begins, marked by neoclassicism, translation of Western works, and the rise of journalism.
- **Late 19th-20th C.** — The modern Arabic novel emerges as a key form of social critique; modern theatre develops.
- **Post-WWII** — Poets like Nazik Al-Malaika and Badr Shakir al-Sayyab pioneer the free verse movement in Arabic poetry.
- **1988** — Naguib Mahfouz becomes the first Arabic writer to win the Nobel Prize in Literature.

## Body

### Pre-Islamic and Early Islamic Literature
Before Islam, literature was primarily oral, centered on poetry recited at markets like Souq Okaz. Poets served as tribal propagandists and historians, preserving ideals of honour and lineage in works like the *Mu'allaqat*. The Qur'an, revealed in the 7th century, had the greatest lasting effect on Arabic culture, marking the beginning of Islamic literature and helping to unify and standardize the language. Under the Rashidun and Umayyad caliphates, literature, especially poetry, served the spread of Islam and political factions.

### The Islamic Golden Age and Classical Forms
The Abbasid period saw a significant flourishing of literature, with institutions like the House of Wisdom in Baghdad hosting scholars such as Al-Jahiz. This era produced major works of fiction like *One Thousand and One Nights*, as well as influential forms like the *maqama* (rhymed prose narratives) and extensive compilations on topics from cooking to geography. Religious scholarship, including the study of the Qur'an and the life of Muhammad, drove the development of Arabic grammar, lexicography, and literary criticism.

### The Nahda and Modern Literature
The 19th-century Nahda (Renaissance) was a revival marked by neoclassicism and the translation of Western literature, which provided new ideas and forms. This period saw the rise of the modern Arabic novel, with early examples like Muhammad Husayn Haykal's *Zaynab* (1913), and the development of modern theatre. Writers began to use literature for social critique and to explore themes of identity, colonialism, and political upheaval, a trend that continued through the 20th century with authors like Naguib Mahfouz and Tayeb Salih.

### Poetry and Fiction in the Modern Era
After World War II, Arabic poetry underwent a major transformation with the free verse movement, pioneered by Iraqi poets Badr Shakir al-Sayyab and Nazik Al-Malaika. This evolved into prose poetry and further modernist experimentation. The Arabic novel became a dominant form, often focusing on family life as a microcosm of broader Arab society and politics. Contemporary writers, including women like Nawal el-Saadawi and diaspora authors, continue to address themes of gender, politics, and cultural identity.

### Women in Arabic Literature
Women's literary expression was historically limited, with early poetesses like al-Khansa known for elegies. The Nahda saw the emergence of pioneering female writers like Zaynab Fawwaz and the establishment of literary salons. The 20th century witnessed a significant increase in women's literary output, with poets like Nazik Al-Malaika and Fadwa Touqan gaining prominence. Contemporary female authors, such as Hanan al-Shaykh and Ahdaf Soueif, often explore taboo subjects and engage in social activism through their work.

## Terms
- **Adab**: The Arabic word for literature, implying etiquette, culture, and enrichment.
- **Al-shiʿr al-Jāhilī**: "Poetry from the Jahiliyyah," referring to pre-Islamic Arabic poetry.
- **Mu'allaqat**: "The Suspended Ones," a group of famous pre-Islamic poems.
- **Saj'**: Rhymed prose, a form used in the Qur'an and later literature.
- **Maqama**: A literary genre of rhymed prose narratives, often fictionalized accounts of real-life situations.
- **Nahda**: The 19th-century Arab cultural and literary renaissance.
- **I'jaz**: The Islamic doctrine of the inimitability of the Qur'an's style.
- **Hija'**: Satirical or invective poetry, a major genre in classical Arabic literature.
- **Free Verse (shi'r hurr)**: A modernist movement in Arabic poetry that broke from classical meter and rhyme.

## Debates and Open questions
- The genre of the Qur'an is a matter of debate; it is considered by some to be a form of homily or compared to pre-Islamic *saj'*.
- The origins of the Arabic novel are contested, with multiple works from the 19th century claimed as the "first," suggesting it emerged from both European influence and the revival of traditional narrative genres.
- The critical reception of women's poetry in the Arab world has often been lukewarm, with debates ongoing about its adherence to traditional poetic principles and its place in the literary canon.
