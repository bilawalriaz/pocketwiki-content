# Speed of light

## Overview

The speed of light in vacuum, denoted c, is a fundamental physical constant exactly equal to 299,792,458 m/s. It represents the maximum speed at which all energy, matter, and information in the universe can travel, and serves as a cornerstone of modern physics, particularly Einstein's theory of relativity. The finite speed of light has profound implications for our understanding of space, time, and causality, and its precise measurement and definition have driven major advances in metrology and our understanding of the universe.

## Timeline

- **c. 490–430 BCE** — Empedocles proposes light has finite speed
- **1676** — Ole Rømer makes first quantitative estimate of c using Jupiter's moon Io
- **1729** — James Bradley discovers stellar aberration, refines c measurement
- **1856** — Weber and Kohlrausch find electromagnetic constant ratio close to c
- **1865** — Maxwell proposes light is electromagnetic wave traveling at c
- **1887** — Michelson-Morley experiment finds no motion relative to aether
- **1905** — Einstein postulates constancy of c in all inertial frames
- **1946** — Essen and Gordon-Smith measure c via cavity resonance
- **1972** — NBS measures c to 3.5×10⁻⁹ fractional uncertainty
- **1983** — Metre redefined so c is exactly 299,792,458 m/s

## Body

### Fundamental Nature and Definition

The speed of light in vacuum is identical for all electromagnetic radiation and all massless particles, including gravitational waves. This invariance means that regardless of the motion of the source or observer, c remains constant. By international agreement since 1983, the metre is defined as the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second, making c an exact defined constant. The second itself is defined by the hyperfine transition frequency of caesium-133 (9,192,631,770 cycles).

### Role in Relativity

Einstein's special relativity (1905) elevated c from a property of light to a fundamental feature of spacetime. The theory shows that c interrelates space and time, leading to phenomena like time dilation, length contraction, and mass-energy equivalence (E=mc²). The Lorentz factor γ = (1−v²/c²)⁻¹/² governs these effects, becoming significant only at speeds approaching c. Since c is the speed of massless particles, it represents the ultimate speed limit for information transfer; exceeding it would violate causality.

### Propagation in Materials

In transparent materials like glass or water, light travels slower than c. The refractive index n = c/v quantifies this reduction, with glass having n≈1.5 and air n≈1.0003. Different wave components travel at different velocities: phase velocity (crests/troughs), group velocity (pulse envelope), and front velocity (earliest pulse part). While group velocities can exceed c or become negative in exotic conditions, information always travels at or below c. Cherenkov radiation occurs when charged particles exceed the phase velocity of light in a medium.

### Measurement History

Early estimates relied on astronomical observations: Rømer used Io's eclipses (1676), Bradley used stellar aberration (1729). Earth-based methods included Fizeau's cogwheel (1849) and Foucault's rotating mirror (1850). Modern techniques use cavity resonance (Essen, 1946) and laser interferometry (NBS, 1972), achieving uncertainties below 10⁻⁹. The 1983 redefinition made c exact, shifting measurement focus to realizing the metre rather than determining c.

### Practical Implications

The finite speed of light affects telecommunications, computing, and space exploration. In computers, signals travel only ~30 cm per nanosecond, constraining processor placement. Global communications face ~67 ms minimum Earth-circumnavigation delay. Spacecraft communication delays range from 3 seconds (Moon) to 4–24 minutes (Mars). Astronomical observations reveal the universe's history, as distant objects appear as they were when light began its journey—Hubble Deep Field images show galaxies as they were 13 billion years ago.

## Terms

**Refractive index (n)**: Ratio of c to the speed of light in a material (n = c/v)

**Phase velocity**: Speed at which wave crests and troughs propagate

**Group velocity**: Speed at which the overall pulse envelope travels

**Front velocity**: Speed of the earliest part of a light pulse; always equals c

**Lorentz factor (γ)**: γ = (1−v²/c²)⁻¹/²; governs relativistic effects

**Cherenkov radiation**: Electromagnetic shock wave emitted when charged particles exceed phase velocity in a medium

**Einstein synchronization**: Convention for synchronizing distant clocks assuming constant one-way speed of light

**Lorentz invariance**: Symmetry principle requiring physical laws to be the same in all inertial frames

## Debates and Open Questions

**One-way speed of light**: Only the two-way speed can be measured without a synchronization convention. Einstein synchronization makes one-way speed equal to c by definition, but alternative conventions are theoretically possible.

**Varying constants**: Some theories propose c may have changed over cosmic time, though no conclusive evidence exists.

**Quantum gravity effects**: Certain models predict photon speed might depend on energy at Planck-scale energies, though observations of gamma-ray bursts like GRB 090510 found no such variation.

**Scharnhorst effect**: Theoretical prediction that light could travel faster than c between closely spaced conducting plates, though practical implementation appears impossible without violating causality.
