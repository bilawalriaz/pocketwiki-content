# Brandenburg Concertos

## Overview
The Brandenburg Concertos (BWV 1046–1051) are a collection of six instrumental works by Johann Sebastian Bach, presented to Christian Ludwig, Margrave of Brandenburg-Schwedt, in 1721. They are celebrated as masterpieces of the Baroque concerto grosso form, noted for their innovative and diverse instrumentation, which combines multiple solo instruments in unique ways. Their survival and eventual rise to canonical status involved a complex history of neglect, rediscovery, and modern revival.

## Timeline
- **c. 1713** — Possible early composition of material for Concerto No. 1 for a cantata.
- **1719** — Bach travels to Berlin, likely to purchase a new harpsichord for Concerto No. 5.
- **24 March 1721** — Bach dedicates and presents the compiled manuscript to Margrave Christian Ludwig.
- **1734** — Death of Margrave Christian Ludwig; the concertos' whereabouts become uncertain.
- **1849** — Siegfried Dehn rediscovers the manuscript in Princess Amalia's library.
- **1850** — First publication of the concertos, marking the centenary of Bach's death.
- **1936** — First recording of the complete set, directed by Adolf Busch.
- **1977** — A recording of Concerto No. 2 is included on the Voyager Golden Record sent into space.

## Body

### History and Dedication
The composition dates for the individual concertos are uncertain, though material was likely adapted from earlier works. Concerto No. 5 is presumed to be the last written, possibly to showcase a new harpsichord acquired in 1719. Bach compiled the six works in 1721, writing them largely in his own hand, and presented them to Margrave Christian Ludwig with a humble dedication. The title "Six Concertos for several instruments" is considered an understatement by scholars, as the works exhibit an "innovative" and "daring" use of a wide spectrum of orchestral instruments. It is debated whether the collection was tailored to the Margrave's ensemble or to the forces Bach had available in Köthen.

### Later Years and Rediscovery
After presentation, the concertos likely saw little use. They were not listed in the Margrave's library inventory after his death in 1734. The manuscript later passed to Bach's pupil Johann Kirnberger, then to Princess Anna Amalia, and eventually to the Berlin State Library. The collection was nearly lost during World War II when a librarian escaped a bombed train with the scores hidden under his coat. After Bach's death, only Concerto No. 5 received attention. The full set was rediscovered in 1849 and published in 1850, but widespread popularity came with 20th-century recording technology and the historically informed performance movement.

### Musical Characteristics and Individual Concertos
The concertos are defined by their varied and innovative scoring. Concerto No. 1 is unique in the set for having four movements. Concerto No. 2 features a notoriously difficult natural trumpet part. Concerto No. 3 is scored for three violins, three violas, and three cellos, and is the shortest of the six. Concerto No. 4 highlights a virtuosic violin part alongside two recorders ("flauti d'echo"). Concerto No. 5 is pivotal as an early example of a solo keyboard concerto, with the harpsord serving as both a solo and ensemble instrument. Concerto No. 6 is unusual for its absence of violins, instead featuring two violas da braccio and two older violas da gamba.

## Terms
- **Concerto Grosso**: A Baroque composition for a small group of soloists (concertino) contrasted with a larger ensemble (ripieno).
- **Basso Continuo**: The continuous bass line and harmonic foundation of a Baroque piece, typically played by a keyboard instrument and a cello or bass.
- **Ritornello Form**: A structure where a main theme (ritornello) played by the full ensemble alternates with episodes for the soloists.
- **Clarino**: A high-register playing style for natural trumpets, requiring specialized skill.
- **Viola da Braccio**: The modern orchestral viola, played "on the arm."
- **Viola da Gamba**: An older, fretted string instrument held between the legs, with a softer tone.
- **Phrygian Half Cadence**: A harmonic progression ending on a half cadence, often used to create tension or expectation.
- **Historically Informed Performance**: A movement aiming to perform music using instruments, techniques, and aesthetics from the composer's era.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the intended performers and occasion for the concertos. One view, noted by Heinrich Besseler, is that the instrumentation matches the 17 players Bach had in Köthen. Christoph Wolff contradicts this, arguing the collection does not reflect a specific ensemble. Another debate concerns the "fiauti d'echo" in Concerto No. 4, with theories suggesting they were recorders, transverse flutes, or flageolets. The purpose of the single-measure second movement in Concerto No. 3 is also uncertain, with performance practices ranging from simple chords to elaborate improvised cadenzas.
