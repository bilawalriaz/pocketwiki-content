# Prime meridian

## Overview
A prime meridian is an arbitrary line of longitude defined as 0° in a geographic coordinate system, dividing a celestial body into eastern and western hemispheres. Unlike the equator, it has no natural physical basis and has been defined differently throughout history for practical and political reasons. The current international standard is the IERS Reference Meridian, which evolved from the Greenwich Meridian adopted in 1884.

## Timeline
- **c. 190 BCE** — Hipparchus develops the concept of longitude in Greece.
- **c. 150 CE** — Ptolemy uses the "Fortunate Isles" as a prime meridian in his *Geographia*.
- **1494** — Treaty of Tordesillas establishes a line of demarcation in the Atlantic.
- **1541** — Mercator draws his prime meridian through Fuerteventura in the Canaries.
- **1765–1811** — Nevil Maskelyne's *Nautical Almanac* promotes the Greenwich meridian.
- **1884** — International Meridian Conference adopts the Greenwich Meridian as the world standard.
- **1984** — The IERS Reference Meridian is established as the new international standard.

## Body

### Historical Development
The concept of longitude was developed by Greek scholars like Eratosthenes and Hipparchus. Ptolemy was the first to use a consistent prime meridian for world maps, placing it west of Africa. For centuries, various meridians were used, often based on magnetic declination or specific islands like the Canaries or Azores. The 18th-century race to solve the longitude problem at sea, aided by the marine chronometer and lunar distance method, increased the importance of a standard reference. The Greenwich meridian gained dominance through the widespread use of the British *Nautical Almanac*.

### Adoption of Greenwich and the IERS Meridian
In 1884, the International Meridian Conference in Washington, D.C., voted to adopt the Greenwich meridian as the global prime meridian. The French abstained and continued using the Paris meridian until 1911. The historic Greenwich meridian was defined by the Airy Transit Circle at the Royal Observatory. In 1984, the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service (IERS) established the IERS Reference Meridian (IRM). This modern meridian is based on satellite and quasar observations, is centered on Earth's mass, and differs from the historic Greenwich line by about 102 meters (112 yards) eastward at Greenwich's latitude due to the correction for local gravity variations.

### Prime Meridians on Other Bodies
Prime meridians for other celestial bodies are also arbitrarily defined, often using a prominent surface feature. Examples include the crater Hun Kal on Mercury, Ariadne on Venus, and Airy-0 on Mars. For tidally locked moons like Earth's Moon or Jupiter's Galilean satellites, the prime meridian typically runs through the center of the face that always points toward the parent planet.

## Terms
- **Prime Meridian**: The meridian (line of longitude) in a geographic coordinate system at which longitude is defined to be 0°.
- **Meridian**: A line of longitude on a globe, connecting points of equal longitude.
- **Anti-meridian**: The meridian 180° from the prime meridian, forming the other half of the great ellipse that divides a body into hemispheres.
- **IERS Reference Meridian (IRM)**: The current international standard prime meridian, defined by the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service using space-geodetic techniques.
- **Greenwich Meridian**: The historic prime meridian established at the Royal Observatory, Greenwich, and adopted as the world standard in 1884.
- **Airy Transit Circle**: The astronomical instrument at Greenwich that defined the historic prime meridian from 1851.
- **Prograde Rotation**: Rotation in the same direction as a body's orbit (e.g., Earth).
- **Retrograde Rotation**: Rotation opposite to the direction of a body's orbit.

## Debates and Open Questions
The selection of any prime meridian is fundamentally arbitrary and has been a subject of historical and political debate. At the 1884 conference, the French argued for a neutral line, such as through the Bering Strait. The adoption of the Greenwich meridian was largely due to its practical dominance in maritime navigation. The shift from the historic Greenwich meridian to the IERS Reference Meridian in 1984 reflects an ongoing scientific need for a more precise, Earth-centered coordinate system that accounts for plate tectonics and variations in Earth's rotation.
