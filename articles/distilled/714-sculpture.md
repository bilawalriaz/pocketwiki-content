# Sculpture

## Overview
Sculpture is the three-dimensional branch of visual arts, creating form in height, width, and depth. It is one of the plastic arts, historically rooted in carving (material removal) and modeling (material addition), though modernism expanded materials and processes nearly without limit. Surviving ancient sculpture is dominated by durable media—stone, metal, ceramics—while perishable materials like wood rarely endure, and most ancient works were originally painted, much of which has been lost. Sculpture has long served religious, political, and commemorative functions, with large works typically funded by institutions rather than individuals, making it a marker of cultural sophistication and social organization.

## Timeline
- **c. 35–40,000 BP** — Löwenmensch and Venus of Hohle Fels, oldest known sculptures
- **c. 9,000 BCE** — Urfa Man, earliest large stone figure
- **c. 323 BCE** — Start of Hellenistic period in Greek sculpture
- **146 BCE** — Roman conquest of Greece; Greek sculptors brought to Rome
- **1403** — Renaissance sculpture begins with Ghiberti’s Baptistry doors competition
- **1600s** — Baroque sculpture peaks with Gian Lorenzo Bernini
- **1917** — Marcel Duchamp’s *Fountain* introduces readymade sculpture
- **1967** — Picasso’s Chicago Picasso, major public modernist commission

## Body

### Types and Distinctions
Sculpture is divided into **sculpture in the round** (free-standing, fully three-dimensional) and **relief** (attached to a background). Relief is classified by projection: **low relief** (bas-relief), **high relief**, and **mid-relief**; **sunk relief** is an Egyptian specialty. **Monumental sculpture** refers to large works, often building-attached. Small forms include **figurines** (under 18 inches), **plaquettes**, **coins**, and **hardstone carvings**. Modern forms include **kinetic sculpture** (motion-based), **land art**, **site-specific art**, and **soft sculpture** (using fabric, rubber, etc.).

### Materials and Techniques
Traditional materials include **stone**, **metal** (especially bronze), **wood**, **ceramics**, and **ivory**. **Carving** removes material; **modeling** adds it. **Casting** uses molds (lost-wax, sand, plaster). **Welding** and **forging** join or shape metal. **Polychromy** (painting) was common in antiquity but often lost. Modern sculptors use **plastics**, **found objects**, **ice**, **sand**, and **light**.

### Purposes and Subjects
Religious devotion is a primary function: **cult images**, **Buddhist figures**, **Hindu lingams**, and **Christian icons**. Monumental sculpture signals power—pharaohs, emperors, rulers. **Portrait sculpture** began in Egypt and Mesopotamia, flourished in Rome, and evolved through the Renaissance. **Totem poles** and **hill figures** reflect cultural identity. **Animals** and **mythical creatures** are frequent subjects. **Commemorative portraiture** honors elites, while **small figurines** serve personal or decorative roles.

### Historical Development
**Prehistoric Europe** produced Venus figurines and cave art. The **Ancient Near East** contributed the Urfa Man and Sumerian reliefs. **Egyptian sculpture** emphasized idealization and used sunk relief. **Greek sculpture** evolved from Cycladic abstraction to Classical naturalism (Phidias, Myron) and Hellenistic emotion (Laocoön, Dying Gaul). **Roman sculpture** borrowed Greek styles but emphasized portraiture and narrative relief. **Medieval sculpture** was largely religious (Gothic cathedrals). The **Renaissance** revived classical forms (Donatello, Michelangelo). **Mannerism** and **Baroque** introduced dynamism (Bernini). **Neoclassicism** returned to idealism (Canova). **Modernism** broke conventions—Picasso’s assemblages, Brâncuși’s abstraction, Calder’s kinetics. **Contemporary art** embraces conceptual, environmental, and multimedia approaches.

### Social Status of Sculptors
Historically, sculptors were artisans, often anonymous. In Greece, Phidias gained fame but remained a craftsman. Medieval sculptors like Gislebertus were respected. Renaissance masters (Michelangelo, Donatello) achieved celebrity status. Women were largely excluded until the 20th century.

### Anti-Sculpture Movements
**Aniconism** in Judaism and Islam rejected figurative art. **Christianity** initially opposed religious images but later embraced them. **Protestant iconoclasm** destroyed sculptures. **Taliban destruction** of Buddhas (2001) exemplifies modern iconoclasm.

## Terms
- **Carving**: Subtractive sculpting technique removing material from a block.
- **Modeling**: Additive technique shaping malleable material like clay.
- **Relief**: Sculpture attached to a background, varying in projection depth.
- **Monumental sculpture**: Large-scale works, often public or architectural.
- **Polychromy**: Application of paint to sculpture, common in antiquity.
- **Kinetic sculpture**: Art incorporating movement, often motorized or wind-driven.
- **Readymade**: Found object presented as art, pioneered by Duchamp.
- **Lost-wax casting**: Process using a wax model to create a mold for metal casting.
- **Sunk relief**: Technique where design is cut into a surface, used in Egypt.
- **Figurine**: Small human or animal sculpture, typically under 18 inches.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent and appearance of **prehistoric wood sculpture** remains speculative due to poor preservation.
- The **social status** of sculptors in antiquity versus their modern celebrity status is debated.
- The **original appearance** of painted ancient sculptures is still being reconstructed through scientific analysis.
- The **causes** behind the decline of Roman sculptural technique in the 3rd century CE remain unclear.
- The **impact** of African and Oceanic sculpture on European Modernism is acknowledged but still analyzed.
- The **preservation bias** toward stone and bronze skews understanding of ancient artistic practices.
