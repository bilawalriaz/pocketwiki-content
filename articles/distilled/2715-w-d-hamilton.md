# W. D. Hamilton

## Overview
William Donald Hamilton (1936–2000) was a British evolutionary biologist whose theoretical work provided a rigorous genetic basis for altruism, fundamentally shaping the gene-centered view of evolution. His insights are central to sociobiology and the study of social evolution, explaining how cooperation and self-sacrifice can evolve through natural selection.

## Timeline
- **1 August 1936** — Born in Cairo, Egypt.
- **1946** — Discovers E.B. Ford's *Butterflies*, sparking interest in evolution.
- **1964** — Publishes "The Genetical Evolution of Social Behaviour," formulating Hamilton's rule.
- **1964–1977** — Lecturer at Imperial College London.
- **1967** — Publishes "Extraordinary sex ratios" in *Science*.
- **1978** — Becomes Professor of Evolutionary Biology at the University of Michigan.
- **1980** — Elected Fellow of the Royal Society.
- **1984** — Becomes Royal Society Research Professor at Oxford University.
- **1993** — Awarded the Crafoord Prize and the Kyoto Prize.
- **7 March 2000** — Dies in London from complications following a field trip to the Congo.

## Body

### Hamilton's Rule and Social Evolution
Hamilton's seminal 1964 papers provided a mathematical solution to the problem of how altruism—behavior that reduces an actor's fitness to benefit another—could evolve. He realized that the key was genetic relatedness, formalized as **Hamilton's rule**: a costly altruistic action (C) is favored if the benefit to the recipient (B) multiplied by their coefficient of relatedness (r) exceeds the cost (rB > C). This explained altruism in terms of inclusive fitness, where an individual's genes are propagated through relatives. The work was initially overlooked but became foundational, especially for understanding eusociality in haplodiploid insects like ants and bees, where females share more genes with sisters than with offspring. Hamilton also clarified the "green-beard effect," where genes for social behavior are linked to genes for recognizing the same allele in others.

### Sex Ratios and the Evolution of Sex
Hamilton's work on sex ratios explained deviations from the expected 1:1 ratio predicted by Fisher. His 1967 paper introduced the concept of an "unbeatable strategy," which later evolved into the **evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS)**, a key concept in game theory. He was also an early proponent of the **Red Queen hypothesis** for the evolution of sex, which posits that sexual reproduction persists because it generates genetic diversity, allowing hosts to continuously evade co-evolving parasites.

### Spite, Cooperation, and Later Work
Hamilton extended his framework to explain spiteful behavior—harming others at a cost to oneself—as potentially increasing an actor's fitness by harming less-related individuals. He collaborated with Robert Axelrod on the evolution of cooperation using the prisoner's dilemma. His later interests included the coevolution theory of autumn leaf color as a signal and the "Spora and Gaia" hypothesis, which proposed that marine algae promote cloud formation to aid their global dispersal. He also investigated the controversial and now-discredited hypothesis linking HIV origin to oral polio vaccine trials.

## Terms
- **Hamilton's rule**: The inequality rB > C, which predicts when altruistic behavior will be favored by natural selection.
- **Coefficient of relatedness (r)**: The probability that two individuals share an allele due to common descent, ranging from 0 to 1.
- **Inclusive fitness**: An organism's total genetic success, measured by its own reproduction and the reproduction of relatives who share its genes.
- **Eusociality**: The highest level of social organization in animals, characterized by cooperative brood care, overlapping generations, and reproductive division of labor.
- **Haplodiploidy**: A sex-determination system where males develop from unfertilized eggs (haploid) and females from fertilized eggs (diploid), affecting genetic relatedness.
- **Evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS)**: A strategy which, if adopted by a population, cannot be invaded by any alternative mutant strategy.
- **Red Queen hypothesis**: A theory that organisms must constantly adapt to survive in the face of co-evolving parasites and competitors.
- **Green-beard effect**: A hypothetical genetic mechanism where a single gene (or linked genes) codes for a trait, a preference for that trait in others, and altruism toward bearers of the trait.

## Debates and Open questions
- **Peer review delay**: Hamilton believed John Maynard Smith's review of his 1964 papers delayed publication, allowing Maynard Smith to publish similar ideas first, causing lasting friction.
- **Origin of HIV**: Hamilton supported the now-discredited "OPV AIDS hypothesis," which claimed HIV originated from contaminated polio vaccines. This remains a controversial episode in his career.
- **Cause of death**: While officially due to gastrointestinal hemorrhage, reports linked his death to complications from malaria contracted during his final research trip to the Congo, a connection the coroner called "entirely indirect."
