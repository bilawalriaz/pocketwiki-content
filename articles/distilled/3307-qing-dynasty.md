# Qing dynasty

## Overview
The Qing dynasty (1636/1644–1912) was the last imperial dynasty of China, founded by the Manchu people. It assembled the territorial base for modern China, becoming the largest and most populous empire in Chinese history. Its rule saw a peak of power and expansion in the 18th century, followed by a long decline marked by internal rebellions, foreign invasions, and failed reforms, culminating in its overthrow by the Xinhai Revolution.

## Timeline
- **1616** — Nurhaci founds the Later Jin dynasty, uniting Jurchen (Manchu) tribes.
- **1636** — Hong Taiji renames the dynasty "Great Qing" and declares himself Emperor.
- **1644** — Qing forces seize Beijing after Ming general Wu Sangui opens the Shanhai Pass.
- **1683** — Qing conquest of China proper is completed with the surrender of Taiwan.
- **1689** — Treaty of Nerchinsk signed with Russia, stabilizing the northern border.
- **1790s** — Qing Empire reaches its greatest territorial extent and population (~300 million).
- **1839–42** — First Opium War ends with the Treaty of Nanjing, opening treaty ports.
- **1850–64** — Taiping Rebellion causes massive devastation and millions of deaths.
- **1894–95** — First Sino-Japanese War ends with the cession of Taiwan to Japan.
- **1900** — Boxer Uprising and invasion by the Eight-Nation Alliance.
- **1911** — Wuchang Uprising triggers the Xinhai Revolution.
- **1912** — The Xuantong Emperor (Puyi) abdicates, ending the dynasty.

## Body

### Formation and Conquest
The Qing was founded by the Manchus, a Tungusic people from Manchuria. Their leader Nurhaci unified Jurchen tribes, created the Eight Banners military system, and proclaimed the Later Jin dynasty in 1616. His son Hong Taiji adopted the name "Manchu," formally established the Qing dynasty in 1636, and built a Chinese-style bureaucracy. After Ming control collapsed to peasant rebels in 1644, the Qing entered China proper by allying with Ming general Wu Sangui. They captured Beijing and presented themselves as heirs to the Ming mandate. Complete conquest took until 1683, overcoming Ming loyalists and the Revolt of the Three Feudatories.

### The High Qing Era (1683–1796)
The reigns of the Kangxi, Yongzheng, and Qianlong emperors marked the dynasty's zenith. The Kangxi Emperor consolidated rule, suppressed the Three Feudatories, and expanded into Central Asia and Taiwan. The Yongzheng Emperor strengthened central administration and finances. The Qianlong Emperor oversaw the "Ten Great Campaigns," expanding the empire to its largest size and compiling massive cultural projects. This era was characterized by population growth (to over 300 million), economic prosperity, and the effective rule of a multi-ethnic empire, where the emperor acted as Confucian sage, Mongol khan, and Tibetan Buddhist patron.

### Decline and Collapse
The 19th century brought severe challenges. The Opium Wars (1839–42, 1856–60) forced China to accept unequal treaties, cede territory, and open ports to foreign trade. Massive internal rebellions, notably the Taiping Rebellion (1850–64), caused immense death and fiscal crisis. The Self-Strengthening Movement attempted to adopt Western military technology but failed to prevent defeat by Japan in 1895. Reform efforts, like the Hundred Days' Reform (1898), were blocked by conservatives. The Boxer Uprising (1900) and subsequent foreign invasion further weakened the state. Late Qing reforms, including abolishing the imperial examination system (1905), came too late. The Wuchang Uprising (1911) sparked revolution, leading to the abdication of the last emperor in 1912.

### Government and Military
The Qing government adopted Ming bureaucratic structures but maintained a dual system of Manchu and Han appointments. The emperor ruled absolutely, supported by an "Inner Court" centered on the Grand Council. The military was initially based on the multi-ethnic Eight Banners, later supplemented by the Green Standard Army of Han Chinese soldiers. By the 19th century, the military was outdated and ineffective against Western powers, leading to failed modernization efforts and the rise of regional armies under Han Chinese officials.

### Society and Economy
Qing society was hierarchical, with a scholar-gentry class recruited through civil service exams. The population grew dramatically, fueled by New World crops and internal migration. The economy transitioned toward a market system, with widespread commercialization and the use of silver as currency. Trade with the West, initially managed through the Canton Cohong system, created a favorable balance until the opium trade reversed it, leading to war. Government revenues remained low relative to the population, contributing to fiscal weakness.

## Terms
*   **Eight Banners:** A Manchu social, military, and administrative system that organized Manchu, Mongol, and Han Chinese populations into units for governance and warfare.
*   **Mandate of Heaven:** The traditional Chinese belief that heaven grants a ruler the right to govern, which can be lost due to misrule, justifying rebellion.
*   **Tributary System:** A traditional East Asian diplomatic framework where neighboring states acknowledged Chinese superiority through ritual tribute, in exchange for trade rights and recognition.
*   **Unequal Treaties:** A series of 19th-century agreements imposed on China by Western powers and Japan, granting extraterritoriality, trade concessions, and indemnities.
*   **Self-Strengthening Movement:** A 1860s–1890s policy of adopting Western military and industrial technology while preserving Confucian values and institutions.
*   **Queue Order:** A 1645 decree forcing Han Chinese men to adopt the Manchu hairstyle (shaving the front of the head and braiding the rest) as a sign of submission.
*   **Grand Council:** The key advisory body to the emperor in the Inner Court, which became the de facto cabinet handling military and administrative affairs.
*   **Lifan Yuan:** The Qing government agency responsible for managing relations with Mongolia, Tibet, and other Inner Asian territories.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **New Qing History:** A historiographical school emphasizing the distinct Manchu character of the dynasty, arguing rulers maintained a separate identity and used Central Asian models of rule alongside Confucianism, rather than simply sinicizing.
*   **Definition of "China":** Scholars debate when and how the Qing transformed the concept of "China" from a primarily ethnic Han state to a multi-ethnic empire that included Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang, and Tibet as integral parts.
*   **Causes of Declow:** Historians weigh the relative importance of internal factors (corruption, fiscal crisis, rebellions) versus external pressures (Western imperialism, technological gap) in the dynasty's collapse.
