# Harriet Tubman

## Overview
Harriet Tubman (c. 1822–1913) was an American abolitionist, social activist, and Union Army scout who escaped slavery and became the most famous "conductor" on the Underground Railroad, leading approximately 70 people to freedom. Her life of resistance, from covert rescue missions to military leadership, made her an enduring icon of courage and freedom.

## Timeline
- **c. March 1822** — Born Araminta Ross in Dorchester County, Maryland.
- **1849** — Escapes slavery in Maryland, reaching Philadelphia.
- **1850** — Fugitive Slave Act passed; Tubman begins guiding escapees to Canada.
- **1858** — Meets and supports abolitionist John Brown.
- **1859** — Purchases a farm in Auburn, New York.
- **1861** — Begins serving the Union Army as a nurse and cook.
- **1863** — Leads the Combahee River Raid, liberating over 700 enslaved people.
- **1869** — Marries Nelson Davis, a Civil War veteran.
- **1896** — Begins efforts to establish a home for the aged.
- **1908** — Harriet Tubman Home for the Aged opens in Auburn.
- **March 10, 1913** — Dies of pneumonia in Auburn, New York.

## Body

### Early Life and Escape
Born into slavery, Tubman endured severe beatings as a child. A traumatic head injury from an overseer's thrown weight caused lifelong seizures, headaches, and vivid dreams she interpreted as divine premonitions. This injury and her devout faith shaped her lifelong belief in divine guidance. In 1849, she escaped to Philadelphia, but soon returned to Maryland to rescue her family.

### The Underground Railroad
Tubman made approximately 13 missions back to Maryland, personally guiding dozens of enslaved people to freedom using the Underground Railroad, a network of safe houses and activists. She traveled by night, used disguises and coded spirituals, and carried a revolver for protection. She was nicknamed "Moses" for leading her people to freedom. After the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, she guided escapees farther north to Canada. She never lost a passenger.

### Civil War Service
When the Civil War began, Tubman served the Union Army, first as a nurse and cook, then as an armed scout and spy. In 1863, she guided Union gunboats up the Combahee River in South Carolina, helping to liberate more than 700 enslaved people in a raid she is widely credited as the first woman to lead in the United States. She continued her service for the duration of the war.

### Later Life and Activism
After the war, Tubman settled in Auburn, New York, caring for her family and the needy. She faced financial hardship and was slow to receive compensation for her military service. In her later years, she became an active speaker for the women's suffrage movement. She helped establish the Harriet Tubman Home for the Aged, where she spent her final years.

## Terms
*   **Underground Railroad:** The informal network of activists and safe houses that helped enslaved people escape to free states and Canada.
*   **Conductor:** A guide who led enslaved people along Underground Railroad routes.
*   **Fugitive Slave Act of 1850:** A federal law that required citizens in free states to assist in capturing escaped slaves and imposed heavy penalties for aiding escape.
*   **Contraband:** A term used by Union forces during the Civil War for enslaved people who escaped to Union lines, treated as seized property rather than immediately freed.
*   **Combahee River Raid:** An 1863 Union military operation in South Carolina, guided by Tubman, that liberated over 700 enslaved people.
*   **Suffrage:** The right to vote in political elections.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Birth Year:** Tubman's exact birth year is uncertain; sources list 1815, 1820, and 1825, but modern biographers accept c. 1822 based on historical documents.
*   **Reward for Capture:** A popular legend claims a $40,000 reward was offered for her capture, but historians consider this a manufactured figure with no contemporary evidence.
*   **"Go or Die" Stories:** Accounts of Tubman threatening to shoot escapees who wanted to turn back are examined by scholars as reflecting the extreme dangers of the journey, though there is no evidence she killed anyone.
