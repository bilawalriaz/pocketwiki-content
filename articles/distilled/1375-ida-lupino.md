# Ida Lupino

## Overview

Ida Lupino (1918–1995) was a British-American actress and filmmaker who worked primarily in the United States, becoming a citizen in 1948. She is widely regarded as the most prominent female filmmaker working in the 1950s Hollywood studio system, and was the first woman to direct a film noir (*The Hitch-Hiker*, 1953). Through her independent production company The Filmakers Inc., she wrote, produced, and directed low-budget, socially conscious films tackling taboo subjects like rape, bigamy, and unwed pregnancy—work now seen as pioneering proto-feminist filmmaking. As an actress, she appeared in 59 films over 48 years, earning critical acclaim for her realistic, direct style.

## Body

### Early Life and Family

Lupino was born at 33 Ardbeg Road in Herne Hill, London, into the theatrical Lupino family; her father Stanley was a music hall comedian, her mother Connie O'Shea an actress. Her great-grandfather was an acrobatic clown descended from Italian puppeteers. She wrote her first play at age seven, toured with a theatre company as a child, and memorized leading Shakespearean female roles by age ten. Her uncle Lupino Lane helped her get background work at British International Studios. Though she wanted to be a writer, she enrolled in the Royal Academy of Dramatic Art to please her father. She felt pushed into acting by family history and disliked many early roles, often playing prostitutes or "bad girls."

### Acting Career

Lupino made her first film appearance in 1931 and worked under Allan Dwan at age 14. After early British films, Paramount discovered her in *Money for Speed* (1933), though she claimed scouts saw only her "sweet girl" role, not her prostitute part. She starred in over a dozen Paramount films before leaving in late 1937, unsatisfied with her roles. After a year off screen, she worked at Columbia and 20th Century-Fox, then returned to Paramount for *The Light That Failed* (1939)—a role she got by barging into director William Wellman's office demanding an audition. This breakthrough led to better parts, including *They Drive by Night* (1940) and *High Sierra* (1941) with Humphrey Bogart. Her performance in *The Hard Way* (1943) won the New York Film Critics Circle Award for Best Actress. She frequently clashed with Warner Bros. boss Jack Warner over casting and scripts, spending much time suspended—time she used to observe filming and editing, sparking her interest in directing.

### Directing: The Filmakers Inc.

Lupino and Collier Young formed The Filmakers Inc. in 1948 to produce low-budget, issue-oriented films. The company produced 12 features; Lupino directed or co-directed six, wrote or co-wrote five, acted in three, and co-produced one. Their mission was social consciousness and realism, shooting B pictures in two weeks for under $200,000. Her first directing job came in 1949 when director Elmer Clifton suffered a heart attack; Lupino finished *Not Wanted* without taking credit out of respect. *Never Fear*, about polio—which she had personally experienced—was her first credited direction; it was released in 1950. Howard Hughes agreed to finance and distribute their next three features through RKO. She directed *Outrage* (1950), one of the first films about rape, and *The Bigamist* (1953), which received rave reviews. *The Hitch-Hiker* (1953) made her the first woman to direct a film noir. She was a budget-conscious filmmaker, reusing sets, using product placement, and shooting in public places to cut costs. The company ceased operations in 1955.

### Television

From 1956 through 1968, Lupino directed episodes of more than 30 US TV series, including *Alfred Hitchcock Presents*, *The Twilight Zone*, *Have Gun – Will Travel*, *Gilligan's Island*, *The Fugitive*, and *Bewitched*. She was the only woman to direct an episode of the original *Twilight Zone* ("The Masks") and the only person to both act in one episode ("The Sixteen-Millimeter Shrine") and direct another. She continued acting in television through the 1970s, starring with husband Howard Duff in *Mr. Adams and Eve* (1957–58) and guest-starring in numerous shows. Her final acting appearance was in the 1979 film *My Boys Are Good Boys*.

### Themes

Lupino's Filmakers movies dealt with controversial subjects studio producers avoided: out-of-wedlock pregnancy, bigamy, and rape. She described her work as "films that had social significance and yet were entertainment... based on true stories." She focused on women's issues and liked characters with "intestinal fortitude." Martin Scorsese noted her heroines were "young women whose middle-class security was shattered by trauma—unwanted pregnancy, polio, rape, bigamy, parental abuse." However, biographer Donati argued the solutions to characters' problems were often "conventional, even conservative, more reinforcing the 1950s' ideology than undercutting it."

### Personal Life and Death

Lupino was diagnosed with polio in 1934; her recovery gave her confidence in her intellectual abilities. She became an American citizen in June 1948 and supported John F. Kennedy's presidency. She married and divorced three times: actor Louis Hayward (1938–1945), producer Collier Young (1948–1951), and actor Howard Duff (1951–1983), with whom she had a daughter, Bridget (born 1952). She died from a stroke while undergoing colon cancer treatment on 3 August 1995, aged 77. Her memoirs were published posthumously.

## Terms

- **Film noir**: A style of Hollywood crime drama, typically dark and cynical, popular in the 1940s–50s
- **Proto-feminist**: Early work anticipating feminist themes before the modern feminist movement
- **The Filmakers Inc.**: Lupino's independent production company (1948–1955) making socially conscious B pictures
- **B pictures**: Low-budget films, often shown as the second half of a double feature
- **Femme fatale**: A seductive, dangerous woman character common in film noir
- **Product placement**: Featuring branded commercial products within a film
- **National Film Registry**: US program preserving culturally significant films

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars debate whether Lupino should be considered a feminist filmmaker. Lupino herself rejected the label, saying "men hate bossy females" and that she often pretended to know less than she did to get cooperation. However, critic Carrie Rickey holds her up as "a model of modern feminist filmmaking," noting each of her movies "addresses the brutal repercussions of sexuality, independence and dependence." Critics also debate whether her films' conventional endings reinforced 1950s ideology or undercut it. Ronnie Scheib argues Lupino wasn't concerned with strong people but with "lost, bewildered people" and postwar trauma, comparing her to modernist directors Nicholas Ray, Sam Fuller, and Robert Aldrich.
