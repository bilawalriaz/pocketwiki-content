# Rudaki

## Overview
Rudaki (c. 858–940/41) was a Persian poet, singer, and musician recognized as the first major literary figure to write in New Persian, the language that emerged after the Muslim conquest replaced Middle Persian as the literary standard. Serving as a court poet under the Samanid dynasty (819–999) in Central Asia, he composed a reported 180,000 verses, though only fragments survive, most notably his versification of the *Kalīla wa-Dimna* (a Persian translation of the Indian *Panchatantra* fables). His career flourished under the patronage of the vizier Abu'l-Fadl al-Bal'ami but collapsed after the vizier's downfall in 937, leading to Rudaki's dismissal, poverty, and death in his hometown. He is honored in Iran as the "founder of New Persian poetry" and in Tajikistan as the "father of Tajik literature."

## Timeline
- **c. 858** — Born in the village of Banoj (Panjrud), in the Rudak region between Samarqand and Bukhara.
- **c. 866** — By age eight, reportedly memorized the Qur'an and showed skill in poetry and music.
- **907** — Death of Samanid amir Ismail Samani; Rudaki may have joined the court under his successor, Ahmad Samani (r. 907–914).
- **914** — Accession of Nasr II (r. 914–943); Rudaki firmly established at the Samanid court.
- **923–963** — Abu Ja'far Ahmad ibn Muhammad governs Sistan; Rudaki composes a *qasida* for him, earning 10,000 dinars.
- **936** — Death of Rudaki's student and friend, the poet Shahid Balkhi; Rudaki writes an elegy.
- **937** — Downfall of patron Abu'l-Fadl al-Bal'ami; Rudaki loses favor and is dismissed from court.
- **940/41** — Dies blind and in poverty in his hometown.
- **1958** — 1100th birth anniversary commemorated by Iran and the Tajik SSR; burial site discovered and remains analyzed by Mikhail Gerasimov.
- **1950s** — *Qasida* "Bu-ye ju-ye Mulian" set to music by Ruhollah Khaleqi; becomes a radio hit following the 1953 Iranian coup.

## Body

### Origins and the Rise of New Persian
Rudaki lived during the Samanid Empire, a period when New Persian (*darī* or *parsī-i darī*) emerged as a literary language distinct from Middle Persian (*pārsīk* or Pahlavi), the Zoroastrian literary standard of the Sasanian era (224–651). New Persian was not a direct descendant of literary Middle Persian but evolved from the dialect of the Sasanian court at Ctesiphon and the northeastern region of Khurasan. Rudaki was born c. 858 in Banoj, located in the Rudak district between the major cultural centers of Samarqand and Bukhara. Early sources imply he was blind—later historians like Awfi (d. 1242) claimed he was born blind—though modern scholars question this given the vivid natural imagery in his verse. Besides poetry, he mastered the *chang* (a harp-like instrument) under the musician Abu'l-Abak Bakhtiar, continuing the Sasanian tradition of performing poetry as song.

### Court Career and Patronage
Rudaki’s professional life centered on the Samanid court. Literary scholar Sassan Tabatabai argues he joined under Ahmad Samani (r. 907–914), citing a poem consoling Ahmad on his father Ismail’s death in 907. He rose to prominence under Nasr II (r. 914–943), fulfilling the classical Persian role of the court poet: not merely an entertainer but, per the Sasanian founder Ardashir I, "part of government and the means of strengthening rulership," tasked with eulogy, advice, and moral guidance. His ascent depended on the vizier Abu'l-Fadl al-Bal'ami (d. 940), a key architect of 10th-century Persian literary culture who considered Rudaki superior to all other Persian and Arab poets. Rudaki formed a close intellectual bond with the poet-scholar Shahid Balkhi, mourning his death in 936 with an elegy. When Bal'ami fell from power in 937, Rudaki lost his protector, was dismissed by the amir, and spent his final years in destitution.

### Surviving Works and Themes
Asadi Tusi recorded that Rudaki’s *divan* (collected short poems) exceeded 180,000 verses, but the vast majority is lost. Surviving fragments appear in lexicons like Asadi’s *Lughat-i Furs*. The most substantial complete poem is a *qasida* (formal ode) of nearly 100 verses preserved in the *Tarikh-i Sistan*, addressed to the Sistan governor Abu Ja'far Ahmad ibn Muhammad (r. 923–963). In it, Rudaki praises the governor’s Sasanian ancestry and calls him the "pride of Iran," signaling a conscious continuity of Iranian identity across the Islamic transition. For this, he received 10,000 dinars. His magnum opus was the versification of *Kalīla wa-Dimna*, a collection of Indian fables translated from Arabic to Persian on Nasr II’s orders. Because Rudaki was blind, "interpreters" read the prose translation aloud so he could versify it; only scattered verses remain. His poetry is secular despite pro-Isma'ili sympathies, frequently invoking pre-Islamic Zoroastrian concepts and the *andarz* (ethical counsel) tradition rather than Islamic motifs, reflecting a society where Islam was established but Zoroastrian memory remained vivid.

### Legacy and Reception
Rudaki’s status as the "first major poet of New Persian" rests on the survival and influence of his work, unlike earlier figures such as Abu Hafs Sughdi (d. 902) whose output is lost. Iranologist Richard Foltz notes the Iranian and Tajik titles for him are complementary. He remained the preeminent Persian poet until Ferdowsi (d. 1020/25), though his popularity waned during the Mongol period. His name was later attached to the *Pseudo-Diwan-i Rudaki*, a literary forgery identified by scholars like Francois de Blois as largely the work of Qatran Tabrizi (d. after 1088). A 1958 joint Iran–Tajik SSR conference rediscovered his tomb in Panjrud; Soviet anthropologist Mikhail Gerasimov reconstructed his face from the skull. In the 20th century, his *qasida* "Bu-ye ju-ye Mulian" (The Fragrance of Mulian Creek) was set to music by Ruhollah Khaleqi. Performed by Marzieh and Gholam-Hossein Banan, it dominated Iranian radio after the 1953 coup; Peyman Vahabzadeh argues the song—invoking Rudaki’s call for the Samanid emir to return from Herat to Bukhara—was commissioned to allegorically celebrate the Shah’s restoration.

## Terms
- ****New Persian (Darī/Parsī-i Darī)**** — The stage of the Persian language emerging after the Muslim conquest (7th c.), descended from the Sasanian court dialect and Khurasani vernacular, not literary Middle Persian (Pahlavi); the medium of Rudaki’s poetry.
- ****Samanid Empire (819–999)**** — A Sunni Iranian dynasty ruling Central Asia and Greater Khorasan; patrons of the "Persian Renaissance" and Rudaki’s primary employers.
- ****Vizier (Wazīr)**** — Chief minister in Islamic polities; Abu'l-Fadl al-Bal'ami held this post under Nasr II and was Rudaki’s crucial patron.
- ****Qasida**** — A formal, monorhyme ode (often panegyric) in classical Persian/Arabic poetry; Rudaki’s surviving 100-verse poem for the Sistan governor exemplifies the form.
- ****Chang**** — An angular harp used in Persian court music; Rudaki was a skilled player, continuing the Sasanian tradition of sung poetry.
- ****Kalīla wa-Dimna**** — A collection of animal fables originating in the Sanskrit *Panchatantra*, translated into Arabic (c. 750) then Persian; Rudaki versified the Persian version for the blind Nasr II.
- ****Andarz**** — A genre of pre-Islamic Persian "wisdom literature" offering ethical counsel and advice for conduct; Rudaki employed this style.
- ****Divan**** — A poet’s collected short lyrics (ghazals, qasidas, etc.); Asadi Tusi claimed Rudaki’s contained over 180,000 verses.
- ****Pseudo-Diwan-i Rudaki**** — A later forged collection attributed to Rudaki, exposed as largely the work of the 11th-century poet Qatran Tabrizi.
- ****Isma'ili**** — A branch of Shi'a Islam; Rudaki’s poetry shows sympathies for this movement, which was influential in Samanid lands.

## Debates and open questions
- **Blindness:** Early poets (Daqiqi, Ferdowsi) imply acquired blindness; Awfi (13th c.) claims congenital blindness. Modern scholars debate this, citing the visual richness of his nature descriptions as evidence against lifelong blindness.
- **Court entry date:** Standard biographies link Rudaki to Nasr II (from 914), but Sassan Tabatabai argues for an earlier start under Ahmad Samani (907–914) based on a consolatory poem for Ismail Samani’s death.
- **Ethnic/national designation:** Iran claims him as founder of "New Persian poetry"; Tajikistan claims him as father of "Tajik literature." Scholars (e.g., Foltz) view these as non-contradictory reflections of a shared heritage.
- **Isma'ili affiliation:** Rudaki’s "pro-Isma'ili sympathies" are noted, but the extent of his doctrinal commitment versus poetic convention remains unclear.
- **Authenticity of corpus:** The *Pseudo-Diwan* complicated textual history for centuries; distinguishing genuine fragments from later attributions (especially Qatran Tabrizi’s work) remains a philological task.
