# Death

## Overview

Death is the irreversible cessation of biological functions that sustain a living organism, though identifying the precise moment of death is difficult. The study of death is known as thanatology. Historically defined by cessation of heartbeat and breathing, death is now more commonly defined by brain activity, yet debates persist over which definition is correct. Understanding death matters because it shapes medical practice, legal standards, organ donation, cultural rituals, and religious beliefs about the afterlife.

## Body

### Defining death

Defining death requires distinguishing it from life, which is difficult due to little consensus on how to define life. One approach defines life in terms of consciousness, but many organisms are alive yet probably not conscious, and consciousness itself has many definitions. Some religious traditions hold that death does not entail the end of consciousness, and some cultures view death as a process rather than a single event.

Historically, death was defined as cessation of heartbeat and breathing (clinical death), but CPR and defibrillation made this reversible. This is now called the circulatory definition of death (CDD). Critics argue that while cessation may be permanent, it is not necessarily irreversible if CPR is applied quickly. The debate hinges on defining "permanent" versus "irreversible."

Brain death is now the standard when a definition is required. It presumes that cessation of electrical activity in the brain indicates the end of consciousness. However, some scholars challenge this, noting that brain-dead patients maintained on ventilation can sustain circulation, heal wounds, fight infections, and even gestate fetuses. EEGs can detect spurious impulses, and drugs, hypoglycemia, hypoxia, or hypothermia can temporarily suppress brain activity, so hospitals use protocols with EEGs at widely separated intervals.

Two brain-death variants exist: neocortical death (permanent loss of cognitive function) and total brain death (irreversible cessation of all whole-brain functions). The U.S. Uniform Determination of Death Act follows the whole-brain definition, endorsed by the President's Commission in 1980 for uniformity in law, fiscal resource concerns, and organ donation. In 1995, the American Academy of Neurology set criteria requiring coma with clear etiology, cessation of breathing, and lack of brainstem reflexes; these were updated in 2010, but discrepancies remain.

### Causes of death

The leading cause of death in developing countries is infectious disease; in developed countries, it is atherosclerosis, cancer, and diseases related to obesity and aging. The largest unifying cause in the developed world is biological aging, leading to aging-associated diseases that cause loss of homeostasis, cardiac arrest, and irreversible deterioration of tissues. Of roughly 150,000 daily deaths worldwide, about two-thirds are age-related; in industrialized nations, this approaches 90%.

In developing nations, poor sanitation and lack of medical technology make infectious diseases more common. Tuberculosis killed 1.8 million people in 2015; malaria caused about 2.7 million deaths annually in 2004. According to Jean Ziegler, malnutrition accounted for 58% of total mortality in 2006—over 36 million of 62 million deaths. Tobacco smoking killed 100 million people in the 20th century and could kill 1 billion in the 21st. In 2012, suicide overtook car crashes as the leading cause of injury deaths in the U.S. Natural disasters kill around 45,000 people annually. Predation is a common cause of death in animals; 50% of young foxes die to predators, and young bear cubs in Yellowstone have only a 40% chance of surviving to adulthood.

An autopsy (postmortem examination) determines cause and manner of death, performed by a pathologist. Forensic autopsies address potential criminal matters; clinical autopsies find medical causes for unknown deaths or research. A necropsy is an unregulated postmortem examination, now usually associated with animal corpses.

### Misdiagnosis and death before birth

Anecdotal references describe people declared dead who "came back to life." In 1895, physician J.C. Ouseley claimed 2,700 people were buried prematurely annually in England and Wales, though some estimates suggest closer to 800. CPR for an hour or longer can revive people after electric shock, and the mammalian diving reflex allows survival after icy-water immersion. The concept of information-theoretic death has been suggested as a better definition, though it has few practical applications outside cryonics.

Death before birth includes stillbirth (fetus dies before or during delivery), miscarriage (embryo dies before independent survival), and abortion (artificial termination). Stillbirth affects 1% of U.S. births. The World Health Organization defines miscarriage as expulsion of an embryo or fetus weighing 500g or less; it occurs in 12–15% of clinical pregnancies, potentially 17–22% including losses during menstruation. Abortion may be medical (a pill, up to 11 weeks past last period) or in-clinic (suction, possible after 12 weeks).

### Senescence and life extension

Senescence is death due to old age after surviving all calamities; premature death occurs before old age, such as before age 75. In the U.K., nine of ten daily deaths relate to senescence. Some organisms exhibit negligible senescence or biological immortality, including the jellyfish Turritopsis dohrnii, the hydra, and the planarian. Physiological death is now seen as a process, and clinical death is neither necessary nor sufficient for legal death.

Life extension aims to increase maximum or average lifespan by slowing or reversing aging. Aging is the most common cause of death worldwide, yet little is spent on anti-aging research—a phenomenon Aubrey de Grey calls "pro-aging trance." Calorie restriction is a recognized method of extending maximum lifespan. A U.S. poll found 38% would desire their aging process cured. Cryonics is the low-temperature preservation of humans who cannot be sustained by medicine, hoping for future resuscitation; it is not reversible with current technology, and cryobiologists generally regard it with skepticism.

### Psychology, society, and culture

Death studies is a field within psychology. People inherently fear death as part of the survival instinct, which may cause avoidance of financial planning or hospice. Mortality salience—awareness that death is inevitable—can deepen in-group bonds as a defense mechanism. In one study, those reminded of death set an average bond of $455 for a prostitute versus $50 for those who were not.

Religious traditions address death through belief in resurrection or afterlife (Abrahamic religions), reincarnation (Dharmic religions), or eternal oblivion (secular humanism). Commemoration includes mourning, funerals, and practices like interment, cremation, or sky burial in Tibet. Capital punishment remains culturally divisive, with death penalties for crimes ranging from murder to apostasy and drug trafficking. Suicide and euthanasia are understood differently across cultures; in Japan, seppuku was considered desirable, while traditional Christian and Islamic cultures view suicide as a sin. Death is personified as the Grim Reaper, Azrael, Yama, and Father Time.

Around 1930, most Westerners died at home; by the start of the 21st century, only 20–25% in developed countries died outside a medical institution, a shift termed the "Invisible Death." The origin of death is a myth present in nearly all cultures; in Christianity, death results from the fall of man, and in Greek mythology, Pandora's box releases death.

### Religious views on afterlife

Buddhism: Awareness of death motivated Prince Siddhartha to seek enlightenment. Rebirth as a human is the only state for attaining enlightenment. The mental state at death is significant because the final moment of consciousness conditions the first moment of the next existence.

Christianity: Humans are souls uniting body and spirit. After death, the spirit separates from the body and enters the afterlife; at the Second Coming, resurrection reunifies spirit and body.

Hinduism: Death is the eternal soul (jiva-atma) exiting the temporary material body. The soul enters a new body based on karma and the mind's state at death. Liberation from the cycle (moksa) is the ultimate goal; reincarnation causes forgetfulness of previous lives.

Islam: Death is the separation of soul from body and the beginning of the afterlife (akhirah), one of six main beliefs. Earthly life is a short testing period; true life begins at the Day of Judgement, when the righteous enter jannah. Death is predetermined by God, and only God knows its exact time.

Judaism: Various afterlife beliefs exist, but none contradict the preference for life over death, partly because death ends the possibility of fulfilling commandments.

### In biology

Death plays a role in extinction—the death of the last individual of a species. After death, remains enter the biogeochemical cycle; detritivores (earthworms, woodlice, millipedes) recycle organic material, and microorganisms raise temperature while breaking matter into simpler molecules. Coal forms from undecayed material in swamp ecosystems.

Evolutionary theory sees death as part of natural selection: less-adapted organisms die with fewer offspring, reducing their gene pool contribution. An organism that dies young but leaves numerous offspring displays greater fitness than a long-lived one leaving one. Organisms with only asexual reproduction (bacteria, some protists) are "immortal" except for external hazards; multicellular organisms with a division between mortal somatic cells and immortal germ cells make death an essential part of life. Volvox algae are among the simplest organisms exhibiting this division.

### Language

"Death" comes from Old English dēaþ, from Proto-Germanic *dauþuz, from the Proto-Indo-European stem *dheu-, meaning the "process, act, condition of dying." Euphemisms include "passed away," "passed on," "expired," or "gone." Formal terms include "the deceased" or "decedent." A dead body is a "corpse," "cadaver," or "set of remains"; "carrion" and "carcass" usually refer to dead non-human animals; cremation ashes are called "cremains."

## Terms

- **Thanatology**: The study of death.
- **Clinical death**: Cessation of breathing and heartbeat, historically the definition of death but now reversible with CPR.
- **Brain death**: Cessation of electrical activity in the brain, presumed to indicate the end of consciousness; used as a legal definition of death.
- **CDD (Circulatory definition of death)**: Death defined by permanent loss of circulatory and respiratory function.
- **Senescence**: Biological aging leading to death from old-age causes.
- **Biologically immortal**: Organisms (e.g., Turritopsis dohrnii, hydra) that do not die from aging but can die from other causes.
- **Autopsy**: Medical examination of a corpse to determine cause and manner of death, performed by a pathologist.
- **Mortality salience**: Awareness that death is inevitable, which can affect behavior and social bonds.
- **Cryonics**: Low-temperature preservation of humans with the hope of future resuscitation.
- **Information-theoretic death**: A proposed definition of death based on irreversible loss of information, with few practical applications outside cryonics.

## Debates and open questions

The central debate is whether death should be defined by circulatory cessation, whole-brain death, or neocortical death. Critics of brain death note that brain-dead patients can maintain biological functioning, while proponents argue brain death has reliable, reproducible criteria and the brain determines identity. The dead donor rule—requiring official death before organ procurement—remains controversial, with advocates citing donor protection and critics arguing it does not uphold donor interests or promote donation. The variability of brain-death application across hospitals and specialties persists despite 2010 criteria updates. Whether consciousness ends at death or continues in an afterlife remains unresolved across religious and secular traditions.
