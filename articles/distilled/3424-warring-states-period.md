# Warring States period

## Overview
The Warring States period (c. 475–221 BC) was the final phase of the Zhou dynasty, defined by intense interstate warfare, radical political and military reforms, and the rise of powerful, centralized states. It culminated in the conquest of all rival states by Qin, which established China's first imperial dynasty. This era was foundational for Chinese political philosophy, military strategy, and bureaucratic governance, setting the stage for imperial China.

## Timeline
- **c. 475 BC** — Traditional start of the Warring States period, as chosen by historian Sima Qian.
- **453–403 BC** — Partition of the state of Jin by the Han, Wei, and Zhao families, creating the "Three Jins."
- **403 BC** — The Zhou court officially recognizes the Three Jins as marquises.
- **358–338 BC** — Shang Yang's radical Legalist reforms transform Qin into a powerful, centralized state.
- **307 BC** — King Wuling of Zhao establishes China's first native cavalry unit.
- **260 BC** — Battle of Changping; Qin general Bai Qi reportedly buries 400,000 Zhao prisoners.
- **256 BC** — Qin invades and ends the Zhou royal line.
- **230 BC** — Qin begins its final conquests, starting with Han.
- **228 BC** — Qin conquers Zhao.
- **225 BC** — Qin conquers Wei.
- **223 BC** — Qin conquers Chu.
- **221 BC** — Qin conquers Qi, unifying China. King Zheng declares himself Qin Shi Huang, the First Emperor.

## Body

### Formation and Geography
The period emerged from the decline of Zhou authority during the preceding Spring and Autumn period. Hundreds of small polities coalesced into seven major states: Qin, Qi, Chu, Yan, and the "Three Jins" (Wei, Han, Zhao). The partition of Jin (453–403 BC) is a key starting point, as it created three new powerful states and reshaped the political landscape. The Zhou king remained a symbolic figurehead, while rulers of the major states increasingly claimed the Mandate of Heaven to justify their conquests.

### Political and Military Reform
States implemented sweeping reforms to survive and dominate. The most significant were Shang Yang's Legalist reforms in Qin (358–338 BC), which established a centralized bureaucracy, a harsh legal code, standardized measures, and a census for taxation and conscription. These reforms made Qin a formidable military power. Militarily, the period saw a shift from chariot-based warfare to mass infantry armies equipped with iron weapons and crossbows. Armies grew to hundreds of thousands, and complex logistical systems were required to support them. The first native Chinese cavalry was formed in 307 BC.

### Interstate Relations and Unification
Diplomacy was dominated by shifting alliances aimed at countering Qin's growing power. The "Vertical Alliances" (hézòng) were coalitions of eastern states led by figures like Su Qin to encircle Qin. Qin countered with "Horizontal Alliances" (liánhéng), led by Zhang Yi, to break these coalitions and isolate its rivals. Qin systematically conquered the other states between 230 and 221 BC, exploiting their divisions and weaknesses. The final unification was achieved through overwhelming military force and strategic bribery.

### Culture, Economy, and Philosophy
The era of conflict spurred intellectual flourishing known as the Hundred Schools of Thought. Key philosophies included Confucianism (Mencius, Xunzi), Mohism (Mozi, which opposed warfare), Taoism (Laozi, Zhuangzi), and Legalism (Shang Yang, Han Fei), which provided the ideological basis for Qin's state. Economically, iron tools boosted agriculture, markets prospered, and interstate commerce grew. The period also saw advances in arithmetic, literature (e.g., the *Zuo Commentary*), and the compilation of philosophical texts.

## Terms
- **Mandate of Heaven**: The divine right to rule, claimed by Zhou kings and later by rival state rulers to justify conquests.
- **Three Jins**: The states of Han, Wei, and Zhao, formed from the partition of the state of Jin.
- **Legalism**: A philosophy advocating strict laws, harsh punishments, and centralized state power, most fully implemented in Qin.
- **Hundred Schools of Thought**: The diverse philosophical traditions (e.g., Confucianism, Taoism, Legalism) that emerged during this period.
- **Vertical Alliances (hézòng)**: A diplomatic strategy to form a coalition of states against the rising power of Qin.
- **Horizontal Alliances (liánhéng)**: Qin's counter-strategy to break the vertical alliances by forming bilateral agreements with individual states.
- **Iron Age**: The period's widespread adoption of iron for weapons and tools, increasing agricultural and military efficiency.
- **Crossbow**: A key weapon innovation that changed battlefield tactics, allowing massed infantry to counter chariots.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Start Date**: The traditional date of 475 BC (from Sima Qian) is most cited, but scholars debate other possible starting points.
- **Scale of Warfare**: Historical records report enormous army sizes and casualties (e.g., 240,000 killed at Yique). Scholars like Michael Loewe and Edward L. Shaughnessy suggest these numbers may be exaggerated due to inadequate records, payment systems based on enemy kills, and later Han dynasty interests.
- **Philosophical Influence**: While philosophies like Confucianism and Mohism offered pacifistic ideals, rulers were primarily driven by pragmatic survival, raising questions about the direct impact of these schools on state policy during the period itself.
