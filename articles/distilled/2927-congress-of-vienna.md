# Congress of Vienna

## Overview
The Congress of Vienna (1814–1815) was a series of diplomatic meetings that redrew the map of Europe after the Napoleonic Wars. Chaired by Austrian statesman Klemens von Metternich, it aimed to establish a long-term peace by creating a balance of power among the great nations and restoring conservative monarchies. Its settlement shaped European politics for nearly a century, though it is debated whether it successfully prevented major wars or suppressed democratic movements.

## Timeline
- **March 1814** — Treaty of Chaumont reaffirms allied positions against Napoleon.
- **May 1814** — Napoleon's defeat and surrender ends 23 years of war.
- **September 1814** — Congress opens in Vienna.
- **January 1815** — France, Austria, and Britain sign a secret treaty against Russia and Prussia over the Polish-Saxon crisis.
- **March–July 1815** — Napoleon returns to power during the Hundred Days; negotiations continue.
- **9 June 1815** — The Final Act of the Congress is signed.
- **18 June 1815** — Napoleon is defeated at Waterloo, nine days after the treaty signing.

## Body

### Congress Format and Participants
The Congress was not a single formal session but a diplomatic framework for discussion, pioneered by Metternich. It brought together representatives from over 200 states, corporations, and interest groups in Vienna. The core negotiations were dominated by the five great powers: Austria, Britain, Russia, Prussia, and France. Lesser powers like Spain and Sweden had limited influence. The social events, such as balls and banquets, facilitated informal diplomacy and networking.

### Key Negotiations and Tactics
France, represented by the skilled diplomat Talleyrand, initially faced exclusion but maneuvered into the inner circle. The most contentious issue was the Polish-Saxon Crisis: Russia wanted most of Poland, and Prussia wanted all of Saxony. A deadlock was broken when Talleyrand allied France with Austria and Britain, threatening war against Russia and Prussia. This forced a compromise: Russia received a Congress Poland under its Tsar, while Prussia received three-fifths of Saxony.

### The Final Settlement
The Final Act, signed on 9 June 1815, confirmed major territorial changes. France lost its conquests but kept its pre-1792 borders. Russia gained most of the Duchy of Warsaw. Prussia acquired significant territory, including parts of Saxony and the Rhineland. Austria regained control in Italy and Germany. A German Confederation of 39 states replaced the Holy Roman Empire. The United Kingdom of the Netherlands was created as a buffer against France. Britain retained key overseas colonies. The slave trade was condemned, and navigation rights on major rivers were guaranteed.

### Legacy and Criticism
The Congress established the Concert of Europe, a system of diplomatic cooperation that maintained relative peace until 1914. It is praised for creating a stable international order based on consensus. However, it is criticized for ignoring nationalist and liberal aspirations, imposing a conservative order that suppressed democratic movements, and leaving issues like the Polish partition unresolved. Its model influenced later international bodies like the League of Nations.

## Terms
- **Balance of Power**: A state of affairs where no single nation is dominant enough to impose its will on others, promoting stability.
- **Concert of Europe**: An international political system established by the Congress, emphasizing cooperation among great powers to maintain boundaries and peace.
- **German Confederation**: A loose association of 39 German states created in 1815, replacing the Holy Roman Empire, with Austria as president.
- **Congress Poland**: The Kingdom of Poland created from most of the Duchy of Warsaw, placed under Russian rule with a nominal constitution.
- **Hundred Days**: The period from March to July 1815 when Napoleon Bonaparte returned to power in France before his final defeat.
- **Ancien Régime**: The political and social system of Europe before the French Revolution, characterized by absolute monarchies and aristocratic privilege.
- **Final Act**: The comprehensive treaty signed on 9 June 1815 that embodied all the agreements made at the Congress of Vienna.
- **Talleyrand**: Charles-Maurice de Talleyrand, the French foreign minister whose diplomatic skill helped France regain great-power status at the Congress.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians debate whether the Congress was a reactionary settlement that stifled progress or a pragmatic achievement that secured a century of peace. Some argue it successfully managed great-power relations through the Concert of Europe, while others contend it merely postponed conflicts by ignoring rising nationalism and liberalism. Its effectiveness in preventing large-scale war until 1914 is contrasted with its failure to address underlying political tensions.
