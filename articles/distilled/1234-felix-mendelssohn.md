# Felix Mendelssohn

## Overview

Felix Mendelssohn (1809–1847) was a German composer, pianist, organist, and conductor of the early Romantic era whose works span symphonies, concertos, chamber music, and oratorios. His revival of Bach’s *St Matthew Passion* (1829) marked a pivotal moment in 19th-century music history, reawakening interest in Baroque repertoire. Though stylistically conservative compared to contemporaries like Wagner and Liszt, Mendelssohn’s precision, formal mastery, and lyrical clarity made him one of the most popular Romantic composers. His legacy was later tarnished by antisemitism and shifting tastes but has been reassessed since the mid-20th century.

## Timeline

- **1809** — Born in Hamburg to a converted Jewish family; grandson of philosopher Moses Mendelssohn
- **1816** — Baptized into the Reformed Christian church
- **1821–1823** — Writes 13 string symphonies while studying with Carl Zelter
- **1825** — Composes String Octet; begins studies with Zelter
- **1826** — Writes *A Midsummer Night’s Dream* overture
- **1829** — Revives Bach’s *St Matthew Passion* in Berlin
- **1833** — Becomes musical director in Düsseldorf
- **1835** — Takes post with Leipzig Gewandhaus Orchestra
- **1836** — Premieres oratorio *St. Paul*
- **1843** — Founds Leipzig Conservatory
- **1846** — Premieres oratorio *Elijah* in Birmingham
- **1847** — Dies in Leipzig after a series of strokes

## Body

### Early Life and Education

Born into a prominent Jewish intellectual family, Mendelssohn was baptized at age seven and raised in a culturally rich household that hosted figures like the Humboldt brothers and mathematician Dirichlet. His grandfather, Moses Mendelssohn, remained a source of pride despite the family’s conversion. Musical training began early under his mother and later with Ludwig Berger and Carl Zelter, whose emphasis on Bach and Baroque traditions deeply shaped Mendelssohn’s style.

### Rise to Prominence

Mendelssohn’s breakthrough came in 1829 with his performance of Bach’s *St Matthew Passion*, which sparked a broader revival of Bach’s works across Europe. This success established him as a leading figure in German musical life. He traveled extensively, particularly in Britain, where he premiered many major works and cultivated relationships with publishers and performers.

### Leipzig and Institutional Leadership

Appointed to lead the Leipzig Gewandhaus Orchestra in 1835, Mendelssohn transformed the city’s musical culture through innovative programming and historical concerts. In 1843, he founded the Leipzig Conservatory, promoting rigorous training rooted in classical tradition. His leadership emphasized fidelity to composer intent, influencing generations of musicians.

### Musical Style and Output

Mendelssohn’s compositions balanced classical structure with Romantic expressiveness. Works like the *Italian* and *Scottish* Symphonies, the Violin Concerto, and the oratorios *St. Paul* and *Elijah* showcase his technical brilliance and melodic gift. His *Songs Without Words* remain iconic examples of Romantic piano miniatures.

### Personal Life and Relationships

Married to Cécile Jeanrenaud in 1837, Mendelssohn had five children. He maintained close ties with contemporaries such as Ignaz Moscheles and Robert Schumann, though he often clashed with more radical figures like Wagner and Berlioz. His relationship with Jenny Lind, including unfinished opera *Lorelei*, reflects both personal passion and artistic ambition.

### Death and Immediate Legacy

Mendelssohn died suddenly in 1847, likely from strokes exacerbated by stress and grief following his sister Fanny’s death. He was buried in Berlin, mourned internationally. However, his reputation soon faced criticism from those who viewed his music as overly polished or lacking in emotional depth.

### Later Reception and Reassessment

The Nazi regime banned Mendelssohn’s works due to his Jewish heritage, removing monuments and discontinuing scholarships. Yet his popularity endured in Britain and gradually resurged globally. Scholars since the mid-20th century have reevaluated his contributions, recognizing his role in shaping the Romantic concert repertoire and his influence on later composers.

## Terms

- **Concert Overture**: A standalone orchestral piece evoking a literary or dramatic theme, performed outside a staged work.
- **Oratorio**: A large-scale vocal work for soloists, chorus, and orchestra, typically based on religious themes, performed without costumes or staging.
- **Singspiel**: A form of German opera featuring sung arias alternating with spoken dialogue.
- **Fugue**: A contrapuntal composition technique in which a short melody or phrase is introduced by one part and successively imitated by others.
- **Incidental Music**: Short musical pieces inserted between scenes or acts of a play or drama.
- **Reformed Church**: A Protestant denomination emphasizing simplicity and moral discipline, to which Mendelssohn’s family converted.
- **Baroque Revival**: A movement in the 19th century that sought to restore and perform earlier musical styles, especially those of Bach.
- **Romanticism**: An artistic and literary movement characterized by emotional expression, individualism, and imagination, emerging in the late 18th century.
- **Conservatory**: A specialized institution for advanced musical education and performance training.
- **Antisemitism**: Prejudice against Jews, which contributed to the decline of Mendelssohn’s reputation in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Religious Identity**: While Mendelssohn identified as a Christian, modern scholars debate whether he retained deeper connections to his Jewish heritage, especially given his advocacy for his grandfather’s legacy.
- **Musical Conservatism**: Critics argue Mendelssohn’s adherence to classical forms limited his innovation, while others see his synthesis of old and new as a strength.
- **Relationship with Wagner**: Tensions between Mendelssohn and Wagner reflect broader ideological conflicts within 19th-century German music, particularly regarding nationalism and artistic radicalism.
- **Role of Women in His Music**: The misattribution of Fanny Mendelssohn’s works to Felix raises questions about gender dynamics in 19th-century musical publishing and recognition.
- **Nazi Suppression**: The systematic exclusion of Mendelssohn from German cultural life under National Socialism highlights how political ideologies can distort artistic legacies.
