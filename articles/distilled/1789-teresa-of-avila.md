# Teresa of Ávila

## Overview

Teresa of Ávila (1515–1582) was a Spanish Carmelite nun, mystic, and spiritual reformer who became the central figure of a movement of monastic renewal during the Counter-Reformation. She reformed the Carmelite orders of both women and men, co-founding the Discalced Carmelites with John of the Cross. Her autobiographical and instructional writings—especially *The Life of Teresa of Jesus*, *The Interior Castle*, and *The Way of Perfection*—are foundational works of Christian mysticism and meditation practice. In 1970, she became the first woman proclaimed a Doctor of the Church, recognizing her enduring theological legacy.

## Body

### Early life and vocation

Teresa's paternal grandfather, Juan Sánchez de Toledo, was a *marrano* (a Jew forced to convert to Christianity) condemned by the Spanish Inquisition for allegedly returning to Judaism, though he later assumed a Catholic identity. Her father, Alonso Sánchez de Cepeda, was a successful wool merchant and one of Ávila's wealthiest men, having bought a knighthood. Her mother, Beatriz de Ahumada y Cuevas, raised Teresa as a dedicated Christian. At age seven, Teresa ran away with her brother Rodrigo to seek martyrdom against the Moors; an uncle brought them home. When Teresa was fourteen, her mother died, prompting a deeper devotion to the Virgin Mary. She was sent to an Augustinian nuns' school in Ávila.

Initially resisting a religious vocation, Teresa entered the easy-going Carmelite Convent of the Incarnation in 1534, disappointing her pious father. She read contemplative works including Osuna's *Abecedario espiritual* (1527), a guide to "spiritual self-concentration and inner contemplation." Her zeal for mortification caused severe illness, leaving her bedridden for nearly a year; she attributed her recovery to Saint Joseph's intercession. During this illness she progressed through stages of "recollection," "devotions of silence," and "devotions of ecstasy." Reading Augustine's *Confessions* helped her resolve religious scruples, as she found solace that a great saint was once a sinner.

### Mystical experiences and transverberation

Around 1556, friends suggested her experiences might be diabolical, but her confessor, the Jesuit Francis Borgia, reassured her of their divine origin. On St. Peter's Day 1559, she became convinced Jesus Christ appeared to her bodily, though invisibly; these visions lasted almost uninterruptedly for over two years. In the famous transverberation vision, a seraph drove a golden lance repeatedly through her heart, causing ineffable spiritual and bodily pain. This episode inspired Bernini's sculpture *The Ecstasy of St. Teresa* at Santa Maria della Vittoria in Rome, though some consider Bernini's depiction highly eroticized. Teresa was known for raptures involving levitation, which embarrassed her; she asked sisters to hold her down. Modern neurologists and psychiatrists, including Peter Fenwick and Javier Álvarez-Rodríguez, have speculated she may have suffered from temporal lobe epilepsy, based on her detailed written record.

### Monastic reform

Teresa found the Incarnation convent's observance of enclosure lax, with 150 nuns disturbed by constant visitors of high rank. Supported by the Franciscan Peter of Alcantara, she resolved to found a reformed convent. St. Joseph's, established in 1562, initially scandalized Ávila due to its poverty but won approval through powerful patrons. In March 1563, she received papal sanction for absolute poverty and renunciation of property, formulating a constitution reviving stricter monastic rules with new regulations including weekly ceremonial flagellation and discalceation (going barefoot or wearing sandals). For five years she remained in seclusion, mostly praying and writing.

In 1567, Teresa received a patent from Carmelite General Rubeo de Ravenna to establish further houses, requiring extensive travel across Spain. Between 1567 and 1571, reformed convents were founded at Medina del Campo, Malagón, Valladolid, Toledo, Pastrana, Salamanca, and Alba de Tormes. She convinced John of the Cross and Anthony of Jesus to found the first Discalced Carmelite men's monastery at Duruelo in November 1568. Further foundations followed at Segovia (1571), Beas de Segura (1574), Seville (1575), and Caravaca de la Cruz (1576).

### Opposition and final years

In 1576, the Ancient observance Carmelites persecuted Teresa and her reforms; the general chapter at Piacenza forbade further foundations and ordered her into "voluntary" retirement at Toledo. Her letters to King Philip II secured relief: in 1579, inquisition cases were dropped, and Pope Gregory XIII allowed a special provincial for the newer branch. In her last three years, she founded convents at Villanueva de la Jara, Palencia, Soria, Burgos, and Granada. In total, seventeen convents and as many men's monasteries resulted from her reforms over twenty years. She died in 1582 during the Julian-to-Gregorian calendar transition, either before midnight of 4 October or early on 15 October.

### After death: relics and canonization

Buried at Alba de Tormes, her body was found intact nine months later though clothing had rotted. Parts were distributed: a hand sent to Ávila, a little finger kept by Gracián (redeemed from Barbary corsairs with rings and 20 reales), an arm left at Alba de Tormes. The body was moved to Ávila in 1585 but returned in 1586 after the Duke of Alba de Tormes objected, with Pope Sixtus V ordering it remain there on pain of excommunication. Relics are now distributed across Rome, Lisbon, Ronda, Paris, and elsewhere. Canonized in 1622, Teresa was elected copatron saint of Castile in 1626, affirmed by Pope Urban VIII in 1627, though James Matamoros retained the primary patronage of Spain. In 1970, Pope Paul VI proclaimed her the first female Doctor of the Church, along with Catherine of Siena.

### Writings

*The Life of Teresa of Jesus* (written 1562–1565, published posthumously) was composed as a defense of her ecstatic mystical experiences. *The Way of Perfection* (1566), called a "living book," teaches nuns how to progress through prayer and Christian meditation, describing four stages: meditation, quiet, repose of soul, and perfect union with God. *The Interior Castle* (1577, published 1588) uses the image of a soul as a diamond castle with seven mansions, inspired by a vision of a crystal globe containing seven chambers with the King of Glory at the center. Some scholars, including Christia Mercer, claim René Descartes lifted ideas from Teresa's *Interior Castle* for his *Meditations on First Philosophy*. Other works include *Relaciones*, poems, "Meditations on Song of Songs" (1567), and 342 extant letters.

### Mysticism

Teresa's mystical thought centers on the ascent of the soul to God. In her autobiography, she describes four stages using the image of watering a garden: (1) Devotion of the Heart—mental prayer and meditation; (2) Devotion of Peace—human will surrendered to God with quietude; (3) Devotion of Union—absorption-in-God, an ecstatic state; (4) Devotion of Ecstasy—consciousness of the body disappears, sensory faculties cease, sometimes causing levitation. The *Interior Castle* describes seven mansions: the first three involve purgative active prayer and asceticism; the fourth begins supernatural prayer (Prayer of Recollection and Prayer of Quiet); the fifth is the Prayer of Union; the sixth is betrothal of the soul to God; the seventh is spiritual marriage with clarity in prayer. Scholars debate the precise stages: Aumann synthesizes nine grades of prayer, while Thomas Merton expressed impatience with such systematization, noting Teresa was describing personal experiences, not writing systematic theology.

## Terms

- **Discalced Carmelites**: The reformed branch of the Carmelite order founded by Teresa and John of the Cross, practicing stricter rules including going barefoot or wearing sandals.
- **Transverberation**: Teresa's vision of a seraph piercing her heart with a golden lance, causing both intense pain and spiritual sweetness.
- **Mental prayer**: Prayer performed without a fixed formula, involving meditation and reflection on divine things.
- **Infused contemplation**: A supernatural gift centering the mind and will on God, described as a "divinely originated, general, non-conceptual, loving awareness of God."
- **Prayer of Quiet**: A state of extraordinary peace and rest accompanied by delight in contemplating God as present, though distractions may still occur.
- **Doctor of the Church**: A posthumous papal honor recognizing a saint's significant theological contribution; Teresa was the first woman to receive it.
- **Marrano/converso**: A Jew forced to convert to Christianity or emigrate, often suspected of secretly maintaining Jewish practices.
- **Enclosure**: The monastic rule requiring nuns to remain within convent walls to protect spiritual practice and prayer.

## Debates and open questions

- Whether Teresa's birthplace was Ávila or Gotarrendura is uncertain.
- Modern medical speculation suggests she may have suffered from temporal lobe epilepsy, based on her detailed written accounts of symptoms.
- Bernini's *Ecstasy of St. Teresa* is considered by some to be highly eroticized compared to the preceding artistic Teresian tradition.
- Scholars debate the precise classification of Teresa's stages of prayer; Aumann discerns infused contemplation as a separate stage in the fourth mansion, while Poulain and Thouless do not, and Merton criticized the tendency toward systematization.
- The tradition that Teresa owned the Infant Jesus of Prague statue lacks written historical evidence, though the statue's age approximates her lifetime.
- In 2025, netizens questioned the incorrupt state of her body given its apparent condition when publicly exposed.
