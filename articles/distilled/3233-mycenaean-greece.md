# Mycenaean Greece

## Overview
Mycenaean Greece (c. 1750–1050 BC) was the first advanced, literate civilization on the Greek mainland, characterized by powerful palace-states, a warrior elite, and a writing system (Linear B) that records the earliest form of the Greek language. It represents the final, sophisticated phase of the Bronze Age Aegean world before its collapse, and its legacy forms the historical and mythological foundation for Classical Greece.

## Timeline
- **c. 1750–1675 BC** — Early Mycenaean period begins; shaft graves emerge as elite burials.
- **c. 1450 BC** — Mycenaeans dominate Crete; Knossos palace adopts Linear B script.
- **c. 1400–1200 BC** — "Koine" era: palatial states flourish, trade expands, Cyclopean walls built.
- **c. 1250 BC** — First wave of destruction hits mainland centers like Thebes and Mycenae.
- **c. 1190 BC** — Final destruction of Mycenae; end of its major power.
- **c. 1180 BC** — Palace of Pylos destroyed; Linear B archives preserved by fire.
- **c. 1050 BC** — End of Mycenaean culture; transition to the Greek Dark Ages.

## Body

### Origins and Identity
The Mycenaeans were mainland Greeks whose civilization evolved from local Bronze Age populations with influences from Minoan Crete. Their identity is confirmed by Linear B, which records an early Greek language. They were known to contemporaries as *Ahhiyawa* (Hittites) and *Danaya* (Egyptians), and later as *Achaeans* in Homer's epics. Genetic studies show they were similar to Minoans but with additional ancestry from steppe-related populations, supporting theories of Indo-European language introduction.

### Palatial Society and Economy
Mycenaean society was organized into palace-centered states ruled by a king (*wanax*), supported by a bureaucratic elite. The economy was a redistributive palace system, with Linear B tablets detailing control over agriculture, crafts, and trade. Palaces like Pylos and Mycenae managed vast resources, from sheep flocks to textile production. Long-distance trade was essential, importing raw materials (metals, ivory) and exporting processed goods (oil, pottery, textiles) across the Mediterranean.

### Collapse and Legacy
The civilization collapsed around 1200–1050 BC in a series of destructions, leading to the Greek Dark Ages. Theories for the collapse include invasions (Dorian, Sea Peoples), internal conflict, or natural disasters. The Mycenaeans left a lasting legacy: their myths became the Trojan Epic Cycle, their gods formed the core of the Olympian pantheon, and their engineering (fortifications, roads) and artistic traditions influenced later Greek culture.

## Terms
- **Wanax**: The Mycenaean king, the supreme political, military, and religious authority.
- **Linear B**: A syllabic script adapted from Minoan Linear A, used for administrative records in Mycenaean Greek.
- **Megaron**: The central throne room of a Mycenaean palace, featuring a circular hearth.
- **Cyclopean**: A style of fortification using massive, unworked boulders, named by later Greeks for the mythical Cyclopes.
- **Tholos**: A large, circular burial chamber with a vaulted roof, used for elite interments.
- **Shaft Grave**: An early Mycenaean elite burial type consisting of deep pits, often richly furnished.
- **Koine**: The period (c. 1400–1200 BC) of cultural uniformity and expansion across the Mycenaean world.
- **Da-mo**: The common people or community in Mycenaean society, distinct from the palace elite.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origins**: The exact timing and nature of Indo-European (Greek) language arrival in the Aegean remains debated, with genetic and linguistic evidence offering different perspectives.
- **Collapse**: There is no consensus on the primary cause of the Bronze Age collapse; it likely involved a combination of invasions, internal strife, and environmental factors.
- **Political Structure**: Whether a single "Great King" ruled a unified Mycenaean state or if the palaces were independent but allied is unclear from the fragmentary Linear B records.
- **Gender Roles**: The extent of women's social and economic autonomy, particularly outside religious roles, is debated based on limited textual and archaeological evidence.
