# Outer space

## Overview
Outer space is the near-perfect vacuum beyond Earth's atmosphere, extending between celestial bodies and filled with sparse plasma, radiation, magnetic fields, and dust. It has a baseline temperature of 2.7 K set by the cosmic microwave background (CMB) from the Big Bang, roughly 13.8 billion years ago. While intergalactic space occupies most of the universe's volume, matter has gravitationally condensed into stars and galaxies; ordinary (baryonic) matter constitutes only ~4.6% of the total energy density, with the remainder attributed to dark matter and dark energy. Human access is governed by the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, which declares space the "province of all mankind" and forbids national sovereignty claims. The environment poses severe physiological hazards—vacuum, radiation, and microgravity—limiting crewed flight to low Earth orbit (LEO) and the Moon, while robotic craft have reached all Solar System planets and, with Voyager 1, interstellar space.

## Body

### Formation and State
According to Big Bang theory, the universe began ~13.8 billion years ago in an extremely hot, dense state that rapidly expanded. After ~380,000 years, cooling allowed protons and electrons to combine into hydrogen (recombination), decoupling matter and radiation; photons from this epoch now form the CMB at 2.7 K. Gravitational collapse of matter created stars, galaxies, and the deep vacuum of outer space. Satellite measurements (e.g., WMAP) show the observable universe has "flat" spatial geometry—parallel photons remain parallel—implying a non-zero vacuum energy termed dark energy. The average energy density equals ~5.9 protons/m³, but atoms contribute only 4.6% (one proton per four m³). Density varies from high in galaxies to far lower in voids. Dark energy dominates total mass-energy but is diffuse; within the Milky Way its influence is five orders of magnitude weaker than gravity from matter and dark matter.

### Environment
Outer space is the closest natural approximation to a perfect vacuum. Intergalactic space contains a few hydrogen atoms/m³ versus ~10²⁵ molecules/m³ in air, giving photons a mean free path of ~10²³ km (10 billion light-years). Atmospheres lack sharp upper boundaries; density declines until indistinguishable from space. At the Kármán line (100 km), Earth's pressure is ~0.032 Pa vs. 100,000 Pa at sea level; above this, radiation pressure and solar wind dynamic pressure dominate. Gas and radiation are not in thermodynamic equilibrium: CMB is 2.7 K, but gas temperatures vary widely (Boomerang Nebula 1 K; solar corona 1.2–2.6 million K). Magnetic fields permeate space: spiral galaxies show 5–10 μG turbulent fields; active elliptical galaxies produce jets via magneto-hydrodynamics. Cosmic rays (energetic subatomic particles, mostly protons) traverse space unimpeded by atmospheres, with energies up to 10²⁰ eV; they damage electronics and threaten astronaut health. Astronauts returning from EVA report a burned, metallic odor from atomic oxygen clinging to suits.

### Human Access

#### Effect on Biology and Human Bodies
Some extremophiles survive space exposure: lichen (10 days, 2007), *Arabidopsis* and tobacco seeds (1.5 years), *Bacillus subtilis* (559 days). The lithopanspermia hypothesis posits that rocks ejected from life-bearing planets could transfer viable organisms between worlds (e.g., early Venus, Earth, Mars), potentially enabling galactic-scale panspermia given bacterial longevity.

**Vacuum:** At the Armstrong line (~19.14 km), ambient pressure equals water vapor pressure at body temperature; exposed fluids (saliva, tears, lung lining) boil. Rapid decompression causes pulmonary barotrauma (lung rupture), eardrum/sinus rupture, ebullism (blood boiling below 6.3 kPa), and hypoxia within minutes. Pressure suits (e.g., CAPS, effective to 2 kPa) and supplemental oxygen (≥8 km) mitigate this. Standard suits use 30–39 kPa pure oxygen to prevent ebullism, though decompression sickness from nitrogen bubbles remains a risk.

**Weightlessness and Radiation:** >50% of astronauts experience space motion sickness (1–3 days). Long-term microgravity causes muscle atrophy, bone loss (spaceflight osteopenia), fluid redistribution, cardiovascular deconditioning, reduced red blood cells, immune weakening, and balance disorders; exercise countermeasures help. Cosmic rays cause acute fatigue, nausea, immune damage, and long-term elevated cancer risk plus damage to eyes, nervous system, lungs, and GI tract. On a 3-year Mars mission, a large fraction of an astronaut's cells would be traversed by high-energy nuclei. Spacecraft walls, water, and other shielding reduce but do not eliminate dose; shielding interactions produce secondary radiation. Countermeasures remain under study.

#### Transition Region
No sharp physical boundary separates atmosphere and space; pressure declines steadily until mixing with solar wind. Proposed boundaries range from 30 km to 1.6 million km. A 2009 sounding rocket found the midpoint of charged-particle transition at 118 km. "Near space" typically spans 20–100 km (stratosphere, mesosphere, lower thermosphere); commercial aircraft max out at ~12 km. The Kármán line (100 km), set by the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale and used by the UN, marks where aerodynamic lift becomes insufficient for flight. Spacecraft have survived multiple orbits with perigees as low as 80–90 km; at 120 km, atmospheric drag becomes noticeable, initiating entry for vehicles like the Space Shuttle.

#### Legal Status
No internationally recognized legal altitude limit for national airspace exists, though the Kármán line is most cited. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty (105 parties as of 2017) provides the core framework: space is free for exploration by all states, not subject to sovereignty, and is the "province of all mankind." It bans nuclear weapons in space but not conventional weapons; anti-satellite weapons have been tested by the USA, USSR, China, and India (2019). The 1979 Moon Treaty placed celestial bodies under international jurisdiction but lacks ratification by spacefaring nations. The 1976 Bogotá Declaration by eight equatorial states claimed geostationary orbital segments; these claims are not internationally accepted. Space debris poses growing legal/regulatory challenges. Since 2020, the Artemis Accords (US-led) seek to align lunar exploration governance.

### Earth Orbit
Orbit requires countering gravity and achieving orbital speed (~7.8 km/s for LEO vs. 2.2 km/s max for piloted aircraft). Escape velocity is 11.2 km/s. Reaching 600 km altitude requires ~36 MJ/kg, six times the energy to merely climb that high. Orbit classifications: Very Low Earth Orbit (VLEO) <450 km (suited for small-sat Earth observation); LEO 180–2,000 km (scientific satellites); Medium Earth Orbit 2,000–35,780 km (navigation); High Earth Orbit >35,780 km (weather/communications). Below ~2,000 km, atmospheric drag decays orbits; decay rate depends on satellite area/mass and space-weather-driven density variations. Above 800 km, lifetimes are centuries; below 300 km, days; at 180 km, hours. The Van Allen radiation belts (equatorial toroids bent toward poles) trap solar/galactic radiation; the inner belt peaks at ~3,000 km, overlapping upper LEO. The South Atlantic Anomaly brings particles closest to Earth. Radiation threatens astronauts and electronics and varies with space weather.

### Regions

#### Regions Near Earth
The exosphere extends from the thermopause (250–500 km) outward; molecular collisions become negligible, merging with interplanetary space. **Near-Earth space** spans LEO to geostationary orbit, hosting most satellites and space debris ("space pollution"). **Geospace** includes Earth's upper atmosphere and magnetosphere, bounded by the magnetopause (sunward ~10 Earth radii; nightside magnetotail 100–200 radii) and the ionosphere. The Moon spends ~4 days/month in the magnetotail, shielded from solar wind. Geospace plasmas, controlled by Earth's magnetic field, drive currents into the upper atmosphere during geomagnetic storms, damaging satellites, disrupting radio/GPS, and creating aurorae. **XGEO** (US concept) denotes multiples of geosynchronous altitude (~35,786 km); Earth-Moon L2 is ~10.67 XGEO. **Cislunar space** includes lunar orbits and Earth-Moon Lagrange points; Earth's Hill sphere extends ~1.5 million km (1% of Earth-Sun distance). **Deep space** definitions vary: US government uses "beyond typical LEO" (includes Moon); ITU uses ≥2 million km (~5× lunar distance).

#### Interplanetary Space
The Sun's magnetic field creates a bubble (heliosphere) extending to the heliopause at 110–160 AU (beyond Neptune). The solar wind (5–10 protons/cm³, 350–400 km/s) fills this region, deflecting low-energy galactic cosmic rays. The interplanetary medium includes cosmic rays, gas, plasma, dust, meteors, and organic molecules; dust produces the zodiacal light. Planetary magnetospheres (Jupiter, Saturn, Mercury, Earth) are shaped into teardrops by the solar wind, trapping particles into radiation belts. Planets without magnetic fields (e.g., Mars) suffer atmospheric erosion by the solar wind.

#### Interstellar Space
Interstellar space lies outside stellar astrospheres (e.g., heliosphere). The boundary is the astropause (heliopause for the Sun). The interstellar medium (ISM) is ~70% hydrogen, ~28% helium by mass, enriched with heavier elements from stellar nucleosynthesis (stellar winds, planetary nebulae, supernovae). Average density ~10⁶ particles/m³; cold molecular clouds reach 10⁸–10¹²/m³. Over 200 molecules detected (radio astronomy adds ~4/year); dust grains ~0.1 μm form in dense clouds where collisions and cosmic-ray ionization drive organic chemistry (e.g., trihydrogen cation). The **Local Interstellar Medium** (within 100 pc) coincides with the **Local Bubble**, a cavity in the Orion Arm lacking dense cold clouds, bounded by molecular clouds in Ophiuchus/Taurus. It contains ~10⁴–10⁵ stars and warm clouds (up to 7,000 K, 0.5–5 pc radii). IBEX and Voyager data (2012) showed the Sun lacks a bow shock; a subsonic bow wave mediates the solar wind/ISM transition.

#### Intergalactic Space
Space between galaxies has a foam-like structure: filaments (10% of volume) contain galaxy groups/clusters; voids (7–30 Mpc across) are largely empty. The **intergalactic medium (IGM)** is a rarefied plasma in filamentary structure, ~76% hydrogen by mass, enriched by galactic outflows. Filament density ~1 atom/m³ (5–200× cosmic mean). Gas falling from voids heats to 10⁵–10⁷ K, forming the **warm–hot intergalactic medium (WHIM)**, potentially holding up to half the universe's atomic matter. Gas falling into cluster intersections heats further to >10⁸ K, creating the **intracluster medium (ICM)**.

### History

#### Discovery
Ancient Greek philosophy (Parmenides, Aristotle) held *horror vacui*—nature abhors a vacuum—dominating Western thought until the 17th century. In 2nd-century China, Zhang Heng and the Hsüan Yeh school described infinite, empty heavens. Galileo showed air has weight; Torricelli (1643) created the first partial vacuum/barometer. Pascal and Périer (1648) confirmed pressure drops with altitude. Guericke (1650) built the first vacuum pump, concluding a vacuum exists between Earth and Moon. Cusanus (15th c.) and Bruno (16th c.) speculated on an unbounded, centerless universe filled with aether. The luminiferous aether concept persisted until the Michelson–Morley null result (1887), leading to Einstein's special relativity (light speed constant in vacuum). Bessel (1838) measured the first stellar parallax (61 Cygni, ~10 ly). Hubble (1923) used Leavitt's Cepheid method to prove Andromeda is external, establishing the Hubble constant and an expanding universe. Lemaître (1931) proposed the Big Bang. Early temperature estimates: Guillaume 5–6 K (1896), Eddington 3.18 K (1926), Regener 2.8 K (1933), Alpher & Herman 5 K (1948, from Big Bang theory).

#### Exploration
Pre-rocket exploration used naked eye, then telescopes. Balloons reached 22 km (Explorer II, 1935). German A-4 rocket reached ~80 km (1942). Sputnik 1 (1957) achieved orbit. Gagarin (Vostok 1, 1961) first human in orbit. Apollo 8 (1968) first humans beyond LEO to lunar orbit. Luna 1 (1959) first escape velocity/flyby of Moon. Venera 1 (1961) first planetary probe (detected solar wind). Mariner 2 (1962) first successful planetary flyby (Venus); Mariner 4 (1964) first Mars flyby. All Solar System planets since examined by robots. Voyager 1 entered interstellar space (2012).

### Application
Artificial satellites dominate the space economy: communications, precise navigation (GNSS), weather monitoring, Earth observation (agriculture, hydrology, disease detection, military surveillance, climate tracking). Low drag enables stable global coverage. The vacuum is ideal for astronomy across all wavelengths; Hubble observes light from >13 billion years ago. Infrared telescopes benefit from moving past zodiacal dust; lunar far-side (e.g., Daedalus crater) shields radio telescopes from Earth interference. Deep vacuum could enable ultraclean industrial processes and asteroid mining, but launch costs remain high: $10,000–32,000/kg (2006); Falcon 9 reduced to <$3,500/kg. Concepts for further reduction: fully reusable launchers, non-rocket launch, tethers, space elevators. Interstellar crewed travel remains theoretical; Daedalus fusion concept would take 36 years to Alpha Centauri; alternatives include light sails, ramjets, beam propulsion, antimatter. On Earth, outer space's ultracold temperature enables passive daytime radiative cooling via the atmospheric infrared window, enhanced by photonic metamaterials.

### In Society
Outer space has long been an imagined realm; the space age added material reality. Cultural astronomy (archaeoastronomy, ethnoastronomy, history of astronomy/astrology) studies cultural perceptions. Astrosociology and space archaeology study human presence in space. Spaceflight narratives intertwine with utopianism and colonialism (e.g., Wilkins 1638 likening Moon travelers to Drake/Columbus), shaping ideas of colonization and extraterrestrial life as "otherworldly." Earth observation triggered the "overview effect" and inspired the environmental movement (e.g., Blue Marble image).

## Terms
- ****Kármán line**** — Conventional boundary of outer space at 100 km altitude, where aerodynamic lift becomes insufficient for flight; used in treaties and records.
- ****Recombination epoch**** — ~380,000 years post-Big Bang when protons and electrons combined into neutral hydrogen, decoupling matter and radiation (source of CMB).
- ****Cosmic microwave background (CMB)**** — Thermal radiation filling the universe, remnant of the Big Bang, with a blackbody temperature of 2.7 K.
- ****Dark energy**** — Non-zero vacuum energy inferred from flat geometry and accelerating expansion; dominates cosmic mass-energy but is diffuse.
- ****Armstrong line**** — Altitude (~19.14 km) where ambient pressure equals water vapor pressure at body temperature; exposed bodily fluids boil.
- ****Ebullism**** — Formation of gas bubbles in bodily fluids due to ambient pressure dropping below 6.3 kPa (1 psi); causes swelling and circulation issues.
- ****Van Allen radiation belts**** — Toroidal regions around Earth trapping solar and galactic charged particles; inner belt peaks at ~3,000 km, overlapping upper LEO.
- ****Heliopause**** — Boundary where the solar wind meets the interstellar medium, at 110–160 AU; marks edge of the heliosphere.
- ****Warm–hot intergalactic medium (WHIM)**** — Rarefied plasma at 10⁵–10⁷ K in cosmic filaments, potentially containing up to half the universe's atomic matter.
- ****Lithopanspermia**** — Hypothesis that life can be transferred between planets via rocks ejected by impacts, surviving in space for extended periods.

## Debates and open questions
- **Boundary definition:** No internationally agreed legal altitude separates airspace from outer space; proposals range from 30 km to 1.6 million km, complicating law for suborbital flight.
- **Dark matter/energy nature:** Together constituting ~95% of cosmic mass-energy, both remain undetected directly; their physical identity is unknown.
- **Radiation shielding:** Optimal countermeasures for crewed deep-space missions (e.g., Mars) are unresolved; spacecraft shielding produces secondary radiation.
- **Space weaponization:** Outer Space Treaty bans nuclear weapons but not conventional weapons; anti-satellite tests by multiple nations raise arms-race concerns.
- **Space debris:** Growing orbital debris threatens satellites and crewed missions; international mitigation regulations are incomplete.
- **Lunar/resource governance:** Artemis Accords (US-led) vs. Moon Treaty (unratified) reflect competing frameworks for property rights and resource use on celestial bodies.
- **Bow shock existence:** 2012 data showed the Sun lacks a bow shock, contradicting decades of models; the heliosphere/ISM interaction structure is still refined.
- **Interstellar travel feasibility:** Energy, propulsion, and life-support requirements for crewed missions to even the nearest stars remain theoretical.
