# Clovis culture

## Overview
The Clovis culture is a Paleoindian archaeological culture of North America, defined by distinctive fluted stone points and dated to approximately 13,050–12,750 years Before Present (BP). It represents the earliest widely recognized cultural complex in the continent, known for its association with big-game hunting, particularly of mammoths. Its discovery and study have been central to understanding the initial peopling of the Americas, though the long-held "Clovis First" model has been replaced by evidence of earlier human presence.

## Timeline
- **c. 13,050–12,750** — Main temporal range of the Clovis culture.
- **1927** — Discovery of the Folsom site, confirming a Pleistocene human presence in the Americas.
- **1929** — Ridgely Whiteman discovers the Clovis type site at Blackwater Draw, New Mexico.
- **1932–1937** — Major excavations at Blackwater Draw by Edgar Howard and later John L. Cotter.
- **c. 12,990–12,840** — Date of the Anzick-1 burial, the only known Clovis-associated human interment.
- **c. 12,750–12,600** — End of the Clovis culture; succeeded by regional traditions like Folsom.
- **2000s–present** — Discovery and dating of pre-Clovis sites (e.g., Monte Verde II, Cooper's Ferry) refutes the "Clovis First" model.

## Body

### Discovery and Definition
The Clovis culture was first identified at the Blackwater Draw site near Clovis, New Mexico, where stone tools were found with mammoth remains in 1929. The culture is defined by its toolkit, most notably the Clovis point—a large, bifacial, fluted projectile point. The definition of "Clovis" is sometimes ambiguous, referring to a specific point type, a broader tool complex, or a cultural period. A distinctive manufacturing technique is overshot flaking, where flakes travel across the face of a biface.

### Toolkit and Material Culture
The Clovis toolkit includes Clovis points, blades, bifaces, and bone/ivory tools like beveled rods and shaft wrenches. Clovis points were multifunctional, serving as spear tips and cutting tools. They were made from siliceous stone, thinned by end-thinning, and finished with pressure flaking. Blades were struck from prepared cores and modified into scrapers and gravers. Over 20 caches of Clovis artifacts have been found, where tools were stored for later retrieval.

### Lifestyle and Subsistence
Clovis peoples were highly mobile hunter-gatherers with a diet heavily reliant on megafauna, especially Columbian mammoths, as well as mastodons, bison, and horses. They also consumed smaller animals and plants. Isotope analysis of the Anzick-1 child suggests mammoths comprised ~35–40% of the diet. Sites indicate both large kill sites (e.g., a bison herd trapped in an arroyo at Jake Bluff) and seasonal camps. They transported stone raw materials over long distances, sometimes over 900 km.

### Chronology and Origins
The Clovis culture flourished from c. 13,050 to 12,750 BP. The "Clovis First" model, which posited Clovis as the earliest American culture, is now rejected. Multiple pre-Clovis sites (e.g., Monte Verde II at c. 14,500 BP, Cooper's Ferry at c. 15,800 BP) demonstrate earlier human presence. The origin of Clovis is debated; it may have developed south of the Laurentide Ice Sheet, possibly from the Dyuktai lithic style, and was contemporaneous in western North America with the Western Stemmed Tradition.

### Genetics and Extinction
The only known Clovis burial is Anzick-1, an infant from Montana. Genetic analysis shows he is ancestral to many modern Indigenous peoples of the Americas, particularly in Central and South America, and confirms an Asian origin. The end of the Clovis culture is attributed to normal cultural change, possibly linked to megafauna decline and decreasing mobility. It was succeeded by regional traditions like Folsom. The role of Clovis hunting in Late Pleistocene megafauna extinctions is a major controversy; a 2012 survey indicated most archaeologists favor a combination of factors, including climate change.

## Terms
- **Clovis point**: A distinctive, large, fluted, lanceolate projectile point that is the hallmark of the Clovis culture.
- **Fluting**: The removal of an elongate flake from the base of a projectile point, a key feature of Clovis and some later Paleoindian points.
- **Overshot flaking**: A knapping technique where flakes travel from one edge across the face of a biface, considered a diagnostic Clovis trait.
- **Megafauna**: Large animals, such as mammoths and mastodons, that were a primary prey for Clovis hunters.
- **Paleoindian**: The earliest archaeological period in the Americas, characterized by mobile hunter-gatherers and extinct megafauna.
- **Biface**: A stone tool flaked on both sides, such as a Clovis point or preform.
- **Cache**: A deliberate deposit of artifacts, like stone tools, left for later retrieval.
- **Anzick-1**: The name given to the remains of an infant buried with Clovis artifacts in Montana, providing key genetic data.
- **Younger Dryas**: A period of abrupt climate cooling that began around 12,900 years BP, overlapping with the end of the Clovis culture.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause of megafauna extinction**: Was it primarily driven by human overkill (the "overkill hypothesis"), climate change, or a combination of factors?
- **Clovis origins**: Did the culture develop in situ south of the ice sheet, or did it derive from earlier traditions like the Dyuktai culture in Beringia?
- **Migration route**: Did the ancestors of Clovis peoples enter the Americas via the ice-free corridor or along the Pacific coast?
- **Function of fluting**: Was the flute on Clovis points primarily for hafting, durability, or stylistic reasons?
- **Nature of Clovis diet**: To what extent were Clovis peoples specialized megafauna hunters versus generalist foragers?
