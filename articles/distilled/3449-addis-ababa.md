# Addis Ababa

## Overview
Addis Ababa is the capital and largest city of Ethiopia, serving as a major political, cultural, and economic hub for the country and the African continent. Founded in the late 19th century, it is the highest capital city in Africa and hosts the headquarters of the African Union and the United Nations Economic Commission for Africa. Its history reflects Ethiopia's broader political and social transformations, from imperial rule to modern federalism.

## Timeline
- **c. 100,000 years ago** — Genetic evidence suggests early humans may have migrated out of Africa near the area.
- **Early–mid 15th century** — The fortified medieval city of Barara is established on Mount Entoto.
- **1529** — Barara is razed by Ahmad ibn Ibrahim.
- **1886** — Settlement begins at Finfinne (hot springs) under Empress Taytu Betul; Emperor Menelik II moves the capital from Mount Entoto.
- **1887** — Menelik II forms his imperial palace.
- **1889** — Addis Ababa becomes the empire's capital.
- **1936–1941** — Italian occupation; urbanization continues under a colonial master plan.
- **1941** — City liberated; Emperor Haile Selassie returns.
- **1963** — Organisation of African Unity (later African Union) founded with headquarters in Addis Ababa.
- **1974** — Emperor Haile Selassie is deposed by the Derg military junta.
- **1991** — Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF) seizes the city.
- **2015** — Addis Ababa's light rail system, the first in sub-Saharan Africa, opens.

## Body

### Foundation and Early Growth
The area was inhabited by the Gafat people before being absorbed by Oromo groups. In 1886, Empress Taytu Betul chose the site of Finfinne for its hot springs, persuading Emperor Menelik II to relocate the capital from the cold Mount Entoto. The city was founded as a *katama* (royal camp), with land (*sefer*) allocated to nobility in a layout mirroring a military camp. It grew rapidly, attracting diverse ethnic groups, artisans, merchants, and foreign visitors. Early urbanization was unplanned, with neighborhoods (*sefer*) settled by each noble's retainers.

### 20th Century: Boom, Occupation, and Modernization
A major economic boom occurred in 1926–1927, fueled by coffee profits, leading to new stone houses, imported automobiles, and expanded banking. Following the Italian invasion in 1936, the city was spared bombardment and served as the capital of Italian East Africa. The Italians introduced a master plan focused on monumental architecture and fascist ideology. After liberation in 1941, Emperor Haile Selassie pursued modernization, installing power lines and telephones. Subsequent master plans were designed by British and French consultants (e.g., Sir Patrick Abercrombie in 1946) to model the city as a capital for Africa, emphasizing neighborhood units and ring roads. The city became the headquarters of the Organisation of African Unity (1963) and the UN Economic Commission for Africa (1958).

### Political Transitions and Recent Developments
In 1974, the Derg military junta overthrew Haile Selassie, nationalized rental housing, and implemented a new master plan in 1986. The EPRDF coalition took power in 1991. A 1995 constitution established Addis Ababa as a federally chartered city with self-governing mandates. A controversial 2014 master plan to expand city boundaries into Oromia sparked major protests, leading to its cancellation in 2016. Recent projects include the "Beautifying Sheger" initiative and the "Sheger City" urban development model, which have faced criticism for mass demolitions and regional disparity.

### Geography and Demographics
Located in the Ethiopian Highlands at an elevation of 2,355 metres (7,726 ft), the city has a subtropical highland climate. The 2007 census recorded a population of 2,739,551. Major ethnic groups include the Amhara (47.05%), Oromo (19.51%), and Gurage (16.34%). Amharic is the primary language (70.99%), and Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity is the predominant religion (43%). The city is divided into 10 sub-cities and 99 wards (*kebele*).

### Economy and Infrastructure
Addis Ababa's economy is diverse, with significant activity in trade, manufacturing, and civil administration. It is a major transport hub, served by Bole International Airport and a light rail system opened in 2015. The city hosts numerous international organizations and is experiencing a construction boom. Tourism is a growing industry, though impacted by recent conflicts.

## Terms
- **Katama**: A royal camp or temporary settlement, the initial form of Addis Ababa's founding.
- **Sefer**: A neighborhood or land plot allocated to nobility and their retainers in early Addis Ababa.
- **Kebele**: The smallest administrative unit in Ethiopia; a ward or neighborhood.
- **Derg**: The Marxist-Leninist military junta that ruled Ethiopia from 1974 to 1991.
- **EPRDF**: The Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front, the coalition that ruled from 1991 to 2019.
- **Sub-city (kifle ketema)**: A borough-level administrative division of Addis Ababa.
- **FDRE**: Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia, the official name of the country since 1995.
- **OAU**: Organisation of African Unity, the predecessor to the African Union (AU).

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact location of the medieval city of Barara is debated, though recent archaeological findings on Mount Entoto support its proposed site.
- The relationship between Addis Ababa and the surrounding Oromia Region is politically sensitive, involving historical land dispossession and constitutional provisions for Oromia's "special interest" in the city.
- The impact and equity of recent urban development projects like the 2014 master plan and Sheger City remain contested, with criticisms regarding displacement and ethnic disparity.
