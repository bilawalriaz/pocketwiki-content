# Vertebrate

## Overview
Vertebrates (subphylum Vertebrata) are chordate animals defined by a vertebral column (spine) protecting the spinal cord and a cranium (skull) protecting the brain. They comprise roughly 65,000 described species—including mammals, birds, amphibians, reptiles, and fish—making them the largest subphylum in Chordata, yet less than 5% of all described animal species. The group originated during the Cambrian explosion (~518 million years ago), evolving from jawless forms to jawed fishes, then to lobe-finned fishes whose muscular paired fins became the limbs of tetrapods (four-limbed vertebrates). Since 1970, monitored vertebrate populations have declined by an average of 60% due to habitat loss, overexploitation, climate change, pollution, and invasive species.

## Timeline
- **~518 mya** — First vertebrates (e.g., *Haikouichthys*, *Myllokunmingia*) appear in the Cambrian explosion (Chengjiang biota); jawless, with notochord and rudimentary vertebrae.
- **Late Ordovician / Silurian (~445–420 mya)** — First jawed vertebrates (Gnathostomata) appear; bony fishes (Osteichthyes) originate in the Silurian.
- **Devonian (419–359 mya)** — "Age of Fishes": bony fishes diversify; lobe-finned fishes (Sarcopterygii) evolve lungs and muscular paired fins.
- **Late Devonian (Famennian, ~372–359 mya)** — First tetrapods evolve from lobe-finned fishes; fins become walking legs.
- **Carboniferous (359–299 mya)** — Tetrapods establish on land as amphibians; amniotes (embryo with protective membranes) branch from amphibious tetrapods.
- **Triassic (252–201 mya)** — Mammals and dinosaurs appear after the end-Permian mass extinction.
- **Jurassic (201–145 mya)** — Dinosaurs give rise to birds.
- **End-Cretaceous (66 mya)** — Mass extinction extirpates non-avian dinosaurs and many other groups.
- **Cenozoic (66 mya–present)** — "Age of Mammals": placental mammals dominate Northern Hemisphere; marsupials dominate Southern Hemisphere.
- **1970–2014** — Living Planet Index records 60% average decline in monitored vertebrate populations; freshwater species decline 83%.

## Body

### Defining Characteristics
Vertebrates share the five chordate synapomorphies: a notochord, a hollow dorsal nerve cord, a post-anal tail, an endostyle (homologous to the thyroid gland), and paired pharyngeal gill slits. They are distinguished from other chordates (cephalochordates and tunicates) by a vertebral column and skull of bone or cartilage; a brain divided into three or more regions; a muscular heart with two or more chambers; an inner ear with semicircular canals; camera-type eyes; a nose; kidneys with glomeruli; a thyroid; and a liver. Molecularly, five conserved signature indels in proteins (including elongation factor-2 and adenosine kinase) are exclusive to vertebrates. Two additional markers (Rrp44 and serine C-palmitoyltransferase) support a clade (Olfactores) uniting vertebrates with tunicates, excluding cephalochordates.

### Embryology and Body Plan
As bilaterians, vertebrates exhibit mirror symmetry. Embryos possess a notochord, which in all groups except jawless fishes is replaced during development by a vertebral column of bone or cartilage. Pharyngeal arches support gills in adult fish but develop into other structures (e.g., jaw, ear bones) in tetrapods. The neural tube forms from a folding dorsal cell layer, becoming the spinal cord and brain. Forward locomotion drove cephalisation—the concentration of sense organs and brain at the anterior. The gut runs mouth to anus; the vertebral column typically extends past the anus as a tail. Ancestrally aquatic, vertebrates respire via gills supported by branchial arches; in jawed vertebrates, the first arch pair evolved into jaws. Tetrapods lost gills, converting the swim bladder (a buoyancy organ in fish) into lungs, and transformed the bony fins of lobe-finned fishes into two pairs of weight-bearing limbs anchored by shoulder and pelvic girdles. Size ranges from the frog *Brachycephalus pulex* (6.45 mm snout–vent length) to the blue whale (33 m, 150 tonnes).

### Evolutionary History
**Cambrian Explosion:** The earliest vertebrates (~518 mya) from the Chengjiang biota (*Haikouichthys*, *Myllokunmingia*, *Zhongjianichthys*, possibly *Yunnanozoon*) had a notochord, rudimentary vertebrae, and a defined head/tail but lacked jaws. They may represent basal stem-group craniates rather than crown-group vertebrates. Conodonts (eel-like, known from tooth microfossils, late Cambrian–Triassic) show mineralized hard parts evolved in teeth before a full bony skeleton.

**Paleozoic:** Jawed vertebrates appear in the late Ordovician or Silurian, dominating the Devonian "Age of Fishes." Bony fishes arise in the Silurian; by the Middle Devonian, lobe-finned fishes (Sarcopterygii) with lungs and muscular fins inhabit swampy pools. In the Late Devonian (Famennian), their fins evolve into the two pairs of legs of the first tetrapods. These establish on land as amphibians in the Carboniferous, where amniotes (with membranes allowing embryonic development on dry land) branch off.

**Mesozoic:** The end-Permian extinction devastates large vertebrates. Recovery yields modern lissamphibians, turtles, crocodilians, lizards, mammals, and dinosaurs (which produce birds in the Jurassic). Marine reptiles and new fish groups diversify in the seas. The end-Cretaceous extinction eliminates non-avian dinosaurs.

**Cenozoic:** The "Age of Mammals" sees placental mammals dominate northern continents and marsupials southern continents.

### Classification and Phylogeny
**Taxonomic History:** Lamarck (1801) erected Vertebrata as a phylum (four classes: fish, reptiles, birds, mammals). Haeckel (1866) and Lankester (1877) expanded it to include cephalochordates and tunicates. Balfour (1880–81) restricted Vertebrata to a subphylum within Chordata; Irie et al. (2018) proposed restoring it to phylum rank. The jawless/jawed split (Agnatha/Gnathostomata) was formalized by Cope (1889). The status of hagfish and lampreys (Cyclostomi) was long debated: morphology suggested hagfish were outside Vertebrata (making Craniata the valid clade), but mtDNA (Delarbre et al., 2002) and later morphological analyses (Miyashita et al., 2019) support Cyclostomata monophyly, placing both within Vertebrata.

**Traditional vs. Phylogenetic Taxonomy:** Traditional classification uses seven extant classes (Agnatha, Chondrichthyes, Osteichthyes, Amphibia, Reptilia, Aves, Mammalia), but most are paraphyletic (e.g., "Reptilia" excludes birds; "Osteichthyes" excludes tetrapods). Modern phylogenetic classification (e.g., Benton 2004) organizes by clades: Infraphylum Agnatha (including Cyclostomata: Myxini/hagfish, Petromyzontida/lampreys, plus extinct ostracoderms) and Infraphylum Gnathostomata (Chondrichthyes, Actinopterygii/ray-finned fishes, Sarcopterygii/lobe-finned fishes including Tetrapoda). Tetrapoda splits into Amphibia (Lissamphibia), Synapsida (mammals + extinct relatives), and Sauropsida (reptiles + birds). Fossil groups like *Nuucichthys* and *Palaeospondylus* remain *incertae sedis*.

**External Phylogeny:** Since 2006, molecular data place Tunicata + Vertebrata as sister clade Olfactores, with Cephalochordata as the outgroup.

**Internal Phylogeny:** Crown Vertebrata includes Cyclostomata (hagfish + lampreys) and Gnathostomata. Stem vertebrates (e.g., Myllokunmingiida) possess a cranium but at most a rudimentary vertebral column, placing them outside the crown group but within the total group Vertebrata (equivalent to Craniata).

### Diversity and Conservation
Described extant species are split roughly evenly between non-tetrapod "fish" (paraphyletic) and tetrapods. The IUCN Red List (2014.3) estimates ~65,000 vertebrate species versus ~1.3 million invertebrates (<5% of described animals). The Living Planet Index (16,704 populations, 4,005 species) shows a 60% average population decline (1970–2014), with freshwater species down 83% and tropical South/Central American populations down 89%. The five drivers are land-use change, overexploitation, climate change, pollution, and invasive species. While humans and livestock have exploded in biomass, wild vertebrate populations and biomass are shrinking, potentially heralding a sixth mass extinction.

## Terms
- ****Chordata**** — Phylum defined by five synapomorphies: notochord, dorsal hollow nerve cord, post-anal tail, endostyle, and paired pharyngeal gill slits.
- ****Vertebrata**** — Subphylum (or phylum) of chordates distinguished by a vertebral column (spine) and cranium (skull) of bone or cartilage.
- ****Craniata**** — Clade comprising all animals with a cranium; currently considered a synonym of Vertebrata because hagfish (which lack true vertebrae) are nested within the group.
- ****Gnathostomata**** — "Jawed vertebrates"; clade defined by the evolution of jaws from the first pharyngeal arch pair.
- ****Cyclostomata**** — Clade of living jawless vertebrates: hagfish (Myxini) and lampreys (Petromyzontida); supported by molecular and recent morphological data.
- ****Sarcopterygii**** — Lobe-finned fishes; bony fishes with muscular, jointed paired fins homologous to tetrapod limbs; includes coelacanths, lungfishes, and tetrapods.
- ****Tetrapoda**** — Superclass of four-limbed vertebrates descended from lobe-finned fishes; includes amphibians, mammals (Synapsida), and reptiles/birds (Sauropsida).
- ****Amniota**** — Clade of tetrapods whose embryos develop within protective membranes (amnion, chorion, allantois), enabling reproduction on dry land; includes Synapsida and Sauropsida.
- ****Olfactores**** — Clade uniting Vertebrata and Tunicata (urochordates), supported by shared molecular markers (Rrp44, serine C-palmitoyltransferase); sister to Cephalochordata.
- ****Paraphyletic**** — A taxonomic group that includes an ancestor but not all its descendants (e.g., "Reptilia" excluding birds; "fish" excluding tetrapods).

## Debates and open questions
- **Hagfish placement:** Whether hagfish are degenerate vertebrates (secondary loss of vertebrae) or represent a pre-vertebrate craniate lineage was resolved in favor of Cyclostomata monophyly (hagfish + lampreys) by 2019 morphological analyses, but the evolutionary sequence of character loss remains under study.
- **Stem vs. crown Vertebrata:** The phylogenetic position of Cambrian fossils like *Myllokunmingia* and *Haikouichthys*—whether they fall within the crown group (possessing true vertebrae) or the stem group (cranium only)—is tentative and affects the definition of Vertebrata vs. Craniata.
- **Conodont affinity:** The relationship of conodonts (known from tooth-like elements) to other vertebrates remains uncertain; they inform the debate on whether mineralized tissue evolved first in teeth or the dermal skeleton.
- **Traditional ranks vs. clade names:** Whether to retain Linnaean ranks (class, order) for paraphyletic groups like "Agnatha" and "Reptilia" or adopt a purely rank-free phylogenetic nomenclature is an ongoing methodological debate.
- **Drivers of current decline:** While the five main drivers of population loss are identified, their relative contributions and synergistic interactions across different biomes and clades require further quantification to prioritize conservation.
