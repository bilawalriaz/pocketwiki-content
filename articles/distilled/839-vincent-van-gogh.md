# Vincent van Gogh

## Overview

Vincent van Gogh (1853–1890) was a Dutch Post-Impressionist painter whose bold colours and expressive brushwork helped lay the foundations of modern art. Despite creating roughly 2,100 artworks—including 860 oil paintings—in just over a decade, he sold only a few during his lifetime and died by suicide at age 37 after years of mental illness and poverty. His legacy rests not only on his vivid landscapes, portraits, and self-portraits but also on his deeply personal correspondence with his brother Theo, which reveals his artistic theories and inner turmoil.

## Body

### Early Life and Religious Phase

Van Gogh was born into an upper-middle-class family in Zundert. His father, Theodorus van Gogh, was a Dutch Reformed minister, and his mother, Anna Carbentus, came from a prosperous background. Encouraged to draw as a child, he later worked for the art dealer Goupil & Cie in The Hague and London. After being rejected by his landlady’s daughter, Eugénie Loyer, he became increasingly isolated and turned toward religion. He attempted to train as a missionary but failed theological exams and was dismissed from a missionary post in Belgium for living too humbly among miners.

### Artistic Beginnings and Training

Returning home in 1880, Van Gogh began drawing local peasants and laborers. With financial support from his brother Theo, he studied briefly at the Académie Royale des Beaux-Arts in Brussels. In 1881, he moved to The Hague, where he painted *The Potato Eaters* (1885), a somber depiction of peasant life. Though Theo criticized its dark palette, it marked his commitment to representing working-class struggles.

In 1886, Van Gogh moved to Paris, where he encountered Impressionists and Post-Impressionists like Gauguin, Bernard, and Toulouse-Lautrec. Exposure to Japanese prints and brighter palettes transformed his style. He experimented with Pointillism and began producing self-portraits and still lifes with renewed vigor.

### Arles Period and Breakdown

Seeking solitude and light, Van Gogh relocated to Arles in 1888. There, he painted over 200 works, including *Sunflowers*, *The Yellow House*, and *Starry Night Over the Rhône*. He envisioned an artists’ colony and invited Gauguin to join him. Their cohabitation ended violently when Van Gogh, possibly experiencing a psychotic episode, severed part of his own ear with a razor. Gauguin fled, never to return.

Van Gogh was hospitalized in Arles and later committed himself to the asylum at Saint-Rémy-de-Provence in 1889. During this time, he painted *The Starry Night* and continued exploring themes of nature, spirituality, and suffering through symbolic floral and landscape compositions.

### Final Months and Death

In May 1890, Van Gogh moved to Auvers-sur-Oise to be near Dr. Paul Gachet. Over the next two months, he produced nearly 70 paintings, including *Wheatfield with Crows* and multiple portraits of Gachet. On 27 July, he shot himself in the chest while walking through a wheat field. He died two days later, aged 37. His last words were reportedly, “The sadness will last forever.”

### Style and Themes

Van Gogh’s mature style is defined by swirling forms, intense hues, and thick application of paint (impasto). He believed colour carried emotional and spiritual meaning—yellow symbolized life and divine truth, while red conveyed passion and danger. His brushstrokes were deliberate yet spontaneous, aiming to express inner experience rather than mere visual reality.

He often returned to recurring motifs: sunflowers, cypress trees, wheat fields, and self-portraits. These subjects served both personal expression and symbolic purpose. For instance, the sower—a figure borrowed from Millet—represented resilience and spiritual labor.

His letters reveal a deep engagement with art theory, particularly regarding color relationships and the moral weight of artistic creation. Influenced by contemporaries like Gauguin and Seurat, he synthesized realism with abstraction, laying groundwork for Fauvism and Expressionism.

## Terms

- **Post-Impressionism**: An art movement emerging in the 1880s that rejected naturalistic depiction in favor of emotional and symbolic content; Van Gogh was a key figure.
- **Impasto**: Technique involving thick layers of paint applied to the canvas for texture and emphasis.
- **Pointillism**: Method of applying small dots of pure color that blend optically when viewed from afar; practiced by Seurat and briefly adopted by Van Gogh.
- **Plein air**: French term meaning "open air"; refers to painting outdoors to capture natural light.
- **Ukiyo-e**: Genre of Japanese woodblock prints popular in the West; influenced Van Gogh’s composition and use of line.
- **Fauvism**: Early 20th-century avant-garde movement emphasizing bold, non-naturalistic color; directly influenced by Van Gogh.
- **Expressionism**: Art movement prioritizing subjective emotion over objective representation; German Expressionists drew inspiration from Van Gogh.
- **Oeuvre**: Complete body of an artist’s work.
- **Catalogue raisonné**: Comprehensive listing and documentation of all known works by an artist.
- **Asylum**: Institution for individuals with mental illness; Van Gogh voluntarily entered one in Saint-Rémy.

## Debates and Open Questions

### Mental Health Diagnosis

Van Gogh’s mental state remains debated. Contemporary accounts describe episodes of psychosis, delusions, and self-harm. Modern scholars propose various diagnoses: bipolar disorder (first suggested in 1947), temporal lobe epilepsy, acute intermittent porphyria, or a combination exacerbated by alcoholism, malnutrition, and insomnia. No consensus exists, though his condition likely worsened due to lifestyle factors.

### Ear Incident

The circumstances surrounding Van Gogh’s ear mutilation remain unclear. Gauguin claimed Van Gogh attacked him with a razor, but evidence suggests Gauguin was absent that night. Some historians speculate the act was self-inflicted during a breakdown, possibly triggered by fear of abandonment.

### Authorship and Attribution

While most works attributed to Van Gogh are accepted, some early pieces have disputed authorship. Additionally, debates persist over the number of authentic paintings produced in his final months, especially given the volume created in Auvers-sur-Oise.

### Legacy and Mythologization

Van Gogh’s posthumous fame was shaped significantly by his letters and biographies like Irving Stone’s *Lust for Life*. Critics argue this narrative romanticizes mental illness and suffering as prerequisites for genius—a view challenged by recent scholarship seeking a more nuanced understanding of his life and work.

--- 

## Reputation and Legacy

Van Gogh sold only a handful of paintings in his lifetime. Recognition grew slowly after his death, fueled by exhibitions, critical praise, and the publication of his letters. By the early 20th century, artists like Matisse and Derain embraced his bold palette, contributing to the rise of Fauvism. His influence extended to German Expressionists and later movements.

Today, Van Gogh is among the most recognized figures in art history. His works command record prices at auction, and his story continues to resonate culturally, symbolizing the tragic artist whose brilliance emerged only after death. Institutions like the Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam preserve his legacy, while ongoing research into his life and health keeps scholarly interest alive.
