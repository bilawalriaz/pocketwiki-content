# Board game

## Overview
A board game is a tabletop game defined by the use of a specially designed, patterned surface (the board) on which pieces are placed and moved, often with components like dice or cards. Archaeological evidence places board games in the Neolithic period (early 7000s BC), with distinct traditions evolving independently across Egypt, Mesopotamia, Europe, the Americas, and Asia. The genre experienced a commercial "Golden Age" (1880s–1920s) driven by mass production, followed by a late‑20th‑century "Renaissance" sparked by German‑style designs like *The Settlers of Catan* (1995) that emphasized strategy over luck. Modern board games are studied in ludology for their cognitive benefits, categorized by mechanics (e.g., worker placement, deck‑building), theme, and player interaction, and have grown into a market exceeding $700 million annually in the hobby sector alone.

## Body

### Ancient Origins and Archaeological Evidence
Board games appear in the Neolithic period (early 7000s BC), with 51 boards documented across 14 sites as of 2024. The earliest known pieces—elaborate sculptured stones in sets of four—date to c. 3000 BC in southwest Turkey. *Senet* (Egypt, c. 3500–3100 BC) is often cited as the oldest known board game, though its rules were lost after it fell out of fashion post‑AD 400. *Mehen* (Egypt, c. 3000–2300 BC) and the *Royal Game of Ur* (Mesopotamia, c. 2500 BC) are other early examples; the latter is the oldest with recovered rules (cuneiform tablet, c. 177–176 BC). A 1977 find at Shahr‑i Sokhta, Iran (2600–2400 BC) yielded a complete set with 27 pieces and four dice, also claimed as the oldest playable game. *Mancala*’s age is highly uncertain: Neolithic divots in Jordan (c. 5870 BC) are disputed, while other variants date to 2500–1500 BC (Egypt) and 800–200 BC (Roman sites). Scholars caution that absence of earlier evidence does not prove games did not exist.

### Regional Traditions
Europe’s recorded history begins with *petteia* in Homer’s *Iliad* (8th century BC), which evolved into Roman *ludus latrunculorum*. In Mesoamerica, *patolli* was played by Toltecs and Aztecs. Across Africa and the Middle East, *mancala* remains a dominant archetype with numerous regional variations. Asia’s regional history is noted in the source but not detailed.

### Golden Age and Renaissance
The 1880s–1920s "Golden Age" (coined by Margaret Hofer) saw mass production make games cheaper and ubiquitous; *Monopoly* (1935) became the iconic hit. By the late 1990s, a growing worldwide market prompted a "Board Game Renaissance." *The Settlers of Catan* (1995) catalyzed this shift, introducing German‑style mechanics (strategic depth, reduced luck) to a global audience and displacing 20th‑century staples like *Monopoly* and *Game of Life* (1960). Subsequent hits *Carcassonne* (2000) and *Ticket to Ride* (2004) cemented the designer‑game boom.

### Luck, Strategy, and Diplomacy
Games sit on a spectrum from pure skill (*chess*) to pure luck (*Candy Land*, *Snakes and Ladders*). Most blend both: in *Monopoly*, *Risk*, or *Catan*, luck (dice, cards, spinners) creates variance, but skill prevails over repeated play. German‑style games notably minimize luck compared to North American designs. Symmetry (e.g., letting players choose their die roll or the previous player’s) can reduce luck’s impact. **Diplomacy**—negotiation and deal‑making—appears in games with three or more players (e.g., trading in *Catan*, alliances in *Risk*, formal treaties in *Diplomacy*, 1954). **Information completeness** also varies: *chess* is perfect information; *Stratego* (1946) and *Tigris and Euphrates* (1997) hide information, requiring probability estimation.

### Digital Adaptations and Virtual Play
Many board games exist as **digital board games** (video game versions) playable online against AI or humans. Sites like BoardGameArena and Yucata.de offer real‑time play; others use asynchronous email notification. The internet also enabled **print‑and‑play** distribution. **Virtual tabletop** programs (Vassal, Tabletop Simulator, Tabletopia) simulate physical components without enforcing rules, supporting any board/card game; specialized tools (Roll20, Fantasy Grounds) target role‑playing games. Licensed DLC exists (e.g., Fantasy Grounds for *D&D*), but user modifications also enable unlicensed asset use.

### Market Size and Growth
The board‑game market is smaller than video games but has grown significantly since the late 1990s. Estimates: global market >$1.2B (1991); US/Canada hobby segment $75M (2014) within a total hobby game market >$700M (2014) / ~$900M (2015); Germany led per‑capita sales in 2009. Growth drivers include quality improvements (mechanics, components, art) and internet retail. Crowdfunding is major: $233M raised on Kickstarter in 2020 alone.

### Research and Cognitive Effects
**Ludology** (game studies) examines board games scientifically. Classic games (*chess*, *Go*, *mancala*) are well‑studied; modern titles (*Catan*, *Pandemic*) less so. Chess research (de Groot, Chase, Simon, Gobet) shows **knowledge/pattern recognition** outweighs calculation depth in expertise. Linear board games (e.g., *Snakes and Ladders*) improve children’s spatial‑numerical understanding by mapping numbers to a line rather than logarithmic intuition, boosting counting, estimation, and comprehension. Fine motor skills are exercised by piece manipulation. Play correlates with improved executive function in children and reduced dementia risk in the elderly. **Accessibility research** now produces guidelines for disability inclusion. Therapeutic value is noted: tension/release cycles (per designer Bruce Halpenny) counter repetitive work stress. Educational integration is advocated when gameplay aligns with curriculum.

### Classification Systems
Early taxonomies: H. J. R. Murray (1952) — race, war, hunt, alignment/configuration, mancala; R. C. Bell (1960) — race, war, positional, mancala; David Parlett (1999) — race, space, chase, displace. Modern classification uses **mechanics** (worker placement, deck‑building, etc.), **theme** (abstract vs. thematic/narrative; Golden Age themes: travel, war, capitalist enterprise; modern: varied), **components**, **age range**, **player count** (solitaire, competitive, cooperative), and **promotion type** (marketing channels, not game types). Parlett criticized licensed thematic games masking poor mechanics as "trivial, ephemeral, mind‑numbing… worthlessness."

## Terms
- ****Board game**** — A tabletop game using a specially designed, patterned surface (board) as a necessary and sufficient condition; pieces are placed/moved per rules, often with dice/cards.
- ****Tabletop game**** — Broader category including board games, card games (non‑standard decks), and games with neither board nor cards.
- ****Senet**** — Ancient Egyptian game (c. 3500–3100 BC); oldest known board game per archaeological fragments, but rules lost after c. AD 400.
- ****Royal Game of Ur**** — Mesopotamian race game (c. 2500 BC); oldest with recovered rules (cuneiform tablet, c. 177–176 BC).
- ****Mancala**** — Family of games with two rows of pits/bowls; wide geographic spread (Africa, Middle East, Asia) and disputed dating (Neolithic to Iron Age).
- ****Golden Age (1880s–1920s)**** — Period of mass‑produced, affordable board games; term coined by art historian Margaret Hofer.
- ****German‑style game / Eurogame**** — Design school emphasizing strategic depth, reduced luck, indirect conflict, and elegant mechanics; popularized globally by *Catan* (1995).
- ****Board Game Renaissance**** — Early 21st‑century surge in designer‑driven hobby games (*Carcassonne*, *Ticket to Ride*) displacing Golden Age classics.
- ****Ludology**** — Academic field of game studies; examines psychology, cognition, and cultural impact of games.
- ****Perfect information game**** — Game where all players know the complete state at all times (e.g., *chess*); contrasts with hidden‑information games (*Stratego*).
- ****Print‑and‑play**** — Digital game files purchased and printed by the user for physical play.
- ****Virtual tabletop (VTT)**** — Software simulating a physical table (pieces, boards, dice) without enforcing rules; supports user‑created content.
- ****Worker placement**** — Mechanic where players assign limited tokens to board spaces to claim actions/resources; common in modern Eurogames.
- ****Deck‑building**** — Mechanic where players start with a basic deck and acquire new cards during play to improve it.
- ****Cooperative board game**** — Multiplayer game where all players win or lose as a team against the game system.

## Debates and open questions
- **Oldest game title**: *Senet*, *Mehen*, *Royal Game of Ur*, Shahr‑i Sokhta game, and *mancala* all have claims depending on definition (board vs. pieces, rule recovery, dating certainty).
- **Mancala’s true antiquity**: Neolithic Jordanian pits (c. 5870 BC) are proposed as *mancala* boards but disputed; the family’s wide variant spread complicates a single origin date.
- **Neolithic boards as "first games"**: Absence of earlier finds ≠ evidence of absence; older organic materials may not survive.
- **Market trajectory**: Some sources say board games "made a comeback" (Guardian, 2012); others argue they never declined, only grew.
- **Research gap**: Extensive literature on classic games (*chess*, *Go*); comparatively little on modern hobby games (*Catan*, *Agricola*, *Pandemic*).
- **Theme vs. mechanics**: Debate over whether licensed themes mask poor design (Parlett/Edwards criticism) or legitimately broaden appeal.
- **Digital vs. physical**: Unlicensed asset use on VTTs raises IP questions; impact of digital play on physical sales is unresolved.
