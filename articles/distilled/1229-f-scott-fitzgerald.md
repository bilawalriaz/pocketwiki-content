# F. Scott Fitzgerald

## Overview

F. Scott Fitzgerald (1896–1940) was an American novelist and short-story writer whose works captured the spirit and excess of the Jazz Age. Though he achieved fame in the 1920s with *This Side of Paradise* and *The Great Gatsby*, critical acclaim eluded him during his lifetime. Posthumously, his reputation grew, and he is now considered one of the greatest American writers of the 20th century.

## Body

### Early Life and Education

Born into a middle-class Catholic family in St. Paul, Minnesota, Fitzgerald was named after his distant cousin, Francis Scott Key. His father’s business failures led the family to move frequently, eventually settling back in St. Paul. Fitzgerald showed early literary promise, publishing his first story at age 13. He attended Princeton University, where he befriended Edmund Wilson and John Peale Bishop. A failed romance with Chicago socialite Ginevra King deeply affected him and influenced his later characters.

### Literary Breakthrough

After dropping out of Princeton to serve in WWI, Fitzgerald met Zelda Sayre, whom he married in 1920 after the success of *This Side of Paradise*. The novel made him a literary sensation, establishing him as a voice of his generation. His second novel, *The Beautiful and Damned*, continued his exploration of wealth and disillusionment. However, *The Great Gatsby* (1925), despite critical praise, was a commercial failure during his lifetime.

### Personal Struggles and Later Career

Fitzgerald’s marriage to Zelda deteriorated as her mental health declined, leading to her institutionalization for schizophrenia. Financial difficulties mounted during the Great Depression, and he turned to Hollywood as a screenwriter. His relationship with columnist Sheilah Graham provided companionship in his final years. Despite achieving sobriety briefly, Fitzgerald died of a heart attack in 1940 at age 44.

### Posthumous Recognition

After his death, Fitzgerald’s work gained renewed attention. Edmund Wilson edited and published *The Last Tycoon*, and *The Great Gatsby* became widely celebrated, especially after distribution to WWII soldiers. Critics reevaluated his contributions, recognizing him as a master of American literature.

## Terms

- **Jazz Age**: A term coined by Fitzgerald to describe the cultural vibrancy and excess of the 1920s.
- **Lost Generation**: A group of American expatriate writers in 1920s Paris, including Hemingway and Gertrude Stein.
- **Bildungsroman**: A coming-of-age novel, exemplified by *This Side of Paradise*.
- **Magnum opus**: One's greatest work; *The Great Gatsby* is considered Fitzgerald’s.
- **Parvenu**: A person who has risen socially but lacks refinement; a recurring theme in Fitzgerald’s critique of wealth.
- **Nouveau riche**: Newly rich individuals, often portrayed negatively in Fitzgerald’s works.
- **Sobriety**: Fitzgerald struggled with alcoholism and briefly achieved sobriety before his death.
- **Schizophrenia**: A severe mental disorder that afflicted Zelda Fitzgerald, impacting their marriage.
- **Commercial failure**: *The Great Gatsby* sold poorly initially but gained fame posthumously.
- **Cultural elite**: The sophisticated literary and artistic circles Fitzgerald engaged with in New York and Paris.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Critical reception of *The Great Gatsby***: Initially a commercial failure, its status as the "Great American Novel" is debated among scholars.
- **Fitzgerald’s political engagement**: Critics argue his works lack socio-political depth, while others see them as reflections of the Jazz Age zeitgeist.
- **Zelda’s influence**: Her mental illness and personality significantly shaped Fitzgerald’s writing, but interpretations of her role vary.
- **Hollywood’s impact**: Whether Fitzgerald’s screenwriting career hindered or helped his literary legacy remains contested.
- **Appropriation in fiction**: Fitzgerald’s use of personal letters and experiences of others in his work raises questions about artistic ethics.
