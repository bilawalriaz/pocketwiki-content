# Middle Ages

## Overview
The Middle Ages (c. 5th–late 15th centuries) form the middle epoch of Western history between classical antiquity and the modern period. The era began with the fragmentation of the Western Roman Empire under Germanic migrations and ended during the transitions of the Renaissance, Reformation, and Age of Discovery. A defining feature was the synthesis of Roman, Germanic, and Christian traditions into new political, social, and cultural structures. The period divides into three phases: the Early Middle Ages (c. 5th–10th centuries), marked by political fragmentation, the rise of Islam, and the Carolingian Empire; the High Middle Ages (c. 1000–1300), characterised by population growth, feudalism, the Crusades, and the rise of centralised monarchies and universities; and the Late Middle Ages (c. 1300–1500), defined by the Black Death, the Hundred Years' War, papal schisms, and the dawn of the printing press and oceanic exploration.

## Body

### The Later Roman Empire and the Migration Period
The Roman Empire reached its greatest extent in the 2nd century AD but faced the Crisis of the Third Century (inflation, usurpers, Sasanian wars). Diocletian (r. 284–305) split administration into eastern and western halves (286); Constantine I refounded Byzantium as Constantinople (330). Despite reforms, excessive taxation, declining birthrates, and frontier pressures persisted. In 376, Goths fleeing the Huns were allowed to settle in Thracia; mishandling led to the Battle of Adrianople (378), where Valens died. The 5th century saw successive invasions: Visigoths sacked Rome (410); Vandals, Alans, and Suevi crossed the Rhine (406) into Gaul and Spain; Huns under Attila ravaged the Balkans, Gaul, and Italy (440s–450s). By 476, the western empire had dissolved into Germanic kingdoms (Ostrogoths in Italy, Visigoths in Spain, Franks in Gaul, Vandals in Africa). The Eastern (Byzantine) Empire survived, preserving Roman law (*Corpus Juris Civilis*, 529–534) and administration.

### Early Middle Ages: New Societies, Byzantium, and the Rise of Islam
**New Kingdoms and Social Change:** Germanic kings ruled through fusion of tribal custom and Roman institutions. Taxation decayed as armies were paid via land grants rather than cash. Slavery declined; society ruralised. The Merovingian Franks (Clovis I, r. 509–511) converted to Catholic Christianity, gaining papal support. Lombards settled Italy (568); Anglo-Saxons and Britons established kingdoms in Britain. Latin evolved into Romance languages; Greek persisted in the East; Slavic languages spread in the Balkans.

**Byzantine Survival:** The East enjoyed 5th-century peace with Persia and economic revival. Justinian I (r. 527–565) codified law, built the Hagia Sophia, and reconquered Africa and Italy (Belisarius), though plague (542) and overextension halted gains. Subsequent wars with Persia (602–628) exhausted both empires. Slavs and Avars pressured the Balkans; the Avars extracted tribute until 796.

**Rise of Islam:** Muhammad (d. 632) unified Arabia. Successors conquered Syria (634–635), Persia (637–642), Egypt (640–641), North Africa, and Iberia (711). The Umayyad Caliphate (661–750) ruled from Damascus; its defeat at Tours (732) by Charles Martel stopped European expansion. The Abbasid revolution (750) shifted the capital to Baghdad, fragmenting Muslim rule (Umayyads in Spain, Aghlabids in Africa, Tulunids in Egypt). Mediterranean trade reoriented: Franks traded timber, furs, swords, and slaves for silks, spices, and metals.

**Carolingian Europe:** Charles Martel’s victory at Tours (732) solidified Carolingian power. Pippin III deposed the Merovingians (753) with papal backing. Charlemagne (r. 768–814) conquered Lombardy (774), Saxony, and the Spanish March, creating an empire covering France, northern Italy, and Germany. Crowned Emperor by Pope Leo III (800), he asserted parity with Byzantium. Administration relied on *counts* and *missi dominici* (roving inspectors); the economy was rural, trade minimal.

**Carolingian Renaissance:** Centered at Aachen under Alcuin of York. Carolingian minuscule standardised script; Medieval Latin replaced Classical Latin. Liturgical reform imposed Roman rite and Gregorian chant. Monasteries copied classical texts, preserving Latin literature.

**Breakup and Invasions:** Louis the Pious (r. 814–840) faced civil wars among sons. Treaty of Verdun (843) split the empire: West Francia (Charles the Bald), East Francia (Louis the German), Middle Kingdom (Lothair I). External attacks followed: Vikings (settled Normandy 911), Magyars (defeated at Lechfeld 955), and Saracens (raiding Italy/Sicily).

**New Kingdoms and Byzantine Revival:** Alfred the Great (r. 871–899) contained Vikings in England; successors unified the kingdom. Otto I (r. 936–973) defeated Magyars, intervened in Italy, and was crowned Emperor (962), founding the Ottonian Holy Roman Empire. Byzantium revived under the Macedonian dynasty (Basil I, Basil II); borders expanded, administration standardised, and the Macedonian Renaissance flourished. Missionary work converted Slavs and Magyars, founding states (Poland, Hungary, Kievan Rus', Bulgaria).

### High Middle Ages: Expansion, Institutions, and Culture
**Population and Economy:** Europe’s population doubled (35–80 million, 1000–1347) due to the Medieval Warm Period, the heavy plough, the horse collar, and the three-field system (two-thirds land in use vs. one-half). *Manorialism* organised peasants (serfs and free) on manors owing labour/rents to lords. *Feudalism* structured military society: lords granted *fiefs* (hereditary after 11th century) to vassals (knights) in return for cavalry service. Castles (stone from 10th century) anchored noble power. Towns grew (≤10% population); trade revived via the Hanseatic League (Baltic) and Italian maritime republics (Venice, Genoa, Pisa). Gold coinage returned (13th century); double-entry bookkeeping and letters of credit emerged.

**Rise of State Power:** Capetian France expanded from Île-de-France; Philip II Augustus (r. 1180–1223) seized Normandy from England (1204). England: Norman Conquest (1066) created cross-channel empire; Henry II (r. 1154–1189) ruled England and western France via marriage to Eleanor of Aquitaine. John’s loss of Normandy (1204) and fiscal exactions provoked Magna Carta (1215). Iberia: *Reconquista* advanced; by 1150 five Christian kingdoms (León, Castile, Aragon, Navarre, Portugal) faced the Almohad Caliphate; Seville fell 1248. Germany: Ottonians succeeded by Salians (1024); Investiture Controversy (Henry IV vs. Gregory VII, 1076–1122) weakened imperial authority over German princes. Frederick II (r. 1220–1250) clashed with the papacy; Mongol invasions (1241, 1259, 1287) struck Eastern Europe. Papal Monarchy peaked under Innocent III (r. 1198–1216).

**Crusades:** Seljuk Turks took Jerusalem (1070) and defeated Byzantium at Manzikert (1071). Urban II preached the First Crusade (1095); Jerusalem captured (1099). Crusader states established; military orders (Templars, Hospitallers) founded. Subsequent crusades (Third, 1189–1192; Fourth, 1204) failed to hold the Holy Land; the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople (1204), fatally weakening Byzantium. Crusades also targeted pagans in the Baltic (Teutonic Knights) and heretics in France (Albigensian Crusade, 1209).

**Intellectual Life:** Rediscovery of Aristotle (via Arabic translations) sparked debate on universals (realists vs. nominalists). *Scholasticism* fused faith and reason (Peter Abelard, Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*). Cathedral schools shifted learning to towns; universities founded at Bologna (law), Paris (theology), Oxford. Roman law revived via *Corpus Juris Civilis* (Bologna, c. 1100); Gratian’s *Decretum* (c. 1140) systematised canon law. Arabic/Greek influence introduced Hindu-Arabic numerals and algebra. Medicine advanced at Salerno.

**Technology and Military:** Windmills, mechanical clocks, distilled spirits, astrolabe, spectacles (c. 1286). Heavy plough + horse collar + three-field system boosted agriculture. Rib-and-plank ships, lateen sails, stern-post rudders improved shipping. Crossbows proliferated (prompting plate armour); gunpowder known by mid-13th century; cannon used 1320s; hand-guns 1360s. Infantry specialists (crossbowmen, sappers) joined heavy cavalry.

**Architecture, Art, Music:** *Romanesque* (10th–12th c.): massive walls, round arches, barrel vaults, sculpted portals (Mosan metalwork, illuminated Bibles). *Gothic* (12th–16th c.): rib vaults, pointed arches, flying buttresses, stained glass (Chartres, Reims, Salisbury). Panel painting advanced in Italy (Cimabue, Giotto). Secular art grew (ivories, tapestries). Vernacular literature flourished (*chansons de geste*, Arthurian legends, Dante, Chaucer).

**Church Life:** Cluniac Reforms (from 909) enforced monastic austerity and papal protection. Investiture Controversy established papal independence. New orders: Cistercians (Bernard of Clairvaux), Carthusians. Mendicants (Franciscans, Dominicans, 13th c.) embraced apostolic poverty. Heresies suppressed: Waldensians, Cathars (Albigensian Crusade, Inquisition). Pilgrimages and relic veneration thrived.

### Late Middle Ages: Crisis, Transformation, and Transition
**War, Famine, Plague:** The Little Ice Age ended the Medieval Warm Period; Great Famine (1315–1317). Black Death (1347–1350) killed ~35 million (⅓ of Europe). Labour scarcity raised wages; lords commuted labour services to cash rents. Serfdom declined in West (90% → ~50%) but intensified in East. Peasant revolts: *Jacquerie* (France), Peasants’ Revolt (England), Ciompi (Florence). Flagellant movements and anti-Jewish violence erupted.

**Society and Economy:** Marginal lands abandoned; banking expanded in Italy (papal finance, royal loans). Jews expelled: England (1290), France (1306), Spain (1492). Literacy rose among laity; chivalric culture spread to urban elites.

**State Resurgence:** Strong monarchies emerged. Hundred Years’ War (1337–1453): English longbow victories (Crécy 1346, Poitiers 1356); French revival under Joan of Arc (1429–1431); France lost ~half its population; English national identity solidified. War drove taxation and representative bodies (English Parliament, French Estates General). Holy Roman Empire remained elective/fragmented. Poland, Hungary, Bohemia grew. Iberia: Portugal explored Atlantic; Castile/Aragon completed Reconquista (Granada 1492). England: Wars of the Roses (1455–1487) ended with Henry VII (1485). Scandinavia: Union of Kalmar (1397–1523). Hanseatic League dominated Baltic trade. Scotland independent under Robert the Bruce (1328).

**Collapse of Byzantium:** Palaiologoi recaptured Constantinople (1261) but held only fragments. Ottomans expanded from Anatolia (13th c.); Bulgaria vassalised (1366); Serbia crushed at Kosovo (1389); Crusade of Nicopolis failed (1396). Constantinople fell to Mehmed II (1453).

**Church Controversy:** Avignon Papacy (1309–1376) and Western Schism (1378–1417, 2–3 rival popes). Council of Constance (1414–1418) elected Martin V. Heresies: Wycliffe (Lollardy), Hus (Hussitism, burned 1415). Knights Templar suppressed (1312). Laity excluded from Eucharistic wine; mysticism (Eckhart, *Imitation of Christ*) and witch-hunting (*Malleus Maleficarum*, 1486) grew.

**Scholars and Exploration:** Ockham and Duns Scotus separated reason from faith (nominalism). Roman law spread (except England). Universities proliferated; literacy ~10% male, 1% female (1500). Vernacular literature: Dante, Petrarch, Boccaccio; Chaucer, Langland; Villon, Christine de Pizan. Printing press (Gutenberg, c. 1450); 30,000 incunabula editions by 1500. Iberian exploration: Henry the Navigator (Azores, Cape Verde); Dias (Cape of Good Hope, 1486); da Gama (India, 1498); Columbus (Americas, 1492); Cabot (Newfoundland, 1497).

**Technology and Military:** Infantry and light cavalry rose; English longbowmen unique. Plate armour countered crossbows/guns. Pike formations (Swiss/Flemish). Spinning wheel tripled wool output; buttons improved clothing; tower mill windmills; blast furnace (Sweden, c. 1350); Venetian patent law (1447).

**Art and Architecture:** Late Gothic/International Gothic (Très Riches Heures). Italian Trecento/Early Renaissance (Giotto, van Eyck). Secular patronage (merchants) commissioned portraits, luxury goods (tapestries, maiolica, silk). Printed books replaced manuscripts; woodcuts made images affordable.

## Terms
- ****Manorialism**** — The socio-economic system organising peasants into villages (manors) owing rent, labour, and dues to a lord in exchange for protection and land tenure.
- ****Feudalism**** — The political-military hierarchy whereby a lord granted a *fief* (land/revenue) to a vassal in return for homage and military service (typically heavy cavalry).
- ****Scholasticism**** — The 12th–13th century intellectual method using Aristotelian logic to reconcile Christian theology with reason, culminating in Thomas Aquinas’s *Summa Theologica*.
- ****Investiture Controversy**** — Conflict (1076–1122) between Pope Gregory VII and Emperor Henry IV over whether secular rulers could appoint bishops; resolved by the Concordat of Worms (1122).
- ****Crusades**** — Papally authorised military expeditions (1095–1291) to reclaim the Holy Land from Muslim rule; later extended to Iberia, the Baltic, and heretical groups.
- ****Black Death**** — Bubonic/pneumonic plague pandemic (1347–1350) killing roughly one-third of Europe’s population, causing massive demographic, economic, and social upheaval.
- ****Hundred Years' War**** — Dynastic conflict (1337–1453) between England and France over French succession and English territories; accelerated royal centralisation and national identities.
- ****Western Schism**** — Split in the Roman Catholic Church (1378–1417) with two (later three) rival popes; ended by the Council of Constance.
- ****Corpus Juris Civilis**** — Justinian I’s 6th-century codification of Roman law (Code, Digest, Institutes, Novels); rediscovered c. 1070, became foundation of European civil law.
- ****Carolingian Minuscule**** — Standardised, legible script developed under Charlemagne; basis for modern lowercase typefaces and key to preserving classical texts.

## Debates and open questions
- **Periodisation:** No universal start/end dates. 476 (fall of West Rome) vs. later dates for peripheral regions; 1453 (Constantinople), 1492 (Columbus/Reconquista), 1517 (Reformation), or 1648 (Westphalia) as endpoints. Historians debate whether the Renaissance/Reformation are "medieval" or "early modern."
- **"Dark Ages" label:** 19th-century historians applied this to the entire era; modern scholarship restricts it (if used) to the Early Middle Ages, noting cultural continuity and innovation.
- **Feudalism vs. Manorialism:** Historians debate whether "feudalism" describes a coherent system or a retrospective construct; the relationship between military vassalage and peasant manorial obligations remains contested.
- **Causes of 14th-century crisis:** Relative weight of climate change (Little Ice Age), Malthusian population pressure, monetary contraction, and institutional rigidity in causing the Great Famine and pre-plague stagnation.
- **Black Death mortality and agency:** Exact death tolls debated (30–60%); debate continues on whether peasant revolts were reactive or proactive drivers of serfdom’s decline in the West.
- **Byzantine "decline":** Whether the 1204 Fourth Crusade sack or structural weaknesses (fiscal, military, dynastic) were the primary cause of the empire’s inability to recover before 1453.
- **Continuity vs. rupture:** The extent to which medieval institutions (parliaments, universities, common law, banking) directly founded modern Europe versus representing distinct medieval solutions.
