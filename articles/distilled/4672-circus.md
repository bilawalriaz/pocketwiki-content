# Circus

## Overview
A circus is a company of performers presenting diverse entertainment acts, including acrobats, clowns, and trained animals. The modern circus format, credited to Philip Astley in 1768, evolved from equestrian exhibitions into a global entertainment industry. Its history reflects shifts in performance style, venue, and public taste, from traditional big-top shows to contemporary theatrical productions.

## Timeline
- **1768** — Philip Astley begins performing trick horse riding in a London field.
- **1770** — Astley hires acrobats, jugglers, and a clown, creating the foundational variety format.
- **1782** — The "Royal Circus" opens in London, coining the term "circus."
- **1793** — John Bill Ricketts gives America's first complete circus performance in Philadelphia.
- **1825** — Joshuah Purdy Brown is the first to use a large canvas tent for circus performances.
- **1870s** — P. T. Barnum and William Cameron Coup launch the traveling circus and first freak show.
- **1919** — Soviet Russia nationalizes circuses, aiming to make them "the people's art-form."
- **1970s** — The "new circus" movement begins, focusing on human artistry and theatrical themes.
- **1984** — Cirque du Soleil is founded in Quebec, becoming a major success in new circus.
- **1995** — The "contemporary circus" genre is considered to begin with a French school performance.

## Body

### Origins and Etymology
The word "circus" derives from the Latin *circus* and Ancient Greek *kirkos*, meaning "circle." While the modern circus is a 250-year-old format, its name and some concepts trace back to ancient Rome. Roman circuses were rectangular arenas for chariot races, equestrian shows, and gladiatorial combat, not circular like modern rings. Historians debate whether a direct lineage exists from Roman spectacles to the modern circus, with some tracing a path through medieval performers to Philip Astley.

### The Modern Circus: Astley and Expansion
Philip Astley, a skilled equestrian, is credited as the father of the modern circus. In 1768, he performed horse riding tricks in a circle, discovering that a 42-foot (13-meter) diameter ring was optimal for such acts. In 1770, he added other performers like acrobats and clowns between his equestrian acts, creating the variety show format. This model was quickly adopted and expanded. The first circus building in the U.S. opened in 1793, and by the 1820s, canvas "big tops" allowed circuses to tour widely. Innovators like P. T. Barnum and William Cameron Coup revolutionized the American circus in the 1870s with multiple rings, circus trains, and the traveling "Greatest Show On Earth" format, which influenced European circuses.

### Evolution of Venue and Form
Early modern circuses performed in open-air structures. Purpose-built wooden circus buildings became common from the late 18th to 19th centuries. The iconic "big top" tent was introduced in the mid-19th century, enabling the rise of the touring circus. Today, performances occur in tents, theaters, casinos, and other venues. The traditional format, with a ringmaster introducing choreographed acts to music, dominated until the 1970s.

### New Circus and Contemporary Circus
A revival began in the late 1970s with "new circus" (or *cirque nouveau*), which typically avoids animal acts to focus on human artistry. It uses theatrical techniques, original music, and character-driven stories to convey themes. Cirque du Soleil, founded in 1984, is its most commercially successful example. Since the 1990s, "contemporary circus" has emerged as a more avant-garde genre, aligning closer to performance art and dance by avoiding linear narratives and developing new apparatus based on individual performers' skills. The term "21st Century Circus" is sometimes used to encompass all modern styles.

### Performance and Acts
A traditional circus is led by a ringmaster and features acts grouped into disciplines like acrobatics, aerial arts, juggling, equilibristics (balance acts), and clowning. Famous clowns include Joseph Grimaldi and Emmett Kelly. Animal acts, starting with equestrian displays and later including big cats and elephants, were once central but are now rare in new and contemporary circus due to ethical concerns and changing tastes. Sideshow and daredevil acts, like the human cannonball, are also part of circus history.

## Terms
- **Circus:** A company of performers presenting diverse entertainment acts; also the field of performance and community.
- **Ringmaster:** The master of ceremonies who introduces acts and guides the show.
- **Discipline:** A specific physical or creative skill performed in the circus, such as juggling or aerial arts.
- **Equilibristics:** Circus acts focused on balance, including tightwire, slackline, and unicycle.
- **New Circus (cirque nouveau):** A movement from the 1970s that combines traditional skills with theatrical storytelling, often without animals.
- **Contemporary Circus:** A genre from the 1990s that uses circus techniques in avant-garde, interdisciplinary performances aligned with dance or visual arts.
- **Big Top:** The large tent traditionally used as a venue for traveling circuses.
- **Artiste/Artist:** Terms for circus performers; "artiste" is traditional, while "artist" is preferred by some newer performers.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origins:** Historians disagree on whether the modern circus has a direct lineage from ancient Roman circuses or truly begins with Philip Astley in the 18th century.
- **Terminology:** The use of "contemporary circus" can be confusing, as it refers both to a specific avant-garde genre and generically to "circus of today," leading some to use "21st Century Circus" instead.
- **Animal Use:** The role and ethics of animal acts have been a major point of criticism and change, leading to their decline in many modern circus forms.
