# John, King of England

## Overview
King John of England (r. 1199–1216) is a pivotal but controversial monarch whose reign saw the collapse of the Angevin Empire in France, a major conflict with the papacy, and a baronial revolt that led to the sealing of Magna Carta. His failures in war and his oppressive rule contributed to the growth of French power and, paradoxically, to the development of English constitutional principles.

## Timeline
- **1166/7** — Born to Henry II and Eleanor of Aquitaine.
- **1177** — Appointed Lord of Ireland by Henry II.
- **1199** — Becomes King of England after Richard I's death.
- **1200** — Treaty of Le Goulet with Philip II of France.
- **1202–04** — Loses Normandy and most Angevin lands in France.
- **1208–14** — Interdict placed on England; John excommunicated in 1209.
- **1213** — Submits England as a papal fief to Pope Innocent III.
- **1214** — Final French campaign fails after the Battle of Bouvines.
- **1215** — Seals Magna Carta at Runnymede; First Barons' War begins.
- **1216** — Dies of dysentery at Newark Castle.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
John was the youngest son of Henry II and Eleanor of Aquitaine, nicknamed "Lackland" as he was not expected to inherit significant lands. He became his father's favorite after a failed revolt by his older brothers. During the reign of his brother Richard I, John attempted an unsuccessful rebellion while Richard was on the Third Crusade. After Richard's death in 1199, John was crowned king, though his nephew Arthur of Brittany also had a claim, supported by Philip II of France.

### Loss of the Angevin Empire
John's early reign was marked by the loss of his continental lands. A peace treaty with Philip II in 1200 was short-lived, reigniting war in 1202. Despite an early victory at Mirebeau, John's harsh treatment of prisoners and allies led to widespread desertions. The probable murder of his nephew Arthur further eroded his support. By 1204, Philip II had captured Normandy, Anjou, and Poitou, leaving John with only Aquitaine in France.

### Kingship and Administration
John was an active and hard-working administrator who reformed the English legal system, extending the reach of royal justice. However, he ruled with a style of "vis et voluntas" (force and will), relying on a "narrow clique" of foreign mercenaries and officials. He used his royal prerogative of *ira et malevolentia* (anger and ill-will) to punish barons, exemplified by the starvation of William de Braose's wife and son.

### Economy and Revenue
To fund campaigns to reclaim Normandy, John intensified feudal revenue collection. He levied scutage (a tax in lieu of military service) eleven times, often without a campaign. He maximized income from fines, inheritance fees (reliefs), and the sale of charters. He also imposed a new tax on income and movable goods in 1207 and heavily taxed the Jewish community. These measures were deeply unpopular and a major cause of baronial discontent.

### Conflict with the Papacy
A dispute over the appointment of the Archbishop of Canterbury led to a major conflict with Pope Innocent III. From 1208 to 1214, England was under a papal interdict, and John was excommunicated from 1209. In 1213, facing the threat of a French invasion, John submitted, making England a papal fief and paying an annual tribute. This secured him crucial papal support for the remainder of his reign.

### The Barons' War and Magna Carta
The failure of John's 1214 French campaign, culminating in the allied defeat at Bouvines, triggered a baronial revolt. In 1215, rebel barons captured London and forced John to agree to Magna Carta at Runnymede. The charter limited royal authority and protected certain baronial rights, but neither side complied. The Pope annulled the charter, and civil war erupted. John died in 1216 during the conflict, which was eventually won by his son Henry III's supporters.

## Terms
- **Angevin Empire**: The collection of territories (including England, Normandy, and Aquitaine) held by Henry II and his sons, named for the Angevin title.
- **Scutage**: A feudal tax paid by a vassal in lieu of military service to their lord.
- **Interdict**: A papal sanction that suspends religious services in a territory.
- **Magna Carta**: The 1215 charter that limited the king's power and is considered a foundation of English constitutional law.
- **Vis et voluntas**: Latin for "force and will," describing the Angevin style of arbitrary royal rule.
- **Ira et malevolentia**: Latin for "anger and ill-will," the king's prerogative to express displeasure and punish subjects.
- **Routiers**: Mercenary soldiers, often from Brabant, hired for campaigns.
- **Demesne**: Land directly owned and administered by the king or a lord, as opposed to land granted to tenants.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Historians debate the extent of John's personal responsibility for the loss of Normandy versus structural factors like the Capetians' growing power.
- The fate of Arthur of Brittany remains uncertain; while modern historians believe John had him murdered, the exact circumstances are not known.
- Interpretations of Magna Carta have shifted: it is now often seen as a failed peace treaty between partisan factions rather than a purely constitutional document.
- There is ongoing debate about the nature of John's Irish policies and the stability of his rule there by 1216.
