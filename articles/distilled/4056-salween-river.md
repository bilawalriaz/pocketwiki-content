# Salween River

## Overview
The Salween is a major Southeast Asian river, approximately 3,289 km long, flowing from the Tibetan Plateau through China and Myanmar to the Andaman Sea. It is one of the world's most ecologically diverse river basins and a critical water source for agriculture and fisheries. Historically, it has defined political frontiers and remains a focal point for ethnic conflict, conservation efforts, and controversial dam proposals.

## Timeline
- **c. 5 million years ago** — The river's course begins to form due to the collision of the Indian subcontinent with Asia.
- **c. 1.5 million years ago** — Volcanic activity diverts the river west towards the Andaman Sea, creating its modern path.
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Mon people migrate south from China, settling in the Salween delta.
- **c. 1000 BCE** — Ancestors of the Karen migrate down the river area from the Tibetan Plateau.
- **1060s** — King Anawrahta of the Pagan kingdom conquers the Salween delta, extending Burmese rule.
- **1548** — Burmese King Bayinnaung's army crosses the Salween to invade Siam.
- **1826** — After the First Anglo-Burmese War, Britain gains control of the Tenasserim coast south of the Salween.
- **1942** — Japanese forces invade Burma, crossing the Salween at Mawlamyine.
- **1944-45** — Allied forces launch the Salween Campaign to retake Burma from the Japanese.
- **1948** — Burma gains independence; ethnic insurgencies begin in the Salween basin.
- **1970s** — Myanmar and Thai governments begin planning massive hydroelectric dams on the river.
- **2016** — China drops plans to dam the upper Salween, favoring a national park.

## Body

### Geography and Naming
The Salween basin covers about 283,500 km², with 48% in China, 44% in Myanmar, and 8% in Thailand. Known as the Nu Jiang in China and Thanlwin in Myanmar, "Salween" is a 19th-century British anglicization. It is one of Asia's least fragmented large rivers, with only small dams in its headwaters. Average flow at the Myanmar-Thailand border is 6,300 m³/s, with 89% occurring during the monsoon season.

### Geology and Formation
The Salween's course formed about 5 million years ago as the Indian subcontinent collided with Asia. Prior to this, the upper Irrawaddy, Salween, Mekong, and Yangtze may have flowed into the Red River. About 1.5 million years ago, volcanic activity diverted the Salween west. Its deep canyons were carved as it incised into rising mountain ranges along parallel fault zones.

### Ecology
The basin is one of the world's most ecologically diverse regions, supporting over 25% of global animal species. The Three Parallel Rivers region in Yunnan, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, is a plant diversity hotspot. The river hosts about 151 fish species and has the greatest turtle diversity of any river. It provides habitat for endangered species like the snow leopard, black snub-nosed monkey, and Indochinese tiger. The world's smallest bat, Kitti's hog-nosed bat, is found in Thai limestone caves along the river.

### Economic Uses
The Salween sustains agriculture, particularly rice cultivation in its delta, the basin's most productive region. It supports subsistence and commercial fisheries. Logging of tropical hardwoods like teak has been a major industry since the British colonial period, though deforestation has increased. Mining for limestone, sand, and gravel also occurs, with associated pollution concerns.

### History and Conflict
Human presence along the upper Salween dates to at least 31,000–39,000 years ago. For centuries, the river defined frontiers between Burmese empires, Siam, and China. After British colonization, Mawlamyine served as the capital of British Burma. Since independence in 1948, the basin has been a battleground for the Myanmar civil war, with ethnic militias in Shan and Karen states fighting the central government. The river remains a barrier for refugees.

### Dams and Development
The Salween has extremely high hydropower potential. Since the 1970s, Myanmar and Thailand have planned seven massive dams on the main stem, with a combined capacity exceeding 20,000 MW. The largest proposed is the Mong Ton Dam in Shan State. China planned 13 dams on the upper Salween but dropped these plans in 2016, citing earthquake risks. Local communities and activists oppose the dams, citing displacement, loss of agricultural land, and ecological damage. A "Salween Peace Park" has been proposed as a sustainable alternative.

## Terms
- **Hydropower potential**: The theoretical capacity of a river to generate electricity from falling water.
- **Three Parallel Rivers**: A UNESCO World Heritage Site in Yunnan, China, where the Salween, Mekong, and Yangtze flow through parallel gorges.
- **Shan States**: A historical collection of semi-independent principalities in the middle Salween region, now part of Myanmar's Shan State.
- **Karst**: A landscape formed from the dissolution of soluble rocks like limestone, characterized by caves, sinkholes, and underground drainage.
- **Monsoon season**: The seasonal prevailing wind in South and Southeast Asia, bringing heavy rainfall from mid-May to November.
- **Terrane**: A fragment of crustal material formed on, or broken off from, one tectonic plate and accreted to another.
- **Endemic**: A species native to and found only in a specific geographic area.
- **Salween Peace Park**: A proposed community-managed conservation area in Myanmar aimed at protecting the river's ecology and indigenous lands.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The future of dam projects in Myanmar and Thailand remains uncertain, with conflict between development plans and local opposition.
- The environmental and social impacts of proposed dams, including sediment trapping, loss of fisheries, and displacement, are major points of contention.
- The effectiveness of conservation measures versus pressures from logging, mining, and agricultural expansion is debated.
- The river's role in ongoing ethnic conflicts and whether development projects exacerbate or mitigate tensions is a critical open question.
