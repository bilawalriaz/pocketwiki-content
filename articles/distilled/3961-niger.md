# Niger

## Overview
Niger is a large, landlocked country in West Africa, predominantly covered by the Sahara Desert. Its population, one of the world's youngest and fastest-growing, is concentrated in the south and west. The nation's history is marked by its position at a crossroads of trans-Saharan trade, successive empires, French colonial rule, and a post-independence era characterized by political instability, including multiple military coups. Its economy is heavily reliant on subsistence agriculture and uranium exports, and it faces significant challenges from poverty, climate change, and regional insecurity.

## Timeline
- **c. 1230–17th century** — Mali Empire dominates parts of the region.
- **11th–1591** — Songhai Empire controls much of western Niger.
- **c. 1449–1906** — Sultanate of Aïr (Agadez) is a key trading state.
- **8th–19th century** — Kanem–Bornu Empire dominates the Lake Chad region.
- **1804–1903** — Sokoto Caliphate conquers most Hausa states.
- **1922** — Niger becomes a distinct colony within French West Africa.
- **3 August 1960** — Niger gains independence from France.
- **1974** — First military coup led by Seyni Kountché.
- **1991** — National Conference initiates transition to multi-party democracy.
- **1996, 1999, 2010** — Further military coups occur.
- **2011** — Seventh Republic established after a 2010 coup.
- **26 July 2023** — Military coup overthrows President Mohamed Bazoum.

## Body

### Pre-colonial Empires and Kingdoms
The territory of modern Niger was influenced by several powerful states. The Mali Empire (13th–17th century) and later the Songhai Empire (11th–1591) controlled western regions, with the Songhai conquering Agadez in 1496. To the east, the Kanem–Bornu Empire (8th–19th century) dominated the Lake Chad basin. In the north, the Sultanate of Aïr (c. 1449–1906) was a strategic trading hub. The south was home to the Hausa Kingdoms, which were largely conquered in the early 19th century by the Sokoto Caliphate following a jihad led by Usman Dan Fodio. Some Hausa states, like Katsina, survived by moving south to areas like Maradi.

### Colonial Rule and Independence
French exploration in the 19th century led to military expeditions, notably the brutal Voulet–Chanoine Mission (1899–1900). France established the Military Territory of Niger in 1904, with borders finalized by the 1930s. Colonial rule used indirect governance, favoring groups like the Zarma. Resistance, such as the Kaocen Revolt (1916–1917), was suppressed. After World War II, political parties formed, and Niger became autonomous in 1958. It gained full independence on 3 August 1960 under President Hamani Diori.

### Post-Independence Political Instability
Niger's post-colonial history has been marked by coups. Diori's single-party rule ended in a 1974 coup by Colonel Seyni Kountché, who promoted a "development society" funded by uranium. After his death, his successor Ali Saibou oversaw a transition to multi-party democracy, leading to the Third Republic in 1993. This period saw political paralysis and Tuareg rebellions. A 1996 coup brought Ibrahim Baré Maïnassara to power; he was assassinated in 1999. The Fifth Republic (1999–2009) under Mamadou Tandja was stable until he attempted to extend his term, triggering a 2010 coup. The Seventh Republic (2011–2023) under Mahamadou Issoufou and then Mohamed Bazoum faced severe security threats from jihadist groups. The most recent coup occurred on 26 July 2023, led by General Omar Tiani, establishing the current military junta.

### Economy and Demographics
Niger's economy is based on subsistence agriculture and uranium mining; it was the main uranium supplier to the EU in 2021. The country is one of the world's poorest, with high population growth (3.3%) and fertility rates (6.49 births per woman). The population is predominantly young and rural. The Hausa are the largest ethnic group (55.4%), followed by Zarma/Songhay (21%) and Tuareg (9.3%). French was the official language until 2023, when Hausa was given that status. Islam is practiced by 99.3% of the population.

## Terms
- **Semi-presidential system**: A system of government where a president shares executive power with a prime minister.
- **Coup d'état**: A sudden, illegal seizure of power from a government.
- **Trans-Saharan trade**: Historic trade routes crossing the Sahara Desert, linking sub-Saharan Africa with North Africa and the Mediterranean.
- **Sokoto Caliphate**: An Islamic empire in present-day Nigeria and surrounding areas, founded in 1809 after a Fulani jihad.
- **Sultanate of Aïr**: A Tuareg-led state centered in Agadez, a key node on trans-Saharan trade routes.
- **Tuareg**: A large Berber ethnic group traditionally inhabiting the Sahara and Sahel regions.
- **Hausa**: The largest ethnic group in Niger, also predominant in northern Nigeria.
- **Uranium**: A radioactive metal; Niger is a major global producer and exporter.
- **ECOWAS**: The Economic Community of West African States, a regional political and economic union.

## Debates and open questions
- The origin of the Niger River's name is uncertain, with theories linking it to a Tuareg phrase or the writings of Ptolemy, but not the Latin word for "black."
- The exact number of deaths during the 1990–1995 Tuareg Rebellion is disputed, with estimates ranging from 70 to 1,000.
- The 2023 coup and the junta's subsequent pivot away from France and the West towards Russia (including cooperation with the Wagner Group) represent a major, ongoing shift in Niger's foreign relations and geopolitical alignment.
