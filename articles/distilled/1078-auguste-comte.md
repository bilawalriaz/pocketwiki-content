# Auguste Comte

## Overview

Auguste Comte (1798–1857) was a French philosopher who formulated positivism, the doctrine that only knowledge verified through empirical observation and scientific method is valid. Regarded as the first modern philosopher of science, he sought to reorganize society on scientific principles after the social upheaval of the French Revolution. His work laid the foundation for sociology as a discipline and influenced 19th-century thinkers including John Stuart Mill, George Eliot, and Herbert Spencer.

## Timeline

- **1798** — Born in Montpellier under the French First Republic
- **1817** — Becomes secretary to Henri de Saint-Simon in Paris
- **1822** — Publishes *Plan de travaux scientifiques nécessaires pour réorganiser la société*
- **1826** — Hospitalized for mental health; stabilized by Jean-Étienne Dominique Esquirol
- **1830–1842** — Publishes *Cours de Philosophie Positive* (six volumes)
- **1838** — Coins the term "sociologie"
- **1848** — Publishes *Discours sur l'ensemble du positivisme*
- **1851–1854** — Publishes four volumes of *Système de politique positive*
- **1856** — Publishes first volume of *La Synthèse Subjective*
- **1857** — Dies in Paris from stomach cancer

## Body

### Positivism and the Classification of Sciences

Comte developed positivism as a response to the social disorder following the French Revolution. He argued that society must be reorganized according to scientific principles, with the physical sciences serving as a model. In *The Course in Positive Philosophy* (1830–1842), he classified the sciences hierarchically based on their degree of "positivity"—the extent to which phenomena can be exactly determined. Mathematics, being the most exact, sits at the top, followed by astronomy, physics, chemistry, biology, and finally sociology, which he called the "Queen science" because of its complexity and importance.

Comte also formulated the "encyclopedic law," which posits that knowledge should be organized in a systematic, hierarchical manner. He distinguished between social statics (how society holds together) and social dynamics (how society changes), comparing social structures to the human body. Language, religion, and division of labor function as connective tissues in society.

### The Law of Three Stages

Comte proposed that both individual human thought and societal development progress through three stages: the theological, metaphysical, and positive. In the **theological stage**, phenomena are explained through supernatural agents—beginning with fetishism, advancing to polytheism, and culminating in monotheism. This stage was necessary as a provisional framework that allowed early humans to organize observations and escape a "vicious circle" between observation and theory.

The **metaphysical stage** replaces supernatural causes with abstract entities. It serves as a transitional phase, deemed least important by Comte, because the human mind cannot leap directly from theological to positive thinking. During this stage, people begin to notice facts themselves, preparing the ground for scientific inquiry.

The **positive stage** is characterized by the recognition that phenomena are governed by natural laws discoverable through reason and observation. Comte believed his own era (the 19th century) had entered this stage. He emphasized that observation is guided by theory, and that the dead "govern the living" through the cumulative nature of scientific knowledge.

### The Religion of Humanity

In his later years, Comte developed the "Religion of Humanity," a secular faith designed to fulfill the social cohesion once provided by traditional religion. Influenced by his unrequited love for Clotilde de Vaux, he created rituals, a calendar reform (the positivist calendar), and a catechism. This system was rejected by many of his followers, including John Stuart Mill, who distinguished between the "good Comte" (author of the *Course*) and the "bad Comte" (author of the religious system). Despite its limited success, it influenced later secular humanist movements.

### Other Contributions and Influences

Comte may have coined the term "altruism," defining it as a moral theory that regards the good of others as the end of moral action. He was influenced by counter-revolutionary thinkers like Joseph de Maistre, sharing concerns about moral anarchy but rejecting Catholicism and royalism. In his later works, he reassessed Islam, praising its doctrinal simplicity and proposing that elements like the qibla (fixed direction of worship) be adopted by the Religion of Humanity. His confidant Pierre Laffitte even referred to Comte's apartment as "our Kaaba."

## Terms

- **Positivism**: The epistemological doctrine that only knowledge verified through empirical observation and scientific method is valid.
- **Law of Three Stages**: Comte's theory that human thought and societal development progress through theological, metaphysical, and positive stages.
- **Sociology**: The science of society, coined by Comte in 1838; he viewed it as the "Queen science" and final science.
- **Enyclopedic Law**: Comte's principle that knowledge should be organized systematically and hierarchically.
- **Social Statics**: The branch of sociology studying the forces that hold society together.
- **Social Dynamics**: The branch of sociology studying the causes of social change.
- **Religion of Humanity**: Comte's secular religious system designed to replace traditional worship with a cult of humanity.
- **Altruism**: A moral theory, possibly coined by Comte, that regards the good of others as the end of moral action.
- **Sociocracy**: Comte's concept of governance by people who know each other—friends or allies.
- **Positivity**: The degree to which phenomena can be exactly determined, used by Comte as a criterion for ranking the sciences.

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars debate the extent to which Comte appropriated ideas from his mentor Henri de Saint-Simon, with some arguing that Comte's contributions were derivative. The originality of his coining of "sociology" is also questioned, as Emmanuel Joseph Sieyès had used the term in 1780 with a different meaning. Comte's prediction that the chemical composition of stars could never be determined was proven wrong within thirty years by the development of spectroscopy, highlighting the limitations of his scientific foresight. Additionally, his grand vision of sociology as the central science integrating all knowledge has not been realized, and elements of his work are now viewed as eccentric or unscientific. The influence of his later religious ideas on modern secular humanism remains a subject of scholarly discussion.
