# Marie Curie

## Overview
Marie Curie (1867–1934), born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw under Russian rule, was a physicist and chemist who pioneered the study of radioactivity—a term she coined. She remains the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different scientific fields: Physics (1903, shared with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel) for research on radiation phenomena, and Chemistry (1911) for isolating the elements polonium and radium. Her work overturned the assumption that atoms were indivisible, laid the foundation for nuclear physics and cancer radiotherapy, and established the Curie Institutes in Paris and Warsaw as enduring research centres. Overcoming systemic barriers as a woman in science, she became the first female professor at the University of Paris (1906) and directed mobile X-ray units ("petites Curies") during World War I. She died of aplastic anaemia caused by chronic radiation exposure; her notebooks remain radioactive today.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Maria Skłodowska was born into a family of Polish educators impoverished by patriotic involvement in national uprisings against Russian rule. Her father, Władysław, taught mathematics and physics, bringing laboratory equipment home after Russian authorities banned it from Polish schools. Her mother died of tuberculosis in 1878; her eldest sister Zofia died of typhus in 1876. These losses led Maria to abandon Catholicism for agnosticism. Barred from regular university because she was a woman, she studied at the clandestine Flying University in Warsaw and worked as a governess to fund her sister Bronisława’s medical studies in Paris, with the agreement that Bronisława would later support her. A failed romance with her employer’s son, Kazimierz Żorawski, and a denial of a position at Kraków University due to sexism cemented her decision to leave Poland. In late 1891, she arrived in Paris, adopting the name Marie, and enrolled at the University of Paris, living in poverty while earning degrees in physics (1893) and mathematics (1894).

### Partnership with Pierre Curie and the Discovery of Radioactivity
Introduced by Józef Wierusz-Kowalski, Marie began working in Pierre Curie’s laboratory at ESPCI Paris. Their shared scientific passion led to marriage in July 1895. For her doctoral thesis, Marie investigated Henri Becquerel’s discovery of spontaneous uranium radiation. Using Pierre’s sensitive electrometer, she measured the ionization of air by uranium rays, proving the activity depended solely on the quantity of uranium present. She hypothesized the radiation originated from the atom itself—a radical challenge to the dogma of indivisible atoms. Pierre abandoned his crystal research to join her in 1898. Analyzing pitchblende and chalcolite, they found these ores far more radioactive than their uranium content could explain, predicting new elements. In July 1898 they announced **polonium** (named for Poland); in December 1898, **radium** (from Latin *radius*, "ray"). They coined the term **radioactivity**. To prove their existence, they processed tonnes of pitchblende in a leaky shed, isolating 0.1 g of radium chloride by 1902 and pure radium metal by 1910. Polonium, with a 138-day half-life, eluded isolation. Between 1898 and 1902 they published 32 papers, noting radium destroyed tumour cells faster than healthy ones.

### Nobel Prizes and Institutional Struggles
The 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics was initially nominated for Pierre and Becquerel only; Swedish mathematician Magnus Gösta Mittag-Leffler intervened to add Marie, making her the first female laureate. The prize money funded their first lab assistant. In 1906, Pierre was killed by a horse-drawn carriage. The University of Paris offered Marie his chair, making her its first female professor. She drove the creation of the Radium Institute (Curie Institute), a joint venture of the University and Pasteur Institute, completed in 1914. In 1911, she won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for isolating radium and polonium, becoming the first person to win two Nobels. The award was nearly derailed by a press scandal over her affair with physicist Paul Langevin; the Nobel chair, Svante Arrhenius, urged her to skip the ceremony, but she attended, asserting the prize honored her science, not her private life. The French Academy of Sciences rejected her membership by one or two votes in 1911, electing Édouard Branly instead; a woman (Marguerite Perey, her student) was not elected until 1962.

### World War I and Radiology
Recognizing that immediate surgery saved limbs and lives, Curie mastered radiology, anatomy, and automotive mechanics to create mobile X-ray units ("petites Curies"). As director of the Red Cross Radiology Service, she established France’s first military radiology centre by late 1914. Assisted by her 17-year-old daughter Irène, she deployed 20 mobile vehicles and 200 stationary units in the first year, later training women as aides. She also produced radon-filled needles for sterilizing infected tissue, using her own one-gram radium supply. Over a million wounded soldiers were treated. Despite this, she received no formal recognition from the French government. She attempted to donate her Nobel medals to the war effort (the Bank refused) and invested her prize money in war bonds.

### Postwar Leadership and Legacy
After the war, Curie led the Radium Institute to become one of the world’s four major radioactivity labs (alongside Cavendish, Vienna, and Kaiser Wilhelm Institutes). It produced four more Nobel laureates, including her daughter Irène Joliot-Curie and son-in-law Frédéric Joliot-Curie. She served on the League of Nations’ International Committee on Intellectual Cooperation (1922–1934) alongside Einstein and Lorentz. U.S. tours in 1921 and 1929 raised funds to equip the Warsaw Radium Institute (opened 1932). She defined the international radioactivity standard, the **curie**, and authored *Radiology in War* (1919) and a biography of Pierre (1923). She died in 1934 of aplastic anaemia; a 1995 exhumation suggested her wartime X-ray work, rather than radium ingestion, was the primary cause. Her papers and cookbooks remain highly radioactive, stored in lead-lined boxes.

## Terms
- **Radioactivity**: Term coined by the Curies for the spontaneous emission of radiation by atomic nuclei; discovered by Becquerel (1896), systematized by Marie Curie.
- **Polonium (Po, Z=84)**: First element discovered by the Curies (July 1898), named for Poland; chemically similar to bismuth; half-life 138 days.
- **Radium (Ra, Z=88)**: Second element discovered by the Curies (December 1898), isolated as pure metal in 1910; chemically similar to barium; intense radioactivity made it vital for early cancer therapy and atomic physics.
- **Pitchblende**: Uranium ore (primarily UO₂) in which the Curies detected excess radioactivity, leading to the discovery of polonium and radium.
- **Electrometer**: Sensitive instrument for measuring electric charge; developed by Pierre and his brother Jacques, used by Marie to quantify ionization caused by uranium rays.
- **Curie (Ci)**: Unit of radioactivity (3.7×10¹⁰ decays/second), originally based on the activity of 1 g of radium-226; named for Pierre and/or Marie.
- **Petites Curies**: Mobile radiological units (vehicles with X-ray apparatus and generators) developed by Marie Curie for field hospitals in WWI.
- **Radium Institute (Institut du Radium / Curie Institute)**: Research centre in Paris founded 1914 (Pasteur Institute + University of Paris), directed by Marie; sister institute in Warsaw opened 1932.
- **Flying University**: Clandestine Polish institution of higher learning admitting women; where Maria Skłodowska studied before leaving for Paris.
- **Aplastic anaemia**: Bone marrow failure causing deficiency of all blood cell types; Curie’s cause of death, attributed to chronic ionizing radiation exposure.

## Debates and open questions
- **Attribution of the "curie" unit**: The international commission that named the unit never clarified whether it honours Pierre, Marie, or both.
- **Primary cause of death**: The 1995 exhumation by OPRI concluded she could not have absorbed lethal radium levels internally, suggesting her fatal aplastic anaemia stemmed mainly from unshielded X-ray work in WWI field hospitals rather than laboratory radium handling.
- **French Academy of Sciences rejection (1911)**: Whether the narrow loss (1–2 votes) was driven by scientific merit, xenophobia, anti-Semitic rumours (she was Catholic-born, agnostic), or the Langevin affair remains debated; the Academy did not elect a woman until 1962.
- **Patent decision**: Curie deliberately refused to patent the radium-isolation process to keep research open; historians debate how this altruism affected the development of the radium industry and her personal finances.
- **Langevin affair impact**: The 1911 scandal was exploited by academic opponents and right-wing press; its role in the Academy rejection and the Nobel committee’s attempt to bar her from the Chemistry ceremony illustrates the intersection of gender, nationality, and morality in early 20th-century science.
