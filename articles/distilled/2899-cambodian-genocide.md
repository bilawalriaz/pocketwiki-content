# Cambodian genocide

## Overview
The Cambodian genocide (1975–1979) was the systematic persecution and mass killing of Cambodian citizens by the Khmer Rouge regime under Pol Pot. It resulted in the deaths of approximately 1.5 to 2 million people, about 25% of Cambodia's 1975 population, through executions, forced labor, starvation, and disease. The genocide ended with a Vietnamese invasion and remains a defining Cold War event, illustrating the catastrophic consequences of radical ideology and geopolitical maneuvering.

## Timeline
- **1968** — Khmer Rouge launches nationwide insurgency.
- **1970** — Premier Lon Nol deposes King Sihanouk, establishing the Khmer Republic.
- **1970–1973** — Massive U.S. bombing campaign devastates rural Cambodia.
- **17 April 1975** — Khmer Rouge captures Phnom Penh, ending the Cambodian Civil War.
- **1975** — Khmer Rouge empties cities, marches population to rural labor camps.
- **1976** — Country renamed Democratic Kampuchea.
- **June 1975** — Pol Pot meets Mao Zedong in Beijing, receiving ideological approval.
- **1978** — Khmer Rouge conducts major internal purges, especially in the Eastern Zone.
- **December 1978** — Vietnam launches full-scale invasion of Cambodia.
- **January 1979** — Vietnamese forces topple the Khmer Rouge regime, ending the genocide.
- **2003** — UN and Cambodian government agree to establish the Khmer Rouge Tribunal.
- **2010–2019** — Tribunal convicts senior Khmer Rouge leaders of crimes against humanity and genocide.

## Body

### Rise of the Khmer Rouge
The Khmer Rouge insurgency grew during the Cambodian Civil War (1968–1975). After Lon Nol's 1970 coup, deposed King Sihanouk allied with the Khmer Rouge from exile, boosting its recruitment. A massive U.S. bombing campaign (1970–1973) devastated the countryside, with death estimates from 30,000 to 500,000. Historians debate its impact, but some argue it increased peasant support for the Khmer Rouge by destroying rural society and fueling anti-American sentiment. By 1975, the Khmer Rouge, with critical support from North Vietnam and China, defeated Lon Nol's forces.

### Ideology and International Support
Pol Pot's ideology blended Marxism-Leninism with ultra-Maoism, aiming to create a self-sufficient agrarian socialist republic. China was the primary backer, providing at least 90% of foreign aid, including $1 billion in 1975. Pol Pot received political training in China and met Mao in June 1975, who endorsed his radical path. Despite warnings from Chinese Premier Zhou Enlai against repeating China's Great Leap Forward mistakes, the Khmer Rouge implemented its own catastrophic economic plan, "Maha Lout Ploh."

### The Genocide (1975–1979)
After seizing power, the Khmer Rouge emptied cities and forced the population into rural labor camps. The regime targeted perceived enemies: former officials, intellectuals, professionals, and ethnic and religious minorities. This "autogenocide" involved mass executions, forced labor, starvation, and disease. The Khmer Rouge operated 196 prisons, including the notorious Security Prison 21 (S-21), where torture and medical experiments were common. Victims were often executed at "Killing Fields" with simple tools to save bullets. The regime also forcibly recruited children to commit atrocities.

### Targeting of Ethnic and Religious Minorities
The Khmer Rouge systematically persecuted ethnic minorities, including Vietnamese, Chinese, Cham Muslims, and Thai Cambodians. The Cham, a Muslim minority, faced a particularly fierce extermination campaign aimed at destroying their language, culture, and religion. An estimated 90,000 to 500,000 Cham were killed. Chinese Cambodians, stereotyped as capitalist traders, were also massacred, with their population halved from 425,000 in 1975 to 200,000 by 1979. All religions were banned, and up to 50,000 Buddhist monks were killed.

### Aftermath and Justice
The genocide ended with a Vietnamese invasion in 1978–1979. The subsequent Khmer Rouge Tribunal (Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia), established in 2003, convicted senior leaders Kang Kek Iew, Nuon Chea, and Khieu Samphan of crimes against humanity and genocide. Pol Pot died in 1998 before trial. The tribunal's work, supported by extensive documentation from the Documentation Center of Cambodia, has been crucial for historical record and victim recognition, though it has also faced criticism for its limited scope.

## Terms
- **Khmer Rouge**: The communist movement and regime that ruled Cambodia from 1975 to 1979, led by Pol Pot.
- **Democratic Kampuchea**: The official name of Cambodia under Khmer Rouge rule (1976–1979).
- **Autogenocide**: A term for a genocide where perpetrators and victims are largely from the same ethnic group.
- **Killing Fields**: Sites across Cambodia where mass executions were carried out and victims buried in mass graves.
- **Security Prison 21 (S-21)**: A former high school in Phnom Penh converted into a notorious torture and execution center.
- **Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia (ECCC)**: The UN-backed tribunal established to try senior Khmer Rouge leaders.
- **Maha Lout Ploh**: The Khmer Rouge's economic plan, modeled on China's Great Leap Forward, which led to mass starvation.
- **Cham**: An ethnic and religious minority in Cambodia, predominantly Muslim, systematically targeted for extermination.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Death Toll Estimates**: Figures vary widely, from 1 million to 3 million, with most scholarly estimates between 1.5 and 2 million. The exact proportion of deaths from execution versus starvation, disease, and forced labor is debated.
- **Genocide Classification**: Some scholars, like David Chandler and Michael Vickery, argue the atrocities were not genocide but rather "chauvinist" policies or spontaneous excesses. Others, like Ben Kiernan and Gregory Stanton, insist it meets the legal definition of genocide, particularly regarding the Cham and Vietnamese.
- **Role of U.S. Bombing**: Historians debate whether the massive U.S. bombing campaign (1970–1973) was a primary cause of the Khmer Rouge's rise by destabilizing the country and fueling recruitment, or a secondary factor.
- **China's Responsibility**: China has consistently denied supporting the Khmer Rouge's "wrong policies" and has never apologized for its extensive military and economic aid during the genocide. The full extent of its knowledge and influence remains a point of contention.
- **Tribunal Limitations**: The ECCC has been criticized for its high cost, slow pace, and limited number of convictions, leaving many perpetrators unaccountable.
