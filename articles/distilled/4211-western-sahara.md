# Western Sahara

## Overview
Western Sahara is a disputed territory in Northwest Africa, considered the last African colony without independence. Its status remains unresolved, with sovereignty contested between Morocco, which controls most of the territory, and the Polisario Front, which seeks full independence and is recognized by the UN as the legitimate representative of the Sahrawi people. The conflict has led to a long-standing stalemate over a promised self-determination referendum, a divided territory, and significant humanitarian and geopolitical implications.

## Timeline
- **1884** — Spain establishes a colony in Western Sahara.
- **1975** — Spain signs the Madrid Accords, transferring administration to Morocco and Mauritania.
- **1975** — Morocco initiates the Green March; war erupts with the Polisario Front.
- **1979** — Mauritania withdraws its claims; Morocco occupies the entire territory.
- **1991** — A UN-sponsored ceasefire is agreed, with a referendum on independence planned.
- **1997** — The Houston Agreement attempts to revive the stalled referendum process.
- **2003** — The UN-backed Baker II plan is accepted by Polisario but rejected by Morocco.
- **2006** — Morocco proposes an autonomy plan under its sovereignty.
- **2010** — Moroccan security forces dismantle the Gadaym Izik protest camp.
- **2020** — The ceasefire breaks down; the US recognizes Moroccan sovereignty.
- **2023** — Israel recognizes Moroccan sovereignty over Western Sahara.
- **2025** — The UN Security Council references Morocco's autonomy proposal as a basis for negotiations.

## Body

### Colonial History and the Onset of Conflict
The territory was colonized by Spain from 1884 until 1975. As decolonization pressures mounted, Spain signed the Madrid Accords with Morocco and Mauritania, attempting to transfer administration despite an International Court of Justice advisory opinion that neither country had sovereignty. This triggered a war with the pro-independence Polisario Front, which proclaimed the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic (SADR). Mauritania withdrew in 1979, and Morocco secured control of about 70% of the territory, constructing a 2,700 km sand berm to contain guerrilla fighters.

### The Stalled Peace Process
A 1991 ceasefire, overseen by the UN mission MINURSO, was meant to lead to a referendum on independence or integration with Morocco. The process stalled almost immediately over voter eligibility, with the Polisario insisting on the 1974 Spanish census and Morocco demanding a broader electorate. Multiple UN mediation attempts, including the Baker Plans, failed. Morocco has since rejected any referendum including independence, offering instead an autonomy plan under its sovereignty, which it has promoted internationally. The Polisario, backed by Algeria, continues to demand a self-determination referendum.

### Current Status and Geopolitics
The territory remains divided by the Moroccan Berm, with Morocco controlling the west and the Polisario Front the east. The SADR government is in exile in Tindouf, Algeria. In the 2020s, diplomatic momentum shifted toward Morocco, with several states opening consulates in Western Sahara and major powers like the US, France, and Spain backing its autonomy proposal. The UN Security Council now references this proposal as a basis for negotiations. The ceasefire officially broke down in late 2020, leading to ongoing low-level clashes.

### Economy and Resources
Western Sahara's economy is based on fishing and phosphate mining, with the Bou Craa mine producing about 10% of Morocco's output. The exploitation of these resources is legally contentious, as a 2002 UN legal opinion stated that activities disregarding the wishes of the Sahrawi people would violate international law. The EU's top court has ruled that products from Western Sahara must be labeled with their territory of origin.

### Demographics and Culture
The indigenous population are the Sahrawis, a nomadic, Hassaniya-speaking people of mixed Arab-Berber heritage. The 1974 Spanish census recorded about 74,000 Sahrawis. War caused massive displacement, creating a large refugee population in Tindouf, Algeria. Moroccan settlement in the occupied territories has altered the demographic balance. Sahrawi culture is characterized by nomadic traditions, Sunni Islam, and a significant role for women in society and resistance movements.

## Terms
- **Polisario Front**: A Sahrawi nationalist movement seeking independence for Western Sahara, recognized by the UN as the legitimate representative of the people.
- **Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic (SADR)**: The self-declared state proclaimed by the Polisario Front, with a government-in-exile in Tindouf, Algeria.
- **MINURSO**: The United Nations Mission for the Referendum in Western Sahara, established to monitor the 1991 ceasefire and organize a self-determination referendum.
- **The Berm**: A 2,700 km-long Moroccan sand wall fortified with landmines, dividing the territory between Moroccan-controlled and Polisario-controlled zones.
- **Green March**: A 1975 mass demonstration of 350,000 unarmed Moroccans into Western Sahara, marking the start of Morocco's military intervention.
- **Madrid Accords**: A 1975 agreement between Spain, Morocco, and Mauritania to transfer administration of Western Sahara, which was rejected by the Polisario Front.
- **Baker Plans**: UN-mediated proposals (2000, 2003) for a transitional autonomy period followed by a referendum, which were ultimately rejected by Morocco.
- **Hassaniya**: The Arabic dialect spoken by the Sahrawi people and in much of Mauritania.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Voter Eligibility**: The core dispute over the stalled referendum is who qualifies to vote, based on the 1974 Spanish census versus a broader electorate including Moroccan settlers and tribes.
- **Sovereignty vs. Self-Determination**: Morocco insists on its "inalienable right" to the territory and offers only autonomy, while the Polisario Front and Algeria demand a referendum with independence as an option.
- **Resource Exploitation**: The legality of Morocco's exploitation of Western Sahara's natural resources (phosphates, fisheries) is contested under international law, with courts and UN opinions stating it requires the consent of the Sahrawi people.
- **Role of Algeria**: Morocco views the Polisario as an Algerian proxy, while Algeria maintains it is merely a host for refugees and supports a UN-led solution.
