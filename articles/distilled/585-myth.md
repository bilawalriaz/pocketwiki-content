# Myth

## Overview
Myth is a genre of folklore comprising narratives that play a fundamental role in a society, distinct from the colloquial sense of a false belief; for scholars, a narrative's veracity is irrelevant to its status as myth. Myths are often endorsed by authorities, feature non-human protagonists (gods, demigods), and are set in a remote, primordial past. They serve to explain origins (cosmogony, customs, institutions), sanctify rituals, and provide behavioral models. The academic study of myth has evolved from 19th-century theories framing myth as obsolete "primitive" thought (nature mythology, ritualism) to 20th-century structuralist, functionalist, and psychoanalytic approaches, and finally to 21st-century postmodern views emphasizing the plasticity of myths across media and rejecting the search for an "original version."

## Timeline
- **c. 320 BCE** — Euhemerus proposes euhemerism: myths are distorted accounts of historical humans deified over time.
- **5th century CE** — Fabius Planciades Fulgentius writes *Mythologiæ*, treating Greco-Roman myths as allegories requiring interpretation.
- **c. 1425** — John Lydgate's *Troy Book* provides the first attested English use of "mythology" (meaning exposition of fables).
- **1830** — First recorded use of the singular word "myth" in English (preceded by "mythology" and loanwords *mythos*, *mythus*).
- **19th century (late)** — Max Müller and Edward Burnett Tylor develop "nature mythology": myths as personifications of natural phenomena; Tylor posits cultural evolution from myth to science.
- **1899** — Sigmund Freud publishes *The Interpretation of Dreams*, introducing the Oedipus complex derived from Classical myth.
- **c. 1960s–1980s** — Structuralist era (Lévi-Strauss): myths reflect fixed mental structures (binary oppositions) rather than historical events.
- **1950s** — Roland Barthes publishes *Mythologies*, analyzing modern myths in popular culture; early post-structuralist approach.
- **Late 20th century** — Postmodern turn (e.g., A. K. Ramanujan): myths are inherently plastic; no "original version" exists; focus shifts from texts to transmedial narrative materials (*Erzählstoffe*).
- **21st century** — Hylistic myth research (Zgoll & Zgoll) formalizes analysis of minimal plot units (hylemes) for transmedial comparison; digital media expands mythological discourse.

## Body

### Etymology and Terminology
The word *myth* derives from Ancient Greek *mȳthos* ('speech', 'narrative', 'fiction'). *Mythología* ('story-telling') entered Latin via Fulgentius’s 5th-century *Mythologiæ*, passed into Middle French, and reached English as "mythology" by c. 1425 (Lydgate). Until the 17th–18th century, "mythology" meant moral fable or allegory understood as false; the singular "myth" appeared only in 1830. Related terms include **mythology** (a culture's myth corpus or its study), **mythography** (compilation/description of myths, e.g., Ovid, Snorri Sturluson), **mythos** (Aristotelian 'plot point' or a body of interconnected myths, sometimes preferred to avoid pejorative connotations), and **mythopoeia** (Tolkien's term for conscious myth-making).

### Definitions and Boundaries
Scholarly definitions vary. Lauri Honko defines myth as a religious account of beginnings and exemplary divine deeds that confirms societal values, sanctions ritual, and provides behavioral patterns. José Manuel Losada emphasizes a functional, symbolic narrative with a transcendent referent lacking historical testimony. The Zgolls define myth as polymorphic *Erzählstoff* (narrative material) combining transcendent interpretations into a *hyleme* sequence claiming relevance for the human condition. Myths differ from **legends** (usually human protagonists, historical basis) and **folktales** (often recognized as fiction, e.g., fairy tales), though boundaries blur (e.g., *Iliad*, *Aeneid*). Historical kernels can mythologize over centuries (Matter of Britain, Matter of France). Colloquially, "myth" implies falsehood—a pejorative usage originating in labeling other cultures' religions as incorrect—creating controversy when applied to living religious traditions. Biblical scholars use "myth" technically to describe actions of the "other-worldly in terms of this world" (Creation, Fall).

### Interpretive Theories
**Comparative mythology** systematically compares cultures to find common themes or a shared *protomythology*. **Functionalism** (Malinowski, Eliade, Honko) argues myths legitimize social norms ("mythic charter"), provide religious experience by returning participants to the mythical age, and validate rituals through reenactment. **Euhemerism** (Euhemerus, Herodotus) posits myths as distorted history: humans deified over retellings (e.g., wind-god Aeolus from a king teaching sailing). **Allegory** (Müller) suggests myths began as symbolic descriptions of nature (Apollo = sun) or philosophy (Athena = wisdom) later taken literally. **Personification** claims ancients worshiped natural forces (fire, air), gradually deifying them. **Ritualism** (Smith, Frazer) ties myth to ritual: myths arise to explain forgotten ritual origins (Frazer: magic → religion → science progression).

### History of the Discipline
**Ancient Greece:** Presocratics began critical interpretation; Euhemerus rationalized myths as history. Sallustius categorized myths (theological, physical, animistic, material, mixed). Plato criticized poetic myth's literal danger but used myths symbolically; Neoplatonists (Plutarch, Proclus, Damascius) developed allegorical exegesis. **Renaissance:** Revival of interest; early mythographies appear (e.g., *Theologia Mythologica*, 1532). **19th Century:** Driven by Romantic Nationalism (Jacob Grimm) and colonial encounters (Rigveda, Gilgamesh, Indigenous oral traditions). Evolutionary frameworks dominated: myth as primitive counterpart to science. **Nature Mythology** (Müller, Tylor): myths as "disease of language" or animistic explanations of nature; abandoned by modern scholarship for lack of evidence. **Ritual School** (Frazer): myths misinterpret magical rituals; progression from magic to religion to science. **20th Century:** **Psychoanalysis** (Freud's Oedipus complex, Jung's universal archetypes). **Structuralism** (Lévi-Strauss): myths as fixed mental structures (binary oppositions). **Functionalism/Structuralism synthesis** (Malinowski): myths as ideology/history linked to power. **Post-structuralism** (Barthes): myths exist in modern popular culture. **Religion:** Secularization allowed analysis of Abrahamic narratives as myth; theologians debated demythologization (Bultmann) vs. myth as vehicle of ultimate truth (Hyers, Eliade). **21st Century:** **Postmodernism/Pluralism:** Rejection of "original version" reconstruction (Ramanujan's "Three Hundred Ramayanas"); myths as inherently plastic *Erzählstoffe* adaptable across media (visual arts, landscape, film). **Hylistics** (Zgoll & Zgoll): formal method extracting minimal plot units (hylemes) for transmedial comparison and stratification analysis.

### Modernity and Media
Mythological discourse now reaches global audiences via digital media. Film transmits myths to mass audiences (Disney "reinventing" childhood myths; blockbusters using mythic archetypes—cautionary technology tales, creation stories—often framed as cyberpunk/fantasy). Authors adapt mythology for modern settings (Rick Riordan’s *Percy Jackson*). Fan studies scholars (Ika Willis, Henry Jenkins) link fan fiction to myth: reclaiming corporate-owned stories, critiquing hegemony, or creating a "universal story world" via *hyperseriality* (Johnston)—hyperconnected universes where characters/stories interweave. Jenkins argues fanfiction repairs a culture where contemporary myths are corporate property rather than folk property.

## Terms
- ****Myth**** — A genre of folklore: narratives fundamental to a society, often sacred, featuring non-human protagonists, set in a primordial past; veracity irrelevant to classification.
- ****Mythology**** — The collected myths of a cultural group (e.g., Greek mythology) or the scholarly study of such myths.
- ****Mythography**** — The compilation, description, or scholarly anthology of myths (e.g., Ovid’s *Metamorphoses*, Snorri’s *Prose Edda*).
- ****Euhemerism**** — Theory (Euhemerus, c. 320 BCE) that myths are distorted historical accounts where humans were gradually deified.
- ****Nature Mythology**** — 19th-century theory (Müller, Tylor) interpreting myths as personifications of natural phenomena; now largely abandoned.
- ****Structuralism**** — Mid-20th-century approach (Lévi-Strauss) analyzing myths as expressions of fixed mental structures, particularly binary oppositions.
- ****Mythic Charter**** — Malinowski’s concept: origin myths legitimize (charter) cultural norms and social institutions.
- ****Erzählstoff / Hyleme**** — Zgolls' terms: *Erzählstoff* = polymorphic narrative material; *hyleme* = minimal standardized plot unit for transmedial comparison.
- ****Hyperseriality**** — Sarah Iles Johnston’s term for a hyperconnected story universe where characters and narratives are interwoven (applied to fan fiction).
- ****Mythopoeia**** — Tolkien’s term for the conscious, artificial creation of mythology.

## Debates and open questions
- **Definition and Scope:** No consensus on a single definition; scholars debate whether myth requires sacredness, specific protagonists, or a primordial setting, and how to distinguish it from legend and folktale given extensive overlap.
- **Truth Value and Pejorative Use:** The colloquial equation of "myth" with "falsehood" creates ethical and methodological tension when studying living religious traditions; scholars disagree on whether to label all religious narratives as myths (neutrality) or avoid the term (respect).
- **Origin vs. Function:** 19th-century theories sought origins (nature, history, ritual); 20th-century theories prioritized function (social charter, psychological archetype, structural pattern). The field remains divided between those seeking universal structures (Campbell, Eliade) and those emphasizing cultural specificity.
- **Original Version vs. Plasticity:** 19th/20th-century scholarship often sought to reconstruct an "ur-myth"; postmodern and hylistic approaches argue myths are inherently variable with no original form, only competing variants.
- **Text vs. Transmediality:** Traditional focus on textual sources is challenged by arguments that visual arts, landscape, performance, and digital media are equally or more significant carriers of mythic narrative material.
