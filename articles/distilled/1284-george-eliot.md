# George Eliot

## Overview  
Mary Ann Evans (1819–1880), writing under the pen name George Eliot, was one of the most influential novelists of the Victorian era. Her seven novels—ranging from *Adam Bede* (1859) to *Daniel Deronda* (1876)—are celebrated for their realism, psychological depth, and moral complexity. Her unconventional life, including a long-term partnership with George Henry Lewes and a late marriage to John Cross, mirrored the progressive and often controversial ideas explored in her fiction.

## Body  

### Early Life and Education  
Mary Ann Evans was born into a middle-class family in Nuneaton. Her father, Robert Evans, managed the Arbury Hall estate, granting her access to its library and fostering her early intellectual development. She received formal schooling from ages five to sixteen, including instruction from evangelical teachers. Her classical education and exposure to both wealth and poverty on the estate deeply influenced her later writing.

### Move to Coventry and Intellectual Awakening  
After her mother’s death in 1836, Evans became housekeeper for her father. In Coventry, she encountered radical thinkers like Charles and Cara Bray, whose circle included Robert Owen and Harriet Martineau. This environment introduced her to liberal theology and German philosophy, prompting her to translate works by David Strauss and Ludwig Feuerbach. These translations established her reputation as a serious intellectual and critic of orthodox Christianity.

### Career in Journalism and Editing  
Returning to London in 1850, Evans worked closely with publisher John Chapman and became assistant editor of *The Westminster Review* in 1851. There, she wrote essays critiquing Victorian society, religion, and politics, aligning herself with progressive causes. Her editorial work honed her analytical skills and deepened her commitment to social reform.

### Relationship with George Henry Lewes  
In 1854, Evans began cohabiting with Lewes, a philosopher and critic already trapped in an open marriage. Their partnership lasted over two decades and provided emotional and intellectual support crucial to her literary career. Despite societal condemnation, they lived openly as husband and wife, with Evans adopting the name Mary Ann Evans Lewes.

### Literary Career and Major Works  
Evans launched her fiction career with *Scenes of Clerical Life* (1858), followed by *Adam Bede* (1859), which brought her immediate acclaim. She continued producing major novels including *The Mill on the Floss*, *Silas Marner*, *Romola*, *Felix Holt*, *Middlemarch*, and *Daniel Deronda*. Her work combined realism with ethical inquiry, often focusing on marginalized figures navigating rigid social structures.

### Political and Feminist Views  
Eliot supported abolition, Irish home rule, and women’s rights. She admired John Stuart Mill and endorsed his advocacy for gender equality. Though her female characters are complex and not uniformly feminist, her personal beliefs strongly favored educational and legal reforms for women.

### Marriage to John Cross and Death  
Eighteen months after Lewes’s death, Evans married John Cross, a younger man and longtime friend. The marriage sparked fresh scandal due to the age gap. During their honeymoon in Venice, Cross attempted suicide by jumping into the Grand Canal. Evans died in 1880 from kidney disease and a throat infection, aged 61.

## Terms  

- **Pen name**: A pseudonym adopted by an author; Evans used “George Eliot” to avoid prejudice against female writers.  
- **Realism**: A literary movement emphasizing accurate, detailed representation of everyday life.  
- **Agnosticism**: The view that knowledge of God or ultimate reality is uncertain or unknowable.  
- **Nom-de-plume**: French term for a pen name.  
- **Open marriage**: A marital arrangement allowing partners to pursue extramarital relationships.  
- **Proto-Zionism**: Early expressions of support for Jewish national self-determination, present in *Daniel Deronda*.  
- **Psychological realism**: A style focusing on internal thoughts and motivations of characters.  
- **Social outsider**: A character excluded from mainstream society due to class, gender, or behavior.  
- **Moral complexity**: Ethical ambiguity in characters or themes, common in Eliot’s novels.  
- **Victorian era**: The period of British history during Queen Victoria’s reign (1837–1901).

## Debates and Open Questions  

- **Identity of George Eliot**: For years, readers speculated about the author’s identity, with some believing the works were penned by a man or a clergyman. Only after *Adam Bede*’s success did Evans acknowledge her authorship.  
- **Feminist interpretation**: Scholars debate whether Eliot’s female protagonists represent feminist ideals. While some argue her personal views supported equality, others note that many heroines conform to traditional roles.  
- **Religious stance**: Though critical of institutional Christianity, Eliot retained respect for religious tradition’s role in maintaining social order. Her humanistic approach remains debated among critics.  
- **Legacy of *Daniel Deronda***: The novel’s ending and its portrayal of Jewish identity have drawn varied interpretations, particularly regarding its proto-Zionist elements.  
- **Posthumous reputation**: Eliot’s image was shaped significantly by her husband’s biography, which idealized her character. Modern scholarship seeks to reconcile this with her actual life and writings.
