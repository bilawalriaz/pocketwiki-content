# Southern United States

## Overview
The Southern United States, defined by the Census Bureau as 16 states and Washington, D.C., is a culturally distinct region shaped by slavery, the Civil War, and segregation. Historically agrarian and conservative, it has transformed since the mid-20th century into the nation's fastest-growing region.

- **1964–1965** — Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts end legal segregation.
- **1970s–present** — "New South" economy booms; region becomes a Republican stronghold.

## Body

### Geography and Subregions
The South features a humid subtropical climate, the Appalachian Mountains, coastal plains, and pine forests. The Census Bureau divides it into the South Atlantic, East South Central, and West South Central divisions. Cultural subregions include the **Deep South** (e.g., Alabama, Georgia), the **Upper South** (e.g., Kentucky, Virginia), and Southern Appalachia. Historically, it was defined as states south of the Mason–Dixon line, the Ohio River, and the 36°30′ parallel.

### History: From Colonization to Civil War
English colonization began in the 17th century, establishing plantation economies reliant on enslaved African labor. The 1793 cotton gin made slavery immensely profitable, expanding it across the Deep South. Political conflicts over tariffs and slavery's expansion intensified sectional tensions. Eleven states seceded after Abraham Lincoln's 1860 election, forming the Confederacy and sparking the Civil War (1861–1865), which devastated the region.

### Reconstruction, Jim Crow, and the Civil Rights Movement
Reconstruction (1865–1877) saw federal efforts to rebuild the South and establish civil rights via the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments. White supremacist groups regained control by the late 1870s. From 1890 to 1908, states enacted constitutions disenfranchising Black voters through poll taxes and literacy tests, enforced by **Jim Crow laws**. The **Civil Rights Movement** of the 1950s–1960s, led by figures like Martin Luther King Jr., challenged this system, culminating in the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965.

### Economy and Politics
Historically agrarian, the South industrialized post-WWII. By the late 20th century, service, manufacturing, and high-tech industries boomed, with major corporate headquarters in Atlanta, Charlotte, and Dallas. Politically, the **Solid South** voted Democratic from Reconstruction until the mid-1960s. Following the national Democratic Party's support for civil rights, conservative white Southerners shifted to the Republican Party, making the South a Republican stronghold today.

### Culture and Society
Southern culture blends English, Scotch-Irish, and African traditions, featuring unique dialects, cuisine, and music (blues, jazz, country). It is part of the **Bible Belt**, with high evangelical Protestant attendance. The region has the nation's largest African American population and a growing Hispanic population. While a distinct Southern identity persists, it has declined due to migration and cultural integration.

## Terms
- **Solid South:** Southern states voting reliably Democratic from 1877 to the mid-1960s.
- **Jim Crow Laws:** State laws enforcing racial segregation from the late 19th to mid-20th century.
- **Bible Belt:** Region with dominant evangelical Protestantism and high church attendance.
- **Great Migration:** Movement of six million African Americans from the South to other regions (1910–1970).
- **New South:** Post-Civil War emphasis on economic modernization and industrialization.
- **Deep South:** Subregion including South Carolina, Georgia, Florida, Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana.
- **Border States:** Slave states that did not secede: Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri.
- **Dixie:** Colloquial name for the Southern U.S., often linked to former Confederate states.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Defining the South:** Boundaries are debated as cultural and political alignments may differ from Census definitions.
- **Decline of Southern Identity:** Researchers question its persistence amid economic integration, population growth, and diversity.
- **Political Realignment:** Causes of the shift from Democratic to Republican dominance are analyzed, considering race, economic conservatism, and the religious right.
