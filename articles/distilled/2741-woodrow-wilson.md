# Woodrow Wilson

## Overview
Woodrow Wilson (1856–1924) was the 28th U.S. President, serving from 1913 to 1921. A leading figure of the Progressive Era, he enacted major domestic reforms and led the nation through World War I. He was the primary architect of the League of Nations, embodying an idealistic foreign policy known as Wilsonianism. His legacy is complex, marked by significant governmental reforms and a deeply controversial record on race relations.

## Timeline
- **1856** — Born in Staunton, Virginia.
- **1886** — Awarded a Ph.D. in history and government from Johns Hopkins University.
- **1902** — Becomes president of Princeton University.
- **1910** — Elected Governor of New Jersey.
- **1912** — Elected President, defeating Taft and Roosevelt.
- **1913** — Signs the Revenue Act of 1913 (income tax) and the Federal Reserve Act.
- **1914** — U.S. declares neutrality at the outbreak of World War I.
- **1916** — Re-elected, campaigning on "He Kept Us Out of War."
- **1917** — Asks Congress for a declaration of war against Germany.
- **1918** — Delivers the Fourteen Points speech outlining post-war aims.
- **1919** — Attends Paris Peace Conference; suffers a debilitating stroke.
- **1920** — Senate rejects the Treaty of Versailles and U.S. membership in the League of Nations.
- **1924** — Dies in Washington, D.C.

## Body

### Early Life and Academic Career
Born in Virginia, Wilson grew up in the South during the Civil War and Reconstruction. He earned a Ph.D. from Johns Hopkins University, becoming the only U.S. president to hold a doctorate. As a professor and later president of Princeton University (1902–1910), he gained fame as a progressive reformer. His 1887 article "The Study of Administration" is considered foundational to the field of public administration.

### Governor and 1912 Election
As Governor of New Jersey (1911–1913), Wilson broke with party bosses to pass progressive reforms. In the 1912 presidential election, he defeated the split Republican vote of incumbent William Howard Taft and former president Theodore Roosevelt. Wilson's "New Freedom" platform called for breaking up trusts and lowering tariffs.

### Domestic "New Freedom" Agenda
Wilson's first term focused on sweeping reforms. The Revenue Act of 1913 established the modern federal income tax. The Federal Reserve Act created a central banking system to stabilize the economy. He also signed the Clayton Antitrust Act and created the Federal Trade Commission to regulate business. Later legislation included the Keating–Owen Act (child labor law, later struck down by the Supreme Court) and the Adamson Act (establishing an eight-hour workday for railroad workers).

### World War I and Foreign Policy
Initially maintaining U.S. neutrality in World War I, Wilson shifted to intervention in 1917 after Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare. He framed the war as a crusade for democracy, articulating the "Fourteen Points" as a basis for peace, which included the creation of a League of Nations. After the Allied victory, Wilson led the U.S. delegation to the Paris Peace Conference. He secured the inclusion of the League of Nations in the Treaty of Versailles but failed to win Senate ratification, as he refused to compromise with Republican "reservationists" led by Henry Cabot Lodge.

### Race Relations and Segregation
Wilson's record on race is widely condemned. He authorized the widespread segregation of federal workplaces, rolling back decades of progress for Black civil servants. His administration saw a resurgence of the Ku Klux Klan, and he screened the pro-KKK film *The Birth of a Nation* at the White House. While he spoke against lynching, he took little federal action to stop racial violence.

### Final Years and Incapacity
Wilson suffered a severe stroke in October 1919, leaving him incapacitated. His wife, Edith, and physician controlled access to him, effectively managing the presidency. The Republicans won a landslide in the 1920 election, repudiating Wilson's policies. He died in 1924.

## Terms
- **Wilsonian idealism**: Wilson's foreign policy doctrine emphasizing self-determination, open diplomacy, and collective security through international organizations.
- **New Freedom**: Wilson's domestic platform focused on antitrust enforcement, tariff reduction, and banking reform to promote economic competition.
- **Fourteen Points**: Wilson's 1918 speech outlining principles for post-WWI peace, including freedom of the seas, open diplomacy, and a League of Nations.
- **League of Nations**: An international organization proposed by Wilson to mediate disputes and prevent war; the U.S. never joined.
- **Federal Reserve System**: The central banking system created in 1913 to provide a stable monetary and financial system.
- **Progressive Era**: A period of widespread social activism and political reform across the U.S. from the 1890s to the 1920s.
- **Irreconcilables**: A group of U.S. Senators, mostly Republicans, who completely opposed the Treaty of Versailles and U.S. membership in the League of Nations.
- **Reservationists**: U.S. Senators who accepted the League of Nations concept but sought modifications to the treaty to protect U.S. sovereignty.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historical Ranking**: Historians generally rank Wilson as an above-average president for his domestic reforms and vision for international order, but his legacy is heavily debated due to his racist policies and failure to secure the Treaty of Versailles.
- **Racial Legacy**: Scholars critically examine Wilson's role in institutionalizing segregation in the federal government and its long-term economic impact on Black civil servants.
- **Foreign Policy Impact**: Debate continues over whether Wilsonian idealism was a visionary approach to international relations or an impractical doctrine that contributed to future conflicts.
- **Health and Governance**: The extent to which Wilson's 1919 stroke and subsequent incapacity affected key decisions, particularly the final negotiations over the Treaty, remains a subject of historical inquiry.
