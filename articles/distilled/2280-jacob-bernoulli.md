# Jacob Bernoulli

## Overview
Jacob Bernoulli (1655–1705) was a foundational Swiss mathematician who helped establish the Leibnizian calculus in Europe and made seminal contributions to probability theory. His work, particularly the posthumous *Ars Conjectandi*, laid the groundwork for the law of large numbers and introduced the constant *e*, making him a pivotal figure in the development of modern mathematics.

## Timeline
- **1655** — Born in Basel, Swiss Confederation.
- **1676–1682** — Traveled Europe, studying mathematics and science.
- **1684** — Submitted doctoral dissertation; began studying Leibniz's calculus.
- **1687** — Appointed professor of mathematics at the University of Basel.
- **1690** — Published paper introducing the term "integral" and solving the isochrone problem.

- **1713** — *Ars Conjectandi* published posthumously.

## Body

**Early Life and Career**
Jacob Bernoulli was born into a family of merchants in Basel. Against his parents' wishes for him to study theology, he pursued mathematics and astronomy. After traveling Europe to learn from leading scientists, he returned to Basel, teaching mechanics and earning his doctorate. In 1687, he became a professor of mathematics, a position he held for life.

**Contributions to Calculus and Analysis**
Bernoulli, along with his brother Johann, was among the first to understand and apply Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz's newly published calculus. Their early collaboration later turned into a famous rivalry. Jacob made key contributions, including solving the isochrone problem (a curve of constant descent) using separation of variables and introducing the term "integral" with its modern meaning in 1690. He also solved the Bernoulli differential equation and studied curves like the lemniscate and logarithmic spiral.

**Probability and the Law of Large Numbers**
Bernoulli's most important work is *Ars Conjectandi*, published in 1713. It is a cornerstone of probability theory, reviewing combinatorics and applying probability to games of chance. Its most significant result is the first version of the **law of large numbers**, which states that as the number of trials increases, the observed proportion of an event will converge to its theoretical probability. This work also introduced the term "Bernoulli trial."

**Discovery of the Constant *e***
In 1683, while studying compound interest, Bernoulli discovered the mathematical constant *e*. He analyzed the limit of (1 + 1/n)^n as *n* approaches infinity, finding it was a number greater than 2.5 but less than 3. This constant, later named *e* by Euler, is fundamental to continuous growth and compound interest calculations.

**Legacy and Personal Life**
Bernoulli married Judith Stupanus in 1684. He was a member of the Royal Academies of Paris and Berlin. He died in 1705 from a chronic illness. He had requested a logarithmic spiral and the motto "Eadem mutata resurgo" ("Although changed, I rise again the same") for his tombstone, but an Archimedean spiral was engraved instead.

## Terms
- **Law of Large Numbers:** A theorem stating that the average of results from a large number of trials should be close to the expected value.
- **Calculus of Variations:** A field of mathematical analysis for finding extrema (maxima/minima) of functionals, co-founded by the Bernoulli brothers.
- **Bernoulli Differential Equation:** A specific nonlinear differential equation of the form y' = p(x)y + q(x)y^n.
- **Isochrone:** A curve along which a particle descends under gravity in the same time, regardless of its starting point.
- **Lemniscate of Bernoulli:** A plane curve defined by the locus of points such that the product of distances from two fixed points is constant.
- **Bernoulli Trial:** A random experiment with exactly two possible outcomes (success or failure), with a fixed probability of success.
- **Integral:** In calculus, the concept of accumulation, whose modern notation and meaning were advanced by Bernoulli.
- **Compound Interest:** Interest calculated on the initial principal and also on the accumulated interest of previous periods.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that Bernoulli's proof of the divergence of the harmonic series (∑1/n) was not new, having been proved earlier by Nicole Oresme in the 14th century and Pietro Mengoli. His work on infinite series also could not find a closed form for ∑1/n², a problem later solved by Euler in 1737. The relationship between Jacob and his brother Johann is highlighted as a significant shift from collaboration to bitter rivalry.
