# Eleanor of Aquitaine

## Overview
Eleanor of Aquitaine (c. 1124–1204) was one of the most powerful and wealthy figures of the High Middle Ages. As Duchess of Aquitaine in her own right, she was Queen of France (1137–1152) and later Queen of England (1154–1189), and mother to two English kings. Her life spanned the political and cultural heart of medieval Europe, and her legacy is shaped by both contemporary chronicles and later legend.

## Timeline
- **c. 1124** — Born, eldest child of Duke William X of Aquitaine.
- **1137** — Becomes Duchess of Aquitaine; marries Louis, heir to France.
- **1137** — Louis VI dies; Eleanor becomes Queen of France.
- **1147–1149** — Participates in the Second Crusade with Louis VII.
- **1152** — Marriage to Louis VII annulled; she marries Henry of Normandy.
- **1154** — Henry becomes King Henry II; Eleanor becomes Queen of England.
- **1173** — Imprisoned by Henry II for supporting a revolt by their sons.
- **1189** — Released after Henry II's death; acts as regent for Richard I.
- **1194** — Retires to Fontevraud Abbey after Richard I's return from captivity.
- **1199** — Campaigns for son John to succeed Richard I.
- **1204** — Dies at Fontevraud Abbey; buried there.

## Body

### Early Life and Inheritance
Eleanor was the eldest child of Duke William X of Aquitaine, inheriting the duchy upon his death in 1137. Her father had named King Louis VI of France as her guardian, who swiftly arranged her marriage to his son, Louis. This union brought the vast and wealthy Aquitaine into the French royal sphere. Eleanor was crowned Queen of France in 1137.

### Queen of France and the Second Crusade
Eleanor's marriage to Louis VII was strained by political conflicts and the lack of a male heir. She accompanied Louis on the Second Crusade (1147–1149), where she was blamed for a disastrous military defeat at Mount Cadmus. Rumors of an affair with her uncle Raymond of Poitiers in Antioch damaged her reputation. The marriage was annulled in 1152 on grounds of consanguinity.

### Queen of England
Within weeks of her annulment, Eleanor married Henry, Duke of Normandy, who became King Henry II in 1154. They had five sons and three daughters. Eleanor acted as regent during Henry's frequent absences. However, she supported the 1173 revolt of their sons against Henry and was imprisoned for the remainder of his reign (1173–1189).

### Widowhood and Queen Mother
Released by her son Richard I in 1189, Eleanor served as regent of England during his absence on the Third Crusade and later during his captivity (1192–1194). After Richard's death in 1199, she secured the succession for her youngest son, John. In her final years, she traveled to Castile to bring her granddaughter Blanche to marry the French heir, and defended Mirebeau Castle against her grandson Arthur. She died at Fontevraud Abbey in 1204.

## Terms
*   **Duchy of Aquitaine:** A large, wealthy feudal territory in southwestern France, which Eleanor ruled in her own right.
*   **Consanguinity:** The state of being related by blood. Eleanor's marriages to both Louis VII and Henry II were later questioned or dissolved on these grounds.
*   **Regent:** A person appointed to administer a state because the monarch is a minor, absent, or incapacitated. Eleanor served as regent for Richard I.
*   **Angevin Empire:** The collection of territories ruled by Henry II and his sons, stretching from England to the Pyrenees.
*   **Troubadour:** Poet-musicians of southern France who composed lyric poetry, often about courtly love, and were associated with Eleanor's courts.
*   **Black Legend:** A body of negative and often unsubstantiated stories about Eleanor, originating in contemporary clerical chronicles.
*   **Golden Myth:** A 20th-century romanticized view of Eleanor as a feminist heroine and patron of courtly love, which modern scholarship has sought to correct.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Historical Sources:** There is a scarcity of contemporary biographies. Most accounts come from clerical chroniclers who were often hostile to women in power, leading to a "Black Legend." Modern scholarship struggles to separate fact from legend.
*   **The Court of Love:** The tradition that Eleanor presided over a "Court of Love" in Poitiers is a later literary invention, largely based on a satirical 12th-century text with no evidence linking it to her.
*   **Role in Revolts:** Chroniclers blamed Eleanor for inciting the 1173 revolt of her sons against Henry II, but evidence is circumstantial, often prefaced with "so it was said."
*   **Imprisonment:** The exact nature and conditions of her 16-year imprisonment by Henry II are poorly documented.
