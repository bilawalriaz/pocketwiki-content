# Yang Chen-Ning

## Overview
Yang Chen-Ning (1922–2025) was a Chinese-American theoretical physicist whose work fundamentally reshaped modern physics. His most famous contributions are the Yang–Mills theory, which provides the mathematical foundation for the Standard Model of particle physics, and the discovery of parity non-conservation in weak interactions, for which he won the Nobel Prize. His career bridged statistical mechanics, condensed matter physics, and particle theory, influencing at least ten other Nobel laureates.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Yang was born in Hefei, China, in 1922. His family moved during the Second Sino-Japanese War, and he studied at National Southwestern Associated University in Kunming, earning his bachelor's degree in 1942 and master's in 1944. He came to the United States in 1946 on a Boxer Indemnity Scholarship, earning his Ph.D. from the University of Chicago in 1948 under Edward Teller.

### Career and Major Contributions
After a postdoctoral year with Enrico Fermi, Yang joined the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton in 1949, where he began his collaboration with Tsung-Dao Lee. His most celebrated work is the **Yang–Mills theory** (1954), developed with Robert Mills. This non-abelian gauge theory describes the fundamental forces (except gravity) and is the foundation of the Standard Model.

In 1956, Lee and Yang proposed that **parity** (mirror symmetry) is not conserved in weak nuclear interactions, solving the τ–θ puzzle. This was confirmed experimentally by Chien-Shiung Wu's team in 1956, leading to their 1957 Nobel Prize. Later, Yang worked on topological aspects of gauge theory, including the Wu–Yang monopole (1975).

### Later Career and Legacy
Yang moved to Stony Brook University in 1965, founding its Institute for Theoretical Physics (now named after him). He retired in 1999 and returned to China, holding positions at Tsinghua University. He was a vocal opponent of the proposed Circular Electron Positron Collider (CEPC) in China, calling it speculative. His work is cited in the Nobel speeches of at least ten other physicists, underscoring his profound impact on the field.

## Terms
- **Non-abelian gauge theory:** A type of field theory where the symmetry transformations do not commute, forming the mathematical basis for the Standard Model's description of fundamental forces.
- **Parity (P):** A symmetry operation that mirrors a physical system. Its conservation was assumed to be universal until Lee and Yang's 1956 proposal.
- **Weak interaction:** One of the four fundamental forces, responsible for radioactive decay; the force where parity violation occurs.
- **Standard Model:** The theoretical framework describing elementary particles and the electromagnetic, weak, and strong nuclear forces.
- **Statistical mechanics:** A branch of physics using probability theory to explain the thermodynamic behavior of systems composed of many particles.
- **Integrable systems:** Physical systems that are exactly solvable, often described by equations like the Yang–Baxter equation.
- **Fiber bundle:** A mathematical structure used in gauge theories to describe how fields transform over spacetime.
- **ODLRO (Off-diagonal long-range order):** A concept in condensed matter physics characterizing macroscopic quantum phenomena like superconductivity and superfluidity.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Parity Violation:** The proposal was initially controversial, challenging a fundamental symmetry law, but was quickly confirmed by multiple experiments.
- **CEPC Opposition:** Yang publicly opposed the construction of the CEPC in China, arguing it was speculative and not guaranteed to yield benefits for the Chinese people, reflecting a debate on big-science project priorities.
- **Citizenship:** Yang's decision to become a U.S. citizen was a source of personal conflict with his father, and he later renounced it in 2015, highlighting the complex relationship between scientists and their homelands.
