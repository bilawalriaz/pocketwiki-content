# Diary

## Overview
A diary is a record of events, thoughts, and experiences arranged by date, traditionally intended for private use. It serves as a personal historical document and, when published, becomes a key source for autobiographical literature and historical research. The form has evolved from ancient logbooks to digital blogs, reflecting changes in technology and the desire for self-expression.

## Timeline
- **c. 2600 BC** — The Diary of Merer, an ancient Egyptian logbook, is the earliest known book resembling a diary.
- **2nd century AD** — Roman Emperor Marcus Aurelius writes *To Myself* (Meditations), displaying diary characteristics.
- **11th century** — The diary of Abu Ali ibn al-Banna is the earliest known to be arranged in chronological date order.
- **1405–1449** — The anonymous *Journal d'un bourgeois de Paris* provides subjective commentary on current events.
- **1605** — Ben Jonson's *Volpone* contains the earliest recorded use of the word 'diary' for a daily record book.
- **1633–1703** — Samuel Pepys keeps his famous diary, a primary source for the English Restoration period.
- **19th century** — Pocket diaries become a popular stationery item; posthumous publication of notable diaries begins.
- **1942–1944** — Anne Frank writes *The Diary of a Young Girl* while in hiding in Amsterdam.
- **1994** — Claudio Pinhanez's *Open Diary* is thought to be the first online diary.
- **1998–1999** — Web-based services like Open Diary and LiveJournal streamline online publishing.
- **2023** — Apple releases a dedicated journal app with its iOS 17.2 update.

## Body

### Definition and Etymology
A diary is a written or audiovisual record with discrete, dated entries. The word derives from the Latin *diarium* ("daily allowance," from *dies*, "day"). A related term, "journal," comes from the same root but generally implies less frequent entries. While traditionally handwritten, diaries are now often digital. A personal diary typically excludes comments on events outside the writer's direct experience.

### Historical Development
The earliest known diary-like work is the ancient Egyptian Diary of Merer. The oldest extant diaries come from Middle Eastern and East Asian cultures. In the medieval Near East, Arabic diaries appeared, with the 11th-century diary of Abu Ali ibn al-Banna being the earliest arranged by date. Precursors in the modern sense include the daily notes of medieval mystics. From the Renaissance onward, individuals began recording personal opinions and emotions alongside events, as seen in works like the *Journal d'un bourgeois de Paris*.

### Publication and Notable Examples
Many diaries of notable figures have been published, forming important autobiographical literature. Samuel Pepys (1633–1703) was among the first to move the diary beyond business notation into the personal realm. His contemporary John Evelyn also kept a notable diary. The practice of posthumous publication began in the 19th century with diarists like Dorothy Wordsworth and Fanny Burney. One of the most famous modern diaries is Anne Frank's *The Diary of a Young Girl*, written during the 1940s and published after the war. From the 20th century, diary-writing was also practiced as a conscious act of self-exploration by figures like Carl Jung and Anaïs Nin.

### Digital Evolution
With common internet access, online diaries emerged in the mid-1990s, with Claudio Pinhanez's *Open Diary* (1994) considered the first. Services like LiveJournal (1999) streamlined publishing. The internet also brought unpublished historical diaries, like that of the enslaved Michael Shiner, to public attention. The rise of blogs expanded personal storytelling. More recently, mobile apps have popularized digital journaling, emphasizing ease, portability, and searchability. Some software now offers privacy features like encryption.

### Specialized Forms
Diaries take many specialized forms. A personal organizer lists daily tasks. Freewriting involves unconstrained daily writing for self-discovery. A gardening journal tracks horticultural efforts. A gratitude diary records things for which one is thankful, a practice in positive psychology. A sleep diary logs sleep patterns for diagnosis or dream analysis. The German *Tagebuch* encompasses working journals as well as personal diaries. A travel journal documents journeys, and a war diary is an official military record of a unit's activities.

## Terms
- **Diarist:** A person who keeps a diary.
- **Hansard:** The official report of debates in the British Parliament, an example of an institutional diary.
- **Pocket diary:** A popular 19th-century stationery item with preprinted dates and limited space per entry.
- **Tagebuch:** A German term for a diary, also encompassing workbooks or working journals.
- **War diary:** An official, regularly updated record of a military unit's administration and activities during wartime.
- **Gratitude journal:** A diary focused on recording things for which the writer is grateful.
- **Freewriting:** A practice of writing without constraints to foster self-discovery.
- **Blog:** A website or webpage, regularly updated, often used for personal online journaling.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes a scholarly debate regarding the earliest examples of diary-like writing, with works like the *Meditations* of Marcus Aurelius and the pillowbooks of Japanese court ladies being considered precursors that display "some aspects" of the genre but rarely consist exclusively of daily records.
