# Thomas Paine

## Overview
Thomas Paine (1737–1809) was an English-born American Founding Father, political philosopher, and revolutionary pamphleteer whose writings, especially *Common Sense* and *Rights of Man*, were instrumental in shaping the American and French Revolutions. He championed Enlightenment ideals of human rights, republicanism, and reason, but his radical critiques of monarchy, organized religion, and established leaders eventually led to widespread ostracism in his later years.

## Timeline
- **1737** — Born in Thetford, England.
- **1774** — Emigrates to the American colonies with a letter of introduction from Benjamin Franklin.
- **1776** — Publishes *Common Sense*, catalyzing public support for American independence.
- **1776–1783** — Publishes *The American Crisis* pamphlet series to inspire Patriot forces.
- **1787** — Returns to London; later writes *Rights of Man* (1791) in defense of the French Revolution.
- **1792** — Tried in absentia for seditious libel in England; flees to France and is elected to the National Convention.
- **1793** — Arrested in Paris and imprisoned in the Luxembourg Prison.
- **1794** — Released from prison with the help of American ambassador James Monroe.
- **1796** — Publishes a scathing open letter criticizing President George Washington.
- **1797** — Publishes *Agrarian Justice*, proposing a basic income and old-age pension.
- **1802** — Returns to the United States at Thomas Jefferson's invitation.
- **1809** — Dies in New York City; only six people attend his funeral.

## Body

### Early Life and Immigration
Born to a Quaker father and Anglican mother, Paine had a varied early career as a stay-maker, privateer, and excise officer in England. His first marriage ended with the death of his wife and child. After being dismissed from the excise service and facing debt, he met Benjamin Franklin in London in 1774. Franklin recommended him to America, and Paine arrived in Philadelphia in late 1774, soon becoming editor of the *Pennsylvania Magazine*.

### The American Revolution
Paine’s pamphlet *Common Sense* (1776) provided a powerful, accessible argument for independence, attacking monarchy and King George III directly. It sold an estimated 100,000 copies in three months and is credited with crystallizing Patriot sentiment. He later wrote *The American Crisis* papers to boost morale during the war. Paine served as secretary to the Congressional Committee on Foreign Affairs but resigned after controversy involving diplomat Silas Deane. He later helped secure critical French loans for the war effort.

### The French Revolution and *Rights of Man*
After returning to London, Paine wrote *Rights of Man* (1791) to refute Edmund Burke’s defense of the French monarchy. The work advocated for representative government and social welfare programs, leading to a seditious libel charge in Britain. Paine fled to France, where he was elected to the National Convention. He opposed the execution of Louis XVI, arguing for exile instead. This stance, along with his association with the losing Girondin faction, led to his arrest and imprisonment by the Montagnards in 1793. He narrowly escaped execution due to a jailer’s error and was released in 1794.

### Later Years and Legacy
Paine’s *The Age of Reason* (1793–1794) advocated Deism and criticized organized religion, alienating many religious Americans. His public denunciation of George Washington in 1796 further damaged his reputation. Returning to the U.S. in 1802, he was largely shunned. His final major work, *Agrarian Justice* (1797), proposed a national fund to provide a basic income and old-age pensions. Despite his unpopularity at death, Paine’s writings on liberty, republicanism, and social justice have influenced subsequent generations of radicals, reformers, and political thinkers worldwide.

## Terms
- **Deism**: A religious philosophy that rejects organized religion and divine revelation, emphasizing reason and the belief in a creator God who does not intervene in the universe.
- **Seditious Libel**: The crime of publishing statements that criticize or bring the government or its officials into hatred or contempt.
- **Girondins**: A moderate political faction during the French Revolution, often associated with provincial deputies, who were later purged by the more radical Montagnards.
- **Montagnards**: The radical, populist faction in the French National Convention, also known as "The Mountain" for their seating in the assembly.
- **Fiat Currency**: Government-issued money not backed by a physical commodity like gold or silver, which Paine condemned as state counterfeiting.
- **Agrarian Justice**: Paine's 1797 pamphlet arguing that land ownership is a social construct and proposing a tax on inheritance to fund social welfare.
- **Common Sense Realism**: A philosophical school holding that ordinary people can make sound judgments on major issues through innate, shared understanding.
- **Civic Republicanism**: A political philosophy emphasizing the importance of citizens' active participation in government for the common good.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authorship of "African Slavery in America"**: The 1775 essay is often attributed to Paine based on Benjamin Rush's later recollection, but definitive proof is lacking.
- **Involvement in Drafting the Declaration of Independence**: A 2018 discovery of an inscription "T.P." on a draft copy suggests possible involvement, but the degree of his contribution remains unclear and debated.
- **Scale of *Common Sense*'s Impact**: While its popularity is undisputed, some historians question Paine's estimated sales figures and debate whether it directly influenced the Continental Congress's decision for independence or primarily shaped public opinion.
