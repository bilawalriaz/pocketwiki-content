# Mesolithic

## Overview
The Mesolithic is the Old World archaeological period between the Upper Paleolithic and the Neolithic, representing the final phase of hunter-gatherer societies before the widespread adoption of agriculture. It marks a significant transition in human adaptation, characterized by the development of smaller, more sophisticated stone tools and a shift from hunting large game to a broader subsistence strategy. The period's timing and cultural expression vary greatly across Eurasia, reflecting local environmental changes following the last Ice Age.

## Timeline
- **c. 20,000–10,000** — Mesolithic/Epipaleolithic period in the Middle East.
- **c. 15,000** — Beginning of the Mesolithic in the Balkans.
- **c. 14,000** — Start of the Azilian culture in the Franco-Cantabrian region.
- **c. 11,500** — Mesolithic begins in other parts of Europe with the start of the Holocene.
- **c. 9,500** — Earliest known pottery fragments from Xianrendong cave, China.
- **c. 9,000** — "Ceramic Mesolithic" pottery appears in the Yelshanka culture on the Volga.
- **Before c. 9,000** — Early ritual/astronomical constructions at Warren Field and Stonehenge.
- **c. 8,500–5,500** — Mesolithic ends in Europe with the regional spread of farming.
- **c. 5,700** — Evidence of a Mesolithic meal (hazelnuts, duck) from chewed birch pitch in Denmark.

## Body

### Terminology and Definition
The term "Mesolithic" (Greek for "middle stone") was proposed by Hodder Westropp in 1866 as an intermediate period between the Paleolithic and Neolithic. Its use was initially controversial, with some scholars arguing for a continuous transition and others for a gap. The term "Epipaleolithic" is often used synonymously, especially outside Northern Europe. Conventions vary: "Mesolithic" is standard in Northern European archaeology, while "Epipaleolithic" is more common in the Near East. Some authors reserve "Mesolithic" for cultures directly succeeded by the Neolithic Revolution, using "Epipaleolithic" for other final hunter-gatherer societies.

### Technology and Subsistence
A defining feature of the Mesolithic is the use of **microlithic technology**—composite tools made with small, shaped stone blades (microliths). This contrasts with the larger tools of the Paleolithic and the polished stone tools of the Neolithic. Subsistence shifted from group hunting of large animals to a broader hunter-gatherer economy exploiting diverse resources like fish, shellfish, and plants. More permanent settlements developed, often near coasts or inland waters. Some Mesolithic communities used pottery and textiles, blurring the line with the Neolithic.

### Settlement and Social Complexity
Mesolithic societies are generally seen as less complex than later Neolithic ones, with simple burials. However, there is evidence for early construction with possible ritual or astronomical purposes, such as post alignments at Stonehenge and pit circles at Warren Field in Scotland, both predating 9,000 BP. The spread of the "Neolithic package" (farming, herding, pottery) eventually marginalized Mesolithic lifeways, though some communities, like those at Blätterhöhle in Germany, maintained foraging for over 2,000 years after farming arrived.

### Art and Material Culture
Surviving Mesolithic art is less abundant than from the preceding or following periods. Rock art, such as in the Iberian Mediterranean Basin, often depicts small, schematic human figures in scenes of daily life, contrasting with the large animal art of the Paleolithic. Notable artifacts include the carved wooden Shigir Idol from the Urals, the Ain Sakhri figurine from Palestine, and modified red deer skull frontlets from Star Carr in Britain. Fine woven baskets and shoes have been found in sites like Cueva de los Murciélagos in Spain.

### Ceramic Mesolithic and Regional Variations
A "ceramic Mesolithic" tradition, characterized by pottery made by non-farming peoples, developed in northeastern Europe, Siberia, and parts of southern Europe and North Africa between c. 9,000 and 5,850 BP. This pottery, with distinct forms and manufacturing techniques, spread from a possible origin near Lake Baikal. The earliest known pottery in the world, however, comes from Xianrendong cave in China (c. 20,000–19,000 BP), made by mobile foragers long before agriculture.

### Outside Europe
The Mesolithic concept is less consistently applied elsewhere. In China, sites once considered Mesolithic are now often classified as Early Neolithic. In India, the Mesolithic (c. 12,000–8,000 BP) remains a recognized period. In the Americas, a similar transitional phase is called the Archaic period. In North Africa, Saharan rock art depicts a wetter, savanna-like environment with species like hippos, reflecting a different Mesolithic adaptation.

## Terms
- **Mesolithic**: The Old World archaeological period between the Upper Paleolithic and Neolithic, characterized by final hunter-gatherer societies.
- **Epipaleolithic**: A term often used synonymously with Mesolithic, especially in the Near East and for cultures not directly succeeded by agriculture.
- **Microlith**: A small, shaped stone blade used in composite tools, characteristic of Mesolithic technology.
- **Macrolith**: A large stone tool; used in some Mesolithic regions like Ireland and Portugal.
- **Holocene**: The current geological epoch, beginning c. 11,700 years ago, marking the end of the last Ice Age.
- **Neolithic Revolution**: The widespread transition from hunting and gathering to agriculture and settled life.
- **Ceramic Mesolithic**: A term for pottery-making hunter-gatherer cultures that existed without farming.
- **Subneolithic**: A term for societies that maintained a foraging lifestyle long after the arrival of farming neighbors.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise boundary and relationship between the terms "Mesolithic" and "Epipaleolithic" remain debated, with usage varying by region and scholarly tradition.
- The degree of social complexity in Mesolithic societies is unclear, as evidenced by simple burials alongside sophisticated constructions like Warren Field.
- The processes by which Mesolithic communities adopted or rejected the "Neolithic package" are not fully understood, with evidence for both long-term coexistence and rapid transition.
- The origins and spread of the "ceramic Mesolithic" pottery tradition, and its relationship to Neolithic pottery, require further investigation.
