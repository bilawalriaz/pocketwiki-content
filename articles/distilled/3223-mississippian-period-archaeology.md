# Mississippian period (archaeology)

## Overview
The Mississippian period (c. 1000–1600 CE) was a major archaeological era in the Eastern Woodlands of North America, defined by the rise of large, maize-farming chiefdoms with monumental platform mounds and complex social hierarchies. It represents the peak of pre-Columbian social complexity in the region and is ancestral to many historic Native American nations.

## Timeline
- **c. 1000** — Transition from Late Woodland period begins; adoption of intensive maize agriculture.
- **c. 1050** — Founding of the city of Cahokia, which becomes the largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico.
- **c. 1124** — Cahokia reaches its peak population (est. 22,400).
- **c. 1150–1200** — A decades-long drought in Illinois contributes to political instability and the exodus of ~50% of Cahokia's population.
- **c. 1200–1400** — Middle Mississippian period; apex of cultural traits and influence.
- **c. 1300** — Influx of new religious concepts, notably the Datura Ritual Complex, from the west.
- **c. 1350–1400** — Population of Cahokia disperses.
- **c. 1400–1600** — Late Mississippian period; marked by warfare, political turmoil, and the rise of larger, confederated paramount chiefdoms.
- **1539–1543** — Hernando de Soto's expedition makes first extensive European contact with Mississippian peoples.
- **1567–1568** — Spanish Fort San Juan is built and destroyed at Joara (North Carolina), marking early interior colonization.

## Body

### Origins and Core Traits
The Mississippian culture emerged from the preceding Late Woodland period, but the break was incomplete. While maize agriculture became predominant, older Eastern Agricultural Complex crops remained in use. Key characteristics included the construction of large, flat-topped platform mounds (with summit structures), shell-tempered pottery, widespread trade networks, and the development of chiefdoms with institutionalized social inequality and centralized political-religious power. The Southeastern Ceremonial Complex (SECC) was a unifying religious and artistic tradition.

### The Rise and Fall of Cahokia
Founded c. 1050, Cahokia was a planned metropolis and ceremonial center built by diverse peoples. It established missionary outposts and pilgrimage sites like Angel Mounds. Its decline began with the end of the Medieval Climate Anomaly and a severe drought (1150–1200), causing political instability and mass migration. This exodus, to areas like Nashville and Etowah, helped catalyze the Middle Mississippian period.

### Regional Variants and Late Period
The culture diversified into regional variants. The **Middle Mississippian** core (Mississippi/Ohio Valleys) featured the most influential centers like Cahokia and Moundville. The **South Appalachian** variant (Southeast) adopted traits later, with sites like Etowah and Ocmulgee. The **Caddoan** variant (western plains edge) had lower population density and less fortification. The **Plaquemine** variant (lower Mississippi) was ancestral to the Natchez. The Late Mississippian period (1400–1600) saw a shift to larger, confederated paramount chiefdoms, increased warfare, and defensive settlement patterns, coinciding with the global climate change of the Little Ice Age.

### European Contact and Aftermath
Hernando de Soto's expedition (1539–43) provides crucial written records of Mississippian societies. While direct Spanish influence was limited, indirect effects were catastrophic. European diseases caused massive fatalities, undermining social orders and leading to political collapse. By the time of later European contact, the Mississippian way of life had irrevocably changed, though some groups maintained oral traditions of their mound-building past.

## Terms
- **Platform Mound:** A large, flat-topped earthwork pyramid used as a base for important structures like temples or elite residences.
- **Chiefdom:** A form of hierarchical political organization in non-industrial societies usually based on kinship, with a central authority.
- **Complex Chiefdom:** A chiefdom with a powerful central center and a less powerful periphery.
- **Paramount Chiefdom:** A larger political entity formed by a confederation of chiefdoms, headed by a paramount leader (*primus inter pares*).
- **Southeastern Ceremonial Complex (SECC):** A widespread set of artistic motifs and ritual practices, also called the Southern Cult.
- **Eastern Agricultural Complex:** A suite of native plants (e.g., squash, sunflower) cultivated in eastern North America before maize.
- **Shell-tempered Pottery:** Ceramics made with crushed shell as a tempering agent, a diagnostic Mississippian trait.
- **Vacant Quarter:** A sparsely settled buffer zone between growing paramount chiefdoms.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact causes of Cahokia's decline are debated, with factors including drought, deforestation, overhunting, and political instability.
- The nature of the relationship between Cahokia and its outposts (e.g., missionary vs. pilgrimage site) is interpreted differently by scholars.
- The degree of social stratification and the specific political structures (e.g., theocracy) of Mississippian chiefdoms remain subjects of archaeological investigation.
