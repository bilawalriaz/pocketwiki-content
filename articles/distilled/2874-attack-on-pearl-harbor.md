# Attack on Pearl Harbor

## Overview
The Attack on Pearl Harbor was a surprise military strike by the Empire of Japan on the U.S. Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, on December 7, 1941. It was a pivotal event that directly led to the United States' entry into World War II, fundamentally altering the course of the conflict. The attack aimed to neutralize the U.S. fleet to enable Japan's expansion into Southeast Asia but ultimately failed to destroy key infrastructure and carriers, allowing a rapid American recovery.

## Timeline
- **July 1941** — U.S. ceases oil exports to Japan following the seizure of French Indochina.
- **November 26, 1941** — Japanese attack fleet (Kido Butai) departs for Pearl Harbor.
- **December 7, 1941** — Japan launches a surprise air attack on Pearl Harbor and other U.S. and Allied territories.
- **December 8, 1941** — U.S. and UK declare war on Japan.
- **December 11, 1941** — Germany and Italy declare war on the U.S.; the U.S. reciprocates.

## Background and Planning
Tensions between the U.S. and Japan escalated throughout the 1930s due to Japanese expansion in China and French Indochina. The U.S. responded with economic sanctions, culminating in a full oil embargo in July 1941, which Japan viewed as an existential threat. Negotiations failed, with the U.S. demanding Japan's complete withdrawal from China (the Hull note, November 26, 1941).

Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto conceived the attack as a pre-emptive strike to cripple the U.S. Pacific Fleet, preventing it from interfering with Japan's planned conquest of the "Southern Resource Area" (the Dutch East Indies and Southeast Asia). The plan was modeled on the 1940 British air attack on the Italian fleet at Taranto. Emperor Hirohito approved the attack plan on November 5, 1941, with final authorization on December 1.

## The Attack
On December 7, 1941, the Japanese Mobile Strike Force (Kido Butai), commanded by Vice Admiral Chūichi Nagumo, launched two waves of aircraft from six carriers. The first wave of 183 planes, led by Commander Mitsuo Fuchida, struck at 7:48 a.m. Hawaiian time, targeting battleships and airfields. The second wave of 171 planes followed, focusing on airfields and other ships.

The attack lasted about 90 minutes. Of eight U.S. battleships present, all were damaged and four were sunk (Arizona, Oklahoma, West Virginia, California). However, the three U.S. carriers were at sea and unharmed. Over 180 U.S. aircraft were destroyed. American casualties totaled 2,403 killed and 1,178 wounded. Japanese losses were 29 aircraft, 5 midget submarines, and 130 personnel.

Crucially, the Japanese did not attack Pearl Harbor's vital oil storage facilities, naval repair yards, or submarine base. Admiral Nagumo decided against a third strike to target these, fearing increased losses and the unknown location of U.S. carriers.

## Aftermath and Strategic Implications
The attack prompted immediate U.S. entry into World War II. President Franklin D. Roosevelt called December 7 "a date which will live in infamy." The U.S. declared war on Japan on December 8, and Germany and Italy declared war on the U.S. on December 11.

The attack was a tactical victory but a strategic failure for Japan. By sparing the repair yards and oil farms, the U.S. was able to recover its fleet's operational capability far more quickly than anticipated. The survival of the aircraft carriers, which became the centerpiece of U.S. naval power, was particularly consequential. The attack also unified American public opinion and industrial capacity for total war. As Rear Admiral Chūichi Hara later summarized, "We won a great tactical victory at Pearl Harbor and thereby lost the war."

## Terms
- **Kido Butai**: The Japanese Navy's Mobile Strike Force, the aircraft carrier task force that attacked Pearl Harbor.
- **Hull note**: A U.S. diplomatic proposal delivered on November 26, 1941, demanding Japan's complete withdrawal from China, which was seen as a final ultimatum.
- **Decisive battle doctrine (Kantai Kessen)**: A naval strategy focused on winning the war with a single, climactic fleet engagement, which influenced Japanese planning.
- **Midget submarine**: A small, two-person submarine used by Japan in the attack; five were launched, with limited success.
- **Armor-piercing bomb**: A heavy bomb designed to penetrate the thick armor of warship decks before exploding.
- **Combat air patrol (CAP)**: Defensive fighter aircraft launched from carriers to protect the fleet.
- **Southern Resource Area**: The Japanese term for the oil-rich Dutch East Indies and Southeast Asia, the primary objective of their expansion.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Intelligence failure**: Debate persists over why the U.S. was caught by surprise, despite some warnings and pre-war war games demonstrating Pearl Harbor's vulnerability. Conspiracy theories suggesting foreknowledge by U.S. officials are rejected by mainstream historians.
- **Third wave decision**: Historians debate whether a third Japanese strike targeting oil tanks and repair facilities would have crippled the U.S. Pacific Fleet for over a year, significantly prolonging the war.
- **Midget submarine contribution**: There is dispute over the effectiveness of the midget submarine attack, including whether one fired torpedoes that sank or heavily damaged battleships like the *West Virginia* and *Oklahoma*.
- **Japanese declaration of war**: Research has challenged the long-held view that the delayed delivery of Japan's 14-part message was a mere accident, suggesting internal debate within the Japanese government about providing proper notice.
