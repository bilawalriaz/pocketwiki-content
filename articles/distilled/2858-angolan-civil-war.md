# Angolan Civil War

## Overview
The Angolan Civil War (1975–2002) was a devastating conflict that began immediately after Angola's independence from Portugal. It was primarily a power struggle between the MPLA government, backed by the Soviet Union and Cuba, and the UNITA rebel movement, supported by the United States and apartheid South Africa. The war became a major Cold War proxy conflict, causing immense human suffering, displacing millions, and destroying the country's infrastructure. Its resolution came only with the death of UNITA's leader in 2002.

## Timeline
- **15 Jan 1975** — Alvor Accords signed, setting timeline for Angolan independence.
- **11 Nov 1975** — MPLA declares independence as the People's Republic of Angola.
- **1975–1976** — Cuban intervention (Operation Carlota) decisively aids MPLA against South African/UNITA forces.
- **9 Feb 1976** — U.S. Clark Amendment becomes law, banning covert aid to Angolan rebels.
- **27 May 1977** — Failed coup attempt by MPLA faction leader Nito Alves.
- **10 Sep 1979** — MPLA leader Agostinho Neto dies; José Eduardo dos Santos succeeds him.
- **11 Jul 1985** — U.S. repeals the Clark Amendment, allowing renewed covert aid to UNITA.
- **22 Dec 1988** — New York Accords signed, granting Namibian independence and ending direct foreign troop involvement.
- **31 May 1991** — Bicesse Accords signed, establishing a peace process and multi-party elections.
- **31 Oct 1992** — Post-election violence erupts in Luanda, shattering the peace process.
- **31 Oct 1994** — Lusaka Protocol signed, attempting to reintegrate UNITA into government.
- **22 Feb 2002** — UNITA leader Jonas Savimbi is killed in combat.
- **4 Apr 2002** — Ceasefire agreement signed in Luena, effectively ending the war.

## Body

### Origins and Main Combatants
The war's roots lay in Angola's colonial history and deep ethnic divisions among the Bantu populations, notably the Mbundu, Bakongo, and Ovimbundu. Three anti-colonial movements emerged: the urban, Mbundu-based MPLA; the rural, Bakongo-based FNLA; and the Ovimbundu-based UNITA, an FNLA offshoot. After independence, the MPLA, with Cuban and Soviet support, won the initial conventional war and became the government. The FNLA disintegrated, but UNITA, backed by the U.S. and South Africa, continued a guerrilla war.

### Cold War Proxy Conflict
The conflict became a central Cold War battleground. The Soviet Union and Cuba provided massive military aid to the MPLA government, with Cuban troops numbering up to 40,000 by 1985. The U.S., after initially covertly aiding both the FNLA and UNITA, focused on UNITA after the Clark Amendment's repeal in 1985. South Africa intervened directly to support UNITA and counter perceived communist expansion. The war was intertwined with the South African Border War and the Second Congo War.

### Major Phases and Peace Efforts
The war saw three major periods of fighting. A key turning point was the 1988 Battle of Cuito Cuanavale, a costly conventional stalemate that contributed to the New York Accords and the withdrawal of foreign troops. The 1991 Bicesse Accords led to elections in 1992, but post-election violence by MPLA forces and UNITA's return to war caused massive casualties. The 1994 Lusaka Protocol failed due to mutual distrust and continued arms flows, notably UNITA's diamond-funded purchases. The MPLA government launched a decisive offensive in 1999, crippling UNITA.

### Conclusion and Aftermath
The death of Jonas Savimbi in February 2002 led to a rapid ceasefire. The war killed an estimated 500,000–800,000 people and displaced over 4 million. The aftermath included a catastrophic humanitarian crisis, with widespread poverty, disease, and over 15 million landmines littering the countryside. UNITA demobilized and became a political party, but the country faced immense challenges in reconstruction and reconciliation.

## Terms
- **MPLA (People's Movement for the Liberation of Angola):** The communist, urban-based movement that became Angola's ruling party after 1975, backed by the Soviet Union and Cuba.
- **UNITA (National Union for the Total Independence of Angola):** The anti-communist, rural-based rebel movement led by Jonas Savimbi, supported by the U.S. and South Africa.
- **FNLA (National Front for the Liberation of Angola):** An early anti-colonial movement, primarily Bakongo, that was largely defeated in the initial phase of the civil war.
- **FLEC (Front for the Liberation of the Enclave of Cabinda):** A separatist group fighting for the independence of the oil-rich Cabinda province.
- **Cold War Proxy Conflict:** A war where major powers (like the U.S. and USSR) support opposing sides to advance their own interests without direct confrontation.
- **Clark Amendment:** A 1976 U.S. law banning covert aid to Angolan rebels; repealed in 1985.
- **Bicesse Accords (1991):** The first major peace agreement, which set the stage for the 1992 elections.
- **Lusaka Protocol (1994):** A peace agreement that attempted to reintegrate UNITA into the government and military after the 1992 election failure.
- **Landmines:** Explosive devices buried in the ground, which caused massive civilian casualties and hindered post-war recovery.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Responsibility for Post-Election Violence (1992):** The extent to which the MPLA government orchestrated the "Halloween Massacre" of UNITA supporters versus the role of spontaneous militia violence is a point of contention.
- **Battle of Cuito Cuanavale (1988):** Both sides claimed victory. Scholars debate whether the battle was a strategic stalemate or a decisive moment that forced South Africa to negotiate, with some viewing it as a turning point in the Cold War in Southern Africa.
- **Role of External Actors:** The degree to which the war was driven by internal Angolan dynamics versus the strategic interests of the U.S., USSR, Cuba, and South Africa remains a central debate in understanding the conflict's longevity and intensity.
- **UNITA's Diamond Trade:** The extent to which the international diamond trade, including the role of companies like De Beers, sustained UNITA's war effort despite sanctions is a key question in the conflict's economics.
