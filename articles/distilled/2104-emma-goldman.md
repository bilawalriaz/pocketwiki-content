# Emma Goldman

## Overview
Emma Goldman (1869–1940) was a Lithuanian-born anarchist revolutionary, writer, and lecturer who became one of the most influential figures in anarchist philosophy in North America and Europe. She championed anarchism, free speech, women's rights, and birth control, facing repeated imprisonment and deportation for her activism. Her life and writings, particularly her critiques of capitalism, the state, and the Soviet Union, have made her an enduring icon for feminist and anarchist movements.

## Timeline
- **1869** — Born in Kaunas, Lithuania (then Russian Empire).
- **1885** — Immigrated to the United States.
- **1892** — Co-conspirator in the failed assassination attempt on Henry Clay Frick during the Homestead Strike.
- **1893** — Imprisoned for "inciting to riot" after a speech in New York.
- **1901** — Arrested (but not charged) in connection with President McKinley's assassination.
- **1906** — Founded the anarchist journal *Mother Earth*.
- **1917** — Sentenced to two years in prison for conspiring against the draft.
- **1919** — Deported to Russia with Alexander Berkman during the First Red Scare.
- **1923** — Published *My Disillusionment in Russia*, criticizing the Bolshevik regime.
- **1931** — Published the first volume of her autobiography, *Living My Life*.
- **1936–1939** — Supported the anarchist revolution during the Spanish Civil War.
- **1940** — Died in Toronto, Canada.

## Body

### Early Life and Radicalization
Born into an Orthodox Jewish family, Goldman's childhood was marked by her father's violence and a rigid patriarchal household. Witnessing a peasant being whipped and experiencing sexual assault as a teenager fostered her lifelong distaste for violent authority. After immigrating to the U.S. in 1885, she was radicalized by the aftermath of the 1886 Haymarket affair. In New York, she met anarchist Johann Most, who trained her in public speaking, and Alexander Berkman, who became her lifelong friend and lover.

### Activism and Imprisonment
Goldman became a renowned lecturer on anarchism, women's rights, and social issues. In 1892, she supported Berkman's attempted assassination of industrialist Henry Clay Frick, an act of "propaganda of the deed" intended to inspire workers' revolt. The attempt failed, and Berkman was imprisoned. Goldman was later imprisoned for "inciting to riot" (1893) and for illegally distributing birth control information (1916). In 1906, she founded the anarchist journal *Mother Earth*. During World War I, she and Berkman organized the No-Conscription League, leading to their imprisonment and subsequent deportation to Russia in 1919.

### Disillusionment and Later Years
Initially supportive of the Bolshevik Revolution, Goldman quickly became disillusioned with the Soviet state's repression, particularly after the violent suppression of the Kronstadt rebellion in 1921. She left Russia and published *My Disillusionment in Russia* (1923). Living in exile in Europe and Canada, she wrote her autobiography, *Living My Life* (1931/1935). In her final years, she actively supported the anarchist revolution in the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939), editing the CNT-FAI Information Bulletin. She died in Toronto in 1940.

### Philosophy and Legacy
Goldman's anarchism was intensely personal, believing that anarchists must live their ideals. She advocated for the liberation of the mind from religion, the body from property, and society from government. While she initially supported targeted violence as a revolutionary tactic, her experiences in the Soviet Union led her to argue that institutionalized terrorism becomes counter-revolutionary. A passionate critic of capitalism, the state, and the prison system, she was also a pioneering advocate for women's sexual freedom, birth control, and gay rights. Though her fame faded after her death, she was rediscovered by feminist and anarchist scholars in the 1970s, becoming an enduring icon of rebellion and personal liberty.

## Terms
- **Anarchism**: A political philosophy advocating the abolition of the state and all forms of hierarchical authority in favor of free, voluntary association and self-governed societies.
- **Propaganda of the deed**: An anarchist concept advocating targeted violence or dramatic actions to inspire the masses to revolt against oppressive systems.
- **Attentat**: A specific act of political assassination or violence intended as propaganda of the deed.
- **Free love**: The belief that individuals should be free to engage in romantic or sexual relationships without restriction from the state, religion, or social convention.
- **Anarcha-feminism**: A branch of anarchism that analyzes and opposes patriarchy as a hierarchy to be resisted alongside the state and capitalism.
- **Syndicalism**: A revolutionary trade union movement that advocates worker control of the economy through direct action and general strikes.
- **Comstock Law**: A U.S. federal law (1873) that criminalized the distribution of materials deemed "obscene," including information about contraception.
- **Palmer Raids**: A series of raids and mass arrests of suspected radicals and anarchists in the U.S. during the First Red Scare (1919–1920).

## Debates and Open Questions
- **On Violence**: Goldman's evolving stance on violence—initially supporting "propaganda of the deed" but later condemning state institutionalized terrorism—remains a central debate in anarchist theory regarding means and ends.
- **On the Soviet Union**: Her sharp critique of the Bolshevik regime, which she saw as a betrayal of revolutionary ideals, contributed to a lasting schism between anarchists and authoritarian socialists.
- **On Feminism**: While she rejected first-wave feminism's focus on suffrage, her advocacy for sexual liberation and reproductive rights has led to her being claimed as a foundational figure in anarcha-feminism, raising questions about the relationship between anarchism and gender politics.
- **Legacy and Iconography**: The popularization of the quote "If I can't dance, I don't want to be in your revolution"—which she likely never said—highlights the tension between Goldman as a historical figure and her symbolic status in popular culture.
