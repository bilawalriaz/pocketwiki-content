# Virus

## Overview

A virus is a submicroscopic infectious agent that replicates only inside the living cells of a host organism. Viruses infect all life forms—from animals and plants to bacteria and archaea—and are the most numerous biological entities on Earth. They consist of genetic material (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein capsid, sometimes enclosed in a lipid envelope. The study of viruses, virology, is a subspecialty of microbiology. Since the discovery of the tobacco mosaic virus in the 1890s, over 16,000 virus species have been described.

## Timeline

- **1892** — Dmitri Ivanovsky demonstrates filterable infectious agent in tobacco plants
- **1898** — Martinus Beijerinck identifies tobacco mosaic virus, founding virology
- **1926** — Thomas Milton Rivers defines viruses as obligate parasites
- **1931** — Transmission electron microscope invented, enabling virus visualization
- **1950** — Barbara McClintock discovers transposons ("jumping genes")
- **1962** — Lwoff, Horne, and Tournier develop first virus classification system
- **1966** — International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) formed
- **1981** — HIV/AIDS first recognized
- **2002** — First synthetic virus created
- **2019** — SARS-CoV-2 emerges in Wuhan, China

## Body

### Structure and Morphology

Virions—the independent viral particles outside host cells—consist of genetic material surrounded by a protein capsid made of capsomeres. Some viruses possess a lipid envelope derived from host membranes. Five main morphological types exist: helical (tobacco mosaic virus), icosahedral (most animal viruses), prolate (bacteriophage heads), enveloped (influenza, HIV), and complex (poxviruses). Giant viruses like Mimivirus (400 nm capsid) challenge size-based definitions of viruses.

### Genome Diversity

Viruses exhibit extraordinary genomic diversity, containing either DNA or RNA genomes that may be single-stranded or double-stranded, segmented or continuous. RNA viruses generally have smaller genomes due to higher replication error rates. The smallest genomes belong to circoviruses (2 kb), while pandoraviruses possess the largest (2 Mb). Genome size correlates with replication fidelity: DNA viruses typically have larger genomes than RNA viruses.

### Replication Cycle

The viral life cycle follows six stages: attachment (specific receptor binding determines host range), penetration (entry via endocytosis or membrane fusion), uncoating (capsid removal), replication (genome multiplication and protein synthesis), assembly (self-organization of components), and release (lysis or budding). Some viruses integrate into host genomes as proviruses/prophages during lysogenic cycles.

### Origins and Evolution

Three main hypotheses attempt to explain viral origins: regressive (from parasitic cells losing genes), escape (from plasmids/transposons), and co-evolution (virus-first from prebiotic molecules). Viruses are ancient entities predating the divergence of life's three domains. They serve as major agents of horizontal gene transfer, driving genetic diversity across species.

### Classification Systems

The ICTV maintains a hierarchical taxonomy (realm to species) with 15 ranks as of 2018. The Baltimore classification system groups viruses by mRNA synthesis mechanisms into seven groups based on genome type and replication strategy. Both systems complement each other in modern virology.

### Host Interactions and Disease

Viruses cause diseases ranging from common cold to Ebola. Mechanisms include cell lysis, apoptosis, and immune evasion. Some establish latent infections (herpesviruses), while others cause chronic disease (HIV, hepatitis). Viruses are established carcinogens, with human papillomavirus, hepatitis B/C, Epstein-Barr virus, and others linked to various cancers.

### Immune Defense and Treatment

Host defenses include innate immunity (interferons, RNA interference), humoral immunity (antibodies IgM/IgG), and cell-mediated immunity (T cells). Vaccines provide acquired immunity using live-attenuated, killed, subunit, or RNA approaches. Antiviral drugs target specific replication stages, with nucleoside analogues and protease inhibitors among major classes.

## Terms

**Virion**: Complete virus particle consisting of nucleic acid surrounded by protein coat, capable of infecting host cells.

**Capsid**: Protein coat surrounding viral genetic material, composed of protein subunits called capsomeres.

**Baltimore classification**: System grouping viruses into seven classes based on genome type and mRNA synthesis mechanism.

**Quasispecies**: Population of closely related viral variants with slightly different genome sequences, common in RNA viruses.

**Provirus/Prophage**: Viral genome integrated into host chromosome during lysogenic cycle.

**Antigenic drift**: Accumulation of point mutations in viral genomes conferring evolutionary advantages.

**Antigenic shift**: Major genomic reassortment creating novel viral strains, particularly in influenza.

**Viroplasm**: Cytoplasmic inclusion body where some viruses replicate.

**Virome**: Complete set of viruses present in an organism or environment.

**Oncolytic virus**: Genetically modified virus engineered to selectively destroy cancer cells.

## Debates and Open Questions

The fundamental question of whether viruses constitute life remains unresolved. While they possess genes, evolve through natural selection, and reproduce, they lack cellular structure and independent metabolism. The virocell model proposes considering infected cells as viral "living forms" with virions analogous to spores.

Viral origins remain debated among three competing hypotheses, none definitively proven. Molecular evidence suggests viruses predate the last universal common ancestor, but their exact evolutionary pathway is unclear.

The classification of giant viruses challenges traditional boundaries between viruses and cellular life, raising questions about the minimum requirements for viral identity.

Whether all known viruses share a common ancestor is uncertain, with evidence suggesting multiple independent origins through various mechanisms.
