# Carl Linnaeus

## Overview
Carl Linnaeus (1707–1778) was a Swedish biologist and physician who formalized binomial nomenclature, the modern system for naming organisms. His work established the foundational framework for taxonomy, the science of classification, and he is regarded as the "father of modern taxonomy." His system and publications became the global standard for organizing biological knowledge.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Linnaeus was born to a Lutheran minister who taught him Latin and botany. After early tutoring and schooling in Växjö, he was guided by physician Johan Rothman toward medicine and botany. He studied at Lund University (1727) before moving to Uppsala University (1728), where he began lecturing on botany in 1730. He developed his own plant classification system based on the number of stamens and pistils.

### Expeditions and the Dutch Republic
Linnaeus conducted several government-funded expeditions through Sweden (e.g., Lapland in 1732) to catalog flora and fauna. In 1735, he traveled to the Dutch Republic, where he quickly earned a medical doctorate. There, he published the first edition of *Systema Naturae* (1735) and worked for botanist George Clifford, producing *Hortus Cliffortianus*. He also visited England, promoting his classification system to botanists like Philip Miller.

### Professorship and Major Works
Returning to Sweden in 1738, Linnaeus became a professor at Uppsala in 1741. He published regional floras (*Flora Suecica*, 1745) and the comprehensive *Species Plantarum* (1753), which described over 7,300 plant species and is the accepted starting point for botanical nomenclature. The 10th edition of *Systema Naturae* (1758) did the same for zoology. He was ennobled as Carl von Linné in 1761.

### Taxonomic System and Human Classification
Linnaeus created a hierarchical classification system (kingdom, class, order, genus, species) based on shared physical characteristics. He popularized binomial nomenclature (a two-part scientific name) and the symbols ♂ and ♀ for sex. Notably, he classified humans (*Homo sapiens*) within the animal kingdom, grouping them with primates. His initial human taxonomy, which divided *Homo sapiens* into four varieties based on continent and color, has been criticized for cementing colonial stereotypes and providing foundations for scientific racism.

### The "Apostles" and Legacy
Linnaeus trained many students, sending seventeen of his most devoted "apostles" on global expeditions to collect specimens. This network spread his taxonomic system worldwide. His collections and library were purchased after his death by James Edward Smith, who founded the Linnean Society of London in 1788. His work remains the cornerstone of modern biological classification.

## Terms
- **Binomial Nomenclature**: The formal system of naming species using two Latinized names (genus and specific epithet), formalized by Linnaeus.
- **Taxonomy**: The science of classification, especially of organisms; Linnaeus established its modern framework.
- **Systema Naturae**: Linnaeus's major work, first published in 1735, which outlined his classification system for nature.
- **Species Plantarum**: Published in 1753, this work is the internationally accepted starting point for the nomenclature of plants.
- **Type Specimen**: The single specimen upon which the description and name of a new species is based. Linnaeus designated himself as the type for *Homo sapiens*.
- **Apostles**: Linnaeus's term for his most promising students who traveled globally to collect specimens using his system.
- **Anthropomorpha**: Linnaeus's original term for the primate group that included humans, later replaced by *Primates*.
- **Cameralism**: An economic doctrine emphasizing state intervention in the economy, which influenced Linnaeus's applied science.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Human Taxonomy and Scientific Racism**: Linnaeus's classification of humans into four varieties based on skin color and continent has been criticized for establishing a biological basis for racial stereotypes and colonial ideologies. Scholars debate whether his intent was purely descriptive or if it inherently implied hierarchy.
- **Theological Concerns**: His placement of humans within the animal kingdom, alongside apes, challenged the prevailing "great chain of being" and theological views of human uniqueness, drawing criticism from contemporaries.
- **Legacy of Classification**: While his hierarchical system is foundational, modern taxonomy has significantly revised his groupings and principles, incorporating genetics and evolutionary relationships. The debate continues on how to contextualize his 18th-century framework within modern science.
