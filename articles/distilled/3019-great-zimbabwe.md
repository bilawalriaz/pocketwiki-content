# Great Zimbabwe

## Overview
Great Zimbabwe was a medieval city in southeastern Africa, serving as the capital of the Kingdom of Great Zimbabwe from the 13th century. It is the largest stone structure in precolonial Southern Africa and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, representing a major center of political power, trade, and craftsmanship built by ancestors of the Shona people. Its ruins and the subsequent colonial controversy over their origins are central to understanding the region's history and the formation of the modern nation of Zimbabwe.

## Timeline
- **c. 1000 AD** — Settlement of the Great Zimbabwe area begins.
- **11th–15th centuries** — Major construction of the stone city complexes occurs.
- **c. 1300** — Great Zimbabwe rises to prominence, replacing Mapungubwe as the regional power.
- **1531** — First recorded European description by Portuguese captain Vicente Pegado.
- **1867–1871** — Ruins rediscovered by Europeans Adam Render and Karl Mauch.
- **1890s–1920s** — Site is looted and pillaged by European antiquarians.
- **1905–1906** — First scientific excavations by David Randall-MacIver confirm medieval, African origins.
- **1929** — Gertrude Caton-Thompson's excavations further confirm Bantu origin.
- **1950s** — Consensus on African origins is established among archaeologists.
- **1980** — The modern state of Zimbabwe is named after the site.

## Body

### Construction and Society
Construction of the stone buildings began in the 11th century and continued for over 300 years. The site's growth was fueled by agriculture, cattle herding, and crucial long-distance trade. The ruling dynasty used wealth to build elaborate stone enclosures and daga (earthen daub) houses. The city's population is debated; traditional estimates suggest a peak of 18,000, while recent studies indicate it likely did not exceed 10,000. The stone structures were likely royal or elite buildings, while the majority of the population lived in mud-and-wood houses.

### Architecture and Features
The ruins consist of three main architectural groups: the Hill Complex (11th–13th centuries), the Great Enclosure (13th–15th centuries), and the Valley Complex (14th–16th centuries). The Great Enclosure features dry-stone walls up to 11 meters high and a Conical Tower. Scholars debate whether these complexes represent successive royal residences or had distinct ritual, residential, and administrative functions. Notable artifacts include eight carved soapstone Zimbabwe Birds, gold jewelry, and imported goods like Chinese porcelain, indicating extensive trade networks.

### Trade and Economy
Great Zimbabwe became a major trading hub after 1300, dominating routes from the goldfields of the Zimbabwean Plateau to the Swahili coast. It exported gold and ivory via river networks to coastal cities like Sofala, which linked it to Indian Ocean trade. Archaeological evidence shows imports from China and Persia. Gold working was a significant craft, with crucibles and tools found on site. The kingdom's wealth and influence depended on controlling these trade networks.

### Decline and Abandonment
The cause of Great Zimbabwe's decline is unknown. Factors may include resource depletion (possibly water or agricultural land), a global economic downturn reducing gold demand from the early 15th century, and political fragmentation. Oral tradition cites a search for salt as a reason for migration. By the late 15th century, power shifted to new dynasties like the Mutapa Empire to the north and the Kingdom of Butua to the west. The site was likely abandoned by the 17th century.

### The "Zimbabwe Controversial" and Colonial Era
The origins of Great Zimbabwe were long disputed, a debate termed the "Zimbabwe controversy." Colonial authorities and early European visitors, like Karl Mauch, attributed the ruins to non-African builders (e.g., Phoenicians, Arabs, or biblical figures) to deny indigenous African achievement. This narrative was enforced by the Rhodesian government, which censored archaeologists. Scientific excavations by Randall-MacIver (1905) and Caton-Thompson (1929) proved the structures were built by medieval Bantu-speaking ancestors of the Shona. Consensus on African origins was reached by the 1950s.

### Modern Significance
After independence in 1980, the site became a national symbol. The modern state was named Zimbabwe, derived from the site's name. The soapstone birds were incorporated into the national flag and coat of arms. The site remains a living cultural landscape for local communities, though tensions exist between its management as an archaeological monument and its spiritual significance. A university was established near the ruins in the early 21st century.

## Terms
- **Dry stone**: Construction technique using stones stacked without mortar.
- **Daga**: An earthen building material, a mix of mud and gravel, used for walls and floors.
- **Zimbabwe**: Shona term meaning "houses of stone" or "venerated houses," referring to the stone ruins.
- **Mambo**: The title for the king or ruler of the Kingdom of Great Zimbabwe.
- **Svikiro**: A spirit medium in Shona religion, believed to communicate with ancestors.
- **Mhondoro**: A powerful ancestral spirit, often of a founding ruler, consulted through mediums.
- **Gokomere**: An early Iron Age Bantu culture in the region, considered ancestral to the builders of Great Zimbabwe.
- **Zimbabwe Birds**: Carved soapstone bird monoliths found at the site, likely symbolic of the bateleur eagle.
- **Mapungubwe**: An earlier southern African state that declined around 1300, preceding Great Zimbabwe's rise.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Population Size**: Estimates vary significantly, from traditional figures of 18,000 to recent archaeological models suggesting a peak of no more than 10,000.
- **Function of Complexes**: Scholars disagree on whether the Hill Complex, Great Enclosure, and Valley Complex were successive royal residences or served distinct ritual, administrative, and domestic functions.
- **Cause of Decline**: The exact reasons for abandonment are unknown. Proposed causes include climate change (considered unlikely), resource depletion (water, salt, agricultural land), economic shifts, and political fragmentation.
- **Local vs. Archaeological Perspectives**: There is ongoing tension between treating the site as a historical archaeological monument and recognizing it as a living cultural and spiritual landscape for local communities.
