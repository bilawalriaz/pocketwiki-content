# Narrative

## Overview
A narrative is an account of connected events or experiences, presented through words, images, or both, ranging from brief anecdotes to multi-volume epics. It is a fundamental human activity—predating writing and spanning all cultures—serving as the primary vehicle for transmitting history, values, and identity. Narratology, the formal study of narrative, identifies essential components (character, conflict, plot, setting, theme, narrative mode) and analyzes how these structures function across literature, film, psychology, sociology, and even military strategy. Scholars debate whether narrative is an innate cognitive framework for sense-making or a constructed rhetorical device, and how its power to simplify complexity creates both understanding and the "narrative fallacy."

## Timeline
- **Prehistory** — Oral storytelling emerges as a ubiquitous component of human communication and culture.
- **c. 2600–1900 BCE** — Indus Valley Civilization (Lothal) artifacts depict scenes resembling fables later recorded in the *Panchatantra* (birds/fish/fox-like animal; thirsty crow/deer).
- **Antiquity** — Written narratives survive from Egyptian, Greek, Chinese, and Indian civilizations recording histories and mythologies.
- **15th century** — Terms "narration" and "narrative" enter English from French (ultimately from Latin *narrare*, "to tell").
- **18th century** — Rise of the novel establishes the narrator as distinct from the author, prompting literary theory on perspective.
- **1921** — Janet Bacon publishes *The Voyage of The Argonauts*, categorizing folklore into Myth (explanatory), Legend (historical tradition), and Folktale (imaginative entertainment).
- **1979** — Lawrence Stone detects a return to narrative in historiography amid the rise of social-science models.
- **1981** — Georges Dumézil publishes essay applying trifunctionalism to Norse gods (Odin/Tyr sovereignty).
- **2000** — Gubrium and Holstein publish *The Self We Live By*, developing a constructionist framework for narrative identity (institutional vs. everyday stories).
- **2009/2012** — Gubrium and Holstein publish *Analyzing Narrative Reality* and *Varieties of Narrative Analysis*, formalizing sociological narrative inquiry.

## Body

### Definition and Scope
A narrative recounts a sequence of connected events—actual or fictitious—to an audience via a narrator (explicit in written forms). It differs from mere description by requiring agents and temporal progression. Narratives encompass nonfiction (biography, journalism, historiography) and fiction (novels, myths, films), with the latter departing from factual basis through imaginary or supernatural elements. The term derives from Latin *narrare* ("to tell"), from *gnarus* ("knowing/skilled"), entering English via French in the 15th century.

### Basic Elements of Narrative
Scholars identify six essential elements, often termed the elements of fiction:
*   **Character:** Agents (protagonists, antagonists, ensemble casts) whose choices drive the plot. Archetypes (hero, villain) and stock characters provide recognizable patterns; consistency of characterization governs audience empathy and expectation.
*   **Conflict:** The tension driving thought and action, classified as character vs. character, nature, society, fate, or self. Internal conflict (anxiety, indecision) often accompanies external struggles. Resolution occurs when conflict ends.
*   **Narrative Mode:** The author’s framing choices: perspective (first-person "I" vs. third-person impersonal), scope of information, language style, and medium. *Narration* (commentary) is required in writing but optional elsewhere.
*   **Plot:** The causal sequence of events (scenes, plot points) structured via *emplotment*. Cause-and-effect links create suspense (anxious anticipation) as protagonists confront and resolve conflict.
*   **Setting:** Time, place, and social context; can function as an active agent affecting plot and character.
*   **Theme:** Abstract, subjective underlying ideas open to audience interpretation, often diverging from authorial intent.

### History and Early Evidence
Storytelling predates recorded history. The earliest archaeological evidence comes from the Indus Valley site Lothal (c. 2600–1900 BCE). On one large vessel, the artist depicts birds with fish in their beaks resting in a tree, while a fox-like animal stands below—a scene bearing resemblance to "The Fox and the Crow" in the *Panchatantra*. On a miniature jar, the story of the thirsty crow and deer is depicted: the deer could not drink from the narrow mouth of the jar, while the crow succeeded by dropping stones into it. Written narratives from ancient Egypt, Greece, China, and India preserved mythologies and histories. Oral tradition remained the dominant mode until literate societies enabled fixed written records.

### Narratology: Formal Study of Narrative
**Structural Roots:** Russian Formalism (Victor Shklovsky, Vladimir Propp) initiated structural analysis; Propp identified 31 functional components in folk tales. The Prague School and French scholars (Lévi-Strauss, Barthes) expanded this, raising questions about text, culture, and genre division.
**Literary Theory:** Until the late 19th century, criticism focused on poetry (where narrator ≈ author). The 18th-century novel introduced distinct narrators, making perspective and interpretive knowledge central. Todorov’s model posits a cyclical three-part structure: equilibrium → disruption → restoration. Critics (e.g., Didier Costa) argue such models are Western-centric; framing and cultural context are vital for oral societies.
**Aesthetics:** Narrative art relies on structure (exposition–climax–denouement), temporality (past/present/future), character ("most important component," per David Lodge), polyphonic voices (Bakhtin), rhetorical thrust (Booth), tropes (Hayden White), intertextuality, and *Bildungsroman* (identity development).
**Psychology:** Narrative constructs self-identity and memory; its breakdown links to psychosis, repair to recovery (narrative therapy). Owen Flanagan writes, "Evidence strongly suggests that humans in all cultures come to cast their own identity in some sort of narrative form. We are inveterate storytellers," noting narrative may refer to psychological processes in self-identity, memory, and meaning-making. Illness narratives follow *restitution* (cure), *chaos* (decline, e.g., Alzheimer’s), or *quest* (transformation, e.g., cancer survivorship) patterns. Language in self-narratives correlates with Big Five personality traits (e.g., extraversion ↔ social words; neuroticism ↔ negative emotion words).
**Social Sciences:** Narratives provide explanatory frameworks where statistical analysis fails (case studies). They reveal dense, contextual social forces. Gubrium and Holstein’s constructionist approach analyzes the interplay of "big stories" (institutional discourses) and "little stories" (everyday accounts).
**Inquiry & Fallacy:** Humans impose story structures on complex experience (epistemological assumption of *narrative inquiry*). This creates the *narrative fallacy*: preferring meaningful stories over data, leading to unwarranted causal claims. Validity criteria differ: statisticians demand reliability/validity; interpretive scholars prefer believability and interpretive validity.
**Mathematical Sociology:** Comparative narratives model action-driven events as directed graphs (states, actors, actions). *Bayesian narratives* (Peter Abell) compute likelihood ratios for causal links ("action *a* causes *b*") using subjective evidence ("I did *b* because of *a*") when comparative cases are insufficient.

### Narrative in Specific Media
**Music:** Linearity and terms (fugue: exposition, discussion) suggest narrativity. Adorno argues music "narrates without narrative"; Abbate finds "narrating voices" in disruptive gestures; Nattiez contends narrative resides only in the listener’s imagined plot (influenced by titles). Consensus remains unsettled.
**Film:** Lacks a textual narrator and the reader’s unique visual manifestation. Instead, it substitutes *cinematic devices*: cinematography, editing, sound design (diegetic/non-diegetic), and *mise-en-scène* (onscreen arrangement). These create a "visual narrative instance" (Jose Landa) unbound by theatrical place/time constraints.

### Mythology and Cultural Storytelling
**Categories (Frazer/Bacon):**
*   **Myth:** Explanatory intent (natural phenomena, rituals); source in *reason*; corresponds to *science*. Cosmological perspective, no verifiable author, sacred/true status.
*   **Legend:** True tradition based on real people/places (Agamemnon, King Arthur, Davy Crockett); source in *memory*; corresponds to *history*.
*   **Folktale:** Pure imagination for entertainment; no belief required; source in *imagination*; corresponds to *romance*.
**Mythic Structure (Dumézil):** Indo-European myths reflect *trifunctionalism*: (1) Sovereignty (magical/juridical—kings/gods like Odin/Tyr: cosmic order/justice); (2) Heroism (warriors/Thor: strength, Valhalla afterlife); (3) Fertility/Prosperity (commoners/Freyr: crops, pleasure). This structure permeated life, reframing death as heroic transcendence and prefiguring the Christian Trinity.
**Cultural Function:** Oral storytelling transmits history, values, and identity. Indigenous pedagogies use open-ended narratives to foster holistic thinking and self-reflection (e.g., Western Apache warning tales, Métis "Humorous Horse Story," Navajo dead-animal metaphors). Stories teach behavior, communal belonging, and interconnectedness.

### Historiography and Military Strategy
**Historiography:** Lawrence Stone (1979) defines historical narrative as chronological, single-story, descriptive, people-focused, and particular. Mark Bevir argues narratives explain actions by appealing to the beliefs and desires of actors and by locating webs of beliefs in the context of historical traditions—an alternative form of explanation to natural science. Social-science historians criticize narrative’s anecdotal narrowness.
**Military:** The "battle of the narratives" is an information warfare concept: shaping the cognitive dimension (reasons/outcomes of conflict) just as physical warfare shapes terrain.

### Storytelling Rights
Storytelling rights may be broadly defined as the ethics of sharing narratives (including—but not limited to—firsthand, secondhand, and imagined stories). Amy Shuman defines it as "the important and precarious relationship between narrative and event and, specifically, between the participants in an event and the reporters who claim the right to talk about what happened." The ethics of retelling other people's stories implicates questions of consent, empathy, and accurate representation: whose story is being told and how, what is the story's purpose or aim, what does the story promise (empathy, redemption, authenticity, clarification)—and at whose benefit? While storytelling can function as a powerful tool for agency and advocacy, it can also lead to misunderstanding and exploitation. Academic disciplines such as performance, folklore, literature, anthropology, cultural studies, and other social sciences may involve the study of storytelling rights, often hinging on ethics.

### Specialized Applications
*   **Narrative environment:** Architectural/exhibition design ("stories in space") or virtual game worlds.
*   **Narrative film:** Fiction or documentary (filmed reality) or animation.
*   **Narrative history:** Chronological factual writing (vs. thematic).
*   **Narrative photography/poetry:** Image/verse telling a story.
*   **Narrative-based learning:** Stories as pedagogical tools.
*   **Metanarrative (master narrative):** Higher-level cultural schema ordering knowledge/experience (e.g., masterplots: recurrent skeletal stories shaping identity and values).

## Terms
- ****Narratology**** — The formal academic study of stories, storytelling, and narrative structure.
- ****Emplotment**** — The process of structuring and ordering a narrative’s events into a causal plot.
- ****Narrative Mode**** — The set of choices (perspective, scope, language, medium) determining *how* a story is told.
- ****Unreliable Narrator**** — A viewpoint character whose account is presented as suspicious, doubtful, or unbelievable (common in noir).
- ****Trifunctionalism**** — Dumézil’s theory that Indo-European mythology mirrors three societal functions: sovereignty, heroism, and fertility.
- ****Narrative Fallacy**** — The cognitive bias to impose meaningful story structures on random/complex data, obscuring statistical reality.
- ****Bayesian Narratives**** — Peter Abell’s method using subjective evidence to compute likelihood ratios for causal links in a directed graph when statistical cases are insufficient.
- ****Mise-en-scène**** — The arrangement of subjects, setting, and visual elements onscreen in film; a substitute for textual narration.
- ****Metanarrative**** — A master cultural schema (grand narrative) that orders and explains knowledge and experience; related to recurrent masterplots.
- ****Restitution/Chaos/Quest Narratives**** — Three archetypal illness narrative patterns: temporary detour/cure, permanent decline, or transformative opportunity.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Narrativity:** What specific quality distinguishes narrative from non-narrative discourse? (No consensus.)
*   **Western Bias:** Are structural models (Todorov, Propp) universally applicable or ethnocentric? Costa argues for a transformative model inclusive of oral cultures.
*   **Literariness:** Can narrative exist without literature, and vice versa? Critics challenge the necessity of "literariness" for narrative analysis.
*   **Music:** Is narrative intrinsic to music (Abbate’s disruptive voices) or purely a listener’s metaphorical construction (Nattiez)?
*   **Validity Criteria:** Should narrative research use statistical validity/reliability or interpretive criteria (believability, emotional/moral clarity)?
*   **Cognitive Primacy:** Is narrative the *fundamental* nature of self (Flanagan) or a rhetorical tool among others?
*   **Myth Origins:** Without fixed texts, can the precise origin of any myth be reliably traced?
