# Gulf of Mexico

## Overview
The Gulf of Mexico is a large oceanic basin and marginal sea of the Atlantic Ocean, surrounded by the United States, Mexico, and Cuba. It is a major hub for global shipping, petroleum production, and biodiversity, while also significantly influencing regional weather patterns, including hurricanes and tornadoes.

## Timeline
- **c. 300 million years ago** — Gulf basin begins forming due to plate tectonics.
- **Late Jurassic (c. 145–150 million years ago)** — Seafloor spreading stops; connection to Atlantic established.
- **c. 1200–400 BC** — Olmec civilization flourishes along the southern Gulf coast.
- **1517** — Francisco Hernández de Córdoba discovers the Yucatán Peninsula for Spain.
- **1519** — Hernán Cortés leads expedition leading to the Conquest of Mexico.
- **1550** — Name "Gulf of Mexico" first appears on a world map.
- **1699** — First permanent European settlement, Fort Maurepas, founded by France.
- **1979–1980** — Ixtoc I oil spill in the Bay of Campeche becomes the gulf's largest.
- **2010** — Deepwater Horizon oil spill occurs off the Louisiana coast.
- **January 2025** — U.S. President issues executive order to rename the gulf "Gulf of America."

## Body

### Geology and Formation
The Gulf of Mexico basin formed about 300 million years ago through plate tectonics. Before the late Triassic, the area was dry land within the supercontinent Pangaea. Rifting occurred in two main phases: first in the late Triassic–early Jurassic, creating rift valleys, and then through the early and middle Jurassic, which stretched and thinned the continental crust. This process eventually led to seafloor spreading and the formation of oceanic crust, flooding the basin to create an enclosed sea. Seafloor spreading ceased at the end of the Jurassic (c. 145–150 million years ago). Subsequent cooling, subsidence, and massive sediment deposition from rivers like the Mississippi have shaped the basin to its current state.

### Hydrology and Circulation
Water circulation in the Gulf is part of the larger Atlantic system. The Yucatán Current flows in from the Caribbean Sea, and the Florida Current exits through the Straits of Florida, becoming the Gulf Stream. These currents are connected by the Loop Current, a powerful, clockwise-flowing current that dominates the Gulf's circulation. The Loop Current sheds large, warm-core eddies that drift westward. The Gulf's relatively narrow connections to the open ocean result in small tidal ranges.

### Human History and Exploration
The earliest known human remains in the region date to 10,000 BC. After climatic changes around 5,000 BC, more stable waterways supported fishing, agriculture, and permanent settlements. The Maya civilization used the Gulf as a major trade route. Spanish exploration began in the early 16th century, with expeditions by Hernández de Córdoba (1517) and Cortés (1519) leading to colonization. The first permanent European settlement, Fort Maurepas, was founded by the French in 1699.

### Economy and Resources
The Gulf is one of the world's largest offshore petroleum production regions, supplying 14% of U.S. crude oil. Its wide continental shelves support major fishing industries for shrimp, oysters, and fish like red snapper. Other key industries include shipping, petrochemical processing, and tourism.

### Environmental Threats
Major threats include agricultural runoff, oil drilling, and pollution. A hypoxic "dead zone" along the Texas-Louisiana coast, caused by nutrient pollution, reached a record 22,730 km² in 2017. The Gulf has experienced catastrophic oil spills, most notably the Ixtoc I blowout (1979–1980) and the Deepwater Horizon disaster (2010), which released an estimated 62,000 barrels per day at its peak.

### Meteorological Significance
The Gulf's warm waters (averaging 28°C in summer) fuel powerful Atlantic hurricanes. Its moisture is a primary factor in the formation of tornadoes in the U.S. "Tornado Alley." Sea surface temperatures in the Gulf increased by approximately 1°C from 1970 to 2020, about twice the global ocean average rate.

## Terms
- **Marginal sea:** A body of water partially enclosed by land but connected to a larger ocean.
- **Continental shelf:** The gently sloping, submerged edge of a continent.
- **Loop Current:** A powerful, warm ocean current that flows into the Gulf of Mexico from the Caribbean and exits through the Florida Straits.
- **Evaporites:** Sedimentary rocks (like salt) formed by the evaporation of seawater.
- **Salt tectonics:** The deformation of salt layers under pressure, causing them to flow and form structures like domes.
- **Hypoxic dead zone:** An area of water with low oxygen levels that cannot support most marine life, often caused by nutrient pollution.
- **Chemosynthetic communities:** Ecosystems where organisms derive energy from chemical reactions (e.g., at cold seeps) rather than sunlight.
- **Passive margin:** A continental margin not at a tectonic plate boundary, characterized by a wide continental shelf.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The 2025 U.S. executive order to rename the Gulf of Mexico the "Gulf of America" is controversial, with objections from Mexico and mixed public opinion in the U.S.
- The exact identity of the early 19th-century shipwreck known as the "Mardi Gras" remains a mystery.
- The long-term ecological impacts of major oil spills like Deepwater Horizon are still being studied.
