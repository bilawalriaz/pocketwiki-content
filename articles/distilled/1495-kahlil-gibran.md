# Kahlil Gibran

## Overview
Kahlil Gibran (1883–1931) was a Lebanese-American writer, poet, and visual artist whose work bridged Arabic and Western literary traditions. Born in Bsharri, Ottoman Mount Lebanon, he immigrated to Boston in 1895, where his artistic talent attracted the patronage of photographer F. Holland Day and, crucially, Mary Haskell, who funded his art studies in Paris (1908–1910) and edited his English prose. Gibran wrote in both Arabic and English, pioneering a modernist, colloquial Arabic style that broke from classical forms, while his English works—most famously *The Prophet* (1923)—synthesized Biblical cadences, Blakean symbolism, Sufi mysticism, and Nietzschean philosophy into a universalist spirituality. He co-founded the New York Pen League (1920), uniting Mahjar (émigré) poets. Despite dying at 48 from cirrhosis and tuberculosis, he achieved transatlantic fame; *The Prophet* has sold millions of copies in over 100 languages. His legacy endures as a cultural icon in Lebanon and a touchstone for 1960s counterculture and New Age movements globally.

## Body

### Childhood and Immigration
Gibran was born into a poor Maronite family in Bsharri. His father, Khalil, was imprisoned for embezzlement in 1891, leading to property confiscation. His mother, Kamila, emigrated with her four children to Boston’s South End in 1895. Gibran attended Quincy School and art classes at Denison House, where teachers introduced him to F. Holland Day. Day became an early mentor, exhibiting Gibran’s drawings and introducing him to Josephine Preston Peabody. Seeking to ground him in his heritage, his family sent him back to Beirut at 15 to study at the Maronite-run Collège de la Sagesse (1898–1901), where he excelled in Arabic and French and co-founded a student magazine. He toured Europe en route to Paris for art studies but returned to Boston in 1902 after his sister Sultana’s death; his half-brother Boutros and mother died in 1903, leaving him reliant on his sister Marianna.

### Patronage, Paris, and Political Awakening
In 1904, Gibran met Mary Haskell, a headmistress nine years his senior, who became his primary benefactress, editor of his English works, and close companion (the romantic nature of their relationship remains debated). She funded his 1908–1910 stay at the Académie Julian in Paris. There, Gibran studied under Jean-Paul Laurens and Pierre Marcel-Béronneau, solidifying his Symbolist aspirations. He encountered Syrian political dissidents advocating revolt against the Ottomans after the 1908 Young Turk Revolution, influencing his anti-clerical writings like *Spirits Rebellious* (1908), which was burned in Beirut. He also began his "Temple of Art" pencil portraits of cultural heroes. A fraught episode occurred when his model Émilie Michel followed him to Paris, suffered an ectopic pregnancy, and underwent an abortion, straining his loyalty to Haskell.

### New York, The Pen League, and *The Prophet*
Gibran settled in New York in 1911, inhabiting the Tenth Street Studio Building ("The Hermitage") until his death. He joined the Golden Links Society, lecturing for Syrian independence. He published Arabic works including *Broken Wings* (1912) and *A Tear and a Smile* (1914), corresponding critically with Egyptian writer May Ziadeh. His 1914 Montross Gallery exhibition attracted painter Albert Pinkham Ryder, whose death prompted Gibran’s prose poem, bringing him first widespread U.S. attention. During WWI, he served the Syrian–Mount Lebanon Relief Committee and met Mikhail Naimy, a close friend and future biographer. In 1918, *The Madman* marked his English-language debut. He re-founded the Pen League in 1920 with Naimy, Rihani, and others. *The Prophet* (1923), a series of poetic sermons by the fictional Almustafa, sold well despite mixed reviews. Later works included *Sand and Foam* (1926) and *Jesus, the Son of Man* (1928). Declining health forced periods of convalescence, but he completed *The Earth Gods* (1931) shortly before his death.

### Writings: Forms, Themes, and Language
Gibran utilized diverse forms: poetry, parables, fables, essays, letters, and aphorisms. Themes included religion, justice, free will, love, and death. His style blended innovation, symbolism, patriotism, and a melancholic, oratorical tone. Scholars place him in the Mahjar (émigré) Romantic/neo-Romantic movement. Crucially, he wrote Arabic in a colloquial, accessible register drawn from his Bsharri childhood and Boston immigrant community, rather than classical *fusha*, a choice born of isolation that resonated widely with Arab immigrants. He defended this in the poem "You Have Your Language and I Have Mine" (1924).

### Influences: Bible, Blake, Marrash, Nietzsche
The King James Bible was Gibran’s "inexhaustible" source, providing parabolic structure and incantational rhythms; he viewed it as "Syriac literature in English words." William Blake was a pivotal influence: Gibran called him "the God-man," agreeing with his apocalyptic vision, likely deepened via Auguste Rodin in Paris. Syrian writer Francis Marrash, studied at Collège de la Sagesse, shaped Gibran’s ideas on universal love, women’s liberation, and social corruption. Walt Whitman influenced his universality and nature mysticism. Friedrich Nietzsche’s impact appears later (*The Tempests*), but Gibran distinguished his protagonist Almustafa’s teachings from Zarathustra’s, aligning them instead with his vision of Jesus.

### Visual Art
Gibran produced over 700 visual works. He preferred oils (1908–1914) but worked extensively in pencil, ink, watercolor, and gouache. Confirmed as a Symbolist after studying with Marcel-Béronneau, he cited J.M.W. Turner as the greatest English artist, adopting Turner’s technique of layering raw colors on canvas (e.g., *Rose Sleeves*, 1911). Major holdings exist at the Gibran Museum (Bsharri), Telfair Museums (Savannah—largest U.S. public collection, donated by Haskell in 1950), Museo Soumaya (Mexico City), Mathaf (Doha), Brooklyn Museum, Metropolitan Museum of Art, and Harvard Art Museums.

### Religious Views
Raised Maronite, Gibran synthesized Christianity, Islam (especially Sufi mysticism), theosophy, and Jungian psychology into a belief in the "fundamental unity of religions," reinforced by Lebanon’s sectarian strife. He called himself a "lifeist." Meeting ʻAbdu'l-Bahá (Baháʼí leader) in 1911–12 profoundly moved him, inspiring *Jesus, the Son of Man* (portraying Jesus through 77 contemporaries). In 1921, he participated in a forum on "Do We Need a New World Religion?"

### Political Thought
Gibran advocated Syrian independence from Ottoman rule, writing "Pity the Nation" and "Dead Are My People" (on the 1915–18 famine, which he blamed on Turkish policy). He sketched "Free Syria" upon Ottoman defeat. He supported Arabic as Syria’s national language. A draft play reveals a distinct Syrian nationalism, distinct from Lebanese or Pan-Arab variants, coexisting with internationalism. He was skeptical of socialism, fearing it sought the "lowest common denominator." He declined pressure to lead in Lebanon, stating, "I am not a politician... they would not be led."

### Legacy and Cultural Impact
*The Prophet* surged in popularity during the 1960s counterculture and New Age movements, never going out of print. It influenced musicians: Elvis Presley (lifelong reader), John Lennon (adapted *Sand and Foam* for "Julia"), David Bowie (referenced in "The Width of a Circle"), Johnny Cash (recorded *The Eye of the Prophet*). President Kennedy’s 1961 inaugural line ("Ask not what your country can do for you...") echoes Gibran’s 1925 essay *The New Frontier*. Adaptations include a 2014 animated film and a 2018 musical *Broken Wings*. Memorials include the Gibran Museum (Bsharri), gardens in Beirut and Washington D.C., schools in New York, a Mercury crater (2009), and a Copley Square plaque.

## Terms
- ****Mahjar**** — The Arabic literary movement of émigré writers in the Americas (early 20th century); Gibran was a central figure.
- ****Pen League (al-Rabita al-Qalamiyya)**** — Arabic-language literary society re-founded by Gibran in New York (1920) to promote modernist Arabic literature.
- ****Symbolism (Art)**** — Late 19th-century movement emphasizing spiritual/mythological suggestion over realism; Gibran adopted this as a painter.
- ****Sufism**** — Islamic mysticism emphasizing inward divine experience; a major influence on Gibran’s spiritual synthesis.
- ****King James Bible**** — 1611 English translation; Gibran’s primary literary model for rhythm, parable, and vocabulary.
- ****Colloquial Arabic**** — Spoken vernacular; Gibran broke with classical *fusha* to write in this accessible register.
- ****ʻAbdu'l-Bahá**** — Leader of the Baháʼí Faith (1844–1921); his 1912 U.S. visit and meeting with Gibran inspired *Jesus, the Son of Man*.
- ****The Hermitage**** — Gibran’s studio at 30 Tenth Street, New York (1911–1931), a hub for his literary and artistic work.
- ****Mary Haskell**** — Headmistress, patron, editor, and lifelong friend; funded Gibran’s Paris studies and managed his English publications.
- ****Temple of Art**** — Gibran’s series of pencil portraits (begun 1909) depicting contemporary artists and historical heroes.

## Debates and open questions
- **Nature of the Haskell–Gibran relationship:** Biographers disagree whether their intense, lifelong bond was romantically consummated; Haskell’s family allegedly opposed marriage, she briefly accepted then broke an engagement (1910–11), and she later married another man while remaining Gibran’s patron.
- **Cause of cirrhosis:** Robin Waterfield attributes it to alcoholism; the source text notes this as an argument, not a settled fact.
- **Rihani–Gibran rift:** Ameen Rihani was an early mentor/role model; unexplained enmity arose after May 1912, noted by Haskell and Youssef Howayek.
- **Literary classification:** Western critics long neglected Gibran, debating whether his English works fit Western traditions or constitute a distinct Arab-American hybrid form.
- **Political nationalism:** Scholars debate whether Gibran’s nationalism was specifically Syrian, Lebanese, or Pan-Arab; a draft play suggests a distinct "Syrian nationalism" coexisting with internationalism.
