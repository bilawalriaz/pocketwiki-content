# Isambard Kingdom Brunel

## Overview
Isambard Kingdom Brunel (1806–1859) was a preeminent English civil and marine engineer of the Industrial Revolution. He is celebrated for his visionary, often groundbreaking, designs in railways, bridges, and steamships, though his career was marked by frequent financial and technical setbacks. His work fundamentally shaped modern engineering and infrastructure.

## Timeline
- **9 April 1806** — Born in Portsmouth, Hampshire.
- **1825–1828** — Works as resident engineer on the Thames Tunnel project; seriously injured in a collapse.
- **1831** — Wins competition to design the Clifton Suspension Bridge.
- **1833** — Appointed engineer for the Great Western Railway (GWR).
- **1838** — SS *Great Western* makes its first transatlantic crossing.
- **1843** — SS *Great Britain*, the first iron-hulled, propeller-driven transatlantic ship, is launched.
- **1841–1848** — GWR main line from London to Bristol opens; experimental atmospheric railway on the South Devon Railway fails.
- **1859** — Royal Albert Bridge opens; SS *Great Eastern* suffers an explosion on its maiden voyage.
- **15 September 1859** — Brunel dies in London, aged 53.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Born to French-British engineer Marc Isambard Brunel, Isambard received an elite education in England and France, studying mathematics and engineering. He began his career assisting his father, working on projects like the Thames Tunnel. The tunnel, using Marc Brunel's patented tunnelling shield, was a pioneering but dangerous project. A collapse in 1828 nearly killed Isambard, and he left the project, which was completed later without him. While recovering, he designed the Clifton Suspension Bridge, which was halted by the 1831 Bristol riots and only completed after his death.

### Railway Career
In 1833, Brunel was appointed engineer for the GWR, designing its London–Bristol main line. He championed a broad gauge of 7 ft 1⁄4 in (2,140 mm) for superior speed and comfort, a decision that led to the "Gauge War" with standard-gauge railways. His designs prioritized minimal gradients and curves, featuring innovative structures like the Maidenhead Railway Bridge and the Box Tunnel, then the world's longest. His career was marked by conflict with the GWR board and financial pressures. He later engineered the Royal Albert Bridge, a successful self-supporting design completed shortly before his death. His attempt to build an atmospheric railway on the South Devon Railway (1847–1848) was a costly failure due to technical issues like leaking valves and rat damage.

### Maritime Career
Seeking to extend the GWR's reach to America, Brunel designed three revolutionary steamships. The SS *Great Western* (1838) was the first purpose-built transatlantic steamship, proving the viability of regular ocean service. The SS *Great Britain* (1843) was the first iron-hulled, propeller-driven transatlantic ship, though it ran aground in 1846, bankrupting its parent company. His final and largest ship, the SS *Great Eastern* (1858), was an engineering marvel but a commercial failure due to immense cost and technical problems, including a fatal explosion on its maiden voyage. It later found success laying transatlantic telegraph cables.

### Later Work and Personal Life
During the Crimean War, Brunel designed the prefabricated Renkioi Hospital, which had a dramatically lower fatality rate than existing facilities. He married Mary Elizabeth Horsley in 1836, and they had three children. A heavy smoker, he was diagnosed with Bright's disease (nephritis) and died in 1859, aged 53, shortly after inspecting the SS *Great Eastern*.

## Terms
- **Broad Gauge:** A railway track width of 7 ft 1⁄4 in (2,140 mm) championed by Brunel for the GWR, wider than the standard gauge of 4 ft 8+1⁄2 in (1,435 mm).
- **Tunnelling Shield:** A movable protective structure, patented by Marc Brunel, that allowed workers to excavate tunnels in soft ground safely.
- **Atmospheric Railway:** A system using vacuum pressure in a pipe to propel trains, which Brunel experimented with on the South Devon Railway.
- **Blue Riband:** An unofficial award for the fastest transatlantic crossing by a passenger liner.
- **Caisson:** A watertight retaining structure used for underwater construction, like bridge piers.
- **Gauge War:** The conflict in the 1840s over whether Britain should adopt Brunel's broad gauge or the narrower standard gauge.
- **Bright's Disease:** An archaic term for nephritis, a kidney disease from which Brunel suffered.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Legacy of the Clifton Suspension Bridge:** Scholars debate how much of the final bridge, completed in 1864, reflects Brunel's original 1831 design.
- **Cause of the Great Eastern's Failure:** While its engineering was revolutionary, its lack of commercial success is attributed to being "too far ahead of its time" economically, rather than being a technical failure.
- **Impact of the Gauge War:** Brunel's insistence on the broad gauge, though technically superior in some respects, is seen as a costly miscalculation that the GWR had to reverse after his death.
