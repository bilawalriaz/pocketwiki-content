# Pottery

## Overview  
Pottery is the process and product of forming vessels and objects from clay and related raw materials, then firing them at high temperatures to produce hard, durable ceramics. It is among humanity’s oldest inventions, with fired clay figurines dating to 29,000–25,000 BC and the earliest known pottery vessels appearing in China around 18,000 BC. Beyond utilitarian and artistic uses, pottery serves as a primary archaeological record, offering insights into past societies’ technology, trade, and cultural practices.

## Timeline  
- **29,000–25,000 BC** — Gravettian Venus of Dolní Věstonice figurine (Czech Republic)
- **18,000 BC** — Earliest known pottery vessels (Jiangxi, China)
- **10,500 BC** — Jōmon pottery begins (Japan)
- **9,400 BC** — Earliest African pottery (Mali)
- **7,000–6,000 BC** — Cord-marked pottery in South Asia
- **6,000–4,000 BC** — Potter’s wheel invented (Mesopotamia)
- **1,200–1,400 °C** — Porcelain developed (China, Tang–Song dynasties)
- **1709** — First European porcelain (Germany)
- **1785** — Stoke-on-Trent industrializes (UK)
- **1970s** — Pressure casting developed (sanitaryware)
- **2025** — Large-scale hydrogen-fired kilns deployed

## Body  

### Main Types  
Pottery is traditionally classified into three types based on firing temperature and clay composition: **earthenware**, **stoneware**, and **porcelain**. Earthenware is fired below 1,200 °C, is porous unless glazed, and includes terracotta. Stoneware is fired between 1,100–1,300 °C, is non-porous, and was developed early in China but only reached Europe in the late Middle Ages. Porcelain, fired at 1,200–1,400 °C, is prized for its strength, translucence, and whiteness, achieved through vitrification and mullite formation. Historic pottery is further categorized as “fine” (expensive, decorative) or “coarse” (utilitarian).

### Production Process  
Production involves preparing the clay body, shaping, drying, firing, and optionally glazing or decorating. Shaping methods include hand-building, wheel-throwing, press molding, slip casting, and modern techniques like roller-head machines and 3D printing. Drying stages—plastic, leather-hard, bone-dry—must be carefully controlled to prevent cracking. Firing induces irreversible chemical changes; bisque (first) firing hardens the body, and glost (second) firing melts the glaze. Kiln atmospheres (oxidizing or reducing) and fuels (wood, gas, hydrogen, biomass) influence final appearance and environmental impact.

### Archaeological Significance  
Pottery is a durable artifact that survives long after organic materials decay, making it crucial for archaeological analysis. **Fabric analysis** examines clay matrix, inclusions, firing conditions, and decoration to infer cultural traits, trade, and technological development. Techniques like **trace-element analysis** and **thermoluminescence** help identify clay sources and date sites. The **chaîne opératoire**—the sequence of production steps—reveals patterns of cultural interaction and technical diffusion. For example, similar visible techniques (e.g., decoration) may indicate distant trade, while shared clay selection suggests closer contact.

### Regional Histories  
Pottery developed independently in multiple regions. In East Asia, China led innovations in stoneware and porcelain, exporting blue-and-white wares via the Silk Road. Japan and Korea adopted and adapted Chinese techniques. In South Asia, early cord-marked pottery dates to 7,000 BC, with the Indus Valley Civilization producing distinctive wheel-made wares. In the Middle East, the Pottery Neolithic (c. 7000 BC) saw rapid stylistic evolution. Europe’s oldest pottery appeared in Russia (c. 6700 BC), spreading through cultures like the Linear Pottery. The UK’s Stoke-on-Trent became a global ceramics hub by the 18th century. In the Americas, pottery arose independently, with Mesoamerican and Andean traditions producing intricate painted and sculptural works. Africa’s earliest pottery (9,400 BC, Mali) predates similar developments elsewhere, with later traditions linked to Bantu migrations.

## Terms  
- **Earthenware**: Low-fired pottery (below 1,200 °C), porous unless glazed.  
- **Stoneware**: High-fired (1,100–1,300 °C), non-porous, durable.  
- **Porcelain**: Very high-fired (1,200–1,400 °C), white, translucent, vitrified.  
- **Terracotta**: Unglazed reddish-brown earthenware, often used for sculpture.  
- **Fabric analysis**: Archaeological study of clay body composition and structure.  
- **Slip**: Liquid clay applied as a decorative coating or adhesive.  
- **Glaze**: Glassy coating fused to pottery during firing for decoration and impermeability.  
- **Bisque firing**: First firing of shaped clay to produce porous, hard "greenware."  
- **Glost firing**: Final firing that melts the glaze onto the surface.  
- **Chaîne opératoire**: Complete sequence of production steps in artifact creation.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- **Independent invention vs. diffusion**: Whether pottery arose independently in Africa, East Asia, and the Americas, or spread through cultural contact, remains debated.  
- **Timing of porcelain development**: Chinese sources do not distinguish porcelain from stoneware, complicating efforts to pinpoint its origin.  
- **Link to agriculture**: While some argue pottery emerged only after sedentary farming, the 28,000-year-old Dolní Věstonice figurine challenges this, suggesting earlier cognitive or symbolic motivations.  
- **Sub-Saharan African origins**: The relationship between early African pottery and later Bantu-speaking populations is contested, with no consensus on migration patterns or cultural transmission.  
- **Environmental sustainability**: The shift toward hydrogen and biomass fuels in modern kilns raises questions about scalability and long-term viability.
