# Selma Lagerlöf

## Overview
Selma Ottilia Lovisa Lagerlöf (1858–1940) was a Swedish novelist and the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in Literature (1909) and gain membership in the Swedish Academy (1914). Born at Mårbacka in Värmland, she overcame a childhood hip injury and a family financial crisis to become a teacher and then a full-time writer. Her work blended realism, folklore, and spiritual idealism, often set in her native Värmland. She used her literary fame to advocate for women's suffrage and, late in life, intervened to save the Jewish writer Nelly Sachs from Nazi Germany.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Lagerlöf was born the fifth of six children to Lieutenant Erik Gustaf Lagerlöf and Louise Wallroth at the Mårbacka estate. A hip injury at birth and a subsequent illness at age three left her temporarily lame. She was educated at home, reading deeply and writing poetry from a young age; her grandmother’s storytelling nurtured her narrative imagination. The family’s forced sale of Mårbacka in 1884, driven partly by her father’s alcoholism, was a formative trauma. Her father opposed her further education and involvement in the women’s movement, but she enrolled at the Royal Women's Superior Training Academy in Stockholm (1882–85), where she reacted against the literary realism of contemporaries like August Strindberg.

### Literary Breakthrough and Major Works
While teaching in Landskrona (1885–95), Lagerlöf drafted *Gösta Berling's Saga* (1891), a novel depicting the cavalier life of Värmland. It won a publishing contract via a magazine contest but only achieved wide acclaim after critic Georg Brandes praised the Danish translation. Financial support from Fredrika Limnell allowed her to quit teaching in 1895. A 1900 visit to the American Colony in Jerusalem inspired the two-part novel *Jerusalem* (1901–02), which earned comparisons to Homer and Shakespeare. Commissioned by the National Teachers' Association to write a geography reader, she produced *The Wonderful Adventures of Nils* (1906–07), a fantasy journey across Sweden that became her most translated work. Other major novels include *The Miracles of the Antichrist* (1897), set in Sicily, and *The Emperor of Portugallia* (1914). Her *Ring* trilogy (1925–28) explored generational curses in Värmland.

### Relationships and Personal Life
Lagerlöf formed two intense, exclusive emotional bonds with women. In 1894 she met Sophie Elkan, a writer who became her companion and sharpest literary critic; their correspondence (published 1993) reveals deep intellectual intimacy. Around 1900, she grew close to Valborg Olander, a teacher and suffrage activist who served as her literary assistant and secretary; their letters (published 2006) contain passages implying erotic and physical passion. A strong rivalry existed between Elkan and Olander. Homosexual relations between women were illegal and taboo in Sweden at the time, so neither relationship was publicly acknowledged as romantic. Lagerlöf eventually bought back Mårbacka with her Nobel money and lived there until her death.

### Public Advocacy and Later Years
Lagerlöf was an active speaker for the National Association for Women's Suffrage. Her prestige lent weight to the cause; she opened the 1911 International Suffrage Congress in Stockholm and celebrated the 1919 victory granting women the vote. In 1919, she sold film rights to all unpublished works to Svenska Biografteatern, enabling seminal silent film adaptations by Victor Sjöström and Mauritz Stiller (notably *The Phantom Carriage*, 1921). At the start of World War II, she donated her Nobel and Academy medals to Finland to fund their war against the Soviet Union; Finland returned the medals after raising funds by other means. Shortly before her death in 1940, she successfully petitioned the Swedish royal family to secure exit visas and asylum for the German-Jewish poet Nelly Sachs and Sachs’s mother on the last flight from Berlin to Stockholm.

### Awards and Legacy
Lagerlöf received the Nobel Prize in 1909 for "lofty idealism, vivid imagination, and spiritual perception," following a contentious internal Academy struggle. She received the Academy’s great gold medal (1904), the Litteris et Artibus (1909), and the Illis quorum (1926), plus honorary doctorates from Uppsala (1907) and Greifswald (1928). Posthumous honors include the Selma Lagerlöf Prize (established 1983), her portrait on the 20-kronor banknote (1991, first woman on Swedish currency), a museum at Mårbacka, and a stairway named for her in Jerusalem, Israel.

## Terms
- **Gösta Berling's Saga** (1891): Lagerlöf’s debut novel; a romantic chronicle of a defrocked minister and a band of cavaliers in Värmland, blending realism and folklore.
- **The Wonderful Adventures of Nils** (1906–07): A children’s geography reader framed as a fantasy; a boy shrunk to thumb-size tours Sweden on a goose’s back, learning the country’s provinces, history, and nature.
- **American Colony in Jerusalem**: A utopian Christian community founded by Americans and Swedes in Jerusalem; Lagerlöf’s 1900 visit inspired her novel *Jerusalem* (1901–02).
- **Sophie Elkan** (1853–1921): Swedish-Jewish writer; Lagerlöf’s close friend, companion, and literary critic from 1894 until Elkan’s death.
- **Valborg Olander** (1861–1943): Teacher, suffrage activist, and Lagerlöf’s literary assistant/secretary from the 1900s; their correspondence suggests a passionate romantic relationship.
- **Svenska Biografteatern** (Swedish Cinema Theatre): Film company that bought rights to Lagerlöf’s unpublished works in 1919, producing landmark silent films directed by Victor Sjöström and Mauritz Stiller.
- **National Association for Women's Suffrage**: Swedish organization for which Lagerlöf spoke publicly, notably opening the 1911 International Suffrage Congress in Stockholm.
- **Nelly Sachs** (1891–1970): German-Jewish poet and later Nobel laureate; Lagerlöf secured her escape from Nazi Germany in 1940.
- **Mårbacka**: The family estate in Värmland where Lagerlöf was born, lost in 1884, repurchased with Nobel funds, and now preserved as a museum.
- **Swedish Academy**: The body that awards the Nobel Prize in Literature; Lagerlöf became its first female member in 1914.

## Debates and open questions
- **Nature of Lagerlöf’s intimate relationships**: The surviving correspondence with Olander contains explicitly erotic passages, while letters to Elkan emphasize intellectual and spiritual intimacy. Scholars debate whether these were romantic/sexual partnerships or intense "romantic friendships" typical of the era; the illegality of homosexual acts between women at the time enforced silence.
- **Nobel Prize decision (1909)**: The award followed a "harsh internal power struggle" within the Swedish Academy. The specific factions and arguments (e.g., literary merit vs. traditionalism, gender bias) are not detailed in the source.
- **Literary classification**: Critics initially gave *Gösta Berling's Saga* mild reviews; Brandes’s endorsement reversed its reception. The source notes comparisons to Homer and Shakespeare for *Jerusalem*, but the critical consensus on her place between realism, romantic nationalism, and modernism remains implicit.
- **Film rights sale (1919)**: Lagerlöf sold rights to *all* unpublished works en bloc. The strategic reasoning (financial security vs. artistic control) and its long-term effect on her literary reputation versus her cinematic legacy are not explored.
