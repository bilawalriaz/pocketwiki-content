# Singing

## Overview
Singing is the art of creating musical sounds with the human voice, considered the oldest form of musical expression. It involves coordinated use of the lungs, larynx, vocal tract, and articulators to produce pitched sounds, and ranges from informal personal enjoyment to professional performance across genres. Singing differs from rapping in its engagement with tonality and pitch accuracy.

## Timeline
- **Ancient Greece**: Origins of vocal pedagogy as a studied discipline
- **13th century**: First recorded use of "chest voice" and "head voice" terms by Johannes de Garlandia and Jerome of Moravia
- **19th century**: Development of bel canto technique; chest, passagio, and head registers defined
- **1922**: Max Schoen links vibrato to neuromuscular tremor
- **1995**: Sa Re Ga Ma Pa, first televised singing competition, launches in India
- **2002**: American Idol debuts in the US
- **2000s**: Widespread use of Auto-Tune pitch correction in popular music

## Body

### Voices and Physical Technique
Singing relies on four coordinated mechanisms: the lungs (air supply), larynx (vocal fold vibration), thoracic cavity (amplification), and articulators (tongue, palate, teeth, lips). The diaphragm controls inhalation while abdominal and intercostal muscles manage exhalation. Each voice is unique due to vocal fold shape, body structure, and resonance patterns. Vocal resonation enhances sound through seven cavities: chest, tracheal tree, larynx, pharynx, oral cavity, nasal cavity, and sinuses.

### Vocal Classification
In European classical music, voice classification evaluates range, weight, tessitura, timbre, and transition points. Women are categorized as soprano, mezzo-soprano, or contralto; men as countertenor, tenor, baritone, or bass; children as treble. Choral music divides voices by range alone (SATB), often forcing medium voices into unsuitable parts. Contemporary commercial music lacks an authoritative classification system, and applying classical terms is considered controversial due to different techniques and amplification use.

### Vocal Registration
Vocal registration refers to distinct tone series produced by consistent vocal fold vibratory patterns. Speech pathologists identify four registers: vocal fry, modal, falsetto, and whistle. The passaggio describes transition areas between registers in classical singing, with primo and secondo passaggi connected by zona di passaggio in males. Classical training aims for even timbre across transitions. The three main registers—chest, middle (mixed), and head voice—are named for where singers feel resonance vibrations.

### Vocal Pedagogy and Technique
Vocal pedagogy studies singing instruction, covering anatomy, vocal health, breathing, phonation, resonation, registration, classification, and style. Proper singing integrates four processes: inhalation, phonation, resonation, and articulation. Vocal development requires coordinated muscle reflexes, systematic practice, and instructor guidance. Range extension follows three factors: energy (breath pressure and dynamics), space (mouth opening and palate/larynx position), and depth (physical and mental concepts). Good posture—aligned spine, relaxed shoulders, balanced stance—optimizes breath support and resonance.

### Vibrato and Extended Techniques
Vibrato is a rapid pitch oscillation between higher and lower tones, occurring naturally from breath support and relaxed vocal apparatus. Extended techniques include rapping, screaming, growling, overtone singing, yodeling, belting, and vocal fry. Sound reinforcement systems enable intimate styles like crooning and effects processing.

### Vocal Music and Genres
Vocal music, likely the oldest music form, features sung lyrics or non-linguistic syllables. Genres span popular (blues, jazz, rock, hip-hop), art music (Lieder, opera), religious, and secular traditions. Popular music distinguishes lead singers from backing vocalists, with microphones enabling diverse expressive techniques. Hip-hop uses rhythmic speech (rapping) rather than pitched singing.

### Careers and Competitions
Professional singing careers involve contract-based work, requiring musical skills, vocal excellence, showmanship, and continuous coaching. Singers market through auditions, demos, and agents. Televised competitions like American Idol and The Voice have become major talent discovery platforms since the 1990s.

### Language and Neurology
Every language's musicality affects pitch, phrasing, and accent in singing. Stroke victims can regain speech through melodic intonation therapy, activating right-hemisphere "singing centers." Brain areas like Broca's and Wernicke's process both musical and linguistic syntax. Singing accents often differ from speaking accents, influenced by musical style rather than regional origin.

## Terms
- **Vocal fold**: Paired folds of tissue in the larynx that vibrate to produce sound
- **Resonation**: Enhancement of vocal sound through air-filled body cavities
- **Register**: Series of tones produced by consistent vocal fold vibratory patterns
- **Passaggio**: Transition area between vocal registers in classical singing
- **Vibrato**: Rapid pitch oscillation in sustained notes
- **Bel canto**: Italian operatic singing method emphasizing beautiful tone
- **Falsetto**: Register producing lighter, higher tones through partial vocal fold vibration
- **Tessitura**: The most comfortable vocal range for a given voice
- **SATB**: Standard choral voicing: soprano, alto, tenor, bass
- **Phonation**: Process of producing vocal sound via vocal fold vibration

## Debates and Open Questions
Voice classification systems remain contested, particularly applying classical Fach terminology to contemporary commercial music. The chest voice/head voice distinction is debated between traditional pedagogy and modern physiological understanding, with some educators arguing these terms describe resonance rather than true registers. The effectiveness and artistic value of Auto-Tune and electronic pitch correction in popular music generates ongoing controversy. Whether rap constitutes singing or a distinct vocal practice remains discussed among music scholars.
