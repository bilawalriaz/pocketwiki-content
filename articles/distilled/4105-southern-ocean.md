# Southern Ocean

## Overview
The Southern Ocean, also known as the Antarctic Ocean, encircles Antarctica, generally defined as waters south of 60° S latitude. It is the second-smallest of the five principal oceans and is critically important for global climate regulation due to its role in thermohaline circulation and its unique, highly productive ecosystem.

## Timeline
- **1675** — Anthony de la Roché discovers South Georgia south of the Antarctic Convergence.
- **1772-1775** — James Cook's second voyage proves waters encompass southern latitudes and crosses the Antarctic Circle.
- **1819** — William Smith sights Livingston Island, the first land south of 60° S.
- **1820** — First confirmed sightings of mainland Antarctica by Bellingshausen, Bransfield, and Palmer.
- **1928** — IHO's first *Limits of Oceans and Seas* delineates the Southern Ocean with land-based limits.
- **1937** — IHO's second edition moves the ocean's northern limits southward, away from land masses.
- **1953** — IHO's third edition omits the Southern Ocean, leaving its definition to local offices.
- **1959** — Antarctic Treaty signed, limiting military activity and territorial claims.
- **2000** — IHO votes to redefine the Southern Ocean as waters south of 60° S, based on ocean currents.
- **2019** — Five Deeps Expedition surveys maximum depth (7,434 m) and proposes name "Factorian Deep."

## Body

### Definition and Naming
The Southern Ocean's official recognition has been historically debated. Early explorers like Cook proved its existence, but geographers disagreed on its northern boundary—whether it should be the Antarctic Convergence or the southern limits of the Pacific, Atlantic, and Indian oceans. The IHO first delineated it in 1928 but omitted it in 1953 due to disagreement. In 2000, the IHO settled on a definition based on oceanography: waters south of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current's northern limit, generally taken as 60° S. This definition is used by many organizations, though not formally adopted by the IHO due to naming disputes. Australia defines the ocean as lying immediately south of its coast.

### Exploration and History
Exploration was initially driven by the myth of *Terra Australis*. Key milestones include Bartolomeu Dias rounding the Cape of Good Hope (1487), Cook's voyages (1770s) disproving a habitable southern continent, and first sightings of land south of 60° S by William Smith (1819) and the mainland by Bellingshausen, Bransfield, and Palmer (1820). The 20th century saw major expeditions like Shackleton's Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition (1914) and Operation Highjump (1946). The Antarctic Treaty (1959) established a framework for peace and scientific cooperation. Modern milestones include the first single-handed sail (1972), the first baby born on the continent (1978), and the first autonomous circumnavigation by an unmanned saildrone (2019).

### Geography and Physical Oceanography
Geologically, the Southern Ocean is the youngest, formed when Antarctica and South America separated ~30 million years ago, opening the Drake Passage. This allowed the formation of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC), the world's longest ocean current, transporting 130 million cubic metres of water per second. Its northern boundary does not abut a landmass. Key features include the Antarctic Convergence, where cold Antarctic waters sink beneath warmer subantarctic waters, creating a nutrient-rich region supporting high phytoplankton productivity. The ocean also features large-scale upwelling and two major clockwise gyres: the Ross and Weddell Gyres.

### Biodiversity and Ecosystem
The ecosystem is highly productive, anchored by phytoplankton and the keystone species Antarctic krill (*Euphausia superba*), with a biomass of ~500 million tonnes. Krill supports a food web including whales, seals, penguins, and fish. The ocean hosts diverse life, from penguins (like the emperor penguin, which breeds in winter) and seals (including the abundant crabeater seal) to fish with unique adaptations like antifreeze proteins (e.g., icefish) and, in the case of crocodile icefish, blood lacking hemoglobin. Benthic communities are dense and feature deep-sea gigantism. Over 235 marine organisms are bipolar, living in both polar regions.

### Environment and Human Impact
Threats include increased UV radiation from the ozone hole, which reduces phytoplankton productivity, and illegal, unreported, and unregulated (IUU) fishing, particularly for Patagonian toothfish. Long-line fishing for toothfish also causes high seabird mortality. Governance includes the Antarctic Treaty System, the Southern Ocean Whale Sanctuary (prohibiting commercial whaling south of 40° S), and the Convention for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CCAMLR). The economy is based on limited fisheries (primarily krill and toothfish) and scientific research; major ports are operated by research stations.

## Terms
- **Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC):** The world's longest ocean current, flowing eastward around Antarctica, driven by strong westerly winds.
- **Antarctic Convergence:** An oceanic zone where cold, northward-flowing Antarctic waters meet and sink beneath warmer subantarctic waters, creating a nutrient-rich boundary.
- **Thermohaline circulation:** The global ocean circulation driven by density differences due to temperature and salinity; the Southern Ocean's overturning circulation is a key component.
- **Antarctic Bottom Water:** A very cold, dense, saline water mass formed under sea ice that sinks and spreads into the global ocean abyss.
- **Antarctic krill:** A small, abundant crustacean (*Euphausia superba*) that is the keystone species of the Southern Ocean ecosystem.
- **Antarctic Treaty:** An international agreement (signed 1959) that designates Antarctica for peaceful purposes and scientific research, prohibiting military activity and new territorial claims.
- **CCAMLR:** The Convention for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources, an international treaty focused on conserving marine life in the Southern Ocean.
- **Notothenioidei:** A suborder of fish (including icefish) endemic to the Southern Ocean, many of which possess antifreeze glycoproteins in their blood.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The formal, internationally adopted definition of the Southern Ocean's northern boundary remains unresolved due to IHO impasses, with some organizations using the 60° S parallel and others using the Antarctic Convergence or extending other oceans to Antarctica.
- The impact of climate change on the Southern Ocean's overturning circulation is a major concern, with potential for substantial slowing or collapse, which would have global consequences for weather and marine ecosystems.
- The sustainability of fisheries, particularly for Patagonian toothfish, is threatened by IUU fishing, the scale of which is estimated to be five to six times the regulated catch.
