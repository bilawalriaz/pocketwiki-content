# François Villon

## Overview
François Villon (c. 1431 – after 1463) is the most celebrated French poet of the Late Middle Ages, renowned for transforming courtly poetic forms into vehicles for autobiography, criminal slang, and social satire. Born in Paris and raised by a foster father, a canon law professor, Villon earned a Master of Arts from the University of Paris but pursued a life of crime, including theft, a fatal street brawl, and the robbery of a college chapel. His two major works—*Le Petit Testament* (1456) and *Le Testament* (1461–62)—are mock wills bequeathing imaginary goods to real acquaintances, blending gallows humor, technical virtuosity, and vivid portraits of the Parisian underworld. Repeatedly imprisoned, tortured, and sentenced to hang (commuted to banishment in 1463), he vanished from the historical record thereafter. His innovations in diction and theme made him a pivotal figure bridging medieval and Renaissance literature.

## Body

### Birth, Names, and Upbringing
Villon was born in Paris in 1431. His birth name appears in documents as François de Montcorbier or François des Loges, though he exclusively used "Villon" in his poetry, adopting the surname from his foster father, Guillaume de Villon. Guillaume was a chaplain at the collegiate church of Saint-Benoît-le-Bétourné and a professor of canon law who took the impoverished boy into his house; Villon described him as "more than a father to me." His mother was still alive when he was thirty.

### Education and the "Lost Years"
Villon entered the Faculty of Arts around age twelve. He earned a bachelor’s degree in 1449 and a master’s degree in 1452. The period between 1452 and 1455 is undocumented; the *Encyclopædia Britannica* (11th ed.) dismisses conjectural reconstructions of a "Bohemian" student life as futile.

### Criminal Career and Legal Encounters
Villon’s documented criminality begins on 5 June 1455, when a scuffle in the Rue Saint-Jacques resulted in the death of Jean le Hardi (also called Sermaise), a Master of Arts. Villon fled but received a royal pardon from Charles VII in January 1456 after two petitions claimed Sermaise had forgiven him. The pardon documents identify him variously as "François des Loges, autrement dit Villon" and "François de Montcorbier," and note he gave the alias "Michel Mouton" to a barber-surgeon.

Around Christmas 1456, Villon participated in the burglary of the Collège de Navarre chapel, stealing 500 gold crowns. The theft was discovered in March 1457; by May, police tracked a student gang via the confession of Guy Tabarie. Tabarie later turned king’s evidence, naming Villon as ringleader who had fled to Angers. Villon was sentenced to banishment and spent the next four years as a wanderer, possibly alongside known thieves Regnier de Montigny and Colin des Cayeux.

### Imprisonment at Meung-sur-Loire and *Le Testament*
The next certain record places Villon in the bishop’s prison at Meung-sur-Loire in summer 1461. His offense is unknown, but in *Le Testament* he attacks Bishop Thibault d’Aussigny of Orléans. He was likely released in a general amnesty upon the accession of Louis XI (2 October 1461). That year, at age thirty, he composed *Le Testament* (also *Le Grand Testament*), his masterwork. By autumn 1462, he was back in the cloisters of Saint-Benoît.

### Final Arrest, Banishment, and Disappearance
In November 1462, Villon was jailed at the Grand Châtelet for theft; the Navarre robbery charge was revived. Bail was accepted, but he was quickly re-arrested after a street quarrel. He was tortured, condemned to be hanged (*pendu et étranglé*), but the *parlement* commuted the sentence to banishment on 5 January 1463. His fate thereafter is unknown. Scholar Anthony Bonner speculated he died "broken in health and spirit"—perhaps in a tavern, a cell, a street fight, or on a provincial gallows.

### Major Works
**Le Petit Testament (Le Lais), 1456:** Written after the Navarre robbery, this ironic, comic mock-will lists bequests to friends and acquaintances, establishing Villon’s signature blend of autobiography and parody.

**Le Testament (Le Grand Testament), 1461–62:** A longer, more complex mock-will composed of *huitains* (eight-line stanzas) interspersed with ballades. It is widely judged his greatest work. Villon innovates by subverting courtly ideals—celebrating lowlifes, employing Parisian thieves' slang (*jargon*), and filling verses with real names of Parisian officials, lawyers, prostitutes, and police. The poem is a record of poverty, trial, and "gallows humor" intended for an audience sharing those conditions.

**Shorter Poems:** Sixteen shorter poems survive, ranging from serious to light-hearted. Eleven additional poems in thieves' jargon were early attributed to Villon, but many scholars now consider them imitations by other poets.

### English Translations
Key translations include John Heron Lepper and George Heyer (both 1924), Anthony Bonner (1960), Galway Kinnell (1965), Peter Dale (1974), Barbara Sargent-Baur (1994, including disputed poems), and David Georgi (2013, facing-page edition emphasizing wordplay). Notable partial translations: Dante Gabriel Rossetti (1867, including "The Ballad of Dead Ladies" with the famous refrain "Where are the snows of yesteryear?"), W. E. Henley (criminal slang versions), and Richard Wilbur (*Collected Poems*, 2004).

### Critical Reception
Villon achieved immediate posthumous popularity. Pierre Levet printed his poems in 1489, followed by numerous editions. In 1533, humanist Clément Marot published a landmark edition, recognizing Villon as a major French poet and correcting textual corruptions from earlier printers.

## Terms
- ****Le Testament / Le Grand Testament**** — Villon’s 1461–62 masterwork: a long mock-will in *huitains* and ballades, satirizing courtly conventions and bequeathing imaginary goods to real Parisian figures.
- ****Le Petit Testament / Le Lais**** — Villon’s 1456 mock-will, shorter and more comic, written after the Collège de Navarre robbery.
- ****Huitain**** — An eight-line stanza, the primary verse form of *Le Testament*.
- ****Ballade**** — A fixed medieval verse form (typically three stanzas plus an envoi) used by Villon within *Le Testament* for set-piece poems (e.g., "Ballade des dames du temps jadis").
- ****Jargon / Coquillard slang**** — The specialized argot of 15th-century French thieves and beggars (*coquillards*), which Villon incorporates into poems to authenticate underworld voices.
- ****Parlement**** — The highest judicial court in *Ancien Régime* France (here, the Parlement of Paris), which commuted Villon’s death sentence to banishment in 1463.
- ****Coquillards**** — Organized gangs of thieves and vagabonds in 15th-century France; Villon’s associates Montigny and Cayeux were members.
- ****Mock Testament**** — A satiric literary genre where the poet writes a fake will, bequeathing ironic or worthless "gifts" to enemies and friends; Villon perfected the form.
- ****Ballade des dames du temps jadis**** — Villon’s 1461 ballade containing the line "Mais où sont les neiges d'antan?" ("Where are the snows of yesteryear?"), famously translated by Rossetti.
- ****O.S. (Old Style)**** — Julian calendar dating; Villon’s birth is recorded as 19 April 1432 O.S. (equivalent to 1 April 1431 in the modern Gregorian reckoning used by the source).

## Debates and open questions
- **Birth date and name:** Sources conflict on the exact date (1431 vs. 1432 O.S.) and birth surname (Montcorbier vs. des Loges).
- **1452–1455 gap:** No records exist for these three years after his master’s degree; biographers debate whether he lived as a scholar, a criminal, or both.
- **Authorship of the *jargon* poems:** Eleven poems in thieves' slang were traditionally attributed to Villon, but many modern scholars argue they are imitations by followers.
- **Crime at Meung-sur-Loire:** The specific offense that landed Villon in the bishop’s prison in summer 1461 is unrecorded.
- **Final fate:** Villon disappears after January 1463; theories range from death in obscurity (tavern, cell, gallows) to survival under a new identity, but no evidence confirms any outcome.
