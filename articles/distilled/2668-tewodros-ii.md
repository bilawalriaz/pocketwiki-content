# Tewodros II

## Overview
Tewodros II (c. 1818–1868) was Emperor of Ethiopia from 1855 to 1868. His reign marks the beginning of modern Ethiopia, as he ended the decentralized *Zemene Mesafint* (Era of the Princes) by reunifying the empire through military conquest and attempting administrative reforms. His conflict with Great Britain culminated in the British Expedition to Abyssinia and his suicide, making him a symbol of defiant independence.

## Timeline
- **c. 1818** — Born as Kassa Hailu in Qwara.
- **1855** — Crowned Emperor Tewodros II after defeating rivals, ending the *Zemene Mesafint*.
- **1855–1861** — Campaigns to subdue rebellious provinces, including Shewa and Gojjam.
- **1862** — Writes a letter to Queen Victoria requesting skilled workers and aid.
- **1863** — Orders the execution of 7,000 prisoners after defeating a rebel.
- **1864** — Imprisons British Consul Cameron and other Europeans after no reply to his letter.
- **1868** — British Expedition to Abyssinia under Robert Napier arrives to free hostages.
- **10 April 1868** — British defeat Tewodros's army at the Battle of Arogye.
- **15 April 1868** — Tewodros II commits suicide at Magdala rather than surrender.

## Body

### Rise to Power and Unification
Born Kassa Hailu, Tewodros II rose from the chaos of the *Zemene Mesafint*, a period of civil war where regional princes controlled a puppet emperor in Gondar. He began as a *shifta* (outlaw), but his benevolent treatment of peasants in Qwara garnered popular support. After a brief, forced marriage into the ruling family of Empress Menen and Ras Ali II, he rebelled. Through a series of military victories (1852–1855), he defeated Ras Ali, captured the Empress, and deposed Emperor Yohannes III. His final major rival was Dejazmach Wube, whom he defeated in 1855. He was then crowned Emperor, taking the throne name Tewodros II to fulfill a prophecy that a ruler named Tewodros would restore the empire.

### Reign and Reforms
Tewodros II's primary goal was to re-establish a strong, centralized Ethiopian state. He moved the capital from Gondar to Magdala and forcibly reincorporated regions like Shewa, Gojjam, and Wollo under direct imperial rule. He imprisoned rival nobles, including the young Prince of Shewa, Sahle Maryam (the future Emperor Menelik II), whom he raised as his own son. His reforms included establishing salaried officials, a professional standing army, tax codes, and a centralized administration. He also attempted to tax church lands to finance the government, which earned him powerful enemies. His reign was marked by constant campaigns to suppress regional rebellions, which drained resources and limited other reforms.

### Conflict with Britain and Death
Tewodros II sought British assistance against internal and external threats, writing to Queen Victoria in 1862. When the British response was delayed and inadequate, he imprisoned the British consul and other Europeans in 1864. This led to the 1868 British Expedition to Abyssinia. The British force defeated Tewodros's army at Arogye. Rather than surrender, Tewodros committed suicide with a pistol on 15 April 1868 as British troops stormed Magdala. His young son, Prince Alemayehu, was taken to England, where he died at age 19.

## Terms
*   **Zemene Mesafint**: "Era of the Princes," a period of decentralized rule in Ethiopia (c. 1769–1855) where regional nobles controlled a puppet emperor.
*   **Shifta**: An outlaw or rebel who refuses to recognize feudal authority.
*   **Negus/Atse**: Titles for King/Emperor in Ethiopia.
*   **Dejazmatch**: A noble title, roughly equivalent to an earl or count.
*   **Ras**: A high-ranking noble title, often translated as "head" or "prince."
*   **Amba**: A flat-topped mountain or fortress, often used as a stronghold (e.g., Magdala).
*   **Ye Maru Qemas**: "The taste of the honey," a personal fiefdom in Dembiya.
*   **Kosso**: A traditional Ethiopian medicinal plant, sometimes used pejoratively to imply poverty.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact circumstances of his mother's financial status are debated; rumors she sold *Kosso* are considered false by several historians.
*   The extent of his Solomonic lineage is noted as a "slim margin" compared to rivals, though he claimed descent from Emperor Fasilides.
*   His personal character is described variously by contemporaries as both "terribly wrathful" and "generous to excess," highlighting his complex and often brutal personality.
*   The fate of his first wife's alleged child with Menelik II and the legitimacy of certain descendants remain subjects of rumor and speculation.
