# Joseph Conrad

## Overview
Joseph Conrad (1857–1924), born Józef Teodor Konrad Korzeniowski in Russian-ruled Ukraine to Polish nationalist parents, became one of the English language’s greatest novelists despite not speaking English fluently until his twenties. His early life was defined by Polish resistance to partition, the exile and deaths of his parents, and a nineteen-year career in the French and British merchant marines. These experiences—particularly his 1890 voyage up the Congo River—provided the raw material for fiction that exposed the brutality of imperialism and the fragility of human morality in an indifferent universe. Writing in his third language, Conrad developed a distinctive impressionistic style characterized by narrative complexity, psychological depth, and a pervasive irony that questioned the certainties of civilization, progress, and national identity. His major works—*Heart of Darkness*, *Lord Jim*, *Nostromo*, *The Secret Agent*, and *Under Western Eyes*—anticipated modernist techniques and influenced generations of writers, even as his depictions of race and empire, particularly in *Heart of Darkness*, sparked enduring controversy.

## Body

### Early Life and Polish Heritage
Conrad was born into the *szlachta* (Polish nobility) bearing the Nałęcz coat-of-arms in a region once part of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth but partitioned since 1795 among Russia, Prussia, and Austria. His father, Apollo Korzeniowski, was a writer and "Red" faction activist advocating for Polish independence, land reform, and abolition of serfdom; his paternal grandfather had fought in the 1830 November Uprising. This heritage instilled a sense of public duty and a "psychological heritage" of ambition and stress regarding reputation. After Apollo’s arrest in 1861 and the family’s exile to Vologda (1862) and Chernihiv (1863), Conrad’s mother died in 1865 and his father in 1869, leaving him orphaned at eleven. Raised by his maternal uncle Tadeusz Bobrowski, Conrad was a poor student but absorbed Polish Romantic literature—especially Mickiewicz and Słowacki—which he later credited for the "Polishness" in his works. His 1883 letter to Stefan Buszczyński affirmed he never forgot his uncle’s admonition: "wherever you may sail, you are sailing towards Poland."

### Merchant Marine Career
At sixteen, Conrad left Kraków for Marseille (1874) to serve on French ships. After four years, the Russian consul refused him papers, leading to debt and a suicide attempt (March 1878). He then joined the British merchant marine (April 1878), serving fifteen years under the Red Ensign, rising from steward to captain. Of his nineteen years at sea (1874–94), he spent just over eight years actually sailing. His sole command was the *Otago* (1888–89). A pivotal 1890 voyage to the Congo Free State, hired by Albert Thys of the Société Anonyme Belge, exposed him to the atrocities of Leopold II’s regime; he befriended Roger Casement, later a human rights investigator. Conrad’s 1885–86 letters to Joseph Spiridion, his first preserved English texts, reveal a shift from Panslavic hope to a painful acceptance of Polish hopelessness and England as a refuge. Friendships with John Galsworthy and Edward Sanderson aboard the *Torrens* (1892–93) marked his entry into literary circles.

### Literary Career
Conrad began *Almayer's Folly* in 1889, treating writing as a "serious undertaking" rather than amusement. Published in 1895, it launched his career but typecast him as a teller of exotic tales—a label he resented. Edward Garnett championed his work; Constance Garnett viewed his foreignness as a merit. Conrad chose non-English colonial settings (Dutch Borneo, Belgian Congo) to avoid divided loyalties and because he lacked knowledge of British domestic life. His fiction drew on maritime experience but transformed "particular to general" to explore universal emotions. Financial success came late, with *Chance* (1913), though intellectuals recognized his talent earlier. A government pension (1910) eased his chronic poverty. Conrad wrote for a vast array of periodicals, often discussing work-in-progress with peers like Garnett and Ford Madox Ford, with whom he collaborated on *The Inheritors* and *Romance*. He suffered frequent writer’s block and adjusted views in letters to correspondents’ predilections.

### Themes, Style, and Language
Conrad’s works depict crises of individuality in an "indifferent, inscrutable, and amoral world." He is claimed as both a literary impressionist and early modernist, using narrative frames (e.g., Marlow) to present knowledge as contingent and provisional. His prose aspired to "make you hear, to make you feel... to make you see," employing a rhythm "not built in the rhythm of ordinary prose" (T.E. Lawrence). Recurring themes: the hypocrisy of imperialism ("piracy with good PR"), the fragility of order, the "haggard utilitarian lies of our civilisation," and the search for "some saving truth" amid fatality. Characters often meet lethal fates (Kurtz, Jim, Nostromo, Verloc); survivors like Razumov (*Under Western Eyes*) are crippled morally or physically.

Conrad wrote in English, his third language after Polish and French. He claimed English was "plastic"—"if you haven't got a word you need you can make it"—unlike "crystallized" French. Yet his English bore "Franglais" and "Poglish" spillovers (Gallicisms, Polonisms). Najder argues he was an "English writer who grew up in other linguistic and cultural environments," his work situated in the "borderland of auto-translation." He avoided Polish, valuing its literature too much for his "clumsy efforts," and resented being labeled a "Slavonic" writer, preferring "Polonism." His trilingualism and outsider status—"a bloody foreigner"—fueled a unique perspective on language as "instruments for exciting blurred emotions."

### Politics and Controversy
Conrad was "passionately concerned with politics" (Najder), viewing history as a struggle between law/violence, freedom/autocracy. His 1905 essay "Autocracy and War" predicted Russia’s transition from autocracy to dictatorship and warned of Prussia as Europe’s future aggressor. He distrusted democracy as a playground for demagogues, rejected "international fraternity" as verbal exercise, and resented socialists ignoring partitioned Poland. He never voted in British elections.

The 1975 Chinua Achebe essay "An Image of Africa" called Conrad a "thoroughgoing racist" for *Heart of Darkness*’s dehumanization of Africans. Defenders argue Achebe conflates Marlow’s view with Conrad’s, noting the novella condemns colonial brutality (E.D. Morel called it "the most powerful thing written on the subject"). Peter Firchow contends Conrad acknowledges racial distinctions but asserts no essential superiority. Nidesh Lawtoo links Conrad’s "frenzy" imagery to possession trance rituals also central to Achebe’s *Things Fall Apart*. Hochschild and Zins root Conrad’s anti-imperialism in his Polish experience of conquest and serfdom.

### Personal Life and Death
Conrad was reserved, sceptical, and plagued by gout, malaria, neuralgia, depression, and a dental phobia ("every novel... cost him a tooth"). His 1878 suicide attempt followed financial collapse in Marseille. He married Jessie George (1896), sixteen years his junior; friends found her an "inexplicable choice," but biographers credit her devotion with sustaining his career. They moved frequently, seeking psychological regeneration; at Capel House (1910–19) he wrote *The Rescue*, *Victory*, *The Arrow of Gold*. A 1914 Poland visit, cut short by WWI, deepened Jessie’s understanding of his temperament. He declined a knighthood (1924) and honorary degrees, keeping distance from official structures. He died of a heart attack at Oswalds; his gravestone bears lines from Spenser’s *The Faerie Queene*: "Sleep after toyle, port after stormie seas / Ease after warre, death after life, doth greatly please."

## Terms
- ***Szlachta*** — Polish nobility; Conrad’s family bore the Nałęcz coat-of-arms.
- **"Red" faction** — Polish political group seeking pre-partition boundaries, land reform, and abolition of serfdom; Apollo Korzeniowski was a member.
- **Panslavism** — Ideology advocating unity of Slavic nations; Conrad initially supported it but abandoned it after 1885–86.
- **Red Ensign** — Flag of the British merchant marine; Conrad served under it for fifteen years.
- ***Le mot juste*** — Flaubert’s term for "the right word"; Conrad emulated this precision in his acquired English.
- **Impressionism (literary)** — Style emphasizing subjective perception, sensory detail, and the contingency of knowledge; Conrad a key practitioner.
- **Marlow** — Recurring narrator (e.g., *Heart of Darkness*, *Lord Jim*, *Chance*); a seaman-storyteller who mediates Conrad’s fragmented perspective.
- **Civil list pension** — Government grant (£100/year, 1910) that alleviated Conrad’s chronic financial struggles.
- ***Bibuła*** — Underground self-published literature in the Polish People’s Republic; *Under Western Eyes* circulated as such in the 1980s.
- **Postcolonial mimesis** — Nidesh Lawtoo’s term for Achebe’s *Things Fall Apart* as a counter-narrative to *Heart of Darkness* that repeats with difference.

## Debates and open questions
- **Racism in *Heart of Darkness*:** Achebe’s charge of "thoroughgoing racism" vs. defenses that Conrad critiques imperialism through irony and Marlow’s unreliable narration; Firchow’s "weak sense" racism (acknowledging difference without superiority); Lawtoo’s ritualistic reading complicating the binary.
- **Conrad’s political stance:** Whether his anti-democratic, anti-socialist, pro-nationalist views reflect a coherent philosophy or the "hopeless fidelity" of a Polish exile; the tension between his irony ("blanket incredulity") and insistence on "some saving truth."
- **Language and identity:** Whether writing in English was a pragmatic choice, an artistic liberation ("auto-translation"), or a betrayal of Polish literature; the extent to which "Poglish" syntax shapes his unique style.
- **Autobiography vs. fiction:** Najder’s warning against "uncritical linking" of life and work; Said’s view that letters reformulate into fiction; the degree to which Conrad’s "view of the world" is a construct of both spheres.
- **Modernist credentials:** Whether Conrad is a late realist, an impressionist, or an early modernist; Robson’s comparisons to Picasso, Eliot, and Rashomon; the priority of his narrative innovations (e.g., Marlow) over Henry James’s later experiments.
