# Robert Bresson

## Overview
Robert Bresson (1901–1999) was a French filmmaker whose ascetic, minimalist style redefined cinematic language. Rejecting theatrical performance, he directed non-professional "models" through exhaustive repetition to strip away artifice, creating films noted for their spiritual gravity and formal rigor. His 13 features, including *A Man Escaped* (1956), *Pickpocket* (1959), and *Au hasard Balthazar* (1966), consistently rank among the greatest ever made. Deeply influenced by Catholicism and his experience as a prisoner of war, Bresson explored themes of grace, confinement, and redemption in a materialist world. His 1975 theoretical work *Notes on the Cinematographer* and his "actor-model" method profoundly shaped the French New Wave and subsequent generations of global auteurs.

## Timeline
- **25 Sep 1901** — Born in Bromont-Lamothe, Puy-de-Dôme, France.
- **1934** — Completed first short film, *Les affaires publiques* (Public Affairs).
- **1940** — Captured by German forces during WWII; held as POW for over a year (informs *A Man Escaped*).
- **1943** — Released first feature, *Les Anges du péché* (Angels of Sin).
- **1956** — Released *A Man Escaped*; wins Best Director at Cannes.
- **1959** — Released *Pickpocket*.
- **1966** — Released *Au hasard Balthazar*.
- **1967** — Released *Mouchette*.
- **1971** — Released *Four Nights of a Dreamer*.
- **1975** — Published *Notes on the Cinematographer*.
- **1977** — Released *The Devil Probably*.
- **1983** — Released final feature, *L'Argent* (Money); wins Best Director at Cannes.
- **18 Dec 1999** — Died in Droue-sur-Drouette, France, aged 98.

## Body

### Artistic Minimalism and the "Model" Theory
Bresson defined "cinematography" not as filmed theater but as a distinct language of moving images and sounds. To achieve this, he rejected professional actors and the very concept of "acting" (movement from interior to exterior), preferring non-professional "models" who move from the exterior to the interior. He directed models through dozens of takes until all traces of performance vanished, leaving a raw, opaque presence that critic Roger Ebert argued allows audiences to internalize emotions rather than merely observe them. Susan Sontag described this as a "reflective mode" that disciplines the viewer’s emotions to induce a state of spiritual balance. Bresson also pared sound design to essentials, associating specific noises with characters or actions while largely eschewing non-diegetic musical scoring, a restraint that became a hallmark of minimalist cinema.

### Catholicism, Jansenism, and Spiritual Themes
Bresson identified as Catholic but held heterodox views, expressing affinity for Jansenism—a theology emphasizing predestination and human depravity—over Jesuit thought, and doubting the resurrection of the body. He stopped attending Mass after the Second Vatican Council (1962–65), feeling the new liturgy obscured the divine presence. His films treat salvation, redemption, and the soul’s contact with God as structural metaphors rather than explicit sermons. *A Man Escaped* functions as a metaphor for the mysterious process of grace; *Mouchette* (1967) embodies universal victimhood; *The Devil Probably* (1977) critiques the post-Vatican II Church’s failure to satisfy spiritual hunger. Bresson deliberately embedded religious undercurrents in secular narratives—crime, imprisonment, betrayal—believing modern audiences were too materialistic for overt piety. He sought to make viewers *feel* a divine presence in ordinary reality without naming it, stating, "I don't want to shoot something in which God would be too transparent."

### French New Wave and Auteur Theory
Bresson opposed the pre-war *Tradition de la Qualité* (studio-bound, script-driven cinema) by insisting on the director as the singular author (*auteur*). François Truffaut cited him as a rare genuine auteur capable of filming the "unfilmable." Jean-Luc Godard equated his centrality to French cinema with Dostoevsky’s to the Russian novel. Alain Cavalier positioned Bresson as the "father" of French cinema (representing the law/strictness) alongside Renoir as "mother" (warmth). While the New Wave revered him as a progenitor, Bresson remained distinct: he was less experimentally playful, less overtly political, and his Jansenist Catholicism alienated many leftist *Cahiers du Cinéma* alumni.

### Global Influence and Reception
Bresson’s influence spans continents and generations. Andrei Tarkovsky named him and Bergman as his only two critical references, calling Bresson "perhaps the only artist in cinema" to perfectly fuse theory and work. Paul Schrader analyzed him in *Transcendental Style in Film* (1972). The Dardenne brothers modeled *L'Enfant* on *Pickpocket*; Christopher Nolan cited *Pickpocket* and *A Man Escaped* for *Dunkirk*; Yorgos Lanthimos called *Pickpocket* "the most moving film I've ever seen." Admirers include Ingmar Bergman, Werner Herzog, Abbas Kiarostami, Béla Tarr, Martin Scorsese, and Rainer Werner Fassbinder, who threatened to quit the 1977 Berlin jury unless *The Devil Probably* received recognition. In the *Sight and Sound* critics' polls, Bresson holds the record for most films (seven) in the all-time top rankings.

## Terms
- ****Cinematography (Bressonian sense)**** — The creation of a new language of moving images and sounds, distinct from "filmed theatre."
- ****Model**** — Bresson’s term for non-professional performers; they move from exterior gesture to interior truth, opposite to actors.
- ****Jansenism**** — A Catholic theological movement emphasizing original sin, human depravity, predestination, and the necessity of divine grace; Bresson felt closer to this than Jesuit theology.
- ****Tradition de la Qualité**** — Pre-war French "tradition of quality" cinema: studio-bound, literary adaptations reliant on screenwriters and stars, opposed by the New Wave.
- ****Auteur theory**** — Critical framework (championed by Truffaut) positing the director as the primary author of a film; Bresson is a paradigmatic example.
- ****Transcendental Style**** — Term coined by Paul Schrader describing a slow, austere cinematic mode (Bresson, Ozu, Dreyer) that evokes the spiritual through formal restraint.
- ****Ellipsis**** — Narrative omission of significant events (e.g., the escape in *A Man Escaped*), forcing focus on process and consequence rather than spectacle.
- ****Diegetic sound**** — Sound originating from the film’s world (footsteps, doors, machinery); Bresson privileged this over non-diegetic score.

## Debates and open questions
- **"Christian atheist" label**: Several writers claim Bresson described himself this way, but the source context is unknown and the statement unverified in the text.
- **Genesis project**: Bresson developed a *Book of Genesis* adaptation from the 1960s onward with backing from Dino De Laurentiis, but 1980s production attempts failed due to funding; the project remains unrealized.
- **Political vs. spiritual reading**: Critics debate whether Bresson’s critique of modern society (e.g., *L'Argent*) is primarily a theological diagnosis of grace’s absence or a radical political indictment of capitalism.
- **New Wave paternity**: While revered by Truffaut and Godard, Bresson’s Jansenist rigor and apolitical stance contrasted sharply with the New Wave’s leftist, experimental ethos, complicating his status as a direct "precursor."
