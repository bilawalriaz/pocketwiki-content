# John Keats

## Overview
John Keats (1795–1821) was an English Romantic poet whose career spanned barely six years and whose three published volumes sold roughly 200 copies in his lifetime. He died of tuberculosis at 25, convinced he had left "no immortal work behind me." Posthumously, his reputation grew rapidly; by the late Victorian era he was canonized as a master of sensory verse, his odes and medievalist narratives ("La Belle Dame sans Merci," "The Eve of St. Agnes") shaping the Pre-Raphaelite movement and influencing writers from Tennyson to Borges. Keats’s letters—especially his concepts of "negative capability" (the capacity to dwell in uncertainty without grasping for fact) and the "chameleon poet" (the writer who has no fixed identity but inhabits others)—became foundational texts for literary criticism. His life dramatizes the tension between medical training and poetic ambition, between poverty and the pursuit of beauty, and between a brief, intense love for Fanny Brawne and the "family disease" that killed his mother, brother Tom, and ultimately himself.

## Body

### Early Life and Medical Training
Keats was born into a stable-keeping family; his father managed the Swan and Hoop Inn. After his father’s death (1804) and mother’s remarriage and departure, the four children were raised by their grandmother in Edmonton. Unable to afford Eton or Harrow, Keats attended John Clarke’s liberal school in Enfield (1803–11), where Charles Cowden Clarke fostered his love of classics and Renaissance literature. At 14, following his mother’s death from tuberculosis, he was apprenticed to Thomas Hammond, a surgeon-apothecary. The apprenticeship was later called "the most placid time in Keats’s life," but he also began writing poetry, producing "An Imitation of Spenser" (1814). In 1815 he entered Guy’s Hospital as a medical student, quickly becoming a dresser—a role demanding skill and nerve. His family expected a secure medical career, yet Keats felt torn: his brother George recorded that John "feared that he should never be a poet, & if he was not he would destroy himself." In 1816 he gained his apothecary’s licence but announced to his guardian Richard Abbey that he would pursue poetry instead.

### Literary Circles and Early Publication
Through Cowden Clarke, Keats met Leigh Hunt, editor of the liberal *Examiner*, who published "O Solitude" (May 1816) and introduced Keats to a circle including Charles Lamb, William Hazlitt, and John Hamilton Reynolds. Hunt’s 1817 essay "Three Young Poets" grouped Keats with Shelley and Reynolds. Keats’s first volume, *Poems* (1817), published by the Olliers, was a critical failure; the publishers were ashamed, and Keats switched to Taylor and Hessey, who became enthusiastic backers. Their lawyer, Richard Woodhouse, recognized Keats’s genius and began collecting "Keatsiana," a primary source for later biographers. In December 1816 Keats wrote to Bailey: "I am certain of nothing but the holiness of the Heart's affections and the truth of the imagination"—a formulation that became the closing lines of "Ode on a Grecian Urn" ("Beauty is truth, truth beauty"). Despite financial desperation—he lent £700 to his brother George and borrowed heavily from Abbey—Keats committed fully to poetry.

### Illness, Travel, and the *Annus Mirabilis*
In 1817 Keats moved to Hampstead with his brothers to nurse Tom, who had tuberculosis. A walking tour of Scotland and the Lake District with Charles Brown (June–August 1818) exhausted Keats; he caught a severe cold on the Isle of Mull, and some scholars date his own infection to this period or to nursing Tom. Tom died 1 December 1818. Keats then moved to Wentworth Place, Brown’s new house on Hampstead Heath. The winter of 1818–19 began his *annus mirabilis*: inspired by Hazlitt’s lectures and meetings with Wordsworth, he composed five of his six great odes in April–May 1819 ("Ode to Psyche," "Ode to a Nightingale," "Ode on a Grecian Urn," "Ode on Melancholy," "Ode on Indolence"), plus "The Eve of St. Agnes," "La Belle Dame sans Merci," "Hyperion," "Lamia," and the play *Otho the Great*. Brown recorded that "Ode to a Nightingale" was written under a plum tree in the garden, though co-owner Charles Dilke denied the story. *Endymion* (1818), dedicated to Thomas Chatterton, had been savaged by *Quarterly Review* (John Wilson Croker) and *Blackwood’s Magazine* (John Gibson Lockhart), the latter coining the pejorative "Cockney School" for Hunt’s circle—an attack as much political (class, education) as literary. Keats’s 1820 volume, *Lamia, Isabella, The Eve of St. Agnes, and Other Poems*, finally earned serious acclaim.

### Relationships: Isabella Jones and Fanny Brawne
Keats met Isabella Jones in May 1817 at Bo Peep near Hastings. She was well-read, financially independent, and sexually experienced; Keats admitted his attraction, writing he "warmed with her" and "kissed her." She may have inspired "The Eve of St. Agnes," "Bright Star" (first version), and the lyric "Hush, Hush! ['o sweet Isabel']." By late 1818 Keats met Frances (Fanny) Brawne, 18, who moved into Wentworth Place in April 1819. Their daily contact deepened into an intense, unconsummated love; Keats gave her "Bright Star" (revised until his death) as a declaration. He wrote: "I have two luxuries to brood over in my walks: your loveliness, and the hour of my death." Jealousy, poverty, and his failing health prevented formal engagement. In September 1820 Keats left for Rome, knowing he would not return; he could not bear to read her letters. Brawne mourned six years, married in 1833, and outlived Keats by over 40 years.

### Final Months in Rome
Keats suffered two lung haemorrhages in early February 1820; on coughing blood he told Brown, "That drop of blood is my death warrant." Doctors advised a warmer climate. He sailed with Joseph Severn on 13 September 1820; the voyage was plagued by storms, calm, and a ten-day cholera quarantine in Naples. He reached Rome 14 November, too late for beneficial weather. He lodged at the Spanish Steps (now Keats–Shelley Memorial House) under Dr. James Clark, who diagnosed "mental exertion" as the cause, prescribed a starvation diet (an anchovy and bread daily), and bled him—standard treatment but likely hastening his decline. Keats begged for laudanum (opium) to ease pain or allow suicide; Severn and Clark refused. His last letter (30 November) spoke of a "posthumous existence." He died 23 February 1821, aged 25. Severn described his final hours: Keats asked to be lifted, said "I shall die easy," and sank quietly. The autopsy showed lungs almost disintegrated.

### Posthumous Reception and Legacy
Keats published for only four years; lifetime sales ~200 copies. Shelley’s *Adonais* (1821) mythologized him as a hypersensitive genius killed by critics. Victorian taste for sensory luxury fitted Keats perfectly; the Cambridge Apostles (including Tennyson) championed him. The Pre-Raphaelites (Rossetti, Millais, Morris, Swinburne) painted scenes from his medievalist poems, cementing his visual legacy. *Encyclopædia Britannica* (1888) ranked "Ode to a Nightingale" among "final masterpieces." In the 20th century, Yeats and Wilfred Owen (who kept Keats’s death date as a day of mourning) drew directly on him; T.S. Eliot found in Keats’s letters a blueprint for modernist theory. Helen Vendler called the odes "the English language finding an ultimate embodiment"; Walter Jackson Bate deemed "To Autumn" "one of the most nearly perfect poems in English." Major manuscript collections reside at Harvard’s Houghton Library, the British Library, Keats House (Hampstead), the Keats–Shelley House (Rome), and the Pierpont Morgan Library.

### The Letters as Philosophy
Keats’s letters, first published 1848 and 1878, were dismissed by Victorian critics but revered by modernists. T.S. Eliot called them "the most notable and most important ever written by any English poet." Written in an "unself-conscious stream of consciousness," they blend daily news, parody, and profound aesthetic theory. In 1817–19 Keats coined "negative capability"—being "capable of being in uncertainties, Mysteries, doubts without any irritable reaching after fact & reason"—and the "chameleon poet": "A Poet is the most unpoetical of any thing in existence; because he has no Identity—he is continually in for—and filling some other Body." He conceived the world as a "vale of Soul-making" (anticipating the odes) and declared "What the imagination seizes as Beauty must be truth." His letters to George in America became a "spiritual journal" and contain drafts of major poems. In September 1819 a walk in stubble fields inspired the final stanza of "To Autumn," later judged by M.R. Ridley "the most serenely flawless poem in our language."

## Terms
- ****Negative capability**** — Keats’s term for the poet’s ability to remain in "uncertainties, Mysteries, doubts without any irritable reaching after fact & reason," trusting intuition over logic.
- ****Chameleon poet**** — Keats’s concept that the poet "has no Identity" but continually inhabits other beings—"the Sun, the Moon, the Sea and Men and Women"—lacking a fixed self.
- ****Vale of Soul-making**** — Keats’s metaphor (letter to George, 1819) for the world as a place where suffering and experience forge the soul, contrasting with the Christian "vale of tears."
- ****Cockney School**** — A derogatory label coined by *Blackwood’s Magazine* (Lockhart) for Hunt’s circle (including Keats), attacking their lower-class origins, lack of elite education, and "low diction."
- ****Dresser**** — A junior surgical assistant at a hospital (equivalent to a modern junior house surgeon); Keats held this post at Guy’s Hospital, indicating medical aptitude.
- ****Consumption**** — The contemporary term for tuberculosis; not identified as a single infectious disease until 1820, and stigmatized as linked to weakness or repressed passion.
- ****Annus mirabilis**** — Latin for "year of wonders"; refers to Keats’s extraordinary creative burst (winter 1818–spring 1819) producing the major odes and narrative poems.
- ****Apothecary**** — A medical practitioner who prepared and sold medicines; Keats’s 1816 licence allowed him to practice as apothecary, physician, and surgeon.
- ****Laudanum**** — A tincture of opium in alcohol, used as a painkiller and sedative; Keats sought it for pain relief and possibly suicide; Severn and Clark withheld it.
- ****Posthumous existence**** — Keats’s phrase (letter to Brown, 30 Nov 1820) for the sense that his real life had ended and he was merely lingering after death.

## Debates and open questions
- **Order of the odes**: Scholars debate the exact composition sequence of the five 1819 odes; Brown’s plum-tree anecdote for "Ode to a Nightingale" is contested by Dilke.
- **Onset of tuberculosis**: Whether Keats contracted TB on the 1818 walking tour (Isle of Mull cold) or while nursing Tom remains uncertain; the disease’s infectious nature was not established until 1820.
- **Medical treatment in Rome**: Dr. Clark’s starvation diet and bleeding likely accelerated Keats’s physical collapse; whether opium (laudanum) should have been granted for palliative care is a historical-ethical dispute.
- **Financial guardianship**: Keats was unaware of an £800 legacy from his grandfather; blame has fallen on guardian Richard Abbey, but solicitor William Walton also failed to inform him.
- **Biographical mythmaking**: No biography was written by someone who knew Keats; early accounts (Hunt, Shelley, Brown, Severn, Brawne) contradict each other, embedding legends—especially the "humble origins" narrative and the "poet killed by critics" trope—that still shape popular perception.
- **Fanny Brawne’s letters**: None survive; the relationship’s full texture is known only through Keats’s side, published 1870, which emphasized tragedy and drew contemporary criticism.
- **Political dimension of the "Cockney School" attacks**: The *Blackwood’s*/*Quarterly* reviews targeted Keats’s class and politics as much as his verse; the extent to which this shaped his reception versus genuine aesthetic judgment is debated.
