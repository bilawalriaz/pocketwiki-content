# Legal history

## Overview
Legal history studies how law has evolved and why it has changed, connecting legal development to broader social and civilizational history. It examines legal institutions as complex systems interacting with society, moving beyond traditional jurisprudence to analyze law through social-science inquiry.

## Body

### Ancient Legal Systems
Ancient Egyptian law (c. 3000 BC) emphasized tradition, social equality, and impartiality through the concept of Ma'at. The first known law code came from Sumerian ruler Ur-Nammu (22nd century BC), using "if... then..." statements. Babylonian King Hammurabi (c. 1760 BC) further developed law by publicly inscribing codes on stone stelae. Ancient Greek law distinguished between divine law (thémis), human decree (nómos), and custom (díkē), contributing constitutional innovations to democracy.

### Asian Legal Traditions
Ancient India and China developed independent legal traditions. Indian treatises like the Arthashastra (400 BC) and Manusmriti (100 BCE) provided authoritative guidance, with Manu emphasizing tolerance and pluralism. Islamic sharia was later established during Muslim conquests, notably through the Mughal Empire's Fatawa-e-Alamgiri. British colonialism eventually supplanted Hindu and Islamic law with common law in India and other Asian territories.

### Eastern Asian Modernization
Japan pioneered Western-style legal modernization by importing French and primarily German civil codes in the late 19th century. China followed with private law codes based on the Japanese model of German law during the Qing dynasty's final years. Taiwan retained these codifications after the 1949 split between nationalists and communists. The People's Republic of China initially adopted Soviet Socialist law emphasizing administrative law, but has since reformed economic rights, exemplified by the 1999 contract code and 2001 WTO accession.

### Canon Law
Catholic canon law represents the oldest continuously functioning Western legal system, originating later than Roman law but predating modern European civil law traditions. Its history divides into four periods: jus antiquum, jus novum, jus novissimum, and the modern Code. The 1917 Code of Canon Law was the first comprehensive codification, followed by the 1983 Code promulgated by John Paul II, who also codified Eastern Catholic canon law in 1990.

### Islamic Law
Islamic law and jurisprudence developed major legal institutions during the Middle Ages, including the Hawala—an informal value transfer system mentioned in 8th-century texts. This institution later influenced European legal concepts like the French Aval and Italian Avallo.

### European Legal Evolution
Roman law, heavily influenced by Greek teachings, formed the bridge to modern legal systems. Emperor Justinian's 6th-century codification (Corpus Juris Civilis) consolidated existing Roman law. After the Western Empire's fall, Germanic customary law ("folk-right") blended with Roman law in former territories. Charlemagne centralized law to strengthen royal courts, but feudalism later created decentralized legal cultures favoring customary law.

The 11th-century rediscovery of Justinian's Code through Byzantine texts sparked Roman law revival at the University of Bologna, though civil law rivaled customary law for centuries. In England, Norman conquest introduced legal concepts that evolved into common law through royal judges developing precedent, particularly under Henry II's reforms including the 1166 Assize of Clarendon. The Law Merchant, based on Scandinavian trade customs and Hanseatic League standards, emerged by the 10th century as a precursor to modern commercial law.

### Modern European and American Law
Modern European law divides into codified civil law systems (influenced by the French Napoleonic Code and German Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch) and English common law based on case law. European Union law combines codified treaties with precedent from the European Court of Justice. The United States primarily developed from English common law, except Louisiana which maintained French civil law, with some Spanish law concepts persisting in former Mexican Cession states.

### African Law
African legal systems originally based on ethnic customs and oral traditions, with elders serving as mediators. The Mali Empire's Kouroukan Fouga (1222-1236 AD) served as an official constitution. Colonialism introduced the Native Courts system, after which Buddhism, Hinduism, and Judaism remained as major faiths.

## Terms
- **Ma'at**: Ancient Egyptian concept of truth, balance, and cosmic order underlying law
- **Casuistic statements**: Legal formulations using "if... then..." conditional structures
- **Corpus Juris Civilis**: Justinian's 6th-century codification of Roman law
- **Folk-right**: Germanic customary law used alongside Roman law after the Western Empire's fall
- **Law Merchant**: Medieval commercial law based on trade customs, precursor to modern commercial law
- **Civil law**: Legal system based on codified statutes rather than case law precedent
- **Common law**: Legal system based on judicial precedent and case law
- **Hawala**: Early Islamic informal value transfer system
- **Jus commune**: Legal system resulting from cultural exchange between secular and ecclesiastical law

## Debates and Open Questions
The article notes scholarly debate about whether legal history should be viewed as recording legal evolution for understanding concepts (traditional view) or as a branch of intellectual history. Twentieth-century historians shifted toward contextualized social history approaches, analyzing legal institutions through social-science methods rather than traditional jurisprudence. The relationship between civil and common law traditions continues evolving, with signs of convergence in modern legal systems.
