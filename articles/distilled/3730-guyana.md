# Guyana

## Overview
Guyana is a country on the northern coast of South America, part of the Guianas region. It is the only mainland South American nation with English as its official language and is a member of the Caribbean Community (CARICOM). Historically a plantation colony, its modern economy has been transformed by massive offshore oil discoveries since 2015, making it one of the world's fastest-growing economies, though it faces significant challenges including poverty and a long-standing territorial dispute with Venezuela.

## Timeline
- **1581–1752** — Dutch establish colonies: Pomeroon, Essequibo, Berbice, and Demerara.
- **1796** — British assume control of the Dutch colonies.
- **1814** — Anglo-Dutch Treaty formally cedes Demerara-Essequibo and Berbice to Britain.
- **1831** — British colonies merge to form British Guiana.
- **1899** — International tribunal rules the Essequibo territory belongs to Great Britain.
- **26 May 1966** — Guyana gains independence from the UK as a dominion.
- **23 February 1970** — Guyana becomes a republic within the Commonwealth.
- **1978** — Jonestown mass murder-suicide occurs.
- **5 October 1992** — First internationally recognized free and fair elections since 1964 are held.
- **2015** — Major offshore oil reserves are discovered by ExxonMobil.
- **2019** — Commercial offshore oil drilling begins.
- **December 2023** — Venezuela holds a referendum on annexing the Essequibo region.

## Body

### History and Colonial Legacy
The region was inhabited by Indigenous tribes, including the Lokono and Kalina, for millennia. The Dutch were the first Europeans to establish colonies, which were later ceded to Britain in 1814. British Guiana operated a plantation economy reliant on enslaved African labour. After emancipation in 1838, indentured labourers from India were brought to the country, creating the demographic foundation for the modern Indo-Guyanese and Afro-Guyanese populations. The country gained independence in 1966 and became a republic in 1970. Post-independence politics were often tense and divided along ethnic lines, with periods of authoritarian rule. The first free and fair elections since 1964 were held in 1992.

### Geography and Biodiversity
Guyana is one of the world's most sparsely populated countries, with most of its population living on a narrow coastal plain. The country's interior features dense rainforests, savannahs, and mountains, including Mount Roraima. It is part of the Guiana Shield, a region of extreme biodiversity. Over 80% of Guyana is covered by forests, which are largely pristine. The country is home to thousands of plant and animal species, including the giant otter and harpy eagle. Conservation efforts include the Kanashen Community-Owned Conservation Area and the Iwokrama International Centre.

### Government and Politics
Guyana is a parliamentary representative democratic republic. Its politics have historically been dominated by two parties organized along ethnic lines: the People's National Congress (PNC) and the People's Progressive Party (PPP). A major ongoing issue is the territorial dispute with Venezuela over the Essequibo region, which Venezuela claims but has been under Guyanese control since a British colonial-era arbitration in 1899. Guyana has taken the case to the International Court of Justice. The country also has minor border disputes with Suriname.

### Economy
Guyana's economy was traditionally based on agriculture (sugar, rice) and mining (bauxite, gold). The discovery of massive offshore oil reserves in 2015 has led to an economic transformation, with GDP growing by 49% in 2020. By 2025, oil production reached 261.1 million barrels, and the country is poised to become a major per capita oil producer. However, the economy faces risks such as overheating and the need to manage revenues to avoid neglecting non-oil sectors. Poverty remains a significant issue, with 41% of the population living below the poverty line in 2017.

### Demographics and Culture
Guyana's population is ethnically diverse, with Indo-Guyanese (43.5%) and Afro-Guyanese (30.2%) as the largest groups. English is the official language, but Guyanese Creole is widely spoken. The culture reflects a blend of Caribbean, Indian, African, and Indigenous influences, seen in festivals like Mashramani and Phagwah. Cricket is the major sport, and Guyana is part of the West Indies cricket team.

## Terms
- **CARICOM**: The Caribbean Community, an organization of Caribbean nations for economic and political cooperation, headquartered in Georgetown, Guyana.
- **Creole**: A language that develops from a mixture of different languages; Guyanese Creole is an English-based creole.
- **Essequibo**: The largest river in Guyana and the region west of it that is the subject of a territorial dispute with Venezuela.
- **Guiana Shield**: A 1.7-billion-year-old Precambrian geological formation in northern South America, known for its high biodiversity.
- **Indentured labour**: A system of bonded labour where workers are contracted to work for a specific period, used to replace enslaved labour after emancipation.
- **REDD+**: Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation, a UN-backed framework to incentivize forest conservation.
- **Tepui**: A table-top mountain or mesa found in the Guiana Highlands of South America.

## Debates and open questions
- **Territorial Dispute**: The core debate is the validity of the 1899 Paris Arbitral Award that granted the Essequibo region to British Guiana. Venezuela argues the 1966 Geneva Agreement nullified it, while Guyana maintains the award is valid and binding. The International Court of Justice is adjudicating the case.
- **Economic Management**: A key question is how Guyana will manage its oil wealth to ensure broad-based development, avoid the "resource curse," and address existing poverty and inequality without harming its non-oil sectors and pristine environment.
- **Ethnic Politics**: The deep ethnic division in politics between Indo-Guyanese and Afro-Guyanese communities remains a source of tension and a challenge to national cohesion.
