# Mamluk

## Overview
The Mamluks were a unique military class in the Islamic world, composed of enslaved non-Arab mercenaries (primarily Turkic, Circassian, and Caucasian) who were purchased as children, converted to Islam, and trained as elite soldiers. Upon manumission, they formed a powerful, self-replicating military caste that often held supreme political power, most notably ruling Egypt and Syria as the Mamluk Sultanate (1250–1517). Their system, which lasted nearly a millennium, profoundly shaped the political and military history of the Middle East.

## Timeline
- **9th century** — The Mamluk system of military slavery develops under the Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad.
- **1250** — Mamluk factions seize power in Egypt, founding the Mamluk Sultanate after defeating the Seventh Crusade.
- **1260** — The Mamluks defeat the Mongol Ilkhanate at the Battle of Ain Jalut, halting their advance into Egypt.
- **1302** — The Mamluk Sultanate expels the last Crusaders from the Levant, ending the Crusades.
- **1382** — The Burji dynasty, composed mainly of Circassians, takes control of the Mamluk Sultanate.
- **1501** — Hostilities begin between the Mamluk Sultanate and the Ottoman Empire over relations with Persia.
- **1516** — The Ottoman Sultan Selim I defeats and kills the Mamluk Sultan at the Battle of Marj Dabiq.
- **1517** — The Ottoman Empire conquers Cairo, ending the Mamluk Sultanate, though Mamluks retain influence as a ruling class.
- **1798** — Napoleon Bonaparte invades Egypt, defeating the Mamluk cavalry at the Battle of the Pyramids.
- **1811** — Muhammad Ali Pasha massacres the leading Mamluks in Cairo, effectively destroying their power in Egypt.

## Body

### Origins and System
The Mamluk phenomenon began in the 9th century under the Abbasid Caliph al-Muʿtaṣim, who purchased Turkic slaves to form a loyal military force. Historians distinguish the later, systematic Mamluk system from the earlier *Ghilman* system; it involved the systematic training of young non-Muslim slaves in military and courtly skills within barracks. Upon completion of training, they were freed (*kharj* ceremony) but remained attached to their patron, forming an elite military caste with no biological family ties, which made them loyal to the sultan. This system was adopted by various Muslim dynasties, including the Fatimids and Ayyubids.

### The Mamluk Sultanate of Cairo
The most powerful Mamluk state was the Sultanate in Cairo, which began in 1250. It is divided into two dynasties: the Bahri (1250–1382), primarily Turkic, and the Burji (1382–1517), primarily Circassian. The Mamluks are famed for defeating the Mongols at Ain Jalut in 1260 and for expelling the Crusaders from the Levant by 1302. Their rule was characterized by frequent internal power struggles, with sultans often having short reigns.

### Decline and Fall
The Mamluk Sultanate faced growing threats from the Ottoman Empire and European powers. After conflicts with the Ottomans beginning in 1501, Sultan Selim I conquered Egypt in 1516–1517. Although the Mamluks were subjugated, they remained a powerful feudal class under Ottoman rule. Their final destruction came in Egypt under Muhammad Ali Pasha, who massacred their leaders in 1811. A remnant fled to Sudan, where they were later dispersed by Egyptian forces in 1820.

### Other Mamluk Regimes
Mamluk systems existed elsewhere, including the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1290) in India and a Mamluk-ruled province in Iraq (1704–1831). In these regions, Mamluks often established autonomous or semi-autonomous rule.

## Terms
*   **Mamluk**: Literally "one who is owned"; a slave-soldier, typically of non-Arab origin, trained for military service in the Islamic world.
*   **Ghilman/Ghulam**: A broader term for slave soldiers, often used interchangeably with Mamluk, though some historians distinguish the earlier Ghilman system.
*   **Kharj**: The ceremony of manumission for a Mamluk, marking his transition from slave to free ex-slave soldier.
*   **Bahri Dynasty**: The first Mamluk dynasty in Cairo (1250–1382), named for their barracks on the Nile's Rhoda Island.
*   **Burji Dynasty**: The second Mamluk dynasty in Cairo (1382–1517), named for their barracks at the Citadel of Cairo.
*   **Atabeg**: A title for a regent or guardian, often a powerful Mamluk amir acting as a regional governor.
*   **Devşirme**: The Ottoman system of collecting young Christian boys for conversion and service, notably in the Janissary corps.
*   **Saqaliba**: A term for Slavic or other European slaves, sometimes used to describe early Mamluk recruits.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Ghilman vs. Mamluk**: Historians debate the distinction between the earlier *Ghilman* system and the later, more systematic Mamluk institution. The current consensus suggests the Mamluk system was a later, more organized development.
*   **Impact on Political Development**: Scholars argue that the Mamluk system allowed Muslim rulers to bypass local elites, which may have hindered the development of representative government compared to Europe, where rulers relied on local lords for military forces.
*   **Mamluk Identity**: The extent to which Mamluks maintained ties to their homelands (e.g., Georgian Mamluks sending money and gifts to family) versus forming a new, exclusive identity is a subject of study.
