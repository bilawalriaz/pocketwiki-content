# Jöns Jacob Berzelius

## Overview
Jöns Jacob Berzelius (1779–1848) was a Swedish chemist considered a founder of modern chemistry. He revolutionized the field by establishing the law of definite proportions, creating the modern system of chemical notation, and discovering or isolating several elements. His work provided the quantitative foundation for atomic theory and shaped the language of chemistry.

## Body

### Foundational Work in Stoichiometry
Berzelius began his scientific career by analyzing inorganic compounds to write a chemistry textbook. This empirical work led him to formulate the law of definite proportions, which states that chemical compounds are composed of elements in fixed, whole-number ratios by mass. His meticulous experiments provided strong evidence for John Dalton's atomic theory and disproved Prout's hypothesis that elements are multiples of hydrogen's atomic weight. His final table of atomic weights, with oxygen set to 100, was published in 1826.

### Chemical Notation and Theory
To represent chemical compositions, Berzelius developed a system using letter symbols for elements (e.g., O for oxygen) and superscript numbers to indicate atom counts (e.g., H²O). This notation, which later evolved to use subscripts, became the universal standard. He also proposed the theory of electrochemical dualism, which posited that compounds are held together by the electrostatic attraction between electropositive and electronegative constituents. While useful, this theory was eventually superseded.

### Discovery of Elements and New Terms
Berzelius is credited with discovering cerium (1803) and selenium (1817), and he was the first to isolate silicon and thorium (1824). His laboratory also yielded discoveries of lithium, lanthanum, and vanadium. He coined many chemical terms still in use, including "catalysis," "polymer," "isomer," "allotrope," and "protein." He was the first to formally distinguish between organic (carbon-containing) and inorganic compounds.

### Biology and the Vitalism Debate
Berzelius advised Gerardus Johannes Mulder on organic analysis and coined the term "protein" in 1838. He initially supported the vitalism hypothesis, which held that organic compounds required a "vital force" from living organisms for their synthesis. This view was challenged in 1828 when Friedrich Wöhler synthesized urea, an organic compound, from inorganic materials. Berzelius corresponded with Wöhler on this finding, but the full rejection of vitalism came with further abiotic syntheses.

## Terms
- **Law of definite proportions**: The principle that a chemical compound always contains its component elements in fixed ratio (by mass) and does not depend on its source and method of preparation.
- **Stoichiometry**: The branch of chemistry that deals with the quantitative relationships between the reactants and products in chemical reactions.
- **Electrochemical dualism**: A theory proposed by Berzelius that chemical compounds are formed by the electrostatic attraction between electropositive and electronegative components.
- **Chemical notation**: A system of symbols and formulas used to represent elements and the composition of chemical compounds.
- **Isomerism**: The phenomenon where compounds have the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements and properties.
- **Allotropy**: The property of some chemical elements to exist in two or more different forms, known as allotropes, in the same physical state.
- **Catalysis**: The acceleration of a chemical reaction by a substance (a catalyst) that is not consumed in the reaction.
- **Vitalism**: The historical hypothesis that the compounds formed only by living organisms are created by a "vital force" and cannot be synthesized from inorganic materials.

## Debates and open questions
- **Vitalism vs. synthetic chemistry**: Berzelius's advocacy for vitalism was a central scientific debate of his era, ultimately resolved by Wöhler's synthesis of urea and subsequent work.
- **Electrochemical dualism**: His theory of bonding was influential but proved insufficient to explain all chemical compounds, leading to its eventual replacement by newer theories of chemical bonding.
- **Prout's hypothesis**: Berzelius's precise atomic weight measurements provided key evidence against the idea that all atomic weights are integer multiples of hydrogen's weight.
