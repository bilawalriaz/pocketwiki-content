# Herbert Spencer

## Overview
Herbert Spencer (1820–1903) was an English polymath whose sweeping intellectual project sought to unify all knowledge under a single evolutionary law. He coined "survival of the fittest" and built a systematic philosophy—his *Synthetic Philosophy*—that applied evolution to biology, psychology, sociology, and ethics, arguing that the universe progresses from simplicity to complexity. His ideas achieved unprecedented popular success in the late 19th century but fell into sharp decline after 1900, though they continue to influence libertarian and conservative thought today.

## Timeline
- **1820** — Born in Derby, England, to a dissenting father influenced by Pestalozzian education
- **1851** — Publishes *Social Statics*, advocating individual liberty and minimal state
- **1855** — Publishes *Principles of Psychology*, grounding mind in biological natural law
- **1857** — Publishes "Progress: Its Law and Cause," articulating his evolutionary worldview
- **1862** — Begins *First Principles*, foundation of his *Synthetic Philosophy*
- **1864** — Publishes *Principles of Biology* and coins "survival of the fittest"
- **1870s–1880s** — Achieves massive international fame; sells over a million copies
- **1884** — Publishes *The Man Versus the State*, critiquing welfare statism
- **1903** — Dies in Brighton; ashes buried in Highgate Cemetery beside Karl Marx

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Spencer was born into a household steeped in dissent and progressive pedagogy. His father, William George Spencer, rejected orthodox Christianity and ran a school based on Pestalozzi's methods. The Derby Philosophical Society, founded by Erasmus Darwin, exposed young Spencer to pre-Darwinian evolutionary ideas. His uncle, the Reverend Thomas Spencer, instilled free-trade and anti-statist views. Largely self-taught, Spencer became an autodidact whose intellectual development was shaped by radical journals and salons rather than formal institutions.

### The Synthetic Philosophy
Beginning in 1858, Spencer embarked on his *System of Synthetic Philosophy*, a ten-volume work intended to demonstrate that a single principle—evolution—governs all phenomena. He posited that the universe develops from simple, undifferentiated homogeneity to complex, differentiated heterogeneity. This law applied equally to cosmic structures, biological organisms, the human mind, and human societies. Unlike Darwin, Spencer saw evolution as directional and teleological, culminating in a final state of equilibrium. He incorporated Lamarckian use-inheritance as the primary mechanism of change, only grudgingly accepting natural selection after reading *On the Origin of Species*.

### Psychology and Ethics
In *Principles of Psychology* (1855), Spencer sought to ground mental phenomena in biological law, reconciling associationist psychology with phrenology. He argued that repeated associations form brain tissue strands passed to offspring via Lamarckian inheritance. His ethical system, developed in *Principles of Ethics*, distinguished between "Absolute Ethics" (the ideal code of non-interference and mutual liberty) and "Relative Ethics" (accounting for current human imperfections). He believed suffering and the natural consequences of action were necessary for moral evolution, opposing state intervention in poverty, education, and vaccination.

### Sociology and Social Theory
Spencer adapted Comte's term "sociology" but rejected positivist ideology, instead applying his evolutionary framework. He classified societies as either "militant" (hierarchical, coercive) or "industrial" (voluntary, contractual), arguing that the latter represents evolutionary advancement. He conceptualized society as an organism evolving toward greater differentiation. Despite popular association with Social Darwinism, his sociological works focused on structural growth rather than competitive elimination. Critics like Lester Frank Ward and Émile Durkheim challenged his laissez-faire assumptions, but his influence on structural functionalism was significant.

### Political Evolution and Later Years
Spencer's early radicalism—advocating women's suffrage, land nationalization, and worker cooperatives—shifted dramatically by the 1880s toward conservatism. *The Man Versus the State* (1884) attacked welfare reform, compulsory education, and imperialism, warning that state intervention would lead to socialism and slavery. He remained consistent in opposing militarism and imperialism, notably criticizing the Boer War. His later years were marked by hypochondria, social isolation, and declining readership. He died in 1903, having been nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature the previous year.

### Legacy and Influence
Spencer was the most famous intellectual of the late Victorian era, selling over a million copies of his works. His ideas permeated literature, influencing writers from George Eliot to H.G. Wells, and shaped political discourse across Europe, America, China, and Japan. In the 20th century, his reputation collapsed—Talcott Parsons asked in 1937, "Who now reads Spencer?"—but libertarian thinkers like Murray Rothbard and Friedrich Hayek revived interest in his theories of spontaneous order and equal liberty. His metaphor of society-as-organism influenced Polish Positivism and continues to inform debates on individual rights and state power.

## Terms
- **Lamarckism**: The theory that acquired characteristics can be inherited, proposed by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck; Spencer adopted this as the mechanism of evolutionary change.
- **Social Darwinism**: A misnomer for Spencer's application of evolutionary principles to sociology; he emphasized cooperation over competition and rejected racial hierarchy in his sociological works.
- **Synthetic Philosophy**: Spencer's ten-volume system attempting to unify all scientific knowledge under the single principle of evolution.
- **Militant Society**: Spencer's term for hierarchical, coercive social organization based on obedience and force.
- **Industrial Society**: Spencer's term for complex, differentiated social organization based on voluntary cooperation and contract.
- **Absolute Ethics**: Spencer's ideal moral code based on non-interference and mutual liberty.
- **Relative Ethics**: Spencer's pragmatic moral code accounting for current human imperfections and social conditions.
- **Survival of the Fittest**: Term coined by Spencer in 1864 to describe natural selection; he used it more broadly than Darwin.
- **Positivism**: Auguste Comte's philosophical system emphasizing empirical science; Spencer adopted its method but rejected its ideology.
- **Use-Inheritance**: The Lamarckian mechanism by which traits acquired through use or lost through disuse are transmitted to offspring.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars continue to debate whether Spencer was truly a Social Darwinist or whether this label misrepresents his actual sociological theory, which focused on structural differentiation rather than competitive elimination. His political evolution—from radical individualist to conservative statist—remains contentious, with some arguing it reflects genuine intellectual development and others seeing opportunistic adaptation. The relationship between his agnosticism and residual deism creates ongoing interpretive challenges. Contemporary libertarians claim him as a predecessor, while critics argue his later conservatism undermines this legacy. His influence on non-Western modernization movements, particularly in China and Japan, raises questions about the global circulation of liberal ideas.
