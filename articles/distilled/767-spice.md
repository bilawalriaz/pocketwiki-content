# Spice

## Overview  
A spice is a dried seed, fruit, root, bark, or other plant substance used primarily for flavoring or coloring food, distinct from herbs (leaves, flowers, stems). Beyond cuisine, spices have served roles in medicine, religious ritual, cosmetics, and perfumery. Their trade shaped global exploration and economic history, particularly from the 15th century onward.

## Timeline  
- **c. 2000 BCE** — Spice trade develops across Indian subcontinent, Middle East, and East Asia
- **1550 BCE** — Ebers Papyrus records Egyptian medicinal use of herbs and spices
- **1st century CE** — Romans possess cloves, documented by Pliny the Elder
- **8th–15th century** — Venice monopolizes European spice trade via Middle Eastern routes
- **1499** — Vasco da Gama reaches India, securing cheaper pepper trade
- **1506–1511** — Portuguese capture key trade hubs: Socotra, Ormuz, Goa, Malacca
- **16th century** — New World introduces allspice, chili peppers, vanilla, and chocolate

## Body  

### Definition and Classification  
Defining “spice” is debated. The Oxford English Dictionary ties it to tropical plant origins, while Redgrove (1933) specifies dried roots, rhizomes, flowers, fruits, seeds, and bark. The Oxford Companion to Food notes exceptions like ambergris (animal-derived). Spices differ from herbs botanically and culinarily. They are classified into spices, spice seeds, and herbal categories. Forms include fresh, whole, dried, ground, grated, or tinctured. Whole dried spices last longer and cost less per serving than fresh ones, though fresh versions often taste stronger.

### Historical Development  
Early spice use is hard to trace archaeologically due to minimal residue. By 2000 BCE, cinnamon and black pepper were traded widely. Ancient Egypt, China, and India left the earliest written records. The Egyptians used spices in cooking and mummification. Spices appear in the Old Testament—e.g., Joseph sold to spice merchants in Genesis. Cloves reached Mesopotamia by 1700 BCE; nutmeg entered Europe by the 6th century BCE.

During the Middle Ages, spices were luxury imports in Europe. Black pepper, cinnamon, nutmeg, ginger, and cloves were highly prized. Medieval humorism drove demand for balancing bodily "humors." European elites viewed spices as symbols of paradise and wealth. Venice dominated trade until the late medieval period. Annual imports included ~1,000 tons of pepper and other spices, valued at the grain supply for 1.5 million people.

In the early modern era, Portuguese and Spanish voyages sought direct access to Asian spice markets. Vasco da Gama’s arrival in India (1499) undercut Venetian prices. The Portuguese captured strategic ports, enabling direct trade with Siam, China, and the Maluku Islands. The Columbian Exchange introduced New World spices like chili peppers, vanilla, and allspice, sustaining the trade into the 19th century.

### Function and Use  
Spices primarily enhance flavor and add variety. They also perfume cosmetics and incense. Historically, many were used medicinally. Due to their expense and rarity, conspicuous consumption signaled social status.

Contrary to popular belief, spices were not effective preservatives. They are less effective than salting, smoking, or drying, and do not mask spoiled meat. Contemporary cookbooks suggest adding spices late in cooking, limiting any preservative effect. Some spices show antimicrobial properties in lab tests, but pepper—the most common—is relatively ineffective.

### Handling and Safety  
Whole spices should be stored dry and ground just before use to preserve volatile oils. Shelf life is about two years for whole, six months for ground. Flavor infuses slowly in water-based dishes, so spices are added early; herbs go in later.

A 2007–2009 FDA study found 7% of U.S. spice imports contaminated with Salmonella, including antibiotic-resistant strains. Shipments from India and Mexico showed highest contamination rates. Cooking usually neutralizes risk, but raw spices like table pepper pose concerns. Irradiation reduces this risk.

### Production and Standardization  
India accounts for 75% of global spice production. The International Organization for Standardization covers spices under ISO 67.220.

## Terms  
- **Humorism**: Medieval medical theory holding that health depends on balancing four bodily fluids ("humors").  
- **Volatile oils**: Aromatic compounds in spices that oxidize or evaporate when exposed to air.  
- **Monsoon winds**: Seasonal trade winds crucial to ancient Indian Ocean navigation.  
- **Columbian Exchange**: Transfer of plants, animals, and goods between Old and New Worlds post-1492.  
- **Tincture**: Alcohol-based extraction of a spice’s active compounds.  
- **Antimicrobial**: Substance capable of killing or inhibiting microorganisms.  
- **Irradiation**: Use of ionizing radiation to sterilize food.  
- **Grains of paradise**: West African pepper substitute once common in medieval European cooking.  
- **Saffron**: Most expensive spice, prized for color and flavor.  
- **Spice rub**: Mixture of ground spices applied to food before cooking.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Whether spices originated exclusively in tropical regions remains debated, given examples like caraway from temperate zones. The extent of their use as preservatives versus flavorings in medieval times is contested, with evidence suggesting limited preservative value. The classification of certain plant parts (e.g., garlic, horseradish) as spices or non-spices lacks consensus. Additionally, the validity of many traditional health claims awaits rigorous clinical testing.
