# Cuba

## Overview
Cuba is the largest island nation in the Caribbean, with a complex history shaped by indigenous settlement, Spanish colonization, and a communist revolution. Its strategic location and political system have made it a focal point of Cold War tensions and ongoing geopolitical disputes, particularly with the United States. The country is known for its unique socialist model, which has achieved notable social indicators like high literacy and healthcare access, but also faces severe economic challenges and political restrictions.

## Timeline
- **c. 4000 BC** — Humans first settle Cuba.
- **1492** — Christopher Columbus arrives and claims the island for Spain.
- **1511** — First Spanish settlement founded at Baracoa.
- **1868** — Carlos Manuel de Céspedes begins the Ten Years' War for independence from Spain.
- **1898** — Spanish–American War ends Spanish rule; U.S. occupies Cuba.
- **1902** — Cuba gains formal independence as a republic, with U.S. oversight.
- **1933** — Fulgencio Batista leads the Sergeants' Revolt, beginning a long period of military influence.
- **1959** — Fidel Castro's 26th of July Movement overthrows Batista's government.
- **1962** — Cuban Missile Crisis brings the world to the brink of nuclear war.
- **1991** — Dissolution of the Soviet Union triggers Cuba's severe "Special Period" economic crisis.
- **2008** — Fidel Castro retires; Raúl Castro becomes president.
- **2021** — Miguel Díaz-Canel becomes First Secretary of the Communist Party, the first non-Castro leader since 1959.

## Body

### Pre-Columbian and Colonial Era
Cuba was inhabited as early as the 4th millennium BC. By the time of Spanish colonization in the 15th century, the Taíno and Guanahatabey peoples were present. Christopher Columbus claimed the island for Spain in 1492. The Spanish established settlements and forced the indigenous population to work under the encomienda system. Within a century, the Taíno population was devastated by disease and harsh conditions. Spain developed Cuba as a colonial outpost, and by the 19th century, the island's economy relied heavily on sugar and tobacco, worked by a large enslaved African population imported through the transatlantic slave trade.

### Independence Movements and Republic
Cuba's struggle for independence from Spain began in 1868 with the Ten Years' War. A second war of independence, led by José Martí, began in 1895. Spanish suppression, including the use of reconcentrados (concentration camps), drew international condemnation. The sinking of the USS Maine in 1898 led to the Spanish–American War, after which Spain ceded Cuba to the United States. Cuba gained formal independence in 1902, but the U.S. retained the right to intervene and leased Guantánamo Bay. The early republic was marked by political instability and U.S. influence. A 1933 coup brought Fulgencio Batista to power, who dominated politics for 25 years. A 1940 constitution introduced progressive reforms, but Batista seized power again in a 1952 coup, establishing an autocratic regime.

### Revolution and Cold War
The Batista government was overthrown in January 1959 by Fidel Castro's 26th of July Movement. The new government nationalized industries and aligned with the Soviet Union, leading to a U.S. embargo. The failed 1961 Bay of Pigs invasion and the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis cemented Cuba's role as a Cold War flashpoint. From the 1970s to the late 1980s, Cuba intervened in conflicts across Africa and Latin America, supported by substantial Soviet aid. The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 caused a severe economic downturn known as the Special Period.

### Contemporary Cuba
Following the Special Period, Cuba sought new allies like China and Venezuela. Fidel Castro retired in 2008, succeeded by his brother Raúl, who implemented limited economic reforms. In 2018, Miguel Díaz-Canel became president, and in 2021, he succeeded Raúl Castro as Communist Party leader. The country remains a one-party socialist state with significant political and economic restrictions. The U.S. embargo, in place since 1960, continues to impact the economy. Cuba faces ongoing challenges, including poverty, food shortages, and emigration, while maintaining its universal healthcare and education systems.

## Terms
- **Encomienda system:** A colonial labor system where Spanish settlers were granted control over indigenous people, who were forced to work.
- **Reconcentrados:** Fortified towns where Spanish forces herded rural Cubans during the independence wars, leading to mass civilian deaths from starvation and disease.
- **Platt Amendment:** A 1901 U.S. law that gave the United States the right to intervene in Cuban affairs and leased Guantánamo Bay.
- **Special Period:** The severe economic crisis in Cuba following the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, which ended billions in annual subsidies.
- **Command economy:** An economic system where the government controls production and prices, as opposed to a market economy.
- **Libreta:** A ration book entitling Cuban households to a monthly supply of basic food and staples at nominal cost.
- **Helms–Burton Act:** A 1996 U.S. law that strengthened and codified the embargo against Cuba.
- **Cuban Missile Crisis:** The 1962 confrontation between the U.S. and Soviet Union over Soviet ballistic missiles deployed in Cuba, considered the closest the world came to nuclear war.

## Debates and Open questions
- The impact and morality of the long-standing U.S. embargo is heavily debated. Critics argue it is a violation of international law and causes humanitarian hardship, while others contend it is a necessary pressure tool for political change and that the Cuban government uses it as a scapegoat for its own economic failures.
- The true racial composition of Cuba is contested. Official census data, based on self-identification, reports a majority white population, but independent researchers argue this significantly under-reports the Black and mixed-race population due to differing racial classification systems.
- The effectiveness and legacy of Cuba's socialist model are debated. While it has achieved high literacy and health outcomes, critics point to persistent poverty, political repression, and economic stagnation as failures of the system.
