# History of psychology

## Overview
Psychology is the scientific study of behavior and mental processes. Its history traces the evolution from philosophical speculation about the mind to an empirical science, marked by competing schools of thought that each emphasized different methods and subjects of study. Understanding this history reveals how psychology established its identity, methods, and diverse subfields.

## Timeline
- **c. 1510–1517** — Marko Marulić likely uses the term "psychologia" in a Latin treatise title.
- **1854** — Gustav Fechner creates the first theory of sensory judgment (Signal Detection Theory) in Leipzig.
- **1879** — Wilhelm Wundt founds the first psychological laboratory in Leipzig, Germany.
- **1890s** — Applied psychology emerges; Sigmund Freud develops psychoanalysis in Vienna.
- **1913** — John B. Watson publishes the behaviorist manifesto, "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It."
- **1912** — Max Wertheimer's article on the phi-phenomenon initiates Gestalt psychology.
- **1920s** — Behaviorism becomes the dominant school in American psychology.
- **1930s** — Humanistic psychology emerges as the "third force" in response to behaviorism and psychoanalysis.
- **1959** — Noam Chomsky's review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* challenges radical behaviorism.
- **1960s–1970s** — The "cognitive revolution" leads to the rise of cognitivism and cognitive science.

## Body

### Early Psychological Thought
Philosophical interest in the mind dates to ancient civilizations. Greek philosophers like Plato and Aristotle developed theories of the psyche, with Plato's tripartite soul anticipating later concepts. In Asia, Chinese texts described neuropsychiatric illnesses, and Indian Vedanta and Buddhist philosophies formulated theories of the self and mind. Medieval Muslim physicians like al-Balkhi and Avicenna discussed disorders of both body and soul. The term "psychology" first appeared in print in 1525, but its modern usage was popularized by Christian Wolff in the 1730s.

### Emergence of Experimental Psychology
Psychology became an experimental science in the 19th century, driven by advances in physiology. Wilhelm Wundt, influenced by Hermann Helmholtz, used physiological lab equipment to study conscious experience. He founded the first psychology lab in Leipzig in 1879. Concurrently, Sigmund Freud in Vienna developed psychoanalysis, using case studies to explore the unconscious mind. In the U.S., William James's 1890 textbook *The Principles of Psychology* was highly influential, and G. Stanley Hall founded the American Psychological Association in 1892.

### Major Schools of Thought
Early 20th-century psychology saw intense theoretical debates. **Structuralism**, led by Edward Titchener, aimed to analyze consciousness into basic elements via introspection. **Functionalism**, associated with James and John Dewey, focused on the purpose of consciousness and behavior, influenced by Darwinian evolution. **Gestalt psychology**, founded by Max Wertheimer, argued that perception is organized into meaningful wholes, not just sums of parts. **Behaviorism**, championed by John B. Watson and later B.F. Skinner, rejected the study of the mind, focusing instead on observable behavior and learning through conditioning.

### The Cognitive Revolution and Modern Psychology
By the mid-20th century, behaviorism's dominance was challenged. Noam Chomsky's critique of Skinner's language theory highlighted the need for internal mental structures. The rise of computer science provided a new metaphor for the mind as an information processor. This led to **cognitivism**, which returned the study of internal mental processes to scientific respectability. Concurrently, **humanistic psychology** (the "third force") emphasized free will and personal growth. Today, psychology is an interdisciplinary field, with **cognitive science** integrating neuroscience, linguistics, and philosophy to study the mind.

## Terms
- **Introspection**: A method of self-observation used by early psychologists like Wundt and Titchener to report conscious experiences.
- **Psychoanalysis**: Sigmund Freud's theory and therapy method focusing on unconscious desires and conflicts as causes of behavior.
- **Behaviorism**: A school of psychology that studies only observable behavior, rejecting the scientific study of internal mental states.
- **Operant Conditioning**: B.F. Skinner's theory that behavior is shaped by its consequences (reinforcement or punishment).
- **Gestalt**: A German term meaning "form" or "configuration"; Gestalt psychology studies how the mind organizes sensory input into meaningful wholes.
- **Cognitivism**: The study of mental processes such as memory, perception, and problem-solving, often using the computer as a model for the mind.
- **Humanistic Psychology**: A perspective emphasizing human potential, free will, and the drive for self-actualization.
- **Cognitive Science**: An interdisciplinary field that studies the mind, drawing on psychology, neuroscience, computer science, philosophy, and linguistics.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source highlights several enduring scholarly debates:
- The **imageless thought controversy** between Wundt and the Würzburg School over whether higher thought could be studied experimentally.
- The **structuralism vs. functionalism** debate over psychology's proper subject matter and methods.
- The **nature vs. nurture** debate, anticipated by thinkers like Ibn Tufail and central to later theories.
- The ongoing debate about the **scientific status of psychoanalysis** and its concepts like the unconscious.
- The question of **how to integrate** the diverse approaches (biological, cognitive, social, clinical) into a unified science of the mind.
