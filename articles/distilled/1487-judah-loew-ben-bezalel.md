# Judah Loew ben Bezalel

## Overview
Judah Loew ben Bezalel (c. 1524–1609), known as the Maharal of Prague, was a towering Talmudic scholar, philosopher, and mystic who served as chief rabbi in Moravia and Bohemia. He constructed a unique philosophical system using rationalist terminology to defend classical rabbinic literature against medieval rationalist critiques, arguing that the apparent disarray of rabbinic texts reflects a divine multiplicity of meaning rather than confusion. His methodology emphasized allegorical interpretation to reconcile rabbinic narratives with history and science, while his ethics stressed intellectual honesty over the pilpul dialectic then popular in yeshivas. Though later folklore credits him with creating the Golem of Prague to defend the ghetto, historians regard this as a 19th-century German literary invention.

## Timeline
- **c. 1524** — Born in Poznań, Poland, to a family originating in Worms; birth year uncertain (sources also cite 1512, 1520, 1526).
- **c. 1536–1553** — Studies in yeshivas in Poland under R. Yaakov Pollak and R. Yitzchak Clover; learns alongside Maharshal and Rema; spends ~20 years in study before marriage.
- **1553** — Appointed Landesrabbiner of Moravia at Mikulov (Nikolsburg); directs communal affairs and Talmud curriculum.
- **1573** — Retires from Moravia; moves to Prague to replace R. Isaac Hayoth as rabbi.
- **23 Feb 1592** — Audience with Emperor Rudolf II (with brother Sinai and son-in-law Isaac Cohen), likely discussing Kabbalah.
- **1592** — Elected Chief Rabbi of Poland; moves to Poznań; composes *Netivoth Olam* and part of *Derech Chaim*.
- **1598** — Publishes *Be'er ha-Golah*, responding to Azariah de Rossi’s historical critiques of rabbinic texts.
- **c. 1600** — Returns to Prague.
- **17 Sep 1609** — Dies in Prague; buried in the Old Jewish Cemetery, Josefov.
- **1834** — Earliest known source for Golem legend appears in Friedrich Korn’s *Der Jüdische Gil Blas*.

## Body

### Name and Identity
Loew’s surname derives from the German *Löwe* (“lion”), a *kinnui* (substitute name) for the Hebrew Judah, referencing Genesis 49:9 (“Judah is a lion’s whelp”). The combined name Judah Loew (Yehudah Lavi) reflects the Jewish custom of pairing Hebrew and vernacular names. His acronym Maharal stands for *Moreinu ha-Rav Loew* (“Our Teacher, Rabbi Loew”). His tomb in Prague bears a heraldic lion with two intertwined tails (*queue fourchee*), alluding to both his name and the Bohemian coat of arms.

### Early Life and Education
Born in Poznań to Rabbi Bezalel (family roots in Worms), Loew’s genealogy was later claimed by Perels (1853) to descend from the Davidic line via Sherira Gaon, though modern scholars (e.g., Otto Muneles) challenge this. Earlier traditions traced his descent through Rashi to Yokhanan the Sandlar (d. 140 CE). His uncle Jakob ben Chajim was *Reichsrabbiner* of the Holy Roman Empire; his elder brother Chaim of Friedberg was a noted scholar. Lubavitch sources state that at age 12 he entered yeshivas in Poland, studying under Yaakov Pollak and later Yitzchak Clover (grandfather of the Maharshal). He studied alongside the Maharshal (two years older) and the Rema (six years younger) for roughly five years total, marrying only after two decades of study.

### Rabbinic Career
In 1553 Loew became Landesrabbiner of Moravia at Mikulov, where he set the Talmud tractate for provincial study and revised communal statutes on elections and taxation. He retired in 1573 but remained a recognized authority. In Moravia he campaigned against *Nadler*—slanderous challenges to family legitimacy that obstructed marriage matches—using a major sermon (1583) to denounce the practice. In 1573 he moved to Prague, succeeding Isaac Hayoth, and immediately reiterated his opposition to *Nadler*. His 1592 audience with Emperor Rudolf II, a known enthusiast of Kabbalah, included his brother Sinai and son-in-law Isaac Cohen. Later that year he accepted election as Chief Rabbi of Poland in Poznań, where he wrote *Netivoth Olam* and part of *Derech Chaim*. He returned to Prague toward the end of his life.

### Philosophical Methodology
Loew reacted against medieval rationalist Jewish thought (exemplified by Maimonides), which privileged systematic abstraction over the vivid, ad-hoc imagery of classical rabbinic commentary. He argued that the multiplicity of opinions in rabbinic literature is not chaotic but reflects the diverse meanings inherent in a single divine concept. He developed a technical vocabulary to map this structure:
- **Seder** (order) and **nivdal** (transcendence): every realm has an internal order but may contain exceptions utterly unlike it in nature.
- **Guf** (body/material), **nefesh** (life-force), **sechel** (intellect): three levels of a single reality. *Guf* is bounded and passive; *nefesh* is unbounded, both active and passive; *sechel* is unbounded and purely active.
- **Pail** (active) and **nifal** (acted upon): relational categories between reality’s levels.
- **Yesodot** (bases), **taarovot** (mixtures), **tarkovot** (combinations): modes of elemental interaction—remaining distinct, blending, or generating a novel entity.
- **Ribui** (multiplicity) and **ahadut** (unity).

Example: a midrash states the world was created for *challah*, *maaser*, and *bikkurim*. Loew reads *bikkurim* (first fruits, given individually) as *yesodot*; *maaser* (tithe, gathered then separated) as *taarovot*; *challah* (dough, a new substance from ingredients) as *tarkovot*.

To resolve tensions between rabbinic narratives and external history, Loew favors allegorization. He interprets stories like Titus and the gnat (*yetosh*) as moral lessons on divine retribution, not literal history. He critiques Azariah de Rossi’s skeptical historicism but avoids rejecting rabbinic texts, instead reinterpreting them to preserve their metaphysical integrity. Though conversant with Kabbalah, he did not espouse it as a system.

### Metaphysics and Ethics
Loew posits a single Cause sustaining all caused phenomena; randomness is impossible, as it would imply a defect in the Cause’s omnipotence/omniscience. This causal uniformity grounds objective moral order: science describes phenomena but cannot rank them; moral preference derives from Torah, the “higher intellect” (*sechel elyon*). He championed intellectual honesty, criticizing the *pilpul* dialectic then dominant in yeshivas as obfuscatory. He advised delaying study of Tosafot until advanced mastery, suggesting that printing Rabbeinu Asher’s halacha-focused commentaries instead would promote practical legal study. Like Judah Halevi, he viewed the Jewish people as essentially spiritual, distinct from all other nations and natural phenomena.

### Disciples and Legacy
Major disciples from his Prague period include **Yom-Tov Lipmann Heller**, who promoted mass Mishnah study and authored the standard Mishnah commentary *Tosefoth Yom Tov*, and **David Gans**, historian/astronomer (author of *Tzemach David*) who corresponded with Tycho Brahe—both Loew and Gans were in contact with Brahe. Loew is commemorated by the moshav Kerem Maharal (Israel), joint Czech-Israeli stamps (1997, 2009), a Czech commemorative coin (2009), and a statue in Prague.

### The Golem Legend
The tale that Loew fashioned a clay *golem* to defend the Prague ghetto from blood libel attacks using esoteric knowledge of Adam’s creation is, per historians, a German literary invention of the early 19th century. The earliest traceable source is Friedrich Korn’s *Der Jüdische Gil Blas* (1834); the story was subsequently repeated and adapted.

### Major Works (Chronological)
Loew began publishing at age 66 (1578). Titles echo I Chronicles 29:11 (“greatness, might, glory, victory, majesty”):
- **Gur Aryeh al HaTorah** (Prague 1578) – five-volume supercommentary on Rashi’s Torah commentary.
- **Gevuroth Hashem** (Cracow 1582) – on Exodus and miracles, for Passover (“might”).
- **Derech Chaim** (Cracow 1589) – commentary on Mishnah *Avot* (“Way of Life”).
- **Derashot** (Prague 1589, 1593) – collected sermons.
- **Netivoth Olam** (Prague 1595–96) – ethical treatise (“Pathways of the World”).
- **Be'er ha-Golah** (Prague 1598) – defends Talmudic/Midrashic Aggadah against Azariah de Rossi (“Well of Exile”).
- **Netzach Yisrael** (Prague 1599) – on Tisha B’Av, exile, and redemption (“Eternity/Victory of Israel”).
- **Tif'ereth Yisrael** (Venice 1599) – philosophical exposition on Torah, for Shavuot (“Glory of Israel”).
- **Or Chadash** (Prague 1600) – on Purim (“New Light”).
- **Ner Mitzvah** (Prague 1600) – on Hanukkah (“Candle of the Commandment”).
- **Chiddushei Aggadot** – novellae on Talmudic narrative, discovered 20th century.
- **Divrei Negidim** – commentary on Passover Seder, published by a descendant.
- **Chiddushim al Ha-Shas** – Talmud commentaries (Bava Metzia, Shabbat, Eruvin) recently published from manuscript; others may follow.
- Lost works: treatises on Sabbath, Sukkot, Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, and the “greatness” (*gedula*) volume for Sabbath.

## Terms
- ****Maharal**** — Acronym for *Moreinu ha-Rav Loew* (“Our Teacher, Rabbi Loew”), the honorific by which Judah Loew ben Bezalel is universally known.
- ****Kinnui**** — A secular/substitute name (here *Löwe*, “lion”) paired with a Hebrew name (Judah) in Jewish naming tradition.
- ****Landesrabbiner**** — “State rabbi” or chief rabbi of a territory (here Moravia), vested with administrative and judicial authority over Jewish communities.
- ****Nadler**** — Slanderous challenges to a family’s marital legitimacy, used to block marriage matches; Loew campaigned against this practice.
- ****Pilpul**** — A sharp, hair-splitting dialectical method of Talmud study popular in 16th-century yeshivas; Loew criticized it as intellectually dishonest.
- ****Seder / Nivdal**** — “Order” and “transcendence”: Loew’s terms for a realm’s internal structure and the exceptions that utterly transcend that structure.
- ****Guf / Nefesh / Sechel**** — “Body,” “life-force,” “intellect”: three hierarchical levels of a single reality, differing in boundedness and agency.
- ****Yesodot / Taarovot / Tarkovot**** — “Bases,” “mixtures,” “combinations”: modes of elemental interaction—remaining distinct, blending, or generating a novel entity.
- ****Allegorization**** — Loew’s hermeneutic for resolving historical contradictions: reading rabbinic narratives as conveying esoteric moral/metaphysical truths rather than literal facts.
- ****Golem**** — In folklore, an artificial humanoid animated by mystical means; the Prague Golem legend attaches to Loew but is deemed a 19th-century literary fiction.

## Debates and open questions
- **Birth year and place**: Sources vary (1512, 1520, 1524, 1526); Poznań is probable, but Perels cites Worms.  
- **Davidic genealogy**: Perels (1853) claims descent via Sherira Gaon; older traditions trace it through Rashi to Yokhanan the Sandlar; modern scholars (Muneles) dispute both.  
- **Relationship to Kabbalah**: Loew was familiar with Kabbalah and discussed it with Rudolf II, yet his writings avoid kabbalistic terminology and he “did not espouse kabbalah or other Jewish mystical traditions.” The extent of his engagement remains debated.  
- **Historicity of the Golem legend**: Universally rejected by historians as a post-1834 German literary invention; no contemporary or near-contemporary source mentions it.  
- **Attribution of late-discovered manuscripts**: *Chiddushei Aggadot* (20th-century discovery) and *Chiddushim al Ha-Shas* (recent manuscript publications) are ascribed to Loew, but their authenticity and completeness are subjects of ongoing scholarly verification.
