# Fred Astaire

## Overview
Fred Astaire (1899–1987) was an American dancer, actor, singer, and choreographer whose 76-year career defined the art of dance on film. Widely regarded as the greatest popular-music dancer of all time, he revolutionized the Hollywood musical by insisting on full-figure camerawork that captured dance in long takes, and by integrating routines into the narrative rather than treating them as spectacle. His partnership with Ginger Rogers in ten RKO films (1933–1949) became the gold standard for screen dance duos, while his later MGM work with partners like Cyd Charisse and Audrey Hepburn extended his influence. A perfectionist who termed his eclectic blend of tap, ballroom, and ballet "outlaw style," Astaire also introduced dozens of Great American Songbook standards, earning acclaim from composers like Irving Berlin and Jerome Kern as the supreme male interpreter of their work.

## Body

### Early Life and Vaudeville Foundations
Born in Omaha to an Austrian immigrant father and a German-American mother, Astaire entered show business at his mother's urging to escape provincial life. After the family moved to New York in 1905, he and sister Adele trained at the Alvieni Master School and debuted a vaudeville act in November 1905. A two-year hiatus (1909–1911) allowed Fred to catch up in height to Adele. Upon returning, they incorporated ballroom dances learned from Aurelio Coccia, influenced by Vernon and Irene Castle. By age 14, Fred managed the act's music and choreography. A pivotal 1916 meeting with song-plugger George Gershwin sparked a creative relationship that shaped both careers.

### Broadway and London Stardom (1917–1932)
The Astaires broke into Broadway in 1917 with *Over the Top*. Critics quickly singled out Fred's technique; Heywood Broun noted he "stood out" even alongside Adele's intuitive brilliance. Fred's perfectionism complemented Adele's instinct, though he felt artistically shadowed by her. They triumphed in Gershwin's *Lady, Be Good* (1924) and *Funny Face* (1927), and in *The Band Wagon* (1931). In London, Fred studied piano at Guildhall with Noël Coward and judged the 1926 Charleston Championship. A 1932 Paramount screen test failed, but the partnership ended when Adele married Lord Charles Cavendish. Fred's solo debut in *Gay Divorce* (1932) with Claire Luce produced the romantic "Night and Day" routine, which Luce recalled required her to remind him, "I'm not your sister." This number became the template for his film dance style.

### The RKO Years and the Rogers Partnership (1933–1939)
Despite a discouraging screen test report ("Can't act. Slightly bald. Also dances."), RKO's David O. Selznick signed Astaire, captivated by his charm. Loaned to MGM for *Dancing Lady* (1933), he returned to RKO for *Flying Down to Rio*, where fifth-billed Ginger Rogers stole scenes. Initially resistant to another fixed partnership—"I've just managed to live down one partnership"—Astaire accepted after producer Pandro Berman offered 10% of the series' profits. They made nine films at RKO, six becoming the studio's top grossers. Katharine Hepburn summarized their alchemy: "He gives her class and she gives him sex."

**Innovations in Film Dance.** Astaire secured two revolutionary controls: (1) **Camera autonomy**—a tracking dolly filmed routines in 4–8 cuts, keeping dancers full-figure throughout, creating the illusion of a single take. "Either the camera will dance, or I will," he declared, maintaining this from *The Gay Divorcee* (1934) to *Finian's Rainbow* (1968). This contrasted sharply with Busby Berkeley's fragmented, aerial-kaleidoscope style. (2) **Narrative integration**—every routine advanced the plot. A typical Astaire film contained three dance types: a solo "sock solo," a partnered comedy number, and a partnered romantic duet.

**Assessment of Rogers.** Dance historians (Croce, Hyam, Mueller) and collaborators (Hermes Pan, Stanley Donen) rank Rogers as Astaire's greatest partner—not for technical superiority, but because her acting continued through dance, conveying the thrill of dancing with him. Astaire acknowledged she "faked it an awful lot" initially but improved rapidly, adding, "She deserves most of the credit for our success." Rogers described his relentless perfectionism: late-night calls with new ideas, "no loafing on the job." Despite success, Astaire resisted exclusivity, attempting a solo vehicle *A Damsel in Distress* (1937) with non-dancer Joan Fontaine (a flop). The final two RKO Rogers films (*Carefree*, *The Story of Vernon and Irene Castle*) lost money due to rising costs, and the Independent Theatre Owners of America labeled him "box office poison," prompting his 1939 departure.

### Freelance and Columbia Years (1939–1947)
Astaire freelanced widely. With Eleanor Powell in *Broadway Melody of 1940*, he performed the celebrated "Begin the Beguine" routine; he praised her masculine attack: "She 'put 'em down' like a man." At Columbia, Harry Cohn paired him with Rita Hayworth in *You'll Never Get Rich* (1941) and *You Were Never Lovelier* (1942), launching her stardom. *Holiday Inn* (1942) with Bing Crosby featured his virtuoso "Let's Say it with Firecrackers" solo. *The Sky's the Limit* (1943) at RKO introduced Johnny Mercer's "One for My Baby" in a dark, bar-counter solo—a departure from his debonair persona that confused critics. At MGM, Vincente Minnelli directed him in *Ziegfeld Follies* (1944/46), where he danced "The Babbit and the Bromide" with Gene Kelly (a song debuted with Adele in 1927), and the surreal *Yolanda and the Thief* (1945), both financial failures. Convinced his career was fading, Astaire retired during *Blue Skies* (1946), staging "Puttin' On the Ritz" as a farewell, and founded his dance studios.

### MGM Golden Era and Second Retirement (1948–1957)
MGM lured him back to replace an injured Gene Kelly in *Easter Parade* (1948) with Judy Garland, reviving his popularity. *The Barkleys of Broadway* (1949) reunited him with Rogers in Technicolor. *Royal Wedding* (1951) featured the rotating-room "ceiling dance." *The Band Wagon* (1953) earned rave reviews but lost money initially. MGM dismissed most stars amid television's rise. At 20th Century-Fox, *Daddy Long Legs* (1955) proceeded despite wife Phyllis's terminal cancer; Johnny Mercer and studio heads convinced Astaire to continue. *Funny Face* (1957) with Audrey Hepburn and *Silk Stockings* (1957) with Cyd Charisse both lost money. Astaire announced his second retirement from film dancing, leaving a legacy of 30 musicals in 25 years.

### Television, Dramatic Roles, and Final Years (1957–1987)
Astaire shifted to television, producing four Emmy-winning specials (1958, 1959, 1960, 1968) with Barrie Chase. The first, *An Evening with Fred Astaire*, won nine Emmys and was the first major prerecorded color videotape broadcast; its 1988 restoration won a technical Emmy. He pursued dramatic roles: *On the Beach* (1959, Golden Globe nomination), *The Towering Inferno* (1974, sole competitive Oscar nomination, plus Golden Globe and BAFTA wins), and the cult *The Amazing Dobermans* (1976). He voiced the mailman in Rankin/Bass specials (*Santa Claus Is Comin' to Town*), appeared in *That's Entertainment!* compilations, and danced a final screen duet with Kelly in *That's Entertainment, Part II* (1976) at age 76. His last on-screen dance was a 1979 *Battlestar Galactica* episode written for him by Donald P. Bellisario. His final film was the horror *Ghost Story* (1981). He died of pneumonia in 1987 at 88.

### Style and Influence
Astaire's "outlaw style" fused tap, classical dance, and the Castles' elevated ballroom into an unpredictable, instinctive blend. His technical control was astonishing: a 1994 split-screen comparison of two takes of "I Wanna Be a Dancin' Man" (filmed months apart for *The Belle of New York*) showed frame-for-frame identity. Jerome Robbins marveled at the "surprising and inventive" understructure beneath the apparent simplicity. Astaire viewed choreography as musical composition: "If the dance is right, there shouldn't be a single superfluous movement. It should build to a climax and stop!" He was primary choreographer on all routines, though he valued collaborators like Hermes Pan; dance historian John Mueller argues Astaire's style remained consistent with or without Pan. Routines were built around "gimmicks" (dancing on walls in *Royal Wedding*, with shadows in *Swing Time*), developed in weeks of secluded rehearsal with a pianist (often Hal Borne) before filming. His perfectionism was legendary—two weeks of rehearsal before shooting, agonizing over rushes, confessing "I've never yet got anything 100% right." Co-choreographer Michael Kidd noted Astaire prioritized technique over emotional motivation: "Let's do the steps. Let's add the looks later." Astaire cited the Nicholas Brothers as his tap heroes.

### Influence on Popular Song
Though modest about his light voice, Astaire was the premier introducer of Great American Songbook standards. Composers revered his interpretive gifts: Irving Berlin called him "as good as Jolson, Crosby or Sinatra... for his conception of projecting a song"; Jerome Kern deemed him the supreme male interpreter; George Gershwin wrote many songs for him despite criticizing his singing. Astaire introduced Porter's "Night and Day" (*Gay Divorce*, 1932), Berlin's "Cheek to Cheek" and "Top Hat, White Tie and Tails" (*Top Hat*, 1935), Kern's "The Way You Look Tonight" (*Swing Time*, 1936), Gershwin's "They Can't Take That Away from Me" (*Shall We Dance*, 1937), Mercer's "One for My Baby" (*The Sky's the Limit*, 1943), and Warren/Freed's "This Heart of Mine" (*Ziegfeld Follies*, 1946), among dozens. He also co-introduced standards with Adele (Gershwin's "Fascinating Rhythm," 1924), Rogers (Kern's "Pick Yourself Up," 1936), Garland (Berlin's "A Couple of Swells," 1948), and the *Band Wagon* ensemble ("That's Entertainment!," 1953). A songwriter himself, his "I'm Building Up to an Awful Letdown" (with Mercer) hit #4 in 1936. *The Astaire Story* (1952, with Oscar Peterson) won a 1999 Grammy Hall of Fame Award.

### Personal Life
Astaire married Phyllis Potter in 1933 over family objections; her 1954 death from lung cancer devastated him. They had two children (Fred Jr., 1936; Ava, 1942) plus her son Peter. At 81, he married jockey Robyn Smith (45 years his junior). Intensely private, he avoided Hollywood nightlife, preferring horse racing (his horse Triplicate won the 1946 Hollywood Gold Cup), golf, drums, and songwriting. Friends included David Niven, Clark Gable, and Gregory Peck. A Republican and charter member of the Hollywood Republican Committee, he remained a fashion icon, favoring casual sport jackets and silk-scarf belts over his hated formal tails. He took up skateboarding in his late 70s, earning a National Skateboard Society life membership, and broke his wrist at 78. He refused all biopic permissions, embedding a clause in his will against portrayal; nonetheless, Tom Holland was announced for a 2021 biopic directed by Paul King.

## Terms
- **Outlaw style**: Astaire's term for his eclectic, instinctive blending of tap, classical dance, and ballroom into an unpredictable personal idiom.
- **Sock solo**: Astaire's name for his signature solo dance showcase in each film.
- **Tracking dolly shot / Full-figure camerawork**: Astaire's mandated filming technique—camera follows dancers on a dolly in long takes (4–8 cuts), keeping head-to-toe in frame to simulate a continuous take.
- **Narrative integration**: Astaire's principle that all song-and-dance routines must advance the plot, not serve as standalone spectacle.
- **Great American Songbook**: The canon of enduring American popular songs and jazz standards (1920s–1950s), many introduced by Astaire.
- **Busby Berkeley musicals**: Contrasting 1930s Warner Bros. musicals featuring massive chorus geometries, aerial shots, rapid editing, and body-part close-ups—antithetical to Astaire's style.
- **Hermes Pan**: Astaire's principal choreographic collaborator at RKO and MGM; credited as co-choreographer but viewed by historian John Mueller as executing Astaire's lead vision.
- **Gerry Society**: New York Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children; its child-labor enforcement prompted the Astaires' 1909–1911 hiatus.
- **American Smooth**: Ballroom dance style (Waltz, Tango, Foxtrot, Viennese Waltz) allowing open positions and underarm turns; heavily shaped by Astaire's screen aesthetic.
- **Color videotape broadcast**: *An Evening with Fred Astaire* (1958) was the first major network program prerecorded on color videotape rather than kinescope film.

## Debates and open questions
- **Screen test legend**: The exact wording of the lost 1933 RKO report is disputed—folklore claims "Can't sing. Can't act. Balding. Can dance a little"; Astaire insisted it read "Can't act. Slightly bald. Also dances." Producer Pandro Berman denied the story existed in the 1930s.
- **Rogers's technical parity**: Critics debate whether Rogers was technically Astaire's equal. The consensus (Mueller, Croce, Pan, Donen) holds her greatness lay in acting-through-dance, not technique; Pauline Kael is more neutral; Richard Schickel argues nostalgia obscures other partners.
- **Choreographic authorship**: While Astaire credited collaborators, John Mueller argues Astaire was lead choreographer on all solos and partnered dances throughout his career, citing stylistic consistency with or without Hermes Pan.
- **Singing ability**: Astaire claimed he "could not sing," yet Berlin, Kern, and Burton Lane ("world's greatest musical performer") ranked him among the finest interpreters. George Gershwin was critical but wrote for him extensively.
- **Biopic prohibition**: Astaire's will forbade portrayal of his life, yet his estate sold film rights to his autobiography *Steps in Time* to Sony, enabling a 2021 announced biopic with Tom Holland—raising questions about estate control vs. artist intent.
