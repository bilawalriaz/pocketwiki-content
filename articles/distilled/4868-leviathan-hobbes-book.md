# Leviathan (Hobbes book)

## Overview
Thomas Hobbes's *Leviathan* (1651) is a foundational work of political philosophy that argues for an absolute sovereign as the only solution to the destructive "state of nature." Written during the English Civil War, it uses a mechanistic view of human nature to justify a social contract where individuals surrender their rights to a single authority in exchange for peace and security.

## Timeline
- **1642–1651** — English Civil War provides the immediate context for Hobbes's writing.
- **1651** — *Leviathan* is first published in English.
- **1668** — A revised Latin edition is published.

## Body

### The State of Nature and Social Contract
Hobbes begins with a materialist view of humans as complex machines driven by appetites and aversions. Without a common power to keep them in awe, humans exist in a "state of nature"—a "war of all against all." This condition is not necessarily constant fighting but a constant readiness for conflict, making life "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." The greatest evil (*summum malum*) is the fear of violent death. To escape this, rational individuals agree to a social contract, surrendering their natural rights to a sovereign authority in exchange for protection and order.

### The Commonwealth and the Sovereign
The commonwealth is an "artificial man" created by this contract. The sovereign, whether a monarch or an assembly, holds absolute and indivisible power. Its primary purpose is to enforce the covenant and ensure peace. The sovereign's rights include controlling doctrine, making laws, judging disputes, waging war, and appointing officials. Subjects cannot lawfully rebel, as the sovereign is the author of all their actions and cannot breach the covenant. Hobbes argues monarchy is the most practical form, as the monarch's private interest aligns with the public good.

### Religion and the Sovereign's Authority
Hobbes asserts that the civil sovereign must have supreme authority over religious matters to prevent discord from competing claims of divine revelation. He argues that miracles have ceased, so the only reliable guide is Scripture, whose authority as law can only be established by the sovereign. Therefore, Christian kings are the "supreme pastors" of their people, with power to ordain teachers and interpret doctrine. This subordinates the church to the state.

### The Kingdom of Darkness
Hobbes identifies the "kingdom of darkness" not as Hell, but as ignorance spread by deceivers to gain power. He attributes this to four causes: misinterpreting Scripture to undermine civil authority, accepting pagan demonology, mixing Scripture with erroneous Greek philosophy (especially Aristotle's), and relying on false traditions. He concludes that the beneficiaries of this darkness are the churches and churchmen.

## Terms
*   **Leviathan:** The metaphorical "mortal god" or absolute sovereign created by the social contract to maintain order.
*   **State of Nature:** The hypothetical condition of humanity without government, characterized by war and insecurity.
*   **Social Contract:** The agreement by which individuals surrender their natural rights to a sovereign in exchange for security.
*   **Sovereign:** The supreme authority in a commonwealth, holding absolute and indivisible power.
*   **Commonwealth:** The political entity formed by the social contract; an "artificial man."
*   **Summum Malum:** The "greatest evil," which Hobbes identifies as the fear of violent death.
*   **Laws of Nature:** Principles of reason that suggest seeking peace and forming a commonwealth, but which are not enforceable without a sovereign.
*   **Corpuscularianism:** Hobbes's materialist philosophy that everything, including the mind, is composed of physical matter in motion.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **The Nature of the Sovereign:** Is the sovereign truly bound by the social contract, or is the contract only between subjects? Hobbes argues the sovereign cannot breach it, as they are not a party to it.
*   **Right of Revolution:** Hobbes denies subjects any right to rebel, even if the sovereign fails to protect them. This remains a central point of critique.
*   **Religious Interpretation:** Hobbes's argument that the sovereign determines religious doctrine was seen as highly controversial and potentially atheistic, as it subordinated divine law to civil law.
*   **Monarchy vs. Other Forms:** While Hobbes argues for monarchy's practical superiority, his logic for absolute sovereignty applies to any undivided authority, whether monarchic, aristocratic, or democratic.
