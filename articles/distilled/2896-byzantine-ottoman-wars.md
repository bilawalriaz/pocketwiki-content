# Byzantine–Ottoman wars

## Overview
The Byzantine–Ottoman wars were a series of conflicts from the 13th to 15th centuries that culminated in the fall of Constantinople in 1453. This event ended the Byzantine Empire, the last remnant of the Roman Empire, and established the Ottoman Empire as a dominant power in the Eastern Mediterranean, marking a pivotal transition from the Middle Ages to the early modern period.

## Timeline
- **1261** — Byzantines recapture Constantinople from the Latin Empire.
- **c. 1265–1289** — Ottomans under Osman I begin raiding Byzantine Anatolia, taking Söğüt and Eskişehir.
- **1302** — Byzantine defeat at Bapheus; Ottomans lay siege to Nicaea.
- **1326** — Ottomans capture Bursa.
- **1329** — Byzantine relief army defeated at Pelekanos; Nicaea falls in 1331.
- **1354** — Ottomans capture Gallipoli, gaining a European foothold.
- **1371** — Ottomans defeat Serbs at Battle of Maritsa, securing Balkan dominance.
- **1389** — Ottomans defeat Serbs at Battle of Kosovo.
- **1390** — Ottomans seize Philadelphia, ending Byzantine rule in Anatolia.
- **1394–1402** — Ottoman siege of Constantinople, broken by Timur's invasion.
- **1422** — Ottoman siege of Constantinople and Thessalonika.
- **1453** — Fall of Constantinople to Sultan Mehmed II.

## Body

### Rise of the Ottomans (1265–1328)
After retaking Constantinople in 1261, the Byzantine Empire was weakened and faced threats from Latins, Serbs, and Bulgarians. Emperor Michael VIII Palaiologos focused on the west, allowing Turkic warriors (ghazis) to raid Anatolia. The decay of the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum created instability, enabling the beylik of Osman I to expand. Byzantine attempts to counter this, including hiring the Catalan Company, failed due to internal strife. A civil war (1321–1328) further weakened the empire, allowing the Ottomans to capture Bursa in 1326.

### Byzantine Counter and Balkan Invasion (1328–1371)
Emperor Andronikos III won the civil war but could not halt Ottoman advances. The Byzantine defeat at Pelekanos (1329) led to the fall of Nicaea (1331) and Nicomedia (1337). While Andronikos III achieved some successes in Greece and the Aegean, these were overshadowed by territorial losses. His death in 1341 triggered a devastating civil war (1341–1347) between regents, which the Ottomans exploited. By 1354, the Ottomans captured Gallipoli, entering Europe. Further civil conflict and the Ottoman victory at Maritsa (1371) reduced Byzantium to a vassal state paying tribute.

### Vassalage and Resumed Hostilities (1371–1424)
Byzantium became an Ottoman vassal, a status that continued until 1394. Ottoman expansion in the Balkans continued, subjugating Serbia and Bulgaria. Byzantine civil wars between John V and his sons further eroded imperial power. In 1390, the Ottomans took Philadelphia, ending Byzantine rule in Anatolia. War resumed in 1394 when Sultan Bayezid I besieged Constantinople. The siege was lifted by Timur's invasion in 1402, which caused an Ottoman civil war. The Byzantines temporarily recovered territory, but the rise of Murad II led to renewed conflict and the imposition of heavy tribute after 1422.

### Ottoman Victory (1424–1453)
The final decades saw the Ottomans consolidate power despite European resistance at the Crusades of Varna (1444) and Kosovo (1448). Sultan Mehmed II "the Conqueror" and Emperor Constantine XI Palaiologos were antagonistic. Mehmed built a fortress on the Bosporus to blockade Constantinople. The siege began on April 6, 1453. The city was defended by a small force, including about 2,000 foreign mercenaries, against an Ottoman army outnumbering them more than ten to one. Despite a union of the Catholic and Orthodox Churches, significant Western aid did not materialize. The city fell on May 29, 1453. The Ottomans subsequently conquered the remaining Byzantine territories: the Morea (1460), Trebizond (1461), and finally Epirus (1479).

### Causes of Byzantine Defeat
The Byzantines were undermined by constant civil wars, crushing taxes that alienated the Anatolian peasantry, and the strategic dilemma of fighting on multiple fronts against Latins and Turks. The Latin presence after the Fourth Crusade fragmented the region. In contrast, the Ottomans benefited from a unified command, a system of vassalage that conserved resources, and the support of local populations weary of Byzantine taxation. They also mastered siege warfare and exploited the disunity of their Christian opponents.

## Terms
*   **Ghazi:** A warrior engaged in raiding or warfare against non-Muslims, often motivated by religious zeal and the prospect of booty.
*   **Beylik:** A Turkish principality or state, especially one of the Anatolian states that emerged after the decline of the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum.
*   **Pronoia:** A Byzantine system of land grants in exchange for military service, similar to a fief.
*   **Janissary:** An elite infantry unit in the Ottoman army, originally composed of conscripted Christian boys.
*   **Hyperpyron:** The standard gold coin of the later Byzantine Empire.
*   **Vassalage:** A political relationship where a weaker state (vassal) pledges loyalty and tribute to a stronger state (suzerain) in exchange for protection.
*   **Crusade of Varna:** A 1444 military campaign by a coalition of Christian forces against the Ottomans, ending in a decisive Ottoman victory.
*   **Interregnum:** A period of time when a normal line of succession to a throne is interrupted, as occurred in the Ottoman Empire after Timur's invasion in 1402.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The degree to which the Byzantine civil wars (especially 1341–1371) were the primary cause of the empire's collapse versus other factors like Ottoman military superiority or Latin interference.
*   The extent to which the failure of Western Europe to provide substantial military aid was due to political disunity, religious schism, or competing priorities like the Hundred Years' War.
*   The role of the Anatolian peasantry's perceived preference for Ottoman rule over Byzantine taxation in facilitating the early Ottoman conquests.
