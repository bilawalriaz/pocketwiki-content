# Otto Hahn

## Overview
Otto Hahn (1879–1968) was a German chemist and pioneer of radiochemistry, widely regarded as the father of nuclear chemistry. His most famous achievement was the 1938 discovery of nuclear fission, the process that makes nuclear reactors and weapons possible. His work fundamentally changed physics and chemistry, leading to the atomic age and profound ethical debates about the use of scientific discoveries.

## Timeline
- **8 March 1879** — Born in Frankfurt am Main, Germany.
- **1901** — Receives doctorate in organic chemistry from Marburg University.
- **1905** — Discovers radiothorium (thorium-228) while working in London.
- **1906** — Returns to Berlin; begins collaboration with Lise Meitner.
- **1912** — Becomes head of the Radioactivity Department at the new Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Chemistry (KWIC).
- **1918** — With Meitner, isolates the longest-lived isotope of protactinium.
- **1921** — Discovers nuclear isomerism with "uranium Z" (protactinium-234).
- **1938** — With Strassmann, identifies barium as a product of uranium bombardment, the key evidence for nuclear fission.
- **1939** — Meitner and Frisch provide the theoretical explanation for fission; Hahn and Strassmann publish the term "Uranspaltung."
- **1944** — Awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry alone for the discovery of fission.
- **1945–46** — Detained at Farm Hall, England, with other German nuclear scientists.
- **1948** — Becomes founding president of the Max Planck Society.
- **1957** — Co-authors the Göttingen Manifesto against nuclear armament for West Germany.
- **28 July 1968** — Dies in Göttingen, Germany.

## Body

### Early Career and Collaborations
After his doctorate, Hahn shifted from organic chemistry to the new field of radiochemistry. Working in London and Montreal, he discovered several new radioactive substances, including radiothorium. Returning to Berlin in 1906, he was given a basement workshop as a lab. In 1907, he began a lifelong collaboration with Austrian physicist Lise Meitner. Together, they made groundbreaking discoveries, including the isolation of protactinium in 1918 and the phenomenon of radioactive recoil.

### World War I and the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute
Hahn served in World War I, earning the Iron Cross and later working in Fritz Haber's chemical warfare unit. After the war, he became head of the KWIC. In the 1920s, he founded "applied radiochemistry," using radioactive tracers to study chemical processes, a field that became the basis for nuclear chemistry.

### The Road to Nuclear Fission
In the 1930s, Hahn, Meitner, and Fritz Strassmann bombarded uranium with neutrons, initially believing they had created transuranium elements. After Meitner fled Nazi Germany in 1938, Hahn and Strassmann continued the work. In December 1938, their experiments showed that uranium, when bombarded, produced barium—a much lighter element. Hahn, a chemist, was reluctant to propose a physical explanation. In early 1939, Meitner and her nephew Otto Frisch provided the theoretical interpretation, coining the term "nuclear fission." Hahn and Strassmann then predicted the release of neutrons, opening the possibility of a chain reaction.

### Nazi Era and World War II
Hahn opposed Nazism and the persecution of Jews, helping colleagues like Meitner and Strassmann. However, during the war, he worked on the German nuclear weapons program, cataloguing fission products. He was arrested by Allied forces in 1945 and detained at Farm Hall, where he learned of the atomic bombings of Japan.

### Post-War Leadership and Advocacy
After the war, Hahn became a leading figure in rebuilding German science. He was the last president of the Kaiser Wilhelm Society and the first president of its successor, the Max Planck Society (1948–1960). He became a prominent advocate for the peaceful use of atomic energy and against nuclear weapons, co-founding the Mainau Declaration (1955) and the Göttingen Manifesto (1957).

## Terms
- **Radiochemistry:** The study of radioactive substances and their chemical behavior.
- **Isotope:** Variants of a particular chemical element which differ in neutron number, and consequently in nucleon number. All isotopes of a given element have the same number of protons.
- **Nuclear Fission:** A nuclear reaction in which the nucleus of an atom splits into two or more smaller nuclei, along with free neutrons and photons, releasing a large amount of energy.
- **Protactinium (Pa):** A radioactive, silvery-gray metal which is one of the rarest and most expensive natural elements.
- **Nuclear Isomerism:** A phenomenon where an atomic nucleus exists in an excited state for a measurable period, longer than typical nuclear excited states.
- **Transuranium Elements:** Chemical elements with atomic numbers greater than 92 (uranium).
- **Kaiser Wilhelm Society (KWS):** A German scientific organization founded in 1911, renamed the Max Planck Society in 1948.
- **Mainau Declaration (1955):** A statement by Nobel laureates warning of the dangers of nuclear weapons.
- **Göttingen Manifesto (1957):** A public protest by 18 German scientists against the proposed nuclear armament of West Germany's military.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nobel Prize Attribution:** Hahn was awarded the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry alone for the discovery of fission. The exclusion of Lise Meitner and Fritz Strassmann is widely debated, with arguments citing sexism, antisemitism, and the Nobel Committee's traditional separation of chemistry and physics.
- **Hahn's Role During Nazism:** While Hahn opposed Nazi policies and helped Jewish colleagues, his political passivity and post-war efforts to rehabilitate the reputations of scientists with Nazi ties (issuing "whitewash certificates") have been criticized as morally compromised.
- **Scientific Credit:** The discovery of fission involved a complex interplay between Hahn's chemical experiments and Meitner's physical interpretation. Determining the precise division of credit remains a topic of historical analysis.
