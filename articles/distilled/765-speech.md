# Speech

## Overview
Speech is the human use of vocal sounds to convey language, combining vowels and consonants into meaningful units like words. It serves both intentional functions (informing, persuading, directing) and unintentional ones (revealing social traits such as age, sex, or education), and can occur solo—for memory, prayer, or self-cognition, as proposed by Lev Vygotsky. The study of speech spans linguistics, psychology, cognitive science, and medicine, and intersects with debates about its evolutionary origins, neural underpinnings, and developmental trajectory.

## Timeline
- **c. 1861** – Paul Broca identifies brain region linked to speech production; damage causes expressive aphasia.
- **1874** – Carl Wernicke links posterior left superior temporal gyrus damage to receptive aphasia.
- **1995** – Masur finds correlation between novel word repetition in children and later vocabulary size.
- **Modern era** – Neuroimaging reveals multiple brain streams involved in speech, beyond Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas.

## Body

### Production
Speech production is an unconscious, multi-step process: thoughts become words selected from the lexicon, arranged via syntax, then articulated using phonetic properties. Articulatory phonetics studies how the tongue, lips, jaw, and vocal cords create sounds, categorized by manner and place of articulation. Normal speech uses pulmonic airflow from the lungs, generating phonation in the larynx, shaped by the vocal tract. Alaryngeal speech—esophageal, pharyngeal, or buccal (Donald Duck talk)—bypasses lungs and glottis. Errors, especially in children (e.g., over-regularizing “-ed”), reveal acquisition patterns and brain-language mappings; aphasic patients’ struggles with regular vs. irregular forms show that inflected words are generated, not stored.

### Perception
Speech perception involves interpreting spoken sounds, drawing on phonetics, phonology, and cognitive psychology. It is categorical: listeners group sounds into discrete categories rather than perceiving continuous spectra. Voice onset time (VOT) exemplifies this—Hebrew speakers detect shifts across categorical boundaries (e.g., /b/ to /p/) more readily than equal changes within a category. Applications include speech-recognition technology and aids for hearing-impaired individuals.

### Development
Children begin proto-speech babbling around four to six months, say first words by age one, use two-to-three-word phrases by three, and form short sentences by four. Speech repetition—mapping heard input to motor output via phonological memory—is crucial for vocabulary growth. Masur (1995) showed that children who repeat more novel words develop larger lexicons.

### Problems and Treatment
Speech disorders stem from organic or psychological factors: lung/vocal cord diseases, brain injuries (e.g., aphasias, dysarthria), hearing loss, articulatory issues (stuttering, cleft palate), or psychiatric conditions affecting vocal features (e.g., lowered pitch in depression). Speech-language pathologists (SLPs) assess, diagnose, and treat these conditions.

### Brain Physiology
The classical Wernicke-Geschwind model locates language in Broca’s area (inferior prefrontal cortex) and Wernicke’s area (posterior superior temporal gyrus), connected by the arcuate fasciculus. Auditory input reaches Wernicke’s for lexical access, then moves to Broca’s for syntax and articulation planning, finally to the motor cortex. Broca’s aphasia causes slow, effortful speech with poor syntax; Wernicke’s aphasia yields fluent but meaningless speech and poor comprehension. Modern models expand beyond these regions and the left hemisphere, recognizing dynamic, learning-adapted neural circuits.

### Animal Communication
Some animals produce sounds or gestures resembling human language, but none exhibit full grammar, syntax, recursion, or displacement. Trained apes like Washoe and Kanzi use signs, yet whether this constitutes true language remains debated.

## Terms
- **Lexicon**: The mental inventory of words and word forms in a language.
- **Manner of articulation**: How speech organs interact to shape airflow (e.g., stop, fricative).
- **Place of articulation**: Where airflow is constricted in the vocal tract (e.g., labial, alveolar).
- **Pulmonic speech**: Normal speech produced using lung-generated airflow.
- **Alaryngeal speech**: Speech produced without lungs or glottis, e.g., esophageal or pharyngeal speech.
- **Categorical perception**: Perceiving sounds as belonging to distinct categories rather than a continuum.
- **Voice onset time (VOT)**: Timing of vocal cord vibration relative to consonant release; key to distinguishing sounds like /b/ and /p/.
- **Aphasia**: Language disorder from brain damage; Broca’s affects production, Wernicke’s affects comprehension.
- **Arcuate fasciculus**: Nerve bundle connecting Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas.
- **Diglossia**: Coexistence of distinct spoken and written language varieties.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origin of speech**: Whether human speech evolved uniquely from primate vocalizations, given that no animal uses tongue and lips phonomically.
- **FOXP2 gene**: Its role in speech evolution is noted but not detailed here.
- **Animal language**: Whether trained apes’ sign use constitutes genuine language or sophisticated imitation.
- **Neuroplasticity**: How brain circuits for speech adapt dynamically with learning and experience.
