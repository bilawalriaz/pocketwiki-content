# Gustav Kirchhoff

## Overview
Gustav Robert Kirchhoff (1824–1887) was a German physicist and mathematician whose work laid foundational principles for electrical circuit theory, spectroscopy, and thermal radiation. His laws are fundamental to electrical engineering and physics, and his spectroscopic work with Robert Bunsen pioneered the chemical analysis of stars, leading to the discovery of new elements.

## Timeline
- **12 March 1824** — Born in Königsberg, Prussia.
- **1845** — Formulated his two circuit laws as a seminar exercise.
- **1847** — Graduated from the University of Königsberg; became a Privatdozent at the University of Berlin.
- **1850** — Offered a professorship at the University of Breslau.
- **1854** — Called to the University of Heidelberg; began collaboration with Robert Bunsen.
- **1857** — Calculated that an electric signal travels at the speed of light in a resistanceless wire.
- **1858** — Published his law of thermochemistry.
- **1859** — Proposed his law of thermal radiation; identified sodium in the Sun's spectrum.
- **1860** — Coined the term "black body."
- **1861** — Discovered the elements caesium and rubidium with Bunsen.
- **1862** — Awarded the Rumford Medal for his spectroscopic research.
- **1875** — Returned to the University of Berlin.
- **17 October 1887** — Died in Berlin.

## Body

### Circuit Laws
In 1845, Kirchhoff formulated two laws that are ubiquitous in electrical engineering. The first law (Kirchhoff's Current Law) states that at any node (junction) in a circuit, the sum of currents entering equals the sum leaving. The second law (Kirchhoff's Voltage Law) states that the algebraic sum of potential drops around any closed loop is zero.

### Spectroscopy
Working with Robert Bunsen, Kirchhoff improved the spectroscope and pioneered astrophysical spectroscopy. In 1859, he showed the Sun contains sodium by matching its dark spectral lines to the bright emission lines of sodium in a lab flame. This demonstrated that celestial bodies are composed of the same elements found on Earth. He formalized three laws of spectroscopy: (1) a solid, liquid, or dense gas emits a continuous spectrum; (2) a low-density gas emits a line (emission) spectrum; (3) a continuous spectrum passing through a cool, low-density gas produces an absorption spectrum. These laws explained the nature of Fraunhofer lines but did not explain their origin, which was later understood through quantum mechanics.

### Thermal Radiation and Thermochemistry
Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation (1859) proposed a universal relationship between the emissivity and absorptivity of a body for heat and light, leading him to define the concept of a "black body." This theoretical problem later motivated Max Planck's work on quantum mechanics. In thermochemistry, Kirchhoff showed in 1858 that the variation of a reaction's heat with temperature is determined by the difference in heat capacities between products and reactants.

### Graph Theory
Kirchhoff also contributed to mathematics, proving the matrix tree theorem in graph theory, which relates the number of spanning trees in a graph to properties of its matrix representation.

## Terms
- **Privatdozent**: An academic title for a lecturer who is qualified to teach but is not a salaried professor.
- **Spectroscope**: An instrument used to disperse light into its component wavelengths (a spectrum) for analysis.
- **Continuous spectrum**: A spectrum containing light of all wavelengths, typically emitted by dense, hot objects.
- **Emission spectrum**: A spectrum consisting of bright lines at specific wavelengths, emitted by low-density, excited gases.
- **Absorption spectrum**: A spectrum with dark lines at specific wavelengths, created when light from a continuous source passes through a cooler gas.
- **Black body**: An idealized physical body that absorbs all incident electromagnetic radiation, regardless of frequency or angle of incidence.
- **Thermochemistry**: The study of heat released or absorbed during chemical reactions.
- **Matrix tree theorem**: A formula in graph theory that counts the number of spanning trees in a graph.

## Debates and Open questions
The source notes that Kirchhoff's laws of spectroscopy described the *pattern* of spectral lines but did not explain their physical origin. The existence of discrete spectral lines was known from Fraunhofer (1814), and their mathematical pattern was described by Johann Balmer in 1885. The explanation of lines as electron transitions came with the Bohr model in 1913, which helped lead to quantum mechanics.
