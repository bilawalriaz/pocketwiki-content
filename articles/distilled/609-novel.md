# Novel

## Overview  
The novel is an extended work of narrative fiction, typically in literary prose, with a continuous history of roughly two millennia rooted in ancient Greek and Roman prose narratives, medieval chivalric romance, and the Italian Renaissance novella. Its defining features—fictionality, literary prose, intimate reader experience, and length—have evolved alongside printing technology, shifting social contexts, and changing aesthetic theories, from classical antiquity through the digital age.

## Timeline  
- **c. 1st century BC – 2nd century AD** — Earliest surviving Western novels: Chariton's *Callirhoe*, Petronius' *Satyricon*, Apuleius' *The Golden Ass*
- **1439** — Invention of the printing press by Johannes Gutenberg
- **1605** — Publication of Miguel de Cervantes' *Don Quixote* (first part)
- **1719** — Daniel Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe*
- **1740** — Samuel Richardson's *Pamela, or Virtue Rewarded*
- **1759–1767** — Laurence Sterne's *Tristram Shandy*
- **1814** — Walter Scott's *Waverley*, establishing the historical novel
- **1922** — James Joyce's *Ulysses*, exemplifying stream-of-consciousness modernism
- **1954/55** — J. R. R. Tolkien's *The Lord of the Rings*
- **21st century** — Rise of ebooks, web fiction, and graphic novels

## Body  

### Defining the Genre  
A novel is a long, fictional narrative distinguished primarily by its fictionality, though this boundary has often been blurred—historians historically included invented speeches and embellishments, while some novels, like *Ông cố vấn*, are explicitly non-fictional. Literary prose became the standard form, replacing earlier verse epics, though verse novels persisted into the 19th century and beyond. The novel also fostered intimate reading experiences, cultivating individualistic expression and personal reflection. Length distinguishes the novel from the novella, though precise boundaries remain debated; E. M. Forster proposed a minimum of 50,000 words, while György Lukács linked length to the novel’s capacity to encompass the totality of life.

### Early Novels  
The earliest surviving Western novels emerged in classical antiquity, including Chariton’s *Callirhoe* (mid-1st century), Petronius’ *Satyricon*, and Apuleius’ *The Golden Ass*. These works drew from oral traditions and reflected their cultural milieux. Parallel developments occurred in Classical Sanskrit literature (5th–8th centuries) with works like *Vasavadutta* and *Kadambari*. In China, the 7th-century *You Xian Ku* is considered an early example of the novel, with longer vernacular works emerging during the Ming and Qing dynasties following the spread of print culture.

### Medieval Period (1100–1500)  
European novelistic development awaited the printing press (c. 1439). Chivalric romances dominated aristocratic circles, emphasizing courtly love and heroic quests, distinguishing them from epics like the *chanson de geste*. Prose versions gained prominence in the 13th century (e.g., *Romance of Flamenca*), enabling closer association with serious historical texts. The novella tradition—short, often satirical or exemplary stories—flourished in collections like Boccaccio’s *Decameron* (1354) and Chaucer’s *Canterbury Tales* (1386–1400), rooted in oral storytelling and religious instruction.

### Renaissance Period (1500–1700)  
The distinction between history and fiction was fluid in the 16th century; works like Caxton’s *Le Morte d’Arthur* (1485) were marketed as true histories despite fantastical content. The printing press created a market for cheap chapbooks, while the Spanish *Amadis de Gaula* became the archetypal romance. In France, pastoral novels like *L’Astrée* (1610) and heroic romances revived medieval themes. Satirical romances, from *Lazarillo de Tormes* (1554) to *Don Quixote* (1605), critiqued social norms through witty protagonists. The romance format allowed authors to navigate censorship by claiming fictional status while hinting at truth.

### 18th-Century Novels  
The 18th century marked the novel’s emergence as a distinct literary form. Ian Watt’s *The Rise of the Novel* (1957) argues that realism became the genre’s defining feature. Early novels were printed in small formats (octavo, duodecimo), mimicking devotional and travel narratives, and often published anonymously or as true accounts. The epistolary form gained traction, exemplified by Aphra Behn’s *Love-Letters* (1684–87). Philosophical novels like Voltaire’s *Candide* (1759) and experimental works like Sterne’s *Tristram Shandy* (1759–67) expanded formal possibilities. The sentimental novel, led by Richardson’s *Pamela* (1740), emphasized emotional refinement and moral instruction. Social context shaped content: novels addressed class mobility, gender roles, and urban life, while circulating libraries broadened readership.

### 19th-Century Novels  
The Victorian era saw novels become a dominant literary form, addressing industrialization, social reform, and psychological complexity. Romanticism revived the romance genre, with Gothic fiction (*The Castle of Otranto*, 1764) and historical novels (*Waverley*, 1814) blending imagination with research. Authors like Dickens and Eliot tackled social issues, while foreign writers like Flaubert and Dostoevsky explored realism and existential themes. Science fiction emerged with Wells and Verne, reflecting technological anxieties. The period solidified the novel’s role as a vehicle for national identity and social critique.

### 20th Century  
Modernism revolutionized narrative technique: Joyce’s *Ulysses* (1922) employed stream-of-consciousness, while Proust, Woolf, and Faulkner explored interiority. Postmodernism, seen in Pynchon and Barth, fragmented narrative structure and questioned authorship. Genre fiction—mystery, science fiction, fantasy—flourished commercially, with Tolkien’s *Lord of the Rings* (1954/55) redefining fantasy. Social upheaval influenced themes: war (*All Quiet on the Western Front*), totalitarianism (*1984*), and identity politics. The century also witnessed the rise of mass-market publishing and genre-specific marketing.

### 21st Century  
Digital media transformed novel publication: ebooks, web serials, and audiobooks expanded accessibility. Graphic novels gained literary recognition, while self-publishing platforms democratized authorship. Web fiction dominates in China and South Korea, with platforms like Joara generating significant revenue. Despite digital growth, print remains the preferred format in many markets. Contemporary novels continue to explore global issues, identity, and hybrid forms, reflecting an increasingly interconnected literary landscape.

## Terms  
- **Novella**: A shorter narrative prose fiction, positioned between the novel and the short story.  
- **Chapbook**: Small, inexpensive printed book of the early modern period, often containing popular tales or almanacs.  
- **Epistolary novel**: A novel written in the form of letters, allowing multiple perspectives and intimate narration.  
- **Stream of consciousness**: A narrative technique mimicking the continuous flow of thoughts and feelings in a character’s mind.  
- **Belles lettres**: Elegant or refined literature, distinct from technical or academic writing, popular in the 17th–18th centuries.  
- **Gothic romance**: A genre emphasizing mystery, horror, and the supernatural, emerging in the late 18th century.  
- **Historical novel**: A novel set in the past, combining fictional characters with real historical events.  
- **Magic realism**: A literary style blending realistic narrative with fantastical elements, prominent in Latin American literature.  
- **Postmodernism**: A late 20th-century movement characterized by fragmentation, intertextuality, and skepticism toward grand narratives.  
- **Graphic novel**: A novel presented in comic-strip format, encompassing both fiction and non-fiction.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Scholars debate whether the novel originated in classical antiquity, medieval romance, or the 18th-century realist tradition. Ian Watt’s emphasis on realism as the novel’s defining trait is contested by those who highlight earlier non-realist works. The distinction between novel and romance remains fluid, with some authors (e.g., Hawthorne, Melville) preferring the latter. The role of digital media in reshaping publication and readership continues to evolve, raising questions about the future of print and the definition of the novel itself.
