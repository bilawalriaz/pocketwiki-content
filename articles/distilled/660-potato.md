# Potato

## Overview

The potato (*Solanum tuberosum*) is a starchy tuberous crop domesticated in the Andes of southern Peru and northwestern Bolivia roughly 7,000–10,000 years ago. It became a global staple after Spanish introduction to Europe in the 16th century, fueling population growth and later suffering catastrophic failure during the Irish Great Famine. Today it is one of the world’s most important food crops, with over 5,000 varieties and ongoing challenges from climate change, disease, and genetic bottlenecks.

## Body

### Domestication and Origins

Wild potato species span from the southern United States to southern Chile. Cultivated potato (*S. tuberosum*) originated in the Lake Titicaca region of present-day southern Peru and extreme northwestern Bolivia, domesticated 7,000–10,000 years ago from the *S. brevicaule* complex. Archaeological evidence confirms tuber consumption at Ancon, Peru by 2500 BC. The most widely cultivated variety, *S. tuberosum tuberosum*, is indigenous to Chile’s Chiloé Archipelago and was cultivated pre-conquest.

### Spread and Global Impact

After the Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire, potatoes entered Europe in the late 16th century as part of the Columbian Exchange. Initial adoption was slow, but post-1750 the crop became central to European agriculture and demography. Conservative estimates attribute one-quarter of Old World population and urbanization growth (1700–1900) to the potato. However, limited initial genetic diversity left crops vulnerable—most notably during the 1845 Irish Great Famine caused by *Phytophthora infestans*.

Modern genetic analysis reveals that over 99% of current varieties descend from a lowland Chilean subspecies (*S. tuberosum tuberosum*), not the original Andean domestication zone. The International Potato Center in Lima maintains 4,870 potato germplasm accessions.

### Taxonomy and Genetics

Potatoes belong to the nightshade family (Solanaceae) and genus *Solanum*, sharing lineage with tomatoes and eggplants. The cultivated species is a tetraploid (48 chromosomes). Four diploid species (*S. stenotum*, *S. phureja*, *S. goniocalyx*, *S. ajanhuiri*), two triploid species (*S. chaucha*, *S. juzepczukii*), and one pentaploid species (*S. curtilobum*) are also cultivated. A 2025 study by Zhang et al. places all potato species under *S. tuberosum*, identifying over 55 diploid species within the *Petota* lineage, with landraces tracing to a single origin in *S. candolleanum*.

### Breeding and Biotechnology

Potatoes are self-incompatible, requiring cross-pollination for sexual reproduction. In 2021, Wageningen University and Solynta cloned the *Sli* gene enabling self-compatibility, allowing CRISPR-Cas9 introduction of self-compatibility in diploid lines. This enables faster, more focused breeding. Wild relatives like *S. demissum* and *S. bulbocastanum* provide critical resistance genes (e.g., *Rpi-blb1*) against late blight. GM varieties include Monsanto’s ‘New Leaf’ (Bt toxin for Colorado potato beetle resistance) and BASF’s ‘Amflora’ (high amylopectin starch). Despite regulatory approval, public resistance led Monsanto to discontinue ‘New Leaf’ in 2001 and BASF to halt European GMO development in 2012.

### Cultivation and Production

Potatoes are propagated via "seed potatoes"—disease-free tubers grown in restricted regions (15 U.S. states, Scottish seed potato zones). Growth occurs in five phases: sprouting, leaf/stolon development, tuber initiation, tuber bulking, and maturation. Commercial cultivation requires well-drained neutral soil (pH 6–7), deep tillage, and hilling to prevent greening. Harvesting uses mechanical harvesters; post-harvest curing at 10–16°C with high humidity promotes wound healing. Storage requires darkness, ventilation, and temperatures near 4°C for long-term preservation.

Global production reached 390 million tonnes in 2024 across 17.1 million hectares, led by China (24%) and India. Per capita production remains highest in Northern and Eastern Europe, while 21st-century expansion is fastest in southern and eastern Asia.

### Climate Change Impacts

Climate change threatens potato production through rising temperatures (>30°C causes physiological damage), altered precipitation, and shifting pest/disease distributions. A 2003 estimate projects 18–32% global yield decline by 2040–2069 without adaptation. While CO₂ fertilization may boost growth, potatoes are more drought-sensitive than wheat or maize. Late blight may intensify in cooler regions (e.g., Finland) but diminish in others (e.g., UK). Adaptation strategies include developing heat/drought-tolerant cultivars and shifting cultivation to higher altitudes and latitudes (Canada, Russia).

### Nutrition and Toxicity

A 100g serving of boiled potato with skin provides 87 calories, 20% carbohydrates, 2% protein, and negligible fat. It is a moderate source of vitamin C (14% DV), vitamin B6, pantothenic acid, and potassium (13% DV). Raw potatoes contain glycoalkaloids (solanine, chaconine) concentrated in green tissues and sprouts. Light exposure triggers solanine accumulation; the toxic ‘Lenape’ variety was withdrawn in 1970 for excessive levels. Commercial varieties are tested to maintain glycoalkaloids below 200 mg/kg.

### Uses and Cultural Significance

Potatoes feature in global cuisines—from Peruvian chuño to British fish and chips, Italian gnocchi, and Chinese stir-fries. They are used in spirits (vodka, poitín), livestock feed, and industrial starch production. In mythology, Inca goddess Axomamma and Iroquois Earth Woman symbolize potato origins. Artistically, Van Gogh’s *The Potato Eaters* (1885) and Millet’s *The Potato Harvest* depict peasant life. The toy Mr. Potato Head (1952) and film *The Martian* (2015) reflect cultural penetration.

## Terms

- **Tuber**: Underground stem structure storing nutrients, not a root; forms from thickened rhizomes at stolon tips.
- **Solanine**: Glycoalkaloid toxin in nightshades; accumulates in potatoes exposed to light or damage.
- **Self-incompatible**: Reproductive system preventing self-pollination; requires cross-pollination for seed production.
- **Late blight**: Disease caused by *Phytophthora infestans*; responsible for the Great Irish Famine.
- **Landrace**: Traditional crop variety maintained by local communities through informal seed saving.
- **Glycoalkaloid**: Class of plant compounds including solanine and chaconine; defensive chemicals toxic to animals.
- **CRISPR-Cas9**: Gene-editing technology used to introduce traits like self-compatibility in potatoes.
- **Wound healing**: Post-harvest process where potato skins toughen and wounds seal during curing.
- **Lenticel**: Pore-like structure on potato skins facilitating gas exchange.
- **Andean potato**: Subspecies *S. tuberosum andigena*, adapted to short-day conditions of equatorial mountains.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Single vs. multiple domestication origins**: Genetic studies increasingly support a single origin in southern Peru/Bolivia, though some debate persists regarding contributions from other wild populations.
- **Chilean vs. Andean ancestry**: Modern varieties predominantly descend from Chilean *S. tuberosum tuberosum*, raising questions about the role of Andean landraces in contemporary breeding.
- **GM acceptance**: Despite regulatory approval and agronomic benefits, public resistance to GM potatoes remains high in the U.S. and EU, limiting commercial deployment.
- **Climate adaptation efficacy**: While shifting cultivation to cooler regions may offset some losses, competition for land and water resources poses unresolved challenges.
- **Nutritional classification**: UK NHS excludes potatoes from 5-A-Day recommendations despite their nutritional value, reflecting ongoing dietary guideline debates.
