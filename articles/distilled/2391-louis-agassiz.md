# Louis Agassiz

## Overview
Louis Agassiz (1807–1873) was a Swiss-born American naturalist who became one of the most influential scientists of the 19th century. He made foundational contributions to ichthyology (the study of fish), geology, and the founding of glaciology, while also establishing key scientific institutions in the United States. His legacy is complex, as his scientific work is overshadowed by his advocacy for polygenism—a theory of separate human origins that provided a basis for scientific racism.

## Body

### Early Life and Career in Europe
Born in Switzerland, Agassiz studied at several universities, earning doctorates in philosophy and medicine. In Paris, he was mentored by Georges Cuvier and Alexander von Humboldt, who launched his careers in zoology and geology. His early work focused on ichthyology, classifying Brazilian fish and later fossil fish from European sites. His monumental five-volume *Recherches sur les poissons fossiles* (1833–1843) established his fame and required a new classification of fish based on scales and dermal appendages.

### Founding Glaciology
In 1836, Agassiz became convinced by the glacial theories of Jean de Charpentier and Ignaz Venetz. In 1837, he proposed that the Earth had experienced a past Ice Age, with massive glaciers covering much of the Northern Hemisphere. His 1840 work *Études sur les glaciers* detailed glacial movements and their role in shaping landscapes. Fieldwork in the Alps and, with William Buckland, in the British Isles provided evidence for his theory, which revolutionized historical geology.

### Career in the United States
Agassiz moved to the U.S. in 1846, settling at Harvard University where he became professor of zoology and geology. He headed the Lawrence Scientific School and founded the Museum of Comparative Zoology in 1859. A prolific lecturer and writer, he published *Contributions to the Natural History of the United States* (1857–1862), in which he criticized Darwin's theory of evolution, advocating instead for a "plan of creation" with new life created after each ice age. He led major scientific expeditions to Brazil (1865–66) and South America (1871).

### Polygenism and Scientific Racism
After arriving in the U.S., Agassiz was influenced by anthropologist Samuel George Morton's work on cranial capacity. Agassiz became a proponent of polygenism, the theory that human races were separately created. He argued that black and white people were distinct species, a view he first announced in his 1846 Lowell Lectures. While he opposed slavery, he advocated for strict racial segregation to prevent "miscegenation," which he viewed as a "sin against nature." He commissioned daguerreotypes of enslaved people in 1850 to support his theories of racial difference.

## Terms
- **Ichthyology:** The branch of zoology devoted to the study of fish.
- **Glaciology:** The scientific study of glaciers and ice sheets.
- **Polygenism:** The theory that human races have separate origins, as opposed to monogenism (a single origin).
- **Dermal appendages:** External structures on an animal's skin, such as scales, fins, or teeth, which Agassiz used to classify fossil fish.
- **Roches moutonnées:** Rock formations shaped by glacial erosion, giving them a smoothed, asymmetrical appearance.
- **Daguerreotype:** An early photographic process using a silvered copper plate, producing a single, unique image.
- **Miscegenation:** The mixing of different racial groups through marriage or procreation, a term often used in historical contexts of racial prejudice.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Scientific Legacy vs. Racism:** Scholars debate how to reconcile Agassiz's monumental contributions to natural history with his role in promoting scientific racism. This has led to the renaming of institutions and landmarks named in his honor.
- **Glacial Theory in the Tropics:** Agassiz's hypothesis that the Amazon region was glaciated was later refuted by his own student, Charles Hartt, who found evidence of marine and fluvial processes instead. However, modern research confirms the Amazon rainforest was fragmented during the Last Glacial Maximum, though by non-glacial mechanisms.
- **Motivations for Polygenism:** Historians like Stephen Jay Gould argue Agassiz's polygenism stemmed from personal racism upon encountering African Americans, while others suggest he saw it as a testable scientific hypothesis, albeit one that aligned with and reinforced existing prejudices.
