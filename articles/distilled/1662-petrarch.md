# Petrarch

## Overview
Francesco Petrarch (1304–1374) was an Italian scholar, poet, and early humanist whose rediscovery of Cicero’s letters helped ignite the Italian Renaissance. He established the sonnet as the dominant lyric form in Europe through his *Canzoniere*, a sequence of 366 poems centered on his unrequited love for Laura, and coined the concept of the "Dark Ages" to describe the cultural decline he perceived between antiquity and his own time. Petrarch argued that secular learning and classical virtue were compatible with Christian faith, shaping the humanist movement that defined Renaissance intellectual life.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Petrarch was born in Arezzo but spent his youth in Avignon and Carpentras, where his father, a notary, had relocated to serve the Avignon Papacy. Forced to study law at Montpellier (1316–1320) and Bologna (1320–1326), Petrarch considered the seven years wasted, preferring Latin literature. His father once burned his books to discourage this interest. Petrarch later claimed his guardians cheated him of his inheritance through legal trickery, cementing his view of law as "the art of selling justice." After his parents died, he returned to Avignon in 1326, taking minor clerical posts that afforded time for writing.

### Classical Scholarship and the "Dark Ages"
Petrarch’s *Africa*, a Latin epic on Scipio Africanus, made him a European celebrity. In 1341, he became the second poet laureate since antiquity, crowned on the Capitoline Hill. He traveled widely—serving as an ambassador and climbing Mont Ventoux for pleasure—collecting Latin manuscripts and promoting the recovery of classical knowledge. He encouraged Leontius Pilatus’s translation of Homer but lacked Greek himself, lamenting, "Homer was dumb to him, while he was deaf to Homer." In 1345, he personally discovered a lost collection of Cicero’s letters (*Epistulae ad Atticum*) in Verona. Disdaining his era’s ignorance, he formulated the concept of the "Dark Ages," a periodization modern scholars consider inaccurate.

### Mont Ventoux and the Inner Turn
On 26 April 1336, Petrarch climbed Mont Ventoux (1,912 m) with his brother and servants, later describing the ascent in a letter to the monk Dionigi di Borgo San Sepolcro. Inspired by Philip V of Macedon’s climb of Mount Haemo, he sought the view for aesthetic gratification—a strikingly "modern" motive noted by Jacob Burckhardt. At the summit, he opened Augustine’s *Confessions* and read: "And men go about to wonder at the heights of the mountains... but themselves they consider not." He turned from the outer world to the "inner world of soul," descending in silence. James Hillman argues this descent—the "return to the valley of soul"—marks the true beginning of the Renaissance sensibility.

### Poetry, Laura, and the *Canzoniere*
Petrarch’s Italian fame rests on the *Rerum vulgarium fragmenta* (Fragments of Vernacular Matters), known as the *Canzoniere* (songbook), containing 366 lyrics (mostly sonnets), and the narrative poem *I trionfi*. On 6 April 1327, he saw Laura in Avignon’s church of Sainte-Claire, sparking a lifelong passion. She may have been Laura de Noves, wife of Count Hugues de Sade; she was married, refused him, and died in 1348. Petrarch channeled unrequited love into exclamatory poems exploring the conflict between sensual desire and Christian mysticism (e.g., Rima 134: "I find no peace, and yet I make no war:/and fear, and hope: and burn, and I am ice"). He denied Laura was fictional, though her name puns on *lauro* (laurel). Francesco de Sanctis praised his verse’s music; Gianfranco Contini termed his language "unilinguismo" (single, refined vernacular), contrasting Dante’s "plurilinguismo."

### Latin Works and Letters
Petrarch wrote mostly in Latin: *Secretum* (dialogue with an Augustinian figure), *De viris illustribus* (moral biographies), *De vita solitaria* and *De otio religiosorum* (praise of contemplative life), *De remediis utriusque fortunae* (a long-popular self-help book), invectives, the pastoral *Bucolicum carmen*, and the unfinished *Africa*. He published letters in collections—*Familiares*, *Seniles*, *Liber sine nomine*, *Disperse*, *Metricae*—modeled on Cicero’s correspondence. His "Letter to Posterity" (*Seniles* finale, c. 1371–72) is the first such autobiography since Augustine. Only one musical setting from his lifetime survives: Jacopo da Bologna’s *Non al suo amante* (c. 1350).

### Philosophy: Humanism and the Active vs. Contemplative Life
Petrarch is called the "father of humanism" and "father of the Renaissance." In *Secretum*, he argued that secular achievement does not bar a relationship with God; humans should use their God-given intellectual potential fully. A devout Catholic, he saw no conflict between faith and classical study, though some label him a proto-Protestant. His introspective struggles—especially between the active and contemplative life—became central humanist debates. In *De vita solitaria* (1346), he praised Pope Celestine V’s 1294 resignation as virtuous solitude. Leonardo Bruni later countered with "civic humanism," advocating the active life. This tension shaped Renaissance leaders’ self-understanding.

### Petrarchism and Legacy
Petrarchism, a 16th-century movement treating the *Canzoniere* as canonical, spread across Italy, France, Spain, and England. Followers included Pietro Bembo (who codified modern Italian on Petrarch’s model), Michelangelo, Vittoria Colonna, Clément Marot, Garcilaso de la Vega, Thomas Wyatt, Henry Howard, Edmund Spenser, Philip Sidney, and Bálint Balassi. Bembo’s *Prose of the Vernacular Tongue* (1525) made Petrarch the standard for verse. Later influence appears in Serafino Ciminelli, Marin Držić, Franz Liszt’s *Tre sonetti del Petrarca*, and works by Elliott Carter and Kaija Saariaho. In 2003, an exhumation in Arquà confirmed skeletal injuries matching Petrarch’s writings (a donkey kick at 42), though the skull was not his. He is also credited as the first notable numismatist, buying ancient coins from peasants in Rome to identify emperors.

### Petrarch vs. Dante
Petrarch differed sharply from Dante. Dante’s *Commedia* is rooted in Florentine politics and uses language from low to sublime; Petrarch confessed to Boccaccio he never read it. Dante evolved from courtly love to philosophy; Petrarch’s style remained uniform, endlessly revising the *Canzoniere*. Dante engaged politics; Petrarch withdrew, reflecting the decline of the free commune and rise of the *signoria*. Petrarch perfected the sonnet (inherited from Giacomo da Lentini, used by Dante in *Vita nuova*), adopting ABBA–ABBA quatrains, excluding imperfect Sicilian rhymes, and using enjambment for longer semantic units. The Petrarchan sonnet bears his name.

## Terms
- ****Humanism**** — Intellectual movement emphasizing the study of classical antiquity (human thought and action) to realize human potential, compatible with Christian faith in Petrarch’s view.
- ****Canzoniere**** — "Songbook"; Petrarch’s collection of 366 vernacular poems (*Rerum vulgarium fragmenta*), mostly sonnets addressed to Laura.
- ****Dark Ages**** — Petrarch’s term for the post-classical era he saw as culturally ignorant; modern scholars reject the label as inaccurate.
- ****Petrarchan sonnet**** — Sonnet form perfected by Petrarch: ABBA–ABBA quatrains, varied sestet, enjambment linking lines; became the European lyric standard.
- ****Avignon Papacy**** — Period (1309–1377) when popes resided in Avignon, France; Petrarch’s family moved there in 1309.
- ****Laura**** — Woman Petrarch saw in 1327; likely Laura de Noves, married to Hugues de Sade; died 1348; muse for the *Canzoniere*.
- ****Secretum**** — "My Secret Book"; Petrarch’s imaginary dialogue with an Augustinian figure, exploring faith vs. secular glory.
- ****Civic humanism**** — Leonardo Bruni’s philosophy (early 1400s) advocating active political life over Petrarch’s contemplative ideal.
- ****Unilinguismo**** — Contini’s term for Petrarch’s consistent, refined vernacular style, contrasted with Dante’s stylistic variety (*plurilinguismo*).
- ****Numismatics**** — Study/collection of coins; Petrarch is called the first famous aficionado, buying ancient coins from Roman peasants.

## Debates and open questions
- **Laura’s historicity**: Whether Laura was a real person (possibly Laura de Noves) or an idealized/pseudonymous figure; Petrarch denied the latter, but her name puns on *lauro* (laurel).
- **Petrarch’s reading of Dante**: He told Boccaccio he never read the *Commedia*; scholars debate if this was true or a rhetorical distancing.
- **"Dark Ages" validity**: Petrarch’s periodization is widely rejected by modern historians as a polemical construct ignoring medieval achievements.
- **Mont Ventoux "first ascent"**: Jean Buridan and Anno II of Cologne climbed it earlier; Petrarch’s claim of novelty is literary, not factual.
- **Skull authenticity**: The 2003 exhumation found the skull in the tomb was not Petrarch’s (DNA mismatch), though the skeleton matches his described injuries; the skull’s whereabouts remain unknown.
- **Library disposition**: Petrarch promised his manuscripts to Venice for the Palazzo Molina; the deal collapsed when he moved to Padua (Venice’s enemy) in 1368, and the da Carrara lords seized the library, scattering it across Europe.
