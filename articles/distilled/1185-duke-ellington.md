# Duke Ellington

## Overview
Edward Kennedy "Duke" Ellington (1899–1974) was an American jazz pianist, composer, and bandleader whose orchestra defined the big band era and expanded jazz into extended concert works. Based in New York from the mid-1920s, he gained national fame through Cotton Club radio broadcasts, mastering the three-minute 78 rpm format while composing over one thousand pieces, many of which became standards. His nearly 35-year collaboration with Billy Strayhorn produced landmark suites such as *Black, Brown and Beige* and *Such Sweet Thunder*. A 1956 Newport Jazz Festival appearance sparked a global revival, leading to worldwide tours, film scores, and sacred concerts. Ellington rejected the label "jazz," preferring "beyond category" and "American music," and was awarded a posthumous Pulitzer Prize Special Award in 1999.

## Timeline
- **April 29, 1899** — Born in Washington, D.C., to James Edward and Daisy Kennedy Ellington.
- **1914** — Writes first composition, "Soda Fountain Rag," while working as a soda jerk.
- **1917** — Forms first group, The Duke's Serenaders; works as sign painter and booking agent.
- **1923** — Moves to New York City; secures four-year engagement at the Hollywood Club (later Club Kentucky).
- **1924** — Takes over leadership of The Washingtonians; makes first eight records.
- **Dec 4, 1927** — Opens as house band at Harlem's Cotton Club; weekly radio broadcasts bring national exposure.
- **1929** — Appears in first film, *Black and Tan*; works with Will Vodery on *Show Girl*, absorbing chromatic orchestration techniques.
- **1931** — Records first extended piece, "Creole Rhapsody"; Cotton Club engagement ends.
- **1933** — Tours England, Scotland, France, and Netherlands; wins praise from composer Constant Lambert.
- **1939** — Billy Strayhorn joins as lyricist/arranger, beginning a decades-long collaboration.
- **1939–1941** — Bassist Jimmy Blanton and tenor saxophonist Ben Webster join; orchestra reaches creative peak.
- **Jan 23, 1943** — Premieres *Black, Brown and Beige* at Carnegie Hall, launching annual concert series.
- **July 1941** — Debuts musical *Jump for Joy* in Los Angeles; runs 122 performances.
- **1950** — Tours Western Europe (74 dates); records first LP, *Masterpieces by Ellington*; presents *Harlem* score to President Truman.
- **July 7, 1956** — Newport Jazz Festival performance (Paul Gonsalves's 27-chorus solo) triggers major career revival.
- **1959** — Scores *Anatomy of a Murder*, landmark non-diegetic Hollywood film score by African Americans.
- **1965** — Premieres First Sacred Concert; shortlisted for Pulitzer Prize (no award given).
- **1969** — Receives Presidential Medal of Freedom.
- **1973** — Releases final collaboration with Teresa Brewer; performs final full concert March 20, 1974.
- **May 24, 1974** — Dies in New York City of lung cancer and pneumonia, aged 75.
- **1999** — Awarded posthumous Pulitzer Prize Special Award.

## Body

### Early Life and Musical Formation
Ellington grew up in Washington, D.C.'s Black middle class, surrounded by racial pride and pianists—both parents played. Nicknamed "Duke" by a friend for his elegant bearing, he initially preferred baseball but was drawn to piano by the poolroom players he heard at age 14. He learned harmony from teacher Henry Lee Grant and technique from Oliver "Doc" Perry, while absorbing ragtime and stride styles from James P. Johnson, Luckey Roberts, and Eubie Blake. In 1914, he composed "Soda Fountain Rag" by ear, varying its tempo to simulate a repertoire. He turned down an art scholarship to Pratt Institute in 1916 and dropped out of commercial art school three months before graduation to pursue music.

### Building the Orchestra: Washington to Harlem
From 1917, Ellington built a business playing society dances, using his sign-painting job to secure bookings. His first band, The Duke's Serenaders, included childhood friend Otto Hardwick and drummer Sonny Greer. When Greer joined the Wilbur Sweatman Orchestra in New York, Ellington followed, entering the Harlem Renaissance scene. After a discouraging stint hustling pool and playing rent parties, a 1923 engagement at the Hollywood Club (49th and Broadway) provided stability. When banjoist Elmer Snowden left in 1924, Ellington assumed leadership of The Washingtonians. The group developed a distinctive "Jungle Style" via trumpeter Bubber Miley's growl plunger mute and trombonist Charlie Irvis's wah-wah effects, recorded on labels from Brunswick to dime-store imprints under manager Irving Mills.

### The Cotton Club Years and National Profile
In December 1927, Ellington's expanded 11-piece orchestra became the Cotton Club house band. The club's whites-only clientele and weekly national radio broadcasts made Ellington a household name. He composed for revues alongside Jimmy McHugh and Dorothy Fields, while his own pieces—"East St. Louis Toodle-Oo," "Black and Tan Fantasy," and "Creole Love Call" (with Adelaide Hall)—established his sound. Miley's alcoholism forced his departure in 1929; Cootie Williams succeeded him, extending the growl trumpet vocabulary. In 1929, Ellington worked with Ziegfeld musical supervisor Will Vodery on *Show Girl*; Vodery's chromatic harmony deepened Ellington's orchestral palette, a influence Ellington later cited as more formative than direct study of Debussy or Ravel.

### The Blanton-Webster Era and Strayhorn Partnership
The 1939 arrival of bassist Jimmy Blanton and tenor saxophonist Ben Webster, coupled with Billy Strayhorn's hiring, marked a creative zenith. Blanton revolutionized the double bass as a melodic solo voice; Webster became the orchestra's first featured tenor soloist. Strayhorn, classically trained, became Ellington's "right arm," composing, arranging, and polishing works—most famously "Take the 'A' Train" (1941), which replaced "East St. Louis Toodle-Oo" as the band's theme. The 1940–42 Victor/Bluebird recordings ("Cotton Tail," "Harlem Air Shaft," "Jack the Bear") captured this peak. Blanton's terminal illness ended his tenure by late 1941; Webster left in 1943.

### Extending the Form: Carnegie Hall and Extended Works
Ellington aimed to transcend the three-minute 78 rpm limit. *Creole Rhapsody* (1931) and *Reminiscing in Tempo* (1935) were early attempts. With Strayhorn's formal expertise, extended compositions became regular: *Black, Brown and Beige* (1943), a tone parallel to Black history, debuted at Carnegie Hall to mixed reviews, initiating an annual series. The musical *Jump for Joy* (1941) addressed Black identity but failed to reach Broadway. The 1942–44 recording ban and shifting economics—big bands becoming financially precarious—strained the orchestra, subsidized by Ellington's songwriting royalties.

### Postwar Persistence and European Triumph
While many big bands disbanded, Ellington toured Western Europe for 77 days in 1950, playing 74 dates. He completed *Harlem* (1950), presenting the score to President Truman, and scored Orson Welles's *Time Runs in Paris*. The first LP, *Masterpieces by Ellington* (1950), featured extended "concert arrangements." Key personnel changes followed: Johnny Hodges, Lawrence Brown, and Sonny Greer departed in 1951, replaced by Paul Gonsalves (tenor, lifetime), Clark Terry (trumpet), and Louie Bellson (drums). By 1955, Ellington lacked a steady recording contract.

### The Newport Revival and Late-Period Suites
The July 7, 1956 Newport Jazz Festival appearance—highlighted by Gonsalves's 27-chorus solo on "Diminuendo and Crescendo in Blue"—generated a *Time* cover, a Columbia Records contract, and the best-selling LP of Ellington's career (partly re-recorded with fake crowd noise). Renewed stability produced Shakespearean suite *Such Sweet Thunder* (1957), *The Queen's Suite* (1958, for Elizabeth II), and film scores *Anatomy of a Murder* (1959, landmark non-diegetic jazz score) and *Paris Blues* (1961). Ellington recorded with former rivals (Count Basie, Louis Armstrong) and younger innovators (John Coltrane, Charles Mingus, Max Roach).

### Sacred Concerts and Final Compositions
Ellington premiered the First Sacred Concert in 1965, a jazz Christian liturgy he called "the most important thing I've done," followed by Second (1968) and Third (1973). World tours inspired *Far East Suite* (1966), *New Orleans Suite* (1970), *The Afro-Eurasian Eclipse* (1971), and *Latin American Suite* (1972). He scored Shakespeare's *Timon of Athens* (1963) for the Stratford Festival. His only opera, *Queenie Pie* (with Maurice Peress), remained unfinished. Final recordings included a 1967 album with Frank Sinatra and a 1972 solo piano session (released 2017). His last work, *Three Black Kings*, honored Balthazar, Solomon, and Martin Luther King Jr.

### Personal Life
Ellington married Edna Thompson in 1918; son Mercer was born 1919. They separated when Edna returned to Washington. From 1929, Mildred Dixon managed Tempo Music and raised Mercer. In 1938, Ellington began a stormy relationship with Beatrice "Evie" Ellis, later adding Fernanda de Castro Monte in the 1960s; he supported both. Sister Ruth ran Tempo Music; Mercer managed the band and led the "ghost band" until his 1996 death. Ellington was an Alpha Phi Alpha member and Prince Hall Freemason.

## Terms
- **Jungle Style**: Ellington's early signature sound (c. 1927–31) featuring growl plunger mute trumpet (Bubber Miley) and wah-wah trombone, evoking a primitive exoticism for Cotton Club revues.
- **Three-minute limit**: The maximum duration of a 78 rpm record side; Ellington mastered this "miniature" form before extending compositions across multiple sides or LPs.
- **Billy Strayhorn (1915–1967)**: Composer-arranger-pianist; Ellington's primary collaborator from 1939. Classically trained, he composed "Take the 'A' Train," arranged, polished, and conducted for Ellington, functioning as a "second Ellington."
- **Jimmy Blanton (1918–1942)**: Bassist whose 1939–41 tenure transformed the double bass from a rhythm-section timekeeper into a melodic solo instrument.
- **Carnegie Hall concerts**: Annual series (1943–47) premiering Ellington's extended works, beginning with *Black, Brown and Beige*; unprecedented for a jazz orchestra in a classical venue.
- **Newport Jazz Festival (1956)**: Career-reviving performance where Paul Gonsalves's 27-chorus solo on "Diminuendo and Crescendo in Blue" ignited the crowd and press.
- **Non-diegetic score**: Film music whose source is not visible on screen (e.g., orchestral underscore). *Anatomy of a Murder* (1959) was the first significant Hollywood example by African Americans.
- **Sacred Concerts**: Three jazz liturgies (1965, 1968, 1973) blending orchestral jazz, choir, tap dance, and soloists; Ellington considered them his most important work.
- **"Beyond category"**: Ellington's self-chosen label, rejecting "jazz" as limiting; he viewed his music as part of the broader "American music" tradition.
- **Ghost band**: The Duke Ellington Orchestra continued under Mercer Ellington (1974–1996) after Duke's death, recording and touring as a repertory ensemble.

## Debates and open questions
- **Ellington vs. Strayhorn authorship**: Because Strayhorn often arranged, polished, and completed Ellington's sketches—and sometimes filled in for him at piano and conductor—scholars debate the precise division of authorship on many late works.
- **Reception of extended works**: Critics and audiences consistently received Ellington's longer compositions (*Black, Brown and Beige*, *A Drum Is a Woman*, Sacred Concerts) less favorably than his three-minute masterpieces; the reason—formal weakness, genre prejudice, or performance conditions—remains contested.
- **Newport 1956 album authenticity**: The original *Ellington at Newport* LP (1956) contained only ~40% live material; the rest was studio re-creation with added crowd noise, undisclosed until the 1999 release of the actual concert tapes.
- **Commercial vs. artistic priorities**: Ellington's statement "jazz is music, the swing is business" reflects tension between his compositional ambition and the dance-band economics that sustained the orchestra; scholars weigh how much this compromise shaped his output.
- **Pulitzer Prize 1965**: The jury recommended Ellington for the Pulitzer Prize in Music, but the board declined to award it that year; the 1999 Special Award was widely seen as belated correction, though the 1965 decision remains a noted controversy.
