# History of Christianity

## Overview
Christianity, the world's largest religion, originated with Jesus of Nazareth's teachings in first-century Judea. Its core beliefs in Jesus's incarnation, crucifixion, and resurrection fueled its growth from a small Jewish sect to a global faith with over two billion adherents. Its history involves theological development, institutional growth, schisms, and cultural adaptation, profoundly shaping Western civilization and global history.

## Timeline
- **c. 30–33 AD** — Crucifixion of Jesus in Jerusalem.
- **c. 50** — Council of Jerusalem addresses requirements for Gentile converts.
- **313** — Edict of Milan legalizes Christianity under Constantine.
- **325** — First Council of Nicaea convenes; Nicene Creed formulated.
- **451** — Council of Chalcedon defines Christ's two natures, leading to Oriental Orthodox schism.
- **476** — Fall of the Western Roman Empire; monks preserve culture.
- **1054** — East–West Schism separates Eastern Orthodoxy and the Catholic Church.
- **1095–1272** — Crusades launched to aid Eastern Christians against Muslim Turks.
- **1517** — Martin Luther posts his Ninety-five Theses, beginning the Protestant Reformation.
- **1962–65** — Second Vatican Council (Vatican II) enacts Catholic reforms.

## Body

### Early Christianity (c. 27–312)
Christianity began as an urban movement within Judaism, centered on Jesus as the Messiah. It spread along trade routes to major Roman cities, driven by inclusive community and challenging teachings despite sporadic persecution. By 250, Christians numbered over a million, leading to formal church structures and gradual separation from Judaism.

### Late Antiquity (313–c. 600)
Constantine's legalization in 313 transformed Christianity from a persecuted sect to a favored religion. This era saw flourishing Christian art, architecture, and literature, including Jerome's Vulgate. Monasticism began. Theological disputes led to major councils and schisms: Nicaea (325) addressed Arianism, while Ephesus (431) and Chalcedon (451) over Christ's nature caused permanent splits, forming the Church of the East and Oriental Orthodoxy. After the Western Empire's fall in 476, the Byzantine Empire preserved Roman and Christian traditions.

### Early Middle Ages (c. 600–1000)
Following Islamic conquests, Christianity declined in the Middle East and North Africa but expanded in Europe. Monasteries became learning centers, preserving classical knowledge. The faith spread: the Church of the East reached China, while Byzantine missionaries Cyril and Methodius Christianized the Slavs, creating the Cyrillic alphabet. In the West, Charlemagne's coronation (800) by the Pope linked secular and religious authority, and the papacy gained prestige under figures like Gregory I.

### High Middle Ages (c. 1000–1300)
The Western Church became centralized. The Gregorian Reform (11th c.) fought simony and lay investiture, asserting papal supremacy. The Crusades (1096–1272), though militarily unsuccessful, increased European interaction with the East and fueled religious fervor. New monastic orders like the Cistercians and Dominicans promoted reform and fought heresy (e.g., the Albigensian Crusade against Cathars). The Medieval Inquisition prosecuted heresy. Intellectual revival, fueled by Aristotle, led to scholasticism and the 12th-century Renaissance.

### Renaissance and Reformation (c. 1300–1650)
The late medieval Church faced crises, including the Avignon Papacy and the Western Schism (1378–1417). Calls for reform by figures like John Wycliffe and Jan Hus were suppressed. The Renaissance revived art and learning but also criticized Church corruption. The Protestant Reformation, initiated by Martin Luther in 1517, fractured Western Christianity, emphasizing Scripture's authority and the priesthood of all believers, leading to Lutheran, Reformed, and Anglican traditions. The Catholic Counter-Reformation, led by the Council of Trent, reformed doctrine and practice. Religious conflicts culminated in wars like the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648).

### Modern Period (1650–1945)
The Enlightenment challenged religious authority, promoting secularism and biblical criticism. In response, Pietism and Great Awakenings sparked revivals, producing new denominations like Methodism. Christianity influenced social reforms, including abolition and women's rights. The 19th century saw massive missionary expansion, especially in Africa and Asia. The 20th century brought decline in the West but growth in the Global South. World Wars and totalitarian regimes caused severe persecution. The World Council of Churches (1938) promoted global cooperation.

### After World War II
Christianity's center shifted to the Global South, particularly sub-Saharan Africa and Asia. In the West, secularization reduced church attendance. Vatican II (1962–65) modernized the Catholic Church. Ecumenical efforts increased, though divisions persist. New theological movements emerged, including liberation, feminist, and postcolonial theologies. Pentecostalism became the fastest-growing movement. In the 21st century, Christianity is the world's largest and most diverse religion, present in all continents and over 3,000 languages, but faces challenges from secularism and persecution.

## Terms
- **Incarnation**: God becoming embodied in Jesus Christ.
- **Resurrection**: Jesus rising from the dead after crucifixion.
- **Canon**: The officially recognized collection of sacred texts.
- **Schism**: A formal split within a religious body over doctrine or authority.
- **Heresy**: Belief contrary to orthodox doctrine.
- **Monasticism**: A religious way of life under vows, often in community.
- **Scholasticism**: A medieval method reconciling Christian theology with classical philosophy.
- **Reformation**: The 16th-century movement creating Protestant churches.
- **Secularization**: The process reducing religion's role in public life.
- **Pentecostalism**: A movement emphasizing direct personal experience of God through the Holy Spirit.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent and nature of early Christian persecution by Rome are debated, with newer studies suggesting it was often localized and exaggerated.
- The causes of Christianity's rapid early growth are analyzed through sociological (e.g., Rodney Stark's "intense community" theory) and theological lenses.
- The role of the Crusades is contested: were they primarily religious, or also driven by political, economic, and social factors?
- The Reformation's impact on European wars of religion is debated, with emphasis on religious liberty versus state-building and economics.
- The future demographic shift to the Global South raises questions about theological and cultural transformations within the faith.
