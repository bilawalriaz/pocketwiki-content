# Angkor Wat

## Overview
Angkor Wat is a vast temple complex in Cambodia, originally built as a Hindu state temple and mausoleum for King Suryavarman II in the early 12th century. It is the largest religious monument in the world and a defining symbol of Cambodian national identity. Its significance lies in its monumental scale, architectural harmony, and its continuous evolution from a Hindu site to a major center of Theravada Buddhism.

## Timeline
- **1113–c.1150 CE** — Reign of King Suryavarman II; construction of Angkor Wat as a Hindu temple dedicated to Vishnu.
- **1177–1181 CE** — Angkor is sacked by the Chams; later restored by Jayavarman VII.
- **Late 13th century onward** — Gradual transformation of Angkor Wat from Hindu to a Buddhist temple.
- **1586** — First recorded European visit by Portuguese friar António da Madalena.
- **1860** — "Rediscovery" by French naturalist Henri Mouhot, popularizing it in the West.
- **1908** — Conservation d'Angkor established by the École française d'Extrême-Orient (EFEO).
- **1992** — Angkor region designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **1993** — International Coordinating Committee for the Safeguarding and Development of the Historic Site of Angkor (ICC-Angkor) established.
- **2004** — Angkor Wat removed from the List of World Heritage in Danger.

## Body

### Construction and Original Purpose
Commissioned by King Suryavarman II, construction began in 1122 CE and was completed in 1150 CE. The temple was built as the king's state temple in the capital Yaśodharapura (Angkor) and is generally considered his intended mausoleum. Its design combines the temple-mountain and galleried temple forms, symbolically representing Mount Meru, the home of the gods in Hindu cosmology. The complex is oriented west, unusual for Khmer temples, which has led scholars to hypothesize its funerary function.

### Religious Transformation and Continuity
After the death of Suryavarman II and the Cham sack of Angkor in 1178, the Khmer Empire was restored by Jayavarman VII, who promoted Mahayana Buddhism. From the late 13th century, Angkor Wat was gradually converted for Theravada Buddhist worship, with Hindu sculptures replaced by Buddhist art. Unlike many Angkorian temples, it was never completely abandoned, maintaining a continuous religious presence that aided its preservation. Japanese pilgrims established settlements there in the 17th century, and it remains an active Buddhist center.

### Architecture and Design
Angkor Wat is the prime example of classical Khmer architecture. Its layout features a broad moat, an outer wall, and three concentric galleries rising to a central quincunx (five-tower) of towers symbolizing Mount Meru's peaks. The central tower rises 65 meters above ground. The complex is famed for its extensive bas-reliefs, covering nearly 1,000 square meters, depicting scenes from Hindu epics like the Ramayana and Mahabharata, as well as historical processions. It is adorned with over 1,796 devata (divine figures) and apsaras (celestial dancers).

### Construction Techniques
Built primarily from sandstone quarried from Mount Kulen about 40 km away, the monument comprises 5 to 10 million blocks, some weighing up to 1.5 tons. Laterite was used for hidden structural parts and the outer wall. The binding agent for the blocks is unknown. Recent LIDAR scans revealed the temple was built upon roughly 3 million cubic meters of fill. The labor force likely numbered in the thousands, including highly skilled artisans.

### Modern History, Restoration, and Tourism
The site was "rediscovered" by Europeans in the 16th century and popularized by Henri Mouhot in 1860. France, as Cambodia's protectorate, began restoration work in the early 20th century. This was interrupted by the Cambodian Civil War and Khmer Rouge era (1970s–80s), which caused damage. Since the 1990s, international teams (from France, Japan, India, Germany, etc.) have coordinated restoration under the ICC-Angkor. Angkor Wat is a major tourist destination, attracting over 2.5 million visitors annually pre-pandemic, though this influx poses conservation challenges like erosion and structural strain.

## Terms
- **Bas-relief**: A type of sculpture where figures are carved to project slightly from a flat background.
- **Devata**: A divine being or deity depicted in Khmer art, often as a decorative figure on temple walls.
- **Gopuram**: A monumental entrance tower, typically at the cardinal points of a temple enclosure.
- **Khmer architecture**: The architectural style of the Khmer Empire, characterized by temple-mountains, concentric galleries, and ornate decoration.
- **Mount Meru**: A sacred five-peaked mountain in Hindu and Buddhist cosmology, considered the center of the universe.
- **Quincunx**: An arrangement of five objects with four at the corners of a square and one in the center, like the five towers of Angkor Wat's central shrine.
- **Sandstone**: A durable sedimentary rock, the primary building material for Angkor Wat's visible structures.
- **Theravada Buddhism**: A branch of Buddhism emphasizing monastic discipline and the pursuit of individual enlightenment, dominant in Cambodia.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Original Function**: Scholars debate whether Angkor Wat was primarily a state temple, a funerary temple for Suryavarman II, or both. Its westward orientation and counter-clockwise bas-reliefs support a funerary interpretation, while others attribute the orientation to its dedication to Vishnu.
- **Astronomical Alignment**: The temple's alignment with the sunrise on the spring equinox is generally accepted as intentional, but broader theories (e.g., representing the constellation Draco) are met with skepticism.
- **Residential Use**: Recent archaeological findings suggest the temple precinct may have housed a low-density population (3,000–4,300 people), challenging the view it was exclusively for the priestly elite.
- **Construction Route**: The exact route used to transport sandstone from Mount Kulen is debated; a shorter 35 km canal discovered via satellite imagery is a leading theory.
- **Tourism Impact**: There is ongoing controversy over balancing tourism revenue with the preservation of the site's structural integrity and the cultural values of local communities.
