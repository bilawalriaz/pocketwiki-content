# Selim III

## Overview
Selim III (r. 1789–1807) was an Ottoman sultan remembered as an enlightened reformer who attempted to modernize the empire's military and administration in the face of European pressure and internal decay. His ambitious "New Order" (Nizam-i Cedid) reforms threatened traditional power structures, leading to his deposition and assassination by the Janissaries. His reign highlights the central conflict between modernization and entrenched conservative forces in the late Ottoman Empire.

- **1797** — Establishes the Nizam-i Cedid ("New Order") army, drilled by European officers.
- **1 July 1798** — Napoleon invades Ottoman Egypt; Selim declares war on France.
- **1802** — Peace with France is concluded.
- **1806** — The Edirne Incident: Nizam-i Cedid forces clash with Janissaries and ayans, retreating from Rumelia.
- **1806** — Janissaries and conservative elites, led by the Sheikh ul-Islam's fatwa, revolt.
- **29 May 1807** — Deposed and imprisoned by the Janissaries; his cousin Mustafa IV is enthroned.
- **28 July 1808** — Assassinated in the palace by order of Mustafa IV.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Selim III was the son of Sultan Mustafa III, a reform-minded ruler who feared Russian invasion. After his father's death in 1774, Selim was raised by his uncle, Sultan Abdul Hamid I. Highly educated in languages, literature, and the arts, Selim ascended the throne in 1789 with expectations of reform, having had extensive contact with European ideas.

### Military and Administrative Reforms
Facing threats from Russia and Austria, Selim used the peace after 1792 to implement reforms. He abolished the military tenure of fiefs and, in 1797, created the Nizam-i Cedid, a new infantry corps of Anatolian peasant youths trained and equipped in the European style. This force, which grew to about 23,000 by 1806, was intended to bypass the corrupt and rebellious Janissaries. However, Selim failed to integrate it with the regular army or deploy it against domestic opponents, limiting its effectiveness.

### Foreign Relations and Crises
Selim's reign was marked by constant external pressure. The empire lost territory to Russia in earlier wars, and Napoleon's 1798 invasion of Egypt forced Selim into an alliance with Russia and Britain. In the Balkans, provincial governors like Osman Pazvantoğlu acted independently, and Serbian conditions deteriorated after the return of the Janissaries. Selim's attempt to assert central control through military reform was ultimately unsuccessful.

### Deposition and Assassination
The Janissaries, the powerful Derebeys (feudal vassals), and the ulama (religious scholars) saw the Nizam-i Cedid and other European-inspired reforms as a threat to their privileges. After the failed Edirne Incident of 1806, they revolted in 1807. A fatwa was issued accusing Selim of disrespecting Islamic tradition. He was deposed and imprisoned, and his successor, Mustafa IV, pledged to end the reforms. When a loyalist army marched to reinstate Selim, Mustafa IV ordered his assassination. Selim was stabbed to death on 28 July 1808, becoming the only Ottoman sultan killed by a sword.

### Patronage of Arts and Music
A cultured ruler, Selim III was a significant composer and poet (writing under the pen name İlhami). He created 14 melodic types (makams) for Turkish classical music and commissioned the Hamparsum notation system. He patronized artists and musicians, invited an opera troupe in 1797, and was a member of the Mevlevi Sufi order.

## Terms
- **Nizam-i Cedid**: "New Order," the modern infantry corps established by Selim III, trained by European officers and composed of Anatolian peasant youths.
- **Janissaries**: The elite infantry corps of the Ottoman Empire, which by Selim's time had become a conservative, privileged, and often rebellious force resistant to reform.
- **Derebeys**: Feudal lords or vassals who controlled provincial territories and revenues, often acting independently of the central government.
- **Ulama**: The class of Islamic religious scholars and jurists who held significant social and political influence.
- **Fatwa**: A ruling on a point of Islamic law given by a qualified religious authority.
- **Ayan**: Provincial notables or magnates who wielded local power, sometimes in alliance with or opposition to the central state.
- **Timar**: A grant of land revenue given to a military officer (sipahi) in exchange for service; Selim confiscated these to fund his new army.
- **Mevlevi Order**: A Sufi (mystical Islamic) order known for its "whirling dervish" ceremonies; Selim III was a member.

## Debates and Open Questions
The article presents a clear narrative of reform thwarted by conservative reaction. A central scholarly debate concerns the **effectiveness and intent of Selim's reforms**. Were they a genuine attempt at modernization to save the empire, or merely a conservative effort to restore central authority using new tools? The text notes his reforms were "intended to do no more than preserve the tradition of the Ottomans." Another open question is the **role of foreign influence**: while Selim engaged European instructors and diplomats, the article states French influence "did not revive" after Napoleon's invasion, yet he later joined Napoleon's Continental System, suggesting complex and shifting diplomatic calculations.
