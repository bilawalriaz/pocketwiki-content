# Yuan dynasty

## Overview
The Yuan dynasty (1271–1368) was a Mongol-led imperial dynasty of China, established by Kublai Khan. It was the first dynasty founded by a non-Han ethnicity to rule all of China proper, succeeding the Song dynasty and preceding the Ming dynasty. The Yuan represented the division of the Mongol Empire into separate khanates, with the Yuan emperors claiming nominal supremacy over the others. Its rule integrated Mongol and Chinese administrative traditions and facilitated significant cultural and technological exchange across Eurasia.

## Timeline
- **1206** — Genghis Khan unites Mongol tribes and is proclaimed Great Khan.
- **1234** — Mongols destroy the Jin dynasty, conquering northern China.
- **1259** — Möngke Khan dies, leading to a civil war between Kublai Khan and Ariq Böke.
- **1260** — Kublai Khan is elected Great Khan at a kurultai in Kaiping.
- **1264** — Ariq Böke surrenders, ending the civil war. Kublai moves the capital to Khanbaliq (modern Beijing).
- **1271** — Kublai formally proclaims the Great Yuan dynasty, claiming the Mandate of Heaven.
- **1276** — Yuan forces capture the Song capital of Hangzhou.
- **1279** — Battle of Yamen ends the Song dynasty, reunifying China.
- **1294** — Kublai Khan dies; succeeded by his grandson Temür Khan.
- **1315** — Imperial examinations are reintroduced under Emperor Ayurbarwada Buyantu Khan.
- **1351** — Red Turban Rebellion begins, marking the start of widespread uprisings.
- **1368** — Ming dynasty forces capture Khanbaliq; Yuan rulers retreat to Mongolia, beginning the Northern Yuan.

## Body

### Founding and Kublai Khan's Rule
Kublai Khan, grandson of Genghis Khan, became Great Khan in 1260 after a civil war. He adopted Chinese administrative models while maintaining Mongol leadership. In 1271, he formally established the Yuan dynasty, claiming succession from earlier Chinese dynasties. The dynasty's name, "Great Yuan," derives from the *I Ching* and signifies "primal force." Kublai completed the conquest of the Southern Song in 1279, reunifying China. His reign promoted trade along the Silk Road, expanded the Grand Canal, and welcomed foreign visitors like Marco Polo. However, costly military campaigns against Japan, Vietnam, and others strained the treasury.

### Government and Social Structure
The Yuan government was a hybrid of Mongol and Chinese systems. The Central Secretariat managed civil administration, while a stratified four-class system ranked the population: Mongols, Semu (Central Asian foreigners), Han (northern Chinese), and Southerners (former Song subjects). This hierarchy was based on loyalty and timing of surrender, not strictly ethnicity. The imperial examinations were suspended until 1315, when they were reintroduced with quotas favoring Mongols and Semu. The Yuan employed foreign administrators across its empire to prevent local power consolidation.

### Culture, Religion, and Technology
The Yuan period saw rich cultural exchange due to the Pax Mongolica. Tibetan Buddhism became the state religion, administered by the Bureau of Buddhist and Tibetan Affairs. Islam, Christianity, and other faiths were also practiced, though with varying degrees of tolerance. Technological advances included the development of blue-and-white porcelain, improvements in printing (including paper money), and significant work in mathematics and astronomy by figures like Guo Shoujing. The dynasty also facilitated the spread of crops, medical knowledge, and artistic styles between East and West.

### Decline and Fall
The dynasty weakened due to internal strife, financial mismanagement, and natural disasters from the late 1340s. The Red Turban Rebellion in 1351 escalated into a nationwide uprising. The Ming dynasty, founded by Zhu Yuanzhang, captured the capital in 1368. The Yuan court retreated to Mongolia, continuing as the Northern Yuan until 1635. The Ming adopted some Yuan administrative practices, such as the garrison system.

## Terms
- **Kurultai**: A Mongol assembly to elect a Great Khan or make major decisions.
- **Mandate of Heaven**: The traditional Chinese belief that heaven grants a dynasty the right to rule.
- **Semu**: A class of non-Mongol foreigners from Central and Western Asia who served the Yuan.
- **Kheshig**: The Mongol imperial guard, operating outside regular government structures.
- **'Phags-pa script**: A unified script created in 1269 for writing Mongolian, Tibetan, and Chinese.
- **Jiaochao**: Paper money issued by the Yuan government, printed on mulberry bark.
- **Tusi**: Local chieftains in southwestern China recognized as imperial officials by the Yuan.
- **Pax Mongolica**: The period of relative peace and stability across the Mongol Empire, facilitating trade and exchange.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The existence and nature of the four-class system is debated. Some historians argue it was a rigid social hierarchy, while others, like Frederick W. Mote, contend it defined legal privileges rather than social power or wealth. Japanese scholars have questioned the very existence of the Semu as a distinct class.
- The degree of sinicization among Yuan emperors is debated. While some, like Tugh Temür, actively patronized Chinese culture, others remained more attached to Mongol traditions.
- The legitimacy of Yuan rule in Chinese historiography is contested. Traditional narratives often view it as a legitimate dynasty, but many Han Chinese at the time saw it as a period of foreign domination.
