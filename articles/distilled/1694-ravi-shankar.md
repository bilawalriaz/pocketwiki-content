# Ravi Shankar

## Overview
Pandit Ravi Shankar (1920–2012) was an Indian sitarist and composer who became the world’s most recognized exponent of Hindustani classical music in the second half of the 20th century. After rigorous training under court musician Allauddin Khan, he composed for film and radio in India before touring the West, where his collaborations with violinist Yehudi Menuhin and Beatles guitarist George Harrison catalyzed a global fascination with Indian music and instruments. Shankar systematized a distinctive sitar style, introduced over 30 new ragas (melodic frameworks), and pioneered the *jugalbandi* (duet) concert format. He served in India’s Parliament (1986–1992) and received the Bharat Ratna, India’s highest civilian honor, alongside four Grammy Awards and an Academy Award nomination.

## Body

### Early Life and Gurukul Training
Born in Benares as the youngest of seven brothers, Shankar spent his first decade there before joining his brother Uday’s dance troupe in Paris at age 10. Touring Europe and America through the 1930s, he learned French, absorbed Western classical music and jazz, and encountered Allauddin Khan, the lead musician of the Maihar court, at a 1934 conference. In 1938, with his parents deceased and war looming, Shankar abandoned dancing to enter Khan’s *gurukul* (traditional residential discipleship) in Maihar. For seven years he studied sitar, surbahar (bass sitar), and vocal styles—*dhrupad*, *dhamar*, *khyal*—alongside Khan’s children, Ali Akbar Khan and Annapurna Devi. This immersion established the technical and aesthetic foundation for his later innovations.

### Career in India: Radio, Film, and Orchestral Fusion
Completing training in 1944, Shankar moved to Mumbai, composing for the Indian People’s Theatre Association and recomposing the patriotic song "Sare Jahan Se Achcha." As Music Director of All India Radio (1949–1956), he founded the Indian National Orchestra, deliberately blending Western orchestral instrumentation with classical Indian structures. He scored Satyajit Ray’s internationally acclaimed *Apu Trilogy* (mid-1950s) and several Hindi films, establishing a reputation as a composer who could bridge traditions.

### International Breakthrough and the "Harrison Effect"
Introduced to Yehudi Menuhin in 1952, Shankar toured the West from 1956, educating audiences on *raga* (melodic framework) and *tala* (rhythmic cycle). His 1966 meeting with George Harrison—prompted by The Byrds hearing Shankar’s recordings—triggered a cultural phenomenon. Harrison’s study with Shankar in Srinagar (1968) and use of the sitar on "Norwegian Wood" launched the "raga rock" trend, influencing the Rolling Stones, the Byrds, and blues artists like Michael Bloomfield. Shankar’s 1967 Monterey Pop Festival performance and 1969 Woodstock appearance brought him mass exposure, but he publicly recoiled from the drug culture surrounding the scene, insisting Indian music was a spiritual discipline, not recreation.

### Concerts, Concertos, and Institutional Roles
Shankar composed two concertos for sitar and orchestra: *Concerto for Sitar & Orchestra* (1970, London Symphony, André Previn) and *Raga Mala* (1981, Zubin Mehta). The 1971 Concert for Bangladesh, organized by Harrison, yielded a Grammy-winning live album. Despite a heart attack during a 1974 North American tour with Harrison, Shankar maintained a rigorous global schedule through the 1980s, including a massive 1988 Moscow concert with 140 musicians. Nominated by Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi, he served in the Rajya Sabha (1986–1992). He collaborated with minimalist composer Philip Glass on *Passages* (1990) and continued performing annually into the 2000s, often with daughter Anoushka.

### Style, Innovations, and Pedagogy
Shankar synthesized a unique style: performances opened with *alap*, *jor*, *jhala* (unmetered, pulsed, and fast-pulsed introductions) rooted in the austere *dhrupad* genre, followed by *khyal*-style compositions with tabla accompaniment, often closing in light-classical *thumri*. He popularized the bass octave for *alap*, developed a signature middle-register technique using rapid string deviations and percussive stops, and favored unconventional rhythmic cycles. He introduced at least 31 new ragas (e.g., *Nat Bhairav*, *Bairagi*, *Gangeshwari*) and, in 2011, a percussive "Goonga Sitar" technique (strings muffled with cloth). His interplay with tabla maestro Alla Rakha elevated the drum’s status in Hindustani music. He founded the Kinnara School of Music in Mumbai (1962) and Los Angeles (1967), and taught at institutions including CalArts (chair, Department of Indian Music, 1970) and UC San Diego (Regents’ Professor, 1997).

### Personal Life and Legacy
Shankar married Annapurna Devi (1941); their son Shubhendra (1942) toured with him but died in 1992. Relationships with Kamala Shastri (1940s–1981), Sue Jones (mother of Norah Jones, b. 1979), and Sukanya Rajan (mother of Anoushka Shankar, b. 1981; married 1989) overlapped. A Hindu devotee of Hanuman and Sri Anandamayi Ma, he lived his final decades in Encinitas, California. Daughters Norah Jones (10 Grammys) and Anoushka Shankar (multiple nominations) became acclaimed musicians. Shankar’s final concert was November 4, 2012; he died December 11, 2012.

## Terms
- ****Sitar**** — A long-necked, fretted plucked lute with sympathetic strings, central to Hindustani classical music.
- ****Surbahar**** — A bass sitar, lower in pitch, used for serious *dhrupad*-style *alap*.
- ****Raga**** — A melodic framework for improvisation and composition, defined by specific note sets, phrases, and mood.
- ****Tala**** — A cyclic rhythmic framework (e.g., 16-beat *tintal*) structuring time in Indian classical music.
- ****Gurukul**** — Traditional residential discipleship system where the student lives with the guru (teacher).
- ****Dhrupad / Khyal / Thumri**** — Major Hindustani vocal genres: *dhrupad* (austere, slow), *khyal* (elaborate, dominant), *thumri* (light-classical, romantic).
- ****Alap / Jor / Jhala**** — Structural sections of a raga performance: unmetered introduction (*alap*), pulsed (*jor*), fast-pulsed (*jhala*).
- ****Jugalbandi**** — A duet concert format featuring two soloists (often different instruments) in dialogue.
- ****All India Radio (AIR)**** — India’s national public broadcaster; Shankar directed its New Delhi station and founded its National Orchestra.
- ****Rajya Sabha**** — The upper house of India’s Parliament; Shankar served as a nominated member (1986–1992).

## Debates and open questions
- **Stylistic adoption:** Hans Neuhoff argued Shankar’s highly individualized playing style—especially his rapid string deviations and rhythmic novelties—was not widely adopted by subsequent sitarists, who favored more lyrical melodic approaches.
- **Western popularization vs. tradition:** Shankar’s association with the 1960s counterculture (Monterey, Woodstock) brought unprecedented visibility but troubled him; he explicitly rejected the equation of Indian classical music with drug use, framing it instead as a spiritual path.
- **Fusion authenticity:** His orchestral concertos and collaborations (Menuhin, Glass, Harrison) expanded the sitar’s repertoire but raised perennial questions about the integrity of *raga* syntax when harmonized or placed in Western formal structures.
- **Pedagogical lineage:** While Shankar trained countless students (including daughters Anoushka and Norah Jones’s early exposure), the transmission of his specific *baaj* (playing style) and numerous invented ragas remains a subject of musicological tracking.
