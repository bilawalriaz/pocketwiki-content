# Battle of Marathon

## Overview
The Battle of Marathon (490 BC) was a pivotal conflict in the first Persian invasion of Greece, where a smaller Athenian-led force defeated a much larger Persian army. This victory was a turning point in the Greco-Persian Wars, proving Persian invincibility was a myth and boosting Athenian confidence and prestige, which helped lay the foundation for the Classical Greek civilization.

## Body

### Background and Causes
The first Persian invasion was a direct response to Athenian and Eretrian support for the Ionian Revolt, which had threatened Persian imperial integrity. After the revolt was crushed, King Darius I sought to punish the Greek cities and secure his empire's western frontier. Athens had become hostile to Persia after refusing to accept the return of the exiled tyrant Hippias, who had sought Persian aid. The Persian expedition of 490 BC, commanded by Datis and Artaphernes, was tasked with subjugating the Cyclades and then destroying Eretria and Athens.

### Prelude to Battle
After capturing Eretria, the Persians landed at Marathon on the advice of Hippias. The Athenian army, led by General Miltiades, marched to block the exits from the plain. They sent the runner Pheidippides to Sparta for aid, but the Spartans refused to march during a religious festival. The Athenians were reinforced by 1,000 hoplites from Plataea. For about five days, the armies faced each other in a stalemate. The reason for the eventual battle is debated: one theory suggests the Persian cavalry left, prompting an Athenian attack; another suggests the Persians initiated combat.

### The Battle
The Athenian line, about 1,500 meters from the Persians, was arranged with a thin center and strong flanks. Miltiades ordered an advance, with the hoplites running the final 200 meters to minimize exposure to Persian archers. The Persian center, containing elite troops like the Persians and Sakae, pushed back the weaker Greek center. However, the superior Athenian and Plataean flanks routed their opponents and then turned inward to envelop the Persian center. The Persians broke and fled to their ships, suffering heavy losses. Herodotus records 6,400 Persian dead versus 192 Athenian and 11 Plataean dead.

### Aftermath and Significance
Following their defeat, the Persian fleet attempted to sail around Cape Sounion to attack undefended Athens, but the Athenian army marched back in time to thwart the landing. The Spartans arrived the next day. The victory was a watershed moment: it proved the Persians could be beaten, boosted Athenian morale and prestige, and demonstrated the effectiveness of the hoplite phalanx against lighter-armed troops. While the Persian Empire remained powerful, Marathon delayed its plans for conquest and inspired Greek resistance, culminating in later victories at Salamis and Plataea.

## Terms
- **Hoplite**: A heavily armed ancient Greek infantryman, equipped with a large round shield, spear, and bronze armor.
- **Phalanx**: A rectangular battle formation of hoplites, presenting a solid front of shields and spears.
- **Trireme**: A Greek warship with three banks of oars, the dominant naval vessel of the era.
- **Ionian Revolt (499–494 BC)**: An unsuccessful uprising of Greek city-states in Ionia (modern-day Turkey) against Persian rule, which triggered the Greco-Persian Wars.
- **Strategos**: An Athenian general; ten were elected annually, one from each tribe.
- **Polemarch**: The war archon of Athens, a senior magistrate with military command.
- **Satrap**: A provincial governor in the Persian Empire.
- **Achaemenid Empire**: The Persian Empire founded by Cyrus the Great, which ruled from 550 to 330 BC.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Reason for the Battle**: Scholars debate why the Athenians attacked before Spartan reinforcements arrived. Theories include the departure of the Persian cavalry or a Persian offensive move.
- **Troop Numbers**: Ancient sources give wildly varying figures for the Persian army (from 200,000 to 600,000). Modern estimates for the Persian infantry range from 20,000 to 100,000, with a consensus around 25,000.
- **Date of the Battle**: The conventional date is September 12, 490 BC, but it could be August 12 if the Spartan calendar was a month ahead of Athens's.
- **Herodotus's Account**: While the primary source, some details in Herodotus, like the rotating generalship, are considered problematic or inaccurate by modern historians.
