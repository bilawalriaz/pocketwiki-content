# Military history

## Overview
Military history is the study of armed conflict and its impact on societies, economies, and international relations. It analyzes causes of war, doctrine, logistics, leadership, technology, strategy, and tactics to understand how these elements evolve. As an applied field, it is taught at military academies to help commanders recognize historical parallels and avoid past mistakes, guided by the motto "Past is Prologue." The discipline is dynamic, driven by rapid technological shifts such as the Industrial Revolution, the nuclear age, and the information age, often conceptualized through the "Revolution in Military Affairs" (RMA)—short bursts of rapid change followed by stability. Despite immense public popularity, academic military history remains marginalized in university history departments, a divide noted by scholars like William H. McNeill.

## Timeline
- **c. 3150 BC** — Confrontation between Upper and Lower Egypt; earliest documented military history.
- **c. 2700 BC** — Conflict between Sumer and Elam near modern Basra.
- **15th century BC** — Battle of Megiddo recorded by Egyptian scribe Tjaneni; first battle in relatively reliable detail.
- **484–425 BC** — Herodotus writes *The Histories*; Thucydides writes *History of the Peloponnesian War*, establishing critical historiographical methods.
- **c. 500 BC** — Crossbow developed in China.
- **10th century** — Gunpowder developments in China lead to new weapons; used in siege warfare against Mongols by mid-13th century.
- **Early 14th century** — Cannons first used in Europe.
- **1453** — Fall of Constantinople to Ottoman artillery; massive cannons fire 800 lb projectiles.
- **1575** — Oda Nobunaga implements volley fire (countermarch) tactics in Japan, decades before Europe.
- **1648–1789** — *Kabinettskriege* (Princes' warfare) period: limited wars by monarchical states relying on mercenaries.
- **1789** — French Revolution; rise of conscription and total war concepts.
- **1903** — Wright Brothers achieve first controlled, powered flight.
- **1915** — First large-scale use of lethal chemical warfare (chlorine gas) by Germans at Second Battle of Ypres.
- **1945** — Atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki; end of WWII and start of nuclear age.
- **1955** — First nuclear submarine launched; enables underwater missile platforms and nuclear triad.

## Body

### Definition, Scope, and Professional Status
Military history examines the causes of war, social and cultural foundations, doctrine, logistics, leadership, technology, strategy, and tactics. Just war theory addresses the moral dimensions, seeking a doctrine of military ethics. The field is applied at service schools to instill "historical mindedness"—the ability to perceive parallels during battle—prioritizing themes and context over rote memorization. However, a sharp divide exists between popular and academic history. William H. McNeill (1982) described the field as an "intellectual ghetto," split between popular works by journalists and professional work produced within the military establishment. University historians often display disdain for the subject, though courses remain popular with students. The Society for Military History (founded 1937) provides a scholarly home, publishing *The Journal of Military History* and boasting ~2,300 members.

### Historiography and Methodological Challenges
Historiography—the study of historical method—is essential because battle descriptions by leaders are often unreliable, tending to minimize failure and exaggerate success. Jeremy Black identifies inherited biases: Eurocentricity, technological determinism, focus on dominant powers/systems, separation of land/sea/air domains, focus on state-to-state conflict, and neglect of political "tasking" (how forces are used). Records are frequently missing—destroyed or classified as military secrets (e.g., Greek fire composition, records from Operations Enduring Freedom and Iraqi Freedom). Jeffrey Kimball (early 1980s) found historians' political views moderately correlate with their interpretations of 20th-century war causes; military historians surveyed were 40% Conservative, 35% Liberal, 18% None, 8% Other, 0% Socialist.

### Early Historiography and Foundational Texts
Documentation begins with Egypt (c. 3150 BC) and Sumer/Elam (c. 2700 BC). The Battle of Megiddo (15th c. BC) is the first reliably detailed battle, recorded by scribe Tjaneni. Heroic epics (Gilgamesh, Iliad, Mahabharata) contain military detail but challenged historicity. More credible Israelite records appear in Biblical books post-Joshua. Greek historiography establishes critical standards: Herodotus ("father of history"), Thucydides (impartial, document-based, eyewitness interviews), Xenophon (leader-centric *Anabasis*), and Polybius. Roman historians (Sallust, Livy, Appian, Cassius Dio) chronicled Rome's rise. Julius Caesar’s commentaries (*Gallic War*, *Civil War*) allow comparative campaign analysis. In the East, India’s *Arthashastra* and China’s *Art of War*, *Book of Lord Shang*, and *Guanzi* codified strategic doctrine during the Axial Age. Sima Qian’s *Records of the Grand Historian* details the Warring States and Qin unification.

### Technological Evolution: Ancient Era
Chariots (c. 2000 BC) provided mobile missile platforms, crucial to New Kingdom Egypt, Shang China, and Zhou states. Infantry became the core for agrarian civilizations: Greeks/Romans used rigid phalanxes; Macedonians adopted sarissa pikes; Romans later switched to flexible maniples. Cavalry proved decisive at Cannae (216 BC) and Carrhae (53 BC). Steppe peoples (Parthians, Scythians, Mongols) mastered horse archery. By 3rd–4th century AD, heavily armored cataphracts/clibanarii spread across Parthia, Sassanid Persia, Byzantium, and Han/Three Kingdoms China. War elephants originated in India, adopted by Persians and Carthage (Hannibal); Chandragupta Maurya gifted 500 to Seleucus I. Naval warfare evolved from Cretan galleys (3rd millennium BC) to Greek triremes (Salamis, 480 BC). Romans invented the *corvus* boarding bridge (First Punic War). Vikings developed the *drakkar* (8th c. AD); Song China invented watertight bulkheads (12th c. AD); Han China invented rudders (2nd c. BC). Fortifications (hill-forts, Roman rectangular forts) drove siege engine development.

### Technological Evolution: Middle Ages to Gunpowder
The stirrup (Dark Ages) revolutionized cavalry, enabling heavy shock combat. Crossbows (China, c. 500 BC) and English/Welsh longbows (12th c.) dominated battlefields (Crécy, Agincourt). Gunpowder evolved slowly in China (4th c. alchemical experiments → 10th c. weapons → 13th c. siege incendiaries). Joseph Needham notes China developed fire lances, bombs, rockets, and metal-barrel handguns first; transmission to Islam and West remains mysterious. Cannons appear in Europe early 14th c. (stone balls, welded cylinders); used at Crécy (1346) and Agincourt (1415). Firearms evolved: fire lance → hand cannon → matchlock arquebus (mid-15th c.) → wheel/snap/flintlock mechanisms (c. 1500–early 17th c.). Fire ships (16th c.) scattered Spanish Armada (1588). Naval mines (17th c.) saw mass use in US Civil War and WWI/II. Submarines: Drebbel (1624), Peral (1885), Bushnell’s *Turtle* (Revolution), Fulton’s *Nautilus*. Howitzers (17th c.) fired high-trajectory shells. Bayonets (Bayonne, 16th c.) enabled infantry hand-to-hand combat. Balloons (1783) allowed aerial observation. Tipu Sultan used iron-cased rockets vs. British (late 18th c.); William Hale improved accuracy (1844). Rifles advanced 1860s (Winchester repeaters, Springfields); machine guns late 19th c.; automatic rifles/light MGs early 20th c. Self-propelled torpedo (late 19th c.); *HNoMS Rap* first torpedo boat.

### Early Guns and Artillery in Europe
The fire lance (China, 10th–11th c., bamboo then metal barrels) is the gun’s predecessor. Earliest European evidence: Holkham Hall Milemete manuscript illustration (1326) of "pot-shaped gun" firing arrows; archaeological "gun arrows" at Eltz Castle (feud 1331–36). Oldest surviving European gun: Loshult gun (14th c., bronze, 11.8 in.), replica tested to 1300 m range but inaccurate past 200 m. French records (1338) show cannons on ships at Rouen firing iron bolts. Philip the Bold (1363–1404) built Burgundy’s artillery power via mass manufacturing; used 450 lb cannonballs to capture Odruik. Mehmed the Conqueror mass-produced massive cannons (800 lb balls) to breach Constantinople’s walls in 55 days (1453).

### Early Firearm Tactics: The Military Revolution in East Asia
Michael Roberts identifies a "Military Revolution" (late 16th c. Europe) driven by volley fire—infantry ranks alternating loading/firing. However, evidence shows earlier adoption in East Asia. Korea: King Sejong ordered "fire barrels" fired in squads of five (1447). Japan: Oda Nobunaga implemented volleying/countermarch drills successfully in 1575, 20 years before Europe (Lord William Louis of Nassau, mid-1590s). During the Imjin War (1592–98, "First Great East Asian War"), Japanese firepower overwhelmed Korean arrow volleys; post-war Korean reforms adopted volley techniques via Ming General Qi Jiguang’s manuals. Qi emphasized repetitive drilling, small-group tactics, psychological warfare, and synthesizing cohesive units from specialized sub-groups.

### Modern Technologies: World Wars to Nuclear Age
WWI introduced flamethrowers, armored cars (French, 1902), armored troop carriers (British, 1918), and tanks (British/French superiority vs. few German A7Vs). Aviation: Wright Brothers (1903); first ship takeoff (1911); carrier development; aces in WWI. Chemical warfare: Germans used non-lethal gas at Bolimov (Jan 1915), lethal chlorine at Second Ypres (Apr 1915); gas masks neutralized it; banned 1920s. WWII: strategic bombing, carrier warfare (Midway), radar (independent Allied/Axis invention), Molotov cocktails (Spanish Civil War), atomic bomb (Manhattan Project, 1945). Cold War: nuclear arms race (A-bombs, H-bombs), space race, spy satellites, ballistic/cruise missiles, nuclear submarines (1955, enabling quiet underwater missile platforms and nuclear triad).

### Periods of Military History

**Prehistoric Warfare:** Conflict without written records. Tollense Valley (13th c. BC) is Europe’s oldest large-scale battlefield (>4,000 warriors).

**Ancient Warfare:** Military history dominates ancient records because states required force to maintain control, weapons/armor survive archaeologically, and elites commemorated conquests. Key powers: Egypt, Assyria, Babylon, Persia, Greeks (Sparta, Macedon), India (Magadha, Chola), China (Qin, Han), Xiongnu, Rome, Carthage. Mesopotamia saw sequential conquests (Sumerians, Akkadians, Babylonians, Assyrians, Persians). Iranians first introduced cavalry. Egypt fell to successive invaders. India: Battle of Ten Kings; *Arthashastra* details espionage/elephants; Alexander defeated Porus (Hydaspes); Chandragupta Maurya unified North; Ashoka unified South Asia post-Kalinga. China: Shang/Zhou collapse → Warring States → Qin unification (Qin Shi Huang) → Han expansion (Silk Road, conflict with Xiongnu) → Three Kingdoms collapse (3rd c. AD). Persia: Achaemenids (Cyrus, Cambyses) → Alexander → Seleucids → Parthians/Sassanids (Roman rivals). Greece: Persian Wars (Marathon, Salamis, Plataea) → Peloponnesian War (Athens vs. Sparta, plague, Syracuse disaster, Aegospotami) → Macedonian hegemony (Philip II, Alexander) → fragmentation. Rome: Republic (Punic Wars: naval 1st, Hannibal/Scipio 2nd, Carthage destroyed 3rd) → Mediterranean dominance (Macedon, Near East, Gaul, Germanic tribes) → Parthian disaster (Carrhae, 54 BC) → Civil wars (Marius/Sulla, Caesar/Pompey, Octavian/Antony) → Empire (Octavian) → Peak (Marcus Aurelius) → Decline, West falls 476 AD, Byzantium survives.

**Medieval Warfare:** Stirrups transform cavalry; feudalism and castles dominate Europe. Islamic expansion: Rashidun/Umayyads (Middle East, N. Africa, Iberia, Indus) → stopped at Tours (Franks) and by Byzantium/Bulgaria; Abbasids defeat Tang at Talas (751) → fall to Seljuks/Mongols → Baghdad 1258. China: Sui (reunify, fail Korea/Vietnam) → Tang (Turks, Tibetans, collapse via *jiedushi*) → Song (Greek fire, gunpowder vs. Jurchens). Mongols (Genghis → Kublai) conquer Eurasia (China, Persia, Russia); Yuan dynasty fragments. Japan: Medieval period extends to 19th c. Africa: Sahel/Sudan states (Sennar, Fulani) use medieval tactics post-Europe. New Zealand: Māori warrior culture; Moriori pacifism leads to 1830s massacre. Hawaii: Unified under Kamehameha I (1780s–90s).

**Early Modern Warfare:** Gunpowder spreads Song China → Ottomans → Safavids/Mughals → Europe (arquebus, Italian Wars). Ends armored cavalry dominance; decline of feudalism allows professional standing armies. Africa: Ahmad ibn Ibrihim al-Ghazi first uses gunpowder in Ethiopian–Adal War (1529–1543). *Kabinettskriege* (1648–1789): limited cabinet wars, mercenaries, shifting alliances. 18th–19th c.: mobile field artillery, open-order infantry, rifles replace muskets, dragoons replace cavalry.

**Military Revolution (Concept):** Michael Roberts (1950s) argues 1560–1660 (Sweden/Dutch) saw musket volley tactics → need for standing armies → larger forces → new fiscal-administrative institutions → modern state. Geoffrey Parker (1990s) emphasizes fortification/siege warfare (trace italienne). Critics (Duffy, Black) call it misleading, exaggerated, simplistic; current models favor slow evolution with technology playing minor role vs. organization/logistics.

**Industrial Warfare:** Conscription replaces professional reliance (Napoleon, World Wars). Technology disparities decide outcomes (Sadowa). Total war: Napoleon innovates; Sherman’s March, Sheridan’s Valley (US Civil War); strategic bombing (WWII).

**Modern Warfare (Post-1945):** Technological arms races (nuclear, biological, cyber, space). Tank tech matures (composite armor, advanced targeting). Distinctive decline in interstate wars/casualties; fighting shifts to civil wars/insurgencies. Exceptions: Indo-Pakistani 1971, Iran–Iraq 1980–88, Gulf War 1990–91, Russo-Ukrainian War.

## Terms
- ****Historiography**** — The study of the history and method of the discipline of history; used in military history to assess conflicts by analyzing source reliability and bias.
- ****Revolution in Military Affairs (RMA)**** — A concept explaining how emerging technologies (e.g., gunpowder) shape warfare via short bursts of rapid change followed by periods of stability.
- ****Military Revolution**** — A debated schema (Roberts, 1950s) arguing that early modern European advances in technology, finance, and administration transformed armies and created the modern state.
- ****Kabinettskriege**** — "Princes' warfare" (1648–1789): limited wars waged by monarchical states, decided by cabinets, relying on mercenaries and shifting alliances.
- ****Total War**** — Warfare targeting an enemy's entire capacity to fight (civilian economy, infrastructure), pioneered by Napoleon and exemplified by WWII strategic bombing.
- ****Volley Fire / Countermarch**** — Tactical drill where infantry ranks alternate loading and firing to maintain continuous firepower; first documented in Korea (1447), Japan (1575), Europe (1590s).
- ****Trace Italienne**** — Low, thick, angled bastion fortifications developed in early modern Europe to withstand cannon fire; central to Parker’s Military Revolution thesis.
- ****Nuclear Triad**** — A three-pronged nuclear force structure (land-based missiles, strategic bombers, submarine-launched missiles) enabled by nuclear submarines (1955).
- ****Just War Theory**** — A doctrine exploring the moral dimensions of warfare to establish military ethics and limit war's destructiveness.
- ****Fire Lance**** — The predecessor of the gun, invented in China (10th–11th c.), initially using bamboo barrels to project flames and shrapnel.

## Debates and open questions
- **Academic Marginalization:** Why military history remains an "orphan" in university history departments despite public popularity and pedagogical value.
- **Military Revolution Validity:** Whether the concept (Roberts/Parker) accurately explains early modern transformation or is a misleading, simplistic construct; current consensus favors slow evolutionary models.
- **Historiographical Biases:** How to overcome inherited Eurocentricity, technological determinism, focus on state-on-state conflict, and neglect of political "tasking" and non-Western systems.
- **Source Limitations:** The persistent inability to access classified records (e.g., recent US operations) or reconstruct lost secrets (e.g., Greek fire), creating permanent gaps in the record.
- **Political Influence on Interpretation:** The demonstrated correlation (Kimball) between historians' contemporary political ideologies and their causal explanations for 20th-century wars.
- **Gunpowder Transmission:** The exact time and route of gunpowder/firearm technology migration from China to the Islamic world and Europe remains a mystery.
- **Periodization Boundaries:** Debates over dating (e.g., whether Japanese "medieval" warfare extends to the 19th century) and the applicability of Western periodization globally.
