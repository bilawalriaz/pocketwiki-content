# John Bardeen

## Overview
John Bardeen (1908–1991) was an American physicist and the only person to win the Nobel Prize in Physics twice. His work on the transistor launched the Information Age, and his theory of superconductivity (BCS theory) explained a fundamental quantum phenomenon, with applications in medical imaging and quantum computing. His career highlights the collaborative nature of major scientific breakthroughs and the personal dynamics behind them.

## Body

### Education and Early Career
Bardeen earned bachelor's and master's degrees in electrical engineering from the University of Wisconsin before switching to physics. He worked as a geophysicist for Gulf Oil before pursuing a Ph.D. in mathematical physics at Princeton, studying under Eugene Wigner. After a fellowship at Harvard, he became an assistant professor at the University of Minnesota and later led a group at the Naval Ordnance Laboratory during World War II.

### The Transistor at Bell Labs
In 1945, Bardeen joined a solid-state physics group at Bell Labs led by William Shockley, tasked with finding a solid-state alternative to vacuum tubes. Initial experiments based on Shockley's field-effect theory failed. Bardeen proposed that "surface states"—electronic states at the semiconductor's surface—were blocking the electric field. This insight redirected the research. On December 23, 1947, Bardeen and Walter Brattain created the first point-contact transistor, achieving amplification. The transistor was a tiny, reliable, low-power device that replaced vacuum tubes, enabling the miniaturization of electronics. Shockley publicly claimed most credit, straining his relationship with Bardeen and Brattain. The three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize.

### BCS Theory and Superconductivity
After leaving Bell Labs in 1951, Bardeen joined the University of Illinois. There, he collaborated with Leon Cooper and John Robert Schrieffer to develop the BCS theory (1957), the first successful microscopic theory of superconductivity. The theory explains how electrons form "Cooper pairs" and move without resistance through a material. This work earned Bardeen his second Nobel Prize in 1972, making him the first person to win two Nobel Prizes in the same field. Bardeen strategically nominated researchers in superconducting tunneling (like the Josephson effect) to increase the chances of the BCS theory being recognized.

### Later Work and the Josephson Effect
In the 1960s, Bardeen initially challenged Brian Josephson's prediction of a supercurrent tunneling effect, but later accepted it after experimental proof. He continued research into charge density waves (CDWs) at Illinois, proposing they were a collective quantum phenomenon—a view later supported by experiments. He remained active in research until shortly before his death.

## Terms
- **Transistor:** A semiconductor device used to amplify or switch electronic signals and electrical power. It is the fundamental building block of modern electronics.
- **Superconductivity:** A phenomenon where a material exhibits exactly zero electrical resistance and expels magnetic fields when cooled below a critical temperature.
- **BCS Theory:** The first successful microscopic theory of superconductivity, proposed by Bardeen, Cooper, and Schrieffer. It explains superconductivity as a macroscopic quantum phenomenon involving Cooper pairs.
- **Cooper Pair:** A pair of electrons (or other fermions) that are bound together at low temperatures in a superconductor, enabling resistance-free current flow.
- **Surface States:** Electronic states found at the surface of a material, which can affect its electrical properties and, as Bardeen proposed, block electric fields in semiconductors.
- **Point-Contact Transistor:** The first type of transistor, invented by Bardeen and Brattain, using a sharp metal point in contact with a semiconductor.
- **Josephson Effect:** The phenomenon of electric current flowing across a weak link between two superconductors without any applied voltage, predicted by Brian Josephson.
- **Charge Density Wave (CDW):** A quantum phenomenon in some materials where electrons form a periodic modulation of charge density, which can conduct electricity.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Credit for the Transistor:** William Shockley publicly took the lion's share of credit for the transistor invention, leading to a deterioration of his relationship with Bardeen and Brattain. Bell Labs management, however, presented all three as a team.
- **Josephson Effect Controversy:** Bardeen initially challenged Brian Josephson's theoretical prediction of supercurrent tunneling, but publicly withdrew his opposition in 1963 after experimental evidence confirmed the effect.
- **Nobel Prize Strategy:** Bardeen was concerned the Nobel Committee might not award the BCS theory due to his already having a Nobel Prize. He strategically nominated researchers in superconducting tunneling to increase the BCS theory's chances of recognition.
