# Biography

## Overview
A biography is a detailed account of a person's life that goes beyond basic facts to portray the subject's experiences and personality. It is a foundational literary genre that has evolved from ancient commemorative inscriptions to modern multimedia forms, reflecting changing historical, psychological, and cultural perspectives on individual lives.

## Timeline
- **c. 26th century BC** — Earliest biographical texts appear in ancient Egypt as tomb inscriptions.
- **21st century BC** — The Epic of Gilgamesh, a famous biography, is composed in Mesopotamia.
- **c. 430–354 BC** — Xenophon writes the biography of Cyrus the Great.
- **44 BC** — Cornelius Nepos publishes *Lives of Outstanding Generals*.
- **c. 80 AD** — Plutarch publishes *Parallel Lives*, pairing Greek and Roman biographies.
- **c. 121 AD** — Suetonius writes *De vita Caesarum* (Lives of the Caesars).
- **1550** — Giorgio Vasari's *Lives of the Artists* becomes a landmark secular biography.
- **1791** — James Boswell's *The Life of Samuel Johnson* establishes the modern biography.
- **1918** — Lytton Strachey's *Eminent Victorians* revolutionizes biographical writing.
- **1970** — Nancy Milford's *Zelda* marks a turning point in women's biography.
- **2001** — Ralph Ueltzhoeffer creates "Biography-Portraits," an early multimedia form.

## Body

### Origins and Early History
Biography is the earliest literary genre, beginning with ancient Egyptian funerary inscriptions that recounted the careers of officials. The genre developed through Mesopotamian, Hebrew, Greek, and Roman works, with authors like Xenophon, Plutarch, and Suetonius creating influential collections. During the early Middle Ages, biographical writing in Europe was largely controlled by the Roman Catholic Church, focusing on saints and church figures (hagiography), while secular examples like Einhard's life of Charlemagne also appeared. Parallel developments occurred in other cultures, such as the Jain *Prabandhas* in India and early Islamic biographical dictionaries.

### Emergence as a Distinct Genre
The independent genre of biography emerged in the 18th century, moving away from being a subsection of history. James Boswell's *The Life of Samuel Johnson* (1791) is considered the first modern biography, setting a standard with its extensive research, narrative style, and honest depiction of the subject. The 19th century saw a proliferation of biographies due to an expanding reading public and cheaper printing, though the quality often stagnated. Autobiographies also gained popularity during this period.

### Modern and Contemporary Biography
The 20th century brought significant changes, influenced by psychology and sociology. Biographers began to emphasize childhood, personality, and environmental factors over the "great man" theory. Lytton Strachey's *Eminent Victorians* (1918) pioneered a concise, irreverent, and myth-debunking style. This led to a biographical "boom" in the 1920s and 30s, with both literary and popular "celebrity voyeurism" forms. In the latter half of the century, feminist scholars like Carolyn Heilbrun reshaped women's biography, and political biography evolved to focus more on critical analysis. Recently, multimedia forms (documentaries, TV channels, online archives) have become popular, and biography studies is emerging as an independent academic discipline.

## Terms
- **Biography**: A detailed, non-fiction account of a person's life, portraying their experiences and personality.
- **Autobiography**: A biography written by its subject, sometimes with assistance.
- **Hagiography**: A biography, especially of a saint, that idealizes or idolizes the subject.
- **Authorized biography**: Written with the permission and cooperation of the subject or their heirs.
- **Unauthorized biography**: Written without such permission or participation.
- **Prabandha**: A semi-historical Jain biographical narrative genre from medieval India.
- **Legacy writing**: An in-depth form of biographical coverage.
- **Microhistory**: A historical approach focusing on small-scale events and individuals, adapted by some biographical scholars.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate whether all biographies are inherently fictional, as they are shaped by the biographer's contemporary perspective and memory. Hermione Lee argues that "biographical truths are constantly shifting." There are also critical issues regarding the relationship between a subject's private and public life, with Paul James noting that biographies often assume the private realm is foundational, even for public figures who project a persona. The importance of space in life-writing is another area of discussion.
