# Sardinia

## Overview
Sardinia is the second-largest island in the Mediterranean Sea and an autonomous region of Italy. Its strategic location has made it a crossroads of civilizations, from the prehistoric Nuragic culture to Phoenician, Roman, Aragonese, and Spanish rule. Today, it is known for its unique cultural heritage, distinct language, and a modern economy focused on tourism and technology.

- **1948** — Sardinia becomes an autonomous region of the Italian Republic.

## Body

### Prehistory and the Nuragic Civilization
Sardinia has been inhabited since the end of the Paleolithic era. The Neolithic period (6th millennium BC) saw the arrival of farmers and the spread of Cardium pottery. The island's most notable prehistoric culture is the Nuragic civilization, which flourished from the 18th century BC. It is characterized by thousands of stone tower-fortresses called *nuraghes*, sacred wells, and Giants' graves. The Nuragic people were skilled metallurgists and traders, with artifacts found from Cyprus to Spain. Some scholars link them to the Sherden, a tribe of the Sea Peoples.

### Ancient and Medieval Rule
Phoenicians established trading posts from the 9th century BC, followed by Carthaginian conquest in the 6th century BC. In 238 BC, Rome annexed the island, ruling for nearly 700 years and making it a key grain and mineral supplier. After the Vandal conquest (456–534), the Byzantine Empire retook Sardinia. As Byzantine control waned due to Arab raids and distance, local rulers known as *Judicates* (judges) emerged by the 10th century. Four independent kingdoms—Cagliari, Arborea, Gallura, and Logudoro—developed, maintaining semi-democratic assemblies.

### Aragonese and Spanish Domination
In 1324, the Crown of Aragon, allied with the Judicate of Arborea, began conquering the island. The Judicates resisted, with Arborea's *Carta de Logu* legal code (14th century) granting advanced civil rights. Arborea was the last to fall in 1420. Sardinia remained under the Crown of Aragon and later Spain for about 400 years, a period marked by feudalism, Spanish cultural influence, and frequent famines.

### Savoyard Rule and Unification
The island passed to the Austrian Habsburgs in 1708 and then to the House of Savoy in 1718. The Savoyards introduced Italian and began integrating Sardinia with their mainland territories. In 1847, the Sardinian parliament renounced autonomy to join the Italian unification process. In 1861, the Kingdom of Sardinia became the Kingdom of Italy. The 19th and early 20th centuries saw economic hardship, deforestation, and significant military losses in the World Wars.

### Modern Autonomy and Economy
After World War II, Sardinia became an autonomous region in 1948. A major campaign eradicated malaria by 1951, boosting tourism. The 1960s "Rebirth Plans" brought industrialization, including petrochemical plants, but the 1973 oil crisis caused unemployment and emigration. Today, the economy is service-based, with tourism as the main industry. Sardinia is a leader in Italian renewable energy and digital technology, hosting major military installations.

## Terms
*   **Nuraghe**: A prehistoric stone tower-fortress unique to Sardinia, central to the Nuragic civilization.
*   **Judicate**: An independent, hereditary kingdom in medieval Sardinia, ruled by a *judike* (judge/king).
*   **Carta de Logu**: A 14th-century legal code of the Judicate of Arborea, notable for its advanced civil rights provisions.
*   **Autonomous Region**: A special administrative status in Italy granting limited legislative powers in defined domains.
*   **Sardinian (Sardu)**: The indigenous Romance language of the island, distinct from Italian.
*   **Cantu a Tenore**: A traditional form of Sardinian polyphonic singing, recognized by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage.
*   **Pane Carasau**: A traditional thin, crisp flatbread, a staple of Sardinian cuisine.
*   **Blue Zone**: A demographic area with an unusually high concentration of centenarians; Sardinia was the first discovered.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact date and nature of the earliest human settlement on Sardinia is debated, with evidence from Corbeddu Cave (c. 20,000 years ago) contested.
*   The identification of the Nuragic Sards with the Sherden of the Sea Peoples is a scholarly speculation.
*   The extent of Aghlabid (Muslim) control versus mere raiding in 9th-century Sardinia is disputed among historians.
*   The causes of the decline of the Judicates and the transition to Aragonese rule involve complex interplay between external conquest and internal political fragmentation.
