# Ancient Rome

## Overview

Ancient Rome was the Roman civilization from the founding of the Italian city of Rome in the 8th century BC to the collapse of the Western Roman Empire in the 5th century AD. It encompassed the Roman Kingdom (753–509 BC), the Roman Republic (509–27 BC), and the Roman Empire (27 BC–476 AD). At its height, the empire controlled the North African coast, the Iberian and Balkan peninsulas, most of Southern and Western Europe, and much of the Middle East, covering around 5 million square kilometres with an estimated 50 to 90 million inhabitants—roughly 20% of the world's population. Rome's contributions to law, government, language, engineering, and architecture made it the progenitor of Western civilization.

## Body

### Founding and Kingdom

Archaeological evidence of settlement around Rome emerges c. 1000 BC, with large-scale organisation appearing c. 800 BC. The Romans had a founding myth attributing the city to Romulus and Remus, offspring of Mars and a princess of Alba Longa; after a dispute, Romulus killed Remus and became sole founder. The story dates at least to the 3rd century BC, and Varro placed the foundation at 753 BC. Another legend, recorded by Dionysius of Halicarnassus, says Prince Aeneas led Trojans to the Tiber's banks, where a woman named Roma torched their ships to prevent departure. Literary and archaeological evidence confirms kings existed in Rome. By the end of the 6th century BC, Rome controlled some 780 square kilometres with a population perhaps as high as 35,000.

### Republic

The Republic was established c. 509 BC when Tarquin the Proud was deposed, creating a system of annually elected magistrates and representative assemblies. The most important magistrates were the two consuls, who held imperium (military command) and worked with the Senate. In 390 BC, Gauls defeated the Romans at the Battle of the Allia and sacked Rome. The Romans gradually subdued the Italian peninsula, defeating Pyrrhus of Epirus in 281 BC. The Punic Wars against Carthage resulted in Rome's first overseas conquests—Sicily, Hispania, and Africa—and its rise as a significant imperial power.

### Late Republic and the Rise of Emperors

Foreign dominance led to internal strife: senators grew rich, soldiers lost their land, and a new merchant class called equestrians emerged. The Gracchi brothers attempted land reform but were killed. Marius reformed the military by levying the poor, holding an unprecedented seven consulships. Sulla marched on Rome with his legions, beginning the crisis of the Republic. Julius Caesar formed the First Triumvirate with Crassus and Pompey, conquered Gaul, and crossed the Rubicon in 49 BC, becoming pre-eminent before his murder in 44 BC. Octavian, Caesar's adopted heir, formed the Second Triumvirate with Antony and Lepidus, defeated Caesar's assassins at Philippi, and defeated Antony and Cleopatra at Actium in 31 BC.

### Empire – the Principate

In 27 BC, Octavian took the name Augustus, beginning the Roman Empire. The Julio-Claudian dynasty (Augustus, Tiberius, Caligula, Claudius, Nero) instituted imperial tradition. Augustus created the Praetorian Guard and a standing army of 28 legions. The Flavian dynasty saw the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 AD and construction of the Colosseum. The Nerva–Antonine dynasty included the "five good emperors"—Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, and Marcus Aurelius—under whom the empire reached its greatest territorial extent. The Severan dynasty followed, with Caracalla issuing the Edict of 212 granting citizenship to all free men.

### Crisis, Tetrarchy, and Fall

After Alexander Severus's death in 235 AD, the state suffered civil wars, invasions, and economic depression—26 emperors ruled in 49 years. Diocletian established the Tetrarchy (rule by four emperors) in 284 AD and initiated the last major Christian persecution. Constantine assumed power in 306, issued the Edict of Milan in 313, and rebuilt Byzantium as Constantinople. The Western Empire fell in 476 when Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustus; the Eastern (Byzantine) Empire survived until 1453.

### Society, Law, and Class

Roman society was hierarchical: slaves at the bottom, freedmen above them, free-born citizens at top. The patrician-plebeian division became less important in the late Republic. The Law of the Twelve Tables (449 BC) and Justinian's codification (c. 530 AD) formed the basis of Roman law, which applied throughout most of Europe until the end of the 17th century. Women shared basic rights but could not vote. The Republic had no fixed bureaucracy, collecting taxes through tax farming, which proved inefficient for the expanding dominion.

### Military and Economy

The early army was a citizen militia using hoplite tactics; by the 3rd century BC, it adopted the more flexible maniple system. By Augustus's time, legions were fully professional. The navy was dominated by non-Romans and allowed to atrophy in peacetime. Rome's economy focused on farming and trade, with slavery increasingly important—slaves constituted around 20% of the Empire's population and 40% in Rome. The economy had a developed coinage system, and some economists consider it a market economy comparable to 17th-century Netherlands.

### Culture and Technology

Roman life revolved around the city of Rome, with monumental structures like the Colosseum and Pantheon. Latin was the native language, evolving into the Romance languages. Religion moved from vague sacred spirits (numina) to an officially supported Christianity after 313 AD. Roman engineering achievements included roads, aqueducts, and concrete (invented in the late 3rd century BC), with many advancements not rivalled until the 19th and 20th centuries.

## Terms

- **Imperium**: Military command authority held by consuls and emperors
- **Res publica**: Literally "public business"; the Roman Republic, inspiration for modern republics
- **Patricians/Plebeians**: Aristocratic families who could trace ancestry to founding patriarchs vs. common people
- **Equestrians (equites)**: Wealthy mercantile class, originally those who could afford a warhorse
- **Pater familias**: Oldest male citizen heading a household, holding lawful authority (patria potestas)
- **Pax Romana**: Two-century period of relative peace under the Principate
- **Tetrarchy**: Diocletian's system of rule by four emperors (two senior, two junior)
- **Jus gentium**: "Law of nations" applying to foreigners and their dealings with Roman citizens
- **Toga**: Traditional Roman garment, replaced by the paenula by the 4th century
- **Latifundia**: Large estates that reduced availability of paid work for soldiers

## Debates and open questions

Various reasons for Rome's fall have been proposed, including loss of Republicanism, moral decay, military tyranny, class war, slavery, economic stagnation, environmental change, disease, the decline of the Roman race, and the inevitable ebb and flow all civilizations experience. Some historians consider Julius Nepos, who ruled from Dalmatia until 480, the last Western emperor instead of Romulus Augustus. Some historians speculate that lead pipes in plumbing led to widespread lead poisoning contributing to the fall, though lead content would have been minimised. The primary sources themselves are problematic: Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus wrote 500 years after the founding of the republic, and gaps in Roman history are filled by unreliable works such as the Historia Augusta.
