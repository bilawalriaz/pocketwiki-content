# Cradle of civilization

## Overview
A cradle of civilization is a region where a complex society—featuring a state, social stratification, urbanization, and writing—emerged independently. These foundational civilizations developed agriculture to support centralized governments and public works. Six primary cradles are generally identified: Mesopotamia, Ancient Egypt, Ancient India, Ancient China, the Caral–Supe civilization of Peru, and the Olmec civilization of Mexico.

## Timeline
- **c. 12,000 BC** — Natufian culture in the Levant becomes sedentary, a precursor to agriculture.
- **c. 10,000 BC** — Neolithic Revolution begins in Western Asia; earliest proto-urban settlements emerge.
- **c. 3500 BC** — Egyptian civilization begins during the Gerzean period of the Naqada culture.
- **c. 3300 BC** — Indus Valley Civilization begins with the Early Harappan Phase; proto-writing appears in the Near East.
- **c. 3200 BC** — Caral–Supe civilization emerges in coastal Peru.
- **c. 3150 BC** — Unification of Upper and Lower Egypt under King Narmer.
- **c. 3100 BC** — First cities of tens of thousands (Uruk, Ur, Memphis) are established.
- **c. 2600 BC** — Mature Harappan Phase begins in the Indus Valley; Old Kingdom starts in Egypt.
- **c. 2500 BC** — Eannatum of Lagash establishes the first verifiable empire in Mesopotamia.
- **c. 2350 BC** — Akkadian Empire emerges under Sargon the Great.
- **c. 1600 BC** — Shang dynasty begins in China, with earliest known Chinese writing (oracle bone script).
- **c. 1500 BC** — Olmec civilization fully coalesces at San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán in Mexico.

## Body

### The Rise of Civilization
The transition began with the Neolithic Revolution, where nomadic groups adopted sedentary agriculture. Starting in the Levant around 12,000 BC, this created permanent villages and eventually cities. The process was driven by managing water and agricultural surplus, supporting specialized labor, social hierarchies, and centralized authority. Writing, emerging from proto-writing systems, is a key marker separating historic from prehistoric times. While often seen as a sequence (agriculture → cities → writing), some scholars argue it was an accelerated process culminating in the Bronze Age.

### Multiple Independent Cradles
Once thought to have spread from the Fertile Crescent, scholarship now recognizes complex societies arose independently in at least six distinct regions. This conclusion is based on evidence that sociocultural developments occurred along different timeframes and that early "sedentary" and "nomadic" communities interacted. The concept focuses on where inhabitants first developed cities, writing, metallurgy, animal domestication, and complex social structures.

### The Fertile Crescent and Mesopotamia
The Fertile Crescent, a region of West Asia, is the earliest identified cradle. Within it, Mesopotamia (modern Iraq) saw the rise of the first agrarian societies due to the fertile Tigris-Euphrates river system. The Ubaid period (c. 6500–3800 BC) saw the start of urbanization. The subsequent Uruk period (4000–3100 BC) witnessed the emergence of urban life and the earliest cuneiform writing (c. 3300 BC). Sumerian city-states were likely theocratic, led by priest-kings. By the Early Dynastic Period (c. 2900 BC), secular rulers (Lugals) gained prominence. The region saw the rise and fall of empires, including the Akkadian Empire (c. 2350 BC) and the Third Dynasty of Ur, before the Semitic Akkadian peoples coalesced into Assyria and Babylonia.

### Northeastern Africa and Ancient Egypt
Civilization in the Nile Valley was influenced by migrations from the Fertile Crescent and the Horn of Africa. Forced by the desiccation of the Sahara, populations settled along the Nile, developing agriculture by 5500 BC. The Naqada culture (c. 4400–3000 BC) showed increasing social complexity. Egyptian civilization coalesced around 3500 BC and was unified under a god-king (pharaoh) by c. 3150 BC. The Old Kingdom (c. 2686–2181 BC) was a period of major architectural and administrative achievement, including the Giza pyramids. Its decline, possibly due to drought and eroded central power, led to the First Intermediate Period.

### Ancient India
The earliest Neolithic site in South Asia is Mehrgarh (7000 BC). The Indus Valley Civilization began c. 3300 BC, evolving into large urban centers like Harappa and Mohenjo-daro by 2600 BC. These cities featured advanced urban planning, sanitation systems, and standardized weights and measures. The civilization declined around 1800–1700 BC, likely due to climate change and the drying of the Ghaggar-Hakra river system, leading to population dispersal.

### Ancient China
Chinese civilization developed from interactions among distinct cultures in the Yellow River, Yangtze, and Liao regions. Early agriculture (millet, rice) dates to 7000–6500 BC. The Peiligang culture (7000–5000 BC) was likely egalitarian. Later, the Yangshao (5000–3000 BC) and Longshan (3000–1900 BC) cultures showed increasing social stratification. The Erlitou culture (1900–1500 BC) is considered the first state-level society in East Asia, possibly correlating with the semi-legendary Xia dynasty. The Shang dynasty (1600–1046 BC) is the earliest with both archaeological and written evidence (oracle bone script).

### Ancient Peru
The Caral–Supe (Norte Chico) civilization emerged c. 3200 BC in coastal Peru. It is notable for its large platform mounds and sunken plazas, but a complete lack of ceramics and visual art. Its economy was based on agriculture and maritime resources, with cotton as a key trade good. Society was likely theocratic, with elites controlling resources and labor for monumental construction. The civilization used quipu (knotted strings) for record-keeping. It declined c. 1800 BC as more powerful centers arose elsewhere.

### Mesoamerica
The Olmec civilization, considered the "mother culture" of Mesoamerica, emerged c. 1600–1500 BC in the Gulf Coast lowlands. Its first major center was San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán, which flourished until c. 900 BC. The Olmec are known for colossal stone heads and a complex hierarchical society. La Venta became the next major center until c. 400 BC. The Olmec developed early forms of writing and the Mesoamerican ballgame, influencing later cultures like the Maya and Aztec. Their decline is attributed to environmental changes.

## Terms
- **Cradle of civilization**: A region where a complex society (state, cities, writing) emerged independently.
- **Neolithic Revolution**: The transition from hunting and gathering to agriculture and sedentary living.
- **Social stratification**: The division of society into hierarchical classes or groups.
- **Proto-writing**: Systems of symbols that convey meaning but are not yet full writing systems.
- **Cuneiform**: An early writing system using wedge-shaped marks on clay tablets, developed in Mesopotamia.
- **Theocratic**: A system of government in which priests rule in the name of a deity.
- **Quipu**: A recording device made of knotted strings used by Andean cultures for encoding information.
- **Maritime Foundation of Andean Civilization**: A debated theory that the Caral–Supe civilization relied primarily on marine resources rather than agriculture.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Single vs. Multiple Origins**: While the multi-regional model is dominant, the precise mechanisms of independent development versus cultural diffusion between early societies are debated.
- **Role of Environment**: The exact impact of climate change (e.g., Sahara desiccation, monsoon weakening) on the rise or fall of specific civilizations is continually studied.
- **Olmec as "Mother Culture"**: The degree and nature of Olmec influence on subsequent Mesoamerican civilizations is a long-standing scholarly debate.
- **Caral–Supe Economy**: The extent to which the Caral–Supe civilization depended on maritime resources versus agriculture remains hotly debated.
- **Xia Dynasty**: Whether the Erlitou archaeological culture corresponds to the semi-legendary Xia dynasty of Chinese historical records is unresolved.
