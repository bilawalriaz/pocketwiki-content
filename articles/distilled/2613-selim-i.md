# Selim I

## Overview
Selim I (r. 1512–1520) was an Ottoman sultan whose brief but transformative reign dramatically expanded the empire's territory and shifted its center of gravity from the Balkans to the Middle East. His conquest of the Mamluk Sultanate brought the holy cities of Mecca and Medina under Ottoman control, establishing the empire as the pre-eminent Muslim state and laying the foundation for the Ottoman Caliphate.

## Timeline
- **10 October 1470** — Born in Amasya as son of Şehzade Bayezid.
- **1487** — Sent by his father to serve as governor of Trabzon.
- **1511** — Defeated at the Battle of Tekirdag, fled to Crimea.
- **1512** — Dethroned his father, Bayezid II, with Janissary support.
- **1513** — Defeated his brother Ahmet at the Battle of Yenişehir.
- **1514** — Defeated Shah Ismail I at the Battle of Chaldiran.
- **1516** — Defeated the Mamluks at the Battle of Marj Dabiq.
- **1517** — Defeated the Mamluks at the Battle of Ridaniya, captured Cairo.
- **22 September 1520** — Died, succeeded by his son Suleiman I.

## Body

### Securing the Sultanate
Selim's path to the throne was marked by conflict. As governor of Trabzon, he repeatedly clashed with the Safavid Empire, defeating their forces in 1505, 1507, and 1510. Fearing his brother Ahmet would become sultan, Selim rebelled against his father, Bayezid II, in 1511 and again in 1512. With Janissary support, he deposed his father and immediately defeated Ahmet at the Battle of Yenişehir. To secure his rule, Selim executed his brothers and nephews, a fratricidal policy motivated by past civil strife.

### Conflict with the Safavids
The Safavid Empire, led by Shah Ismail I, was a major threat due to its promotion of Shia Islam among Ottoman subjects. After the Şahkulu Rebellion in 1511, Selim's mufti issued a fatwa declaring Ismail's lands the "abode of war." In 1514, Selim invaded, massacring thousands of Alevis en route. He decisively defeated Ismail at the Battle of Chaldiran, where Ottoman artillery proved superior. The victory allowed Selim to annex Eastern Anatolia and Upper Mesopotamia, though effective control was not established until later treaties.

### Conquest of the Mamluk Sultanate
Selim's most significant achievement was the conquest of the Mamluk Sultanate. He defeated the Mamluks at the Battle of Marj Dabiq in 1516, leading to the capture of Syria and Palestine. In 1517, he won the Battle of Ridaniya and captured Cairo, executing the last Mamluk sultan, Tumanbay II. This conquest brought Egypt, the Hejaz, and the holy cities of Mecca and Medina under Ottoman control. Selim accepted the title "Servant of the Two Holy Cities" rather than "Ruler."

### The Caliphate and Legacy
The last Abbasid caliph, al-Mutawakkil III, was sent to Istanbul and swore allegiance to Selim. While stories of an official transfer of the caliphal title are a later invention, this event is seen as establishing the Ottoman Caliphate. Selim's conquests made the Ottoman Empire the dominant Muslim power, with its territory growing by 70% to 3.4 million km². His reign set the stage for the empire's golden age under his son, Suleiman the Magnificent.

## Terms
- **Sultan**: The title for the sovereign of the Ottoman Empire.
- **Janissaries**: Elite infantry units of the Ottoman army, originally composed of Christian conscripts.
- **Fatwa**: A legal opinion or decree issued by an Islamic religious leader.
- **Takfir**: The act of declaring a Muslim to be an apostate or non-believer.
- **Mamluk**: A class of soldier-slaves, originally of Turkic or Circassian origin, who formed the ruling military elite of Egypt and Syria.
- **Caliph**: The title for the political and religious successor to the Prophet Muhammad, considered the leader of the Muslim community.
- **Sanjak**: An administrative division of the Ottoman Empire, smaller than a province (eyalet).
- **Shia Islam**: The branch of Islam that believes the Prophet Muhammad's successor should have been his son-in-law Ali, in contrast to Sunni Islam.
- **Sufi**: A Muslim who seeks divine love and knowledge through direct personal experience of God.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The nature of the transfer of the caliphate from the Abbasids to the Ottomans is debated. The source states that stories of an official transfer were a "later invention," but the event nonetheless established the Ottoman Caliphate.
- The exact cause of Selim's death is uncertain. The official cause was a carbuncle, but historians suggest it may have been cancer, poison, or plague.
- The extent to which Selim's brutal policies against Alevis and Shiites were motivated by religious conviction versus political necessity is a subject of historical analysis.
