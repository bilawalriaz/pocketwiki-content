# Philip IV of France

## Overview
Philip IV of France (r. 1285–1314), known as Philip the Fair, was a pivotal king who strengthened royal authority at the expense of nobles, clergy, and international rivals. His reign marked France's transition from a feudal to a more centralized state, but his aggressive policies—including conflicts with the papacy, the expulsion of Jews, and the destruction of the Knights Templar—created lasting financial and political crises. His death without surviving male heirs triggered a succession crisis that eventually led to the Hundred Years' War.

## Timeline
- **1268** — Born at Fontainebleau to the future Philip III.
- **1284** — Marries Joan I of Navarre, uniting Champagne to the French crown.
- **1285** — Becomes King of France after his father's death.
- **1294–1303** — Gascon War with England over Aquitaine.
- **1302** — French nobility suffers a major defeat at the Battle of the Golden Spurs in Flanders.
- **1303** — French forces attack Pope Boniface VIII at Anagni.
- **1306** — Expels Jews from France and confiscates their property.
- **1307** — Orders the arrest of the Knights Templar across France.
- **1309** — The papacy moves to Avignon, beginning the "Babylonian Captivity."
- **1314** — Dies following a hunting stroke; succeeded by his son Louis X.

## Body

### Centralizing the Monarchy
Philip IV sought to reduce the power of the nobility and clergy, relying instead on professional civil servants like Guillaume de Nogaret and Enguerrand de Marigny. He compelled vassals through wars and restricted feudal privileges, paving the way for a centralized early modern state. Domestically, his reign saw the consolidation of key institutions like the King's Council and the Parlement.

### Foreign Policy and Wars
Philip's reign involved major conflicts with England and Flanders. The war with England (1294–1303) over the duchy of Aquitaine was inconclusive but led to the Auld Alliance with Scotland. A Flemish uprising culminated in the humiliating French defeat at the Battle of the Golden Spurs (1302). Philip later won a decisive victory at Mons-en-Pévèle (1305), forcing a harsh peace that added wealthy Flemish cities like Lille and Douai to the crown lands. He also pursued diplomacy with the Mongol Ilkhanate for a potential alliance against the Mamluks, though no military action resulted.

### Finance and Debasement
Philip inherited debt and faced constant deficits from wars. To cover costs, he debased the coinage, reducing its silver content. This caused inflation, damaged creditors, and led to social unrest. After the Flemish war, he revalued the currency in 1306, which caused riots and forced him to seek refuge in the Temple. He financed his government through seizures from Lombard merchants, taxes on the clergy, and the confiscation of Jewish property after their 1306 expulsion.

### Conflict with the Papacy
Philip's attempts to tax the clergy and assert royal control led to a violent dispute with Pope Boniface VIII. After Boniface issued the bull *Unam Sanctam* (1302) asserting papal supremacy, Philip had the pope arrested at Anagni in 1303. Boniface died shortly after. Philip's influence then led to the election of the French archbishop Clement V as pope, who moved the papal seat to Avignon in 1309, beginning the "Babylonian Captivity" under French control.

### Suppression of the Knights Templar
Heavily indebted to the Knights Templar, Philip used accusations of heresy to destroy the order. On October 13, 1307, hundreds of Templars in France were arrested. Under torture, many confessed to heresy. Despite Pope Clement V's attempts at proper trials, Philip used forced confessions to execute many Templars. The last Grand Master, Jacques de Molay, was burned at the stake in 1314 after recanting his confession. The move eliminated Philip's debts and allowed the crown to seize the order's assets.

### Tour de Nesle Affair and Succession
In 1314, Philip's three daughters-in-law were accused of adultery in the Tour de Nesle affair. This scandal tarnished the royal family. Philip died later that year, succeeded by his son Louis X. However, his three sons died in quick succession without male heirs, ending the direct Capetian line and leading to a succession crisis that contributed to the Hundred Years' War.

## Terms
- **Feudalism**: A medieval social and political system where land was held by lords in exchange for service and loyalty from vassals.
- **Royal Demesne**: The lands directly controlled and administered by the king, not granted out to feudal lords.
- **Parlement**: A high court of justice in medieval France, which evolved into a key institution of royal administration under Philip IV.
- **Debasement**: The practice of reducing the precious metal content in coins, leading to inflation and currency depreciation.
- **Babylonian Captivity**: The period from 1309 to 1376 when the papacy was under the strong influence of the French crown, based in Avignon.
- **Estates General**: An assembly representing the clergy, nobility, and commoners of France, convened by the king for consultation.
- **Auld Alliance**: A treaty between France and Scotland, first established in 1295, for mutual support against England.
- **Ilkhanate**: A Mongol khanate established in Persia and the Middle East in the 13th century.

## Debates and open questions
- The primary motive for Philip's destruction of the Knights Templar is debated: was it primarily to erase his debts, assert royal theocracy over the papacy, or address genuine concerns about heresy?
- The extent to which Philip's financial policies (like debasement) were a pragmatic response to war versus a cause of long-term economic damage is a point of scholarly discussion.
- The role of the Tour de Nesle affair in shaping the succession crisis and the later Hundred Years' War is analyzed for its political versus personal impact.
