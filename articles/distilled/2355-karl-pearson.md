# Karl Pearson

## Overview
Karl Pearson (1857–1936) was an English mathematician and biostatistician who established the discipline of mathematical statistics. He founded the world's first university statistics department and developed foundational statistical methods still in use today. His legacy is complex, as he was also a prominent proponent of eugenics and scientific racism.

## Timeline
- **1857** — Born in Islington, London.
- **1879** — Graduates as Third Wrangler in Mathematics from King's College, Cambridge.
- **1884** — Appointed Goldsmid Professor of Applied Mathematics at University College London (UCL).
- **1891** — Appointed Gresham Professor of Geometry; meets zoologist W.F.R. Weldon.
- **1892** — Publishes *The Grammar of Science*.
- **1901** — Co-founds the journal *Biometrika* with Weldon and Francis Galton.
- **1911** — Founds the world's first university statistics department at UCL; becomes first Galton Professor of Eugenics.
- **1914–1930** — Publishes three-volume biography of Francis Galton.
- **1925** — Founds the journal *Annals of Eugenics*.
- **1936** — Dies in Coldharbour, Surrey.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into a Quaker family, Pearson studied mathematics at Cambridge, then traveled to Germany to study physics, metaphysics, and Darwinism. He later studied law in London but never practiced, instead lecturing on diverse topics including science, literature, and socialism.

### Career and Statistical Foundations
Pearson's academic career was centered at University College London (UCL), where he held the chair of Applied Mathematics. His collaboration with zoologist W.F.R. Weldon, and later with Weldon's mentor Francis Galton, shifted his focus to applying mathematics to biological problems of heredity and evolution. After Galton's death, Pearson became the first Galton Professor of Eugenics and founded the Department of Applied Statistics at UCL, combining the Biometric and Galton Laboratories.

Pearson's work established the core of modern statistical theory. He developed the correlation coefficient (r), the chi-squared test, the method of moments, and the concept of the p-value. He also introduced the histogram and founded the journal *Biometrika* to promote statistical theory. His approach focused on mathematical description of empirical data rather than identifying causal biological mechanisms.

### Biometrics and the Mendelian Debate
Pearson was a founder of the biometric school, which used statistical analysis to study inheritance and evolution. His "Law of Ancestral Heredity" proposed that traits were inherited from parents and more distant ancestors. This approach clashed with the emerging Mendelian genetics, which proposed discrete "genes" as the mechanism of inheritance. Pearson criticized biologists who did not use statistical methods, arguing that scientific claims required quantitative demonstration. The biometric approach eventually lost to Mendelian theory, but Pearson's statistical techniques remain vital.

### Philosophy, Politics, and Eugenics
Pearson was a zealous atheist, freethinker, and socialist who refused state honors. His book *The Grammar of Science* argued that scientific laws are products of the human mind and that an observer traveling at light speed would perceive no motion, ideas that influenced the young Albert Einstein.

A committed eugenicist, Pearson applied Social Darwinism to nations, viewing war against "inferior races" as a logical outcome of evolution. He opposed Jewish immigration into Britain, alleging it would create a "parasitic race." In his later years, he expressed some caution about hasty eugenic legislation but maintained his core views. In 2020, UCL renamed buildings previously named after him due to his eugenicist legacy.

## Terms
- **Biometrics**: The application of statistical methods to biological data, particularly in the study of heredity and evolution.
- **Correlation coefficient (r)**: A statistical measure (developed by Pearson) that quantifies the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables.
- **Chi-squared test**: A statistical hypothesis test (developed by Pearson) used to determine if there is a significant difference between observed and expected frequencies in categorical data.
- **Eugenics**: A now-discredited pseudoscientific movement aiming to improve the genetic quality of a human population, often through selective breeding and immigration control.
- **Galton Chair of Eugenics**: An academic position at UCL established with Francis Galton's bequest, later renamed the Galton Chair of Genetics.
- **Mendelian genetics**: The theory of inheritance based on discrete units (genes) passed from parents to offspring, proposed by Gregor Mendel.
- **Method of moments**: A technique (introduced by Pearson) for estimating population parameters by equating sample moments to theoretical moments.
- **p-value**: In Pearson's original formulation, the probability of observing a test statistic as extreme as, or more extreme than, the value observed, under the assumption that the null hypothesis is true.
- **Social Darwinism**: A theory that societies evolve through competition and the survival of the fittest, often used to justify social inequality and imperialism.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Biometrics vs. Mendelism**: A major early 20th-century debate over the mechanism of heredity. Pearson's biometric school emphasized continuous variation and statistical description, while Mendelians focused on discrete, particulate inheritance. The Mendelian view ultimately prevailed.
- **Pearson's Scientific Racism**: His eugenic views and opposition to immigration are now widely condemned as scientific racism. Institutions like UCL have taken steps to distance themselves from this aspect of his legacy.
- **Influence on Einstein**: The extent to which Pearson's philosophical ideas in *The Grammar of Science* directly influenced Einstein's theories of relativity is a subject of historical interest.
