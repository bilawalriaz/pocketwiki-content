# Musicology

## Overview
Musicology is the academic, research-based study of music, distinct from composition or performance. It intersects with fields like psychology, sociology, acoustics, and computer science. The discipline is traditionally divided into historical, systematic, and ethnomusicological branches, each examining music from different scholarly perspectives.

## Timeline
- **c. 1880** — Comparative methods become widespread in musicology.
- **1915** — *The Musical Quarterly* journal begins publication.
- **1928** — *Acta Musicologica* journal begins publication.
- **1948** — *Journal of the American Musicological Society* begins publication.
- **1953** — *Ethnomusicology* journal begins publication.
- **1960s-1970s** — Some musicologists obtain professor positions with an MA.
- **1977** — *19th-Century Music* journal begins publication.
- **1980s** — "New musicology" emerges; popular music studies gain academic traction.
- **1981** — *Popular Music* journal and the International Association for the Study of Popular Music are founded.
- **2010s** — The PhD becomes the standard minimum credential for tenure-track professor positions.

## Body

### Traditional Branches and Parent Disciplines
Musicology is traditionally divided into three branches: historical musicology, systematic musicology, and ethnomusicology. Its parent disciplines include general history, cultural studies, philosophy, ethnology, psychology, physiology, acoustics, and information sciences. It also has two central, practically oriented sub-disciplines with no parent discipline: performance practice and research, and the theory, analysis, and composition of music.

### Historical Musicology
Historical musicology studies the composition, performance, reception, and criticism of music over time. It typically focuses on Western Art music of the European tradition. Methods include source studies, palaeography, philology, style criticism, historiography, musical analysis, and iconography. Scholars may examine specific issues, like the relationship between words and music, or take a broader view using techniques from economics or sociology.

### New Musicology
Emerging in the late 1980s, new musicology emphasizes cultural study, analysis, and criticism, often using feminist, gender, queer, or postcolonial theory. It was a reaction against traditional historical musicology's perceived avoidance of musical signification. The term is now less common, as its concerns have become mainstream.

### Ethnomusicology
Ethnomusicology, formerly comparative musicology, is the study of music in its cultural context, often called the anthropology of music. It primarily studies non-Western music but also examines Western music from an anthropological perspective. It is characterized by intensive, long-term fieldwork within a community.

### Popular Music Studies
Known misleadingly as popular musicology, this field emerged in the 1980s as musicologists and ethnomusicologists began studying popular music. It is characterized by a polarized "musicological" and "sociological" approach.

### Music Theory, Analysis, and Composition
Music theory describes the elements of music and develops methods for composing and analyzing it. Theorists explain compositional techniques by establishing rules and patterns or model the experience of listening. In the Western tradition, theory focuses on harmony and counterpoint to explain large-scale structure and melody.

### Music Psychology and Performance Practice
Music psychology applies psychology to understand how music is created, perceived, and responded to. Its branches include cognitive musicology and the cognitive neuroscience of music. Performance practice uses historical musicology to determine how music was performed in the past. Music performance research, associated with psychology, systematically documents how music is actually performed.

### Education and Careers
A PhD is the standard minimum credential for tenure-track professor positions. Musicologists typically work as professors, teaching classes, conducting research, publishing, presenting at conferences, and supervising graduate students. The job market is very competitive.

## Terms
- **Musicology**: The academic, research-based study of music.
- **Historical Musicology**: The study of the history of musical traditions, origins of works, and composer biographies.
- **Ethnomusicology**: The study of music in its cultural context, often involving long-term fieldwork.
- **Systematic Musicology**: An umbrella term for music theory, aesthetics, pedagogy, acoustics, organology, and the musical implications of various sciences.
- **New Musicology**: A late-20th-century approach emphasizing cultural study and criticism, often using gender or postcolonial theory.
- **Performance Practice**: Research into how music was performed in specific historical contexts.
- **Cognitive Musicology**: The study of cognitive modeling of music.
- **Organology**: The science and technology of musical instruments.
- **Palaeography**: The study of ancient writing, used in musicology for manuscript analysis.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The boundaries and definitions of historical musicology are debated, particularly regarding whether its focus is exclusively on Western Art music.
- The term "new musicology" is contested; some argue its concerns are now mainstream, making the "new" label obsolete.
- There is a noted polarization between "musicological" and "sociological" approaches within popular music studies.
