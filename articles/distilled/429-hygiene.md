# Hygiene

## Overview
Hygiene encompasses the practices and environmental conditions that preserve health and prevent disease transmission, distinct from mere cleanliness. It operates by breaking the chain of infection—interrupting the spread of pathogens from sources like people, raw food, water, and animals via highways such as hands, surfaces, and air. The field spans home, personal, medical, food, and sleep hygiene, unified by microbiological principles but often treated as separate domains. Historically, hygiene practices have been shaped by religious mandates, technological innovations (e.g., sanitation, soap), and scientific breakthroughs like germ theory, though cultural variations persist. Modern "targeted hygiene" uses a risk-based approach (HACCP) to focus interventions on critical moments, balancing infection prevention with the immunological benefits of microbial exposure.

## Timeline
- **1600–1046 BCE** — Shang dynasty oracle bones record bathing in China.
- **7th century** — Islamic hygienical jurisprudence establishes rules for ritual purity (wudu, ghusl) and toilet hygiene.
- **8th–13th centuries** — Abbasid Caliphate (Baghdad) operates 65,000 baths with sewer systems and water supply networks.
- **1025** — Ibn Sina (Avicenna) proposes contagion theory in *The Canon of Medicine*, noting transmission via breath, water, and dirt.
- **15th century** — Soap manufacturing becomes virtually industrialized in Christendom (Antwerp, Castile, Marseille, Naples, Venice).
- **Mid-19th century** — Ignaz Semmelweis (Vienna) and Florence Nightingale (England) independently recognize hand washing's critical role in preventing puerperal fever and hospital infections.
- **Late 19th century** — Germ theory (Pasteur) and antiseptic surgery (Lister) establish hygiene as synonymous with health; flush toilets and indoor plumbing widely adopted after John Snow links cholera to fecal water contamination.
- **1970s** — Sleep hygiene recommendations developed for mild-to-moderate insomnia.
- **2009** — Up to 20% of population comprises "at-risk" groups (elderly, immunocompromised) cared for at home, requiring medical hygiene.
- **2013** — International Scientific Forum on Home Hygiene reviews 30 laundering studies; finds high variability prevents confident guidelines.
- **2014** — Evidence for individual sleep hygiene recommendations assessed as "limited and inconclusive."
- **2030 (Target)** — UN Sustainable Development Goal 6.2: universal access to adequate sanitation and hygiene, ending open defecation.

## Body

### Definition and Core Principles
Hygiene refers to preventive practices that reduce the incidence and spread of pathogenic microbes (bacteria, viruses, fungi) leading to disease. While often conflated with cleanliness, hygiene specifically targets disease prevention; cleaning (e.g., handwashing with soap) removes microbes and dirt, serving as a primary means to achieve hygiene. In manufacturing (food, pharmaceuticals, cosmetics), hygiene is a critical quality assurance component. Practices vary culturally but share underlying microbiological principles: pathogens shed from sources (people, raw food, water, pets) spread via "highways" (hands, contact surfaces, cleaning cloths, air, linens) to susceptible hosts. "Targeted hygiene" applies Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point (HACCP) principles to identify critical intervention points, breaking the infection chain where the infectious dose can be as low as 10–100 viable units.

### Home and Everyday Hygiene
Home hygiene prevents disease spread in domestic and social settings (workplace, transport). Key components include hand hygiene, respiratory hygiene, food/water hygiene, environmental surface hygiene, animal care, and home healthcare for at-risk groups (up to 20% of population as of 2009).

**Hand and Respiratory Hygiene:** Regular handwashing with soap is among the most effective interventions, significantly reducing gastrointestinal and respiratory infections. Respiratory hygiene—catching coughs/sneezes in tissues (or elbow), immediate disposal—reduces pathogen spread during cold/flu seasons.

**Kitchen, Bathroom, and Toilet Hygiene:** Routine cleaning of high-touch surfaces (flush handles, taps, worktops) reduces pathogen spread. Flush toilets pose low risk if maintained, though flushing can aerosolize pathogens during diarrhea. Baths/showers accumulate microbial scum/scale; molds on tiles, curtains, and damp surfaces cause infections, allergies, and odors. "Hygienic cleaning" requires either: (1) mechanical removal with soap/detergent plus thorough rinsing; (2) in situ pathogen inactivation via microbicidal products (disinfectants, alcohol sanitizers, heat); or (3) combined kill-and-removal (e.g., laundering). Deep-cleaning targets neglected areas biannually.

**Laundry Hygiene:** Soiled clothing/linens (underwear, towels, facecloths, nappies, cleaning cloths) transmit pathogens, though quantitative domestic risk data is lacking. Risks increase significantly in healthcare contexts, with immunocompromised individuals, or with antibiotic-resistant strains (MRSA, ESBL/NDM-1 enterobacteria). Laundering reduces contamination via thermal inactivation (temperature), chemical inactivation (surfactants, oxygen bleach, hypochlorite), physical removal (rinse/spin), and drying/ironing. Sunlight drying reduces pathogens. A 2013 review of 30 studies (room temp to 70°C) found extreme variability in methods (cycle time, rinses) preventing standardized guidelines.

**Medical Hygiene at Home:** Carers for at-risk groups (elderly, very young, post-hospital, immunosuppressed, invasive device users) require hygiene knowledge. Special procedures (catheter/dressing changes) carry higher infection risks; antiseptics prevent sepsis in wounds. Standard hygiene practices apply but failure consequences are severe.

**Disinfectants and Antibacterials:** Disinfectants kill pathogens (bactericidal; some fungicidal, sporicidal, virucidal—check label). "Antibacterial" products may only inhibit multiplication, not kill; not necessarily antifungal/antiviral. Sanitizers clean and disinfect; alcohol hand sanitizers are ineffective on soiled hands. Biocides broadly control living organisms (antiseptics, disinfectants, pesticides).

### Personal Hygiene
Personal hygiene maintains bodily health/wellbeing via cleanliness, motivated by illness reduction, healing, wellbeing, social acceptance, and preventing transmission. Practices are culture-specific and evolve over time.

**Regular Activities:** Generally accepted practices include regular bathing/showering, handwashing (especially pre-food), face/hair washing, hair management, clean clothing, toothbrushing, nail trimming, and sex-specific practices (menstruation). Routines cover cough etiquette, tissue disposal, toilet/food-area cleanliness. Some cultures avoid kissing/handshaking to reduce contact transmission. Personal grooming (deodorants, shaving, combing) extends hygiene for appearance, not necessarily health.

**Ear Hygiene:** Ear canals are sensitive and self-cleaning; skin migrates from eardrum outward, transporting earwax. Excessive cleaning pushes debris inward, risking infection/irritation.

**Oral Hygiene:** Recommendations: brush twice daily (softly, correct technique), replace brush ~3 months. NHS England advises *not* rinsing after brushing—spit excess only—to retain fluoride for decay prevention. Avoid brushing immediately after acidic drinks (including sparkling water). Floss daily with fresh floss. Amorphous calcium phosphate products (e.g., Tooth Mousse) effectiveness debated. Dental checkups annually minimum.

**Sleep Hygiene:** Behavioral/environmental practices for better sleep, developed late 1970s for insomnia. As of 2014, evidence for individual recommendations is "limited and inconclusive." Clinicians assess and tailor recommendations: regular sleep schedule, careful napping, avoiding late exercise/stimulants (alcohol, nicotine, caffeine), limiting worry/light exposure pre-sleep, stimulus control (bed only for sleep), and dark/comfortable environment.

**Personal Care Services Hygiene:** Sterilization of instruments (hairdressers, aestheticians); autoclave sterilization for piercing/tattooing; hand cleaning.

**Hygiene Hypothesis and Allergies:** Popular folklore sometimes asserts "dirt is healthy," undermining public health behaviors. Targeted hygiene focuses on critical transmission points while sustaining normal environmental microbial exposure for immune balance. Evidence shows some early microbial exposures protect against allergies, but *not* that harmful infections or clinical disease are necessary, nor that hygiene measures (handwashing, food hygiene) increase atopic disease. Expert consensus points to lifestyle changes reducing exposure to immunoregulatory species (e.g., helminths), though specific factors remain uncertain.

### Medical Hygiene
Practices preventing disease spread during medical care, mostly developed in the 19th century and established by mid-20th century. Includes: isolation of infectious persons/materials; sterilization of surgical instruments; proper wound bandaging/dressing; safe medical waste disposal (refined post-AIDS/Ebola); disinfection of reusables (linen, pads, uniforms); surgical scrubbing/handwashing; ethanol-based sanitizers.

### Food Hygiene
Culinary hygiene specifies safe handling, storage, preparation, serving, and eating to prevent contamination, food poisoning, and cross-transmission to humans/animals.

### Hygiene in Low- and Middle-Income Countries
Universal water/sanitation access plus hygiene promotion is essential for reducing infectious diseases, integrated into SDG 6.2 (2030 target). ~2 million die annually from diarrheal diseases (mostly children <5 in extreme poverty). Handwashing with soap could reduce diarrhea ~50% and respiratory infections ~25%, plus skin/eye diseases (trachoma) and intestinal worms (ascariasis, trichuriasis). Safe waste disposal, surface hygiene, and animal care break infection chains. Toilet/handwash facility cleaning prevents odors and ensures social acceptance—critical where open defecation remains an alternative.

**Household Water Treatment and Safe Storage:** Ensures safe drinking water; 120 million in European region lack access. Point-of-use interventions reduce diarrheal disease where water quality is poor or supply breaks down. Safe storage prevents recontamination (dirty hands/vessels). Methods: chemical disinfection (chlorine/iodine), boiling, ceramic filtration, solar disinfection (effective without chemicals), UV irradiation (batch/flow-through), combined flocculation/disinfection sachets, multibarrier systems, portable purifiers.

### History

**Asia**
*China:* Bathing traced to Shang dynasty (1600–1046 BCE). *Book of Rites* (Zhou dynasty) recommends hot shower every 5 days, hair wash every 3 days. Han dynasty: officials bathed every 5 days; bath beans (soap mixture) recorded. Tang dynasty: luxurious hot-spring bathhouses. Song dynasty (960–1279 CE): public bathhouses popular, offering massage, nail cutting, ear cleaning, food. Yuan dynasty: Marco Polo notes coal-heated bathhouses, daily winter showers across classes. Ming dynasty: slabbed floors, brick domes, boiler/tunnel systems, water wheels.
*Japan:* Origin in *misogi* (ritual purification). Heian period (794–1185): elite baths for leisure. 17th century: Europeans record daily mixed-sex bathing.
*Indian Subcontinent:* Earliest elaborate hygiene codes in Hindu texts (*Manusmriti*, *Vishnu Purana*). Bathing is a *nitya karma* (daily duty) in Hinduism. Ayurveda stresses *sattvic* diet, digestion, excretion; hygiene includes oil pulling, tongue scraping, detoxification.

**Americas**
*Mesoamerica:* Spanish chronicles describe universal daily bathing (Moctezuma bathed daily; commoners used fountains/rivers). *Temazcal* (steam bath): volcanic rocks heated by firebox, water poured for steam (*tlasas*), directed with bough, massage, stone scrub, herbal rinse. Ritual (goddess Toci) and therapeutic uses; still practiced in Mexico.
*United States:* Native Americans used plant-based methods (yucca soap/shampoo, chew sticks, sweat lodges, peppermint breath, charcoal whitening), regular river bathing exceeding European standards. Unwashed colonists transmitted novel microbes, devastating immunologically naïve populations.

**Europe**
*Antiquity:* Roman civilization hallmark: elaborate public baths (pools, saunas, spas, depilation), aqueduct-fed flow, large sewers (Cloaca Maxima), continuous-flow toilets, scented oils. Christianity emphasized hygiene; Church Fathers (Clement, Tertullian) encouraged bathing; Church built sex-separated facilities near monasteries/pilgrimage sites.
*Middle Ages:* Bathing/sanitation not lost post-Rome. Early Middle Ages: popes placed baths in basilicas/monasteries; Gregory the Great promoted bathing as bodily need. Biblical toilet etiquette encouraged post-defecation washing; bidets used where water essential. Public bathhouses common in larger towns (Constantinople, Paris, Regensburg, Rome, Naples). Cluniac monasteries (11th–12th c.) had bathhouses for upper class; ritual immersion at Christian festivals. Augustinian/Benedictine rules referenced ritual purification; Benedictines promoted therapeutic bathing/spas. Moralists suspected bathing/grooming as prelude to sin; cities regulated baths (Paris 26 baths overseen late 13th c.); guilds banned prostitutes. 14th c. Tuscany: newlyweds bathed together. Renaissance: public bathing popular (Hans Folz' *Bath Booklet*, Dürer's *Women's Bath*); Protestantism spurred British spa culture.
*Modernity:* Until late 19th c., only Western urban elite had indoor facilities; majority used communal cesspool facilities. John Snow (cholera = fecal water contamination) drove sewer adoption, flush toilets, indoor private bathrooms (decades for acceptance). Modern sanitation widespread 19th–20th c.; Lynn Thorndike notes Medieval Europeans likely bathed more than 19th-century people. Pasteur (germ theory), Lister (antisepsis) linked hygiene to health. Mid-19th c.: Semmelweis (Vienna) and Nightingale (England) pioneered hand hygiene for vulnerable mothers/soldiers; miasma theory (foul odors) still dominated.

**Middle East**
Islam stresses cleanliness; hygienical jurisprudence (7th c.) mandates *taharah* (ritual purity): *wudu* (ablution) for five daily *salah*, regular *ghusl* (bathing), water washing post-toilet. Abbasid Caliphate (8th–13th c.): Baghdad had 65,000 baths, sewers, hydraulic water systems for mosques/hammams. Medieval Islamic cities (Baghdad, Córdoba, Fez, Fustat) had sophisticated sewage/interconnected sewers; Fustat had 6-story tenements with flush toilets connected to water supply/underground channels. Ibn Sina (Avicenna) proposed contagion theory (1025): transmission via breath, TB contagion, water/dirt. Concept of invisible contagion (*najasat*) accepted; Ibn al-Haj al-Abdari (c. 1250–1336) warned of contagion contaminating water/food/garments. 9th c.: Ziryab invented deodorant, promoted morning/evening baths, likely invented functional/pleasant toothpaste popularized in Islamic Iberia.

**Sub-Saharan Africa**
West Africa: Yoruba used black soap for skin diseases. Southern Africa: Zulu used water stored in pottery at Ulundi. Himba (Namibia/Angola): smoke and *otjitze* mixtures for skin diseases in water-scarce regions.

**Soap and Soap Makers**
Hard scented toilet soap invented in Middle East during Islamic Golden Age; soap-making industrialized. Al-Razi (c. 865–925) described recipes, glycerine from olive oil. Syria: olive oil + alkali + lime; exported widely. Islamic innovations: bar soap (al-Razi), scented perfumes. 15th c.: Christendom manufacture industrialized (Antwerp, Castile, Marseille, Naples, Venice). 17th c.: Spanish manufacturers bought Castile soap monopoly. Late 18th c.: industrial bar soaps; advertising linked cleanliness to health. Christian missionaries distributed soap in Africa, China, Guatemala, India, Indonesia, Korea; hygiene became a marker of Christian identity.

### Society and Culture
**Religious Hygienic Customs:** Many religions mandate ritual purification. Islam: *wudu*/*ghusl* for prayer; post-toilet rules. Baháʼí Faith: hand/face washing before obligatory prayers. Orthodox Judaism: *mikveh* bath post-menstruation/childbirth; handwashing on waking and before bread. Christianity: water in rituals; Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church prescribes multiple handwashings (post-latrine/lavatory/bathhouse, pre-prayer, post-meal, ritual).

### Etymology
First attested in English 1676. From French *hygiène*, Latinization of Greek *hygieinē technē* ("art of health"), from *hygieinos* ("good for health"), from *hygiēs* ("healthful, sound, wholesome"). In Greek religion, Hygeia personified health, cleanliness, and hygiene.

## Terms
- ****Hygiene**** — Practices and conditions that maintain health and prevent disease spread, distinct from cleanliness (removal of dirt/microbes).
- ****Targeted Hygiene**** — Risk-based approach (using HACCP principles) focusing hygiene practices on critical times/places to break the chain of infection transmission.
- ****Chain of Infection**** — The sequence linking pathogen sources (people, food, water, animals) via highways (hands, surfaces, air, linens) to susceptible hosts.
- ****Infectious Dose**** — The number of viable pathogen units required to cause infection; can be very small (10–100 units or fewer for some viruses).
- ****Hygienic Cleaning**** — Procedures eliminating pathogens from critical surfaces: mechanical removal + rinsing, in situ inactivation (disinfectants/heat), or combined (laundering).
- ****Medical Hygiene**** — Practices preventing disease spread during medical care (isolation, sterilization, waste disposal, hand hygiene, antiseptics).
- ****Sleep Hygiene**** — Behavioral/environmental practices promoting quality sleep (schedule, stimulus control, avoiding stimulants/light); evidence for individual recommendations limited (2014).
- ****WASH**** — Acronym for Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene; integrated development cooperation framework for low-/middle-income countries (SDG 6).
- ****HACCP**** — Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point; systematic preventive approach to food/process safety adapted for targeted hygiene risk assessment.
- ****Miasma Theory**** — Pre-germ theory belief that infections were caused by foul odors ("miasmas"); dominant until mid-19th century.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Hygiene Hypothesis vs. Infection Prevention:** While early microbial exposure may protect against allergies, there is no evidence that harmful infections or clinical disease are necessary, nor that standard hygiene measures (handwashing, food hygiene) increase atopic disease. The conflict is largely perceived; consensus suggests lifestyle changes (reduced helminth exposure, etc.) drive immune dysregulation, but specific factors remain unidentified.
- **Laundering Guidelines:** Extreme variability in study designs (temperatures, cycle times, rinse counts, detergents) prevents standardization of hygienic laundering recommendations; agencies give significantly different advice.
- **Sleep Hygiene Efficacy:** As of 2014, evidence for the effectiveness of individual sleep hygiene recommendations is "limited and inconclusive," though clinicians still use assessment-based recommendations.
- **Antibiotic Resistance and Domestic Hygiene:** Healthy community carriers of resistant strains (MRSA, ESBL/NDM-1 enterobacteria) spread "silently"; laundry effectiveness may define community spread rates, but quantitative domestic transmission data is lacking.
- **Cultural Relativism vs. Universal Standards:** Hygiene practices vary culturally (e.g., anal cleansing methods, bathing frequency, contact avoidance), raising questions about universal guidelines versus context-specific adaptation, especially in global health (WASH) programs.
