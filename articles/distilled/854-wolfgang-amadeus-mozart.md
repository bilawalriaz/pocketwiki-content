# Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

## Overview

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart (1756–1791) was a Classical-era composer and virtuoso who produced over 800 works across every major genre of his time—symphonies, concertos, chamber music, opera, and choral music. Born in Salzburg as a child prodigy trained by his father Leopold, Mozart toured Europe extensively before settling in Vienna as a freelance musician. His music epitomizes the Classical style through clarity, balance, and emotional depth, yet also pushed boundaries with chromatic harmony and counterpoint. Despite financial struggles and early death at 35, Mozart’s legacy became legendary almost immediately, influencing generations of composers and securing his place among history’s greatest.

## Timeline

- **1756** — Born in Salzburg; baptized as Joannes Chrysostomus Wolfgangus Theophilus Mozart
- **1762–1773** — Grand tours of Europe and Italy with father and sister Nannerl as child prodigies
- **1773** — Employed as court musician in Salzburg under Prince-Archbishop Colloredo
- **1777–1779** — Left Salzburg to seek employment; toured Paris, Mannheim, Munich; mother died in Paris
- **1781** — Dismissed from Salzburg court; settled permanently in Vienna
- **1782** — Married Constanze Weber; premiered *Die Entführung aus dem Serail*
- **1784** — Joined Freemasonry; composed Masonic works
- **1786–1787** — Collaborated with Lorenzo Da Ponte on *Le nozze di Figaro* and *Don Giovanni*
- **1791** — Composed final masterpieces including *Die Zauberflöte*, Clarinet Concerto, and Requiem
- **1791** — Died in Vienna at age 35; buried in common grave at St. Marx Cemetery

## Body

### Sources and Biographical Challenges

Modern biographies of Mozart rely on approximately 1,500 pages of family letters, early biographies informed by relatives like Nannerl and Constanze, archival documents, and surviving manuscripts including sketches and autographs. However, these sources often conflict due to biased motivations—for instance, publisher Friedrich Rochlitz fabricated sensational stories to boost posthumous sales. Over time, scholars have grown less credulous and more attuned to historical context, leading to more nuanced interpretations of Mozart’s life and character.

### Early Life and Education

Born in Salzburg to Leopold Mozart, a minor composer and violinist, and Anna Maria Pertl, Wolfgang was the youngest of seven children, five of whom died in infancy. His elder sister Maria Anna ("Nannerl") was his first musical companion. Under Leopold’s strict tutelage, Mozart displayed extraordinary precocity—composing by age five and performing before European royalty during extensive childhood tours. These journeys exposed him to diverse musical influences, notably Johann Christian Bach in London and Italian opera in Milan. His early compositions, such as the solo motet *Exsultate, jubilate*, stand out even from this formative period.

### Career at the Salzburg Court

Returning from Italy in 1773, Mozart secured employment at the Salzburg court under Prince-Archbishop Hieronymus Colloredo. Though productive—writing violin concertos, piano concertos, masses, and operas like *La finta giardiniera*—he grew increasingly dissatisfied with low pay (150 florins/year) and limited opportunities for operatic work. Repeated failed attempts to secure positions elsewhere, including trips to Vienna and Munich, deepened his frustration. In 1777, he resigned and embarked on another job-seeking journey with his mother, visiting Munich, Augsburg, Mannheim, and Paris. Her death in Paris in 1778 marked a personal tragedy, but also freed him from familial obligation.

### Vienna Years: Rise and Struggles

After a final return to Salzburg in 1779, Mozart moved definitively to Vienna in 1781 following a bitter quarrel with Colloredo, who dismissed him "with a kick in the arse." In Vienna, he built a successful freelance career as performer and composer. His marriage to Constanze Weber in 1782 marked stability amid financial uncertainty. Major achievements included *Die Entführung aus dem Serail*, the Haydn Quartets, and numerous piano concertos. He joined the Freemasons in 1784, an affiliation that influenced later works like *Die Zauberflöte*. Collaborations with Lorenzo Da Ponte yielded *Le nozze di Figaro*, *Don Giovanni*, and *Così fan tutte*, establishing him as a leading operatic voice. Despite acclaim, economic hardship persisted; he borrowed heavily and lived modestly until late in life.

### Final Years and Death

Mozart’s last year was extraordinarily fertile. He composed *Die Zauberflöte*, the Clarinet Concerto, the final three symphonies (including the "Jupiter"), and the incomplete Requiem. Financial prospects improved slightly with distant patrons and imperial commissions, but he remained poor. Falling ill in November 1791, he died on December 5 at age 35. The official cause—"hitziges Frieselfieber" (severe miliary fever)—remains uncertain, with theories ranging from streptococcal infection to mercury poisoning. His modest funeral reflected Viennese custom, not public indifference; memorial services drew large crowds, and his reputation soared posthumously.

### Musical Style and Innovations

Mozart’s music embodies the Classical ideals of clarity, balance, and transparency, yet transcends them through emotional intensity and structural innovation. Early works reflect galant influences, but maturity brought integration of Baroque techniques—especially counterpoint learned from Bach and Handel via Gottfried van Swieten. Chromatic harmony enriched his palette, exemplified in the "Dissonance" Quartet (K. 465). He revolutionized the piano concerto, developing it into a symphonic form with profound expressive range. In opera, he elevated both comedy (*Figaro*) and drama (*Don Giovanni*) through psychological insight and sophisticated orchestration. His ability to blend beauty with raw emotion—"violence and sensuality"—defines his enduring genius.

### Legacy and Influence

Mozart’s impact extends far beyond his lifetime. Immediate efforts to publish his complete works began within months of his death, culminating in the Alte Mozart-Ausgabe (1877–1883) and later the comprehensive Neue Mozart-Ausgabe (1956–2007). His thematic catalog, initiated by Mozart himself and systematized by Ludwig von Köchel in 1862, uses K-numbers to identify works universally. Composers from Beethoven to Chopin honored him, while his operas remain central to the repertoire. Today, Salzburg celebrates him through museums, festivals, and the International Mozarteum Foundation. Notably, his music traveled beyond Earth—excerpts from *Die Zauberflöte* were included on the Voyager Golden Record.

## Terms

- **Köchel Catalogue (K/KV)**: Thematic catalog of Mozart’s works, compiled by Ludwig von Köchel in 1862, assigning numbers to each composition for identification.
- **Fortepiano**: An early version of the piano, lighter and more responsive than the harpsichord, which Mozart adopted in his later career.
- **Galant Style**: A mid-18th-century musical aesthetic emphasizing simplicity, clear phrasing, and light textures, contrasting with Baroque complexity.
- **Homophonic Texture**: A musical texture dominated by a single melodic line accompanied by harmony, characteristic of the Classical era.
- **Sturm und Drang**: A German literary and musical movement marked by emotional intensity and dramatic expression, influencing Mozart’s minor-key symphonies.
- **Singspiel**: A German form of opera featuring spoken dialogue interspersed with sung numbers, used in *Die Zauberflöte*.
- **Common Grave**: A burial practice in 18th-century Vienna for non-aristocrats, involving shared plots reused after a decade.
- **Autograph**: The composer’s original handwritten score of a completed work, crucial for authenticating and dating compositions.
- **Counterpoint**: The technique of combining independent melodic lines, revived in Mozart’s mature works under Bach’s influence.
- **Chromatic Harmony**: Use of notes outside the diatonic scale to create tension and color, prominent in Mozart’s late quartets and symphonies.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several aspects of Mozart’s life and death remain contested. The circumstances of his dismissal from Salzburg—whether it was truly as humiliating as reported—are debated, as are the exact nature of his relationship with his father, Leopold, whose controlling influence shaped much of his early career. The authenticity of certain anecdotes, such as his rivalry with Clementi or the legendary tale of writing down Allegri’s *Miserere* from memory, are questioned by scholars. Most enduring is the mystery surrounding his death: while "miliary fever" was recorded, over a hundred alternative diagnoses have been proposed, including rheumatic fever, trichinosis, and kidney disease. Some speculate poisoning, though no credible evidence supports this. Additionally, debates persist regarding the extent of his financial struggles versus his actual prosperity, and whether his late productivity reflected genuine artistic fulfillment or desperate attempts to earn a living.
