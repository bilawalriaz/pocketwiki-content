# Milton Friedman

## Overview
Milton Friedman (1912–2006) was an American economist and statistician whose work reshaped 20th-century macroeconomics and public policy. As the intellectual leader of the Chicago school of economics, he challenged the prevailing Keynesian consensus by reviving the quantity theory of money, establishing monetarism as a dominant paradigm. His research demonstrated that monetary contraction—not market failure—caused the Great Depression, and he introduced the permanent income hypothesis to explain consumption behavior. Friedman argued that inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon" and that government intervention often produces outcomes opposite to its intentions. His advocacy for free markets, floating exchange rates, school vouchers, a volunteer military, and a negative income tax influenced the Reagan and Thatcher administrations and post-communist reforms globally. He received the 1976 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.

## Timeline
- **July 31, 1912** — Born in Brooklyn, New York, to Jewish immigrants from Carpathian Ruthenia.
- **1932** — Graduates Rutgers University; enters University of Chicago for graduate study (M.A. 1933).
- **1935–1943** — Works in Washington, D.C. on New Deal programs (National Resources Planning Board, Treasury); helps invent payroll withholding tax.
- **1946** — Earns Ph.D. from Columbia University; joins University of Chicago faculty (30-year tenure begins).
- **1957** — Publishes *A Theory of the Consumption Function*, introducing the permanent income hypothesis.
- **1962** — Publishes *Capitalism and Freedom*, advocating school vouchers, negative income tax, volunteer military, and drug liberalization.
- **1963** — Co-authors *A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960* with Anna Schwartz, arguing the Fed caused the Great Depression.
- **1968** — Presidential address to AEA introduces natural rate of unemployment and vertical long-run Phillips curve; predicts stagflation.
- **1976** — Awarded Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.
- **1977** — Retires from University of Chicago; becomes senior research fellow at Hoover Institution, Stanford.
- **1980** — *Free to Choose* PBS series and bestselling book (with Rose Friedman) broadcast; advises Reagan campaign.
- **1988** — Receives Presidential Medal of Freedom and National Medal of Science.
- **Nov 16, 2006** — Dies in San Francisco at age 94.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Friedman was born in Brooklyn to working-class Jewish immigrants from Beregszász, Hungary (now Berehove, Ukraine). The family moved to Rahway, New Jersey; his father died during Friedman’s senior year of high school. A scholarship student, he graduated Rutgers in 1932, initially intending actuarial work but switching to economics due to the Depression. At the University of Chicago (M.A. 1933), he studied under Jacob Viner, Frank Knight, and Henry Simons, and met his future wife, Rose Director. A fellowship at Columbia (1933–34) with Harold Hotelling deepened his statistical training. He formed lifelong intellectual partnerships with George Stigler and W. Allen Wallis at Chicago.

### Public Service and Wartime Work
Unable to secure academic posts, Friedman joined New Deal agencies in Washington (1935). He supported job-creation programs (WPA, CCC) but opposed price/wage controls of the NRA and AAA, arguing they disrupted essential resource-signaling mechanisms. At the National Resources Planning Board, a consumer budget survey seeded his later consumption theory. At the National Bureau of Economic Research (1937), he assisted Simon Kuznets on professional income, producing *Incomes from Independent Professional Practice* (published 1945), which introduced permanent/transitory income concepts and argued licensing (e.g., by the AMA) restricted supply and raised physician wages. At the Treasury (1941–43), he advocated Keynesian tax policy and co-invented the payroll withholding system to fund WWII—a role he later regretted as enabling big government. He spent WWII (1943–45) at Columbia’s Division of War Research as a mathematical statistician working on weapons design and proximity fuzes.

### Chicago School and Major Theoretical Contributions
Returning to Chicago in 1946, Friedman built the "Workshop in Money and Banking," reviving monetary economics. With Anna Schwartz, he produced *A Monetary History of the United States* (1963), arguing the Fed’s failure to prevent a one-third decline in money supply (1929–33) turned a recession into the Great Depression—a view later acknowledged by Fed Chair Ben Bernanke.

**Permanent Income Hypothesis (1957):** In *A Theory of the Consumption Function*, Friedman challenged Keynes’s marginal propensity to consume. He modeled income as *y = y_p + y_t* (permanent + transitory components), arguing rational consumers base spending on expected long-term (permanent) income, smoothing consumption; windfall gains are mostly saved. This became mainstream.

**Monetarism and the Natural Rate (1968):** Friedman posited a stable demand-for-money function, asserting inflation is purely a monetary phenomenon. He introduced the **natural rate of unemployment**—determined by labor-market frictions—arguing the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at this rate. Expansionary policy only lowers unemployment temporarily if inflation is unexpected; once anticipated, unemployment returns to its natural rate with higher inflation (predicting 1970s stagflation). He advocated a **k-percent rule**: steady, small money-supply growth via a mechanical rule (or computer), rejecting discretionary fine-tuning.

**Methodology:** His 1953 essay "The Methodology of Positive Economics" argued theories should be judged by predictive accuracy, not realism of assumptions—a hallmark of the Chicago approach.

### Policy Advocacy and *Capitalism and Freedom*
*Capitalism and Freedom* (1962) translated technical economics into policy prescriptions: **volunteer military** (ended draft), **floating exchange rates** (articulated in 1953 paper), **school vouchers** (1955 article, later implemented in Chile, Sweden), **negative income tax** (cash-based poverty floor replacing welfare bureaucracy), **occupational licensure abolition** (including medical licenses), **drug legalization**, and **privatization** (e.g., postal monopoly). He viewed government’s role as protecting against force/fraud and providing true public goods, but warned intervention creates visible benefits for concentrated groups and hidden diffuse harms.

### Post-Retirement Influence
After 1977, Friedman advised Reagan (Economic Policy Advisory Board, 1981–89) and Thatcher (via IEA networks). The *Free to Choose* series (1980) popularized his ideas globally. He advised Eastern European and Chinese reformers (meeting Zhao Ziyang in 1988). His work on economic freedom measurement (with Michael Walker, Fraser Institute) produced the *Economic Freedom of the World* index.

### Controversies: Chile and Criticism
In March 1975, Friedman visited Pinochet’s Chile (post-1973 coup), lecturing on inflation control ("shock treatment": drastic money-supply reduction, spending cuts, labor-market liberalization). Chicago School graduates ("Chicago Boys") implemented reforms; inflation fell, growth resumed, and a 1990 transition to democracy followed. Friedman argued economic freedom fosters political freedom, but faced protests for engaging a dictatorship. He maintained he advised on technical economics, not politics, and gave identical lectures in communist states.

**Major Critiques:**
- **Nicholas Kaldor (Post-Keynesian):** Money is endogenously created by commercial banks, not exogenously by central banks.
- **David Hendry/Econometricians:** Empirical errors in UK money-demand estimates; t-ratios overstated ~100%.
- **Paul Krugman:** 2008 crisis showed central banks cannot control broad money (M3) in recession; money-GDP link breaks down.
- **Barry Eichengreen:** Gold standard constrained Fed in 1930s, limiting Friedman’s proposed remedy.
- **Austrian economists (Rothbard, Block):** Opposed central banking entirely; called Friedman a "socialist" for supporting the Fed.
- **Lawrence Mishel:** Friedman doctrine empowered corporate elites, suppressed wages, weakened unions, and drove inequality (1980–2020: productivity +70%, median compensation +12%, top 0.1% earnings +341%).

## Terms
- ****Permanent Income Hypothesis**** — Theory that consumers base spending on expected long-term average income (*y_p*), not current income; transitory income (*y_t*) is mostly saved, yielding consumption smoothing.
- ****Monetarism**** — Macroeconomic view that money supply is the primary determinant of nominal output and inflation; advocates steady money-growth rule (k-percent rule) over discretionary policy.
- ****Natural Rate of Unemployment**** — Unemployment rate determined by structural labor-market frictions; long-run Phillips curve is vertical at this rate—policy cannot permanently lower unemployment below it.
- ****Great Contraction**** — Friedman’s term (with Anna Schwartz) for 1929–33, when Fed inaction allowed money supply to fall ~⅓, turning a recession into the Great Depression.
- ****k-Percent Rule**** — Proposal that the central bank increase money supply at a fixed annual rate (e.g., 3–5%) via mechanical rule, eliminating discretionary policy.
- ****Negative Income Tax**** — Cash transfer system replacing welfare bureaucracy: households below an income floor receive a subsidy (negative tax), phasing out as earnings rise, preserving work incentives.
- ****School Vouchers**** — Public funding follows student to chosen school (public or private), introducing competition; proposed in 1955, later adopted in Chile (1981), Sweden (1992).
- ****Adaptive vs. Rational Expectations**** — Friedman assumed agents form inflation expectations adaptively (based on past); Lucas’s rational expectations (using all available info) revised the Phillips curve, making policy ineffective even short-run if anticipated.
- ****Sequential Sampling**** — Statistical quality-control method developed at Columbia’s Division of War Research; became standard for inspection by applying economic cost-benefit logic to sampling.
- ****Chicago School of Economics**** — Neoclassical tradition at University of Chicago (Viner, Knight, Simons, Friedman, Stigler, Lucas) emphasizing free markets, monetarism, price theory, and empirical rigor; produced multiple Nobel laureates.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Endogeneity of Money:** Whether central banks control money supply exogenously (Friedman) or accommodate endogenous credit creation by banks (Post-Keynesians, Kaldor). The 2008 crisis and quantitative easing revived this debate.
- **Feasibility of k-Percent Rule:** The Fed missed money-targets 1978–82; most nations adopted inflation targeting instead. Friedman himself later doubted quantity targeting.
- **Chile Legacy:** Whether Friedman’s technical advice legitimized Pinochet’s repression, or whether resulting economic liberalization enabled the 1990 democratic transition (Friedman’s "economic freedom → political freedom" thesis).
- **Inequality and Labor Power:** Critics (Mishel) argue Friedmanite policies (deregulation, anti-union, globalization) caused wage stagnation and soaring top incomes; defenders attribute trends to technology and global labor supply.
- **Methodological Positivism:** Whether predictive success justifies unrealistic assumptions (e.g., rational utility maximization, perfect competition) remains contested in philosophy of economics.
- **Fiscal vs. Monetary Dominance:** Friedman insisted monetary policy dominates; New Keynesians and MMT proponents argue fiscal-monetary interaction matters, especially at zero lower bound.
