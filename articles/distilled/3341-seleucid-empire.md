# Seleucid Empire

## Overview
The Seleucid Empire was a major Hellenistic state in West Asia, founded in 312 BC by Seleucus I Nicator after the division of Alexander the Great's empire. It was a vast, multi-ethnic realm that served as a primary conduit for Greek culture and administration across the Near East, but its immense size made it vulnerable to internal fragmentation and external pressures, leading to its gradual decline and eventual absorption by Rome in 63 BC.

## Timeline
- **323 BC** — Death of Alexander the Great; his empire is divided among his generals (the Diadochi).
- **312 BC** — Seleucus I Nicator recaptures Babylon, founding the Seleucid Empire.
- **305–303 BC** — Seleucid–Mauryan War ends with a treaty; Seleucus cedes territories east of the Indus to Chandragupta Maurya.
- **301 BC** — Victory at the Battle of Ipsus secures Seleucid control over eastern Anatolia and northern Syria.
- **c. 245 BC** — Diodotus declares independence in Bactria, forming the Greco-Bactrian Kingdom.
- **c. 238 BC** — Arsaces invades Parthia, establishing the Arsacid (Parthian) dynasty.
- **223–187 BC** — Reign of Antiochus III the Great, who revives Seleucid power but is ultimately defeated by Rome.
- **190 BC** — Seleucid defeat at the Battle of Magnesia leads to the Treaty of Apamea (188 BC), forcing territorial and financial concessions to Rome.
- **167–160 BC** — Maccabean Revolt in Judea against Seleucid rule and religious persecution.
- **129 BC** — Death of Antiochus VII Sidetes marks the final loss of the empire's eastern provinces to Parthia.
- **83 BC** — Tigranes the Great of Armenia invades Syria, effectively ending Seleucid rule.
- **63 BC** — Roman general Pompey annexes Syria, formally ending the Seleucid Empire.

## Body

### Foundation and Expansion
The empire was established after Seleucus I, a former general of Alexander, regained his satrapy of Babylonia in 312 BC. Through a series of wars against rival Diadochi, he expanded his control to encompass the former Achaemenid territories from Anatolia to the borders of India. A key early event was the war with Chandragupta Maurya (c. 305–303 BC), which ended in a treaty. Seleucus ceded territories along the Indus, including parts of modern Afghanistan and Pakistan, and received 500 war elephants in return, which proved decisive at the Battle of Ipsus (301 BC). He founded new capitals at Antioch in Syria and Seleucia on the Tigris.

### Hellenistic Culture and Administration
The Seleucid state was a major center of Hellenistic culture, where Greek language and customs were privileged. A Greek urban elite, reinforced by immigration from Greece, formed the dominant political and military class. The empire managed its diverse populations by generally tolerating local traditions while establishing hundreds of new Greek-style cities (*poleis*) for trade and administration. This process of Hellenization saw local elites adopt Greek language, philosophy, and names. The military relied on a core of Greek phalanxes, supplemented by native and mercenary troops, with soldier-settlers (*katoikoi*) granted land in exchange for service.

### Decline and Collapse
The empire's decline began with costly defeats by Rome, notably the Battle of Magnesia (190 BC), which led to the Treaty of Apamea. This treaty forced the Seleucids to pay a large indemnity and abandon all territory west of the Taurus Mountains. Concurrently, the empire's vast eastern provinces began to break away. The Greco-Bactrian Kingdom and the Parthian Empire emerged as independent powers in the 3rd century BC. Internal stability was further undermined by frequent civil wars. A major crisis was the Maccabean Revolt (167–160 BC) in Judea, sparked by the religious persecutions of Antiochus IV Epiphanes. By the 1st century BC, the Seleucid realm was a rump state in Syria, which was finally annexed by Rome in 63 BC.

### Economy
The Seleucid economy was primarily agricultural, with 80–90% of the population employed in this sector. The state extracted wealth through tribute from cities and temples, and a proportional land-tax on royal estates. A sophisticated monetary system existed, though it varied regionally: coastal areas adopted the Greek Attic standard, while Mesopotamia retained the older shekel system. The state paid its army in silver but also popularized bronze coinage for local trade. Recent scholarship indicates a partially market-oriented economy, where prices for goods like barley were subject to supply and demand, as evidenced by Babylonian astronomical diaries. The state actively managed irrigation and land reclamation, especially in Mesopotamia, to maintain agricultural productivity.

## Terms
*   **Diadochi**: The "successors," the generals of Alexander the Great who fought for control of his empire after his death.
*   **Hellenization**: The spread of Greek culture, language, and ideas to non-Greek peoples.
*   **Phalanx**: A dense infantry formation of soldiers armed with long pikes (sarissas), the core of the Macedonian and Seleucid armies.
*   **Satrapy**: A province of the ancient Persian (and later Seleucid) empire, governed by a satrap.
*   **Poleis** (sing. *polis*): Greek-style cities, which served as centers of administration, trade, and culture.
*   **Katoikoi**: Military settler-soldiers granted land in exchange for service in the Seleucid army.
*   **Attic Standard**: A system of coinage weights and measures originating in Athens, widely used in the Hellenistic Mediterranean.
*   **Anabasis**: A journey or expedition from the coast into the interior of a country.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The nature of the Seleucid economy is debated. While once characterized as either "modernist" (market-driven) or "primitivist" (autarchic), current scholarship views it as a hybrid system with partial market orientation but significant non-market production.
*   The exact outcome and details of the Seleucid–Mauryan War are unclear. Ancient sources do not definitively name a victor, and the treaty terms are reconstructed from later accounts.
*   The status and definition of "royal land" versus land owned by temples and cities (*poleis*) remain contested, affecting our understanding of state revenue and power.
