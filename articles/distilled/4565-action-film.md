# Action film

## Overview
The action film is a cinematic genre defined by sequences of physical spectacle—chases, fights, explosions, and stunts. Its scholarly definition is debated, with some arguing it prioritizes spectacle over narrative, while others see spectacle as integral to storytelling. The genre emerged as a distinct form in the late 1960s and 1970s, drawing from Westerns, crime, and war films, and has since evolved through distinct phases, heavily influenced by Hong Kong cinema and globalized production.

## Timeline
- **Late 1920s** — First Chinese-language martial arts films emerge in Shanghai.
- **1960s-early 1980s** — "Formative" phase of Hollywood action films, featuring anti-heroes and car chases.
- **1970s** — Hong Kong kung fu film sub-genre rises, led by Bruce Lee.
- **1980s** — "Classical" period of Hollywood action films, with weaponized heroes and generic formulas.
- **1986** — John Woo's *A Better Tomorrow* launches the "heroic bloodshed" genre in Hong Kong.
- **1990s** — "Post-classical" Hollywood era begins, influenced by Hong Kong aesthetics and CGI.
- **2000** — Ang Lee's *Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon* brings Chinese martial arts films to global prominence.
- **2003-2004** — Quentin Tarantino's *Kill Bill* films spark a return to 1970s-style vengeance narratives.
- **2010** — *The Expendables* culminates the neoclassical phase, resurrecting 1980s-era stars and themes.

## Body

### Characteristics and Scholarly Debate
The action film's core traits include chase sequences, fights, shootouts, explosions, and stunt work. A central scholarly debate concerns the relationship between spectacle and narrative. David Bordwell and Wheeler Winston Dixon argue that spectacle often overrides story, making the genre emblematic of Hollywood's worst tendencies. Conversely, Geoff King contends that spectacle can serve the narrative. Harvey O'Brien offers a synthesis, defining the action film as a "fusion of form and content" where the ethic of action is paramount. Yvonne Tasker notes the genre lacks consistent iconography, with its most common theme being a character's quest for freedom from oppression.

### Hollywood Action Film History
The contemporary Hollywood action film emerged from New Hollywood in the late 1960s and 1970s. Its **formative phase** featured anti-heroes and rogue cops (e.g., *Dirty Harry*, 1971) and emphasized car chases as spectacle. The **classical phase** of the 1980s solidified these tropes into a generic formula, producing heroes who were avengers, rogue officers, or weaponized men (e.g., *Die Hard*, 1988). The **post-classical phase** (1990s) was defined by Hong Kong aesthetics (wire-work) and CGI, with a decline in overt masculinity. Following the September 11, 2001 attacks, a **neoclassical phase** began, revisiting 1970s vengeance tropes and culminating in films like *The Expendables* (2010) that resurrected classical-era stars.

### Hong Kong Action Cinema
Chinese-language martial arts films began in Shanghai in the late 1920s but were banned in 1932. The genre revived in Hong Kong in the late 1940s. The 1960s saw a surge in "new school" swordplay films (wuxia) from studios like Shaw Brothers. The 1970s transitioned to the kung fu film, focusing on hand-to-hand combat and propelled to global fame by Bruce Lee. After a period of stagnation, the genre was revitalized in the 1980s by kung fu comedies (Jackie Chan) and modernized gangster films (John Woo). By the 2000s, Hollywood began importing Hong Kong talent, and films like *Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon* (2000) achieved global release status.

### Subgenres and Hybridity
Action films frequently hybridize with other genres, such as comedy, science fiction, and crime, complicating easy categorization. Key subgenres include:
*   **Wuxia:** The oldest Chinese martial arts genre, emphasizing chivalry and phantasmagoric swordplay.
*   **Kung Fu Film:** Emerged in the 1970s, focusing on ground-based martial arts and fighting schools.
*   **Heroic Bloodshed:** A Hong Kong genre of the late 1980s/early 1990s featuring gangsters, gunplay, and stylized violence, pioneered by John Woo.

### Gender in Action Cinema
Women have historically appeared in action films as romantic interests or sidekicks. The 1970s saw revenge-driven female leads, while the 1980s introduced physically muscular, transgressive characters like Ellen Ripley (*Aliens*, 1986). The 1990s and 2000s popularized the hypersexualized female action lead, a figure debated as either empowering or objectifying. This character often exists in a "post-feminist" discourse, combining active physicality with eroticized presentation, though her empowerment is frequently shown as accessible primarily to white, middle-class women.

## Terms
*   **Anti-hero:** A central character who lacks conventional heroic qualities like idealism or moral goodness.
*   **Wuxia:** A Chinese genre of martial arts fiction emphasizing chivalry, gallantry, and often fantastical swordplay.
*   **Kung Fu Film:** A martial arts film genre focusing on hand-to-hand combat, often rooted in specific fighting schools.
*   **Heroic Bloodshed:** A Hong Kong action genre characterized by stylized gunplay, melodramatic male bonding, and themes of honor and brotherhood.
*   **Wire-work:** A stunt technique using wires to create the illusion of weightlessness or superhuman agility, prominent in Hong Kong cinema.
*   **Neoclassical Phase:** The post-2001 era of Hollywood action films that revisited and updated tropes from the genre's classical period.
*   **Hybrid Genre:** A film that combines elements from two or more distinct genres, such as action-comedy or action-science fiction.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Spectacle vs. Narrative:** A core debate is whether action films prioritize visual spectacle at the expense of coherent storytelling.
*   **Genre vs. Mode:** Scholars increasingly question whether "action" is a distinct genre or a broader "mode" or "narrative form" that operates across multiple genres.
*   **Gender Representation:** There is ongoing debate about whether hypersexualized female action leads represent empowerment or objectification, and how their portrayal intersects with race and class.
