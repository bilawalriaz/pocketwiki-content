# Bengali language

## Overview
Bengali (Bangla) is a classical Indo-Aryan language of the Indo-European family, spoken natively by over 242 million people primarily in Bangladesh and the Indian states of West Bengal, Tripura, and Assam’s Barak Valley. It is the official language of Bangladesh and the second-most spoken language in India. The language evolved over 1,400 years from eastern Magadhi Prakrit through Apabhraṃśa, absorbing heavy Sanskrit, Persian, and Arabic influences. A 1948–1956 language movement against Urdu imposition in Pakistan catalyzed Bengali nationalism, leading to Bangladesh’s independence in 1971; UNESCO commemorates this struggle as International Mother Language Day. Bengali exhibits strong diglossia between the formal literary standard (Sadhu bhasha) and the modern colloquial standard (Chôlito bhasha), and its Brahmic-derived abugida script retains historical spellings that often diverge from modern pronunciation.

## Timeline
- **3rd century BCE** — Indo-Aryan arrival begins Sanskritisation of Bengal; eastern Magadhi Prakrit spoken.
- **782 CE** — Chinese poet Li-Yen’s Sanskrit-Chinese dictionary records 51 Bengali words, attesting Old Bengali.
- **6th century** — Ardhamagadhi evolves into Abahatta (Purbi Apabhraṃśa), competing with Bengali’s ancestor.
- **7th century** — Arab/Persian merchants arrive in Pala Empire, initiating Islamic linguistic influence.
- **13th century** — Turco-Persian conquests settle Arabic/Persian vocabulary; Dobhashi register emerges.
- **14th–18th century** — Middle Bengali period: elision of word-final *ô*, spread of compound verbs, Sultanate court patronage.
- **19th–early 20th century** — Modern standard Bengali codified on West-Central (Nadia/Kushtia) dialect; Sadhu vs. Chôlito styles formalised.
- **1948–1956** — Bengali Language Movement in East Pakistan; 21 Feb 1952 protests kill five activists.
- **1956** — Bengali recognised as a state language of Pakistan.
- **1971** — Bangladesh emerges as independent nation after Liberation War rooted in linguistic identity.
- **1999** — UNESCO declares 21 February International Mother Language Day.
- **3 Oct 2024** — Government of India grants Bengali classical language status.

## Body

### Historical Development
Bengali descends from eastern Magadhi Prakrit, the Middle Indo-Aryan vernacular of the Greater Magadha realm. During the Gupta Empire, Sanskrit dominated literary culture, exerting strong influence on the vernacular. By the end of the first millennium, Magadhi Prakrit evolved into Ardha Magadhi, then into Apabhraṃśa (locally Purbi Apabhraṃśa or Abahatta). The distinct Bengali language emerged gradually; the 782 CE Li-Yen dictionary provides the earliest secure attestation. The ancestor of Bengali served as the court language of the Pala Empire and Sena dynasty. During the early period, multiple dialects coexisted, and authors frequently wrote in more than one.

### Medieval Transformations and Islamic Contact
Middle Bengali (c. 1400–1800) is marked by the elision of the word-final inherent vowel *ô* and the proliferation of compound verbs derived from Sanskrit. From the 7th century, Muslim merchants introduced Arabic and Persian elements; the 13th-century Turco-Persian conquests entrenched this influence. Bengali absorbed vast Arabic and Persian vocabulary, creating *Dobhashi*, a highly Persianised register. Under Sultan Jalaluddin Muhammad Shah, Bengali supplanted Persian as the court vernacular, and subsequent Sultans patronised major works like *Yusuf-Zulekha* and *Srikrishna Kirtana*. Mughal conquest in the late 16th/early 17th century ended this court patronage.

### Modern Standardisation and Diglossia
The modern literary standard was forged in the 19th and early 20th centuries on the West-Central dialect spoken in Shantipur (Nadia district) and Shilaidaha (Kushtia district). This produced a profound diglossia: *Sadhu bhasha* (formal, Sanskritised, preserving Old Bengali inflections) and *Chôlito bhasha* (colloquial, simplified verbs, modelled on spoken West-Central speech). *Chôlito* became the dominant written standard through the works of Peary Chand Mitra, Pramatha Chaudhuri, and later Rabindranath Tagore. Vocabulary draws from Magadhi Prakrit/Pali (tadbhava), direct Sanskrit borrowings (tatsama), Persian/Arabic, Austroasiatic languages, and English.

### Language Movement and National Identity
In 1948, Pakistan’s imposition of Urdu as sole state language triggered the Bengali Language Movement in East Bengal. Protests peaked on 21 February 1952, when police killed five students and activists near Dhaka University—the first martyrs for a mother tongue. Bengali gained state language status in 1956. The movement forged a distinct Bengali nationalism that culminated in the 1971 Liberation War and the creation of Bangladesh. UNESCO recognised 21 February as International Mother Language Day in 1999.

### Geographical Distribution and Official Status
Bengali is the sole official language of Bangladesh (98% first-language speakers) and an official language in India’s West Bengal, Tripura, and Assam’s Barak Valley; it is a second official language in Jharkhand (since 2011). Significant communities exist in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, neighbouring Indian states, and a global diaspora (Middle East, North America, Europe, Southeast Asia). In Pakistan, it is a recognised secondary language in Karachi. The national anthems of Bangladesh (*Amar Sonar Bangla*) and India (*Jana Gana Mana*) were both written by Rabindranath Tagore in Bengali.

### Dialects
Bengali dialects form a continuum. Suniti Kumar Chatterji grouped them into four clusters: Rarhi, Vangiya, Kamrupi, Varendri. The West-Central (Rarhi/Nadia) dialect underpins the modern standard in both Bengals. Eastern dialects (Barisal, Dhaka) realise western stops/affricates as fricatives (e.g., *ch* [tʃ] → [ts/s]). Tibeto-Burman contact explains the loss of nasalised vowels and alveolar articulation of “cerebral” consonants in the east. Sylheti, Chittagonian, and Chakma possess contrastive tone. Mutual intelligibility varies: Chittagonian is barely understood by other Bengalis. Most speakers are bidialectal, fluent in Standard Colloquial Bengali (SCB) and a regional variety. Vocabulary in SCB also splits by religion: Muslims prefer Perso-Arabic and tadbhava forms; Hindus prefer tatsama.

### Phonology
Standard Bengali has 29 consonants, 7 oral vowels, and 7 nasalised vowels. It features numerous diphthongs (17–31 estimated); only /oi̯/ (ঐ) and /ou̯/ (ঔ) have dedicated graphemes. Stress is predominantly initial and trochaic (primary on first syllable, secondary on odd syllables). Native phonotactics forbid initial consonant clusters (max syllable CVC); speakers often simplify borrowings (e.g., *gram* → *geram*, *skul* → *iskul*).

### Writing System
The Bengali–Assamese script is an abugida derived from a modified Brahmic script c. 1000 CE. It has 11 vowel/diphthong graphemes, 39 consonant/modifier graphemes, and a distinctive horizontal headstroke (*matra*) linking letters. Consonants carry an inherent vowel ([ɔ] or [o]); the *hôsôntô* diacritic suppresses it. Vowel allographs (*kar*) form ligatures with consonants. Three other diacritics mark nasalisation (*chôndrôbindu*), velar nasal (*ônusbar*), and glottal fricative/gemination (*bisôrgô*). Consonant clusters (*juktôbênjôn*) are written as ligatures (nearly 285), often opaque; recent textbooks introduce “transparent” forms, but both coexist. Punctuation uses the *daṛi* (।) for full stop; other marks are Western. No uniform collating sequence exists yet.

### Orthographic Depth and Alternative Scripts
The script is shallow for tadbhava words but deep for tatsama words, preserving Sanskrit spellings despite sound mergers (e.g., three letters for [ʃ], two for [dʒ], silent letters in clusters). Inherent vowel quality ([ɔ] vs [o]) and deletion are not marked. Historically, Bengali has been written in Odia, Devanagari, Kaithi, Tirhuta, Newar, Sylhet Nagri (standardised c. 1869), Perso-Arabic (for *Dobhashi*), and Roman scripts. Romanisation efforts persist (e.g., *Benglish Books* 2018), but no standard prevails.

### Grammar
Bengali nouns lack grammatical gender. Nouns/pronouns decline for four cases (nominative, objective, genitive, locative) based on animacy; definite articles (-*ṭa* singular, -*gulo* plural) mark number. Counting requires measure words (classifiers) between numeral and noun (e.g., -*ṭa* generic, -*jôn* for humans), making all nouns behave like mass nouns. Verbs are heavily inflected for person (3), tense (3), aspect (3), and honour (3 levels), but not number; a single root can yield >200 forms. Word order is SOV (head-final); postpositions replace prepositions; determiners follow nouns. Yes–no questions use a falling (HL) tone on the final syllable plus optional particles (*ki*, *na*); wh-questions front the wh-word. Bengali is a zero-copula language in the present tense (*se shikkhôk* “he teacher” = “he is a teacher”).

### Vocabulary
Estimated ~100,000 words: 16% tadbhava (inherited Indo-Aryan), 40% tatsama (direct Sanskrit loans), 16% *deśi* (indigenous/Austroasiatic), 28% *bideśi* (foreign: Persian, Arabic, Turkish, English, Portuguese, French, Dutch). Persian was the official language for ~600 years until 1836, leaving deep lexical and grammatical influence. Contact with Austroasiatic languages, the Delhi/Bengal/Mughal Sultanates, and European colonialism drove borrowing. Modern colloquial usage shows increasing foreign-word adoption.

## Terms
- ****Abugida**** — A segmental writing system where consonant letters carry an inherent vowel modified by diacritics.
- ****Apabhraṃśa**** — “Corrupted” Middle Indo-Aryan stage (c. 6th–13th c.) transitional between Prakrit and modern languages.
- ****Chôlito bhasha**** — “Running language”: modern standard colloquial written Bengali with simplified verbs, based on West-Central speech.
- ****Dobhashi**** — A medieval register of Bengali heavily mixed with Arabic and Persian vocabulary, used for Muslim literary culture.
- ****Diglossia**** — Coexistence of two distinct language varieties (here, formal Sadhu and colloquial Chôlito) for different functions.
- ****Matra**** — The horizontal headstroke linking Bengali letters; its presence/absence distinguishes characters (e.g., ত vs ৩).
- ****Sadhu bhasha**** — “Upright language”: formal literary Bengali preserving Old Bengali inflections and tatsama vocabulary.
- ****Tadbhava**** — Words inherited from Sanskrit via natural phonological evolution in Prakrit (native core vocabulary).
- ****Tatsama**** — Words borrowed directly from Sanskrit with minimal phonological change; often retain archaic spellings.
- ****Zero copula**** — Grammatical feature where the verb “to be” is omitted in present tense equational sentences.

## Debates and open questions
- **Origins**: Whether Bengali derives from Gaudi Prakrit (M. Shahidullah) or eastern Magadhi Prakrit (S.K. Chatterji) remains debated; the 782 CE dictionary supports an 8th-century presence but the exact lineage is unresolved.
- **Dialect classification**: Multiple competing schemes exist (Chatterji’s four clusters vs. Sarkar’s 13+ dialects); the status of Sylheti, Chittagonian, and Chakma as dialects vs. separate languages is contested.
- **Glossia level**: Scholars dispute whether Bengali exhibits diglossia, triglossia, or heteroglossia given the range of written and spoken varieties.
- **Script reform**: The tension between preserving traditional opaque consonant ligatures and adopting “transparent” forms for learners remains unresolved; no uniform collation standard has been adopted.
- **UN official language**: Bangladesh and West Bengal have proposed Bengali for UN official status since 2010; as of 2023 no action has been taken, though the UN adopted it as an unofficial language in 2022.
