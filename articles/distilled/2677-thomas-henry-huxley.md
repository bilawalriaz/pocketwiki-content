# Thomas Henry Huxley

## Overview
Thomas Henry Huxley (1825–1895) was a leading English biologist and anthropologist, renowned as "Darwin's Bulldog" for his vigorous public advocacy of Charles Darwin's theory of evolution. His work in comparative anatomy, his famous debates with religious and scientific opponents, and his influence on scientific education were pivotal in establishing evolution as a scientific fact and shaping modern biology.

## Timeline
- **4 May 1825** — Born in Ealing, Middlesex.
- **1846–1850** — Serves as Assistant Surgeon on HMS *Rattlesnake* voyage to Australia and New Guinea, studying marine invertebrates.
- **1851** — Elected Fellow of the Royal Society at age 26.
- **1854** — Becomes Professor of Natural History at the Royal School of Mines.
- **1859** — Publishes *The Oceanic Hydrozoa*; Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* is published.
- **1860** — Debates Bishop Samuel Wilberforce at Oxford; publishes anonymous review of *Origin* in *The Times*.
- **1863** — Publishes *Evidence as to Man's Place in Nature*, addressing human evolution.
- **1864** — Founds the X Club, a dining club to advance science.
- **1869** — Coins the term "agnosticism."
- **1870** — Elected to the London School Board, influencing school science education.
- **1882** — Serves as pallbearer at Charles Darwin's funeral.
- **1885** — Retires from the Royal School of Mines due to ill health.
- **29 June 1895** — Dies in Eastbourne.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Huxley had little formal schooling, leaving at age 10 after his father's school closed. He became a determined autodidact, teaching himself German, Latin, and Greek, and mastering theology and science through extensive reading. After brief medical apprenticeships, he passed his First M.B. examination at the University of London with a gold medal but did not complete his degree, instead joining the Royal Navy.

### Voyage of the HMS *Rattlesnake* (1846–1850)
As Assistant Surgeon on the *Rattlesnake*, Huxley devoted himself to studying marine invertebrates collected during the voyage to Australia and New Guinea. His work on Medusae (jellyfish) led him to unite hydroids and medusae into a new class, Hydrozoa, recognizing their shared two-cell-layer structure—a key insight for the phylum Cnidaria. This research, published in *The Oceanic Hydrozoa* (1859), established his reputation and secured his election to the Royal Society.

### Advocacy for Darwin and the Evolution Debate
Initially skeptical of evolutionary theories, Huxley was convinced by Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859). He became Darwin's chief public defender, most famously in the 1860 Oxford debate with Bishop Samuel Wilberforce. Though the exact words are unrecorded, Huxley's rebuttal to Wilberforce's jibe about ape ancestry was widely seen as a victory, boosting public acceptance of evolution. His support was instrumental in countering opposition from figures like Richard Owen.

### Man's Place in Nature
Huxley directly challenged Richard Owen's claim that humans possessed unique brain structures absent in apes. Through public dissections and lectures, Huxley demonstrated that apes share key anatomical features (like the hippocampus minor) with humans. This work, culminating in *Evidence as to Man's Place in Nature* (1863), was crucial in establishing the biological kinship between humans and other primates, predating Darwin's *Descent of Man* (1871).

### Vertebrate Palaeontology and the Horse Evolution
Huxley's work on fossils revealed evolutionary relationships. He correctly linked birds to small carnivorous dinosaurs after comparing *Archaeopteryx* and *Compsognathus*. His study of fossil horse series from North America, provided by O.C. Marsh, demonstrated gradual evolutionary change from small forest-dwellers to large grazers, convincing Huxley of Darwinian gradualism.

### Educational Reform and Public Duties
As Professor at the Royal School of Mines, Huxley revolutionized biology education by emphasizing laboratory work and comparative anatomy, training a generation of leading biologists (e.g., Michael Foster, Ray Lankester). Elected to the London School Board in 1870, he advocated for broad secular education, including Bible reading for its moral and literary value, but opposed state-funded denominational teaching. He served on numerous Royal Commissions and was President of the Royal Society (1883–1885).

### Agnosticism and Later Writings
Huxley coined "agnosticism" in 1869, defining it as a method based on reason and evidence, not a creed. He argued that claims about the unknowable should not be treated as certain. In his 1893 Romanes Lecture, *Evolution and Ethics*, he contended that while human mental traits evolved, morality is a human construct, not derived from natural selection—a view opposing his friend Herbert Spencer.

## Terms
- **Comparative Anatomy**: The study of similarities and differences in the anatomy of different species, used to infer evolutionary relationships.
- **Hydrozoa**: A class of aquatic animals (e.g., jellyfish, hydroids) that Huxley unified based on their two-cell-layer structure.
- **Agnosticism**: A philosophical position, coined by Huxley, holding that the existence of God or the supernatural is unknown or unknowable, emphasizing evidence-based reasoning.
- **Gradualism**: The evolutionary theory that species evolve through the slow, steady accumulation of small changes over long periods.
- **Natural Selection**: The process whereby organisms better adapted to their environment tend to survive and produce more offspring, proposed by Darwin as the main mechanism of evolution.
- **Sauropsida**: A group defined by Huxley to include both reptiles and birds, highlighting their fundamental anatomical affinity.
- **Hippocampus Minor**: A brain structure in the cerebral ventricle; Huxley used its presence in apes to refute Owen's claim of human uniqueness.
- **X Club**: A dining club founded by Huxley in 1864 to promote science and scientific education in Britain.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The 1860 Oxford Debate**: While its outcome is seen as a victory for evolution, some historians question whether aspects of the surviving story are later fabrications, as no verbatim transcript exists.
- **Huxley's View on Natural Selection**: Huxley remained empirically cautious, arguing that natural selection could not be proven until it was observed to produce infertile varieties, a reservation that influenced later doubters of Darwinism.
- **Racial Hierarchies**: Huxley's classification of human races into categories based on physical traits has been criticized as supporting a racial hierarchy, though he was a staunch abolitionist and opponent of polygenism (the theory of separate human origins).
