# Mathematical proof

## Overview

A mathematical proof is a deductive argument that demonstrates a statement logically follows from accepted assumptions (axioms) and previously established results. Proofs establish logical certainty—unlike empirical or inductive reasoning, which only suggest plausibility—and are foundational to all of mathematics. The practice evolved from ancient Greek geometry through modern proof theory, which treats proofs as formal data structures.

## Timeline

- **c. 600–500 BCE** — Thales and Hippocrates of Chios give some of the first known geometric proofs
- **300 BCE** — Euclid introduces the axiomatic method in *Elements*
- **10th century CE** — Al-Hashimi proves algebraic propositions using "lines" rather than geometric measurements
- **1000 CE** — Al-Karaji introduces inductive proof in *Al-Fakhri*
- **1931** — Gödel's incompleteness theorem shows many axiomatic systems contain undecidable statements
- **1976** — Four color theorem proved via computer-assisted proof by exhaustion

## Body

### History and Etymology

The word *proof* derives from Latin *probare* ("to test"). Plausibility arguments using pictures and analogies preceded strict proof. Ancient Greek mathematics, particularly Euclid's *Elements* (c. 300 BCE), established the axiomatic method: starting from undefined terms and self-evidently true axioms, theorems are derived through deductive logic. Euclid's work covered geometry (e.g., Pythagorean theorem) and number theory (e.g., irrationality of √2, infinitude of primes). Medieval Islamic mathematicians advanced proof techniques: Al-Hashimi treated numbers abstractly rather than as geometric measurements, and Al-Karaji developed inductive reasoning for arithmetic progressions and the binomial theorem.

### Nature and Purpose

A proof is a rigorous natural-language argument intended to convince an audience of a statement's truth, though standards of rigor vary historically and communally. Formal proofs—sequences of formulas in a formal language, each a logical consequence of preceding ones—are studied in proof theory. This field reveals that most axiomatic systems generate undecidable statements. Philosophical debates persist: Kant viewed mathematical proofs as synthetic, while Quine challenged the analytic-synthetic distinction. Proofs are also valued aesthetically; Paul Erdős famously described elegant proofs as coming from "The Book."

### Methods of Proof

**Direct proof** combines axioms, definitions, and theorems to reach a conclusion (e.g., sum of two even integers is even). **Proof by mathematical induction** proves a base case and an implication rule (P(n) → P(n+1)), establishing truth for all natural numbers. **Proof by contraposition** proves "if p then q" by showing "if not q then not p." **Proof by contradiction** (*reductio ad absurdum*) assumes a statement is true and derives a logical impossibility (e.g., irrationality of √2). **Proof by construction** exhibits a concrete example to establish existence. **Proof by exhaustion** divides a conclusion into finitely many cases (e.g., four color theorem with 1,936 cases). **Nonconstructive proofs** show existence without providing a method to find the object. **Probabilistic proofs** use probability theory to establish existence with certainty. **Combinatorial proofs** show two expressions count the same object in different ways.

### Undecidability and Limits

A statement is *undecidable* from a set of axioms if neither it nor its negation can be proved. The parallel postulate is undecidable from Euclid's other axioms, leading to non-Euclidean geometry. Gödel's first incompleteness theorem demonstrates that sufficiently rich axiomatic systems (like ZFC set theory) necessarily contain undecidable statements.

## Terms

- **Axiom**: A self-evidently true assumption from which theorems are derived.
- **Conjecture**: A proposition believed true but not yet proved.
- **Deductive reasoning**: Logical inference where conclusions necessarily follow from premises.
- **Inductive reasoning**: Reasoning that establishes plausibility rather than certainty.
- **Induction (mathematical)**: A deductive method proving a base case and an implication rule to establish truth for all natural numbers.
- **Reductio ad absurdum**: Proof by contradiction; assuming a statement leads to a logical impossibility.
- **Undecidable statement**: A statement neither provable nor disprovable from a given set of axioms.
- **Q.E.D.**: Latin *quod erat demonstrandum* ("that which was to be demonstrated"); marks the end of a proof.

## Debates and Open Questions

Whether mathematical proofs are analytic or synthetic remains debated, with Kant arguing synthetic and Quine challenging the distinction. The validity of computer-assisted proofs is contested due to potential programming or runtime errors. Some philosophers argue that probabilistic evidence (e.g., Rabin's primality test) can constitute genuine proof, while most mathematicians reject this. The role of experimental mathematics and heuristic arguments in establishing mathematical truth continues to evolve with computing power.
