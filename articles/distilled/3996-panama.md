# Panama

## Overview
Panama is a country at the southern end of Central America, strategically located on the isthmus connecting North and South America. Its geography has defined its history, making it a vital crossroads for trade and a focal point for geopolitical interests, most notably through the Panama Canal. The nation's modern identity and economy are deeply intertwined with the canal, which it has controlled since 1999.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Isthmus of Panama forms, creating a land bridge between continents.
- **1501** — Rodrigo de Bastidas becomes the first European to explore the isthmus.
- **1519** — Pedro Arias Dávila founds Panama City.
- **1821** — Panama declares independence from Spain and joins Gran Colombia.
- **1903** — Panama declares independence from Colombia with U.S. backing.
- **1904-1914** — The Panama Canal is constructed by the United States.
- **1968** — Military coup begins the dictatorship of Omar Torrijos.
- **1977** — Torrijos–Carter Treaties signed, agreeing to transfer the Canal to Panama.
- **1989** — U.S. invasion (Operation Just Cause) removes dictator Manuel Noriega.
- **1999** — Panama assumes full control of the Panama Canal.
- **2016** — Panama Canal expansion project is completed.

## Body

### Etymology and Pre-Columbian History
The origin of the name "Panama" is uncertain, with theories linking it to a native tree, a Guna word for "distant," or a legendary fishing village meaning "abundance of fish." The isthmus was formed about three million years ago, affecting the dispersal of people and species across the Americas. Before European contact, it was inhabited by diverse Indigenous groups, including the Cueva. Early cultures like Monagrillo (c. 2500–1700 BC) produced some of the Americas' first pottery. The Indigenous population at contact is debated, with estimates ranging from 200,000 to two million.

### Colonial Era and Independence
Spanish colonization began in the early 16th century, with Panama becoming a crucial transit point for silver from Peru to Europe. The colonial period lasted nearly 300 years, during which Panama was part of the Viceroyalty of Peru and later the Viceroyalty of New Granada. Its strategic importance fostered a distinct identity but also vulnerability to pirate attacks and internal unrest. Panama declared independence from Spain in 1821 and joined Gran Colombia. After Gran Colombia dissolved in 1831, it became part of Colombia, enduring numerous secessionist attempts.

### The Canal and U.S. Influence
U.S. interest in a trans-isthmian canal led to Panama's secession from Colombia in 1903, tacitly supported by the U.S. The subsequent Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty granted the U.S. sovereign rights over a Canal Zone. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers built the canal, completed in 1914. The canal's strategic importance was paramount, especially during World War II. Growing Panamanian pressure for sovereignty led to the 1977 Torrijos–Carter Treaties, which provided for the phased transfer of the Canal and surrounding territory to Panama, completed on December 31, 1999.

### Military Dictatorship and U.S. Invasion
A 1968 military coup brought Omar Torrijos to power, initiating a populist dictatorship that reformed the constitution and expanded social services. After Torrijos's death in 1981, General Manuel Noriega consolidated control. His regime became involved in drug trafficking and repression, leading to U.S. sanctions. Following a fraudulent 1989 election, the United States invaded Panama (Operation Just Cause) to remove Noriega. The invasion caused significant civilian casualties and economic damage but restored elected government.

### Modern Panama
Since 1989, Panama has experienced peaceful transfers of power. Its economy is a high-income, service-based system driven by the Panama Canal, commerce, banking, and tourism. The canal expansion project, finished in 2016, doubled its capacity. Panama is a founding member of the United Nations and other international organizations. It abolished its standing army in 1990, maintaining only public security forces.

## Terms
*   **Isthmus of Panama:** A narrow strip of land connecting North and South America, flanked by the Caribbean Sea and the Pacific Ocean.
*   **Camino Real (Royal Road):** The colonial overland route across the isthmus used to transport silver from the Pacific to the Caribbean for shipment to Spain.
*   **Cimarrons:** Communities of Africans who escaped slavery and established independent settlements in colonial Panama.
*   **Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty (1903):** The agreement that granted the United States rights to build and control the Panama Canal Zone "in perpetuity."
*   **Torrijos–Carter Treaties (1977):** Agreements that provided for the gradual transfer of the Panama Canal and the Canal Zone from U.S. to Panamanian control.
*   **Darién Gap:** A remote, nearly impenetrable jungle region on the border with Colombia, breaking the Pan-American Highway.
*   **Colón Free Trade Zone:** The largest free trade zone in the Western Hemisphere, a major driver of Panama's commerce.
*   **Balboa:** The official currency of Panama, pegged 1:1 to the U.S. dollar, which is also legal tender.
*   **Pollera:** The traditional, elaborate white dress worn by Panamanian women for festivals and ceremonies.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact origin of the name "Panama" remains uncertain, with multiple competing theories.
*   The size of Panama's Indigenous population at the time of European colonization is debated, with estimates varying widely.
*   The precise number of Panamanian civilian casualties during the 1989 U.S. invasion is disputed, with estimates ranging from 200 to 4,000.
*   While Panama has made progress in financial transparency, the IMF and others note ongoing needs to strengthen its fiscal structure and fully combat its reputation as a tax haven.
