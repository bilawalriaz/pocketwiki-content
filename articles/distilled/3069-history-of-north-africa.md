# History of North Africa

## Overview
North Africa stretches from Morocco to Egypt, between the Sahara Desert and the Mediterranean Sea. Its history blends indigenous Berber (Amazigh) cultures, Mediterranean civilizations, Islamic conquests, and European colonialism, shaping the modern Maghreb and Egypt. Its strategic location made it a crossroads of trade, religion, and power for millennia.

## Timeline
- **c. 260,000 BC** — Earliest known humans live in North Africa.
- **c. 6000 BC** — Neolithic farmers become predominant in central North Africa.
- **332 BC** — Alexander the Great conquers Egypt; Ptolemaic rule begins.
- **146 BC** — Rome destroys Carthage, ending the Punic Wars.
- **30 BC** — Roman Emperor Octavian conquers Egypt, unifying the North African coast.
- **429 AD** — Vandal invasion disrupts Roman rule in North Africa.
- **642 CE** — Muslim armies conquer Egypt from the Byzantine Empire.
- **670 CE** — Uqba ibn Nafi founds Kairouan in modern Tunisia.
- **703 CE** — Arab forces defeat the Berber queen Al-Kahina.
- **909 CE** — The Fatimid Caliphate is established in Ifriqiya.
- **1041-1051** — The Zirid dynasty renounces Fatimid suzerainty, triggering Hilalian invasions.
- **1147-1269** — The Almohad Caliphate rules the Maghreb and Al-Andalus.
- **1229-1574** — The Hafsids rule Ifriqiya (modern Tunisia).
- **1574** — The Ottoman Empire conquers Tunis, ending Hafsid rule.
- **1869** — The Suez Canal opens in Egypt.
- **1950s-1960s** — Most North African states gain independence from European colonial powers.
- **2010-2011** — The Arab Spring begins with revolutions in Tunisia and Egypt.

## Body

### Prehistory and Ancient Civilizations
The earliest humans inhabited North Africa around 260,000 BC. During the Stone Age, a wetter Sahara supported hunter-gatherer societies like the Aterian culture. The gradual drying of the Sahara from 5500 BP onward created a barrier, pushing populations toward the Nile Valley. This desiccation increased population density there, contributing to the rise of ancient Egypt. Mainstream scholars view predynastic southern Egypt as a foundational community in northeast Africa, with pharaonic populations showing biological affinities to both southern African and Levantine/Mediterranean groups.

### Classical Period and Roman Rule
Greek and Phoenician colonists settled along the coast, drawing prosperity from Mediterranean trade. The Phoenician city of Carthage grew into a major power controlling the Western Mediterranean until its destruction by Rome in 146 BC. Rome subsequently annexed Carthage's empire and, in 30 BC, conquered Egypt, unifying the North African coast. Roman rule was coastal, facing constant threats from southern Berber tribes, which it managed with a network of frontier forts. The Vandal invasion of 429 AD disrupted this order until the Byzantine reconquest in the 6th century.

### The Arab Conquest and Islamic Dynasties
In the 7th century CE, the Rashidun Caliphate's armies conquered Egypt (642 CE) and advanced into the Maghreb, defeating Byzantine and Berber forces. After a pause due to the First Fitna, the Umayyad Caliphate continued the conquest, facing significant resistance from Berber leaders like Queen Al-Kahina. The Abbasid Caliphate later controlled Egypt and central North Africa, but western regions remained under local Berber dynasties, often adhering to Kharijite Islam. The Fatimid Caliphate, established with Berber support in 909 CE, later conquered Egypt (969 CE) and moved its capital to Cairo.

### Muslim Berber Empires
Following the Fatimids, a series of powerful Berber-led empires emerged. The Almoravids (11th century) and Almohads (12th century) were religious reform movements that unified the Maghreb and Al-Andalus. The Almohad Empire fragmented in the 13th century, succeeded by the Marinids, Zayyanids, and Hafsids. The Hafsids ruled Ifriqiya (modern Tunisia) from 1229 to 1574, presiding over commercial growth with Europe but also piracy that provoked European retaliation. Their empire was eventually caught between Spanish and Ottoman power struggles.

### Ottoman and Colonial Eras
From the 16th century, the Ottoman Empire loosely controlled coastal cities like Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli, while Morocco remained independent. In the 18th and 19th centuries, European powers colonized the region: France took Algeria, Tunisia, and Morocco; Britain occupied Egypt (especially after the 1869 opening of the Suez Canal); and Italy colonized Libya. The Suez Canal became a point of major international contention, leading to the 1956 Suez War after its nationalization by Egypt.

### Post-Colonial Period
Most North African states gained independence in the 1950s and 1960s. The post-colonial era has been marked by the unresolved dispute over Western Sahara, the rise of pan-Arab nationalism, and economic ties with Europe, formalized through agreements like the Global Mediterranean Policy (1972). The 2010-2011 Arab Spring uprisings began in Tunisia and Egypt, leading to government overthrows and regional instability.

## Terms
- **Berber (Amazigh):** The indigenous ethnic group of North Africa, with a distinct language and culture predating Arab conquest.
- **Maghreb:** The Arabic term for the western part of North Africa, typically including Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, and Libya.
- **Ifriqiya:** The historical region corresponding roughly to modern Tunisia, eastern Algeria, and western Libya.
- **Kharijism:** An early Islamic sect that rejected the caliphates of both Sunni and Shia Islam, influencing several Berber dynasties.
- **Fatimid Caliphate:** An Ismaili Shia caliphate founded in 909 CE that ruled across North Africa and the Middle East.
- **Almoravids & Almohads:** Successive Berber-led Islamic reform movements and empires that dominated the western Maghreb and Al-Andalus (Iberian Peninsula) in the 11th-13th centuries.
- **Hilalian invasions:** The 11th-century migration of Arab tribes (Banu Hilal and Banu Sulaym) into the Maghreb, which accelerated Arabization and linguistic change.
- **Suez Canal:** A man-made waterway in Egypt connecting the Mediterranean and Red Seas, a critical global trade route and geopolitical flashpoint since its 1869 opening.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Genetic Impact of Arab Migrations:** Studies debate whether Arab migrations to the Maghreb were primarily a demographic process involving significant gene flow or a more limited cultural and linguistic replacement.
- **Origins of Pharaonic Egyptians:** Scholars recognize population variability in pharaonic Egypt, with Upper Egyptians showing biological affinities to southern African populations and Lower Egyptians closer to Levantine/Mediterranean groups.
- **Almoravid Role in Ghana Empire's Decline:** While traditionally blamed for the destruction of the West African Ghana Empire, some scholars argue this is folklore and that Almoravid political agitation, not military conquest, was a more significant factor.
- **Classification of Genetic Haplogroups:** Researchers question the categorization of certain mitochondrial DNA haplogroups (e.g., U6, M1) as "Eurasian" in North African studies, arguing this reflects outdated racial typologies given their long-established presence in ancient African populations.
