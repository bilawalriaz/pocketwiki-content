# Green Revolution

## Overview
The Green Revolution (c. 1940s–1980s) was a period of technology transfer that dramatically increased global crop yields, particularly for cereals like wheat and rice. It is credited with saving over a billion people from starvation and reducing poverty, but its reliance on chemical inputs and monocultures has also led to significant environmental and social debates.

## Timeline
- **1940** — Henry Wallace's visit to Mexico leads to Rockefeller Foundation funding for agricultural research.
- **1943** — Mexico founds the International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center (CIMMYT).
- **1962** — A key rice cross is made at the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI) in the Philippines.
- **1966** — IR8 "Miracle Rice" cultivar is developed.
- **1968** — William S. Gaud first uses the term "Green Revolution" in a speech.
- **1970** — Norman Borlaug receives the Nobel Peace Prize for his work.
- **1971** — The Consultative Group on International Agricultural Research (CGIAR) is established.
- **1974** — India becomes self-sufficient in cereal production.
- **1990** — M. S. Swaminathan coins the term "Evergreen Revolution" to address ecological concerns.

## Body

### Origins and Spread
The Green Revolution began with research in Mexico, funded by U.S. foundations and supported by the Mexican government, to hybridize corn and wheat for arid climates. Led by agronomist Norman Borlaug, this effort made Mexico food-self-sufficient by 1956. The model, based on high-yielding varieties (HYVs) requiring irrigation, fertilizers, and pesticides, was then exported globally. It was adopted in the Philippines with IR8 rice and in India, where wheat and rice yields soared, averting predicted famines. Brazil later adapted the approach by amending its cerrado soil with lime.

### Technology and Production
The core innovation was the development of semi-dwarf, high-yielding cereal varieties bred to resist lodging (falling over) and respond well to chemical fertilizers. These were part of a "package" that included synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and controlled irrigation. This package significantly boosted production; cereal output in developing nations more than doubled between 1961 and 1985. However, the system became heavily dependent on fossil fuels for inputs and machinery, and the energy input per unit of crop increased over time.

### Socioeconomic and Health Impacts
The Green Revolution is widely credited with reducing poverty, raising incomes, and averting hunger. It contributed to a 25% increase in daily calorie consumption in the developing world and a substantial reduction in infant mortality. However, the benefits were uneven. The high cost of inputs often excluded small-scale farmers, leading to increased inequality and debt in some regions, as seen in the Philippines' credit scheme. The focus on staple grains also led to concerns about dietary quality and malnutrition.

### Environmental Impact
The environmental legacy is mixed. On one hand, higher yields per hectare reduced the need for agricultural land expansion. On the other, intensive monoculture farming led to soil degradation, water pollution from agrochemical runoff, and loss of biodiversity. The heavy use of nitrogen fertilizers increased emissions of nitrous oxide, a potent greenhouse gas. The IPCC notes that while the technologies reduced hunger, they also caused "inappropriate and excessive use of agrochemicals" and "significantly reduced crop and varietal diversity."

## Terms
- **High-Yielding Varieties (HYVs):** Crop cultivars, especially semi-dwarf wheat and rice, bred for increased grain production and responsiveness to chemical fertilizers.
- **Lodging:** The bending over or breaking of crop stems, which can prevent harvest; a problem HYVs were bred to avoid.
- **CIMMYT:** The International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center, a key research institution for Green Revolution wheat.
- **IR8:** A high-yielding rice cultivar developed in 1966, dubbed "Miracle Rice" for its productivity.
- **CGIAR:** The Consultative Group on International Agricultural Research, a global partnership of agricultural research centers.
- **Monoculture:** The agricultural practice of growing a single crop species over a wide area, a common feature of Green Revolution farming.
- **Ejido:** A system of communal land tenure in Mexico, relevant to the social context of the revolution's start.
- **Evergreen Revolution:** A concept aiming for productivity gains without ecological harm, proposed as a sustainable successor to the original Green Revolution.

## Debates and Open questions
- **Malthusian Critique:** Did the Green Revolution simply delay a Malthusian population crisis by temporarily boosting food supply, or did it lead to reduced population growth as some studies suggest?
- **Food Security vs. Production:** Did increased cereal production truly enhance food security, or did it neglect nutritional quality and dietary diversity, as argued by critics like Amartya Sen?
- **Environmental Sustainability:** Can the model of high-input agriculture be sustained, or does it cause irreversible damage to soil, water, and biodiversity?
- **Social Equity:** Did the Green Revolution exacerbate inequality by favoring large landowners with access to credit and inputs over small-scale farmers?
