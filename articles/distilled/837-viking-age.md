# Viking Age

## Overview  
The Viking Age (c. 800–1050 CE) was a period of Scandinavian expansion marked by raiding, colonization, trade, and conquest across Europe and into North America. It began with the 793 raid on Lindisfarne and ended with the 1066 Battle of Stamford Bridge. The era shaped the political, cultural, and genetic landscape of Europe, giving rise to kingdoms like Normandy, Kievan Rus', and the Danelaw.

## Timeline  
- **793** — Raid on Lindisfarne monastery, often cited as start of Viking Age
- **845** — Viking expedition reaches Paris via the Seine
- **866–867** — Great Heathen Army conquers Northumbria; establishes Danelaw
- **879** — Treaty of Wedmore ends Alfred the Great’s conflict with Vikings in England
- **911** — Treaty of Saint-Clair-sur-Epte grants Normandy to Rollo
- **985** — Erik the Red establishes Norse settlement in Greenland
- **1000** — Iceland converts to Christianity
- **1066** — Battle of Stamford Bridge; Harald Hardrada defeated, ending major Viking activity

## Body  

### Origins and Causes  
The Viking Age emerged from a mix of demographic pressure, economic opportunity, political fragmentation abroad, and technological innovation. Overpopulation in Scandinavia, especially among landless youth, combined with the wealth of urbanizing Europe and weak kingdoms created incentives for expansion. The development of advanced longships enabled long-distance travel and river navigation. Scholars debate whether push factors (internal Scandinavian pressures) or pull factors (wealth abroad) were primary drivers.

### Expansion and Settlement  
Vikings settled widely—from the British Isles and Iceland to Greenland and briefly North America (L'Anse aux Meadows). They founded key cities like Dublin, York (Jórvík), and Novgorod. In Eastern Europe, they became the Varangians, trading along the rivers to Constantinople and founding Kievan Rus'. In France, they established Normandy under Rollo. These settlements often evolved into distinct cultures, such as the Norse-Gaels and Normans.

### Military and Technology  
Vikings used clinker-built longships for speed and shallow-water access, enabling hit-and-run tactics and deep inland raids. They were skilled warriors using axes, spears, and swords, and employed tactics like chain-linking ships in battle. Their naval dominance declined as European powers adopted raised-deck warships. Navigation methods remain debated, possibly involving sunstones and polarizing crystals.

### Religion and Conversion  
Most Vikings practiced Norse paganism, venerating gods like Odin and Thor. Conversion to Christianity began in the 10th century, accelerating under monarchs like Harald Hardrada and Olaf Tryggvason. By 1000, Iceland had converted, marking the end of pagan dominance. Christianization was gradual and sometimes contested, as seen in Norway’s civil wars.

### Trade and Economy  
Vikings operated extensive trade networks linking Scandinavia to the Islamic world, Byzantium, and Central Asia. Key ports included Hedeby, Birka, and York. They traded furs, amber, slaves, and silver, and served as mercenaries (e.g., Varangian Guard). The Volga and Dnieper routes connected them to the Silk Road. Silver hoards like the Cuerdale Hoard reflect their economic reach.

### Culture and Society  
Old Norse was the common language, evolving into modern Scandinavian tongues. The Icelandic sagas preserve much of Viking literature and history. Women had legal rights, including property ownership and divorce. Runestones and burial goods reveal social hierarchies. Viking art blended animal and geometric styles. Their legacy includes linguistic influence on English and place names across Europe.

### Genetics  
Recent DNA studies confirm significant Viking migration and intermarriage. Modern populations in England, Ireland, and the Baltics show up to 6% Viking ancestry. Isotopic analysis traces individuals from Sweden to Byzantium, confirming historical accounts of Viking mobility.

## Terms  
- **Longship**: Clinker-built vessel with shallow draft, ideal for raiding and exploration.  
- **Knarr**: Broader cargo ship used for trade.  
- **Varangian**: Scandinavian trader/mercenary active in Eastern Europe.  
- **Danelaw**: Region of England under Danish control.  
- **Thing**: Open-air assembly for legal and political decisions.  
- **Runestone**: Inscribed stone commemorating the dead or recording events.  
- **Leidang**: Scandinavian fleet levy system.  
- **Norse-Gaels**: Mixed Norse and Gaelic culture in Ireland and Scotland.  
- **Kievan Rus'**: Early East Slavic state founded by Scandinavian rulers.  
- **Old Norse**: North Germanic language spoken during the Viking Age.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Scholars debate the relative importance of push vs. pull factors in Viking expansion. The "overpopulation" thesis is contested, with some arguing ideological motives (e.g., response to Christianization) played a larger role. The extent of Viking violence versus integration varies by region. The reliability of later sources like the Icelandic sagas as historical records remains disputed. Genetic evidence continues to refine understanding of Viking migration patterns and cultural assimilation.
