# Late antiquity

## Overview
Late antiquity (c. 3rd–7th centuries CE) marks the transformation from classical antiquity to the early Middle Ages across Eurasia, the Mediterranean, and the Near East. It encompasses the Roman Empire's reorganization, the Sasanian Empire's rise, the spread of Christianity and rabbinic Judaism, the formation of post-Roman kingdoms, and the emergence of Islam. Historians view it as an era of both disruption and continuity, where ancient institutions eroded while medieval ones began, crucial for understanding modern religious, political, and cultural foundations.

## Timeline
- **c. 224 CE** — Ardashir I founds the Sasanian Empire, replacing the Parthian dynasty.
- **284–305 CE** — Diocletian's reforms reorganize the Roman Empire, including the Tetrarchy.
- **312–313 CE** — Constantine the Great issues the Edict of Milan, legalizing Christianity.
- **325 CE** — Council of Nicaea establishes imperial sponsorship of church councils.
- **380 CE** — Theodosius I makes Nicene Christianity the state church via the Edict of Thessalonica.
- **395 CE** — Death of Theodosius I leads to permanent division of the Roman Empire.
- **410 CE** — Visigoths sack Rome.
- **476 CE** — Deposition of Romulus Augustulus marks the end of the Western Roman Empire.
- **527–565 CE** — Reign of Justinian I: legal codification, reconquest, and Hagia Sophia.
- **541 CE** — First outbreak of the Plague of Justinian.
- **602–628 CE** — Final Byzantine–Sasanian War exhausts both empires.
- **630s–650s CE** — Arab conquests under the Rashidun Caliphate overthrow the Sasanian Empire and seize eastern Roman territories.

## Body

### Definition and Historiography
The term "late antiquity" (from German *Spätantike*) was popularized by Peter Brown's *The World of Late Antiquity* (1971), challenging older "decline and fall" narratives (e.g., Edward Gibbon) by emphasizing transformation and renewal. Chronologically, it covers the late Roman Empire to the early Middle Ages. Interpretively, it examines how ancient worlds were reshaped. Its geographic scope extends beyond Rome to include the Sasanian Empire, Arabia, the Caucasus, Ethiopia, and diverse Near Eastern societies. Exact boundaries remain debated.

### Political History
The late Roman state emerged from the third-century crisis, with Diocletian and Constantine creating a mobile court, reformed army, and bureaucratic tax system. The eastern empire, centered on Constantinople, outlasted the west. The western provinces saw the Migration Period, where Germanic groups formed post-Roman kingdoms blending Roman and non-Roman traditions. Justinian I's reign (527–565) attempted western restoration through conquest and legal codification, but wars and plague strained the empire. The seventh century brought the devastating Byzantine–Sasanian War (602–628), followed by Arab conquests that ended the Sasanian Empire and altered the Near East's political geography.

### Society and Economy
The late antique economy was tied to state fiscal needs, with taxation funding armies and administration. Land was the primary wealth source, held by aristocracies, churches, and villages. Labor included slavery, tenant farming, and wage work. Rural communities exercised local agency through negotiation. Long-distance trade persisted in the eastern Mediterranean but declined in parts of the west, leading to more regional production. Cities transformed as Christian institutions gained prominence, with urban populations varying regionally—declining sharply in Britain but remaining vibrant in Constantinople.

### Religion and Philosophy
Religion was central. Christianity expanded from a persecuted sect to the Roman state religion, shaping law, education, and urban life. Rabbinic Judaism institutionalized through texts like the Mishnah and Talmud. Zoroastrianism was closely tied to Sasanian kingship. Manichaeism spread despite persecution. Islam emerged in seventh-century Arabia, leading to conquests that reshaped the Near East. Traditional polytheistic cults declined under imperial pressure, while classical philosophy (e.g., Neoplatonism) influenced both pagan and Christian thought.

### Law, Administration, and Institutions
Roman law was codified in the Theodosian Code (438) and Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis. Legal practice involved courts, advocates, and bishops as arbitrators. Administration relied on written communication and layered authority. Post-Roman kingdoms adapted Roman traditions, creating hybrid institutions. The late antique state governed through negotiation, facing corruption and tax evasion, revealing limits of imperial control.

### War, Migration, and Frontiers
Frontiers were zones of defense, diplomacy, and exchange. The Roman–Sasanian rivalry dominated eastern frontiers. The Migration Period involved overlapping movements of Goths and Huns, leading to post-Roman settlements. Militarization affected taxation and political legitimacy. The final Byzantine–Sasanian War (602–628) exhausted both empires, paving the way for Arab conquests.

### Plague, Climate, and Demography
Environmental factors like the Late Antique Little Ice Age (cooling from 536 CE) and the Justinianic Plague (first outbreak 541 CE) impacted agriculture and politics, though their scale is debated. Demographic changes are hard to measure, with regional variations. Population movements included soldiers, merchants, monks, and refugees, contributing to cultural exchange.

### Literature, Languages, and Education
Classical education in grammar and rhetoric persisted, adapted to Christian contexts. Greek and Latin remained key, but Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, and other languages flourished. The codex became the dominant book form, with monasteries preserving texts. Translation activity spread knowledge across regions.

### Art, Architecture, and Material Culture
Art reflected changes in patronage and religion, with imperial imagery incorporating Christian symbols. Church building transformed cities, while fortifications increased. Archaeology reveals everyday life through pottery, coins, and inscriptions.

### Regional Surveys
Regions followed diverse paths: the eastern Roman Empire persisted as Byzantium; western Europe saw post-Roman kingdoms; Sasanian Iran was a rival power; Egypt was a fiscal and monastic center; Syria and Palestine were dynamic hubs; Arabia led to Islam's rise; North Africa transitioned through Roman, Vandal, and Arab rule; and Central Asia and the Caucasus were strategic zones.

## Terms
- **Tetrarchy**: System of four simultaneous emperors introduced by Diocletian.
- **Codification**: Compilation of laws into authoritative codes, e.g., Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis.
- **Migration Period**: Movements of Germanic, Hunnic, and Slavic groups into the Roman Empire.
- **Plague of Justinian**: First pandemic of bubonic plague (first outbreak 541 CE).
- **Late Antique Little Ice Age**: Period of climatic cooling from c. 536 CE.
- **Rashidun Caliphate**: First caliphate after Muhammad, leading early Arab conquests (632–661 CE).
- **Chalcedonian**: Refers to Christ's two natures accepted at Chalcedon (451 CE), causing divisions.
- **Miaphysite**: Christian tradition rejecting Chalcedon, prominent in Egypt and Syria.
- **Zoroastrianism**: State religion of the Sasanian Empire, emphasizing dualism.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact chronological and geographical boundaries remain debated.
- Historians dispute whether the period was one of "decline and fall" or "transformation and continuity."
- The demographic and economic impact of the Justinianic Plague is contested.
- The role of climate change in political instability is debated, with regional variations.
- The nature of post-Roman kingdoms is debated—sharp break or continuity.
- The extent of urban decline in the west versus resilience in the east is discussed.
- The Sasanian Empire's relationship with diverse religious communities is reevaluated as a "state of mixture."
