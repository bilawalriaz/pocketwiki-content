# Wari culture

## Overview
The Wari were a Middle Horizon civilization (c. 500–1000 AD) in the south-central Andes of modern Peru. They formed the first known empire in the Andean region, unifying diverse ecological zones through administrative control, agricultural innovation, and a distinctive artistic style. Their collapse around 1000 AD, likely due to prolonged drought, marked the end of the Middle Horizon and the beginning of the Late Intermediate Period.

## Timeline
- **c. 500–600 AD** — Wari culture begins to flourish in the south-central Andes.
- **c. 600 AD** — Initial expansion; Wari take control of villages in the Carahuarazo Valley, establishing the administrative center of Jincamocco.
- **c. 600–800 AD** — Wari occupation of the Carahuarazo Valley, introducing terrace agriculture.
- **Pre-800 AD** — Wari expand to include the oracle center of Pachacamac and dominate former Moche territories.
- **c. 800 AD** — Centuries of drought begin; Wari culture starts to deteriorate.
- **c. 1000 AD** — The city of Wari is dramatically depopulated, signaling the empire's collapse.

## Body

### Expansion and Control
The Wari empire expanded through a combination of military conquest, agricultural dissemination, and possibly religious conversion. Archaeological evidence from the Carahuarazo Valley (c. 600 AD) shows they established administrative centers like Jincamocco, introduced terrace farming, and built storage facilities. Their influence extended to the coast, including the ancient oracle at Pachacamac, though the nature of control there is debated. The empire's reach encompassed much of the territory of earlier cultures like the Moche.

### Government and Society
Wari administration is not well understood due to a lack of written records, though they may have used *khipu* (knotted cords for recording). Social hierarchy is evident from burial evidence. A royal tomb at El Castillo de Huarmey (discovered 2013) contained wealthy female burials, indicating elite power. Excavations at Conchopata revealed a stratified society with servants, elites, and possibly governors. Bioarchaeological analysis of skulls from Conchopata shows patterns of violence, with more head wounds in males, suggesting systematic social roles and conflict.

### Architecture
Wari administrative centers were distinctive, often large, rectangular compounds made of rough fieldstones coated in white plaster. They lacked central ceremonial plazas and had few entrances, contrasting with the more open, federalized architecture of the contemporary Tiwanaku polity. A unique Wari architectural form was the small, D-shaped temple. These centers, found along a road network similar to the later Inca highway, helped project control over the countryside.

### Religion and Art
The Wari worshipped the Staff God, a major Andean deity, with some of its oldest depictions found on Wari textiles and pottery. They practiced animal sacrifice, as evidenced by complete camelid and guinea pig remains at Conchopata. Wari art is renowned for its highly standardized textiles, which featured abstract geometric motifs and may have encoded elite status. They also produced sophisticated polychrome ceramics and metalwork in silver, copper, and gold. Conchopata was a major ceramic production center.

## Terms
- **Middle Horizon**: An archaeological period in the Andes (c. 600–1000 AD) characterized by the rise of the Wari and Tiwanaku states.
- **Khipu**: A recording device consisting of knotted strings, used for accounting and possibly narrative, believed to have been used by the Wari before the Inca.
- **Staff God**: A major deity in Andean religions, often depicted holding staffs, considered a predecessor to Inca gods.
- **Terrace agriculture**: A farming method of cutting steps into hillsides to create flat planting areas, which the Wari helped spread.
- **Ritual wasteful consumption**: The practice of intentionally not consuming all food at a feast to display the host's wealth and social capital.
- **Polychrome**: Using multiple colors, a common feature of Wari ceramics.
- **D-shaped structure**: A distinctive small temple architecture unique to the Wari.
- **Late Intermediate Period**: The era following the collapse of the Middle Horizon states (c. 1000–1476 AD), marked by regional kingdoms.

## Debates and open questions
- The nature of Wari control over the Central Coast is debated: did they politically dominate the region, or did they interact with independent commercial states?
- The primary drivers of Wari imperial expansion are uncertain, with theories including religious conversion, the spread of agricultural technology, or military conquest.
- The specific causes of the Wari collapse around 1000 AD are linked to drought, but the exact social and political mechanisms of the decline are still studied.
