# Research

## Overview
Research is creative, systematic work undertaken to increase the stock of knowledge through the collection, organization, and analysis of evidence, with particular attention to controlling bias and error. It encompasses diverse forms—scientific, humanities, artistic, and others—distinguished by their epistemological foundations and methods. The process typically follows an iterative cycle of problem identification, literature review, hypothesis formation, data collection, analysis, and dissemination, though approaches vary significantly across disciplines. Research is validated through peer review and replication, and its professionalization has produced formal career structures and a global publishing system.

## Body

### Etymology and Definitions
The word "research" derives from Middle French *recherche* ("to go about seeking"), from Old French *recerchier* (re- + *cerchier/sercher*, "search"), first recorded in 1577. Definitions emphasize intentional knowledge-seeking: Creswell describes it as a process of steps to collect and analyze information to increase understanding, intersecting philosophical worldviews (postpositivist, constructivist, transformative, pragmatic), research designs (quantitative, qualitative, mixed methods), and methods (data collection, analysis, interpretation). Merriam-Webster includes studying existing knowledge: "investigation or experimentation aimed at the discovery and interpretation of facts, revision of accepted theories or laws in the light of new facts, or practical application of such new or revised theories or laws."

### Forms of Research
**Original (primary) research** produces new knowledge rather than synthesizing existing publications; its degree of originality is a major criterion for journal publication, typically assessed via peer review. **Scientific research** systematically gathers data to explain nature and enable practical applications, funded by public, charitable, and private sources. It follows a structural process: observations/topic formation, hypothesis (testable prediction), conceptual and operational definitions, data gathering (valid/reliable instruments), analysis, interpretation, hypothesis testing/revision, and conclusion. **Humanities research** employs methods like hermeneutics and semiotics, exploring issues contextually (social, historical, political, cultural, ethnic) rather than seeking a single correct answer; historical research uses primary sources and external/internal criticism. **Artistic research** (practice-based research) treats creative works as both research and object, accepting subjectivity and using qualitative/intersubjective tools; it is advanced by the Society for Artistic Research (SAR) and the Journal for Artistic Research (JAR). The 2016 Florence Principles specify seven points distinguishing the Arts doctorate from the scientific PhD.

### Steps in Conducting Research
Research often follows the **hourglass model**: broad spectrum → focused method → expanded discussion/results. Major steps: identification of research problem, literature review, specifying purpose, determining research questions, conceptual framework/hypotheses, methodology choice, data collection, verification, analysis/interpretation, reporting/evaluation, and communication. The process is iterative, not fixed. A literature review identifies gaps justifying the study; a gap engenders a research question, which may parallel a hypothesis. Data tests the hypothesis; analysis uses statistical methods (empirical research); results reject or fail to reject the null hypothesis. A reverse approach (findings → problem identification) is also advocated due to the transactional nature of research. Rudolph Rummel stresses that confidence requires consistent tests across many data, researchers, and methods. Plato's Meno paradox highlights the inherent difficulty: "If you know what you're searching for, why search? If you don't know, what are you searching for?"

### Research Methods
Three main forms: **exploratory** (identify/define a problem), **constructive** (test theories/propose solutions), **empirical** (test feasibility via evidence). Empirical research splits into **qualitative** (subjective, non-quantitative; seeks meanings, definitions, symbols; types include ethnography—culture of groups—and phenomenology—suited for health professions education) and **quantitative** (systematic empirical investigation of quantitative properties via numerical data and statistical methods; designs: experimental, correlational, survey; linked to positivism). Quantitative methods use random sampling and structured instruments for generalizability; true experiments require random assignment to treatments. **Primary data** is collected for the study (interviews, questionnaires); **secondary data** already exists (census data); ethical practice favors secondary data where possible. **Mixed-method research** combines qualitative and quantitative elements for broader insights. **Big data** reduces data collection effort and spawns new analytical methods. **Non-empirical (theoretical) research** develops theory from existing knowledge (e.g., drug prototyping, business process flow charts, cosmology, mathematics proving theorems); it complements empirical research in a cycle: observations → theoretical explanation → testable hypotheses → empirical testing → new observations.

### Research Ethics and Problems
**Metascience** (research on research) uses research methods to detect bias, methodological flaws, and inefficiencies, finding low reproducibility rates across fields. Key problems include the **replication crisis**, **academic bias**, **funding bias**, and **publication bias**. **Non-Western methods** challenge Western dominance: indigenous participation highlights cultural insensitivity in data collection (e.g., "Hua Oranga" for Māori psychological evaluation uses dimensions *taha wairua* [spiritual], *taha hinengaro* [mental], *taha tinana* [physical], *taha whanau* [family]). Simon Marginson argues for a plural university world, noting East Asian Confucian models may rise due to increased research funding in China/South Korea versus cuts in the UK/US. **Language bias (linguicism)** favors English; periphery scholars face translation burdens and misinterpretation of communicative styles as incompetence. Geographic bias correlates: French/Arabic official language countries are less studied; in Africa, English-speaking countries are overrepresented. **Generalizability** is limited by narrow scope (e.g., single-country studies in comparative politics, which have risen since the late 2000s; Western countries overrepresented, Latin America rising since 2000, Oceania/Caribbean underrepresented). **Peer review** maintains quality but is unpaid, leading to reviewer decline; periphery country publications rarely reach elite status. **Open research/open science/open access** movements assert knowledge should be free (public domain/humanity), but this clashes with indigenous conceptions where access is relationship-determined; a double standard is alleged: digital rights management for privacy is celebrated, while similar controls by cultural groups are denounced as censorship.

### Professionalisation and Publishing
In Russia and post-Soviet states, *nauchny sotrudnik* (researcher) is both a generic term and a formal job title with ranks: Junior Researcher, Researcher, Senior Researcher, Leading Researcher, Chief Researcher. **Academic publishing** enables peer review and wider dissemination; most work appears as journal articles or books, with theses/dissertations in dedicated databases. STM publishing covers science, technology, medicine. Fields have dedicated journals, though many are interdisciplinary. Business models shifted in the early 1990s to electronic licensing; open access is a major trend: **open access publishing** (free from publication) and **self-archiving** (author posts copy online). Studies suggest findings not frequently replicated should be discounted; all published studies should be assessed for validity/reliability.

### Research Statistics and Funding
Most scientific research funding comes from three sources: corporate R&D departments, private foundations, and government research councils (e.g., US National Institutes of Health, UK Medical Research Council), managed primarily through universities and sometimes military contractors. Senior researchers spend significant time applying for grants, which are necessary for research and serve as merit indicators. ## Terms
- ****Basic research**** — Research aimed at documentation, discovery, interpretation, and R&D of methods/systems for advancing human knowledge, as opposed to applied research.
- ****Epistemology**** — A theory of knowledge that shapes research approaches; varies within and between humanities and sciences.
- ****Meta-research (Metascience)**** — The scientific study of research practices; uses research methods to detect bias, flaws, and inefficiencies to increase quality and reduce waste.
- ****Peer review**** — Self-regulation by qualified professionals to maintain quality, improve performance, and provide credibility; in academia, determines a paper's suitability for publication.
- ****Primary data**** — Data collected specifically for the research (e.g., interviews, questionnaires).
- ****Secondary data**** — Data that already exists (e.g., census data) and can be re-used; ethical practice favors its use where possible.
- ****Hourglass model**** — Research structure: broad spectrum → focused method (neck) → expanded discussion/results.
- ****Hypothesis**** — A testable prediction designating the relationship between two or more variables.
- ****Operational definition**** — Details defining variables and how they will be measured/assessed in the study.
- ****Florence Principles**** — Seven points (2016) specifying the Doctorate/PhD in the Arts compared to a scientific doctorate, endorsed by AEC, CILECT, CUMULUS, and SAR.

## Debates and open questions
- **Reproducibility crisis**: Meta-research finds low replication rates across many fields, questioning the reliability of published findings.
- **Western methodological dominance**: Whether Western data collection methods are appropriate for non-Western societies; indigenous methods (e.g., Hua Oranga) challenge this hegemony.
- **East Asian vs. Western research models**: Divergent funding trends (East Asian growth vs. Western cuts) may shift global research leadership.
- **Linguicism and geographic bias**: English-language dominance excludes periphery scholars and skews research focus toward Anglophone countries.
- **Generalizability vs. depth**: Rising single-country studies sacrifice broad applicability for contextual detail; Western overrepresentation persists.
- **Peer review sustainability**: Unpaid review leads to reviewer fatigue and potential bias against periphery country publications.
- **Open access vs. indigenous knowledge sovereignty**: Open movements assume knowledge belongs to humanity; indigenous communities assert relational access control, creating a perceived double standard with digital privacy norms.
- **Artistic research legitimacy**: Ongoing debate over accepting subjectivity and practice-based outputs as rigorous research equivalent to scientific methods.
