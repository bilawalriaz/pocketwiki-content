# Fountain (Duchamp)

## Overview
*Fountain* is a 1917 readymade sculpture by Marcel Duchamp, consisting of a porcelain urinal signed "R. Mutt." It is a landmark of 20th-century art, fundamentally challenging traditional notions of artistic creation, authorship, and the definition of art itself by prioritizing the artist's conceptual choice over manual craft.

## Timeline
- **April 1917** — *Fountain* is submitted under the pseudonym "R. Mutt" to the inaugural Society of Independent Artists exhibition in New York.
- **April 1917** — The work is "suppressed" (hidden from view) by the exhibition committee, despite rules stating all fee-paying entries must be accepted.
- **May 1917** — A photograph of *Fountain* is published in the Dada journal *The Blind Man*, sparking public debate.
- **1917** — The original porcelain urinal is lost, likely discarded after the exhibition.
- **1935–1966** — Duchamp creates miniature reproductions for his *Boîte-en-valise* (box in a suitcase).
- **1950** — The first authorized 1:1 replica is made for a New York exhibition.
- **1964** — An edition of eight authorized replicas is manufactured in glazed earthenware.
- **2004** — *Fountain* is voted the most influential artwork of the 20th century by 500 British art professionals.

## Body

### Origin and Submission
Marcel Duchamp, recently arrived in the U.S., was involved in the New York proto-Dada movement. In early 1917, he purchased a standard Bedfordshire model urinal, reoriented it 90 degrees from its functional position, and signed it "R. Mutt 1917." He submitted it to the Society of Independent Artists exhibition, of which he was a board member, under a pseudonym. The committee, unaware of his involvement, debated its status as art. Though the society's rules mandated acceptance of all paid entries, *Fountain* was hidden from the show. Duchamp resigned in protest, calling the act one of "suppression."

### Controversy and Early Reception
The New York Dadaists publicized the controversy in *The Blind Man*. An editorial argued the work's importance lay in the artist's choice, not its making: "He CHOSE it. He took an ordinary article of life... created a new thought for that object." The piece was defended as a conceptual challenge, with one writer noting, "The only works of art America has given are her plumbing and her bridges." The original was lost after the exhibition, possibly discarded by photographer Alfred Stieglitz.

### Authorship Debate
Duchamp's authorship has been contested. In a 1917 letter, he wrote that "one of my female friends" submitted the work. Candidates include the Dadaist Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven and the poet Louise Norton. Recent research by Glyn Thompson identifies handwriting on the urinal as von Freytag-Loringhoven's and notes the specific urinal model was only sold in Philadelphia, where she lived and Duchamp never visited. Thompson also argues the signature "R. Mutt" could be a German pun on *Armut* (poverty), aligning with von Freytag-Loringhoven's known political commentary.

### Interpretations and Meaning
*Fountain* is interpreted as a radical gesture that shifted art from "retinal" (visual) to "cerebral" (conceptual). Duchamp stated he chose a urinal to select "the object which has the least chance of being liked." The reorientation and title transform its function, inviting interpretations from a "Buddha of the bathroom" to a sexualized "feminine" receptacle. The signature "R. Mutt" has been read as a pun on the Mott plumbing company, the comic strip *Mutt and Jeff*, and French slang for "moneybags." The work's power lies in its decontextualization, forcing a re-evaluation of what constitutes art.

### Legacy and Reinterpretations
*Fountain* invented conceptual art and severed the link between artistic labor and merit. It has inspired numerous interventions and reinterpretations. Artists have urinated in it (Kendell Geers, 1993; Brian Eno, 1993) or attacked it (Pierre Pinoncelli, 2006) as performance art. Others have created homage pieces, such as Sherrie Levine's bronze copies (1991, 1996) and Mike Bidlo's *Fractured Fountain* (2015). The work remains a central reference point for debates about authorship, originality, and the role of the artist.

## Terms
- **Readymade**: An ordinary, mass-produced object selected and designated as art by an artist, challenging traditional craftsmanship.
- **Proto-Dada**: An anti-rational, anti-art movement preceding the formal Dada movement, emphasizing absurdity and chance.
- **Suppression**: Duchamp's term for the act of hiding *Fountain* from the exhibition, distinct from formal rejection.
- **Cerebral art**: Art that prioritizes ideas and concepts over visual or aesthetic pleasure ("retinal art").
- **Acheropoietoi**: A term for an image not made by human hands, applied to *Fountain* as a found object.
- **Appropriation art**: The practice of borrowing, copying, or altering existing images and objects to create new work.
- **Catalogue raisonné**: A comprehensive, annotated listing of all known artworks by an artist.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authorship**: Was *Fountain* created by Marcel Duchamp, or was it submitted by him on behalf of a female friend, most likely Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven?
- **Originality of the Object**: Is the specific urinal model a unique Philadelphia product, as argued by Glyn Thompson, contradicting Duchamp's claim of purchasing it from J. L. Mott Iron Works in New York?
- **Meaning of the Signature**: Does "R. Mutt" primarily reference the Mott company, the *Mutt and Jeff* comic, a German pun, or French slang?
- **Status as Art**: Does the act of selection and recontextualization alone qualify an ordinary object as a work of art, or does this represent an "arrogant" overreach of artistic privilege?
