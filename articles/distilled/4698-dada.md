# Dada

## Overview
Dada was an international art movement that emerged during World War I (1916) as a radical rejection of the cultural, social, and political norms that its participants believed had led to the war. It was not a unified style but a network of anti-art practices—using nonsense, chance, and provocation—that spread from Zürich to major cities worldwide. Its legacy includes foundational techniques for modern art, performance, and institutional critique, directly influencing Surrealism, pop art, and Fluxus.

## Timeline
- **1916** — Cabaret Voltaire opens in Zürich, Switzerland; Hugo Ball and Emmy Hennings co-found it.
- **1916** — Hugo Ball writes the first Dada Manifesto.
- **1917** — The journal *Dada* begins publication in Zürich.
- **1918** — Tristan Tzara publishes his influential Dada Manifesto.
- **1918** — Richard Huelsenbeck delivers the first Dada speech in Berlin.
- **1920** — The First International Dada Fair is held in Berlin.
- **1921** — Marcel Duchamp's *Fountain* (a readymade) is rejected by the Society of Independent Artists in New York.
- **1923** — A riot at a Paris performance of Tzara's play *The Gas Heart* marks the split between Dada and the emerging Surrealism.
- **1924** — Dada activity in Paris largely dissolves into Surrealism.

## Body

### Origins and Principles
Dada coalesced in 1916 among émigré artists in neutral Zürich, Switzerland, fleeing the war. It was a protest against war, nationalism, and bourgeois culture. The movement had no single aesthetic; its character varied by location. Scholarship distinguishes between **destructive** tendencies (mockery, provocation) and **constructive** ones (seeking new art forms). Key figures included Hugo Ball, Tristan Tzara, Jean Arp, Marcel Duchamp, and Hannah Höch.

### Techniques and Style
Dadaists worked across media to blur art and life. They used **collage** (pasting heterogeneous fragments), **photomontage** (assembling press photographs, especially in Berlin), and **assemblage** (3D constructions from found objects). Marcel Duchamp's **readymades**—everyday objects declared art—became emblematic of Dada's **anti-art** stance. In poetry, they invented **sound poetry** (e.g., Hugo Ball) and used chance methods, like Tzara's technique of cutting words from a newspaper to create poems.

### Circulation and Centers
Dada spread quickly through journals, manifestos, and performances. Its main centers were:
*   **Zürich (1916–):** The birthplace, focused on performance and poetry at the Cabaret Voltaire.
*   **Berlin (c. 1918–1920):** More overtly political, using photomontage for social satire.
*   **New York (c. 1915–1923):** Less political, driven by irony and Duchamp's readymades.
*   **Paris (c. 1919–1924):** The final major hub, where internal disputes led to the rise of Surrealism.
Other significant groups existed in Cologne, Hanover (Kurt Schwitters' **Merz**), and internationally.

### Legacy
By the mid-1920s, Dada's energy in Paris merged into Surrealism. Its strategies of appropriation, performance, and institutional critique profoundly influenced 20th-century avant-garde movements, including pop art, Fluxus, and postmodern art. Its experiments with montage and intermedia laid groundwork for experimental film and performance art.

## Terms
*   **Anti-art:** A practice that challenges or negates conventional definitions and values of art.
*   **Readymade:** An everyday, mass-produced object selected and designated as a work of art by an artist (e.g., Duchamp's *Fountain*).
*   **Photomontage:** A technique of creating an image by assembling fragments of photographs, often for political satire.
*   **Sound poetry:** A form of poetry that emphasizes the sounds of words over their semantic meaning.
*   **Merz:** Kurt Schwitters' personal artistic practice, using discarded materials to create new unities, closely associated with but distinct from Dada.
*   **Simultaneous poem (poème simultané):** A performance where multiple speakers recite different texts at once, creating a chaotic effect.
*   **Cabaret Voltaire:** The Zürich venue where Dada performances and manifestos were first presented.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Destruction vs. Construction:** A core debate within Dada was whether its acts of negation were purely destructive or meant to clear ground for new art. Figures like Tzara emphasized mockery, while Schwitters and Arp sought constructive renewal.
*   **Origin of the Name:** There is no agreed origin for "Dada." Stories include it being found randomly in a dictionary, its infantile sound, or its multilingual neutrality.
*   **Political vs. Aesthetic Focus:** The movement varied by location; Berlin Dada was intensely political, while New York Dada was more theoretical and ironic. This raises questions about what unified the movement.
*   **Relationship to Futurism:** Dada was indebted to Futurist techniques (manifestos, noise) but rejected its nationalism and celebration of war.
