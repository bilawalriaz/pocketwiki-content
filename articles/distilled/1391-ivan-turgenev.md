# Ivan Turgenev

## Overview
Ivan Turgenev (1818–1883) was a Russian novelist, short story writer, and playwright whose work defined the golden age of Russian realism. His 1852 collection *A Sportsman's Sketches* is credited with shifting public opinion toward the abolition of serfdom, achieved in 1861. His masterpiece, *Fathers and Sons* (1862), introduced the archetype of the nihilist through the character Bazarov, dramatizing the generational clash between liberal aristocrats and radical youth. Unlike contemporaries Leo Tolstoy and Fyodor Dostoyevsky, Turgenev avoided religious moralizing, favoring a pessimistic, humanist liberalism influenced by Western European Enlightenment ideals and his close friendship with Gustave Flaubert. He spent much of his adult life in Western Europe, primarily near the opera singer Pauline Viardot, with whom he had a lifelong affair.

## Timeline
- **9 Nov 1818** — Born in Oryol to noble parents Sergei Turgenev and Varvara Lutovinova.
- **1827** — Family relocates to Moscow for the children's education.
- **1834–1837** — Studies Classics and philology at the University of Saint Petersburg; father dies (1834).
- **1838–1841** — Studies philosophy (Hegel) and history at the University of Berlin; adopts Westernizing views.
- **1843–1845** — Serves in the Ministry of Interior.
- **1852** — Publishes *A Sportsman's Sketches*; writes banned obituary for Gogol, leading to month-long imprisonment and exile to estate (writes *Mumu* during exile).
- **1854** — Moves to Western Europe (Baden-Baden/Paris), largely to follow Pauline Viardot.
- **1856–1862** — Publishes major novels: *Rudin*, *A Nest of the Gentry*, *On the Eve*, *Fathers and Sons*.
- **1862** — Hostile reception to *Fathers and Sons* prompts permanent departure from Russia.
- **1867** — Publishes *Smoke*; quarrels with Dostoyevsky in Baden-Baden.
- **1877** — Publishes final major novel, *Virgin Soil*.
- **1879** — Awarded honorary Doctor of Civil Law by University of Oxford.
- **3 Sep 1883** — Dies of spinal abscess (metastatic liposarcoma) at Bougival, near Paris; buried in Volkovo Cemetery, St. Petersburg.

## Body

### Early Life and Westernizing Formation
Born into an old but impoverished aristocratic family, Turgenev was raised at the Spasskoye-Lutovinovo estate by his authoritarian mother, Varvara, who controlled over 500 serfs. Her personality later inspired the tyrannical landlady in *Mumu*. Educated by foreign governesses, Turgenev became fluent in French, German, and English. After his father’s death in 1834, he studied at the University of Berlin (1838–1841), where he absorbed Hegelian philosophy and Enlightenment ideals. He returned convinced that Russia must reform by adopting Western European ideas, specifically opposing serfdom. A formative trauma occurred in 1837 when, aged 19, he was accused of cowardice during a steamboat fire in Germany—a rumor that haunted his reputation and inspired the story "A Fire at Sea."

### Literary Breakthrough and Conflict with Authority
Turgenev’s early poems attracted the praise of critic Vissarion Belinsky, who steered him toward sociological realism. His first major work, *A Sportsman's Sketches* (1852), depicted peasant life with unprecedented empathy and is widely credited with influencing the 1861 Emancipation reform. In 1852, his obituary for Nikolai Gogol—proclaiming "Gogol is dead!... He is gone, that man whom we now have the right... to call great"—was banned by the St. Petersburg censor but published in Moscow. Turgenev was imprisoned for a month and exiled to his estate for nearly two years. During this confinement, he wrote *Mumu* (1854), a devastating indictment of serfdom in which a deaf-mute peasant is forced to drown his beloved dog.

### The Major Novels and the "Superfluous Man"
Following the accession of Alexander II (1855) and a relaxation of censorship, Turgenev produced his greatest novels while living primarily in Western Europe. *Rudin* (1856) portrayed the "superfluous man"—an idealistic intellectual paralyzed by the inertia of Nicholas I’s Russia. *A Nest of the Gentry* (1859) and *On the Eve* (1860) explored nostalgic love and the rise of revolutionary commitment, respectively. *Fathers and Sons* (1862), set against the backdrop of the Crimean War defeat and Emancipation, centered on the nihilist Eugene Bazarov, "the first Bolshevik in Russian literature." The novel was attacked by both conservatives (for glorifying nihilism) and radicals like Dimitri Pisarev (for caricaturing it). The critical failure drove Turgenev from Russia and into a period of reduced output.

### Relationships with Tolstoy, Dostoyevsky, and Flaubert
Turgenev’s lack of religious motive and preference for Western Europe strained ties with the Slavophile-leaning Tolstoy and Dostoyevsky. Tolstoy initially despised him, challenging him to a duel in 1861; they did not speak for 17 years until Tolstoy wrote a reconciliatory letter in 1878, acknowledging Turgenev’s role in his own fame. Dostoyevsky parodied Turgenev as the vain novelist Karmazinov in *The Devils* (1872), but the two reconciled after Dostoyevsky’s 1880 Pushkin Speech, which moved Turgenev to tears. By contrast, Turgenev shared a deep, harmonious friendship with Gustave Flaubert; both rejected political extremes, favored artistic form over didacticism, and held a shared, pessimistic worldview.

### Later Years and Death
Turgenev’s later novels, *Smoke* (1867) and *Virgin Soil* (1877), received cooler receptions in Russia. He turned to shorter, personal works like *First Love* (1860), *King Lear of the Steppes* (1870), and *Poems in Prose* (1877–1882). He remained a fixture in European literary circles, serving as vice-chairman of the 1878 Paris literary congress alongside Victor Hugo and receiving an honorary doctorate from Oxford in 1879. In January 1883, a malignant liposarcoma was removed, but it had metastasized to his spinal cord. He died in agony on 3 September 1883 at Bougival. On his deathbed, he urged Tolstoy: "My friend, return to literature!"—prompting Tolstoy’s late masterpieces *The Death of Ivan Ilyich* and *The Kreutzer Sonata*. His brain, weighing 2,012 g, was recorded as one of the largest on record.

## Terms
- ****Serfdom**** — The feudal system binding Russian peasants to the land and the will of their noble owners; abolished in 1861.
- ****Superfluous Man**** — A 19th-century Russian literary archetype: an aristocratic intellectual alienated from society, unable to translate idealism into effective action.
- ****Nihilism**** — A philosophy rejecting established authority, traditions, and moral principles; in *Fathers and Sons*, embodied by Bazarov’s materialist skepticism.
- ****Westernizer**** — A member of the Russian intelligentsia who believed Russia’s progress depended on adopting Western European political and cultural models.
- ****Slavophile**** — An intellectual opponent of Westernizers, arguing Russia’s unique destiny lay in its Orthodox traditions and peasant commune (the *mir*).
- ****Intelligentsia**** — The educated, socially conscious class in 19th-century Russia engaged in cultural and political debate.
- ****Povest'**** — A Russian prose form longer than a short story but shorter than a novel; often translated as "novella" (e.g., *First Love*, *Asya*).
- ****Age of Enlightenment**** — 18th-century intellectual movement emphasizing reason, science, and individual liberty; the primary influence on Turgenev’s reformist views.
- ****Pauline Viardot**** — Celebrated Spanish-French opera singer; Turgenev’s lifelong romantic partner and hostess of his European salon.
- ****Vissarion Belinsky**** — Leading Russian literary critic of the 1840s; championed sociological realism and mentored Turgenev’s transition from Romanticism.

## Debates and open questions
- **Bazarov’s Intent**: Critics debated whether *Fathers and Sons* glorified or satirized the "new men" of the 1860s; radicals (except Pisarev) felt Bazarov was a caricature, while conservatives saw a dangerous hero.
- **Art vs. Propaganda**: Turgenev’s refusal to adopt the religious-moral framework of Tolstoy and Dostoyevsky raised the question of whether literature should serve social utility or aesthetic autonomy—a debate central to Russian criticism.
- **Nabokov’s Ranking**: Vladimir Nabokov ranked Turgenev fourth among 19th-century Russian prose writers (behind Tolstoy, Gogol, Chekhov; ahead of Dostoyevsky), praising his prose style but criticizing "labored epilogues" and "banal handling of plots."
- **Westernizer vs. Russian Soul**: Turgenev’s lifelong residence abroad and French-speaking household fueled accusations (notably from Tolstoy and Dostoyevsky) that he lacked authentic connection to the Russian *narod* (people).
