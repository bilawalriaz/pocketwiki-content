# Nebuchadnezzar II

## Overview
Nebuchadnezzar II (r. 605–562 BC) was the second and greatest king of the Neo-Babylonian Empire. His 43-year reign saw the empire become the dominant power in the ancient Near East, primarily through military campaigns in the Levant, most famously the destruction of Jerusalem and the Kingdom of Judah. He is also renowned for his massive building projects that transformed Babylon into one of the ancient world's most magnificent cities.

## Timeline
- **605 BC** — Defeats Egyptian Pharaoh Necho II at the Battle of Carchemish, securing Babylonian dominance.
- **605 BC** — Becomes king after the death of his father, Nabopolassar.
- **601 BC** — Fails in a major invasion of Egypt, weakening Babylonian prestige.
- **597 BC** — Captures Jerusalem, deports King Jeconiah, and installs Zedekiah as vassal.
- **586 BC** — Destroys Jerusalem and the Kingdom of Judah, initiating the Babylonian captivity.
- **c. 573 BC** — Ends a long siege of Tyre, destroying its mainland city.
- **562 BC** — Dies; succeeded by his son Amel-Marduk.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Nebuchadnezzar II was the eldest son of Nabopolassar, founder of the Neo-Babylonian Empire. His name, *Nabû-kudurri-uṣur*, means "Nabu, watch over my heir." He gained military renown as crown prince, notably commanding the army at the decisive Battle of Carchemish in 605 BC. He rushed back to Babylon upon his father's death and was crowned king within weeks.

### Military Campaigns and the Fall of Judah
Nebuchadnezzar's early reign was marked by a disastrous failed invasion of Egypt in 601 BC, which emboldened vassal states to rebel. In response, he conducted a series of campaigns in the Levant. His most consequential achievement was the siege and destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC, which ended the Kingdom of Judah and led to the deportation of much of its population to Babylon (the Babylonian captivity). He later besieged and captured the mainland city of Tyre after a long campaign. While these actions restored Babylonian dominance, his empire's borders did not significantly expand, and he never conquered Egypt.

### Building Projects
Nebuchadnezzar was a prolific builder who extensively renovated his capital, Babylon. His projects included the restoration of the Esagila temple (for Marduk), the completion of the Etemenanki ziggurat, and the construction of the magnificent Ishtar Gate and Processional Street, decorated with glazed bricks. He also built palaces, canals, and defensive walls like the Median Wall, using conquest wealth to fund these endeavors.

### Death and Succession
Nebuchadnezzar died in 562 BC after a 43-year reign. He was succeeded by his son Amel-Marduk, whose accession may have been troubled, as he was not the eldest living son and was possibly imprisoned by his father earlier.

## Terms
*   **Neo-Babylonian Empire:** The last great Mesopotamian empire, founded by Nabopolassar after the fall of the Neo-Assyrian Empire.
*   **Babylonian captivity:** The deportation and exile of the Jews from Judah to Babylon following its destruction in 586 BC.
*   **Vassal state:** A state that owes allegiance and tribute to a more powerful sovereign state.
*   **Ziggurat:** A massive stepped pyramid structure, typically a temple complex, common in ancient Mesopotamia.
*   **Cuneiform:** The wedge-shaped writing system used in ancient Mesopotamia.
*   **Babylonian Chronicle:** A series of cuneiform tablets recording historical events in Babylonia, providing a key source for Nebuchadnezzar's reign.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Historical Sources:** The scarcity of cuneiform sources from the middle of Nebuchadnezzar's reign forces historians to rely heavily on later biblical and classical texts, which may contain biases or legendary material.
*   **Military Effectiveness:** While celebrated for Carchemish and the destruction of Jerusalem, his failed invasion of Egypt and the subsequent rebellions suggest his military record was mixed.
*   **Succession:** The reasons for choosing Amel-Marduk as heir over older sons like Marduk-nadin-ahi are unclear, and their relationship appears to have been strained.
*   **Hanging Gardens:** Tradition credits him with building the Hanging Gardens of Babylon for his wife Amytis, but no archaeological evidence has been found to confirm their existence.
