# Refrigeration

## Overview
Refrigeration is the artificial cooling of a space or substance to maintain temperatures below the ambient level. Historically limited to harvesting natural ice, the technology evolved in the 19th century through mechanical vapor-compression and absorption systems, enabling reliable cold chains for food, industrial processes, and human comfort. This shift transformed global settlement patterns—allowing cities like Houston and Las Vegas to thrive in hot climates—revolutionized agriculture by centralizing production and reducing spoilage, and reshaped diets by making perishable goods available year-round. Modern refrigeration relies primarily on the vapor-compression cycle using synthetic refrigerants, though environmental concerns have driven regulation of ozone-depleting substances and research into alternative methods like magnetic and elastocaloric cooling.

## Timeline
- **<1000 BC** — Seasonal harvesting of snow and ice begins; Chinese *Shijing* describes ice cellar ceremonies.
- **1755** — William Cullen demonstrates first artificial refrigeration using diethyl ether under vacuum.
- **1805** — Oliver Evans describes a closed vapor-compression refrigeration cycle using ether.
- **1834** — Jacob Perkins builds the first working vapor-compression refrigeration system (closed-cycle).
- **1851** — James Harrison builds first mechanical ice-making machine in Geelong, Victoria.
- **1859** — Ferdinand Carré develops first gas absorption system using ammonia dissolved in water (aqua ammonia).
- **1876** — Carl von Linde patents improved method for liquefying gases (ammonia, SO₂, methyl chloride), enabling practical industrial refrigerants.
- **1882** — *Dunedin* completes first commercially successful refrigerated meat shipment (New Zealand to UK), founding the global frozen meat trade.
- **1911** — General Electric releases first gas-powered household refrigeration unit.
- **1927** — GE releases the "Monitor Top," the first electric household refrigerator.
- **1930** — Frigidaire synthesizes Freon (CFCs), enabling safer, cheaper home refrigerators; US household ownership exceeds 50% by 1940.
- **1987** — Montreal Protocol curtails worldwide use of CFC refrigerants due to ozone depletion.

## Body

### Early Cooling and Ice Harvesting
Before mechanical refrigeration, societies relied on consumable coolants: harvested ice, snow, and evaporative cooling. Ancient Chinese, Persian (Yakhchal pits), Greek, Roman, Egyptian, and Indian cultures stored ice or used evaporation to cool beverages and, in Persia, possibly preserve food. In the 19th-century US, Frederic Tudor commercialized New England ice harvests, shipping to the Caribbean and Southern states. Nathaniel Wyeth’s 1825 horse-drawn ice cutter reduced waste from 66% to 8%, turning ice into a mass-market commodity; New York City consumption rose from 12,000 tons (1843) to 100,000 tons (1856). This "cooling culture" normalized cold storage, paving the way for mechanical adoption.

### Mechanical Refrigeration Research
Artificial refrigeration began with William Cullen’s 1755 ether vacuum experiment. Benjamin Franklin and John Hadley (1758) proved volatile liquid evaporation could freeze water. Key 19th-century breakthroughs included Michael Faraday liquefying ammonia under pressure (1820), Jacob Perkins’ first working vapor-compression machine (1834), and James Harrison’s practical commercial systems (1850s) using ether, alcohol, or ammonia for breweries and meat-packing. Ferdinand Carré’s 1859 absorption system (ammonia-water) and Carl von Linde’s 1876 gas liquefaction patent established ammonia, sulfur dioxide, and methyl chloride as standard industrial refrigerants until the late 1920s.

### Commercial Adoption and Cold Chains
Pollution of natural ice and germ theory fears drove demand for manufactured ice by the early 1900s. The meat-packing industry led adoption: Chicago packing houses (Armour, Swift, Wilson) used ammonia-cycle refrigeration by 1900; nearly all locations used artificial refrigeration by 1914. Refrigerated rail cars (1840s, initially ice-cooled) enabled national distribution of perishables. The 1882 voyage of the *Dunedin*—fitted with a compression unit by William Soltau Davidson—launched the global frozen meat trade from New Zealand to the UK. By the 1890s, refrigeration was vital for food distribution; refrigerated trucks emerged mid-20th century, replacing rail by the 1960s with the interstate highway system.

### Home and Consumer Use
Households used iceboxes (Thomas Moore’s 1803 design) until ~1910. GE overcame size, cost, and safety barriers (toxicity, fire risk) of commercial units: a gas-powered unit (1911) reduced size, followed by the electric Monitor Top (1927). Frigidaire’s 1930 synthesis of Freon (chlorofluorocarbons/CFCs) replaced toxic refrigerants (ammonia, methyl chloride, SO₂), cutting prices from $275 to $154 and driving US ownership past 50% by 1940. CFCs were later found to deplete atmospheric ozone, leading to the 1987 Montreal Protocol phase-out.

### Impact on US Settlement and Agriculture
Refrigerated rail cars broke geographic dependence on rivers and harbors. Beef packers dominated early rail refrigeration, creating national markets and regional specialization: California (grapes, stone fruit), Georgia (peaches), Gulf States (strawberries), Mississippi (tomatoes), Southwest (cantaloupes). Dairy farms supplied cities from 400+ miles away. "Galactic cities" (Los Angeles, Houston, Phoenix, Las Vegas)—coined by Lewis Mumford—arose in previously inhospitable hot climates, sustained by refrigerated transport of food/water and agricultural productivity gains. US farm count fell from 6.8 million (1935) to <1% of population by 2007, yet output per capita soared due to technology, rural electrification (REA, 1935), and reduced spoilage.

### Effects on Lifestyle, Diet, and Nutrition
Refrigeration decoupled diets from seasons and locality. Supermarkets became feasible; out-of-season produce and distant meats/dairy became affordable. Daily shopping shifted to weekly trips, increasing leisure time. Nutrition improved: US dairy consumption rose 1.7% and protein intake 1.25% annually post-1890s; refrigeration accounts for ≥5.1% of adult stature increase via better nutrition. Studies link household refrigerator count to lower gastric cancer mortality. Sodium intake dropped as preservation shifted from salting to cold storage.

### Current Applications
Beyond food storage (optimal 3–5 °C) and air conditioning, refrigeration liquefies industrial gases (O₂, N₂, propane, methane), purifies compressed air, maintains low-temp processes in oil/petrochemical plants (e.g., alkylation for high-octane gasoline), tempers steel, and enables transport of temperature-sensitive goods. It was pivotal for Japan’s sushi/sashimi industry; Zojirushi’s mid-century refrigerator breakthroughs made safe raw fish handling accessible.

### Methods of Refrigeration
**Non-cyclic:** Melting ice, sublimating dry ice (to 195 K), or evaporating liquid nitrogen in portable coolers or chemistry baths.
**Cyclic (thermodynamic cycles):** Heat pumped from low-temp source to high-temp sink via external work.
- *Vapor-compression (reverse-Rankine):* Dominant method. Refrigerant (low-boiling hydrocarbon/HFC) compressed (1→2), condensed at constant pressure/temp (2→3→4), expanded via throttle valve causing flash evaporation (4→5), evaporated in coils absorbing heat (5→1). Cascade two-stage systems achieve very low temps.
- *Sorption cycles:* Replace compressor with absorber/pump/generator.
  - *Absorption:* Refrigerant dissolved in liquid absorbent (ammonia-water, water-LiBr). Low coefficient of performance (~1/5 of vapor-compression); used where heat fuel available but electricity scarce (RVs, industrial waste heat).
  - *Adsorption:* Refrigerant (adsorbate) binds to solid adsorbent (silica gel, activated carbon, zeolite). Noiseless, non-corrosive, environmentally friendly; researched heavily last 30 years.
- *Gas cycle (reverse Brayton):* Working fluid (usually air) compressed/expanded without phase change. Less efficient, bulky; used in jet aircraft (compressed air from engines) for cooling/pressurization.
**Thermoelectric:** Peltier effect at junction of two materials. Low efficiency (COP), compact; used for portable coolers, electronics, multi-stage cascade for cryogenic sensors.
**Magnetic (adiabatic demagnetization):** Magnetocaloric effect in paramagnetic salts (e.g., cerium magnesium nitrate). Align dipoles with strong field → heat released to sink → insulate → remove field → temp drops below sink. Limited to cryogenics/research (few materials work at room temp).
**Emerging/Other:** Elastocaloric (superelastic shape-memory alloys like nitinol: 16–20 K temp change, 66 J/cm³; hysteresis losses a challenge); vortex tube (spot cooling); thermoacoustic (sound waves); steam jet; thermoelastic; Stirling cycle; cryocoolers (Gifford-McMahon, Joule-Thomson, pulse-tube, dilution for mK range). **Fridge Gate:** Theoretical single-logic-gate cycle using ground/excited particle states for maximal thermodynamic efficiency. **Passive systems:** Radiative cooling + insulation + evaporation extends food shelf life 40% (humid) to 200% (desert) without power; requires water recharge every 4–30 days.

### Capacity Ratings
Capacity = evaporator enthalpy rise × mass flow rate. Units: kW or BTU/h (domestic/commercial in kJ/s or BTU/h; industrial in kW, except North America uses ton of refrigeration and BTU/h). **Coefficient of Performance (CoP)** = cooling capacity (kW) / energy input (kW). North American industry often uses **Performance Factor (PF)** = energy input (hp) / capacity (ton of refrigeration). Both defined at specific operating conditions. Residential AC uses **SEER**; commercial uses **EER** and **IEER**.

## Terms
- ****Refrigerant**** — Working fluid (heat-transfer fluid) that absorbs heat at low temperature and releases it at high temperature, typically undergoing phase change in a vapor-compression cycle.
- ****Vapor-compression cycle**** — Dominant refrigeration cycle: refrigerant compressed as vapor, condensed to liquid rejecting heat, expanded through throttle valve causing flash evaporation, then evaporated absorbing heat.
- ****Absorption refrigeration**** — Cycle using heat energy (not mechanical compression) to drive refrigerant circulation; refrigerant dissolved in liquid absorbent (e.g., ammonia in water), pumped to high pressure, then boiled out by heat.
- ****Adsorption refrigeration**** — Cycle where refrigerant (adsorbate) adheres to surface of solid adsorbent (e.g., silica gel, zeolite); pressure changed by heating/cooling the solid bed.
- ****Coefficient of Performance (CoP)**** — Ratio of cooling capacity (kW) to energy input (kW); primary efficiency metric for refrigeration systems.
- ****Ton of refrigeration (TR)**** — Cooling capacity unit: heat absorption rate to melt 1 short ton (2000 lb) of ice at 0 °C in 24 hours ≈ 3.517 kW or 12,000 BTU/h.
- ****Freon / CFCs**** — Trademark (DuPont) for chlorofluorocarbon synthetic refrigerants; non-toxic, non-flammable, but deplete stratospheric ozone; phased out by 1987 Montreal Protocol.
- ****Cold chain**** — Temperature-controlled supply chain (production, storage, transport) for perishable goods, enabled by refrigeration technology.
- ****Magnetocaloric effect**** — Temperature change in a magnetic material (paramagnetic salt) when magnetic field is applied/removed adiabatically; basis for magnetic refrigeration.
- ****Elastocaloric effect**** — Temperature change in superelastic materials (e.g., nitinol) under mechanical stress; exothermic on loading (austenite→martensite), endothermic on unloading.

## Debates and open questions
- **Refrigerant environmental impact:** Transition from CFCs → HCFCs → HFCs → natural refrigerants (CO₂, ammonia, hydrocarbons) continues; trade-offs between ozone depletion, global warming potential, flammability, toxicity, and energy efficiency remain unresolved.
- **Magnetic refrigeration at room temperature:** No known materials exhibit sufficient magnetocaloric effect near room temperature for commercial viability; research focuses on novel alloys and composite materials.
- **Elastocaloric hysteresis losses:** Energy dissipation during phase transformation (austenite↔martensite) limits efficiency; alloy tuning to minimize incompatibility between phases is an active materials science challenge.
- **Passive cooling scalability:** Radiative-evaporative hybrid systems show promise for off-grid food preservation, but water consumption (recharge every 4–30 days) and humidity dependence limit deployment in arid regions.
- **Fridge Gate and theoretical limits:** Single-logic-gate thermodynamic cycles remain theoretical; practical implementation faces particle-state control and energy-neutral swap engineering hurdles.
