# James Prescott Joule

## Overview
James Prescott Joule (1818–1889) was an English physicist whose precise experiments established the relationship between heat and mechanical work. This work provided the experimental foundation for the law of conservation of energy and the first law of thermodynamics, fundamentally changing the understanding of energy and leading to the SI unit of energy, the joule, being named in his honor.

## Timeline
- **24 Dec 1818** — Born in Salford, son of a wealthy brewer.
- **c. 1840** — Began investigating electric motors for brewery use, leading to his first scientific work.
- **1841** — Discovered Joule's first law (relationship between current, resistance, and heat).
- **1843** — Published first results on energy transformations, challenging the caloric theory of heat.
- **1845** — Presented his famous paddle-wheel experiment measuring the mechanical equivalent of heat.
- **1847** — Presented at British Association meeting in Oxford, where he met William Thomson (Lord Kelvin).
- **1848** — Married Amelia Grimes; met Thomson in Chamonix for an experiment.
- **1850** — Published a refined measurement of the mechanical equivalent of heat.
- **1852–1856** — Collaborated with Thomson, discovering the Joule-Thomson effect.
- **11 Oct 1889** — Died at home in Sale, England.

## Body

### Early Work and Challenge to Caloric Theory
Joule, tutored by John Dalton, managed his family's brewery while pursuing science as a serious hobby. His initial goal was to compare the efficiency of steam engines and electric motors. This led him to discover in 1841 that heat generated in a conductor is proportional to the square of the current and resistance (Joule's first law). By 1843, he published experiments showing this heat was generated within the conductor, directly challenging the dominant caloric theory, which held that heat was an indestructible fluid. This put the young, non-academic Joule at odds with established scientists like Sadi Carnot.

### Establishing the Mechanical Equivalent of Heat
To prove the convertibility of work and heat, Joule conducted a series of increasingly precise experiments. He measured the heat generated by forcing water through a perforated cylinder, by compressing a gas, and, most famously, by using a falling weight to spin a paddle wheel in water. His 1845 paddle-wheel experiment yielded a value of 819 foot-pounds per British thermal unit. A refined 1850 measurement of 772.692 foot-pounds per BTU (4.155 J/cal) was close to modern estimates. His insistence on extreme precision, aided by instrument-maker John Benjamin Dancer, was key to his credibility.

### Reception and Collaboration with Thomson
Initial reception was mixed due to the required precision and the conceptual leap of a kinetic theory of heat. A pivotal moment came in 1847 when William Thomson (later Lord Kelvin) heard Joule present. Though initially skeptical and a defender of Carnot's caloric-based theory, Thomson became convinced of Joule's work over the next two years. Their collaboration from 1852 to 1856, focused on experiments like the Joule-Thomson effect, helped secure general acceptance of the conservation of energy. Thomson's 1851 synthesis declared the theory of heat's motive power was founded on propositions from both Joule and Carnot/Clausius.

### Kinetic Theory and Legacy
Joule believed heat was a form of rotational molecular motion, a key idea in the kinetic theory of gases. This required assuming perfectly elastic molecular collisions, a concept difficult to prove at the time as atomic theory was not yet widely accepted. He sought precursors for his ideas in earlier scientists like Rumford. His work, alongside that of Julius Robert von Mayer and Hermann von Helmholtz, established the conservation of energy. He received numerous honors, including the Royal Medal (1852) and Copley Medal (1870), and is memorialized in Westminster Abbey.

## Terms
- **Mechanical equivalent of heat**: The amount of mechanical work required to produce a given amount of heat, measured by Joule as approximately 4.18 joules per calorie.
- **Caloric theory**: The obsolete theory that heat is a self-repellent fluid called "caloric" that flows between objects, which Joule's work disproved.
- **Joule's first law**: The relationship stating that the heat produced in a conductor is proportional to the square of the electric current multiplied by the resistance.
- **Kinetic theory of heat**: The theory that heat is a form of energy associated with the motion of particles within matter.
- **Vis viva**: An early term for kinetic energy, meaning "living force," used by Joule in his writings.
- **Joule-Thomson effect**: The temperature change of a real gas when it is forced through a valve or porous plug while kept insulated, discovered in Joule's collaboration with Thomson.
- **Absolute thermodynamic temperature scale**: A temperature scale (later the Kelvin scale) based on the principles of thermodynamics, developed by Joule and Thomson.

## Debates and Open questions
- **Priority and recognition**: Joule's work was initially neglected, and the conservation of energy is also credited to Julius Robert von Mayer and Hermann von Helmholtz. The relative contributions and timing of their discoveries are a subject of historical analysis.
- **Conceptual barriers**: Joule's kinetic theory required the then-unproven assumptions of perfectly elastic molecular collisions and the existence of atoms/molecules, which were not widely accepted for another 50 years.
- **Theological motivation**: Joule's rejection of the caloric theory was partly driven by a theological belief that the "annihilation of force" was only possible for the Creator, adding a philosophical dimension to the scientific debate.
