# Concerto

## Overview
A concerto is an instrumental composition, typically for a soloist accompanied by an orchestra, that evolved from vocal music in the late 16th century. It became a central genre of Western classical music, showcasing virtuosity and the dialogue between soloist and ensemble, and its form has continuously expanded to include a vast array of instruments.

## Timeline
- **1587** — First use of "concerto" in a music print title (Andrea & Giovanni Gabrieli).
- **Late 17th C.** — Instrumental concerto emerges with Italian composers like Corelli and Torelli.
- **Early 18th C.** — Standard three-movement structure (fast-slow-fast) becomes common.
- **c. 1720s** — Antonio Vivaldi publishes hundreds of violin concertos and concerti grossi.
- **c. 1730s** — First keyboard concertos appear (Handel's organ, Bach's harpsichord).
- **Late 18th C.** — Classical era: Mozart, Haydn, and Beethoven write major piano and violin concertos.
- **19th C.** — Romantic era: Concertos become longer, more virtuosic, and emotionally ambitious.
- **Early 20th C.** — Modernist innovations (atonality, new techniques) reshape the concerto form.
- **1945** — Béla Bartók's *Concerto for Orchestra* treats the orchestra itself as a virtuosic soloist.
- **Late 20th-21st C.** — Proliferation of concertos for non-traditional and folk instruments.

## Body

### Origins and Baroque Era
The concerto originated as a genre of vocal music in the late 16th century, where instruments had independent parts rather than simply doubling voices. The instrumental concerto developed in the late Baroque period, beginning with the *concerto grosso* form of Arcangelo Corelli, which featured a small group of soloists (*concertino*) against the full orchestra (*ripeno*). Antonio Vivaldi later standardized the solo concerto, writing hundreds for violin and other instruments. The typical three-movement structure (fast-slow-fast) became standard in the early 18th century. The first keyboard concertos, by Handel and Bach, appeared around this time.

### Classical and Romantic Eras
In the Classical era, composers like Mozart, Haydn, and Beethoven refined the concerto, integrating it with sonata form. Mozart wrote 27 piano concertos, while Beethoven's five increased the work's scale and symphonic ambition. The piano became the dominant concerto instrument. During the 19th-century Romantic era, the concerto flourished as a vehicle for virtuosity, with composers like Chopin, Liszt, Brahms, and Tchaikovsky writing complex, emotionally charged works. The cello also gained prominence as a solo concerto instrument.

### 20th and 21st Centuries
The 20th century saw radical innovations in musical language (e.g., atonality, twelve-tone technique) that affected the concerto, redefining virtuosity to include extended techniques and new sounds. Composers like Schoenberg, Berg, and Bartók wrote influential concertos. A major trend was the expansion of the repertoire to include nearly all orchestral instruments (clarinet, viola, French horn) and even non-traditional instruments like the accordion, harmonica, and rock band. The *concerto for orchestra*, where the orchestra itself is the virtuosic force, was pioneered by Bartók. This expansion continues into the 21st century.

## Terms
- **Concerto**: An instrumental composition for one or more soloists accompanied by an orchestra or ensemble.
- **Concerto Grosso**: A Baroque form featuring a small group of soloists (*concertino*) contrasted with the full orchestra (*ripeno*).
- **Ripieno**: The full orchestra or ensemble in a concerto grosso, contrasting with the soloists.
- **Concertino**: The small group of soloists in a concerto grosso.
- **Sonata Form**: A musical structure (exposition, development, recapitulation) commonly used in the first movement of Classical-era concertos.
- **Virtuosity**: Exceptional technical skill and brilliance in performance, a key feature of Romantic-era concertos.
- **Concerto for Orchestra**: A work where different sections or instruments of the orchestra are treated as soloists, with the orchestra itself as the primary virtuosic force.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the distinction between a *concertino* (or *Konzertstück*) and a full concerto "has never been formalised," with many concertinos being longer than original Baroque concertos. The evolution of the concerto's role—from a vehicle for a performer-composer to showcase their own skill to a form primarily written for others—is also highlighted as a changing practice.
