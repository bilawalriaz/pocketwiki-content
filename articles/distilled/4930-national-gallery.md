# National Gallery

## Overview
The National Gallery in London is a major art museum housing over 2,300 Western European paintings from the 13th to 19th centuries. Founded in 1824, it is a public institution with free entry, established not by nationalizing a royal collection but through government purchase and private donation. Its collection is encyclopaedic, representing the development of Western painting from Giotto to Cézanne, and its history reflects ongoing debates about art conservation, public access, and architectural design.

## Timeline
- **1824** — Gallery founded; opens in John Julius Angerstein's former townhouse at Pall Mall after government purchase of his 38 paintings.
- **1832** — Construction begins on a new building by William Wilkins on the site of the old Royal Mews in Trafalgar Square.
- **1838** — Wilkins's building opens to the public.
- **1851** — Appointment of the first director, Sir Charles Lock Eastlake, following a critical House of Commons report.
- **1897** — National Gallery of British Art (Tate Gallery) established, allowing British works to be moved off-site.
- **1906** — Velázquez's *Rokeby Venus* acquired; first major purchase by the National Art-Collections Fund.
- **1939-45** — Collection evacuated to slate quarries in Wales during World War II.
- **1991** — Sainsbury Wing, designed by Robert Venturi and Denise Scott Brown, opens.
- **2014** — First major American painting purchased: George Bellows's *Men of the Docks*.
- **2025** — Plans announced for a £375 million expansion, 'Project Domani', with a new wing by Kengo Kuma and Associates.

## Body

### Foundation and Early History
The National Gallery was established in 1824 when the British government purchased the collection of the banker John Julius Angerstein for £57,000. Unlike continental European galleries formed from royal collections, it began with this private purchase, supplemented by a key offer of 16 paintings from Sir George Beaumont. It initially occupied Angerstein's townhouse at Pall Mall before moving to a purpose-built, though widely criticized, building by William Wilkins in Trafalgar Square, which opened in 1838. The location was chosen to be accessible to all social classes.

### Growth and Directorship
Early acquisitions focused on High Renaissance Italian masters. The appointment of Sir Charles Lock Eastlake as the first director in 1851 marked a shift towards acquiring Northern and Early Italian Renaissance works. His successors continued to shape the collection through purchases and major bequests, such as the Turner Bequest (1851) and the Mond Collection (1909). Space limitations led to the creation of the Tate Gallery for British art in 1897. The gallery's conservation practices, particularly its cleaning campaigns, have been a source of recurring controversy since the 1840s.

### The World Wars and Post-War Era
During World War II, the collection was evacuated to Welsh slate quarries, a move that confirmed the importance of stable environmental conditions for paintings. The empty Trafalgar Square building hosted morale-boosting lunchtime concerts and art exhibitions. Post-war, acquisitions became more difficult due to rising art prices, necessitating public appeals for major purchases like Leonardo's cartoon of *The Virgin and Child with Saint Anne and Saint John the Baptist* (1962). The directorship of Neil MacGregor in the late 20th century introduced a chronological hang, moving away from classification by national school.

### Architecture and Expansion
The Wilkins building was criticized from its inception for its cramped design and low façade. Subsequent expansions, like the Barry Rooms (1870s), added space but were sometimes at odds with the building's function. The 20th century saw a shift from modernist interventions to a restoration of Victorian interiors. The most significant late-20th-century addition was the Sainsbury Wing (1991), a Postmodernist extension for Renaissance paintings. A major new expansion, 'Project Domani', is planned for the early 2030s.

## Terms
- **Non-departmental public body**: A public body that is not a government department but carries out administrative or executive functions under the sponsorship of a government department.
- **Bequest**: A gift of personal property by will, such as a collection of paintings left to the gallery.
- **Deaccession**: The formal process of removing an object from a museum's permanent collection.
- **Chronological hang**: An arrangement of artworks in the order they were created, rather than by school, genre, or artist.
- **Postmodernist architecture**: A style that often quotes or references historical styles in a playful or ironic way, as seen in the Sainsbury Wing.
- **Conservation**: The professional practice of protecting and restoring artworks to preserve their physical integrity and appearance.
- **Old Master**: A term for a distinguished European painter who worked before about 1800.
- **En plein air**: A French term meaning "in the open air," used to describe the practice of painting outdoors, associated with Impressionism.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Conservation Controversies**: The gallery has faced persistent criticism for its cleaning and restoration techniques, from the 1840s to the post-WWII era, with debates centering on whether varnish removal damages original glazes.
- **Acquisition Challenges**: The gallery is largely priced out of the market for major Old Master and Impressionist works, relying on public appeals and donations for significant acquisitions.
- **Architectural Criticism**: The original Wilkins building was widely derided, and later additions have sparked debates about style and functionality, exemplified by the "monstrous carbuncle" controversy.
- **Collection Scope**: The 1996 agreement set 1900 as the cut-off date for the collection, but the planned 'Project Domani' expansion includes a policy change to acquire works created after 1900, potentially altering the gallery's remit.
