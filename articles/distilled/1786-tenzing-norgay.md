# Tenzing Norgay

## Overview

Tenzing Norgay (May 1914 – 9 May 1986) was a Nepalese-Indian Sherpa mountaineer who, with Edmund Hillary, made the first confirmed ascent of Mount Everest on 29 May 1953. Born Namgyal Wangdi, he participated in numerous Everest expeditions over two decades, becoming the foremost Sherpa climber of his era. His achievement transformed mountaineering, opened the southern route to Everest, and made him a global symbol of human endurance. Time named him one of the 100 most influential people of the 20th century.

## Body

### Early life and identity

Conflicting accounts surround Norgay's origins. His autobiography states he was born and raised in Tengboche, Khumbu, Nepal; a 1985 interview says his parents came from Tibet but he was born in Nepal; other accounts, including one co-written by his son, place his birth at Tse Chu in Tibet's Kama Valley. He spent early childhood in Kharta, near Tibet's north, then went to Nepal as a child to work for a Sherpa family in Khumbu, near Everest (called Chomolungma, "Holy Mother," by Tibetans and Sherpas). He was Buddhist.

His birth date is unknown; he knew it was late May from weather and crops, and after 1953 celebrated on 29 May. His year of birth, the Tibetan Year of the Rabbit, likely means 1914, agreeing with expedition leader Hunt's statement that he was 39 in 1953. Originally named Namgyal Wangdi, his name was changed on advice of the head lama of Rongbuk Monastery; "Tenzing Norgay" means "wealthy-fortunate-follower-of-religion." His father was a Tibetan yak herder, Ghang La Mingma (d. 1949); his mother, Dokmo Kinzom, lived to see him climb Everest. He was the 11th of 13 children. He ran away twice in his teens—to Kathmandu, then Darjeeling—briefly entered a monastery but left, and settled in Darjeeling at 19, eventually acquiring Indian citizenship.

### Early mountaineering career

At age 20, Norgay joined the 1935 British Everest reconnaissance expedition after two others failed medical tests; his friend Ang Tharkay recommended him, and Eric Shipton was taken by his smile. He served as high-altitude porter on three official British attempts from Tibet in the 1930s. In the early 1940s he lived in Chitral (then India, later Pakistan) as batman (servant) to Major Chapman; his first wife died and was buried there. He returned to Darjeeling with two daughters during the 1947 Indian partition, crossing India by train without a ticket by wearing one of Chapman's old uniforms.

In 1947 he joined an illegal Everest attempt with Earl Denman and Ange Dawa Sherpa; a storm at 22,000 feet (6,700 m) forced retreat. That year he became a sirdar (expedition guide) for the first time and reached the summit of Kedarnath (22,769 ft / 6,940 m). He joined the 1951 British reconnaissance expedition.

### 1952 Swiss expeditions

The two Swiss expeditions of 1952 were the first serious attempts from the southern (Nepalese) side. On 28 May, Raymond Lambert and Norgay reached about 8,595 metres (28,199 ft) on the southeast ridge—a new altitude record. Norgay was for the first time treated as a full expedition member, which he called "the greatest honour that had ever been paid me." The route opened the way for the 1953 success and forged lasting friendships, especially with Lambert. The autumn expedition was stopped by bad weather at 8,100 metres (26,575 ft).

### The 1953 summit

Norgay joined John Hunt's 1953 expedition—his seventh Everest attempt. Edmund Hillary, saved from a crevasse fall by Norgay's quick ice-axe rope work, considered him the ideal climbing partner. The expedition numbered over 400 people, including 362 porters and 20 Sherpa guides, with 10,000 pounds (4,500 kg) of baggage. Hillary later recalled Norgay as larger than most Sherpas, strong, with an irresistible smile and "substantially greater personal ambition than any Sherpa I had met."

After Tom Bourdillon and Charles Evans turned back on 26 May when Evans's oxygen failed (they had reached the South Summit, within 300 vertical feet / 91 m of the top), Hunt directed Norgay and Hillary to attempt the summit. Snow and wind held them at the South Col for two days. On 28 May they pitched a tent at 27,900 feet (8,500 m); the next morning Hillary spent two hours warming his frozen boots. They climbed with 30-pound (14 kg) packs, negotiating a 40-foot (12 m) rock face later named the "Hillary Step" by wedging up a crack. They reached the 29,028-foot (8,848 m) summit at 11:30 a.m., spending about 15 minutes there. Hillary photographed Norgay with his ice axe; Norgay offered to photograph Hillary, but Hillary declined. They took additional photos looking down to prove the ascent genuine. Descending, drifting snow had covered their tracks, complicating the return; George Lowe met them with hot soup.

Hillary and Hunt were knighted; Norgay received the George Medal, as he was not a British subject and thus ineligible for knighthood. Some have suggested Indian prime minister Jawaharlal Nehru refused permission for a knighthood. National Geographic later noted "growing disquiet at the lack of official recognition." Journalists persistently asked who was first; Hunt declared, "They reached it together, as a team." Norgay ended speculation in his 1955 autobiography (ghost-written by James Ramsay Ullman, as Norgay could speak several languages but not read or write): "Hillary stepped on top first. And I stepped up after him."

### Later life and legacy

Norgay became the first Director of Field Training of the Himalayan Mountaineering Institute in Darjeeling in 1954. In 1975 he served as sirdar for the first American tourist party allowed into Bhutan, meeting the King of Sikkim. He founded Tenzing Norgay Adventures in 1978; as of 2021 it was run by his son Jamling, who summited Everest in 1996.

He was married three times. First wife Dawa Phuti died in 1944; their son Nima Dorje died at age four, and daughters Pem Pem (whose son Tashi Tenzing climbed Everest) and Nima survived. Second wife Ang Lahmu, a cousin of the first, adopted the daughters. Third wife Dakku (married while the second was alive, as Sherpa custom allowed polygyny) bore sons Norbu, Jamling, and Dhamey, and daughter Deki. Nephews Nawang Gombu and Topgay were on the 1953 expedition. Norgay died of a cerebral hemorrhage in Darjeeling on 9 May 1986, aged 71; his remains were cremated at the Himalayan Mountaineering Institute. Dakku died in 1992.

## Terms

- **Sherpa**: An ethnic group from the Himalayas, traditionally porters and guides for mountaineering expeditions
- **Sirdar**: The chief guide or leader of porters on a Himalayan expedition
- **High-altitude porter**: A Sherpa employed to carry loads at extreme elevations
- **Batman**: A military servant or orderly
- **South Col**: A saddle between Everest and Lhotse at about 25,900 feet (7,900 m), used as a staging camp
- **Hillary Step**: A 40-foot (12 m) rock face near the summit, named after Edmund Hillary
- **George Medal**: A British civilian decoration for bravery, awarded to Norgay in 1953
- **Padma Bhushan**: India's third-highest civilian award, given to Norgay in 1959
- **Polygyny**: A marriage custom allowing a man multiple wives simultaneously

## Debates and open questions

Norgay's birthplace is disputed: his autobiography says Nepal, a 1985 interview says his parents were Tibetan but he was born in Nepal, and later accounts (including his son's book) place his birth in Tibet. His exact birth date is unknown; he chose 29 May after the 1953 summit. Whether Norgay deserved a knighthood remains contentious—he was ineligible as a non-British subject, but some allege Nehru blocked it. The question of who reached the summit first was resolved only by Norgay's 1955 autobiography, which stated Hillary stepped first.
