# Francis I of France

## Overview
Francis I (1494–1547) was King of France from 1515 to 1547, a pivotal monarch of the French Renaissance. His reign was defined by a cultural flourishing, the expansion of royal power, and a relentless military rivalry with the Holy Roman Emperor Charles V. He is remembered as a great patron of the arts and letters, a centralizer of the French state, and a king who navigated the complex religious and geopolitical shifts of the 16th century.

## Timeline
- **12 September 1494** — Born at Château de Cognac.
- **1498** — Becomes heir presumptive to the French throne after Louis XII's accession.
- **18 May 1514** — Marries Claude, Duchess of Brittany.
- **1 January 1515** — Inherits the throne of France upon Louis XII's death.
- **13–15 September 1515** — Wins the Battle of Marignano, capturing Milan.
- **7 June 1520** — Meets Henry VIII at the Field of the Cloth of Gold.
- **24 February 1525** — Defeated and captured at the Battle of Pavia.
- **14 January 1526** — Signs the Treaty of Madrid under duress, later repudiating it.
- **17 October 1534** — The "Affair of the Placards" turns Francis against Protestantism.
- **1539** — Signs the Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts, making French the administrative language.
- **31 March 1547** — Dies at Château de Rambouillet; succeeded by his son Henry II.

## Body

### Patron of the Arts and Letters
Francis I was a prodigious patron who actively promoted the French Renaissance. He attracted leading Italian artists to his court, most famously Leonardo da Vinci, who brought works like the *Mona Lisa* to France. He commissioned grand architectural projects, transforming medieval fortresses into Renaissance palaces such as Chambord and Fontainebleau, and rebuilt the Louvre. As a "Man of Letters," he expanded the royal library, appointed humanist Guillaume Budé as its chief librarian, and decreed in 1537 that a copy of every book sold in France be given to it. His support for writers and his own poetic efforts earned him the title *le Père et Restaurateur des Lettres*.

### Military Rivalry with Charles V
Francis's reign was dominated by the Italian Wars and his intense personal rivalry with Holy Roman Emperor Charles V, whose Habsburg territories encircled France. After his victory at Marignano (1515), Francis sought alliances against Charles, including a famous but unsuccessful meeting with Henry VIII at the Field of the Cloth of Gold (1520). His fortunes reversed with a catastrophic defeat and capture at the Battle of Pavia (1525). He was forced to sign the harsh Treaty of Madrid (1526) but repudiated it upon his release. He continued the struggle through the League of Cognac and later wars, ultimately holding off his enemies but failing to secure lasting control in Italy.

### Exploration and the Ottoman Alliance
To counterbalance Habsburg power, Francis pursued global contacts. He sponsored exploratory voyages to the Americas, including those of Giovanni da Verrazzano (1524) and Jacques Cartier (1534), which laid the groundwork for New France. In a scandalous but pragmatic move, he formed the Franco-Ottoman alliance with Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent, the first formal alliance between a Christian and Muslim empire. This partnership provided a strategic counterweight to Charles V and facilitated French trade in the Mediterranean.

### Centralization and Religious Policy
Francis strengthened the French state through bureaucratic and linguistic reforms. The Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539) made French the official language of administration, replacing Latin, and mandated parish registers for vital statistics. Initially tolerant of the Protestant Reformation, which he saw as a tool against Charles V, Francis turned to fierce persecution after the "Affair of the Placards" (1534). He executed Protestants, censored printing, and ordered the massacre of the Waldensians (1545), codifying repression in the Edict of Fontainebleau.

## Terms
- **Italian Wars (1494–1559):** A series of conflicts primarily between France and the Habsburg Empire for control of the Italian peninsula.
- **Salic Law:** The French legal tradition that barred women from inheriting the throne, which secured Francis's succession.
- **Humanism:** A Renaissance intellectual movement emphasizing classical learning and human potential, which influenced Francis's education and court.
- **League of Cognac (1526):** An alliance of France, the Papacy, and Italian states formed to oppose Emperor Charles V's dominance in Italy.
- **Franco-Ottoman alliance:** A controversial 16th-century diplomatic and military partnership between France and the Ottoman Empire against the Habsburgs.
- **Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539):** A landmark edict that established French as the language of legal and administrative documents.
- **Collège Royal:** Founded in 1530, it taught Greek, Hebrew, and later Arabic, promoting advanced scholarship outside the medieval university system.
- **Maîtresse-en-titre:** The official title given to a king's chief mistress at court, a practice Francis formalized.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Legacy and Reputation:** Francis's historical reputation is mixed. While celebrated for his cultural patronage, he is also remembered for his military defeat at Pavia and the subsequent captivity. Historians debate whether his reign was a net success, with some, like Jules Michelet, painting a negative image, while others, like Glenn Richardson, argue he successfully enhanced royal power and left an immense cultural legacy.
- **The Franco-Ottoman Alliance:** The alliance was a watershed moment in diplomacy, prioritizing *raison d'état* (reason of state) over religious solidarity. Its long-term impact on European power dynamics and interfaith relations remains a subject of scholarly analysis.
- **Religious Policy:** The shift from Francis's early tolerance to later severe persecution of Protestants is a key point of analysis. Scholars examine the influence of personal events like the Affair of the Placards versus broader political and social pressures in shaping his policy.
