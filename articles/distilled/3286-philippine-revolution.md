# Philippine Revolution

## Overview
The Philippine Revolution (1896–1898) was a war of independence waged by the revolutionary Katipunan society against Spanish colonial rule. It was the culmination of over three centuries of Spanish domination and was influenced by Enlightenment ideas, economic changes, and the rise of Filipino nationalism. The revolution's success was intertwined with the Spanish-American War, leading to a brief declaration of independence before the islands were ceded to the United States, setting the stage for the Philippine-American War.

## Timeline
- **1892** — Katipunan secret society founded by Andrés Bonifacio.
- **Aug 1896** — Discovery of Katipunan triggers the start of the revolution.
- **Mar 1897** — Tejeros Convention elects Emilio Aguinaldo as president; Bonifacio rejects results.
- **May 1897** — Execution of Andrés and Procopio Bonifacio.
- **Dec 1897** — Pact of Biak-na-Bato signed; revolutionary leaders go into exile in Hong Kong.
- **Apr 1898** — U.S. declares war on Spain (Spanish-American War).
- **May 1898** — U.S. Navy defeats Spain at Battle of Manila Bay; Aguinaldo returns to the Philippines.
- **Jun 1898** — Aguinaldo declares Philippine independence.
- **Dec 1898** — Treaty of Paris signed; Spain cedes the Philippines to the U.S. for $20 million. |
- **Feb 1899** — Philippine-American War begins.
- **Mar 1901** — Aguinaldo captured by U.S. forces.
- **Jul 1902** — U.S. proclaims amnesty, ending the Philippine-American War.

## Body

### Origins and Causes
The revolution emerged from a combination of economic and intellectual changes. Spain's opening of Manila to world trade in 1834 fostered a new middle class, the *ilustrados*, who were exposed to liberal ideas. The 1868 Spanish Revolution briefly introduced liberal governance, but the subsequent execution of three secular priests (Gomburza) in 1872 after a mutiny in Cavite radicalized many Filipinos. This event inspired the Propaganda Movement, where exiled intellectuals like José Rizal used writings like *Noli Me Tángere* to condemn Spanish abuses and seek reforms.

### Rise of Nationalism and the Katipunan
The Propaganda Movement's failure to achieve reforms led to the founding of La Liga Filipina by Rizal in 1892, which was quickly disbanded by Spanish authorities. More radical members, led by Andrés Bonifacio, formed the Katipunan (Supreme and Venerable Society of the Children of the Nation) to pursue independence through armed revolt. The Katipunan grew rapidly, drawing members from the lower and middle classes. Its discovery by Spanish authorities in August 1896 forced the premature start of the revolution.

### Course of the Revolution and Internal Strife
The revolution began with the Cry of Pugad Lawin and early battles around Manila, which were largely unsuccessful for Bonifacio's forces. Success was greater in Cavite, where Emilio Aguinaldo's faction won key victories. This disparity led to a power struggle between Bonifacio's Magdiwang and Aguinaldo's Magdalo factions. The 1897 Tejeros Convention elected Aguinaldo president, but Bonifacio declared the election void. He was later arrested, tried for treason, and executed in May 1897, a move that demoralized some revolutionaries.

### Biak-na-Bato and the Spanish-American War
Facing Spanish counterattacks, Aguinaldo's government established the Republic of Biak-na-Bato and negotiated the Pact of Biak-na-Bato in December 1897. The pact provided for revolutionary exile in Hong Kong and Spanish payments, but hostilities never fully ceased. The outbreak of the Spanish-American War in 1898 changed the conflict's dynamics. After the U.S. victory at Manila Bay, Aguinaldo returned, resumed fighting, and declared independence on June 12, 1898. However, the U.S. did not recognize this independence.

### End of Spanish Rule and U.S. Takeover
The Treaty of Paris (December 1898) ended Spanish rule and transferred sovereignty to the United States for $20 million, without Filipino representation. The U.S. established a military government and staged a mock battle to capture Manila from Spain, deliberately excluding Filipino forces. This act ended Filipino-American collaboration and led directly to the Philippine-American War, which began in February 1899. The revolution effectively ended with Aguinaldo's capture in 1901, though fighting continued until 1902.

## Terms
- **Katipunan**: Secret revolutionary society founded in 1892 to seek Philippine independence through armed revolt.
- **Ilustrados**: Educated Filipino middle class who led the Propaganda Movement, advocating for reforms.
- **Propaganda Movement**: Campaign by Filipino expatriates in Europe to publicize abuses and seek reforms from Spain.
- **Gomburza**: The three secular priests (Gomez, Burgos, Zamora) executed in 1872, whose deaths fueled nationalist sentiment.
- **Pact of Biak-na-Bato**: 1897 agreement between Spanish authorities and Filipino revolutionaries that temporarily halted hostilities and exiled leaders to Hong Kong.
- **Malolos Constitution**: The first constitution of the Philippine Republic, ratified in 1899, establishing the First Philippine Republic.
- **Benevolent Assimilation**: U.S. policy announced in 1898, framing American colonization as a civilizing mission.
- **First Philippine Republic**: The revolutionary government established in 1899 with Emilio Aguinaldo as president, which fought the Philippine-American War.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The Cry of Pugad Lawin/Balintawak**: The exact date and location of the event that symbolically started the revolution are disputed among historians.
- **Bonifacio's Execution**: The trial and execution of Andrés Bonifacio remain controversial, with debates about its legality and its negative impact on revolutionary unity.
- **U.S. Promises to Aguinaldo**: Whether U.S. officials made any verbal promises of recognition or support for Philippine independence in exchange for cooperation during the Spanish-American War is debated.
- **Nature of the Revolution**: Scholars debate whether it was a bourgeois revolution led by the ilustrados or a mass movement of the lower classes.
