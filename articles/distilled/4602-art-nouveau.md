# Art Nouveau

## Overview
Art Nouveau was an international style of art, architecture, and decorative arts popular from approximately 1890 to 1910. It reacted against the academicism and historicism of the 19th century, seeking to break down the distinction between fine and applied arts. Inspired by natural forms, it used modern materials like iron and glass to create dynamic, asymmetrical designs. It was a transitional style that paved the way for modernism.

## Timeline
- **1859** — Red House, an early prototype, built in Britain with interiors by William Morris.
- **1880s** — The term "Art Nouveau" first used in the Belgian journal *L'Art Moderne*.
- **1892–1893** — Victor Horta's Hôtel Tassel, a landmark Art Nouveau house, completed in Brussels.
- **1895** — Siegfried Bing opens the Maison de l'Art Nouveau gallery in Paris, popularizing the name.
- **1897** — Brussels International Exposition showcases the new style.
- **1900** — Paris Exposition Universelle marks the peak of Art Nouveau.
- **1901** — Hector Guimard's Paris Métro entrances open, becoming a style icon.
- **1902** — Esposizione internazionale d'arte decorativa moderna in Turin spreads the style.
- **c. 1905** — Art Nouveau begins to fade in France, replaced by newer styles.
- **1914** — Start of World War I largely ends the movement.
- **1970** — Major Hector Guimard exhibition at MoMA sparks renewed critical interest.

## Body

### Origins and Influences
Art Nouveau's roots lie in Britain's Arts and Crafts movement, founded by William Morris, which valued craftsmanship and medieval inspiration. The style was also strongly influenced by the architectural theories of Eugène Viollet-le-Duc, who advocated for functional form, unity of arts, and the use of modern materials. A major aesthetic influence was Japonism, the European enthusiasm for Japanese woodblock prints, which provided stylized natural motifs and asymmetrical compositions. New printing technologies allowed art magazines to spread these ideas rapidly across Europe.

### Development and Peak (1890s–1900)
The first Art Nouveau architecture appeared almost simultaneously in Brussels in 1893 with Paul Hankar's Hankar House and Victor Horta's Hôtel Tassel. Horta's innovative use of open interiors, iron structures, and curving vegetal forms was highly influential. The style moved to Paris, where Hector Guimard adapted it for the Paris Métro entrances. The 1900 Paris Exposition Universelle was the style's high point, showcasing its application in glass, furniture, jewelry, and architecture to a global audience.

### Spread and Local Variations
From its centers in Brussels and Paris, Art Nouveau spread across Europe, adapting to local cultures and often acquiring new names. In Germany and Scandinavia, it was called Jugendstil ("Youth Style"). In Austria-Hungary, it became the Vienna Secession, which favored more geometric forms. In Spain, it was known as Modernisme, with Antoni Gaudí as its most famous practitioner. In Italy, it was Stile Liberty. Each variation maintained the core principle of unifying arts and drawing from nature, but with distinct regional characteristics.

### Decline and Legacy
By 1914, Art Nouveau was largely exhausted, criticized for its extravagance and inability to be mass-produced. It was superseded by the more geometric Art Deco and then modernism. However, the style experienced a revival in the late 1960s and 1970s with the rise of postmodernism, which re-evaluated its decorative and organic principles. Art Nouveau's legacy includes its role in elevating decorative arts and its influence on later design movements.

## Terms
- **Gesamtkunstwerk**: A German term for a "total work of art," a key Art Nouveau goal of unifying architecture, furnishings, and art in a single style.
- **Whiplash line**: A dynamic, sinuous curve inspired by plant forms, a signature motif of early Art Nouveau.
- **Japonism**: The influence of Japanese art and design on Western culture, providing Art Nouveau with motifs and compositional ideas.
- **Secession**: A breakaway movement of artists, most famously in Vienna, who rejected traditional academic art to pursue new styles.
- **Modernisme**: The Catalan variant of Art Nouveau, centered in Barcelona and associated with architects like Antoni Gaudí.
- **Trencadís**: A mosaic technique using broken ceramic pieces, pioneered by Antoni Gaudí.
- **Sgraffito**: A decorative technique of applying layers of tinted plaster to create murals on building façades.
- **Favrile glass**: A type of iridescent art glass patented by Louis Comfort Tiffany, characterized by its luminous colors.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise boundaries of Art Nouveau are debated, as it overlapped with and influenced contemporaneous movements like the Arts and Crafts movement, Symbolism, and the Vienna Secession, which had distinct characteristics.
- The style's relationship to later modernism is complex; while it rejected historicism, its emphasis on ornament led to its dismissal by early 20th-century modernist critics who championed functionalism.
- The extent to which Art Nouveau was a unified international movement versus a collection of parallel national styles remains a point of scholarly discussion.
