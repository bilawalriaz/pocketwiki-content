# Columbian exchange

## Overview
The Columbian Exchange was the widespread transfer of plants, animals, cultures, human populations, technologies, and diseases between the Americas (New World) and the Old World (Afro-Eurasia) following Christopher Columbus's 1492 voyage. It fundamentally reshaped global ecosystems, economies, and societies, leading to massive demographic shifts in the Americas and significant agricultural and population growth in the Old World.

## Timeline
- **c. 1492** — Christopher Columbus's first voyage initiates sustained contact between the hemispheres.
- **1493** — First written descriptions of syphilis appear in Europe; an epidemic of swine influenza begins in the Caribbean.
- **1494–1495** — First large syphilis outbreak in Europe during the French invasion of Naples.
- **1518** — Smallpox is first recorded in the Americas, becoming the deadliest imported disease.
- **1520** — Smallpox kills an estimated 40% of the population of Tenochtitlan.
- **1526** — The pre-contact population of Hispaniola (est. 500,000+) is reduced to fewer than 500.
- **16th century** — Spanish introduce maize, sweet potatoes, and other American crops to Asia; Portuguese introduce cassava to Africa.
- **1590** — Spanish are first Europeans to grow cacao.
- **1700–1900** — Potatoes contribute to an estimated 12–25% of population growth in Afro-Eurasia.
- **1720** — Coffee is introduced to the Americas from Africa/Middle East.
- **16th–19th centuries** — The Atlantic slave trade forcibly transports 11.7 million Africans to the Americas.

## Body

### Biological Exchanges: Plants
The exchange of crops transformed global agriculture. American staples like potatoes, maize, tomatoes, cassava, sweet potatoes, and tobacco became vital worldwide. Potatoes, for example, were unknown outside South America before 1500 but became a European staple, contributing to significant population growth and urbanization between 1700 and 1900. In the other direction, Old World crops like rice, wheat, and sugarcane became central to New World economies. The introduction of crops often created a temporary "yield honeymoon" due to the absence of their endemic fungi in new regions.

### Biological Exchanges: Animals
The animal exchange was initially one-directional, from the Old World to the New. Europeans introduced horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, and chickens, which were rapidly adopted by Indigenous peoples for transport, food, and warfare. For instance, the Mapuche of Araucanía used horses to improve military capabilities. In contrast, only a few New World domestic animals, like the turkey and Muscovy duck, were transferred to Europe.

### Biological Exchanges: Diseases
The disease exchange was catastrophic for the Americas. Indigenous populations had no prior exposure or immunity to Old World diseases. Smallpox, measles, and other illnesses caused an estimated 80–95% reduction in the Native American population within 100–150 years of 1492. The population of Mexico fell from an estimated 20 million to barely over a million in the 16th century. The origin of syphilis is debated, but its first major European outbreak followed Columbus's return. Conversely, the discovery of quinine from Andean cinchona trees provided the first effective treatment for malaria, aiding European exploration.

### Cultural and Demographic Exchanges
The movement of people caused a "clash of cultures," transferring European concepts like private property and Christianity to the Americas. The Atlantic slave trade was a direct consequence of the demographic collapse of Indigenous populations and the labor demands of plantation agriculture. Between the 16th and 19th centuries, 11.7 million Africans were forcibly transported to the Americas, far outnumbering the approximately 3.4 million European migrants. This created new African-American cultures and ethnicities, with descendants of enslaved Africans forming majorities in some Caribbean nations.

## Terms
- **Columbian Exchange**: The widespread transfer of plants, animals, culture, human populations, technology, diseases, and ideas between the Americas and the Old World following Columbus's 1492 voyage.
- **Old World**: The Eastern Hemisphere, specifically Europe, Asia, and Africa.
- **New World**: The Western Hemisphere, specifically the Americas.
- **Ecological release / "yield honeymoon"**: A temporary increase in crop yields when a plant is introduced to a new region free of its endemic pests and diseases.
- **Quinine**: A medicinal compound derived from the bark of Andean cinchona trees, used to treat malaria.
- **Atlantic slave trade**: The forced transportation of enslaved Africans to the Americas, primarily from West Africa, between the 16th and 19th centuries.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The origin of syphilis is debated: one hypothesis suggests it was carried from the Americas to Europe by Columbus's crew, while another proposes it existed unrecognized in Europe prior to 1492.
- The extent and impact of possible pre-Columbian trans-oceanic contacts (e.g., between Polynesians and South Americans around 1200) remain subjects of scholarly speculation and investigation.
