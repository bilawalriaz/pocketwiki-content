# Photon

## Overview

The photon is an elementary particle representing a quantum of the electromagnetic field, serving as the force carrier for the electromagnetic force. Massless and always traveling at the speed of light in a vacuum, photons exhibit wave–particle duality and belong to the class of bosons. Their concept, developed in the early 20th century by Einstein and others, revolutionized physics, enabling advances such as lasers, quantum electrodynamics, and quantum computing.

## Timeline

- **c. 1900** — Max Planck proposes energy quanta to explain black-body radiation
- **1905** — Einstein introduces light quanta to explain the photoelectric effect
- **1916** — Einstein develops theory of stimulated and spontaneous emission
- **1926** — Gilbert N. Lewis popularizes the term "photon"
- **1925–1927** — Born, Heisenberg, Jordan, and Dirac develop quantum field theory of photons
- **1927** — Compton scattering experiment confirms photon momentum
- **1927** — BKS theory fails; conservation laws confirmed for individual interactions
- **1974–1986** — Photon-correlation experiments demonstrate quantum photon effects
- **1995** — Bose–Einstein condensation observed experimentally
- **2018** — MIT announces discovery of bound photon triplets

## Body

### Physical Properties

Photons have no electric charge, zero rest mass (experimental upper limit ~10⁻⁵³ g), and are stable. They travel at the speed of light in vacuum and have two polarization states. As gauge bosons for electromagnetism, photons carry spin angular momentum of ±ħ, corresponding to circular polarization states. They obey Bose–Einstein statistics, allowing multiple photons to occupy the same quantum state.

### Energy and Momentum

Photon energy is given by E = hν = ℏω = hc/λ, where h is the Planck constant and c is the speed of light. Photon momentum is p = ℏk = h/λ, leading to the relativistic relation E = pc for massless particles. This satisfies the energy–momentum relation E² = p²c² + m²c⁴ when m = 0.

### Historical Development

Early theories oscillated between particle and wave models of light. Newton's particle theory dominated until the 19th century, when Young and Fresnel demonstrated light's wave properties. Maxwell's 1865 electromagnetic theory seemed definitive, but failed to explain frequency-dependent phenomena like the photoelectric effect. Planck's quantum hypothesis (1900) and Einstein's light quanta (1905) resolved these issues, though the wave-particle duality remained debated until Compton's 1922 experiment confirmed photon momentum.

### Quantum Field Theory

In quantum electrodynamics (QED), photons are quantized excitations of electromagnetic field modes. The electromagnetic field decomposes into Fourier modes equivalent to harmonic oscillators with energy levels E = nhν. Dirac's second quantization treats photon creation and annihilation as transitions between these modes. Virtual photons mediate static electromagnetic interactions but may violate E = pc and have extra polarization states. Renormalization corrects infinite contributions from higher-order perturbation calculations.

### As a Gauge Boso

The photon arises from requiring U(1) gauge symmetry at every spacetime point. This symmetry mandates that photons be massless, uncharged bosons with spin ±1. In the Standard Model, the photon is one of four electroweak gauge bosons (alongside W⁺, W⁻, Z⁰), unified by Glashow, Salam, and Weinberg. Grand unified theories attempt to connect these with gluons, though proton decay remains unobserved.

### Applications and Technology

Photons enable numerous technologies: photomultiplier tubes and CCDs detect single photons via the photoelectric effect; lasers operate through stimulated emission; two-photon microscopy achieves high resolution; and quantum cryptography uses photon entanglement. Random number generators exploit photon beam-splitter outcomes, while fluorescence resonance energy transfer studies protein interactions.

## Terms

- **Boson**: Particle with integer spin obeying Bose–Einstein statistics
- **Gauge boson**: Force-carrying particle arising from gauge symmetry
- **Wave–particle duality**: Property where entities exhibit both wave and particle characteristics
- **Stimulated emission**: Process where an incoming photon induces an excited atom to emit a coherent photon
- **Fock state**: Quantum state describing a definite number of particles in each mode
- **Virtual photon**: Transient electromagnetic field excitation contributing to interaction probabilities
- **Renormalization**: Technique correcting infinite results in quantum field theory calculations
- **Polariton**: Quasi-particle formed by coupling photons with quantum excitations of matter
- **Coherent state**: Quantum state minimizing uncertainty, used in quantum optics
- **Second quantization**: Formalism treating fields themselves as quantum operators

## Debates and Open Questions

The fundamental nature of wave-particle duality remains conceptually challenging, particularly regarding photon localization and the proper formulation of uncertainty principles for photons. While photon-correlation experiments since the 1970s have definitively demonstrated quantum behavior, some semiclassical models persist. The exact mechanism of photon mass generation (if via Higgs mechanism) affects experimental mass limits. Additionally, the unification of photons with other gauge bosons in grand unified theories remains unverified, with proton decay yet to be observed.
