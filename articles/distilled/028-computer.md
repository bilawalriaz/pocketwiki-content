# Computer

## Overview

A computer is a machine that can be programmed to automatically carry out sequences of arithmetic or logical operations (computation). Modern digital electronic computers can perform generic sets of operations known as programs, enabling a wide range of tasks. From a few machines in the 1930s, computers have become ubiquitous, powering everything from key fobs and microwave ovens to supercomputers, smartphones, and the Internet. The defining feature of modern computers is their programmability, which distinguishes them from all other machines.

## Timeline

- **c. 2400 BCE** — Roman abacus developed from devices used in Babylonia.
- **c. 100 BCE** — Antikythera mechanism, earliest known mechanical analog computer, dated to this period.
- **1822** — Charles Babbage announces his difference engine; later conceives the analytical engine, the first design for a general-purpose Turing-complete computer.
- **1936** — Alan Turing proposes the universal Turing machine, laying the theoretical foundation for the modern computer.
- **1941** — Konrad Zuse completes the Z3, the world's first working electromechanical programmable, fully automatic digital computer.
- **1944** — Colossus, the world's first electronic digital programmable computer, is delivered to Bletchley Park.
- **1945** — ENIAC, the first electronic programmable computer in the U.S., becomes fully operational; it is Turing-complete.
- **1948** — The Manchester Baby runs its first program, becoming the world's first stored-program computer.
- **1953** — The first transistorized computer becomes operational at the University of Manchester.
- **1958** — Jack Kilby demonstrates the first working integrated circuit; Robert Noyce later invents the first true monolithic IC.
- **1971** — The Intel 4004, largely undisputed as the first single-chip microprocessor, is designed and realized by Federico Faggin and colleagues.

## Body

### Etymology

The word "computer" originally referred to a human who carried out calculations. The first known use, per the Oxford English Dictionary, was in a 1613 book by Richard Brathwait. By 1943, most human computers were women, often hired because they could be paid less than men. The Online Etymology Dictionary dates the meaning "calculating machine" to 1897 and the modern sense of "programmable digital electronic computer" to 1945 (theoretical sense from 1937, as Turing machine).

### Pre-20th Century Computing

Devices for computation date back thousands of years, mostly using one-to-one correspondence with fingers. The earliest counting device was likely a tally stick; later aids included calculi (clay spheres, cones) sealed in clay containers in the Fertile Crescent. The abacus was used for arithmetic, with the Roman abacus developed from Babylonian devices as early as 2400 BCE. The Antikythera mechanism (c. 100 BCE), discovered in 1901, is believed to be the earliest known mechanical analog computer, designed to calculate astronomical positions; devices of comparable complexity did not reappear until the fourteenth century.

The slide rule, invented around 1620–1630 by William Oughtred, was a hand-operated analog computer for multiplication and division. In the 1770s, Pierre Jaquet-Droz built a mechanical doll that could be "programmed" to write different messages by switching its internal wheels. In 1831–1835, Giovanni Plana devised a Perpetual Calendar machine predicting the calendar from 0 CE to 4000 CE. The tide-predicting machine (1872) by Sir William Thomson aided navigation. The differential analyser, a mechanical analog computer for solving differential equations, was advanced by the torque amplifier, leading to Vannevar Bush's machines starting in the 1920s.

### First Computer and Electromechanical Machines

Charles Babbage, considered the "father of the computer," conceptualized the analytical engine in 1833, a general-purpose design incorporating an arithmetic logic unit, conditional branching, loops, and integrated memory—the first design describable as Turing-complete. It was about a century ahead of its time; all parts had to be made by hand, and the project was dissolved when the British Government ceased funding. His son Henry completed a simplified version of the mill in 1888, demonstrating it in 1906. In 1914, Leonardo Torres Quevedo published a design for a machine controlled by a read-only program with conditional branching, introducing floating-point arithmetic; in 1920 he presented the Electromechanical Arithmometer.

### Analog Computers

During the first half of the 20th century, scientific computing relied on increasingly sophisticated analog computers, which used a direct mechanical or electrical model of the problem. These were not programmable and generally lacked versatility and accuracy. The first modern analog computer was Thomson's tide-predicting machine (1872). The art peaked with Bush's differential analyzer at MIT in 1931. By the 1950s, digital computers ended most analog computing, though analog machines remained in education (slide rule) and aircraft control systems.

### Digital Computers

Claude Shannon's 1937 master's thesis laid the foundations of digital computing by applying Boolean algebra to switching circuits. Early digital computers were electromechanical, using relays; Konrad Zuse's Z2 (1939) was an early example. His Z3 (1941) was the world's first working electromechanical programmable, fully automatic digital computer, using 2000 relays, a 22-bit word length, and floating-point numbers. Zuse's Z4 (1950) became the world's first commercial computer, inspiring the Swiss ERMETH.

Purely electronic circuits soon replaced electromechanical ones. Tommy Flowers built the Colossus (delivered 18 January 1944) for British code-breakers at Bletchley Park; it was the world's first electronic digital programmable computer, using vacuum tubes, but was not Turing-complete. The ENIAC (completed 1945) was the first electronic programmable computer in the U.S., was Turing-complete, and could add or subtract 5000 times a second. It weighed 30 tons, used 200 kilowatts, and contained over 18,000 vacuum tubes; its programmers were six women known as the "ENIAC girls."

### Modern Computers

Alan Turing's 1936 paper proposed the universal Turing machine, proving it capable of computing anything computable by executing instructions stored on tape. The fundamental concept is the stored program, where all instructions are stored in memory. Von Neumann acknowledged this paper as the source of the central concept. The Manchester Baby (1948) was the world's first stored-program computer, built as a testbed for the Williams tube. It led to the Manchester Mark 1, then the Ferranti Mark 1 (1951), the first commercially available general-purpose computer.

The first working transistor was built at Bell Labs in 1947 by John Bardeen and Walter Brattain, followed by Shockley's bipolar junction transistor in 1948. From 1955, transistors replaced vacuum tubes, giving rise to "second generation" computers—smaller, more reliable, and requiring less power. The MOSFET, invented at Bell Labs between 1955 and 1960, was the first truly compact transistor that could be miniaturized and mass-produced; it became the fundamental building block of digital electronics and led to the microcomputer revolution.

The integrated circuit (IC) was first publicly described by Geoffrey W.A. Dummer in 1952. Jack Kilby demonstrated the first working IC in 1958 (a hybrid IC), and Robert Noyce invented the first true monolithic IC chip, made of silicon using the planar process. The first commercial MOS IC appeared in 1964. The development of MOS ICs led to the microprocessor; the Intel 4004, designed by Federico Faggin and colleagues, is largely undisputed as the first single-chip microprocessor. Modern System on a Chip (SoCs) are complete computers the size of a coin, integrating billions of transistors and consuming only a few watts.

### Hardware

Hardware covers all tangible physical parts of a computer. A general-purpose computer has four main components: the arithmetic logic unit (ALU), the control unit, memory, and input/output devices (I/O), interconnected by buses. Circuits represent bits (binary digits) arranged in logic gates. The control unit reads and decodes program instructions, transforming them into control signals; the program counter tracks the next instruction's location. The ALU performs arithmetic and logic operations (AND, OR, XOR, NOT). Memory is a list of cells with numbered addresses, storing binary numbers in groups of eight bits (bytes); RAM can be read and written, while ROM is preloaded and read-only. Registers are fast memory cells within the CPU. Multitasking, achieved via interrupts, gives the appearance of running several programs simultaneously; multiprocessing distributes work across several CPUs.

### Software

Software is the encoded information determining a computer's operation, including programs, libraries, and non-executable data. It is divided into system software and application software; firmware is a subset controlling physical hardware. Programs are read from memory and executed in order, with jump instructions allowing loops and conditional execution. Machine code assigns each instruction a unique number (opcode); assembly language uses mnemonics like ADD or JUMP, translated by an assembler. Programming languages are designed to be unambiguous and concise, translated by compilers or interpreters. Errors in programs are called "bugs"; Admiral Grace Hopper is credited with first using the term after a dead moth was found shorting a relay in the Harvard Mark II in 1947.

### Networking and the Internet

Computers have coordinated information between locations since the 1950s, starting with the U.S. military's SAGE system. In the 1970s, ARPA funded the ARPANET, linking research computers. The Church–Turing thesis states that any Turing-complete computer can, in principle, perform the same tasks as any other, given enough time and storage. In the 20th century, artificial intelligence was predominantly symbolic; machine learning models instead adjust parameters during training. Progress in parallel computing hardware, mainly GPUs, has rapidly improved machine learning efficiency, potentially leading to artificial general intelligence (AGI).

## Terms

- **ALU (Arithmetic Logic Unit)**: The component that performs arithmetic and logic operations.
- **Analog computer**: A computer using a direct mechanical or electrical model of the problem for computation.
- **Bug**: An error in a computer program.
- **Integrated circuit (IC)**: A set of electronic circuits on one small chip of semiconductor material.
- **Microprocessor**: A CPU constructed on a single integrated circuit chip.
- **MOSFET**: Metal–oxide–silicon field-effect transistor; the fundamental building block of digital electronics.
- **Stored program**: The concept where all instructions for computing are stored in memory.
- **Turing-complete**: Having algorithm execution capability equivalent to a universal Turing machine.
- **Vacuum tube**: An electronic device used in early computers before transistors.

## Debates and open questions

- The exact device that was the first microprocessor is contentious, partly due to lack of agreement on the definition of "microprocessor," though the Intel 4004 is largely undisputed as the first single-chip microprocessor.
- The Z3 was not itself a universal computer but could be extended to be Turing complete.
- The first completely transistorized computer is debated: the University of Manchester machine (1953) used valves for clock waveforms, while the Harwell CADET (1955) is credited as the first completely transistorized computer.
