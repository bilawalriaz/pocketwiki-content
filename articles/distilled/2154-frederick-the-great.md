# Frederick the Great

## Overview
Frederick II of Prussia (1712–1786), known as Frederick the Great, transformed Prussia from a minor state into a major European power through military conquest, administrative reform, and Enlightenment patronage. His reign established Prussia as a formidable military and political force, laying the groundwork for its later role in German unification.

## Timeline
- **24 January 1712** — Born in Berlin Palace to Crown Prince Frederick William and Sophia Dorothea of Hanover.
- **31 May 1740** — Ascends to the throne as King in Prussia after his father's death.
- **16 December 1740** — Invades and occupies Silesia, beginning the First Silesian War.
- **10 April 1741** — Commands his first major battle at Mollwitz; Prussian infantry wins despite his early retreat.
- **25 December 1745** — Signs Treaty of Dresden, securing Silesia and earning the title "the Great."
- **29 August 1756** — Preemptively invades Saxony, triggering the Seven Years' War.
- **12 August 1759** — Suffers major defeat at Kunersdorf; Prussia's survival later called the "Miracle of the House of Brandenburg."
- **January 1762** — Death of Russian Empress Elizabeth leads to Prussophile Peter III ending the war against Prussia.
- **1772** — Acquires Royal Prussia in the First Partition of Poland, connecting his eastern provinces.
- **1778–1779** — Defeats Austrian attempts to acquire Bavaria in the War of the Bavarian Succession.
- **1784** — Forms the Fürstenbund (League of Princes) to counter Austrian influence in Germany.
- **17 August 1786** — Dies at Sanssouci; succeeded by his nephew Frederick William II.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Frederick's youth was marked by conflict with his militaristic father, Frederick William I, who disapproved of his son's interests in music, French culture, and philosophy. A failed escape attempt in 1730 led to the execution of his friend Hans Hermann von Katte and Frederick's imprisonment. He was forced to marry Elisabeth Christine of Brunswick-Bevern in 1733, a union that remained childless and eventually estranged. Upon inheriting the throne in 1740, Frederick took control of a highly militarized state where the army consumed 86% of the budget.

### Military Campaigns and Territorial Expansion
Frederick's reign was defined by wars to secure and expand Prussia's power. He seized the wealthy province of Silesia from Austria in 1740, nearly doubling Prussia's population and territory. He successfully defended this conquest in the First and Second Silesian Wars (1740–1745). The Seven Years' War (1756–1763) saw Prussia fight a coalition of Austria, France, Russia, and others. Despite severe defeats and near-collapse, Frederick retained Silesia, aided by the sudden death of the Russian Empress Elizabeth and the accession of the pro-Prussian Peter III. He later secured Prussia's eastern border by acquiring Royal Prussia in the First Partition of Poland (1772), which connected his provinces and gave Prussia control of the Vistula River's mouth.

### Administrative and Social Reforms
Frederick embodied "enlightened absolutism," viewing the ruler as the "first servant of the state." He modernized Prussia's bureaucracy and legal system, working with Grand Chancellor Samuel von Cocceji to increase judicial efficiency and equality before the law, a process culminating in the Prussian Law Code of 1794. He reformed the currency, promoted trade and agriculture (including introducing the potato), and drained marshlands to create new farmland. His policies encouraged immigration by people of diverse backgrounds to repopulate areas devastated by war.

### Religious Policy and Enlightenment Patronage
A religious sceptic, Frederick practiced pragmatic tolerance. While Protestantism remained the favored faith, he allowed religious freedom for Catholics and Jews, though his views contained prejudice. He was a Freemason and a patron of the Enlightenment, corresponding with figures like Voltaire and revitalizing the Prussian Academy of Sciences. He supported music, composing works and hosting composers like C.P.E. Bach and Johann Sebastian Bach. His architectural projects, such as the Sanssouci palace, exemplified the Frederician Rococo style.

### Military Theory and Legacy
Frederick became an influential military theorist, emphasizing speed, mobility, and the "oblique order of battle" to concentrate force against an enemy flank. His successes, particularly at Rossbach and Leuthen, made the Prussian army a model for Europe. His leadership style, which involved personal risk and aggressive maneuvering, influenced later military thinkers, including Napoleon. His legacy was later glorified by 19th-century German nationalists and the Nazis, but modern historians view him as a capable monarch whose reforms and military prowess fundamentally shaped Prussia's rise.

## Terms
- **Enlightened Absolutism**: A form of government where an absolute monarch applies rational principles to reform the state, viewing themselves as the "first servant" of the people.
- **Oblique Order of Battle**: A military tactic of attacking one flank of an enemy's line with overwhelming force while refusing or delaying contact on the other flank.
- **Interior Lines**: A strategic principle of using shorter, central routes to move forces between multiple enemy armies, allowing a weaker force to engage each enemy separately.
- **Pragmatic Sanction**: A decree (1713) intended to ensure the Habsburg dynastic inheritance, which Frederick refused to endorse.
- **Fürstenbund**: A league of German princes formed by Frederick in 1784 to oppose Austrian Emperor Joseph II's attempts to acquire Bavaria.
- **Frederician Rococo**: An architectural style developed by Frederick and Georg Wenzeslaus von Knobelsdorff, characterized by light, elegant, and ornate designs.
- **Peuplierungspolitik**: Frederick's "peopling policy" aimed at increasing Prussia's population through immigration and land reclamation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Historians debate the originality of Frederick's achievements, noting that many of his administrative and military reforms built upon developments already underway in Prussia.
- The label "enlightened absolutist" is questioned, as his military reputation and actions like the brutal suppression of Polish culture after the partition contrast with Enlightenment ideals of liberty.
- The extent of his personal relationships, particularly his homosexuality and his marriage, remains a subject of scholarly study and interpretation.
