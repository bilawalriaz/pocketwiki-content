# Mark Twain

## Overview
Samuel Langhorne Clemens (1835–1910), writing as Mark Twain, was an American humorist, novelist, and essayist whom William Faulkner called "the father of American literature." His masterpiece, *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* (1884), pioneered a distinctly American literary voice built on vernacular speech and unflinching social criticism, particularly regarding slavery and racism. Twain’s life traced the arc of 19th-century America: from a Mississippi River pilot and Western miner to a celebrated lecturer and friend of presidents and industrialists. His career combined immense commercial success with disastrous financial speculation, leading to bankruptcy and a grueling world lecture tour to repay creditors in full. In his later years, Twain became a fierce anti-imperialist and critic of organized religion, views that grew increasingly radical as he endured the deaths of his wife and three of his four children.

## Body

### Early Life and River Piloting
Born in Florida, Missouri, Clemens moved to Hannibal at age four. The river town, where slavery was legal, became the model for St. Petersburg in his major novels. After his father’s death in 1847, he left school for a printer’s apprenticeship, later joining the International Typographical Union. His "permanent ambition" was to become a steamboat pilot, a position of high prestige and salary ($150–$250/month). He apprenticed under Horace Bixby for $500, memorizing 1,200 miles of shifting channels, snags, and reefs. He earned his license after over two years. The pen name "Mark Twain" derived from the leadsman’s cry marking a safe depth of two fathoms (12 feet). Piloting ended in 1861 when the Civil War halted river traffic. A defining trauma occurred in 1858 when his brother Henry died in the *Pennsylvania* boiler explosion; Clemens felt lifelong guilt, believing he had foreseen it in a dream, sparking an interest in parapsychology.

### The American West and Literary Breakthrough
Clemens traveled west by stagecoach in 1861 to join Orion, Nevada Territory’s secretary. He failed at mining the Comstock Lode but found work at the *Virginia City Territorial Enterprise*. On February 3, 1863, he first signed "Mark Twain" to a humorous travel letter. In 1864, he moved to San Francisco, meeting Bret Harte and Artemus Ward. His breakthrough came November 18, 1865, when *The Saturday Press* published "The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County," a tale heard at Angels Camp, bringing international attention. A 1866 assignment to the Sandwich Islands (Hawaii) for the *Sacramento Union* yielded popular letters that launched his lecturing career. In 1867, the *Alta California* and *New-York Tribune* funded a Mediterranean voyage on the *Quaker City*, producing *The Innocents Abroad* (1869), a bestseller satirizing European and Middle Eastern pretensions through American eyes.

### Marriage, Hartford, and Major Novels
Twain courted Olivia Langdon, sister of a *Quaker City* passenger, marrying her in February 1870. Through her liberal, abolitionist family, he met Harriet Beecher Stowe, Frederick Douglass, and William Dean Howells. After a difficult stint in Buffalo (where their son Langdon died of diphtheria in 1872), the family settled in Hartford, Connecticut (1874–1891), summering at Quarry Farm in Elmira. In a dedicated octagonal study, Twain wrote his core works: *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer* (1876), *The Prince and the Pauper* (1881), *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* (1884), and *A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court* (1889). *Huckleberry Finn*, written in fits over years, combined vernacular mastery with a searing critique of slavery; its controversial ending remains debated. *Life on the Mississippi* (1883) blended memoir and travelogue, explaining his pen name’s origin.

### Financial Ruin and World Lecture Tour
Despite literary wealth, Twain lost heavily on investments, notably the Paige Compositor, a complex mechanical typesetter rendered obsolete by the Linotype. He sank $300,000 (c. $10 million today) into it between 1880–1894. His publishing house, Charles L. Webster & Co., failed after a Pope Leo XIII biography flopped. Forced to close the Hartford mansion in 1891, the family moved to Europe. With the aid of Standard Oil executive Henry Huttleston Rogers, Twain filed bankruptcy in April 1894, transferring copyrights to Olivia to protect them. To honorably repay creditors despite legal discharge, he undertook a grueling 14-month world lecture tour (July 1895–July 1896), crossing the Pacific to Fiji, Australia, India, and South Africa. The Indian leg anchored *Following the Equator* (1897). He returned to the U.S. debt-free in 1900.

### Anti-Imperialism and Political Radicalism
Twain’s politics shifted radically. Initially pro-expansionist (favoring Hawaiian annexation, calling the 1898 Spanish–American War "the worthiest"), he reversed course in 1899 after reading the Treaty of Paris, realizing the U.S. intended to "subjugate," not free, the Philippines. From 1901 until death, he served as vice-president of the American Anti-Imperialist League. He condemned the Moro Crater Massacre (600 killed), King Leopold II’s Congo atrocities (*King Leopold's Soliloquy*, 1905), and European imperialism broadly. His pacifist story *The War Prayer* (rejected by *Harper's Bazaar* in 1905, published 1916) argued war contradicts Christian love. He supported labor (Knights of Labor), women’s suffrage, and civil rights—funding Black students at Yale Law School and a southern university—yet his earlier writings on Native Americans (*The Noble Red Man*, 1870) advocated extermination, a view he did not fully retract in *Fenimore Cooper's Literary Offenses* (1895). He broke with the Republican Party in 1884 to back Grover Cleveland as a Mugwump, later condemning both major parties as "insane."

### Religion, Science, and Late Works
A Presbyterian critical of orthodoxy, Twain wrote, "Faith is believing what you know ain't so." He questioned Providence after family deaths, later declaring Christianity "bad, bloody, merciless... a hundred times better than the Christianity of the Bible, with its prodigious crime – the invention of Hell" (*Autobiography*, 2010). He befriended Nikola Tesla, patented a self-pasting scrapbook (25,000+ sold), and championed fingerprinting in *Life on the Mississippi* and *Pudd'nhead Wilson* (1894). *A Connecticut Yankee* (1889) pioneered the alternate-history trope. After Olivia’s death (1904), he published suppressed works: *The Mysterious Stranger* (three versions, 1897–1905) and *Letters from the Earth* (published 1962). His final project, the *Autobiography*, dictated non-chronologically, saw Volume 1 published in 2010—100 years post-mortem per his wish—becoming a bestseller.

### Censorship and Copyright Battles
Twain fought unauthorized Canadian editions (e.g., Belford Brothers’ *Tom Sawyer* costing an estimated $10,000). He made multiple trips to Canada (1881, 1883, 1884, 1885) to register copyrights under residency requirements, securing British and Canadian rights for *Huckleberry Finn* and *Life on the Mississippi*. *Huckleberry Finn* has faced recurrent school bans, notably by Brooklyn Public Library in 1905, for racial language. A complete bibliography remains elusive due to obscure newspaper pieces, multiple pen names ("Josh," "Thomas Jefferson Snodgrass"), and lost lectures; new material surfaced as recently as 2015.

## Terms
- **Mark Twain**: Leadsman’s cry for "two fathoms" (12 feet/3.7 m), indicating safe water for steamboats; adopted as pen name c. 1863.
- **Cub pilot**: Apprentice steamboat pilot learning river navigation; Clemens served under Horace Bixby 1857–1859.
- **Paige Compositor**: Mechanical typesetting machine in which Twain invested $300,000 (1880–1894); failed due to complexity and obsolescence by the Linotype.
- **Mugwumps**: Republican reformers who bolted the party in 1884 to support Democrat Grover Cleveland; Twain joined them, later advocating independent voting.
- **American Anti-Imperialist League**: Organization (1898–1921) opposing U.S. annexation of the Philippines; Twain was vice-president 1901–1910.
- **The War Prayer**: Unpublished (1905) pacifist story contrasting Christian love with war’s brutality; rejected by *Harper's Bazaar*, published 1916.
- **Sagebrush School**: Group of Western humorists (1860s Nevada/California); Twain its most famous member, mentored by Dan DeQuille and Tom Fitch.
- **Quarry Farm**: Elmira, NY summer home of Olivia’s sister Susan Crane; Twain wrote major works in an octagonal study built there in 1874.
- **Halley’s Comet**: Visible at Twain’s birth (1835) and death (1910); he predicted he would "go out with it."
- **Pudd’nhead Wilson** (1894): Novel featuring fingerprinting as forensic proof; written rapidly to stave off bankruptcy; criticized for rough organization.

## Debates and open questions
- **Huckleberry Finn’s ending**: Critics (Leo Marx, Ernest Hemingway) argue the final chapters—Tom Sawyer’s elaborate rescue of Jim—constitute a "failure of nerve" or "cheating" that undermines the novel’s moral gravity; defenders cite structural unity.
- **Pen name origin**: Twain claimed he adopted Captain Isaiah Sellers’ discarded river nom de plume; rivals suggest it referred to a bar tab at John Piper’s Virginia City saloon. Twain denied this in his autobiography.
- **Native American views**: Early writings (*The Noble Red Man*, 1870) advocate genocide and depict Indians as irredeemably treacherous; later works maintain ridicule (Cooper critique, 1895). Scholars debate whether this reflects fixed racism or period-specific polemic.
- **Religious heterodoxy**: Twain’s *Autobiography* (2010) attacks Christianity as "terrible" and Hell as a "prodigious crime," yet he professed belief in an almighty God and Providence elsewhere. The extent of his theism vs. deism vs. agnosticism remains contested.
- **Autobiography structure**: Twain dictated non-chronologically for humor and flow; editors have rearranged it conventionally, potentially altering his intended "whims and tangents." The Mark Twain Project publishes the unexpurgated, chronological edition.
