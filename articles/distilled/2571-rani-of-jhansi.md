# Rani of Jhansi

## Overview
The Rani of Jhansi (born Manikarnika Tambe, c. 1828–1830 – 18 June 1858) was the queen consort of the princely state of Jhansi who became a leading figure of the Indian Rebellion of 1857. After the British East India Company annexed her state using the Doctrine of Lapse, she resisted British rule, fought several battles, and died in combat. Her life and deeds have made her a potent symbol of Indian nationalism and a celebrated heroine in modern India.

## Body

### Early Life and Marriage
Manikarnika Tambe was born into a Marathi family in Varanasi. Her birth year is disputed, with British sources favoring 1827 and Indian sources 1835; historians Tapti Roy and Rudrangshu Mukherjee argue for a date between 1828 and 1830. After her mother's death, she moved to the court of Baji Rao at Bithur. According to legend, she was literate and trained in horsemanship, fencing, and firearms. She married Gangadhar Rao, Raja of Jhansi, in 1842, taking the name Lakshmibai. The couple had one son who died in infancy. On his deathbed in 1853, Gangadhar Rao adopted a young relative, Damodar Rao, as his successor.

### Annexation and the Doctrine of Lapse
The British East India Company, led by Governor-General Lord Dalhousie, refused to recognize the adopted heir. Under the "doctrine of lapse," a policy where Indian states without a natural heir were annexed, the Company took control of Jhansi in 1854. The Rani protested vigorously through letters and legal appeals, arguing the annexation violated treaties and Hindu tradition. She was granted a pension but was required to vacate the fort. The unresolved issue of her husband's debts and other disputes, such as the lifting of a ban on cattle slaughter, fueled her opposition to British rule.

### The Rebellion and Siege of Jhansi
The Indian Rebellion of 1857 began in May. In Jhansi, sepoy troops mutinied in June, and British residents were massacred. The Rani's complicity in these events is debated; she claimed she was forced by the mutineers. She assumed rulership, recruited an army, and defended Jhansi against neighboring states. Initially neutral, her position became untenable as the British, under Major General Hugh Rose, decided to treat her as an enemy. Rose besieged Jhansi from March to April 1858. Despite a strong defense, the city fell after a breach was made in the walls. The Rani escaped overnight, reportedly with her adopted son tied to her back.

### Final Battles and Death
After escaping, the Rani joined other rebel leaders at Kalpi and later Gwalior Fort. She advised caution but was often overruled by leaders like Tatya Tope. At Gwalior, she died fighting British forces on 18 June 1858. The exact circumstances are unknown, but all accounts agree she died in battle. Her death demoralized the rebels, and Gwalior fell shortly after. British commander Hugh Rose later described her as "the best and bravest of the rebels."

### Cultural Legacy
The Rani became a legendary figure in Indian nationalism, associated with Hindu goddesses like Durga and Shakti. Her story was transformed into myth, especially through oral traditions and influential literary works like Subhadra Kumari Chauhan's 1930 poem "Jhansi Ki Rani" and Vrindavan Lal Verma's 1946 novel. She is a symbol of resistance, though Dalit communities sometimes view her negatively, promoting alternative heroes like Jhalkaribai. Her legacy persists in Indian media, education, and politics.

## Terms
- **Doctrine of Lapse**: A British policy under which Indian princely states were annexed if the ruler died without a natural, biological heir.
- **Sepoy**: An Indian soldier serving under British command.
- **Raja**: A king or prince in India.
- **Rani**: A queen or princess in India.
- **Regent**: A person appointed to administer a state because the monarch is a minor, absent, or incapacitated.
- **Durbar**: A ceremonial court gathering.
- **Swaraj**: A concept meaning "self-rule," central to the Indian independence movement.
- **Dalit**: A term for communities historically subjected to the caste system's lowest tier, formerly known as "untouchables."

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Complicity in the 1857 Massacre**: The Rani's role in the killing of British residents in Jhansi is contested. Some contemporary accounts accuse her of instigating or participating, while she claimed she was a victim of the mutineers.
- **Birth Year**: Her birth year is disputed between British sources (1827) and Indian sources (1835), with modern historians suggesting a date between 1828 and 1830.
- **Circumstances of Death**: While she died fighting at Gwalior, the exact manner of her death is unknown, with multiple accounts offering different details.
- **Historical vs. Legendary Figure**: Much of her early life and actions during the rebellion are based on legend and oral tradition rather than verified fact, making a definitive biography difficult.
