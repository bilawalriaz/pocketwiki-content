# Inquisition

## Overview
The Inquisition was a series of Catholic Church tribunals established to investigate and prosecute heresy, apostasy, and other perceived threats to religious orthodoxy. Originating in the 12th century, it evolved into a powerful institution used by both ecclesiastical and secular authorities across Europe and its colonies, employing judicial procedures that included torture and public punishment. Its legacy is a subject of significant historical debate, involving issues of religious authority, state power, and human rights.

## Timeline
- **1184** — Pope Lucius III issues the bull *Ad Abolendam*, condemning heresy; the first Inquisition is temporarily established in Languedoc, France.
- **1209–1229** — The Albigensian Crusade against Cathar heresy in southern France paves the way for a permanent Inquisition.
- **1229** — The Inquisition is permanently established by the Council of Toulouse.
- **1252** — Pope Innocent IV's bull *Ad extirpanda* authorizes the use of torture by inquisitors to extract confessions.
- **1478** — King Ferdinand II and Queen Isabella I establish the Spanish Inquisition, operating under royal authority.
- **1536** — The Portuguese Inquisition is formally established at the request of King João III.
- **1542** — Pope Paul III creates the Supreme Sacred Congregation of the Roman and Universal Inquisition (the Roman Inquisition).
- **1808** — Napoleon's government abolishes the Inquisition in Venice.
- **1821** — The Portuguese Inquisition is abolished following the Liberal Revolution.
- **1834** — The Spanish Inquisition is officially outlawed.
- **2000** — Pope John Paul II issues a formal apology for sins committed by members of the Church, including during the Inquisition.

## Body

### Legal Background and Terminology
The Inquisition was based on a revived Roman inquisitorial procedure, which replaced older, less evidence-based trials like trial by ordeal. This new system, adopted first in ecclesiastical courts after the Fourth Council of the Lateran (1215), required the testimony of two or more witnesses for conviction in capital crimes, which increased the reliance on confessions obtained through threats or torture. The term "inquisition" comes from the Latin *inquisitio*, meaning "inquiry," and referred to this judicial process before it became popularly associated with the specific tribunals.

### Medieval Inquisitions (12th–15th centuries)
The first inquisitions, known as Episcopal Inquisitions, were run by local bishops to combat movements like the Cathars and Waldensians. From the 1250s, the duty was largely transferred to the Dominican Order. These tribunals operated in France, Italy, and parts of Central Europe, using procedures that included secret trials, protection of witness identities, and the use of torture (authorized by *Ad extirpanda*) to corroborate evidence. Convicted heretics who did not repent were handed over to secular authorities for punishment, which often meant execution by burning.

### Spanish and Portuguese Inquisitions (15th–19th centuries)
The Spanish Inquisition (est. 1478) and Portuguese Inquisition (est. 1536) were unique in being controlled by the monarchy rather than the Pope. They primarily targeted *conversos* (Jewish converts to Christianity) and *Moriscos* (Muslim converts), suspecting them of secretly practicing their former religions. These inquisitions also operated in colonial empires, such as the Goa Inquisition in India and tribunals in Mexico and Peru. They used public ceremonies called *autos-da-fé* for sentencing and were abolished in the early 19th century.

### The Roman Inquisition and the Reformation
In response to the Protestant Reformation, the Roman Inquisition was established in 1542 as a central supervisory body. It focused on combating Protestant heresy and enforcing Catholic orthodoxy within the Papal States, famously trying figures like Galileo Galilei. Its scope expanded to include witchcraft, especially after the publication of the *Malleus Maleficarum* (1486), though its involvement in witch-hunts was less systematic than often believed.

### Procedures, Torture, and Punishment
Inquisitorial proceedings began with a "period of grace" for voluntary confession. Denunciations, including anonymous ones, were accepted, creating an atmosphere of suspicion. Torture, such as the strappado or water cure, was used to extract confessions but was theoretically regulated to prevent death or permanent injury. Many infamous torture devices (e.g., the Iron Maiden, pear of anguish) are later fabrications. Punishments ranged from penances and fines to imprisonment, property confiscation, and, for unrepentant heretics, execution by the secular arm.

## Terms
*   **Inquisitorial Procedure:** A legal system where judges actively investigate crimes, as opposed to an adversarial system. It relied on written evidence and witness testimony, with torture used to corroborate information.
*   **Heresy:** Belief or opinion contrary to orthodox religious doctrine, especially Christian.
*   **Converso/Marrano:** A Jew who converted to Christianity, often under duress; "Marrano" was a pejorative term for those suspected of secretly practicing Judaism.
*   **Morisco:** A Muslim who converted to Christianity, often forcibly, following the Reconquista in Spain.
*   **Auto-da-fé:** A public ceremony in which the sentences of the Inquisition were pronounced and carried out.
*   **Secular Arm:** The civil authority responsible for executing the physical punishments (including burning) decreed by an ecclesiastical court.
*   **Sambenito:** A garment of shame worn by those condemned by the Inquisition as a mark of penance or infamy.
*   **Grand Inquisitor:** The chief official of an inquisition, often heading a national tribunal.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historical scholarship on the Inquisition has evolved significantly, especially with the opening of Spanish and Roman archives. Revisionist historians argue that earlier views were based on Protestant "Black Legend" propaganda and that the tribunals were often more procedurally fair and less bloody than popularly believed. They point to the use of legal technicalities that saved many defendants and the relatively low number of executions compared to secular courts. However, other scholars maintain that the Inquisition's use of torture, secret proceedings, and the climate of terror it created had profound and damaging social effects, regardless of the exact statistics. The debate continues over the institution's overall brutality, its role in state-building, and the sincerity of the Catholic Church's modern apologies for its actions.
