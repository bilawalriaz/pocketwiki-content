# Saudi Arabia

## Overview  
Saudi Arabia, officially the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), is a West Asian nation occupying most of the Arabian Peninsula. It is the largest country in the Middle East and the 12th-largest globally, with a population of approximately 33.7 million. Founded in 1932 by King Abdulaziz through the unification of Hejaz and Nejd, it operates as an absolute monarchy under the House of Saud, with Islam as the state religion and Arabic as the official language. Rich in oil reserves, Saudi Arabia plays a pivotal role in global energy markets and regional geopolitics, while undergoing gradual social and economic reforms under initiatives like Vision 2030.

## Body  

### Prehistory and Ancient Civilizations  
Human habitation in the Arabian Peninsula dates back to approximately 125,000 years ago, with evidence suggesting early modern humans migrated from Africa around 75,000 years ago. The Neolithic culture of Al-Magar, centered in southwestern Najd, represents one of the earliest instances of animal domestication, particularly horses. Archaeological findings include 8,000-year-old rock engravings depicting what may be the earliest known images of domesticated dogs. During the Bronze Age (c. 3000 BCE), metallurgy flourished, and elaborate burial practices emerged. The Dilmun civilization, centered in the Eastern Province and later Bahrain, became a significant trading power linked to Mesopotamia. By the late Bronze Age, the Midianites established a confederation in northwestern Arabia, known for camel domestication and trade networks extending into Palestine.

### Rise of Islam and Medieval Period  
The 7th century saw the emergence of Islam under the Prophet Muhammad, who united the tribes of the Arabian Peninsula into a single Islamic polity. After his death in 632 CE, Arab dynasties originating from the region founded the Rashidun, Umayyad, Abbasid, and Fatimid caliphates, spreading Islamic influence across North Africa, Asia, and Iberia. From the 10th century onward, Mecca and Medina were governed by the Sharif of Mecca under various Islamic empires. The Qarmatians, Isma'ili-Shi'ite rulers, dominated the Persian Gulf until their defeat by the Uyunid dynasty in the 11th century. Subsequent centuries witnessed the rise and fall of local powers, including the Jabrids and Bani Khalid, all operating under fluctuating Ottoman suzerainty.

### Unification and the Saudi Dynasty  
The modern Saudi state traces its origins to the 18th-century alliance between Muhammad bin Saud and the religious reformer Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, whose Wahhabi movement provided ideological legitimacy to Saudi expansion. The First Saudi State (1727–1818) expanded rapidly but was destroyed by Ottoman forces. The Second Saudi State (1824–1891) emerged in Nejd but was eventually overthrown by the Al Rashid tribe. In the early 20th century, Ibn Saud reestablished Saudi rule, capturing Riyadh in 1902 and expanding his control through military campaigns supported by the Ikhwan, a Wahhabi-inspired tribal army. The conquest of the Hejaz in 1924–1925 culminated in the formal unification of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia in 1932.

### 20th Century Developments  
The discovery of oil in 1938 transformed Saudi Arabia’s economy, leading to rapid modernization and increased international influence. The nationalization of Aramco in stages during the 1970s enhanced Saudi control over its resources. The 1973 oil embargo underscored Saudi Arabia’s geopolitical leverage. Domestically, the 1979 Grand Mosque Seizure prompted a crackdown on dissent and stricter adherence to Wahhabi doctrine. Political instability followed King Saud’s deposition in 1964, succeeded by King Faisal, whose assassination in 1975 led to King Khalid’s reign. The 1991 Gulf War, triggered by Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait, saw Saudi Arabia host U.S. troops, fueling internal Islamist opposition. The 21st century has been marked by gradual reforms, including limited municipal elections, women’s suffrage, and Vision 2030’s push for economic diversification.

### Geography and Environment  
Saudi Arabia spans roughly 2.15 million km², covering 80% of the Arabian Peninsula. Its landscape is dominated by arid deserts, including the Rub' al Khali (Empty Quarter), the world’s largest contiguous sand desert. The country lacks permanent rivers, relying on desalination and groundwater for water supply. Mountainous regions in the southwest, such as the Asir and Hijaz, experience cooler temperatures and receive seasonal rainfall influenced by the Indian Ocean monsoon. The Red Sea and Arabian Gulf coastlines provide strategic maritime access, while the nation hosts over 1,300 islands.

### Government and Politics  
Saudi Arabia functions as an absolute monarchy under the House of Saud, with the king serving as head of state and government. The Basic Law of 1992 establishes Islam and Arabic as foundational elements, with Sharia law as the primary legal framework. The Consultative Assembly, appointed by the king, advises on legislation but holds no executive power. The royal family, estimated at over 7,000 members, dominates governance, with key positions reserved for the Al Saud lineage. The Al ash-Sheikh family, descendants of Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, maintain a symbiotic relationship with the monarchy, ensuring religious legitimacy. Political opposition stems from Sunni Islamists, liberals, Shia minorities, and tribal groups, though open dissent is suppressed.

### Economy  
Saudi Arabia’s economy is heavily dependent on oil, accounting for 63% of budget revenue and 67% of export earnings. It holds the world’s second-largest proven oil reserves and is the largest oil exporter. The non-oil private sector contributes significantly but employs mostly foreign workers. Vision 2030 aims to diversify the economy through investments in renewable energy, tourism, and infrastructure. Despite oil wealth, challenges include unemployment, youth population growth, and the need for economic resilience amid fluctuating energy markets. The kingdom ranks among the G20 nations and maintains a high-income economy classification by the World Bank.

### Society and Culture  
Saudi society is deeply influenced by Islamic traditions and tribal affiliations. The Wahhabi interpretation of Sunni Islam shapes laws, education, and social norms, though recent reforms have relaxed some restrictions. Women’s rights have seen notable progress, including driving rights (2018) and expanded participation in public life. Education is free and highly valued, with literacy rates exceeding 95%. The population is young, with over half under 25 years old, and increasingly urbanized. Cultural expressions include traditional music, dance (e.g., ardah), and literature, though artistic freedom remains constrained by religious and governmental oversight.

### Foreign Relations  
Saudi Arabia maintains strategic alliances with the United States and regional Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) members. Its foreign policy emphasizes Islamic solidarity, Arab unity, and support for multilateral institutions like the UN and OPEC. The kingdom has engaged in proxy conflicts with Iran, reflecting broader Sunni-Shia tensions. Recent years have seen efforts to balance relations with China and Russia while addressing regional security concerns, including the ongoing conflict in Yemen. Saudi Arabia’s role in global counterterrorism and energy markets underscores its influence in international affairs.

## Terms  
- **Wahhabism**: An ultraconservative branch of Sunni Islam founded by Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab in the 18th century, emphasizing literal interpretation of the Quran and rejection of religious innovations.  
- **Ikhwan**: A tribal army inspired by Wahhabism, instrumental in Ibn Saud’s military campaigns during the early 20th century.  
- **Sharia**: Islamic law derived from the Quran and the teachings of the Prophet Muhammad, forming the basis of Saudi Arabia’s legal system.  
- **Vision 2030**: A strategic plan launched in 2016 to diversify Saudi Arabia’s economy and society, reducing dependence on oil revenues.  
- **Kafala System**: A sponsorship-based labor system governing the employment of foreign workers in Saudi Arabia, criticized for enabling exploitation and restricting worker rights.  
- **Haia (Mutaween)**: The religious police responsible for enforcing moral and social codes in public spaces, with powers curbed since 2016.  
- **Qisas**: Retaliatory justice in Islamic law, allowing victims or their families to demand equivalent punishment for crimes such as murder or bodily harm.  
- **Diyya**: Blood money paid to victims or their families in lieu of qisas, often used in cases of accidental death or injury.  
- **Najdi Dialect**: A major Arabic dialect spoken primarily in central Saudi Arabia, reflecting the region’s historical and cultural identity.  
- **Ardah**: The national dance of Saudi Arabia, a traditional sword dance performed in groups, symbolizing unity and heritage.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Several contentious issues surround Saudi Arabia’s domestic and international policies. The extent of corruption within the royal family and government institutions remains debated, with Transparency International ranking the country poorly on its Corruption Perceptions Index. The role of the Wahhabi religious establishment in shaping governance and social norms continues to evolve, particularly amid reforms under Vision 2030. Human rights concerns, including the treatment of women, minorities, and political dissidents, draw sustained international criticism. The kingdom’s involvement in regional conflicts, such as the Yemeni Civil War, raises questions about its foreign policy objectives and humanitarian responsibilities. Additionally, debates persist regarding the sustainability of its oil-dependent economy and the effectiveness of diversification efforts. The balance between modernization and traditional values, especially in education and media, remains a subject of scholarly and public discourse.
