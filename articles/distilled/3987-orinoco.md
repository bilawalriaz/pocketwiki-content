# Orinoco

## Overview
The Orinoco is one of South America's longest rivers (2,140 km) and the world's third largest by discharge volume. Its vast drainage basin, known as the Orinoquia, covers about 1,000,000 km², split between Venezuela (65%) and Colombia (35%). The river is a critical transportation artery for the region and supports extremely diverse ecosystems.

## Timeline
- **1 August 1498** — Christopher Columbus documents the river's mouth at the Atlantic Ocean.
- **16th century** — German expeditions under Ambrosius Ehinger explore the Orinoco and its tributaries.
- **1531** — Diego de Ordaz sails up the river from the delta to the Meta River.
- **1595** — Sir Walter Raleigh sails down the Orinoco while seeking El Dorado.
- **April–May 1800** — Alexander von Humboldt and Aimé Bonpland explore the river, proving its connection to the Amazon via the Casiquiare canal.
- **1951** — The river's source at Cerro Delgado–Chalbaud is discovered by a French-Venezuelan expedition.
- **1967** — The first bridge, the Angostura Bridge, is completed at Ciudad Bolívar.
- **1968** — A hovercraft expedition travels from the Amazon to the Orinoco via the Casiquiare canal.
- **1981** — The first powerline crossing of the Orinoco is completed.
- **2006** — The Orinoquia Bridge is completed near Ciudad Guayana.

## Body

### Geography and Course
The Orinoco's course forms a wide arc around the Guiana Shield and is divided into four sections: the Upper Orinoco (286 km) through mountains; the Middle Orinoco (805 km), which flows along the Colombia-Venezuela border; the Lower Orinoco (959 km) with a wide alluvial plain; and the Delta Amacuro (200 km), a massive 22,500 km² delta emptying into the Gulf of Paría. The river's gradient is very low, at 0.05% overall.

### Tributaries and Hydrology
The Guaviare River is the main tributary. A unique feature is the Casiquiare canal, a natural distributary that connects the Orinoco to the Rio Negro, part of the Amazon River system. The river's high discharge (39,000 m³/s at the delta) is due to high precipitation in its catchment area.

### Ecology and Economy
The basin is a biodiversity hotspot, home to species like the boto (pink river dolphin), giant otter, and the critically endangered Orinoco crocodile. Over 1,000 fish species have been recorded, with about 15% being endemic. The river is navigable for most of its length, with ocean ships reaching Ciudad Bolívar. Major economic activities include iron ore mining (discovered in 1926) and potential oil production from tar sands in the Orinoco oil belt.

### Modern Infrastructure and Recreation
Major infrastructure includes the Angostura Bridge (1967) and the Orinoquia Bridge (2006). The river hosts significant sporting events, such as the *Internacional Rally Nuestros Rios son Navegables*, the world's longest fluvial motorboat rally, and the *Paso a Nado Internacional*, an international swim meet.

## Terms
- **Drainage Basin (Orinoquia):** The area of land where all water drains into the Orinoco River, covering ~1,000,000 km².
- **Discharge:** The volume of water flowing through a river channel per unit of time; the Orinoco's is 39,000 m³/s.
- **Distributary:** A stream that branches off and flows away from a main river channel (e.g., the Casiquiare canal).
- **Alluvial Plain:** A flat area of land created by the deposition of sediment over a long period by a river.
- **Endemic:** A species native and restricted to a certain place, like 15% of the Orinoco's fish.
- **Delta:** A landform created by sediment deposited as a river flows into slower-moving or stagnant water (e.g., Delta Amacuro).
- **Gradient:** The slope of a river, calculated as the drop in elevation over distance; the Orinoco's is 0.05%.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source of the Orinoco was only discovered in 1951, highlighting the historical difficulty in fully mapping the river. The article notes the river's potential for future oil production from tar sands, implying ongoing economic and environmental considerations regarding its development.
