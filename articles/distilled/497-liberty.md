# Liberty

## Overview
Liberty is the state of being free within society from restrictions imposed by authority on one's way of life, behavior, or political views. The concept varies by perspective and context. In US constitutional law, ordered liberty means creating a balanced society where individuals have the freedom to act without unnecessary interference (negative liberty) and access to opportunities and resources to pursue their goals (positive liberty), all within a fair legal system. Sometimes liberty is differentiated from freedom by using "freedom" to mean the ability to do as one wills and what one has the power to do, and "liberty" to mean the absence of arbitrary restraints, taking into account the rights of all involved. In this sense, the exercise of liberty is subject to capability and limited by the rights of others; it entails the responsible use of freedom under the rule of law without depriving anyone else of their freedom. Liberty can be taken away as punishment; in many countries, people are deprived of liberty if convicted of criminal acts. Its modern political form originates in the Greek contrast between the free citizen and the slave, evolving through Roman law, the Enlightenment, and social contract theory. A 2024 study finds that a global conception of liberty took form during the late Middle Ages (c. 1000–1600) through translingual legal and religious exchanges across Afro-Eurasia, rather than solely from Western expansion. Central philosophical tensions persist between negative liberty (freedom from interference) and positive liberty (capacity for self-mastery), and between liberal, republican, socialist, and anarchist frameworks.

## Body

### Philosophy: Defining Liberty
Philosophers from the earliest times have considered the question of liberty. Roman Emperor Marcus Aurelius (121–180 AD) wrote of "a polity in which there is the same law for all, a polity administered with regard to equal rights and equal freedom of speech, and the idea of a kingly government which respects most of all the freedom of the governed." Thomas Hobbes defined a free man as one who "in those things which by his strength and wit he is able to do is not hindered to do what he hath the will to do." John Locke rejected this, arguing liberty in the state of nature means subjection only to the law of nature, while in political society it means subjection only to laws established by consent—freedom from the "inconstant, uncertain, unknown, and arbitrary wills of others." John Stuart Mill, in his 1859 work *On Liberty*, was the first to recognize the difference between liberty as the freedom to act and liberty as the absence of coercion. Isaiah Berlin later formalized this as **negative liberty** (a protective sphere of non-interference against tyranny and the arbitrary exercise of authority) and **positive liberty** (self-mastery, freedom from inner compulsions like fear or weakness). In US constitutional law, **ordered liberty** balances these: negative freedom from interference and positive access to opportunities within a fair legal system.

### Politics: Historical Origins and Social Contract
The Greek concept of freedom—living without a master—was inextricably linked to democracy (Aristotle), though limited to free men. The Persian Empire (550 BC) and Maurya Empire (Ashoka’s edicts) offered early models of universal rights, religious tolerance, and abolition of slavery. Roman law provided limited liberties to citizens, preserved largely for nobility through the Middle Ages. The **social contract** theory (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau) shifted sovereignty to the people, positing natural rights given by "Nature and Nature's God." Montesquieu culminated this by conceiving law as a relationship between individuals, making individual liberty a fundamental, universal reality. Mill framed politics as a continuous antagonism between liberty and authority, requiring constant adjustment between individual independence and social control.

### Global Conceptual History
A 2024 study challenges the view of liberty as a Western export. During the late Middle Ages (c. 1000–1600), a global conception formed across Afro-Eurasia. Multilingual governance, courtly encounters, and religious translation created interconnected "emic terms" for liberty. Bilingual legal documents, shared religious texts, and bureaucratic glossaries show words for liberty paralleling and resonating across languages. The study concludes that "the concept(s) of liberty, instead of being a product of the West, is more accurately seen as a result of interactions among different parts of the world; the globalization of liberty did not start with the global expansion of the Euro-American empires but has a rich and complex history that predates them."

### England and Great Britain: The Documentary Tradition
English liberty developed through specific legal instruments. William the Conqueror assented to the London Charter of Liberties (1066). The Charter of Liberties (1100) and Assize of Clarendon (1166) reformed law toward trial by jury. The *Tractatus de legibus et consuetudinibus regni Anglie* (1187–1189) contained authoritative definitions of freedom and servitude. Magna Carta (1215) became the global cornerstone. The Petition of Right (1628) and Habeas Corpus Act (1679) protected citizens from arbitrary state power. The Bill of Rights (1689) secured parliamentary speech. *Somerset v Stewart* (1772) rejected slavery under common law. Mill’s *On Liberty* (1859) argued for toleration: silencing an opinion assumes infallibility. British representatives failed to add legal force to the UDHR (1948); the ICCPR finally enacted it in 1976.

### United States: Slavery, Rights, and Competing Ideologies
The Declaration of Independence (1776) proclaimed liberty a natural right, yet legalized Black slavery persisted for 90 years. Slave owners claimed property liberty; the Supreme Court upheld this in *Dred Scott* (1857). The 14th Amendment (1866) extended rights to persons of color; the 19th Amendment (1920) enfranchised women. By the late 20th century, liberty expanded to protect intimate personal choices (*Griswold v. Connecticut*, 1965). Modern US factions diverge: **Liberals** link equality to freedom; **Progressives** target business monopoly; **Libertarians** prioritize economic/individual freedom; the **Tea Party** opposes "big government."

### France: Revolution and Reaction
France supported the American Revolution, then overthrew its own monarchy in 1789 under "Liberté, égalité, fraternité." The ensuing Reign of Terror discredited revolutionary liberty for many. Edmund Burke condemned the French as "architects of ruin," founding a conservative critique of abstract liberty untethered from tradition.

### Ideologies: Rival Frameworks
**Liberalism** aims to preserve individual rights and maximize choice (Concise Oxford Dictionary). Its core is **toleration** (John Gray): mutual non-interference. It is attacked by the Right for social permissiveness and by the Left for prioritizing individual property over collective needs. **Libertarianism** holds liberty as the supreme value (Britannica), opposing all state coercion except that preventing interpersonal coercion. It is governed by the **Non-Aggression Principle (NAP)**: initiating force, fraud, or involuntary taxation against peaceful persons is immoral. **Republican liberty** (Skinner, Pettit) defines freedom not as non-interference but as **non-domination**: absence of arbitrary dependence on another's will, rooted in Roman *Digest* and Machiavelli’s self-governing republic. Hobbes’ *Leviathan* countered this with non-interference to ensure stability. **Socialism** views freedom as a concrete material condition, not an abstract ideal. It requires economic conditions and social relations allowing agency to pursue creative interests without coercive survival labor. **Marxism** posits true freedom only under communism: superabundance eliminates alienated labor (forced work), expanding the "realm of freedom" (discretionary time) for individual development. **Anarchism** (Bakunin) rejects all authority (state, capital, nation). Liberty is the "fullest development of all faculties" via education and prosperity—eminently social, realized only in society—and negatively, the revolt against all divine, collective, and individual authority.

## Terms
- ****Negative Liberty**** — A protective condition where an individual is shielded from tyranny and the arbitrary exercise of authority (Isaiah Berlin).
- ****Positive Liberty**** — The freedom derived from self-mastery; the capacity to act autonomously, free from inner compulsions like weakness or fear (Isaiah Berlin).
- ****Ordered Liberty**** — US constitutional concept balancing freedom from interference (negative) with access to opportunities/resources (positive) within a fair legal system.
- ****Non-Aggression Principle (NAP)**** — Libertarian axiom: initiating force, fraud, or coercion against a peaceful person or their property is inherently immoral.
- ****Non-Domination (Republican Liberty)**** — Freedom as the absence of arbitrary dependence on the will of another; not merely non-interference, but structural independence from discretionary power (Skinner, Pettit).
- ****Alienated Labor**** — Marxist term: work performed under compulsion for survival, rather than as an expression of creative agency; the negation of socialist freedom.
- ****Social Contract**** — Theory (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau) that legitimate political authority arises from consent of the governed to secure natural rights.
- ****Habeas Corpus**** — Legal writ (codified 1679 in England) prohibiting unlawful or arbitrary detention; a procedural safeguard of liberty.
- ****Magna Carta**** — 1215 English charter limiting royal power; foundational document for Anglo-American constitutional liberty.
- ****Emic Terms**** — Concepts or categories meaningful within a specific culture/language; used in the 2024 study to trace indigenous words for "liberty" across Afro-Eurasia.

## Debates and open questions
- **Western vs. Global Origins**: Whether liberty is a distinctively Western construct exported via empire, or a polycentric concept emerging from medieval Afro-Eurasian translation and legal practices (2024 study).
- **Negative vs. Positive Liberty**: Whether liberty is fundamentally a shield against interference (Berlin’s negative) or a capacity for self-realization requiring resources (positive), and whether the latter risks authoritarian "forcing men to be free."
- **Non-Interference vs. Non-Domination**: Whether republican freedom (structural independence from arbitrary power) is a necessary correction to liberal negative liberty, or an expansion inviting state overreach.
- **Property vs. Personhood**: In the US, whether liberty primarily protects property rights (slave owners' argument, libertarian economics) or bodily autonomy and intimate association (*Griswold*, modern liberalism).
- **Material Preconditions**: Whether socialism correctly identifies that formal legal liberty is meaningless without the economic means to exercise it, or whether state provision of those means inevitably violates negative liberty.
- **Scope of Coercion**: Whether the libertarian NAP correctly identifies all taxation and regulation as aggression, or whether democratic law-making constitutes the "consent" Locke required.
