# William Henry Bragg

## Overview
Sir William Henry Bragg (1862–1942) was a British physicist and Nobel laureate who, with his son Lawrence, pioneered X-ray crystallography—a technique for determining the atomic structure of crystals using X-ray diffraction. Their work provided the first direct evidence of atomic arrangements in solids, laying the foundation for modern materials science, chemistry, and biology. Bragg also made significant contributions to early radio, radioactivity research, and anti-submarine warfare during World War I.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
William Henry Bragg was born in 1862 and raised by an uncle after his mother's death. He was educated at King William's College and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he graduated as third Wrangler in mathematics in 1884. His mathematical training formed the basis for his later physical research.

### Career in Australia (1886–1908)
Bragg spent 23 years at the University of Adelaide, where he transformed the science school. His early interests included electromagnetism and the newly discovered X-rays. In 1896, he demonstrated X-ray imaging to local doctors, using a Crookes tube. He also pioneered wireless telegraphy in Australia, conducting the first public demonstration in 1897 and later collaborative experiments with his father-in-law, astronomer Sir Charles Todd. A turning point came in 1904 with his research on the ionization of gases, which led to a series of papers on radioactivity and his first book, *Studies in Radioactivity* (1912).

### Development of X-ray Crystallography
Returning to England in 1909, Bragg became Cavendish Professor at Leeds. He invented the X-ray spectrometer, an instrument for measuring the angles and intensities of X-rays diffracted by crystals. Collaborating with his son Lawrence, then a research student at Cambridge, they formulated Bragg's law (nλ = 2d sinθ), which relates the wavelength of X-rays to the spacing of atomic planes in a crystal. This work enabled the first precise determination of crystal structures, founding the field of X-ray crystallography. They shared the 1915 Nobel Prize in Physics for this achievement.

### World War I Contributions
During World War I, Bragg's younger son Robert was killed at Gallipoli in 1915. Bragg himself was appointed scientific director of the Admiralty's hydrophone research establishment. His group developed improved directional hydrophones (early sonar) to detect submarines. Inspired by Lawrence's sound-ranging methods for locating enemy guns, they also adapted acoustic techniques for precise naval positioning. By the war's end, sonar was operational on British vessels.

### Later Career and Leadership
After the war, Bragg worked at University College London before becoming Director of the Davy Faraday Research Laboratory at the Royal Institution in 1923. He was elected President of the Royal Society in 1935. Anticipating World War II, he helped establish a scientific advisory committee to the War Cabinet. He was listed on the Nazi "Black Book" for arrest in the event of a British invasion.

## Terms
- **X-ray crystallography**: A technique for determining the atomic and molecular structure of a crystal by analyzing the diffraction pattern produced when X-rays strike it.
- **Bragg's law**: The equation nλ = 2d sinθ, where n is an integer, λ is the X-ray wavelength, d is the spacing between atomic planes, and θ is the angle of incidence; it defines the condition for constructive interference in X-ray diffraction.
- **X-ray spectrometer**: An instrument invented by Bragg to measure the wavelengths and intensities of X-rays diffracted by a crystal.
- **Crookes tube**: An early gas-discharge tube used to generate X-rays.
- **Hydrophone**: An underwater microphone used for detecting sound, a precursor to modern sonar.
- **Ionization of gases**: The process by which electrons are removed from gas atoms, creating ions; a key phenomenon in early radioactivity research.
- **Wrangler**: A student who achieves first-class honors in the Mathematical Tripos examination at Cambridge University.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the initial demonstration of X-rays in Adelaide was made possible by apparatus supplied by a local pharmaceutical chemist, highlighting the collaborative and sometimes improvised nature of early experimental physics. The text also references the broader historical debate about the slow initial recognition of the military value of science, as seen in the frustrating delays before Bragg's expertise was employed in World War I—a lesson that influenced the creation of scientific advisory bodies before World War II.
