# Serfdom

## Overview
Serfdom was a system of debt bondage and indentured servitude where peasants were legally bound to the land they worked, forming the labor base of feudal manorialism. It differed from slavery as serfs could not be individually sold and had certain rights, but they were not free. The institution evolved from late antiquity, peaked in the Middle Ages, and was abolished at different times across Europe and beyond, with its decline in the West contrasting with its strengthening in the East.

## Timeline
- **c. 3rd century AD** — Roman *coloni* (tenant farmers) become increasingly bound to the land due to tax systems.
- **c. 10th century** — Medieval serfdom begins with the breakup of the Carolingian Empire.
- **1086** — Domesday Book records the social composition of England, including serfs, villeins, and cottars.
- **1347** — The Black Death reaches Europe, contributing to serfdom's decline in the West.
- **15th century** — Serfdom becomes dominant in Eastern Europe.
- **1781** — Habsburg Emperor Joseph II issues the Serfdom Patent, abolishing serfdom in his monarchy.
- **1791** — Polish Constitution of May 3 begins steps toward abolishing serfdom.
- **1792** — Prussia declares serfdom unacceptable.
- **1807** — Prussia formally abolishes serfdom.
- **1861** — Serfdom is abolished in Russia by Tsar Alexander II.
- **1956** — The UN Supplementary Convention prohibits serfdom as a practice similar to slavery.

## Body

### Origins and Nature
Serfdom emerged from social institutions in late antiquity, resembling the status of ancient Greek helots. By the 3rd century AD, Roman landowners relied on tenant farmers (*coloni*) due to labor shortages. A tax system under Emperor Diocletian tied these farmers to the land for census purposes, eroding their freedom. Medieval serfdom truly began around the 10th century as feudal lords established it as a source of agricultural labor. The manor was the basic unit, where a lord provided protection and justice in exchange for a serf's labor on his fields, mines, and roads.

### Social Status and Rights
Serfs occupied a specific place in the feudal hierarchy. Unlike slaves, they could not be individually bought or sold, though they could be transferred with the land. They had rights to cultivate land for their own subsistence and could accumulate personal property. However, they were bound to the manor, required the lord's permission to marry or leave, and their children inherited their status. Social classes within the peasantry included freemen (rare), villeins (unfree tenants with some rights), cottars/bordars (lower-status workers with minimal land), and, at the bottom, slaves like the Russian *kholops*.

### Duties and Variations
A serf's primary duty was labor on the lord's demesne (estate), often taking precedence over work on their own plot. They also paid taxes and fees, typically in produce like wheat, eggs, or geese. Labor requirements varied greatly by region and time; in Poland, they increased from a few days per year in the 13th century to six days per week in the 18th century. Serfs could gain freedom by fleeing to a chartered town for a year and a day, purchasing their freedom, or being manumitted.

### Decline and Abolition
The Black Death (1347) caused massive fatalities, disrupting labor systems and contributing to serfdom's decline in Western Europe. Conversely, serfdom strengthened in Central and Eastern Europe in a phenomenon called "second serfdom." Abolition occurred unevenly: France abolished it in 1789 during the Revolution; Prussia in 1807; the Habsburg monarchy in 1781 (though corvée labor persisted until 1848); and Russia in 1861. In Scandinavia, feudalism was never fully established. Outside Europe, forms of serfdom existed in the Qing dynasty of China and Tibet (the latter's status is contested), and Bhutan abolished it by 1959.

## Terms
- **Serf**: A peasant legally bound to a manor, owing labor and fees to a lord in exchange for protection and land use.
- **Manorialism**: The economic and social system of the Middle Ages based on the manor, where a lord's estate was worked by serfs.
- **Villein**: An unfree tenant in medieval England, higher in status than a serf but bound to the lord and lacking full freedom.
- **Corvée**: Unpaid labor owed by a serf to their lord, often in the form of work on the lord's fields.
- **Demesne**: The part of a manor's land retained by the lord for his own use, worked by serfs.
- **Coloni**: Tenant farmers in the late Roman Empire, considered predecessors to medieval serfs.
- **Kholops**: The lowest class of serfs in medieval Russia, with status similar to slaves and subject to trade.
- **Second Serfdom**: The strengthening and expansion of serfdom in Central and Eastern Europe from the 15th century onward, contrasting with its decline in the West.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which the Black Death directly caused serfdom's decline in Western Europe versus other factors like peasant revolts and economic change.
- The classification of Tibetan social structures before 1959 as serfdom is contested by scholars like Melvyn Goldstein.
- The degree to which serfdom in Russia had evolved into *de facto* slavery by the 19th century, despite its name.
