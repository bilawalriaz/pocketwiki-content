# Great Smoky Mountains National Park

## Overview
Great Smoky Mountains National Park is a U.S. national park straddling the border of Tennessee and North Carolina. It protects a significant portion of the Great Smoky Mountains, a subrange of the Appalachian Mountains, and is renowned for its biodiversity, old-growth forests, and preserved historical structures. As the most visited national park in the United States, it is a major economic driver for the region and was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983.

## Timeline
- **c. 14,000 years ago** — Native Americans likely began hunting in the Great Smoky Mountains.
- **1838** — Most Cherokee are forced west on the Trail of Tears; some evade removal and become the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians.
- **1901-1939** — Major logging operations, like the Little River Lumber Company, remove two-thirds of the virgin forests.
- **1923** — The idea for a national park is formally proposed by Anne May Davis.
- **1926** — President Calvin Coolidge signs legislation authorizing the park.
- **1928** — John D. Rockefeller Jr. contributes $5 million to fund land purchases. |
- **1934** — Congress officially establishes the park; it is the first with federal funding.
- **1940** — President Franklin D. Roosevelt dedicates the park.
- **1983** — Designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2016** — Deadly wildfires burn over 17,900 acres in and around the park.

## Body

### Geography and Geology
The park covers 522,419 acres, roughly equally divided between Tennessee and North Carolina. It protects the majority of the Great Smoky Mountains, with elevations ranging from 875 feet to 6,643 feet at Kuwohi, the highest point in Tennessee. The park contains 16 peaks over 6,000 feet. Geologically, the mountains are composed primarily of late Precambrian metamorphic rocks from the Ocoee Supergroup. The landscape was shaped by ancient tectonic collisions and later modified by glacial periods, which created features like blockfields and allowed northern plant species to persist at high elevations.

### Climate and Environment
The park has a humid subtropical to temperate oceanic climate, with high annual precipitation (50–80 inches) that supports its lush forests. Air pollution has been a significant issue; a 2004 report called it the most polluted national park, though visibility has improved markedly since the Clean Air Act of 1970. The park is the most biologically diverse in the U.S., with an estimated 19,000 known species and potentially 100,000 more. This diversity is due to its role as a glacial refuge and its varied elevations.

### Flora and Fauna
Forests cover 95% of the park, including large tracts of old-growth forest. Three main forest zones exist: cove hardwood forests in the valleys, northern hardwood forests on higher slopes, and spruce-fir forests at the highest elevations. The park is home to over 1,500 flowering plant species. Notable fauna includes the densest population of American black bears east of the Mississippi, 65 mammal species, and over 240 bird species. The park is known as the "Salamander Capital of the World," hosting up to 31 species.

### Human History and Park Establishment
The Cherokee called the mountains "Shaconage" ("place of blue smoke"). European settlement began in the late 18th century, leading to the Cherokee's removal in the 1830s. The early 20th century saw extensive logging, which destroyed two-thirds of the old-growth forest. Preservation efforts began in the 1920s, led by local citizens and funded by states, private donors like John D. Rockefeller Jr., and federal programs. The park was created through the complex purchase and consolidation of thousands of private parcels, displacing existing communities. It was officially established in 1934 and dedicated in 1940.

### Visitation and Modern History
The park is the most visited in the U.S., with over 14.1 million recreational visitors in 2021. Major attractions include Cades Cove, the Appalachian Trail, and scenic drives like Newfound Gap Road. The surrounding tourism industry, anchored by attractions like Dollywood, contributes an estimated $2.5 billion annually to the local economy. The park faced a major disaster in 2016 with wildfires that killed 14 people and destroyed over 2,000 structures.

## Terms
- **Ocoee Supergroup**: A group of late Precambrian metamorphosed sedimentary rocks that form the majority of the park's geology.
- **Cove hardwood forest**: A highly diverse forest type found in the park's moist valleys, dominated by trees like tulip poplar and yellow birch.
- **Spruce-fir forest**: A "boreal" or "Canadian" forest relict from the ice ages, found at the highest elevations and dominated by red spruce and Fraser fir.
- **Southern Sixers**: Mountains in Tennessee and North Carolina with elevations higher than 6,000 feet; the park contains 16.
- **Appalachian Trail**: A marked hiking trail in the Eastern United States that runs 71.6 miles through the park.
- **Cades Cove**: A broad, flat valley within the park containing numerous preserved historic buildings and a popular scenic loop road.
- **Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians**: A federally recognized tribe descended from Cherokee who evaded forced removal in the 1830s and now live on the Qualla Boundary south of the park.
- **Mission 66**: A National Park Service program from 1956 to 1966 to upgrade park facilities for its 50th anniversary, which built new visitor centers and towers in the Smokies.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact number of black bears in the park is variable and difficult to pinpoint.
- The cause of "Appalachian balds"—treeless mountaintop meadows—is not fully understood.
- The reason why beech trees monopolize certain high mountain gaps (beech gaps) while other species like red spruce fail to encroach is unknown.
- The successful regeneration of Fraser fir on some peaks following the devastating balsam woolly adelgid infestation of the 1960s is a subject of ongoing ecological monitoring.
