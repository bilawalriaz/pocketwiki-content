# Uruk

## Overview
Uruk (modern Warka, Iraq) was a pioneering city in ancient Sumer, central to the development of urbanization, writing, and monumental architecture in the 4th millennium BC. It is the type site for the Uruk period and, at its peak c. 3100 BC, was likely the world's largest city. Its long history, from its founding to its abandonment in the early Islamic period, illustrates the rise, decline, and cultural resilience of Mesopotamian urban centers.

## Timeline
- **c. 4000–3500 BC** — Early Uruk period; city forms from the merger of Unug and Kullaba settlements.
- **c. 3500–3100 BC** — Late Uruk period; city expands, monumental temples built, proto-cuneiform writing emerges.
- **c. 3100 BC** — Peak population (up to 50,000); city wall constructed during the Jemdet Nasr period.
- **27th century BC** — Legendary rule of Gilgamesh, according to the Sumerian King List.
- **c. 2334 BC onwards** — Uruk loses primacy after the rise of the Akkadian Empire.
- **c. 2000 BC** — Decline following the collapse of the Ur III dynasty.
- **c. 850 BC** — Neo-Assyrian annexation; city revives as a provincial capital.
- **250 BC** — Construction of the 'Head Temple' (Bīt Reš), a center for Babylonian astronomy.
- **200 BC** — 'Great Sanctuary' of Ishtar built during the Seleucid period.
- **141 BC** — Uruk continues under Parthian rule after the Seleucid loss of Mesopotamia.
- **c. 300 AD** — City mostly abandoned; later settled by Mandaeans.
- **c. 700 AD** — Final abandonment, shortly before or after the Islamic conquest.

## Body

### Urbanization and the Uruk Period
Uruk was the leading force in the shift from small agricultural villages to complex urban societies during the Uruk period (c. 4000–3200 BC). The city grew from two earlier settlements, Unug and Kullaba, which merged to form Uruk. Their core temples became the Eanna District (dedicated to the goddess Inanna) and the Anu District. By the Late Uruk period, the city's influence extended across Sumer and into colonies in upper Mesopotamia and Syria. Its culture, including writing and architecture, was exported by traders and colonists, though it could not maintain long-term military control over these distant sites.

### Architecture and Districts
Uruk's architecture represents some of the earliest monumental construction in history. The city was divided into two main sacred precincts. The **Eanna District**, dedicated to Inanna, was a walled complex of temples, workshops, and courtyards. Key structures include the early Stone-Cone Temple and the later Limestone Temple, both featuring tripartite plans and cone-mosaic façades. The **Anu District** was built on a massive terrace (the Anu ziggurat). The city was also known for its extensive canal system, connecting it to the Euphrates for trade and irrigation. A 9 km long defensive wall was built around c. 3000 BC.

### Political History and Decline
Uruk exerted hegemony over nearby settlements from the Early Uruk period (c. 3800 BC). According to the Sumerian King List, it was a seat of kingship in the Early Dynastic II period (c. 2800–2600 BC), a time sometimes called its "Heroic Age." Power later shifted to Ur. Uruk was annexed by the Akkadian Empire and experienced a revival under the Neo-Sumerian Ur III dynasty, which restored the Eanna District. After Ur's collapse (c. 2000 BC), Uruk declined until revived by the Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian empires. It remained a significant cultural and religious center through the Seleucid and Parthian periods before its final abandonment. A shift in the Euphrates River's course may have contributed to its long-term decline.

### Archaeology and Key Finds
Systematic excavation began with William Loftus (1850–1854) and was dominated by the German Oriental Society from 1912 onward. Archaeologists have uncovered multiple building levels (Uruk XVIII to I). Major finds include thousands of proto-cuneiform tablets, the earliest known writing mentioning a person's name ("Kushim"), and the Mask of Warka (c. 3100 BC), a marble face likely depicting Inanna. In 2003, magnetometer scans suggested a structure in the dry riverbed of the Euphrates that *might* correspond to the legendary tomb of Gilgamesh, though this remains unconfirmed.

## Terms
- **Uruk Period:** A cultural period (c. 4000–3200 BC) characterized by urbanization, writing, and monumental architecture, named after the city.
- **Eanna District:** The central temple complex at Uruk dedicated to the goddess Inanna, containing major administrative and religious buildings.
- **Anu District:** A temple precinct at Uruk built on a massive terrace, traditionally associated with the sky god Anu.
- **Tripartite Plan:** A temple architectural style with three parallel halls, a central one flanked by two smaller ones.
- **Cone Mosaic:** A decorative technique using small, colored clay cones pressed into mudbrick façades to create geometric patterns.
- **Sumerian King List (SKL):** A cuneiform document listing the kings of Sumer and their reign lengths, blending history and mythology.
- **Jemdet Nasr Period:** The archaeological period (c. 3100–2900 BC) following the Late Uruk period, during which Uruk's great wall was built.
- **Proto-cuneiform:** The earliest form of writing found on clay tablets from Uruk, consisting of pictographs and numerical signs.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The historicity of early rulers listed in the Sumerian King List, such as Enmerkar and Gilgamesh, is debated; they may be legendary figures.
- The exact function of many Uruk-period buildings (e.g., whether Building E was a palace or communal structure) is uncertain.
- The cause of the ritual destruction of major Eanna temples at the end of the Uruk IV period is unknown.
- The identification of the structure found in the former Euphrates bed as Gilgamesh's tomb is highly speculative and unproven.
- The precise reasons for Uruk's final decline in Late Antiquity are unclear, though river shift and salinization from irrigation are suggested factors.
