# Gone with the Wind (film)

## Overview
*Gone with the Wind* is a 1939 American epic historical romance film adapted from Margaret Mitchell's 1936 novel. It is a landmark of Hollywood cinema, renowned for its scale, production values, and enduring cultural impact. The film is also a subject of significant controversy for its romanticized portrayal of the antebellum South and its depictions of race and slavery.

## Timeline
- **June 1936** — David O. Selznick purchases film rights to Margaret Mitchell's novel for $50,000. |
- **August 1938** — Selznick strikes a deal with MGM to secure Clark Gable for the role of Rhett Butler.
- **January 13, 1939** — Vivien Leigh is announced as the actress cast in the role of Scarlett O'Hara.
- **January 26, 1939** — Principal photography begins under director George Cukor.
- **February 1939** — Cukor is fired and replaced by Victor Fleming.
- **July 1, 1939** — Principal photography concludes.
- **December 15, 1939** — The film premieres in Atlanta, Georgia.
- **1940** — The film wins a record ten Academy Awards, including Best Picture.
- **1942** — Selznick sells his share in the film to MGM, giving the studio full ownership.
- **1989** — The film is selected for preservation in the U.S. National Film Registry.
- **June 9, 2020** — The film is temporarily removed from HBO Max amid debate over its racial depictions.

## Body

### Production and Casting
Producer David O. Selznick's pursuit of Clark Gable for Rhett Butler delayed filming for two years. The search for Scarlett O'Hara was a massive publicity campaign, interviewing 1,400 women before selecting English actress Vivien Leigh. The production was marked by turmoil, including the firing of director George Cukor weeks into filming and extensive script revisions by multiple writers, including Ben Hecht. The final screenplay, credited solely to Sidney Howard, was a condensed version of his original, which was deemed too long for a single film.

### Reception and Legacy
Upon its 1939 release, the film was a colossal commercial success, becoming the highest-earning film of its time and holding that record for over 25 years. It received widespread critical acclaim, particularly for its performances and technical achievements, and won a record ten Academy Awards. Hattie McDaniel's win for Best Supporting Actress made her the first African-American to win an Oscar. The film is preserved in the National Film Registry and remains one of the most popular and culturally significant films in history.

### Controversy and Re-evaluation
*Gone with the Wind* has been criticized since its release for perpetuating the "Lost Cause" myth and glorifying slavery. African-American commentators have condemned its stereotypical characterizations and "whitewashing" of history. In the 21st century, these criticisms led to its temporary removal from streaming platforms, sparking debates about historical context versus censorship. The film also contains a scene depicting marital rape, which has been widely condemned for conflating romance and sexual violence.

## Terms
- **Lost Cause:** A historical ideology that romanticizes the Confederate States of America and its cause in the Civil War, often minimizing the role of slavery.
- **Technicolor:** A color motion picture process that was a major innovation in the 1930s, used extensively in the film's production.
- **Roadshow engagement:** A limited-release theatrical strategy where a film is shown in select cities at premium prices before a wider release.
- **Production Code:** A set of industry guidelines (c. 1930–1968) that governed the content of American motion pictures.
- **Jim Crow laws:** State and local laws that enforced racial segregation in the Southern United States.
- **Carpetbagger:** A derogatory term for Northerners who moved to the South after the Civil War, often for political or economic gain.
- **Shanty town:** A makeshift settlement, often for impoverished people, as depicted in the film's Reconstruction-era Atlanta.
- **National Film Registry:** A list of films preserved by the U.S. Library of Congress as being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant."

## Debates and open questions
- **Historical Accuracy vs. Myth:** Scholars debate whether the film is a faithful reflection of its era's attitudes or a harmful perpetuation of the "Lost Cause" myth and white supremacist ideology.
- **Artistic Merit vs. Producer's Vision:** Critics like Andrew Sarris have argued the film lacks a singular directorial vision, being more a product of producer David Selznick's obsessive control and literal adaptation of the novel.
- **Cultural Legacy:** There is ongoing discussion about how to present the film in the modern era—whether to contextualize it, restrict it, or present it as a historical artifact that reveals the prejudices of its time.
