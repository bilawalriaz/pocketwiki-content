# William Shakespeare

## Overview

William Shakespeare (baptized 26 April 1564 – died 23 April 1616) was an English playwright, poet, and actor, widely regarded as the greatest writer in the English language. Born in Stratford-upon-Avon, he rose from a provincial background to become a leading figure in London’s theatre scene, co-owning the Lord Chamberlain’s Men (later the King’s Men) and helping build the Globe Theatre. Over roughly three decades, he authored 39 plays, 154 sonnets, and several narrative poems, producing masterpieces across comedy, history, tragedy, and tragicomedy. His works, first collected in the 1623 First Folio, remain globally performed and studied, shaping English literature, language, and culture.

## Body

### Early Life and Education

Shakespeare was the third of eight children of John Shakespeare, a glover and alderman, and Mary Arden, from a wealthy Recusant Catholic family. Baptized on 26 April 1564, his birthdate is traditionally celebrated on 23 April (St. George’s Day). He likely attended the King’s New School in Stratford, receiving a rigorous Latin education typical of grammar schools. At 18, he married Anne Hathaway, then 26. Their children—Susanna (1583), and twins Hamnet and Judith (1585)—were born quickly into the marriage. Hamnet died at 11 in 1596.

### Lost Years and Entry into Theatre

Between 1585 and 1592, Shakespeare vanished from records, prompting the term “lost years.” Legends suggest he may have worked as a schoolmaster or fled Stratford after a poaching incident. By 1592, he had emerged in London as a playwright and actor. Robert Greene’s satirical jab in *Groats-Worth of Wit* refers to him as an “upstart Crow,” marking the earliest known reference to Shakespeare in print.

### London Career and Theatrical Success

By 1594, Shakespeare was a shareholder in the Lord Chamberlain’s Men, performing at The Theatre and The Curtain in Shoreditch. In 1599, the company dismantled The Theatre and rebuilt it as the Globe. After King James I ascended the throne in 1603, the company became the King’s Men and gained royal patronage. Shakespeare also invested in real estate, buying New Place in Stratford (1597) and acquiring tithes (1605), making him financially secure.

### Later Years and Death

Shakespeare gradually withdrew from London life after 1608, possibly due to plague closures and aging. His final plays—*The Winter’s Tale*, *The Tempest*, *Henry VIII*, and *The Two Noble Kinsmen*—were collaborations, likely with John Fletcher. He returned to Stratford permanently around 1613. He died on 23 April 1616, aged 52, leaving a will that notably bequeathed his wife Anne “my second best bed.” He was buried in Holy Trinity Church.

### Plays and Literary Development

Shakespeare’s career evolved through distinct phases. His early works—*Henry VI*, *Richard III*, *Titus Andronicus*, *The Comedy of Errors*, and *The Taming of the Shrew*—were influenced by medieval drama, Senecan tragedy, and contemporary dramatists like Marlowe and Kyd. These plays often drew from Holinshed’s Chronicles and Plutarch’s *Parallel Lives*.

His middle period saw the rise of mature comedies (*A Midsummer Night’s Dream*, *Much Ado About Nothing*, *As You Like It*, *Twelfth Night*) and histories (*Richard II*, *Henry IV*, Parts 1 and 2*, *Henry V*). Tragedies began with *Romeo and Juliet* and *Julius Caesar*, leading to his greatest works: *Hamlet*, *Othello*, *King Lear*, and *Macbeth*. These explored complex psychology, moral ambiguity, and fatal flaws.

His final phase produced tragicomedies or “romances” (*Cymbeline*, *The Winter’s Tale*, *The Tempest*) featuring themes of redemption, time, and reconciliation. Collaborations with Fletcher marked his twilight years.

### Style and Innovation

Shakespeare began writing in conventional Elizabethan styles but soon revolutionized drama. He mastered blank verse in iambic pentameter, using it to express inner turmoil and heightened emotion. His soliloquies—especially Hamlet’s “To be or not to be”—delved deeply into character consciousness. He blended comedy and tragedy, expanded character complexity, and reshaped narrative structure to reflect multiple perspectives.

### Poems and Sonnets

During plague closures (1593–1594), Shakespeare published narrative poems *Venus and Adonis* and *The Rape of Lucrece*, both dedicated to Henry Wriothesley, Earl of Southampton. The 154-sonnet sequence, published in 1609, explores love, beauty, mortality, and time, addressing a “fair youth” and a “dark lady.” Though their autobiographical nature is debated, they stand as profound meditations on human experience.

### Textual Transmission

Many early plays appeared in unauthorized “bad quartos,” criticized for inaccuracies. The 1623 First Folio, compiled by Heminges and Condell, preserved 36 plays, 18 previously unpublished. Variants between quartos and folios reveal possible revisions. Modern editors continue to grapple with textual fidelity, especially in *King Lear*, where versions differ significantly.

### Legacy and Influence

Shakespeare’s impact spans centuries. He enriched English vocabulary and idiom, influenced poets like Milton and the Romantics, and inspired composers (Mendelssohn, Verdi), painters (Blake, Hogarth), and filmmakers (Kurosawa, Welles). His global reach includes translations into over 80 languages and adaptations across cultures. Despite evolving critical views—from Restoration preferences for Jonson to Romantic adulation and modernist reinterpretations—his reputation endures. T.S. Eliot praised his timeless relevance, while Harold Bloom called him “larger than Plato.” Today, he remains the world’s most performed playwright.

## Terms

- **Blank verse**: Unrhymed iambic pentameter, Shakespeare’s primary poetic form.
- **First Folio**: The 1623 collected edition of Shakespeare’s plays, compiled by Heminges and Condell.
- **Lord Chamberlain’s Men / King’s Men**: Shakespeare’s acting company, renamed under royal patronage in 1603.
- **Problem plays**: Term coined by Frederick S. Boas for ambiguous dramas like *Measure for Measure* and *Troilus and Cressida*.
- **Romances/tragicomedies**: Late plays blending tragic and comic elements (*The Tempest*, *The Winter’s Tale*).
- **University Wits**: Group of educated playwrights including Marlowe and Nashe, whom Shakespeare rivaled.
- **Bad quartos**: Poorly transcribed early printed editions of Shakespeare’s plays.
- **Dark Lady**: Central figure in 154 sonnets, representing sensual, illicit love.
- **Fair Youth**: Young male beloved addressed in many sonnets, possibly a patron.
- **Globe Theatre**: Open-air playhouse co-owned by Shakespeare, built in 1599.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Authorship**: Despite fringe theories proposing Bacon, Marlowe, or Oxford, scholarly consensus supports Shakespeare’s authorship.
- **Religious beliefs**: Evidence points to both Catholic and Protestant influences; his true convictions remain unclear.
- **Sexuality**: Sonnets suggest deep emotional bonds with men, but interpretations range from romantic to platonic.
- **Portraiture**: No verified contemporary images survive; the Chandos portrait is considered the most credible.
- **Textual integrity**: Disputes persist over whether Shakespeare revised plays between quarto and folio editions.
- **Chronology**: Precise dating of plays remains uncertain due to limited records.
- **Biographical details**: Sparse documentation fuels speculation about his personality, motivations, and daily life.
