# Patanjali

## Overview
Patanjali is a name associated with one or more ancient Indian authors credited with foundational works on yoga, grammar, and medicine. The identity of the author(s) is a major scholarly debate, as the works are vastly different in subject and likely date from different centuries. The most famous works are the *Yoga Sūtras*, a classical text on yoga philosophy, and the *Mahābhāṣya*, a celebrated commentary on Sanskrit grammar.

## Timeline
- **c. 2nd century BCE** — The *Mahābhāṣya* (Great Commentary) on Pāṇini's grammar is composed.
- **c. 2nd century BCE – 5th century CE** — The *Yoga Sūtras* are compiled (dating is debated).
- **c. 5th century CE** — Bhartṛhari writes, possibly referencing an expert in yoga, medicine, and grammar.
- **c. 8th–10th centuries CE** — Medical texts attributed to a Patanjali (e.g., *Patanjalatantra*) possibly originate.
- **11th century CE** — Bhoja's commentary *Rājamārttanda* first conflates the authorship of the yoga and grammar texts.
- **12th–19th centuries CE** — The *Yoga Sūtras* fall into obscurity.
- **Late 19th century CE** — The *Yoga Sūtras* are revived, notably by Swami Vivekananda.

## Body

### The Question of Identity
The central issue is whether one person authored the works attributed to "Patanjali." The *Mahābhāṣya* (grammar) and the *Yoga Sūtras* are completely different in subject, language, and vocabulary. Western scholarship generally accepts they were written by different authors. However, a tradition of a single, multifaceted sage emerged later, notably in the 11th century with Bhoja's commentary. This view is still prevalent in traditional and yoga circles.

### The Grammarian Patanjali
This Patanjali is firmly dated to the mid-2nd century BCE. His *Mahābhāṣya* is a monumental commentary on Pāṇini's grammar, defending its principles and elaborating on word-meaning relationships. He introduced concepts like *sphoṭa* (the invariant sound-unit, a precursor to the modern phoneme) and had metaphysical motivations, such as preserving scripture. His work influenced Buddhist and Jain grammarians and remained an authority for over two millennia.

### The Yogi Patanjali
The author of the *Yoga Sūtras* is estimated to have lived between the 2nd century BCE and 5th century CE. This text, consisting of 196 aphorisms, is the foundational work of classical Yoga philosophy. It was widely translated in the medieval era but later faded from prominence before its modern revival. The text focuses on the theory and practice of yoga, with minimal discussion of physical postures.

### Medical and Other Attributions
A medical text called *Patanjalatantra* is attributed to a Patanjali, possibly from the 8th–10th centuries CE. This author is cited as an authority in later medical works. A fourth scholar, also named Patanjali, wrote a commentary on the *Charaka Saṃhitā* in the 8th century. These medical authors are generally considered distinct from the grammarian.

## Terms
- **Sūtra**: A concise aphorism or rule, forming the basis of many Indian philosophical and scientific texts.
- **Mahābhāṣya**: "Great Commentary," Patanjali's influential commentary on Pāṇini's grammar.
- **Sphoṭa**: A linguistic concept for the invariant sound-unit or phoneme, distinct from its variable pronunciation.
- **Samādhi**: A state of intense concentration and meditative absorption.
- **Samkhya**: A school of Hindu philosophy that provides the metaphysical framework for the *Yoga Sūtras*.
- **Bhāṣya**: A commentary or explanatory treatise.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historicity**: Were the yoga, grammar, and medical texts written by one person or multiple authors named Patanjali? The consensus among Western scholars is that they are different individuals.
- **Dating**: The precise date of the *Yoga Sūtras* is contested, with estimates ranging from the 2nd century BCE to the 5th century CE.
- **Authorship of the Bhāṣya**: Some scholars debate whether the commentary (*Bhāṣya*) on the *Yoga Sūtras* was written by the same author as the sutras themselves or by a later editor.
- **Modern Relevance**: Scholar David Gordon White questions the relevance of the *Yoga Sūtras* to modern physical yoga practice, noting its lack of discussion on postures and breathing.
