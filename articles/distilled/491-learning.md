# Learning

## Overview
Learning is the process of acquiring understanding, knowledge, behaviors, skills, values, and preferences through interaction with the environment. It is observed in humans, animals, some machines, and evidence suggests it occurs in certain plants. Learning ranges from immediate, single-event changes (e.g., burning a hand) to gradual accumulation via repeated experience, and can occur consciously or without awareness. It is studied across fields including educational psychology, neuroscience, and cognitive science, which have identified distinct mechanisms such as habituation, conditioning, play, and social observation. The efficiency and nature of learning shift across the lifespan, influenced by neurobiology, motivation, environment, and instructional design.

## Timeline
- **1913** — John B. Watson publishes "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It," advocating observable behavior over introspection.
- **1935** — Konrad Lorenz discovers filial imprinting in birds, showing rapid learning independent of behavioral consequences.
- **1989** — Moore identifies three core interaction types (learner–learner, learner–instructor, learner–content) for quality online learning.
- **1992** — Perkins and Salomon distinguish near/far transfer and positive/negative transfer in learning application.
- **1993** — Moore proposes Theory of Transactional Distance, linking structure and dialogue to bridge geographical gaps in education.
- **2016** — Gagliano et al. publish evidence for associative learning in garden pea plants (*Pisum sativum*) using classical conditioning paradigms.
- **2016** — Gagliano et al. publish findings on habituation in *Mimosa pudica*, sparking debate on plant cognition definitions.

## Body

### Mechanisms of Learning
**Non-associative learning** involves a permanent change in response strength to a single stimulus after repeated exposure, excluding sensory adaptation or fatigue. It comprises **habituation** (diminished innate response to a repeated stimulus, distinct from extinction which involves reward removal) and **sensitization** (progressive amplification of a response following repeated stimulus, often defensive). Habituation is stimulus-specific and faster for high-rate or intense stimuli; observed across animals, *Mimosa pudica*, and *Stentor coeruleus*.

**Associative learning** links two stimuli or events. **Classical conditioning** pairs a neutral stimulus with an unconditioned stimulus (US) eliciting a reflexive unconditioned response (UR); eventually the neutral stimulus becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS) eliciting a conditioned response (CR). Ivan Pavlov demonstrated this with dogs (bell + meat powder → salivation); John B. Watson extended it to human emotion ("Little Albert"). It occurs in honeybees and, per recent evidence, garden peas. **Operant conditioning** shapes voluntary behavior via consequences: **reinforcement** (increases behavior) and **punishment** (decreases behavior). Both have *positive* (adding a stimulus) and *negative* (removing a stimulus) subtypes. Effective operant conditioning requires immediate reinforcement (trace conditioning) and focuses on conscious behavior rather than reflexes. **Observational learning** acquires behavior by watching social models (parents, peers) without requiring direct reinforcement. **Imprinting** is rapid, phase-specific learning (e.g., Lorenz’s birds bonding to a moving object) independent of consequences.

### Play and Development
Play is behavior with no immediate end that improves future performance, costly in energy and risk but evolutionarily preserved, suggesting significant benefits. Seen mostly in mammals and birds, it peaks in youth, linking it to learning. **Lev Vygotsky** argued play is pivotal for development: it is the first form of learning language, communication, rules, and symbols, linking learning to semiosis (meaning-making). Five intersecting types exist: *sensorimotor/functional* (repetition), *roleplay* (from ~age 3), *rule-based*, *construction*, and *movement/physical*. All foster creative thinking and problem-solving.

### Social and Cultural Learning
**Enculturation** is acquiring the values, behaviors, language, and rituals of one’s native culture through parents, adults, and peers (e.g., Mazahua collaborative practices, Mexican *acomedido* helpfulness, Peruvian weaving). It differs from **acculturation** (adopting a foreign culture). **Episodic learning** is behavior change from a specific event recorded in episodic memory (e.g., fear of dogs after a bite), distinct from semantic memory (facts). Humans show high accuracy in episodic recognition without intentional memorization, implying large attentional storage capacity.

### Educational Formats and Strategies
**Formal learning** is organized, goal-directed, teacher-led (schools, workplaces), often credentialed. **Non-formal learning** is organized but outside formal systems (clubs, workshops), may lack explicit objectives. **Informal learning** is unstructured, incidental, self-directed, arising from daily life (meals, exploration, mistakes); it is voluntary, context-relevant, and often yields applicable knowledge. Systems increasingly combine these (e.g., crediting informal workshop experience). **Active learning** (learner controls experience, monitors understanding via metacognition) outperforms passive instruction. **Meaningful learning** integrates new knowledge with existing schema, contrasting **rote learning** (verbatim memorization via repetition, criticized for killing intrinsic motivation). **Evidence-based learning** applies scientific findings (e.g., spaced repetition) to accelerate acquisition. **Multimedia learning** uses auditory/visual dual-coding. **E-learning** is computer-enhanced; **mobile learning (m-learning)** uses phones. **Augmented learning** dynamically tailors context-driven digital content (text, video, audio) to the learner’s environment, improving lifetime performance. Moore’s transactional distance theory posits structure and dialogue bridge geographical gaps in online education. **Tangential learning** is self-education sparked by exposure in an enjoyed context (e.g., games inspiring instrument practice); intrinsic integration of content is key. **Dialogic learning** centers on dialogue. **Incidental learning** occurs as an unplanned byproduct of activity; instructors can engineer environments to prompt it (e.g., placing desired items out of reach to elicit language).

### Domains and Transfer
**Bloom’s taxonomy** identifies three domains: *Cognitive* (recall, analysis, problem-solving), *Psychomotor* (physical skills like driving), *Affective* (emotions, values like appreciation). They overlap (e.g., chess requires rules, piece manipulation, and eventual appreciation). **Transfer of learning** applies skill/knowledge to novel problems. It is infrequent, most common when cued, primed, and guided. Types: *near transfer* (similar context), *far transfer* (different context). *Positive transfer* aids novel solving; *negative transfer* inhibits performance (e.g., third-language interference). Promoting transfer requires aligning instruction with practice ("hugging") and bridging prior knowledge to current content.

### Factors Affecting Learning
**Instructional techniques** enhancing retention: spacing effect (spaced repetition > cramming), desirable difficulty, learning by teaching, self-explaining, and testing effect/low-stakes quizzing. **Genetics** influence intelligence and learning speed. **Socioeconomic/physical conditions**: malnutrition, fatigue, poor health, ventilation, lighting, hygiene, and classroom design (size, comfort, furniture, technology, aesthetics) impact success. **Psychological factors**: intrinsic motivation (curiosity) sustains learning better than extrinsic (grades); praise enhances performance; fault-finding and embarrassment are counterproductive. Speed, accuracy, retention depend on aptitude, attitude, interest, attention, energy, and motivation. **Epigenetic factors**: learning involves dynamic gene expression changes in neurons via DNA methylation/demethylation and histone acetylation/deacetylation. DNA repair processes (non-homologous end joining, base excision repair) introduce epigenetic alterations during memory formation. **Adult vs. child learning**: Children learn more efficiently due to heightened neuroplasticity, more "silent synapses," and rapid GABA boosting during critical periods. However, perceived adult deficits may stem from less time, support, and different attitudes rather than inherent inability. Adults have greater autonomy in selecting *what* and *how* to learn; early learning sets life trajectories.

### Evolutionary Perspectives
Animals rely on **learning** (gathering/using environmental info) and **innate knowledge** (genetically inherited, e.g., foal walking). Learning evolves when environments change within a lifetime but are not constant: stable environments favor innate knowledge (learning unnecessary); chaotic environments favor non-learning (learned info instantly obsolete). Cost-benefit analysis determines the strategy: if knowledge cost > benefit, non-learning evolves. *Drosophila* studies show learning can decrease productivity due to energy costs or memory interference. In moderately changing environments (e.g., aquatic spatial cues), learning is favored for adaptation.

### Plant Learning
Plant physiologists investigate learning and memory as responses to external cues. **Monica Gagliano** (2016) demonstrated **associative learning** in garden peas (*Pisum sativum*) using a Y-maze: seedlings paired light (US) with fan wind (CS); later, they grew toward the fan alone predicting light location, unlike controls. This distinguishes active associative learning from innate phototropism. Proposed mechanism: coupling of mechanosensory (MS ion channels, Ca2+ influx, turgor pressure) and photosensory pathways via auxin signaling. Gagliano also showed **habituation** in *Mimosa pudica* (diminished leaf-folding to repeated dropping). Controversy persists: critics (Abrahmson, Pollan) argue definitions of "learning" and "cognition" are inconsistent; researchers accept data but dispute terminology. Future directions include testing circadian modulation of plant learning and standardizing definitions.

### Machine Learning
A branch of artificial intelligence constructing systems that learn from data (e.g., spam detection). Most models are probabilistic, associating inputs with output probabilities.

## Terms
- ****Habituation**** — Non-associative learning where an innate response to a specific stimulus diminishes with repeated exposure; distinct from extinction (associative).
- ****Sensitization**** — Non-associative learning where response to a stimulus progressively amplifies, often following a threatening stimulus; underlies adaptive and maladaptive processes.
- ****Classical Conditioning**** — Associative learning pairing a neutral stimulus with an unconditioned stimulus (US) until the neutral stimulus becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS) eliciting a conditioned response (CR).
- ****Operant Conditioning**** — Learning where voluntary behavior is shaped by consequences: reinforcement (increases behavior) or punishment (decreases behavior), each with positive (add stimulus) and negative (remove stimulus) subtypes.
- ****Imprinting**** — Rapid, phase-specific learning (e.g., filial bonding in birds) apparently independent of behavioral consequences.
- ****Enculturation**** — Process of learning the values, behaviors, language, and rituals of one's native culture through social interaction.
- ****Episodic Learning**** — Behavior change resulting from a specific event, recorded in episodic memory (autobiographical experience), distinct from semantic memory (facts).
- ****Transfer of Learning**** — Application of skill/knowledge to a novel problem; *near transfer* (similar context), *far transfer* (different context); can be positive (helps) or negative (hinders).
- ****Neuroplasticity**** — The brain's ability to reorganize synaptic connections, heightened during critical developmental periods in children, facilitating flexible learning.
- ****Epigenetic Mechanisms**** — Chemical modifications (DNA methylation, histone acetylation) altering gene expression in neurons without changing DNA sequence, underlying long-term memory formation.

## Debates and open questions
- **Plant cognition terminology**: Whether plant behaviors (associative learning, habituation) constitute true "learning" and "cognition" or require new definitions; researchers disagree on consistent definitions (Abrahmson, Pollan).
- **Mechanism of plant associative learning**: The physiological pathway coupling mechanosensory and photosensory systems in plants remains unknown.
- **Circadian modulation in plants**: Whether endogenous circadian rhythms gate or modulate learning capacity in plants is untested.
- **Adult learning deficits**: Whether age-related learning decline is inherent (neurobiological: fewer silent synapses, GABA dynamics) or situational (time, support, motivation, environment) is contested.
- **Transfer promotion**: Despite long history, the precise instructional conditions reliably producing far transfer remain elusive; debate continues on "hugging" vs. "bridging" efficacy.
- **Evolutionary model standardization**: Inconsistent definitions of "ecological change" in models of learning evolution hinder theory-empirical links.
