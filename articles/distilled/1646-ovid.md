# Ovid

## Overview
Publius Ovidius Naso (43 BC – AD 17/18), known as Ovid, was a Roman poet ranked alongside Virgil and Horace as a canonical figure of Latin literature. He achieved immense popularity writing elegiac poetry on erotic themes (*Amores*, *Ars Amatoria*) before completing his masterpiece, the *Metamorphoses*, a 15-book hexameter epic cataloguing nearly 250 myths of transformation. In AD 8, Emperor Augustus exiled him to Tomis on the Black Sea for *carmen et error* ("a poem and a mistake"), a vague charge scholars link to the subversive nature of his *Ars Amatoria* against Augustan moral legislation or a personal court scandal. He died in exile after a decade, leaving the *Fasti* (a poetic calendar) unfinished. His work, blending rhetorical wit, psychological depth, and genre experimentation, became the primary vehicle for classical mythology in Western art and literature.

## Body

### Life and Career
Ovid was born in Sulmo (modern Sulmona) to an important equestrian family. Educated in rhetoric in Rome under Arellius Fuscus and Porcius Latro, he was groomed for law but, according to Seneca the Elder, favored the emotional over the argumentative pole of rhetoric. Following his brother’s death at age 20, he abandoned law, travelled, held minor magistracies (*tresviri capitales*, *decemviri litibus iudicandis*), and resigned to pursue poetry c. 29–25 BC against his father’s wishes. He entered the literary circle of patron Marcus Valerius Messalla Corvinus and knew poets like Propertius, Horace, and Tibullus. He married three times (divorced twice by age 30), had a daughter, and his third wife, connected to the *gens Fabia*, supported him in exile.

### The Exile (AD 8)
In AD 8, Augustus banished Ovid to Tomis (modern Constanța, Romania) by imperial decree, bypassing the Senate. Ovid cited *carmen et error*—identifying the *carmen* as likely the *Ars Amatoria* (published c. AD 2) and the *error* as an unspecified indiscretion. The exile coincided with the banishment of Augustus’s granddaughter Julia the Younger and grandson Agrippa Postumus, suggesting a broader dynastic purge. The *Ars Amatoria*’s instruction on adultery violated the Julian marriage laws (18 BC), but the decade-long gap between publication and exile fuels debate: some argue the poem was a pretext for a personal offense, perhaps Ovid’s knowledge of a conspiracy involving Julia’s husband, Lucius Aemilius Paullus (executed AD 8). In Tomis, Ovid learned Sarmatian and Getic, wrote the despairing *Tristia* and *Epistulae ex Ponto* pleading for recall, and claimed to have composed a poem in the local language. He died there after nine or ten years.

### The "Exile Hoax" Debate
In 1923, J. J. Hartman proposed Ovid never left Rome and invented his exile poetry. Fitton Brown revived this in 1985, arguing exile is only attested by Ovid (and "dubious" later references), that the poet separated his poetic "I" from reality, and that Tomis geography was known from Virgil and Herodotus. Most scholars reject this, primarily because Ovid would not have left his *Fasti*—his bid for imperial poet laureate—unfinished voluntarily.

### Major Works: Elegiac Poetry
*   **Heroides** (c. 19 BC): 21 epistles in elegiac couplets from mythological heroines (Penelope, Dido, Medea) to absent lovers. The first 14 are the core collection; Letter 15 (Sappho) and the double letters (16–21) are debated as spurious or later additions. The work adapts *ethopoeia* (speaking in character) and *suasoriae* (persuasive speeches) from rhetorical schools, transferring epic/tragic figures into elegy.
*   **Amores** (3 books, c. 8–3 BC): Elegiac poems centered on the poet’s affair with Corinna. Ovid innovates by making *Amor* (Cupid), not the poet, the protagonist, using love as a metaphor for poetry. Notable for self-conscious playfulness (e.g., Corinna’s hair dye disaster, 1.14; the dead parrot, 2.6).
*   **Ars Amatoria** (3 books, AD 2): Didactic elegy teaching seduction (Book 1–2 for men, Book 3 for women). Ovid styles himself *praeceptor amoris* (teacher of love), parodying didactic tradition with mythological digressions (Sabine women, Icarus, Vulcan’s net). The *Remedia Amoris* (AD 2) offers a mock-medical "cure" for love, closing the didactic cycle.
*   **Medicamina Faciei Femineae**: Fragmentary didactic elegy on female cosmetics, parodying Hellenistic predecessors like Nicander.
*   **Fasti** (6 books, unfinished): Elegiac poem on the Roman calendar (Jan–Jun), explaining festivals, astronomy, and agriculture. Interrupted by exile; Ovid calls himself *vates* (seer/poet) and emphasizes plebeian traditions, possibly subverting Augustan ideology.

### Major Works: Epic and Exile Poetry
*   **Metamorphoses** (15 books, completed AD 8): Hexameter epic cataloguing ~250 myths from chaos to Julius Caesar’s apotheosis. Organized by loose chronology, geography, and thematic contrast (e.g., pious Baucis/Philemon vs. wicked Erysichthon). It fuses epic grandeur with elegiac psychology and Alexandrian erudition, serving as a "gallery of literary genres" (Conte). Key concept: *pia mendacia fraude* (pious fraud/white lie).
*   **Tristia** (5 books, AD 9–12): Exile elegies expressing despair, defending his poetry (Book 2), describing Tomis (Book 3), and addressing wife/friends (Books 4–5).
*   **Epistulae ex Ponto** (4 books, AD 13–16): Verse letters to friends/imperial family desperately seeking recall. More urgent than *Tristia*; details life in Tomis (climate, geography, native friendships).
*   **Ibis** (644 lines): Elegiac curse poem against an unnamed enemy in exile, modeled on Callimachus’ *Ibis*, deploying mythological exempla of torment.

### Style and Innovation
Quintilian called Ovid the last and most "sportive" love elegist. Unlike Catullus, Tibullus, or Propertius, Ovid’s persona is emotionally detached; scholars widely view Corinna as a fictional construct or metapoetic symbol for elegy itself. He varies tone from sexually explicit to tender, and generic conventions from *paraklausithyron* (shut-out lover) to unique inventions. Heinze and Otis distinguish his "elegiac style" (sentimental, tender, asymmetrical, familiar address) from his "epic style" (solemn, awe-inspiring, continuous, symmetrical, concealed narrator). In the *Metamorphoses*, he engages the full classical tradition (Hesiod, Callimachus, Virgil), fusing erotic psychology with epic form.

### Legacy and Reception
Ovid was famous in his lifetime but criticized for insolence (*Remedia Amoris*). In the Middle Ages, the *Ovide moralisé* (c. 1300) allegorized the *Metamorphoses*, influencing Chaucer and Dante (who ranks him with Homer, Virgil, Horace, Lucan). The Renaissance saw him as a humanist cornerstone; Golding’s 1567 English translation shaped Marlowe and Shakespeare. Jesuit schools taught him but censored "corrupting" passages (1599 burning in England). Romantics (19th c.) found him "stuffy" but embraced the exile figure (Delacroix, Baudelaire, Pushkin). Modern receptions include Joyce (*Dedalus* from Daedalus), Mandelstam (*Tristia*), Ransmayr (*The Last World*), Malouf (*An Imaginary Life*), and feminist retellings (Ali Smith, Madeleine Miller, Fiona Benson, Nina MacLaughlin) critiquing sexual violence in the *Metamorphoses*. Bob Dylan borrowed lines from Peter Green’s *Poems of Exile* translation for *Modern Times* (2006).

## Terms
- ****Elegiac couplet**** — A poetic meter of a dactylic hexameter followed by a pentameter, standard for Roman love elegy.
- ****Dactylic hexameter**** — The meter of epic poetry (six feet per line), used for the *Metamorphoses* and *Fasti* (though *Fasti* uses elegiacs).
- ****Praeceptor amoris**** — "Teacher of love"; Ovid’s self-designated persona in the *Ars Amatoria*.
- ****Ethopoeia**** — A rhetorical exercise of speaking in the character of another person; basis for the *Heroides*.
- ****Suasoriae**** — Rhetorical exercises in persuasive deliberative speech; influences the argumentative structure of the *Heroides*.
- ****Carmen et error**** — "A poem and a mistake"; Ovid’s cryptic phrase for the dual cause of his exile.
- ****Vates**** — A prophet, seer, or inspired poet; title Ovid claims in the *Fasti* to assert religious authority.
- ****Pia mendacia fraude**** — "Pious fraud" or "white lie"; concept from *Metamorphoses* describing a deception for a higher truth.
- ****Paraklausithyron**** — A literary topos where a lover laments outside a locked door; used by Ovid in *Amores* 1.6.
- ****Tomis**** — Ovid’s place of exile (modern Constanța, Romania), a Greek colony in Moesia on the Black Sea.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Cause of Exile:** Was it solely the *Ars Amatoria* (offending moral legislation), a specific "error" (witnessing a dynastic scandal involving Julia the Younger/Agrippa Postumus), or a combination? The decade gap between poem and punishment complicates the "poem" theory.
*   **Historicity of Exile:** A minority view (Hartman, Brown) argues Ovid never left Rome and the exile works are fiction. The consensus rejects this, citing the unfinished *Fasti* as proof of involuntary interruption.
*   **Authenticity of *Heroides* 15 and 16–21:** Letter 15 (Sappho) and the double letters are absent from some manuscripts and stylistically distinct; scholars debate if they are Ovidian, later additions, or spurious.
*   **Corinna’s Reality:** Is the *Amores* mistress a real person, a composite, or a metapoetic fiction representing the elegiac genre? Most scholars favor fiction.
*   **Fasti’s Incompletion:** Did Ovid abandon it due to lack of libraries in Tomis, loss of imperial patronage (Augustus died AD 14), or despair? He claimed to have revised it in exile.
*   **Feminist Critique:** Since Richlin (1992) and #MeToo (2017), scholars debate whether the *Metamorphoses*’ pervasive sexual violence reflects cultural documentation, poetic sensationalism, or misogynistic ideology, prompting modern creative resistance.
