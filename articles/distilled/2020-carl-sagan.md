# Carl Sagan

## Overview
Carl Sagan (1934–1996) was an American astronomer, planetary scientist, and science communicator who became one of the most influential public figures in science. He made significant contributions to planetary science, championed the search for extraterrestrial life, and popularized scientific skepticism and critical thinking for a global audience through works like the documentary series *Cosmos*.

## Timeline
- **Nov 9, 1934** — Born in Brooklyn, New York.
- **1951** — Graduated from Rahway High School.
- **1960** — Earned Ph.D. in astronomy and astrophysics from the University of Chicago.
- **1962** — Mariner 2 confirmed his conclusions on Venus's high surface temperature.
- **1963–1968** — Served as an assistant professor at Harvard University.
- **1968** — Denied tenure at Harvard; joined Cornell University faculty.
- **1972** — Helped design the Pioneer 10 plaque, the first physical message sent into space.
- **1977** — Co-created the Voyager Golden Record; won the Pulitzer Prize for *The Dragons of Eden*.
- **1980** — Co-wrote and narrated the documentary series *Cosmos: A Personal Voyage*.
- **1983** — Co-authored the "TTAPS" paper introducing the term "nuclear winter."
- **1994** — Received the National Academy of Sciences Public Welfare Medal.
- **Dec 20, 1996** — Died of pneumonia at age 62 in Seattle, Washington.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born in Brooklyn to a Ukrainian-born garment worker and a housewife, Sagan was instilled with skepticism and wonder. A visit to the 1939 New York World's Fair sparked his fascination with the cosmos. He attended the University of Chicago, influenced by scientists like Enrico Fermi and G. P. Kuiper, earning his Ph.D. in 1960. During graduate studies, he worked on the classified Project A119, a plan to detonate a nuclear device on the Moon.

### Scientific Career and Achievements
Sagan's early work centered on planetary atmospheres. He correctly theorized that Venus had a hot, dense atmosphere due to a runaway greenhouse effect, confirmed by Mariner 2 in 1962. He hypothesized that Jupiter's moon Europa might have a subsurface ocean and that Saturn's moon Titan could have liquid hydrocarbon seas. He contributed to NASA's Mariner, Viking, and Voyager missions and was central to the search for extraterrestrial life, helping design the Pioneer plaques and Voyager Golden Record. He was a founding member and first president of the Planetary Society.

### Science Communication and Popularization
A prolific communicator, Sagan authored over 600 scientific papers and numerous popular books. His 1980 PBS series *Cosmos: A Personal Voyage* became the most widely watched series in American public television history, reaching over 500 million people. He won a Pulitzer Prize for *The Dragons of Eden* (1977). He argued scientists have a duty to share their work, popularizing the phrase "billions and billions" (though he never actually said it) and the maxim "Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence."

### Skepticism and Social Advocacy
Sagan was a vocal advocate for scientific skepticism, outlining a "Baloney Detection Kit" in *The Demon-Haunted World*. He criticized pseudoscience, astrology, and nuclear weapons. As a key researcher in the "nuclear winter" hypothesis, he posited that a large-scale nuclear war could cause catastrophic global cooling. He was arrested twice for protesting at the Nevada Test Site and advocated for free speech and civil liberties.

### Personal Life and Beliefs
Sagan was married three times: to biologist Lynn Margulis, artist Linda Salzman, and author Ann Druyan, with whom he had two children. He described himself as an agnostic, skeptical of an anthropomorphic God but open to the "God of Spinoza and Einstein." He saw science as a profound source of spirituality and was an advocate for marijuana. He died in 1996 from pneumonia related to myelodysplasia.

## Terms
- **Greenhouse effect:** The process by which a planet's atmosphere traps heat, raising its surface temperature. Sagan argued this process made Venus extremely hot.
- **Pale Blue Dot:** A photograph of Earth taken by Voyager 1 from a great distance, which Sagan famously described as a "mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam."
- **Voyager Golden Record:** A gold-plated copper record containing sounds and images of Earth, sent with the Voyager probes as a message to potential extraterrestrial finders.
- **Nuclear winter:** A hypothesized period of global cooling and darkness following a large-scale nuclear war, due to soot and dust blocking sunlight.
- **SETI:** The Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence, a scientific effort to detect signals from intelligent alien civilizations.
- **Sagan standard:** The principle that "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence," a cornerstone of scientific skepticism.
- **Baloney Detection Kit:** A set of critical-thinking tools outlined by Sagan to help identify flawed arguments and pseudoscience.
- **Drake equation:** A probabilistic argument used to estimate the number of active, communicative extraterrestrial civilizations in the Milky Way galaxy.
- **Fermi paradox:** The apparent contradiction between the high probability of extraterrestrial civilizations and the lack of evidence for them.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Academic Reception:** Sagan's reputation was polarized. Critics like Nobel laureate Harold Urey felt he was too focused on publicity, contributing to his denial of tenure at Harvard. Others later acknowledged his popular advocacy benefited science.
- **Nuclear Winter:** The hypothesis he helped popularize was met with skepticism from some scientists and politicians who felt he used media to disseminate an uncertain idea for persuasive purposes.
- **UFOs:** While Sagan rejected extraterrestrial explanations for UFO reports, he argued they were a legitimate subject for scientific study due to public interest and potential pedagogical benefits.
