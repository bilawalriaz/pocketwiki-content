# Ethiopian Highlands

## Overview
The Ethiopian Highlands, also called the Abyssinian Highlands, are a vast, rugged mountain massif in Northeast Africa, forming the continent's largest continuous area of high elevation. Known as the "Roof of Africa," this region is geologically and ecologically significant, bisected by the Great Rift Valley and home to unique flora and fauna. Its high altitude creates a temperate climate in the tropics and has shaped human adaptation and settlement patterns for millennia.

## Timeline
- **c. 75 million years ago** — The Ethiopian Highlands begin to rise as magma uplifts the Arabian-Nubian Shield.
- **c. 30 million years ago** — A flood basalt plateau forms from voluminous lava flows.
- **c. 30–31 million years ago** — The Great Rift Valley begins to bisect the highlands, giving rise to shield volcanoes.
- **Ordovician Period** — The oldest planation surface in the northern highlands forms.
- **Last Ice Age** — The region is populated with some Eurasian (palearctic) flora.
- **Medieval period** — The Kingdom of Kaffa, a source of coffee, exists in the southern highlands.
- **Past 5000 years** — Genetic adaptations to hypoxia develop among the Amhara people.

## Body

### Physical Geography and Geology
The highlands are divided diagonally by the Main Ethiopian Rift into northwestern and southeastern portions. The northwestern Abyssinian Massif includes the Semien Mountains, with Ethiopia's highest peak, Ras Dashen (4,550 m), and the source of the Blue Nile, Lake Tana. The southeastern Harar Massif contains the Bale Mountains, with peaks over 4,000 m. Most major cities, including Addis Ababa, are located at elevations of 2,000–2,500 m.

Geologically, the highlands formed from the uplift of ancient rocks and massive flood basalt eruptions. The subsequent rifting created the Great Rift Valley, splitting the original dome and separating the African and Arabian landmasses. The northern highlands contain ancient planation surfaces, the oldest dating to the Ordovician Period.

### Climate and Ecology
Despite its tropical latitude, the highlands' altitude creates a predominantly alpine climate with a temperate character. The mountains catch monsoon rains from the Indian Ocean, causing a rainy season from June to mid-September that historically flooded the Nile.

The ecology is characterized by Afromontane flora and fauna, with distinct ecoregions based on elevation. The Ethiopian montane forests (1,100–1,800 m) have been largely converted to agriculture. The Ethiopian montane grasslands and woodlands (1,800–3,000 m) are the largest ecoregion and are heavily populated and farmed. Above 3,000 m lie the high Ethiopian montane moorlands, the largest Afroalpine region in Africa, featuring grassland and shrubby plants adapted to harsh conditions.

### Fauna and Human Adaptation
The highlands host numerous endemic species, including the endangered walia ibex and gelada baboon in the north, and the mountain nyala in the south. The critically endangered Ethiopian wolf is another key endemic. Human populations, such as the Amhara, show genetic adaptations to hypoxia (low oxygen) and low atmospheric pressure, likely developed over the past 5,000 years. The region's history includes the medieval Kingdom of Kaffa, the origin of coffee.

## Terms
- **Abyssinian Massif**: The northwestern portion of the Ethiopian Highlands, containing the Semien Mountains and Lake Tana.
- **Harar Massif**: The southeastern portion of the Ethiopian Highlands, bounded by the Rift Valley and lowlands.
- **Main Ethiopian Rift**: The section of the Great East African Rift System that bisects the Ethiopian Highlands.
- **Flood basalt**: A massive outpouring of lava from fissures, forming a plateau; the Ethiopian Highlands are built from such flows.
- **Planation surface**: A large-scale, low-relief erosional surface formed over long periods of geological time.
- **Afromontane**: A term for the distinctive flora and fauna found in the mountainous regions of Africa.
- **Afroalpine**: The high-altitude ecosystem above the tree line in African mountains, characterized by grasses, herbs, and shrubs.
- **Hypoxia**: A condition of low oxygen levels in the body, to which highland populations have adapted genetically.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the planation surfaces in northern Ethiopia do not appear to be pediplains or etchplains, contrary to suggestions for much of Africa, indicating a debate about their formation process. The precise timing and drivers of human genetic adaptations to high altitude are also an area of ongoing research.
