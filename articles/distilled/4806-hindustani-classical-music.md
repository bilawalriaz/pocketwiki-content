# Hindustani classical music

## Overview
Hindustani classical music is the classical music tradition of the Indian subcontinent's northern regions. It is a melodic system centered on ragas (melodic modes) and talas (rhythmic cycles), with no concept of harmony. It diverged from the southern Carnatic tradition around the 12th century and evolved through a synthesis of Hindu and Persian musical ideas, becoming a core part of North Indian culture.

## Timeline
- **2nd–3rd century CE** — Bharata Muni composes the *Natya Shastra*, a foundational treatise on music and performance.
- **c. 3rd–4th century CE** — The *Dattilam* text is composed, defining early melodic structures (jatis) and tonal frameworks.
- **c. 1100 CE** — Narada's *Sangita Makarandha* is written, containing rules similar to modern Hindustani music.
- **12th century** — Jayadeva's *Gita Govinda* is composed, an early musical composition in the classical tradition.
- **13th century** — Sharangadeva's *Sangita Ratnakara* is composed, the last text shared by both Hindustani and Carnatic traditions.
- **1253–1325** — Amir Khusrau, a Sufi composer, influences the music of the Delhi Sultanate, introducing new ragas and genres.
- **1486–1516** — Raja Mansingh Tomar of Gwalior patronizes the development of the dhrupad form and shifts compositions to local languages.
- **1901** — Vishnu Digambar Paluskar founds the Gandharva Mahavidyalaya, a public music school.
- **1909–1932** — Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande publishes *Hindustani Sangeeta Paddhati*, standardizing notation and the thaat system.
- **1902** — Fred Gaisberg makes the first recordings of Indian music, featuring Gauhar Jan.

## Body

### History and Development
Hindustani music diverged from Carnatic music around the 12th century. Its core principles of raga and tala were refined in ancient texts like the *Natya Shastra* and *Dattilam*. During the medieval period, Persian musical ideas fused with local traditions under the Delhi Sultanate and Mughal courts, spurring new forms like qawwali and khyal. Composers like Amir Khusrau and Tansen were influential. After the Mughal era, music diversified into regional schools called gharanas, patronized by princely courts. In the early 20th century, reformers like Bhatkhande and Paluskar standardized the music, moved it from royal courts to public schools, and helped spread it to the masses.

### Core Principles and Structure
The music is based on a 12-note scale, but unlike Western music, the base frequency is not fixed. The melodic foundation is the raga, characterized by specific ascending and descending note sequences and emphasized notes. Rhythmic organization is based on the tala. A performance typically begins with an alap, a rhythmically free improvisation that introduces the raga, followed by a bandish or gat, a fixed composition set to rhythmic accompaniment. The system uses a movable-do solfege called Sargam (Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni).

### Major Vocal Forms
The principal classical vocal forms are dhrupad, khyal, and tarana. Dhrupad is an ancient, austere, and primarily devotional style. Khyal, meaning "imagination," is the modern, more ornate, and popular form that allows greater improvisational freedom. Tarana is a fast-paced, rhythmic form often performed at a concert's conclusion. Semi-classical forms like thumri, dadra, and ghazal prioritize lyrical expression over strict raga rules.

### Instruments
While vocal-centric, Hindustani music has a rich instrumental tradition. Key string instruments include the sitar and sarod (plucked), sarangi (bowed), and the tambura (drone). Important wind instruments are the bansuri (flute) and shehnai. The primary percussion instruments are the tabla and pakhavaj.

## Terms
- **Raga**: A melodic mode or framework for improvisation, defined by a specific set of notes, ascending/descending patterns, and characteristic phrases.
- **Tala**: A rhythmic cycle or pattern that provides the temporal framework for a performance.
- **Gharana**: A school or lineage of Hindustani classical music, each with its own distinct style and repertoire, traditionally passed down through a guru-shishya (teacher-student) relationship.
- **Alap**: The initial, rhythmically free improvisational section of a performance that introduces and explores the notes and mood of a raga.
- **Bandish/Gat**: A fixed, composed piece of music set to a specific raga and tala, serving as the basis for further improvisation.
- **Sargam**: The solmization system used in Indian music, with syllables Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni representing the seven notes of the scale.
- **Thaat**: A parent scale or mode, a classification system for ragas based on their constituent notes, standardized by Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande.
- **Dhrupad**: An ancient, formal, and devotional vocal style characterized by a slow, meditative alap and strict adherence to raga.
- **Khyal**: The dominant modern vocal form, meaning "imagination," known for its elaborate ornamentation and improvisational scope.

## Debates and Open Questions
The origin of the khyal vocal form is controversial; while sometimes attributed to Amir Khusrau, the record of his compositions does not support this, and the modern form is more closely associated with the court musician Sadarang. Bhatkhande's thaat system for classifying ragas, while useful, is described as "very flawed" and contains inconsistencies and ambiguities. The precise point of divergence between Hindustani and Carnatic music is debated, though the 13th-century *Sangita Ratnakara* is often cited as a marker.
