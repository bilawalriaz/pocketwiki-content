# Meroë

## Overview
Meroë was the southern capital of the Kingdom of Kush, an ancient African state in modern Sudan. It flourished from around 590 BC to the 4th century AD, becoming a major center of iron production, trade, and unique Nubian pyramid-building. Its history illustrates the rise and fall of a powerful Nile Valley civilization and its complex interactions with neighboring powers like Rome and Aksum.

## Timeline
- **c. 591 BC** — King Aspelta moves the Kushite capital from Napata to Meroë, possibly after the sack of Napata by Pharaoh Psamtik II.
- **c. 542–315 BC** — First Meroitic Period: Kings rule from Meroë, but the main Amun temple remains in Napata.
- **5th century BC** — Greek historian Herodotus describes Meroë as a "great city" and "mother city" of other Ethiopians.
- **c. 280 BC** — Royal burials transfer from Napata to Meroë under King Arakamani, beginning the Pyramids of Meroë.
- **23 BC** — Roman governor Publius Petronius invades Nubia, sacking Napata in retaliation for Meroitic raids.
- **22 BC** — Peace treaty signed between Rome (Augustus) and Meroë (Queen Amanirenas).
- **61 AD** — Emperor Nero's expedition reaches Meroë and proceeds up the White Nile until stopped by the Sudd swamps.
- **1st–4th century AD** — Fourth Meroitic Period: The kingdom's power declines.
- **c. 330 AD** — Inscriptions indicate the Aksumite king Ezana claims rule over regions including Meroë.
- **350 AD** — Meroë is destroyed by the Kingdom of Aksum.
- **2011** — Archaeological Sites of Meroë are listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Capital and Kingdom
Meroë served as the southern capital of the Kingdom of Kush (c. 800 BC – c. 350 AD), which was one of Africa's earliest and most advanced states. The capital was relocated from Napata to Meroë around 591 BC, possibly due to its strategic location on the fringe of the summer rainfall belt, rich iron ore deposits, access to hardwood for fuel, and trade routes to the Red Sea. The city became the seat of government and royal palace, while Napata retained religious importance for its Temple of Amun. The kingdom's rulers controlled the Nile Valley for over 1,000 km at its peak.

### Economy and Industry
Meroë's wealth was centered on a strong iron industry, earning it the nickname "the Birmingham of Africa" from Archibald Sayce, though the scale of production is debated. Metalworking likely used bloomeries and blast furnaces. The kingdom also traded ivory, slaves, skins, ostrich feathers, copper, ebony, cotton textiles, and jewelry. Pottery was another prominent industry, with fine wares holding social and ritual significance. The economy relied on centralized control of production and distribution, with power linked more to control of labor than territory.

### Society and Religion
The king was an autocratic ruler who shared authority with the Queen Mother, or *Kandake*. The administration included treasurers, seal bearers, and chief scribes. The Meroitic people worshipped both southern deities like the lion-god Apedemak and ancient Egyptian gods such as Amun, Isis, and Horus. The collapse of external trade is considered a prime cause of the kingdom's decline in the 3rd and 4th centuries AD.

### Conflict with Rome and Decline
Following Rome's conquest of Egypt, border skirmishes led to a Roman punitive expedition in 23 BC under Publius Petronius, who sacked Napata. A peace treaty was signed in 22 BC. Later, in 61 AD, a Roman expedition under Nero reached Meroë but was halted by the Sudd swamps. The kingdom began to fade in the 1st or 2nd century AD, weakened by war with Rome and the decline of traditional industries. It was ultimately destroyed by the Kingdom of Aksum around 350 AD.

### Language and Writing
The Meroitic language, attested from 300 BC and extinct by 400 AD, was written in two scripts: Meroitic Hieroglyphic for royal/religious documents and Meroitic Cursive for general records. The alphabetic script, derived from Egyptian hieroglyphs, replaced Egyptian script by the 3rd century BC. Its linguistic family is debated, with proposals linking it to either the Afro-Asiatic or Nilo-Saharan families.

### Archaeology and Legacy
The site was first described for Europeans by Frédéric Cailliaud in 1821. Major excavations by Giuseppe Ferlini (1834), C. R. Lepsius (1844), and E. A. Wallis Budge (1902, 1905) uncovered pyramids, temples, and artifacts. John Garstang's work (1910) revealed palace and temple ruins. The site features over 200 distinctive Nubian pyramids. In 2024, during the Sudanese civil war, airstrikes damaged the nearby UNESCO-listed sites of Naqa and Musawwarat es-Sufra.

## Terms
- **Kingdom of Kush**: An ancient African state in the Nile Valley, centered in modern Sudan, which lasted from c. 800 BC to c. 350 AD.
- **Napata**: The earlier northern capital of the Kingdom of Kush, home to the main Temple of Amun.
- **Kandake**: The title for the Queen Mother of Meroë, who shared authority with the king.
- **Meroitic script**: An alphabetic writing system developed in the 3rd century BC, derived from Egyptian hieroglyphs, used in two forms: hieroglyphic and cursive.
- **Bloomery**: A type of furnace once widely used for smelting iron from its oxides.
- **Sakia**: A water wheel used for irrigation to increase crop production.
- **Apedemak**: A lion-headed god worshipped in the Meroitic kingdom.
- **Sudd**: A vast swamp region on the White Nile in South Sudan that impeded ancient travel.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact reason for the capital's move from Napata to Meroë is debated, with factors including strategic security, economic resources, and trade access proposed.
- The scale and importance of Meroë's iron industry, and whether it was a major export center, is a matter of scholarly debate.
- The linguistic classification of the Meroitic language remains unresolved, with competing theories placing it in the Afro-Asiatic or Nilo-Saharan families.
- The primary cause of the kingdom's final collapse is debated: some emphasize the destructive campaign by Aksum around 350 AD, while others point to earlier economic and political decline around 300 AD.
