# Scientific method

## Overview
The scientific method is an empirical, iterative process for acquiring knowledge through observation, hypothesis formation, prediction, and experimental testing. Originating in ancient and medieval thought and formalized during the 17th-century Scientific Revolution, it remains central to scientific inquiry, though its exact form and universality have been debated since the 19th century.

## Timeline
- **c. 1027 CE** — Alhazen uses experimental method in *Book of Optics*
- **16th–17th c.** — Francis Bacon, Galileo, and Newton develop empiricism and inductivism
- **1687** — Newton publishes *Principia*, refining the scientific method
- **1910** — Dewey’s *How We Think* popularizes the method in education
- **1975** — Feyerabend publishes *Against Method*, challenging universal rules
- **1990s–2000s** — NGSS replaces singular method with broader scientific practices

## Body

### Historical Development
The scientific method evolved from ancient empiricist traditions. Early contributors included Aristotle, Alhazen, Avicenna, and Roger Bacon. During the Scientific Revolution, Francis Bacon championed empirical observation, while René Descartes emphasized rational deduction. Isaac Newton synthesized these approaches, establishing principles still foundational to modern science. The term "scientific method" emerged in the 19th century amid institutional growth and debates over induction, realism, and demarcation between science and pseudoscience.

### Core Elements of Inquiry
The method involves iterative cycles of characterization (observation), hypothesis formation, prediction, and experimentation. These steps are not rigidly sequential; not all occur in every inquiry. Peer review ensures scrutiny of each stage. The process builds cumulative knowledge, refining models over time.

### Hypothesis and Prediction
Hypotheses must be falsifiable—capable of being disproven by evidence. Predictions derived from hypotheses guide experiments. For example, Watson and Crick predicted DNA’s helical structure would produce X-shaped diffraction patterns, confirmed by Franklin’s Photo 51.

### Experimentation and Validation
Experiments test predictions under controlled conditions. Replication by independent researchers validates findings. Large-scale projects like CERN’s LHC require rigorous documentation and data sharing. Historical examples include Galileo’s controlled observations and Alhazen’s optical experiments.

### Iteration and Refinement
Scientific inquiry is cyclical. Failed hypotheses prompt re-evaluation of questions or methods. Successive refinements lead to deeper understanding. Newton’s laws were later subsumed by Einstein’s relativity, illustrating how theories evolve rather than collapse entirely.

### Philosophical Foundations
Methodological naturalism assumes an objective, consistent reality knowable through reason and observation. Logical positivism, falsificationism (Popper), and pragmatism (Peirce) offer competing frameworks. Popper emphasized falsifiability as the criterion for scientific validity.

### Modern Critiques and Pluralism
By the mid-20th century, philosophers like Kuhn and Feyerabend challenged the idea of a single, universal method. Kuhn introduced paradigms and paradigm shifts; Feyerabend argued for epistemological anarchism ("anything goes"). Scientific pluralism recognizes diverse methods across disciplines.

### Role of Statistics and Uncertainty
Statistical inference quantifies uncertainty and tests hypotheses. However, misuse of p-values and small sample sizes contribute to irreproducible results. Ioannidis (2005) argued many published findings are false due to methodological flaws and publication bias.

### Education and Public Understanding
Traditional education taught a linear version of the method. Recent reforms (e.g., NGSS) emphasize scientific practices, crosscutting concepts, and core ideas over rote procedure. Critics argue oversimplification fosters misconceptions about how science operates.

### Sociology of Scientific Knowledge
Sociologists examine how social structures influence scientific practice. Fleck’s “thought collectives” and Latour’s “epistemic cultures” highlight discipline-specific norms. Theory-ladenness of observation (Hanson) shows how conceptual frameworks shape perception.

## Terms
- **Falsifiability**: Criterion that a hypothesis must be testable and potentially disprovable.
- **Inductive reasoning**: Drawing general conclusions from specific observations.
- **Deductive reasoning**: Deriving specific predictions from general principles.
- **Peer review**: Anonymous expert evaluation of research before publication.
- **Replication**: Repeating experiments to verify results.
- **Parsimony**: Principle favoring simpler explanations when equally valid.
- **Theory-ladenness**: Concept that observations are influenced by existing theories.
- **Epistemic culture**: Shared practices and assumptions within a scientific community.
- **Methodological naturalism**: Assumption that natural causes explain phenomena without supernatural intervention.
- **Scientific revolution**: Period (16th–17th c.) marking emergence of modern empirical science.

## Debates and Open Questions
Key debates include whether a single scientific method exists or if methods vary by discipline (pluralism vs. unificationism). The role of chance in discovery, the reliability of statistical inference, and tensions between postmodern critiques and scientific realism remain contested. Whether the scientific method is a myth or idealization continues to be discussed, especially in education and policy contexts.
