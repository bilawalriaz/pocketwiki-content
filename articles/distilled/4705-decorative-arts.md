# Decorative arts

## Overview
Decorative arts are crafts focused on designing and creating objects that are both beautiful and functional, such as furniture, ceramics, and textiles. They are often distinguished from "fine arts" (like painting and sculpture), which are created primarily for aesthetic contemplation. This distinction, which emerged in Renaissance Europe, has been challenged over time, leading to a greater appreciation for decorative arts and their role in culture and identity.

## Timeline
- **c. 14th-17th century** — Renaissance theorists like Vasari promote fine arts over decorative arts, valuing artistic imagination over material cost or technical virtuosity.
- **19th century (2nd half)** — The Arts and Crafts movement begins in England, challenging the hierarchy between fine and decorative arts.
- **1882** — Arthur Heygate Mackmurdo organizes the Century Guild, championing no meaningful difference between fine and decorative arts.
- **1911** — The UK Copyright Act extends protection to works of "artistic craftsmanship," reflecting the movement's influence.
- **1970s** — Writers like Amy Goldin and Anne Swartz formally challenge the view of decoration as a 'lesser art'.
- **1980s** — The Pattern and Decoration movement in New York galleries promotes a more inclusive evaluation of decorative art.

## Body

### Distinction from Fine Arts
The separation between decorative and fine arts is largely a post-Renaissance Western concept. In many other cultures and periods, this distinction is not meaningful. For example, Islamic art often consists entirely of decorative arts, and in early Medieval Europe, the most prestigious works were in goldsmithing or ivory carving, not wall painting. The status of certain media has also shifted; mosaics and tapestries were once considered the most magnificent artforms but are now often categorized as decorative.

### Influence of Materials and History
Modern understanding of art history is distorted by the privileging of fine art media and differing survival rates. Works in precious metals were often melted down for wealth, while illuminated manuscripts survived better due to church preservation. Renaissance thought, which valued the individual master's imagination over expensive materials, cemented the fine arts' higher status. This contrasts with Medieval values and with Chinese traditions, where both literati ink wash painting (valuing imagination) and industrial-scale ceramics (valuing different artistic values) co-existed for centuries.

### Arts and Crafts Movement and Modern Status
The Arts and Crafts movement of the late 19th century began to narrow the status gap between decorative and fine arts. This led to legal changes, such as the 1911 UK Copyright Act protecting "artistic craftsmanship." In the context of modern mass production, individuals often customize identical objects through decorative techniques (like decoupage or IKEA hacking) to construct personal identity and lifestyle.

## Terms
- **Decorative arts**: Arts or crafts whose aim is to design and manufacture objects that are both beautiful and functional.
- **Fine arts**: Arts, such as painting and sculpture, that produce objects solely for their aesthetic quality and capacity to stimulate the intellect.
- **Applied arts**: A category largely overlapping with decorative arts, often placed under the modern umbrella of "design."
- **Ars sacra**: A term sometimes used for medieval Christian art executed in valuable materials like metal, ivory, and textiles.
- **Arts and Crafts movement**: A 19th-century aesthetic movement that championed craftsmanship and challenged the hierarchy between fine and decorative arts.
- **Pattern and Decoration movement**: A short-lived 1980s art movement that promoted a more inclusive evaluation of decorative art objects.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source highlights a central, ongoing debate: the validity and usefulness of the distinction between decorative and fine arts. This hierarchy, formalized in the Renaissance, has been challenged by post-modernist thought and movements like Arts and Crafts and Pattern and Decoration, which argue for a more inclusive evaluation of artistic value across different media and traditions.
