# 1931 China floods

## Overview
The 1931 Yangtze–Huai River floods were a series of catastrophic floods in China from June to August 1931, caused by extreme weather following a multi-year drought. They inundated a vast area, destroyed cities and farmland, and caused a massive humanitarian crisis with a death toll that remains debated, making it one of the deadliest natural disasters in recorded history.

## Timeline
- **1928–1931** — A long drought afflicts China.
- **Winter 1930–31** — A harsh winter deposits heavy snow and ice in mountains.
- **Early 1931** — Melting snow and heavy spring rains cause continuous flooding in the Yangtze's middle course.
- **July 1931** — Nine cyclones hit the region (average is two per year), causing extreme rainfall.
- **19 August 1931** — Water levels at Hankou, Wuhan, reach a record 16 m above average.
- **27 July 1931** — A catastrophic dike failure inundates most of Wuhan.
- **25 August 1931** — Dikes breach along Lake Gaoyou, causing massive fatalities.
- **May 1932** — A subsequent cholera epidemic officially reports 31,974 deaths.
- **1953** — Mao Zedong promotes the Three Gorges Dam project for flood control.
- **2012** — The Three Gorges Dam begins full operation.

## Body

### Meteorological Causes and Physical Consequences
The floods resulted from a sequence of extreme weather. A drought from 1928 to 1931 was followed by a severe winter that left heavy snowpack. In early 1931, rapid snowmelt combined with heavy spring rains created a single, continuous deluge instead of the usual three high-water periods. The summer brought unprecedented cyclonic activity, with nine cyclones in July alone, leading to record rainfall. The Yangtze River reached its highest level since records began in the mid-19th century. The floods inundated approximately 180,000 square kilometers, affecting eight core provinces and areas as far as Guangdong, Manchuria, and Sichuan.

### Death Toll and Damage
The death toll is widely disputed. A contemporary survey by John Lossing Buck found at least 150,000 drowned in the first 100 days, with total fatalities possibly exceeding 600,000. Chinese historians later calculated 422,420 deaths, while some Western sources cite 3.7 to 4 million. The government estimated 25 million people were affected, though historians suggest it may have been 53 million. The floods destroyed about 15% of the Yangtze Valley's wheat and rice crops, caused severe economic shock, and led to widespread famine, with reports of people eating tree bark, weeds, and earth. Diseases like cholera, malaria, and dysentery swept through displaced populations. Major cities like Wuhan and Nanjing were severely damaged; in Wuhan, a dike failure left nearly 800,000 homeless, and in Gaoyou County, 18,000 drowned with 58,000 dying later from famine and disease.

### Government Reactions
The Kuomintang government established the National Flood Relief Commission, led by T.V. Soong, which employed Chinese and foreign experts and secured League of Nations assistance. Relief efforts were hampered by the Japanese invasion of Manchuria in 1931, which collapsed the bond market, though a U.S. wheat loan was secured. Post-disaster, commissions like the Huai River Conservancy were formed but lacked funding due to subsequent wars. Superstition played a role; the pre-flood demolition of a Dragon King Temple was blamed for the disaster, leading officials to perform ritual ceremonies. After the Communist victory in 1949, Mao Zedong championed the Three Gorges Dam as a monumental flood control project, though it was not completed until 2012 due to political and resource challenges.

## Terms
- **Dike**: An embankment built to prevent the overflow of a river.
- **Cyclonic activity**: The occurrence of cyclones, which are large-scale weather systems with low-pressure centers that bring strong winds and heavy rain.
- **Schistosomiasis**: A disease caused by parasitic worms released by freshwater snails, often spread in flood conditions.
- **National Flood Relief Commission**: The government body established in 1931 to coordinate disaster response.
- **Three Gorges Dam**: A massive hydroelectric dam on the Yangtze River, promoted as a long-term flood control solution.
- **Meiyu**: The East Asian rainy season, which was abnormally heavy in 1931.

## Debates and Open Questions
The exact death toll remains highly contested, with estimates ranging from hundreds of thousands to several million, reflecting challenges in historical data and the indirect effects of famine and disease. The long-term impact of the disaster on Chinese politics and society, and its role in shaping later infrastructure projects like the Three Gorges Dam, is also a subject of historical analysis.
