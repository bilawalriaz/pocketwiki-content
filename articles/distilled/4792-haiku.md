# Haiku

## Overview
Haiku is a form of short-form poetry originating in Japan, traditionally consisting of three phrases with a 5-7-5 pattern of sound units (*on*), a seasonal reference (*kigo*), and a "cutting word" (*kireji*). It began as the opening stanza (*hokku*) of collaborative linked verse (*renga*) before evolving into a standalone art form. Today, haiku is written globally, with diverse adaptations that often diverge from the traditional Japanese rules.

## Timeline
- **17th century** — Matsuo Bashō elevates *hokku* and incorporates it into prose works (*haibun*).
- **1644–1694** — Life of Matsuo Bashō, a foundational haiku master.
- **1716–1784** — Life of Yosa Buson, known for his painterly haiku style.
- **1763–1827** — Life of Kobayashi Issa, noted for his humanistic and accessible haiku.
- **1867–1902** — Life of Masaoka Shiki, who modernized and renamed standalone *hokku* as "haiku."
- **Late 19th century** — Masaoka Shiki formally separates haiku from collaborative poetry and coins the modern term.
- **Early 20th century** — Haiku begins to influence Western poets, such as the Imagists.
- **1949–1952** — R. H. Blyth publishes his four-volume *Haiku*, introducing the form to the post-war English-speaking world.
- **1957** — Kenneth Yasuda publishes *The Japanese Haiku*, presenting a critical theory for English haiku.
- **1958** — Harold G. Henderson publishes *An Introduction to Haiku*.
- **1998** — Posthumous publication of Richard Wright's haiku collection *Haiku: This Other World*.
- **2025** — Academic study highlights haiku's global use for social commentary and critique.

## Body

### Traditional Elements
Traditional Japanese haiku is defined by three elements. First is the *kireji* ("cutting word"), which creates a pause or juxtaposition, structurally supporting the poem as an independent work. Second is the *on*, a sound unit distinct from a syllable; traditional haiku uses 17 *on* in a 5-7-5 pattern, though classical masters like Bashō sometimes deviated. Third is the *kigo* ("season word"), drawn from a prescribed list (*saijiki*) to evoke a specific time of year. Non-Japanese haiku often adapt or omit these elements.

### Origin and Development
Haiku originated as the *hokku*, the opening stanza of collaborative linked verse (*renga*). By the time of Matsuo Bashō in the 17th century, *hokku* began appearing as standalone poems. In the late 19th century, Masaoka Shiki formally renamed the standalone form "haiku," separating it from its collaborative context and advocating for a style of "sketching from life" (*shasei*). This modernization dealt a severe blow to traditional *renku* schools.

### Key Masters and Styles
Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694) raised *hokku* from a playful game to sublime poetry and was later deified. Yosa Buson (1716–1784) followed with a painterly style, while Kobayashi Issa (1763–1827) offered a humanistic, accessible approach. Masaoka Shiki (1867–1902) was the key modernizer who established the contemporary form and term. A minority modern Japanese movement (*gendai-haiku*), supported by Ogiwara Seisensui, has also varied from the 17-*on* tradition.

### Global Spread and Adaptation
Haiku was introduced to the West in the early 20th century, influencing movements like Imagism. Key figures in its English-language dissemination include R. H. Blyth, Kenneth Yasuda, and Harold G. Henderson. The form has since been adopted worldwide, with notable traditions in French, Bengali, Italian, Spanish, and Southeastern European languages. English-language haiku often uses three lines and varies in syllable count, with some scholars like Yasuda proposing a "haiku moment" as its core motive.

### Related Forms
Haiku is related to other Japanese art forms. *Haibun* combines prose and haiku, as in Bashō's travel journals. *Haiga* integrates haiku with painting or other visual art. *Kuhi* are poem monuments carved with famous haiku.

## Terms
- **Haiku**: A short-form Japanese poem, traditionally 17 *on* in a 5-7-5 pattern, containing a *kigo* and often a *kireji*.
- **Hokku**: The opening stanza of a *renga* or *renku*; the precursor to standalone haiku.
- **On (mora)**: A Japanese sound unit distinct from a syllable; one *on* for a short vowel, two for a long vowel or doubled consonant.
- **Kireji ("cutting word")**: A word that creates a pause or juxtaposition in a haiku, analogous to a caesura.
- **Kigo ("season word")**: A word or phrase that implies the season, drawn from a *saijiki*.
- **Renga**: A form of collaborative linked verse poetry.
- **Shasei ("sketching from life")**: A style of haiku advocated by Shiki that focuses on direct, realistic depiction of nature.
- **Haibun**: A Japanese literary form combining prose and haiku.
- **Haiga**: A Japanese art form combining haiku with painting or other visual art.
- **Gendai-haiku**: A modern Japanese haiku movement that varies from traditional 17-*on* structure and nature themes.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The appropriate syllable count for haiku in English is debated; the traditional 5-7-5 pattern is often considered too long, with some suggesting around 12 syllables is a closer equivalent to 17 Japanese *on*.
- The necessity of a *kigo* (season word) in non-Japanese or modern haiku is contested, as many contemporary poets omit it.
- The role and translation of the *kireji* (cutting word) into languages without a direct equivalent remains a challenge for poets and translators.
