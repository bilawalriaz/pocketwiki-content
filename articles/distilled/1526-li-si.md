# Li Si

## Overview
Li Si (c. 280–208 BC) was the chief architect of the Qin dynasty’s centralized bureaucracy, serving as Chancellor under Qin Shi Huang and his successor Qin Er Shi. A student of the Confucian thinker Xunzi, he adopted Legalist administrative techniques—drawing on Shen Buhai’s methods, Han Fei’s theory, and Shang Yang’s laws—to unify China’s laws, weights, measures, currency, axle widths, and the written script (small seal). He authored the 213 BC edict ordering the destruction of private historical and philosophical texts, an event later paired with the alleged burying alive of 460 scholars. After Qin Shi Huang’s death, Li Si conspired with the eunuch Zhao Gao to forge a succession edict installing Qin Er Shi, but Zhao Gao later turned on him; Li Si was tortured, executed by waist chop, and his clan exterminated to the third degree.

## Timeline
- **c. 280 BC** — Born in Cai, state of Chu.

- **246 BC** — Appointed Chancellor of Qin by King Zheng (later Qin Shi Huang).
- **237 BC** — Persuades King Zheng to rescind expulsion order against foreigners; urges annexation of Han.

- **230 BC** — State of Han conquered.

- **210 BC** — Qin Shi Huang dies; Li Si and Zhao Gao suppress will naming Fusu heir, install Qin Er Shi.
- **208 BC** — Zhao Gao frames Li Si for treason; Li Si executed by waist chop; clan exterminated to third degree.

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Li Si was born in Cai, a city in the southern state of Chu. As a minor clerk, he observed that rats in a privy were starving while rats in a granary thrived, concluding that human worth, like a rat’s condition, depended on circumstance rather than fixed morality. Determined to escape obscurity, he studied under the Confucian philosopher Xunzi, whose emphasis on ritual and human artifice may have shaped Li Si’s later bureaucratic focus. Finding no advancement in Chu, he migrated to Qin, the most powerful Warring State, to pursue a political career.

### Rise in Qin and Elimination of Rivals
In Qin, Li Si became a retainer of Chancellor Lü Buwei and gained access to King Zheng. He argued that Qin could not unify China if the other six states allied against it; Zheng adopted his strategy of bribing foreign intellectuals and assassinating key figures in rival courts. In 237 BC, a court faction urged the expulsion of all foreigners. Li Si submitted a famous memorial listing Qin’s gains from foreign talent—including “the sultry girls of Zhao”—and retained his position. That same year he advocated attacking Han to intimidate the remaining states. When Han Fei, a Han aristocrat and former classmate, arrived as an envoy, Li Si—envying his intellect—convinced Zheng that Han Fei could neither be released nor trusted. Han Fei was imprisoned and forced to commit suicide in 233 BC; Han fell in 230 BC.

### Imperial Standardization and Thought Control
After Zheng declared himself Qin Shi Huang (First Emperor) in 221 BC, Li Si directed the empire-wide standardization of laws, ordinances, weights, measures, chariot axle widths, and the written script. He promulgated the small seal script as the sole legal standard, proscribing variant graphs within Qin and scripts from conquered regions, a move that culturally unified China for millennia. He also authored the *Cangjiepian*, the earliest surviving Chinese primer. Li Si abolished hereditary enfeoffment for imperial relatives, staffing government solely by merit, and melted the weapons of defeated states into bells and statues. He softened the harshest punishments of Shang Yang’s code and lowered taxes.

Believing that political texts in private hands fueled dissent, Li Si drafted the 213 BC edict ordering the destruction of all historical records except the Qin annals, and all philosophical works except those on medicine, agriculture, and divination. Private schools teaching the classics were banned; only state academies could train officials. Tradition holds that 460 Confucian scholars were subsequently buried alive, though the source notes this as a common belief rather than a confirmed fact.

### Coup, Betrayal, and Death
When Qin Shi Huang died in 210 BC during an inspection tour, Li Si and the chief eunuch Zhao Gao concealed the death. Fearing that the designated heir Fusu—allied with general Meng Tian—would replace him, Li Si joined Zhao Gao in forging an edict ordering Fusu’s suicide and installing the younger son Huhai as Qin Er Shi. Once enthroned, Qin Er Shi filled the court with Zhao Gao’s clients. Zhao Gao then accused Li Si of treason; the emperor, regarding Zhao Gao as a teacher, accepted the charge. Li Si was tortured into confession, his appeal to the throne intercepted, and in 208 BC subjected to the Five Punishments (mutilating penalties) before being executed by waist chop in a public market. His entire clan was exterminated to the third degree. Sima Qian records his final words to his son: a wish to walk once more with their brown dog through the eastern gate of Shang Cai chasing a hare.

## Terms
- **Legalism (Fajia)**: A Warring States school emphasizing impersonal law, administrative technique, and state power; Li Si synthesized its major strands.
- **Small seal script (Xiaozhuan)**: Standardized Qin calligraphic form mandated by Li Si as the empire’s sole legal script, suppressing regional variants.
- **Five Punishments (Wuxing)**: A series of mutilating corporal penalties (tattooing, nose-cutting, foot-amputation, castration, death) used in Qin law; applied to Li Si before execution.
- **Third-degree extermination (San zu)**: Collective punishment extending execution to the convict’s parents, siblings, spouse, and children.
- **Cangjiepian**: Early character primer compiled by Li Si to teach the standardized script; fragments survive.
- **Shen Buhai**: Statesman of Han whose administrative technique (*shu*) Li Si admired and employed.
- **Han Fei**: Legalist philosopher, former classmate of Li Si; imprisoned and forced to suicide by Li Si in 233 BC.
- **Shang Yang**: Qin reformer whose harsh penal code Li Si followed for law but later softened.
- **Xunzi**: Confucian philosopher (c. 310–235 BC) under whom Li Si studied; emphasized ritual and human effort.
- **Qin Er Shi (Huhai)**: Second Qin emperor (r. 210–207 BC), installed by Li Si and Zhao Gao; later turned on Li Si.

## Debates and open questions
- **Extent of the “burning of books and burying of scholars”**: The source notes the burial of 460 scholars as “commonly thought” rather than established fact; the scale and intentionality remain debated.
- **Li Si’s intellectual allegiance**: He studied under Confucian Xunzi but implemented Legalist policies; scholars dispute whether he “converted” or pragmatically fused traditions.
- **Authorship of the “Preserving Han” memorial**: The *Han Feizi* attributes this text to Li Si, but attribution is uncertain.
- **Role in the succession coup**: The source presents Li Si as a willing co-conspirator with Zhao Gao; some historians argue he was coerced or acted to preserve stability.
- **Effectiveness of standardization**: While credited with cultural unity, the speed and coercion of script reform may have caused administrative disruption not fully assessed in the source.
