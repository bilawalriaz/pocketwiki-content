# Lyre

## Overview
The lyre is an ancient stringed instrument classified as a yoke lute, where strings are attached to a crossbar (yoke) connected to two arms rising from a soundbox. It originated in the Fertile Crescent around 3000 BCE and was central to the musical cultures of ancient Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Greece, evolving into distinct eastern (flat-based) and western (round-based) forms before spreading across Europe and the Middle East.

## Timeline
- **c. 2700 BCE** — Earliest known lyre depictions appear in Mesopotamia.
- **c. 2500 BCE** — The Lyres of Ur (bull lyres) are crafted; the oldest surviving stringed instruments.
- **c. 1750 BCE** — The round-based (western) lyre dies out in the eastern Mediterranean.
- **c. 1700–1400 BCE** — The round-based lyre reappears in ancient Greece.
- **c. 1400 BCE** — The earliest picture of a Greek lyre appears on the Hagia Triada sarcophagus.
- **c. 1353–1336 BCE** — Giant lyres reach peak popularity in Egypt under Pharaoh Akhenaten.
- **c. 330 BCE** — Hellenistic period blurs the geographic divide between eastern and western lyre types.
- **1st millennium CE** — Germanic lyres (e.g., Sutton Hoo) appear in northern European warrior graves.
- **2001** — A well-preserved Germanic lyre is recovered from a grave in Trossingen, Germany.

## Body

### Classification and Etymology
The lyre is a chordophone (stringed instrument) classified in the Hornbostel–Sachs system as a lute, specifically a yoke lute. The earliest reference to the word is the Mycenaean Greek *ru-ra-ta-e* ("lyrists"). In classical Greek, "lyre" could refer specifically to an amateur instrument or generally to the family including the professional *cithara*.

### Ancient Eastern Lyres
The oldest lyres, originating in the Fertile Crescent, are the flat-based eastern lyres. They are divided into four main types: **bull lyres** (with a bull's head, e.g., Lyres of Ur), **thick lyres** (with a thicker soundbox and box-bridge), **thin lyres** (with an open base, played with a plectrum), and **giant lyres** (immense instruments often requiring two players). The *kinnor*, an ancient Israelite instrument, is thought to be a type of thin lyre.

### Ancient Western Lyres
The round-based western lyre also originated in Syria/Anatolia but died out there by c. 1750 BCE. It reappeared in Greece, where it was the sole lyre form from 1400–700 BCE. Greek sub-types included the *phorminx*, *kitharis* (which evolved into the concert kithara), and the *barbitos*. By the Hellenistic period, both flat- and round-based lyres were found across the Mediterranean.

### Construction and Use in Greece
A classical Greek lyre had a soundbox (often a turtle shell), two arms, and a crossbar yoke. Strings, made of gut, were stretched between the yoke and a bridge. It was played by strumming with a plectrum (pick) held in the right hand, while the left hand's fingers silenced unwanted strings. The number of strings varied (commonly 3, 4, 7, or 8), with a tetrachord (four-note series) as a foundational tuning unit.

### European and Bowed Lyres
Lyres with distinct forms, like the Germanic lyre, developed in central and northern Europe from the Iron Age onward. Later, the bow was applied to smaller lyres, creating bowed lyres such as the Scandinavian *talharpa*, Finnish *jouhikko*, and Welsh *crwth*. These are not considered ancestors of modern orchestral bowed instruments.

## Terms
- **Yoke lute**: A lute where strings are attached to a yoke (crossbar and arms) in the same plane as the sound table.
- **Chordophone**: A stringed instrument that produces sound via vibrating strings.
- **Hornbostel–Sachs**: A system for classifying musical instruments, dividing them into categories like chordophones.
- **Plectrum**: A pick used to strum or strike the strings of an instrument.
- **Tetrachord**: A series of four notes filling the interval of a perfect fourth, a basic unit of ancient Greek musical theory.
- **Soundbox/resonator**: The hollow body of the instrument that amplifies the sound of the vibrating strings.
- **Kithara**: A professional, larger version of the Greek lyre, often with a flat base.
- **Kinnor**: An ancient Israelite instrument, likely a type of thin lyre, mentioned in the Old Testament.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact identification of the biblical *kinnor* is unclear, though it is generally translated as "lyre" or "harp."
- The precise number of strings on the earliest Greek lyres is debated, as ancient vase paintings may not be accurate.
- The evolutionary details and dates of origin for European bowed lyres are disputed among scholars.
- The cultural pathways for the lyre's introduction into Greece (from Thrace, Lydia, or Egypt) are a matter of scholarly hypothesis.
