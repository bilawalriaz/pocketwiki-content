# Gabon

## Overview
Gabon is a resource-rich equatorial country on Central Africa's Atlantic coast. Its modern history is defined by French colonization, a long post-independence dynasty under the Bongo family, and an economy heavily dependent on oil and manganese. Despite high per capita income, wealth is unevenly distributed, and the country has faced political instability, culminating in a 2023 military coup.

## Body

### History and Politics
Gabon's original inhabitants were the Bambenga, later joined by Bantu migrants. European contact began in the 15th century, leading to trade in ivory and enslaved people. France colonized the region in the late 19th century, ruling it as part of French Equatorial Africa until independence in 1960.

The first president, Léon M'ba, established an authoritarian one-party state. After his death in 1967, Vice President Omar Bongo succeeded him, ruling for 42 years. Bongo created the Gabonese Democratic Party (PDG) as the sole legal party. In 1990, economic discontent forced political reforms, leading to a multi-party system and a 1991 constitution. Despite this, the PDG remained dominant. Bongo's son, Ali Bongo, won a disputed election in 2009 after his father's death. In August 2023, following another contested election, the military overthrew Ali Bongo, ending the Bongo dynasty's 55-year rule. General Brice Oligui Nguema was named transitional leader and won a presidential election in 2025.

### Economy and Resources
Gabon's economy is dominated by petroleum, which accounts for roughly 46% of government revenue and 81% of exports. It is also the world's second-largest producer of manganese. This reliance has created a high per capita income but extreme inequality, with about a third of the population living in poverty. The government has pursued reforms with the IMF and launched the "Gabon Emergent" program to diversify the economy away from oil. In 2025, it announced a ban on raw manganese ore exports, effective 2029, to encourage domestic processing.

### Geography and Environment
Located on the equator, Gabon is 89.3% forested, with coastal plains, central mountains, and eastern savanna. Its largest river is the Ogooué. The country is a global leader in conservation, with 13 national parks covering about 10% of its territory. It is considered the most carbon-positive country in the world due to its vast forests acting as a net carbon sink. However, it is vulnerable to climate change, with rising sea levels threatening coastal areas.

### Demographics and Society
Gabon has a population of about 2.3 million, making it one of Africa's least densely populated countries. It is highly urbanized, with 92% living in cities, primarily Libreville and Port-Gentil. French is the official language, spoken by a majority. The population is over 95% Bantu, with Christianity the predominant religion (85%). Health indicators are mixed; while life expectancy is relatively high for the region, HIV/AIDS prevalence is significant.

## Terms
- **Bantu**: A large group of ethnic and linguistic groups in central, eastern, and southern Africa.
- **Indigénat**: A colonial legal code that assigned inferior status and forced labor to indigenous populations.
- **Corvée**: Unpaid, forced labor, often imposed by colonial authorities.
- **Thalassocracy**: A state with primary power derived from its control of the sea.
- **Françafrique**: A term for the often neocolonial relationship between France and its former African colonies.
- **CFA franc**: A currency used by 14 African countries, historically pegged to the French franc.
- **Oklo reactor**: A natural nuclear fission reactor in Gabon, active about 2 billion years ago.
- **Mvett**: A traditional stringed instrument and epic narrative form of the Fang people.
- **Nyembwe**: A palm nut sauce central to Gabonese cuisine, especially in the national dish *Poulet Nyembwe*.

## Debates and open questions
- The fairness of elections under both Omar and Ali Bongo was frequently disputed by opposition groups and some international observers, with allegations of fraud and irregularities.
- The long-term success of the "Gabon Emergent" program to diversify the economy away from oil is uncertain, given persistent challenges like poor infrastructure and a small domestic market.
- The political stability of the post-2023 coup transitional government and its promised return to democracy remain open questions.
