# Franklin D. Roosevelt

## Overview
Franklin Delano Roosevelt (1882–1945) was the 32nd U.S. president, serving an unprecedented four terms from 1933 until his death. He is a defining figure of the 20th century, leading the nation through the Great Depression with his New Deal programs and through most of World War II. His presidency fundamentally expanded the role of the federal government in the economy and society, and he helped establish the post-war international order.

## Timeline
- **Jan 30, 1882** — Born in Hyde Park, New York.
- **1910–1913** — Serves in the New York State Senate.
- **1913–1920** — Serves as Assistant Secretary of the Navy.
- **1920** — Runs for Vice President on the losing Democratic ticket.
- **Aug 1921** — Contracts polio, resulting in permanent paralysis from the waist down.
- **1928–1932** — Serves as Governor of New York.
- **Nov 1932** — Elected President in a landslide over Herbert Hoover.
- **Mar 1933** — Begins the "First Hundred Days," launching the New Deal.
- **1935** — Signs the Social Security Act.
- **Nov 1936** — Wins re-election in one of the largest landslides in U.S. history.
- **Sep 1939** — Begins secret correspondence with Winston Churchill as WWII begins in Europe.
- **Dec 7, 1941** — Japan attacks Pearl Harbor; the U.S. enters World War II.
- **Nov 1944** — Wins an unprecedented fourth presidential election.
- **Apr 12, 1945** — Dies of a cerebral hemorrhage in Warm Springs, Georgia.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Born into wealth, Roosevelt was educated at Groton, Harvard, and Columbia Law. He entered politics as a Democrat, serving in the New York State Senate and as Assistant Secretary of the Navy under Woodrow Wilson during WWI. In 1921, he was paralyzed by polio (later possibly diagnosed as Guillain–Barré syndrome), but with his wife Eleanor's encouragement, he returned to public life, becoming Governor of New York in 1928. As governor, he pioneered relief programs to combat the Great Depression.

### The New Deal (1933–1938)
Elected in 1932, Roosevelt launched the New Deal, a series of programs, public works projects, and regulations aimed at "relief, recovery, and reform." The First New Deal (1933–34) focused on immediate relief and recovery, creating agencies like the Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC) and the Agricultural Adjustment Administration (AAA). It also established key financial reforms like the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) and the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). The Second New Deal (1935–36) emphasized social welfare and labor rights, most notably with the Social Security Act and the National Labor Relations Act. These programs created the "New Deal coalition," a political alliance that realigned American politics. His 1937 attempt to expand the Supreme Court failed, and a conservative coalition in Congress subsequently blocked further major reforms.

### World War II Leadership
Roosevelt navigated U.S. foreign policy from isolationism to intervention. He implemented the "Europe first" strategy after the attack on Pearl Harbor, mobilized the American economy for war production, and worked closely with Allied leaders Churchill and Stalin. He was instrumental in planning the post-war world, advocating for the United Nations and the Bretton Woods system. His administration also initiated the Manhattan Project to develop the atomic bomb. Domestically, the war effort ended the Depression, but his legacy is also marked by the controversial internment of Japanese Americans under Executive Order 9066.

### Legacy and Controversy
Roosevelt is consistently ranked among the greatest U.S. presidents for his leadership during two major crises. He permanently expanded the power of the presidency and the federal government. However, his legacy includes significant criticism: the internment of Japanese Americans, his administration's limited response to the Holocaust, and his failure to pass federal anti-lynching legislation. His breaking of the two-term tradition led to the 22nd Amendment, which formally limited presidents to two terms.

## Terms
- **New Deal:** The series of programs, public works projects, financial reforms, and regulations enacted by President Roosevelt between 1933 and 1939 in response to the Great Depression.
- **Fireside Chats:** Informal radio addresses given by Roosevelt to the American public, used to explain his policies and build support.
- **Fifth Party System:** The era of American political history (beginning in the 1930s) dominated by the Democratic Party and the New Deal coalition.
- **New Deal Coalition:** The alignment of voting blocs that supported the Democratic Party from the 1930s to the 1960s, including labor unions, minorities, farmers, and Southern whites.
- **Social Security Act:** A 1935 law that established a system of old-age benefits for workers, benefits for victims of industrial accidents, unemployment insurance, and aid for dependent mothers and children.
- **Good Neighbor Policy:** A foreign policy principle of non-intervention and non-interference in the domestic affairs of Latin American countries, promoted by Roosevelt.
- **Lend-Lease:** A program under which the U.S. supplied Allied nations with war materials during WWII without immediate payment.
- **Manhattan Project:** The research and development undertaking during WWII that produced the first nuclear weapons.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Effectiveness of the New Deal:** Historians debate whether the New Deal effectively ended the Great Depression or if it was ultimately the massive government spending of World War II that restored full employment. Some argue it did not go far enough, while others contend it overextended federal power.
- **Roosevelt and the Holocaust:** Scholars continue to assess Roosevelt's response to the Nazi genocide. Critics argue he could have done more to admit Jewish refugees and bomb concentration camps, while defenders note the political constraints of isolationism and the priority of winning the war.
- **Yalta Conference:** Roosevelt's role at the 1945 Yalta Conference remains controversial. Critics charge he naively conceded Eastern Europe to Soviet domination, while supporters argue he secured vital Soviet cooperation for the war's end and the United Nations, with little leverage to do otherwise.
- **Health and Decision-Making:** The extent to which Roosevelt's severe health decline in 1944–45 impaired his judgment and affected critical wartime and post-war decisions is a subject of ongoing historical analysis.
