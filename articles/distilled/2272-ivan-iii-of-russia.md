# Ivan III of Russia

## Overview
Ivan III Vasilyevich, known as Ivan the Great, was Grand Prince of Moscow from 1462 to 1505. He is a pivotal figure in Russian history for transforming the principality of Moscow into a centralized, sovereign state. He achieved this by vastly expanding its territory, ending Mongol-Tatar dominance, adopting imperial symbolism and titles, and laying the foundations for autocratic rule.

## Timeline
- **1440** — Ivan III is born.
- **1462** — Ivan III becomes Grand Prince of Moscow.
- **1471** — Muscovite victory at the Battle of Shelon against Novgorod.
- **1472** — Ivan marries Sophia Palaiologina, a Byzantine princess.
- **1478** — The Republic of Novgorod is formally annexed by Moscow.
- **1480** — The Great Stand on the Ugra River ends the "Tatar Yoke."
- **1485** — The Principality of Tver is annexed.
- **1498** — Ivan's grandson Dmitry is crowned as heir.
- **1502** — Ivan's son Vasily is made co-ruler, reversing the succession.
- **1503** — A peace treaty with Lithuania cedes several towns to Moscow.
- **1505** — Ivan III dies, succeeded by his son Vasily III.

## Body

### Territorial Expansion and Centralization
Ivan III's reign was defined by the "gathering of the Russian lands," bringing independent principalities under Moscow's direct control. His primary target was the wealthy Novgorod Republic, which he defeated militarily in 1471 and formally annexed in 1478 after political maneuvering. This acquisition nearly doubled Moscow's realm. He subsequently absorbed other principalities, including Tver (1485) and Vyatka (1489). Crucially, Ivan ended the tradition of dividing the realm among his sons. His will stipulated that all domains of his kinsfolk would revert to the reigning grand prince, eliminating semi-independent appanages and consolidating Muscovite power.

### Domestic Policy and Autocracy
Ivan III's government shifted toward a new autocratic form. He reduced the political and economic power of the boyar (high-ranking noble) class, creating a new service nobility granted estates called *pomestie* in exchange for military duty. A new law code, the *sudebnik*, was compiled, which mandated severe penalties for rebellion and restricted peasant mobility, laying the groundwork for serfdom. Ivan also oversaw a major building program in Moscow, inviting Italian architects like Ridolfo di Fioravante to rebuild the Kremlin, including the Dormition Cathedral and the Palace of Facets.

### Imperial Ideology and Foreign Policy
Following his marriage to Sophia Palaiologina, niece of the last Byzantine emperor, Ivan adopted Byzantine imperial symbolism, including the double-headed eagle as the state coat of arms. He began using the title *tsar* (from "Caesar") in foreign correspondence, asserting Moscow's status as the successor to Byzantium and the "Third Rome." This period saw the formal end of Mongol-Tatar dominance in 1480. Ivan's foreign policy established Moscow as a major European power, engaging in diplomacy with states from Venice to the Ottoman Empire. He also waged wars against the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, gaining significant territories by 1503.

## Terms
- **Appanage:** A portion of land given to a younger son of a ruler, which was semi-independent.
- **Boyar:** A member of the highest rank of the feudal Bulgarian, Russian, or Wallachian aristocracy, second only to the ruling prince.
- **Pomestie:** A type of conditional land grant given to service nobility in exchange for military or administrative duties.
- **Sudebnik:** A legal code; Ivan III's version was compiled in 1497.
- **Tsar:** A title used for Slavic monarchs, derived from the Latin "Caesar," signifying an emperor-level sovereign.
- **Veche:** A popular assembly in medieval Slavic cities, a form of direct democracy.
- **Gosudar:** A title meaning "sovereign" or "ruler," which Ivan III used to assert his authority.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes a scholarly debate on the impact of Ivan III's reign. While he was successful in military and economic consolidation, historian John Fennell argued his reign was also marked by "cultural depression, lack of freedom, and isolation from the West." The exact nature and extent of this cultural and political isolation versus consolidation remains a point of analysis.
