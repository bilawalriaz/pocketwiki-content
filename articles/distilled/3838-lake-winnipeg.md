# Lake Winnipeg

## Overview
Lake Winnipeg is a large, shallow freshwater lake in Manitoba, Canada, and the 12th largest lake on Earth. It is a remnant of the prehistoric Glacial Lake Agassiz and serves as a critical hydrological hub, draining a vast watershed into Hudson Bay. The lake is ecologically significant, supporting diverse fish and bird populations, but faces severe environmental threats from nutrient pollution and invasive species. It is also vital for regional transportation, recreation, and commercial fisheries.

## Timeline
- **c. 4,000 years ago** — Evidence suggests the south basin of Lake Winnipeg was desiccated (dried up).
- **1670** — Hudson's Bay Company chartered; its territory, Rupert's Land, included Lake Winnipeg's watershed.
- **1690** — Henry Kelsey becomes the first European known to see the lake.
- **1730s** — La Vérendrye builds forts near the lake and records its name as "Ouinipigon."
- **1953** — White bass introduced into Lake Ashtabula, North Dakota, a tributary source.
- **1963** — White bass first recorded in Lake Winnipeg.
- **1998** — Pipestone Rocks counted as hosting 3.7% of the world's American white pelican population.
- **2002** — Smallmouth bass first recorded in Lake Winnipeg.
- **2013** — Global Nature Fund declares Lake Winnipeg the "threatened lake of the year."
- **2015** — Major uptick in invasive zebra mussels is observed in the lake.
- **2016** — The Anishinaabe name *Weenipagamiksaguygun* is approved by the Geographical Names Board of Canada.
- **2018** — The lake's eastern shore is inscribed as the Pimachiowin Aki UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Hydrography and Geology
Lake Winnipeg has the largest watershed of any lake in Canada, covering about 982,900 km² and receiving water from four provinces and four U.S. states. It drains northward into the Nelson River, forming part of the Hudson Bay drainage basin. The lake and nearby Lake Manitoba are remnants of prehistoric Glacial Lake Agassiz. It is relatively shallow, with a mean depth of 12 metres and a deepest channel of 36 metres.

### Natural History
The lake's diverse habitats support more fish species than any other lake in Canada west of the Great Lakes, including 60 of Manitoba's 79 native species. Two native species, the shortjaw cisco and bigmouth buffalo, are at risk. Non-native species like common carp, white bass, and smallmouth bass have been introduced. The lake is crucial for birds, with globally significant nesting sites for colonial waterbirds like American white pelicans and terns on its islands. It is also a key staging area for fall migrations of waterfowl and shorebirds, including the endangered piping plover.

### Environmental Issues
Lake Winnipeg suffers from severe eutrophication, an over-enrichment of nutrients, primarily from excessive phosphorus. This causes explosive blooms of cyanobacteria (blue-green algae), which can be harmful to human health. In 2015, the invasive zebra mussel population surged, threatening the lake's ecology due to a lack of natural predators. These issues led to its designation as the "threatened lake of the year" in 2013.

### History and Economy
The lake is named from the Cree word *wīnipēk*, meaning "muddy waters." It lies along a historic fur trade route between Hudson Bay and the interior. Today, its communities rely on tourism and recreation. The lake is a premier angling destination, particularly for walleye, supporting a significant economic contribution. It also hosts important commercial fisheries, producing over half of Manitoba's total fish harvest by weight and value, with walleye and whitefish as the primary species. Manitoba Hydro uses the lake as a major reservoir.

## Terms
- **Watershed**: The area of land where all water drains to a common outlet, like a lake or river.
- **Eutrophication**: Excessive richness of nutrients in a body of water, frequently due to runoff from the land, which causes a dense growth of plant life and depletion of oxygen.
- **Cyanobacteria**: Photosynthetic bacteria, often called blue-green algae, that can form harmful algal blooms in nutrient-rich water.
- **Boreal forest**: A biome characterized by coniferous forests consisting mostly of pines, spruces, and larches.
- **Put and take program**: A fisheries management practice where fish are stocked for immediate recreational harvest, not for self-sustaining populations.
- **Colonial nesting birds**: Birds that nest in large, dense groups, often on islands for protection.
- **Glacial Lake Agassiz**: A massive prehistoric lake that covered much of central North America at the end of the last Ice Age.
