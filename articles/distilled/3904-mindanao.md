# Mindanao

## Overview
Mindanao is the second-largest island in the Philippines and the seventh-most populous island in the world. It is a major agricultural breadbasket for the country but is also a deeply-divided society marked by a complex history of cultural influences, colonization, and ongoing conflicts related to religion, ethnicity, and land.

## Timeline
- **c. 4500–2000 BC** — Subanon people settle in the Zamboanga Peninsula during the Neolithic era.
- **10th century** — Butuan Rajahnate, a Hinduized kingdom, is mentioned in Chinese records.
- **Mid-14th century** — First mosque in the Philippines built in Simunul, Tawi-Tawi.
- **1521** — Antonio Pigafetta, with Magellan's expedition, writes of reaching 'Maingdano.'
- **1543** — Ruy López de Villalobos is the first Spaniard to reach Mindanao.
- **1898** — Spain sells the Philippines, including Mindanao, to the United States in the Treaty of Paris.
- **1968** — Jabidah massacre is cited as a major flashpoint for the Moro insurgency.
- **1989** — Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (ARMM) is established.
- **2017** — President Duterte declares martial law on all of Mindanao after the Marawi siege.
- **2019** — Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (BARMM) replaces the ARMM.

## Body

### Etymology and Geography
The name "Mindanao" derives from the Maguindanao words *min* (past tense prefix) and *danao* ('lake'), meaning "land of where the lakes are found." The island is mountainous, home to Mount Apo, the country's highest peak (2,964 m). It is surrounded by four seas and includes the Sulu Archipelago. Its geography features extensive plateaus, large river valleys like the Agusan and Rio Grande de Mindanao, and significant lakes such as Lake Lanao.

### Prehistory and Early Influences
Archaeological evidence indicates human activity on Mindanao for about ten thousand years. Austronesian people spread throughout the Philippines around 1500 BC. From 900 AD, the island was heavily influenced by Hindu-Buddhist culture from Indonesia and Malaysia, introducing scripts, clothing, and weaving techniques. This era saw the rise of rajahnates like Butuan and Sanmalan, and epic narratives like the Darangen.

### Islam, Spanish Colonization, and Christianity
Islam arrived in the 14th century via Muslim merchants, leading to the establishment of sultanates in Sulu, Lanao, and Maguindanao by the 16th century. Spanish colonization began in the 1520s, leading to centuries of conflict with the Muslim sultanates. Spain established settlements and forts, converting many coastal tribes to Catholicism, while the sultanates resisted. By the late 18th century, Spain had geographic dominance but continued battling Muslim polities until the end of the 19th century.

### American Period and Migration
After the U.S. acquired the Philippines in 1898, the Commonwealth government encouraged migration from Luzon and Visayas to Mindanao in the early 1900s. This influx of Christian settlers, mostly Cebuanos, Ilocanos, and Ilonggos, began to displace indigenous Lumad and Moro (Muslim) communities, setting the stage for future conflicts.

### The Marcos Era and Insurgency
Under Ferdinand Marcos (1965–1986), Christian settlement intensified. The administration encouraged settler militias like the Ilaga, which committed abuses against Moro and Lumad people. The 1968 Jabidah massacre sparked the Moro insurgency, leading to the formation of secessionist groups like the Moro National Liberation Front (MNLF) and Moro Islamic Liberation Front (MILF). Martial Law in 1972 further radicalized movements, including the New People's Army.

### Post-1986 Autonomy and Conflict
The post-Marcos era saw efforts at peace and autonomy. The Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (ARMM) was created in 1989. Conflicts with the MILF continued, including a government "All Out War" in 2000. Major violent incidents persisted, such as the 2009 Maguindanao massacre and the 2013 Zamboanga City crisis. The 2017 Marawi siege by terrorist groups led to a five-month battle and widespread displacement. In 2019, the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (BARMM) was established to replace the ARMM.

### Economy and Infrastructure
Mindanao's economy accounts for 14% of the national GDP, with agriculture, forestry, and fishing comprising over 40% of its market. It is the country's top supplier of pineapples and bananas. Metro Davao is the primary growth corridor, with other centers in Cagayan de Oro, General Santos, and Zamboanga City. The island faces a significant power deficit, relying heavily on the silted Agus-Pulangui hydropower complex. Major infrastructure projects, including the Mindanao Railway, are planned.

## Terms
- **Lumad**: Indigenous non-Muslim and non-Christian peoples of Mindanao.
- **Moro**: A term for the Muslim ethnic groups of the southern Philippines.
- **Rajahnate**: A pre-colonial Philippine polity ruled by a raja, often influenced by Hindu-Buddhist culture.
- **Sultanate**: A state led by a sultan, established in Mindanao following the spread of Islam.
- **BARMM**: Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao, the current autonomous region for Muslim Filipinos.
- **Ilaga**: A Christian settler militia formed during the Marcos era, known for human rights abuses.
- **MNLF**: Moro National Liberation Front, a major secessionist group formed in the 1970s.
- **MILF**: Moro Islamic Liberation Front, a major insurgent group that signed a peace agreement with the government.
- **Agus-Pulangui complex**: Mindanao's primary hydropower source, currently operating below capacity due to siltation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Religious Demographics**: Reports on religious demographics are often considered unreliable due to Mindanao's deep divisions and concerns about discrimination. The National Commission on Muslim Filipinos (NCMF) argues the government's methodology for measuring religious diversity is flawed.
- **Secession Movements**: Calls for Mindanao's secession from the Philippines have resurfaced periodically, most recently in 2024, though they remain a minority position.
- **Autonomy and Peace**: The effectiveness and future of the BARMM in achieving lasting peace and development, and resolving issues of ancestral domain and self-governance, remain ongoing challenges.
