# Turin

## Overview
Turin (Torino) is a major city in northwest Italy, serving as the capital of the Piedmont region. Historically, it was the capital of the Duchy of Savoy, the Kingdom of Sardinia, and the first capital of the Kingdom of Italy (1861-1865). It is a global centre for automotive manufacturing, culture, and technology, known for its Baroque architecture, museums, and role in Italian unification.

## Body

### Historical Development
The area was originally inhabited by the Celto-Ligurian Taurini people. After a Roman colony was founded c. 28 BC, the city fell under various rulers, including the Lombards and Franks. In the 10th century, the Contea di Torino was established, eventually coming under the control of the House of Savoy. In 1563, Duke Emmanuel Philibert made Turin the capital of the Duchy of Savoy, initiating a major urban redesign. Following the 1706 Battle of Turin, the Duke of Savoy acquired the title of King of Sardinia, making Turin the capital of a European kingdom.

Turin was the political and intellectual centre of the Risorgimento, the movement for Italian unification, earning it the nickname "the cradle of Italian liberty." It became the first capital of the unified Kingdom of Italy in 1861, but the capital was moved to Florence in 1865. The late 19th century saw rapid industrialization, particularly in the automotive sector with the founding of Fiat (1899) and Lancia (1906). During World War II, the city was heavily bombed due to its industries and was a centre of anti-fascist resistance. Postwar, its automotive industry drove the "Italian economic miracle," attracting massive internal migration. A crisis in the 1970s-80s led to population decline, which has only recently begun to reverse.

### Economy and Industry
Turin's economy developed as a Fordist model, heavily reliant on automotive and aerospace manufacturing. It remains the historic base for Fiat (now part of Stellantis) and other automotive companies like Lancia and Alfa Romeo. The aerospace sector is significant, with companies like Leonardo producing modules for the International Space Station. Since the 1980s, the economy has diversified towards services, technology, and innovation, with a growing number of startups and patent applications. In 2008, its GDP was $68 billion, accounting for 8% of Italy's total.

### Urban Landscape and Architecture
Turin's city centre is renowned for its Baroque architecture, developed under the Savoyard state. Key features include arcaded streets like Via Po, grand squares such as Piazza Castello and Piazza San Carlo, and numerous palaces. The Residences of the Royal House of Savoy are a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The Mole Antonelliana, originally a synagogue, is the city's architectural symbol and houses the National Museum of Cinema. The city's grid plan, established in Roman times, is still evident. Notable districts include the historic Quadrilatero Romano, the elegant Crocetta, and the multi-ethnic Aurora.

### Culture and Society
Turin is a major cultural hub with numerous museums, including the Egyptian Museum (one of the world's most important collections of Egyptian antiquities) and the National Museum of Cinema. It is the home of the Shroud of Turin, a linen cloth revered by many Christians. The city has a rich literary and publishing history, notably through the Einaudi publishing house. It is also known for its cuisine, including gianduiotto chocolate and bicerin. Sport is central to its identity, hosting two major football clubs, Juventus and Torino, and the 2006 Winter Olympics. Turin was the birthplace of Italian cinema, with the first Italian film screening in 1896.

## Terms
- **Risorgimento:** The 19th-century political and social movement that led to the consolidation of different states of the Italian peninsula into a single state, the Kingdom of Italy.
- **Fordist city:** An urban economy based on mass production and consumption, typically centered on a single dominant industry, like automotive manufacturing in Turin.
- **Baroque architecture:** A style of architecture originating in the 17th century, characterized by grandeur, drama, and movement, prominent in Turin's historic centre.
- **Gianduiotto:** A traditional Turin chocolate, shaped like an ingot, made from cocoa and hazelnut paste.
- **Shroud of Turin:** A linen cloth bearing the image of a man, believed by some to be the burial shroud of Jesus Christ.
- **Spina Centrale:** A major urban redevelopment project in Turin involving the covering of a railway line to create a central boulevard.
- **VAL system:** A type of automated light metro system, used for the first line of Turin's metro (Metrotorino).

## Debates and open questions
- The authenticity of the Shroud of Turin remains a subject of scientific and religious debate.
- The long-term economic impact of the shift from a manufacturing-based to a service-based economy is an ongoing challenge for the city.
- The completion and expansion of the Metrotorino subway system and the Spina Centrale project face financial and logistical hurdles.
