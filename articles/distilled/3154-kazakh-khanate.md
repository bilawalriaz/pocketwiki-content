# Kazakh Khanate

## Overview
The Kazakh Khanate was a nomadic state in Central Asia that existed from c. 1465 to the early 19th century. It was the first nation-state in the region created by the modern Kazakh ethnic group and represented the political consolidation of the Kazakh people after the fragmentation of the Golden Horde. Its history is marked by periods of powerful centralization, internal fragmentation, and eventual absorption into the Russian Empire.

## Timeline
- **c. 1465** — Founding of the Kazakh Khanate by Janibeg and Kerei Khan after splitting from the Abu'l-Khayr Khanate.
- **1511–1521** — Reign of Kasym Khan, marking the beginning of an independent Kazakh state and its greatest early strength.
- **1521–1538** — Period of rapid decline and civil war following Kasym Khan's death.
- **1538–1581** — Reign of Haqnazar Khan, who restored control over the eastern steppe and defeated the Nogai Horde.
- **1598–1628** — Reign of Esim Khan, who moved the capital to Turkestan and created a new legal code.
- **1643** — Battle of Orbulaq, where Kazakh forces repelled a major Dzungar invasion.
- **1680–1718** — Reign of Tauke Khan, who codified Kazakh law in the "Jeti Zhargy" (Seven Laws).
- **1718** — Death of Tauke Khan leads to the effective split of the Khanate into three independent *zhuzes* (hordes).
- **1731** — Khan of the Junior Jüz swears nominal fealty to the Russian Empire.
- **1771–1781** — Reign of Ablai Khan, who briefly reunited all Kazakhs and navigated between Russia and China.
- **1822–1824** — The Russian Empire formally abolishes the institution of the Khanate in the Middle Jüz.
- **1837–1847** — Rebellion of Kenesary Khan, the last ruler to unite the Kazakhs and resist Russian incorporation.

## Body

### Formation and Early Expansion
The Kazakh Khanate emerged from the disintegration of the Golden Horde (Ulus of Jochi). In the 1460s, princes Janibeg and Kerei Khan, descendants of Urus Khan, fled the rule of Abu'l-Khayr Khan of the Uzbek Khanate and established a base near the Chu River. Following Abu'l-Khayr's death in 1470–1471, many tribes joined them, forming the "Uzbeg-Kazák." By the late 15th century, a truce divided territories with the Shaybanid Uzbeks, solidifying Kazakh control over the northern steppe. The reign of Kasym Khan (1511–1521) is widely seen as the start of a fully independent state, with authority extending to the Ural Mountains and a population reaching one million.

### Periods of Strength and Conflict
After a post-Kasym decline, the Khanate was restored under Haqnazar Khan (r. c. 1538–1581), who defeated the Nogai Horde and expanded eastward. The late 16th century saw conflict with the powerful Shaybanid ruler Abdallah Khan II. Under Esim Khan (r. 1598–1628), the Khanate experienced a third rise, moving its capital to Turkestan and creating the "Yesim Salghan Eski Zhol" legal code. A major new threat emerged in the 17th century: the Dzungar Khanate. The Kazakhs won a key victory at the Battle of Orbulaq in 1643 but were ultimately pushed back, losing eastern territories.

### Decline and Fragmentation
The reign of Tauke Khan (r. 1680–1718) saw a final period of internal reform with the "Jeti Zhargy" legal code, but also growing Russian encroachment. After Tauke's death, centralized authority collapsed, and the Khanate split into three *zhuzes* (Senior, Middle, and Junior). The Junior Jüz accepted Russian suzerainty in 1731, a relationship initially nominal. Ablai Khan (r. 1771–1781) temporarily reunited the Kazakhs but could not halt Russian expansion. By the early 19th century, Russia abolished the khanates, leading to uprisings, most notably the final national liberation struggle led by Kenesary Khan until his death in 1847.

### Economy and the Slave Trade
Located on the Silk Road, the Khanate's economy was based on nomadic herding and trade in horses, cattle, and furs. The 18th century was marked by frequent raids and a mutual slave trade with Russian settlements. Kazakh raiders captured Russians and sold them in Central Asian markets, while Russian Cossacks also raided Kazakh lands. The Russian government eventually took steps to end the trade, and by the early 19th century, raids had largely ceased as Russian control solidified.

## Terms
- **Zhuz (Horde):** The three major tribal confederations (Senior, Middle, Junior) into which the Kazakh Khanate was divided after the early 18th century.
- **Desht-i-Kipchak:** The "Kipchak Steppe," a historical name for the vast Central Asian steppe region north of the Aral and Caspian Seas, core territory of the Khanate.
- **Golden Horde (Ulus of Jochi):** The Mongol and later Turkicized state in Eastern Europe and Central Asia from which the Kazakh Khanate emerged.
- **Shaybanids/Astrakhanids:** Successive Uzbek dynasties that ruled the Khanate of Bukhara and were rivals to the Kazakhs for control of Transoxiana.
- **Dzungar Khanate:** A powerful Oirat Mongol state in the 17th–18th centuries that was the primary eastern rival and military threat to the Kazakh Khanate.
- **Jeti Zhargy (Seven Laws):** A codified system of Kazakh customary law established under Khan Tauke in the late 17th century.
- **Biy:** A judge or member of the council of advisors to the Khan, whose role was formalized under Tauke Khan.
- **Suzerainty:** A relationship where a lesser state (the Kazakh Jüz) acknowledges the overlordship of a greater power (Russia) while retaining internal autonomy.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Date of Establishment:** There is no consensus. Russian historiography often cites 1470–1471 (death of Abu'l-Khair Khan), while Kazakh tradition commonly uses 1465, based on the *Tarikh-i Rashidi*.
- **Nature of Russian Suzerainty:** Historians debate whether the 1731 allegiance of the Junior Jüz was a meaningful incorporation into Russia or a purely nominal act of vassalage that left the Kazakhs effectively independent for decades.
- **Role of the Slave Trade:** The scale and impact of the mutual slave trade between Kazakhs and Russians in the 18th century, and its role in shaping border relations, is a point of historical focus.
