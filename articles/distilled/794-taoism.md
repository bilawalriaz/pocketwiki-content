# Taoism

## Overview

Taoism (or Daoism) is an indigenous Chinese philosophical and religious tradition centered on harmony with the Tao (道), an ineffable, transformative process underlying reality. It has profoundly shaped Chinese culture, influencing ethics, governance, medicine, and the arts, while also absorbing elements from Confucianism, Buddhism, and folk religion. Taoist practice emphasizes self-cultivation, wu wei (effortless action), naturalness, and the pursuit of longevity or immortality.

## Body

### Classical Foundations and Early Development

Taoist thought crystallized during the Warring States period (c. 450–300 BCE) with the composition of the *Tao Te Ching* and *Zhuangzi*. These texts, though attributed to Laozi and Zhuang Zhou respectively, likely emerged from multiple authors and oral traditions. Early Taoism drew from Shang/Zhou religious practices, Confucianism, Mohism, Legalism, and the *I Ching*. Scholars like Harold Roth suggest early Taoism involved "inner-cultivation lineages," while others like Russell Kirkland argue no unified "Taoism" existed before the Han dynasty.

### Institutionalization and Syncretism

The first organized form of Taoism was the Way of the Celestial Masters, which developed from the Five Pecks of Rice movement at the end of the 2nd century CE. Zhang Daoling, who had a vision of Laozi in 142 CE, founded this movement, claiming the world was ending and teaching repentance to prepare for a new era of great peace. A related movement, the "Way of Great Peace" in Shandong, sought to replace the Han dynasty and led to the Yellow Turban Rebellion. The Celestial Masters survived this period without participating in efforts to replace the Han, growing influential during the Three Kingdoms period and gaining official recognition from Cao Cao in 215 CE.

During the Six Dynasties (316–589 CE), the Shangqing and Lingbao schools emerged. Shangqing was based on revelations to Yang Xi between 364 and 370 CE, while Lingbao scriptures were compiled by Ge Chaofu between 397 and 402 CE. By the 5th century, Lu Xiujing unified diverse traditions under the "three caverns" schema and compiled the first Daozang canon. The Tang dynasty (618–907) marked the height of Taoist influence, with imperial patronage and the rise of Quanzhen monasticism under Wang Chongyang in the 12th century.

### Core Teachings and Practices

Central to Taoist philosophy is the Tao—an indescribable, monistic reality that is both immanent and transcendent. Key concepts include:

- **Wu Wei**: Effortless action aligned with natural rhythms.
- **Ziran**: Natural spontaneity and self-so-being.
- **De**: Virtue or power manifesting through harmony with the Tao.
- **Qi**: Vital energy circulating through body and cosmos.
- **Xian**: Immortals or transcendent beings embodying Taoist ideals.

Practices encompass meditation (quiet sitting, visualization), internal alchemy (*neidan*), ritual observance, divination (*I Ching*, feng shui), and ethical precepts. The body is viewed as a microcosm of the universe, with elixir fields (*dantian*) and energy channels (*meridians*) central to cultivation.

### Modern Revival and Global Spread

The 20th century brought severe suppression during the Cultural Revolution, but post-1979 reforms enabled a revival. Today, Taoism is one of China's five officially recognized religions, regulated through the Chinese Taoist Association. Significant communities exist in Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Southeast Asia. Western adoption includes both traditional lineages and syncretic movements like tai chi and New Age interpretations.

## Terms

- **Tao/Dao**: The ineffable Way or ultimate reality underlying existence.
- **Wu Wei**: Non-action or effortless action in accordance with nature.
- **Ziran**: Naturalness or spontaneous self-so-being.
- **De/Te**: Virtue or power expressing one's harmony with the Tao.
- **Qi**: Vital breath or cosmic energy animating all things.
- **Xian**: Immortal beings or transcendent sages in Taoist belief.
- **Neidan**: Internal alchemy practices aimed at spiritual transformation.
- **Daozang**: The canonical collection of Taoist scriptures.
- **Daoshi**: Ordained Taoist clergy or master.
- **Yin-Yang**: Complementary cosmic forces representing dynamic balance.

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars debate whether "Philosophical Taoism" and "Religious Taoism" represent distinct traditions or artificial Western constructs. Many argue the daojia/daojiao distinction is "wholly inaccurate and untenable," noting that early texts already contained religious elements like cosmology and mystical union. The historicity of Laozi remains contested, with some viewing him as legendary. Disagreements persist over whether pre-Han practices constituted "real Taoism" or were later syntheses. The classification of diverse movements—from Yellow Turban rebels to modern qigong groups—as "Taoist" reflects ongoing definitional challenges. Contemporary Western "Taoism" often blends authentic traditions with New Age spirituality, raising questions about cultural authenticity and appropriation.
