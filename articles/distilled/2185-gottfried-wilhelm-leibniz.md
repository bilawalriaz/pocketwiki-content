# Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

## Overview
Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) was a German polymath and a leading figure of the 17th-century Enlightenment. He is credited, alongside Isaac Newton, with the independent development of calculus and made foundational contributions to philosophy, logic, and technology. His work spanned mathematics, metaphysics, law, and engineering, making him one of the last great universal geniuses before the era of specialization.

## Timeline
- **1646** — Born in Leipzig, Electorate of Saxony.
- **1666** — Earns doctorate in law from University of Altdorf; publishes *De Arte Combinatoria*.
- **1672** — Moves to Paris; meets Christiaan Huygens and begins serious study of mathematics.
- **1673** — Demonstrates a calculating machine to the Royal Society in London.
- **1676** — Begins work as counsellor and librarian for the House of Hanover.
- **1684** — Publishes his first paper on calculus, *Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis*.
- **1700** — Becomes first president of the Berlin Academy of Sciences.
- **1710** — Publishes *Théodicée*, his major work on theodicy and optimism.
- **1714** — Composes the *Monadologie*, a summary of his metaphysical system.
- **1716** — Dies in Hanover, largely out of favor with the new British king, George I.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Leibniz was born in Leipzig to a professor of moral philosophy. After his father's death, he gained early access to a scholarly library, mastering Latin by age 12. He studied philosophy and law at the University of Leipzig, earning bachelor's and master's degrees. After Leipzig refused him a doctorate in law due to his youth, he moved to the University of Altdorf, where he quickly obtained his doctorate in 1666. He declined an academic post, choosing instead a career in public service and intellectual pursuits.

### Career and Diplomatic Work
Leibniz's first position was as a secretary to an alchemical society in Nuremberg. He soon entered the service of Johann Christian von Boyneburg and the Elector of Mainz, working on legal reform and diplomatic projects. In 1672, he was sent to Paris to propose a plan to distract Louis XIV by encouraging a French conquest of Egypt, a scheme that became irrelevant with the outbreak of the Franco-Dutch War. This stay in Paris was pivotal; mentored by Christiaan Huygens, Leibniz began his major mathematical work. After the deaths of his patrons, he accepted a position in 1675 as counsellor and librarian to the House of Hanover, a role he held for the rest of his life. He spent decades researching a commissioned history of the Brunswick family, a project he never completed.

### Philosophy and Metaphysics
Leibniz was a leading rationalist who developed a comprehensive metaphysical system. His core principles include the Principle of Sufficient Reason (nothing happens without a reason) and the Identity of Indiscernibles (no two distinct things can share all properties). His most famous doctrine is that of **monads**: simple, indivisible, non-material substances that are the true units of reality. Each monad is a unique, self-contained "mirror of the universe," and their apparent interactions are governed by a **pre-established harmony** ordained by God. In his *Théodicée*, Leibniz defended the idea that God created the best of all possible worlds, where apparent evil serves a greater good. This optimistic view was later satirized by Voltaire in *Candide*.

### Mathematics and Science
Independently of Newton, Leibniz developed differential and integral calculus, publishing his findings in 1684. His notation (e.g., the integral sign ∫ and the differential *d*) became the standard. He also invented the modern binary number system, which is the foundation of digital computing. In physics, he proposed a relational theory of space and time (contrary to Newton's absolute space) and the concept of *vis viva* (living force), an early form of kinetic energy. He made contributions to geology, embryology, and psychology, anticipating ideas about the unconscious through his theory of *petites perceptions* (unnoticed small perceptions).

### Technology and Library Science
Leibniz was a practical inventor and engineer. He designed and built one of the first mechanical calculators, the "stepped reckoner," capable of all four arithmetic operations. He envisioned machines for integrating differential equations and conceptualized a computer based on binary numbers and punched cards. As librarian for the ducal libraries in Hanover and Wolfenbüttel, he developed advanced cataloging systems and advocated for libraries as essential tools for research, helping to found the field of library science.

## Terms
- **Monads**: The simple, non-material, indivisible substances that Leibniz posited as the fundamental constituents of reality.
- **Pre-established Harmony**: The doctrine that monads do not interact; their states are synchronized by God at creation.
- **Principle of Sufficient Reason**: The philosophical principle that for any fact, there is a sufficient reason why it is so and not otherwise.
- **Theodicy**: A theological work attempting to justify the goodness of God in the face of evil and suffering in the world.
- **Calculus Ratiocinator**: Leibniz's concept of a universal symbolic language and calculus for resolving disputes through calculation.
- **Vis Viva**: Latin for "living force," Leibniz's term for what is now recognized as kinetic energy (proportional to mv²).
- **Petites Perceptions**: Unconscious or unnoticed perceptions that collectively influence conscious thought.
- **Identity of Indiscernibles**: The principle that if two things share all the same properties, they are in fact one and the same thing.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Calculus Priority Dispute**: A bitter, decades-long controversy with Isaac Newton over who invented calculus first. Modern historians generally credit both with independent discovery, noting Leibniz's superior notation.
- **Interpretation of Monads**: Scholars debate the precise nature of monads—whether they are mental, physical, or something else—and how they interact with the physical world.
- **Leibniz and Chinese Thought**: The extent to which Leibniz's philosophy, particularly his monads and binary system, was influenced by his reading of Chinese classics like the *I Ching* is a subject of ongoing study.
- **Legacy in Logic**: While Leibniz's unpublished work on formal logic was profound, its direct influence on the later development of symbolic logic by figures like Frege and Russell is debated.
