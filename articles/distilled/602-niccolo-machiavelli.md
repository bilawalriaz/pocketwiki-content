# Niccolò Machiavelli

## Overview
Niccolò Machiavelli (1469–1527) was a Florentine diplomat, philosopher, and historian of the Italian Renaissance, best known for *The Prince* (1513), a treatise advocating pragmatic, often ruthless statecraft. His works, especially *The Discourses on Livy* (1517), profoundly shaped modern political thought, influencing republican theory and realist approaches to politics.

## Timeline
- **1469** — Born in Florence to a minor noble family
- **1498–1512** — Served as Florentine secretary in diplomatic and military affairs
- **1502** — Witnessed Cesare Borgia’s execution of political rivals in Sinigaglia
- **1512** — Expelled from office after Medici restoration; tortured on suspicion of treason
- **1513** — Began writing *The Prince* during exile
- **1517** — Composed *Discourses on Livy*
- **1520** — Commissioned by the Medici to write *Florentine Histories*
- **1527** — Died in Florence; buried at Santa Croce

## Body

### Diplomatic Career and Early Influences
Machiavelli entered Florentine civil service in 1498, rising to head the second chancery and serving as secretary to the Dieci di Libertà e Pace. His diplomatic missions exposed him to powerful rulers like Cesare Borgia, whose ruthless consolidation of power deeply influenced his political thinking. Observing Borgia’s use of force and deception—particularly the 1502 massacre at Sinigaglia—shaped Machiavelli’s belief in effective governance through necessity rather than morality.

### Exile and Literary Turn
In 1512, the Medici returned to power with Spanish support, leading to Machiavelli’s dismissal and false imprisonment. After his release, he retired to his farm near Sant’Andrea in Percussina, where he focused on writing political treatises. During this period, he developed *The Prince*, intended as a guide for new rulers seeking to establish and maintain authority through pragmatic means, including fear and force when necessary.

### Major Works and Political Thought
*The Prince* argues that rulers must prioritize state stability over moral virtue, famously asserting that it is better to be feared than loved if both cannot be achieved. He emphasizes *virtù*—the ability to adapt and act decisively—as essential for overcoming *fortuna* (fortune or chance). In contrast, *The Discourses on Livy* explores republican governance, praising mixed constitutions and civic participation while still endorsing strong leadership to preserve liberty.

Machiavelli’s views on religion were critical; he saw Christianity as weakening political resolve and preferred pagan ideals that promoted martial vigor. He also analyzed the role of *stato* (state) as a tool for expansion and control, diverging from classical thinkers like Plato and Aristotle, whom he rarely cited directly.

### Reception and Legacy
Initially controversial, *The Prince* was banned by the Catholic Church in 1559. Critics like Innocent Gentillet accused Machiavelli of promoting atheism and tyranny. However, later thinkers such as Rousseau interpreted it satirically. Machiavelli’s influence extended to Enlightenment philosophers and the American Founding Fathers, who admired his republican ideals. His concept of political realism became foundational in international relations theory.

## Terms
- **Virtù**: A ruler’s capacity for decisive action, adaptability, and strategic thinking to overcome fortune.
- **Fortuna**: Fortune or chance, depicted as an unpredictable force that virtù must counteract.
- **Stato**: The state or regime, viewed as a mechanism for acquiring and maintaining power.
- **Glory**: Public recognition of achievement, especially in war or public service, central to Machiavelli’s political ethos.
- **Republicanism**: Governance by representative institutions, favored by Machiavelli for its flexibility and reliance on civic virtue.
- **Humors**: Two opposing forces in society—the ruling elite and the people—whose conflict defines political life.
- **Necessity**: The imperative driving rulers to act immorally when required for state survival.
- **Mirror of Princes**: A genre of literature advising rulers, which Machiavelli reimagined with secular, pragmatic counsel.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate whether Machiavelli’s works are internally consistent or contradictory. Some argue *The Prince* and *Discourses* reflect opposing views—tyranny versus republicanism—while others propose a synthesized intent. The extent of his atheism or religious skepticism remains contested. Additionally, interpretations vary on whether his advice was meant for tyrants or general rulers, and whether his writings were ironic or earnest. His legacy as a "teacher of evil" persists, though many modern scholars emphasize his contribution to empirical political analysis and republican theory.
