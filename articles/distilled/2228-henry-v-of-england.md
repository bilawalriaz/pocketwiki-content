# Henry V of England

## Overview
Henry V (1386–1422) was King of England whose military victories in the Hundred Years' War, particularly at Agincourt (1415) and the conquest of Normandy, made England the dominant power in France. His reign represents the high-water mark of English fortunes in the war, culminating in the Treaty of Troyes (1420), which recognized him as heir and regent of France. He was also a forceful domestic ruler who restored public order, worked closely with Parliament, and enforced strict religious orthodoxy.

## Timeline
- **16 September 1386** — Born at Monmouth Castle, eldest son of the future Henry IV.
- **21 July 1403** — Fought at the Battle of Shrewsbury at age 16, where he was wounded by an arrow.
- **20 March 1413** — Succeeded his father as King of England.
- **25 October 1415** — Won the Battle of Agincourt against a larger French army.
- **19 January 1419** — Captured Rouen after a prolonged siege, completing the conquest of Normandy.
- **10 September 1419** — Murder of John the Fearless, Duke of Burgundy, at Montereau.
- **21 May 1420** — Treaty of Troyes signed, making him heir and regent of France.
- **6 December 1421** — Birth of his son, the future Henry VI.
- **31 August 1422** — Died at Vincennes, aged 35.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Henry of Monmouth was born in 1386 to the future Henry IV and Mary de Bohun. After his father usurped the throne from Richard II in 1399, Henry became Prince of Wales and heir apparent. He gained early military experience in Wales against Owain Glyndŵr and at the Battle of Shrewsbury. As his father's health declined, he took an increasingly central role in government, though political disagreements led to conflict. He succeeded to the throne in March 1413 and revived the long-standing English claim to the French throne.

### The Hundred Years' War
Henry renewed warfare with France in 1415, opening the Lancastrian phase of the Hundred Years' War. His first campaign included the siege of Harfleur and the celebrated victory at Agincourt, which strengthened his domestic position. A second expedition from 1417 aimed to conquer Normandy, a duchy lost two centuries earlier. By 1419, his army stood before Paris. Exploiting the civil war between Armagnac and Burgundian factions, and allying with Burgundy after the murder of Duke John the Fearless, Henry imposed the Treaty of Troyes in 1420. This treaty made him regent and heir to the French throne, disinherited the Dauphin Charles, and provided for his marriage to Charles VI's daughter, Catherine of Valois. Henry died in August 1422, seven weeks before Charles VI, leaving his infant son, Henry VI, as his successor.

### Domestic Rule and Religion
At home, Henry restored order after his father's turbulent reign, strengthening justice and working closely with Parliament. He promoted the use of English in royal government and upheld strict religious orthodoxy, crushing the Lollard movement and helping to end the Western Schism at the Council of Constance. His government was characterized by active personal engagement and effective administration, even while campaigning abroad.

### Military Organization and Legacy
Henry's armies were heavily weighted towards archers using the English longbow, and he made effective use of artillery in sieges. He also invested in a royal fleet to control the Channel. His premature death left the fragile dual monarchy he constructed to his infant son. The subsequent loss of his French conquests under Henry VI has been linked to the domestic conflicts that led to the Wars of the Roses. His reputation has been debated by historians, with some condemning him as a warmonger and others hailing him as one of England's greatest kings.

## Terms
- **Hundred Years' War**: A series of conflicts between England and France from 1337 to 1453.
- **Treaty of Troyes (1420)**: Agreement recognizing Henry V as heir and regent of France, disinheriting the Dauphin.
- **Lollardy**: A Christian reform movement in England, considered heretical by the Catholic Church.
- **Western Schism**: A period of multiple claimants to the papacy (1378–1417).
- **Council of Constance (1414–1418)**: Church council that resolved the Western Schism.
- **Salic Law**: A legal principle cited by the French to bar inheritance through the female line, used against English claims.
- **Armagnacs and Burgundians**: The two main factions in the French civil war during Henry's reign.
- **English longbow**: A powerful bow used by English archers, a key weapon in battles like Agincourt.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Motives for War**: Historians debate whether Henry's war was driven by genuine dynastic claims, economic grievances, or a need to divert attention from domestic issues.
- **Assessment of Reign**: Scholars are divided on whether Henry was a brilliant ruler and military genius or a warmonger whose ambition bankrupted England and weakened his dynasty.
- **Legacy of Conquests**: There is debate over whether the loss of Henry's French conquests was inevitable or contingent on events after his death, such as the early death of Charles VI.
- **Character**: Contemporary accounts range from admiration for his discipline to portrayals of him as a tyrant, and modern interpretations vary widely.
