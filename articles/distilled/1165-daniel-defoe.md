# Daniel Defoe

## Overview

Daniel Defoe (c. 1660 – 24 April 1731) was an English writer, journalist, merchant, and spy whose career spanned the late 17th and early 18th centuries. He is best known for pioneering the English novel with works such as *Robinson Crusoe* (1719), *Moll Flanders* (1722), and *Roxana: The Fortunate Mistress* (1724). Beyond fiction, Defoe was a prolific pamphleteer, political commentator, and government agent whose writings engaged with major issues of his day—including religious dissent, economic policy, and the Act of Union 1707 between England and Scotland. His life was marked by financial instability, imprisonment, and close ties to political power brokers like Robert Harley. Defoe’s legacy lies not only in literature but also in shaping public discourse through journalism and satire.

## Body

### Early Life and Education

Daniel Foe was born into a middle-class family in Fore Street, London. His father, James Foe, was a tallow chandler of probable Flemish origin, while his mother, Alice, died when he was around ten years old. The young Defoe experienced traumatic events firsthand—surviving the Great Plague of London in 1665 and witnessing the aftermath of the Great Fire in 1666. These formative experiences shaped his worldview and later influenced works like *A Journal of the Plague Year*.

Educated at dissenting academies under figures like Charles Morton, Defoe received a rigorous intellectual training despite being barred from Oxford or Cambridge due to his Presbyterian faith. This outsider status would define much of his writing and political engagement.

### Business Career and Political Involvement

Before turning fully to writing, Defoe worked as a general merchant dealing in hosiery, wine, and other goods. Though ambitious—he bought land, ships, and even kept civets for perfume—he remained chronically in debt. In 1685, he supported the Monmouth Rebellion but secured a pardon, avoiding execution during the Bloody Assizes.

His political alignment shifted over time. Initially aligned with Whig ideals, he became a staunch supporter of William III and later served as a secret agent for the Tory ministry under Robert Harley. However, his radical views often brought him into conflict with authority. In 1703, he was pilloried and imprisoned for *The Shortest-Way with the Dissenters*, a satirical attack on religious intolerance.

### Writing and Journalism

Defoe began publishing seriously in the late 1690s with *An Essay Upon Projects* (1697), proposing reforms across society—from banking to education. His most famous early work, *The True-Born Englishman* (1701), mocked English nationalism and anti-immigrant sentiment, becoming a bestseller.

He launched *A Review of the Affairs of France* in 1703, a weekly periodical that ran until 1713. It provided detailed coverage of the War of the Spanish Succession and helped establish Defoe as a pioneer of modern journalism. During this period, he also played a crucial role in advocating for the Act of Union 1707, acting as a covert operative in Scotland to sway public opinion toward unification.

### Novels and Literary Legacy

Though active as a writer for decades, Defoe turned to fiction relatively late in life. Beginning with *Robinson Crusoe* in 1719, he produced several groundbreaking novels over five years:

- **Robinson Crusoe (1719):** Often considered the first English novel, it explores themes of survival, faith, and colonialism. The protagonist’s isolation mirrors Defoe’s own periods of solitude and introspection.
- **Moll Flanders (1722):** A picaresque tale of a woman navigating poverty, crime, and moral ambiguity. Its sympathetic portrayal of female agency challenged traditional gender norms.
- **Roxana (1724):** A darker narrative exploring seduction, betrayal, and moral decay without redemption.

These works introduced realism and psychological depth to English fiction, influencing generations of authors.

### Later Works and Death

In his final decade, Defoe continued producing diverse texts—conduct manuals, travelogues, and treatises on economics and religion. Notable among these are *The Complete English Tradesman* (1726), which celebrated commercial virtue, and *A Tour thro' the Whole Island of Great Britain* (1724–1727), offering insights into pre-industrial British society.

He died in 1731, impoverished and still entangled in legal disputes. Buried in Bunhill Fields, he left behind a vast corpus of over 500 attributed works, many published anonymously or under pseudonyms.

## Terms

- **Dissenters / Nonconformists**: Protestants who worshipped outside the Church of England; legally marginalized and often persecuted.
- **Pen Name**: A pseudonym used by writers, especially common in 18th-century publishing.
- **Picaresque Novel**: A genre featuring a roguish protagonist’s episodic adventures, typically reflecting societal critique.
- **Seditious Libel**: Written material deemed threatening to state authority; punishable by law in early modern England.
- **Pillory**: A wooden framework with holes for the head and hands, used to publicly humiliate offenders.
- **Robinsonade**: A subgenre of fiction involving castaways stranded on islands, derived from *Robinson Crusoe*.
- **Standing Army**: A permanent military force maintained by the state; controversial in 17th-century England due to fears of tyranny.
- **Tallow Chandler**: A craftsman who made candles from animal fat; Defoe’s father’s profession.
- **Treaty of Ryswick (1697)**: Ended the Nine Years' War; led to debates over military funding and disarmament.
- **War of the Spanish Succession (1702–1714)**: Major European conflict over succession rights; covered extensively in Defoe’s *Review*.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several aspects of Defoe’s life and work remain contested:

- **Authorship Attribution**: Due to widespread use of pseudonyms and anonymous publication, scholars debate which of the ~545 attributed works are genuinely his. Some lists include up to 550 titles, though fewer than 75 were published under his real name.
- **Religious Conversion in Fiction**: While *Robinson Crusoe* and *Moll Flanders* end with spiritual awakenings, *Roxana* notably lacks such resolution. Critics question whether this reflects Defoe’s evolving beliefs or artistic choice.
- **Historical Accuracy vs. Fiction**: Many of Defoe’s novels claim to be true accounts (*e.g.*, *Journal of the Plague Year*). Scholars continue debating how closely these align with actual events.
- **Political Motivation in Union Advocacy**: Defoe’s role in promoting the 1707 Union remains ambiguous. Was he a genuine believer in British unity or merely a propagandist serving government interests?
- **Literary Innovation**: Though hailed as the father of the English novel, some argue that earlier writers like Aphra Behn laid more foundational groundwork. Defoe’s innovations lie more in popularization than invention.
