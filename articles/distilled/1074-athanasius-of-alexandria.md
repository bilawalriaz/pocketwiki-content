# Athanasius of Alexandria

## Overview

Athanasius I of Alexandria (c. 296–298 – 2 May 373) was a Christian theologian and Patriarch of Alexandria, serving intermittently for 45 years. He became the chief proponent of Trinitarianism against Arianism, defending the Nicene Creed's assertion that the Son is "of the same substance" (homoousion) as the Father. His career was defined by five exiles ordered by four Roman emperors, reflecting the intense political and theological struggles of the 4th century. His writings profoundly shaped Christian orthodoxy, and he is venerated as one of the four Great Greek Church Fathers.

## Body

### Early Life and Education

Born into a Christian family in Alexandria or Damanhur, Athanasius received a fine secular education despite not belonging to the Egyptian aristocracy. His fluency in both Greek and Coptic reflects the multicultural character of Alexandria. As a youth, he reportedly performed valid baptisms, catching the attention of Bishop Alexander, who took him as secretary and ordained him deacon in 319. His earliest works, *Contra Gentes* and *De Incarnatione*, written circa 318 before Arianism had begun to make itself felt, show no awareness of Arianism and reflect Origenist Alexandrian thought in an orthodox framework.

At about 30 years old when elected in 328, Athanasius faced controversy over whether he had reached the canonical age of 35, as the Arians accused him of not having yet attained that age. The Orthodox Church places his birth around 297.

### Conflict with Arianism

Around 319, Arius, a presbyter advocating subordinationist Christology—that the Son was "made, not begotten"—clashed with Bishop Alexander. Arius taught that the Son was of a distinct substance from the Father, gaining support from influential bishops like Eusebius of Nicomedia. At the First Council of Nicaea (325), convened by Constantine, the term *homoousios* ("consubstantial") was adopted to affirm the Son's full divinity. Athanasius, serving as Alexander's assistant, became the leading defender of this Nicene position throughout his career.

### Patriarchate and Exiles

Elected patriarch in 328, Athanasius faced immediate opposition from Arian factions. His first exile (335) followed the Synod of Tyre, where Eusebius of Nicomedia and allies deposed him on charges including mistreatment of Arians and Meletians. He was exiled to Augusta Treverorum in Gaul (now Trier in Germany). Constantine initially supported the deposition but later reconciled with Athanasius before his death in 337.

Under Constantius II, Athanasius was repeatedly exiled. His return to Rome in 338 was to appeal his case before Pope Julius, who convened a synod vindicating him. The Council of Serdica (343) reaffirmed his innocence, and its emissary reported findings to Constantius, who reconsidered due to a letter from Constans. However, political pressures led to further banishments. During his third exile (356), he lived among desert monks, producing major theological works including *Four Orations Against the Arians*, *Letter to the Monks*, *Apology to Constantius*, *Apology for His Flight*, and *History of the Arians*, in which he described Constantius as a precursor of the Antichrist.

Julian the Apostate's brief restoration allowed Athanasius to return in 362, convening a council that emphasized Trinitarian orthodoxy and defended the divinity of the Holy Spirit. However, Julian soon ordered his expulsion again, making the return brief. Under Valens, his fifth exile began in 364, though he was permitted to return shortly before his peaceful death in 373.

### Writings and Theological Contributions

Athanasius was not a speculative theologian but a staunch defender of apostolic tradition. His *On the Incarnation* articulated the orthodox view that the eternal Word became human to restore humanity's fallen nature. His *Letters to Serapion* defended the divinity of the Holy Spirit against Macedonianism. His *Letter Concerning the Decrees of the Council of Nicaea* (De Decretis) provided an important historical account of the council. His *Epistle to Marcellinus* addressed psalm-singing practices.

His Festal Letters, particularly the 39th Festal Letter written in 367, are significant for listing the 27-book New Testament canon. This letter also included the Book of Baruch and Letter of Jeremiah among canonical books, and placed Esther among "7 books not in the canon but to be read" along with the Wisdom of Solomon, Book of Sirach, Book of Judith, Book of Tobit, the Didache, and The Shepherd of Hermas.

His *Life of Anthony* became foundational to Christian monasticism, translated into several languages and becoming a bestseller that inspired both Eastern and Western ascetic traditions. Athanasius also used Coptic alongside Greek in his pastoral work, marking him as the first Alexandrian patriarch to do so.

## Terms

- **Homoousios**: Greek term meaning "of the same substance," affirming the Son's full divinity as defined at Nicaea.
- **Subordinationism**: Theological view that the Son is subordinate to the Father in nature or rank, associated with Arianism.
- **Meletian Schism**: Schism led by Meletius of Lycopolis, whose followers refused to accept Nicaean decisions.
- **Festal Letters**: Annual pastoral letters Athanasius wrote to his flock, often addressing theological and canonical issues.
- **Nicene Creed**: The creed formulated at the First Council of Nicaea (325), affirming Christ's divinity.
- **Macedonianism**: Heretical belief denying the full divinity of the Holy Spirit.
- **Desert Fathers**: Early Christian monastics of Egypt whose ascetic practices influenced Athanasius.
- **Canon**: Official list of books recognized as divinely inspired Scripture.

## Debates and Open Questions

### New Testament Canon

Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter (367) is widely regarded as a milestone in establishing the 27-book New Testament canon. However, scholars debate whether he was the first to list exactly these books, as some argue Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* may have preceded him. The letter also distinguished between canonical books and those "to be read" but not canonical, including the *Didache* and *Shepherd of Hermas*.

### Historical Allegations

Ancient critics accused Athanasius of various misdeeds, including defiling altars, selling church grain, and even murdering Bishop Arsenius. Sir Isaac Newton and others questioned his legitimacy as bishop, citing claims that he had not reached canonical age (35) when elected in 328. Modern scholars acknowledge these allegations reflect genuine political tensions but generally view them as partisan attacks rather than factual accounts.

### Arian Diversity

Contemporary scholarship emphasizes that "Arianism" was not a monolithic movement. The theological positions labeled "Arian" spanned a wide spectrum, from subordinationist views to more moderate positions. Many bishops condemned as "Arian" did not consider themselves followers of Arius, who was merely a presbyter, while they were ordained bishops. This complexity challenges traditional narratives of Athanasius's singular opposition to a unified Arian party.

### Legacy Interpretations

While Gregory of Nazianzus called Athanasius the "Pillar of the Church," some modern historians question whether his uncompromising stance prolonged rather than resolved theological conflicts. The tension between doctrinal purity and ecclesiastical unity remains a subject of scholarly debate regarding his historical significance.

## Veneration

Athanasius is venerated as a saint in the Catholic Church, Eastern Orthodox Church, Oriental Orthodox Church, Church of the East, the Anglican Communion, and Lutheranism. The Catholic Church considers him a Doctor of the Church. Western Christians observe his feast day on 2 May, the anniversary of his death. Eastern Orthodox liturgical calendars remember him on 18 January. For Coptic Christians, his feast day is Pashons 7 (now circa 15 May).

His remains were originally buried in Alexandria, later transferred to the Chiesa di San Zaccaria in Venice, Italy. During Pope Shenouda III's visit to Rome in 1973, Pope Paul VI gave the Coptic Patriarch a relic of Athanasius, which was brought back to Egypt and is currently preserved under the new Saint Mark's Coptic Orthodox Cathedral in Cairo. However, the majority of Athanasius's corpse remains in the Venetian church.

The Athanasian Creed is misattributed to him and is today generally seen as being of 5th-century Galician origin.
