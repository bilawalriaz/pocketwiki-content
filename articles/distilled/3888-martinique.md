# Martinique

## Overview
Martinique is a French overseas department and region in the Lesser Antilles of the Caribbean. Its history is defined by colonial conquest, plantation economies, and volcanic disaster, shaping a society with a blended French, African, and Creole identity. Today, it is an integral part of France and the European Union, grappling with its colonial past and environmental challenges.

## Timeline
- **1st century AD** — Saladoid people arrive from South America.
- **c. 11th century** — Kalinago people arrive from the Venezuelan coast.
- **15 June 1502** — Christopher Columbus lands on Martinique.
- **15 September 1635** — French settlers establish the first European colony.
- **1636** — First major Kalinago revolt against French settlers.
- **1658** — Final Kalinago revolt; survivors are expelled from the island.
- **1685** — Edict of Revocation causes most Huguenot settlers to flee.
- **1720s** — Coffee plantations introduced, making Martinique the first coffee-growing area in the Western Hemisphere.
- **23 May 1848** — Slavery is abolished in Martinique.
- **8 May 1902** — Mount Pelée erupts, destroying Saint-Pierre and killing ~30,000 people.
- **14 July 1943** — Free French forces take control from the Vichy regime.
- **1946** — Martinique becomes an Overseas Department of France.
- **24 January 2010** — Voters approve a "single collectivity" status under Article 73 of the French Constitution.
- **2 February 2023** — Martinique adopts its new official flag.

## Body

### History and Colonial Rule
The island was first inhabited by Saladoid and later Kalinago peoples. After Columbus's visit in 1502, France formally claimed Martinique in 1635, establishing a colony that relied on enslaved African labor for sugar and coffee plantations. The Kalinago were violently suppressed and expelled by 1658. The colony was repeatedly contested by the British, who occupied it for most of the period from 1794 to 1815. Slavery was abolished in 1848, following rebellions and abolitionist campaigns. The catastrophic eruption of Mount Pelée in 1902 destroyed the capital, Saint-Pierre, shifting the capital to Fort-de-France. During World War II, the island was controlled by the pro-Nazi Vichy regime until 1943. It became an Overseas Department of France in 1946.

### Governance and Status
Martinique is a special territorial collectivity of France, with its inhabitants holding full French citizenship. It is an outermost region of the European Union but not part of the Schengen Area or Customs Union. The political debate has centered on its constitutional status: assimilationists favor remaining under Article 73 of the French Constitution (where French law applies directly), while autonomists and independentists have sought change under Article 74 (granting more autonomy). A 2010 referendum rejected a move to Article 74 status but approved a "single collectivity" under Article 73, merging regional and departmental councils into the Assembly of Martinique.

### Economy and Environment
The economy is heavily dependent on tourism, limited agriculture, and financial aid from mainland France. Historically based on sugar and bananas, agriculture has declined; banana cultivation remains the main activity but is hampered by contamination from the pesticide chlordecone, used until 1993. This contamination has led to health concerns and economic restrictions. The island is a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve (2021), and the volcanoes and forests of Mount Pelée and the Pitons du Carbet were listed as UNESCO World Heritage Sites in 2023.

### Society and Culture
The population is predominantly of African descent, mixed with European, Amerindian, Indian, and other influences. The official language is French, but Martinican Creole, based on French with African and Caribbean elements, is widely spoken in informal contexts. The Créolité movement, led by writers like Patrick Chamoiseau, has worked to valorize Creole language and identity. The culture blends French, African, and Caribbean traditions, evident in cuisine (e.g., Colombo curry), music (zouk), and literature (Aimé Césaire, Frantz Fanon).

## Terms
- **Overseas Department/Region**: A French administrative division with the same status as departments in mainland France, subject to French law.
- **Special Territorial Collectivity**: A status for French overseas territories with more autonomy than a department but less than a fully autonomous overseas collectivity.
- **Kalinago**: The indigenous people of the Lesser Antilles, also known as Caribs.
- **Béké**: The descendants of the first European (primarily French) settlers on Martinique.
- **Chlordecone**: A persistent organic pollutant, a pesticide used in banana plantations until 1993, which has contaminated soil and water, posing health risks.
- **Créolité**: A literary and cultural movement asserting a distinct Creole identity, separate from both European and African roots.
- **Zouk**: A genre of popular music originating in the French Caribbean, blending Caribbean rhythms with electronic sounds.
- **Article 73 / Article 74**: Articles of the French Constitution defining different levels of autonomy for overseas territories. Article 73 applies laws directly; Article 74 allows for legislative specialization and greater autonomy.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Constitutional Status**: The core political debate remains between assimilationists (favoring integration under Article 73) and autonomists/independentists (seeking more self-governance under Article 74 or independence), though a 2010 referendum strongly rejected a move to Article 74.
- **Chlordecone Contamination**: There is ongoing controversy over the French state's responsibility for authorizing the pesticide, the adequacy of its response plans, and the long-term health and environmental consequences for the population.
- **Identity and Language**: The role and status of Martinican Creole versus French continues to evolve, with efforts to promote bilingual education and greater use of Creole in public life to preserve cultural identity.
