# John Rawls

## Overview
John Rawls (1921–2002) was an American philosopher whose work revived normative political philosophy in the late 20th century. His seminal book *A Theory of Justice* (1971) introduced "justice as fairness," a liberal egalitarian framework arguing that rational individuals, deliberating behind a "veil of ignorance" about their own social position, would choose principles guaranteeing equal basic liberties, fair equality of opportunity, and that social inequalities benefit the least advantaged. In *Political Liberalism* (1993), he addressed how a diverse society can achieve legitimate political authority despite deep moral and religious disagreements, proposing that citizens justify laws using only "public reasons" accessible to all. His final major work, *The Law of Peoples* (1999), extended these ideas to international relations, distinguishing between "liberal" and "decent" well-ordered peoples while rejecting global application of his distributive difference principle.

## Body

### Early Life and Formative Experiences
Rawls was born into a prominent Baltimore family; his father was an attorney and his mother active in Democratic politics and women's suffrage. At age seven, he contracted diphtheria and infected his brother Bobby, who died; the next year, he contracted pneumonia and infected another brother, Tommy, who also died. Biographer Thomas Pogge identifies these deaths as the pivotal trauma of Rawls’s childhood, later linked to his stutter and aversion to public attention. At Princeton, Rawls wrote an intensely religious senior thesis attacking Pelagianism and considered the Episcopal priesthood. His World War II service—combat in New Guinea and the Philippines, followed by occupation duty in Japan where he witnessed Hiroshima—shattered his faith and shaped his later focus on the moral limits of state power and the stability of just institutions.

### Academic Career and Influence
After a Fulbright year at Oxford (1952–53) engaging with Isaiah Berlin and H.L.A. Hart, Rawls taught at Cornell, MIT, and finally Harvard (1962–1991). At Harvard, he held the James Bryant Conant University Professorship and supervised a remarkable cohort of students who became leading figures in moral and political philosophy, including Thomas Nagel, T.M. Scanlon, Onora O’Neill, Christine Korsgaard, and Thomas Pogge. He briefly joined the Mont Pèlerin Society at Milton Friedman’s invitation but withdrew in 1971, just before *A Theory of Justice* appeared. Rawls rarely gave interviews, preferring academic and family life; his wife Margaret was a major collaborator, editing and indexing his manuscripts.

### *A Theory of Justice*: The Original Position and Two Principles
Rawls sought to reconcile freedom and equality not by compromise but by integrating them into a "seamless unity" called justice as fairness. His method, **reflective equilibrium**, involves mutually adjusting general principles and considered judgments on particular cases until they cohere.

The **original position** is a hypothetical choice situation modeling the correct moral standpoint for deliberating about justice. Parties select principles for society’s **basic structure** (the judiciary, economy, constitution) behind a **veil of ignorance** that strips them of knowledge about their gender, race, class, talents, religion, and conception of the good. They know only that they possess two **moral powers**: the capacity to form, pursue, and revise a conception of the good, and the capacity to develop a sense of justice. This ignorance forces impartiality: no one would risk proposing racist or sexist laws because they might occupy the disadvantaged position.

From this position, Rawls argues rational parties would adopt two **lexically ordered** principles (priority of the first over the second):
1.  **The Liberty Principle**: Each person has an equal claim to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic rights and liberties (conscience, association, expression, democratic rights, personal property), compatible with the same scheme for all. Equal political liberties are guaranteed their "fair value."
2.  **The Equality Principle** (two parts, lexically ordered):
    a.  **Fair Equality of Opportunity**: Offices and positions open to all under conditions where those with similar talents and motivation have roughly similar life chances.
    b.  **The Difference Principle**: Social and economic inequalities are permitted only if they are to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged members of society.

Rawls conceived this as **ideal theory** for a "well-ordered society" under favorable conditions, distinguishing his view from classical liberalism (Locke) and libertarianism (Nozick) by grounding property in moral capacities and self-respect rather than natural self-ownership.

### *Political Liberalism*: Legitimacy, Public Reason, and Overlapping Consensus
Rawls recognized that *A Theory of Justice* relied on a Kantian conception of human flourishing (autonomous moral agency) that citizens could reasonably reject. In a pluralistic democracy, citizens hold conflicting **comprehensive doctrines** (religious, philosophical, moral). The challenge: how can political power be legitimate when its deep justification is contestable?

His answer: the liberal state must be **political, not metaphysical**. It must rest on an **overlapping consensus**—reasonable comprehensive doctrines (Catholic, Muslim, secular, etc.) all affirm the same public political conception of justice for their own internal reasons. The public conception itself is justified only by **public reasons**: reasons whose status as reasons is shared by all citizens (e.g., child welfare, not scriptural interpretation).

This requires the **ideal of public reason** and a **duty of civility**: citizens and officials in the **public political forum** (courts, legislatures, voting booth) must offer only public reasons. The deep justifications of public values remain private to each doctrine. "Unreasonable" doctrines (incompatible with freedom, equality, fairness) are excluded not by force but because the project aims to show liberal legitimacy is internally coherent for those already committed to it, not to convert fundamentalists.

### *The Law of Peoples*: International Justice
Rawls extended his framework to a **Society of Peoples**, distinct from traditional state-centric international relations. "Well-ordered" peoples are either **liberal** (democratic, rights-respecting) or **decent** (hierarchical, possibly with state religions and consultation hierarchies instead of elections, but non-aggressive and respecting basic human rights). "Outlaw states," "burdened societies," and "benevolent absolutisms" fall outside this toleration.

Contra cosmopolitans like Charles Beitz, Rawls **rejected global application of the Difference Principle**. He argued peoples (unlike citizens) are self-sufficient in cooperative enterprises; a world state is unstable. Aid to burdened societies aims only to help them achieve decent or liberal institutions, not global equality, to avoid moral hazard (subsidizing irresponsible governance). He condemned bombing civilians (including WWII Allied bombing), discussed immigration and nuclear proliferation, and controversially held that severe human rights violations can legitimize military intervention, though peaceful reform via example is preferred.

### Reception and Major Debates
Rawls’s influence is vast: *A Theory of Justice* sold over 300,000 copies; protesters at Tiananmen Square (1989) brandished it; courts in the US and Canada cite him; a 2008 survey ranked him the most impactful political theorist of the prior 20 years.

Key critical engagements include:
*   **Communitarian critique** (Sandel, MacIntyre, Taylor, Walzer): Challenged the "unencumbered self" of the original position, arguing selves are constituted by community and tradition. Rawls responded in *Political Liberalism* by making his theory "political, not metaphysical."
*   **Analytical Marxism** (G.A. Cohen, Jon Elster, John Roemer): Used Rawls’s framework to critique capitalism; Cohen argued the Difference Principle should apply to personal choices, not just institutions.
*   **Habermas–Rawls debate**: Jürgen Habermas engaged deeply, leading to mutual appreciation and bridging analytic and critical theory traditions (e.g., Rainer Forst).
*   **Feminist extensions** (Eva Kittay): Applied Rawls to dependency and cognitive disability.
*   **Cosmopolitan critique** (Beitz, Pogge): Argued global institutions (Bretton Woods, WTO) constitute a global basic structure requiring global distributive justice.
*   **Amartya Sen** (*The Idea of Justice*, 2009): Praised Rawls’s focus on fairness but criticized ideal theory for neglecting practical injustice remediation and comparative justice assessments.
*   **Daniel Chandler** (*Free and Equal*, 2023): Argued Rawls synthesized classical liberal freedom and socialist equality, but was marginalized in mainstream politics by neoliberalism and his own abstraction.

## Terms
- ****Original Position**** — A hypothetical choice situation where rational parties select principles of justice for society's basic structure behind a veil of ignorance.
- ****Veil of Ignorance**** — An epistemic constraint in the original position depriving parties of knowledge about their specific social position, talents, race, gender, religion, and conception of the good.
- ****Reflective Equilibrium**** — A method of moral justification achieved by mutually adjusting general principles and considered judgments on particular cases until they cohere.
- ****Basic Structure**** — The fundamental social institutions (political constitution, legal system, economy) that distribute fundamental rights, duties, and primary social goods.
- ****Two Moral Powers**** — The capacities parties in the original position know they possess: (1) to form, pursue, and revise a conception of the good; (2) to develop a sense of justice.
- ****Difference Principle**** — The requirement that social and economic inequalities be arranged to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged members of society.
- ****Lexical Priority**** — A strict ordering where the first principle (basic liberties) must be fully satisfied before the second principle (equality) is applied; liberty cannot be traded for economic gain.
- ****Comprehensive Doctrine**** — A broad religious, philosophical, or moral view covering all of life (e.g., Catholicism, utilitarianism, Kantianism), as opposed to a narrowly political conception.
- ****Overlapping Consensus**** — Agreement on a political conception of justice by holders of diverse reasonable comprehensive doctrines, each for their own internal reasons.
- ****Public Reason**** — Reasons whose status as reasons is shared by all citizens (e.g., political values of freedom, equality), required for justification in the public political forum.
- ****Well-Ordered Peoples**** — In *The Law of Peoples*, societies that are either "liberal" (democratic, rights-based) or "decent" (hierarchical but non-aggressive and respecting basic human rights).

## Debates and Open Questions
1.  **Ideal vs. Non-Ideal Theory**: Does focusing on principles for a perfectly just "well-ordered society" (ideal theory) distract from diagnosing and remedying actual injustices in non-ideal conditions? (Sen, Simmons).
2.  **Scope of the Difference Principle**: Should it apply globally (cosmopolitanism), or only within bounded societies? Rawls restricted it to domestic basic structure; critics argue global institutions create a global basic structure.
3.  **The "Unencumbered Self"**: Communitarians argue the original position assumes a self detached from constitutive attachments; Rawls replied his later work is "political, not metaphysical," avoiding deep metaphysical commitments about the self.
4.  **Public Reason's Restrictiveness**: Does the duty of civility illegitimately exclude religious reasons from public debate, or is it necessary for mutual respect in a pluralistic society? (Habermas, Eberle, Wolterstorff).
5.  **Feasibility of Overlapping Consensus**: Is it realistic to expect deep convergence on a liberal political conception from doctrines that are fundamentally illiberal in structure, or does it exclude too many citizens?
6.  **Incentives and the Difference Principle**: Does allowing inequalities to incentivize productivity (benefiting the worst off) undermine the ethos of justice and community Rawls seeks? (Cohen).
7.  **Status of "Decent Peoples"**: Is Rawls’s toleration of non-liberal but rights-respecting "decent" hierarchies a pragmatic necessity or a betrayal of liberal equality?
