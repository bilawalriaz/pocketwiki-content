# Skagerrak

## Overview
The Skagerrak is a strait connecting the North Sea to the Kattegat and, by extension, the Baltic Sea. It is a vital international shipping lane and a biologically diverse marine ecosystem, but it faces significant environmental pressures from human activity and climate change.

## Timeline
- **c. 1784** — The Eider Canal is constructed, providing an alternative to the Skagerrak as the only sea access to the Baltic.
- **1862** — The Thyborøn Channel is built in Denmark, creating a shortcut from the North Sea to the Kattegat.
- **31 May – 1 June 1916** — The Battle of Jutland (Battle of the Skagerrak), the largest naval battle of World War I, takes place in the strait.
- **World War II** — Germany invades Denmark and Norway partly to control the Skagerrak, the only sea access to the Baltic.
- **1983** — Gullmarsfjorden in Sweden becomes the country's first marine conservation area.
- **2009** — Ytre Hvaler National Park is established in Norway.
- **2013** — Approximately 7,500 individual vessels (excluding fishing vessels) visit the Skagerrak.
- **2016** — Raet National Park is established in Norway.
- **2020** — A seafloor mapping project in Jammerbugten, Denmark, reveals greater biodiversity than previously thought.

## Body

### Geography and Name
The Skagerrak is 240 km long and 80–140 km wide, deepening towards the Norwegian coast to over 700 m at the Norwegian Trench. Its name likely derives from Skagen, a Danish town, and "rak," meaning "straight waterway" in Dutch, suggesting it was named by Dutch seafarers. Historically, it was the sole maritime route to the Baltic Sea until the Eider Canal opened in 1784.

### Traffic and Strategic Importance
The strait is one of the world's busiest shipping lanes. In 2013, it was visited by about 7,500 vessels, predominantly cargo ships and tankers. Its strategic importance for controlling access to the Baltic Sea made it a key theater in both World Wars, most notably during the 1916 Battle of Jutland. This heavy traffic has left the seabed with numerous shipwrecks.

### Biology and Habitats
The Skagerrak supports approximately 2,000 marine species. Habitats range from shallow sandy reefs to the deep Norwegian Trench. A unique variety of Atlantic cod, the Skagerrak cod, spawns off the Norwegian coast. The ecosystem features vertical migration patterns (Vinogradov's ladder of migrations) and includes extensive cold-water coral reefs, such as the Tisler Reef in Norway, Europe's largest known coral reef. Rare "bubbling reefs" formed around methane seeps are also found, primarily in Danish waters.

### Environmental Concerns and Protections
The ecosystem is under strain from cumulative human activities, climate change, and ocean acidification. Issues include inputs of hazardous materials, marine litter, and nutrient runoff causing algal blooms. In response, several marine protected areas have been established, including Ytre Hvaler and Raet National Parks in Norway, Kosterhavet National Park in Sweden, and a protected sea area near Grenen in Denmark.

## Terms
- **Strait:** A narrow passage of water connecting two larger bodies of water.
- **Kattegat:** The sea area between the Skagerrak and the Baltic Sea.
- **Norwegian Trench:** A deep underwater trough running along the southern coast of Norway, reaching depths over 700 m.
- **Cold-water coral reef:** A reef structure built by corals in deep, cold water, unlike tropical reefs. *Lophelia* is a key species.
- **Bubbling reef:** A rare biological reef formed around cold seeps of geological gases, usually methane.
- **Vinogradov's ladder of migrations:** A model describing the vertical movement of marine species between benthic (bottom-dwelling) and pelagic (open-water) layers.
- **Benthic:** Relating to the bottom of a body of water.
- **Pelagic:** Relating to the open sea, away from the shore and the bottom.
- **Natura 2000:** A network of nature protection areas in the European Union.
