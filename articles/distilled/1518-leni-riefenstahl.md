# Leni Riefenstahl

## Overview
Helene "Leni" Riefenstahl (1902–2003) was a German filmmaker, photographer, and actress whose technical innovations in cinema are inseparable from her role as the primary visual propagandist for the Nazi regime. A celebrated dancer and mountain-film star during the Weimar era, she gained Adolf Hitler’s patronage, directing *Triumph of the Will* (1935) and *Olympia* (1938)—works widely regarded as the most effective propaganda films ever made. Her pioneering use of tracking shots, slow motion, aerial photography, and musical scoring redefined documentary and sports cinematography. After World War II, Allied courts classified her as a "fellow traveller" (Mitläufer) rather than a war criminal, but she faced lifelong condemnation for her proximity to Hitler, her use of Romani concentration camp inmates as extras in *Tiefland*, and her persistent denial of knowledge regarding the Holocaust. Her later career pivoted to photography of the Nuba people in Sudan and underwater filmmaking, yet her legacy remains a focal point for debates on the relationship between artistic genius and moral complicity.

- **1933** — Directs *Der Sieg des Glaubens* (Victory of Faith), a film on the 1933 Nuremberg Rally, funded by the NSDAP.
- **1935** — Releases *Triumph of the Will* (*Triumph des Willens*), documenting the 1934 Nuremberg Rally; globally acclaimed for technical innovation.
- **1935** — Directs *Tag der Freiheit: Unsere Wehrmacht* (Day of Freedom: Our Armed Forces) at the Nuremberg Rally.
- **1938** — Releases *Olympia*, documenting the 1936 Berlin Olympics; pioneers sports cinematography techniques; wins Mussolini Cup at Venice.
- **1939–1940** — Serves as war correspondent in Poland; witnesses civilian executions in Końskie; films Hitler’s victory parade in Warsaw.
- **1940–1944** — Produces *Tiefland* using Romani and Sinti inmates from Salzburg-Maxglan and Marzahn detention camps as forced extras; many later deported to Auschwitz.
- **1945–1948** — Detained in Allied internment camps; tried four times for denazification; classified as a "fellow traveller" (Mitläufer).
- **1954** — *Tiefland* premieres in Stuttgart; denied entry to Cannes Film Festival.
- **1974/1976** — Publishes photographic books *The Last of the Nuba* and *The Nuba People of Kau*; criticized by Susan Sontag for "fascist aesthetics."
- **2002** — Releases final film, *Impressionen unter Wasser* (Underwater Impressions), on 100th birthday.
- **8 Sep 2003** — Dies at age 101 in Pöcking, Bavaria.

## Body

### Early Life and Artistic Formation
Riefenstahl was born into a prosperous Berlin family; her father owned a heating and ventilation firm and desired a business career for his only daughter, while her mother secretly supported her artistic ambitions. A talented swimmer and gymnast, Riefenstahl began painting and writing poetry at age four. At 16, a performance of *Snow White* ignited a passion for dance. Against her father’s wishes, her mother enrolled her at the Grimm-Reiter Dance School, where she excelled in interpretive dance. She toured Europe with Max Reinhardt’s company in a production funded by Jewish producer Harry Sokal, earning up to 700 Reichsmarks per performance. A series of foot injuries and knee surgery ended her dancing career, prompting a pivot to film after she saw a poster for Arnold Fanck’s *Mountain of Destiny* (1924).

### The Mountain Film Era and Directorial Debut
Riefenstahl met Fanck and actor Luis Trenker, convincing Fanck to cast her in *The Holy Mountain* (1926). She starred in five "mountain films" between 1925 and 1929, performing dangerous stunts—barefoot rock climbing, jumping into icy streams, enduring avalanches—that established her physical courage and screen presence. She learned editing and directing techniques from Fanck. In 1932, she wrote, produced, directed, and starred in *The Blue Light* (*Das Blaue Licht*), a mystical tale of a peasant girl protected by a glowing grotto. The film won the Silver Medal at the Venice Film Festival. Hitler, an admirer, saw in her the embodiment of "Aryan womanhood" and initiated a close, 12-year relationship. For the 1938 re-release, the credits of Jewish co-writer Béla Balázs and producer Harry Sokal were removed; reports suggest Riefenstahl requested this.

### Propaganda Films: *Triumph of the Will* and *Olympia*
Mesmerized by Hitler’s oratory at a 1932 rally, Riefenstahl accepted a commission to film the 1933 Nuremberg Rally (*Der Sieg des Glaubens*). After the Night of the Long Knives purge of SA leader Ernst Röhm—who appeared prominently in the film—Hitler allegedly ordered all copies destroyed, though Riefenstahl disputed this. Hitler then commissioned *Triumph of the Will* (1935) for the 1934 rally. Riefenstahl later claimed she resisted, preferring to direct *Tiefland*, but Hitler insisted on the condition she would make no further party films. The film mobilized massive resources (over one million participants) and introduced revolutionary techniques: moving cameras on tracks and elevators, telephoto lenses, and a Wagnerian musical score replacing diegetic sound to fuse documentary with the "fantastic." Scholar Tom Saunders argues the film’s iconography mechanizes humans into "ants" while animating flags as potent, distinct symbols of masculine national dominance.

Despite her alleged vow, she directed *Tag der Freiheit: Unsere Wehrmacht* (1935) to appease the Army. For the 1936 Berlin Olympics, she produced *Olympia* (1938), secretly funded by the Nazis but commissioned by the IOC. *Olympia* invented modern sports cinematography: tracking rails for athletes, underwater diving shots, extreme high/low angles, slow motion, and reversed footage to defy gravity. It featured Jesse Owens prominently. The film won the Mussolini Cup at Venice. During a 1938 US publicity tour coinciding with *Kristallnacht*, Riefenstahl publicly defended Hitler, calling him "the greatest man who ever lived."

### World War II and *Tiefland*
When Germany invaded Poland in September 1939, Riefenstahl accompanied the Wehrmacht as a war correspondent. In Końskie, she witnessed the execution of 30 civilians (allegedly Jews). Her memoir claims she tried to intervene; a German officer’s account alleges she asked for Jews to be cleared from a market for a shot, prompting the massacre. She filmed Hitler’s Warsaw victory parade weeks later but made no further Nazi films. In 1940, on Hitler’s direct order, the state paid 7 million Reichsmarks for *Tiefland*. Filming used Romani from Salzburg-Maxglan camp (1940) and Sinti/Roma from Marzahn camp (1942) as forced extras. Overwhelming evidence confirms most were deported to Auschwitz after filming. Riefenstahl denied this for decades, suing filmmaker Nina Gladitz for libel. A German court ruled she knew the extras came from a concentration camp but not that they were sent to Auschwitz. In 2002, a Roma group sued her for denying the Romani genocide; she apologized, acknowledging many were murdered.

### Post-War Trials and Denazification
Arrested by US forces in 1945, Riefenstahl was held in various Allied camps until 1948. She was tried four times for denazification. The courts never charged her with war crimes, classifying her as a *Mitläufer* ("fellow traveller")—one who sympathized with and benefited from the regime without being a core perpetrator. She was never a formal NSDAP member. She won over 50 libel suits against accusations of complicity, yet consistently denied knowledge of the Holocaust, claiming she was "fascinated but politically naive." Critics, including Steven Bach, labeled her the voice of the "how could we have known?" defense. In a 2024 documentary, a private letter to Hitler surfaced where she enthused about *Triumph of the Will*’s propaganda impact.

### Later Career: Photography, Africa, and Underwater Film
Barred from feature filmmaking by protests and industry blacklisting, Riefenstahl turned to still photography. From the 1960s, inspired by George Rodger and Ernest Hemingway, she lived intermittently with the Nuba people in Sudan, publishing *The Last of the Nuba* (1974) and *The Nuba People of Kau* (1976). Susan Sontag famously critiqued these works as extensions of "fascist aesthetics"—celebrating physical purity, strength, and the "Noble Savage" while excluding critical reflection. Eudora Welty, conversely, praised the photographic artistry. Riefenstahl received Sudanese citizenship, becoming the first foreign passport holder. She photographed the 1972 Munich Olympics, Mick Jagger, and Siegfried & Roy. In 1978, she published *Coral Gardens* and later *Wonders under Water*. At age 72, she learned scuba diving (lying about her age for certification). Her final film, *Impressionen unter Wasser* (2002), was an idealized ocean documentary. She survived a helicopter crash in Sudan in 2000 while searching for Nuba friends during the civil war.

## Terms
- ****Mountain Film (Bergfilm)**** — A Weimar-era German genre pioneered by Arnold Fanck, set in alpine landscapes, emphasizing physical endurance, nature's grandeur, and melodramatic plots; launched Riefenstahl's acting career.
- ****Triumph of the Will (Triumph des Willens)**** — 1935 Nazi propaganda film documenting the 1934 Nuremberg Rally; renowned for technical innovations (tracking shots, aerial photography, musical scoring) that redefined documentary cinema.
- ****Olympia**** — 1938 two-part film on the 1936 Berlin Olympics; pioneered modern sports cinematography (slow motion, underwater shots, tracking rails, extreme angles) and is a landmark in documentary aesthetics.
- ****Mitläufer ("Fellow Traveller")**** — Lowest category in Allied denazification tribunals; designated individuals who passively supported or benefited from the Nazi regime without active participation in crimes. Riefenstahl received this classification in 1948.
- ****Diegetic Sound**** — Audio originating from the film's narrative world (dialogue, ambient noise). Riefenstahl largely replaced this in *Triumph of the Will* with a composed musical score to heighten emotional grandeur.
- ****Romani / Sinti**** — Ethnic groups targeted by the Nazi regime for persecution and genocide (Porajmos). Riefenstahl used inmates from Romani/Sinti detention camps as forced extras in *Tiefland*; most were later murdered at Auschwitz.
- ****Fascist Aesthetics**** — Term popularized by Susan Sontag describing a visual style glorifying physical perfection, hierarchy, mass choreography, and the subordination of the individual to the collective—identified in both Riefenstahl's Nazi films and her later Nuba photography.
- ****Porajmos**** — The Nazi genocide of European Roma and Sinti ("the Devouring"); estimates range from 250,000 to 500,000 victims. Riefenstahl's *Tiefland* extras were victims of this policy.
- ****Night of the Long Knives**** — June–July 1934 purge of SA leadership (including Ernst Röhm) ordered by Hitler. Riefenstahl's film *Der Sieg des Glaubens* featured Röhm prominently; Hitler allegedly ordered its destruction afterward.
- ****Kristallnacht**** — "Night of Broken Glass" (Nov 9–10, 1938), state-sponsored pogrom against Jews across Germany. Riefenstahl arrived in New York five days later for an *Olympia* tour and publicly defended Hitler.

## Debates and open questions
- **Degree of Agency vs. Coercion**: Riefenstahl claimed she was pressured by Hitler into making *Triumph of the Will* and *Olympia* and was an apolitical artist. Critics (e.g., Taylor Downing, Abraham Cooper) argue her work was essential to Nazi legitimation and the Holocaust's execution, making her an "unindicted co-conspirator." A 2024 documentary revealed a private letter expressing enthusiasm for *Triumph of the Will*’s propaganda efficacy, contradicting her postwar claims of reluctance.
- **Knowledge of the Holocaust and Romani Genocide**: She consistently denied knowing about the Final Solution or the fate of *Tiefland* extras. Courts found she knew extras came from camps but not their deportation to Auschwitz. Scholars debate whether her proximity to Hitler and presence in occupied Poland make total ignorance implausible.
- **Artistic Merit vs. Moral Taint**: Critics remain split. Pauline Kael called her films "the two greatest films ever directed by a woman"; Susan Sontag argued her aesthetics *are* fascist. The core dispute: can technical innovation be separated from the ideology it served?
- **Removal of Jewish Credits**: The 1938 re-release of *The Blue Light* omitted Béla Balázs and Harry Sokal. Riefenstahl blamed critics for the film's initial reception ("many of whom were Jewish") but denied ordering the credit removal; the source notes "some reports say this was at Riefenstahl's behest."
- **Końskie Incident (1939)**: Conflicting accounts exist: Riefenstahl claimed she tried to stop an execution; a German officer alleged her request to clear Jews from a market triggered the massacre. Photographs show her distraught, but her role remains unresolved.
- **Nature of Relationship with Hitler**: Riefenstahl claimed a platonic, artistic bond; her press secretary and others alleged a romantic affair. Fritz Wiedemann (Hitler's adjutant) denied she was Hitler's lover. The 2024 documentary suggests a deeper ideological affinity than she admitted.
