# Arius

## Overview

Arius (c. 250/256–336) was a Libyan-born presbyter of the Alexandrian church whose Christological dispute with Bishop Alexander in 318 ignited the Arian controversy, a defining theological crisis of the fourth-century Christian Church. His teaching that the Son was subordinate to the Father—generated before creation but not coeternal—clashed with emerging Nicene orthodoxy, leading to the First Council of Nicaea (325), which condemned Arianism and affirmed the Son’s coequality with the Father through the term *homoousios* ("of the same essence"). Modern scholarship, particularly by Richard Hanson and Rowan Williams, has revised the traditional view of Arius as a radical innovator, instead framing him as a conservative theologian representing pre-existing subordinationist currents within early Christianity. The controversy reshaped imperial and ecclesiastical relations, influencing doctrinal development, political alliances, and the eventual dominance of Nicene Christianity.

## Body

### Early Life and Personality

Arius was of Berber descent, son of Ammonius. His education likely included study under Lucian of Antioch, though Rowan Williams cautions that references to Lucian do not definitively prove formal discipleship. In 313, he was appointed presbyter of the Baucalis district in Alexandria. Described as tall, ascetic, and persuasive, Arius was seen by supporters as a resourceful thinker, while opponents portrayed him as heretical. His character and motivations remain debated due to biased sources.

### Arius's Writings and Reliability

Surviving works attributed to Arius include a confession of faith to Alexander of Alexandria, a letter to Eusebius of Nicomedia, and a confession to Constantine. His major theological work, the *Thalia*, survives only in fragments quoted by opponents like Athanasius. These sources are problematic because they were written by critics who sought to discredit him. Scholars note that Athanasius may have misrepresented Arius’s views through rhetorical framing. Constantine reportedly ordered the destruction of Arius’s writings, contributing to their scarcity.

### The Arian Controversy: Origins and Development

The controversy began in 318 when Arius challenged Bishop Alexander’s emphasis on eternal generation, accusing him of Sabellianism. Arius argued that if the Father begat the Son, the Son must have had a beginning, implying “there was a time when the Son was not.” This subordinationist stance reflected broader theological tensions rooted in earlier debates, including those involving Origen and Paul of Samosata. Arius’s ideas gained traction among Eastern bishops, notably Eusebius of Nicomedia and Eusebius of Caesarea, though the latter stopped short of full endorsement.

### The First Council of Nicaea

By 325, the dispute had escalated beyond Alexandria. Emperor Constantine convened the First Council of Nicaea to resolve the crisis, sending Hosius of Córdoba to mediate. Approximately 250–300 bishops attended, with 22 supporting Arius. The council rejected Arianism, adopting *homoousios* to affirm the Son’s coequality with the Father. Arius and two followers were exiled, while others signed under imperial pressure. The Nicene Creed became a cornerstone of Christian orthodoxy, explicitly opposing Arian theology.

### Exile, Return, and Death

Despite exile, Arius was recalled in 336 after Constantine pressured authorities to reconcile. Bishop Alexander of Constantinople refused to receive him, praying for Arius’s death before compliance. Arius died suddenly the next day, an event interpreted by some as divine judgment and by others as possible poisoning. His death did not end the controversy, which persisted for centuries.

### Arianism After Arius

Arianism flourished under Constantius II, reaching its peak at the Third Council of Sirmium (357), where the “Blasphemy of Sirmium” rejected *homoousios* and *homoiousios*. Emperor Valens persecuted Nicene bishops, but Theodosius I ended Arianism among Eastern elites via imperial decree and the First Council of Constantinople (381). In the West, Arianism survived among Germanic peoples through missionaries like Ulfilas, lasting until the seventh century. Later movements, such as the Polish Brethren and modern groups like Jehovah’s Witnesses, have been labeled Arian for their subordinationist Christologies.

## Terms

- **Subordinationism**: The belief that the Son is subordinate to the Father in nature or status.
- **Homoousios**: Greek term meaning “of the same essence,” affirming the Son’s full divinity.
- **Homoiousios**: Greek term meaning “of similar essence,” a compromise position rejected at Nicaea.
- **Christology**: The study of the nature and person of Christ.
- **Trinitarianism**: The doctrine that God exists as three persons—Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—in one essence.
- **Sabellianism**: The heretical view that the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are modes of one divine person.
- **Eternal Generation**: The belief that the Son is begotten of the Father from eternity, not created.
- **Logos**: The divine Word or Son, central to discussions of Christ’s nature.
- **Ecumenical Council**: A church council regarded as representative of universal Christianity.
- **Nicene Creed**: The creed adopted at Nicaea in 325, affirming Christ’s divinity and opposing Arianism.

## Debates and Open Questions

Modern scholarship debates whether Arius was an innovator or a conservative theologian. Richard Hanson and Rowan Williams argue that “Arianism” was a construct shaped by Nicene polemics, not Arius’s original teaching. The reliability of sources—especially Athanasius—is questioned, as they may reflect doctrinal agendas rather than historical accuracy. The exact cause of Arius’s death remains uncertain, with theories ranging from natural illness to political assassination. Additionally, the extent of Origen’s influence on Arius and the continuity of subordinationist thought prior to 318 are ongoing areas of scholarly inquiry.
