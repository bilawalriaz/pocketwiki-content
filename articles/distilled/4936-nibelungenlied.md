# Nibelungenlied

## Overview
The *Nibelungenlied* is a Middle High German epic poem written around 1200, based on older oral traditions of Germanic heroic legend. It is a foundational work of German literature, recounting the tragic story of the hero Siegfried, his marriage to Kriemhild, his murder, and her subsequent revenge, which leads to the destruction of the Burgundian royal house. Its legacy is immense, shaping later literature, opera, and national identity, though it was also infamously appropriated for nationalist propaganda.

## Timeline
- **5th-6th centuries** — Historical events (destruction of Burgundian kingdom, death of Attila) provide a core for the oral saga.
- **c. 1200** — The *Nibelungenlied* is composed in Middle High German, likely by a poet from the Passau region.
- **c. 1204/5** — Wolfram von Eschenbach references a character from the poem, providing a terminus ante quem for its composition.
- **c. 1225–1300** — The three oldest complete manuscripts (C, B, A) are created.
- **After 1500** — The poem falls out of circulation and is forgotten.
- **1755** — Manuscript C is rediscovered by Jacob Hermann Obereit; Johann Jacob Bodmer publicizes it.
- **1827** — Karl Simrock publishes an influential modern German translation.
- **1876** — Richard Wagner's operatic cycle *Der Ring des Nibelungen* premieres, based largely on Old Norse sources.
- **1909/1940s** — The poem is heavily appropriated for anti-democratic and Nazi propaganda.
- **2009** — The three main manuscripts are inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World Register.

## Body

### Manuscripts and Versions
Thirty-seven manuscripts and fragments survive, with eleven essentially complete. Texts vary considerably, though the core plot is stable. Scholars differentiate two early versions: the *Not*-version (manuscripts A and B) and the *Lied*-version (manuscript C). Manuscript B (c. 1233–1266) is generally considered closest to the original *Not*-version. By 1300, at least five versions circulated, including mixed forms. Later manuscripts sometimes include additional material, like a story of Siegfried's youth.

### Synopsis
The poem is divided into two parts. In Part I, the hero Siegfried travels to Worms to woo the Burgundian princess Kriemhild. He helps her brother, King Gunther, win the warrior-queen Brünhild by using his magical cloak of invisibility (*Tarnkappe*) to win her contests. Siegfried marries Kriemhild, but a rivalry between the two queens escalates until Brünhild learns Siegfried, not Gunther, took her virginity. The vassal Hagen, with Gunther's consent, murders Siegfried by spearing him in the back at a spring, exploiting a vulnerability Kriemhild had revealed.

In Part II, the widowed Kriemhild marries King Etzel of the Huns, planning revenge. After thirteen years, she lures her brothers and Hagen to Etzel's court. A feast erupts into a massive battle when Hagen kills Kriemhild and Etzel's son, Ortlieb. The Burgundians barricade themselves in the hall, which is set on fire. All are killed except Hagen and Gunther, who are captured by the hero Dietrich von Bern. Kriemhild demands Hagen reveal the location of Siegfried's treasure hoard. He refuses until Gunther is killed, then admits the hoard is lost forever. Enraged, Kriemhild beheads Hagen with Siegfried's sword, and is then killed by the hero Hildebrand.

### Authorship and Origins
The poem is anonymous, though its poet likely came from the Passau region and may have been patronized by Bishop Wolfger von Erla (1191–1204). The work synthesizes an amorphous oral tradition (the "Nibelungen saga") into a single narrative. This tradition has historical roots: the destruction of the Burgundians reflects the 436/437 fall of the Burgundian kingdom on the Rhine, and the death of Attila (Etzel) in 453. However, the poet personalized political events and merged figures from different centuries. Siegfried's origins are more mythological, and his story was likely separate from the Burgundian destruction in earlier traditions. The poet also suppressed many fantastical elements of Siegfried's youth found in other versions.

### Form, Style, and Medieval Reception
The poem is written in a distinctive four-line stanzaic form, likely meant to be sung. The first three lines advance the plot, while the fourth often provides commentary or foreshadowing. This structure, with its formulaic language, evokes an oral storytelling tradition. The *Nibelungenlied* was the first heroic epic written in the German vernacular, founding a genre. Its tragic ending troubled medieval audiences, prompting the immediate creation of a sequel, the *Nibelungenklage*, which sought to explain the horror and absolve Kriemhild. The poem was widely copied and referenced by other medieval authors.

### Modern Reception and Legacy
After being forgotten for two centuries, the poem was rediscovered in 1755 and dubbed the "German Iliad." Its reception has been turbulent. In the 19th century, it was embraced as a national epic embodying German virtues. Richard Wagner's famous opera cycle, *Der Ring des Nibelungen*, drew primarily on Old Norse sources. In the 20th century, the poem was cynically exploited by Nazi propaganda, which glorified Hagen's self-sacrifice and compared the Soviets to Attila's Huns. Post-war adaptations are often parodic. The poem remains a central, if contested, work in German cultural history.

## Terms
- **Nibelungenlied**: The title, meaning "The Song of the Nibelungs," referring to the Burgundian royal house.
- **Middle High German**: The form of the German language spoken and written from c. 1050 to 1350.
- **Siegfried**: The central hero of Part I, who acquires a treasure hoard and impenetrable skin.
- **Kriemhild**: A Burgundian princess, wife of Siegfried and later of Etzel, whose quest for revenge drives the plot.
- **Hagen**: A Burgundian vassal and primary antagonist, who murders Siegfried and is later killed by Kriemhild.
- **Brünhild**: A supernaturally strong queen of Iceland, whose rivalry with Kriemhild triggers the tragedy.
- **Tarnkappe**: A magical cloak of invisibility used by Siegfried.
- **Etzel**: King of the Huns, Kriemhild's second husband.
- **Dietrich von Bern**: A heroic figure (based on the historical Theodoric the Great) who tries to mediate the conflict.
- **Nibelungenklage**: A sequel poem written shortly after the *Nibelungenlied* to address its tragic ending.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authorship and Date**: The poet is anonymous. While c. 1200 is conventional, some scholars argue for an earlier version (c. 1150) based on its stanzaic form.
- **Relationship to Oral Tradition**: Did the poet create a definitive version from an amorphous oral tradition, or did variant written forms always exist? Jan-Dirk Müller argues the written form is entirely new, while others posit an earlier written version.
- **Historical vs. Mythological Core**: The precise historical origins of figures like Siegfried are debated. Some speculate a mythologized connection to Arminius, while others see a purely mythological origin.
- **Kriemhild's Motivation**: The poet likely transformed Kriemhild from a traditionally treacherous figure into a courtly lady motivated by love. Scholars debate how successfully this new motivation is integrated with older, darker elements of her character.
- **Narrative Coherence**: The poem's structure, with its sometimes disconnected stanzas and doubled motivations for events, has led to debate about whether it reflects oral composition techniques or deliberate artistic choice.
