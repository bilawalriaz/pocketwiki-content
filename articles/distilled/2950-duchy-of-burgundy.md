# Duchy of Burgundy

## Overview
The Duchy of Burgundy was a major feudal territory in medieval France, evolving from a Carolingian county into a powerful, semi-independent state under the Valois dukes. Its history is defined by a series of succession crises and the expansion of its rulers into the Low Countries, creating a vast Burgundian State that rivaled the French crown before its final absorption into France in the late 15th century.

## Timeline
- **918** — Richard the Justiciar is first attested as Duke of Burgundy.
- **1002** — Death of Duke Henry the Venerable triggers a succession war.
- **1032** — King Henry I of France grants the duchy to his brother, Robert I, founding the Capetian ducal line.
- **1361** — Death of Duke Philip I (Philip of Rouvres) ends the Capetian male line; the duchy reverts to the French crown.
- **1363** — King John II of France cedes the duchy to his younger son, Philip the Bold, establishing the Valois-Burgundy line.
- **1369** — Philip the Bold marries Margaret of Dampierre, uniting the duchy with Flanders and other northern territories.
- **1477** — Death of Duke Charles the Bold at the Battle of Nancy ends the Valois-Burgundy dynasty.
- **1477** — King Louis XI of France annexes the Duchy of Burgundy proper.
- **1482** — Treaty of Arras confirms French control of the duchy; the Burgundian Netherlands pass to Habsburg rule.

## Body

### Origins and Early Dukes
The duchy emerged from the northwestern counties of the former Kingdom of the Burgundians, which were divided from the southern regions by the Partition of Verdun in 843. Powerful local lords, like Richard the Justiciar, consolidated these counties into a feudal agglomeration, with Richard first bearing the title "Duke of Burgundy" in 918. The duchy's independence was contested by the Robertian family, but it was secured for the Capetian dynasty when King Robert the Pious granted it to his son, Robert I, in 1032.

### The Capetian Dukes (1032–1361)
The Capetian dukes methodically rebuilt ducal power after a period of anarchy. They expanded the ducal demesne (personal land) through escheat (reversion of land without heirs), purchase, and financial management. Under Duke Robert II (r. 1271–1306), the duchy reached new political and economic prominence. The line culminated in Duke Philip I (Philip of Rouvres), whose death in 1361 without heirs triggered a major succession crisis.

### The Succession Crisis of 1361
Philip I's will left his territories to the next heirs by local custom. The Duchy of Burgundy itself presented a legal conflict between two principles: primogeniture (inheritance by the senior line) favored Charles II of Navarre, while proximity of blood (closeness of descent) favored King John II of France. During the Hundred Years' War, the Burgundian nobility, hostile to the pro-English Charles II, supported John II. With the backing of other co-heirs, John II took possession, but the Burgundian estates firmly declared the duchy would remain a separate entity, not be merged into the French crown.

### The Valois-Burgundy State (1363–1477)
To resolve the situation, John II granted the duchy to his youngest son, Philip the Bold, in 1363, founding the Valois-Burgundy line. Philip's marriage to Margaret of Dampierre united the duchy with the wealthy County of Flanders, creating a powerful Burgundian State. Under Philip's son, John the Fearless, Burgundy became a major, often antagonistic, political player in France. The state reached its zenith under Philip the Good and Charles the Bold, who sought full independence. Charles's death in 1477 ended the dynasty; King Louis XI of France immediately annexed the Duchy of Burgundy proper, while the northern territories passed to Charles's daughter, Mary, and her Habsburg heirs.

## Terms
- **Duchy (Ducatus):** The territory or domain ruled by a duke.
- **Escheat:** The reversion of feudal land to a lord when a vassal dies without a direct heir.
- **Primogeniture:** The right of the firstborn child, especially the eldest son, to inherit the entire estate of one or both parents.
- **Proximity of Blood:** A principle of inheritance favoring the heir closest in genealogical descent to the last holder, regardless of seniority of line.
- **Demesne:** Land directly owned and administered by a lord, as opposed to land granted to vassals.
- **Capetian Dynasty:** The royal dynasty of France founded in 987, from which the first Burgundian ducal line descended.
- **Valois-Burgundy:** The cadet branch of the French royal House of Valois that ruled the Duchy of Burgundy from 1363 to 1477.
- **Burgundian Netherlands:** The northern territories (Flanders, Holland, etc.) acquired by the Valois dukes, forming the core of the Burgundian State.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The legal basis for the 1361 succession was complex, pitting primogeniture against proximity of blood. The outcome was determined more by political allegiance during the Hundred Years' War than by strict legal precedent.
- The Burgundian estates' successful resistance to King John II's attempt to merge the duchy into the French crown in 1361 is a key moment in asserting the duchy's distinct political identity.
