# Taejo of Joseon

## Overview
Taejo (Yi Sŏnggye) was the founder and first monarch of the Joseon dynasty, which ruled Korea for over five centuries. He overthrew the Goryeo dynasty in 1392, establishing a new regime that emphasized stability and continuity with the past. His reign was marked by diplomatic efforts to secure relations with Ming China and Japan, but it ended in personal tragedy due to a violent succession struggle among his sons.

## Timeline
- **1335** — Born in Ssangseong Prefecture, Yuan dynasty frontier.
- **1356** — Yi family defects to Goryeo, helping it seize Ssangseong.
- **1388** — Leads army but executes the Wihwado Retreat, refusing to attack Ming China and instead seizing power in Goryeo.
- **1392** — Forces King Gongyang to abdicate, ending Goryeo. Establishes the Joseon dynasty.
- **1393** — Changes dynasty name to Joseon.
- **1394** — Establishes new capital at Hanseong (present-day Seoul).
- **1398** — First Strife of Princes: his son Yi Pang-wŏn kills rivals, including Crown Prince Yi Pang-sŏk. Taejo abdicates.
- **1408** — Dies in Changdeokgung, ten years after abdication.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Born on the Yuan dynasty frontier, Yi Sŏnggye's family returned to Goryeo in 1356. He built a prestigious military career, defeating Red Turban invaders, Mongol remnants, and Japanese pirates. In the late 14th century, the Goryeo court was divided between pro-Ming and pro-Yuan factions. When ordered to invade Ming China in 1388, Yi Sŏnggye instead led his army back to the capital in the Wihwado Retreat, a pivotal coup that placed him as the power behind the throne. He eliminated rivals, including the revered scholar Chŏng Mong-ju, and in 1392, deposed the last Goryeo king to found Joseon.

### Reign and Policy
Taejo's rule emphasized continuity. He did not create new institutions or conduct massive purges, and the same elite families largely continued to govern. His primary achievements were diplomatic: he improved relations with Ming China by refusing to attack them and successfully re-established amicable ties with Japan. He also received envoys from the Ryūkyū Kingdom and Siam. In 1394, he moved the capital to Hanseong (Seoul).

### Abdication and Succession Crisis
A major conflict arose over the succession. Taejo's fifth son, Yi Pang-wŏn, had been instrumental in his rise to power but hated the king's key allies, Chŏng To-jŏn and Nam Ŭn. Chŏng To-jŏn persuaded Taejo to name a younger son, Yi Pang-sŏk, as crown prince. In 1398, fearing a plot, Yi Pang-wŏn launched the First Strife of Princes, killing Chŏng To-jŏn and his own half-brothers. Horrified by the fratricide, Taejo immediately abdicated in favor of his second son, Yi Pang-gwa (King Jeongjong), and retired to Hamhung, where he refused to see Yi Pang-wŏn again. Jeongjong later abdicated in favor of the victorious Yi Pang-wŏn, who became King Taejong.

## Terms
- **Wihwado Retreat**: Yi Sŏnggye's 1388 decision to turn his army back from the Ming border and seize control of the Goryeo government, a pivotal coup.
- **Sinjin Faction**: A reform-minded faction of scholar-officials in late Goryeo that supported alliance with Ming China.
- **Gwonmun Faction**: The old aristocratic families and generals in late Goryeo, some of whom supported the Yuan dynasty.
- **First Strife of Princes (1398)**: A violent succession conflict in which Taejo's son Yi Pang-wŏn killed rivals, including the crown prince, leading to Taejo's abdication.
- **Hamhung Chasa**: A Korean term meaning "a person who never comes back," originating from the story of Taejo refusing to meet his son's emissaries after retiring to Hamhung.
- **Wangshin**: A "king god" in Korean shamanism; Taejo is worshipped as one.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historical Legacy**: Some scholars, particularly in North Korea, view Taejo as a traitor to the Goryeo regime, while others see him as a decisive ruler who saved the nation from an obsolete system and foreign threats.
- **Ming-Joseon Genealogy Dispute**: An error in Ming records, which misidentified Taejo's lineage and accused him of killing Goryeo kings, caused diplomatic friction for over 200 years until it was formally corrected in the late 16th century.
- **The Hamhung Emissaries**: While tradition holds that Taejo executed his son's emissaries, recent studies suggest they died in regional revolts, not by his order.
