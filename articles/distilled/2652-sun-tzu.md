# Sun Tzu

## Overview
Sun Tzu is a legendary Chinese military strategist and philosopher, traditionally credited as the author of *The Art of War*, one of history's most influential texts on strategy. His historical existence is debated, but the work attributed to him has profoundly shaped military, political, and business thinking across East Asia and the world for centuries.

## Timeline
- **c. 544–496 BC** — Traditional lifetime of Sun Tzu (Sun Wu), as placed by historian Sima Qian.
- **506 BC** — Sun Tzu is said to have led the state of Wu to victory against Chu at the Battle of Boju.
- **c. 97 BC** — Sima Qian writes the earliest biography of Sun Tzu in the *Shiji* (*Records of the Grand Historian*).
- **12th century** — Song dynasty scholar Ye Shi begins doubting Sun Tzu's historicity, noting his absence from the earlier *Zuo Zhuan*.
- **1972** — Bamboo slips containing a version of *The Art of War* and the lost text *Sun Bin's Military Methods* are discovered at Yinque Shan.

## Body

### Historical Existence and Biography
The earliest account of Sun Tzu comes from Sima Qian's *Shiji* (c. 97 BC), which places him as a minister to King Helü of Wu. This biography includes the famous story of Sun Tzu training the king's concubines into soldiers by executing two favored concubines who disobeyed orders. However, his historical existence is uncertain. The *Zuo Zhuan*, a detailed contemporary chronicle of the Spring and Autumn period, does not mention Sun Tzu despite covering the Battle of Boju. The name "Sun Wu" does not appear in any text prior to the *Shiji* and may be a descriptive cognomen. Furthermore, the earliest parts of *The Art of War* likely date to at least a century after his supposed lifetime, containing anachronisms like crossbows and philosophical ideas from the later Warring States period.

### The Art of War
*The Art of War* is a Classical Chinese text from the Warring States period (475–221 BC), a time of intense conflict that fostered strategic writing. The work consists of 13 chapters outlining theories of battle, advocating for diplomacy, and incorporating Taoist rhetoric that favors frugality and avoiding direct confrontation. The 1972 discovery of bamboo slips at Yinque Shan, sealed between 134 and 118 BC, provided an early version of the text, bolstering the scholarly consensus that it was composed in the early Warring States period. The same discovery included *Sun Bin's Military Methods*, a lost work by a later, historically verified military author. This resolved the long confusion between Sun Wu and Sun Bin, both referred to as "Sun Tzu," and suggests the two texts may represent a single, developing intellectual tradition.

### Legacy and Influence
*The Art of War* became a foundational military text in East Asia. It was a lead text in the *Seven Military Classics* anthology formalized in 1080 and was introduced to Japan in 760 AD, influencing its unification. The earliest known commentary was by the warlord Cao Cao in the 3rd century AD. The text was first translated into French in 1772 and into English in 1910. In the 20th century, leaders like Mao Zedong, Võ Nguyên Giáp, and Norman Schwarzkopf Jr. drew inspiration from it. Modern Chinese scholars and strategists explicitly rely on its principles, and its applications are widely cited in business, espionage, and popular culture.

## Terms
- **Sun Tzu**: Honorific meaning "Master Sun," the traditional name for the author of *The Art of War*.
- **Sun Wu**: The purported birth name of Sun Tzu.
- **The Art of War**: A Classical Chinese military strategy text traditionally attributed to Sun Tzu.
- **Warring States period**: Era of Chinese history (475–221 BC) marked by intense interstate warfare, during which *The Art of War* was likely composed.
- **Spring and Autumn period**: Earlier era of Chinese history (771–476 BC) during which Sun Tzu is traditionally placed.
- **Shiji**: *Records of the Grand Historian*, a foundational Chinese historical text by Sima Qian (c. 97 BC) containing the earliest biography of Sun Tzu.
- **Zuo Zhuan**: A detailed historical chronicle of the Spring and Autumn period that does not mention Sun Tzu.
- **Sun Bin**: A later, historically verified military strategist and author of *Military Methods*, whose rediscovered work helped distinguish him from Sun Wu.
- **Seven Military Classics**: An anthology of Chinese military texts formalized in 1080, with *The Art of War* as a lead text.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the historical existence of Sun Tzu. Scholars question whether "Sun Wu" was a real person or a legendary figure, noting the lack of contemporary records and the anachronisms in the text attributed to him. The discovery of Sun Bin's work clarified that two different authors were both called "Sun Tzu," but it also supports the theory that the historical figure of Sun Wu may have been inspired by or conflated with the later, verified Sun Bin. The exact date of composition for *The Art of War* remains settled only in broad terms (Warring States period), with the 1972 discovery providing a firm *terminus ante quem* (latest possible date) of 118 BC.
