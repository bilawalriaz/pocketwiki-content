# Wood

## Overview
Wood is a natural composite material found as secondary xylem in the stems and roots of trees and other woody plants. Composed primarily of cellulose fibers embedded in a lignin and hemicellulose matrix, it provides mechanical support and water transport in living plants. Humans have exploited wood since prehistoric times for fuel, tools, shelter, and construction, and it remains a vital renewable resource for construction, paper production, and engineered materials.

## Timeline
- **c. 395–400 million years ago**: Earliest known plants to grow wood appear (New Brunswick discovery)
- **c. 15,000 BC**: Hunters use wooden and bone spear-launchers
- **Neolithic period**: Longhouses built primarily of wood in Europe
- **Early 20th century**: Wood science emerges as a distinct field of study
- **2000–2021**: Global roundwood production rises from 3.5 to 4 billion m³
- **2023**: Nearly 4 billion cubic meters of wood harvested globally
- **2025**: Worldwide forest growing stock reaches ~630 billion cubic metres

## Body

### Structure and Composition
Wood is a heterogeneous, hygroscopic, cellular, and anisotropic (orthotropic) material. Its cell walls contain microfibrils of cellulose (40–50%) and hemicellulose (15–25%) impregnated with lignin (15–30%). Coniferous (softwood) species consist mainly of tracheids, making them more uniform than hardwoods, which have complex vessel-based water-conducting systems. Hardwoods are classified as ring-porous (large earlywood vessels, e.g., oak, ash) or diffuse-porous (evenly distributed vessels, e.g., maple, birch).

### Growth Characteristics
Trees increase in diameter through secondary growth—cell division in the vascular cambium (lateral meristem) producing new woody layers. Distinct seasonal differences create growth rings: earlywood (springwood, wider, lighter) and latewood (summerwood, denser, darker). Ring width decreases as trees mature and trunks widen. Knots form from enclosed branch bases, disrupting grain direction and reducing structural strength, particularly under tension.

### Heartwood and Sapwood
All wood begins as sapwood—the living outer layer conducting water and storing nutrients. Heartwood (duramen) forms through chemical transformation, becoming more decay-resistant. Heartwood often appears darker due to extractive deposits, though color doesn't necessarily indicate strength differences. Sapwood is typically stronger than heartwood in young trees due to fewer knots, but this relationship reverses in mature trees.

### Physical Properties
Wood's hygroscopic nature means it retains 8–16% moisture when air-dried. Water content softens wood; drying significantly increases strength, especially crushing strength and modulus of rupture. Specific gravity (ratio of wood density to water density) is the key quality indicator, correlating with both pulp yield and lumber strength. Density varies by species, age, site conditions, and position within the tree.

### Chemical Composition
Wood contains ~50% carbon, 42% oxygen, 6% hydrogen, and 1% nitrogen by weight, plus trace elements. Beyond cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, wood contains extractives—low molecular weight compounds (fats, terpenes, phenolics) that influence color, durability, and processing behavior. Hardwood lignin derives from sinapyl and coniferyl alcohol; softwood lignin primarily from coniferyl alcohol.

### Uses and Applications
Wood serves as fuel, construction material, pulp source, and raw material for engineered products (glulam, plywood, MDF). It's used in furniture, tools, musical instruments, and artistic works. Global production reached 4 billion m³ in 2021, with wood fuel comprising 49% of total harvest. Engineered wood products are increasingly important in construction.

## Terms
- **Orthotropic**: Having three mutually perpendicular planes of symmetry; describing wood's directional properties
- **Vascular cambium**: Lateral meristem producing secondary xylem (wood) inward and phloem outward
- **Earlywood/Springwood**: Lighter, wider-celled portion of growth ring formed early in growing season
- **Latewood/Summerwood**: Denser, darker portion of growth ring formed later in growing season
- **Heartwood (Duramen)**: Inner, chemically transformed, decay-resistant wood at trunk center
- **Sapwood (Alburnum)**: Outer, living wood conducting water and storing nutrients
- **Specific Gravity**: Ratio of wood density to water density; key quality indicator
- **Ring-porous**: Hardwood with large earlywood vessels forming distinct rings (e.g., oak)
- **Diffuse-porous**: Hardwood with evenly distributed vessels throughout growth ring (e.g., maple)
- **Extractives**: Non-structural organic compounds affecting wood color, durability, and processing

## Debates and Open Questions
The exact mechanisms determining earlywood and latewood formation remain unclear, with growth rate alone insufficient to explain variations. Whether heartwood cells truly die during formation is debated, as they can still chemically react to decay organisms. The relationship between growth rate and wood quality differs between ring-porous and diffuse-porous species, with optimal growth rates varying by type. The role of symbiotic bacteria in cellulose degradation remains poorly understood.
