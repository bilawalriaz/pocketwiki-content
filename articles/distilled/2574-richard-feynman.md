# Richard Feynman

## Overview
Richard Feynman (1918–1988) was an American theoretical physicist whose work fundamentally shaped 20th-century physics. He shared the 1965 Nobel Prize for his contributions to quantum electrodynamics (QED) and is celebrated for his intuitive, pictorial approach to complex problems, his influential role in science education, and his charismatic public persona.

- **1986** — Served on the Rogers Commission investigating the Challenger disaster.
- **February 15, 1988** — Died in Los Angeles, California.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born to Jewish parents in Queens, Feynman was encouraged by his father to question orthodox thinking. He showed early talent for engineering and mathematics, teaching himself calculus before college. After being denied admission to Columbia University due to a Jewish quota, he attended MIT, switching from mathematics to physics. He completed his Ph.D. at Princeton in 1942, where his thesis on the principle of least action laid the groundwork for the path integral formulation of quantum mechanics.

### Manhattan Project and Early Career
During World War II, Feynman was recruited for the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos, where he worked in Hans Bethe's theoretical division. He contributed to fission bomb yield calculations and developed safety procedures for uranium enrichment facilities. After the war, he joined Cornell University. There, grappling with post-war depression and the "infinities" plaguing quantum electrodynamics (QED), he developed his intuitive, diagram-based approach to the theory.

### Nobel Prize-Winning Work in QED
At the 1947 Shelter Island Conference, experimental results like the Lamb shift demanded a new theoretical framework. While Julian Schwinger and Shin'ichirō Tomonaga developed a covariant formulation of QED, Feynman independently developed a visually intuitive method using diagrams to represent particle interactions. Initially met with confusion, his approach was later shown by Freeman Dyson to be mathematically equivalent and more practical. Feynman, Schwinger, and Tomonaga shared the 1965 Nobel Prize for this work.

### Later Scientific Contributions
At Caltech, Feynman made significant contributions across multiple fields. He provided a quantum explanation for the superfluidity of liquid helium. With Murray Gell-Mann, he developed the V-A theory of the weak nuclear force. He proposed the parton model to explain high-energy particle collisions, which complemented the quark model. He also pioneered concepts in quantum computing and, in a famous 1959 lecture, "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom," outlined the potential for nanotechnology.

### Public Figure and Educator
Feynman was a gifted teacher and communicator. His *Feynman Lectures on Physics* remain a classic resource. He popularized science through books like *QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter* and the anecdotal *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!*. He gained widespread public recognition for his role on the Rogers Commission, where his demonstration with an O-ring and ice water famously illustrated the cause of the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster.

## Terms
- **Quantum Electrodynamics (QED):** The relativistic quantum field theory of electrodynamics, describing how light and matter interact.
- **Feynman Diagram:** A pictorial representation of the mathematical expressions governing the behavior of subatomic particles.
- **Path Integral Formulation:** A formulation of quantum mechanics that sums over all possible histories of a system.
- **Renormalization:** A mathematical procedure in quantum field theory to handle infinities that arise in calculations, yielding finite, physical results.
- **Lamb Shift:** A small difference in energy between two energy levels of the hydrogen atom, which QED successfully explained.
- **Parton Model:** A model of hadrons (like protons) as composed of point-like constituents (partons), later identified with quarks and gluons.
- **Superfluidity:** A phase of matter characterized by the complete absence of viscosity when flowing, observed in liquid helium at very low temperatures.
- **Cargo Cult Science:** A term Feynman used for research that follows the apparent form and ceremony of scientific investigation but lacks its integrity and self-criticism.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Parton vs. Quark Model:** The relationship between Feynman's parton model and Gell-Mann's quark model was initially murky. Gell-Mann derisively called partons "put-ons." The models were later understood as complementary descriptions of the same underlying physics.
- **Pedagogical Impact:** The effectiveness of Feynman's undergraduate lectures is debated. While older students and faculty found them inspirational, the intended first-year audience often found them intimidating.
- **Personal Conduct:** Feynman's behavior, including alleged sexism and stories of seducing women, has been a subject of criticism and debate, contrasting with his scientific legacy.
