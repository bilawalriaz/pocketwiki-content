# Umberto Eco

## Overview

Umberto Eco (1932–2016) was an Italian medievalist, philosopher, semiotician, novelist, and cultural critic. He is best known internationally for his 1980 novel *The Name of the Rose*, a historical mystery combining semiotics, biblical analysis, medieval studies, and literary theory, and for his 1988 novel *Foucault's Pendulum*. Eco wrote prolifically across fiction, academic theory, journalism, and children's books, and his work on semiotics—the study of signs and meaning—helped establish it as a distinct academic field in Europe.

## Body

### Early life and education

Eco was born in Alessandria, Piedmont, in 1932. The spread of Italian fascism influenced his childhood; at age ten he won a provincial award for a writing prompt on dying for Mussolini. During World War II he moved with his mother to a Piedmontese mountain village, which was liberated in 1945, exposing him to American comic books, the European Resistance, and the Holocaust. He received a Salesian education. His father urged him to become a lawyer, but Eco entered the University of Turin, writing his thesis on medieval philosopher Thomas Aquinas under Luigi Pareyson, earning his philosophy degree in 1954.

### Medieval aesthetics and early career (1954–1964)

After graduating, Eco worked for state broadcaster RAI in Milan producing cultural programming. Following his first book in 1956, he became an assistant lecturer at Turin, then completed 18 months of military service in 1958. In 1959, publisher Valentino Bompiani recruited him to edit a series, having noticed Eco's pamphlet *Filosofi in libertà* published under the pseudonym Daedalus. That year Eco published *Sviluppo dell'estetica medievale*, a scholarly monograph building on his Aquinas work. He earned his *libera docenza* (teaching qualification) in aesthetics in 1961, became a lecturer in 1963, and moved to the University of Milan as lecturer in Architecture in 1964.

### Semiotics and popular culture (1961–1975)

Eco's 1961 essay "Phenomenology of Mike Bongiorno" critically analyzed a popular quiz show host, arguing Bongiorno "represents an ideal that nobody need strive to reach because everyone is already at his level." The essay brought him public notoriety and was collected in *Diario minimo* (1963). In 1962, *Opera aperta* argued that literary texts are "open"—fields of meaning that are internally dynamic and psychologically engaged—rather than closed strings with a single unequivocal line. Eco reached these positions through language study and semiotics, not psychology or historical analysis. His 1964 book *Apocalittici e integrati* analyzed mass communication sociologically.

From 1965 to 1969, Eco was Professor of Visual Communications at the University of Florence, where he delivered the influential lecture "Towards a Semiological Guerrilla Warfare," coining the term "semiological guerrilla" and influencing guerrilla television and culture jamming. His approach became known as "interpretative semiotics," first elaborated in *La struttura assente* (1968). He became Professor of Semiotics at Milan Polytechnic in 1969, spending his first year as visiting professor at New York University. In 1971 he joined the University of Bologna, spent 1972 as a Fulbright scholar at Northwestern, and was promoted to Professor of Semiotics in 1975 after publishing *A Theory of Semiotics*.

### Major novels (1975–2000)

Eco drew on his medievalist background for *The Name of the Rose* (1980), a historical mystery set in a 14th-century monastery where Franciscan friar William of Baskerville investigates murders. The novel contains metatextual references requiring reader detective work. The title references a Latin verse: "the ancient rose remains in name; we hold [only] the bare names." The character Jorge of Burgos pays tribute to Jorge Luis Borges, a major influence; the labyrinthine library alludes to Borges's "The Library of Babel." William of Baskerville evokes both William of Ockham and Sherlock Holmes. The novel was later adapted into a film and television mini-series.

*Foucault's Pendulum* (1988) follows three under-employed editors who invent a conspiracy theory called "The Plan"—an intricate plot by a secret order descended from the Knights Templar to take over the world. The game turns dangerous when outsiders believe the men have discovered the secret to the Templars' lost treasure.

Eco published *The Limits of Interpretation* in 1990. His third novel, *The Island of the Day Before* (1994), set in the 17th century, concerns a man stranded on a ship within sight of an island he believes lies across the international date-line; trapped by his inability to swim, he spends the book reminiscing. His 1995 essay "Ur-Fascism" described fascism as a "fuzzy" set of recurring traits, listing fourteen features of "Eternal Fascism"; it gained renewed attention in the 21st century.

### Anthropology of the West and later work (1988–2016)

In 1988, Eco founded the Department of Media Studies at the University of the Republic of San Marino, and in 1992 the Institute of Communication Disciplines at Bologna. He created an unusual program, Anthropology of the West from the perspective of non-Westerners, based on an idea from Alain le Pichon, resulting in conferences in Guangzhou (1991), Timbuktu, Bologna, Brussels, Paris, Goa, and Beijing (2007), and the book *The Unicorn and the Dragon* on knowledge creation in China and Europe. His interest in East–West dialogue correlated with his interest in the international auxiliary language Esperanto.

From 1992–1993, Eco was the Charles Eliot Norton Professor of Poetry at Harvard; his lectures became *Six Walks in the Fictional Woods* (1994). He returned to semiotics with *Kant and the Platypus* (1997), which he reputedly warned fans away from as "a hard-core book." From 2001–2002 he was Weidenfeld Visiting Professor at Oxford.

*Baudolino* (2000) follows a polyglot Piedmontese scholar who saves Byzantine historian Niketas Choniates during the sack of Constantinople in the Fourth Crusade; a self-proclaimed liar, he recounts his life from peasant childhood through his role as adopted son of Emperor Frederick Barbarossa to a mission to find the mythical realm of Prester John. *The Mysterious Flame of Queen Loana* (2005) concerns an antique bookseller emerging from a coma who must choose between recovering his past and living his future. *The Prague Cemetery* (2010) narrates the rise of modern antisemitism through the Dreyfus affair and *The Protocols of the Elders of Zion*. *Numero Zero* (2015), set in 1992, satirizes Italy's kickback culture and fascist legacy. Eco criticized social media, saying it gives "legions of idiots the right to speak" with the same authority as a Nobel Prize winner.

### Influences, reception, and legacy

Eco befriended the Neoavanguardia (Gruppo '63), avant-garde artists and writers, at RAI. In 1971 he co-founded *Versus*, a semiotics journal that helped establish semiotics as an academic field; most well-known European semioticians published there. He collaborated with artists and philosophers on tongue-in-cheek texts about 'pataphysics, an imaginary science. He cited James Joyce and Jorge Luis Borges as his two greatest modern influences. He did not consider hypertexts valid for novels, arguing multimedia added nothing to cultural value. He translated Raymond Queneau's *Exercices de style* and Gérard de Nerval's *Sylvie* into Italian.

Critics divided sharply on Eco. Philosopher Roger Scruton attacked his "rhetoric of technicality," while art historian Nicholas Penny accused him of pandering to "relevance." Others praised his levity and encyclopedic knowledge; Frank Kermode called *The Name of the Rose* "a wonderfully interesting book." Gilles Deleuze cited *The Open Work* approvingly in *Difference and Repetition*. An obituary described Eco as "the critical conscience at the center of Italian humanistic culture." In 2017, a retrospective appeared in the Library of Living Philosophers series.

### Personal life, religion, and death

In September 1962, Eco married Renate Ramge, a German graphic designer, with whom he had a son and daughter. During university he ceased to believe in God and left the Catholic Church, later co-founding the Italian skeptic organization CICAP. He maintained a 30,000-volume library in Milan and 20,000 volumes near Urbino. He died of pancreatic cancer on 19 February 2016, aged 84, having been professor emeritus at Bologna since 2008.

## Terms

- **Semiotics**: The study of signs, symbols, and meaning-making processes.
- **Open text**: Eco's term for literary works that function as dynamic fields of multiple meanings, engaging the reader actively.
- **Closed text**: A text limiting understanding to a single, unequivocal line; Eco considered these least rewarding.
- **Semiological guerrilla**: Eco's concept of tactical resistance against mainstream mass media culture, influencing guerrilla television and culture jamming.
- **Ur-Fascism / Eternal Fascism**: Eco's term for a "fuzzy" set of fourteen recurring political and cultural traits he believed comprise fascist ideologies.
- **Intertextuality**: The interconnectedness of all literary works; Eco's fiction is full of subtle, often multilingual references to literature and history.
- **Neoavanguardia (Gruppo '63)**: Avant-garde artists, painters, musicians, and writers Eco befriended at RAI who influenced his career.
- **'Pataphysics**: An imaginary science about which Eco collaborated with artists and philosophers on tongue-in-cheek texts.
- **Libera docenza**: An Italian teaching qualification in a university subject.

## Debates and open questions

Critics divided sharply over whether Eco's theorizing was brilliant or an "unnecessary vanity project obsessing over minutiae" (Scruton attacked his esoteric tendencies; Penny accused him of pandering to "relevance"). Others praised his ability to make abstruse academic subjects accessible. Eco's fiction simultaneously stunned critics with its complexity and popularity. His 1995 "Ur-Fascism" essay, listing fourteen properties of fascism, has been debated and gained renewed attention in the 21st century.
