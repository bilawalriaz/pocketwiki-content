# Music

## Overview

Music is the arrangement of sound to create some combination of form, harmony, melody, rhythm, or otherwise expressive content. It is generally agreed to be a cultural universal present in all human societies, though definitions vary widely, and there is no consensus on its necessary elements. Music plays a key role in social events, religious ceremonies, and entertainment, and the culture surrounding it extends into academic study, journalism, philosophy, psychology, and therapy. The music industry encompasses songwriters, performers, sound engineers, producers, and distributors of instruments, sheet music, and recordings.

## Body

### Etymology and terminology

The modern English word *music* came into use in the 1630s, descending from Middle English *musike*, Old French *musique*, Latin *mūsica*, and ultimately Ancient Greek *mousiké technē* ("art of the Muses"). The Muses were nine Greek deities presiding over the arts and sciences; Polyhymnia became most associated with music. The Western universal conception of music is not shared worldwide: languages such as Indonesian (*musik*) and Shona (*musakazo*) recently adopted words for this scope, while pre-Western-contact Japan and China had no single equivalent word. The Chinese word *yue* shares a character with *le* (joy) and originally referred to all arts. The Hindi *sangita* properly refers to art music, and Arabic *musiqi* usually means instrumental and metric music, while *khandan* identifies vocal and improvised music.

### Origins and prehistory

Scholars debate whether music developed before, after, or simultaneously with language, and whether it resulted from natural selection or was a byproduct (a "spandrel") of evolution. Charles Darwin proposed in 1871 that music arose as sexual selection via mating calls—a theory heavily criticized but still promoted by some 21st-century scholars. Other theories include music assisting labor organization, long-distance communication, communication with the divine, community cohesion, or predator defense. The disputed Divje Babe flute (a perforated cave bear femur, at least 40,000 years old) may or may not be a musical instrument. The earliest widely accepted instruments are eight bone and mammoth-ivory flutes from Germany's Swabian Jura, dated to the Aurignacian (Upper Paleolithic).

### Antiquity and Asian cultures

Egyptian musical instruments are attested from the Old Kingdom (harps, flutes, double clarinets), with percussion, lyres, and lutes added by the Middle Kingdom. Egyptian folk music, including Sufi *dhikr* rituals, is the closest contemporary genre to ancient Egyptian music. In ancient Greece, music was a main subject taught to children from age six, considered important for soul development; instruments included the double-reed *aulos* and the lyre (especially the *kithara*). Greek musical modes became the basis for Western religious and classical music. Indian classical music is among the oldest traditions, with Indus Valley sculptures showing dance and a seven-holed flute; the *Rigveda* contains musical notation. It is monophonic, based on a single melody line (*raga*) organized through *talas*. Indonesian music, formed since the Bronze Age (2nd–3rd centuries BCE), features percussion (kendang, gongs) and the gamelan ensemble. Chinese classical music spans about 3,000 years and is pentatonic-diatonic, with twelve notes to an octave.

### Western classical

The medieval era (500–1400) began with monophonic chanting in Catholic services; notation was introduced by the Church to standardize religious music across the empire. The only pre-800 written repertory is Gregorian chant. Renaissance music (c. 1400–1600) focused more on secular themes; the printing press (c. 1450) made sheet music cheaper, spreading styles faster. Composers wrote richly polyphonic sacred music; the Franco-Flemish composers held important positions throughout Europe. The Baroque era (1600–1750) began with the first operas; polyphonic contrapuntal music remained important, and forms like the fugue, invention, sonata, and concerto were created. The Classical period (1730–1820) imitated Greek and Roman ideals of balance and proportion; homophony (a prominent melody with chordal accompaniment) replaced Baroque complexity, and the fortepiano replaced the harpsichord. Public concerts developed, allowing composers to survive without aristocratic employment. Romantic music (c. 1820–1900) emphasized emotion, individualism, and nature; composers expanded classical forms and explored chromatic alterations of tonality, while the Industrial Revolution created better instruments and larger orchestras.

### 20th and 21st century

Radio broadcasting and gramophone records replaced sheet music as the main way new compositions became known, allowing lower-income people to hear music they could not afford to attend live. World War I influenced composers to explore darker, harsher sounds. Igor Stravinsky, Arnold Schoenberg, and John Cage were influential. Sound recording enabled new subgenres (acousmatic and *musique concrète*), and multitrack recording allowed overdubbing, creating sounds impossible live. Jazz originated in the late 19th–early 20th centuries in African American communities in the Southern US, evident in blue notes, improvisation, polyrhythms, syncopation, and swung notes. Rock developed in the 1950s from rock and roll, rockabilly, blues, and country. The 1980s brought digital synthesizers and drum machines; the 1990s brought computerized hardware and software; by the 2020s, bedroom producers could create electronic dance music at home.

### Creation: composition, performance, improvisation

Composition is the act of creating music; in Western classical music it includes creating notation (a score), while in popular music it may involve a lead sheet (melody, lyrics, chord progression). Notation does not specify all elements, so performers make interpretive choices; the standard body of choices at a given time and place is *performance practice*. Aleatoric (chance-based) music is associated with John Cage, Morton Feldman, and Witold Lutosławski. Performance is the physical expression of music; classical performers may gradually change phrasing or tempo, while popular and traditional performers have more freedom. Improvisation—spontaneous creation within a harmonic framework—is central to blues, jazz, and jazz fusion, and was an important skill in the Baroque and Classical eras (e.g., improvised ornaments and cadenzas), though it declined in 20th-century institutionalized Western art music.

### Elements

The elements of music include pitch, rhythm, melody, harmony, texture, timbre, dynamics, expression, articulation, form, and structure, though lists vary by curriculum (Australia, UK, US identify different sets). Pitch reflects whether a sound is "higher" or "lower." Melody is a series of pitches in succession, often using scales or modes. Harmony refers to pitches played or sung together, creating chords; the key determines the "home note" (tonic). Rhythm is the arrangement of sounds and silences in time, organized into meters (e.g., 2/4, 3/4, 4/4). Texture describes how melodic, rhythmic, and harmonic materials combine: monophony (single melody), heterophony (same melody with variations), polyphony (multiple independent lines), and homophony (melody with accompaniment). Timbre ("color") is what makes sounds of the same pitch and loudness differ (e.g., oboe vs. piano). Expression involves phrasing, vibrato, dynamics, tempo fluctuations, and articulation. Form describes overall structure, such as AABA thirty-two-bar form or verse-chorus form.

### Philosophy, psychology, and sociology

The philosophy of music examines fundamental questions about music's definition, relationship to mind, history, emotions, and meaning. Ancient Greek aesthetics explored mathematical and cosmological dimensions; in the 18th century, focus shifted to the experience of hearing music (Baumgarten, Kant). Plato proposed in *The Republic* that music directly affects the soul and should be regulated by the state; Confucius believed music and rites were harmonious with nature. Modern music psychology is empirical, studying musical behavior and experience. Cognitive neuroscience uses fMRI, TMS, MEG, EEG, and PET to study brain mechanisms. Evolutionary musicology debates whether music was adaptive (Darwin's protolanguage view) or a byproduct ("auditory cheesecake"). Cultural effects show preferences biased toward culturally familiar traditions. Sociologically, music is a participatory, community-based activity; a "high culture"/"low culture" divide separated classical from jazz, blues, and country, though musicologists note a "redrawing of high-low cultural-aesthetic boundaries" in the 20th century. Women have been underrepresented as composers, producers, conductors, and rock instrumentalists, though they dominated music education in the late 19th–20th centuries.

### Media, technology, education, and therapy

Live music can be broadcast or recorded since the 20th century; talking pictures eliminated many moviehouse orchestra jobs in the 1920s. The Internet and streaming services transformed music access and distribution, enabling "prosumers" who both create and consume content. Generative AI systems (Music Metacreation) are increasingly used to create music. Music education ranges from non-institutional (elementary through university) to institutional (conservatories, universities offering degrees in performance, history, theory, composition, education). Musicology has three sub-disciplines (systematic, historical, comparative/ethnomusicology); music theory studies music technically; zoomusicology studies non-human animal music; ethnomusicology studies non-Western music. Music therapy is an interpersonal process using music to improve health, used for psychiatric disorders, physical disabilities, and aging; Al-Farabi (10th century) described vocal music's effect on feelings, and Robert Burton's *The Anatomy of Melancholy* (17th century) called music "a sovereign remedy against despair and melancholy."

## Terms

- **Monophony**: A single melody with no accompaniment or harmony part.
- **Polyphony**: Multiple independent melody lines interweaving simultaneously.
- **Homophony**: A clear melody supported by chordal accompaniment.
- **Lead sheet**: Basic notation of melody, lyrics, and chord progression used in popular music.
- **Raga**: The single melody line basis of Indian classical music, rhythmically organized through *talas*.
- **Gamelan**: Indonesian ensemble of tuned percussion instruments (metallophones, drums, gongs, spike fiddles, bamboo suling).
- **Aleatoric music**: Music making heavy use of randomness and chance.
- **Performance practice**: The standard body of choices and techniques present at a given time and place.
- **Psychoacoustics**: The scientific study of psychological and physiological responses to sound.
- **Ethnomusicology**: The study of non-Western music and the cultural study of music.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether music developed before, after, or simultaneously with language, and whether it was the intentional result of natural selection or a byproduct ("spandrel") of evolution. Darwin's 1871 sexual selection theory has been heavily criticized for inconsistencies with other sexual selection methods, though some 21st-century scholars promote it. The Divje Babe flute's status as a musical instrument is disputed—it may be an object formed by animals. There is no consensus on what the necessary elements of music are, and lists of "rudimentary" versus "perceptual" elements vary; researchers note that perceptual elements still require further study. Whether animals have music is debated: some argue bird songs follow repetition-transformation principles, while others contend that "it is a human being who decides what is and is not musical," making music uniquely human.
