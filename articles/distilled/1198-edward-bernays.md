# Edward Bernays

## Overview
Edward L. Bernays (1891–1995) was an Austrian-American public relations pioneer who systematized the use of psychology, sociology, and crowd theory to shape public opinion for corporations, politicians, and governments. His campaigns—from promoting women’s smoking as “Torches of Freedom” to engineering the 1954 CIA-backed coup in Guatemala—established modern propaganda techniques that blurred the line between persuasion and manipulation, raising enduring questions about autonomy and democracy.

## Timeline
- **1891** — Born in Vienna to a Jewish family; nephew of Sigmund Freud
- **1912** — Graduated Cornell; began journalism career
- **1917** — Co-edited *Medical Review of Reviews*; promoted controversial play *Damaged Goods*
- **1918–1919** — Served in U.S. Committee on Public Information (CPI) during WWI
- **1923** — Published *Crystallizing Public Opinion*
- **1928** — Published *Propaganda*
- **1929** — Launched “Torches of Freedom” campaign for Lucky Strikes
- **1939** — Publicity director for New York World’s Fair
- **1954** — Facilitated CIA-backed coup in Guatemala via United Fruit Company
- **1995** — Died at age 103 in Cambridge, Massachusetts

## Body

### Early Career and Intellectual Foundations
Bernays leveraged his familial connection to Sigmund Freud to ground his approach in psychoanalysis and crowd psychology. Influenced by thinkers like Gustave Le Bon, Wilfred Trotter, and Walter Lippmann, he viewed the masses as irrational and susceptible to manipulation by an elite few. This philosophy underpinned his belief that skilled practitioners could engineer consent without public awareness.

### Medical Editor and Theater Promotion
Before entering public relations, Bernays co-edited *Medical Review of Reviews*, where he championed controversial causes such as sex education through the play *Damaged Goods*. He also worked as a press agent for theatrical productions, using emotional appeals and symbolic associations—techniques that foreshadowed his later corporate strategies.

### Government Service and Psychological Warfare
During World War I, Bernays served in the CPI’s Bureau of Latin-American Affairs, conducting what he termed “psychological warfare.” This experience taught him that wartime propaganda methods could be applied to peacetime commercial and political goals—an insight central to his post-war practice.

### Corporate Campaigns and Techniques
Bernays pioneered the use of third-party endorsements and scientific research to legitimize products. For bacon, he enlisted physicians to endorse heavy breakfasts; for Ivory soap, he orchestrated grassroots enthusiasm. His most famous campaign, “Torches of Freedom,” encouraged women to smoke publicly by framing cigarettes as symbols of liberation—an effort that successfully shifted social norms.

### Political Influence and Cold War Activities
Bernays advised multiple U.S. presidents and political figures, including Calvin Coolidge and Herbert Hoover. During WWII and the Cold War, he worked with the Office of War Information and the U.S. Information Agency. His most controversial political act was aiding the United Fruit Company in vilifying Guatemala’s democratically elected government, contributing to the 1954 CIA-backed coup.

### Philosophy and Legacy
Bernays believed propaganda was inevitable and that democratic societies allowed for competing narratives, unlike authoritarian regimes. He advocated for centralized control and planning, positioning public relations as a tool for social order. Though criticized for undermining autonomy, he remained unapologetic, arguing that competent professionals should guide public sentiment.

## Terms
- **Public Relations Counsel**: Bernays’ self-styled role as a strategic advisor who manages organizational image and influences public perception.
- **Engineering of Consent**: Bernays’ term for the deliberate shaping of public opinion through psychological and sociological techniques.
- **Torches of Freedom**: Bernays’ 1929 campaign encouraging women to smoke cigarettes as symbols of feminist emancipation.
- **Third Party Technique**: Strategy of using independent voices (e.g., doctors, celebrities) to promote a product or idea without revealing corporate sponsorship.
- **Propaganda**: Bernays’ neutral definition of organized persuasion aimed at influencing group behavior and beliefs.
- **Crowd Psychology**: Theory that individuals in groups behave irrationally and can be manipulated en masse.
- **Psychological Warfare**: Use of propaganda and misinformation to influence enemy or neutral populations during conflict.
- **Monroe Doctrine**: Referenced by Bernays as a precedent for U.S. intervention in Latin American affairs.
- **Operation PBSuccess**: CIA codename for the 1954 coup in Guatemala, supported by Bernays’ PR efforts.
- **Mass Mind**: Bernays’ concept of collective consciousness shaped by external stimuli rather than individual reasoning.

## Debates and Open Questions
Bernays’ legacy is deeply contested. Supporters praise him as a visionary who professionalized public relations and enabled effective communication between institutions and the public. Critics argue his methods deliberately deceived audiences and eroded democratic discourse. His role in the Guatemalan coup exemplifies concerns about corporate influence over foreign policy. Scholars debate whether his techniques were uniquely manipulative or simply reflected broader trends in 20th-century media and power structures. The extent to which modern PR continues to employ his strategies remains a subject of ongoing scrutiny.
