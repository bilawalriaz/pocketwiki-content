# Niagara Falls

## Overview
Niagara Falls is a group of three waterfalls on the Niagara River, straddling the border between Ontario, Canada, and New York, USA. It is renowned for its immense beauty, its status as a major tourist destination, and its significant role in hydroelectric power generation. The falls were formed by glacial activity and are continuously shaped by erosion, presenting an ongoing challenge to balance natural preservation with commercial and industrial use.

## Timeline
- **c. 10,900 years ago** — Niagara Falls forms as glaciers recede, with the river carving a path over the Niagara Escarpment.
- **1604** — Samuel de Champlain's party reports the falls to him during exploration.
- **1677** — Father Louis Hennepin provides the first European eyewitness description.
- **1801** — Theodosia Burr Alston and Joseph Alston are the first recorded couple to honeymoon there.
- **1848** — An ice jam causes the river and falls to freeze completely for about 40 hours.
- **1885** — New York creates the Niagara Reservation, its first state park; Ontario establishes the Queen Victoria Niagara Falls Park.
- **1895** — A large-scale alternating current (AC) hydroelectric power system is activated.
- **1950** — A U.S.-Canada treaty regulates water diversion to preserve the falls' scenic flow.
- **1969** — The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers temporarily dams the American Falls for study and reinforcement.
- **2013** — Ontario's Niagara Tunnel Project for hydroelectric power is completed.

## Body

### Formation and Characteristics
Niagara Falls was created about 10,000 years ago when meltwater from the Wisconsin glaciation carved a path over the Niagara Escarpment. The falls consist of three sections: the large, crescent-shaped Horseshoe Falls (mostly in Canada), the American Falls, and Bridal Veil Falls (both in the U.S.). The combined falls have the highest flow rate in North America. The water's green color comes from dissolved salts and rock flour generated by erosion. Flow is regulated by the International Control Dam and a 1950 treaty to ensure a scenic "curtain of water" while allowing for hydroelectric diversion.

### Geology and Erosion
The falls are carved through layers of rock: a hard caprock of limestone and dolomite (Lockport Formation) overlies weaker shale (Rochester Formation). The river erodes the soft shale, undercutting the hard caprock, which collapses in chunks. This process has caused the falls to retreat southward about 10.9 km (6.8 mi) from their original location near present-day Queenston and Lewiston. The current erosion rate is about 30 cm (12 in) per year, significantly slower than the historical average due to engineering efforts.

### Preservation and Development
Since the 19th century, efforts have been made to protect the falls from industrialization and over-commercialization. The "Free Niagara" movement, led by figures like Frederick Law Olmsted, advocated for public ownership, leading to the creation of state and provincial parks in 1885. Preservation continues through treaties regulating water diversion and engineering projects to control erosion, such as the 1969 temporary damming of the American Falls. Balancing Olmsted's vision of natural beauty with modern tourism and urban development remains an ongoing challenge.

### Hydroelectric Power
The falls' energy has been harnessed for power since the 18th century. The first hydroelectric station was built in 1881. By 1896, power was being transmitted to Buffalo. Today, Niagara is New York's largest electricity producer, with a capacity of 2.4 GW. Up to 75% of the river's flow is diverted through tunnels to power plants like the Lewiston and Robert Moses facilities. A 1950 treaty mandates minimum water flow over the falls during tourist hours to preserve their appearance.

### Tourism and Daredevils
Tourism became the area's main industry in the 19th century, with the falls becoming a famous honeymoon destination. The Maid of the Mist boat cruise has operated since 1846. The falls also attract daredevils; Annie Edson Taylor was the first to go over in a barrel in 1901. Since then, numerous people have attempted jumps, plunges, and tightrope walks, often with fatal results. Such stunts are now illegal and fined.

## Terms
- **Niagara Escarpment:** A steep cliff or ridge of rock that the Niagara River flows over to create the falls.
- **Caprock:** The hard, erosion-resistant layer of limestone and dolomite (Lockport Formation) that forms the top of the falls.
- **Rock Flour:** Very finely ground rock generated by glacial erosion, which gives the water its green color.
- **International Control Dam:** A weir with movable gates upstream from Horseshoe Falls used to regulate water flow and diversion.
- **Niagara River Treaty (1950):** An agreement between the U.S. and Canada that regulates water diversion to balance power generation with scenic preservation.
- **Hydroelectric Power:** Electricity generated from the energy of falling or flowing water.
- **Alternating Current (AC):** An electric current that reverses direction periodically, a system pioneered for long-distance power transmission from Niagara.
- **Talus:** A pile of broken rock fragments at the base of a cliff, such as that found at the base of the American Falls.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact etymology of "Niagara" is debated, with theories linking it to Indigenous words for "thundering water," "a neck," or a local tribe's name.
- The precise location of Horseshoe Falls is disputed; some sources state it is now entirely in Canada, while others say "most of" it is.
- The impact of tall buildings on the Canadian side on local weather patterns, such as increasing mist days, is debated among studies.
- The long-term future of the falls is a subject of scientific projection; at the current erosion rate, they could cease to exist in about 50,000 years.
