# Mesoamerican architecture

## Overview
Mesoamerican architecture encompasses the monumental building traditions of pre-Columbian civilizations in the region, known for its pyramids, urban planning, and deep integration with religious cosmovision. These structures served as physical embodiments of cultural beliefs, with city layouts and building orientations reflecting mythological and calendrical systems. The architectural styles, while regionally diverse, were interconnected through centuries of cultural exchange, making these sites crucial for understanding pre-Columbian society.

## Timeline
- **c. 1000 BCE** — Early Mesoamerican societies begin constructing monumental architecture.
- **c. 200 BCE** — The Pyramid of the Sun is constructed at Teotihuacan.
- **c. 200 CE** — The Temple of the Feathered Serpent is built at Teotihuacan, featuring the talud-tablero style.
- **c. 250–900 CE** — Classic Period: Flourishing of Maya styles at sites like Palenque, Tikal, and Copán.
- **c. 600–1000 CE** — Puuc style develops in the northern Maya lowlands.
- **1325 CE** — The Aztec city of Tenochtitlan is founded on an island in Lake Texcoco.
- **1521 CE** — Tenochtitlan is destroyed by Spanish conquistadors under Hernán Cortés.
- **2010 CE** — A robot discovers an arch-roofed passageway under the Pyramid of Quetzalcoatl at Teotihuacan.

## Body

### Urban Planning and Cosmovision
Mesoamerican cities were designed as microcosms of the universe, reflecting a religious division between the underworld and the human world. The northern half often contained structures like tombs representing the underworld, while the southern half housed residential and market areas symbolizing life and sustenance. A central plaza, frequently containing a stela (carved stone monument) and a ballcourt, acted as the axis mundi, or world tree, connecting these realms. Architectural orientation was also significant; many structures aligned with celestial events, such as solar positions marking dates in the sacred 260-day calendar, to facilitate agricultural and ritual planning.

### Monumental Structures
The most iconic Mesoamerican structures are pyramids, which are the largest such constructions outside of Ancient Egypt. Temples were typically built atop these pyramids, serving as the closest point to the heavens. The Pyramid of the Sun at Teotihuacan is one of the largest in the Western Hemisphere, standing about 216 feet tall. The Temple of the Feathered Serpent at the same site introduced the talud-tablero style, a platform-and-slope design that became widespread. The Aztec Templo Mayor in Tenochtitlan was enlarged seven times, maintaining its dual symbolism for the gods Tlaloc and Huitzilopochtli.

### Ballcourts and Residential Architecture
Over 1,300 ballcourts have been identified, all sharing a general shape of a long alley flanked by sloping or vertical walls. These courts symbolized a journey between the underworld and the living world and were often located at the symbolic center of cities. Elite residences, or palaces, were typically one-story structures with multiple chambers and interior courtyards, located near city centers. Some, like the North Acropolis at Tikal, also served as burial sites for rulers.

### Building Materials and Technology
Construction relied on abundant manpower and local materials, primarily limestone, which was soft when quarried but hardened later. Mortar and stucco were made from crushed limestone. In central Mexico, volcanic tezontle rock was common. Mesoamerican cultures did not invent the true arch, instead using the corbelled arch, where horizontal stone courses are layered inward. However, some scholars argue that true arches and advanced trapezium truss systems using cast concrete were also employed, as evidenced by discoveries at Teotihuacan.

### Architectural Styles
Styles evolved regionally and chronologically. The megalithic style used large limestone blocks covered in stucco. The talud-tablero style, originating in Teotihuacan, featured a sloped base (talud) with a rectangular panel (tablero). Classic Maya styles are noted for their corbelled arches. The Puuc style, from the northern Maya lowlands, is characterized by plain lower facades and richly decorated upper facades with geometric patterns and masks of the rain god Chaac. The "Toltec" style, seen at Chichén Itzá, features chacmools (reclining stone figures) and Atlantean figures.

## Terms
- **Cosmovision**: The cultural worldview that integrates religious, mythological, and geographical concepts, which directly influenced city layout and building orientation.
- **Talud-tablero**: An architectural style consisting of a sloping plane (talud) topped by a rectangular, framed panel (tablero), first common at Teotihuacan.
- **Corbelled arch**: A "false" arch built by layering horizontal stone courses inward from each side until they meet, supporting less weight than a true arch.
- **Axis mundi**: A symbolic center or connection point between the heavens, earth, and underworld, often represented in Mesoamerican plazas by a stela or ballcourt.
- **Stela**: A carved or inscribed stone slab or monument, often erected in public plazas.
- **Puuc**: An architectural style from the Puuc hills region of the northern Maya lowlands, featuring plain lower walls and ornate upper facades with geometric patterns and Chaac masks.
- **Chacmool**: A type of stone sculpture depicting a reclining figure with its head turned 90 degrees from the body, often found in temples.
- **Tezontle**: A light, porous volcanic rock commonly used as a building material in central Mexico.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The purpose of the light-and-shadow effect on the "El Castillo" pyramid at Chichén Itza during equinoxes is debated; while popularly believed to be intentional, evidence for this specific design intent is lacking.
- The true nature of Mesoamerican arches is contested. Traditional scholarship holds they used only corbelled arches, but recent engineering analysis and archaeological discoveries suggest knowledge of true arches and more advanced trapezium truss systems using concrete.
- The precise function of some structures, like the Pyramid of the Sun, is inferred from artifact finds (e.g., ritual objects) but remains a subject of ongoing research.
