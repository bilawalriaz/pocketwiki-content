# Scramble for Africa

## Overview  
The Scramble for Africa (c. 1881–1914) was the rapid invasion, conquest, and colonisation of nearly the entire African continent by seven Western European powers—Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, Portugal, Spain, and the United Kingdom—driven by the Second Industrial Revolution and the era of "New Imperialism". It was motivated by economic needs (raw materials, surplus capital, open markets), strategic rivalries (naval bases, control of trade routes like the Suez Canal), and national prestige, culminating in the 1884–85 Berlin Conference, which formalised the rules of European colonisation and effective occupation.

## Timeline  
- **1881** — France occupies Tunisia; Britain occupies Egypt
- **1884–85** — Berlin Conference regulates European claims in Africa
- **1885** — Congo Free State established under Leopold II
- **1889** — Fashoda Incident: French and British forces face off in Sudan
- **1899–1902** — Second Boer War; Britain secures South Africa
- **1904–08** — Herero and Maji Maji revolts in German colonies
- **1905** — First Moroccan Crisis; Kaiser Wilhelm II visits Tangier
- **1906** — Algeciras Conference settles First Moroccan Crisis
- **1911** — Second Moroccan Crisis (Agadir Crisis)
- **1914** — Start of WWI halts further expansion
- **1919** — German colonies partitioned among Britain, France, and Belgium
- **1936** — Italy occupies Ethiopia, one of two remaining independent African states

## Body  

### Causes and Motivations  
By the 1870s, Europeans controlled only 10% of Africa, mostly coastal areas. Industrialisation brought steamships, railways, and telegraphs, while medical advances like quinine reduced mortality from malaria, enabling deeper inland penetration. Economic motives included securing raw materials (ivory, rubber, palm oil, diamonds) and finding markets for surplus capital. Strategic interests centred on controlling trade routes, especially the Suez Canal, completed in 1869, which Britain acquired shares in by 1875. National pride and the "White Man’s Burden" ideology also fueled expansion.

### The Berlin Conference and Rules of Partition  
In 1884, Otto von Bismarck convened the Berlin Conference to prevent conflict among European powers. It established the principle of "effective occupation"—territory could not be claimed without actual administrative control—and required notification of claims. The Congo River basin was designated for Leopold II’s Congo Free State as a neutral zone. Though intended to regulate peaceful division, the conference accelerated rather than slowed the scramble.

### Key Colonial Powers and Their Territories  
**Britain** focused on securing communication lines to India, occupying Egypt (1882) and expanding in East and Southern Africa. The Cape to Cairo vision aimed to link British territories across the continent.  
**France** expanded from West African coasts toward the Sahel and Sudan, seeking a trans-Saharan empire.  
**Germany**, unified in 1871, pursued Weltpolitik under Kaiser Wilhelm II, acquiring colonies including Togoland, Cameroon, and German East Africa.  
**Italy** expanded in the Horn of Africa and North Africa, attempting to conquer Ethiopia but failing at Adwa (1896).  
**Portugal** leveraged its long-standing coastal presence to claim Angola and Mozambique, promoting the "Pink Map" to connect them.  
**Belgium** controlled the Congo Free State, notorious for Leopold II’s brutal exploitation.  
**Spain** held minimal territory, mainly in North Africa and Equatorial Guinea.

### Resistance and Violence  
Indigenous resistance was widespread. The Dervish Movement in Somaliland, led by Sayyid Muhammad Abdullah Hassan, resisted British and Italian forces from 1899 to 1920. In German East Africa, the Maji Maji Rebellion (1905–07) protested forced labour. The Herero and Nama genocides in German South West Africa (1904–08) resulted in the deaths of up to 65,000 Herero and 10,000 Nama through starvation, thirst, and concentration camps. The Congo Free State saw up to 8 million deaths due to forced labour and violence under Leopold II.

### Aftermath and Legacy  
By 1914, only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent. Between 1885 and 1914, Britain took nearly 30% of Africa's population under its control; 15% for France, 11% for Portugal, 9% for Germany, 7% for Belgium and 1% for Italy. The scramble intensified European rivalries, contributing to the tensions leading to World War I. After the First World War, Germany's possessions were partitioned among Britain (which took a sliver of western Cameroon, Tanzania, western Togo, and Namibia), France (which took most of Cameroon and eastern Togo) and Belgium (which took Rwanda and Burundi). Decolonisation began after WWII, with most African nations gaining independence during the Cold War. The Organisation of African Unity (1964) upheld colonial borders to prevent fragmentation.

## Terms  
- **New Imperialism**: Late 19th-century wave of imperial expansion characterised by formal colonisation and economic penetration.  
- **Effective Occupation**: Principle established at the Berlin Conference requiring actual administrative control before territorial claims were recognised.  
- **Weltpolitik**: Germany’s late 19th-century foreign policy under Kaiser Wilhelm II aimed at building a global empire.  
- **Mission Civilisatrice**: French colonial ideology asserting a duty to civilise "backward" peoples.  
- **Cape to Cairo**: British imperial ambition to link southern and northern African territories by rail.  
- **Pink Map**: Portuguese plan to connect Angola and Mozambique across Central Africa.  
- **Human Zoos**: Public exhibitions of indigenous peoples in Europe and America, often displayed in cages for scientific and entertainment purposes.  
- **Proletarian Nationalism**: Concept developed by Enrico Corradini linking socialism with nationalist imperialism.  
- **Prazo System**: Portuguese colonial land tenure system in Mozambique granting leaseholds in exchange for military service.  
- **Scramble for Africa**: Rapid European colonisation of Africa between 1881 and 1914.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Historians debate the primacy of economic versus political factors. John A. Hobson argued that capitalist overproduction and surplus capital drove imperialism, while William Easterly contends that colonialism was more about state-led development than corporate expansion. The role of the Berlin Conference is also debated: while it formalised partition, some argue it merely legitimised already ongoing conquests. The extent of indigenous agency in resisting or collaborating with colonial powers remains contested, particularly regarding the use of African soldiers and auxiliaries in Portuguese campaigns. Additionally, estimates of population loss in the Congo Free State vary widely due to lack of reliable pre-colonial census data.
