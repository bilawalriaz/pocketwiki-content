# Sikh Empire

## Overview
The Sikh Empire was a major South Asian power based in the Punjab region, existing from 1799 to 1849. Founded by Maharaja Ranjit Singh, it was the last major Indian subcontinent state to be conquered by the British Empire. At its peak, it was a religiously diverse and militarily formidable empire, whose rapid rise and fall have been compared to that of Napoleon.

## Timeline
- **1799** — Ranjit Singh captures Lahore, founding the Sikh Empire.
- **12 April 1801** — Ranjit Singh is formally crowned Maharaja.
- **1809** — Treaty of Amritsar with the British East India Company sets the Sutlej as the eastern border.
- **1813** — Capture of Attock Fort ends Afghan influence east of the Indus.
- **1818** — Conquest of Multan completes Sikh control of Punjab.
- **1819** — Annexation of the Kashmir Valley after the Battle of Shopian.
- **1834** — Formal annexation of Peshawar.
- **1839** — Death of Maharaja Ranjit Singh.
- **1845–1846** — First Anglo-Sikh War; Sikhs are defeated.
- **1848–1849** — Second Anglo-Sikh War; the empire is defeated and annexed by the British.
- **30 March 1849** — Sikh Empire is formally dissolved.

## Body

### Founding and Expansion
The empire emerged from the Sikh *misl* confederacy. Ranjit Singh, leader of the Sukerchakia *misl*, captured Lahore in 1799 and was crowned in 1801. He unified the Sikh misls by 1813 and progressively expelled Afghan influence from Punjab, culminating in the capture of Multan in 1818. The empire expanded north to Kashmir (1819) and Ladakh, and west to Peshawar and the Khyber Pass. Ranjit Singh modernized the Sikh Khalsa Army with contemporary training and artillery.

### Administration and Society
The empire was ruled from the Lahore Darbar (court), which was secular in practice despite Sikh theocratic foundations. The court and military were composed of Sikhs, Hindus, Muslims, and Europeans, with appointments based on merit and loyalty. The government maintained Persian administrative institutions and court etiquette. Land revenue was the primary source of state income.

### Religious Policy
Ranjit Singh's rule was characterized by religious tolerance and pluralism. He employed officials from all faiths, including the Muslim Fakir brothers as key advisors. He visited shrines of other faiths and issued orders for equal treatment of all religions. However, some policies, such as a ban on cow slaughter and the seizure of mosques in Kashmir, alienated parts of the Muslim population.

### Decline and Fall
After Ranjit Singh's death in 1839, the empire was weakened by internal power struggles, assassinations, and political mismanagement. The British East India Company exploited these divisions. The First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–46) resulted in a costly defeat and territorial concessions. The Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–49), triggered by a rebellion in Multan that the British implicated the Sikh court in, ended with the empire's dissolution and annexation. The last ruler, Maharaja Duleep Singh, was deposed and exiled.

## Terms
- **Misl**: A constituent member of the Sikh confederacy, a sovereign state ruled by a chieftain.
- **Khalsa**: The collective body of initiated Sikhs, established by Guru Gobind Singh; also refers to the Sikh army.
- **Lahore Darbar**: The royal court and government of the Sikh Empire based in Lahore.
- **Sarkar-i-Khalsa**: The Persian term for the Sikh government, meaning "Rule of the Khalsa."
- **Jagir**: A grant of land revenue given to an official in lieu of a salary.
- **Suba**: A province of the empire.
- **Kardar**: A tax collector or local administrator.
- **Waqia-navis**: A news-writer or intelligence reporter for the court.
- **Vakil**: An agent or emissary representing a foreign power.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the cause of the empire's rapid decline. Some historians attribute it primarily to British manipulation and internal betrayals (e.g., by Lal Singh and Tej Singh). Others, like Patwant Singh, argue the decline was due to internal factors, specifically Ranjit Singh's failure to establish a stable succession and his neglect of the Khalsa's republican traditions in favor of monarchy. The exact population and administrative divisions of the empire also vary among scholarly estimates.
