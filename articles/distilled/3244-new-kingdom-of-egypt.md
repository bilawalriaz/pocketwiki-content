# New Kingdom of Egypt

## Overview
The New Kingdom (c. 1570–1069 BC) was ancient Egypt's most powerful and prosperous era, marking the peak of its imperial expansion and cultural achievement. It followed the Second Intermediate Period of foreign Hyksos rule and concluded with the Third Intermediate Period of decline, representing a golden age of military conquest, monumental building, and religious innovation.

## Timeline
- **c. 1570–1544 BC** — Establishment of the New Kingdom, beginning the Eighteenth Dynasty
- **c. 1550–1525 BC** — Ahmose I expels the Hyksos, reunifies Egypt, and founds the Eighteenth Dynasty
- **c. 1479–1458 BC** — Hatshepsut rules as pharaoh, re-establishes trade networks and builds extensively
- **c. 1479–1425 BC** — Thutmose III conducts 16+ military campaigns, expanding Egypt to its greatest territorial extent
- **c. 1390–1352 BC** — Reign of Amenhotep III, a period of peak wealth and monumental construction
- **c. 1353–1336 BC** — Akhenaten introduces worship of Aten, possibly history's first monotheism
- **c. 1332–1323 BC** — Reign of Tutankhamun, who restores traditional religion
- **1295–1292 BC** — Ramesses I founds the Nineteenth Dynasty
- **c. 1279–1213 BC** — Ramesses II reigns, builds Abu Simbel and fights the Battle of Kadesh against the Hittites
- **c. 1186–1155 BC** — Ramesses III defeats the Sea Peoples, the last great pharaoh of the New Kingdom
- **c. 1077 BC** — End of the New Kingdom; Smendes founds the Twenty-first Dynasty

## Body

### Rise and Imperial Expansion
The New Kingdom emerged from the trauma of Hyksos foreign rule during the Second Intermediate Period. Pharaoh Ahmose I completed the expulsion of the Hyksos and launched campaigns into the Levant to prevent future invasions. His successors, particularly Thutmose I and Thutmose III, expanded Egypt's empire deep into the Near East and Nubia. Thutmose III, a military genius, conducted at least 16 campaigns in 20 years, capturing 350 cities and extending Egyptian control from the Euphrates to Nubia. This expansion was driven by a desire to secure Egypt's borders and control valuable trade routes.

### The Eighteenth Dynasty's Cultural and Religious Revolution
The Eighteenth Dynasty produced some of Egypt's most famous rulers. Hatshepsut, who ruled as a female pharaoh, re-established trade networks and built extensively at Karnak. Amenhotep III presided over a period of immense wealth and monumental construction, including the Luxor Temple. His son Akhenaten (originally Amenhotep IV) introduced a revolutionary religious shift, elevating the Aten (a solar disc representing the god Ra) as the sole deity—a move often interpreted as history's first monotheism. This religious fervor, along with his neglect of foreign affairs, allowed the Hittite Empire to expand into the Levant. After Akhenaten's death, traditional religion was restored under Tutankhamun and his successors.

### The Ramesside Period and Peak Power
The Nineteenth Dynasty, founded by Ramesses I, marked the height of Egyptian imperial power. Seti I fought wars in Asia and Nubia, capturing the Syrian town of Kadesh from the Hittites. His son Ramesses II sought to recover lost territories, culminating in the famous Battle of Kadesh against Hittite king Muwatalli II. Though the battle was inconclusive, it led to the world's first recorded peace treaty. Ramesses II built extensively, including the temples of Abu Simbel and the Ramesseum, using art as propaganda for his victories. His reign brought stability and wealth, though his successors faced increasing court troubles and civil conflict.

### Decline and the End of the New Kingdom
The Twentieth Dynasty saw the last great pharaoh, Ramesses III, who defeated invasions by the Sea Peoples and Libyan tribes. However, the cost of warfare drained Egypt's treasury. The first recorded labor strike occurred during his reign due to food shortages, possibly linked to climate events like the Hekla 3 volcanic eruption. Ramesses III was assassinated in a palace conspiracy. After his death, Egypt suffered from droughts, famine, and weak pharaohs. The power of the priests of Amun grew until they controlled Upper Egypt, while Smendes ruled Lower Egypt, leading to the fragmented Third Intermediate Period.

## Terms
- **Hyksos**: A people of mixed Asiatic origin who ruled Lower Egypt during the Second Intermediate Period.
- **Levant**: A historical region in the Eastern Mediterranean, including modern-day Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Israel, and Palestine.
- **Nubia**: A region along the Nile River, south of Egypt, rich in gold and other resources.
- **Aten**: The solar disc god promoted as the sole deity by Pharaoh Akhenaten.
- **Monotheism**: The belief in a single god, as possibly practiced under Akhenaten.
- **Hittites**: An Anatolian people who formed an empire in modern-day Turkey and Syria, rivals to Egypt.
- **Sea Peoples**: A confederation of naval raiders who attacked the Eastern Mediterranean in the late Bronze Age.
- **Amarna Period**: The era of Akhenaten's religious revolution, named after his new capital city, Akhetaten (modern Amarna).
- **High Priests of Amun**: The powerful priests of the god Amun who eventually gained control of Thebes and Upper Egypt.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact chronology of the New Kingdom's start is debated, with radiocarbon dating placing it between 1570 and 1544 BC.
- The nature of Akhenaten's religion is debated: was it true monotheism, henotheism (worship of one god while acknowledging others), or monolatry (worship of one god without denying others' existence)?
- The identity and fate of Ramesses III's assassins, and whether he died immediately from the plot, remained uncertain until a 2012 CT scan revealed a fatal throat wound.
- The cause of the climate event that limited sunlight and agricultural production around 1159 BC is disputed, with the Hekla 3 eruption being one proposed but unconfirmed explanation.
