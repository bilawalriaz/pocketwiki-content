# Cossack Hetmanate

## Overview
The Cossack Hetmanate (1648–1764) was a stratocratic, or military-administrative, state established by Registered Cossacks in Dnieper Ukraine. It emerged from the Khmelnytsky Uprising against the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth and represented the first major, de facto independent polity uniting ethnic Ukrainian lands. Its history is defined by a struggle for sovereignty, caught between the competing interests of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Tsardom of Russia, and the Ottoman Empire, ultimately leading to its gradual absorption into the Russian Empire.

## Timeline
- **1648** — Khmelnytsky Uprising begins against the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth.
- **1649** — Treaty of Zboriv legally recognizes the Hetmanate in eastern Commonwealth territories.
- **1654** — Treaty of Pereiaslav establishes a protectorate with the Tsardom of Moscow.
- **1658** — Treaty of Hadiach proposes a tripartite Polish-Lithuanian-Ruthenian Commonwealth.
- **1667** — Treaty of Andrusovo partitions the Hetmanate along the Dnieper River.
- **1687** — Ivan Mazepa elected Hetman, beginning a period of stability and cultural flourishing.
- **1708** — Mazepa allies with Sweden's Charles XII against Russia.
- **1709** — Battle of Poltava defeats the Swedish-Cossack forces; Hetmanate's autonomy severely curtailed.
- **1710** — Pylyp Orlyk elected Hetman in exile; Constitution of Pylyp Orlyk drafted.
- **1764** — Empress Catherine II officially abolishes the position of Hetman.
- **1781** — Regimental administrative system abolished, completing integration into the Russian Empire.

## Body

### Establishment and Early Foreign Policy
The Hetmanate was founded following the Khmelnytsky Uprising, which sought to end corruption, social disenfranchisement, and restrictions on the Cossack population within the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. After military successes, Hetman Bohdan Khmelnytsky secured legal recognition via the Treaty of Zboriv (1649). Facing an unreliable alliance with the Crimean Khanate, Khmelnytsky sought a foreign protector. In 1654, the Cossack elite swore allegiance to the Tsar of Moscow in the Treaty of Pereiaslav, gaining recognition of self-government in exchange for military support against Poland. This alliance was formalized in the March Articles but led to the Russo-Polish War (1654–1667).

### The Ruin and Partition
The period known as "the Ruin" (1657–1687) was marked by civil wars and foreign intervention after Khmelnytsky's death. Hetmans like Ivan Vyhovsky and Petro Doroshenko alternately allied with Poland, Russia, or the Ottomans in attempts to preserve autonomy. The Treaty of Andrusovo (1667), negotiated without Cossack representation, formally split the Hetmanate along the Dnieper: the Left Bank became a Russian protectorate, while the Right Bank remained under Polish control (later briefly under Ottoman influence). This partition nullified the Cossacks' struggle for a unified state.

### Mazepa's Reign and the Battle of Poltava
Ivan Mazepa's hetmanship (1687–1708) brought stability and a cultural renaissance, including the Cossack Baroque architectural style. He elevated the Cossack officer class (*starshyna*) into a new nobility. However, the burdens of the Great Northern War (1700–1721) and Russian interference led Mazepa to ally with Sweden's Charles XII in 1708. The decisive Russian victory at the Battle of Poltava (1709) crushed this alliance. Mazepa died in exile, and his successor, Pylyp Orlyk, drafted a democratic constitution but failed to restore the Hetmanate from abroad.

### Incorporation into the Russian Empire
After Poltava, the Hetmanate's autonomy became nominal. Russian authorities established the Collegium of Little Russia (1722) to oversee administration, directly appointing officials and limiting Cossack self-governance. While brief restorations of the hetmanship occurred under Danylo Apostol and Kyrylo Rozumovsky, Russian control tightened. Catherine II formally abolished the hetman's office in 1764. The final administrative dissolution came in 1781, when the regimental system was replaced by Russian provinces, and Cossack elites were integrated into the imperial nobility.

### Government and Society
The Hetmanate was a stratocracy led by a Hetman, initially elected by a General Cossack Council but later chosen by the officer council (*starshyna*) and confirmed by the Tsar. It was divided into military-administrative districts called regiments. Society was hierarchical: the *starshyna* (officer-nobility), Cossacks, clergy, townspeople, and peasants. The Orthodox Church held significant land and influence. While the uprising reduced serfdom, social tensions persisted between the elite and rank-and-file Cossacks, and between Cossacks and peasants.

### Economy and Culture
The economy was agrarian-feudal, with a complex tax system funding the Military Treasury. Agriculture was the base, though distilling and saltpeter production were key industries. The Hetmanate experienced a cultural flowering, especially under Mazepa, with high literacy rates, the establishment of the Kyiv-Mohyla Academy, and the development of Ukrainian Baroque architecture and music.

## Terms
- **Hetman**: The supreme military and political leader of the Cossack Host.
- **Stratocracy**: A form of government where the state and military are unified.
- **Zaporozhian Sich**: A semi-autonomous Cossack military and political center in the lower Dnieper region.
- **Starshyna**: The Cossack officer class that evolved into a landed nobility.
- **Registered Cossacks**: Cossacks officially enrolled in service to the Polish Crown, with associated privileges.
- **Ruin**: The period of civil war and foreign intervention in the Hetmanate (1657–1687).
- **Collegium of Little Russia**: A Russian imperial administrative body created to oversee and control the Hetmanate.
- **Magdeburg Law**: A legal charter granting towns self-governance and their own courts.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The nature of the Pereiaslav Agreement (1654) is heavily debated: was it a treaty between equals, a submission to Muscovite suzerainty, or a strategic military alliance?
- The degree of autonomy the Hetmanate possessed under Russian protection, especially after 1654 and again after 1709, is a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion.
- The social impact of the "Ruin" and the extent to which the Cossack state was a "democratic" entity versus an evolving oligarchy are contested interpretations.
