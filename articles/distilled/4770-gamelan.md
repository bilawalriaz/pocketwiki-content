# Gamelan

## Overview
Gamelan is the traditional ensemble music of the Javanese, Sundanese, and Balinese peoples of Indonesia, consisting primarily of percussive instruments like metallophones, gongs, and drums. It is a core element of Indonesian cultural identity, used in ceremonies, rituals, and performances, and was inscribed on the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2021. Its history reflects indigenous development with later Hindu-Buddhist and Islamic influences, and its unique tuning systems and communal performance philosophy have influenced global music.

## Timeline
- **c. 230 AD** — Javanese mythology credits the god Sang Hyang Guru with creating the first gong and gamelan set.
- **8th century** — Earliest visual depiction of a musical ensemble on bas-reliefs of Borobudur temple.
- **9th century** — Ancient *gamelan selonding* instruments exist in Bali during the reign of Sri Kesari Warmadewa.
- **12th century** — Oldest known court ensembles, Gamelan Munggang and Gamelan Kodok Ngorek, are established in Java.
- **15th century** — Gamelan Reyog is used in the Ponorogo region; Gamelan Sakati is gifted to the Cirebon Sultanate.
- **16th century** — Sundanese gamelan art is documented in the manuscript *Sanghyang Siksa Kandang Karesian*.
- **17th century** — "Loud" and "soft" musical styles mix in Java, forming the basis for modern regional varieties.
- **1889** — Javanese gamelan is performed at the Paris Exposition, influencing Western composers like Debussy.
- **1977** — A Javanese gamelan recording is sent into space on NASA's Voyager probes.
- **2014** — Gamelan is recognized as part of Indonesia's National Intangible Cultural Heritage.
- **2021** — Gamelan is inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

## Body
### Origins and History
Gamelan predates the Hindu-Buddhist cultures that later dominated Indonesia, representing an indigenous art form. The earliest known image of a musical ensemble is on the 8th-century Borobudur temple reliefs, which show instruments like metallophones, xylophones, and drums that are part of modern gamelan. The instruments developed into their current form during the Majapahit Empire (14th-15th centuries), which had a government office overseeing performing arts. In Java, the oldest sacred court gamelans date to the 12th century. The 17th century saw the mixing of "loud" and "soft" musical styles, which largely created the diversity of modern Balinese, Javanese, and Sundanese gamelan.

### Instruments and Tuning
A gamelan ensemble is multi-timbre, featuring metallophones (played with mallets), xylophones, gongs, bamboo flutes, bowed strings (rebab), and hand-drums (kendang) that control tempo. Vocalists (sindhen for women, gerong for men) may also participate. Javanese gamelan uses two main tuning systems: *sléndro* (five notes per octave) and *pélog* (seven notes per octave, played in five-note subsets). Each gamelan set is tuned uniquely. Balinese gamelan instruments are built in pairs tuned slightly apart to create a shimmering "ombak" (wave) effect from interference beats. Notation was traditionally oral; modern *kepatihan* notation, using numbers, developed around 1900.

### Regional Varieties
The principal varieties are Javanese, Balinese, and Sundanese. Javanese gamelan is often soft and slow, reflecting life's harmony, and is divided into sacred court ensembles (*gamelan pakurmatan*) and public ensembles (*gamelan ageng*). Balinese gamelan is strong, dynamic, and fast, used for both religious ceremonies and entertainment, with styles evolving from ancient (*wayah*) to modern (*anyar*). Sundanese gamelan from West Java is noted for its flute-dominated, mellow sound and includes types like *gamelan salendro* and *gamelan degung*.

### Cultural Context and Function
Gamelan is integral to Indonesian life, accompanying religious rituals, ceremonies (weddings, births, deaths), traditional dances, dance-dramas, and *wayang* (puppet theatre). It symbolizes community cooperation and mutual respect. Specific gamelans are tied to specific rituals, such as the *Gamelan Sekaten* for the Prophet Muhammad's birthday. The art form is also performed as standalone entertainment and at festivals. In 2021, UNESCO recognized its role in fostering national identity, wellbeing, and interpersonal skills.

### Production and Global Influence
Gamelan instruments are primarily made of bronze or brass, with a five-stage crafting process: melting, casting, forging, examining, and tuning (*melaras*). Production centers are in Java and Bali. Gamelan has significantly influenced Western classical music (e.g., Debussy, Satie, Britten), jazz, and contemporary popular music, film scores (e.g., *Akira*), and video games. It is now practiced in over 25 countries outside Indonesia.

## Terms
- **Gamelan**: A traditional Indonesian ensemble music, predominantly featuring percussive instruments.
- **Metallophone**: A musical instrument consisting of tuned metal bars struck with mallets.
- **Kendang**: A pair of hand-played drums that control tempo and rhythm in a gamelan.
- **Sléndro**: A Javanese tuning system with five notes per octave and large intervals.
- **Pélog**: A Javanese tuning system with seven notes per octave and uneven intervals, typically played in five-note subsets.
- **Ombak**: The "wave" effect in Balinese gamelan created by pairing instruments tuned slightly apart to produce interference beats.
- **Kepatihan**: A modern numerical notation system for gamelan music developed in Surakarta around 1900.
- **Karawitan**: A term for classical gamelan music and performance practice.
- **Wayang**: Traditional Indonesian puppet theatre (e.g., *wayang kulit* shadow puppets) accompanied by gamelan.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise origins and development of gamelan before the 8th-century Borobudur reliefs are debated, as it predates written records.
- The extent of Indian influence on gamelan is contested; the source states the only obvious influence is in singing styles and *wayang* themes.
- The claim by ethnomusicologist Colin McPhee that "there are as many scales as there are gamelans" due to tuning variations is noted as contested by some gamelan teachers.
