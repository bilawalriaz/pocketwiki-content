# Ancient Greek literature

## Overview
Ancient Greek literature encompasses works written in the Ancient Greek language from the earliest texts until the Byzantine Empire. It forms the foundational canon of Western literature, with its epic, dramatic, philosophical, and historical works establishing genres and themes that have been emulated for millennia.

## Timeline
- **c. 12th century BC** — Destruction of Mycenaean palaces; Linear B tablets (prosaic records) cease.
- **c. 750–700 BC** — Composition of the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* (Homer) and Hesiod's *Theogony* and *Works and Days*.
- **7th century BC** — Archilochus writes iambic poetry; lyric poetry develops.
- **c. 630–570 BC** — Life of Sappho, the most revered lyric poet.
- **5th century BC** — Golden Age of Athenian drama (Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, Aristophanes) and historiography (Herodotus, Thucydides).
- **4th century BC** — Philosophical works of Plato and Aristotle; Xenophon's historical writings.
- **3rd century BC** — Hellenistic period begins; Library of Alexandria founded; Apollonius of Rhodes writes *The Argonautica*.
- **2nd century AD** — Lucian of Samosata writes satire; Ptolemy writes the *Almagest*.
- **3rd century AD** — Heliodorus of Emesa writes *Aethiopica*; the *Alexander Romance* is composed.
- **4th–5th centuries AD** — Late Antique epic (Nonnus' *Dionysiaca*); New Testament written in Koine Greek.

## Body

### Foundations: Epic and Hesiodic Poetry
The earliest major works are Homer's epics, the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, set in an idealized Mycenaean past. They are written in dactylic hexameter and the Homeric dialect. Hesiod, a contemporary, provided a creation narrative (*Theogony*) and a guide to rural life (*Works and Days*). These texts were foundational to Greek religion and literary tradition.

### Lyric, Elegiac, and Iambic Poetry
Following the epics, poetry diversified. Elegiac and iambic poetry emerged in the Ionic dialect. Lyric poetry, originally sung to a lyre, reached its peak with the "Nine Lyric Poets," most famously Sappho (Aeolic dialect) and Pindar (choral lyric).

### Drama: Tragedy and Comedy
Athenian drama, performed at festivals of Dionysus, evolved from choral song. Tragedy, exploring heroic themes, was perfected by Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides. Comedy, often satirical, developed from Old Comedy (Aristophanes) to New Comedy (Menander). All surviving plays are in the Attic dialect.

### Historiography and Biography
Prose literature began with Herodotus' *The Histories*. Thucydides' *History of the Peloponnesian War* set a standard for analytical history. Later historians like Xenophon, Polybius, and Diodorus Siculus expanded the scope. Ancient biography (*bios*), exemplified by Plutarch's *Parallel Lives*, focused on character and moral example.

### Philosophy
Philosophical writing evolved from pre-Socratic fragments to the systematic dialogues of Plato (e.g., *The Republic*) and the treatises of Aristotle (e.g., *Politics*, *Poetics*). Later, Plotinus developed Neoplatonism.

### Hellenistic and Roman-Period Literature
After Alexander the Great, Alexandria became the cultural center. Key genres included bucolic poetry (Theocritus), scholarly poetry (Callimachus), and epic (Apollonius of Rhodes). Under Rome, Greek literature flourished, with notable satire from Lucian of Samosata and the development of prose fiction, including novels like *Daphnis and Chloe*.

### Science, Mathematics, and Christian Literature
Greek scientific writing included Euclid's *Elements*, Archimedes' mathematical treatises, and Ptolemy's *Almagest*, which dominated astronomy for over 1,300 years. The New Testament was written in Koine Greek, and early Christian philosophers like Origen engaged with Greek philosophy.

## Terms
- **Dactylic hexameter**: The meter of epic poetry, consisting of six feet per line.
- **Homeric dialect**: A literary mixture of Ionic, Aeolic, and Attic Greek used by Homer.
- **Elegiac couplet**: A poetic form pairing a dactylic hexameter line with a dactylic pentameter line.
- **Attic dialect**: The dialect of Athens, used for classical drama and prose.
- **Koine Greek**: The common dialect of the Hellenistic and Roman periods, in which the New Testament was written.
- **Bucolic poetry**: A genre of pastoral poetry depicting rural life, pioneered by Theocritus.
- **Milesian tale**: A genre of bawdy, adventurous prose fiction originating with Aristides of Miletus.
- **Neoplatonism**: A philosophical school developed by Plotinus, blending Platonic and other ideas.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Homeric Question**: The authorship and composition of the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*—whether they were the work of a single poet (Homer) or compiled from older oral traditions.
- **Authorship of specific tragedies**: The authorship of *Prometheus Bound* (traditionally Aeschylus) and *Rhesus* (traditionally Euripides) is questioned.
- **Survival of texts**: The vast majority of ancient Greek literature is lost. The survival of works is heavily skewed by papyrus finds, with Homer dominating the record.
