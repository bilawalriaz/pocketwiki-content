# Flute

## Overview
The flute is a woodwind musical instrument that produces sound when a player's airstream is directed across an opening, creating a vibrating column of air. It is one of the oldest and most widespread instrument families, with archaeological evidence spanning from the Paleolithic era to the present day across Europe, Asia, and the Americas. Its simple design and diverse forms have made it a fundamental part of musical traditions worldwide.

## Timeline
- **c. 43,000–35,000 years ago** — Earliest known flutes are made from bird and mammoth bones in the Swabian Jura, Germany.
- **c. 2600–2700 BC** — Earliest written reference to a flute appears on a Sumerian cuneiform tablet.
- **c. 12th–11th centuries BC** — Chinese written sources mention the xīao, an end-blown bamboo flute.
- **9th century BC** — The chi (ch'ih), the oldest documented transverse flute, appears in Chinese records.
- **1st–4th centuries AD** — The Indian cross flute (vāṃśī) is depicted in Buddhist reliefs at Sanchi and Amaravati.
- **c. 800 AD** — Transverse flutes are depicted in Greek art, having entered Europe via Byzantium.
- **14th century** — The word "flute" first appears in English, in Geoffrey Chaucer's *The Hous of Fame*.
- **1832–1847** — Theobald Boehm evolves the modern concert flute's key system and tone hole design.
- **1998** — Avinash Balkrishna Patwardhan develops a methodology for tuning flutes for Indian classical music.

## Body

### Origins and Ancient History
The flute is among the oldest identifiable musical instruments. Paleolithic flutes with hand-bored holes, such as those found in Germany's Swabian Jura dating to 53,000–45,000 years ago, indicate a developed musical tradition from the earliest period of modern human presence in Europe. While the oldest confirmed flutes are European, ancient flute cultures also existed in Asia (a 9,000-year-old Chinese bone flute) and the Americas (flutes from Peru and Labrador dating back 5,000–7,500 years). Early written references include Sumerian tablets (c. 2600–2700 BC) and mentions in the Chinese *Shi Jing* and the Hebrew Bible.

### Etymology and Terminology
The English word "flute" emerged in the Middle English period, possibly from Old French or Middle High German roots. A musician who plays the flute is called a flutist (attested from 1603) or a flautist (adopted from Italian in the 19th century). Older, now obsolete terms include fluter and flutenist.

### Acoustics and Materials
A flute produces sound when an airstream directed across a hole creates a vibration (a Bernoulli effect) that excites the air in the instrument's resonant cavity. Pitch is changed by opening and closing holes to alter the effective length of the resonator. Historically, flutes were made from reed, bamboo, wood, bone, or glass. Most modern Western concert flutes are made of metal, primarily silver alloys, though gold, platinum, and wood are also used. Studies suggest the wall material has little appreciable effect on sound color.

### Types and Classification
Flutes are broadly classified as either **fipple flutes** (like recorders, with a duct directing air onto the edge) or **edge-blown flutes** (where the player blows directly across an opening). They can also be **side-blown (transverse)** or **end-blown**. The Western concert flute, a transverse instrument pitched in C, was standardized by Theobald Boehm's 19th-century design. Other major regional families include the Indian bamboo bansuri and venu, the Chinese dizi (with a distinctive buzzing membrane), the Korean daegeum, and the Japanese fue (including the shakuhachi).

### Regional Traditions
The bamboo flute is central to Indian classical music, with the bansuri (six holes) used in Hindustani music and the venu (eight holes) in Carnatic music. The Chinese dizi family features various sizes and a unique *di mo* resonance membrane. The Korean daegeum and Japanese fue also have distinct timbres due to buzzing membranes or specific construction. In Madagascar, the end-blown sodina is an ancient instrument likely brought by settlers from Borneo.

## Terms
- **Aerophone**: A musical instrument that produces sound primarily by causing a body of air to vibrate.
- **Edge-blown aerophone**: A flute where sound is produced by blowing a stream of air across an opening or edge.
- **Fipple flute**: A flute with a built-in duct (fipple) that directs the player's breath onto the edge, such as a recorder.
- **Transverse flute**: A flute played by blowing across a hole on the side of the tube, like the Western concert flute.
- **End-blown flute**: A flute played by blowing across an opening at one end of the tube, like the shakuhachi or ney.
- **Embouchure hole**: The opening in a transverse flute that the player blows across to produce sound.
- **Bernoulli effect (siphon)**: The fluid dynamics principle where a fast-moving stream of air creates low pressure, causing vibration.
- **Resonator**: The hollow tube of the flute where the air column vibrates to produce sound.
- **Di mo**: A thin membrane on the Chinese dizi flute that vibrates to create a bright, buzzing timbre.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact origin and classification of the Divje Babe flute fragment (c. 43,000 years ago) from Slovenia is disputed; it may be the oldest flute but is not universally accepted as such.
- There is no clear consensus among manufacturers on the ideal head joint geometry for optimal acoustic performance and tone in modern flutes.
- The claim that flute wall material (e.g., silver vs. gold) significantly affects sound color is not supported by blind listening studies, which found no appreciable difference.
