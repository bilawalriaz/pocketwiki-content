# Persepolis

## Overview
Persepolis was the ceremonial capital of the Achaemenid Empire (c. 550–330 BC), located in modern Iran's Fars province. It was not a large, permanent city but a grand, seasonally occupied palace complex built to showcase imperial power and host ceremonies, most notably the Persian New Year (Nowruz). Its destruction by Alexander the Great in 330 BC is a pivotal event in ancient history, and its ruins have been crucial for understanding Achaemenid culture and for the decipherment of cuneiform script.

## Timeline
- **c. 515 BC** — Earliest construction remains date to this period, initiated by Darius I.
- **519 BC** — Construction of a broad stairway begins.
- **330 BC** — Alexander the Great's army captures and loots Persepolis.
- **330 BC** — The wooden parts of the complex are destroyed by fire.
- **316 BC** — Persepolis is still noted as the capital of Persia as a province of the Macedonian Empire.
- **1602** — Portuguese traveler António de Gouveia writes about the cuneiform inscriptions.
- **1618** — García de Silva Figueroa first links the site known as "Chehel Minar" to classical Persepolis.
- **1930s** — First scientific excavations are conducted by Ernst Herzfeld and Erich Schmidt.
- **1971** — The site hosts the 2,500-year celebration of the Persian Empire.
- **2024** — The first-ever concert is held at Persepolis.

## Body

### Construction and Purpose
Construction of the terrace and palaces began under Darius I around 515 BC, possibly modeled on his palace at Susa. The complex was built on a raised, walled platform and included multiple "palaces" or halls, such as the Apadana for official audiences and the Throne Hall. Its exact function is debated, but it was likely a ceremonial center for events like Nowruz, where nobles and tributaries presented gifts to the king, as depicted in its famous stairway reliefs. It was not a large, permanent city but a seasonal seat of governance and celebration.

### Destruction by Alexander
In 330 BC, Alexander the Great's army stormed the Persian Gates and captured Persepolis. After allowing his troops to loot the city, a fire destroyed its wooden structures. The cause is debated: one theory holds it was deliberate revenge for the Persian burning of Athens in 480 BC; another, from later Greek and Roman accounts, suggests it was ignited accidentally during a drunken feast, possibly at the urging of the courtesan Thaïs. The fire may have also served the political purpose of destroying a symbol of Persian monarchy that could inspire resistance.

### Archaeological Discovery and Research
European travelers noted the ruins from the 14th century onward. Early reports in the 17th century described the site and its cuneiform inscriptions. The first scientific excavations began in 1930 under the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago. These digs uncovered key structures and the Persepolis Fortification Archive, a collection of clay tablets. The study of the site's trilingual inscriptions was instrumental in the 19th-century decipherment of cuneiform.

### Architecture and Remains
The architecture is characterized by the Persian column and grand reliefs. Key structures include the Gate of All Nations, the Apadana Palace with its foundation tablets and coin hoard, the Throne Hall (or Hall of a Hundred Columns), and the Tachara Palace, which is the most intact building. The site also includes tombs and, nearby at Tol-e Ajori, a monumental gateway resembling Babylon's Ishtar Gate, which likely predates Persepolis itself.

## Terms
- **Achaemenid Empire**: The first Persian Empire, ruling from c. 550–330 BC.
- **Cuneiform**: An ancient system of writing using wedge-shaped marks, used for languages like Old Persian.
- **Apadana**: The great audience hall built by Darius I at Persepolis.
- **Nowruz**: The Persian New Year, celebrated at the spring equinox.
- **Persepolis Fortification Archive**: A collection of clay tablets from the site, containing administrative records.
- **Tachara**: A palace at Persepolis, considered the most intact structure, built by Darius I.
- **Throne Hall**: Also called the Hall of a Hundred Columns, the second-largest building, used for receptions.
- **Tripylon**: Also known as the Council Hall or "Triple Gate."

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Primary Function**: Was Persepolis primarily a ceremonial complex for Nowruz and royal receptions, or did it have a more central administrative role?
- **Cause of the Fire**: Was the destruction in 330 BC a deliberate act of revenge by Alexander, an accident during a drunken celebration, or a calculated political move?
- **Royal Quarters**: The location of the king's private living quarters within the complex remains unclear.
- **Sivand Dam Impact**: There is ongoing concern and debate about whether the nearby Sivand Dam threatens the archaeological sites of Persepolis and Pasargadae.
