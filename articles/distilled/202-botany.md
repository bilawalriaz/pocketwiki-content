# Botany

## Overview
Botany is the scientific study of plants, encompassing their structure, function, genetics, ecology, and evolution over 3.5 billion years. It originated in prehistoric herbalism to identify edible, medicinal, and poisonous plants, evolving through medieval physic gardens and university botanical gardens into a modern discipline integrating molecular genetics, genomics, and epigenetics. Plants—defined strictly as embryophytes (land plants) but historically including algae, fungi, and bacteria—underpin almost all animal life by performing oxygenic photosynthesis, fixing carbon, and forming the base of food webs. Modern research focuses on molecular mechanisms of gene expression, plant physiology, and applications in agriculture, biotechnology, and environmental management.

## Timeline
- **Before 1100 BCE** — Early botanical texts appear in ancient India, Egypt, China, and Avestan writings.
- **c. 371–287 BCE** — Theophrastus ("Father of Botany") writes *Enquiry into Plants* and *On the Causes of Plants* in Ancient Greece.
- **1st century CE** — Pedanius Dioscorides writes *De materia medica*, a herbal encyclopedia used for 1,500+ years.
- **828–896 CE** — Abū Ḥanīfa Dīnawarī writes *Book of Plants* in the medieval Muslim world.
- **1545** — Padua botanical garden founded, the first university garden still in its original location.
- **1665** — Robert Hooke discovers and names "cells" in cork using an early microscope.
- **1753** — Carl Linnaeus publishes *Species Plantarum*, establishing binomial nomenclature and a sexual classification system.
- **1831** — Robert Brown describes the cell nucleus.
- **1849** — Matthias Schleiden publishes *Principles of Scientific Botany*, co-founding cell theory.
- **1859** — Darwin publishes *Origin of Species*, requiring classification to reflect evolutionary relationships.
- **1902** — Gottlieb Haberlandt proposes all plant cells are totipotent.
- **1948** — Kenneth V. Thimann identifies auxin plant hormones.
- **1998** — Angiosperm Phylogeny Group publishes DNA-sequence-based phylogeny of flowering plants.
- **2000** — *Arabidopsis thaliana* becomes the first plant with a fully sequenced genome.

## Body

### Etymology and Scope
The term "botany" derives from Greek *botanē* ("pasture, herbs, grass, fodder") from *boskein* ("to feed, graze"). Historically, botany included fungi (mycology), algae (phycology), and bacteria (bacteriology), groups still within the International Botanical Congress's purview. The strict definition limits "plants" to embryophytes (land plants): seed plants (gymnosperms and angiosperms) and free-sporing cryptogams (ferns, clubmosses, liverworts, hornworts, mosses). Embryophytes are multicellular eukaryotes with alternating haploid (gametophyte) and diploid (sporophyte) generations; the gametophyte nurtures the embryo sporophyte. Cyanobacteria, the first oxygenic photosynthesizers, entered an endosymbiotic relationship with an early eukaryote, becoming chloroplasts and driving the Great Oxidation Event over 2 billion years ago.

### Historical Development
**Early Botany:** Botany began as herbalism. Theophrastus established foundational principles in Greece. *De materia medica* dominated for 1,500 years. Medieval Muslim scholars (Ibn Wahshiyya, Dīnawarī, Ibn Bassal, al-Nabati, Ibn al-Baitar) advanced systematic study. **Early Modern Botany:** 16th-century Italian university gardens (Padua, 1545) shifted focus to academic study. German "fathers of botany" (Fuchs, Brunfels, Bock) broke from copying ancients to original observation and classification. Hooke's 1665 discovery of cells launched cytology. **18th Century:** Linnaeus's *Species Plantarum* (1753) standardized binomial nomenclature and a sexual system (24 classes based on stamen number). Natural systems (Adanson, de Jussieu, Candolle) replaced artificial ones by using shared characters. Darwin (1859) mandated classification reflect common descent. Upper-class women (e.g., Marianne North) contributed accurate illustrations, popularizing the field. Schleiden's textbook (1849) and cell theory, plus Fick's diffusion laws (1855), formalized plant physiology. **Late Modern Botany:** Weismann proved inheritance occurs only via gametes. Katherine Esau's anatomy texts became foundational. Plant ecology emerged (Warming, Raunkiær, Cowles, Tansley, Clements), introducing concepts of plant communities, succession, climax vegetation, and ecosystems. Vavilov mapped centers of origin for economic plants. Mid-20th-century advances in biophysics (transpiration, stomatal gas exchange), statistics (Fisher, Yates), and hormones (auxin, 2,4-D) enabled precise physiological analysis and biotechnology. Molecular techniques (genomics, proteomics) now allow gene knockout, GFP tagging, and bioreactor synthesis of pharmaceuticals.

### Branches of Botany
Botany divides by organism group (phycology, mycology, bacteriology, bryology, pteridology, agrostology), guild (dendrology), biological level (plant anatomy, morphology, physiology, taxonomy, systematics, ecology, genetics), prefix (*phyto-*chemistry, *phyto-*geography), fossil focus (palaeobotany), and applied intersections (agronomy, horticultural botany, phytopathology, phytopharmacology). Subfields also target specific structures: xylology (wood), carpology (fruit), palynology (pollen/spores). Phytosociology classifies plant communities.

### Plant Biochemistry and Photosynthesis
Plants are photoautotrophs using chloroplasts—derived from endosymbiotic cyanobacteria—to perform oxygenic photosynthesis. Chlorophyll *a* (and *b* in plants/green algae) absorbs blue-violet and orange/red light, driving electron transport to make ATP and NADPH. The Calvin cycle uses rubisco to fix CO₂ into glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (G3P), the first sugar product, leading to glucose, starch (energy store in most plants), fructose polymers (in Asteraceae), and sucrose (transport sugar). Chloroplasts also synthesize fatty acids (for membranes and cutin) and most amino acids. Unique plant polymers include cellulose, pectin, xyloglucan (cell walls), lignin (strengthens xylem, major wood component), and sporopollenin (protects spores/pollen, fossil marker for Ordovician land plants). To cope with low modern CO₂, some plants evolved C4 and Crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) pathways to minimize photorespiration losses inherent in the ancestral C3 pathway.

### Medicine, Materials, and Human Nutrition
Phytochemistry studies secondary metabolites: toxins (coniine), aromatics (peppermint oil, capsaicin), and pharmaceuticals (opium, THC, caffeine, morphine, nicotine). Aspirin derives from salicylic acid (willow bark); heroin from modified morphine. Stimulants (caffeine, nicotine) and alcohol (fermented barley, rice, grapes) are plant-derived. Ethnobotany/archaeobotany records indigenous plant knowledge for drug discovery. Commercial materials include dyes (anthocyanins, indigo, gamboge), fibers (cotton, linen, hemp), wood/paper, oils, rubber, and cellulose derivatives (rayon, cellophane, biobutanol). Biofuels (biodiesel from sugarcane, rapeseed, soy) offer fossil fuel alternatives. Staple crops (maize, rice, wheat, pulses, banana) result from millennia of selection from wild ancestors. Botanists study yield increase, weed control, and pathogen biology for food security.

### Plant Ecology
Plant ecology examines functional relationships between plants and habitats. Ecologists study flora composition, biodiversity, adaptation, and interactions (competition, mutualism). Plants depend on edaphic (soil) and climatic factors but modify them (albedo, runoff, soil stabilization, temperature). They form vegetation at population/community scales, defining biomes (tundra, rainforest). Interactions include herbivory, plant defenses, parasitism, carnivory, and mutualisms: mycorrhizal fungi/rhizobia trade nutrients for carbon; ant plants recruit defenders; pollinators (bees, bats) enable reproduction; animals disperse seeds/spores. **Climate and Environmental Change:** Plant phenology proxies historical temperature. Palynology (fossil pollen) reconstructs past climates. Stomatal density and leaf morphology in fossils estimate palaeo-CO₂. Ozone depletion increases UV-B, reducing growth. Systematics and taxonomy are essential for tracking vegetation change, habitat destruction, and extinction.

### Genetics and Molecular Genetics
Plant inheritance follows Mendelian principles (discovered in peas), but with distinctions: weaker species boundaries allow frequent hybridization (e.g., peppermint, wheat). Outcrossing mechanisms (self-incompatibility, dioecy) mask deleterious mutations (heterosis); inbreeding exposes them (inbreeding depression). Asexual reproduction (apomixis, tubers, bulbils) is common, especially in arctic/alpine habitats, yielding clonal populations. Polyploidy (autopolyploidy, allopolyploidy) drives speciation (e.g., tetraploid durum wheat, hexaploid bread wheat, triploid banana). Organelle inheritance is non-Mendelian: chloroplasts paternally inherited in gymnosperms, usually maternally in angiosperms. **Molecular Genetics:** Model organisms (*Arabidopsis thaliana*—first sequenced plant genome, 2000; rice; *Brachypodium*; *Chlamydomonas*; *Physcomitrella*) enable study of molecular biology, photosynthesis, and chloroplast function. *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* Ti plasmid is the primary vector for transgenic crop creation.

### Epigenetics
Epigenetics studies heritable changes in gene function without DNA sequence alteration. Mechanisms include DNA methylation (silencing genes) and repressor proteins binding silencer regions. Epigenetic marks regulate cellular differentiation: a totipotent zygote activates/inhibits specific genes to produce parenchyma, xylem, phloem, guard cells, etc. Unlike animals, many plant cells (parenchyma) remain totipotent, enabling regeneration from single cells or leaves. Exceptions: lignified sclerenchyma/xylem (dead at maturity) and enucleate phloem sieve tubes. Plants may use positional/environmental cues rather than fixed lineage for fate determination. Paramutations—epigenetic allele interactions violating Mendelian rules—can be inherited transgenerationally.

### Plant Evolution
Chloroplasts share biochemical, structural, and genetic traits with cyanobacteria, supporting an endosymbiotic origin. Algae are polyphyletic; Charophyta (sister to Chlorophyta) contains the ancestor of true plants. Charophyceae + Embryophyta form clade Streptophytina. Nonvascular embryophytes (bryophytes) lack xylem/phloem. Vascular pteridophytes (true xylem/phloem, spore reproduction) diversified in Silurian/Devonian. Late Devonian saw independent evolution of heterospory (megaspory/microspory) and endospory (gametophyte retained in megasporangium). Seeds (integumented megasporangia) appear in latest Devonian. Seed plants diversified into extinct seed ferns, modern gymnosperms (naked seeds: conifers, cycads, Ginkgo, Gnetales), and angiosperms (seeds enclosed in carpel/ovary). Molecular phylogenetics indicates angiosperms are sister to gymnosperms.

### Plant Physiology and Hormones
Plant physiology covers internal chemical/physical activities. Photoautotrophs capture sunlight; heterotrophs consume organic molecules. Respiration oxidizes carbon compounds, reversing photosynthesis. Transport occurs at multiple scales: subcellular (membranes), xylem (water/minerals from roots via transpiration stream), phloem (sucrose from leaves), and hormone signaling. Essential nutrients (N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S) come from soil mineral breakdown. **Hormones:** Auxins (IAA) mediate tropic responses (light/gravity) and enable tissue culture. Cytokinins (zeatin) promote cell division, bud development, chloroplast greening. Gibberellins (diterpenes) break seed dormancy, elongate stems, induce flowering. Abscisic acid (ABA) inhibits division, promotes seed maturation/dormancy/stomatal closure. Ethylene (gaseous, from methionine) regulates fruit ripening and abscission; synthetic ethephon used industrially. Jasmonates (from jasmine oil) regulate wound response and systemic acquired resistance. Light acts as a signal via phytochromes, driving photomorphogenesis.

### Plant Anatomy and Morphology
Anatomy studies internal structure; morphology studies external form. Plant cells feature primary walls (cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin), large vacuoles, and plastids (chloroplasts, amyloplasts, elaioplasts). Streptophytes divide via phragmoplast/cell plate. Vascular plants have aerial shoots (stems, photosynthetic leaves, reproductive structures) and subterranean roots (root hairs, non-photosynthetic). Non-vascular plants lack true roots; most tissues photosynthesize. Root and shoot systems are interdependent (food vs. water/minerals) and can regenerate each other (adventitious roots/shoots, callus dedifferentiation). Xylem and phloem connect them. Stems support, store (water in cacti, food in tubers), or reproduce vegetatively (stolons). Leaves (foliage leaves) gather light. Gymnosperms have naked seeds; angiosperms have flowers and enclosed seeds. Woody plants (many gymnosperms/angiosperms) undergo secondary growth producing wood (secondary xylem) and bark (secondary phloem + cork). Morphological categories (root, stem, leaf, trichome) form a continuum linked by intermediate forms.

### Systematic Botany
Systematic botany classifies organisms by evolutionary history (phylogeny). Linnaean taxonomy (Kingdom, Phylum/Division, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species) uses binomial nomenclature (*Genus species*, italicized) governed by the International Code of Nomenclature (ICN). Modern taxonomy aligns with Darwinian common descent, not just shared morphology. Cladistics distinguishes homoplasies (convergent), plesiomorphies (ancestral), and apomorphies (derived/shared innovations); only apomorphies evidence common ancestry, summarized in cladograms. Since the 1990s, molecular phylogenetics (DNA sequences) dominates, providing "direct access to the genetic basis of evolution." This reclassified fungi as closer to animals than plants. The 1998 Angiosperm Phylogeny Group DNA analysis resolved flowering plant family relationships. Ongoing debates persist on taxonomic ranks and classification methods, accelerated by computational and microscopical advances.

## Terms
- ****Embryophytes**** — Land plants; multicellular eukaryotes with alternating haploid (gametophyte) and diploid (sporophyte) generations, including bryophytes and vascular plants.
- ****Binomial nomenclature**** — Linnaeus's two-part naming system (Genus + specific epithet) providing a single worldwide scientific name for each species.
- ****Oxygenic photosynthesis**** — Process in chloroplasts using light energy to convert CO₂ and H₂O into sugars, releasing O₂ as a by-product.
- ****Totipotency**** — Capacity of a single plant cell to dedifferentiate and regenerate a whole organism; proposed by Haberlandt (1902).
- ****Apomixis**** — Asexual reproduction via seeds without fertilization, producing embryos genetically identical to the parent.
- ****Polyploidy**** — Condition of having more than two paired chromosome sets; autopolyploidy (within species) or allopolyploidy (hybrid origin); major speciation mechanism.
- ****Epigenetics**** — Heritable changes in gene expression (e.g., DNA methylation, repressor proteins) without DNA sequence alteration; regulates cellular differentiation.
- ****Phylogenetics**** — Study of evolutionary relationships; modern molecular phylogenetics uses DNA sequences to construct cladograms (tree diagrams).
- ****Phytohormones**** — Endogenous signaling molecules (auxins, cytokinins, gibberellins, ABA, ethylene, jasmonates) coordinating growth, development, and stress responses.
- ****Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG)**** — International collaboration publishing a DNA-sequence-based classification of flowering plants (first 1998).

## Debates and open questions
- **Classification stability:** Despite molecular phylogenetics, taxonomists debate optimal ranks and groupings for plant taxa; the Linnaean hierarchy may not perfectly reflect continuous evolutionary divergence.
- **Species concepts in plants:** Weak species boundaries, frequent hybridization, polyploidy, and apomixis challenge the biological species concept; no universal alternative is agreed upon.
- **Epigenetic inheritance:** The extent, mechanisms, and evolutionary significance of transgenerational epigenetic inheritance (paramutations) remain under investigation.
- **Morphological continuum:** The traditional organ categories (root, stem, leaf) are linked by intermediates; whether process-based morphology supersedes structural typology is unresolved.
- **Plant stewardship and global change:** How plant management can mitigate climate change, biodiversity loss, food insecurity, and invasive species is an urgent applied research frontier.
- **Origin of angiosperms:** While APG resolved family relationships, the identity and characteristics of the earliest angiosperm lineages remain active research areas.
- **Non-Mendelian organelle inheritance:** The mechanisms and evolutionary reasons for differential paternal/maternal chloroplast inheritance in gymnosperms vs. angiosperms are not fully understood.
