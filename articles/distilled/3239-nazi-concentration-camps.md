# Nazi concentration camps

## Overview
Nazi Germany operated a vast system of concentration camps from 1933 to 1945, imprisoning millions in inhumane conditions. The system evolved from temporary detention centers for political opponents into a permanent apparatus of terror, forced labor, and mass murder, becoming a central instrument of Nazi persecution and the Holocaust.

## Timeline
- **30 Jan 1933** — Adolf Hitler becomes Chancellor of Germany.
- **3 Mar 1933** — First concentration camp, Nohra, is established.
- **26 Jun 1933** — Theodor Eicke becomes commandant of Dachau, creating a model for future camps.
- **30 Jun 1934** — SA purge; SS takes exclusive control of all concentration camps.
- **17 Jun 1936** — Heinrich Himmler is appointed Chief of German Police, consolidating SS power.
- **1938** — Mass arrests of "habitual criminals" and "asocials" cause rapid camp expansion.
- **Nov 1938** — 26,000 Jewish men are arrested after Kristallnacht and sent to camps.
- **Sep 1939** — Prisoners are murdered in false-flag operations to justify the invasion of Poland.
- **Apr 1941** — SS orders the systematic murder of ill and exhausted prisoners unable to work.
- **1942** — Camp system shifts focus to war production; death rates skyrocket.
- **Jan 1945** — Death marches begin as the Allies advance; hundreds of thousands die.
- **1944-45** — Camps are gradually liberated by Allied forces.

## Body

### Origins and Early System (1933–1937)
The camps were created in 1933 to suppress opponents after Hitler's rise. The Reichstag Fire Decree provided the legal basis for detention without trial. Initially, about 70 makeshift camps held an estimated 50,000 prisoners, 80% of whom were Communists. Following the 1934 SA purge, the SS gained exclusive control. Under Eicke, Dachau became the model camp with a brutal disciplinary code and prisoner functionaries (kapos). By 1935, the system had shrunk to five camps, but Himmler's 1936 appointment as Chief of German Police led to reorganization and expansion with new, purpose-built camps like Sachsenhausen (1936) and Buchenwald (1937).

### Expansion and Radicalization (1937–1941)
The prisoner population tripled between 1937 and 1939, driven by mass arrests of those labeled "habitual criminals" and "asocials," making political prisoners a minority. The SS began exploiting prisoner labor for construction. After the annexation of Austria and the Sudetenland, and especially after Kristallnacht in November 1938, thousands of Jews were arrested and sent to camps. World War II led to further expansion, with new camps like Auschwitz (1940) and the first satellite camps. The 1941 invasion of the Soviet Union triggered systematic murder, beginning with the killing of ill prisoners and selected Soviet POWs.

### The Camp System in World War II
During the war, the camps became increasingly lethal. The SS Main Economic and Administrative Office (WVHA) integrated them into the war economy, and from 1942, prisoner labor was used for armaments production, creating hundreds of subcamps near factories. Auschwitz became the largest camp and a major Holocaust center, with over 70,000 registered prisoners by August 1943. Conditions were brutal, with starvation, disease, and arbitrary violence. Treatment was determined by nationality, with Jews, Slavs, and other "undesirables" facing the harshest conditions. The death rate peaked in 1942, with an estimated half of all prisoners admitted that year dying within months.

### Organization, Conditions, and Forced Labor
The camps were run by the SS-Totenkopfverbände (Death's Head Units). Prisoners were forced into hard labor, evolving from valueless tasks before the war to critical armaments production by 1943, though this constituted only about 1% of Germany's arms output. Conditions were designed to be deadly; wartime deprivation led to mass death from malnutrition and disease. A hierarchy among prisoners, with German "functionaries" often wielding power, created a brutal internal dynamic.

### Public Perception and Statistics
Nazi propaganda portrayed prisoners as dangerous criminals. During the war, the visibility of camps increased through subcamps, leading to widespread civilian awareness, though often met with fear or indifference. Historians estimate about 1.65 million people were registered as prisoners, of whom at least 1 million died during imprisonment. Including approximately 1 million Jews gassed immediately upon arrival at Auschwitz, the total death toll is estimated at 1.8 to over 2 million. The system comprised 27 main camps and over 1,100 satellite camps by 1945.

### Death Marches and Liberation
As Allied forces advanced in 1944-45, the SS evacuated camps in "death marches" to keep prisoners as bargaining chips. Hundreds of thousands died from exhaustion, exposure, and execution. The liberation of the camps by Western and Soviet forces in 1944-45 revealed the atrocities to the world. Many prisoners died shortly after liberation due to their weakened state.

## Terms
- **Konzentrationslager**: German term for concentration camp.
- **SS-Totenkopfverbände**: The "SS Death's Head Units" that guarded the camps.
- **Concentration Camps Inspectorate (IKL)**: The SS office that administered the camp system.
- **SS Main Economic and Administrative Office (WVHA)**: The SS office that integrated the camps into the war economy from 1942.
- **Prisoner functionaries (Kapos)**: Prisoners assigned by the SS to oversee other prisoners.
- **Death marches**: Forced evacuations of camps ahead of Allied advances, resulting in mass deaths.
- **Wiedergutmachung**: German policy of "making good again," providing compensation to some survivors.
- **Satellite camps (Subcamps)**: Smaller camps administratively linked to a main camp, often near work sites.

## Debates and Open questions
- The exact number of subcamps is debated, with estimates ranging from 186 at the end of 1939 to over 1,100 cumulatively by 1945.
- The precise death toll is uncertain; estimates for registered prisoners who died range from nearly 1 million to at least 1.1 million, with the total including immediate gassing victims reaching over 2 million.
- The extent of German public knowledge and complicity remains a subject of historical analysis.
