# Journey to the West

## Overview
*Journey to the West* is a 16th-century Chinese novel attributed to Wu Cheng'en, widely regarded as one of the great works of Chinese literature. It is a fictionalized account of the 7th-century Buddhist monk Xuanzang's pilgrimage to India, embellished with fantasy, folklore, and spiritual allegory. The novel is a foundational text for East Asian popular culture, blending comic adventure with satire and religious reflection.

## Timeline
- **629 AD** — The historical monk Xuanzang leaves Chang'an for India, defying an imperial travel ban.
- **646 AD** — Xuanzang returns to Chang'an with Buddhist scriptures.
- **1592** — The earliest surviving edition of *Journey to the West* is published in Nanjing.
- **1625** — Earliest known record attributing the novel to Wu Cheng'en.
- **1942** — Arthur Waley publishes the influential abridged English translation, *Monkey*.
- **1977–83** — Anthony C. Yu publishes the first complete, scholarly English translation.

## Body

### Historical Basis and Creation
The novel is based on the real journey of the monk Xuanzang (602–664), who traveled to India to obtain Buddhist scriptures. His own account, *Records of the Western Regions*, provided the broad outline. Stories of his journey circulated in Chinese folklore for centuries before being compiled into the novel. The modern 100-chapter form dates to the 16th century, with the 1592 Nanjing edition being the earliest surviving text. While traditionally attributed to Wu Cheng'en, this authorship is debated by scholars, as the work is a synthesis of earlier folk tales, dramas, and oral traditions.

### Plot and Structure
The novel's 100 chapters are divided into four parts. The first introduces Sun Wukong, the Monkey King, who gains immense power and immortality, rebels against Heaven, and is imprisoned by the Buddha for 500 years. The second part introduces the monk Tang Sanzang (also called Tripitaka), who is sent by the Buddha to retrieve scriptures from India. The third and longest section details the episodic journey, where Tang Sanzang, freed Sun Wukong, and three other disciples—Zhu Bajie (Pigsy), Sha Wujing (Sandy), and the White Dragon Horse—face 81 tribulations, battling demons and overcoming obstacles. The final chapter describes their return and heavenly rewards.

### Characters and Allegory
The main characters are allegorical. Sun Wukong, born from a stone, represents the "monkey mind" of human thought and impulse; he is a powerful trickster controlled only by a magical headband. Tang Sanzang symbolizes the human heart or spirit, physically weak but spiritually resolute. Zhu Bajie, a former celestial marshal reborn as a pig-man, embodies base desires like greed and lust. Sha Wujing, a former general, represents obedience and conformity. The White Dragon Horse signifies the human will. Their cooperation is essential for spiritual success.

### Themes and Cultural Impact
The novel is deeply rooted in Chinese folk religion, Buddhism, Taoism, and Confucianism. It is a progenitor of the *xianxia* genre, which combines martial arts with fantasy. Its pantheon of deities and demons reflects enduring Chinese religious attitudes. The work is simultaneously a comic adventure, a satire of bureaucracy, and a spiritual allegory. It has inspired countless adaptations in East Asian media, including manga, anime, and television series.

## Terms
- **Xuanzang**: The historical 7th-century Chinese Buddhist monk whose pilgrimage to India is the novel's basis.
- **Sun Wukong**: The Monkey King, a powerful, immortal monkey who becomes Tang Sanzang's first disciple.
- **Tang Sanzang (Tripitaka)**: The Buddhist monk protagonist undertaking the journey to retrieve scriptures.
- **Zhu Bajie (Pigsy)**: A former celestial marshal, now a pig-like monster, representing human greed and lust.
- **Sha Wujing (Sandy)**: A former celestial general, now a river ogre, representing obedience.
- **Guanyin**: The Bodhisattva of Compassion who orchestrates the pilgrimage and provides the disciples.
- **81 Tribulations**: The series of trials the pilgrims must endure before attaining enlightenment.
- **Faxiang**: The school of Buddhism founded by the historical Xuanzang through his translation work.

## Debates and Open Questions
The authorship of the novel is a major scholarly debate. While traditionally credited to Wu Cheng'en, this attribution is contested. Evidence includes a 1625 local gazetteer mentioning Wu wrote a work called *Journey to the West*, but scholars note this could refer to an earlier version. The novel's synthesis of folk material makes definitive authorship difficult to establish. Another debate concerns the novel's interpretation: whether it is primarily a secular folk comedy, as argued by scholar Hu Shi, or a work with deeper spiritual and allegorical meaning, a view supported by more recent scholarship.
