# Cartography

## Overview
Cartography is the science and art of making and using maps to model reality or imagined realities, communicating spatial information effectively. It combines technical processes like projections and generalization with aesthetic design, forming the foundation for modern geographic information systems (GIS) and shaping how we understand and interact with the world.

## Timeline
- **c. 7th millennium BCE** — Possible earliest known map: a wall painting at Çatalhöyük, Anatolia.
- **4th millennium BCE** — Alpine rock carvings at Mount Bego and Valcamonica interpreted as depicting cultivated plots.
- **c. 1600 BCE** — Minoan "House of the Admiral" wall painting shows a seaside community.
- **14th–12th centuries BCE** — Engraved map of Nippur from the Kassite period in Babylonia.
- **9th century BCE** — Oldest surviving world maps from Babylonia.
- **6th century BCE** — Greek cartography begins with Anaximander.
- **2nd century CE** — Ptolemy writes *Geographia*, containing his world map of the Ecumene.
- **1092 CE** — Chinese scientist Su Song publishes star maps on an equidistant cylindrical projection.
- **1154 CE** — Arab geographer al-Idrisi produces the *Tabula Rogeriana*, the most accurate world map for three centuries.
- **1492 CE** — Martin Behaim creates the oldest extant globe of the Earth.
- **1507 CE** — Martin Waldseemüller's world map first uses the name "America."
- **1569 CE** — Gerardus Mercator publishes his navigational Mercator projection map.
- **1570 CE** — Abraham Ortelius creates the first true modern atlas, *Theatrum Orbis Terrarum*.
- **1620 (c.)** — Creation of the Selden map, a Chinese mercantile map challenging historical misconceptions.
- **1689 CE** — Treaty of Nerchinsk uses cartography to settle a border dispute between Russia and the Qing Dynasty.
- **1715 CE** — Herman Moll publishes the "Beaver Map," an example of common plagiarism in Enlightenment-era cartography.
- **1748 CE** — Jean B. B. d'Anville creates the first map of Africa with blank spaces for unknown territories.
- **1884–1885 CE** — Berlin Conference uses maps to cement European territorial claims in Africa.
- **2000 CE** — Removal of Selective Availability in GPS improves consumer-grade accuracy.
- **2004 CE** — OpenStreetMap is invented, enabling global, collaborative digital mapping.
- **2005 CE** — Google Earth is launched, revolutionizing public access to satellite imagery and virtual globes.

## Body

### History and Evolution
Cartography evolved from prehistoric depictions to a precise science. Ancient civilizations in Babylonia, Greece, Rome, China, and India created early maps for navigation, land management, and cosmology. The medieval period saw the production of *mappae mundi* in Europe and highly accurate atlases like al-Idrisi's *Tabula Rogeriana* in the Arab world. The Renaissance and Age of Discovery, fueled by new technologies like the compass and printing press, transformed cartography. Innovations included the Mercator projection (1569) for navigation and the first modern atlas by Ortelius (1570). The Enlightenment saw widespread use of copper-plate intaglio printing but also rampant plagiarism. The modern period introduced aerial photography, satellites, and digital technologies like GIS, GPS, and the internet, democratizing map creation and access.

### Map Design and Process
The cartographic process involves selecting, generalizing, and symbolizing geographic information for a specific purpose and audience. Key design elements include **map projections** (flattening the Earth's surface, which always introduces distortion), **generalization** (simplifying detail for clarity at a given scale), **symbology** (using visual variables like color and shape), **typography**, and **layout**. The goal is to create a map that is both aesthetically pleasing and functionally effective. Modern technology has shifted focus from production skill to design quality.

### Map Types and Deconstruction
Maps are broadly categorized as **general** (for a wide audience, like topographic maps) or **thematic** (showing specific data, like population density). **Topographic maps** detail physical terrain, while **topological maps** (like subway maps) prioritize relational information over geographic accuracy. The study of **deconstruction** examines how maps can be biased instruments of power. For example, the Mercator projection has been criticized for distorting the size of continents in the Global South, and maps were historically used as tools of European imperialism and colonization in Africa.

### Deliberate Errors and Professionalism
Some maps contain deliberate errors, such as "trap streets" for copyright protection or fictitious features like "Mount Richard" as a prank. The field is supported by professional societies (e.g., International Cartographic Association) and academic journals that promote research and standards in cartography and GIScience.

## Terms
- **Cartography**: The study and practice of making and using maps.
- **Map Projection**: A systematic transformation of the latitudes and longitudes of locations on the surface of a sphere into locations on a plane.
- **Generalization**: The process of adjusting the level of detail in geographic information to be appropriate for the scale and purpose of a map.
- **Thematic Cartography**: The practice of making maps that show the spatial distribution of one or more data themes for a specific geographic area.
- **Topographic Map**: A detailed and accurate map showing both natural and man-made features of the terrain.
- **Topological Map**: A map that represents spatial relationships and connectivity, often disregarding scale and precise geography for clarity.
- **GIS (Geographic Information System)**: A framework for gathering, managing, and analyzing spatial and geographic data.
- **Deconstruction**: The critical analysis of maps to understand their inherent biases, cultural influences, and use as instruments of power.
- **Intaglio**: A printmaking technique in which an image is incised into a plate, used for high-quality map printing from the Renaissance onward.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The definition and identification of the earliest known map is debated due to ambiguous artifacts like the Çatalhöyük wall painting.
- The precise date of creation for the Selden map (c. 1620) is debated, challenging historical misconceptions about East Asian cartography.
- The origins of color use in maps are debated: was it first for information or aesthetics?
- Deconstructionist scholarship debates the extent to which cartography was an inherent tool of European imperialism and how projections like the Mercator symbolize subjugation.
