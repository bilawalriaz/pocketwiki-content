# Alexander the Great

## Overview

Alexander III of Macedon (356–323 BC), known as Alexander the Great, was king of the ancient Greek kingdom of Macedon. He succeeded his father Philip II in 336 BC at age 20 and spent most of his reign campaigning through Asia and Egypt, creating one of the largest empires in history—stretching from Greece to northwestern India—by age 30. Undefeated in battle, he is considered one of history's greatest military commanders. His conquests spread Greek culture across the known world, ushering in the Hellenistic period and influencing regions as far east as the Indian subcontinent.

- **336 BC** — Philip II assassinated; Alexander proclaimed king at age 20

- **323 BC** — Dies in Babylon at age 32; empire disintegrates into wars among the Diadochi

## Body

### Early life and education

Alexander was born in Pella to Philip II of the Argead dynasty and Olympias, daughter of King Neoptolemus I of Epirus. Legends surrounded his birth: Plutarch reports Olympias dreamed a thunderbolt struck her womb, and Philip dreamed he sealed it with a lion's image—interpreted as signs of divine parentage. Alexander could claim descent from Heracles through his father and from Achilles through his mother's family. He was raised by a nurse, Lanike, and tutored by the strict Leonidas and Lysimachus of Acarnania. At age ten, he tamed the horse Bucephalas, which Philip bought for him; the horse carried him to India and a city was named after it upon its death.

At age 13, Philip chose Aristotle as Alexander's tutor, providing the Temple of the Nymphs at Mieza as a classroom; in return, Philip rebuilt Aristotle's hometown of Stageira, which he had razed, and repopulated it by buying and freeing enslaved ex-citizens or pardoning those in exile. Alexander studied medicine, philosophy, morals, religion, logic, and art alongside the children of Macedonian nobles—including Ptolemy, Hephaestion, and Cassander—who became his "Companions" and future generals. Under Aristotle, Alexander developed a passion for Homer's *Iliad*, which he carried on campaigns. Persian exiles at the Macedonian court also gave him knowledge of Persian affairs.

### Heir of Philip II

At age 16, Alexander became regent while Philip campaigned against the Thracians. He suppressed a revolt by the Maedi tribe and founded the city Alexandropolis. In 338 BC, father and son marched south; at the Battle of Chaeronea, Philip commanded the right wing and Alexander the left. Philip deliberately retreated to break the Athenian line, and Alexander was first to break the Theban lines, securing victory. Philip then established the League of Corinth and was named Hegemon (Supreme Commander), announcing plans to attack Persia.

Philip's marriage to Cleopatra Eurydice in 338 BC threatened Alexander's succession, since any son of hers would be fully Macedonian while Alexander was only half-Macedonian. At the wedding banquet, her uncle Attalus prayed for a legitimate heir; Alexander threw a cup at him, and Philip drew his sword but slipped and fell. Alexander fled with his mother to Illyria, returning after six months through the mediation of Demaratus. A subsequent offer by the Carian satrap Pixodarus to marry his daughter to Alexander's half-brother Philip Arrhidaeus led Alexander to intervene; Philip stopped the negotiations and exiled four of Alexander's friends.

### King of Macedon

Philip was assassinated in 336 BC by Pausanias, captain of his bodyguards, and Alexander was proclaimed king at age 20. He eliminated rivals: his cousin Amyntas IV was executed, and Cleopatra Eurydice and her daughter were burned alive by Olympias. News of Philip's death sparked revolts; Alexander responded quickly, forcing Thessaly to surrender by outflanking its army over Mount Ossa, and was recognized as leader of the Amphictyonic League. At Corinth, he took the title of Hegemon and met Diogenes the Cynic, who asked him to step out of the sunlight—a reply that delighted Alexander.

In 335 BC, Alexander secured his northern borders, defeating the Thracians, Triballi, and Getae, then the Illyrian chieftain Cleitus and King Glaukias. While he campaigned north, Thebes rebelled; Alexander razed the city, cowing Athens and leaving Greece temporarily at peace. He left Antipater as regent and set out for Asia.

### Conquest of the Persian Empire

Alexander's invasion is considered a supreme example of a "strategic line" of war: he secured his Greek base, conquered the coastline to deny the Persians naval bases, then confronted the Persian army directly. He crossed the Hellespont in 334 BC with approximately 48,100 soldiers, 6,100 cavalry, and 120 ships, throwing a spear into Asian soil to signal his intent to conquer. After victories at the Granicus and Issus, he captured the Levant, besieged and destroyed Tyre, and took Egypt, where he was declared son of the deity Amun at Siwa and founded Alexandria.

At Gaugamela in 331 BC, Alexander decisively defeated Darius III, who fled and was later killed by his own satrap Bessus. Alexander captured Babylon, Susa, and Persepolis, where a fire destroyed the palace—possibly deliberate revenge for Xerxes' burning of Athens. He pursued Bessus into Central Asia, founding cities including Kandahar and Alexandria Eschate, and defeated the Sogdian rebel Spitamenes. Alexander adopted Persian dress and the custom of proskynesis (prostration before superiors), which alienated his Greek troops; he eventually abandoned it. Plots against his life led to the executions of Philotas and Parmenion, and Alexander personally killed Cleitus the Black in a drunken argument.

### Indian campaign and last years

After marrying Roxana to cement relations with his new satrapies, Alexander invaded India in 327/326 BC. He defeated the Aspasioi and Assakenoi, captured the fortress of Aornos, and won an epic victory over King Porus at the Battle of the Hydaspes, appointing Porus as satrap. He founded two cities, Bucephala and Nicaea. Fearing further campaigns against the Nanda Empire, his army mutinied at the Beas River, forcing him to turn back. He suffered a near-fatal arrow wound during the Mallian campaign and lost many men crossing the Gedrosian Desert.

Returning to Persia, Alexander executed misbehaving satraps, paid off his soldiers' debts, and faced a mutiny at Opis over his adoption of Persian customs. He held a mass marriage of his officers to Persian noblewomen at Susa. His closest friend Hephaestion died, devastating him. Alexander died in Babylon on 10 or 11 June 323 BC at age 32, possibly from poisoning (white hellebore has been proposed) or natural causes such as typhoid fever. His last plans—including the conquest of Arabia and Carthage—were rejected by his successors.

## Terms

- **Hegemon**: Greek title meaning "leader" or "Supreme Commander," held by Philip and Alexander over the League of Corinth.
- **League of Corinth**: Hellenic alliance of Greek city-states (except Sparta) created by Philip II, used to launch the invasion of Persia.
- **Diadochi**: "The Successors"—Alexander's generals who fought 40 years of wars after his death, eventually dividing the empire into Ptolemaic Egypt, Seleucid Syria/Persia, and Antigonid Macedonia.
- **Hellenistic period**: The era after Alexander's death (c. 323 BC) marked by the spread of Greek culture, language, and influence across the eastern Mediterranean and Asia.
- **Proskynesis**: Persian custom of prostration or kissing the hand before a superior; Greeks saw it as reserved for deities, and Alexander's adoption of it alienated his troops.
- **Sarissa**: A 6-meter (20 ft) spear used by the Macedonian phalanx, developed by Philip II.
- **Koine**: The "common" Greek dialect that emerged from the mixing of Greeks in Alexander's army and became the lingua franca of the Hellenistic world.
- **Alexander Romance**: A legendary text, falsely ascribed to Callisthenes, that coalesced centuries of myths about Alexander; it went through over one hundred recensions and translations.
- **Greco-Buddhism**: The syncretism of Hellenistic and Buddhist cultures in Central and South Asia, influencing Buddhist art and the portrayal of the Buddha.

## Debates and open questions

- **Cause of death**: Ancient sources differ—Plutarch describes a fever after drinking; Diodorus reports pain after a bowl of unmixed wine. Poisoning theories implicate Antipater, but the twelve-day illness argues against available poisons; modern proposals include white hellebore, typhoid fever, malaria, and other diseases.
- **Succession**: Alexander's reported deathbed reply "to the strongest" (tôi kratistôi) may have been a mishearing of "to Craterus" (tôi Kraterôi); Arrian and Plutarch claim he was speechless, suggesting the story is apocryphal.
- **Historicity of "last plans"**: Diodorus records plans for conquering Arabia and Carthage, building 1,000 ships, and transplanting populations; scholars debate whether these were exaggerated by Perdiccas or invented by later authors.
- **Sexuality**: Ancient sources are ambiguous; Athenaeus claims Alexander was "excessively keen on boys," but no contemporary explicitly describes a sexual relationship with Hephaestion, though they were compared to Achilles and Patroclus.
- **Divine claims**: Whether Alexander truly believed he was the son of Zeus-Ammon or used the claim pragmatically to rule culturally disparate peoples remains debated.
- **Reliability of sources**: All contemporary accounts are lost; later writers (Arrian, Diodorus, Curtius, Plutarch, Justin) relied on now-lost originals, with Arrian generally considered most reliable.
