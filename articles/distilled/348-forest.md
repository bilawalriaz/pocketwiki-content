# Forest

## Overview
A forest is a tree-dominated terrestrial ecosystem covering approximately 31 percent of Earth’s land area (4.14 billion hectares in 2025). Forests are the planet’s largest terrestrial ecosystems by area and account for 75 percent of gross primary production and 80 percent of plant biomass. They are classified into biomes—boreal, temperate, tropical, and subtropical—driven by latitude, precipitation, and temperature. Human activity has reduced global forest cover significantly over millennia; while the net rate of loss has slowed since the 1990s, deforestation driven by commodity production (wood, beef, soy, palm oil) continues, especially in the tropics. Forests provide critical ecosystem services—carbon sequestration, rainfall regulation, water purification, and biodiversity habitat—and support the livelihoods of forest-dependent peoples, including Indigenous communities who manage lands with higher biodiversity intactness. Sustainable forest management seeks to balance ecological, social, and economic values.

## Timeline
- **~390 million years ago (Middle Devonian)** — First known forests appear with cladoxylopsid plants (e.g., *Calamophyton*).
- **Late Devonian** — *Archaeopteris* spreads globally; first plant to cast shade, form soil, and create forest floor duff, promoting freshwater fish.
- **Past 2,000 years** — European forest cover reduced from ~80% to 34%.
- **1990–2020** — Global forest area decreased by 178 million hectares (net loss).
- **1990s** — Net forest loss averaged 7.8 million hectares per year.
- **1998** — China bans logging following erosion and flooding.
- **2000–2010** — Net loss slows to 5.2 million hectares per year due to reforestation (China, Russia) and natural expansion.
- **2010–2020** — Net loss further slows to 4.7 million hectares per year; annual deforestation rate estimated at 10 million hectares (2015–2020).
- **2025** — Global forest area estimated at 4.14 billion hectares (31% of land); legally protected forest area reaches 813 million hectares (~20% of total).

## Body

### Definitions and Classification
No single definition of "forest" is universally accepted; over 800 exist. The FAO defines forest as land >0.5 hectares with trees >5 meters tall and >10% canopy cover, excluding agricultural or urban land. Definitions fall into three categories: **administrative** (legal designation, regardless of vegetation), **land-use** (primary purpose, e.g., timber harvest, even if cleared), and **land-cover** (physical tree density/canopy thresholds). Land-cover definitions vary on the forest–woodland–savanna boundary (canopy cover thresholds from 10% to 100%). Forests are classified by biome (boreal, temperate, tropical/subtropical), leaf longevity (evergreen/deciduous), dominant leaf type (broadleaf/coniferous/mixed), physiognomy (old-growth vs. second-growth), and human alteration. The UNEP-WCMC system simplifies these into 26 major types grouped into six categories: temperate needleleaf, temperate broadleaf/mixed, tropical moist, tropical dry, sparse trees/savanna, and plantations.

### Ecology and Structure
Forests contain ~606 gigatonnes (Gt) of living biomass and 59 Gt of dead wood (2025 estimate: 709 Gt total biomass, 171 tonnes/ha). Carbon stock is ~714 Gt (172 tonnes/ha), with 46% in soil, 44% in living biomass, and 10% in litter/deadwood. Vertical structure creates distinct layers: **forest floor** (decomposing litter, detritivores, mycorrhizal networks), **understory** (shade-tolerant shrubs/young trees), **canopy** (intertwined mature crowns, peak photosynthesis), and **emergent layer** (tropical rainforests only, scattered giants above canopy). This three-dimensional complexity increases with lower disturbance and higher tree diversity. Biodiversity peaks in tropical moist forests (e.g., Amazon, Congo, Southeast Asia) and montane regions; temperate forests host fewer species with wider ranges. Human population density and agriculture correlate with biodiversity loss (Europe, parts of Asia, North America). Fragmentation: 49% of forest area is relatively intact, 9% highly fragmented; 80% occurs in patches >1 million hectares.

### Forest Types (UNEP-WCMC Categories)
- **Temperate needleleaf**: Higher northern latitudes, nutrient-poor soils; dominated by conifers (*Pinus, Picea, Larix, Abies, Pseudotsuga, Tsuga*). Southern Hemisphere conifers mix with broadleaf.
- **Temperate broadleaf and mixed**: Warmer temperate zones; include deciduous forests (US, China, Japan), evergreen rainforests (Japan, Chile, Tasmania), sclerophyllous forests (Mediterranean, California, Australia), and *Nothofagus* forests (Chile, New Zealand).
- **Tropical moist**: Lowland evergreen rainforests (Amazon *terra firme*, *várzea/igapó*, Congo Basin, Southeast Asian dipterocarp), peat swamps, and montane forests (upper/lower formations). Seasonal tropical forests ("jungle") extend to Tropics of Cancer/Capricorn.
- **Tropical dry**: Seasonal drought drives deciduousness; sclerophyllous on poor soils; thorn forest with prolonged drought/grazing; transitions to savanna on very poor soils with fire/herbivory.
- **Sparse trees and savanna**: Transition zones. Boreal: open taiga, lichen woodland, forest-tundra. Tropical: savanna (open canopy, continuous grass layer) despite high tree density.
- **Plantations**: Mono-specific, even-aged, intensively managed for timber/pulpwood; generally lower biodiversity value but can provide ecosystem services (carbon, watershed protection) if managed accordingly.

### Area and Change Dynamics
Global forest area: 4.14 billion ha (32% of land, 0.50 ha/person). Tropical domain holds 45%, followed by boreal, temperate, subtropical. Top five countries (Russia, Brazil, Canada, USA, China) hold 54%. Europe has largest regional area (25%); South America highest proportion (49% of land). Net annual loss declined from 7.8 Mha (1990s) to 4.7 Mha (2010–2020). Deforestation (gross) estimated at 10 Mha/yr (2015–2020), down from 12 Mha/yr (2010–2015). Since 1990, 420 Mha lost to deforestation. **Forest transition** (net loss to net gain) occurs via commercial plantations, agroforestry, or spontaneous regeneration on abandoned farmland, driven by economic, ecosystem-service, or cultural motives. IPCC 1.5°C pathway requires adding forest area equal to Canada (10 million km²) by 2050. Protected forest area: 813 Mha (20% of total, 2025), up 251 Mha since 1990; Asia leads regional share (26%).

### Societal Significance
**Ecosystem services**: Carbon sequestration (sink), oxygen production (~100 kg/tree/yr), rainfall induction (Amazon trees trigger wet season 2–3 months early), climate regulation, water purification, flood mitigation, genetic reserve, timber, recreation, subsistence (fuelwood, food, fodder). **Risks**: Forests can become carbon sources if diversity/density/area decline (tropical forests may flip by 2060s; European sinks saturating). IPCC recommends combining carbon stock increases with sustainable harvest. **Forest-dependent people**: ~22% of India’s population; Indigenous peoples, Ghanaian timber/bushmeat users. Richer households extract more cash value; poorer households rely more on subsistence. **Indigenous peoples**: Manage lands with 36%+ of intact forest, higher biodiversity, lower deforestation. Legal recognition of Indigenous lands increased since 2002, but corporate land acquisition in low-income countries often bypasses consultation. Indigenous management correlates with higher plant diversity, fewer invasives, better regeneration (e.g., Wisconsin, Amazon agroforestry).

### Management
**Sustainable forest management (SFM)** integrates ecological, social, economic values with stakeholder consultation. Anthropogenic pressures: logging, urban sprawl, human-caused fires, acid rain, invasives, swidden agriculture. Natural drivers: fires, insects, disease, weather, competition. Only ~20% of original forests remain in large intact tracts (1997), mostly in Russian/Canadian boreal and Brazilian Amazon. **Case studies**: **Canada** – 4 million km² forest, 90% public, 50% allocated for harvest under SFM, 8% legally protected, 40% under varying protection; ~1.2 million km² certified (2006); clearcutting regulated. **Latvia** – 3.27 Mha (50.5% of land), increasing via afforestation of non-agricultural land; 46% public, 54% private; birch dominant (28%). **United States** – Net loss ~2 Mha (1997–2020) including urban conversion; stable/increasing in northern states; fire risk linked to lack of thinning.

## Terms
- ****Canopy cover**** — The percentage of ground covered by the vertical projection of tree crowns; used as a threshold in land-cover definitions of forest (FAO: >10%).
- ****Gross primary production (GPP)**** — Total carbon fixed by photosynthesis; forests account for ~75% of Earth’s biosphere GPP.
- ****Net primary production (NPP)**** — GPP minus plant respiration; estimated at 21.9 Gt/yr (tropical), 8.1 (temperate), 2.6 (boreal).
- ****Old-growth forest**** — Forest with natural biodiversity patterns, established seral stages, and predominantly native species; minimal human alteration.
- ****Secondary forest**** — Forest regrowing after major disturbance (e.g., timber harvest); may contain non-native species.
- ****Forest transition**** — Shift from net forest loss to net gain in a region, via plantations, agroforestry, or spontaneous regeneration.
- ****Mycorrhizal network**** — Symbiotic fungal connections between plant roots that transfer nutrients and carbon; critical for forest floor ecology.
- ****Sclerophyllous**** — Vegetation with hard, leathery, often evergreen leaves adapted to drought or nutrient-poor soils (e.g., Mediterranean, Australian forests).
- ****Carbon sink / source**** — Ecosystem that net absorbs (sink) or emits (source) atmospheric CO₂; forests risk becoming sources if degraded.
- ****Agroforestry**** — Land-use system integrating trees with crops/livestock; practiced by forest-dependent communities and Indigenous peoples.

## Debates and open questions
- **Definition consensus**: No universal definition exists; administrative, land-use, and land-cover frameworks yield different forest extents and trends, complicating global monitoring and policy targets.
- **Carbon sink permanence**: Whether tropical forests will become net carbon sources by the 2060s (as some models project) and whether European sink saturation will reverse remains uncertain; depends on climate feedbacks, deforestation rates, and management.
- **Amazon tipping point**: Deforestation and climate change may push the Amazon past a threshold where rainfall recycling collapses, triggering large-scale transition to savanna; the exact threshold (often cited ~20–25% deforestation) is debated.
- **Plantation vs. natural forest equivalence**: New plantations increase area but not biodiversity, resilience, or carbon density comparable to old-growth; how to weight them in global targets (e.g., UN Strategic Plan for Forests) is contested.
- **Indigenous land tenure vs. corporate acquisition**: Legal recognition of Indigenous territories has expanded since 2002, yet large-scale land deals in lower-income countries often proceed without free, prior, and informed consent, undermining both rights and conservation outcomes.
- **Fire management in temperate/boreal zones**: Whether active thinning and prescribed burning can sustainably reduce catastrophic wildfire risk without compromising carbon storage or biodiversity is an active management debate (e.g., US national forests).
