# Glass

## Overview
Glass is an amorphous (non-crystalline) solid formed by rapid cooling of a melt, lacking the long-range atomic order of crystals. Its transparency, chemical inertness, and ability to be shaped when hot have made it essential across practical, technological, and artistic domains—from ancient beads and windows to modern optics, telecommunications, and smartphone screens.

## Timeline
- **c. 3600 BC** — Earliest glass objects (beads) in Mesopotamia, Egypt, or Syria
- **c. 1600 BC** — Sustained glass production begins in Mesopotamia
- **c. 1500 BC** — Sustained glass production begins in Egypt
- **Late Roman Empire** — Term "glass" originates at Trier; late-Latin *glesum*
- **13th century** — Murano, Venice becomes major glassmaking hub
- **1675** — George Ravenscroft invents lead crystal glass
- **1950s** — Pilkington develops float glass process
- **21st century** — Chemically strengthened glass (e.g., Gorilla Glass) for touchscreens

## Body

### Microscopic Structure
Glass is defined as a non-crystalline solid formed by rapid melt quenching, though more broadly it refers to any amorphous solid exhibiting a glass transition upon heating. Unlike crystalline solids, glass lacks long-range atomic periodicity but retains short-range order in local atomic polyhedra. The long-held belief that glass flows appreciably at room temperature is unsupported; measured viscosities (~10^17–10^18 Pa·s) confirm negligible flow over human timescales.

### Formation from a Supercooled Liquid
If cooling is rapid enough relative to crystallization kinetics, the disordered structure of a supercooled liquid is frozen into the solid state at the glass transition temperature (Tg). This ability is termed glass-forming ability, predictable via rigidity theory. Glasses are structurally metastable compared to their crystalline counterparts, though exceptions like atactic polymers lack a crystalline analogue.

### Occurrence in Nature
Natural glasses include obsidian (volcanic), impactite (meteorite impact), fulgurite (lightning-struck sand), trinitite (nuclear test residue), and Edeowie glass (possibly from ancient fires or impacts).

### History
Obsidian was used in the Stone Age for sharp tools. Synthetic glassmaking began around 3600 BC, initially producing opaque, impure beads. True transparent glass emerged by 1500 BC in Egypt. The Romans advanced techniques, including cameo glass. Medieval Europe used glass for stained windows. The Renaissance saw Venetian innovations like cristallo. Industrial advances in the 19th–20th centuries—float glass, chemical strengthening—enabled mass production and new applications.

### Physical Properties

#### Optical
Glass refracts, reflects, and transmits light. Key properties—refractive index (1.4–2.4), Abbe number (15–100), and transmission—depend on composition. Transparency arises from the absence of light-scattering grain boundaries. Silicate glasses are opaque to IR beyond 4 μm, while heavy-metal fluoride and chalcogenide glasses transmit IR up to 18 μm. Metallic oxides impart color through selective absorption.

#### Other
Glass is brittle but can be tempered or laminated for strength. It is chemically inert under most conditions but corrodes with high alkali content. Density ranges from 2.2 g/cm³ (fused silica) to 7.2 g/cm³ (dense flint glass). Theoretical tensile strength reaches 14–35 GPa, reduced to 14–175 MPa commercially due to surface flaws. Drawn fibers can achieve up to 11.5 GPa.

#### Reputed Flow
Old windows appearing thicker at the bottom do not indicate flow; this results from historical manufacturing imperfections. A 2017 study estimated medieval glass flow at 1 nm per billion years—imperceptible on human timescales.

### Types

#### Silicate Glasses
- **Soda–lime glass**: ~70% silica, with Na₂CO₃, CaO, MgO, Al₂O₃. Most common (~90% of production). Used for windows, bottles, light bulbs.
- **Borosilicate glass**: Contains B₂O₃. Low thermal expansion, resistant to thermal shock. Used in labware, cookware.
- **Lead glass**: PbO lowers melting point, increases refractive index and brilliance. Used in fine glassware, enamels.
- **Aluminosilicate glass**: High Al₂O₃. Excellent thermal resistance. Used in fiberglass, cookware, halogen bulbs.
- **Glass-ceramics**: Partially crystalline. Near-zero thermal expansion achieved at ~70% crystalline phase, sustaining temperature changes up to 1000 °C. Used in cooktops, industrial applications.
- **Fiberglass**: Glass fiber-reinforced plastic (GRP). Lightweight, corrosion-resistant. Used in insulation, boats, aerospace.

#### Non-Silicate Glasses
Include metallic, phosphate, borate, chalcogenide, fluoride, and polymer glasses. Amorphous metals (bulk metallic glasses) and polymer glasses (e.g., polycarbonate) offer specialized properties. Molecular liquids and molten salts can form glasses under rapid cooling.

### Production
Raw materials are melted in furnaces, homogenized, and formed via blowing, pressing, or the float process. Annealing removes internal stresses. Surface treatments enhance durability, strength, or optical performance. Laboratory melts use pure chemicals and platinum crucibles.

### Color
Color arises from metal ions: FeO (green), CoO (blue), Cr₂O₃ (dark green), CuO (turquoise), CdS (red), and sulfur compounds (amber). Manganese dioxide counteracts green tints.

### Uses
- **Architecture**: Float glass in windows, structural glazing, tempered/bent forms.
- **Tableware**: Drinking glasses, lead crystal stemware.
- **Packaging**: Soda–lime bottles and jars; recyclable and chemically durable.
- **Laboratories**: Flasks, pipettes, custom-blown apparatus.
- **Optics**: Lenses, fiber optics, telescopes, microscopes.
- **Radioactive Waste**: Alkali borosilicate glasses immobilize high-level waste.
- **Art**: Stained glass, cameo glass, studio glass techniques.

## Terms
- **Amorphous solid**: Non-crystalline solid lacking long-range atomic order.
- **Glass transition temperature (Tg)**: Temperature at which a supercooled liquid becomes a glass.
- **Quenching**: Rapid cooling to prevent crystallization.
- **Float glass**: Flat glass made by floating molten glass on tin.
- **Refractive index**: Measure of light-bending ability.
- **Abbe number**: Dimensionless quantity indicating dispersion.
- **Vitreous enamel**: Glassy coating fused to a substrate at high temperature.
- **Thermal shock**: Cracking due to rapid temperature change.
- **Chemical durability**: Resistance to corrosion by water or chemicals.
- **Glass-forming ability**: Tendency of a material to form glass rather than crystallize.

## Debates and Open Questions
The classification of the glass transition as a true phase transition remains debated—it resembles a second-order transition but does not conform to equilibrium thermodynamics. Whether glass is a solid or a slow-moving liquid has been resolved empirically, yet public misconceptions persist. The exact atomic-scale dynamics during vitrification and the role of fragility in glass-forming liquids continue to be active research areas.
