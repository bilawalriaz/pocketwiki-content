# George III

## Overview
George III (1738–1820) was King of Great Britain and Ireland for 60 years, a reign defined by major wars, the loss of the American colonies, and the personal struggle of his recurrent mental illness. His rule saw the United Kingdom formed through the Acts of Union 1800 and the abolition of the transatlantic slave trade, marking a pivotal era in British imperial and constitutional history.

## Timeline
- **4 June 1738** — Born in London, first Hanoverian monarch born in Britain.
- **25 October 1760** — Succeeds his grandfather, George II, as king.
- **8 September 1761** — Marries Princess Charlotte of Mecklenburg-Strelitz.
- **1763** — Seven Years' War ends; Britain gains Canada and territories in India.
- **1775–1783** — American War of Independence; Britain loses its thirteen North American colonies.
- **1788–1789** — First major episode of mental illness; Regency crisis averted by his recovery.
- **1 January 1801** — Acts of Union take effect, creating the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland.
- **25 March 1807** — Signs the Slave Trade Act, banning the transatlantic slave trade in the British Empire.
- **1810** — Suffers final, permanent relapse into mental illness; Prince Regent appointed in 1811.
- **12 October 1814** — Becomes King of Hanover following the Congress of Vienna.
- **29 January 1820** — Dies at Windsor Castle, aged 81.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
George was born prematurely in 1738, the eldest son of Frederick, Prince of Wales. After his father's death in 1751, he became heir apparent. Educated by private tutors, he was the first British monarch to study science systematically. He succeeded to the throne in 1760 and married Charlotte of Mecklenburg-Strelitz in 1761; they had 15 children. George was a devoted family man who never took a mistress, contrasting with his predecessors and his own sons.

### Early Reign and American Crisis
George's reign began with victory in the Seven Years' War (1763), which made Britain the dominant power in North America and India. However, post-war policies, including the Royal Proclamation of 1763 and the Stamp Act (1765), aimed at taxing the American colonies to pay for their defense, sparked the principle of "no taxation without representation." Although the Stamp Act was repealed, subsequent measures like the Tea Act led to the Boston Tea Party (1773) and the Intolerable Acts. The ensuing American War of Independence (1775–1783) ended with British defeat and the recognition of American independence in the Treaty of Paris (1783). Historians debate George's personal responsibility, noting he acted as a constitutional monarch supporting his ministers' policies.

### Political and Personal Struggles
After the war, George's political influence was demonstrated in 1783 when he helped secure the appointment of William Pitt the Younger as prime minister, despite Pitt lacking a Commons majority. The King's popularity soared during the wars against Revolutionary and Napoleonic France (1793–1815). His reign saw the Acts of Union 1800, which created the United Kingdom. George's staunch opposition to Catholic emancipation, which he believed violated his coronation oath, led to Pitt's resignation in 1801. The Napoleonic Wars concluded with victory at the Battle of Waterloo in 1815.

### Mental Illness and Regency
George suffered recurrent episodes of acute mania, with a major crisis in 1788–89 and permanent illness from 1810. The exact cause is debated: a 1966 study proposed the blood disease porphyria, possibly triggered by arsenic in medicines, but recent scholarship favors a psychiatric diagnosis, most likely bipolar disorder. His final illness led to the Regency Act of 1811, with his son acting as Prince Regent until George's death in 1820.

### Slavery and Legacy
While George III never personally owned slaves, his government delayed abolition for decades. However, he signed the Slave Trade Act in 1807. Earlier, during the American Revolution, British proclamations offered freedom to enslaved people who joined the British cause, leading thousands to fight for the Crown. George was dubbed "Farmer George" for his interest in agriculture and science, funding astronomer William Herschel. His reign saw the monarchy's political power wane as it became a symbol of national identity.

## Terms
- **Constitutional monarchy**: A system where the monarch's powers are limited by law and exercised through elected ministers.
- **Regent**: A person appointed to govern when the monarch is incapacitated, as with George III's son from 1811.
- **Porphyria**: A group of metabolic disorders potentially causing neurological symptoms, once proposed as the cause of George's illness.
- **Bipolar disorder**: A mental health condition marked by extreme mood swings between mania and depression, a modern theory for George's illness.
- **Stamp Act (1765)**: A tax imposed by the British Parliament on the American colonies, requiring a stamp on printed materials, which fueled revolutionary sentiment.
- **Intolerable Acts (1774)**: Punitive laws passed by Britain after the Boston Tea Party, closing Boston's port and altering Massachusetts' government.
- **Acts of Union 1800**: Legislation merging the Kingdom of Great Britain and the Kingdom of Ireland into the United Kingdom.
- **Slave Trade Act 1807**: A law that banned the transatlantic slave trade in the British Empire.
- **Civil list annuity**: A sum granted by Parliament to cover the monarch's official household and government expenses.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Responsibility for the American Revolution**: Was George III a tyrant who stubbornly prolonged war, or a constitutional monarch acting on ministerial advice? Modern scholars increasingly see him as a victim of circumstance.
- **Nature of his illness**: Was his condition physiological (porphyria) or psychiatric (bipolar disorder)? Recent research discounts porphyria and favors a mental health diagnosis.
- **Historical reputation**: His legacy has shifted from a 19th-century view of a tyrant to a 20th-century reassessment as a much-maligned figure who embodied national resistance during the Napoleonic Wars.
