# François Mitterrand

## Overview
François Mitterrand (1916–1996) was President of France from 1981 to 1995, serving two full terms. The first left-wing president under the Fifth Republic, he remains the longest-serving republican head of state in French history. His presidency shifted from radical socialist policies to economic austerity, enacted major social reforms, and strongly advanced European integration.

## Timeline
- **26 Oct 1916** — Born in Jarnac, Charente.
- **1940–1941** — Serves as a POW; escapes twice.
- **1941–1943** — Works for the Vichy regime; begins resistance activities.
- **1943–1945** — Fully engages in the Resistance, leading the RNPG network.
- **1946–1958** — Holds multiple ministerial posts under the Fourth Republic.
- **1958** — Opposes Charles de Gaulle and the Fifth Republic's establishment.
- **1965** — First left-wing candidate to force a presidential runoff against de Gaulle.
- **1971** — Becomes First Secretary of the Socialist Party (PS).
- **10 May 1981** — Elected President, defeating Valéry Giscard d'Estaing.
- **1983** — Abandons nationalization for austerity ("tournant de la rigueur").
- **1986–1988** — First "cohabitation" with conservative PM Jacques Chirac.
- **1988** — Re-elected president, defeating Chirac.
- **1993–1995** — Second "cohabitation" with conservative PM Édouard Balladur.
- **8 Jan 1996** — Dies in Paris from prostate cancer, which he had concealed.

## Body

### Early Life and World War II
Born to a devout Catholic, conservative family, Mitterrand's early sympathies were on the nationalist right. He served in the army, was wounded and captured in 1940, and escaped German POW camps. After his escape, he worked for the Vichy regime aiding prisoners of war, a period later sparking major controversy. He is described as a "Vichysto-résistant"—someone who initially supported Marshal Pétain's regime before joining the Resistance. By 1943, he had fully committed to the Resistance, building a network of former POWs. His actions, including receiving the Vichy honorific Order of the Francisque, remain debated.

### Rise in Politics
After the war, Mitterrand entered politics, holding eleven ministerial portfolios under the Fourth Republic. He was a vocal opponent of Charles de Gaulle's return to power in 1958 and the Fifth Republic. After losing his parliamentary seat in 1958, he endured a political "crossing of the desert." He re-emerged as a unifying figure for the fragmented left, becoming president of the Socialist Party in 1971. He led the left to a narrow defeat in 1974 but won the presidency in 1981.

### First Presidential Term (1981–1988)
Mitterrand's first term began with a radical left-wing agenda: nationalizing key industries, increasing the minimum wage, reducing the work week to 39 hours, and abolishing the death penalty. He liberalized the media and launched major cultural "Grands Projets." Facing economic crisis, he executed a sharp "austerity turn" in 1983, abandoning nationalization for fiscal restraint. His government enacted progressive social reforms, including expanded pensions and parental leave. He faced the loss of his parliamentary majority in 1986, leading to the first "cohabitation" with conservative PM Jacques Chirac. A major foreign policy scandal was the 1985 bombing of the Greenpeace ship *Rainbow Warrior* by French agents, which he personally authorized.

### Second Presidential Term (1988–1995)
Re-elected in 1988, Mitterrand's second term created the minimum welfare income (RMI) and continued cultural projects like the Louvre Pyramid. He appointed Édith Cresson as France's first female prime minister in 1991. His presidency was later marred by scandals, including the contaminated blood affair and illegal wiretapping by an Elysée "anti-terror cell." He faced a second cohabitation with PM Édouard Balladur from 1993 after a crushing Socialist defeat in legislative elections.

### Foreign Policy and European Integration
Mitterrand's foreign policy continued Gaullist independence but strongly reversed de Gaulle's skepticism toward European integration. He forged a close partnership with German Chancellor Helmut Kohl, central to the signing of the Maastricht Treaty in 1992. He accepted German reunification in 1990, reportedly securing a commitment from Kohl to adopt the Euro. His 1990 La Baule speech tied French development aid to democratic reforms in former African colonies. His legacy in Africa is complicated by France's role during the 1994 Rwandan genocide, where Paris supported the Hutu-led government.

## Terms
*   **Cohabitation**: A period when the President and Prime Minister are from opposing parties, forcing them to share executive power.
*   **Fifth Republic**: The current French government, established in 1958 by Charles de Gaulle, which strengthened presidential power.
*   **Vichysto-résistant**: A term for individuals who initially supported the Vichy regime but later joined the Resistance.
*   **Tournant de la rigueur**: The 1983 "austerity turn," when Mitterrand abandoned expansionary socialist policies for fiscal restraint to combat inflation and stay within the European Monetary System.
*   **Grands Projets**: Major cultural and architectural projects initiated during Mitterrand's presidency, such as the Louvre Pyramid and the Grande Arche.
*   **Maastricht Treaty**: The 1992 treaty that created the European Union and paved the way for the Euro currency.
*   **RNPG (Rassemblement national des prisonniers de guerre)**: The "POWs National Rally," a resistance network for former prisoners of war that Mitterrand built and led during WWII.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Vichy Past**: The extent of Mitterrand's collaboration with the Vichy regime before 1943 is heavily debated. Critics point to his receipt of the Order of the Francisque and continued respect for Pétain, while defenders argue he was a "Vichysto-résistant" who used his position to aid the Resistance.
*   **Responsibility for the Holocaust**: In 1994, Mitterrand stated the French state bore no responsibility for the deportation of Jews, attributing it solely to "Vichy France." This position was publicly rejected by his successor, Jacques Chirac, in 1995.
*   **Rwandan Genocide**: France's role during the 1994 genocide is controversial. While France provided military support to the Hutu-led government before the genocide and later launched Opération Turquoise, the nature and intent of its intervention remain subjects of debate.
*   **Medical Secrecy**: The concealment of his prostate cancer throughout most of his presidency, revealed after his death, sparked debate about the ethics of medical confidentiality for public officials.
