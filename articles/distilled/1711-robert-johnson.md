# Robert Johnson

## Overview
Robert Johnson (1911–1938) was an American blues musician whose 29 recorded songs, cut in two sessions during 1936–1937, became the bedrock of Delta blues and a primary catalyst for the 1960s blues revival and rock music. Though he died at 27 with little public recognition, his synthesis of Delta slide guitar, boogie-woogie bass patterns, jazz-inflected chords, and poetic lyricism influenced Eric Clapton, Bob Dylan, Keith Richards, and Robert Plant. His poorly documented life spawned the enduring legend that he sold his soul to the devil at a crossroads for musical mastery—a narrative originally attached to unrelated bluesman Tommy Johnson. Modern scholarship, notably the 2019 biography *Up Jumped the Devil*, has reconstructed his biography from fragmented records, revealing an educated, itinerant professional who mastered multiple styles beyond the "primitivist" image later projected onto him.

- **Jun 19–20, 1937** — Second recording session (13 songs) for Don Law at Vitagraph Building, Dallas.

- **2019** — Conforth and Wardlow publish *Up Jumped the Devil*, the definitive biography.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Johnson was born in Hazlehurst, Mississippi, possibly May 8, 1911, to Julia Dodds and Noah Johnson. His mother was married to Charles Dodds, a prosperous landowner forced to flee by a lynch mob. Johnson spent roughly eight years in Memphis (c. 1912–1919) attending Carnes Avenue Colored School, receiving a formal education in music, arithmetic, and geography that distinguished him from most Delta bluesmen. Around 1919–1920, he rejoined his mother in the Mississippi Delta near Robinsonville, living on the Abbay & Leatherman Plantation with her new husband, sharecropper Will "Dusty" Willis. Census records list him as Robert Spencer. After learning of his biological father, he adopted the surname Johnson. He married Virginia Travis in February 1929; her death in childbirth—interpreted by her family as divine punishment for singing secular "devil's music"—precipitated his disappearance for ~18 months.

### Musical Apprenticeship and Itinerant Career
During his absence (c. 1930–1931), Johnson sought his natural father near Martinsville and apprenticed with guitarist Isaiah "Ike" Zimmerman. Zimmerman, rumored to have learned supernaturally in graveyards, taught Johnson in cemeteries at night for quiet. Upon returning to Robinsonville, Johnson stunned Son House—who remembered him as a bad guitarist—with a mature technique. House’s equivocal answers about a "pact with the devil" cemented the legend. Johnson married Caletta Craft in 1931; she died in 1933. From 1932 until death, he lived as a "walking" musician, traveling between Memphis, Helena, and Delta towns, occasionally reaching Chicago, New York, and Canada with Johnny Shines. He used at least eight surnames, maintained concurrent relationships in different towns, and played for tips on street corners and at dances. Associates noted he pleased audiences with pop standards and jazz, not just his dark originals, and possessed an uncanny ability to establish local rapport.

### Recording Sessions
In 1936, talent scout H.C. Speir connected Johnson to ARC salesman Ernie Oertle, who brought him to producer Don Law.
*   **San Antonio (Nov 23–25, 1936):** Recorded 16 selections (with alternate takes) in Room 414 of the Gunter Hotel. Key tracks: "Terraplane Blues" (first release, sold ~10,000 copies), "Sweet Home Chicago," "Cross Road Blues," "I Believe I'll Dust My Broom."
*   **Dallas (Jun 19–20, 1937):** Recorded 13 songs in a makeshift studio at the Vitagraph Building. These takes are noted as more "somber and introspective." Johnson recorded two takes of most songs, providing a unique comparative record for the era. He uniquely structured compositions to fit the three-minute 78-rpm side limit.

### Death and Burial
Johnson died August 16, 1938, near Greenwood, MS, at 27. No autopsy was performed; the death certificate lists no cause. Theories include:
*   **Poisoning:** David "Honeyboy" Edwards claimed a jealous husband gave Johnson whiskey laced with poison (possibly naphthalene/mothballs per Conforth/Wardlow 2019, exploiting an ulcer/esophageal varices). Tom Graves disputes strychnine due to taste/odor and speed of death.
*   **Syphilis:** The plantation owner where he died opined he died of syphilis; congenital syphilis is considered likely.
*   **Marfan Syndrome:** A 2006 hypothesis based on photos showing long fingers and a bad eye, suggesting aortic dissection.
Three sites claim his grave: Mount Zion Missionary Baptist Church (1990 cenotaph), Payne Chapel (1990 marker), and Little Zion Church (Sony marker, supported by 2019 biography).

### The Crossroads Legend
The legend that Johnson sold his soul to the devil at a crossroads originated with **Tommy Johnson** (unrelated), who claimed the pact at Dockery Plantation. The narrative transferred to Robert Johnson via Son House (explaining his rapid improvement), then expanded by writers Greil Marcus and Robert Palmer (adding the "large black man" detail). Scholars debate the "devil" figure: Harry Hyatt and others argue it reflects the African trickster deity **Legba** (crossroads wisdom), not the Christian Satan. Alan Lomax dismissed the myth, noting all blues musicians were considered "children of the Devil" by the church. David Evans counters that blues devil imagery is purely Western folkloric, with no mention of African deities in lyrics. Amiri Baraka and Cornel West dispute Lomax/Evans, framing blues as a sacred response to catastrophe.

### Musical Style and Innovations
Johnson is a master of **Delta blues** but was respected in his time for stylistic range: slide guitar, jazz chords, pop, and ragtime ("They're Red Hot").
*   **Guitar:** He played "like an orchestra" (Keith Richards), simultaneously executing driving **boogie bass patterns** (fifth-sixth oscillation on bass strings, mimicking piano boogie-woogie) and melodic lines. Johnnie Temple confirmed learning this from Johnson c. 1932–33. This "walking bass" became the foundation for Chicago blues (Elmore James, Jimmy Reed) and rock (Chuck Berry).
*   **Voice:** Masterful use of **blue notes** (microtonal pitch inflections) for emotional intensity. Eric Clapton called it "the most powerful cry in the human voice." He used the guitar as a "talking instrument"—a complementary vocal voice rooted in African tradition.
*   **Lyrics:** Highly poetic, densely sexualized metaphors ("check my oil," "don't shake my tree"). Bill Ferris compares him to Keats and Shelley.
*   **Influences:** Fused Son House (raw Delta), Skip James (haunting melody), Kokomo Arnold (vocal style), Leroy Carr (urban song structure), Lonnie Johnson (crooning/ragtime guitar), Blind Blake (ragtime), and Peetie Wheatstraw.

### Legacy and Influence
**Blues Standards:** "Sweet Home Chicago," "Dust My Broom," "Cross Road Blues," "Hellhound on My Trail," "Love in Vain," "Stop Breaking Down." Elmore James electrified "Dust My Broom" (1951), creating the Chicago shuffle beat adopted by Jimmy Reed.
**Rock Music:** Chuck Berry adapted Johnson’s boogie pattern for "Johnny B. Goode" (straight 4/4 drive). The Rock and Roll Hall of Fame (1986) cites four Johnson songs as shaping the genre.
**Key Admirers:**
*   **Eric Clapton:** "Most important blues musician ever"; recorded tribute album *Me and Mr. Johnson* (2004).
*   **Bob Dylan:** Used Johnson’s lyrical "code of language" and song forms in 1964–65.
*   **Keith Richards:** Marvelled at his one-guitar "orchestra" and thematic bending of blues form.
*   **Robert Plant:** Credits Johnson as foundational for English musicians; Led Zeppelin covered "Traveling Riverside Blues."

### Biographical Problems and Photographs
Until the 2019 Conforth/Wardlow biography, Johnson’s life was reconstructed from contradictory oral histories (Son House, Johnny Shines, David Edwards, Robert Lockwood Jr.). Researcher Mack McCormick amassed a legendary archive (donated to Smithsonian 2020) but never published.
**Photographs:** Only three verified images exist: a "studio portrait" (summer 1936) and "dime-store photo" set (Dec 1937/Jan 1938), copyrighted by Stephen LaVere. A 2008 photo with Johnny Shines was debunked by facial recognition, anachronistic clothing, and timeline errors (Johnson met Shines in 1937, photo dated 1933–34). A 2020 smiling photo from the same dime-store session, held by stepsister Annye Anderson, is accepted as authentic by Elijah Wald.

### Descendants and Estate
Johnson left no will. In 1998, the Mississippi Supreme Court ruled **Claud Johnson** (born 1931 to Virgie Jane Smith) the sole heir, awarding over $1M in royalties. Claud died in 2015.

## Terms
- ****Delta blues**** — A regional style originating in the Mississippi Delta, characterized by slide guitar, rhythmic drive, and raw vocal intensity.
- ****Boogie bass pattern / Walking bass**** — A guitar technique oscillating between the fifth and sixth degrees of the scale on bass strings, mimicking a piano boogie-woogie left hand; Johnson’s key technical innovation.
- ****Blue notes**** — Microtonal pitch inflections (flattened 3rd, 5th, 7th) essential to blues expression; Johnson used them with exceptional precision.
- ****Talking instrument**** — African/Afro-American tradition where an instrument (drums, then guitar) mimics vocal speech patterns, acting as a complementary voice.
- ****Crossroads legend**** — Faustian myth that Johnson traded his soul to the devil at a rural intersection for guitar mastery; originally attributed to Tommy Johnson.
- ****Legba**** — West African trickster deity of crossroads and communication; argued by some scholars as the cultural referent for the "devil" in blues lore.
- ****78-rpm side**** — The ~3-minute recording limit of a single side of a 10-inch shellac disc, which Johnson composed to fit precisely.
- ****Blues standard**** — A blues song widely performed/recorded over decades, recognized as essential repertoire (e.g., "Sweet Home Chicago").
- ****27 Club**** — Cultural trope listing musicians who died at age 27; Johnson is considered the inaugural member.
- ****Itinerant musician**** — A traveling performer ("walking musician") with no fixed venue, playing for tips on streets, in juke joints, and at dances.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Cause of death:** Poisoning (naphthalene vs. strychnine vs. other), syphilis complications, or Marfan syndrome aortic dissection remain unresolved; no autopsy was performed.
*   **Birth date:** May 8, 1911 is most accepted (per half-sister Carrie Thompson), but marriage licenses and census records conflict (1912/1913).
*   **Origin of the crossroads myth:** Whether it transferred organically from Tommy Johnson via Son House, or was constructed later by white revivalists (Wald, Marcus, Palmer).
*   **Nature of the "Devil":** Christian Satan vs. African Legba vs. metaphor for secular music's sinfulness in the Black church (Lomax).
*   **Historical magnitude:** Elijah Wald argues Johnson was a "minor figure" in the evolution of Black music, overshadowed by urban artists; others (Guralnick, Hall of Fame) view him as a pivotal genius.
*   **Photograph authenticity:** Only two verified images exist; several published candidates (2008, 2015) have been debunked by forensic and historical analysis.
