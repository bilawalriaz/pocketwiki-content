# Unification of Italy

## Overview
The Risorgimento ("Resurgence") was the 19th-century political and social movement that unified the Italian Peninsula into the Kingdom of Italy. Inspired by the French Revolution and fueled by nationalism, it ended centuries of foreign domination and fragmented rule. The process, marked by wars, revolutions, and diplomacy, culminated in 1861 with the proclamation of the Kingdom of Italy, though key territories like Rome and Venetia were added later.

## Timeline
- **1797** — The Italian tricolour is first adopted as a national flag by the Cispadane Republic.
- **1815** — The Congress of Vienna restores pre-Napoleonic divisions, placing much of Italy under Austrian control.
- **1820-21** — Revolts in the Kingdom of Two Sicilies and Piedmont are crushed by Austrian forces.
- **1831** — Giuseppe Mazzini founds "Young Italy" to advocate for a unified republic.
- **1848-49** — Revolutions erupt across Italy; the First Italian War of Independence ends in defeat for Piedmont.
- **1859** — The Second Italian War of Independence results in Piedmont annexing Lombardy.
- **1860** — Garibaldi's Expedition of the Thousand conquers the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies.
- **1861** — The Kingdom of Italy is proclaimed, with Victor Emmanuel II as king.
- **1866** — The Third Italian War of Independence leads to the annexation of Venetia.
- **1870** — Italian troops capture Rome, making it the capital in 1871.
- **1918-20** — Italy annexes Trentino, Trieste, and other territories after World War I, completing unification.

## Body

### Background and Early Nationalism
After the fall of the Western Roman Empire, Italy remained politically fragmented for centuries, divided among city-states, the Papal States, and foreign powers like Austria and Spain. A sense of Italian national identity began to emerge during the Renaissance with writers like Dante and Machiavelli. The French Revolution and Napoleonic era (1792–1814) destroyed old feudal structures and introduced modern ideas of citizenship and nationalism. After Napoleon's defeat, the Congress of Vienna (1815) restored conservative monarchies and Austrian dominance, suppressing liberal and nationalist movements.

### Revolutionary Movements and Key Figures
Secret societies like the Carbonari led early revolts in the 1820s and 1830s, which were suppressed by Austrian intervention. Intellectuals like Giuseppe Mazzini, founder of "Young Italy," advocated for a unified republic. The Revolutions of 1848 saw widespread uprisings and the First Italian War of Independence, but were ultimately crushed. These failures taught patriots that unification required a strong monarchy (Piedmont-Sardinia) and foreign allies, notably France. Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour, became Piedmont's prime minister in 1852, pursuing modernization and diplomacy to achieve this goal.

### Wars of Unification and the Role of Garibaldi
Cavour allied with France to defeat Austria in the Second Italian War of Independence (1859), gaining Lombardy. In 1860, Giuseppe Garibaldi led a volunteer army, the "Thousand," to conquer the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies in the south. He then handed his conquests over to King Victor Emmanuel II. The Sardinian army marched south, defeating papal forces and the remaining Neapolitan army. On 17 March 1861, the Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed. Venetia was annexed after the Third Italian War of Independence (1866), allied with Prussia against Austria.

### Completion and the "Roman Question"
Rome, the desired capital, remained under the Pope's temporal rule, protected by French troops. After the Franco-Prussian War began in 1870, France withdrew its garrison. Italian troops entered Rome in September 1870, and it was annexed following a plebiscite. The capital was officially moved from Florence to Rome in 1871. The Pope declared himself a prisoner in the Vatican, refusing to recognize the Italian state until the Lateran Treaty of 1929.

### Post-Unification Challenges and Irredentism
Unification was achieved largely on Piedmont's terms, imposing its laws, bureaucracy, and taxes on the rest of Italy. This "Piedmontization" caused resentment, especially in the south, where economic disparities and social unrest grew. Many ethnic Italian speakers remained outside the new kingdom, particularly in areas like Trentino and Trieste, fueling Italian irredentism. This movement sought to annex all territories with Italian populations, a goal partially achieved after World War I with the Treaty of Rapallo (1920).

## Terms
- **Risorgimento**: The 19th-century movement for Italian unification and national awakening.
- **Carbonari**: A secret revolutionary society active in the early 19th century, advocating for constitutional government and Italian independence.
- **Cavour**: Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour, the pragmatic prime minister of Piedmont-Sardinia who orchestrated its diplomatic and military alliances to achieve unification.
- **Garibaldi**: Giuseppe Garibaldi, a charismatic general and patriot who led volunteer forces, most famously the Expedition of the Thousand, to conquer southern Italy.
- **Mazzini**: Giuseppe Mazzini, a republican revolutionary and theorist who founded "Young Italy" and inspired nationalist sentiment.
- **Expedition of the Thousand**: Garibaldi's 1860 military campaign with a volunteer army that conquered the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies.
- **Piedmontization**: The process by which the institutions, laws, and culture of Piedmont-Sardinia were imposed on the newly unified Italy.
- **Irredentism**: The political movement advocating for the annexation of all territories considered to be part of Italy's "natural borders" due to Italian-speaking populations.
- **Roman Question**: The political problem of the Pope's temporal power in Rome and its conflict with the Italian state, resolved in 1929.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Historians debate whether the Risorgimento was a true popular revolution or a top-down process led by elites, with limited participation from the masses.
- The economic and social integration of the south remains a contentious issue. Some argue that the imposition of northern free-market policies on the south's protectionist economy caused long-term underdevelopment (the "Southern Question").
- The political significance of cultural expressions, especially Giuseppe Verdi's operas, is debated. Some see them as patriotic anthems, while others argue their political dimension was exaggerated by later nationalist historians.
- The extent to which the unification process was complete by 1871 is questioned, as the irredentist movement continued to shape Italian politics and foreign policy into the 20th century.
