# Neoclassicism

## Overview
Neoclassicism was a major Western cultural movement in the arts, literature, and architecture that drew inspiration from the art and culture of classical antiquity. It emerged in the mid-18th century as a reaction against the Rococo style and was closely linked to the Age of Enlightenment, emphasizing ideals of simplicity, symmetry, and rational order. The movement peaked in the early-to-mid 19th century and remained influential in architecture well into the 21st century.

## Timeline
- **c. 1750** — Neoclassicism begins in Rome, inspired by the rediscovery of Pompeii and Herculaneum and the writings of Johann Joachim Winckelmann.
- **1755** — Winckelmann publishes *Thoughts on the Imitation of Greek Works in Painting and Sculpture*.
- **1764** — Winckelmann publishes *History of Ancient Art*, distinguishing between Greek and Roman art.
- **c. 1760** — European Neoclassical visual arts begin in opposition to the Rococo style.
- **1769** — Christoph Willibald Gluck publishes his preface to *Alceste*, outlining a Neoclassical approach to opera.
- **1785** — Jacques-Louis David's *Oath of the Horatii* is exhibited, becoming a defining Neoclassical painting.
- **1774–1789** — The Louis XVI style marks the transition from Rococo to Classicism in French decorative arts.
- **1804–1815** — The Empire style, a second phase of Neoclassicism, flourishes under Napoleon in France.
- **1920s–1950s** — Neoclassicism in music emerges as a 20th-century movement reacting against late-Romanticism.
- **1930s–1950s** — Totalitarian regimes in the Soviet Union, Nazi Germany, and Fascist Italy adopt Neoclassical styles for state architecture.
- **1966** — Robert Venturi's *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture* questions Modernism, leading to Postmodern revivals of classical elements.

## Body

### Origins and Theory
Neoclassicism was born in Rome, largely due to the writings of Johann Joachim Winckelmann during the rediscovery of Pompeii and Herculaneum. Winckelmann's books distinguished sharply between Ancient Greek and Roman art and defined periods within Greek art. He believed art should aim at "noble simplicity and calm grandeur" and emphasized close copying of Greek models. The movement was initially a reaction against the excesses of the Rococo style, which emphasized grace, ornamentation, and asymmetry, while Neoclassicism was based on the principles of simplicity and symmetry drawn from Ancient Rome and Greece. The term "Neoclassical" was not invented until the mid-19th century; the style was initially described as "the true style" or "reformed."

### Painting and Sculpture
Neoclassical painting began c. 1760 but struggled due to a lack of ancient painting models. Artists like John Flaxman used simple line drawing, while Jacques-Louis David became the movement's leader with works like *Oath of the Horatii* (1785), combining idealist style with drama. David's student Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres maintained a classicist style throughout his long career. In sculpture, the movement had an excess of models, mostly Roman copies of Greek works. Leading sculptors included Antonio Canova, known for lightness and grace, and Bertel Thorvaldsen, who was more severe. Jean-Antoine Houdon was notable for portraits that balanced idealism with personality.

### Architecture and Decorative Arts
Neoclassical architecture was based on principles of simplicity and symmetry from classical antiquity. It first gained influence in Britain and France through art students trained in Rome. A second, more severe wave is associated with the Napoleonic Empire. In France, the Louis XVI style (1774–1789) marked the transition from Rococo, while the Empire style (1804–1815) was based on Imperial Roman styles to idealize Napoleon. The style was international, with examples including Karl Friedrich Schinkel's museums in Berlin, Sir John Soane's Bank of England, and the United States Capitol. Indoors, Neoclassicism sought to recreate authentic Roman interiors inspired by discoveries at Pompeii and Herculaneum.

### Fashion and Music
Neoclassical fashion influenced simpler women's dresses, often white and in an "Empire silhouette," which spread across Europe after the French Revolution. Men's fashion was less affected, though hairstyles like the "coiffure à la Titus" became popular. In music, 20th-century Neoclassicism was a reaction against late-Romanticism, seeking cleanness and simplicity by reviving 17th–18th century styles. Igor Stravinsky was a key composer, and the movement coincided with a vogue for reconstructed "classical" dancing in ballet.

### Later Neoclassicism and Totalitarian Use
After the mid-19th century, Neoclassicism was replaced by Eclecticism but persisted in architecture. In the 20th century, totalitarian regimes adopted Neoclassical styles for state buildings to convey permanence and power. The Soviet Union used it as socialist realism, while Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy fused it with modern sensibilities. Postmodernism later revived classical elements, and in the 21st century, Neoclassicism has seen a resurgence under the umbrella of New Classical Architecture.

## Terms
- **Neoclassicism**: A Western cultural movement drawing inspiration from classical antiquity, emphasizing simplicity, symmetry, and rational order.
- **Rococo**: An 18th-century style emphasizing grace, ornamentation, and asymmetry, which Neoclassicism opposed.
- **Grand Tour**: A traditional trip through Europe, especially Italy, undertaken by upper-class young men for education and art collection.
- **Empire style**: A phase of Neoclassicism in architecture and decorative arts during Napoleon's rule, based on Imperial Roman styles.
- **Socialist realism**: The official artistic style in the Soviet Union under Stalin, using Neoclassical forms to idealize communist ideals.
- **Augustan literature**: The English literary movement parallel to Neoclassicism in the visual arts.
- **Biedermeier**: A style of furniture and interior design in German-speaking lands corresponding to the Empire style.
- **Federal style**: An American architectural style influenced by Neoclassicism, popular in the late 18th and early 19th centuries.
- **New Classical Architecture**: A 21st-century resurgence of Neoclassical principles in architecture.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The relationship between Neoclassicism and Romanticism is often oversimplified; some artists, like Ingres, combined elements of both.
- A fierce dispute raged for decades over the relative merits of Greek and Roman art, with Winckelmann and his fellow Hellenists generally winning.
- The extent to which Neoclassical architecture in totalitarian regimes was a genuine revival versus a political tool remains debated.
- The term "Neoclassical" was not used contemporaneously; the style was initially called "the true style" or "reformed," complicating historical analysis.
