# Jordan River

## Overview
The Jordan River is a 251-kilometre endorheic river (a river that drains into an inland basin with no outlet) in the Levant, flowing north to south through the Sea of Galilee to the Dead Sea. It forms borders between Jordan, Syria, Israel, and Palestine, and is of immense religious significance in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Historically a vital water source, its flow has been drastically reduced by modern diversions, leading to severe ecological degradation.

## Body

### Geography and Course
The Jordan River is formed by the merger of the Dan and Hasbani rivers in northern Israel. Its upper course drops rapidly through the Hula Valley to the Sea of Galilee. South of the lake, the lower course meanders through the Jordan Valley, a rift valley below sea level, before emptying into the Dead Sea. Major tributaries in the lower course include the Yarmouk and Zarqa rivers. The river's path forms international borders and the boundary of the Israeli-occupied Golan Heights and West Bank.

### History and Water Use
In the 20th century, the river became a critical water source. Israel's National Water Carrier (1964) and Jordan's East Ghor Canal diverted significant flow. Conflict over these diversions was a factor in the Six-Day War. By 2010, diversions had reduced the river's flow to the Dead Sea from 1.3 billion cubic metres per year to just 20–30 million. This, combined with pollution from sewage and agricultural runoff, has severely damaged the ecosystem and accelerated the shrinking of the Dead Sea.

### Ecology
The river basin is a unique meeting point for fish from different biogeographic regions, including native species like the Jordan bream and Levantine scraper. However, introduced species such as common carp and Nile tilapia threaten native populations. The ecosystem is critically endangered due to reduced flow, high salinity, and pollution, with the most degraded section being the 100-kilometre stretch from the Yarmouk confluence to the Dead Sea.

### Religious Significance
In the Hebrew Bible, the Jordan is the river the Israelites crossed into the Promised Land and the site of miracles by Elijah and Elisha. In the New Testament, John the Baptist baptized Jesus in the Jordan, making it a central site for Christian pilgrimage. Al-Maghtas is traditionally identified as the baptism site. In Islam, the river is part of a "blessed land" associated with prophets. The river's symbolism of crossing to freedom is reflected in music and literature, and Jordan water is used for royal christenings in some Christian houses.

## Terms
- **Endorheic**: A drainage basin that retains water and allows no outflow to other external bodies of water.
- **Tributary**: A river or stream that flows into a larger river.
- **Ichthyoffauna**: The fish life of a particular region or habitat.
- **Biogeographic region**: An area with uniform species composition, shaped by geography and climate.
- **National Water Carrier**: Israel's major water infrastructure project, completed in 1964, for transporting water.
- **Brackish water**: Salty water, but less so than seawater.
- **Wadi**: A valley or streambed that is dry except in the rainy season.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise etymology of the river's name is debated, with hypotheses linking it to Semitic roots meaning "to flow down" or to an Egyptian word for "big river."
- The ecological rescue of the Jordan River is considered a decades-long challenge due to ongoing pollution and political complexities in water management among Israel, Jordan, Syria, and Palestine.
