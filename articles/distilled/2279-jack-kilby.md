# Jack Kilby

## Overview
Jack St. Clair Kilby (1923–2005) was an American electrical engineer whose invention of the integrated circuit in 1958, alongside Robert Noyce, is considered the birth of modern microelectronics. This invention solved a fundamental problem in circuit design and enabled the digital revolution, earning Kilby a share of the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics. He also co-invented the handheld calculator and pioneered solar power applications.

## Timeline
- **November 8, 1923** — Born in Jefferson City, Missouri.
- **c. 1940s** — Served as an electronics technician in the U.S. Army during World War II.
- **1947** — Earned a B.S. in electrical engineering from the University of Illinois.
- **1950** — Earned an M.S. from Milwaukee State Teachers College (now UW–Milwaukee).
- **Mid-1958** — While at Texas Instruments, conceived the integrated circuit to solve the "tyranny of numbers."
- **September 12, 1958** — Demonstrated the first working integrated circuit to TI management.
- **February 6, 1959** — Filed U.S. Patent 3,138,743 for "Miniaturized electronic circuits."
- **1970** — Took a leave from TI to explore independent inventions, including solar power.
- **1978–1984** — Served as Distinguished Professor of Electrical Engineering at Texas A&M University.
- **1983** — Retired from Texas Instruments.
- **2000** — Shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for the integrated circuit.
- **June 20, 2005** — Died of cancer in Dallas, Texas.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Kilby's interest in electronics was sparked by a childhood event: a major ice storm that disrupted communication lines, forcing his father's rural electric company to rely on amateur radio operators. After WWII service, he earned his engineering degrees while working at Centralab, a division of Globe Union, Inc.

### Invention of the Integrated Circuit
The key problem Kilby addressed was the "tyranny of numbers"—the increasing complexity and unreliability of assembling individual electronic components (transistors, resistors, etc.) into circuits. His solution was to manufacture all components en masse within a single piece of semiconductor material. He demonstrated this concept on a germanium substrate in September 1958. His patent, filed in 1959, described a circuit with multiple components on one substrate. Robert Noyce of Fairchild Semiconductor independently developed a similar circuit shortly after, leading to shared credit for the invention.

### Later Career and Applications
Following his breakthrough, Kilby led teams that applied integrated circuits to military systems and computers. He co-invented the handheld calculator. In the 1970s, he explored using silicon technology for solar power generation. He later held an academic position at Texas A&M University before retiring from TI in 1983.

### Legacy and Commemoration
Kilby's work is commemorated through numerous awards, named institutions (like TI's Kilby Labs), and statues. His personal archives were donated to Southern Methodist University, which also hosted a 50th-anniversary celebration of his invention. The Kilby Award Foundation and an IEEE medal were established in his honor.

## Terms
- **Integrated Circuit (IC):** An electronic circuit where components like transistors and resistors are fabricated together on a single semiconductor substrate.
- **Semiconductor:** A material (like silicon or germanium) whose electrical conductivity is between that of a conductor and an insulator, forming the basis for modern electronics.
- **Substrate:** The base material (e.g., a silicon or germanium wafer) upon which an integrated circuit is built.
- **"Tyranny of numbers":** The design challenge where the number of individual components in a circuit became so large that assembly and reliability were severely hindered.
- **Handheld calculator:** A portable electronic device for mathematical computations, co-invented by Kilby.
- **Thermal printer:** A printer that uses heat to transfer an image onto paper, for which Kilby held a patent.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source text notes that Kilby and Robert Noyce are "generally credited as co-inventors" of the integrated circuit, acknowledging the parallel and independent work that led to the invention. This reflects the historical debate over sole credit, which was resolved by recognizing both contributions.
