# Architecture of India

## Overview
Indian architecture is a vast and diverse tradition spanning millennia, shaped by the subcontinent's history, religions, and cultural exchanges. It encompasses a wide range of styles, from ancient rock-cut caves and Hindu temples to Mughal palaces and modern skyscrapers, reflecting the evolution of Indian society and its interactions with foreign influences.

## Timeline
- **c. 6500–1400 BCE** — South Indian Neolithic period begins, characterized by ash mounds.
- **c. 2600–1900 BCE** — Mature phase of the Indus Valley Civilisation, featuring advanced urban planning.
- **c. 321–185 BCE** — Maurya Empire period; early stone architecture like the Pataliputra capital and Ashoka pillars emerges.
- **c. 4th–6th centuries CE** — Gupta period sees the beginnings of free-standing Hindu temple architecture.
- **7th–13th centuries CE** — Proliferation of regional Hindu temple styles (Nagara, Dravidian, Vesara).
- **1206–1526 CE** — Delhi Sultanate period marks the development of Indo-Islamic architecture.
- **1526–1857 CE** — Mughal Empire period, considered the zenith of Indo-Islamic architecture.
- **18th–20th centuries** — British colonial era introduces European styles and the Indo-Saracenic revival.
- **1947 onwards** — Post-independence India adopts modernist architecture, influenced by figures like Le Corbusier.

## Body

### Early and Ancient Foundations
The earliest surviving Indian architecture is rock-cut, as early wooden structures have not endured. The Indus Valley Civilisation (c. 2600–1900 BCE) demonstrated remarkable civic planning with fired brick, drainage systems, and wells, though its buildings were utilitarian. Following its decline, the Maurya Empire (c. 321–185 BCE) saw the advent of monumental stone architecture, likely influenced by Achaemenid Persian and Hellenistic styles, as seen in the Pataliputra capital and the Pillars of Ashoka.

### Hindu Temple Architecture
Hindu temple architecture is defined by a core sanctuary (*garbhagriha*) housing a deity, crowned by a tower (*shikhara* in the north, *vimana* in the south). By the 7th century CE, three main styles were identified: the curvilinear *Nagara* of the north, the pyramidal *Dravidian* of the south, and the hybrid *Vesara* of the Deccan. Regional variations flourished, such as the intricate Hoysala temples and the sculpturally rich Khajuraho complex. The Dravidian style evolved to feature towering gateway towers (*gopurams*) that often dwarf the central sanctuary.

### Buddhist and Jain Architecture
Buddhist architecture, originating in the 6th century BCE, includes monasteries (*viharas*), prayer halls (*chaityas*), and stupas like the Sanchi Stupa. It heavily utilized rock-cut caves, as seen at Ajanta and Ellora. Jain architecture closely mirrors Hindu temple forms but is often distinguished by large, open-air statues of *tirthankaras* and elaborate decorative carving, exemplified by the Dilwara Temples. The *Māru-Gurjara* style became a pan-Indian Jain architectural idiom.

### Indo-Islamic and Mughal Architecture
Beginning with the Delhi Sultanate (1206), Indo-Islamic architecture fused Indian and Islamic elements, replacing the trabeate (post-and-lintel) system with the arcuate (arch and dome) style. The Mughal Empire (1526–1857) perfected this synthesis, creating a distinctive style characterized by bulbous domes, minarets, and symmetrical gardens (*charbagh*). The Taj Mahal is its most famous example. Mughal influence also shaped Rajput and Sikh architecture.

### Colonial and Modern Architecture
The British colonial period (1757–1947) introduced European styles like Neoclassical and Gothic Revival, which were later amalgamated with Indian elements to form the Indo-Saracenic style. After independence in 1947, India embraced modernism, with Swiss-French architect Le Corbusier designing the city of Chandigarh. Economic liberalization has since spurred contemporary urban architecture and skyscraper construction.

## Terms
- **Garbhagriha**: The innermost sanctum of a Hindu temple, housing the primary deity.
- **Shikhara**: A rising tower, typically curvilinear, over the *garbhagriha* in North Indian (Nagara) temples.
- **Vimana**: A pyramidal tower over the *garbhagriha* in South Indian (Dravidian) temples.
- **Gopuram**: A monumental, ornate gateway tower at the entrance of a Dravidian temple compound.
- **Chaitya**: A Buddhist prayer hall, often rock-cut, with a vaulted ceiling.
- **Vihara**: A Buddhist monastery or residential hall.
- **Charbagh**: A Persian-style garden layout divided into four quadrants by walkways or water channels.
- **Trabeate**: An architectural system using horizontal beams (lintels) supported by vertical posts.
- **Arcuate**: An architectural system using arches and domes for support.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The origins of the true arch in Indian architecture are debated. While its use in Hindu temples is rare, archaeological evidence suggests its presence in pre-Islamic structures like the Mahabodhi Temple and Bhitargaon temple, challenging earlier assumptions.
- The extent of foreign influence on early Indian stone architecture is contested. Some scholars propose direct influence from Lycian rock-cut tombs via the Achaemenid Empire for the *chaitya* arch form, while others argue for an indigenous evolution from local wooden prototypes.
- The classification and precise chronological boundaries of the *Vesara* temple style remain a subject of scholarly disagreement, with some viewing it as a distinct hybrid and others as a regional variant of the Dravidian style.
