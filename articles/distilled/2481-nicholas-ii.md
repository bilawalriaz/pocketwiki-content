# Nicholas II

## Overview
Nicholas II was the last Emperor of Russia, ruling from 1894 until his forced abdication in 1917. His reign saw significant industrial growth but was ultimately defined by his resistance to political reform, military defeats in the Russo-Japanese War and World War I, and the collapse of the Romanov dynasty after 304 years of rule. His family's execution in 1918 marked the end of imperial Russia.

## Timeline
- **18 May 1868** — Born at Alexander Palace, Tsarskoye Selo.
- **1 March 1881** — Becomes heir apparent (Tsesarevich) after assassination of Alexander II.
- **26 November 1894** — Marries Princess Alix of Hesse (Alexandra Feodorovna).
- **26 May 1896** — Formal coronation in Moscow.
- **27 January 1904** — Russo-Japanese War begins.
- **22 January 1905** — "Bloody Sunday" massacre in St. Petersburg.
- **30 October 1905** — Issues October Manifesto, creating the Duma.
- **1 August 1914** — Russia enters World War I.
- **15 March 1917** — Abdicates the throne.
- **17 July 1918** — Executed with his family in Yekaterinburg.
- **17 July 1998** — Remains interred at St. Peter and Paul Cathedral, St. Petersburg.
- **14 August 2000** — Canonized as a passion bearer by the Russian Orthodox Church.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Nicholas was the eldest son of Emperor Alexander III and Empress Maria Feodorovna. He was privately educated and trained for military service but was widely considered ill-prepared for ruling a vast empire. He became heir in 1881 after his grandfather's assassination. Nicholas married his cousin Princess Alix in 1894, less than a month after his father's death. They had five children: four daughters and a son, Alexei, who suffered from hemophilia B. The family's desperation for a cure led them to rely on the mystic Grigori Rasputin, whose influence damaged the monarchy's reputation.

### Reign and Domestic Policy
Nicholas resisted political reform, believing his authority came directly from God. He was forced to concede the October Manifesto in 1905 after the 1905 Revolution, which established the Duma (parliament) and civil liberties. However, he retained the power to veto laws and dissolve the Duma, which he did twice. His relations with the Duma were poor, and he relied on ministers like Sergei Witte and Pyotr Stolypin, though Stolypin was later undermined by court intrigues. Nicholas pursued Russification policies, notably in Finland, which restricted its autonomy.

### Foreign Policy and War
Nicholas pursued a policy of strengthening the Franco-Russian Alliance and promoted international peace, notably through the Hague Peace Conferences. However, his aggressive stance in Manchuria and Korea led to the Russo-Japanese War (1904-1905), a humiliating defeat that exposed Russia's military weaknesses and fueled domestic unrest. In 1914, Russia entered World War I. Nicholas's decision to assume personal command of the army in 1915 linked him directly to the catastrophic losses. The war caused severe economic hardship and military failures, eroding public confidence.

### Revolution and Abdication
By early 1917, Russia was on the verge of collapse. Food shortages and war-weariness sparked the February Revolution in Petrograd. With the garrison mutinying and the Duma demanding change, Nicholas was isolated at military headquarters. Faced with the loss of loyal troops and the threat of civil war, he abdicated on 15 March 1917, first for his son Alexei and then for his brother Michael, who declined the throne. This ended the Romanov dynasty.

### Imprisonment and Execution
The imperial family was held under house arrest, first at Tsarskoye Selo, then in Tobolsk, Siberia. After the Bolsheviks seized power, they were moved to the Ipatiev House in Yekaterinburg in 1918. On 17 July 1918, Nicholas, Alexandra, their five children, and four retainers were executed by a Bolshevik firing squad. The bodies were hidden in a mine shaft and later buried. The remains were discovered in 1979, officially identified in 1998, and interred in St. Petersburg. A second set of remains, those of Alexei and one daughter, were found in 2007 and confirmed in 2009.

## Terms
*   **Autocracy:** A system of government by one person with absolute power. Nicholas II believed his authority was granted by God and resisted sharing it.
*   **Duma:** The legislative assembly or parliament established in Russia after the 1905 Revolution. Nicholas had the power to dissolve it.
*   **Hemophilia B:** A hereditary blood-clotting disorder. Nicholas's son Alexei suffered from it, passed down from Queen Victoria.
*   **Russification:** The policy of promoting Russian language and culture in non-Russian areas of the empire, often suppressing local autonomy.
*   **Passion bearer:** A Christian who suffers and dies for their faith in a Christ-like manner. The Russian Orthodox Church canonized Nicholas and his family as passion bearers, not martyrs.
*   **Provisional Government:** The liberal government that took power in Russia after the February Revolution of 1917, before being overthrown by the Bolsheviks.
*   **Bolsheviks:** The radical communist faction led by Vladimir Lenin that seized power in the October Revolution of 1917.
*   **Ipatiev House:** The building in Yekaterinburg where the Romanov family was imprisoned and executed in 1918.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Responsibility for the Execution:** While the local Ural Soviet ordered the execution, historical debate continues over whether the order came directly from Lenin and the Bolshevik leadership in Moscow. An official Russian investigation found no trustworthy evidence to confirm this.
*   **Nicholas's Competence:** Historians widely agree Nicholas was a well-intentioned but poor ruler, indecisive and ill-suited to the challenges of his time. The extent to which he was manipulated by advisors like Rasputin versus his own stubborn adherence to autocracy is debated.
*   **Canonization Controversy:** The Russian Orthodox Church's decision to canonize Nicholas and his family was controversial. Critics argued his weak governance led to the revolution and his family's suffering, making him partially responsible for his own fate. The Church ultimately recognized them as passion bearers for their humble acceptance of suffering.
*   **Identification of Remains:** While DNA testing has conclusively identified the main family remains, the Russian Orthodox Church has historically expressed skepticism about the 1991 discovery, initially standing by accounts that the bodies were destroyed.
