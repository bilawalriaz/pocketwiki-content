# Frederick II, Holy Roman Emperor

## Overview
Frederick II (1194–1250) was Holy Roman Emperor, King of Sicily, and a pivotal medieval ruler who sought to create a unified, centralized empire across Italy and Germany. His reign was defined by a relentless struggle with the papacy, innovative state-building in Sicily, and a reputation as a polymath and "Wonder of the World" (*Stupor mundi*). His complex legacy marks a high point of medieval imperial ambition and a precursor to early modern statecraft.

## Timeline
- **26 Dec 1194** — Born in Jesi, Italy, son of Emperor Henry VI and Constance of Sicily.
- **17 May 1198** — Crowned King of Sicily at age three.
- **9 Dec 1212** — Crowned King of Germany in Mainz.
- **20 Nov 1220** — Crowned Holy Roman Emperor in Rome.
- **1220–1230s** — Deports Sicilian Muslims to Lucera, creating a loyal military force.
- **1229** — Signs Treaty of Jaffa, recovering Jerusalem via diplomacy; crowned King of Jerusalem.
- **1231** — Issues the Constitutions of Melfi, centralizing authority in Sicily.
- **1237** — Wins Battle of Cortenuova, defeating the Lombard League.
- **1245** — Council of Lyons declares him deposed; he is excommunicated for the fourth time.
- **13 Dec 1250** — Dies in Apulia; his empire begins to fragment after his death.

## Body

### Early Life and Consolidation of Power
Born to Emperor Henry VI and Constance of Sicily, Frederick became King of Sicily at age three. His minority was turbulent, marked by regents and baronial power grabs. After coming of age in 1208, he reasserted royal authority in Sicily. He was elected King of Germany in 1212, rivaling Otto IV, and secured his position after Otto's defeat at Bouvines in 1215. He was crowned Emperor in 1220 after negotiating with the papacy, which sought to keep his Sicilian and imperial titles separate.

### State-Building in the Kingdom of Sicily
Frederick transformed the Kingdom of Sicily into a highly centralized, bureaucratic state. Through the Assizes of Capua (1220) and Messina (1221), he reclaimed crown lands and subjugated rebellious barons. His masterpiece was the **Constitutions of Melfi** (1231), a comprehensive legal code that established a secular bureaucracy, regulated society, and made the emperor the supreme source of law. He relocated Sicilian Muslims to Lucera, granting them autonomy in exchange for military service, and founded the University of Naples in 1224 to train state officials.

### The Sixth Crusade and Conflict with the Papacy
Frederick repeatedly delayed his crusade, leading to excommunication in 1227. He sailed in 1228 while excommunicated and, through diplomacy with Sultan al-Kamil, recovered Jerusalem in 1229 via the Treaty of Jaffa. This bloodless success was condemned by the Church. Upon his return, he defeated a papal invasion in the War of the Keys (1229–1230), leading to a temporary reconciliation. Conflict resumed with Pope Gregory IX, who excommunicated him again in 1239. Frederick's victory at the Battle of Giglio (1241) and his manipulation of a papal election further strained relations.

### Final Struggles and Legacy
Pope Innocent IV declared Frederick deposed at the Council of Lyons (1245). Frederick responded with brutal repression and propaganda. His power was challenged by the Lombard League and papal-backed anti-kings in Germany. A major defeat at Parma (1248) and the capture of his son Enzo (1249) were personal blows. However, by 1250, his fortunes had revived significantly. He died that year, leaving his empire to his sons. The Hohenstaufen dynasty collapsed within two decades, ending the medieval ideal of a universal Christian empire.

## Terms
- **Stupor mundi**: Latin for "Wonder of the World," a contemporary epithet for Frederick II.
- **Constitutions of Melfi**: A 1231 legal code that centralized royal authority in the Kingdom of Sicily, creating a modern bureaucratic state.
- **Regno**: The Kingdom of Sicily, Frederick's core power base and most sophisticated state.
- **Lombard League**: An alliance of northern Italian cities that resisted imperial authority.
- **Guelphs and Ghibellines**: Factions supporting the Pope (Guelphs) and the Holy Roman Emperor (Ghibellines), respectively.
- **Great Interregnum**: The period (1254–1273) of instability in the Holy Roman Empire after the fall of the Hohenstaufen dynasty.
- **Augustalis**: A high-quality gold coin introduced by Frederick II, modeled on Roman imperial coinage.
- **Privilegium potestatis**: The papal claim to supreme spiritual and temporal authority, which Frederick challenged.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Religious Sincerity**: Was Frederick a sincere Christian, a religious skeptic, or an atheist? His personal views remain debated, fueled by his conflicts with the papacy and alleged quotes mocking dogma.
- **"First Modern Man"**: Historians debate whether his centralized, bureaucratic state in Sicily makes him a precursor to early modern absolutism or if he was a conventional medieval ruler operating within existing structures.
- **Imperial Vision**: Did Frederick pursue a coherent, step-by-step plan to build a unified Italo-German empire, or was his policy a pragmatic, reactive response to immediate crises?
- **Legacy of the Constitutions**: To what extent did the Constitutions of Melfi influence later European state-building and legal traditions?
