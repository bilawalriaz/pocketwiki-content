# Dacians

## Overview
The Dacians were an ancient Indo-European people, considered a subgroup of the Thracians, who inhabited the region of Dacia near the Carpathian Mountains and west of the Black Sea. Their civilization developed into a powerful kingdom that rivaled Rome, leading to the Dacian Wars and eventual Roman conquest. The study of Dacians is central to understanding the pre-Roman history of Southeast Europe and the ethnogenesis of modern Romanians.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Indo-European expansion into the region begins, mixing with indigenous Danubian farmers.
- **8th–7th centuries BC** — Scythian migrations push the Agathyrsi into Dacian territory, where they are assimilated.
- **4th century BC** — Celtic La Tène influence arrives; Dacians absorb Celtic cultural elements.
- **335 BC** — Alexander the Great attacks the Getae on the lower Danube.
- **82–44 BC** — King Burebista unites the Dacians, creating a powerful kingdom.
- **87–106 AD** — Reign of King Decebalus, the last Dacian king.
- **101–102 AD** — First Dacian War; Emperor Trajan invades Dacia.
- **105–106 AD** — Second Dacian War; Trajan conquers Dacia, Decebalus commits suicide.
- **106 AD** — Dacia becomes a Roman province.
- **c. 271–275 AD** — Emperor Aurelian evacuates Roman Dacia due to pressure from Carpi, Goths, and others.

## Body

### Origins and Ethnogenesis
The proto-Dacian or proto-Thracian people developed from a mixture of indigenous Danubian farmers and Indo-European warrior-herders from the steppes during the Early Bronze Age (3,300–3,000 BC). By the Iron Age, these groups evolved into the Geto-Dacians. Their culture was influenced by the Scythians and, from the 4th century BC, by Celtic invaders. The Dacians and Getae were considered Thracians by ancient writers, though they had distinct religious and cultural practices.

### Identity and Language
Ancient sources used the names "Getae" and "Dacians" interchangeably or with confusion. Strabo distinguished them geographically, with Dacians in the west (Transylvania) and Getae near the Black Sea. The Dacian language is classified as a satem Indo-European language, closely related to Thracian. Scholars debate whether it was a dialect of Thracian or a separate language. Evidence comes from place names (often ending in *-dava*), personal names, and plant names preserved in ancient texts.

### Dacian Kingdoms and Conflict with Rome
Dacian polities were confederacies united by powerful kings. Burebista (82–44 BC) created a large kingdom, moving the capital to Sarmizegetusa. After a period of division, King Decebalus (87–106 AD) reunified the Dacians and raided Roman territory. Emperor Trajan conquered Dacia in two wars (101–102, 105–106 AD), partly to seize its gold mines. The conquest is celebrated on Trajan's Column in Rome.

### Roman Rule and Aftermath
The Roman province of Dacia (106–c. 271/275 AD) covered parts of modern Romania. It was a Latin-speaking province with a mixed population. "Free Dacians" like the Carpi and Costoboci remained outside Roman control. Emperor Aurelian evacuated the province around 271–275 AD due to pressure from Germanic tribes and the Carpi. This led to a complex legacy of Romanization and cultural continuity in the region.

### Society, Economy, and Warfare
Dacian society was divided into an aristocracy (*tarabostes*) and commoners (*comati*). Their economy was based on agriculture, mining (especially gold and silver), and trade. They built fortified cities with distinctive *murus dacicus* walls. Their most famous weapon was the *falx*, a curved blade that forced Roman army reforms. Dacian religion featured the god Zalmoxis and a powerful priestly class.

## Terms
- **Geto-Dacians**: Modern term for the Dacians and Getae, emphasizing their close relationship.
- **Satem**: A group of Indo-European languages (including Indo-Iranian, Balto-Slavic, and others) characterized by the pronunciation of certain consonants as sibilants or affricates.
- **Murus Dacicus**: A Dacian fortification technique using double-skinned ashlar masonry with rubble fill and tie beams.
- **Falx**: A single-edged, scythe-like weapon used by Dacian warriors.
- **Tarabostes**: The Dacian aristocracy, who had the right to cover their heads.
- **Comati**: The common people of Dacia, meaning "long-haired."
- **Zalmoxis**: A semi-legendary Dacian religious reformer and deity, associated with the belief in immortality.
- **Sarmizegetusa**: The political and spiritual capital of the Dacian kingdom, located in the Orăștie Mountains.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Linguistic Relationship**: Scholars debate whether Dacian was a dialect of Thracian or a separate, though closely related, Indo-European language.
- **Ethnic Composition of Tribes**: The ethnic identity of certain tribes like the Costoboci (Dacian, Celtic, or mixed) and the Carpi (Dacian or Slavic) is debated.
- **Impact of Roman Conquest**: The extent of population loss, displacement, and cultural Romanization after the Dacian Wars is uncertain. Evidence suggests both conflict and continuity.
- **Post-Roman Continuity**: The degree of cultural and demographic continuity between the Roman province of Dacia and the later formation of Romanians is a subject of significant historical and political debate.
