# Switzerland

## Overview
Switzerland is a landlocked federal republic in Central Europe, renowned for its political neutrality, direct democracy, and multilingual society. It originated as a defensive alliance in the Late Middle Ages and evolved into a modern, prosperous nation with a highly developed economy and a unique political system that emphasizes cantonal autonomy and citizen participation.

## Timeline
- **1291** — Federal Charter signed by Uri, Schwyz, and Unterwalden, considered the founding document of the Old Swiss Confederacy.
- **1648** — Peace of Westphalia formally recognizes Swiss independence from the Holy Roman Empire.
- **1798** — Revolutionary France invades, imposing the centralist Helvetic Republic.
- **1803** — Napoleon's Act of Mediation reinstates a confederation of 19 cantons.
- **1815** — Congress of Vienna fully re-establishes Swiss independence and permanent neutrality.
- **1848** — A new constitution establishes the current federal system after the Sonderbundskrieg civil war.
- **1971** — Women gain the right to vote at the federal level.
- **1999** — A completely revised federal constitution is adopted by popular vote.
- **2002** — Switzerland becomes a full member of the United Nations.

## Body

### Origins and the Old Swiss Confederacy
The region's earliest farming settlements date to c. 5300 BC. The Helvetii, a prominent La Tène culture tribe, were defeated by Julius Caesar in 58 BC, and the area was later integrated into the Roman Empire. After the fall of Roman authority, the region was settled by Germanic tribes and became part of the Frankish Empire. The Old Swiss Confederacy began as a defensive alliance among Alpine valley communities, formalized by the Federal Charter of 1291. This alliance expanded through victories against the Habsburgs and Burgundy, achieving de facto independence after the Swabian War of 1499. Formal independence from the Holy Roman Empire was recognized in 1648.

### From Napoleonic Era to the Federal State
In 1798, France imposed the short-lived, unpopular Helvetic Republic. After Napoleon's defeat, the Congress of Vienna (1815) restored the confederation and recognized its permanent neutrality. Internal conflict between liberal and conservative cantons culminated in the brief Sonderbundskrieg civil war of 1847. This led directly to the 1848 constitution, which created a federal state with a bicameral parliament, enshrined individual rights, and established a system balancing central authority with cantonal self-rule. The constitution has been revised twice, in 1874 and 1999, strengthening elements of direct democracy.

### Neutrality and International Role
Switzerland has maintained a policy of armed neutrality since 1515, avoiding international wars since 1815. It was a founding member of the League of Nations and joined the UN in 2002. While not an EU member, it participates in the Schengen Area and has extensive bilateral agreements with the EU. Its neutrality and stability have made it a host for numerous international organizations, including the UN, WHO, WTO, and the Red Cross.

### Political System and Direct Democracy
The Swiss Confederation consists of 26 cantons, each with its own constitution and government. The federal government has three branches: the bicameral Federal Assembly (legislative), the seven-member Federal Council (executive), and the federal courts (judicial). A hallmark of the system is direct democracy, allowing citizens to challenge laws via optional referendums (requiring 50,000 signatures) or propose constitutional amendments via popular initiatives (requiring 100,000 signatures). Constitutional amendments require a double majority of the national popular vote and a majority of cantons.

### Economy and Society
Switzerland has a highly developed, stable economy with one of the world's highest GDPs per capita. Key sectors include pharmaceuticals, finance, precision instruments, and tourism. It is a global leader in innovation. The population of about 9 million is multilingual, with four national languages: German (62%), French (22.7%), Italian (8.2%), and Romansh (0.5%). Christianity is the predominant religion, though the population is increasingly secular. The country has a high life expectancy and performs well on international health and environmental metrics.

## Terms
- **Federal Charter of 1291:** The foundational pact of the Old Swiss Confederacy, signed by the cantons of Uri, Schwyz, and Unterwalden.
- **Helvetic Republic:** The centralized, French-imposed republic that replaced the Swiss Confederacy from 1798 to 1803.
- **Direct Democracy:** A political system where citizens can vote directly on initiatives and referendums, bypassing or overturning parliamentary decisions.
- **Double Majority:** A voting requirement for constitutional amendments in Switzerland, needing both a majority of the national popular vote and a majority of the cantons.
- **Canton:** A member state of the Swiss Confederation, possessing a high degree of autonomy with its own constitution, parliament, and government.
- **Armed Neutrality:** Switzerland's long-standing foreign policy of not participating in wars between other states, maintained since 1515.
- **Willensnation:** A term describing Switzerland as a "nation of volition," where national identity is based on shared civic values rather than a single ethnicity or language.
- **Magic Formula:** An informal power-sharing agreement since 1959 that allocates seats in the Federal Council among the four major political parties.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **EU Membership:** Switzerland has consistently rejected EU membership in referendums, but maintains close ties through bilateral agreements. The future direction of this relationship remains a key political question.
- **Immigration and Free Movement:** Initiatives to restrict immigration and end the free movement of people from the EU have been put to popular votes, reflecting ongoing tension between economic integration and national sovereignty.
- **Banking Secrecy:** International pressure has led to reforms, but Switzerland's status as a financial center and its tradition of banking secrecy continue to be subjects of global debate.
