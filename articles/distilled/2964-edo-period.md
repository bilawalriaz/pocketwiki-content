# Edo period

## Overview
The Edo period (1603–1868) was an era of unprecedented peace, stability, and isolation under the Tokugawa shogunate, following centuries of civil war. It saw the consolidation of a rigid social order, significant economic growth, and a flourishing of popular culture, but its isolationist policies ultimately left Japan vulnerable to Western pressure, leading to its collapse and the Meiji Restoration.

## Timeline
- **1600** — Battle of Sekigahara; Tokugawa Ieyasu establishes hegemony.
- **1603** — Ieyasu is appointed shōgun, founding the Tokugawa shogunate.
- **1615** — Siege of Osaka; destruction of the Toyotomi clan, securing Tokugawa authority.
- **1635** — Shōgun Iemitsu formalizes the *sankin-kōtai* (alternate attendance) system.
- **1639** — *Sakoku* (closed country) edicts completed; all foreigners except the Dutch are expelled.
- **1688** — Peak of Chinese trade at Nagasaki; over 200 ships visit annually.
- **1782–1788** — Great Tenmei famine, a major demographic and economic crisis.
- **1853** — U.S. Commodore Matthew Perry forces Japan to open to trade.
- **1868** — Meiji Restoration; the Tokugawa shogunate is abolished.

## Body

### Establishment and Governance
The Tokugawa shogunate was established after Tokugawa Ieyasu's victory at the Battle of Sekigahara (1600) and his appointment as shōgun (1603). The political system evolved into a *bakuhan* structure, combining centralized shōgunal authority with regional domains (*han*) ruled by *daimyō* (feudal lords). To control the *daimyō*, the shogunate implemented the *sankin-kōtai* system, requiring lords to spend alternate years in Edo and leave their families as permanent residents. The *daimyō* were classified into three ranks: *shinpan* (related houses), *fudai* (hereditary vassals), and *tozama* (outside lords), with the latter excluded from central government. This system, described as "parcellized sovereignty," lacked a unified national treasury or judiciary.

### Foreign Relations and Isolation
Initially, Japan engaged in active trade under the *shuinjō* (vermilion seal) permit system. However, following the Shimabara Rebellion (1637–38), which was blamed on Christian influence, the shogunate enacted *sakoku* edicts (1635–1639). This policy expelled most foreigners, restricted trade to Nagasaki (with the Dutch confined to Dejima), and prohibited Japanese from leaving the country. The primary motivations were to suppress Christianity, prevent regional lords from gaining independent power, and force reliance on domestic production. Trade with Korea, China, and the Ryūkyū Islands continued under strict regulation, often managed by specific domains like Tsushima and Satsuma.

### Society and Economy
The social order was a rigid hierarchy, traditionally described as *shi-nō-kō-shō* (samurai, peasants, artisans, merchants), though modern scholarship views these as classifications rather than a strict peacetime hierarchy. Samurai, now largely administrators, lived in castle towns (*jōkamachi*). Peasants, comprising about 80% of the population, bore the tax burden. Merchants, though low in status, prospered and developed sophisticated financial systems, including futures markets in Osaka. The *sankin-kōtai* system stimulated a national road network and consumer economy. By the 18th century, Japan was highly urbanized, with Edo (modern Tokyo) becoming one of the world's largest cities.

### Culture and Intellectual Life
A vibrant commoner culture (*chōnindō*) emerged in cities, featuring arts like *kabuki* theater, *ukiyo-e* woodblock prints, and *bunraku* puppet theater. Literacy rates were high for a pre-industrial society, supported by *terakoya* (temple schools) for commoners and *hankō* (domain schools) for samurai. Neo-Confucianism became the dominant philosophy, emphasizing social order and duty. The *kokugaku* (national learning) movement studied ancient Japanese texts, fostering a sense of unique national identity. Western sciences (*rangaku*) were studied through Dutch texts, introducing medicine, astronomy, and engineering.

### Decline and End
The shogunate faced internal crises, including famines, peasant unrest, and financial difficulties. Western pressure intensified in the 19th century, culminating in Commodore Perry's forced opening of Japan (1853). The resulting treaties damaged shōgunal prestige and sparked the *sonnō jōi* ("revere the emperor, expel the barbarians") movement. A coalition of domains, notably Satsuma and Chōshū, overthrew the Tokugawa in the Boshin War (1868–69), leading to the Meiji Restoration and the end of the Edo period.

## Terms
- **Bakuhan**: The political system of the Tokugawa period, combining the central shōgunal government (*bakufu*) with regional domains (*han*).
- **Daimyō**: Feudal lords who ruled over domains (*han*) and owed allegiance to the shōgun.
- **Sankin-kōtai**: The system of "alternate attendance" requiring *daimyō* to spend alternate years in Edo, with their families residing there permanently.
- **Sakoku**: The "closed country" policy of isolationism, restricting foreign trade and travel from 1639 to 1853.
- **Chōnin**: The urban merchant and artisan class, which grew prosperous despite low social status.
- **Rangaku**: "Dutch studies," the study of Western sciences and technologies through Dutch sources.
- **Kokugaku**: "National learning," an intellectual movement that studied classical Japanese texts to define a native cultural identity.
- **Ukiyo-e**: "Pictures of the floating world," a genre of woodblock prints depicting urban life, entertainment, and landscapes.
- **Bushidō**: The "way of the warrior," a code of ethics for samurai that was formalized during this period of peace.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Social Hierarchy**: The traditional *shi-nō-kō-shō* model is debated; modern research suggests peasants, artisans, and merchants were social classifications rather than a strict peacetime hierarchy.
- **Causes of Sakoku**: While the suppression of Christianity is the stated motive, scholars also emphasize the goal of preventing regional lords from gaining independent foreign contacts and wealth.
- **Economic Impact of Isolation**: Historical records indicate trade volume with the Dutch and Chinese actually increased after the expulsion of the Portuguese, challenging the assumption that *sakoku* caused economic decline.
- **Decline of the Shogunate**: Historians debate the relative importance of internal factors (financial crises, social unrest) versus external pressure (Western intrusion) in causing the Tokugawa collapse.
