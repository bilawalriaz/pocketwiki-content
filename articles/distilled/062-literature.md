# Literature

## Overview

Literature is any collection of written work, and more narrowly, writings considered an art form—especially novels, plays, and poems. It includes print and digital writing, and in recent centuries has expanded to include transcribed oral literature. Literature serves to record, preserve, and transmit knowledge and entertainment, and can have social, psychological, spiritual, or political roles. Literary criticism, one of the oldest academic disciplines, examines the literary merit or intellectual significance of texts, while textual criticism and the history of the book study texts as artifacts. Developments in print technology have enabled ever-growing distribution of written works, while the digital era has blurred lines between electronic literature and other modern media.

## Timeline

- **c. 4th millennium BC** — Writing emerges in Mesopotamia due to trade/administration complexity; also possibly in ancient Egypt and Mesoamerica for recording historical/environmental events
- **26th–22nd century BC** — Old Kingdom Egypt: literary works include funerary texts, epistles, hymns, and autobiographical texts
- **21st–17th century BC** — Early Middle Kingdom Egypt: narrative literature is created
- **1500–1000 BC** — Sanskrit literature begins with the Vedas
- **c. 1600–1100 BC** — Mycenaean Greek writings in Linear B script (prosaic records only; no literature found)
- **Late 8th/early 7th century BC** — Homer's Iliad and Odyssey composed, likely transmitted orally
- **c. 700 BC** — Hesiod's Works and Days and Theogony, among the earliest and most influential works of ancient Greek literature
- **1046–c. 600 BC** — Shijing (Book of Songs) collected by Chinese aristocracy
- **c. 1450** — Gutenberg invents movable type in Europe, making books cheaper and more widely available
- **1609** — Newspaper publishing begins in Germany; magazines follow in 1663
- **Late 1820s** — English literary study proposed for London University, developing into the university discipline
- **1909** — Selma Lagerlöf becomes first woman to win Nobel Prize in Literature

## Body

### Definitions

Definitions of literature have varied over time. In Western Europe before the 18th century, literature denoted all books and writing. The term derives from Latin *literatura/litteratura*, "learning, writing, grammar," originally "writing formed with letters." A value judgment definition considers literature as solely high-quality writing forming part of the belles-lettres ("fine writing") tradition—the 1910–1911 Encyclopædia Britannica called it "the best expression of the best thought reduced to writing." Modern cultural studies have returned to more inclusive notions, including popular and minority genres alongside canonical works.

### Oral Literature

The term "literature" poses issues for oral traditions given its Latin root meaning "letter," but the word is widely used. Australian Aboriginal culture has thrived on oral traditions passed down through tens of thousands of years. A February 2020 study showed Budj Bim and Tower Hill volcanoes erupted 34,000–40,000 years ago, providing a "minimum age constraint for human presence in Victoria" and possibly evidence for Gunditjmara oral histories of volcanic eruptions—among the oldest oral traditions in existence. An axe found under volcanic ash in 1947 had already proved human habitation before Tower Hill's eruption.

Oral tradition is found in "all corners of the world." The earliest poetry was likely recited or sung to remember history, genealogy, and law. Ancient Indian scriptures were preserved orally with elaborate mnemonic techniques. The early Buddhist texts are generally believed to be of oral tradition. All ancient Greek literature was to some degree oral; Homer's epics were largely composed, performed, and transmitted orally. Native North Americans (north of Mesoamerica) had no writing systems before European contact; oral storytelling functioned as practical lessons from tribal experience, using fictional or exaggerated characters to teach morals and exert social pressure without direct embarrassment. Oratory (public speaking) was considered a literary art from ancient Greece to the late 19th century, with rhetoric central to Western education.

### Early Written Literature

Ancient Egyptian and Sumerian literatures are the world's oldest. Egyptian primary genres—didactic texts, hymns, prayers, and tales—were written almost entirely in verse. Many early works had covert moral or didactic purposes, such as the Sanskrit Panchatantra (200 BC–300 AD). Drama and satire developed with urban cultures providing larger audiences. In ancient China, early literature focused on philosophy, historiography, military science, agriculture, and poetry; China produced the world's first print cultures. The Hundred Schools of Thought period occurred during the Eastern Zhou dynasty (769–269 BC), producing Confucian, Daoist, Mohist, and Legalist classics, plus Sun Tzu's The Art of War (c. 5th century BC) and Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian (c. 94 BC). An exemplary piece of narrative history was the Zuo Zhuan, compiled no later than 389 BC and attributed to the blind 5th-century BC historian Zuo Qiuming. Ancient Indian literature includes the Vedas (1500–1000 BC) and the epics Mahabharata and Ramayana (composed/redacted 6th–1st centuries BC, with later redactions to the 4th century AD).

The earliest known Greek writings are Mycenaean (c. 1600–1100 BC) in Linear B script, containing only prosaic trade records. Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, composed around the late 8th or early 7th century BC, were the greatest influence on ancient Greek culture; to Plato, Homer "has taught Greece." Hesiod's Works and Days (c. 700 BC) and Theogony are some of the earliest and most influential works of ancient Greek literature. Classical Greek genres included philosophy (Plato, Aristotle), lyric poetry (Sappho, Pindar), historiography (Herodotus, Thucydides), and drama—only plays by Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides survive from hundreds of tragedies. The Torah is widely seen as a product of the Persian period (539–333 BC, probably 450–350 BC). Roman literature begins in 240 BC with a Latin version of a Greek play. The Qur'an (610–632 AD) marked the beginning of Islamic literature and unified/standardized Arabic. Medieval European literature included Latin theological works and vernacular texts like Beowulf and Icelandic sagas; the romance developed into the novel during the Renaissance, aided by Gutenberg's printing press (c. 1440).

### Publishing and University Discipline

Printing became practical with Gutenberg's invention; earlier, works were copied manually by scribes. The Chinese inventor Bi Sheng made earthenware movable type c. 1045; Koreans invented metal type around 1230, which spread to Europe between the late 14th and early 15th centuries. Early printed works before 1501 are known as incunables. In late 1820s England, utilitarians and Benthamites promoted English literary study at the newly formed London University, developing into the idea of literature as "the ideal carrier for the propagation of the humanist cultural myth of a well educated, culturally harmonious nation."

### Women and Children's Literature

Literature was mostly male-dominated until widespread female education in the 19th century. Notable exceptions include George Sand (Amantine Dupin, 1804–1876), more renowned than Victor Hugo and Honoré de Balzac in England in the 1830s–1840s; Jane Austen (1775–1817), the first major English woman novelist; and Aphra Behn, an early female dramatist. Of 117 Nobel Prizes in Literature awarded 1901–2020, only 16 went to women; Selma Lagerlöf (1858–1940) was the first, in 1909. Feminist scholars since the twentieth century have sought to expand the literary canon. Children's literature emerged as a separate genre in the eighteenth century with the development of the concept of childhood, beginning with educational books, conduct books, and ABCs.

### Study, Criticism, and Types

Literary theory's fundamental question is "what is literature?"—though many contemporary theorists believe it cannot be defined or can refer to any use of language. Literary fiction explores facets of the human condition and is often regarded as having more artistic merit than genre fiction, though this has been contested with serious academic study of genre fiction. Some researchers suggest literary fiction aids psychological development; psychologists use literature therapeutically. Psychologist Hogan argues literature can unite communities by provoking universal emotions and allowing access to different cultures. Maslow's ideas help critics understand how characters reflect their culture and history.

Poetry is traditionally distinguished from prose by greater use of aesthetic language qualities—assonance, alliteration, rhyme, rhythm—and by being set in lines and verses. This distinction is complicated by hybrid forms (digital poetry, prose poetry) and the fact that prose possesses rhythm. Before the 19th century, poetry meant metrical lines. Prose developed later; in ancient Greece, formal prose was "a comparatively late development, an 'invention' properly associated with the classical period." Latin, especially Cicero's work, influenced European prose; Descartes, Bacon, and Spinoza published in Latin. The novel—a long fictional narrative in prose—emerged in the eighteenth century; the term came from Romance languages in the late 15th century meaning "news." Walter Scott defined romance as involving "marvelous and uncommon incidents" versus the novel's "ordinary train of human events." Related forms include the novella (17,000–40,000 words), short story, graphic novel (combining artwork, dialogue, and text), and electronic literature (created exclusively for digital devices). Non-fiction includes essay, biography, travel literature, journalism, and scientific writings. Drama is literature intended for performance; Greek drama developed from religious and civic festivals. In the twentieth century, scripts for radio, television, and film were added.

### Religious Texts, Law, and Awards

Religion has had a major influence on literature through works like the Vedas, the Torah, the Bible, and the Quran. The King James Version of the Bible has been called "the most influential version of the most influential book in the world, in what is now its most influential language" and "arguably the most celebrated book in the English-speaking world," principally because of its literary style and widespread distribution. Prominent atheist figures Christopher Hitchens and Richard Dawkins praised it as "a giant step in the maturing of English literature" and "a great work of literature," respectively. The law and literature movement focuses on the interdisciplinary connection between law and literature.

Copyright gives its owner exclusive rights to copy a creative work, protecting original expression but not ideas. Literary works have been protected in the UK since at least 1710; US copyright was established federally in 1790 and revised in 1976; EU copyright is harmonized through directives including the Copyright Term Directive and Information Society Directive. Japan joined the Berne Convention in 1899; in 2018, following Trans-Pacific Partnership negotiations, copyright was extended to 70 years for all works, not retroactively. Censorship is employed by states and religious bodies to control what can be portrayed; James Joyce's Ulysses was banned in the US from 1921–1933 for obscenity but is now a central literary text. Literary awards are typically limited by form, genre, language, nationality, or output; the Nobel Prize in Literature, established by Alfred Nobel's 1895 will, is awarded for an author's body of work. Other international prizes include the Neustadt International Prize, Man Booker International Prize, Pulitzer Prize, and Hugo Award.

## Terms

- **Belles-lettres**: "Fine writing"; the tradition of high-quality literary works
- **Incunables**: Early printed books, single sheets, and images created before 1501 in Europe
- **Oral tradition**: Knowledge and art transmitted by speech rather than writing
- **Literary fiction**: Fiction exploring the human condition, often with social commentary and artistic merit
- **Novella**: A prose narrative "too short to be a novel, too long to be a short story" (17,000–40,000 words)
- **Closet drama**: A play written to be read rather than performed
- **Electronic literature**: Literary works created exclusively on and for digital devices
- **Copyright**: Intellectual property giving the owner exclusive right to copy a creative work, usually for limited time
- **Synecdoche**: A figure of speech where a part represents the whole (e.g., "writing" for literature)

## Debates and Open Questions

- Whether "literature" can be defined at all; many contemporary theorists believe it cannot be defined or can refer to any use of language
- Whether the term "literature" should apply to oral traditions, given its Latin root meaning "letter" (writing); alternatives like "oral forms" have been suggested
- Whether complex rituals in ancient Greek and Roman civilizations were exclusively products of oral tradition; some scholars disagree
- Whether the Vedic texts were purely oral: Goody argues they likely involved both written and oral tradition, calling them "parallel products of a literate society"
- Whether literary fiction genuinely has more artistic merit than genre fiction; this has been contested with the serious study of genre fiction in universities
- How to define the "short story" and whether to distinguish it from any short narrative; its origin is contested (Bible, Edgar Allan Poe, and others proposed)
