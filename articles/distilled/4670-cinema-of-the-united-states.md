# Cinema of the United States

## Overview
The film industry of the United States, centered in Hollywood, has been a dominant global cultural and economic force since the early 20th century. It pioneered key filmmaking techniques and developed the studio system, which shaped cinematic storytelling for decades. Its evolution reflects broader technological, economic, and social changes, from the silent era to the digital age, and continues to influence worldwide media consumption.

## Timeline
- **1894** — World's first commercial motion-picture exhibition held in New York City using Thomas Edison's kinetoscope.
- **1907** — Kalem Company begins using Fort Lee, New Jersey, as a filming location, making it an early film capital.
- **1911** — Nestor Studios builds the first film studio in the Hollywood neighborhood.
- **1913** — Many filmmakers migrate to California to avoid Thomas Edison's patent fees and enforcement.
- **1927** — Warner Bros. releases *The Jazz Singer*, the first feature-length film with synchronized dialogue, ending the silent era.
- **1930** — The Hays Code (Production Code) is created to establish censorship guidelines for films.
- **1937** — Walt Disney's *Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs* becomes the highest-grossing film to that point.
- **1939** — *Gone with the Wind* is released; 1939 is considered the apex of the Golden Age of Hollywood.
- **1948** — The U.S. Supreme Court rules in *United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc.* that major studios' ownership of theaters violates antitrust law.
- **1967** — *Bonnie and Clyde* marks the beginning of the New Hollywood era.
- **1984** — The PG-13 film rating is introduced.
- **1993** — *Jurassic Park* demonstrates the potential of computer-generated imagery (CGI).
- **2023** — Major strikes by the Writers Guild of America (WGA) and Screen Actors Guild (SAG-AFTRA) highlight disputes over streaming residuals and AI.

## Body

### Origins and Migration to California
The American film industry began on the East Coast, with early production hubs in New Jersey (Fort Lee) and New York City. Filmmakers migrated to Southern California in the 1910s for its favorable climate, varied landscapes, and distance from Thomas Edison's patent enforcement agents. By the 1930s, virtually all filmmaking had shifted to the West Coast, establishing Hollywood as the industry's center.

### The Studio System and Golden Age
From the 1920s to the late 1940s, the "Big Five" studios (MGM, Paramount, Warner Bros., 20th Century Fox, RKO) dominated through vertical integration, owning production facilities, distribution networks, and theater chains. This system produced a high volume of formulaic films under contract with actors, directors, and writers. The era, known as the Golden Age or Classical Hollywood cinema, was defined by a consistent narrative style and the star system. Its decline was triggered by the 1948 Paramount antitrust decision, which forced studios to divest their theaters, and the rise of television.

### New Hollywood and the Blockbuster Era
The late 1960s and 1970s saw the emergence of New Hollywood, a generation of film school-trained directors (e.g., Coppola, Spielberg, Scorsese) who created artistically ambitious films. This period transitioned into the modern blockbuster era, initiated by films like *Jaws* (1975) and *Star Wars* (1977), which emphasized high-concept stories, special effects, and wide commercial appeal. The 1990s accelerated this trend with digital filmmaking technologies like CGI and digital sound.

### Contemporary Challenges and Globalization
Since the 2000s, Hollywood has been dominated by franchise films, particularly superhero genres, and has increasingly outsourced production to other countries. The rise of streaming platforms (e.g., Netflix, Disney+) has disrupted traditional theatrical models, leading to debates about films being treated as "content" rather than art. The industry also faces ongoing issues with underrepresentation of women and minorities, as well as labor disputes over compensation in the streaming era.

## Terms
- **Classical Hollywood cinema**: A technical and narrative style characteristic of American films from roughly 1913 to 1962, emphasizing continuity editing and clear storytelling.
- **Studio system**: The method of film production and distribution dominant in Hollywood from the 1920s to the 1950s, where major studios controlled all aspects of filmmaking.
- **Hays Code**: A set of industry censorship guidelines for American films, in effect from 1934 to 1968, that regulated moral content.
- **Blockbuster**: A high-budget film with extensive marketing, designed for mass commercial appeal and wide theatrical release.
- **New Hollywood**: A filmmaking movement in the late 1960s and 1970s characterized by auteur directors and more personal, often controversial, themes.
- **CGI (Computer-Generated Imagery)**: The use of computer graphics to create or enhance images in film, becoming a standard tool in the 1990s.
- **Vertical integration**: A business model where a company owns multiple stages of production and distribution, such as studios owning theaters.
- **Celluloid ceiling**: A term describing the underrepresentation of women in key creative roles in the film industry.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Art vs. Content**: Critics like Martin Scorsese argue that the industry's focus on franchise films and streaming devalues cinema as an art form, reducing it to commercial "content."
- **Representation**: Persistent underrepresentation of women and minorities in creative and leadership roles remains a central criticism, with studies like the "Celluloid Ceiling" report quantifying the disparity.
- **Economic Model**: The shift to streaming has sparked conflicts over fair compensation for creators (as seen in the 2023 strikes) and the long-term viability of the traditional theatrical window.
- **Global Influence and Censorship**: Hollywood's need to access markets like China raises questions about self-censorship and the alteration of content to comply with foreign political standards.
