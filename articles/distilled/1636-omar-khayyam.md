# Omar Khayyam

## Overview
Omar Khayyam (1048–1131) was a Persian polymath from Nishapur who made foundational contributions to mathematics, astronomy, and philosophy during the Seljuk era. As a mathematician, he produced the first systematic geometric solution for all cubic equations using conic sections and advanced the theory of parallels and real numbers. As an astronomer, he led a team that measured the solar year with extraordinary precision (365.24219858156 days) and designed the Jalali calendar, a solar system with a 33-year intercalation cycle more accurate than the Gregorian calendar and still the basis of the modern Iranian calendar. His philosophical works, written in the Peripatetic tradition of Avicenna, address existence, determinism, and theodicy. A corpus of quatrains (*rubāʿiyāt*) attributed to him achieved global fame through Edward FitzGerald’s 1859 translation, *Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam*, though the authenticity of the poetry and its reflection of his personal beliefs—variously read as agnostic hedonism, Sufi mysticism, or orthodox piety—remains a subject of intense scholarly debate.

## Body

### Life and Career
Khayyam was born into a Persian family in Nishapur, a major Seljuk metropolis. His *nisba* "Khayyam" (tent-maker) likely denotes an ancestral trade. Educated in Quranic sciences, literature, and the *Almagest* of Ptolemy, he studied under Imam Muwaffaq Nishaburi and possibly Bahmanyar, a disciple of Avicenna. After periods in Bukhara and Samarkand—where he wrote his algebraic treatise—he was summoned to the court of Sultan Malik-Shah I by Grand Vizier Nizam al-Mulk in 1074. He directed an observatory in Isfahan with eight scholars to reform the calendar. Following the assassination of Malik-Shah and Nizam al-Mulk (c. 1092), Khayyam lost patronage, performed the Hajj (partly to deflect accusations of unorthodoxy), and later served Sultan Sanjar in Marv before retiring to Nishapur as a recluse. He died in 1131; his disciple Nizami Aruzi later verified his prophecy regarding his tomb’s location beneath a blossoming garden wall.

### Mathematics

#### Theory of Parallels and Real Numbers
In his *Commentary on the Difficulties Concerning the Postulates of Euclid's Elements* (1077), Khayyam offered the first critique of the parallel postulate (Postulate V) not based on *petitio principii* (begging the question). Rejecting motion-based proofs (e.g., Ibn al-Haytham’s) per Aristotle, he analyzed a quadrilateral with equal sides perpendicular to a base—later termed the **Khayyam–Saccheri quadrilateral**. He systematically examined the three hypotheses for the summit angles: right, obtuse, and acute. He proved the right-angle hypothesis implies Postulate V, while refuting the obtuse and acute cases as self-contradictory within Euclidean assumptions. This work implicitly recognized the logical consistency of non-Euclidean geometries (elliptic and hyperbolic) centuries before Gauss, Bolyai, and Lobachevsky. His commentary reached Europe via Nasir al-Din al-Tusi; John Wallis translated Tusi’s commentary, influencing Girolamo Saccheri’s 1733 work.

In the same treatise, Khayyam addressed the theory of proportions. Dissatisfied with Euclid’s definition of equal ratios, he redefined number using continued fractions to express ratios, placing irrational quantities on the same operational scale as rational numbers. Historians (Youschkevitch, Rosenfeld, Struik) regard this as a revolutionary step toward the modern concept of the **real number**.

#### Geometric Algebra and Cubic Equations
Khayyam’s *Treatise on Algebra* (c. 1079) unified algebra and geometry. In *On the Division of a Quadrant of a Circle*, he applied algebra to geometry, deriving cubic and quadratic equations from curve constructions. In the *Treatise on Algebra*, he classified all cubic equations involving lines, squares, and cubes (three binomial, nine trinomial, seven tetranomial). He solved first- and second-degree equations numerically via geometric construction. For the fourteen irreducible cubic types, he proved compass-and-straightedge construction impossible and provided geometric solutions using intersections of conic sections (parabolas, circles, hyperbolas), identifying the positive root as the abscissa of the intersection point. He acknowledged the arithmetic (algebraic) solution remained unknown; this was achieved only in 16th-century Italy (Cardano, del Ferro, Tartaglia). His systematic classification and rigorous conic method surpassed earlier sporadic attempts (Menaechmus, Abu al-Jud), earning praise for "power of generalization" (Woepcke).

#### Binomial Theorem and Root Extraction
Khayyam referenced a lost work, *Difficulties of Arithmetic*, on extracting the *n*th root of natural numbers via a non-geometric law. Historians (Struik) infer he possessed the general binomial theorem for $(a+b)^n$. His predecessor al-Karaji had described the triangular coefficient array (Pascal’s triangle); Khayyam popularized it in Iran, where it bears his name.

### Astronomy and the Jalali Calendar
Commissioned in 1074, Khayyam’s team at the Isfahan observatory conducted large-scale observations to fix the vernal equinox (Nowrūz) precisely. The resulting **Jalali calendar** (inaugurated 15 March 1079) was a true solar calendar: month lengths matched the Sun’s transit through zodiacal signs. It employed a unique **33-year intercalation cycle** comprising 25 common years (365 days) and 8 leap years (366 days), distributed via quadrennial and quinquennial leap intervals. The measured year length (365.24219858156 days) is remarkably accurate—the modern value is 365.242190 days. The Jalali calendar’s error accumulates one day per 5,000 years, surpassing the Gregorian (one day per 3,330 years). It served Greater Iran until 1925, when it was simplified into the modern Iranian calendar. Khayyam reportedly disbelieved in astrological prediction; the term *‘ilm al-nujūm* in sources denotes mathematical astronomy, not astrology.

### Other Scientific Works
Khayyam wrote a treatise on **Archimedes' principle** (*On the Deception of Knowing the Two Quantities of Gold and Silver in a Compound*), describing a hydrostatic weighing method (weighing in air and water) to determine the composition of a gold-silver alloy more accurately than volume measurement allowed. Eilhard Wiedemann judged his solution superior to those of al-Khazini and al-Nayrizi. Another short treatise on **music theory** systematically classified musical scales and defined the mathematical relationships between notes, intervals (minor, major), and tetrachords.

### Poetry: Authenticity and Transmission
The earliest reference to Khayyam as a poet is Imad al-Din al-Isfahani (1174). Quatrains appear in works by Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (c. 1160), Daya (c. 1230), Juvayni (c. 1226–83), and Jajarmi (1340). The Bodleian MS Ouseley 140 (1460) contains 158 quatrains. However, the tradition is widely considered **pseudepigraphic**. Hans Heinrich Schaeder (1934) argued Khayyam should be "struck out from the history of Persian literature" due to lack of confidently attributable material. De Blois notes attribution began in the 13th century. Edward Granville Browne (1906) concluded it is "hardly possible... to assert positively that he wrote any of those ascribed to him." Twenty-five Arabic poems are also attributed. Some scholars suggest poetry was a leisure amusement for scholars like Avicenna, Ghazali, and Tusi.

### Philosophy and Religious Views
Khayyam identified as a student of Avicenna; six philosophical treatises (on existence, determinism, theodicy, natural phenomena) survive, written in the Peripatetic (Aristotelian) style, explicitly theistic, offering prayers and affirming Divine Unity. Biographers like Al-Bayhaqi describe him as pious and orthodox, using honorifics *Ghīyāth al-Dīn* (Patron of Faith) and *Hujjat al-Haqq* (Evidence of Truth).

Conversely, a literal reading of the *rubāʿiyāt* suggests **agnosticism, fatalism, Epicureanism, and materialism** (Christensen, Schaeder, Boyle, Sarton, Hedayat). Al-Qifti (c. 1248) claimed the poems were outwardly Sufi but inwardly anti-religious, noting Khayyam was indicted for impiety. The "Wine" quatrains are interpreted either literally (hedonism) or metaphorically (Sufi *baqāʾ*, divine rapture). Defenders of the Sufi reading (Nicolas, Idries Shah) argue FitzGerald’s translation distorted the esoteric meaning. Critics (Foroughi, Minovi, Aminrazavi) reject the Sufi label, noting Khayyam was disliked by contemporary Sufi masters (Shams Tabrizi, Attar, Najm al-Din Daya). Seyyed Hossein Nasr argues philosophy must be derived from his authenticated prose works, not dubious verse. The contradiction between the theistic prose and the skeptical verse leaves his personal conviction an open question.

## Terms
- ****Khayyam–Saccheri Quadrilateral**** — A quadrilateral with two equal sides perpendicular to the base; used by Khayyam to test the parallel postulate by examining summit angles (right, acute, obtuse).
- ****Conic Sections**** — Curves (circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola) formed by intersecting a plane with a cone; Khayyam used their intersections to geometrically solve cubic equations.
- ****Jalali Calendar**** — A solar calendar instituted 1079 with a 33-year intercalation cycle (8 leap years, 25 common years), more accurate than the Gregorian calendar; basis of the modern Iranian calendar.
- ****Rubāʿiyāt (Quatrains)**** — Four-line poetic stanzas; the form of the verses attributed to Khayyam, popularized globally by FitzGerald’s 1859 translation.
- ****Real Number (conceptual precursor)**** — Khayyam’s redefinition of number via continued fractions to include irrational ratios on the same operational scale as rationals, a step toward the modern continuum.
- ****Petitio Principii**** — "Begging the question"; a logical fallacy where the conclusion is assumed in the premise. Khayyam rejected prior parallel postulate proofs on this ground.
- ****Peripatetic Philosophy**** — The Aristotelian philosophical tradition followed by Avicenna and Khayyam in their prose treatises, emphasizing logic, metaphysics, and natural theology.
- ****Pseudepigrapha**** — Works falsely attributed to a historical figure; the dominant scholarly view regarding the poetic corpus ascribed to Khayyam.
- ****Nowrūz**** — The Persian New Year, fixed by the Jalali calendar at the precise moment the Sun crosses the vernal equinox (Sun enters Aries before noon).
- ****Intercalation**** — The insertion of extra days (leap years) into a calendar to synchronize it with the solar year; the Jalali calendar uses a complex 33-year cycle for this.

## Debates and open questions
1.  **Poetic Authenticity**: Did Khayyam write any of the *rubāʿiyāt* attributed to him? The earliest manuscripts appear decades after his death; the corpus grew over centuries. Most scholars consider the attribution largely pseudepigraphic, though a core may be genuine.
2.  **Religious Identity**: Was Khayyam an orthodox Muslim (per prose works and pious biographies), a Sufi mystic (per metaphorical readings of the poetry), or a skeptic/materialist/atheist (per literal readings of the poetry and Al-Qifti’s account)? The contradiction between his philosophical treatises and the attributed verse is unresolved.
3.  **Transmission of Geometry**: Did Khayyam’s work on the parallel postulate (via Tusi and Wallis) directly influence Girolamo Saccheri and the European development of non-Euclidean geometry, or was the similarity independent? Saccheri used Tusi’s lemma and figure lettering, but the extent of causal influence is debated.
4.  **Binomial Theorem**: Did Khayyam possess a general proof for $(a+b)^n$, or only the specific root-extraction algorithms mentioned in his lost *Difficulties of Arithmetic*? The evidence is inferential.
5.  **Observatory Fate**: The Isfahan observatory was disused after 1092; the extent to which its instruments or data survived to influence later Islamic astronomy (e.g., Maragheh) is unclear.
