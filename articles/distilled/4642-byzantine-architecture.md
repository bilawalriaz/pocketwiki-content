# Byzantine architecture

## Overview
Byzantine architecture is the architectural style of the Eastern Roman Empire, centered in Constantinople, from the founding of the capital in AD 330 until the empire's fall in 1453. It evolved from late Roman architecture but developed a distinct identity, characterized by the innovative use of domes, mosaics, and centralized church plans. This style profoundly influenced medieval architecture across Europe and the Near East, particularly in Orthodox Christian and early Islamic traditions.

## Timeline
- **AD 330** — Constantine the Great establishes Constantinople as the new Roman capital.
- **6th century** — Emperor Justinian I's reign sees the construction of Hagia Sophia and other major churches, introducing pendentives for dome support.
- **7th century** — Architectural development continues in the provinces (e.g., Thessaloniki, Sinai).
- **9th-10th centuries** — The cross-in-square church plan becomes dominant after the end of Iconoclasm.
- **11th-12th centuries** — "Cloisonné masonry" (mixed stone and brick walls) becomes a common exterior treatment.
- **13th-15th centuries** — The Paleologan period sees intricate brickwork and glazed ceramics on exteriors, with less emphasis on vertical height.
- **1453** — Fall of Constantinople to the Ottoman Empire.
- **16th-18th centuries** — Byzantine architectural traditions persist in post-Byzantine schools in Eastern Orthodox countries.
- **19th century** — Neo-Byzantine architecture emerges as a revival style in Europe and Russia.

## Body

### Characteristics and Evolution
Early Byzantine architecture was stylistically indistinguishable from late Roman architecture, using arches, vaults, and domes. A distinct style gradually emerged, marked by the use of gold-background wall mosaics, rich marble interiors, and the development of the Greek cross plan for churches. The most significant structural breakthrough was the invention of pendentives—a system for transitioning from a square base to a circular dome—exemplified in Justinian's Hagia Sophia. Church interiors were designed for Christian liturgy, with light filtered through alabaster windows to illuminate mosaics and frescoes.

### Church Plans and Types
Sacred architecture evolved through several plan types. The basilican plan was dominant from the 4th to 7th centuries. The domed basilica featured a central dome over a vaulted structure. The cruciform plan had a tripartite division (sanctuary, nave, narthex). Centralized plans, like martyria, used a vertical axis. The cross-in-square plan, with a central dome over four equal arms, became the standard from the 9th century onward. The Athonite variant, with added apses and narthexes, developed in the 10th century.

### Structural and Decorative Elements
Columns varied, often evolving from the Corinthian order with undercut ornamentation and unique designs, such as "wind-blown" leaves. The dosseret (a block above the capital) was used to support wider arches. Exteriors featured decorative brickwork patterns, sometimes imitating Cufic script, and were covered with lead or tile. Interiors were adorned with mosaics, frescoes, and marble revetments (thin stone plates) arranged in symmetrical patterns.

### Key Monuments
**Hagia Sophia** (6th century, Istanbul) is the iconic example, combining longitudinal and centralized plans with a vast dome system. **Hagia Irene** (4th century, Istanbul) is an early basilica with two domes and a large atrium. Other significant early works include San Vitale (Ravenna) and the Basilica Cistern (Constantinople). Middle Byzantine examples include Hosios Lukas (Greece) and the Church of the Holy Apostles (Thessaloniki). Late Byzantine architecture is represented by St. Savior at Chora (Istanbul) and the Palace of the Despots (Mistra).

## Terms
- **Pendentive**: A triangular segment of a sphere that allows a circular dome to be placed over a square room.
- **Tessera**: A small piece of stone, glass, or ceramic used to make a mosaic.
- **Cloisonné masonry**: A wall construction technique using a regular mix of stone and brick, often with more brick.
- **Iconostasis**: A screen or wall bearing icons that separates the sanctuary (bema) from the nave in an Orthodox church.
- **Narthex**: An entrance vestibule or porch at the western end of a church.
- **Bema**: The sanctuary area of a church, containing the altar.
- **Ambo**: A raised platform in the nave from which scriptures were proclaimed.
- **Synthronon**: Rows of rising seats in the apse for the clergy, with the patriarch's throne at the center.
- **Dosseret**: A block placed above a column capital to provide a wider base for supporting an arch.

## Debates and Open Questions
The article notes that the terminology "Byzantine" was introduced by modern historians to designate the medieval Roman Empire as a distinct cultural entity. The transition from Roman to Byzantine style is described as a gradual drift rather than a sharp break. The influence of Eastern (e.g., Persian) decorative motifs on Byzantine brickwork patterns is suggested but presented as probable rather than definitively proven.
