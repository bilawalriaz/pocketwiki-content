# Pope Innocent III

## Overview
Pope Innocent III (r. 1198–1216) was one of the most powerful medieval popes, asserting papal supremacy over European monarchs and reshaping the Catholic Church through legal reforms, crusades, and councils. His papacy marked the height of medieval papal authority, influencing politics, heresy suppression, and church doctrine across Christendom.

## Timeline
- **1160/1161** — Born Lotario de' Conti di Segni in Gavignano, near Anagni
- **1198** — Elected pope on 8 January, the day of Celestine III’s death
- **1198** — Became guardian of Frederick, King of Sicily, after Constance I’s death
- **1201** — Publicly supported Otto IV in the Holy Roman Empire succession dispute
- **1202–1204** — Organized the Fourth Crusade, which sacked Constantinople
- **1208** — Launched the Albigensian Crusade after papal legate’s murder
- **1209** — Approved the Franciscan Order founded by Francis of Assisi
- **1213** — King Peter II of Aragon killed at the Battle of Muret
- **1214** — Battle of Bouvines defeated Otto IV, securing Frederick II’s imperial position
- **1215** — Convened the Fourth Lateran Council, issuing major doctrinal and disciplinary decrees
- **1216** — Died suddenly at Perugia on 16 July

## Body

### Early Life and Election
Lotario de' Conti was born into a noble Roman family that produced several popes. He studied theology in Paris and possibly jurisprudence in Bologna, later serving as a cardinal under Clement III. He wrote the popular ascetic work *De Miseria Condicionis Humane*. Elected pope at age 37, he took the name Innocent III, possibly referencing his predecessor’s assertion of papal authority over the emperor.

### Assertion of Papal Supremacy
Innocent III claimed absolute spiritual authority over all Christian rulers, using excommunication and interdict to enforce compliance. He intervened in imperial elections, supporting Otto IV initially but later backing his ward Frederick II when Otto threatened papal interests in Italy. The papal decree *Per Venerabilem* (1202) outlined the pope’s right to investigate and approve imperial candidates. His political maneuvering helped secure papal control over central Italy and weakened Hohenstaufen influence.

### Crusades and Heresy Suppression
Innocent III launched multiple crusades: the Fourth Crusade (1202–1204) diverted to sack Constantinople against his orders, the Livonian Crusade against Baltic pagans, and the Albigensian Crusade against Cathars in southern France. The Albigensian Crusade, sparked by the murder of a papal legate, led to widespread violence and the eventual integration of Occitan territory into the French crown. He also approved the Livonian Brothers of the Sword and established the ecclesiastical state of Terra Mariana.

### Church Reform and Legacy
Innocent III convened the Fourth Lateran Council (1215), which defined doctrines like transubstantiation, mandated annual confession, and issued disciplinary reforms including restrictions on Jews and Muslims. He approved new religious orders, including the Franciscans, and imposed the first direct tax on clergy to fund crusades. His papacy expanded papal power but also sowed long-term conflicts, such as with Frederick II and the Eastern Orthodox Church after Constantinople’s sack.

## Terms
- **Interdict**: A papal censure suspending religious services in a territory to compel obedience.
- **Decretals**: Papal letters defining or clarifying canon law.
- **Fourth Lateran Council**: A 1215 church council that reformed doctrine and discipline.
- **Albigensian Crusade**: A campaign against Cathar heretics in southern France (1209–1229).
- **Livonian Crusade**: A series of campaigns against Baltic pagans in present-day Latvia and Estonia.
- **Transubstantiation**: The doctrine that bread and wine become Christ’s body and blood in the Eucharist.
- **Canon law**: The legal system governing the Catholic Church.
- **Patrimony of Saint Peter**: The papal states in central Italy.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which Innocent III’s claim of papal supremacy over secular rulers was accepted or resisted by contemporary monarchs.
- Whether his support for the Fourth Crusade’s diversion to Constantinople was strategically beneficial or a long-term setback for Christian unity.
- The balance between his pastoral reforms and the violent methods used in the Albigensian Crusade.
- The impact of his imperial interventions on the stability of medieval Europe.
