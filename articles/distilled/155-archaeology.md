# Archaeology

## Overview

Archaeology is the study of human activity through the recovery and analysis of material culture—artifacts, architecture, biofacts, sites, and cultural landscapes. It spans human history from the first stone tools at Lomekwi in East Africa 3.3 million years ago to recent decades, and is distinct from palaeontology (the study of fossil remains). Because over 99% of the human past is prehistoric (lacking written records), archaeology is the primary means of understanding early human societies. The discipline developed from antiquarianism in 19th-century Europe and now encompasses numerous sub-disciplines and scientific techniques.

## Body

### Early history and antiquarianism

The first archaeologist is considered King Nabonidus (c. 550 BC), who discovered and analysed a foundation deposit of the Akkadian ruler Naram-Sin (ruled c. 2200 BC). He led excavations that found foundation deposits of temples in Sippar and Harran, and had the temples restored to their former glory. He was also the first to date an archaeological artifact; his estimate was inaccurate by about 1,500 years but reasonable given available technology.

Antiquarianism, the precursor to archaeology, focused on empirical evidence—encapsulated in Richard Colt Hoare's motto "We speak from facts, not theory." In Song dynasty China (960–1279), Ouyang Xiu and Zhao Mingcheng established Chinese epigraphy (study of inscriptions). Shen Kuo (book published 1088) criticized scholars for attributing bronze vessels to sages rather than artisans. These pursuits were considered a branch of historiography, not a separate discipline. In Renaissance Europe, Cyriacus of Ancona—called "father of classical archaeology"—recorded buildings, statues, and inscriptions across Greece and the Eastern Mediterranean, including the Parthenon and Egyptian pyramids. Flavio Biondo created a systematic guide to ancient Rome's ruins. The Oxford English Dictionary first cites "archaeologist" in 1824. "Archaeology," from 1607 onward, initially meant what we would call "ancient history" generally, with the narrower modern sense first seen in 1837.

### Development of method

Early excavations at Stonehenge (John Aubrey, 1626–1697) and Pompeii/Herculaneum (Roque Joaquín de Alcubierre, beginning 1738 and 1748) were often haphazard, overlooking stratification and context. Johann Joachim Winckelmann applied stylistic categories systematically to art history. William Cunnington (1754–1810) made meticulous recordings of Neolithic and Bronze Age barrows in Wiltshire. Thomas Jefferson's 1784 trench-method excavations of Virginia mounds led him to conclude Native American ancestors could have built them.

Nineteenth-century archaeology borrowed stratigraphy (overlapping strata tracing successive periods) from geology and palaeontology (William Smith, James Hutton, Charles Lyell). Jacques Boucher de Perthes and Christian Jürgensen Thomsen arranged artifacts chronologically. Augustus Pitt Rivers, excavating in the 1880s, insisted all artifacts be collected and catalogued, arranging them by type and chronology. William Flinders Petrie developed pottery-based dating layers, revolutionizing Egyptology, and mentored Howard Carter (who later discovered Tutankhamun's tomb). The first widely popular stratigraphic excavation was at Hissarlik (Troy) in the 1870s by Heinrich Schliemann, Frank Calvert, and Wilhelm Dörpfeld, revealing nine overlapping cities from prehistory to the Hellenistic period. Arthur Evans' work at Knossos revealed Minoan civilization. Mortimer Wheeler's grid system (1920s–1930s), refined by Kathleen Kenyon, brought disciplined excavation. By the mid-20th century, archaeology was a university profession; maritime, urban, and rescue archaeology emerged later.

### Purpose and theory

Archaeology's purpose is understanding past societies, especially the 99% of human development occurring in prehistoric cultures. It illuminates developments including fire use, stone tools, metallurgy, religion, and agriculture. For literate cultures, written records are often incomplete and biased—literacy was typically restricted to elites—so material records may better represent broader society. Writing did not exist until the 4th millennium BC, and only in a few civilizations; Homo sapiens has existed at least 200,000 years.

Archaeological theory has evolved through several schools. Cultural-historical archaeology (late 19th century) explained why cultures changed. The direct historical approach compared past and contemporary ethnic groups. Processual archaeology (1960s, led by Lewis Binford and Kent Flannery) emphasized hypothesis testing and scientific method. Post-processual archaeology (1980s, led by Michael Shanks, Christopher Tilley, Daniel Miller, Ian Hodder) questioned scientific positivism and emphasized reflexivity; processualists criticize it as lacking rigor. Historical processualism seeks to combine both. Theory now borrows from systems theory, phenomenology, Marxism, feminist archaeology, queer theory, postcolonial thought, and posthumanism.

### Methods

Archaeological investigation involves survey, excavation, and analysis. Remote sensing identifies sites before digging. Passive instruments detect natural radiation (e.g., satellite imagery); active instruments emit energy and record reflections. Lidar (light detection and ranging) uses lasers to measure distances; laser altimeters measure platform height; drones (costing as little as £650) create 3D models—in Peru, producing models in days instead of months.

Field survey locates sites systematically. Gordon Willey pioneered regional settlement pattern survey in 1949 in Peru's Viru Valley. Surface survey involves combing an area on foot; it cannot detect fully buried sites. Aerial survey uses cameras on airplanes, balloons, or kites; plants growing above buried structures develop differently, revealing outlines. Geophysical survey uses magnetometers (detecting magnetic field deviations from iron, kilns, ditches) and electrical resistivity devices (stone/brick has higher resistivity than soil; organic deposits lower). Metal detectors are debated but used formally, including in the UK's Portable Antiquities Scheme.

Excavation remains the primary data source, revealing stratigraphy and three-dimensional structure. Modern techniques require recording provenance (precise location) and association (relationship to nearby objects). Features consist of a cut (edge meeting natural soil) and fill (contents), given consecutive numbers. Excavation is expensive and destructive, so few sites are fully excavated. Mechanical equipment removes topsoil; hand-cleaning with trowels follows.

Post-excavation analysis is usually the most time-consuming phase. Artifacts are cleaned, catalogued, and compared typologically. Archaeological science offers dating and compositional analysis; zooarchaeology, paleoethnobotany, palynology (pollen study), and stable isotopes analyze organic remains.

### Subfields

Historical archaeology studies cultures with writing—e.g., medieval unbaptized children's burials, New York's 18th-century African Burial Ground, WWII Siegfried Line. Ethnoarchaeology studies living people to interpret the archaeological record, gaining prominence in the 1960s. Experimental archaeology applies the experimental method to understand processes creating the archaeological record. Archaeometry systematizes measurement, applying physics, chemistry, and engineering to determine chemical composition and spatial characteristics. Digital archaeology applies IT and data management, including 3D virtual models, photogrammetry, agent-based modelling, and data mining.

Cultural resource management (CRM), also called cultural heritage management (CHM) in the UK, examines sites threatened by development. It accounts for most archaeological research in the US and much in western Europe. The US National Historic Preservation Act (1966) mandates consideration of archaeological effects on federal projects; UK PPG 16 (1990) requires planners to consider archaeology in development applications. CRM is criticized for time pressure and budget-driven bidding, though offices increasingly employ advanced-degree workers.

### Protection and public engagement

International protection of archaeological sites involves UNESCO and Blue Shield International, which has conducted fact-finding missions in Libya, Syria, Egypt, and Lebanon. Karl von Habsburg noted cultural destruction destroys identity, causing displacement.

Public archaeology aims to halt looting, curb pseudoarchaeology, and preserve sites through education. The US Forest Service operates Passport in Time (PIT) volunteer program. The Mardi Gras Shipwreck Project and Queen Anne's Revenge Shipwreck Project used webcasting for educational outreach. UK programs like Time Team increased public interest. The Archaeological Legacy Institute (founded 1999, Oregon) runs The Archaeology Channel. Public benefits include counteracting racism, documenting ignored communities, and contributing to tourism and social cohesion.

### African diaspora archaeology

African Diaspora Archaeology studies those forcibly transported through the Atlantic, Trans-Saharan, and Indian Ocean slave trades, as well as their descendants. Most research has been conducted in the Americas and Africa. In the 1990s, anthropologist Michael Blakey directed research during the New York African Burial Ground Project, initiating collaboration with the African descendant community. In 2011, the Society of Black Archaeologists was created in the US; co-founders Ayana Omilade Flewellen and Justin Dunnavant intend to build a restorative justice-based structure in archaeology. In December 2020, the US Senate unanimously passed a bill protecting historic African burial grounds, potentially creating an African American Burial Grounds Network. Barbados, eight days after becoming a republic on November 30, 2021, announced plans for the Newton Enslaved Burial Ground Memorial, led by architect David Adjaye, commemorating an estimated 570 West Africans buried in unmarked graves at the former Newton sugar plantation. In 2022, residents on Sint Eustatius spoke out against what they found were unethical excavations of their ancestors on the Godet and Golden Rock African Burial Grounds.

## Terms

- **Material culture**: Physical objects created or modified by humans, including artifacts, architecture, and ecofacts
- **Stratigraphy**: Overlapping layers of soil/strata tracing successive periods of occupation
- **Typology**: Arranging artifacts by type and chronology to highlight evolutionary trends
- **Processual archaeology**: 1960s movement emphasizing hypothesis testing and scientific method
- **Post-processual archaeology**: 1980s movement questioning scientific positivism, emphasizing reflexivity
- **Provenance/provenience**: Precise location of objects or features during excavation
- **Ethnoarchaeology**: Ethnographic study of living people to interpret the archaeological record
- **Archaeometry**: Systematic measurement of archaeological remains using physics, chemistry, and engineering
- **Cultural resource management (CRM)**: Professional practice examining sites threatened by development
- **Pseudoarchaeology**: Activities falsely claiming to be archaeological but violating scientific practices

## Debates and open questions

The validity of processualism versus post-processualism remains under debate; processualists criticize post-processualism as lacking scientific rigor, while post-processualists question processualism's claims to impartiality. Tensions between archaeologists and descendant peoples persist—exemplified by Kennewick Man—concerning sacred burial sites versus academic benefit; NAGPRA (1990) sought compromise by limiting institutions' rights to possess human remains. Repatriation raises questions about ancestral relationships: remains reburied at Kitigan Zibi in 2005 might be Iroquoian or from an earlier culture, not directly Algonquin. AI in archaeology risks oversimplifying complex data, perpetuating outdated ideas, and fostering deterministic views; Kansa has called for resisting commercial AI summaries that prioritize engagement over truth. Climate change projections indicate rainfall, temperature, and sea-level changes affecting archaeological deposits, while glacial melting creates the new field of glacial archaeology.
