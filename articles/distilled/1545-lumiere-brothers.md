# Lumière brothers

## Overview
The Lumière brothers, Auguste (1862–1954) and Louis (1864–1948), were French industrialists who manufactured photographic equipment in Lyon. They are best known for the **Cinématographe** (patented 13 February 1895), a lightweight, hand-cranked device that combined a camera, printer, and projector into a single unit. Their first commercial public screening on 28 December 1895 at the Salon Indien du Grand Café in Paris is traditionally regarded as the birth of cinema. Unlike Thomas Edison’s Kinetoscope, which required individual viewing through a peephole, the Cinématographe projected moving photographic images onto a screen for a collective audience—a model that quickly became the industry standard. The brothers viewed film as a scientific novelty rather than an entertainment medium and exited the film business by 1905 to focus on colour photography, where they achieved lasting commercial success with the **Autochrome Lumière** (launched 1907), the first practical colour photography process.

## Timeline
- **1862, 1864** — Auguste and Louis Lumière born in Besançon, France.
- **1870** — Family moves to Lyon; father Charles-Antoine establishes photographic plate factory.
- **12 Feb 1892** — Léon Guillaume Bouly patents the name "cinématographe" for a motion picture device.
- **13 Feb 1895** — Lumière brothers patent their own version of the Cinématographe (camera, printer, projector).
- **22 Mar 1895** — First projected screening (private) for Société d'encouragement pour l'industrie nationale in Paris; shows *Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory*.
- **28 Dec 1895** — First commercial public screening at Salon Indien du Grand Café, Paris; 10 short films shown to ~40 paying spectators.
- **1896** — International tour begins (Mexico City, Brussels, Bombay, London, Montreal, New York, etc.); screenings in Egypt (Alexandria, Cairo) begin November 1896.
- **1900** — Exhibit 'bichromated glue' subtractive colour process at Exposition Universelle in Paris.
- **1903** — Patent the Autochrome Lumière colour process.
- **1905** — Withdraw from film production.
- **1907** — Autochrome Lumière launched commercially; becomes dominant colour process for decades.
- **1948, 1954** — Deaths of Louis (6 June 1948) and Auguste (10 April 1954); buried in New Guillotière Cemetery, Lyon.

## Body

### Origins and Technical Development
The brothers were born in Besançon to Charles-Antoine Lumière, a photographer who moved the family to Lyon in 1870 to establish a photographic plate factory. Both brothers attended La Martiniere, Lyon’s major technical school. Their entry into motion pictures built on existing technologies: they adopted **film perforations** (originally implemented by Émile Reynaud) to advance film steadily through the mechanism, a critical solution for intermittent motion. While Léon Bouly had patented the name "cinématographe" in 1892, the Lumières engineered a practical, portable version (approx. 5 kg) that used a claw mechanism inspired by sewing machines to advance film at 16 frames per second. This three-in-one device could record, develop, and project film, eliminating the need for separate studio cameras and arcade projectors.

### First Screenings and Global Expansion
On 22 March 1895, Louis Lumière presented projected motion pictures to the Society for the Development of the National Industry in Paris. The program focused on colour photography (polychromy), but the black-and-white moving image *Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory* stole the audience's attention. The historic commercial debut followed on 28 December 1895 at the Salon Indien du Grand Café. The program comprised ten **actuality films** (non-fiction shorts documenting real life), each roughly 17 meters (56 feet) long, running approximately 50 seconds when hand-cranked. Titles included *The Sprinkler Sprinkled* (a staged comedy), *The Photographical Congress Arrives in Lyon*, and *The Sea*. In 1896, the brothers dispatched operators worldwide—Brussels, Bombay, London, New York, Buenos Aires, and Egypt (Alexandria 5 November, Cairo shortly after)—establishing the first global film distribution network. Their lightweight projector and the ability to shoot locally allowed rapid international adoption.

### Exit from Cinema and Colour Photography
Despite their foundational role, the Lumières viewed cinema as "an invention without any future" and refused to sell cameras to filmmakers like Georges Méliès, limiting their direct influence on narrative film development. By 1905, they ceased film production. They redirected their expertise to colour photography, experimenting in the 1890s with the Lippmann process (interference heliochromy) and a subtractive 'bichromated glue' process shown at the 1900 Exposition Universelle. Their breakthrough was the **Autochrome Lumière** (patented 1903, marketed 1907), an additive colour process using microscopic potato starch grains dyed red-orange, green, and blue-violet as colour filters on a glass plate. It was the first commercially viable colour photography process, dominating the market for decades and securing the Lumière company’s status as a major European photographic manufacturer until the brand disappeared following a merger with Ilford.

### Predecessors and Competitive Context
The Cinématographe succeeded where earlier devices failed by recording and projecting **photographic reality** rather than hand-drawn animations. Precursors included the phénakisticope, zoetrope, and Émile Reynaud’s Théâtre Optique (drawn images); Eadweard Muybridge’s Zoopraxiscope (painted silhouettes from chronophotography); and Ottomar Anschütz’s Electrotachyscope (high-quality photographic loops). Key rivals included Thomas Edison’s Kinetoscope (1894), a peepshow device Edison believed superior to projection—a miscalculation, as audiences preferred shared screen viewing. The Skladanowsky brothers’ Bioscop offered projected screenings in Berlin from 1 November 1895, but their bulky machinery and shorter films lost ground to the Lumières. Louis Le Prince’s *Roundhay Garden Scene* (1888) survives as the oldest film, but his 1890 disappearance prevented public demonstration. Kazimierz Prószyński built the Pleograph in 1894, and Lauste and Latham’s Eidoloscope showed longer films (up to 20 minutes) in the US from April/May 1895, though their company dissolved in 1896.

## Terms
- ****Cinématographe**** — A portable, hand-cranked device patented by the Lumière brothers (13 Feb 1895) that functioned as a camera, film printer, and projector in one unit.
- ****Actuality film**** — A non-fiction short film documenting real events, places, or people (e.g., workers leaving a factory, a train arriving), characteristic of early Lumière output.
- ****Film perforations**** — Regularly spaced holes along the edges of film stock used to advance the strip frame-by-frame through a camera or projector; adopted by the Lumières from Émile Reynaud’s implementation.
- ****Kinetoscope**** — Thomas Edison’s 1894 motion picture device allowing only one viewer at a time to watch films through a peephole; contrasted with the Lumières’ projection model.
- ****Autochrome Lumière**** — The first practical colour photography process (patented 1903, launched 1907), using dyed potato starch grains on a glass plate as a mosaic colour filter.
- ****Polychromy**** — Early term for colour photography; the stated focus of Louis Lumière’s lecture at the 22 March 1895 private screening.
- ****Théâtre Optique**** — Émile Reynaud’s 1892 projection system for hand-drawn animated images, a direct precursor to photographic motion pictures.
- ****Pleograph**** — A camera-projector device built by Polish inventor Kazimierz Prószyński in 1894, preceding the Lumière Cinématographe.
- ****Eidoloscope**** — A projection system by Lauste and Latham demonstrated to the press 21 April 1895 and opened to the public 20 May 1895 on Broadway; showed longer films at higher frame rates.
- ****Subtractive colour process**** — A colour reproduction method (like the Lumières' 'bichromated glue' process) where dyes subtract wavelengths from white light, as opposed to additive methods (like Autochrome) that mix coloured lights.

## Debates and open questions
- **Date of the first film:** Louis Lumière claimed in a 1948 interview to have shot *Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory* in August 1894, before the Kinetoscope arrived in France. Historians generally dispute this, arguing a functional Lumière camera did not exist before early 1895.
- **Priority of projected cinema:** The Skladanowsky brothers screened projected films to a paying public in Berlin from 1 November 1895, nearly two months before the Lumière brothers' 28 December 1895 Paris debut. The Lumières are traditionally credited with the "birth of cinema" due to the Cinématographe’s technical superiority, portability, and immediate global commercial impact.
- **Le Prince’s precedence:** Louis Le Prince patented cameras and projectors in the 1880s and shot *Roundhay Garden Scene* (1888), the oldest surviving film. His 1890 disappearance prevented public demonstration, leaving his claim to "first" unresolved in popular history.
- **Bouly’s patent:** Léon Bouly patented the name "cinématographe" in 1892 but failed to renew it or develop a working system; the Lumières adopted the name for their distinct, functional device.
