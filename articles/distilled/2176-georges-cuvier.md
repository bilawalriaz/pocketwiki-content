# Georges Cuvier

## Overview
Georges Cuvier (1769–1832) was a French naturalist and zoologist who established extinction as a scientific fact and founded the fields of comparative anatomy and paleontology. His work comparing fossils with living animals provided the foundation for vertebrate paleontology and strongly influenced 19th-century scientific thought, though his opposition to evolutionary theories and his racial studies remain controversial.

## Timeline
- **23 August 1769** — Born in Montbéliard (then part of the Duchy of Württemberg)
- **1788** — Began work as a tutor in Normandy, where he started comparing fossils with living forms
- **1795** — Moved to Paris; became assistant at the Jardin des Plantes
- **1796** — Published seminal papers proving extinction by analyzing mammoth, mastodon, and Megatherium fossils
- **1802** — Appointed titular professor at the Jardin des Plantes; became permanent secretary of the Academy of Sciences
- **1812** — Published *Recherches sur les ossemens fossiles*, establishing catastrophism
- **1817** — Published *Le Règne Animal*, his classification of the animal kingdom
- **1819** — Created a peer for life (Baron Cuvier)
- **1830** — Engaged in famous debate with Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire on function vs. morphology
- **13 May 1832** — Died in Paris during a cholera epidemic

## Body

### Establishing Extinction and Comparative Anatomy
Cuvier's 1796 papers on living and fossil elephants and the Megatherium were a turning point in paleontology. By comparing fossil jaws and teeth with those of living species, he proved that mammoths and mastodons were distinct, extinct species—not modern elephants living elsewhere. This ended the long-running debate about whether extinction was real. His method relied on the **principle of the correlation of parts**: all organs in an animal are interdependent, so the form of one part (e.g., a tooth) reveals the form of others (e.g., claws, skeleton). This allowed reconstruction of extinct animals from fragmentary fossils.

### Catastrophism and Stratigraphy
Cuvier proposed that extinct species were wiped out by periodic catastrophic floods, becoming the leading proponent of **catastrophism**. With Alexandre Brongniart, he studied the strata of the Paris basin, using characteristic fossils to establish **biostratigraphy**—the principle that sedimentary rock layers contain distinct fossil assemblages deposited over time. Their work, alongside William Smith's geological map of England, founded the discipline of stratigraphy.

### Opposition to Evolution
Cuvier strongly opposed evolutionary theories of Lamarck and Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire. He argued that fossil forms appear abruptly in the geological record and remain unchanged, and that any anatomical change would render an organism non-viable. He cited Egyptian mummies (from Napoleon's expedition) as evidence that species had not changed over thousands of years. In the famous 1830 debate with Geoffroy, Cuvier defended functional anatomy over morphological transformation. His influence discouraged evolutionary speculation until Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859).

### Classification and Major Works
Cuvier expanded Linnaean taxonomy by grouping classes into **phyla** (major body-plan divisions). He established four *embranchements*: Vertebrata, Mollusca, Articulata, and Radiata. His masterwork, *Le Règne Animal* (1817), classified animals based on functional integration of organs. He also described the first pterosaur (*Pterodactylus*) and identified the Mosasaurus, correctly speculating that reptiles once dominated before mammals.

### Racial Studies and Sarah Baartman
Cuvier conducted racial studies that contributed to scientific racism. He categorized three races (Caucasian, Mongolian, Ethiopian) based on skull measurements and perceived mental abilities. He subjected Sarah Baartman, a South African Khokhoi woman exhibited as the "Hottentot Venus," to examinations and, after her death in 1815, dissected her body, comparing her features disparagingly to monkeys. Her remains were displayed in Paris until 2002, when they were returned to South Africa.

## Terms
- **Paleontology**: Study of fossils to understand prehistoric life.
- **Comparative anatomy**: Comparison of body structures across species to determine relationships and function.
- **Extinction**: The permanent disappearance of a species, which Cuvier proved was a real natural process.
- **Catastrophism**: Geological theory that Earth's features were shaped by sudden, violent events.
- **Biostratigraphy**: Using fossils in rock layers to date and correlate geological strata.
- **Principle of correlation of parts**: The idea that all organs in an organism are functionally interdependent, so one part reveals the whole.
- **Phylum** (pl. phyla): A major taxonomic rank grouping organisms by fundamental body plan.
- **Embranchement**: Cuvier's term for the highest-level divisions of the animal kingdom.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Evolution vs. fixity of species**: Cuvier's rejection of evolution was influential but ultimately overturned by Darwinian theory. His observation of abrupt fossil appearances later inspired the concept of punctuated equilibrium.
- **Catastrophism vs. uniformitarianism**: Cuvier's catastrophist theory lost ground to Lyell's uniformitarianism after his death, but modern understanding of mass extinctions has renewed interest in catastrophic events.
- **Human fossils**: Cuvier stated no human fossils had been found but was not dogmatic; he later acknowledged a possible example when new evidence emerged.
- **Racial classification**: His racial theories, based on skull measurements and mental hierarchies, are now discredited but were foundational to scientific racism.
