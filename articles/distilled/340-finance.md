# Finance

## Overview
Finance is the discipline that studies the management, creation, and study of monetary resources, encompassing the processes by which capital is channeled from savers and investors to entities—individuals, businesses, and governments—that require funding. It functions as the backbone of economic activity by facilitating the allocation of assets and liabilities over space and time, balancing risk against expected return while incorporating the time value of money. The field is broadly divided into three interconnected domains: personal finance (household budgeting and planning), corporate finance (maximizing shareholder value through capital structure and investment decisions), and public finance (managing sovereign revenue, expenditure, and debt). Underpinning these areas are specialized subfields such as investment management, risk management, and quantitative finance, which employ advanced mathematics and financial engineering to price derivatives, optimize portfolios, and ensure regulatory compliance. **Multidisciplinary fields explicitly identified as foundations of business and accounting include mathematical finance, financial law, financial economics, financial engineering, financial technology (fintech), and financial econometrics—the branch of financial economics using econometric techniques to parameterize relationships. Computational finance, the branch of applied computer science dealing with practical finance problems and numerical methods, also supports these areas.**

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Earliest historical evidence of finance; banking originates in West Asian temples/palaces for grain storage.
- **c. 1792–1750 BCE** — Code of Hammurabi includes laws governing banking operations; Babylonians charge ~20% annual interest.
- **c. 1200 BCE** — Cowrie shells used as money in China.
- **700–500 BCE** — Coins introduced as money; Lydians use crude coins (c. 687 BCE) and open permanent retail shops (c. 640 BCE).
- **595–570 BCE** — Greek cities (Aegina, Athens, Corinth) begin minting own coins.
- **342 BCE** — Roman Lex Genucia reforms outlaw interest (largely unenforced).
- **c. 1st Century CE** — Julius Caesar sets interest rate ceiling at 12%; later lowered by Justinian to 4–8%.
- **1531** — First stock exchange opens in Antwerp.
- **1773** — London Stock Exchange founded.
- **1793** — New York Stock Exchange founded.
- **1900** — Louis Bachelier defends doctoral thesis, considered the first scholarly work in financial mathematics.
- **1960s–1970s** — Earliest doctoral programs in finance established.
- **Late 19th century** — Global financial system formed.

## Body

### The Financial System
The financial system facilitates the flow of capital between savers (surplus units) and borrowers (deficit units) across personal, corporate, and public sectors. Entities with income exceeding expenditure lend or invest surplus funds to earn returns via interest or dividends; entities with expenditure exceeding income raise capital through debt (loans, bonds) or equity (selling shares/stock). This intermediation is often indirect: banks aggregate deposits and lend to borrowers, profiting from the interest rate spread, while investment banks facilitate equity and debt issuance (initial public offerings, bond underwriting) and securities exchanges provide secondary market liquidity. Institutional investors (pension funds, insurance companies) and retail investors participate in these markets. Large-scale inter-institutional activity constitutes "wholesale finance," characterized by bespoke derivatives (options, swaps, structured products), financial engineering, and the employment of quantitative analysts ("quants") to manage risk, regulatory capital, and compliance.

### Areas of Finance
**Personal Finance** involves budgeting to meet needs while managing risk, covering income, spending, saving, investing, and protection (insurance). Key steps include purchasing insurance, understanding tax and credit effects, planning for large purchases and retirement, and maintaining banking relationships.

**Corporate Finance** focuses on maximizing firm value for shareholders through three primary decisions: *Capital budgeting* (selecting investment projects where accurate valuation is critical); *Dividend policy* (reinvesting excess funds vs. returning them to shareholders); and *Capital structure* (optimizing the debt-equity mix to minimize the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)). Financial managers handle short-term "working capital management" (inventory, credit, debtors) to ensure liquidity for operational expenses and debt service.

**Public Finance** manages finances of sovereign states and sub-national entities over long-term horizons (typically 5+ years). It covers expenditure identification, revenue sourcing (tax and non-tax), budgeting, and sovereign/municipal debt issuance. Central banks (e.g., Federal Reserve, ECB, Bank of England) act as lenders of last resort and influence monetary conditions. Related fields include *Development finance* (non-commercial funding for economic projects, often via public-private partnerships) and *Climate/Environmental finance* (funding mitigation strategies).

**Investment Management** is the professional management of securities (shares, bonds, real estate, alternatives) to meet investor goals. Core tasks include *asset allocation* (diversification across asset classes per the client's risk profile/horizon), *portfolio optimization*, *fundamental analysis* (valuing securities), and *technical analysis* (forecasting prices via past data). Performance is largely driven by asset mix. Managers use hedging, cash flow matching (bonds), "Greeks" (derivatives), and tracking error monitoring. *Quantitative funds* replace human judgment with mathematical models and automated algorithmic trading.

**Risk Management** measures and mitigates financial risks to protect corporate value. Key risk types: *Credit risk* (borrower default), *Market risk* (losses from price/exchange rate movements), and *Operational risk* (process/system failures). In corporates, risk management links to prior capital decisions and credit policy. In banks/wholesale institutions, it focuses on hedging trading positions, calculating economic and regulatory capital (under Basel III), and managing counterparty credit risk via "Middle Office" groups. Insurers focus on solvency: life insurers manage longevity and interest rate risk; property/casualty insurers manage catastrophe and claims volatility, maintaining capital reserves for unexpected losses.

**Quantitative Finance** (Mathematical Finance/Financial Engineering) applies sophisticated mathematical models to derivatives pricing, structuring bespoke OTC products, risk management (hedging, economic capital, Basel compliance), and quantitative investment strategies (algorithmic/high-frequency trading). It overlaps heavily with financial economics and computational finance.

### Financial Theory
**Managerial Finance** provides the theoretical underpinning for corporate financial decision-making, drawing on managerial accounting (profitability/performance analysis) and corporate finance (structure optimization, working capital). It aligns financial techniques with broader business strategy.

**Financial Economics** studies the interrelation of financial variables (prices, rates, shares) rather than real variables (goods/services). Its two pillars are *Asset Pricing* (investor perspective: determining risk-appropriate discount rates, derivative pricing via CAPM and Black–Scholes, extending neoclassical models when assumptions fail) and *Corporate Finance Theory* (user perspective: funding/dividend decisions under certainty via Fisher Separation and Modigliani–Miller, increasingly incorporating uncertainty via real options analysis). *Financial Econometrics* parameterizes these relationships.

**Financial Mathematics** (originating with Bachelier, 1900) focuses on modeling derivatives, interest rate/credit risk, insurance math, and portfolio management. Key tools: Itô’s stochastic calculus, PDEs, and simulation for derivatives; Value at Risk, stress testing, copulas, and "Greeks" for risk; advanced optimization for portfolios. A critical distinction exists between *risk-neutral probability ("Q")* for derivatives pricing (arbitrage-free) and *physical probability ("P")* for risk/portfolio management, linked by the Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing.

**Experimental Finance** creates artificial market settings to observe agent behavior, trading flows, and price mechanisms, testing the predictive power of financial economics theories and discovering new principles.

**Behavioral Finance** integrates psychology to explain deviations from classical rationality (e.g., market anomalies, trading biases). It bridges observed market behavior with theory and has evolved toward *Social Finance* (studying social interaction structures and idea diffusion) and *Quantitative Behavioral Finance* (mathematically modeling biases alongside valuation).

**Quantum Finance** applies quantum mechanical/computational approaches (econophysics) to solve high-complexity problems like option pricing, where classical computers struggle with convergence speed in rapidly changing markets. Models include quantum continuous and multi-step quantum binomial models.

### History of Finance
Finance originated with Bronze Age state formation and trade (c. 3000 BCE). Early banking in West Asia used temples/palaces for grain, cattle, and precious material storage. Sumerian Uruk utilized lending and interest (*mas*, "calf"); Greek (*tokos*) and Egyptian (*ms*) terms for interest meant "to give birth," reflecting a lender-centric view of value increase. The Code of Hammurabi regulated banking; Babylonians charged ~20% annually. Coinage emerged 700–500 BCE (Lydia c. 687 BCE, Greece 595–570 BCE). Rome outlawed interest (342 BCE), later capping it (Caesar: 12%; Justinian: 4–8%). Modern exchanges began with Antwerp (1531), London (1773), and New York (1793). Academic finance emerged with Bachelier’s thesis (1900) and expanded via doctoral programs in the 1960s–70s.

## Terms
- ****Capital Structure**** — The mix of debt (bonds, loans) and equity (shares) a firm uses to fund operations; optimized to minimize the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC).
- ****Financial Intermediary**** — An institution (e.g., bank, investment bank) that channels funds from savers to borrowers, earning a spread or fee for arranging the transaction.
- ****Risk-Neutral Probability ("Q")**** — A theoretical probability measure used in derivatives pricing where assets are assumed to earn the risk-free rate, enabling arbitrage-free valuation.
- ****Physical Probability ("P")**** — The actual, real-world probability measure used for risk management and portfolio optimization, reflecting true expected returns and risks.
- ****Value at Risk (VaR)**** — A statistical technique measuring the maximum potential loss in a portfolio over a specific time horizon at a given confidence level.
- ****The Greeks**** — Sensitivities (Delta, Gamma, Vega, Theta, Rho) measuring how a derivative's price changes relative to underlying parameters (price, volatility, time, rates).
- ****Basel III**** — A global regulatory framework for banks requiring minimum capital reserves, stress testing, and liquidity requirements to mitigate systemic risk.
- ****Modigliani–Miller Theorem**** — A proposition stating that, in perfect markets, a firm's value is unaffected by its capital structure (debt/equity mix).
- ****Quantitative Analyst ("Quant")**** — A specialist who designs and implements complex mathematical models for pricing, hedging, risk management, or algorithmic trading.
- ****Time Value of Money**** — The principle that money available now is worth more than the same amount in the future due to its potential earning capacity.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Model Validity vs. Reality:** Neoclassical models (CAPM, Black–Scholes) rely on assumptions of rationality and market efficiency that frequently fail during crises, prompting debate over how to incorporate "fat tails," volatility clustering, and behavioral biases into robust frameworks.
*   **Q vs. P Measure Bridging:** The theoretical link between risk-neutral pricing ("Q") and real-world risk management ("P") via the Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing remains a complex area of calibration and model risk.
*   **Behavioral vs. Traditional Finance:** The extent to which psychological biases (overconfidence, loss aversion) invalidate efficient market hypotheses and require new asset pricing paradigms is unresolved; the shift toward "Social Finance" adds network effects to this debate.
*   **Regulatory Efficacy:** Post-2008 Basel III capital/liquidity requirements aim to stabilize banks, but debate persists on whether they excessively constrain lending or fail to address shadow banking and systemic interconnectedness.
*   **Computational Limits:** The complexity of high-dimensional derivatives and risk calculations drives research into Quantum Finance and alternative computing, but practical advantage over classical Monte Carlo/PDE methods for real-time trading remains unproven at scale.
*   **Climate Finance Integration:** How to accurately price climate risk (transition and physical) into assets, structure green bonds, and align financial regulation with net-zero targets lacks standardized methodology.
