# Chemical element

A chemical element is a species of atom defined by its number of protons, called its atomic number. The term also refers to a pure chemical substance consisting of a single element. Elements are the building blocks of nearly all baryonic matter in the universe, organized in the periodic table, which groups elements by recurring physical and chemical properties. By November 2016, the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) recognized 118 elements, with the first 94 occurring naturally on Earth and the remaining 24 being synthetic.

## Body

### Description and occurrence

The term "chemical element" has two related meanings: a chemical substance consisting of a single kind of atom (a free element), or that kind of atom as a component of various chemical substances. For example, water (H₂O) consists of the elements hydrogen and oxygen even though it does not contain the substances H₂ and O₂. French distinguishes *élément chimique* (kind of atoms) and *corps simple* (substance); Russian similarly distinguishes *химический элемент* and *простое вещество*.

The lightest elements, hydrogen and helium, were created by Big Bang nucleosynthesis in the first 20 minutes of the universe in a ratio of about 3:1 by mass, with tiny traces of lithium and beryllium. Almost all other elements were made by natural nucleosynthesis. Of the 118 known elements, the first 94 have been detected directly on Earth as primordial nuclides or as natural fission/transmutation products of uranium and thorium. Six occur in extreme trace amounts: technetium (43), promethium (61), astatine (85), francium (87), neptunium (93), and plutonium (94). Less than twenty elements, including gold, platinum, and iron group metals, can be found uncombined as native element minerals.

### Atomic nucleus properties

An atom consists of a dense nucleus of charged protons and neutral neutrons, surrounded by an electron cloud. The nucleus is held together by the short-ranged strong nuclear force, which overcomes proton repulsion. The neutron–proton ratio determines stability; as proton number increases, more neutrons are needed (lead requires about 3 neutrons per 2 protons).

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different neutron numbers. Carbon, for instance, has three main isotopes: carbon-12 (about 98.9% of natural carbon), carbon-13 (about 1.1%), and carbon-14 (about 1 atom per trillion). The neutron number usually has little effect on chemical properties, so atomic number—not mass number—identifies an element.

Elements with atomic numbers 1 through 82 each have at least one stable isotope, except technetium and promethium. Elements 83 through 94 are unstable enough that decay of all isotopes can be detected; bismuth, thorium, and uranium have isotopes with half-lives long enough to survive from before the Solar System formed. The remaining 24 heaviest elements have half-lives so short they must be synthesized. Bismuth-209 has the longest known alpha decay half-life at 2×10¹⁹ years; tellurium-128 transmutes through double beta decay with a half-life of 2.25×10²⁴ years.

The mass number (A) counts total nucleons; the atomic mass of a nuclide is expressed in daltons (Da). The relative atomic mass of an element is the abundance-weighted average of its isotopes' masses. The standard atomic weight of chlorine is 35.453 u, reflecting about 76% chlorine-35 and 24% chlorine-37. Chemists define a pure element by atomic number; nuclear scientists define it by single-isotope purity.

### Chemical and physical properties

Quantum mechanics organizes electrons into shells, each holding a fixed number of electrons given by 2n² (2, 8, 18, 32…). Shell configuration determines chemical properties and the structure of the periodic table. Elements are broadly classified as metals (conduct electricity), nonmetals (do not), and metalloids (intermediate, often semiconductors). Specialized sets include actinides, alkali metals, halogens, lanthanides, transition metals, and noble gases.

Most elements are solid at standard temperature and pressure; only bromine and mercury are liquid at 0 °C and 1 atmosphere. Caesium and gallium melt at 28.4 °C and 29.8 °C respectively. Allotropy is the ability of an element to exist in multiple structural forms: carbon appears as diamond, graphite, graphene, fullerenes, and carbon nanotubes. Solid elements crystallize in seven families of structures; 40% form close-packed crystals. Density generally increases with atomic number, with noble gases and alkali metals being lowest.

### Nomenclature and symbols

Elements are identified by atomic numbers (1–118), names, and symbols. Names for anciently known elements (gold, mercury, iron) were kept, but national differences emerged: German uses "Wasserstoff" for hydrogen, and French uses "azote" for nitrogen. Since 1947, IUPAC has determined official names and symbols. New elements are named for properties, minerals, locations, mythical subjects, astronomical objects, or scientists. Element names are not capitalized in English, but symbols always are.

The current chemical notation system was invented by Berzelius in 1814, using abbreviations based on Latin names. Eleven elements have symbols from different roots than English names: Fe (iron), Hg (mercury), Sn (tin), Au (gold), Ag (silver), Pb (lead), Cu (copper), Sb (antimony), Na (sodium), K (potassium), and W (tungsten). General symbols include X (variable group, usually halogen), R (radical), and M (general metal). Isotopes are written with superscript mass numbers (¹²C, ²³⁵U); hydrogen's isotopes have special symbols: H (protium), D (deuterium), T (tritium).

### Origin and abundance

Only about 4.6% of the universe's mass is ordinary matter. The 94 natural elements came from at least four astrophysical processes: Big Bang nucleosynthesis (hydrogen, helium, trace lithium), stellar nucleosynthesis (carbon through iron), explosive nucleosynthesis in supernovae and neutron star mergers (elements heavier than iron), and cosmic ray spallation (lithium, beryllium, boron). Primordial abundance was ~75% protium, 25% helium-4, and 0.01% deuterium.

On Earth, trace elements continue to be produced by cosmic rays (carbon-14 from nitrogen) and radioactive decay (argon-40 from potassium-40). Elements heavier than iron generally decrease in abundance with atomic number. Earth's crust differs from solar abundance due to selective loss of light and volatile elements; oxygen is most abundant by mass in the Earth as a whole. The human body follows seawater composition, with extra carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus. All life consists chiefly of six elements—CHNOPS: carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur.

### History

Ancient philosophy proposed classical elements (earth, water, air, fire); Plato first used the term "elements" around 360 BCE, and Aristotle added aether. In 1661, Robert Boyle's *The Sceptical Chymist* proposed corpuscularism, arguing matter consists of irreducible particles. Isaac Watts' 1724 *Logick* enumerated elements including sulfur, salt, earth, water, and "spirit." Lavoisier's 1789 *Elements of Chemistry* listed 33 elements; by 1818, Berzelius had determined atomic weights for 45 of 49 accepted elements. Mendeleev's 1869 table had 63 elements.

Moseley's 1913 discovery that nuclear charge determines atomic number led to the current definition. IUPAC defines an element to exist if it has isotopes with lifetimes longer than 10⁻¹⁴ seconds. Eleven prehistoric materials are now known to be elements; arsenic and bismuth were recognized before 1500 AD. Most remaining natural elements were identified by 1900. The first transuranium element (neptunium) was discovered in 1940; element 101 (mendelevium) was named in 1955. Since 1999, the IUPAC/IUPAP Joint Working Party has considered discovery claims; as of January 2016, all 118 elements have been confirmed.

## Terms

- **Atomic number**: The number of protons in an atom's nucleus, defining the element.
- **Isotope**: Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
- **Nuclide**: A class of atoms characterized by proton number, neutron number, and nuclear energy state.
- **Nucleosynthesis**: The process by which new atomic nuclei are created from existing nucleons.
- **Allotropy**: The ability of an element to exist in multiple structural forms with different properties.
- **Mass number**: The total number of protons and neutrons in an atomic nucleus.
- **Transuranic element**: An element with an atomic number greater than 92 (uranium).
- **Metalloid**: An element with intermediate properties between metals and nonmetals, often a semiconductor.
- **Standard atomic weight**: The abundance-weighted average of an element's isotope masses relative to the atomic mass unit.

## Debates and open questions

In the 1920s, there was controversy over whether isotopes deserved recognition as separate elements if they could be separated by chemical means. The naming of elements with atomic numbers 104 and higher was delayed by disputes over which research group actually discovered each element, a question handled by the IUPAC/IUPAP Joint Working Party since 1999. Nationalistic naming disputes occurred in the late 19th century (e.g., lutetium vs. cassiopeium; niobium vs. columbium). The discovery and synthesis of further new elements remains an ongoing area of scientific study.
