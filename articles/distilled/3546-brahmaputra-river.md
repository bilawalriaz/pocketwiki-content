# Brahmaputra River

## Overview
The Brahmaputra is a major trans-boundary river flowing through China, India, and Bangladesh. It is the 9th largest river by discharge and 15th longest in the world. Its course is geologically significant, cutting through the Himalayas in deep gorges, and it is vital for irrigation, transportation, and the livelihoods of millions in its delta region.

## Timeline
- **c. 1762** — An 8.8 magnitude earthquake causes the main channel to shift south, creating the Jamuna course in Bangladesh.
- **1884–1886** — Exploration establishes the river's identity as the Yarlung Tsangpo in Tibet.
- **1962** — The first combined railroad/roadway bridge (Old Saraighat Bridge) is opened in Assam, India.
- **1998** — The Bangabandhu Bridge (Jamuna Bridge) opens in Bangladesh.
- **2015** — China operationalizes the Zangmu Dam on the upper course in Tibet.

## Body

### Course and Geography
The river originates as the Yarlung Tsangpo on the Angsi Glacier near Mount Kailash in Tibet. It flows east for about 1,100 km before cutting through the Himalayas in the Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon. It enters India's Arunachal Pradesh as the Siang/Dihang River, then becomes the Brahmaputra in Assam. In Bangladesh, it is called the Jamuna, which merges with the Ganges (Padma) and finally the Meghna to empty into the Bay of Bengal. The river is antecedent to the Himalayas, meaning it existed before the mountains rose and entrenched its course.

### Hydrology and Flooding
The Brahmaputra has an average discharge of about 22,000 m³/s, with floods reaching 103,000 m³/s. Its flow is driven by two high-water seasons: early summer snowmelt and late summer monsoon rains. The river is prone to catastrophic flooding, which deposits fertile alluvium but also causes immense damage. The 1998 flood inundated over 70% of Bangladesh, affecting 31 million people. Deforestation in its watershed has increased siltation and flash floods.

### Channel Morphology and Engineering
The Brahmaputra is a classic braided river with highly mobile channels. Its course has shifted dramatically; a major avulsion between 1776 and 1850 moved its main channel 80 km west in Bangladesh. Bank erosion, often via slab failure, is a constant challenge. Engineering efforts include the Bangabandhu Bridge in Bangladesh and numerous bridges in India, such as the Bogibeel Bridge. China has built several bridges and the Zangmu Dam on its upper course.

### International Cooperation and Significance
The river's waters are shared by China, India, and Bangladesh. Chinese dam construction, like the Zangmu Dam, is a source of geopolitical tension, with downstream countries concerned about flow impacts. The Brahmaputra's delta is home to 130 million people, and its annual floods are essential for agriculture and fisheries. The river is sacred to Hindus and has a rare masculine name, meaning "son of Brahma."

## Terms
- **Antecedent river**: A river that existed before the landscape it flows through was uplifted, allowing it to maintain its course by downcutting.
- **Avulsion**: The sudden abandonment of a river channel and the formation of a new one.
- **Braided river**: A river with multiple, interwoven channels separated by temporary islands or bars.
- **Discharge**: The volume of water flowing through a river channel per unit of time.
- **Floodplain**: The flat area of land adjacent to a river, formed by deposited sediment and subject to flooding.
- **Riparian**: Relating to the banks of a river.
- **Slab failure**: A type of bank erosion where large blocks of cohesive sediment collapse into the river.
- **Tidal bore**: A strong tide that pushes up a river against the direction of the current.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the impact of Chinese upstream dams, like the Zangmu Dam, on downstream flow and sediment. China asserts no significant effect, while concerns persist in India and Bangladesh. The cause of the Brahmaputra's major course change in Bangladesh is also debated, with theories including tectonic activity, changes in the Teesta River's flow, and gradual avulsion due to bank erosion.
