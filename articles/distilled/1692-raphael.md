# Raphael

## Overview
Raffaello Sanzio da Urbino (1483–1520), known as Raphael, was an Italian painter and architect of the High Renaissance. Alongside Leonardo da Vinci and Michelangelo, he forms the traditional trinity of great masters of the period. His work is celebrated for its clarity of form, ease of composition, and visual achievement of the Neoplatonic ideal of human grandeur. Raphael synthesized the innovations of his predecessors—Perugino’s grace, Leonardo’s sfumato and pyramidal composition, and Michelangelo’s monumental figures—into a harmonious, "classic" style that defined academic art for centuries. His career unfolded in three phases: early years in Umbria under Perugino, a formative period absorbing Florentine art (c. 1504–1508), and a triumphant twelve years in Rome (1508–1520) working for Popes Julius II and Leo X. There, he decorated the Vatican Stanze, ran an exceptionally large workshop, and served as architect of St. Peter’s. Though his influence was eclipsed by Michelangelo immediately after his death, the 18th and 19th centuries enshrined him as the supreme model of decorum and balance, a status later explicitly rejected by the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood.

## Body

### Background and Early Training
Raphael was born in Urbino, a small but highly cultured court within the Papal States. His father, Giovanni Santi, was court painter and poet to Duke Federico da Montefeltro, exposing Raphael early to humanist culture and advanced Northern Italian and Netherlandish art. After his father’s death in 1494, the eleven-year-old likely helped manage the family workshop. Traditional accounts (Vasari) place him as an apprentice to Pietro Perugino around 1500, though some scholars argue for training under Timoteo Viti in Urbino. Regardless, by December 1500 he was documented as a "master." His early works (Baronci Altarpiece, *Mond Crucifixion*, *Marriage of the Virgin*) show complete assimilation of Perugino’s static, sweet style and shared technique: thick oil varnish in shadows, thin application on flesh, and a resin-rich medium prone to cracking.

### Florentine Influence (c. 1504–1508)
Raphael led a "nomadic" life in Northern Italy but spent significant time in Florence. A 1504 letter from the Duchess of Urbino recommends him to the Gonfaloniere as a gifted, well-mannered youth come to study. There, he encountered Leonardo da Vinci (resident 1500–1506), whose influence proved transformative. Raphael adopted Leonardo’s *sfumato* for subtle flesh modelling, the pyramidal Holy Family composition, and the three-quarter-length portrait format seen in the *Mona Lisa*. He also made studies of fighting nudes, a Florentine obsession. From Fra Bartolomeo he gained a new monumental quality in figures. However, Raphael softened Leonardo’s enigmatic psychological tension, retaining Perugino’s clear light and developing his own "interplay of glances" between figures. His *Deposition of Christ* (1507) attempted a classical sarcophagus composition but remained an isolated experiment in literal classicism.

### Roman Period: The Vatican Stanze
In 1508, Pope Julius II summoned Raphael to Rome, likely at the suggestion of architect Donato Bramante (a relative from near Urbino). He was commissioned to fresco the Pope’s private library (the Stanza della Segnatura), displacing other artists. This room—containing *The School of Athens*, *The Parnassus*, and the *Disputation of the Holy Sacrament*—is widely considered his masterpiece. It achieves *sprezzatura* (a term coined by his friend Castiglione): studied nonchalance concealing immense artistry. Figures possess "superhuman clarity and grace in a universe of Euclidian certainties" (Michael Levey).

The concurrent painting of Michelangelo’s Sistine Chapel ceiling profoundly affected Raphael. Vasari claims Bramante gave him secret access. Raphael responded by integrating Michelangelo’s muscular *ignudi* and sibyls into his own cohesive style, notably portraying Michelangelo as Heraclitus in *The School of Athens*. Michelangelo later accused him of plagiarism, claiming "everything he knew about art he got from me." Julius’s death in 1513 did not halt work; his successor, Leo X (a Medici), became an even closer patron. Raphael completed three rooms, increasingly delegating execution to his large workshop; a fourth room was finished posthumously. Later Stanze compositions involving dramatic action are considered less successful in conception and workshop execution.

### Architecture and Antiquarianism
After Bramante’s death in 1514, Raphael was appointed architect of St. Peter’s. His design (mostly lost, altered by Michelangelo) featured massive piers down the nave, criticized posthumously by Antonio da Sangallo the Younger as "like an alley." He designed several palaces (Palazzo Branconio dell'Aquila, Palazzo Jacopo da Brescia) and the Villa Madama for Cardinal Giulio de’ Medici—the most sophisticated villa design yet seen in Italy, influencing later architects including Palladio. He also designed the Chigi Chapel in Santa Maria del Popolo for banker Agostino Chigi.

In 1515, Leo X made Raphael Prefect of Antiquities. He regulated excavations, forbade destruction of inscriptions without permission, and proposed a visual survey of Rome’s antiquities. He intended to create an archaeological map of ancient Rome and offered to move an obelisk to St. Peter’s Square. Four archaeological drawings survive; his premature death halted these projects.

### Other Painting Projects
Beyond the Stanze, Raphael painted portraits of Julius II and Leo X (the former considered a masterpiece), friends like Castiglione, and diplomatic gifts for Francis I of France. For Agostino Chigi, he painted the *Triumph of Galatea* at the Villa Farnesina and designed mosaics for the Chigi Chapel. He created the *Raphael Cartoons* (10 designs, 7 survive) for tapestries of Saints Peter and Paul for the Sistine Chapel, woven in Brussels. He decorated the Vatican loggias with Roman *grottesche*. Major altarpieces include *The Ecstasy of St. Cecilia* and the *Sistine Madonna*. His final work, the *Transfiguration* (unfinished at death), signals a shift toward proto-Baroque dynamism and away from Mannerism.

### Workshop and Collaborative Practices
Raphael ran an exceptionally large workshop of perhaps fifty pupils and assistants—arguably the largest under any Old Master. It included established masters (Gianfrancesco Penni, Perino del Vaga) and sub-contractors. Key figures were Giulio Romano (c. 21 at Raphael’s death) and Penni, who inherited drawings and continued the workshop. Giovanni da Udine specialized in stucco and grotesques. Vasari praised Raphael’s harmonious management, contrasting it with Michelangelo’s stormy relations. However, many later wall paintings and some easel works are stronger in design (Raphael’s drawings) than execution (assistants). The 1527 Sack of Rome scattered the workshop, diffusing Raphael’s style across Italy.

### Drawings and Printmaking
Raphael was among the finest draftsmen in Western art, using drawings to shift resources "away from production to research and development" (John Shearman). He laid out stock drawings on the floor to compose rapidly; over forty sketches survive for the *Disputa* alone. He refined poses through numerous variants, produced full-scale cartoons for transfer via pouncing, and used a blind stylus for incised guidelines (visible in *The School of Athens*). His drawings are precise and highly finished, lacking the kinetic energy of Leonardo or Michelangelo but aesthetically satisfying. He was a late user of metalpoint and an early adopter of female models for preparatory studies.

Though he made no prints himself, Raphael collaborated with engraver Marcantonio Raimondi from c. 1510. This partnership produced ~fifty famous prints (e.g., *Judgement of Paris*, *Massacre of the Innocents*), establishing the reproductive print as a major medium. Designs were created both from paintings and *ex novo* for print. Baviero Carocci ("Il Baviera"), a trusted assistant, inherited the plates and became a successful print publisher.

### Private Life and Death
From 1517, Raphael lived grandly in the Bramante-designed Palazzo Caprini. He never married; a 1514 engagement to Maria Bibbiena (Cardinal Bibbiena’s niece) lapsed upon her death in 1520. A long-term companion was Margherita Luti ("La Fornarina"), a baker’s daughter. He held court honors: Groom of the Chamber and Knight of the Golden Spur. Vasari claims he considered a cardinal’s hat.

Raphael died on Good Friday, 6 April 1520, after a fifteen-day illness. Vasari attributed death to exhaustion from "unceasing romantic interests"; modern theories suggest infectious disease complicated by bloodletting. He remained composed, confessed, dictated a will providing for La Fornarina (via Baviera) and leaving studio contents to Giulio Romano and Penni. He was buried in the Pantheon with a grand funeral attended by four cardinals; Pope Leo X kissed his hand. Pietro Bembo’s epitaph reads: "Here lies that famous Raphael by whom Nature feared to be conquered while he lived, and when he was dying, feared herself to die."

### Critical Reception and Legacy
Raphael was admired by contemporaries but less influential than Michelangelo in the 16th century. Mannerism and Baroque moved art "in a direction totally opposed" to his qualities; "with Raphael's death, classic art—the High Renaissance—subsided" (Walter Friedländer). Mid-16th-century critics (Dolce, Aretino) upheld him as the ideal balanced painter—universal, graceful, rule-obeying—against Michelangelo’s "eccentric genius" lacking restraint. Vasari, though loyal to Michelangelo, later echoed this view.

From the late 17th to late 19th century, Raphael became the cornerstone of academic training and the supreme model for history painting (the highest genre). Winckelmann made him formative for Neoclassicism. Reynolds praised his "simple, grave, and majestic dignity," judging him superior in the *combination* of high qualities (composition, drawing, taste, assimilation of Michelangelo’s energy and antique beauty), while conceding Michelangelo the "sublime." Raphael’s Madonnas achieved massive 19th-century popularity through reproduction. In Germany, he inspired the Nazarene and Düsseldorf schools. In England, the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood (influenced by Ruskin) explicitly rejected him, arguing his "perfection of execution and beauty of feature" made artists prioritize finish over thought and beauty over truth. By 1900, his popularity waned behind Michelangelo and Leonardo, partly in reaction to etiolated academic "Raphaelism." Yet modern scholars note his influence remains the most continuous of all Renaissance masters.

## Terms
- ****High Renaissance**** — The brief period (c. 1490–1527) of exceptional artistic achievement in Italy, epitomized by Leonardo, Michelangelo, and Raphael, characterized by harmony, balance, and classical idealism.
- ****Neoplatonic ideal**** — A philosophical concept (revived in the Renaissance) positing that physical beauty reflects a higher, divine reality; in art, the depiction of human grandeur as a reflection of spiritual perfection.
- ****Sfumato**** — A painting technique (perfected by Leonardo) using subtle gradations of tone rather than sharp outlines to model form, creating a "smoky" atmospheric effect.
- ****Sprezzatura**** — Coined by Castiglione (*Book of the Courtier*, 1528): "a certain nonchalance which conceals all artistry and makes whatever one says or does seem uncontrived and effortless."
- ****Cartoon**** — A full-scale preparatory drawing for a fresco, tapestry, or painting; pricked and pounced (dusted with soot) to transfer the design onto the final surface.
- ****Grottesche**** — Decorative motifs (fantastic hybrid creatures, foliage, architecture) discovered in Nero’s *Domus Aurea* ("grottoes") in Rome; widely imitated by Raphael in the Vatican loggias.
- ****Prefect of Antiquities**** — Official post granted by Leo X (1515) giving Raphael authority over all ancient artifacts unearthed in or near Rome, including power to halt destruction of inscriptions.
- ****Reproductive print**** — An engraving or etching made after an existing artwork to disseminate its image; Raphael’s collaboration with Marcantonio Raimondi pioneered this as a major industry.
- ****Mannerism**** — Style emerging c. 1520 (at Raphael’s death) characterized by artificiality, elongation, instability, and intellectual complexity—explicitly opposed to High Renaissance balance.
- ****Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood**** — English reform group (founded 1848) rejecting the academic tradition derived from Raphael, seeking a return to pre-Renaissance "truth to nature" and moral purpose.

## Debates and open questions
- **Apprenticeship to Perugino**: Vasari claims Raphael entered Perugino’s workshop as a boy; the evidence is only Vasari and one other source. Some historians argue for training under Timoteo Viti in Urbino, noting eight was unusually early for apprenticeship. Consensus holds he worked as Perugino’s assistant from c. 1500.
- **Authorship in late workshop productions**: Many later Stanze frescoes and easel paintings were executed by assistants (Giulio Romano, Penni) from Raphael’s drawings. Distinguishing Raphael’s hand from theirs remains difficult and contested; quality varies significantly.
- **Cause of death**: Vasari cites exhaustion from sexual excess. Modern medical historians propose infectious disease (possibly typhoid or malaria) exacerbated by bloodletting, a standard contemporary treatment.
- **St. Peter’s architectural design**: Raphael’s plans (mostly lost) are reconstructed from few drawings. Sangallo the Younger criticized them as gloomy ("like an alley"); the extent to which Michelangelo’s later design rejected or adapted Raphael’s intent is debated.
- **Extent of antiquarian project**: Raphael’s 1519 offer to move an obelisk and his planned archaeological map of Rome were unrealized. The scope and methodology of his proposed visual survey of antiquities are known only from his letter to Leo X and diary entries by Marino Sanuto.
