# Jerome

## Overview
Jerome (c. 342–347 – 30 September 420) was a priest, theologian, and historian from Stridon (Illyria) who became one of the four Great Latin Church Fathers. He is best known for the Vulgate, a Latin translation of the Bible commissioned by Pope Damasus I (382–405) that replaced the Septuagint-based *Vetus Latina* with a version translated directly from Hebrew for the Old Testament. This scholarly achievement, combined with extensive biblical commentaries, letters, and the *Chronicon*, established the textual and exegetical foundation for Western Christianity for over a millennium. His work was deeply shaped by patronage from aristocratic Roman women—especially Paula, who funded his Bethlehem monastery—and his rigorous asceticism, which drew both devoted followers and fierce clerical opposition.

## Body

### Early Life and Conversion
Born at Stridon around 342–347, Jerome received a classical education in Rome under the philologist Aelius Donatus, mastering Latin and some Koine Greek. He engaged in the "superficial escapades" of Roman student life but suffered intense guilt, visiting the catacombs on Sundays to contemplate mortality. Though initially afraid of Christianity, he converted and, seized by a desire for ascetic penance, withdrew to the desert of Chalcis (the "Syrian Thebaid") circa 373–379. There he began learning Hebrew under a converted Jew, corresponded with Jewish Christians in Antioch, and copied a Hebrew Gospel (the *Gospel of the Hebrews*), translating portions into Greek.

### Ministry in Rome and Conflict
Returning to Rome as protégé of Pope Damasus I (382–384), Jerome revised the *Vetus Latina* Gospels using Greek manuscripts and updated the Roman Psalter from the Septuagint. He became the spiritual director for a circle of aristocratic women—widows Lea, Marcella, and Paula, and Paula’s daughters Blaesilla and Eustochium—guiding them toward monastic renunciation. Approximately 40% of his surviving epistles address women. His unsparing criticism of the secular clergy and the women’s rejection of Roman luxury provoked hostility. After Damasus’s death (10 December 384), a clerical inquiry into his relationship with Paula forced his departure. Public outrage intensified when Blaesilla died four months into his prescribed ascetic regimen, and Jerome insisted she not be mourned.

### The Vulgate Translation (382–405)
Settling in a Bethlehem monastery funded by Paula near the Church of the Nativity, Jerome undertook his magnum opus. He began (382) by correcting the *Vetus Latina* New Testament. By 390 he shifted to translating the Old Testament directly from Hebrew (*iuxta Hebraeos*), rejecting the Septuagint used by all prior Latin versions. He argued mainstream Rabbinical Judaism had rejected the Septuagint for mistranslations and Hellenistic corruptions. This decision contradicted Augustine and most contemporaries, who viewed the Septuagint as inspired. Modern scholarship debates whether Jerome’s primary source was the Hebrew text or the Greek Hexapla, but detailed studies affirm his competence as a Hebraist. He completed the translation by 405.

### Scholarly Corpus: Bibliography, Onomastica, and Commentaries
Between 392–393 Jerome composed *De Viris Illustribus*, a biobibliography of Christian writers from the apostolic age to himself, modeled on Greek and Latin precedents. It served as an apologetic demonstrating Christian literary achievement and defined a canon of knowledge. He also produced two *onomastica* (lists of biblical names and places with etymologies): *Liber de Nominibus Hebraicis* (based on Philo/Origen) and a translation/expansion of Eusebius’s *Onomasticon*. From 405 until his death he wrote extensive commentaries, explaining his Hebrew-based translation choices. These align with Jewish tradition and employ allegorical methods akin to Philo and the Alexandrian school. In prologues (notably the *Helmeted Preface* to Samuel/Kings), he distinguished protocanonical books (*Hebraica veritas*) from deuterocanonical/apocryphal works (Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobias, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees), asserting the latter were "not in the canon."

### Historical and Hagiographic Writings
Jerome’s *Chronicon* (c. 380), a translation and continuation of Eusebius’s chronicle, became a standard Latin historical reference despite errors. He did not consider himself bound by strict historiographical rules. His hagiography of the hermit Hilarion contains what may be the earliest clinical description of severe vitamin A deficiency: progressive night blindness, skin eruptions, and "stony roughness" resolved by adding oil to a diet of barley bread and vegetables.

### Letters
Jerome’s epistles—covering scholarship, conscience, comfort, polemic against clerical immorality, and ascetic exhortation—form a vital historical source. Blurring private and public discourse, they were widely circulated. Many were commissioned by consecrated virgins seeking lifestyle guidance, reflecting his role as a spiritual director for elite women.

### Theological Writings

#### Eschatology
Jerome identified the "mystery of iniquity" (2 Thess 2:7) as active in his day, restrained by the Roman Empire; its collapse would unleash the Antichrist. Against Porphyry’s claim that Daniel referred only to Antiochus IV Epiphanes (2nd century BC), Jerome upheld the traditional four-kingdom schema: Neo-Babylonian, Medo-Persian, Macedonian, and Rome. He interpreted the "little horn" (Dan 7) as a future human Antichrist in whom Satan dwells bodily, arising after Rome fragments into ten kingdoms. He identified Cyrus as the higher horn of the Medo-Persian ram (Dan 8:3) and the he-goat as Greece smiting Persia.

#### Soteriology
Opposing Pelagianism (c. 417), Jerome taught eternal punishment for the Devil and unbelievers but *temporal* punishment for Christian sinners who fall away—a view influenced by Origenism despite his anti-Origen polemics. Augustine criticized this position (without naming Jerome) in *On Faith and Works*.

### Reception and Legacy
Jerome is the second-most voluminous Latin Christian writer after Augustine. The Catholic Church names him patron of translators, librarians, and encyclopedists. The Council of Trent (1546) declared the Vulgate authoritative for public use. Historians note his "outstanding scholarship" (Edward Power) and the "great historical importance" of his correspondence (Andrew Louth), while Peter Heather characterizes him as an "appalling self-publicist" who "jumped on the ascetic bandwagon." He is commemorated on 30 September by the Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Lutheran, and Anglican churches.

### Iconography
Artistic depictions derive from the *Golden Legend* (13th c.). Common attributes: a lion (from the Androcles/Gerasimus tale), cardinal’s hat (anachronistic), skull, hourglass, candle, and *Cogita Mori* ("Think upon death") inscriptions reflecting the *vanitas* motif. He appears either as a penitent in the wilderness (ragged, beating his breast) or a scholar in a well-appointed study. The owl symbolizes wisdom; writing materials and the trumpet of judgment complete his iconography.

## Terms
- **Vulgate**: Jerome’s Latin Bible translation (382–405), commissioned by Damasus I, later declared authoritative by the Council of Trent.
- **Vetus Latina**: The collective pre-Vulgate Latin biblical texts, translated from the Greek Septuagint.
- **Septuagint (LXX)**: The Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures (3rd–2nd c. BC), the basis for all Latin OT translations before Jerome.
- **Hebraica veritas**: "Hebrew truth"; Jerome’s term for the Hebrew canonical text, which he privileged over the Septuagint.
- **Deuterocanonical / Apocrypha**: Books in the Septuagint but not the Hebrew canon (e.g., Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees); Jerome placed them outside the canon in his prologues.
- **Syrian Thebaid**: The desert of Chalcis near Antioch, populated by hermits (*eremites*), where Jerome practiced asceticism c. 373–379.
- **De Viris Illustribus**: "On Illustrious Men" (392–393), Jerome’s biobibliography of Christian authors, establishing a literary canon.
- **Onomasticon**: A list of biblical proper names (persons or places) with etymologies; Jerome produced two.
- **Pelagianism**: A theology (associated with Pelagius) emphasizing human free will and the possibility of sinless perfection; Jerome opposed it c. 417.
- **Four Latin Church Fathers**: Ambrose, Augustine, Jerome, and Pope Gregory I, recognized as the foundational doctors of the Western Church.

## Debates and open questions
- **Hebrew competence**: Scholars dispute whether Jerome translated the OT directly from Hebrew manuscripts or relied heavily on the Greek Hexapla (Origen’s six-column edition); the source text notes "some modern scholars believe that the Greek Hexapla is the main source" while "detailed studies have shown that to a considerable degree Jerome was a competent Hebraist."
- **Canon boundaries**: Jerome’s prologues inconsistently label deuterocanonical books—sometimes explicitly "apocryphal" (not in canon), sometimes merely "not read among the Hebrews" (Baruch)—leaving the precise status of certain texts debated in his own work.
- **Soteriology of the baptized**: His view that Christian apostates suffer temporal rather than eternal punishment (influenced by Origenism) was criticized by Augustine; the exact nature and duration of this punishment remain interpretively open.
- **Historical reliability of the *Chronicon***: The source acknowledges the work "is not without errors" and that Jerome "did not consider himself bound by the rules of historians," raising questions about its use as a primary historical source.
- **Lion legend**: The iconic lion-taming story is identified as "a figment" from the 13th-century *Golden Legend*, likely borrowed from the Androcles tale or confused with Gerasimus; its origin is a settled scholarly matter but persists in iconography.
