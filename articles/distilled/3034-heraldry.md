# Heraldry

## Overview
Heraldry is the system of designing, displaying, and regulating armorial bearings (coats of arms) to identify individuals, families, and institutions. It originated in medieval Europe as a practical means of identification on the battlefield and in tournaments, evolving into a complex symbolic language governed by strict rules. Today, it remains a living tradition used by governments, corporations, and private persons to represent heritage and identity.

## Timeline
- **c. 1066** — Bayeux Tapestry depicts shields, but no consistent heraldic designs are shown.
- **c. 1135-1155** — Earliest known seals with distinctly heraldic character appear in Europe.
- **1151** — Death of Geoffrey Plantagenet; his tomb enamel (c. 1155-1160) shows an early heraldic achievement.
- **1198** — Richard I of England first uses the arms of three lions passant-guardant.
- **c. 1350** — Bartolus de Saxoferrato writes *De Insigniis et Armis*, an early work of heraldic law.
- **1390** — The Scrope v Grosvenor case, a famous English heraldic dispute.
- **1419** — Henry V of England issues a proclamation regulating the assumption of arms.
- **1484** — The College of Arms is incorporated in England.
- **1530** — First heraldic visitation in England begins under Henry VIII.
- **1700** — Last heraldic visitation in England is carried out.
- **1882** — The Ottoman Empire adopts a European-style coat of arms.

## Body

### Origins and Precursors
While symbols like Egyptian serekhs, Roman shield markings, and biblical standards are precursors, true heraldry—with regular, hereditary designs—developed in the High Middle Ages. The common claim that it arose from knights' helmets obscuring faces is unsupported. Early heraldic art appears on seals and monuments from the 12th century, such as the tomb enamel of Geoffrey Plantagenet (d. 1151). The Crusades (1096–1487) likely helped disseminate heraldic practices and introduced elements like the surcoat (origin of "coat of arms") and mantling.

### Heralds and Regulation
The spread of arms created the role of the herald, an officer who learned and recorded pedigrees and devices. By the 14th century, the principle that arms belonged to a single individual was accepted, leading to disputes and the establishment of heraldic authorities. England's College of Arms (1484) and Scotland's Court of the Lord Lyon regulate grants. In many other countries, no official authority exists, and arms can be assumed freely if not infringing on others.

### The Heraldic Achievement
A full heraldic achievement consists of a shield (the essential element), a helmet, a crest (a figure atop the helmet), mantling, and often a motto, supporters, and other insignia. The shield is divided into a field, which may be a single tincture or divided by lines of partition. Tinctures are categorized as metals (or, argent), colours (gules, azure, sable, vert, purpure), and furs (ermine, vair). The "rule of tincture" prohibits placing a metal on a metal or a colour on a colour for contrast.

### Blazon and Design
Blazon is the formal language used to describe arms. Charges are objects placed on the shield, including animals (in specific attitudes like rampant or passant), geometric shapes, and ordinary lines. Marshalling combines arms through impalement (side-by-side) or quartering (divided into quarters) to show inheritance or marriage. Cadency marks (e.g., crescent, mullet) are used in English heraldry to distinguish younger sons from the senior line.

### National Styles
Heraldic traditions diverged into broad styles: German-Nordic (simple, with crests often repeating the shield design), Gallo-British (using cadency marks and controlled by authorities), Latin (lacking crests, with unique shield shapes), and Eastern European (often territorial, with simple shields). Modern traditions, like Canadian and South African heraldry, incorporate local symbols.

## Terms
- **Blazon**: The formal language and syntax used to describe a coat of arms.
- **Charge**: Any object or figure placed on a heraldic shield.
- **Crest**: A three-dimensional figure atop the helmet, part of a full achievement.
- **Escutcheon**: The heraldic term for the shield.
- **Field**: The background surface of the shield.
- **Ordinary**: A basic geometric shape (e.g., fess, pale, bend) used as a charge.
- **Rule of Tincture**: The principle that metals should not be placed on metals, nor colours on colours.
- **Tincture**: The limited palette of colours, metals, and furs used in heraldry.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise origin of heraldry is debated; while the Crusades disseminated it, the art likely developed independently in Western Europe.
- The extent to which ancient and medieval symbols (e.g., Roman shield markings) constitute true heraldry is disputed, as they lacked hereditary consistency.
- The legal status and regulation of heraldry vary widely globally, with some countries having strict authorities and others treating arms as unprotected intellectual property.
