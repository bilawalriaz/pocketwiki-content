# Alexander Pushkin

## Overview
Alexander Pushkin (1799–1837) is widely regarded as the greatest Russian poet and the founder of modern Russian literature. Born into nobility with African ancestry through his great-grandfather Abram Gannibal, Pushkin synthesized Church Slavonic, French neoclassicism, and colloquial speech to create the modern Russian literary language. His career was defined by a tension with autocratic power: exiled for liberal verses in his youth, he later negotiated a complex relationship with Nicholas I, serving as a court historian while enduring censorship. His masterpieces—the verse novel *Eugene Onegin*, the drama *Boris Godunov*, and the poem *The Bronze Horseman*—established the realist tradition and provided the libretti for Russia’s operatic canon. He died at 37 from wounds sustained in a duel defending his wife’s honor.

## Timeline
- **6 June 1799** — Born in Moscow to noble parents; maternal great-grandfather is Abram Petrovich Gannibal, an African general raised by Peter the Great.
- **1811–1817** — Attends the Imperial Lyceum at Tsarskoye Selo; publishes first poem at 15; influenced by liberal professor Alexander Kunitsyn.
- **1820** — Publishes first long poem *Ruslan and Ludmila*; exiled from St. Petersburg for political poems ("Ode to Liberty") to the south (Caucasus, Crimea, Bessarabia).
- **1823–1824** — Writes *The Prisoner of the Caucasus* and *The Fountain of Bakhchisaray*; transferred to Odessa, then exiled to mother’s estate Mikhailovskoye (1824–1826).
- **1825** — Writes *Boris Godunov* and *Eugene Onegin* (serialized 1825–1832) at Mikhailovskoye; *Ode to Liberty* found among Decembrist rebels' papers.
- **1826** — Summoned to Moscow by Nicholas I; released from exile but placed under strict censorship and travel bans.
- **1828–1830** — Meets Natalia Goncharova; engages (May 1830); delayed wedding due to cholera.
- **1831** — Marries Goncharova (Feb 1831); meets Nikolai Gogol; founds literary magazine *The Contemporary* (1836).
- **1833** — Publishes *The Queen of Spades* and *The Bronze Horseman*; completes *The Captain's Daughter*.
- **27 Jan 1837** — Mortally wounded in a barrier duel with Georges d'Anthès at Black River.
- **10 Feb 1837** — Dies of peritonitis; buried at Svyatogorsky monastery near Pskov.

## Body

### Ancestry and Early Life
Pushkin’s father, Sergei Lvovich, descended from a 12th-century noble family. His mother, Nadezhda Gannibal, was the granddaughter of **Abram Petrovich Gannibal** (1696–1781), an African page kidnapped to Constantinople, gifted to Peter the Great, and educated in France as a military engineer. Gannibal rose to *Général en Chef* (third-highest rank), governing Reval and building Russian sea forts. While Gannibal claimed origin from "Lagon," research by Dieudonné Gnammankou and Hugh Barnes established his birth in Central Africa near Lake Chad (modern Cameroon). Pushkin spoke French until age ten, learning Russian from household serfs and his beloved nanny, Arina Rodionovna. He entered the inaugural class of the prestigious Imperial Lyceum at Tsarskoye Selo (1811), where he studied under David de Boudry (brother of revolutionary Jean-Paul Marat) and absorbed Kantian liberalism from Alexander Kunitsyn and French Enlightenment thought, particularly Voltaire.

### Exile and Southern Period
Graduating in 1817, Pushkin plunged into St. Petersburg’s radical youth culture. His 1820 poem *Ruslan and Ludmila* stirred controversy, but his political verses (notably "Ode to Liberty") provoked Emperor Alexander I to exile him in May 1820. He traveled through the Caucasus and Crimea to Bessarabia (Chișinău, Kamianka). There, he joined the **Filiki Eteria**, a secret society supporting the Greek Revolution against Ottoman rule, and kept a diary of the uprising. In Chișinău (1820–1823), he wrote the Romantic "Southern poems"—*The Prisoner of the Caucasus* and *The Fountain of Bakhchisaray*—gaining acclaim. Transferred to Odessa in 1823, he clashed again with authorities, resulting in stricter exile at his mother’s rural estate, **Mikhailovskoye** (1824–1826).

### Mikhailovskoye and the Decembrists
At Mikhailovskoye, isolated but productive, Pushkin wrote nostalgic love poems (dedicated to Elizaveta Vorontsova), advanced *Eugene Onegin*, and completed the historical drama *Boris Godunov* (1825). The play was banned until 1830; its uncensored version premiered only in 2007. The **Decembrist Uprising** (Dec 1825) implicated Pushkin: his "Ode to Liberty" was found among rebel papers. Though many Decembrists were friends, they distrust his impulsiveness ("he had a big mouth"). After petitioning by friends and family, Nicholas I summoned Pushkin to Moscow in 1826, pardoned him, and appointed him Titular Counsel of the National Archives. However, the Tsar became Pushkin’s personal censor, restricting travel and publication.

### Court Life, Marriage, and *The Contemporary*
Pushkin’s 1826 audience with Nicholas I shifted his politics; he concluded the Tsar would advance Russia toward liberal ideals, expressing gratitude in verse ("No, I am not a flatterer..."). His 1831 patriotic poem *To the Slanderers of Russia* (on the Polish uprising) alienated liberals. He befriended Polish poet Adam Mickiewicz (c. 1825–1829) and traveled to the Caucasus front during the Russo-Turkish War (1829). Denied permission to travel abroad (1830), he married **Natalia Goncharova** (Feb 1831) after a year’s delay. The marriage was happy but strained by her flirtatiousness and his jealousy. In 1831, he met **Nikolai Gogol**, championed his *Evenings on a Farm Near Dikanka*, and founded the influential literary magazine **Sovremennik (The Contemporary)** in 1836, publishing Gogol and others.

### Death and Duel
By late 1836, Pushkin faced debt and rumors of Natalia’s affair with **Georges-Charles de Heeckeren d'Anthès** (Dantes-Gekkern), a French officer. Pushkin challenged d'Anthès (Nov 4); the duel was delayed, then cancelled after d'Anthès proposed to Natalia’s sister, Ekaterina (Nov 17). The marriage did not stop d'Anthès’s pursuit. On Jan 26 (Feb 7), 1837, Pushkin sent a "highly insulting letter" to d'Anthès, forcing a challenge. Pushkin’s chosen second, Arthur Magenis, declined at the last moment. The **barrier duel** (Jan 27 / Feb 8) at Black River required duelists to walk toward each other and fire at will; the first shooter must stand still for the return shot. D'Anthès fired first, the bullet entering Pushkin’s hip and abdomen. Pushkin’s return shot lightly wounded d'Anthès’s arm. Pushkin died of peritonitis two days later (Jan 29 / Feb 10). He was buried at Svyatogorsky monastery beside his mother.

### Literary Legacy
Pushkin created the modern Russian literary language by fusing three styles (high Church Slavonic, medium literary, low colloquial) with French gallicisms and parody. Critics trace his trajectory from Neoclassicism through Romanticism to Realism. **Vladimir Nabokov** noted his idiom combined Derzhavin, Zhukovsky, Batyushkov, Karamzin, and Krylov. **Vissarion Belinsky** produced the definitive critical study. Pushkin originated nearly every genre: lyric and narrative poetry (*The Bronze Horseman*), verse novel (*Eugene Onegin*—so complex Nabokov needed two volumes to translate it), drama (*Boris Godunov*, *Little Tragedies* including *Mozart and Salieri*), short stories (*Tales of Belkin*, *The Queen of Spades*), fairy tales in verse (*The Golden Cockerel*), and historical prose (*The Captain's Daughter*, *History of Pugachev*). He augmented the lexicon via calques and founded Russian magazine culture.

### Musical and Cultural Influence
Pushkin’s works are the bedrock of Russian opera: **Glinka** (*Ruslan and Lyudmila*), **Tchaikovsky** (*Eugene Onegin*, *The Queen of Spades*, *Mazeppa*), **Mussorgsky** (*Boris Godunov*), **Rimsky-Korsakov** (*Mozart and Salieri*, *Tale of Tsar Saltan*, *The Golden Cockerel*), **Dargomyzhsky** (*Rusalka*, *The Stone Guest*), **Rachmaninoff** (*Aleko*, *The Miserly Knight*), and others. Ballets, cantatas, and hundreds of songs (including settings of his French poems) derive from his verse. Western writers like **Henry James** acknowledged his influence despite translation barriers.

### Soviet and Post-Soviet Memory
The 1937 death centennial was a massive Soviet celebration (second only to Tolstoy’s 1928 centennial), renaming Tsarskoye Selo to **Pushkin** and plastering his image on propaganda. This clashed with his aristocratic libertinism, prompting "repressive revisionism" akin to the sanitization of Tolstoy. Post-2022, Ukraine removed Pushkin monuments and renamed streets under a law banning symbols justifying "Russian imperial policy." Honors persist globally: a minor planet (2208 Pushkin), a Mercury crater, UN Russian Language Day (June 6), museums in Russia, and statues in Washington D.C., Manila, Asmara, and Ontario.

## Terms
- **Abram Petrovich Gannibal** (1696–1781): Pushkin’s maternal great-grandfather; African-born military engineer educated in France, raised by Peter the Great, attained rank of *Général en Chef*.
- **Imperial Lyceum (Tsarskoye Selo)**: Elite boarding school (opened 1811) where Pushkin received his education and formed lifelong literary/political connections.
- **Filiki Eteria**: Secret 19th-century organization aiming to overthrow Ottoman rule in Greece; Pushkin joined during southern exile.
- **Decembrist Uprising (1825)**: Failed revolt by army officers against Nicholas I’s accession; Pushkin’s poems found among rebels, leading to his interrogation.
- **Barrier Duel**: Formal duel type where opponents walk toward each other from a set distance; the first to fire must stand still for the opponent’s return shot.
- **Sovremennik (The Contemporary)**: Literary magazine founded by Pushkin in 1836; pivotal for Russian magazine culture and publishing Gogol, Turgenev, Tolstoy.
- **Verse Novel (*Eugene Onegin*)**: Novel written in sonnet stanzas (Onegin stanza); Pushkin’s "grand opus" serialized 1825–1832, foundation of Russian realist novel.
- **Little Tragedies**: Four short philosophical dramas (1830): *The Miserly Knight*, *Mozart and Salieri*, *The Stone Guest*, *A Feast in Time of Plague*.
- **Calque**: Loan translation; Pushkin devised calques to fill gaps in Russian vocabulary, expanding the lexicon.
- **Vissarion Belinsky (1811–1848)**: Leading Russian literary critic; authored the definitive critical study of Pushkin, establishing his canonical status.

## Debates and open questions
- **Gannibal’s African origin**: Gannibal claimed "Lagon"; son-in-law Rotkirch mythologized it. Nabokov doubted the claim. Gnammankou and Barnes (1990s–2000s) argued conclusively for Central Africa (Cameroon/Chad region), but popular references sometimes still cite Ethiopia.
- **Dedication of "To***" (1825)**: The famous lyric poem’s addressee is disputed: Anna Kern (prevailing view), serf Olga Kalashnikova (Mikhail Dudin), Empress Elizaveta Alekseyevna (Kira Victorova), or fictional Tatyana Larina (Vadim Nikolayev).
- **Nature of the fatal shot**: Dostoevsky’s *The Idiot* suggests d'Anthès’s low hit (hip/abdomen) was accidental/fluke, implying he aimed higher (chest/head). Forensic certainty is lacking.
- **Pushkin’s political stance post-1826**: Scholars debate whether his cooperation with Nicholas I was pragmatic survival, genuine monarchism, or a complex negotiation preserving inner freedom. His "To the Slanderers of Russia" (1831) complicates the "liberal martyr" narrative.
- **Translation of *Eugene Onegin***: Nabokov’s literal prose translation (1964) vs. verse translations (e.g., Charles Johnston, James Falen) remains a live debate on fidelity vs. poetic form.
