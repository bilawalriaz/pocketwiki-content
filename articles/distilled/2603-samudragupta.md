# Samudragupta

## Overview
Samudragupta (c. 318–375 CE) was the second emperor of the Gupta Empire, whose military campaigns transformed it into a vast empire across ancient India. He is celebrated for his extensive conquests, performance of the Ashvamedha sacrifice to assert sovereignty, and patronage of arts, making his reign a pinnacle of the "Golden Age" of India.

## Body

### Ascension and Early Reign
Samudragupta was the son of Emperor Chandragupta I and the Licchavi princess Kumaradevi. He was chosen as successor due to his "devotion, righteous conduct, and valour." His ascension may have faced some internal rivalry, but he consolidated power, as evidenced by his early victories over kings like Achyuta and Nagasena.

### Military Campaigns and Territorial Expansion
The Allahabad Pillar inscription, a eulogy by his courtier Harisena, details his conquests. In the north (Aryavarta), he "forcibly uprooted" and annexed kingdoms of rulers like Rudradeva and Ganapatinaga. In the south (Dakshinapatha), he captured and later released kings such as Vishnugopa of Kanchi, likely reinstating them as tributaries. He also reduced forest region kings to subservience and received tribute from frontier kingdoms (e.g., Samatata, Nepal) and tribal groups (e.g., Malavas, Yaudheyas). His empire's core directly controlled territory from the Ravi River in the west to the Brahmaputra in the east.

### Coinage and Cultural Patronage
Samudragupta's gold coins, derived from Kushan standards, depict him in various roles: as a warrior (archer, tiger-slayer), a musician playing the veena, and performing the Ashvamedha. The coins' legends praise his victories and valour. He was a poet and musician, earning the epithet "king of poets," and a generous donor to Brahmins.

### Religion and Imperial Ideology
A devotee of Vishnu, Samudragupta was also tolerant of Buddhism, permitting a monastery at Bodh Gaya. He performed Brahmanical ceremonies like Sattra and Diksha. His Ashvamedha sacrifice was a key assertion of his status as a Chakravartin (universal ruler), a concept reflected in his Allahabad inscription. He is compared to legendary kings and deities for his qualities.

### Relations with Foreign Powers
The inscription mentions that rulers like the "Daivaputra-Shahi-Shahanushahi" (likely Kushan princes), Shaka-Murundas, and Simhala's king sought his favour through marriage offers or use of his seal. An Indian embassy may have reached Rome in 351 CE, possibly reflecting anti-Sassanian alliances.

### Legacy and Succession
Vincent Smith called him the "Indian Napoleon" for his military genius and empire-building. His expansionist policy was continued by his son and successor, Chandragupta II. Some sources suggest he was initially succeeded by Ramagupta, who was later dethroned by Chandragupta II.

## Terms
- **Gupta Empire**: An ancient Indian empire considered a "Golden Age" of Indian culture, science, and politics.
- **Prashasti**: A eulogy or panegyric, often inscribed on pillars or rocks, praising a ruler's achievements.
- **Ashvamedha**: An ancient Vedic horse sacrifice performed by kings to assert imperial sovereignty and universal rule.
- **Chakravartin**: A universal ruler or emperor in ancient Indian political theory.
- **Aryavarta**: The historical region of northern India, roughly the Indo-Gangetic Plain.
- **Dakshinapatha**: The historical term for the southern region of the Indian subcontinent.
- **Licchavi**: An ancient republican clan or tribe in India, whose princess Kumaradevi married Chandragupta I.
- **Veena**: A traditional Indian stringed musical instrument.
- **Tributary state**: A kingdom that pays tribute to a more powerful ruler in exchange for autonomy or protection.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Reign dates**: The start of Samudragupta's reign is uncertain (c. 319–350 CE) due to debates over whether he or his father founded the Gupta era.
- **Identity of Kacha**: Coins of a Gupta ruler named Kacha may represent an earlier name for Samudragupta or a distinct, rival king.
- **Southern campaign's success**: Some scholars argue Samudragupta only reached the Krishna River and was forced to retreat, but the inscription's claim of releasing kings aligns with ancient Indian political ideals of a "righteous conqueror."
- **Interpretation of "Daivaputra-Shahi-Shahanushahi"**: Scholars debate whether this refers to Kushan princes, Sasanian rulers, or a combination, reflecting complex northwest Indian politics.
- **Succession**: Official records name Chandragupta II as successor, but a play suggests Ramagupta may have ruled briefly first.
