# Aeneid

## Overview
The *Aeneid* is a Latin epic poem by Virgil (29–19 BC) that tells the legendary story of Aeneas, a Trojan who travels to Italy to become the ancestor of the Romans. It is a foundational national epic that ties Rome to the legends of Troy, glorifies traditional Roman virtues, and legitimizes the rule of Augustus Caesar. It is widely considered Virgil's masterpiece and one of the greatest works of Latin literature.

## Timeline
- **c. 6th century BC** — Greek vases depict Aeneas founding a home in Etruria; altars at Lavinium indicate early worship.
- **c. 5th century BC** — Fragment attributed to Hellanicus of Lesbos states Rome was founded by Aeneas.
- **280 BC** — Romans likely become interested in Greek myths after war with King Pyrrhus of Epirus.
- **29–19 BC** — Virgil writes the *Aeneid*.
- **19 BC** — Virgil dies, leaving the poem unfinished; Augustus orders its publication.
- **2nd century AD** — The *Aeneid* becomes a standard text for Latin education.
- **1513** — Gavin Douglas completes the first full English translation (*Eneados*).
- **1688** — Henry Purcell's opera *Dido and Aeneas* is first performed.
- **1798** — Ivan Kotliarevsky publishes *Eneida*, a Ukrainian burlesque parody.
- **2006–2021** — Major modern English translations by Fagles, Ahl, Ruden, and Bartsch are published.

## Body

### Mythology and Origins
The myth of Aeneas's post-Troy adventures predates Virgil by centuries. Greek colonists in Magna Graecia and Sicily used the story to link their new homes to mythology. The Etruscans adopted the story first in Italy. The Romans later subsumed the legend, likely becoming fully interested after the war against King Pyrrhus in 280 BC, as Troy offered a way to insert Rome into Greek historical tradition.

### Story and Structure
The poem is divided into two halves: Books 1–6 detail Aeneas's journey from Troy to Italy (mirroring Homer's *Odyssey*), and Books 7–12 recount the Trojans' war against the Latins (mirroring the *Iliad*). The narrative begins *in medias res* with a storm sent by the goddess Juno, who is wrathful against the Trojans. Aeneas's fleet reaches Carthage, where he recounts his wanderings to Queen Dido, including the fall of Troy and his fated journey. Their love affair ends tragically when Jupiter commands Aeneas to leave; Dido kills herself, cursing his descendants. After funeral games in Sicily, Aeneas visits the underworld, where his father Anchises reveals the future glory of Rome. In Italy, war erupts despite King Latinus's oracles. The poem culminates in a duel where Aeneas kills his rival Turnus, but in a moment of rage, not mercy.

### Themes
**Pietas** (dutiful respect) is the central Roman virtue embodied by Aeneas, who shows devotion to his family, gods, and people. **Divine intervention** is constant, with gods like Juno and Venus manipulating events, though they cannot alter **Fate**—the preordained destiny for Aeneas to found Rome and for Rome to rule the world. The poem is marked by **violence and conflict**, from Troy's fall to the final duel, which may comment on the recent Roman civil wars. The work also functions as **propaganda**, paralleling Aeneas's leadership with Augustus's restoration of peace and directly praising him in Book 6.

### Reception and Legacy
The *Aeneid* was an immediate success and became central to Latin education. Its influence is vast, shaping works like Dante's *Divine Comedy* (which features Virgil as a guide) and Milton's *Paradise Lost*. It has been translated into English many times, from Gavin Douglas (1513) to modern verse translations. The story has inspired operas, films, and numerous parodies, including the first literary work published in modern Ukrainian.

## Terms
- **Dactylic hexameter**: The metrical form of the poem, consisting of six feet per line, each made of dactyls (long-short-short) or spondees (long-long).
- **Pietas**: The Roman ideal of dutiful respect toward family, gods, and country, embodied by Aeneas.
- **In medias res**: A narrative technique that begins the story in the middle of the action.
- **Sibyl**: A prophetess; the Cumaean Sibyl guides Aeneas through the underworld.
- **Furor**: Fury or rage, which overtakes Aeneas in the final duel, contrasting with his pietas.
- **Augustan regime**: The period of rule by Emperor Augustus Caesar, during which Virgil wrote.
- **Gens Julia**: The family of Julius Caesar, which claimed descent from Aeneas's son Iulus.
- **Teleology**: A drive toward a climax or predetermined end, seen in the poem's prophecies of Rome's future.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Tone**: Is the poem ultimately a celebration of Augustus's new era or a pessimistic, subversive commentary on the cost of empire?
- **Aeneas's Character**: Does Aeneas's final, rage-filled killing of Turnus undermine his role as the embodiment of pietas?
- **Allegory**: What is the significance of Aeneas exiting the underworld through the "gate of false dreams"? Does it imply the history of Rome is a lie?
- **Unfinished State**: Did Virgil intend to revise the ending, or is the abrupt conclusion a purposeful artistic choice?
