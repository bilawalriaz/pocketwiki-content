# Tibetan Empire

## Overview
The Tibetan Empire was a powerful state centered on the Tibetan Plateau, formed in the 7th century under the Yarlung dynasty. It became a major Central Asian power, rivaling the Chinese Tang dynasty and expanding from the Tarim Basin to Bengal. Its history is marked by military expansion, the adoption of Buddhism, and a dramatic collapse in the mid-9th century, leading to a long period of political fragmentation.

## Timeline
- **c. 127 BC** — Yarlung dynasty founded in the Yarlung Valley.
- **c. 618** — Namri Songtsen assassinated; his son Songtsen Gampo takes power.
- **641** — Chinese Princess Wencheng arrives in Tibet, traditionally marking Buddhism's introduction.
- **645** — Songtsen Gampo conquers the Zhangzhung kingdom.
- **670** — Tibetans defeat the Tang dynasty at the Battle of Dafeichuan, gaining control of the Tarim Basin.
- **763** — Tibetan troops occupy the Tang capital Chang'an for fifteen days.
- **821–823** — Sino-Tibetan peace treaty is agreed and inscribed on a pillar in Lhasa.
- **838** — Emperor Tritsuk Detsen (Ralpachen) is murdered; his brother Langdarma is enthroned.
- **842** — Langdarma is assassinated, triggering the empire's collapse.

## Body

### Founding and Early Expansion
The Tibetan state originated at Taktsé Castle. Namri Songtsen unified neighboring clans and controlled the area around Lhasa before his assassination c. 618. His son, Songtsen Gampo, expanded power beyond the Yarlung Valley, defeating the Tuyuhun and conquering Zhangzhung. Diplomatic and military pressure on the Tang dynasty secured a marriage alliance with Princess Wencheng in 641. The empire's administrative and military structures were consolidated under subsequent rulers like Mangsong Mangtsen, who defeated the Tuyuhun and gained control of the Tarim Basin after the Battle of Dafeichuan in 670.

### Zenith of Imperial Power
The empire reached its greatest extent under Trisong Detsen and Tritsuk Detsen (Ralpachen). Trisong Detsen's reign saw the Tibetan army reach the Tang capital Chang'an in 763. The empire's territory stretched from the Pamirs to the Chinese provinces of Sichuan and Yunnan, and south into Bengal. Tritsuk Detsen, a devout Buddhist, was a generous patron of the religion, promoting translations and scholarship. His reign marked the peak of Tibetan political power, formalized by the Sino-Tibetan treaty of 821/822.

### Religion and Dissolution
Buddhism was introduced during Songtsen Gampo's reign but became a state-sponsored religion under Trisong Detsen, who invited Guru Padmasambhava to establish the Vajrayana tradition. The empire's "Religious Kings" were patrons of Buddhism. However, Emperor Langdarma, who took the throne after Tritsuk Detsen's murder in 838, persecuted Buddhists and destroyed monasteries. His assassination in 842 led to civil war (the Battle of U-Yor) and the "Era of Fragmentation," where the unified empire dissolved into semi-autonomous polities. The empire's vast, difficult terrain and internal competition between Buddhist and Bön factions also contributed to its decline.

## Terms
- **Yarlung Dynasty:** The royal lineage that founded and ruled the Tibetan Empire.
- **Vajrayana:** A form of Buddhism that became prominent in Tibet, often translated as "Diamond Vehicle."
- **Bön:** The pre-Buddhist, indigenous religion of Tibet.
- **Lamellar armour:** A type of armour made from small plates laced together, used by Tibetan soldiers.
- **Tarim Basin:** A large endorheic basin in Northwest China, a key region of conflict between Tibet and the Tang dynasty.
- **Era of Fragmentation:** The period following the empire's collapse, characterized by political disunity and regional warlordism.
- **Mahavyutpatti:** A Sanskrit-Tibetan lexicon developed to standardize Buddhist translation.
- **Chieftains:** Local leaders who ruled semi-autonomous regions after the empire's dissolution.

## Debates and Open questions
- The exact timing of the conquest of Zhangzhung is debated, with some sources placing it under Songtsen Gampo and others under Trisong Detsen.
- The causes of the empire's dissolution are multifaceted, involving internal religious strife, administrative challenges from its vast size, and competition between central authority and regional power blocs.
