# Fall of Constantinople

## Overview
The Fall of Constantinople on 29 May 1453, after a 53-day siege, marked the end of the Byzantine Empire and the Roman Empire, which had endured for nearly 1,500 years. Led by Sultan Mehmed II, the Ottoman victory was a watershed moment in history, signaling the end of the Middle Ages and demonstrating the transformative impact of gunpowder artillery on medieval fortifications.

## Timeline
- **330** — Constantinople consecrated as Roman capital by Constantine the Great.
- **1204** — Sack of Constantinople during the Fourth Crusade; Latin Empire established.
- **1261** — Byzantine Empire re-established under the Palaiologos dynasty.
- **1451** — Mehmed II becomes Ottoman Sultan.
- **1452** — Construction of Rumeli Hisarı fortress on the Bosphorus completed.
- **6 April 1453** — Ottoman siege of Constantinople begins.
- **20 April 1453** — A small Christian fleet breaks the Ottoman blockade into the Golden Horn.
- **22 April 1453** — Ottomans drag ships overland into the Golden Horn, bypassing the chain.
- **29 May 1453** — Constantinople falls after a final Ottoman assault.

## Body

### Background and Preparations
By 1450, the Byzantine Empire had shrunk to a few territories around Constantinople. The city itself was depopulated, its population halved by the Black Death in the 14th century. When Mehmed II became Sultan in 1451, he began preparing for conquest, building the Rumeli Hisarı fortress to control the Bosphorus and repairing roads for heavy artillery. Emperor Constantine XI sought Western aid, but the religious schism between Eastern and Western churches, and the failed Council of Florence union, meant support was minimal. A small force of Genoese and Venetian volunteers arrived, but no major Western fleet came.

### The Siege and Defenses
The city's formidable Theodosian walls had protected it for 800 years. The Byzantine defenders, numbering about 7,000, were vastly outnumbered by an Ottoman army estimated between 50,000 and 80,000. The Ottomans deployed massive cannons, including a famous bombard built by the Hungarian engineer Orban, which could fire 500 kg stone balls over 1.5 km. The Byzantines used a chain to block the Golden Horn, but Mehmed had his ships dragged overland to bypass it. After weeks of bombardment and failed mining attempts, the Ottomans launched a final assault on 29 May. The defenders were overwhelmed after the Genoese commander Giustiniani was wounded and retreated.

### Aftermath and Significance
Mehmed II made Constantinople the new Ottoman capital. The Hagia Sophia was converted into a mosque, and the Greek Orthodox Church was allowed to continue under a new patriarch. The conquest triggered a migration of Greek scholars to Italy, contributing to the Renaissance. The fall ended the Roman Empire and exposed Christian Europe to a powerful Ottoman state. It also marked a military revolution, proving that gunpowder artillery could breach even the most advanced medieval walls.

## Terms
- **Theodosian Walls**: The fifth-century triple-wall fortification system that protected Constantinople from land attacks for centuries.
- **Janissaries**: Elite infantry corps of the Ottoman army, often composed of Christian converts.
- **Bombard**: A large, early cannon used for siege warfare, capable of firing massive stone balls.
- **Golden Horn**: The natural harbor of Constantinople, protected by a defensive chain during the siege.
- **Millet System**: A system of religious autonomy for non-Muslim communities in the Ottoman Empire (though its origin is debated).
- **Palaiologos dynasty**: The last ruling dynasty of the Byzantine Empire (1261–1453).
- **Renaissance**: A cultural rebirth in Europe, fueled in part by the influx of Greek scholars and texts after 1453.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ottoman Army Size**: Contemporary sources give wildly varying numbers (160,000 to 300,000), while modern estimates suggest 50,000–80,000.
- **Fate of Constantine XI**: His body was never identified; legends claim he was turned to marble or will return.
- **Impact on the Renaissance**: The extent to which the migration of Byzantine scholars directly caused the Renaissance is debated.
- **Millet System**: The idea that Mehmed II created a formal system of religious autonomy for Christians is now considered a later myth.
