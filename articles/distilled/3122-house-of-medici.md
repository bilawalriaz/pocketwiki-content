# House of Medici

## Overview
The House of Medici was an Italian banking family and political dynasty that rose to prominence in Florence in the 15th century. They became the de facto rulers of the Florentine Republic, later establishing hereditary ducal and grand ducal rule over Tuscany. Their immense wealth, derived from banking and trade, fueled unparalleled patronage of art and science, making them central figures of the Italian Renaissance and the Counter-Reformation.

## Timeline
- **1230** — First documented mention of the Medici family.
- **1397** — Giovanni di Bicci de' Medici founds the Medici Bank in Florence.
- **1434** — Cosimo de' Medici returns from exile and becomes the leading figure in Florence.
- **1478** — The Pazzi conspiracy attempts to assassinate Lorenzo and Giuliano de' Medici; Giuliano is killed.
- **1494** — The Medici are expelled from Florence after Piero II submits to Charles VIII of France.
- **1512** — The Medici return to power in Florence.
- **1513** — Giovanni de' Medici becomes Pope Leo X.
- **1532** — The family acquires the hereditary title Duke of Florence.
- **1569** — The Duchy of Florence is elevated to the Grand Duchy of Tuscany under Cosimo I.
- **1737** — Death of Gian Gastone de' Medici, the last male of the main line; the dynasty's rule ends.
- **1743** — Death of Anna Maria Luisa de' Medici, who bequeathed the family's art and property to the Tuscan state.

## Body

### Rise to Power
The Medici originated in the Mugello region and first gained prominence in Florence's wool trade. Their decisive rise began with Giovanni di Bicci de' Medici, who founded the Medici Bank in 1397. The bank became Europe's largest and most prosperous, providing the financial base for political influence. Through strategic marriages and a central position in Florence's social network, the Medici outmaneuvered rival families like the Albizzi. Cosimo de' Medici, Giovanni's son, consolidated power in 1434, ruling unofficially as the head of the republic. His grandson, Lorenzo "the Magnificent," further solidified Medici dominance, though his neglect of the banking business led to its ruin.

### The Medici Popes and Exile
The family's influence extended to the papacy with the election of Leo X (1513) and Clement VII (1523). Their pontificates were marked by lavish artistic patronage but also financial crisis and political turmoil, including the Sack of Rome in 1527. This period saw the Medici temporarily expelled from Florence twice (1494–1512 and 1527–1530). Pope Clement VII later secured the title Duke of Florence for his illegitimate nephew, Alessandro, establishing hereditary Medici rule. The "senior" branch's line ended with Alessandro's assassination in 1537, passing power to the "junior" branch descended from Lorenzo the Elder.

### Grand Dukes of Tuscany
Cosimo I, from the junior branch, conquered Siena and in 1569 was proclaimed Grand Duke of Tuscany. His successors, including Ferdinando I and Cosimo II, promoted economic growth, infrastructure, and scientific patronage, notably of Galileo Galilei. However, the state treasury was often strained by wars and lavish spending. By the reign of Cosimo III (1670–1723), the grand duchy was bankrupt, its population had declined, and its international influence had waned.

### The Fall of the Dynasty
The last male Medici, Gian Gastone de' Medici, died in 1737. His sister, Anna Maria Luisa, signed the "family pact," bequeathing the Medici's vast art collections and personal property to the Tuscan state, on the condition nothing be removed from Florence. European powers allocated the sovereignty of Tuscany to Francis Stephen of Lorraine, ending Medici rule. The Habsburg-Lorraine dynasty succeeded them, though through a female-line connection to the Medici.

## Terms
- **Signore**: An unofficial title for a ruler of an Italian city-state, like the Medici in Florence.
- **Pater Patriae**: "Father of the Country," a title given to Cosimo de' Medici.
- **Gran Maestro**: The unofficial head of the Florentine Republic, a position held by Cosimo de' Medici.
- **Double-entry bookkeeping**: An accounting system tracking credits and debits, pioneered by the Medici Bank.
- **Arte della Lana**: The wool guild of Florence, central to the Medici's early wealth.
- **Mordant**: A substance (like alum) used to fix dyes to cloth; a Medici monopoly after 1433.
- **Pazzi conspiracy**: A 1478 plot to assassinate Lorenzo and Giuliano de' Medici.
- **Bonfire of the Vanities**: A 1497 event where puritanical friar Savonarola had luxury items and art burned.
- **Patto di Famiglia**: The 1737 "family pact" by which Anna Maria Luisa bequeathed Medici property to Tuscany.

## Debates and open questions
The origin of the Medici name and coat of arms is uncertain. Theories for the name range from "medical doctors" to other etymologies. The balls on their coat of arms have been variously interpreted as coins, pills, blood oranges, or symbols from Etruscan art. The family's legacy is also debated; they were criticized as corrupt by contemporaries like Machiavelli, while later nationalists viewed them more favorably as patrons of the Renaissance.
