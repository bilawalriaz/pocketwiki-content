# Sheba

## Overview
The Kingdom of Sheba (Saba) was an ancient South Arabian civilization centered in modern-day Yemen, flourishing from the 1st millennium BCE to the 3rd century CE. It was a major power in the spice trade, particularly of frankincense and myrrh, and its legacy is preserved in monumental inscriptions, architectural ruins, and biblical and Quranic narratives. The kingdom's history is primarily reconstructed from its own extensive epigraphic record, making it a unique case study in ancient history.

## Timeline
- **c. 1000–800 BCE** — Probable emergence of the Sabaean kingdom.
- **8th–6th BCE** — Height of power under the "mukarrib" period; conquests of Karib'il Watar unify much of South Arabia.
- **6th–1st BCE** — "Kingly" period of decline; loss of territory to Ma'in, Qataban, and Hadhramaut.
- **26/25 BCE** — Failed Roman siege of Marib under Aelius Gallus.
- **1st–3rd CE** — Second Sabaean kingdom re-emerges, with Sanaa as a secondary capital.
- **c. 275 CE** — Permanent annexation by the Himyarite Kingdom ends Sabaean civilization.

## Body

### Origins and First Kingdom
The Sabaean kingdom likely began between c. 1000 BCE and 800 BCE, with its heartland around the oasis cities of Marib and Sirwah. Its formative period concluded by the 10th century BCE with the development of a full script and complex architecture. The first major phase, the "mukarrib" period (8th–6th centuries BCE), saw Saba dominate South Arabia. Under King Karib'il Watar, Sabaean territory expanded through warfare to include Najran, the Gulf of Aden, and parts of the Hadhramaut plateau. This era was characterized by the title "mukarrib" (federator), denoting hegemony over other tribes.

After the 6th century BCE, Saba entered a long decline, losing territory to neighboring kingdoms like Qataban and Ma'in. Its leaders adopted the title "malik" (king). The economy, based on caravan trade, suffered when Roman conquests of Syria and Egypt redirected trade routes to maritime paths controlled by others. A failed Roman siege in 26/25 BCE further weakened the state, leading to its first annexation by the Himyarite Kingdom.

### Second Kingdom and End
The Sabaean kingdom re-emerged in the 1st century CE after the disintegration of the first Himyarite Kingdom. This second phase differed, with power shifting from desert oases to highland tribes. A new capital was established at Sanaa, and the kingdom flourished for about 150 years. However, it was permanently conquered by the Himyarites around 275 CE following a civil war among Yemenite dynasties. Saba then reverted to a tribe centered on Marib.

### Economy and Society
The Sabaeans were key players in the lucrative trade of aromatics like frankincense and myrrh, acting as intermediaries between the Near East, India, and Africa. Their society was tribal, with identity tied to lineage and the national god, Almaqah. The king was a warrior and builder, legitimized by ritual ties to the gods, but was not deified. Succession was often non-familial until the late period. Wealth came from royal lands, war booty, and rents, not taxation.

### Religion and Culture
Saba's pantheon was headed by Almaqah, the national god, whose main cult center was the Temple of Awwam at Marib. Other major deities included Athtar, Haubas, and two goddesses. The Sabaic language, written in a monumental script, is the best-documented of the Old South Arabian languages. The civilization left a vast epigraphic record, with over 6,500 known Sabaic inscriptions.

### Horn of Africa Connections
Sabaean influence is strongly attested in the Horn of Africa, particularly in the kingdom of Dʿmt (c. 800 BCE) in modern Ethiopia and Eritrea. Evidence includes Sabaean temples, inscriptions, and the use of the title "mukarrib." Scholars debate whether this reflects colonization, elite migration, or trade-driven cultural diffusion. Later Sabaean inscriptions and a temple have also been found in modern Somaliland and Puntland.

### Urban Centers
Marib was the primary capital, a large oasis city protected by massive walls. It was home to the great Temple of Awwam and the Marib Dam, an engineering marvel that irrigated the oasis for centuries. Sirwah was the second major urban center, smaller but significant as a religious and administrative site. Both cities were connected by an ancient road.

## Terms
- **Mukarrib**: A title meaning "federator," used by Sabaean rulers during their period of hegemony (8th–6th centuries BCE), considered more prestigious than "king."
- **Malik**: The title for "king," adopted by Sabaean rulers after the 6th century BCE as their territory contracted.
- **Almaqah**: The national god and high deity of the Sabaean pantheon, often hypothesized to be a moon god.
- **Sabaic**: The Old South Arabian language spoken in the Kingdom of Saba, with a rich epigraphic record.
- **Marib Dam**: A major irrigation dam near the capital Marib, crucial for the oasis's agriculture and mentioned in the Quran.
- **Dʿmt**: An ancient kingdom in the Horn of Africa (c. 800 BCE) showing strong Sabaean cultural influence.
- **Frankincense and Myrrh**: Aromatic resins that were the primary exports of the South Arabian trade networks.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact origin date of the Sabaean kingdom is debated, with estimates ranging from c. 1200 BCE to the 8th century BCE.
- The nature of Sabaean involvement in the Horn of Africa is contested: scholars of South Arabian archaeology favor migration or colonization, while African archaeologists stress indigenous development.
- The historicity of the biblical Queen of Sheba narrative is considered legendary by historians, as contemporary epigraphic sources show no evidence of such a diplomatic mission or female rulers.
