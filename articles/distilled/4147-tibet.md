# Tibet

## Overview
Tibet is a high-altitude region in East Asia, the traditional homeland of the Tibetan people, and the source of major Asian rivers. Its history is marked by the rise and fall of a powerful empire, periods of rule by Mongol and Chinese dynasties, and a modern political struggle over its status and autonomy within the People's Republic of China.

## Timeline
- **c. 7th century** — Yarlung dynasty expands; Songtsen Gampo founds the Tibetan Empire.
- **9th century** — Tibetan Empire dissolves into the Era of Fragmentation.
- **13th–14th centuries** — Tibet is under the administration of the Mongol Yuan dynasty.
- **1642** — The 5th Dalai Lama unifies Tibet under the Gelug school, establishing the Ganden Phodrang government.
- **1720–late 19th century** — Qing dynasty exerts military and administrative control, while granting political autonomy.
- **1912** — Following the Xinhai Revolution, Qing forces are expelled; the 13th Dalai Lama declares independence.
- **1950** — The People's Republic of China annexes Tibet after the Battle of Chamdo.
- **1959** — The 14th Dalai Lama flees to India after a failed uprising; his government is abolished.

## Body

### History and Political Status
Tibet's early history includes the Zhang Zhung culture and the rise of the Yarlung dynasty. Under King Songtsen Gampo in the 7th century, the Tibetan Empire expanded significantly. After its 9th-century collapse, Tibet was fragmented until the 5th Dalai Lama unified the heartland in 1642 with Mongol aid. From the 13th to 18th centuries, Tibet often fell under Chinese or Mongol patronage, with the Qing dynasty establishing direct rule after expelling Mongols in 1720. Following the 1912 Xinhai Revolution, the 13th Dalai Lama declared independence, though this was not recognized internationally. The People's Republic of China annexed Tibet in 1950, and after the 1959 uprising, the Dalai Lama's government was abolished. The Tibetan independence movement is now led by the diaspora.

### Geography and Climate
Tibet is the highest region on Earth, with an average elevation of 4,380 m (14,000 ft). It contains Mount Everest (8,848 m) and is the source of major rivers like the Yangtze, Yellow, Mekong, and Brahmaputra. The climate is severely dry for most of the year, with low temperatures and high winds in the west. The Tibetan cultural area includes the regions of Amdo, Kham, and Ü-Tsang.

### Language
The Tibetan language belongs to the Tibeto-Burman branch of the Sino-Tibetan family. It has numerous regional dialects, but the written form based on Classical Tibetan is consistent. The script is derived from ancient Indian Brāhmī. "Greater Tibetan" is spoken by approximately 6 million people, with about 150,000 speakers in exile.

### Religion and Culture
Tibetan Buddhism is the dominant religion, with five main traditions: Gelug, Kagyu, Nyingma, Sakya, and Jonang. The indigenous Bön religion also exists. Religion deeply influences Tibetan art (e.g., thangka paintings), architecture (e.g., the Potala Palace), music, and festivals like Losar (New Year). Staple foods include tsampa (barley flour), yak meat, and butter tea.

### Economy
The economy is based on subsistence agriculture and livestock herding. Tourism has grown to become the largest sector, accounting for 50.1% of GDP in 2020. The central government provides significant financial support and has invested in infrastructure like the Qingzang railway (opened 2006). Tibet is ranked lowest among China's provinces on the Human Development Index.

## Terms
- **Ganden Phodrang**: The Tibetan government established by the 5th Dalai Lama in 1642, ruled by the Dalai Lama and his regents.
- **Gelug**: The "Yellow Hat" school of Tibetan Buddhism, founded by Je Tsongkhapa, to which the Dalai Lama belongs.
- **Seventeen Point Agreement**: The 1951 agreement affirming Chinese sovereignty over Tibet while granting autonomy, later repudiated by the Dalai Lama.
- **Sinicization**: The process of making something Chinese in character, a term used in debates about cultural assimilation in Tibet.
- **Tsampa**: Roasted barley flour, the staple food of Tibet.
- **Thangka**: A traditional Tibetan Buddhist painting on cotton or silk, often depicting deities or mandalas.
- **Amban**: A Qing-dynasty imperial resident commissioner stationed in Lhasa.
- **Kashag**: The governing council of Tibet, led by the Dalai Lama.

## Debates and open questions
- The proportion of non-Tibetan migrants in Tibet is disputed, with the Central Tibetan Administration accusing China of altering demographics and Chinese census data reporting Tibetans as 90% of the population in the Tibet Autonomous Region.
- The extent of restrictions on civil liberties, religious practices, and cultural suppression (sinicization) in Tibet is debated among scholars and international observers, with reports varying.
- The legal and historical status of Tibet—whether it was an independent state or has always been part of China—is a core point of contention between the Tibetan independence movement and the Chinese government.
