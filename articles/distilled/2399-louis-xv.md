# Louis XV

## Overview
Louis XV (1710–1774) was King of France for nearly 59 years, the second-longest reign in French history. His rule saw significant cultural flourishing but also costly wars, financial decline, and growing conflict with the nobility, which weakened the monarchy and set the stage for the French Revolution.

## Timeline
- **1715** — Louis XV becomes king at age five; Regency under Philippe II, Duke of Orléans begins.
- **1723** — Louis reaches majority; Duke of Bourbon becomes Prime Minister.
- **1726** — Cardinal Fleury becomes chief minister, effectively ruling until 1743.
- **1733-38** — War of the Polish Succession; France gains the Duchy of Lorraine (1766).
- **1740-48** — War of the Austrian Succession; France wins at Fontenoy (1745) but returns conquered territories in the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748).
- **1745** — Madame de Pompadour becomes the King's chief mistress and advisor.
- **1756-63** — Seven Years' War; France loses most of its North American colonies to Britain.
- **1757** — Assassination attempt by Robert-François Damiens.
- **1764** — Jesuit Order suppressed in France.
- **1768** — Corsica incorporated into France.
- **1770** — Duke de Choiseul dismissed; Triumvirate government begins.
- **1774** — Louis XV dies of smallpox, succeeded by his grandson Louis XVI.

## Body

### Early Life and Regency
Louis XV inherited the throne at age five after a rapid succession of deaths in the royal family. His great-uncle, Philippe II, Duke of Orléans, served as Regent, annulling Louis XIV's restrictive will to gain full powers. The Regency was marked by the financial crisis of John Law's Mississippi Company collapse (1720) and the beginning of the Enlightenment, with Voltaire and Montesquieu publishing early works.

### Government of Fleury (1726–1743)
After a brief ministry by the Duke of Bourbon, Cardinal Fleury, Louis's former tutor, became chief minister. Fleury's long tenure was characterized by relative peace, financial stabilization, and the expansion of commerce and infrastructure. However, he enforced the papal bull *Unigenitus* against Jansenists, suppressing dissent and alienating the Parlements (regional noble assemblies). His diplomacy secured the future French acquisition of Lorraine but failed to prevent the costly War of the Austrian Succession.

### Personal Rule and the Seven Years' War
After Fleury's death, Louis XV attempted to rule directly. His reign was marked by conflicts with the Parlements over taxation and authority, particularly the "flagellation" speech of 1766 asserting absolute royal power. The disastrous Seven Years' War (1756–1763) resulted in the loss of New France and severely damaged French prestige and finances. The king's personal life, including his famous mistresses Madame de Pompadour and Madame du Barry, further eroded public respect for the monarchy.

### Final Years and Legacy
The final years saw the brief, conservative government of the Triumvirate, which suppressed the Parlements and attempted fiscal reform. Louis XV's reign ended with France enlarged by Lorraine and Corsica but financially weakened and diplomatically isolated. Historians generally view his reign as one of missed opportunities, where incompetence, extravagance, and failure to reform discredited absolute monarchy and left a legacy of crisis for his successor, Louis XVI.

## Terms
- **Parlements**: Regional assemblies of nobles with judicial powers, which increasingly resisted royal authority and taxation.
- **Regent**: A person appointed to administer a state because the monarch is a minor, absent, or incapacitated.
- **Unigenitus**: A 1713 papal bull condemning Jansenism, whose enforcement in France caused religious and political conflict.
- **Jansenism**: A Catholic doctrine emphasizing predestination and strict morality, allied with Gallicanism (French church independence).
- **Gallicanism**: A movement advocating for the autonomy of the Catholic Church in France from direct papal control.
- **Vingtième**: A direct tax of five percent on net income, introduced to tax all citizens, including the nobility and clergy.
- **Dixième**: A wartime tax of ten percent on revenue, replaced by the vingtième.
- **Lettres de cachet**: Royal orders for imprisonment or exile without trial, used to silence opponents.
- **Secret du Roi**: The king's private intelligence and diplomatic service, operating outside official channels.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historical Reputation**: Was Louis XV a weak, incompetent king whose actions led to the Revolution, or was his negative reputation largely created by revolutionary propaganda? Some scholars argue he was popular during his lifetime and that his reforms, if maintained, might have prevented the Revolution.
- **Role of Mistresses**: To what extent did his mistresses, particularly Madame de Pompadour, influence policy and contribute to ministerial instability and military failures?
- **Economic Policies**: Were his economic policies, such as the liberalization of the grain trade, a cause of social unrest, or were they sound reforms undermined by poor harvests and political opposition?
- **Conflict with Parlements**: Was the struggle with the Parlements a fundamental constitutional crisis over sovereignty, or primarily a fiscal and administrative conflict?
