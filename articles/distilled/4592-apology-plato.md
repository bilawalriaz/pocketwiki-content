# Apology (Plato)

## Overview
The *Apology of Socrates* is a foundational philosophical text by Plato, written as a Socratic dialogue. It presents the speech of legal self-defence Socrates gave at his 399 BC trial in Athens, where he was charged with impiety (not believing in the city's gods) and corrupting the youth. The work is a primary source for understanding Socrates' philosophy, his method of questioning, and the events leading to his execution, though its historical accuracy is debated.

## Timeline
- **469 BC** — Socrates is born in Athens.
- **423 BC** — Aristophanes' play *The Clouds*, which satirizes Socrates, is performed.
- **404 BC** — The oligarchic Thirty Tyrants rule Athens; Socrates is associated with this period.
- **399 BC** — Socrates is tried and executed in Athens.
- **399 BC** — Plato writes the *Apology*, one of several accounts of the trial.

## Body

### The Trial and Accusations
Socrates faced two formal accusations: *asebeia* (impiety) for introducing new spiritual agencies (*daimonia*) and corrupting the Athenian youth. He argued these charges stemmed from long-standing prejudice, partly fueled by Aristophanes' earlier satire. His three accusers—Anytus (representing politicians and craftsmen), Meletus (representing poets), and Lycon (representing rhetoricians)—each had specific grievances against him.

### Socrates' Defence
Socrates' defence centres on the Oracle at Delphi's proclamation that no one was wiser than him. To solve this divine paradox, he interrogated politicians, poets, and craftsmen, concluding that his wisdom lay only in knowing he knew nothing. He argued that corrupting youth would be illogical, as it would harm him too, and that no one had come forward to testify he was corrupted. He cross-examined Meletus, exposing a contradiction in the charge of atheism.

### The Verdict and Sentence
The jury found Socrates guilty by a narrow margin. When asked to propose a penalty, Socrates provocatively suggested he be rewarded with free meals at the Prytaneum for his service to Athens. He then offered a small fine of 100 drachmae. The jury rejected these and voted for the death penalty. Socrates accepted the verdict, stating that fearing death is foolish, as it may be a good thing, and that he owed greater obedience to the god than to the court.

## Terms
*   **Socratic dialogue**: A literary form, used by Plato, featuring a conversation that explores philosophical ideas through questioning.
*   **Impiety (*asebeia*)**: Lack of proper reverence for the gods or established religious customs.
*   **Daimonia**: Spiritual agencies or divine powers; Socrates claimed to be guided by a personal *daimonion*.
*   **Oracle at Delphi**: A sacred site where the Pythia, a priestess, delivered prophecies believed to come from the god Apollo.
*   **Sophist**: A professional teacher of rhetoric and philosophy in ancient Greece, often criticized for prioritizing persuasion over truth.
*   **Prytaneum**: A public dining hall in Athens where honored citizens and benefactors were given free meals.
*   **Drachma**: A standard unit of currency in ancient Athens.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Historical Reliability**: Scholars debate how accurately Plato's dialogue represents the actual trial and Socrates' words, given it was written after the events and is a literary work.
*   **Socrates' Guilt**: Philosophers and historians continue to argue whether Socrates was justly convicted under Athenian law or was a martyr for free inquiry.
*   **The Role of Rhetoric**: The text is interpreted both as a critique of manipulative oratory and as an implicit model for a more ethical, truth-seeking form of rhetoric.
