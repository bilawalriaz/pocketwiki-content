# Johannes Kepler

## Overview
Johannes Kepler (1571–1630) was a German astronomer and mathematician whose laws of planetary motion fundamentally transformed astronomy from a geometric exercise into a physical science. His work, blending rigorous mathematics with deep religious conviction, provided the foundation for Isaac Newton's theory of universal gravitation and established the modern scientific method.

## Timeline
- **1571** — Born in Weil der Stadt, Germany.
- **1596** — Publishes *Mysterium Cosmographicum*, proposing a model of nested Platonic solids for planetary orbits.
- **1600** — Expelled from Graz; joins Tycho Brahe in Prague.
- **1601** — Succeeds Tycho Brahe as Imperial Mathematician to Emperor Rudolf II.
- **1604** — Observes and writes about Kepler's Supernova (SN 1604).
- **1609** — Publishes *Astronomia Nova*, containing his first two laws of planetary motion.
- **1612** — Moves to Linz as District Mathematician.
- **1619** — Publishes *Harmonice Mundi*, which contains his third law of planetary motion.
- **1621** — Publishes an expanded second edition of *Mysterium Cosmographicum*.
- **1627** — Publishes the *Rudolphine Tables*, accurate planetary tables based on his laws.
- **1630** — Dies in Regensburg, Germany.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Kepler was born prematurely into a declining family. A childhood bout of smallpox left him with weak vision and crippled hands. He showed exceptional mathematical talent early, observing the Great Comet of 1577 and a lunar eclipse in 1580. After attending various schools, he entered the University of Tübingen in 1589 to study for the Lutheran ministry. There, under Michael Maestlin, he learned both the Ptolemaic and Copernican systems, becoming a committed Copernican. He was denied ordination due to theological views contrary to the Formula of Concord and instead accepted a teaching position in Graz in 1594.

### Graz and the *Mysterium Cosmographicum*
In Graz, Kepler published his first major work, *Mysterium Cosmographicum* (1596). Inspired by a geometric insight, he proposed that the six known planets were spaced according to the five Platonic solids nested between their orbital spheres. This model, though ultimately incorrect, was a crucial first step in cleansing the Copernican system of Ptolemaic remnants and established his reputation. Religious tensions in Catholic-controlled Graz led to his expulsion in 1600, prompting his move to Prague to join the astronomer Tycho Brahe.

### Prague and the *Astronomia Nova*
After Tycho Brahe's death in 1601, Kepler became Imperial Mathematician. Using Tycho's precise observational data, he spent years analyzing the orbit of Mars. Rejecting traditional circular orbits, he introduced the revolutionary concept of a planetary orbit as a physical path. He discovered that Mars's orbit was an ellipse with the Sun at one focus (his first law) and that planets sweep out equal areas in equal times (his second law). These findings were published in *Astronomia Nova* (1609), a work that transformed astronomy into a physical science.

### Linz, *Harmonice Mundi*, and Later Works
Kepler moved to Linz in 1612, where he was excommunicated from the Lutheran church for refusing to endorse the Formula of Concord. He completed the *Rudolphine Tables* (1627), accurate planetary tables based on his laws. In *Harmonice Mundi* (1619), he explored cosmic harmonies and discovered his third law: the square of a planet's orbital period is proportional to the cube of its semi-major axis. He also published works on optics (*Astronomiae Pars Optica*, *Dioptrice*), mathematics (*Nova stereometria doliorum vinariorum*), and defended his mother against witchcraft charges.

### Optics and Mathematics
Kepler's *Astronomiae Pars Optica* (1604) is the foundation of modern optics, describing the inverse-square law of light intensity and the function of the eye's lens. In *Dioptrice* (1611), he described the theoretical basis for telescopes and invented the improved Keplerian telescope. His mathematical work included early contributions to infinitesimal methods and the Kepler conjecture on sphere packing, which was not formally solved until 2017.

### Astrology and Tides
Kepler considered astrology the counterpart to astronomy but sought to reform it on a scientific footing. He published astrological calendars and treatises like *De Fundamentis* (1601). He also theorized that tides were caused by the Moon's gravitational attraction on Earth's waters, a concept rejected by Galileo but later validated by Newton.

## Terms
- **Platonic solids**: The five regular convex polyhedra (tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, icosahedron).
- **Heliocentrism**: The astronomical model in which the Earth and planets revolve around the Sun.
- **Elliptical orbit**: An orbit shaped like an ellipse, with the central body at one focus.
- **Ephemeris**: A table of calculated positions of a celestial object at regular intervals.
- **Rudolphine Tables**: Planetary tables published by Kepler in 1627, based on Tycho Brahe's observations and Kepler's laws.
- **Formula of Concord**: A Lutheran statement of faith adopted in 1577.
- **Keplerian telescope**: A refracting telescope with two convex lenses, providing a wider field of view than the Galilean telescope.
- **Kepler conjecture**: A mathematical statement about the densest way to pack spheres, proven in 2017.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Religious conflict**: Kepler's life was marked by tension between his Lutheran faith and the Catholic Counter-Reformation, as well as his own unorthodox views that led to excommunication.
- **Reception of his laws**: His laws were not immediately accepted; contemporaries like Galileo ignored them, and astronomers like Maestlin objected to his introduction of physics into astronomy.
- **Gravity vs. motive force**: Kepler proposed a quasi-spiritual "motive species" from the Sun to explain planetary motion, not a gravitational force. His concept of mutual gravitational attraction between bodies was not extended to a universal theory, which awaited Newton.
- **Astrology's validity**: Kepler defended astrology against both uncritical acceptance and outright dismissal, seeking a naturalistic basis for its influences.
