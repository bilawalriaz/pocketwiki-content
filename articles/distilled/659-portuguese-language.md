# Portuguese language

## Overview
Portuguese is a Western Romance language of the Indo-European family, written in the Latin script, with approximately 267 million native speakers, making it the fifth-most spoken native language globally and the most widely spoken in the Southern Hemisphere. It is the official language of eight sovereign states—Portugal, Brazil, Angola, Mozambique, Cape Verde, Guinea-Bissau, São Tomé and Príncipe, and East Timor—and co-official in Macau and Equatorial Guinea. The language evolved from Vulgar Latin dialects in the medieval Kingdom of Galicia and County of Portugal, retaining Celtic phonological influences, and developed distinct European and Brazilian standard varieties through colonial expansion and internal migration.

## Body

### Origins and Roman Period
Before Roman expansion, the Iberian Peninsula housed diverse peoples including the Gallaeci, Lusitanians, Celts, and Iberians, whose languages left a substratum in Portuguese vocabulary related to nature, agriculture, and material culture. Roman control expanded from 218 BC, completing by 19 BC under Augustus, organizing the territory into provinces like Lusitania and Gallaecia. Latin became the administrative and ecclesiastical language, with Vulgar Latin—the spoken variety of soldiers, settlers, and officials—gradually supplanting indigenous tongues. In Gallaecia, Vulgar Latin developed distinctive features such as the loss of intervocalic *l* and *n* (e.g., *luna* → *lua*) and simplification of geminate consonants, distinguishing it from neighboring Romance varieties.

### Arab Conquest and Old Galician–Portuguese
The 711 Muslim conquest led to Islamic rule over most of the peninsula, while northern Christian kingdoms preserved Romance varieties. Galician–Portuguese emerged as a unified linguistic system from the 9th to 14th centuries, serving as the literary language of lyric poetry across the Iberian Peninsula. The *Cancioneiro da Ajuda* (late 13th century) contains some of the earliest known Galician–Portuguese verses, including the *Cantiga da Ribeirinha* (c. 1189/1198). The earliest document in Old Portuguese is debated: the *Notícia de Fiadores* (1175) is cited by some scholars, though others question its classification, while the Will of Afonso II (1214) is widely accepted as an early fully Portuguese text.

### Divergence of Galician and Portuguese
From the 11th century, Galician and Portuguese began diverging, with Portuguese adopting digraphs like *lh* and *nh* by 1250. King Denis of Portugal (late 13th century) mandated Portuguese for royal administration, replacing Latin in the chancery, interpreted as a strategy to strengthen royal authority. After the 1385 Battle of Aljubarrota, the political center shifted south to Lisbon and Coimbra, marginalizing northern regions. Around 1420 marks the transition to Middle Portuguese, with the term "Portuguese" (*português*) first appearing in a 1430 letter by Peter, Duke of Coimbra. The 16th–17th centuries saw Latinate renewal, reintroducing Latin and Greek vocabulary through Spanish influence, and the publication of early grammars by Fernão de Oliveira (1536) and João de Barros (1540).

### Overseas Expansion and Brazilian Development
Portuguese expansion began in 1415 with the conquest of Ceuta, extending to Madeira, the Azores, Cape Verde, Angola, and Mozambique. The 1500 arrival in Brazil introduced Portuguese to a region with over 1,175 Indigenous languages, primarily Tupi–Guarani. Jesuit missionaries systematized Indigenous languages into grammars, while the transatlantic slave trade brought 200–300 African languages. Gold discoveries in Minas Gerais (1690s) strengthened Portuguese in central Brazil, and the 1757 *Diretório dos Índos* imposed Portuguese as the sole official language. The 1808 transfer of the royal court to Rio de Janeiro reinforced European Portuguese and introduced the printing press. Post-independence (1822), intellectuals debated incorporating Indigenous and popular terms versus adhering to European norms.

### Geographic Distribution and Official Status
Portuguese is spoken by approximately 200 million in South America, 30 million in Africa, 15 million in Europe, 5 million in North America, and 0.33 million in Asia and Oceania. It is the native language of 95% of populations in Portugal, Brazil, and São Tomé and Príncipe. In Lusophone African countries, it functions as a lingua franca among diverse native languages. The CPLP, established in 1996, unites nine countries with Portuguese as an official language, including Equatorial Guinea, which joined in 2014 after adopting Portuguese as its third official language in 2011.

### Linguistic Features
Portuguese preserves more Latin verbal inflections than any other major Romance language. Unique features include the future subjunctive mood, used in dependent clauses denoting future conditions, and the personal infinitive, which inflects by person and number. The present perfect has an iterative sense in Galician–Portuguese, denoting repeated or ongoing actions. Phonologically, Portuguese has nasal vowels, complex consonant variations, and distinct guttural R realizations in European and Brazilian varieties. European Portuguese is stress-timed with vowel reduction, while Brazilian Portuguese is more syllable-timed with better-preserved unstressed vowels.

### Vocabulary and Influences
The Portuguese lexicon contains around 228,500 entries, primarily derived from Latin. Pre-Roman influences include over 3,000 words from Gallaecian and other Celtic languages. Germanic invasions (5th century) contributed approximately 500 words, especially in warfare (*guerra*), nature (*suíno*), and emotions (*orgulho*). Arabic influence (9th–13th centuries) added 400–600 words, often recognizable by the initial *al-* (e.g., *aldeia*, *azeite*). Asian contact (15th century onward) introduced words like *chá* (tea) and *catana* (cutlass). African and Amerindian influences include *batata* (potato) from Taino and *cafune* from Kimbundu. European loans, especially from French and English, are significant in modern usage.

### Dialects and Varieties
Two primary standardized varieties exist: European Portuguese (based on Coimbra and Lisbon dialects) and Brazilian Portuguese. Brazilian dialects include Caipira, Cearense, Baiano, Fluminense, Gaúcho, Mineiro, Nordestino, Nortista, Paulistano, and Carioca, each with distinct phonological and prosodic features. African varieties generally follow the European written standard but display unique phonological and lexical traits. The *você* pronoun dominates formal and colloquial speech in most regions, though *tu* usage varies by region and social context. Portuguese-based creoles, such as Cape Verdean Creole and Papiamento, are independent languages spoken by over three million people.

## Terms
- **Lusophone**: A Portuguese-speaking person or nation.
- **Vulgar Latin**: The spoken variety of Latin used by common people, distinct from Classical Latin used in literature.
- **Galician–Portuguese**: The medieval linguistic continuum from which modern Galician and Portuguese diverged.
- **Mesoclisis**: A clitic insertion between verb stem and ending, preserved in Portuguese (e.g., *dar-te-ei*).
- **Future subjunctive**: A mood expressing hypothetical or uncertain future conditions, developed from medieval West Iberian Romance.
- **Personal infinitive**: An infinitive form that inflects according to person and number.
- **CPLP**: Community of Portuguese Language Countries, established in 1996 to promote cooperation among Lusophone states.
- **Portuñol**: A form of code-switching between Portuguese and Spanish, common in border regions.
- **Nasal vowels**: Vowels pronounced with air flowing through the nose, a distinctive feature of Portuguese phonology.
- **Synthetic pluperfect**: A past perfect tense preserved in Portuguese (e.g., *eu estivera*).

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the identification of the oldest Portuguese document: Ana Maria Martins argues for the *Notícia de Fiadores* (1175), though she acknowledges this is not consensual, while others contend its limited Romance elements are insufficient for classification. The classification of Galician and Portuguese as separate languages versus co-dialects of a single language remains contested, with many linguists viewing them as co-dialects despite political separation. The periodization of Middle Portuguese around 1420, attributed to scholars like Luís Lindley Cintra and Serafim da Silva Neto, reflects ongoing scholarly discussion about linguistic transitions. The extent of Portuguese influence on other languages, particularly in Asia and Africa, continues to be studied, with debates over the degree of creolization and the preservation of Portuguese-based creoles.
