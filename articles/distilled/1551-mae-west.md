# Mae West

## Overview
Mary Jane "Mae" West (1893–1980) was an American entertainer whose seven-decade career spanned vaudeville, Broadway, film, radio, television, and recording. She became a cultural icon by portraying sexually confident, quick-witted characters who deployed double entendres in a signature contralto voice. West wrote, produced, and often directed her own material, turning censorship battles into publicity that fueled her fame. Her 1926 play *Sex* led to a brief jail sentence for "corrupting the morals of youth," establishing her "bad girl" brand. In the early 1930s, she became Paramount Pictures' highest-paid star, saving the studio from bankruptcy with hits like *She Done Him Wrong* (1933) and *I'm No Angel* (1933). The enforcement of the Production Code in 1934 curtailed her film persona, prompting a return to live performance where she retained creative control. She remained a visible pop-culture figure into the 1970s, releasing rock albums and appearing in films like *Sextette* (1978). The American Film Institute ranked her 15th among the greatest female screen legends.

## Body

### Early Life and Vaudeville Roots
West was born in Brooklyn to Mathilde Delker, a German immigrant and fashion model, and John Patrick "Battlin' Jack" West, a former prizefighter and private investigator. The eldest surviving of three children, she began entertaining at church socials by age five and won local talent contests by seven. At 14, she joined the Hal Clarendon Stock Company, performing as "Baby Mae" and later experimenting with male impersonation. Her distinctive walk was influenced by female impersonators Bert Savoy and Julian Eltinge, stars of the "Pansy Craze." She made her Broadway debut in 1911 in *A La Broadway* and married fellow vaudevillian Frank Szatkus (stage name Frank Wallace) the same year, though they never cohabited and divorced decades later.

### Broadway Stardom, Censorship, and Jail
West transitioned to writing her own risqué plays under the pen name Jane Mast. Her 1926 play *Sex*—which she wrote, produced, directed, and starred in—drew strong ticket sales but provoked a police raid following complaints from religious groups. Convicted of "corrupting the morals of youth," West chose a 10-day jail sentence over a fine for the publicity. She served eight days on Welfare Island, famously dining with the warden and claiming she wore silk underwear instead of prison-issue burlap. The media frenzy branded her a "bad girl" and amplified her career. Her follow-up, *The Drag* (1927), addressed homosexuality explicitly; it was banned from Broadway by the New York Society for the Suppression of Vice. West was an early advocate for gay rights and women's liberation, though her 1959 autobiography expressed reservations about homosexuality that contradicted her later "live and let live" stance in *Sex, Health, and ESP* (1975). *Diamond Lil* (1928) became her biggest stage hit, running 323 performances and becoming a signature vehicle she revived for decades.

### Hollywood Peak and the Production Code
Paramount signed West in 1932 at nearly 40 years old. She debuted in *Night After Night*, rewriting her lines to deliver the famous retort, "Goodness had nothing to do with it, dearie." She insisted on casting Cary Grant in *She Done Him Wrong* (1933), an adaptation of *Diamond Lil*, which saved Paramount from bankruptcy and earned a Best Picture nomination. *I'm No Angel* (1933) became her highest-grossing film, making her a ubiquitous cultural presence referenced by Cole Porter, Frida Kahlo, and F. Scott Fitzgerald. By 1935, she was the second-highest-paid person in the U.S., after William Randolph Hearst. The strict enforcement of the Production Code on July 1, 1934, forced heavy edits to her scripts. West countered by inserting deliberately extreme lines to distract censors from her preferred material. Films like *Belle of the Nineties* (1934), *Goin' to Town* (1935), and *Klondike Annie* (1936) suffered from cuts; Hearst even banned advertising for *Klondike Annie* in his papers after a joke about his mistress. *Go West, Young Man* (1936) and *Every Day's a Holiday* (1937) followed, but censorship eroded her screen persona. She declined the role of Belle Watling in *Gone with the Wind* as too small. Her final 1930s film, *My Little Chickadee* (1940) with W.C. Fields, was a hit despite their personal animosity. *The Heat's On* (1943), where she was barred from writing her dialogue, was a critical failure that drove her from filmmaking for 27 years.

### Radio Controversy and Ban
On December 12, 1937, West appeared on Edgar Bergen's *Chase and Sanborn Hour*, flirting with dummy Charlie McCarthy ("all wood and a yard long") and performing an *Adam and Eve* sketch with Don Ameche laden with double entendres ("get me a big one... I feel like doin' a big apple!"). Conservative groups flooded the sponsor, Chase & Sanborn Coffee, with complaints. The FCC deemed the broadcast "vulgar and indecent." NBC banned West and any mention of her name from its airwaves. She did not return to radio until 1950 on *The Chesterfield Supper Club*.

### Stage, Nightclubs, and Television
Post-film, West dominated live entertainment. *Catherine Was Great* (1944), a satire on Catherine the Great produced by Mike Todd, ran 191 performances. She revived *Diamond Lil* on Broadway in 1949 to acclaim. In the 1950s, she headlined a Las Vegas show at the Sahara Hotel, flanked by bodybuilders, quipping, "Men come to see me, but I also give the women something to see: wall to wall men." She declined the role of Norma Desmond in *Sunset Boulevard* (1950), preferring to uplift audiences rather than portray pathos. She also turned down roles in *Pal Joey*, *Roustabout*, and Fellini's *Juliet of the Spirits* and *Satyricon*. Television appearances included a standing ovation at the 1958 Academy Awards, *The Dean Martin Variety Show* (1959), *Mister Ed* (1964), and a 1976 CBS special with Dick Cavett.

### Recording Career
West recorded film songs on 78 rpm records in the early 1930s. Her LP career began with *The Fabulous Mae West* (1955). In the 1960s and 70s, she embraced rock and novelty material: *Way Out West* (1966), *Wild Christmas* (1966), and *Great Balls of Fire* (1972), covering The Doors and working with producer Ian Whitcomb. She released singles as late as 1973.

### Final Film Comeback and Legacy
After 27 years, West returned as Leticia Van Allen in *Myra Breckinridge* (1970), though her role was reduced in editing. In 1971, UCLA students voted her "Woman of the Century." Her final film, *Sextette* (1978), written in 1959, featured an all-star cast (George Raft, Tony Curtis, Timothy Dalton, Ringo Starr, Alice Cooper) and reunited her with costume designer Edith Head. Failing eyesight required her lines to be fed via a speaker in her wig. The film flopped commercially. West received a Hollywood Walk of Fame star and was inducted into the American Theater Hall of Fame.

### Personal Life and Death
West's secret 1911 marriage to Frank Wallace was exposed in 1935; they divorced in 1943. A reported 1914 marriage to accordionist Guido Deiro ended after an abortion at her mother's urging that left her infertile. She had a long professional and romantic partnership with manager James Timony (1916–mid-1930s) and was linked to Cotton Club owner Owney Madden. From her sixties until her death, she lived with Chester Rybinski (Paul Novak), 30 years her junior. She resided at The Ravenswood in Hollywood from 1930 until her death. After a fall and stroke in August 1980, she died on November 22, 1980, at 87. She was entombed in the family mausoleum at Cypress Hills Cemetery, Brooklyn.

## Terms
- ****Vaudeville**** — A theatrical genre of variety entertainment popular in the U.S. from the 1880s to the 1930s, featuring unrelated acts (singers, dancers, comedians) on a single bill.
- ****Double Entendre**** — A figure of speech in which a phrase is devised to be understood in two ways, typically with one meaning being risqué or ironic.
- ****Production Code (Hays Code)**** — A set of industry moral guidelines (enforced 1934–1968) governing the content of U.S. motion pictures, restricting depictions of sex, violence, and crime.
- ****Pansy Craze**** — A cultural phenomenon (1920s–30s) where female impersonators and LGBTQ+ performers gained mainstream visibility in nightclubs and theater.
- ****Contralto**** — The lowest female singing voice type; West used this deep, resonant vocal quality as a signature of her delivery.
- ****Welfare Island**** — The former name (1921–1973) of Roosevelt Island in New York City's East River, then home to hospitals and a penitentiary where West served her sentence.
- ****Box Office Poison**** — A 1938 list published by the Independent Theatre Owners Association labeling stars (including West, Hepburn, Dietrich) whose high salaries did not match ticket sales.
- ****Myra Breckinridge**** — A 1968 satirical novel by Gore Vidal (film 1970) featuring a trans woman protagonist; West played the lecherous talent agent Leticia Van Allen.
- ****Mae West (life vest)**** — WWII Allied slang for yellow inflatable life preservers, named for their resemblance to West's torso and rhyming slang for "breasts."
- ****Sextette**** — West's final film (1978), a self-written vehicle in which she played a woman marrying a man decades younger; produced despite her failing health.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Guido Deiro Marriage**: West's son claimed she married accordionist Guido Deiro in 1914, but no license has been found; the relationship reportedly ended after an abortion that caused infertility.
*   **Contradictory Views on Homosexuality**: West was an early public supporter of gay rights (1920s), yet her 1959 autobiography called homosexuality a "danger to the entire social system," while her 1975 book advocated "live and let live." Scholars debate whether the 1959 text reflects ghostwriter Stephen Longstreet's influence or a genuine shift.
*   **Age Ambiguity**: West actively obscured her age throughout her career; Paramount publicity often listed her birth year as 1900 or later, though the 1893 date is confirmed by census and birth records.
*   **Censorship Strategy**: Historians debate whether West's practice of "baiting" censors with extreme lines was a calculated survival tactic or a genuine expression of her artistic limits.
*   **Authorship of Late Works**: The extent of West's sole authorship versus collaboration (e.g., with Ian Whitcomb on *Great Balls of Fire* or ghostwriters on autobiographies) remains unclear for her 1960s–70s output.
