# Suicide

## Overview  
Suicide is the intentional act of causing one’s own death, representing approximately 1.1% of global deaths annually (about 727,000 in 2021). It is the third-leading cause of death among 15- to 29-year-olds and disproportionately affects low- and middle-income countries, where nearly three-quarters of suicides occur. Rooted in complex interactions between biological, psychological, social, and cultural factors, suicide has been shaped by evolving religious, legal, and philosophical perspectives across history. Prevention strategies include means restriction, mental health treatment, media guidelines, and psychosocial interventions.

## Timeline  
- **c. 452 CE** — Council of Arles condemns suicide as the work of the Devil
- **1670** — Louis XIV enacts harsh penalties for suicide in France
- **1777** — David Hume publishes *Essays on Suicide*, arguing against its criminalization
- **1879** — English law distinguishes suicide from homicide
- **1882** — Daylight burial permitted for suicides in England
- **1961** — England and Wales decriminalize suicide via the Suicide Act
- **1993** — Republic of Ireland decriminalizes suicide
- **2002** — Netherlands legalizes physician-assisted suicide and euthanasia
- **2011** — Centre for Suicide Prevention calls for destigmatizing suicide language
- **2022** — U.S. records highest number of suicides (49,500)

## Body  

### Definitions and Terminology  
Suicide refers to the act of intentionally ending one’s own life. Related terms include *attempted suicide* (non-fatal self-injury with intent), *assisted suicide* (providing means or advice), and *euthanasia* (another person actively ending life). *Suicidal ideation* involves thoughts without action; *suicidality* denotes broader risk. *Murder-suicide* combines homicide and suicide, while *extended suicide* sees victims as extensions of the self. Advocacy groups promote neutral language like “died by suicide” over “committed suicide,” which implies criminality.

### Pathophysiology  
No single cause explains suicide. It arises from interactions among behavioral, socioeconomic, and psychological factors. Low brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and serotonin levels are linked to suicidal behavior, though direct evidence is limited. Epigenetic changes may mediate environmental influences on gene expression related to suicide risk.

### Risk Factors  
Mental disorders—especially depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and borderline personality disorder—are present in up to 90% of cases. Substance misuse, trauma, childhood abuse, and chronic illness also elevate risk. Previous attempts strongly predict future ones. Social isolation, poverty, unemployment, and discrimination contribute significantly. LGBTQ+ individuals face higher rates due to stigma and minority stress. Environmental exposures like air pollution and seasonal variations show associations. Genetics account for 38–55% of suicidal behaviors.

### Prevention  
Effective prevention includes restricting access to lethal means (e.g., firearms, pesticides), treating mental illness and substance abuse, and implementing responsible media reporting. Psychotherapies such as dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) show promise. Crisis hotlines exist widely but lack robust evidence. Screening tools like IS PATH WARM help identify at-risk individuals. Lithium reduces suicide risk in mood disorders by up to 87%. Caring letters—a low-cost intervention—have shown efficacy post-attempt.

### Methods  
Common methods vary globally: hanging, pesticide poisoning, and firearms dominate. In the U.S., firearms account for 50% of suicides. Method choice correlates with availability; reducing access lowers overall rates. Drug overdoses are frequent attempts but less lethal. Jumping and charcoal burning are region-specific.

### Epidemiology  
Globally, suicide rates are highest in males, particularly those over 70. Rates differ sharply by country: South Korea (28.9/100k), India (23.2/100k), and Lithuania top lists. China and India account for over half of all suicides due to population size. Rates rose 60% from the 1960s to 2012, especially in developing regions.

### History and Culture  
Historically, suicide was criminalized in Christian Europe. Ancient Athens denied proper burial to suicides; Rome later criminalized it. The Renaissance saw early defenses of suicide through reason-based ethics. Secularization shifted views toward individual autonomy. Samurai seppuku was culturally honored. Modern attitudes emphasize mental health and prevention.

### Religion and Philosophy  
Most religions oppose suicide. Christianity frames it as sin; Catholic doctrine allows reduced culpability under grave fear or mental illness. Judaism values life but permits martyrdom. Islam considers suicide haram. Hinduism discourages it except via fasting (Prayopavesa). Philosophers debate rationality: Kant condemned it categorically, while Hume defended it conditionally. Contemporary bioethics explores autonomy vs. duty.

### Legislation  
Suicide remains illegal in some nations. Most Western countries have decriminalized it. The Netherlands pioneered legalized euthanasia (2002). Germany prohibits active euthanasia. Australia criminalizes aiding suicide. India decriminalized suicide in 2014. Malaysia still penalizes attempts. North Korea criminalized suicide as treason in 2023.

## Terms  
- **BDNF**: Brain-derived neurotrophic factor, a protein linked to neuronal health and mood regulation.  
- **DBT**: Dialectical Behavior Therapy, a psychotherapy targeting emotion dysregulation.  
- **CBT**: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, focusing on modifying harmful thought patterns.  
- **IS PATH WARM**: Mnemonic for assessing suicide risk (Ideation, Substance abuse, etc.).  
- **Werther Effect**: Media-induced suicide contagion following publicized deaths.  
- **Papageno Effect**: Protective media portrayal encouraging help-seeking.  
- **Suicidality**: Broad term encompassing thoughts, plans, and attempts at suicide.  
- **Means Restriction**: Limiting access to methods used in suicide to reduce fatalities.  
- **Heritability**: Genetic contribution to trait; ~36% for ideation, 17% for attempts.  
- **Lethal Means**: Methods with high case-fatality rates (firearms, hanging).

## Debates and Open Questions  
- Is suicide ever rational? Philosophers and ethicists continue debating whether rational suicide exists outside terminal illness.  
- Should assisted dying expand beyond terminal conditions to include psychiatric suffering?  
- How effective are crisis hotlines? Despite widespread use, empirical support remains weak.  
- Can media coverage be regulated without infringing free speech? Balancing awareness and contagion risk remains contentious.  
- Why do rates rise in developing countries? Economic instability and social fragmentation are hypothesized drivers.  
- What role does religion play in prevention? While protective in many contexts, its influence varies widely.  
- Are current screening practices beneficial or harmful? High false positives raise concerns about resource allocation and labeling effects.
