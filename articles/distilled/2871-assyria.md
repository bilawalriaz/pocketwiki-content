# Assyria

## Overview
Assyria was a major ancient Mesopotamian civilization centered in Upper Mesopotamia (modern northern Iraq). From a city-state, it evolved into a vast empire dominating the ancient Near East, pioneering innovations in warfare, administration, and knowledge preservation. Its history spans the Early to late Iron Age, and its cultural legacy continues with the modern Assyrian people.

- **c. 240 AD** — Sasanian Empire sacks Assur.

## Body

### Origins and Early Period
Assyrian civilization began in northern Mesopotamia. The city of Assur, founded c. 2600 BC, became an independent city-state c. 2025 BC under Puzur-Ashur I. In the Old Assyrian period, it was a major trading hub with a vast network evidenced by cuneiform tablets at Kültepe, Turkey. It experienced foreign rule, notably under Amorite king Shamshi-Adad I (c. 1808–1776 BC), before the rise of the Middle Assyrian Empire.

### The Assyrian Empires
Assyria's rise as a territorial state began with Ashur-uballit I (r. c. 1363–1328 BC), who freed it from Mitanni domination. After a decline during the Late Bronze Age collapse, the Neo-Assyrian Empire emerged under Ashur-dan II (r. 934–912 BC). Through conquest, kings like Ashurnasirpal II, Tiglath-Pileser III, and the Sargonid dynasty built the largest empire yet seen, from Iran to Egypt. Characterized by a powerful military, sophisticated administration, and deportation, it collapsed after Ashurbanipal's death due to civil war and a coalition of Babylonians and Medes, who sacked Assur (614 BC) and Nineveh (612 BC).

### Government and Military
Assyrian kingship evolved from an oligarchic system to an autocracy justified by the divine mandate of the god Ashur. The empire was administered through a provincial system with governors and a sophisticated communication network. The military, initially levies, became a professional standing army in the Neo-Assyrian period, renowned for iron weapons, cavalry, and siege warfare.

### Society and Economy
Old Assyrian society was relatively egalitarian, with women having significant legal rights. Social hierarchy became more pronounced later. The economy shifted from trade-based to state-dominated in the Neo-Assyrian Empire, which controlled agriculture and resources. The state used the *ilku* system (land grants for service) and mass deportations to manage its population.

### Language and Culture
The primary language was Assyrian, a dialect of Akkadian written in cuneiform. From the 9th century BC, Aramaic became the lingua franca. Assyrian art, especially Neo-Assyrian monumental wall reliefs and *lamassu* (human-headed winged bulls/lions), served royal propaganda. The Library of Ashurbanipal preserved vast Mesopotamian literature, including the *Epic of Gilgamesh*.

### Religion
The chief deity was Ashur, the national god who commanded imperial expansion. Assyrian religion was polytheistic, sharing a pantheon with Babylonia. Temples were major economic and religious centers. After the empire's fall, the ancient religion persisted before being gradually replaced by Christianity from the 1st century AD onward.

## Terms
- **Akkadian**: A family of Semitic languages, including Assyrian, written in cuneiform.
- **Cuneiform**: A writing system using wedge-shaped marks on clay tablets.
- **Deportation**: The policy of forcibly relocating conquered populations.
- **Ilku system**: Land grants in exchange for goods and military service.
- **Limmu**: A yearly office-holder used to date documents in the Old Assyrian period.
- **Lamassu**: Protective human-headed winged bull or lion colossi.
- **Middle Assyrian Empire**: The period (c. 1363–912 BC) when Assyria first became a major territorial power.
- **Neo-Assyrian Empire**: The final and largest phase (911–609 BC).
- **Sargonid dynasty**: The last ruling dynasty (722–609 BC), including Sennacherib and Ashurbanipal.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact nature of the Old Assyrian city assembly is unknown.
- The enforcement degree of the Middle Assyrian Laws, especially concerning women, is uncertain.
- The status of *ḫarımtū* women in the Middle Assyrian period is debated; they are now often interpreted as independent women.
- The extent to which Neo-Assyrian kings' annals served as history versus propaganda is discussed.
- The origins of modern Assyrian identity, including the 19th-century re-adoption of the term "Assyrian," involve complex factors.
