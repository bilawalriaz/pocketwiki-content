# Disco

## Overview
Disco is a genre of dance music and a subculture that emerged in the late 1960s from the urban nightlife scene of the United States. It became a dominant force in popular music during the mid-to-late 1970s, characterized by its four-on-the-floor beat, lush orchestration, and association with a vibrant club culture centered on dancing, fashion, and social liberation. Its influence is foundational to many subsequent electronic and dance music genres.

## Timeline
- **Early 1940s** — Parisian nightclubs begin playing jazz records during Nazi occupation, pioneering the discothèque concept.
- **1960** — Le Club, a members-only discothèque, opens in Manhattan, importing the concept to the U.S.
- **Late 1960s** — Disco music develops as a mixture of sounds from African American, Latino, and Italian American communities in NYC, Baltimore, and Philadelphia.
- **1973** — Vince Aletti writes one of the first articles describing disco as a music genre in *Rolling Stone*.
- **1975** — The Hustle dance style becomes dominant; Tom Moulton pioneers the 12-inch single format for longer mixes.
- **1977** — *Saturday Night Fever* is released, contributing to disco's mainstream peak.
- **1979** — Disco Demolition Night in Chicago marks the beginning of a major backlash and decline in U.S. popularity.
- **1980s** — Disco declines in the U.S. but remains popular in Europe and influences new genres like house and hip-hop.
- **1990s** — A resurgence of interest in disco occurs as part of 1970s nostalgia in pop culture.
- **2013** — Daft Punk's "Get Lucky" signals a major revival of disco-influenced pop music.
- **2020** — Disco-influenced hits by Dua Lipa, Lady Gaga, and BTS dominate global charts, marking a major resurgence.

## Body

### Origins and Musical Characteristics
Disco originated as a reaction to the dominance of rock music and the stigmatization of dance music in the 1960s counterculture. Its sound is typified by a steady four-on-the-floor beat, syncopated basslines, and a rich "wall of sound" created by layered instrumentation, including string sections, horns, electric pianos, synthesizers, and "chicken-scratch" rhythm guitars. The genre often incorporated Latin rhythms and harmonies more common in jazz than pop. Early disco records were typically three-minute versions until the development of the 12-inch single allowed for longer mixes tailored for club DJs.

### Club Culture and Production
The "disco sound" was expensive to produce, requiring large bands, orchestras, and sophisticated multitrack recording and mixing techniques. By the late 1970s, major U.S. cities had thriving club scenes centered on nightclubs like Studio 54 in New York, which was notorious for its hedonism. Powerful, bass-heavy sound systems and elaborate lighting were key to the club experience. DJs became central figures, using reel-to-reel tape machines and mixers to create seamless, continuous sets that shaped the dance floor. This culture was accompanied by distinctive fashions, such as leisure suits and platform shoes, and a thriving drug subculture involving cocaine and Quaaludes ("disco biscuits").

### Social Significance and Legacy
Disco was intrinsically linked to the sexual liberation of the 1970s and provided a space for marginalized communities, particularly gay men, to express themselves freely. The music's full-body eroticism and the club environment facilitated a culture of promiscuity and public sex. Disco's legacy is profound, directly influencing the development of electronic dance music, house, hip-hop, new wave, and post-disco. Its DJ-centric model and production techniques laid the groundwork for modern club culture and rave scenes.

## Terms
- **Discothèque**: A nightclub or venue where recorded music is played for dancing, derived from the French word for "library of phonograph records."
- **Four-on-the-floor**: A rhythm pattern where the bass drum hits on every beat (1, 2, 3, 4) in 4/4 time, a hallmark of disco.
- **Syncopation**: The accenting of unexpected beats or off-beats in a rhythm.
- **12-inch single**: A vinyl record format with longer playing time, developed for disco DJs to create extended mixes.
- **Chicken-scratch guitar**: A rhythm guitar technique involving lightly pressing and quickly releasing strings while strumming near the bridge to create a muted, percussive sound.
- **Post-disco**: A genre emerging in the late 1970s and early 1980s that incorporated disco elements with electronic, R&B, and post-punk influences.
- **Nu-disco**: A 21st-century genre reviving 1970s/80s disco, Italo disco, and Euro disco aesthetics.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source material highlights the scholarly debate surrounding disco's social impact, particularly its role in sexual liberation and queer expression. It also notes the genre's complex legacy, including the intense backlash it faced (e.g., Disco Demolition Night) and its subsequent, often cyclical, revivals in mainstream pop music. The precise origins of the genre are described as a mixture from various urban communities, suggesting its development was a collaborative, multicultural evolution rather than a single-point invention.
