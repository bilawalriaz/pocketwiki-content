# Seleucus I Nicator

## Overview
Seleucus I Nicator (c. 358–281 BC) was a Macedonian general and successor of Alexander the Great who founded the Seleucid Empire, one of the major Hellenistic powers. Rising from a secondary role in the Wars of the Diadochi, he eventually controlled most of Alexander's Asian conquests, from Asia Minor to the Indus River, until his assassination in 281 BC.

## Timeline
- **c. 358 BC** — Born in Europus, Macedonia.
- **334 BC** — Accompanies Alexander the Great into Asia.
- **323 BC** — After Alexander's death, supports regent Perdiccas; appointed Commander of the Companions.
- **321 BC** — Becomes Satrap of Babylon after the Partition of Triparadisus.
- **312 BC** — Returns to Babylon with Ptolemy's support, marking the start of the Seleucid era.
- **305–303 BC** — Fights and makes a treaty with Chandragupta Maurya, receiving 500 war elephants.
- **301 BC** — Defeats Antigonus at the Battle of Ipsus with the help of war elephants.
- **306 BC** — Assumes the title of king (basileus).
- **281 BC** — Defeats Lysimachus at the Battle of Corupedium.
- **281 BC** — Assassinated by Ptolemy Ceraunus in Thrace.

## Body

### Early Career and Rise to Power
Seleucus served as an officer under Alexander the Great, commanding the elite Hypaspistai infantry. After Alexander's death in 323 BC, he initially supported the regent Perdiccas. Following Perdiccas's assassination in 321 BC, Seleucus was appointed Satrap of Babylon. He was soon forced to flee by the powerful Diadochus Antigonus but returned in 312 BC with Ptolemy's aid, an event later considered the founding of the Seleucid Empire.

### Expansion and the Seleucid Empire
From 312 BC, Seleucus ruthlessly expanded eastward, conquering Media, Susiana, and other provinces to control the entire eastern portion of Alexander's empire. His ambition in the east led to conflict with the Mauryan Empire under Chandragupta Maurya. A treaty (c. 303 BC) resolved this, with Seleucus ceding eastern satrapies in exchange for 500 war elephants and a marriage alliance. These elephants proved decisive at the Battle of Ipsus (301 BC), where Seleucus and his allies defeated and killed Antigonus, securing Seleucid control over Syria and the Levant.

### Later Reign and Death
Seleucus became the sole surviving companion of Alexander after defeating Lysimachus at Corupedium in 281 BC. He founded numerous cities, including Antioch (300 BC) and Seleucia on the Tigris (c. 305 BC), which became his new capital and eventually depopulated Babylon. While attempting to seize Lysimachus's European territories, Seleucus was assassinated by Ptolemy Ceraunus in 281 BC. He was succeeded by his son, Antiochus I.

## Terms
- **Diadochi**: The "successors," the rival generals who fought for control of Alexander the Great's empire after his death.
- **Hypaspistai**: An elite Macedonian infantry unit, also known as the "Shield-bearers" or later "Silvershields."
- **Satrap**: A provincial governor in the Persian and Hellenistic empires.
- **Basileus**: The Greek title for "king," assumed by the Diadochi after 306 BC.
- **Seleucid Era**: A calendar system beginning in 312 BC, marking Seleucus's return to Babylon.
- **War Elephants**: Large military assets used in battle, crucial at Ipsus; Seleucus obtained 500 from Chandragupta Maurya.
- **Epigamia**: A treaty or agreement recognizing the right of intermarriage between two peoples or royal houses.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Birth Year**: Sources conflict: Justin (358 BC), Appian (354 BC), and Eusebius (356 BC). The latter may be propaganda to equate him with Alexander.
- **Extent of Mauryan Cession**: The exact territories Seleucus ceded to Chandragupta are debated; sources like Strabo suggest areas along the Indus, but the inclusion of regions like Aria and Gedrosia is questioned by modern scholars.
- **Nature of the Mauryan Conflict**: Details of the war are scarce, and it is unclear if a major pitched battle occurred or if the conflict was brief.
- **Prophecy of Antiochus**: A story claims Chaldean astrologers predicted Seleucus would rule Asia, prompting Antigonus to pursue him; its historicity is doubted by scholars.
