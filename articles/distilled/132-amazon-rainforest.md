# Amazon rainforest

## Overview

The Amazon rainforest (also called Amazonia) is the largest and most biodiverse tropical rainforest on Earth, covering most of the Amazon basin of South America. It spans 7 million km² across nine nations, contains an estimated 390 billion trees in about 16,000 species, and is home to over 30 million people including 350 ethnic groups. The forest plays a critical global role in carbon storage, oxygen turnover, and climate regulation, but faces severe threats from deforestation, fires, and climate change that could push it toward an irreversible "tipping point" into savanna.

## Timeline

- **~11,200 years ago** — First human settlement in the Amazon region, per excavation at Caverna da Pedra Pintada
- **66–34 Mya** — Rainforest spread across the continent after the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event
- **56–33.9 Mya** — Rainforest likely formed during the Eocene era
- **AD 1–1250** — Geoglyphs created on deforested land, evidence of Pre-Columbian civilizations
- **AD 1250** — Late-prehistoric settlements along forest periphery induced forest cover alterations
- **1542** — Francisco de Orellana became first European to travel the length of the Amazon River
- **~1900** — Native indigenous population had fallen to 1 million (from ~5–8 million in 1500)
- **1970s** — Trans-Amazonian highway construction began; geoglyphs first discovered by Ondemar Dias in 1977
- **2005** — Worst drought in one hundred years hit the Amazon basin
- **2019** — Record fires; Brazil's environmental protections slashed under President Bolsonaro
- **2023** — Deforestation in Brazilian Amazon decreased ~50% under Lula's conservation policies

## Body

### Etymology and Early History

The name "Amazon" arises from a war Francisco de Orellana fought with the Tapuyas and other tribes, whose women fought alongside men. Orellana derived "Amazonas" from the Amazons of Greek mythology described by Herodotus and Diodorus.

For a long time, scholars believed the Amazon was never densely populated, as poor soil supposedly couldn't sustain agriculture. Archeologist Betty Meggers argued a maximum population density of 0.2 inhabitants per km² through hunting. However, recent findings suggest the region was densely populated. Some 5 million people may have lived there in AD 1500; one estimate suggests over 8 million in 1492. By 1900 the indigenous population had fallen to 1 million, and by the early 1980s to less than 200,000.

### Pre-Columbian Civilization

The BBC's *Unnatural Histories* presents evidence that Orellana's observations of a complex civilization in the 1540s were accurate, not exaggerated. Pre-Columbian agriculture was advanced enough to support populous societies, which were later devastated by European diseases like smallpox. British explorer Percy Fawcett investigated this civilization in the early 20th century; his expeditions were inconclusive, and he disappeared on his last trip, naming the lost civilization the "City of Z."

Since the 1970s, numerous geoglyphs dating between AD 1–1250 have been discovered, furthering claims about Pre-Columbian civilizations. Terra preta (fertile dark soil) is now widely accepted as a product of indigenous soil management, allowing agriculture in previously hostile environments. This means large portions of the rainforest are probably the result of centuries of human management. In 2003, Michael Heckenberger found remains of large settlements in the Xingu region, including roads, bridges, and plazas.

### Indigenous Peoples and Conflict

Several tribes practiced warfare. The Jivaroan group (including the Shuar) practised headhunting and headshrinking. More than a third of Yanomamo males died from warfare on average. The Munduruku were a warlike tribe that expanded along the Tapajós river, pacified by Brazilians in the early 19th century. In contrast, the Xingu were described as a "peaceful" society. In the early 20th century, thirty tribes were listed as "peaceful" and eighty-three as "warlike." During the Amazon rubber boom, diseases brought by immigrants (typhus, malaria) killed 40,000 native Amazonians. In 1961, British explorer Richard Mason was killed by the uncontacted Panará tribe.

### Geography and Natural History

Nine countries share the Amazon basin: Brazil (58.4%), Peru (12.8%), Bolivia (7.7%), Colombia (7.1%), Venezuela (6.1%), Guyana (3.1%), Suriname (2.5%), French Guiana (1.4%), and Ecuador (1%).

The rainforest likely formed during the Eocene era (56–33.9 million years ago), following a global reduction of tropical temperatures when the Atlantic widened enough to provide a warm, moist climate. It has existed for at least 55 million years. During the mid-Eocene, the drainage basin was split by the Purus Arch—water flowed east to the Atlantic and west to the Pacific. As the Andes rose, a lake formed (the Solimões Basin); within the last 5–10 million years, accumulating water broke through the arch, joining the easterly flow.

More than 56% of the dust fertilizing the Amazon comes from the Bodélé depression in Northern Chad, blown by wind across the Atlantic. This dust contains phosphorus, essential for plant growth, replacing what rains wash away yearly. NASA's CALIPSO satellite measured an average of 182 million tons of dust blown from the Sahara annually, 15% of which falls over the Amazon basin (22 million tons of phosphorus).

### Biodiversity

Wet tropical forests are the most species-rich biome, and the Amazon has unparalleled biodiversity—one in ten known species lives there. The region hosts about 2.5 million insect species, at least 40,000 plant species, 2,200 fishes, 1,294 birds, 427 mammals, 428 amphibians, and 378 reptiles. One in five of all bird and fish species are found there. A 2001 study found a quarter square kilometer of Ecuadorian rainforest supports more than 1,100 tree species. Each hectare contains around 1 billion invertebrates.

Hazardous species include the black caiman, jaguar, cougar, anaconda, electric eels, piranha, and poison dart frogs. Vampire bats can spread rabies; malaria, yellow fever, and dengue fever can be contracted. Over 90% of Amazonian plant and vertebrate species (13,000–14,000 total) may have been impacted by fires.

### Deforestation and Threats

Deforestation is the conversion of forested areas to non-forested areas, mainly from human settlement and land development. In 2022, about 20% of the rainforest was already deforested, with a further 6% "highly degraded." Research suggests that upon reaching 20–25% deforestation, a tipping point could flip it into degraded savannah in eastern, southern, and central Amazonia.

Prior to the 1960s, the forest remained basically intact. Farms established then used slash-and-burn methods, but poor soil fertility forced farmers to constantly clear new land. The Trans-Amazonian highway (1970s) remains incomplete, limiting damage. Between 1991 and 2000, forest loss rose from 415,000 to 587,000 km², with most becoming cattle pasture. Seventy percent of formerly forested land and 91% of land deforested since 1970 is livestock pasture. Research shows more logging reduces precipitation and lowers yields per hectare—there has been no economic advantage from converting forest to pasture.

Deforestation rose 18% from 2000–2005 versus the previous five years. Though it declined between 2004 and 2014, it has since increased. Under President Bolsonaro, protections were slashed; deforestation rose more than 50% in early 2020 and reached a 15-year high in 2021. Oil drilling (affecting 9.4% of the territory) and mining (17%) are major drivers. The EU–Mercosur free trade agreement has been denounced for potentially expanding market access to Brazilian beef.

### Fires and Droughts

In 2019, there were 72,843 fires in Brazil, more than half in the Amazon. Deforestation rose more than 88% in June 2019 versus 2018. In 2020, a 17% rise marked the worst start to the fire season in a decade; Bolsonaro called the fires a "lie" despite his own government's data.

The 2005 drought was the worst in one hundred years; the Woods Hole Research Center found the forest could survive only three years of drought. The 2010 drought affected approximately 3,000,000 km² (versus 1,900,000 km² in 2005). In a typical year the Amazon absorbs 1.5 gigatons of carbon dioxide; in 2005 it released 5 gigatons, and in 2010, 8 gigatons. A 2020 study found about 40% of the forest is at risk of becoming savanna-like due to reduced rainfall. Research from 2025 suggests even temporarily passing 1.5°C of global warming would trigger significant risk of dieback.

### Climate Change and Conservation

Amazonian evergreen forests account for about 10% of the world's terrestrial primary productivity and 10% of carbon stores—about 1.1 × 10¹¹ metric tonnes of carbon. In 2021, the Amazon emitted more greenhouse gases than it absorbed for the first time. A 2009 study found a 4°C rise would kill 85% of the rainforest; a 3°C rise would kill about 75%.

In 1989, C.M. Peters calculated one hectare in the Peruvian Amazon is worth $6,820 if sustainably harvested, $1,000 if clear-cut for timber, or $148 as cattle pasture. From 2002–2006, conserved land almost tripled and deforestation dropped up to 60%. In 2023, under President Lula, deforestation decreased around 50% compared to 2022, despite a severe drought. The World Bank's 2023 report stated economic losses from deforestation could reach $317 billion per year—about 7 times the cost of all commodities produced through deforestation—proposing a non-deforestation-based economic program.

Remote sensing is dramatically improving conservation knowledge. Indigenous tribes use handheld GPS and Google Earth to map ancestral lands. Silvopasture (integrating trees, forage, and grazing) and ecotourism can help stop deforestation.

## Terms

- **Terra preta**: Fertile dark soil produced by indigenous soil management, enabling agriculture in otherwise poor Amazon soils
- **Slash-and-burn**: Farming method involving cutting and burning forest to clear land for crops
- **Tipping point**: Threshold (~20–25% deforestation) beyond which the rainforest may irreversibly flip to savanna
- **Savanisation**: Process by which rainforest degrades into savanna ecosystem
- **Geoglyphs**: Large ground drawings dating AD 1–1250, evidence of Pre-Columbian civilizations
- **Silvopasture**: Land management integrating trees, forage, and grazing to reduce deforestation
- **Ecotourism**: Low-impact tourism that can provide income while supporting conservation
- **Agro-suicide**: Term for deforestation's severe harm to agriculture through disrupted rainfall and increased temperatures

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Population density**: Whether the Amazon was sparsely populated (Meggers) or densely populated (recent findings) pre-Columbus remains debated, with estimates ranging from 5 to 8+ million people in 1500.
- **LGM forest extent**: Scientists debate whether the rainforest at the last glacial maximum was reduced to small, isolated refugia or remained largely intact but less extensive. The debate is hard to resolve because data sampling is biased away from the basin's center.
- **Climate model uncertainty**: Simulations of Amazon basin climate change vary widely—from weak increases to strong decreases in rainfall—making future projections uncertain.
- **Orellana's claims**: Whether his 1540s observations of complex civilization were accurate (supported by *Unnatural Histories*) or exaggerated (previously believed).
