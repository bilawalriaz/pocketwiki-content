# Biotechnology

## Overview
Biotechnology is the application of biological systems, organisms, or processes to develop products and services, integrating natural and engineering sciences. It spans ancient practices like fermentation and selective breeding to modern genetic engineering and synthetic biology, impacting medicine, agriculture, industry, and environmental management. The field balances innovation with ethical, regulatory, and societal challenges.

## Timeline
- **Prehistory** — Plant cultivation and animal domestication begin
- **c. 3000–500 BCE** — Fermentation used in Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, India
- **1857** — Louis Pasteur explains fermentation
- **1917** — Chaim Weizmann uses pure culture for acetone production
- **1919** — Term "biotechnology" coined by Károly Ereky
- **1928** — Alexander Fleming discovers penicillin
- **1971** — Paul Berg performs first successful gene splicing
- **1972** — Boyer and Cohen transfer genes into bacteria
- **1980** — U.S. Supreme Court allows patenting of genetically modified organisms (Diamond v. Chakrabarty)
- **1988** — NIH establishes Biotechnology Training Programs

## Body

### Definitions and Scope
Biotechnology integrates natural sciences and engineering to use organisms or their components for products and services. The American Chemical Society defines it as applying biological organisms or systems to improve materials and organisms like pharmaceuticals, crops, and livestock. Bioengineering focuses more on systems-level approaches for interfacing with living things, while biomedical engineering overlaps with biotechnology in areas like tissue engineering and genetic engineering.

### Historical Foundations
Early agriculture and fermentation represent the earliest biotechnological practices. Farmers selected high-yield crops during the Neolithic Revolution, inadvertently altering plant genetics through breeding and environmental exposure. Fermentation in ancient civilizations produced beer, wine, bread, and preserved foods like soy sauce. Selective breeding, refined by Darwin’s theory of natural selection, improved livestock and crops over millennia.

### Modern Biotechnology Emergence
The field advanced with microbiology in the early 20th century. Weizmann’s industrial use of pure cultures (1917) and Fleming’s penicillin discovery (1928) laid groundwork for pharmaceutical production. Gene splicing by Berg (1971) and Boyer/Cohen (1972) initiated genetic engineering. The 1980 Supreme Court ruling in *Diamond v. Chakrabarty* enabled patenting of genetically modified organisms, catalyzing commercial biotech.

### Techniques and Tools
Genetic engineering modifies organisms by inserting genes across species. Tissue culture grows cells in vitro for research and medicine. Fermentation produces alcohols, acids, and pharmaceuticals. Biosensors, including BioFETs like ISFET (1970), detect biological and chemical signals. CRISPR and synthetic biology enable precise genome editing and metabolic pathway design for industrial production.

### Applications by Sector
**Medicine**: Includes pharmacogenomics, genetic testing, biopharmaceuticals (e.g., synthetic insulin), gene therapy, and personalized medicine. Oncology leads pharmaceutical biotech value (2021).  
**Agriculture**: Genetically modified crops (GM) resist pests, herbicides, and stress; enhance nutrition (e.g., Golden Rice). Adoption grew from 17,000 km² (1996) to 1.6 million km² (2011).  
**Industry**: Industrial (white) biotechnology uses microbes and enzymes for chemicals, biofuels, and materials. Synthetic biology engineers microbes like *E. coli* for paclitaxel and 1,4-butanediol production.  
**Environment**: Bioremediation cleans contaminated sites; CityTrees filter urban air. Risks include gene flow from GMOs to wild strains.

### Regulation and Ethics
GMO regulation varies globally, especially between the U.S. and EU. The EU maintains separate approvals for cultivation and import. The EUginius database tracks GMO presence and detection. Ethical concerns include genetic modification, intellectual property, and public perception versus scientific consensus on GM safety.

## Terms
- **Genetic Engineering**: Direct modification of an organism’s genome using recombinant DNA technology.
- **Fermentation**: Metabolic process converting organic substrates into alcohols, acids, or gases via microorganisms.
- **BioFET**: Biosensor using a field-effect transistor to detect biological or chemical analytes.
- **Pharmacogenomics**: Study of how genes affect individual responses to drugs.
- **GM Crops**: Plants with altered DNA via genetic engineering for traits like pest resistance.
- **Synthetic Biology**: Engineering discipline designing and constructing novel biological devices or systems.
- **Bioremediation**: Use of organisms to detoxify or remove environmental pollutants.
- **Metabolic Engineering**: Modifying cellular metabolism to enhance production of specific compounds.
- **Bioleaching**: Use of microorganisms to extract metals from ores.
- **Personalized Medicine**: Tailoring medical treatment to individual genetic profiles.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Whether GM crops are more environmentally sustainable than conventional agriculture.
- Public trust in GM food safety versus scientific consensus affirming no greater risk than conventional foods.
- Regulatory harmonization across countries, particularly U.S.-EU differences in GMO oversight.
- Coexistence of GM and non-GM crops and its impact on farming incentives.
- Ethical implications of patenting life forms and genetic modifications.
- Long-term ecological risks of gene flow from transgenic organisms to wild populations.
