# John Constable

## Overview
John Constable (1776–1837) was an English Romantic landscape painter who revolutionized the genre by insisting on painting directly from nature (*en plein air*) rather than composing idealized studio scenes. Born into a wealthy Suffolk milling family, he rejected the family business to pursue art, enduring decades of professional neglect in England where landscape was considered a "minor" genre. His breakthrough came with the monumental "six-footers" (1819–1825)—large-scale Stour Valley scenes like *The Hay Wain*—which won a gold medal at the 1824 Paris Salon and profoundly influenced French Romantics (Géricault, Delacroix) and the Barbizon School. Elected to the Royal Academy at 52, he spent his later years lecturing on landscape as a scientific discipline while grieving his wife Maria’s death from tuberculosis. His oil sketches and cloud studies, painted with broken brushwork to capture fleeting light, are now recognized as proto-Impressionist masterpieces.

## Timeline
- **11 June 1776** — Born in East Bergholt, Suffolk, to Golding (corn merchant/mill owner) and Ann Constable.
- **1799** — Enters Royal Academy Schools after persuading father to allow art career; studies old masters (Claude, Gainsborough, Rubens, Ruisdael).
- **1802** — Refuses drawing master post at Great Marlow Military College; writes letter declaring intent to be a "natural painter" rejecting *bravura*.
- **1806** — Tours Lake District; finds mountains oppressive, preferring landscapes with "human associations" (villages, churches).
- **Oct 1816** — Marries Maria Elizabeth Bicknell at St Martin-in-the-Fields after family opposition; honeymoon on south coast inspires new colour/brushwork.
- **1817** — Exhibits first "six-footer" *Flatford Mill*; praised but unsold.
- **1819** — Sells first major canvas *The White Horse* (100 guineas to John Fisher); elected Associate of Royal Academy; begins "six-footer" series.
- **1821** — Exhibits *The Hay Wain* at Royal Academy; viewed by Géricault and Nodier, sparking French interest.
- **1824** — *The Hay Wain* and *View on the Stour* exhibited at Paris Salon; *Hay Wain* wins gold medal from Charles X; Delacroix repaints *Massacre de Scio* background after seeing them.
- **1824–28** — Family lives in Brighton for Maria’s tuberculosis; Constable paints coastal scenes (*Chain Pier*), finds Brighton "Piccadilly by the seaside."
- **23 Nov 1828** — Maria dies aged 41; Constable wears black, suffers melancholy, raises seven children alone.
- **Feb 1829** — Elected full Royal Academician (age 52).
- **1831** — Appointed Visitor at Royal Academy; exhibits *Salisbury Cathedral from the Meadows*.
- **1836** — Delivers lectures at Royal Institution: painting is "scientific as well as poetic"; imagination cannot rival reality; no great painter self-taught.
- **31 Mar 1837** — Dies of apparent heart failure; buried with Maria at St John-at-Hampstead.

## Body

### Early Life and Artistic Formation
Constable was born in East Bergholt on the River Stour, the second son of a wealthy corn merchant who owned Flatford and Dedham Mills. Expected to inherit the business (his elder brother was intellectually disabled), he worked in the corn trade but sketched the Suffolk/Essex countryside obsessively—"the sound of water escaping from mill dams... willows, old rotten planks... I love such things." Collector George Beaumont showed him Claude Lorrain’s *Hagar and the Angel*, a pivotal inspiration. In 1799 his father granted a small allowance for Royal Academy Schools, where he copied old masters (Gainsborough, Rubens, Ruisdael, Carracci) and read poetry/sermons, becoming notably articulate. In 1802 he refused a secure drawing-master post, writing to John Dunthorne: "The great vice of the present day is *bravura*, an attempt to do something beyond the truth." His early style already showed freshness of light and Claudean composition, but his ordinary rural subjects were unfashionable against the era’s taste for wild, ruinous Romantic landscapes.

### Marriage, the "Six-Footers," and French Triumph
From 1809 Constable courted Maria Bicknell, granddaughter of East Bergholt’s rector Dr. Rhudde, who deemed the Constables social inferiors and threatened disinheritance. After both fathers died (Constable inheriting a fifth share of the family business), they married in October 1816. The south-coast honeymoon (Weymouth, Brighton) triggered brighter colour and vivacious brushwork (*Osmington Bay*). To earn money he painted portraits (which he found dull) and country houses (*Wivenhoe Park*, 1816). In 1817 he began *Flatford Mill*, his largest outdoor canvas yet, aiming to command attention at the Royal Academy and rival classical landscape scale. Though unsold, its intricate execution won praise. The 1819 sale of *The White Horse* (100 guineas to friend John Fisher) brought financial relief, Associate status, and launched the six "six-footers" (c. 6 ft wide): *Stratford Mill* (1820), *The Hay Wain* (1821), *View on the Stour near Dedham* (1822), *The Lock* (1824), *The Leaping Horse* (1825). Their size forced visibility in crowded exhibitions; critics finally compared him with Turner. *The Hay Wain* (1821) failed to sell in London but stunned Géricault and Nodier at the Academy. Dealer John Arrowsmith bought it and *View on the Stour* (£250 total) for the 1824 Paris Salon, where they caused a sensation—*Hay Wain* won a gold medal from Charles X. Delacroix wrote Constable’s greens "can be applied to every tone" and repainted his *Massacre de Scio* background after seeing them.

### Later Years: Grief, Science, and Legacy
Maria’s tuberculosis drove the family to Brighton (1824–28) for sea air; Constable found the resort vulgar ("Piccadilly by the seaside") but painted *Chain Pier, Brighton* (1827). He sold only ~20 paintings in England but >20 in France in a few years, yet refused invitations abroad: "I would rather be a poor man [in England] than a rich man abroad." A 1825 quarrel with Arrowsmith lost his French outlet. Maria died in November 1828 (aged 41); Constable wore black thereafter, prey to "melancholy and anxious thoughts," raising seven children alone. He speculated her £20,000 inheritance disastrously on a mezzotint publication with engraver David Lucas (40 prints, one with 13 proof stages), which failed financially. His late style grew broken and expressive: *Hadleigh Castle* (1829) and *Salisbury Cathedral from the Meadows* (1831) reflect inner turmoil. Elected full Academician (1829), appointed Visitor (1831), he lectured on landscape history, arguing (1836) that painting "is scientific as well as poetic," imagination cannot match reality, and "no great painter was ever self taught." He opposed Gothic Revival as mere "imitation." His final lecture (1835) was "cheered most heartily." He died 31 March 1837, buried beside Maria at Hampstead.

### Working Method and Technical Innovations
Constable "quietly rebelled" against academic imagination-based composition: "When I sit down to make a sketch from nature, the first thing I try to do is to forget that I have ever seen a picture." He painted *en plein air* constantly, never satisfied with formulas: "no two days are alike... neither were there ever two leaves of a tree alike." He produced full-scale oil sketches for major compositions—revolutionary at the time (preceded only by Pierre-Henri de Valenciennes’ Roman oil sketches c. 1780). These sketches (*The Leaping Horse*, *The Hay Wain*) show vigour and expressiveness absent from finished works, marking him as avant-garde. To capture light/movement he used broken brushstrokes, scumbled over lighter passages for sparkling effects. *Seascape Study with Rain Cloud* (c. 1824) uses slashing dark strokes for an exploding cumulus shower. He studied rainbow effects (*Salisbury Cathedral from the Meadows*, 1831) and made countless cloud/sky studies annotated with weather, light direction, time—believing the sky "the key note, the standard of scale, and the chief organ of sentiment." He owned and annotated Thomas Forster’s *Researches About Atmospheric Phaenomena*, engaging with meteorologist Luke Howard’s cloud classification. His watercolours were equally free; *Stonehenge* (1835) with its double rainbow is considered among the greatest ever painted. Constable’s honest techniques inspired Géricault, Delacroix, the Barbizon School, and French Impressionists.

## Terms
- **en plein air** – French for "in the open air"; painting outdoors directly from nature rather than in a studio.
- **bravura** – A display of technical skill and flamboyance in painting; Constable used the term pejoratively for art that prioritizes showiness over truth to nature.
- **six-footers** – Constable’s series of six monumental Stour Valley landscapes (c. 1819–1825), each approximately six feet wide, painted to command attention at Royal Academy exhibitions.
- **oil sketch** – A preparatory painting in oils, often full-scale, executed rapidly to test composition and capture fleeting natural effects; Constable’s were revolutionary for being done outdoors.
- **scumbling** – A technique of dragging a thin, opaque layer of paint over a dried lighter passage to create a broken, luminous texture.
- **Barbizon School** – Mid-19th-century French landscape painters (e.g., Millet, Rousseau) who worked in the Forest of Fontainebleau, directly inspired by Constable’s naturalism.
- **Luke Howard (1772–1864)** – British meteorologist who classified clouds (cirrus, cumulus, stratus, etc.); Constable studied his work and annotated his own copy of Forster’s *Researches About Atmospheric Phaenomena*.
- **mezzotint** – A printmaking technique using a roughened copper plate to produce rich tonal gradations; Constable collaborated with David Lucas on 40 mezzotints after his landscapes.
- **Royal Academy of Arts (RA)** – London institution founded 1768; Constable entered its Schools in 1799, became Associate (1819), full Academician (1829), and Visitor (1831).
- **Paris Salon** – Official annual art exhibition of the Académie des Beaux-Arts; Constable’s 1824 triumph there catalyzed French Romantic and Realist landscape painting.

## Debates and open questions
- **Relative influence of nature vs. social connections**: Constable attributed his gift to "all that lay on the Stour river," but biographer Anthony Bailey argues his development depended on well-to-do relative Thomas Allen’s London contacts.
- **Finished paintings vs. oil sketches**: Scholars debate whether Constable’s revolutionary status rests on his polished exhibition pieces or the freer, more expressive full-scale oil sketches that anticipate Impressionism.
- **Commercial failure in England vs. French acclaim**: The stark contrast—~20 sales in England vs. >20 in France in a few years—raises questions about national taste, the Academy’s hierarchy of genres (landscape as "minor"), and the role of scale ("six-footers") in forcing critical attention.
- **Late style as decline or breakthrough**: The "broken and accented" brushwork of *Hadleigh Castle* and *Salisbury Cathedral from the Meadows* is read either as evidence of grief-induced deterioration or as a deliberate expressive advance toward modernism.
- **Scientific vs. poetic landscape**: Constable’s 1836 lectures framed painting as "scientific as well as poetic" and denied imagination’s creative parity with nature—a position at odds with mainstream Romantic theory and later modernist abstraction.
