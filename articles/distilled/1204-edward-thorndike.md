# Edward Thorndike

## Overview
Edward Lee Thorndike (1874–1949) was an American psychologist whose experimental work on animal learning established the law of effect, a foundational principle linking behavior to its consequences. His research, conducted primarily at Teachers College, Columbia University, bridged comparative psychology and educational practice, shaping both behaviorism and modern educational psychology.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Thorndike was born into a Methodist minister’s family in Massachusetts. He studied at Wesleyan University (B.S., 1895) and Harvard University (M.A., 1897), where he worked with William James on animal learning. He completed his PhD at Columbia University in 1898 under James McKeen Cattell. His dissertation, *Animal Intelligence*, pioneered the use of nonhuman subjects in psychological research and laid the groundwork for comparative psychology.

### Connectionism and the Puzzle Box Experiments
To test whether animals learned through insight or trial and error, Thorndike designed puzzle boxes—wooden enclosures with mechanical latches that cats could open only by pressing a lever or stepping on a button. He recorded escape times across repeated trials, producing learning curves that showed gradual improvement rather than sudden insight. Cats initially wandered aimlessly but gradually pressed the correct lever faster with each trial. Thorndike concluded that learning occurred through trial and error, not insight, and formulated the **law of effect**: behaviors followed by satisfying outcomes become more likely, while those followed by annoying outcomes become less likely.

### Adult Learning and Educational Psychology
During World War I, Thorndike helped develop the Army Beta test to assess illiterate and non-English-speaking recruits. He believed instruction should aim for socially useful goals and that learning ability declines only after age 35. He identified three forms of intelligence: abstract, mechanical, and social. His theories emphasized incremental, automatic learning and the role of reinforcement and punishment in shaping behavior.

### Laws of Learning
Thorndike proposed several principles:
- **Law of Effect**: Satisfying consequences strengthen associations; annoying ones weaken them.
- **Law of Exercise**: Use strengthens, disuse weakens.
- **Law of Readiness**: Responses vary in readiness to act.
- **Identical Elements Theory of Transfer**: Learning transfers when situations share elements.
- **Law of Recency**: Most recent responses are most likely to recur.
- **Multiple Response**: Problem solving through trial and error.
- **Set or Attitude**: Animals are predisposed to act in specific ways.
- **Prepotency of Elements**: Subjects filter out irrelevant aspects of a problem.
- **Response by Analogy**: Responses from similar contexts may be used in new contexts.
- **Associative Shifting**: Responses shift from one stimulus to another.
- **Identifiability**: Situations are recognized and connected through identification.
- **Availability**: Ease with which a specific response can be made.

In 1929, he revised his views, abandoning the law of exercise completely and rejecting punishment as ineffective, emphasizing instead that consequences drive learning.

### Eugenics and Social Views
Thorndike supported eugenics, arguing that selective breeding could improve human capacity for learning, sanity, and morality. He believed improving human nature was as important as improving environment. He also held biological views on gender differences, asserting innate distinctions between male and female behavior, though he acknowledged environmental influences.

### Influence and Criticism
Thorndike’s work influenced B.F. Skinner and Clark Hull, and his law of effect became central to behaviorism. He advanced psychometrics, educational testing, and industrial psychology. However, his connectionism was criticized as overly simplistic, and his eugenic and gender views drew condemnation. In 2020, Teachers College removed his name from Thorndike Hall due to his “racist, sexist, and antisemitic ideals.”

## Terms
- **Law of Effect**: Behavior followed by satisfaction is strengthened; behavior followed by discomfort is weakened.
- **Puzzle Box**: Enclosure requiring a specific action to escape, used to study animal learning.
- **Learning Curve**: Graph showing performance improvement over trials.
- **Trial and Error**: Learning method involving repeated attempts until success.
- **Reinforcement**: Consequence increasing the likelihood of a behavior.
- **Transfer of Learning**: Extent to which prior learning affects new learning.
- **Connectionism**: Theory that learning involves forming associations between stimuli and responses.
- **Identical Elements Theory**: Transfer depends on shared elements between situations.
- **Readiness**: Degree to which a response is prepared to occur.
- **Availability**: Ease with which a response can be made.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the extent to which Thorndike’s law of effect anticipated later behaviorist principles versus his rejection of strict stimulus-response models. His puzzle box methodology has been criticized for anthropomorphic interpretations and limited ecological validity. His eugenic stance remains controversial, raising questions about separating scientific contributions from personal ideology. The role of punishment in learning continues to evolve, with Thorndike’s later rejection of its efficacy aligning with modern behavior analysis.
