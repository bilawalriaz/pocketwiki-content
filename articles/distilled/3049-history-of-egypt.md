# History of Egypt

## Overview
Egypt is one of the world's oldest continuous civilizations, unified around 3150 BC. Its history encompasses native pharaonic rule, conquest by Persians, Greeks, Romans, Arabs, and Ottomans, and modern independence, revealing a distinct culture, the impact of foreign rule, and the path to its current republican government.

## Timeline
- **c. 3150 BC** — Unification of Upper and Lower Egypt under King Narmer (or Menes).
- **c. 2700–2200 BC** — Old Kingdom period, marked by pyramid construction.
- **c. 1650 BC** — Hyksos conquer Lower Egypt.
- **c. 1550–1070 BC** — New Kingdom, Egypt's imperial age under pharaohs like Ramesses II.
- **525 BC** — Persian Achaemenid Empire conquers Egypt.
- **332 BC** — Alexander the Great conquers Egypt, beginning the Ptolemaic dynasty.
- **30 BC** — Roman annexation after the death of Cleopatra VII.
- **639–642 AD** — Arab Islamic conquest from the Byzantine Empire.
- **1517** — Ottoman Turks conquer Egypt.
- **1882** — British forces establish a protectorate.
- **1952** — Egyptian Revolution led by the Free Officers Movement.
- **1953** — Egyptian Republic declared.
- **2011** — Revolution ousts President Hosni Mubarak.
- **2013** — Military, led by Abdel Fattah el-Sisi, removes President Mohamed Morsi.

## Body

### Predynastic and Dynastic Foundations
Before 3150 BC, the Nile Valley transitioned from hunter-gatherers to settled agricultural communities. Distinct cultures in Upper and Lower Egypt, like the Badari and Naqada, preceded pharaonic civilization. Early hieroglyphic writing appeared c. 3200 BC. Unification under Narmer (or Menes) c. 3150 BC began the dynastic era. The Old Kingdom (c. 2700–2200 BC) was a "golden age" of pyramid building. After disunity, the Middle Kingdom (c. 2040 BC) saw renewed prosperity. The Hyksos conquest (c. 1650 BC) was followed by their expulsion by Ahmose I, founding the powerful New Kingdom (c. 1550–1070 BC), an empire stretching to Syria and Nubia. This era featured famous pharaohs and the monotheistic experiment of Atenism. Later, Egypt was ruled by Libyans, Nubians, and Assyrians before regaining native rule.

### Foreign Domination: Persians, Greeks, and Romans
Native rule ended with the Persian conquest in 525 BC. After a brief independence, Persia reconquered Egypt in 343 BC. Alexander the Great's conquest in 332 BC began the Ptolemaic Kingdom, a Hellenistic state ruled by a Greek dynasty that adopted Egyptian traditions. The kingdom declined amid internal strife, ending with Cleopatra VII's suicide in 30 BC. Egypt became a vital Roman province, its grain feeding the empire and Alexandria serving as a major intellectual hub. Christianity spread early, leading to the distinct Coptic Church after 451 AD. A brief Sasanian conquest occurred from 619 to 629 AD.

### Islamic and Ottoman Rule
The Arab conquest (639–642 AD) brought Sunni Islam, blending with indigenous beliefs. Egypt became a center of the Islamic world, especially under the Fatimid Caliphate in Cairo. The Mamluks, a military caste, took power c. 1250 and ruled until the Ottoman conquest in 1517. Ottoman rule saw economic decline. Napoleon's French invasion (1798) was short-lived but sparked modern Egyptology.

### Modern Egypt: Muhammad Ali to the Republic
After the French expulsion, Muhammad Ali emerged as ruler in 1805, founding a dynasty that modernized the country but incurred heavy debt. The Suez Canal (completed 1869) increased European influence. British control became official in 1882 after crushing a nationalist revolt. A 1919 revolution led to a 1922 declaration of independence, though British influence persisted. The 1952 Revolution ended the monarchy, and a republic was declared in 1953. The Nasser era (1956–1970) saw the Suez Crisis, a union with Syria, and defeat in the 1967 Six-Day War. Anwar Sadat (1970–1981) shifted allegiance to the U.S., launched the 1973 October War, and made peace with Israel in 1979, leading to his assassination. Hosni Mubarak's long rule (1981–2011) was marked by stability and a severe Islamist insurgency.

### 21st Century Upheaval
Widespread protests in 2011 forced Mubarak to resign. The subsequent transition led to the election of Mohamed Morsi of the Muslim Brotherhood in 2012. His rule was marked by political crisis and mass protests. In July 2013, the military, led by Abdel Fattah el-Sisi, removed Morsi from power. El-Sisi won the presidency in 2014 and was re-elected in 2018. His tenure has seen constitutional changes strengthening the military's role and a return to authoritarian governance.

## Terms
*   **Predynastic Period:** The era before unification and the First Dynasty, c. 3150 BC.
*   **Pharaoh:** The title of the ancient Egyptian monarch, considered a living god.
*   **Hyksos:** A people of mixed Semitic-Asiatic origin who ruled Lower Egypt during the Second Intermediate Period (c. 1650–1550 BC).
*   **Hellenistic:** Relating to Greek culture, especially outside Greece after Alexander the Great's conquests.
*   **Coptic:** Refers to the Christian minority in Egypt and their language, derived from ancient Egyptian.
*   **Mamluks:** A military caste of Turkic and Circassian origin who ruled Egypt from c. 1250 to 1517.
*   **Khedive:** The title used by the rulers of Egypt from 1867 to 1914, under Ottoman suzerainty.
*   **Wafd Party:** An Egyptian nationalist political party active in the early 20th century.
*   **Free Officers Movement:** The group of military officers who led the 1952 Egyptian Revolution.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact identity and historicity of King Menes, traditionally credited with unifying Egypt, is debated; he is often identified with Narmer.
*   The nature of Akhenaten's religious revolution (Atenism) is debated—whether it was true monotheism or a form of monolatry.
*   The genetic origins of ancient Egyptians are an active area of research. A 2017 study of mummies showed close affinity to Near Eastern populations, while a 2025 full-genome analysis of an Old Kingdom individual suggested a two-source model with significant ancestry from both the Levant and Mesopotamia.
