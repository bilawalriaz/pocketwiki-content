# Old Kingdom of Egypt

## Overview
The Old Kingdom (c. 2700–2200 BC) was the first of three "golden ages" in ancient Egyptian history, marking a sustained peak of civilization in the lower Nile Valley. It is renowned as the "Age of the Pyramids," a period of centralized power, monumental architecture, and the establishment of the king as a living god, which laid the foundational cultural and artistic principles for millennia.

## Timeline
- **c. 2686–2625 BC** — Reign of Djoser (Third Dynasty), who commissioned the Step Pyramid at Saqqara.
- **c. 2613–2494 BC** — Fourth Dynasty, the zenith of pyramid building under Sneferu, Khufu, Khafre, and Menkaure.
- **c. 2494–2345 BC** — Fifth Dynasty, marked by the growing importance of the sun god Ra and trade expeditions.
- **c. 2345–2181 BC** — Sixth Dynasty, during which pharaonic power weakened in favor of regional nomarchs.
- **c. 2278–2214 BC** — Reign of Pepi II, one of the longest in history, after which internal disorders set in.
- **c. 2200–2150 BC** — A severe drought in the region contributed to the collapse of the Old Kingdom.
- **After 2181 BC** — The Old Kingdom ends, followed by the First Intermediate Period of disunity and famine.

## Body

### Rise and Centralization
The Old Kingdom began with the Third Dynasty under King Djoser, whose architect Imhotep pioneered stone construction with the Step Pyramid. This era saw formerly independent states become nomes (provinces) under direct royal rule. The king was believed to be the incarnation of the god Horus, responsible for maintaining cosmic order and stability.

### Height of Pyramid Building
The Fourth Dynasty represented the peak of royal power and pyramid construction. King Sneferu perfected the art, building three major pyramids. His successors Khufu, Khafre, and Menkaure commissioned the Great Pyramids of Giza. The king's absolute authority allowed him to command the vast resources and labor required for these projects. Military expeditions expanded Egyptian influence into Nubia and Canaan.

### Trade, Religion, and Art
The Fifth Dynasty saw the rise of the cult of the sun god Ra, leading to the construction of sun temples. Egypt expanded its trade networks, building ships to navigate the Red Sea to the Kingdom of Punt for luxury goods like ebony and incense. Old Kingdom art was functional, designed to serve religion and ideology. Its style was defined by three principles: **frontality** (figures face forward for direct interaction), **composite composition** (multiple perspectives for clarity), and **hierarchy of scale** (size indicates importance). Sculpture used hard stones like granite, with colors chosen for symbolic meaning (e.g., black for fertile soil, green for rebirth).

### Decline and Collapse
During the Sixth Dynasty, the pharaoh's power declined as nomarchs (regional governors) became hereditary and independent. The incredibly long reign of Pepi II was followed by succession struggles and civil war. A final blow was a severe, multi-decadal drought around 2200–2150 BC that disrupted Nile flooding, leading to famine and the collapse of central authority into the First Intermediate Period.

### Genetic Insights
A 2025 genetic study of a high-status Old Kingdom individual (c. 2855–2570 BC) revealed ancestry primarily from North African Neolithic populations, with about 20% sourced to the eastern Fertile Crescent (Mesopotamia). This provides direct evidence of ancient migration flows from Mesopotamia to Egypt, accompanying the earlier exchange of cultural practices and domesticated species.

## Terms
- **Nome**: A province of ancient Egypt, ruled by a nomarch.
- **Nomarch**: A regional governor; their power grew at the expense of the pharaoh in the late Old Kingdom.
- **Frontality**: An artistic principle where figures are depicted facing directly forward.
- **Composite Composition**: An artistic technique using multiple perspectives in a single image for clarity.
- **Hierarchy of Scale**: An artistic convention where a figure's size indicates its relative importance.
- **Prenomen**: The throne name of a pharaoh, part of the royal titulary.
- **Pyramid Texts**: Religious inscriptions inside pyramids, first appearing in the pyramid of Unas (late Fifth Dynasty).
- **First Intermediate Period**: The era of disunity and decline that followed the Old Kingdom.

## Debates and Open questions
- The exact cause of the Old Kingdom's collapse is debated, with factors including internal political fragmentation, drought, and famine.
- The attribution of the Great Sphinx of Giza is uncertain; traditional thinking credits Khafre, but some evidence suggests it was commissioned by Djedefre as a monument to his father Khufu.
- The inclusion of the Seventh and Eighth Dynasties as a continuation of the Old Kingdom is a matter of Egyptological debate.
