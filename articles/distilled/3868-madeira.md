# Madeira

## Overview
Madeira is an autonomous archipelago of Portugal in the North Atlantic Ocean, known for its subtropical climate, unique biodiversity, and historical role in the Age of Discovery. It is a significant tourist destination and a special territory of the European Union, with a distinct cultural identity shaped by centuries of settlement, trade, and emigration.

## Timeline
- **c. 900–1030 AD** — Possible Viking visits to the islands.
- **1418** — Portuguese captains João Gonçalves Zarco and Tristão Vaz Teixeira discover Porto Santo.
- **1419–1420** — The larger island of Madeira is discovered and claimed for Portugal.
- **c. 1420–1425** — Portuguese settlement begins under three captaincies.
- **1455** — Sugar production exceeds 6,000 arrobas, becoming the primary economic engine.
- **1480s** — Antwerp becomes a major hub for the Madeira sugar trade.
- **18th century** — Sugar plantations are replaced by vineyards; English merchants dominate the wine trade.
- **1801–1814** — British forces occupy Madeira during the Napoleonic Wars.
- **1916** — German U-boat SM U-38 attacks Funchal harbour, bringing WWI to Portugal.
- **1931** — Military uprising against the National Dictatorship is crushed.
- **1976** — The Portuguese Constitution grants Madeira autonomous administration.
- **1980s** — The Madeira International Business Centre (free trade zone) is established.

## Body

### Discovery and Settlement
Madeira appears in medieval manuscripts before its documented discovery. In 1418, Portuguese captains Zarco and Vaz Teixeira, blown off course, found Porto Santo. The following year, they organized an expedition with Bartolomeu Perestrello, claimed the islands for the Portuguese Crown, and discovered the larger, forested island they named Madeira ("wood"). Colonization began around 1420–1425 with settlers including farmers, fishermen, and former prisoners, who initially grew wheat for sustenance.

### Economic Evolution: Sugar and Wine
Grain production declined, prompting a shift to sugar cane cultivation in the mid-15th century. Using water-driven mills and Genoese financing, Madeira became a major sugar producer by the 1480s, overtaking Cyprus. This boom relied on enslaved labor, initially Guanches and Portuguese convicts. By the 18th century, sugar plantations were replaced by vineyards. English merchants dominated the trade, exporting Madeira wine to Britain and the American colonies, where it was famously toasted at the Declaration of Independence.

### British Influence and Tourism
Britain had a long-standing connection to Madeira, occupying it during the Napoleonic Wars (1801–1814). In the 19th century, the island's mild climate attracted tourists, especially those seeking cures for tuberculosis. The first tourist guide appeared in 1850, and the iconic Belmond Reid's Palace hotel opened in 1891. Tourism remains a vital economic sector today.

### World Wars and Political Upheaval
During WWI, German U-boats bombarded Funchal in 1916 and 1917. The exiled last Austrian Emperor, Charles I, died in Madeira in 1922. In WWII, neutral Portugal hosted 2,000 evacuees from Gibraltar. The Great Depression and economic crisis led to strikes, a 1931 military revolt, and accelerated emigration. Following the 1974 Carnation Revolution, a separatist movement (FLAMA) carried out bomb attacks, but the 1976 Constitution granted political autonomy.

### Geography and Biodiversity
The archipelago consists of the islands of Madeira, Porto Santo, and the Desertas, plus the Savage Islands. Madeira Island is a massive shield volcano, with its highest peak, Pico Ruivo, at 1,862 m. The climate is subtropical with many microclimates. The islands host endemic species, including the Trocaz pigeon and the critically endangered Deserta Grande wolf spider. The ancient laurel forests (laurisilva) are a UNESCO World Heritage Site. A unique feature is the network of *levadas*, centuries-old irrigation channels that also generate hydroelectric power.

### Government and Economy
Madeira is an autonomous region of Portugal with its own legislature and government. As an EU Outermost Region, it receives derogations from some EU policies. Its economy relies on tourism, the Madeira International Business Centre (a free trade zone with tax incentives), and agriculture (notably wine and bananas). The region has worked to reduce significant public debt incurred in the early 21st century.

### Culture and Diaspora
Madeiran culture features folklore music using instruments like the *machete* and *cavaquinho*, the latter of which influenced the creation of the ukulele in Hawaii. Cuisine includes dishes like *espetada* (beef skewered on bay laurel) and *espada com banana*. Madeira wine is a globally recognized fortified wine with Protected Designation of Origin. A large Madeiran diaspora exists, particularly in the United States, South Africa, and Venezuela, stemming from 19th and 20th-century emigration.

## Terms
- **Autonomous Region**: A political entity with its own legislature and government, but within a sovereign state (Portugal).
- **Outermost Region (OMR)**: A territory of the EU that, due to remoteness and other factors, is entitled to derogations from certain EU policies.
- **Laurisilva**: A type of subtropical laurel forest, a relic from the Tertiary period, found in Madeira and other Macaronesian islands.
- **Levadas**: A network of stone-lined irrigation channels, some centuries old, that transport water from the wetter north to the drier south of Madeira.
- **Captaincy**: A system of colonial administration where a captain-major governed a designated territory.
- **Madeira Wine**: A fortified wine from the Madeira Islands, known for its unique production process involving heating and oxidation.
- **International Business Centre (MIBC)**: Madeira's free trade zone offering tax and other incentives to attract foreign investment.
- **Macaronesia**: A collection of five volcanic archipelagos in the North Atlantic, including Madeira, the Azores, and the Canary Islands.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact nature and extent of pre-Portuguese visits (e.g., Vikings, Phoenicians) to Madeira are debated, with archaeological evidence being inconclusive.
- The motivations behind the FLAMA separatist movement in the 1970s are interpreted differently: as genuine ethnic separatism or as a reaction by elites against the political left following the Carnation Revolution.
