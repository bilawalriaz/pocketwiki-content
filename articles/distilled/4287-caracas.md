# Caracas

## Overview
Caracas is the capital and largest city of Venezuela, serving as the nation's political, economic, and cultural center. Located in a mountain valley near the Caribbean Sea, its development has been shaped by colonial history, oil wealth, rapid urbanization, and ongoing social and economic crises.

## Timeline

- **1577** — Caracas becomes the capital of the Venezuela Province.
- **1595** — English privateers sack the city during the Preston–Somers expedition.
- **1777** — Luis de Unzaga creates the Captaincy General of Venezuela, with Caracas as capital.
- **1811** — Venezuela declares independence, with Caracas as the center of the movement.
- **1812** — An earthquake destroys much of Caracas.
- **1821** — Simón Bolívar defeats royalists at the Battle of Carabobo, securing independence.
- **1936** — Venezuela's total population matches the estimated population of Greater Caracas in 2000.
- **1950s** — Development of the Gran Caracas metropolitan area and intensive modernization begins.
- **1983** — The Caracas Metro system begins operations.
- **2010** — Caracas is reported to have the highest murder rate in the world.
- **2026** — U.S. strikes on Caracas lead to the capture of President Nicolás Maduro.

## Body

### History and Founding
Before its 1567 founding, the Caracas Valley was inhabited by indigenous peoples. Early Spanish settlement attempts from Coro were destroyed by native groups led by figures like Guaicaipuro. Captain Diego de Losada successfully established the city by dividing and defeating native resistance. Its proximity to the coast and fertile valley facilitated a trade network with the interior and the island of Margarita. Caracas became the provincial capital in 1577, cementing its importance.

### Colonial Economy and Conflicts
The city's economy initially relied on wheat exports to Spanish colonies. In the 1620s, cacao cultivation became a major export, leading to a shift from native to African slave labor. The city was sacked by English pirates in 1595 but rebuilt. Its economy suffered in the mid-17th century due to a cacao blight, the Mexican Inquisition targeting Portuguese traders, and increased competition. The 1641 earthquake further contributed to a decline, leading to illegal trade with the Dutch. The Guipuzcoan Company of Caracas, founded in 1728, revitalized the cacao trade.

### Independence and Modernization
Caracas was the center of early independence movements, including the 1797 Gual and España conspiracy. After the 1811 declaration and the devastating 1812 earthquake, the Venezuelan War of Independence concluded with Bolívar's victory in 1821. The city remained colonial until urban reforms under Antonio Guzmán Blanco in the late 19th century. The early 20th-century oil boom spurred massive growth and modernization, including the construction of landmarks like the University City of Caracas (a UNESCO World Heritage Site) and the expansion of the metropolitan area.

### Geography and Climate
Caracas lies in a valley of the Venezuelan Coastal Range, separated from the Caribbean by the 2,200-meter Cerro El Ávila. The Guaire River flows through the city. The climate is a tropical savanna (Aw), moderated by altitude, with an average annual temperature of 23.4 °C. A local weather phenomenon called "Pacheco" brings fog and sharp nighttime temperature drops in December and January. The city is seismically active, with notable earthquakes in 1641, 1967, and 2026.

### Urban Structure and Demographics
The city has grown vertically due to mountainous terrain, with about 45% of the population living in improvised hillside settlements called *ranchos*. The Metropolitan District of Caracas, which includes parts of Miranda state, had an estimated population of over 5 million in 2011. The population is predominantly mixed-race, with significant European, African, and Indigenous ancestry, shaped by immigration waves in the 20th century. Caracas has extremely high crime rates; in 2010 it had the world's highest murder rate.

### Economy and Culture
Caracas has a service-based economy, hosting the headquarters of Petróleos de Venezuela (PDVSA), the Caracas Stock Exchange, and major private companies. It is Venezuela's cultural capital, home to museums, theaters, and the Simón Bolívar Symphony Orchestra (part of "El Sistema"). The city hosted the 1983 Pan American Games and is the seat of major universities, including the Central University of Venezuela (founded 1721).

### Transportation
Public transport includes the Caracas Metro (operational since 1983), the Metrocable aerial tramway for hillside communities, and the BusCaracas system. The Simón Bolívar International Airport is the country's main air hub. Road networks are extensive but suffer from severe congestion. A mysterious road substance known as "La Mancha Negra" has been a safety hazard since 1986.

## Terms
- **Ranchos**: Improvised, substandard housing built on mountain slopes without official planning, housing a large portion of the city's population.
- **Pacheco**: A local term for a weather phenomenon in December and January involving abundant fog and a sudden nightly temperature drop.
- **La Mancha Negra**: A mysterious black substance that has oozed from roads since 1986, causing traffic accidents.
- **El Sistema**: The colloquial name for Venezuela's social action music program, connected to the Simón Bolívar Symphony Orchestra.
- **PDVSA**: Petróleos de Venezuela, the state-run oil company and largest company in Venezuela, headquartered in Caracas.
- **Metropolitan District of Caracas**: The administrative region comprising the Libertador Municipality and four municipalities from Miranda state; its legal personality was suppressed in 2017.
- **Guipuzcoan Company of Caracas**: A company founded by the Spanish king in 1728 to manage and grow the cacao trade.
- **Captaincy General of Venezuela**: A Spanish colonial administrative unit created in 1777 with Caracas as its capital.

## Debates and Open questions
- The exact center of the city is debated, with some identifying the historic Caracas Cathedral on Bolívar Square and others pointing to Plaza Venezuela in Los Caobos.
- The source notes that the city's population was "initially sparse" after its founding, raising questions about the pace of early colonial settlement.
- The long-term impact of the 2026 U.S. intervention and the capture of President Maduro on the city's governance and stability is an immediate open question.
