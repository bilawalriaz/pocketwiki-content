# Bratislava

## Overview
Bratislava is the capital and largest city of Slovakia, situated on the Danube River at the border of Austria and Hungary. Its strategic location has made it a historical crossroads of cultures and a key political center, serving as the coronation city and legislative capital of the Kingdom of Hungary from 1536 to 1783. Today, it is Slovakia's political, economic, and cultural heart, and the only national capital bordering two other countries.

- **1536** — Designated the new capital of the Kingdom of Hungary after the Battle of Mohács.
- **1805** — Peace of Pressburg signed between the Austrian and French Empires.

- **1945** — Liberated by the Soviet Red Army.
- **1989** — Became a center of the anti-Communist Velvet Revolution.
- **1993** — Became the capital of the newly independent Slovak Republic after the Velvet Divorce.

## Body

### Etymology and Name
The city's name has evolved through multiple languages and periods. The medieval "Brezalauspurc" (907) is linked to the Hungarian "Pozsony" (first mentioned 1002) and the German "Pressburg." The modern name "Bratislava" was officially adopted in 1919, derived from a 19th-century scholarly misinterpretation of the medieval name "Braslav." The city was historically known by many names, including the Latin "Posonium" and the Hungarian "Pozsony."

### Early History and Medieval Period
The area has been settled since the Neolithic era. The Celtic oppidum was later part of the Roman Danubian Limes. Slavs arrived in the 5th-6th centuries, and the region was part of Great Moravia in the 9th century. After the Battle of Pressburg in 907, the territory became part of the Kingdom of Hungary. It was granted town privileges in 1291 and became a free royal city in 1405.

### Capital of Hungary and Habsburg Era
Following the Ottoman defeat of Hungary at Mohács in 1526, Pressburg was designated the new capital in 1536. It became the coronation site for eleven Hungarian kings and queens. The city flourished under Queen Maria Theresa in the 18th century but later lost importance as central offices moved to Buda. It was a center for the Slovak national movement and published the first Slovak newspaper in 1783.

### 19th Century to World War I
The 19th century brought industrialization, including the first railway in Hungary (1840). The city was part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire from 1867. Before WWI, its population was 42% German, 41% Hungarian, and 15% Slovak.

### 20th Century: Wars and Independence
After WWI, the city was contested but became part of Czechoslovakia in 1919. During WWII, it was the capital of the Nazi-aligned first Slovak Republic, and most of its Jewish population was deported. The city was heavily bombed and liberated by Soviet forces in 1945. Under Communist rule (1948-1989), it became the capital of the Slovak Socialist Republic and saw major construction, including large housing estates. It was a key site in the 1989 Velvet Revolution and became the capital of independent Slovakia in 1993.

### Geography and Climate
Bratislava lies in southwestern Slovakia, straddling the Danube and Morava rivers. It is the only capital bordering two countries (Austria and Hungary). The city's terrain ranges from the Danube floodplain to the Little Carpathians. It has a humid subtropical climate with four distinct seasons.

### Demographics and Politics
Historically multi-ethnic, the city's population became predominantly Slovak (around 90%) after WWII due to the expulsion of Germans and Hungarians. It is the seat of all Slovak national government institutions, including the parliament, presidency, and supreme court. Administratively, it is divided into five districts and 17 boroughs.

### Economy and Tourism
The Bratislava Region is Slovakia's wealthiest, contributing about 26% of its GDP. The economy is service-based, with major headquarters for companies like Volkswagen and Slovnaft. Tourism is significant, with over 1.2 million visitors in 2024, primarily from neighboring countries.

### Culture and Education
Bratislava is Slovakia's cultural hub, home to the Slovak National Theatre, Philharmonic Orchestra, and numerous museums and galleries. It has a rich musical history, visited by composers like Mozart and Haydn. The city hosts several universities, including Comenius University, and the Slovak Academy of Sciences.

### Transport
The city is a major transport hub with an international airport, extensive rail and road connections to neighboring countries, and a river port on the Danube. Public transport consists of buses, trams, and trolleybuses.

## Terms
*   **Oppidum:** A large, fortified Iron Age settlement, typically of the Celts.
*   **Danubian Limes:** The frontier system of the Roman Empire along the Danube River.
*   **Free Royal City (Libera Regia Civitas):** A town in the Kingdom of Hungary with special privileges and self-governance, granted directly by the king.
*   **Velvet Revolution:** The non-violent transition of power in Czechoslovakia that ended Communist rule in 1989.
*   **Velvet Divorce:** The peaceful dissolution of Czechoslovakia into the Czech Republic and Slovakia on January 1, 1993.
*   **Panelák:** A colloquial term for a prefabricated concrete apartment building, common in former Eastern Bloc countries.
*   **Borough (Mestská časť):** An administrative division of Bratislava, each with its own local government.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact location of the medieval settlement "Brezalauspurc" mentioned in 907 is under scholarly debate.
*   The identification of Bratislava and Devín castles as the fortresses of Great Moravia is debated due to a lack of convincing archaeological evidence.
*   The etymology of the city's name remains a subject of scholarly discussion, with competing theories linking it to personal names like "Bratislav," "Božan," or "Poson."
