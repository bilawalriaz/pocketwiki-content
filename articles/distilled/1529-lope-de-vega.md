# Lope de Vega

## Overview
Félix Lope de Vega y Carpio (1562–1635) was the dominant playwright of the Spanish Golden Age, establishing the three-act *comedia* as the national dramatic standard and producing a staggering volume of work—roughly 500 extant plays, 3,000 sonnets, and numerous novels and epic poems. Rejecting neoclassical unities of time, place, and action in his manifesto *Arte nuevo de hacer comedias* (1609), he argued that the Spanish public ("the vulgar") demanded entertainment over Aristotelian rules, creating a commercial theatre of intricate plots (*imbroglio*) drawn from Spanish history, legend, and contemporary life. His turbulent biography—marked by exile, naval service (including the 1588 Armada), multiple affairs, the deaths of wives and children, and a late ordination to the priesthood—fed directly into his exploration of honor, jealousy, and social conflict. Second only to Cervantes in Spanish literary estimation, he defined the *capa y espada* (cloak-and-dagger) genre and influenced European drama through admirers like Goethe. He was also a close friend of Sebastián Francisco de Medrano, founder and president of the Medrano Academy (Poetic Academy of Madrid), attending from 1616 to 1622; their relationship is evident in *El Laurel de Apolo* (1630).

## Body

### Youth and Early Formation
Lope was born in Madrid shortly after it became the capital (1561). His father, an embroiderer from the valley of Carriedo, provided a modest upbringing. A child prodigy, Lope read Spanish and Latin by five, translated Latin verse by ten, and claimed to have written his first play at twelve. He studied under poet Vicente Espinel and at the Jesuit Colegio Imperial, where he absorbed the spiritual exercises later used in his sacred poetry. He fled to a Portuguese military campaign but was rescued by the Bishop of Ávila, who sent him to the University of Alcalá. Though groomed for the priesthood, he abandoned it after falling in love, leaving without a degree to work as a secretary and playwright. In 1583, he saw naval action at the Battle of Ponta Delgada under the future Marquis of Santa Cruz, beginning a lifelong connection to maritime themes.

### Exile and the Valencia School (1588–1590)
A five-year affair with Elena Osorio ("Filis"), daughter of a theater director, ended violently when she left him. Lope’s libelous attacks on her family led to imprisonment and a sentence of eight years’ banishment from court and two years’ exile from Castile. He fled with 16-year-old Isabel de Urbina ("Belisa"), marrying her under family pressure in May 1588. Weeks later, he enlisted in the Spanish Armada—likely a condition set by her family to accept the marriage. His ship, the *San Juan*, survived the disaster. In Valencia, he joined the *Academia de los nocturnos*, a literary tertulia including dramatists Guillén de Castro and Carlos Boyl. There he refined his dramatic formula, violating the unity of action by weaving two plots into one play, a technique termed *imbroglio*.

### Court Service and Personal Catastrophe (1590–1614)
At the end of his exile, Lope served the Marquis of Malpica and then the 5th Duke of Alba in Toledo (1592–1595), becoming a gentleman of the bedchamber. He studied Juan del Encina to perfect the *donaire* (grace/wit) in his characters. It was around this time that Lope wrote his pastoral novel *La Arcadia*, which included many poems and was based on the Duke's household in Alba de Tormes. Isabel died of postpartum complications in 1594. In 1595, following Isabel's death in childbirth, he left the Duke's service and – eight years having passed – returned to Madrid. There were other love affairs and other scandals: Antonia Trillo de Armenta, who earned him another lawsuit, and Micaela de Luján, an illiterate but beautiful actress, who inspired a rich series of sonnets, rewarded him with four children and was his lover until around 1608. In 1598, he married Juana de Guardo, the daughter of a wealthy butcher. Nevertheless, his trysts with others – including Micaela – continued. The 1610s brought crushing blows: his favorite son Carlos Félix died (1612), Juana died in childbirth (1613), and Lope survived an assassination attempt (1611). In 1614, he was ordained a priest, channeling grief into *Rimas sacras* (Sacred Rhymes), which juxtapose bodily decay with the soul’s aspiration to heaven using Jesuit spiritual exercises and Counter-Reformation iconography.

### Priesthood, Honors, and Final Years (1614–1635)
Ordination did not end Lope’s romantic life; he continued affairs and procured female companions for his employer, the Duke of Sessa. His most enduring relationship was with Marta de Nevares (1616–1632). He was a close friend of Sebastián Francisco de Medrano, founder and president of the Medrano Academy (Poetic Academy of Madrid), attending from 1616 to 1622; the relationship is evident in *El Laurel de Apolo* (1630). In 1627, he was made a knight of the Order of Malta, a long-sought honor reflecting his chivalric interests (evident in his 1603 play *El valor de Malta*). He wrote the first Spanish opera, *La selva sin amor* (1627), with music by Alessandro Piccinini. His poetic masterpiece, *Rimas humanas y divinas del licenciado Tomé de Burguillos* (1634), invents a heteronym, the licentiate Tomé de Burguillos, whose intimate, skeptical voice clashes with Lope’s public persona. In 1635, his poet-son Lope (by Micaela) drowned off Venezuela, and his youngest daughter Antonia was abducted. Lope took to his bed and died of scarlet fever on 27 August 1635, buried in St. Sebastian’s Church, Madrid.

### Dramatic Theory and Practice
In *Arte nuevo de hacer comedias en este tiempo* (1609), Lope defended his breach of the neoclassical "three unities" (time, place, action). He argued that the Spanish "vulgar" public paid for entertainment, not rules: "Let us then speak to him in the language of fools, since it is he who pays us." He established the three-act *comedia* as the definitive Spanish form, mixing tragic and comic elements, high and low characters, and varied verse forms (romances, redondillas, sonnets) suited to each situation. He wrote with immense speed—claiming over 100 plays took only 24 hours each, and composing fifteen acts in fifteen days in Toledo. Lope's own records indicate that by 1604 he had composed 230 three-act plays (comedias). The figure had risen to 483 by 1609, to 800 by 1618, to 1000 by 1620, and to 1500 by 1632. Montalbán, in his *Fama Póstuma* (1636) set down the total of Lope's dramatic productions at 1800 comedias and more than 400 shorter sacramental plays. Of these, 637 plays are known by their titles, but only about 450 are extant. Many were printed in twelve-play volumes by Lope himself or pirated from actors' manuscripts.

### Themes, Sources, and Genres
Lope’s drama is fundamentally a **drama of intrigue**: plot determines character and theme. His primary source was **Spanish history**, from the legendary King Pelayo to his own era, staging few national subjects he did not touch. His most celebrated works are **capa y espada** ("cloak and dagger") plays—love intrigues and honor conflicts among the petty nobility of medieval Spain. Key examples: *El perro del hortelano* (The Dog in the Manger), *El castigo sin venganza* (Punishment without Revenge), *El maestro de danzar* (The Dance Teacher). Occasionally he illustrated moral maxims: *Las Flores de Don Juan* defends virtuous poverty against opulent vice and critiques primogeniture. In *El villano en su rincón* (The Peasant in His Corner), a peasant-philosopher confronts Francis I of France, asserting dignity before royal magnificence. Generally, however, Lope aimed to "amuse and stir," prioritizing plot over instruction.

### Non-Dramatic Works
Lope was a premier poet alongside Góngora and Quevedo. His early Moorish and pastoral poems (1580s–90s) encoded his affairs under personae like the Moor Zaide or shepherd Belardo. Major collections: *Rimas* (1602/1604), *Rimas sacras* (1614), *Rimas humanas y divinas del licenciado Tomé de Burguillos* (1634). Epic poems include *La Dragontea* (1598, on Drake’s death), *La Hermosura de Angélica* (1602, sequel to Ariosto’s *Orlando Furioso*), and *Jerusalén conquistada* (1609). Prose fiction: *La Arcadia* (1598, pastoral romance), *El peregrino en su patria* (1604, Byzantine novel), *Pastores de Belén* (1612, divine prose/verse), *Novelas a Marcia Leonarda* (four novellas, 1621–24), and *La Dorotea* (1632, a *Celestina*-style novel in dialogue).

### Legacy and Reception
Lope found a disorganized theatre—variable acts, haphazard versification—and imposed order, expanding subject matter to encompass Bible, mythology, saints’ lives, history, Italian novellas, current events, and 17th-century daily life. He deepened character portrayal with socially specific language and dress. He standardized national verse forms from the *romance* couplet to Italianate lyrics. Critics attacked him: Pedro de Torres Rámila’s Latin satire *Spongia* (1617) assailed his sacramental plays and morals, but public backlash was fierce; scholars like Juan Rodríguez de Fonseca defended him in *Exposulatio Spongiae*. Modern honors include a municipality in Northern Samar (Philippines, 1980), a Manila street, and a 2017 Google Doodle. He appears in fiction: Harry Turtledove’s *Ruled Britannia* (2002, alternate-history Shakespeare encounter), the 2010 film *Lope* (Alberto Ammann), and TV series *El Ministerio del Tiempo* (Víctor Clavijo). In 2023, AI analysis identified an anonymous manuscript in the National Library of Spain, *La francesa Laura* (1628–30), as a lost Lope comedy.

## Terms
- ****Comedia**** — The three-act Spanish play form standardized by Lope, mixing tragic/comic, high/low, and varied verse; distinct from classical "comedy."
- ****Three Unities**** — Neoclassical rules (from Aristotle via Italian critics) requiring a play to observe unity of action, time (24 hours), and place (single locale). Lope rejected them.
- ****Imbroglio**** — A plotting technique developed in Valencia (c. 1589) of weaving two distinct plot lines into a single play, violating unity of action.
- ****Capa y espada**** — "Cloak and dagger": Lope’s signature genre of love intrigues and honor duels among the petty nobility of medieval Spain.
- ****Donaire**** — Grace, wit, or urbane charm in character portrayal; Lope refined it studying Juan del Encina.
- ****Heteronym**** — A fully realized alternative literary persona with distinct biography and voice; Lope created **Tomé de Burguillos** (1634) as a skeptical, intimate counter-self.
- ****Sacramental plays** (*autos sacramentales*)** — Short allegorical plays on the Eucharist; Lope wrote 400+. Torres Rámila attacked them in *Spongia*.
- ****Byzantine novel**** — A Greek prose romance type (separated lovers, travel, recognition); Lope’s *El peregrino en su patria* (1604) adapts it.
- ****Order of Malta**** — Sovereign military order; Lope became a knight (1627), wore its habit in portraits, and wrote *El valor de Malta* (1603).
- ****Academia de los nocturnos**** — Valencia literary academy (c. 1589) where Lope, Guillén de Castro, and others developed the new *comedia*.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authorship and canon**: Only ~450 of ~1,800 claimed plays survive; 637 titles known. The 2023 AI attribution of *La francesa Laura* (confirmed by scholars) shows the canon remains fluid.
- **Speed vs. quality**: Lope’s claims of 24-hour composition and 15 acts in 15 days are either hyperbolic or evidence of a workshop/collaborative method not fully understood.
- **Priesthood and conduct**: His 1614 ordination coexisted with ongoing affairs and procuring for the Duke of Sessa; scholars debate whether this reflects hypocrisy, Counter-Reformation complexity, or a separation of sacramental status from daily life.
- **Moral intent**: Lope stated he aimed to "amuse and stir," not instruct, yet plays like *Las Flores de Don Juan* embed sharp social critique (primogeniture, poverty). The balance between entertainment and subversion is contested.
- **Neoclassical rejection**: *Arte nuevo* frames the break as populist pragmatism ("the vulgar pays"), but Lope also invoked his classical education to justify innovation; the manifesto’s sincerity vs. self-fashioning is debated.
- **Female agency in *capa y espada***: Heroines often drive intrigue, but the genre’s honor code ultimately subordinates them to male violence or marriage; feminist readings differ on whether Lope critiques or reinforces patriarchy.
