# Kingdom of Kongo

## Overview
The Kingdom of Kongo was a powerful and centralized state in Central Africa, existing from c. 1390 to 1862. It was a major political and economic power in the region, notable for its early and complex interactions with Portugal, including the adoption of Christianity and involvement in the Atlantic slave trade. Its history illustrates the dynamics of African state formation, the impact of European contact, and the internal conflicts that led to its eventual fragmentation and colonization.

## Timeline
- **c. 1390** — Kingdom founded through a political marriage between the rulers of Mpemba Kasi and Mbata.
- **1483** — Portuguese explorer Diogo Cão reaches the Kongo coast, initiating contact.
- **1491** — King Nzinga a Nkuwu is baptized, beginning the kingdom's Christianization.
- **1509** — Afonso I ascends the throne, establishing Catholicism as the state cult.
- **1568** — Jaga invasion forces King Álvaro I to seek Portuguese aid, leading to a Portuguese colony in Luanda.
- **1622** — First Kongo-Portuguese War begins, ending in a Kongo victory in 1623.
- **1641** — Dutch capture Luanda, forming an alliance with King Garcia II against Portugal.
- **1665** — Portuguese defeat Kongo at the Battle of Mbwila, killing King António I.
- **1678** — Sack of the capital, São Salvador, triggers a 40-year civil war between the Kimpanzu and Kinlaza factions.
- **1859** — King Pedro V accepts vassalage to Portugal, ending the kingdom's full independence.
- **1914** — Portugal abolishes the titular monarchy after suppressing a revolt.

## Body

### Foundation and Early Expansion
The kingdom was founded c. 1390 through a marital alliance between Nima a Nzima of Mpemba Kasi and Lukeni lua Nsanze of Mbata. Their son, Lukeni lua Nimi, began a period of expansion, conquering neighboring territories and establishing the capital at M'banza-Kongo. The early kings, of the Kilukeni house, centralized power by relocating war captives to the capital, creating a dense population center that fueled the economy and strengthened the monarchy.

### Contact with Portugal and Christianization
Portuguese contact began in 1483. King Nzinga a Nkuwu converted to Christianity in 1491. His successor, Afonso I (r. 1509–1542), made Catholicism the state religion and worked to create a syncretic Kongolese church, using local terms like *nkanda ukisi* (holy book) for the Bible. He sent his son Henrique to Europe, who became the first Kongolese bishop. Afonso also expanded the kingdom and attempted to regulate the growing slave trade with Portugal.

### The Slave Trade and Portuguese Rivalry
The slave trade, initially limited, expanded dramatically after Portuguese colonization of São Tomé. Afonso I complained to Portugal about merchants violating trade monopolies and kidnapping free Kongolese. The trade eroded royal power as Portuguese merchants traded directly with nobles. Tensions led to the First Kongo-Portuguese War (1622–23), which Kongo won. Later, King Garcia II allied with the Dutch against Portugal (1641–1648), but the alliance collapsed. The decisive Battle of Mbwila (1665) saw the Portuguese kill King António I, crippling the central monarchy.

### Civil War and Fragmentation
The death of António I triggered a 40-year civil war between the Kimpanzu and Kinlaza royal factions. The capital, São Salvador, was sacked in 1678, and the population dispersed into fortified mountain settlements. The kingdom fragmented into rival states, with the once-vassal County of Soyo becoming independent. The civil war increased the export of Kongolese slaves. Although the monarchy was partially restored in the 18th century, it never regained its former power, facing challenges from entrepreneurial nobles and Portuguese encroachment.

### Final Century and Vassalage
In the 19th century, civil wars continued, and the slave trade persisted illegally. In 1859, King Pedro V, with Portuguese support, defeated a rival claimant but accepted a treaty of vassalage to Portugal. Portugal gained nominal authority, though it withdrew its garrison in 1866. The kingdom's autonomy further declined due to the rise of powerful, clan-based trading magnates. Portugal formally abolished the monarchy in 1914 after suppressing a revolt.

## Terms
- **Manikongo**: The title for the king of Kongo, derived from the Portuguese version of *Mwene Kongo* ("lord or ruler of the Kongo kingdom").
- **Kanda**: A matrilineal lineage or "house," the fundamental social and political unit; royal succession was determined by kanda affiliation.
- **M'banza-Kongo**: The capital city of the kingdom, a major urban center whose population and concentration of power were key to royal authority.
- **Nzimbu**: The universal currency of the region, consisting of shells from the sea snail *Olivella nana*, collected on the island of Luanda.
- **Kimpanzu and Kinlaza**: The two primary royal factions whose rivalry dominated the kingdom's politics from the 17th century onward, leading to civil war.
- **Soyo**: A wealthy coastal province that became increasingly autonomous and eventually an independent power, often rivaling the central monarchy.
- **Imbangala**: Mercenary African warrior groups used by the Portuguese in their wars against Kongo and Ndongo.
- **Mestres**: Kongolese lay teachers who formed the backbone of the Christian church in Kongo due to a shortage of ordained clergy.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The origins and identity of the "Jaga" invaders of 1568 are debated; they may have been external invaders or internal rebels.
- The precise nature of the early alliance between the founders of Kongo and the role of the Mbata province (whether it was a senior partner) is unclear from sources.
- The extent to which the 18th-century oral traditions about the kingdom's founder reflect early history versus later clan-based narratives is a scholarly question.
