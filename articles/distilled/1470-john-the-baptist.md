# John the Baptist

## Overview
John the Baptist (c. 6 BC – c. AD 30) was a Jewish itinerant preacher active near the Jordan River who used baptism—a ritual immersion signifying repentance and purification—as the central rite of his messianic movement. He is revered as a major prophet in Christianity, Islam, Mandaeism, the Baháʼí Faith, and the Druze faith. The New Testament portrays him as the divinely appointed forerunner of Jesus, whom he baptized; the historian Josephus confirms his execution by Herod Antipas, who feared John’s popular influence. His movement persisted after his death, with the Mandaeans tracing their lineage directly to his disciples.

## Timeline
- **c. 6 BC** — Birth to Zechariah (priest) and Elizabeth (relative of Mary) in Judea.
- **c. AD 28–29** — Begins public ministry in the wilderness of Judea/Jordan River valley, preaching repentance and baptizing.
- **c. AD 28–30** — Baptizes Jesus of Nazareth; recognizes him as the coming Messiah (per Gospel accounts).
- **c. AD 30** — Arrested by Herod Antipas for denouncing his marriage to Herodias.
- **c. AD 30** — Beheaded at the fortress of Machaerus; disciples bury his body.
- **2nd century AD** — Followers continue to exist; some proclaim John as the Messiah.
- **Modern era** — Mandaeans survive as an ethnoreligious group venerating John as their final and greatest prophet.

## Body

### Historical Sources and Josephus
The primary sources for John’s life are the four canonical Gospels, the Acts of the Apostles, and the *Antiquities of the Jews* by Flavius Josephus (c. AD 93–94). Josephus describes John as a "good man" who commanded Jews to exercise virtue and piety and to undergo baptism for bodily purification, presupposing the soul was already purified by righteousness. Josephus states Herod Antipas executed John at Machaerus because he feared John’s massive following could spark a rebellion. Notably, Josephus attributes the later destruction of Herod’s army to divine punishment for John’s killing. Scholars note a chronological tension: Josephus links the execution to a war with Aretas IV culminating c. AD 36, while the Gospels place it c. AD 30.

### Gospel Portrayals: Divergent Theologies
All four Gospels open Jesus’ ministry with John’s appearance, but they differ significantly in theology and detail.

*   **Mark (earliest Gospel):** Identifies John as the fulfillment of a composite prophecy (Isaiah, Malachi, Exodus). He wears camel’s hair, eats locusts and wild honey, and preaches a "baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins." He announces a stronger one who will baptize with the Holy Spirit. Mark narrates Jesus’ baptism with a private vision (heavens open, Spirit descends like a dove, voice addresses Jesus: "You are my Son"). John’s death results from Herodias’s grudge; Herod reluctantly executes him after a rash oath to Herodias’s daughter (traditionally Salome). Mark’s Herod calls John "righteous and holy."
*   **Matthew:** Follows Mark but heightens John’s role as Jesus’ prototype. John critiques Pharisees and Sadducees ("brood of vipers") and preaches imminent judgment. At the baptism, John recognizes Jesus and objects ("I need to be baptized by you"). The heavenly voice addresses the crowd ("This is my Son"). Matthew shifts blame for the execution onto Herod, who *wants* John dead. Jesus explicitly identifies John as "Elijah who is to come" (Malachi 4:5).
*   **Luke:** Adds a detailed infancy narrative. John is the miraculous son of elderly, priestly parents (Zechariah, of the division of Abijah; Elizabeth, a daughter of Aaron), making him a descendant of Aaron on both sides. The angel Gabriel announces his birth. Luke alone states Elizabeth is a "relative" of Mary (viewed by scholars like Raymond Brown as historically dubious). John teaches ethics (charity, honest tax collecting, soldier conduct) and travels the Jordan region. He is imprisoned and beheaded, but the Salome dance narrative is omitted. In Acts, disciples of John become followers of Jesus.
*   **Gospel of John:** Presents a high Christology. John the Baptist is "a man sent from God" to witness to the Light; he denies being the Christ, Elijah, or "the Prophet." He does not baptize Jesus but testifies to seeing the Spirit descend like a dove on him, declaring Jesus the "Lamb of God" and "Son of God." He tells his disciples, "He must increase, but I must decrease." Jesus later calls John "a burning and shining lamp."

**Comparative Points:**
*   **Isaiah Prophecy:** Mark’s quotation is a conflation of Isaiah 40:3, Malachi 3:1, and Exodus 23:20; Matthew and Luke drop the Malachi/Exodus portions.
*   **Baptism Vision:** Mark/Luke: private to Jesus ("You are my Son"). Matthew: public declaration ("This is my Son"). John: Baptist sees the dove as objective proof.
*   **Knowledge of Jesus:** Mark: John does not recognize Jesus at the baptism. Matthew: Immediate recognition. Luke: Familial relationship known from birth. John: Recognition via the sign of the dove.
*   **Elijah Identity:** Matthew/Mark: Attire recalls Elijah (2 Kings 1:8); Jesus says John *is* Elijah. Luke: John goes in the "spirit and power of Elijah." Gospel of John: John explicitly denies being Elijah.

### Relationship with the Essenes and Qumran
Some scholars hypothesize John belonged to or was influenced by the **Essenes**, a semi-ascetic Jewish sect (associated with the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran) practicing ritual baptism and awaiting a messiah. Similarities include wilderness location, asceticism, baptismal practice, and apocalyptic expectation. However, differences exist: John’s baptism was a once-for-all rite of repentance for *all* Israel, whereas Qumran practiced repeated lustrations for sect members only. Barbara Thiering identified John with the "Teacher of Righteousness," but this is widely rejected as superficial.

### Death and Relics
The Gospels and Josephus agree Herod Antipas beheaded John at Machaerus c. AD 30. The Synoptics cite John’s condemnation of Herod’s marriage to Herodias (wife of his brother Herod Philip/Herod II) as the cause. Mark details a banquet where Herodias’s daughter dances, securing John’s head on a platter. Matthew says Herod wanted John dead; Luke omits the banquet.

**Relic Traditions:** Competing claims for John’s head exist at the Umayyad Mosque (Damascus), San Silvestro in Capite (Rome), Amiens Cathedral (France), and the Residenz Museum (Munich). Right arm/hand relics are claimed by the Serbian Orthodox monastery at Cetinje, Siena Cathedral (donated by Pius II, 1464), Topkapi Palace (Istanbul, index finger), and the Armenian Church in Chinsurah (India). Other claimed relics include a decapitation cloth (Aachen Cathedral), bones on Sveti Ivan island (Bulgaria, radiocarbon dated to 1st century AD Middle Eastern male), and relics in Egypt, Nagorno-Karabakh, and Kansas City (Nelson-Atkins Museum).

### Religious Views Across Traditions

**Christianity:**
*   **Catholic/Protestant:** Forerunner of Christ; last Old Testament prophet. Two feasts: Nativity (June 24) and Beheading (Aug 29). Catholic theology holds John was sanctified in utero at the Visitation (Luke 1:41).
*   **Eastern Orthodoxy:** "Saint John the Forerunner." Bridge between Old and New Covenants. After death, he preached in Hades. Six feast days, including Conception (Sep 23), Synaxis (Jan 7), Nativity (Jun 24), Beheading (Aug 29), and three Findings of the Head. Tuesday is dedicated to him. Iconographically depicted with wings (messenger/angel).
*   **Latter-day Saints:** John ordained by an angel at 8 days old to "overthrow the kingdom of the Jews." Appeared as a resurrected being to Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery (May 15, 1829) to restore the Aaronic Priesthood. Ministry spans three dispensations.
*   **Unification Church:** John failed his mission to unite with Jesus and lead the Jewish people to accept him; his denial of being Elijah blocked the Messiah’s acceptance.
*   **Gnosticism (Ebionites):** Viewed John, Jesus, and James as vegetarians; amended Gospel of Matthew to change "locusts" to "honey cakes/manna."

**Mandaeism:** John (Yuhana Maṣbana / Yuhana bar Zakria) is the **greatest and final prophet**. Mandaeans trace descent from his original disciples (though their religion originates with Adam). He is a Nasoraean (priestly initiate), renewer of the faith, messenger of Light (nhura) and Truth (kushta), possessing full Gnosis (manda). Mandaean texts (*Ginza Rabba*, *Mandaean Book of John*) depict him as married to Anhar with children. Scholars (Macúch, Drower, Buckley, Gündüz) affirm a likely historical link to John’s disciples.

**Islam:** Yahya ibn Zakariyya is a prophet, maternal cousin of Jesus, and witness to God’s word heralding Jesus. The Quran (19:7–10) notes the unique naming of "Yahya." He received wisdom in childhood, was pure, devout, dutiful to parents, and denounced sin. He met Muhammad during the Mi'raj (ascension) in the second heaven. His tomb in the Umayyad Mosque is a pilgrimage site. Sufis revere his chastity and youthful wisdom.

**Druze:** Honor John as a central prophet/mentor. Believe in reincarnation; identify John as the return of Elijah (al-Khidr).

**Baháʼí Faith:** John is a "lesser Prophet" sent to prepare the way for Jesus. Bahá'u'lláh identifies the Báb (his own forerunner) as the spiritual return of John the Baptist.

### Scholarly Debates on the Historical John
*   **Jesus’ Relationship:** **James McGrath** and many historians view Jesus as a disciple of John who later broke away. **John Dominic Crossan** contrasts John’s "monopoly" (movement died with him) with Jesus’ "franchise" (communal praxis survived). Crossan labels John an **apocalyptic eschatologist** (God imminently destroys evil); Jesus an **ethical eschatologist** (God demands *we* act against evil).
*   **Baptism's Meaning:** **L. Michael White** sees it as Jewish repurification/rededication. **Harold Attridge** agrees John was apocalyptic; the "forerunner" motif is an early church construct to resolve the embarrassment of Jesus' subordination via baptism.
*   **John’s Doubt/Challenge:** **Robert Deffinbaugh** reads John’s question from prison ("Are you the one?") as a public challenge expecting a fiery Messiah. **Charles Croll** counters that John was affirming his own ministry’s end and handing off the witness.
*   **Discipleship:** **Michael Crosby** argues John never became a disciple of Jesus due to conflicting messianic expectations; only two followers switched (per scripture). **Croll** notes five disciples followed Jesus immediately, four becoming apostles.
*   **Competition:** **Candida Moss** views them as "de facto competitors"; after John’s death, Jesus’ followers had to counter the rumor Jesus was John raised from the dead (Matt 16:14).

### Art, Music, and Cultural Legacy
*   **Art:** Earliest depictions in Baptism of Christ scenes (5th c.). Standard iconography: gaunt, bearded, long hair, camel hair garment, staff, lamb, scroll "Ecce Agnus Dei." **Byzantine/Eastern:** Deesis composition (flanking Christ Pantocrator with Mary), often winged. **Western:** Narrative cycles (birth, preaching, baptism, Salome’s dance, beheading, Salome with head). Major cycles by Giotto, Andrea del Sarto, Ghirlandaio, Filippo Lippi. Caravaggio painted numerous Johns (youths, execution). Pre-Raphaelite (Millais) and Symbolist (Moreau) revivals.
*   **Music:** Guido d'Arezzo derived the solfège scale (Ut/Do, Re, Mi...) from the hymn *Ut Queant Laxis* (for John's feast). Settings by Orlando Gibbons, J.S. Bach (Cantata BWV 7), Alessandro Stradella. Richard Strauss’s opera *Salome* (Jokanaan). References in Bob Dylan, Al Kooper, Father John Misty, Om.
*   **Commemoration:** Nativity (June 24) aligns with summer solstice/Midsummer ("He must increase, I must decrease"). Major festivals: Porto (Portugal), Brazil (Festas Juninas), Puerto Rico (patron), Quebec (Fête Nationale), Philippines (water dousing), Florence, Genoa, Malta, Perth (Scotland), Penzance (UK).
*   **Patronage:** Knights Hospitaller (Knights of Saint John), Freemasons (with John the Evangelist), Florence, Puerto Rico, French Canada, Charleston (SC), numerous Philippine towns.

## Terms
- ****Baptism**** — Ritual immersion in water used by John as a sign of repentance and purification; distinct from repeated Jewish lustrations and later Christian sacramental baptism.
- ****Eschatology**** — Study of "last things"; John is often classed as an *apocalyptic eschatologist* expecting imminent divine intervention to destroy evil.
- ****Forerunner (Prodromos)**** — Title for John in Eastern Christianity; one who goes before to prepare the way, applied to his role for Jesus in life and in Hades after death.
- ****Mandaeans**** — Gnostic ethnoreligious group (Iraq/Iran/diaspora) venerating John (Yuhana) as their supreme prophet; practice frequent baptism (masbuta).
- ****Nasoraean**** — Mandaean term for a priestly initiate or "guardian of knowledge"; applied to John in Mandaean texts.
- ****Qumran/Essenes**** — Jewish sect (Dead Sea Scrolls community) practicing asceticism, communal living, and ritual purity; hypothesized link to John.
- ****Deesis**** — Byzantine iconographic composition: Christ Pantocrator flanked by the Virgin Mary and John the Baptist interceding for humanity.
- ****Aaronic Priesthood**** — In Latter-day Saint theology, the lesser priesthood restored by the resurrected John the Baptist to Joseph Smith in 1829.
- ****Mi'raj**** — Muhammad's nocturnal ascension through the heavens; Islamic tradition places John (Yahya) in the second heaven with Jesus.
- ****Synoptic Gospels**** — Matthew, Mark, and Luke; share similar structure/content, allowing side-by-side comparison (synopsis).

## Debates and open questions
1.  **Historicity of the Infancy Narrative:** Luke’s account of John’s priestly parentage, miraculous conception, and familial link to Jesus (via Elizabeth/Mary) is widely regarded by critical scholars (e.g., Brown, Vermes) as theological construction modeled on Samuel’s birth (1 Samuel 1), not history.
2.  **Essene Connection:** Whether John was a member, associate, or entirely independent of the Qumran community remains unresolved. Similarities (wilderness, baptism, apocalypticism) are balanced by sharp differences (inclusivity vs. sectarianism, single vs. repeated baptism).
3.  **Date of Death:** Josephus implies a date closer to AD 36 (linked to war with Aretas); Gospels imply c. AD 30 (linked to Jesus' ministry). The discrepancy affects the chronology of Jesus' ministry.
4.  **Nature of John’s Baptism:** Was it primarily for *forgiveness of sins* (Mark), *bodily purification* assuming prior righteousness (Josephus), or *repentance/rededication* (White)? The relationship to later Christian baptism is debated.
5.  **John’s Self-Understanding:** Did John view himself as the Messiah, Elijah, or solely a forerunner? The Gospels disagree: Mark/Matthew imply Jesus identified him as Elijah; John’s Gospel has him deny it; Luke has the angel predict the "spirit and power of Elijah."
6.  **Continuity of Movement:** Why did John’s movement not survive his death (Crossan’s "monopoly") while Jesus’ did? Was it solely structural (centralized vs. communal), or did the resurrection belief provide the necessary catalyst?
7.  **Mandaean Origins:** While scholars accept a historical link between Mandaeans and John’s disciples, the exact process of separation, gnosticization, and survival into modernity is debated.
