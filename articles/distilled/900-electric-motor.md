# Electric motor

## Overview
An electric motor converts electrical energy into mechanical energy (torque) via the Lorentz force generated by the interaction of magnetic fields and current-carrying conductors. The inverse device, an electric generator, converts mechanical energy back into electrical energy. Motors are the primary consumers of electricity globally (≈50%), powering applications from industrial machinery and transportation to household appliances and disk drives. They are classified by power source (DC/AC), commutation method (brushed/brushless), phase count, flux geometry (axial/radial), and cooling method. The development of efficient motors—specifically the recognition of the critical air gap and the advent of AC polyphase systems—revolutionized industry by enabling distributed, point-of-use power control, replacing line shafts and belts.

## Body

### Components and Magnetic Circuit
An electric motor comprises a **rotor** (moving part delivering mechanical power) and a **stator** (stationary part surrounding the rotor). Electrically, these correspond to the **armature** (current-carrying windings on a ferromagnetic core where Lorentz force acts) and **field magnets** (electromagnets or permanent magnets creating the flux). The field magnet is typically on the stator; the armature on the rotor, though this can be reversed. The stator core uses insulated **laminations** (electrical steel) to suppress eddy-current losses. An **air gap** separates rotor and stator; it must be minimized for performance but kept large enough to prevent friction. The **commutator** (in DC/universal motors) is a rotary switch on the rotor that reverses current in the windings every half-turn (180°) via stationary **brushes** (carbon), maintaining unidirectional torque. **Bearings** support the rotor shaft, transferring axial/radial loads to the housing; the shaft extends externally to drive the load (overhung load).

### Historical Development: From Electrostatics to AC Dominance
Early motors (1740s) were electrostatic, requiring high voltage and impractical for work. Volta’s battery (1799) and Ørsted/Ampère’s electromagnetism (1820) enabled electromagnetic motors. Faraday’s 1821 homopolar motor demonstrated rotation but lacked utility. Jedlik’s commutator (1827–28) solved continuous rotation. Sturgeon (1832) and Davenport (1837) built early DC motors, but high battery costs prevented commercial success. Jacobi (1834–38) achieved practical mechanical output. The critical breakthroughs for DC were Pacinotti’s ring armature (1864), Gramme’s commercialization (1871), the drum rotor and laminated core (Siemens, 1872–73), and slotted rotors (Wenström, 1880). Sprague’s 1886 non-sparking DC motor with constant speed under load enabled trolleys, elevators, and subways, launching widespread industrial adoption.

AC development paralleled DC. Arago identified rotating fields (1824); Baily demonstrated primitive induction (1879). Ferraris (1885–86) and Tesla (1887–88) independently invented the induction motor. Tesla’s two-phase patents were bought by Westinghouse, but Dolivo-Dobrovolsky’s three-phase system (1889)—with cage/wound rotors and the first long-distance transmission (Frankfurt, 1891)—became the global standard. GE and Westinghouse cross-licensed the squirrel-cage design (1896). By the 21st century, AC motors (driven by VFDs) dominate, with DC market share declining since the 1980s.

### Motor Types and Operating Principles
Motors operate via **magnetism** (dominant), **electrostatics**, or **piezoelectricity**. Magnetic motors form fields in both rotor and stator; torque arises from the vector product of these fields. One or both fields must rotate (via switching poles or AC supply).

**DC Motors:**
*   **Brushed (Commutated):** Mechanical commutator/brushes feed rotor windings. Sub-types: shunt, series, compound, permanent magnet (PM), separately excited. PM motors eliminate field winding losses but lack field control. Brushes cause friction, sparking, wear, RFI, and speed limits.
*   **Brushless DC (BLDC/EC):** Electronic commutation (Hall sensors) replaces brushes. Trapezoidal back-EMF waveform. 85–96.5% efficiency. Used in disk drives, fans, EVs (up to ~100 kW). Advantages: long life, low noise, no sparking, precise speed control.
*   **Switched Reluctance (SRM):** No magnets or rotor currents. Torque from sequential stator energization aligning salient rotor poles. Rugged, used in appliances/vehicles.

**AC Motors (Externally Commutated):**
*   **Universal:** Series-wound commutated motor running on AC or DC. High speed (>10,000 rpm), high starting torque, compact. Used in portable tools, vacuums, blenders. Lower efficiency/life on AC due to eddy currents.
*   **Induction (Asynchronous):** Rotor power via electromagnetic induction (transformer action). Stator = primary, rotor = secondary.
    *   **SCIM (Squirrel-Cage):** Rotor = aluminum/copper bars shorted by end rings. Rugged, low maintenance. Bar shape tailors speed-torque curve. Dominant industrial motor.
    *   **WRIM (Wound-Rotor):** Rotor windings connected to slip rings. External resistors allow high starting torque/low current and speed control (slip-energy recovery possible). Used for high-inertia loads.
    *   **Torque Motor:** Designed to operate stalled indefinitely (constant tension/force). Used in tape drives, throttle control.
*   **Synchronous:** Rotor locks to stator field frequency (zero slip). Types: DC-excited rotor (slip rings), reluctance (flats on cage rotor), hysteresis (smooth cylinder magnetizes), PM (cup rotor). Used in clocks, precision drives, large industrial.
*   **Doubly-Fed:** Two independent multiphase winding sets (stator + rotor), both feeding power. Electronic control of rotor enables twice the constant-torque speed range of singly-fed machines. Requires slip rings; control difficult near synchronous speed.

**Advanced Rotary:**
*   **Coreless/Ironless:** Rotor lacks iron core (winding cylinder or self-supporting). Extreme acceleration (mechanical time constant <1 ms), no cogging, no eddy currents. Requires forced cooling; used in disk drive actuators, medical/robotic servos.
*   **Pancake/Axial Flux:** Disc armature between axial magnet arrays. Flat profile, no cogging, high torque density. Magnax yokeless design (stator between two rotors) achieves 15 kW/kg peak. Used in tape drives, servo systems, EVs.
*   **Transverse Flux (TFM):** Flux path transverse to rotation via U-shaped cores. Decouples pole count from winding size → high torque density. Complex/expensive; niche use.
*   **Servomotor:** Motor + feedback (encoder/resolver) for closed-loop position/speed control. Requires predictable dynamics, high torque/inertia ratio. Uses BLDC, PMSM, induction, SRM.
*   **Stepper:** Moves in discrete steps via sequential coil energization. Open-loop positioning (relies on not missing steps). Microstepping enables smooth motion. Used in printers, scanners, legacy disk drives, quartz watches.

**Linear Motors:** "Unrolled" rotary motors producing linear force. Commonly induction or stepper. Used in maglev trains, roller coasters, pen plotters.

**Non-Magnetic:**
*   **Electrostatic:** Force from charge attraction/repulsion. High voltage, low current. Dual of magnetic motors. Used in MEMS (<100 V); biological molecular motors.
*   **Piezoelectric:** Shape change of piezoelectric material under electric field → ultrasonic vibrations → linear/rotary motion (caterpillar-like).
*   **Electric Propulsion:** Spacecraft propulsion via electrically accelerated propellant or electrodynamic tethers.

### Inputs, Control, and Performance Parameters
**Power Supply:** DC via split-ring commutator. AC via slip rings or external commutation (VFD). Universal motors accept both.
**Control:** DC: voltage adjustment or PWM. AC fixed-speed: direct grid/soft starters. AC variable-speed: VFD, inverters, electronic commutators.
**Force/Torque:** Lorentz force $F = I \ell \times B$. Torque $T$ integral of force vectors × radius.
**Power:** Mechanical $P_{em} = T\omega = Fv$. Electrical $P_e = IV$. Efficiency $\eta = P_m/P_e$. Peak efficiency typically at 75% rated load; larger motors more efficient. Range: 15–20% (shaded pole) to 98% (PM).
**Back EMF:** Voltage induced in armature opposing supply ($V_{back} \propto \omega$). Enables DC speed self-regulation: load ↑ → speed ↓ → back EMF ↓ → current ↑ → torque ↑.
**Losses:** Resistive ($I^2R$), core (hysteresis/eddy), mechanical (bearings), aerodynamic (fans), commutation (sparking/heat).
**Goodness Factor (Laithwaite):** $G = \omega / (\text{resistance} \times \text{reluctance})$. $G > 1$ implies efficiency. Favors large magnetic poles; applies to non-PM motors.
**Torque Density:** Continuous torque limited by air-gap area, back-iron depth, and saturation. Liquid cooling ~4× air-cooled density. PM and induction topologies debate relative density.
**Power Density:** Up to 20 kW/kg (torque density × constant-torque speed range).
**Acoustic Noise:** Mechanical (bearings), aerodynamic (fans), magnetic (Maxwell/magnetostriction forces → "whining").

### Standards
Key standards: API 541/546/547 (petroleum/chemical), IEEE 112/115/841 (testing/severe duty), IEC 60034/60072 (international rotating machines), NEMA MG-1, UL 1004.

## Terms
- ****Lorentz Force**** — Force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field: $F = I \ell \times B$; the fundamental mechanism of motor torque.
- ****Commutator**** — Rotary electrical switch on rotor shaft that reverses current in rotor windings every half-turn to maintain unidirectional torque (DC/universal motors).
- ****Back EMF**** — Voltage induced in armature windings by rotation through magnetic field, opposing the supply voltage; proportional to speed, enables self-regulation.
- ****Slip (s)**** — Difference between synchronous speed (stator field) and rotor speed in induction motors, expressed as fraction of synchronous speed; required for torque production.
- ****Squirrel-Cage Rotor (SCIM)**** — Induction rotor with solid conductor bars (Al/Cu) shorted by end rings; rugged, maintenance-free, dominant industrial type.
- ****Wound-Rotor (WRIM)**** — Induction rotor with insulated windings connected to slip rings; allows external resistance for starting torque/speed control.
- ****Brushless DC (BLDC/EC)**** — Motor with permanent magnet rotor and electronically commutated stator windings (Hall sensors); no brushes, high efficiency, long life.
- ****Variable-Frequency Drive (VFD)**** — Electronic controller varying AC frequency/voltage to control speed/torque of AC motors.
- ****Cogging**** — Torque ripple caused by magnetic attraction between rotor iron teeth and stator magnets; absent in coreless/axial flux designs.
- ****Goodness Factor (G)**** — Laithwaite’s metric: $G = \omega \mu \sigma A_m A_e / (l_m l_e)$; predicts efficiency potential based on geometry and material properties.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Torque Density Superiority:** Sources conflict on whether PM synchronous (PMSM), induction (IM), or switched reluctance (SRM) topologies achieve highest continuous torque density; one source claims parity for optimally designed armatures, others show PMSM significantly higher than IM up to 1 MW.
*   **Transverse Flux Commercialization:** TFMs offer high torque density via decoupled pole/winding geometry but suffer from cogging, ripple torque, and high manufacturing complexity/cost, limiting them to niche roles.
*   **Doubly-Fed Control Near Synchronism:** Doubly-fed machines offer twice the constant-torque speed range but face control instability near synchronous speed; brushless power transfer to rotor (BWRSDF) remains a theoretical solution requiring precision torque-angle/slip control.
*   **Coreless Thermal Limits:** Ironless rotors achieve extreme acceleration but lack thermal mass; overheating remains a constraint even for small motors, mandating aggressive cooling.
*   **Axial Flux Scaling:** Yokeless axial flux designs (e.g., Magnax) demonstrate high specific power (15 kW/kg peak) and stackability, but long-term reliability and mass-production maturity versus radial flux are evolving.
