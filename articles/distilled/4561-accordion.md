# Accordion

## Overview
The accordion is a family of box-shaped, bellows-driven free-reed aerophones (instruments that produce sound as air flows past a reed in a frame). Its essential characteristic is combining a melody section (usually right-hand) with an accompaniment section (left-hand) in one instrument. Invented in early 19th-century Europe, it spread globally through migration and became central to numerous folk, popular, and classical music traditions worldwide.

## Timeline
- **1822** — Basic form believed invented in Berlin by Christian Friedrich Ludwig Buschmann.
- **1829** — Cyrill Demian patents an instrument named "accordion" in Vienna, featuring automatically coupled bass chords.
- **c. 1828** — Accordion introduced from Germany to Britain.
- **Mid-1840s** — Accordion becomes popular in New York.
- **c. 1830** — Earliest simple accordions made in Tula, Russia, by Ivan Sizov and Timofey Vorontsov.
- **1844** — Charles Wheatstone patents the concertina, combining chords and keyboard.
- **1854** — Adolf Müller describes many accordion types in *Schule für Accordion*.
- **1900s–1960s** — "Golden age of the accordion" in popular music.
- **Late 1950s–1960s** — Accordion declines in popular music due to rise of rock and roll.
- **2005** — Veli Kujala develops a quarter-tone accordion with Pigini.
- **2010s** — Electronic and digital accordions with MIDI sensors become available.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
The accordion is one of several European inventions of the early 19th century using free reeds and bellows. The oldest name for the instrument family is *harmonika*, from Ancient Greek. The first patent for an instrument named "accordion" was granted to Cyrill Demian in Vienna in 1829. His instrument was primarily a left-hand bass accompaniment device, capable of sounding a full chord with one key (bisonoric, meaning different pitches on bellows push/pull). It quickly spread to Britain and the United States. In Russia, production began around 1830 and grew exponentially, with Tula factories producing over 700,000 instruments annually by 1874. Subsequent innovations, like Wheatstone's concertina (1844), combined melody and chord functions, leading to the modern accordion's dual manual design.

### Construction and Types
All accordions share core components: a pleated bellows (the primary means of articulation and volume control), a body with two boxes housing reed chambers, and a pallet mechanism that controls airflow across internal brass or steel reeds. Accordions are broadly classified by their right-hand keyboard: piano accordions use a piano-style keyboard, while button accordions use a buttonboard, which can be chromatic (notes arranged chromatically) or diatonic (notes arranged in a diatonic scale, often bisonoric). The left-hand keyboard typically uses the Stradella bass system (preset bass notes and chords in a circle of fifths) or free-bass systems for playing melodies and complex chords. Reed banks produce different timbres, selectable via register switches. Modern accordions may include electronics, microphones, and MIDI sensors.

### Global Spread and Musical Use
The accordion spread worldwide via European migration, becoming integral to diverse genres. In the Americas, it is central to styles like Argentine Chamamé, Brazilian forró and sertanejo, Colombian vallenato, Dominican merengue, and Mexican norteño. In Europe and North America, it features in folk, dance-pop, cajun, zydeco, jazz, klezmer, and classical music. Many conservatories have classical accordion departments. The instrument's "golden age" in popular music (1900s–1960s) featured virtuosos like Pietro Frosini and the Deiro brothers. It later experienced a decline but remains vital in traditional and contemporary contexts, from Chinese mass popularity to North Korean state-mandated learning.

## Terms
- **Free-reed aerophone**: An instrument that produces sound as air flows past a reed mounted in a frame.
- **Bellows**: The pleated, expandable component that pumps air across the reeds; its motion controls volume and expression.
- **Bisonoric**: Producing different pitches depending on the direction of bellows movement (push or pull).
- **Unisonoric**: Producing the same pitch regardless of bellows direction.
- **Stradella bass system**: A standard left-hand layout with bass notes and preset major, minor, seventh, and diminished chords arranged in a circle of fifths.
- **Free-bass system**: A left-hand layout allowing individual note selection for playing melodies and complex chords.
- **Register switch**: A control that selects different combinations of reed banks to change the instrument's timbre.
- **Reed rank**: A set of reeds tuned to the same pitch but possibly at different octaves, housed in a sounding bank.
- **Chromatic button accordion**: An accordion with right-hand buttons arranged chromatically, allowing play in any key.
- **Diatonic button accordion**: An accordion with right-hand buttons arranged for specific diatonic scales, often bisonoric.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact origin is debated; while Buschmann is credited with an 1822 invention, an instrument discovered in 2006 appears to have been built earlier.
- The extent to which inventors like Wheatstone were aware of and influenced by existing instruments like Müller's described types or Demian's patent is a matter of historical speculation.
- The accordion's decline in mid-20th century popular music is attributed to the rise of rock and roll, but its persistent and evolving role in folk, world, and classical music challenges a simple narrative of obsolescence.
