# Historical method

## Overview
Historical method is the set of techniques historians use to research and write about the past, involving the identification, evaluation, and synthesis of sources like primary documents, secondary accounts, and material evidence. It is fundamental to constructing reliable historical knowledge, though specific standards are not universally agreed upon, especially in contested fields like religion or social justice. The study of this method and its variations is called historiography.

## Timeline
- **5th century BC** — Herodotus, the "father of history," writes his histories.
- **5th century BC** — Thucydides develops a more critical method, emphasizing chronology and human causation.
- **145–90 BC** — Sima Qian establishes professional historiography in East Asia with the *Records of the Grand Historian*.
- **1377** — Ibn Khaldun's *Muqaddimah* warns of common historical errors and introduces a scientific approach.
- **1851** — Herbert Spencer critiques historians for neglecting scientific theory.
- **1889/1898** — Bernheim, Langlois & Seignobos propose a seven-step procedure for source criticism.
- **20th century** — Focus shifts from nationalistic narratives to social science approaches (e.g., Annales school).
- **1994** — Keith Windschuttle's *The Killing of History* defends historical practice against postmodern challenges.

## Body

### Source Criticism
Source criticism is the process of evaluating an information source's validity, reliability, and relevance. It involves a series of inquiries, often divided into external criticism (establishing authenticity) and internal criticism (assessing content credibility). Key questions include: When and where was the source produced? By whom? From what material? What is its evidential value? Historians like Louis Gottschalk stress that each particular in a document must be evaluated separately, even if the author is generally trustworthy.

### Evaluating Contradictory and Eyewitness Evidence
When sources conflict, historians follow procedures such as preferring eyewitness accounts, sources with greater authority, or those confirmed by independent sources. For eyewitness testimony, R. J. Shafer provides a checklist considering the observer's ability, potential bias, timing, and intention. Eyewitnesses are generally preferred, but their statements must be scrutinized for literal meaning, observational capacity, and inherent probability.

### Indirect Witnesses, Oral Tradition, and Anonymous Sources
Most historical information comes from indirect witnesses (secondary sources). Historians test these by tracing their basis in primary testimony. Oral tradition can be accepted if it meets conditions like an unbroken chain of witnesses, public importance, and lack of contemporary challenge. Anonymous texts are also used to establish facts.

### Historical Reasoning and Synthesis
After assessing sources, historians form hypotheses using reasoning methods. The "argument to the best explanation" (C. Behan McCullagh) requires a hypothesis to have greater explanatory scope, power, and plausibility than alternatives. Statistical inference uses probabilistic syllogisms from general patterns. Argument from analogy suggests hypotheses but is not conclusive unless it functions as a best explanation.

### Development of Historical Method
The method evolved from ancient Greek historians like Thucydides to sophisticated traditions in China and the Islamic world (Ibn Khaldun). In the West, modern methods developed in the 17th-18th centuries. Leopold von Ranke emphasized objective examination of sources in the 19th century. The 20th century saw a turn toward social science, quantitative history (Annales school), and multidisciplinary approaches, alongside debates about history as art versus science. Marxist, feminist, and postmodern perspectives have also shaped methodology.

## Terms
- **Historiography**: The study of historical method and the writing of history.
- **Source criticism**: The process of evaluating the qualities (validity, reliability) of an information source.
- **External criticism**: The inquiry into a source's authenticity, including date, location, authorship, and form.
- **Internal criticism**: The inquiry into the evidential value and credibility of a source's content.
- **Primary source**: An eyewitness account or document created at the time of the event.
- **Secondary source**: A document or account created after the event, often based on primary sources.
- **Argument to the best explanation**: A reasoning method where a hypothesis is accepted if it explains the evidence better than competing hypotheses.
- **Annales school**: A 20th-century French historical movement emphasizing quantitative data and long-term social history.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Historians agree on general principles but lack widely observed or agreed-upon specific canons of historical proof.
- Fields like religion, art, and social justice are considered "essentially contested," requiring diverse, field-specific interpretive tools.
- There is ongoing debate about whether history is a social science (seeking generalizations) or an art (relying on narrative and imagination).
- Postmodernists challenge the validity of historical study, arguing all history is based on personal interpretation of sources.
