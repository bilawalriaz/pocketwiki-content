# Belief

## Overview
Belief is a subjective mental attitude toward a proposition, treating it as true regardless of active introspection. In epistemology, beliefs are the primary bearers of truth-value and serve as the foundation for knowledge, traditionally analyzed as "justified true belief." Philosophers dispute the ontology of belief: representationalists view beliefs as internal mental representations (propositional attitudes); functionalists define them by their causal role linking perception to action; dispositionalists equate them with behavioral dispositions; and interpretationists (Dennett, Davidson) treat belief ascription as a predictive tool relative to an interpreter. Formal epistemologists challenge the bivalent "full belief" model with probabilistic "credences." Beliefs can be occurrent (active) or dispositional (stored), de dicto (sensitive to wording) or de re (transparent to reference), and individual or collective. They form holistic, revision-resistant systems influenced by upbringing, emotion, social pressure, and cognitive biases. Religious belief constitutes a codified subsystem often tied to identity, morality, and existential security. Psychology treats belief as a basic building block of cognition, while eliminativists (Churchland) argue the folk concept will be replaced by neuroscience.

## Timeline
- **Ancient Greece** — Plato’s *Theaetetus* examines knowledge as justified true belief; concepts of *pistis* (trust), *doxa* (opinion), *dogma* (school doctrine) distinguished.
- **17th c.** — René Descartes attempts to rebuild belief system from indubitable axioms; later cited as example of holistic conservatism.
- **Enlightenment** — "Justified true belief" becomes standard definition of knowledge; "justified" contrasted with "revealed."
- **1963** — Edmund Gettier publishes counterexamples showing justified true belief insufficient for knowledge.
- **1970s–80s** — Jerry Fodor defends representationalism and the language of thought hypothesis ("mentalese").
- **1980s** — Daniel Dennett develops the "intentional stance" (interpretationism); Hilary Putnam proposes twin Earth thought experiment for semantic externalism.
- **1986** — Paul and Patricia Churchland advance eliminativism: folk concept of belief will be discarded like phlogiston.
- **1990s–2000s** — Bayesian formal epistemology models belief as degrees of credence (0–1); Jonathan Glover analyzes belief systems as holistic, boat-like structures resistant to revision.
- **2013** — DSM-5 defines delusions as fixed false beliefs resistant to conflicting evidence.
- **2018** — Study estimates global belief in witchcraft varies 9–90% by nation, correlating with lower innovative activity and higher anxiety.

## Body

### Conceptions of Belief
**Representationalism** treats beliefs as internal mental representations—propositional attitudes with a mind-to-world direction of fit (aiming at truth). Beliefs differ from desires, which have a world-to-mind direction of fit. Two models of realization compete: the **language of thought hypothesis** (Fodor), where beliefs are discrete, syntactically structured "sentences" in "mentalese," and the **map-conception**, where the belief system is a holistic map encoding information geometrically.

**Functionalism** defines beliefs by their causal role—typically caused by perceptions and causing actions—regardless of physical realization (human, alien, computer). **Dispositionalism**, a functionalist variant, identifies beliefs solely with dispositions to behave (e.g., believing a pie is in the pantry disposes one to go there when hungry). Critics argue behavior is too complex to isolate a single belief’s contribution (e.g., one might hide the pie or avoid it if poisoned).

**Interpretationism** (Dennett, Davidson) holds that belief attribution is relative to an interpreter’s predictive strategy. Dennett’s **intentional stance** ascribes beliefs/desires to complex systems (chess players, computers) to predict behavior; multiple equally good interpretations may exist. Davidson’s **radical interpretation** argues a speaker really has the beliefs that make their behavior intelligible in principle. Interpretationism can support eliminativism (no real beliefs, but ascriptions are useful instruments) or realism (ascribed beliefs are causally efficacious).

### Types of Belief
**Occurrent vs. dispositional**: Occurrent beliefs are actively entertained; dispositional beliefs are stored and activated when relevant. Most beliefs are dispositional (e.g., "57 > 14" before reading this). This distinction overlaps with but does not equal conscious/unconscious; unconscious occurrent states can drive reasoning.

**Full vs. partial (credence)**: Full beliefs are all-or-nothing; partial beliefs admit degrees of certainty (credences), formalized as probabilities 0–1. **Bayesianism** uses probability laws to normatively govern credence revision. The **Lockean thesis** reduces full belief to credence above a threshold (e.g., >0.9); opponents reduce credence to full belief about probabilities ("I fully believe the probability of rain is 90%"). A third view treats both as full beliefs differing in stability (resistance to revision).

**Belief-that vs. belief-in**: Belief-that is a propositional attitude (true/false). Belief-in expresses trust/faith in persons or ideals (e.g., belief in God, marriage, oneself). Reductive accounts translate belief-in into belief-that (e.g., "God exists with attributes X"); non-reductive views hold belief-in adds an irreducible positive evaluative attitude.

**De dicto vs. de re**: Concerns substitution of co-referring terms in belief ascriptions. *De dicto* (referentially opaque): Lois believes Superman is strong but not Clark Kent (she doesn’t know they’re identical). *De re* (transparent): Lois does believe Clark Kent is strong, because he *is* Superman.

**Collective belief**: Not merely the sum of individual beliefs ("we believe" ≠ "we all believe"). Durkheim treated collective beliefs as social facts inhering in groups; Gilbert analyzes them as joint commitments to accept a proposition as a body, where individuals need not personally believe it. Collective beliefs function in social control and boundary-policing (heresy, deviationism).

**Lay theory**: Informal, common-sense explanations for social behavior, often diverging from scientific consensus, yet shaping interpretation, decision-making, and action.

### Contents: Internalism, Externalism, Holism
**Atomism** denies that a belief’s content depends on other beliefs; **molecularism** allows dependence among closely related beliefs; **holism** (Quine) holds any belief’s content depends on the entire web. Holism explains concept acquisition (learning "force" requires "mass") but risks making genuine disagreement impossible (disputants never share the exact web).

**Internalism** claims belief content supervenes on internal states (a molecule-for-molecule duplicate has identical beliefs). **Externalism** (Putnam’s twin Earth) argues content depends on environment: "water is wet" on Earth refers to H₂O; on twin Earth (where "water" is XYZ) it refers to XYZ, despite identical internal states. Critics note this content difference yields no behavioral/causal difference, questioning its explanatory value.

### Epistemology: Justification and Knowledge
The **justified true belief (JTB)** analysis (Plato’s *Theaetetus*, canonized in Enlightenment) requires: (1) P is true, (2) S believes P, (3) S is justified in believing P. **Gettier problems** (1963) show JTB is insufficient: one can have a justified true belief by luck (e.g., believing a stopped clock shows the right time). Responses include Nozick’s **tracking condition** (justification must be such that if P were false, S wouldn’t believe it) and **reliabilism** (belief formed by a reliable process). Many epistemologists consider JTB fatally flawed; others defend it as the standard Platonic account.

**Belief systems** (Glover) are mutually supportive networks (religious, political, ideological). No belief exists in isolation; revising one requires adjusting others. Systems resist total revision—like boats, not houses: you must keep enough intact to stay afloat. This explains the tenacity of "claptrap" systems (homeopathy, alien abduction) and the difficulty of radical doubt (Descartes rebuilt his system only to recover 17th-century French beliefs).

### Religion
Religious beliefs are codified, often concerning deities, divine intervention, or deontological teachings from spiritual authorities. **Forms**: *Fundamentalism* (strict scriptural adherence, anti-modernist); *Orthodoxy* (adherence to prevailing authority, e.g., Magisterium); *Modernism/Reform* (integrating Enlightenment rationality, e.g., Reform Judaism, Liberal Christianity).

**Attitudes to other religions**: *Exclusivism* (one true faith, others error/corruption; correlates with fundamentalism); *Inclusivism* (truth in all faiths, e.g., Baháʼí, New Age); *Pluralism* (each valid within its culture); *Syncretism* (blending traditions, e.g., Unitarian Universalism).

**Adherence motives**: Moral necessity, aesthetic/religious experience, community, promise of spiritual freedom/ultimate justice, existential security (Maslow: Holocaust survivors with strong beliefs coped better), emotional needs (love, belonging, explanation). **Apostasy motives**: Doctrines seen as illogical/unsupported; moral objections (inferiority of groups, eternal damnation); religious violence (Huguenots, Partition, Ireland, Israel-Palestine); incompatibility with personal values.

### Psychology
Mainstream psychology treats belief as a basic mental representation; philosophy scrutinizes its ontology. **Lynne Rudder Baker’s four approaches**:
1. **Mental sentence theory** (Fodor): Folk psychology is correct; beliefs are coherent entities.
2. **Instrumentalism** (Stich): Folk psychology is approximately predictively useful but ultimately false.
3. **Eliminativism** (Churchlands): Belief is a prescientific concept (like phlogiston) to be replaced by neuroscience.
4. **Intentional stance** (Dennett, Baker): Beliefs aren’t scientifically real, but treating systems as believers is a successful predictive strategy at a higher explanatory level.

**Belief formation/revision**: Models include *conditional inference* (weighting memory evidence), *linear/regression* (multiple weighted factors), and *information processing* (thoughts at encoding matter more than objective recall). Influences: childhood internalization (political/religious beliefs track community), charismatic leaders (cognitive dissonance resolves contradictions), advertising (repetition, emotion, sleeper effect), physical trauma. Yet even experts cling to beliefs against self-interest. Glover: beliefs can be held fixed via ad hoc hypotheses; systems are hard to change; awareness of contingency emerges ~age 12–15.

**Prediction**: Robert Wyer’s subjective probability model treats belief ratings as probabilities obeying standard laws; works for single events, less for conjunctive/disjunctive combinations.

**Delusion**: DSM-5 defines delusions as fixed false beliefs unresponsive to counterevidence.

**Belief studies**: Global witchcraft belief (9–90%) correlates with lower innovation, higher anxiety, lower life expectancy, higher religiosity. Misinformation beliefs resist correction via cognitive/social/affective vulnerabilities. "False social reality": widespread misperceptions of public opinion (e.g., underestimated U.S. support for climate policy). Psychedelics can shift beliefs toward panpsychism/fatalism and increased consciousness attribution.

**Emotion**: Research shows emotion and cognition jointly produce beliefs; emotion is vital to formation and maintenance.

## Terms
- **Propositional attitude**: A mental state directed at a proposition (e.g., believing, desiring) with a specific mode (mind-to-world or world-to-mind direction of fit).
- **Direction of fit**: The normative aim of a mental state: beliefs aim to match the world (mind-to-world); desires aim to change the world (world-to-mind).
- **Credence**: A graded degree of belief (0–1), interpreted as subjective probability in Bayesian epistemology.
- **Lockean thesis**: The view that full belief reduces to credence above a threshold (e.g., >0.9).
- **De dicto / de re**: Distinction in belief ascriptions: *de dicto* is sensitive to the wording/concept (referentially opaque); *de re* is transparent to the object referred to.
- **Intentional stance** (Dennett): Predictive strategy of ascribing beliefs/desires to a system to explain its behavior, without committing to their ontological reality.
- **Twin Earth** (Putnam): Thought experiment for semantic externalism: a duplicate Earth where "water" is XYZ shows belief content depends on external environment.
- **Confirmational holism** (Quine): Individual hypotheses cannot be confirmed in isolation; confirmation applies to the whole theoretical web.
- **Gettier problem**: Counterexamples where a subject has a justified true belief that is only accidentally true, challenging the JTB definition of knowledge.
- **Eliminativism** (Churchlands): The claim that the folk-psychological concept of "belief" refers to no real natural kind and will be eliminated by mature neuroscience.

## Debates and open questions
- **Ontology of belief**: Are beliefs real internal representations (Fodor), functional states (Putnam), behavioral dispositions (Chisholm), interpretive constructs (Dennett), or nonexistent (Churchlands)?
- **Full vs. partial belief**: Are credences fundamental (Lockean thesis) or reducible to full beliefs about probabilities? Can a unified theory accommodate both?
- **Content determination**: Internalism vs. externalism—does a molecule-for-molecule duplicate share all belief contents? If externalism is true, what is the causal relevance of wide content?
- **Holism vs. atomism**: Does belief content depend on the entire web (making disagreement nearly impossible) or only local connections?
- **Belief-in vs. belief-that**: Is religious belief-in reducible to belief-that (existence + attributes) or does it involve an irreducible evaluative/trust component?
- **Collective belief**: Can groups believe what no member believes individually? How must individualistic accounts of belief be revised?
- **Epistemic justification**: Post-Gettier, what replaces or repairs JTB? Tracking, reliabilism, virtue epistemology, or knowledge-first approaches?
- **Belief revision**: Why are belief systems so resistant to change (boat-like)? What roles do emotion, identity, and ad hoc hypotheses play in maintaining "claptrap"?
- **Neuroscience vs. folk psychology**: Will advances in neuroscience eliminate the concept of belief (eliminativism) or vindicate it in a revised form?
