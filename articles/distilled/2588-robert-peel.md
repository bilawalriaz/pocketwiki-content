# Robert Peel

## Overview
Sir Robert Peel (1788–1850) was a pivotal British statesman who served twice as Prime Minister and is considered the father of modern British policing and a founder of the modern Conservative Party. His career was defined by pragmatic reforms that often involved reversing his own party's traditional stances, most notably on Catholic emancipation and the repeal of the Corn Laws, which prioritized national economic health over party dogma.

## Timeline
- **5 Feb 1788** — Born in Bury, Lancashire, to a wealthy textile manufacturer.
- **1809** — Enters the House of Commons as MP for Cashel.
- **1822–1827** — First term as Home Secretary; reforms criminal law and founds the Metropolitan Police.
- **1828–1830** — Second term as Home Secretary; guides the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829 through Parliament.
- **1829** — Establishes the Metropolitan Police Force for London.
- **Dec 1834** — Becomes Prime Minister for the first time; issues the Tamworth Manifesto.
- **Apr 1835** — Resigns as Prime Minister after a vote of no confidence.
- **1841** — Wins a general election, forming a majority government for his second premiership.
- **1842** — Reintroduces a 3% income tax and begins major tariff reductions.
- **1844** — Passes the Factory Act, regulating hours for women and children.
- **1845–1846** — The Great Irish Famine influences his decision to repeal the Corn Laws.
- **29 Jun 1846** — Resigns as Prime Minister after the Corn Laws are repealed, splitting the Conservative Party.
- **2 Jul 1850** — Dies from injuries sustained in a riding accident.

## Body

### Early Life and Political Rise
Peel was the first Prime Minister from an industrial business background. He excelled academically, earning a double first at Oxford, and entered Parliament in 1809. He quickly rose through Tory ranks, serving as Chief Secretary for Ireland (1812–1818), where he laid the groundwork for the Royal Irish Constabulary. Initially a staunch opponent of Catholic emancipation, he reversed his position in 1829, arguing that "civil strife was a greater danger" than emancipation. This decision cost him the trust of many Tories but was crucial for political stability.

### Home Secretary and Policing
As Home Secretary (1822–1827, 1828–1830), Peel enacted significant legal reforms, reducing capital punishments and consolidating criminal statutes. His most enduring legacy was the creation of the Metropolitan Police Force in 1829. The force's civilian officers, nicknamed "bobbies" or "peelers," were designed to police by consent, establishing a model for modern policing.

### First Premiership and the Conservative Party
Peel's first term as Prime Minister (1834–1835) was a minority government. In 1834, he issued the Tamworth Manifesto, which laid the principles for the modern Conservative Party by endorsing modest reform and free trade. His government fell in 1835 after a vote of no confidence, and he spent the next six years as Leader of the Opposition.

### Second Premiership and Economic Reforms
Peel's second government (1841–1846) had a parliamentary majority and tackled an economic recession. His 1842 budget reintroduced a 3% income tax to fund the removal of over 1,200 tariffs, advancing free trade. His domestic reforms included the Factory Act 1844, which limited hours for women and children in factories. His administration also passed the Mines and Collieries Act 1842 and the Railway Regulation Act 1844.

### The Corn Laws and Resignation
The Great Irish Famine (1845–1849) prompted Peel to advocate for the repeal of the Corn Laws, which protected agricultural interests by restricting grain imports. He believed free trade was essential to alleviate the famine. The repeal bill passed in 1846 with Whig and Radical support, but it split the Conservative Party. Peel resigned after his Irish Coercion Bill was defeated by a coalition of his opponents.

### Later Career and Legacy
After 1846, Peel led a faction of "Peelites" who remained influential in Parliament, supporting further free trade measures like the repeal of the Navigation Acts. He died in 1850 from a riding accident. His Peelite followers eventually merged with the Whigs to form the Liberal Party. Peel is remembered as a pragmatic statesman who prioritized national interest over party loyalty, fundamentally reshaping British politics and institutions.

## Terms
*   **Tamworth Manifesto (1834):** A political statement by Peel that laid the foundation for the modern Conservative Party, endorsing moderate reform and free trade principles.
*   **Corn Laws:** Tariffs and restrictions on imported grain designed to protect domestic agricultural profits, repealed in 1846 under Peel's leadership.
*   **Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829:** Legislation that allowed Catholics to sit in Parliament, which Peel guided through the Commons despite his earlier opposition.
*   **Metropolitan Police:** The modern police force for London established by Peel in 1829, whose officers were nicknamed "bobbies" or "peelers."
*   **Peelites:** A political faction of Conservatives who followed Peel after the 1846 split over the Corn Laws, later merging with the Whigs.
*   **Factory Act 1844:** A law that restricted the working hours of women and children in factories and set basic safety standards.
*   **Income Tax:** A direct tax on personal income, reintroduced by Peel in 1842 at a rate of 3% to fund tariff reductions.
*   **Bedchamber Crisis (1839):** A political dispute where Queen Victoria refused Peel's request to dismiss some of her Whig-aligned household staff, preventing him from forming a government.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Motivation for Repealing the Corn Laws:** Historians debate whether Peel's primary motivation was humanitarian concern for the Irish Famine or a long-held intellectual commitment to free trade, using the famine as a political catalyst.
*   **Impact of the Famine:** The effectiveness of Peel's limited food subsidies during the famine is questioned, with some arguing they had little tangible effect due to the prevailing laissez-faire economic policies.
*   **Political Legacy:** Peel's career is analyzed as a tension between pragmatic national leadership and party loyalty; his reversals on key issues are seen by some as principled and by others as treacherous to the Tory cause.
