# John Jacob Astor

## Overview
John Jacob Astor (1763–1848) was a German-born immigrant who became the United States' first multi-millionaire by building a fur trade monopoly, engaging in the China trade (including opium), and investing heavily in Manhattan real estate. Arriving in the U.S. after the Revolutionary War, he leveraged the 1794 Jay Treaty to expand into the Great Lakes and Pacific Northwest, establishing the American Fur Company and the first U.S. settlement on the Pacific coast at Fort Astoria. Anticipating New York City's northward expansion, he liquidated his trading enterprises in the 1830s to become a dominant landlord. At his death, his estate represented roughly 1% of U.S. GDP, and his bequests founded the Astor Library, a core component of the New York Public Library.

## Body

### Early Life and Migration
Johann Jakob Astor was born in 1763 in Walldorf, near Heidelberg, the youngest son of a butcher. Family tradition held they were Waldensians who fled French religious persecution. At 16, he moved to London to join his brother George at their uncle’s piano and flute manufacturing firm, Astor & Broadwood, where he learned English and adopted the name John Jacob Astor. In November 1783, weeks after the Treaty of Paris ended the American Revolutionary War, he sailed for the United States, arriving in Baltimore by March 1784. He married his landlady’s daughter, Sarah Cox Todd, in 1785; she contributed a $300 dowry and, according to Astor, business judgment superior to most merchants, managing his affairs during his frequent absences.

### Building the Fur Trade Empire
Astor entered the fur trade after a chance shipboard meeting with a trader. He initially bought raw hides from Native Americans, processed them, and sold them in London. The 1794 **Jay Treaty** (a diplomatic agreement resolving post-war disputes and opening British North American ports to U.S. trade) was pivotal: Astor immediately contracted with the Montreal-based **North West Company** (a major fur trading consortium rivaling the Hudson's Bay Company) to import furs through New York to Europe. By 1800, he had accumulated over $250,000. Following the model of the *Empress of China* (the first U.S. vessel to trade with China), he entered the Canton trade, exchanging furs, sandalwood, and increasingly **opium** for tea and silks.

The **U.S. Embargo Act of 1807** (a Jefferson administration measure halting trade with Britain and Canada) crippled his Canadian supply lines. With President Jefferson’s explicit permission, Astor incorporated the **American Fur Company** on April 6, 1808, forming subsidiaries—the **Pacific Fur Company** and **Southwest Fur Company**—to control the Great Lakes and Columbia River regions. He financed the 1810–1812 **Astor Expedition** (overland and by sea) to establish **Fort Astoria** at the Columbia River mouth in April 1811, the first U.S. settlement on the Pacific coast. The overland party discovered **South Pass**, a wide, low crossing of the Rocky Mountains that later became the primary route for the Oregon, Mormon, and California Trails. The War of 1812 saw the British capture his Pacific posts, but the 1817 **protectionist law** barring foreign traders from U.S. territories restored his dominance, granting the American Fur Company a monopoly in the Great Lakes region, headquartered from 1822 at the Robert Stuart House on Mackinac Island.

### China Trade and Opium
Astor’s China trade began around 1800 at the port of Canton. While trading legitimate goods like furs and ginseng, he "greatly benefited" from the opium trade. In 1816, he formally entered opium smuggling: the American Fur Company purchased ten tons of Ottoman-produced opium and shipped it to Canton aboard the packet ship *Macedonian*. He later withdrew from the Chinese opium market, restricting his opium sales to Britain. This trade provided the capital surge that financed his pivot to real estate.

### Real Estate Investment and Retirement
Astor bought his first New York waterfront land in 1799. After 1800, flush with China profits, he invested systematically. In 1803, he purchased a 70-acre farm (Hellgate, between 42nd and 46th Streets, Broadway to Hudson River) and acquired additional holdings from the disgraced former Vice President Aaron Burr. In the 1830s, foreseeing Manhattan’s inevitable northward expansion, he sold the American Fur Company and all other ventures. He purchased vast tracts beyond the city limits, rarely building but leasing land for rent, creating a perpetual income stream. He retired to patronage, supporting ornithologist John James Audubon and Henry Clay’s presidential campaigns.

### Personal Life, Death, and Legacy
Astor and Sarah had eight children; several died young. His eldest son, John Jr., was "sickly and mentally unstable," so the bulk of the estate went to second son William Backhouse Astor Sr. Astor died March 29, 1848, the wealthest man in the U.S., leaving $20–30 million. His will bequeathed $400,000 for the **Astor Library** (later merged into the New York Public Library), $50,000 for a poorhouse in Walldorf, and $20,000 to the German Society of New York. His name endures in New York’s Astor Place and Astoria, Queens; Astoria, Oregon (site of Fort Astoria); Astor Park, Green Bay, Wisconsin; and the marble lions "Patience" and "Fortitude" at the New York Public Library (originally named Leo Astor and Leo Lenox).

## Terms
- **Jay Treaty (1794)**: Agreement between U.S. and Great Britain resolving post-Revolutionary War issues; opened British North American ports to U.S. commerce, enabling Astor's Great Lakes expansion.
- **North West Company**: Montreal-based fur trading consortium (rival to Hudson's Bay Company) that partnered with Astor to supply furs from the Canadian interior.
- **American Fur Company (1808)**: Astor's chartered enterprise, formed with Jefferson's approval to circumvent the Embargo Act; later dominated Great Lakes trade after 1817 protectionist law.
- **Pacific Fur Company (1810)**: American Fur Company subsidiary created to exploit the Columbia River watershed and Pacific coast trade.
- **Fort Astoria (1811)**: Pacific Fur Company post at the Columbia River mouth; first U.S. settlement on the Pacific coast; later sold to North West Company during War of 1812.
- **South Pass**: Rocky Mountain crossing discovered by Astor's 1810–1812 overland expedition; became the main gateway for westward migration on the Oregon, Mormon, and California Trails.
- **Embargo Act of 1807**: U.S. law prohibiting American ships from trading in foreign ports; devastated Astor's Canadian import/export business, prompting creation of American Fur Company.
- **Protectionist Law of 1817**: Congressional act barring foreign fur traders from U.S. territories; secured American Fur Company's monopoly in the Great Lakes region.
- **Astor Library (1848)**: Public research library funded by Astor's $400,000 bequest; consolidated with Lenox Library and Tilden Trust in 1895 to form the New York Public Library.
- **Waldensians**: Pre-Reformation Christian sect persecuted in France; Astor family tradition claims descent from Waldensian refugees.

## Debates and open questions
- **Scale and nature of opium trade**: The source notes Astor "greatly benefited" from opium at Canton by 1800 and shipped 10 tons in 1816, but the full extent, duration, and profitability of his narcotics trafficking versus legitimate China trade (furs, ginseng, sandalwood) are not quantified.
- **Monopoly mechanisms**: The text describes the American Fur Company’s agents as "ruthless in competition" and notes the 1817 law barred foreign traders, but does not detail specific anti-competitive practices (e.g., predatory pricing, supply control, political lobbying) used to absorb competitors.
- **Native American relations**: The source states Astor purchased furs "from Native Americans" and his agents operated "throughout the western areas," but does not describe the terms of trade, dependency creation, or role in displacement—central questions in modern scholarship on the fur trade.
- **Aaron Burr land purchases (1803–1804)**: The source mentions Astor bought "considerable holdings" from the "disgraced" former Vice President but does not specify the acreage, price, location, or whether the transaction was investigated given Burr’s treason trial context.
- **GDP proportion methodology**: The claim that Astor’s estate represented 0.9–1.35% of U.S. GDP cites a 1839–1840 campaign remark by Nathaniel P. Tallmadge ("one in every 100 dollars"), but the source does not explain the economic methodology behind the modern percentage calculation.
