# Nuclear arms race

## Overview
The nuclear arms race was a Cold War competition for nuclear supremacy between the United States, the Soviet Union, and their allies. It began with the Manhattan Project, escalated with thermonuclear weapons, and was defined by the doctrine of Mutual Assured Destruction (MAD), which paradoxically helped prevent direct superpower conflict. The race involved warhead development and diverse delivery systems, later joined by other nuclear-armed states.

## Timeline
- **1945** — US atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki; US nuclear monopoly begins.
- **1949** — Soviet Union tests its first atomic bomb (RDS-1), ending the US monopoly.
- **1952** — UK tests its first atomic bomb; US tests first thermonuclear device (Ivy Mike).
- **1953** — Soviet Union tests a deployable thermonuclear device.
- **1957** — UK tests its first hydrogen bomb; Soviet Union launches Sputnik 1, demonstrating ICBM capability.
- **1960** — France tests its first atomic bomb.
- **1961** — Soviet Union detonates the Tsar Bomba, the largest nuclear weapon ever tested.
- **1962** — Cuban Missile Crisis brings the world to the brink of nuclear war.
- **1963** — Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty ends atmospheric testing.
- **1964** — China tests its first atomic bomb.
- **1968** — Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) is opened for signature.
- **1972** — SALT I and the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty are signed.
- **1987** — Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty eliminates a class of missiles.
- **1991** — START I treaty is signed; Soviet Union dissolves, ending the Cold War arms race.
- **2010** — New START treaty is signed, further reducing US and Russian arsenals.
- **2019** — US withdraws from the INF Treaty.
- **2023** — Russia suspends participation in the New START treaty.

## Body

### World War II Origins
The race began during World War II with the Allied Manhattan Project, which developed the first nuclear weapons. The Soviet Union, aided by atomic spies, accelerated its own program after the war. The US atomic bombings of Japan in 1945 demonstrated the weapon's power and initiated the post-war nuclear age.

### Early Cold War: Warhead Development and Delivery
Following the war, the US held a nuclear monopoly until the Soviet Union tested its first bomb in 1949. This triggered an all-out effort to develop more powerful thermonuclear (hydrogen) bombs, first achieved by the US in 1952 and the USSR in 1953. Competition shifted to delivery vehicles, evolving from strategic bombers to intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs) and submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs), creating the nuclear "triad." This led to the doctrine of **Mutual Assured Destruction (MAD)**, where both sides possessed a guaranteed second-strike capability, making a first attack suicidal.

### Proliferation and Crises
The UK, France, and China developed nuclear weapons during the 1950s and 1960s. The most dangerous confrontation occurred during the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis, when the US discovered Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba. A tense standoff ended with a secret agreement: the USSR withdrew the missiles, and the US later removed its Jupiter missiles from Turkey.

### Détente and Arms Control
The 1970s saw a period of reduced tensions called **détente**, marked by arms control treaties. The 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty ended atmospheric testing. The 1972 SALT I treaty froze the number of ICBMs and SLBMs and limited anti-ballistic missile systems. However, the development of multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicles (MIRVs) allowed single missiles to carry many warheads, undermining these limits.

### Renewed Tensions and the End of the Cold War
In the 1980s, tensions renewed with the US deployment of new missiles in Europe and President Reagan's Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI), a proposed space-based missile defense system. Under Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, negotiations resumed, leading to the 1987 INF Treaty, which eliminated an entire class of missiles, and the 1991 START I treaty, which began deep reductions in strategic arsenals. The dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991 ended the Cold War arms race.

### Post-Cold War and 21st Century
Russia and the US maintained the world's largest stockpiles, with treaties like New START (2010) continuing reductions. However, tensions have resurged. The US withdrew from the ABM Treaty (2002) and INF Treaty (2019), and Russia suspended New START participation (2023). A new competition is emerging, particularly with China's nuclear expansion and the development of hypersonic weapons.

### Other Nuclear-Armed States
India and Pakistan have engaged in their own arms race since India's 1974 "peaceful nuclear explosion." Both tested weapons in 1998. Other countries that developed nuclear weapons during the Cold War include Israel, South Africa, and North Korea.

## Terms
- **Mutual Assured Destruction (MAD):** A doctrine where a full-scale nuclear use by two sides would cause the complete annihilation of both attacker and defender.
- **Thermonuclear Weapon:** A nuclear weapon using a fusion reaction, with yields vastly greater than fission bombs.
- **Intercontinental Ballistic Missile (ICBM):** A long-range ballistic missile with a minimum range of 5,500 km, designed to deliver nuclear warheads.
- **Submarine-Launched Ballistic Missile (SLBM):** A ballistic missile capable of being launched from a submarine.
- **Nuclear Triad:** A three-pronged force structure of strategic bombers, land-based ICBMs, and submarine-based SLBMs.
- **MIRV (Multiple Independently Targetable Re-entry Vehicle):** A missile payload containing several warheads, each aimed at a different target.
- **Détente:** The easing of strained political relations between the US and USSR during the Cold War.
- **Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM):** A missile designed to counter ballistic missiles in their flight path.
- **Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT):** An international treaty to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons and weapons technology.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The effectiveness of the Baruch Plan (1946) and other early disarmament proposals is debated, as both were rejected by the UN.
- The actual size of the "bomber gap" and "missile gap" in the 1950s and 1960s was a subject of political accusation and intelligence failure, with later evidence showing the gaps were largely exaggerated or non-existent.
- The strategic necessity and technological feasibility of the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI) were highly contested during the 1980s.
- The current trajectory of the nuclear arms race is uncertain, with concerns about a "Second Cold War" driven by US-Russia tensions, China's expansion, and the breakdown of arms control treaties.
