# Josquin des Prez

## Overview
Josquin Lebloitte dit des Prez (c. 1450–1455 – 27 August 1521) was a Franco-Flemish composer widely regarded as the central figure of the Franco-Flemish School and the first Western composer to retain enduring posthumous fame. Building on predecessors like Johannes Ockeghem, he revolutionized Renaissance polyphony by replacing lengthy melismatic lines with short, imitative motivic cells that moved fluidly between independent voices, creating structural unity and expressive text-setting. His career spanned the major courts of Europe—including Milan, Rome, Ferrara, and the French royal chapel—where his music was printed extensively by Ottaviano Petrucci. Josquin’s synthesis of intellectual rigor and expressive fantasy established the stylistic norms for 16th-century composers such as Palestrina and Lassus, though modern scholarship debates whether his historical stature has been inflated at the expense of contemporaries like Jacob Obrecht.

## Timeline
- **c. 1450–1455** — Born in northeastern France or Belgium (possibly near the Haine or Escaut rivers); father Gossard Lebloitte dit des Prez, a policeman at Ath.
- **19 April 1477** — First firm record: singer in the chapel of René of Anjou at Aix-en-Provence.
- **February 1483** — Returns to Condé-sur-l'Escaut to claim inheritance from aunt and uncle (possibly killed in 1478 siege by Louis XI).
- **20 June 1484** — Enters service of Cardinal Ascanio Sforza in Milan; receives dispensation as rector of Saint Aubin.
- **June 1489 – April 1494** — Member of the papal choir in Rome under Popes Innocent VIII and Alexander VI; graffito "JOSQUINJ" later found in Sistine Chapel.
- **c. 1498–1503** — Period in France; likely serves Louis XII; exchanges canonry at Saint-Quentin (confirmed 30 May 1503).
- **30 May 1503** — Arrives in Ferrara to serve Ercole I d'Este at 200 ducats (highest ducal salary); writes *Miserere* and *Missa Hercules Dux Ferrariae*.
- **April 1504** — Leaves Ferrara due to plague outbreak; appointed provost of Notre-Dame, Condé-sur-l'Escaut (3 May 1504).
- **1504–1521** — Final years in Condé; composes late masterpieces (*Missa Pange lingua*, *Pater noster–Ave Maria*, *Mille regretz*); works published by Petrucci.
- **27 August 1521** — Dies in Condé; buried before high altar (tomb later destroyed).
- **1971** — International Josquin Festival-Conference cements his central place in Renaissance music history.
- **2021** — 500th death anniversary widely commemorated.

## Body

### Biography and Career
Josquin’s early life is obscure; he was likely born c. 1450–1455 in the modern Franco-Belgian border region. A 1483 Condé document identifies him as the son of Gossard Lebloitte dit des Prez and nephew of Gille Lebloitte dit des Prez. He may have been a choirboy at Cambrai or Saint-Quentin, though no records survive. The first certain employment is April 1477 in René of Anjou’s chapel at Aix-en-Provence. After a five-year gap (1478–1483), he entered Cardinal Ascanio Sforza’s household in Milan (June 1484), traveling to Rome with him. From June 1489 to at least April 1494, Josquin sang in the papal choir, acquiring multiple benefices across France and the Low Countries. A lacuna in papal records (1494–1500) obscures his movements, but he likely served Louis XII of France c. 1498–1503, evidenced by a canonry exchange at Saint-Quentin. In May 1503, Ercole I d'Este recruited him to Ferrara at a record 200 ducats; a courtier’s letter noted Josquin “composes when he wants to and not when one wants him to.” He fled the 1503 plague, becoming provost of Notre-Dame in Condé (May 1504), where he administered a large chapter and composed his final works. He died in 1521, endowing performances of his *Pater noster* at processions past his house.

### Musical Style and Innovations
Josquin was a professional singer whose output is almost exclusively vocal (masses, motets, chansons). Musicologists credit him with three pivotal shifts from the Du Fay/Ockeghem generation: (1) replacing extended melismas with short, recognizable **motivic cells** (melodic fragments) passed imitatively among voices for inner unity; (2) perfecting **imitative polyphony** where all voices participate equally, merging rational structure with rhythmic drive; (3) prioritizing **text expression** (early **word painting**), letting music serve textual meaning. Jeremy Noble argues these innovations bridge early Renaissance style to the High Renaissance of Willaert, Arcadelt, Palestrina, and Lassus. Richard Taruskin cautions that a reliable chronology remains elusive due to source loss and the complexity of attribution.

### Masses
Josquin composed roughly 18–20 masses, mostly for four voices. *The Josquin Companion* categorizes them by technique:
*   **Canonic masses:** *Missa ad fugam* (early, strict canon in top voice) and *Missa sine nomine* (late, flexible canon with free voices integrated).
*   **Cantus firmus masses:** Early *Missa L'ami Baudichon* (secular tune); two famous *L'homme armé* settings—*super voces musicales* (technical tour de force with mensuration canons on each hexachord note) and *sexti toni* (fantasia with fragments in all voices, closing with retrograde canon).
*   **Paraphrase masses:** Source melody embellished across all voices. *Missa Ave maris stella* (early, short); *Missa de Beata Virgine* (late, Lady Mass, most popular in 16th century); *Missa Pange lingua* (probable last mass, extended fantasia on Aquinas’s hymn, blending fantasy and control in a new synthesis).
*   **Parody masses:** Based on polyphonic models, using motifs from all voices. *Missa Di dadi* (dice notation for rhythmic augmentation); *Missa Fortuna desperata* (each voice of Italian song used as cantus firmus); *Missa Mater Patris* (earliest true parody mass, no cantus firmus trace); *Missa D'ung aultre amer* (on Ockeghem’s chanson).
*   **Solmization masses:** *Missa Hercules Dux Ferrariae* (soggetto cavato: vowels of "Hercules Dux Ferrarie" yield Re–Ut–Re–Ut–Re–Fa–Mi–Re); *Missa La sol fa re mi* (on "laisse faire moy").

### Motets
Josquin’s motets are his most celebrated works, varying from homophonic block chords to dense imitative fantasias and psalm settings with rhetorical text-painting foreshadowing the madrigal. He standardized four-voice writing but innovated with five and six voices. Structural devices include cantus firmus, canon (sometimes audible, sometimes hidden), and recurring **motto** themes. Imitation is pervasive: *Ave Maria ... Virgo serena* (c. 1485) features fugal entries on each textual line. He pioneered polyphonic psalm settings—*Miserere* (Psalm 51, Savonarola-influenced, widely distributed), *Memor esto verbi tui* (Psalm 119), two *De profundis* (Psalm 130). He also wrote **motet-chansons** (e.g., *Que vous madame/In pace*), combining a Latin chant cantus firmus in the lowest voice with secular French texts in the upper voices.

### Secular Music
Josquin left numerous French **chansons** (3–6 voices), often using cantus firmus or strict imitation rather than rigid *formes fixes* (rondeau, virelai, ballade). He equalized voices and used melodic repetition at rhymes. Famous examples: *Nymphes des bois* (lament for Ockeghem, quoting his *Alma Redemptoris mater*), *Mille regretz*, *Nymphes, nappés*, *Plus nulz regretz* (on a poem by Jean Lemaire de Belges celebrating Charles V and Mary Tudor). Some chansons (e.g., *Vive le roy*) appear idiomatic for instruments. In Italy he adopted the **frottola**—simple, syllabic, homophonic songs like *El grillo* and *In te domine speravi*—which remain among his most performed pieces.

### Reception and Legacy
During his life, Josquin was praised by Gaffurius, Molinet, and Aron; Petrucci issued three mass anthologies (1502, 1504, 1514), the first single-composer collections. Posthumously, Luther called him “master of the notes”; Glarean and Zarlino enshrined him in theory. In the Baroque era, Palestrina eclipsed him. The 19th-century revival (Ambros, Forkel, Fétis) restored his stature. The 1971 International Josquin Festival-Conference and Lowinsky’s 1976 monograph cemented his centrality. The New Josquin Edition began in 1987.

### Revisionist Debates
Since the 1990s, scholarship has sharply reduced the canon (Grove 2001 lists <200 works vs. >370 previously). Biography has been rewritten due to confusion with namesakes (e.g., Josquin Dor, Johannes de Stokem). Some scholars (Wegman) argue Obrecht was more esteemed in Josquin’s time; others (Noble) cite Josquin’s elite posts and publications as proof of contemporary renown. Sherr concludes that while the “apotheosis” is tempered, Josquin remains a “towering figure” on the basis of his core masterpieces. His music is now central to early-music ensembles (Hilliard, Orlando Consort, Tallis Scholars), with the 2021 quincentenary prompting global commemoration.

## Terms
- ****Polyphony**** — Musical texture with two or more simultaneous independent melodic lines (voices).
- ****Motivic cell**** — A short, recognizable melodic fragment used as a building block, passed imitatively between voices for structural unity.
- ****Imitative polyphony**** — Polyphony where voices enter successively with the same or similar melodic material (like a fugue exposition).
- ****Cantus firmus**** — A pre-existing melody (often chant) used as the structural foundation of a polyphonic composition, typically in one voice.
- ****Paraphrase mass**** — A mass where a monophonic source tune is embellished and distributed across all voices, rather than confined to one.
- ****Parody mass**** — A mass based on a polyphonic model (chanson or motet), borrowing motifs and imitation points from all its voices.
- ****Soggetto cavato**** — (Carved-out subject) A technique where the solmization syllables of a name or phrase (e.g., vowels in *Hercules Dux Ferrarie*) generate the cantus firmus.
- ****Motet-chanson**** — A hybrid genre: lowest voice sings a Latin chant cantus firmus; upper voices sing a secular French text symbolically related.
- ****Formes fixes**** — Fixed poetic/musical forms of French secular song (rondeau, virelai, ballade) with strict repetition patterns.
- ****Frottola**** — A popular Italian secular song form (late 15th–early 16th c.), typically syllabic, homophonic, and simpler than the chanson.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Birthplace and date:** “Beyond Noir Eauwe” (Black Water) in a legal document is variously interpreted as the L'Eau Noire river (Ardennes), the Haine river (coal transport), or the Escaut near Condé; c. 1450–1455 remains approximate.
*   **Early training:** No documentary evidence; possible links to Cambrai Cathedral or Saint-Quentin (records destroyed 1669); study with Ockeghem is a later inference, not documented.
*   **Service in Hungary:** Cardinal Aleandro (1539) reports Archbishop Varday naming Josquin at Matthias Corvinus’s court in Vienna (1485–1489); Fallows finds it plausible but no original documents confirm it.
*   **Sistine Chapel graffito:** “JOSQUINJ” discovered 1997–98; likely Josquin des Prez but not a certain autograph (could be “Josquinus”; other Josquins signed fully).
*   **Chronology of works:** Taruskin states modern scholarship is “nowhere near a wholly reliable chronology”; few pre-1500 manuscripts survive; dating relies on contextual guesswork.
*   **Canon and attribution:** Works dropped from >370 to <200 (Grove 2001) due to confusion with similarly named composers (Josquin Dor, de Stokem) and misattribution by publishers exploiting his fame.
*   **Historical stature:** Whether Josquin’s modern “greatest of the Renaissance” status reflects contemporary reality or a 19th/20th-century construct (Beethoven analogy) that marginalizes Obrecht, Isaac, and others.
