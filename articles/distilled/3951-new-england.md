# New England

## Overview
New England is a region of six northeastern U.S. states (Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, and Vermont) with a distinct cultural identity shaped by early Puritan settlement, a pivotal role in the American Revolution, and a pioneering industrial economy. It is the nation's most densely populated and historically significant region, known for its educational institutions, political liberalism, and transition from manufacturing to a high-tech and service-based economy.

## Timeline
- **c. 10,500 BC** — Humans first reach the region after glacial retreat.
- **1620** — Pilgrims establish Plymouth Colony, the second successful British settlement in America.
- **1630** — Puritans establish Massachusetts Bay Colony, with Boston as its major town.
- **1636** — Roger Williams founds Providence Plantations (Rhode Island) after banishment from Massachusetts.
- **1675-1678** — King Philip's War, a major conflict between colonists and Native American tribes.
- **1763** — British defeat of French in French and Indian Wars opens the Connecticut River Valley for settlement.
- **1773** — Boston Tea Party protests British taxation.
- **1775** — Battles of Lexington and Concord mark the start of the American Revolutionary War.
- **1787** — First American cotton mill founded in Beverly, Massachusetts.
- **1820** — Maine achieves statehood as part of the Missouri Compromise.
- **1920s-1970s** — Deindustrialization leads to the decline of textile and shoe manufacturing.
- **1992-present** — Region becomes a consistent stronghold for the Democratic Party in presidential elections.

## Body

### History and Settlement
Humans inhabited the region by at least 10,500 years ago. Prior to European contact, it was home to Algonquian-speaking tribes like the Abenaki, Wampanoag, and Pequot. English colonization began with the 1620 Plymouth Colony and the 1629 Massachusetts Bay Colony. The region's name was officially sanctioned in 1620. Settlement was driven by religious groups, including Puritans and, later, Roger Williams's followers seeking religious tolerance. The colonies fought in a series of French and Indian Wars against New France and its Native allies until the British victory in 1763.

### Revolution and Early Nationhood
New England colonists led resistance to British taxation, exemplified by the Boston Tea Party (1773). Britain's punitive "Intolerable Acts" against Massachusetts led to the first battles of the Revolutionary War in 1775. The region was a center of the abolitionist movement, with Vermont and Massachusetts abolishing slavery by 1783. Politically, it was initially a Federalist stronghold, later becoming the heart of the Whig Party. The Hartford Convention (1814) saw New England Federalists debate grievances and even secession over the War of 1812.

### Industrial Revolution and Deindustrialization
New England was the first U.S. region to industrialize, beginning with textile mills in the Blackstone and Merrimack river valleys in the late 18th century. Towns like Lowell, Massachusetts, became manufacturing centers, initially relying on rural "mill girls" and later on immigrants. By 1850, it was the nation's most industrialized and literate region. This industrial base collapsed in the 20th century due to competition and offshoring, leading to economic decay in former mill towns. The economy has since transformed into one based on high technology, finance, education, and healthcare.

### Geography and Climate
The region's landscape was shaped by glacial retreat, featuring a coastal plain, rolling hills, and the northern Appalachian Mountains. The Connecticut River bisects the region. The climate varies from long, cold winters with heavy snow in the north to milder, shorter winters in the south. Southern coastal areas have a transition to a subtropical climate.

### Demographics and Politics
New England is the most densely populated U.S. region, with over three-quarters of its population in the southern states. It is predominantly white, with Irish, English, and Italian as the top European ancestries. The region is the least religious in the U.S. Politically, it has shifted from Republican to become the most reliably Democratic region in the country, with all six states voting for the Democratic presidential nominee in every election since 1992 (except New Hampshire in 2000). It is known for its tradition of town meeting government, a form of direct democracy.

### Economy and Education
The modern economy is driven by services, including higher education, medicine, finance, and technology. Boston is a global financial center. Agriculture is limited but includes dairy, potatoes, and cranberries. The region is home to some of the world's most prestigious universities, including Harvard (founded 1636), Yale (1701), and MIT. It also has a strong tradition of elite preparatory schools.

## Terms
- **Town Meeting:** A form of direct democratic local government in many New England towns where citizens gather to vote on issues.
- **Puritan:** A member of a group of English Protestants seeking to "purify" the Church of England, who were the dominant early settlers of Massachusetts.
- **Algonquian:** A family of Native American languages spoken by tribes throughout New England, such as the Wampanoag and Abenaki.
- **French and Indian Wars:** A series of colonial conflicts (1689-1763) primarily between New England and New France, with their respective Native American allies.
- **Intolerable Acts:** Punitive laws passed by Britain in 1774 to punish Massachusetts for the Boston Tea Party, which galvanized colonial resistance.
- **Deindustrialization:** The decline of industrial activity in a region, as seen in New England's 20th-century loss of textile and shoe manufacturing.
- **BosWash:** The megalopolis stretching from Boston to Washington, D.C., of which southern New England is an integral part.
- **NEIPA:** New England India Pale Ale, a style of beer developed in Vermont in the 2010s.

## Debates and Open Questions
The effectiveness and applicability of the New England town meeting model of direct democracy is still discussed by scholars. The region's political transformation from a Republican to a Democratic stronghold is attributed to demographic shifts and the Republican Party's adoption of socially conservative platforms in the late 20th century. The economic future of former industrial cities that were slow to adapt to the service economy remains a point of analysis.
