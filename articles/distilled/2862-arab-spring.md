# Arab Spring

## Overview
The Arab Spring was a series of pro-democracy protests, uprisings, and armed rebellions that spread across the Arab world in the early 2010s. Sparked by the self-immolation of a Tunisian street vendor, the movement challenged entrenched authoritarian regimes, leading to the ousting of several long-standing leaders. While it initially raised hopes for democratic reform, the aftermath was marked by political instability, civil wars, and the rise of new conflicts, a period often termed the "Arab Winter."

## Timeline
- **17 Dec 2010** — Mohamed Bouazizi self-immolates in Tunisia, sparking protests.
- **14 Jan 2011** — Tunisian President Zine El Abidine Ben Ali flees to Saudi Arabia.
- **25 Jan 2011** — Egyptian protests begin, leading to Hosni Mubarak's resignation on 11 Feb.
- **15 Feb 2011** — Anti-government protests begin in Libya, escalating into civil war.
- **14 Mar 2011** — GCC forces, led by Saudi Arabia, intervene in Bahrain to suppress protests.
- **23 Aug 2011** — Libyan rebels capture Tripoli; Muammar Gaddafi is killed in October.
- **27 Feb 2012** — Ali Abdullah Saleh formally transfers power in Yemen.
- **3 Jul 2013** — Egyptian military overthrows President Mohamed Morsi.
- **2018–2019** — A "Second Arab Spring" leads to leadership changes in Iraq, Lebanon, Sudan, and Algeria.
- **8 Dec 2024** — The Assad regime in Syria collapses after a rebel offensive.

## Body

### Causes and Catalysts
The uprisings were driven by widespread dissatisfaction with authoritarian rule, political corruption, economic decline, unemployment, and demographic pressures, particularly a large, educated but underemployed youth population. The immediate catalyst was the self-immolation of Tunisian street vendor Mohamed Bouazizi on 17 December 2010, which galvanized protests against police corruption and ill-treatment. Social media platforms like Facebook and Twitter played a significant role in organizing protests and spreading information, though their influence is debated, especially in countries with low internet penetration like Yemen and Libya.

### Protests and Regime Changes
The wave of unrest began in Tunisia and quickly spread to other countries. By early 2012, rulers had been forced from power in Tunisia, Egypt, Libya, and Yemen. In Bahrain, protests were met with a violent crackdown and a military intervention by Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) forces. Major sustained demonstrations also occurred in countries like Morocco, Iraq, Algeria, and Jordan. The movement was not led by Islamists, though they participated; in Egypt, the Muslim Brotherhood joined reluctantly after pressure from its youth members.

### The Syrian Civil War and Regional Conflicts
Protests in Syria, beginning in March 2011, were brutally suppressed by the Assad regime, escalating into a devastating civil war that caused hundreds of thousands of deaths and a massive refugee crisis. The conflict drew in regional and international powers. The war concluded in December 2024 with the fall of the Assad regime. The instability also contributed to the rise of the Islamic State (ISIS) in Iraq and Syria, and prolonged civil wars in Libya and Yemen, where a Saudi-led intervention began in 2015.

### Outcomes and the "Arab Winter"
The initial revolutionary wave was followed by a period of instability, violence, and counter-revolution known as the "Arab Winter." Outcomes varied by country: Tunisia achieved a democratic transition but faced economic struggles; Egypt experienced a military coup and a return to authoritarianism; Syria, Libya, and Yemen descended into prolonged civil wars. The strength of state institutions, the role of the military, and the degree of media censorship were key factors in determining whether uprisings led to reform, regime change, or conflict.

### Second Arab Spring and Ongoing Legacy
A new wave of protests in 2018–2019, sometimes called the "Second Arab Spring," led to the resignation of prime ministers in Iraq and Lebanon and the overthrow of presidents in Sudan and Algeria. This demonstrated that the underlying grievances of the original Arab Spring persisted. As of 2025, conflicts originating from the period continue, and the region remains marked by political repression, sectarian tensions, and struggles for social justice, though the movements also fostered a lasting legacy of challenging fear and demanding change.

## Terms
- **Arab Spring**: The series of anti-government protests and uprisings across the Arab world beginning in 2010.
- **Arab Winter**: A term for the aftermath of the Arab Spring, characterized by civil wars, instability, and the return of authoritarianism.
- **Self-immolation**: The act of setting oneself on fire as a form of protest, as done by Mohamed Bouazizi.
- **GCC (Gulf Cooperation Council)**: A regional intergovernmental political and economic union of Arab states bordering the Persian Gulf.
- **Muslim Brotherhood**: An Islamist organization founded in Egypt that played a significant political role after the 2011 uprising.
- **Deep State**: A type of governance where secretive networks within the military and government control state policy independently of elected leaders.
- **Sectarianism**: Prejudice, discrimination, or hatred arising from attaching differences between subgroups of the same religion, such as Sunni and Shia Islam.
- **Counter-revolution**: Organized efforts to reverse the results of a revolution and restore the old regime.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Role of Social Media**: Scholars debate whether social media was a primary driver of the uprisings or merely a tool for organization and communication, especially in countries with low internet access.
- **Revolutionary vs. Reformist Nature**: Some analysts, like Asef Bayat, argue the movements lacked a revolutionary intellectual anchor and were more about "refolution" (reform through revolution) than systemic change, as they did not aim to capture the state.
- **Sectarianism as a Primary Driver**: While often cited, some scholars like Hussein Agha and Robert Malley argue that sectarianism is a "politically expedient fable" used to mask power struggles, noting that the bloodiest conflicts occurred within the Sunni world.
- **Impact of Foreign Intervention**: The degree to which foreign powers (e.g., Saudi Arabia in Bahrain, international coalitions in Libya) shaped outcomes versus internal dynamics remains a key question.
- **Long-Term Legacy**: Whether the Arab Spring ultimately advanced democratic ideals or primarily led to repression and conflict is an ongoing assessment, with the "Second Arab Spring" suggesting the struggle for political change continues.
