# Nikolai Gogol

## Overview
Nikolai Gogol (1809–1852) was a Russian novelist, short-story writer, and playwright of Ukrainian origin whose grotesque, proto-surrealist fiction reshaped Russian and world literature. Born in the Cossack town of Sorochyntsi, he moved to St. Petersburg in 1828, where his early collections *Evenings on a Farm Near Dikanka* (1831–32) and *Mirgorod* (1835) blended Ukrainian folklore with sophisticated satire. His comedy *The Government Inspector* (1836), staged only through Tsar Nicholas I’s intervention, satirized provincial bureaucracy, while *Dead Souls* (1842)—planned as a Dantean trilogy depicting Inferno, Purgatory, and Paradise—established him as a master of prose. Gogol’s technique of *defamiliarization* (presenting the familiar as strange) and his concept of *poshlost’* (spiritual triviality) influenced writers from Dostoevsky and Kafka to Nabokov and Bulgakov. His later years were marked by religious crisis, the burning of *Dead Souls* Part II manuscripts, and death by self-starvation. Scholarly debate persists over his national identity: whether he is a Russian writer of Ukrainian origin, a Ukrainian writer operating in an imperial lingua franca, or a figure of irreducible duality.

## Timeline
- **1 April 1809 (O.S. 20 March)** — Born in Sorochyntsi, Poltava Governorate, Russian Empire.
- **1820–1828** — Attends Nezhin Gymnasium; begins writing; destroys failed poem *Hans Küchelgarten* (1828).
- **1831–1832** — Publishes *Evenings on a Farm Near Dikanka* (2 vols.) under pen name "Rudy Panko"; immediate success.
- **1835** — Publishes *Mirgorod* (incl. *Taras Bulba*, *Viy*) and *Arabesques*; fails as Professor of Medieval History at St. Petersburg University (1834–35).
- **19 April 1836** — *The Government Inspector* premieres at Alexandrinsky Theatre; Tsar Nicholas I attends; Gogol commits to literature.
- **1836–1848** — Lives abroad (Germany, Switzerland, Paris, Rome); writes *Dead Souls*, revises *Taras Bulba*, writes *The Overcoat*, *Marriage*.
- **1842** — *Dead Souls* Part I published as *The Adventures of Chichikov* (censorship title); *Taras Bulba* revised edition; *Marriage* completed.
- **1847** — Publishes *Selected Passages from Correspondence with Friends*, defending autocracy and Orthodoxy; attacked by Belinsky.
- **April 1848** — Returns from pilgrimage to Jerusalem; intensifies asceticism under spiritual elder Matvey Konstantinovsky.
- **24 February 1852 (O.S.)** — Burns manuscripts of *Dead Souls* Part II; takes to bed, refuses food.
- **4 March 1852 (O.S. 21 February)** — Dies in Moscow; buried at Danilov Monastery.
- **1931** — Remains transferred to Novodevichy Cemetery; body found face down, fueling "buried alive" theory.

## Body

### Early Life and Ukrainian Roots
Gogol was born into the petty gentry (*pomeshchiks*) in Sorochyntsi. His father, Vasily Gogol-Yanovsky, claimed descent from Cossack hetman Ostap Hohol and wrote poetry and plays for a home theater; the family was trilingual (Ukrainian, Russian, Polish). At Nezhin Gymnasium (1820–28), Gogol was nicknamed "mysterious dwarf," developed mimicry skills, and harbored "boundless ambition." In 1828 he moved to St. Petersburg, self-published a Romantic poem under "V. Alov," faced universal derision, bought and burned all copies, and swore off poetry.

### Literary Development and the St. Petersburg Turn
In St. Petersburg, the 1830 November Uprising heightened Russian nationalism; Poles became "suspect." Gogol dropped the Polish "Ianovskii" from his surname, admonishing his mother to call him only "Gogol." Tsarist policy encouraged Ukrainian intellectuals to sever Polish ties and embrace a folkloric Ukrainian particularism within the empire. His *Evenings on a Farm Near Dikanka* (1831–32) fit this trend, earning immediate acclaim. Critics Nikolai Polevoy and Nikolai Nadezhdin classified him as a regional Ukrainian writer; later, Stepan Shevyrev and Vissarion Belinsky reclassified him as Russian. Gogol’s early prose, rich in Ukrainianisms (glossaries appended), shared themes with contemporary Ukrainian-language writers like Hryhory Kvitka-Osnovyanenko but surpassed them in satirical sophistication. A passion for Cossack history produced *Taras Bulba* (1835) after a university appointment was blocked despite Pushkin’s support. In 1834–35, Gogol held a disastrous professorship at St. Petersburg University, memorizing one lecture then feigning toothache at exams. The 1836 premiere of *The Government Inspector*—a satire of provincial bureaucracy staged only by Nicholas I’s order—convinced Gogol of his literary vocation.

### Exile, *Dead Souls*, and the Dantean Plan
From 1836 to 1848 Gogol lived abroad, primarily in Rome, where he studied art, opera, and Italian literature. Pushkin’s death (1837) profoundly affected him. His principal work became *Dead Souls*, conceived as a modern *Divine Comedy*: Part I (the Inferno) exposes a catalog of Russian vices through the swindler Chichikov buying deceased serfs; Part II (Purgatory) would depict Chichikov’s purification; Part III (Paradise) was barely sketched. Part I appeared in 1842 under the censorship title *The Adventures of Chichikov*, cementing his reputation. During this period he also revised *Taras Bulba* (1842), completed the comedy *Marriage* (1842), wrote the fragment *Rome*, and produced "The Overcoat."

### Later Life, Religious Crisis, and Death
After *Dead Souls*, Gogol was hailed as a satirist of Imperial Russia, but his secret theological ambition remained. In 1847, *Selected Passages from Correspondence with Friends* defended autocracy, serfdom, and the Orthodox Church, provoking a furious attack from Belinsky (now a Marxist), who accused him of betraying his readership. In April 1848 Gogol returned from a Jerusalem pilgrimage, wandering Russia and deepening ties with the *starets* Matvey Konstantinovsky, who convinced him his imaginative work was sinful. Exaggerated asceticism ruined his health. On the night of 24 February 1852, he burned most of *Dead Souls* Part II, calling it a "practical joke played on him by the Devil." He refused food and died nine days later.

### Style: Grotesque, Defamiliarization, and *Poshlost’*
D.S. Mirsky called Gogol’s universe "one of the most marvellous, unexpected... worlds ever created." His Gothic stories ("A Terrible Vengeance," "A Bewitched Place") feature fantastic spatial metamorphoses. Nature descriptions heap detail into "unconnected chaos"; characters are caricatures exaggerated to "geometrical pattern" yet possess "convincingness... that seems to beggar the visible world." Viktor Shklovsky identified *defamiliarization*—making the familiar strange to renew perception—as Gogol’s core technique. Andrey Bely saw him as a forerunner of Gothic romance, absurdism, and impressionism. The mature Gogol’s lens is *poshlost’* (triviality, banality, moral-spiritual inferiority pervasive in society). Like Sterne, he destroyed prohibitions and romantic illusions, making vulgarity reign where the sublime once stood. His comedy often pivots abruptly into metaphysical horror; "The Tale of How Ivan Ivanovich Quarreled with Ivan Nikiforovich" ends: "It is dull in this world, gentlemen!"

### Politics: Slavophile Conservatism
Gogol was a Slavophile who believed in a divinely ordained mission for the Romanovs and the Orthodox Church. He was stunned when critics read *The Government Inspector* as an indictment of Tsarism. Like Dostoevsky, he opposed constitutional monarchy and disestablishment of the Church, viewing his satire as corrective, not subversive. *Selected Passages* (1847) explicitly defended the status quo, causing a definitive break with the radical critic Belinsky.

### National Identity: Contested Duality
Gogol’s identity is a major scholarly fault line. Edyta Bojanowska argues his Ukraine is depicted with folkloric-historical depth ("united by organic culture, historical memory, and language"), while Russia appears only as present-tense bureaucracy and corruption; *Dead Souls* presents "Russian uniqueness as a catalog of faults." She notes Gogol was surprised to be perceived in St. Petersburg as a Ukrainian *khokhol* (hick), making him "self-consciously Ukrainian." Kathleen Scollins sees his "double-voicedness" and "pidginized Russian" of Cossacks as strategies of resistance against imperial homogenization. Daniel Green emphasizes the mutual shaping of Russian and Ukrainian literatures in an imperial culture. Oleh Ilnytzkyj contends Gogol should be classified as a Ukrainian writer operating in Russian as an imperial *lingua franca*; his "Russian" classification stems from "all-Russianness," an assimilationist ideology. Ilnytzkyj reads the revised *Taras Bulba* (1842) as a profound assertion of Ukrainian nationalism, linking Cossacks to Kyivan Rus' via the term *russkii* to challenge the "all-Russian" narrative. He argues Gogol’s post-1842 religious crisis and non-fiction cannot negate the Ukrainian nationalist core of his fiction.

### Influence and Interpretations
Belinsky anointed Gogol the first Russian realist and head of the "natural school" (Goncharov, Turgenev, et al.), though Gogol himself doubted the movement’s existence. Modernists revived him: the Serapion Brothers (1920s) imitated his techniques; Zamyatin and Bulgakov followed his path. Meyerhold’s 1926 *Government Inspector* framed it as "comedy of the absurd." Andrei Bely’s 1934 technical study informed Nabokov’s lectures. Nabokov proclaimed "The Overcoat" the work of "the greatest artist that Russia has yet produced," arguing its "holes in the language" allow a supernatural reading. "The Nose" resisted interpretation until postmodern and postcolonial readings emerged. Portrayals of Jewish characters (especially Yankel in *Taras Bulba*) drew charges of antisemitism from Ze'ev Jabotinsky, Felix Dreizin, David Guaspari, and Léon Poliakov. Yet Jewish writers like Sholem Aleichem adopted Gogol’s rural East European landscape and laughter-as-defense, rewriting his stereotypes from a Jewish perspective.

### Adaptations and Cultural Legacy
Over 135 films adapt Gogol’s work. Shostakovich’s *The Nose* (1928) was the first Soviet opera; Schnittke wrote a *Gogol Suite*; Auerbach composed a full-length opera for Gogol’s 2009 bicentennial. BBC Radio 4 produced *Three Ivans, Two Aunts and an Overcoat* (2002). "Christmas Eve" inspired operas by Lysenko (1872), Tchaikovsky (1874/1885), and Rimsky-Korsakov (1894). "Viy" spawned four Russian films (1967, 2006, 2014, 2018), a video game, and inspired Mario Bava’s *Black Sunday* (1960). The TV-3 series *Gogol* (2017–18) fictionalizes his life. "The Nose" received landmark pinscreen animation (1963) and a 2020 feature. Monuments include Andreyev’s Symbolist statue (1909, praised by Tolstoy, replaced by Socialist Realist version in 1952, later restored) and the Golgotha stone at Novodevichy Cemetery (reused for Bulgakov’s tomb). Streets worldwide bear his name; commemorative coins and stamps abound.

## Terms
- ****Defamiliarization**** — A literary technique (identified by Viktor Shklovsky) whereby common things are presented in an unfamiliar or strange way to renew the reader’s perception.
- ****Poshlost’**** — A Russian term denoting spiritual triviality, banality, moral inferiority, and vulgarity widespread in society; the central target of Gogol’s mature satire.
- ****Natural School**** — A 1840s Russian literary grouping (Goncharov, Turgenev, Grigorovich, et al.) identified by Belinsky as founded by Gogol, focused on observational realism; Gogol himself was skeptical of its existence.
- ****Starets**** — A spiritual elder in the Russian Orthodox tradition; Matvey Konstantinovsky served this role for Gogol in his final years, intensifying his fear of damnation.
- ****Khokhol**** — A derogatory Russian term for a Ukrainian (literally "topknot"); Gogol was called this in St. Petersburg, prompting self-consciousness about his Ukrainian identity.
- ****All-Russianness**** — An imperial ideology (per Oleh Ilnytzkyj) aimed at assimilating East Slavs into a singular "Russian" nation, used to appropriate Gogol for Russian literature.
- ****Pomeshchik**** — A member of the landed gentry who owned serfs; Gogol’s father belonged to this class, owning the village of Vasylivka.
- ****Serapion Brothers**** — A 1920s group of Russian short-story writers who claimed Gogol as a precursor and consciously imitated his techniques.
- ****Golgotha**** — A large stone marker (resembling the biblical hill) placed on Gogol’s grave at Danilov Monastery; later moved to Novodevichy Cemetery and reused for Mikhail Bulgakov’s tomb.
- ****Pinscreen animation**** — A technique using a screen of movable pins to create shadows; used by Alexandre Alexeieff and Claire Parker for the 1963 NFB film of "The Nose."

## Debates and open questions
- **National classification**: Is Gogol a Russian writer of Ukrainian origin, a Ukrainian writer writing in the imperial lingua franca, or a figure of irreducible duality? (Bojanowska, Scollins, Green, Ilnytzkyj offer competing frameworks.)
- **Ukrainian vs. Russian identity in the texts**: Does the revised *Taras Bulba* assert a distinct Ukrainian-Rus'ian identity challenging imperial narratives, or does it serve an all-Russian synthesis?
- **Antisemitism vs. literary influence**: To what extent do stereotyped Jewish characters (e.g., Yankel) reflect Gogol’s personal prejudice versus period conventions? How to weigh his influence on Jewish writers (Sholem Aleichem) who both inherited and corrected his tropes?
- **Realism vs. grotesque/surrealism**: Was Gogol a "realist" (Belinsky, Soviet criticism) or a writer of the fantastic whose "holes in the language" (Nabokov) open supernatural readings?
- **Religious crisis and artistic silence**: Did Gogol’s post-1842 asceticism and manuscript burning represent a genuine spiritual transformation or a pathological breakdown? Does his later non-fiction negate his earlier fiction?
- **Buried alive?**: The 1931 exhumation found the body face down, generating a persistent conspiracy theory; scholarly consensus rejects it, but the legend endures.
