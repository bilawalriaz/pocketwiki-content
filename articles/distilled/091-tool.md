# Tool

## Overview

A tool is an object that extends an individual's ability to modify the surrounding environment or accomplish a particular task. Prototypically, tools are solid, hand-operated, non-biological objects with a single broad purpose, lacking the multiple functions of machines or computers. While humans are proportionally the most active tool users and makers in the animal kingdom—with stone tool use dating back hundreds of millennia—many animals also demonstrate tool use. The development of tools, from early stone implements to machine tools and automation, has been central to human evolution, the rise of civilizations, and the Industrial Revolution.

## Timeline

- **c. 3.4 million years ago** — Possible earliest stone tool use by hominins (Australopithecus afarensis) suggested by 2010 study
- **c. 2.6–2.5 million years ago** — Actual stone artifacts date back; finds in Ethiopia at least 2.6 million years old
- **5th millennium BC** — Potter's wheel (wheel and axle mechanism) invented in what is now Iraq
- **Early 4th millennium BC** — Wheeled vehicle invented in Mesopotamia
- **c. 3000 BC** — Lever used in shadoof water-lifting device (first crane machine) in Mesopotamia
- **Early 2nd millennium BC** — Earliest evidence of pulleys in Mesopotamia
- **911–609 BC** — Screw invented in Mesopotamia during Neo-Assyrian period
- **c. 688 BC** — Jerwan Aqueduct built with stone arches and waterproof concrete
- **Before 350 BC** — Earliest water wheels and watermills in Persian Empire (Mesopotamia and Persia)
- **18th century** — Machine tools originate from clock, watch, and scientific instrument makers
- **Late 15th century** — Screwdriver invented (for driving fastening screws)

## Body

### Definition

Several formal definitions of tool use have been proposed. Benjamin Beck's 1981 definition, later modified, describes it as the external employment of an unattached or manipulable attached environmental object to alter more efficiently the form, position, or condition of another object, organism, or the user itself, when the user holds and directly manipulates the tool during or prior to use. Briefer definitions include: "an object carried or maintained for future use," "the use of physical objects other than the animal's own body or appendages as a means to extend the physical influence realized by the animal," and "an inanimate object that one uses or modifies in some way to cause a change in the environment, thereby facilitating one's achievement of a target goal."

### History

Anthropologists believe tool use was an important step in human evolution. Since both humans and wild chimpanzees use tools extensively, it is widely assumed that routine tool use predates the divergence between the two species. Early tools were likely made of perishable materials like sticks, or were unmodified stones indistinguishable from other stones. Stone artifacts date back to about 2.5 million years ago, though a 2010 study suggests Australopithecus afarensis used stone implements to carve animal carcasses about 3.4 million years ago. One of the earliest distinguishable stone tool forms is the hand axe.

Early human tools were used for hunting, food preparation, weapons manufacture, and working materials for clothing, pottery, housing, and infrastructure. Recent scholarship recognizes that tools served many activities beyond hunting, including nutting, leatherworking, grain harvesting, and woodworking. The idea of "man the hunter" as the catalyst for hominin change has been questioned; bone marks at archaeological sites suggest pre-humans were scavenging off other predators' carcasses rather than killing their own food. Early humans progressively invented tools and techniques for trapping animals.

Several of the six classic simple machines (wheel and axle, lever, pulley, inclined plane, wedge, screw) were invented in Mesopotamia. The wheel and axle appeared with the potter's wheel in the 5th millennium BC, leading to the wheeled vehicle in the early 4th millennium BC. The lever appeared in the shadoof water-lifting device c. 3000 BC. The screw, the last simple machine invented, appeared during the Neo-Assyrian period (911–609 BC); King Sennacherib (704–681 BC) claimed to have invented automatic sluices and used water screw pumps cast with two-part clay molds. The earliest water wheels and watermills date to the 4th century BC in the Persian Empire, constituting perhaps the first use of mechanical energy. Mechanical devices expanded greatly in Ancient Greece and Rome with systematic use of waterwheels, and later windmills were added through the Dark Ages.

### Machine Tools

Machine tools occasioned a surge in producing new tools during the Industrial Revolution. Pre-industrial machinery was built by millwrights, carpenters, smiths, and turners; wooden components had disadvantages, changing dimensions with temperature and humidity, and joints tending to rack (work loose) over time. As the Industrial Revolution progressed, machines with metal parts became more common. Demand for metal parts in firearms and threaded fasteners created a need for precision, enabling interchangeability of parts and standardization. Machine tools originated in the 18th century from clock, watch, and scientific instrument makers who batch-produced small mechanisms. Before machine tools, metal was worked manually with hammers, files, scrapers, saws, and chisels—laborious, costly, and imprecise. Machine tools enabled economical production of interchangeable parts. Nanotechnology advocates expect a similar surge as tools become microscopic.

### Types

Tools can be classified by basic functions: cutting and edge tools (knife, sickle, axe) are wedge-shaped implements producing shearing force along a narrow face; the edge must be harder than the material cut and requires periodic sharpening. Moving tools include levers giving mechanical advantage—hammers and mauls apply compression, screwdrivers apply rotational force (torque), and anvils concentrate force by preventing movement. Writing implements deliver fluid via compression. Other categories include tools enacting chemical changes (lighters, blowtorches); guiding, measuring and perception tools (ruler, glasses, microscope, clock); shaping tools (molds, jigs, trowels); fastening tools (welders, rivet guns, nail guns); and information manipulation tools (computers, spreadsheets). Some tools combine categories—an alarm clock combines a measuring tool and a perception tool. There is debate about whether protective gear counts as tools, since they protect workers rather than directly performing work, though they meet the general definition.

### Tool Substitution and Multi-Use Tools

Tools may share key functional attributes, allowing substitution either "by-design" (multi-purpose tools designed for multiple tasks) or "makeshift" (using a tool for an unintended purpose, such as using a screwdriver to separate a car's control arm from a ball joint). Many designed secondary functions are not widely known—for example, many wood-cutting hand saws integrate a square in their specially-shaped handle for marking 90° and 45° angles. The saying "all tools can be used as hammers" illustrates makeshift substitution. Cheap tools often substitute for missing mechanical parts in older devices. A multi-tool incorporates several tools into a single portable device; the Swiss Army knife is one of the earliest examples. Lineman's pliers incorporate gripper, cutter, and hammer functions. These tools were revolutionary for traveling crafters with limited space for equipment.

### Use by Other Animals

Tool use by animals achieves goals such as acquiring food, grooming, defense, communication, recreation, or construction. Originally thought unique to humans, some tool use requires sophisticated cognition. Confirmed tool-using species include monkeys, apes, elephants, several birds, and sea otters. Humans are considered unique as the only species that uses tools to make other tools. Primates use tools for hunting, gathering, rain cover, and self-defense; chimpanzees are the most famous subjects, particularly through Jane Goodall's work. Novel tool use may arise in localized primate cultures and be transmitted through cultural learning. Elephants create tools with trunks and feet for swatting flies, scratching, and plugging waterholes. Dolphins in Shark Bay use sea sponges to protect their beaks while foraging. Sea otters use rocks to dislodge and break open shellfish. Corvids (crows, ravens, rooks) are known for tool use; New Caledonian crows manufacture probes from twigs and wood to impale larvae. Tailorbirds manufacture pouches for nests; woodpecker finches insert twigs to catch larvae. Some birds exploit human activity—carrion crows in Japan drop nuts in front of cars to crack them. Several fish species use tools for hunting and nesting. Among cephalopods, octopuses use tools relatively frequently, gathering coconut shells for shelter or using rocks for barriers.

### Non-Material Usage

Concepts supporting systematic or investigative thought are often called "tools" or "toolkits." Examples include "tools of reflection" for trainee teachers, a "Birth Choice tool" for maternity decisions, and the International Labour Organization's "toolkit" for improving global labour relations. A telephone is a communication tool interfacing between users and between users and networks. Media scholar John M. Culkin famously said, "We shape our tools and thereafter our tools shape us." Scholars have expanded this: "Humans create inspiring and empowering technologies but also are influenced, augmented, manipulated, and even imprisoned by technology."

## Terms

- **Simple machine**: One of six basic mechanical devices (wheel and axle, lever, pulley, inclined plane, wedge, screw) that change the direction or magnitude of a force
- **Machine tool**: A powered mechanical device used to manufacture parts with precision, enabling interchangeable parts and standardization
- **Hand axe**: One of the earliest distinguishable stone tool forms
- **Shadoof**: A water-lifting device using a lever; the first crane machine, appearing c. 3000 BC in Mesopotamia
- **Multi-tool**: A hand tool incorporating several tools into a single portable device, such as the Swiss Army knife
- **Torque**: Rotational force applied by tools like screwdrivers and wrenches
- **Makeshift substitution**: Using a tool for an unintended purpose through human ingenuity
- **Corvids**: The bird family including crows, ravens, and rooks, known for large brains and tool use
- **Australopithecus afarensis**: A hominin species possibly using stone implements c. 3.4 million years ago

## Debates and open questions

- Whether early hominins were hunters or scavengers is debated; bone marks suggest scavenging of other predators' carcasses rather than active hunting
- There is considerable discussion about the definition of what constitutes a tool and which animal behaviours qualify as true tool use
- Whether protective gear items (gloves, safety glasses, biohazard suits) should be considered tools is debated, since they protect rather than directly perform work
- The full extent of wild tool use in primates remains poorly documented, as many are observed only distantly or briefly in natural environments
