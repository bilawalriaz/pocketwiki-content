# Drama

## Overview
Drama is a mode of fiction represented in performance, encompassing plays, opera, ballet, and other forms enacted before an audience. It is a collaborative art form whose structure is shaped by collective production and reception. Originating in ancient Greece, drama has evolved through distinct historical periods and cultural traditions, serving as a vehicle for storytelling, social critique, and entertainment.

## Timeline
- **c. 335 BC** — Aristotle's *Poetics*, the earliest work of dramatic theory, contrasts drama with epic and lyrical modes.
- **5th century BC** — Athenian theatrical culture institutionalizes tragedy, comedy, and satyr play in competitions for the god Dionysus.
- **240 BC** — Beginning of regular Roman drama, following Rome's encounter with Greek theatre.
- **c. 925** — Earliest known liturgical drama, the Easter trope *Quem-Quaeritis*, emerges in churches.
- **16th–17th c.** — A great flowering of drama in England, including the works of Shakespeare, Marlowe, and Jonson.
- **1660–1710** — Restoration comedy flourishes in England, known for its sexual explicitness and urban wit.
- **19th–20th c.** — Modern drama is dominated by the innovative contributions of Henrik Ibsen and Bertolt Brecht.

## Body

### Origins and Classical Foundations
The term "drama" derives from the Greek word for "deed" or "act." Western drama originated in classical Athens, where tragedy, comedy, and satyr play were performed in festivals for Dionysus. Thespis is credited with introducing the first actor who spoke in character. Only a fraction of works by playwrights like Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, Aristophanes, and Menander survives. Roman drama began in 240 BC, adapting Greek models. Playwrights like Plautus and Terence developed comedy, while Seneca was a key figure in tragedy. Roman theatre was more varied and extensive than its Greek predecessor.

### Medieval to Renaissance
After the Roman period, drama re-emerged in medieval Europe through liturgical dramas in churches. By the 11th century, these had spread across Europe. Secular forms also developed, including mystery plays performed by trade guilds in England and morality plays like *Everyman* that used allegorical characters. The Elizabethan and Jacobean eras saw a major resurgence, with playwrights like Shakespeare drawing on classical and historical sources.

### Modern and Postmodern Drama
The 19th and 20th centuries were dominated by the innovations of Henrik Ibsen and Bertolt Brecht, who blended modernism with realism and social critique. Other major modern playwrights include Anton Chekhov, George Bernard Shaw, Samuel Beckett, and Arthur Miller. Their works often incorporated formal experimentation and meta-theatricality.

### Opera and Other Performance Forms
Opera, which arose during the Renaissance, combines drama with music and dance. Richard Wagner later sought to balance music and drama more equally. Pantomime is a family-oriented musical comedy form popular in the UK, using stock characters and audience participation. Mime tells stories through body movement alone, a tradition refined by practitioners like Étienne Decroux. Ballet uses dance to express narrative and emotion, as in Tchaikovsky's *Swan Lake*.

### Asian Drama
Indian drama's earliest form is Sanskrit theatre, for which the *Nātyaśāstra* is a key ancient treatise. Famous playwrights include Kalidasa. After a decline, regional theatre revived from the 15th century. Modern Indian drama was pioneered by Rabindranath Tagore. Chinese theatre has a long history, with Beijing opera being a well-known form. Japanese drama includes the serious Nō, the comic Kyōgen, and the popular Kabuki.

## Terms
- **Drama**: A mode of fiction represented in performance, such as a play or opera.
- **Tragedy**: A drama dealing with tragic events and unhappy ending, especially the death of a hero.
- **Comedy**: A humorous work of entertainment, often with a happy ending.
- **Satyr play**: A brief, burlesque play with chorus and exotic characters, performed after a trilogy of tragedies in ancient Greece.
- **Liturgical drama**: Dramatized versions of biblical events staged by churches in the Middle Ages.
- **Morality play**: An allegorical drama where characters represent ethical ideals, popular in the 15th and 16th centuries.
- **Restoration comedy**: English comedies from 1660–1710, known for sexual explicitness and witty dialogue.
- **Opera**: A dramatic art form combining sung dialogue, music, and often dance.
- **Nō**: A serious Japanese dramatic form combining drama, music, and dance, developed in the 14th–15th centuries.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The origins of Greek drama remain obscure, though it was institutionalized by the 5th century BC.
- The exact date of composition for the Indian *Nātyaśāstra* is uncertain, with estimates ranging from 200 BC to 200 AD.
- The authorship of the Roman tragedy *Octavia* is unknown; it was once mistakenly attributed to Seneca.
