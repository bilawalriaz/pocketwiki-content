# Romanticism

## Overview

Romanticism was an artistic and intellectual movement originating in late 18th-century Europe, peaking roughly 1800–1850, as a reaction against the Enlightenment's rationalism and the social dislocations of the Industrial Revolution. It championed subjectivity, imagination, emotion, and nature, idealizing the medieval past and the exotic while emphasizing individual artistic genius and authentic self-expression. Its influence extended across literature, visual arts, music, architecture, philosophy, politics, and science, leaving a lasting legacy in nationalism, environmentalism, and modern aesthetic values.

- **1800–1850** — Romanticism peaks across Europe

- **1815** — End of Napoleonic Wars; Romantic themes respond to political upheaval

- **1910** — Musical Romanticism largely ends with rise of modernism
- **1940s** — Last "Late Romantic" figures like Rachmaninoff die

## Body

### Defining Characteristics and Philosophy

Romanticism prioritized the artist's unique imagination and emotional authenticity over classical form and rational structure. Artists like Caspar David Friedrich declared that "the artist's feeling is his law," while Wordsworth advocated poetry as the "spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings." Romantics believed in "romantic originality"—creating works *ex nihilo* without reliance on existing models. They emphasized intense emotion, the sublime, and experiences of awe and terror, particularly through nature, which they saw as spiritually beneficial and morally instructive.

### Origins and Influences

Romanticism emerged from the German Sturm und Drang movement and was shaped by the French Revolution and Napoleonic Wars. Goethe's *The Sorrows of Young Werther* (1774) and the Gothic novel tradition, beginning with Horace Walpole's *The Castle of Otranto* (1764), were key precursors. The movement also drew from folk traditions, medievalism, and non-Western cultures, reflecting a fascination with the exotic and mysterious.

### Regional Developments

**Germany**: Centered in Jena, German Romanticism was influenced by philosophers like Fichte and Schelling. Key figures included the Schlegel brothers, Novalis, and E.T.A. Hoffmann. The Brothers Grimm's fairy tale collections (starting 1812) exemplified Romantic interest in folklore.

**Britain**: The Lake Poets (Wordsworth, Coleridge, Southey) and later Keats, Shelley, and Byron defined English Romanticism. Sir Walter Scott pioneered the historical novel with *Waverley* (1814). Scottish Romanticism, led by Burns and Scott, emphasized national identity.

**France**: French Romanticism developed later, centered on Parisian theater. Victor Hugo's *Hernani* (1830) and Alexandre Dumas's historical novels were pivotal. The movement emphasized individual expression and rejected classical rules.

**Poland**: Polish Romanticism was deeply tied to national independence struggles. Adam Mickiewicz's messianic vision of Poland as the "Christ of Nations" and the works of Słowacki and Krasiński reflected political resistance.

**United States**: American Romanticism emphasized frontier themes, the sublime landscape (Hudson River School), and psychological depth. Poe, Hawthorne, Melville, and the Transcendentalists (Emerson, Thoreau) were key figures.

### Decline and Legacy

Romanticism declined due to the rise of Realism, Darwin's theories, and changing social conditions. By World War I, it had largely dispersed into modernist movements. However, its influence persisted in film scores, environmentalism, political theory, and continued "Late Romantic" artistic production into the 1940s.

## Terms

- **Sturm und Drang**: German "Storm and Stress" movement (1770s) emphasizing emotion and individualism, precursor to Romanticism
- **Sublime**: Aesthetic concept describing overwhelming beauty or terror that evokes awe
- **Gothic Revival**: 19th-century architectural and literary movement drawing on medieval Gothic forms
- **Byronic hero**: Literary archetype of a brooding, rebellious, charismatic male protagonist
- **Naturphilosophie**: Philosophical approach seeking unity between nature and spirit, influential among Romantic scientists
- **Neoromantic**: 20th-century revival movements resisting modernist abstraction
- **Messianic nationalism**: Idea that a nation has a divine mission to suffer and redeem others
- **Romantic originality**: Concept that artistic genius creates ex nihilo, independent of tradition
- **Folk revival**: 19th-century movement collecting and celebrating traditional folk culture
- **Historical novel**: Genre pioneered by Scott, blending fictional narrative with historical settings

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars debate Romanticism's precise definition and boundaries. Isaiah Berlin argued it disrupted Western traditions of rationality and moral absolutes, contributing to nationalism, fascism, and totalitarianism. Arthur Lovejoy highlighted the difficulty of defining "Romanticisms" pluralistically. Some scholars see Romanticism as continuous with the present, while others view it as the inauguration of modernity. The relationship between Romanticism and the French Revolution remains contested—some emphasize revolutionary sympathies, others conservative nationalism. In musicology, debates persist over periodization, with some extending musical Romanticism to 1910 and others including 20th-century expressionism. Spanish Romanticism's uniqueness is debated, with some scholars viewing it as Proto-Existentialism. The movement's impact on historiography is also contested, with critics arguing Romantic nationalism distorted historical narratives through heroic mythmaking.
