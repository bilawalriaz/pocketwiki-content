# Human sexuality

## Overview
Human sexuality encompasses the biological, psychological, physical, erotic, emotional, social, and spiritual ways people experience and express themselves. It is a broad concept lacking a precise definition due to historical and cultural variation. Sexual orientation—an enduring pattern of attraction to the opposite sex, same sex, or both—is now understood to have strong biological underpinnings, particularly for males, with evidence from genetics, prenatal hormones, and cross-cultural studies outweighing support for social causes. Sexuality develops across the lifespan, from childhood curiosity through adult relationships and late-life intimacy, and is regulated by complex sociocultural, legal, and religious systems that vary widely across societies and historical periods.

## Timeline
- **Pre-agriculture** — Hunter-gatherer societies maintain less restrictive sexual standards emphasizing pleasure, though with definite rules; art emphasizes male prowess but blurs gender lines.
- **Rise of agriculture** — Collective supervision of sexual behavior increases due to urbanization; patriarchal systems emerge to control female sexuality and ensure paternity certainty for land inheritance.
- **12th century** — Christian church hostility toward homosexuality spreads through religious and secular institutions.
- **1905** — Sigmund Freud publishes *Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality*, establishing child sexuality as a serious field of study and introducing psychosexual development concepts.
- **1948–1953** — Alfred Kinsey publishes *Sexual Behavior in the Human Male* and *Female*, initiating modern sex research via large-scale interviews revealing high rates of masturbation, oral sex, and homosexual experience.
- **1950** — Ernst Gräfenberg reports the G-spot.
- **1965** — William Masters and Virginia Johnson open the first sex therapy clinic after directly observing 10,000 episodes of human sexual response in laboratory settings.
- **1973** — American Psychiatric Association removes homosexuality from the DSM, declassifying it as a mental disorder.
- **1981** — First reported cases of AIDS diagnosed in the U.S.; epidemic disproportionately affects gay and bisexual men, particularly Black and Latino men.
- **2010** — Affordable Care Act signed, requiring Health Insurance Marketplace plans to cover all forms of contraception.

## Body

### Development of Sexual Orientation
There is considerably more evidence supporting innate, non-social causes of sexual orientation than learned ones, especially for males. Key evidence includes: cross-cultural correlation of homosexuality with childhood gender nonconformity; moderate genetic influences found in twin studies; prenatal hormonal effects on brain organization; the fraternal birth order effect (each older biological brother increases the odds of homosexuality in later-born males); and cases where infant males surgically reassigned as females nevertheless developed attraction to females. Social causes are supported only by weak evidence distorted by confounding factors. Cross-culturally, tolerance does not increase incidence: the Sambia people ritually require adolescent homosexual behavior, yet most men become heterosexual; British boarding school alumni show no higher rates of adult homosexual behavior. Two leading evolutionary hypotheses explain the persistence of homosexuality-associated genes: kin selection (homosexuals invest in relatives' offspring), supported in Samoa but not Western cultures; and sexually antagonistic genes (reducing male reproduction but increasing female fecundity), supported in both Western and non-Western samples.

### Gender Differences
Multiple psychological theories (neo-analytic, sociobiological, social learning, social role, script theory) converge in predicting men are more approving of casual sex and more promiscuous than women. U.S. data largely confirm this for pre-marital casual sex attitudes. However, observed differences in number of partners are modest. Other domains—sexual satisfaction, oral sex incidence, attitudes toward homosexuality and masturbation—show little to no gender difference.

### Biological and Physiological Aspects

#### Neuroendocrine Control
The hypothalamus is the primary brain center for sexual functioning, linking the limbic system to the pituitary gland. The pituitary secretes four key hormones: **oxytocin** (released during orgasm, childbirth, and breastfeeding; facilitates bonding and milk ejection), **prolactin** (induces milk production), **follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)** (triggers egg maturation in females, sperm production in males), and **luteinizing hormone (LH)** (triggers ovulation in females).

#### Male Anatomy
External genitalia include the penis (three cylinders of spongy tissue—two *corpora cavernosa* and one *corpus spongiosum*—that fill with blood during erection) and the scrotum (holds testes below body temperature for spermatogenesis). The penis lacks a bone (baculum) and has minimal muscle tissue. Internal structures: testes (produce sperm and androgens like testosterone in seminiferous tubules and Leydig cells); a four-part duct system (epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory ducts, urethra); prostate gland (produces seminal fluid, source of sexual stimulation via anal penetration); seminal vesicles (secrete fructose, prostaglandins, and alkaline buffers); and Cowper’s glands (pre-ejaculate).

#### Female Anatomy
External genitalia (vulva) include the mons pubis, labia majora/minora, clitoris (homologous to penis; dense nerve endings; primary orgasm source), urethral and vaginal openings, and Bartholin’s/Skene’s glands. Breasts (modified sweat glands) contain 15–20 mammary lobes; nipple stimulation activates the genital sensory cortex. Internal organs: vagina (elastic, self-cleaning canal; expands to 10 cm for birth; possible G-spot on anterior wall); uterus (muscular womb; endometrium thickens for implantation, sheds during menstruation); fallopian tubes (transport ovum, site of fertilization); ovaries (store ~60,000 ova at birth, ~400 mature across lifespan; produce estrogen and progesterone).

#### Sexual Response Cycle (Masters & Johnson EPOR Model)
**Excitement**: intrinsic motivation; vasocongestion (penile erection, clitoral/labial swelling, vaginal lubrication), tachycardia, myotonia. **Plateau**: intensification of excitement-phase changes. **Orgasm**: release of tension; rhythmic contractions (0.8-sec intervals in males); male emission (vas deferens/prostate/seminal vesicle contractions) followed by expulsion (ejaculation); female pelvic/uterine/anal contractions centered in clitoris. **Resolution**: return to unaroused state; males enter a refractory period (lengthens with age); females can experience multiple orgasms without a mandatory refractory period.

#### Sexual Dysfunction
DSM-IV-TR defines disorders as disturbances in desire or psychophysiological response causing marked distress. Causes are physical (hormonal imbalance, diabetes, cardiovascular disease) or psychological (stress, anxiety, depression). Female categories: desire, arousal (lubrication failure), orgasmic (anorgasmia), and pain disorders (dyspareunia). Male categories: desire disorder, ejaculation disorders (premature, retarded, retrograde), and erectile dysfunction (inability to initiate/maintain erection).

### Psychological Aspects
Sexuality generates profound emotional responses; Freud positioned it as the central source of personality via psychosexual development and the Oedipus complex. **Gender identity** is one’s internal sense of being male, female, or non-binary, which may or may not align with assigned sex. **Sexual orientation** is an enduring pattern of romantic/sexual attraction (heterosexual, gay/lesbian, bisexual). Media stereotypes linking gender nonconformity to homosexuality lack empirical support; conformity to gender roles does not predict orientation. By the early 21st century, homosexuality was no longer considered a pathology. Theories link genetic, anatomical, birth-order, and prenatal hormonal factors to homosexuality. Beyond procreation, motives for sex include physical attraction, goal attainment (e.g., resources), emotional connection, and insecurity reduction (Meston & Buss, 2007).

### Sexuality Across the Lifespan
**Childhood**: Pre-Freud, children were viewed as asexual. Freud (1905) and Kinsey documented childhood curiosity, genital exploration ("playing doctor"), and sex play with peers/siblings, which typically decreases with age while romantic interest emerges in adolescence. **Adulthood**: Sexuality matures and develops; it is not fixed. **Late adulthood**: Stereotypes of asexuality are false; desire and capacity persist. Frequency may decline and expression change, but intimacy often deepens in long-term relationships.

### Sociocultural Aspects

#### Sex Education
Content and starting age vary globally. Australia and much of Europe begin age-appropriate education in preschool; others delay until pre-teen/teen years. In the U.S., 74% of teens cite peers and media as primary sources vs. 10% for parents/courses. **Abstinence-only** programs urge restraint; **comprehensive** programs teach safe, healthy, pleasurable sex. Data show many abstinence-pledgers fail to maintain it and often neglect contraception when they do have sex.

#### Historical Trajectory
Hunter-gatherers balanced pleasure and procreation with symbolic male dominance. Agriculture brought collective sexual supervision, patriarchal control of female sexuality, and paternity certainty for inheritance. Classical civilizations (China, Greece, Rome, Persia, India) produced distinct sex manuals. Christian tolerance of homosexuality shifted to hostility by the 12th century; by the 19th century it was pathologized. The Industrial Revolution introduced barrier contraception, medical authority over sexuality, pornography industries, earlier puberty, and a shift toward love-based marriage. Key researchers: Havelock Ellis (homosexuality as inborn), Freud (orientation via Oedipal resolution), Kinsey (behavioral surveys), Masters & Johnson (laboratory physiology), Evelyn Hooker (no psychological maladjustment in homosexuals).

#### Colonialism, Race, and Sexuality
European colonizers imposed heterosexual cisnormativity, using sexual "immorality" to justify racial oppression (e.g., Balboa executing gender-nonconforming Indigenous men in 1516). In North America, 155+ tribes recognized **two-spirit** people (a third gender with spiritual roles), but colonizers labeled this homosexuality and suppressed it via laws like the 1880s Religious Crime Code. Joane Nagel argues sexual meanings enforce racial boundaries via stereotypes (Mammy/Jezebel for Black women; lotus blossom/dragon lady for Asian women; spicy Latina; welfare queen), creating dehumanizing dichotomies.

#### Reproductive and Sexual Rights
A modern, controversial framework applying human rights to reproduction and sexuality: contraception, LGBT rights, abortion, sex education, partner choice, bodily integrity, freedom from forced/child marriage, freedom from female genital mutilation, and access to contraception. The Swedish government defines sexual rights as bodily autonomy and reproductive rights as control over child number/spacing. Globally, criminalization of consensual acts, marital rape exemptions, and restricted contraception remain common.

#### U.S. Contraceptive Stigma
Emma Goldman and Margaret Sanger opposed the Comstock Law (1915), framing birth control as economic/reproductive freedom for lower-class women. Stigma faded after *U.S. v. One Package* (1936) legalized prescription for health; 347 clinics existed by 1938. Eleanor Roosevelt’s support (1933–45) helped; federal funding began under LBJ (1966), continued via the Family Planning Services Act (1970). The Affordable Care Act (2010) mandated coverage of all contraceptive methods.

#### AIDS Epidemic Stigma and Activism
First U.S. cases in 1981; disproportionately affected gay/bisexual men, especially Black and Latino men. Reagan administration apathy (press secretary called it "gay plague"); religious figures framed it as divine punishment. Activism promoted "Safe Sex is Hot Sex" (condom use); government campaigns used scare tactics and banned funding for "homosexual activity" promotion (1987). The Denver Principles (1983) demanded rights/dignity for people with AIDS. International prevention campaigns fostered global gay identity formation in countries where homosexuality was not a primary identity marker.

### Sexual Behavior

#### General Activities and Health
Intercourse benefits: improved smell, reduced stress/blood pressure, increased immunity, lower prostate cancer risk. Oxytocin release during intimacy/orgasm facilitates bonding/trust. Masturbation confers stress reduction and is healthy development. A 3,500-person study (Weeks) linked sexual activity to appearing 4–7 years younger, though causation is unclear (may reflect reduced stress, better sleep). STI burden: 19 million new U.S. cases annually; 340+ million globally; >50% in ages 15–24; 1 in 4 U.S. teenage girls infected. Only ~80% of U.S. 15–19-year-olds used condoms at first intercourse; >75% of young women 18–25 perceived low STI risk.

#### Relationship Formation
Processes include **flirting** (indirect verbal/non-verbal cues—gazing, proximity, comments—to test interest without direct rejection; styles vary culturally) and **seduction** (deliberate enticement to sexual behavior the person would not otherwise do; Latin *seducere* "to lead astray" carries negative valence).

#### Sexual Attraction
Attraction based on sexual desire; **sexual attractiveness** (sex appeal) is the quality of arousing that interest. Influenced by physical traits, aesthetics, movement, voice, smell, adornments, and individual genetic/psychological/cultural factors. Largely subjective: orientation directs attraction (gay/lesbian → same sex; bisexual → both; asexual → neither, though romantic attraction may exist). Interpersonal attraction factors: similarity, familiarity, complementarity, reciprocal liking. Subjective attractiveness underpins advertising, media, modeling, and sex work.

#### Non-Normative Sexuality
Refers to behaviors violating a given culture’s norms (paraphilia, fetishism, kink). Norms are dynamic across time/cultures; what is normative in one context may be deviant in another. Social control operates via stigma, religious dogma, and law.

#### Legal Issues
Laws criminalize specific behaviors, grant privacy/autonomy, protect equality/non-discrimination, regulate marriage/family, and protect against violence. U.S. approaches: "black letter" (precedent-based rules) vs. socio-legal (law-society relationship). Late 20th-century gay liberation brought sexuality into courts. **Sexual privacy** claims are criticized as too narrow; law is slow to address private coercion (FGM, forced marriage, reproductive access denial). Prostitution debates: criminalization vs. decriminalization; feminist split on whether sex work is inherently exploitative or agential. Criminalization leaves workers vulnerable: 2003 NYC survey found 80% of street-based workers experienced violence; 27% from police. "Walking while trans" laws target transgender women via loitering statutes.

### Religious Sexual Morality
**Conservatives** (especially Abrahamic) define sexuality by behavior; promote celibacy/conversion therapy for gay people; may view homosexuality as mental illness, immoral, caused by bad parenting, and same-sex marriage as a societal threat. **Liberals** define sexuality by attraction/self-identification; view same-sex activity as morally neutral, not pathological, biologically/environmentally caused (not parenting), fixed; support same-sex marriage.

#### Judaism
Sex within heterosexual marriage is sacred and obligatory; celibacy is sinful. Orthodox Judaism considers homosexuality a capital sin per Leviticus.

#### Christianity
**Early**: Augustine (5th c.) framed spontaneous desire as proof/penalty of original sin; Paul saw desire as a force Christians must control. **Roman Catholic**: Sexuality is "noble and worthy" with unitive and procreative ends; ideal context is heterosexual marriage open to life. Pope Francis (*Amoris laetitia*) emphasizes conscience formation over rigid rules. **Anglican**: Sexuality is a divine gift for monogamous heterosexual marriage; singleness/celibacy are Christ-like; same-sex attracted people are full members; leadership varies on homosexual expression/ordination. Lists "promiscuity, prostitution, incest, pornography, pedophilia, predatory behavior, sadomasochism, adultery, violence against wives, female circumcision" as sinful. **Evangelical**: Promotes virginity pledges (purity rings) and early marriage; views satisfying sexuality as a marital gift. Homosexuality views range from fundamentalist condemnation (some linking it to terrorism) to moderate sympathy, neutral local-option, and gay-affirming denominations. Marriage as protection against misconduct challenged by leadership scandals; theologians re-emphasize celibacy as a gift (Jesus, Paul).

#### Islam
Sexual desire is natural and should be fulfilled responsibly within marriage (*nikah*); celibacy discouraged after marriage. Polygyny permitted with full support for each wife. Intercourse is worship fulfilling emotional/physical needs; procreation contributes to God’s creation. Homosexuality strictly forbidden; some scholars advocate death penalty. Approach described as open/playful within marriage, free of lewdness/adultery.

#### Hinduism
Sex appropriate only between husband and wife; premarital sex interferes with intellectual development, especially during *brahmacharya* (birth to ~25). *Kama* (sensual pleasure) is one of four *purusharthas* (life aims). *Kama Sutra* addresses intercourse but is not exclusively sexual/religious.

#### Sikhism
Chastity vital; divine spark (*Waheguru*) resides in the body. Sex limited to married couples; extramarital sex forbidden. Marriage is spiritual companionship; monogamy emphasized. Celibacy and homosexuality discouraged. Sexuality not considered paramount compared to other religions.

## Terms
- ****Sexual orientation**** — An enduring pattern of romantic or sexual attraction (or both) to persons of the opposite sex, same sex, or both sexes.
- ****Gender identity**** — A person's internal sense of their own gender (male, female, non-binary), which may or may not correspond to sex assigned at birth.
- ****Hypothalamus**** — The brain region integrating limbic input and controlling pituitary hormone release; essential for sexual behavior and response.
- ****Oxytocin**** — Hormone released during orgasm, childbirth, and breastfeeding; facilitates bonding, trust, and milk ejection ("love hormone").
- ****Sexual response cycle (EPOR)**** — Masters and Johnson’s four-phase model: Excitement, Plateau, Orgasm, Resolution.
- ****Refractory period**** — Post-orgasm recovery phase in males during which further orgasm/erection is physiologically impossible; lengthens with age.
- ****Two-spirit**** — An Indigenous North American gender category (third gender) encompassing mixed gender roles, often with spiritual significance.
- ****Sexually antagonistic genes**** — Genes that reduce reproductive output in one sex (e.g., male homosexuality) but increase it in the other (e.g., female fecundity).
- ****Comstock Law**** — 1873 U.S. federal law criminalizing distribution of "obscene" materials, including contraceptive information and devices.
- ****Conversion therapy**** — Discredited practices attempting to change a person’s sexual orientation from homosexual to heterosexual.

## Debates and open questions
- **Evolutionary persistence of homosexuality**: Kin selection vs. sexually antagonistic genes vs. other mechanisms; inconsistent cross-cultural support for kin selection.
- **G-spot existence and nature**: Researchers dispute its structure, existence, or whether it is a clitoral extension; location and size vary; absent in some women.
- **Abstinence-only vs. comprehensive sex education**: Whether comprehensive curricula increase sexual activity or merely equip youth for inevitable behavior; data show abstinence pledgers often fail to use protection.
- **Prostitution legalization**: Criminalization (moral objection) vs. decriminalization (harm reduction); feminist debate over inherent exploitation vs. worker agency.
- **Sexual privacy as legal framework**: Whether privacy rights are too narrow to address private coercion (FGM, forced marriage, marital rape) and state duty to intervene.
- **Religious doctrine vs. LGBT rights**: Tension between conservative religious views (behavior-based, changeable, pathological) and liberal/secular views (attraction-based, fixed, neutral) regarding same-sex marriage and anti-discrimination law.
