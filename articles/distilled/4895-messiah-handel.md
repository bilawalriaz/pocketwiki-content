# Messiah (Handel)

## Overview
*Messiah* (HWV 56) is an English-language oratorio by George Frideric Handel, composed in 1741 to a libretto by Charles Jennens compiled from the King James Bible and the Coverdale Psalter. It is not a dramatization of Jesus's life but a theological reflection on his role as the Messiah. After an initially modest reception, it became one of the most famous and frequently performed choral works in Western music, known for its "Hallelujah" chorus.

## Timeline
- **10 July 1741** — Charles Jennens sends Handel the libretto for *Messiah*.
- **22 Aug–14 Sept 1741** — Handel composes the entire work in 24 days.
- **13 April 1742** — World premiere in Dublin at the Great Music Hall, Fishamble Street.
- **23 March 1743** — London premiere at Covent Garden Theatre, presented as a "New Sacred Oratorio."
- **1750** — First annual charity performance for the Foundling Hospital in London.
- **1784** — Large-scale commemorative concerts begin at Westminster Abbey.
- **1789** — Mozart creates a re-orchestrated version (*Der Messias*).
- **1902** — Ebenezer Prout publishes a new scholarly edition of the score.
- **1928** — First near-complete recording of the work, conducted by Thomas Beecham.
- **1965** — Watkins Shaw's Novello edition, now the standard, is published.

## Body

### Background and Composition
Handel, a naturalized British subject since 1727, was a leading composer of Italian opera. By the early 1730s, public taste was shifting toward English-language works, prompting Handel to write English oratorios. His librettist for *Messiah* was Charles Jennens, a wealthy, devout Anglican who intended the text to challenge Deism by affirming divine intervention. Handel received the text in July 1741 and completed the music with extraordinary speed, finishing on 14 September. He adapted material from his own earlier Italian works. The original manuscript, showing signs of haste but few errors, is held in the British Library.

### Structure and Premiere
*Messiah* is in three parts. Part I covers prophecies of the Messiah's coming and the nativity. Part II focuses on the Passion, Resurrection, and the spread of the Gospel, culminating in the "Hallelujah" chorus. Part III deals with the resurrection of the dead and Christ's glorification. The work is not a drama; singers do not impersonate characters. The premiere in Dublin on 13 April 1742 was a charity benefit that earned unanimous praise and raised funds to release 142 indebted prisoners. The London premiere in 1743 was less warmly received, partly due to objections about performing sacred music in a theatre.

### Performance History and Editions
After Handel's death, *Messiah* was performed with increasingly large forces. A tradition of massive festivals began in 1784 at Westminster Abbey. In 1789, Mozart re-orchestrated the work for contemporary tastes, adding instruments like flutes and clarinets. This version, along with others by arrangers like Ebenezer Prout, dominated performances for nearly two centuries. The 20th century saw a movement toward "authentic" performances using Handel's original, smaller-scale orchestration and historically informed practices. Watkins Shaw's 1965 edition became the standard for this approach. Today, while large-scale "singalong" performances continue, historically informed renditions are the norm in professional recordings and concerts.

### Music
Handel's score is noted for its orchestral restraint, particularly its limited use of trumpets, which makes their appearances in choruses like "Glory to God" and the "Hallelujah" especially effective. The tonal scheme moves toward D major, a key associated with glory. The music is celebrated for its masterful word-painting, such as the pastoral "Pifa" and the dramatic contrasts in Part II. The "Hallelujah" chorus, while iconic, is not the work's final climax; that honor belongs to the closing "Amen" chorus.

## Terms
- **Oratorio**: A large-scale musical composition for orchestra, choir, and soloists, typically on a sacred theme, but without costumes, scenery, or action.
- **Libretto**: The text of an opera, oratorio, or cantata.
- **Da capo**: A musical form (A-B-A) where the singer repeats the first section, often with ornamentation.
- **Basso continuo**: The continuous bass line in Baroque music, typically played by a keyboard instrument (harpsichord, organ) and a cello or bassoon.
- **Recitative**: A style of delivery in operas and oratorios that mimics the rhythms of speech, used to advance the plot.
- **Aria**: A self-contained piece for one voice, with orchestral accompaniment, expressing a character's emotions.
- **Chorus**: A group of singers performing together, often commenting on the action in an oratorio.
- **Period instruments**: Musical instruments built and played according to historical specifications, used in "authentic" performances.

## Debates and open questions
- **Performance scale**: A long-standing debate exists between "big Messiah" productions with huge forces and "authentic" performances using smaller, period-appropriate ensembles.
- **Orchestration**: The extent to which later arrangers like Mozart "improved" or distorted Handel's original intentions remains a point of scholarly discussion.
- **Definitive version**: It is generally agreed that no single definitive version exists, as Handel himself made numerous revisions, and decisions about ornamentation and instrumentation involve personal judgment.
