# Joseph Dalton Hooker

## Overview
Sir Joseph Dalton Hooker (1817–1911) was a preeminent English botanist and explorer whose work in geographical botany and plant taxonomy was foundational to the field. As Charles Darwin's closest friend and a key defender of evolution, he played a critical role in the history of science. His 20-year directorship of the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, transformed it into a world-renowned scientific institution.

- **10 December 1911** — Died at his home in Sunningdale, Berkshire.

## Body

### Early Life and Antarctic Voyage
Hooker was the son of the botanist Sir William Jackson Hooker. He qualified as a doctor and joined Captain James Clark Ross's Antarctic expedition (1839–1843) on HMS *Erebus* as Assistant-Surgeon. This last major voyage of exploration under sail visited locations including the Kerguelen Islands, where Hooker identified numerous plant species, and Antarctica itself. The expedition successfully located the South Magnetic Pole and charted much of the continent's coastline.

### Himalayan Expedition and Early Career
After a failed bid for a university chair, Hooker worked for the Geological Survey before embarking on a three-year expedition to the Himalayas and India (1847–1851). Based in Darjeeling, he was the first European to collect plants in the region. He explored Sikkim, where he was briefly imprisoned by the Dewan, and the Khasi Hills in Assam. His collections, particularly of rhododendrons, were significant, and his account was published as the *Himalayan Journals* (1854).

### Relationship with Darwin and Support for Evolution
Hooker met Charles Darwin after his Antarctic voyage and began classifying Darwin's plant specimens. They formed a lifelong friendship and correspondence. Hooker was one of the first people Darwin confided in about his theory of evolution by natural selection. When Alfred Russel Wallace's similar theory arrived, Hooker helped arrange the joint presentation of their ideas in 1858. He became the first major scientist to publicly support Darwin's theory in his 1859 essay for the *Flora Tasmaniae* and defended it vigorously at the 1860 Oxford debate.

### Directorship of Kew Gardens
In 1865, Hooker succeeded his father as Director of the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, a post he held for 20 years. He expanded its scientific work and collections, facing political opposition from figures like Acton Smee Ayrton, who sought to curtail its independence and funding. With support from prominent scientists, Hooker defended Kew's value, and its status was secured. His major botanical works include the *Genera Plantarum* (with George Bentham) and the *Flora of British India*.

### Later Expeditions and Honours
Hooker continued to travel, visiting Palestine (1860), Morocco (1871), and the western United States (1877) with Asa Gray to study plant geography. He received numerous honours, including the presidency of the Royal Society (1873–1878), the Order of Merit (1907), and the Copley Medal (1887).

## Terms
*   **Geographical Botany:** The study of the distribution of plant species across the globe, a field Hooker helped found.
*   **Herbarium:** A collection of dried plant specimens mounted and labeled for scientific study.
*   **Phytogeography:** The branch of botany concerned with the geographic distribution of plant species.
*   **Systemist/Systematist:** A scientist who classifies organisms into systematic groups.
*   **Linnean Society:** The world's oldest active biological society, where the Darwin-Wallace papers were presented.
*   **X-Club:** An influential dining club of nine scientists who supported evolutionary theory and dominated British science in the 1870s-80s.
*   **Order of Merit:** A prestigious award for exceptional achievement in the arts, sciences, or military.
*   **Bentham & Hooker system:** A major system for classifying flowering plants, based on their collaborative work.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The rivalry between Kew Gardens and the British Museum's herbarium, particularly with Richard Owen, was intense and personal, centering on institutional independence and scientific authority.
*   Hooker's directorship faced a serious political threat from Acton Smee Ayrton, who questioned Kew's management and scientific value, leading to a parliamentary inquiry that ultimately supported Hooker.
*   The precise causes of the disjunct distributions of plant floras between eastern North America and eastern Asia, which Hooker investigated with Asa Gray, were debated, with proposed explanations including glacial periods and ancient land connections.
