# Calligraphy

## Overview
Calligraphy is the art of giving form to signs in an expressive, harmonious, and skillful manner, elevating handwriting to visual art. It is a prominent element in the art of East Asia and the Islamic world, and its modern Western practice ranges from functional inscriptions to fine art. The tools, styles, and cultural significance of calligraphy vary widely across global traditions, reflecting the history and values of the societies that practice it.

## Timeline
- **c. 600 BCE** — Latin alphabet appears in ancient Rome.
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Earliest known Chinese characters (oracle bone script) are carved.
- **220 BCE** — Emperor Qin Shi Huang imposes character unification, creating 3300 standardized small seal characters.
- **4th–5th centuries** — Roman Empire falls; monasteries become main preservers of calligraphic traditions in Europe.
- **7th–9th centuries** — Heyday of Celtic illuminated manuscripts in Northern Europe (e.g., Book of Kells).
- **783** — First manuscript in Carolingian minuscule, the Godescalc Evangelistary, is finished.
- **11th century** — Caroline script evolves into the more compact blackletter ("Gothic") script.
- **1454** — Johannes Gutenberg develops the first printing press, adopting the Gothic style as its first typeface.
- **1650** — Louis Barbedor publishes influential works on French financial and italic scripts.
- **1898** — Edward Johnston is introduced to manuscript study, inspiring his modern calligraphy revival.
- **1906** — Johnston publishes the influential handbook *Writing & Illuminating, & Lettering*.
- **1921** — The Society of Scribes & Illuminators (SSI) is founded.

## Body

### Tools and Materials
In the West, principal tools are pens (with flat, round, or pointed nibs) and brushes. In East Asia, the "Four Treasures of the Study" are ink brushes, Chinese ink, paper, and inkstones. Calligraphy ink is typically water-based and less viscous than printing ink. Specialty papers, parchment, or vellum are used for their absorption and texture. Lightboxes and templates help achieve straight lines.

### East Asian Calligraphy
Chinese calligraphy (*shūfǎ*) is the origin for Japanese (*shodō*) and Korean (*seoye*) traditions. Its history progresses from ancient oracle bone script to standardized styles like small seal script (imposed c. 220 BCE) and regular script (*kǎi shū*), finalized by Zhong You (151–230) and still in use. Cursive styles like semi-cursive (*xíngshū*) and grass script (*cǎoshū*) are faster and less constrained. The final form of characters is influenced by brush type, ink, paper, and the calligrapher's technique, including pressure, angle, and speed.

### European Calligraphy
European calligraphy evolved from Roman scripts (imperial capitals, rustic capitals, cursive) through uncial and half-uncial styles used in monasteries. The Carolingian minuscule, developed under Charlemagne (c. 783), is the ancestor of modern book type. It evolved into the dominant Gothic script, which Gutenberg adopted for printing. The 15th-century rediscovery of Carolingian texts inspired humanist minuscule. In the 17th century, French officials standardized legal documents to three hands, including the Rhonde (Round hand). The modern revival began in the late 19th century with Edward Johnston, who taught the "Foundational Hand" and influenced a generation of typographers.

### Islamic Calligraphy
Islamic calligraphy, often based on Arabic letters, is a venerated art form providing a visible link to the spiritual world. It is associated with geometric art on mosques and manuscripts. The Qur'an played a key role in its development. The Nasta'liq style is the most popular contemporary classical Persian script. Ottoman calligraphy is especially prominent, with inscriptions found throughout Istanbul.

### Other Regional Traditions
- **Southeast Asia:** The Philippines has ancient *suyat* scripts (e.g., Hanunó'o, Tagbanwa), four of which are UNESCO-listed. Others, like Baybayin, are being revived. Vietnamese calligraphy (*thư pháp*) is based on historical Chinese characters.
- **South Asia:** Calligraphy primarily preserves religious texts. Nepalese calligraphy uses the Ranjana script for sacred Buddhist texts. Nastaliq was common in Mughal India.
- **Africa:** Egyptian hieroglyphs were a formal writing system. Ethiopian calligraphy began with the Ge'ez script (c. 5th century BCE), which is still used for languages like Amharic.
- **Americas:** Maya calligraphy used glyphs, which are still used on seals and monuments in the Yucatán Peninsula.

### Modern Revival and Influence
The modern calligraphy revival began in the late 19th century, influenced by William Morris and the Arts and Crafts movement. Edward Johnston is considered its father; his teachings and books (e.g., *Writing & Illuminating, & Lettering*, 1906) revived the art. He designed the Foundational Hand and influenced the London Underground typeface. His students and contemporaries, like Graily Hewitt and Rudolf Koch, spread calligraphy across Europe and the US. Contemporary computer typefaces have roots in historical calligraphy.

## Terms
- **Calligraphy:** Fine handwriting taken to the level of visual art.
- **Nib:** The pointed part of a pen that touches the writing surface.
- **Makta:** A penknife used to cut reed pens in Asian calligraphy.
- **Four Treasures of the Study:** The essential tools of East Asian calligraphy: ink brush, ink, paper, and inkstone.
- **Oracle bone script:** The oldest known Chinese characters, carved on animal bones for divination.
- **Regular script (*kǎi shū*):** A standardized, regularized style of Chinese characters finalized c. 151–230 and still in use.
- **Carolingian minuscule:** A clear, standardized script developed under Charlemagne (c. 783), ancestor of modern book type.
- **Blackletter (Gothic):** A compact, dominant script in medieval Europe, adopted by Gutenberg for the first printing press.
- **Nasta'liq:** A popular contemporary style of Persian calligraphy, known as the "bride of calligraphy scripts."
- **Foundational Hand:** A crafted, round calligraphic style developed by Edward Johnston for teaching.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The source notes that the shape of Chinese characters has evolved over time (e.g., the character 广), with small differences between historical dictionaries like the Kangxi Dictionary (1716) and modern forms, though stroke order remains consistent.
- The revival of extinct *suyat* scripts in the Philippines is an ongoing cultural effort in protest of colonial history.
- The distinction between "calligraphy" and "hand-lettering" or "type design" is noted, though some practitioners practice both.
