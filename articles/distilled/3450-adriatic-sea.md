# Adriatic Sea

## Overview
The Adriatic Sea is a semi-enclosed arm of the Mediterranean Sea separating the Italian and Balkan peninsulas. It is a vital geographic, economic, and ecological region, hosting significant biodiversity, major shipping routes, and a complex history of political control. Its unique hydrology and geology make it a distinct and dynamic marine environment.

## Timeline
- **c. 6100–5900 BC** — Earliest settlements appear on the eastern Adriatic coast.
- **7th–6th centuries BC** — Greek colonization begins, founding cities like Epidamnos and Apollonia.
- **229–168 BC** — Illyrian Wars lead to Roman control of the eastern shore.
- **402 AD** — Western Roman capital moves to Ravenna on the Adriatic coast.
- **1082** — The Republic of Venice receives a Byzantine tax exemption, boosting its maritime power.
- **1453** — Fall of Constantinople; Ottoman expansion reaches the Adriatic coast.
- **1797** — The Republic of Venice is abolished after French conquest.
- **1815** — Congress of Vienna awards the eastern Adriatic coast to Austria.
- **1918** — Collapse of Austria-Hungary; eastern coast passes to Yugoslavia and Albania.
- **1991–1992** — Dissolution of Yugoslavia; Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Montenegro become independent coastal states.

## Body

### Geography and Hydrology
The Adriatic extends 800 km from the Strait of Otranto to the Po Valley. It is divided into three basins: the shallow Northern Adriatic, the Middle Adriatic, and the deep Southern Adriatic, with a maximum depth of 1,233 m. The sea contains over 1,300 islands, mostly along its rugged eastern coast. Its waters are less saline than the Mediterranean because it receives about a third of the freshwater flowing into the Mediterranean, primarily from the Po River. Prevailing currents flow counterclockwise. Tidal movements are normally slight but can cause significant coastal flooding (*acqua alta*), especially in Venice, due to a combination of astronomical, meteorological, and geographic factors.

### Geology and Climate
The Adriatic sits on the Apulian or Adriatic Microplate, which separated from the African Plate in the Mesozoic era. The Italian Peninsula formed in the Late Oligocene, separating the Adriatic Basin. The eastern coast is characterized by karst topography, while the western coast is largely alluvial. The climate ranges from humid subtropical in the north to Mediterranean in the south. The predominant winter winds are the cold, dry bora and the warm, humid sirocco.

### Ecology and Biodiversity
The Adriatic is a unique biogeochemical environment, acting as a mineralization site. Its counterclockwise current results in clearer waters along the eastern coast, supporting higher biodiversity, including rare species like the monk seal and common bottlenose dolphin. Over 7,000 species are native to the sea, with many endemic to its karst habitats. However, the ecosystem is threatened by pollution, especially nutrient runoff from rivers like the Po, and overfishing, which has depleted stocks of species like sardines and hake. Numerous marine protected areas have been established along its coasts.

### History and Political Control
Early settlements were followed by Greek colonization and Roman conquest, which made the Adriatic a Roman lake. After the Roman Empire's decline, control shifted among the Ostrogoths, Lombards, and Byzantine Empire. The Republic of Venice rose to dominate the sea for centuries. From the 15th century, the Ottoman Empire expanded to the eastern shore, leading to conflicts with Venice and the Habsburg monarchy. The Napoleonic Wars resulted in Austrian control of the eastern coast. Following World War I, the eastern shore passed to Yugoslavia and Albania. After Yugoslavia's breakup in the 1990s, four new coastal states emerged, leading to ongoing maritime border disputes.

### Economy
Fisheries and tourism are major economic sectors. The Adriatic hosts 19 seaports handling over a million tonnes of cargo annually, with the Port of Trieste being the largest by cargo volume. Natural gas is extracted from fields in the northern and central basins. Tourism is particularly vital for Croatia and Montenegro, with the Adriatic coast being a primary destination.

## Terms
- **Karst topography:** A landscape formed from the dissolution of soluble rocks like limestone, characterized by sinkholes, caves, and rugged terrain.
- **Dilution basin:** A semi-enclosed sea that receives a large influx of freshwater, lowering its salinity compared to the open ocean.
- **Acqua alta:** Italian for "high water," referring to the periodic tidal flooding in Venice.
- **Bora:** A cold, dry, northerly wind that blows down from the Dinaric Alps.
- **Sirocco:** A warm, humid southerly wind that often carries Saharan dust.
- **Apulian (Adriatic) Microplate:** A small tectonic plate on which the Adriatic Sea and the Apennine Peninsula lie.
- **Endemic:** A species native and restricted to a certain place or area.
- **Overfishing:** Catching fish faster than stocks can replenish, leading to population decline.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Maritime Border Disputes:** Following Yugoslavia's dissolution, successor states have disputed maritime borders, notably between Croatia and Slovenia in the Gulf of Piran and between Croatia and Montenegro in the Bay of Kotor.
- **Environmental Threats:** The long-term impact of pollution, climate change, and potential oil/gas exploration on the Adriatic's unique ecosystems and biodiversity remains a significant concern.
- **Venice's Future:** The effectiveness and environmental consequences of the MOSE flood barrier project in protecting Venice from rising sea levels and *acqua alta* are subjects of ongoing debate.
