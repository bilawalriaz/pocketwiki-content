# Rembrandt

## Overview
Rembrandt Harmenszoon van Rijn (1606–1669) was a Dutch Golden Age painter, printmaker, and draughtsman widely considered one of the greatest visual artists in Western history. He produced roughly 300 paintings, 300 etchings, and hundreds of drawings spanning portraits, self-portraits, landscapes, genre scenes, and biblical narratives. Unlike many contemporaries, he never traveled abroad but absorbed Italian Renaissance and Baroque influences through Dutch artists who had studied in Italy. His career traced an arc from early commercial success as a portraitist in Amsterdam to later bankruptcy and personal tragedy, yet his artistic reputation remained high throughout his life. His approximately 100 self-portraits (over 40 painted) form an unparalleled visual autobiography, while his innovative use of *chiaroscuro* (strong light-dark contrasts) and rough, tactile brushwork in later works revolutionized the depiction of psychological depth and material presence.

## Body

### Early Life and Training
Rembrandt was born in Leiden to a well-to-do family; his father was a miller, his mother a baker’s daughter. His parents belonged to different faiths (mother Catholic, father Dutch Reformed), a detail scholars link to the religious complexity of his art. After Latin school and brief enrollment at Leiden University (1620), he apprenticed with history painter Jacob van Swanenburg for three years. A pivotal six-month apprenticeship with Pieter Lastman in Amsterdam (c. 1624–25) introduced him to dramatic biblical compositions and Caravaggesque lighting. He may have also studied briefly with Jacob Pynas. By 1625, he had opened an independent studio in Leiden, sharing space with Jan Lievens, and began accepting students like Gerrit Dou.

### Amsterdam Success and Personal Tragedy (1631–1642)
Rembrandt moved to Amsterdam in late 1631, lodging with Hendrick van Uylenburgh, whose workshop supplied portrait commissions. He married Hendrick’s cousin Saskia in 1634, gaining citizenship and guild membership. The 1630s brought high-profile patrons (Constantijn Huygens, Prince Frederik Hendrik, Andries de Graeff) and major works: *The Anatomy Lesson of Dr. Nicolaes Tulp* (1632), *The Blinding of Samson* (1636), and *Belshazzar’s Feast* (c. 1635–38). He purchased a grand house on the Breestraat in 1639 (13,000 guilders), situating himself in the emerging Jewish quarter, whose residents modeled for his Hebrew Bible scenes. Despite affluence, the couple lost three infants; only Titus (b. 1641) survived. Saskia died in 1642, weeks after *The Night Watch* was completed. Rembrandt’s drawings of her deathbed are among his most affecting works.

### Domestic Turmoil and Financial Collapse (1642–1658)
After Saskia’s death, Geertje Dircx became Titus’s nurse and Rembrandt’s lover. Their relationship ended in a 1649 breach-of-promise suit; Dircx pawned a diamond ring from Saskia’s estate. The court ordered Rembrandt to pay maintenance, but after Dircx violated terms, he had her committed to a Gouda correction house (1650). Meanwhile, Hendrickje Stoffels entered the household as maid (1649), becoming Rembrandt’s common-law wife; their daughter Cornelia was born in 1654. Hendrickje was summoned by the Reformed Church for "whoredom," admitted guilt, and was barred from communion; Rembrandt was not summoned. Remarriage would have forfeited Titus’s trust from Saskia’s will.

Rembrandt’s spending on art, curiosities, and the Breestraat mortgage outpaced income. Despite a 1655 sale of paintings and a 1656 voluntary *cessio bonorum* (surrender of assets to creditors), proceeds were disappointing. The 1657 auction catalog listed 363 items: Old Master paintings, Roman busts, Greek statues, Asian porcelain, natural history specimens, and works by Raphael, Mantegna, and Giorgione. The house was foreclosed in 1658; the family moved to Rozengracht. A dummy corporation formed by Hendrickje and Titus (1660) allowed Rembrandt to keep working, though he lost guardianship of Titus and control of his estate.

### Late Career and Death (1658–1669)
Despite insolvency, Rembrandt received major commissions: *Sampling Officials* (1662), *The Jewish Bride* (c. 1665–69), and the Town Hall’s *Conspiracy of Claudius Civilis* (1661–62), which was rejected and cut down (only a fragment survives in Stockholm). His late style featured richer colors, pronounced brushwork, jagged light, and intimate, portrait-like biblical figures (e.g., *James the Apostle*, 1661). He painted fifteen self-portraits from 1652–1669, culminating in *Self-Portrait at the Age of 63* (1669). Cosimo III de’ Medici visited twice, acquiring a self-portrait. Hendrickje died in 1663; Titus died in 1668. Rembrandt died on 4 October 1669, buried in a rented grave in the Westerkerk. Cornelia emigrated to Batavia (1670); Titus’s daughter Titia inherited his estate.

### Artistic Style and Periods
Rembrandt’s style evolved from an early "smooth" manner (fine detail, illusionistic form) to a late "rough" manner (tactile, variegated paint surfaces suggesting form through texture). In Leiden (1625–31), Lastman’s influence yielded small, detailed works with religious/allegorical subjects and *tronies* (character studies). Early Amsterdam (1632–36) saw large, high-contrast biblical/mythological scenes emulating Rubens (*The Blinding of Samson*, *Danaë*). By the late 1630s, landscapes appeared (*The Three Trees*, 1643), often dramatic with ominous skies. Post-*Night Watch* (1642), compositions grew more classical: frontal lighting, saturated color, figures parallel to the picture plane, possibly reflecting Venetian influence (*Susanna and the Elders*, 1637–47). The 1650s brought the "rough" style: coarse brushwork, harsh light, minimal shine, layered paint suggesting form individually—critics called it unfinished, but Rembrandt dissuaded close viewing. Late biblical works shifted from group drama to intimate, psychologically charged figures (*The Jewish Bride*).

### Graphic Works: Etchings and Drawings
Rembrandt etched from 1626 to 1660 (forced to sell his press). He mastered the medium’s freedom, moving from drawing-based line work to painting-based masses of line and multiple acid bitings. The *Hundred Guilder Print* (c. 1648), reworked across the 1640s, marks the emergence of his mature etching style. In the 1650s, he improvised on the plate (up to eleven states), used hatching for darks, printed on Japanese paper and vellum, exploited drypoint’s fuzzy burr, and employed "surface tone" (leaving ink film for atmospheric effect). Subjects mirrored paintings but with more self-portraits (27 etchings) and fewer portraits of others. Landscapes (c. 30 etchings, 200 drawings, only 8 paintings) set the graphic standard until the 19th century. One-third of etchings are religious, often homely; a few erotic compositions have no painted equivalents. He collected works by Caravaggio, Mantegna, Van Dyck, Raphael, Titian, Rubens, Seghers, and Castiglione, whose influences permeate his prints.

### Asian Inspiration
In the 1650s, Rembrandt drew approximately 23 copies of Mughal miniatures (possibly from an album he owned), depicting emperors Shah Jahan, Akbar, Jahangir, and Dara Shikoh. These may have influenced costumes and details in his biblical and historical scenes.

### The Night Watch (1640–42)
Commissioned for the Kloveniersdoelen (musketeers’ hall), *The Militia Company of Captain Frans Banninck Cocq* broke convention: instead of a static, formal group portrait, Rembrandt depicted a dynamic moment—the firing of a musket—affecting figures differentially. The title "Night Watch" is a misnomer; darkness comes from aged varnish, and the scene is a daytime ceremony. The work operates at multiple levels: lifelike individual portraits within a contrived, theatrical structure; an invented street setting; allusions to Raphael’s *School of Athens*; possible humor or parody. The calm foreground officers, continuing duty amid chaos behind them, have been read as embodying "moral excellence."

### Workshop and Pupils
Rembrandt ran a large workshop with many pupils, blurring lines between assistants, imitators, and independent followers. A partial list includes Ferdinand Bol, Gerrit Dou, Willem Drost, Carel Fabritius, Govert Flinck, Aert de Gelder, Samuel van Hoogstraten, Nicolaes Maes, Godfrey Kneller, and Christopher Paudiß. Students copied his self-portraits as training; he sometimes retouched their work for sale as originals or authorized copies. His evolving style and uneven quality, plus later imitations and damaging restorations, complicate attribution.

### Attribution and the Rembrandt Research Project (RRP)
Founded in 1968, the RRP used technical diagnostics to reassess attributions, producing a new *corpus raisonné*. It reduced the accepted painting count from ~600 (early 20th c.) to ~300. Controversial cases include *The Polish Rider* (Frick Collection): attributed to pupil Willem Drost by RRP’s Josua Bruyn (1980s), but defended as Rembrandt by Simon Schama and Ernst van de Wetering (1990s); the Frick retains the Rembrandt label. *Aristotle with a Bust of Homer* (Met) was challenged by Schama/Paul Crenshaw, arguing the figure is painter Apelles, not Aristotle. *Pilate Washing His Hands* is widely assigned to an anonymous pupil (possibly Aert de Gelder). Re-attributions continue: four paintings upgraded to Rembrandt in 2005; *Old Man Sitting in a Chair* (1652) defended as a key experimental work by Van de Wetering (2014). Studio practice—student copies, master retouching, stylistic fluidity—remains the core attribution challenge.

### Painting Materials
Pigment analysis (Hermann Kühn, 1977; later National Gallery studies) reveals a palette of lead white, ochres, van Dyke brown, bone/charcoal/lamp black, vermilion, madder lake, azurite, ultramarine, yellow lake, lead-tin-yellow. Rarely used pure blue/green (exception: *Belshazzar’s Feast*). Synthetic orpiment (arsenic yellow) appeared in shadows of *The Jewish Bride*; gamboge reported in *Saskia as Flora*. Technical data, infrared, and radiography are compiled in the Rembrandt Database.

### Name and Signature
"Rembrandt" (with *d*) was adopted in 1633, modifying his birth spelling "Rembrant." The change was visual, not phonetic. Early signatures: "R" or "RH" (c. 1625); "RHL" (1629, L for Leiden); "RHL-van Rijn" (early 1632); "Rembrant" (late 1632); "Rembrandt" (1633 onward). Documents during his life mostly retained "Rembrant." Signing by first name alone followed Raphael, Leonardo, and Michelangelo; later emulated by Vincent van Gogh.

### Museum Collections
The U.S. holds the most paintings (86), notably at the Met, Frick, National Gallery (DC), MFA Boston, Getty. Germany has 69 (Berlin, Dresden, Kassel); UK 51 (National Gallery, Royal Collection); Netherlands 49 (Rijksmuseum—*Night Watch*, *Jewish Bride*; Mauritshuis). Major drawing collections: Rijksmuseum, Louvre, British Museum. The Rembrandt House Museum holds most etchings. Major print rooms (Rijksmuseum, British Museum, Morgan Library) hold 1,000+ impressions of the ~300 plates; 79 original copper plates survived as of 1986.

## Terms
- **Chiaroscuro**: Theatrical use of strong light-dark contrasts to model volume and direct attention; derived from Caravaggio or Dutch Caravaggisti, adapted by Rembrandt for psychological depth.
- **Tronie**: A Dutch term for a character or type study (not a commissioned portrait), often featuring exaggerated expression or costume; common in Rembrandt’s early Leiden period.
- **Cessio bonorum**: A legal surrender of assets to creditors (voluntary bankruptcy) under Dutch law; Rembrandt invoked this in July 1656 to avoid forced liquidation.
- **Surface tone**: In etching, deliberately leaving a thin film of ink on the plate during wiping to create atmospheric, tonal effects in the printed impression.
- **Drypoint**: An intaglio technique where the needle raises a burr on the copper plate, yielding a rich, fuzzy line in early impressions; Rembrandt exploited this for landscapes.
- **Hundred Guilder Print**: *Christ Healing the Sick* (c. 1648), so named because a print seller allegedly bought an impression back from Rembrandt for 100 guilders; the "critical work" bridging his early and mature etching styles.
- **Rembrandt Research Project (RRP)**: Founded 1968; interdisciplinary team using scientific diagnostics to authenticate works and compile a new *corpus raisonné*; reduced accepted paintings to ~300.
- **Impasto**: Thick application of paint creating tactile texture; central to Rembrandt’s late "rough" manner, where paint itself suggests form and light.
- **Kloveniersdoelen**: The Amsterdam headquarters of the civic militia’s musketeer branch; *The Night Watch* was commissioned for its new hall.
- **Mughal miniatures**: Small, detailed Indian court paintings (Mughal Empire); Rembrandt copied ~23 in the 1650s, influencing his costume and figure details.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authorship of contested paintings**: *The Polish Rider* (Rembrandt vs. Willem Drost), *Aristotle with a Bust of Homer* (subject: Aristotle vs. Apelles), *Pilate Washing His Hands* (Rembrandt vs. anonymous pupil/Aert de Gelder), *Old Man Sitting in a Chair* (experimental autograph work vs. studio copy).
- **Extent of autograph oeuvre**: Painting count stabilized near 300 by RRP, but drawings remain disputed—some experts argue only ~75 drawings are "certain," others reject this low figure.
- **Faith and religious affiliation**: Never formally joined a church; mother Catholic, father Reformed; biblical subjects span Hebrew Bible and New Testament. Scholars debate whether his faith was personal, ecumenical, or pragmatic for patrons.
- **Cause of financial ruin**: Overspending on art/collections vs. market downturn (plague, war) vs. mortgage structure; degree to which he overvalued his own collection in insolvency inventories.
- **Workshop production vs. master’s hand**: Blurred boundaries due to student copies, master retouching, authorized copies, and stylistic evolution make definitive attribution impossible for many works.
- **Meaning of *beweegelijkheid***: In a letter to Huygens, Rembrandt cited "de meeste en de natuurlijkste beweegelijkheid" ("the greatest and most natural movement/emotion/motive") as his artistic aim; whether this refers to narrative momentum, emotional expression, or physical gesture remains unresolved.
- **Rejection of *Claudius Civilis***: Why the Town Hall mayors rejected it (composition, subject, political subtext?) and why Rembrandt cut it down—only a quarter survives.
