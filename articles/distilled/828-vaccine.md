# Vaccine

## Overview
A vaccine is a biological preparation that provides active acquired immunity against specific infectious or malignant diseases. It typically contains an agent resembling a disease-causing microorganism—made from weakened, killed, or partial forms of the microbe, its toxins, or surface proteins—which stimulates the immune system to recognize and destroy the agent and "remember" it for future encounters. Vaccination is the most effective method of preventing infectious diseases, responsible for the global eradication of smallpox and the restriction of polio, measles, and tetanus. The WHO reports licensed vaccines for twenty-five preventable infections. While overwhelming scientific consensus confirms vaccines are safe and effective, limitations exist due to host factors (genetics, age, immune status) and vaccine-specific factors (strain mismatch, waning immunity). Vaccine hesitancy—delay or refusal despite availability—was characterized by the WHO in 2019 as a top ten global health threat.

## Timeline
- **10th century** — Taoists in China reportedly practice a form of inoculation (oral tradition; not written down).
- **15th century** — Oldest documented use of variolation (nasal insufflation of powdered smallpox scabs) in China.
- **1721** — Lady Mary Wortley Montagu introduces variolation from Turkey to Britain; variolates her daughter.
- **1796** — Edward Jenner inoculates James Phipps with cowpox, demonstrating protection against smallpox.
- **1798** — Jenner publishes *Inquiry into the Variolae vaccinae*, coining the term "vaccine" (from *Variolae vaccinae*, cowpox).
- **1840** — Smallpox inoculation (variolation) banned in England; vaccination with cowpox preferred.
- **1880s** — Louis Pasteur develops second-generation vaccines for chicken cholera and anthrax.
- **1881** — Pasteur proposes extending terms "vaccine" and "vaccination" to all protective inoculations.
- **1931** — Woodruff and Goodpasture grow fowlpox virus in embryonated chicken eggs.
- **1935** — Yellow fever vaccine developed using egg-based propagation.
- **1945** — Influenza vaccine developed using egg-based propagation.
- **1950s** — Polio vaccine developed.
- **1958** — US records 763,094 measles cases and 552 deaths; post-vaccine median drops to 56 cases/year.
- **1959** — Growth media and cell culture replace eggs as standard virus propagation method.
- **1960s–1970s** — Smallpox eradicated globally.
- **2003** — Transgenic plants identified as potential bioreactors for vaccine production.
- **2005** — Bananas engineered to produce human hepatitis B vaccine.
- **2016** — DNA vaccine for Zika virus begins testing at NIH.
- **2019** — WHO lists vaccine hesitancy as a top ten global health threat.
- **2020** — mRNA vaccines (e.g., BNT162b2) developed via Operation Warp Speed for COVID-19.
- **2021** — Karikó and Weissman receive Horwitz Prize for mRNA vaccine technology.

## Body

### Mechanism and Effectiveness
Vaccines stimulate the immune system to recognize an agent as foreign, destroy it, and develop immunological memory. Upon later exposure to the virulent pathogen, the body neutralizes the target before it enters cells and destroys infected cells before the agent multiplies. This memory response involves innate immunity (activated within ~12 hours) and adaptive immunity (1–2 weeks to fully develop). Effectiveness is demonstrated by the eradication of smallpox and drastic reductions in polio, measles, mumps, rubella, and tetanus. High population coverage creates **herd immunity**, making outbreaks difficult. Vaccines also reduce antibiotic resistance by lowering infection incidence (e.g., *Streptococcus pneumoniae* pneumonia). The measles vaccine alone prevents an estimated one million deaths annually.

### Limitations
Protection can fail due to vaccine factors (attenuation failures, regimen errors) or host factors. Host non-response occurs in an estimated 2–10% of individuals due to genetics, immune status, age, health, or nutrition. **Immunosenescence** describes reduced response in older adults. **Adjuvants** (e.g., alum) are added to boost response, especially in older populations. Immunity may be partial (<100% effective) or temporary (waning over time), but still raises the reinfection threshold and mitigates severity. Breakthrough infections in vaccinated individuals are typically less severe and less transmissible. Efficacy depends on the disease, vaccine strain match, schedule adherence, and host factors (ethnicity, age, genetics). Effective programs require epidemiological modeling, post-introduction surveillance, and maintenance of high immunization rates even when diseases become rare.

### Safety
Vaccines are generally safe; adverse effects are usually mild (fever, injection site pain, muscle aches). Severe side effects are extremely rare (e.g., varicella vaccine complications in immunodeficient individuals; rotavirus vaccine association with intussusception). Allergic reactions to ingredients can occur. At least 19 countries operate no-fault compensation programs for severe adverse events (US: National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act; UK: Vaccine Damage Payment). **Thiomersal** (a mercury-containing preservative) was removed from most childhood vaccines in affluent countries as a precaution, though no convincing evidence links it to autism. A 10–11 year study of 657,461 children found the MMR vaccine does not cause autism and reduced autism risk by 7%.

### Vaccine Types
Vaccines employ different strategies to induce immunity while minimizing risk.

*   **Attenuated (Live):** Contain live, weakened microorganisms (viruses or bacteria) that replicate without causing disease. Examples: yellow fever, measles, mumps, rubella (MMR), typhoid, BCG (tuberculosis). Advantages: durable immunity, elicits both cellular and humoral responses. Disadvantages: unsafe for immunocompromised; rare reversion to virulence.
*   **Inactivated (Killed):** Microorganisms killed by physical/chemical means. Examples: IPV (polio), hepatitis A, rabies, most influenza vaccines. Cannot revert to virulence but often require boosters.
*   **Toxoid:** Inactivated toxins (toxoids) rather than the organism. Examples: tetanus, diphtheria, *Crotalus atrox* toxoid (rattlesnake bites for dogs).
*   **Subunit:** Use specific fragments (proteins/polysaccharides) of the pathogen. Examples: hepatitis B (surface proteins in yeast), HPV (virus-like particles), influenza (hemagglutinin/neuraminidase).
*   **Conjugate:** Link poorly immunogenic bacterial polysaccharide coats to carrier proteins to elicit T-cell dependent responses. Example: *Haemophilus influenzae* type B (Hib).
*   **Outer Membrane Vesicle (OMV):** Naturally immunogenic vesicles from bacterial membranes. Example: Serotype B meningococcal vaccines.
*   **Heterotypic (Jennerian):** Use pathogens from other animals that cause mild/no disease in the target species. Example: Cowpox/smallpox; BCG (*M. bovis*) for tuberculosis.
*   **Genetic (Third Generation):** Deliver nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) into host cells to produce antigen.
    *   *Viral Vector:* Safe virus delivers pathogen genes (e.g., adenovirus, VSV vectors).
    *   *RNA (mRNA):* RNA in lipid nanoparticles instructs cells to make antigen (e.g., Pfizer-BioNTech, Moderna COVID-19 vaccines).
    *   *DNA:* Plasmid DNA encoding antigen; stable, inexpensive, stimulates B- and T-cell responses.
*   **Experimental:** Dendritic cell vaccines (cancer), recombinant vectors (RVSV-ZEBOV for Ebola), T-cell receptor peptides, bacterial vectors, inverse vaccines (induce tolerance), synthetic peptides.

### Valence and Interactions
**Monovalent** vaccines target a single antigen/organism; **multivalent (polyvalent)** target multiple strains or organisms (e.g., bivalent, trivalent, quadrivalent). When combined, components can interfere, especially live attenuated vaccines where a robust strain suppresses others. This occurred in the trivalent Sabin polio vaccine (serotype 2 suppressed 1 and 3, requiring dose adjustment) and dengue vaccines (DEN-3 predominated).

### Composition (Excipients)
Beyond the antigen, vaccines contain:
*   **Adjuvants** (Aluminum salts/alum): Enhance immune response, allow lower antigen dose.
*   **Preservatives** (Thiomersal, phenoxyethanol, formaldehyde): Prevent bacterial/fungal contamination in multi-dose vials. A 1928 diphtheria vaccine lacking preservative caused 12 of 21 children to die from *Staphylococcus* infection.
*   **Stabilizers** (MSG, 2-phenoxyethanol): Maintain potency during heat/light/humidity exposure.
*   **Residuals:** Antibiotics (prevent bacterial growth during production), egg protein (influenza, yellow fever), formaldehyde (inactivation), thiomersal (multi-dose vials, now <1µg mercury/dose).

### Licensing and Regulation
Licensure requires successful Phase I–III clinical trials demonstrating safety, immunogenicity, efficacy, and duration of protection. High safety standards apply as vaccines are given to healthy populations. **WHO** pre-qualifies vaccines for UNICEF procurement, requiring Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) compliance. **EU** licenses centrally (all states), decentralized (some states), or nationally via EMA/CHMP. **US (FDA)** requires a Biologics License Application with facility inspection and labeling compliance. **India** regulates via the Drugs Controller General (CDSCO). **Postmarketing surveillance (Phase IV)** is mandatory: WHO coordinates globally; US uses VAERS (Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System).

### Scheduling
Children are vaccinated as soon as immune systems respond adequately, with boosters for full immunity. Schedules are complex (up to 24 injections by age two in US). WHO’s Strategic Advisory Group of Experts issues global recommendations; national committees (e.g., US ACIP) adapt for local epidemiology, equity, and finances. Combination vaccines (Pentavalent, MMRV) improve compliance. Specific vaccines target adolescents (HPV), pregnant women (rubella screening), elderly (pneumonia, influenza, shingles), and pandemic scenarios (dose-sparing strategies).

### Economics and Production
Development faces market failures: diseases needing vaccines most (HIV, malaria, TB) prevail in poor countries; financial returns in wealthy nations are minimal. Development relies on "push" funding (governments, non-profits). **Patents** are not the primary barrier to production in developing countries; rather, substantial financial, infrastructure, and skilled workforce requirements dominate. Vaccines are complex biologics with no true generics; new facilities require full clinical testing. During COVID-19, IP waivers were debated to scale manufacturing.

Production is distinct from standard pharma due to administration to millions of healthy people, demanding extreme rigor. Facilities cost $50M–$500M; development takes 10–15 years. Top manufacturers (2013): Merck, Sanofi, GSK, Pfizer, Novartis (70% sales in EU/US). **Stages:** 1) Antigen generation (viruses in eggs/cell lines; bacteria in bioreactors; recombinant proteins in yeast/cells). 2) Isolation/purification (inactivation, ultrafiltration, chromatography). 3) Formulation (adjuvants, stabilizers, preservatives). 4) Fill/finish (vialing/packaging—often a bottleneck). **Trends:** Cultured mammalian cells replacing eggs; recombination for detoxified toxoids; combination vaccines using pathogen-associated molecular patterns to reduce antigen load. Developing country manufacturers (Serum Institute of India, Sinopharm, Brazil) increasingly supply domestic/regional markets.

### Delivery Systems
*   **Injection:** Most common.
*   **Oral:** No blood contamination risk; solid forms stable, reduce cold chain needs. Oral polio vaccine enabled administration by untrained volunteers.
*   **Microneedle:** Arrays of microscopic projections create skin pathways (in development).
*   **Dermal Patch:** Stamp-size patch with ~20,000 projections/cm²; needle-free, potentially more effective with less antigen (animal testing).

### Veterinary Medicine
Vaccines prevent disease in pets/livestock and zoonotic transmission to humans. Wild populations vaccinated via bait (e.g., rabies in raccoons). Rabies vaccination of dogs often legally required. **DIVA vaccines** (Differentiating Infected from Vaccinated Animals) use strains lacking a specific epitope (e.g., gE gene deletion in pseudorabies/Aujeszky's disease) paired with a diagnostic test detecting antibodies to that epitope. This allows eradication programs to distinguish vaccine-induced from infection-induced immunity. Successfully used to eradicate pseudorabies in several countries; BoHV-1 DIVA vaccines used in EU control programs. Veterinary vaccines can be orders of magnitude cheaper than human equivalents (notably rabies). Human exposure to veterinary pathogens (e.g., *Bordetella bronchiseptica* via aerosol vaccination) has increased.

### History
Pre-Jenner: Variolation (deliberate smallpox infection) practiced in China (10th/15th century nasal insufflation), Turkey, and introduced to Britain (1721). **Jenner (1796):** Cowpox protects against smallpox; arm-to-arm transfer reduced reliance on cows. **Pasteur (1880s):** Second generation (chicken cholera, anthrax); vaccines become national prestige, leading to compulsory laws. **20th Century:** Egg-based propagation (1931), yellow fever (1935), influenza (1945), cell culture replaces eggs (1959). Polio vaccine (1950s), smallpox eradication (1960s–70s). Maurice Hilleman: most prolific 20th-century developer. Elusive targets remain: herpes, malaria, gonorrhea, HIV.

### Generations of Vaccines
*   **First:** Whole organism (live attenuated or killed). Live induce killer T-cell (CTL), helper T-cell (Th), and antibody responses; risk reversion in immunocompromised. Killed lack CTL response.
*   **Second:** Subunit (purified proteins, toxoids, recombinant proteins). Induce Th and antibody; no CTL response. Safer.
*   **Third:** Genetic (DNA, RNA). DNA vaccine for Zika tested 2016; mRNA vaccines (BNT162b2) deployed 2020 for COVID-19 (Operation Warp Speed). Karikó/Weissman Horwitz Prize 2021.

### Trends
Since ~2013: Synthetic third-generation vaccines reconstructing viral surfaces to prevent resistance. Tailor-made vaccines for non-infectious diseases (cancers, autoimmune, hypertension e.g., CYT006-AngQb). Drivers: translational medicine, demographics, regulatory science, socio-political factors. **Plants as bioreactors:** Transgenic tobacco, potato, tomato, banana producing vaccines (hepatitis B in bananas, 2005).

## Terms
- ****Active acquired immunity**** — Immunity developed by the host's own immune system in response to an antigen (e.g., from a vaccine), creating immunological memory.
- ****Adjuvant**** — Substance added to a vaccine (e.g., aluminum salts/alum) to enhance the magnitude and durability of the immune response.
- ****Attenuated vaccine**** — Vaccine containing live microorganisms weakened so they replicate but do not cause disease in healthy hosts.
- ****Breakthrough infection**** — Infection occurring in a vaccinated individual; typically less severe and less transmissible than in unvaccinated cases.
- ****Cold chain**** — Temperature-controlled supply chain required to maintain vaccine potency from manufacture to administration.
- ****DIVA vaccine**** — "Differentiating Infected from Vaccinated Animals" vaccine; lacks a specific epitope found in wild pathogen, allowing diagnostic distinction between vaccine and infection antibodies.
- ****Herd immunity**** — Population-level protection occurring when a high proportion of individuals are immune, disrupting transmission chains and protecting non-immune individuals.
- ****Immunosenescence**** — Age-related decline in immune function, resulting in reduced vaccine response in older adults.
- ****mRNA vaccine**** — Genetic vaccine delivering messenger RNA in lipid nanoparticles; host cells translate the RNA to produce the target antigen.
- ****Variolation**** — Historical practice of deliberate inoculation with smallpox virus (scabs/pus) to induce immunity; higher risk than vaccination.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Eradication timelines:** Polio eradication (endemic in Afghanistan, Nigeria, Pakistan) repeatedly missed due to access difficulties, cultural misunderstandings, and disinformation.
*   **Vaccine hesitancy:** Despite overwhelming consensus on safety/efficacy, hesitancy (refusal/delay) drives outbreaks; declared a top 10 global health threat by WHO (2019).
*   **IP vs. Capacity:** Debate on whether patent waivers (e.g., COVID-19) effectively increase global supply versus the view that infrastructure/workforce are the true bottlenecks.
*   **Universal vaccines:** Research into synthetic/reconstructed vaccines targeting conserved viral structures to prevent resistance and cover multiple strains.
*   **Non-infectious targets:** Expanding vaccinology to cancer, autoimmune disorders, and chronic conditions (e.g., hypertension) remains experimental.
*   **Plant-based production:** Scalability, regulatory approval, and dosage consistency of transgenic plant bioreactors are unproven at scale.
*   **Delivery innovation:** Microneedle patches and dermal systems promise cold-chain independence and dose-sparing but remain largely experimental.
