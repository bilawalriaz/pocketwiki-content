# Jacques Cartier

## Overview
Jacques Cartier (1491–1557) was a French-Breton navigator who conducted three voyages to the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the St. Lawrence River between 1534 and 1542. Commissioned by Francis I to find a passage to Asia and precious metals, Cartier became the first European to map the Gulf, name the territory "Canada" (from the Iroquoian *kanata*, meaning village), and document the St. Lawrence Iroquoians at Stadacona (Quebec City) and Hochelaga (Montreal). His voyages established France’s territorial claim to the interior of North America, later colonized as New France, though his own colonization attempt at Charlesbourg-Royal (1541–42) failed due to harsh winters, scurvy, mineral misidentification, and deteriorating Indigenous relations.

## Timeline
- **31 Dec 1491** — Born in Saint-Malo, Brittany.
- **20 Apr 1534** — Departs on first voyage; explores Gulf of St. Lawrence, claims Gaspé for France, kidnaps two sons of Chief Donnacona.
- **19 May 1535** — Departs on second voyage with three ships and 110 men; reaches Stadacona and Hochelaga; blocked by Lachine Rapids.
- **Nov 1535 – Apr 1536** — Winter at Stadacona; scurvy kills 25 Frenchmen; Iroquoian remedy (annedda/arbor vitae) saves survivors.
- **May 1536** — Kidnaps Chief Donnacona; returns to Saint-Malo 16 July 1536.
- **23 May 1541** — Departs on third voyage (five ships) to find "Kingdom of Saguenay" and establish settlement; Jean-François de La Rocque de Roberval named lieutenant general.
- **Aug–Sep 1541** — Founds Charlesbourg-Royal at Cap-Rouge; collects "diamonds" and "gold" later identified as quartz and iron pyrite.
- **Jun 1542** — Abandons colony after Iroquoian attacks kill ~35 settlers; flees to France, evading Roberval.
- **1 Sep 1557** — Dies in Saint-Malo during an epidemic, possibly typhus.
- **18 Aug 2006** — Archaeologists announce rediscovery of Charlesbourg-Royal site via 16th-century Italian pottery and timber remains.

## Body

### Early Life and Commission
Born in the Breton port of Saint-Malo, Cartier was a respected mariner who married into the aristocratic des Granches family in 1520. In 1534, two years after Brittany’s formal union with France via the Edict of Union, Bishop Jean Le Veneur introduced Cartier to Francis I. Citing Cartier’s experience in Newfoundland and Brazil, Le Veneur secured a royal commission to seek a western passage to the East Indies and lands rumored to hold gold.

### First Voyage (1534): Reconnaissance and Claim
Cartier crossed the Atlantic in twenty days, exploring Newfoundland, the Strait of Belle Isle, Labrador, the Gaspé Peninsula, Prince Edward Island, Anticosti, and the Magdalen Islands. At Îles aux Oiseaux, his crew slaughtered roughly 1,000 great auks (extinct by 1852). He encountered Mi'kmaq at Chaleur Bay and St. Lawrence Iroquoians at Gaspé Bay. On 24 July, he planted a 10-metre cross inscribed "Long Live the King of France," claiming the land. The Iroquoians’ hostile reaction indicated they understood the claim’s implication. Cartier kidnapped Chief Donnacona’s two sons, Domagaya and Taignoagny, who revealed the region was called *Honguedo*. Cartier returned to France believing he had reached Asia.

### Second Voyage (1535–36): Penetration and Wintering
Sailing upriver for the first time, Cartier reached Stadacona and, using his smallest ship, continued to Hochelaga (2 Oct 1535), a palisaded town of over 1,000 people. The Lachine Rapids blocked further progress; convinced the river was the Northwest Passage, Cartier named the rapids *La Chine* (China). Forced to winter at Stadacona (Nov 1535–Apr 1536), the fleet froze in ice over a fathom thick. Scurvy devastated both populations; Cartier estimated 50 Iroquoian deaths. Domagaya provided the cure: a decoction from *annedda* (likely *Thuja occidentalis*, arbor vitae, or spruce beer), which saved the 85 surviving Frenchmen. Before departing in May 1536, Cartier kidnapped Chief Donnacona to testify in France about the "Kingdom of Saguenay," a northern land allegedly rich in gold and rubies.

### Third Voyage (1541–42): Failed Colonization
Francis I ordered Cartier to support a colonization project as "captain general," but in Jan 1541, the Huguenot noble Jean-François de La Rocque de Roberval was appointed lieutenant general, demoting Cartier to chief navigator. Cartier sailed ahead in May 1541 with five ships. Abandoning the search for a passage, the goals were now Saguenay’s riches and a permanent settlement. Finding Stadacona’s Iroquoians numerous and "worrisome," Cartier settled at Cap-Rouge, founding the fortified **Charlesbourg-Royal**. Colonists planted gardens and collected minerals believed to be diamonds and gold. In September, Cartier reconnoitered toward Saguenay but was halted by rapids and weather at Hochelaga. Upon return, relations had collapsed: Iroquoians killed ~35 settlers. Despite the scurvy cure (Thuja infusion), Cartier, convinced his force was insufficient, abandoned the colony in June 1542. He slipped away from Roberval’s fleet off Newfoundland, arriving in France with worthless quartz and pyrite—origin of the phrase *"faux comme les diamants du Canada."* Roberval briefly occupied the site but abandoned it in 1543.

### Legacy and Historical Significance
Cartier located the St. Lawrence entrance, opening the primary artery for French penetration of the continent. He documented the name *Canada* (derived from *kanata*, misapplied from "village" to the territory and river) and *Canadiens* (the Iroquoians). Though not the first European in the region (Norse, Basque, Breton, and possibly Corte-Real or Cabot preceded him), he was the first to systematically explore the interior via the St. Lawrence, consolidating France’s claim to New France. His three voyages lost no ships and navigated ~50 unknown harbours, marking him as a highly competent navigator. He was also an early European to recognize the New World as a landmass distinct from Asia.

## Terms
- ****St. Lawrence Iroquoians**** — Indigenous people inhabiting the St. Lawrence Valley (Stadacona, Hochelaga) in the 16th century; distinct from later Haudenosaunee (Five Nations) and Wendat (Huron); extinct or dispersed by c. 1580.
- ****Kanata**** — Iroquoian word for "village" or "settlement"; recorded by Cartier as *Canada*, initially applied to Stadacona and the river, later to the French colony and eventually the modern nation.
- ****Lachine Rapids**** — Impassable rapids near Montreal (Sault Saint-Louis); named *La Chine* by Cartier, who believed they were the only barrier to the Northwest Passage to China.
- ****Annedda / Arbor Vitae**** — Tree (*Thuja occidentalis*, white cedar) whose bark/needles produced a Vitamin C-rich decoction curing scurvy; taught to Cartier by Domagaya in 1536.
- ****Kingdom of Saguenay**** — Legendary northern realm described by Donnacona’s sons as rich in gold, rubies, and spices; drove French exploration and colonization policy but never found.
- ****Charlesbourg-Royal**** — Cartier’s 1541 fortified settlement at Cap-Rouge (near Quebec City); first French colonization attempt in Canada since the Norse; abandoned 1542.
- ****Faux comme les diamants du Canada**** — French proverb ("false as Canadian diamonds") originating from the worthless quartz and iron pyrite Cartier brought back in 1542.
- ****Edict of Union (1532)**** — Treaty formally uniting the Duchy of Brittany with the Kingdom of France, contextualizing Cartier’s service to the French crown.

## Debates and open questions
- **Location of Hochelaga**: The exact site of the Iroquoian town visited in 1535 remains archaeologically unconfirmed, though Cartier places it near the Sainte-Marie Sault (modern Montreal).
- **Identity of *annedda***: While *Thuja occidentalis* is the leading candidate, some scholars argue for spruce (*Picea* spp.) or balsam fir (*Abies balsamea*) as the source of the scurvy cure.
- **Fate of the St. Lawrence Iroquoians**: The population encountered by Cartier vanished before Champlain’s arrival (1603); causes debated include dispersal, warfare with Haudenosaunee/Mi'kmaq, and epidemic disease.
- **Cartier’s desertion of Roberval (1542)**: Sources differ on whether Cartier fled due to genuine strategic despair or insubordination; his "slipping off under cover of darkness" suggests a breach of command.
- **Mineral fraud vs. error**: Whether Cartier knowingly shipped worthless ore to secure funding or genuinely misidentified quartz/pyrite remains unresolved.
