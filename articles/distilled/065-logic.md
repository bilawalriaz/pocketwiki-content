# Logic

## Overview
Logic is the study of correct reasoning, encompassing formal logic (the study of deductively valid inferences and logical truths based on argument structure) and informal logic (associated with fallacies, critical thinking, and argumentation theory). It examines how conclusions follow from premises, playing a central role in philosophy, mathematics, computer science, and linguistics. Logic has been studied since antiquity, evolving from Aristotelian syllogisms to modern formal systems like classical logic, with extended and deviant logics addressing specialized fields and alternative principles.

## Timeline
- **Antiquity** — Aristotle develops term logic (syllogisms) in his Organon; logic also develops independently in India (Nyaya, Buddhism, Jainism) and China (School of Names, Mohism).
- **c. 11th century** — Ibn Sina (Avicenna) founds Avicennian logic, replacing Aristotelian logic in the Islamic world; develops temporally modalized syllogistic and inductive methods.
- **1323** — William of Ockham releases Summa Logicae, a comprehensive treatise on logic.
- **19th century** — Modern symbolic logic emerges; George Boole invents Boolean algebra, Charles Peirce develops the logic of relatives, and Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift is often seen as the birthplace of modern logic.
- **Early 20th century** — Alfred North Whitehead and Bertrand Russell condense insights in Principia Mathematica; logicism program (reducing mathematics to logic) fails via Russell's paradox and Gödel's incompleteness theorems.
- **20th century** — Classical logic (propositional and first-order logic) becomes the standard system; extended logics (modal, higher-order) and deviant logics (intuitionistic, multi-valued, paraconsistent) are developed.

## Body

### Definition and Formal vs. Informal Logic
The word "logic" originates from the Greek λόγος (logos), meaning 'reason', 'discourse', or 'language'. Traditionally defined as the study of the laws of thought or correct reasoning, logic is understood in terms of inferences (the activity of drawing conclusions) and arguments (the outward expression of inferences, consisting of premises supporting a conclusion). Formal logic (or symbolic logic) uses abstract symbols to examine the logical form of arguments independent of content, making it topic-neutral. It focuses on deductively valid arguments, where true premises ensure a true conclusion, and on logical truths—propositions true solely due to their logical structure, like "either it is raining, or it is not." Formal logic uses formal languages with limited vocabulary and exact syntactic rules to construct well-formed formulas. Informal logic analyzes arguments in natural language, prompted by difficulties applying formal logic to everyday discourse; it addresses ambiguity, vagueness, and context-dependence, and is variously defined as the study of non-deductive arguments, informal fallacies, or the normative standards of argumentation.

### Basic Concepts
Premises and conclusions are truth-bearers (true or false), seen either as propositions (abstract objects, the denotations of sentences) or as sentences (concrete linguistic objects); a psychological approach (psychologism) is not widely accepted today. Propositions have internal structure: complex propositions link simpler ones via connectives like "and" (∧) or "if...then" (→), while simple propositions consist of singular terms and predicates. Truth tables show how connectives work—for example, "p ∧ q" is true only when both p and q are true. Arguments are correct if premises support conclusions; deductive arguments guarantee truth, while ampliative arguments (inductive and abductive) arrive at new information but only make conclusions probable. Inductive arguments are statistical generalizations (e.g., inferring all ravens are black from observations); abductive arguments are inferences to the best explanation (e.g., a doctor diagnosing a disease from symptoms). Fallacies are arguments with flawed reasoning, divided into formal fallacies (errors in form, like denying the antecedent) and informal fallacies (errors in content or context, like false dilemmas). Definitory rules determine which inferences are correct, while strategic rules specify how to reach conclusions effectively. Formal systems consist of a formal language, axioms (statements accepted without proof), a proof system, and often a semantics mapping expressions to denotations; a system is sound if its proof system cannot derive false conclusions and complete if it can derive all true conclusions.

### Systems of Logic
Aristotelian logic (term logic or syllogistics) dominated the West for over two thousand years. A syllogism has three propositions (two premises, one conclusion), each with a subject, predicate, and copula; it classifies syllogisms as valid or invalid based on their form. Classical logic encompasses propositional logic (built from atomic propositions using connectives) and first-order logic (which articulates internal structure via singular terms, predicates, and quantifiers like "some" and "all"). It is based on intuitions like the law of excluded middle, double negation elimination, the principle of explosion, and bivalence of truth. Extended logics accept classical principles and add vocabulary: modal logic introduces symbols for possibility (◊) and necessity (◻), with variants like deontic logic (obligation/permission), temporal logic, and epistemic logic (knowing vs. believing); higher-order logics allow quantification over predicates, increasing expressive power but with meta-logical drawbacks. Deviant logics reject classical intuitions: intuitionistic logic excludes double negation elimination and the law of excluded middle, based on truth as verification by proof; multi-valued logics reject bivalence (e.g., Łukasiewicz and Kleene's ternary logics, fuzzy logics with infinite degrees of truth); paraconsistent logics avoid the principle of explosion, often motivated by dialetheism (the view that contradictions are real). Informal logic frameworks include the dialogical approach (arguments as speech acts in games of persuasion, per Douglas N. Walton) and the epistemic approach (arguments aiming to increase knowledge, including Bayesian approaches based on degrees of belief or credences).

### Areas of Research
Philosophy of logic examines the scope, nature, and presuppositions of logic; philosophical logic applies logical methods to philosophical problems. Metalogic studies properties of formal systems, including soundness, completeness, consistency, decidability, and the relation between syntax and semantics. Mathematical logic (in the restricted sense) studies logic within mathematics, covering model theory, proof theory, set theory, and computability theory; it includes the logicism program (Frege, Whitehead, Russell) and set theory's origins in Georg Cantor's study of the infinite. Computational logic implements reasoning using computers, including automatic theorem provers and logic programming languages like Prolog; Claude Shannon showed how Boolean logic can implement computer circuits via logic gates. Formal semantics uses formal tools to give precise theories of natural language meaning, often in relation to truth conditions and the principle of compositionality, with early work by Richard Montague and Barbara Partee. The epistemology of logic studies how one knows arguments are valid; the traditional view holds this is a priori knowledge, but theorists like Hilary Putnam and Penelope Maddy argue logical truths depend on the empirical world, citing quantum mechanics as potentially refuting classical principles like distributivity.

### History
Logic developed independently in several cultures. Aristotle's term logic was highly regarded in classical and medieval times, remaining in wide use until the early 19th century. Ibn Sina founded Avicennian logic, which replaced Aristotelian logic in the Islamic world and influenced Western medieval writers; Fakhr al-Din al-Razi criticized Aristotelian syllogistics and formulated an early system of inductive logic. In the Middle Ages, Boethius's translations and textbooks were influential, and Islamic philosophers' works expanded the range of ancient texts available to Christian scholars. In China, the School of Names (including Gongsun Long's white horse paradox) and Mohism were influential. In India, logic was pursued by Nyaya, Buddhist, and Jainist schools, often in the context of epistemology; later Navya-Nyāya theories resemble modern logic, such as Frege's distinction between sense and reference. Modern symbolic logic developed in the mid-19th century, with Frege's Begriffsschrift often seen as its birthplace, Leibniz's idea of a universal formal language as a forerunner, and contributions from Boole and Peirce. First-order logic became the standard system, enabling the formalization of mathematics, Tarski's model theory, and the foundation of modern mathematical logic.

## Terms
- **Premise**: A proposition that supports a conclusion in an argument.
- **Conclusion**: The proposition that follows from premises in an argument.
- **Deductive validity**: An argument property where true premises guarantee a true conclusion.
- **Ampliative argument**: An argument whose conclusion contains information not found in its premises (inductive or abductive).
- **Fallacy**: An incorrect argument with a flaw in reasoning, not necessarily a false conclusion.
- **Formal system**: A formal language plus axioms and a proof system for drawing inferences.
- **Soundness**: A system property where the proof system cannot derive false conclusions.
- **Completeness**: A system property where the proof system can derive all true conclusions.
- **Syllogism**: An Aristotelian argument form with two premises and a conclusion sharing three terms.
- **Modal logic**: An extended logic adding symbols for possibility and necessity.

## Debates and open questions
- Whether premises and conclusions are propositions (abstract objects) or sentences (concrete objects) is debated; philosophical naturalists reject abstract objects, while sentence-based approaches face context-dependence issues.
- Whether deductive inferences are uninformative is contested; some distinguish surface information (explicit) from depth information (implicit and explicit), arguing deductions can be informative on the surface level.
- There is no general agreement on the precise definition of informal logic; characterizations include the study of natural language arguments, non-deductive arguments, informal fallacies, or the normative standards of argumentation.
- Whether higher-order logics qualify as logics in the strict sense is disputed, with some theorists arguing only logically complete systems like first-order logic qualify.
- Whether logic is knowable a priori is debated; some argue logical truths depend on the empirical world, citing quantum mechanics as evidence for quantum logic replacing classical logic.
