# Sergei Rachmaninoff

## Overview

Sergei Rachmaninoff (1873–1943) was a Russian composer, virtuoso pianist, and conductor, widely considered one of the finest pianists of his day and one of the last great representatives of Romanticism in Russian classical music. His personal idiom is notable for song-like melodicism, expressiveness, dense contrapuntal textures, and rich orchestral colours, with the piano featuring prominently in his output. His life was shaped by a disastrous symphony premiere that triggered a three-year depression, his permanent departure from Russia after the 1917 Revolution, and a subsequent career as a touring pianist in the US that sharply reduced his compositional output.

## Body

### Early life and education

Rachmaninoff was born into a family of Russian aristocracy with strong musical and military leanings; his paternal grandfather studied with Irish composer John Field. His father's financial incompetence forced the sale of the family's five estates, and the family moved to Saint Petersburg. After his sister Sofia died of diphtheria and his father left the family, his maternal grandmother helped raise the children and took him to Russian Orthodox Church services, where he first encountered liturgical chants and church bells—two features he would incorporate in his compositions. After failing his spring exams at the Saint Petersburg Conservatory, his mother sought advice from Alexander Siloti, who recommended transferring him to the Moscow Conservatory to study under the stricter Nikolai Zverev.

At the Moscow Conservatory, Rachmaninoff studied advanced piano with Siloti, counterpoint with Sergei Taneyev, and free composition with Anton Arensky. He graduated in 1892 with the Great Gold Medal, a distinction previously awarded only to Taneyev and Arseny Koreshchenko. His one-act opera Aleko, written in seventeen days based on Pushkin's *The Gypsies*, premiered successfully at the Bolshoi Theatre and earned him the highest mark. His public debut as a pianist came in September 1892 with the premiere of his landmark Prelude in C-sharp minor, which became one of his most popular and enduring pieces.

### Depression and recovery

Following Tchaikovsky's death in 1893, Rachmaninoff entered a decline. The premiere of his Symphony No. 1 in March 1897 was brutally panned by critic César Cui, who likened it to a depiction of the seven plagues of Egypt. The performance, conducted by Alexander Glazunov, was poor—Glazunov made poor use of rehearsal time and may have been drunk. Rachmaninoff experienced a depression lasting three years, describing it as being "like a man who had suffered a stroke." He composed little until, between January and April 1900, he underwent daily hypnotherapy and supportive therapy sessions with physician Nikolai Dahl, which reignited his desire to compose. He completed the Piano Concerto No. 2 in April 1901, dedicated to Dahl; it was enthusiastically received and earned him the first of five Glinka Awards.

### Career and emigration

Rachmaninoff married his first cousin Natalia Satina in 1902; because the marriage was forbidden under Russian Orthodox canon law, they organised a small ceremony in a Moscow suburb army barracks chapel. He conducted at the Bolshoi Theatre from 1904 to 1906, pioneering the modern arrangement of orchestra players in the pit and the custom of standing while conducting. Unhappy with political turmoil in Russia, he moved to Dresden, Germany, in 1906, where he wrote his Symphony No. 2 and The Isle of the Dead, inspired by a reproduction of Arnold Böcklin's painting. His first US tour in 1909–10 included 26 performances; the second performance of Piano Concerto No. 3 was conducted by Gustav Mahler, an experience Rachmaninoff treasured.

After the February 1917 Revolution, Rachmaninoff found his Ivanovka estate seized by Social Revolutionary Party members. He accepted an offer to perform ten piano recitals across Scandinavia as an excuse to obtain exit permits, leaving Russia on 22 December 1917. After declining three US offers, he accepted an advance loan and arrived in New York on 12 November 1918. He chose Steinway pianos—the only manufacturer that did not offer him money—beginning a lifelong association. His compositional output slowed dramatically: in the 24 years between his arrival in the US and his death, he completed just six new pieces. He admitted: "I left behind my desire to compose: losing my country, I lost myself also." He completed his final composition, the Symphonic Dances, in 1940.

### Works and compositional style

Rachmaninoff wrote five works for piano and orchestra: four concertos (No. 1 in F-sharp minor, No. 2 in C minor, No. 3 in D minor, No. 4 in G minor) and the Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini (1934). He composed three symphonies, four symphonic poems, 24 Preludes traversing all 24 major and minor keys, two sets of Études-Tableaux, two piano sonatas, two major a cappella choral works (Liturgy of St. John Chrysostom and All-Night Vigil), three completed one-act operas, and 83 songs, all written before he left Russia.

His style was initially influenced by Tchaikovsky, but by the mid-1890s began showing a more individual tone. He made distinctive use of unusually widely spaced chords for bell-like sounds, and frequently used the Dies irae chant motif. His writing featured chromatic counterpoint, learned from his studies with Taneyev. His later works showed a near-Impressionist style with less emphasis on pure melody and more on colouring, as in the Op. 38 songs and Op. 39 Études-Tableaux.

### Pianist and legacy

Rachmaninoff ranked among the finest pianists of his time, famed for a clean and virtuosic technique, unusually powerful left hand, and crystal-clear textures. He possessed large hands that could play a twelfth. His interpretations were carefully planned around each piece's "culminating point," a practice learned from bass Feodor Chaliapin. He recorded for Edison Records in 1919, then signed with Victor Talking Machine Company in 1920, recording until 1942. He also produced 35 piano rolls for the American Piano Company (Ampico) from 1919 to 1929.

His reputation as a composer generated varied opinions; the 1954 Grove Dictionary dismissed his music as "monotonous in texture," a statement Harold C. Schonberg called "one of the most outrageously snobbish and even stupid statements ever to be found in a work that is supposed to be an objective reference." Monuments to Rachmaninoff stand in Moscow, Veliky Novgorod, and Knoxville, Tennessee.

## Terms

- **Romanticism**: A musical movement emphasising emotion, individuality, and expressiveness; Rachmaninoff was one of its last great representatives in Russian music.
- **Counterpoint**: The combination of two or more independent melodic lines; Rachmaninoff had great command of this, thanks to his studies with Taneyev.
- **Études-Tableaux**: "Study pictures"—demanding piano pieces that combine technical exercises with pictorial or emotional imagery.
- **Dies irae**: A Gregorian chant of the Catholic Requiem Mass; Rachmaninoff frequently used fragments of its first phrase as a motif.
- **A cappella**: Choral music performed without instrumental accompaniment, as in Rachmaninoff's All-Night Vigil.
- **Ampico**: The American Piano Company's reproducing piano system; Rachmaninoff made 35 piano rolls for it.
- **Glinka Award**: A Russian music prize; Rachmaninoff received five throughout his life.
- **Marfan syndrome**: A hereditary disorder of connective tissue suggested as an explanation for Rachmaninoff's large hands and various ailments, though he did not show many typical signs.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars have speculated that Rachmaninoff's hand size, height, and slender frame indicate he may have had Marfan syndrome, which would account for back pain, arthritis, and eye strain. An article in the Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine instead suggested acromegaly, which could explain stiffness in his hands, repeated periods of depression, and possibly even his melanoma. The disastrous premiere of Symphony No. 1 remains debated: critics did not comment on the deficiencies of Glazunov's conducting, but witnesses suggested he made poor use of rehearsal time and may have been drunk.
