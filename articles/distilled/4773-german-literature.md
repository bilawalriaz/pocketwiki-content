# German literature

## Overview
German literature encompasses literary works written in the German language across Germany, Austria, Switzerland, and other regions. It spans from the medieval period to the present, reflecting major historical shifts and philosophical movements. Its study reveals the evolution of a major European literary tradition, marked by distinct periods and influential authors.

## Timeline
- **c. 750–1050** — Old High German period; key works include *Hildebrandslied* and *Heliand*.
- **1170–1230** — Middle High German "golden age"; flourishing of Minnesang and courtly romances like *Parzival*.
- **1600–1720** — Baroque period; literature reflects the Thirty Years' War, e.g., Grimmelshausen's *Simplicius Simplicissimus*.
- **1750s–1770s** — Sensibility (Empfindsamkeit) movement, ending with Goethe's *The Sorrows of Young Werther* (1774).
- **1760s–1780s** — Sturm und Drang movement, emphasizing emotion over Enlightenment rationalism.
- **1786–1805** — Weimar Classicism, led by Goethe and Schiller.
- **Late 18th–early 19th c.** — German Romanticism is the dominant literary movement.
- **1815–1848** — Biedermeier period, contrasting with the preceding Romantic era.
- **1848–1890** — Poetic Realism focuses on everyday life and bourgeois society.
- **1880–1900** — Naturalism aims for scientific objectivity in depicting social conditions.
- **1933–1945** — Nazi regime leads to exile literature (Exilliteratur) and "internal emigration."
- **1945–1989** — Post-war literature emerges in West and East Germany, including Group 47 and Holocaust literature.

## Body

### Medieval and Early Modern Periods
Medieval German literature begins with the Carolingian dynasty and ends around the Reformation (1517). The Old High German period (until mid-11th century) produced early translations and heroic verse. The Middle High German period (12th–14th centuries) saw a "golden age" of courtly love poetry (Minnesang) and Arthurian romances. The Baroque period (1600–1720) was highly fertile, with writers like Grimmelshausen and Opitz responding to the Thirty Years' War.

### 18th and 19th Centuries
The Enlightenment gave way to Sensibility, which culminated in Goethe's *Werther*. This was followed by Sturm und Drang, which stressed emotion and intuition. Weimar Classicism, led by Goethe and Schiller, sought balance and form. German Romanticism then became dominant, valuing humor, the medieval past, and the irrational. The Biedermeier period (1815–1848) offered a more domestic focus. Later, Poetic Realism depicted everyday life with restraint, while Naturalism (1880–1900) used stark realism to expose social injustice.

### 20th Century and Beyond
Early 20th-century literature included Expressionism and Dada. Under the Nazi regime, authors either went into exile (Exilliteratur) or practiced "internal emigration." Post-1945, literature dealt with the war's aftermath in West Germany (e.g., Trümmerliteratur, Group 47) and the GDR. Contemporary literature includes diverse genres and migrant perspectives. German-language authors have won the Nobel Prize in Literature fourteen times (as of 2023).

### Children's Literature
The Brothers Grimm collected and published folktales, first in *Children and Household Tales* (1812), establishing a methodology for folklore studies. Other classics include Heinrich Hoffmann's *Der Struwwelpeter* (1845) and Wilhelm Busch's *Max and Moritz* (1865).

## Terms
- **Minnesang**: The German tradition of courtly love poetry, flourishing in the Middle Ages.
- **Sturm und Drang**: A late 18th-century movement emphasizing individual emotion and extremes of feeling in response to Enlightenment rationalism.
- **Weimar Classicism**: A cultural movement (c. 1786–1805) led by Goethe and Schiller, seeking harmony and classical ideals.
- **Biedermeier**: The period (1815–1848) in literature and art focused on domesticity and the bourgeois, contrasting with Romanticism.
- **Poetic Realism**: A 19th-century literary movement (1848–1890) depicting everyday life with accuracy and artistic refinement.
- **Naturalism**: A late 19th-century movement aiming for scientific objectivity in portraying social and environmental determinism.
- **Exilliteratur**: Literature produced by German-speaking authors who went into exile during the Nazi regime.
- **Innere Emigration**: "Internal emigration"; the practice of authors remaining in Nazi Germany while avoiding direct collaboration through subtle dissent.
- **Trümmerliteratur**: "Rubble literature"; post-WWII German literature dealing with the physical and moral devastation of the war.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact end date of the German literary Middle Ages is debated, with the Reformation (1517) being the last possible cut-off.
- Periodization becomes more contested the closer it gets to the present day.
- The relationship and boundaries between movements like Sturm und Drang, Weimar Classicism, and early Romanticism are subjects of scholarly discussion.
