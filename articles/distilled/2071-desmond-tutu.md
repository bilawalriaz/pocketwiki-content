# Desmond Tutu

## Overview
Desmond Tutu (1931–2021) was a South African Anglican bishop and theologian who became a global symbol of the anti-apartheid struggle. As a leading non-violent activist, he used his moral authority and international platform to campaign for racial equality and human rights. His legacy is defined by his role in South Africa's transition to democracy and his advocacy for reconciliation, justice, and human dignity worldwide.

## Timeline
- **7 October 1931** — Born in Klerksdorp, South Africa.
- **1960** — Ordained as an Anglican priest.
- **1962–1966** — Studies theology at King's College London.
- **1975** — Becomes the first black Dean of St Mary's Cathedral, Johannesburg.
- **1978–1985** — Serves as General-Secretary of the South African Council of Churches (SACC).
- **1984** — Awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for his role in the anti-apartheid struggle.
- **1985–1986** — First black Bishop of Johannesburg.
- **1986–1996** — First black Archbishop of Cape Town.
- **1994** — Attends the inauguration of Nelson Mandela as South Africa's first democratically elected president.
- **1996–1998** — Chairs the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC).
- **26 December 2021** — Dies in Cape Town at age 90.

## Body

### Early Life and Theological Formation
Born to a poor family of mixed Xhosa and Motswana heritage, Tutu initially trained as a teacher. He left the profession in protest of the 1953 Bantu Education Act, which enforced racial segregation in schools. Influenced by the priest Trevor Huddleston, he entered the Anglican clergy, being ordained in 1960. A period of study at King's College London (1962–1966) was formative, freeing him from the daily indignities of apartheid and shaping his theological approach, which later fused black theology with African theology.

### Anti-Apartheid Activism and Church Leadership
Tutu rose to prominence as General-Secretary of the SACC (1978–1985), using the position to become one of apartheid's most visible opponents. He consistently advocated for non-violent protest and international economic sanctions, warning that the system would lead to racial violence. His stance drew criticism from both the white-minority government, which confiscated his passport, and some black activists who deemed him too moderate. He was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1984. As Bishop of Johannesburg (1985–1986) and then Archbishop of Cape Town (1986–1996), he continued his activism, mediating conflicts and calling for sanctions until the dismantling of apartheid.

### The Truth and Reconciliation Commission
Following the end of apartheid, Tutu was appointed by President Nelson Mandela to chair the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC), established in 1995. The TRC's mandate was to investigate past human rights abuses committed by all sides. Tutu advocated for "restorative justice" over retribution, based on the African philosophy of *ubuntu* (a person is a person through other persons). The process involved public hearings where victims and perpetrators testified, with amnesty offered in exchange for full disclosure. While the TRC was praised for helping avoid a racial civil war, it also faced criticism for potentially granting impunity.

### Later Life and Global Advocacy
After retiring as archbishop in 1996, Tutu remained a prominent global voice. He campaigned for gay rights within the Anglican Church, criticized the policies of South African presidents Thabo Mbeki and Jacob Zuma, and spoke out against the Iraq War and the Israeli government's treatment of Palestinians, which he likened to apartheid. He chaired the global group The Elders from 2007 to 2013 and continued to advocate for climate justice and human rights until his death.

## Terms
*   **Apartheid:** The system of institutionalized racial segregation and discrimination enforced by the National Party government in South Africa from 1948 until the early 1990s.
*   **Black Theology:** A theological perspective which originated in African-American churches, interpreting the Bible as a message of liberation for the oppressed and focusing on the struggle for social justice.
*   **Ubuntu:** A Nguni Bantu term meaning "humanity," often translated as "a person is a person through other persons," emphasizing community, compassion, and interconnectedness.
*   **Restorative Justice:** A model of justice that focuses on the needs of victims and the repair of harm, rather than solely on punishing the offender. It was the guiding principle of the TRC.
*   **Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC):** A court-like restorative justice body assembled in South Africa after the end of apartheid to investigate past human rights abuses.
*   **Bantu Education Act:** A 1953 South African law that enforced racially segregated education, designed to prepare black students for a life of manual labor.
*   **South African Council of Churches (SACC):** An ecumenical council of Christian churches in South Africa that became a major anti-apartheid organization under Tutu's leadership.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Effectiveness of Sanctions:** Scholars debate whether international economic sanctions were the most effective tool to end apartheid or if they primarily harmed ordinary black South Africans.
*   **The TRC's Legacy:** There is ongoing debate about whether the TRC successfully achieved reconciliation or if it prioritized peace over justice by allowing perpetrators to avoid full criminal punishment.
*   **Tutu's Political Stance:** Tutu was frequently criticized as being "too moderate" by militant anti-apartheid activists and "too radical" by white conservatives and government officials, placing him in a perpetual middle ground that satisfied neither side.
*   **Theological Influence:** The extent to which Tutu successfully fused black theology and African theology, versus simply advocating for both, remains a point of scholarly discussion.
