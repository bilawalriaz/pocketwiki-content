# Elbe

## Overview
The Elbe is one of Central Europe's major rivers, flowing 1,094 km from the Czech Republic's Giant Mountains through Germany to the North Sea. It has historically served as a critical geographical, political, and economic boundary, delineating empires, trade routes, and even the Cold War divide between East and West Germany.

## Timeline
- **c. 200,000 years ago** — Humans first lived in the northern Elbe region during the Middle Paleolithic.
- **9 AD** — The Battle of the Teutoburg Forest ends Roman attempts to expand their border from the Rhine to the Elbe.
- **Middle Ages** — The Elbe forms the eastern limit of Charlemagne's Empire and is vital to Hanseatic League trade.
- **10th century** — The Ottonian Dynasty begins conquering Slavic lands east of the Elbe, starting a process of Germanization.
- **1922** — The statute of the International Commission of the Elbe, established by the Treaty of Versailles, is signed.
- **25 April 1945** — Western Allied and Soviet forces link up near Torgau on the Elbe, marking "Elbe Day."
- **1949–1990** — The Elbe forms part of the Inner German border between East and West Germany.

## Body

### Course and Geography
The Elbe rises at 1,386 m in the Czech Giant Mountains. It flows south through Bohemia, joining major tributaries like the Vltava at Mělník and the Ohře at Litoměřice. After entering Germany, it flows northwest across the North German Plain, passing cities like Dresden, Magdeburg, and Hamburg. Its basin covers 148,268 km² across four countries, primarily Germany (65.5%) and the Czech Republic (33.7%).

### Navigation and Engineering
The river has long been a key trade route, linked by canals to Berlin and the Baltic Sea. The Magdeburg Water Bridge allows shipping to cross the Elbe without entering the river. In Hamburg, historical dikes created branch streams like the Gose Elbe and Dove Elbe to protect marshlands and improve the port. The tidal section, the Unterelbe, begins downstream of the Geesthacht sluice.

### Historical Significance
The Elbe has been a defining European boundary. The Romans knew it as *Albis* but failed to make it their frontier. In the Middle Ages, it marked the edge of Charlemagne's Empire and the extent of Hanseatic trade. East of the river, in "East Elbia," stricter serfdom and larger estates led to the rise of the *Junkers* landowning class. In World War II, the river became the meeting point of Allied and Soviet forces in 1945 and later the Inner German border.

## Terms
- **Catchment area:** The total land area from which a river and its tributaries collect water.
- **Anabranch:** A section of a river that splits off and rejoins the main channel.
- **Reaction ferry:** A cable ferry that uses the river's current for propulsion.
- **Soccage:** A historical system of land tenure where peasants owed labor or produce to a lord.
- **Junkers:** A class of Prussian landowners, particularly dominant east of the Elbe.
- **Polabian Slavs:** Slavic tribes who settled east of the Elbe and Saale rivers from the 6th century.
- **Unterelbe (Low Elbe):** The tidal section of the Elbe downstream from Geesthacht.
- **Niederelbe (Lower Elbe):** The section of the river from the Mühlenberger Loch bay to the North Sea.
