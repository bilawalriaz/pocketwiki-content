# Port-au-Prince

## Overview
Port-au-Prince is the capital and most populous city of Haiti, serving as the nation's primary economic, political, and cultural center. Its history reflects the broader narrative of Hispaniola, from indigenous Taíno settlement through colonial exploitation, revolutionary upheaval, and modern challenges including natural disasters and severe gang violence.

## Timeline
- **c. 2600 BC** — Taíno people arrive in Hispaniola.
- **1492** — Christopher Columbus arrives; Spanish colonization begins.
- **1606** — Spanish abandon the region.
- **1697** — Treaty of Ryswick: Spain renounces claims to western Hispaniola.
- **1749** — Port-au-Prince is founded by the French.
- **1770** — Port-au-Prince replaces Cap-Français as capital of Saint-Domingue.
- **1804** — Haiti declares independence; Port-au-Prince becomes its capital.
- **1915–1934** — American occupation of Haiti.
- **12 January 2010** — A 7.0 magnitude earthquake devastates the city.
- **2020–present** — Port-au-Prince is paralyzed by extensive gang violence.

## Body

### Etymology and Founding
The name "Port-au-Prince" translates to "Prince's Port," possibly named after the ship *Le Prince* (1706), though the bay's islets were called *les îlets du Prince* earlier. The area was previously known as "Hôpital" after a hospital for French filibusters (pirates). After the filibusters were subdued, the French colonial administration chose the site for a new capital in 1749 due to its central location and defensible topography, replacing other contenders like Léogâne and Petit-Goâve.

### Colonial and Revolutionary History
The region was part of the Taíno Xaragua chiefdom before Spanish colonization decimated the native population. After Spain abandoned the area in 1606, French filibusters established a presence, which grew into the colony of Saint-Domingue. Port-au-Prince became the colony's capital in 1770. It was burned during the 1791 slave revolt, captured by the British in 1794, and became the capital of independent Haiti in 1804. Following the assassination of Emperor Jacques I in 1806, the city served as the capital of the southern republic under Alexandre Pétion until Haiti's reunification in 1820.

### 20th Century to Present
The city was garrisoned during the American occupation (1915–1934). On 12 January 2010, a catastrophic 7.0 earthquake destroyed much of the city's historic center, including the National Palace and Parliament. The government estimated the death toll at 230,000. Since 2020, the city has been crippled by gang violence, with kidnappings, massacres, and rapes becoming common, often with alleged police and political complicity.

### Geography and Demographics
The city is laid out like an amphitheater on the Gulf of Gonâve, with commercial districts near the natural harbor and residential areas on the hills. The metropolitan area includes communes like Delmas, Cité Soleil, and the affluent Pétion-Ville. Population estimates vary widely due to rapid slum growth; the metropolitan area was estimated at 2.6 million in 2022. The population is predominantly of African descent, with prominent biracial, Arab, and Asian minorities.

### Economy and Infrastructure
Port-au-Prince is Haiti's main economic hub, exporting coffee and sugar and hosting food-processing and textile factories. High unemployment and underemployment are offset by a large informal economy. The Toussaint Louverture International Airport is the primary international gateway. The Port international de Port-au-Prince handles most of the country's shipping but is in poor condition and underused. Public transportation relies heavily on "tap-taps," privately owned, brightly painted share taxis.

### Culture and Society
Cultural life centers on the Champ de Mars plaza and the National Palace grounds. Key sites include the National Museum, the Musée d'Art Haïtien, and the Hotel Oloffson. The city hosts the annual Fet Gede Vodou celebration at the national cemetery. Education ranges from international schools like Union School to the State University of Haiti. Crime rates are high, with specific zones like Cité Soleil and Bel Air experiencing extreme violence.

## Terms
- **Filibustiers**: French pirates and privateers who established bases in the Caribbean during the 17th century.
- **Taíno**: The indigenous people of the Caribbean, including Hispaniola, who were decimated after European contact.
- **Tap-taps**: Privately owned, brightly painted pickup trucks converted into share taxis, forming the backbone of public transportation in Port-au-Prince.
- **Caco rebels**: Haitian insurgents who fought against the U.S. occupation (1915–1934).
- **Cité Soleil**: A notorious, densely populated slum commune within the Port-au-Prince metropolitan area, known for extreme poverty and violence.
- **Pétion-Ville**: An affluent suburban commune southeast of downtown Port-au-Prince, home to much of the Haitian elite.
- **Champ de Mars**: The central square plaza in Port-au-Prince, site of the National Palace and other key government buildings.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact origin of the name "Port-au-Prince" is debated, with theories linking it to a ship, pre-existing islet names, or an unknown prince.
- The true population of the metropolitan area is difficult to ascertain due to rapid, unplanned growth of slums and a lack of recent, reliable census data.
- The causes and extent of police and political complicity in the city's ongoing gang violence are subjects of ongoing investigation and concern.
