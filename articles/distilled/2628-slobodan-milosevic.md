# Slobodan Milošević

## Overview
Slobodan Milošević was a Yugoslav and Serbian politician who served as President of Serbia (1989–1997) and President of the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (1997–2000). He played a central role in the Yugoslav Wars of the 1990s and became the first sitting head of state charged with war crimes. His rule is characterized by the rise of Serbian nationalism, authoritarian governance, and economic turmoil, culminating in his overthrow, extradition to The Hague, and death during his trial.

## Timeline
- **20 August 1941** — Born in Požarevac, Kingdom of Yugoslavia.
- **1987** — Emerges as a political force by supporting Serbs in Kosovo; ousts mentor Ivan Stambolić.
- **1988–1989** — Leads the "Anti-Bureaucratic Revolution," installing allies in Vojvodina and Montenegro.
- **28 March 1989** — Serbian Assembly approves constitutional amendments reducing provincial autonomy.
- **1990** — Elected President of Serbia; Socialist Party of Serbia founded.
- **1991–1995** — Yugoslav Wars in Slovenia, Croatia, and Bosnia and Herzegovina.
- **24 May 1999** — Indicted for war crimes by the ICTY during the Kosovo War.
- **24 September 2000** — Loses Yugoslav presidential election to Vojislav Koštunica.
- **5 October 2000** — Overthrown following mass protests (the "Bulldozer Revolution").
- **1 April 2001** — Arrested by Yugoslav authorities on corruption charges.
- **28 June 2001** — Extradited to the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY).
- **11 March 2006** — Dies of a heart attack in his cell in The Hague before his trial concludes.

## Body

### Rise to Power and Political Style
Milošević rose through the League of Communists of Serbia, leveraging his connection to Ivan Stambolić. His political breakthrough came in 1987 during a visit to Kosovo, where he declared support for Serbs claiming oppression by Albanian authorities. This stance, and the subsequent "Anti-Bureaucratic Revolution" (1988–1989), allowed him to oust political rivals and install allies in the autonomous provinces of Vojvodina and Kosovo, as well as in Montenegro, giving his faction control over half the votes in the Yugoslav presidency. His rule is described as authoritarian, populist, and opportunistic, relying on ethnic ultranationalism and socialist rhetoric for mass mobilization. Scholars have drawn parallels between his regime and fascism in practice, noting its use of paramilitary formations and propaganda that vilified other ethnic groups.

### Role in the Yugoslav Wars
Milošević's nationalist agenda, which aimed to unite Serb-populated areas, is considered a key factor in the breakup of Yugoslavia. The ICTY alleged he endorsed a plan for a "Greater Serbia" achieved by forcibly removing non-Serbs. While direct orders for atrocities were never found, he provided crucial support—personnel, arms, and funding—to Serb forces in Croatia and Bosnia. He denied Serbia was at war, claiming only indirect involvement. His government's media promoted fear and hostility towards other ethnicities. Significant anti-war protests occurred within Serbia, and hundreds of thousands deserted the army or emigrated to avoid conscription.

### Economic Policies and Civil Rights
Milošević's economic policies combined socialist and liberal ideas, but his rule was marked by kleptocracy, transferring state assets to allies. UN sanctions due to the wars caused economic collapse and hyperinflation, which peaked at 313 million percent in January 1994. A later stabilization program ended hyperinflation, but growth was insufficient to recover pre-war levels. Civil rights were severely restricted, with media censorship, authoritarian penal codes punishing criticism of the state, and suppression of press freedom.

### Downfall, Trial, and Death
Milošević was forced from power in October 2000 after losing a presidential election he had scheduled prematurely. Following his arrest in 2001, he was extradited to the ICTY in The Hague under pressure from the international community. He was charged with genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity in Kosovo, Croatia, and Bosnia. He conducted his own defense, denouncing the tribunal as illegal. He died of a heart attack in 2006 before a verdict was reached. Posthumous tribunal verdicts found he was part of a joint criminal enterprise to remove non-Serbs through ethnic cleansing, though the International Court of Justice found no evidence he directly committed genocide in Bosnia.

## Terms
*   **Anti-Bureaucratic Revolution:** A series of protests (1988–1989) that led to the ousting of governments in Vojvodina and Montenegro and their replacement with Milošević allies.
*   **ICTY (International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia):** A United Nations court of law established to try war crimes committed during the Yugoslav Wars.
*   **Joint Criminal Enterprise:** A legal doctrine used by the ICTY to assign criminal responsibility to individuals who participated in a common plan to commit crimes.
*   **Kleptocracy:** A form of corrupt government where leaders use their power to exploit national resources and steal public funds for personal gain.
*   **Greater Serbia:** A nationalist ideology aiming to unite all ethnic Serb-populated territories into a single state, often cited as a goal of Milošević's policies.
*   **Ethnic Cleansing:** The forced removal of an ethnic or religious group from a given area, often involving violence and murder, to create an ethnically homogeneous territory.
*   **Bulldozer Revolution:** The popular name for the mass protests in Belgrade on 5 October 2000 that led to Milošević's resignation.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Spontaneity of the Anti-Bureaucratic Revolution:** Supporters claim it was a genuine grassroots movement, while critics argue Milošević cynically orchestrated it to seize power.
*   **Milošević's Nationalism:** Was he a genuine Serbian nationalist or a political opportunist who exploited nationalism as a tool for power? Observers like U.S. Ambassador Warren Zimmermann described him as the latter.
*   **Direct Responsibility for War Crimes:** While the ICTY's posthumous verdicts found him part of a joint criminal enterprise, the court in the Radovan Karadžić case noted a lack of evidence that he agreed to the specific plan for ethnic cleansing in Bosnia, though it acknowledged he provided assistance to Bosnian Serbs.
*   **Genocide in Bosnia:** The International Court of Justice (ICJ) concluded in 2007 that there was no evidence linking Milošević directly to genocide committed by Bosnian Serb forces, but found he had violated the Genocide Convention by failing to prevent it.
