# Mahabharata

## Overview
The Mahābhārata is one of the two major Sanskrit epics of ancient India, revered in Hinduism. It narrates the dynastic struggle between the Kaurava and Pandava cousins for the throne of Hastinapura, culminating in the great Kurukshetra War. Beyond its narrative, the epic is a vast repository of philosophical, devotional, and ethical teachings, including the Bhagavad Gita, and is considered a foundational text of Indian culture and religion.

## Timeline
- **c. 4th century BCE** — Earliest external references to the epic (Panini's Ashtadhyayi).
- **c. 3rd century BCE – 3rd century CE** — Bulk of the Mahābhārata is compiled.
- **c. 4th century CE** — Text likely reaches its final form in the early Gupta period.
- **200 CE** — Oldest surviving Sanskrit manuscript dates to the Kushan Period.
- **1919–1966** — The Critical Edition is produced by the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute.

## Body

### Textual History and Structure
Traditionally attributed to the sage Vyāsa, the Mahābhārata is classified as an *itihasa* (history). It employs a frame-story structure, with the narrative first recited by the sage Vaiśampāyana to King Janamejaya, and later by the storyteller Ugraśrava Sauti to sages in the Naimiṣa Forest. The text grew over centuries through accretion and redaction. Scholars identify at least three recognized versions: the core *Jaya* (8,800 verses), the *Bhārata* (24,000 verses), and the final *Mahābhārata* (over 100,000 verses). The redaction emphasized the numbers 18 and 12, resulting in the epic's division into 18 *parvas* (books).

### Historical Context and Dating
The historicity of the Kurukshetra War is debated. The setting has a precedent in the Iron Age Kuru kingdom (c. 1200–800 BCE). Various methods for dating the war have been proposed: Puranic genealogies suggest dates around 950 BCE or 1400 BCE; archaeoastronomy yields estimates from the late 4th to mid-2nd millennium BCE; and the traditional date calculated by Aryabhata is 3102 BCE. The epic's core narrative likely relates to events from a period prior to most of the Rig Veda.

### Synopsis and Core Narrative
The story centers on the succession dispute between the hundred Kaurava brothers, led by Duryodhana, and the five Pandava brothers—Yudhishthira, Bhima, Arjuna, Nakula, and Sahadeva. After a series of provocations, including a rigged dice game where the Pandavas lose their kingdom and their wife Draupadi is humiliated, the Pandavas are exiled for thirteen years. Upon their return, negotiations fail, leading to the eighteen-day Kurukshetra War. The Pandavas emerge victorious but at a catastrophic cost. The epic concludes with the Pandavas' renunciation, their journey to heaven, and the beginning of the Kali Yuga, the final age of decline.

### Themes and Philosophical Content
The Mahābhārata explores profound themes of duty (*dharma*), justice, and the human condition. It presents one of the earliest theories of *dharmayuddha* (just war), discussing criteria like proportionality and just cause. The Bhagavad Gita, a philosophical dialogue between Krishna and Arjuna before the battle, elaborates on paths to spiritual liberation and righteous action, becoming a concise guide to Hindu philosophy.

### Translations and Cultural Influence
The epic has been translated into numerous Indian languages for centuries, with early Tamil, Kannada, Telugu, and Bengali versions dating from the 9th to 16th centuries. The first complete English translation was by Kisari Mohan Ganguli (1883–1896). A monumental Critical Edition was produced between 1919 and 1966. The Mahābhārata has inspired countless derivative works, including plays, novels, films, and television series, and remains a vital cultural touchstone in India and beyond.

## Terms
- **Smriti**: "That which is remembered," a category of Hindu scriptures that includes the epics, as opposed to the shruti (revealed) Vedas.
- **Itihasa**: "Thus it was," a genre of literature considered historical narrative, specifically referring to the Mahābhārata and Ramayana.
- **Puruṣārtha**: The four "goals of life" in Hindu philosophy: dharma (duty), artha (prosperity), kāma (pleasure), and moksha (liberation).
- **Shloka**: A Sanskrit verse, typically a couplet, forming the basic metric unit of the epic.
- **Parva**: A "book" or section of the Mahābhārata; the epic is divided into 18 parvas.
- **Swayamvara**: A ceremony where a princess chooses her husband from among assembled suitors.
- **Dharmayuddha**: A "righteous war" or "just war," governed by ethical and moral codes.
- **Kali Yuga**: The fourth and final age of the Hindu cycle of time, characterized by decline and strife.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historicity**: The exact date and historical basis of the Kurukshetra War remain highly debated, with estimates varying by over a millennium.
- **Composition**: Scholars debate the nature of the epic's growth, with theories ranging from a single author to a fluid, orally-transmitted text that accumulated layers over centuries. The existence of a core *Jaya* of 8,800 verses is contested by some scholars.
- **Authorship**: While traditionally ascribed to Vyāsa, the epic is understood to be the product of multiple authors and redactors over a long period.
