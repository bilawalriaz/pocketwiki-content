# Sound

## Overview
Sound is a mechanical wave of pressure or particle displacement that propagates through an elastic medium (solid, liquid, gas, or plasma) but cannot travel through a vacuum. It is defined dually by the ANSI/ASA S1.1‑2013 standard: (a) as a physical oscillation in a medium, and (b) as the auditory sensation evoked by that oscillation. Human hearing typically spans 20 Hz to 20 kHz; frequencies above this range are ultrasound, and those below are infrasound. Acoustics is the interdisciplinary science studying these waves, with applications ranging from medical imaging and architectural design to noise control and psychoacoustics.

## Timeline
- **17th century** — Isaac Newton makes the first significant effort to measure the speed of sound, proposing an isothermal formula ($c = \sqrt{p/\rho}$).
- **Early 19th century** — Pierre‑Simon Laplace corrects Newton’s formula, identifying sound propagation as adiabatic rather than isothermal, introducing the factor $\sqrt{\gamma}$ (Newton–Laplace equation: $c = \sqrt{K/\rho}$).
- **1994** — ANSI S1.1‑1994 standard defines reference sound pressures (20 µPa in air, 1 µPa in water) for decibel calculations.
- **2013** — ANSI/ASA S1.1‑2013 publishes the current American National Standard for Acoustical Terminology, formalizing the two-part (physical/perceptual) definition of sound.

## Body

### Definition and Dual Nature
The ANSI/ASA S1.1‑2013 standard explicitly defines sound in two parts: (a) a propagated oscillation in pressure, stress, or particle displacement within a medium possessing internal forces (elastic or viscous), and (b) the auditory sensation evoked by that oscillation. This distinction separates the physical stimulus (a mechanical wave) from the perceptual response. Physics and engineering texts often favor a purely physical definition—sound as a propagating mechanical disturbance consisting of compressions and rarefactions—omitting the perceptual clause for conceptual precision.

### Acoustics and Subdisciplines
Acoustics is the scientific study of mechanical waves (sound, ultrasound, infrasound, vibration) in gases, liquids, and solids. Practitioners include acousticians (research scientists), acoustical engineers (applied technology), and audio engineers (recording and reproduction). Major subdisciplines cover aeroacoustics, architectural acoustics, bioacoustics, electroacoustics, environmental noise, musical acoustics, noise control, psychoacoustics, speech, ultrasound, underwater acoustics, and vibration.

### Physics of Propagation
Sound waves are generated by a vibrating source (e.g., a loudspeaker diaphragm) and propagate at the local speed of sound. The medium’s particles oscillate but do not travel with the wave; only the disturbance and its mechanical energy propagate. Sound requires a material medium (solid, liquid, gas, or plasma) and cannot travel in a vacuum. Propagation speed depends primarily on the medium’s elastic bulk modulus ($K$) and density ($\rho$) via the Newton–Laplace equation $c = \sqrt{K/\rho}$, modified by temperature. Medium motion (e.g., wind) adds vectorially to wave speed. Viscosity governs attenuation, which is negligible in air and water for many purposes. Theoretical work suggests sound waves carry an extremely small, typically negative effective gravitational mass arising from nonlinear stress–energy corrections.

### Wave Types and Behaviors
In fluids (gases, liquids, plasmas), sound travels as **longitudinal waves** (compression waves) with alternating pressure deviations (compressions and rarefactions). In solids, sound propagates as both longitudinal and **transverse waves** (shear stress perpendicular to propagation); transverse waves exhibit **polarisation**. Wave behaviors include reflection, transmission, refraction (bending in non‑homogeneous media), diffraction, absorption, and attenuation. A received signal at a point is fully described by a time‑varying pressure waveform. Complex waveforms are analyzed as linear combinations of sinusoidal components (Fourier analysis) characterized by frequency, wavelength, amplitude, speed, and direction.

### Speed of Sound
The speed of sound is a fundamental material property. Newton’s initial isothermal model ($c = \sqrt{p/\rho}$) was corrected by Laplace, who recognized adiabatic compression/expansion, yielding $c = \sqrt{\gamma p/\rho} = \sqrt{K/\rho}$. Speed varies with ambient conditions: in air at 20 °C it is ~343 m/s (formula $v = 331 + 0.6T$ m/s); in fresh water ~1,482 m/s; in steel ~5,960 m/s; and highest in solid atomic hydrogen (~36,000 m/s). Speed also shows slight amplitude dependence (non‑linear effects, e.g., harmonic generation) and requires relativistic Euler equations under extreme conditions.

### Sound Pressure Level (SPL)
Sound pressure is the deviation from equilibrium pressure. Its root‑mean‑square (RMS) value is used to define Sound Pressure Level (SPL) on a logarithmic decibel scale: $L_p = 20 \log_{10}(p/p_{\text{ref}})$ dB. Standard references (ANSI S1.1‑1994) are 20 µPa in air and 1 µPa in water. Because human hearing is not spectrally flat, frequency weightings are applied: **A‑weighting** (dBA) approximates the ear’s noise response; **C‑weighting** measures peak levels.

### Perception and Psychoacoustics
Perception distinguishes the physiological/psychological experience from the physical wave. The classic "tree falling in a forest" question highlights this duality. Human pitch sensitivity spans ~20 Hz–20 kHz (upper limit declines with age); below 20 Hz stimuli are felt as discrete pulses. Other species have different ranges (e.g., dogs hear >20 kHz). Sound serves biological functions: danger detection, navigation, predation, communication. Humans have developed cultural technologies (music, telephone, radio) for generation, recording, and broadcast. **Noise** denotes unwanted sound or a signal‑obscuring component, yet contributes to timbre and source identification. **Soundscape** is the human‑perceivable component of the acoustic environment.

#### Perceptual Attributes (Six Classical Dimensions)
1. **Pitch**: Perceived "lowness" or "highness"; for simple tones corresponds to fundamental frequency; for complex sounds involves pre‑conscious harmonic analysis. White noise sounds higher than pink noise due to greater high‑frequency content.
2. **Duration**: Perceived "length"; based on neural onset/offset signals. In noise, gapped sounds may appear continuous if offset cues are masked (beneficial for interrupted speech intelligibility).
3. **Loudness**: Perceived intensity; reflects overall auditory‑nerve activity pattern. Governed by **temporal summation** (~200 ms integration window): brief sounds seem softer than longer ones at equal physical intensity. Spectral complexity (broader fiber activation) increases loudness for a given amplitude.
4. **Timbre**: Sound quality/identity (e.g., oboe vs. piano); derived from frequency transients, noisiness, unsteadiness, pitch, and overtone spread/intensity over time. Temporal evolution provides the primary identification cues.
5. **Sonic Texture**: Cognitive separation of auditory objects; relates to number of sources and their interactions (e.g., unison, polyphony, homophony in music; cacophony in a busy café).
6. **Spatial Location**: Cognitive placement in environment (horizontal/vertical angle, distance, room characteristics). In dense textures, spatial cues combine with timbre to segregate sources.

### Frequency Ranges
- **Ultrasound**: >20 kHz (up to several GHz). Physically identical to audible sound but inaudible to humans. Widely used in medical diagnostics and therapy.
- **Infrasound**: <20 Hz. Inaudible as pitch but perceived as discrete pulses (e.g., idling motorcycle). Detected by whales, elephants; used for volcanic eruption monitoring and in some music.

## Terms
- ****Longitudinal wave**** — A wave in which particle displacement is parallel to the direction of propagation; consists of alternating compressions and rarefactions.
- ****Transverse wave**** — A wave in which particle displacement (shear stress) is perpendicular to the direction of propagation; occurs in solids and exhibits polarisation.
- ****Newton–Laplace equation**** — $c = \sqrt{K/\rho}$; relates speed of sound ($c$) to the medium’s bulk modulus ($K$) and density ($\rho$), correcting Newton’s isothermal assumption with Laplace’s adiabatic factor.
- ****Sound Pressure Level (SPL)**** — Logarithmic measure of sound pressure relative to a reference: $L_p = 20 \log_{10}(p/p_{\text{ref}})$ dB; standard references are 20 µPa (air) and 1 µPa (water).
- ****A‑weighting (dBA)**** — Frequency‑weighting curve approximating the human ear’s sensitivity to noise at moderate levels.
- ****Temporal summation**** — Auditory integration of sound energy over a ~200 ms window; sounds shorter than this are perceived as softer than longer sounds of equal physical intensity.
- ****Timbre**** — Perceptual quality allowing identification of a sound source (e.g., instrument, voice); determined by spectral envelope, temporal evolution, transients, and noisiness.
- ****Soundscape**** — The portion of the acoustic environment that can be perceived by humans, understood in context.
- ****Ultrasound**** — Sound waves with frequencies above 20 kHz, inaudible to humans but used in medical imaging and industrial applications.
- ****Infrasound**** — Sound waves with frequencies below 20 Hz, perceived as discrete pulses; used by some animals for communication and in geophysical monitoring.

## Debates and open questions
- **Definition scope**: Whether "sound" should be defined solely as a physical wave (physics/engineering preference) or must include the perceptual sensation (ANSI standard, psychoacoustics). This affects answers to philosophical questions like the "tree falling in a forest" scenario.
- **Effective gravitational mass of sound**: Theoretical prediction that sound waves carry a tiny, typically negative effective gravitational mass due to nonlinear stress–energy corrections. The effect is extremely small (nonlinear order) and has not been experimentally confirmed.
- **Non‑linear propagation effects**: Amplitude‑dependent speed of sound leads to harmonic generation and mixed tones (parametric arrays); the full implications for high‑amplitude wave propagation in various media remain an area of active research.
- **Perceptual attribute taxonomy**: The classical six attributes (pitch, duration, loudness, timbre, texture, spatial location) are historically established, but newer research proposes **temporal envelope** and **temporal fine structure** as additional or alternative perceptually relevant dimensions.
