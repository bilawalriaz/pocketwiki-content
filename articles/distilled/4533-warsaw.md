# Warsaw

## Overview
Warsaw is the capital and largest city of Poland, situated on the Vistula River. It is a major political, economic, and cultural hub for Central Europe, classified as an alpha global city. Its modern identity is profoundly shaped by near-total destruction during World War II and a subsequent, largely successful, reconstruction effort.

- **c. 1300** — Prince Bolesław II of Masovia establishes the modern city.
- **1413** — Warsaw becomes the capital of the Duchy of Masovia.
- **1596** — King Sigismund III moves the Polish capital from Kraków to Warsaw.
- **1795** — Warsaw is annexed by Prussia in the Third Partition of Poland.
- **1806** — Napoleon makes Warsaw the capital of the Duchy of Warsaw.
- **1939** — Warsaw is bombed and besieged at the start of World War II.
- **1943** — The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising is crushed by Nazi forces.
- **1944** — The Warsaw Uprising fails; the city is systematically razed.
- **1945** — Soviet and Polish troops liberate the ruins of Warsaw.
- **1980** — Warsaw's reconstructed Old Town is listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **1995** — The first line of the Warsaw Metro opens.
- **2004** — Poland joins the European Union, spurring Warsaw's economic boom.

## Body

### History and Destruction
Warsaw rose to prominence after 1596 when it became the capital of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. It flourished in the 18th century, earning the nickname "Paris of the North." After the partitions of Poland, it was part of Prussia and then the Russian Empire, experiencing rapid industrialization and population growth in the 19th century. Following World War I, it became the capital of the reborn Polish state. The city's history was catastrophically interrupted by World War II. After the 1939 invasion, its large Jewish population was confined to the Warsaw Ghetto. The 1943 Ghetto Uprising was followed by the 1944 Warsaw Uprising, a 63-day struggle by the Polish Home Army against the German occupiers. In retaliation, Hitler ordered the city razed, destroying approximately 85% of its buildings, including the historic Old Town.

### Reconstruction and Modern Development
After liberation in 1945, Warsaw was rebuilt from rubble. The "Bricks for Warsaw" campaign and large-scale prefabricated housing projects addressed the shortage. While some historic buildings were restored, others were demolished for modernist urban planning, including the construction of the Palace of Culture and Science, a Soviet-era skyscraper. The fall of communism in 1989 led to a major economic boom, with Warsaw becoming a leading business center in Central Europe. The city's infrastructure has expanded significantly, including the Warsaw Metro system and modern road networks.

### Geography and Climate
Warsaw lies in east-central Poland on the Vistula River, which divides the city. The terrain is part of the Masovian Plain, with an average elevation of 100 meters. The climate is classified as either oceanic or humid continental, featuring cold, cloudy winters and warm, stormy summers. It is one of Europe's driest major cities, with average annual rainfall of 550 mm.

### Urban Landscape and Architecture
Warsaw's cityscape is a mix of contemporary and reconstructed historic architecture. The Old Town, meticulously rebuilt after the war, is a UNESCO site showcasing Gothic, Renaissance, and Baroque styles. The city has a high concentration of skyscrapers, primarily in the Śródmieście and Wola districts, including the Varso Tower, the tallest building in the EU. Green spaces cover about a quarter of the city, including historic parks like Łazienki and the Saxon Garden, one of the world's first public parks (1727).

### Demographics and Society
Warsaw was historically one of Europe's most diverse cities, with a Jewish population of about 30% before WWII. The war decimated this community. Today, the population is predominantly Polish, but has grown due to internal migration and, more recently, a large influx of Ukrainian refugees following the 2022 invasion. The city is the political center of Poland, hosting all major government institutions and international organizations like Frontex.

### Economy and Transport
Warsaw is Poland's economic engine, generating nearly a fifth of the country's GDP. It is a major hub for finance, services, and technology, with the Warsaw Stock Exchange being the largest in Central and Eastern Europe. The city is a key transport node, served by three international airports, with Warsaw Chopin Airport being the busiest. Public transport includes an expanding metro, buses, trams, and commuter rail.

### Culture and Education
As the national cultural center, Warsaw hosts major institutions like the National Museum, the Royal Castle, and the Grand Theatre. It is home to the University of Warsaw, founded in 1816, and numerous other universities. The city's culinary tradition reflects its multicultural past, and it maintains a vibrant café culture. Annual events include the Warsaw Film Festival and the Wianki (Wreaths) festival on Midsummer's Night.

## Terms
- **Duchy of Warsaw:** A French client state established by Napoleon in 1806 from territories of the former Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth.
- **Masovian Voivodeship:** The administrative province (voivodeship) in which Warsaw is located.
- **Plattenbau:** A style of prefabricated concrete apartment building, widely used in post-war Warsaw to address housing shortages.
- **Gmina:** The basic unit of territorial division and local government in Poland. Warsaw is an urban gmina with the status of a city with powiat rights.
- **Dzielnica:** An auxiliary district within a Polish city, of which Warsaw has 18.
- **Varsovian:** A native or resident of Warsaw.
- **Syrenka:** The mermaid, the official symbol of Warsaw, depicted on its coat of arms.
- **Frontex:** The European Union agency for border and coast guard, headquartered in Warsaw.
- **ODIHR:** The Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights, a principal institution of the OSCE, based in Warsaw.
