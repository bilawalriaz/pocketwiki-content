# Kazimir Malevich

## Overview
Kazimir Malevich (1879–1935) was a Russian avant-garde artist and theorist of Polish ethnicity, born near Kiev in the Russian Empire, who founded Suprematism—a system of pure geometric abstraction on monochromatic grounds. His 1915 *Black Square*, exhibited at the Last Futurist Exhibition 0,10 in Petrograd, marked a decisive break with representational painting by rejecting the "circle of objects" for the "circle of spirit." Malevich’s trajectory mirrored the Russian Revolution: he held prominent teaching and administrative posts in Vitebsk, Leningrad, and Kiev during the early Soviet period, but Stalin’s imposition of Socialist Realism in the 1930s forced a return to figuration, led to his 1930 arrest by the OGPU, and suppressed his work until his death from cancer in 1935. His 1927 exhibitions in Warsaw and Berlin—the only time he left Russia—established his Western reputation, influencing contemporaries like El Lissitzky and later Minimalists. His nationality remains debated; scholars and museums variously identify him as Russian, Polish, or Ukrainian, reflecting his shifting self-identification and the imperial/Soviet contexts of his life.

- **19 Dec 1915** — Opens Last Futurist Exhibition 0,10 in Petrograd, showing 39 Suprematist works including *Black Square*; publishes *From Cubism and Futurism to Suprematism*.
- **1918–1922** — Teaches at Vitebsk Practical Art School with Marc Chagall; founds UNOVIS collective; serves on Narkompros arts collegium.
- **1923–1926** — Directs Petrograd State Institute of Artistic Culture (GINKhUK); forced to close after Communist Party condemnation.
- **Mar–May 1927** — Sole trip abroad: exhibitions in Warsaw and Berlin (Great Berlin Art Exhibition); meets Bauhaus figures; leaves most works in Germany.
- **1928–1930** — Teaches at Kiev Art Institute; publishes in *Nova Generatsiia*.

- **15 May 1935** — Dies of cancer in Leningrad, age 56; buried near Nemchinovka under a white cube with black square (destroyed WWII).

## Body

### Early Life and Artistic Formation (1879–1905)
Malevich was born into a Polish Catholic family displaced by the failed 1863 January Uprising against Russian rule. The first of fourteen children (nine surviving), he grew up speaking Polish, Russian, and Ukrainian while his father managed sugar refineries, prompting frequent moves across Ukraine and Russia. In Parkhomovka (1889–1893), he attended agricultural school and painted in a peasant style. In Kursk (1896–1904), he worked as a technical draughtsman, encountered Russian Realist painters (the Peredvizhniki), and began exhibiting. A 1904 move to Moscow proved decisive: through the private collections of Sergei Shchukin and Ivan Morozov, he accessed French Impressionism, Cézanne, Gauguin, Picasso, and Matisse. His *Apple Tree in Blossom* (1904) shows Sisley’s influence. Politically, he later claimed involvement in the December 1905 Moscow barricades during the failed revolution.

### Moscow Avant-Garde: Symbolism to Cubo-Futurism (1906–1915)
Settling in Moscow (1906), Malevich studied under Fedor Rerberg but failed admission to the Moscow art school. The 1907 Blue Rose Symbolist exhibition left a deep impression, visible in mystical works like *The Triumph of Heaven* (1907). By 1908, he engaged deeply with Russian icons and folk art (lubok), while the Golden Fleece exhibitions brought Van Gogh, Matisse, Braque, Gauguin, and Cézanne to Moscow. He joined the Knave of Diamonds group (founded by Goncharova and Larionov) in 1910, which sought to fuse Cézanne’s modernism with Russian folk traditions. Commercial work included the 1911 *Severny* perfume bottle (iceberg base, polar bear stopper). When Goncharova and Larionov split to form the Donkey’s Tail (1912), emphasizing a "primitive" peasant aesthetic, Malevich exhibited *Floor Polishers* and *Washerwoman* (1911–12). By 1913, Italian Futurism’s influence peaked: he adopted *zaum* (transrational language), designed the *Victory Over the Sun* opera (libretto by Kruchenykh, music by Matyushin), and worked in a self-described "Cubo-Futurist" style, fragmenting forms to suggest movement (*Knifegrinder*, 1912). The opera’s square curtain marked the *Black Square*’s conceptual debut. In 1914, he showed at the Paris Salon des Indépendants and produced the collage *Reservist of the First Division* and propagandist lubok prints for the war effort—both using flat geometric planes that anticipated Suprematism.

### Suprematism: Theory and Practice (1915–1918)
In March 1915, Malevich exhibited Cubo-Futurist collages at Tramway V in Petrograd but privately rejected the style’s reliance on the object. Over summer 1915, he produced the first abstract composition: a black quadrilateral on white. At the Last Futurist Exhibition 0,10 (19 Dec 1915), he hung 39 Suprematist oils, placing *Black Square* in the icon corner—a deliberate displacement of religious tradition. The brochure *From Cubism and Futurism to Suprematism* declared he had "destroyed the ring of the horizon and escaped from the circle of objects, moving from the horizon-ring to the circle of spirit." Student Anna Leporskaya recalled he could not eat, drink, or sleep for a week after creating it. Suprematism claimed spiritual supremacy of pure feeling over representation. Malevich painted multiple *Black Square* versions (1915, c.1923, 1929, late 1920s/early 1930s) and *White on White* (1918). Radiologist Milda Victurina identified a signature technique: layering paints (e.g., black under red) to create color spots with "a touch of darkness," a forensic marker for authentication. In 1918, he decorated Mayakovsky’s *Mystery-Bouffe* (dir. Meyerhold) and explored aerial photography as a source for abstraction.

### Post-Revolutionary Career and Stalinist Suppression (1918–1935)
After the October Revolution, Malevich served on Narkompros commissions (1918–19) and taught at Vitebsk (1919–22) with Chagall, founding UNOVIS. He directed the Petrograd State Institute of Artistic Culture (GINKhUK, 1923–26) until a Party newspaper condemned it as a "counterrevolutionary monastery." His book *The Non-Objective World* (Munich, 1926) systematized Suprematist theory. The 1927 Warsaw/Berlin trip was pivotal: the Berlin show (70+ works) became the primary Western source on his oeuvre for 50 years; manuscripts left there, rediscovered in 1954, formed a second pillar. In Warsaw, he influenced Polish Constructivists (Strzemiński, Kobro, Stażewski) but faced criticism from utilitarian avant-gardists. Back in the USSR, he taught at the Kiev Art Institute (1928–30). Stalin’s turn against "bourgeois" abstraction led to confiscations, dismissal, and his 1930 OGPU arrest on espionage charges. The 1934 decree imposing Socialist Realism banned avant-garde art. Forced into figuration, Malevich painted representational works until cancer killed him in 1935. His funeral featured a *Black Square* banner; a memorial cube at his burial site was destroyed in WWII.

### Nationality and Identity
Malevich’s nationality is contested. Western scholarship historically labeled him "Russian/Soviet" due to his career in Moscow/Petrograd. Since the 1990s, and especially post-2022, scholars and museums (Metropolitan, Stedelijk) have reattributed him as "Ukrainian" or "Ukrainian of Polish origin," emphasizing his birth near Kiev, formative Ukrainian years, and a 1930 NKVD file where he declared Ukrainian nationality (possibly to avoid anti-Polish discrimination). He signed works *Kazimierz Malewicz* (Polish), claimed Polish nationality on a 1926 French visa, and his mother wrote Polish poetry. Polish artists honored him (Warpechowski, 1985; *Rectangular Circle of Friends*, 2013). Russian authorities reject reattribution. As of 2023, art historians agree the discussion—rooted in Russian imperial/colonial history—remains unresolved.

### Legacy, Market, and Collections
Alfred Barr included Malevich in MoMA’s *Cubism and Abstract Art* (1936). Guggenheim’s Museum of Non-Objective Painting (1939) drew on his spiritual abstraction. Major retrospectives: Guggenheim (1973), Stedelijk (1989, with Khardzhiev collection), Fundación Juan March (1993). Key holdings: Tretyakov Gallery (Moscow), MoMA, Guggenheim, Stedelijk (24 paintings, largest outside Russia), Thessaloniki State Museum of Contemporary Art. Market milestones: a 1920s *Black Square* sold for $250k (1993), $1M (2002, to Hermitage via Potanin); *Suprematist Composition* (1916) set Russian records at $60M (Sotheby’s, 2008) and $85M (Christie’s, 2018). The Stedelijk restituted five Berlin-left works to heirs in 2008. Cultural footprint: novels (*Red Square*, *The Art Thief*), film (*Melancholia*), 2014 Sochi Olympics projections, monuments in Konotop (2015) and Nemchinovka (destroyed).

## Terms
- ****Suprematism**** — Malevich’s system (1915) of pure geometric abstraction—basic forms (square, circle, cross) on monochromatic grounds—asserting the "supremacy of pure feeling" over representation.
- ****Cubo-Futurism**** — A hybrid Russian style (c.1912–15) merging Cubist fragmentation with Futurist dynamism; Malevich’s immediate precursor to Suprematism.
- ****Zaum**** — "Transrational" language (invented sounds/words bypassing reason) used by Russian Futurists; Malevich applied its logic to visual form.
- ****Knave of Diamonds**** — Moscow avant-garde group (1910) founded by Goncharova/Larionov, fusing Cézanne-inflected modernism with Russian folk art; Malevich exhibited in its first show.
- ****Donkey’s Tail**** — Splinter group (1912) by Goncharova/Larionov emphasizing "primitive" peasant imagery (lubok) over Western models; Malevich participated in its 1912 exhibition.
- ****UNOVIS**** — *Affirmers of the New Art*; collective founded by Malevich in Vitebsk (1919) to implement Suprematism in design, architecture, and pedagogy.
- ****GINKhUK**** — Petrograd State Institute of Artistic Culture (1923–26), directed by Malevich; closed after Party condemnation as "counterrevolutionary."
- ****Socialist Realism**** — State-mandated Soviet style (officially 1934) depicting idealized proletarian life; banned abstraction, forcing Malevich’s late figuration.
- ****Lubok**** — Russian popular folk print (broadside); source for Neo-Primitivist flattening and bold color; Malevich used its format for 1914 war propaganda.
- ****The Non-Objective World**** — Malevich’s theoretical book (Munich, 1926; English 1959) outlining Suprematism’s progression from planar to linear to "economical" (white-on-white) phases.

## Debates and open questions
- **Nationality attribution**: Whether Malevich should be classified as Russian, Polish, Ukrainian, or a hybrid reflects unresolved tensions between imperial/Soviet art-historical categories and postcolonial reassessment; his own shifting self-identifications (Polish on 1926 visa, Ukrainian to NKVD in 1930) complicate a definitive label.
- **Black Square dating and authorship**: Four versions exist (1915, c.1923, 1929, late 1920s/early 1930s); the 1915 date on the last version’s reverse is widely considered apocryphal, raising questions about Malevich’s retrospective mythmaking.
- **Spiritual vs. materialist readings**: Scholars debate whether Suprematism’s "zero of form" expresses mystical transcendence (Malevich’s rhetoric) or a materialist break with bourgeois illusionism aligned with early Soviet utopianism.
- **Late figuration: coercion or evolution?**: The 1930s representational works are contested as either capitulation to Stalinist terror or a dialectical continuation of Suprematism’s "new realism."
- **Provenance and restitution**: Heirs’ claims (successful at Stedelijk, 2008) and disputed museum titles reflect the chaotic dispersal of works left in Berlin (1927), seized by Nazis as "Degenerate Art," and later acquired by Western and Russian institutions.
