# Hamlet

## Overview
*The Tragedy of Hamlet, Prince of Denmark* is a tragedy by William Shakespeare, written between 1599 and 1601. It is Shakespeare's longest play and is widely considered one of the greatest works in the English language. The play depicts Prince Hamlet's quest to avenge his father's murder by his uncle, Claudius, who has seized the throne and married Hamlet's mother, Gertrude. Its exploration of revenge, madness, and mortality has made it endlessly adaptable and a cornerstone of world literature.

## Timeline
- **c. 13th century** — Saxo Grammaticus writes the Latin "Life of Amleth," a key source for the Hamlet legend.
- **1570** — François de Belleforest publishes a French translation and embellishment of Saxo's story.
- **c. 1589** — The hypothetical "Ur-Hamlet," possibly by Thomas Kyd or Shakespeare, may have been written.
- **1598** — Francis Meres's *Palladis Tamia* lists twelve Shakespeare plays but not *Hamlet*, suggesting it was not yet written.
- **1599-1601** — Shakespeare writes *Hamlet*.
- **1603** — The First Quarto (Q1), a shorter "bad" text, is published.
- **1604** — The Second Quarto (Q2), a longer and more complete text, is published.
- **1623** — The First Folio (F1) is published, containing a version of the play with unique passages.
- **1676** — The first known division of the play into five acts appears in a quarto edition.
- **1948** — Laurence Olivier's film adaptation wins Academy Awards for Best Picture and Best Actor.

## Body

### Sources and Composition
The core "hero-as-fool" theme of *Hamlet* is possibly Indo-European in origin, with precursors in Scandinavian sagas and Roman legends. Shakespeare's most direct sources were likely the 13th-century *Life of Amleth* by Saxo Grammaticus and its 1570 French adaptation by François de Belleforest. A lost earlier play, the "Ur-Hamlet," may also have been a source; scholars debate whether it was written by Thomas Kyd or an early work by Shakespeare himself. The play was written between 1599 and 1601, with evidence including allusions to Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar* (1599) and a 1602 Stationers' Register entry.

### Textual History
Three early, differing versions of the play survive: the First Quarto (Q1, 1603), the Second Quarto (Q2, 1604), and the First Folio (F1, 1623). Q1 is about half the length of the later texts and may be a memorial reconstruction or an abridged version for touring. Q2 is the longest edition, while F1 contains unique passages, likely omitted from Q2 to avoid offending Queen Anne of Denmark. Modern editors often conflate Q2 and F1 to create a composite text, though some scholars argue for presenting the versions separately, as no single "authentic" text exists.

### Plot and Structure
The play follows Prince Hamlet, who is visited by the ghost of his father. The ghost reveals he was murdered by Claudius, Hamlet's uncle, and demands revenge. Hamlet feigns madness to investigate, staging a play to "catch the conscience of the King." His actions lead to the accidental killing of Polonius, the madness and death of Ophelia, and a final duel where most main characters die. The structure is driven by Hamlet's character development, with soliloquies revealing his internal conflict. A major theme is the contrast between appearance and reality, explored through spying and the play-within-a-play.

### Language and Length
*Hamlet* is Shakespeare's longest play, with the Q2 text containing 28,628 words. Its language ranges from the courtly, elaborate discourse of characters like Polonius to the simpler speech of gravediggers. Hamlet's soliloquies are famous for their rhetorical complexity, using puns, metaphors, and devices like hendiadys (e.g., "the expectancy and rose of the fair state"). The phrase "To be, or not to be" is a defining example of the play's philosophical depth.

### Critical and Interpretive History
Critical views have shifted dramatically. Restoration critics saw the play as primitive, while 18th-century critics viewed Hamlet as a heroic figure. Romantic critics focused on his internal conflict, and 20th-century psychoanalytic readings, influenced by Freud, explored the Oedipus complex. Feminist critics have re-examined Gertrude and Ophelia, arguing against traditional interpretations of their madness and sexuality. The play is also noted for its religious ambiguity, blending Catholic and Protestant elements, and its philosophical themes of relativism and existentialism.

### Performance History
The role was likely created for Richard Burbage. The play has been continuously performed and adapted, with notable actors including David Garrick, Sarah Siddons (the first known actress to play Hamlet), Edwin Booth, and Laurence Olivier. 20th-century productions often used modern dress and political overtones, such as Leopold Jessner's 1926 Berlin production parodying Kaiser Wilhelm's court. The play has been adapted globally, including into Japanese Kabuki and Nō styles, and remains one of the most frequently staged plays worldwide.

## Terms
- **First Quarto (Q1):** The 1603 published text of *Hamlet*, significantly shorter and of disputed quality, possibly a memorial reconstruction.
- **Second Quarto (Q2):** The 1604 published text, the longest early version and the basis for most modern editions.
- **First Folio (F1):** The 1623 collected edition of Shakespeare's plays, containing a version of *Hamlet* with unique passages not in Q2.
- **Ur-Hamlet:** A hypothetical lost earlier play, possibly by Thomas Kyd or Shakespeare, that may have been a source for the tragedy.
- **Soliloquy:** A dramatic device where a character speaks their thoughts aloud, alone on stage, revealing inner conflict.
- **Hendiadys:** A rhetorical device using two words joined by "and" to express a single idea (e.g., "the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune").
- **Oedipus complex:** A psychoanalytic theory, developed by Freud from analyzing *Hamlet*, describing a child's unconscious sexual desire for the opposite-sex parent and rivalry with the same-sex parent.
- **Breeches role:** A theatrical part in which a female actor plays a male character, as Sarah Siddons did with Hamlet.

## Debates and open questions
- **The Ur-Hamlet:** Did an earlier play exist, and if so, was it by Thomas Kyd or an early work by Shakespeare? No copy survives.
- **The "Nunnery" Line:** Does Hamlet's phrase "get thee to a nunnery" contain a double meaning (a place of chastity vs. a slang term for brothel), or is it a straightforward command?
- **Polonius's Inspiration:** Was the character Polonius modeled on William Cecil (Lord Burghley), Queen Elizabeth I's chief advisor?
- **Connection to Hamnet Shakespeare:** Is the play's hero linked to Shakespeare's son, Hamnet, who died in 1596? Most scholars reject this, noting the name's popularity, though Stephen Greenblatt has argued for a connection.
- **Textual Authority:** Should *Hamlet* be presented as a single conflated text or as separate Q2 and F1 versions, given their significant differences?
