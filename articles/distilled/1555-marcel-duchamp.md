# Marcel Duchamp

## Overview
Marcel Duchamp (1887–1968) was a French-American artist whose radical redefinition of the art object shifted the focus of modern art from visual aesthetics ("retinal art") to intellectual conception, laying the groundwork for conceptual art. His most notorious gesture was the *readymade*—ordinary manufactured objects (a bottle rack, a urinal, a snow shovel) selected, signed, and presented as art—challenging the necessity of craftsmanship and the authority of taste. Duchamp’s major works, *Nude Descending a Staircase, No. 2* (1912), *The Large Glass* (1915–23), and the secretive tableau *Étant donnés* (1946–66), fuse scientific analogy, chance operations, erotic allegory, and esoteric symbolism. After 1923 he largely withdrew from art production to pursue competitive chess, yet continued to influence the avant-garde through exhibition design, pseudonyms (Rrose Sélavy), and strategic silence, becoming a cult figure for postwar artists like Robert Rauschenberg and Jasper Johns.

## Timeline
- **28 July 1887** — Born in Blainville-Crevon, France, to a family of artists (brothers Jacques Villon, Raymond Duchamp-Villon; sister Suzanne Duchamp-Crotti).
- **1904–05** — Studies at Académie Julian, Paris; prefers billiards to classes.
- **1912** — Paints *Nude Descending a Staircase, No. 2*; controversy at Salon des Indépendants; visits Munich, sees Lucas Cranach; attends *Impressions d’Afrique* adaptation.
- **1913** — Exhibits *Nude* at Armory Show, New York; creates *3 Standard Stoppages* (chance experiment); installs *Bicycle Wheel* in studio.
- **1914** — Produces first "pure" readymade, *Bottle Rack*; WWI begins; exempted for heart murmur.
- **1915** — Emigrates to New York; befriends Arensbergs, Man Ray, Beatrice Wood; begins *The Large Glass*; creates *In Advance of the Broken Arm* (snow shovel).
- **1917** — Submits *Fountain* (urinal, signed "R. Mutt 1917") to Society of Independent Artists; work rejected; resigns from board.
- **1918–20** — Lives in Buenos Aires (plays chess intensely); returns to Paris, then New York.
- **1920** — Co-founds Société Anonyme with Katherine Dreier and Man Ray; builds *Rotative plaques verre* (kinetic).
- **1921** — Creates female alter ego Rrose Sélavy (Man Ray photos); produces *Why Not Sneeze Rose Sélavy?* and *Belle Haleine*.
- **1923** — Declares *The Large Glass* "Unfinished"; glass cracks in transit; effectively stops making art, dedicates life to chess.
- **1925** — Designs poster for Third French Chess Championship; earns master title (3–3, two draws).
- **1926** — Films *Anémic Cinéma* (Rotoreliefs); suggests term "mobiles" for Alexander Calder’s kinetic sculptures.
- **1932** — Publishes chess treatise *L’opposition et cases conjuguées sont réconciliées* with Vitaly Halberstadt.
- **1938** — Designs installation *Twelve Hundred Coal Bags* for Exposition Internationale du Surréalisme, Paris.
- **1942** — Designs "mile of string" installation for First Papers of Surrealism, New York.
- **1946–66** — Secretly constructs *Étant donnés* in Greenwich Village studio.
- **1954** — Marries Alexina "Teeny" Sattler.
- **1955** — Becomes U.S. citizen.
- **1963** — First retrospective, Pasadena Art Museum; iconic photo playing chess with nude Eve Babitz.
- **2 Oct 1968** — Dies of heart failure in Neuilly-sur-Seine, France; buried in Rouen with epitaph: "Besides, it's always the others who die."

## Body

### Early Formation and the Break with Cubism
Duchamp grew up in a cultured Norman family; four of six surviving siblings became artists. After desultory study at the Académie Julian (1904–05) and military service learning typography, he exhibited Fauve- and Cézanne-influenced paintings at the 1908 Salon d’Automne and 1909 Salon des Indépendants. By 1911–12 he was exploring "elementary parallelism"—linear decomposition suggesting fourth-dimensional movement—in works like *Sad Young Man on a Train* and the machine paintings *Coffee Mill* (1911) and *Chocolate Grinder* (1914). *Nude Descending a Staircase, No. 2* (1912) synthesized Cubist fragmentation with Futurist dynamism. When the Cubist organizers of the Salon des Indépendants asked him to withdraw or retitle it, Duchamp removed the painting himself, a decisive break from group affiliation. The work became the *succès de scandale* of the 1913 Armory Show in New York, introducing American audiences to European modernism.

### Munich, Chance, and the Turn from "Retinal Art"
A two-month stay in Munich (1912) proved pivotal. Duchamp studied Lucas Cranach’s muted palette at the Alte Pinakothek daily, began notes for *The Large Glass*, and attended a stage adaptation of Raymond Roussel’s *Impressions d’Afrique*, whose self-referential plots and humanoid machines inspired his shift toward a mental, non-retinal art. Reading Max Stirner’s *The Ego and Its Own* reinforced radical individualism. Back in Paris, he painted few canvases, adopting technical drawing to eliminate "painterly" effects. At the Bibliothèque Sainte-Geneviève (1913) he studied Henri Poincaré’s conventionalist philosophy of science—laws as mental constructs, not absolute truths—which validated his use of chance and his tolerance for any viewer’s interpretation. *3 Standard Stoppages* (1913) materialized this: three threads dropped from one meter onto canvas, fixed in their random curves, then replicated as wooden templates, parodying the metric standard. *Unhappy Readymade* (1919) exposed a geometry textbook to weather, warping Euclidean certainty.

### Readymades and the Strategy of Indifference
The readymade emerged from Duchamp’s desire to "discourage aesthetics" by choosing objects at a "point of indifference"—neither beautiful nor ugly. *Bottle Rack* (1914) is considered the first "pure" readymade; *In Advance of the Broken Arm* (snow shovel, 1915) followed. *Fountain* (1917), a porcelain urinal signed "R. Mutt," was rejected by the supposedly jury-free Society of Independent Artists, exposing institutional hypocrisy and making the work an icon of anti-art. In 1919 he defaced a *Mona Lisa* reproduction with a mustache and the inscription *L.H.O.O.Q.* (phonetically "Elle a chaud au cul"—"She has a hot ass"), a Freudian and linguistic joke. Research later suggested some "found" objects may have been fabricated or altered by Duchamp, complicating the readymade’s premise.

### The Large Glass: Erotic Mechanics and Esoteric Systems
*The Bride Stripped Bare by Her Bachelors, Even* (1915–23), executed on two glass panes with lead foil, fuse wire, and dust, depicts a mechanistic erotic encounter between a "Bride" (upper panel) and nine "Bachelors" (lower). Chance operations (the *3 Standard Stoppages* curves), plotted perspective, and laborious craftsmanship coexist. *The Green Box* notes invent a pseudo-physics and mythology. The title’s French *même* puns on "even," "same," and "loves me" (*m’aime*), suggesting the Bride and Bachelors are aspects of a single system. Scholars read the work through alchemy (Moffitt: *coniunctio oppositorum*), nondual Śākta-Śaiva tantra (Baas: cyclical manifestation/refinement/dissolution), and autoerotic techno-sexuality (Nechvatal). Its transparent support makes it both window and mirror, implicating the viewer. Cracked in transit in 1923, Duchamp repaired the large fracture but kept smaller cracks as accepted chance.

### Kinetic, Optical, and Musical Experiments
Duchamp’s interest in visual phenomena persisted. *Rotative plaques verre* (1920, with Man Ray) spun painted glass segments to create illusory concentric circles; a belt break shattered the apparatus. *Rotative Demisphère* (1923) used a spinning half-globe with circles appearing to move in depth. *Rotoreliefs* (1935)—cardboard disks spun on a turntable to produce 3D illusions—were marketed at a Paris inventors’ fair (commercial failure) but noted by optical scientists for potential vision therapy. The film *Anémic Cinéma* (1926) recorded them. In 1931 Duchamp coined "mobiles" for Alexander Calder’s kinetic sculptures. Musically, *Erratum Musical* (1913, pub. 1934) and an unfinished mechanical piece used chance operations, predating John Cage; in 1968 Duchamp and Cage played chess on a photoelectric board generating aleatoric music ("Reunion").

### Rrose Sélavy and the Play of Identity
The pseudonym Rrose Sélavy (phonetic: *Éros, c’est la vie* / *arroser la vie*) debuted in 1921 Man Ray photographs of Duchamp in drag. The persona signed readymades (*Why Not Sneeze Rose Sélavy?*, *Belle Haleine*), the film *Anémic Cinéma*, and appeared in Robert Desnos’s aphorisms (1939), where "mar-chand-du-sel" (salt merchant) phonetically anagrams "Marcel Duchamp." The name may reference Belle da Costa Greene, J.P. Morgan’s librarian. Rrose embodies Duchamp’s lifelong destabilization of authorship, gender, and the romantic artist myth.

### Chess: The Parallel Career
From 1918 (Buenos Aires) onward, chess absorbed Duchamp. He carved his own set, earned the French master title (1925), competed in Olympiads (1928–33), published an endgame treatise with Vitaly Halberstadt (1932), and turned to correspondence chess and journalism. He declared chess "purer than art—it cannot be commercialized" and "all chess players are artists." An enigmatic 1943 endgame problem ("White to play and win") printed on translucent paper remains unsolved by grandmasters. Samuel Beckett’s *Endgame* (1957) and the 1968 "Reunion" concert with Cage reflect chess’s structural influence on his thinking.

### Surrealism, Exhibition Design, and the Secret Masterpiece
Though never a Surrealist member, Duchamp collaborated from the mid-1930s: editing *VVV* with Breton and Ernst, designing the 1938 *Exposition Internationale du Surréalisme* (coal bags, flashlights, sensory overload) and the 1942 "First Papers of Surrealism" (mile of string, children playing). He advised collectors (Peggy Guggenheim) and curated retrospectives. From 1946–66, in total secrecy, he built *Étant donnés*: a peep-show tableau visible through two holes in a wooden door, revealing a nude female torso (modeled on lover Maria Martins) holding a gas lamp against a landscape with a waterfall. Its 1969 unveiling at the Philadelphia Museum of Art shocked a world that believed he had quit art in 1923.

### Reception and Legacy
Duchamp’s influence lay dormant until the late 1950s, when Rauschenberg, Johns, and the Neo-Dada/Pop generation "rediscovered" him. Robert Lebel’s 1959 catalogue raisonné and the 1963 Pasadena retrospective cemented his status. He accepted honors reluctantly, comparing art to religion as a social construct to be transcended. The 2004 poll naming *Fountain* the most influential 20th-century artwork and the 2000 establishment of the Prix Marcel Duchamp confirm his centrality. His insistence that "the spectator completes the work" remains a foundational tenet of contemporary practice.

## Terms
- ****Readymade**** — A manufactured object selected, sometimes altered or signed, and designated as art by the artist, challenging the necessity of manual craft and aesthetic judgment.
- ****Retinal art**** — Duchamp’s dismissive term for art appealing primarily to the eye and visual pleasure, which he sought to replace with intellectual/conceptual engagement.
- ****The Large Glass**** — Short title for *The Bride Stripped Bare by Her Bachelors, Even* (1915–23), a large work on two glass panes combining chance, perspective, and a pseudo-mechanical erotic mythology.
- ****Rrose Sélavy**** — Duchamp’s female alter ego (pun on *Éros, c’est la vie*), used from 1921 for photographs, readymade signatures, and writings, destabilizing authorship and gender.
- ****3 Standard Stoppages**** — 1913 chance experiment: three 1-meter threads dropped from 1 meter height, fixed in random curves, then replicated as wood slats—parodying the metric standard via Poincaré’s conventionalism.
- ****Étant donnés**** — Duchamp’s final major work (1946–66), a peep-show tableau of a nude female figure with gas lamp and waterfall, viewed through holes in a door, secretly built while he claimed to have abandoned art.
- ****Société Anonyme**** — 1920 organization co-founded with Katherine Dreier and Man Ray to promote modern art in the U.S., predating MoMA as the first American modern art collection.
- ****Rotoreliefs**** — Painted cardboard disks spun on a turntable to create 3D optical illusions (1935); marketed as "play toys," studied for vision therapy; filmed in *Anémic Cinéma*.
- ****Corresponding squares**** — Chess endgame theory (Lasker–Reichhelm position) treated in Duchamp and Halberstadt’s 1932 treatise *L’opposition et cases conjuguées sont réconciliées*, using enneagram-like folding charts.
- ****Art coefficient**** — Duchamp’s definition: "an arithmetical relationship between the unexpressed but intended and the unintentionally expressed," framing art as a gap between intention and realization completed by the spectator.

## Debates and open questions
- **Alchemy and esotericism**: Did Duchamp consciously employ alchemical/Hermetic structures (Moffitt), or did they emerge "without knowing it" (*sans le savoir*) as he claimed? Baas’s tantric/Śākta-Śaiva reading adds Asian esotericism, but documentary evidence of initiation is absent.
- **Authenticity of readymades**: Research by Rhonda Roland Shearer suggests some "found" objects (e.g., *Bottle Rack*, *Mona Lisa* reproduction) may have been fabricated or modeled on Duchamp’s face, challenging the readymade’s premise of pure selection.
- **Chess vs. art**: Was chess a genuine abandonment of art or a strategic continuation? The secret 20-year construction of *Étant donnés* suggests the latter, but Duchamp’s own statements emphasize chess’s purity and non-commerciality.
- **Fountain’s authorship**: Some scholars (e.g., Irene Gammel) argue Baroness Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven conceived or contributed to *Fountain*; the source text notes the *Bicycle Wheel* idea came from her, but attributes *Fountain* to Duchamp.
- **Rrose Sélavy’s inspiration**: The pseudonym may reference Belle da Costa Greene, but this remains speculative; Desnos’s aphorisms independently developed the persona.
- **The Large Glass’s "Unfinished" status**: Declared unfinished in 1923, yet the crack repair and *Green Box* notes imply a deliberate open-endedness; scholars debate whether it is a ruin, a completed system, or a perpetual generator of interpretations.
