# Spanish American wars of independence

## Overview
The Spanish American wars of independence (c. 1808–1833) were a series of conflicts that dissolved the Spanish Empire in the Americas, creating new republics. Triggered by Napoleon's invasion of Spain, which created a power vacuum, the wars pitted Royalists, who favored a unified Spanish monarchy, against Patriots, who sought autonomous or independent states. The conflicts reshaped the political map of the Western Hemisphere, leading to the "Balkanization" of Hispanic America into numerous new nations.

## Timeline
- **1808** — Napoleon forces Spanish Bourbons to abdicate; Peninsular War begins.
- **1809** — First juntas established in Chuquisaca, La Paz, and Quito.
- **1810** — Juntas form across Spanish America after the fall of the Supreme Central Junta in Spain.
- **1811** — Venezuela, New Granada, and Paraguay declare independence.
- **1814** — Ferdinand VII restored to Spanish throne; abolishes liberal Constitution of 1812.
- **1815** — Spain sends largest expeditionary force (10,500 troops) to reconquer South America.
- **1817** — José de San Martín's Army of the Andes crosses into Chile.
- **1819** — Simón Bolívar crosses the Andes; victory at Battle of Boyacá secures New Granada.
- **1820** — Rafael del Riego's revolt in Spain reinstates the 1812 Constitution, halting reinforcements.
- **1821** — Agustín de Iturbide's Plan of Iguala leads to Mexican independence.
- **1822** — Battle of Pichincha secures independence for Quito (Ecuador).
- **1824** — Battle of Ayacucho decisively defeats the last major Spanish army in South America.
- **1825** — Upper Peru declares independence as Bolivia.
- **1833** — Death of Ferdinand VII ends Spain's plans for reconquest.

## Body

### Causes and Context
The wars emerged from a power vacuum created by Napoleon's 1808 invasion of Spain. Long-term causes included the Bourbon Reforms, which increased Spanish control, restricted Creole (American-born Spanish) access to office, and weakened the Catholic Church's economic power, alienating key elites. Enlightenment ideas and the examples of the American and French Revolutions also fueled desires for reform. Initially, most Spanish Americans sought to preserve autonomy under a restored Ferdinand VII, not outright independence. The formation of local juntas (governing councils) in 1808–1810 was a direct response to the crisis in Spain, but these bodies soon became vehicles for independence movements.

### Military Campaigns and Civil War
The conflicts were both conventional wars and civil wars, with social and racial tensions playing a major role. Rural populations, like the Llaneros in Venezuela and peasants following Miguel Hidalgo in Mexico, often joined for grievances unrelated to independence. Royalist armies were predominantly composed of Spanish Americans, not European troops. Key turning points included the 1814 restoration of Ferdinand VII, which hardened positions against reconciliation, and the 1820 liberal revolt in Spain, which prevented reinforcements from reaching the Americas. Pro-independence forces, led by figures like Simón Bolívar in the north and José de San Martín in the south, executed bold campaigns crossing the Andes to liberate neighboring regions.

### Consolidation of Independence
Independence was consolidated through a combination of military victory and political negotiation. In Mexico and Central America, conservative and liberal royalists allied under Agustín de Iturbide to achieve independence via the Plan of Iguala (1821). In South America, Bolívar and San Martín led a pincer movement. After securing Chile, San Martín invaded Peru but ultimately ceded command to Bolívar. The 1824 Battle of Ayacucho was the decisive defeat of Spanish forces on the continent. The new republics faced immediate challenges: unstable borders, economic disruption from the collapse of the Spanish trade monopoly, and a prolonged struggle between liberal and conservative factions over the shape of new societies.

### Effects and Legacy
Independence led to the abolition of the Inquisition and noble titles, but slavery persisted until the 1850s in most countries. Creole elites replaced Spanish-born officials, maintaining social dominance. Economically, the wars destroyed the colonial trade system, and new nations became exporters of raw materials. Politically, the 19th century was marked by instability, the rise of military caudillos (strongmen), and the collapse of large states like Gran Colombia and the Federal Republic of Central America. Socially, while women participated as spies, nurses, and soldiers, their roles were often confined to supportive spheres.

## Terms
*   **Creole (Criollo):** A person of full Spanish descent born in the Americas.
*   **Peninsular (Peninsulare):** A person born in Spain (the Iberian Peninsula).
*   **Royalist (Realista):** A supporter of the Spanish monarchy and the unity of the empire.
*   **Patriot (Patriota):** A supporter of independence from Spain.
*   **Junta:** A governing council, formed in the absence of a legitimate monarch.
*   **Cortes:** The representative legislative assembly of Spain.
*   **Caudillo:** A military and political strongman, a common form of leadership in post-independence Latin America.
*   **Bourbon Reforms:** A series of 18th-century administrative and economic changes by the Spanish Crown to increase control and revenue from its colonies.
*   **Plan of Iguala:** The 1821 treaty that secured Mexican independence, guaranteeing independence, Catholicism, and equality between Creoles and Peninsulares.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Motivations for Independence:** Historians debate whether the wars were primarily driven by elite Creole economic interests, Enlightenment ideals, or popular social grievances. The source notes that "there was little interest in outright independence" initially.
*   **Role of Social Conflict:** The extent to which the wars were a social revolution versus a political separation is contested. While slavery was eventually abolished, the Creole elite largely preserved the existing social hierarchy.
*   **Inevitability of Balkanization:** Scholars question whether the fragmentation into many small republics was inevitable or a result of regional rivalries and the collapse of central authority during the wars.
