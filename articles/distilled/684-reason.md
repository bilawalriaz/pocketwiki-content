# Reason

## Overview
Reason is the human capacity to consciously apply logic, drawing valid conclusions from information to seek truth. It underpins philosophy, science, mathematics, and language, and is historically viewed as the defining trait separating humans from other animals. The concept has evolved from a classical view of reason as a cosmic, teleological principle shared with the divine order, to a modern "subject-centred" faculty grounded in the individual knower, and finally to a fragmented set of formal procedures (cognitive-instrumental, moral-practical, aesthetic). Central debates persist regarding reason’s relationship to truth, emotion, faith, and whether it evolved primarily for individual cognition or collective argumentation.

## Timeline
- **Classical Greece (c. 5th–4th c. BC)** — Pythagoras/Heraclitus posit cosmic reason (*logos*); Plato identifies reason as the soul’s monarch; Aristotle defines humans as "rational animals" and links reason to *eudaimonia*.
- **Late Antiquity (3rd–5th c. AD)** — Plotinus (Neoplatonism) frames the cosmos as a single rational soul; early Church Fathers (Augustine, Basil, Gregory) synthesize Neoplatonism with Christian theology.
- **High Middle Ages (12th–13th c.)** — Islamic scholars (Averroes, Avicenna, Alhazen) preserve and extend Aristotelian logic; Scholastics (Aquinas, Roger Bacon, Albertus Magnus) integrate reason with Natural Law and early scientific method.
- **17th Century** — Descartes rejects "rational animal" for "thinking thing" (*cogito*), founding subject-centred reason; Hobbes redefines reason as "calculative" (addition/subtraction).
- **18th Century** — Hume argues reason is "slave of the passions" and denies necessary causal knowledge; Kant responds with "transcendental" reason, establishing the categorical imperative and limits of pure reason.
- **19th–20th Century** — Hegel, Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger, Foucault critique universal/instrumental reason; Habermas splits reason into three formal spheres (cognitive-instrumental, moral-practical, aesthetic).
- **Late 20th Century – Present** — Cognitive science (Wason test, dual-process theories) and neuroscience study reasoning mechanisms; Sperber & Mercier propose "argumentative theory" (reason evolved for social argumentation, not solo truth-seeking).

## Body

### Etymology and Core Definitions
The English "reason" derives via French *raison* from Latin *ratio*, used to translate Greek *logos* (speech, explanation, account, or ratio). In philosophy, *logos*, *ratio*, *raison*, and "reason" were treated as interchangeable by early modern writers like Bacon, Hobbes, and Locke. The adjective is "rational." A "reason" (count noun) is a consideration justifying a belief or action; "reasoning" is the process of providing good reasons. Reasoning subdivides into **deductive** (conclusion follows necessarily from premises), **inductive** (probable generalizations from observations), and **abductive** (inference to the best explanation from incomplete data).

### Classical Philosophy: Reason as Cosmic Order
For Plato and Aristotle, nature was **teleological**—every thing has a definitive purpose within an ordered cosmos. Reason (*logos*) was not merely a human trait but a cosmic principle linking the immortal human mind to the divine. Plato’s *psyche* (soul) is ruled by reason as "natural monarch" over spiritedness (*thumos*) and passions. Aristotle defined humans as *rational animals*, locating the highest good (*eudaimonia*) in a life lived in accordance with reason. Neoplatonists like Plotinus radicalized this: the cosmos possesses a single World Soul, the seat of all reason, which provides form to matter and draws individual souls back to their source. This framework became the bridge to monotheism, grounding the soul’s immortality and divinity.

### Christian and Islamic Synthesis: Reason and Revelation
Early Church Fathers (Augustine, Basil, Gregory) adopted the Neoplatonic view of the rational soul. Medieval Islamic philosophers (Averroes, Avicenna, Alhazen) preserved Aristotle and advanced optics and scientific methodology. In the Latin West, **Scholasticism** fused these streams. **Thomas Aquinas** placed reason at the heart of **Natural Law**: because reason is a "spark of the divine," every human life is invaluable, equal, and endowed with intrinsic rights—a foundation later expanded by the School of Salamanca. Meanwhile, Roger Bacon and Albertus Magnus, following Alhazen, emphasized reason as an intrinsic ability to decode the created order, instrumental in the birth of the scientific method at early universities.

### Early Modernity: The Subject-Centred Turn
The teleological worldview collapsed. Nature was no longer seen as human-like or purpose-driven; humans became "thinking things" (*res cogitans*) subject to the same laws as inanimate matter.
*   **Descartes** (17th c.): Doubted all knowledge except the thinking self (*cogito*). He unified the incorporeal soul (reason, intellect, understanding) as one indivisible entity, founding **epistemological/subject-centred reason**—the knowing subject studying objects to master them.
*   **Hobbes**: Reduced reason to "calculative" addition/subtraction, not limited to numbers.
*   **Hume** (18th c.): Radical skepticism. Denied necessary causal connections; declared reason "the slave of the passions." Argued reason is not qualitatively distinct from animal association, only more complex.
*   **Kant** (18th c.): Answered Hume by positing a **transcendental self** ("I") as a necessary condition of experience. Reason (Vernunft) legislates universal laws: **theoretical reason** posits laws of nature; **practical reason** (moral autonomy) legislates the **categorical imperative** ("Act only according to that maxim whereby you can... will that it should become a universal law"). Kant claimed "transcendental logic" could solve metaphysical problems, including morality’s foundations.

### Modern Fragmentation: Substantive vs. Formal Reason
**Habermas** argued the "substantive unity" of reason (answering "How should I live?") has dissolved. Reason is now strictly **formal/procedural**, split into three autonomous expert spheres:
1.  **Cognitive–instrumental reason**: Sciences; prediction, control, intervention.
2.  **Moral–practical reason**: Moral/political deliberation via universalizable procedures (Kant’s imperative).
3.  **Aesthetic reason**: Art/literature; novel world-disclosure.
Philosophers must mediate these expert domains with the "lifeworld."

### The Critique of Reason
A persistent tradition questions reason’s authority.
*   **Skeptics** (Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Rorty): Doubt universal, instrumental, or subject-centred reason entirely.
*   **Intersubjectivity advocates** (Hegel): Argue reason obscures "spirit" or shared communal life.
*   **Pluralists** (Foucault): Identify neglected "other forms of reason" essential to modern life; suggest multiple, potentially conflicting rationalities with no super-rational arbiter.
*   **Re-orientations**: Habermas proposes **communicative reason** (cooperative, linguistically intersubjective). Kompridis views reason as practices preserving "openness" for social change. Taylor (via Heidegger) adds a faculty of **disclosure** (everyday sense-making). Foucault distinguishes **private reason** (role-bound, "cog in a machine") from **public reason** (free, universal, "member of reasonable humanity").

### Reason Compared to Logic, Causality, and Symbolism
*   **Logic vs. Reason**: Logic codifies formal rules *inside* a system (Aristotle’s syllogistic); reason operates *outside* systems (skipping steps, changing rules, diagramming). Rationality involves **metacognition**—consciousness and control over inferences—exceeding mere logicality.
*   **Cause/Effect & Association**: Animals associate cause/effect (dog avoids kicker) but lack reason. Human reason requires **explicable** mediation (e.g., a third idea/syllogism per Locke) linking smoke to fire.
*   **Symbolic Thinking**: **Peirce** and **Hobbes** argue strict reason requires creating/manipulating symbol systems (language), where symbols have only habitual connections to referents. **Donald** and **Plato** (*eikasia*) emphasize the human ability to distinguish representations (icons, dreams, mimicry) from reality—**mimesis** (intentional representation) vs. mere imitation. **Anamnēsis** (voluntary recollection/search) distinguishes human memory from animal *mneme*.

### Psychology, Cognitive Science, and Evolution
*   **Psychology**: Studies rationality (theoretical/practical) via behavioral experiments (Wason test, conditional reasoning, spatial/temporal deduction, counterfactuals) and developmental trajectories (Piaget, neo-Piagetian theories).
*   **Neuroscience**: Maps brain structures/functions for reasoning; clinical psychology alters unhelpful reasoning habits.
*   **Computer Science**: **Automated reasoning** (theorem proving, formal verification); **Meta-reasoning** (systems reasoning about their own operation via reflection).
*   **Evolution (Sperber & Mercier)**: Reason is cognitively difficult and biased (confirmation bias). **Argumentative theory**: Reason evolved primarily for **collective** argumentation—winning arguments and justifying actions to others—not solitary truth-seeking. Group selection favors effective collective reasoning.

### Political Philosophy and Ethics
*   **Aristotle**: Humans are *political animals* because they possess *logos* (reasoned speech), which reveals advantage/harm, just/unjust. The *polis* (city-state) is natural; justice is political. Without virtue, reason-armed humans are the "most unholy and savage."
*   **Rousseau** (Second Discourse): Inverted Aristotle. Reason, language, and politics are **not natural** but developed accidentally via cooperative habits solving problems, later forced by defense needs. Human nature is **malleable**; the "natural" state was ape-like. This implies a wider range of possible societies (influencing Marx) but Rousseau himself favored classical city-states (Geneva).
*   **Reason vs. Passion**: Stoics saw emotions as false judgements; Hume made reason passion’s slave; Damasio (Somatic Marker Hypothesis) argues emotions ("gut feelings") are necessary inputs for rational decision-making. **Rationalization** masks desire as logic.
*   **Reason vs. Faith/Tradition**: Tensions framed as **Athens vs. Jerusalem**. Responses: **Reconciliation** (Plantinga: theism explains reason’s efficacy); **Non-overlapping magisteria** (Gould: separate domains); **Tu quoque** (MacIntyre, Taylor, Feyerabend: secular liberalism/science also suppress reasoning); **Unification** (Ratzinger/Benedict XVI: Christianity *is* the religion of the *Logos*; Enlightenment rationality has Christian origin). Strauss’s "Greater West" includes Muslim lands shaped by Greek rationalism/Abrahamic revelation tension.

## Terms
- ****Logos**** — Greek root of "logic"; means speech, explanation, account, or reason; the cosmic rational principle in classical philosophy.
- ****Ratio**** — Latin translation of *logos* (also "account of money"); source of "reason" via French *raison*.
- ****Teleology**** — The view that nature/purposes have inherent aims or ends; central to Aristotle’s account of reason.
- ****Eudaimonia**** — Aristotle’s term for highest human happiness/flourishing: life lived excellently in accordance with reason.
- ****Natural Law**** — Aquinas’s doctrine: reason is a divine spark, grounding intrinsic human equality, rights, and moral law.
- ****Subject-centred reason**** — Descartes’ model: the knowing subject (*cogito*) studies objects to master them; foundation of modern epistemology.
- ****Categorical Imperative**** — Kant’s supreme moral principle: act only on maxims you can will as universal law; basis of moral autonomy.
- ****Transcendental Logic**** — Kant’s "theoretical science" of the conditions of experience (time, space, categories), distinct from formal logic.
- ****Mimesis**** — Intentional representation/invention of images (Plato, Aristotle, Donald); distinct from mimicry; basis of language and reason.
- ****Eikasia**** — Plato’s term: the ability to perceive an image as an image (e.g., dream, reflection) distinct from reality; prerequisite for reason.
- ****Anamnēsis**** — Voluntary recollection/search of memory (vs. passive *mneme*); "autocueing" enabling representational thinking.
- ****Argumentative Theory**** — Sperber & Mercier: reason evolved for social argumentation (justifying/convincing), not individual truth-seeking.
- ****Somatic Marker Hypothesis**** — Damasio: emotional "gut feelings" guide decision-making; rationality requires emotional input.
- ****Non-overlapping Magisteria (NOMA)**** — Gould’s thesis: science/reason and religion/faith hold authority in separate, non-conflicting domains.

## Debates and open questions
1.  **Epistemological Foundation**: Can reason attain certain truth if its first principles (*archai*) rest on fallible sense perceptions (Empiricism) or require a "higher" intellectual intuition (Idealism/Platonism)?
2.  **Reason vs. Passion**: Is reason the ruler, slave, or partner of the passions? (Plato/Stoics vs. Hume vs. Damasio).
3.  **Unity vs. Pluralism**: Is there one universal Reason (Kant), a formal procedural unity (Habermas), or multiple incommensurable rationalities (Foucault, Nietzsche)?
4.  **Evolutionary Function**: Did reason evolve for individual cognition (navigation, tool use) or collective argumentation (Sperber & Mercier)?
5.  **Reason and Rights**: Are human rights discoverable by reason alone (Natural Law/Aquinas), or are they historical constructions (Rousseau/Marx)?
6.  **Faith and Reason**: Are they compatible (Plantinga, Ratzinger), separate (Gould), or in irreducible tension (Athens vs. Jerusalem)?
