# Prussia

## Overview
Prussia was a German state centered on the North European Plain that originated from the 1525 secularization of the Teutonic Order's territory. Ruled by the House of Hohenzollern, it grew from a duchy into a dominant European power and the core of the German Empire, which it unified in 1871. Prussia's history, marked by military prowess and administrative efficiency, profoundly shaped modern Germany before its official dissolution by the Allies in 1947.

- **1740-1763** — Frederick the Great's Silesian Wars establish Prussia as a great power.
- **1807** — Treaties of Tilsit severely reduce Prussia after defeat by Napoleon.

- **1867** — Prussia leads the formation of the North German Confederation.
- **1871** — Prussia unifies German states into the German Empire.
- **1918** — German Revolution ends the monarchy; Prussia becomes a Free State in the Weimar Republic.
- **1932** — The "Preußenschlag" coup deposes the democratically elected Prussian government.
- **1947** — Allied Control Council Law No. 46 officially dissolves Prussia.

## Body

### Origins and Rise to Kingdom
The name derives from the Old Prussians, a Baltic people conquered by the German crusading Teutonic Knights in the 13th century. The Knights' monastic state was largely Germanized through immigration. After defeat by Poland-Lithuania, the Second Peace of Thorn (1466) split the territory. In 1525, Grand Master Albert I secularized the eastern Duchy of Prussia, becoming a vassal of the Polish crown. This duchy united with the Margraviate of Brandenburg in 1618, forming Brandenburg-Prussia. The "Great Elector" Frederick William I (r. 1640–1688) built a powerful army and bureaucracy. In 1701, his son Frederick III crowned himself King Frederick I *in* Prussia, elevating the state's status.

### Great Power Status and Enlightenment
Under Frederick the Great (r. 1740–1786), Prussia seized the rich province of Silesia from Austria, initiating a century of rivalry. Prussia became a leading European power, practicing "enlightened absolutism" with legal and educational reforms. It participated in the Partitions of Poland (1772, 1793, 1795), connecting its core territories. After a devastating defeat by Napoleon in 1806, Prussia implemented modernizing reforms, including the abolition of serfdom and the introduction of compulsory military service. Victory in the Napoleonic Wars led to significant territorial gains at the Congress of Vienna (1815), including the industrial Ruhr region.

### Unification of Germany
In the 19th century, Prussia led German unification through "blood and iron." Minister President Otto von Bismarck engineered three wars: against Denmark (1864), Austria (1866), and France (1870–71). The victory over Austria ended Habsburg influence and led to the North German Confederation (1867), dominated by Prussia. The Franco-Prussian War prompted the southern German states to join, and on 18 January 1871, the German Empire was proclaimed with the Prussian king as Kaiser. Prussia comprised three-fifths of the new empire's territory.

### Decline and Dissolution
The German Revolution of 1918 ended the monarchy. As a "Free State" in the Weimar Republic, Prussia was a democratic stronghold under Social Democrat Otto Braun. This democracy was destroyed by the conservative "Preußenschlag" coup of 1932, which transferred power to the Reich government. The Nazis formally dissolved Prussian state institutions in 1934–35. After World War II, its eastern territories were annexed by Poland and the Soviet Union, with German populations expelled. Allied Law No. 46 of 1947 officially abolished Prussia, deeming it "a bearer of militarism and reaction."

## Terms
- **Junkers**: The landed aristocratic class of Prussia, especially dominant in the east, who provided the officer corps and administrative elite.
- **Ostsiedlung**: The medieval eastward expansion and colonization of Central and Eastern Europe by German settlers.
- **Constructive vote of no confidence**: A constitutional mechanism, pioneered in Weimar Prussia, where a head of government can only be removed if a successor simultaneously receives a majority.
- **Zollverein**: The German Customs Union, established in 1834 under Prussian leadership, which fostered economic integration preceding political unification.
- **Kulturkampf**: The "culture struggle" of the 1870s–80s, Bismarck's campaign to reduce the influence of the Catholic Church in Prussia and Germany.
- **Preußenschlag**: The "Prussian coup" of July 1932, when the Reich government deposed the democratically elected Prussian state government.
- **Free State (Freistaat)**: The republican form of government adopted by Prussia and other German states after 1918.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which Bismarck deliberately planned German unification versus opportunistically exploiting circumstances is debated by historians.
- The legacy of Prussia is contested: is it primarily defined by militarism and authoritarianism, or also by its traditions of efficient administration, legal reform, and religious tolerance?
- The international status of Prussia's former eastern territories was disputed until the Treaty on the Final Settlement with Respect to Germany (1990), but their return remains a cause for some German revanchist groups.
