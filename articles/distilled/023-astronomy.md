# Astronomy

## Overview
Astronomy is the natural science that studies celestial objects—planets, moons, stars, nebulae, galaxies, meteoroids, asteroids, and comets—and phenomena originating beyond Earth's atmosphere, including supernova explosions, gamma-ray bursts, quasars, pulsars, and cosmic microwave background radiation. It uses mathematics, physics, and chemistry to explain their origin and evolution, with cosmology as the branch studying the universe as a whole. One of the oldest natural sciences, astronomy has evolved from ancient calendar-making and navigation into a physics-driven discipline, and it remains one of the few sciences where amateurs actively contribute to discoveries.

## Timeline
- **c. 150–80 BC** — Antikythera mechanism, an early analog computer for calculating Sun, Moon, and planet positions, is built.
- **4th century BC** — Heracleides Ponticus proposes Earth rotates on its own axis.
- **3rd century BC** — Aristarchus of Samos proposes a heliocentric model and estimates sizes/distances of Moon and Sun.
- **2nd century BC** — Hipparchus calculates Moon's size/distance, invents the astrolabe, and catalogs 1020 stars.
- **964** — Al-Sufi describes the Andromeda Galaxy in the Book of Fixed Stars.
- **Renaissance** — Copernicus proposes a heliocentric model with circular orbits.
- **1610** — Galileo observes Venus's phases, supporting heliocentrism; around the same time Kepler devises elliptical planetary motion.
- **1781** — William Herschel discovers Uranus, the first new planet found.
- **1922–1923** — Edwin Hubble proves Andromeda and Triangulum are galaxies outside the Milky Way.
- **1929** — Hubble publishes Hubble's law: galaxies recede with velocity proportional to distance.
- **1965** — Cosmic microwave background radiation discovered, cementing Big Bang evidence.
- **2015** — LIGO detects gravitational waves from a binary black hole.

## Body

### Etymology and terminology
Astronomy derives from Greek *astron* ("star") and *nomos* ("law" or "rule"), meaning study of celestial objects. It should not be confused with astrology, a belief system claiming human affairs correlate with celestial positions; the two share a common origin but diverged, with astronomy supported by physics. "Astronomy" and "astrophysics" are broadly synonymous today, though some use "astronomy" for qualitative study and "astrophysics" for physics-oriented work. Fields like astrometry are purely astronomy, while research departments choose terms based on historical affiliation with physics.

### Pre-historic and classical history
Early astronomy was driven by practical needs like agricultural calendars. Prehistoric sites such as Stonehenge and the Nebra sky disc (with symbols for sun, moon, stars) show ancient interest, while Nabta Playa in Upper Egypt aligned with Sirius's heliacal rising to calibrate the Nile flood calendar. The early civilizations in recorded history made methodical observations of the night sky, including the Egyptians, Babylonians, Greeks, Indians, Chinese, Maya, and many ancient indigenous peoples of the Americas. Babylonians began mathematical astronomy and discovered lunar eclipses recur in a saros cycle of 223 synodic months. Greek astronomy sought rational explanations: Heracleides Ponticus proposed Earth's rotation, Aristarchus estimated lunar/solar sizes and proposed heliocentrism, and Hipparchus invented the astrolabe, observed precession, and cataloged 1020 stars. The Antikythera mechanism (c. 150–80 BC) was an analog computer for celestial positions; similar complexity reappeared only in 14th-century Europe.

### Post-classical and medieval astronomy
After the Greeks, the geocentric Ptolemaic system (from Claudius Ptolemy's *Almagest*) dominated for over a thousand years, offering the most accurate predictions available despite being eventually discredited. Hellenistic astronomy reached India through trade, where Āryabhaṭa improved planetary motion and eclipse calculations, building on indigenous Vedic calendrical traditions. Islamic astronomy flourished from the early 9th century: al-Sufi described the Andromeda Galaxy in 964, Ali ibn Ridwan observed the SN 1006 supernova in 1006, and Al-Biruni showed the Sun's apogee was mobile, not fixed. West African sites like Great Zimbabwe and Timbuktu may have housed observatories; Songhai historian Mahmud Kati documented a meteor shower in 1583. In medieval Europe, Richard of Wallingford invented the first astronomical clock and the Albion equatorium; Nicole Oresme discussed Earth's rotation; Jean Buridan developed impetus theory. The Roman Catholic Church supported astronomy for over six centuries, partly to determine Easter's date.

### Copernicus to modern cosmology
Copernicus's Renaissance heliocentric model, though retaining circular orbits, allowed calculating planetary orbit sizes and periods; its simplicity won adoption before Galileo's 1610 telescopic confirmation via Venus's phases. Kepler, analyzing Tycho Brahe's observations, replaced circular with elliptical motion but lacked a theoretical basis; Newton supplied it with celestial dynamics and gravitation, also inventing the reflecting telescope. Telescopes revealed the Milky Way as numerous stars (Galileo, 1610), and Herschel discovered Uranus in 1781. The 18th–19th centuries saw the three-body problem refined by Euler, Clairaut, and d'Alembert, with Lagrange and Laplace estimating planetary masses from perturbations. Spectroscopy advanced when Fraunhofer found 574 dark solar spectral lines (1814–15) and Kirchhoff attributed them to elements (1859). Herschel mapped the Milky Way as a disk of stars; by the 20th century, Leavitt's Cepheid variables (1912) enabled distance measurement, Shapley mapped the Milky Way, and Hubble proved external galaxies existed (1922–23). Einstein's 1917 general relativity began modern cosmology; Friedman (1922) showed expanding/contracting universe solutions; Hubble's 1929 law indicated expansion, supporting Lemaître's Big Bang concept. Big bang nucleosynthesis models developed in the late 1940s–early 1950s, and the 1965 cosmic microwave background discovery cemented the Big Bang. LIGO detected gravitational waves in 2015.

### Observational astronomy by wavelength
Observational astronomy is categorized by electromagnetic spectrum region. Radio astronomy (1 mm–15 m wavelengths) detects hydrogen's 21 cm spectral line, pulsars, supernovae, and active galactic nuclei. Infrared astronomy studies cold objects like planets and dust-obscured regions; most infrared is absorbed by the atmosphere, so observatories sit at high, dry sites or in space (e.g., James Webb Space Telescope for distant galaxies redshifted into infrared). Optical astronomy, the oldest form, progressed from hand drawings to photography to CCD digital detectors, covering roughly 380–700 nm plus some near-ultraviolet and infrared. Ultraviolet astronomy requires space-based observation and studies hot blue OB stars. X-ray astronomy, also needing high-altitude platforms, examines X-ray binaries, supernova remnants, and the solar corona. Gamma-ray astronomy uses satellites or Cherenkov telescopes (which detect visible flashes from gamma-ray absorption in the atmosphere) to study cosmic rays, dark matter annihilation, and gamma-ray bursts—the brightest phenomena in the universe. Non-electromagnetic methods include neutrino astronomy (underground detectors like SAGE, GALLEX, Kamioka; 24 neutrinos from supernova 1987A), cosmic ray detection, and gravitational-wave astronomy (LIGO's first detection on 14 September 2015). Combining these is multi-messenger astronomy. Astrometry, one of the oldest fields, measures celestial positions for navigation and calendars; celestial mechanics uses planetary position measurements to understand gravitational perturbations, while stellar parallax provides a fundamental cosmic distance baseline.

### Subfields by scale
Physical cosmology studies the universe's large-scale structure, accepting the Big Bang—the universe began dense and hot, expanding over 13.8 billion years—widely accepted after 1965's microwave background discovery; dark matter and dark energy are thought to form 96% of the universe's mass. Extragalactic astronomy studies galaxy formation, evolution, morphology, and clusters. Galactic astronomy examines the Milky Way, a barred spiral galaxy in the Local Group; kinematic studies show a dark matter halo dominates its mass, though its nature is undetermined. Stellar astronomy covers star formation in giant molecular clouds, protostars, main-sequence nuclear fusion, and evolution ending in supernovae or white dwarfs; supernova remnants are neutron stars or black holes (if stellar mass is at least three times the Sun's). Solar astronomy studies the Sun, a G2 V main-sequence dwarf about 4.6 billion years old, including sunspot cycles and layers from core to corona. Planetary science studies Solar System bodies and exoplanets, covering differentiation, magnetic fields, and heat sources (collisions, radioactive decay, tidal heating) driving volcanism and tectonics.

### Interdisciplinary and amateur astronomy
Astrochemistry overlaps astronomy and chemistry, studying molecular abundances and reactions in the Solar System and interstellar medium. Astrobiology (or exobiology) studies life's origin and development beyond Earth, using astronomy, biochemistry, geology, and microbiology to investigate extraterrestrial life and biosignatures. Other links include archaeoastronomy (ancient astronomies in cultural context), astrostatistics (statistical analysis of astrophysical data), and forensic astronomy (solving art history and legal problems). Amateurs contribute significantly, observing with consumer or homemade equipment, targeting Messier (110 objects) and Herschel 400 catalogues, discovering comets, measuring occultations to refine minor planet orbits, and advancing astrophotography; Karl Jansky, a pioneer of amateur radio astronomy, discovered a radio source at the Milky Way's center.

## Terms
- **Cosmology**: Branch of astronomy studying the universe as a whole.
- **Heliocentric model**: Solar system model with Earth and planets orbiting the Sun, proposed by Aristarchus and later Copernicus.
- **Geocentric model (Ptolemaic system)**: Earth-centered universe model from Ptolemy's *Almagest*, dominant for over a millennium.
- **Cepheid variable stars**: Stars with periodic luminosity changes used to determine true luminosity and distance.
- **Hubble's law**: Relation that galaxies recede with velocity proportional to distance, indicating universal expansion.
- **Big Bang**: Concept that the universe began extremely dense and hot and expanded over 13.8 billion years.
- **Cosmic microwave background radiation**: Radiation discovered in 1965 that cemented Big Bang evidence.
- **Astrometry**: Measurement of celestial object positions, one of the oldest scientific fields.
- **Multi-messenger astronomy**: Combining observations from electromagnetic radiation, neutrinos, or gravitational waves.
- **Dark matter and dark energy**: Components thought to form 96% of the universe's mass, whose physics remains poorly understood.

## Debates and open questions
Important unanswered questions remain in 21st-century astronomy. Cosmic-scale puzzles include the nature of dark matter and dark energy that dominate cosmic evolution, the ultimate fate of the universe, and why lithium abundance is four times lower than the standard Big Bang model predicts. More specific questions concern whether the Solar System is normal or atypical, the origin of the stellar mass spectrum (initial mass function) regardless of initial conditions, the formation of the first galaxies, the origin of supermassive black holes, the source of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays, and whether other life—especially intelligent life—exists in the universe.
