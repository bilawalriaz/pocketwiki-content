# Computer animation

## Overview
Computer animation is the process of digitally generating moving images, a successor to traditional and stop-motion animation. It allows artists to create motion without physical models or actors, using software to manipulate digital equivalents frame-by-frame. This technique is fundamental to modern film, television, and video games, enabling everything from stylized cartoons to photorealistic simulations.

- **1976** — *Futureworld* becomes the first feature film to use 3D computer-generated imagery (CGI).
- **1982** — *Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan* and *Tron* incorporate CGI.

- **2019** — *The Lion King* remake achieves photorealism marketed as live-action.

## Body

### Principles and Methods
Computer animation creates the illusion of movement by displaying images in rapid succession, typically at 24, 25, or 30 frames per second. To avoid jerkiness, animation often requires a minimum of 12 frames per second. The core methods are **keyframing**, where an animator sets poses at key points in time and the computer generates the in-between frames (**interpolation**), and **motion capture**, which records a live actor's performance to drive a digital character. These methods can be used separately or in combination.

### 3D Computer Animation
In 3D animation, digital models are built and rigged with a virtual skeleton. Animators manipulate this skeleton by setting keyframes for attributes like position, rotation, and scale. The software interpolates between these keys, often using Bézier curves for smooth motion. Advanced techniques include **procedural animation**, using algorithms like noise to simulate organic movement (e.g., swarms, smoke), and **node-based animation**, which builds complex rules for chaotic or organic shapes. The entire process, from modeling to rendering, is known as the **3D animation pipeline**.

### 2D Computer Animation
2D computer animation uses digital illustrations on separate layers. **Computer-assisted animation** (or digital ink and paint) involves hand-drawn or digitally drawn frames placed on keyframes, with the computer generating the in-between frames. Moving objects are often called **sprites**. This method retains the stylistic look of traditional animation while speeding up production. It was famously used in Disney films like *Beauty and the Beast* and *The Lion King*.

### History and Evolution
Early experiments occurred in the 1960s. A major milestone was the 3D wire-frame imagery in *Futureworld* (1976). The field advanced rapidly with annual SIGGRAPH conferences. The release of *Toy Story* (1995) launched the era of feature-length computer-animated films. Subsequent decades saw CGI integrated into live-action films (*Avatar*) and achieve photorealism (*The Lion King*, 2019).

### Realism and Challenges
A key goal is often photorealism, but this can lead to the **uncanny valley**, where near-human replicas evoke negative responses. Many films avoid this by using non-realistic, anthropomorphic characters. Computer animation also mimics other styles, like stop-motion. Traditional principles like squash and stretch remain important for believable, non-photorealistic movement.

## Terms
- **Computer-Generated Imagery (CGI):** The broader term for both still and moving images created digitally.
- **Keyframing:** Setting specific poses (keyframes) at points in time; the computer generates the motion between them.
- **Interpolation:** The automatic generation of in-between frames by the computer based on keyframes.
- **Rigging:** The process of creating a virtual skeleton and controllers for a 3D model to enable animation.
- **Motion Capture:** Recording the movement of a live actor to apply to a digital character.
- **Tweening:** Short for "in-betweening," the process of generating intermediate frames between two images.
- **Sprite:** A 2D image or animation with a location, used in 2D computer animation.
- **Uncanny Valley:** The hypothesis that human replicas which appear almost, but not exactly, like real humans can cause revulsion.
- **Render Farm:** A network of powerful computers used together to process the complex calculations needed for rendering animation.
