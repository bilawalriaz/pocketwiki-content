# Baroque music

## Overview
Baroque music is the dominant style of Western classical music from approximately 1600 to 1750, bridging the Renaissance and Classical periods. It is foundational to the modern "classical music" canon, establishing core harmonic principles, instrumental techniques, and genres like opera and the concerto that remain central to musical study and performance.

## Timeline
- **c. 1580–1630** — Early Baroque period; Florentine Camerata experiments lead to the first operas (e.g., Jacopo Peri's *Dafne*).
- **1600s** — Development of *basso continuo* and *figured bass*; Claudio Monteverdi bridges Renaissance and Baroque styles.
- **1630–1700** — Middle Baroque; rise of centralized courts (e.g., Louis XIV) and public music; Jean-Baptiste Lully dominates French opera.
- **1630s** — Emergence of vocal forms: cantata, oratorio, and opera in Italy.
- **Late 1600s** — Arcangelo Corelli publishes influential instrumental music and develops the *concerto grosso*.
- **1680–1750** — Late Baroque; *opera seria* and the *da capo aria* become standard; modern orchestra begins to form.
- **c. 1700** — Bartolomeo Cristofori perfects the fortepiano, which later replaces the harpsichord.
- **1730s** — Italian composers begin shifting to the *galant style*; German composers like J.S. Bach continue in Baroque style until 1750.
- **1734** — Term "baroque" first applied to music in a critique of Rameau's *Hippolyte et Aricie*.
- **1919** — Curt Sachs first systematically applies the term "Baroque" to music history.

## Body

### Definition and Chronology
The term "baroque" derives from the Portuguese for an irregularly shaped pearl. It was initially used pejoratively in the 18th century to describe music seen as extravagant or dissonant. Historians later adopted it to define the period between Renaissance and Classical music. The exact boundaries are debated; musicologist Manfred Bukofzer proposed phases of "early" (1580–1630), "middle" (1630–1680), and "late" (1680–1730) Baroque, while others like Suzanne Clercx-Lejeune used different dates.

### Characteristics and Theory
Baroque music is characterized by its focus on *affective expression*—using music to stir specific emotions—prompted by a rediscovery of classical rhetoric. It saw the formalization of *common-practice tonality*, where music is organized around a central key, and the use of *chromaticism* (notes outside the standard scale) for expression. Composers experimented with fuller instrumental sounds, leading to the modern orchestra, and developed *figured bass*, a notation system where symbols above the bass line guide chord improvisation. Dense *polyphony* (multiple independent melodies) remained important, especially in sacred music.

### Early Baroque (c. 1580–1650)
The period began in Italy with the Florentine Camerata, a group that sought to revive ancient Greek drama, leading to the invention of opera. Claudio Monteverdi was pivotal, developing both the old polyphonic style (*prima pratica*) and the new basso continuo-driven style (*seconda pratica*). In France, an idiomatic lute texture called *style brisé* influenced keyboard music. Heinrich Schütz brought Italian innovations to Germany.

### Middle Baroque (1630–1700)
Music became tied to powerful courts and churches. Jean-Baptiste Lully established French opera and a standardized orchestra. In Italy, composers like Giacomo Carissimi and Francesco Cavalli refined vocal forms, elevating melody to equal importance with text. Arcangelo Corelli popularized the *concerto grosso*, a form contrasting a small group of soloists with the full orchestra, and his published works spread Italian style across Europe. Dieterich Buxtehude was a major church composer in Germany.

### Late Baroque (1680–1750)
Italian *opera seria* (serious opera) dominated, featuring the *da capo aria* and large orchestras, which helped standardize the modern orchestra's string section. Instrumental music flourished with composers like Antonio Vivaldi and J.S. Bach. The harpsichord was the main keyboard instrument, though the fortepiano was invented around 1700. Musical forms became more regular, and the key of a piece became a fundamental structural element. Toward the mid-18th century, a "gradual simplification" of harmony and texture began, leading toward the *galant style*.

### Forms and Genres
The *dance suite* was a core instrumental form, typically containing an allemande, courante, sarabande, and gigue, often preceded by a French overture. Other key forms included the *sonata*, *fugue*, and *toccata*. Vocal genres expanded to include opera, oratorio, and cantata. The *basso continuo* group (e.g., cello and harpsichord) provided harmonic foundation for most ensembles.

## Terms
- **Basso Continuo**: A continuous bass line and improvised chordal accompaniment, typically played by a keyboard instrument (like harpsichord) and a bass instrument (like cello).
- **Figured Bass**: A shorthand notation using numbers and symbols above the bass line to indicate chords to be improvised by the performer.
- **Common-Practice Tonality**: The system of organizing music around a central key or "home note," which became standard in the Baroque era.
- **Polyphony**: A musical texture with two or more independent, simultaneous melodic lines.
- **Monody**: A style of solo vocal music with simple accompaniment, contrasting with Renaissance polyphony.
- **Concerto Grosso**: A Baroque composition for a small group of soloists (*concertino*) contrasted with the full orchestra (*ripieno*).
- **Da Capo Aria**: A common aria form in three sections (ABA), where the first section is repeated after a contrasting middle section.
- **Opera Seria**: A serious, often heroic, form of Italian opera dominant in the 18th century.
- **Galant Style**: A transitional style of the mid-18th century emphasizing light, elegant melodies and simpler harmony, moving away from Baroque complexity.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the precise start and end dates of the Baroque period, as stylistic changes occurred at different times across Europe. The application of the term "Baroque"—originally a pejorative from visual arts criticism—to music is also contested, with some arguing it imposes non-musical categories on the repertoire. There is disagreement about the degree of stylistic unity across the era, given the diversity between national styles (e.g., French vs. Italian) and between sacred and secular music.
