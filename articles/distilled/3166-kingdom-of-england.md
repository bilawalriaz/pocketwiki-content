# Kingdom of England

## Overview
The Kingdom of England was a sovereign state on the island of Great Britain from 927, when Anglo-Saxon kingdoms were united under Æthelstan, until 1 May 1707, when it merged with Scotland to form the Kingdom of Great Britain. It was a major European power whose political evolution, from early unification through dynastic changes and civil conflict, established the constitutional monarchy that influenced modern governance.

## Timeline
- **886** — Alfred the Great reoccupies London, adopting the title "King of the Anglo-Saxons."
- **927** — Æthelstan conquers York, becoming the first ruler of a unified Kingdom of England.
- **1016** — Cnut the Great unites England, Denmark, and Norway in the North Sea Empire.
- **1066** — Norman Conquest; William the Conqueror is crowned king after the Battle of Hastings.
- **1284** — Edward I completes the conquest of Wales, placing it under the English crown.
- **1337–1453** — Hundred Years' War with France, ending with England losing all continental lands except Calais.
- **1455–1487** — Wars of the Roses, civil wars between the Houses of Lancaster and York.
- **1535–1542** — Laws in Wales Acts fully incorporate Wales into England.
- **1603** — James VI of Scotland becomes James I of England, uniting the crowns.
- **1649** — Execution of Charles I; monarchy abolished during the Interregnum.
- **1688** — Glorious Revolution establishes parliamentary sovereignty over the crown.
- **1707** — Acts of Union merge England and Scotland into the Kingdom of Great Britain.

## Body

### Formation and Early Unification
The Kingdom of England emerged from the Heptarchy, a group of early medieval Anglo-Saxon kingdoms. Viking invasions in the 9th century disrupted this balance, allowing the House of Wessex to rise. Alfred the Great's reoccupation of London in 886 was a turning point, and his successors expanded Wessex's authority. Æthelstan completed the unification by conquering the Viking kingdom of York in 927, becoming the first "King of the English." The kingdom was briefly part of the Danish North Sea Empire under Cnut (1016–1042) before returning to Anglo-Saxon rule.

### Norman Conquest and Dynastic Rule
The Norman Conquest of 1066, following the Battle of Hastings, transferred power to William the Conqueror. This led to the replacement of the Anglo-Saxon elite and the establishment of new feudal structures. The capital shifted from Winchester to Westminster, and London became the primary commercial center. Historians conventionally divide subsequent history by ruling dynasties: Norman/Angevin (1066–1216), Plantagenet (1216–1485), Tudor (1485–1603), and Stuart (1603–1707). All monarchs after 1066 descend from the Normans.

### Territorial Expansion and Conflict
Under the Plantagenets, England expanded its control. Edward I conquered Wales by 1284, and English kings claimed the French throne, leading to the Hundred Years' War. This conflict fostered a distinct English identity but ended with England losing nearly all French territories. The subsequent Wars of the Roses weakened the nobility, paving the way for the Tudor dynasty. The Tudors, particularly Henry VIII, fully integrated Wales and initiated the English Reformation, breaking from the Catholic Church. Elizabeth I established England as a major power and began colonial expansion.

### Civil War and Constitutional Monarchy
The Stuart period saw intense conflict over royal authority. Charles I's disputes with Parliament led to the English Civil War (1641–1645), his execution in 1649, and the establishment of a republic under Oliver Cromwell. The monarchy was restored in 1660, but the Glorious Revolution of 1688 cemented the principle that monarchs could not govern without Parliament's consent, effectively creating a constitutional monarchy.

### Union with Scotland
By the early 18th century, both England and Scotland faced succession crises. The Acts of Union 1707, motivated by English concerns over the Scottish succession and mutual economic benefits, abolished both parliaments and created the Kingdom of Great Britain with a single Parliament at Westminster. England ceased to exist as a separate political entity, though its legal system remained distinct.

## Terms
- **Heptarchy**: The collective name for the seven early Anglo-Saxon kingdoms of England.
- **Bretwalda**: A title for a high king acknowledged as overlord by other Anglo-Saxon kings.
- **Fyrd**: The Anglo-Saxon militia, where all free men owed military service.
- **Feudalism**: The medieval social system where land was held in exchange for military service and loyalty.
- **Scutage**: A payment made by a vassal to a lord in lieu of direct military service.
- **Interregnum**: The period (1649–1660) between the execution of Charles I and the restoration of the monarchy, when England was a republic.
- **Constitutional monarchy**: A system where the monarch's powers are limited by law and Parliament.
- **Acts of Union 1707**: The laws that merged the Kingdoms of England and Scotland into the Kingdom of Great Britain.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise nature and extent of Alfred the Great's authority over other Anglo-Saxon kingdoms is debated.
- The conventional dating of dynastic periods (e.g., the start of the Plantagenet era) is a historical simplification rather than a clear-cut break.
- The motivations behind the Union of 1707 are multifaceted, involving English strategic concerns about succession, Scottish financial incentives, and the removal of English trade sanctions.
