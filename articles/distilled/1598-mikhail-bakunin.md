# Mikhail Bakunin

## Overview
Mikhail Bakunin (1814–1876) was a Russian nobleman turned revolutionary anarchist who became the 19th century’s most prominent advocate for stateless socialism. Rejecting both capitalism and the Marxist "dictatorship of the proletariat," he argued that any state—even a workers’ state—inevitably becomes a tool of minority domination. Instead, he envisioned a global federation of self-governing communes and workplaces organized "from below upward" through voluntary association. His bitter rivalry with Karl Marx split the International Workingmen’s Association (First International) in 1872, birthing a distinct anarchist movement that shaped labor struggles in Spain, Switzerland, and Russia, and later influenced the New Left and anti-globalization movements. Bakunin’s prediction that Marxist regimes would become one-party dictatorships ruling *over* the proletariat is widely cited as prescient.

## Body

### Early Intellectual Formation
Born into a wealthy noble family with over 500 serfs, Bakunin received a Rousseau-inspired education at his family’s Priamukhino estate. After rejecting a military career—deserting the St. Petersburg Artillery School in 1835—he moved to Moscow, immersing himself in German Idealism. He became the foremost Russian expert on Hegel by 1837, producing the first Russian translation of Hegel’s works. Funded by Alexander Herzen, Bakunin studied at the University of Berlin (1840), where he gravitated toward the Young Hegelians, radicalizing Hegel’s dialectic into a call for revolutionary action. His 1842 essay *The Reaction in Germany*, published under a pseudonym by Arnold Ruge, marked his shift from philosophy to revolutionary rhetoric, urging the spread of the French Revolution to Russia.

### Revolutionary Apprenticeship and Imprisonment
Throughout the 1840s, Bakunin agitated across Europe. Defying a Tsarist order to return, he was stripped of noble rights and sentenced *in absentia* to Siberian penal labor. In Paris (1844), he met Marx and Proudhon, the latter deeply shaping his anti-state federalism. His 1847 speech supporting Polish independence against Tsarism made him famous across Europe and triggered his expulsion from France. During the 1848 revolutions, he fought in the Prague Slavic Congress uprising and the 1849 Dresden uprising. Captured, he was shuttled between Austrian and Russian prisons, enduring death sentences, solitary confinement in the Peter and Paul Fortress (1851–1854), and Shlisselburg Fortress (1854–1857). Prison hardened but did not break his resolve, though he wrote a controversial *Confession* to the Tsar seeking mercy—a document later used to question his revolutionary purity.

### Exile, Escape, and Anarchist Synthesis
Permitted to move to Siberian exile in 1857, Bakunin married Antonia Kwiatkowska. In 1861, he executed a dramatic escape: across the Amur River to Japan, by ship to San Francisco, across the Isthmus of Panama, to New York and Boston, finally reaching London. In the US, he sympathized with the Union in the Civil War but criticized Northern hypocrisy regarding the South, while admiring the US as "the finest political organization that ever existed." In London, he clashed with Herzen and Ogarev over their moderate reformism. After a failed attempt to aid the 1863 Polish January Uprising, he settled in Italy (1864–1867), where, in his early 50s, he synthesized his mature anarchist program: the abolition of the state and hierarchical institutions, replaced by a free federation of voluntary producer associations.

### The First International and the Marx-Bakunin Split
Bakunin joined the International Workingmen’s Association (First International) in 1868, rapidly expanding the anarchist faction’s influence, particularly in Spain and the Swiss Jura Federation. The conflict with Marx was both personal and structural. Bakunin viewed Marx as authoritarian and arrogant; Marx distrusted Bakunin’s "Russian reactionism" and alleged secret organizational maneuvers. Ideologically, the rupture was fundamental: Marx advocated a transitional "dictatorship of the proletariat" using the state apparatus to socialize production, believing the state would eventually wither away. Bakunin countered that *any* state—even a revolutionary one—concentrates power in a new ruling minority (a "red bureaucracy"), arguing "socialism without liberty is slavery and brutality." He championed spontaneous mass action and federalism over Marx’s centralized party politics.

At the 1872 Hague Congress, Marx engineered Bakunin’s expulsion in absentia (Bakunin could not attend), accusing him of maintaining a secret society within the International. The split destroyed the First International but formalized the historic divide between authoritarian (Marxist) and libertarian (anarchist) socialism. Bakunin founded the Anti-Authoritarian International the same year.

### Final Works and Actions
Bakunin’s major theoretical works—*The Knouto-Germanic Empire and the Social Revolution* (1871), *God and the State* (published posthumously 1882), and *Statism and Anarchy* (1873)—were fragmentary, prolific, and often unfinished. *God and the State*, his most famous text, attacks religion and the state as twin pillars of oppression, asserting that human freedom requires the destruction of both. *Statism and Anarchy*, written anonymously in Russian to spark revolt, identified the new German Empire as the citadel of reaction and warned that Marx’s "dictatorship" would be a dictatorship *over* the proletariat by autocrats. In 1870, he joined the abortive Lyon Commune; in 1874, he plotted the failed Bologna insurrection. Declining health forced his retirement to Switzerland, where he died in 1876.

### Core Political Thought: Authority, Federalism, and Revolution
**Anti-Statism and Federalism:** Bakunin defined the state as a "regulated system of domination and exploitation by a privileged, ruling class," applicable to monarchies and republics alike. He dismissed representative democracy as a paradox where politicians represent abstractions, not the people. His alternative was a "free federation" of communes and workplaces organized from the bottom up, where coordination occurs through mandated, recallable delegates—not representatives—with no central authority.

**Critique of Revolutionary Dictatorship:** Bakunin rejected the "dictatorship of the proletariat" as a contradiction in terms. He argued that placing revolutionaries in state positions corrupts them instantly; "even the best revolutionary placed on the Russian throne would become worse than Czar Alexander." He denied the state could "wither away," insisting it must be abolished *during* the revolution, not captured.

**Authority vs. Expertise:** Bakunin distinguished *authority* (coercive, hierarchical) from *expertise* (voluntary, consultative). One consults a bootmaker on boots or a scientist on physics freely, but neither may impose their will. Authority must be a "continual voluntary exchange," never constant subordination.

**The "Invisible Dictatorship":** Bakunin proposed a "collective dictatorship" or "invisible dictatorship"—a loose, informal international network of revolutionaries with no official power, ranks, or rewards. Their role was propaganda and organizing "natural influence" to help the masses self-organize and demolish the state. Critics (like historian Peter Marshall) argued this unseen, unaccountable vanguard risked greater tyranny than a visible party. Defenders (like Iain McKay, Andrew Flood) contend it was merely a metaphor for anarchist agitation under conditions of illegality, not a blueprint for secret government.

### Personal Life and Controversies
Bakunin’s marriage to Antonia Kwiatkowska (26 years his junior) in Siberia was distant; she bore three children by an Italian disciple during Bakunin’s lifetime. Biographers speculate on the psychosexual roots of his revolutionary intensity. His legacy is shadowed by antisemitic passages in his writings—particularly attacks on Marx invoking Jewish stereotypes and conspiracy theories (e.g., the Rothschilds). Scholars debate centrality: Mark Leier and Eirik Eiglad argue antisemitism was incidental to his core thought; Marcel Stoetzler argues the trope of Jewish world domination was central. Paul Avrich summarizes Bakunin’s paradox: "a nobleman who yearned for a peasant revolt, a libertarian with an urge to dominate others, an intellectual with a powerful anti-intellectual streak."

## Terms
- ****Anarchism (Bakuninist)**** — A revolutionary doctrine advocating the immediate abolition of the state, capitalism, and hierarchical religion, replaced by a voluntary federation of self-governing communes and worker associations organized from below.
- ****Collectivist Anarchism**** — Bakunin’s specific tendency: social ownership of the means of production, distribution according to labor contribution (not need, as in communist anarchism), organized through federalist worker communes.
- ****Federalism**** — The organizational principle of free association: local units (communes, workplaces) federate horizontally via mandated, recallable delegates for coordination, rejecting central sovereignty.
- ****First International (IWMA)**** — The International Workingmen’s Association (1864–1876), the first major international socialist body, split in 1872 between Marx’s state-socialist majority and Bakunin’s federalist anarchist minority.
- ****Dictatorship of the Proletariat**** — Marx’s concept of a transitional workers’ state seizing state power to suppress the bourgeoisie; Bakunin rejected this as a "red bureaucracy" that would rule *over* workers.
- ****Invisible / Collective Dictatorship**** — Bakunin’s term for an informal, secret international network of revolutionaries influencing the masses through propaganda and natural personal influence, holding no official power or hierarchy.
- ****God and the State**** — Bakunin’s most famous work (published 1882), arguing that religion and the state are inseparable instruments of oppression; human liberation requires abolishing both.
- ****Statism and Anarchy**** — Bakunin’s last major work (1873), written in Russian, analyzing the German Empire as the fortress of reaction and predicting Marxist dictatorship would become a new tyranny.
- ****Anti-Authoritarian International**** — The anarchist international founded by Bakunin in 1872 after expulsion from the First International, organized on federalist, anti-centralist principles.
- ****Young Hegelians**** — A radical German intellectual circle (1830s–40s) that inverted Hegel’s philosophy to critique religion and the Prussian state; Bakunin’s bridge from idealism to revolutionary politics.

## Debates and open questions
- **Antisemitism: Central or Peripheral?** Scholars dispute whether Bakunin’s antisemitic rhetoric (especially against Marx and "Jewish finance") was a regrettable prejudice common to his milieu or a structural component of his conspiracy-theoretic worldview.
- **"Invisible Dictatorship": Vanguardism or Agitation?** Was Bakunin’s "collective dictatorship" a dangerous elitist vanguard (Marshall) or a necessary metaphor for covert revolutionary networking under repression (McKay, Flood)?
- **Authoritarian Personality vs. Libertarian Theory:** Paul Avrich and others highlight the tension between Bakunin’s anti-authoritarian writings and his personal tendency to dominate disciples and create secret hierarchical societies.
- **Predictive Validity:** Noam Chomsky and others cite Bakunin’s forecast that Marxist "dictatorship of the proletariat" would become a one-party dictatorship *over* the proletariat as a rare successful prediction in social science; critics argue it conflates Leninism with Marx’s original intent.
- **Revolutionary Strategy: Spontaneity vs. Organization:** Bakunin oscillated between trusting spontaneous popular insurrection and building secret revolutionary societies; the unresolved tension informs debates on anarchist organization today.
