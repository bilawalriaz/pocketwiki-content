# Rice

## Overview
Rice (Oryza sativa and O. glaberrima) is a cereal grain that serves as the staple food for over half the world's population, particularly in Asia and Africa. Domesticated independently in China ~9,000 years ago and West Africa ~3,000 years ago, it is cultivated across diverse environments—from flooded lowlands to upland hills—and remains central to global food security, climate discussions, and cultural traditions. Modern rice agriculture faces challenges including yield losses from pests and diseases, post-harvest waste, and climate change impacts, driving advances in breeding, biotechnology, and sustainable farming.

## Timeline
- **~13,500–8,200 BCE** — Oryza sativa domesticated in China from wild O. rufipogon
- **~3,000 BCE** — Oryza glaberrima independently domesticated in West Africa
- **~6,000–5,600 BCE** — Rice introduced to northern China
- **~5,500–3,200 BCE** — Rice reaches Korea, Japan, and Taiwan
- **1966** — First Green Revolution variety IR8 developed at IRRI
- **1999** — World record rice yield set by Yuan Longping (17.1 t/ha)
- **2005** — Rice becomes first crop plant with fully sequenced genome
- **2013** — China becomes world’s largest rice importer
- **2022** — Rice cultivation emits 5.7 Gt CO₂eq (1.2% of global GHGs)

## Body

### Description and Biology
Rice plants grow 1–5 meters tall depending on water depth, completing their lifecycle from seed to harvest in about six months. The plant produces self-fertile flowers in panicles containing up to 350 spikelets, each with both male and female parts. As a member of the Poaceae family, rice belongs to the BOP clade and thrives in warm, water-rich environments, often grown as an annual but capable of perennial growth in tropical regions.

### Agronomy
Rice cultivation depends on biotic factors like variety and pests, and abiotic factors such as soil type, water availability, temperature, and sunlight. Two main systems dominate: lowland rice grown in flooded fields surrounded by bunds, and upland rice cultivated without flooding in hilly areas. Water-efficient methods like alternate wetting and drying reduce usage. Harvesting occurs when grain moisture reaches 20–25%, involving reaping, threshing, and sun-drying to prevent mold.

### Evolution and Domestication
Genetic evidence indicates a single domestication event for O. sativa in China, with identical nonshattering alleles in both indica and japonica subspecies. Indica rice likely emerged when japonica reached India ~4,500 years ago and hybridized with local wild or proto-domesticated types. Rice spread globally through migration and trade—reaching Southeast Asia via Austronesian expansion, and the Americas post-1492 through the Columbian Exchange. African rice (O. glaberrima) was separately domesticated and later introduced to the Americas by the Spanish.

### Production and Commerce
In 2023, global rice production reached 800 million tonnes, led by China and India (52% combined), with Asia accounting for 90%. Despite massive output, only ~8% is traded internationally. Yield averages 4.7 t/ha globally, though records exceed 17 t/ha using hybrid varieties and the System of Rice Intensification. Post-harvest losses remain high in developing nations—up to 25% in Nigeria—due to poor storage and transport infrastructure.

### Processing and Consumption
Milling removes the husk and bran layers, producing products from brown rice to polished white rice; parboiling enhances nutritional retention. Rice texture varies by starch composition: long-grain (indica) stays separate and fluffy; medium-grain (japonica/indica hybrids) is moist and sticky; short-grain (japonica) is highly sticky. Varieties like basmati and jasmine offer aromatic profiles, while glutinous rice is used in mochi and sushi. Nutritionally, cooked white rice provides 29% carbohydrates and 2% protein but lacks complete amino acids.

### Climate Change Impacts
Rice cultivation contributes 1.2% of global greenhouse gas emissions, primarily methane from flooded soils. Mitigation strategies include improved water management, reduced flooding, and genetic modifications like introducing the barley SUSIBA2 gene to cut emissions by up to 97%. Rising temperatures threaten yields—projected to decline 3.2% per °C increase—with flowering failure above 35°C. Drought and salinity also stress crops, prompting development of resilient varieties such as Sub1A flood-tolerant and DRO1 deep-rooted lines.

### Pests, Weeds, and Diseases
Yield losses stem from weeds, insects (armyworms, planthoppers, stem borers), rodents, birds, and diseases. Rice blast (Magnaporthe oryzae) and bacterial leaf streak (Xanthomonas oryzae) are the most damaging pathogens globally. Integrated pest management emphasizes biodiversity, host resistance, and reduced pesticide use—demonstrated by IRRI’s 1993 finding that 87.5% pesticide reduction lowered overall pest populations. Traditional polycultures like rice-duck farming provide natural control while adding economic value.

### Biotechnology and Breeding Advances
The Green Revolution introduced high-yielding semi-dwarf varieties like IR8, boosting productivity through fertilizer responsiveness. Modern efforts focus on C4 photosynthesis engineering, Golden Rice (biofortified with vitamin A), and stress-tolerant cultivars. Genetic tools have enabled flood tolerance (Sub1A), drought resistance (DRO1), salt tolerance (IR56 × O. coarctata hybrids), and cold adaptation. Rice also serves as a model organism for studying meiosis and DNA repair mechanisms.

### Human Culture
Rice holds deep cultural significance across religions and traditions. In Hindu weddings, rice symbolizes fertility and prosperity; in Japan and the Philippines, rice wine features in ceremonies. The goddess Dewi Sri represents rice in Indo-Malaysian mythology, and seasonal rituals like the Royal Ploughing Ceremony mark planting cycles in Nepal and Cambodia.

## Terms
- **Caryopsis**: A dry, one-seeded fruit typical of grasses where the seed coat is fused to the fruit wall.
- **Panicle**: A branched flower cluster; the structure bearing rice spikelets.
- **Spikelet**: The basic unit of the rice inflorescence, containing florets with stamens and pistils.
- **Alternate Wetting and Drying (AWD)**: An irrigation method cycling between flooding and draining fields to conserve water and reduce methane emissions.
- **System of Rice Intensification (SRI)**: A farming approach using younger seedlings, wider spacing, and organic amendments to boost yields.
- **PAMP-triggered immunity (PTI)**: Plant defense mechanism activated upon detection of pathogen-associated molecular patterns.
- **Effector-triggered immunity (ETI)**: Stronger immune response triggered when plant resistance proteins recognize specific pathogen effectors.
- **NERICA**: New Rice for Africa—high-yielding interspecific hybrids developed for Sub-Saharan Africa.
- **Glutinous rice**: Sticky, waxy rice variety (often O. sativa var. glutinosa) used in mochi and sushi.
- **Deepwater rice**: Flood-tolerant rice varieties adapted to standing water over 50 cm deep.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Whether indica rice originated from a distinct second domestication or hybridization between japonica and an unknown wild ancestor remains debated.
- Projections on future rice yields under climate change diverge—some models predict steady declines, others suggest initial increases before plateauing.
- The extent to which genetically modified rice varieties like Golden Rice will be adopted amid public skepticism and regulatory hurdles continues to evolve.
- Balancing intensive Green Revolution practices with agroecological approaches like rice-duck systems raises questions about scalability and sustainability.
- The role of rice as a model organism in plant biology research versus its practical agricultural applications presents ongoing tension in funding priorities.
