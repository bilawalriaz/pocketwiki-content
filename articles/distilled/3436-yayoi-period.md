# Yayoi period

## Overview
The Yayoi period (c. 950 BC – AD 300) marks Japan's transition from a hunter-gatherer society to a settled, agricultural one based on wet-rice cultivation. This era saw the introduction of new technologies, social stratification, and the formation of early political entities, laying the foundation for the subsequent Kofun period and the emergence of the Japanese state.

## Timeline
- **c. 950–300 BC** — Yayoi period begins with the introduction of wet-rice agriculture and new pottery styles.
- **c. 300 BC** — Traditional start date for the period based on archaeological evidence.
- **1st century AD** — Large regional powers emerge in western Japan; iron tools and weapons come into use.
- **AD 57** — The "Na state of Wo" receives a golden seal from the Chinese Emperor Guangwu of Han.
- **Late 2nd century** — The political entity known as Wa-koku (倭国) forms.
- **3rd century AD** — Chinese text *Wei Zhi* records the kingdom of Yamataikoku and its ruler, Queen Himiko.
- **Mid-3rd century AD** — Yayoi period transitions into the Kofun period, marked by the emergence of keyhole-shaped burial mounds.

## Body

### Cultural and Technological Shift
The Yayoi period is defined by the shift from the hunter-gatherer economy of the preceding Jōmon period to a productive economy based on irrigated, wet-rice agriculture. This new lifestyle, introduced via the Korean Peninsula and southern China, led to permanent settlements. Yayoi pottery was simpler and more utilitarian than Jōmon ware. Metallurgical techniques for bronze and iron were adopted, used for ceremonial objects (like *dōtaku* bells) and later for agricultural tools and weapons. Society became more stratified, with wealth accumulated through land and grain storage, leading to distinct social classes.

### Origins of the Yayoi People
The origin of Yayoi culture involves migration and assimilation. Archaeological evidence points to an influx of agricultural migrants from the Korean Peninsula and regions to the south, who brought new farming techniques and cultural traits. These migrants gradually mixed with the indigenous Jōmon population. Skeletal analysis shows Yayoi people were taller and had different facial features compared to the Jōmon. By the Kofun period, the Yayoi type was predominant in Japan. The degree of Yayoi influence varied regionally, with stronger Jōmon traits persisting in areas like Tōhoku.

### Political and Social Development
As Yayoi society grew more complex, conflicts between settlements increased, evidenced by moated villages and violent finds like headless skeletons. Chinese historical texts from the 1st to 3rd centuries AD provide the first written records of Japan, describing it as a land of hundreds of scattered tribal communities called "Wo." By the 3rd century, a more centralized political entity, Yamataikoku, emerged under the rule of the spiritual leader Queen Himiko. The location of Yamataikoku is debated, with Makimuku in Nara Prefecture being a leading candidate based on recent research.

## Terms
- **Wet-rice agriculture**: A method of farming rice in flooded paddies, which became the economic foundation of Yayoi society.
- **Jōmon period**: The preceding era in Japan characterized by a hunter-gatherer economy and distinctive cord-marked pottery.
- **Dōtaku**: Bronze ceremonial bells produced by Yayoi craft specialists.
- **Yayoi people**: A collective term for agricultural migrants from the continent and their descendants who assimilated with the Jōmon population.
- **Wa-koku (倭国)**: An early Chinese name for the political entity in Japan that formed by the late 2nd century.
- **Yamataikoku**: A 3rd-century kingdom in Japan, mentioned in Chinese texts, ruled by Queen Himiko.
- **Kofun period**: The era following the Yayoi, characterized by the construction of large keyhole-shaped burial mounds.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise start date of the Yayoi period is debated; while traditionally c. 300 BC, some radiocarbon evidence suggests a date up to 500 years earlier (c. 1000–800 BC).
- The exact location of Yamataikoku and the identity of Queen Himiko remain subjects of archaeological and historical research.
- Scholars debate whether the population increase from the Jōmon to Yayoi periods was primarily due to migration or the demographic shift enabled by agriculture.
- The linguistic origins of the Japonic language family and its relationship to Koreanic languages are complex, with theories involving migrations from the Korean Peninsula.
