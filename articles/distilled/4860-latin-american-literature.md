# Latin American literature

## Overview
Latin American literature encompasses the oral and written traditions of the region, primarily in Spanish, Portuguese, and indigenous languages. It gained global prominence in the 20th century, largely through the magical realism style of the "Boom" period, but its roots extend back centuries, reflecting a complex history of indigenous cultures, colonialism, independence movements, and modern political and social change.

## Timeline
- **Pre-Columbian** — Primarily oral traditions; Maya and Aztec codices; Inca quipu records.
- **1539** — First printing press in North America established in Mexico City.
- **1816** — Publication of *El Periquillo Sarniento*, considered the first Latin American novel.
- **1845** — Domingo Sarmiento's *Facundo* pioneers the "civilization or barbarism" theme.
- **1888** — Rubén Darío's *Azul* founds the Modernismo movement.
- **1929** — Rómulo Gallegos's *Doña Barbara* becomes a major criollismo novel.
- **1960-1967** — The Latin American Boom reaches its peak with seminal works published.
- **1967** — Gabriel García Márquez publishes *One Hundred Years of Solitude*; Miguel Ángel Asturias wins Nobel Prize.
- **1974** — Augusto Roa Bastos's *Yo, el supremo* is published, often seen as the Boom's culmination.
- **2000s** — Contemporary literature expands into diverse genres and global themes.

## Body

### Pre-Columbian and Colonial Foundations
Literature began as oral traditions, with the Maya and Aztecs producing codices and the Incas using quipu. After European contact, chroniclers like Columbus and Bernal Díaz del Castillo documented the conquest. A debate on colonial ethics emerged, exemplified by Bartolomé de las Casas. Indigenous and mestizo authors, such as El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, offered contrasting perspectives. The church dominated written culture, producing figures like Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz. By the late 18th century, a criollo (American-born Spanish) literary tradition began, with early novels and writers like Simón Bolívar contributing to independence movements.

### 19th Century: Nation-Building and Emerging Trends
Following independence, literature focused on forging national identities. This era produced "foundational fictions," novels in Romantic or Naturalist styles exploring themes like the conflict between civilization and barbarism (e.g., Sarmiento's *Facundo*). Movements evolved from Neoclassicism to Romanticism, Realism, and Naturalism. Women writers like Gertrudis Gómez de Avellaneda and Clorinda Matto de Turner emerged, critiquing social inequalities and patriarchal structures. The period concluded with the rise of Modernismo, a distinctly Latin American poetic movement.

### Modernismo and the Avant-Garde
Founded by Rubén Darío's *Azul* (1888), Modernismo was the first Latin American movement to achieve international influence, emphasizing aestheticism and pan-American connections. Figures like José Martí used it to critique U.S. imperialism. The early 20th century saw the rise of *indigenismo*, representing indigenous cultures and injustices. Avant-garde movements like Stridentism emerged in Mexico. Jorge Luis Borges pioneered the philosophical short story, while Rómulo Gallegos's *Doña Barbara* (1929) became a landmark of criollismo. In the 1930s-40s, writers like Arturo Uslar Pietri and Alejo Carpentier developed the concepts that would underpin magical realism.

### The Latin American Boom
From roughly 1960 to 1967, a generation of novelists gained international fame. They experimented with narrative structure, language, and style, influenced by modernist writers like Faulkner and Joyce. The Boom was defined by works like Julio Cortázar's *Rayuela* (1963) and, most famously, Gabriel García Márquez's *One Hundred Years of Solitude* (1967), which cemented the association of Latin American literature with magical realism. The movement launched the region's literature onto the world stage, though its works often focused on universal themes over local social issues.

### Post-Boom and Contemporary Literature
After the Boom, writers reacted against its perceived elitism and magical realist stereotype. The Post-Boom is characterized by irony, humor, and the use of popular genres. Chilean Alberto Fuguet coined "McOndo" as an antidote to "Macondo-ism." Contemporary literature is vibrant and diverse, spanning genres from crime fiction to speculative fiction. Authors like Roberto Bolaño, Isabel Allende, and Giannina Braschi have gained global readership. The testimonio genre, collaborating with subaltern subjects, and a new tradition of literary chronicle have also flourished.

## Terms
- **Magical realism**: A literary style, prominent in the Boom, that incorporates fantastical or mythical elements into otherwise realistic narratives.
- **Latin American Boom**: The rapid rise and international popularity of Latin American literature in the 1960s and 1970s.
- **Modernismo**: A late 19th-century poetic movement founded by Rubén Darío, emphasizing aesthetic refinement and pan-American identity.
- **Criollismo**: A literary trend focusing on the unique landscapes, characters, and conflicts of Latin America, often exploring the civilization vs. barbarism dichotomy.
- **Indigenismo**: A literary and social movement dedicated to representing indigenous cultures and advocating for their rights.
- **Foundational fictions**: Novels from the 19th century that helped shape national identities after independence.
- **Testimonio**: A genre of non-fiction narrative, often collaborative, that gives voice to marginalized individuals and communities.
- **McOndo**: A literary movement coined as a reaction against the magical realist "Macondo" stereotype, favoring urban, globalized settings.

## Debates and Open questions
- The source notes that Latin American literature is often **solely associated with magical realism and the Boom**, a reductive view that many Post-Boom writers have denounced.
- There is an ongoing scholarly discussion about the **Boom's focus on universal/metaphysical themes versus local social and political issues**.
- The role and recognition of **women writers** throughout history is a point of analysis, with their contributions often emerging in contrast to male-dominated traditions.
- The **definition and boundaries of the Post-Boom** are fluid, with various reactions and new movements (like McOndo) emerging.
