# Anatolia

## Overview
Anatolia, also known as Asia Minor, is a peninsula in West Asia forming the majority of modern Turkey. It is a critical historical crossroads, serving as a bridge between Europe and Asia and a cradle for early farming, diverse ancient civilizations, and the transition from Greek to Turkish cultural and linguistic dominance.

## Timeline
- **c. 9,000 years ago** — Major migration of Anatolian Neolithic farmers into Europe begins.
- **c. 2000 BC** — Earliest recorded history via Assyrian trade colony tablets at Kültepe.
- **c. 1700–1200 BC** — Hittite Kingdom dominates central Anatolia.
- **12th–6th centuries BCE** — Post-Hittite era sees rise of Phrygia, Lydia, and Greek coastal settlements.
- **7th century BCE** — Minted coinage invented in Lydia.
- **6th century BCE** — Conquered by the Persian Achaemenid Empire.
- **334 BCE** — Alexander the Great conquers Anatolia, initiating the Hellenistic period.
- **133 BCE** — Kingdom of Pergamum bequeathed to the Roman Republic.
- **1071 CE** — Battle of Manzikert; Seljuk Turks begin major migration into Anatolia.
- **1320s** — Ottoman Turks mint coins in their own name, signaling independence.
- **1517** — Ottomans complete conquest of the peninsula.
- **1894–1924** — Millions of non-Turkic Christians (Greeks, Armenians, Assyrians) are killed or expelled.

## Body

### Geography and Etymology
Anatolia is a peninsula bounded by the Mediterranean, Aegean, and Black Seas. Its traditional eastern limit is an indefinite line from the Gulf of Alexandretta to the Black Sea, corresponding to the Anatolian Plateau. The name derives from the Greek *Anatolḗ*, meaning "the East." The term "Asia Minor" was used in Late Antiquity to distinguish it from the broader "Asia." Following the Armenian genocide, the Turkish government renamed the historical Armenian Highlands as "Eastern Anatolia," a term some scholars consider an "ahistorical imposition."

### Prehistoric and Ancient Peoples
Human habitation dates to the Paleolithic. Anatolia was an early center for farming, with major Neolithic settlements like Çatalhöyük. The earliest recorded inhabitants were the non-Indo-European Hattians and Hurrians. They were gradually absorbed by Indo-European peoples who spoke Anatolian languages (e.g., Hittite, Luwian). After the Hittite collapse, Anatolia was home to Phrygians, Lydians, and others. Greek settlements, grouped as Aeolis, Ionia, and Doris, emerged on the western coast from c. 1200–1000 BCE onward.

### Classical and Hellenistic Periods
In the 6th century BCE, all of Anatolia was conquered by the Persian Achaemenid Empire. The Ionian Revolt (499 BCE) against Persian rule sparked the Greco-Persian Wars. Alexander the Great's conquest in 334 BCE began a period of Hellenization, during which local Anatolian languages were largely supplanted by Greek. After Alexander's death, Hellenistic kingdoms like the Attalids and Seleucids ruled. The region was gradually absorbed by the Roman Republic, culminating in full control by the 1st century BCE.

### Byzantine and Seljuk Periods
Anatolia became the core of the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire, a wealthy, Christian, and Greek-speaking heartland. Its urbanism declined from the 5th century, a shift debated among historians. The Battle of Manzikert (1071) enabled Seljuk Turk migration, introducing the Turkish language and Islam. This began the region's slow transition from Christian/Greek to Muslim/Turkish. Control was later split between the Byzantines and the Seljuk Sultanate of Rûm.

### Ottoman and Modern Era
The Ottoman Turks emerged as a dominant power in the 14th century, absorbing the Anatolian beyliks (principalities) and completing the conquest by 1517. The empire's 19th-century decline led to the resettlement of Muslim populations from the Balkans and Caucasus into Anatolia. The early 20th century saw the Armenian, Greek, and Assyrian genocides, which removed most ancient Christian communities. The 1923 population exchange between Greece and Turkey finalized this demographic transformation, making Anatolia predominantly Turkish and Muslim.

## Terms
- **Anatolian languages**: An extinct branch of Indo-European languages (e.g., Hittite, Luwian) once spoken in Anatolia.
- **Hellenization**: The spread of ancient Greek culture, language, and identity.
- **Seljuk Turks**: A medieval Turkic dynasty that introduced Islam and the Turkish language to Anatolia after 1071.
- **Beyliks**: Anatolian Turkish principalities that emerged after the decline of Seljuk authority.
- **Ottoman Empire**: The Turkic empire that ruled Anatolia and southeastern Europe from the 14th to early 20th century.
- **Armenian Highlands**: A mountainous region in eastern Anatolia, historically inhabited by Armenians, renamed "Eastern Anatolia" by Turkey.
- **Hattians**: The earliest historically attested, non-Indo-European people of central Anatolia.
- **Ionian Revolt**: A rebellion of Greek city-states in Anatolia against Persian rule (499 BCE), which triggered the Greco-Persian Wars.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The cause of urban decline in Byzantine Anatolia between the 6th and 7th centuries is debated, with theories including the Plague of Justinian, the Byzantine–Sasanian War, and Arab invasions.
- The use of the term "Eastern Anatolia" for the historical Armenian Highlands is contested by scholars as an "ahistorical imposition" that obscures the region's Armenian past.
- The origin of the Indo-European language family is debated, with some proposals placing it in Anatolia, though linguists often favor a later origin in the Pontic-Caspian steppe.
