# Charles I of England

## Overview
Charles I was King of England, Scotland, and Ireland from 1625 until his execution in 1649. His reign was defined by a fundamental conflict with Parliament over royal authority, religion, and taxation, which escalated into the English Civil War and culminated in his unprecedented trial and death. His actions and beliefs, particularly his advocacy for the divine right of kings, directly led to the temporary abolition of the monarchy and the establishment of a republic.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Charles was the second son of King James VI of Scotland, who became James I of England in 1603. Charles was a sickly child who remained in Scotland until 1604. He became heir apparent in 1612 after his elder brother Henry's death. As Prince of Wales, he and the Duke of Buckingham undertook a failed secret trip to Spain in 1623 to arrange a marriage to the Infanta Maria Anna, a diplomatic humiliation that turned public opinion against Spain. He succeeded his father in 1625 and married the Catholic Henrietta Maria of France, deepening Protestant suspicions.

### Conflict with Parliament and Personal Rule
Charles's reign was immediately marked by quarrels with Parliament over taxation (especially tonnage and poundage) and his support for the unpopular Duke of Buckingham. Parliament's Petition of Right (1628) sought to limit his prerogative, but Charles dissolved it in 1629 and ruled without Parliament for 11 years, a period known as the Personal Rule. To raise revenue, he revived feudal dues like ship money, which was levied nationally and in peacetime, causing widespread resentment. His religious policies, led by Archbishop William Laud, promoted high Anglican practices and persecuted Puritans, further alienating his subjects.

### Religious Conflict and the Bishops' Wars
Charles's attempt to impose a new Anglican-style prayer book on Presbyterian Scotland in 1637 provoked the National Covenant and rebellion. The resulting Bishops' Wars (1639-40) were a military and financial disaster for Charles. To fund a new campaign, he was forced to recall Parliament in 1640, first the Short Parliament (which he dissolved after a month) and then the Long Parliament, which was dominated by his opponents.

### Civil War and Execution
The Long Parliament impeached Charles's chief minister, Strafford, and passed laws curbing royal power. In January 1642, Charles's failed attempt to arrest five MPs in the House of Commons destroyed his remaining authority and forced him to flee London. This led to the English Civil War. After initial stalemates, the Parliamentarian New Model Army, under Oliver Cromwell, won a decisive victory at Naseby in 1645. Charles surrendered to the Scots in 1646 and was later handed over to Parliament. He secretly allied with the Scots, triggering a Second Civil War, but was defeated and captured. Tried for treason by a specially created court, he refused to recognize its authority, insisting on his divine right. He was found guilty and beheaded on 30 January 1649.

## Terms
*   **Divine Right of Kings:** The doctrine that a monarch's authority comes directly from God and is not subject to earthly powers like Parliament.
*   **Petition of Right (1628):** A parliamentary declaration that the king could not levy taxes without Parliament's consent, imprison subjects without cause, or quarter soldiers in private homes.
*   **Personal Rule (1629-1640):** The 11-year period when Charles I governed without summoning Parliament, relying on prerogative taxes and other revenues.
*   **Ship Money:** A traditional tax levied on coastal towns for naval defense, which Charles extended to the entire country in peacetime, provoking legal challenges.
*   **Arminianism:** A theological movement within the Church of England that emphasized clerical authority and free will in salvation, viewed by Puritans as crypto-Catholic.
*   **Long Parliament:** The Parliament that assembled in November 1640 and sat, with interruptions, until 1660; it was dominated by opposition to Charles I.
*   **New Model Army:** The professional, centrally organized Parliamentarian army created in 1645, which proved decisive in defeating the royalists.
*   **Rump Parliament:** The remnant of the Long Parliament after Pride's Purge (December 1648), which tried and executed Charles I.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   Historians debate Charles's competence, with some viewing him as an intransigent tyrant and others as a well-intentioned but politically inept ruler.
*   The extent to which Charles's religious policies (Arminianism) were a deliberate plot to restore Catholicism is contested.
*   The causes of the English Civil War are complex, involving long-term constitutional, religious, and economic tensions, not just Charles's personality.
*   The legality of Charles's trial and execution remains a point of historical and legal debate, as it challenged the established concept of sovereign immunity.
