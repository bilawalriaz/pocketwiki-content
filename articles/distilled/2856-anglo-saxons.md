# Anglo-Saxons

## Overview
The Anglo-Saxons were a cultural group of Germanic settlers who, from the 5th century, dominated what is now England and southeastern Scotland. Their period, from c. 450 to 1066, saw the development of a distinct "Englisc" identity, the foundation of the English language, and the political structures that preceded the medieval Kingdom of England. Their legacy is profound, shaping the language, law, and culture of modern Britain.

## Timeline
- **c. 430–450** — Traditional date for the arrival of Germanic settlers (Angles, Saxons, Jutes) as *foederati* invited by a British ruler.
- **597** — Augustine of Canterbury begins the Gregorian mission, converting King Æthelberht of Kent to Christianity.
- **664** — Synod of Whitby establishes Roman ecclesiastical practice over Irish tradition in Northumbria.
- **793** — Viking raid on Lindisfarne marks the beginning of major Scandinavian attacks.
- **878** — Alfred the Great defeats the Vikings at Edington, leading to the Treaty of Wedmore and the establishment of the Danelaw.
- **899** — Death of Alfred the Great; his legal and educational reforms lay the groundwork for a unified English kingdom.
- **927** — Æthelstan is recognized as the first king of all England.
- **973** — Edgar the Peaceful receives a second, "imperial" coronation, symbolizing a unified and reformed English church and state.
- **1016** — Cnut the Great conquers England, establishing a North Sea empire.
- **1066** — Norman Conquest; Harold II is killed at the Battle of Hastings, ending Anglo-Saxon rule.

## Body

### Origins and Settlement
The Anglo-Saxon period began with the migration of Germanic tribes from northern Europe (modern Germany, Denmark, the Netherlands) to Britain following the decline of Roman authority. Early Roman and British sources describe these groups as "Saxons," a term for coastal raiders. The 8th-century scholar Bede later distinguished three main groups: Angles, Saxons, and Jutes. The exact nature of the settlement—whether a mass invasion or a gradual migration—is debated, but archaeological and genetic evidence indicates large-scale immigration, particularly into eastern England, which mixed with the existing Romano-British population. By the 7th century, this interaction had fostered a new cultural identity known as "Englisc."

### Christianity and Early Kingdoms
The conversion to Christianity, beginning with Augustine's mission to Kent in 597, was a transformative process. It introduced literacy, Latin learning, and continental influences, leading to a "Golden Age" of monasticism and scholarship in the 7th and 8th centuries. Politically, the early period saw numerous small kingdoms competing for dominance. The traditional "Heptarchy" (seven kingdoms) is a simplification; in reality, power shifted among kingdoms like Kent, East Anglia, Northumbria, and Mercia. A powerful king could achieve overlordship, recognized as a *bretwalda* (ruler of Britain), but this status was often short-lived.

### Viking Invasions and the Rise of Wessex
From 793, Viking raids escalated into full-scale invasions, culminating in the "Great Army" of 865 which conquered Northumbria, East Anglia, and much of Mercia. The kingdom of Wessex, under Alfred the Great, became the last English stronghold. After a decisive victory in 878, Alfred reorganized the military, built a network of fortified towns (*burhs*), and promoted a revival of learning. His successors, particularly Æthelstan and Edgar, gradually reconquered the Danelaw, unifying England under a single monarchy and a reformed church by the late 10th century.

### Late Anglo-Saxon England and the Norman Conquest
The 11th century was marked by renewed Viking pressure and internal strife. King Æthelred the Unready's reign saw devastating raids and the payment of *Danegeld* (tribute). In 1016, the Danish king Cnut conquered England, ruling effectively but leaving Anglo-Saxon institutions largely intact. After his dynasty ended, Edward the Confessor's reign was dominated by the rivalry between the houses of Godwine and Leofric. Edward's death in 1066 triggered a succession crisis, leading to the Norman invasion by William, Duke of Normandy, and the defeat of the last Anglo-Saxon king, Harold II, at Hastings.

### Society and Culture
Anglo-Saxon society was hierarchical, centered on kinship and the bond between a lord and his warriors (*comitatus*). The main social ranks were the king, nobles (*thegns*), and freemen (*ceorls*), with slaves at the bottom. Law was based on custom, with compensation for crimes (*wergild*) and a system of local courts. Culture was expressed through intricate metalwork, manuscript illumination, and a rich vernacular literature, including epic poetry like *Beowulf*. The Old English language, a West Germanic tongue, is the direct ancestor of modern English.

## Terms
- **Bretwalda**: An Old English term for an overlord king recognized as having supremacy over other Anglo-Saxon kingdoms.
- **Burh**: A fortified settlement or fortress, part of a defensive network built by Alfred the Great and his successors.
- **Ceorl**: A free peasant or commoner in Anglo-Saxon society, the lowest rank of free men.
- **Danegeld**: A tax levied to raise money for tribute payments to Viking raiders.
- **Danelaw**: The area of northern and eastern England where Danish law and settlement prevailed after the Viking invasions.
- **Heptarchy**: A traditional term for the seven main Anglo-Saxon kingdoms (Kent, Sussex, Wessex, Essex, East Anglia, Mercia, Northumbria), though more than seven existed.
- **Thegn**: A noble or lord, a member of the king's retinue who held land in exchange for military service.
- **Weregild**: The monetary compensation paid to the family of a person who was killed or injured, determined by the victim's social status.
- **Witan**: The council of advisors to an Anglo-Saxon king, comprising nobles and churchmen.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nature of Settlement**: Historians debate whether the Anglo-Saxon takeover was a violent invasion by Germanic tribes or a more gradual process of cultural assimilation and elite replacement.
- **Continuity with Roman Britain**: The degree to which Romano-British culture, institutions, and population survived and influenced the emerging Anglo-Saxon society is contested.
- **Role of Women**: While evidence shows women could own property and wield influence (especially as abbesses), the extent of their legal and social autonomy compared to later periods is debated.
- **The "Heptarchy" Model**: The traditional concept of seven stable kingdoms is considered an oversimplification; scholars now emphasize fluid political boundaries and shifting overlordships.
