# Ancient Roman architecture

## Overview
Ancient Roman architecture, flourishing from the Republic through the Empire, adapted Greek classical forms into a new, innovative style defined by engineering prowess. Its legacy lies in the revolutionary use of concrete, arches, and domes to create monumental public works and infrastructure, establishing a design vocabulary that has influenced Western architecture for millennia.

## Timeline
- **c. 509 BC** — Establishment of the Roman Republic; early architecture heavily influenced by Etruscans and Greeks.
- **146 BCE** — Sack of Corinth; Greek artworks and models transferred to Rome, intensifying Greek influence.
- **c. 273 BC** — First known use of concrete by Romans at Cosa.
- **27 BC–14 AD** — Reign of Augustus; major building boom, use of marble facing, and development of Imperial fora.
- **c. 40 BC–230 AD** — Peak period of Roman architectural achievement.
- **1st century AD** — Widespread use of arches springing from columns; construction of the Colosseum and Pantheon.
- **104–109 AD** — Construction and dedication of the Baths of Trajan.
- **122 AD** — Construction of Hadrian's Wall begins.
- **203–205 AD** — Arch of Septimius Severus erected in Rome.
- **312 AD** — Arch of Constantine built; last great triumphal arch in Rome.
- **4th century AD** — Period ends; architecture reclassified as Late Antique or Byzantine.

## Body

### Origins and the Architectural Revolution
Roman architecture began by borrowing from Etruscan and Greek models, with Greek influence intensifying after the conquest of Greece in 146 BCE. The pivotal shift, known as the "Roman architectural revolution" or "concrete revolution," occurred with the widespread use of Roman concrete (*opus caementicium*). This material, a mix of lime mortar, aggregate, and pozzolana ash, freed builders from the constraints of stone and brick, enabling the bold use of arches, vaults, and domes. This innovation allowed for the creation of vast, unsupported interior spaces and monumental structures like aqueducts, amphitheatres, and basilicas.

### Key Innovations and Materials
The Romans perfected fired clay bricks and, most importantly, concrete, which became the primary building material. Concrete's flexibility and strength allowed for daring designs, such as the dome of the Pantheon, whose size remained unsurpassed until the 19th century. They also developed new architectural orders: the Tuscan (a simplified Doric) and the Composite (combining Ionic scrolls with Corinthian foliage). While they adopted the classical orders from Greece, these columns became largely decorative elements on massive concrete walls rather than primary structural supports.

### Major Building Types
Roman architecture produced several distinct building types. The **amphitheatre** (e.g., the Colosseum) was a new form for public spectacles. The **basilica** was a large public hall for legal and business transactions, later adopted for Christian churches. **Thermae** (public baths) were elaborate complexes for bathing and socializing, featuring advanced engineering like the hypocaust underfloor heating system. **Temples** followed a raised podium model with a pronounced front portico, often using the Corinthian order. **Insulae** were multi-story apartment blocks housing the urban population, while the elite lived in **domus** (townhouses) or **villas** (country estates).

### Infrastructure and Engineering
The Romans were master civil engineers. They constructed an extensive network of **roads** for military and commercial use. **Aqueducts** used gravity to transport water over long distances into cities, supplying baths, fountains, and private homes. They built durable **bridges** with stone arches and were the first to use concrete in their construction. **Defensive walls** fortified cities, with notable examples like the Aurelian Walls in Rome and frontier walls like Hadrian's Wall in Britain.

### Decorative and Commemorative Structures
Triumphal **arches** were free-standing monuments commemorating military victories, decorated with sculptures and inscriptions. **Victory columns**, like Trajan's Column, were monolithic structures with spiral reliefs. **Mosaics**, made from small stone or glass tesserae, decorated floors and walls with geometric and pictorial designs. **Roman gardens** were influenced by Greek and Persian styles and were common in both public and private spaces.

## Terms
- **Opus Caementicium:** Roman concrete, a mixture of lime mortar, aggregate, and pozzolana ash, which was stronger than previous concretes and enabled revolutionary construction.
- **Hypocaust:** An ancient Roman underfloor heating system where hot air from a furnace circulated through spaces under a raised floor and within wall cavities.
- **Classical Orders:** Systems of column and entablature proportions (Doric, Ionic, Corinthian, Tuscan, Composite) originally from Greek architecture, used decoratively by Romans.
- **Cella:** The inner chamber of a Roman temple, which housed the cult statue of the deity.
- **Insula:** A multi-story apartment block in ancient Roman cities, housing the majority of the urban population.
- **Thermae:** A large public bathhouse complex in a Roman city, used for bathing, exercise, and socializing.
- **Basilica:** A large public building used for legal and business proceedings, characterized by a central nave and aisles, later adapted for Christian churches.
- **Amphitheatre:** An oval or circular open-air venue with tiered seating, used for gladiatorial contests and public spectacles.
- **Tessera:** A small block of stone, glass, or ceramic used to create mosaics.
- **Vomitoria:** Passageways in Roman amphitheatres and theatres that allowed large crowds to enter and exit quickly.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the idealized proportions described by the architect Vitruvius do not always reflect actual Roman practice, which was variable. There is also scholarly debate about the precise meaning and social implications of the word *insula*, which referred to both apartment buildings and the city blocks they occupied. The origins of the triumphal arch form are described as unclear, with precursors in Etruscan gates and Greek architectural elements.
