# Lipid

## Overview
Lipids are a diverse group of hydrophobic or amphiphilic small organic molecules—including fats, waxes, sterols, phospholipids, and fat-soluble vitamins—that serve as the primary structural components of cell membranes, dense energy stores, and vital signaling messengers. They are biochemically defined by their origin from two distinct building blocks: ketoacyl groups (yielding fatty acyls, glycerolipids, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, saccharolipids, and polyketides) and isoprene groups (yielding sterol lipids and prenol lipids). Because humans cannot synthesize certain essential fatty acids (linoleic and α-linolenic acid) or the isoprene-based fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), these must be obtained from the diet. Lipid metabolism balances synthesis (lipogenesis) and degradation (beta-oxidation) to maintain energy homeostasis and membrane integrity.

## Timeline
- **1815** — Henri Braconnot classifies lipids (graisses) into suifs (solid greases/tallow) and huiles (fluid oils).
- **1823** — Michel Eugène Chevreul develops a detailed classification including oils, greases, tallow, waxes, resins, balsams, and volatile oils.
- **1827** — William Prout recognizes fat ("oily" alimentary matters) as a key nutrient alongside protein and carbohydrate.
- **1844** — Théophile-Jules Pelouze synthesizes the first synthetic triglyceride (tributyrin).
- **1847** — Theodore Gobley discovers phospholipids (lecithins) in mammalian brain and hen egg.
- **1912** — Rosenbloom and Gies propose replacing "lipoid" with "lipin".
- **1920** — Bloor introduces classification: simple, compound, and derived lipoids.
- **1923** — Gabriel Bertrand coins "lipide" (from Greek *lipos*, fat); approved by Société de Chimie Biologique; later anglicized to "lipid".
- **1947** — T. P. Hilditch defines "simple lipids" as greases and waxes (true waxes, sterols, alcohols).

## Body

### Chemical Classification and Building Blocks
Lipids are broadly defined by physical solubility (hydrophobic or amphiphilic) rather than a single chemical structure. All biological lipids originate from two biochemical subunits: **ketoacyl** groups (via condensation of acetyl-CoA/malonyl-CoA) and **isoprene** groups (via condensation of isopentenyl diphosphate/dimethylallyl diphosphate). The Lipid MAPS consortium organizes lipids into eight categories: six derived from ketoacyl subunits (fatty acyls, glycerolipids, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, saccharolipids, polyketides) and two from isoprene subunits (sterol lipids, prenol lipids). **Fatty acyls** (fatty acids and derivatives) feature a hydrocarbon chain with a terminal carboxylic acid, giving a polar hydrophilic head and nonpolar hydrophobic tail; chain length (typically C4–C24), saturation, and *cis/trans* geometry dictate membrane fluidity and function. **Glycerolipids** are mono-, di-, or tri-substituted glycerols; **triglycerides** (triacylglycerols) are the main energy-storage form. **Glycerophospholipids** (phospholipids) possess a glycerol backbone, two fatty acyl "tails," and a polar phosphate "headgroup" (e.g., phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine), making them the primary structural lipids of bilayers. **Sphingolipids** share a sphingoid base (sphingosine in mammals) synthesized from serine and fatty acyl-CoA; they include ceramides, sphingomyelins, and glycosphingolipids (cerebrosides, gangliosides). **Sterol lipids** (e.g., cholesterol, ergosterol, phytosterols) are steroids with a hydroxyl at C-3 and a fused four-ring core; they modulate membrane fluidity and serve as hormone precursors. **Prenol lipids** (isoprenoids) are built from C5 isoprene units; they include carotenoids, quinones (ubiquinones, vitamin E, vitamin K), and polyprenols (dolichols in animals, bactoprenols in prokaryotes). **Saccharolipids** replace glycerol with a sugar backbone (e.g., acylated glucosamine in Lipid A of Gram-negative bacterial lipopolysaccharide). **Polyketides** are polymerized acetyl/propionyl subunits yielding diverse secondary metabolites (e.g., erythromycin, tetracycline, epothilones).

### Biological Membranes
Eukaryotic cells use lipid bilayers to compartmentalize organelles. **Glycerophospholipids** are the main structural component; their amphipathic nature drives spontaneous bilayer formation in water via the **hydrophobic effect**: polar heads face the aqueous phase, hydrophobic tails cluster away from water, forming vesicles, micelles, or planar bilayers. Animal plasma membranes also contain **sphingomyelin** and **cholesterol**; plant/algal chloroplast membranes are rich in non-phosphorus galactolipids (monogalactosyldiacylglycerol, sulfoquinovosyldiacylglycerol). Despite unusual compositions, thylakoid membranes maintain a dynamic bilayer. **Cardiolipins** (four acyl chains, three glycerols) are concentrated in the inner mitochondrial membrane and activate oxidative phosphorylation enzymes. The self-assembly of lipids into protocell membranes is a key step in abiogenesis models.

### Energy Storage
**Triglycerides** stored in adipose tissue are the principal energy reserve in animals and plants. Complete oxidation yields ~38 kJ/g (9 kcal/g), more than double carbohydrates or proteins (17 kJ/g). Adipocytes continuously synthesize and hydrolyze triglycerides; hormone-sensitive lipase controls breakdown. Migratory birds rely on triglycerides for sustained flight.

### Signaling
Lipid signaling operates through G protein-coupled receptors, nuclear receptors, and direct protein interactions. Key messengers: **sphingosine-1-phosphate** (from ceramide) regulates calcium, growth, apoptosis; **diacylglycerol** and **phosphatidylinositol phosphates (PIPs)** activate protein kinase C; **prostaglandins** (eicosanoids from arachidonic/eicosapentaenoic acid) mediate inflammation/immunity; **steroid hormones** (estrogen, testosterone, cortisol) govern reproduction, metabolism, blood pressure; **oxysterols** (e.g., 25-hydroxy-cholesterol) activate liver X receptors. **Phosphatidylserine** externalization (via flippase inactivation/scramblase activation) flags apoptotic cells for phagocytosis.

### Other Functions
**Fat-soluble vitamins** (A, D, E, K) are isoprene-based lipids stored in liver/fatty tissues with diverse roles (vision, calcium homeostasis, antioxidant, coagulation). **Acyl-carnitines** shuttle fatty acids into mitochondria for beta-oxidation. **Polyprenol phosphates** transport oligosaccharides across membranes for glycosylation (peptidoglycan synthesis in bacteria, N-glycosylation in eukaryotes). Lipids also form the scaffold for **steroid hormones**.

### Metabolism
#### Biosynthesis
Excess dietary carbohydrate → acetyl-CoA → fatty acids via **fatty acid synthases** (single multifunctional protein in animals/fungi; separate enzymes in plants/bacteria). Cycles of condensation, reduction, dehydration, reduction extend acyl chains. **Desaturation** introduces double bonds (e.g., stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 makes oleic acid). Mammals cannot synthesize linoleic (18:2 ω-6) or α-linolenic (18:3 ω-3) acids → **essential fatty acids**. Triglyceride assembly occurs in the endoplasmic reticulum (glycerol-3-phosphate pathway). **Isoprenoids/sterols**: mevalonate pathway (animals/archaea) or non-mevalonate pathway (plants/bacteria) produce C5 donors → squalene → lanosterol → cholesterol/ergosterol.

#### Degradation
**Beta-oxidation** in mitochondria/peroxisomes sequentially removes two-carbon units (acetyl-CoA) from the carboxyl end via dehydrogenation, hydration, oxidation, thiolysis. Acetyl-CoA enters the citric acid cycle and electron transport chain; palmitate yields ~106 ATP. Unsaturated and odd-chain fatty acids require extra enzymes. When glucose is scarce, fat-derived acetyl-CoA fuels the citric acid cycle.

### Nutrition and Health
Dietary fat (triglycerides, cholesterol, phospholipids) enables absorption of fat-soluble vitamins and carotenoids. **Essential fatty acids**: linoleic acid (ω-6, abundant in safflower, sunflower, corn oils) and α-linolenic acid (ω-3, in flax, rapeseed, walnut, soy, green leaves). Fish oils provide long-chain ω-3 (EPA, DHA); studies link ω-3 intake to benefits in infant development, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and mental health (depression, ADHD, dementia). **Trans fats** (from partial hydrogenation or overheating) increase cardiovascular risk. Large trials (Women’s Health Initiative, Nurses’ Health Study, Health Professionals Follow-up) found no consistent link between total fat percentage and obesity, diabetes, cancer, or heart disease; current consensus (Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health) holds that total dietary fat amount is not strongly linked to weight or disease.

## Terms
- ****Amphiphilic**** — Molecule possessing both hydrophobic (water-repelling) and hydrophilic (water-attracting) regions, enabling self-assembly into bilayers, micelles, or vesicles in aqueous environments.
- ****Beta-oxidation**** — Catabolic pathway in mitochondria/peroxisomes that sequentially cleaves two-carbon acetyl-CoA units from fatty acyl-CoA, generating energy via the citric acid cycle.
- ****Cis/trans isomerism**** — Geometric configuration around a carbon–carbon double bond; *cis* bends the chain (increasing membrane fluidity), *trans* keeps it straighter (more solid at room temperature).
- ****Essential fatty acids**** — Polyunsaturated fatty acids (linoleic acid ω-6, α-linolenic acid ω-3) that mammals cannot synthesize de novo and must obtain from diet.
- ****Glycerophospholipid**** — Lipid with a glycerol backbone, two ester-linked fatty acyl chains, and a phosphate-linked polar headgroup; primary structural component of cellular lipid bilayers.
- ****Hydrophobic effect**** — Thermodynamic drive causing nonpolar molecules/regions to aggregate in water, minimizing ordered water "cages"; basis of lipid bilayer self-assembly.
- ****Isoprene unit**** — Five-carbon building block (C₅H₈) derived from isopentenyl/dimethylallyl diphosphate; polymerized to form prenol lipids (terpenes, sterols, vitamins E/K).
- ****Lipogenesis**** — Metabolic synthesis of fatty acids from acetyl-CoA and their esterification into triglycerides, stimulated by excess dietary carbohydrate.
- ****Sphingolipid**** — Lipid class built on a sphingoid base (e.g., sphingosine) from serine + fatty acyl-CoA; includes ceramides, sphingomyelins, glycosphingolipids; roles in membrane structure and signaling.
- ****Triglyceride (triacylglycerol)**** — Glycerol esterified to three fatty acids; main form of stored energy in adipose tissue; hydrolyzed to glycerol and free fatty acids for fuel.

## Debates and open questions
- **Dietary fat quantity vs. quality**: While trans fats are clearly harmful and ω-3 fats beneficial, large epidemiological trials have failed to link total fat percentage to obesity, diabetes, cancer, or heart disease, leaving optimal macronutrient distribution unresolved.
- **Lipid variability and disease**: Long-term fluctuations in LDL and triglycerides correlate with cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and heart failure, but whether variability is a causal risk factor or a marker of metabolic instability remains unclear.
- **Essential lipid synthesis**: The evolutionary reason mammals lost desaturases for ω-6 and ω-3 fatty acids (requiring dietary intake) is not fully explained.
- **Membrane lipid diversity**: The functional necessity for hundreds of distinct lipid species in a single membrane (lipidomics) is incompletely understood; hypotheses include fine-tuning protein function, curvature stress, and domain formation.
- **Abiogenesis**: The prebiotic synthesis of amphiphilic lipids capable of forming stable, functional protocell membranes remains a central gap in origin-of-life models.
