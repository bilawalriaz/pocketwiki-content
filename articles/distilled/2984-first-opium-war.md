# First Opium War

## Overview
The First Opium War (1839–1842) was a military conflict between the British Empire and Qing China, triggered by China's crackdown on the British-led opium trade. The British victory led to the Treaty of Nanking, which forced China to open ports, pay indemnities, and cede Hong Kong Island. This conflict is widely considered the beginning of modern Chinese history and the start of a "century of humiliation" for China, fundamentally altering Sino-Western relations and initiating a period of foreign domination.

## Timeline
- **1729** — Qing dynasty issues first restrictions on opium (banning madak).
- **1757** — Qing authorities formulate the "Canton System," restricting foreign trade to Guangzhou.
- **1796** — Qing dynasty issues an outright ban on opium.
- **1834** — British East India Company's monopoly on trade with China ends.
- **1839** — Qing Commissioner Lin Zexu seizes and destroys British opium stocks in Guangzhou.
- **1840** — British expeditionary force arrives in China; captures Zhoushan (Chusan) Island.
- **1841** — British capture Hong Kong Island (26 January); Convention of Chuenpi signed but rejected by both governments.
- **1842** — British capture Shanghai and Nanjing; Treaty of Nanking signed (29 August).

## Body

### Background and Causes
Trade between Europe and China was long regulated by the Qing dynasty's Canton System, which confined foreign merchants to Guangzhou and required them to deal through licensed Chinese merchants (the Cohong). A major trade imbalance existed, as European demand for Chinese tea, silk, and porcelain was high, but Chinese demand for European goods was low, leading to a drain of silver from Europe to China. To reverse this deficit, British merchants, especially after the East India Company's monopoly ended in 1834, massively increased the opium trade from British India. Opium addiction spread in China, causing social problems and reversing the flow of silver. The Qing court debated legalization but ultimately chose suppression, appointing the moralistic official Lin Zexu to eradicate the trade in 1839.

### Escalation and War
Lin Zexu confiscated and destroyed over 1,400 tonnes of British opium and blockaded foreign merchants in Guangzhou. Tensions escalated after a brawl involving British sailors resulted in a Chinese death, which the British superintendent, Charles Elliot, refused to resolve under Chinese law. After skirmishes at Kowloon and Chuenpi, the British government, under Foreign Secretary Lord Palmerston, decided on a punitive expedition to protect its commercial interests and national honor. The British forces, technologically superior with steamships like the *Nemesis* and better artillery, systematically captured key Chinese ports and river systems, including Zhoushan, Amoy, Ningbo, and Shanghai. The campaign culminated in the capture of Nanjing and the interception of imperial tax barges on the Yangtze River, crippling Qing finances.

### Treaty and Aftermath
Facing military defeat and economic disruption, the Qing court agreed to negotiate. The Treaty of Nanking (1842) was the first of the "unequal treaties." Its key terms included: a large indemnity of 21 million silver taels; the opening of five treaty ports (Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo, Shanghai) to British trade; the cession of Hong Kong Island to Britain in perpetuity; and the abolition of the Cohong monopoly system. A supplementary treaty granted British subjects extraterritoriality (exemption from Chinese law). The opium trade was not addressed and continued to grow, with opium legalized in China in 1858. The war's outcome weakened the Qing dynasty, contributing to internal unrest like the Taiping Rebellion, and initiated a period of foreign economic and political influence in China.

## Terms
- **Canton System**: The Qing dynasty's system (formalized 1757) that restricted all Western maritime trade to the port of Guangzhou and required foreign merchants to deal only with licensed Chinese merchant guilds (the Cohong).
- **Cohong**: A guild of Chinese merchants in Guangzhou licensed by the Qing government to act as the sole intermediaries for foreign trade under the Canton System.
- **Unequal Treaty**: A term used to describe treaties imposed on China by Western powers after the Opium Wars, which were seen as violating Chinese sovereignty through extraterritoriality, indemnities, and forced trade concessions.
- **Extraterritoriality**: The legal principle that foreign nationals in a country are subject only to the laws of their own country, not the laws of the host nation, as granted to British subjects in the Treaty of the Bogue.
- **Lin Zexu**: The Qing imperial commissioner appointed in 1839 to eradicate the opium trade in Guangzhou, whose actions directly precipitated the war.
- **Charles Elliot**: The British Superintendent of Trade in China who commanded British forces during the early stages of the war and signed the Convention of Chuenpi.
- **Treaty of Nanking**: The 1842 treaty that ended the First Opium War, imposing major concessions on China including indemnities, port openings, and the cession of Hong Kong.
- **Congreve rocket**: A type of military rocket used by the British forces, which was effective against Chinese ships and fortifications.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause of the War**: Historians debate whether the war was fundamentally about opium profits, Britain's desire to uphold free trade principles and national honor, or inevitable conflict between an expanding Western power and a rigid Qing system. Some argue it was not primarily about opium but about diplomatic equality and market access.
- **Inevitability**: Scholars question whether the conflict could have been avoided. Some point to the lack of diplomatic mechanisms and China's refusal to negotiate as equals, while others argue that British commercial expansion made some form of conflict likely.
- **Chinese Internal Politics**: Revisionist views emphasize that Qing court factionalism and internal concerns about corruption and military decay (due to opium) were key drivers of the crackdown, rather than just a simple moral stance against the drug.
- **Moral Judgment**: The war remains morally contentious. In Britain, it was opposed by figures like William Gladstone as an unjust war to protect a "disgraceful" drug trade, while proponents framed it as a fight for free trade and diplomatic respect.
