# South China Sea

## Overview
The South China Sea is a marginal sea of the Western Pacific Ocean, bounded by China, Taiwan, the Philippines, Malaysia, Brunei, Indonesia, and Vietnam. It is a critical global trade route, carrying $3.4 trillion in maritime shipping in 2016, and is rich in natural resources like fish and hydrocarbons. The sea is the focus of intense and overlapping territorial claims by several nations, making it a major potential flashpoint for international conflict.

## Timeline
- **c. 45 million years ago** — The South China Sea basin begins to open as the "Dangerous Ground" rifts away from southern China.
- **c. 30 million years ago** — Seafloor spreading begins, forming the V-shaped basin.
- **1046–771 BCE** — The earliest known Chinese name for the sea, *Nanfang Hai*, appears in the *Yi Zhou Shu*.
- **16th century** — Portuguese sailors refer to it as the *China Sea* (*Mare da China*).
- **1941** — Japan gains naval control of most of the sea during World War II.
- **1974** — China seizes full control of the Paracel Islands from South Vietnam in a brief conflict.
- **March 1988** — A naval clash between China and Vietnam in the Spratly Islands results in over 70 Vietnamese deaths.
- **2008** — The International Court of Justice settles the Pedra Branca sovereignty dispute between Singapore and Malaysia.
- **January 2013** — The Philippines initiates arbitration proceedings against China over maritime claims.
- **July 2016** — An arbitral tribunal rules largely in favor of the Philippines, stating China has "no legal basis" for historic rights within its nine-dash line.
- **July 2017** — Indonesia renames the northern part of its exclusive economic zone in the sea as the North Natuna Sea.
- **June 2020** — An ASEAN summit statement affirms the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) as the basis for maritime entitlements.

## Body

### Etymology and Naming
The dominant English name, "South China Sea," derives from early European trade routes to China. Historical Chinese names include *Nanfang Hai* (Southern Sea) and *Nan Hai* (South Sea). In Southeast Asia, it was once called the Champa Sea. Modern names reflect political claims: Vietnam calls it the East Sea (*Biển Đông*); the Philippines officially uses "West Philippine Sea" for areas within its exclusive economic zone (EEZ); and Indonesia has designated parts as the North Natuna Sea.

### Hydrography and Geology
The sea has an average depth of 1,212 meters and a maximum depth of over 5,500 meters. It lies above a drowned continental shelf and opened due to tectonic rifting and seafloor spreading. Its formation is debated, with one theory linking it to the extrusion of Indochina caused by India's collision with Asia. Major rivers, including the Mekong, Pearl, and Red, deposit large sediment volumes, creating deltas rich in oil and gas.

### Islands and Resources
The sea contains over 250 small islands, atolls, and reefs, most uninhabited. Key groups are the Spratly and Paracel Islands. These features are the primary focus of territorial disputes. The sea is a vital source of fish, accounting for 14% of the world's commercial fishing catch in 2010. While significant oil and natural gas reserves exist, most proven reserves are near undisputed coastlines, not in the contested Spratly and Paracel areas.

### Territorial Claims and Disputes
Multiple countries have overlapping claims. China and Taiwan claim almost the entire sea via the "nine-dash line." Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, Brunei, and Indonesia claim areas within their respective EEZs under UNCLOS. Disputes have led to naval clashes, standoffs, and land reclamation. The 2016 arbitral tribunal ruling, which China rejected, invalidated China's historic rights claims within the nine-dash line and criticized its island-building. The U.S. conducts "freedom of navigation" operations to challenge excessive claims, while ASEAN seeks a binding Code of Conduct with China.

## Terms
- **Marginal Sea:** A sea adjacent to or partially enclosed by a continent and islands.
- **Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ):** A maritime zone under UNCLOS where a coastal state has sovereign rights to explore and exploit natural resources, extending up to 200 nautical miles from its coast.
- **UNCLOS (United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea):** An international treaty establishing a legal framework for all ocean activities, including maritime boundaries and resource rights.
- **Nine-dash line:** A demarcation line used by China (and formerly Taiwan) to claim most of the South China Sea, with no clear legal basis under UNCLOS.
- **Freedom of Navigation:** A principle of international law that allows ships and aircraft to transit through another state's territorial waters or EEZ without prior permission, for purposes like trade or military transit.
- **Arbitral Tribunal:** A panel of judges established to resolve disputes under international law, such as UNCLOS. Its rulings are legally binding on participating states.
- **Code of Conduct (COC):** A proposed agreement between ASEAN member states and China to manage disputes and prevent conflict in the South China Sea.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historic Rights vs. UNCLOS:** Does China's "nine-dash line" claim represent legitimate historic rights, or is it superseded by the modern legal framework of UNCLOS? The 2016 tribunal ruled against historic rights.
- **Role of External Powers:** Should non-claimant states like the United States and Australia be involved in resolving disputes, or does their involvement escalate tensions? China prefers bilateral talks, while many ASEAN states seek multilateral solutions.
- **Enforcement of Rulings:** How can international legal rulings, like the 2016 arbitration, be enforced when a major power like China rejects them?
- **Resource Management:** Can joint development agreements for oil, gas, and fisheries be successfully implemented without first resolving underlying sovereignty disputes?
