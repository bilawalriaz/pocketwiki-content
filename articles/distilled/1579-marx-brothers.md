# Marx Brothers

## Overview
The Marx Brothers were an American comedy troupe who achieved success in vaudeville, on Broadway, and in 13 motion pictures. The five brothers were Chico Marx, Harpo Marx, Groucho Marx, Gummo Marx, and Zeppo Marx, though Gummo and Zeppo both left the group over time, leaving Chico, Harpo, and Groucho as a trio. They are considered by critics, scholars and fans to be among the greatest and most influential comedians of the 20th century, a recognition underscored by the American Film Institute (AFI) selecting five of their thirteen feature films to be among the top 100 comedy films (with two in the top fifteen) and including them as the only group of performers on AFI's 100 Years...100 Stars list of the 25 greatest male stars of Classical Hollywood cinema. Managed by their mother Minnie, they developed distinct, enduring personas: Groucho’s rapid-fire wordplay and greasepaint mustache, Chico’s Italian-accented schemer, and Harpo’s silent, harp-playing anarchist. Their films, particularly the Paramount era (*Duck Soup*, *Horse Feathers*) and the MGM Thalberg productions (*A Night at the Opera*, *A Day at the Races*), satirized authority, pretension, and social convention through a blend of verbal wit, slapstick, and musical interludes.

## Body

### Family Background and Early Life
The brothers were born in New York City to Jewish immigrants: Minnie (née Schoenberg), from a family of German funfair entertainers, and Samuel Marx, a French Alsatian tailor. Their maternal uncle, Al Shean, was half of the vaudeville duo Gallagher and Shean. The family lived in Yorkville, then moved to Chicago in 1909 to access vaudeville agencies. Their firstborn, Manfred, died in infancy (1886). The five performing brothers were Leonard "Chico" (1887), Adolph "Harpo" (1888), Julius "Groucho" (1890), Milton "Gummo" (1892), and Herbert "Zeppo" (1901). Minnie acted as manager under the name "Minnie Palmer," driving the act’s formation and negotiating contracts.

### Stage Beginnings: Vaudeville to Broadway
**1905–1914: Rise in Independent Vaudeville**  
Groucho debuted in 1905; Gummo and Harpo joined by 1907 as "The Three Nightingales." A 1909 booking mishap forced Harpo into the act. In Nacogdoches, Texas, a runaway mule interrupted their show; Groucho’s angry ad-libs ("Nacogdoches is full of roaches") drew laughter, pivoting the act from singing to comedy. By 1910 they were "The Six Mascots," briefly including Minnie and Aunt Hannah. Chico joined in 1912 after a failed solo act (Marx and Gordon). Stage names were coined during a poker game by monologist Art Fisher, inspired by the "O" nickname fad and *Sherlocko the Monk*: Groucho (moody or "grouch bag"), Chico (chasing "chicks," pronounced "Chick-o"), Harpo (harp), Gummo (rubber-soled shoes/gumshoe), and Zeppo (origin contested: chimpanzee Zippo, "Zeke and Zeb," or zeppelins).

**1914–1922: *Home Again*, War, and Vaudeville Collapse**  
Uncle Al Shean wrote *Home Again* (expanded *Mr. Green's Reception*), cementing personas: Groucho as fast-talking German (later American "Jones" due to WWI anti-German sentiment), Harpo as silent (Shean cited lisp; Harpo cited better reviews), Chico as Italian schemer, Gummo/Zeppo as romantic straight men. *Home Again* secured a United Booking Office (UBO) contract (1914), sharing bills with Jack Benny and W.C. Fields. In 1915, 14-year-old Zeppo joined for the only known five-brother performance (Flint, Michigan). WWI draft efforts failed; Gummo drafted 1918, replaced by mechanic Zeppo. A 1921 silent short, *Humor Risk*, screened once and was shelved. A 1922 UK tour without Albee’s permission triggered a UBO blacklist. A Shubert-circuit show, *The Twentieth Century Revue*, failed due to weak supporting acts and lawsuits.

**1924–1929: Broadway Success**  
Banned from major vaudeville, the brothers nearly split. *I'll Say She Is* (1923–24), built from a failed show’s assets, toured to Broadway (313 performances). Alexander Woollcott’s rave review brought them to the Algonquin Round Table and prompted public use of stage names. *The Cocoanuts* (1925, Kaufman/Berlin, 276 performances) introduced Margaret Dumont as wealthy foil and the "Why a Duck?" routine. *Animal Crackers* (1928, Kaufman/Ryskind, Kalmar/Ruby, 171 performances) added "Hooray for Captain Spaulding" (Groucho’s theme) and the stolen-painting plot.

### Motion Pictures
**1929–1933: Paramount Era**  
Paramount filmed *The Cocoanuts* (1929) at Astoria Studios during *Animal Crackers* Broadway run; primitive sound tech required water-sprayed paper props and soundproofed cameras. It succeeded despite technical limits. Minnie died Sept. 13, 1929; the crash wiped out Groucho/Harpo investments. *Animal Crackers* (1930) ended their stage career. *Monkey Business* (1931), first original screenplay, used writers Perelman, Johnstone, Sheekman, Shean, and Nat Perrin (lifelong Groucho friend). *Horse Feathers* (1932), satirizing college/Prohibition, hit #1 and *Time* cover. *Duck Soup* (1933, dir. Leo McCarey), satirizing war/dictatorship, is AFI’s highest-rated Marx film; sparked a dispute with Fredonia, NY over "Freedonia." Concurrent radio show *Flywheel, Shyster and Flywheel* supplied film material.

**1933–1949: MGM, RKO, United Artists**  
Post-Paramount, Zeppo left to co-found a major talent agency (with Gummo). Irving Thalberg signed the trio to MGM, imposing structure: sympathetic characters, romantic subplots, villain-targeted jokes, a "low point," and pre-filming audience testing. He restored Harpo’s harp and Chico’s piano solos. *A Night at the Opera* (1935, stateroom scene) and *A Day at the Races* (1937, "Tootsie Frootsie" routine) were their peaks; Groucho later called them their best. Thalberg died Sept. 1936 (pneumonia, 37); without his advocacy, quality waned. *Room Service* (1938, RKO) preceded three final MGM films (*At the Circus*, *Go West*, *The Big Store*, 1939–41). Announced retirement reversed for *A Night in Casablanca* (1946, UA) to cover Chico’s gambling debts. *Love Happy* (1949, UA), originally a Harpo vehicle, added Chico and Groucho for financing; final team film.

### Later Years and Solo Careers
Groucho hosted *You Bet Your Life* (1947–61, NBC radio/TV), authored books (*Groucho and Me*, *Memoirs of a Mangy Lover*). Chico led a big band (vocalist Mel Tormé); Harpo performed solo. A 1957 *Tonight! America After Dark* appearance was the only five-brother TV reunion. A planned 1960 Billy Wilder film (*A Day at the U.N.*) collapsed after Chico’s death (1961, arteriosclerosis, 74). Harpo died 1964 (heart attack, 75). Gummo (1977), Groucho (1977), Zeppo (1979) followed. A 1947 *Newsweek* report of a biopic (*The Life and Times of the Marx Brothers*) re-enacting unfilmed vaudeville material never materialized.

### Screen and Theatrical Persona
Personas mirrored actual traits: Groucho’s misanthropic wit, Chico’s street-smart con artist, Harpo’s childlike chaos. Zeppo, considered funniest offstage, played straight men but could impersonate any brother (replaced Groucho during *Animal Crackers* appendicitis). They satirized high society and hypocrisy via improvisation (e.g., Harpo chasing chorus girl during Groucho monologue; Groucho: "First time I ever saw a taxi hail a passenger"). In *Duck Soup*, all three in greasepaint/nightcaps played mirror images. Out of costume, they resembled each other (receding hairlines); Zeppo played Groucho’s son in *Horse Feathers*.

### Legacy
**Contemporary Influence (1930s–40s)**  
Cameos in Disney (*Mickey's Gala Premier*, *Mother Goose Goes Hollywood*), *Flip the Frog*, Tex Avery cartoons. Harpo inspired Dopey (*Snow White*); Groucho shaped Bugs Bunny (explicit in *Slick Hare*, *Wideo Wabbit*).

**Post-Golden Age Rediscovery (1960s–70s)**  
TV reruns and *You Bet Your Life* sustained popularity. *Duck Soup* became anti-Vietnam War anthem. Failed animation pilots (Screen Gems 1960, Filmation 1966). Rankin-Bass *The Mad, Mad, Mad Comedians* (1970) featured Groucho’s voice. Broadway *Minnie's Boys* (1970, by Arthur Marx) lost $750k but won awards for Lewis Stadlen. 1974 *Animal Crackers* re-release (copyright cleared) caused near-riot at NYC premiere. TV homages: *M*A*S*H*, *All in the Family*, *Mary Tyler Moore*, *Welcome Back, Kotter* (Gabe Kaplan’s Groucho impressions). Woody Allen references: *Take the Money and Run*, *Annie Hall*, *Manhattan*, *Hannah and Her Sisters* (Duck Soup epiphany), *Everyone Says I Love You* (French "Captain Spaulding"). Music: Queen albums *A Night at the Opera*, *A Day at the Races*; The Damned "There Ain't No Sanity Clause"; Sparks (originally Sparks Brothers); Jacques Brel "Le Gaz" (stateroom scene). Vlasic Pickles stork mascot (Groucho mannerisms, 1974–present). Broadway *A Day in Hollywood/A Night in the Ukraine* (1980, 2 Tonys).

**Late 20th Century–Present**  
Film references: *Brazil* (1985), *Twelve Monkeys* (1996), *Brain Donors* (1992, Zucker brothers). Animation: *Aladdin* (Robin Williams/Genie), *Animaniacs*, *Tiny Toons*. *Spitting Image* puppets (1990); *Cerebus the Aardvark* (Lord Julius/Duke Leonardi). Stage revivals: *The Cocoanuts*, *Animal Crackers* internationally; 2016 off-Broadway *I'll Say She Is* reconstruction (Diamond/Sisk). Frank Ferrante’s *An Evening with Groucho* (1985–present, PBS 2022). Radio *Flywheel, Shyster, and Flywheel* scripts found in Library of Congress (1980s), adapted for BBC and 2010 NY Musical Theatre Festival (*The Most Ridiculous Thing You Ever Hoid*).

### Awards and Honors
- 1933: Handprints at Grauman’s Chinese Theatre (Chico, Groucho, Harpo, Zeppo).
- 1974: Groucho receives honorary Academy Award (standing ovation) on behalf of all four; praises Margaret Dumont.
- 1999: AFI names Marx Brothers collectively #20 on 25 Greatest Male Screen Legends (only group honored).
- National Film Registry: *Duck Soup* (1990), *A Night at the Opera* (1993).

## Terms
- **Vaudeville**: North American variety entertainment (1880s–1930s) featuring unrelated acts (singers, comedians, acrobats); the Marx Brothers’ training ground.
- **Straight man**: A performer who feeds lines to a comic, reacting seriously; Gummo and Zeppo filled this role.
- **Greasepaint**: Theatrical makeup; Groucho’s painted mustache/eyebrows became his trademark (later grown real for *You Bet Your Life*).
- **Grouch bag**: A small purse worn around the neck for valuables; one theory for Groucho’s nickname.
- **Algonquin Round Table**: 1920s NYC intellectual circle (Woollcott, Kaufman, Parker); Harpo became a member via Woollcott.
- **"Why a Duck?"**: *The Cocoanuts* routine where Groucho explains a map; Chico hears "viaduct" as "why a duck," escalating absurdity.
- **Stateroom scene**: *A Night at the Opera* climax: dozens crowd into a tiny ship’s cabin; widely imitated.
- **Freedonia**: Fictional country in *Duck Soup*; prompted protest from Fredonia, NY.
- **Low point**: Thalberg’s structural requirement: a moment where all seems lost for protagonists, heightening comic payoff.
- **Pre-filming audience testing**: Thalberg’s innovation: performing scripts live to gauge laughs before shooting.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Zeppo’s nickname origin**: Competing theories—Harpo cites chimpanzee "Zippo"; family cites "Zeke and Zeb" humor; Chico’s daughter cites "Zeb" joke; Groucho cites zeppelins (post-1924).
- **Harpo’s silence**: Shean attributed to lisp; Harpo claimed reviews praised his silence; possibly both.
- **Groucho’s nickname**: "Moody temperament" vs. *Sherlocko the Monk* character vs. "grouch bag" purse.
- **Gummo’s nickname**: Rubber-soled shoes (dancer, hypochondriac, or "gumshoe" sneaking per Harpo).
- ***Humor Risk* (1921)**: Only known screening poorly received; no copies survive; plot details (Groucho villain, Harpo romantic lead) from source only.
- **Biopic *The Life and Times of the Marx Brothers* (1947)**: Announced in *Newsweek* with all five brothers; would have re-enacted unfilmed vaudeville/Broadway material; never produced.
