# Great Wall of China

## Overview
The Great Wall of China is a vast series of fortifications built across China's historical northern borders to defend against nomadic invasions from the Eurasian Steppe. Constructed over centuries by multiple dynasties, it represents one of history's most impressive architectural feats, symbolizing both defensive strategy and the enduring interaction between settled and nomadic peoples.

## Timeline
- **7th century BC** — Earliest walls built by individual Chinese states during the Spring and Autumn period.
- **221 BC** — Qin Shi Huang connects existing walls after unifying China, forming the first "Great Wall."
- **206 BC–AD 220** — Han dynasty expands and rebuilds the wall to protect Silk Road trade.
- **1368–1644** — Ming dynasty constructs the most extensive and best-known sections using brick and stone.
- **1644** — Manchus cross the wall at Shanhai Pass after an alliance with Ming general Wu Sangui, ending Ming rule.
- **1987** — The Great Wall is designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2007** — Voted one of the New 7 Wonders of the World.
- **2012** — A comprehensive survey measures the total length of all historical wall sections at 21,196.18 km.
- **2023** — A section in Shanxi province is severely damaged by construction workers widening a gap.

## Body

### Construction and Purpose
The wall's primary purpose was defense against northern nomadic groups. It was not a single continuous structure but a system of walls, trenches, and fortresses. Early walls (7th–3rd centuries BC) were built by rival states using rammed earth and stone. After unification in 221 BC, Emperor Qin Shi Huang ordered the connection of these walls along the northern frontier, though the guiding principle was "build and move on," not a fixed border. Successive dynasties, including the Han, Northern dynasties, and Sui, repaired and expanded the wall at great cost. The Ming dynasty, following a major defeat to the Oirats, built the most substantial sections with bricks and stone, incorporating watchtowers, barracks, and signaling capabilities. Beyond defense, the wall facilitated border control, immigration regulation, and trade along the Silk Road.

### Course and Scale
The wall's full extent is difficult to define, as it comprises multiple defensive lines. A 2012 Chinese government survey measured all associated sites at 21,196.18 km (13,170.70 mi). The Ming Great Wall alone accounts for 8,850 km (5,500 mi). The wall arcs from Liaodong in the east to Lop Lake in the west, roughly delineating the edge of the Mongolian steppe. Famous Ming sections include Badaling, Jinshanling, and Mutianyu near Beijing, and the eastern terminus at Shanhai Pass, where the wall meets the Bohai Gulf. The western terminus of the Ming wall is Jiayu Pass in Gansu province.

### Condition and Visibility
While sections near Beijing are preserved and renovated, much of the wall is in disrepair due to erosion, human activity, and natural decay. A 2012 report stated that 22% of the Ming Great Wall (1,961 km) has disappeared. The popular myth that the wall is visible from the Moon with the naked eye is false; its width is too narrow. Astronauts report it is barely visible from low Earth orbit under perfect conditions, and is not more conspicuous than other human-made objects.

## Terms
- **Rammed earth**: A construction technique where earth is compacted between frames to form solid walls.
- **Xiongnu**: A nomadic people from the Eurasian Steppe who were a primary threat to early Chinese dynasties.
- **Silk Road**: An ancient network of trade routes connecting China to the West, whose commerce the wall helped regulate.
- **Ming dynasty**: The ruling dynasty of China from 1368 to 1644, responsible for the most famous sections of the Great Wall.
- **Shanhai Pass**: The traditional eastern terminus of the Ming Great Wall, where it meets the sea.
- **Jiayu Pass**: The traditional western terminus of the Ming Great Wall in Gansu province.
- **Sticky rice mortar**: A binding agent made from sticky rice soup and slaked lime, used extensively in Ming-era wall construction.
- **UNESCO World Heritage Site**: A landmark or area with legal protection for having cultural, historical, or scientific significance.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Defining the "Great Wall"**: There is no formal, agreed-upon definition of what constitutes the "Great Wall," making its total length and course difficult to describe definitively.
- **Visibility from space**: While the myth of visibility from the Moon is debunked, debate continues about the conditions under which the wall is visible from low Earth orbit, with varying astronaut testimonies.
- **Historical naming**: The wall had many names in Chinese history (e.g., "Long Wall," "Border Wall"), and the modern English name "Great Wall of China" evolved from early European accounts.
