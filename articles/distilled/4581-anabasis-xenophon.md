# Anabasis (Xenophon)

## Overview
*Anabasis* is the most famous work by the ancient Greek soldier and writer Xenophon. It is a first-hand account of the expedition and retreat of the Ten Thousand, a Greek mercenary army hired in 401 BC to help Cyrus the Younger seize the Persian throne. The narrative is a classic tale of military adventure, leadership, and survival, and has been influential in both military history and literature.

## Timeline
- **c. 401 BC** — Cyrus the Younger hires the Ten Thousand Greek mercenaries to march against his brother, King Artaxerxes II of Persia.
- **401 BC** — The Battle of Cunaxa: Cyrus's army wins a tactical victory, but Cyrus is killed, stranding the Greeks.
- **401 BC** — The Greek generals are treacherously captured and killed by the Persian satrap Tissaphernes.
- **401-400 BC** — Xenophon is elected one of three new leaders and guides the army on a grueling northward retreat.
- **c. 400 BC** — The army reaches the Black Sea at Trabzon, famously crying "Thalatta! Thalatta!" ("The sea! The sea!").
- **c. 400 BC** — The army fights for Seuthes II in Thrace before being recruited into the Spartan army of Thibron.
- **c. 370 BC** — Xenophon composes the *Anabasis*.

## Body

### Authorship and Composition
Xenophon's authorship is the mainstream scholarly view, though the work was once attributed to "Themistogenes of Syracuse." Xenophon himself, in his *Hellenica*, refers the reader to an account by Themistogenes. The 10th-century Suda also lists the work under Themistogenes but notes it is "preserved among the works of Xenophon." The ancient writer Plutarch later suggested Xenophon used a pseudonym to distance himself as the subject from himself as the author. The seven books were composed around 370 BC.

### The Expedition and Retreat
The narrative begins with Cyrus's march from Ionia into the Persian interior. After the victory at Cunaxa and Cyrus's death, the Greek army is stranded. Following the treacherous capture of their senior officers, Xenophon emerges as a key leader. He persuades the army to march north toward the Black Sea rather than surrender. The bulk of the *Anabasis* details this arduous retreat through hostile territory, including the deserts of Mesopotamia, the mountains of Armenia, and the lands of the Carduchii. The army fights numerous battles, faces severe hardships like snow and lack of food, and makes ad hoc decisions about leadership and tactics.

### The Journey's End and Legacy
The army's arrival at the Greek city of Trapezus on the Black Sea marks a major turning point. The journey continues with campaigns for local rulers like Seuthes II of Thrace and ends with the army's integration into the Spartan military under Thibron. Xenophon's account, noted for its simple and direct prose, became a famous story in Greece. It may have later inspired Philip of Macedon to believe a disciplined Greek army could defeat the Persians. Beyond military history, the *Anabasis* is also used to teach classical philosophy, as the army's principles of statesmanship are seen as exemplifying Socratic thought.

### Educational and Cultural Influence
The *Anabasis* is traditionally one of the first unabridged Greek texts studied by students due to its clear Attic dialect. Its frequent use of the verb *exelauno* ("march forth") led to the American student tradition of "Exelauno Day" on March 4. The work has inspired numerous literary and artistic adaptations, including Arrian's *Anabasis of Alexander*, Sol Yurick's novel *The Warriors*, and various modern historical and fantasy works.

## Terms
- **Anabasis**: Literally "ascent" or "march inland." The title refers to Cyrus's expedition from the coast into the Persian interior, though most of the book covers the return march.
- **Ten Thousand**: The collective name for the large army of Greek mercenaries hired by Cyrus the Younger.
- **Cyrus the Younger**: Persian prince who hired the Greek mercenaries to overthrow his brother, King Artaxerxes II.
- **Artaxerxes II**: King of Persia whom Cyrus the Younger attempted to depose.
- **Tissaphernes**: Persian satrap (governor) who treacherously captured and killed the Greek senior officers.
- **Clearchus**: The senior Spartan general of the Greek mercenaries, killed by Tissaphernes's treachery.
- **Thalatta! Thalatta!**: The Greek cry of "The sea! The sea!" shouted by the soldiers upon reaching the Black Sea, signifying safety.
- **Attic dialect**: The dialect of ancient Greek spoken in Athens, noted for its clarity in Xenophon's prose.
- **Exelauno**: A Greek verb meaning "march out" or "march forth," used frequently in the text.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authorship**: While Xenophon is the accepted author, the ancient attribution to "Themistogenes of Syracuse" and Xenophon's own reference to this name in the *Hellenica* have fueled long-standing scholarly debate about pseudonymity and literary self-presentation.
- **Historical Accuracy**: Scholars debate the precise chronology and details of the march, as well as the degree to which Xenophon's account may be shaped by literary conventions or personal justification.
- **Philosophical Interpretation**: There is discussion about the extent to which the *Anabasis* is a vehicle for Socratic political philosophy, with the army's organization and Xenophon's leadership presented as practical applications of Socratic principles.
