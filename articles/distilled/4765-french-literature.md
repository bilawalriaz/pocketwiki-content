# French literature

## Overview
French literature encompasses works written in the French language, primarily by French citizens, and is a cornerstone of national identity and European literary history. It has been profoundly influential, with France leading in Nobel Prizes in Literature, and its evolution reflects major political, social, and artistic shifts from the Middle Ages to the present.

## Timeline
- **c. 1100** — *The Song of Roland*, the first major work in the *chansons de geste* cycle, is composed.
- **11th century** — Literature in medieval French emerges as one of the oldest vernacular literatures in Western Europe.
- **14th century** — The prominence of French literature is temporarily eclipsed by vernacular literature in Italy.
- **16th century** — French literature undergoes a major creative evolution.
- **17th century** — Through the Ancien Régime, French literature comes to dominate European letters.
- **18th century** — French becomes the literary *lingua franca* and diplomatic language of Western Europe.
- **1901** — Sully Prudhomme wins the first-ever Nobel Prize in Literature.
- **1903** — The Prix Goncourt is created for "the best and most imaginative prose work of the year."
- **1964** — Jean-Paul Sartre is awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature but declines it.
- **2022** — Annie Ernaux becomes the fifteenth French author to win the Nobel Prize in Literature.

## Body

### Historical Development and National Identity
French literature, written in a Romance language derived from Latin, began as a key vernacular tradition in the Middle Ages. After a period of Italian overshadowing in the 14th century, it evolved creatively in the 16th century and achieved European dominance in the 17th century under the Ancien Régime. By the 18th century, French was the continent's literary and diplomatic *lingua franca*. This heritage is a source of profound national pride, deeply embedded in the education system and cultural institutions like the Académie française. The state heavily sponsors literary arts, and prizes are major news, reflecting literature's central role in French identity.

### Major Literary Forms and Key Texts
French literature is rich across genres. Fiction began with medieval epics like *The Song of Roland* and romances by Chrétien de Troyes. It evolved through Renaissance works by Rabelais, the classical novels of Madame de Lafayette, Enlightenment philosophies from Voltaire and Rousseau, and the 19th-century realism of Balzac, Flaubert, and Zola. The 20th century saw modernist and existentialist works by Proust, Camus, and Sartre. Poetry progressed from troubadours and the Pléiade group to Romanticism (Hugo, Baudelaire), Symbolism (Mallarmé, Rimbaud), and Surrealism. Theatre flourished with classical tragedies by Racine, comedies by Molière, and 20th-century absurdism by Ionesco and Beckett. Nonfiction includes foundational philosophical essays by Montaigne and Descartes, and later works by Foucault and Derrida.

### Awards and International Recognition
France holds the record for Nobel Prizes in Literature, with fifteen winners as of 2022. Notable laureates include Sully Prudhomme (the first), Albert Camus, and Jean-Paul Sartre, who declined the prize in 1964 to avoid becoming an "institution." Major French literary awards include the Prix Goncourt (est. 1903), Prix Femina (est. 1904), and Prix Renaudot (est. 1926). Beyond France, Francophone literature from countries like Belgium, Canada, and Senegal contributes to and transforms the French literary experience.

## Terms
- **Vernacular**: The everyday language of a country or region, as opposed to a literary or classical language like Latin.
- **Chansons de geste**: A genre of Old French epic poetry celebrating heroic deeds.
- **Ancien Régime**: The political and social system of the Kingdom of France before the 1789 Revolution.
- **Lingua franca**: A language used for communication between people who do not share a native language.
- **Francophone literature**: Literature written in French by authors from countries where French is not the sole or primary language.
- **Académie française**: A French council for the regulation of the French language, founded in 1635.
- **Négritude**: A literary and ideological movement developed by francophone Black intellectuals in the 1930s.
- **Symbolism**: A late 19th-century artistic movement emphasizing the expression of ideas through symbolic images and indirect suggestion.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source text notes that the European prominence of French literature was "eclipsed in part" by Italian literature in the 14th century, suggesting a scholarly debate about the precise timing and nature of this shift in literary dominance. Additionally, the definition of "French literature" itself is a point of discussion, as it can include works by citizens of other nations writing in French (Francophone literature) and by people living in France who speak traditional languages other than French.
