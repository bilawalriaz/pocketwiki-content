# Tokugawa shogunate

## Overview
The Tokugawa shogunate was the feudal military government that ruled Japan from 1603 to 1868. Established after a decisive civil war victory, it imposed a rigid social order and strict isolationist foreign policy, which brought over 250 years of internal peace but ultimately led to its overthrow by imperial loyalists seeking modernization.

## Timeline
- **1600** — Tokugawa Ieyasu wins the Battle of Sekigahara, securing central authority.
- **1603** — Ieyasu is appointed shōgun, establishing the Tokugawa shogunate.
- **1615** — Tokugawa victory at the Siege of Osaka solidifies control; a long peace begins.
- **1635** — Seclusion laws (sakoku) are fully implemented, banning most foreign trade and travel.
- **1638** — The Shimabara Rebellion is crushed, leading to the Danka system to suppress Christianity.
- **1716–1745** — Reign of Tokugawa Yoshimune, who implements Kyoho reforms to address financial issues.
- **1853** — U.S. Commodore Matthew Perry arrives, forcing Japan to end its isolation.
- **1868** — The Meiji Restoration overthrows the shogunate; imperial rule is restored.
- **1869** — Defeat of the Republic of Ezo at the Battle of Hakodate ends Tokugawa resistance.

## Body

### Establishment and Control
The shogunate was founded by Tokugawa Ieyasu after his victory at Sekigahara (1600), ending the Sengoku period's civil wars. It governed Japan through the **bakuhan system**, a feudal structure splitting power between the shogunate in Edo and domains (**han**) ruled by lords (**daimyō**). To ensure loyalty, the shogunate implemented key policies: the **sankin-kōtai** system required daimyō to alternate residence in Edo, leaving their families as hostages; the **ikkoku ichijyō** law limited each domain to one castle; and the **buke shohatto** laws regulated daimyō and samurai conduct.

### Society and Economy
Society was stratified into a rigid class hierarchy: daimyō and samurai at the top, followed by farmers, artisans, and merchants. This system became inflexible, causing financial strain as fixed peasant taxes lost value over time, leading to numerous peasant rebellions. Despite this, the era saw significant economic growth, urbanization, and the rise of a wealthy merchant class. By 1800, Japan had several large cities, with Edo likely the world's most populous.

### Foreign and Religious Policy
Initially tolerant, the shogunate soon viewed Christianity as a threat to stability. After 1613, persecution intensified, culminating in the **sakoku** isolationist policy of the 1630s. This banned most foreign trade (limited to Chinese, Korean, and Dutch ships) and forbade Japanese from leaving the country. The **Danka** system, mandating Buddhist temple affiliation for all households, was used to eradicate Christianity.

### Decline and Fall
The shogunate weakened during the **Bakumatsu** period (1853–1867) after Commodore Perry's arrival forced Japan's opening. Resentment grew among "outside" daimyō (**tozama**) and pro-imperial factions. An alliance of these groups, led by the Four Western Clans, overthrew the shogunate in the Meiji Restoration (1868). The last shōgun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, resigned, and loyalist forces were defeated by 1869.

### Government Structure
The shōgun was the supreme military ruler, though the Emperor was a nominal figurehead. Key officials included the **rōjū** (senior councillors), **wakadoshiyori** (junior councillors), and **metsuke** (inspectors). The shogunate directly administered its own vast estates (**tenryō**) and controlled key cities and mines. Daimyō were classified as **shinpan** (relatives), **fudai** (hereditary vassals), or **tozama** (outsiders), with the latter holding the largest domains but least initial trust.

## Terms
- **Shōgun**: The supreme military dictator of Japan, nominally appointed by the Emperor.
- **Daimyō**: Feudal lords who ruled autonomous domains (han) under the shogunate.
- **Samurai**: The warrior class serving the shōgun and daimyō.
- **Bakuhan system**: The feudal political structure combining the shogunate (baku) and domains (han).
- **Sankin-kōtai**: The "alternate attendance" policy requiring daimyō to reside in Edo every other year.
- **Sakoku**: The isolationist foreign policy banning most foreign trade and travel.
- **Tenryō**: The shōgun's directly administered estates.
- **Tozama daimyō**: "Outside" lords who submitted to Tokugawa rule after Sekigahara, often with large domains.
- **Buke shohatto**: Laws regulating the conduct of daimyō and the samurai class.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which the rigid class system caused the shogunate's financial decline versus other factors like inflation and peasant unrest.
- The primary cause of the Shimabara Rebellion: whether it was mainly a Christian uprising or a peasant revolt against misrule.
- The degree to which the tozama daimyō's long-standing resentment, rather than immediate post-Perry politics, was the decisive factor in the shogunate's overthrow.
