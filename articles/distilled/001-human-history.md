# Human history

## Overview

Human history is the record of humankind from prehistory to the present, tracing the evolution of modern humans in Africa around 300,000 years ago to the globally interconnected societies of today. The field matters because it explains how hunter-gatherers developed agriculture, built civilizations, created empires and religions, and ultimately transformed the planet through industrialization and globalization. The study synthesizes insights from history, archaeology, anthropology, linguistics, and genetics to construct a coherent narrative across cultures and time periods.

## Timeline

- **c. 300,000 years ago** — *Homo sapiens* evolved in Africa
- **c. 10,000 BCE** — Neolithic Revolution began with the development of agriculture
- **3300–2200 BCE** — Early civilizations emerged in Mesopotamia, Egypt, Peru, the Indus Valley, and China
- **800–200 BCE** — Axial Age saw the rise of transformative philosophies and religions
- **500 BCE–500 CE** — Regional empires of unprecedented size developed worldwide
- **c. 500–1500 CE** — Post-classical period: rise of Islam, Mongol Empire, and expanded trade
- **c. 1450–1500** — Early modern period began with the fall of Constantinople, printing, and voyages of discovery
- **c. 1770** — Industrial Revolution began in Great Britain
- **1914–1918** — World War I caused 10–22.5 million deaths and collapsed four empires
- **1939–1945** — World War II killed 55–80 million people and ended with nuclear weapons
- **1945–1991** — Cold War between the US and Soviet Union ended peacefully with Soviet collapse

## Body

### Prehistory

Humans evolved in Africa from great apes through hominins, which arose 7–5 million years ago. Hominins developed bipedalism and began using rudimentary stone tools c. 3.3 million years ago, marking the Paleolithic era. The genus *Homo* evolved from *Australopithecus*, with *Homo habilis* appearing by 2.3 million years ago and *H. erectus* about 2 million years ago, the first to leave Africa. Human evolution involved interbreeding between related species, including Neanderthals and Denisovans.

*Homo sapiens* evolved around 300,000 years ago and became anatomically modern by 125,000 years ago. Paleolithic humans lived as nomadic hunter-gatherers, created cave paintings suggesting animism or shamanism, and made bone flutes dated around 40,000 years old. Humans migrated out of Africa in waves beginning 194,000–177,000 years ago, with all modern non-Africans descended from a single group that left 70,000–50,000 years ago. By the end of the Ice Age 12,000 years ago, humans had colonized nearly all ice-free parts of the globe.

The Neolithic Revolution beginning around 10,000 BCE marked the development of agriculture in at least 11 separate centers of origin. Cereal cultivation and animal domestication occurred in Mesopotamia by 8500 BCE, rice in China by 8000–7000 BCE, and squash in South America by 8500 BCE. Agriculture created food surpluses supporting denser populations and the first cities and states. Metalworking began with copper around 6400 BCE, with bronze not widely used until the 3rd millennium BCE.

### Ancient period

The Bronze Age saw early civilizations arise near rivers: Mesopotamia (3300 BCE), Egypt (3200 BCE), Norte Chico in Peru (3100 BCE), the Indus Valley (2500 BCE), and China (2200 BCE). These societies developed central governments, complex economies, writing, and monumental architecture. Writing independently developed in at least four civilizations, with Mesopotamian cuneiform the earliest system. Trade created vast commercial networks and the beginnings of archaic globalization.

The Axial Age (800–200 BCE) saw the emergence of transformative ideas: Confucianism, Buddhism, Jainism, Jewish monotheism, and Greek philosophy. The first democratic government was instituted in Athens in 508 BCE. These ideas shaped subsequent intellectual history, with Buddhism reaching China in about the 1st century CE and Greek philosophy diffusing throughout the Mediterranean after Alexander the Great's conquests.

The millennium from 500 BCE to 500 CE saw empires of unprecedented size. The Achaemenid (550–330 BCE), Parthian (247 BCE–224 CE), and Sasanian (224–651 CE) empires succeeded each other in Iran. Alexander the Great (356–323 BCE) founded an empire from Greece to India, spreading Greek culture through Hellenization. The Roman Republic became an empire reaching its peak under Trajan (53–117 CE), with the Pax Romana bringing unprecedented peace. Christianity was legalized in 313 CE and became the sole official religion in 380 CE. In China, the Qin dynasty (221–206 BCE) united the country, followed by the Han dynasty (202 BCE–220 CE), which invented the compass. The Maya developed a writing system and the concept of zero.

The ancient empires faced problems maintaining armies and bureaucracies. The Han dynasty fell in 220 CE, while Rome split into western and eastern regions. The Western Roman Empire fell in 476 CE, but the Byzantine Empire endured.

### Post-classical period (500–1500 CE)

This period saw the rise and spread of major religions and intensified trade. The birth of Islam in the 7th century created a new contender that quickly surpassed the Byzantine and Sasanian Empires. The Abbasid Caliphate oversaw the Islamic Golden Age. The Seljuk Turks arrived in the mid-11th century, and the Ottoman Empire was founded around 1299. The Mongols under Genghis Khan united in 1206 and created the largest contiguous empire in history, divided into four successor states after 1259.

Europe's Early Middle Ages featured depopulation and barbarian invasions. The High Middle Ages after 1000 saw population increase, feudalism, and the founding of universities. The Black Death killed approximately 75–200 million people between 1347 and 1350. In Africa, the Mali and Songhai Empires rose in West Africa, while the Kingdom of Kongo emerged in the 14th century. China was reunified by the Sui dynasty in 589, followed by the Tang and Song dynasties, with the Ming dynasty founded in 1368. The Khmer Empire's capital at Angkor was the most extensive city in the world before the industrial age.

### Early modern period (c. 1450–1800)

The early modern period saw the fall of Constantinople to the Ottomans in 1453, the spread of printing, and European voyages of discovery. Christopher Columbus first arrived in the Americas in 1492, initiating the Columbian exchange of plants, animals, diseases, and culture between hemispheres. European powers colonized large parts of the world through maritime empires, first Portuguese and Spanish, then French, English, and Dutch.

The Renaissance, beginning in Italy in the 14th century, comprised the rediscovery of classical culture. The Reformation started by Martin Luther created Protestant Christianity. The Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment applied observation and experiment to understanding nature and society. Johannes Gutenberg's movable type printing in 1440 helped spread new ideas. Diseases introduced by Europeans devastated American societies, killing 60–90 million people by 1600 and reducing the population by 90–95%.

### Modern period (1800–present)

The long nineteenth century (1789–1914) saw the global spread of the Industrial Revolution, which began in Great Britain around 1770. Industrialization raised living standards but caused upheaval over wages and working conditions. European empires expanded, with Britain controlling India and other territories, while the United States completed westward expansion. By the late 19th century, European powers conquered almost all of Africa; only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent. Most of the world abolished slavery in the 19th century.

World War I (1914–1918) collapsed four empires and led to the League of Nations. The Russian Revolution of 1917 created the Soviet Union. World War II (1939–1945) involved all great powers, with Germany killing 6 million Jews and Japan murdering millions of Chinese; nuclear weapons brought unprecedented destruction. The United Nations was founded in 1945, adopting the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in 1948.

The Cold War (1945–1991) between the United States and Soviet Union ended peacefully with Soviet collapse. The second half of the 20th century saw the eradication of smallpox, the Green Revolution, moon landings, and the Internet. World population quadrupled to six billion during the 20th century, while life expectancy rose from about 31 years in 1900 to over 66 years in 2000. Decolonization gave African, Asian, and Oceanian colonies their independence. The early 21st century saw growing economic globalization, the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, and the AI boom in the early 2020s.

## Terms

- **Neolithic Revolution**: The transition beginning around 10,000 BCE from hunting and gathering to agriculture and permanent settlements
- **Axial Age**: Period from 800–200 BCE when transformative philosophical and religious ideas emerged independently in different places
- **Hellenization**: The spread of Greek culture throughout conquered regions following Alexander the Great's conquests
- **Pax Romana**: A period of unprecedented peace, prosperity, and political stability in most of Europe under Roman rule
- **Feudalism**: Medieval European system including manorialism (peasants owing rents to nobles) and vassalage (knights owing military service to overlords)
- **Columbian exchange**: The exchange of plants, animals, foods, human populations, diseases, and culture between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres
- **Great Divergence**: The debated process by which European powers rose to global dominance
- **Proto-globalization**: The early forms of global interconnection in the early modern period
- **Decolonization**: The postwar process by which African, Asian, and Oceanian colonies of European empires won formal independence

## Debates and open questions

Historians debate the causes of the Neolithic Revolution, with theories identifying population growth as either cause or effect of improved food supply, alongside climate change, resource scarcity, and ideology. The causes of Europe's rise (the Great Divergence) remain contested. Scholars also debate whether early human migrations died out and whether extinctions of megafauna and Neanderthals were caused by climate change, human activity, or both. Periodization itself is debated, with historians drawing on different approaches—political events, modes of production, inter-society relations, or religious/cultural turning points—to divide human history.
