# Yellow River

## Overview
The Yellow River (Huanghe) is China's second-longest river and the sixth-longest river system on Earth. It is the historical cradle of Chinese civilization but is also known as "China's Sorrow" due to its frequent, devastating floods, which have shaped the region's history, politics, and engineering for millennia.

## Timeline
- **c. 2100 BC** — Traditional origin of the Xia dynasty on the river's banks.
- **602 BC** — First major recorded course change, shifting east from the ancient "Yu River" path.
- **1128** — Song troops breach dikes, causing the river to shift south and merge with the Huai River for over 700 years.
- **1344** — A major flood contributes to rebellions that end the Yuan dynasty.
- **1642** — A man-made flood intended to stop rebels destroys Kaifeng, killing an estimated 300,000.
- **1855** — The river returns to a northern course, adopting its present-day path.
- **1887** — A catastrophic flood kills an estimated 900,000 to 2 million people.
- **1931** — A massive flood kills an estimated 1–4 million people.
- **1938** — Nationalist troops intentionally breach dikes to halt Japanese advance, causing a flood that kills 500,000–900,000.
- **1947** — The 1938 breach is closed, restoring the river's pre-1938 course.
- **1999** — Unified water flow regulation begins, leading to 25 consecutive years of continuous flow to the sea as of 2024.

## Body

### Cradle of Civilization and Historical Dynamics
The Yellow River basin is regarded as a cradle of Chinese civilization. The river's fertile soil supported agriculture, but its massive silt load from the Loess Plateau caused frequent, catastrophic floods. Historically, it flooded 1,593 times between 595 BC and 1946 AD, shifting its course 26 times. This duality earned it the nicknames "China's Pride" and "China's Sorrow." The river's path has been a major political and military factor, with control of key passes like Hangu Pass being strategically vital.

### Course Changes and Flood Control
The river's course has shifted dramatically throughout history. A major southern course from 1128 to 1855 saw it merge with the Huai River. Flood control has been a perennial challenge, sparking debates between "Confucian" advocates for high levees to maximize farmland and "Taoist" proponents for wider, lower levees. Traditional techniques included levees and overflow basins. In the modern era, the 1938 flood was a deliberate military tactic, and post-1949, the People's Republic of China initiated comprehensive plans for flood control and water development, including major dams.

### Modern Challenges and Management
Today, the river basin supports over 120 million people but receives only 2% of China's water runoff. Increased irrigation has caused the river to frequently run dry before reaching the sea, a trend that peaked in the 1990s. Since 1999, unified water management has restored continuous flow. The South–North Water Transfer Project aims to alleviate water shortages. Pollution is severe, with over a third of the river system deemed unfit for any use as of 2007.

### Geography and Ecology
The river flows through seven provinces and two autonomous regions, commonly divided into upper, middle, and lower reaches. The middle reaches through the Loess Plateau contribute 92% of its silt, creating the "Earth Suspended River" in the lower reaches, where the riverbed is elevated above the surrounding plain. The basin is home to over 160 native fish species, but many are threatened by dams, pollution, and introduced species. Conservation efforts include fishing bans and habitat restoration.

## Terms
- **Loess Plateau**: A highland region in north-central China covered in fine-grained, wind-deposited silt (loess), which erodes easily and gives the Yellow River its characteristic color and high sediment load.
- **Ordos Loop**: The large, rectangular-shaped bend in the river's upper-middle course through Inner Mongolia.
- **North China Plain**: A large, flat alluvial plain formed by sediment from the Yellow River, where the river's lower course is confined by levees.
- **Earth Suspended River**: A term for the lower reaches of the Yellow River, where centuries of silt deposition have raised the riverbed several meters above the surrounding land.
- **Levee**: An embankment built to prevent the overflow of a river.
- **Avulsion**: The rapid abandonment of a river channel and the formation of a new one.
- **South–North Water Transfer Project**: A massive infrastructure project to divert water from the Yangtze River to the arid north, including the Yellow River basin.
- **Sanjiangyuan**: The "Three Rivers' Sources" National Nature Reserve at the headwaters of the Yellow, Yangtze, and Mekong rivers.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historical Course Control**: There was a long-running imperial debate on whether to invest in returning the river to its old channels after a shift or to adapt to the new course.
- **Modern Water Management**: The balance between agricultural irrigation, urban water supply, ecological flow requirements, and flood control remains a critical challenge, especially given climate change and pollution.
- **Ecological Restoration**: The effectiveness of fishing bans and habitat restoration in recovering native fish populations and the overall health of the river ecosystem is an ongoing concern.
