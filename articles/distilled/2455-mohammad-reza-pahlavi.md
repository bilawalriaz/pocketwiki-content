# Mohammad Reza Pahlavi

## Overview
Mohammad Reza Pahlavi (1919–1980) was the last Shah of Iran, ruling from 1941 until the 1979 Islamic Revolution. His reign combined ambitious modernization and oil-fueled economic growth with authoritarian rule and human rights abuses. His overthrow ended the Pahlavi dynasty and established the Islamic Republic of Iran.

## Timeline
- **26 October 1919** — Born in Tehran to Reza Khan.
- **16 September 1941** — Ascends throne after father's forced abdication during Anglo-Soviet invasion.
- **19 August 1953** — Returns to power following CIA/MI6-backed coup against PM Mosaddegh.
- **1963** — Launches the White Revolution, a series of modernizing reforms.
- **26 October 1967** — Takes the title Shahanshah ("King of Kings").
- **October 1971** — Hosts celebrations for the 2,500-year anniversary of the Persian Empire at Persepolis.
- **1973** — Leads OPEC in a major oil price hike, dramatically increasing Iran's revenue.
- **1975** — Abolishes the two-party system, establishing the single-party Rastakhiz Party.
- **8 September 1978** — The Jaleh Square massacre (Black Friday) kills protesters, turning opposition against the regime.
- **16 January 1979** — Leaves Iran for exile.
- **11 February 1979** — Monarchy formally dissolved after military declares neutrality.
- **27 July 1980** — Dies of cancer in exile in Cairo, Egypt.

## Body

### Early Life and Ascension
Born to Reza Khan, founder of the Pahlavi dynasty, Mohammad Reza was proclaimed Crown Prince in 1926. Educated in Switzerland, he developed a lifelong Francophilia. He ascended the throne in 1941 during WWII after the Anglo-Soviet invasion forced his father's abdication. His early reign was marked by a lack of self-confidence and struggle to assert authority against powerful prime ministers and foreign powers.

### Consolidation of Power and the 1953 Coup
The defining event of his early rule was the 1953 coup. After PM Mohammad Mosaddegh nationalized British-owned oil, the Shah, with CIA/MI6 support, dismissed him. After a failed first attempt and his flight, he returned following a successful second coup. This restored him to power but made him reliant on Western support. He spent the 1950s and early 1960s skillfully playing political factions against each other to transform from a figurehead into an effective autocrat.

### The White Revolution and Modernization
In 1963, the Shah launched the White Revolution, a top-down reform program. Key measures included land redistribution, profit-sharing for industrial workers, and women's suffrage. These reforms, alongside massive state investment in infrastructure, education, and industry, spurred decades of rapid economic growth. However, they alienated the Shia clergy and traditional elites, while unevenly distributed benefits fueled social discontent.

### Apex of Power and Foreign Policy
By the 1970s, the Shah's power was at its zenith. He took the title Shahanshah in 1967 and used oil wealth to build the world's fifth-strongest military. He dominated OPEC, orchestrating the 1973 oil price hike. His assertive foreign policy included settling border disputes with Iraq in the 1975 Algiers Accords. His relationship with the U.S., solidified under the Nixon Doctrine, made Iran a key anti-Soviet ally, though he increasingly dictated terms.

### Downfall and Revolution
The regime faced growing opposition from Islamists, leftists, and nationalists angered by repression, corruption, and Westernizing policies. A 1978 newspaper article attacking Ayatollah Khomeini sparked protests that escalated into nationwide strikes. The Shah's health deteriorated due to undiagnosed cancer, leading to indecisiveness. The Jaleh Square massacre in September 1978 destroyed any hope of compromise. After Western allies indicated they would not save him at the 1979 Guadeloupe Conference, he left Iran in January 1979. The monarchy was abolished weeks later.

### Exile and Death
The Shah spent his final months in exile, moving from Egypt to Morocco, the Bahamas, Mexico, and the U.S., where he received cancer treatment. His presence in the U.S. contributed to the Iran hostage crisis. He was eventually granted asylum in Egypt by President Anwar Sadat. A botched surgery led to a fatal infection, and he died on 27 July 1980. He was given a state funeral in Cairo and buried in the Al Rifa'i Mosque.

## Terms
- **Shahanshah**: "King of Kings," an ancient Persian imperial title adopted in 1967.
- **White Revolution**: Modernizing reforms launched in 1963, including land reform and women's suffrage.
- **SAVAK**: The Shah's secret police, notorious for repression and torture of dissidents.
- **OPEC**: Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries; the Shah used it to raise oil prices in 1973.
- **Rastakhiz Party**: Single political party established in 1975, with compulsory membership.
- **Nixon Doctrine**: U.S. policy (1969) encouraging allies to handle their own defense, leading to massive U.S. arms sales to Iran.
- **Algiers Accords**: 1975 agreement with Iraq settling border disputes, notably over the Shatt al-Arab waterway.
- **Aryamehr**: "Light of the Aryans," a title bestowed in 1965 to emphasize pre-Islamic Iranian identity.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Role of the U.S.**: Scholars debate the extent of U.S. influence, especially after 1953. While initially seen as a "puppet," later analysis shows the Shah often acted independently, especially in the 1970s.
- **Causes of the Revolution**: Historians weigh factors like economic inequality, political repression, the Shah's health, clergy opposition, and Ayatollah Khomeini's mobilizing power.
- **Economic Legacy**: The era saw unprecedented growth, but critics argue it was uneven, fueled by unsustainable oil revenues, and created inflation and social dislocation contributing to the revolution.
- **Human Rights Record**: The extent and systematic nature of torture and repression under SAVAK remain a central point of condemnation.
