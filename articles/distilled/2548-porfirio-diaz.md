# Porfirio Díaz

## Overview
Porfirio Díaz was a Mexican general and politician who ruled as dictator from 1876 to 1911, a period known as the Porfiriato. His regime ended decades of political instability and fostered significant economic growth through foreign investment, but at the cost of extreme social inequality and the suppression of civil liberties. His failure to manage a political succession and his reversal on democratic promises directly triggered the Mexican Revolution.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Career
Born to a modest Oaxacan family, Díaz initially studied for the priesthood before switching to law, where he was mentored by future president Benito Juárez. He became active in the Liberal Party, fighting in the Plan of Ayutla (1854-55) and the Reform War (1857-61). During the Second French Intervention, he gained fame for his role in the Battle of Puebla (1862). He later commanded Republican forces in southern Mexico, was captured by the French in 1865, and escaped twice. He ultimately captured Mexico City in 1867, helping to restore the Republic.

### Rise to Power
After the French withdrawal, Díaz rebelled against Presidents Juárez and Lerdo de Tejada on the principle of "no re-election." He succeeded in a coup in 1876 via the Plan of Tuxtepec, which criticized electoral fraud. After a term, his ally Manuel González served as president (1880-84). In 1884, Díaz abandoned the "no re-election" principle, had the constitution amended to allow it, and remained in power continuously until 1911.

### The Porfiriato: Politics and Economics
Díaz's regime, known as the Porfiriato, was a de facto dictatorship that maintained a façade of liberal democracy. He established the "Paz Porfiriana" (Porfirian Peace) through a mix of co-optation ("bread") and repression ("bludgeon"). His government was dominated by a group of technocrats called the *científicos* ("scientists"). Economically, his policies of "defensive modernization" attracted massive foreign investment, particularly in railroads, mining, and large-scale agriculture (*haciendas*). This spurred growth but also led to the violent dispossession of communal peasant and Indigenous lands, creating severe social inequality.

### Relations with the Church and Military
Unlike previous liberal governments, Díaz was not anti-clerical. He pragmatically left the anti-clerical Reform Laws on the books but did not enforce them, allowing the Catholic Church to regain significant economic and social influence. Regarding the military, he systematically reduced the power of provincial strongmen by centralizing control, rotating commanders, and expanding the presidential police force, the *Rurales*. While his cabinet was initially military-dominated, it became increasingly civilian over time.

### Downfall and Exile
In 1908, Díaz told a U.S. journalist that Mexico was ready for democracy and he would retire. This inspired opposition, including from reformer Francisco I. Madero. However, Díaz reversed his position, ran for an eighth term in 1910, and had Madero jailed. After being declared the winner amid massive fraud, Madero issued the Plan of San Luis Potosí, calling for armed rebellion. The ensuing Mexican Revolution forced Díaz to resign in May 1911 via the Treaty of Ciudad Juárez. He died in exile in Paris in 1915.

## Terms
*   **Porfiriato:** The period of Porfirio Díaz's rule (1876-1911), characterized by political stability and economic growth under an authoritarian regime.
*   **Científicos:** A group of technocrats and advisors in Díaz's government who promoted economic modernization and foreign investment.
*   **Paz Porfiriana:** The "Porfirian Peace," the internal order and stability Díaz established to enable economic development, maintained through both patronage and repression.
*   **Hacienda:** A large landed estate, often consolidated through legal abuse and violence during the Porfiriato, frequently owned by foreign investors.
*   **Rurales:** A rural police force under direct presidential control, used to suppress banditry and enforce order.
*   **Plan of Tuxtepec:** The 1876 revolutionary manifesto led by Díaz that ousted President Lerdo de Tejada, focusing on opposition to re-election and electoral fraud.
*   **Plan of San Luis Potosí:** The 1910 document issued by Francisco I. Madero calling for armed rebellion against Díaz after the fraudulent election.
*   **Reform Laws:** Liberal laws from the 1850s that curtailed the Catholic Church's privileges and landholdings; Díaz left them in place but did not enforce them.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Legacy Revisionism:** Díaz's historical image has shifted from a tyrant to a more benign figure in some modern analyses ("Neo-Porfirismo"), especially as his economic policies align with later neoliberal reforms.
*   **Succession Failure:** A central debate is whether Díaz's regime collapsed primarily because he failed to institutionalize a process for presidential succession, creating a power vacuum.
*   **Economic Impact:** Scholars debate whether the economic growth of the Porfiriato was a genuine national development or primarily a transfer of wealth to foreign investors and a small Mexican elite, at the direct expense of the rural masses.
