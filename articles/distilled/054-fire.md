# Fire

## Overview

Fire is the rapid oxidation of a fuel in the exothermic chemical process of combustion, releasing heat, light, and various reaction products. Fire, in its most common form, has the potential to result in conflagration, which can lead to permanent physical damage. Fire directly affects land-based ecological systems worldwide. The benefits of fire include stimulating plant growth and maintaining ecological balance. Its harms include hazards to life and property, atmospheric pollution, and water contamination. When fire removes protective vegetation, heavy rainfall can cause soil erosion. The burning of vegetation releases nitrogen into the atmosphere, unlike other plant nutrients such as potassium and phosphorus, which remain in the ash and are quickly recycled into the soil. This loss of nitrogen produces a long-term reduction in the fertility of the soil. However, it can be recovered by nitrogen-fixing plants—some of them of agricultural value, such as clover, peas, and beans, by decomposition of animal waste and corpses, and by natural phenomena such as lightning.

Fire is one of the four classical elements and has been used throughout human history for purposes that now include cooking, generating heat and light, clearing land for agriculture, signaling, propulsion, smelting, forging, incineration of waste, cremation, rituals, weapons, and destruction, including in warfare.

## Timeline

- **470 million years ago** — Land plants emerge in the Middle Ordovician, raising atmospheric oxygen.
- **420 million years ago** — First wildfire recorded in the Late Silurian fossil record via charred plants.
- **6–7 million years ago** — Grasses dominate ecosystems, providing tinder for more rapid fire spread.
- **1 million years ago** — Occasional cooked food evidence suggests controlled fire use.
- **c. 400,000 years ago** — Oldest clear evidence of deliberate firemaking at Barnfield, England (pyrite struck against flint).
- **50–100 thousand years ago** — Widespread evidence of regular fire use; resistance to air pollution evolves in humans.
- **c. 1000 CE** — Fire lance, a flame-thrower precursor to gunpowder weapons, appears in China.
- **February 1915** — First modern flamethrowers used by German troops near Verdun in World War I.
- **1930s** — Molotov cocktails deployed during the Spanish Civil War; incendiary bombs hit Guernica.
- **1944** — Napalm used in July, late in World War II; later gains attention in the Vietnam War.

## Body

### Chemistry and physics

Fire is a chemical process in which a fuel and an oxidizing agent react, yielding carbon dioxide and water, proceeding through intermediates rather than directly. The oxidizing agent is typically oxygen, but other compounds can fulfill the role—for instance, chlorine trifluoride is able to ignite sand. Fires start when a flammable or combustible material, with sufficient oxidizer, is exposed to heat above the flash point and sustains rapid oxidation producing a chain reaction. This is commonly called the fire tetrahedron: fuel, oxidizer, heat, and chain reaction. Fire cannot exist without all of these elements in place and in the right proportions. Some fuel-oxygen mixes may require a catalyst, a substance that is not consumed, when added, in any chemical reaction during combustion, but which enables the reactants to combust more readily.

Once ignited, fires sustain their own heat through continued release of heat energy and may propagate, provided there is a continuous supply of an oxidizer and fuel. Gravity is necessary for convection, which removes combustion products and brings oxygen; without gravity, a fire rapidly surrounds itself with its own combustion products and non-oxidizing gases, which exclude oxygen and extinguish the fire, making spacecraft fire risk small during inertial flight. Fire can be extinguished by removing any tetrahedron element: turning off the fuel supply, covering the flame completely to smother it, application of an inert gas such as carbon dioxide, application of water to remove heat faster than the fire can produce it, or application of a retardant chemical such as Halon (largely banned in some countries as of 2023). Fire intensifies with stoichiometric fuel-oxidizer balance, increased input, higher ambient temperature, or catalysts.

### Flames

A diffusion flame emits visible, infrared, and sometimes ultraviolet light, with frequency spectrum depending on the chemical composition of the burning material and intermediate reaction products. Hydrocarbon burning or incomplete gas combustion produces soot—incandescent solid particles—giving the familiar red-orange glow with a continuous spectrum. Complete gas combustion has a dim blue color due to single-wavelength radiation from electron transitions in excited molecules. Oxygen is usual, but hydrogen burning in chlorine produces hydrogen chloride; other combinations include fluorine with hydrogen, and hydrazine with dinitrogen tetroxide. Boron burns with an intense green flame, nicknamed "Green Dragon."

Flame glow combines black-body radiation from soot, gas, and fuel particles (though soot particles are too small to behave like perfect blackbodies) with photon emission from de-excited atoms and molecules. Color depends on temperature for black-body radiation and chemical makeup for emission spectra. Under normal gravity, convection makes soot rise, as in a candle, producing yellow flames. In microgravity, flames become spherical, more blue, and more efficient, though they may go out as CO2 from combustion does not disperse as readily. There are several possible explanations for this difference, the most likely being that the temperature is sufficiently uniform that soot does not form and complete combustion occurs. NASA experiments show microgravity diffusion flames allow more soot to be completely oxidized, with potential fuel-efficiency applications.

Typical adiabatic flame temperatures (stable combustion temperature for a fuel-oxidizer pair) include: oxy–dicyanoacetylene 4,990 °C; oxy–acetylene 3,997 °C; oxyhydrogen 3,473 °C; air–acetylene 2,500 °C; blowtorch (air–MAPP gas) 2,020 °C; Bunsen burner (air–natural gas) 1,300–1,600 °C; candle (air–paraffin) 1,000 °C.

### History and human control

The fossil record shows fire first became possible when land plants raised atmospheric oxygen above 13%, with wildfire first recorded 420 million years ago. Oxygen levels correlate closely with charcoal in the fossil record. Fire became more abundant with grasses 6–7 million years ago, potentially initiating a positive feedback process producing warmer, drier climates. The period of history characterized by the influence of human-caused fire activity on Earth has been dubbed the pyrocene. This epoch includes the burning of fossil fuels, especially for technological uses.

Controlling fire changed early human habits: cooking increased nutrient variety and reduced disease by killing pathogens, heat enabled living in cooler climates, and fire kept nocturnal predators at bay. By the Neolithic Revolution, people used fire as a landscape management tool, employing controlled "cool fires" in spring and autumn to clear undergrowth and encourage diversity, versus uncontrolled "hot fires" that damage soil. Slash-and-burn agriculture remains common in tropical Africa, Asia, and South America, though population growth, forest fragmentation, and warming climate make escaped fires more likely. Globally, as much as 5 million square kilometres burns in a given year.

In the 17th century, Jan Baptist van Helmont discovered burning charcoal released a gas he called sylvestris. This fed Phlogiston theory, formalized by Johann Joachim Becher in 1667 and Georg Ernst Stahl in 1697. Antoine Lavoisier demonstrated combustion involved uptake, not release, and in 1777 proposed a theory based on reaction with a component of air, which he termed oxygène; by 1791, his concepts had been widely adopted by young scientists, and the Phlogiston theory had been rejected. Fire has also been used for centuries as a method of torture and execution, including death by burning and the heated iron boot.

### Fire in war and energy

Fire was the basis of all early thermal weapons, including incendiary devices and heated projectiles, prominent in naval battles and sieges—the Byzantine fleet used Greek fire. Gunpowder in China led to the fire lance around 1000 CE. Modern flamethrowers first appeared in World War I at Verdun in February 1915, later mounted on armored vehicles in World War II. Incendiary bombs were dropped on Coventry, Tokyo, Rotterdam, London, Hamburg, and Dresden; firestorms were deliberately caused in the latter two, where a ring of fire was drawn inward by updraft. The U.S. Army Air Force devastated Japanese cities built of wood and paper. Napalm was used from July 1944.

Burning fuel converts chemical energy to heat; wood has been used as fuel since prehistory. The International Energy Agency states nearly 80% of world power has consistently come from fossil fuels. Power station fires heat water into steam driving turbines that spin generators. Fire also provides mechanical work via thermal expansion in external and internal combustion engines. Unburnable solid remains are called clinker if melting point is below flame temperature (fusing then solidifying) and ash if above.

### Ecology, firefighting, and prevention

Every natural land ecosystem has its own fire regime, with organisms adapted to or dependent upon it. Fire creates a mosaic of habitat patches at different succession stages, allowing more species to exist within a landscape. Climate changes can significantly modify wildfire risk. Firefighting services in developed areas use fire apparatus, water mains, hydrants, and A and B class foam. Fire lookout towers peaked in 1938 and declined since; infrared sensors and aircraft now perform most surveillance.

Fire management—controlling size, shape, and intensity—is a skilled activity involving fuel choice, arrangement, stoking, heat modulation, and banking. Wildfire prevention uses wildland fire use (monitored natural fires) and prescribed burns under less dangerous weather. Fire prevention reduces ignition sources and includes education; buildings conduct fire drills. Arson is a crime in most jurisdictions. Model building codes require passive and active fire protection, including sprinklers; materials are tested for fire-resistance, combustibility, and flammability. Studies show functioning smoke detectors reduce death risk by 50%, and sprinklers can reduce mortality by 100%. In the U.S., cooking equipment is the leading cause of residential fires; cigarettes cause about 28% of home fires involving upholstered furniture and 58% of fatalities in such fires. Fire-safe cigarettes are associated with a 45% reduction in cigarette-caused fires. Fire insurance mitigates financial impact where prevention fails.

### Culture

Fire has been an important element of human culture since the Lower Paleolithic. Fire worship dates to prehistory, with dedicated structures from at least the Chalcolithic period; Zoroastrianism is closely linked. Some societies saw fire as a deity or divine manifestation; the hearth fire symbolized Heavenly Fire. In Greek myth, Prometheus stole heavenly fire for humanity. Pyre funerary practices date to Ancient Roman times in the West and about 4,000 years ago on the Indian subcontinent; cremation became widespread in the 19th century. Bonfires mark traditions like Guy Fawkes Night in England; barbecue is a U.S. cultural tradition; fireworks celebrate New Year. Book burning serves as protest, as does burning in effigy. Humans lack an instinctual fascination with fire; children in fire-dependent societies lose interest around age 7. Arson is intentional fire-setting; pyromania is an impulse-control disorder to start fires; pyrophobia is an irrational fear, a less common phobia.

## Terms

- **Combustion**: Exothermic chemical reaction between fuel and oxidizer releasing heat and light.
- **Fire tetrahedron**: Four required elements for fire—fuel, oxidizer, heat, and chain reaction.
- **Flash point**: Temperature above which a fuel/oxidizer mix can ignite.
- **Diffusion flame**: Flame where reacting gases and solids emit light; soot produces red-orange glow.
- **Adiabatic flame temperature**: Temperature at which a fuel-oxidizer pair achieves stable combustion.
- **Phlogiston theory**: Discredited 17th–18th century concept that combustion released a substance; replaced by Lavoisier's oxygen-based theory.
- **Fire regime**: The characteristic pattern of fire in an ecosystem to which organisms are adapted.
- **Pyrocene**: Proposed epoch characterized by human-caused fire activity, including fossil fuel burning.
- **Pyromania**: Impulse-control disorder involving repeated failure to resist starting fires deliberately.
- **Pyrophobia**: Irrational fear of fire, a less common anxiety disorder.

## Debates and open questions

The fossil record shows a controversial gap in the Late Devonian where charcoal is absent, with scholars debating its cause. There are several possible explanations for the difference in microgravity flames' efficiency and color, the most likely being that the temperature is sufficiently uniform that soot does not form and complete combustion occurs. The balance between fire's ecological benefits (stimulating plant growth, maintaining diversity) and harms (soil fertility loss through nitrogen release, erosion, pollution) continues to inform management debates, particularly regarding traditional cool fires versus fire suppression in modern timber forests.
