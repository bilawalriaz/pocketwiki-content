# Choir

## Overview
A choir is a musical ensemble of singers, with choral music being the repertoire written for such groups. Choirs are fundamental to both sacred and secular musical traditions, performing a wide range of music from classical to popular genres, often led by a conductor. Their structure, history, and role in worship and education make them a significant and enduring cultural institution.

## Timeline
- **c. 2nd century BC** — The Delphic hymns, among the oldest surviving choral music, are composed in ancient Greece.
- **4th–6th century** — The tradition of Gregorian chant, unison choir singing, is established in the Catholic Church.
- **c. 1420** — The Old Hall Manuscript provides the first evidence of polyphony with more than one singer per part.
- **Renaissance era** — Sacred choral music, particularly a cappella polyphony, becomes the principal notated music in Western Europe.
- **c. 1600** — The Baroque period begins, developing the figured bass and stile concertato, which combines voices and instruments.
- **1730** — J.S. Bach submits his "Entwurff" memo to the Leipzig City Council, detailing requirements for church music.
- **Late 18th century** — Composers like Haydn and Mozart revive large-scale choral works, including masses and oratorios.
- **19th century** — Sacred music moves to the concert stage; amateur "singing clubs" and part-songs flourish.
- **20th century** — Mixed choirs are pioneered in the U.S.; secular choral music gains popularity.
- **2010s** — In English cathedral choirs, female choristers outnumber male choristers for the first time.

## Body

### Structure and Performance
Choirs are typically led by a conductor, who unifies performers, sets tempo, and shapes the ensemble's sound. The most common structure is a four-part harmony (soprano, alto, tenor, bass), though works can have many more parts. Choirs can perform a cappella (without instruments) or with accompaniment ranging from a single piano to a full orchestra. The term "choir" is often used for church-affiliated groups, while "chorus" may imply a larger ensemble in a concert hall, though the distinction is not rigid.

### Role in Worship
Historically, choral music is divided into sacred and secular. Early Christian consensus favored unaccompanied human voice in worship, a practice still followed by Eastern Orthodox churches and some Protestant and Jewish groups. In Western Rite churches, the organ is the typical accompanying instrument. Church choirs lead congregational singing and may perform full liturgies, including propers specific to the liturgical year. The Second Vatican Council affirmed that choirs exercise a "genuine liturgical function" in services like the Mass.

### Types of Choirs
Choirs are classified by the gender and age of their members. The most common type is the adult mixed choir (SATB). Other types include male choirs (with boys singing treble parts), men's choruses (TTBB), boys' choirs, women's choirs (SSAA), and children's choirs. Choirs are also categorized by their institution (e.g., church, school, professional) or the music they perform (e.g., gospel, barbershop, show choir). In U.S. schools, choir participation often declines among male students during adolescence due to voice change and scheduling issues.

### Historical Development
Choral music's origins lie in ancient Greek drama and unison singing. Medieval music saw the development of Gregorian chant and later polyphony. The Renaissance was a golden age for a cappella sacred polyphony. The Baroque era introduced instrumental accompaniment and new genres like the oratorio and cantata, with composers like Monteverdi and Bach expanding choral possibilities. The Classical and Romantic periods saw a decline in choral focus before a revival of large-scale sacred works for the concert stage. The 20th century saw the rise of mixed choirs and the popularization of secular choral music.

## Terms
- **Choir/Chorus**: A musical ensemble of singers.
- **A cappella**: Singing without instrumental accompaniment.
- **Conductor**: The director of a musical performance, using gestures to guide tempo and interpretation.
- **SATB**: Abbreviation for soprano, alto, tenor, bass—the standard four-part voice classification.
- **Polyphony**: Music with two or more independent melodic lines.
- **Motet**: A choral composition, often sacred, typically for a cappella voices.
- **Oratorio**: A large-scale musical work for orchestra, choir, and soloists, usually on a sacred theme.
- **Cantata**: A vocal composition with instrumental accompaniment, often in several movements.
- **Basso continuo**: A Baroque-era accompaniment part providing harmonic foundation, typically played by a chordal instrument and a bass instrument.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Rifkin Hypothesis**: A scholarly debate questions whether J.S. Bach's choral music was intended to be performed with one singer per part, rather than with larger choral forces.
- **Male Participation**: Research continues to explore why male participation in school choirs is lower than female participation and what solutions might address this imbalance.
- **Historical Performance Practice**: There is ongoing dispute over the role of instruments in Renaissance choral music and the extent of their use.
