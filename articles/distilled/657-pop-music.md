# Pop music

## Overview

Pop music is a genre of popular music that originated in its modern form during the mid-1950s in the United States and the United Kingdom. Initially synonymous with rock and roll, pop music became a distinct category from the late 1960s onward, characterized by its commercial orientation, accessibility, and emphasis on light entertainment rather than artistic ambition. It is defined by repeated choruses and hooks, verse-chorus structures, danceable rhythms, and songs typically lasting two to three and a half minutes. Pop music draws from numerous other genres—including rock, hip-hop, electronic, Latin, and country—and has evolved through technological advances in recording, radio, television, and digital streaming, becoming a dominant global cultural force.

## Timeline

- **1926** — Term "pop song" first used to describe music with popular appeal
- **Mid-1950s** — "Pop music" originates in Britain as label for rock and roll and related youth styles
- **Late 1950s** — Pop music distinguished from rock music; associated with commercial, ephemeral appeal
- **1960s** — Transistor radios expand pop's reach; MTV emerges in early 1980s to emphasize visual appeal
- **1967** — Division between "progressive" pop and mass/chart pop solidifies
- **1980s** — Digital recording and synthesizers give rise to synth-pop and electronic influences
- **2000s** — Internet downloads and piracy reshape music industry; pop begins blending genres
- **2010s** — Dance-influenced "recession pop" and moody alternative pop dominate charts
- **2020s** — Streaming platforms elevate artists like the Weeknd and Olivia Rodrigo to global prominence

## Body

### Definitions and Etymology

The term "pop music" originated in Britain in the mid-1950s as a description for rock and roll and the new youth music styles it influenced. While earlier meanings referred to concerts appealing to wide audiences, from the late 1950s onward, "pop" came to signify non-classical music performed by artists such as The Beatles and The Rolling Stones. Musicologist Simon Frith defines pop music as produced "as a matter of enterprise not art," designed to appeal to everyone, but "doesn't come from any particular place or mark off any particular taste." Frith adds that it is "not driven by any significant ambition except profit and commercial reward ... and, in musical terms, it is essentially conservative." It is "provided from on high (by record companies, radio programmers, and concert promoters) rather than being made from below. ... Pop is not a do-it-yourself music but is professionally produced and packaged." The term competed with "beat music" in England during the early 1960s and overlapped with "rock and roll" in the US.

### Characteristics

Pop music aims to appeal to a general audience rather than specific subcultures or ideologies. Frith identifies three core traits: light entertainment, commercial imperatives, and personal identification. Songs are typically short (two to three and a half minutes), structured around verse-chorus forms, and feature catchy hooks, simple melodies, and danceable beats. Lyrics often focus on love and relationships, avoiding political or resistant themes. Harmony tends to follow classical European tonality but simplified, with common progressions like ii–V–I and blues-scale influences. A 2016 study found contemporary pop uses a smaller variety of pitch progressions, greater average volume, less diverse instrumentation and recording techniques, and less timbral variety than 1960s pop.

### Technology and Media

Improved microphone design in the 1940s enabled intimate vocal styles, while 45 rpm records in the 1950s revolutionized single distribution. Television in the 1950s required pop stars to cultivate visual presence. The 1960s brought portable transistor radios, expanding teen access. MTV's rise in the early 1980s favored visually striking artists like Michael Jackson and Madonna. Multi-track recording and digital sampling further shaped production techniques. By the late 20th century, the boundary between art and pop blurred, leading to increased recognition of pop's legitimacy through movements like "poptimism."

### Stylistic Evolution

Early pop drew from traditional pop, Tin Pan Alley, Broadway, and show tunes. The 1960s saw experimentation with orchestration, reverb, and effects—exemplified by Phil Spector's Wall of Sound. Producers gained creative control during the mid-1960s economic boom, allowing artistic risks. The 1980s introduced synthesizers and electronic genres. In the 2000s, genre boundaries blurred, with Britney Spears' *Blackout* mixing EDM, R&B, and hip-hop. The 2010s emphasized dance-influenced "recession pop" and emotionally introspective alternative pop from artists like Billie Eilish and Taylor Swift.

### International Spread and Crosspollination

Pop music has been dominated by American and British industries, creating an international monoculture. However, regional variations exist worldwide, including Japan's prolific Western-style pop industry and Latin pop's global breakthrough via hits like "Despacito." Non-Western adoption reflects processes of globalization, modernization, and cultural exchange. Latin pop rose in the U.S. through figures like Ritchie Valens, Los Lobos and Chicano rock, Selena, and Los del Río. Global superstars of the late 20th century include Whitney Houston, Michael Jackson, Madonna, Prince, and George Michael.

### 21st Century

The 2000s marked a shift toward digital consumption, with file-sharing disrupting traditional revenue models. Genre blending intensified, exemplified by Spears' *Blackout*. By 2010, club culture influenced chart trends, giving rise to "recession pop." The 2010s saw alternative pop gain traction, with moody, emotionally complex artists gaining mainstream success. In the 2020s, streaming platforms have elevated new stars like the Weeknd, whose "Blinding Lights" became the most-streamed song on Spotify, signaling pop's continued dominance in the digital age.

## Terms

- **Verse–chorus structure**: A song format alternating verses and repeating choruses, central to most pop music.
- **Hook**: A memorable musical or lyrical phrase designed to grab listener attention.
- **Wall of Sound**: Production technique pioneered by Phil Spector using dense layering of instruments for a full sound.
- **Synth-pop**: Subgenre using synthesizers as primary instruments, prominent in the 1980s.
- **Poptimism**: Critical approach embracing pop music's artistic value and cultural significance.
- **Recession pop**: Upbeat dance-oriented pop reflecting escapism during economic downturns, notably post-2008.
- **Europop**: Regional pop style originating in Europe, sometimes achieving global popularity.
- **Latin pop**: Spanish-language pop incorporating Latin American musical elements.
- **Multi-track recording**: Technique allowing separate recording of multiple sound sources for mixing.
- **Timbre**: Quality of sound distinguishing different types of voices or instruments.

## Debates and Open Questions

Defining pop music remains contentious due to its lack of fixed sonic identity. As Billboard noted in 2023, "pop" fundamentally means "popular," so any widely successful song can be labeled pop, making genre boundaries fluid. Scholars debate whether pop should be viewed as a distinct genre or a marketing category. Additionally, questions persist about pop's artistic merit versus commercial function, with some critics arguing it lacks depth while others defend its cultural impact and innovation. The role of technology in shaping pop's evolution—from vinyl to streaming—also continues to generate discussion regarding authenticity and accessibility.
