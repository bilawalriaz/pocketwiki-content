# Nizam al-Mulk

## Overview
Niẓām al-Mulk (1018–1092) was a Persian scholar and vizier who served as the de facto ruler of the Seljuk Empire for twenty years. He is a pivotal figure in medieval Islamic statecraft, known for establishing a vast network of schools (the Niẓāmiyyas) and authoring the *Siyāsatnāma* (Book of Government), a treatise on political administration. His policies and bureaucratic frameworks profoundly shaped Perso-Islamic governance for centuries.

## Timeline
- **1018** — Born in Radkan, near Tus, Iran.
- **c. 1040** — Follows his father to Ghazni after the Seljuk defeat of the Ghaznavids.
- **c. 1043** — Enters the service of the Seljuk Turks.
- **1059** — Becomes chief administrator of the Khorasan province.
- **1064** — Helps Alp Arslan secure the Seljuk throne; is appointed vizier and given the title Niẓām al-Mulk.
- **1071** — Misses the Battle of Manzikert while on a separate campaign.
- **1072** — Becomes the de facto ruler after Sultan Alp Arslan's assassination.
- **1074** — Helps Sultan Malik-Shāh I defeat the rival claimant Kavurt.
- **1092** — Assassinated by a member of the Assassins (Ismāʿīlīs) on 14 October.

## Body

### Rise to Power and Administrative Reforms
Born to a *dehqan* (landowning) family, Niẓām al-Mulk first served the Ghaznavids before joining the Seljuks around 1043. He rose to become the chief administrator of Khorasan by 1059. After helping Alp Arslan secure the throne in 1064, he was appointed vizier, a post he held under both Alp Arslan and his son Malik-Shāh I. Niẓām al-Mulk was the architect of the Seljuk state's administrative organization. He established military *iqṭāʿ* (fiefs) to support the army and integrate nomadic Turks with sedentary Iranian populations, creating a sustainable economic base for the empire.

### Policies and Political Objectives
As the dominant power behind the throne, Niẓām al-Mulk's policies aimed to stabilize the empire. He contained the Ghaznavids, rolled back the Fatimids in Syria, and maintained the Abbasid Caliphs as symbolic figures. His objectives included employing nomadic Turkmens to prevent instability, demonstrating the Sultan's power, using relatives as provincial governors, and preventing succession disputes. He favored Iranian soldiers like the Dailamites and Khorasanis in the military. His power was such that he could challenge the Sultan, as seen in his famous speech warning Malik-Shāh that the stability of the royal "cap" was bound to his "vizierial inkstand."

### Legacy: The Niẓāmiyyas and the *Siyāsatnāma*
Niẓām al-Mulk's most enduring legacies are institutional and literary. He systematically founded a network of higher education schools across the empire, known as the Niẓāmiyyas, in cities including Baghdad, Isfahan, and Nishapur. He also authored the *Siyāsatnāma* (Book of Government), a political treatise using historical anecdotes to discuss justice, effective rule, and the necessity of state surveillance. The work was accepted as a constitutional guide for the nation. He was also known for his piety, charity, and affinity for Sufis and scholars.

### Death and the "Three Schoolfellows" Legend
Niẓām al-Mulk was assassinated in 1092 by a member of the Assassins, a sect led by Ḥasan-i Ṣabbāḥ. A popular but likely apocryphal story, the "Three Schoolfellows," claims he, Omar Khayyam, and Ḥasan-i Ṣabbāh were friends who made a pact to help each other. Scholars doubt this, noting the three were not of similar ages and probably were not students together.

## Terms
- **Vizier:** The chief minister or advisor to a ruler in an Islamic state.
- **Dehqan:** A member of the landowning gentry class in pre-Islamic and early Islamic Iran.
- **Iqṭāʿ:** A form of land grant or fief given to military officials in lieu of a salary.
- **Niẓāmiyya:** A network of institutions of higher learning founded by Niẓām al-Mulk.
- **Siyāsatnāma:** "Book of Government," a political treatise on statecraft and administration.
- **Ghulam:** A slave soldier, often of Turkic origin, who served in Islamic armies.
- **Atabeg:** A title for a guardian or regent, often a senior military commander.
- **Divan:** The bureaucratic administration or council of state.

## Debates and Open Questions
The historicity of the "Three Schoolfellows" story involving Niẓām al-Mulk, Omar Khayyam, and Ḥasan-i Ṣabbāh is widely disputed by scholars due to chronological inconsistencies. The exact nature and extent of Niẓām al-Mulk's personal piety and his relationship with Sufi orders are subjects of historical interpretation. While he is portrayed as the archetypal "good vizier," the balance between his administrative genius and his ruthless political maneuvering (such as the poisoning of Kavurt) remains a point of analysis.
