# Prithvi Narayan Shah

## Overview
Prithvi Narayan Shah (1723–1775) was the King of Gorkha who initiated the Unification of Nepal, transforming a fragmented collection of over 50 small kingdoms into a single, centralized state. His military campaigns and strategic policies laid the foundation for modern Nepal, earning him the title "Father of the Nation." His legacy is central to Nepalese national identity and geopolitical thought.

## Body

### Early Life and Ascension
Born prematurely in 1723, Prithvi Narayan Shah was groomed for rule under the strict guidance of his mother, who isolated him from hedonistic pursuits. He married Indra Kumari Devi of Makwanpur in 1737. After his father's death in 1743, he became King of Gorkha at age 20. He valued direct communication with his people and pursued a policy of sealed borders and friendly diplomacy with immediate neighbors, except the East India Company.

### Unification Campaign and Strategy
Before Shah's campaigns, Nepal comprised 54 states, including the Baisi and Chaubise Rajya, the three Sen states, and the Malla kingdoms of the Kathmandu Valley. Shah's strategy involved diplomatic isolation of enemies, economic blockades, and military conquest. His first major success was capturing Nuwakot in 1744, a vital commercial link to Tibet. He later conquered Makwanpur (1763), bringing him into conflict with the Nawab of Bengal, whose forces were ambushed and destroyed. His repeated attacks on Kirtipur, a strategic post commanding the valley, culminated in victory in 1765 after two earlier defeats.

### Conquest of the Kathmandu Valley
Shah exploited chronic dissensions among the Malla rulers. He gained the support of Bhadgaon's Ranjit Malla against his rivals, then took possession of the city in 1767. He besieged Kathmandu and Patan, creating a crippling blockade. The desperate Malla ruler, Jaya Prakash Malla, sought military aid from the East India Company. The Company dispatched the Kinloch expedition in 1767, but it failed catastrophically due to flash floods, a Gorkhali counter-blockade, and the treacherous terrain. This failure allowed Shah to capture all three valley cities by 1768. He entered Kathmandu unopposed on 25 September 1768.

### Governance and Philosophy
Towards the end of his life, Shah compiled his political and social philosophy into the *Divyopadesh*. He defined Nepal's geopolitical position as "a yam between two boulders" (between China and British India). A staunch Hindu, he expelled European missionaries and declared Nepal the "Asal Hindustan" (Authentic Land of the Hindus). His economic policy emphasized absolute nationalism, import substitution, and the protection of national wealth from foreign merchants. He founded the modern Nepal Army in 1762 and fortified strategic passes and heights.

## Terms
- **Unification of Nepal**: The 18th-century process led by Prithvi Narayan Shah to consolidate dozens of small kingdoms into a single sovereign state.
- **Baisi Rajya & Chaubise Rajya**: The 22 and 24 kingdoms, respectively, that existed in western Nepal before unification.
- **Malla Kings**: The ruling dynasties of the three principal cities in the Kathmandu Valley (Kantipur, Lalitpur, Bhadgaon) before their conquest.
- **Divyopadesh**: A political testament delivered by Prithvi Narayan Shah, outlining his vision for Nepal's governance and foreign policy.
- **Kinloch expedition**: A failed 1767 military campaign by the British East India Company to aid the Malla kings against the Gorkhalis.
- **Asal Hindustan**: A term used by Shah to designate his unified kingdom as the "Authentic Land of the Hindus."
- **Import substitution**: An economic policy promoting domestic production to replace foreign imports, advocated by Shah.
- **Bhardaari-sabha**: An unofficial, high-level council meeting where Shah delivered his final instructions.

## Debates and Open questions
The article presents Shah's actions as foundational to the modern Nepalese state, but his legacy is complex. His campaigns involved significant violence, as noted in the brutal conquest of Kirtipur. His strict Hindu orthodoxy and expulsion of Europeans shaped the nation's early identity but also set a precedent for exclusion. The precise motivations and outcomes of his economic policies, particularly their long-term impact, remain subjects of historical analysis. His comparison to figures like Bismarck or Washington highlights his role as a nation-builder, but the specific context of 18th-century South Asia makes direct parallels complex.
