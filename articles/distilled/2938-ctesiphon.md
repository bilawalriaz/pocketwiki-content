# Ctesiphon

## Overview
Ctesiphon was an ancient city in modern Iraq that served as the royal capital of the Parthian and Sasanian empires for over 800 years. Its strategic location on the Tigris made it a major political, commercial, and cultural metropolis, sometimes referred to as "The Cities" (al-Mada'in) due to its merger with surrounding settlements like Seleucia. Its decline began after the Muslim conquest in 637 AD, and it was eventually supplanted by Baghdad.

## Body

### Parthian Foundation and Capital
Ctesiphon was founded in the late 120s BC by the Parthian king Mithridates I as a military camp across the Tigris from the Hellenistic city of Seleucia. It became the empire's capital around 58 BC under Orodes II, likely due to its distance from nomadic threats. The city grew by merging with Seleucia and other settlements, becoming a cosmopolitan metropolis. Its importance made it a frequent target for Rome; it was captured three times in the 2nd century AD by Trajan (116), Avidius Cassius (164), and Septimius Severus (197).

### Sasanian Zenith
After the Sasanian Empire replaced the Parthians in 226 AD, Ctesiphon flourished as their capital. The city expanded into distinct districts: the "Old City" on the east with the White Palace; the southern Asbānbar with the Taq Kasra arch; and the western Veh-Ardashir, populated by Jews and Christians. It remained a Roman objective, sacked by Emperor Carus in 283 AD. In the 6th century, Khosrow I built the new city of Veh-Antiok-Xusrō for deportees from Antioch. The city's fame later waned as Khosrow II favored his new residence at Dastagerd.

### Conquest and Decline
In the 630s, Muslim Arab forces defeated the Sasanians at the Battle of al-Qādisiyyah and captured Ctesiphon in early 637. The Sasanian royal family had fled, leaving the city desolate but rich in treasury spoils. The Taq Kasra was briefly used as a mosque. Ctesiphon's decline accelerated after the founding of the Abbasid capital Baghdad in the 760s; Caliph Al-Mansur repurposed its materials for the new city. By the end of the 8th century, Ctesiphon was largely a ghost town.

### Population and Archaeology
Under the Sasanians, Ctesiphon's population was ethnically mixed (Arameans, Persians, Greeks, Assyrians) and religiously diverse (Zoroastrianism, Christianity, Judaism, Manichaeism). Major archaeological excavations occurred in the 1920s-30s by German and American teams, and in the 1960s-70s by an Italian team. The most prominent surviving structure is the Taq Kasra, a massive iwan (vaulted hall) from the Sasanian palace, estimated to date between the 3rd and 6th centuries AD.

## Terms
- **Iwan**: A rectangular hall or space, usually vaulted, walled on three sides, with one end entirely open. The Taq Kasra is a famous example.
- **Hellenistic**: Relating to Greek culture, art, or language, especially in the period following Alexander the Great's conquests.
- **Parthian Empire**: An ancient Iranian empire (247 BC – 224 AD) that succeeded the Seleucid Empire and preceded the Sasanian Empire.
- **Sasanian Empire**: The last Persian imperial dynasty before the Muslim conquest (224–651 AD).
- **al-Mada'in**: Arabic for "The Cities," the name given to the Ctesiphon metropolitan area after its merger with Seleucia and other settlements.
- **Veh-Ardashir**: A district of Sasanian Ctesiphon, meaning "the good city of Ardashir" in Middle Persian.
- **Taq Kasra**: The monumental vaulted hall (iwan) of the Sasanian palace at Ctesiphon, also called the Arch of Ctesiphon.
- **Jizya**: A per capita tax levied on non-Muslim subjects in an Islamic state.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact reason for the Parthian capital's westward relocation from earlier sites like Hecatompylos is debated, with proximity to Scythian incursions being a proposed factor.
- Whether the Roman general Galerius captured Ctesiphon in the early 3rd century is not explicitly stated in sources and remains a matter of scholarly debate.
