# Newfoundland (island)

## Overview
Newfoundland is a large island off Canada's eastern coast, forming the core of the province of Newfoundland and Labrador. It is historically significant as the site of the first confirmed European settlement in North America (Norse, c. 1000 AD) and as a major fishing ground that shaped colonial rivalries. Its population is concentrated on the Avalon Peninsula, and its culture reflects a unique blend of British, Irish, and French heritage.

## Timeline
- **c. 4,000 years ago** — Dorset culture (Late Paleo-Eskimo) settles the island.
- **c. 1000 AD** — Norse establish a settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows.
- **1497** — John Cabot makes the first post-Norse European landing.
- **1583** — Sir Humphrey Gilbert claims Newfoundland as England's first overseas colony.
- **1713** — Treaty of Utrecht transfers French-controlled areas to Britain.
- **1825** — Newfoundland is organized as a British colony.
- **1907** — Newfoundland becomes a self-governing Dominion of the British Empire.
- **1934** — Self-government is suspended; a Commission of Government rules.
- **1949** — Newfoundland joins Canada as a province (effective March 31).

## Body

### Indigenous Inhabitants and Early European Contact
The first known inhabitants were the Paleo-Eskimo, followed by the Dorset culture around 4,000 years ago. The Beothuk, migrating from Labrador, were the primary indigenous group when Europeans arrived. The Norse built a temporary base at L'Anse aux Meadows around 1000 AD, the only authenticated pre-Columbian Norse settlement in North America. After the Norse, European visitors were primarily migratory fishermen from Portugal, Spain, France, and England. John Cabot's 1497 voyage, commissioned by Henry VII, marked the beginning of sustained European interest.

### Colonization and Colonial Society
England formally claimed the island in 1583. Early permanent settlements like Cuper's Cove (1610) struggled to profit. By the 17th century, English fishermen dominated the east coast and French fishermen the south and north coasts. The 1713 Treaty of Utrecht gave Britain control of the island, though France retained the nearby islands of Saint Pierre and Miquelon. The Crown initially discouraged year-round settlement, but by the late 18th century, a permanent society emerged, built by English, Irish, and French immigrants. This society was distinct from mainland North America, centered on the migratory and resident cod fisheries and characterized by isolated outport communities.

### Path to Confederation
Newfoundland was self-governing from 1855 until a financial crisis led to the suspension of its legislature in 1934, after which it was ruled by a Commission of Government. Two referendums in 1948 narrowly decided (52.3% to 47.7%) in favor of joining Canada. The confederation campaign was led by Joseph "Joey" Smallwood. Newfoundland officially became Canada's tenth province on March 31, 1949. Despite union, a strong local identity persists, with most residents identifying first as Newfoundlanders.

## Terms
- **Outport**: A small, isolated coastal settlement, historically the primary form of community in Newfoundland.
- **Grand Banks**: A group of underwater plateaus southeast of Newfoundland, one of the world's richest fishing grounds, especially for cod.
- **Beothuk**: An indigenous people of Newfoundland, now considered extinct, who spoke the Beothuk language.
- **Dorset culture**: A Late Paleo-Eskimo archaeological culture that inhabited Newfoundland around 4,000 years ago.
- **Commission of Government**: A non-elected body that administered Newfoundland from 1934 to 1949, after self-government was suspended.
- **Avalon Peninsula**: The southeastern part of Newfoundland, home to the capital St. John's and over 60% of the province's population.
- **Vinland**: The name given by Norse explorers to the area of North America they reached, believed to include Newfoundland.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact landing site of John Cabot in 1497 is unknown, with popular theories pointing to Cape Bonavista or Cape Bauld.
- The relationship between the Beothuk and Mi'kmaq is debated; European histories suggest conflict, but indigenous oral history refutes this.
- The precise number of Beothuk on the island at the time of European contact is uncertain, with estimates typically around 700.
