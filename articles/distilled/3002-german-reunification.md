# German reunification

## Overview
German reunification was the 1990 process of re-establishing Germany as a single sovereign state by merging the communist German Democratic Republic (GDR, East Germany) into the democratic Federal Republic of Germany (FRG, West Germany). It marked the end of the Cold War division of Europe, creating a unified, powerful nation within European and Atlantic alliances.

## Timeline
- **8 May 1945** — Unconditional surrender of German armed forces, ending WWII in Europe.
- **23 May 1949** — Federal Republic of Germany (FRG) established in Western zones.
- **7 October 1949** — German Democratic Republic (GDR) established in Soviet zone.
- **13 August 1961** — Construction of the Berlin Wall begins.
- **9 November 1989** — Fall of the Berlin Wall following mass protests.
- **18 March 1990** — First free GDR elections won by parties favoring rapid reunification.
- **1 July 1990** — Monetary, Economic, and Social Union takes effect; Deutsche Mark replaces East German mark.
- **31 August 1990** — Unification Treaty signed by the two German governments.
- **12 September 1990** — Two Plus Four Treaty signed by the two Germanys and four Allied powers.
- **3 October 1990** — GDR dissolved; its states and Berlin join the FRG. German Unity Day celebrated.
- **15 March 1991** — Two Plus Four Treaty enters into force, granting full sovereignty.
- **31 August 1994** — Last Russian occupation troops leave Germany.

## Body

### Precursors and Division
After Germany's 1945 defeat, Allied powers (US, UK, France, USSR) divided the country into occupation zones, leading to two states in 1949: the democratic FRG and communist GDR. The Berlin Wall (1961) symbolized this division. Both states pursued separate identities and joined opposing Cold War alliances (NATO and the Warsaw Pact).

### The Path to Reunification (1989-1990)
The "Peaceful Revolution" began with mass protests and Hungary opening its border with Austria in 1989, allowing thousands of East Germans to flee. The Berlin Wall's fall on 9 November 1989 accelerated events. After the GDR's first free elections, West German Chancellor Helmut Kohl pushed for rapid unification. A critical step was the economic merger on 1 July 1990. Legally, reunification occurred not by creating a new state, but by East Germany dissolving itself and acceding to the FRG under Article 23 of West Germany's Basic Law.

### International Negotiations and Final Settlement
Reunification required Allied approval. The US strongly supported it, while the UK and France feared a resurgence of German power. The Soviet Union, under Mikhail Gorbachev, agreed, securing German commitments to reduce its military, renounce weapons of mass destruction, and accept the Oder–Neisse line as its eastern border with Poland. The Two Plus Four Treaty (September 1990) formally ended Allied occupation rights and granted full sovereignty. The last foreign troops left in 1994.

### Domestic Opposition and "The Wall in the Head"
Some West Germans feared economic costs and revived nationalism. Many East Germans advocated a reformed, democratic socialist GDR ("third path") and feared capitalist "annexation." After unification, a cultural and economic divide—the "wall in the head" (*Mauer im Kopf*)—persisted. Significant subsidies flowed West to East, and many East Germans felt like second-class citizens.

### Effects and Legacy
Reunification made Germany a leading European power but cost an estimated €1.5–2 trillion. The eastern economy underwent painful deindustrialization with high unemployment. Socially, stereotypes between "Ossis" (Easterners) and "Wessis" (Westerners) persisted. Berlin was rebuilt as the capital but lagged economically. Despite challenges, by 2019, most Germans viewed reunification positively, and life satisfaction in the East had risen dramatically since 1991.

## Terms
*   **German Democratic Republic (GDR):** Communist state established 1949 in the Soviet zone, ruled by the Socialist Unity Party (SED).
*   **Federal Republic of Germany (FRG):** Democratic state established 1949 in Western zones.
*   **Basic Law (Grundgesetz):** West Germany's constitution, serving as the basis for the enlarged FRG after reunification.
*   **Article 23:** Provision allowing new states to join the FRG, used as the legal mechanism for reunification.
*   **Two Plus Four Treaty:** 1990 treaty between the two German states and four Allied powers granting full sovereignty.
*   **Die Wende ("The Turning Point"):** Term for the 1989-90 events leading to the GDR's collapse and reunification.
*   **Solidarity Surcharge (Solidaritätszuschlag):** Tax introduced to fund eastern reconstruction.
*   **Mauer im Kopf ("Wall in the Head"):** Metaphor for the persistent cultural and psychological divide after 1990.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Annexation vs. Reunification:** Some scholars and politicians, including a 2015 Russian proposal, characterize the process as an annexation, a view rejected by others like Gorbachev.
*   **Economic Policy:** The rapid "shock-therapy" privatization in the East, led by the Treuhandanstalt, is debated. Critics argue it caused unnecessary deindustrialization; defenders see it as inevitable.
*   **"Inner Reunification":** Whether true social and cultural unity has been achieved remains open. Chancellor Angela Merkel stated in 2009 that "the process of German unity has not ended yet."
*   **Historical Responsibility:** Opponents like Günter Grass argued that Germany's history, particularly the Holocaust, deprived it of the right to exist as a unified nation-state, fearing revived nationalism.
