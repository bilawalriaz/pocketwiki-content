# Guy de Maupassant

## Overview
Guy de Maupassant (1850–1893) was a French author celebrated as a master of the short story and a key figure in the naturalist literary movement. A protégé of Gustave Flaubert, he wrote with an economy of style and efficient dénouements (plot resolutions), often depicting human lives and social forces in disillusioned, pessimistic terms. His work is distinguished by a Schopenhauerian anthropological pessimism—harsh depictions of human nature driven by environment and physiology rather than Zola’s strict physiological determinism. He produced 300 short stories, six novels, and three travel books in a fertile decade (1880–1891) before succumbing to syphilis-induced madness and early death.

## Timeline
- **5 Aug 1850** — Born at Château de Miromesnil, Normandy; family legally adopts particule "de" to claim noble status.
- **1861** — Parents legally separate; mother Laure Le Poittevin retains custody, becoming dominant influence.
- **1867** — Meets Gustave Flaubert at Croisset; begins literary mentorship.
- **Oct 1868** — Saves poet Algernon Swinburne from drowning off Étretat.
- **1870–71** — Volunteers for French Army during Franco-Prussian War; moves to Paris as Navy Department clerk.
- **1880** — Publishes "Boule de Suif," his first short story, to instant acclaim; Flaubert calls it a masterpiece.
- **1881–85** — Publishes *La Maison Tellier* (1881), *Une Vie* (1883), *Bel-Ami* (1885); achieves wealth and fame.
- **1887** — Signs protest letter against Eiffel Tower construction; declines Legion of Honour.
- **1888** — Publishes *Pierre et Jean*, often considered his greatest novel.
- **1885–86** — Attends Jean-Martin Charcot’s psychiatry lectures; writes fantastic tales like "Le Horla."
- **2 Jan 1892** — Attempts suicide by cutting throat; committed to Esprit Blanche asylum.
- **6 Jul 1893** — Dies in asylum from syphilis at age 42.

## Body

### Early Life and Family Dynamics
Maupassant was born into a *petite noblesse* family whose noble status was recently formalized by a royal decree allowing the use of the particule "de." His childhood was shattered at age 11 when his independent-minded mother risked social disgrace to obtain a legal separation from his violent father. Laure Le Poittevin, a well-read lover of Shakespeare, became the central figure in his life, fostering his literary tastes during happy years at Étretat. A hostile reaction to religious discipline at the Institution Leroy-Petit led to his expulsion, after which he excelled at the Lycée Pierre-Corneille in Rouen, indulging in poetry and theater.

### The Flaubert Mentorship and Literary Debut
Gustave Flaubert acted as Maupassant’s literary guardian from 1867 onward, introducing him to Émile Zola, Ivan Turgenev, and the realist/naturalist circles. Flaubert taught him a concise, measured style and narrative distance. Maupassant worked as a civil servant—first in the Navy Department, then the Ministry of Public Instruction—while writing in his spare time. His breakthrough came in 1880 with "Boule de Suif," a Franco-Prussian War story depicting the futility of conflict and the corruption of civilians. Flaubert declared it "a masterpiece that will endure," launching Maupassant into immediate fame.

### Peak Productivity and Major Works
The decade 1880–1891 was extraordinarily fertile; Maupassant produced two to four volumes annually with methodical efficiency and shrewd business sense. *La Maison Tellier* (1881) reached a twelfth edition in two years. *Une Vie* (1883) sold 25,000 copies in under a year. *Bel-Ami* (1885) had thirty-seven printings in four months. *Pierre et Jean* (1888) is frequently cited as his finest novel. He traveled extensively (Algeria, Italy, England, Brittany, Sicily, Auvergne), often on his yacht *Bel-Ami*, converting each voyage into a travel volume. He moved among literary celebrities—Alexandre Dumas *fils*, Hippolyte Taine—while avoiding salon gossip, notably breaking with the Goncourt brothers.

### Themes: Realism, the Fantastic, and Psychiatry
Taking a cue from Balzac, Maupassant wrote in both high-realist and fantastic modes. Novels like *Bel-Ami* recreate Third Republic France realistically, while stories like "Le Horla" and "Qui sait?" describe apparently supernatural phenomena. Crucially, the supernatural is often an implicit symptom of the protagonist’s troubled mind. Fascinated by the nascent field of psychiatry, Maupassant attended Jean-Martin Charcot’s public lectures (1885–86), informing his portrayal of mental disintegration.

### Physical Decline and Death
Maupassant contracted syphilis in his youth (possibly congenital, as his brother Hervé may also have suffered from it). In later years, the disease caused a constant desire for solitude, obsession with self-preservation, fear of death, and paranoia of persecution. On 2 January 1892, he attempted suicide by cutting his throat and was committed to the private asylum of Esprit Blanche at Passy, where he died on 6 July 1893. His self-penned epitaph reads: "I have coveted everything and taken pleasure in nothing."

## Terms
- ****Naturalism**** — A literary movement depicting human lives as determined by social forces, heredity, and environment, often in pessimistic terms; Maupassant’s version is Schopenhauerian (anthropological pessimism) rather than Zola’s physiological determinism.
- ****Dénouement**** — The final resolution of a plot’s complexities; Maupassant’s are noted for their efficiency and seemingly effortless execution.
- ****Particule**** — The "de" in a French surname (e.g., "de Maupassant"), historically indicating noble birth; his father obtained legal right to use it in 1846.
- ****Franco-Prussian War (1870–71)**** — Conflict between France and Prussian-led German states; the setting for Maupassant’s first masterpiece "Boule de Suif" and many early stories.
- ****Schopenhauerian pessimism**** — Philosophical view (from Arthur Schopenhauer) that human existence is driven by blind will and characterized by suffering; describes Maupassant’s harsh depiction of human nature.
- ****Jean-Martin Charcot (1825–1893)**** — Pioneering neurologist and professor of anatomical pathology; his public lectures on hysteria and hypnosis (1885–86) influenced Maupassant’s fantastic tales.
- ****Le Horla**** — An 1887 fantastic short story featuring an invisible entity; often read as a fictionalization of the author’s own syphilitic mental deterioration.
- ****Bel-Ami**** — Title of Maupassant’s 1885 novel (37 printings in 4 months) and the name of his private yacht.
- ****Third Republic**** — The French republican government (1870–1940) that forms the realistic social backdrop of novels like *Bel-Ami* and *Pierre et Jean*.
- ****Pseudonyms**** — Pen names used by Maupassant, including "Joseph Prunier," "Guy de Valmont," and "Maufrigneuse" (1881–85).

## Debates and open questions
- **Nature of his naturalism**: Scholars debate whether Maupassant is a true naturalist like Zola; Kornelije Kvas argues his pessimism is Schopenhauerian (anthropological) rather than physiological, with environment influencing but not mechanically determining action.
- **Origin of syphilis**: It is suggested the disease may have been congenital, as his brother Hervé reportedly also suffered from it, though this remains unconfirmed.
- **Supernatural vs. psychological**: In fantastic stories like "Le Horla," critics dispute whether the supernatural elements are literal or purely projections of the protagonist’s deteriorating mind, informed by Charcot’s psychiatry.
- **Literary paternity of the modern short story**: While often called a "father of the modern short story" alongside Chekhov, the precise lineage of his influence on later writers like Somerset Maugham and O. Henry (particularly regarding twist endings and plotting) is a subject of ongoing comparative study.
