# Robert Boyle

## Overview
Robert Boyle (1627–1691) was an Anglo-Irish natural philosopher and a foundational figure in modern chemistry and experimental science. He is best known for Boyle's law, which describes the inverse relationship between gas pressure and volume, and for his influential work *The Sceptical Chymist*. His emphasis on rigorous experimentation and his integration of scientific inquiry with theology made him a pivotal figure in the Scientific Revolution.

## Timeline
- **1627** — Born at Lismore Castle, Ireland.
- **1635** — Sent to Eton College after his mother's death.
- **1641** — Traveled to Italy, studying Galileo's work.
- **1644** — Returned to England; began scientific research at Stalbridge.
- **1654** — Moved to Oxford to pursue experiments more effectively.
- **1659** — Completed the "Pneumatical Engine" (improved vacuum pump) with Robert Hooke.
- **1660** — Published *New Experiments Physico-Mechanical*, detailing air-pump experiments.
- **1661** — Published *The Sceptical Chymist*, criticizing traditional alchemical principles.
- **1662** — First published mention of Boyle's law in a defense of his work.
- **1663** — Became a founding member and councilor of the Royal Society.
- **1668** — Moved to London, living with his sister Katherine Jones.
- **1691** — Died in London, one week after his sister's death.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Boyle was the seventh son of the wealthy 1st Earl of Cork. After his mother's death in 1635, he attended Eton College and later traveled in Europe, where he studied the works of Galileo Galilei in Florence. This exposure to continental science shaped his future interests.

### Scientific Work and Method
Boyle settled in Oxford in 1654, where he conducted experiments with an improved vacuum pump designed with Robert Hooke. He championed an experimental method that avoided preconceived hypotheses, insisting on testing theories through direct observation. His work emphasized that knowledge should be pursued for its own sake, though he also valued practical applications.

### Chemistry and Physics
Boyle's *The Sceptical Chymist* (1661) challenged the classical four-element theory and the alchemical principles of salt, sulphur, and mercury. He defined elements as undecomposable constituents of matter and distinguished between mixtures and compounds. In physics, he formulated Boyle's law (1662), which states that for a gas at constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely proportional. He also studied the propagation of sound in air, the properties of "factitious airs" (artificial gases), and the nature of heat as particle motion.

### Theology and Later Life
A devout Anglican, Boyle believed natural philosophy could provide evidence for God's existence. He funded missionary work and the translation of the Bible into Irish. In his will, he established the Boyle Lectures to defend Christianity against "infidels." He declined the presidency of the Royal Society in 1680 due to his scruples about oaths. He died in 1691, leaving a legacy of integrating science and faith.

## Terms
- **Boyle's law**: The principle that the pressure of a gas is inversely proportional to its volume at constant temperature.
- **The Sceptical Chymist**: Boyle's 1661 book that criticized traditional alchemical theories and promoted a modern definition of chemical elements.
- **Pneumatical Engine**: An improved vacuum pump Boyle developed with Robert Hooke for experiments on air and vacuums.
- **Factitious airs**: Boyle's term for artificially produced gases, such as hydrogen.
- **Corpuscular philosophy**: The theory that matter is composed of tiny particles (corpuscles), which Boyle used to explain chemical and physical phenomena.
- **Invisible College**: The informal group of scholars that later became the Royal Society of London.
- **Boyle Lectures**: A series of lectures endowed by Boyle to defend Christianity against atheism and other beliefs.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The formulation of Boyle's law is debated: while Boyle published it in 1662, Henry Power first hypothesized it in 1661, and Edme Mariotte independently published it in 1676.
- Boyle's views on race, including his monogenist belief that all humans descended from Adam and Eve and his theories on skin color, were controversial among his contemporaries and remain a subject of historical analysis.
- The extent of his sister Katherine Jones's influence on his scientific work is acknowledged by contemporaries but was often omitted in later historical accounts.
