# Greenland ice sheet

## Overview
The Greenland ice sheet is the world's second-largest body of ice, covering about 80% of Greenland. It is a critical component of the global climate system, and its melting due to anthropogenic global warming is a major contributor to current and future sea level rise. Understanding its history, dynamics, and response to warming is essential for predicting future climate impacts.

## Timeline
- **c. 18 million years ago** — Evidence of major glaciers and ice caps in Greenland begins.
- **c. 2.6 million years ago** — A single ice sheet first covers most of the island.
- **c. 1 million years ago** — The last period when the ice sheet extended significantly beyond its current boundaries.
- **1970s** — The last decade when the Greenland ice sheet gained mass overall.
- **1996** — The last year the ice sheet saw net mass gain.
- **1997** — A shift in ocean circulation brought warmer water to West Greenland glaciers, accelerating retreat.
- **2002** — The annual melting season broke all previous records.
- **2012** — A massive melting event covered 97% of the ice sheet's surface; net loss set a record of 464 Gt.
- **2019** — A larger melting event occurred, setting a new record net loss of 586 Gt.
- **2021** — Another record melting event occurred; rain fell for the first time at the summit station.

## Body

### Formation and Geological History
Greenland has hosted glaciers for at least 18 million years, but a coherent ice sheet formed around 2.6 million years ago when atmospheric CO2 dropped to 280-320 ppm. The ice sheet's size has fluctuated, with 11 known periods where it extended up to 120 km beyond its current boundaries. Ice cores reveal the oldest ice is about 1 million years old. Evidence suggests the ice sheet nearly disappeared at least once in the past 1.4 million years, with less than 10% of its current volume remaining during periods less than 2.5°C warmer than preindustrial conditions.

### Recent Melting and Glacier Retreat
Since 1996, the ice sheet has lost mass annually. The rate of loss has accelerated dramatically, from an average of 51 Gt/year in the 1980s to 286 Gt/year between 2010-2018. This loss is driven by both surface melting and the retreat of outlet glaciers. Major glaciers like Jacobshavn Isbræ and Kangerlussuaq have shown rapid acceleration and retreat, often linked to warming ocean waters melting their fronts from below. The retreat is amplified by processes like the "Jacobshavn effect," where thinning reduces friction and increases flow speed, and by meltwater lubricating the glacier base.

### Surface Melting and Albedo Feedback
Summer surface melting is increasing, with the melt zone expanding since 1979. Record-breaking "massive melting events" have occurred in 2012, 2019, and 2021. The ice sheet's high albedo (reflectivity) is reduced by darker melt ponds and growing algae, creating a positive feedback loop that accelerates warming and melting. Increased rainfall, which forms darker ice, also contributes to this feedback.

### Geophysical and Biochemical Role of Meltwater
Greenland's meltwater releases nutrients like iron, which enhance ocean primary production, particularly in the Labrador Sea. It also carries dissolved organic carbon and potentially toxic substances like mercury (though recent studies suggest contamination in earlier reports). The influx of fresh meltwater may be influencing ocean circulation patterns, potentially contributing to the slowdown of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC).

### Future Projections and Thresholds
Projections for 2100 sea level rise from Greenland range from about 6 cm under optimistic Paris Agreement goals to 13 cm under high-emission scenarios. The total melting of the ice sheet would raise sea levels by ~7.4 meters. Scientific estimates for the global temperature threshold that would commit the ice sheet to eventual total loss have narrowed, with a most likely threshold around 1.5°C to 2.3°C above preindustrial levels. Crossing this threshold would lead to irreversible loss over centuries to millennia.

## Terms
- **Ice sheet:** A mass of glacial ice covering more than 50,000 km².
- **Glaciation:** The process of glacier formation and growth.
- **Firn:** Granular, partially compacted snow that is an intermediate stage between snow and glacial ice.
- **Calving:** The breaking off of chunks of ice from the edge of a glacier.
- **Albedo:** The measure of the reflectivity of a surface; ice has a high albedo.
- **Ice-albedo feedback:** A positive feedback loop where melting ice reduces reflectivity, causing more absorption of solar energy and further melting.
- **AMOC (Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation):** A large system of ocean currents that transports warm water northwards in the Atlantic.
- **Isostatic rebound:** The rise of land masses after the removal of the weight of ice sheets.
- **Moulins:** Vertical shafts in a glacier through which surface meltwater drains to the base.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact contribution of meltwater plumes versus ocean warming in driving glacier retreat.
- The future trajectory of the AMOC and Greenland's role in its potential slowdown or collapse.
- The precise temperature thresholds that will commit the ice sheet to irreversible, large-scale loss.
- The extent to which increased snowfall from a warmer atmosphere could offset melting losses.
- The long-term fate of subglacial carbon stores and potential toxic waste from historical military sites like Camp Century.
