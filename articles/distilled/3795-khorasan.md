# Khorasan

## Overview
Khorasan is a historical region in the Iranian Plateau and Central Asia whose boundaries have shifted over nearly 1,500 years. Originally an eastern province of the Sasanian Empire, it became a major cultural and political center during the Islamic Golden Age, producing many influential scientists and scholars. The region's name, meaning "where the sun arrives from," reflects its eastern position, and its legacy remains a source of cultural identity, particularly among Tajiks.

## Timeline
- **c. 6th century** — Established as an eastern administrative division of the Sasanian Empire.
- **c. 651 CE** — Conquered by the Rashidun Caliphate following the fall of the Sasanian Empire.
- **747–750** — Serves as the headquarters for the Abbasid Revolution against the Umayyads.
- **821** — First independent kingdom established by Tahir ibn Husayn.
- **1221** — Mongol invasion under Genghis Khan's son Tolui devastates the region.
- **1506–1722** — Parts contested between Safavid and Uzbek dynasties.
- **1729** — Recaptured by Nader Shah, who makes Mashhad his capital.
- **1857** — Treaty of Paris ends the Anglo-Persian War; Iran withdraws from Herat.
- **1881** — Treaty of Akhal sees Iran cede northern areas (including Merv) to the Russian Empire.

## Body

### Definition and Geography
The term "Khorasan" (from Middle Persian *Xwarāsān*, "Eastern Province") has had varying geographical meanings. In its strict historical sense, it encompassed northeastern Iran, western and northern Afghanistan, and southern parts of Central Asia, with major centers at Nishapur, Merv, Herat, and Balkh. A looser definition, sometimes called "Greater Khorasan," extended to include most of Transoxiana (modern Uzbekistan) and areas west to the Caspian Sea. The region was often divided into four quarters, each centered on one of the four major cities.

### Ancient and Sasanian Era
The Sasanian Empire formalized Khorasan as one of four military-administrative divisions (*kust*) in the 6th century. It was bordered by Sistan and Kerman to the south, central Iranian deserts to the west, and China and India to the east. The last Sasanian king, Yazdegerd III, moved his throne to Khorasan during the Arab invasions before being assassinated in 651.

### Islamic Conquest and Early Caliphates
The Rashidun Caliphate conquered Khorasan around 651, completing the takeover of Persia. Under the Umayyads, it became a key province for eastward expansion into Transoxiana, leading to conflicts with Turkic groups like the Turgesh. The province was a center of tribal rivalry between northern (Qaysi) and southern (Yamani) Arab factions. Khorasan later became the base for the Abbasid Revolution, which overthrew the Umayyads in 750.

### Medieval Dynasties and Mongol Invasion
After the Abbasids, Khorasan was ruled by a succession of powerful dynasties, including the Tahirids, Saffarids, Samanids, Ghaznavids, and Seljuqs. The region was a major cultural and intellectual hub until the Mongol invasion of 1221, which caused widespread destruction from which it never fully recovered. Later dynasties like the Timurids (1370–1506) revived its importance.

### Modern Era
From the 16th to 18th centuries, Khorasan was contested between Safavid Persia and Uzbek rulers. In the 19th century, its territory was reduced by treaties with the British and Russian Empires, notably the 1881 Treaty of Akhal, which ceded Merv to Russia. The former Khorasan Province of Iran (1906–2004) covered only the western portion of the historical region.

## Terms
*   **Kust**: A Middle Persian term for one of the four cardinal administrative-military divisions of the Sasanian Empire.
*   **Marzban**: A Sasanian military commander or governor of a border province.
*   **Transoxiana**: The historical name for the region between the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers, roughly modern Uzbekistan and southern Kazakhstan.
*   **Hephthalites**: A group of nomadic peoples who established an empire in Central Asia during the 5th and 6th centuries.
*   **Qaysi/Yamani**: Rival political factions in the early Islamic Caliphate, representing northern and southern Arab tribes, respectively.
*   **Shu'ūbiyya**: A social and intellectual movement that challenged Arab supremacy in the early Islamic Caliphate, advocating for the equality or superiority of non-Arab peoples, especially Persians.

## Debates and Open questions
*   The precise boundaries of "Greater Khorasan" are debated, with definitions ranging from a strict core to a loose sphere of influence extending to the Indus Valley.
*   The origin of the name is debated; while it is Persian, some scholars argue it is a calque (loan translation) from the Bactrian name *Miirosan*, which also meant "sunrise, east."
*   The extent of the "Greater Khorasan Civilization" (GKC) as a distinct Bronze Age interaction sphere in Central Asia (c. 2400–1500 BCE) is a subject of recent scholarly consideration.
