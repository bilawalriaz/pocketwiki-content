# Mesopotamia

## Overview
Mesopotamia ("land between rivers") is the historical region within the Tigris–Euphrates river system, roughly corresponding to modern Iraq. It hosted the earliest Neolithic Revolution (c. 10,000 BC) and the world’s first urban, literate civilizations. The Sumerians and Akkadians dominated Mesopotamia from the beginning of recorded history (c. 3100 BC) to the fall of Babylon in 539 BC. The Sumerians invented cuneiform writing and pioneered the sexagesimal numeral system (base 60); city-states produced the first codified law codes (Urukagina, Lipit-Ishtar, Hammurabi); and the need for irrigation drove the development of centralized states. Subsequent empires—Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian, Achaemenid, Seleucid, Parthian, Roman, and Sassanid—contested the region until the 7th-century Muslim conquests renamed it Iraq. Mesopotamia was one of the four riverine civilizations where writing was invented, alongside the Nile, Indus, and Yellow River valleys. Its cultural legacy underpins later Near Eastern and Mediterranean civilizations.

## Timeline
- **c. 10,000 BC** — Neolithic Revolution begins; Pre-Pottery Neolithic A starts.
- **c. 4000–3100 BC** — Uruk period: urbanization, pictographic proto-cuneiform, temple economies.
- **c. 3100 BC** — Beginning of recorded history; Early Dynastic period follows.
- **c. 2350 BC** — Sargon of Akkad founds the Akkadian Empire, first multi-city empire.
- **2112–2004 BC** — Third Dynasty of Ur (Ur III) centralizes administration and irrigation.
- **c. 1800 BC** — Hammurabi establishes Old Babylonian state; Code of Hammurabi codifies law.
- **911–612 BC** — Neo-Assyrian Empire (911 BC – 612 BC); from 900 to 612 BC asserted control over much of the ancient Near East.
- **626–539 BC** — Neo-Babylonian Empire rules as final independent Mesopotamian realm.
- **539 BC** — Achaemenid Empire (Cyrus the Great) conquers Babylon.
- **332 BC** — Alexander the Great conquers region; Seleucids rule after Diadochi wars.
- **c. 150 BC** — Parthian Empire controls Mesopotamia; Roman–Parthian frontier established.
- **226 AD** — Sassanid Empire (Ardashir I) takes eastern Mesopotamia.
- **mid-7th century AD** — Muslim conquests end ancient period; region becomes Iraq.

## Body

### Geography and Environment
Mesopotamia lies between the Tigris and Euphrates, both sourced in the Armenian highlands. The north (Upper Mesopotamia/Jazira) receives rain-fed agriculture; the south (Lower Mesopotamia) requires irrigation from a high water table and mountain snowmelt. The southern alluvium includes a 15,000 km² marshland. Lacking stone, timber, and metals, the region relied on long-distance trade of agricultural surplus. Irrigation demanded organized labor for canal construction and maintenance, driving urbanization and centralized authority. Periodic salinization, climatic instability, invasions, and city-state centrifugalism caused recurring systemic collapses.

### Historical Periodization
Prehistory spans Lower Paleolithic to the Uruk IV period (late 4th millennium BC), when proto-cuneiform appears. Recorded history begins c. 3100 BC with Early Dynastic kings. The Akkadian Empire (c. 2350–2100 BC) first unified the region. The Ur III period (2112–2004 BC) saw bureaucratic centralization. The early second millennium BC polarized into Assyria (north) and Babylonia (south). The Old Babylonian period (Hammurabi, c. 1780 BC) produced famous law codes. The Late Bronze Age featured Kassite Babylon and Middle Assyria, ending in the 12th–11th century BC collapse. The Iron Age brought the Neo-Assyrian Empire (911–612 BC) and Neo-Babylonian Empire (626–539 BC). Classical antiquity saw Achaemenid, Seleucid, Parthian, and Roman/Sassanid rule. Native states (Adiabene, Osroene, Hatra) existed 1st century BC–3rd century AD. The 7th-century Muslim conquests concluded the ancient era.

### Language and Writing
Sumerian (a language isolate) was the earliest written language; Semitic Akkadian became dominant during the Akkadian and Assyrian empires, while Sumerian persisted for scholarly and liturgical use until the 1st century AD. Subartuan (possibly Hurro-Urartian) appears in names and geography. Old Aramaic became the imperial administrative language under the Neo-Assyrian and Achaemenid empires (Imperial Aramaic). Cuneiform ("wedge-shaped") emerged mid-4th millennium BC for Sumerian, evolving from pictograms to a mixed logographic-syllabic script. Early literacy was restricted to scribes; syllabic standardization under Sargon broadened literacy. The last Akkadian texts date to the late 1st century AD.

### Literature
Temple and palace libraries preserved texts. The *Epic of Gilgamesh* (twelve tablets, standardized by Sîn-lēqi-unninni) is the best-known work, translated from Sumerian and structured on astronomical principles. Babylonian literature heavily adapted Sumerian originals; scribal schools compiled vocabularies, grammars, and commentaries. Women as well as men could become scribes, requiring mastery of Sumerian and a complex syllabary.

### Science and Technology
**Mathematics:** Sexagesimal (base-60) system underlies the 60-minute hour, 24-hour day, and 360° circle. Babylonians approximated π as 3 (or 25/8 = 3.125 on one tablet), calculated circle area as 1/12 circumference², and solved quadratic problems algorithmically. Tablet YBC 7289 (c. 1800–1600 BC) gives √2 ≈ 1;24,51,10 (six-decimal accuracy). Plimpton 322 (c. 1900–1600 BC) lists Pythagorean triples.
**Astronomy:** Temple priesthoods correlated events with planetary positions from Sumerian times; Assyrian *Limmu* lists enabled absolute chronology. Babylonian astronomers predicted eclipses and solstices, developed a 12-month lunar calendar, and in the 8th–7th centuries BC introduced predictive planetary theory based on internal logic—called by some the "first scientific revolution." Seleucus of Seleucia (b. 190 BC) uniquely supported heliocentrism. Babylonian methods founded later Greek, Indian, Islamic, and European astronomy.
**Medicine:** Old Babylonian texts (early 2nd millennium BC) introduce diagnosis, prognosis, physical examination, and prescriptions. The *Diagnostic Handbook* by Esagil-kin-apli (c. 1069–1046 BC) applies empiricism, logic, and rationality to symptom-disease mapping, including detailed epilepsy descriptions. Treatments combined pharmacology (bandages, creams, pills) and exorcism for incurable cases.
**Technology:** Innovations include the invention of the wheel, copper/bronze/iron metallurgy, glassmaking, textile weaving, flood control, water storage, and irrigation. The Archimedes screw may have been used by Sennacherib (7th century BC) at Nineveh/Babylon, though debated. The "Baghdad Battery" (Parthian/Sasanian) may represent an early electrochemical cell.

### Religion and Philosophy
Religion was polytheistic; the world was viewed as a flat disc surrounded by water, with heaven above. The Sumerian *an-ki* (universe) derives from sky-god An and earth-goddess Ki; their son Enlil (air) headed the pantheon. Regional variations existed. Philosophy emerged in wisdom literature (proverbs, dialogues, epics) embodying ethics and dialectic. Babylonian thought used open-systems ontology and logic in astronomy/medicine, influencing early Greek philosophy (e.g., Thales, Sophists, Plato’s dialogues). The *Dialogue of Pessimism* anticipates the Socratic method. Abrahamic religions, especially Genesis, show Mesopotamian literary and cultural influence.

### Culture
**Festivals:** Monthly rituals keyed to lunar phase, agricultural cycle, equinoxes/solstices, local patron deities, royal success, and the *Akitu* (New Year at spring equinox). **Music:** Songs for gods and historical events, transmitted orally then in writing, preserving collective memory. **Games:** Hunting (Assyrian kings), boxing, wrestling, polo (on shoulders), and the *Royal Game of Ur* (board game). **Family:** Society grew increasingly patriarchal (law codes of Urukagina, Lipit-Ishtar, Hammurabi show declining women’s rights). Early Sumer may have had gender-balanced councils. Schooling limited to elites; women could own property and divorce. **Burials:** Family graves under houses (Ur), children in jars, royal graves with rich goods; some elites sought burial in Dilmun (Bahrain).

### Economy
**Agriculture:** Irrigation spread south from Zagros foothills c. 5000 BC. Temples owned up to one-third of land in Ur III; *Ensi* officials managed temple agriculture. Villeins worked temple/palace lands. Crops: barley, onions, grapes, turnips, apples. Beer and wine production early. Salinization eventually degraded southern soils, shifting power north to Akkad. **Trade:** Indus Valley trade by 3rd millennium BC; Egypt from 4th millennium BC. Mesopotamia functioned as an east-west (Silk Road) and north-south (Volga route) nexus until the Cape route (1498) and Suez Canal (1869) redirected traffic. Temples acted as early banks; Babylonians developed commercial banking resembling post-Keynesian credit systems.

### Government
Geography fragmented early Sumer into independent city-states; unification attempts failed until Eannatum, then Sargon (Akkad). The Akkadian Empire achieved the first lasting multi-generational succession. Assyrian imperial administration divided territory into provinces (named for capitals: Nineveh, Samaria, Damascus) with governors handling taxes, levies, labor, and law. Hammurabi expanded Babylon into a major learning center. **Warfare:** Walled cities appear post-Uruk; first recorded war c. 3200 BC, common by 2500 BC. *Stele of the Vultures* (c. 2600–2350 BC) is the earliest victory monument. Empires warred for tribute, resources, and slaves; Assyrians deported populations widely; Neo-Babylonians used deportation more locally. **Law:** City-states produced the first law codes (Urukagina, Lipit-Ishtar, Hammurabi c. 1780 BC). Hammurabi’s 200+ laws show progressive erosion of women’s rights and harsher slave treatment.

### Art and Architecture
**Art:** Rivaled Egypt in grandeur (4th millennium BC–6th century BC). Emphasis on durable stone/clay sculpture; painting mostly geometric/floral. *Protoliterate* (Uruk): Warka Vase, cylinder seals. *Early Dynastic*: large-eyed alabaster worshippers, *Ram in a Thicket*, Lyres of Ur. *Assyrian*: colossal narrative palace reliefs (war/hunt), human-headed *lamassu* guardians (five-legged for dual view), refined cylinder seals. **Architecture:** Brick dominant (stone scarce). *Ziggurat* is the hallmark form. Key remains: Uruk temple complexes (4th millennium BC), Early Dynastic temples/palaces (Diyala valley), Ur III ziggurats (Nippur, Ur), Middle/Late Bronze palaces (Ebla, Mari, Hattusa, Ugarit), Iron Age Assyrian/Babylonian/Urartian/Neo-Hittite palaces. Ishtar Gate (Neo-Babylonian, polychrome brick) exemplifies monumental gateways. Textual sources include Gudea’s cylinders and royal inscriptions.

### Genetics
Modern Iraqi groups (Mesopotamian Arabs, Assyrians, Kurds, Yazidis, Marsh Arabs) show significant genetic continuity. Y-DNA reveals no significant differences among Arabs, Assyrians, Kurds; J and R macrohaplogroups dominate, attributed to pre-Last Glacial Maximum Near Eastern events. Assyrians/Yazidis may retain strongest continuity with ancient Mesopotamian stock. Marsh Arabs are hypothesized to link closely to ancient Sumerians. Assyrians show assimilation with post-Neo-Babylonian settlers.

## Terms
- **Cuneiform**: "Wedge-shaped" script impressed on wet clay with a reed stylus; evolved from pictograms to logographic-syllabic system (mid-4th millennium BC).
- **Sexagesimal**: Base-60 numeral system originating in Mesopotamia; source of 60-minute hour, 360° circle.
- **Ziggurat**: Massive terraced step-pyramid temple platform, the distinctive Mesopotamian monumental form.
- **Akkadian**: East Semitic language that became lingua franca of the Akkadian/Assyrian empires; written in cuneiform.
- **Imperial Aramaic**: Standardized Old Aramaic used as official administrative language of Neo-Assyrian and Achaemenid empires.
- **Limmu lists**: Assyrian eponym-year records associating each year with a magistrate and celestial events, enabling absolute chronology.
- **Ensi**: Sumerian title for the governor/administrator of a city-state or temple estate, especially overseeing agriculture.
- **Akitu**: Annual New Year festival at the first full moon after the spring equinox, central to the ritual calendar.
- **Lamassu**: Protective deity depicted as a human-headed winged bull/lion, sculpted as colossal palace guardians (five legs for complete frontal and side views).
- **Diagnostic Handbook**: Babylonian medical compendium by Esagil-kin-apli (c. 1069–1046 BC) applying empirical observation and logic to diagnosis/prognosis.

## Debates and open questions
- **Sumerian-to-Akkadian language shift**: Exact timing of Akkadian replacing Sumerian as spoken vernacular (turn of 3rd/2nd millennium BC) is debated.
- **Archimedes screw attribution**: Whether Sennacherib used the screw at Nineveh/Babylon (7th century BC) or it is a later Greek invention.
- **Baghdad Battery function**: Whether Parthian/Sasanian-era jars with copper/iron rods functioned as galvanic cells or served another purpose.
- **Heliocentrism in Babylon**: Extent of Seleucus of Seleucia’s proof for heliocentrism; arguments he used are unknown beyond tidal theory.
- **Early state formation**: Relative weight of irrigation management vs. trade/defense in driving initial urbanization and centralization.
- **Salinization vs. political collapse**: Degree to which soil salinization caused the south’s decline versus political/military factors.
- **Genetic continuity**: Precise relationship of modern groups (especially Marsh Arabs) to ancient Sumerians/Akkadians given limited ancient DNA.
