# Simón Bolívar

## Overview
Simón José Antonio de la Santísima Trinidad Bolívar y Palacios (24 July 1783 – 17 December 1830) was a Venezuelan military leader and statesman who orchestrated the independence of six South American nations—Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador, Panama, Peru, and Venezuela—from Spanish rule. Known as "El Libertador," he shaped the political geography of Latin America through both military campaigns and constitutional vision, advocating for a unified, centralized republic while grappling with the tensions of race, democracy, and authoritarianism.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into a wealthy criollo family in Caracas, Bolívar lost both parents young and was raised by an enslaved woman named Hipólita. His early education was erratic, marked by rebellion and frequent changes in guardianship. Sent to Madrid in 1799, he encountered Enlightenment thought and married María Teresa Rodríguez del Toro y Alaysa, who died of yellow fever in 1803. A Grand Tour ending in Rome in 1805 solidified his commitment to Latin American independence.

### Rise to Military Leadership
Returning to Venezuela in 1807, Bolívar joined the independence movement after Napoleon’s invasion of Spain destabilized colonial authority. He fought in the Venezuelan War of Independence and later in New Granada, where he issued the *Cartagena Manifesto* (1812) while en route to his military posting, calling for unity among republican forces. His *Admirable Campaign* (1813) led to the capture of Caracas and his proclamation as "El Libertador."

### Exile and Resurgence
After the collapse of the Second Republic in 1814, Bolívar fled to Jamaica in 1815 and then to Haiti in late 1815/early 1816, where he secured support from Pétion in exchange for pledging to abolish slavery. Returning to Venezuela, he rebuilt republican forces and delivered the *Angostura Address* (1819), outlining his vision for a centralized, racially inclusive republic.

### Founding of Gran Colombia
In 1819, Bolívar crossed the Andes and defeated Spanish forces at Boyacá in August, leading to the creation of Gran Colombia by decree on 17 December 1819—a union of Venezuela, New Granada, and Quito. He served as its first president, promoting liberal reforms and a strong executive branch. The Congress of Cúcuta (1821–1822) later ratified this structure, electing Santander as vice president.

### Southern Campaigns and Constitutional Vision
From 1821 to 1825, Bolívar extended his influence southward, liberating Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia. The decisive Battle of Ayacucho on 9 December 1824, where Sucre defeated the Royalists, marked the end of Spanish rule in South America. Despite military success, he struggled with governance, clashing with local leaders and imposing authoritarian measures. His draft constitutions emphasized centralized power, reflecting his belief that Spanish America was unready for pure democracy.

### Final Years and Decline
By the late 1820s, Bolívar faced growing opposition from regional caudillos and liberal factions. He assumed supreme power on 27 August 1828 after the Ocaña convention crisis, and a failed assassination attempt on 25 September 1828 further consolidated his authoritarian rule. Disillusioned and in poor health, he resigned the presidency on 27 April 1830 and died of tuberculosis on 17 December 1830 at the Quinta de San Pedro Alejandrino near Santa Marta.

## Terms
- **Criollo**: American-born Spaniards who formed the colonial elite.
- **Llaneros**: Horsemen of the Llanos plains, key actors in Venezuelan conflicts.
- **Pardo**: Mixed-race individuals in Spanish colonial caste system.
- **Gran Colombia**: Republic encompassing present-day Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, and Panama.
- **Jamaica Letter**: Bolívar’s 1815 manifesto outlining his political philosophy.
- **Cartagena Manifesto**: 1812 document explaining causes of Venezuela’s defeat and strategy for renewal.
- **Angostura Address**: 1819 speech advocating for centralized government and racial equality.
- **Dictator**: In Bolívar’s context, a leader with near-absolute executive powers.
- **Benevolent Dictator**: Bolívar’s ideal ruler—authoritarian yet reformist.
- **Cult of Bolívar**: Posthumous reverence for Bolívar as a national hero.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authoritarianism vs. Liberty**: Bolívar championed independence but ruled increasingly autocratically, raising questions about his true commitment to democracy.
- **Race and Governance**: His vision of racial unity clashed with actions like executing General Piar, a pardo leader, fueling debate over his racial attitudes.
- **Poisoning Conspiracy**: Claims by Hugo Chávez that Bolívar was poisoned remain unproven; exhumation results were inconclusive.
- **Constitutional Legacy**: His constitutional models were rejected by many regions, contributing to post-independence fragmentation.
