# Maize

## Overview
Maize (*Zea mays*), also called corn, is a tall annual grass domesticated once in southern Mexico roughly 9,000 years ago from the wild grass teosinte. It became the foundational staple crop of pre-Columbian Mesoamerica, where it was processed via nixtamalization (alkali treatment) to unlock essential nutrients. Following the Columbian exchange, maize spread globally due to its adaptability to diverse climates and high productivity, surpassing wheat and rice in total annual production (1.16 billion tonnes in 2020). Modern agriculture relies heavily on hybrid breeding and genetic modification (92% of the 2016 U.S. crop) to maximize yield, pest resistance, and stress tolerance. Beyond direct human consumption (tortillas, polenta, sweeteners, whiskey), maize dominates animal feed and industrial uses, including ethanol biofuel, starch-based plastics, and adhesives.

## Timeline
- **c. 9,000 years ago** — Single domestication event from Balsas teosinte (*Zea mays* subsp. *parviglumis*) in the Balsas River valley, Mexico.
- **c. 8,700 years ago** — Stone milling tools with maize residue deposited in a cave near Iguala, Guerrero.
- **c. 7,300 years ago** — Maize pollen found at San Andres, Tabasco, on the Caribbean coast.
- **c. 6,250 years ago** — Early maize ears found at Guila Naquitz Cave, Oaxaca Valley.
- **c. 4,500 years ago** — Maize begins spreading northward into the present-day U.S. Southwest.
- **c. 1,200–1,500 BC** — Nixtamalization (alkali processing) developed in Mesoamerica to liberate niacin and hulls.
- **c. AD 900** — Large-scale adoption of maize agriculture in eastern North America.
- **1492** — Columbian exchange initiates global spread of maize to Europe, Africa, and Asia.
- **1909** — G. H. Shull develops hybrid breeding using selected inbred lines.
- **1918/1922** — D. F. Jones develops double-cross hybrids using four inbred lines.
- **1983** — Barbara McClintock wins Nobel Prize for transposon ("jumping gene") theory validated in maize.
- **1997** — Commercial cultivation of genetically modified (Bt) maize begins in U.S. and Canada.
- **2008** — Maize genome sequencing completed; genome is 85% transposons, containing 32,540 genes.
- **2020** — World production reaches 1.16 billion tonnes; U.S. produces 31%, China 22.4%.

## Body

### Domestication and Taxonomy
Maize is the domesticated variant of four teosinte species, genetically closest to Balsas teosinte (*Zea mays* subsp. *parviglumis*) native to Mexico’s Balsas River valley. The dramatic morphological shift—from a short, bushy, multi-stalked teosinte to a tall, single-stalked maize with a large ear—is controlled largely by two genes: *teosinte branched-1* (*tb1*) and *grassy tillers-1* (*gt1*). Genetic evidence confirms a single domestication event ~9,000 years ago, refuting earlier hybridization theories involving *Tripsacum* (gamagrass). Maize belongs to the PACMAD clade of grasses, closely related to sorghum and *Tripsacum*, but distant from rice and wheat (BOP clade).

### Pre-Columbian Diffusion and Nixtamalization
From the Mexican highlands, maize spread along two major paths across the Americas, reaching highland Ecuador by 8,000 years ago and the Colombian Andes by 7,000–6,000 years ago. It arrived in the U.S. Southwest ~4,100 years ago and became dominant in eastern North America by ~AD 900, driving landscape clearance. In Mesoamerica, the Olmec and Maya developed nixtamalization—soaking kernels in alkali (ash/lime) water—by at least 1200–1500 BC. This process removes hulls and, critically, frees bound niacin (vitamin B3), preventing pellagra, a deficiency disease that later plagued populations adopting maize without this technique.

### Columbian Exchange and Global Adoption
Post-1492, Spanish traders introduced maize to Europe, Africa, and Asia. Despite initial European preference for wheat (required for communion bread), maize became a peasant staple in Southern Europe (polenta in Italy) and Africa by the 17th–18th centuries. Its global success stemmed from high productivity and adaptability to diverse climates (cold, hot, dry, wet). However, wherever maize became a dietary staple without nixtamalization or dietary diversity, pellagra epidemics followed. The disease receded in the developed world only after alkali processing and balanced diets were adopted, though it persists in food-insecure regions reliant on donated maize.

### Genetics and Breeding
Maize is diploid (20 chromosomes) with 83% of its allelic variation derived from teosinte. It is a premier genetic model organism; Barbara McClintock’s discovery of transposons ("jumping genes") in maize won the 1983 Nobel Prize. The genome (sequenced 2008) is 85% transposons and contains 32,540 genes, heavily reshuffled by helitrons. Modern breeding evolved from mass selection (1860s, e.g., Reid’s Yellow Dent) to ear-to-row selection, single-cross hybrids (Shull, 1909), and double-cross hybrids (Jones, 1918/1922). Since the 1940s, first-generation hybrids from optimized inbred lines have dominated, selecting for yield, drought tolerance, pest resistance, and high-density planting. Tropical landraces remain a vital, underused reservoir of resistance alleles for disease and herbivores.

### Genetic Engineering
Genetically modified (GM) maize was among 26 GM crops grown commercially in 2016, predominantly *Bt* maize (expressing *Bacillus thuringiensis* insecticidal proteins). By 2016, 92% of the U.S. maize crop was GM. Herbicide-tolerant and insect-resistant varieties were each grown in over 20 countries as of 2011. A notable regulatory failure occurred in 2000 when Starlink maize (approved only for animal feed) contaminated the human food supply, prompting a $50 million recall.

### Cultivation Physiology and Practice
Maize is a C4 carbon fixation plant, making it more water-efficient than C3 crops (e.g., soybeans). It is cold-intolerant, requiring spring planting in temperate zones and warm days (>10°C/50°F) plus specific night lengths (short-day photoperiodism) to flower. Shallow roots confer susceptibility to drought (critical at silk emergence), nutrient deficiency, and wind lodging. Traditional Native American "Three Sisters" polyculture intercropped maize (support), beans (nitrogen fixation via rhizobia), and squash (weed suppression/moisture retention). Modern harvesting uses combine harvesters with corn heads that strip ears and shred stalks for soil organic matter. Grain must be dried to low moisture content to prevent mycotoxin contamination (aflatoxin, fumonisin) by *Aspergillus* and *Fusarium* fungi.

### Pests and Diseases
Major insect pests cause ~$1 billion annual losses each in the U.S.: European corn borer (*Ostrinia nubilalis*) and corn rootworms (*Diabrotica* spp.). Fall armyworm (*Spodoptera frugiperda*) is a severe invasive threat. The maize weevil (*Sitophilus zeamais*) attacks stored grain. Nematodes (especially *Pratylenchus* lesion nematodes) ubiquitously parasitize roots, causing stunting exacerbated by water stress. Key diseases include tar spot (2022 U.S. leader: 116.8M bushels lost), northern corn leaf blight, gray leaf spot, Goss’s wilt, and ear/stalk rots producing mycotoxins. Weed competition (grasses and broadleaf forbs) is managed via mechanical removal, flame weeding, or herbicides.

### Uses: Food, Feed, Industry, Fuel
**Culinary:** Nixtamalized dough (masa) is the base for tortillas, tamales, and atole. Non-nixtamalized meal makes polenta, grits, ugali, and mieliepap. Sweet corn (high sugar, low starch) is eaten fresh. Corn smut (*huitlacoche*) is a Mexican delicacy. **Nutrition:** Kernels are ~76% water, 19% carbs, 3% protein, 1% fat; protein is deficient in tryptophan and lysine, complemented by legumes. **Feed:** ~40% of U.S. domestic use (64% including exports/byproducts); fed as grain, silage (chopped fermented whole plant), or stover. **Industrial:** Starch yields plastics, fabrics, adhesives; corn steep liquor (wet milling byproduct) is a microbial culture medium. **Biofuel:** Major feedstock for ethanol; kernels fuel specialized stoves/furnaces; cobs serve as biomass. Biofuel demand raises maize prices, indirectly increasing food costs.

### Cultural Significance
In Mesoamerica, maize was deified (often as a female maize god) and central to Olmec/Maya calendars, language, and myth. In the U.S., maize motifs appear in the Capitol building architecture, the Corn Palace (Mitchell, SD), and the *Field of Corn* sculpture (Dublin, OH). It appears on the Croatian 1 lipa coin. Historically, maize kernels symbolized cowardice (fed to chickens); in 1973 Chile, anti-Allende protesters threw maize at barracks as a political gesture.

## Terms
- ****Teosinte**** — The wild grass ancestor of maize (*Zea* spp.), short and bushy with multiple stalks; Balsas teosinte is the direct progenitor.
- ****Nixtamalization**** — Process of soaking and cooking maize in an alkaline solution (lime/ash water) to remove hulls and free bound niacin, preventing pellagra.
- ****Caryopsis**** — The botanical fruit type of grasses (including maize), where the seed coat is fused to the fruit wall; commonly called a kernel or grain.
- ****Monoecious**** — Having separate male and female flowers on the same plant; maize has a male tassel (top) and female ears (mid-stalk).
- ****C4 Carbon Fixation**** — A photosynthetic pathway (used by maize) that concentrates CO2, conferring higher water-use efficiency and heat tolerance than C3 plants.
- ****Transposon**** — A mobile genetic element ("jumping gene") that can change position within the genome; discovered in maize by Barbara McClintock.
- ****Heterosis (Hybrid Vigor)**** — The phenomenon where first-generation (F1) hybrids from inbred lines exhibit superior yield and vigor compared to parents; basis of modern maize seed.
- ****Bt Maize**** — Genetically modified maize expressing insecticidal proteins from *Bacillus thuringiensis*, targeting pests like European corn borer.
- ****Silage**** — Fermented, high-moisture stored fodder made by chopping green maize plants; more digestible/palatable for ruminants than dry grain/stover.
- ****Pellagra**** — A disease caused by niacin (vitamin B3) deficiency, historically epidemic in populations relying on non-nixtamalized maize as a staple.

## Debates and open questions
- **Yield Potential Ceiling:** Conflicting evidence exists on whether maize yield potential per individual plant has increased in recent decades; gains may stem solely from agronomic traits (leaf angle, lodging resistance, density tolerance) rather than intrinsic yield potential.
- **Tropical Germplasm Utilization:** Tropical landraces harbor valuable resistance alleles for disease and herbivores, but remain "underused" in mainstream breeding programs; efficient introgression without yield drag is a challenge.
- **Biofuel vs. Food Trade-offs:** The diversion of maize to ethanol production increases demand and prices, indirectly raising food costs; the net energy balance and land-use change impacts of maize ethanol remain contested.
- **GM Trait Durability:** Evolution of resistance in target pests (e.g., corn rootworm to *Bt* traits) and weeds (to herbicide tolerance) threatens the long-term efficacy of dominant GM technologies.
- **Mycotoxin Management:** Climate change may expand the range and severity of *Aspergillus* and *Fusarium* ear rots, complicating food/feed safety and requiring new resistance breeding or post-harvest strategies.
