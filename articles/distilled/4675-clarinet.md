# Clarinet

## Overview
The clarinet is a single-reed woodwind instrument with a nearly cylindrical bore and a flared bell. It is the largest woodwind family, ranging from the BB♭ contrabass to the A♭ piccolo, with the B♭ soprano being the most common. Invented around 1700 by modifying the earlier chalumeau, the clarinet's development involved adding keys and airtight pads to improve its range and playability. It is now a standard instrument in orchestras, concert bands, and genres from classical to jazz and klezmer.

## Timeline
- **c. 1700** — Johann Christoph Denner invents the clarinet by adding a register key to the chalumeau.
- **1710** — First known use of the word "clarinet" in an order to Jacob Denner.
- **c. 1742** — Johann Melchior Molter writes six clarinet concertos for clarinet in D.
- **1771** — Mozart first uses the clarinet in his Divertimento K. 113.
- **1791** — Mozart composes the Clarinet Concerto in A major for the basset clarinet.
- **1812** — Iwan Müller presents a 13-key "clarinet omnitonic."
- **1839-1843** — Hyacinthe Klosé and Louis Auguste Buffet adapt the Boehm system for the clarinet.
- **1848** — Eugène Albert develops the Albert system clarinet.
- **1924** — George Gershwin's "Rhapsody in Blue" features a famous clarinet glissando.
- **1927** — The clarinet choir ensemble first emerges.

## Body

### Development and Key Systems
The clarinet evolved from the Baroque chalumeau, a single-reed instrument with a limited range. Around 1700, Johann Christoph Denner added a register key, extending the range upward. Over the 18th and 19th centuries, makers added more keys to improve tuning and chromatic ability. A major advance came in 1812 with Iwan Müller's 13-key "omnitonic" clarinet, which used stuffed pads for airtight seals. In the 1840s, the Boehm system, adapted from flute keywork by Hyacinthe Klosé and Louis Auguste Buffet, simplified fingering and became the global standard. A competing German system, refined by Oskar Oehler in the early 20th century, is still used in Germany and Austria. The Albert system, a simpler design, persists in some jazz and folk music.

### Acoustics and Sound Production
The clarinet's cylindrical bore causes it to overblow at the twelfth (an octave and a perfect fifth) rather than the octave, creating three distinct registers: the dark chalumeau, the bright clarion, and the piercing altissimo. Sound is produced when air blown past the single reed causes it to vibrate against the mouthpiece, creating compression waves that travel down the tube. The player varies pitch by covering and uncovering tone holes to change the air column's length. The register key forces the reed to vibrate at a higher frequency, accessing the upper registers. The bell improves the tone of the lowest notes but does not amplify sound.

### Construction and Materials
Clarinet bodies are most commonly made from African blackwood (grenadilla), but also from plastic, hard rubber, or metal. The single reed is typically made from cane (*Arundo donax*). The instrument consists of a mouthpiece, barrel, upper and lower joints with keys and tone holes, and a flared bell. The Boehm system, with its ring keys and needle springs, is the most common keywork. The Oehler system uses more tone holes and different fingerings. The embouchure—the formation of the lips around the mouthpiece and reed—is crucial for tone and intonation.

### The Clarinet Family and Performance
The clarinet family has the largest pitch range of common woodwinds. Orchestras typically use two clarinetists playing B♭ and A soprano clarinets, which transpose to different keys. The use of multiple clarinets dates to the Classical period when limited keywork made playing in all keys difficult. Concert bands use larger families, including E♭, alto, bass, and contrabass clarinets. The clarinet choir, an ensemble of various clarinets, emerged in 1927.

### Repertoire and Genres
The clarinet entered the orchestra in the mid-18th century and was championed by Mozart, who wrote major works for it. It became a staple of the Romantic orchestra and a key solo instrument. In the early 20th century, it was central to jazz, with players like Benny Goodman, before being largely supplanted by the saxophone. The clarinet remains vital in classical music, klezmer, Brazilian choro, and other folk traditions.

## Terms
- **Chalumeau**: The Baroque single-reed instrument from which the clarinet evolved; also the name of the clarinet's low register.
- **Register key**: A key that, when opened, forces the reed to vibrate at a higher harmonic, allowing access to the clarion and altissimo registers.
- **Boehm system**: A key system for the clarinet, adapted from the flute, that uses ring keys and needle springs to simplify fingering.
- **Oehler system**: A German key system for the clarinet with more tone holes and different fingerings than the Boehm system.
- **Albert system**: A simpler key system based on the Müller clarinet, used in some jazz and folk music.
- **Embouchure**: The formation of the lips, facial muscles, and tongue around the mouthpiece and reed to control sound.
- **Transposing instrument**: An instrument for which the written note differs from the concert pitch produced; most clarinets are transposing.
- **Bore**: The interior tube of the instrument; the clarinet's is nearly cylindrical.
- **Ligature**: The device (often metal) that fastens the reed to the mouthpiece.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact origin of the word "clarinet" is debated, with possible roots in French, Provençal, or Latin.
- The specific contributions of Johann Christoph Denner versus his son Jacob Denner to the instrument's invention are uncertain.
- The tonal differences between the French (Boehm) and German (Oehler) clarinet systems are noted by composers like Richard Strauss, though the practical significance of these differences in modern instruments is debated.
