# Archaeological culture

## Overview
An archaeological culture is a recurring assemblage of artifacts, buildings, and monuments from a specific period and region, used by archaeologists to classify and interpret the material remains of a past human society. The concept is fundamental to culture-historical archaeology, providing a framework to order data and trace ancient groups, though its interpretation in terms of ethnic or political identities remains a subject of long-standing debate.

## Timeline
- **1780 onwards** — German scholars, notably Gustav Klemm, produce *Kulturgeschichte* (culture history) works, reflecting growing interest in ethnicity.
- **1836** — Christian Thomsen uses the term "culture" in an archaeological context in *Ledetraad til Nordisk Oldkyndighed*.
- **Late 19th C.** — Archaeologists in Scandinavia and central Europe increasingly use the German concept of culture to describe groups in the archaeological record.
- **Early 20th C.** — Gustaf Kossinna makes the idea of archaeological cultures central to archaeology, associating them strongly with race and ethnic groups.
- **Post-WWII** — Kossinna's racist theories lose influence, but the general culture-history approach becomes dominant.
- **Mid-20th C.** — Vere Gordon Childe formulates the classic definition of archaeological culture as a consistently recurring complex of associated traits.
- **1960s** — Processual archaeology critiques the concept, seeking more scientific explanations beyond description and classification.

## Body

### Concept and Definition
An archaeological culture is defined as a recurring assemblage of associated traits—such as pottery types, implements, ornaments, burial rites, and house forms—from a specific time and place. The classic definition, from Gordon Childe, posits that such a complex is the material expression of "a people." The concept is normative, assuming artifacts express cultural norms, and polythetic, meaning a combination of traits, not a single one, is required for classification. Cultures are typically named after a diagnostic artifact type (e.g., Linear Pottery culture) or a type site (e.g., Hallstatt culture).

### Development and Application
The term entered archaeology from 19th-century German ethnography, where *Kultur* described the distinctive ways of life of a particular *Volk* (people). Gustaf Kossinna later used the concept to reconstruct the movements of prehistoric ancestors of modern ethnic groups, strongly associating cultures with race. After World War II, Childe and others adopted the basic concept stripped of its racial aspects, using it as a classifying device. Culture-historians used it to explain change through mechanisms like diffusion (spread of ideas) or migration (movement of peoples).

### Criticism and Modern Use
The concept has been criticized for assuming a straightforward link between material culture and human societies. Archaeologists now recognize that what was thought to be a single culture may represent several discrete groups (e.g., the Windmill Hill culture), or that a single group may have radically different material cultures based on lifestyle (e.g., sedentary vs. nomadic Bedouin). Processualists criticized it as "idealist" and a mere "rag-tag assemblage of ideas." Despite critiques, the archaeological culture remains a useful tool for sorting artifacts, especially in European archaeology.

## Terms
- **Archaeological culture**: A recurring assemblage of artifacts and features from a specific period and region, used to classify material remains.
- **Culture-historical archaeology**: An archaeological paradigm that uses the concept of archaeological cultures to trace ancient peoples and explain change via diffusion or migration.
- **Normative culture**: The view that artifacts are expressions of cultural norms that define a culture.
- **Polythetic classification**: A system where a group is defined by a combination of traits, not a single necessary and sufficient one.
- **Diffusion**: The spread of cultural traits or ideas from one group to another.
- **Migrationism**: The explanation of cultural change in the archaeological record as resulting from the movement of peoples.
- **Type site**: The site at which an archaeological culture was first defined and described.
- **Kultur-Gruppen**: German term for "culture groups," used by Kossinna.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the epistemological validity of equating archaeological cultures with past human societies, ethnic groups, or political entities. Critics argue the concept is "idealist" and treats culture as a static assemblage of ideas, while proponents find it a necessary classifying tool. There is ongoing discussion about whether material culture differences reflect distinct peoples or are due to environmental factors, lifestyle variations, or other non-ethnic causes.
