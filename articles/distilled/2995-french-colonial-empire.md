# French colonial empire

## Overview
The French colonial empire was a global system of overseas territories, protectorates, and mandates under French rule from the 16th century until the mid-20th century. It is divided into two major phases: a "first empire" that collapsed after the Napoleonic Wars, and a "second empire" built from 1830 onward, which became the world's second-largest by 1914. The empire was justified by the ideology of the *mission civilisatrice* ("civilizing mission") and was characterized by economic exploitation, cultural assimilation policies, and significant violence. Its legacy persists through the Francophonie, economic ties, and overseas territories that remain part of France today.

## Timeline
- **1605** — Founding of Port Royal in Acadia, marking the start of the first French colonial empire in North America.
- **1763** — Treaty of Utrecht ends the Seven Years' War; France loses most of New France (Canada) and Indian outposts to Britain.
- **1804** — Haiti gains independence after a successful slave revolt, ending French rule in its richest colony.
- **1830** — French invasion of Algiers begins the second French colonial empire.
- **1887** — French Indochina is formally established, uniting French territories in Southeast Asia.
- **1912** — France establishes a protectorate over Morocco.
- **1946** — The French Union is created by the Fourth Republic's constitution, nominally replacing the colonial empire.
- **1954** — French defeat at the Battle of Điện Biên Phủ leads to withdrawal from Indochina.
- **1962** — Algerian independence is recognized after a brutal war, marking the effective end of the French empire.
- **1960** — Most remaining African colonies gain independence during the "Year of Africa."

## Body

### First French Colonial Empire (16th century–1814)
France began establishing colonies in the Americas, the Caribbean, and India in the 16th century. The first permanent settlement was Port Royal (1605) in Acadia, followed by Quebec (1608), which became the heart of the fur-trading colony of New France. This empire relied on alliances with Indigenous peoples and was less populated than British colonies. In the Caribbean, France developed profitable plantation economies in islands like Saint-Domingue (Haiti), which became the world's richest sugar colony, built on enslaved African labor. French presence in Africa began with trading posts in Senegal and Madagascar, also focused on the slave trade and plantation agriculture.

The first empire was destroyed by a series of conflicts with Britain, culminating in the Seven Years' War (1756–1763), which cost France nearly all its North American and Indian territories. The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) resulted in the loss of its most valuable colony. Napoleon's sale of Louisiana to the United States in 1803 and the final defeat in the Napoleonic Wars left France with only a few remnants of its former empire.

### Second French Colonial Empire (post-1830)
The second empire began with the conquest of Algeria in 1830, a process of brutal military subjugation that lasted decades and caused massive Algerian casualties. Under Napoleon III (1852–1870), France expanded into Indochina (Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos) and parts of Africa. After the Franco-Prussian War (1870–1871), the Third Republic aggressively pursued a "Scramble for Africa," creating vast federations like French West Africa and French Equatorial Africa. By 1914, the empire stretched across Africa, the Middle East (Syria, Lebanon), and the Pacific.

The empire's core ideology was the *mission civilisatrice*, the duty to spread French language, culture, and republican values. In practice, this meant a rigid hierarchy: a small European settler population (largest in Algeria) held political and economic power, while the vast majority of colonized people were "subjects" with limited rights. Education was used as a tool of assimilation, creating a small Westernized elite but often fueling nationalist movements.

### Decolonization
After World War II, anti-colonial movements intensified. France fought costly wars to retain control of Indochina (1946–1954) and Algeria (1954–1962), both of which it ultimately lost. The Algerian War, in particular, caused a political crisis in France and led to the fall of the Fourth Republic. Most African colonies gained independence peacefully in 1960. However, France maintained significant influence through neocolonial ties, military interventions, and economic control (e.g., the CFA franc). Some territories, like New Caledonia and Mayotte, chose to remain part of France as overseas departments or collectivities.

## Terms
- **Mission civilisatrice**: The ideological justification for French colonialism, claiming a duty to spread French civilization, language, and republican values to "uncivilized" peoples.
- **Assimilation**: The colonial policy aiming to transform colonized peoples into culturally French citizens, though full citizenship was rarely granted in practice.
- **Pieds-noirs**: European settlers, primarily of French, Spanish, and Italian descent, who lived in Algeria before its independence in 1962.
- **Évolués**: A term for colonized people who had adopted French culture and were considered "evolved" enough to be granted limited citizenship rights.
- **Francophonie**: The global community of French-speaking peoples and nations, a legacy of the colonial empire.
- **CFA franc**: A currency used by 14 African countries, historically tied to the French franc and later the euro, symbolizing ongoing economic ties.
- **Protectorate**: A form of colonial rule where a local ruler remained in place but was subject to French control over foreign policy and key administrative areas.
- **Scramble for Africa**: The late 19th-century period of rapid colonization of Africa by European powers, including France.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Legacy of violence**: Historians debate the scale and intentionality of violence during conquest, particularly in Algeria, where estimates of deaths during the conquest range from 500,000 to 1,000,000.
- **Assimilation vs. association**: Scholars analyze whether French colonial policy was primarily aimed at cultural assimilation (making subjects French) or association (ruling through indirect rule and preserving local structures), with evidence for both approaches in different colonies and periods.
- **Impact of colonial education**: There is debate over whether French education systems were primarily tools of control and cultural domination or whether they inadvertently created the intellectual and political elites who led independence movements.
- **Neocolonialism**: Critics argue that formal independence did not end French dominance, pointing to ongoing political, military, and economic interventions in former colonies (sometimes termed *Françafrique*) as evidence of a continued imperial relationship.
