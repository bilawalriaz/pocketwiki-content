# Buster Keaton

## Overview

Buster Keaton (1895–1966) was an American actor, comedian, and filmmaker whose silent-era comedies established him as one of cinema’s most innovative visual artists. Known as "The Great Stone Face" for his deadpan expression, Keaton combined precise physical stunts with cinematic ingenuity, creating classics like *The General* (1926) and *Sherlock Jr.* (1924). His career peaked between 1920 and 1929, declined under MGM’s studio system, and experienced a late-life revival through television and critical reevaluation.

## Timeline

- **1895** — Born Joseph Frank Keaton in Piqua, Kansas, into a vaudeville family.
- **1906–1916** — Performed in *The Three Keatons*, a family vaudeville act known for roughhouse comedy.
- **1917** — Met Roscoe "Fatty" Arbuckle at Talmadge Studios; began film career.
- **1920** — Starred in first feature, *The Saphead*; launched independent production unit.
- **1923–1928** — Directed and starred in major silent features: *Our Hospitality*, *Sherlock Jr.*, *The General*, *Steamboat Bill, Jr.*
- **1928** — Signed with MGM; lost creative control over his work.
- **1933** — Fired by MGM; career as leading man ended.
- **1934–1936** — Made comedies for Educational Pictures; brief comeback.
- **1939–1941** — Starred in two-reel comedies for Columbia Pictures.
- **1940** — Married Eleanor Norris; personal life stabilized.
- **1950s–1960s** — Revived via TV appearances and cameos; silent films re-released.
- **1966** — Died of lung cancer in Woodland Hills, California.

## Body

### Early Life and Vaudeville

Keaton was born into a vaudeville family and began performing at age three in *The Three Keatons*, a act where his father threw him around the stage. The physically demanding routine earned the family the nickname "The Roughest Act That Was Ever in the History of the Stage." Keaton learned to take falls safely, developing the technique that would define his screen persona. He adopted his famous deadpan expression after noticing that laughing disrupted audience engagement.

### Silent Film Career

Keaton transitioned to film in 1917, working with Arbuckle before launching his own production unit in 1920. His early two-reel comedies—*One Week* (1920), *The Playhouse* (1921), *The Electric House* (1922)—showcased his knack for visual gags and mechanical precision. He moved to features with *Our Hospitality* (1923) and *Three Ages* (1923), culminating in *The General* (1926), a Civil War epic praised by Orson Welles as “perhaps the greatest film ever made.” Despite mixed contemporary reviews, *The General* became his most celebrated work.

### MGM and Decline

In 1928, Keaton signed with MGM, trading independence for financial security. The studio system stifled his creativity; he was denied input on scripts and forced to use stunt doubles. Personal turmoil followed: his wife Natalie divorced him in 1932, and he descended into alcoholism. Fired in 1933, he briefly worked in European productions and Educational Pictures shorts before returning to MGM as a gag writer.

### Comeback and Later Years

Keaton’s career revived in the 1940s with character roles and cameos in prestige films like *Sunset Boulevard* (1950) and *Limelight* (1952). Television appearances in the 1950s and 1960s reintroduced him to new audiences. He collaborated with younger comedians like Woody Allen and Mel Brooks, who cited him as a major influence. His final film role was in *The Scribe* (1966), completed days before his death.

## Terms

- **Deadpan**: A facial expression showing no emotion, used for comic effect.
- **Two-reel comedy**: A short film format lasting roughly 20 minutes, common in early cinema.
- **Slapstick**: A genre of comedy involving exaggerated physical actions and violence.
- **Pork pie hat**: A small, round hat with a narrow brim, Keaton’s signature headwear.
- **Gag writer**: A screenwriter specializing in comedic material and visual jokes.
- **Studio system**: The centralized Hollywood production model dominated by major studios in the 1930s–1950s.
- **States-rights market**: A distribution method where regional buyers purchased exclusive film rights.
- **Kinescope**: A recording of a live television broadcast, used before widespread videotape.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Nickname origin**: The story of Keaton being called "Buster" after falling down stairs at 18 months is widely repeated but possibly apocryphal.
- **Creative decline at MGM**: While Keaton blamed the studio system for his artistic downfall, some argue his personal struggles and the transition to sound contributed equally.
- **Legacy of *The General***: Though now hailed as a masterpiece, its initial reception was mixed, raising questions about how historical context shapes film appreciation.
- **Unreleased works**: Several of Keaton’s projects, including *Ten Girls Ago* (1962) and *El Moderno Barba Azul* (1946), remain obscure or poorly received, prompting debate over their place in his legacy.
