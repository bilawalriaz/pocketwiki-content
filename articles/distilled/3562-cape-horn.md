# Cape Horn

## Overview
Cape Horn is the southernmost headland of the Tierra del Fuego archipelago in Chile, marking the northern boundary of the Drake Passage where the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans meet. Historically, it was a critical but extremely hazardous milestone on the global clipper trade route, and remains a legendary challenge for modern sailors.

## Timeline
- **1526** — Spanish explorer Francisco de Hoces is blown south of the Strait of Magellan, becoming the first European to sight the area.
- **1578** — Sir Francis Drake is blown south of Tierra del Fuego, confirming it is an island with open sea to its south.
- **1616** — Dutchmen Willem Schouten and Jacob Le Maire are the first to round and name the cape "Kaap Hoorn."
- **1624** — The Horn is discovered to be an island, not the southern tip of a continent.
- **1814** — The clipper route via Cape Horn becomes a major artery for global trade.
- **1914** — The opening of the Panama Canal greatly reduces commercial shipping around the Horn.
- **1949** — The *Pamir* becomes the last commercial sailing ship to round Cape Horn with cargo.
- **1968-69** — Robin Knox-Johnston completes the first solo non-stop circumnavigation via Cape Horn.

## Body

### Geography and Navigation
Cape Horn is located on Hornos Island in the Hermite Islands group, within Chile's Cabo de Hornos National Park. The Chilean Navy maintains a station on the island. The cape's extreme southern latitude (56° S) places it in the path of the "roaring forties," "furious fifties," and "screaming sixties"—bands of powerful, uninterrupted westerly winds. The Andes and Antarctic Peninsula funnel these winds into the Drake Passage, creating severe conditions. Large ocean waves become shorter and steeper in the shallow waters south of the Horn, and rogue waves can reach 30 meters (98 ft). Icebergs are a significant hazard, especially from August when they can extend north of 40° S.

### Historic Trade Route
From the 18th to early 20th centuries, Cape Horn was a vital part of the clipper routes carrying wool, grain, gold, and other goods between Europe, the Americas, and the Far East. The passage exacted a heavy toll due to its hazards; the only nearby servicing facilities were in the Falkland Islands, which were notoriously expensive. German steel-hulled sailing ships like the Flying P-Liners were specifically designed to withstand the Horn's conditions. The route's importance declined with the advent of steamships and the Panama Canal.

### Modern Significance
Today, there are no regular commercial routes around the Horn. It is primarily traversed by cruise ships and is a major goal for recreational and competitive sailors. A "rounding" of Cape Horn is considered the yachting equivalent of climbing Mount Everest. Major yacht races like the Vendée Globe and Volvo Ocean Race include the Horn in their course. The fastest round-the-world sailing routes still follow this path.

### Discovery and Early Exploration
The cape was first sighted by Europeans in 1526. In 1616, Schouten and Le Maire, seeking an alternative route to the Dutch East India Company's monopoly, became the first to round it, naming it after the city of Hoorn. The discovery that the Horn was an island, not a continent's tip, came in 1624. The extreme difficulty of the passage is underscored by the fact that Antarctica, only 650 km (400 mi) away, was not discovered until 1820.

## Terms
- **Clipper route:** The historical sailing route around the three great southern capes (Horn, Good Hope, Leeuwin) used for global trade in the 19th century.
- **Drake Passage:** The strait between South America and Antarctica, considered one of the most hazardous shipping lanes in the world.
- **Rogue wave:** An unusually large, unpredictable, and dangerous ocean wave that can reach heights of up to 30 meters (98 ft) near Cape Horn.
- **Williwaw:** A sudden, violent down-slope wind common in the region's narrow channels.
- **Pitchpole:** A sailing accident where a boat somersaults end-over-end, as happened to the yacht *Tzu Hang* near the Horn.
- **Doubling the Horn:** The traditional definition of sailing from above 50° S in the Pacific, around the Horn, to above 50° S in the Atlantic, and back against prevailing winds.
- **Flying P-Liners:** A fleet of German steel-hulled sailing ships built in the late 19th/early 20th century, designed to withstand the Horn's conditions.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact point considered to be "Cape Horn" is debated; the Chilean Navy station and memorial are on a point about one mile east-northeast of the authentic, more inaccessible headland.
- The first person to truly round the Horn in a small boat is disputed; while Joshua Slocum passed the area in 1895, he likely used inshore channels, with Conor O'Brien's *Saoirse* (1923-25) often cited as the first to sail outside the Horn proper.
