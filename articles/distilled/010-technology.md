# Technology

## Overview
Technology is the application of conceptual knowledge to achieve practical goals, especially in a reproducible way, encompassing both tangible products (tools, machines) and intangible ones (software). It plays a critical role in science, engineering, and everyday life, driving economic growth and social change while also raising ethical and environmental concerns. From the earliest stone tools to artificial intelligence, technology has reshaped human society, communication, and the environment, sparking ongoing philosophical and political debates about its role and mitigation of its downsides.

## Timeline
- **c. 2 Mya** — Hominids make first stone tools by hammering flakes off a pebble
- **c. 1.5 Mya** — Continuous human fire-use; cooking hypothesis links fire to brain growth
- **c. 450 kya** — Archaeological evidence of shelter; clothing appears 90–120 kya
- **c. 10 kya** — Neolithic Revolution begins; polished stone axes enable farming
- **c. 4,000 BCE** — Bronze and brass alloys discovered; wheel invented (estimates 5,500–3,000 BCE)
- **c. 3,500 BCE** — First drawings of wheeled carts; first long-distance road in use
- **c. 3,000 BCE** — First two-wheeled carts in Mesopotamia and Iran
- **312 BCE** — First Roman aqueduct built; final one in 226 CE
- **18th century** — Steam power triggers Industrial Revolution in the UK
- **1947** — Transistor invented, compacting computers and leading to digital transition
- **1957** — Sputnik 1 launched, beginning the Space Age

## Body

### Etymology
The term "technology" dates to the early 17th century, meaning "systematic treatment," from Greek *tékhnē* ("craft, art") and *-logía* ("study, knowledge"). In the 19th century, continental Europeans used *Technik* (German) or *technique* (French) for "way of doing," while *Technologie* referred to the academic or political discipline studying arts and crafts. English lacked this distinction, translating both as "technology." In the 20th century, following scientific progress and the Second Industrial Revolution, technology took on its modern meaning: the systemic use of knowledge to practical ends.

### Prehistoric and Neolithic History
Tools were developed through observation and trial and error. Around 2 Mya, hominids made stone hand axes by hammering flakes off pebbles, refined into pressure flaking by 75 kya. Fire use, described by Darwin as "possibly the greatest ever made by man," dates to at least 1.5 Mya; cooking increased food digestibility and nutrient value. The cooking hypothesis proposes this promoted hominid brain growth, though some researchers find evidence inconclusive. Hearths dated to 790 kya likely intensified socialization and may have contributed to language emergence. Clothing (90–120 kya) and shelter (450 kya) enabled migration out of Africa around 200 kya.

The Neolithic Revolution accelerated innovation and social complexity. Polished stone axes allowed forest clearance and farming, feeding larger populations and enabling sedentism. Labor specialization increased, leading to the first cities like Uruk and civilizations like Sumer, driven by hierarchical structures, trade, war, and collective action for irrigation. Writing spread cultural knowledge, becoming the basis for history and libraries. Furnace and bellows enabled smelting of native metals (gold, copper, silver, lead) from about 10 kya; alloys like bronze and brass appeared around 4,000 BCE, and iron alloys like steel around 1,800 BCE.

### Ancient to Modern History
Ancient societies harnessed wind and water power. The earliest sailing ship was a Nile boat around 7,000 BCE; Egyptians used annual Nile flooding for irrigation, and Sumerians built canal systems. The wheel, invented independently in Mesopotamia, the Northern Caucasus, and Central Europe (estimates 5,500–3,000 BCE), revolutionized trade and war. The oldest-known wooden wheel (as of 2024) was found in Slovenia's Ljubljana Marsh, dated 5,100–5,350 years old. The potter's wheel enabled mass pottery production, while water wheels and windmills revolutionized nonhuman power. The Minoans built a fully paved 50 km road around 2,000 BCE; Romans built aqueducts extending over 450 km and the Cloaca Maxima sewer, still in use today.

The Middle Ages saw silk production, the horse collar, and horseshoes; simple machines combined into wheelbarrows, windmills, and clocks. Universities like Oxford and Cambridge spread scientific ideas. The Renaissance introduced the movable type printing press to Europe. The 18th-century Industrial Revolution in the UK brought steam power and the factory system; the Second Industrial Revolution followed with electricity, railroads, automobiles, and airplanes. The 20th century brought nuclear fission, analog computers, and the transistor (1947), enabling digital computing. The Information Age began with optical fiber and the Internet; the Space Age started with Sputnik 1 in 1957. Modern medicine developed CT, PET, and MRI scanning, dialysis machines, and defibrillators.

### Impact
Technological change is the largest cause of long-term economic growth. Fire enabled a wider diet and smelting; the agricultural revolution allowed settled societies with militaries and organized religion. Technologies improve welfare but can disrupt social hierarchies, cause pollution, and harm groups. Since the 1970s, environmental impacts have been criticized, spurring investment in clean energy. Social media's rise has potential repercussions on democracy, with research investigating disinformation, polarization, hate speech, and propaganda.

Regarding jobs, past automation substituted and complemented labor, creating new higher-paying jobs; computers did not create significant net technological unemployment. A 2017 survey found no consensus on whether AI would increase long-term unemployment. The World Economic Forum predicted AI would replace 85 million jobs and create 97 million by 2025. A study by MIT economist Daron Acemoglu (1990–2007) found one robot per 1,000 workers decreased employment-to-population ratio by 0.2% and lowered wages by 0.42%. Security concerns include cryptocurrency laundering; in March 2022, North Korea used Blender.io to launder over $20.5 million from Axie Infinity, leading the U.S. Treasury to sanction the mixer—its first action against one.

### Philosophy, Ethics, and Futures
Philosophy of technology studies the "practice of designing and creating artifacts" and emerged as a discipline over the past two centuries. Early views saw technology as an extension of human faculties; Marx saw it as a capitalist tool but potentially liberating. Later philosophers shifted to daily life; modern scholarship analyzes sociotechnical systems and value judgments. Debates split between technological determinism (technology causes unavoidable social change) and social construction (technology is shaped by culture and politics). Neil Postman distinguished tool-using societies from "technopolies" dominated by technological ideology.

The ethics of technology analyzes ethical implications and mitigations, covering bioethics (cloning, genetic engineering), computer ethics, cyberethics (privacy, censorship), nanoethics, and engineering ethics. AI ethics addresses AI alignment and algorithmic bias; some researchers warn of hypothetical AI takeover risks. Futures studies explores plausible futures and human values, using surveys, modeling, and simulations. Existential risk researchers analyze threats like artificial general intelligence, nuclear warfare, and climate change; philosopher Nick Bostrom introduced the "vulnerable world" concept, questioning assumptions that technological progress is always beneficial.

### Emerging Technologies and Movements
Emerging technologies include nanotechnology, biotechnology, robotics, 3D printing, and blockchains. Ray Kurzweil (2005) predicted the next revolution would rest on genetics, nanotechnology, and robotics. Genetic engineering could enable directed evolution, raising concerns about eugenics; nanobots could destroy cancer cells; autonomous robots may replace humans in dangerous tasks. Half of machine learning experts surveyed in 2018 believe AI will accomplish every task better than humans by 2063 and automate all jobs by 2140, prompting debates about universal basic income.

Movements include appropriate technology (1960s counterculture preference for local, sustainable tech), technological utopianism (belief that technology can bring utopia, including transhumanism and singularitarianism), and anti-technology backlash. The earliest revolt was Luddism against textile automation. Ted Kaczynski's Unabomber Manifesto (1970s–1990s) denounced technology's impacts. Subcultures like off-the-grid and ecovillage movements advocate withdrawal or harmony with nature.

### Relation to Science and Engineering
Engineering is the process by which technology is developed, requiring problem-solving under constraints. Technology is "action-oriented" while science is explanatory; philosopher Henryk Skolimowski framed it: "science concerns itself with what is, technology with what is to be." Most technologies derive from engineering, tinkering, and chance rather than scientific knowledge—jet engines were invented through trial-and-error, and penicillin was discovered by accident. However, modern technology increasingly relies on scientific knowledge: by 1989, patents averaged one citation of scientific literature each, and a 2021 analysis found science-based patents are 26% more valuable. Tool use is also found in non-human animals, including chimpanzees, dolphins, crows, and capuchin monkeys; beaver dams dramatically impact river ecosystems.

## Terms
- **Cooking hypothesis**: Proposal that cooking food promoted increased hominid brain size
- **Neolithic Revolution**: First Agricultural Revolution; transition to farming and sedentism
- **Technological determinism**: Idea that technologies cause unavoidable social changes
- **Social construction**: View that technologies are shaped by cultural values, laws, and politics
- **Technological unemployment**: Job loss resulting from automation replacing human labor
- **AI alignment**: Ensuring AI behaviors align with creators' intended goals and interests
- **Existential risk**: Risks that could lead to human extinction or civilizational collapse
- **Technological singularity**: Hypothetical event horizon where machine superintelligence accelerates progress incomprehensibly
- **Appropriate technology**: Locally autonomous, sustainable, decentralized technology
- **Technosignatures**: Signs of technology use given off by alien civilizations

## Debates and open questions
- Whether cooking promoted brain growth: some researchers find the evidence inconclusive
- What triggered the progression from Neolithic villages to first cities is not specifically known
- Whether AI will follow past automation trends or create significant net technological unemployment; a 2017 survey found no consensus among economists
- Whether cryptocurrency needs more transparency and stability versus privacy
- Whether climate engineering can halt global warming; remains highly controversial
- Whether technological progress is always beneficial, scientific openness always preferable, or societies can afford to wait before preparing mitigations for dangerous technologies
- Whether directed evolution could lead to eugenics or extreme social inequality
- The direction of causality between scientific discovery and technological innovation
