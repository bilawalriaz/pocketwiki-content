# Writing

## Overview

Writing is the act of creating a persistent, usually visual representation of language on a surface. As a structured system of communication, writing is also known as written language, and it historically emerged to record corresponding spoken languages. A particular set of symbols (a script) plus the rules by which they encode a spoken language constitutes a writing system. Writing matters because it extends the capacities of spoken language—transmitting it across space and storing it for future reading—and allows humans to externalize thinking in forms easier to reflect on, process slowly, elaborate, reconsider, and revise. Literacy, the ability to read and write, is a key driver of social mobility and civic participation.

## Timeline

- **c. 8000 BC** — Mesopotamians begin using clay tokens to count goods
- **c. 3200–3100 BC** — Cuneiform emerges in Mesopotamia; Egyptian hieroglyphs develop
- **c. 3200–2900 BC** — Proto-Elamite script in use across modern-day Iran
- **c. 2600 BC** — Indus script appears (undeciphered; status debated); earliest coherent Egyptian texts
- **c. 1800 BC** — Proto-Sinaitic script invented by Canaanite miners—the only known invention of the alphabet
- **c. 1450–1200 BC** — Linear B used by Mycenaean Greeks
- **c. 1250–1200 BC** — Earliest Chinese writing: oracle bone inscriptions from the Late Shang
- **c. 1050 BC** — Phoenician alphabet, a direct descendant of Proto-Sinaitic
- **c. 800 BC** — Greek alphabet, the first to represent vowel sounds, adapted from Phoenician
- **c. 3rd century BC** — Earliest identified Maya inscription
- **1st century AD** — Latest cuneiform texts in Akkadian discovered thus far

## Body

### Tools, materials, and motivations

Writing involves interaction among tools, intentions, cultural customs, cognitive routines, genres, and system constraints. Implements include fingers, styluses, ink brushes, pencils, pens, and lithography; surfaces include stone, clay tablets, bamboo slips, papyrus, wax tablets, vellum, parchment, paper, copperplate, and slate. The typewriter and digital word processor allow mechanically consistent text via keyboard. Recent software, including generative pre-trained transformers, can generate longer, register-appropriate texts from a prompt without additional human input.

Historically, writing emerged to address the needs of societies growing in economic and social complexity: tracking produce and wealth, recording history, codifying knowledge, and aiding administration through legal codes, census records, contracts, and treaties. Individual motivations include operating beyond memory limits (to-do lists, recipes, maps), disseminating ideas, creativity and storytelling, maintaining social networks, business correspondence, and life writing. Digital communication systems have made writing an increasingly important feature of daily life; substantial amounts of everyday writing characterize most workplaces in developed countries.

### Contemporary uses

Writing permeates modern work, civic participation, household management, and leisure. In business and finance, written documents underpin regulations, reports, records, internal coordination, client communications, contracts, tax records, and accounting reports. Financial institutions are particularly dependent on written records to maintain integrity.

Governance and law rely on written constitutions, rules, and procedures. Legislatures draft laws; executive branches present regulations; governments maintain records on citizens concerning identities, life events, licenses, criminal charges, and tax liability.

In science and scholarship, research is published as journal articles or monographs. Grants require proposals; data is collected in lab notebooks; preprints are presented at conferences. Peer review determines whether work is worth publishing—publication does not establish truth, only that work merits specialist attention; codification as reliable knowledge occurs through citation and aggregation.

Journalism is central to citizen engagement with government, economics, disasters, geopolitics, sports, and entertainment. Rapid changes in news production and distribution have created challenges for journalism ethics developed over the past century.

Formal education is the social context most strongly associated with learning to write. Students write on exams, in essays, notes, homework, and assessments—some directed at learning writing, much focused on subject learning.

### Relationship with spoken and signed language

Writing, speech, and signing are three distinct modalities of language. Spoken and signed language is more immediate, reflecting local context and emotion via paralinguistic cues, with less premeditation, informal vocabulary, shorter sentences, and turn-taking elements. Written language is more structured and formal, permanent, allows planning and revision, and must convey meaning without tone of voice or body language, resulting in more explicit descriptions. Written languages generally change more slowly than spoken ones, potentially retaining archaic features and contributing to diglossia.

Grammatically, written language is predominantly declarative, with fewer imperatives, interrogatives, and exclamatives. Noun phrases are more third-person; spoken English uses simple aspect more than perfect or progressive. In information packaging, written English relatively favors structures like "On the hill, there was a tree" over "There was a tree on the hill," and uses passive voice more. Written language has higher lexical density, fewer first/second-person pronouns, fewer interjections, more nouns, and fewer verbs with content clause complements.

### Classification of writing systems

Writing systems are classified by what units of language their symbols represent. Phonographies represent sounds—alphabets represent phonemes, syllabaries represent syllables. Logographies represent units of meaning (words or morphemes).

Logographic systems include Chinese characters (used for Chinese and Japanese, historically Korean and Vietnamese), cuneiform, and Maya script. Syllabaries, best suited to languages with simple syllable structure like Japanese, include Linear B and Cherokee. Alphabets represent consonants and vowels. Abjads (consonantaries) generally have letters only for consonants, with optional diacritics for vowels; the earliest alphabets were abjads, mostly native to the Middle East. Abugidas (alphasyllabaries), common in India and Southeast Asia, indicate vowels through diacritics or consonant modification; the term derives from the Geʽez script of Ethiopia and Eritrea.

### History and origins

Writing developed independently in Mesopotamia and Ancient Egypt (c. 3200–3100 BC), Ancient China (c. 1250 BC), and Mesoamerica (c. 1 AD). The first writing dates to the Neolithic era with clay tablets tracking livestock; the first example is from the Sumerian city of Uruk at the end of the 4th millennium BC. Cuneiform was pictographic at first, later evolving wedge-shaped signs representing language phonemically.

In Mesopotamia, Denise Schmandt-Besserat's 1970s theory linked cuneiform to clay tokens from the Zagros region of Iran. Tokens were placed in sealed clay containers (bulla); quantities were impressed on surfaces, then tokens were dispensed with entirely. Cuneiform emerged c. 3200 BC; phonetic elements appeared by the 29th century BC. From the 26th century BC it was adapted for Akkadian, then Elamite, Hattian, Hurrian, and Hittite. The latest Akkadian cuneiform texts date from the 1st century AD.

Egyptian hieroglyphs developed from pictographic proto-writing. The earliest known hieroglyphs are clay labels for ruler "Scorpion I" (c. 33rd century BC) or the Narmer Palette (c. 3100 BC). Around 800 hieroglyphs were used in the Old, Middle, and New Kingdom periods (2686–1077 BC); by the Greco-Roman period (30 BC–642 AD), over 5,000 glyphs are attested. Literacy was concentrated among an educated elite of scribes.

The Maya script, the best-developed and only deciphered pre-Columbian Mesoamerican script, dates to the 3rd century BC and used around 800 symbols—mainly logograms with syllabograms. The earliest Chinese writing—oracle bone inscriptions for divination—dates from c. 1200 BC. The Proto-Elamite script (c. 3200–2900 BC) used over 1,000 signs; Elamite cuneiform (c. 2500–331 BC) used around 130 symbols at any time.

The alphabet was invented only once, by Canaanite turquoise miners in the Sinai Peninsula c. 1800 BC, adapting concepts and letterforms from Egyptian hieroglyphs with West Semitic sound values. The Phoenician alphabet (c. 1050 BC) descended from Proto-Sinaitic; Greek (c. 800 BC) added vowels; Latin, Cyrillic, and other scripts descended from Greek variants, while Aramaic script gave rise to Square Hebrew, Arabic, and Brahmic scripts.

### Influence on society

Written language facilitates preservation and transmission of culture, history, and knowledge, enabling complex legal, administrative, and educational systems—for example, the Code of Hammurabi. Plato, through Socrates in *Phaedrus*, worried writing would weaken memory and that written words cannot answer questions. Marshall McLuhan (1911–1980), in *The Gutenberg Galaxy* (1962), argued the printing press shifted society from oral tradition to written culture, fostering individualism, nationalism, linear thinking, and detached reasoning; he asserted "the medium is the message." Critics argue he overemphasized the medium and was overly deterministic.

Diglossia occurs when two varieties of a language—often one written, one spoken—are used in different contexts: the "high variety" (often written) is standardized and formal; the "low variety" (often spoken) is dynamic and informal, as with Modern Standard Arabic versus regional Arabic varieties. Digraphia occurs when a language is written in different scripts, as Serbian uses Cyrillic or Latin, and Hindustani uses Devanagari or the Urdu alphabet.

### Orthography

Orthography comprises rules and conventions for writing a language, including how graphemes correspond to speech. Shallow orthographies (Serbo-Croatian, Finnish) have one-to-one phoneme–grapheme correspondence; deep orthographies (English, French) have complex sound–symbol relationships—e.g., /f/ as ⟨f⟩, ⟨ph⟩, or ⟨gh⟩. Orthographies also include punctuation, capitalization, word breaks, and emphasis rules.

## Terms

- **Script**: A particular set of symbols used in writing.
- **Writing system**: A script plus the rules by which it encodes a spoken language.
- **Literacy**: The ability to read and write.
- **Logogram**: A written character representing a word or morpheme.
- **Syllabary**: A set of symbols representing syllables.
- **Abjad**: An alphabet with letters generally only for consonants.
- **Abugida**: An alphabet where vowels are indicated by diacritics or consonant modification.
- **Diglossia**: Two distinct varieties of a language used in different social contexts.
- **Digraphia**: A language written in different scripts.
- **Orthography**: Rules and conventions for writing a language, including grapheme–phoneme correspondence.

## Debates and open questions

- The Indus script (c. 2600–2000 BC) remains undeciphered, and whether it functioned as true writing is not agreed upon.
- The Proto-Elamite script's inscriptions "have been, and will remain, highly problematic in a discussion of writing because they represent a very unclear period of literacy."
- The Proto-Sinaitic script's precise dating and the graphical origins of many letterforms remain unclear; the script remains undeciphered.
- Whether notation systems for signed languages like SignWriting constitute a written form of sign language is not universally agreed.
- McLuhan's theories are critiqued as overemphasizing the medium and being overly deterministic.
