# Medieval music

## Overview
Medieval music is the sacred and secular music of Western Europe from approximately the 6th to 15th centuries, forming the first major era of Western classical music. It encompasses the development of liturgical chant, the invention of polyphony (multiple independent melodic lines), and the creation of foundational music notation systems that enabled the documentation and transmission of musical ideas, moving beyond purely oral tradition.

## Timeline
- **c. 500–1000** — Early Medieval period; development of regional chant traditions (Gregorian, Ambrosian, Mozarabic).
- **9th century** — Earliest treatises on organum (e.g., *Musica enchiriadis*) describe early polyphony.
- **c. 1000–1300** — High Medieval period; flowering of Notre-Dame school polyphony (Léonin, Pérotin).
- **c. 1150–1250** — Ars antiqua style; development of rhythmic modes and the motet from clausulae.
- **12th–13th centuries** — Height of troubadour (Occitan) and trouvère (Old French) secular song traditions.
- **1310–1314** — Publication of *Roman de Fauvel*, marking the start of the Ars nova.
- **c. 1320** — Philippe de Vitry's treatise *Ars Nova* introduces new rhythmic notation.
- **14th century** — Ars nova style; Guillaume de Machaut composes the first complete polyphonic mass ordinary.
- **Late 14th century** — Ars subtilior style emerges, characterized by extreme rhythmic and notational complexity.
- **Early 15th century** — Transitional period; English *contenance Angloise* influences continental composers like Dufay.

## Body

### Genres and Forms
Medieval music included both sacred and secular genres. Liturgical music was dominated by monophonic (single-line) Gregorian chant. Polyphony began with **organum**, which added voices to chant, evolving from strict parallel motion to more free and florid styles. By the High Middle Ages, complex polyphonic forms like the **motet** (with multiple texts) and **conductus** emerged, often associated with the Notre-Dame school. Secular music included the monophonic songs of **troubadours** and **trouvères** on themes of courtly love, and later, polyphonic forms like the Italian **madrigal** and **caccia** (a canonic form). Dance music, such as the **estampie**, represents most surviving purely instrumental music.

### Instruments
A wide variety of instruments accompanied or performed music. Woodwinds included wooden **flutes**, **recorders**, and the **gemshorn**. Plucked strings like the **lute** (a predecessor to the guitar), **gittern**, and **psaltery** were common. Bowed strings included the Byzantine **lyra** and the **hurdy-gurdy**, a mechanical string instrument. Early versions of the **pipe organ**, **fiddle** (vielle), and **sackbut** (a trombone precursor) were also used.

### Notation and Theory
The period saw the evolution of music notation from memory aids to a comprehensive system. Early **neumes** (signs above text) indicated melodic contour but not exact pitch. The development of **heighted neumes** and then a **staff** (credited to Guido d'Arezzo, c. 1000–1050) allowed for precise pitch notation. Rhythmic notation advanced from the 13th-century **rhythmic modes** (codified by Johannes de Garlandia) to **Franco of Cologne's** system of mensural notation (c. 1280), where note shapes indicated duration. The **Ars nova** of Philippe de Vitry (c. 1320) introduced **mensuration signs** (precursors to time signatures) and allowed for duple subdivisions of the beat. The theoretical system of **church modes** (eight scales like Dorian and Phrygian) governed melodic construction.

### Early Medieval Music (500–1000)
The era began with the standardization of chant. Charlemagne promoted **Gregorian chant** across his empire by combining Roman and Gallican traditions. Early experiments in polyphony, **organum**, began around the 9th century. The period also saw the origin of **liturgical drama**, sung plays derived from tropes (poetic additions to chant), such as the *Quem Quaeritis*.

### High Medieval Music (1000–1300)
This era was marked by the **Ars antiqua** and the **Notre-Dame school** of polyphony in Paris, which developed complex organum, motets, and used rhythmic modes. Secular traditions flourished, including the **Goliards** (itinerant Latin poet-musicians), **troubadours** and **trouvères** (vernacular lyric poets), and the German **Minnesänger**. The *Cantigas de Santa Maria* (c. 1270–1280) is a major collection of monophonic songs from this period.

### Late Medieval Music (1300–1400)
The **Ars nova** style, centered in France, introduced sophisticated rhythmic notation and secular polyphony. **Guillaume de Machaut** perfected the **isorhythmic motet** and composed the first complete polyphonic mass. In Italy, the **Trecento** style featured lyrical madrigals and caccie. The era concluded with the highly complex and manneristic **Ars subtilior**. The transition to the Renaissance began in the early 15th century, influenced by English **faburden** technique and a growing emphasis on thirds as consonances.

## Terms
- **Monophonic**: Music consisting of a single melodic line without accompaniment.
- **Polyphonic**: Music with two or more independent melodic lines performed simultaneously.
- **Organum**: An early form of polyphony that adds voices to a pre-existing chant melody.
- **Neume**: A basic element of early musical notation, a sign placed above text to indicate melodic contour.
- **Staff**: A set of horizontal lines and spaces on which musical notes are placed to indicate precise pitch.
- **Rhythmic Modes**: A system of six rhythmic patterns used in 13th-century polyphony, based on groupings of long and short notes.
- **Mensural Notation**: A system where the shape of a note determines its rhythmic value.
- **Church Modes**: The eight-scale system (e.g., Dorian, Phrygian) that governed melody in medieval music.
- **Ars nova**: "New art"; a 14th-century musical style characterized by rhythmic innovation and new notation.
- **Ars subtilior**: "Subtler art"; a late 14th-century style marked by extreme rhythmic and notational complexity.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact origin and development of neumes are debated, though they likely derive from Greek and Roman grammatical signs.
- The precise rhythmic performance of music from the early medieval period, before the development of rhythmic modes, is uncertain and subject to scholarly debate.
- There is controversy over whether sections of imperfect tempus in Ars nova music were performed with a breve of equal length or a changed proportion.
- The attribution of the *Ars Nova* treatise to Philippe de Vitry is questioned by some scholars, who consider it anonymous.
- The role of instrumental accompaniment in liturgical dramas is unclear, as stage directions do not specify instruments.
