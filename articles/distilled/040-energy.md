# Energy

## Overview

Energy is the quantitative property transferred to a body or physical system, recognizable in the capacity to do work and in the form of heat and light. It is a conserved quantity: energy can be converted in form, but not created or destroyed. The SI unit of energy is the joule (J). Energy underpins all physical, chemical, biological, and cosmological processes, from cellular metabolism to stellar fusion, and its conservation is a fundamental law of physics.

## Timeline

- **4th century BC** — Aristotle's work possibly first uses the word *energeia*, as a qualitative philosophical concept.
- **Late 17th century** — Gottfried Leibniz proposes *vis viva* (mass × velocity²), believing total *vis viva* conserved.
- **Early 18th century** — Émilie du Châtelet proposes conservation of energy in marginalia of her translation of Newton's *Principia*.
- **1807** — Thomas Young possibly first to use "energy" in its modern sense.
- **1829** — Gustave-Gaspard Coriolis describes "kinetic energy" in its modern sense.
- **1845** — James Prescott Joule discovers the link between mechanical work and heat generation.
- **1853** — William Rankine coins the term "potential energy."
- **1905** — Albert Einstein's special relativity shows rest mass corresponds to equivalent rest energy (E₀ = m₀c²).
- **1913** — Niels Bohr proposes quantized energy levels in the Bohr theory of the atom.
- **1918** — Noether's theorem shows conservation of energy is a consequence of time-translation symmetry.
- **1926** — Erwin Schrödinger and Werner Heisenberg advance quantum mechanical explanation of energy levels.

## Body

### Forms of Energy

Total energy can be subdivided into potential energy (based on an object's position within a field or what is stored within the field) and kinetic energy (determined by the movement of an object or its components). These categories are not mutually exclusive. Combinations are often named separately: mechanical energy is the sum of translational and rotational kinetic and potential energy; nuclear energy refers to combined potentials within an atomic nucleus from the nuclear force or weak force. Other forms include elastic, chemical, radiant, internal, and rest energy.

### History of the Concept

The word "energy" derives from Ancient Greek *energeia* ('activity, operation'), possibly first appearing in Aristotle's work in the 4th century BC as a qualitative concept broad enough to include happiness and pleasure. Leibniz's *vis viva* (mass × velocity²) differed from modern kinetic energy only by a factor of two; he theorized thermal energy consisted of motions of constituent parts of matter, though this took over a century to be accepted. Du Châtelet's early 18th-century translation marginalia first formulated a conserved measurable quantity distinct from momentum. The law of conservation of energy was postulated in the early 19th century; it was debated whether heat was a substance (caloric) or a quantity. Joule's 1845 discovery linked mechanical work and heat, leading to thermodynamics, formalized largely by William Thomson (Lord Kelvin). Clausius, Gibbs, and Nernst advanced chemical explanations; Clausius formulated entropy; Stefan introduced laws of radiant energy. Einstein's 1905 special relativity showed rest mass converts to and from other energy forms; conservation of energy and conservation of mass became one equivalent law. Quantization evidence began with spectral lines observed by Fraunhofer and Wollaston in the early 1800s; Bohr's 1913 theory proposed quantized levels; Schrödinger and Heisenberg's 1926 quantum mechanics explained them.

### Units of Measure

In dimensional analysis, energy's base units are M L² T⁻². The SI derived unit is the joule: energy expended applying one newton through one meter. Power (energy per time) is the watt (one joule per second); a kilowatt-hour equals 3.6 million joules. Other units include the CGS erg, imperial foot-pound, electronvolt, food calorie, thermodynamic kilocalorie, and BTU.

### Scientific Use

**Classical mechanics:** Energy is a conserved quantity; work equals force times distance (line integral of force along a path). Work and energy are frame-dependent. The total energy is called the Hamiltonian; the Lagrangian (kinetic minus potential energy) is often more convenient for non-conservative systems. Noether's theorem (1918) states any differentiable symmetry of a system's action has a corresponding conservation law, but it does not apply to systems that cannot be modeled with a Lagrangian (e.g., dissipative systems).

**Chemistry:** Energy is an attribute of a substance's atomic, molecular, or aggregate structure. Reactions are exothermic/exergonic if the final state is lower in energy, endothermic if higher. Reactions usually require surmounting an activation energy barrier; the rate's exponential temperature dependence is the Arrhenius equation.

**Biology:** All living creatures rely on external energy—radiant energy from the Sun for green plants, chemical energy for animals. Photosynthesis converts carbon dioxide and water into carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and oxygen. Cellular respiration releases stored energy. The basal metabolism rate measures food energy expenditure at rest. The human equivalent (H-e) uses an average human expenditure of 6,900 kJ/day and 80-watt basal metabolic rate as a standard. Living organisms are remarkably inefficient physically; the second law of thermodynamics explains why concentrated energy requires spreading greater energy as heat, underlying the ecological pyramid of biomass. In cells, mitochondria generate chemical energy; ATP is the primary energy transporter, continually broken down and synthesized.

**Earth sciences:** Continental drift, mountains, volcanoes, and earthquakes reflect energy transformations in Earth's interior; meteorological phenomena result from solar energy transformations in the atmosphere. Sunlight drives Earth's energy budget. Radioactive decay (primarily Uranium-235, Potassium-40, Thorium-232) supplies more than half of Earth's internal heat budget, driving plate tectonics. Landslides and earthquakes release stored gravitational or elastic potential energy, ultimately from heavy atoms created in supernovae.

**Cosmology:** Stars, novas, supernovae, quasars, and gamma-ray bursts are the universe's highest-output energy transformations, driven by gravitational collapse or nuclear fusion. Hydrogen left unfused after the Big Bang represents stored potential energy released by fusion in stars. The Penrose process is a theoretical method to extract energy from a rotating black hole; Hawking radiation causes steady mass loss.

**Quantum mechanics:** Energy is defined via the Hamiltonian operator as a time derivative of the wave function. The Schrödinger equation yields discrete energy levels for bound systems (quanta). For electromagnetic waves, E = hν (Planck relation), where h is the Planck constant and ν the frequency; these states are photons.

**Relativity:** Einstein discovered rest energy (E₀ = m₀c²)—energy every massive body possesses at rest. Electron–positron annihilation converts rest mass to photon radiant energy; total mass and energy remain constant. The stress–energy tensor sources the gravitational field in general relativity. Energy is a scalar, the canonical conjugate to time; invariant under spatial rotations but not boosts.

### Transformation

Energy transforms between forms at various efficiencies via transducers (battery, dam, heat engine). Carnot's theorem and the second law limit heat-to-work conversion in cyclic processes. Transformations are directed by entropy considerations; larger transformations to more concentrated forms are statistically improbable. The universe's evolution involves potential energy from the Big Bang being "released" by triggering mechanisms (nucleosynthesis, nuclear decay, chemical explosions). A pendulum exemplifies reversible conversion between potential and kinetic energy. In a gravitational field, mass and energy both give weight; E = mc² quantifies mass–energy equivalence. Part of rest energy may convert to other forms, but neither energy nor mass is destroyed; 1 kg of rest mass equals 9×10¹⁶ joules (21.5 megatonnes of TNT). Thermodynamics divides transformations into reversible and irreversible processes; irreversible dissipation leads to the hypothesized heat death of the universe, where available energy for work continually decreases.

### Conservation of Energy

The first law of thermodynamics states a closed system's energy is constant unless transferred as work or heat. Most energy forms (gravitational energy excepted) obey strict local conservation. Noether's theorem shows conservation of energy is the mathematical consequence of time-translation symmetry. The energy–time uncertainty principle (ΔEΔt ≥ ℏ/2) limits how precisely energy can be defined over short intervals; it permits virtual particles, which carry momentum and mediate fundamental forces. Internal energy is the sum of all microscopic energy forms; thermodynamics concerns changes in it, not absolute values. The equipartition principle states total energy splits equally among all degrees of freedom on average; entropy measures the evenness of energy distribution. The second law holds simply only near equilibrium; for non-equilibrium systems, the principle of maximum entropy production is a guiding idea.

## Terms

- **Joule (J):** SI derived unit of energy; work done applying one newton through one meter.
- **Kinetic energy:** Energy of motion, determined by an object's movement.
- **Potential energy:** Stored energy based on position within a field or stored within the field itself.
- **Vis viva:** Leibniz's "living force," mass × velocity²; differs from kinetic energy by a factor of two.
- **Entropy:** Measure of the evenness of energy distribution among parts of a system.
- **Hamiltonian:** The total energy of a system; an operator in quantum mechanics.
- **Lagrangian:** Kinetic energy minus potential energy; a formalism for deriving equations of motion.
- **Activation energy:** Energy barrier that reactants must surmount for a chemical reaction to occur.
- **ATP (adenosine triphosphate):** The primary energy transporter in living cells.
- **Rest energy:** Energy every massive body possesses at rest, given by E₀ = m₀c².

## Debates and open questions

- Whether heat was a physical substance (caloric) or a physical quantity was argued for years until Joule's 1845 work.
- During wave function collapse, conservation of energy does not hold locally, though statistically it holds on average; it does apply in Everett's many-worlds interpretation.
- For non-equilibrium systems, the governing laws are still debatable; the principle of maximum entropy production is one guiding principle.
- The hypothesis of the thermodynamic heat death of the universe remains a theoretical projection of irreversible energy dissipation.
