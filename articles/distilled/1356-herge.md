# Hergé

## Overview  
Georges Remi (22 May 1907 – 3 March 1983), known by the pen name Hergé, was a Belgian comic strip artist best known for creating *The Adventures of Tintin*, one of the most popular European comics of the 20th century. His distinctive *ligne claire* (clear line) drawing style and meticulously researched narratives influenced generations of cartoonists. Beyond Tintin, he created *Quick & Flupke* and *The Adventures of Jo, Zette and Jocko*. His career spanned political controversy, artistic innovation, and global acclaim.

## Timeline  
- **1907**: Born in Etterbeek, Brussels  
- **1926**: Began *The Adventures of Totor* for *Le Boy-Scout Belge*  
- **1929**: Created *The Adventures of Tintin* for *Le Vingtième Siècle*  
- **1930**: Launched *Quick & Flupke*  
- **1934**: *The Blue Lotus* marked a shift toward realism and research  
- **1940**: Continued Tintin in *Le Soir* under Nazi occupation  
- **1944**: Arrested on 3 September after being named in the "Gallery of Traitors"; blacklisted by SHAEF proclamation of 8 September  
- **1946**: Co-founded *Tintin* magazine with Raymond Leblanc  
- **1950**: Established Studios Hergé  
- **1983**: Died in Brussels on 3 March at the age of 75  

## Body  

### Early Life and Career Beginnings  
Hergé was born into a lower-middle-class Catholic family in Etterbeek, Brussels. His Walloon father, Alexis Remi, worked in a confectionery factory; his Flemish mother, Elisabeth Dufour, was a housewife. Growing up in bilingual Brussels, he spoke French as his primary language and learned Dutch, developing a Marollien accent from his maternal grandmother. Influenced by cinema and literature, he joined the Boy Scouts, where his artistic talent was encouraged. His first published work appeared in *Le Boy-Scout Belge*, leading to the creation of *Totor* (1926), an early precursor to Tintin.

### The Birth of Tintin and Political Context  
Working for the conservative Catholic newspaper *Le Vingtième Siècle*, Hergé created Tintin in 1929 under the guidance of editor Abbé Norbert Wallez. Early stories—*Tintin in the Land of the Soviets*, *Tintin in the Congo*, and *Tintin in America*—were designed as conservative propaganda for children. These works were serialized in *Le Petit Vingtième*, a children’s supplement that carried strong Catholic and fascist messages, many of its passages being explicitly antisemitic.

### Artistic Evolution and Global Influence  
From 1934, influenced by his Chinese friend Zhang Chongren, Hergé emphasized historical accuracy and cultural sensitivity, notably in *The Blue Lotus*. This marked a turning point, earning critical acclaim. He expanded his output with *Jo, Zette and Jocko* and *Quick & Flupke*, while collaborating with Casterman for book publications. His work gained international syndication, establishing him as a major figure in European comics.

### Wartime Controversy and Collaboration  
During the German occupation (1940–1944), Hergé worked for *Le Soir*, a Nazi-controlled newspaper. His Tintin stories during this period, such as *The Shooting Star*, espoused an antisemitic and anti-American attitude in keeping with *Le Soir*'s editorial standpoint. His association with the regime led to post-war accusations of collaboration. He was briefly arrested but never charged. The experience deeply affected him, contributing to lifelong trauma and guilt.

### Post-War Revival and Institutional Legacy  
After the war, Hergé co-founded *Tintin* magazine in 1946 with Raymond Leblanc, reviving his career. He established Studios Hergé in 1950, hiring assistants like Bob de Moor and Jacques Martin. Despite personal struggles—including marital breakdown and health issues—he continued producing acclaimed stories like *Destination Moon* and *Explorers on the Moon*. In later years, he explored abstract painting but returned to comics until his death in 1983.

## Terms  
- **Ligne claire**: Hergé’s clean, precise drawing style characterized by bold lines and flat colors without shading  
- **Le Vingtième Siècle**: Conservative Catholic newspaper where Hergé began his career  
- **Le Petit Vingtième**: Children’s supplement of *Le Vingtième Siècle* featuring early Tintin stories  
- **Zhang Chongren**: Chinese artist and friend who influenced Hergé’s approach to cultural authenticity  
- **Studios Hergé**: Professional studio founded in 1950 to support Hergé’s comic production  
- **Tintin magazine**: Weekly comic magazine launched in 1946, showcasing Hergé and other artists  
- **Rastapopoulos**: Recurring villain in Tintin stories, often misinterpreted as antisemitic stereotype  
- **Captain Haddock**: Major Tintin character introduced in *The Crab with the Golden Claws*  
- **Kuifje**: Dutch name for Tintin, used in Flemish editions of the magazine  

## Debates and Open Questions  
Hergé’s wartime activities remain contentious. While some argue he was apolitical and merely trying to survive, others view his work for *Le Soir* as complicity with Nazi propaganda. His early portrayals of non-European cultures have also sparked debate—some see them as products of their time, others as perpetuating harmful stereotypes. Additionally, questions persist about his alleged antisemitism, particularly regarding *The Shooting Star* and the character of Rastapopoulos. Despite these controversies, his artistic legacy endures, with ongoing reassessments of his work in light of modern values.
