# Volga Bulgaria

## Overview
Volga Bulgaria was a medieval Bulgar state (9th–13th centuries) at the confluence of the Volga and Kama rivers in modern European Russia. It was a multi-ethnic, trade-focused polity that adopted Islam in 922 and played a key role in Eurasian commerce before its destruction by the Mongols. Its legacy is debated, particularly regarding its relationship to the later Kazan Khanate and the modern Chuvash people.

## Timeline
- **c. 630** — Founding of Old Great Bulgaria, later destroyed by Khazars in 668.
- **8th century** — Bulgar tribes reach the Volga region.
- **Late 9th century** — Unification process begins; capital established at Bolghar.
- **922** — Volga Bulgaria adopts Islam as state religion.
- **Late 10th century** — Gains full independence after the destruction of the Khazar Khaganate.
- **11th–12th centuries** — Faces increasing raids and pressure from Rus' principalities.
- **1220** — Grand Duke Yuri II of Vladimir captures Ustiug and besieges Aşlı.
- **1223** — Mongol advance guard defeated at the Battle of Samara Bend.
- **1236–1240** — Mongols conquer and subjugate Volga Bulgaria.
- **1430s** — Kazan Khanate established as a successor state.
- **1552** — Ivan the Terrible captures Kazan, ending the Kazan Khanate.

## Body

### Origins and State Formation
The Bulgars were an Oghuric Turkic people whose early origins are debated. They migrated from the Pontic-Caspian steppe, coming under Khazar overlordship. After the destruction of Old Great Bulgaria in 668, a group under Kotrag moved to the Volga region. They became dominant by the late 9th century, assimilating local Finno-Ugric and other tribes. Initially under Khazar dominance, Volga Bulgaria gradually gained independence, which was secured after the Khazars' defeat by Sviatoslav in the late 10th century.

### Trade and Islam
Volga Bulgaria's strategic position on the Volga River made it a vital trade hub between Europe and the Muslim world. Its main exports were furs and slaves, traded for silver dirhams and silk. In 922, ruler Almış formally adopted Islam, sending an embassy to the Abbasid Caliphate. This made Volga Bulgaria a Muslim state decades before the Christianization of Kievan Rus'. Its capital, Bolghar, and other cities like Bilär and Suar were major commercial centers.

### Conflict with Rus' and Mongol Conquest
The Rus' principalities to the west were the primary military threat. From the 11th century, Volga Bulgaria suffered repeated raids, and by the early 13th century, rulers like Andrew the Pious and Vsevolod III of Vladimir systematically pillaged its cities. In 1220, Grand Duke Yuri II captured key Bulgar towns. The Mongol invasions delivered the final blow: after a defeat in 1223, they returned in 1236 and fully conquered the weakened state by 1240, incorporating it into the Golden Horde.

### Legacy and Successor States
After the Mongol conquest, Volga Bulgaria was divided into vassal principalities. By the 1430s, the Kazan Khanate emerged as the most important successor state. Following the Russian capture of Kazan in 1552, the Bulgar lands were separately subjugated. The modern Chuvash people are considered by many scholars to be the descendants of the Volga Bulgars, as their language is the only surviving member of the Oghuric Turkic branch.

## Terms
- **Oghuric**: A branch of the Turkic language family, of which the Bulgar language was a member.
- **Khazar Khaganate**: A powerful Turkic state that dominated the Pontic-Caspian steppe and over which the early Bulgars were vassals.
- **Saqaliba**: An Arabic term used by sources like Ibn Fadlan, often translated as "Slavs," but possibly referring to the Bulgars' role in the slave trade.
- **Golden Horde**: The Mongol and later Turkicized khanate that ruled over Russia and the former Volga Bulgaria after the 13th century.
- **Daruga**: An administrative division within the Kazan Khanate, controlling dependent lands.
- **Chuvash**: A Turkic people of the Volga region, considered by many scholars to be the direct descendants of the Volga Bulgars.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise origins of the Bulgars and their relationship to the Huns remains a topic of scholarly debate.
- The linguistic and ethnic relationship between the Volga Bulgars and the modern Chuvash is a key question, with evidence supporting a direct link.
- The exact nature of the transition from Volga Bulgaria to the Kazan Khanate, and the degree of cultural continuity, is discussed by historians.
