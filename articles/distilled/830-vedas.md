# Vedas

## Overview
The Vedas are the oldest layer of Sanskrit literature and the foundational scriptures of Hinduism, composed in Vedic Sanskrit between c. 1500 and 500 BCE. They are classified as *śruti* ("what is heard"), considered *apauruṣeya* (authorless, superhuman revelations perceived by ancient sages), and were preserved for millennia through an elaborate oral tradition prioritizing exact phonetic recitation over semantic meaning. The corpus comprises four canonical collections—Rigveda, Yajurveda, Samaveda, and Atharvaveda—each subdivided into four text types: Samhitas (mantras), Brahmanas (ritual commentaries), Aranyakas (forest treatises), and Upanishads (philosophical speculations). The Upanishads, composed latest (c. 800 BCE onward), introduce the concepts of Brahman (Ultimate Reality) and Ātman (the self), forming the basis of Vedanta and later Hindu philosophy. Indian philosophical schools accepting Vedic authority are termed *āstika* (orthodox); those rejecting it, such as Buddhism and Jainism, are *nāstika* (heterodox).

## Timeline
- **c. 1500–1200 BCE** — Composition of the Rigveda Samhita (books 2–9) in the Punjab region.
- **c. 1200–900 BCE** — Composition of Rigveda Book 10 and the Yajurveda, Samaveda, and Atharvaveda Samhitas; rise of the Kuru kingdom.
- **c. 1000–500 BCE** — Composition of "circum-Vedic" texts (Brahmanas, Aranyakas, early Upanishads); redaction of Samhitas.
- **c. 900–700 BCE** — Flourishing of Brahmanas (oldest c. 900 BCE; Shatapatha Brahmana complete by c. 700 BCE).
- **c. 800 BCE – end of Vedic period** — Composition of the Principal Upanishads (Bṛhadāraṇyaka, Chāndogya, etc.).
- **c. 600 BCE** — Final codification of Brahmanas (pre-Buddhist era); end of Vedic period marked by rise of Mahajanapadas, Buddha, and Pāṇini.
- **c. 500 BCE** — Vedas written down for the first time; oral transmission remains authoritative.
- **4th c. BCE** — Yāska writes *Nirukta* (etymology); Pāṇini composes *Aṣṭādhyāyī* (grammar).
- **1st c. BCE** — Earliest traceable literary tradition (Kanva recension of Yajurveda).
- **14th c. CE** — Sāyaṇa writes *Vedārtha Prakāśa*, a major commentary on the Vedas.
- **2007** — Rigveda manuscripts inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World Register.

## Body

### Etymology and Definitions
The Sanskrit *véda* derives from the root *vid-* ("to know"), cognate with Greek *eidos*, Latin *videō*, and English *wit*. As a common noun, it means "knowledge" or "wisdom"; in specific Rigvedic contexts, it can denote "obtaining wealth" or "a bunch of grass" for ritual fire. The texts are defined as *śruti* ("what is heard"), distinguishing them from *smṛti* ("what is remembered") texts like the Mahabharata and Puranas. Orthodox Hindu theology holds the Vedas to be *apauruṣeya*—impersonal, authorless revelations heard by sages (*ṛṣis*) in deep meditation, though the hymns themselves credit the sages with skillful composition.

### Structure of the Vedic Corpus
Each of the four Vedas contains four chronological layers:
1.  **Samhitas**: Collections of metric mantras (c. 1500–900 BCE). The Rigveda Samhita (1,028 hymns) is the oldest; the Yajurveda provides prose sacrificial formulas; the Samaveda adapts Rigvedic verses for melodic chanting; the Atharvaveda contains spells, healing charms, and speculative hymns.
2.  **Brahmanas**: Prose commentaries explaining ritual procedures and meanings (c. 900–700 BCE). Each Vedic school (*śākhā*) maintained its own Brahmana; 19 survive.
3.  **Aranyakas**: "Forest treatises" composed by recluses, bridging ritual action and symbolic interpretation.
4.  **Upanishads**: Philosophical dialogues (c. 800 BCE onward) exploring *Brahman* (Absolute) and *Ātman* (Self). They are the foundation of Vedanta and the most widely known Vedic texts today.
Later tradition classifies the Samhitas and Brahmanas as *karma-kāṇḍa* (ritual action section) and the Upanishads as *jñāna-kāṇḍa* (knowledge section).

### The Four Vedas
*   **Rigveda (RV)**: Oldest text (c. 1500–1200 BCE), 1,028 hymns in 10 books (*maṇḍalas*). Composed in Punjab by various priestly poets. Structured by deity (Agni, Indra, Soma), hymn length, and meter. Shifts from deity praise to metaphysical speculation (e.g., Nāsadīya Sūkta on cosmic origin). Only the Śākalya recension survives.
*   **Yajurveda (YV)**: Prose mantras for ritual action (c. 1200–800 BCE). Exists in two major branches: *Kṛṣṇa* (Black, interspersed Samhita/Brahmana; four schools survive) and *Śukla* (White, separated Samhita and Shatapatha Brahmana; two schools survive). Youngest layer contains major Upanishads.
*   **Samaveda (SV)**: 1,549 stanzas, almost entirely drawn from the Rigveda, set to melody for the *udgātṛ* (singer) priests (c. 1200–1000 BCE). Organized into melody collections (*gāna*) and verse books (*ārcika*). Two major recensions survive (Kauthuma/Ranayaniya, Jaiminiya).
*   **Atharvaveda (AV)**: Hymns of the Atharvan and Angirasa poets (c. 900 BCE compilation; some material older). Contains 760 hymns; 160 shared with Rigveda. Two recensions survive (Paippalāda, Śaunakīya). Once excluded from the "triple Veda" (*trayī vidyā*), it was accepted as a fourth Veda late in the 1st millennium BCE. Documents folk healing, royal rites, and philosophical theosophy.

### Oral Transmission and Textual Fidelity
The Vedas were transmitted orally for over two millennia via *sampradāya* (teacher-student lineage). Fidelity was ensured through extreme mnemonic rigor: memorization in up to eleven *pathas* (recitation modes), including *jaṭā-pāṭha* (mesh recitation reversing word order), synchronized physical gestures (*mudrās*), and group chanting. This "memory culture" preserved the Rigveda without variant readings within the surviving school. Writing was introduced post-500 BCE (earliest manuscripts 11th century CE), but only oral recitation is considered authoritative due to the necessity of exact phonetics (*śabda*). The sounds themselves are held to be "primordial rhythms of creation"; recitation regenerates the cosmos regardless of the reciter's semantic understanding.

### Vedic Learning and Interpretation
The Vedas, Vedic rituals, and their ancillary sciences called the Vedangas were part of the curriculum at ancient universities such as Taxila, Nalanda, and Vikramashila. The *Vedāṅgas* (auxiliary sciences: phonetics, meter, grammar, etymology, ritual, astronomy) arose c. mid-1st millennium BCE to interpret the archaic language. Two hermeneutic tensions persist:
1.  **Phonology vs. Meaning**: Ritual transmission prioritized sound (*śabda*) over sense (*artha*); by the late Vedic period, mantra meanings were obscure, prompting etymological works (*Niruktas*).
2.  **Ritual Mastery vs. Mystical Insight**: Mīmāṃsā scholars (e.g., Sāyaṇa, 14th c. CE) viewed the Veda as a ritual repertoire to be mastered for sacrifice (*yajña*). Conversely, the Upanishads and later Vedānta treat the Veda as a vehicle for realizing the identity of *Ātman* and *Brahman* through meditation and renunciation.

### Vedic Schools (*Śākhās*) and Recensions
Each Veda was transmitted through numerous regional schools (*śākhās*), each with its own canon and recensions. In the 2nd millennium BCE, there was no single "Vedic Scripture," only school-specific canons. Most texts are lost; the surviving Rigveda represents only the Śākalya school of Videha (North Bihar). Schools varied theologically: polytheistic, henotheistic, monotheistic, agnostic, and monistic (positing an absolute reality beyond gods).

### Post-Vedic Literature
*   **Vedāṅgas**: Six auxiliary sciences (phonetics, meter, grammar, etymology, ritual, astronomy) that influenced later Hindu philosophy and law (*Dharmaśāstras* derive from *Kalpa* Vedāṅga).
*   **Pariśiṣṭas**: Supplements detailing ritual logistics; extensive for the Atharvaveda (79 works).
*   **Upavedas**: Applied knowledge: Archery (Dhanurveda), Architecture (Sthāpatyaveda), Music/Dance (Gāndharvaveda), Medicine (Āyurveda).
*   **"Fifth Veda" Claims**: The *Chāndogya Upaniṣad* designates drama/dance (*Nāṭya*) as the fifth Veda; later texts (Mahabharata, Puranas, Tamil *Divya Prabandha*) claim the title for themselves.
*   **Puranas**: Encyclopedic mythological literature (18 *Mahāpurāṇas*, 400,000+ verses), considered *Vaidika* (congruent with Vedas) and influential in the Bhakti movement.

### Authority and Reception
*   **Orthodox (*Āstika*)**: Six schools of Hindu philosophy (Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta) accept Vedic authority.
*   **Heterodox (*Nāstika*)**: Cārvāka, Ājīvika, Buddhism, Jainism reject Vedic authority.
*   **Internal Dissent**: Some Tantric traditions (Vaishnava Sahajiyā, Shaiva Āgamas) explicitly reject the Vedas as impure or limited.
*   **Modern Reformers**: Arya Samaj and Brahmo Samaj uphold Vedic authority; figures like Tagore, Sen, and Ambedkar rejected it.
*   **Western Indology**: Study began 17th century; Schopenhauer popularized Upanishads (early 19th c.); Müller's *Sacred Books of the East* (1879–1910) and Griffith's translations (1889–1899) established Western textual access.

## Terms
- ****Śruti**** — "What is heard"; the highest category of Hindu scripture (Vedas and embedded texts), considered authorless revelation.
- ****Smṛti**** — "What is remembered"; scriptures of human authorship (epics, Puranas, law codes), subordinate to Śruti.
- ****Apauruṣeya**** — "Not of a man"; the doctrine that the Vedas are superhuman, impersonal, and authorless.
- ****Saṃhitā**** — "Collection"; the oldest layer of each Veda, consisting of metric mantras.
- ****Brāhmaṇa**** — Prose commentaries on Vedic rituals, explaining procedures and theological significance.
- ****Āraṇyaka**** — "Forest treatise"; texts composed by recluses, transitioning from ritual action to symbolic philosophy.
- ****Upaniṣad**** — Philosophical dialogues (c. 800 BCE+) exploring *Brahman* and *Ātman*; foundation of Vedanta.
- ****Śākhā**** — "Branch"; a specific Vedic school preserving its own recension of texts and ritual tradition.
- ****Karma-kāṇḍa / Jñāna-kāṇḍa**** — Ritual action section (Samhitas, Brahmanas) vs. Knowledge section (Upanishads); classification by Adi Shankara.
- ****Vedāṅga**** — "Limbs of the Veda"; six auxiliary sciences (phonetics, meter, grammar, etymology, ritual, astronomy) for textual interpretation.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Absolute Chronology**: While relative sequence is clear, absolute dates rely on linguistic strata and the Mitanni treaty (14th c. BCE) as a *terminus ante quem* for Rigvedic Indo-Aryan; estimates for the Rigveda range from c. 1700–1100 BCE.
*   **Oral vs. Written Composition**: Scholars debate whether the texts' structural complexity implies a literate culture (Goody) or demonstrates the capacity of advanced oral "memory culture" (Staal, Falk).
*   **Śruti/Smṛti Boundary**: The classification is linguistically messy (Upanishads blend with Aranyakas; Brahmanas contain older Samhita language), but retained for tradition and editorial convention.
*   **Semantic Priority**: Ritualists (Mīmāṃsā) argue mantra efficacy depends solely on phonetic purity; philosophers (Vedānta) and some Rigvedic passages insist meaning (*artha*) is essential for true knowledge.
*   **Atharvaveda's Status**: Its late canonical acceptance (late 1st millennium BCE) and "magical" content raise questions about the boundaries of orthodoxy and the integration of folk traditions.
*   **Lost Recensions**: The vast majority of *śākhās* and their texts are lost; the surviving corpus represents a tiny, geographically specific fraction of the original Vedic diversity.
