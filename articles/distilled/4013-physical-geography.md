# Physical geography

## Overview
Physical geography is the branch of natural science that studies the processes and patterns of the natural environment, including the atmosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere, and geosphere. It is one of the three main branches of geography, alongside human geography and technical geography, and is fundamental to understanding Earth's natural systems and their changes over time.

## Timeline
- **Ancient Greek period** — Geography emerges as a science, focused on location and description of places.
- **16th century** — Martín Fernández de Enciso's *Summa de Geografía* (early 1500s) first describes the New World.
- **18th century** — Controversy between uniformitarianism (Hutton) and catastrophism (Cuvier) influences geography.
- **Mid-18th century** — Mikhail Lomonosov conducts Siberian surveys, founds glaciology, and promotes geography education in Russia.
- **19th century** — European colonial expansion leads to institutionalization of geography in universities and societies.
- **Mid-19th century** — Charles Darwin's theory of evolution provides an impetus for biogeography.
- **Late 19th century** — Wladimir Köppen develops climate classification and contributes to paleoclimatology.
- **Late 19th/early 20th century** — William Morris Davis proposes the cycle of erosion theory, revolutionizing geomorphology.
- **20th century** — Vasily Dokuchaev establishes pedology (soil science) as a distinct geographic study.

## Body

### Core Definition and Scope
Physical geography, also known as physiography, is the natural science branch of geography. It focuses on the natural environment's processes and patterns across four main spheres: the atmosphere (air), hydrosphere (water), biosphere (life), and geosphere (land). This contrasts with human geography, which studies the built environment, and technical geography, which focuses on spatial information tools. The three branches overlap significantly.

### Major Sub-branches
Physical geography is divided into several specialized fields. **Geomorphology** studies Earth's surface and the processes (like tectonic and climatic forces) that shape landforms. **Hydrology** examines the movement and quality of water on and near the land surface, typified by the hydrological cycle. **Glaciology** focuses on ice sheets, glaciers, and the cryosphere. **Biogeography** analyzes geographic patterns of species distribution, influenced by evolution and plate tectonics. **Climatology** studies long-term weather patterns and their natural and human influences. **Soil geography** and **pedology** deal with soil distribution and formation. **Palaeogeography** reconstructs past continental positions using geological evidence. **Coastal geography** and **oceanography** study the dynamic interfaces and processes of oceans and coasts. **Quaternary science** examines the last 2.6 million years. **Landscape ecology** and **environmental geography** address spatial variations in ecological processes and human-environment interactions, respectively. **Geomatics** involves the gathering and analysis of spatial information.

### Historical Development
Until the late 19th century, geography was almost exclusively a natural science. Its development was significantly influenced by two 19th-century events: European colonial expansion, which institutionalized geography in academia, and Darwin's theory of evolution, which advanced biogeography. The Russian school made key contributions, with Lomonosov founding glaciology and Dokuchaev establishing pedology. A major revolution came from American geographer William Morris Davis, whose "cycle of erosion" theory provided a paradigm for understanding landscape evolution and helped establish geomorphology as a distinct subfield. His work spurred research across multiple branches of physical geography.

## Terms
- **Physiography**: Another name for physical geography.
- **Hydrosphere**: The total amount of water on a planet, including surface, underground, and atmospheric water.
- **Geosphere**: The solid parts of the Earth, including the crust and mantle.
- **Cryosphere**: The portions of Earth's surface where water is in solid form, including ice sheets, glaciers, and sea ice.
- **Pedology**: The study of soils in their natural environment, focusing on their formation, morphology, and classification.
- **Cycle of erosion**: A theory by William Morris Davis describing a cyclical process of landscape evolution from uplift to the formation of a low-relief plain (peneplain).
- **Peneplain**: A near-flat plain representing the final stage of the Davisian erosion cycle.
- **Proxy evidence**: Indirect evidence (e.g., fossils, ice cores) used to reconstruct past environmental conditions.
