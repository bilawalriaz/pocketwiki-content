# Joseph II, Holy Roman Emperor

## Overview
Joseph II (1741–1790) was Holy Roman Emperor and sole ruler of the Habsburg monarchy, remembered as a leading "enlightened despot." He attempted radical secular, legal, and administrative reforms to modernize his diverse empire, but his impatient and autocratic methods provoked widespread resistance, leading to the failure of many of his programs and a foreign policy that left Austria isolated.

## Timeline
- **13 March 1741** — Born in Vienna, son of Empress Maria Theresa and Emperor Francis I.
- **18 August 1765** — Becomes Holy Roman Emperor after his father's death; co-rules with his mother.
- **29 November 1780** — Becomes sole ruler of the Habsburg monarchy upon Maria Theresa's death.
- **1781** — Issues the Edict of Toleration for Protestants and Orthodox; abolishes serfdom.
- **1784** — Orders German to replace Latin as the language of administration.
- **1787–1791** — Enters the costly Austro-Turkish War.
- **1789** — Faces the Brabant Revolution in the Austrian Netherlands.
- **20 February 1790** — Dies in Vienna, succeeded by his brother Leopold II.

## Body

### Early Life and Co-Regency
Joseph II was the eldest son of Maria Theresa and Francis I, receiving a comprehensive education supervised by his mother. His first marriage to Isabella of Parma was happy but brief; she died in 1763. His second, politically motivated marriage to Maria Josepha of Bavaria was loveless and also ended with her death from smallpox in 1767. As co-regent from 1765, Joseph had limited power, mainly in military and court administration, but he advocated for Enlightenment reforms like religious toleration and reducing church influence. He gained experience in fiscal administration, pushing for centralized bookkeeping and reduced court expenditure. His foreign policy was ambitious but risky; he planned to seize Prussian territory if Frederick the Great died in 1775, and his attempt to acquire Bavaria led to the inconclusive "Potato War" (1778–1779), resolved by the Treaty of Teschen with only minor gains for Austria.

### Domestic Reforms as Sole Ruler
After Maria Theresa's death in 1780, Joseph II issued over 6,000 edicts to centralize and rationalize the empire. He made German the compulsory official language, abolished serfdom, and reformed the legal system, simplifying courts and making punishments more uniform. He effectively abolished the death penalty in practice, replacing it with harsh imprisonment. Civil reforms included making marriage a civil contract and granting equal inheritance rights. Education was made compulsory for all children, and he founded the Allgemeines Krankenhaus hospital in Vienna. His policies, known as Josephinism, aimed to create a uniform, efficient state under his personal autocracy.

### Religious and Cultural Policies
Joseph II pursued aggressive secularization, dissolving about one-third of monasteries that did not engage in education or healthcare. He issued the Edict of Toleration (1781) granting limited rights to Protestants and Orthodox, and in 1782, he allowed Jews to enter professions and attend schools. These reforms, aimed at subordinating the church to the state, provoked opposition from the Pope and conservative Catholics. A patron of the arts, he supported composers like Mozart and Salieri, and opened public parks like the Prater, fostering Vienna's cultural scene.

### Foreign Policy and Downfall
Joseph's foreign policy was expansionist and belligerent. He allied with Russia against the Ottoman Empire in 1781, leading to the costly Austro-Turkish War (1787–1791). His attempts to acquire Bavaria or exchange it for the Austrian Netherlands were blocked by Prussia, which formed the Fürstenbund league against him. His centralizing reforms sparked major revolts: the Brabant Revolution (1789) in the Austrian Netherlands and widespread resistance in Hungary, where nobles opposed his imposition of German and curtailment of their privileges. By his death in 1790 from tuberculosis, Joseph was forced to retract many reforms, and his reign was seen as a failure due to his impatience and disregard for local traditions.

## Terms
- **Enlightened Absolutism/Despotism**: A form of government where an absolute ruler implements reforms inspired by Enlightenment ideals of reason and efficiency, but without surrendering autocratic power.
- **Josephinism**: The set of secular, liberalizing, and centralizing policies enacted by Joseph II, characterized by state control over the church and society.
- **Edict of Toleration (1781)**: Joseph II's decree granting limited religious freedom and civil rights to Protestants, Orthodox Christians, and later Jews.
- **Serfdom**: A feudal system binding peasants to the land and its lord; abolished by Joseph II in 1781.
- **Cameralism**: A German school of public administration emphasizing state efficiency, economic development, and centralized fiscal management, which influenced Habsburg policy.
- **Fürstenbund**: A league of German princes formed in 1785 by Frederick the Great of Prussia to oppose Joseph II's designs on Bavaria.
- **Brabant Revolution (1789)**: A revolt in the Austrian Netherlands against Joseph II's centralizing reforms, leading to a brief independent state.
- **Allgemeines Gesetzbuch über Verbrechen und deren Bestrafung**: The General Code of Crimes and Punishments (1787), which reformed criminal law and effectively abolished the death penalty.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate whether Joseph II was a genuine reformer inspired by Enlightenment ideals or a power-hungry despot. Some, like Mitrofanov, argue his liberalism was a myth and he was more despotic than his mother. Others, like Padover, celebrate him as a great liberator. Historians like Blanning stress contradictions in his policies, such as encouraging small peasant holdings which retarded modernization. The "false Constantinople letters" have also distorted his historical image, exaggerating his radicalism. A key question remains whether his reforms failed primarily due to his impatience and autocratic methods, or because of deeper economic and social backwardness in the Habsburg Empire.
