# House of Plantagenet

## Overview
The House of Plantagenet ruled England from 1154 to 1485. Originating in Anjou, France, they transformed England into a more centralized state with defined royal duties and a sophisticated legal system. Their reign saw the loss of vast French territories, the development of Parliament, and culminated in the Wars of the Roses, ending with the Tudor dynasty.

## Timeline
- **1154** — Henry II, first Plantagenet, becomes king.
- **1215** — King John signs Magna Carta, limiting royal power.
- **1265** — Simon de Montfort assembles first Parliament with commoners.
- **1295** — Edward I summons "Model Parliament," setting representation precedent.
- **1337** — Hundred Years' War with France begins under Edward III.
- **1348** — Black Death kills about a third of England's population.
- **1399** — Henry IV deposes Richard II, founding House of Lancaster.
- **1415** — Henry V wins decisive victory at Battle of Agincourt.
- **1453** — Hundred Years' War ends; England loses all French lands except Calais.
- **1455** — Wars of the Roses begin with First Battle of St Albans.
- **1485** — Richard III killed at Bosworth Field, ending Plantagenet rule.

## Body

### Origins and the Angevin Kings
The Plantagenets descended from Anjou's counts. The dynasty began when Geoffrey of Anjou married Empress Matilda, heir to the English throne. Their son Henry II inherited England in 1154, creating a vast "Angevin Empire" from Scotland to the Pyrenees. This early period, dominated by Henry II, Richard I, and John, is the Angevin era. Henry II's reign featured conflict with the Church, exemplified by Thomas Becket's murder, and struggles with rebellious sons. John lost most continental Angevin lands and was forced to sign Magna Carta in 1215, limiting royal authority.

### Consolidation and Constitutional Change
Under Henry III and Edward I, the monarchy consolidated power in England while losing ground in France. Henry III's long reign saw baronial conflict and the Second Barons' War, advancing Parliament's development. Edward I conquered Wales, attempted to subdue Scotland, and reformed law with statutes like Mortmain. His reign established parliamentary consent for taxation. Edward II faced baronial opposition and was deposed and murdered.

### The Hundred Years' War and the Black Prince
Edward III's reign marked Plantagenet military power's height. He initiated the Hundred Years' War in 1337, claiming the French throne. His son Edward the Black Prince won victories at Crécy and Poitiers. War costs and the Black Death strained the kingdom. The Treaty of Brétigny (1360) temporarily secured vast French territories, later lost. Edward III's children founded cadet branches—Lancaster and York—that would later fight for the throne.

### The Wars of the Roses
Richard II's 1399 deposition by Henry IV (House of Lancaster) created disputed succession. The rival House of York, descended from Edward III's fourth son, challenged Lancaster. This conflict, the Wars of the Roses, erupted in 1455. It saw Henry VI's brief deposition and Edward IV's reign. After Edward's death, his brother Richard III seized the throne, leading to the disappearance of Edward's sons (the "Princes in the Tower"). Richard was defeated and killed by Henry Tudor at Bosworth Field in 1485, ending Plantagenet rule.

### Legacy and the Tudor Dynasty
Henry VII, a Lancastrian claimant, founded the Tudor dynasty. He married Elizabeth of York, uniting the warring houses. The Tudors centralized royal power, avoiding late Plantagenet baronial conflicts. This stability enabled the English Renaissance. Every subsequent English and British monarch descends from the Plantagenets. Their legacy includes the common law system, parliamentary foundations, and a distinct English national identity.

## Terms
- **Angevin**: Refers to the first three Plantagenet kings (Henry II, Richard I, John) or sometimes the entire dynasty.
- **Magna Carta**: The 1215 charter forcing King John to accept the king was subject to law.
- **Feudalism**: The medieval system where land was held by lords in exchange for military service and loyalty to the king.
- **Parliament**: The assembly of nobles, clergy, and later commoners that gained power to approve taxation and advise the king.
- **Hundred Years' War**: Conflicts (1337–1453) between Plantagenet kings of England and Valois kings of France over territory and dynasty.
- **Wars of the Roses**: Civil wars (1455–1487) between Lancaster (red rose) and York (white rose) for the throne.
- **Cognatic primogeniture**: Inheritance traced through both male and female lines, used by Yorkists to claim superior right.
- **Statute of Rhuddlan**: The 1284 law establishing English rule over Wales after Edward I's conquest.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The "Angevin Empire"**: Historians debate if 12th-century Plantagenet territories were a unified "empire" or separate feudal holdings with a common ruler.
- **The Princes in the Tower**: The fate of Edward V and his brother Richard, who disappeared in 1483 after being lodged in the Tower by Richard III, remains unknown.
- **Richard III's Usurpation**: Scholars analyze if Richard III's seizure of the throne was political survival or ruthless usurpation.
- **The Causes of the Wars of the Roses**: Origins are debated, including Henry VI's weak rule, the disputed 1399 succession, and empowered nobles with private armies.
