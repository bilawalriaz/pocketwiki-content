# Angle

## Overview
An angle is a geometric figure formed by two lines (or rays) meeting at a common point called the vertex, with each line being a side of the angle. Angles are fundamental in geometry, trigonometry, and applied fields like geography and astronomy, where they describe spatial relationships, rotations, and directions. The concept bridges shape (the figure itself) and quantity (its angular measure), with measurement traditionally tied to circles and rotation.

## Timeline
- **Ancient Greece** — Euclid defines a plane angle as the inclination of two meeting lines; Thales proves the vertical angle theorem.
- **c. 300 BCE** — Euclid's *Elements* formalizes angle definitions and polygon interior-angle sums.
- **1837** — Pierre Wantzel proves most angles cannot be trisected with compass and straightedge.
- **1748** — Euler describes the relationship between circular and hyperbolic angles in *Introduction to the Analysis of the Infinite*.

## Body

### Fundamentals and Measurement
An angle is commonly defined as two rays in a plane sharing a common endpoint (the vertex), though alternative conceptions include the opening between the rays, the enclosed planar region, or the rotation of one ray onto the other. The sides of an angle divide the plane into an interior (angular sector) and exterior region. Notation uses the angle symbol ∠ with one or three points (e.g., ∠BAC, vertex always in the middle); measure is written m∠BAC. Greek or Roman letters often denote angle size. Angular measure is scalar, but signed angles (positive anti-clockwise, negative clockwise) are used in physics and mathematics.

### Units and Systems
The degree (°), radian (rad), and turn are the primary units. A full rotation equals 1 turn or 360°; radians are defined via the ratio of arc length to radius (s/r), making a full angle 2π rad. Degrees were historically chosen so a straight angle is 180°. The centesimal system divides a right angle into 100 gradians. In the International System of Quantities, angles are dimensionless quantities, which simplifies equations but complicates dimensional analysis.

### Types of Angles
Angles are classified by magnitude: zero (0°), acute (<90°), right (90°), obtuse (90°–180°), straight (180°), reflex (180°–360°), and full (360°). Oblique angles are not multiples of a right angle. Adjacent angles share a vertex and side without overlapping interiors; vertical angles are opposite pairs formed by intersecting lines and are always equal. A transversal crossing parallel lines creates corresponding, alternate, and consecutive angles.

### Combining Angle Pairs
Complementary angles sum to 90°, supplementary to 180°, and explementary (or conjugate) to 360°. Adjacent complementary angles form a right angle; adjacent supplementary angles form a linear pair. In right triangles, the acute angles are complementary. Consecutive parallelogram angles and opposite cyclic quadrilateral angles are supplementary.

### Polygon and Plane Angles
Interior angles of a triangle sum to 180°; a convex quadrilateral's sum to 360°; an n-sided convex polygon's sum to (n−2)×180°. An exterior angle is supplementary to its adjacent interior angle; the sum of exterior angles of a convex polygon is 360°. A dihedral angle is formed between two planes, defined as the acute angle between their normal lines.

### Advanced Measurement and Generalizations
Circular measurement defines angle size θ = s/r (radians) or s/C (turns), independent of circle size. Signed angles in standard position use the positive x-axis as the initial side; positive rotations are anti-clockwise. Coterminal angles differ by full turns. Reference angles reduce any angle to an acute value relative to the x-axis. The dot product formula u·v = cos(θ)‖u‖‖v‖ extends angles to vectors and higher-dimensional spaces, including inner product and Riemannian geometries. Hyperbolic angles, arguments of hyperbolic functions, are unbounded and contrast with circular angles.

### Applications
In geography, latitude and longitude are angles subtended at Earth's center. In astronomy, angular separation between stars and angular diameter of objects (e.g., the Moon ≈ 0.5°) are measured. Bearings are azimuths relative to north, positive clockwise. The small-angle formula converts angular measurements to distance/size ratios.

### History and Etymology
The word "angle" derives from Latin *angulus* ("corner"), related to Greek *ankylos* ("crooked") and the Proto-Indo-European root *ank-* ("to bend"). Philosophers debated whether angles are qualities, quantities, or relationships; Euclid adopted the relational view. Proclus categorized angle concepts accordingly.

## Terms
- **Vertex**: The common endpoint of the two sides of an angle.
- **Radian**: A unit of angular measure defined as the angle subtended by an arc equal in length to the radius.
- **Coterminal angles**: Angles that share terminal sides but differ by an integer multiple of a full turn.
- **Dihedral angle**: The angle between two intersecting planes, measured between their normal lines.
- **Reference angle**: The acute angle between the terminal side of an angle in standard position and the x-axis.
- **Transversal**: A line intersecting two or more other lines, creating various angle pairs.
- **Complementary angles**: Two angles whose measures sum to a right angle (90°).
- **Supplementary angles**: Two angles whose measures sum to a straight angle (180°).
- **Explementary angles**: Two angles whose measures sum to a full angle (360°).
- **Signed angle**: An angle with a sign indicating direction of rotation (positive anti-clockwise, negative clockwise).

## Debates and Open Questions
There is no universally agreed formal definition of an angle; valid definitions exist for specific contexts but no single definition captures all aspects of the concept. Philosophers and educators debate whether angles are fundamentally shapes, quantities, or relationships. Some scientists propose treating angles as having their own fundamental dimension to improve dimensional analysis, though this would require revising many established formulas. The historical attribution of the vertical angle theorem to Thales remains a matter of scholarly discussion.
