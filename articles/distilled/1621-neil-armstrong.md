# Neil Armstrong

## Overview
Neil Alden Armstrong (1930–2012) was an American astronaut, aeronautical engineer, naval aviator, and test pilot who commanded the 1969 Apollo 11 mission, becoming the first human to walk on the Moon. His career traced the arc of U.S. aerospace development: from combat missions in the Korean War, through pioneering hypersonic flight in the X-15 rocket plane, to the first orbital docking on Gemini 8, and finally the lunar landing that fulfilled President Kennedy’s 1961 goal. Armstrong’s engineering mindset—colleagues called him the most technically capable pilot of his generation—shaped his calm handling of emergencies, from a stuck thruster on Gemini 8 to a last-second manual landing on the Moon with seconds of fuel remaining. After NASA, he taught aerospace engineering at the University of Cincinnati, served on the Apollo 13 and Challenger investigation commissions, and largely avoided the public spotlight, embodying a reluctant heroism that made him a global icon of exploration and humility.

## Timeline
- **August 5, 1930** — Born in Washington Township, Auglaize County, Ohio.
- **1947–1955** — Attended Purdue University on the Holloway Plan (B.S. Aeronautical Engineering, 1955).
- **1949–1952** — Naval aviator; flew 78 combat missions in the Korean War from USS *Essex* (F9F Panther).
- **1955–1962** — NACA/NASA test pilot at Edwards Air Force Base; flew X-15 seven times (max Mach 5.74, 207,000 ft).
- **September 13, 1962** — Selected for NASA Astronaut Group 2 ("The New Nine").
- **March 16, 1966** — Launched on Gemini 8; achieved first orbital docking; aborted after stuck thruster caused dangerous roll.
- **May 6, 1968** — Ejected from Lunar Landing Research Vehicle (LLRV) seconds before crash at Edwards AFB.
- **July 16, 1969** — Apollo 11 launched on Saturn V from Kennedy Space Center.
- **July 20, 1969** — Lunar Module *Eagle* landed on the Moon at 20:17:40 UTC; Armstrong stepped onto surface at 02:56 UTC July 21, stating "one small step for [a] man."
- **July 24, 1969** — Apollo 11 splashed down in Pacific; crew quarantined 18 days.
- **1971** — Resigned from NASA; began teaching at University of Cincinnati (until 1980).
- **1970, 1986** — Served on Apollo 13 review board and Rogers Commission (Challenger disaster).
- **August 25, 2012** — Died in Cincinnati, Ohio, at age 82, from complications of coronary bypass surgery.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Armstrong was born in rural Ohio and moved 16 times before settling in Wapakoneta in 1944. His fascination with flight began at age two at the Cleveland Air Races; he earned a student pilot certificate at 16, before a driver’s license. An Eagle Scout, he carried a World Scout Badge to the Moon. In 1947 he entered Purdue University on the Holloway Plan, which funded tuition in exchange for Navy service. He chose Purdue over MIT after watching a 1945 football game and on an uncle’s advice. His studies were interrupted by Navy flight training (1949–1952); he returned to Purdue, raised his GPA to 4.8/6.0, and graduated in January 1955. He later earned an M.S. in Aerospace Engineering from USC (1970).

### Naval Service and Korean War
Called to active duty in January 1949, Armstrong trained at Pensacola and Corpus Christi, qualifying as a naval aviator in August 1950. Assigned to VF-51 aboard USS *Essex*, he flew the Grumman F9F Panther. On September 3, 1951, during a low bombing run, his wing struck a cable strung across hills, shearing off 6 feet (1.8 m). He flew the damaged jet to friendly territory and ejected; his parachute drifted over land, where a jeep retrieved him. He flew 78 missions (121 hours), earning the Air Medal with two gold stars. His regular commission ended in February 1952; he served in the Navy Reserve until resigning his commission in 1960.

### Test Pilot Career at Edwards
After Purdue, Armstrong joined the NACA High-Speed Flight Station at Edwards AFB (July 1955). He flew chase for air-drops, piloted modified bombers, and served as project pilot on Century Series fighters (F-100, F-101, F-104, F-105, F-106). He flew the Bell X-1B and the North American X-15 seven times, reaching Mach 5.74 and 207,000 ft (63 km). On his sixth X-15 flight (April 20, 1962), a g-limiting demonstration caused the aircraft to balloon to 140,000 ft; he overshot Edwards by 40 miles but landed safely—the longest X-15 flight by time and ground track. Colleagues praised his engineering insight: Milt Thompson called him "the most technically capable of the early X-15 pilots," while Bill Dana said his mind "absorbed things like a sponge." Air Force pilots like Chuck Yeager and Pete Knight, lacking engineering degrees, viewed his style as "more mechanical than flying." Armstrong logged 2,400 flying hours at Edwards and became a NASA employee when NACA was absorbed on October 1, 1958.

### Astronaut Selection and Gemini Program
Armstrong was ineligible for Project Mercury (restricted to military test pilots) but joined the X-20 Dyna-Soar pilot group in 1960. In June 1962, NASA opened Gemini selection to civilians; Armstrong applied a week late, but a colleague slipped his application into the pile. He was selected September 13, 1962, as one of two civilians in "The New Nine."

**Gemini 5 (backup):** Armstrong and Elliot See backed up Cooper and Conrad (August 1965).  
**Gemini 8 (prime):** Launched March 16, 1966, with David Scott. They achieved the first docking of two spacecraft (with an Agena target). A stuck OAMS thruster sent the docked pair into a roll approaching one revolution per second. Armstrong undocked, but the roll accelerated; he engaged the Reentry Control System (RCS), stabilizing the craft. Mission rules required immediate reentry, cutting the flight short and canceling Scott’s EVA. Post-flight analysis blamed damaged wiring; Gene Kranz later acknowledged controllers had failed to treat the docked pair as a single spacecraft. Armstrong received a $678 raise, becoming NASA’s highest-paid astronaut.  
**Gemini 11 (backup):** Served as backup command pilot and CAPCOM (September 1966).

### Apollo Program and Apollo 11
After the Apollo 1 fire (January 1967), Slayton told the astronaut corps that the men in the room would fly the first lunar missions. Armstrong was assigned backup commander for Apollo 9, then—after crew rotations driven by LM delays and Michael Collins’ medical issue—commander of Apollo 11 with Collins (CMP) and Buzz Aldrin (LMP). Slayton offered to replace Aldrin with Jim Lovell; Armstrong declined, believing Lovell deserved his own command.

**Training:** Armstrong flew the Lunar Landing Research Vehicle (LLRV) to simulate 1/6th gravity. On May 6, 1968, controls degraded at 100 ft; he ejected safely seconds before the vehicle crashed. He insisted the LLRV/LLTV was essential for lunar landing success.

**Lunar Descent (July 20, 1969):** Three minutes into powered descent, Armstrong saw craters passing early, indicating a long landing. Computer alarms (1202, 1201) signaled executive overflows caused by the docking radar left on (Aldrin’s choice). At ~500 ft, Armstrong took manual control to avoid a boulder field, flying past the planned site. Fuel warnings sounded; post-mission analysis showed 45–50 seconds of propellant remained at touchdown (20:17:40 UTC). Armstrong announced, "Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed."

**First Moon Walk (July 21, 02:56 UTC):** Armstrong descended the ladder and stepped onto the surface, stating the prepared line: "That's one small step for [a] man, one giant leap for mankind." The indefinite article "a" is inaudible in recordings; Armstrong later conceded he likely dropped it, though digital analysis has been claimed to detect it. An estimated 530 million viewers watched live. Aldrin joined him 19 minutes later. They deployed experiments, planted the U.S. flag (rod failed to fully extend, giving a wavy appearance), and received a call from President Nixon. Armstrong walked 65 yards to East Crater, the farthest traverse of the mission. EVA lasted 2.5 hours.

**Return:** A broken ascent-engine ignition switch was bypassed with a pen. *Eagle* docked with *Columbia*; the crew splashed down July 24, 1969, and completed an 18-day quarantine and 38-day world tour.

### Life After Apollo
**Teaching (1971–1980):** Armstrong resigned from NASA in 1971 to become University Professor of Aerospace Engineering at the University of Cincinnati. He taught core classes, created graduate courses in aircraft design and experimental flight mechanics, and was known as a tough grader. He avoided NASA-funded research to prevent favoritism perceptions. He resigned in 1980 when the university became a state school, citing increased bureaucracy and collective bargaining.

**NASA Commissions:**  
- **Apollo 13 (1970):** On Edgar Cortright’s board, Armstrong traced the explosion to a 28-volt thermostat switch in an oxygen tank that should have been 65-volt. He opposed the $40M tank redesign, arguing only the switch needed fixing.  
- **Rogers Commission (1986):** Vice chairman investigating Challenger. He conducted private interviews to identify the O-ring failure cause and limited recommendations to nine to ensure NASA action.  
- **National Commission on Space (1986):** Chaired by Thomas Paine; recommended a permanent lunar base by 2006 and Mars by 2015—largely ignored post-Challenger.

**Business Activities:** Spokesman for Chrysler (1979), General Time, Bankers Association of America (U.S. firms only). Board member: Gates Learjet (chaired technical committee, set business-jet records), Cincinnati Gas & Electric, Taft Broadcasting, Thiokol (post-Challenger), United Airlines, Eaton Corporation (chaired subsidiary AIL Systems until 2002).

**Expeditions:** Honorary President of 1976 Cueva de los Tayos cave expedition (Ecuador). Joined 1985 North Pole trip with Edmund Hillary, Steve Fossett, and others (kept private).

**Public Profile:** Described as a "reluctant American hero." Declined most interviews; hosted *First Flights* (A&E, 1991–93). Guarded his likeness: refused MTV (1981), sued Hallmark (1994, settled, donated to Purdue). Stopped autographs c. 1993 after discovering forgeries online. Wrote Eagle Scout congratulations until the 1990s.

**Personal Life:** Married Janet Shearon (1956–1994); three children. Daughter Karen died of brain tumor complications (1962). Married Carol Held Knight (1994). Suffered mild heart attack (1991); reattached severed fingertip (1978). Identified as deist; target of Islam-conversion hoaxes (1983 onward). Visited ancestral seat Langholm, Scotland (1972), made freeman.

### Illness, Death, and Legacy
Armstrong underwent bypass surgery August 7, 2012; complications caused his death August 25. President Obama called him "among the greatest of American heroes." Family requested a private funeral; cremated remains scattered from USS *Philippine Sea* September 14. A 2014 malpractice suit against Mercy Health–Fairfield Hospital (alleging delayed surgery after pacemaker wire removal caused fatal bleeding) settled for $6M under confidentiality.

**Honors:** Presidential Medal of Freedom (1969), Congressional Space Medal of Honor (1978), Congressional Gold Medal (2009), Collier Trophy (1969), Sylvanus Thayer Award (1971), Wright Brothers Memorial Trophy (2001). Elected to National Academy of Engineering (1978), American Philosophical Society (2001). Lunar crater *Armstrong* and asteroid 6469 *Armstrong* named for him. NASA Dryden renamed Neil A. Armstrong Flight Research Center (2014); Plum Brook Station renamed Neil A. Armstrong Test Facility. U.S. Navy research vessel *RV Neil Armstrong* delivered 2015. Purdue’s Neil Armstrong Hall of Engineering dedicated 2007. Authorized biography *First Man* (2005, James R. Hansen); film 2018. Memorabilia auctions (2018–19) totaled $16.7M; papers donated to Purdue (450+ boxes).

## Terms
- **Holloway Plan**: U.S. Navy program (1946–1950) funding two years of college, then flight training and service, then degree completion.
- **NACA (National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics)**: U.S. federal agency (1915–1958) for aeronautical research; absorbed into NASA.
- **X-15**: Rocket-powered research aircraft (1959–1968) exploring hypersonic flight and spaceplane reentry; flown by Armstrong seven times.
- **Gemini 8**: 1966 NASA mission achieving first orbital docking; aborted after a stuck OAMS thruster induced a dangerous roll.
- **OAMS (Orbit Attitude and Maneuvering System)**: Gemini spacecraft thrusters for orbital maneuvering and attitude control.
- **RCS (Reentry Control System)**: Redundant thruster system used for reentry; engaged by Armstrong to stop the Gemini 8 roll.
- **LLRV/LLTV (Lunar Landing Research/Training Vehicle)**: "Flying Bedsteads" using a turbofan to simulate 1/6th gravity for LM descent practice.
- **1202/1201 Alarms**: Apollo Guidance Computer executive overflow alarms during lunar descent, caused by docking radar data overload.
- **Tranquility Base**: Name assigned by Armstrong to the Apollo 11 landing site in Mare Tranquillitatis.
- **Rogers Commission**: Presidential commission (1986) investigating the Space Shuttle *Challenger* disaster; Armstrong served as vice chairman.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The "a" in the first-step quote**: Recordings do not clearly capture "for a man"; Armstrong insisted he said it, later conceded he may have dropped it. Digital audio analyses have claimed to detect the missing article, but linguists remain skeptical; NASA transcripts show "a" in parentheses.
- **Gemini 8 responsibility**: Some in the Astronaut Office initially criticized Armstrong and Scott for "botching" the mission; later analysis (Gene Kranz) shifted blame to ground control for not treating docked vehicles as one spacecraft and for inadequate malfunction procedures.
- **Apollo 11 crew selection**: Slayton offered Armstrong the chance to replace Aldrin with Lovell; Armstrong declined. The decision for Armstrong to exit first was attributed post-hoc to LM hatch geometry (inward-opening to the right) and protocol (commander first), though the hatch rationale was unknown to decision-makers at the time.
- **Medical care at death**: A 2014 malpractice suit alleged that after bypass surgery, nurses removed temporary pacemaker wires, causing internal bleeding; doctors moved Armstrong to the catheterization lab instead of directly to the OR, delaying surgery. Two reviewing physicians called this a serious error; the hospital settled for $6M with a confidentiality clause.
