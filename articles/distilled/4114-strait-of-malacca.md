# Strait of Malacca

## Overview
The Strait of Malacca is a narrow, 900-kilometer-long waterway between the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra, connecting the Indian and Pacific Oceans. It is one of the world's most vital shipping lanes, carrying approximately 25% of global traded goods, including over 35% of seaborne oil. Its strategic importance has made it a historically contested region and a modern focal point for international trade security.

## Timeline
- **7th century** — The Srivijaya empire rises, gaining control of the strait and dominating regional trade for centuries.
- **15th century** — The Malacca Sultanate becomes a major power controlling the strait.
- **17th century** — The strait becomes the main shipping channel between the Indian and Pacific Oceans.
- **19th-20th centuries** — Control passes to the British Straits Settlements and later involves the city-state of Singapore.
- **2003** — Chinese President Hu Jintao coins the term "Malacca dilemma" to describe China's vulnerability.
- **2004** — Regional navies increase patrols after a rise in piracy incidents.
- **2005** — The strait is declared a high-risk area due to increased attacks; later removed after improved cooperation.
- **2008** — Over 94,000 vessels pass through, making it the world's busiest strait.
- **2024** — Over 35% of seaborne oil and 20% of gas flows through the strait.

## Body

### Etymology and Extent
The name "Malacca" is traditionally linked to the Malacca tree (Phyllanthus emblica). According to tradition, Parameswara, founder of the Malacca Sultanate, named the location "Melaka" after resting under such a tree. The International Hydrographic Organization defines the strait's precise geographical limits, with its western entrance between Sumatra and Thailand and its eastern exit near Singapore.

### Historical Significance
Before the 7th century, traders from Arabia, Africa, India, and Persia used ports like Kedah on the Malay Peninsula. The rise of the Srivijaya empire in the 7th century marked the beginning of direct control over the strait itself, which it maintained for about 700 years by dominating the spice trade. Control later passed to the Malacca Sultanate, the Johor Sultanate, and European colonial powers like the British, before the modern era of independent states.

### Economic and Strategic Importance
As the primary shipping channel between the Indian and Pacific Oceans, the strait links major Asian economies. It is a critical chokepoint, narrowing to just 2.8 km at the Phillip Channel near Singapore. The maximum vessel size that can transit is known as the "Malaccamax." Its strategic value is highlighted by China's "Malacca dilemma," a term coined in 2003 to describe its vulnerability, as roughly 80% of China's imported crude oil passes through the strait.

### Security and International Cooperation
Piracy and smuggling are persistent concerns. The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) grants coastal states territorial waters, which has sometimes complicated anti-piracy efforts as incidents in national waters are classified as armed robbery. In response, regional cooperation initiatives like the Malacca Strait Patrols (2004) and MALSINDO trilateral agreement were established. These efforts, supported by voluntary contributions from countries like Japan and China, have helped reduce piracy incidents from highs in the 2000s to near zero in recent years.

### Shipping Hazards
Beyond piracy, hazards include 34 shipwrecks in the commercial shipping channel, annual haze from Sumatran wildfires reducing visibility, and the risk of collisions in congested waters, as demonstrated by the 2017 USS John S. McCain collision.

## Terms
- **Malaccamax**: The maximum size of a vessel that can pass through the Strait of Malacca, determined by its shallowest depth.
- **Chokepoint**: A narrow passage through which shipping traffic must pass, making it strategically vulnerable.
- **Malacca dilemma**: A term coined in 2003 to describe China's strategic vulnerability due to its heavy reliance on the strait for energy imports.
- **UNCLOS (United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea)**: An international treaty establishing a legal framework for all marine and maritime activities, including territorial waters.
- **EEZ (Exclusive Economic Zone)**: A maritime zone where a state has special rights regarding the exploration and use of marine resources.
- **Traffic Separation Scheme (TSS)**: A maritime traffic-management system that separates opposing streams of traffic into defined lanes.
- **Littoral states**: Countries that border a major body of water; for the strait, these are Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary ongoing tension is between state sovereignty and the need for international security cooperation. Littoral states are historically reluctant to cede control over their territorial waters and EEZs, even for security purposes. While cooperative mechanisms exist, they rely on voluntary contributions and coordination, raising questions about their long-term sustainability and effectiveness against evolving threats like environmental degradation and potential future disruptions.
